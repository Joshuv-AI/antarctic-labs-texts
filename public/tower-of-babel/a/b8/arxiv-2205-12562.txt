Power-based Safety Layer for Aerial Vehicles in Physical Interaction using Lyapunov Exponents
Eugenio Cuniato, Nicholas Lawrance, Marco Tognon, Roland Siegwart

Abstract
As the performance of autonomous systems increases, safety concerns arise, especially when operating in non-structured environments. To deal with these concerns, this work presents a safety layer for mechanical systems that detects and responds to unstable dynamics caused by external disturbances. The safety layer is implemented independently and on top of already present nominal controllers, like pose or wrench tracking, and limits power flow when the system’s response would lead to instability. This approach is based on the computation of the Largest Lyapunov Exponent of the system’s error dynamics, which represent a measure of the dynamics’ divergence or convergence rate. By actively computing this metric, divergent and possibly dangerous system behaviors can be promptly detected. The Largest Lyapunov Exponent is then used in combination with Control Barrier Functions to impose power limit constraints on a jerk controlled system. The proposed architecture is experimentally validated on an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle both in free flight and interaction tasks.



I
Introduction

Aerial robotics is a promising research field with an increasing number of applications as well as innovative aerial systems
[
1
,
2
]
.
Among them, the development of fully actuated Micro Aerial Vehicles has grown rapidly in recent years
[
3
,
4
,
5

.
A specific class of these are tiltrotor Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicles (see Fig.
1
), capable of generating a six degrees of freedom control wrench independently of their attitude.
Their actuation capability has proved fundamental for obtaining high disturbance rejection and performance in flight, while also allowing physical interaction tasks that might be otherwise impossible for standard multirotors
[
6
,
7
]
.

As these platforms are employed in more and more tasks, safety concerns start arising, especially during Aerial Physical Interaction.
In case of unexpected disturbances, some controllers might lead to instability or damages of the platform if the danger is not detected and addressed in time.
A possibility in this case would be resorting to robust control techniques with nonlinear disturbance observers for trajectory tracking
[
8
]
or complex interaction
[
9
]
tasks.
Another well-known approach in the literature employs passivity-based control laws, that are notably robust against disturbances
[
10
,
11
]
.
In this scenario, energy tanks have been successfully exploited to increase the robustness of the overall control algorithm
[
12
,
13
]
.
They can be used as a versatile safety layer on top of non-passive nominal controllers to enforce the passivity of the tank-augmented dynamics.
In the energy tank theory, the passivity constraint acts to limit the system’s energy, but only implicitly the power.
This requires the definition of an amount of energy that the tank can allow before entering into a strict passivity regime.
Some insights and interesting approaches on how to choose this energy budget can be found in the literature
[
14
,
15
]
.
However, how to generally approach this problem remains an open question.
An insufficient energy budget can lead to a premature interruption of the task, whereas excess energy no longer ensures safety.

Fig. 1
:
Picture of the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle aerial robot equipped with a rigid interaction tool.

The main goal of this paper is to overcome the limitation of energy budgets while still retaining the versatility of the safety layer approach.
To accomplish this goal, two elements are necessary:
(i)
to impose constraints directly on the power level; and
(ii)
a metric that can change the power limit according to a
danger level
.
Regarding the first point, Control Barrier Functions have been widely used to enforce safety constraints on mechanical systems
[
16
]
.
This approach has already been employed to enforce energy tank constraints in integral form
[
17
]
, as well as power passivity constraints in
[
18
]
.
This last work constantly enforces a passive constraint (zero power flow limit) in all cases, without allowing for positive or negative limits.
In our framework we allow instead for the full spectrum of power limits, allowing for positive limits when the system is safely accomplishing the task, or imposing dissipation when some unexpected dangerous event happens (sudden impacts, changes in the environment, loss of contact during interaction, etc.).

Power safety policies were already employed inside an energy tank framework in
[
19
]
. In this work, different power flow adaptation laws were presented specifically tailored for the proposed interaction task.
In our work we employ a finite-time computation of the Largest Lyapunov Exponent of the error dynamics
[
20
]
as a metric for power adaptation, without the necessity of the energy tank framework.
The computation of Lyapunov exponents, and in particular of the Largest Lyapunov Exponent, gives a measure of convergence of adjacent state trajectories, showing possible unstable or chaotic behaviors. For example, Largest Lyapunov Exponent has been used in
[
21
]
to predict vessel capsizes.

Contributions

In this work we design a power flow safety layer with an Largest Lyapunov Exponent-based metric.
The Largest Lyapunov Exponent metric measures the closed-loop system convergence rate and can be used to determine when the system is showing a divergent behavior.
This allows the system to be passive and even enforce dissipation only when divergence is detected, otherwise allowing the system to complete the commanded task.

Importantly, we analytically characterize the constraints imposed on the system dynamics by the chosen safety policy, showing that the control objectives remain reachable.
Moreover, the proposed approach does not require the definition of energy budgets, a common limitation with existing energy-tank based approaches, which can lead to premature interruption of the desired task.
In the end, the safety layer is experimentally validated on both pose and wrench tracking tasks with an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle.
In particular, we focus on the interaction with moving objects, like a cart pushing task.
Notice that we propose this framework for aerial robots because for this class of systems safety is of particular importance.
However, this safety layer can be generally applied to any fully actuated mechanical system.

III
Safety layer

This section presents the power flow-based safety layer.
We first show how to compute the Largest Lyapunov Exponent on the closed loop error dynamics.
Then we present the CBF-based optimization problem used to control the system’s power flow.
Finally, a Largest Lyapunov Exponent-based power adaptation law is designed and enforced with the CBF framework.
The safety layer is schematically represented in Fig.
2
, together with overall system architecture.

III-A

Largest Lyapunov Exponent computation

Consider a linear (or locally linearized) autonomous dynamic system

\dot{\bm{x}}=\bm{U}\bm{x},

(11)

with system’s state
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Jacobian matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
We assume
\bm{U}
to be diagonalizable with
n
distinct eigenvalues.
These dynamics can be projected along the system’s eigenvectors
\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to obtain the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
as

\dot{x}_{i}=\lambda_{i}x_{i},

(12)

where
x_{i}=\bm{x}\cdot\bm{w}_{i}
is the component of the vector
\bm{x}
in the direction of
\bm{w}_{i}
, with
i=1\dots n
.
As it is well known from the theory of linear systems, all these single evolutions contribute together to the system’s modal decomposition

\bm{x}(t)=c_{1}\bm{w}_{1}e^{\lambda_{1}t}+c_{2}\bm{w}_{2}e^{\lambda_{2}t}+\dots+c_{n}\bm{w}_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}t},

(13)

where the coefficients
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
depend on the system’s initial conditions.
Considering
\lambda^{*}
as the biggest eigenvalue, the other contributions in (
13
) can be assumed negligible as
t\rightarrow\infty
, with the dynamics evolving according to
\bm{x}=c^{*}\bm{w}^{*}e^{\lambda^{*}t}
. We refer to
\lambda^{*}
as the Largest Lyapunov Exponent, computed with

\lambda^{*}=\frac{\bm{x}^{\top}\dot{\bm{x}}}{\|\bm{x}\|^{2}}.

(14)

In practice, this punctual computation of
\lambda^{*}
will be affected by both noise and oscillatory behaviors of the dynamics, that will not be perfectly exponential
[
20
]
.
We then estimate the Largest Lyapunov Exponent by low-pass filtering the quantity in (
14
), obtaining
\hat{\lambda}^{*}
.
This value determines if the system is currently asymptotically stable
\hat{\lambda}^{*}<0
or unstable
\hat{\lambda}^{*}>0
.
Notice that this quantity is well defined also when asymptotically stable dynamics approach the equilibrium in
\left(\dot{\bm{x}},\bm{x}\right)=\left(0,0\right)
.
In that case, it is

\lim_{\left(\dot{\bm{x}},\bm{x}\right)\to\left(0,0\right)}\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{\text{max}}[U],

(15)

with
\lambda_{\text{max}}[U]
the biggest real part of the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix
\bm{U}
. The Largest Lyapunov Exponent estimation for both pose and wrench controllers can be now derived as follows.

and
\bm{A}_{\tau}
are diagonal, power flow and control input constraints can be independently imposed for each degree of freedom.

III-C

Power adaptation law

We want to dissipate power when the closed loop dynamics start to diverge (
\hat{\lambda}^{*}>0
).
At the same time, we allow for power generation when the dynamics are converging (
\hat{\lambda}^{*}<0
).
One possible adaptation law could just be proportional
\bar{p}=-k_{\lambda}\hat{\lambda}^{*}
.
Since the imposed power dissipation must always be feasible (it is not possible to dissipate if the system has no energy left), we choose to adapt the power flow limit
\bar{p}(\hat{\lambda}^{*},e_{v,i})
as

\begin{cases}\bar{p}=-k_{\lambda}\hat{\lambda}^{*}&\text{if}\;\hat{\lambda}^{*}<0\\
\bar{p}=-k_{\lambda}\hat{\lambda}^{*}e_{v,i}^{2}&\text{if}\;\hat{\lambda}^{*}>0\end{cases}.

(29)

This way, the gain
k_{\lambda}
represents a damping coefficient when a power dissipation is required.

IV
Safe sets analysis

The presented safety layer constrains the system’s evolution into the safe sets
\mathcal{C}_{s,p}
and
\mathcal{C}_{s,\tau}
for the position and wrench tracking dynamics, respectively.
Importantly, we show which limitations these sets impose and that the control objective is always reachable even when these limits are enforced.

IV-A

Pose tracking set

Fig. 3
:
On the left, forward invariant safe set (white) for the system’s position dynamics. In the center, the set without acceleration measurements exceeds the original set. On the right, the set is scaled to fit inside the original one. The dashed lines represent the asymptotes of the set. The red line represents the scaling direction. Sets obtained obtained considering
m_{i}=$4.58\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}$

and
k_{\lambda}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.

The safe set for the decoupled pose tracking controller dynamics is given by
\mathcal{C}_{s,p}=\{(\tilde{e}_{p,i},\tilde{e}_{v,i})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}:\tilde{e}_{v,i}\tau_{p,i}\leq\bar{p}(\hat{\lambda}^{*},e_{v,i})\}
, where
\tau_{p,i}
represents the corresponding element of the pose control vector in (
4
).
By considering the position dynamics only, the obtained safe set is shown on the left in Fig.
3
.
From the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{s,p}
, considering (
4
) and (
29
), one can find the boundaries of the pose safe set to be an hyperbola in the state space, with asymptotes
\tilde{e}_{v,i}=0
and
\tilde{e}_{v,i}=-\frac{k_{p,i}}{d_{v,i}}\tilde{e}_{p,i}
.
Fig.
3
shows how the power flow adaptation law in (
29
) represents a safe set that contains the equilibrium at the origin
(\tilde{e}_{p,i},\tilde{e}_{v,i})=(0,0)
.
Moreover, the safe set cuts out of the feasible space most of the bottom-left and top-right quadrants, in which the position and velocity errors are aligned.
This is the most dangerous situation according to the Largest Lyapunov Exponent metric, in which the position error norm is increasing.

In case the Largest Lyapunov Exponent is computed using (
18
) but the acceleration error is approximated to be
\dot{\tilde{e}}_{v,i}=0
, the real value of the acceleration results in a mismatch on the
\hat{\lambda}^{*}
estimation.
This causes an enlargement of the safe set around the origin compared to the original one, as shown in Fig.
3
.
This especially expands in the dangerous regions in which the position and velocity errors are aligned.
In these regions, from Fig.
3
we see an increase in the maximum velocity of around double the original value (from
\sim$0.25\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
to
\sim$0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
).
This translates to a possible increase of the system kinetic energy and thus a reduction of the controller safety.

Since the enlarged set still resembles an hyperbola with the same asymptotes of the nominal one, it might be scaled to fit inside the latter.
The scaling can be performed by imposing stricter limits on the power flow as
\bar{p}_{s}=k_{\lambda}^{\prime}\bar{p}
, where
\bar{p}_{s}
is the new imposed power limit and
k_{\lambda}^{\prime}<1
.
The direction in which the biggest enlargement happens is the bisector of the two asymptotes, which has equation
\tilde{e}_{v,i}=\frac{1}{k_{p,i}}\left(d_{v,i}-\sqrt{k_{p,i}^{2}+d_{v,i}^{2}}\right)\tilde{e}_{p,i}
.
By enforcing the two sets to match along this direction, one finds
k_{\lambda}^{\prime}=-\frac{\hat{\lambda}^{*}}{c_{1}-\hat{\lambda}^{*}}
, with

c_{1}=\frac{1}{2m_{i}}\left(d_{v,i}+\sqrt{(d_{v,i}^{2}+k_{p,i}^{2})}\right).

(30)

IV-B

Wrench tracking set

Since we are interested on the dynamics of the platform under the wrench tracking control action, we define the wrench control safe set in terms of the platform speed and wrench as
\mathcal{C}_{s,\tau}=\{(\tau_{f,i},\prescript{}{B}{v_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}:\prescript{}{B}{v_{i}}\tau_{f,i}\leq\bar{p}(\hat{\lambda}^{*},\prescript{}{B}{v_{i}})\}
.

Fig. 4
:
Safe sets considering a force setpoint
\bm{\tau}_{\text{ext}}^{\text{ref}}=4N
(indicated with a black cross) with a surface with damping coefficient
d_{s,i}=$5\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
and stiffness coefficient
k_{s,i}=$30\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
(left) or
k_{s,i}=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
(right). The power coefficient is
k_{\lambda}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{W}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.

First, consider the case of activation of the wrench tracking controller when there is no interaction surface, or the case in which the interaction surface moves away making the wrench tracking not possible anymore.
Without the presented safety layer, the wrench tracking errors would then increase causing the platform to accelerate in the desired force direction and diverge.
With the proposed power limitation, as soon as the force dynamics start to diverge, the correspondent Largest Lyapunov Exponent becomes positive, enforcing a dissipative behavior and eventually stopping the platform.
When this is the case, no wrench reference is possible to be achieved and the safe set collapses to the axis
\prescript{}{B}{v_{i}}=0
.
On the other hand, when there is a surface on which the platform can push, the safe set assumes the shape in Fig.
4
.
To obtain this figure it is necessary to specify a model for the environment such to define an interaction force feedback
\bm{\tau}_{\text{ext}}
on the vehicle.
In this case we suppose to interact with a fixed environment, whose restitution force we model as
\tau_{\text{ext,i}}=-d_{s,i}\prescript{}{B}{v_{i}}-k_{s,i}\tilde{e}_{s,i}
, with
\tilde{e}_{s,i}
elements of a position error vector
\tilde{\bm{e}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
between the vehicle and the surface.

The set in Fig.
4
depends on the damping and stiffness characteristics of the environment but the peculiarity is that the force setpoint is always part of the set and thus reachable.
Moreover, the system’s speed is limited more and more as we approach the desired force.
By changing the surface parameters, specifically when increasing the ratio between stiffness and damping, the gap of allowed velocity narrows, but the desired force remains feasible.
This might be explained by the higher restitution force of the surface.
With higher forces applied on the platform, the safety layer would impose stricter limits on the system’s velocity in order to keep the power flow limited.
Notice that the specific surface properties are here used just to give an hint of what effect these power limitations have while in interaction.
They are not necessary to implement the proposed safety framework.

VI
Conclusions

This work presented a safety layer for an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle under pose tracking or wrench tracking controllers.
The method is based on power flow regulation and applicable to other fully actuated mechanical systems, since it relies only on the description of their closed loop dynamics.
Moreover, we imposed the power flow adaptive law with Control Barrier Functions and proved it experimentally on the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle platform.
Future works will investigate the proposed safety framework also in Human Robot Interaction tasks.
