On the correspondence between perfect matchings and compatible pairs for affine cluster algebra
Ivan Ip, Duy Phan

Abstract
We study cluster algebra of affine type A 1 ( 1 ) A_{1}^{(1)} by using two methods including counting the numbers of perfect matchings on snake graphs and compatible pairs on maximal Dyck paths. We find that the sum of coefficients of the terms in the Laurent polynomials of these cluster variables are odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers. In addition, we prove that the numbers of non-decreasing Dyck paths of even lengths are also odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers. As a consequence, we define explicit bijective correspondences among three combinatorial models, including perfect matchings on the snake graph, compatible pairs on the maximal Dyck path, and non-decreasing Dyck paths of even lengths.

2.1
Cluster algebra

We review the construction of cluster algebra, first introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky

To define cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
, we must first fix its ground ring.

Definition 2.1
.

A
semifield

(\mathbb{P},\oplus,\circ)
is an abelian multiplicative group
(\mathbb{P},\circ)
together with a binary operation
\oplus
such that

\oplus:\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}

(p,q)\mapsto p\oplus q

(2.1)

is commutative, associative, and distributive:

p\circ(q\oplus r)=p\circ q\oplus p\circ r

(2.2)

Remark 2.2
.

The binary operation
\oplus
may not be invertible.

Let
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
be the group ring of
(\mathbb{P},\circ)
, which is torsion free. Hence we can define the field of rational functions
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})
in
n
variables with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
seed
is a triple
\Sigma=(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},B)
where

•

\textbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}\}
is a transcendental basis of
\mathcal{F}
over
\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}
.

•

\textbf{y}=\{y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{n}\}
is an
n
-tuple of elements
y_{i}\in\mathbb{P}
.

•

B=(b_{ij})
is a skew-symmetrizable
n\times n
integer matrix.

The set
x
is called a
cluster
and its elements
x_{i}
are the
cluster variables
. The set
y
is called the
coefficient tuple
and
B
is called the
exchange matrix
.

Throughout the paper, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we will use the notation

\displaystyle[x]_{+}:=\max(x,0).

(2.3)

Definition 2.4
.

A
mutation

\mu_{k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},B)
is a new seed
\Sigma^{\prime}:=(\textbf{x}^{\prime},\textbf{y}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
such that

•

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:=(\mathbf{x}\setminus\{x_{k}\})\cup\{x_{k}^{\prime}\}
where
x_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
is determined by the
exchange relation

x_{k}^{\prime}:=\frac{y_{k}\Pi x_{i}^{[b_{ik}]_{+}}+\Pi x_{i}^{[-b_{ik}]_{+}}}{(y_{k}\oplus 1)x_{k}},

(2.4)

•

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}:=(y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime},...,y_{n}^{\prime})
where

y_{j}^{\prime}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{k}^{-1}&\text{ if }j=k,\\
y_{j}y_{k}^{[b_{kj}]_{+}}(y_{k}\oplus 1)^{-b_{kj}}&\text{ if }j\neq k,\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

•

B^{\prime}:=(b_{ij}^{\prime})
where

b_{ij}^{\prime}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-b_{ij}&\text{ if }i=k\text{ or }j=k,\\
b_{ij}+[-b_{ik}]_{+}b_{kj}+b_{ik}[b_{kj}]_{+}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

Definition 2.5
.

Two seeds
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
are called

if there is a finite sequence of mutations
\mu=\mu_{i_{s}}...\mu_{i_{2}}\mu_{i_{1}}
such that
\mu\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the union of all clusters
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
belonging to a seed
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
that is mutation equivalent to the initial seed
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
. Now, we are ready to define cluster algebra.

Definition 2.6
.

The
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
is the
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
-subalgebra of the field
\mathcal{F}
generated by the set of all cluster variables

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B):=\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}]

(2.7)

We say that the cluster algebra is
coefficient free
if
\mathbb{P}=1
, in which
\mathbb{ZP}=\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
tropical semifield

(\text{Trop}(u_{1},...,u_{m}),\oplus,\circ)
is defined to be the semifield of monomials in independent variables
u_{j}
’s with usual multiplication and semifield addition
\oplus
given by

\prod_{j}u_{j}^{a_{j}}\oplus\prod_{j}u_{j}^{b_{j}}=\prod_{j}u_{j}^{\min(a_{j},b_{j})}.

(2.8)

Note that the group ring of
\text{Trop}(u_{1},...,u_{m})
is isomorphic to the ring of Laurent polynomials in the variables
u_{j}
.

Definition 2.8
.

A cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
is said to have
principal coefficients
if the coefficient semifield
\mathbb{P}=\text{Trop}(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{n})
is the tropical semifield with the initial coefficient tuple
\mathbf{y}=\{y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{n}\}
as a set of generators. In this case, we say that the corresponding cluster algebra is of
geometric type
.

Any rank

In the following, a rank

\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(b,c)
defined by the exchange matrix

-c&0\end{pmatrix}

x_{n-1}x_{n+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{n}^{b}+1&\text{for $n$ odd},\\

\end{array}\right.

(2.17)

To express greedy elements in
\mathcal{A}(b,c)

Definition 2.22

Lemma 4.1

(4.1)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.31

\sigma_{1}(x[a_{1},a_{2}])=x[a_{1},2[a_{1}]_{+}-a_{2}]\text{ and }\sigma_{2}(x[a_{1},a_{2}])=x[2[a_{2}]_{+}-a_{1},a_{2}].

x_{3}=\sigma_{2}(x_{1})=\sigma_{2}(x[-1,0])=x[1,0].

Suppose that

\displaystyle x_{n+2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}(\sigma_{1}(x_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}(\sigma_{1}(x[n-2,n-3]))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}(x[n-2,n-1])

\displaystyle=x[n,n-1].

Hence the statement is true by induction.

Lemma 4.4
.

(S_{1},S_{2})
is a compatible pair in
\mathcal{D}^{(n+1)\times n}
if and only if

\{j-i|u_{i}\in S_{1},v_{j}\in S_{2}\}\cap\{0,1\}=\emptyset.

(4.7)

Proof.

One direction is clear:

•

if
u_{i}\in S_{1},v_{i}\in S_{2}
for some
1\leq i\leq n
, then
(A_{i}A_{i+1})^{\circ}=\{B_{i}\}
. However,

f(B_{i},A_{i}A_{i+1})=g(B_{i},A_{i}A_{i+1})=-2,

so
(S_{1},S_{2})
is not compatible by Proposition
4.3
;

•

if
u_{i}\in S_{1},v_{i+1}\in S_{2}
for some
0\leq i\leq n-1
then for any
C\in(A_{i}A_{i+2})^{\circ}=\{B_{i},A_{i+1},B_{i+1}\}
,

so
(S_{1},S_{2})
is not compatible again by Proposition
4.3
.

Conversely, if

\{j-i|u_{i}\in S_{1},v_{j}\in S_{2}\}\cap\{0,1\}=\emptyset

then for any
u_{i}\in S_{1},v_{j}\in S_{2}
, either
j-i>1
or
i>j
. Suppose on the contrary that both

f(A_{j},A_{i}A_{j+1})\leq-1\text{ and }g(A_{i+1},A_{i}A_{j+1})\leq-1.

Case 1:
Let
u_{i}\in S_{1}
and
v_{j}\in S_{2}
with
j-i>1
. Then we have

\displaystyle|(A_{i}A_{j})_{1}\cap S_{1}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(|(A_{i}A_{j})_{2}|-f(A_{j},A_{i}A_{j+1})\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigg((j-i-1)-(-1)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(j-i),

(note that
|(A_{i}A_{j})_{2}|=j-i-1
when
i=0
) and

\displaystyle|(A_{i+1}A_{j+1})_{2}\cap S_{2}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(|(A_{i+1}A_{j+1})_{1}|-g(A_{i+1},A_{i}A_{j+1})\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigg((j-i)-(-1)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(j-i+1).

Thus

which implies

|A_{i}A_{j+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|\geq j-i+1.

On the other hand,
|A_{k}A_{k+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|\leq 1
because of (

) for any

, so that

|A_{i}A_{j+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|\leq j-i+1.

Therefore
|A_{i}A_{j+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|=j-i+1
and we must require
|A_{k}A_{k+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|=1
for any
i\leq k\leq j
for this to hold.

However, if we take the smallest index

such that

, which exists by assumption, then
u_{k-1}\notin S_{1}
and
v_{k}\notin S_{2}
, which implies
|A_{k-1}A_{k}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|=0
, a contradiction.

Case 2:
Let
u_{i}\in S_{1}
and
v_{j}\in S_{2}
with
i>j
. Then similarly we have

|(A_{i}A_{j})_{1}\cap S_{1}|\geq\frac{1}{2}(n+j-i+1)

and

|(A_{i+1}A_{j+1})_{2}\cap S_{2}|\geq\frac{1}{2}(n+j-i+2).

(Recall that by convention we loop back from
(n+1,n)
to
(0,0)
when we consider the subpaths in this case.)

Therefore

|A_{i}A_{j+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|\geq n+j-i+2.

On the other hand, by (
4.7
) again,
|A_{k}A_{k+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|\leq 1
for any
k\geq i
or
k\leq j
, so

|A_{i}A_{j+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|\leq n+j-i+2.

Therefore
|A_{i}A_{j+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|=n+j-i+2
and we must again require
|A_{k}A_{k+1}\cap(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|=1
for any
k\geq i
or
k\leq j
for this to hold. In particular,

which implies
u_{0},u_{1}\in S_{1}
and

by (

). Then taking

and

with

we reduce to Case 1.

Therefore we conclude that for any

which implies

is a compatible pair by Proposition

.
∎

Finally, we recall a well-known fact that

Lemma 4.5
.

The odd-indexed Fibonacci sequence satisfies the recurrence relation

\displaystyle F_{2n+1}=3F_{2n-1}-F_{2n-3},\quad n>1

(4.8)

with initial terms

.

be the set of all compatible pairs on

.

Theorem 4.6
.

We have the following recurrence relation for
n>1
:

|T_{n}|=3|T_{n-1}|-|T_{n-2}|.

(4.9)

In particular the number of compatible pairs in
\mathcal{D}^{(n+1)\times n}
is
F_{2n+3}
.

Proof.

Define the following subsets of
T_{n}
:

O_{n}:=\{(S_{1},S_{2})|u_{n}\notin S_{1},v_{n}\notin S_{2}\},

U_{n}:=\{(S_{1},S_{2})|u_{n}\in S_{1},v_{n}\notin S_{2}\},

V_{n}:=\{(S_{1},S_{2})|u_{n}\notin S_{1},v_{n}\in S_{2}\}.

Note that if
u_{n}\in S_{1},v_{n}\in S_{2}
, then
(S_{1},S_{2})
cannot be a compatible pair. Hence

T_{n}=O_{n}\sqcup U_{n}\sqcup V_{n}.

From Lemma
4.4
, we see that
|O_{n}|=|U_{n}|=|T_{n-1}|
and
|V_{n}|=|O_{n-1}|+|V_{n-1}|
. Applying the two relations above, we get:

\displaystyle|T_{n}|

\displaystyle=|O_{n}|+|U_{n}|+|V_{n}|

\displaystyle=|T_{n-1}|+|T_{n-1}|+(|O_{n-1}|+|V_{n-1}|)

\displaystyle=2|T_{n-1}|+(|T_{n-1}|-|U_{n-1}|)

\displaystyle=3|T_{n-1}|-|T_{n-2}|.

Since the initial terms
|T_{0}|=F_{3}=2
and
|T_{1}|=F_{5}=5
, by Lemma
4.5
the theorem is proven.
∎

5
Nondecreasing Dyck paths of length
2n

In this section, we will count the number of the so-called
nondecreasing Dyck paths
of length
2n
. For further details, the reader is referred to
[
3
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
Dyck path of length
2n
is a discrete path consisting of diagonal segments going from
(0,0)
to
(2n,0)
in the first quadrant
\{(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
, such that each step equals to a vector
\vec{u}:=(1,1)
or
\vec{v}:=(1,-1)
.

In particular a Dyck path
w
of length
2n
is equivalent to a sequence of
2n
vectors
\vec{w}_{1},\vec{w}_{2},...,\vec{w}_{2n}
where
\vec{w}_{i}
equals
\vec{u}
or
\vec{v}
.

Remark 5.2
.

The notion of
“Dyck path”
here has a different meaning compared to that in Section
2.4
. However, they are the same if we rotate the path defined here counterclockwise by 45 degrees. Specifically, a Dyck path of length
2n
is equivalent to a normal Dyck path on
\mathcal{D}^{n\times n}
in Section
2.4
.

Definition 5.3
.

Suppose
(x,y)=\vec{w}_{1}+...+\vec{w}_{i}
for some
1\leq i<2n
is a point on the Dyck path
w
. It is a
peak
if
\vec{w}_{i}=\vec{u}
and
\vec{w}_{i+1}=\vec{v}
. Conversely, it is a
valley
if
\vec{w}_{i}=\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}_{i+1}=\vec{u}
.

Definition 5.4
.

We call
y
the
altitude
of a point
(x,y)
. A Dyck path of length
2n
is
nondecreasing
if, viewed horizontally, the sequence of altitudes of the valleys is nondecreasing.

Definition 5.5
.

A
mountain
of a nondecreasing Dyck path
w
of length
2n
is the subpath from a valley to the next valley on the right, or from the last valley to the endpoint
(2n,0)
. The
magnitude
of a mountain is a pair of positive integers
(d,e)
such that
d,e\geq 1
are the numbers of vectors
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
in the mountain’s representation.

Proposition 5.6
.

A nondecreasing Dyck path can be viewed as a consecutive of
k
mountains with mountain magnitudes
(d_{i},e_{i})
where
d_{i}\geq e_{i}
respectively, except the last mountain where we have
d_{k}\leq e_{k}
.

Proof.

The altitudes of the valleys are nondecreasing, implying
d_{i}\geq e_{i}
. The altitude of the last valley is nonnegative so
d_{k}\leq e_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

We have the following recurrence relations:

\displaystyle|A_{n}|

\displaystyle=|S_{n-1}|,

(5.2)

\displaystyle|B_{n}|

\displaystyle=|S_{n-1}|,

(5.3)

\displaystyle|C_{n}|

\displaystyle=|B_{n-1}|+|C_{n-1}|.

(5.4)

Proof.

Consider the map
A_{n}\to S_{n-1}
defined by

\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k},e_{k})\bigg)\mapsto\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k}-1,e_{k}-1)\bigg).

(5.5)

It is clearly injective, with the inverse given by

\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k},e_{k})\bigg)\mapsto\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k}+1,e_{k}+1)\bigg)

which is well-defined because
d_{k}+1\geq 2

Next, the map

defined by

\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1},e_{k-1}),(1,e_{k})\bigg)\mapsto\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1},d_{k-1}+e_{k}-1)\bigg)

(5.6)

is clearly injective with the inverse given by

\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1},e_{k-1})\bigg)\mapsto\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1},d_{k-1}),(1,e_{k-1}-d_{k-1}+1)\bigg)

which is well-defined because
(d_{k-1},e_{k-1})
is the last mountain, so
e_{k-1}\geq d_{k-1}
and

e_{k-1}-d_{k-1}+1\geq 1.

Finally consider the map

defined by

\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1},e_{k-1}),(1,e_{k})\bigg)\mapsto\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1}-1,e_{k-1}),(1,e_{k}-1)\bigg).

(5.7)

Since
d_{k-1}>e_{k-1}\implies d_{k-1}-1\geq e_{k-1}
, it is clearly well-defined and injective. The inverse is given by

\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1},e_{k-1}),(1,e_{k})\bigg)\mapsto\bigg((d_{1},e_{1}),...,(d_{k-1}+1,e_{k-1}),(1,e_{k}+1)\bigg),

which is well-defined because
d_{k-1}\geq e_{k-1}\implies d_{k-1}+1>e_{k-1}
.
∎

Theorem 5.10
.

We have the following recurrence relation for
n>1
:

|S_{n+1}|=3|S_{n}|-|S_{n-1}|.

(5.8)

In particular the number of nondecreasing Dyck paths of length

is

.

Proof.

Applying Lemma

, we have:

\displaystyle|S_{n+1}|

\displaystyle=|A_{n+1}|+|B_{n+1}|+|C_{n+1}|

\displaystyle=|S_{n}|+|S_{n}|+(|B_{n}|+|C_{n}|)

\displaystyle=2|S_{n}|+(|S_{n}|-|A_{n}|)

\displaystyle=3|S_{n}|-|S_{n-1}|.

We also have the initial conditions
|S_{2}|=F_{3}=2
and
|S_{3}|=F_{5}=5
, hence by Lemma
4.5

|S_{n}|=F_{2n-1}
as required.
∎

6.2
Compatible pairs and nondecreasing Dyck paths

Using the notation from Section
4
and Section

, we construct the map

\theta:\{\text{compatible pair on }\mathcal{D}^{(n+1)\times n}\}\to\{\text{nondecreasing Dyck path of length }2n+4\}

in four steps as follows.

Step 1:

A compatible pair on
\mathcal{D}^{(n+1)\times n}
is equivalent to a sequence of
n+1
letters from
\{O,U,V\}
by the following rules:

–

if
u_{0}\in S_{1}
, then the first letter is
U
, otherwise, the first letter is
O
,

while for
1\leq i\leq n
,

–

if
u_{i}\notin S_{1},v_{i}\notin S_{2}
, then the
i^{th}
letter is
O
,

–

if
u_{i}\in S_{1},v_{i}\notin S_{2}
, then the
i^{th}
letter is
U
,

–

if
u_{i}\notin S_{1},v_{i}\in S_{2}
, then the
i^{th}
letter is
V
.

Note that the sequence is well-defined because we cannot have
u_{i}\in S_{1}
and
v_{i}\in S_{2}
simultaneously on a compatible pair. Moreover, if
u_{i}\in S_{1}
then
v_{i+1}\notin S_{2}
, which means that the letter
U
cannot be followed by the letter
V
.

Step 2:

We can always divide a sequence of letters into blocks of the form

[U,...,U,O,V,...,V]

(except for the last block, it can be empty or contain only a sequence of
U
’s) such that

–

each block contains exactly one letter
O
,

–

there is only a consecutive number of
U
’s (maybe zero) before the letter
O
,

–

there is only a consecutive number of
V
’s (maybe zero) after the letter
O
.

Suppose the number of letter
O
is
k
, then there are
k+1
blocks. For
1\leq i\leq k
, let

–

a_{i}\geq 0
be the number of letters
U
in the
i^{th}
block,

–

b_{i}\geq 0
be the number of letters
V
in the
i^{th}
block.

Step 3:

Then we construct
k+1

1\leq i\leq k

i^{th}

(d_{i},e_{i}):=(a_{i}+b_{i}+1,a_{i}+1),

while the last mountain has magnitude

(d_{k+1},e_{k+1}):=\left(n+2-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+b_{i}+1),n+2-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+1)\right).

(6.7)

Step 4:

Connect these
k+1

.

Theorem 6.6
.

The map
\theta
gives a one-to-one correspondence between the compatible pairs on
\mathcal{D}^{(n+1)\times n}
and nondecreasing Dyck paths of length
2n+4
.

Example 6.7

When

is a compatible pair on

We divide it into four blocks

To prove the theorem, we have the following lemmas.

Lemma 6.8
.

The map
\theta
is well-defined.

Proof.

The corresponding Dyck path is nondecreasing because for any
1\leq i\leq k
,

d_{i}=a_{i}+b_{i}+1\geq a_{i}+1=e_{i}.

Furthermore, the corresponding Dyck path has a length of
2n+4
because

\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}d_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}e_{i}=n+2.

∎

The inverse map
\theta^{-1}
is determined by reversing the steps of the map
\theta
. From a nondecreasing Dyck path with
k+1
mountains
(d_{i},e_{i})
of length
2n+4
, we have

a_{i}=e_{i}-1,\quad\quad b_{i}=d_{i}-e_{i}

for any
1\leq i\leq k
. Next, we construct
k
blocks (
1\leq i\leq k
)

[\;\;\underbrace{U,...,U}_{a_{i}}\;,O,\;\underbrace{V,...,V}_{b_{i}}\;\;]

while the last block just includes
d_{k+1}-1
letters
U
. After that, we get a sequence of
d_{1}+d_{2}+...+d_{k}+(d_{k+1}-1)=n+1
letters. Then there is a compatible pair
(S_{1},S_{2})
that is equivalent to this sequence by the correspondence in Step 1 above.

Lemma 6.9
.

The map
\theta^{-1}
is well-defined.

Proof.

Note that the first letter in the sequence is
O
or
U
. Moreover, by the way of connecting the blocks, there is no letter
U
followed by a letter
V
. So the sequence is indeed induced from a compatible pair.
∎

Proof of Theorem.

By the lemmas, the map
\theta
is injective. Since the cardinalities

\displaystyle F_{2k+3}

\displaystyle=\text{number of compatible pair on }\mathcal{D}^{(n+1)\times n}

\displaystyle=\text{number of nondecreasing Dyck path of length }2n+4

are the same by Theorem
4.6
and Theorem
5.10
, the map
\theta
gives a one-to-one correspondence with inverse
\theta^{-1}
.
∎
