Lipschitz Continuous Algorithms for Graph Problems
Soh Kumabe, Yuichi Yoshida

Abstract
Graph algorithms are widely used for decision making and knowledge discovery. To ensure their effectiveness, it is essential that their output remains stable even when subjected to small perturbations to the input because frequent output changes can result in costly decisions, reduced user trust, potential security concerns, and lack of replicability. In this study, we consider the Lipschitz continuity of algorithms as a stability measure and initiate a systematic study of the Lipschitz continuity of algorithms for (weighted) graph problems. Depending on how we embed the output solution to a metric space, we can think of several Lipschitzness notions. We mainly consider the one that is invariant under scaling of weights, and we provide Lipschitz continuous algorithms and lower bounds for the minimum spanning tree problem, the shortest path problem, and the maximum weight matching problem. In particular, our shortest path algorithm is obtained by first designing an algorithm for unweighted graphs that are robust against edge contractions and then applying it to the unweighted graph constructed from the original weighted graph. Then, we consider another Lipschitzness notion induced by a natural mapping from the output solution to its characteristic vector. It turns out that no Lipschitz continuous algorithm exists for this Lipschitz notion, and we instead design algorithms with bounded pointwise Lipschitz constants for the minimum spanning tree problem and the maximum weight bipartite matching problem. Our algorithm for the latter problem is based on an LP relaxation with entropy regularization.

Introduction

Graph algorithms are widely used for decision making and knowledge discovery.
To ensure their effectiveness, it is essential that their output remains stable even when subjected to small perturbations to the input.
Failure to do so may result in several problems:

•

The output is often used for resource allocation, and it is important to avoid frequent changes as it can result in additional costs.

•

Frequent changes to decisions made by the algorithm can erode user trust.

•

Inconsistency of the output in critical applications such as financial or medical can lead to unsafe decisions being made.

•

The lack of consistency in the output during the process of knowledge discovery can significantly contribute to the difficulty of replicating scientific findings.

Unfortunately, many fundamental graph algorithms are vulnerable to small perturbations to the input.
For example, consider Dijkstra’s algorithm, a classical algorithm for the shortest path problem that can be found in any textbook.
Let
G
be an edge-weighted graph consisting of two vertices
s
and
t
connected by two disjoint paths
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
with almost the same but different lengths.
Given
s
and
t
as source and target vertices, respectively, Dijkstra’s algorithm outputs the shorter path from
s
to
t
, say,
P_{1}
.
However, if the length of an edge in
P_{1}
is slightly increased such that
P_{2}
becomes shorter than
P_{1}
, then Dijkstra’s algorithm will output
P_{2}
.
This indicates the vulnerability of Dijkstra’s algorithm to input perturbations, which is not desirable for some applications because the output path is often used to determine a transportation route and changing it might incur a huge cost.

In this study, we consider mitigating the effects of input perturbations on weighted graph algorithms.
As a stability measure, we consider the
Lipschitz continuity
of graph algorithms, which guarantees that a small change in the input does not significantly change the output.
Then, we design algorithms with small Lipschitz constants for various graph problems.

Before presenting our algorithmic results, we discuss how the Lipschitz continuity of a graph algorithm must be defined.

1.1
Lipschitz Continuity

Metrics.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
over edges, outputs an edge set
\mathcal{A}(G,w)\subseteq E
.
Then, how should we define the Lipschitz constant of
\mathcal{A}
, or more specifically, which metric should we impose on the input and output spaces?

In this study, we always adopt the
\ell_{1}
metric for the input space, that is, the distance between two weight vectors
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
is defined to be
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}
.
We do so because for combinatorial problems, it is natural to assume that the distance between
w
and
w^{\prime}
is calculated as the sum of the distances between
w(e)
and
w^{\prime}(e)
over
e\in E
, and the
\ell_{1}
metric satisfies this property.

We also use the
\ell_{1}
metric for the output space.
Depending on how we map the output edge set to a vector in the
\ell_{1}
space, we can think of the following two variations.

(Unweighted mapping)

We map an edge set
F\subseteq E
to the characteristic vector
\bm{1}_{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
of
F
, where
\bm{1}_{F}(e)=1
if
e\in F
and
0
otherwise.
Then for two edge sets
F,F^{\prime}\subseteq E
, we define

d_{u}(F,F^{\prime}):=\|\bm{1}_{F}-\bm{1}_{F^{\prime}}\|_{1}=|F\triangle F^{\prime}|.

(Weighted mapping)

We map an edge set
F\subseteq E
to a vector
\sum_{e\in F}w(e)\bm{1}_{e}
using the weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, where
\bm{1}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
is the characteristic vector of
e\in E
, that is,
\bm{1}_{e}(f)=1
if
f=e
and
0
otherwise.
Then for two edge sets
F,F^{\prime}\subseteq E
and weight vectors
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, we define

\displaystyle d_{w}((F,w),(F^{\prime},w^{\prime})):=\left\|\sum_{e\in F}w(e)\bm{1}_{e}-\sum_{e\in F^{\prime}}w^{\prime}(e)\bm{1}_{e}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in F\cap F^{\prime}}|w(e)-w^{\prime}(e)|+\sum_{e\in F\setminus F^{\prime}}w(e)+\sum_{e\in F^{\prime}\setminus F}w^{\prime}(e).

We note that
d_{w}((F,w),(F^{\prime},w))=\sum_{e\in F\triangle F^{\prime}}w(e)
holds.

To understand the difference between the unweighted and weighted mappings, consider the shortest path problem, where the graph and weight vector model the road network and the time required to pass through roads, respectively, and the output path represents the roads used in the trip.
The distance
d_{u}
considers how many roads are changed between two trips, whereas the distance
d_{w}
considers the time spent on different roads between the two trips.

The weighted mapping is more natural than the unweighted one for Lipschitzness.
To see this, let us consider a shortest path algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.
It is natural to ask
\mathcal{A}
to output the same path regardless of whether the distance is measured in kilometers or miles.
This implies that
\mathcal{A}
is
scale invariant
, that is, it outputs the same path when edge weights are multiplied by a constant.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
be a weight vector measured in kilometers, and consider another weight vector
w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
obtained from
w
by setting
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)+\delta
, where
e\in E
and
\delta>0
is measured in kilometers.
Then, if we measure the distance between outputs using the weighted mapping, the relative change of the output is
d_{w}((\mathcal{A}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime}))/\delta
.
Let
c\approx 1.609
be the ratio of a mile to a kilometer.
Then, if we calculate edge weights in miles, the relative change of the output is

\displaystyle\frac{d_{w}((\mathcal{A}(G,w/c),w/c),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}/c),w^{\prime}/c))}{\delta/c}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{w}((\mathcal{A}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime}))/c}{\delta/c}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{w}((\mathcal{A}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime}))}{\delta},

and hence the two relative changes coincide.
We do not have this property if we use the unweighted mapping.
Hence, we first focus on the weighted mapping and then discuss the unweighted one later.

First Attempt: Lipschitz continuity of deterministic algorithms.

Using the metric based on the weighted mapping imposed on the input and output spaces as mentioned previously, we can define the Lipschitz constant of a deterministic algorithm as follows:

Definition 1.1

(Lipschitz constant of a deterministic algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a deterministic algorithm that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, outputs an edge set
\mathcal{A}(G,w)\subseteq E
.
Then, the
Lipschitz constant
of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E},\\
w\neq w^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{w}((\mathcal{A}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime}))}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}.

Note that we only take the supremum over weight vectors and not over underlying graphs.
To explain why we adopt this definition, let us consider the shortest path problem again.
The weight vector can frequently change owing to traffic jams or inclement weather, whereas the underlying graph may change because of construction or disasters, which occur less frequently.
Hence, it would be more useful to consider the former type of changes than the latter.

Another reason for not taking the supremum over pairs of graphs is that the change in the underlying graph often forces any (reasonable) algorithm to change its output drastically, and hence it is impossible to bound the Lipschitz constant if we allow changes in the underlying graph.
For example, consider an instance of the shortest path problem such that there are two disjoint paths—one short and the other long—between source and target vertices.
Any algorithm with a reasonable approximation guarantee must output the shorter path.
However, if an edge in the shorter path is removed, the algorithm must change its output to the longer path.

Unfortunately, even though we do not take the supremum over underlying graphs in Definition
1.1
, any (reasonable) deterministic algorithm for the shortest path problem is not Lipschitz continuous:

Theorem 1.2
.

Any deterministic algorithm for the shortest path problem with a finite approximation ratio is not Lipschitz continuous, that is, its Lipschitz constant is unbounded.

To see the reason, consider a graph having two disjoint paths between the source and target vertices and the transition from a weight vector for which the first path is shorter to one for which the second path is shorter.
Because the algorithm is deterministic, there is some point in the transition where the output path discontinuously changes from the first path to the second one, which implies that the algorithm is not Lipschitz.

Second Attempt: Lipschitz continuity of randomized algorithms.

To remedy the aforementioned issue, we consider the Lipschitz continuity of randomized algorithms.
First, we extend
d_{w}
, which is a metric over outputs, to a metric over output distributions.
For two probability distributions
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
over subsets of
E
, the
earth mover’s distance
between
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is defined as

\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left((\mathcal{F},w),(\mathcal{F}^{\prime},w^{\prime})\right):=\min_{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(F,F^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}d_{w}\left((F,w),(F^{\prime},w^{\prime})\right),

where the minimum is taken over
couplings
of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}
, that is, distributions over pairs of sets such that its marginal distributions on the first and second coordinates are equal to
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, respectively.
We note that
\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}
coincides with
d_{w}
if the distributions
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are supported by single edge sets.

For a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, a graph
G=(V,E)
, and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, let
\mathcal{A}(G,w)
denote the (random) output of
\mathcal{A}
on
G
and
w
.
Abusing the notation, we often identify it with its distribution.
Then, we define the Lipschitz constant of a randomized algorithm as follows:

Definition 1.3

(Lipschitz constant of a randomized algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a randomized algorithm that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, outputs a (random) edge set
\mathcal{A}(G,w)\subseteq E
.
Then, the
Lipschitz constant
of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E},\\
w\neq w^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left((\mathcal{A}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime})\right)}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
Lipschitz continuous
if its Lipschitz constant is bounded for any graph
G=(V,E)
and is
L
-Lipschitz
if its Lipschitz constant is at most
L
.

If the algorithm is deterministic, then this definition coincides with Definition
1.1
.

Consider again the graph having two disjoint paths between the source and target vertices and the transition from a weight vector for which the first path is shorter to one for which the second path is shorter.
Then, the output of a randomized algorithm can also make a transition from a distribution with most of its mass on the first path to one with most of its mass on the second path, and hence we can alleviate the issue of deterministic algorithms.
However, designing Lipschitz continuous algorithms is a nontrivial task because we need to bound the ratio in Definition
1.3
for any pair of weight vectors, which can be very close.

As we will discuss in Section
1.2
, we can design randomized Lipschitz continuous algorithms for various graph problems.
However, it is not still apparent whether bounding Lipschitz constant of a randomized algorithm is practically relevant because, even if the output distributions for two weighted graphs
(G,w)
and
(G,w^{\prime})
are close, the paths we obtain by running the algorithm on
(G,w)
and
(G,w^{\prime})
independently may be completely different.

Third Attempt: Lipschitz constant of randomized algorithms with shared randomness.

To overcome the issue mentioned above, we consider randomized algorithms such that the outputs for two similar weighted graphs are close in expectation over the internal randomness.
More specifically, for a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, let
A_{\pi}
denote the deterministic algorithm obtained from
\mathcal{A}
by fixing its internal randomness to
\pi
.
Then, we define the Lipschitz constant with shared randomness of
\mathcal{A}
as follows:

Definition 1.4

(Lipschitz constant of a randomized algorithm with shared randomness)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a randomized algorithm that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, outputs a (random) edge set
\mathcal{A}(G,w)\subseteq E
.
Then, the
Lipschitz constant under shared randomness
of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E},\\
w\neq w^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\left[\frac{d_{w}\left((\mathcal{A}_{\pi}(G,w),w),(\mathcal{A}_{\pi}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime})\right)}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right],

where
\pi
represents the internal randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
Lipschitz continuous under shared randomness
if its Lipschitz constant under shared randomness is bounded for any graph
G=(V,E)
and is
L
-Lipschitz under shared randomness
if its Lipschitz constant with shared randomness is at most
L
.

Note that if a randomized algorithm is
L
-Lipschitz under shared randomness, then it is
L
-Lipschitz, and hence the former is a stronger property.

Suppose that we have a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for the shortest path problem with a small Lipschitz constant under shared randomness and we want to compute short paths on a graph with varying weights in a stable fashion.
By fixing the randomness
\pi
first and then using
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
throughout, we can guarantee that the output paths for two similar weights are similar in expectation over
\pi
.

Then, the distance

1.3
Pointwise Lipschitz Continuity for Unweighted Mapping

In this section, we discuss Lipschitz continuity in the case where the distances between outputs are measured using the unweighted mapping.
First, we define the earth mover’s distance between output distributions
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
with respect to the unweighted mapping as follows:

\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\right):=\min_{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(F,F^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}d_{u}(F,F^{\prime}),

where the minimum is taken over couplings of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

We cannot hope that a scale-invariant algorithm has a bounded Lipschitz constant with respect to the unweighted mapping.
To see this, let
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
be arbitrary weight vectors.
Then for any constant
c>0
, we have

\frac{d_{u}(\mathcal{A}(G,w/c),\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}/c))}{\|w/c-w^{\prime}/c\|_{1}}=\frac{c\cdot d_{u}(\mathcal{A}(G,w),\mathcal{A}(G,w))}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}},

which implies that the Lipschitz constant is unbounded.
Hence, we consider the following variant that look at the relative change in a local neighborhood:

Definition 1.5

(Pointwise Lipschitz constant of a randomized algorithm with shared randomness with respect to the unweighted mapping)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a randomized algorithm that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, outputs a (random) edge set
\mathcal{A}(G,w)\subseteq E
.
Then, the
pointwise Lipschitz constant under shared randomness
of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
at a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
with respect to the unweighted mapping is

\displaystyle\mathop{\lim\sup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E},w^{\prime}\to w\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\left[\frac{d_{u}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\pi}(G,w),\mathcal{A}_{\pi}(G,w^{\prime})\right)}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right],

where
\pi
represents the internal randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.

In contrast to Lipschitz constant, the pointwise Lipschitz constant can depend on the weight vector
w
, and hence a non-scale-invariant algorithm can have a bounded pointwise Lipschitz constant.

1.4
Application: Online Algorithm with Small Recourse

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a weighted graph problem for which, given a weighted graph
(G=(V,E),w)
, the goal is to output a (feasible) edge set
F\subseteq E
that minimizes or maximizes the objective function
f_{\mathcal{P}}:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Let us consider the online version of
\mathcal{P}
in which a graph
G=(V,E)
is given at the beginning, and then weight vectors
w_{1},\ldots,w_{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
are given online.
Then, our goal is to design a (possibly randomized) online algorithm that outputs solutions
F_{1},\ldots,F_{T}
, where
F_{t}
solely depends on
G
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{t}
, such that
F_{t}
is a good solution to the weighted graph
(G,w_{t})
and the
recourse

\sum_{t=2,\ldots,T}d_{w}((F_{t},w_{t}),(F_{t-1},w_{t-1}))/\|w_{t}-w_{t-1}\|_{1}
is small.

Online algorithms with small recourse have been studied for various problems such as the set cover problem
[
12
]
, the facility location problem
[
3
]
,
the edge coloring problem
[
2
]
, and the
k
-clustering problem
[
17
]
, though they studied the setting that, at each time step, a small part of the input is given instead of a weight vector.

We can use a Lipschitz continuous algorithm
\mathcal{A}
under shared randomness to design an online algorithm with a small recourse:
Fix the randomness
\pi
first, and then run the deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
on the weighted graph
(G,w_{t})
to obtain a solution
F_{t}
for every
t=1,\ldots,T
.
Then, from the Lipschitzness guarantee of
\mathcal{A}
, we can bound the expected recourse as in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose that there exists a randomized
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for a weighted graph problem
\mathcal{P}
that is
L
-Lipschitz under shared randomness.
Then, there exists an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the online version of
\mathcal{P}
whose expected recourse is at most
LT
.

Combining Theorem
1.6
with our Lipschitz continuous algorithms, we immediately obtain online algorithms with small recourse for the minimum spanning tree problem, the shortest path problem, and the maximum weight matching problem.

1.5
Related Work

Worst-case and average sensitivity.

Lipschitz continuity is closely related to the
sensitivity
of algorithms introduced in
[
20
,
24
]
.
The
worst-case and average sensitivities
of a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on an (unweighted) graph
G=(V,E)
are defined as

\displaystyle\max_{e\in E}\mathrm{EM}_{u}(\mathcal{A}(G),\mathcal{A}(G\setminus e))\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{e\in E}\mathrm{EM}_{u}(\mathcal{A}(G),\mathcal{A}(G\setminus e)),

(1)

respectively, where
G\setminus e
is the graph obtained from
G
by deleting the edge
e\in E
.
Clearly, the average sensitivity is bounded from above by the worst-case sensitivity.
The sensitivity of algorithms has been investigated for various graph problems including the minimum cut problem
[
24
]
, the maximum matching problem
[
24
,
30
]
, and spectral clustering
[
21
]
.
It is known that there is no algorithm with
o(n)
worst-case/average sensitivity for the shortest path problem
[
24
]
.
As the definition of sensitivity (
1
) can be easily generalized, other non-graph problems such as dynamic programming problems
[
15
,
16
]
and Euclidean
k
-means
[
28
]
have also been studied from the viewpoint of sensitivity.

To see the connection to Lipschitz continuity, suppose that in the supremum of Definition
1.3
, we fix
w
to be the all-one vector
\bm{1}_{E}
and we restrict the domain of
w^{\prime}
to
\{0,1\}
-valued vectors.
Then by the triangle inequality, the (modified) Lipschitz constant on a graph
G=(V,E)
can be bounded from above as

\displaystyle\max_{w^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{E}}\frac{\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}((\mathcal{A}(G,\bm{1}_{E}),\bm{1}_{E}),(\mathcal{A}(G,w^{\prime}),w^{\prime}))}{\|\bm{1}_{E}-w^{\prime}\|}=\max_{F\subseteq E}\frac{\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}((\mathcal{A}(G,\bm{1}_{E}),\bm{1}_{E}),(\mathcal{A}(G,\bm{1}_{E\setminus F}),\bm{1}_{E\setminus F}))}{|F|}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{e\in E}\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}((\mathcal{A}(G,\bm{1}_{E}),\bm{1}_{E}),(\mathcal{A}(G,\bm{1}_{E\setminus\{e\}}),\bm{1}_{E\setminus\{e\}})).

(2)

For the shortest path problem, the weighted graph
(G,w)
for a
\{0,1\}
-valued weight vector
w
is equivalent to the graph obtained from
G
by contracting edges
e\in E
with
w(e)=0
.
In particular, the weighted graph
(G,\bm{1}_{E\setminus\{e\}})
is equivalent to
G/e
, which is the graph obtained from
G
by contracting the edge
e
.
Hence, (
2
) can be seen as a variant of the worst-case sensitivity, where the operation applied to the graph is edge contraction instead of edge deletion, that is,

\displaystyle\max_{e\in E}\mathrm{EM}_{u}(\mathcal{A}(G),\mathcal{A}(G/e)).

(3)

Indeed, our Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the shortest path problem is based on an algorithm with a bounded sensitivity with respect to edge contraction.

A notable difference between the sensitivity and the Lipschitz constant is that the former is trivially bounded by the maximum solution size whereas it is not clear a priori whether there is an algorithm for which the latter is bounded.

Replicability

Note that, when an algorithm has a small Lipschitz constant under shared randomness, it has the ability to replicate the output with respect to weight perturbations. Another form of replicability has been introduced for the stochastic setting by Impagliazzo et al.
[
14
]
. Consider two sets of
n
i.i.d. elements,
X=(x_{1},...,x_{n})
and
Y=(y_{1},...,y_{n})
. Then, a randomized algorithm
A
is called
\rho
-replicable
for
\rho>0
if
\Pr_{X,Y,\pi}[A_{\pi}(X)=A_{\pi}(Y)]\geq 1-\rho
, where
\pi
denotes the internal randomness of
A
. Impagliazzo et al.
[
14
]
showed a replicable algorithm for learning halfspaces, and since then, replicable algorithms have been developed for bandit problems
[
7
]
and clustering problems
[
8
]
.

Adversarial attacks and Lipschitz continuity of neural networks.

In the field of machine learning, it is known that the predictions of the trained model can be significantly changed by slight perturbations of the input, and such perturbations are called
adversarial attacks

[
10
,
22
]
.
Because adversarial attacks threaten the security of machine learning-based systems, there is active research on training models that are robust against them
[
4
,
18
]
.
See surveys
[
1
,
27
,
31
]
for more details.

To mitigate the effects of adversarial attacks, neural networks with small Lipschitz constants have been proposed and their properties have been investigated
[
5
,
9
,
23
,
25
,
26
]
.
It is also reported that bounding Lipschitz constants of neural networks stabilizes the training process and often produces models with better output quality
[
11
,
19
,
29
]
.

We note that bounding the Lipschitz constant of a neural network is often easy because it is bounded by the product of the Lipschitz constants of the activation functions and linear transformations used in the neural network, which are easy to calculate.
However, to design Lipschitz continuous algorithms for graph problems, we need to bound approximation ratio and Lipschitz constant simultaneously, and we often need nontrivial techniques as we will see in this paper.

1.6
Technical Overview

We first discuss designing Lipschitz continuous algorithms (without shared randomness) and then briefly discuss the necessary modifications that ensure Lipschitz continuity under shared randomness.

Minimum spanning tree.

It is known that Kruskal’s algorithm has (worst-case) sensitivity
O(1)
against edge deletions
[
24
]
.
However, it is not Lipschitz continuous because it is deterministic (see Theorem
1.2
), and hence some modification is required.

Our Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the minimum spanning tree problem works as follows:
Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, we sample
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)
uniformly from
[\bm{w}(e),(1+\epsilon)\bm{w}(e)]
for each edge
e\in E
, and then apply Kruskal’s algorithm to the new weight vector
\widehat{\bm{w}}
.
This algorithm clearly achieves
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation.

We can show that, to bound the Lipschitz constant, it suffices to consider a pair of weight vectors
(w,w^{\prime})
such that
w^{\prime}
is obtained from
w
by setting
w^{\prime}(e)=w(e)+\delta
for some
e\in E
and
\delta>0
.
Then, the total variation distance between
\widehat{\bm{w}}
and
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
is
O(\epsilon^{-1}\delta/w(e))
, where
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
is constructed from
w^{\prime}
in the same way as
\widehat{\bm{w}}
is constructed from
w
.
Then, we can define a coupling, i.e., a joint distribution, between
\widehat{\bm{w}}
and
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
such that
\widehat{\bm{w}}\neq\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
with probability
O(\epsilon^{-1}\delta/w(e))
. Also, we can show that when
\bm{w}\neq\bm{w}^{\prime}
occurs in the coupling, the distance between the output spanning trees is
O(w(e))
.
This implies that the earth mover’s distance is
O(\epsilon^{-1}\delta)
and hence the Lipschitz constant is
O(\epsilon^{-1})
.

Our algorithm and analysis for pointwise Lipschitzness with respect to the unweighted mapping is similar though we need some care because we use the optimal value to determine the range from which we sample
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)
and it varies depending on the weight vector.

Shortest path.

To obtain Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the shortest path problem, we first design an algorithm for the (unweighted) shortest path problem with a low sensitivity with respect to edge contraction (see (
3
)).
We will use this algorithm as a subroutine in our Lipschitz continuous algorithm.

The subroutine takes an unweighted graph
\widehat{G}
and two vertices
s,t\in V(G)
as the input and returns an approximate
s
-
t
shortest path in
\widehat{G}
.
This subroutine is recursive: It samples a vertex
\bm{v}
called a
pivot
, recursively computes
s
-
\bm{v}
and
\bm{v}
-
t
walks that are nearly optimal, and then returns the walk obtained by concatenating the two walks.
We choose the pivot
\bm{v}
so that it is roughly in the middle of a nearly optimal
s
-
t
path.
By doing so, we can bound the depth of the recursion by
O(\log n)
and guarantee that the output walk is nearly optimal.

Now, we turn to analyzing the sensitivity of the subroutine with respect to edge contraction.
Let
e\in E(\widehat{G})
and we want to bound the earth mover’s distance between the output distributions for
\widehat{G}
and
\widehat{G}/e
.
Informally, we say that a recursion call for computing a
u
-
v
shortest path is
active
if there is a nearly optimal
u
-
v
path passing through
e
and is
inactive
otherwise.
Then, we can show the following:

•

recursion calls invoked in an inactive recursion call are also inactive, and

•

with high probability, at most one of the two recursion calls invoked in an active recursion call is active.

These properties imply that the expected number of active recursion calls in each recursion depth is
O(1)
.
Also, we can prove that in an inactive call, the pivot is sampled from exactly the same distributions for
\widehat{G}
and
\widehat{G}/e
, and thus it does not contribute to the sensitivity at all.
Additionally, we can show that each active call contributes to the sensitivity by
O(\log^{2}n)
.
Regarding that the number of active calls is
O(\log n)
, the sensitivity of the algorithm can be bounded by
O(\log^{3}n)
.

To obtain a Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the shortest path problem, we construct an unweighted graph
\widehat{\bm{G}}(w)
from the input weighted graph
(G,w)
, and apply the subroutine to it to obtain a walk in
\widehat{\bm{G}}(w)
, and then output the corresponding walk in
G
.
Here, the graph
\widehat{\bm{G}}(w)
is obtained by replacing each edge of the input graph
G
with a path of suitable length so that each path in
G
naturally corresponds to a path in
\widehat{\bm{G}}(w)
.
The length of each path is sampled from a certain distribution so that the total variation distance between
\widehat{\bm{G}}(w)
and
\widehat{\bm{G}}(w^{\prime})
is proportional to
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}
.
Then, we can bound the Lipschitz continuity of the algorithm by using the sensitivity of the subroutine.

Maximum weight matching.

Our algorithm is based on the algorithm for the maximum weight matching proposed by Yoshida and Zhou
[
30
]
.
For a parameter
\alpha>2
, their algorithm first classifies edges
e
according to the value
\left\lfloor{\log_{\alpha}w(e)}\right\rfloor
.
Then, it runs a randomized greedy to compute a matching for each edge class, and then returns a matching obtained by combining them.

Although their algorithm has a bounded
weighted sensitivity
, a discrete analogue of Lipschitz constant, its Lipschitz constant is not bounded.
This is because an arbitrarily small change in the weight of an edge may cause the edge to be classified into a different class.
To resolve this issue, we sample a parameter
\bm{b}\in[1,\alpha]
and classify edges
e
according to the value
\left\lfloor{\log_{\alpha}\frac{w(e)}{\bm{b}}}\right\rfloor
.
Then, the total variation distance between the classifications obtained from weight vectors
w
and
w^{\prime}
is proportional to
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}
, and we can bound the Lipschitz constant.

Maximum weight bipartite matching.

In contrast to the previous Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the maximum weight matching problem, our pointwise Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the maximum weight bipartite matching problem is based on linear programming (LP).
The standard LP relaxation for the maximum weight bipartite matching problem is not stable against perturbations to the edge weight.
Hence, we consider LP with
entropy regularization

[
6
]
.
Although entropy regularization was originally introduced to speed up the computation of the earth mover’s distance, we use it here to stabilize the computation of LP.
For a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, let
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(w)
denote the LP for the weighted graph
(G,w)
with entropy regularization.
Then, we can show that (i) the solution to
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}
is nearly optimal to the original LP, and (ii) for any weight vector
w^{\prime}
, the
\ell_{1}
distance between the solutions of
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(w)
and
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(w^{\prime})
is proportional to
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}
.
Then, we carefully round the obtained fractional solution to an integral one in such a way that for any two fractional solutions
x
and
x^{\prime}
, the earth mover’s distance between the (random) integer solutions obtained from
x
and
x^{\prime}
is proportional to
\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}
with respect to the unweighted mapping.

Lipschitz continuity under shared randomness

In our algorithm, all randomness is caused by sampling processes from continuous or discrete distributions. For example, in our Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the minimum spanning tree, randomness is caused by sampling each edge’s modified weight
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)
from a uniform distribution over
[w(e),(1+\epsilon)w(e)]
. Let us consider fixing this randomness using shared randomness
\pi
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a function that maps weight vectors
w\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{\geq 0}
to probability distributions, and suppose that in our algorithm, we sample values from the distribution
\mathcal{F}(w)
at some point. We define a sampling process
Sample
(
\cdot,\pi
) to be
stable
for
\mathcal{F}
if it satisfies the following two conditions:

•

For any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{\geq 0}
, when
\pi
is sampled from a uniform distribution, the output distribution of
Sample
(
\mathcal{F}(w),\pi
) is equal to
\mathcal{F}(w)
.

•

For any
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{\geq 0}
, when
\pi
is sampled from a uniform distribution, the probability that
Sample
(
\mathcal{F}(w),\pi
) and
Sample
(
\mathcal{F}(w^{\prime}),\pi
) differ is proportional to the total variation distance between
\mathcal{F}(w)
and
\mathcal{F}(w^{\prime})
.

By replacing all sampling processes in the algorithm with stable processes, we can make our algorithm Lipschitz continuous under shared randomness.

There are four types of sampling processes in the algorithm. Three of them sample from a continuous uniform distribution
\mathcal{F}(w)=\mathcal{U}[l(w),r(w)]
, where
\mathcal{U}[a,b]
denotes the uniform distribution over
[a,b]
, with
l(w)
and
r(w)
being constant functions, with
l(w)
and
r(w)
having a constant ratio, and with
l(w)
and
r(w)
having a constant difference. The last one samples from a discrete distribution with fixed support.

For each of these processes, we construct a stable algorithm. Furthermore, by discussing how to modify each part of the five algorithms mentioned above, we obtain Lipschitz continuous algorithms under shared randomness with the same approximation ratio and Lipschitz constant, as well as polynomial-time complexity.

Lipschitz-continuous algorithm for the minimum spanning tree problem

Lemma 2.2

The approximation ratio of

Proof.

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}=\sum_{e\in\bm{T}}\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in T^{*}}\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in T^{*}}(1+\epsilon)w(e)=(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{opt},

\bm{T}

For a weight vector

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left((\textsc{LipMST}(G,w,\epsilon),w),(\textsc{LipMST}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},\epsilon),w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f})\right).

(4)

Lemma 2.4

We have

\Pr_{(\widehat{\bm{w}},\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f})\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\widehat{\bm{w}}\neq\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}\right]=\Pr_{(\widehat{\bm{w}},\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f})\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)\neq\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)\right]\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon)\delta}{\epsilon(w(f)+\delta)}.

The equality holds by the design of the coupling

Now, we show the inequality.

\displaystyle\Pr_{(\widehat{\bm{w}},\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f})\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)\neq\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)\right]\leq 1-\Pr\left[\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)\in\left[w(f),(1+\epsilon)w(f)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon)(w(f)+\delta)-(1+\epsilon)w(f)}{(1+\epsilon)(w(f)+\delta)-(w(f)+\delta)}=\frac{(1+\epsilon)\delta}{\epsilon(w(f)+\delta)},

as desired.

Lemma 2.5

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\left(\textsc{LipMST}(G,w,\epsilon),w\right),\left(\textsc{LipMST}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},\epsilon),w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(\widehat{\bm{w}},\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f})\sim\mathcal{W}}d_{w}\left((T(\widehat{\bm{w}}),w),(T(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}),w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{(\widehat{\bm{w}},\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f})\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)\neq\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)\right]\cdot((1+\epsilon)w(f)+(1+\epsilon)(w(f)+\delta))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon)\delta}{\epsilon\cdot(w(f)+\delta)}\cdot 2(1+\epsilon)(w(f)+\delta)=\frac{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}\delta}{\epsilon}\leq O\left(\frac{\delta}{\epsilon}\right).

Therefore, the Lipschitz constant is bounded as

\sup_{\delta>0,f\in E}\frac{\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\left(\textsc{LipMST}(G,w,\epsilon),w\right),\left(\textsc{LipMST}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},\epsilon),w+\mathbf{1}_{f}\right)\right)}{\delta}\leq O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right).\qed

Theorem
2.1
follows by combining Lemmas
2.2
and
2.5
.

Contraction Sensitivity of Shortest Path

In this section, we present an algorithm for the shortest path problem that is stable against edge contractions.
We will use this algorithm as a subroutine in our Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the shortest path problem in Section
4
.
In this section, we only consider unweighted graphs.

To define the sensitivity to edge contractions, we first formally define edge contraction.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
e=\{u,v\}\in E
be an edge.
Then, the
contraction
of an edge
e
in
G
is an operation that yields a graph
G/e=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
with

\displaystyle V^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(V\setminus e)\cup\{v_{e}\},

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle=E\setminus(\{\{u,x\}:x\in N(u)\}\cup\{\{v,x\}:x\in N(v)\})\cup\{\{v_{e},x\}:x\in N(u)\cup N(v)\setminus\{u,v\}\},

where
v_{e}
is a newly introduced vertex and
N(a)
denotes the set of neighbors of a vertex
a\in V
.
Then, we define the sensitivity to edge contraction for the shortest path problem as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a (randomized) algorithm for the shortest path problem that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and two vertices
s,t\in V
, outputs an
s
-
t
walk.
Then, the
contraction sensitivity
of
\mathcal{A}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is

\max_{e\in E,e\cap\{s,t\}=\emptyset}\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}(\mathcal{A}(G),\mathcal{A}(G/e)).

Recall that when calculating the distance
\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}
, a walk
P=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell})
is mapped to a vector
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bm{1}_{e_{i}}
, and hence an edge that appears multiple times in
P
contributes by the same number of times in the vector.
We only consider contracting edges not incident to
s
and
t
, because we do not need to contract such edges in our Lipschitz continuous algorithm in Section
4
, and this simplifies the analysis.

The goal of this section is to show the following:

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the shortest path problem with contraction sensitivity
O\left(\epsilon^{-1}\log^{3}|V|\right)
.

Varma and Yoshida
[
24
]
showed that any algorithm with a finite approximation ratio must have
\Omega(n)
sensitivity with respect to edge deletion.
This is because in the edge-deletion case, an edge in the output walk may disappear and we may need to completely change the output walk.
However, in the edge-contraction case, we can use (essentially) the same path even if an edge in the path is contracted.

In Section
3.1
, we present our algorithm and an overview of the analysis.
We analyze the approximation ratio in Section
3.2
and the contraction sensitivity in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.

Proof Overview

In this section, we present our algorithm for the unweighted shortest path problem (pseudocode is presented in Algorithm

To construct a walk between two vertices

\bm{v}
-

Before discussing the approximation and contraction sensitivity of the algorithm, we introduce useful notation.
Let

For two vertices

For a vertex

\mathrm{opt}_{G}(s,t,e)

s

-

-

If the underlying graph

Approximation Ratio

Let us discuss the approximation ratio of the algorithm above.
First, the choice of the range from which we sample
\bm{l}
in Line

ensures that restricting the feasible domain to the family of
s
-
t
walks passing through the pivot
\bm{v}
increases the optimal value only by a
(1+O(\gamma))
factor.
Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{opt}(s,t,\bm{v})=\mathrm{opt}(s,\bm{v})+\mathrm{opt}(\bm{v},t)\leq(1+2l)\mathrm{opt}(s,t)\leq(1+4\gamma)\mathrm{opt}(s,t).

According to the choice of the range from which we sample
\bm{d}
in Line

and
(\bm{v},t)

Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\max\{\mathrm{opt}(s,\bm{v}),\mathrm{opt}(\bm{v},t)\}\leq\frac{3}{4}\mathrm{opt}(s,t).

This implies that the recursion depth is
O(\log|V|)

It follows that the approximation ratio is bounded by

\displaystyle(1+O(\gamma))^{O(\log|V|)}\leq 1+O(\gamma\log|V|)=1+O(\epsilon).

Contraction Sensitivity

Now, we consider the contraction sensitivity of the algorithm.
First, we focus on a particular call of
Rec
and show that the pivot distributions for
Rec
(
G,s,t,\gamma
) and
Rec
(

O\left(\frac{\log|V|}{\gamma\cdot\mathrm{opt}(s,t)}\right)

To bound the earth mover’s distance in the definition of contraction sensitivity, we consider transporting the probability mass of
Rec
(
G,s,t,\gamma
) corresponding to a particular choice of the pivot
\bm{v}
to that of
Rec
(

) corresponding to the same pivot as far as possible.
We transport the remaining probability mass arbitrarily, which increases the contraction sensitivity of the algorithm by

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\log|V|}{\gamma\cdot\mathrm{opt}(s,t)}\right)\cdot(1+O(\gamma\log|V|))\mathrm{opt}(s,t)\leq O\left(\frac{\log|V|}{\gamma}\right),

where
(1+O(\gamma\log|V|))\mathrm{opt}(s,t)
is the upper bound on the length of the output walk.

Next, we consider the contraction sensitivity of the whole algorithm.
A recursion call

(

(

•

the distribution of the pivots chosen in

(

(

•

the recursion calls
Rec
(

Rec
(
G,\bar{v},t,\gamma

Thus, inactive recursion calls are irrelevant to the contraction sensitivity.

Assume that

(

\bm{v}

(
G,s,t,\gamma
) and
Rec
(

Then, we can show that, with probability

Suppose that

4
Lipschitz Continuity of Shortest Path

In this section, we provide our algorithm for the shortest path problem with a bounded Lipschitz constant and prove the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a polynomial-time
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the shortest path problem with Lipschitz constant
O\left(\epsilon^{-1}\log^{3}n\right)
, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input graph.

Our idea is to convert the input weighted graph
(G,w)
into an unweighted graph
\widehat{\bm{G}}
by replacing each edge with a path whose length is determined by its weight, apply Algorithm
2
on
\widehat{\bm{G}}
, and then convert the obtained walk on
\widehat{\bm{G}}
back to a walk on
G
.

However, it is unclear how to convert a walk in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
back to a walk in
G
in such a way that a small contraction sensitivity of the former implies a small Lipschitz constant of the latter because the former may walk around in a path in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
corresponding to an edge in
G
.
To resolve this issue, we replace each edge of
G
with two
directed
paths oriented in opposite directions.
Then, we can naturally map a walk in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
to a walk in
G
because a walk entering one end of a path must leave from the other end.
For this idea to work, we extend Algorithm
2
for directed graphs.
We discuss this directed version in detail in Section
4.1
.

We describe our algorithm in Section
4.2
, and analyze its approximation and Lipschitz continuity in Sections
4.3
and
4.4
, respectively.

4.1
Directed Shortest Path

In the
(unweighted) directed shortest path problem
, we are given an unweighted directed graph

, and a target vertex

We extend the algorithm in Section

We must be careful when discussing contraction sensitivity for the directed shortest path problem because the contraction of a directed edge may affect the reachability relation between vertices and significantly change the optimal values.

LipSP
is
1+\epsilon
.

Proof.

Let
P
be a path that attains
\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)
.
Note that
P
does not include any edge
e
with
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)>12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+3
, because we have
w(e)\geq(\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)-3)\bm{b}>12\epsilon^{-1}|V|\bm{b}\geq\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)
.
Therefore, we can take a path
P^{\prime}
in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
corresponding to
P
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\textsc{LipSP}(G,w,s,t,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\mathrm{opt}_{\widehat{\bm{G}}}(s,t)\bm{b}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\left|E(P^{\prime})\right|\bm{b}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\sum_{e\in E(P)}\left(\left\lfloor{\frac{w(e)}{\bm{b}}}\right\rfloor+3\right)\bm{b}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\left(\sum_{e\in E(P)}w(e)+3\bm{b}\left|E(P)\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\left(\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)+3\bm{b}\left|E(P)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\left(\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)+\frac{\epsilon|E(P)|\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{2|V|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t),

where the first inequality is from Lemma
3.5
and the fact that
w(e)\leq\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)\bm{b}
holds for all
e\in E
, the second inequality is from the fact that each edge
e
in
G
corresponds to a path in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
with length at most
\left\lfloor{\frac{w(e)}{\bm{b}}}\right\rfloor+3
, and the last inequality is from
\epsilon\leq 1
.
∎

4.4
Lipschitz Continuity

Next, we analyze the Lipschitz continuity of Algorithm
3
.
Let
\delta>0
and
f\in E
.
By Lemma
1.7
, it suffices to bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w,s,t,\epsilon),\textsc{LipSP}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon)\right).

for sufficiently small
\delta>0
.
Specifically, we assume
\delta\leq\frac{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{12|V|}
.
To distinguish the variables used in
LipSP
(
G,w,s,t,\epsilon
) from those used in
LipSP
(
G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon
), we add superscripts
\delta f
to the latter, e.g.,
\bm{b}^{\delta f}
.

We consider the coupling
\mathcal{D}
between
(\bm{b},\bm{x})
and
(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f})
defined as follows: For each parameter
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we transport the probability mass for
\bm{b}=b
to that for
\bm{b}^{\delta f}=b
as far as possible in such a way that for each choice of
x\in[0,1)
, the probability mass for
\bm{x}=x
is transported to that for

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\delta f}(e)=\begin{cases}x(e)&\text{if }e\in E\setminus\{f\},\\
x(e)-\frac{\delta}{b}&\text{if }e=f,x(f)>\frac{\delta}{b},\\
x(e)-\frac{\delta}{b}+1&\text{if }e=f,x(f)\leq\frac{\delta}{b}.\\
\end{cases}

(7)

The remaining mass is transported arbitrarily.
Clearly, (
7
) is a one-to-one correspondence from
[0,1)
to itself for all
e\in E
.
Therefore, any pair
\left((b,x),(b^{\delta f},x^{\delta f})\right)
in the support of the remaining mass satisfies
b\neq b^{\delta f}
.

We begin by presenting the following useful property of the coupling.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\left((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f})\right)
be a pair sampled from
\mathcal{D}
conditioned on
\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{\delta f}
.
Then we have
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(e)-1
if
e=f
and
\bm{x}(e)\leq\frac{\delta}{\bm{b}}
hold, and we have
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(e)
otherwise.

We defer the proof to Section
4.4.1
and continue the analysis of the Lipschitz continuity of
LipSP
.

When
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(e)
holds,
G
and
G^{\delta f}
are exactly the same and so are
LipSP
(
G,w,s,t,\epsilon
) and
LipSP
(
G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon
).
The case of
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(e)-1
can be divided into the following three cases:

(i)

\bm{b}\neq\bm{b}^{\delta f}
;

(ii)

\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{\delta f}
, and the two paths corresponding to
f
exist in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
but do not exist in
\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}
; and

(iii)

\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{\delta f}
, and the two paths corresponding to
f
exist in both
\widehat{\bm{G}}
and
\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}
, but the length of the paths in
\widehat{\bm{G}}
is smaller than that in
\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}
by
1
.

Let
p_{\neq}
,
p_{\mathrm{path}}
, and
p_{\mathrm{len}}
be the probabilities that Cases (i), (ii), and (iii) occur, respectively, in the coupling
\mathcal{D}
.
We will bound these probabilities.
First, we consider Case (i).

Lemma 4.6
.

We have

\displaystyle p_{\neq}\leq\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle p_{\neq}=\mathrm{TV}\left(\bm{b},\bm{b}^{\delta f}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bm{b}^{\delta f}\in\left[\frac{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{6|V|},\frac{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f}}(s,t)}{6|V|}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{6|V|}\cdot\frac{12|V|}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f}}(s,t)}=\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f}}(s,t)}\leq\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w(s,t)}},

where the first inequality is from
\delta>0
and hence the interval of
\bm{b}
is to the left of that of
\bm{b}^{\delta f}
, and the second inequality is from
\mathrm{opt}_{G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f}}(s,t)\leq\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)+\delta
.
∎

Next we consider Case (ii).

Lemma 4.7
.

We have

p_{\mathrm{path}}\leq\frac{6\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}.

Proof.

Assume
b=b^{\delta f}
.
Case (ii) happens only when
\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\left\lfloor{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right\rfloor+3
and
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)=\left\lfloor{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right\rfloor+4
.
This implies
\bm{l}(f)
is either
\left\lfloor{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right\rfloor
or
\left\lfloor{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right\rfloor+1
, and it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{w(f)}{\bm{b}}-1

\displaystyle\leq\left\lfloor{\frac{w(f)}{\bm{b}}}\right\rfloor=\bm{l}(f)\leq\left\lfloor{\frac{12|V|}{\epsilon}}\right\rfloor+1\leq\frac{12|V|}{\epsilon}+1,

\displaystyle\frac{w(f)}{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{w(f)}{\bm{b}}}\right\rfloor=\bm{l}(f)\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{12|V|}{\epsilon}}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{12|V|}{\epsilon}-1.

Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle\bm{b}\in\left[\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2},\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1}\right].

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{path}}

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{\delta f}\in\left[\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2},\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1}\right]\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{\delta}{\bm{b}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bm{b}\in\left[\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2},\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1}\right]\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{\delta}{\bm{b}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}\cdot\Pr\left[\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{\delta}{\bm{b}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}\cdot\frac{\delta\cdot 12|V|}{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\cdot\frac{\delta\cdot 12|V|}{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\leq\frac{6\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)},

where the second inequality is from

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\bm{b}\in\left[\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2},\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\cdot\max\left(0,\min\left(\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1},\frac{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{6\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right)-\max\left(\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2},\frac{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\cdot\max\left(0,\min\left(\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1},\frac{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{6\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right)-\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\cdot\max\left(0,\min\left(\frac{w(f)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1},\frac{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{6\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\right)\cdot\frac{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\cdot\max\left(0,\frac{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{6\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\cdot\frac{\left(12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2\right)-\left(12\epsilon^{-1}|V|-1\right)}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{6\epsilon^{-1}|V|}\cdot\frac{3}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}=\frac{6}{12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+2}.\qed

Finally, we consider Case (iii).

Lemma 4.8
.

For
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, let
\mathrm{E}_{b}
be the event where Case (iii) occurs with
\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{\delta f}=b
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w,s,t,\epsilon)\right),\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon)\right)\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right)\cdot b+\delta.

Proof.

Let
\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}
be the two paths in
\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}
corresponding to
f
.
Because
\widehat{w}(f)\geq 2
(and thus
\widehat{w}^{\delta f}(f)\geq 3
), we can take edges
\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2}
on
\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}
, respectively, such that both of the endpoints have indegree and outdegree
1
, i.e., we can assume that
\bm{e}_{1}
and
\bm{e}_{2}
are contractible.
Therefore by Lemma
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}/e_{1},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{3}|\widehat{\bm{V}}|}{\epsilon}\right).

Clearly,
\bm{e}_{2}
is still contractible in
\widehat{\bm{G}}/\bm{e}_{1}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}((\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}/e_{1})/e_{2},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}/e_{1},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}/e_{1},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}((\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}/e_{1})/e_{2},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{3}|\widehat{\bm{V}}|}{\epsilon}\right).

Let
b
and
x,x^{\delta f}\in[0,1)^{E}
be such that
((b,x),(b,x^{\delta f}))
is in the support of
\mathcal{D}
and Case (iii) occurs when
\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{\delta f}=b,\bm{x}=x,\bm{x}^{\delta f}=x^{\delta f}
.
Let
\widehat{G}
(resp.,
\widehat{G}^{\delta f}
) be the graph constructed from
(G,w)
(resp.,
(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})
) using
(b,x)
(resp.,
(b,x^{\delta f})
).
Let
(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{P}})
be a pair sampled from the coupling
\mathcal{D}_{\widehat{G}^{\delta f},\widehat{G}}
that attains
\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{G}^{\delta f},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{G},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)
.
Let
\bm{P}
and
\bm{P}^{\delta f}
be the walks in
G
corresponding to
\widehat{\bm{P}}
and
\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f}
, respectively.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P})}w(e)\bm{1}_{e}-\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P}^{\delta f})}(w(e)+\delta\bm{1}_{e=f})\bm{1}_{e}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P})}w(e)\bm{1}_{e}-\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P}^{\delta f})}w(e)\bm{1}_{e}\right\|_{1}+\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P})\triangle E(\bm{P}^{\delta f})}w(e)+\delta\leq\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P})\triangle E(\bm{P}^{\delta f})}(l(e)+1)b+\delta

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P})\triangle E(\bm{P}^{\delta f})}\widehat{w}(e)b+\delta=\left|E(\widehat{\bm{P}})\triangle E(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f})\right|\cdot b+\delta.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w,s,t,\epsilon)\right),\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon)\right)\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D},\\
(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{P}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{G}}}\end{subarray}}\left[\left\|\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P})}w(e)\bm{1}_{e}-\sum_{e\in E(\bm{P}^{\delta f})}(w(e)+\delta\bm{1}_{e=f})\bm{1}_{e}\right\|_{1}\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D},\\
(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{P}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{G}}}\end{subarray}}\left[\left|E(\widehat{\bm{P}})\triangle E(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f})\right|\cdot b+\delta\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D},\\
(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{P}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},\widehat{\bm{G}}}\end{subarray}}\left[\left|E(\widehat{\bm{P}})\triangle E(\widehat{\bm{P}}^{\delta f})\right|\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]\right)\cdot b+\delta

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f},s,t,\epsilon/4),\textsc{DiSP}(\widehat{\bm{G}}^{\delta f}/e_{1},s,t,\epsilon/4)\right)\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]\cdot b+\delta

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{b},\bm{x}),(\bm{b}^{\delta f},\bm{x}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[O\left(\frac{\log^{3}|\widehat{\bm{V}}|}{\epsilon}\right)\mid\mathrm{E}_{b}\right]\cdot b+\delta

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right)\cdot b+\delta,

where the last inequality is from
|\widehat{\bm{V}}|\leq|E|\cdot\left(12\epsilon^{-1}|V|+1\right)+|V|\leq O(\epsilon^{-1}|V||E|)
.
∎

Now, we show that Algorithm
3
is Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 4.9
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w,s,t,\epsilon)\right),\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon)\right)\right)\leq O\left(\frac{\delta\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right).

Proof.

We bound the Lipschitz constant by summing up the contributions of Events (i), (ii), and (iii).
We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w,s,t,\epsilon)\right),\left(\textsc{LipSP}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},s,t,\epsilon)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq p_{\neq}\cdot(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)+p_{\mathrm{path}}\cdot(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)+p_{\mathrm{len}}\cdot\left(O\left(\frac{\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right)\cdot\bm{b}+\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\right)\cdot(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)+\left(\frac{6\delta}{\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}\right)\cdot(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(O\left(\frac{\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right)\cdot\bm{b}+\delta\right)\cdot\frac{\delta}{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\delta\right)+O(\delta)+O\left(\frac{\delta\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\delta\log^{3}(|V||E|)}{\epsilon}\right),

where the last inequality is from
\delta\leq\frac{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G,w}(s,t)}{12|V|}\leq\bm{b}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Combining Lemmas
4.4
and
4.9
yields the claim.
∎

4.4.1
Proof of Lemma
4.5

To prove Lemma
4.5
, we first present several properties of the coupling
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

In the coupling
\mathcal{D}
, we have
\widehat{\bm{w}}(e)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(e)
for any
e\in E\setminus\{f\}
.

Proof.

The claim holds because
\bm{x}(e)=\bm{x}^{\delta f}(e)
and
\frac{(\bm{l}(e)+1)b-w(e)}{b}=\frac{(\bm{l}^{\delta f}(e)+1)b-w(e)}{b}
holds if
e\neq f
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

In the coupling
\mathcal{D}
, we have
\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)
if
\bm{x}(f)>\frac{\delta}{b}
.

Proof.

Note that
\bm{l}^{\delta f}(e)
is equal to either
\bm{l}(e)
or
\bm{l}(e)+1
because
\delta\leq\frac{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}_{G}(s,t)}{12|V|}
.

Suppose
\bm{l}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+2

\displaystyle\iff\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(f)}{b}

\displaystyle\iff\bm{x}(f)-\frac{\delta}{b}\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(f)}{b}-\frac{\delta}{b}

\displaystyle\iff\bm{x}^{\delta f}(f)\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-(w(f)+\delta)}{b}

\displaystyle\iff\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+2.

Suppose
\bm{l}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+1
.
Then from the definitions of
\bm{l}(f)
and
\bm{l}^{\delta f}(f)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{w(f)}{b}<\bm{l}(f)+1\leq\frac{w(f)+\delta}{b}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\bm{x}(f)>\frac{\delta}{b}=\frac{w(f)+\delta}{b}-\frac{w(f)}{b}\geq\bm{l}(f)+1-\frac{w(f)}{b}=\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(f)}{b},

which implies
\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+3
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{x}(f)-\frac{\delta}{b}\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{b}\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(f)}{b}+1-\frac{\delta}{b}=\frac{(\bm{l}^{\delta f}(f)+1)b-(w(f)+\delta)}{b},

where the last inequality is from
(\bm{l}(f)+1)b\geq w(f)
.
Therefore
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+3=\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)
, and the claim holds.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

In the coupling
\mathcal{D}
, we have
\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)-1
if
\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{\delta}{b}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\bm{l}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{\delta}{b}=\frac{w(e)+\delta}{b}-\frac{w(e)}{b}<\bm{l}(f)+1-\frac{w(e)}{b}=\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(e)}{b}.

Thus,
\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+2
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{x}(f)-\frac{\delta}{b}+1>1-\frac{\delta}{b}\geq\frac{\bm{l}(f)b-w(f)}{b}+1-\frac{\delta}{b}=\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-(w(f)+\delta)}{b},

where the last inequality is from
\bm{l}(f)b\leq w(f)
. Therefore
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+3=\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)+1
.

Suppose
\bm{l}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{w}}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+2

\displaystyle\iff\bm{x}(f)\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(f)}{b}

\displaystyle\iff\bm{x}(f)-\frac{\delta}{b}+1\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+1)b-w(f)}{b}-\frac{\delta}{b}+1

\displaystyle\iff\bm{x}^{\delta f}(f)\leq\frac{(\bm{l}(f)+2)b-(w(f)+\delta)}{b}

\displaystyle\iff\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\delta f}(f)=\bm{l}(f)+3.

Therefore, the lemma is proved.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Combining the three lemmas above yields the lemma.
∎

5
Maximum Weight Matching

In this section, we present a Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the maximum weight matching problem and prove the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

For any

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{w}}\left((\textsc{LipMWM}(G,w,\alpha),w),(\textsc{LipMWM}(G,w-\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},\alpha),w-\delta\mathbf{1}_{f})\right)

Lemma 7.3

7.2
Maximum Weight Bipartite Matching

In this section, we present a pointwise Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the maximum weight bipartite matching problem and prove the following:

Theorem 7.7
.

There exists a polynomial-time
(1/2-\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the maximum weight bipartite matching problem with a pointwise Lipschitz constant with respect to the unweighted mapping
O(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot n^{3/2}\log m/\mathrm{opt})
, where
n
and
m
are the numbers of vertices in the left and right parts of the input bipartite graph, respectively, and
\mathrm{opt}
is the maximum weight of a matching.

Let
G=(U\cup V,E)
be a complete bipartite graph, that is,
E=U\times V
, and
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
be a weight vector.
We assume that vertices in
U
and
V
are indexed as
\{1,\dots,|U|\}
and
\{1,\dots,|V|\}
, respectively.
Let us consider the following LP relaxation for the maximum weight bipartite matching problem.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{LP}(G,w):=\max&\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}w(e)x_{e}\\
\text{s.t.}&\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}x_{i,j}\leq 1&(i=1,\dots,|U|)\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}x_{i,j}\leq 1&(j=1,\dots,|V|)\\
x_{e}\geq 0&(e\in E).\end{cases}\end{array}

The downside of this LP is that its optimal solution is not stable for small changes in the input weight vector
w
.
To avoid this issue, we use
entropy regularization

[
6
]
.
Specifically, we consider the following optimization problem.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w,B):=\max&\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}w(e)x_{e}-B\sum_{e\in E}x_{e}\log x_{e}\\
\text{s.t.}&\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}x_{i,j}\leq 1&(i=1,\dots,|U|)\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}x_{i,j}\leq 1&(j=1,\dots,|V|)\\
x_{e}\geq 0&(e\in E),\end{cases}\end{array}

where
B>0
is a parameter.
The second term (including the negative sign) of the objective function is non-negative;
\log x_{e}
is always non-positive for
0\leq x_{e}\leq 1
.

Our algorithm solves
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w,\bm{B})
for a suitably chosen
\bm{B}
and then rounds the obtained fractional solution
\bm{x}
.
The rounding process is as follows: First, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}
we sample an index
\bm{p}(i)\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
using
\{\bm{x}_{i,j}\}_{j\in V}
. Intuitively,
\bm{p}(i)
represents the candidate of the vertex in
|V|
that will be matched to
i
.
Because there may be multiple indices
i
with the same
\bm{p}(i)
value, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
, we choose an index
\bm{q}(j)\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}
with
\bm{p}(i)=j
and include
(\bm{q}(j),j)
in the output matching.
See Algorithm
6
for details.

Procedure

PLipMWBM
(
G=(U\cup V,E),w,\epsilon
)

1

Sample
\bm{B}
uniformly from
\left[\frac{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}{|U|\log|V|},\frac{2\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}{|U|\log|V|}\right]
;

2

Let
\bm{x}
be the optimal solution to
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w,\bm{B})
;

3

Let
\bm{C}_{j}\leftarrow\emptyset
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
;

4

for

i\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}

do

5

Set

\bm{p}(i)=\begin{cases}j&\text{with probability }\bm{x}_{i,j}\text{ for }j\in\{1,\ldots,|V|\},\\
\bot&\text{with the remaining probability}.\end{cases}

if

\bm{p}(i)\neq\bot

then

6

Add
i
to
\bm{C}_{\bm{p}(i)}
.
\bm{M}\leftarrow\emptyset
;

7

for

j\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}

do

8

if

\bm{C}_{j}\neq\emptyset

then

9

Sample
\bm{q}(j)
uniformly from
\bm{C}_{j}
;

10

Add the edge
\{u_{\bm{q}(j)},v_{j}\}
to
\bm{M}
.

.

7.2.1
Approximation Ratio

The goal of this section is to show the following:

Lemma 7.8
.

The approximation ratio of
PLipMWBM
is (at least)
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
.

Throughout this section, we fix a complete bipartite graph
G=(U\cup V,E=U\times V)
and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
.
First, we analyze the loss caused by entropy regularization.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be the vector as constructed in
\textsc{PLipMWBM}(G,w,\epsilon)
.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}w(e)\bm{x}_{e}\geq(1-2\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}(G,w).

Proof.

Let
x^{*}
be the optimal solution to
\mathrm{LP}(G,w)
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{opt}(G,w)=\sum_{e\in E}w(e)x^{*}_{e}\leq\sum_{e\in E}w(e)x^{*}_{e}-\bm{B}\sum_{e\in E}x^{*}_{e}\log x^{*}_{e}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E}w(e)\bm{x}_{e}-\bm{B}\sum_{e\in E}\bm{x}_{e}\log\bm{x}_{e}\leq\sum_{e\in E}w(e)\bm{x}_{e}-\bm{B}|U|\log|V|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E}w(e)\bm{x}_{e}-2\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}(G,w)\leq(1-2\epsilon)\mathrm{opt}(G,w),

where the first inequality is from the non-negativeness of the regularization term, the second inequality is from the optimality of
x^{*}
, the third inequality is from

\displaystyle-\sum_{e\in E}\bm{x}_{e}\log\bm{x_{e}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\log|V|\leq|U|\log|V|,

and the last inequality is from
\bm{B}\leq\frac{2\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}{|U|\log|V|}
.
∎

Next, we analyze the loss caused by rounding.
Let
y\in[0,1]^{n}
be a vector, and consider tossing
n
independent coins with the
t
-th coin turning up with probability
y_{i}
.
Then, the
Poisson binomial distribution

[
13
]
with respect to
y
, denoted
P(y)
, is the probability distribution of the number of coins turning up.
The following shows that this distribution plays an important role in the analysis of the performance of the rounding.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
i^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}
. Then,
|\bm{C}_{\bm{p}(i^{\prime})}|-1
follows the Poisson binomial distribution with respect to the vector
(x_{1,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})},\dots,x_{i^{\prime}-1,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})},x_{i^{\prime}+1,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})},\dots,x_{|U|,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})})
.

Proof.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
, we have
\Pr\left[\bm{p}(i)=j\right]=\bm{x}_{i,j}
.
Additionally,
\bm{p}(i)\;(i\in\{1,\dots,|U|\})
are pairwise independent.
Therefore,
|\bm{C}_{p(i^{\prime})}|-1
can be regarded as the number of coins turning up when we toss
|U|
independent coins with turning-up probabilities of
x_{1,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})},\ldots,x_{i^{\prime}-1,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})},x_{i^{\prime}+1,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})},\dots,x_{|U|,\bm{p}(i^{\prime})}
.
∎

Let

\displaystyle Y=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},\sum_{t=1}^{n}y_{t}\leq 1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\Pr_{\bm{k}\sim P(y)}\left[\bm{k}=k\right]\right).

Then, the output matching size can be bounded by using
Y
as can be seen in the following:

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be the vector and
\bm{M}
be the matching as constructed in
\textsc{PLipMWBM}(G,w,\epsilon)
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{e\in\bm{M}}w(e)\right]\geq Y\sum_{e\in E}w(e)\bm{x}_{e}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{e\in\bm{M}}w(e)\right]=\sum_{e\in E}\left(w(e)\cdot\Pr[e\in\bm{M}]\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left(w(i,j)\cdot\Pr\left[\bm{p}(i)=j\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\bm{q}(j)=i\mid\bm{p}(i)=j\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\left(\Pr\left[\bm{q}(\bm{p}(i))=i\right]\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left(w(i,j)\cdot\Pr\left[\bm{p}(i)=j\right]\right)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{\left|\bm{C}_{\bm{p}(i)}\right|}\right]\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}w(i,j)\bm{x}_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot\Pr\left[P((\bm{x}_{1,\bm{p}(i)},\dots,\bm{x}_{i-1,\bm{p}(i)},\bm{x}_{i+1,\bm{p}(i)}\dots,\bm{x}_{|U|,\bm{p}(i)}))=k\right]\right)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}w(i,j)\bm{x}_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq Y\sum_{e\in E}w(e)\bm{x}_{e}.\qed

Now we evaluate
Y
.

Lemma 7.12
.

Y=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

For
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\leq 1
, let

\displaystyle Y(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\Pr_{\bm{k}\sim P(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})}\left[\bm{k}=k\right]\right).

Suppose
n\geq 2
.
We show that deleting two elements of
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
, say
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, and appending their sum does not increase the value.
To show this, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we let
Q(k)=\Pr_{\bm{k}\sim P(y_{3},\ldots,y_{n})}\left[\bm{k}=k\right]
.
We have

\displaystyle Y(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot(1-y_{1})(1-y_{2})+\frac{1}{k+2}\cdot((1-y_{1})y_{2}+y_{1}(1-y_{2}))+\frac{1}{k+3}\cdot y_{1}y_{2}\right)Q_{k}\right),

\displaystyle Y(y_{1}+y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot(1-(y_{1}+y_{2}))+\frac{1}{k+2}\cdot(y_{1}+y_{2})\right)Q_{k}\right).

Then, we have

\displaystyle Y(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})-Y(y_{1}+y_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot y_{1}y_{2}-\frac{1}{k+2}\cdot 2y_{1}y_{2}+\frac{1}{k+3}\cdot y_{1}y_{2}\right)Q_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=y_{1}y_{2}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{2}{k+2}+\frac{1}{k+3}\right)Q_{k}\right)\geq 0.

Hence, the minimum of
Y(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is attained when
n=1
.
We have

\displaystyle Y(y_{1})=(1-y_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\cdot y_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2},

where the equality holds when
y_{1}=1
, and hence the lemma holds.
∎

Proof of Lemma
7.8
.

Combining Lemmas
7.9
,
7.11
and
7.12
yields the claim.
∎

7.2.2
Stability of Entropy Regularization

In this section, we discuss the stability of the solution of the LP with entropy regularization.

For a graph
G=(U\cup V,E)
, a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, and
B>0
, let

f(x)=\sum_{e\in E}w(e)x_{e}-B\sum_{e\in E}x_{e}\log x_{e}

be the objective function of
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}
with a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
.
We first discuss the following property of
f
.

Lemma 7.13
.

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
with
\sum_{j\in V}x_{i,j}\leq 1
and
\sum_{j\in V}y_{i,j}\leq 1
, we have

f(x)-f(y)\geq\nabla f(x)^{\top}(x-y)+\frac{B}{2}\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-y_{i,j}|\right)^{2}

Proof.

Because
f
is concave, for
z=x-y
, it suffices to show

z^{\top}H(x)z\leq-B\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|z_{i,j}|\right)^{2},

where
H(x)
is the Hessian of
f
at
x
.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle z^{\top}H(x)z=-B\sum_{e\in E}\frac{z_{e}^{2}}{x_{e}}=-B\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{j\in V}\frac{z_{i,j}^{2}}{x_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\leq-B\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}\frac{z_{i,j}^{2}}{x_{i,j}}\cdot\sum_{j\in V}x_{i,j}\right)\leq-B\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|z_{i,j}|\right)^{2}.\qed

Lemma 7.14
.

Let
G=(U\cup V,E)
be a graph,
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
be a weight vector,
f\in E
, and
\delta>0
. Let
x,x^{\delta f}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be the optimal solutions to
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w,B)
and
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},B)
, respectively.
Then, we have

\|x-x^{\delta f}\|_{1}=O\left(\frac{\delta\sqrt{|U|}}{B}\right).

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle f^{\delta f}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E\setminus\{f\}}w(e)x_{e}+(w(f)+\delta)x_{f}-B\sum_{e\in E}x_{e}\log x_{e}.

be the objective function of
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}
with the weight vector
w+\delta\bm{1}_{f}
.

By Lemma
7.13
, we have

\displaystyle f(x)-f(x^{\delta f})\geq\nabla f(x)^{\top}(x-x^{\delta f})+\frac{B}{2}\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\right)^{2}\geq\frac{B}{2}\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\right)^{2}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle f^{\delta f}(x^{\delta f})-f(x)\geq\frac{B}{2}\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\right)^{2}.

Also, we have

\displaystyle f(x)-f(x^{\delta f})+f^{\delta f}(x^{\delta f})-f^{\delta f}(x)=\delta(x^{\delta f}_{f}-x_{f}).

Combining the above three, we have

\displaystyle B\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\right)^{2}\leq\delta(x^{\delta f}_{f}-x_{f}).

(8)

In particular, this implies

B(x^{\delta f}_{f}-x_{f})^{2}\leq\delta(x^{\delta f}_{f}-x_{f}),

and hence we have
|x^{\delta f}_{f}-x_{f}|\leq\delta/B
.
By substituting this into (
8
), we obtain

\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{B^{2}}.

Then, we have

\|x-x^{\delta f}\|_{1}=\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\in U}\left(\sum_{j\in V}|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}|\right)^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i\in U}1^{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{|U|}\delta}{B}.\qed

7.2.3
Pointwise Lipschitzness

The goal of this section is to show the following:

Lemma 7.15
.

For any bipartite graph
G=(U\cup V,E)
, a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
,
\delta>0
, and
f\in E
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}(\textsc{PLipMWBM}(G,w,\epsilon),\textsc{PLipMWBM}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},\epsilon))\leq O\left(\frac{\delta\epsilon^{-1}|U|^{\frac{3}{2}}\log|V|}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}\right).

To bound the earth mover’s distance between the output matchings for
(G,w)
and
(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})
, we consider the coupling
\mathcal{W}
between
(\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q})
and
(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f})
, defined as follows: For each number
B\in\mathbb{R}
, we transport the probability mass for
\bm{B}=B
to that for
\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B
as far as possible in such a way that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B\right],

is minimized, where
\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}
is the number of indices
j
with
\bm{q}(j)\neq\bm{q}^{\delta f}(j)
.
The remaining mass is transported arbitrarily.
Note that
\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}
is equal to
d_{u}(\bm{M},\bm{M}^{\delta f})
, where
\bm{M}
and
\bm{M}^{\delta f}
are the matchings constructed using
\bm{q}
and
\bm{q}^{\delta f}
, respectively.

First, we evaluate the remaining mass.

Lemma 7.16
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\left(\bm{B},\bm{B}^{\delta f}\right)\leq\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\left(\bm{B},\bm{B}^{\delta f}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\bm{B}^{\delta f}\in\left[\frac{2\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}{|U|\log|V|},\frac{2\mathrm{opt}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f})}{|U|\log|V|}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=2\cdot\frac{\mathrm{opt}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f})-\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f})}\leq\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}.\qed

Now we evaluate the distance between
\bm{p}
and
\bm{p}^{\delta f}
.

Lemma 7.17
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and let
x,x^{\delta f}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
be the optimal solutions to
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w)
and
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w+\mathbf{1}_{\delta})
, respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B\right]\leq\|x-x^{\delta f}\|_{1}.

Proof.

First, we have

\displaystyle\left|\Pr\left[\bm{p}(i)=j\right]-\Pr[\bm{p}^{\delta f}(i)=j]\right|=\left|x_{i,j}-x^{\delta f}_{i,j}\right|

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
.
Because the
\bm{p}(i)
values are independent of each other when
\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B
, taking the product of the transportation for all
i\in\{1,\dots,|U|\}
yields the lemma.
∎

Now we evaluate the distance between
\bm{q}
and
\bm{q}^{\delta f}
.

Lemma 7.18
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and let
x
and
x^{\delta f}
be the optimal solutions to
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w)
and
\mathrm{LP}_{\mathrm{ent}}(G,w+\mathbf{1}_{\delta})
, respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B\right]\leq 2\cdot\|x-x^{\delta f}\|_{1}.

Proof.

Fix
B,p,p^{\delta f}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and let
(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\delta f})
be sampled from the marginal distribution of
\mathcal{W}
conditioned on
\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B
,
\bm{p}=p
, and
\bm{p}^{\delta f}=p^{\delta f}
. Note that under this condition,
C
and
C^{\delta f}
are no longer random variables because they are deterministically determined by
p
and
p^{\delta f}
, respectively.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B,\bm{p}=p,\bm{p}=p^{\delta f}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left|\Pr\left[\bm{q}(j)=i\right]-\Pr[\bm{q}^{\delta f}(j)=i]\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left|\frac{1}{|C_{j}|}\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}-\frac{1}{|C^{\delta f}_{j}|}\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left|\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}-\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}\right|+\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left(|C_{j}|\cdot\left|\frac{1}{|C_{j}|}-\frac{1}{|C^{\delta f}_{j}|}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\|p-p^{\delta f}\|_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left(|C_{j}|\cdot\left|\frac{1}{|C_{j}|}-\frac{1}{|C^{\delta f}_{j}|}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|p-p^{\delta f}\|_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left|\frac{|C^{\delta f}_{j}|-|C_{j}|}{|C^{\delta f}_{j}|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|p-p^{\delta f}\|_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left||C^{\delta f}_{j}|-|C_{j}|\right|

\displaystyle=\|p-p^{\delta f}\|_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\sum_{i=1}^{|U|}\left|\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}-\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}\right|

\displaystyle=2\cdot\|p-p^{\delta f}\|_{0},

(9)

where the first inequality is from the fact that
\bm{q}(j)
(and
\bm{q}^{\delta f}(j)
) are pairwise independent for
j\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
and the second inequality is from

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|C_{j}|}\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}-\frac{1}{|C^{\delta f}_{j}|}\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}\right|\leq\begin{cases}1&(\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}\neq\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}),\\
\left|\frac{1}{|C_{j}|}-\frac{1}{|C^{\delta f}(j)|}\right|&(\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}=\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}=1),\\
0&(\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}=\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}=0).\end{cases}

and the fact that there are at most
|C_{j}|
indices
i
with
\bm{1}_{p(i)=j}=\bm{1}_{p^{\delta f}(i)=j}=1
.
By integrating (
9
) over
p
and
p^{\delta f}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[2\cdot\|\bm{p}-\bm{p}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}=B\right]\leq 2\cdot\|x-x^{\delta f}\|_{1},

where the second inequality is from Lemma
7.17
.
∎

Now we can bound the pointwise Lipschitz constant as follows.

Proof of Lemma
7.15
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{u}}(\textsc{PLipMWBM}(G,w,\epsilon),\textsc{PLipMWBM}(G,w+\delta\mathbf{1}_{f},\epsilon))

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}\left(\bm{B},\bm{B}^{\delta f}\right)\cdot|U|+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[\|\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{\delta f}\|_{0}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}\left(\bm{B},\bm{B}^{\delta f}\right)\cdot|U|+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{((\bm{B},\bm{p},\bm{q}),(\bm{B}^{\delta f},\bm{p}^{\delta f},\bm{q}^{\delta f}))\sim\mathcal{W}}\left[2\cdot\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\delta f}\|_{1}\mid\bm{B}=\bm{B}^{\delta f}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\delta}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}\cdot|U|+O\left(\delta\sqrt{|U|}\cdot\frac{|U|\log|V|}{\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}\right)=O\left(\frac{\delta\epsilon^{-1}|U|^{\frac{3}{2}}\log|V|}{\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}\right),

where the second inequality is from Lemma
7.18
, and the third inequality is from Lemma
7.14
and
7.16
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.7
.

The approximability result follows by Lemma
7.8
.
Let
w,w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
be two weight vectors with
\mathrm{opt}(G,w)>0
and
\|w-w^{*}\|\leq\frac{\mathrm{opt}(G,w)}{2}
. Then by Lemma
7.15
, we have
