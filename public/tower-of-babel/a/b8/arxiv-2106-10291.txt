EFPE: Efficient fully precessing eccentric gravitational waveforms for binaries with long inspirals
Antoine Klein

Abstract
In this paper, we derive a set of equations of motions for binaries on eccentric orbits undergoing spin-induced precession that can efficiently be integrated on the radiation-reaction timescale. We find a family of solutions with a computation cost improved by a factor 10 10 - 50 50 down to ∼ 10 \sim 10 ms per waveform evaluation compared to waveforms obtained by directly integrating the precession equations, that maintain a mismatch of the order 10 − 4 10^{-4} - 10 − 6 10^{-6} for waveforms lasting a million orbital cycles and a thousand spin-induced precession cycles. We express it in terms of parameters that make the solution regular in the equal-mass limit, thus bypassing a problem of previous similar solutions. We point to ways in which the solution presented in this paper can be perturbed to take into account effects such as general quadrupole momenta and post-Newtonian corrections to the precession equations. This new waveform, with its improved efficiency and its accuracy, makes possible Bayesian parameter estimation using the full spin and eccentricity parameter volume for long lasting inspiralling signals such as stellar-origin black hole binaries observed by LISA.

Gravitational wave (GW) observations have opened a new field of astronomy. The ground-based network

, potentially having implications on its astrophysical source

;

The LISA detector

;

, extreme mass ratio inspirals of stellar-mass compact objects around massive black holes

, galactic white dwarf and neutron star binaries

;
Korol et al. 2017
, and early-inspiral stellar-mass black hole binaries

.

The physics of inspiral binaries is rich, including effects such as spin-induced precession

;
Apostolatos et al. 1994
, tidal interactions
Hinderer et al. 2010
, and orbital eccentricity

;

. The simultaneous measurement of those effects is an important ingredient in discriminating between different black hole binary formation scenarios

;
Zevin et al. 2021
, and massive black hole binary evolution

. Efforts towards building waveforms for preccessing and/or eccentric waveforms have been carried out using various formalisms

;
Lundgren and O’Shaughnessy 2014
;

;
Khan et al. 2019
;

;

;

;

Building waveforms for precessing binaries is complicated by the fact that they experience variations on three different timescales: the longer radiation-reaction timescale, the intermediate precession timescale, and the faster orbital timescale
Apostolatos et al. 1994
. Including eccentricity adds a fourth timescale, the periastron precession timescale, of the same order as the precession timescale
Damour et al. 2004
. It is possible to express the solutions in such a way as to have to integrate the equations of motion only on the precession timescale, inducing a significant speed-up in the computation of those waveforms
Cornish and Key 2010
;
Klein et al. 2018
. However, for systems with long inspirals such as neutron star or low-mass black hole binaries in third-generation ground-based detectors, or stellar-origin black hole binaries with LISA, using such a solution is still computationally prohibitive. In this work, we use a previous expression of the circular equations of motion that can be integrated analytically in the absence of radiation reaction
Kesden et al. 2015
;
Gerosa et al. 2015
, and slightly modify it in order to regularize the equal-mass limit and include orbital eccentricity. We find a complete analytical solution to these equations, that we can perturb in order to take into account radiation-reaction effects. The additional equations can be integrated on the radiation-reaction timescale, leading to a waveform with a computational cost similar to ones describing nonprecessing binaries. Such a waveform was recently used to demonstrate its applicability for Bayesian parameter estimation in the full 17 dimensional parameter space of precessing eccentric binaries
Buscicchio et al. 2021
.

While the precession equations had been solved in simplified situations such as equal-mass binaries or binaries with a single nonvanishing spin
Apostolatos et al. 1994
;
Klein et al. 2013
;
Chatziioannou et al. 2013
;
Lundgren and O’Shaughnessy 2014
, and generic spin configuration waveforms had been mapped to simpler ones
Hannam et al. 2014
, a general solution to these equations had eluded the modelling community for a long time.

Kesden et al. 2015

Kesden et al. 2015
;
Gerosa et al. 2015
were able to express the problem in such a way as to retain only one dynamical parameter by expressing the equations of precession in a non-inertial frame, thus finding a solution in the absence of radiation reaction requiring the integration of a single equation. This was later extended to account for radiation reaction effects, including an analytical solution to this equation
Chatziioannou et al. 2017a
;
Chatziioannou et al. 2017b
. One problem remained in this formulation: the dynamical variable chosen to express the solutions was the norm of the total spin
S
, which becomes constant in the equal-mass limit, which introduces a singularity in the solution. While this problem can safely be ignored in a large portion of the parameter space
Gerosa et al. 2017
, it can almost completely disappear by choosing a different parameter in which to express the solutions, as we show in Sec.
II
.

Throughout this paper, we use geometric units with
G=c=1
, we write vectors in boldface, and we write unit vectors with a hat. For convenience, we write angular momenta as dimensionless quantities with e.g.
\bm{S}=\bm{S}^{\text{phys}}/M^{2}

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II

III

IV

V

VI

VII

II.1
Solution in a rotating frame

We can express the angular momenta in a frame where the
z

\bm{J}

\bm{L}
is perpendicular to the

\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\bm{\hat{x}}\geq 0

In this frame, the angular momenta can all be expressed in terms of one variable. As we mentioned earlier, the choice of

\displaystyle\delta\chi

\displaystyle=\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\left(\bm{s}_{1}-\bm{s}_{2}\right).

(11)

We can express the orbital angular momentum and the total spin in the precessing frame as

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{L}}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta_{L}\bm{\hat{z}}+\sin\theta_{L}\bm{\hat{x}},

(12)

\displaystyle\bm{S}

\displaystyle=S\cos\theta_{S}\bm{\hat{z}}-S\sin\theta_{S}\bm{\hat{x}},

(13)

\displaystyle S^{2}

\displaystyle=J^{2}-L^{2}-L(\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\delta\chi),

(14)

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2J}\left(2L+\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\delta\chi\right),

(15)

\displaystyle\cos(\theta_{L}+\theta_{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2S}\left(\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\delta\chi\right),

(16)

where

are related to

The fact that

II.2
Solutions in an inertial frame

In order to reconstruct the angular momenta in an inertial frame, we need to compute the angular velocity of the rotation of the precessing frame with respect to an inertial one. Since the precessing frame is defined so that
\bm{\hat{L}}
stays in the

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\phi_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta_{L}}\left(\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{L}}\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\hat{J}}\times\bm{\hat{L}}\right).

Approximations of the solution

The full solution presented above can be sped up by making some approximations. Since the solutions for

In the limit

III
Addition of radiation reaction

In order to build a fast and accurate waveform for a precessing binary on an elliptic orbit, one can take advantage of this analytical conservative solution to construct a set of evolution equations that can be integrated on the radiation reaction timescale. The solution presented in the previous section is given in terms of several quantities that become dynamical when the loss of energy of the system due to gravitational wave emission is taken into account. The norms of the orbital angular momentum and of the total angular momentum
L
and
J
, the square eccentricity
e^{2}
, as well as the direction of the total angular momentum
\bm{\hat{J}}
become dynamical. The norms of the individual spins
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
and the effective spin
\chi_{\text{eff}}
however are still conserved.

The evolution of
L
and
e^{2}
can be expressed as post-Newtonian series
Klein et al. 2018
, to which we can add evolution equations for the mean orbital phase
\lambda
and the argument of periastron
\delta\lambda
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}y

\displaystyle=y^{9}\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\left(y,e^{2},\bm{\hat{L}},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\right)y^{n},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}e^{2}

\displaystyle=y^{8}\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}\left(y,e^{2},\bm{\hat{L}},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\right)y^{n},

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\lambda

\displaystyle=y^{3},

(60)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\delta\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{ky^{3}}{1+k},\quad k=y^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}k_{n}\left(y,e^{2},\bm{\hat{L}},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\right)y^{n},

(61)

where
k
is the periastron advance.

Those series are given in terms of
y
and
e^{2}
that vary on the radiation reaction timescale, but include spin couplings that depend on the precession timescale. In order to eliminate this dependency from the equations, a natural choice is to substitute them with their time average, computed on the precession timescale.

III.1
Spin couplings averaging

The 1.5PN spin-orbit couplings can be written in terms of

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i},

(62)

with
i\in\{1,2\}
.
We can compute their averages using our solution and find

\displaystyle\left\langle\beta_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\chi_{\text{eff}}+\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right),

(63)

\displaystyle\left\langle\beta_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\chi_{\text{eff}}-\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right),

(64)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta\chi_{\text{av}}-\frac{2\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}}{m}\left[\frac{E(m)}{K(m)}-1+\frac{m}{2}\right].

(65)

The 2PN spin-spin couplings can be expressed as linear combinations of three different types of couplings:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{s}_{i}^{2},

(66)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)^{2},

(67)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{s}_{i}\right|^{2}\cos 2\psi_{i},

(68)

with
i\in\{0,1,2\}
,
\bm{s}_{0}=\bm{s}_{1}+\bm{s}_{2}
, and
\psi_{i}
denotes the angle subtended by the periastron line and the projection of
\bm{s}_{i}
onto the orbital plane.

\sigma_{1,2}^{(1)}=s_{1,2}^{2}
and
\sigma_{0}^{(2)}=\chi_{\text{eff}}^{2}
are conserved, and we can find the precession averages for the remaining
\sigma_{i}^{(1,2)}
:

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{1}^{(2)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\chi_{\text{eff}}+\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right)^{2}+\frac{\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}^{2}}{8}+\frac{\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}^{2}}{m^{2}}\bigg[\frac{E(m)^{2}}{K(m)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2E(m)}{3K(m)}(m-2)+\frac{m^{2}}{8}-\frac{m}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\bigg],

(69)

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{2}^{(2)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\chi_{\text{eff}}-\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right)^{2}+\frac{\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}^{2}}{8}+\frac{\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}^{2}}{m^{2}}\bigg[\frac{E(m)^{2}}{K(m)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2E(m)}{3K(m)}(m-2)+\frac{m^{2}}{8}-\frac{m}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\bigg],

(70)

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{0}^{(1)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu}\big[J^{2}-L^{2}

\displaystyle-\delta\mu\left(\mu_{1}s_{1}^{2}-\mu_{2}s_{2}^{2}\right)-L(\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle)\big],

(71)

where

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to 0}\frac{1}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{E(m)^{2}}{K(m)^{2}}+\frac{2E(m)}{3K(m)}(m-2)

\displaystyle+\frac{m^{2}}{8}-\frac{m}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\bigg]=0.

(72)

In order to estimate the effect of
\sigma_{i}^{(3)}
, it is useful to express the relevant quantities involved in a frame tied to the orbital plane. We can find such a frame by rotating the precessing frame tied to
\bm{\hat{J}}
by an angle
\theta_{L}
about
\bm{\hat{y}}
. In this frame,
\bm{\hat{\bar{z}}}=\bm{\hat{L}}
,
\bm{\hat{\bar{y}}}\propto\bm{\hat{J}}\times\bm{\hat{L}}
, and
\bm{\hat{\bar{x}}}=\bm{\hat{\bar{y}}}\times\bm{\hat{\bar{z}}}
. We can write

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\bar{s}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}\cos\bar{\phi}_{i},\sin\bar{\theta}_{i}\sin\bar{\phi}_{i},\cos\bar{\theta}_{i}\right),

(73)

\displaystyle\psi_{i}

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}_{i}-\delta\lambda,

(74)

where
\delta\lambda
is the angle subtended by the periastron line and
\bm{\hat{x}}^{\prime}
. Relativistic effects will cause this angle to acquire a derivative given by the periastron precession rate

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\delta\lambda

\displaystyle=3y^{5}+\mathcal{O}\left(y^{6}\right).

(75)

Spin precession effects will cause
\bar{\phi}_{i}
to acquire a derivative. We can compute it using

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bar{\phi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\bar{\theta}_{i}}\left(\mathcal{D}\bm{\bar{s}}_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\hat{\bar{z}}}\times\bm{s}_{i}\right),

(76)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bm{\bar{s}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\bm{s}_{i}-\left(\bm{\hat{\bar{x}}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{\bar{x}}}-\left(\bm{\hat{\bar{y}}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{\bar{y}}}-\left(\bm{\hat{\bar{z}}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{\bar{z}}}.

(77)

We find

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bar{\phi}_{1}

\displaystyle=y^{5}\left(\frac{3\mu_{2}}{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(y^{6}\right),

(78)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bar{\phi}_{2}

\displaystyle=y^{5}\left(\frac{3\mu_{1}}{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(y^{6}\right).

(79)

We find that
\bar{\phi}_{1,2}
and
\delta\lambda
evolve on the same timescale, but their derivatives cannot cancel each other, which eliminates the possibility of a resonance. Therefore, we can make the approximation that
\sigma_{1,2}^{(3)}
average to zero, through the averaging of
\cos 2\psi_{1,2}
.

In order to look at the average of
\sigma_{0}^{(3)}
, we first note that if we define
\bm{\hat{A}}
to be a unit vector in the direction of the periastron line, such as the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector, and
\bm{\hat{B}}=\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{\hat{A}}
, we can write

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\hat{A}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)^{2}-\left(\bm{\hat{B}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)^{2}.

(80)

We can see from that relation that we can rewrite

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}^{(3)}+\sigma_{2}^{(3)}+2\left|\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{s}_{1}\right|\left|\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{s}_{2}\right|\cos(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}).

(81)

We can derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})

\displaystyle=-y^{5}\left(\frac{9}{2}-\frac{\nu}{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(y^{6}\right),

(82)

which justifies neglecting the precession average of
\sigma_{0}^{(3)}
as well.

III.2
Total angular momentum

Through radiation reaction, the total angular momentum will acquire a derivative that we can express as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bm{J}

\displaystyle=-|\mathcal{D}L|\bm{\hat{L}}

(83)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nu\mathcal{D}y}{y^{2}}\bm{\hat{L}}.

(84)

Therefore, if we decompose
\bm{\hat{L}}
into a parallel and a perpendicular parts as

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{L}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\hat{L}}_{\parallel}+\bm{\hat{L}}_{\perp},

(85)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{L}}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta_{L}\bm{\hat{J}},

(86)

we can separate the angular momentum loss contribution to
J
from the one to
\bm{\hat{J}}
. We find

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}J

\displaystyle=-\frac{L\mathcal{D}y}{2Jy}\left(2L+\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\delta\chi\right),

(87)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{J}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{L\mathcal{D}y}{y}\sin\theta_{L}\left(\cos\phi_{z}\bm{\hat{x}}+\sin\phi_{z}\bm{\hat{y}}\right),

(88)

where
\bm{\hat{x}}
and
\bm{\hat{y}}
are part of an inertial triad together with
\bm{\hat{J}}^{(0)}
, a vector aligned with
\bm{\hat{J}}
at some reference time. Since
\phi_{z}
is increasing on the spin precession timescale, much shorter than the radiation reaction timescale, we can neglect the evolution of the total angular momentum direction
Apostolatos et al. 1994
.
We can use the conservative solution for
\delta\chi
in order to find a leading-order multiple-scale analysis solution for
J
. Separating it into a secular and a periodic parts, we find

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=J_{0}+\delta J,

(89)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{D}J_{0}}{\mathcal{D}y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{L}{2J_{0}y}\left(2L+\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(y\right),

(90)

\displaystyle\delta J

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu\delta\mu\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}}{m\sqrt{Y_{3}-Y_{-}}}\left(\frac{64}{15}+\frac{56}{15}e^{2}\right)y^{2}

\displaystyle\times\left\{E[\text{am}(\hat{\psi}_{p},m);m]-\frac{E(m)}{K(m)}\hat{\psi}_{p}\right\}+\mathcal{O}\left(y^{3}\right).

(91)

Since
J_{0}=\mathcal{O}\left(y^{-1}\right)
and
\delta J=\mathcal{O}\left(y^{2}\right)
, we can neglect the contribution of
\delta J
in our solution.

Note that, provided a PN expansion for
\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle
, this leaves us with an equation that we can integrate

\displaystyle J_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu^{2}}{y^{2}}+\frac{\nu}{y}\left[\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle^{(0)}\right]

\displaystyle+C_{0}^{2}-\nu\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle^{(1)}\log y+\mathcal{O}\left(y\right),

(92)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle^{(n)}y^{n},

(93)

with
C_{0}
an arbitrary constant. However, we choose not to use this solution in the following since finding the PN expansion for
\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle
is not trivial in practice, and integrate Eq. (
90
) which varies on the radiation reaction timescale.

III.3
Effects of the evolution of the elliptic parameter

Through radiation reaction, the coefficients of the cubic equation for
\delta\chi
given in Eq. (
22
) evolve. Therefore, the parameter
m
of the Jacobi elliptic function describing the solution does as well. Since this parameter influences the period
2K(m)
of this function, the phase
\psi_{p}
accumulated early on in the evolution will correspond to a different number of cycles later on when the period has changed, causing a dephasing in the solution. We can remedy this by not tracking the phase of the Jacobi elliptic function directly, but a quantity proportional to the corresponding accumulated number of cycles. For example, we can define
\bar{\psi}_{p}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2K(m)}\mathcal{D}\psi_{p},

(94)

and recover the correct phase of the Jacobi elliptic function when we need to evaluate it, with

\displaystyle\psi_{p}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2K[m(t)]}{\pi}\bar{\psi}_{p}(t).

(95)

III.4
Averaged spin parameters for the characterization of precessing binaries

It is useful for the characterization of binary observations through GWs to describe relevant parameters, the observation of which is indicative of the presence of some physical effect influencing the radiation. In the case of the binary components’ spins, one linear combination of the spins’ projection onto the orbital angular momentum enters the frequency evolution at leading order in the eccentricity and at lowest PN order. Thus, it forms a natural parameter to describe the presence of nonzero spins:

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{94}{113}\chi_{\text{eff}}+\frac{19}{113}\delta\mu\delta\chi.

(96)

As a sign of the presence of precession, the parameter
\chi_{p}
was introduced in
Schmidt et al. 2015
. It was recently argued that using a precession averaged version of this parameter could be even more useful
Gerosa et al. 2021
. Following those works, we define

\displaystyle\chi_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{1}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{L}}\right|^{2},

(97)

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}y^{6}}{2}[\mu_{1}+3(1-y\chi_{\text{eff}})].

(98)

Using our solution, we can find an analytic expression for the precession average of those parameters. We find

\displaystyle\left\langle\beta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{94}{113}\chi_{\text{eff}}+\frac{19}{113}\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle,

(99)

where
\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle
is given in Eq. (
65
),
and

\displaystyle\left\langle\left|\mathcal{D}\bm{\hat{L}}\right|^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{y^{10}}{16}\left[c-b\,y\,\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle-y^{2}\delta\mu^{2}\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{y^{12}\delta\mu^{2}\delta\chi_{\text{diff}}^{2}}{4m^{2}}\bigg[\frac{E(m)^{2}}{K(m)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2E(m)}{3K(m)}(m-2)-\frac{m-1}{3}\bigg],

(100)

where the constants
b
and
c
are given in appendix
C
. Note that we were able to find an analytic expression for
\langle|\chi_{p}|^{2}\rangle^{1/2}
, where
\chi_{p}
is defined as the “generalized” parameter introduced in
Gerosa et al. 2021
, which slightly differs from
\langle\chi_{p}\rangle
as was proposed there.

III.5
Summary of the full solution

We are now ready to present a full set of evolution equations that vary exclusively on the radiation reaction timescale, that we can integrate to construct a full gravitational waveform valid over many precessional cycles of evolution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}y

\displaystyle=y^{9}\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\left(y,e^{2}\right)y^{n},

(101)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}e^{2}

\displaystyle=y^{8}\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}\left(y,e^{2}\right)y^{n},

(102)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\lambda

\displaystyle=y^{3},

(103)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\delta\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{ky^{3}}{1+k},\quad k=y^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}k_{n}\left(y,e^{2}\right)y^{n},

(104)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}J_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{L\mathcal{D}y}{2J_{0}y}\left(2L+\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right),

(105)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{3(1-y\chi_{\text{eff}})y^{6}}{4}\frac{\pi}{2K(m)}\sqrt{Y_{3}-Y_{-}},

(106)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\phi_{z,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{Jy^{6}}{2}+\frac{3(1-y\chi_{\text{eff}})y^{6}}{4\nu K(m)}\left(P_{+}+P_{-}\right),

(107)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\zeta_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(2L+\chi_{\text{eff}}+\delta\mu\left\langle\delta\chi\right\rangle\right)y^{6}}{4}

\displaystyle-\frac{3(L+\nu\chi_{\text{eff}})(1-y\chi_{\text{eff}})y^{6}}{2\nu}

\displaystyle+\frac{3(1-y\chi_{\text{eff}})y^{6}}{4\nu K(m)}\left(P_{+}-P_{-}\right),

(108)

\displaystyle P_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\pm}}{B_{\pm}-C_{\pm}}\Pi\left(\frac{-2C_{\pm}}{B_{\pm}-C_{\pm}},m\right),

(109)

where we have made use of the averaged spin couplings derived earlier. In all the expressions for the coefficients of the conservative solution, we further substitute
J\to J_{0}
. The absence of the spin-precession timescale in these equations allows one to integrate them efficiently, significantly reducing the computational cost of an implementation of a solution to the precession equations, as we will discuss in Sec.
V.3
.

Waveform construction

Using our solution to the evolution equations, we can describe the gravitational radiation emitted in the Fourier domain by expanding the wave amplitudes for small eccentricities
Yunes et al. 2009
;
Mishra et al. 2015
;
Boetzel et al. 2019
;

We first express the waveform in a fixed polarization basis as

\displaystyle h_{ab}(t)

\displaystyle=h_{+}(t)e_{ab}^{+}+h_{\times}(t)e_{ab}^{\times},

(110)

\displaystyle e_{ab}^{+}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{ab}^{+}\cos 2\psi-\epsilon_{ab}^{\times}\sin 2\psi,

(111)

\displaystyle e_{ab}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{ab}^{+}\sin 2\psi+\epsilon_{ab}^{\times}\cos 2\psi,

(112)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ab}^{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}^{a}\hat{p}^{b}-\hat{q}^{a}\hat{q}^{b},

(113)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ab}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}^{a}\hat{q}^{b}+\hat{q}^{a}\hat{p}^{b},

(114)

where
\bm{\hat{p}}
and
\bm{\hat{q}}
form an orthonormal triad together with the wave propagation vector
\bm{\hat{k}}
.
We can express the wave polarizations
h_{+}
and

through a rotated spin-weighted spherical harmonic decomposition. Using an inertial frame aligned with the constant vector
\bm{\hat{J}}^{(0)}

Schmidt et al. 2011
;
Boyle et al. 2011

\displaystyle h_{+}-ih_{\times}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\geq 2}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}H_{lm}Y_{lm}

(115)

\displaystyle Y_{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}=-l}^{l}D_{m^{\prime},m}^{l}(\phi_{z},\theta_{L},\zeta)\left.{}_{-2}Y_{lm^{\prime}}(\theta_{s},\phi_{s})\right.,

(116)

where
H_{l,-m}=(-1)^{l}H_{lm}^{*}
,

are the spherical angles of
\bm{\hat{L}}
in the inertial frame,
(\theta_{s},\phi_{s})
are the spherical angles of
\bm{\hat{k}}
in the same frame, and
\zeta
satisfies

.
\left.{}_{-2}Y_{lm^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)\right.
are the spin-weighted spherical harmonics, and

are the Wigner D-matrices. The conventions used in this work are given in appendix
A
.

Similarly as in
Klein et al. 2018
, we can expand the wave amplitudes

with a Fourier decomposition in the mean orbital phase
\lambda
and the argument of periastron
\delta\lambda
as

\displaystyle H_{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{lm}^{(m-n)}e^{i(m-n)\delta\lambda}e^{in\lambda},

(117)

\displaystyle G_{lm}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{|p|}\right).

(118)

We can construct an eccentric precessing waveform based on the solution described in Sec.
III.5
, along the same principles as laid out in
Klein et al. 2018
:

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{+,\times}(f)=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{n\geq 1}\tilde{h}_{n}^{(0)}(f)\tilde{h}_{n}^{\text{PP,$+,\times$}}(f)\,,

(119)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{n}^{(0)}(f)=

\displaystyle\;\sqrt{2\pi}\;T_{n}\exp\Big[i\Big(2\pi ft_{n}-n\lambda(t_{n})-\frac{\pi}{4}\Big)\Big]\,,

(120)

\displaystyle 2\pi f=

\displaystyle\;n\dot{\lambda}(t_{n})\,,

(121)

\displaystyle T_{n}=

\displaystyle\;\left[n\ddot{\lambda}(t_{n})\right]^{-1/2}\,,

(122)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{n}^{\text{PP,$+,\times$}}(f)=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{p=-P}^{P}e^{i\Delta\Psi_{n,p}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=-k_{\text{max}}}^{k_{\text{max}}}a_{k,k_{\text{max}}}\mathcal{A}_{n,p}^{+,\times}(t_{n}+\Delta t_{n,p}+kT_{n})\,,

(123)

where

\displaystyle\Delta t_{n,p}=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{P}\frac{1}{q!}\left(-\frac{p}{n}\right)^{q}D^{q-1}\left(\frac{\delta\dot{\lambda}^{q}}{\ddot{\lambda}}\right)\,,

(124)

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi_{n,p}=\;

\displaystyle-p\delta\lambda+n\sum_{q=2}^{P+1}\frac{1}{q!}\left(-\frac{p}{n}\right)^{q}D^{q-2}\left(\frac{\delta\dot{\lambda}^{q}}{\ddot{\lambda}}\right)\,,

(125)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ddot{\lambda}}\frac{d}{dt}\,,

(126)

P\geq 0
and

are arbitrary integers,

satisfy the linear system of equations

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\text{max}}}a_{k,k_{\text{max}}}+\frac{1}{2}a_{0,k_{\text{max}}}=1\,,

(127)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\text{max}}}a_{k,k_{\text{max}}}\frac{k^{2q}}{(2q)!}=\frac{(-i)^{q}}{2^{q}q!}\,,\quad q\in\{1,\ldots,k_{\text{max}}\}\,,

(128)

\displaystyle a_{-k,k_{\text{max}}}=a_{k,k_{\text{max}}}\,,

(129)

and the amplitudes are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n,p}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\geq|n-p|}\left.G_{l,p-n}^{(p)}\right.^{*}\left[(-1)^{l}Y_{l,n-p}+Y_{l,p-n}^{*}\right],

(130)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n,p}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{l\geq|n-p|}\left.G_{l,p-n}^{(p)}\right.^{*}\left[(-1)^{l}Y_{l,n-p}-Y_{l,p-n}^{*}\right].

VI
Further extensions of the solution

The solutions presented here are based on a few assumptions that it might be preferable to relax in some situations. For example, when extended objects such as neutron stars are considered, the equations of precession are modified in such a way that the effective spin
\chi_{\text{eff}}
is no longer conserved
Barker and Oconnell 1979
;
Poisson 1998
. Furthermore, the solution presented in this work is based on neglecting certain terms in the spin precession equations, such as next-to-leading order spin-orbit and spin-spin terms
Bohé et al. 2013
;
Bohé et al. 2015
, or cubic-in-spin terms
Marsat 2015
. The extent to which this approximation is valid remains to be assessed, and to do so we need to construct a waveform that includes those terms. The present work can be used as a basis to accomplish such tasks, since such effects appear at higher PN order than the one we included.
In the following, we briefly sketch a possible avenue to construct such an extension, by using quadrupole-monopole terms
Barker and Oconnell 1979
;
Poisson 1998
as an example.

The equations of precession acquire a modification when applied to extended bodies:

\bm{\Omega}_{i}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\mu_{i}+\frac{3}{2}\left[1-y\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\left(\kappa_{i}\bm{s}_{i}+\bm{s}_{j}\right)\right]\right\}\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{s}_{i}\\
+\frac{1}{2}y\bm{s}_{j}\times\bm{s}_{i},

(136)

where
\kappa_{i}
is the quadrupole parameter of body
i
. In the case of black holes, we have
\kappa_{i}=1
, so that we can write

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Omega}_{i}^{\text{BH}}-\frac{3}{2}y\delta\kappa_{i}\left(\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}\right)\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{s}_{i},

(137)

with
\delta\kappa_{i}=\kappa_{i}-1
.

Eqs. (
14
-
21
) being purely geometrical, they are not affected by a modification of the precession equations. On the other hand, we find as the derivatives of
\delta\chi
and
\chi_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\delta\chi

\displaystyle=6y^{6}\left[1-y\chi_{\text{eff}}-\frac{y}{2}\left(\delta\kappa_{1}\chi_{1}+\delta\kappa_{2}\chi_{2}\right)\right]V,

(138)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\chi_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=3y^{7}\left[\delta\kappa_{1}\chi_{1}-\delta\kappa_{2}\chi_{2}\right]V,

(139)

where we defined
\chi_{i}=\bm{\hat{L}}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}
and
V=\left(\bm{\hat{L}}\times\bm{s}_{1}\right)\cdot\bm{s}_{2}
.

We can observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\chi_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(y\right)\times\mathcal{D}\delta\chi.

(140)

Furthermore, since the form of Eq. (
22
) follows from the unmodified Eqs. (
14
-
21
), we can still write

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}\delta\chi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{4}A^{2}y^{11}\left(\delta\mu\delta\chi^{3}+B\delta\chi^{2}+C\delta\chi+D\right),

(141)

with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=1-y\chi_{\text{eff}}-\frac{y}{2}(\delta\kappa_{1}\chi_{1}+\delta\kappa_{2}\chi_{2}),

(142)

and
B
,
C
, and
D
unmodified with respect to the black hole solutions.

This presents a few complications from the fact that
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
are no longer constants. However, Eqs. (
140
), (
142
), and (
155
-
157
) lead us to postulate that if we write

\displaystyle\chi_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\chi_{\text{eff}}^{(k)}y^{k},

(143)

\displaystyle\delta\chi

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta\chi^{(k)}y^{k},

(144)

we can solve order by order by using for
\chi_{\text{eff}}^{(0)}
and
\delta\chi^{(0)}
the solutions of the black hole case derived in Sec.
II
. The new solutions that we will then derive for
\chi_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
and
\delta\chi^{(1)}
will be linear in
\delta\kappa_{i}
, and so on. Therefore, we can in principle derive new solutions as perturbations of the solution presented in this paper, still varying on the radiation reaction timescale only. We argue that this will continue to be true as long as the modifications of the precession equations can be written as perturbations of Eqs. (
1
-
3
).

We stress that we intend the discussion presented in this section as a mere sketch of a possible avenue for including a wide range of extra effects into our solution. If more work is needed to verify its applicability, we believe that its importance makes it worth mentioning.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have constructed a new class of gravitational waveform models for inspiralling precessing binary systems on eccentric orbits, by taking advantage of an analytic solution of the conservative problem. This solution allows for the modification of the complete set of equations of motion, such that it only varies on the radiation reaction timescale. This permits the construction of eccentric precessing waveforms with computational costs comparable to ones valid for spin-aligned systems. While we observe a modest efficiency gain of a factor
2
-
5
for binaries in the late inspiral, we observe a substantial gain of a factor
20
-
50
for binaries in the early inspiral such as stellar-origin binaries observed with LISA. This improvement comes at a modest cost in accuracy, not likely to lead to unwanted biases in parameter recovery for systems with signal-to-noise ratios lower than several hundreds. This solution opens the way for Bayesian parameter estimation studies of eccentric, precessing stellar-origin binaries with LISA, an important class of sources for the study of black hole binary formation mechanisms. Such waveforms have been implemented to model the LISA time-delay interferometry response, and have been shown to require a computational cost of about
10
-
30
ms per waveform evaluation, well inside the range of possibilities for Bayesian parameter estimation studies.

We point to possible extensions of this model based on perturbations of the spin-induced precession equations. Important such extensions include quadrupole-monopole interactions relevant to the modelling of binaries involving neutron stars, higher post-Newtonian order spin interactions, and higher order in spin interactions. While these interactions are likely to have relatively small effects on the precession physics, they can still be relevant for stellar-origin binaries in the LISA band as they acquire thousands of spin-induced precession cycles. Studies determining their importance therefore need to be conducted, and we point to future work for their implementation.
