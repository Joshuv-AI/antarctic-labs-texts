Self-restricting Noise and Exponential Relative Entropy Decay Under Unital Quantum Markov Semigroups
Nicholas LaRacuente 1,∗ 1 Indiana University Bloomington, Bloomington, IN 47408, USA ∗ nlaracu@iu.edu

Abstract
. States of open quantum systems often decay continuously under environmental interactions. Quantum Markov semigroups model such processes in dissipative environments. It is known that finite-dimensional quantum Markov semigroups with GNS detailed balance universally obey complete modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities (CMLSIs), yielding exponential decay of relative entropy to a subspace of fixed point states. We analyze continuous processes that combine dissipative with Hamiltonian time-evolution, precluding this notion of detailed balance. First, we find counterexamples to CMLSI-like decay for these processes and determine conditions under which it fails. In contrast, we prove that despite its absence at early times, exponential decay re-appears for unital, finite-dimensional quantum Markov semigroups at finite timescales. Finally, we show that when dissipation is much stronger than Hamiltonian time-evolution, the rate of eventual, exponential decay toward the semigroup’s decoherence-free subspace is bounded inversely in the decay rate of the dissipative part alone. Dubbed self-restricting noise, this inverse relationship arises when strong damping suppresses effects that would otherwise spread noise beyond its initial subspace.

Introduction

A primary challenge for quantum information theory is to understand noise-induced decay of quantum states, estimate rates, and invent strategies to improve control. Decay and decoherence are major challenges for quantum computing

Existing theory shows tensor-stable, exponential decay for a range of open quantum systems. Recall the notion of a quantum Markov semigroup (QMS): a family of parameterized quantum channels (completely positive, trace-preserving maps)
(\Phi^{t})_{t\geq 0}

[

\rho

In general, when a QMS has a fixed point subspace to which it induces decay, we define CMLSI with respect to the projection to that subspace. If a QMS induces persistent rotation without decay (for example, if it is unitary), then it may be more appropriate to regard
\mathcal{E}
as a decoherence-free subspace projection such that
\Phi^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\circ\Phi^{t}=R^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\circ R^{t}

Detailed balance (as formally recalled Section
2

Here we aim to extend beyond the detailed balance setting by incorporating non-trivial, system-internal Hamiltonian time-evolution alongside dissipation. When Hamiltonian dynamics do not commute with the dissipator’s fixed point subspace projection, they reduce the fixed point subspace. Early-time decay, however, is still toward the original fixed point subspace up to linear order in time. Hence some information that would be eventually destroyed may escape initial exponential decay.

\braket{x,y}_{\omega}=\text{tr}(\omega x^{\dagger}y)

(2)

Ways to Compare States and Channels

To study decay of quantum states, we recall several quantitative notions of distance or similarity.

By
\rho\geq\sigma
, we mean that
\rho
is greater than
\sigma

is a non-negative matrix. We say for a pair of quantum channels

and other channel

2.2.
Decay Under Quantum Channels

There are many ways to quantify and model decay of quantum states, often closely related to the theory of decoherence
[
1
,
27
]
, and several known strategies to forestall it
[
28
]
. A recurring question is when quantum information decays exponentially, a general but not entirely universal scenario
[
29
]
.

The Stinespring dilation models a quantum channel as unitary evolution on a system plus its initially uncorrelated environment, followed by a partial trace of the environment:

\Phi(\rho)=\text{tr}_{E}(R_{U}(\rho\otimes|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|^{E}))\hskip 2.84544pt,

(10)

where
E
is the environment system,
|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|^{E}
is an arbitrary initial state (which without loss of generality, we may assume pure), and
U
is a unitary on the joint system-environment. This model is however extremely general. For example, one may embed complex or even universal quantum computations in a non-Markovian environment
[
30
]
. To effectively study decay properties, we usually must impose additional assumptions.

In this work, we assume Markovianity of a time-parameterized family of quantum channels. Let
\Phi^{r,s}
denote a channel for each
s\geq r\geq 0
such that

\Phi^{s,t}\circ\Phi^{r,s}=\Phi^{r,t}

(11)

whenever
t\geq s\geq r\geq 0
. Equation (
11
) defines the property of
divisibility
in time. We assume that
\Phi^{t,t}=\text{Id}
for any
t\geq 0
, reflecting that time-evolution for zero time has no effect on the state. Moreover, we assume differentiability, recalling the potentially time-varying
Lindbladian

\mathcal{L}[t]:=\frac{d}{ds}\Phi^{t,t+s}\Big|_{s=0}\hskip 2.84544pt.

The Lindbladian decomposes into a Hamiltonian and a dissipative part,

\mathcal{L}[t](\rho)=i[H[t],\rho]+\mathcal{S}[t](\rho)\hskip 2.84544pt,

(12)

where
H
is a Hamiltonian that would on its own generate unitary time-evolution. Canonical decompositions into Hamiltonian and dissipative parts exist
[
8
,
31
]
. We also consider discrete compositions of channels
\Phi_{1}\circ...\circ\Phi_{k}
. A QMS denoted
(\Phi_{t})^{t\geq 0}
further assumes the semigroup property:
\Phi^{t}\circ\Phi^{s}=\Phi^{t+s}
. A QMS generally admits a time-independent Lindbladian as derivative, such that
\Phi^{t}=\exp(-\mathcal{L}t)
. An often-studied problem is to derive a model of divisible time-evolution as a coarse-grained description of dynamics induced by general system-environment interactions as in Equation (
10
). The Markovian approximation is usually derived for dissipative environments, in which information lost to the environment is quickly spread to eliminate backaction of that information on the system’s subsequent environment
[
32
]
.

In general, a dissipator as in Equation (
12
) has the form

\mathcal{S}=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\Big(\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{S}_{i}^{*}\mathcal{S}_{i}\rho+\rho\mathcal{S}_{i}^{*}\mathcal{S}_{i})-\mathcal{S}_{i}\rho\mathcal{S}_{i}^{*}\Big)\hskip 2.84544pt,

where each
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is known as a jump operator. A particuarly common case we will study in several places herein is that of depolarizing noise, which replaces an input density by complete mixture. The Lindbladian for depolarizing noise,
\mathcal{S}_{dep}
, is given by

\begin{split}S_{dep}(\rho)&:=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\frac{1}{2}(X_{i}X_{i}^{*}\rho+\rho X_{i}X_{i}^{*})-X_{i}\rho X_{i}=\rho-\frac{\hat{1}}{d}\end{split}

(13)

in dimension
d
, where
X_{0}=\hat{1},X_{1}=X,X_{2}=Y
and
X_{3}=Z
are the Pauli matrices normalized to have eigenvalues
{}^{+}_{-}1
. The depolarizing Lindbladian generates the time-parameterized depolarizing QMS,

\Phi^{t}_{\mathrm{dep}(\lambda)}(\rho):=e^{-\lambda\mathcal{S}}(\rho)=e^{-\lambda t}\rho+(1-e^{-\lambda t})\frac{\hat{1}}{d}\hskip 2.84544pt.

(14)

where
\lambda\geq 0
controls the decay rate.

There are many ways to quantify the extent to which a quantum system has decayed. A strong notion from open systems research is the modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality (MLSI) introduced by Kastoryano and Temme
[
14
]
. A QMS
(\Phi^{t})
satisfies
\lambda
-MLSI if

D(\Phi^{t}(\rho)\|\Phi^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}(\rho))\leq e^{-\lambda t}D(\rho\|\mathcal{E}(\rho))

for all inputs
\rho
. Classically stochastic versions of this inequality appear in earlier literature
[
33
,
34
]
. MLSI differs from but was inspired by the earlier notion of logarithmic Sobolev inequalities
[
35
,
36
]
. It was more recently noted that the original log-Sobolev inequality fails for QMSs that lack a unique fixed point state
[
37
]
. In contrast the modified version remains valid
[
38
]
in the presence of more general fixed-point subspaces, generalizing from decay toward a fixed-point state to decay toward a fixed-point subspace. This observation motivates the strengthening known as CMLSI
[
13
]
, which automatically incorporates a potentially untouched auxiliary system of unspecified dimension. A finite-dimensional QMS
(\Phi^{t})
generated by
\mathcal{L}
with fixed-point subspace projection
\mathcal{E}
has
\lambda
-CMLSI if

\begin{split}&D\big(\big(\Phi^{t}\otimes\text{Id}^{B}\big)(\rho)\big\|\big(\Phi^{t}\otimes\text{Id}^{B}\big)\circ(\mathcal{E}\otimes\text{Id}^{B})(\rho)\big)\leq e^{-\lambda t}D(\rho\|(\mathcal{E}\otimes\text{Id}^{B})(\rho))\end{split}

(15)

for all extensions by a finite-dimensional auxiliary subsystem
B
. A discrete analog to CMLSI is the complete, strong data processing inequality (CSDPI), which for decay to a fixed point takes the form:

D((\Phi\otimes\text{Id}^{B})(\rho)\|((\Phi\circ\mathcal{E})\otimes\text{Id}^{B})(\rho))\leq\lambda D(\rho\|(\mathcal{E}\otimes\text{Id}^{B})(\rho))

for
\lambda\in[0,1)
. As some particularly useful properties of CMLSI:

•

For a time-varying Lindbladian and associated divisible family of channels with the same fixed point projection, divisibility also applies to estimated decay rates. Assume that for each
t\geq 0
, a time-parameterized Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}[t]
generates a QMS
s\mapsto\exp(-\mathcal{L}[t]s)
with time-independent fixed-point subspace projection
\mathcal{E}
and
\lambda[t]
-(C)MLSI. Then

\begin{split}D(\Phi^{s,t}(\rho)\|\Phi^{s,t}\circ\mathcal{E}(\rho))\leq e^{-\int_{s}^{t}\lambda[r]dr}D(\rho\|\mathcal{E}(\rho))\end{split}

(16)

for all input densities
\rho
. Relatedly, (C)MLSI implies decay from arbitrarily small initial relative entropy.

•

CMLSI and CSDPI are tensor-stable: if
(\Phi^{t})
and
(\Psi^{t})
are QMSs with
\lambda_{1}
-CMLSI and
\lambda_{2}
-CMLSI respectively, then
(\Phi^{t}\otimes\Psi^{t})
has
\min\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}
-CMLSI.

Via Pinsker’s inequality as recalled in Equation (
8
), decay under CMLSI at sufficiently long times upper-bounds the diamond-norm distance between a channel and its fixed-point subspace projection. Tensor-stability enables CMLSI to upper-bounded regularized capacities
[
39
]
, which are defined as optimizing over potentially entangled inputs between many copies of a quantum channel. CMLSI and CSDPI can also upper bound extends of quantum advantage in certain settings
[
40
]
.

CMLSI is known to hold with some constant for all finite-dimensional QMSs having GNS detailed balance with respect to a faithful state and a uniquely defined fixed-point subspace conditional expectation
\mathcal{E}
. This result was derived in a self-contained way in
[
41
]
. It was simultaneously derived in
[
11
]
for QMSs that are self-adjoint with respect to the trace, which via
[
12
]
also extends to all finite-dimensional QMSs with detailed balance. Furthermore, it was shown in
[
41
]
that CSDPI holds for quantum channels with GNS detailed balance and some additional, commonly satisfied assumptions.

Follow-up works have shown applications in the theory of many-body systems
[
42
,
5
,
43
]
. The tensor-stability and divisibility of CMLSI, as well as some technical properties of the relative entropy
[
44
]
, allowed many of these works to build up optimal decay rate bounds on whole systems from the existence of CMLSI constants for single-site constituents.

A primary limitation of known CMLSI results is that detailed balance is usually incompatible with the presence of a non-trivial Hamiltonian term as in Equation (
12
). For example, we may consider detailed balance with respect to complete mixture, which reduces to self-adjointness under the trace. Even if the dissipator
\mathcal{S}
is self-adjoint, we still find that

\mathcal{L}_{*}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\mathcal{S}(\rho)\hskip 2.84544pt,

reversing the sign of the Hamiltonian term compared with
\mathcal{L}
as in Equation (
12
) (physically, the unitary part of the process is time-reversed). In general,
-H\neq H
and generates distinct continuous-time evolution. Motivating examples of where CMLSI estimates fail are presented in Section
4
.

3.
Main Results and Proofs

For a channel
\Phi
in finite dimension defined on the predual of a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
, the multiplicative domain (properly of
\Phi^{*}
, its adjoint with respect to the trace) is

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{\Phi}:=\{&x\in\mathcal{M}:\Phi^{*}(y)\Phi^{*}(x)=\Phi^{*}(yx),\text{ and }\Phi^{*}(x)\Phi^{*}(y)=\Phi^{*}(xy)\text{ }\forall y\in\mathcal{M}\}\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

The multiplicative domain
\mathcal{N}_{\Phi}
is a subalgebra, and as such, it comes with a unique projection
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\Phi}
, which is a conditional expectation. Multiplicative domains have been previously applied in the theory of quantum error correction for unital quantum channels, in which they often act as decoherence-free subspaces. Leveraging this connection and
[
45
]
, results of
[
46
]
(later generalized in
[
47
]
) imply that the multiplicative domain
\mathcal{N}_{\Phi}
coincides with the fixed point subspace of
\Phi\circ\Phi^{*}

[
48
, Theorem 1.3]
when
\Phi
is unital. Other results have specifically characterized the decoherence-free subspaces of channels and Lindbladians
[
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
, on which the Lindbladian acts unitarily. Before directly addressing whether decay is exponential, we first use the analytical structure of the semigroup to infer that decay to the decoherence-free subalgebra begins immediately - a semigroup never admits initially non-decaying states that are not contained in its decoherence-free subspace.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\Phi^{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a QMS with generator bounded in diamond norm and
\rho
be an input density.
Assume that
(\Phi^{t})_{t\geq 0}
has (not necessarily unique) full rank fixed point density
\omega
. If there exists a
\tau>0
for which
D(\Phi^{\tau}(\rho)\|\Phi^{\tau}(\omega))=D(\rho\|\omega)
on density
\rho
, then for all
t\geq 0
,
\Phi^{t}_{*\omega}\Phi^{t}(\rho)=\rho
, and
D(\Phi^{t}(\rho)\|\Phi^{t}(\omega))=D(\rho\|\omega)
.

Proof.

For all
s>0
and every bounded generator
\mathcal{L}
,

\Phi^{s}_{*}\circ\Phi^{s}(\rho)=e^{-s\mathcal{L}_{*}}e^{-s\mathcal{L}}(\rho)=\sum_{j,k=0}^{\infty}s^{j+k}\frac{(-1)^{j+k}}{j!k!}\mathcal{L}^{j}_{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{k}(\rho)

is manifestly analytic in
s
within finite dimension and equal to its Taylor series around
s=0
, even after extending its domain from
s\in[0,\infty)
to the complex plane. Because
\Phi^{s}_{*}\circ\Phi^{s}(\rho)
is constant on the interval
[0,\tau)
, it must hold that

\frac{d^{(k)}}{ds^{(k)}}\big(\Phi^{s}_{*}\circ\Phi^{s}(\rho)\big)\Big|_{s=0}=0

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence
\Phi^{s}_{*}\circ\Phi^{s}(\rho)=\rho
is constant for all
s\geq 0
. Via the data processing inequality of relative entropy,

D(\rho\|\omega)=D(\Phi^{t}(\rho)\|\Phi^{t}(\omega))=D(\Phi^{\tau}(\rho)\|\Phi^{\tau}(\omega))

for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. Then by the properties of the Petz recovery map as in Equation (
9
),
\Phi^{t}_{*\omega}\Phi^{t}(\rho)=\rho
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. Since
\Phi^{t}_{*\omega}\circ\Phi^{t}
is constant for all
t\in(0,\tau)
,
\Phi_{*\omega}^{t}\circ\Phi^{t}(\rho)=\rho
for all
t>\tau
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(\Phi^{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a unital QMS with diamond norm-bounded generator. Let
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
denote the multiplicative domain projection of
\Phi^{\tau}
. For any
\tau>0
,
\Phi^{t}_{*}\circ\Phi^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}\circ\Phi^{t}_{*}\circ\Phi^{t}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
is unital,
\hat{1}/d=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}(\hat{1}/d)
. Recall as noted in
[
50
,
41
]
that
\Phi^{\tau}
is an isometry on its multiplicative domain, so

\begin{split}D(\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\rho)\|\hat{1}/d)&=D(\Phi^{\tau}(\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}(\rho))\|\Phi^{\tau}(\hat{1}/d))\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

Since
\Phi^{\tau}
does not decrease relative entropy between
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}(\rho))
and
\hat{1}/d
, it is inverted by its Petz recovery map
[
26
]
with respect to
\hat{1}/d
on this subspace, which is equal to
\Phi^{\tau}_{*}
, its (pre-)adjoint under the trace. Hence
\Phi^{\tau}_{*}\circ\Phi^{\tau}(\rho)=\rho
. Applying Lemma
3.1
,
\Phi^{t}_{*}\circ\Phi^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}(\rho)
for any
t\geq 0
and input state
\rho
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
is self-adjoint with respect to the trace,
(\Phi^{t}_{*}\circ\Phi^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}})_{*}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}\circ\Phi_{*}^{t}\circ\Phi^{t}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(\Phi^{t})
be a unital QMS, either for discrete
t\in\mathbb{N}
or continuous
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Assume there exists a conditional expectation
\mathcal{E}
such that
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=R^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}R^{t}
for time-dependent unitary rotation
R^{t}
and that
\Phi^{t}
approaches
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}
in diamond norm for sufficiently large
t
. Then for any
\tau>0
,
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
, the multiplicative domain projection of
\Phi^{\tau}
.

This Lemma follows from results of
[
45
]
combined with
[
49
]
. For completeness and simplicity, we present an elementary proof here.

Proof.

We may easily extend rotations to negative
t
by identifying
R^{-t}=R^{t}_{*}
, the adjoint map under the trace. Then by our original assumptions,
\|\Phi^{t}-R^{t}\mathcal{E}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon
for arbitrarily small
\epsilon
and sufficiently large
t
. Furthermore,

\Phi^{t}_{*}\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t})_{*}=(\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E})_{*}=(R^{t}\mathcal{E})_{*}=(\mathcal{E}R^{t})_{*}=R^{-t}\mathcal{E}\hskip 2.84544pt.

Hence using contractiveness of the diamond norm under quantum channels,
\|\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t}-\mathcal{E}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon
. Using Lemma
3.2
,

\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t})\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}\hskip 2.84544pt.

By self-adjointness of
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
, we also find immediately that
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
. This part of the proof shows that the subspace projected to by
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}
is contained in that projected to by
\mathcal{E}
.

To complete the proof, we now must show that
\mathcal{E}_{\Phi^{\tau}}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}
. Let
U^{t}
be defined such that
R^{t}(\cdot)=U^{t}\cdot U^{t}_{\dagger}
. Then for any operators
x,y
and all
t\geq 0

\begin{split}&\Phi^{t}(\mathcal{E}(x))\Phi^{t}(y)=U^{t}\mathcal{E}(x)U^{t}_{\dagger}\Phi^{t}(y)=U^{t}(\mathcal{E}(x)U^{t}_{\dagger}\Phi^{t}(y)U^{t})U^{t}_{\dagger}=U^{t}(\mathcal{E}(x)R^{-t}\Phi^{t}(y))U^{t}_{\dagger}\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

By the assumption that
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=R^{t}\mathcal{E}
and because
R^{-t}
was defined equal to
R^{t}_{*}
,
R^{-t}\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}
. Let
R^{-t}\Phi^{t}
have the Kraus representation

R^{-t}\Phi^{t}(\omega)=\sum_{i}K_{i}\omega K_{i}^{\dagger}

for all inputs
\omega
. Via
[
53
, Theorem 4.25]
and for any input
x
,
\mathcal{E}(x)
being in the fixed point subspace of the unital channel
R^{-t}\Phi^{t}
implies that
[\mathcal{E}(x),K_{i}]=0
for every
i
. Since
(R^{-t}\Phi^{t})_{*}=\Phi^{t}_{*}R^{t}
has the same Kraus decomposition up to the exchange
K_{i}\leftrightarrow K_{i}^{\dagger}
and also leaves
\mathcal{E}(x)
invariant,
[\mathcal{E}(x),K_{i}^{\dagger}]=0
for every
i
as well. Commutation with the Kraus operators shows that
R^{-t}\Phi^{t}
is a bimodule map for the space projected to by
\mathcal{E}
, hence
\mathcal{E}(x)R^{-t}\Phi^{t}(y)=R^{-t}\Phi^{t}(\mathcal{E}(x)y)
. After cancelling unitary rotations, we find that
\mathcal{E}
outputs to a subspace of the multiplicative domain of
\Phi^{\tau}
, completing the Lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Phi
be a unital quantum channel such that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
that for all
k\geq n
,
\|(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{k}-\mathcal{E}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon
, and
\|(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{k}-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon
for projections
\mathcal{E}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
. Further assume that
\|\Phi-\text{Id}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\delta
. Then
\|\mathcal{E}-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\Diamond}\leq 2\epsilon+2\delta
.

Proof.

The channel family
(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{m}
is a (discrete-time) semigroup in
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Via
[
54
, Theorem 6.7]
, since
\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t}
is trace-symmetric,
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{m}=\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
for some fixed point projection
\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
that a priori might depend on
t
. Within finite dimension, this convergence can be taken in the diamond norm. The same holds for
(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{m}
.

We observe that

\Phi(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{m}=(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{m}\Phi\hskip 2.84544pt.

(17)

Since quantum channels are diamond norm contractions, upper bounds on the diamond norm are stable when concatenated with some other channel applications. By the assumption that
\|\Phi-\text{Id}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\delta
and by the monotonicity of the diamond norm under pre- or post-application of quantum channels,

\begin{split}&\|\Phi(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{m}-(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{m}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\delta\text{, and }\|(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{m}\Phi-(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{m}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\delta\hskip 2.84544pt,\end{split}

so

\|(\Phi_{*}\Phi)^{m}-(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{m}\|_{\Diamond}\leq 2\delta\hskip 2.84544pt.

Combining via the triangle inequality with the assumed convergences completes the Lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(\Phi^{t})
be a finite-dimensional, unital QMS. Then there exists a trace-symmetric conditional expectation
\mathcal{E}
such that for any
\epsilon>0
and
t>0
, there is a sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
that for every
m\geq n
,

\begin{split}&\|(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t})^{m}-\mathcal{E}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon\hskip 2.84544pt,\hskip 2.84544pt\|(\Phi^{t}\Phi^{t}_{*})^{m}-\mathcal{E}\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon,\text{ and }\|[\Phi^{t},(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t})^{m}]\|_{\Diamond}\leq\epsilon\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

Proof.

The first two inequalities simply apply Lemma
3.4
for channels corresponding to semigroups at fixed time.

We easily see that
\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
is self-adjoint with respect to the trace, so it is a conditional expectation. Using the monotonicity of diamond norm under channel application to both arguments and that
\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
is a fixed point projection, we obtain the existence of such
n
for any desired
\epsilon
in the first inequality. It still remains to show that
\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
does not actually depend on
t
. By the data processing inequality for relative entropy,

\begin{split}&D(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}_{(t)}(\rho)\|\hat{1}/d)\leq D(\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}_{(t)}(\rho)\|\hat{1}/d)\leq D(\mathcal{E}_{(t)}(\rho)\|\hat{1}/d)=D(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}_{(t)}(\rho)\|\hat{1}/d)\hskip 2.84544pt,\end{split}

so all of these expressions are equal. Using Lemma
3.1
, it holds for every
s\geq 0
that
\Phi_{*}^{s}\Phi^{s}\mathcal{E}_{(t)}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{(t)}(\rho)
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{(s)}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi^{s}_{*}\Phi^{s})^{m}
for every
s
,
\mathcal{E}_{(s)}\mathcal{E}_{(t)}=\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
. Since the projections are self-adjoint,
\mathcal{E}_{(t)}\mathcal{E}_{(s)}=\mathcal{E}_{(t)}
too. Switching the roles of
t
and
s
,
\mathcal{E}_{(t)}=\mathcal{E}_{(s)}
. This equality holds for every
t,s>0
, so all of the fixed point projections are the same map. We henceforth drop the subscript and call this projection
\mathcal{E}
.

To obtain the 2nd inequality, we set
t
sufficiently small to apply Lemma
3.4
for sufficiently small
\delta
and use the first inequality to obtain arbitrarily small
\epsilon
for that Lemma’s assumptions.

Recalling Equation
17
with
\|(\Phi^{t}\Phi^{t}_{*})^{m}-(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t})^{m}\|_{\Diamond}\leq 2\epsilon
recovers the final commutator bound for all
t\geq 0
. To go from
2\epsilon
to
\epsilon
, we can simply choose the original
\epsilon
smaller.
∎

Combining previous Lemmas, the multiplicative domain projection universally acts as a fixed point projection up to rotation for unital channel families with the appropriate series expansion:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(\Phi^{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a finite-dimensional, unital QMS. Then there is a unique, trace-symmetric conditional expectation
\mathcal{E}
projecting to the multiplicative domain of
\Phi^{t}
for every
t>0
. Moreover, there exists a family of unitary rotations
(R^{t})_{t\geq 0}
for which
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=R^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}R^{t}
.

Proof.

First let
\mathcal{E}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi^{\tau}_{*}\Phi^{\tau})^{m}
for some fixed
\tau>0
. Lemma
3.5
confirms that
\mathcal{E}
is a unital conditional expectation that does not depend on the choice of
\tau
. Moreoever, Lemma
3.5
implies that
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}
for every
t\geq 0
. Hence by Lemma
3.3
,
\mathcal{E}
is the multiplicative domain projection for channel
\Phi^{t}
independently of
t
for all
t>0
. Using that
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\Phi^{t}\circ\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t})^{m}
,
\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}
.
It was shown in
[
15
, Proposition 2.1]
and
[
16
, Proposition 2]
that
\Phi^{t}\circ\mathcal{E}(\rho)=e^{-iHt}\mathcal{E}(\rho)e^{iHt}
, assuming that the Lindbladian is written with a Hamiltonian
H
and traceless, orthonormal jump operators. Hence
R^{t}=e^{-iHt}
.
We identify
R^{-t}:=R^{t}_{*}
. Since
\mathcal{E}
is self-adjoint under the trace,
\mathcal{E}R^{t}=(R^{-t}\mathcal{E})_{*}
. We then apply the same arguments for
(\Phi^{t}_{*})
, using the same
\mathcal{E}
via Lemma
3.5
and obtaining that
\Phi^{t}_{*}\mathcal{E}=\tilde{R}^{t}\mathcal{E}
for some
\tilde{R}^{t}
. Furthermore,

\begin{split}&\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\Phi^{t}_{*}\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=\tilde{R}^{t}\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=\tilde{R}^{t}\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\tilde{R}^{t}R^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

Hence
\tilde{R}^{t}\mathcal{E}=R^{t}_{*}\mathcal{E}
. This observation yields the expected commutation of
R^{t}
and
\mathcal{E}
:

\mathcal{E}R^{t}=(R^{-t}\mathcal{E})_{*}=(\Phi^{t}_{*}\mathcal{E})_{*}=\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=R^{t}\mathcal{E}\hskip 2.84544pt.

We thereby conclude that

\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=R^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}R^{t}

for each value of
t
. Recalling Lemma
3.3
with the discrete semigroup given by
m\rightarrow(\Phi^{t}_{*}\Phi^{t})^{m}
,
\mathcal{E}
is the multiplicative domain projection of
\Phi^{t}
.
∎

3.1.
Adding a Hamiltonian to a Dissipator that has (C)MLSI

We delineate the regimes in which CMLSI appears and note a somewhat counter-intuitive relationship between the decay rate induced by
\mathcal{S}
and that of
\mathcal{L}
for QMSs in the form of Equation (
12
).

We first address the case of a Lindbladian for which the dissipative generator
\mathcal{S}
would on its own induce decay to a fixed point subspace with projection
\mathcal{E}_{0}
. We show that general CMLSI still holds when the Hamiltonian commutes with
\mathcal{E}_{0}
so that
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{0}
:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindbladian with fixed point conditional expectation
\mathcal{E}
. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
be a Lindbladian generating time-evolution that commutes with
\mathcal{E}
,
\Psi_{1},...,\Psi_{m}
be quantum channels commuting with
\mathcal{E}
,
\Phi^{s}
be the channel generated by
\mathcal{L}+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
from time
0
to
s
, and

\Theta:=\Psi_{1}\circ\Phi^{t_{2}-t_{1}}\circ\Psi_{2}\circ\Phi^{t_{3}-t_{2}}\circ...\Psi_{m-1}\circ\Phi^{t_{m}-t_{m-1}}\circ\Psi_{m}\hskip 2.84544pt.

If
\mathcal{L}
has
\lambda
-(C)MLSI, then
\mathcal{L}+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
has
\lambda
-CMLSI. Moreover,

\begin{split}&D(\Theta(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}\circ\Theta(\rho))\leq e^{-t\lambda}D(\rho\|\mathcal{E}(\rho))\end{split}

for any
t_{1},...,t_{m}>0
such that
t_{1}+...+t_{m}=t
.

Proof.

First, assume that
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=0
. For each
j\in 1...m
, let

\Theta_{j}:=\Psi_{j}\Phi^{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}...\Phi^{t_{m}-t_{m-1}}\Psi_{m}\hskip 2.84544pt.

Via the data processing inequality and commutation of
\Psi_{j}
and
\Phi^{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}
with
\mathcal{E}
,

\begin{split}&D(\Theta_{j}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}\circ\Theta_{j}(\rho))\leq D(\Phi^{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}\Theta_{j+1}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}(\Theta_{j+1}(\rho)))\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

Then using assumed (C)MLSI,

\begin{split}&D(\Phi^{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}\Theta_{j+1}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}(\Theta_{j+1}(\rho)))\leq e^{-(t_{j+1}-t_{j})\lambda}D(\Theta_{j+1}(\rho)\|\mathcal{E}(\Theta_{j+1}(\rho)))\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

Iterating the inequality from
j=1
to
j=m
completes this case.

We apply the Suzuki-Trotter expansion. We subdivide the time interval
[t_{1},t_{m}]
into smaller intervals of length
(t_{m}-t_{1})/k
, where
k
is chosen to be an integer multiple of
m
. Let
s_{j}
label the start of each such interval for each
j\in 1...k-1
, and
s_{k}=s_{m}
. Let
\tilde{\Psi}_{j}
be equal to
\Psi_{j/(k/m)}
when
jm/k
is an integer
\leq k

Remark 3.16
.

Theorem
3.15
is
not
a consequence of dynamical decoupling, strong coupling, or feed-forward error correction. Furthermore, analogous effects are unlikely to arise from emergent, passive error correction in the primary settings considered.

One may consider the unitary in Equation (
10
) to be generated by a combination of internal dynamics,
H_{\text{sys}}
, environmental dynamics,
H_{\text{env}}
and system-environment coupling,
H_{\text{sys-env}}
. It was previously observed
[
62
,
63
]
that within finite dimension, and if
H_{\text{env}}
is not too fast, then

\lim_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}e^{i(\gamma H_{\text{sys}}+H_{\text{env}}+H_{\text{sys-env}})t}=e^{i\gamma H_{\text{sys}}t}e^{i(H_{\text{env}}+\mathcal{E}(H_{\text{sys-env}}))t}.

In this case,
\mathcal{E}
is a projection to the subalgebra of operators that commute with
H_{\text{sys}}
. If
H_{\text{sys}}
is replaced by a series of discrete interruptions, one obtains a form of dynamical decoupling
[
66
,
67
]
. For a more comprehensive review of dynamical decoupling and related techniques, see
[
51
,
28
]
.

In the Markov regime, however, adding a Hamiltonian generally does not induce dynamical decoupling or strong coupling against the dissipative term
[
68
]
. Primarily, the dissipative assumption requires that
H_{\text{env}}
acts much more quickly than
H_{\text{sys-env}}
, effectively de-localizing correlations between the environment and the system’s past extremely quickly. This assumption yields the divisibility of entropy decay. To illustrate, we recall the depolarizing semigroup
(\Phi^{t}_{\mathrm{dep}(1)})
given in Equation (
14
).
At any
t\geq 0
, this channel commutes with application of any local unitary. Hence if one applies a series of dynamical decoupling unitaries
R_{0},...,R_{k}
, one finds that

R_{0}\circ\prod_{j=1}^{k}(R_{j}\circ\Phi^{t/k})=R_{0}\circ...\circ R_{k}\circ\Phi_{\mathrm{dep}(1)}^{t}\hskip 2.84544pt.

Similarly, for the Lindbladian
\mathcal{S}_{dep}
generating depolarizing noise,

e^{-(i[H_{\text{sys}},\cdot]+\mathcal{S}_{dep})t}=e^{-i[H{\text{sys}},\cdot]t}e^{-\mathcal{S}_{dep}t}\hskip 2.84544pt.

No unitary may undo the entropy-generating effects of
\Phi_{\mathrm{dep}(1)}^{t}
. Hence unitary interruption does not forestall the decay predicted by (C)MLSI, as generalized and formalized in Lemma
3.7
. To apply dynamical decoupling to this and related forms of noise, one must use fast pulses that bypass the dissipative approximation, exposing the underlying system-environment unitary coupling. The pulse rate needed depends not just on
H_{\text{sys-env}}
, but on
H_{\text{env}}
.

In contrast, Theorem
3.15
concerns the regime in which
H_{\text{sys-env}}
and
H_{\text{env}}
generate faster dynamics than
H_{\text{sys}}
, formalized by the effective dissipator
\mathcal{S}
having
\lambda_{0}
-(C)MLSI such that
\lambda_{0}
is large compared to the maximum eigenvalue of
H_{\text{sys}}
. This is the opposite regime from that of the strong coupling limit or that in which dynamical decoupling is expected to perform well.

Since the examples we will consider do not include feed-forward or explicitly describe measurements, there is no active error correction. There is some connection to error reduction via Zeno-based dissipative error reduction
[
69
]
. In the scenario of this paper, however, noise restriction via Zeno-like effects is self-mediated, rather than constructed through additional interactions. It is also possible for dissipative interactions to induce passive error correction
[
70
]
. As we primarily focus on unital Lindbladians, the emergence of passive error correction is however unexpected in this regime.

Remark 3.17
.

An immediate consequence of Proposition
3.10
is that (C)MLSI fails for asymptotically small time. As studied in the following Subsection
3.2
, one can often obtain (C)MLSI-like decay at finite timescales, suggesting that (C)MLSI might still be a reasonable effective model at physically relevant scales. Theorem
3.15
, however, upper bounds decay rates if either
\|H\|_{\infty}
is small or the decay induced by
\mathcal{S}
alone large. Recalling Remark
3.16
, here we see that noise becomes constrained when the dissipative interactions overpower the system’s internal Hamiltonian, a regime
opposite
to that of usual dynamical decoupling or strong coupling.

3.2.
Repeatable CSDPI for Unital Markov Processes at Finite Timescales

The breakdown of (C)MLSI in Proposition
3.10
for unital QMSs when
\mathcal{E}\neq\mathcal{E}_{0}
arises from the fact that at small timescales, the dissipator is still effectively decaying the system toward the subspace of
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, but at long timescales toward that of
\mathcal{E}
. The underlying component that fails is divisibility: the exponential decay rate in relative entropy toward
\mathcal{E}
at time
t>0
is not the integral of decay rates from times 0 to
t
, violating Equation (
16
). As noted in Remark
3.16
, the action of a Hamiltonian cannot reverse relative entropy decay toward a unital fixed point subspace projection. Hence a natural question is whether an indivisible generalization of CMLSI holds more generally for unital QMSs. In this Section, we answer this question in the affirmative.

Previously,
[
41
, Theorem 2.6]
showed that for any unital quantum channel
\Phi
, there is a CSDPI constant
\lambda_{\Phi}\in[0,1)
such that

D(\Phi(\rho)\|\Phi\circ\mathcal{E}_{\Phi}(\rho))\leq\lambda_{\Phi}D(\rho\|\mathcal{E}_{\Phi}(\rho))

and gave a procedure to calculate
\lambda_{\Phi}
in terms of the behavior of
(\Phi\Phi_{*})^{k}
for sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
. An immediate question is whether
\Phi^{k}
immediately has (C)SDPI constant
\lambda_{\Phi}^{k}
by iteration. If so, such a result would immediately lead to a discrete-time (C)MLSI-like bound. As shown in Counterexample
4.4
, however, such iteration is not always possible. In general,
[\Phi,\mathcal{E}_{\Phi}]\neq 0
, so the same (C)SPDI result no longer applies after the first step. More strikingly, we also see that sometimes there is no initial decay to the large-
k
fixed point of
\Phi^{k}
, since the eventual fixed point subspace projection may not coincide with the multiplicative domain projection. Other results of
[
41
]
avoid this barrier by assuming GNS detailed balance.

The intuition for our adjustment is to show that the semigroup property suffices to ensure that
\Phi^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\Phi^{t}=R^{t}\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}R^{t}
for some unitary conjugation family
R^{t}
, and for a projection
\mathcal{E}
that does not depend on the choice of
t\geq 0
. A natural intuition would be that as
\Phi^{t}
is analytic for
t\geq 0
, certain qualitative properties must be preserved for all
t\geq 0
. If this analyticity passes to the induced decay, then it should be impossible for a QMS that induces late-time decay not to induce decay in any interval
(0,\tau)
for arbitrarily small
\tau>0
.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
(\Phi^{t})_{t\geq 0}

\mathcal{E}
, and for each
\tau\geq 0

\begin{split}&D((\Phi^{\tau}\otimes\hat{1}^{B})(\rho)\|((\mathcal{E}\circ\Phi^{\tau})\otimes\hat{1}^{B})(\rho))\leq(1-1/2k_{\tau})D(\rho\|(\mathcal{E}\otimes\hat{1}^{B})(\rho))\end{split}

Proof.

The first part of the Lemma is the assertion that for some

\mathcal{E}

Example 4.3

Counterexample 4.4

(Non-iterability of (C)SPDI)
.

In Section
3.2
, we considered whether the CSPDI inequality implied by
[

generally iterates to imply a discrete-time (C)MLSI-like inequality. Here we show a counterexample. Consider a channel on the
n
-qubit spin chain:

\Phi(\rho):=U_{cyc}((1-\epsilon)\frac{\hat{1}}{2}\otimes\rho^{(>2)}+\epsilon\rho)U_{cyc}^{\dagger}\hskip 2.84544pt,

where
\rho^{(>2)}
denotes the state on all but the first qubit, and

applies the leftward cyclic qubit permutation
1\leftarrow 2,2\leftarrow 3,...,n-1\leftarrow n,n\leftarrow 1
. One may easily see that
\Phi^{n}
is a completely depolarizing channel. For any
m\in

Examining

[

Example 4.5

(Cross-Resonance Hamiltonian with Markovian Dephasing)
.

In principle, inequalities such as CMLSI and CSDPI lower bound decoherence rates, as well as quantum capacities and other key notions for quantum information. The imposition of detailed balance is however a substantial restriction, as it precludes studying systems governed by unitary drift or during active quantum processing. Noise during quantum processing or other dynamics is often larger than that during idle times, and the majority of such noise in superconducting qubits often arises from two-qubit interactions
[
75
, p1026]

A\otimes B
. One entangling gate is via the cross-resonance (CR) interaction between two qubits. Let

\begin{split}&H_{0}=\frac{Z}{2}\otimes\big(\omega_{ZI}\hat{1}+\omega_{ZX}X+\omega_{ZY}Y+\omega_{ZZ}Z\big)\text{ ,}\\
&H_{1}=\frac{\hat{1}}{2}\otimes\big(\omega_{IX}X+\omega_{IY}Y+\omega_{IZ}Z\big)\text{ , and }\\
&\mathcal{S}(\rho)=\frac{1}{T_{2}}\big(2\rho-(Z\otimes\hat{1})\rho(Z\otimes\hat{1})-(\hat{1}\otimes Z)\rho(\hat{1}\otimes Z)\big)\hskip 2.84544pt.\end{split}

(33)

The CR Hamiltonian is given by
H_{CR}=H_{0}+H_{1}

as described in
[
76
]

\omega_{??}
are hardware-specific. Although the gate’s precise implementation involves a time-dependent turning on and off of the Hamiltonian,
[

notes a reasonable approximation is that the unitary gate

Passive noise in qubits is often modeled as a combination of non-unital relaxation with characteristic time denoted

Though the decoherence rate will depend on specifically on the coefficients in Equation (

A long-term motivation to study noise is understanding decoherence affecting quantum computers, memories, communication links, and related systems. Though fault-tolerant error correction presents an eventual solution to noise in quantum computation, there are still major challenges in the near term
[
2
,
78
]
. In the long term, noise will still contribute to the number of physical qubits required to implement fault-tolerant error correction, so understanding and reducing noise will help reduce the cost and energy demands of fault-tolerant quantum computers. Furthermore, noise in long-distance communication links and long-term memory will not simply disappear with error correction but impact error-corrected capacities
[
3

. As in Example
4.5
, extending CMLSI-like bounds to situations that combine unitary dynamics with noise is a step toward modeling systems that often appear in quantum information, including quantum processing. There are several relevant issues to address in future work, including time-dependent Hamiltonians and how they combine with non-unital noise, in order to complete the picture of CMLSI for pulsed gates with Markovian noise. In full generality, one likely must consider non-Markovian dynamics to fully understand the noise arising during quantum gates.

5.
Conclusions and Further Open Problems

As Theorem
3.15
, conditions were determined for when adding a Hamiltonian term to a trace-symmetric Lindbladian generator breaks or preserves (C)MLSI. A point we re-emphasize is that unlike in dynamical decoupling, adding a Hamiltonian to unital, Markovian noise does not slow the growth of entropy. Rather, CMLSI typically fails because the decoherence-free subspace of the new Lindbladian is smaller than that of the original, so the original dissipator no longer induces the full range of eventual decay.

A long-running problem in semigroup theory is to understand the most general class of canonical dissipator
[
31
]
. In this paper, we focus on how adding a Hamiltonian affects decay rate. It remains open whether there exist canonically dissipative (having no extractable, non-trivial Hamiltonian part), finite-dimensional generators that do not decay states to a fixed point subspace or do so slower than exponentially.

Left open by Theorem
3.19
is whether an analogous decay estimate holds for Lindbladians of the form in Equation (
12
), but where
\mathcal{S}
has (C)MLSI with a
non-unital

\mathcal{E}_{0}
. In this scenario, the fixed point subspace projected to be
\mathcal{E}
is not obviously contained in that by
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, or the full time-evolution may exhibit limit cycles
[
79
,
80
]
. The Hamiltonian might more directly counteract effects of the dissipator. Mathematically, the primary barrier to extending Theorem
3.19
beyond unital channels is to generalize properties of the multiplicative domain to the non-unital case. For example, an appropriate generalization of
[
48
, Theorem 1.3]
would strongly hint at the proper definition of a fixed point up to rotation in the non-unital case. As noted in
[
46
]
, however, the connection between the multiplicative domain, unitarily correctable subspace, and fixed point subspace of
\Phi\circ\Phi^{*}
for a channel
\Phi
is weaker without unitality. Deriving and applying an analog of
[
48
, Theorem 1.3]
without this correspondence might be the topic for another work. We therefore leave this generalization to future investigation.

Relatedly, another natural question is whether all QMSs decay states to a fixed point subspace up to persistent rotations. In this work, we exploited a connection to the multiplicative domain for which more in the unital channel setting. Physically, the restriction to unitality in Theorem
3.19
ensures that coherent dynamics ultimately commute with projection to the overall fixed point subspace, so the fixed point subspace of the dissipative component is always at least as large as that of the combined process. Such an assumption is potentially violated when combining a non-unital dissipator with Hamiltonian time-evolution. Furthermore, initial relative entropy is unbounded for fixed point states with arbitrarily small components. These complications naturally extend to those encountered with non-Markovian processes. In full generality, time-varying unitary dynamics might preclude an analogous notion of a fixed point or decoherence-free subspace, requiring a different formulation of decay.
