Diffusion approximations of Oja’s online principal component analysis
Jian-Guo Liu, Zibu Liu

Abstract
. Oja’s algorithm of principal component analysis (PCA) has been one of the methods utilized in practice to reduce dimension. In this paper, we focus on the convergence property of the discrete algorithm. To realize that, we view the algorithm as a stochastic process on the parameter space and semi-group. We approximate it by SDEs, and prove large time convergence of the SDEs to ensure its performance. This process is completed in three steps. First, the discrete algorithm can be viewed as a semigroup: S k ​ φ = 𝔼 ⁡ [ φ ⁡ ( 𝐖 ⁡ ( k ) ) ] S^{k}\varphi=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbf{W}(k))] . Second, we construct stochastic differential equations (SDEs) on the Stiefel manifold, i.e. the diffusion approximation, to approximate the semigroup. By proving the weak convergence, we verify that the algorithm is ’close to’ the SDEs. Finally, we use reversibility of the SDEs to prove long time convergence.



1.
Introduction

Principal component anlysis (PCA) is a basic tool in dimension reduction. Due to explosion of data, command of efficient PCA algorithms is increasing. In this paper, we focus on the online PCA algorithm proposed by Oja in
[
14
]
, which is also named as the stochastic gradient ascent (SGA) method.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a mean zero random variable (R.V.). Let

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}]

be the covariance matrix. Traditional PCA algorithms diagonalize
\mathbf{A}
to derive principal eigenvectors (i.e. the principal components) of
\mathbf{A}
. However, due to limitation of storage and high dimension of data in recent fields such as deep learning, explicit form of the dense matrix
\mathbf{A}
may not be available. Therefore, practitioners prefer ’online’ algorithms: it only requires a limited amount of samples of
\mathbf{x}
in each iteration. To solve this problem, Oja proposed the following SGA method in
[
14
]
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w_{j}}(k)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w_{j}}(k-1)+\eta(k)\mathbf{x}^{T}(k)\mathbf{w_{j}}(k-1)[\mathbf{x}(k)-(\mathbf{x}^{T}(k)\mathbf{w_{j}}(k-1))\mathbf{w_{j}}(k-1)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\mathbf{x}^{T}(k)\mathbf{w_{i}}(k-1))\mathbf{w_{i}}(k-1)],\ j=1,\ 2,\ ...,\ p.

This algorithm iterates the first
p
principal components
\mathbf{w_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},j=1,2,...,p
. Here
\mathbf{x}(k),\ k=1,2,...
are independent samples of
\mathbf{x}
,
\eta(k),\ k=1,2,...
are learning rates.

Algorithm (
1.2
) determines a discrete time Markovian process, i.e.
\mathbf{W}(k)=[\mathbf{w_{1}}(k),\mathbf{w_{2}}(k),...,\mathbf{w_{p}}(k)]
. The main goal of this paper is to gain a good understanding of this random process (R.P.) from the view of semigroups, diffusion approximations and SDEs.

First of all, as
\eta\to 0
, replacing
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}
by
\mathbf{A}
in (
1.2
), we derive the corresponding ODE:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\mathbf{\dot{q_{1}}}&=\mathbf{Aq_{1}}-\mathbf{(q_{1}\cdot Aq_{1})q_{1}},\\
\mathbf{\dot{q_{j}}}&=\mathbf{Aq_{j}}-\mathbf{(q_{j}\cdot Aq_{j})q_{j}}-2\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\mathbf{(q_{i}\cdot Aq_{j})q_{i}},\ j=2,\ 3,\ ...,\ n.\\
\mathbf{q_{i}}(0)&=\mathbf{q_{i,0}},\ i=1,\ 2,\ ...,\ p.\end{split}\right.

Convergence properties including global convergence, stable manifolds and exponential convergence were thoroughly investigated in our previous work
[
10
]
. In particular, we proved that for almost every initial value
\mathbf{Q_{0}}\in O(n)
, the solution exponentially converges to the eigenbasis (up to a sign). Moreover, the eigenvectors are aligned in a descending order of the eigenvalues. See Theorem 5.2 in
[
10
]
. As far as we know, this is the first complete result providing global exponential convergence and closed formula for stable manifolds of a PCA flow
[
1
]
.

Given convergence of the corresponding ODE, we aim at proving similar result for the discrete algorithm (
1.2
) in this paper. We consider this problem in three steps: Viewing the SGA iteration as a semigroup, we construct proper diffusion approximations and prove convergence of diffusion approximations to ensure the performance of the algorithm.

First, we view the SGA method as a semigroup. It can be reformulated in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}(k+1)=\mathbf{W}(k)+\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k+1)\mathbf{x}^{T}(k+1),\mathbf{W}(k)).

Here
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is defined in (
2.1
). For arbitrary test function
\varphi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n\times p})
, define

(1.4)

\displaystyle S\varphi(\mathbf{W}):=\mathbb{E}\varphi(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})).

Under this notation, if the initial datum of the SGA method is
\mathbf{W_{0}}
, then the Markovian property yields

(1.5)

\displaystyle S^{k}\varphi(\mathbf{W}_{0})=\mathbf{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(\mathbf{W}(k)).

Thus, convergence of the SGA method can also be interpreted as the convergence of the semigroup
\{S^{k}\},k=1,2,...
.

Second, we construct appropriate diffusion approximations. Although the SGA method does not preserve
\mathbf{W}(k)
to stay on the Stiefel manifold
O(n\times p)
, the desired result, i.e. the eigenbasis, is in
O(n\times p)
. Thus, we aim at deriving a good diffusion approximation of the semigroup
S
and the SGA method. It should be an SDE that stays on the Stiefel manifold. The classical method to derive an SDE on a certain manifold is to project a Stratanovich SDE onto the desired manifold
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}\circ\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}).

Here
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{M}}
is the projection operator onto the tangent space at
\mathbf{W}
on
\mathcal{M}
. If the semigroup is close to the diffusion process, then by proving convergence of the diffusion process in some sense, we can also guarantee the performance of the algorithm.

Finally, we prove convergence of the diffusion process. The way to prove it is by seeking ’reversibility’. In fact, if the Fokker-Planck equation of the SDE can be recast in the following form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla U+\nabla\rho)=\nabla\cdot\left(e^{-U}\nabla\left(\dfrac{\rho}{e^{U}}\right)\right)

for some potential
U
, then the diffusion process satisfies detailed-balance condition, i.e., the process is reversible. Then, Poincare’s inequality can ensure the exponential convergence of
\rho
in a certain
L^{2}
sense. This proves the convergence of SDEs, which also finishes our analysis.

Under this framework of analysis, we will provide our main results and revise previous literature.

1.1.
Previous results and unsolves problems

One of the important features of (
1.2
) which other algorithms do not possess is its semi-decoupling feature: iteration of
\mathbf{w_{j}}
does not depend on
\mathbf{w_{i}},\ i>j
. This feature facilitates its implementation in neural networks
[
13
]
, thus researchers focus on it. This feature was also extended to the corresponding ODE, i.e. (
2.5
). Based on this semi-decoupling property, we also proved all convergence results of (
2.5
) in
[
10
]
.

However, a satisfying convergence result for (
1.2
) is still wanting. Since Oja and Karhunen proposed (
1.2
) in
[
14
]
, its convergence behavior has always been a focus in analysis of online PCA. Oja and Karhunen used stochastic approximation to derive almost sure convergence of (
1.2
) under an implicit condition on the distribution of
\mathbf{x}

[
14
,
12
,
13
]
. This implicit condition requires the iteration to visit a compact set containing the equilibrium for infinitely many times. However, this condition is difficult to verify in practice.

To improve Oja’s result, more recently, authors in
[
7
]
derived weak convergence of the first component of (
1.2
) to a multidimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Following
[
7
]
, the algorithm conducting full orthonormalization was considered and the weak convergence of all components was derived
[
9
]
.

The diffusion approximation of the first component of (
1.2
) was also considered in our previous work
[
3
]
in which both first -order and second-order approximation were derived. As a corollary, the weak convergence of the first component of the SGA method was verified.
However, a diffusion approximation of the whole SGA iteration method (
1.2
) is still an open problem.

The main tool utilized in
[
3
]
is the Lax equivalence theorem. An alternative stochastic analysis approach to prove the convergence is developed by Milstein
[
11
]
, which was adopted to derive diffusion approximations of the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) method
[
8
]
.

1.2.
Main results

First of all, we investigated properties of the semigroup
S^{k},\ k=1,2...,
. In particular, we proved the stability and the regularity of it. For the stability, we proved that for a fixed terminal time
T
, if
\|\mathbf{W_{0}}\|_{F}\leq r
, then there exist constants
C
and
\eta_{0}
that depend on
r,T
and the distribution of
\mathbf{x}
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{W}(k)\|_{F}\leq C

holds for all
k=1,2,...,\left[\dfrac{T}{\eta}\right]
. Here
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
is the learning rate in (
1.2
). See Lemma
3.1
. We proved the stability because it is necessary for the application of the Lax equivalence theorem. For the regularity, we prove that
S^{k}\varphi,\ k=1,2,...
admit the same order regularity as
\varphi
, i.e.

\displaystyle\|S^{k}\varphi\|_{C^{m}(B(\mathbf{0},r))}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{C^{m}(B(\mathbf{0},r^{\prime}))}.

Here
C
and
r^{\prime}
are constants that depend on
m,r,T
and the distribution of
\mathbf{x}
. See Theorem
3.1
for details.

Second, we constructed the desired diffusion approximations. We proved that the following family of SDEs

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W})+\sqrt{\eta}\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\dot{\mathbf{Z}},\ \mathbf{W}(0)=\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n)

will stay on the Stiefel manifold for all
t>0
. See Lemma
3.5
. Here

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}=(\dot{Z}_{ij})_{n\times n},\ \dot{Z}_{ij}(\mathbf{W})=H_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})\circ\dot{B}_{kl},

where
\mathbf{H}=(H_{ijkl})_{n\times n\times n\times n}
are coefficients and
\dot{B}_{kl}
is the white noise. See (
3.13
) for detail.

In fact, the SDE (
3.13
) serves as the first-order diffusion approximation of the SGA method. we proved that under proper regularity conditions of the test function
\varphi
, there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\mathbf{x},T,\eta)
such that

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbf{W}\in O(n),k\eta\leq T}|S^{k}\varphi(\mathbf{W})-u(\mathbf{W},k\eta)|\leq C_{1}(\mathbf{x},T,\varphi)\eta.

Here
u(\mathbf{W},t)
is the solution to the Kolmogorov equation determined by (
3.13
), with the initial value
\varphi
. See Theorem
3.2
. The main idea of the proof comes from the Lax equivalence theorem
[
6
]
: stability and consistence is equivalent to convergence. The consistence is ensured by Taylor’s expansion, see Section
6
for details.

A natural question is that whether higher- order approximation exists. Unfortunately, the answer is no. We proved that the possible second order approximation, which is an SDE, does not stay on the Stiefel manifold. See Lemma
3.6
. This instability is probably due to the omitted higher-order terms in the SGA algorithm: second and higher-order (w.r.t.
\eta
) terms were neglected in (
1.2
) when conducting the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization.

Finally, for two special cases, we proved the exponential convergence of the SDE. As we introduced before, we seek for reversibility to prove the exponential convergence.

First, we consider the overdamped Langevin equation on the Stiefel manifold:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}(t)=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}\circ(-\nabla U(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}(t)).

The exponential convergence of it is proved in Section
3
. If we select the potential
U
as the weighted Rayleigh quotient (see
[
10
]
) and let
\sigma=0
, then the Oja-Brockett flow
[
2
]
is recovered. We have to emphasize that the overdamped Langevin equation is not a special case of (
3.13
) since
\mathbf{G}
in (
1.2
) is not a gradient of a certain potential.

Second, for
n=2
of (
3.13
), the SDE is rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{F_{1}}(\mathbf{W})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta}\cdot c(\mathbf{W})\mathbf{W}\circ\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Z}.

Here
\mathbf{F_{1}}
is defined in (
2.5
) and
c(\mathbf{W})
is a scalar. See details in (
4.11
). Exponential convergence of this case is proved in Theorem
4.1
. The main approach is to consider the dynamics of the rotational angle of
\mathbf{W}\in O(2)
, which is a one-dimensional SDE, and the reversibility automatically holds.

2.
Premier

First, we rewrite (
1.2
) by matrices. For
\mathbf{\Lambda},\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{\Lambda},\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\mathbf{E_{j}}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda QE_{k}}-\mathbf{E_{k}}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda QE_{j}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{\Lambda},\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{\Lambda Q}-\mathbf{QQ}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{\Lambda},\mathbf{Q}).

Then (
1.2

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\mathbf{A}(k)&=\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}^{T}(k),\\
\mathbf{W}(k)&=\mathbf{W}(k-1)+\eta_{k}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A}(k),\mathbf{W}(k-1)).\end{split}\right.

In our previous paper
[
10
]

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\mathbf{\dot{Q}}&=\mathbf{Q}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}(\mathbf{E_{j}}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{AQE_{k}}-\mathbf{E_{k}}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{AQE_{j}}),\\
\mathbf{Q}(0)&=\mathbf{Q_{0}}\in O(n).\end{split}\right.

We define

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F_{1}(Q)}:=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Q}).

Thus one can rewrite (
2.3
) as

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\mathbf{\dot{Q}}&=\mathbf{F_{1}(Q)},\\
\mathbf{Q}(0)&=\mathbf{Q_{0}}\in O(n).\end{split}\right.

From now on, we will use (
2.5
) in all proofs.

3.
Diffusion approximation of the online PCA algorithm

In this section, we consider the iteration scheme (
1.2
).
We also assume that the learning rates are constant, i.e.
\eta_{k}=\eta>0,k=1,2,...
. Under these assumptions, we derived the diffusion approximation of (
1.2
). Our main results imply that (
2.5
) is the weak limit of (
1.2
) as
\eta
approaches 0. We also derived families of matrix-valued SDEs (invariant in the Steifel manifold) which serve as first order weak approximations. In particular, (
2.5
) is understood as a special case of these SDEs by taking the time step size
\eta=0
.

3.1.
The semigroup

The matrix-valued discrete time Markov process defined in (
1.2
), i.e.
\mathbf{W}(k),k=1,\ 2,\ ...
, is time homogeneous because
\mathbf{x}(k)
share the same distribution.

Following the notations in
[
3
]
, we denote the expectation under the distribution of this Markov chain starting from
\mathbf{W_{0}}
as
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}
. In our discussion,
\mathbf{W_{0}}
is assumed to be deterministic though it could be a random variable in general contexts. Denote the law of
\mathbf{W}(k)
(starting from
\mathbf{W_{0}}
) as
\mu^{k}(\cdot;\mathbf{W_{0}})
and the transition probability as
\mu(\mathbf{V},\cdot)
. Then by the Markov property, for any Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

\displaystyle\mu^{k+1}(E;\mathbf{W_{0}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\mu(\mathbf{V},E)\mu^{k}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V};\mathbf{W_{0}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\mu^{k}(E;\mathbf{U})\mu(\mathbf{W_{0}},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{U}).

For a fixed test function
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
, define

(3.1)

\displaystyle u^{k}(\mathbf{W_{0}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}\left[\varphi(\mathbf{W}(k))\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\varphi(\mathbf{V})\mu^{k}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V};\mathbf{W_{0}}),\ k=0,1,2,...

Here
\mathbf{W}(k)
is defined in (
1.2
). The Markov property yields

\displaystyle u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W_{0}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}[\varphi(\mathbf{W}(k+1))|\mathbf{W}(1)]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}[u^{k}(\mathbf{W}(1))]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W_{1}},\mathbf{W_{0}})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\varphi(\mathbf{V})\mu^{k}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V};\mathbf{W_{1}}).

Then by (
1.2
), we derive that for any
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbb{E}u^{k}(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})):=Su^{k}(\mathbf{W}),

hence
u^{0}(\mathbf{W})=\varphi(\mathbf{W})
and
\{S^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
forms a semigroup.

Before discussing the diffusion approximation, we derive some basic properties of the Markov chain and the semigroup.

Lemma 3.1
.

(stability)
Fix a real number
r>0
and a terminal time
T>0
. Let
\mathbf{W}(k),k=0,1,2,...
be the Markov chain generated by (
1.2
) with an initial datum
\mathbf{W_{0}}
satisfying
\|\mathbf{W_{0}}\|_{F}\leq r
. Then there exist constants
\eta(r,M,T)>0
and
C(r,M,T)>0
which depend on
r
,
T
and
M
in (
2.6
) such that for any
0<\eta\leq\eta(r,M,T)
and
k=0,1,2,...,\left[\dfrac{T}{\eta}\right]
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\mathbf{W}(k)\|_{F}^{2}\leq C(r,M,T)\right)=1,

i.e.,
\mathbf{W}(k)
is uniformly bounded for any time discretization with time step size less than
\eta(r,M,T)
.

See Section
6
for the proof of this lemma.
Based on Lemma
3.1
, we prove that

possesses the same regularity as the test function
\varphi
. Admissible sets of test functions are

(3.4)

\displaystyle C_{b}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}):=\left\{f\in C^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\ \big|\|f\|_{C^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}|D^{\alpha}f|_{\infty}\right\},\ m=1,2,....

We denote the open ball in
(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\|\cdot\|_{F})
centered at
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with radius

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{M},r):=\{\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\|\mathbf{N-M}\|_{F}<r\}.

Theorem 3.1
.

(properties of the semigroup) Fix a real number
r>0
and a terminal time
T>0
. Let
\{u^{k}\}_{k\geq 0},\ \{S^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

be the upper bound in (

(1)

(

(2)

(regularity) there exist constants

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|u^{k}\|_{C^{m}(B(\mathbf{0},r))}\leq C(r,m,M,T)\|\varphi\|_{C^{m}(B(\mathbf{0},e^{T(2M^{2}+1)/2}r))}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, for any
\eta\leq\eta(r,M,T)
, there exists
C(r,M,T)>0
such that
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}\leq C(r,M,T)

. Notice that
\mathbf{G}
is a polynomial w.r.t.
\mathbf{W}
, so there exists a constant
C^{\prime}(r,M,T)>0
such that for any
\mathbf{W}\in B(\mathbf{0},r)
and indices

and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right|\leq C^{\prime}(r,M,T).

Here
(\mathbf{G})_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
represents the entry at the
i^{\prime}
th row and

th column in the matrix

.

According to the proof of Lemma

, we know that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{W}(k)\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+(2M^{2}+1)\eta)\|\mathbf{W}(k-1)\|_{F}^{2},\ k=1,2,...,[T/\eta].

Thus if
\mathbf{W}\in B(\mathbf{0},r)
, then

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W})\in B(\mathbf{0},r\sqrt{1+(2M^{2}+1)\eta})\subset B\left(\mathbf{0},\left(1+\dfrac{(2M^{2}+1)\eta}{2}\right)r\right).

Denote
C^{\prime\prime}=(2M^{2}+1)/2
. Now we proceed to prove the theorem.

(1) Suppose that
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
, then the
L^{\infty}
contraction is derived directly by (
3.2
):

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|S\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}|\mathbb{E}\psi(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W}))|

\displaystyle\leq\sup\limits_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}|\psi(\mathbf{W})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}.

(2)
We prove the case
m=1
by induction. The proof for the case of
m\geq 1
is similar. Because
u^{0}=\varphi
, so the conclusion holds for
k=0
. Now for
k\geq 0
, by the dominant convergence theorem (DCT), we have for any
\mathbf{W}\in B(\mathbf{0},r)
and
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial u^{k+1}}{\partial w_{i,j}}(\mathbf{W})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))\cdot\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i,j}}(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))\right]

\displaystyle+\eta\sum_{1\leq i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))\cdot\dfrac{\partial(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right].

Remember that (
3.8
) holds, so

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i,j}}(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))\right]\right|\leq\left\|\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)r))}

Substituting this into (
3.10
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{\partial u^{k+1}}{\partial w_{i,j}}(\mathbf{W})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)r))}

\displaystyle+\eta C^{\prime}(r,M,T)\sum_{1\leq i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\leq n}\left|\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}(k)\mathbf{x}(k)^{T},\mathbf{W}))\right|.

Then using (
3.7
), taking
L^{\infty}
norm on both sides and summing up over
1\leq i,j\leq n
, we derive

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\left\|\dfrac{\partial u^{k+1}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{0},r))}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\left\|\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{i,j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)r))}

\displaystyle+n^{2}C^{\prime}(r,M,T)\eta\|u^{k}\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)r))}.

This inequality yields

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|u^{k+1}\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},r))}\leq(1+n^{2}C^{\prime}(r,M,T)\eta)\|u^{k}\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)r))}.

By induction, we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\|u^{k}\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},r))}

\displaystyle\leq(1+n^{2}C^{\prime}(r,M,T)\eta)^{k}\|u^{0}\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)^{k}r))}

\displaystyle\leq(1+n^{2}C^{\prime}(r,M,T)\eta)^{T/\eta}\|\varphi\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},(1+C^{\prime\prime}\eta)^{T/\eta}r))}

\displaystyle\leq e^{n^{2}C^{\prime}(r,M,T)T}\|\varphi\|_{C^{1}(B(\mathbf{0},e^{T(2M^{2}+1)/2}r))}.

∎

3.2.
The diffusion approximation

In this section, we discuss the diffusion approximation of the semigroup (
3.2
).

3.2.1.
SDEs on the Stiefel manifold

Because the SGA method aims to derive the correct unit eigenbasis, so we expect that our SDE should stay on the Stiefel manifold. Therefore, we consider the following family of SDEs:

(3.13)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W})+\sqrt{\eta}\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\dot{\mathbf{Z}},\ \mathbf{W}(0)=\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n).

Here
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}
is the projection onto the tangent space of
O(n)
at
\mathbf{W}
, see (
6.5
);
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}
is defined as

(3.14)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{W})=(\dot{Z}_{ij})_{n\times n},\ \dot{Z}_{ij}(\mathbf{W})=H_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})\circ\dot{B}_{kl};

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W})=(H_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W}))_{n\times n\times n\times n}
is the coefficient tensor of the Brownian motion;
\mathbf{B}=(B_{ij})_{n\times n}
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
; The notation ’
\circ
’ represents that (
3.13
) is an SDE in the Stratonovich sense.

By letting
\eta=0
in (
3.13
), the SDE degenerates to the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}),\ \mathbf{W}(0)=\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n).

This is exactly (
2.5
). Thus we expect that (
3.13
) serves as the diffusion approximation of the SGA method.

We first check that for arbitrary
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n\times n}
, (
3.13
) stays on the Stiefel manifold if
\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n)
. As preparation, we first rewrite the projection operator.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}
defined as in (
6.5
) for some
\mathbf{W}=(w_{ij})_{n\times n}\in O(n)
. Let

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}:=(P_{ijkl})_{n\times n\times n\times n},\ P_{ijkl}:=\dfrac{1}{2}(w_{is}w_{ks}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{il}).

Then we have:

(i)

(projection) for any
\mathbf{F}=(f_{ij})_{n\times n}
,

(3.16)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F})_{ij}=P_{ijkl}f_{kl}=\dfrac{1}{2}(f_{ij}-w_{il}f_{kl}w_{kj});

(ii)

(symmetry)
P_{ijkl}=P_{klij}
, i.e.
\mathbf{P}
is symmetric;

(iii)

(idempotence)
P_{ijkl}P_{klrs}=P_{ijrs}
, i.e.
\mathbf{P}^{2}=\mathbf{P}
.

Proof.

Because
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, so
w_{is}w_{ks}=\delta_{ik}
and

(3.17)

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{il}).

We first prove (i). Because
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{W})
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F})_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{2}(f_{ij}-w_{il}f_{kl}w_{kj}).

By (
3.17
), we derive

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}f_{kl}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{il})f_{kl}=\dfrac{1}{2}(f_{ij}-w_{il}f_{kl}w_{kj}).

Thus

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F})_{ij}=P_{ijkl}f_{kl}=\dfrac{1}{2}(f_{ij}-w_{il}f_{kl}w_{kj}).

(ii) is obvious:

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{il})=\dfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{ki}\delta_{lj}-w_{il}w_{kj})=P_{klij}.

For (iii), we have

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}P_{klrs}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{il})(\delta_{kr}\delta_{ls}-w_{rl}w_{ks})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}(\delta_{ir}\delta_{js}-w_{rj}w_{is}-w_{rj}w_{is}+w_{kj}w_{il}w_{rl}w_{ks}).

Because
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, we have

\displaystyle w_{il}w_{rl}=\delta_{ir},w_{kj}w_{ks}=\delta_{js}.

Thus

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}P_{klrs}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{ir}\delta_{js}-w_{rj}w_{is})=P_{ijrs}.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

We define
P_{ijkl}
as in (
3.15
) instead of (
3.17
), because in this case, even though
\mathbf{W}\notin O(n)
, the following equality still holds:

(3.18)

\displaystyle w_{ij}P_{tjkl}+w_{tj}P_{ijkl}=0.

This is helpful in proving invariance of Stiefel manifold, i.e. Lemma
3.3
.

Now we are ready to check that the Stiefel manifold is invariant for (
3.13
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Consider (
3.13
). If
\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n)
, then
\mathbf{W}(t)\in O(n)
holds for all
t>0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, we can rewrite (
3.13
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}w_{ij}=g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\mathrm{d}t+\eta P_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})f_{kl}(\mathbf{W})\mathrm{d}t+P_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})H_{klrs}(\mathbf{W})\circ\mathrm{d}B_{rs}.

Then for
1\leq i,t\leq n
, remember that the SDE is in the Stratonovich sense, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(w_{ij}w_{tj})

\displaystyle=w_{ij}\circ\mathrm{d}w_{tj}+w_{tj}\circ\mathrm{d}w_{ij}

\displaystyle=(w_{ij}g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+w_{tj}g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}))\mathrm{d}t+(w_{ij}P_{tjkl}+w_{tj}P_{ijkl})(f_{kl}\mathrm{d}t+H_{klrs}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{rs}).

Notice that

\displaystyle w_{ij}P_{tjkl}+w_{tj}P_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}[w_{ij}(w_{ts}w_{ks}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{tl})+w_{tj}(w_{is}w_{ks}\delta_{jl}-w_{kj}w_{il})]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}(w_{il}w_{ts}w_{ks}-w_{ij}w_{kj}w_{tl}+w_{is}w_{ks}w_{tl}-w_{il}w_{tj}w_{kj})=0.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(w_{ij}w_{tj})=(w_{ij}g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+w_{tj}g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}))\mathrm{d}t,

which is an ODE system. We can rewrite this in matrices as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T})}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{G}^{T}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\mathbf{W}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}-2\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}.

Thus
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}
satisfies the algebraic Riccati equation:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}-2\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X},

with initial value
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{I_{n}}
. Thus by results in
[
18
]
, we know that
\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{I_{n}}
holds. Therefore,
\mathbf{W}(t)\in O(n)
.
∎

3.2.2.
Fokker-Planck equation and Kolmogorov equation

In the Itô sense, (
3.13
) reads as

(3.19)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W})+\dfrac{\eta}{2}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{W})+\sqrt{\eta}\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\dot{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{W}),

Here
\mathbf{Y}
is defined as

(3.20)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{W})=(\dot{Y}_{ij})_{n\times n},\ \dot{Y}_{ij}(\mathbf{W})=H_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})\dot{B}_{kl}.

The correction term
\mathbf{J}
is then given by

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=(K_{ijkl})_{n\times n\times n\times n},\ K_{ijkl}=P_{ijrs}H_{rskl}

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}

\displaystyle=(J_{ij})_{n\times n},\ J_{ij}(\mathbf{W}):=K_{rsml}\dfrac{\partial K_{ijml}}{\partial w_{rs}}.

Then (
3.19
) could be rewritten as

\displaystyle\dot{w_{ij}}

\displaystyle=g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}+\sqrt{\eta}K_{ijkl}\dot{B_{kl}}

\displaystyle=g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}+\sqrt{\eta}P_{ijkl}H_{klrs}\dot{B_{rs}}

The corresponding backward Kolmogorov equation of (
3.19
) is

(3.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(\mathbf{W},t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}u(\mathbf{W},t),\ u(\mathbf{W},0)=\varphi(\mathbf{W}),

where
\mathcal{L}
is an elliptic operator defined as

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u

\displaystyle:=\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}\right)\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial w_{ij}}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial w_{ij}w_{kl}}

\displaystyle=\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}\right)\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial w_{ij}}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}P_{ijzv}H_{zvrs}P_{klxy}H_{xyrs}\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial w_{ij}w_{kl}}.

The solution of (
3.22
) is given by

(3.24)

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{W},t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}\varphi(\mathbf{W})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W}}[\varphi(\mathbf{X}(t))],

here
\mathbf{X}(t)\in O(n)
is the random process (before vectorization) determined by (
3.13
) (or equivalently (
3.19
)) with starting point
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
. For more details, we refer readers to
[
15
]
.

On the other hand, the solution of the forward Kolmogorov equation (the Fokker-Planck equation) is denoted as
\rho({\mathbf{W}},t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{W})
:

(3.25)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho,\ \rho({\mathbf{W}},t)=\rho_{0}({\mathbf{W}}),

where
\rho_{0}
is the initial distribution of
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is defined as

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho

\displaystyle:=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}\left[\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}\right)\rho\right]+\dfrac{\eta}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{ij}w_{kl}}\left(\rho K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}\right)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}\left[\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}\right)\rho\right]+\dfrac{\eta}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w_{ij}w_{kl}}\left(\rho P_{ijzv}H_{zvrs}P_{klxy}H_{xyrs}\right).

The solution
\rho(\mathbf{W},t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{W})
is interpreted as the probability distribution of the random process
\mathbf{X}
in (
3.13
) with the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
.

Notice that
\{e^{t\mathcal{L}}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}\}_{t\geq 0}
form two semigroups, we have the following basic properties:

Lemma 3.4
.

Consider
\{e^{t\mathcal{L}}\}_{t\geq 0}
in (
3.23
) and
\{e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}\}_{t\geq 0}
in (
3.26
). Then:

(1)

(probability and positivity preserving) if
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}(O(n))
and
\rho_{0}\geq 0
, then
e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}\rho_{0}\geq 0
and

(3.27)

\displaystyle\|e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}\rho\|_{L^{1}(O(n))}=\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}(O(n))}.

In particular,
e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}}
is a contraction from
L^{1}(O(n))
to
L^{1}(O(n))
.

(2)

(
L^{\infty}
contraction) Suppose that
\varphi:O(n)\to R
is a continuous function.
e^{t\mathcal{L}}:L^{\infty}(O(n))\to L^{\infty}(O(n))
is a contraction, i.e.

(3.28)

\displaystyle\|e^{t\mathcal{L}}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}.

Proof.

The proof of (1) can be found in
[
3
]
, here we just prove (2). Because
\varphi
is continuous and
O(n)
is compact, we have
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(O(n))
. Suppose
\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n)
, let
\mathbf{W}(t)
be the stochastic process generated by (
3.13
) starting from
\mathbf{W_{0}}
. Then we have

(3.29)

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}}\varphi(\mathbf{W_{0}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}\left[\varphi(\mathbf{W}(t))\right].

By Lemma
3.3
, we know that
\mathbf{W}(t)\in O(n)
for all
t\geq 0
, thus

(3.30)

\displaystyle|e^{t\mathcal{L}}\varphi(\mathbf{W_{0}})|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{W_{0}}}|\varphi(\mathbf{W}(t))|\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}.

This holds for any
\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n)
, thus (
3.28
) holds.
∎

More discussion on contraction and positivity preserving is available in
[
16
]
.

Now we are ready to verify that (
3.13
) serves as a weak diffusion approximation of the SGA iteration.

3.2.3.
First-order diffusion approximations

The method to validate that (
3.13
) serves as a diffusion approximation originates from the idea of the Lax equivalence theorem
[
6
]
, which was first adopted in
[
3
]
for the same purpose.

Theorem 3.2
.

(first-order diffusion approximation) Fix time
T>0
. Consider
\{u^{k}(\mathbf{W})\}_{k\geq 0}
defined in (
3.1
). Suppose that
\varphi\in C_{b}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
and
u(\mathbf{W},t)
is the solution to (
3.22
) with the initial value
u(\cdot,0)=\varphi(\cdot)
. Then there exist
\eta_{1}(M,T)>0
and
C_{1}(M,T,\varphi)>0
such that for any
\eta\in(0,\ \eta_{1}(M,T)]
,

(3.31)

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathbf{W}\in O(n),k\eta\leq T}|u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-u(\mathbf{W},k\eta)|\leq C_{1}(M,T,\varphi)\eta.

Here
M
is the constant in (
2.6
).

Proof.

In the following proof,
C
is a general constant that varies among equations.

By Theorem
3.1
, there exist constants
\eta(M,T)
and
C(M,T)
such that for all
k=0,1,2...,[T/\eta]
and
\eta\in(0,\ \eta(M,T)]
,

(3.32)

\displaystyle\|u^{k}\|_{C^{4}(B(0,\sqrt{n+1}))}\leq C(M,T)\|\varphi\|_{C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})}.

Then by Taylor’s expansion w.r.t.
\eta
, we have that for any
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
,

(3.33)

\displaystyle\left|u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})-u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-\eta g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{ij}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(M,T)\|u^{k}\|_{C^{4}(B(0,\sqrt{n+1}))}\eta^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(M,T,\varphi)\eta^{2}.

Details of this computation can be found in Section
6
.

By Lemma
3.4
,
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
is a contraction from
L^{\infty}(O(n))
to itself. Therefore for any
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, there exists
\eta^{\prime}\in(0,\eta]
such that

\displaystyle|e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-\eta\mathcal{L}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}|e^{\eta^{\prime}\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}^{2}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\|\mathcal{L}^{2}u^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}

\displaystyle\leq C(M,T,\|u^{k}\|_{C^{4}(B(0,\sqrt{n+1}))})\eta^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(M,T,\varphi)\eta^{2}.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{L}
in (
3.23
), we can rewrite the above estimate as

(3.34)

\displaystyle\left|e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-\eta g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial u^{k}}{\partial w_{ij}}\right|\leq C(M,T,\varphi)\eta^{2}

See Section
6
for details of this computation.

Thus by (
3.33
) and (
3.34
), we have

(3.35)

\displaystyle\|u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})-e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}\leq C(M,T,\varphi)\eta^{2}.

Now for
k=0,1,2...,[T/\eta]
, define

(3.36)

\displaystyle e_{k}:=\|u^{k}(\mathbf{W})-u(\mathbf{W},k\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))},\ r_{k}:=\|u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})-e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}.

Thus
r_{k}\leq C(M,T,\varphi)\eta^{2}
for
k=1,2,...,[T/\eta]
. Notice that
u(\mathbf{W},(k+1)\eta)=e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u(\mathbf{W},k\eta)
, we have

(3.37)

\displaystyle e_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\|u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})-u(\mathbf{W},(k+1)\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}

\displaystyle=\|u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})-e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u(\mathbf{W},k\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{k+1}(\mathbf{W})-e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}u^{k}(\mathbf{W})\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}+\|e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}(u(\mathbf{W},k\eta)-u^{k}(\mathbf{W}))\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}

\displaystyle\leq r_{k}+e_{k}.

The last step in (
3.37
) used that
e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}
is a contraction on
L^{\infty}(O(n))
. Thus summing up (
3.37
) in
k
yields

(3.38)

\displaystyle e_{k}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}r_{l}\leq\dfrac{TC^{\prime\prime}(M,T,\varphi)}{\eta}\cdot\eta^{2}=C_{1}(M,T,\varphi)\eta.

This holds uniformly in
k
, so the claim is proven.
∎

3.2.4.
Unstable second order approximation

According to the proof of Theorem
3.2
, the key step that ensures first order approximation is the following estimate on the truncation error, which is of second order:

\displaystyle\|S\varphi(\mathbf{W})-e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}\varphi(\mathbf{W})\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}\leq C\eta^{2}.

Here
\mathcal{L}
is defined in (
3.23
).

To derive second order approximation, we need to carefully select the drift term
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{H}
in (
3.13
) such that

(3.39)

\displaystyle\|S\varphi(\mathbf{W})-e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}\varphi(\mathbf{W})\|_{L^{\infty}(O(n))}\leq C\eta^{3}.

Direct calculation yields

\displaystyle S\varphi(\mathbf{W})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(\mathbf{W}+\eta\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W}))\right]

\displaystyle=\varphi(\mathbf{W})+\eta g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}}+\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{ij}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})g_{kl}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})\right]\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}\partial w_{kl}}+O(\eta^{3}).

Meanwhile,

\displaystyle e^{\eta\mathcal{L}}\varphi(\mathbf{W})=\varphi(\mathbf{W})+\eta\mathcal{L}\varphi(\mathbf{W})+\dfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{W})+O(\eta^{3}).

By (
3.23
), we have

(3.40)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}^{2}\varphi&=\mathcal{L}\left[g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}}+O(\eta)\right]\\
&=g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}}\right)+O(\eta)\\
&=\left(g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})}{\partial w_{kl}}\right)\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}}+g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}\partial w_{kl}}+O(\eta).\end{aligned}

Comparing the coefficients of
\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}\partial w_{kl}}
and
\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w_{ij}}
, we derive

(3.41)

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}f_{kl}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}J_{ij}-\dfrac{1}{2}g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})}{\partial w_{kl}},

\displaystyle P_{ijuv}H_{uvrs}P_{klxy}H_{xyrs}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[g_{ij}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})g_{kl}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})].

Here
\mathbf{F}=(f_{ij})_{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{H}=(H_{ijkl})_{n\times n\times n\times n}
are coefficients in (
3.13
). Details of the derivation of (
3.41
) is summarized in Section
6
.

To interpret (
3.41
), one can see that the R.H.S. of the second equation in (
3.41
) is exactly the covariance tensor of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})
. We denote it as

(3.42)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{W})=(M_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W}))_{n\times n\times n\times n},\ M_{ijkl}=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{ij}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}-\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})g_{kl}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}-\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\right].

Therefore, we desire suitable
\mathbf{H}
such that

(3.43)

\displaystyle P_{ijuv}H_{uvrs}P_{klxy}H_{xyrs}=M_{ijkl}.

This can be realized if
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, which is sufficient for our purpose.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider
\mathbf{M}
in (
3.42
). Then there exists a unique
\mathbf{N}=(N_{ijkl})_{n\times n\times n\times n}
that satisfy

(i)

(symmetry)
N_{ijkl}=N_{klij}
;

(ii)

(positive semidefinite) for any
(m_{ij})_{n\times n}
,
m_{ij}N_{ijkl}m_{kl}\geq 0;

(iii)

(square root of
\mathbf{M}
)
N_{ijrs}N_{klrs}=M_{ijkl}
.

Moreover, if
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, then

(3.44)

\displaystyle P_{ijrs}N_{rskl}=N_{ijkl},

i.e.
\mathbf{PN}=\mathbf{N}
.

See Section
6
for the proof of this lemma. In the view of Lemma
3.5
, we also denote
\mathbf{N}
as

(3.45)

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}=\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}.

If we take

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{N}=\sqrt{\mathbf{M}},

in (
3.13
), then

\displaystyle P_{ijuv}H_{uvrs}P_{klxy}H_{xyrs}=P_{ijuv}N_{uvrs}P_{klxy}N_{xyrs}=N_{ijrs}N_{klrs}=M_{ijkl}

holds if
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
. Thus (
3.43
) is satisfied.

Now we take

(3.46)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0},\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{N}=\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}

in (
3.13
). By Lemma
3.5
, we know that (
3.13
) stays on
O(n)
, so (
3.13
) could be rewritten as

(3.47)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}w_{ij}

\displaystyle=g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta}P_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})N_{klrs}(\mathbf{W})\circ\mathrm{d}B_{rs},

\displaystyle=g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta}N_{ijrs}(\mathbf{W})\circ\mathrm{d}B_{rs}.

This SDE seems to serve as the second order approximation for the SGA method: we have
g_{ij}
in (
1.2
) as the drift term; covariance of
g_{ij}
is also reflected in the Brownian motion. However, because
\mathbf{F}
in (
3.46
) does not satisfy (
3.41
), (
3.47
) is not a second order approximation, but a first order approximation by Theorem
3.2
.

Moreover, there is even no solution to the first equation of (
3.41
). This excludes the possibility of deriving a second order approximation on the Stiefel manifold.

Even with the last try, we require
\mathbf{H}
satisfy (
3.41
) and just replace
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{F}
in (
3.13
) by

(3.48)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=(L_{ij})_{n\times n},\ L_{ij}:=-\dfrac{1}{2}J_{ij}-\dfrac{1}{2}g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})}{\partial w_{kl}},

then resulted SDE still does not stay on the Stiefel manifold. Therefore, we have no second order diffusion approximation that stays on the Stiefel manifold.

Lemma 3.6
.

(unstable second order approximation) Consider (
3.41
),
\mathbf{L}
in (
3.48
), and
\mathbf{J}=(J_{ij})_{n\times n}
in (
3.21
). Then

(i)

Suppose that
\mathbf{H}
satisfies (
3.41
). Then there is no
\mathbf{F}=(f_{ij})_{n\times n}
that satisfies

\displaystyle P_{ijkl}f_{kl}=L_{ij},

i.e. the first equation of (
3.41
) admits no solution.

(ii)

Consider the solution
\mathbf{W}(t)
to the following SDE:

(3.49)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}w_{ij}=\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta L_{ij}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta}N_{ijrs}(\mathbf{W})\circ\mathrm{d}B_{rs},\ \mathbf{W}(0)=\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n).

Then there is no time interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
such that
\mathbf{W}(t)\in O(n)
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Here
\mathbf{N}=(N_{ijkl})_{n\times n\times n\times n}
is the square root of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{M}
(see Lemma
3.5
).

Proof.

We first prove (i) by contradiction. Suppose there is a solution
\mathbf{F}
, then by (
3.18
),

\displaystyle w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij}=w_{ij}P_{tjkl}f_{kl}+w_{tj}P_{ijkl}f_{kl}=0.

Notice

\displaystyle-2(w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij})=\underbrace{w_{ij}J_{tj}+w_{tj}J_{lj}}_{\text{I}}+\underbrace{g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\left(w_{ij}\dfrac{\partial g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})}{\partial w_{kl}}+w_{tj}\dfrac{\partial g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})}{\partial w_{kl}}\right)}_{\text{II}}.

By definition of
\mathbf{J},\mathbf{K}
in (
3.21
), we have

\displaystyle\text{I}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{rs}}\left(\left(w_{ij}P_{tjuv}+w_{tj}P_{ijuv}\right)H_{uvml}K_{rsml}\right)-K_{tjml}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{rs}}(w_{ij}K_{rsml})-K_{ijml}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{rs}}(w_{tj}K_{rsml}).

By (
3.18
), the first term of I is zero, so we have

I

\displaystyle=-K_{tjml}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{rs}}(w_{ij}K_{rsml})-K_{ijml}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{rs}}(w_{tj}K_{rsml})

\displaystyle=-2K_{tjml}K_{ijml}-w_{ij}P_{tjuv}H_{uvml}K_{rsml}-w_{tj}P_{ijuv}H_{uvml}K_{rsml}

\displaystyle=-2K_{tjml}K_{ijml}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}[g_{tj}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})g_{ij}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})].

For II, we have

II

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}[(w_{ij}g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+w_{tj}g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}))g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ -g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}(w_{ij}g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}))-g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}(w_{tj}g_{kl}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}))

\displaystyle=-2g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+g_{kl}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}(g_{tj}w_{ij}+g_{ij}w_{tj}).

Thus

\displaystyle-2(w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij})

\displaystyle=\text{I}+\text{II}

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}[g_{tj}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})g_{ij}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})]-2g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +g_{kl}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}(g_{tj}w_{ij}+g_{ij}w_{tj})

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}[g_{tj}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})g_{ij}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{W})]+g_{kl}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}(g_{tj}w_{ij}+g_{ij}w_{tj}).

This can not be zero for general R.V.
\mathbf{x}
, because the first term depends on the fourth-order momentum while the second term only depends on the second-order momentum. This is a contradiction, so

w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij}\neq 0
and (
3.41
) admits no solution.

For (ii), we still prove by contradiction. Suppose that for some time interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
,
\mathbf{W}(t)\in O(n)
. Then

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T})}{\mathrm{d}t}=0,\ t\in(t_{0},t_{1}).

By Lemma
3.5
, we know that if
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, then
P_{ijrs}N_{rskl}=N_{ijkl}
. So we can rewrite (
3.49
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}w_{ij}=\left(g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta L_{ij}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta}P_{ijrs}N_{rskl}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{kl}

However,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(w_{ij}w_{tj})

\displaystyle=w_{ij}\mathrm{d}w_{tj}+w_{tj}\mathrm{d}w_{ij}

\displaystyle=(w_{ij}g_{tj}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+w_{tj}g_{ij}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}))\mathrm{d}t+\eta(w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij})\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\sqrt{\eta}(w_{ij}P_{tjrs}+w_{tj}P_{ijrs})N_{rskl}\circ B_{kl}.

Remember that if
\mathbf{W}\in O(n)
, then
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n)}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})
. So
g_{ij}=P_{ijkl}g_{kl}
, which yields that for all
t\in(t_{0},t_{1})
,

\displaystyle w_{ij}g_{tj}+w_{tj}g_{ij}=(w_{ij}P_{tjrs}+w_{tj}P_{ijrs})g_{rs}=0

by (
3.18
). Due to the same reason,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\eta}(w_{ij}P_{tjrs}+w_{tj}P_{ijrs})N_{rskl}\circ B_{kl}=0.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(w_{ij}w_{tj})=\eta(w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij})\mathrm{d}t.

However,
(w_{ij}L_{tj}+w_{tj}L_{ij})\neq 0
by (i). Thus
\mathrm{d}(w_{ij}w_{tj})\neq 0
for
t\in(t_{0},t_{1})
, which contradicts with

\displaystyle w_{ij}w_{tj}=\delta_{it}.

This is a contradiction, the proof is finished.
∎

In the view of Lemma
3.6
, (
3.49
) fails to stay on the Stiefel manifold. In this case, (
3.22
) is a degenerate parabolic PDE with unbounded coefficients, which fails to control the diffusion in the normal direction of the Stiefel manifold. Thus, utilizing solutions of (
3.22
) to approximate the behavior of the semigroup (
3.2
) is meaningless.

4.
Reversible diffusion approximation: exponential convergence

In Theorem
3.2
, we derived diffusion approximations on the Stiefel manifold. A natural question is that whether the SDE is ergodic and converges.

In fact, reversibility and Poincare’s inequality ensure exponential convergence. To see this, the Fokker-Planck operator (
3.26
) can be recast as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}\left[\rho\left(-g_{ij}-\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}\dfrac{\partial\log\rho}{\partial w_{kl}}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}J_{ij}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}\left[\dfrac{\eta}{2}K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}\rho\left(\dfrac{\eta J_{ij}-2g_{ij}-2\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}}{\eta K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}}+\dfrac{\partial\log\rho}{\partial w_{kl}}\right)\right].

If there exists a function
U(\mathbf{W})
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial w_{kl}}=\dfrac{\eta J_{ij}-2g_{ij}-2\eta P_{ijkl}f_{kl}}{\eta K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}},

then solutions to
\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}\left[\dfrac{\eta}{2}K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}\rho\dfrac{\partial(\log\rho+U)}{\partial w_{kl}}\right]=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}\left[\dfrac{\eta}{2}e^{-U}K_{ijrs}K_{klrs}\dfrac{\partial(e^{U}\rho)}{\partial w_{kl}}\right].

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\int_{O(n)}e^{-U(\mathbf{W})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}=1
. Then
multiply
e^{U}\rho-1
on both sides, and by definition of
\mathbf{K}
in (
3.21
), we derive

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{O(n)}e^{-U}|e^{U}\rho-1|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=-\eta\int_{O(n)}e^{-U}\left(K_{ijrs}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}}(e^{U}\rho-1)\right)\left(K_{klrs}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_{kl}}(e^{U}\rho-1)\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=-\eta\int_{O(n)}e^{-U}|\mathbf{H}^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}(e^{U}\rho-1)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}.

Here
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}
is the gradient operator on
(O(n),g_{e})
where
g_{e}
is the Euclidean metric. Then by Poincare’s inequality, we derive exponential convergence.

Therefore, we desire to carefully select
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{F}
such that the potential condition (
4.1
) is satisfied. We provide the following two special cases where reversibility is ensured.

First, we consider the overdamped Langevin on
O(n)
. In particular, we select the potential as

U(\mathbf{W};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{N})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{N}\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W})
, where
\mathbf{N}
is a diagonal matrix. Then we recover the Oja-Brockett flow with Bronwian motion. In fact, the Oja-Brockett flow is the gradient flow of
U(\mathbf{W};\mathbf{A},\mathbf{N})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{N}\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W})
on
(O(n),g_{e})
.

Second, we consider the two-dimensional case, i.e.
n=2
. In this case, orthogonal matrices are determined by the rotational angle. The SDE of the angle is an SDE on
\mathbb{R}
, which automatically satisfy the potential condition.

In each case, Poincare’s inequality is verified, so they converge to the invariant measure exponentially fast.

4.1.
The overdamped Langevin dynamics on
O(n)

Suppose that
U(\mathbf{Q}):O(n)\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function (which serves as the free energy), consider the overdamped Langevin dynamics on
O(n)
:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}(t)=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}\circ(-\nabla U(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}(t)).

Here
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}
is the projection onto
T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)
,
\nabla
represents the derivative w.r.t.
\mathbf{Q}
, i.e.

\displaystyle(\nabla U(\mathbf{Q}))_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial u(\mathbf{Q})}{\partial q_{ij}}.

\mathbf{W}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the standard Brownian motion and
\sigma
is a constant. ’
\circ
’ means that the above SDE is in the Stratonovich sense. In general,
\sigma
can be a matrix that depends on
\mathbf{Q}
. To illustrate the the Langevin dynamics on
O(n)
, we consider the simplest case here which is sufficient for diffusion approximation.

If we take
U(\mathbf{Q})=-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{N}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{AQ})
where
\mathbf{N}
is a diagonal matrix with entries on the diagonal line aligned in the descending order, then (
4.2
) reads as

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}(t)=(\mathbf{AQN}-\mathbf{QNQ}^{T}\mathbf{AQ})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}\circ\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}(t).

Then (
4.3
) should be viewed as the disturbed Oja-Brockett flow
[
2
]
.

By the conversion rule, (
4.2
) should be formulated in Ito’s sense as

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}(t)=\left[-\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}\nabla U(\mathbf{Q})-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}(n-1)}{4}\mathbf{Q}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}(t).

By Ito’s formula, we can derive the Fokker-Planck equation of (
4.2
) which is

(4.5)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)}{\partial t}=\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot(\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}U(\mathbf{Q}))+\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{\mathbf{Q}}\rho(\mathbf{Q},t).

Here
\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot,\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\Delta_{\mathbf{Q}}
are the divergence, gradient and Laplace-Beltrami operator on
(O(n),\ g_{e})
respectively. See Section
6
for more details.

Compactness of
O(n)
implies exponential convergence of (
4.5
). Direct calculation yields that the invariant measure of (
4.5
) is given by

(4.6)

\displaystyle\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q}):=\dfrac{1}{Z}e^{-2U(\mathbf{Q})/\sigma^{2}},\ Z:=\int_{O(n)}e^{-2U(\mathbf{Q})/\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{V}},

where
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}
is the volume form on
(SO(n),g_{e})
. Equation (
4.5
) can be reformulated as

(4.7)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)}{\partial t}=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot\left(\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right)\right):=\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho(\mathbf{Q},t),

and we denote the Fokker-Planck operator as
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. This also implies that the invariant measure of (
4.5
) is unique since

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho=0

\displaystyle\iff\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot\left(\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right)\right)=0

\displaystyle\iff\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\left|\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}{V}=0

\displaystyle\iff\rho(\mathbf{Q})=c\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q}),

where
c
is a constant. If
\rho(\mathbf{Q})
is a probability measure on
SO(n)
, then
c=1
and
\rho=\rho_{eq}
. In the above induction we used the positivity of
\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})
, which is a consequence of the continuity of
U(\mathbf{Q})
and the compactness of
SO(n)
.

Multiplying
\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)/\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})-1
on both sides of (
4.5
) results in

(4.8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\left|\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)-\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}=-{\sigma^{2}}\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\left|\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)-\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}.

If we can prove the Poincare inequality in
L^{2}(\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
, i.e., there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
f\in H^{1}(\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
satisfying
\int_{SO(n)}f(\mathbf{Q})\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}=0
, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\left|{\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\left(f(\mathbf{Q})\right)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}\geq C\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})|f(\mathbf{Q})|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V},

then (
4.8
) yields

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\left|\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)-\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}\leq-C\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\left|\dfrac{\rho(\mathbf{Q},t)-\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}{\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}.

By Gronwall’s inequality, this gives exponential convergence of (
4.5
) whose initial value is a probability measure.

Now we prove the Poincare inequality in
L^{2}(\rho_{eq}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

(Poincare’s inequality) Suppose that
f\in H^{1}(\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
satisfying
\int_{O(n)}f(\mathbf{Q})\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}=0
. Then (
4.9
) holds.

Proof.

To prove this, we prove that for any
\lambda>0
,
(\lambda-\mathcal{L}^{*})^{-1}:\ L^{2}\left(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}/\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\right)\to L^{2}\left(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}/\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q})\right)
is a compact operator. Suppose that
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are two sequences in
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}/\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q}))
such that

\displaystyle(\lambda-\mathcal{L}^{*})u_{n}=g_{n},\ n\geq 1,

and
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}/\rho_{eq}(\mathbf{Q}))
. Then multiplying
u_{n}
on both sides yields

\displaystyle\int_{O(n)}\dfrac{1}{\rho_{eq}}\cdot u_{n}\cdot(\lambda-\mathcal{L}^{*})u_{n}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{O(n)}\rho_{eq}\left|\nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\dfrac{u_{n}}{\rho_{eq}}\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}+\lambda\int_{SO(n)}\rho_{eq}\left|\dfrac{u_{n}}{\rho_{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{O(n)}\dfrac{1}{\rho_{eq}}\cdot g_{n}u_{n}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}.

By Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality, we know that
u_{n}/\rho_{eq}
uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(\rho_{eq}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
. The compact embedding
H^{1}(\rho_{eq}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\rho_{eq}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
(see
[
17
]
) implies that up to subsequences, there exists
u^{*}\in L^{2}(\rho_{eq}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{V})
,

5.
The case of
p<n

All results for the case of
p=n
can be extended to the case of
p<n
, by being careful on the size of tensors and rewrite the projection operator.

First, all regularity and stability results for the semigroup still hold. The proof is exactly the same as in Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
3.1
, by replacing the terminal index
n
by
p
for column indices.

Second, the diffusion approximation is now formulated as

(5.1)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W})+\eta\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{W})+\sqrt{\eta}\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}\dot{\mathbf{Z}},\ \mathbf{W}(0)=\mathbf{W_{0}}\in O(n\times p).

Here
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}
is the projection onto the tangent space of
O(n\times p)
at
\mathbf{W}
, see (
5.3
);
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}
is defined as

(5.2)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{W})=(\dot{Z}_{ij})_{n\times p},\ \dot{Z}_{ij}(\mathbf{W})=H_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W})\circ\dot{B}_{kl};

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W})=(H_{ijkl}(\mathbf{W}))_{n\times p\times n\times p}
is the coefficient tensor of the Brownian motion;
\mathbf{B}=(B_{ij})_{n\times p}
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
; The notation ’
\circ
’ represents that (
5.1
) is an SDE in the Stratonovich sense.

The projection operator onto
T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)
then reads as

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{I_{n}}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T})\mathbf{M}+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{W}).

When
p=n
,
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}=\mathbf{I_{n}}
so
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}
is exactly the projection on
O(n)
; when
p=1
,
\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{W}
are all scalars, then

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{I_{n}}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T})\mathbf{M},

i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{W}}O(n\times p)}
degenerates to the projection onto the unit sphere.

Using the same technique and method as in Lemma
3.3
, one can prove that (
5.1
) stays on
O(n\times p)
; moreover, the diffusion approximation is also of first-order as in Theorem
3.2
.

For reversibility, the overdamped Langevin is still reversible. All the proof in Section
4.1
holds generally on
O(n\times p)
.

6.1.
Riemannian manifolds and the Stiefel manifold

We denote the tangent space at
\mathbf{m}
on manifold
\mathcal{M}
as
T_{\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{M}
, the tangent vector field on
\mathcal{M}
as
\Gamma(T\mathcal{M})
. The tangent bundle (the disjoint union of the tangent spaces) is denoted as
T\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 6.1
.

(Riemannian manifolds)
Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth manifold. A Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
is a smooth mainfold equipped with an inner product
g_{\mathbf{m}}
on
T_{\mathbf{m}}\mathcal{M}
at each
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}
. Moreover, for any tangent vector field
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
and
\dot{\mathbf{y}}
, the function

(6.1)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{m}),\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{m})\rangle_{g_{\mathbf{m}}}:\ \mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}

is smooth.

Given a Riemannian metric
g
on
M
, the gradient of a smooth function
\mathcal{E}
on
\mathcal{M}
is defined as

Definition 6.2
.

(the gradient on the Riemannian manifold) A tangent vector field
\nabla_{g}\mathcal{E}
on
M
is called the gradient of
\mathcal{E}
w.r.t. the metric
g
if for every tangent vector field
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
on
M
,

(6.2)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\ \dot{\mathbf{x}}\rangle_{F}=\langle\nabla_{g}\mathcal{E},\ \dot{\mathbf{x}}\rangle_{g}.

Here
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is the derivative of
\mathcal{E}
, which is a cotangent vector field.

Now we consider the Stiefel manifold with the Euclidean metric
g_{e}
, under the global coordinate
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We first introduce several important properties of
O(n)
. See
[
4
]
for more details.

Lemma 6.1
.

(the Stiefel manifold)
The Stiefel manifold
O(n)
is a smooth, compact manifold of dimension
n(n-1)/2
. The tangent space at
\mathbf{Q}
is given by

(6.3)

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)=\{\mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\ |\ \bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\ \bm{\Omega}+\bm{\Omega}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\},

while the normal space at
\mathbf{Q}
is given by

(6.4)

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\ |\ \bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\ \bm{\Omega}=\bm{\Omega}^{T}\}.

See
[
4
]
for proof of Lemma
6.1
. By Lemma
6.1
, we can prove that for any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the projection on the tangent spaces and the normal spaces are respectively:

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}\mathbf{M}:=\dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{Q}),\ \mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)^{\perp}}\mathbf{M}:=\dfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{Q}).

Lemma 6.2
.

(Gradient on
(O(n),g_{e})
) Suppose that
\varphi(\mathbf{Q}):O(n)\to\mathbb{R}
is a restriction of a smooth function (still denoted as
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
) on
O(n)
. Then gradient of
\varphi
w.r.t.
g_{e}
at point
\mathbf{Q}
is given by

(6.6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{g_{e}}\varphi:=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}(\nabla\varphi)=\dfrac{1}{2}(\nabla\varphi-\mathbf{Q}(\nabla\varphi)^{T}\mathbf{Q}),

here
\nabla\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the gradient of
\varphi
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, i.e.
(\nabla\varphi)_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}}
.

Proof.

We just need to prove that for any
\mathbf{Q}\in O(n)
and any tangent vector at
\mathbf{Q}
, (
6.2
) holds. By Lemma
6.1
, a tangent vector at
\mathbf{Q}
can be represented as
\mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}
where
\bm{\Omega}
is skew-symmetric. Therefore, for any
\bm{\Omega}
that is skew-symmetric, we have

(6.7)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\varphi,\mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}=\langle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}(\nabla\varphi),\ \mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}+\langle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)^{\perp}}(\nabla\varphi),\ \mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)^{\perp}}(\nabla\varphi),\ \mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\nabla\varphi+(\nabla\varphi)^{T}\mathbf{Q}),\ \mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{Q}^{T}\nabla\varphi+(\nabla\varphi)^{T}\mathbf{Q},\ \bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle=0,

since
\langle\mathbf{M},\ \mathbf{N}\rangle_{F}=0
if
\mathbf{M}
is symmetric while
\mathbf{N}
is skew-symmetric. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\varphi,\mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}=\langle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}(\nabla\varphi),\ \mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{F}=\langle\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}(\nabla\varphi),\ \mathbf{Q}\bm{\Omega}\rangle_{g_{e}}.

So
\nabla_{g_{e}}\varphi=\mathcal{P}_{T_{\mathbf{Q}}O(n)}(\nabla\varphi)
.
∎

Under this global coordinate, the divergence on
(O(n),g_{e})
can also be explicitly computed. The
ij
entry of
\nabla_{g_{e}}\varphi
is given by

(6.8)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{g_{e}}\varphi)_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}}-\sum_{k,l}q_{ik}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{lk}}\right).

So given a tangent vector field
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{Q})=(h_{ij}(\mathbf{Q}))
, the divergence of it is defined by

(6.9)

\displaystyle\nabla_{g_{e}}\cdot\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{Q}):=\sum_{i,j}(\nabla_{g_{e}}(h_{ij}(\mathbf{Q})))_{ij}.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator is then defined as:

(6.10)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{e}}\varphi:=\nabla_{g_{e}}\cdot\nabla_{g_{e}}\varphi.

Explicit expression of the Laplace-Beltrami operator is given by the following lemma:

Lemma 6.3
.

(Laplace-Beltrami operator) The Laplace-Beltrami operator on
(O(n),g_{e})
is given by

(6.11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{e}}\varphi=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i,j}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial{q_{ij}^{2}}}-(n-1)\sum_{i,j}q_{ij}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}}-\sum_{i,j,k,l}q_{ik}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}\partial q_{lk}}\right).

Proof.

By the expression of the divergence and gradient, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{g_{e}}\varphi=\sum_{i,j}\left(\dfrac{\partial h_{ij}}{\partial q_{ij}}-\sum_{k,l}q_{ik}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial h_{ij}}{\partial q_{lk}}\right),\ h_{ij}=\sum_{i,j}\left(\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}}-\sum_{k,l}q_{ik}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{lk}}\right).

Thus

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial h_{ij}}{\partial q_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}^{2}}-\sum_{k,l}\delta_{kl}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{lk}}-\sum_{k,l}\delta_{il}q_{ik}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{lk}}-\sum_{k,l}q_{ik}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}q_{lk}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}^{2}}-\sum_{l}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{lj}}-\sum_{k}q_{ik}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{ik}}-\sum_{k,l}q_{ik}q_{lj}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}q_{lk}}\right),

and

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial h_{ij}}{\partial q_{lk}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{ij}\partial q_{lk}}-\sum_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\delta_{il}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}q_{l^{\prime}j}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}}-\sum_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{kj}q_{ik^{\prime}}\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial q_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}}-\sum_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}q_{ik^{\prime}}q_{l^{\prime}j}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial q_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\partial q_{lk}}\right).

Substituting the above formulas into the Laplacian operator, we can derive (
6.11
).
∎

\displaystyle=\left((\mathbb{E}[x_{2}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[x_{1}^{2}])w_{1,1}w_{1,2}+\dfrac{\eta}{4}w_{1,2}^{2}\dfrac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial w_{1,1}}-\dfrac{\eta}{4}w_{1,1}w_{1,2}\dfrac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial w_{1,2}}-\dfrac{\eta}{2}w_{1,1}c(\mathbf{W})^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\eta}c(\mathbf{W})w_{1,2}\mathrm{d}B.

\theta

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\theta=f(\theta)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta}\cdot g(\theta)\mathrm{d}B.

Then Ito’s isometry yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\cos\theta

Replacing
w_{1,1}

\sin\theta
in (

\displaystyle f(\theta)

\displaystyle+\eta\cdot\dfrac{3c_{3}(\theta)}{2}\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

In the following proof,
C
is just a general constant that may vary among equations. Because
\rho(x,t),\ t\geq 0
is the law of
\theta(t)
, so
\rho
solves (
4.22
) on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Thus by the periodic boundary condition,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{d}x=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left[-\dfrac{\partial(f(x)\rho(x,t))}{\partial x}+\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\partial^{2}(\eta g^{2}(x)\rho(x,t))}{\partial x^{2}}\right]\mathrm{d}x=0.

So
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho(x,t)\mathrm{d}x=1,\ t\geq 0
.

Now consider the Fokker-Planck operator
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}
which is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1}:D(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty}),\ \mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}p:=-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\rho_{\infty}(x)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\dfrac{p(x)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)\right).

Here
D(\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1})=H^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\mu)
. A direct calculation yields

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}1^{*}p,q\rangle_{\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho_{\infty}(x)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\dfrac{p(x)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\dfrac{q(x)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)\mathrm{d}x,

thus
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}
is semi-positive definite, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}p=0\iff p(x)=c\rho_{\infty}(x).

So
0
is the simple principle eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}
with
\rho_{\infty}(x)
as the eigenvector.

Moreover,
0
is isolated. For any
\lambda>0
, we prove that
(\lambda+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*})^{-1}
is compact. Let
\{g_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})
be a bounded sequence, with

\displaystyle(\lambda+\mathcal{L}^{*})u_{n}=g_{n},n=1,2,....

To prove that
(\lambda+\mathcal{L}^{*})^{-1}
is compact, we just need to prove that there exists a subsequence of
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
which is Cauchy in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})
. Because
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is semi-positive definite, so
(\lambda+\mathcal{L}^{*})
is bounded, thus
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})
. By the Cauchy-Schwatz inequality, we have

(6.35)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}u_{n},u_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty}}=\langle u_{n},g_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty}}-\lambda\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})}^{2}\leq C.

Here
C
is a constant. Thus

\displaystyle\|u_{n}/\rho_{\infty}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T},\rho_{\infty}\mathrm{d}x)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho_{\infty}(x)\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\dfrac{u_{n}(x)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho_{\infty}(x)\left(\dfrac{u_{n}(x)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})}+\langle\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}u_{n},u_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty}}\leq C.

So
u_{n}/\rho_{\infty}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{T},\rho_{\infty}\mathrm{d}x)
. By the compact embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{T},\rho_{\infty}\mathrm{d}x)\subset\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\rho_{\infty}\mathrm{d}x)
, we know that there exists a subsequence of
u_{n}
(still denoted as
u_{n}
) such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{u_{n}}{\rho_{\infty}}\to\dfrac{u^{*}}{\rho_{\infty}}\ \mathrm{in}\ L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\rho_{\infty}\mathrm{d}x),

or equivalently

\displaystyle u_{n}\to u^{*}\ \mathrm{in}\ L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty}).

So
(\lambda+\mathcal{L}^{*})^{-1}
is a compact operator. Thus the spectrum of
(\lambda+\mathcal{L}^{*})^{-1}
only admits 0 as an accumulation point. So
0
is an isolated point in the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. Thus for any
p\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{T}}p(x)\mathrm{d}x=0
, we have the following Poincare’s inequality: there exists a constant
c>0
such that

(6.36)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho_{\infty}(x)\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\dfrac{p(x)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\langle\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}p,p\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})}\geq c\|p\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{d}x/\rho_{\infty})}^{2}=c\int_{\mathbb{T}}\dfrac{|p(x)|^{2}}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Multiplying
\rho(x,t)/\rho_{\infty}(x)-1
on both sides of (
4.22
) and substituting
p=\rho(x,t)-\rho_{\infty}(x)
in (
6.36
) yields

(6.37)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\dfrac{(\rho(x,t)-\rho_{\infty}(x))^{2}}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho_{\infty}(x)\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\dfrac{\rho(x,t)}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq-c\int_{\mathbb{T}}\dfrac{|\rho(x,t)-\rho_{\infty}(x)|^{2}}{\rho_{\infty}(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Thus by Gronwall’s inequality, (
4.25
) holds.
∎
