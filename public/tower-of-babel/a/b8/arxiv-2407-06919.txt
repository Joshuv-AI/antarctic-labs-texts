A survey on isothermal and isentropic Baer-Nunziato-type models
Maren Hantke, Siegfried Müller, Aleksey Sikstel, Ferdinand Thein

Abstract
Multi-component Baer-Nunziato-type models for isothermal and isentropic fluids are investigated. These are given by balance equations for volume fractions, density and momentum for each component accounting for the relaxation to equilibrium by means of relaxation terms. Mathematical properties of the models are derived such as hyperbolicity and symmetrization. The fields are characterized and corresponding Riemann invariants are determined. Appropriate entropy-entropy flux pairs are derived taking into account the phasic energy equations including the heat flux. Physically meaningful constraints are presented that ensure the entropy inequality to hold. Instantaneous relaxation to equilibrium is investigated and appropriate algorithms are presented. Numerical results for the isothermal Baer-Nunziato model are compared to an isothermal Euler model and to an isothermal phase-field model.



1
Introduction

Compressible multi-component flows where the fluid is a mixture of several components
all of which may be present in different aggregate states
have a wide range of applications,
for instance a mixture of reacting gases or a mixture of a liquid and a gas. They have been successfully modeled by Baer-Nunziato-type models. Originally, these models have been introduced by Baer and Nunziato
[
1
]
proposing a two-fluid model for detonation waves in granular explosives. This model falls into the class of ensemble averaged models introduced by Drew and Passman
[
5
,
6
]
to provide some gross features of the flow. In contrast to volume averaging, time averaging or statistical averaging, the ensemble averaging allows the interpretation of flow phenomena in terms of repeatability of multi-component flows.

Baer-Nunziato-type models are full non-equilibrium models where each component has its own pressure, velocity and temperature and is governed by its own set of fluid equations. In contrast to other two-fluid models separating the fluids, here the fluids are assumed to coexist in each location due to an averaging procedure. The presence of more than one component in the mixture is modeled by the concept of volume fractions
\alpha_{k}
describing the ratio of volume of component
k
to the total volume in a ball
when the volume of the ball tends to zero, see
[
6
]
.

To model the relaxation process between the components relaxation terms have been introduced.
For instance, Saurel and Abgrall
[
55
]

included relaxation terms for the pressure and the velocities of the components.
Further relaxation procedures can be used to drive the temperatures and the Gibbs free energies
into equilibrium,
see Abgrall et al.
[
57
,
47
]
or
Zein et al.
[
63
]
.
By instantaneous relaxation equilibrium values for the pressure, the velocity, the temperature and the
chemical potentials
can be found.

Since mechanical relaxation, thermal relaxation and relaxation of the chemical potentials proceed on different time scales, cf.
[
56
,
43
,
57
]
, reduced models have been derived assuming zero relaxation times for some of the non-equilibrium quantities relaxing much faster than the remaining quantities, see
[
33
,
20
]
. Thus, the stiffness inherent in the non-equilibrium model is avoided that allows for a much faster numerical simulation of the reduced model.
Usually reduced models suffer from some short-comings. For instance, conservation of energy might be violated, the system loses its hyperbolicity or the reduced model is not the asymptotic correct limit of the nonlinear model.
For more details we refer the interested reader to
[
62
]

and the references cited therein.

In
[
8
,
36
]
a hierarchy of
two-fluid models derived from the Baer-Nunziato model
[
1
]
is investigated where mechanical, thermal and chemical relaxation is assumed to proceed in different order. This hierarchy splits into two branches of models distinguished by the assumption of dynamical velocity equilibrium and local velocity equilibrium, where either both fluids have the
same
velocity or the fluids have
different
velocities that coincide for a particular state.

Since Baer-Nunziato-type models are averaged models, the resulting balance laws are underdetermined. To compensate for the loss in information
closure conditions
have to be imposed additionally.
There exists a rich literature on Baer-Nunziato-type models, see for
instance
[
9
,
24
,
53
,
4
,
41
,
23
]

investigating their properties, in particular,
hyperbolicity
and
thermodynamical consistency
. These properties are essential from an analytical, numerical and physical point of view. For instance, hyperbolicity is needed in the analysis of the Riemann problem and the construction of (approximate) Riemann solvers whereas a non-negative entropy production ensures consistency with the 2nd law of thermodynamics and also might ensure well-posedness, although not yet mathematically rigorously verified. The analysis helps to identify physically admissible closure conditions for the interfacial pressure and the interfacial velocity that occur as model parameters and cannot be closed due to the averaging procedure.
However, a unique closure
does not exist. Typically, the interfacial velocity is modeled as a convex combination of the phasic velocities.
Then the interfacial pressures can be uniquely determined
assuming that the induced entropy production term due to interfacial velocity and interfacial pressures vanishes,
cf.
[
24
,
41
]
.

In recent years, Baer-Nunziato-type models have been applied to different types of fluids. Originally, the components of the fluids are assumed to be immiscible. In
[
30
,
29
]

hybrid mixtures consisting of both immiscible and miscible components are investigated. A mixture of weakly compressible fluids is considered in
[
44
]
. Our main interest in the
present work is on
isothermal and isentropic fluids.
These fall in the larger class of barotropic fluids.
For a one-dimensional model
Hèrard
[
25
]
investigates some properties of a
K
-component Baer-Nunziato-type model for barotropic fluids. Choosing the interfacial velocity as a convex combination of the phasic velocities
and assuming that the entropy production due to the interfacial pressures and
the interfacial velocity vanishes
there
uniquely exist interfacial pressures for
K=2,3
and 4. In case of
barotropic fluids a mathematical
entropy inequality
for multi-phase fluids is verified. For a special class of pressure-velocity relaxation the induced entropy production is consistent with the entropy inequality. This also holds when taking into account mass transfer.
Also for a one-dimensional model Saleh and Seguin
[
54
]
have proven weak hyperbolicity by determining eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the existence of a convex mathematical entropy in case of
barotropic
fluids and the existence of a symmetric form. The analysis is confined to a particular choice of the interfacial velocity and interfacial pressures.
In contrast to
[
25
,
54
]
a multi-dimensional three-component model is considered in
[
3
]
.
A mathematical
entropy inequality is proven. Moreover, the corresponding quasi-1D model is verified to be weakly hyperbolic and Riemann invariants corresponding to the linearly degenerated field associated with the interfacial velocity are given. Again, the results are subject to a specific choice for the interfacial velocity and interfacial pressures.
In a recent work a conservative compressible two phase mixture model has been discussed analytically in
[
59
]
and numerically in
[
46
]
. This model is the barotropic version of a model studied in
[
51
,
50
]
and
belongs to
the class of Symmetric Hyperbolic and Thermodynamically Compatible (SHTC) systems.
An overview on the theory of SHTC systems
can be found in
[
16
,
14
,
15
,
42
,
48
,
49
]
and we
refer to the aforementioned literature for further references. In
[
59
]
the instantaneous relaxation to the Kapila model limit is studied. Further it is shown in
[
59
]
that for smooth solutions the conservative
isothermal/isentropic
two fluid SHTC model is equivalent to the
isothermal/isentropic
Baer-Nunziato system for a particular choice of interface quantities and numerical studies are provided. Therefore these results necessitate a further thorough study of the
isothermal/isentropic
Baer-Nunziato system to provide a sound basis for further comparative studies, also including isothermal sharp interface models studied in
[
18
,
21
]
. For further literature on symmetric hyperbolic systems for mixtures we also refer to
[
52
]
.

The main objective of the current work is to fill some gaps in the case of
barotropic
fluids and to extend the analysis
in the particular case of
isothermal fluids as well. In detail, the issues we are addressing in our work are as follows:
(i) eigenvalues and eigenvectors for the quasi-1D model are determined, hyperbolicity away from resonance states is verified,
the corresponding characteristic fields are investigated and a complete set of linearly independent Riemann invariants is given for all fields; note that the analysis
is not restricted to isothermal and isentropic fluids
but applies to general barotropic fluids;
(ii) a mathematical entropy inequality according to Lax’s entropy-entropy flux pair is verified independently for isentropic and isothermal fluids;
(iii) unique interfacial pressures are determined ensuring that the entropy production induced by the interfacial states is thermodynamically consistent; the interfacial pressures depend on the interfacial velocity chosen as a convex combination of the phasic velocities;
(iv) the entropy production due mechanical relaxation and relaxation of chemical potentials (
K=2
(liquid-vapor),
K=3

(liquid-vapor-gas), isothermal fluids) is verified to be thermodynamically consistent;
(v) the quasi-1D model is symmetrized;
(vi) procedures for instantaneous relaxation of velocity, pressure and chemical potentials (isothermal fluids) are provided.
We emphasize that all investigations are performed for mixtures with an arbitrary number of components
K
except for the relaxation of the chemical potentials.
Furthermore we want to remark that the system is Galilean invariant and the proof is in complete analogy to the one for the full system discussed in
[
41
]
.

Thus, the paper is organized as follows:
In Sect.
2
we present the model. Mathematical properties are discussed in Sect.
3
. An entropy-entropy-flux pair is derived in Sect.
4
. In particular, we present a thermodynamically consistent closure for the interfacial pressures and the interfacial velocity. Then in Sect.
5
we investigate instantaneous relaxation to equilibrium and provide appropriate algorithms. Numerical simulations are performed and discussed in Sect.
6
. Finally, in Sect.
7
we summarize our findings and give an outlook on future work.

2
Baer-Nunziato-type models with barotropic equation of state

First of all, we summarize the balance laws for the multi-component Baer-Nunziato-type model where the number of components is arbitrary.
The phasic fluids are assumed to be barotropic, i.e., each of the single fluids is governed by a pressure only depending on the density of the fluid, see Section
2.1
.
To close the model we have to provide appropriate equations of state (EoS) for the phasic pressures. Starting from fundamental relations of thermodynamics we provide the stiffened gas EoS for both isothermal fluids and isentropic fluids, see Section
2.2
.
Finally, in Section
2.3
we discuss appropriate relaxation models.

2.1
Evolution equations

The multi-component flow is described by a non-equilibrium model where all components are present in each point of the space-time continuum. Each component
k=1,\ldots,K
has its own density
\rho_{k}
and velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}
. The amount of each component is determined by its volume fraction
\alpha_{k}
. The volume fractions are related by the
saturation constraint

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}=1,\quad\alpha_{k}\in(0,1).

(2.1)

The evolution of the volume fractions is characterized by the non-conservative equations

{\partial_{t}\alpha_{k}}+{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{k}}=S_{\alpha,k},\ k=1,\ldots,K.

(2.2)

Due to the saturation condition (
2.1
) we only need
K-1
equations.
Without loss of generality we express
\alpha_{K}
by the other volume fractions, i.e.,

\alpha_{K}=1-\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\alpha_{k},\ \nabla\alpha_{K}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\nabla\alpha_{k},\ S_{\alpha,K}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}S_{\alpha,k}.

(2.3)

In analogy to the two-phase model of Saurel and Abgrall
[
55
]

the fluid equations for each component can be written as

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})}=S_{\alpha\rho,k},

(2.4a)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{T}+\alpha_{k}\,p_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}})}+\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{\nabla\alpha_{l}}={\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k},

(2.4b)

where we neglect effects due to viscosity, surface tension and gravity.
The fluid equations are supplemented by a barotropic equation of state

p_{k}=p_{k}(\rho_{k})\quad\mbox{resp.}\quad\rho_{k}=\rho_{k}(p_{k})

(2.5)

for each of the components.
In the present work we will consider both
isentropic
fluids with constant entropies, i.e.,

s_{k}=const.\quad\forall k=1,\ldots,K

(2.6)

and

isothermal
fluids with constant temperatures, i.e.,

T_{k}=T_{ref}=const.\quad\forall k=1,\ldots,K.

(2.7)

The above model is a simplification of the full Baer-Nunziato system,
see e.g.
[
41
]
,
where the system consists of a mass balance, an energy balance and a balance of momentum for each component and additionally
K-1
equations for the volume fractions.
In particular, the temperature and entropy may also vary and, hence, the pressure does not depend on the density alone as in the barotropic case.
Neglecting viscosity but accounting for heat exchange
the energy equation of the full Baer-Nunziato system
reads

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,E_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))}+\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}=-{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}+S_{\alpha\rho E,k}

(2.8)

with the heat flux
{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k}
and total energy
E_{k}=e_{k}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2
determined by
the internal energy
e_{k}
and kinetic energy
u_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2
. We emphasize that in case of an isothermal fluid the energy equation becomes a conditional equation for the heat flux.
This is employed in the investigation of the entropy production, see Section
4.1
.
For isentropic single-component flows the energy equation becomes redundant. Note that this is not the case for multi-component or multi-phase flows. Nevertheless, energy balance equations typically are not taken into account when considering isentropic systems.

The gradient terms on the left-hand side in
(
2.2
), (
2.4b
) and (
2.8
)
account for the interaction between different components.
In particular, the term
P_{k,l}
accounts for different pressures at the phase interface between phase
k
and
l
.
The interfacial velocity is denoted by
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
.

The source terms

{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}

S_{\alpha,k}
,
S_{\alpha\rho,k}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}
and
S_{\alpha\rho E,k}

on the right-hand sides of
(
2.2
), (
2.4
) and (
2.8
)
describe the interaction of the components corresponding to mass, momentum and energy transfer
via different relaxation types, e.g., due to velocity, pressure and chemical potentials

\xi\in\{v,p,\mu\}

S_{\alpha,k}:=\sum_{\xi}S_{\alpha,k}^{\xi},\,S_{\alpha\rho,k}:=\sum_{\xi}S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{\xi},\,{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}:=\sum_{\xi}{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{\xi},\,S_{\alpha\rho E,k}:=\sum_{\xi}S_{\alpha\rho E,k}^{\xi}.

These depend on the specific components at hand that will be discussed in Section

2.3
.

Obviously, the evolution equations are not in conservative form. To respect the physical principles of conservation of mass, momentum and energy we impose the following
conservation constraints
on the source terms

\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\alpha,k}^{\xi}=0,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{\xi}=0,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{\xi}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\alpha\rho E,k}^{\xi}=0

(2.9)

for each relaxation type as well as the interfacial pressures

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}{\nabla\alpha_{l}}=\sum_{l=1}^{K-1}\left(\sum_{k=1,\neq l}^{K}P_{k,l}-\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}P_{k,K}\right){\nabla\alpha_{l}}=0.

(2.10)

Since by the saturation constraint (
2.1
) the gradients
{\nabla\alpha_{l}}
,
l=1,\ldots,K-1
, are linearly independent, the conservation constraint (
2.10
) is equivalent to

\sum_{k=1,\neq l}^{K}P_{k,l}=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}P_{k,K}=:P_{I}\quad\forall l=1,\ldots,K-1.

(2.11)

Then the evolution equations for the mixture quantities

\rho:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k},\ \rho{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}

(2.12)

obtained by summation of the single-component fluid equations (
2.4a
), (
2.4b
)
and employing the saturation constraint (
2.1
) as well as the conservation constraints
(
2.9
) and (
2.11
)
are in conservative form. See
[
41
]
for details.

So far, the model is not yet closed. For this purpose, we have to find closing conditions for the interfacial pressures
P_{k,l}
,
the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
and the relaxation terms
S_{\alpha,k}
,
S_{\alpha\rho,k}
,

{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}
,
S_{\alpha\rho E,k}
.
In the following sections we will derive appropriate constraints. However, these will not specify a unique model but some options are still remaining for the choice of the interfacial velocity and the relaxation terms.

Remark 2.1
.

For
a given barotropic pressure law (
2.5
) we always can introduce an “internal energy” function
{\overline{e}}_{k}={\overline{e}}_{k}(\rho_{k})
as solution of the ODE
{\overline{e}}^{\prime}_{k}(\rho_{k})=p_{k}/\rho_{k}^{2}
. Defining the “ total energy”
{\overline{E}}_{k}={\overline{e}}_{k}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2
an “energy” equation can be derived from (
2.4
)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\overline{E}}_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,({\overline{E}}_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))}+\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}=p_{k}{({\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{k}}+\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho{\overline{E}},k}.

(2.13)

This balance law is different to (
2.8
). In particular,
\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho{\overline{E}},k}
differs from

S_{\alpha\rho E,k}
. In case of an isentropic fluid the “internal and total energies”
{\overline{e}}_{k}
and
{\overline{E}}_{k}
coincide with the energies
e_{k}
and
E_{k}
, respectively. In Section
4.2
for isentropic fluids we use
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}{\overline{E}}_{k}
for a mathematical entropy in Lax’ concept of entropy-entropy flux pairs.

2.2
Some remarks on thermodynamics

In order to investigate thermodynamical properties of the model (
2.2
), (
2.4
),
we assume that the equation of state (
2.5
) is in agreement with thermodynamical principles. For this purpose, we recall from equilibrium thermodynamics that for each component
k
the internal energy
e_{k}
is a function of the entropy and the volume
\tau_{k}
satisfying

de_{k}=T_{k}ds_{k}-p_{k}d\tau_{k}.

(2.14)

Thus, the pressure and the temperature are the partial derivatives of
e_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k})
that are assumed to be positive, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k}):=-\frac{\partial e_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\geq 0,\quad T_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k}):=\frac{\partial e_{k}}{\partial s_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\geq 0.

(2.15)

Furthermore, to ensure thermodynamical stability we assume that the Hessian of
e_{k}
is a convex function with respect to
\tau_{k}
and
s_{k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}e_{k}}{\partial^{2}\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\geq 0,\ \frac{\partial^{2}e_{k}}{\partial^{2}s_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\geq 0,\ \frac{\partial^{2}e_{k}}{\partial^{2}\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\frac{\partial^{2}e_{k}}{\partial^{2}s_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\geq\left(\frac{\partial^{2}e_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}\partial s_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})\right)^{2}.

(2.16)

Finally, in agreement with the third law of thermodynamics we assume

\tau_{k}\geq 0,\quad s_{k}\geq 0.

Alternatively, assuming that
p_{k}
and
T_{k}
are strictly positive, then
e_{k}
becomes a monotone function in
\tau_{k}
and
s_{k}
and we may change variables, i.e.,

s_{k}=s_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})
satisfying

T_{k}ds_{k}=de_{k}+p_{k}d\tau_{k}

(2.17)

with partial derivatives

\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})=\frac{{\check{p}}_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})}{{\check{T}}_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})}>0,\quad\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial e_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})=\frac{1}{{\check{T}}_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})}>0.

(2.18)

and

{\check{p}}_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k}):=p_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})),\quad{\check{T}}_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k}):=T_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})).

It is well-known that
s_{k}=s_{k}(\tau_{k},e_{k})
is a concave function, i.e., the Hessian is negative-definite

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}s_{k}}{\partial^{2}\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})\leq 0,\ \frac{\partial^{2}s_{k}}{\partial^{2}e_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})\leq 0,\ \frac{\partial^{2}s_{k}}{\partial^{2}\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})\frac{\partial^{2}s_{k}}{\partial^{2}e_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})\geq\left(\frac{\partial^{2}s_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}\partial e_{k}}(\tau_{k},e_{k})\right)^{2},

(2.19)

if and only if
e_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k})
is a convex function, i.e., thermodynamic stability holds.

Finally, following
[
58
]
, p. 21 and 23, we introduce the speed of sound
c_{k}
and the fundamental derivative of thermodynamics
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
defined by means of (
2.15
) as

\displaystyle c_{k}^{2}(\tau_{k},s_{k}):=-\tau_{k}^{2}\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},s_{k}),\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k}):=\frac{1}{2}\tau_{k}^{3}\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}p_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}^{2}}(\tau_{k},s_{k})}{c_{k}^{2}(\tau_{k},s_{k})}

(2.20)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle c_{k}^{2}(\rho_{k},s_{k})=\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k},s_{k}),\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(\rho_{k},s_{k})=\frac{1}{c_{k}(\rho_{k},s_{k})}\frac{\partial(\rho_{k}c_{k})}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k},s_{k}),

(2.21)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle c_{k}^{2}(\rho_{k},T_{k})=\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k},T_{k})+\frac{T_{k}}{\rho_{k}^{2}c_{v,k}}\left(\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial T_{k}}(\rho_{k},T_{k})\right)^{2},\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(\rho_{k},T_{k})=1+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2\,c_{k}(\rho_{k},T_{k})}\frac{\partial c_{k}}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k},T_{k}).

(2.22)

Example 2.1

(Stiffened gas EoS)
.

Let the constant material parameters
c_{v,k}
,
\gamma_{k}
,
\pi_{k}
and
q_{k}
denoting the specific
heat capacity at constant volume, the adiabatic exponent, the minimal pressure and the heat of formation of component
k
, respectively, chosen such that

c_{v,k}>0
,
\pi_{k}\geq 0
,
q_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}>1
. Let further
\rho_{0,k}
be some reference density at reference temperature
T_{0,k}
.
By means of these coefficients the internal energy, the pressure and the entropy
are determined by

\displaystyle e_{k}=c_{v,k}T_{k}+\pi_{k}/\rho_{k}+q_{k}\,,

(2.23a)

\displaystyle p_{k}=\rho_{k}(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}T_{k}-\pi_{k}\,,

(2.23b)

\displaystyle s_{k}=c_{v,k}+c_{v,k}\ln\frac{T_{k}}{T_{0,k}}+q_{k}\frac{1}{T_{0,k}}-(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}\ln\frac{\rho_{k}}{\rho_{0,k}}-\frac{\pi_{k}}{\rho_{0,k}}\frac{1}{T_{0,k}}\,.

(2.23c)

Then the speed of sound and the fundamental derivative of gas dynamics are determined by (
2.22
) as

\displaystyle c_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}T_{k}\,c_{v,k}\,(\gamma_{k}-1)},\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{k}+1}{2}.

(2.24)

For a derivation we refer to
[
22
]
.

In the present work we are interested in
isothermal and isentropic fluids where
the pressure is only a function of the density, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{k}(\rho_{k})=p_{k}(\rho_{k},s_{k}=const)\quad\text{or}\quad p_{k}(\rho_{k})=p_{k}(\rho_{k},T_{k}=const)

(2.25)

in (
2.21
) and (
2.29
).
Thus, these fluids fall in the class of barotropic fluids.
In the following we assume for barotropic fluids that

\displaystyle p_{k}^{\prime}(\rho_{k})>0,

(2.26a)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{k}(\rho_{k})=1+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2c_{k}^{2}(\rho_{k})}p_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\rho_{k})>0.

(2.26b)

This ensures positivity of the barotropic speed of sound

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{k}(\rho_{k})=\sqrt{p_{k}^{\prime}(\rho_{k})}>0

(2.27)

and convexity of
the isentropes or isotherms, in the special case of an isentropic or isothermal fluid, respectively.

In case of an isothermal fluid the isothermal speed of sound and the fundamental derivative of thermodynamics are defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}(\tau_{k}):=-\tau_{k}^{2}\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}(\tau_{k},T_{ref}),\quad\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}(\tau_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\tau_{k}^{3}\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}p_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}^{2}}(\tau_{k},T_{ref})}{\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}(\tau_{k},T_{ref})}

(2.28)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}(\rho_{k})=\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k},T_{ref}),\quad\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{k}(\tau_{k})=1+\frac{\rho_{k}}{\tilde{c}_{k}(\rho_{k},Tref)}\frac{\partial\tilde{c}_{k}}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k},T_{ref}).

(2.29)

Note that the barotropic speed of sound
\overline{c}_{k}
, see (
2.27
),
is identical to the thermodynamical speed of sound
c_{k}(\rho_{k},s_{k}=const)
,
see (
2.21
), for an isentropic fluid whereas it is identical to the isothermal speed of sound
\tilde{c}_{k}(\rho_{k})
,
see (
2.29
), for an isothermal fluid.

For our numerical investigations in Section
6
we use the isentropic stiffened gas
EoS and the isothermal stiffened gas EoS which have been verified to be thermodynamically consistent,
see
[
22

. These are summarized in the following.

Example 2.2

(Isothermal stiffened gas EoS)
.

Starting from the fixed temperature
T_{ref}
, i.e.,
T_{k}=T_{ref}
for all

and the corresponding saturation pressures
p_{ref,k}
, we choose
the ratio of specific heats
\gamma_{k}>1
, the minimal pressure
\pi_{k}\geq 0
, the specific heat at constant volume
c_{v,k}
, and the heat of formation
q_{k}
for every component at the reference state.
By means of these coefficients the pressure and the entropy are determined by

\displaystyle p_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}\rho_{k}-\pi_{k},

(2.30)

\displaystyle s_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=-(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}\,\ln\left(\frac{p_{k}+\pi_{k}}{p_{ref,k}+\pi_{k}}\right)+c_{v,k}+\frac{q_{k}}{T_{ref}}.

(2.31)

In this particular case, the isothermal speed of sound and the isothermal fundamental derivative of gas dynamics become constants that are determined by (

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{k}=\sqrt{T_{ref}\,c_{v,k}\,(\gamma_{k}-1)},\quad\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{k}=1.

(2.33)

The fundamental derivatives

and

have the same sign.
For later use we also give the internal energies and the Gibbs free energies

\displaystyle e_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=c_{v,k}T_{ref}+\frac{\pi_{k}}{\rho_{k}}+e_{0,k},\quad e_{0,k}:=q_{k}-(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}T_{ref},

(2.34)

\displaystyle g_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}T_{ref}\ln\left(\frac{p_{k}(\rho_{k})+\pi_{k}}{p_{ref,k}+\pi_{k}}\right).

(2.35)

Example 2.3

(Isentropic stiffened gas EoS)
.

Again, starting from the uniform reference temperature
T_{ref}
and the corresponding saturation pressures

p_{ref,k}
, we choose
the ratio of specific heats

, the minimal pressure

, the specific heat at constant volume

, and the heat of formation

for every component at the reference state.
By means of these coefficients the pressure and the temperature are determined by

\displaystyle p_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=A_{k}\rho_{k}^{\gamma_{k}}-\pi_{k},

(2.36)

\displaystyle T_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=B_{k}\rho_{k}^{\gamma_{k}-1}

(2.37)

with

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{0,k}^{\gamma_{k}}}{(p_{0,k}+\pi_{k})^{\gamma_{k}-1}}\left(c_{v,k}(\gamma_{k}-1)\right)^{\gamma_{k}},

(2.38a)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{0,k}^{\gamma_{k}}}{(p_{0,k}+\pi_{k})^{\gamma_{k}-1}}\left(c_{v,k}(\gamma_{k}-1)\right)^{\gamma_{k}-1}.

(2.38b)

For sake of completeness, we give the constant entropy at the reference state as well as the internal energy

\displaystyle s_{0,k}

\displaystyle=c_{v,k}+\frac{q_{k}}{T_{ref}}

(2.39)

\displaystyle e_{k}(\rho_{k})

\displaystyle=c_{v,k}T_{k}(\rho_{k})+\frac{\pi_{k}}{\rho_{k}}+e_{0,k},\quad e_{0,k}:=q_{k}-(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}T_{ref}.

(2.40)

The speed of sound and the fundamental derivative of gas dynamics are determined by (

) as

\displaystyle c_{k}(\rho_{k})=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}\,A_{k}\,\rho_{k}^{\gamma_{k}-1}},\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{k}+1}{2}.

(2.41)

So far we have discussed the individual components. However, since we are considering a mixture of

de=Tds-pd\frac{1}{\rho}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{k}dN_{k}

where

We do not comment on the mixture temperature

However, in the case of different temperatures for each component we refer to

For later use we want to specify the chemical potential for the mixture of ideal gases.

Example 2.4

(Chemical potential for a mixture of two isothermal ideal gases)

Assume, components

In particular, if component

.

This example becomes relevant in Sect.

2.3
Relaxation Model

The non-equilibrium model without relaxation terms allows for different values for velocities, pressures, temperatures as well as chemical potentials at the same point. The relaxation mechanism described by the source terms drives all these quantities into equilibrium. Typically it is distinguished between mechanical and thermal
relaxation processes that relax either pressures and velocities or temperatures and chemical potentials to equilibrium.
In the following we give the relaxation terms for the full Baer-Nunziato model containing the energy equations. The energy relaxation will be needed in case of isothermal fluids, see Section
4.1
, whereas it does not enter the relaxation process of the isentropic model.

Mechanical relaxation.

The
pressure relaxation
implies volume variations that induce energy variations due to the interfacial pressure work. Following previous work in
[
55
,
62
,
41
]

we model the pressure relaxation in case of barotropic fluids by

S_{\alpha,k}^{p}=\theta_{p}\,\alpha_{k}\,(p_{k}-p),\ S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{p}=0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{p}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\ S_{\alpha\rho E,k}^{p}=\theta_{p}\,\alpha_{k}\,p(p-p_{k})\,.

(2.45)

Here
\theta_{p}
is the pressure relaxation parameter and

p
the mixture pressure defined in Eqn. (
2.43
)
Similarly we apply for the
velocity relaxation

S_{\alpha,k}^{v}=0,\ S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{v}=0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{v}=\theta_{v}\,\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})\,,S_{\alpha\rho E,k}^{v}=\theta_{v}\,\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}}\cdot({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})

(2.46)

with the velocity relaxation parameter
\theta_{v}
and the mixture velocity
v
determined by the conserved
mixture quantities (
2.12
). Performing velocity and pressure relaxation the fluid mixture is in
mechanical equilibrium, i.e.,
p_{k}=p^{\infty}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}^{\infty}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Obviously, the mechanical relaxation terms satisfy the conservation constraints
(
2.9
) due to the definition of the mixture quantities (
2.12
) and (
2.43
).

We like to mention that in
[
25
]
for
barotropic fluids
a general class of models for mechanical relaxation is considered:

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,k}^{p}=\theta_{p}\,\sum_{l=1}^{K}d_{k,l}(p_{k}-p_{l}),\ S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{p}=0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{p}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}\,

(2.47a)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,k}^{v}=S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{v}=0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{v}=\theta_{v}\,\sum_{l=1}^{K}e_{k,l}({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{l}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})\,,

(2.47b)

with state-dependent coefficients
d_{k,l}>0
and
e_{k,l}\geq 0
.
In particular, choosing
d_{k,l}=\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}
and

e_{k,l}=\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}\alpha_{l}\rho_{l}/\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha_{j}\rho_{j}

we note that the mechanical relaxation model
(
2.45
) and (
2.46
) falls into the class of models (
2.47
).

Thermal relaxation.

According to (
2.7
) for isothermal fluids the temperatures are all in equilibrium. For isentropic fluids the temperatures cannot be in equilibrium because all entropies are constant resulting in an overdetermined system. Thus, there exists no thermal relaxation process.

Chemical potential relaxation.

In the following we consider only isothermal fluids because there is already no thermal relaxation for isentropic fluids.
Mass transfer between different phases of the same substance occurs, whenever these phases are not in chemical equilibrium, i.e., when their chemical potentials are not the same. This physical fact is the key idea to model the mass transfer by relaxation of the chemical potentials.
It is obvious, that from now on it is necessary to identify the components.
In particular, we are interested in the
two-phase model, i.e.,
K=2
, with water vapor (
k=1
), liquid water (
k=2
), and in the
three-component model, i.e.,
K=3
, with water vapor (
k=1
), liquid water (
k=2
) and inert gas (
k=3
).

Two-component mixtures.

For a two-component mixture (
K=2
) the relaxation of chemical potentials is
modeled according to
[
62
]

by

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\frac{\dot{m}}{\varrho},\ S_{\alpha\rho,1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}},\ S_{\alpha\rho E,1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\left(\epsilon+\frac{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}^{2}}{2}\right),

(2.48a)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,2}^{\mu}=-S_{\alpha,1}^{\mu},\ S_{\alpha\rho,2}^{\mu}=-S_{\alpha\rho,1}^{\mu},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},2}^{\mu}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},1}^{\mu},S_{\alpha\rho E,2}^{\mu}=-S_{\alpha\rho E,1}^{\mu}

(2.48b)

with the relaxation parameter
\theta_{\mu}
.
For the velocity
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
we choose a convex combination

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}^{v}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k},\quad\beta_{k}^{v}\in[0,1],\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}^{v}=1.

(2.49)

Formulas for the parameters
\varrho
and
\epsilon
can be found in
[
62
]
where they
are determined such that pressure and thermal equilibrium is maintained during the relaxation.
Obviously, the conservation constraints (
2.9
) are satisfied.
Note that due to these constraints we are not allowed to introduce
\epsilon_{k}
and
\varrho_{k}

differently for each component
k=1,2
.
In
[
17
]
another choice for these parameters has been introduced
that ensures non-negativity of the entropy production due to chemical relaxation.

Since for a two-component mixture the Gibbs free energy coincides with the chemical potential, chemical equilibrium is
achieved, if the Gibbs free energies of the two components coincide, i.e.,
g_{1}=g_{2}
.

Three-component mixtures.

Here we consider three components, i.e.,
K=3
, with
water vapor (
k=1
), liquid water (
k=2
) and inert gas (
k=3
),
where the gas phase is assumed to be a mixture of water vapor and some inert gas.
The relaxation terms of chemical potentials for isothermal fluids are given by

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\frac{\dot{m}}{\varrho_{1}},\ S_{\alpha\rho,1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}},\ S_{\rho E,1}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\left(\epsilon_{1}+\frac{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}^{2}}{2}\right),

(2.50a)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,2}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\frac{\dot{m}}{\varrho_{2}},\ S_{\alpha\rho,2}^{\mu}=-\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},2}^{\mu}=-\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}},\ S_{\alpha\rho E,2}^{\mu}=-\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\left(\epsilon_{2}+\frac{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}^{2}}{2}\right),

(2.50b)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,3}^{\mu}=-\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\left(\frac{1}{\varrho_{1}}+\frac{1}{\varrho_{2}}\right),\ S_{\alpha\rho,3}^{\mu}=0,\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},3}^{\mu}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}\,,S_{\alpha\rho E,3}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\left(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}\right)

(2.50c)

with the relaxation parameter
\theta_{\mu}
. Formulas for the parameters
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}

can be found in
[
62
,
64
]
.
For details on the physics see the book of Müller and Müller
[
38
]
.

Chemical equilibrium is
achieved, if the chemical potential of the water vapor phase equals the Gibbs free energy of the liquid
water phase. In the limit case of no inert gas the expression of the
chemical potential of the water vapor phase reduces to the Gibbs free energy.
The mass flux
\dot{m}
between the liquid and the vapor phase is driven by the difference of their chemical
potentials, i.e.,
\dot{m}=\dot{m}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})
.
In particular, the mass flux vanishes if the difference of the chemical potentials is zero, i.e.,
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
or if one of the phases is completely extinct.

Note that the relaxation terms (
2.48
) resp. (
2.50
) differ from the terms given in

[
17
]
due to the different form of the chemical potentials in the isothermal case.

The relaxation terms (
2.45
), (
2.46
), (
2.48
),
(
2.50
) are of major importance when dealing with interface problems,
see for instance Saurel and Abgrall

[
55
]
or Lallemand and Saurel
[
35
]

for mechanical relaxation terms. Typically, it is assumed that
pressure and velocity relax instantaneously, see
[
55
]
, whereas the thermal
relaxation and the relaxation of chemical potentials are much slower, see Zein
[
62
]
.
Here we are interested only in the equilibrium state that is characterized by vanishing relaxation terms
rather than the transient relaxation process itself.
Since the equilibrium state does not depend on the order of relaxation, the relaxation parameters

\theta_{\xi}
,
\xi\in\{p,v,\mu\}
,
drop out and have not to be known explicitly. The same is true for the parameters
\varrho_{k}
,
k=1,2
.

Furthermore we point out, that our modeling of the mass transfer is physically correct
because we take into account chemical potentials instead of Gibbs free energies.
In
[
62
,
64
]

the Gibbs free energy is relaxed, which neglects the effect of mixture
entropy in cases of impure substances.

We would
like to mention that in
[
25
]
for
barotropic fluids
a general class of models for chemical relaxation (mass transfer) is considered:

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,k}^{\mu}=0,\ S_{\alpha\rho,k}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}\Gamma_{k,l},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}^{\mu}=\theta_{\mu}\,\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{k,l}\Gamma_{k,l}

(2.51)

with interfacial mass transfer
\Gamma_{k,l}
and interfacial velocities
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{k,l}=\beta_{k,l}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}+(1-\beta_{k,l}){\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{l}
with state-dependent coefficients
\beta_{k,l}\in[0,1]
. To ensure the conservation constraint (
2.9
) it is assumed that
\Gamma_{k,l}=-\Gamma_{l,k}
and

{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{k,l}={\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{l,k}
.
In particular, it is suggested to use
\beta_{k,l}=1/2
and
\Gamma_{k,l}=f_{k,l}\left((p_{l}/\rho_{l}+e_{l}(\rho_{l}))-(p_{k}/\rho_{k}+e_{k}(\rho_{k}))\right)
for an entropy-consistent closure.
Since in the relaxation models (
2.48
) and (
2.50
)
the change of volume is accounted for in the evolution equations of the volume fractions, i.e.,
S_{\alpha,k}^{\mu}\neq 0
, these models do not fit into the
class (
2.51
).
From a physical point of view, the volume will change when the mass of a component changes due to evaporation or condensation. This has to be accounted for in the relaxation terms
S_{\alpha,k}^{\mu}
.
Furthermore, in
[
25
]
the existence of an equilibrium to (
2.51
) is not investigated.

Finally, we would like to mention that a physically meaningful relaxation model
has to be Galilean invariant. For the models (
2.45
), (
2.46
), (
2.48
) and (
2.50
) this was investigated in
[
41
]
.
In case of
barotropic fluids
Hérard’s relaxation model determined by (

Remark 2.2
.

For the closure of the barotropic model (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and(
2.4

2.5

3
Mathematical Properties

In this section we investigate some mathematical properties of the barotropic Baer-Nunziato model. First of all, we derive in Sec.
3.1
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors for the transport part of the model and verify that it is hyperbolic if the non-resonance condition holds.
Then we consider the corresponding characteristic fields, see Sec.
3.2
, and derive for each field Riemann invariants, see Sec.
3.3
. Finally, we derive a symmetrizer in Sec.
3.4
.

3.1
Hyperbolicity: eigenvectors and eigenvalues

Neglecting the relaxation processes in
the evolution equations (
2.2
), (
2.4
) the model reduces
to a homogeneous first order system describing transport effects only. Therefore this system
should be hyperbolic to ensure that all wave speeds are finite and the system may be locally decoupled. From a
mathematical point of view, this property is helpful in the construction of Riemann solvers.
Therefore we determine here the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Since the corresponding characteristic fields are essential in the construction of a Riemann solver we investigate these fields in more detail.
This will also provide a physically reasonable closure for the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
.
Note that the following analysis holds
for a general barotropic equation of state (
2.5
) satisfying the constraints
(
2.26
) and (
2.27
). In particular, it is applicable to both isentropic and isothermal fluids.

To investigate hyperbolicity we may consider the evolution equations for the primitive variables

\alpha_{k}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}
,
p_{k}
instead of the variables
\alpha_{k}
,
\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}
,
\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}
. This is justified because the change of variables is a bijective mapping under which the eigenvalues are invariant and the eigenvectors are related by the Jacobian of the transformation.
Using the equation of state (
2.5
) we derive from (
2.2
), (
2.4
) the system in primitive variables.

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\alpha_{k}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{I,i}\,\partial_{x_{i}}\alpha_{k}=S_{\alpha,k}

(3.1a)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(v_{k,i}\,\partial_{x_{i}}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}+\sum_{l=1,l\neq k}^{K}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}}(P_{k,l}-p_{k})\,{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{d,i}\,\partial_{x_{i}}\alpha_{l}+\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{d,i}\,\partial_{x_{i}}p_{k}\right)={\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k},

(3.1b)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}p_{k}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(v_{k,i}\,\partial_{x_{i}}p_{k}-\sum_{l=1,l\neq k}^{K}\frac{\rho_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\,\overline{c}^{2}_{k}\,(v_{k,i}-V_{I,i})\,\partial_{x_{i}}\alpha_{l}+\rho_{k}\,\overline{c}_{k}^{2}\,\partial_{x_{i}}v_{k,i}\right)=S_{p,k},

(3.1c)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{d,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the unit vector in the
i
th coordinate direction.
The relaxation terms are determined by

\displaystyle S_{p,k}:=\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{\alpha_{k}}(S_{\alpha\rho,k}-\rho_{k}S_{\alpha,k}),\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}:=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}S_{\alpha\rho,k}\right).

(3.2)

Neglecting the relaxation terms, we now consider the projection of the homogeneous transport system
onto normal direction
\xi:={{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}
for arbitrary unit direction
{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 3.1
.

For a general barotropic fluid the speed of sound
\overline{c}_{k}
is determined by
(
2.27
).
We emphasize that it may differ in general from the thermodynamical speed of sound (
2.20
).
In case of the isothermal stiffened gas it is
identical to
the isothermal speed of sound (
2.28
),
see (
2.30
), (
2.32
) and (
2.33
).

Introducing the vector of primitive variables

{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K-1},{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{K}^{t})^{t},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{k}=({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{t},p_{k})^{t}

(3.3)

the projected system can be written in quasi-conservative form as

{\partial_{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\,\partial_{\xi}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}.

(3.4)

The matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
is determined by the block matrix

{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{i}\,n_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}V_{I,n}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1}&&&\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{1,n}&{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{1,n}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{K,n}&&&{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{K,n}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

(3.5)

with the blocks defined as

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{k}^{t}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\rho_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\,(v_{k,n}-V_{I,n})\,{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{k}^{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{k,n}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d}&\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\\[5.69054pt]
\rho_{k}\,\overline{c}_{k}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}^{t}&v_{k,n}\end{matrix}\right).

(3.6)

Here

{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1}
are the unit matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)\times(K-1)}
,
respectively, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{K-1}
are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{K-1}

with value 0 or 1, respectively. The vectors
{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{k}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{k}

are defined by their components
l=1,\ldots,K-1
as

\displaystyle\beta_{k,l}:=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}\left((P_{k,l}-p_{k})(1-\delta_{k,l})-(P_{k,K}-p_{k})(1-\delta_{k,K})\right),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k,l}:=\overline{c}_{k}^{2}(\delta_{k,l}-\delta_{k,K})

(3.8)

with
\delta_{k,l}
the Kronecker symbol. The normal components of the velocities and the interfacial velocity
are defined as

v_{k,n}:={\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}},\quad V_{I,n}:={\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}.

(3.9)

Obviously, the eigenvalues of the matrix (
3.5
) can now be explicitly determined.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Eigenvalues)

Let be
{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}|
=1. Then the eigenvalues of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
read

\displaystyle\lambda_{I,k}

\displaystyle=V_{I,n},\ k=1,\ldots,K-1,

(3.10a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,i}

\displaystyle=v_{k,n},\ k=1,\ldots,K,\ i=1,\ldots,d-1,

(3.10b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,\pm}

\displaystyle=v_{k,n}\pm\overline{c}_{k},\ k=1,\ldots,K.

(3.10c)

Proof.

Employing the block structure the characteristic polynomial reads

\det({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}-\lambda\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}})=\det(V_{I,n}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1}-\lambda\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1})\,\prod_{k=1}^{K}\det({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}-\lambda\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1})=0.

(3.11)

The eigenvalues of the matrices
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}
are determined by

\det({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}-\lambda\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1})=(v_{k,n}-\lambda)^{d-1}\,((v_{k,n}-\lambda)^{2}-\overline{c}_{k}^{2}).

(3.12)

\square

Next we need to verify that the left and right eigenvectors corresponding to these eigenvalues are linearly
independent. The derivation of the eigenvectors is in complete analogy to the Baer-Nunziato model for general fluids,
see
[
41
]
. For this purpose, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\delta_{k}^{n}:=v_{k,n}-V_{I,n},\ \sigma_{k}:=(\delta_{k}^{n})^{2}-\overline{c}_{k}^{2},\ \kappa_{0}:=\prod_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l}\sigma_{l},\ \kappa_{k}:=\prod_{l=1,l\neq k}^{K}\alpha_{l}\sigma_{l}

(3.13)

for
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Note that
\delta_{k}^{n}
is referred to as drift velocity in the literature. Then we can prove the following

Theorem 3.2
.

(Hyperbolicity)

Let be
{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}|=1
.
Let the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
be chosen such that
the normal interfacial velocity
V_{I,n}
does not coincide with phasic normal velocities
v_{k,n}

and the phasic acoustic wave speeds
v_{k,n}\pm\overline{c}_{k}
, i.e.,
the non-resonance condition

\displaystyle V_{I,n}\neq v_{k,n}\pm\overline{c}_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K,

(3.14)

is satisfied. Then the homogeneous first order system (
3.1
) is hyperbolic,
i.e., (i) the eigenvalues of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
are all real but not necessarily distinct and
(ii) there exist bases of corresponding left and right eigenvectors that are orthonormal.

Proof.

Motivated by the block structure of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
we make the following ansatz for
computing the corresponding left and right eigenvectors

{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n}&&&\\[2.84526pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{1}_{I,n}&{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{1,n}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\[2.84526pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{K}_{I,n}&&&{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{K,n}\end{matrix}\right),\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{0}_{I,n}&&&\\[2.84526pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{1}_{I,n}&{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{1,n}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\[2.84526pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{K}_{I,n}&&&{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{K,n}\end{matrix}\right)

(3.15)

with blocks

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n}:=\kappa_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1},\ \kappa_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{0}_{I,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)\times(K-1)},

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n},{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{k}_{I,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(K-1)},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n},{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)},\ k=1,\ldots,K.

Here the matrices
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{n}
are composed of the right and left eigenvectors in their columns and rows,
respectively. To determine the blocks
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}
we note that
\lambda_{k,i}
and

\lambda_{k,\pm}
are also eigenvalues of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}
and
the eigenvalue problem for
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
decouples into eigenvalue problems for
the matrices
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}
corresponding to a single component.
In a first step, we therefore compute the eigenvectors to these sub-problems where we first determine
an orthonormal basis
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{1},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{d-1}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

{{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{j}=\delta_{i,j}
and
{{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}=0
.
Then the right and left eigenvectors to the eigenvalues (
3.10b
)
and (
3.10c
) are

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{k,i}=({\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}^{t},0)^{t},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{k,\pm}=(\pm\overline{c}_{k}/\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}^{t},\overline{c}_{k}^{2})^{t},

(3.16)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{k,i}=({\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}^{t},0)^{t},\ {\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{k,\pm}=0.5\overline{c}_{k}^{-2}(\pm\overline{c}_{k}\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}^{t},1)^{T}

(3.17)

for
i=1,\ldots,d-1
.
Thus, there exists an eigenvalue decomposition of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}
, i.e.,

{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k,n},

(3.18)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}
are defined by the left and right eigenvectors
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k,n}
is a diagonal matrix with eigenvalues on the diagonal

{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}-\overline{c}_{k}/\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{1}&\ldots&{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{d-1}&\overline{c}_{k}/\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\\
\overline{c}_{k}^{2}&0&\ldots&0&\overline{c}_{k}^{2}\\
\end{matrix}\right),

(3.19)

{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}:=\frac{1}{2\overline{c}_{k}^{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}-\overline{c}_{k}\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}&2\overline{c}_{k}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{1}&\ldots&2\overline{c}_{k}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{d-1}&\overline{c}_{k}\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\\
1&0&\ldots&0&1\\
\end{matrix}\right)^{t},

(3.20)

{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k,n}:=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{k,n}-\overline{c}_{k}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d-1}^{t}&0\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d-1}&v_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d-1}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d-1}\\
0&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d-1}^{t}&v_{k,n}+\overline{c}_{k}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(3.21)

To calculate the eigenvectors to the multiple eigenvalue
\lambda_{I,i}

we employ the knowledge of the matrices
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}
. According to the block structure
of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}
the matrix of corresponding right eigenvectors needs to satisfy

({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}-\lambda_{I,i}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}){\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}\Leftrightarrow({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}-\lambda_{I,i}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}){\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n}=-\kappa_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n},\ k=1,\ldots,K.

Assuming that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{I,i}
does not coincide with one of the eigenvalues

\lambda_{k,i}
and
\lambda_{k,\pm}
, then

{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}-\lambda_{I,i}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}

is regular and there exists a unique solution for
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n}
.
With (
3.13
) these matrices are given by

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1},

(3.22)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\kappa_{0}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}-V_{I,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}\right)^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}=\kappa_{0}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{k}^{t}-\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{k}^{T})\delta_{k}^{n}/(\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k})\\[2.84526pt]
(\alpha_{k}\overline{c}_{k}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{k}^{t}-(\delta_{k}^{n})^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}_{k}^{t})\rho_{k}/(\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k})\end{array}\right).

Since
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
according to (
2.1
), these matrices are regular, i.e., the columns are
linearly independent, if and only if
\sigma_{k}\neq 0
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, i.e.,
the non-resonance condition (
3.14
) holds.
In particular, the inverse

\displaystyle\left({\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}-V_{I,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{matrix}(\delta_{k}^{n})^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d}+\overline{c}_{k}^{2}(\delta_{k}^{n}\sigma_{k})^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}&&-(\rho_{k}\sigma_{k})^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\\[5.69054pt]
-\rho_{k}\overline{c}_{k}^{2}\sigma_{k}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}^{T}&&\delta^{n}_{k}\sigma_{k}^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)

exists.

Thus the corresponding left eigenvectors are determined by the rows of the inverse of
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}
.
Since
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}={\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}
, the blocks turn out to be

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{-1}_{k,n},\ k=1,\ldots,K,

(3.26)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{0}_{I,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n})^{-1}=\kappa_{0}^{-1}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1},

(3.27)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}^{k}_{I,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n}({\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n})^{-1}=-\kappa_{0}^{-1}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n},\ k=1,\ldots,K,

(3.28)

and we obtain for the right eigenvectors

{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{I,l}:=\kappa_{0}\left(({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,l}^{0})^{t},({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,l}^{1})^{t},\ldots,({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,l}^{K})^{t}\right)^{t},\ l=1,\ldots,K-1.

(3.29)

where
{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,l}^{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{l,K-1}
and
{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,l}^{k}=({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}}_{k,l}^{t},{\overline{z}}_{k,l})^{t}

is determined by the components

\displaystyle{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}}_{k,l}

\displaystyle={\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\left(\gamma_{k,l}-\alpha_{k}\beta_{k,l}\right)\delta^{n}_{k}/(\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k}),

(3.30)

\displaystyle{\overline{z}}_{k,l}

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha_{k}\overline{c}_{k}^{2}\beta_{k,l}-(\delta_{k}^{n})^{2}\gamma_{k,l}\right)\rho_{k}/(\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k})

(3.31)

Similar to (
3.29
) the columns of
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{n}
are then given by

{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{I,l}:=\left(({\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{I,l}^{0})^{t},({\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{I,l}^{1})^{t},\ldots,({\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{I,l}^{K})^{t}\right)^{t},\ l=1,\ldots,K-1

(3.32)

with

{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}^{0}_{I,l}=\kappa_{0}^{-1}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{l,K-1},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}^{k}_{I,l}=\frac{\rho_{k}}{2\overline{c}_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k}}(a_{k,l}^{-},{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d-1}^{t},a_{k,l}^{+})^{t}

and

\displaystyle a_{k,l}^{\pm}:=\left(\alpha_{k}\overline{c}_{k}\beta_{k,l}\mp\delta_{k}^{n}\gamma_{k,l}\right)\left(\overline{c}_{k}\pm\delta_{k}^{n}\right).

(3.33)

After having determined the eigenvalues and the corresponding linearly independent right and left eigenvectors
we finally end up with the eigenvalue decomposition of the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}

{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{n}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{n}

(3.34)

with the block-diagonal matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{n}={\mbox{diag}}({\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{0,n},{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{1,n},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{K,n})

and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{0,n}:=V_{I,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1}
. To verify this decomposition we make use of the identity

{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n}={\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{k}_{I,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{0,n}-{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}^{0}_{I,n}
.

\square

For convenience we summarize the representation of the eigenvectors because we want to investigate the type of the characteristic fields.

Proposition 3.1
.

(Eigenvectors)

Let be
{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}|=1
and let be
{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
l=1,\ldots,d-1
, such that
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{1},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{d-1}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

{{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{j}=\delta_{i,j}
and
{{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}=0
.

1.

(Transport waves) The right and left eigenvalues to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k,i}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
,
i=1,\ldots,d-1
, are

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{k,i}={\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{k,i}=({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{K-1}^{t},\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t}}_{(k-1)-times},({\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{i}^{t},0),\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{T},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{T}}_{(K-k)-times})^{t}.

(3.35)

2.

(Acoustic waves) The right and left eigenvalues to the eigenvalues

\lambda_{k,\pm}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, are

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{k,\pm}

\displaystyle=({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{K-1}^{t},\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t}}_{(k-1)-times},{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{k,\pm}^{t},\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t}}_{(K-k)-times})^{t}

(3.36)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{k,\pm}

\displaystyle=({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}}}_{I,k,\pm}^{t},\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t}}_{(k-1)-times},{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}}}_{k,\pm}^{t},\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t}}_{(K-k)-times})^{t}

(3.37)

with

\displaystyle{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{k,\pm}=(\pm\overline{c}_{k}\rho_{k}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}^{t},\overline{c}_{k}^{2})^{t},

(3.38)

\displaystyle{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}}}_{k,\pm}=0.5\overline{c}_{k}^{-2}(\pm\overline{c}_{k}\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}^{t},1)^{t},\ {\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}}}_{I,k,\pm}=0.5\rho_{k}\overline{c}_{k}^{-2}(\alpha_{k}\sigma_{k})^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}^{\pm}

(3.39)

and the components of the vector
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
determined by (
3.33
).

3.

(Material waves) If the non-resonance condition (
3.14
) holds, then the right and left
eigenvalues to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{I,k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K-1
, are

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{I,k}^{T}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{K-1,k}^{t},({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,k}^{1})^{t},\ldots,({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,k}^{K})^{t}\right)^{t},

(3.40)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}_{I,k}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0}^{-1}({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{K-1,k}^{t},\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{d+1}^{t}}_{K-times})^{t},

(3.41)

where
{\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}}_{I,l}^{k}=({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}}_{k,l}^{t},{\overline{z}}_{k,l})^{t}

is determined by the components (
3.30
) and (
3.31
).
Here
{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{K-1,k}
is the
k
th unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
.

In particular, the left and right eigenvectors are orthonormal.

3.2
Characteristic fields

By means of the eigenvalues and the right eigenvectors we can now characterize the characteristic fields.

Proposition 3.2
.

(Characteristic fields)

1.

The fields corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k,i}
are linearly degenerated.

2.

If the assumptions (
2.26
)
hold, then the acoustic waves are genuinely nonlinear.

3.

If the interfacial velocity is a convex combination of the velocities
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}
, i.e.,

{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k},\quad\beta_{k}\in[0,1],\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}=1

(3.42)

with coefficients

\beta_{k}:=\frac{\overline{\gamma}_{k}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}{{\hat{\rho}}},\quad{\hat{\rho}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{\gamma}_{k}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k},

(3.43)

for some arbitrary but fixed convex combination
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{\gamma}_{k}=1
,

\overline{\gamma}_{k}\in[0,1]
,
then the material waves are linearly degenerated.

Proof.

The characteristic fields corresponding to the different waves are characterized by the directional derivative
\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\lambda({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})
.

Obviously, the derivatives of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k,i}=v_{k,n}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, are

\partial_{\alpha_{l}}\lambda_{k,i}=0,\ \partial_{v_{l,j}}\lambda_{k,i}=\delta_{k,l}\,n_{j},\ \partial_{p_{l}}\lambda_{k,i}=0,\

and, thus, we conclude from the corresponding right eigenvectors (
3.35
)

\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\lambda_{k,i}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{k,i}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})=0,

i.e., these fields are linearly degenerated.

Next we consider the acoustic waves corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k,\pm}=v_{k,n}\pm\overline{c}_{k}
. Obviously, it holds

\partial_{\alpha_{l}}v_{k,n}=0,\ \partial_{v_{l,j}}\lambda_{k,i}=\delta_{k,l}\,n_{j},\ \partial_{p_{l}}v_{k,n}=0.

To determine the derivatives of the
barotropic speed of sound

\overline{c}_{k}
we perform a change of variables
\rho_{k}=\rho_{k}(p_{k})
.
Note that
p_{k}=p_{k}(\rho_{k})
is a monotone function according to the assumption (
2.26a
).
Since it holds

\partial_{*}\overline{c}_{k}^{2}(\rho_{k})=2\overline{c}_{k}(\rho_{k})\partial_{*}\overline{c}_{k}(\rho_{k})=p_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\rho_{k})\partial_{*}\rho_{k},

the derivatives of
c_{k}
with respect to the primitive variables
w
read

\partial_{\alpha_{l}}\overline{c}_{k}=0,\ \partial_{v_{l,j}}\overline{c}_{k}=0,\ \partial_{p_{l}}\overline{c}_{k}=\delta_{k,l}\frac{1}{2\,\overline{c}_{k}^{3}(\rho_{k})}p_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\rho_{k})

and we obtain by the right eigenvectors (
3.36
)

\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\lambda_{k,\pm}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{k,\pm}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})=\pm\frac{\overline{c}_{k}}{\rho_{k}}{\mathcal{G}}_{k}

with
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
the fundamental derivative of gas dynamics for a pure barotropic fluid corresponding to component
k
.
According to the assumptions
(
2.26
)
both the barotropic speed of sound
(
2.27
) and the fundamental derivative (
2.26b
)
do not vanish, i.e., the acoustic waves are genuinely nonlinear.

Finally, we consider the characteristic fields corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{I,k}=V_{I,n}
. From the corresponding right eigenvectors (
3.40
) we conclude

{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{I,i}\cdot\nabla_{{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}}\lambda_{I,i}=\kappa_{0}\left(\partial_{\alpha_{i}}V_{I,n}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left({\overline{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}}_{k,i}\cdot\nabla_{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}V_{I,n}+{\overline{z}}_{k,i}\partial_{p_{k}}V_{I,n}\right)\right).

(3.44)

For the gradient of the interfacial velocity it holds

\partial_{\alpha_{i}}V_{I,n}=\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\delta_{i}^{n}-\frac{\beta_{K}}{\alpha_{K}}\delta^{n}_{K},\ \nabla_{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}V_{I,n}={\mbox{\boldmath$n$}},\ \partial_{p_{k}}V_{I,n}=\frac{\beta_{k}}{\rho_{k}}\frac{1}{\overline{c}^{2}_{k}}\delta_{k}^{n}.

(3.45)

By a straightforward calculation incorporating the coefficients (
3.30
), (
3.31
) and using the convex combination (
3.42
) with coefficients (
3.43
) we can verify that the right-hand side in (
3.44
) vanishes, i.e., the material waves are linearly degenerated.

\square

Remark 3.2
.

If the material field is genuinely nonlinear field, then there is no way to cope with the non-conservative products in the phasic momentum, see Eqn. (
2.4b
). However, if fields associated to the non-conservative products only occur in linearly degenerated fields, then Riemann invariants of the associated field can be enforced and, thus, the exact solution of the Riemann problem exists as has been verified for Baer-Nunziato-type models in
[
12
]
. Therefore, it is suggested in
[
9
,
24
]
to determine the interfacial velocity such that the fields associated with the eigenvalues

,
i=1,\ldots,K-1
, are linearly degenerated.
Motivated by Gallouët et al.
[
9
]
and
Hérard
[
24
]
for a two-phase and a three-phase model, respectively, the interfacial velocity is chosen as a convex combination of the velocities of the components, see Eqn. (
3.42
).
For the coefficients of the convex combination the ansatz of Saleh
[
53
]
, Eqn. (4.3.40), in case of a two-phase mixture can be generalized providing (
3.43
).

3.4
Symmetrization

From Kato’s theorem
[
34
]
it follows that there exists a local-in-time smooth solution to the Cauchy problem of the projected system (
3.4
), if the problem is symmetrizable, i.e., there exists a symmetric positive definite matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}={\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})
such that the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
is symmetric.
It can be verified that the quasi-1D system (
3.4
) can be symmetrized.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Symmetrization)

If the non-resonance condition (
3.14
) holds, then the quasi-1D system (
3.4
) is symmetrizable.

Proof.

To construct a symmetrizer for
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
we first observe that the matrix

{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,n}=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{n}^{t}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}\\[5.69054pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}^{t}&1/(2\overline{c}_{k}^{4})\end{matrix}\right)

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{n}:=({\mbox{\boldmath$n$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{1},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}_{d-1})
an orthonormal matrix and
{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}_{k,n}:={\mbox{diag}}(\lambda_{n,k},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d-1})
a diagonal matrix with entries
\lambda_{k,n}:=\rho_{k}^{2}/(2\overline{c}_{k}^{2})
and
\lambda_{i}:=1
,
i=1,\ldots,d-1

is a symmetrizer of the phasic problem

{\partial_{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{k}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}({\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{k})\,\partial_{\xi}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},

i.e.,
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,n}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix and the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{k,n}
is symmetric.
Then we make the following ansatz for a symmetrizer of (
3.4
):

{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}=\left(\begin{matrix}K\,P_{\alpha,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{K-1}&{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{1,\alpha,n}^{t}&\cdots&{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{K,\alpha,n}^{t}\\[2.84526pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{1,\alpha,n}&{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{1,n}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\[2.84526pt]
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{K,\alpha,n}&&&{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{K,n}\end{matrix}\right),

where

{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}={\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}^{t}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k,n}-V_{I,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}\right)^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}_{k,n}
defined by (
3.6
), (
3.19
), (
3.20
) and (
3.21
).
Note that
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}
is well-defined if the non-resonance condition (
3.14
) holds true. Obviously,
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}
is symmetric.
It turns out that
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{n}
is symmetric whenever
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{k,n}
is symmetric. The latter holds true provided that
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}_{k,n}={\mbox{\boldmath$L$}}_{k,n}^{t}
.
It remains to verify that
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}
is positive definite.
For this purpose we have to verify for any
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}=({\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{\alpha}^{t},{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{K}^{t})^{t}\neq{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
with
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
that
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}
is positive. A straightforward calculus yields

{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}=P_{\alpha,n}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}\left(a_{\alpha,i}+({\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k})_{i}/P_{\alpha,n}\right)^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{\alpha,n}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}}:=P_{\alpha,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}^{t}
.
Since
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k}
is symmetric positive definite, the Cholesky decomposition
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$C$}}_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$C$}}_{k}^{t}
exists. Furthermore, the matrix

{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{k}:={\mbox{\boldmath$C$}}_{k}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k,\alpha,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$C$}}_{k}^{t}
is symmetric and, thus, there exists an orthogonal matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{k}
such that

{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{k}^{t}={\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}_{k}
where
{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}_{k}
is a diagonal matrix with the eigenvalues
\mu^{k}_{i}
of
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{k}
as entries. Then we obtain

{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}(P_{\alpha,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}-{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}_{k}){\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}b_{k,i}^{2}\left(P_{\alpha,n}-\mu_{i}^{k}\right)

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}:={\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$C$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}
. Choosing
P_{\alpha,n}>\max_{i,k}\left\{|\mu^{k}_{i}|\right\}>0
the term
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}
is non-negative and is positive for
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{k}\neq{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
.

\square

The idea of the proof is very similar to the proof in case of the quasi-1D full Baer-Nunziato model, see
[

]
. However, there the proof only works for the genuine 1D case, i.e.,
d=1
, because the symmetrizer for the corresponding phasic matrices

was chosen too specific.
It is worthwhile mentioning that modifying the symmetrizer
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}_{k}
for the phasic model in
[

]
, Section 4.3, replacing
0.5(\rho_{k}/\overline{c}_{k})^{2}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d}
by
{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{n}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}_{k,n}{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}_{n}^{t}
, the proof also works for the quasi-1D case.
Furthermore, we would like to mention that Theorem
3.3
generalizes the result for the genuinely 1D case in
[

]
.

4
Mathematical Entropy

From a mathematical point of view, the concept of entropy-entropy flux pairs, cf.
[
13
]
, has been introduced to
characterize a unique weak solution of an initial (boundary) value problem of (inhomogeneous) conservation laws
that in quasi-conservative form reads

{\partial_{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\partial_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\partial_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}):=\partial_{{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}}{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})

(4.1)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\to D\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}_{i}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, denote the vector of
m
conserved quantities, the fluxes in the
i
th coordinate direction,
i=1,\ldots,d
, and the source function, respectively.
Here the last term on the right-hand side contains the non-conservative products in the original system determined by
(
2.2
), (
2.4
), (
2.8
).
Motivated by thermodynamics, the entropy inequality

{\partial_{t}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\leq 0

(4.2)

has to hold in a weak sense for any convex function
U:D\to\mathbb{R}
and functions
F_{i}:D\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, referred to as entropy and entropy flux, that satisfy the compatibility conditions

\nabla_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}$}}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})=\nabla_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}$}}F_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T},\ i=1,\ldots,d.

(4.3)

Due to these conditions we infer for smooth solutions of (
4.1
) the entropy equation

{\partial_{t}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}F_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})=\nabla_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}$}}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T}\,\left({\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\partial_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\right),

(4.4)

Obviously, the entropy inequality (
4.2
) holds if and only if the entropy production is non-positive, i.e.,

\nabla_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}$}}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T}\,\left({\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\partial_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\right)\leq 0.

(4.5)

A sufficient condition is given by

\nabla_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}$}}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\leq 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}$}}U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\partial_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}=0.

(4.6)

4.1
Isothermal case

For an isothermal fluid we have to account for heat exchange in the energy equation.
Similar to the case of isothermal Euler equations, cf.
[
58
]
, we therefore have to start from the full Baer-Nunziato system to derive an appropriate entropy-entropy flux pair. This can be found in

[
41
]
. Neglecting viscosity but accounting for heat exchange the full model reads

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\alpha_{k}}+{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{k}}=S_{\alpha,k},

(4.7a)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})}=S_{\alpha\rho,k},

(4.7b)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{T}+\alpha_{k}\,p_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}})}+\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{\nabla\alpha_{l}}={\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k},

(4.7c)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,E_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))}+\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}=-{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}+S_{\alpha\rho E,k}

(4.7d)

with the heat flux
{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k}
and total
E_{k}=e_{k}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2
determined by
the internal energy
e_{k}
and kinetic energy
u_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2
. This model is complemented
for isothermal fluids by the EoS

p_{k}=p_{k}(\rho_{k})\quad\mbox{and}\quad e_{k}=e_{k}(\rho_{k}).

(4.8)

From (
4.7
) we derive equations for the density and internal energy

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\rho_{k}}+\frac{\rho_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\,{({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I})}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{k}}+{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\cdot{\nabla\rho_{k}}+\rho_{k}{\nabla\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}=S_{\rho,k},

(4.9)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}e_{k}}+{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\cdot({\nabla e_{k}})-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}{P_{k,l}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}\right)}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}+\frac{p_{k}}{\rho_{k}}{\nabla\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}+S_{e,k}

(4.10)

with relaxation terms

\displaystyle S_{\rho,k}:=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\left(S_{\alpha\rho,k}-\rho_{k}S_{\alpha,k}\right),

(4.11)

\displaystyle S_{e,k}:=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}\left(S_{\alpha\rho E,k}-{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}+S_{\alpha\rho,k}(u_{k}-e_{k})\right).

(4.12)

In order to derive the evolution equation for the entropy

s_{k}=s_{k}(\rho_{k})

we rewrite
(
2.17
) as

T_{ref}ds_{k}=de_{k}-\frac{p_{k}}{\rho_{k}^{2}}d\rho_{k}=\left(e_{k}^{\prime}-\frac{p_{k}}{\rho_{k}^{2}}\right)d\rho_{k},

(4.13)

where due to (
2.7
)

\displaystyle T_{k}=T_{ref}=const,\quad\forall\,k=1,\ldots,K.

By means of the evolution equations (
4.9
) and
(
4.10
) for the density
and the internal energy we then deduce the entropy law

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}s_{k}}+{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}}\cdot{\nabla s_{k}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}\left(\Pi_{k}-\frac{1}{T_{ref}}{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}\right)+S_{s,k}

(4.14)

with relaxation term

\displaystyle S_{s,k}:=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}T_{ref}}\left(\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}S_{e,k}-\frac{p_{k}}{\rho_{k}}S_{\alpha\rho,k}+p_{k}S_{\alpha,k}\right)

(4.15)

and production term

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}:=\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\left(\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}{P_{k,l}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}\right)}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}+p_{k}{({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I})}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{k}}\right).

(4.16)

Finally, we obtain together with (
4.7b
) for the volume specific entropy

{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}s_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}s_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})}+\frac{1}{T_{ref}}{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}=\Pi_{k}S_{\alpha\rho s,k}

(4.17)

with relaxation term

S_{\alpha\rho s,k}:=\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}S_{s,k}+s_{k}S_{\alpha\rho,k}.

(4.18)

Next we determine from the energy equation (
4.7d
) the heat flux

\displaystyle{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}=S_{\alpha\rho E,k}-{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,E_{k})}-{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))}-\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}.

(4.19)

Plugging the heat flux into the entropy equation (
4.17
)
we obtain

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,(T_{ref}s_{k}-E_{k}))}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(T_{ref}s_{k}-E_{k}-p_{k}/\rho_{k}))}=

(4.20)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378ptT_{ref}\Pi_{k}+\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}+T_{ref}S_{\alpha\rho E,k}.

Summing these evolution equations over all components and using the conservation constraint
(
2.10

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,(E_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k})\right)}+{\nabla\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))\right)}=

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt-T_{ref}\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\alpha\rho s,k}-T_{ref}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Pi_{k}.

(4.21)

Comparing (
4.21
) with (

\displaystyle U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,(E_{k}-T_{ref}\,s_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,(f_{k}+1/2{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}),

(4.22a)

\displaystyle F_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(g_{k}+1/2{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}))

(4.22b)

with the specific free energy
f_{k}
and Gibbs free energy

\displaystyle f_{k}=e_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k},\quad g_{k}=f_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}.

(4.23)

The compatibility conditions (
4.3
) hold by derivation of (

in case of the full Baer-Nunziato model.

Theorem 4.1
.

(Convexity of entropy function)

Let
e_{k}
be a convex function of
\tau_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Then the Hessian of
U
is positive semi-definite, i.e., the entropy
U
is a convex function of
u
but not necessarily strictly convex.

Proof.

To verify that

U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}(e_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}U_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k})

(4.24)

is a convex function of the quantities
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}:=({\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{t},\alpha_{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{1}^{t},\ldots,\alpha_{K}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}^{t})^{t}
with

{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K-1})^{t}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}:=(\rho_{k},\rho_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{t})^{t}

we proceed in analogy to
[
41
]
for the full multi-component Baer-Nunziato model but using an isothermal fluid.

For this purpose,
we need to prove that the Hessian is positive semi-definite. First of all, we note that by (
2.1
)

\frac{\partial\alpha_{k}}{\partial\alpha_{l}}=\delta_{k,l}-\delta_{k,K},\quad\frac{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}}{\partial\alpha_{l}}=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}(\delta_{k,l}-\delta_{k,K}),\ \frac{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}}{\partial\alpha_{l}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\delta_{k,l}{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d+1}

holds for
k=1,\ldots,K,\ l=1,\ldots,K-1
. Then it follows for the gradient of
U

\frac{\partial U}{\partial\alpha_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})=U_{l}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l})-U_{K}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})-{\frac{\partial U_{l}}{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l})}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}+{\frac{\partial U_{K}}{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K},\ \frac{\partial U}{\partial\alpha_{l}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})=\frac{\partial U_{l}}{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}).

(4.25)

The Hessian of
U
is determined by the second order derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}\partial\alpha_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{k,l}\frac{1}{\alpha_{l}}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}^{t}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{l}}{\partial^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}){\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}^{t}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{K}}{\partial^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}){\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K},\ k,l=1,\ldots,K,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}\partial\alpha_{l}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\alpha_{l}}(\delta_{k,l}-\delta_{K,l})\frac{\partial^{2}U_{l}}{\partial^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}){\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l},l=1,\ldots,K,\ k=1,\ldots,K-1,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}\partial\alpha_{l}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{l}}\delta_{k,l}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{l}}{\partial^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{l}),\ k,l=1,\ldots,K.

For a compact representation of the Hessian we introduce the notation

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}\partial\alpha_{l}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\right)_{l,k=1,\ldots,K-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)\times(K-1)},

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}\partial\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)},

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}\partial\alpha_{1}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}),\ldots,\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}\partial\alpha_{K-1}}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(K-1)}.

According to the above second order derivatives these are determined by

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{K}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}_{K-1}+{\mbox{diag}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}\right)_{k=1,\ldots,K-1}\right),

(4.26)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime},

(4.27)

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}(\delta_{k,l}-\delta_{K,k}){\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}\right)_{l=1,\ldots,K-1}={\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}},\alpha_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}}^{t},

(4.28)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}
denotes the Hessian of the entropy
U_{k}=U_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k})
of component
k
.
Then the Hessian can be represented as block-matrix

{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}^{\prime\prime}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})=\begin{pmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\alpha_{1}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{1}$}}&\ldots&{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\alpha_{1}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}$}}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{1}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{1}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{1}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{1}$},\alpha_{1}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{1}$}}&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{K}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}&&&{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{1}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}$},\alpha_{K}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}$}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(4.29)

To verify positive semi-definiteness of the Hessian we introduce the vector
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}=({\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t},{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{1}^{t},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{K}^{t})^{t}

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
. Then we obtain by the block-structure
(
4.29
) of the Hessian

{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}\right).

(4.30)

By means of (
4.26
), (
4.27
) and (
4.28
) we determine

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}(a{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{K}^{\prime\prime}(a{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}(a_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k})^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}\,(a_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}),\ a:=\sum_{l=1}^{K-1}a_{l},

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}=-\sum_{l=1}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}(\delta_{k,l}-\delta_{k,K}){\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}\,(a_{l}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}),

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}\delta_{k,K}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{K}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{K}^{\prime\prime}\,(a{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}(1-\delta_{k,K}){\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}\,(a_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}),

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$},\alpha_{k}\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}.

Incorporating this into (
4.30
) we finally conclude after some calculus with

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}({\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}-a_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k})^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}\,({\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}-a_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k})+\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}({\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{K}-a{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{K}^{\prime\prime}\,({\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{K}-a{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}).

Finally, we check that the Hessians
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}
are positive semi-definite where
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}
is introduced in
(
4.24
).
For this purpose we consider the energy and the entropy to be given in terms of volume and temperature, i.e.
\tilde{e}_{k}(\tau_{k},T)=e_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k}(\tau_{k},T))
. The first derivatives of the energy and the pressure with respect to the volume are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{e}_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}=\frac{\partial e_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}+\frac{\partial e_{k}}{\partial s_{k}}\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{thermo-pres-temp}}}{{=}}-p_{k}+T_{ref}\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\tau_{k}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sound-fdt-T-tau}}}{{=}}-\frac{\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}}{\tau_{k}^{2}}.

(4.31)

From this we conclude

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{k}}(\rho_{k}(e_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k}))

\displaystyle=e_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k}+\frac{p_{k}}{\rho_{k}},

(4.32)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho_{k}^{2}}(\rho_{k}(e_{k}-T_{ref}s_{k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}}{\rho_{k}}>0.

(4.33)

For the Hessian we then determine

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\left(\begin{matrix}\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}&&-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{t}\\
-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}&&{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}_{d}\end{matrix}\right)

(4.34)

that can be easily verified to be positive definite. This implies
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\geq 0
.
Note that for
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\neq{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
we cannot ensure

{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}^{\prime\prime}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}
to be positive even if
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}
is strictly convex because all the terms
{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{k}-a_{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K-1
, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{K}-a{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K}
may vanish at the same time.

\square

Theorem 4.2
.

(Entropy production due to interfacial states)

For any convex combination (
3.42
) for the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}

there uniquely exist interfacial pressures

P_{k,l}=\beta_{k}p_{l}+p_{k}(1-\beta_{k})

such that the production term

In particular, the interfacial pressures are all positive and

P_{I}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(1-\beta_{k}).

(4.36)

Proof.

The proof is in complete analogy to the proof of Theorem 6 in

for the full multi-component Baer-Nunziato model
assuming local thermal equilibrium where we have to replace the temperatures

Thus, the resulting interfacial pressures (

. We emphasize that the resulting interfacial states are the same for both isentropic and isothermal fluids, see Theorem

\square

We would like to mention that the above result is motivated by Gallouët et al.

Using for the convex combination (

then the interfacial pressure and the interfacial velocity are given by

This closure of the interfacial states ensures consistency with an entropy inequality

For special choices of

For

Obviously,

4.1.2
Entropy production due to relaxation

To be admissible with the entropy inequality (
4.6
) the production term

S_{U}:=-T_{ref}\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\alpha\rho s,k}

(4.41)

has to be
non-positive.
Here we use (
4.18
), (
4.15
) and (
4.12
) to determine the
phasic
terms

S_{\alpha\rho s,k}=\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\left(S_{\alpha\rho E,k}+\left(\frac{1}{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}-g_{k}\right)S_{\alpha\rho,k}+p_{k}S_{\alpha,k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k}\right).

(4.42)

Note that in abuse of physical notation we call this term entropy production rather than energy production with respect to the mathematical concept of entropy-entropy flux pairs because energy is known to be conserved.
In the following we consider one-by-one the contributions of mechanical relaxation and chemical relaxation.
The analysis is similar to
[
41
]
in case of the physical entropy production.
Note that all the relaxation processes are in agreement with the constraints due to conservation (
2.9
).

Theorem 4.3
.

(Entropy production due to mechanical relaxation)

Let the relaxation parameters be non-negative, i.e.,
\theta_{p}
,
\theta_{v}\geq 0
.
Then the entropy production due to mechanical relaxation is
non-positive,
i.e.,

S_{U}^{p,v}=T_{ref}\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{\alpha\rho s,k}^{p,v}\leq 0.

(4.43)

Proof.

According to (
4.42
)
and (
2.45
), (
2.46
)
the phasic entropy production terms for velocity and pressure relaxation are determined by

S^{v}_{\alpha\rho s,k}=-\theta_{v}\frac{\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}}{T_{ref}}({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})^{2},\quad S^{p}_{\alpha\rho s,k}=-\theta_{p}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{T_{ref}}(p-p_{k})^{2}.

(4.44)

This immediately implies the assertion.

\square

For the relaxation of chemical potentials we discuss different scenarios for two-phase and three-phase models.

In case of the two-component mixture, the entropy production term due to relaxation of the chemical potentials is determined by (
4.42
)
and (
2.48
) as

S^{\mu}_{\alpha\rho s,k}=(-1)^{k}\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\left(\epsilon-g_{k}+\frac{p_{k}}{\varrho}-\frac{1}{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}})^{2}\right),\quad k=1,2.

(4.45)

However choosing the parameters
\epsilon
,
\varrho
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
appropriately
the sum of the entropy production terms is non-negative.

Theorem 4.4
.

(Entropy production due to relaxation of Gibbs free energies)

Let the relaxation parameter
\theta_{\mu}
be positive.
The mass flux is chosen as the kinetic relation

\displaystyle\dot{m}=a(g_{2}-g_{1}),\ a\geq 0.

(4.46)

Choosing in (
2.48
) the parameters

\displaystyle\beta_{1}^{v}=\beta_{2}^{v}=\frac{1}{2},\quad\varrho=\frac{1}{c}\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{g_{2}-g_{1}},\ c\leq 1,

(4.47)

then the entropy production is
non-positive,
i.e.,

S^{\mu}_{U}=-T_{ref}\sum_{k=1}^{2}S^{\mu}_{\alpha\rho s,k}\leq 0.

(4.48)

Proof.

First of all, we note that by (
4.47
) the velocity
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
is the average of the phasic velocities and, thus, the entropy production does not depend on the velocity because

\frac{1}{2}\left((\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1})^{2}-(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2})^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1})\cdot(2\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2})={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}.

(4.49)

Then the entropy production reduces to

S^{\mu}_{U}=-\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\left(g_{2}-g_{1}+\frac{p_{1}-p_{2}}{\varrho}\right)=-\theta_{\mu}\,a\,(g_{2}-g_{1})^{2}(1-c)\leq 0.

Thus, the energy production
S_{U}^{\mu}
is
non-positive.

\square

Note that the kinetic relation (
4.46
) is in agreement with the kinetic relation in

[
7
]
. Furthermore, choosing
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
as the average of the phasic velocities is reasonable because the entropy production (
4.48
) should be a product of the relaxed mass flux
\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}
and an interfacial entropy should not depend on any velocity.

Note that (
4.37
)
and the mixture velocities (
2.49
) with coefficients (
4.47
) differ. In
[
26
]
it was recommended to use different interfacial velocities for the convective system and the relaxation terms. This is admissible because the conservation constraint (
2.9
) is satisfied for any convex combination (
3.42
).
Moreover, the source term cannot be derived from the ensemble averaging procedure, see
[
6
]
, Chapter 11, but the averaged model has to be closed
by modeling these terms appropriately. Therefore, we are free to choose another velocity in the chemical relaxation model.
Note that in the Drew-Passman model different interfacial velocities have been introduced in the evolution equations for volume fraction, momentum and energy, see
[
6
]
,
Eqns. (11.8), (11.39) and (11.41).

In case of the three-component mixture, the entropy production terms due to the relaxation of chemical potentials is determined by (
4.59
)
and (
2.50
) as

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{\alpha\rho s,1}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\left(\epsilon_{1}-g_{1}+\frac{p_{1}}{\varrho_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1})^{2}\right),

(4.50a)

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{\alpha\rho s,2}=-\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\left(\epsilon_{2}-g_{2}-\frac{p_{2}}{\varrho_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2})^{2}\right),

(4.50b)

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{\alpha\rho s,3}=\theta_{\mu}\,\dot{m}\,\frac{1}{T_{ref}}\left(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}-p_{3}(\frac{1}{\varrho_{1}}+\frac{1}{\varrho_{2}})\right).

(4.50c)

The phasic relaxation terms may become negative also when
\theta_{\mu}
and
\dot{m}
are positive.
However, choosing the parameters
\varrho_{1}
,
\varrho_{2}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
appropriately the total production term is non-negative.

In particular, we are interested in the three-component model, i.e.,
K=3
, with water vapor (
k=1
), liquid water (
k=2
) and inert gas (
k=3
).
The source terms describing the mass transfer depend on the chemical potentials of the vapor and the liquid. In the model under consideration the gas is a mixture of water vapor and some other constituent, where both components are modeled as an ideal gas. Accordingly the chemical potential of the vapor is given by

\mu_{1}=g_{1}+(\gamma_{1}-1)c_{v,1}T_{ref}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}}\right).

(4.51)

In the special case of vanishing third component, i.e.,
\alpha_{3}=0
,
the chemical potential of the vapor reduces to the vapor Gibbs free energy.
The chemical potential
\mu_{2}
of the liquid equals its Gibbs free energy
g_{2}
, i.e.,

\mu_{2}=g_{2}\,.

In chemical equilibrium the chemical potentials of the vapor and the liquid
equal each other.
For details see the book of Müller and Müller
[
38
]
, Section 8.2.4.

In the following we consider a homogeneous mixture where velocity, pressure and temperature are in equilibrium.
In the total entropy we therefore additionally have to account for the mixture entropy given by

S_{M}=-\sum_{k=1,3}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}(\gamma_{k}-1)c_{v,k}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}}\right),

(4.52)

see
[
32
]
, p. 54, 298, 320.
Again, the chemical potential of the vapor phase is given by (
4.51
) and
the chemical potential of the liquid phase equals its Gibbs free energy, i.e.,
\mu_{2}=g_{2}
.
In chemical equilibrium the chemical potentials of the vapor and the liquid phase equal each other.
Accordingly, the mass flux is now a function of
\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}
, i.e.,

\dot{m}=a(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})

(4.53)

with
a\geq 0

that again is an agreement with the kinetic relation in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

(Entropy production due to relaxation of chemical potentials)

For a homogeneous mixture, i.e.,
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}={\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}=const
,
p_{k}=p=const
and
T_{k}=T=const
, the total entropy production
is
non-positive,
i.e.,

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{U}-T_{ref}S^{\mu}_{S_{M}}\leq 0,

(4.54)

if the relaxation parameter
\theta_{\mu}

as well as the equilibrium temperature
T
are positive.

Proof.

For a homogeneous mixture the entropy production
(
4.41
) reduces to

S^{\mu}_{U}=-\dot{m}\,\theta_{\mu}\,(g_{2}-g_{1})=-\dot{m}\,\theta_{\mu}\,\left(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}+(\gamma_{1}-1)c_{v,1}T_{ref}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}}\right)\right).

In analogy to the derivation of the entropy production, we determine the entropy production of the mixture entropy
S_{M}

S^{\mu}_{S_{M}}=-\dot{m}\,\theta_{\mu}\,(\gamma_{1}-1)c_{v,1}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}}\right).

(4.55)

Then the total entropy production is given by

S^{\mu}_{U}-T_{ref}S^{\mu}_{S_{M}}=-\dot{m}\,\theta_{\mu}\,\left(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\right).

(4.56)

Thus, by (
4.53
) we conclude (
4.54
).

\square

4.2
Isentropic case

In contrast to the isothermal case, we do not have to account for heat exchange in the energy equation.
Since for isentropic fluids the “energies”
{\overline{e}}_{k}
coincide with the energies
e_{k}
, see Remark
2.1
,
we follow Hérard’s original work
[
25
]

for barotropic fluids
and choose
for the mathematical entropy function the total specific energy
E_{k}=e_{k}(\rho_{k})+0.5{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}
with internal energy
e_{k}
determined by

\displaystyle e_{k}^{\prime}(\rho_{k})=\frac{p_{k}(\rho_{k})}{\rho_{k}^{2}}\quad\mbox{or, equiv., }\quad e_{k}^{\prime}(\tau_{k})=-p_{k}(\tau_{k})

(4.57)

motivated by (
2.14
).
For smooth solutions of the model
(
2.2
), (
2.4
)
we obtain the evolution equations for the fractional total energies

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,E_{k})}+{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))}=\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}+{\overline{\Pi}}_{k},

(4.58)

with relaxation term
\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}
and production term
{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}

\displaystyle\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}

\displaystyle=(e_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}-0.5{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2})S_{\alpha\rho,k}-p_{k}S_{\alpha,k}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}_{\alpha\rho\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}$},k},

(4.59)

\displaystyle{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}

\displaystyle=p_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})\cdot\nabla\alpha_{k}-\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{l}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}\left(p_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})+P_{k,l}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\ \right)\cdot\nabla\alpha_{l}.

(4.60)

We emphasize that the relaxation term
\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}
is different from the corresponding term determined by the relaxation models (
2.45
), (
2.46
),
(
2.48
) and (
2.50
).
It is interesting to note that in contrast to the single-phase case the energy equation is not redundant in the multi-component case due to the non-conservative products and the relaxation terms.
Similar to the isothermal case, see Eqn. (
4.19
), we determine the heat flux as

\displaystyle{\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{k})}=S_{\alpha\rho E,k}-\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}-\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}P_{k,l}\,{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}}\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}-{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}

(4.61)

comparing the difference of
(
2.8
) and (
4.58
).

Summing these evolution equations over all components we obtain the evolution equation of the total energy

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,E_{k}\right)}+{\nabla\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}\,(E_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k}))\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\overline{\Pi}}_{k},

(4.62)

Comparing (
4.62
) with (
4.4
) we may choose for the entropy-entropy flux pair

\displaystyle U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}E_{k},

(4.63a)

\displaystyle F_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\alpha_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,v_{k,i}\,(E_{k}+p_{k}/\rho_{k})),\ i=1,\ldots,d.

(4.63b)

The compatibility conditions (
4.3
) hold by derivation of (
4.62
).
In the isentropic case
non-strict convexity of
U
has been proven in
[
54
]
.
Alternatively, the proof of Theorem
4.1
for the isothermal case
also applies in case of isentropic fluids where

U({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\rho_{k}(e_{k}+{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}^{2}/2)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}U_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{k})

(4.64)

with

e_{k}=e_{k}(\tau_{k},s_{k})
and
s_{k}=const
.
For the Hessian
{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}_{k}^{\prime\prime}
we again obtain (
4.34
).
Then convexity of

e_{k}
with respect to
\tau_{k}

according to (
2.16
) ensures that the Hessian is positive semi-definite.

We emphasize that in case of an isothermal fluid the mixture total energy is
not
an admissible entropy function because (
4.58
) does not account for heat exchange.
To ensure the entropy inequality (
4.6
) we have to verify that the right-hand side of (
4.62
) is not positive.
This will provide us with admissibility criteria for the interfacial pressures as well as the relaxation terms.

4.2.1
Entropy production due to interfacial velocity and pressure

Obviously, we cannot control the sign of
\Pi_{k}
. Therefore we determine
the interfacial pressures
P_{k,l}
and the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
such
that the sum
{\overline{\Pi}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}
vanishes and the conservation constraints (
2.10
) or, equivalently, (
2.11
) hold.
Assuming that the interfacial velocity can be written as a linear combination of the velocities of the components according to (
3.42
) there exists a unique closure for the interfacial pressures.

Theorem 4.6
.

(Entropy production due to interfacial states)

For any convex combination (
3.42
) for the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}

there uniquely exist interfacial pressures

P_{k,l}=\beta_{k}p_{l}+p_{k}(1-\beta_{k})

(4.65)

such that the production term
{\overline{\Pi}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}
vanishes and
the conservation constraints (
2.10
) or, equivalently, (
2.11
) hold. In particular, the interfacial pressures are all positive and

P_{I}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(1-\beta_{k}).

(4.66)

Proof.

First of all, we note that by the conservation constraint (
2.10
) and the definition (
4.60
) of
{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}
it holds

\displaystyle{\overline{\Pi}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\overline{\Pi}}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1,\neq k}^{K}(P_{k,l}-p_{k})({\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k})\cdot{\nabla\alpha_{l}}.

(4.67)

Obviously, this term coincides with
T_{ref}\Pi=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Pi_{k}
with
\Pi_{k}
determined by (
4.16
) in the isothermal case.
Since by the saturation constraint (
2.1
) we may substitute

{\nabla\alpha_{K}}
by the linearly independent gradients

{\nabla\alpha_{k}}
,
k=1,\ldots,K-1
, we may rewrite
{\overline{\Pi}}
in terms of the linearly independent gradients.
Then
{\overline{\Pi}}
vanishes if and only if the cofactors vanish, i.e.,

\sum_{k=1,k\neq l}^{K-1}\left((P_{k,l}-p_{k})-(P_{k,K}-p_{k})\right)({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I})+(P_{K,l}-p_{K})({\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{K}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I})={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\quad l=1,\ldots,K-1.

(4.68)

Next we use that
the interfacial velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
is a convex combination of the single component velocities
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}
, i.e.,
(
3.42
) holds. Then we may rewrite the velocity differences
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{k}-{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}_{I}
in terms of the differences
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{i+1}
,
i=1,\ldots,K-1
.
Rearranging (
4.68
) in terms of these
yields
(K-1)^{2}
equations for the

unknowns

,
k,l=1,\ldots,K
,

. Additional

). Altogether we obtain a linear system of equations for the interfacial pressures.
This system is identical to the linear system derived in
[
41
]
, Eqns. (6.20) and (6.22), where the temperatures are formally replaced by 1.
This system has a unique solution. The proof is identical to the one of the analogue theorem in
[
41
]
in case of
the full Baer-Nunziato model.

\square

We would like to mention that in

4.2.2
Entropy production due to relaxation

To be admissible with the
entropy inequality (

) the production
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}
has to be non-positive.
Note that in abuse of physical notation we call this term entropy production rather than energy production with respect to the mathematical concept of entropy-entropy flux pairs because energy is known to be conserved.
In the following we consider one-by-one the contributions of
pressure and velocity relaxation.
The analysis is similar to
[
41
]
in case of the physical entropy production.
Note that all the relaxation processes are in agreement with
the constraints due to conservation (
2.9
).

Theorem 4.7
.

(Entropy production due to pressure relaxation)

Let the relaxation parameter be non-negative, i.e.,
\theta_{p}\geq 0
. Furthermore, let
the coefficients
d_{k,l}
be positive and symmetric, i.e.

\displaystyle 0\leq d_{k,l}=d_{l,k},\quad l,k=1,\ldots,K,\ l\neq k.

(4.69)

Then the entropy production due to
pressure relaxation (
2.47a
) is non-positive, i.e.,

S_{U}^{p}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{U,k}^{p}\leq 0

(4.70)

with entropy production of component
k=1,\ldots,K

\displaystyle S_{U,k}^{p}=\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}^{p}=-\theta_{p}p_{k}\sum_{l=1}^{K}d_{k,l}(p_{k}-p_{l}).

(4.71)

In particular, this holds true for the model (
2.45
).

Proof.

First of all, we note that by symmetry of arbitrary coefficients
a_{k,l}=a_{l,k}
it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{l=k+1}^{K}a_{k,l}=\sum_{l=2}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}a_{k,l}=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}a_{l,k}

From this we conclude with
a_{k,l}=p_{k}^{2}d_{k,l}
and
a_{k,l}=p_{k}d_{k,l}p_{l}

\displaystyle\sum_{l,k=1,l\neq k}^{K}p_{k}^{2}d_{k,l}=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}p_{k}^{2}d_{k,l}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{l=k+1}^{K}p_{k}^{2}d_{k,l}=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(p_{k}^{2}+p_{l}^{2})d_{k,l},

\displaystyle\sum_{l,k=1,l\neq k}^{K}p_{k}p_{l}d_{k,l}=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}p_{k}p_{l}d_{k,l}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\sum_{l=k+1}^{K}p_{k}p_{l}d_{k,l}=2\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}p_{k}p_{l}d_{k,l}.

Combining these results we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{l,k=1,l\neq k}^{K}p_{k}d_{k,l}(p_{k}-p_{l})=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}d_{k,l}(p_{k}^{2}-2p_{k}p_{l}+p_{l}^{2})=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}d_{k,l}(p_{k}-p_{l})^{2}.

Obviously, this term is non-negative and vanishes only if all pressures coincide. Thus, by assumption
the energy production
\overline{S}_{\alpha\rho E,k}^{p}
is non-positive.
In particular, this holds true for the special model (
2.45
) because
d_{k,l}=\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}
satisfies the constraint (
4.69
)

\square

Since the proof holds for an arbitrary number of components

the theorem generalizes the result in
[
25
]
for
K=4
. For
K=2
the result is well-known.

Relaxation to equilibrium

The main objective of our work is to develop new, efficient and robust relaxation procedures. For this purpose

In the cases (i-a) and (ii-a) a bisection method is provided to find the equilibrium state. Due to the fact that the pressure relaxation method is simple and always gives a unique, physical solution,

Condensation.

If a condensation process is identified by

\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0}

value for this expression is
\alpha_{1}^{*}\rho_{1}^{*}=tol>0.

Using the pressure relaxation procedure with
\alpha_{1}^{*}\rho_{1}^{*}
instead of
\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0}

we determine the corresponding values for all
variables of the phases. Using these data one has to check the sign of the difference of the chemical potentials. If still

holds, then
nearly total condensation will occur. We keep
\alpha_{1}^{*}\rho_{1}^{*}=tol>0
and the corresponding data.
Otherwise the interval
[\alpha_{1}^{*}\rho_{1}^{*}=tol,\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0}]

is admissible for the bisection method.

Evaporation.

If an evaporation process is identified by

f_{\mu}(\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0})=\mu_{1}(\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0})-g_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0})<0

the expression
\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0}
is too small
and
\alpha_{1}\rho_{1}
has to increase.
The largest admissible
value for this expression is
\alpha_{1}^{*}\rho_{1}^{*}=M-tol.

Analogously to the previous case we determine the corresponding values for all
variables of the phases and check the sign of the difference of the chemical potentials. Either we identify nearly total evaporation and keep the data or we find the admissible interval
[\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0},M-tol]

for the bisection method.

5.2
Isentropic case

Similar to isothermal fluids we have to solve the
system of ODEs (

) for the pressure relaxation process implying conservation of mass and momentum (

For the full multi-component Baer-Nunziato model
pressure relaxation has been investigated in

[
35
]

for two- and multi-component models. Lallemand et al.
[
35
]
give eight different procedures
based on a closure of the interfacial pressure that become physically non-admissible for more than two components, see
[

These procedures predict different values for the equilibrium pressure.

Pressure-temperature relaxation.

Alternatively, uniqueness can be enforced by the additional condition of temperature equilibrium.
For this purpose it was suggested in
[
17
]
to simultaneously relax
pressure and temperature and, thus, determine a unique equilibrium pressure by avoiding iteration processes. This results in an efficient and stable procedure.
However, for isentropic fluids this approach does not work as can be verified for ideal gases:
in the equilibrium state it must hold

\displaystyle p_{k}^{\infty}=p^{\infty},\,T_{k}^{\infty}=T^{\infty},\,k=1,\dots,K.

(5.45)

Assume
K=2
and both components to be ideal gases, see Example
2.3
with
\pi_{k}=0
. Then from the equations of state we have the relations

\displaystyle p_{k}=A_{k}\rho_{k}^{\gamma_{k}},\,T_{k}=B_{k}\rho_{k}^{\gamma_{k}-1}

with constants

A_{k}
,
B_{k}
determined by (
2.38

From (
5.45
) and (
5.46
) we obtain

\displaystyle(\rho_{1}^{\infty})^{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}}=\left(\frac{A_{2}}{A_{1}}\right)^{\gamma_{2}-1}\left(\frac{B_{1}}{B_{2}}\right)^{\gamma_{2}}\,.

(5.47)

Obviously, for
\gamma_{1}\neq\gamma_{2}
the parameters of the equations of state define a unique equilibrium density, that does not depend on the initial state. Analogously one can find
\rho_{2}^{\infty}
. Using
(
5.14a
)
one can obtain the corresponding volume fractions
\alpha_{1}^{\infty}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\infty}
, that usually will
not
satisfy the saturation condition

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{\infty}+\alpha_{2}^{\infty}=1\,.

(5.48)

Pressure relaxation.

Therefore for isentropic fluids we relax the pressure without simultaneously relaxing the temperature, i.e.,
in the equilibrium state it must hold

\displaystyle p_{k}^{\infty}=p^{\infty},\,k=1,\dots,K.

(5.49)

Assume all
K
components to be stiffened gases. Then from the equations of state we have the relations

\displaystyle p_{k}+\pi_{k}=A_{k}\rho_{k}^{\gamma_{k}}

(5.50)

with constant

A_{k}
determined by (
2.38a
).
Then conservation of mass
(
5.14a

and the equilibrium conditions (

\displaystyle A_{1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0}}{\alpha_{1}^{\infty}}\right)^{\gamma_{1}}-\pi_{1}=A_{k}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}^{0}\rho_{k}^{0}}{\alpha_{k}^{\infty}}\right)^{\gamma_{k}}-\pi_{k},\,k=2,\dots,K\,.

(5.51)

Solving these equations for
\alpha_{k}^{\infty}
gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{\infty}=\left[\frac{A_{k}(\alpha_{k}^{0}\rho_{k}^{0})^{\gamma_{k}}(\alpha_{1}^{\infty})^{\gamma_{1}}}{A_{1}(\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0})^{\gamma_{1}}+(\pi_{k}-\pi_{1})(\alpha_{1}^{\infty})^{\gamma_{1}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}}

The numbering of the components should be chosen such that

\min_{k}\{\pi_{k},k=1,\dots,K\}=\pi_{1}.

Using (

\displaystyle F(\alpha_{1}^{\infty})=\alpha_{1}^{\infty}+\sum_{k=2}^{K}\left[\frac{A_{k}(\alpha_{k}^{0}\rho_{k}^{0})^{\gamma_{k}}(\alpha_{1}^{\infty})^{\gamma_{1}}}{A_{1}(\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0})^{\gamma_{1}}+(\pi_{k}-\pi_{1})(\alpha_{1}^{\infty})^{\gamma_{1}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}}-1=0.

\displaystyle\min_{k=1,\ldots,K}p_{0,k}\leq p^{\infty}\leq\max_{k=1,\ldots,K}p_{0,k}.

This is not true in general for
the full Baer-Nunziato model
as discussed above.

The relaxation procedure is summarized in the following algorithm.

Algorithm 5.5

(Pressure relaxation procedure in case of isentropic stiffened gases)
.

1.

Determine

as the unique root of the function (

2.

\displaystyle p^{\infty}=A_{1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{0}\rho_{1}^{0}}{\alpha_{1}^{\infty}}\right)^{\gamma_{1}}-\pi_{1}=A_{k}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}^{0}\rho_{k}^{0}}{\alpha_{k}^{\infty}}\right)^{\gamma_{k}}-\pi_{k},\,k=2,\dots,K\,.

This forms a coupled system for the volume fractions

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}^{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$u$}})^{T}:=\left({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{K-1}^{T},{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}^{i}_{1}(\alpha_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{1})^{T},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}^{i}_{K}(\alpha_{K},{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{K})^{T}\right),

(6.6)

Here

(6.9)

[

Therefore we use different symbols for the differentiation.

In each time step we thus perform alternately the evolution of the fluid and the relaxation process:

For the fluid equations (

) we apply a path-conservative DG scheme. For this purpose, we extended our in-house Runge-Kutta DG solver
[
31
,
10
]

by a path-conservative discretization of the nonlinear products in (

[
45
]
. For details on the derivation of the spatial path-conservative DG scheme we refer to
[
2
]
.

The efficiency of the scheme is improved by local grid adaption where we employ the
multi-resolution concept based on multiwavelets.
The key idea is to perform a multi-resolution analysis on a sequence of
nested grids providing a decomposition of the data on a coarse scale and a sequence
of details that encode the difference of approximations on
subsequent resolution levels. The detail coefficients become small when the underlying
data are locally smooth and, hence, can be discarded when dropping below a
threshold value
\varepsilon_{thresh}
.
By means of the thresholded sequence a new, locally refined grid
is determined. Details on this concept can be found in

[
31
,
10
,
11
]
.

Since our interest is in equilibrium processes we replace the transient relaxation procedure
(
6.9
) by integration to infinity, i.e., we determine the equilibrium states by algebraic equations according to Sect.
5.2
and Sect.
5.1
in case of an isentropic and isothermal fluid, respectively. The relaxation to equilibrium is performed one-by-one. First we determine the equilibrium velocity by the velocity relaxation according to Algorithm
5.1
. Then we perform pressure relaxation applying Algorithm
5.5
or
5.2
. In addition, in case of an isothermal fluid we determine the state at chemical equilibrium according to Algorithm
5.3
and
5.4
for a two-component and three-component mixture, respectively.
In case of a higher order DG scheme the solution on each element is given by a polynomial of higher degree. Since the equilibrium states are determined locally, for each element we have to perform a projection of the equilibrium states to the polynomial space.

For this purpose, we first determine for each element the equilibrium states in all quadrature points. From these we compute an interpolation polynomial that interpolates these equilibrium states. The polynomial degree of the interpolation polynomial is chosen smaller than the degree of the DG polynomial. Thus, projecting the interpolation polynomial onto the DG space will preserve the equilibrium states in the quadrature points. Note that in the DG scheme we only access the DG polynomials in the quadrature points.

We would like to remark that the relaxation process is applied whenever the states are out of equilibrium, i.e., after applying the limiter, performing grid adaptation or a Runge-Kutta stage in the time evolution.

6.2.1

In the literature one can find numerous numerical examples for two- and multi-phase flows with phase transition at similar states with
different sets of material parameters. Often these parameters are chosen arbitrarily. This in many cases leads to unphysical phase states, unphysical equilibrium states and unphysical processes. This can be avoided when appropriate tables are used, see for example

6.2.2
Numerical example: vapor–vapor compression problem

To investigate nucleation phenomena we consider the following 1d-Riemann problem determined by a compression problem for an initially single component vapor. The initial data are listed in Table
2
with material parameters given in Table
1
.
Two sets of computations are performed where we either perform mechanical relaxation, see Fig. 1,
or mechanical and chemical relaxation, see
Fig. 2,
i.e., the computations are performed with and without mass transfer.
In the figures we present the volume of water vapor (top, left), the pressure of the mixture (top, right), the velocity of the mixture (bottom, left) and the density of the mixture (bottom, right).

The computational domain is
\Omega=(-2,2)
. The simulations are run to time
t=0.001
.
We perform second order
(p=2)
computations using 3 cells on the base level and 10 levels of refinement. The CFL number is 0.1 resulting in about 61,000 time steps to reach the final time. The constant
c_{thresh}
for the thresholding performed in the grid adaptation step is chosen as
10^{-5}
. This small value was needed to ensure that all discontinuities are resolved on the highest refinement level where limiting is performed. At the final time the adaptive grid is about 82% of the uniformly refined grid.

In this configuration we consider “pure” water vapor that is slightly polluted with liquid water because the model does not allow for pure states. Pressure and velocity are chosen in mechanical equilibrium.
The initial pressure is slightly below the saturation pressure. At the center
x=0
the fluid is compressed due to the opposed velocities causing two compression/shock waves running to the left and right of the
center. This results in a significant pressure increase near the center where the pressure exceeds the saturation pressure. According to the pressure relaxation we observe a significant drop in the water vapor volume
that is caused by a rise in the water vapor density.

There is a significant difference between the computations without and with mass transfer.
When no chemical relaxation is performed no mass is transferred from water vapor to liquid water. This is reflected in the results for the density of the mixture. In case of no mass transfer the value is still close to
the initial value. However, when mass transfer occurs the density of the mixture significantly increases in the vicinity of the center. Note that this behavior is not fully resolved by the Baer-Nunziato model because it
requires a much finer grid to fully capture the condensation of the water vapor.
Note that the wave structure consists of two outer shock waves and two phase boundaries in between, which enclose the nucleated liquid.
However, due to the very small volume of the created liquid this cell is not fully resolved and we refer to
[
37
]
for further insights on the numerical difficulties.

To validate the above results
we compare the results with computations performed for an isothermal Euler model where two phases are separated by a sharp interface. Phase transition is accounted for by the jump condition at the interface. For details we refer to
[
18
,
21
]
.
Numerical computations of this model are performed independently applying a finite volume scheme in both of the two domains to either side of the interface. At the interface a two-phase Riemann problem is solved to compute the fluxes where the jump conditions account for mass transfer or neglect mass transfer.

We observe a perfect agreement of the two models in case of no mass transfer, see Fig.
1
.
When accounting for mass transfer then wave speeds of the two models perfectly match but there is an obvious severe discrepancy in the values of the intermediate states, see the red lines in
Fig.
2
. By numerical experiments we observe that in this case a scaling of the kinetic relation in the sharp-interface model by a factor of
300
results in a perfect
agreement of the states.
Note that in the Baer-Nunziato model we relax to the equilibrium state, i.e., the relaxation velocity is infinite, whereas in the isothermal Euler model the relaxation velocity is finite. Thus, increasing the relaxation velocity in the isothermal Euler model it comes closer to the equilibrium state.

7
Conclusion

In this work we have investigated Baer-Nunziato-type models for barotropic fluids with an arbitrary number of components. In the literature frequently barotropic fluids are confined to isentropic fluids, cf.
[
25
,
3
,
54
]
whereas here we also consider isothermal fluids.

First of all, we have determined eigenvalues and eigenvectors for the quasi-1D model in case of general barotropic fluids, i.e., the phasic pressures only depend on the phasic densities. For non-resonance states a full set of linearly independent eigenvectors has been derived. The different fields have been characterized as either genuinely nonlinear or linearly degenerated assuming that the phasic fundamental derivatives of thermodynamics are positive and choosing a particular convex combination for the interfacial velocity. For all fields we have given a full set of Riemann invariants whose gradients are linearly independent.
Furthermore we have verified that the quasi-1D equations is symmetrizable away from non-resonance states constructing a symmetrizer.

In case of isothermal fluids and isentropic fluids we have constructed appropriate entropy-entropy flux pairs and have verified that (i) the (mathematical) entropy functions are convex but not strictly convex even if the phasic entropy functions are strictly convex and (ii) the (mathematical) entropy inequality holds, i.e., the sum of the entropy production due to the non-conservative products and the relaxation processes
is non-increasing. It is worthwhile mentioning that for isothermal fluids and isentropic fluids we have used different entropy functions, namely, the mixture of the phasic entropies and the mixture of phasic total energies, respectively. In both cases we have to account for the heat flux in the balance laws for the phasic total energies to render these equations to be redundant in case of barotropic fluids. From a physical point of view this is mandatory. In the isothermal case heat production due to the work performed in the system has to be removed from the system to keep the temperatures constant. On the other hand, for isentropic fluids there is an energy production due to the relaxation processes.
For both cases corresponding heat fluxes have been identified.
Furthermore, since we cannot control the sign of the energy production due to the non-conservative products we have determined the interfacial velocity and the interfacial pressures such that this production term vanishes. Starting from an interfacial velocity determined by a convex combination of phasic velocities a unique set of interfacial pressures has been identified that coincides for both isothermal fluids and isentropic fluids.

The relaxation processes to the equilibrium state has been investigated for both the isothermal case and the isentropic case. Mechanical relaxation has been considered in both cases whereas relaxation of the chemical potentials is only meaningful for isothermal fluids. Algorithms for the computation of the corresponding equilibrium states are given.

Finally, we perform numerical computations in one space dimension by means of an adaptive path-conservative Runge-Kutta DG scheme.
Computations for isothermal fluids have been performed for the configuration of a vapor-vapor expansion problem near pressure saturation. The results have been validated by means of a sharp-interface model separating two
pure phases modelled by the isothermal Euler equations. The results showed a perfect agreement when neglecting mass transfer. When accounting for phase change then the kinetic relation describing the mass transfer in
the sharp-interface model had to be scaled to obtain a perfect agreement.
For another configuration we considered a mixture of two components with two phases. Computations were performed for mechanical equilibrium but without relaxation of the chemical potentials. The results were compared to a phase field model. Again, the results showed a very good agreement between the models.
