A Fast Convergence Theory for Offline Decision Making
Chenjie Mao maochenjie@pjlab.org.cn, Qiaosheng Zhang zhangqiaosheng@pjlab.org.cn

Abstract
This paper proposes the first generic fast convergence result in general function approximation for offline decision making problems, which include offline reinforcement learning (RL) and off-policy evaluation (OPE) as special cases. To unify different settings, we introduce a framework called Decision Making with Offline Feedback (DMOF), which captures a wide range of offline decision making problems. Within this framework, we propose a simple yet powerful algorithm called Empirical Decision with Divergence (EDD), whose upper bound can be termed as a coefficient named Empirical Offline Estimation Coefficient (EOEC). We show that EOEC is instance-dependent and actually measures the correlation of the problem. When assuming partial coverage in the dataset, EOEC will reduce in a rate of 1 / N 1/N where N N is the size of the dataset, endowing EDD with a fast convergence guarantee. Finally, we complement the above results with a lower bound in the DMOF framework, which further demonstrates the soundness of our theory.

Introduction

Offline decision making
studies learning decisions from a logged dataset that, in most cases, is only
partially correlated
with the learning objective.
The possible partial correlation between the dataset and the learning objective distinguishes offline decision making from
supervised learning

[
Vapnik, 1998
,
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
]
, making it
a promising subject capturing a wide range of real-world problems.
Examples of offline decision making include
offline reinforcement learning
(
Antos et al. [2006]
,
Lange et al. [2012]
,
Levine et al. [2020a]
; offline RL)
and
off-policy evaluation
(
Li et al. [2011]
,
Thomas and Brunskill [2016]
,
Farajtabar et al. [2018]
,
Uehara et al. [2022]
; OPE), both of which are trendy areas in recent years.
Specifically, in the former,
the dataset is a logged interaction between the environment and the so-called behaviour policy while the
learning objective is to learn a good policy that differs from the behaviour policy.
In the latter, the dataset is usually generated from a policy differing from the one to be estimated.
A crucial consideration in the offline decision making problem is
learnability
, which refers to the ability to learn a desired policy from the logged dataset.

On one hand, although research in offline decision making has advanced rapidly, most theoretical results about learnability have been tied to specific problem structures, such as Markovian transition, tabular state-action space (e.g., tabular Markov decision processes (MDPs
[
Puterman, 1994
]
);
Rashidinejad et al. [2021]
,
Xie et al. [2021b]
,
Li et al. [2022]
), and low-rank features (e.g., linear MDPs;
Jin et al. [2020]
,
Huang et al. [2023]
,
Xiong et al. [2023]
), with assumptions like independently sampled data from a data distribution covering some policies
[
Chen and Jiang, 2019
,
Liu et al., 2020
,
Xie and Jiang, 2020
,
Xie et al., 2021a
]
.
These results often differ in their formulations and rely on different assumptions for derivation.
This diversity in problem structures and underlying assumptions not only obstructs a unified understanding of the offline decision making problem but also poses challenges in transferring theoretical results from one problem to another. Given these issues, we would like to ask:

Can we study offline decision making in a unified framework and extract a measure summarizing the learnability of offline decision making problems?

On the other hand, the performance of algorithms in offline decision making in real-world scenarios can often exceed their theoretical results
[
Kumar et al., 2020
,
Levine et al., 2020b
]
, suggesting the potential for more refined results in this area. This is possibly due to the minimax nature of theoretical analysis, which takes undesired instances into consideration. Thus, a question that arises is:

Can we derive an instance-dependent result with faster-convergence potential for offline decision making problems?

This paper positively answers the two posed questions. We first introduce a unified framework for offline decision making, named
Decision Making with Offline Feedback
(DMOF).
We then present an algorithm that achieves instance-dependent regret as well as fast convergence rates in
1/N
where
N
is the sample size.
To complement our result, we further propose a lower bound for offline decision making problems. We detail our contributions as follows.

1.

We present a simple yet powerful algorithm named Empirical Decision with Divergence (EDD;
Eq.

1
), which enjoys the following advantages.

•

(
Theorem

1
) It has an
instance-dependent
upper bound that can adjust to different presentations of the real model.

•

(
Theorem

2
) It attains a fast convergence rate (specifically, a rate of 1/N, where N is the sample size) to the sub-optimality gap in
Markovian sequential problems (
Definition

3
) with partial coverage, where the latter includes Markov decision processes as a special case.

2.

Next, we propose a measure—called
Offline Estimation Coefficient
(
OEC
,
Definition

4
)—that potentially encapsulates the hardness of an offline decision making problem. On top of this, we
provide an information-theoretic lower bound for the regret of any algorithm, expressed as a function of the
OEC
(
Theorem

3
).
We compare this lower bound with the upper bound achieved by EDD, demonstrating the soundness of EDD from lower bound’s perspective.

1.2
Related Works

General Function Approximation in Offline Decision Making

General function approximation
[
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996
,
Vapnik, 1998
,
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
]
formulates the statistical learning problem with a generic function class, and studies the learnability of the problem in terms of the complexity of the function class.
General function approximation is extensively studied in offline decision making, typically in the subjects like offline RL
[
Chen and Jiang, 2019
,
Liu et al., 2020
,
Xie and Jiang, 2020
,
Foster et al., 2021b
,
Xie et al., 2021a
,
Cheng et al., 2022
,
Zhan et al., 2022
]
and OPE
[
Uehara et al., 2019
,
Jiang and Huang, 2020
,
Huang and Jiang, 2022
]
.
In these works, function classes are considered as another crucial aspect of learnability beyond data coverage.
Particular examples of these function classes include the value function class, the policy function class, the density ratio function class
[
Liu et al., 2018
,
Uehara et al., 2019
]
, and the model class
[
Uehara and Sun, 2021
,
Bhardwaj et al., 2023
]
.
General function approximation is widely preferred since it is more adaptable to complex real-world scenarios (both for function approximators and for the state-action space) and can be applied directly to specialized settings (e.g., linear and tabular MDPs) with only minor modifications.
Recently,
Di et al. [2023]
proposes an algorithm named Pessimistic Nonlinear Least-Square Value Iteration for nonlinear function classes and get a minimax optimal instance-dependent regret when specialized to the linear setting. However, their results require uniform coverage over the dataset, which is fairly strong in the offline setting.

Instance-Dependent Bounds in Offline Decision Making

Despite the rapid progress in the development of minimax bounds, instance-dependent analyses in offline decision making are still under development, possibly due to the challenge posed by partial correlation.
The most studied instance hardness measures are the variance and gap
[
Xu et al., 2021
,
Simchowitz and Jamieson, 2019
,
He et al., 2021
]
in the optimal value function, where the latter is defined as the difference of the values between the optimal action and sub-optimal ones across the state space.
Nevertheless, the gap-based instance-dependent bound seems less intuitive since it prefers problems with a large gap, though selecting sub-optimal actions with a small gap should also yield satisfactory results.
Specifically in the offline setting, there are only a few works presenting instance-dependent bounds, and all of them focus on special settings like linear MDPs
[
Duan and Wang, 2020
,
Yin et al., 2022a
,
Min et al., 2022
,
Nguyen-Tang et al., 2023
]
and tabular MDPs
[
Pananjady and Wainwright, 2020
,
Yin and Wang, 2021
,
Wang et al., 2022
]
, with certain strong assumptions like uniform policy coverage
[
Hu et al., 2021
,
Yin et al., 2022c
,
Di et al., 2023
]
.

Faster Convergence Rate in Sequential Problems

There are only a few works in offline decision making concerning faster convergence rate compared with
1/\sqrt{N}
, as far as we are aware. They either adapt the gap of optimal value function into the performance
[
Hu et al., 2021
,
Wang et al., 2022
,
Nguyen-Tang et al., 2023
]
, or assume specific structures for the problem (e.g., deterministic dynamics
[
Uehara et al., 2021
,
Yin et al., 2022b
]
).
In contrast, our fast convergence applies to all Markovian sequential problems without the need for the gap, which yields a more satisfactory theory.

Works Related to DEC

The framework and lower bound in this paper are influenced by the DEC paper for interactive (online) decision making
[
Foster et al., 2021a
]
, but it also introduces significant developments rather than being a simple adaptation.
For example, we use a fixed parameter
\lambda
, and we propose EDD that achieves an instance-dependent bound as well as a fast convergence rate for some particular problems such as supervised learning and Markovian sequential problems with partial coverage.
On the other hand, after the initial publication of
Foster et al. [2021a]
,
there have been subsequent works trying to enhance their result
[
Foster et al., 2023c
,
Foster et al., 2023b
,
Chen et al., 2024
]
and expand it to other settings
[
Foster et al., 2022
,
Foster et al., 2023a
]
,
but none of these studies focuses on offline decision making. In conclusion, we hope that this part of our work can fill this gap and further inspire the development of DEC in the online setting.

Preliminaries on
f
-Divergence

We briefly introduce the concept of
f
-divergence
in this section, and refer the interested readers to Section
7
in
Polyanskiy and Wu [2022]
for a more detailed explanation.
We assume that we operate on a
measurable space

(E,\mathcal{E})
(e.g., the set of
real numbers

\mathbb{R}
and its
Borel sets
), and we have access to a
dominating measure

\nu
(e.g., the
Lebesgue measure
).

For two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
,
we denote the
Radon-Nikodym derivative
of
\mathbb{P}
w.r.t.
\mathbb{Q}
as
\frac{d\mathbb{P}}{d\mathbb{Q}}
.
We also use lowercase letters to denote the Radon-Nikodym derivative w.r.t.
\nu
(e.g.,
p\coloneqq d\mathbb{P}/d\nu
and
q\coloneqq d\mathbb{Q}/d\nu
).

Definition 1

(
f
-divergence)
.

Given a function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, let
A\coloneqq\{x\in E|q(x)>0\}
and
f^{\prime}(\infty)\coloneqq\lim_{x\to 0}xf(1/x)
,
with the agreement that the
f^{\prime}(\infty)\mathbb{P}(E\setminus A)
is
0
if
\mathbb{P}(E\setminus A)=0
,
we define

\displaystyle D_{f}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\int_{A}f\Big(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big)q(x)d\nu(x)+f^{\prime}(\infty)\mathbb{P}(E\setminus A)

as the
f
-divergence between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
if
f
is convex and
f(1)=0
.

In short, the
f
-divergence measures the difference between two probability measures given a convex function
f
.
Examples of several frequently used
f
-divergences are listed below:

•

the
KL divergence
, where
f(x)=x\log(x)
:

\textup{{KL}}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\big[p\log(p/q)\big]
;

•

the
squared Hellinger distance
, where
f(x)=(1-\sqrt{x})^{2}
:

\texttt{H}^{2}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\big[(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q})^{2}\big]
;

•

the
total variation
, where
f(x)=0.5|x-1|
:

\textup{{TV}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}|p-q|/2
.

3
Framework: Decision Making with Offline Feedback (DMOF)

Inspired by the
decision making with structured observation
(DMSO) framework for online decision making problems
[
Foster et al., 2021a
]
,
we introduce a general framework for offline decision making, which is referred to as
Decision Making with Offline Feedback
(DMOF).
The DMOF framework includes an observation space
\mathcal{O}
, a model space
\mathcal{M}
, a decision space
\Pi
, and a loss function
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{M}\times\Pi\to[0,B]
for a constant
B>0
.

The learner interacts with the DMOF according to the following protocol.

1.

Problem Setup:
there is an unknown
real model

M^{\star}\in\mathcal{M}
specified ahead, under which we observe a dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\mathcal{D}^{\star}\sim\mathbb{P}_{M^{\star}}
(we will slightly abuse the notation and denote
\mathcal{D}\sim M
when considering the dataset generation).

2.

Learning:
given the knowledge of
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
as well as the observed dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
, we wish to find
a decision
\widehat{\pi}\in\Pi
that minimizes the loss
\mathcal{L}(M^{\star},\widehat{\pi})
.
We sometimes also learn a stochastic decision characterized by a distribution
p\in\Delta(\Pi)
,
of which the loss is defined as
\mathcal{L}(M^{\star},p)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim p}[\mathcal{L}(M^{\star},\pi)]
.

This framework is carefully crafted for offline decision making problems,
capturing broad settings such as offline RL, off-policy evaluation, and offline POMDPs.

Example

1
provides a detailed explanation of how the DMOF framework is explicitly applied to the offline RL problem.

Example 1

(DMOF in Offline RL)
.

Let us consider the general framework of offline RL (see
Appendix

A
for a more detailed introduction) with a realizable model class
\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{rl}}
that contains
M_{\texttt{rl}}^{\star}
.
In such a setting, DMOF follows that:

\mathcal{M}=\{M=(M_{\texttt{rl}},d^{\mathcal{D}},N)|M_{\texttt{rl}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{rl}}\}
where
\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{rl}}
is the set of MDPs,
d^{\mathcal{D}}
is the data distribution on the state-action space, and
N
is the sample size;
\Pi
is the set of policies (
\Pi_{\texttt{rl}}
);
\mathcal{O}
is the set of all possible dataset with size
N
;
and
\mathcal{L}((M_{\texttt{rl}},d^{\mathcal{D}},N),\pi)
is the gap between the optimal expected return of
M_{\texttt{rl}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{rl}}
and
the expected return of policy
\pi
.

Algorithms (denoted as
\mathfrak{A}
) are mappings from

(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
and the observed dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
to a
(distribution of) decision
\pi\in\Pi
. Since the elements
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
are always fixed, we will only specify
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
in most cases.
We now outline some advantages of the DMOF framework in the following.

\mathcal{M}
as an Information Representor

The complexity of
\mathcal{M}
reflects the information available in advance, and additional side information can help to prune
\mathcal{M}
.
For any assumptions made or any information provided, we can always infer
a model class
\mathcal{M}
representing all necessary details.
For instance, in offline RL, assuming having a value function class
\mathcal{Q}
that contains the optimal value function
is equivalent to assuming having a model class containing the real model, which is composed of all possible models
whose optimal value function is contained by
\mathcal{Q}

[
Mao et al., 2024
]
.

On the Variation of the Loss Function
\mathcal{L}

It is worth noting that we do not put any constraints on the loss function
\mathcal{L}
. It can be any function taking a model and a decision as input, and can even be irrelevant to its inputs.
Thus, one can specify
\mathcal{L}
to adapt to various scenarios.

Essentially, the weak correlation between the loss function
\mathcal{L}
and the observation
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
forms one of the biggest differences between DMOF (i.e., offline decision making) and standard supervised learning problems.

On the Data Distribution

While prior works on offline decision making typically make many assumptions about the data generation process,
these assumptions are made mainly for the ease of analysis and
may not be necessary for learning a good policy in practice.
DMOF circumvents possible over-specification of data assumptions by the model class
\mathcal{M}
,
which, as we will show in the following sections, is
ultimately enough for summarizing the problem and its learnability.

4
Algorithm: Empirical Decision with Divergence (EDD)

This section presents an algorithm named
Empirical Decision with Divergence (EDD)

with an accompanying upper bound (
Theorem

1
), which is instance-dependent and can be adapted to different real models.
Notably, this result is attained without further assumptions beyond the DMOF framework. As an expansion, we will further explain the guarantees of EDD under sequential learning problems like MDPs in
Section

5
, which ensures a fast convergence rate.
We defer the proofs for this section to
Appendix

C
for a clean presentation.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
generated by the real model
M^{\star}
, together with a model class
\mathcal{M}
standing as the information we can access, EDD outputs the policy in the following form:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\mathcal{D}^{\star})=\argmin_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)}\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Big[\mathcal{L}(M,p)+\lambda\log(\mathbb{P}_{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\star}))\Big],

(1)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a hyper-parameter, and
\log(\mathbb{P}_{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\star}))
is the log-likelihood of observing
\mathcal{D}^{\star}
under model
M
.

We will simply take
\widehat{p}
as the output distribution of policies when the context is clear.

In short, EDD selects the policy that is universally good (reflected by
\mathcal{L}(M,\pi)
in
Eq.

1
) among possible real models (reflected by
\log(\mathbb{P}_{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\star}))
), but in a more unified way compared to the other classic model-based methods in RL (e.g.,
Uehara and Sun [2021]
,
Foster et al. [2021a]
), which first estimates a possible real model(s) and then tries to select a good policy.

4.1
Instance-dependent Guarantee of EDD

This subsection elaborates on the upper bounds achieved by EDD. Before that, we first introduce a new concept called the
Empirical Offline Estimation Coefficient
(
EOEC
).

Definition 2
.

Given a DMOF
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
and a dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the
EOEC
w.r.t. a model
M\in\mathcal{M}
and a parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle\textup{{EOEC}}_{\lambda}(M,\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)}\max_{M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\big[\mathcal{L}(M^{\prime},p)

\displaystyle+\lambda\cdot\log(\mathbb{P}_{M^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D})/\mathbb{P}_{M}(\mathcal{D}))\big].

(2)

We then state our first upper bound for EDD with the help of
EOEC
.

Theorem 1
.

For any real
M^{\star}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\star}\sim M^{\star}
, EDD (
Eq.

1
) with a proper choice of its hyper-parameter has its loss bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(M^{\star},\widehat{p}(\mathcal{D}^{\star}))\leq\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\textup{{EOEC}}_{\lambda}(M^{\star},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D}^{\star}).

(3)

As a function of both the real model and dataset,
this upper bound is instance-dependent and adapts automatically to various choices of
M^{\star}
and even to the different occurrences of the dataset we encountered.
Although until now it may be unclear how tight the upper bound is, we will show in the next section that it is actually intriguing in many classic settings.

5
Fast Convergence Rates for EDD under I.I.D. and Correlation

This section derives the fast convergence rate for offline decision making problems that are based on Markovian sequential problems, a typical example of which is the Markov decision process (MDP)—the general framework of offline RL and OPE. We defer the proof of this section to
Appendix

D
.

The key distinguishing property of a Markovian sequential problem is that its loss can be decomposed into a sequential form.
We formally define Markovian sequential problem in the following.

Definition 3

(Markovian Sequential Problems)
.

We say that a DMOF
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
is over a sequential problem
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M},\{\mathcal{S}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},\{\mathcal{A}\}_{h\in[H]},\Pi,\ell,N)
if the following holds.

1.

Given
\pi\in\Pi
and
M\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a stochastic process
\{(s_{h},a_{h})\}_{h\in[H]}
from the space
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}\coloneqq{(\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{1})\times(\mathcal{S}_{2}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})\times\dots(\mathcal{S}_{H}\times\mathcal{A}_{H})}
such that
s_{1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|M,1)
, and for all
h\in[H]
,
a_{h}\sim\pi(\cdot|h,\{s_{i},a_{i}\}_{i\in[h-1]},s_{h})
,
s_{h+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|M,h,s_{h},a_{h})
. We denote the margin of
(s_{h},a_{h})
w.r.t. the process as
\mathcal{T}(\cdot|M,\pi,h)
, and the probability of the whole process as
\mathcal{T}(\cdot|M,\pi)
.

2.

The loss is defined as
\mathcal{L}(M,\pi)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\{(s_{h},a_{h})\}_{h\in[H]}\sim\mathcal{T}(\cdot|M,\pi)}\ell(\{(s_{h},a_{h})\}_{h\in[H]},\pi)
, where
\ell\in((\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A})\times\Pi\to[0,B])
.

In such a case, we refer to
H\geq 1
as the
horizon
of this Markovian sequential problem. We will also add the subscript
M
when referring to concepts specific to
M
.

Remark 1

(A Comparison Between Markovian Sequential Problems and MDPs)
.

The Markovian sequential problem is a generalization of MDPs and has the following comprehensiveness:

•

its policy is non-Markovian and can be based on the whole history;

•

instead of summing scalar rewards across the trajectory in MDPs, its loss (namely, reversed return) can be any function on the whole history (where the reward is merged into the state space
\mathcal{S}
). It can even be a function of the policy, which is typically useful in settings like risk-sensitive offline RL
[
Urpí et al., 2021
,
Rigter et al., 2023
]
.

Based on the above definition, we now make the i.i.d. assumption for
the whole trajectories
in the dataset.
Note that we only assume i.i.d. across trajectories, while samples from the same trajectory can still be dependent.

Assumption 1

(I.I.D. Assumption on a Sequential Problem)
.

Given a DMOF
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
over a Markovian sequential problem
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M},\{\mathcal{S}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},\{\mathcal{A}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},\Pi,\ell,N)
,
the i.i.d. assumption assumes that
for all
M\in\mathcal{M}
, the dataset
\mathcal{D}\sim M
consists of
N
sequences
\{(s_{h,n},a_{h,n},s_{h,n}^{\prime})\}_{h\in[H],n\in[N]}
that are drawn in an i.i.d. manner, and for all
n\in[N]
and
s_{h,n}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|M,\pi,s_{h,n},a_{h,n})
. We denote the distribution of a sequence as
\Phi(M)
and the margin of
(s_{h,n},a_{h,n})
as
d^{\mathcal{D}}_{h}(\cdot|M)
.

Remark 2
.

There are similar assumptions made for MDPs (e.g.,
Rashidinejad et al. [2021]
,
Xie et al. [2021a]
,
Cheng et al. [2022]
,
Zhan et al. [2022]
,
Huang et al. [2023]
) in offline decision making literatures, which is a specification of
Assumption

1
.

5.1
Transferring Our Result to Toy Cases

Unlike other general function approximation methods, model-based algorithms encounter difficulties when applied to toy cases, such as the tabular setting. This is primarily due to the introduction of an improper function class (a function class that contains functions other than probability distributions) in general discretization techniques, such as covering numbers and bracketing numbers, which complicates their application in model-based algorithms. Specifically, the only existing method we are aware of for model-based algorithms is bracketing, as described in
Uehara and Sun [2021]
.
However, this method may be flawed due to the use of two different definitions of the squared Hellinger divergence in an improper function class. A detailed discussion is provided in
Appendix

F
.

This challenge is not entirely surprising: given that minimax bounds in these settings scale as
1/\sqrt{N}
,
it would be unrealistic to expect our upper bound to surpass this limitation without introducing a new factor. Nonetheless, we believe this challenge is intriguing and represents a promising direction for future research. Specifically, it would be interesting to explore new assumptions, alternative methods, or problem-specific measures that could overcome this fundamental obstacle, allowing our results to be applied more smoothly to these cases and yield robust conclusions.

Complementing Our Results with Lower Bounds

This section introduces the Offline Estimation Coefficient (
OEC
) and presents a lower bound (
Theorem

3
) as a function of the
OEC
.
We will then compare the lower bound with the upper bound for EDD.
We defer the proofs for this section to
Appendix

E

We define the
OEC
as a trade-off between the similarities of the representation and the difference in learning objectives for models from DMOF, captured in a max-min-max form.
Compared with
EOEC
,
OEC
replaces the “data-based” log-likelihood ratio with the squared Hellinger distance, leading us to remove the term “empirical” accordingly.

Definition 4
.

Given a DMOF
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
,
its

OEC
w.r.t. a set of probability
A\subseteq\Delta(\mathcal{O})
and a parameter
\lambda\geq 0
is defined as

\displaystyle\textup{{OEC}}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M},A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\rho\in A}\min\limits_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)}\max\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Big[\mathcal{L}(M,p)-\lambda\cdot\textup{{H}${}^{2}$}(\rho,M)\Big].

(9)

where
\textup{{H}${}^{2}$}(\rho,M)
is the squared Hellinger distance between
\rho
and
\mathbb{P}_{M}
.

Below, we provide an interpretation of the formulation and the operational meaning of
OEC
. Technically, the first

\max_{\rho\in A}
part in
OEC
sets a point in the model class
\mathcal{M}
where the real model possibly lies, suggesting that the real model should be similar to
\rho
. The
\min_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)}
indicates
that there is a learner pessimistically chooses a stochastic policy
p\in\Delta(\Pi)
, with the knowledge of
\rho
but without knowing the real model
M\in\mathcal{M}
.
The last
\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}}
part
means that an adversary selects the worst-case real model
M
that is similar to
\rho
after knowing the stochastic policy
p
.

The
OEC
summarizes the learnability property of a DMOF problem: as will be presented in
Theorem

3
, it implies a lower bound for learning. Moreover, the
OEC
is also involved in the upper bound presented in
Theorem

4
.

Remark 7
.

As a further development, the squared Hellinger distance in
Eq.

2
can be replaced by other
f
-divergences
provided they satisfy a refined change of measure property (introduced in
Appendix

G
).
Such
f
-divergences include the total variation distance, the KL divergence, and so forth. We will discuss them in more detail in
Appendix

G
.

6.1
Lower Bounds via
OEC
without Further Assumptions

This subsection presents a lower bound on the loss of
any algorithm, which is specifically expressed as a function of the
OEC
with
\lambda=4B
.
Here, we do not make any assumption on the problem—e.g., assumptions on structure of the problem (like sequential or Markovian) or on the coverage of the dataset—beyond the DMOF framework.
Our result on the lower bound is encapsulated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Given a DMOF
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
,
for any algorithm
\mathfrak{A}
, there exists an
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that the expected loss of the algorithm under model
M
is lower bounded as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim M}\Big[\mathcal{L}(M,\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{D}))\Big]\geq\frac{1}{3}\textup{{OEC}}_{4B}(\mathcal{M},A),

(10)

where
A
can be chosen as any subset of
\Delta(\mathcal{O})
.

Remark 8

(About Data Size)
.

In contrast to normal conditions, there is no explicitly the size of dataset showed in this lower bound.
Inherently, the information of the size of the dataset is measured by the Hellinger distance in
OEC
.

An interesting observation is
that the set
A
can be any set of distributions on
\mathcal{O}
, which, while of course including
\mathcal{M}
itself, are not limited to models from DMOF.
For instance, in the context of time-inhomogenious Markov decision processes, offline RL often makes independent assumptions on samples
[
Chen and Jiang, 2019
,
Liu et al., 2020
,
Rashidinejad et al., 2021
,
Xie et al., 2021a
]
, whereas
\Delta(\mathcal{O})
can contain distributions that are correlated.
This somewhat contrasts with the typical approach to proving lower bounds: to find a lower bound from a problem set
\mathcal{M}
,
we typically construct a subset of
\mathcal{M}
and verify the hardness of the subset.

As discussed above, the purpose of the first
\max
part is merely to serve as a guide for pinpointing the real model.
The lower bound becomes tightest when
A
is taken as
\Delta(\mathcal{O})
, but computing the exact value of
OEC
for
A=\Delta(\mathcal{O})
may require significant effort. When computing the lower bound, one can choose an appropriate
A
that balances accuracy with computational complexity.

As a complement, the following theorem relates
EOEC
with
OEC
, showing that EDD is nearly optimal in the minimax perspective.

Theorem 4
.

For any DMOF
(\mathcal{M},\Pi,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{L})
and model
M\in\mathcal{M}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim M
,

\displaystyle\textup{{EOEC}}_{\lambda}(M,\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D})\leq

\displaystyle\textup{{OEC}}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M},\{M\})+2\lambda\cdot\log(|\mathcal{M}|/\delta).

Consequently, for any DMOF

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(M^{\star},\widehat{p}(\mathcal{D}^{\star}))\leq

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\big[\textup{{OEC}}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M},\{M^{\star}\})+2\lambda\cdot\log(|\mathcal{M}|/\delta)\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\big[\textup{{OEC}}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M})+2\lambda\cdot\log(|\mathcal{M}|/\delta)\big],

(11)

where

Remark 9
.

The cardinality

6.2
Comparing the Upper Bound and the Lower Bound

Apart from the
OEC
term in the lower bound (
Eq.

10
), the upper bound (
Eq.

11
) also contains an additional
2\lambda\cdot\log(|\mathcal{M}|)
.
For a convergent result, one must set
\lambda
to a sufficiently small value compared with the constant value in
Eq.

10
, which still makes sense as we will explain in the following.

Notably, until now, we have not made any assumptions such as “independent and identically distributed” (i.i.d.) or the coverage assumption, except for the framework depicted by DMOF.
Thus, we can expect that either the lower bound (
Eq.

10
) only occurs in extreme cases and may not accurately reflect the tightness of the upper bounds (
Eqs.

3
and
11
), or that the upper bounds can be significantly improved with certain appropriate assumptions.

Similar observations (the additional
\lambda\cdot\log(|\mathcal{M}|/\delta)
and
the values of
\lambda
in upper and lower bounds
) also appear in occur for the PAC bound in the online setting in the spectrum works of DEC
[
Foster et al., 2023b
]
,
which is regarded as an unsolved problem.
An interesting point about the regret bound in the online setting is that, while the addition term remains, we do not have the problem of setting
\lambda
to be too small
[
Foster et al., 2021a
,
Foster et al., 2023b
]
. This is because every distribution (or interaction) has a loss appearing in the regret, which allows us to use a more moderate value about
\lambda
. We refer interested readers to the original papers for more details.

7
Conclusion

This paper proposes the first generic fast convergence result for general function approximation.
We first introduce a general framework called the Decision Making with Offline Feedback (DMOF) for offline decision making,
under which we present an algorithm named EDD, which achieves an instance-dependent upper bound.
We further enhance it with a fast convergence result under Markov sequential problems, which takes MDPs as a special case.
Following this, we introduce a complexity measure called the
offline estimation coefficient (
OEC
), based on which we build an information-theoretic lower bound to complement our results.

Finally, we highlight some possible further directions from this paper.
Firstly, as our results in this paper are based on model-based general function approximation for general offline decision making problems, it would be interesting to concretize our results, extend them to special settings like POMDPs, and to find a way to reasonably apply it to toy cases like tabular settings.
Additionally, while part of this paper can be considered as an offline work influenced by
Foster et al. [2021a]
in the online setting, the results presented here could also have online extensions
and may enhance the results for DEC.

A Short Introduction to Offline Reinforcement Learning

This section would shortly introduce offline reinforcement learning (offline RL), i.e., learning with offline Markov decision processes (MDP). To distinguish offline RL from DMOF, we would add the subscript
rl
when the notations collapse.

For the pre-learning interaction part, we consider the time-inhomogeneous MDP. Suppose that we have a MDP

\displaystyle M_{\texttt{rl}}\coloneqq(\{\mathcal{S}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},\{\mathcal{A}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},d_{1},\{R_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},\{P_{h}\}_{h\in[H]},\{\Pi_{\texttt{rl},h}\}_{h\in[H]}),

where
\{\mathcal{S}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
is the state spaces,
\{\mathcal{A}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
is the action spaces,

is the initial state distribution,
\{R_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
is the reward functions,
\{P_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
is the transition kernel,
\{\Pi_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
is the policy space, and

For simplicity, we further denote
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{S}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
,
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{A}_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
,
P\coloneqq\{P_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
, and
\Pi_{\texttt{rl}}\coloneqq\{\Pi_{\texttt{rl},h}\}_{h\in[H]}
.

In the model-based setting, there will be a model
\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{rl}}
being a set of MDPs which only differ in
\{P_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}

\{R_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
(mostly only

Given a police

•

•

Our goal is to maximize the cumulative reward, namely,

We further denote

For the offline learning part, we are given with the dataset

Typically we will have that

Lemma 2

Given a measurable space

\phi

\displaystyle\int f(x)\frac{d\phi(x)}{d\nu(x)}\nu(dx)=\int f(x)\phi(dx).

We can prove the case that

4
4

4

We follow the definitions from

Defining the set

\displaystyle\int f(x)\phi(dx)=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{B\in\mathcal{B}}f(B)\phi(B)

The last equality is justified by the additivity of the Lebesgue integration

This completes the proof.

B.1
Change of Measure Properties

One of the main properties of the
f

Foster et al. [2021a]

Concretely, the change of measure property is concerned with changing the probability measure underlying an expectation, with the cost of a divergence between the probabilities, plus a possible variation on the function in the expectation.
The change of measure inequalities of
f
-divergences are essential in learning theory, especially in fields like PAC-Bayesian learning
[
Shawe-Taylor and Williamson, 1997
,
McAllester, 1998
,
McAllester, 1999
,
Alquier, 2023
]
.

We briefly state some results about change of measure properties in this subsection, and would like to refer interested readers to
Ohnishi and Honorio [2020]
for a more comprehensive review of the change of measure inequalities.
Also note that this paper would focus on some refined change of measure properties, which would be explained in more detail in
Appendix

G
.

We first state the classic
Donsker and Varadhan variational formula
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(Donsker and Varadhan’s Formula)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable (r.v.) over
\mathcal{X}
,
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function, and
\rho,\pi
be probability measures over
\mathcal{X}
, we have that

\displaystyle\textup{KL}(\rho\rVert\pi)=\max_{h}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\rho}[h(X)]-\log\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[\exp(h(X))]\Big].

(12)

The following shows the change of measure property of the total variation distance.

Lemma 4
.

Given a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{E})
, two probabilities
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, and a positive number
\beta>0
,
for any real-valued measurable function
f
on
\Omega
with range
[0,\beta]
,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f]\big|\leq 2\beta\textup{{TV}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}).

Proof.

Take
\nu\coloneqq\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{Q}
as the dominating measure,
we have that

\displaystyle\textup{{TV}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\Big|\frac{d\mathbb{P}}{d\nu}-\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\nu}\Big|d\nu}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\beta\Big|\frac{d\mathbb{P}(x)}{d\nu(x)}-\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(x)}{d\nu(x)}\Big|\nu(dx)}{2\beta}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\int f(x)\Big|\frac{d\mathbb{P}(x)}{d\nu(x)}-\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(x)}{d\nu(x)}\Big|\nu(dx)}{2\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\Big|f(x)\frac{d\mathbb{P}(x)}{d\nu(x)}-f(x)\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(x)}{d\nu(x)}\Big|\nu(dx)}{2\beta}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\Big|\int\Big(f(x)\frac{d\mathbb{P}(x)}{d\nu(x)}-f(x)\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(x)}{d\nu(x)}\Big)\nu(dx)\Big|}{2\beta}

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Big|\int\big(f(x)\frac{d\mathbb{P}(x)}{d\nu(x)}\big)\nu(dx)-\int\big(f(x)\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(x)}{d\nu(x)}\big)\nu(dx)\Big|}{2\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f]\big|}{2\beta}

(

)

The fifth inequality is due to Jensen’s inequality and the fact that the absolute value function is convex.

This completes the proof.

Lemma 6

(Union Bound of the Log-Likelihood)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of probability measures, and
\mathcal{D}
be a dataset where
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}
for some
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}
, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\leq 2\log(|\mathcal{P}|/\delta).

Proof.

We first fix
p\in\mathcal{P}
, and take

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))-\log{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\star}}\big[\sqrt{p(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\star}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]}.

We have the moment generating function of
L(\mathcal{D})
be
1
at the value
1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\Big[\exp\big(L(\mathcal{D})\big)\Big]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\Big[\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2}\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))-\log{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\star}}\big[\sqrt{p(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\star}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\Big[\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2}\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))\big)\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\star}}\big[\sqrt{p(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\star}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.

We can then apply the Chernoff bound, which yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(L(\mathcal{D})\geq\epsilon\big)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\Big[\exp\big(L(\mathcal{D})\big)\Big]}{\exp(\epsilon)}=\exp(-\epsilon).

Further, by the definition of the squared Hellinger distance,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\geq\epsilon\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+2-2\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\star}}\big[\sqrt{p(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\star}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]\geq\epsilon\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+2-2\exp\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\star}}\big[\sqrt{p(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\star}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]\geq\epsilon\big)

Due to the fact that
\exp(x)\geq x+1
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we further have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\geq\epsilon\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))-2\log{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\star}}\big[\sqrt{p(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\star}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]}\geq\epsilon\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}}\big(L(\mathcal{D})\geq\epsilon/2\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp(-\epsilon/2).

Taking
\delta=\exp(-\epsilon/2)
, we have
\epsilon=2\log(1/\delta)
.
We can conclude that with probability at most
\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}
,

\displaystyle-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\geq 2\log(1/\delta).

Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}
,

\displaystyle-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\leq 2\log(1/\delta).

Finally, taking union bounds on
p\in\mathcal{P}
yields that,
with probability at least
1-\delta
on
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}
, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\leq 2\log(|\mathcal{P}|/\delta).

Since the above argument applies to any
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
, we have that for any
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\star}
, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle-\log(p^{\star}(\mathcal{D})/p(\mathcal{D}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\leq 2\log(|\mathcal{P}|/\delta).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 8

(Union Bound of the Likelihood Under I.I.D.)

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of probabilities, and

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\log(p^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D})/(p^{\prime})^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D}))-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\big[\sqrt{(p^{\prime})^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})/p^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}\big]

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\Big[\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2}\log(p^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D})/(p^{\prime})^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D}))\big)\Big]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\big[\sqrt{(p^{\prime})^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D})/p^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{D})}\big]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\big(L(\mathcal{D})\geq\epsilon\big)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\exp\big(L(\mathcal{D})\big)}{\exp(\epsilon)}=\exp(-\epsilon).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\Big(\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[-\log(p(X_{i})/p^{\prime}(X_{i}))+2-2\exp\log\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\big[\sqrt{p^{\prime}(X)/p(X)}\big]\Big]\geq\epsilon\Big).

\exp(x)\geq x+1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\Big(\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[-\log(p(X_{i})/p^{\prime}(X_{i}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p,p^{\prime})\Big]\geq\epsilon\Big)

p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}

with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim p^{\otimes N}

\displaystyle\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[-\log(p(X_{i})/p^{\prime}(X_{i}))+\textup{{H}}^{2}(p,p^{\prime})\Big]\leq 2\log(|\mathcal{P}|/\delta).

\widehat{\pi}

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}\sim\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{D})

This means that we can insert a

Denoting the distribution of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\max\limits_{\rho\in A}\max\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Big[\mathcal{L}(M,\widehat{p}(\rho))-4B\cdot\textup{{H}${}^{2}$}(\rho,M)\Big]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\max\limits_{\rho\in A}\min\limits_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)}\max\limits_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Big[\mathcal{L}(M,p)-4B\cdot\textup{{H}${}^{2}$}(\rho,M)\Big]

Appendix F
Obstacle of Applying Bracketing in Model-based Algorithms

In
Uehara and Sun [2021]
, the authors propose using the bracketing number to discretize the infinite function class, which allows us to apply results in function approximation to special cases like the tabular setting. One essential flaw of using the bracketing number in model-based methods is that it introduces an improper function class, i.e., a class that contains elements that are not probabilities (e.g.,
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
in Lemma 6 of
Uehara and Sun [2021]
).
This flaw becomes problematic in their proof. Specifically, in their proof, they refer to
Agarwal et al. [2020]
, where the authors utilize the squared-Hellinger distance and use two forms of its definition:

\displaystyle\textup{{H}${}^{2}$}(q\|p)=\int(\sqrt{p(z)}-\sqrt{q(z)})^{2}dz\quad\textup{and}\quad\textup{{H}${}^{2}$}(q\|p)=2-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}\Big[1-\sqrt{p(z)q(z)}\Big].

However, since one of the functions is essentially not a probability (or density), the two definitions contradict each other at a fundamental level. As a result, traditional discretization methods like bracketing and covering cannot be straightforwardly applied in model-based algorithms as they can in model-free ones. We may need to introduce new distance measures or assumptions, which could hopefully explain the mismatch in the order
N
with special cases like tabular settings and provide new insights into the problem of sample complexity in offline RL.

Appendix H
Extension Case: Supervised Learning and Sample-based Full Correlation

In this section, we shows that in the case of considering
supervised learning

[
Vapnik, 1998
,
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
]
in the DMOF framework, EDD (
Eq.

1
) achieves a fast convergence rate compared with the optimal loss.

In supervised learning, there is an input space
\mathcal{X}_{\texttt{sl}}
, an output
\mathcal{Y}_{\texttt{sl}}
, a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{\texttt{sl},i},y_{\texttt{sl},i}\}_{i\in[N]}
5
5

5

[N]
denotes the set of integers
\{1,2,3,\dots,N\}
.
that contains i.i.d. random variables from the distribution
d\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}_{\texttt{sl}}\times\mathcal{Y}_{\texttt{sl}})
. While the distribution
d
is
unknown
, we are given a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq(\mathcal{X}_{\texttt{sl}}\to\mathcal{Y}_{\texttt{sl}})
together with a dataset
\mathcal{D}
and wish to find a

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\sum_{(x_{\texttt{sl},i},y_{\texttt{sl},i})\in\mathcal{D}}L(\widehat{h}(x_{\texttt{sl},i}),y_{\texttt{sl},i})=\mathbb{E}_{(X_{\texttt{sl}},Y_{\texttt{sl}})\sim d}L(\widehat{h}(X_{\texttt{sl}}),Y_{\texttt{sl}}),

(45)

Supervised learning is a special instance of the DMOF with full correlation. We explain supervised learning with the DMOF in the following definition.

Definition 7

(Supervised Learning)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a feature space,
\ell:\mathcal{X}\times\Pi\to[0,B]

We define the loss function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(M,\pi)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim M}\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\ell(x,\pi)\Big].

We refer to this framework as the DMOF
over the supervised learning problem
(
\mathcal{X}
,
\ell
,
N
).

Remark 10
.

Here we explain the correspondence of the above definition and supervised learning:

consists of two parts (the input space and the output space),

Fast Convergence Rate in Supervised Learning

The fast convergence rate of supervised learning has been built in various ways

Nevertheless, as the focus of this paper is the partially correlated offline decision making, we think that

H.1
Proof of

6

\displaystyle\textup{{EOEC}}_{\lambda}(M,\mathcal{M},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\Delta(\Pi)}\max_{M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\big[\mathcal{L}(M^{\prime},p)+\lambda\cdot\sum_{n\in[N]}\log(\mathbb{P}_{M^{\prime}}(X_{n})/\mathbb{P}_{M}(X_{n}))\big]
