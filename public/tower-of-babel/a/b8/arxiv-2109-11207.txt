Callan-Rubakov Effect and Higher Charge Monopoles
T. Daniel Brennan

Abstract
In this paper we study the interaction between magnetic monopoles and massless fermions. In the low energy limit, the monopole’s magnetic field polarizes the fermions into purely in-going and out-going modes. Consistency requires that the UV fermion-monopole interaction leads to non-trivial IR boundary conditions that relate the in-going to out-going modes. These non-trivial boundary conditions lead to what is known as the Callan-Rubakov effect. Here we derive the effective boundary condition by explicitly integrating out the UV degrees of freedom for the general class of spherically symmetric S ​ U ​ ( N ) SU(N) monopoles coupled to massless fermions of arbitrary representation. We then show that the boundary conditions preserve symmetries without ABJ-type anomalies. As an application we explicitly derive the boundary conditions for the stable, spherically symmetric monopoles associated to the S ​ U ​ ( 5 ) SU(5) Georgi-Glashow model and comment on the relation to baryon number violation.

Since the fermions can reach the boundary, we must impose boundary conditions on the fields there that relate the left- and right-handed fermions.

The question of how to describe fermionic boundary conditions is somewhat subtle. One approach is to simply impose boundary conditions of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{i}=M_{ij}\tilde{\psi}_{j}\big|_{r=0}\quad\text{or}\quad\psi_{i}=M_{ij}\tilde{\psi}_{j}^{\dagger}\big|_{r=0}~,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{i}(z)=\sum_{n}H_{i,n}z^{n}\quad,\quad{\widetilde{H}}_{i}(\bar{z})=\sum_{n}{\widetilde{H}}_{i,n}\bar{z}^{n}~,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{i,n}={\cal R}_{ij}{\widetilde{H}}_{j,n}\big|_{r=0}~,\end{split}

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{i}={\cal R}_{ij}\bar{J}_{j}\big|_{r=0}~.\end{split}

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Neumann}&:~(\partial-\bar{\partial})(H_{i}+{\widetilde{H}}_{i})\big|_{r=0}=0\quad\,\Leftrightarrow\quad H_{i,n}={\widetilde{H}}_{i,n}\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Neumann}:&~H_{i}={\widetilde{H}}_{i}\big|_{r=0}\qquad\,\Rightarrow\quad\psi_{i}=\tilde{\psi}_{i}\big|_{r=0}~,\\

Previously, only the boundary conditions for

monopole were known

Callan:1982ac
;

The low energy theory of this configuration is described by

N_{f}
out-going s-wave fermions. These are described by an effective 2D theory of
N_{f}
left- and
N_{f}

is specified by

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal R}_{ij}=\begin{cases}1-\frac{2}{N_{f}}&i=j\\
-\frac{2}{N_{f}}&i\neq j\end{cases}\end{split}

(33)

Here we see that
{\cal R}

corresponds to the boundary condition that preserves a collection of non-anomalous symmetries with charge matrices given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{ai}=\begin{cases}\delta_{a,i}-\delta_{a,i+1}&a<N_{f}\\
1&a=N_{f}\end{cases}\quad,\qquad\bar{Q}_{ai}=\begin{cases}\delta_{a,i}-\delta_{a,i+1}&a<N_{f}\\
-1&a=N_{f}\end{cases}\end{split}

(34)

Here, the
U(1)_{1},...,U(1)_{N_{f}-1}
symmetries are a labeling symmetry where
U(1)_{a}
counts the flux of
\psi_{a},\tilde{\psi}_{a}
minus the flux of
\psi_{a+1},\tilde{\psi}_{a+1}
and
U(1)_{N_{f}}
is the gauge charge. One can explicitly show that the matrix
{\cal R}_{ij}
in (

{\cal R}_{ij}=(\bar{Q}^{-1}Q)_{ij}
using the above charge matrices.

As we show later, the physics of these boundary conditions comes from the fact that the fermions couple to the dynamical dyon degree of freedom (trapped W-bosons) on the monopole world volume. The boundary conditions then encode the physics of a fermion wave which, in scattering off of the monopole, excites the dyon degree of freedom which then radiates away the accumulated gauge charge and energy into all out-going modes that couple to it
Polchinski:1984uw
.

At first glance, it appears strange that the out-going flux for each
\bar{J}_{i}
is generically fractional. However, this is simply a fact that the currents
J_{i},\bar{J}_{i}
are not conserved currents in the quantum theory: each fermion number symmetry is broken by an ABJ anomaly. The magnetic field of the monopole together with the electric field of any low energy charged fermion combine together to turn on a non-trivial gauge background that breaks the conservation of all
J_{i},\bar{J}_{i}
. The fractional flux is simply a reflection of the fact that species number is not a good quantum number in our theory.
Rather, the boundary condition is diagonalized by taking a basis of conserved currents rather than a basis of anomalous species currents whose symmetries are broken in the presence of the monopole. It should not be a surprise that non-perturbative effects are reflected in monopole-fermion scattering even at low energy since monopoles are inherently non-perturbative features of non-abelian gauge theory.

The general effective boundary conditions for higher charge monopoles and/or for fermions in arbitrary representations are not known. It is presumed that the physics is very similar to the case of the standard Callan-Rubakov effect. However, this is not immediately obvious since increasing the magnetic field or dimension of the coupled representations naturally leads to fermions of higher angular momentum which experience an angular momentum barrier that repulses their wave function from the core of the monopole.
Further, it is generally unknown how to characterize what kinds of symmetries are preserved by monopole boundary conditions although it is presumed that any symmetry which experiences an ABJ-type anomaly will be violated. In the rest of this paper, we will derive the general low energy effective boundary conditions for spherically symmetric monopoles coupled to fermions of arbitrary representation and discuss their interplay with global symmetries.

Now we would like to describe the low energy effective gauge theory in the presence of

We can now parametrize the perturbations of the gauge field around this background:

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{SU(N)}=A_{G_{\Phi_{\infty}}}+2T_{3}A_{Dirac}+(W_{+}+W_{-})~,\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\varphi}=&\frac{1}{4\pi}\int m_{W}^{2}r^{2}\,\textrm{csch}\,^{2}(m_{W}r)\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{\varphi}^{2}+gA_{0}\dot{\varphi}\right)d^{2}\Omega\,d^{2}x\\
&=\int\delta(m_{W}x)\left(\dot{\varphi}^{2}+gA_{0}\dot{\varphi}\right)d^{2}x=\int\left(\frac{1}{2}m_{W}\dot{\varphi}^{2}+gA_{0}\dot{\varphi}\right)dt~,\end{split}

(72)

where in the final step we rescaled
\varphi\to m_{W}\varphi
. Here we see that the dyon degree of freedom contributes to the effective low energy theory as a quantum mechanical rotor localized on the monopole world volume. A similar computation holds for the case of dyon degrees of freedom on a general spherically symmetric monopole.

4
Low Energy Fermions

Now we would like to derive the interaction between the low energy fermion modes and the dyon degrees of freedom. Due to the fact that the long range electric field suppresses long lived excitations of the dyon field, any fermion-dyon interaction should be integrated out to give an effective boundary condition for the long range fermion fields.

One way to determine the fermion-dyon interaction is from the explicit form of the fermion zero-modes of the full UV Dirac equation. In solving the Dirac equation, we are treating the gauge field as a classical background so that the solutions encode the tree level interaction of the fermions with the monopole. We can then determine the dyon interaction by matching the low energy fermions onto the large
r
limit of the fermion zero-modes and imposing conservation of gauge symmetry.

4.1
Fermion and Angular Momentum

We would now like to solve for the fermion zero-modes in the presence of a spherically symmetric monopole. The spherical symmetry implies that the fermions can also be decomposed into representations of the total angular momentum generators which in the vector gauge are written:

4.3
Low Energy Effective Action

Now we would like to use the explicit form of the fermion zero modes to derive the fermion-dyon coupling. As we discussed in Section

From the asymptotics of the fermion zero-modes in the previous section, it is clear that the long range degrees of freedom are related by a boundary condition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi_{\mu,m}(t,r=0)\leftrightarrow\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}(t,r=0)~,\end{split}

-bosons. And further, as we demonstrated in the previous section, the only light charged degree of freedom in the core is the dyon degree of freedom. This implies that the boundary condition must be of the form

(104)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{bnd}=\sum_{\mu}\int\delta(r)e^{ic^{I}_{\mu}\varphi_{I}}\chi_{\mu,m}^{\dagger}\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}\,d^{2}x+c.c.~.\end{split}

(105)

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{3}=[t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{N-1}]^{\vee}\quad,\quad I_{3}=[i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N-1}]^{\vee}\quad,\quad\gamma_{m}=[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},...,\gamma_{N-1}]^{\vee}~,\end{split}

(118)

which are related by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{m}=T_{3}-I_{3}=[t_{1}-i_{1},t_{2}-i_{2},...,t_{N-1}-i_{N-1}]^{\vee}~.\end{split}

(119)

The action of the above Lie algebra generators on a vector with weight

\Lambda=[n_{1},...,n_{N-1}]
can easily be computed in this notation: for example

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle T_{3},\Lambda\rangle=\sum_{I}n_{I}t_{I}~.\end{split}

(120)

We can now easily compute the couplings to the different
W
-bosons. Given a weight
\Lambda=[n_{1},...,n_{N-1}]
, the charge conjugate weight is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda\to\Lambda^{\ast}=[-n_{N-1},...,-n_{1}]~.\end{split}

(121)

The number of lowering operators required to map a positive weight
\Lambda\to\Lambda^{\ast}
is then given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C^{I}_{\Lambda}=\left(C_{\Lambda}^{1}\,,\,C_{\Lambda}^{2}\,,\,C_{\Lambda}^{3}\,,\,...\,,\,C_{\Lambda}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor}\,,\,C_{\Lambda}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor+1}\,,\,...\,,\,C_{\Lambda}^{N-1}\right)~,\end{split}

(122)

where
C_{\Lambda}^{I}
is the number of
E^{-}_{I}
required to go from
\Lambda\mapsto\Lambda_{\vee}
which are given explicitly by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C_{\Lambda}^{1}=\sum_{I}n_{I}\\
&C_{\Lambda}^{2}=(n_{1}+n_{N-1})+2\sum_{I\neq 1,N-1}n_{I}\\
&C_{\Lambda}^{3}=(n_{1}+n_{N-1})+2(n_{2}+n_{N-2})+3\sum_{I\neq 1,2,N-1,N-2}n_{I}\\
&\vdots\\
&C_{\Lambda}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor}=\sum_{M}M(n_{M}+n_{N-M})\\
&C_{\Lambda}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor+1}=\sum_{M\neq\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor}M(n_{M}+n_{N-M})+(n_{\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor}+n_{N-\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\right\rfloor})\\
&\vdots\\
&C_{\Lambda}^{N-1}=\sum_{I}n_{I}~.\end{split}

(123)

However, since the low energy effective theory generically has non-abelian gauge symmetry, the
E^{-}_{I}
that are not along broken directions in the
SU(N)
gauge group in some sense “come for free” due to coupling to long range gluons.
Thus, the coupling
c^{I}_{\Lambda}
to the dynamical dyon degrees of freedom is simply the truncation of the
C^{I}_{\Lambda}
to the set of
I
such that
i_{I}=0
.

We then find that the low energy effective theory of fermions and dyons is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}S=\sum_{\mu,m}&\int d^{2}x\Big[i\chi^{\dagger}_{\mu,m}\partial_{-}\chi_{\mu,m}+i\chi^{\dagger}_{\mu^{\ast},m}\partial_{+}\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}+i\delta(r)e^{ic^{\mu}_{I}\varphi_{I}}\chi_{\mu,m}^{\dagger}\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}+c.c.\Big]\\
&+\sum_{I}\int dt~\frac{1}{2}m_{W}\,\dot{\varphi}_{I}^{2}~.\end{split}

(124)

We can interpret the dyon interaction term as a boundary term that imposes the boundary condition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}=e^{-ic^{\mu}_{I}\varphi_{I}}\chi_{\mu,m}\big|_{r=0}~.\end{split}

(125)

We can now “unfold” our theory to recast our theory in a form similar the model studied in
Polchinski:1984uw
. By this, we mean to map the theory with a boundary interaction on the half-plane to a theory with a localized interaction on the full plane.
To do this, let us introduce the coordinate

\displaystyle\begin{split}x=\begin{cases}-r&x<0\\
r&x>0\end{cases}\end{split}

(126)

and identify the single chiral fermion

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi_{\mu,m}(t-x)=\begin{cases}\chi_{\mu,m}(t+x)&x<0\\
\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}(t-x)&x>0\end{cases}\end{split}

(127)

on the
(x,t)
-plane. We will also use the field redefinition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi_{\mu,m}\big|_{r=0}\to e^{i\frac{c^{\mu}_{I}}{2}\varphi_{I}}\chi_{\mu,m}\big|_{r=0}\quad,\quad\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}\big|_{r=0}\to e^{-i\frac{c^{\mu}_{I}}{2}\varphi_{I}}\chi_{\mu^{v}ee,m}\big|_{r=0}\end{split}

(128)

to eliminates the above boundary condition/field discontinuity in exchange for the interaction term

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal L}_{int}=\Theta(r)c^{(\mu)}_{I}\dot{\varphi}_{I}\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mu,m}\Psi_{\mu,m}~,\end{split}

(129)

where here
\Theta(r)=\Theta_{heviside}(r)-\frac{1}{2}
. The unfolded action is now

\displaystyle\begin{split}S=\sum_{\mu\in S_{R},m}\int d^{2}x\left[i\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mu,m}\Big(\partial_{+}-i\Theta(x)c^{I}_{\mu}\dot{\varphi}_{I}\Big)\Psi_{\mu,m}\right]+\sum_{I}\int dt\,\frac{1}{2}m_{W}\dot{\varphi}_{I}^{2}~.\end{split}

(130)

4.4
Effective Boundary Condition

We would now like to examine the dynamics of the effective theory above in order to determine the effective boundary conditions for the fermions. These boundary conditions can be found by simply integrating out the
\varphi_{I}
degrees of freedom.
The physics of the dyon-fermion system are very similar to the toy model considered in
Polchinski:1984uw
. Here we will follow a similar analysis to integrate out the
\varphi_{I}
degrees of freedom.

Let us consider the theory described by the action in (
130
). To facilitate the computations in this section we will introduce an index
A
for the fermions
\Psi_{A}
that encodes both
\mu\in S_{R}
and
m=-j_{\mu},...,j_{\mu}
. With this notation, the action can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}S=\sum_{\mu\in S_{R},m}\int d^{2}x\left[i\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mu,m}\Big(\partial_{+}-i\Theta(x)c^{I}_{\mu}\dot{\varphi}_{I}\Big)\Psi_{\mu,m}\right]+\sum_{I}\int dt\,\frac{1}{2}m_{W}\dot{\varphi}_{I}^{2}~.\end{split}

(131)

This theory has a series of currents that correspond to a
U(1)
rotation of each
\Psi_{A}
individually:

\Psi_{B}\to e^{i\phi\delta_{AB}}\Psi_{B}\quad\Rightarrow\quad J_{A}(x)=\Psi^{\dagger}_{A}(x)\Psi_{A}(x)~.

(132)

The corresponding symmetries have ABJ-type anomalies and consequently their currents are not conserved. Since the coupling to
\varphi_{I}
appears as a gauge coupling, the conservation equation for the current is given

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{+}J_{A}=\dot{\varphi}_{I}c^{I}_{A}\delta(x)~.\end{split}

(133)

The action is also manifestly shift invariant under
\varphi_{I}\to\varphi_{I}+\beta
for any constant
\beta
. There is a corresponding conserved charge:

Q=m_{W}\dot{\varphi}+\int dx\,\Theta(x)\sum_{A}J_{A}(x)~.

(134)

Together, the conservation equations satisfy:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{+}J_{A}=-\frac{c^{I}_{A}}{m_{W}}\Pi_{I}\delta(x)\quad,\quad&\partial_{t}\Pi_{I}=\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}\int dx\,\delta(x)J_{A}(x)~,\end{split}

(135)

where
\Pi_{I}
is the conjugate momentum to
\varphi_{I}
.

Integrating the conservation equations along a line of constant
x-t
(incoming plane wave) produces

\displaystyle\begin{split}&J_{A}^{(out)}=J_{A}^{(in)}-\frac{c^{I}_{A}}{m_{W}}\Pi_{I}\delta(x)~,\\
&\partial_{t}\Pi_{I}=\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}J_{A}^{(in)}-\frac{1}{m_{W}}\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}c^{J}_{A}\Pi_{J}~.\end{split}

(136)

which means that the incoming charge differs from the out-going charge by something proportional to the momentum of the periodic scalar.

We can now integrate out the
\varphi_{I}
in which we effectively set

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\langle\Pi_{I}\rangle=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad c^{I}_{A}\Pi_{I}=m_{W}J_{A}~.\end{split}

(137)

We want to solve this formula for
\Pi_{I}
and plug back into the
J_{A}
current conservation equation, but this is in general non-trivial since
c^{I}_{A}
is a non-rectangular matrix. However, the physics of this equation is clear: since
c^{I}_{A}
is the coupling between
\Pi_{I},J_{A}
any virtual excitation of the
\varphi_{I}
will decay via the bulk current of charge of the associated gauge charge:

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{I}=\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}\int\Theta(x)\dot{\varphi}_{I}J_{A}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varphi_{I}\to J_{I}=\frac{1}{N_{I}}\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}J_{A}~,\end{split}

(138)

where here
N_{I}
is a normalization constant that is fixed by gauge charge conservation.

Each
\varphi_{I}
has charge 2 under the associated
U(1)_{I}
gauge symmetry
13
13

13

This comes from our choice of convention
[H_{I},E^{\pm}_{I}]=\pm 2E^{\pm}_{I}
.
.
For each
\Psi_{A}
there is an associated weight
\mu^{(A)}=[\mu_{1}^{(A)},...,\mu_{N-1}^{(A)}]
. The charge of the out-going
\Psi_{A}
under
U(1)_{I}
is then given by
-\mu^{(A)}_{N-I}
and the total charge of the current is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}N_{I}\times Q_{I}[J_{I}]=-\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}\mu^{(A)}_{N-I}~.\end{split}

(139)

Thus, we find that the normalization
N_{I}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}N_{I}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}\mu^{(A)}_{N-I}~.\end{split}

(140)

Therefore, the current conservation equation is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{A}^{(out)}=J_{A}^{(in)}-\sum_{I,B}\frac{2}{\sum_{D}c^{I}_{D}\mu^{(D)}_{N-I}}c^{I}_{A}c^{I}_{B}j_{B}~.\end{split}

(141)

Translating into the notation of section
2
, the corresponding effective boundary condition is specified by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{J}_{A}={\cal R}_{AB}J_{B}\quad,\quad{\cal R}_{AB}=\delta_{AB}-\sum_{I}\frac{2}{\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}\mu^{(A)}_{N-I}}c^{I}_{A}c^{I}_{B}~.\end{split}

(142)

Thus, when the dyon is excited by an incoming fermion, the dyonic excitation dissipates through all possible fermion number channels. This can be interpreted as soft, charged radiation made up of fermionic modes that carries away the charge and energy from the monopole. This radiation generically can carry fractional flavor charges since the flavor symmetry is not actually preserved in the quantum theory. This soft radiation is sometimes referred to in the literature as “ propagating pulses of vacuum polarization”
Polchinski:1984uw
.

5
Preserved Symmetries

We would now like to determine what global symmetries are preserved by the boundary conditions specified by a choice of
{\cal R}
.
We would like to consider a collection of fermions that transform under representations
\{R_{i}\}
in the presence of an
SU(N)
monopole.

Let us consider a
U(1)
symmetry of the fermion fields that rotates each
\psi_{R_{i}}
individually:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{R_{i}}\to e^{iq_{i}\phi}\psi_{R_{i}}~.\end{split}

(143)

The charges of the in-going/out-going low energy fermion modes define the vectors
q_{A}^{(in)},q_{A}^{(out)}
. The symmetry is preserved iff the in-going charge is equal to the out-going charge:

\displaystyle\begin{split}q_{A}^{(in)}={\cal R}_{AB}\,{q}_{B}^{(out)}~.\end{split}

(144)

For symmetries that commute with the
SU(N)
gauge symmetry
q_{A}^{(in)}=q_{A}^{(out)}=q_{A}

and therefore the associated global symmetry is in fact preserved if
q_{A}
is a unit eigenvector of the matrix
{\cal R}_{AB}
.

More generally, we can consider IR symmetries that are a linear combination of UV gauge symmetry with UV global symmetries such as
B-L
symmetry in the standard model. In this case
q_{A}^{(in)}\neq q_{A}^{(out)}
and the condition that the symmetry is preserved by the boundary condition is (
144
).

In general, the boundary conditions specified by a matrix
{\cal R}
can preserve non-abelian symmetries which we can infer by studying the preserved abelian symmetries
Smith:2020nuf
. Given a choice of
{\cal R}
, one can construct an integer lattice
\Lambda_{\cal R}
that is generated by the charge vectors of the preserved abelian symmetries. The boundary conditions specified by
{\cal R}
then preserve a semi-simple non-abelian symmetry group
G
when
\Lambda_{\cal R}
contains the weight lattice of
G
as a sublattice
\Lambda_{wt}[G]\subset\Lambda_{R}
.

We would now like to determine under what conditions a global symmetry is violated by the monopole boundary conditions.
Due to the UV physics encoded by the monopole boundary conditions it is clear that a global symmetry will be broken if the process

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{I}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{N_{I}}\sum_{A}c^{I}_{A}J_{A}~,\end{split}

(145)

violates the global symmetry.

Let us consider a global symmetry
U(1)_{q}
under which a fermion of representation
R
has charge
q_{R}
. Since
\varphi
is uncharged under
U(1)_{q}
, the violation of
U(1)_{q}
is measured by the charge of the right hand side of (
145
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta Q_{q}^{(I)}=\frac{1}{N_{I}}\sum_{A}q_{A}^{(out)}c^{I}_{A}=\frac{q_{R}}{N_{I}}\sum_{\mu\in S_{R}}m_{\mu}c_{\mu}^{I}~,\end{split}

(146)

where
m_{\mu}
is the number of fermions with weight
\mu\in S_{R}
. Now, since
c^{I}_{\mu}
encodes the gauge charges of the operator
\chi_{\mu,m}^{\dagger}\chi_{\mu^{\ast},m}
, we can compute
c^{I}_{\mu}
as the difference in charge

\displaystyle\begin{split}c^{I}_{\mu}=Q_{I}[\psi_{\mu}]-Q_{I}[\psi_{\mu^{\ast}}]~,\end{split}

(147)

under the corresponding
U(1)_{I}
gauge symmetry generated by

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{I}=\frac{1}{N}{{\rm diag\,}}(\underbrace{N-I,...,N-I}_{I\text{-times}},\underbrace{-I,...,-I}_{N-I\text{-times}})~.\end{split}

(148)

Thus we find that the total charge violation is computed by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta Q_{q}^{(I)}=\frac{1}{N_{I}}\sum_{R}\sum_{\mu\in S_{R}}q_{R}\left(Q_{I}[\psi_{\mu}]-Q_{I}[\psi_{\mu^{\ast}}]\right)m_{\mu}~,\end{split}

(149)

which proportional to the coefficient of the 2D ABJ-type anomaly of
U(1)_{q}
. Since the 2D theory is simply the low energy effective theory of the 4D
SU(N)
theory, the 2D anomaly must match an ABJ anomaly of the full 4D theory. Therefore, if a global symmetry has no ABJ anomaly, then
\Delta Q_{q}^{(I)}=0
and the scattering process will preserve the symmetry regardless of ’t Hooft anomalies.

This relation can be seen explicitly in the case where
{\vec{I}}=0
. From
Brennan:2021ucy
, we know that the multiplicity of fermions with weight
\mu\in S_{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{\mu}=2\langle T_{3},\mu\rangle~,\end{split}

(150)

and additionally,
c^{I}_{\mu}
can be computed by

\displaystyle\begin{split}c^{I}_{\mu}=\langle\rho_{I},\mu\rangle\quad,\quad\rho_{I}=[1,2,3,...,\underbrace{I,...,I}_{\small{N-2I+1}},I-1,...,1]^{\vee}~.\end{split}

(151)

We can now write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\mu\in S_{R}}m_{\mu}c_{\mu}^{I}=2\sum_{\mu\in\Delta_{R}^{+}}\langle T_{3},\mu\rangle\langle\rho_{I},\mu\rangle=\sum_{\mu\in\Delta_{R}}\langle T_{3},\mu\rangle\langle\rho_{I},\mu\rangle~,\end{split}

(152)

where here we used the fact that
c^{I}_{\mu}\geq 0
if
\langle T_{3},\mu\rangle>0
which follows form the fact that
\langle\mu,\rho_{I}\rangle=c^{I}_{\mu}
, which is the number of
E^{-}_{I}
required to go from the weight
\mu\to\mu^{\ast}
, is manifestly positive in the construction of highest weight representations.

We can now rewrite the charge violation coming from
\Psi_{R}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta Q_{q}^{(I)}=\frac{q_{R}}{N_{I}}\times\,\textrm{Tr}\,_{R}[T_{3}\rho_{I}]~,\end{split}

(153)

where
\,\textrm{Tr}\,_{R}
is the trace in the
R
representation and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{I}={{\rm diag\,}}(\underbrace{1,...,1}_{I},0,...,0,\underbrace{-1,...,-1}_{I})~.\end{split}

(154)

The contribution to global charge violation can then be further simplified to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta Q_{q}^{(I)}=\frac{q_{R}I_{2}(R)}{N_{I}}\times\,\textrm{Tr}\,_{f}[T_{3}\rho_{I}]~,\end{split}

(155)

where the trace is now taken in the fundamental representation. The prefactor
q_{R}I_{2}(R)
matches the anomaly coefficient of an ABJ-anomaly between
U(1)_{q}
and
SU(N)
which indicates that the charge violation arises from such anomalies.

Therefore, we see that the fermion boundary conditions on the monopole explicitly violate global symmetries that have ABJ anomalies.

Appendix A

In this appendix we review the different solutions of the Dirac operator that are discussed in the main body.

A.1
The Abelian Monopole

Here we would like to solve for the solutions to the Dirac equation coupled to an abelian monopole connection. Here we will take the connection to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}A=\gamma_{m}A_{Dirac}=\frac{\gamma_{m}}{2}(1-\cos\theta)d\phi~,\end{split}

(217)

where
\gamma_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is the magnetic charge and
\sigma=\pm 1
.
The time independent Dirac operator can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}D_{\mu}=\left(\sigma^{\hat{r}}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\sigma^{\hat{\theta}}\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\sigma^{\hat{\phi}}\partial_{\phi}+\frac{i\gamma_{m}}{2r\sin\theta}\sigma^{\hat{\phi}}(1-\cos(\theta))\right)~,\end{split}

(218)

where we use the notation

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{\hat{r}}=\sigma^{3}\cos\theta+(\sigma^{1}\cos\phi+\sigma^{2}\sin\phi)\sin\theta~,\\
&\sigma^{\hat{\theta}}=-\sigma^{3}\sin\theta+(\sigma^{1}\cos\phi+\sigma^{2}\sin\phi)\cos\theta~,\\
&\sigma^{\hat{\phi}}=-\sigma^{1}\sin\phi+\sigma^{2}\cos\phi~.\end{split}

(219)

If we now frame rotate by

\displaystyle\begin{split}U^{~\beta}_{\alpha}=e^{\frac{i\phi}{2}\sigma^{0}\bar{\sigma}^{3}}e^{\frac{i\theta}{2}\sigma^{0}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}~,\end{split}

(220)

then we find that the Dirac operator becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}U^{\dagger}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}D_{\mu}U=\sigma^{3}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)+\frac{1}{r}\left(\sigma^{1}\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sin(\theta)}\left(\sigma^{2}\partial_{\phi}+\frac{i\gamma_{m}\sigma^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}(i\sigma^{2}\gamma_{m}+\sigma^{1})\cos\theta\right)\right)~.\end{split}

(221)

Now let us act with the Dirac operator on a fermion
\psi_{\mu_{i}}
of charge
\langle\gamma_{m},\mu_{i}\rangle=q_{i}
.
Under the field redefinition

\psi_{\mu_{i}}=\frac{e^{i\left(m-\frac{q_{i}}{2}\right)\phi}}{r}U\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}~,

(222)

the Dirac operator acting on
\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}
further simplifies to

\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}D_{\mu}=\left(\sigma^{3}\partial_{r}-\frac{{\cal K}}{r}\right)\quad,\quad{\cal K}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&L^{+}_{m,\frac{q_{i}-1}{2}}\\
L^{-}_{m,\frac{q_{i}+1}{2}}&0\end{array}\right)^{\dot{\alpha}\alpha}~,

(223)

where

L^{\pm}_{m,m^{\prime}}=\partial_{\theta}\pm\frac{1}{\sin\theta}(m-m^{\prime}\cos\theta)~.

(224)

The
L^{\pm}_{m,m^{\prime}}
operators are diagonalized by the Wigner small
d
functions:

L^{\pm}_{m,m^{\prime}}d^{j}_{m,m^{\prime}}(\theta)=\mp\sqrt{j(j+1)-m^{\prime}(m^{\prime}\pm 1)}d^{j}_{m,m^{\prime}\pm 1}(\theta)~,

(225)

which are only defined for

\displaystyle\begin{split}j\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\quad,\quad j-m,j-m^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\quad,\quad|m|\,,\,|m^{\prime}|\leq j~.\end{split}

(226)

We can then further reduce the ansatz for the Dirac operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{+}(r)\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{i}+1}{2}}(\theta)\\
f_{-}(r)\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{i}-1}{2}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)~,\end{split}

(227)

on which the Dirac operator acts as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}D_{\mu}\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}=\left(\sigma^{3}\partial_{r}+\frac{i\sigma^{2}}{r}\sqrt{\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{q_{i}^{2}}{4}}\right)\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}~.\end{split}

(228)

We can then solve for the spectrum of the Dirac operator by solving the matrix ODE.
While the spectrum is somewhat involved the spectrum of
k=0
modes are explicitly written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{\mu_{i}}^{(k,m)}(r,\theta,\phi)=&c_{+}\,\frac{e^{i\left(m-\frac{q_{i}}{2}\right)\phi}}{r}r^{\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-q_{i}^{2}/4}}\,U(\theta,\phi)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{i}+1}{2}}(\theta)\\
d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{i}-1}{2}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)\\
&+c_{-}\,\frac{e^{i\left(m-\frac{q_{i}}{2}\right)\phi}}{r}r^{-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-q_{i}^{2}/4}}\,U(\theta,\phi)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{i}+1}{2}}(\theta)\\
-d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{i}-1}{2}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)~.\end{split}

(229)

Requiring that the energy density be finite at
r\to 0,\infty
then implies that there are no solutions for
q_{i}=0
and restricts us to the solutions for which

\displaystyle\begin{split}j=j_{i}:=\frac{|q_{i}|-1}{2}~,\end{split}

(230)

in the case for
q_{i}\neq 0
.
When restricted to this class of solutions, which are spin polarized, the full spectrum of the Dirac operator is written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{\mu_{i}}^{(k,m)}=\frac{U(\theta,\phi)}{r}\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}^{(k,m)}=\frac{U(\theta,\phi)}{r}\begin{cases}e^{ikr}e^{i(m-q_{i}/2)\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{j_{i}}_{-m,j_{i}}(\theta)\\
0\end{array}\right)&q_{i}>0\\
e^{-ikr}e^{i(m-q_{i}/2)\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
d^{j_{i}}_{-m,-j_{i}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)&q_{i}<0\end{cases}\end{split}

(231)

We call these modes
plane-wave normalizable because the
1/r
behavior of
\psi
is the leading term in the partial wave expansion. These solutions have time-dependence of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{\mu_{i}}^{(k,m)}=\frac{U(\theta,\phi)}{r}\hat{\psi}_{\mu_{i}}^{(k,m)}=\frac{U(\theta,\phi)}{r}\begin{cases}e^{ik(t+r)}e^{i(m-q_{i}/2)\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{j_{i}}_{-m,j_{i}}(\theta)\\
0\end{array}\right)&q_{i}>0\\
e^{ik(t-r)}e^{i(m-q_{i}/2)\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
d^{j_{i}}_{-m,-j_{i}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)&q_{i}<0\end{cases}\end{split}

(232)

Thus, we see that there are
|q_{i}|
polarized, spherical fermion modes in the IR for each
q_{i}\neq 0
that are in-going for
q_{i}>0
and out-going for
q_{i}<0
.

A.2
The Abelian Monopole in
SU(N)

In order to compare the solutions of the Dirac equation in the abelian monopole background to the fermion zero-modes in the non-abelian monopole background, we need to solve for the fermion modes in the canonical gauge. This can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{can.}=T_{3}(1-\cos\theta)d\phi\pm i\frac{I_{\pm}}{2}e^{\mp i\phi}(d\theta\mp i\sin\theta d\phi)~,\end{split}

(233)

where here
e^{4\pi iT_{3}}=\mathds{1}_{SU(N)}
. After the frame rotation (
220
) the time independent Dirac operator can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Bigg[\sigma^{3}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)+\frac{{\cal K}}{r}&-\left(\sigma^{-}e^{-i\phi}I_{+}-\sigma^{+}e^{i\phi}I_{-}\right)\Bigg]~,\end{split}

(234)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal K}&=\sigma^{1}\left(\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}{\rm cot}(\theta)\right)+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\sigma^{2}\Big((\partial_{\phi}+iT_{3})-i\cos(\theta)T_{3}\Big)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&D^{+}_{T_{3}-\frac{1}{2}}\\
D^{-}_{T_{3}+\frac{1}{2}}&0\end{array}\right)~,\end{split}

(235)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\pm}_{q}=\partial_{\theta}\pm\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\Big(-i(\partial_{\phi}+iT_{3})-q\cos\theta\Big)~.\end{split}

(236)

Now let us consider the time-independent Dirac equation acting on a fermion
\psi_{R}
of representation
R
of
SU(N)
. Let us decompose
\psi_{R}
into weights

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{R}=\sum_{\mu_{a}\in\Delta_{R}}\psi_{R}^{a}\,v_{\mu_{a}}~.\end{split}

(237)

{\cal K}
acts simply on the
SU(2)_{J}
-representations such that given a spin-
j
representation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{R}^{a\,(j)}=U(\theta,\phi)e^{i(m-q_{a}/2)\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{a,+}(r)\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{a}+1}{2}}(\theta)\\
f_{a,-}(r)\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{a}-1}{2}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)\quad,\quad\mu_{a}\in\Delta^{(j)}_{R}~,\end{split}

(238)

where
\langle T_{3},\mu_{a}\rangle=\frac{q_{a}}{2}
and
\psi_{R_{A}}^{(j)}
is the spin-
j
solution of
\psi_{R_{A}}
. Here,

{\cal K}
acts as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal K}\cdot\psi_{R_{A}}^{a\,(j)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}\\
\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}&0\end{array}\right)\psi_{R_{A}}^{a\,(j)}~.\end{split}

(239)

Now, the Dirac equation reduces to a first order matrix ODE for the functions
f_{a,\pm}(r)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Big[r\partial_{r}+{\cal D}\Big]F(r)=0~,\end{split}

(240)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal D}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}\\
-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}&0\end{array}\right)+\sigma^{-}I_{+}+\sigma^{+}I_{-}+1~,\end{split}

(241)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(r)=\sum_{\mu_{a}\in\Delta_{R_{A}}^{(j)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{a,+}(r)\\
f_{a,-}(r)\end{array}\right)\,v_{\mu_{a}}~.\end{split}

(242)

The solutions are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(r)=P\,{\rm exp}\left\{-\int{\cal D}(r)dr\right\}\,F_{0}~,\end{split}

(243)

for
F_{0}
a constant vector. The set of
F_{0}
that corresponds to plane-wave normalizable solutions

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{r\to\infty}r\psi^{(j)}_{R_{A}}<\infty~,\end{split}

(244)

were solved for in
Brennan:2021ucy
. The solutions decompose with respect to the decomposition of
{\vec{I}}=\bigoplus_{i}{\vec{I}}^{(i)}
. Let us denote the decomposition by the set of spins
\{s_{I}\}
of the representations
\{{\vec{I}}^{(i)}\}
and
R_{s_{I}}
to be the restriction of the representation
R
to the spin-
s_{I}
representation of
{\vec{I}}^{(i)}
. By construction, for each
s_{I}
, the magnetic charge is a constant
Q_{s_{I}}
on
\psi_{R}
. The
s_{I}
-solutions of spin-
j
only exist for
j_{min}^{(s_{I})}\leq j\leq j_{max}^{(s_{I})}
where

\displaystyle\begin{split}j_{min/max}^{(s_{I})}=min/max_{\mu\in\Delta_{R_{s_{I}}}}|\langle\mu,T_{3}\rangle|-\frac{1}{2}~.\end{split}

(245)

The solutions are given explicitly by

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{a,+}(r)=0\quad,\quad f_{a,-}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\Big\langle j,Q_{s_{I}}-\frac{1}{2}+s_{I}-a\,\Big|\,|Q_{s_{I}}|-\frac{1}{2},Q_{s_{I}}-\frac{1}{2};s_{I},s_{I}-a\Big\rangle~,\end{split}

(246)

for
Q_{s_{I}}>0
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{a,+}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\Big\langle j,Q_{s_{I}}+\frac{1}{2}+s_{I}-a\,\Big|\,|Q_{s_{I}}|-\frac{1}{2},Q_{s_{I}}+\frac{1}{2};s_{I},s_{I}-a\Big\rangle\quad,\quad f_{a,-}(r)=0~,\end{split}

(247)

for
Q_{s_{I}}<0

where

\mu_{a}\in\Delta_{R_{s_{I}}}
where we have chosen an ordering
\langle I_{3},\mu_{a}\rangle>\langle I_{3},\mu_{a+1}\rangle
.
Note that there is no solution for modes with
Q_{s_{I}}=0
.

Importantly these modes are spin polarized. This means that the spectrum of the Dirac operator is then described by the solutions

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{R}^{a\,(j)}=\frac{e^{i(m-q_{a}/2)\phi}}{r}U(\theta,\phi)\begin{cases}e^{ik(t+r)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
C^{(j)}_{s_{I},a,-}\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{a}-1}{2}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)&Q_{s_{I}}>0\\
e^{ik(t-r)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C^{(j)}_{s_{I},a,+}\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{a}+1}{2}}(\theta)\\
0\end{array}\right)&Q_{s_{I}}<0\end{cases}\end{split}

(248)

for
\mu_{a}\in\Delta^{(j)}_{R_{s_{I}}}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}C^{(j)}_{s_{I},a,\pm}=\Big\langle j,Q_{s_{I}}\pm\frac{1}{2}+s_{I}-a\,\Big|\,|Q_{s_{I}}|-\frac{1}{2},Q_{s_{I}}\pm\frac{1}{2};s_{I},s_{I}-a\Big\rangle~.\end{split}

(249)

Note that in the cases where
\phi_{\infty}=0
, as we consider in the bulk of this paper, the theory has a preserved non-abelian gauge symmetry along the irreducible components
{\vec{I}}^{(i)}
. This means that the above solutions are gauge equivalent to the solutions where
\psi^{(j)}_{R}
is purely along a single weight
\mu\in\Delta_{R_{s_{I}}}
. In the bulk of the paper, we will find it convenient to pick a gauge such that each solution is along the top/bottom weight
\mu_{top/bot}\in\Delta_{R_{s_{I}}}
where
|\langle T_{3},\mu_{top/bot}\rangle|=j+\frac{1}{2}
.

A.3
The Non-Abelian Monopole

Now let us solve for solutions to the Dirac operator coupled to the
SU(N)
non-abelian monopole connection. Here we take the connection to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}A=T_{3}(1-\cos\theta)d\phi+\frac{i}{2}M_{+}(r)e^{-i\phi}(d\theta-i\sin\theta d\phi)-\frac{i}{2}M_{-}(r)e^{i\phi}(d\theta+i\sin\theta d\phi)~.\end{split}

(250)

Here we will be interested in solving for the zero-mode solutions which are spanned by the normalizable solutions in the kernel of the time-independent Dirac operator. Again, after performing a frame rotation, the Dirac operator can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Bigg[\sigma^{3}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\right)+\frac{{\cal K}}{r}&-\left(\sigma^{-}e^{-i\phi}M_{+}-\sigma^{+}e^{i\phi}M_{-}\right)\Bigg]~,\end{split}

(251)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal K}&=\sigma^{1}\left(\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}{\rm cot}(\theta)\right)+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\sigma^{2}\Big((\partial_{\phi}+iT_{3})-i\cos(\theta)T_{3}\Big)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&D^{+}_{T_{3}-\frac{1}{2}}\\
D^{-}_{T_{3}+\frac{1}{2}}&0\end{array}\right)~,\end{split}

(252)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\pm}_{q}=\partial_{\theta}\pm\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\Big(-i(\partial_{\phi}+iT_{3})-q\cos\theta\Big)~.\end{split}

(253)

Now let us consider the time-independent Dirac equation acting on a fermion
\psi_{R}
of representation
R
of
SU(N)
. Let us decompose
\psi_{R}
into weights

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{R}=\sum_{\mu_{a}\in\Delta_{R}}\psi_{R}^{a}\,v_{\mu_{a}}~.\end{split}

(254)

{\cal K}
acts simply on the
SU(2)_{J}
-representations such that given a spin-
j
representation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{R_{A}}^{a\,(j)}=U(\theta,\phi)e^{i(m-q_{a}/2)\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{a,+}(r)\,d^{j}_{-m,\frac{q_{a}+1}{2}}(\theta)\\
f_{a,-}(r)\,d^{j}_{-m,q_{a}-\frac{1}{2}}(\theta)\end{array}\right)\quad,\quad\mu_{a}\in\Delta^{(j)}_{R_{A}}~,\end{split}

(255)

where
\langle T_{3},\mu_{a}\rangle=\frac{q_{a}}{2}
and
\psi_{R}^{(j)}
is the spin-
j
solution of
\psi_{R}
. Here,

{\cal K}
acts as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal K}\cdot\psi_{R_{A}}^{a\,(j)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}\\
\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}&0\end{array}\right)\psi_{R_{A}}^{a\,(j)}~.\end{split}

(256)

Now, the Dirac equation reduces to a first order matrix ODE for the functions
f_{a,\pm}(r)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Big[r\partial_{r}+{\cal D}\Big]F(r)=0\quad,\quad F(r)=\sum_{\mu_{a}\in\Delta_{R_{A}}^{(j)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{a,+}(r)\\
f_{a,-}(r)\end{array}\right)\,v_{\mu_{a}}~,\end{split}

(257)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal D}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}\\
-\sqrt{(j+1/2)^{2}-\frac{q_{a}^{2}}{4}}&0\end{array}\right)+\sigma^{-}M_{+}+\sigma^{+}M_{-}+1~.\end{split}

(258)

The smooth solutions given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(r)=P\,{\rm exp}\left\{-\int{\cal D}(r)dr\right\}\,F_{0}~,\end{split}

(259)

for
F_{0}
a constant vector. As proven in
Brennan:2021ucy
, there exists a vector
F_{0}
that corresponds to a smooth, plane-wave normalizable solution

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{r\to\infty}r\psi^{(j)}_{R_{A}}<\infty~,\end{split}

(260)

of the time-independent Dirac equation iff the top and bottom weights of the spin-
j
representation of
R_{A}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\mu_{top},T_{3}\rangle=-\langle\mu_{bot},T_{3}\rangle=j+\frac{1}{2}\quad,\quad\mu_{top},\mu_{bot}\in\Delta^{(j)}_{R_{A}}~,\end{split}

(261)

have charge of opposite sign under
\gamma_{m}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\mu_{top},\gamma_{m}\rangle\langle\mu_{bot},\gamma_{m}\rangle<0~.\end{split}

(262)

Note that since the asymptotic form of the connection approaches the abelian connection in the canonical gauge, the asymptotic form of the fermion zero-modes can be expressed as a linear combination of the zero-momentum solutions found in the previous subsection.

As shown in
Brennan:2021ucy
, for each irreducible
SU(2)_{T}
-representation
R_{A}
and
j
that satisfies (
262
) there is a unique solution which has the asymptotic form of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{r\to\infty}\psi_{R_{A}}^{(j)}=\psi_{R_{A}}^{(j)-}-\psi_{R_{A}}^{(+)}~,\end{split}

(263)

where
\psi_{R_{A}}^{(j)\pm}
are given by the spin up/down solutions of (
248
).
