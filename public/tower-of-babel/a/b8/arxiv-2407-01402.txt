Superconstant inapproximability of decision tree learning
Caleb Koch, Carmen Strassle, Stanford, Li-Yang Tan, Stanford

Abstract
We consider the task of properly PAC learning decision trees with queries. Recent work of Koch, Strassle, and Tan showed that the strictest version of this task, where the hypothesis tree T T is required to be optimally small, is NP-hard. Their work leaves open the question of whether the task remains intractable if T T is only required to be close to optimal, say within a factor of 2 2 , rather than exactly optimal. We answer this affirmatively and show that the task indeed remains NP-hard even if T T is allowed to be within any constant factor of optimal. More generally, our result allows for a smooth tradeoff between the hardness assumption and inapproximability factor. As Koch et al.’s techniques do not appear to be amenable to such a strengthening, we first recover their result with a new and simpler proof, which we couple with a new XOR lemma for decision trees. While there is a large body of work on XOR lemmas for decision trees, our setting necessitates parameters that are extremely sharp and are not known to be attainable by existing such lemmas. Our work also carries new implications for the related problem of Decision Tree Minimization .

Introduction

Decision trees are a basic and popular way to represent data. Their simple logical structure makes them the prime example of an interpretable model. They are also the base model at the heart of powerful ensemble methods, such as XGBoost and random forests, that achieve state-of-the-art performance in numerous settings. Owing in part to their practical importance, the task of efficiently constructing decision tree representations of data has been intensively studied in the theory community for decades, in a variety of models and from both algorithmic and hardness perspectives. Indeed, on the heels of Cook and Karp’s papers on the theory of NP-hardness,
[
HR76
]
proved that a certain formulation of decision tree learning is NP-hard. Quoting their introduction, “While the proof to be given is relatively simple, the importance of this result can be measured in terms of the large amount of effort that has been put into finding efficient algorithms for constructing optimal binary decision trees.” This effort has only compounded over the years, with a recent surge of interest coming from the interpretable machine learning community; the 2022 survey
[
RCC+22
]
lists decision tree learning as the very first of the field’s “10 grand challenges”.

We consider the problem within the model of PAC learning with queries

Background and Context

2.1
Algorithms for properly learning decision trees

In the language of learning theory, we are interested in the task of properly PAC learning decision trees. We distinguish between
strictly-proper
learning, where the size
s^{\prime}
of the hypothesis decision tree has to exactly match the optimal size

of the target decision tree, and

s^{\prime}

[

]

is larger than

We now overview the fastest known algorithms for both settings.

Strictly-proper learning via dynamic programming.

There is a simple
2^{O(n)}

s

[

,
MR02
]

[
KST23a
]

Weakly-proper learning via Ehrenfeucht–Haussler.

The setting of weakly-proper learning allows for a markedly faster algorithm: a classic algorithm of
[
EH89
]
runs in

s^{\prime}=n^{O(\log s)}
.
[

]

is closer to

. There has been no algorithmic progress on this problem since 1989, and prior to our work, there were also no hardness results ruling out efficient algorithms achieving say
s^{\prime}=2s
.

3
Technical Overview

At a high level, our proof proceeds in two steps:

\circ

Step 1: Slight inapproximaibility.
We first give a new proof of
[
KST23a
]
’s result. In fact, we prove a statement that is (very) slightly stronger than the hardness of strictly-proper learning: we show that it is NP-hard for query learners to construct a decision tree of size
s^{\prime}=(1+\delta)\cdot s
for small constant
\delta<1
.
While such a slight strengthening is not of much independent interest, it is important for technical reasons because it establishes
some
inapproximability factor, albeit a small one, which we then amplify in the next step.

\circ

Step 2: Gap amplification.
We give a reduction that for any integer
r
runs in time
n^{O(r)}
and amplifies the inapproximability factor of
s^{\prime}/s=1+\delta
from the step above into
(1+\delta)^{r}
. In particular, for any arbitrarily large constant
C
this is a reduction that runs in polynomial time and amplifies the inapproximability factor to
C
.

At the heart of this reduction is a new XOR lemma for decision trees: roughly speaking, this lemma says that if decision trees of size
s^{\prime}
incur large error when computing
f
, then decision trees of size
(s^{\prime})^{r}
incur large error when computing the
r
-fold XOR
f^{\oplus r}(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(r)})\coloneqq f(x^{(1)})\oplus\cdots\oplus f(x^{(r)})
.

There is a large body of work on XOR lemmas for decision tree complexity
[
IRW94
,
NRS94
,
Sav02
,
Sha04
,
KŠdW07
,
JKS10
,
Dru12
,
BDK18
,
BB19
,
BKLS20
]
, but our setting necessitates extremely sharp parameters that are not known to be achievable by any existing ones. Most relevant to our setting is one by
[
Dru12
]
, but it only reasons about the error of decision trees of size
(s^{\prime})^{cr}
for some
c<1
instead of
(s^{\prime})^{r}
. This constant factor loss is inherent to
[
Dru12
]
’s proof technique, and we explain in
Remark

10.3
why we cannot afford even a tiny constant factor loss in the exponent.

Preliminaries

Notation and naming conventions.

We write
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. We use lower case letters to denote bitstrings e.g.
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and subscripts to denote bit indices:
x_{i}
for
i\in[n]
is the
i
th index of
x
. The string
x^{\oplus i}
is
x
with its
i
th bit flipped. We use superscripts to denote multiple bitstrings of the same dimension, e.g.
x^{(1)},x^{(2)},...,x^{(j)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. For a set
S
and an integer
r\geq 1
, we write
S^{r}
to denote the
r
-ary Cartesian product of the set.

Distributions.

We use boldface letters e.g.
\bm{x},\bm{y}
to denote random variables. For a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we write
\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,g)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})\neq g(\bm{x})]
. The support of the distribution is the set of elements with nonzero mass and is denoted
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
. For
r\geq 1
, we write
\mathcal{D}^{\otimes r}
to denote the
r
-wise product distribution
\underbrace{\mathcal{D}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{D}}_{r\text{ times }}
.

Decision trees.

The size of a decision tree
T
is its number of leaves and is denoted
|T|
. In an abuse of notation, we also write
T
for the function computed by the decision tree
T
. We say
T
computes a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
if
T(x)=f(x)
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. The decision tree complexity of a function
f
is the size of the smallest decision tree computing
f
and is denoted
\mathrm{DT}(f)
.

Restrictions and decision tree paths.

A restriction
\rho
is a set
\rho\subseteq\{x_{1},\overline{x}_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\overline{x}_{n}\}
of literals, and
f_{\rho}
is the subfunction obtained by restricting
f
according to
\rho
:
f_{\rho}(x^{\star})=f(x^{\star}|_{\rho})
where
x^{\star}|_{\rho}
is the string obtained from
x^{\star}
by setting its
i
th coordinate to
1
if
x_{i}\in\rho
,
0
if
\overline{x}_{i}\in\rho
, and otherwise setting it to
x^{\star}_{i}
. We say an input
x^{\star}
is consistent with
\rho
if
x_{i}\in\rho
implies
x^{\star}_{i}=1
and
\overline{x}_{i}\in\rho
implies
x^{\star}_{i}=0
.

Paths in decision trees naturally correspond to restrictions. A depth-
d
path can be identified with a set of
d
literals:
\pi=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{d}\}
where each
\ell_{i}
corresponds to a query of an input variable and is unnegated if
\pi
follows the right branch and negated if
\pi
follows the left branch.

Boolean Functions.

We use
f
to denote an arbitrary
n
-bit Boolean function,
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
. For a set
D\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, we write
f:D\to\{0,1\}
for the partial Boolean function defined on
D
. We use both partial and total functions and specify the setting by writing either
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
or
f:D\to\{0,1\}
. For
f:D\to\{0,1\}
, the sensitivity of
f
on
x\in D
and the certificate complexity of
f
’s value on
x
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)

\displaystyle=\{x^{\oplus i}\in D:f(x)\neq f(x^{\oplus i})\text{ for }i\in[n]\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cert}(f,x)

\displaystyle=|\pi|\text{ s.t. }\pi\text{ is the shortest restriction consistent with }x\text{ and }

\displaystyle\quad\quad f_{\pi}\text{ is a constant function}.

Note that both of these definitions are with respect to
D
. Also, we refer to the
sensitivity of
f
which is
\mathrm{Sens}(f)\coloneqq\max_{x\in D}|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|
.

Graphs.

An undirected graph
G=(V,E)
has
n
vertices
V\subseteq[n]
and
m=|E|
edges
E\subseteq V\times V
. The degree of a vertex
v\in V
is the number of edges containing it:
|\{e\in E:v\in e\}|
. The graph
G
is degree-
d
if every vertex
v\in V
has degree at most
d
. We often use letters
v,u,w
to denote vertices of a graph
G
.

Learning.

In the PAC learning model, there is an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
and some unknown
target
function
f\in\mathcal{C}
from a fixed
concept
class
\mathcal{C}
of functions over a fixed domain. An algorithm for learning
\mathcal{C}
over
\mathcal{D}
takes as input an error parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and has oracle access to an
example oracle

\textnormal{EX}(f,\mathcal{D})
. The algorithm can query the example oracle to receive a pair
(\bm{x},f(\bm{x}))
where
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
is drawn independently at random. The goal is to output a
hypothesis

h
such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,h)\leq\varepsilon
. Since the example oracle is inherently randomized, any learning algorithm is necessarily randomized. So we require the learner to succeed with some fixed probability e.g.
2/3
. A learning algorithm is
proper
if it always outputs a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{C}
. A learning algorithm with
queries
is given oracle access to the target function
f
along with the example oracle
\textnormal{EX}(f,\mathcal{D})
.

4.1
Vertex Cover

Vertex cover.

A vertex cover for an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
is a subset of the vertices
C\subseteq V
such that every edge has at least one endpoint in
C
. We write
\mathrm{VC}(G)
to denote the size of the smallest vertex cover of
G
. The
Vertex Cover
problem is to decide whether a graph contains a vertex cover of size
\leq k
. For
a>1
, an
a
-approximation of
Vertex Cover
corresponds to the problem of deciding whether a graph contains a vertex cover of size
\leq k
or every vertex cover has size at least
a\cdot k
. There is a polynomial-time greedy algorithm for vertex cover which achieves a
2
-approximation.

Hardness of approximation.

Constant factor hardness of
Vertex Cover
is known, even for bounded degree graphs (graphs whose degree is bounded by some universal constant). This is the main hardness result we reduce from in this work.

Theorem 3

(Hardness of approximating
Vertex Cover
)
.

There are constants
\delta>0
and
d\in\mathds{N}
such that if
Vertex Cover
can be
(1+\delta)
-approximated on
n
-vertex degree-
d
graphs in time
t(n)
, then
SAT
can be solved in time
t(n\mathrm{polylog}n)
.

Theorem

3
follows from the works of
[
PY91
,
AS98
,
ALM+98
]
. The fact that
Theorem

3
holds for constant degree graphs will be essential for our lower bound because it allows us to assume that
k
is large:
\mathrm{VC}(G)=\Theta(m)
.

Fact 4.1

(Constant degree graphs require large vertex covers)
.

If
G
is an
m
-edge degree-
d
graph, then
\mathrm{VC}(G)\geq m/d
.

This fact follows from the observation that in a degree-
d
graph each vertex can cover at most
d
edges.

Patching up a decision tree: Proof of
Lemma

3.3

We start with the following claim about building a decision tree from scratch using certificates.

Claim 5.1

(Building a decision tree out of certificates)
.

Let
f:D\to\{0,1\}
be a function with
D\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, then

\mathrm{DT}(f)\leq 1+\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f,x).

Proof.

Let
T
be the decision tree built iteratively by the following procedure. In the first iteration, pick an arbitrary
x\in f^{-1}(1)
and fully query the indices in
\mathrm{Cert}(f,x)
. Let
T_{i}
be the tree formed after the
i
th iteration. Then
T_{i+1}
is formed by choosing
x\in f^{-1}(1)
which has not been picked in a previous iteration. Then, at the leaf reached by
x
in
T_{i}
, fully query the indices in
\mathrm{Cert}(f,x)
(ignoring those indices which have already been queried along the path followed by
x
in
T_{i}
). Repeating this for
|f^{-1}(1)|
steps, yields a decision tree with at most
\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f,x)

internal nodes
. Therefore, the number of leaves is at most
1+\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f,x)
.

It remains to show that this tree exactly computes
f
. Specifically, we’ll argue that
f_{\pi}
is the constant function for every path
\pi
in the decision tree. If not, then there is an
x\in f^{-1}(1)
so that
x
follows the path
\pi
and
f_{\pi}
is nonconstant. But this is a contradiction since
\pi
consists of a certificate of
x
by construction.
∎

The next lemma shows that we can patch up a decision tree by querying certificates. This recovers
Lemma

3.3
in the setting where
D=\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Lemma 5.2

(Patch-up with respect to
1
-inputs)
.

Let
f:D\to\{0,1\}
be a function with
D\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
T
be a decision tree, then

\mathrm{DT}(f)\leq|T|+\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f_{\pi(x)},x)

Proof.

Let
\Pi
denote the set of paths in
T
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{DT}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathrm{DT}(f_{\pi})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\left(1+\sum_{x\in f_{\pi}^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f_{\pi},x)\right)

(
Claim

5.1
)

\displaystyle=|T|+\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{x\in f_{\pi}^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f_{\pi},x)

(
|\Pi|=|T|
)

\displaystyle=|T|+\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(1)}\mathrm{Cert}(f_{\pi(x)},x).

The last equality follows from the fact that the set of paths
\pi\in\Pi
partition
f^{-1}(1)
.
∎

Hard distribution lemma: Proof of
Lemma

Lemma

3.4
is proved for the
canonical hard distribution
for a partial function

.

Definition 6.1

(Canonical hard distribution)
.

For a function
f:D\to\{0,1\}
with
D\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, the canonical hard distribution,
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, is defined via the following experiment

•

sample
\bm{x}\sim f^{-1}(1)
u.a.r.

•

with probability
1/2
, return
\bm{y}\sim\mathrm{Sens}(f,\bm{x})
u.a.r.

•

with probability
1/2
, return
\bm{x}
.

When
f
is clear from context, we simply write
\mathcal{D}
.
We use the canonical hard distribution to prove the following result which recovers
Lemma

3.4
in the setting where
D=\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Lemma 6.2

(Hard distribution lemma)
.

Let
f:D\to\{0,1\}
be a nonconstant function for
D\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then for all
C\subseteq f^{-1}(1)
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
C
and and all of its sensitive neighbors such that for any decision tree
T
, we have

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(T,f)\geq\frac{1}{2|C|\mathrm{Sens}(f)}\sum_{x\in C}|\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)|.

First we prove a claim which counts the error under
\mathcal{D}
conditioned on the
1
-input obtained in the first sampling step.

Claim 6.3

(Error with respect to the canonical hard distribution conditioned on a
1
-input)
.

Let
T
be a decision tree,
f:D\to\{0,1\}
be a function, and let
\mathcal{D}
be the canonical hard distribution. For all
x\in f^{-1}(1)
,

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}}[T(\bm{z})\neq f(\bm{z})\mid\text{ first sampling }x\text{ from }f^{-1}(1)]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{|\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)|}{\max\{1,|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|\}}.

Proof.

If
|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|=0
then
|\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)|=0
. And if the RHS is
0
then the bound is vacuously true. Assume that
\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)\neq\varnothing
. Both
x
and all
y\in\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)
follow the same path in
T
and have the same leaf label. Since
f(x)=1
and
f(y)=0
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}}\big[T(\bm{z})\neq f(\bm{z})\mid\textnormal{first sampling }x

\displaystyle\textnormal{ from }f^{-1}(1)\big]

\displaystyle\geq\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}}\big[\bm{z}=x\mid\textnormal{first sampling }x\textnormal{ from }f^{-1}(1)\big],

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}}\big[\bm{z}\in\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)\mid\textnormal{first sampling }x\textnormal{ from }f^{-1}(1)\big]\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{|\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)|}{|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|}\bigg\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{|\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)|}{|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|}

where the inequality follows from the fact that the probability of an error is lower bounded by the probability that
\bm{z}=x
when the label for the path in
T
is
0
and is lower bounded by the probability that
\bm{z}\in\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)
when the label for the path is
1
. Note that if
\mathrm{Sens}(f_{\pi(x)},x)\neq\varnothing
then necessarily
|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|\geq 1
and
\max\{1,|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|\}=|\mathrm{Sens}(f,x)|
. Therefore, the proof is complete.
∎

(Sensitivity of

be the minimum vertex cover size of
G
and let
m
denote the number of edges of
G
. Then, if
\mathrm{error}_{\ell\text{-}\mathcal{D}_{G}^{\otimes r}}(T,\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}^{\oplus r})\leq\varepsilon
for the canonical hard distribution

, we have

|T|\geq\big[(\ell+1)(k+m)\big]^{r}-\varepsilon\big[8m(\ell+1)\big]^{r}

Proof.

Since
\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}
has
m
many
1
-inputs over the dataset
\ell\text{-}D_{G}
and
\mathrm{Sens}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge})=2(\ell+1)
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{4m(\ell+1)}\right)^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\ell\text{-}D_{G}^{r}\\
\mathrm{Sens}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{\oplus r},x)\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\max\{1,\mathrm{Sens}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{(i)},x^{(i)})\}

(
Lemma

9.1
)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{4m(\ell+1)}\right)^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\ell\text{-}D_{G}^{r}\\
\mathrm{Sens}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{\oplus r},x)\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\max\{1,\mathrm{Cert}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{(i)},x^{(i)})\}

(
Proposition

7.6
)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{4m(\ell+1)}\right)^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\ell\text{-}D_{G}^{r}\\
\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{\oplus r}\text{ is nonconstant}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\max\{1,\mathrm{Cert}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{(i)},x^{(i)})\}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{8m(\ell+1)}\right)^{r}(\mathrm{DT}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}^{\oplus r})-|T|).

(
Lemma

8.1
)

In this derivation, we used the fact that if an input
x\in\ell\text{-}D_{G}^{r}
is such that
\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{\oplus r}
is nonconstant, then it must be the case that there is some block
i\in[r]
where the path
\pi
does not fully restrict the sensitive coordinates in the edge indicator for block
i
, and therefore it must also be the case that
\mathrm{Sens}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}_{\pi(x)}^{\oplus r},x)\neq\varnothing
. Now, we can rearrange this lower bound on
\varepsilon
to obtain:

\displaystyle|T|

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{DT}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge}^{\oplus r})-\varepsilon\big[8m(\ell+1)\big]^{r}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{DT}(\ell\text{-}\mathrm{\sc IsEdge})^{r}-\varepsilon\big[8m(\ell+1)\big]^{r}

(
Theorem

6
)

\displaystyle\geq\big[(\ell+1)(k+m)\big]^{r}-\varepsilon\big[8m(\ell+1)\big]^{r}

(
Lemma

7.4
)

which completes the proof.
∎

The following proposition allows us to translate the above lower bound into a slightly simpler form.

Proposition 10.1
.

For all
a,b,r>0
such that
a\geq b
, we have
a^{r}-b^{r}\geq(a-b)^{r}
.

Proof.

Since
a\geq b
, we have

1\geq\left(1-\frac{b}{a}\right)^{r}+\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{r}.

Multiplying both sides of the inequality by
a^{r}
and rearranging gives the desired bound.
∎
