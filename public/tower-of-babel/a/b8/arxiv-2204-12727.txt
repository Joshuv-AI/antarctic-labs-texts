Effects of relaxation on photovoltaic effect and possibility of photocurrent within transparent region
Yugo Onishi, Hikaru Watanabe, Takahiro Morimoto, Naoto Nagaosa

Abstract
We theoretically study photocurrents in metals that break both inversion 𝒫 \mathcal{P} and 𝒯 \mathcal{T} symmetries within the transparent region. We find that the system under the ac electric fields is well described with an effective Hamiltonian and the photocurrent is of the order of 𝒪 ⁡ ( ω J / γ ) \order{\omegaoutput/\gamma} if the frequency of the induced current ω J \omega_{J} and the scattering rate γ \gamma satisfy ω J / γ ≪ 1 \omega_{J}/\gamma\ll 1 , and vanishes in the limit of ω J / γ → 0 \omega_{J}/\gamma\to 0 . On the other hand, the effective Hamiltonian description indicates that nonvanishing photocurrent can appear even in the transparent region if the system is thin enough compared to the mean free path in the direction of the induced current (where γ \gamma can be effectively regarded as 0). Candidate materials for the such photovoltaic effect within the transparent region include multiferroics breaking both 𝒫 \mathcal{P} and 𝒯 \mathcal{T} symmetries.



I
Introduction

Photovoltaic effect attracts recent intensive interests from
the viewpoints of both fundamental physics and applications
von Baltz and Kraut 1981
;
Sturman and Fridkin 1992
;
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
;
Young and Rappe 2012
;
Young et al. 2012
;
Sotome et al. 2019a
;
Sotome et al. 2019b
;
Sotome et al. 2021
;
Cook et al. 2017
;
Rangel et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2016
;
Nagaosa and Morimoto 2017
;
Matsyshyn et al. 2021
.
It is now realized that the bulk photovoltaic effects are closely related to the geometric aspects
of electronic states in solids (such as Berry phase
Berry 1984
)
that arise from
the breaking of the inversion symmetry
\cal{P}
in noncentrosymmetric materials.
Shift current and injection current are the two major mechanisms
for the photovoltaic effect in the second order in the electric field of light.
Time-reversal symmetry
\cal{T}

is another important symmetry;
the injection current requires
the broken
\cal{T}
either by the circularly polarized light or the
magnetic field/order of the material,
whereas the shift current does not require the breaking of
\cal{T}
.
When both
\cal{P}
and
\cal{T}
are broken,
the energy dispersion of the electrons becomes asymmetric
between
k
and
-k
, and many nonreciprocal phenomena
are expected including the magnetochiral anisotropy in dc transport
Tokura and Nagaosa 2018
.
Also insulators with broken
\cal{P}
and
\cal{T}
are called
multiferroics, which shows the strong coupling between the
electric and magnetic degrees of freedom such as the magnetoelectric
effect
Mostovoy 2005
;
Fiebig 2005
;
Tokura et al. 2014
.
It has been predicted that the excitation of the electromagnon by light in multiferroics can induce the dc current; the low energy light below the band gap can show the photovoltaic effect without the electronic particle-hole excitation
Morimoto and Nagaosa 2019
;
Morimoto et al. 2021
.
Furthermore, it has been shown that the soft phonon excitation
in ferroelectric BaTiO
3
without
\cal{T}
-breaking produces the dc shift current

Okamura et al. 2022
.
A common feature of all these photovoltaic effect is that
the absorption of light occurs in some form even if
the electronic excitation is virtual. Then, the crucial question here
is if the photovoltaic effect is possible even without the absorption of
light or not.

According to the standard perturbation theory
Parker et al. 2019
;
de Juan et al. 2020
;
Watanabe and Yanase 2021
;
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Gao et al. 2021
, it seems possible that, if both
\cal{T}
and
\cal{P}
symmetries are broken, finite photocurrent is induced even in the transparent region where the incident photon cannot create real excitations of electrons. Histrically, however, Belinicher
et al.
pointed out that there should be no dc photocurrent for the transparent region in the steady state if we properly take into account the effect of the relaxation
Belinicher et al. 1986
. The effect of relaxation on the photocurrent was discussed based on an effective Hamiltonian decades ago and they concluded that there should be no photocurrent in the steady state
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
. However, since their discussion is based on the semiclassical theory, it is not clear how the photocurrent in the presence of relaxation is affected by the interband matrix elements of the current operator, which can be crucial in the photocurrent such as the shift current.

In Ref.
de Juan et al. 2020
, it was also pointed out that the relaxation affects the photocurrent when the relaxation rate is larger than the frequency of the output current, although they studied only the region where the relaxation rate is much smaller than the frequency of the output current.

On the other hand, recent studies suggest that there is a possibility of finite photocurrent even in the transparent region
Shi et al. 2022
;
Golub and Glazov 2022
. In Ref.
Shi et al. 2022
, a system coupled with particle reservoirs is discussed and it is concluded that the photocurrent within the transparent region exists in such systems and their relation to the thermodynamics is also discussed. In Ref.
Golub and Glazov 2022
, Golub
et al.
discussed a Raman-like process utilizing impurity scattering. In this case, the frequency of the incident photon and that of the scattered photon are different, resulting in finite energy absorption to drive the finite current.
Since these studies discussed different situations or processes from the ones in Ref.
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
, it is important to extend the previous discussions in Ref.
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
and investigate the behavior of the photocurrent in the transparent region by incorporating relaxation effects and the interband matrix elements of the current operator.

In this paper, we perform analyses with a fully quantum mechanical theory on the photocurrent within the transparent region in the presence of relaxation effects.
As discussed previously in Ref.
de Juan et al. 2020
, there are two important frequencies (or equivalently time scales) that determine the photocurrent responses: the relaxation rate
\gamma
and the difference of injected and emitted lights frequencies which is equal to the frequency of current
\omega_{J}
. We usually consider the limit
\gamma\to 0
and
\omega_{J}\to 0
, and their order is crucial to understand the photocurrent behavior.
According to our theory, the photocurrent will vanish in the limit of
\omega_{J}/\gamma\to 0
, which is consistent with the previous discussions
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
, while if one can realize the condition
\omega_{J}\gg\gamma
effectively, nonvanishing photocurrent can appear. In this work, we often call the former case as “slow limit”, while the latter case is denoted as “fast limit”. Our theory also enables us to calculate the crossover at
\gamma\sim\omega_{J}
. We also briefly discuss the interpretation of the photocurrent in the fast limit as a Raman process. While phonon excitation and structural phase transition induced through the virtual excitation of electrons were discussed previously
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2021
, we show that a similar Raman process without phonons can leave an electronic excitation with a finite current in the fast limit. A similar Raman-like mechanism of photocurrent is proposed by Golub
et al.
recently
Golub and Glazov 2022
, but our interpretation does not involve impurity scattering in contrast to Ref.
Golub and Glazov 2022
. In terms of applications of the bulk photovoltaic effect to solar cells, if the frequency of the output current is finite but sufficiently small, it is possible to rectify the current to extract power. Therefore, our results show a new possibility of energy harvesting of light within the transparent region.

In general, it is a complicated task to compute nonlinear responses taking into account the effect of scattering. Although one of previous studies
Rostami and Cappelluti 2021
investigated the effect of scattering on the nonlinear optical responses including vertex correction, we employ another approach to this problem.
Specifically, we consider an effective Hamiltonian for the system in the presence of an ac electric field with frequencies smaller than the band gap. The perturbation in the effective Hamiltonian due to the applied field consists of two terms: one is a linear order term with respect to the external field and contains only an intraband contribution. This term does not contribute to the second order response in the clean limit, and thus we neglect it. The other term is second order in the external field and contributes to the second order response.
In particular, this effective Hamiltonian description allows us to use the standard linear response theory to study the second order responses to the external field and facilitates to include the effects of scattering in the calculation.
A similar approach of an effective Hamiltonian but with the Boltzmann equation was used in Ref.
Belinicher et al. 1986
. However, it is not clear in their approach how the interband matrix elements of the current operator can affect their results. We show that the effective current operator is given by the
k
-derivative of the effective Hamiltonian and extend the discussion in Ref.
Belinicher et al. 1986
.

II
, we briefly review the known results from the standard perturbation theory in the clean limit
Parker et al. 2019
;
de Juan et al. 2020
;
Watanabe and Yanase 2021
;
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Gao et al. 2021
. In Sec.
III
, we show the results for the photocurrent response within the transparent region and explain the relation with the previous studies. Sec.
IV
is devoted to discussion and conclusion.

II
Brief review of the standard perturbation theory

In this section, we briefly review the standard perturbation theory in the clean limit described in the literature
Parker et al. 2019
;
de Juan et al. 2020
;
Watanabe and Yanase 2021
;
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Gao et al. 2021
.
Let us consider general noninteracting electronic systems with a periodic potential described by a Hamiltonian of the following form,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}H_{0}({\bf\it k})\hat{\psi}_{{\bf\it k}},

(1)

where
\hat{\psi}_{{\bf\it k}},\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
are the annihilation and creation operators of an electron with wavevector
{\bf\it k}
,
H_{0}({\bf\it k})
is the Bloch Hamiltonian, and
d
is the spatial dimension. The matrix
H_{0}({\bf\it k})
is hermitian and can be diagonalized with a unitary matrix
U_{{\bf\it k}}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{\bf\it k}}

\displaystyle=U_{{\bf\it k}}^{\dagger}H_{0}({\bf\it k})U_{{\bf\it k}},

(2)

where
(\mathcal{E}_{{\bf\it k}})_{ab}=\delta_{ab}\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}a}
is a diagonal matrix, and
\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}a}
is the energy dispersion for the band
a
.

The second order current responses to ac electric fields are described by the third-rank tensor
\sigma^{\mu\alpha\beta}
defined as

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})E^{\alpha}(\omega_{1})E^{\beta}(\omega_{2}).

(3)

with the Fourier component of the electric current and the electric field,
{\bf\it J}(\omega)
and
{\bf\it E}(\omega)
.
In particular, the second order dc current response to ac electric fields is expressed as
\sigma^{\mu\alpha\beta}(0;\omega,-\omega)
.
We can calculate the tensor
\sigma^{\mu\alpha\beta}(0;\omega,-\omega)
with the standard perturbation theory
Parker et al. 2019
;
de Juan et al. 2020
;
Watanabe and Yanase 2021
;
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Gao et al. 2021
.
In most literature, the current response for
\omega
which is equal to or larger than the band gap was studied; for example, the shift current and the injection current with photon energy above the band gap are well understood with the perturbation theory.
However, here we focus on responses to external fields in the transparent region. Namely, we assume the frequency of the external fields
\omega
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\ll\omega<E_{g}

(4)

where
\tau
is the scattering time, and
E_{g}
is the band gap of the system. We set
\hbar=1
here and hereafter. For
\omega
satisfying Eq. (
4
), the external field cannot induce real electronic excitation, and also we can neglect the effects of Drude-like absorption.
In this case, there appears no delta-function term expressing the energy conservation and the absorption of light in the expression of the tensor, but there are still nonzero terms in the clean limit in general, which can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\alpha\beta}(0;\omega,-\omega)\quad\mbox{(clean limit)}\quad

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{2\hbar^{3}\omega^{2}}\int\frac{\differential^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}(-\partial_{\mu}f_{a})

\displaystyle\quad\times\quantity[h^{\alpha\beta}_{aa} + \sum_{b(\neq a)}\qty(\frac{h^{\alpha}_{ab}h^{\beta}_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}-\omega} + \frac{h^{\beta}_{ab}h^{\alpha}_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}+\omega})],

(5)

with
\varepsilon_{ab}=\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}a}-\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}b}
and the Fermi distribution function
f_{a}=(e^{(\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}a}-\mu)/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}+1)^{-1}

Parker et al. 2019
;
de Juan et al. 2020
;
Watanabe and Yanase 2021
;
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Gao et al. 2021
. Here,
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant,
e(<0)
is the charge of an electron,
T
is the temperature and
\mu
is the chemical potential.
\partial_{\alpha}
is the derivative with respect to
k_{\alpha}
, and
h^{\alpha},h^{\alpha\beta}
are defined as

\displaystyle h^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}(\partial_{\alpha}H_{0}({\bf\it k}))U_{{\bf\it k}}=\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{{\bf\it k}}-i\commutator{\bc^{\alpha}}{\Emat_{\vec{k}}},

(6)

\displaystyle h^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}H_{0}({\bf\it k}))U_{{\bf\it k}}=\partial_{\alpha}h^{\beta}-i\commutator{\bc^{\alpha}}{h^{\beta}}.

(7)

where
\xi^{\alpha}
is the interband Berry connection defined by

\displaystyle\xi^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}\partial_{\alpha}U_{{\bf\it k}}.

(8)

h^{\alpha}
corresponds to the velocity operator in the band basis. We note that
\varepsilon_{ab},f_{a},h^{\alpha}
and
h^{\alpha\beta}
are all
{\bf\it k}
-dependent although we suppress the indices
{\bf\it k}
from them for shorthand notation.

Here we emphasize that the result in Eq. (
5
) is valid only in the clean limit, i.e., without any scattering or relaxation. If one properly take into account the effects of relaxation, the photocurrent vanishes with an additional contribution from the relaxation
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
. In the following, we will clarify when the photocurrent in the transparent region is possible.

III
Photocurrent response

In this section, we study the photocurrent response when the chemical potential crosses only one band and that band is well separated from the other bands energetically. Let us consider the case where the system described by Eq. (
1
) is subjected to the ac electric field
{\bf\it E}(t)

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}-eA^{\alpha}(t)h^{\alpha}+\frac{e^{2}}{2}A^{\alpha}(t)A^{\beta}(t)h^{\alpha\beta}+\order{A^3},

is slowly varying compared to frequency

In this case, the effective Hamiltonian

and the effective current operator

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{a}-eA^{\alpha}(t)h^{\alpha}_{aa}+F^{\alpha\beta}b^{\alpha\beta}(t),

(11)

\displaystyle F^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=e^{2}h^{\alpha\beta}_{aa}+e^{2}\sum_{b\neq a}\quantity[\frac{h^{\alpha}_{ab}h^{\beta}_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}-\omega} + \frac{h^{\beta}_{ab}h^{\alpha}_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}+\omega}],

(12)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}H_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)+\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^{\mu}}{t},

(13)

\displaystyle A^{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=a^{\alpha}(t)e^{-i\omega t}+(a^{\alpha}(t))^{*}e^{i\omega t},

(14)

\displaystyle b^{\alpha\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=a^{\alpha}(t)(a^{\beta}(t))^{*},

(15)

where
a
is the band index for the band we are focusing on. In the rest of this paper, we often omit the band index
a

is always used in this paper.

We can confirm that, in the clean limit, the second term in the effective Hamiltonian (Eq. (
11

11
) coincides with the effective Hamiltonian in Ref.
Belinicher et al. 1986

The last term in the effective current operator, Eq. (
13

Resta 1994
;
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993

A

The rest of the effective current operator is given by
k

also induces the modification of the current operator as:

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}\varepsilon_{a}+b^{\alpha\beta}(t)\partial_{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}.

(16)

We emphasize that, our derivation of
J_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mu}(t)
takes into account the interband matrix element in the original current operator. These matrix elements appear in

only through the
k
-derivative of

. For the derivation of the effective Hamiltonian and the detailed expression for

A

Several properties of

should be noted. First, in terms of the time reversal symmetry

satisfies the following relationship if the system preserves

\displaystyle F^{\alpha\beta}({\bf\it k})

\displaystyle=F^{\beta\alpha}(-{\bf\it k}),

(17)

where we explicitly show the
{\bf\it k}
-dependence of
F^{\alpha\beta}
. We also note that
F^{\alpha\beta}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle F^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=e^{2}(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\varepsilon_{a})-ie^{2}\omega\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\mathcal{F}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad+e^{2}\omega^{2}\sum_{b\neq a}\quantity(\frac{\bc^\alpha_{ab}\bc^\beta_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}-\omega} + \frac{\bc^\beta_{ab}\bc^\alpha_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}+\omega}).

(18)

Here
\xi^{\alpha}
is the interband Berry connection and
\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}=i\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\sum_{b(\neq a)}\xi^{\beta}_{ab}\xi^{\gamma}_{ba}
is the Berry curvature of the band

is a label for the other bands separated by the band gap.

is the Levi-Civita symbol. This form also appears in the expression for dynamical Stark shift

IV

Now let us consider the linear response of the electric current to

to calculate the photocurrent response

\displaystyle b^{\alpha\beta}(t)+b^{\beta\alpha}(t)\sim\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\textrm{[slow component of }E^{\alpha}(t)E^{\beta}(t)].

III.1
Slow limit (
\omega_{J}\to 0

The case we are interested in is the dc response limit of

The first term in Eq. (
27
) will vanish in this limit as long as
P^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon-i\eta,\varepsilon+\omega_{J}+i\eta)
does not diverge. For example, if we approximate the self energy as

\displaystyle\Sigma(i\varepsilon)\simeq i\gamma\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon)

(29)

with a constant scattering rate
\gamma
and ignore the vertex correction (see Eq. (
28
)),
P^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon-i\eta,\varepsilon+\omega_{J}+i\eta)
in
\omega_{J}\to 0
limit becomes a Lorenztian as a function of
\varepsilon
, where the peak is located at
\varepsilon=\xi_{{\bf\it k}}
with the width
\gamma
and its peak value is
\propto 1/\gamma^{2}
,
while the factor
f(\varepsilon)-f(\varepsilon+\omega_{J})
effectively behaves as a window function with the width
k_{B}T
and the height
\propto\omega_{J}
. Therefore, the first term in Eq. (
28
) behaves as
\propto(\omega_{J}/\gamma)(\min\{k_{\mathrm{B}}T/\gamma,1\})
.
Consequently, as long as
\omega_{J}\ll\gamma
, the first term vanishes in the
\omega_{J}\to 0
limit.

Then, Eq. (
27
) reduces to the following form:

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega_{J}\to 0}\chi^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\omega_{J}+i\delta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}]\quantity(- f(\ene))\quantity[P^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\ene+i\eta, \ene+i\delta) - P^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\ene-i\delta, \ene-i\eta)]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}]\quantity(- f(\ene))\quantity[\Gamma^{\mu}(\ene+i\eta, \ene+i\delta)\GR(\ene)F^{\alpha\beta}\GR(\ene) - \Gamma^{\mu}(\ene-i\delta, \ene-i\eta)\GA(\ene)F^{\alpha\beta}\GA(\ene)].

(30)

It is useful to use the Ward-Takahashi identity to evaluate this quantity. The Ward-Takahashi identity states the vertex function
\Gamma^{\mu}(\varepsilon\pm i\eta,\varepsilon\pm i\delta)
is related to the Green function as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mu}(\varepsilon+i\eta,\varepsilon+i\delta)=e\partial_{\mu}\quantity(\xi_{\vec{k}}+ \selfeneR(\ene)),

(31)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mu}(\varepsilon-i\delta,\varepsilon-i\eta)=e\partial_{\mu}\quantity(\xi_{\vec{k}} + \selfeneA(\ene)),

(32)

where
\Sigma^{R/A}
is the retarded/advanced self-energy. Therefore, we can prove the following relations between the vertex function and the Green functions:

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{R}}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{\mu}(\varepsilon+i\eta,\varepsilon+i\delta)G^{\mathrm{R}}(\varepsilon)=e\partial_{\mu}(G^{\mathrm{R}}),

(33)

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{A}}(\varepsilon)\Gamma^{\mu}(\varepsilon-i\delta,\varepsilon-i\eta)G^{\mathrm{A}}(\varepsilon)=e\partial_{\mu}(G^{\mathrm{A}}).

(34)

Note that
G^{R/A}
is a number as we focus on a single band.
Inserting these relations into Eq. (
30
) and integrating by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega_{J}\to 0}\chi^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\omega_{J}+i\delta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}]ef(\varepsilon)\partial_{\mu}{F^{\alpha\beta}}\quantity[\GR(\ene)-\GA(\ene)]

(35)

We note that under the approximation
\imaginary G^{\mathrm{R}}(\varepsilon)\simeq-\pi\delta(\varepsilon-\tilde{\xi_{k}})
, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega_{J}\to 0}\chi^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\omega_{J}+i\delta)

\displaystyle\simeq\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}](-2\pi i\delta(\varepsilon-\tilde{\xi}_{{\bf\it k}}))ef(\varepsilon)\partial_{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\int[\differential{k}]e(-f(\tilde{\xi}_{{\bf\it k}}))\partial_{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}.

(36)

with the renormalized energy dispersion
\tilde{\xi}_{{\bf\it k}}
,
which implies its connection to the “diamagnetic contribution”
\propto\partial_{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}
.

We can evaluate the diamagnetic term in Eq. (
21
) as well. The result is

\displaystyle\int[\differential{k}]\expectationvalue{\partial_\mu F^{\alpha\beta}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}](-f(\varepsilon))\partial_{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}\quantity[\GR(\ene)-\GA(\ene)]

(37)

This exactly cancels the contribution from
\chi^{\mu\alpha\beta}
and we find

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega_{J}\to 0}\Phi^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\omega_{J}+i\delta)=0

(38)

This result means that the photocurrent vanishes if we properly take into account the effect of the scattering, which is consistent with Ref.
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
.

III.2
Fast limit and correspondence with the perturbation theory in the clean limit

The results in the previous section does not coincide with the result from the perturbation theory in the clean limit. This is because the perturbation theory employed in, e.g.,
Parker et al. 2019
, assumes a different limit. To see this, we employ the approximation in Eq. (
29
) and take the limit where
\omega_{J}
and
\gamma
approach zero under the condition
\omega_{J}\gg\gamma
, which we call the “fast limit”. In other words, we first take
\gamma\to 0
limit and then
\omega_{J}\to 0
limit.
In this case, the discussion at the beginning in Sec.
III.1
breaks down and the first term in Eq. (

) does not vanish even if
\omega_{J}\to 0
limit. If we neglect the vertex correction, the response function in this limit is

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mu\alpha\beta}(\omega_{J}+i\delta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}](f(\varepsilon)-f(\varepsilon+\omega_{J}))eh^{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}G^{\mathrm{R}}(\varepsilon+\omega_{J})G^{\mathrm{A}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}](f(\varepsilon)-f(\varepsilon+\omega_{J}))eh^{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}\frac{1}{-\omega_{J}-2i\gamma}\quantity(\frac{1}{\ene+\omegaoutput-\xi_{\vec{k}} + i\gamma}-\frac{1}{\ene-\xi_{\vec{k}}-i\gamma})

\displaystyle\to_{\gamma\to 0}\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}]\frac{f(\varepsilon+\omega_{J})-f(\varepsilon)}{\omega_{J}}eh^{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}\quantity(\frac{1}{\ene+\omegaoutput-\xi_{\vec{k}} + i\eta}-\frac{1}{\ene-\xi_{\vec{k}}-i\eta})

\displaystyle\to_{\omega_{J}\to 0}\int\frac{\differential{\ene}}{2\pi i}\int[\differential{k}]f^{\prime}(\varepsilon)eh^{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}\quantity(\frac{1}{\ene-\xi_{\vec{k}} + i\eta}-\frac{1}{\ene-\xi_{\vec{k}}-i\eta})

\displaystyle=\int[\differential{k}](-f^{\prime}(\xi_{{\bf\it k}}))eh^{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}=\int[\differential{k}]e\quantity(-\partial_{\mu}f(\xi_{\vec{k}}))F^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\int[\differential{k}]e^{3}\quantity(-\partial_{\mu}f(\xi_{\vec{k}}))\quantity[h^{\alpha\beta}_{aa} + \sum_{b\in\highband} \qty(\frac{h^{\alpha}_{ab}h^{\beta}_{ba}}{\omega_{ab}-\omega} + \frac{h^{\beta}_{ab}h^{\alpha}_{ba}}{\omega_{ab}+\omega})]

(39)

where we replaced
\gamma
with an infinitesimal positive quantity
\eta

\gamma\to 0

Noting the relation Eq. (

IV
Discussion and conclusion

First we note that
\Phi^{\mu\alpha\beta}
is finite only when the time reversal symmetry
\mathcal{T}
is broken either by magnetic field/order of the system or circularly polarized light. Since the photocurrent response is allowed only when the spatial inversion symmetry
\mathcal{P}
is broken, both breaking
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{P}
is important for the photocurrent discussed in the present paper.

Our discussion clarifies the effects of scattering on the photocurrent. If the scattering rate
\gamma
is sufficiently large compared to the frequency of the output photocurrent
\omega_{J}
, the system supports photocurrent proportional to
\omega_{J}
at the steady state, and the photocurrent vanishes in the limit
\omega_{J}/\gamma\to 0
. This can be understood as a realization of an “equilibrium” state with renomalized energy spectrum
Belinicher et al. 1986
;
Ivchenko et al. 1988
. Indeed, since the system is described by the effective Hamiltonian (
11
), the perturbation is effectively static for the slow limit
\omega_{J}\ll\gamma
, and an “equilibrium” state with the effective Hamiltonian is realized, hence no current occurs. This discussion generalizes a semiclassical result by Belinicher
et al.

Belinicher et al. 1986
to a quantum theoretical treatment.

The effective Hamiltonian derived in this work is closely related to an effect called dynamical Stark effect or optical Stark effect as well
Autler and Townes 1955
;
Bakos 1977
;
Sie et al. 2015
;
Sie et al. 2017
;
Pershoguba and Yakovenko 2022b
;
Pershoguba and Yakovenko 2022a
, where a shift of energy levels is induced by an external ac field. Indeed, the dc component of Eq. (
11
) coincides with the expression for optical Stark shift
Pershoguba and Yakovenko 2022a
. In Ref.
Pershoguba and Yakovenko 2022a
, the dc current induced by the applied ac electric fields with frequency smaller than the band gap is also discussed, and it is concluded that only transient current before the system relaxes into an “equilibrium” state is possible.

We also note that, as a transient current, there would be another contribution, described by
\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^{\mu}}{t}
in Eq. (
13
). This contribution may be interpreted as a polarization current (see Appendix
A
for details), and may be related to the optical rectification effect, where the electric polarization is induced by ac electric fields
Bass et al. 1962
;
Bass et al. 1965
.

On the other hand, the perturbation theory in the clean limit gives finite photocurrent, as we reviewed in Sec.
II
. This limit is recovered in the fast limit (
\gamma\ll\omega_{J}
), as we have seen in Sec.
III.2
. Physically, this limit represents the relaxation rate of the system
\gamma
is sufficiently small compared to the rate of extracting electrons from the system to outside the sample (e.g., to the electrodes attached to the sample). Although usually
\omega_{J}\ll\gamma
holds for dc photocurrent, this limit may be realized in some situations. For example, if the system is thin in the current direction compared to the scattering length, the electron carrying the current quickly flows out of the system before scattering happens, where the effective scattering rate becomes almost zero. In this case, the
\omega_{J}\gg\gamma
limit is effectively realized and nonzero photocurrent within transparent region may be possible. Similar situation is experimentally realized in BaTiO
3
thin film
Spanier et al. 2016
;
Zenkevich et al. 2014
, although
\mathcal{T}
is preserved in BaTiO
3
. In similar setup with
\mathcal{T}
breaking materials or circularly-polarized light, one may observe finite photocurrent within transparent region.

The finite current in the fast limit can be interpreted as a Raman process as well. Namely, the photon with frequency
\omega_{1}
is scattered by the sample, and the scattered photon has a frequency
\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}-\omega_{J}
, leaving an excitation of energy
\hbar\omega_{J}
with a finite current in the sample (Fig.
3
). Here
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\simeq\omega
and
\omega_{J}\ll\omega
. Since the energy of the incident photon is smaller than the band gap, an electron is only virtually excited to the conduction band from the valence band during this process. However, in the presence of finite scattering or relaxation (i.e., the slow limit), the induced current will be canceled.

\omega_{J}\gg\gamma
limit may be related to the recent study by Shi
et al.

Shi et al. 2022
. They discussed systems coupled with particle reservoirs. The coupling to the reservoirs results in the relaxation rate
\Gamma
, and it is shown that finite photocurrent is induced when
\Gamma
is finite. Here, we note that the relaxation rate
\Gamma
in Ref.
Shi et al. 2022
has essentially a different meaning from what we denote as
\gamma
in the present paper.
\Gamma
in Ref.
Shi et al. 2022
represents in a sense the particle exchange rate with the particle reservoir, while
\gamma
in the present work is the scattering rate within the system. Therefore, they can have different effects on the photocurrent behavior, and the results in Ref.
Shi et al. 2022
do not contradict to our results.

Our discussion does not take into account these scattering effects during a virtual excitation since we start from the effective Hamiltonian (
11
). Therefore, those scattering effects may enable finite photocurrent within the transparent region. Indeed, Golub
et al.
recently proposed that finite photocurrent can be induced even within the transparent region if one considers the impurity scattering during a virtual excitation
Golub and Glazov 2022
. Their proposal might look similar to our interpretation of the current in the fast limit as a Raman process, but it is essentially different because they are considering the scattering effect during a virtual excitation of electrons and they also discussed the finite momentum of the light.

Figure 3:
Interpretation of the photocurrent within the transparent region as a Raman-like process. The photocurrent for the fast limit (Eq. (

)) can be understood as a Raman-like process, where the photon with frequency
\omega_{1}
is scattered by the sample but with a different frequency
\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}-\omega_{J}
, leaving an excitation of energy
\hbar\omega_{J}
. Here,
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\simeq\omega
and
\omega_{J}\ll\omega
. The excitation with energy
\hbar\omega_{J}
results in a finite current. However, this current will be canceled if the relaxation rate
\gamma
is sufficiently fast compared with
\omega_{J}
, i.e., in the slow limit.

In conclusion, we have derived the effective Hamiltonian under illumination of light with frequency smaller than the band gap, and clarify the effects of relaxation on photovoltaic effect. We show that if the frequency of the output current
\omega_{J}
is much smaller than the scattering rate
\gamma
, the photocurrent will be proportional to
\omega_{J}/\gamma
and vanishes in the limit of
\omega_{J}/\gamma\to 0
.
In contrast,if the condition
\omega_{J}\gg\gamma
is met in some situations, nonzero photocurrent can appear in the transparent region.

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=H^{\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{HB}}\quantity(T_{ab}H'_{ba'}-H'_{ab}T_{ba'})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{LB}}\quantity(T_{aa''}H'_{a''a'}-H'_{aa''}T_{a''a'})+i\partial_{t}T_{aa^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\quad+\order{A^3}

\displaystyle=H^{\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)+\order{A^3}.

(59)

Therefore,
H^{\prime\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)
coincides with
H^{\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)
.

A.3
Transformed current operator

Since we are interested in the current response, we will need the current operator after the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation. The current operator before the transformation is given by

\displaystyle J^{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}H(t)-i\commutator{\bc^{\mu}}{H(t)},

(60)

where
\xi^{\mu}
is the interband Berry connection.

The new current operator
J^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}(t)J^{\mu}(t)\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}(t)(\partial_{\mu}H(t)-i\commutator{\bc^{\mu}}{H(t)})\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}H^{\prime\prime}(t)-i\commutator{\bc''^\mu(t)+\zeta''^\mu(t)}{H''(t)}

\displaystyle\quad+\partial_{\mu}\zeta^{\prime\prime}_{t}(t)-i\commutator{\bc''^\mu(t)+\zeta''^\mu(t)}{\zeta''_t(t)},

(61)

where
\tilde{U}(t)=V(t)U(t),\xi^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)=\tilde{U}(t)\xi^{\mu}\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)
and

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=i\tilde{U}(t)\partial_{\mu}\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t),

(62)

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime\prime}_{t}(t)

\displaystyle=i\tilde{U}(t)\partial_{t}\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t).

(63)

The third and fourth terms in Eq. (
61
) can be rewritten as a total derivative with respect to time:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\zeta^{\prime\prime}_{t}(t)-i\commutator{\bc''^\mu(t)+\zeta''^\mu(t)}{\zeta''_t(t)}=\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^\mu}{t},

(64)

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{tr}}^{\mu}=\xi^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)+\zeta^{\prime\prime\mu}(t),

(65)

and hence this is a transient current.
P_{\mathrm{tr}}
may be interpreted as a polarization current
Resta 1994
;
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
. Indeed,
\xi^{\prime\prime\mu}
is the Berry connection after the unitary transformation
\tilde{U}(t)
, and
\zeta^{\prime\prime\mu}
is also a Berry connection-like quantity defined with
\tilde{U}(t)
, instead of the unitary transformation diagonalizing the Bloch Hamiltonian without external fields.

In particular,
J^{\prime\prime\mu}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)
for
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{LB}
is given by

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime\mu}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}H^{\prime\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle\quad-i\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{LB}}\left[\quantity(\bc''^\mu_{aa''}(t)+\zeta''^\mu_{aa''}(t))H^{\prime\prime}_{a^{\prime\prime}a^{\prime}}(t)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-H^{\prime\prime}_{aa^{\prime\prime}}(t)\quantity(\bc''^\mu_{a''a'}(t)+\zeta''^\mu_{a''a'}(t))\right]+\partialderivative{P^\mu_{\mathrm{tr},aa'}}{t}+\order{A^3}.

(66)

Here we used
H^{\prime\prime}_{ab}(t)=\order{A^3}
for
a\in\mathcal{LB}
and
b\in\mathcal{HB}
.
Furthermore, since
H^{\prime\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)=\order{A}
if
a\neq a^{\prime}
for
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{LB}
and
\zeta^{\prime\prime\mu}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)=\order{A^2}
for
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{LB}
,
J^{\prime\prime\mu}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)
is simplified to

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime\mu}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}H^{\prime\prime}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle\quad-i\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{LB}}\quantity(\bc''^\mu_{aa''}(t)H''_{a''a'}(t)-H''_{aa''}(t)\bc''^\mu_{a''a'}(t))

\displaystyle\quad-i\zeta^{\prime\prime\mu}_{aa^{\prime}}(t)\varepsilon_{a^{\prime}a}+\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr},aa'}^\mu}{t}+\order{A^3}

(67)

If we denote the projection operator onto
\mathcal{LB}
as
\Pi
, Eq. (
67
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Pi J^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)\Pi

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}\Pi H^{\prime\prime}(t)\Pi-i\commutator{\proj\bc''^\mu\proj}{\proj H''(t)\proj}

\displaystyle\quad-i\commutator{\proj\zeta''^\mu\proj}{\proj\Emat\proj}+\Pi\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^\mu}{t}\Pi+\order{A^3}

(68)

In particular, if
\mathcal{LB}
includes only one band, the second and the third terms in Eq. (
68
) vanish and we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi J^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)\Pi

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}\Pi H^{\prime\prime}(t)\Pi+\Pi\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^\mu}{t}\Pi,

(69)

which corresponds to Eq. (
11
) in the main text. As in the main text, if we neglect the transient current
\partialderivative{P^\mu}{t}
and the term linear in
A^{\alpha}(t)
, the effective current operator is given by

\displaystyle\Pi J^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)\Pi

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}\varepsilon_{a}+b^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\mu}F^{\alpha\beta}_{aa},

(70)

where
a
is the index for the band in
\mathcal{LB}
.

In summary, we have obtained an effective Hamiltonian and the corresponding current operator as

\displaystyle\Pi H^{\prime\prime}(t)\Pi=\Pi\mathcal{E}\Pi-eA^{\alpha}(t)\Pi h^{\alpha}\Pi+b^{\alpha\beta}(t)F^{\alpha\beta}

(71)

\displaystyle F^{\alpha\beta}_{aa^{\prime}}=e^{2}h_{aa^{\prime}}^{\alpha\beta}+\frac{e^{2}}{2}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{HB}}\left[\quantity(\frac{1}{\ene_{ab}-\omega} + \frac{1}{\ene_{a'b}-\omega})h^{\alpha}_{ab}h^{\beta}_{ba^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.+\quantity(\frac{1}{\ene_{ab}+\omega} + \frac{1}{\ene_{a'b}+\omega})h^{\beta}_{ab}h^{\alpha}_{ba^{\prime}}\right],

(72)

\displaystyle\Pi J^{\prime\prime\mu}(t)\Pi=\partial_{\mu}\Pi H^{\prime\prime}(t)\Pi-i\commutator{\proj\bc''^\mu\proj}{\proj H''(t)\proj}

\displaystyle\quad-i\commutator{\proj\zeta''^\mu\proj}{\proj\Emat\proj}+\Pi\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^\mu}{t}\Pi+\order{A^3}

(73)

In the case that
\mathcal{LB}
consists of only one band with index
a
, these results are simplified to the following:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{a}-eA^{\alpha}(t)h^{\alpha}_{aa}+F^{\alpha\beta}b^{\alpha\beta}(t),

(74)

\displaystyle F^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=e^{2}h^{\alpha\beta}_{aa}+e^{2}\sum_{b\neq a}\quantity[\frac{h^{\alpha}_{ab}h^{\beta}_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}-\omega} + \frac{h^{\beta}_{ab}h^{\alpha}_{ba}}{\ene_{ab}+\omega}],

(75)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}H_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)+\partialderivative{P_{\mathrm{tr}}^{\mu}}{t},

(76)

\displaystyle A^{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=a^{\alpha}(t)e^{-i\omega t}+(a^{\alpha}(t))^{*}e^{i\omega t},

(77)

\displaystyle b^{\alpha\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=a^{\alpha}(t)(a^{\beta}(t))^{*},

(78)
