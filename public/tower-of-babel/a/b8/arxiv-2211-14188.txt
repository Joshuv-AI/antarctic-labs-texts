Spectral gap inequalities on nilpotent Lie groups in infinite dimensions
Esther Bou Dagher, Yaozhong Qiu, Boguslaw Zegarlinski, Mengchun Zhang

Abstract
. We develop a general framework for spectral gap inequalities for Gibbs measures on infinite dimensional spin spaces over nilpotent Lie groups in terms of weak U U -bounds and weak single-site spectral gap inequalities. We then provide sufficient conditions on the local specification and give examples of measures constructed using the Kaplan norm and generalising a few results for the Carnot-Carathéodory distance on the Heisenberg group.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we provide a framework for the study of spectral gap inequalities in infinite dimensions that is well-adapted to the case where the ambient space is a nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}
, and in particular when
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{H}
is the Heisenberg group. What we mean by a spectral gap inequality includes the Poincaré inequality

(1.1)

\int_{X}\left\lvert f-\int_{X}f\right\rvert^{2}d\mu\leq C\int_{X}\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{2}d\mu

for
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
a smooth probability measure space,
\nabla
a smooth (sub-)gradient, and with a constant
C>0
independent of
f
belonging to a space with enough regularity conditions to ensure the right hand side makes sense. This inequality appears in the theory of Markov semigroups which play an important role in the study of Markov processes and stochastic differential equations. The infinite-dimensional part of our analysis enters in that our underlying space
X
shall be given as (a suitable subspace of) the infinite product space
\Omega\vcentcolon=\smash{\mathbb{G}^{\mathbb{Z}^{D}}}
for
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
the
D
-dimensional integer lattice.

In the study of Markov semigroups, the spectral gap inequality can be considered in abstract form using the language of generators, carré du champs, and reversible measures; we refer the reader to the works
[
GZ03
,
Bak04
,
Wan06
,
BG10
,
BGL+14
]
for more details. It belongs to the class of so-called coercive functional inequalities and it would be hopeless to give an exhaustive review of all such functional inequalities and their applications. Even within the realm of spectral gap inequalities we may already consider generalisations of (
1.1
). For instance,
[
Wan00
]
studied a version of (
1.1
) in which
C>0
can be made arbitrarily small at the cost of introducing a possibly arbitrarily large
\mathbb{L}^{1}
-norm, and
[
RW01
]
studied the reversed idea of this in which
C
is made large and a
2
-homogeneous functional of
f
is made small. We also remark, since it will be of significant interest in the sequel, that
[
BZ05
]
explored a generalisation of (
1.1
) in which
2
is replaced with a
q\in(1,2]
.

We are interested in spectral gap inequalities for spin systems, which appear naturally in the study of physical phenomena. For instance, one of the earliest models introduced to study ferromagnetism was the Ising model
[
Isi25
]
, where the spin space was the product of the two-point space
\{-1,+1\}
representing the negative and positive states of an elementary magnet respectively. From a mathematical viewpoint, the notion of spin is quite general and indeed in this paper the spin of a particle is regarded as a point in
\mathbb{G}
.
The literature on spectral gap inequalities (and also logarithmic Sobolev inequalities) for spin systems is extensive. At least for unbounded spin systems, a good starting point may be the works of
[
SZ92
,
Zeg96
,
BH99
,
Yos99
,
GR01
,
Led01
]
and references within. Our work can be regarded as being at the intersection of works like
[
Led01
,
BB05
,
OR07
]
which studied the general problem of when a finite-dimensional (that is, on a single spin space) spectral gap or logarithmic Sobolev inequality can be promoted to the entire spin space, and the paper
[
HZ09
]
which studied the problem of finding such finite-dimensional inequalities on the Heisenberg group and more generally on nilpotent Lie groups. The Heisenberg group is one of the simplest subelliptic settings for which the rich theory of Bakry-Émery calculus
[
B“’E85
]
fails, but
[
HZ09
]
developed a new technology, called a
U
-bound, to prove analogues of known results in the Euclidean setting. (For some recent extensions and applications see
[
BDZ21
,
BDZ21a
,
BDZ22
]
,
[
CFZ20
,
CFZ22
]
.)

Our work is partly inspired by
[
IP09
,
IP14
]
which combined the two approaches to obtain an infinite-dimensional inequality for the Heisenberg group with interactions dependent on Carnot-Carathéodory distance. Here we have a similar objective: we wish to start with the
U
-bounds of
[
HZ09
]
to furnish a finite-dimensional inequality on a single spin space and then analyse the conditions under which passage to the infinite-dimensional inequality is possible. In our endeavour we want to develop a general framework for coercive inequalities for Gibbs measures living on a subspace of an infinite product space which allows the inclusion of more examples than those previously existing in the literature, and in particular go beyond the case of an infinite product space over the Heisenberg group.

In our setup, for a given natural sub-gradient operator whose components generate the entire Lie algebra, one can consider single spin interactions dependent on various metrics. The intricacy of that situation is that dependent on the metric one can have essentially different theory as far as coercive inequalities are concerned. For example, as indicated already in
[
HZ09
]
, if one considers the Carnot-Carathéodory distance on the Heisenberg group with a suitable choice of interaction energy then one can prove a logarithmic Sobolev inequality, but if the distance function is smooth outside the origin then the logarithmic Sobolev inequality fails. On the other hand, in the latter case, as demonstrated in
[
Ing10
]
, one can do some interesting analysis and prove that the spectral gap inequality persists also for interactions involving the Kaplan norm on the Heisenberg group.

Organisation of the paper is as follows. In Section 2 we introduce spectral gap inequalities and the infinite-dimensional setup. In Section 3 we present a general framework to prove the spectral gap inequalities for Gibbs measures by introducing a weak form of
U
-bound which is suitable for infinite-dimensional theory. In Section 4 we provide a convenient sufficient condition on the interaction to prove the weak
U
-bound. This condition provides us also with a nice way to generalise Dobrushin’s compactness to our situation. In Section 5 we examine in detail examples of infinite-dimensional theory on the Heisenberg group, and in particular we provide here examples when the interaction depends on the Kaplan norm. In Section 6 we discuss possible generalisations to other Carnot groups. For more details on nilpotent Lie groups we refer the reader to
[
BLU07
]
.

2.
Framework

2.1.
The spectral gap inequality

On a smooth probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\nu)
with a smooth sub-gradient
\nabla
, given
\Gamma^{q}(f)\equiv|\nabla f|^{q}
, for
q\in(1,2]
, one can study a functional inequality of the form

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert f-\int_{\Omega}fd\nu\right\rvert^{q}d\nu\leq C\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{q}(f)d\nu

where
C\in(0,\infty)
does not depend on
f
. Such an inequality is called in the literature the
q
-spectral gap inequality
or
q
-Poincaré inequality (and will be denoted later by
q
-SGI). Its name is derived from the fact that when
q=2
, the Dirichlet form associated to
\Gamma^{2}(f)\equiv\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{2}
together with the measure
\nu
uniquely define a self-adjoint operator
L
on
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\nu)
which, provided (
2.1
) holds, has a spectrum satisfying the containment
\sigma(-L)\subset\{0\}\cup[1/C,\infty)
, i.e.
-L
has a spectral gap between
0
and
1/C
. In particular, if the space is a nilpotent Lie group
\Omega=\mathbb{G}
with Lie algebra
\mathcal{G}
, there exists a (canonical) collection of
1\leq K\leq\dim\mathcal{G}
vector fields forming the canonical subgradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}=(X^{(1)},\cdots,X^{(K)})
. With
\Gamma^{2}(f)=\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\right\rvert^{2}
as the quadratic form and
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}=\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
as the canonical sub-Laplacian, we can consider the probability measure

d\nu=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-U}dx

defined with a measurable function
U
such that
0<Z=\int_{\mathbb{G}}e^{-U}dx<\infty
and
dx
denoting the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{G}
inherited from the stratification of
\mathcal{G}
. Then the operator

L=\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}U\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}

satisfies

\langle f,-Lf\rangle_{\nu}=\int_{\mathbb{G}}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\right\rvert^{2}d\nu,

that is
L
is the self-adjoint operator associated to
\Gamma^{2}
and
\nu
. The Euclidean case is entirely analogous and replaces
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
for
\nabla
, and
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
for
\Delta
.

The
q
-spectral gap inequality for
q\in(1,2)
improves the
2
-spectral gap inequality, in the sense if the underlying space is finite-dimensional, the former implies the latter
[
BZ05
, Proposition 2.3]
. Moreover, the
q
-spectral gap inequality for
q\in(1,2]
enjoys two important properties: it is stable under both tensorisation, meaning if
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
both satisfy the
q
-spectral gap inequality on
\Omega
, then so too does their tensor product
\nu_{1}\otimes\nu_{2}
on
\Omega\times\Omega
, and it is also stable with respect to bounded perturbations, in that if
V
is bounded above and below then
\smash{d\widetilde{\nu}=\frac{1}{\widetilde{Z}}e^{-U-V}dx}
satisfies the inequality also.

The
q
-spectral gap inequality also plays an important role in the study of the (heat) semigroup
(e^{tL})_{t\geq 0}
generated by
L
. Namely, it implies exponentially fast convergence
e^{tL}f\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}fd\nu
to equilibrium in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\nu)
. This inequality belongs to a wider class of functional inequalities called the coercive inequalities which derive their namesake from the fact their existence “coerce” particular behaviours of semigroups. This class also contains the
q
-logarithmic Sobolev inequalities and the classical Sobolev inequality which, respectively, are related to hypercontractivity and ultracontractivity of the semigroup. We refer the reader to the works
[
GZ03
,
Wan06
,
BGL+14
]
for more details on coercive inequalities, and to the paper
[
BZ05
]
which first studied the
q
-spectral gap inequality.

The goal of this paper is to study the spectral gap inequality in the infinite-dimensional setting as well as consider interactions defined in terms of homogeneous norms on
\mathbb{G}
other than the Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d
, which was studied in the infinite-dimensional setting in
[
IP09
]
. We will be especially interested in the Kaplan norm
N
which was proven in
[
Ing10
, Theorem 4.5.5]
to satisfy a finite-dimensional
q
-spectral gap inequality.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
q\in(1,2]
. For
N
the Kaplan norm on the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}
,
p=q/(q-1)
the Hölder conjugate to
q
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the measure

d\nu_{p}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\alpha N^{p}}dx,

satisfies the
q
-spectral gap inequality.

This inequality is also satisfied by
d
replacing
N
, and in fact the measure
\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\alpha d^{p}}dx
satisfies the stronger
q
-logarithmic Sobolev inequality
[
HZ09
, Corollary 4.1]
. However as shown in
[
HZ09
, Theorem 6.3]
a probability defined with any homogeneous norm that is smooth everywhere except at zero, and in particular
N
, can never satisfy the logarithmic Sobolev inequality. Thus the
q
-spectral gap inequality of Theorem
2.1
is the next best result that one could hope for. The main point is that although both
d
and
N
are both natural candidates as choice of metric, and indeed are topologically equivalent, they nonetheless exhibit very different analytic behaviour from the viewpoint of coercive inequalities. We refer the reader to
[
BLU07
, Chapter 5]
for definitions and properties of
d
,
N
, and homogeneous norms on nilpotent Lie groups in general.

In the sequel, for
q\in(1,2]
a fixed constant we shall reserve the notation
p=q/(q-1)
for its Hölder conjugate. Moreover, unless otherwise stated,
\nabla\vcentcolon=\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
, i.e. we always work with the sub-gradient on
\mathbb{G}
and not the Euclidean gradient. (In general,
\nabla
can be any nice enough sub-gradient.) In addition, where convenient we adopt the shorthand notation
\nu(f)=\int_{\Omega}fd\nu
.

2.2.
The infinite-dimensional setting

We will be interested in functional inequalities of the form (
2.1
) where the configuration space
\smash{\Omega=\mathbb{G}^{\mathbb{Z}^{D}}}
is the product of infinitely many copies of a nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}
indexed by the
D
-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
. From the point of view of statistical mechanics, each lattice point
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
represents a particle with unbounded spin taking values in
\mathbb{G}
, while a point
\omega\in\Omega
represents a spin configuration, that is a description of the spin of every particle in the system.

The measures
\nu
on
\Omega
that we consider are constructed in the following way.
First of all we remark that given a measurable function
f
on
(\Omega,\Sigma)
and a subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
we can define a jointly measurable function

f:\mathbb{G}^{\Lambda}\times\mathbb{G}^{\Lambda^{c}}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(2.2)

f_{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x_{\Lambda}):=f(x_{\Lambda}\bullet\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})

where

(x_{\Lambda}\bullet\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})_{j}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}x_{j},&\text{ for }\,j\in\Lambda\\
\omega_{j},&\text{ for }\,j\in\Lambda^{c}.\end{cases}

We define a potential energy
U_{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x_{\Lambda})
for a subsystem in a finite set
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
given external configuration
\omega\in\Omega
as follows

(2.3)

U_{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x_{\Lambda})\vcentcolon=U_{\Lambda}(x_{\Lambda}\bullet\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})=\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\phi(x_{i})+\sum_{i,j\in\Lambda}\beta_{ij}V(x_{i},x_{j})+\sum_{i\in\Lambda,\,j\notin\Lambda}\beta_{ij}V(x_{i},\omega_{j})

where
\phi\in C(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{R})
,
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{G},\mathbb{R})
, and
\lvert\beta_{ij}\rvert<\beta
for some
\beta>0
and each pair
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
. That is, each site
i\in\Lambda
contributes both a phase part
\phi(x_{i})
and an interaction part
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}V(x_{i},x_{j})
, where
x_{j}=\omega_{j}
whenever
j\notin\Lambda
.
If necessary we restrict ourselves to a subspace
\mathcal{S}\subset\Omega
such that

\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\text{ and }\forall\omega\in\mathcal{S},\quad\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\Lambda,\,j\notin\Lambda\end{subarray}}\beta_{ij}V(x_{i},\omega_{j})\right|<\infty,

and

0<Z_{\Lambda}^{\omega}\vcentcolon=\int e^{-U(x_{\Lambda}\bullet\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})}dx_{\Lambda}<\infty

where
dx_{\Lambda}
denotes the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{G}^{\Lambda}
. (If the interaction has finite range
R\in(0,\infty)
, meaning
\beta_{ij}\equiv 0
if
\lVert i-j\rVert\vcentcolon=\lVert i-j\rVert_{l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{D})}>R
,
then both conditions are satisfied on the entirety of
\Omega
.)
Then we can define a probability measure on
\mathcal{S}\subset\Omega
by

d\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}=\delta_{\omega_{\Lambda^{c}}}\otimes\frac{1}{Z_{\Lambda}^{\omega}}e^{-U_{\Lambda}^{\omega}}dx_{\Lambda}

where, for given
\omega\in\Omega
,
\delta_{\omega_{\Lambda^{c}}}
denotes the point measure on
\mathbb{G}^{\Lambda^{c}}
concentrated at
\omega_{\Lambda^{c}}\equiv(\omega_{j},j\in\Lambda^{c})
. The measure
\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}
restricted to the sigma algebra of Lebesgue measurable subsets of
\mathbb{G}^{\Lambda}
is called a
local Gibbs measure
(with external condition
\omega\in\mathcal{S}
).
We denote the integral of a function
f:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
against the measure
d\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}
by

(2.4)

\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}f=\int_{\mathcal{S}}fd\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}=\int_{\mathbb{G}^{\Lambda}}\frac{1}{Z_{\Lambda}^{\omega}}f(x_{\Lambda}\bullet\omega_{\Lambda^{C}})e^{-U_{\Lambda}(x_{\Lambda}\bullet\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})}dx_{\Lambda}

In this way
\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}
can be regarded as a linear functional acting on bounded measurable functions, and it may be promoted to a linear operator
\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}
whose action on
f
is given as
\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}f(\omega)=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}f
. The family
(\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega})_{\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}}^{\omega\in\mathcal{S}}
is called a
local specification
.
It preserves the unit function, positivity and satisfies the compatibility condition

\forall\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}\ \forall\omega\in\mathcal{S}\implies\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}^{\omega}\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{1}}^{\cdot}f=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}^{\omega}f.

A probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{S}
is called a
global Gibbs measure
for the local specification
(\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D};\,\omega\in\mathcal{S}}
if it satisfies the DLR equation

\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\cdot}(f)=\nu(f)

for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
and all integrable functions. This construction, which follows the work of Dobrushin
[
Dob68
]
, Lanford, and Ruelle
[
LR69
]
, means that the measure
\nu
is constructed by first providing a description of the behaviour of
\nu
on finite volume subsystems
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
. The existence of such a measure is not immediately obvious, but Dobrushin gave a criterion
[
Dob70
, Theorem 1]
for spin systems on arbitrary metric spaces which we will use in the sequel to show that the local specifications of interest are well-defined and possess a Gibbs measure
\nu
. Moreover, the proof of the main theorem will go on to show that
\nu
is unique, which is itself a question of independent interest and related to the physical phenomenon of phase transition
[
Geo11
]
.

We can also now be more precise about the functions
f
appearing in (
2.1
): they belong to the space
W^{1,q}(\nu)\subset\mathbb{L}^{q}(\nu)
of functions such that

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Lambda}f\right\rvert^{q}\vcentcolon=\nu\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q}<\infty

for all subsets
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, possibly infinite (and in particular possibly all of
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
itself), and where
\smash{\nabla_{i}f(\omega)\vcentcolon=\nabla_{\{i\}}f_{\{i\}}^{\omega}(\omega_{i})}
and
\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q}
is the
q
-th power of the
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{D})
-norm of
\nabla_{i}f
.

In this paper, we concern ourselves with finding conditions on the phase
\phi
and interaction
V
so that the Gibbs measure
\nu
exists, is unique, and satisfies the
q
-SGI (
2.1
). Afterwards we provide applications of our scheme. For that we choose
\mathbb{G}
to be the Heisenberg group and for the sake of notational simplicity, we consider the case of interactions which are of finite range
R=1
, meaning
\beta_{ij}\equiv 0
whenever
\lVert i-j\rVert>1
.
Already in this setup we obtain a number of generalisations of a few results previously existing in the literature. In particular we provide examples of interactions dependent on a smooth homogeneous norm for which there are no prior results on spectral gap inequalities in the literature for the Gibbs measures of infinite-dimensional systems. The general case of interactions of finite range
1<R<\infty
is discussed briefly at the end of the following chapter, and the case of other nilpotent Lie groups is discussed in Section 6.

3.
Passage from single-site inequalities to global inequalities

In proving inequalities for the global Gibbs measure
\nu
, one may pass from the single-site
q
-SGI to establishing the
q
-SGI holds for every local Gibbs measure
\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega}
uniformly in both the finite volume subsystem
\Lambda
and the boundary conditions
\omega
. In the past this was done, for instance, in
[
Zeg96
, Theorem 4.1]
for the logarithmic Sobolev inequality and in
[
GR01
, Theorem 1.1]
for the
2
-SGI on the single spin space
\mathbb{R}
.
In the event the
q
-SGI cannot be obtained with constant independent of
\Lambda
and
\omega
, it may be possible to work with the weaker uniform single-site inequality where
\Lambda=\{i\}
has unit volume. On the single spin space
\mathbb{H}
, the work of
[
IP09
, Theorem 5.2]
and
[
IP14
, Theorem 2.1]
establishes the inequality for the Gibbs measure in this way for measures defined using the Carnot-Carathéodory distance.

In this chapter, we show that one can get away with less, that is we show that it suffices that a so-called weak
U
-bound and a weak
q
-SGI holds.
U
-bounds were first studied in the context of nilpotent Lie groups in
[
HZ09
]
where they were used to establish conditions under which a
q
-SGI and other coercive inequalities hold for measures on a finite-dimensional space. The one presented here is weak in the sense the right hand side contains defective terms involving the sub-gradients of
f
at other sites. It turns out however that this is sufficient, provided that the defective terms decay sufficiently fast as one moves further and further away from the given site
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
. (A strong
U
-bound analogous to
(A1)
would contain only the sub-gradient of
f
at site
i
, see for instance
[
IP09
, Lemma 4.1]
.) Similarly, the
q
-SGI here is weak in the sense, in contrast to (
2.1
), the right hand side contains more than just
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q}
and instead contains defective terms.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
q\in(1,2]
, and let
\nu
be a Gibbs measure for the local specification
(\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda}^{\omega})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}}^{\omega\in\mathcal{S}}
given in (
2.3
). Let
f\in W^{1,q}(\nu)
and suppose the following two conditions hold for
\beta>0
sufficiently small:

(A1)

for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
with
\lVert j-i\rVert=1
, one has the weak single-site
U
-bound

\nu\left(\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}\left\lvert\nabla_{j}V(x_{i},x_{j})\right\rvert^{q}\right)\leq A\nu\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}+A\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q},

(A2)

and, for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, one has the weak single-site
q
-SGI

\nu\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq A\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

for some constant
A>0
and some symmetric matrix
(M_{ij}\geq 0)_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}
such that

(B1)

A=\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1/2})
as
\beta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, and

(B2)

M_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
and satisfies
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ij}<\infty
.

Then for all
\beta>0
sufficiently small the following global
q
-SGI holds

\nu\left\lvert f-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{SG}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

with some constant
C_{SG}>0
possibly depending on
\beta
,
for all
f\in W^{1,q}(\nu)
.

Remark 3.1
.

The
l^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{D})
-summability of
(M_{i,j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}
of
(B2)
implies
\lVert(M_{ij})\rVert_{l^{\infty}}<\infty
. Without loss of generality we may assume
\lVert(M_{ij})\rVert_{l^{\infty}}\leq 1
by rescaling
M
and absorbing the constant into
A
.

The basic idea is that the summability condition
(B2)
implies that if the interaction matrix
M_{ij}
decays sufficiently fast at infinity (in particular it suffices that it decays exponentially fast), then the defective terms can be dispensed of. The generic case is that
M_{ij}
is a matrix which decays like
\varepsilon^{\lVert i-j\rVert}
where
0<\varepsilon<1
but which may be bounded away from zero in a finite neighbourhood of
i
.

To prove this theorem, we first prove some lemmata. The first is a sweeping out inequality - these were introduced in
[
GZ03
]
in order to prove infinite-dimensional logarithmic Sobolev inequalities. Throughout the remainder of this chapter we fix
q\in(1,2]
and assume the conditions of Theorem
3.1

hold. Moreover, we use the notation
j\sim i
to mean
j
is a neighbour of site
i
, i.e.
j
is adjacent to
i
in the sense
\lVert j-i\rVert=1
, and we use the notation
\{\sim i\}
to denote the set of neighbours of
i
, that is the
2D
points
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
with
\lVert i-j\rVert=1
. (Thus
j\sim i
if and only if
j\in\{\sim i\}
.)
Lastly, whenever we use Landau notation, we implicitly understand it with respect to the limit
\beta\rightarrow 0^{+}
; we will need to track the decay (or growth) of some constants and we will typically reserve uppercase letters such as
K
for large constants that are
\mathcal{O}(1)
and lowercase letters such as
c
for small constants that are
o(1)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, the following single-site sweeping out inequality holds

(3.1)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

with some constants
K_{1}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
c_{1}=o(1)
independent of
i
and
f
.

Proof.

Let us recall that
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f
does not depend on
x_{i}
; in general it depends on every other coordinate
x_{j}
for
j\neq i
, but in the specific case of interactions of unit range, it depends on neighbouring spins
x_{j}
for
j\sim i
. Thus if
j\notin\{\sim i\}
then the probability kernel of the measure
d\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}
does not depend on
j
, and only
f
may depend on
j
. It follows that one simply has
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{i}f)\right\rvert^{q}=\nu\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{i}(\nabla_{j}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}
by interchanging differentiation and integration in the first equality and by Jensen’s inequality together with the DLR equation in the second inequality, so we shall focus on the case
j\sim i
, i.e.
\lVert i-j\rVert=1
. Note also that to emphasise the dependence of the function
\mathbb{E}_{i}f(\omega)=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f
on
\omega
, we will write
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f
with the understanding it replaces
\mathbb{E}_{i}f
even though
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f
is a real number and
\mathbb{E}_{i}f
is a real-valued function. Let
X^{(l)}
for
l=1,\cdots,K
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
be the vector fields generating the Lie algebra
\mathcal{G}
of a nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}
. For
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, let
\smash{\nabla_{j}=(X^{(l)}_{j})_{l=1,..,K}}
denote the sub-gradient on the copy
\mathbb{G}^{\{j\}}
of
\mathbb{G}
at site
j
.

Thus for a bounded differentiable function with bounded derivative

\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f=(X_{j}^{(l)}(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f))_{l=1,\cdots,K}.

If
\rho_{i}\vcentcolon=d\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}/\lambda(dx_{i})
is the density of the measure
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda(dx_{i})
on
\mathbb{G}^{\{i\}}
, then

(3.2)

X_{j}^{(l)}(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)=\int\left(\rho_{i}(X_{j}^{(l)}f)+(X_{j}^{(l)}\rho_{i})f\right)\lambda(dx_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}(X_{j}^{(l)}f)+\int(X_{j}^{(l)}\rho_{i})f\lambda(dx_{i}).

A direct calculation yields

\displaystyle X_{j}^{(l)}\rho_{i}=X_{j}^{(l)}\left(\frac{e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}}{\int e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}}{\int e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i})}-\frac{e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\int(-X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i})}{(\int e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\rho_{i}(X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})+\rho_{i}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}(X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})\vphantom{\frac{(-X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}}{\int e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i})}-\frac{e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\int(-X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i})}{(\int e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}\lambda(dx_{i}))^{2}}}

and therefore

(3.3)

\displaystyle\int(X_{j}^{(l)}\rho_{i})f\lambda(dx_{i})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}((X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega})f)+(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}(X_{j}^{(l)}U_{i}^{\omega}))

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}((f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)(X_{j}^{(k)}U_{i}^{\omega}))\vphantom{\int(X_{j}^{(l)}\rho_{i})f\lambda(dx_{i})}

Hence we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\nabla_{j}f)-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}((f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)(\nabla_{j}U_{i}^{\omega}))

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\nabla_{j}f)-\beta_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}((f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)(\nabla_{j}V(x_{i},\omega_{j})))

Inserting (
3.3
) into (
3.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}(\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\left\lvert\nabla_{j}U_{i}^{\omega}\right\rvert^{q})

\displaystyle\leq 2(\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+\beta^{q}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}(\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\left\lvert\nabla_{j}V(x_{i},\omega_{j})\right\rvert^{q})

by Jensen’s inequality. Integrating with respect to
\nu
, we find

(3.4)

\displaystyle\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+2\beta^{q}\nu(\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\left\lvert\nabla_{j}V(x_{i},x_{j})\right\rvert^{q}\vphantom{\left(\nu\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}C_{\beta}^{\lVert k-i\rVert}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}(f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)\right\rvert^{q}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+2A\beta^{q}\left(\nu\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}(f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)\right\rvert^{q}\right)

where the second inequality is by
(A1)
. For the first term in the bracket, it holds

(3.5)

\nu\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq A\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

by
(A2)
, and for the second term, it holds

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}

\displaystyle M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}(f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\left(\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}+2\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}+2\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

where in the third inequality we have simplified
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}
by considering the two cases
k\sim i
and
k\not\sim i
separately. In the first case,
\lVert(M_{ij})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
by
(B2)
so

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k\sim i}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}+2M_{ji}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}+2\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q},\vphantom{2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\sim i\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}(M_{ki}+M_{kj})\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}+2(M_{ji}+M_{jj})\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}}

and in the second case
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}
and thus it can be absorbed into the first sum
2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}
. Inserting (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) back into (
3.4
), we find

(3.7)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq a_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+a_{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}+a_{3}\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

with

a_{1}\vcentcolon=\frac{2}{1-4A\beta^{q}},\quad a_{2}\vcentcolon=\frac{2A\beta^{q}(A+4)}{1-4A\beta^{q}},\quad a_{3}\vcentcolon=\frac{4A\beta^{q}}{1-4A\beta^{q}}.

By the estimate on
A
of
(B1)
, we may assume
a_{1}<4
and
a_{3}<a_{2}<\beta^{(q-1)/2}<1
, for
\beta
small enough. The difference between (
3.7
) with (
3.1
) is the appearance of the summation
a_{3}\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}
which we claim can be eliminated by the following induction procedure.

For notational convenience, let us denote by

W_{k}\vcentcolon=\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q},\quad Y_{k}\vcentcolon=\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q},\quad S\vcentcolon=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{ki}Y_{k},

in which case (
3.7
) reads, for
\beta>0
small enough and
a_{2}>a_{3}
replacing
a_{3}
for simplicity,

(3.8)

\displaystyle W_{j}

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}Y_{j}+a_{2}S+a_{2}\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}W_{k}.

We can apply (
3.8
) on each
W_{k}
appearing in the sum; one iteration yields

\displaystyle a_{2}\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}W_{k}

\displaystyle\leq a_{2}\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}\left(a_{1}Y_{k}+a_{2}S+a_{2}\sum_{k\neq\ell\sim i}M_{\ell i}W_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}a_{2}S+2da_{2}^{2}S+2da_{2}^{2}\sum_{k\neq\ell\sim i}M_{\ell i}W_{\ell}\vphantom{a_{2}\sum_{k\sim i}\delta\left(a_{1}Y_{k}+a_{2}S+a_{2}\sum_{j\neq\ell\sim i}\delta W_{\ell}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}a_{2}S+a_{2}(2da_{2})S+a_{2}(2da_{2})\sum_{k\neq\ell\sim i}M_{\ell i}W_{\ell}\vphantom{a_{2}\sum_{k\sim i}\delta\left(a_{1}Y_{k}+a_{2}S+a_{2}\sum_{j\neq\ell\sim i}\delta W_{\ell}\right)}

since
\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{\ell i}Y_{k}\leq S
and
\lVert(M_{ij})\rVert_{l^{\infty}}\leq 1
by
(B2)
, and each site has exactly
2D
neighbours. With
\varepsilon=2Da_{2}=o(1)
, we obtain after a second iteration

\displaystyle W_{j}

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}Y_{j}+a_{2}S+a_{1}a_{2}S+\varepsilon a_{2}S+\varepsilon a_{2}\sum_{j\neq\ell\sim i}M_{\ell i}W_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}Y_{j}+a_{2}S+a_{1}a_{2}S+\varepsilon a_{2}S+\varepsilon a_{1}a_{2}S+\varepsilon^{2}a_{2}S+\varepsilon^{2}a_{2}\sum_{j\neq m\sim i}M_{mi}W_{m}

and so on and so forth; iterating this procedure gives in the limit

W_{j}\leq a_{1}Y_{j}+\frac{a_{2}+a_{1}a_{2}}{1-\varepsilon}S

by taking
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(a_{2})=o(1)
small enough. In particular if
\beta
is small enough then since
a_{1}<4=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
a_{2}<\beta^{(q-1)/2}
one obtains the desired inequality with
K_{1}=4
and
c_{1}=\beta^{(q-1)/4}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The estimates given here are somewhat wasteful but can easily be improved. For instance
a_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\beta^{q-1})
so
c_{1}
can be taken as
\mathcal{O}(\beta^{q-1})
as well, but the choice of estimate is intentional and eliminates the need to write down the controlling constant.

Remark 3.3
.

For the limit of the inductive procedure to be well-defined, we need to show
\sum_{j\neq k\sim i}M_{ki}W_{k}\leq\sum_{k\sim i}M_{ki}W_{k}=\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\{\sim i\}}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}
is bounded. Adding
a_{2}M_{ji}W_{j}
to the right hand side of (
3.8
) for simplicity and summing over
j\sim i
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{j\sim i}W_{j}

\displaystyle\leq a_{1}\sum_{j\sim i}Y_{j}+2da_{2}S+2da_{2}\sum_{k\sim i}M_{ki}W_{k}

\displaystyle\leq(a_{1}+2da_{2})\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+2da_{2}\sum_{k\sim i}W_{k}.

Taking the sum
2Da_{2}\sum_{k\sim i}W_{k}
to the left hand side we obtain
(1-2da_{2})\sum_{j\sim i}W_{j}\leq(a_{1}+2da_{2})\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}
; since
a_{2}=o(1)
, if
\beta
is small enough then

\sum_{j\sim i}W_{j}\leq 4a_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}<16\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}<\infty

by assumption of
f\in W^{1,q}(\nu)
.

Using the single-site sweeping out inequality (
3.1
), we will prove a global sweeping out inequality on a carefully chosen infinite volume subsystem. The first step is to form a finite partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
whose components are infinite volume subsystems
(\Gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}
with the property that no interaction exists within each
\Gamma_{n}
which, in our case, translates to each pair of points
i,j\in\Gamma_{n}
satisfying
\lVert i-j\rVert>1
whenever
i\neq j
. The partition chosen here will be consist of
N=2^{D}
translations of the cube
\Gamma_{0}=(2\mathbb{Z})^{D}
translating
0
to another point in
[0,1]^{D}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, although we remark that this is not the only partition. Indeed, one can also use the partition
\Gamma_{0}=\{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\mid\lVert i\rVert\in 2\mathbb{Z}\}
with
\Gamma_{1}=\mathbb{Z}^{D}\setminus\Gamma_{0}
as in
[
IP09
, §5]
; the choice of partition enters the proof through the controlling constants so the partitions are equivalent as far as obtaining the
q
-SGI (with no regard for the constant) is concerned.

We will give a sketch of the proof of how to generalise the results to interactions of finite range
R>1
later, and it shall be convenient to adopt the former partition here instead. (The latter partition generalises to finite range
R>1
, but the proof for the former is somewhat easier.) In any case we fix in the sequel our choice of partition
(\Gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}
as above, and we begin by proving global sweeping out inequalities for
\Gamma_{n}
; the self-similarity of the
\Gamma_{n}
will mean the proof follows for all if it follows for one.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
and
n\in\{0,1,\cdots,N-1\}
, the sub-lattice sweeping out inequality

(3.9)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{2}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Lambda}f\right\rvert^{q}+c_{2}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Lambda^{c}}f\right\rvert^{q}

holds for some constants
K_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
c_{2}=o(1)
independently of
n
and
\Lambda
.

Remark 3.4
.

We emphasise the reason that the sub-gradient is taken over a general subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
of the lattice here instead of at a single site
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
as in Lemma
3.1
is because in the latter case the case
j\not\sim i
is trivial and thus the sweeping out inequality is only meaningful for
\Lambda=\{\sim i\}
.

Proof.

As mentioned earlier, it suffices to prove the case
n=0
. First, rewrite the gradient into its single-site form

(3.10)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f)\right\rvert^{q}=\sum_{j\in\Lambda}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f)\right\rvert^{q}.

Let
i\in\Gamma_{0}
and
j\in\Lambda
. Since
\Gamma_{0}
has no self-interaction, the measure
\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}
can be realised as the product of the single-site measures
\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}=\mathbb{E}_{i_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{i_{2}}\cdots
where
(i_{m})_{m\geq 1}
enumerates
\Gamma_{0}
. This reduces the problem of studying
\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}
to the problem of studying
\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i}
for
i\in\Gamma_{0}
.

Note if
\left\lvert i_{m}-j\right\rvert>1
, that is if site
j
does not interact with site
i_{m}
, then
\nabla_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i_{m}}^{\omega}=\mathbb{E}_{i_{m}}^{\omega}\nabla_{j}f
together with Jensen’s inequality allows us to pull
\mathbb{E}_{i_{m}}^{\omega}
out, which is then eliminated by
\nu
with the DLR equation, so the only issue are sites
i_{m}\in\Gamma_{0}
such that
i_{m}\sim j
. Thinking of
\Gamma_{0}
as partitioning
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
into
D
-dimensional cubes of edge length
2
, each containing exactly
N=2^{D}
points in
\Gamma_{0}
, it follows there is at least one cube containing
j
. (It might be the case
j
falls on the boundary of two cubes; we may without loss of generality choose the cube whose centre is closest to the origin.) Denote by
Q_{j}
this cube. By construction,

\Gamma_{0}\cap\{\sim j\}\subset\Gamma_{0}\cap Q_{j}\vcentcolon=\{i_{1}^{j},i_{2}^{j},\cdots,i_{N}^{j}\}\subset\Gamma_{0}

where
(i_{m}^{j})_{m=1,2,\cdots,N}
enumerates
\Gamma_{0}\cap Q_{j}
. Thus we can reduce the sites in (
3.10
) to

(3.11)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq\sum_{j\in\Lambda}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}f)\right\rvert^{q}=\sum_{j\in\Lambda}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{i_{N}^{j}}\cdots\mathbb{E}_{i_{2}^{j}}\mathbb{E}_{i_{1}^{j}}f)\right\rvert^{q}

Let us fix
j
and drop the superscript, that is write
i_{m}
for
i_{m}^{j}
. Denote by
f_{0}\vcentcolon=f
and
f_{m}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{i_{m}}\cdots\mathbb{E}_{i_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{i_{1}}f
for
m=1,2,\cdots,N
, so that
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{N}\right\rvert^{q}
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have

(3.12)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{k,i_{m}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}.

Note that if
k
is sufficiently far from
Q_{j}
then site
k
does not interact with any of the
N
sites in
Q_{j}
. We can make this observation more precise: if
R_{j}
is the smallest cube strictly containing
Q_{j}
, then for
k\notin R_{j}
one has
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}
by Jensen’s inequality, and

M_{k,i_{m}}\leq M_{k,Q_{j}}\vcentcolon=\sup_{i\in Q_{j}}M_{k,i}

since
i_{m}\in Q_{j}
. Moreover,
M_{k,i_{m}}\leq 1
by
(B2)
, so

(3.13)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{k\in R_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{k\notin R_{j}}M_{k,Q_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}.

In fact, the only dependence on
j
in this equation comes from the fact a choice of
j
gives a choice of
Q_{j}
and therefore a choice of sites
i_{m}
for
m=1,2,\cdots,N
with respect to which we take a conditional expectation. This means we may replace
\nabla_{j}
with
\nabla_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
without replacing
Q_{j}
, and thus
(i_{m})=(i_{m}^{j})
, with
Q_{k}
and
(i_{m}^{k})
respectively. We can then apply Lemma
3.1
as before, and, again, analyse the summation through the decomposition
\mathbb{Z}^{D}=R_{j}\sqcup(R_{j})^{c}
and not
\mathbb{Z}^{D}=R_{k}\sqcup(R_{k})^{c}
. Thus we arrive at

(3.14)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{\ell\in R_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\ell}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{\ell\notin R_{j}}M_{\ell,Q_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\ell}f\right\rvert^{q}.

In fact we only need this for
k\in R_{j}
but the statement holds nonetheless unconditionally.

We now follow an inductive procedure in the spirit of the proof of Lemma
3.1
. Denote by

W_{m}^{j}\vcentcolon=\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q},\quad Y_{m}^{j}\vcentcolon=\sum_{k\in R_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}=\sum_{k\in R_{j}}W_{m}^{k},\quad S^{(j)}\vcentcolon=\sum_{k\notin R_{j}}M_{k,Q_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

so that (
3.14
) now reads

(3.15)

W_{m}^{k}\leq K_{1}W_{m-1}^{k}+c_{1}Y_{m-1}^{j}+c_{1}S^{(j)}.

Noting
\left\lvert R_{j}\right\rvert=5^{D}
, we can sum the left hand side over
k\in R_{j}
to conclude

(3.16)

\displaystyle Y_{m}^{j}\leq\sum_{k\in R_{j}}(K_{1}W_{m-1}^{k}+c_{1}Y_{m-1}^{j}+c_{1}S^{(j)})

\displaystyle\leq(K_{1}+5^{D}c_{1})Y_{m-1}^{j}+5^{D}c_{1}S^{(j)}

\displaystyle\leq 5Y_{m-1}^{j}+S^{(j)}\vphantom{\sum_{k\in R_{j}}(K_{1}W_{m-1}^{k}+c_{1}Y_{m-1}^{j}+c_{1}S^{(j)})}

for
\beta
small enough, since
K_{1}=4
and
c_{1}=o(1)
. Note if we iterate (
3.15
) into itself, we find

\displaystyle W_{m-1}^{j}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}W_{m-2}^{j}+c_{1}Y_{m-2}^{j}+c_{1}S^{(j)}\vphantom{K_{1}^{m-1}W_{0}^{j}+c_{1}\sum_{i=2}^{m}K_{1}^{i-2}Y_{m-i}^{j}+c_{1}\sum_{i=2}^{m}K_{1}^{i-2}S^{(j)}}

The next problem is to use the sweeping out inequality on the sub-lattice to transfer the weak single-site

Lemma 3.3

For any

\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}^{\omega}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{0}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

Proof.

We again consider only the case

, the other cases being similar. Let
(i_{m})_{m\geq 1}
enumerate
\Gamma_{0}
, and denote by as before
f_{0}\vcentcolon=f
and

, so that
\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f=f_{\infty}

\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f\right\rvert^{q}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\nu\left(\mathbb{E}_{i_{m+1}}^{\omega}\left\lvert f_{m}-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f\right\rvert^{q}-\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{i_{m+1}}^{\omega}(f_{m}-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f)\right\rvert^{q}\right).

(In particular the negative part of the summand at

cancels with the positive part of the summand at

only the
\nu\mathbb{E}_{i_{1}}^{\omega}\left\lvert f_{0}-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f\right\rvert^{q}=\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f\right\rvert^{q}

\left\lvert a\right\rvert^{q}-\left\lvert b\right\rvert^{q}\leq\text{sgn}(b)q\left\lvert b\right\rvert^{q-1}(a-b)+2\left\lvert a-b\right\rvert^{q}

and

. We can take

and

, and integrate with respect to

and obtain

\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}-\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}.

(The first term vanishes since

is a constant, and so
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}(f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f)=0
.) Using this bound, we obtain

\nu\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq 2\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\nu\mathbb{E}_{i_{m+1}}^{\omega}\left\lvert f_{m}-\mathbb{E}_{i_{m+1}}^{\omega}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}.

Applying

to each summand, we find

\displaystyle\nu\mathbb{E}_{i_{m+1}}^{\omega}\left\lvert f_{m}-\mathbb{E}_{i_{m+1}}^{\omega}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq A\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq A\sum_{j\in\Gamma_{0}}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+A\sum_{j\in(\Gamma_{0})^{c}}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q},

since there is no self-interaction in
\Gamma_{0}
and

depends only on coordinates in

so that
\nabla_{j}f_{m}=(\nabla_{j}f)_{m}

. (Jensen’s inequality then provides the final simplification.) For the summation over
(\Gamma_{0})^{c}
, we apply the recursion (
3.17
) on each
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert
which gives

(3.24)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq L_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}\sum_{k\in R_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}+c_{1}L_{2}\sum_{k\notin R_{j}}M_{k,Q_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

(Note

here but recall there is a cube

on which
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}(\mathbb{E}_{Q_{j}}f)\right\rvert^{q}
.) We can insert (
3.24
) into (

) and again into (

) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2A}\nu\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}f}\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{j}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle+c_{1}L_{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in(\Gamma_{0})^{c}}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\sum_{k\in R_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle+c_{1}L_{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in(\Gamma_{0})^{c}}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\sum_{k\notin R_{j}}M_{k,Q_{j}}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}

since
L_{1}>1
.

Let us denote by
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}
respectively the three terms. We note as in the previous proof that
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}M_{j,i_{m+1}}\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}M_{i,j}\leq L_{0}
uniformly in

by

since

is symmetric so column sums coincide with row sums, so

is estimated by

.
I_{2}
and

) and (
3.19
) but with the extra appearance of

so

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}L_{0}L_{2}L_{3}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q},

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}L_{0}L_{2}L_{4}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}.

Collecting everything, we arrive at the weak

-SGI

\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{0}\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

with

.
∎

Having obtained a weak
q
-SGI for the sub-lattices, we give a final argument to show this implies the (strong)
q
-SGI on the entire lattice.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{N-1}}\cdots\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}
. Then
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the following four conditions:

(1)

\mathcal{P}
satisfies the DLR equation

\nu f=\nu(\mathcal{P}f).

(2)

There exists a constant
C_{1}
such that

\nu\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}-\nu\left\lvert\mathcal{P}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q},

(3)

There exists
\xi\in(0,1)
such that for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
one has

\nu\left\lvert\nabla(\mathcal{P}^{m}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq\xi^{m}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}.

(4)

The following limit

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}^{m}f=\nu f

holds
\nu
-almost surely.

Proof.

(1)
follows immediately from the DLR equation. To prove
(2)
, denote by
f_{-1}=f
and
f_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}\cdots\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f
, for
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
. (This is a departure from the usual notation since the index of the sub-lattices start at zero to distinguish the index from their points, which start at one.) By applying the sub-quadratic inequality appearing in the previous proof and the weak sub-lattice
q
-SGI (
3.20
) we obtain

\displaystyle\nu\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}-\nu\left\lvert\mathcal{P}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}\left(\left\lvert f_{n-1}\right\rvert^{q}-\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f_{n-1}\right\rvert^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}\left\lvert f_{n-1}-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f_{n-1}\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f_{n-1}\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle=2C_{0}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+2C_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{(\Gamma_{n})^{c}}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f_{n-1})\right\rvert^{q}

since
f_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f_{n-1}
depends only on coordinates in
(\Gamma_{n})^{c}
. The first term requires no further estimation, whilst we can apply Lemma
3.2
inductively on the second term to obtain

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{(\Gamma_{n})^{c}}(\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f_{n-1})\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{2}^{n+1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

which gives

\nu\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}-\nu\left\lvert\mathcal{P}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

which is exactly
(2)
with
C_{1}=2C_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}K_{2}^{n}\leq 2K_{2}^{N}C_{0}
.

To prove
(3)
, we define
\Gamma^{(m)}=\cup_{n=0}^{m}\Gamma_{n}
and apply Lemma
3.2
again to obtain

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma^{(m)}}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}=\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma^{(m-1)}}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{2}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma^{(m-1)}}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{2}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}.

Applying Lemma
3.2
as in the proof for
(2)
, we obtain

(3.25)

\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma^{(m)}}f_{m}\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{2}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma^{(m-1)}}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{2}K_{2}^{m}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}.

This equation can be inductively applied on the first term, that is applying (
3.25
) on
\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma^{(m-1)}}f_{m-1}\right\rvert^{q}
and starting from
m=N-1
we find

\nu\left\lvert\nabla(\mathcal{P}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq K_{2}^{N}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma_{0}}f_{0}\right\rvert^{q}+c_{2}K_{2}^{N}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}=c_{2}K_{2}^{N}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

since
\nabla_{\Gamma_{0}}f_{0}=\nabla_{\Gamma_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}f=0
. Since
K_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
c_{2}=o(1)
, we obtain
(3)
at
m=1
with
\xi=c_{2}K_{2}^{N}
with
\beta
small enough; the general case for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
follows by replacing
f
by
\mathcal{P}f
and proceeding by straightforward induction.

To prove the final statement
(4)
, following the arguments of
(2)
we first bound the
q
-variance with respect to
\mathcal{P}
by

\nu\left\lvert f-\mathcal{P}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq N^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\nu\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}\left\lvert f_{n-1}-\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{n}}f_{n-1}\right\rvert^{q}\leq N^{N}C_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

using the Hölder inequality
(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert)^{q}\leq N^{q-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{q}
for nonnegative
(x_{i})
on the telescoping sum
f-\mathcal{P}f=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f_{n-1}-f_{n}
. For
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, replacing
f
by
\mathcal{P}^{m}f
in the above estimate we obtain

\nu\left\lvert\mathcal{P}^{m}f-\mathcal{P}^{m+1}f\right\rvert^{q}\leq N^{N}C_{1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla(\mathcal{P}^{m}f)\right\rvert^{q}\leq N^{N}C_{1}\xi^{m}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}.

Since
\xi<1
, the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies
\mathcal{P}^{m}
converges
\nu
-almost surely to some function
g
, and similarly
\left\lvert\nabla(\mathcal{P}^{m}f)\right\rvert
converges
\nu
-almost surely to zero by
(3)
, so that

is constant
\nu
-almost everywhere. Note the decay of

with respect to

does not matter since
\beta
is sufficiently small, but fixed. Assuming that

is bounded, by dominated convergence we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}^{m}f=g=\nu(g)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\nu(\mathcal{P}^{m}f)=\nu(f)

with the last equality obtained by
(1)
. The general case of possibly unbounded

is obtained by density.
∎

We can now prove the main theorem of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Denote by
f^{m}=\mathcal{P}^{m}f
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. By applying
(1)
,
(2)
, and
(3)
of Lemma
3.4
in succession,

\displaystyle\nu\left\lvert f-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\nu\left\lvert f_{n}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}-\nu\left\lvert\mathcal{P}(f_{k}-\nu f)\right\rvert^{q}\right)+\nu\left\lvert f_{N}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\vphantom{C_{1}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\xi^{n}\right)\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert f_{n}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f_{n}\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert f_{N}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\vphantom{C_{1}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\xi^{n}\right)\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert f_{n}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\xi^{n}\right)\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert f_{n}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{JC_{1}}{1-\xi}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert f_{N}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}.\vphantom{C_{1}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\xi^{n}\right)\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}+\nu\left\lvert f_{n}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}}

Taking

, the remainder term satisfies
\nu\left\lvert f_{N}-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\rightarrow 0
by
(4)
which gives the global

-SGI

\nu\left\lvert f-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{SG}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

with
C_{SG}=C_{1}/(1-\xi)
.
∎

3.1.
Generalisations to interactions of finite range
R

The analysis can be generalised to the case where the interaction has finite range
R>1
, meaning
\beta_{ij}\equiv 0
for
\lVert i-j\rVert>R
. No modification to the main theorem in this case is necessary: one modifies the definition
j\sim i
to mean
1\leq\lVert j-i\rVert\leq R
, i.e.
j
is a neighbour when
j
is allowed to interact with
i
, and much of the analysis is left unchanged modulo changes to
\mathcal{O}(1)
estimates. In Lemma
3.1
, the only dependence on the range of the interaction (beyond the fact the definition
j\sim i
depends on the range) appears in the definition of
\varepsilon
. When
R=1
, the number of neighbours of
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
is exactly
2D
; when
R>1
the number of neighbours grows like
R^{D}
but for fixed
R
is finite, so that we can replace
\varepsilon=2da_{2}
with
\varepsilon=N_{R}a_{2}
where
N_{R}=\left\lvert\{\sim i\}\right\rvert
is the number of neighbours of
i
.

A generalisation of the sub-lattices
(\Gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}
is also straightforward: it is easy to see the base cube
\Gamma_{0}=(R+1)\mathbb{Z}^{D}
has no self-interaction and that there are
N_{1}=(R+1)^{D}
translations of
\Gamma_{0}
shifting the basepoint
0
to another point in
[0,R]^{D}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
partition
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
. Note one needs to make a distinction between the number
N_{1}
of sub-latties in our partition and the number
N_{2}=2^{D}
of vertices in a
D
-dimensional cube, which coincide for
R=1
. In any case, only
N_{2}
has a role to play in the proof of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, where the sub-lattice is fixed (and so the number of sub-lattices is not relevant) so we only need to change the estimate on
\left\lvert R_{j}\right\rvert=(2R+3)^{D}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, which makes no difference to the validity of the proof because its influence is only felt in the

Lemma 4.1

(4.2)

Proof.

For a compactly supported differentiable function

Proof.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all

(4.3)

\nu(\eta_{i}\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q})\leq K_{2}\left(\nu\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}c_{2}^{\lVert k-i\rVert}\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{k}f\right\rvert^{q}\right)

and

Proof.

For notational convenience, denote by

W_{i}\vcentcolon=\nu(\eta_{i}\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}),\quad Y_{i}\vcentcolon=\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q},\quad Z=\nu\left\lvert f\right\rvert^{q}

so that (
4.1
) reads

(4.4)

W_{i}\leq K_{1}Y_{i}+K_{1}Z+c_{1}\sum_{k\sim i}W_{k}

whilst (
4.3

W_{i}\leq K_{2}Z+K_{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}c^{\lVert k-i\rVert}Y_{k}
. The difference between the induction argument here and that which was given in §3 is that in the previous case case the LHS depended on

also

appear whereas when we apply (

in the sum we expect terms depending on neighbours of neighbours of

should appear.

Indeed, let us look at

(4.5)

\sum_{k\sim i}W_{k}\leq 2DK_{1}Z+K_{1}\sum_{k\sim i}Y_{k}+c\sum_{k\sim i}\sum_{\ell\sim k}W_{\ell},

and in general if the nested summation is denoted by

\mathcal{S}_{M}(W_{i})\vcentcolon=\sum_{k_{1}\sim i}\sum_{k_{2}\sim k_{1}}\cdots\sum_{k_{M}\sim k_{M-1}}W_{k_{M}},

then

where
\mathcal{S}_{M}(Z)
and

are defined similarly. The first term

is easily handled, since

has no dependence on any indices so

The second term

requires some counting: the summation contains, for instance, terms

for which

terms, so a crude estimate is that the contribution of those

is controlled by

for which

terms, and so on so forth; continuing in this way we find

\mathcal{S}_{M}(Y_{i})\leq\sum_{n=0}^{M}\sum_{\lVert k-i\rVert=n}(2D)^{M-n}Y_{k}\leq(2D)^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{M}\sum_{\lVert k-i\rVert=n}Y_{k}.

The final term

is handled by our initial assumption

, implying each

for some constant

so that
\mathcal{S}_{M+1}(W_{i})\leq(2D)^{M+1}L
.

With
\mathcal{S}_{0}(X)=X
where

is one of

\displaystyle W_{i}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\mathcal{S}_{0}(Y_{i})+K_{1}\mathcal{S}_{0}(Z)+c_{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(W_{i})\vphantom{K_{1}\sum_{r=0}^{M}c_{1}^{r}\mathcal{S}_{r}(Y_{i})+K_{1}\sum_{r=0}^{M}c_{1}^{r}\mathcal{S}_{r}(Z)+c_{1}^{M+1}\mathcal{S}_{M+1}(W_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\mathcal{S}_{0}(Y_{i})+K_{1}\mathcal{S}_{0}(Z)+c_{1}K_{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(Z)+c_{1}K_{1}\mathcal{S}_{1}(Y_{i})+c_{1}^{2}\mathcal{S}_{2}(W_{i})\vphantom{K_{1}\sum_{r=0}^{M}c_{1}^{r}\mathcal{S}_{r}(Y_{i})+K_{1}\sum_{r=0}^{M}c_{1}^{r}\mathcal{S}_{r}(Z)+c_{1}^{M+1}\mathcal{S}_{M+1}(W_{i})}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\makebox[0.0pt]{\vdots}}\vphantom{K_{1}\sum_{r=0}^{M}c_{1}^{r}\mathcal{S}_{r}(Y_{i})+K_{1}\sum_{r=0}^{M}c_{1}^{r}\mathcal{S}_{r}(Z)+c_{1}^{M+1}\mathcal{S}_{M+1}(W_{i})}

\displaystyle\nu\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert f-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}f\right\rvert^{q}

\displaystyle=2^{q}\nu\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert f-m_{y}\right\rvert^{q}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\eta_{i}+\overline{\eta}_{i}\leq L_{1}\}}+2^{q}\nu\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert f-m_{y}\right\rvert^{q}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\eta_{i}+\overline{\eta}_{i}>L_{1}\}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B(R)}\left\lvert f(x_{i})-\frac{1}{\left\lvert B(R)\right\rvert}\int_{B(R)}f(y_{i})dy_{i}\right\rvert^{q}\frac{e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}}{Z_{i}^{\omega}}dx_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{S(L_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{M}}{Z_{i}^{\omega}}\int_{B(R)}\left\lvert\frac{1}{B(R)}\int_{B(R)}f(x_{i})-f(y_{i})dy_{i}\right\rvert^{q}dx_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{S(L_{1})}\vphantom{\int_{B(R)}\left\lvert f(x_{i})-\frac{1}{\left\lvert B(R)\right\rvert}\int_{B(R)}f(y_{i})dy_{i}\right\rvert^{q}\frac{e^{-U_{i}^{\omega}}}{Z_{i}^{\omega}}dx_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{S(L_{1})}}

\displaystyle\left\lvert f(x_{i})-f(x_{i}\circ y_{i})\right\rvert^{q}

by Hölder’s inequality.
Since

\displaystyle\leq 4R^{2}e^{2M}\frac{\left\lvert B(2R)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert B(R)\right\rvert}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q}\mathbbm{1}_{S(L_{1})},\vphantom{\frac{2L_{1}e^{M}}{Z_{i}^{\omega}\left\lvert B(R)\right\rvert}\int\int\int_{0}^{d(y_{i})}\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f(x_{i}\circ\gamma(t))\right\rvert^{q}\mathbbm{1}_{B(2R)}(y_{i})\mathbbm{1}_{B(3R)}(x_{i}\circ\gamma(t))dtdy_{i}dx_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{S(L_{1})}}

where in the first inequality we use

Let us recall

\nu I_{1}\leq C\nu(\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q}\mathbbm{1}_{S(L)})\leq C\nu\left\lvert\nabla_{i}f\right\rvert^{q}.

As mentioned earlier, the estimate for

\displaystyle\nu I_{2}

5.
Examples

In this section, we construct examples of potentials and interactions, which may depend on Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d
or a smooth homogeneous norm, satisfying
(C1)
and
(C2)
. Consequently, this will provide new examples where a
q
-SGI holds. To this end, the usual strategy is to find a phase
\phi
and a function
\eta
satisfying the
U
-bound of
(C1)
, and then equipping this with an interaction
V
satisfying
(C2)
. Before we proceed, we first give a brief introduction into the Heisenberg group, which is the prototypical example of a nontrivial nilpotent Lie group and perhaps one of the simplest toy models in the theory of subelliptic (or subriemannian) geometry.

5.1.
The Heisenberg group

In this paper, the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}
is identified as the Lie group
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\circ)
equipped with group law

(5.1)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\\
\end{array}\right)\circ\left(\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\\
y_{3}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}+y_{1}\\
x_{2}+y_{2}\\
x_{3}+y_{3}+2(x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})\\
\end{array}\right).

A property that distinguishes
\mathbb{H}
from the trivial group
(\mathbb{R}^{3},+)
is the fact its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
of left-invariant vector fields is generated by the two vector fields

(5.2)

\displaystyle X^{1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}+2x_{2}\partial_{3},

\displaystyle X^{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}-2x_{1}\partial_{3},

in the sense
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
, together with their commutator
X^{3}\vcentcolon=[X^{1},X^{2}]=-4\partial_{3}
, span all of
\mathfrak{h}
. The importance of the two vector fields
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
is that they allow us to define a differential calculus of sorts in which the only differential operators that exist are
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
, and in particular they allow us to define analogues of objects in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that take into account the group structure of
\mathbb{H}
.

For instance, we may define a metric on
\mathbb{H}
by considering curves whose derivative belongs to the sub-bundle spanned by
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
. A Lipschitz curve
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
is said to be
subunit
with respect to
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
if there exist measurable coefficients
c(t)=(c_{1}(t),c_{2}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
such that
\dot{\gamma}(t)=c_{1}(t)X^{1}(\gamma(t))+c_{2}(t)X^{2}(\gamma(t))
and
c_{1}^{2}(t)+c_{2}^{2}(t)\leq 1
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. It turns out on
\mathbb{H}
we can always connect two points
x,y\in\mathbb{H}
by a subunit curve by Chow’s theorem (see
[
BLU07
, Chapter 19]
) and so we may define

(5.3)

d(x,y)=\min\left\{T>0\mid\exists\text{ a subunit curve }\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}\text{ with }\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma(0)=x\\
\gamma(T)=y\end{array}\right\}.

From this perspective the distance between two points is the minimum amount of time required to connect two points with a curve with bounded coefficients and which can only “travel in the directions” of
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
. Alternatively, we may remove the condition of bounded coefficients and instead, with the length of a curve defined as
\ell(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert c(t)\right\rvert^{2}dt
, define
d
as the minimum length of a curve which connects two points in unit time. In any case the two definitions are equivalent and
d
is a metric
[
JSC87
, Proposition 3.1]
, called the
Carnot-Carathéodory metric
, and it endows
\mathbb{H}
with geodesics that are distinguished from the geodesics of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. More precisely, the geodesics of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are straight lines, but the geodesics of
\mathbb{H}
with respect to
d
are helices
[
HZ15
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Of greater importance to this paper is the fact
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
allow us to define an analogue of the Euclidean gradient
\nabla=(\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\partial_{3})
, namely the
sub-gradient

(5.4)

\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}=(X^{1},X^{2})

on
\mathbb{H}
. Similarly, we may define an analogue of the euclidean Laplacian
\Delta=\partial_{1}^{2}+\partial_{2}^{2}+\partial_{3}^{2}
, called the
sub-Laplacian
as follows

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{H}}=(X^{1})^{2}+(X^{2})^{2}.

The reader may notice the definition of the sub-gradient and the sub-Laplacian depend on the choice of generators
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
of
\mathfrak{h}
, but there is a canonical choice (and thus we may refer to “the” sub-gradient and “the” sub-Laplacian) of generators (which is taken in (
5.2
)) associated to the group law which makes the Jacobian of the left translation operator at zero coincide with the identity matrix, in analogue with the Euclidean case. The sub-Laplacian will not feature prominently in this paper but we refer the reader to
[
BLU07
, Chapter 5]
for more details.

The metric
d
also induces a “norm” on
\mathbb{H}
, by defining with some abuse of notation,

(5.5)

d(x)\vcentcolon=d(x,0).

The fact
d
is a norm requires some qualification since a’priori
d
need not satisfy the triangle inequality nor be positively homogeneous. If these properties are given the expected description which accounts for the group structure on
\mathbb{H}
, then
d

does
satisfy the triangle inequality defined with the group law
\circ
in (
5.1
) instead of the addition law
+
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e.

d(x\circ y)\leq d(x)+d(y).

Similarly, we may equip
\mathbb{H}
with a family of dilations
(\delta_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0}
mirroring the stratification of its Lie algebra:

(5.6)

\delta_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(\lambda x_{1},\lambda x_{2},\lambda^{2}x_{3}).

In doing so, we realise
\mathbb{H}
as a homogeneous Carnot group and we usually write the points of
\mathbb{H}
as
x=(w,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
so that (
5.6
) reads
\delta_{\lambda}(w,z)=(\lambda w,\lambda^{2}z)
. With respect to these dilations,
d

is
positively homogeneous, i.e.
d(\delta_{\lambda}(x))=\lambda d(x)
, and for this reason we call
d
a homogeneous norm.

It turns out that
d
shares a rather important property with the Euclidean norm
x\mapsto\left\lvert x\right\rvert
: it satisfies the eikonal equation away from the centre
\{(0,z)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{H}
.

Proposition 5.1

(
[
Mon00
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

The metric
d
satisifes

\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}d(w,z)\right\rvert=1

for all
w\neq 0
.

In light of this result we might be led to believe that
d
is the most “natural” choice of homogeneous norm on
\mathbb{H}
in that it analogises the Euclidean norm. However it does depart from the Euclidean norm; for instance, the isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{H}
is
not
optimised by
d
-balls
[
Mon00
, Proposition 4.16]
. In this paper, we study also another homogeneous norm
N
, called the Kaplan norm. On a general homogeneous Carnot group, the Kaplan norm is expressed as the fundamental solution of the sub-Laplacian raised to a certain exponent, and in the specific case of
\mathbb{H}
it takes the explicit form

(5.7)

N(w,z)=(\left\lvert w\right\rvert^{4}+16\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2})^{1/4}.

Interestingly enough homogeneous norms are topologically equivalent, and yet may exhibit different analytic behaviour. For instance
d
is smooth away from the centre of
\mathbb{H}
, while
N
is smooth everywhere except at zero. Moreover,
d
satisfies the eikonal equation, whereas for
N
one has

\smash{\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}N(w,z)\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert w\right\rvert}{N}},

and in particular
\smash{\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}N\rvert}
can be arbitrarily small far away from the origin. The proofs of these results on homogeneous norms can be found e.g. in
[
BLU07
, Chapter 5]
.

5.2.
The Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d

The problem of finding
\phi
and
\eta
satisfying
(C1)
on the Heisenberg group has been studied in the literature. As mentioned earlier, one already has the
q
-logarithmic Sobolev inequality courtesy of
[
IP09
, Theorem 5.2]
, which made use of the following
U
-bound for the Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d
first appearing in
[
HZ09
, Theorem 2.3]
:

(5.8)

\int\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{q}d^{q(p-1)}e^{-\alpha d^{p}}dh\lesssim\int\left(\left\lvert\nabla g\right\rvert^{q}+\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{q}\right)e^{-\alpha d^{p}}dh

for
\alpha>0
,
p>1
, and
q\geq 1
. If we consider the interactions
V
for which

\left\lvert\nabla_{i}V_{ij}(h_{i},h_{j})\right\rvert^{q}+\left\lvert\nabla_{j}V_{ij}(h_{i},h_{j})\right\rvert^{q}\leq B(1+d^{q(p-1)}(h_{i})+d^{q(p-1)}(h_{j})),

one starting point is
V_{ij}=P(d_{i},d_{j})
for a polynomial
P
and where
V_{ij}\vcentcolon=V(h_{i},h_{j})
,
d_{i}\vcentcolon=d(h_{i})
, and
d_{j}\vcentcolon=d(h_{j})
, in analogue with the shorthands
\eta_{i}
and
\eta_{j}
in the previous section. The subgradient is

\left\lvert\nabla_{i}V_{ij}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\nabla_{i}d_{i}\right\rvert\left\lvert\partial_{1}P(d_{i},d_{j})\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\partial_{1}P(d_{i},d_{j})\right\rvert

by Proposition
5.1
, and assuming
P
has degree
r\in\mathbb{N}
its subgradient is controlled by a polynomial
\partial_{1}P
of degree at most
r-1
. The same goes for
\left\lvert\nabla_{j}V_{ij}\right\rvert
, which means
\left\lvert\nabla_{i}V_{ij}(h_{i},h_{j})\right\rvert^{q}+\left\lvert\nabla_{j}V_{ij}(h_{i},h_{j})\right\rvert^{q}
is controlled by
1+d_{i}^{q(r-1)}+d_{j}^{q(r-1)}
modulo a constant. It follows
(C2)
amounts to
q(r-1)\leq q(p-1)
, i.e.
r\leq p
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
p>1
, and
q\in(1,2]
be dual to
p
. Define a local specification on
\mathbb{H}^{\mathbb{Z}^{D}}
by the following potential:

U_{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x_{\Lambda})=\alpha\sum_{i\in\Lambda}d^{p}(x_{i})+\sum_{i,j\in\Lambda}\beta_{ij}P(d(x_{i}),d(x_{j}))+\sum_{i\in\Lambda,\,j\notin\Lambda}\beta_{ij}P(d(x_{i}),d(\omega_{j}))

with
\left\lvert\beta_{i,j}\right\rvert<\beta
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, and for
P
a polynomial of degree
\mathbb{N}\ni r\leq p
. Then for all sufficiently small
\beta>0
, there exists a unique Gibbs measure
\nu
which satisfies the
q
-spectral gap inequality

\nu\left\lvert f-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{SG}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}.

Let us now discuss some natural generalisations. The first pertains to whether the phase
\phi=\alpha d^{p}
can be given some lower order terms, that is if
\phi
can be a semibounded generalised polynomial of order
p>1
. The answer is yes.

Proposition 5.2
.

The conclusion of Theorem
5.1
holds for
\phi=\alpha d^{p}+\widetilde{P}(d)
where
\widetilde{P}
is a generalised polynomial comprised of monomials of the form
d^{\alpha}
for
0\leq\alpha<p
.

Proof.

Much of this follows from the perturbation theory of
[
HZ09
, Theorem 2.2]
which implies (
5.8
) is valid for the potential
U=\alpha d^{p}+W
where
W
is any potential whose subgradient is controlled by
d^{p-1}
; in particular this allows
W
to have lower order terms of the form
d^{p-\varepsilon}
for any
0<\varepsilon\leq p
. There is an issue with fractional exponents, e.g.
d^{\varepsilon}
with
0<\varepsilon<1
, since
d^{\varepsilon-1}
is not controlled by
d^{\gamma}
for any positive
\gamma>0
in a neighbourhood of the origin.

Nonetheless such singularities are inconsequential; the purpose of a
U
-bound is control over what happens at infinity. To allow for
W
having singularities near the origin, we can follow the proof of
[
HZ09
, Theorem 2.1]
: decompose
f
into a part localised at the origin and another at infinity. The former is controlled by
\int f^{2}d\mu
modulo a constant, the latter by the arguments of
[
HZ09
, Theorem 2.2]
since we have cut away from the singularities near the origin.
∎

The second generalisation pertains to the interaction. One may hope that
V
may be replaced with a generalised polynomial. Indeed, the arguments for Theorem
5.1
did not rely on the integrality of the powers. Note that at this stage it does not appear possible to relax
(C2)
to allow for singularities in the subgradients
\nabla_{i}V_{ij}
,
\nabla_{j}V_{ij}
, in the same way we allowed for singularities in the phase perturbation
\widetilde{P}
. The problem lies with the fact the interaction in the statement of Theorem
3.1
is the
U
-bound function, and we shall sidestep the unfortunate obstacle by assuming the powers are either zero or at least one.

Proposition 5.3
.

The conclusion of Theorem
5.1
holds for
V_{ij}=P(d_{i},d_{j})
where
P
is a generalised polynomial comprised of monomials of the form
d_{i}^{\alpha}d_{j}^{\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
and
0\leq\alpha+\beta\leq p
.

The third generalisation concerns again the interaction, namely the question of whether other homogeneous norms can enter. Indeed, this boils down to a simple verification of
(C2)
and with enough conditions we can answer affirmatively.

Proposition 5.4
.

The conclusion of Theorem
5.1
holds for
V_{ij}=Q(N^{1}_{i},N^{1}_{j},\cdots,N^{k}_{i},N^{k}_{j})
for
N^{1},\cdots,N^{k}
a collection of homogeneous norms on
\mathbb{H}
smooth almost everywhere with bounded subgradient
\left\lvert\nabla N^{\ell}\right\rvert\leq K_{\ell}
for each
\ell=1,\cdots,k
and
Q
a generalised polynomial comprised of monomials of the form
\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(N^{\ell}_{i})^{\alpha_{\ell}}(N^{\ell}_{j})^{\beta_{\ell}}
for
\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell}\in\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
and
0\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\alpha_{\ell}+\beta_{\ell}\leq p
. In particular it holds for
k=1
and
N^{1}=d
.

Aside from
d
we will study in the next section the Kaplan norm
N
which (along with
d
) is a standard example of a homogeneous norm smooth almost everywhere (in fact, away from the origin) with bounded subgradient. One can also construct from two homogeneous norms another by taking, for instance, linear combinations or geometric means (or square roots of squares, say). In fact we may also work with quasinorms, that is norms which are not positive definite and which may vanish away from the origin. The prototypical example of a quasinorm is the horizontal norm
\left\lvert h\right\rvert
which is introduced in the next section and that plays the role of a “degenerated Euclidean norm” acting only on a proper subset of the coordinates in
\mathbb{H}
.

Note if a homogeneous norm is smooth away from the origin then it automatically has bounded subgradient by homogeneity considerations. However, homogeneous norms smooth almost everywhere in general need not have unbounded subgradient; one such example is the geometric mean
N^{1/2}\left\lvert h\right\rvert^{1/2}
of
N
and
\left\lvert h\right\rvert
whose subgradient blows up along the hyperplane
\{\left\lvert h\right\rvert=0\}
. This is not a norm, but can be made into a norm, for instance by adding a small norm
\varepsilon N
. Note as well a homogeneous norm need not even be smooth anywhere (let alone almost everywhere). For instance, take a smooth homogeneous norm and deform its ball nonsmoothly while preserving homogeneity and antipodal points.

Remark 5.1
.

Note by
[
BLU07
, Proposition 5.16.4]
that a smooth homogeneous norm is Lipschitz with respect to itself and therefore Lipschitz with respect to the Carnot-Carathéodory distance. By
[
Mon01
, Theorem 2.2.1]
such a norm has bounded subgradient.

Proof.

By the Leibniz rule

\left\lvert\nabla_{i}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(N^{\ell}_{i})^{\alpha_{\ell}}(N^{\ell}_{j})^{\beta_{\ell}}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\alpha_{\ell}(N^{\ell}_{i})^{\alpha_{\ell}-1}(N^{\ell}_{j})^{\beta_{\ell}}\left\lvert\nabla_{i}N^{\ell}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\alpha_{\ell}K_{\ell}(N^{\ell}_{i})^{\alpha_{\ell}-1}(N^{\ell}_{j})^{\beta_{\ell}}.

By equivalence of homogeneous norms we can control each
N^{\ell}_{i},N^{\ell}_{j}
with
d_{i},d_{j}
respectively and it now reduces to the case
k=1
and
N^{k}=N^{1}=d
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The monomials
\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(N^{\ell}_{i})^{\alpha_{\ell}}(N^{\ell}_{j})^{\beta_{\ell}}
can be realised in terms of powers of two homogeneous norms
\varrho^{\ell},\delta^{\ell}
; indeed, let us suppose that
(N^{\ell})
is a family of homogeneous norms then
(\prod_{\ell}(N^{\ell})^{\alpha_{\ell}})^{1/\sum_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}}
is itself a homogeneous norm. So we may restate the previous proposition with
Q
replaced by
Q_{ij}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(\varrho^{\ell}_{i})^{\alpha_{\ell}}(\delta^{\ell}_{j})^{\beta_{\ell}}
, i.e.
Q
is a sum of products of powers of homogeneous norms.

Remark 5.3
.

We may also be interested in interactions of the form
V_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})=d(x_{j}^{-1}\circ x_{i})
, that is replacing the Euclidean “arithmetic subtraction”
d(x_{i})-d(x_{j})
with the group subtraction
d(x_{j}^{-1}\circ x_{i})
. In this case the inversion must appear on the correct side; by
[
BLU07
, Proposition 5.2.7]
one finds

\left\lvert\nabla_{a}d(b^{-1}\circ a)\right\rvert\leq 1,\quad\left\lvert\nabla_{b}d(b^{-1}\circ a)\right\rvert\leq 1

since
d
commutes with left translation so
\nabla_{a}d(b^{-1}\circ a)=(\nabla_{a}d)(\tau_{b^{-1}}(a))
while the
\nabla_{b}
bound is exactly the same since
d(h)=d(h^{-1})
. One however can show for
N
the Kaplan norm that the subgradient in the
x_{j}
-coordinate of
N(x_{i}\circ x_{j}^{-1})
is unbounded, for instance in a neighbourhood of
(x,y,z)=(1,1,z)
. In any case by the pseudo-triangle inequality, powers of
d(x_{j}^{-1}\circ x_{i})
can appear in the interaction.

Remark 5.4
.

We remark that in the recent preprint
[
KP19
]
the authors study some models indicated above by partially alternative techniques, focussing on interactions dependent on the Carnot-Carathéodory distance to achieve also the logarithmic Sobolev inequality.
See also some older references
[
Pap10

Pap14
,
Pap18
]
by I. Papageorgiou.

5.3.
The Kaplan norm
N

One serious deficiency in the case of the Kaplan norm
N
is that at the time the
q
-SGI was proven for the measure
Z^{-1}e^{-\alpha N^{p}}
in
[
Ing10
, Theorem 4.5.5]
for
\alpha>0
and
p\geq 2
, the arguments used a degenerate
U
-bound
\eta(h)=N^{p-2}\left\lvert h\right\rvert^{2}
where
\left\lvert h\right\rvert=\left\lvert(x,y,z)\right\rvert=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{1/2}
is the horizontal norm, in the sense
\eta
fails to diverge to infinity in all directions, in particular along the
z

\left\lvert h\right\rvert=x=y=0
. But recently a
U
-bound for the Kaplan norm more suitable for our applications was derived, which we now state.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
BDZ21
, Theorem 1]
)
.

The following

\int\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{q}N^{p-3}e^{-\alpha N^{p}}dh\lesssim\int\left(\left\lvert\nabla g\right\rvert^{q}+\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{q}\right)e^{-\alpha N^{p}}dh

holds for any
\alpha>0
,
p,q\geq 2
, and
g
supported away from the unit
N
-ball
\{N<1\}
.

Remark 5.5
.

By the perturbation theory of
[
HZ09
, Theorem 2.2]
the result can be extended to any semibounded polynomial of order
p\geq 2
. Note of course
N^{p-3}
does not blow up at infinity unless

.

If
p>3
, then
\eta=N^{p-3}
diverges to infinity in all directions as required, and the
U
-bound can be extended to all of
\mathbb{H}
as
N^{p-3}\leq 1
in
\{N<1\}
, meaning we obtain the
U
-bound

(5.9)

\int\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{q}N^{p-3}e^{-\alpha N^{p}}dh\lesssim\int\left(\left\lvert\nabla g\right\rvert^{q}+\left\lvert g\right\rvert^{q}\right)e^{-\alpha N^{p}}dh

for
\alpha>0
,
p>3
, and
q\geq 2
. Note as before it suffices to consider
q=2
. By the same arguments for the Carnot-Carathéodory distance the
U
-bound for
\phi=\alpha N^{p}
and
\eta=N^{p-3}
furnishes a

-SGI for the Gibbs measure
\nu
. Note the

-SGI of
[
Ing10
, Theorem 4.5.5]
in the

-dimensional setting holds for

and with

the Hölder conjugate of

, so the
2
-SGI obtained in the infinite-dimensional setting here is weaker in that

must be larger than
2

cannot be less than
2
. The arguments for generalising the phase and interaction are identical.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
p>3
. Define a local specification on
\mathbb{H}^{\mathbb{Z}^{D}}
by the following potential:

U_{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x_{\Lambda})=\alpha\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\phi(x_{i})+\sum_{i,j\in\Lambda}\beta_{ij}Q(x_{i},x_{j})+\sum_{i\in\Lambda,\,j\notin\Lambda}\beta_{ij}Q(x_{i},\omega_{j}).

Suppose that:

(1)

\phi
is a semibounded generalised polynomial in

,

(2)

\left\lvert\beta_{ij}\right\rvert\leq\beta
for all

, and

(3)

is a finite sum of

(\varrho^{\ell},\delta^{\ell})_{\ell=1,\cdots,k}
with bounded subgradient and for

,

Then for all sufficiently small

, there exists a unique Gibbs measure

-spectral gap inequality

\nu\left\lvert f-\nu f\right\rvert^{2}\leq C_{SG}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{2}.

At this point, we remark that although the problem of replacing the phase with a different function (e.g. a different homogeneous norm not
d

N
) is more delicate as it requires the existence of a
U
-bound as in
(C1)
, the issue of finding valid interactions is generally easier. For instance, we have not done so here to simplify the exposition, but we may easily adapt our analysis to the case where the generalised polynomials are given nonconstant smooth coefficients. In that case it is straightforward to see that it suffices the order

of the monomial

Remark 5.6
.

Interactions given in terms of a (combination) of semibounded homogeneous polynomial would be interesting here. We remark that generally one can represent them as potentials given in terms of some homogeneous norms plus some correction (which could be possibly rearranged to a function of some other homogeneous norms). A nice example of such a reduction is provided in
[
BDWZ22
]
where it was proved that a sum of squares of harmonic functions for the sub-Laplacian in Heisenberg group can be expressed in terms of powers of the corresponding Kaplan norm.

Remark 5.7
.

We remark that given a phase

which depends on a smooth homogeneous norm
one can improve the properties of the one site measure by adding a potential with singularity in the horizontal direction
[
BDZ21a
]
.

6.
Generalisations to other nilpotent Lie groups

The method presented in this paper to prove
q
-SGIs can be extended to a wider class of spaces not and need not be restricted to the Heisenberg group. For instance, the results can be used to prove
q
-SGIs for Gibbs measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It can be shown using
[
HZ09
, Theorem 2.4]
that the
U
-bound (
5.8
) for the Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d
and the sub-gradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}
is entirely analogous in the Euclidean case, in particular it holds with the Euclidean norm
\left\lvert x\right\rvert
replacing
d
and the Euclidean gradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
replacing
\nabla_{\mathbb{H}}
, which extends the results of
[
BZ05
, Theorem 7.2]
in analogue with Theorem
5.1
. This is since the main ingredients in obtaining the
U
-bound is firstly the fact
d
satisfies the eikonal equation almost everywhere, a property shared with the Euclidean norm, and secondly the property
d\Delta_{\mathbb{H}}d
is bounded above by some constant
K
in the sense of distributions, a property which the Euclidean norm satisfies with
K=n-1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
p\geq 2
. Define a local specification on
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\mathbb{Z}^{D}}
by the following potential:

U_{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x_{\Lambda})=\alpha\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{p}+\sum_{i,j\in\Lambda}\beta_{ij}Q(x_{i},x_{j})+\sum_{i\in\Lambda,\,j\notin\Lambda}\beta_{ij}Q(x_{i},\omega_{j})

with
\left\lvert\beta_{ij}\right\rvert<\beta
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, and for

Q(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{r_{k}}\left\lvert x_{j}\right\rvert^{s_{k}}

a finite sum of
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
monomials in
\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert,\left\lvert x_{j}\right\rvert
of degree at most
p
with nonnegative exponents
r_{k},s_{k}
for
k=1,2,\cdots,N
belonging to
\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
. Then for
q
Hölder conjugate to
p
and for all sufficiently small
\beta>0
, there exists a unique Gibbs measure
\nu
which satisfies the
q
-spectral gap inequality

\nu\left\lvert f-\nu f\right\rvert^{q}\leq C_{SG}\nu\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{q}

for
f\in W^{1,q}(\nu)
.

The reason is because in our proof we made no use of any particular properties of the group; thus one could go through the proof and interpret
\nabla
as the Euclidean gradient everywhere, that is with
X^{1},X^{2}
replaced by the trivial vector fields
\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\partial_{3}
, and in general with
\nabla
any finite collection of vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. That being said, one should not expect the
U
-bounds to come for free, and in general one should certainly expect the
U
-bound ingredients
\nabla
,
\phi
, and
\eta
, be intimately related. The class of nilpotent Lie groups in this regard are our typical examples because their structure gives rise to a rich family of nicely behaved examples.
