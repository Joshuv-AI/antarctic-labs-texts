A Generalized Version of Chung’s Lemma and its Applications
Li Jiang lijiang@link.cuhk.edu.cn, Xiao Li lixiao@cuhk.edu.cn, Andre Milzarek andremilzarek@cuhk.edu.cn, Junwen Qiu jwqiu@nus.edu.sg

Abstract
Chung’s Lemma is a classical tool for establishing asymptotic convergence rates of (stochastic) optimization methods under strong convexity-type assumptions and appropriate polynomial diminishing step sizes. In this work, we develop a generalized version of Chung’s Lemma, which provides a simple non-asymptotic convergence framework for a more general family of step size rules. We demonstrate broad applicability of the proposed generalized lemma by deriving tight non-asymptotic convergence rates for a large variety of stochastic methods. In particular, we obtain partially new non-asymptotic complexity results for stochastic optimization methods, such as Stochastic Gradient Descent ( 𝖲𝖦𝖣 {\sf SGD} ) and Random Reshuffling ( 𝖱𝖱 {\sf RR} ), under a general ( θ , μ ) (\theta,\mu) -Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition and for various step sizes strategies, including polynomial, constant, exponential, and cosine step sizes rules. Notably, as a by-product of our analysis, we observe that exponential step sizes exhibit superior adaptivity to both landscape geometry and gradient noise; specifically, they achieve optimal convergence rates without requiring exact knowledge of the underlying landscape or separate parameter selection strategies for noisy and noise-free regimes. Our results demonstrate that the developed variant of Chung’s Lemma offers a versatile, systematic, and streamlined approach to establish non-asymptotic convergence rates under general step size rules.

Introduction

Chung’s Lemma
(
Chung, 1954
)
is widely used to derive convergence rates of stochastic optimization methods; see, e.g.,
(
Bottou et al., 2018
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2019
;
Nguyen et al., 2021
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2023
)
. In his seminal paper,
Chung (1954)
analyzed the stochastic approximation method introduced in
(
Robbins and Monro, 1951
)
for solving a univariate equation, which is considered the precursor to the modern stochastic gradient method (
{\sf SGD}
), under the polynomial step size rule
\alpha_{k}\sim 1/k^{p}
. Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k}
represent the sequence of iterates generated by the method in
(
Robbins and Monro, 1951
)
, and let
x^{*}
denote the corresponding solution. The recursion for
a_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[|x^{k}-x^{*}|^{2}]
analyzed in
(
Chung, 1954
)
can then be expressed as

a_{k+1}\leqslant\Big({1-\frac{c}{k^{p}}}\Big)a_{k}+\frac{d}{k^{p+q}},\quad\text{where}\quad p\in(0,1],\quad c,d,q>0.

(1)

A crucial task in the convergence analysis is to study how fast
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
converges to 0. Chung provided the following asymptotic estimate.

Chung’s Lemma (Informal)

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
be given as in (
1
). If
0<p<1
or
p=1
,
c>q
, then
a_{k}=\mathcal{O}(k^{-q})
.

The classical version of Chung’s Lemma only applies to polynomial step sizes. In order to go beyond such a specific step size rule and to encompass a broader class of strategies (e.g., exponential and cosine step size rules), we consider the following general recursion:

a_{k+1}\leqslant\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}}\Big)a_{k}+\frac{1}{t(b_{k})},

(2)

where
\{b_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
is given and
s,t:\mathbb{R}_{++}\to\mathbb{R}_{++}
are continuously differentiable functions. Clearly,
s(b_{k})
and
t(b_{k})
generalize the terms
k^{p}/c
and
k^{p+q}/d
in (
1
). Existing strategies for solving recursions of the form (
2
) mainly rely on explicit expansions:

a_{K+1}\leqslant a_{0}\prod_{k=0}^{K}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}\Big)+\sum_{k=0}^{K}\Big[{\prod}_{i=k+1}^{K}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)\Big]\frac{1}{t(b_{k})},

(3)

where
\prod_{i=K+1}^{K}(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}):=1
. While this methodology can seemingly deal with any choice of
s
and
t
, it requires to derive tight bounds of the different appearing product and sum terms, which can be highly nontrivial and case-dependent; see, e.g.,
(
Li et al., 2021
)
. Based on these observations, our work is motivated by the following core question:

Can we provide a
simple, non-asymptotic
upper bound for the sequence
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
defined in (
2
) (as in the classic Chung’s Lemma) for more
general step size rules
, i.e., for general
s(b_{k}),t(b_{k})
?

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we address the latter core question affirmatively by imposing an additional (mild) structure on
s
and
t
. We now list our main contributions:

\blacktriangleright

Generalized Chung’s Lemma.
We derive a novel general variant of Chung’s Lemma to characterize the behavior of
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
. An informal version is shown below:

Generalized Chung’s Lemma (Informal
Theorem

2.1
)

Suppose that
\{a_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
follows the recursion (
2
) with
r:=s/t
being convex on some interval
I
and
\{b_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq I
. Then, under certain conditions, we have for all
K\geqslant 0

a_{k+1}\penalty\ \leqslant\penalty\ \underbracket{C_{1}\cdot r(b_{k+1})\vphantom{\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)}}_{\text{$\mathcal{T}$-induced rate}}\penalty\ +\penalty\ \underbracket{C_{2}a_{0}\cdot{\prod}_{i=0}^{k}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)}_{\text{$\mathcal{S}$-induced rate}},\quad\text{for some }\;C_{1},C_{2}>0.

(4)

As indicated in the informal theorem, our generalized variant of Chung’s Lemma naturally decomposes the rate bound into two components, a
\mathcal{T}
- and an
\mathcal{S}
-induced rate. The
\mathcal{T}
-induced (often “slower”) rate is linked with the “error term”
{1}/{t(b_{k})}
which, in turn, is typically associated with stochastic gradient errors or other types of errors. The
\mathcal{S}
-induced (often “faster”) part represents the convergence rate derived from recursion (
2
) without the error term
{1}/{t(b_{k})}
and is solely connected to the mapping
s
.
In Corollary
2.6
, we present evidence demonstrating that the
\mathcal{S}
-induced term usually converges at a faster rate compared to the
\mathcal{T}
-induced part. Thus, the convergence rate of
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
in (
2
) is
\mathcal{O}(s(b_{k})/t(b_{k}))=\mathcal{O}(r(b_{k}))
, which generalizes Chung’s classical result
\mathcal{O}(k^{p}/k^{p+q})=\mathcal{O}(k^{-q})
for recursion (
1
). Overall, our generalized Chung’s Lemma provides a simple tool to obtain
non-asymptotic convergence rates
based on the recursion (
2
). As a direct application, we utilize
Theorem

2.1
to derive a non-asymptotic extension of the classical Chung’s Lemma (cf. Lemma
2.4
).

(\theta,\mu)
-PL Condition, Exponential and Cosine Step Sizes.

{\sf SGD}
and the random reshuffling method (
{\sf RR}

-PL condition; see Theorems
3.9

y_{k+1}\leqslant(1+\ell_{1}\alpha_{k}^{\tau})y_{k}-\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}y_{k}^{2\theta}+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau},

(5)

Lemma

Landscape Adaptivity and Noise Adaptivity of Exponential Step Sizes.
Our convergence analysis reveals that exponential step sizes behave distinctly from the other three variants, particularly regarding noise and landscape adaptivity. Most notably, they are the only schedule to exhibit landscape adaptivity—automatically attaining optimal convergence rates without prior knowledge of the PL exponent
\theta

Related Works

Recursions of the form (
2
) are abundant in stochastic optimization; see, e.g.,
(
Mishchenko et al., 2020
;
Nguyen et al., 2021
;
Bottou et al., 2018
;
Shamir, 2016
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2019
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2021
;
Ahn et al., 2020
;

)
. As mentioned, the functions
s,t
in (

\blacktriangleright

Chung’s Lemma and Extensions.
Chung’s Lemma
(
Chung, 1954
)
is a fundamental tool extensively used in the literature to analyze the convergence rate of stochastic approximation and optimization algorithms; see
(
Chung, 1954
;
Derman, 1956
;
Sacks, 1958
;
Fabian, 1968
;
Nemirovski et al., 2009
;
Moulines and Bach, 2011
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2019
;
Karimi et al., 2016
;
Bottou et al., 2018
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2021
;
Ahn et al., 2020
;
Nguyen et al., 2021
;
Fatkhullin et al., 2022
)
. Several extensions of Chung’s Lemma are derived in
(
Moulines and Bach, 2011
;
Ahn et al., 2020
;
Nguyen et al., 2021
)
, that are tailored to polynomial step size rules. In addition, some studies have considered the recursion (
2
) with specific non-polynomial forms of
s(b_{k})
and
t(b_{k})
; see
(
Li et al., 2021
;
Wang et al., 2021
)
. However, these analyses are conducted only on a case-by-case basis. To the best of our knowledge, there is no generalization of Chung’s Lemma that accommodates general terms
s(b_{k})
and
t(b_{k})
in a unified manner.

\blacktriangleright

Exponential and Cosine Step Sizes.

Exponential step sizes
(
Li et al., 2021
)
, also known as geometrically decaying step sizes
(
Goffin, 1977
;
Ge et al., 2019
)
, were used by
Goffin (1977)
for subgradient methods. This step size strategy is also widely used in machine learning, and is currently one of the learning rate decay schedules in TensorFlow
(
Abadi et al., 2015
)
and PyTorch
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
. The theoretical analysis of exponential step size is limited.
Ge et al. (2019)
provide derivations for least-squares regression problems. Theoretical and broader guarantees for a more general class of functions are shown in
(
Li et al., 2021
;
Wang et al., 2021
;
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
. Cosine step sizes, initially proposed by
Loshchilov and Hutter (2017)
, are used as a popular warm restart scheme in performing stochastic algorithms. Recently, cosine step sizes have been widely employed in the optimization and training of large language models. For instance, the Llama-2 model family
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
is pre-trained using an AdamW optimizer with a cosine step size rule. Its fine-tuning procedure uses the same step size scheme. Therefore, understanding the convergence properties of representative stochastic methods with this step size rule is crucial.
Yet, the convergence behavior of stochastic methods under cosine step sizes has not been well understood. The first complexity bounds for
{\sf SGD}
with cosine step sizes are presented in
(
Li et al., 2021
, Theorem 2)
. In the general nonconvex setting, extensions to an
{\sf RR}
-type method (with momentum) are considered in
(
Tran et al., 2021
, Corollary 4)
.

\blacktriangleright

Stochastic Optimization under PL-type Conditions.
The Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition
(
Polyak, 1963
;
Łojasiewicz, 1959
;
Łojasiewicz, 1963
)
provides a measure of the “strong convexity-like” behavior of a function without requiring it to be convex. The recent frameworks discussed in
(
Fontaine et al., 2021
;
Fatkhullin et al., 2022
)
rely on certain generalizations of the PL condition. In this paper, we consider the
(\theta,\mu)
-PL condition (
Definition

3.3
), which is identical to Assumption 3 in
(
Fatkhullin et al., 2022
)
, and encompasses the PL condition when
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
. The additional PL exponent,
\theta\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, enables this condition to hold for a wider range of functions, particularly those relevant to machine learning applications (see
(
Fatkhullin et al., 2022
, Appendix A)
for pertinent examples). Specifically, in the field of reinforcement learning, the objective function can satisfy the
(1,\mu)
-PL condition for some
\mu>0
; see
(
Wang and Zou, 2022
;
Agarwal et al., 2021
;
Mei et al., 2020
)
. These evidences indicate that analyzing the cases beyond the standard setting
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
holds significance in real applications.

\blacktriangleright

Adaptivity of Step Sizes in Stochastic Optimization Methods.
Adaptivity of algorithms is one of the central concerns in stochastic optimization, which seeks to achieve strong performance without relying on additional, often impractical, information. A substantial body of literature has investigated algorithmic adaptivity in this context, including but not limited to
(
Duchi et al., 2011
;
Li et al., 2021
;
Kingma, 2014
;
Orabona and Pál, 2021
;
Loizou et al., 2021
;
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
; for a comprehensive overview, see
(
Attia and Koren, 2024
)
. In this paper, we focus primarily on the noise adaptivity and landscape adaptivity of various offline step size schedules for stochastic gradient descent (
{\sf SGD}
) and random reshuffling (
{\sf RR}
). The concept of noise adaptivity, first introduced by
Li et al. (2021)
, refers to a step size that achieves optimal convergence rates in both noisy and noiseless settings without requiring prior knowledge of the noise level. Under the
(\frac{1}{2},\mu)
-PL condition,
Li et al. (2021)
demonstrates that the exponential step size exhibits noise adaptivity, whereas the cosine step size achieves only a suboptimal convergence rate in the noisy setting. A similar discussion on exponential step sizes also appears in
(
Vaswani et al., 2022
)
. A comprehensive understanding of noise adaptivity under the more general
(\theta,\mu)
-PL condition remains unexplored. In addition, we introduce the concept of landscape adaptivity, which describes the ability of a step size schedule to achieve the optimal convergence rate under the
(\theta,\mu)
-PL condition without requiring explicit knowledge of
\theta
. We demonstrate that, among the four step size schedules considered, only the exponential step size exhibits this form of adaptivity.

Generalized Chung’s Lemma

The following generalized version of Chung’s Lemma provides a new tool for analyzing the non-asymptotic upper bound for sequences
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
satisfying

a_{k+1}\leqslant\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}}\Big)a_{k}+\frac{1}{t(b_{k})},\quad\forall\penalty\ k\in[K]:=\{0,1,\dots,K\}.

(6)

Theorem 2.1

(Generalized Chung’s Lemma)
.

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R},\,\{b_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and functions
s,t:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given. Assume that
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
follows (
6
) and there is an interval
I
such that
b_{k}\in I
for all
k\in[K]
,
s(x)\geqslant 1
and
t(x)>0
for all
x\in I
, and
r:=s/t
is convex on
I
. If either of the following statements holds:

(a)

There exists a subgradient
g_{k}\in\partial r(b_{k})
, and a non-decreasing sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
such that for all
k\in[K-1]
,

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})\cdot g_{k}\,t(b_{k})\geq-1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{k+1}}.

(7)

(b)

The function
r
is differentiable on
I
and defining
u:=(\log r)^{\prime}s=r^{\prime}t
, there exists a non-decreasing sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
such that for all
k\in[K-1]
,

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})\cdot u(b_{k})\geq-1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{k+1}},

(8)

then we have

a_{k+1}\leqslant\lambda_{k+1}r(b_{k+1})+({a_{0}-\lambda_{0}r(b_{0})})\prod_{i=0}^{k}\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}}\Big),\quad\forall\penalty\ k\in[K-1].

(9)

Proof.

Let us set
\beta_{k}:=b_{k+1}-b_{k}
. By the convexity of the mapping
r
, we have

r(b_{k+1})\geqslant r(b_{k})+g_{k}\beta_{k}=r(b_{k})\Big(1+\frac{g_{k}}{r(b_{k})}\beta_{k}\Big)=r(b_{k})\Big(1+\frac{g_{k}t(b_{k})}{s(b_{k})}\beta_{k}\Big).

(10)

If statement (a) holds, substituting the latter estimate into (
7
), this yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}r(b_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geqslant\lambda_{k+1}r(b_{k})\Big(1+\frac{-1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{k+1}}}{s(b_{k})}\Big)=r(b_{k})\frac{\lambda_{k+1}(s(b_{k})-1)+1}{s(b_{k})}

\displaystyle\geqslant r(b_{k})\frac{\lambda_{k}(s(b_{k})-1)+1}{s(b_{k})}=\lambda_{k}r(b_{k})\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}}\Big)+\frac{1}{t(b_{k})}.

Hence, invoking the recursion (
6
), we obtain
a_{k+1}-\lambda_{k+1}r(b_{k+1})\leqslant(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})})(a_{k}-\lambda_{k}r(b_{k}))
. This verifies (
9
). When statement (b) is satisfied, we have
g_{k}=r^{\prime}(b_{k})
. Noting
u=\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}s=r^{\prime}t
, it holds that
u(b_{k})=g_{k}t(b_{k})
. Therefore, condition (
8
) immediately implies condition (
7
), from which (
9
) follows.
∎

The most significant setting in
Theorem

2.1
arises when the sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
can be chosen to be constant. This will form the basis for the majority of our subsequent analysis. We now provide a simple example to briefly illustrate the application of

.

Example 2.2
.

Let us consider the recursion

\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\beta>0

,
s\equiv\frac{1}{\alpha}
,

\lambda=1

is convex and we have

for all

. Consequently,

2.1
is applicable and we have
a_{k}\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+(1-\alpha)^{k}(a_{0}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha})
for all

.

Remark 2.3
.

The convexity requirement on

in

is not strict since there is flexibility in choosing

Non-asymptotic Extension of Chung’s Lemma.
As a quick application, we use
Theorem

2.1
to obtain a non-asymptotic extension of the classical Chung’s Lemma.

Lemma 2.4

(Non-asymptotic Chung’s Lemma)
.

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
be given satisfying

a_{k+1}\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{c}{(k+\gamma)^{\nu}}\Big)a_{k}+\frac{d}{(k+\gamma)^{\nu+q}},\quad\nu\in(0,1],\penalty\ q>0,\penalty\ c,d>0,\penalty\ \gamma\geqslant c^{\frac{1}{\nu}}.

Then, the following statements hold:

(a)

If
\nu\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\geqslant c^{\frac{1}{\nu}}
and
\gamma>(\frac{q}{c})^{\frac{1}{1-\nu}}
, then

\displaystyle a_{k+1}\leqslant\frac{\lambda d}{c}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\Big(a_{0}-\frac{\lambda d}{c\gamma^{q}}\Big)^{+}\exp\Big(\frac{c\gamma^{1-\nu}}{1-\nu}\Big)\exp\Big(-\frac{c(k+1+\gamma)^{1-\nu}}{1-\nu}\Big),

holds for all
k\in[K]
, where
\lambda=\frac{c\gamma^{1-\nu}}{c\gamma^{1-\nu}-q}
and
(x)^{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
.

(b)

If
\nu=1
,
c>q
, and
\gamma\geqslant c
, then

a_{k+1}\leqslant\frac{d}{c-q}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\gamma^{c}\Big(a_{0}-\frac{d}{(c-q)\gamma^{q}}\Big)^{+}(k+1+\gamma)^{-c}.

Proof.

We apply
Theorem

2.1
(b) to prove the stated non-asymptotic version of the classical Chung’s Lemma. To make use of
Theorem

2.1
(b), we first specify the following sequences and functions:

b_{k}:=k+\gamma,\quad s(x):={x^{\nu}}/{c},\quad\text{and}\quad t(x):={x^{\nu+q}}/{d}.

We only need to check if the conditions in
Theorem

2.1
(b) are fulfilled. Let us note that the mapping
r(x):=s(x)/t(x)=\frac{d}{c}x^{-q}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}_{++}
. Then
(\log(r))^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{q}{x}
, and it holds that

u(x):=(\log(r))^{\prime}(x)s(x)=\Big(-\frac{q}{x}\Big)\cdot\Big(\frac{x^{\nu}}{c}\Big)=-\frac{q}{c}x^{\nu-1}

and
s(x)\geqslant 1
for all
x\geqslant c^{{1}/{\nu}}
. By assumption, we have
\gamma\geqslant c^{{1}/{\nu}}
implying
b_{k}\in I:=[c^{{1}/{\nu}},\infty)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
for all
k\in[K]
. Now, it only remains to verify the condition (
8
). Since
b_{k+1}-b_{k}=1
, we need to find a non-decreasing sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

-\frac{q}{c}(k+\gamma)^{\nu-1}\geqslant-1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{k+1}},\quad\forall\;k\in[K].

(11)

Based on the parameter
p\in(0,1]
, we now discuss different cases.

Case I:

\nu\in(0,1)
. Let us set
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda=\frac{c\gamma^{1-\nu}}{c\gamma^{1-\nu}-q}
. Per assumption, we further have
\gamma>(\frac{q}{c})^{\frac{1}{1-\nu}}
and thus, we can infer
\lambda>0
and

-\frac{q}{c}(k+\gamma)^{\nu-1}\geqslant-\frac{q}{c}\gamma^{\nu-1}=-1+\frac{c\gamma^{1-\nu}-q}{c\gamma^{1-\nu}}=-1+\frac{1}{\lambda}\quad\forall\penalty\ k\in[K].

(12)

Hence, the condition (
11
) is satisfied for this choice of
\lambda_{k}
and
\lambda
. Using
Theorem

2.1
(b), the inequality
1-x\leqslant\exp(-x)
, and the integral test (
Proposition

A.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle a_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda r(b_{k+1})+(a_{0}-\lambda r(\gamma))\cdot{\prod}_{i=0}^{k}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda d}{c}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\Big(a_{0}-\frac{\lambda d}{c\gamma^{q}}\Big){\prod}_{i=0}^{k}\Big(1-\frac{c}{(i+\gamma)^{\nu}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda d}{c}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\Big(a_{0}-\frac{\lambda d}{c\gamma^{q}}\Big)^{+}\exp\Big(-{\sum}_{i=0}^{k}\frac{c}{(i+\gamma)^{\nu}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda d}{c}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\Big(a_{0}-\frac{\lambda d}{c\gamma^{q}}\Big)^{+}\exp\Big(\frac{c\gamma^{1-\nu}}{1-\nu}\Big)\exp\Big(-\frac{c(k+1+\gamma)^{1-\nu}}{1-\nu}\Big),

where the last line is due to
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\,(i+\gamma)^{-\nu}\geq\int_{0}^{k+1}(x+\gamma)^{-\nu}\textup{{d}}x=\frac{1}{1-\nu}[(k+1+\gamma)^{1-\nu}-\gamma^{1-\nu}]
.

Case II:

\nu=1
,
c>q
, and
\gamma\geqslant c
. Setting
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda=\frac{c}{c-q}
, the condition (
11
) is obviously satisfied (cf. (
12
)). Repeating the core steps in
Case I
, we obtain

\displaystyle a_{k+1}\leqslant\penalty

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda d}{c}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\Big(a_{0}-\frac{\lambda d}{c\gamma^{q}}\Big){\prod}_{i=0}^{k}\Big(1-\frac{c}{i+\gamma}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\penalty

\displaystyle\frac{d}{c-q}(k+1+\gamma)^{-q}+\gamma^{c}\Big(a_{0}-\frac{d}{(c-q)\gamma^{q}}\Big)^{+}(k+1+\gamma)^{-c}.

Notice that the difference in the analysis only stems from the integral test (Proposition
A.1
):
{\sum}_{i=0}^{k}\,\frac{1}{i+\gamma}\geqslant{\int}_{\hskip-2.84526pt0}^{k+1}\frac{1}{x+\gamma}\;\textup{{d}}x=\log(k+1+\gamma)-\log(\gamma)
.
∎

Extension Lemma.
We now introduce an additional lemma to enhance
Theorem

2.1
(b) for cases where constructing
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
is only possible for a subset of
[K]
, such as with exponential and cosine step sizes (
Section

3.4
). Our strategy involves partitioning
[K]
: we first find the maximal scalar
\overline{K}
that satisfies (
8
) to bound
a_{\overline{K}+1}
by
Theorem

2.1
(b); in a second step,
Lemma

2.5
can be used to extend this estimate to
a_{K}
.

Lemma 2.5

(Extension Lemma)
.

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
\{b_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
and the functions
s,t:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{++}
be given. Suppose that
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
follows the recursion (
6
). Assume that
s(b_{k})\geqslant 1
for all
k\in[K]
. If there exist
k_{0},\overline{K}\in[K-1]
,
k_{0}\leqslant\overline{K}
,
B,C\in\mathbb{R}
such that
r(b_{k})\leqslant B
for all
k\geqslant\overline{K}+1
and

a_{\overline{K}+1}\leqslant B+C\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{\overline{K}}\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}}\Big),

(13)

then, we have
a_{K}\leqslant B+C\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{K-1}({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}})
.

Proof.

There is nothing to show in the case
\overline{K}=K-1
. We can verify the result for
\overline{K}<K-1
via induction. For any
k=\overline{K}+1,\dots,K-1
, if

a_{k}\leqslant B+C\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{k-1}({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}})
,
and by applying (
6
), we obtain

\displaystyle a_{k+1}-C{\prod}_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}}\Big)\leqslant\penalty\

\displaystyle\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}}\Big)a_{k}+\frac{1}{t(b_{k})}-C{\prod}_{i=k_{0}}^{k}\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\penalty\

\displaystyle\Big({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}}\Big)B+\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}r(b_{k})\leqslant B,

where we used
r(b_{k})\leqslant B
for any
k\geqslant\overline{K}+1
in the last step. Hence, by induction, it follows
a_{K}\leqslant B+C\prod_{i=k_{0}}^{K-1}({1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}})
.
∎

Forgetting Initial Conditions.
The following corollary offers insight into the relationship between the
\mathcal{S}
- and
\mathcal{T}
-induced components in (
4
).

Corollary 2.6
.

Let the conditions in
Theorem

2.1

(b)
hold with
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda>0
. Then, we have

a_{k+1}\leqslant\lambda r(b_{k+1})+\Big(\frac{a_{0}}{r(b_{0})}-\lambda\Big)^{+}\prod_{i=0}^{k}\Big(1-\frac{1/\lambda}{s(b_{i})-1+1/\lambda}\Big)r(b_{k+1}),\quad\forall\;k\in[K].

Proof.

From
Theorem

2.1
(b), we have for all
k\in[K]
,

\displaystyle a_{k+1}\leqslant\penalty\

\displaystyle\lambda r(b_{k+1})+\Big(\frac{a_{0}}{r(b_{0})}-\lambda\Big)^{+}{\prod}_{i=0}^{k}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)r(b_{0})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\lambda r(b_{k+1})+\Big(\frac{a_{0}}{r(b_{0})}-\lambda\Big)^{+}{\prod}_{i=0}^{k}\Big[\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)\frac{r(b_{i})}{r(b_{i+1})}\Big]r(b_{k+1}).

Combining equation (
10
) (where
u(b_{k})=g_{k}t(b_{k})
) and (
8
), we obtain

r(b_{i+1})\geqslant\frac{(b_{i+1}-b_{i})u(b_{i})+s(b_{i})}{t(b_{i})}\geqslant\frac{s(b_{i})-1+{1}/{\lambda}}{t(b_{i})}>0.

Thus, we can establish the upper bound

\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)\frac{r(b_{i})}{r(b_{i+1})}\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{i})}\Big)\frac{s(b_{i})}{s(b_{i})-1+{1}/{\lambda}}=1-\frac{1/\lambda}{s(b_{i})-1+{1}/{\lambda}}.

Substituting this bound into the first estimate finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary

2.6
illustrates that under
Theorem

2.1
(b), the
\mathcal{S}
-induced rate outpaces the
\mathcal{T}
-induced rate by a factor of
\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1-\frac{1/\lambda}{s(b_{i})-1+1/\lambda})
. Since the initial point affects only the
\mathcal{S}
-induced rate, this phenomenon is often termed “forgetting initial conditions”. While prior literature addresses this property only in specific settings—such as for polynomial step sizes in
(
Moulines and Bach, 2011
)
—
Corollary

2.6
holds in a more general context.

Convergence Rates of Stochastic Methods under the (
\theta
,
\mu
)-Polyak Łojasiewicz (PL) Condition

In this section, we establish non-asymptotic rates for stochastic methods under various step size strategies by applying the generalized Chung’s Lemma—
Theorem

2.1
. We consider the problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f(x),

(14)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function.

3.1
Basic Assumptions and Descent Properties

In
Algorithms

1
and
2
, we summarize the main algorithmic steps of the stochastic gradient descent (
{\sf SGD}
) method and the random reshuffling (
{\sf RR}
) method to solve the optimization problem (
14
). We note that, in
{\sf RR}
, the objective function
f
is assumed to have the finite-sum structure
f(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x)
.

Algorithm 1

Stochastic Gradient Descent (
{\sf SGD}
)

1:

Choose an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}
;

2:

while
stopping criterion is not met
do

3:

Generate a new stochastic gradient
g^{k}\approx\nabla f(x^{k})
;

4:

Perform the update
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\alpha_{k}g^{k}
;

5:

end

while

Algorithm 2

Random Reshuffling (
{\sf RR}
)

1:

Choose an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}
;

2:

while
stopping criterion is not met
do

3:

Generate a uniformly random permutation
\pi^{k}
of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
and set
\tilde{x}_{0}^{k}=x^{k}
;

4:

for

i=1,2,\ldots,N

do

5:

Update the inner iterate via

\tilde{x}_{i}^{k}=\tilde{x}_{i-1}^{k}-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{N}\,\nabla{f}_{\pi^{k}_{i}}(\tilde{x}_{i-1}^{k});

6:

end

for

7:

Perform the update
x^{k+1}=\tilde{x}_{N}^{k}
;

8:

end

while

Our analysis will be based on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k},\mathbb{P})
to which the stochastic processes
\{\bm{x}^{k}\}_{k}
generated by these two algorithms are adapted. We make the following standard assumptions for
{\sf SGD}
and
{\sf RR}
.

Assumption 3.1

(Conditions for
{\sf SGD}
)
.

We consider the assumptions:

(A.1)

The objective
f
is
{\sf L}
-smooth, i.e.,
\nabla f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
{\sf L}
-Lipschitz continuous.

(A.2)

The stochastic gradient
\bm{g}^{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})
, and there exist
{\sf A},\sigma\geqslant 0
,
\bar{f}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\bm{x}^{k})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\leqslant{\sf A}(f(\bm{x}^{k})-\bar{f})+\sigma^{2}
a.s. .

Assumption 3.2

(Conditions for
{\sf RR}
)
.

We work with the conditions:

(B.1)

The individual component functions
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N}
are all
{\sf L}
-smooth.

(B.2)

There exist constants
{\sf A},\sigma>0
,
\bar{f}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that

\frac{1}{N}\,{\sum}_{i=1}^{N}\|\nabla f_{i}(x)-\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\leqslant{\sf A}(f(x)-\bar{f})+\sigma^{2}.

(B.3)

The permutations
\{\pi^{k}\}_{k}
are sampled independently (for each
k
) and uniformly without replacement from
\{1,\dots,N\}
.

The Lipschitz smoothness in
(A.1)
and
(B.1)
is ubiquitous in the analysis of optimization methods, see, e.g.,
(
Bolte et al., 2014
;
Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2019
;
HaoChen and Sra, 2019
)
. The assumptions
(A.2)
and
(B.2)
have been extensively employed in the analysis of stochastic gradient-based methods
(
Nguyen et al., 2021
;
Mishchenko et al., 2020
;
Shamir, 2016
;
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2014
)
. In the case
{\sf A}=0
,
(A.2)
and
(B.2)
reduce to bounded variance conditions. Furthermore,
(B.2)
is applicable in a wide range of scenarios. For instance,
(B.2)
is satisfied when each individual component function
f_{i}
is Lipschitz smooth and bounded from below, see
(
Mishchenko et al., 2020

.

For both
{\sf SGD}
and
{\sf RR}

Definition 3.3

Given

Assumption 3.4

(PL Condition for

We consider the following assumption:

(P)

For some

(A.2)

(B.2)

Step 1: Linearization and Relaxation

When

17
) can be reformulated as

y_{k+1}\leqslant(1+\ell_{1}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}-\ell_{2}\alpha_{k})y_{k}+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau},\quad k\in[K-1].

Define

\bar{\alpha}=\max\{\alpha_{k}:0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1\}.

(18)

If
\ell_{1}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}\leqslant\frac{\ell_{2}}{2}\alpha_{k}
, which is equivalent to
\bar{\alpha}\leqslant(\ell_{2}/2\ell_{1})^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}
, then

y_{k+1}\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{\ell_{2}}{2}\alpha_{k}\Big)y_{k}+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau},\quad k\in[K-1].

(19)

Furthermore, if
\alpha_{k}\leqslant\frac{2}{\ell_{2}}
, recursion (
19
) can be analyzed using Theorem
2.1
. The requirements on
\bar{\alpha}
can therefore be summarized as

\bar{\alpha}\leqslant\min\Big\{\Big(\frac{\ell_{2}}{2\ell_{1}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}},\frac{2}{\ell_{2}}\Big\}.

(20)

However, when
\theta\neq\frac{1}{2}
, Theorem
2.1
is no longer directly applicable. This issue can be addressed by replacing the nonlinear term “
y_{k}^{2\theta}
” with a suitable linear approximation. Since the function
h_{\theta}(y):=y^{2\theta}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we can apply the inequality

y^{2\theta}\geqslant x^{2\theta}+2\theta x^{2\theta-1}(y-x)\quad\forall\penalty\ x,y\geqslant 0.

to construct a linear lower bound.
By choosing
x=u_{k}
and
y=y_{k}
, inequality (
17
) can be relaxed to

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1+\ell_{1}\alpha_{k}^{\tau})y_{k}-\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}[u_{k}^{2\theta}+2\theta u_{k}^{2\theta-1}(y_{k}-u_{k})]+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}

(21)

\displaystyle=[1+\ell_{1}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}-2\theta\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta-1}]y_{k}+[(2\theta-1)\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta}+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}],

where
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
is a (free) non-negative auxiliary sequence.

Step 2: Optimal Choice of
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
. The sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
should be selected such that the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

The term “
\theta\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta-1}
” is not smaller than “
\ell_{1}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}
” (for all
k
). Then, (
21
) can be further relaxed to

y_{k+1}\leqslant[1-\theta\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta-1}]y_{k}+[(2\theta-1)\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta}+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}].

(22)

2.

For (
22
), the expected rate
r_{k}\sim\frac{(2\theta-1)\ell_{2}\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta}+\ell_{3}\alpha_{k}^{\tau}}{\alpha_{k}u_{k}^{2\theta-1}}=(2\theta-1)\ell_{2}u_{k}+\ell_{3}\frac{\alpha_{k}^{\tau-1}}{u_{k}^{2\theta-1}}
is optimal (as fast as possible), cf.
Theorem

2.1
.

To satisfy the first requirement, it is sufficient to ensure that

u_{k}\geqslant({\ell_{1}}/{\theta\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta-1}}.

(23)

Meanwhile, to achieve tight estimates,
r_{k}
should be minimized. Optimizing
r_{k}
as a function of
u_{k}
over
\mathbb{R}_{++}
, we obtain the candidate
({\ell_{3}}/{\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}
for
u_{k}
. However, this choice does not necessarily satisfy the condition (
23
). To address this issue, we choose
u_{k}
to be proportional to
\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}
, i.e.,
u_{k}:=\zeta\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}
for some
\zeta>0
. Observe that for all
\theta\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
, we have

\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}}{\theta\ell_{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta-1}}=\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}}{\theta\ell_{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}\bar{\alpha}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta(2\theta-1)}}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\bar{\alpha}}\Big)^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta(2\theta-1)}}\leqslant\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}}{\theta\ell_{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}

To guarantee (
23
), it suffices to ensure that
(\frac{\ell_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}}{\theta\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\leqslant(2\theta-1)\delta
and
\zeta\geqslant(2\theta-1)\delta
for some
\delta>0
. Define

\mathfrak{p}(\theta)=\begin{cases}(2\theta-1)^{2\theta-1},\quad&\text{if $\theta\in(\frac{1}{2},1]$},\\
1,\quad&\text{if $\theta=\frac{1}{2}$.}\end{cases}

It is straightforward to verify that
\mathfrak{p}(\theta)\in[e^{-\frac{1}{e}},1]
for all
\theta\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, and that
\mathfrak{p}
is continuous at
\frac{1}{2}
. Assume that
\bar{\alpha}\leqslant(\frac{\theta\mathfrak{p}(\theta)\ell_{2}\delta^{2\theta-1}}{\ell_{1}})^{\frac{2\theta}{\tau-1}}
(recall that
\bar{\alpha}
is defined in equation (
18
)), which yields
(\frac{\ell_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}}{\theta\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\leqslant(2\theta-1)\delta
for all
\theta\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
. Then, we can choose
u_{k}
as follows:

u_{k}:=\zeta\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}},\quad\text{where $\zeta:=\max\{(2\theta-1)\delta,({\ell_{3}}/{\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\}$.}

(24)

Here,
\delta>0
is a free (algorithmic) parameter that will be specified later. By construction, this choice of
u_{k}
satisfies (
23
). Moreover, the condition
\zeta\geqslant({\ell_{3}}/{\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}
ensures that
\ell_{3}\leqslant\ell_{2}\zeta^{2\theta}
. Substituting
u_{k}
into inequality (
22
) and using the bound
\ell_{3}\leqslant\ell_{2}\zeta^{2\theta}
, we obtain:

y_{k+1}\leqslant(1-\theta\ell_{2}\zeta^{2\theta-1}\alpha_{k}^{1/\varrho})y_{k}+2\theta\ell_{2}\zeta^{2\theta}\alpha_{k}^{\tau},

(25)

where

\varrho:=\Big(1+(2\theta-1)\cdot\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}\Big)^{-1}=\frac{2\theta}{(2\theta-1)\tau+1}.

Denoting

\xi:=\theta\ell_{2}\zeta^{2\theta-1}=\max\{\theta\mathfrak{p}(\theta)\ell_{2}\delta^{2\theta-1},\theta\ell_{2}^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\ell_{3}^{\frac{2\theta-1}{2\theta}}\},

inequality (
25
) can be rewritten as

y_{k+1}\leqslant(1-\xi\alpha_{k}^{1/\varrho})y_{k}+2\zeta\xi\alpha_{k}^{\tau},

(26)

To apply Theorem
2.1
to inequality (
26
), it is additionally required that
\xi\bar{\alpha}^{1/\varrho}\leqslant 1
, i.e.,
\bar{\alpha}\leqslant\xi^{-\varrho}
. Therefore, the overall conditions on
\bar{\alpha}
are given by

\bar{\alpha}\leqslant\min\Big\{\Big(\frac{\theta\mathfrak{p}(\theta)\ell_{2}\delta^{2\theta-1}}{\ell_{1}}\Big)^{\frac{2\theta}{\tau-1}},\xi^{-\varrho}\Big\}.

(27)

Notably, the inequalities (
26
) and (
27
) reduce to (
19
) and (
20
), respectively, when
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
. This unifies the analysis across the entire range
\theta\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, which is not a coincidence, but rather a result of the deliberate design of
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
. The simplified and relaxed recursion (
26
) is the basis of our final step and of the application of the generalized Chung’s Lemma.

Step 3: Application of Chung’s Lemma to (
26
)
. Suppose that the step sizes
\alpha_{k}
can be represented as a function
\eta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
evaluated at
b_{k}
, i.e.,
\alpha_{k}=\eta(b_{k})
. For simplicity, we assume that
\eta
is differentiable. Setting
s(x)=\frac{1}{\xi}\eta(x)^{-1/\varrho}
,
t(x)=\frac{1}{2\zeta\xi}\eta(x)^{-\tau}
, we then have

r(x)=2\zeta\eta(x)^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}},\penalty\ \penalty\ u(x)=\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta\xi}\eta(x)^{-(1+\frac{1}{\varrho})}\eta^{\prime}(x).

(28)

The rest of the derivation involves determining the appropriate function
\eta
and sequence
\{b_{k}\}_{k}
to use for different types of step sizes and verifying the conditions stated in
Theorem

2.1
for such
\eta
and
\{b_{k}\}_{k}
.

Remark 3.6
.

A straightforward approach to ensure condition (
23
) is to directly assume that
({\ell_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}}/{\theta\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}\leqslant({\ell_{3}}/{\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}
and to choose
u_{k}=({\ell_{3}}/{\ell_{2}})^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{\tau-1}{2\theta}}
. However, this leads to an unnecessarily stringent constraint on
\bar{\alpha}
. In particular, under this strategy, the upper bound on
\bar{\alpha}
tends to zero as the noise term
\sigma^{2}
approaches zero (as
\ell_{3}
depends on
\sigma^{2}
). This is undesirable, as smaller noise should allow the use of a larger range of step sizes.

Notations and Important Constants

This subsection introduces the notations and key constants that will be used consistently throughout the remainder of the paper. We use
K
to represent the total iterations in
{\sf SGD}
or total epochs in
{\sf RR}
. For all
k\in[K]
, we denote

We introduce two sets of notations for analyzing the performance of
{\sf SGD}
(see
Algorithm

1
) and
{\sf RR}
(see
Algorithm

2
). For
{\sf SGD}
, we have

and choosing

(29)

For

and setting

\begin{gathered}\varrho_{r}=\frac{\theta}{3\theta-1},\quad\omega_{r}=\frac{1}{3\theta-1},\\

The explicit expressions for

and

\xi_{s}=\max\Big\{\theta\mathfrak{p}(\theta)\mu,\theta\mu^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\Big(\frac{{\sf L}\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big)^{\frac{2\theta-1}{2\theta}}\Big\},\quad\bar{\xi}_{r}=\max\Big\{\frac{\theta\mathfrak{p}(\theta)\mu}{2},\theta\Big(\frac{\mu}{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}\Big(\frac{{\sf L}^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\Big)^{\frac{2\theta-1}{2\theta}}\Big\},

where we adopt the convention

when

. To characterize the specific range of step sizes typically required by non-asymptotic convergence results, we define the following constants for

3.4
The Analysis of Exponential and Cosine Step Sizes

Exponential and Cosine Step Size.
We will use the following formats for exponential step sizes:

\alpha_{k}=\alpha\gamma^{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma=\Big(\frac{\beta}{K}\Big)^{\frac{p}{K}},\quad\alpha,p>0,\quad K>\beta>0,

and for cosine step sizes:

\alpha_{k}=\alpha\Big[{\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}}\Big]^{p},\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha,p>0.

The Splitting Technique.
A direct application of
Theorem

2.1
fails for exponential and cosine step sizes. To illustrate this, consider
{\sf SGD}
with exponential step sizes, setting
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
,
\beta=1
, and
p=1
for simplicity. Following the previous roadmap, we only need to analyze recursion (
26
). Focusing on (
26
) and applying (
28
) with
b_{k}=k
and
\eta(x)=\alpha K^{-\frac{x}{K}}
, we obtain
\varrho_{s}=1
and

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})=\frac{1}{\xi_{s}}\eta(k)^{-2}\eta^{\prime}(k)=-\frac{1}{\xi_{s}\alpha}K^{-(1-\frac{k}{K})}{\log(K)}.

Recall that
Theorem

2.1
(b) requires
(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})\geqslant-1+1/\lambda_{k+1}
for some
\lambda_{k+1}>0
. As
k
approaches
K
(e.g.,
k=K-1
), the left-hand side scales as
-\Theta(\log(K))
, which violates such lower bound for large
K
. Therefore, the condition cannot be satisfied for the entire sequence. However, it holds with
\lambda_{k}\equiv{2}
for the partial index set
k\leqslant\overline{K}
, where
\overline{K}:=\lfloor\log(\frac{\xi_{s}\alpha K}{2\log(K)})\frac{K}{\log(K)}\rfloor
. This allows us to apply
Theorem

2.1
to estimate the intermediate iterate
y_{\overline{K}+1}
. Crucially, using the extension lemma (
Lemma

2.5
), we can propagate this estimate to the final iterate
y_{K}
, establishing the desired convergence result without requiring the condition to hold globally.

Results on Exponential and Cosine Step Sizes.
Following the general roadmap and using the above splitting technique, we are now in the position to show convergence rates for both
{\sf SGD}
and
{\sf RR}
under the general
(\theta,\mu)
-PL condition when using exponential and cosine step sizes. We first provide an important technical lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Consider a non-negative sequence
\{y_{k}\}_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K}
satisfying

y_{k+1}\leqslant(1-c\alpha_{k}^{\nu})y_{k}+d\alpha_{k}^{\nu+q}

(32)

for all
k\in[K-1]
, where
c,d,\nu,q>0
,
K\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

(a)

Let
\alpha_{k}=\alpha\gamma^{k}
be given with
\gamma=(\frac{\beta}{K})^{\frac{p}{K}}
,
K>\beta>0
,
p>0
, and
0<\alpha\leqslant c^{-\frac{1}{\nu}}
; then

y_{K}\leqslant\frac{2d}{c}\max\Big\{\Big[\frac{2pq\log(K/\beta)}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{q}{\nu}},{\alpha^{q}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{K}\Big)^{pq}\Big\}+y_{0}\exp\Big(-\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}}{p\nu}\frac{[1-(\beta/K)^{p\nu}]K}{\log(K/\beta)}\Big).

(b)

Let
\alpha_{k}=\alpha[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}]^{p}
be given with
\alpha,p>0
and
\alpha\leqslant c^{-\frac{1}{\nu}}
; then

y_{K}\leqslant\frac{d}{c}\max\Big\{\Big[\frac{2D}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{q}{\nu}},2\Big(\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\Big)^{pq}\alpha^{\frac{q}{2p\nu+1}}\Big[\frac{2D}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{2pq}{2p\nu+1}}\Big\}+y_{0}\exp\Big(-\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}}{2^{\max\{1,p\nu\}}}K\Big),

where
D=\max\{1,2pq\pi^{2}\}
.

We continue with a detailed proof of
Lemma

3.7
. Upon first reading of the manuscript, the reader may safely skip to
Lemma

3.8
.

Proof.

We first prove part (a). Setting
b_{k}=k
,
s(x)=\frac{1}{c\alpha^{\nu}}\gamma^{-\nu x}
,
t(x)=\frac{1}{d\alpha^{\nu+q}}\gamma^{-(\nu+q)x}
(and
\eta(x)=\alpha\gamma^{x}
), we have

r(x)=\frac{d\alpha^{q}}{c}\gamma^{qx},\quad u(x)=(\log(r))^{\prime}(x)s(x)=\frac{q\log(\gamma)}{c\alpha^{\nu}}\gamma^{-\nu x}.

Due to
0<\gamma<1
,
r
is convex and differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
, which meets the requirements on
r
in
Theorem

2.1
. In addition,
u
is also a decreasing function on
\mathbb{R}
. Next, choosing
\lambda_{k}\equiv 2
, condition (
8
) in
Theorem

2.1
(b) is satisfied if

u(x)\geqslant-\frac{1}{2}\quad\iff\quad\gamma^{-x}\leqslant\alpha\Big[\frac{c}{2q\log(\gamma^{-1})}\Big]=\alpha\Big[\frac{cK}{2pq\log(K/\beta)}\Big]^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\quad\iff\quad x\leqslant\bar{x},

(33)

where
\bar{x}:=\log({\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}K}{2pq\log(K/\beta)}})\frac{K}{\nu p\log(K/\beta)}
.

Case I
. If
\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}K}{2pq\log(K/\beta)}<1
, i.e.,
\alpha<[{\frac{2pq\log(K/\beta)}{cK}}]^{\frac{1}{\nu}}
, it holds that
\bar{x}<0
. Applying (
32
) with
k=0
and
s(b_{0})\geqslant 1
, it follows

y_{1}\leqslant\frac{1}{t(b_{0})}+y_{0}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{0})}\Big)\leqslant r(b_{0})+y_{0}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{0})}\Big).

Using
Lemma

2.5
and setting
B=r(b_{0})
,
C=y_{0}
,
k_{0}=\overline{K}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle y_{K}

\displaystyle\leqslant r(b_{0})+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}\Big)=\frac{d\alpha^{q}}{c}+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d}{c}\Big[\frac{2pq\log(K/\beta)}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{q}{\nu}}+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}}).

Case II
. If
\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}K}{2pq\log(K/\beta)}\geqslant 1
, we can infer
\bar{x}\geqslant 0
. Setting
\overline{K}:=\min\{\lfloor\bar{x}\rfloor,K-1\}
and according to
Theorem

2.1
and
Lemma

2.5
(with
\lambda_{k}\equiv 2
), we have

y_{K}\leqslant 2r(b_{\overline{K}+1})+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}})=2r(\overline{K}+1)+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}}).

It is evident that
r(\overline{K}+1)\leqslant\max\{r(\lfloor\bar{x}\rfloor+1),r(K)\}
. Furthermore, due to
\lfloor\bar{x}\rfloor+1>\bar{x}
and (
33
), we have
\gamma^{-(\lfloor\bar{x}\rfloor+1)}>\alpha[\frac{cK}{2pq\log(K/\beta)}]^{{1}/{\nu}}
which, by the definition of
r
, implies

r(\lfloor\bar{x}\rfloor+1)\leqslant\frac{d}{c}\Big[\frac{2pq\log(K/\beta)}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{q}{\nu}}.

In addition, it holds that
r(K)=\frac{d\alpha^{q}}{c}({\frac{\beta}{K}})^{pq}
. Consequently, combining these estimates and the derivations in
Case I
and
Case II
, we obtain the following

y_{K}\leqslant\frac{{2}d}{c}\max\Big\{\Big[\frac{2pq\log(K/\beta)}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{q}{\nu}},{\alpha^{q}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{K}\Big)^{pq}\Big\}+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}}).

We continue to estimate the product term. Using
1-x\leqslant\exp(-x)
and
\gamma=(\frac{\beta}{K})^{\frac{p}{K}}
, we have

{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}})\leqslant\exp\Big(-c\alpha^{\nu}{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{\nu k}\Big)=\exp\Big(-c\alpha^{\nu}\frac{1-(\beta/K)^{p\nu}}{1-\gamma^{\nu}}\Big).

Due to
\log(\gamma^{\nu})\leqslant\gamma^{\nu}-1
, it follows
1-\gamma^{\nu}\leqslant\log(\gamma^{-\nu})=\frac{p\nu\log(K/\beta)}{K}
and we can infer

{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}\gamma^{\nu k}})\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}}{p\nu}[1-(\beta/K)^{p\nu}]\frac{K}{\log(K/\beta)}\Big)

and

y_{K}\leqslant\frac{{2}d}{c}\max\Big\{\Big[\frac{2pq\log(K/\beta)}{cK}\Big]^{\frac{q}{\nu}},{\alpha^{q}}\Big(\frac{\beta}{K}\Big)^{pq}\Big\}+y_{0}\exp\Big(-\frac{c\alpha^{\nu}}{p\nu}\frac{[1-(\beta/K)^{p\nu}]K}{\log(K/\beta)}\Big).

Next, we present the proof of part (b). Setting
b_{k}=[{\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}}]^{pq}
,
s(x)=\frac{1}{c\alpha^{\nu}}x^{-\frac{\nu}{q}}
,
t(x)=\frac{1}{d\alpha^{\nu+q}}x^{-(1+\frac{\nu}{q})}
(and
\eta(x)=\alpha x^{\frac{1}{q}}
), it holds that

r(x)=\frac{d\alpha^{q}}{c}x,\quad u(x)=(\log(r))^{\prime}(x)s(x)=\frac{1}{c\alpha^{\nu}}x^{-(1+\frac{\nu}{q})}

and
u(b_{k})=\frac{1}{c\alpha^{\nu}}[{\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}}]^{-p(q+\nu)}
.
For
K\geqslant 1
,
k=K-1
, using
Lemma

A.2
(c), we have

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})=-b_{k}u(b_{k})=-\frac{1}{c\alpha^{\nu}}\Big[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]^{-p\nu}\geqslant-\frac{1}{c\alpha^{\nu}}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big)^{-2p\nu}.

For
K\geqslant 2
and
k\leqslant K-2
, applying
Lemma

A.2
(b) with
x=\frac{1+\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})}{2}
,
y=\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}
, and

, it follows that

b_{k+1}-b_{k}\geqslant pq\Big[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]^{pq}\Big[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]^{-1}\Big[\frac{\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})-\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]

and we can infer

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})\geqslant\frac{pq}{c\alpha^{\nu}}\Big[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]^{-p\nu}\Big[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]^{-1}\Big[\frac{\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})-\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}\Big].

Next, using
Lemma

A.2
(c)–(e), we have for any

:

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})\geqslant\frac{4pq}{c\alpha^{\nu}}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big)^{-2p\nu}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big)^{-2}\Big[-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2K}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big)\Big]=-\frac{2pq\pi^{2}}{c\alpha^{\nu}K}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big)^{-(2p\nu+1)}.

For
k=K-1
, noticing

, we can combine the previous estimates. In particular, for all

,
K\geqslant 1
, it holds that

(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})\geqslant-\frac{D}{c\alpha^{\nu}K}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big)^{-(2p\nu+1)},

(34)

where

. As before and based on (

), we now discuss when condition (

(b) holds with
\lambda_{k}\equiv 2
.

Case I
. If

, then we have
\alpha\leqslant(\frac{2D}{cK})^{\frac{1}{\nu}}
and using (
32
) and

, it follows that

y_{1}\leqslant\frac{1}{t(b_{0})}+y_{0}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{0})}\Big)\leqslant r(b_{0})+y_{0}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{0})}\Big).

Clearly, by the definition of

and

, it holds that
r(b_{k})\leqslant r(b_{0})
for all
k\geqslant 1
. Applying
Lemma

2.5
with

,

,

, we obtain

\displaystyle y_{K}

\displaystyle\leqslant r(b_{0})+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}\Big(1-\frac{1}{s(b_{k})}\Big)=\frac{d\alpha^{q}}{c}+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}(1-c\alpha^{\nu}b_{k}^{\frac{\nu}{q}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d}{c}\left[\frac{2D}{cK}\right]^{\frac{q}{\nu}}+y_{0}{\prod}_{k=0}^{K-1}(1-c\alpha^{\nu}b_{k}^{\frac{\nu}{q}}).

Case II
. In the case
K>\frac{2D}{c\alpha^{\nu}}
, based on (
34
), we can infer
(b_{k+1}-b_{k})u(b_{k})\geqslant-\frac{1}{2}
if

Choosing

and
0\leqslant\overline{K}\leqslant K-1

2.1
and

y_{K}\leqslant 2r(b_{\overline{K}+1})+y_{0}\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}({1-c\alpha^{\nu}b_{k}^{\frac{\nu}{q}}})=\frac{2d\alpha^{q}}{c}\Big[\frac{1+\cos(\frac{(\overline{K}+1)\pi}{K})}{2}\Big]^{pq}+y_{0}\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}(1-c\alpha^{\nu}b_{k}^{\frac{\nu}{q}}).

Using
\overline{K}+1>\bar{x}=K(1-[\frac{2D}{c\alpha^{\nu}K}]^{\frac{1}{2p\nu+1}})
and Lemma
A.2
(c), we further obtain

1+\cos\Big(\frac{(\overline{K}+1)\pi}{K}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\Big(1-\frac{\overline{K}+1}{K}\Big)^{2}\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\Big[\frac{2D}{c\alpha^{\nu}K}\Big]^{\frac{2}{2p\nu+1}}

To handle the product term, we notice that

(b) and concludes the proof.

Appendix A
Preparatory Tools

In this first section, we list well-known mathematical facts and tools that will be used repeatedly in the later analysis.

Proposition A.1

(Integral Test)
.

Let
I=[\iota,\infty)
,
\iota\in\mathbb{R}
, be a given interval and let
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and non-increasing. Then, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}\cap I
with
a\leq b
, we have

\int_{a}^{b+1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\sum_{k=a}^{b}f(k)\leq f(a)+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Lemma A.2

(Technical Inequalities)
.

The following inequalities hold true:

(a)

\log(1+x)\leqslant x
,
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+x_{i})\leqslant\exp({\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}})
, for all
x,x_{i}\geqslant-1
.

(b)

x^{r}-y^{r}\geqslant ry^{r}x^{-1}(x-y)
for any
x,y,r>0
.

(c)

2({1-\frac{k}{K}})^{2}\leqslant 1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}({1-\frac{k}{K}})^{2}
, for all
k\in[K]
,
K\geqslant 1
.

(d)

1+\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}({1-\frac{k}{K}})^{2}
, for all
k\in[K-2]
,
K\geqslant 2
.

(e)

\cos(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K})-\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})\geqslant-\frac{\pi^{2}}{K}({1-\frac{k}{K}})
, for all
k\in[K-1]
,
K\geqslant 1
.

(f)

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}[{\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}}]^{r}\geqslant\frac{K}{2^{\max\{1,r\}}}
, for all
K\geqslant 1
,
r>0
.

Proof.

The first inequality in (a) is well-known. The second inequality in (a) can be derived by observing that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+x_{i})=\exp({\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log(1+x_{i})})
and subsequently applying the first inequality.

Part (b) follows from the weighted AM-GM inequality,
\frac{1}{w}(w_{1}x_{1}+w_{2}x_{2})\geq(x_{1}^{w_{1}}x_{2}^{w_{2}})^{1/w}
, for all
x_{1},x_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\geqslant 0
and
w=w_{1}+w_{2}>0
. In particular, setting
x_{1}=(\frac{x}{y})^{r}
,
x_{2}=\frac{y}{x}
,
w_{1}=1
,
w_{2}=r
, we have
(\frac{x}{y})^{r}+\frac{ry}{x}\geqslant(1+r)((\frac{x}{y})^{r}(\frac{y}{x})^{r})^{1/(1+r)}=1+r
for any
x,y,r>0
.

To prove (c), we use the double-angle formula
1-\cos(2a)=2\sin^{2}(a)
. This implies
1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})=1-\cos({\frac{(K-k)\pi}{K}})=2\sin^{2}({\frac{\pi}{2}({1-\frac{k}{K}})})
for
k\in[K]
. Given that
x\mapsto\frac{\sin(x)}{x}
is monotonically decreasing on
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
, we have
\frac{2}{\pi}x\leqslant\sin(x)\leqslant x
for all
x\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
. Substituting
x=\frac{\pi}{2}({1-\frac{k}{K}})
, we complete the proof of (c). Combining (c) with the inequality
(1-\frac{k+1}{K})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}({1-\frac{k}{K}})
for all
k\in[K-2]
and
K\geqslant 2
, we establish (d).

For part (e), we employ the sum-to-product identity
\cos(a)-\cos(b)=-2\sin({\frac{a+b}{2}})\sin({\frac{a-b}{2}})
alongside the properties
\sin(x)=\sin(\pi-x)
and
\sin(x)\leq x
. This yields

\cos\Big(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{K}\Big)-\cos\Big(\frac{k\pi}{K}\Big)=-2\sin\Big(\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2K}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{\pi}{2K}\Big)\geqslant-\frac{\pi^{2}}{K}\Big(1-\frac{k}{K}\Big).

To establish (f), we first prove the inequality

{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}^{r}\geqslant\min\Big\{K^{1-r}\Big({\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}\Big)^{r},{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}\Big\}

(51)

for all
0\leqslant x_{k}\leqslant 1
,
K\geqslant 1
. When
r\geqslant 1
, Jensen’s inequality yields

{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}^{r}\geqslant K\Big(K^{-1}{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}\Big)^{r}=K^{1-r}\Big({\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}\Big)^{r}.

For
0<r<1
, the non-increasing nature of the function
r\mapsto x^{r}
on
\mathbb{R}_{++}
, given
x\in[0,1]
, implies that
x_{k}^{r}\geqslant x_{k}
, leading to
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}^{r}\geqslant\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}
. Combining the two cases, this verifies the inequality (
51
). Next, setting
x_{k}=\frac{1+\cos(\frac{k\pi}{K})}{2}
, we apply Lagrange’s trigonometric identity
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\cos(ka)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sin(\frac{(2n+1)a}{2})}{2\sin(\frac{a}{2})}
to compute

{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}x_{k}=\frac{K}{2}+\frac{1}{2}{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}\cos\Big(\frac{k\pi}{K}\Big)=\frac{K}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sin((K-\frac{1}{2})\frac{\pi}{K})}{2\sin(\frac{\pi}{2K})}\Big]=\frac{K+1}{2}.

Since
\frac{K+1}{2}\geqslant\frac{K}{2}
, substituting this into (
51
) concludes the proof of (f).
∎

on
(0,\infty)
, it follows that

a_{k+1}^{1-2\theta}-a_{k}^{1-2\theta}\geqslant(1-2\theta)a_{k}^{-2\theta}(a_{k+1}-a_{k})\geqslant(2\theta-1)\alpha_{k}\mu
, where the second inequality is due to
a_{k+1}-a_{k}\leqslant-\alpha_{k}\mu a_{k}^{2\theta}
and
1-2\theta<0
. Summing this relation over
i=0,\dots,k-1
yields
a_{k}\leqslant[a_{0}^{1-2\theta}+(2\theta-1)\mu{\sum}_{i=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}]^{-\frac{1}{2\theta-1}}
.

Finally, we provide details about the step size estimates shown in
Section

3.6.2
. According to Theorems
3.13
and
3.16
, the optimally tuned choices for the constant and polynomial step sizes are given by
\alpha_{k}=\alpha=\Theta([\frac{\log(K)}{K}]^{\varrho_{s}})
and
\alpha_{k}=\Theta(k^{-\varrho_{s}})
. Applying
Proposition

A.1
, this yields
{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha_{k}=\Theta([\log(K)]^{\varrho_{s}}K^{1-\varrho_{s}})
and
{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha_{k}=\Theta(K^{1-\varrho_{s}})
, respectively. Similarly, based on
Theorem

3.11
, the optimal cosine step sizes satisfy
\alpha_{k}=\alpha[({1+\cos(\tfrac{k\pi}{K})})/{2}]^{p}
,
\alpha=[\tfrac{\beta\log(K)}{K}]^{\varrho_{s}}
, and using
Lemma

A.2
(f), we have
{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha_{k}=\alpha{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}[({1+\cos(\tfrac{k\pi}{K})})/{2}]^{p}=\Theta([\log(K)]^{\varrho_{s}}K^{1-\varrho_{s}})
. For exponential step sizes, we can choose
\alpha_{k}=\alpha\gamma^{k}
,
\gamma=(\tfrac{\beta}{K})^{\frac{p}{K}}
and following the proof of
Lemma

3.7
, we obtain
{\sum}_{k=0}^{K-1}\alpha_{k}=\alpha\tfrac{1-\gamma^{K}}{1-\gamma}\geq\tfrac{\alpha[1-(\beta/K)^{p}]}{p}\tfrac{K}{\log(K/\beta)}
.
