Dirichlet–Voronoi domain and injectivity radius of flag manifolds - equivariant cell structure on O ⁡ ( 3 ) / O ​ ( 1 ) 3 O(3)/O(1)^{3}
Arthur Garnier

Abstract
. In the first part of this work, we study Dirichlet–Voronoi domains for discrete isometry groups of Riemannian manifolds, in view of constructing cell structures on homogeneous (complete, real) flag manifolds, equivariant with respect to the action of the Weyl group. We give general results, allowing to build such a structure from an admissible one on the domain. In particular, the injectivity radius plays a key role in the method. The second part starts with the computation of the injectivity radius of (real and complex) flag manifolds; a first step towards the application of the method developed in the first part. Then, with the help of the quaternion algebra, we investigate the particular case of the flag manifold O ⁡ ( 3 ) / O ​ ( 1 ) 3 O(3)/O(1)^{3} of S ​ L 3 ​ ( ℝ ) SL_{3}({\mathbb{R}}) : we prove that the results of the first part apply and derive a new 𝔖 3 \mathfrak{S}_{3} -equivariant cell structure on it, whose cellular complex of ℤ ⁡ [ 𝔖 3 ] {\mathbb{Z}}[\mathfrak{S}_{3}] -modules is determined.

Introduction

Our general aim is to construct cell structures for flag manifolds which are equivariant with respect to the Weyl group. In the present paper, we propose an approach using tools from Riemannian geometry. It has the potential to give a general proof eventually, but several obstacles still need to be overcome. Here we tackle one of them: computing the
injectivity radius
of the flag manifold. The answer turns out to be nice and closely related to the combinatorics of the corresponding root system; see the discussion below and the Theorem
4.4
for a precise statement.

Specifically, let
K
be a simple compact Lie group, and
T<K
be a maximal torus. Since
T
is abelian, the
Weyl group

W=N_{K}(T)/T
acts freely on the
flag manifold

\mathcal{F}:=K/T
, a smooth manifold which carries many structures. Namely, recall that the
complexification

G=K^{\mathbb{C}}
of
K
is a complex algebraic group that contains
K
as a (maximal) compact subgroup. We also say that
K
is the
compact real form
of
G
. Choosing a Borel subgroup
B<G
containing
T
, the
Iwasawa decomposition
yields a diffeomorphism
\mathcal{F}\simeq G/B
, so that
\mathcal{F}
also has the structure of a smooth complex projective variety. Moreover, as a complex variety,
\mathcal{F}
has a preferred real structure. Indeed, the complex group
G
also possesses a
split real form

G_{\mathbb{R}}
, thus giving a real structure on
G
and this induces a real structure on
\mathcal{F}
. Letting
B_{\mathbb{R}}:=B\cap G_{\mathbb{R}}
, we get an identification of the real points
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{R}})\simeq G_{\mathbb{R}}/B_{\mathbb{R}}
. For instance, if
K=SU(n)
, then
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
G_{\mathbb{R}}=SL_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
. We can take
B
to be the subgroup of upper-triangular matrices in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and then the flag variety
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
is the classical variety of flags in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. The Iwasawa decomposition in this case is the Gram–Schmidt process and we have diffeomorphisms

\mathcal{F}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})=SL_{n}({\mathbb{R}})/B_{\mathbb{R}}\simeq SO(n)/S(O(1)^{n})\simeq O(n)/O(1)^{n},

where
S(O(1)^{n})
(resp.
O(1)^{n}
) is the subgroup of diagonal matrices in
SO(n)
(resp. in
O(n)
), which is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n-1}
(resp. to
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}
).

Observe now that the compactness of the group
K
ensures that it admits a bi-invariant Riemannian metric, yielding a Riemannian structure (called
normal homogeneous
) on the flag manifold
\mathcal{F}
, as well as on
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{R}})
, by restriction. Then, the action of
W
on
\mathcal{F}
(resp. on
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{R}})
) is isometric. This fact is a first clue suggesting that the normal homogeneous Riemannian geometry on
\mathcal{F}
may contain information on the combinatorics of the action of
W
and, hopefully, could even provide a
W
-equivariant cell structure on
\mathcal{F}
.

In
[
CGS22
]
, such a structure is constructed on the flag manifold
\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})\simeq O(3)/O(1)^{3}
of
SL_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, equivariant with respect to the Weyl group
W=\mathfrak{S}_{3}
. This is done by considering the free action of the
binary octahedral group

\mathcal{O}
(of order 48) on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and noticing that this action gives the same information as the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
on
\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
(i.e. we have a diffeomorphism
\mathbb{S}^{3}/\mathcal{O}\simeq\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})/\mathfrak{S}_{3}
). This manifold carries two natural metrics, which are proportional: the bi-invariant one inherited from
SO(3)
and the one induced by modding out the (standard) round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
by the quaternion group
\mathcal{Q}_{8}
; see §
5.1
for more details. This allows to interpret the cells of the
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-equivariant cell structure from
[
CGS22
]
as unions of open geodesics. For example, the 1-cells are (minimal) geodesic arcs between (the class of)
1
and the reflections of
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
, seen as points of
\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
. See the Remark
5.3
for details.

This suggests that an
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-equivariant cell decomposition can be obtained intrinsically using only a normal homogeneous metric (a purely Lie-theoretical object) and not the special fact that
\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
is a spherical space form. This will be the goal of the second part of this work.

In the more general case where
W\leq\mathrm{Isom}(M)
is a discrete isometry group of a connected complete Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, we introduce the
Dirichlet–Voronoi domain

\mathcal{DV}:=\{x\in M\penalty\ ;\penalty\ \forall w\in W,\penalty\ d(x_{0},x)\leq d(wx_{0},x)\},

where
d
is the geodesic distance on
M
and
x_{0}\in M
is a regular point. The first part is devoted to the exposition of general features of the set
\mathcal{DV}
, such as the Proposition
1.5
, stating that
\mathcal{DV}
is a path-connected fundamental domain for
W
acting on
M
. Moreover, we detail a method for constructing equivariant cell structures from ordinary ones on
\mathcal{DV}
that are admissible, in some sense. The precise statement is the following:

Theorem

(
Theorem
3.2
)
.

Suppose that
W
acts freely on
M
and that
\mathcal{DV}
carries a regular CW structure such that, for any subset
I\subset W\setminus\{1\}
, the “wall”
Z_{I}:=\mathcal{DV}\cap\bigcap_{w\in I}w\mathcal{DV}
is a (possibly empty) disjoint union of closed
|I|
-codimensional cells. Then, the
W
-translates of this structure form a
W
-equivariant CW structure on
M
.

This is the key result that we shall use in the sequel, to derive a new
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-equivariant CW structure on
\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
. As stated in the Proposition
2.1
, the
injectivity radius
of
(M,g)
is an important invariant in our method.

Therefore, we next start the second part by computing the injectivity radius of any (complex or real) flag manifold, equipped with a
normal homogeneous metric
, i.e. one which is induced by a bi-invariant metric on the Lie group. Such a metric is unique up to scalar and we choose to work with the metric induced by (minus) the Killing form on the Lie algebra and we call this metric

. Specifically, after observing that the flag manifold
K/T=:\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
only depends on the root system of

Theorem

(

4.4
)
.

The injectivity radius
\inj(\mathcal{F}_{\Phi})
of a standard homogeneous flag manifold
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
is given by

\inj(\mathcal{F}_{\Phi})=\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}},

where

is the dual Coxeter number of the (simple) root system
\Phi
. Moreover, this is the distance between 1 and any reflection of
W
, whose associated root is long.

In particular, we retrieve the Böttcher–Wenzel inequality
[
BW08
]
for skew-hermitian matrices and the Bloch–Iserles inequalities
[
BI05
]
for skew-symmetric (real) matrices.

We then focus further on the real flag manifold
\mathcal{F}_{3}({\mathbb{R}}):=\mathcal{F}_{A_{2}}({\mathbb{R}})=SO(3)/\{\pm 1\}^{2}
of type

, for which we first prove that the Dirichlet–Voronoi domain

is a 3-cell and the twenty-four extremal points are some of the

Part I
Generalities on Dirichlet–Voronoi domains in Riemannian manifolds and related cell structures

In this first part, we outline a general method for constructing cell structures on Riemannian manifolds, equivariant with respect to a finite group of isometries, using Dirichlet–Voronoi domains; in view of applying it to real flag manifolds.

The first section gives general results on Dirichlet–Voronoi domains. In particular, we exhibit a condition on the
injectivity radius
of the manifold under which the interior of such a domain is a cell; see Propositions
1.5
and
2.1
. We also give a useful criterion (Lemma
2.2
) for a geodesic ball to contain the domain.

Then, we show how controlling the size of the Dirichlet–Voronoi domain in terms of the
injectivity radius
of the manifolds ensures that its interior is a top-cell and that its “walls” tessellate its boundary: a Euclidean sphere. These are preliminary facts towards the construction of a cell structure on the domain, under the injectivity radius condition.

In the third section, we prove that some compatibility assumptions on a (regular) CW-structure on the domain allow to induce an equivariant cell structure on the whole manifold. This key result will be applied in the end of the second part, to build an
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-equivariant CW-structure on the flag manifold of
SL_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
.

1.
Definitions and general properties

In this section, we fix
(M,g)
a connected complete Riemannian manifold, with geodesic distance
d
, and
W\leq\mathrm{Isom}(M)
a discrete subgroup of the isometry group of
(M,g)
. By a classical result (see
[
AKLM07

W

Inspired by the study of Fuchsian groups, we may consider the Dirichlet–Voronoi domain of

\mathcal{F}

Definition 1.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in M

\bullet

The

centered at

is the following subset of

\mathcal{DV}:=\{x\in M\penalty\ ;\penalty\ \forall w\in W,\penalty\ d(x_{0},x)\leq d(wx_{0},x)\}.

\bullet

For

, we denote by

the
dissecting hypersurface

H_{w}:=\{x\in M\penalty\ ;\penalty\ d(x_{0},x)=d(wx_{0},x)\}

Z_{w}:=\mathcal{DV}\cap H_{w}=\mathcal{DV}\cap w\mathcal{DV}.

\bullet

For

Z_{I}:=\bigcap_{w\in I}Z_{w}=\mathcal{DV}\cap\bigcap_{w\in I}w\mathcal{DV}.

If the action if free, we may consider the orbit space

can be interpreted as the set of elements

Remark 1.2
.

As already mentioned, the domains
\mathcal{DV}

[

[

\mathcal{DV}
to be a

injectivity radius

\inj_{x_{0}}(M)
of

at

(see
[
Lee18

[

It follows immediately from the above definition that for

It is reasonable to expect

Another interesting feature of
\mathcal{DV}
is that it is path-connected. More precisely, we have the following result:

Lemma 1.4
.

The domain
\mathcal{DV}
is
geodesically star-shaped
with respect to
x_{0}
, meaning that for every
x\in\mathcal{DV}
and any minimal geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
from
x_{0}
to
x
, we have
\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{DV}
for every
t\in[0,1]
. In particular,
\mathcal{DV}
is path-connected.

Proof.

Let
t\in[0,1]
and
w\in W
. We write

\begin{array}[t]{ll}d(x_{0},w\gamma(t))&\begin{array}[t]{lr}=d(x_{0},w\gamma(t))+d(w\gamma(t),wx)-d(\gamma(t),x)&\text{($w$ is an isometry)}\\
\geq d(x_{0},wx)-d(\gamma(t),x)&\text{(triangular inequality)}\\
\geq d(x_{0},x)-d(\gamma(t),x)&\text{($x_{0}\in\mathcal{DV}$)}\\
=d(x_{0},\gamma(t))&\text{($\gamma$ is minimal)}\end{array}\\
\end{array}

and therefore we have
\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{DV}
, as required.
∎

Proposition 1.5
.

The Dirichlet–Voronoi domain
\mathcal{DV}
is a geodesically star-shaped fundamental domain for
W
acting on
M
.

Proof.

Obviously we have
M=\bigcup_{w\in W}w\mathcal{DV}
. On the other hand, if
x\in\mathcal{DV}\cap w\mathcal{DV}=Z_{w}
for some
w\in W\setminus\{1\}
and if
B=B(x,\delta)
is a small (geodesic) ball centered at
x
with radius
\delta>0
included in
Z_{w}
, then
B\subset H_{w}
. However, if we denote by
\gamma
a minimal geodesic from
x_{0}
to
x
parametrized by arc-length and if
\ell:=d(x_{0},x)=\leng(\gamma)
, then
d(\gamma(t),x)=\ell-t<\delta
for
t>\ell-\delta
and thus
\gamma(t)\in H_{w}
for
\ell-\delta<t\leq\ell
, contradicting Lemma
1.3
. Therefore,
Z_{w}
has empty interior.
∎

2.
Injectivity questions

We intend to use the domain
\mathcal{DV}
to build a
W
-equivariant CW structure on
M
. However, this is too much to ask in the general setting, as the
walls
of
\mathcal{DV}
, i.e. the walls
Z_{w}
containing a non-empty open subset of
H_{w}
, are not necessarily cells. For example, letting the cyclic group
C_{2}=\{1,s\}
act on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
via the antipode, we have that
H_{s}
is a circle. However, we see that if we take again a Dirichlet–Voronoi domain for the induced action of
C_{2}
on
H_{s}
, we finally obtain indeed a
C_{2}
-CW structure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. This gives a hope of a general method for constructing a
W
-equivariant cell structure on complex flag manifolds. However, for now, we stick to the case of real flag manifolds.

The first feature to ask is that the interior of
\mathcal{DV}
should itself be a cell and to ensure this, we have to control the size of
\mathcal{DV}
.

Before stating the result, we introduce some notation: for any
x\in B(x_{0},\inj_{x_{0}}(M))
we denote by
\gamma_{x}
the
unique
minimal geodesic from
x_{0}
to
x
, extended to
{\mathbb{R}}
by completeness of
M
. The geodesic
\gamma_{x}
is defined by
\gamma_{x}(s)=\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{0}}\!\left({su}/{\|u\|}\right)
for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
u:=\dot{\gamma_{x}}(0)\in T_{x_{0}}M
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If there is some
0<\rho<\inj_{x_{0}}(M)
such that
\mathcal{DV}\subset B(x_{0},\rho)
, then the open fundamental domain
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\mathcal{DV}}}
is a
\dim(M)
-cell.

Proof.

Let
\delta:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{DV}}d(x_{0},x)<\rho
. This maximum exists as
\mathcal{DV}
is closed in the compact subset
\overline{B(x_{0},\rho)}\simeq\overline{B_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\rho)}
of
M
. By the Lemma
1.3
, for
y\in B_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\delta)\setminus\{0\}
, there is a unique
0<\delta_{y}\leq\delta
such that
\gamma_{y}(\delta_{y})\in\partial\mathcal{DV}
and in fact we have
\delta_{y}=\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{Exp}(y),\partial\mathcal{DV})
. Thus, the element
\gamma_{y}({\|y\|\delta_{y}}/{\delta})
is in the interior of
\mathcal{DV}
and the assignment

\begin{array}[]{ccc}B_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\delta)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\Phi}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\mathcal{DV}}}\\
y&\longmapsto&\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{0}&\text{if }y=0,\\
\gamma_{y}({\|y\|\delta_{y}}/{\delta})&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.\end{array}

defines a continuous map. Conversely, if
x\in\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\mathcal{DV}}}\setminus\{x_{0}\}
, then there is a unique
0<\delta_{x}\leq\delta
such that
\gamma_{x}(\delta_{x})\in\partial\mathcal{DV}
and if
\ell_{x}:=d(x_{0},x)<\delta_{x}
, then the assignment

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\mathcal{DV}}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\Psi}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}&B_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\delta)\\

\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\gamma_{x}(\delta\ell_{x}/\delta_{x}))&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.\end{array}

is continuous and it is routine to check that it defines an inverse to
\Phi
.
∎

Another observation towards cell structures is the following: assume we can find some
0<\delta<\inj_{x_{0}}(M)
such that
\mathcal{DV}\subset\overline{B(x_{0},\delta)}
. Then, the exponential map
\mathrm{Exp}={\rm Exp}_{x_{0}}
realizes a homeomorphism
\overline{B(x_{0},\delta)}\simeq\overline{B_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\delta)}=\mathbb{B}^{N}
, where
\mathbb{B}^{N}
is the Euclidean

-ball, with

. Thus, we can project the “cells” onto the bounding sphere
\partial\overline{B(x_{0},\delta)}=S(x_{0},\delta)\simeq S_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\delta)
, just as in the case of binary polyhedral groups (see
[
CS17
]
and
[
CGS22
]
). More precisely, we consider the map

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\pi_{\delta}&:&\overline{B(x_{0},\delta)}\setminus\{x_{0}\}&\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow&S_{T_{x_{0}}M}(0,\delta)\simeq\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\\
&&x&\longmapsto&\delta\frac{\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(x)}{\|\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(x)\|}\end{array}

Geometrically, this can also be defined using geodesics: for
x\in\overline{B(x_{0},\delta)}
, as
d(x_{0},x)<\inj_{x_{0}}(M)
, there is a unique minimal geodesic
\gamma
from
x_{0}
to
x
, which we extend until it meets the sphere
S(x_{0},\delta)
and the preimage of this point under
\mathrm{Exp}
is
\pi_{\delta}(x)
. Now, if we have a wall
Z_{w}
and if

x_{0}
to
\mathrm{Exp}(y)

restricts to a homeomorphism

. We may glue these homeomorphisms together to obtain a homeomorphism

One should notice that the walls

are not connected in general: they are rather expected to be finite unions of cells. We shall see this when we investigate the case of

that for complex flag manifolds, the walls may not be unions of cells; thus our method is useful only for real flag manifolds.

Lemma 3.1
.

For

Remark 3.3
.

The first statement still holds if we replace “CW structure” by “stratification”. More precisely, if
\mathcal{DV}
admits a stratification such that for all
1\notin I\subset W
, the wall
Z_{I}
is a (possibly empty) disjoint union of closed
|I|
-codimensional strata, if
\sigma\subset Z_{I}

The following fact can be extracted from the end of the proof : if a discrete group acts freely on a regular CW complex, then the CW structure is equivariant if and only if the group acts on the cells. This is a consequence of Brouwer’s fixed point theorem.

Part II
The case of flag manifolds: injectivity radius and a new equivariant CW-structure on
O(3)/O(1)^{3}

In this second part, we aim to apply the above general results on Riemannian Dirichlet–Voronoi domains to the construction of equivariant cell structures on real flag manifolds, the acting group being the Weyl group
W
.

As we have seen, the injectivity radius plays an important role in this method. This is why the first section is devoted to the computation of this invariant for all complex and real flag manifolds, in terms of their root systems. As we will see, this allows to recover the Böttcher–Wenzel inequality
[
BW08
]
for skew-hermitian matrices and the Bloch–Iserles inequalities
[
BI05
]
for skew-symmetric (real) matrices.

Next, we focus on the particular case of the flag manifold
O(3)/O(1)^{3}
of
SL_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
, for which we provide a new
W
-equivariant cell structure, whose associated cellular chain complex is computed. However, the proof is based on the quaternionic description of
O(3)/O(1)^{3}
given in
[
CGS22
, §4.4]
and is still to be adapted to the higher cases.

4.
The injectivity radius of flag manifolds

4.1.
General setting and notation

Throughout, we let
K
be a connected semisimple compact Lie group, equipped with a maximal torus
T\leq K
and Weyl group
W=N_{K}(T)/T
. We also denote by
\Phi\subset\mathrm{Hom}\,(T,\mathbb{S}^{1})
the root system of
(K,T)
, with chosen positive (resp. simple) roots set
\Phi^{+}
(resp.
\Pi
). For a root
\alpha\in\mathrm{Hom}\,(T,\mathbb{S}^{1})
, we denote by
d\alpha:={\rm d}_{1}\alpha:\mathfrak{t}\rightarrow i{\mathbb{R}}
its differential; an element of
(i\mathfrak{t})^{*}
.

Let
G:=K^{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of
K
, with maximal torus
T^{\mathbb{C}}
. This is a connected, semisimple complex algebraic group, whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is the complexification of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}
of
K
and if
\mathfrak{t}
denotes the Lie algebra of
T
, then
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathfrak{t}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
is the Lie algebra of
T^{\mathbb{C}}
: a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. The
flag manifold

\mathcal{F}_{K}
is the homogeneous space
K/T
which, thanks to the Iwasawa decomposition, is diffeomorphic to
G/B
, where
B\leq G
is the Borel subgroup of
G
containing
T
, associated to
\Phi^{+}
. This allows to regard
\mathcal{F}_{K}
as a smooth complex projective variety.

Since the group
T
is abelian, the Weyl group
W
acts freely on the right of
\mathcal{F}_{K}
as follows: if
w\in W
is represented by
\dot{w}\in N_{K}(T)
and if
x\in\mathcal{F}_{K}
is represented by
g\in K
, then
x\cdot w
is defined as the class of
g\dot{w}
. It should be noted that the resulting action on the projective variety
G/B
is
not
algebraic.

On the other hand, the flag manifold
\mathcal{F}_{K}
can be canonically endowed with the structure of a Riemannian homogeneous space as follows: let
\kappa:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
. It is non-degenerate since
\mathfrak{g}
is (semi-)simple and its restriction to
\mathfrak{k}
is negative-definite, as
K
is compact
[
Bes87
, Lemma 7.36]
. Hence, the form
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>:=-\kappa
is an inner product on
\mathfrak{k}=T_{1}K
, inducing a bi-invariant Riemannian metric
\widetilde{g}_{\kappa}
on
K
. Since it is bi-invariant, the metric
\widetilde{g}_{\kappa}
descends to a
K
-invariant Riemannian metric
g_{\kappa}
on
\mathcal{F}_{K}=K/T
, called the
standard normal homogeneous
metric. In particular, the free (right) action of the Weyl group
W
on
\mathcal{F}_{K}
is isometric. Moreover, by
[
YWL22
, Theorem 2.5]
, the geodesics of
(\mathcal{F}_{K},g_{K})
are orbits of one-parameter subgroups of
K
.

Observe moreover that if
K
is simple and if
K_{\rm sc}
(resp.
K_{\rm ad}
) denotes the simply-connected (resp. adjoint) group of the same type as
K
, then there are isogenies
K_{\rm sc}\twoheadrightarrow K\twoheadrightarrow K_{\rm ad}
whose differentials are the identity on
\mathfrak{k}
: an isometry. Moreover, these isogenies induce isomorphisms of flag manifolds
K_{\rm sc}/T_{\rm sc}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\sim}}}{{\rightarrow}}K/T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\sim}}}{{\rightarrow}}K_{\rm ad}/T_{\rm ad}
that are local isometries. Therefore, these are
W
-equivariant isometries and we may talk about
the
(standard homogeneous) flag manifold
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}:=\mathcal{F}_{K}
, associated to the root system
\Phi
of
K
.

Recall the root space decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\penalty\ |\penalty\ [h,x]=d\alpha(h)x,\penalty\ \forall h\in\mathfrak{h}\}
. Consider the Chevalley–Serre generators
(e_{\alpha},f_{\alpha},h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}
of
\mathfrak{g}
. Then for
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
, we have
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}={\mathbb{C}}e_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}={\mathbb{C}}f_{\alpha}
and the root decomposition reads

\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Pi}{\mathbb{C}}h_{\alpha}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}({\mathbb{C}}e_{\alpha}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}f_{\alpha}).

Now, for
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
, we let

u_{\alpha}:=\frac{e_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}}{2},\penalty\ v_{\alpha}:=\frac{i(e_{\alpha}+f_{\alpha})}{2},\penalty\ w_{\alpha}:=\frac{ih_{\alpha}}{2}.

Then, the collection
(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},w_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}
generates
\mathfrak{k}
, in the sense that

\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{t}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}({\mathbb{R}}u_{\alpha}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha})=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Pi}{\mathbb{R}}w_{\alpha}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}({\mathbb{R}}u_{\alpha}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha}).

We have the
quaternionic

\forall\alpha\in\Phi^{+},\penalty\ [u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha}]=w_{\alpha},\penalty\ [v_{\alpha},w_{\alpha}]=u_{\alpha}\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ [w_{\alpha},u_{\alpha}]=v_{\alpha},

that is, the triple
(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},w_{\alpha})
generates a subalgebra of

isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
. It may also be observed that the family
\{u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}
is orthogonal (for the product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>=-\kappa
) and that this family is itself orthogonal to each

(although the family
\{w_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Pi}
is not orthogonal in general). In particular, the orthogonal of the toral subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
is the subspace
\mathfrak{p}:=\bigoplus^{\perp}_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}({\mathbb{R}}u_{\alpha}\oplus^{\perp}{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha})
. Moreover, if
t_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{h}

, that is,
\kappa(t_{\alpha},-)=d\alpha
(such an element exists, by non-degeneracy of
\kappa
), then we have

\left<u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha}\right>=-\kappa(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha})=\frac{-\kappa(e_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha},e_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha})}{4}=\frac{\kappa(e_{\alpha},f_{\alpha})}{2}=\frac{1}{\kappa(t_{\alpha},t_{\alpha})}=\frac{1}{\|\alpha\|^{2}},

with
\|\alpha\|:=\kappa(t_{\alpha},t_{\alpha})=d\alpha(t_{\alpha})>0
. In the same fashion, we see that
\|v_{\alpha}\|=\|w_{\alpha}\|=1/\|\alpha\|=\|u_{\alpha}\|
. These considerations yield an explicit formula for the norm on
\mathfrak{k}
: writing an element
X\in\mathfrak{k}
as
X=X^{\mathfrak{t}}+X^{\mathfrak{p}}
, where
X^{\mathfrak{p}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\lambda_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}+\mu_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}
(for
\lambda_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}
), we have

\|X\|^{2}=\|X^{\mathfrak{t}}\|^{2}+\|X^{\mathfrak{p}}\|^{2}=\|X^{\mathfrak{t}}\|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{\|\alpha\|^{2}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi}|d\alpha(X^{\mathfrak{t}})|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{\|\alpha\|^{2}}

so that we obtain

(N)

\|X\|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\left(2|d\alpha(X^{\mathfrak{t}})|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{\|\alpha\|^{2}}\right).

This will be useful when we estimate the norm of the map
\mathfrak{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{{\rm ad}}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{k})
(where
\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{k})
is equipped with the operator norm
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ \mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}
associated to the norm
\|\cdot\|=\sqrt{-\kappa}
on
\mathfrak{k}
).

4.2.
Lemmata and main result

From now on, we assume that the root system
\Phi
is irreducible.

Recall that the (dual) Coxeter number
h
(resp.
h^{\vee}
) is defined as
h=1+\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}n_{\alpha}
(resp.
h^{\vee}=1+\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}n_{\alpha}^{\vee}
), where
\alpha_{0}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}n_{\alpha}\alpha
(resp.
\alpha_{0}^{\vee}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}n_{\alpha}^{\vee}\alpha^{\vee}
) is the highest long root (resp. highest short coroot) of
\Phi
. Notice that
\alpha_{0}
is long, while
\alpha_{0}^{\vee}
is short. If
\rho:=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\alpha
(resp.
\rho^{\vee}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\alpha^{\vee}
) denotes the Weyl vector, i.e. half the sum of the positive (co)roots, then we have
h=1+\left<\rho^{\vee},\alpha_{0}\right>
and
h^{\vee}=1+\left<\alpha_{0}^{\vee},\rho\right>
. The following fact is standard, but it will be useful for our analysis. We include an independent proof for completeness.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
Sut98
, Lemma 4]
)
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is simple, the dual Coxeter number
h^{\vee}=1+\left<\alpha_{0}^{\vee},\rho\right>
satisfies

h^{\vee}=\frac{1}{\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Pi}
be the standard basis of
\mathfrak{t}
and denote by
(h_{\alpha}^{\perp})_{\alpha}
its dual basis, with respect to the Killing form
\kappa
. For each
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
, consider also the root vectors
e_{\alpha}
and
f_{\alpha}
and let
e_{-\alpha}:=\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{2}f_{\alpha}
(so that
\kappa(e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha})=1
). We introduce the
Casimir element
for the adjoint representation:

c:=c_{\rm ad}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}{\rm ad}(h_{\alpha}){\rm ad}(h_{\alpha}^{\perp})+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}{\rm ad}(e_{\alpha}){\rm ad}(e_{-\alpha})+{\rm ad}(e_{-\alpha}){\rm ad}(e_{\alpha})\in\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}).

Observe that if
\alpha,\beta\in\Pi
, then we have

\sum_{\delta\in\Pi}\alpha(h_{\delta})\beta(h_{\delta}^{\perp})=\sum_{\delta\in\Pi}\frac{\|\beta\|^{2}}{2}\left<\delta^{\vee},\alpha\right>\left<h_{\beta},h_{\delta}^{\perp}\right>=\frac{\|\beta\|^{2}}{2}\left<\beta^{\vee},\alpha\right>=\alpha(t_{\beta})=\kappa(\alpha,\beta)

and thus,

\forall U,V\in\mathfrak{t},\penalty\ \left<U,V\right>=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}U(h_{\alpha})V(h_{\alpha}^{\perp}).

Now, since
e_{\alpha_{0}}
is a highest weight vector for the adjoint representation, we have
{\rm ad}(e_{\alpha})(e_{\alpha_{0}})=[e_{\alpha},e_{\alpha_{0}}]=0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
. Since
c={\rm id}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, we find

\displaystyle e_{\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle=c(e_{\alpha_{0}})=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}{\rm ad}(h_{\alpha}){\rm ad}(h_{\alpha}^{\perp})(e_{\alpha_{0}})+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}{\rm ad}(e_{\alpha}){\rm ad}(e_{-\alpha})(e_{\alpha_{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}\alpha_{0}(h_{\alpha}^{\perp}){\rm ad}(h_{\alpha})(e_{\alpha_{0}})+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}{\rm ad}[e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha}](e_{\alpha_{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Pi}\alpha_{0}(h_{\alpha})\alpha_{0}(h_{\alpha}^{\perp})e_{\alpha_{0}}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{2}[h_{\alpha},e_{\alpha_{0}}]=\left<\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}\right>e_{\alpha_{0}}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{2}\alpha_{0}(h_{\alpha})e_{\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left(\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{2}\left<\alpha^{\vee},\alpha_{0}\right>\right)e_{\alpha_{0}}=\left(\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\left<\alpha,\alpha_{0}\right>\right)e_{\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left<\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0}+2\rho\right>e_{\alpha_{0}}

and thus,

1=\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}\left(1+\left<\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}},\rho\right>\right)=\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}(1+\left<\alpha_{0}^{\vee},\rho\right>)=\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}h^{\vee},

as required. ∎

We start with the following key lemma on the minimal length a closed geodesic in
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
can have.

Lemma 4.2
.

The minimal length of a non-trivial closed geodesic in
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
is
2\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}
.

Proof.

As mentioned in the Section
4.1
, we may assume that
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}=K/T
, where
K=K_{\rm sc}
is the simply-connected (simple) compact Lie group with root system
\Phi
. A closed geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{K}
sends
s\in[0,1]
to
\gamma(s)=e^{sX}\cdot T
, for some
X\in\mathfrak{k}
with
\|X\|=\leng(\gamma)
. Saying that
\gamma
is non-trivial amounts to say that
X\notin\mathfrak{t}
and since
\gamma
is closed, we have
e^{X}\in T
. We first prove that
\|X\|\geq 2\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}
.

Choose
g\in K
such that
{\rm Ad}_{g}(X)=:Z\in\mathfrak{t}
and assume for contradiction that
2\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}=2\pi/\|\alpha_{0}\|>\|X\|=\|Z\|
. We claim the following equality of centralizers:

(
\mathcal{C}
)

C_{G}(e^{Z})=C_{G}(Z).

The inclusion
C_{G}(Z)\leq C_{G}(e^{Z})
is trivial. Since
K
is simply-connected,
G=K^{\mathbb{C}}
is simply-connected as well, so that by
[
Hum95
, Theorem 2.11]
, the centralizer
C_{G}(e^{Z})=C_{G}(e^{Z})^{o}
is connected and using
[
Hum95
, Theorem 2.2]
, it is generated by
T^{\mathbb{C}}
and those root subgroups
U_{\alpha}
(
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
) for which
\alpha(e^{Z})=1
. Thus, it suffices to prove that
U_{\alpha}\leq C_{G}(Z)
if
\alpha(e^{Z})=1
. Take
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
such that
1=\alpha(e^{Z})=e^{d\alpha(Z)}
. There is some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
d\alpha(Z)=2ik\pi
and by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we arrive at

2|k|\pi=|d\alpha(Z)|=\left|\left<t_{\alpha},Z\right>\right|\leq\|t_{\alpha}\|\|Z\|=\|\alpha\|\|Z\|\leq\|\alpha_{0}\|\|Z\|<2\pi,

so that
k=0
and
d\alpha(Z)=0
. This ensures that
[Z,X_{\alpha}]=0
for each
X_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
so that
U_{\alpha}
centralizes
Z
, as claimed. Now, because the elements
e^{X}
and
e^{Z}
lie in
T
and are
G
-conjugate, we may apply
[
Ada69
, Lemma 4.33]
so find some
\dot{w}\in N_{G}(T^{\mathbb{C}})
such that
e^{X}={\rm Ad}_{g^{-1}}(e^{Z})={\rm Ad}_{\dot{w}}(e^{Z})
. This yields
g\dot{w}\in C_{G}(e^{Z})=C_{G}(Z)
and thus,
X={\rm Ad}_{g^{-1}}(Z)={\rm Ad}_{\dot{w}}(Z)\in\mathfrak{t}
, an absurdity.

Conversely, we find some
X\in\mathfrak{k}\setminus\mathfrak{t}
with norm
\|X\|=2\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}
and such that
e^{X}\in T
. To the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple
(e_{\alpha_{0}},f_{\alpha_{0}},h_{\alpha_{0}})
corresponds an inclusion
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbb{C}})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
, which is the differential of a map
\phi_{0}:SL_{2}({\mathbb{C}})\hookrightarrow G
(restricting to an inclusion
\widetilde{\phi}_{0}:SU(2)\hookrightarrow K
). Then, the reflection
s_{\alpha_{0}}\in W
may be represented by the element
\dot{s}_{\alpha_{0}}=\phi_{0}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and we have

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}=\phi_{0}\left(\exp\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\pi}{2}d\phi_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{\pi(e_{\alpha_{0}}-f_{\alpha_{0}})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(\pi u_{\alpha_{0}})

so that if we let
X_{0}:=2\pi u_{\alpha_{0}}\notin\mathfrak{t}
, then
e^{X_{0}}=\dot{s}^{2}_{\alpha_{0}}\in T
and
\|X_{0}\|=2\pi\|u_{\alpha_{0}}\|=\frac{2\pi}{\|\alpha_{0}\|}
.∎

We now give a sharp estimate on the sectional curvature of flag manifolds:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be the sectional curvature of
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
. For any plane
P\leq T_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
, we have

0\leq\mathcal{K}(P)\leq\frac{1}{h^{\vee}}.

Moreover, this estimate is sharp: if
P_{0}:={\mathbb{R}}u_{\alpha_{0}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha_{0}}
, then
\mathcal{K}(P_{0})=1/h^{\vee}
.

Proof.

Let
(X,Y)
be an orthonormal basis of
P\leq T_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}=\mathfrak{p}
. By the O’Neill formula
[
GHL04
, Theorems 3.61 & 3.65]
, we have

0\leq\mathcal{K}(P)=\mathcal{K}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{4}\|[X,Y]^{\mathfrak{p}}\|^{2}+\|[X,Y]^{\mathfrak{t}}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\|[X,Y]\|^{2}+\frac{3}{4}\|[X,Y]^{\mathfrak{t}}\|^{2}\leq\|[X,Y]\|^{2}.

Since
{\rm Ad}_{k}\in\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{k})
is an isometry for all
k\in K
, to estimate the squared norm
\|[X,Y]\|^{2}
, we may assume that
Y=:H\in\mathfrak{t}
, in which case
X
can be any element in the unit sphere of
\mathfrak{k}
. But writing
X=X^{\mathfrak{t}}+X^{\mathfrak{p}}
, we have
[X,H]=[X^{\mathfrak{p}},H]
, so that we may also assume that
X=X^{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathfrak{p}
, in which case
[X,H]\in\mathfrak{p}
.

Therefore, it suffices to estimate the squared norm
\|[H,X]\|^{2}
for
H\in\mathfrak{t}
in the unit sphere and
X\in\mathfrak{p}
in the (closed) unit ball. Following the Section
4.1
, write
X=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\lambda_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}+\mu_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}
(where
\lambda_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}
). From the formula (
N
), we have
\|X\|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{\|\alpha\|^{2}}\leq 1
. It is immediate to check that, for every
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
, we have
[H,u_{\alpha}]=-id\alpha(H)v_{\alpha}
and
[H,u_{\alpha}]=id\alpha(Z)u_{\alpha}
, so that

[H,X]=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\lambda_{\alpha}[H,u_{\alpha}]+\mu_{\alpha}[H,v_{\alpha}]=i\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}d\alpha(H)(\mu_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}-\lambda_{\alpha}v_{\alpha})

and thus, using (
N
) again,

\displaystyle\|[H,X]\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}|d\alpha(H)|^{2}\frac{\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}^{2}}{\|\alpha\|^{2}}\leq\max_{\alpha\in\Phi}|d\alpha(H)|^{2}\sum_{\beta\in\Phi^{+}}\frac{\lambda_{\beta}^{2}+\mu_{\beta}^{2}}{\|\beta\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}|d\alpha(H)|^{2}\leq\|H\|^{2}\max_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\|\alpha\|^{2}=\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={1}/{h^{\vee}}.

Conversely, the family
(\|\alpha_{0}\|u_{\alpha_{0}},\|\alpha_{0}\|v_{\alpha_{0}})
is an orthonormal basis of
P_{0}
and we have

\mathcal{K}(P_{0})=\frac{\|\alpha_{0}\|^{4}}{4}\|\underbrace{[u_{\alpha_{0}},v_{\alpha_{0}}]^{\mathfrak{p}}}_{=0}\|^{2}+\|\alpha_{0}\|^{4}\|[u_{\alpha_{0}},v_{\alpha_{0}}]^{\mathfrak{t}}\|^{2}=\|\alpha_{0}\|^{4}\|w_{\alpha_{0}}\|^{2}=\|\alpha_{0}\|^{2}=1/h^{\vee}.

∎

Now, the Klingenberg Lemma
[
Car92
, Chapter 13, Proposition 2.13]
(see also
[
Kli82
, Proposition 2.6.8]
) tells us that, if
\ell=2\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}
denotes the minimal length of a closed geodesic in
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
(see the Lemma
4.2
), then we have

\inj(\mathcal{F}_{\Phi})\geq\min\left\{\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\sup_{P}\mathcal{K}(P)}},\frac{\ell}{2}\right\}=\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}.

Since the reverse inequality
\inj(\mathcal{F}_{\Phi})\leq\ell/2=\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}}
is obvious, we arrive at the main result:

Theorem 4.4
.

The injectivity radius of a standard homogeneous flag manifold
\mathcal{F}_{\Phi}
is given by

\inj(\mathcal{F}_{\Phi})=\pi\sqrt{h^{\vee}},

where
h^{\vee}
is the dual Coxeter number of the (simple) root system
\Phi
. Moreover, this is the distance between 1 and any reflection of
W
, whose associated root is long.

Remark 4.5
.

We make the following observations:

•

In the proof of the Lemma
4.3
, we have seen that
\|[X,Y]\|^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{h^{\vee}}\|X\|^{2}\|Y\|^{2}
for any two vectors
X,Y\in\mathfrak{k}
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the Killing norm. In the case where
\mathfrak{g}
is classical, we may interpret this in terms of the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\|\cdot\|
by writing that

\|[X,Y]\|_{F}\leq\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{h^{\vee}}}\|X\|_{F}\|Y\|_{F}.

The value of
\lambda
, depending on the type of the classical algebra, can be found in
[
EW06
, §12.6]
. For instance, if
\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{su}(n)
, then
\lambda=\sqrt{2(n+1)}
and we obtain that

\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{su}(n),\penalty\ \|[X,Y]\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}\|X\|_{F}\|Y\|_{F}.

This is precisely the Böttcher–Wenzel inequality
[
BW08
, Theorem 2.2]
for
\mathfrak{su}(n)
. This inequality turns out to hold on the bigger algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and is sharp. In type
C_{n}
, we have
\lambda=\sqrt{2(n+1)}
, so that we retrieve the same Böttcher–Wenzel inequality for
\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{sp}(n)=\mathfrak{sp}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})\cap\mathfrak{u}(2n)
.

In types
B
and
D
, we have
\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{so}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\lambda^{2}=2\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor+\frac{(-1)^{n}-1}{2}=h^{\vee}
, so that we obtain the following improvement of the BW inequality:

\forall n\geq 5,\penalty\ \sup_{X,Y\in\mathfrak{so}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|[X,Y]\|_{F}}{\|X\|_{F}\|Y\|_{F}}=1.

Moreover, as
\mathfrak{so}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
, we have
\sup_{0\neq X,Y\in\mathfrak{so}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})}\frac{\|[X,Y]\|_{F}}{\|X\|_{F}\|Y\|_{F}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Indeed, the map
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&x&z\\
-x&0&y\\
-z&-y&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapstochar\rightarrow\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-iz&-ix-y\\
-ix+y&iz\end{smallmatrix}\right)
defines an isometry
(\mathfrak{so}_{3}({\mathbb{R}}),\kappa)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\sim}}}{{\rightarrow}}(\mathfrak{su}(2),\kappa)
of Lie algebras and thus

\hskip 38.41121pt\sup_{0\neq X,Y\in\mathfrak{so}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})}\frac{\|[X,Y]\|_{F}}{\|X\|_{F}\|Y\|_{F}}=\sup_{0\neq X,Y\in\mathfrak{so}_{3}({\mathbb{R}})}\frac{\|[X,Y]\|_{\kappa}}{\|X\|_{\kappa}\|Y\|_{\kappa}}=\sup_{0\neq X,Y\in\mathfrak{su}(2)}\frac{\|[X,Y]\|_{\kappa}}{\|X\|_{\kappa}\|Y\|_{\kappa}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

These results were first derived in
[
BI05
, Theorem 6]
, with totally different methods. However, it should be noted that the authors also establish the displayed formula for
n=4
, which is unreachable by our method, as the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{4}({\mathbb{R}})\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)
is not simple.

•

5.
The case of
\mathcal{F}_{A_{2}}({\mathbb{R}})=O(3)/O(1)^{3}

Let

(resp. to

The Weyl group

, described at the beginning of §

. Viewing

Remark 5.1
.

There is another natural metric on

\iota:\mathcal{F}_{n}\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow({\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{n-1})^{n}

To work the case of

Remark 5.10
.

The homeomorphism
\mathcal{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\sim}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{DV}_{3}
crucially depends on the quaternion covering
\mathbb{S}^{3}\twoheadrightarrow SO(3)
. However, the inverse image
\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\mathcal{DV}_{3})\subset\mathfrak{so}(3)
has a convex hull

\mathcal{K}_{\mathfrak{so}}:=\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\mathcal{DV}_{3})),

which is combinatorially equivalent to
\mathcal{K}
. Moreover, each inverse
I
-wall
\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(Z_{I})
projects onto a union of faces of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathfrak{so}}
, corresponding to the faces appearing in
\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(Z_{I})
. Therefore, the combinatorial information carried by
\mathcal{K}
can be seen intrinsically in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(3)
.

To see this, recall the covering
\mu:\mathbb{S}^{3}\twoheadrightarrow SO(3)
and take a quaternion
q=a_{0}+bi+cj+dk
such that
a_{0}:=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\geq|b|,|c|,|d|
(so that
\Pi(q)\in S(1,\delta_{0})
). We have
\mathrm{tr}\,(\mu(q))=4a^{2}-1=\tfrac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2}
and if
\theta_{0}:=\arccos\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{tr}\,(\mu(q))-1}{2}\right)=\arccos\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\in]\tfrac{\mu}{4},\tfrac{\mu}{2}[
, then we have, using the Rodrigues formula
[
CL10
, §2]
,

\log(\mu(q))=\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\sin\theta_{0}}\left(\mu(q)-{}^{t}{\mu(q)}\right)=\frac{\theta_{0}}{2\sin\theta_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-4a_{0}d&4a_{0}c\\
4a_{0}d&0&-4a_{0}b\\
-4a_{0}c&4a_{0}b&0\end{pmatrix}=k_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&-d&c\\
d&0&-b\\
-c&b&0\end{pmatrix},

where
k_{0}:=\frac{4a_{0}\theta_{0}}{2\sin\theta_{0}}=\frac{2(2+\sqrt{2})\arccos(\sqrt{2}/2-1/4)}{\sqrt{7+4\sqrt{2}}}>0
. Observe also that if
(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{K}
is a vertex of
\mathcal{K}
, then
(x,y,z)
belongs to the sphere
\mathcal{S}_{0}
centered at the origin and with radius
\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{8}(5-2\sqrt{2})}
, so
\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(x,y,z)=\Pi(a_{0}+xi+yj+zk)
and thus

\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(x,y,z))=\log(\mu(a_{0}+xi+yj+zk))=k_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&-z&y\\
z&0&-x\\
-y&x&0\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, if
V
denotes the set of vertices of
\mathcal{K}
, then the assignment

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{K}&\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow&\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(V)))\subset\mathfrak{so}(3)\\
\sum_{p}\lambda_{p}p&\longmapsto&\sum_{p}\lambda_{p}\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(p))\end{array}

is an isomorphism of polytopes (where
\sum_{p}\lambda_{p}p
denotes any convex combination of the vertices
p\in V
). On the other hand, each radial segment joining
0\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
to a point on the sphere
S_{\mathfrak{so}(3)}(0,\delta_{0})
intersects exactly one inverse cell
\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(e)\subset\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(Z_{I})\subset\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\partial\mathcal{DV}_{3})
at exactly one point. Furthermore, this segment also intersects exactly one boundary face of
\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(V)))
at exactly one point. This leads to a second isomorphism of polytopes

\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\varphi_{\mathcal{K}}(V)))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\sim}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}(\mathcal{DV}_{3}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\text{df}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{K}_{\mathfrak{so}},

finally yielding an isomorphism of polytopes

\mathcal{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{\sim}}}{{\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}}\mathcal{K}_{\mathfrak{so}}.
