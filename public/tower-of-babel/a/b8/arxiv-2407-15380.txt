Iterative Approach to Reconstructing Neural Disparity Fields from Light-field Data
Ligen Shi, Chang Liu, Xing Zhao*, Jun Qiu*

Abstract
This study proposes a neural disparity field (NDF) that establishes an implicit, continuous representation of scene disparity based on a neural field and an iterative approach to address the inverse problem of NDF reconstruction from light-field data. NDF enables seamless and precise characterization of disparity variations in three-dimensional scenes and can discretize disparity at any arbitrary resolution, overcoming the limitations of traditional disparity maps that are prone to sampling errors and interpolation inaccuracies. The proposed NDF network architecture utilizes hash encoding combined with multilayer perceptrons to capture detailed disparities in texture levels, thereby enhancing its ability to represent the geometric information of complex scenes. By leveraging the spatial-angular consistency inherent in light- field data, a differentiable forward model to generate a central view image from the light-field data is developed. Based on the forward model, an optimization scheme for the inverse problem of NDF reconstruction using differentiable propagation operators is established. Furthermore, an iterative solution method is adopted to reconstruct the NDF in the optimization scheme, which does not require training datasets and applies to light-field data captured by various acquisition methods. Experimental results demonstrate that high-quality NDF can be reconstructed from light-field data using the proposed method. High-resolution disparity can be effectively recovered by NDF, demonstrating its capability for the implicit, continuous representation of scene disparities.



I
Introduction

Computational imaging innovatively incorporates computer vision and signal processing techniques into imaging systems to reconstruct high-dimensional optical signals that carry scene information through novel imaging mechanisms, optical paths, and reconstruction methods, thereby achieving efficient and high-precision observation of scenes
[
1
]
. Computational imaging surpasses the limitations of classical imaging in terms of signal dimensions, scale, and resolution.

From the perspective of imaging methodology, light-field imaging operates as a close-proximity passive direct line-of-sight imaging technology that relies on ambient light to capture scene content within its visual ambit. The characteristic features included narrower baselines, increased uniformity in viewpoint sampling, and diminished occlusions. Notably, substantial domain discrepancies emerge in the light-field data generated by different cameras owing to inherent variations in their imaging modalities, structural designs, and internal parameters.

Deep-learning-based reconstruction methods rely on labeled training data and lack exploration of the inherent consistency within light-field data. Consequently, networks trained using data from one camera model may not be generalizable to other camera models. Given that individual light-field data contain abundant information, exploiting the full potential of their spatial-angular consistency empowers a network to reduce its reliance on specific camera models, thereby promoting generalization across different camera models.

The neural field
[
2
]
represents the scene to be reconstructed as a continuous implicit function, achieving advanced visual quality by the neural network parameterization. The continuous implicit function representation ensures continuity and is not limited by resolution constraints. By employing neural network parameterization of signal values, this representation method adopts a ”discrete-to-continuous” computational approach and utilizes differentiability for solving inverse problems. Compared with traditional methods, the neural field exhibits superior precision, lower complexity, and increased robustness. Furthermore, compared with deep learning approaches, their advantages include not requiring extensive training datasets, being unrestricted by data formats, having fewer parameters, and exhibiting high robustness.

Therefore, we utilize a fully connected neural network to parameterize the disparity. The network constructs a neural disparity field (NDF) by incorporating hash encoding and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with activation functions. This approach implicitly represents the disparity contained in the light field and exploits the spatial-angular consistency within it. The inverse problem-solving model based on the NDF was established to reconstruct the disparity from the light field. This model was trained and iteratively solved through differentiability, effectively transforming the disparity estimation problem into an optimization problem for the NDF. By exploiting the spatial-angular consistency within individual light-field data, the proposed method demonstrates exceptional performance in reconstructing the NDF. This reconstructed field can restore high-quality explicit discrete disparity maps. Unlike depth-learning-based methods for disparity estimation, the NDF reconstruction method does not depend on training datasets. It can be applied to two-plane parameterized light-field data using any collection method. By utilizing the implicit representation of the neural field, this disparity reconstruction method departs from the traditional approach of representing disparity as discrete points, overcoming the inevitable sampling, interpolation, and other discretization calculation issues of explicit discrete disparity and establishing a continuous disparity surface with implicit expression. The innovations of this study are as follows:

1.

Disparity is parameterized using neural networks to establish an NDF, that creates an implicit continuous representation of scene disparity functions. This NDF enhances the expressive capability of complex details of the disparity surface and effectively reduces computational errors introduced by discretization. Unlike traditional hierarchical disparity search estimation methods, a representation based on the NDF allows the formation of a differentiable propagation forward problem model, inverse problem optimization modeling, and iterative solving of the reconstruction algorithm for continuous implicit disparity functions.

2.

From the perspective of spatial-angular consistency, a forward problem model for the disparity propagation of the NDF under non-ideal scene conditions (with occlusion and noise) is constructed. This forward-problem model, described by differentiable propagation operators, can propagate any viewpoint image to a central view via the NDF.

3.

Based on the forward problem model, an optimization model for the inverse problem of the NDF was established using differentiable propagation operators. An iterative approach was proposed to solve the disparity reconstruction from the light-field. This method offers a novel solution to the disparity reconstruction problem. The iterative reconstruction method based on the inverse problem does not rely on training datasets and can be applied to two-plane parameterized light-field data obtained by any collection method. Furthermore, it allows the application of inverse problem-solving strategies in disparity reconstruction.

Existing methods can be broadly classified into two categories: rule-based traditional methods and deep-learning-based methods.

II-A

Rule-based traditional methods

Disparity estimation algorithms that are rule-based traditional methods adhere to a computational approach known as ”discrete-to-discrete”. They aimed to estimate the corresponding discrete disparity images from discretized light-field data. However, this computational approach is influenced by the resolution limitations of disparity and unavoidably introduces various errors. A common source of error is sub-pixel interpolation, given that the accuracy of interpolation influences the estimation precision of the algorithm. For instance, Jeon et al.
[
6
]
proposed the use of the Fourier phase-shifting theory to replace bilinear interpolation, resulting in improved estimation accuracy. Currently, researchers have not noted advancements in the mitigation of discretization errors.

Early disparity estimation followed rule-based estimation methods, exploring the structural information of light-field data to assess the consistency among different views. Wanner et al.
[
7
]
introduced structural tensors into depth estimation from light-field data, enhancing the global consistency of the reconstructed depth by constraining the continuity of depth from different subview images of the light field. However, owing to the insufficient utilization of angular information in light-field data, the estimation performance of this algorithm requires further improvement. Heber et al.
[
8
]
introduced principal component analysis for the computation of stereo matching. They focused on subview images that impacted matching errors while disregarding those with minor influences. This approach improves the robustness of the algorithm and to some extent, its precision for stereo matching. To overcome the challenges of matching small baselines between subview images in light fields, Jeon et al.
[
6
]
applied Fourier phase-shifting theory to improve the accuracy of the matching computation. They adaptively aggregated the gradient costs based on the angular coordinates of the light fields and incorporated feature correspondences between subaperture images as additional constraints. Tao et al.
[
9
]
examined the properties of two depth cues derived from the correspondence and defocus in light-field data. Their findings demonstrate that these cues yield complementary depth information. Consequently, researchers have suggested merging the two depth cues to incorporate both angular and spatial information from the light field, thereby enhancing the performance of the algorithm for disparity estimation. However, this algorithm requires manual parameter adjustment to blend different results, thereby affecting its robustness across different scenes. In addition, it does not account for occlusion effects, leading to estimation errors in occluded regions.

To mitigate the errors arising from occlusions, Wang et al.
[
10
]
expanded the work of
[
9
]
by developing a model for the occlusion effect in light fields. They proposed an anti-occlusion algorithm by studying the relative positional relationships between different subview images in a light field and incorporating prior knowledge of object occlusion into stereo-matching algorithms. Although this algorithm partially addresses the challenges associated with occlusion in depth estimation by modeling occlusion effects in light fields, its straightforward occlusion detection criteria result in limited robustness across different scenes. To further mitigate the impact of occlusion on depth estimation performance, Sheng et al.
[
11
]
devised an estimation algorithm based on epipolar plane images (EPIs) combined with the local consistency of scene depth to address occlusion issues. Zhang et al.
[
12
]
considered the structure of EPIs and proposed a local depth estimation method based on a spinning parallelogram operator(SPO). Utilizing the regions segmented by the SPO in EPIs, they located lines representing depth information by maximizing the distribution distances between areas. Liu et al.
[
13
]
proposed an iterative scheme for scene depth reconstruction based on a 4D light field. The objective function of their optimization problem consisted of a fidelity term representing matching, along with penalty terms comprising gradient and classification terms. It iteratively corrects mismatched pixels to minimize the objective function, ultimately achieving accurate depth estimation. Subsequently, Liu et al.
[
14
]
mapped the pixel differences between matching regions to radial basis functions, where pixels with larger differences in occluded and noisy areas were mapped to smaller values in the radial basis functions, thereby effectively achieving anti-occlusion resistance and antinoise.

Although rule-based traditional disparity estimation algorithms achieve relatively high precision in depth estimation by exploiting the interrelationships among sub-aperture images, optimizing stereo matching computations, and addressing occlusion effects, their performance is still limited owing to the insufficient exploitation of angular and spatial information within the light field. Additionally, these methods, which use a ”discrete-to-discrete” approach, fail to overcome the resolution limitations inherent in the current representation of disparity and remain vulnerable to errors caused by data discretization. By contrast, neural fields with implicit representations, characterized by their inherent continuity, overcome the limitations imposed by finite-resolution discrete disparities and offer enhanced adaptability to complex variations in disparity and precision in their depiction.

II-B

Deep learning-based methods

With the advancements made in deep learning techniques for diverse computer vision tasks, there has been increasing interest in depth estimation from light-field data. Deep-learning-based algorithms primarily depend on supervised learning approaches, which require a large amount of training data annotated with accurate depth labels.

Heber et al.
[
15
]
introduced convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to address the task of depth estimation from light field data. Their approach utilizes supervised learning by providing a network with subaperture images in the horizontal and vertical directions and the corresponding depth labels. Data-augmentation techniques involving rotation, translation, brightness, and color adjustments have been proposed to improve the training process. Although the algorithm demonstrated superior performance on synthetic datasets compared to traditional algorithms, it required a large amount of depth-labeled training data, resulting in substantial data generation costs. In additionally, the domain gap between synthetic and real data poses challenges to the applicability of the algorithm in practical imaging systems. Subsequently, Shin et al.
[
16

proposed a multi-branch network structure with four branches dedicated to extracting features from light-field images in horizontal, vertical, and diagonally tilted directions. These features were then combined using CNNs to reconstruct the scene depth. Although the method showed improved estimation performance on synthetic data compared with traditional disparity estimation algorithms, it exhibited limitations in occluded and specular regions.

Alperovich et al.

developed a fully convolutional autoencoder for simultaneous depth and reflectance estimations. Although this supervised approach partially addresses the occlusion effects, its generalization ability is limited. Peng et al.
[
18

introduced unsupervised learning for depth estimation using light-field data. They proposed a combined loss function that imposed compliance and divergence constraints on the disparity between the warped sub-aperture images and the central view. This approach alleviates the challenge of acquiring labeled data and improves the generalizability of the algorithm across different imaging systems. Zhou et al.
[
19

utilized geometrically constrained EPIs to train networks and reconstruct depth information without requiring ground-truth depth labels. Li et al.
[
20

introduced an Oriented Relation Module that captured spatial relationships in vertical and horizontal EPIs. They enhanced training effectiveness and depth-estimation performance using refocusing-based data augmentation. Tsai et al.
[
21

presented a viewpoint-selection module that generated attention maps to determine the importance of each view for accurate depth estimation. This attention-driven approach improves the effective utilization of light field data. Li et al.
[
22

developed a lightweight model to estimate the disparity. The model employed physics-based multiscale cost-volume convolutional aggregation and an edge-guided subnetwork to enhance the geometric detail recovery near the edges and the overall performance. Huang et al.
[
23

designed an end-to-end trainable lightweight convolutional network that integrated cost volumes and attention modules. The designed network architecture enables the inference of light-field inputs at varying angles and facilitates detailed recovery in occluded regions.

The acquisition of the training and labeling data required by existing deep-learning algorithms poses a challenge, thus limiting their precision and applicability. Most deep learning-based approaches depend on data sourced exclusively from particular camera models during training, impeding their capacity to acclimate to various camera models and diminishing their generalization ability.

III
Method

Traditional methods for disparity estimation from light field data follow a ”discrete-to-discrete” approach, which fails to overcome the resolution limitations inherent in disparity representation. By contrast, with their continuous nature, neural field implicit representations provide superior expression capabilities compared to traditional discrete disparity representations, as they are not bound by finite resolution constraints, thus enabling robust modeling of complex disparity variations.

III-A

Neural disparity field representation

In contrast to traditional implicit representations of 3D shapes, disparity refers to a two-dimensional (2D) geometric manifold in 3D space that is associated with the depth of the scene. It describes the differences between viewpoints instead of directly representing 3D geometric shapes. The implicit continuous parameterization of NDFs allows the modeling of continuous disparities, making it more appropriate for describing continuous variations in disparity within light fields compared to the conventional 2D matrix representations.

The disparity inherent in a scene within a light field is parameterized as a continuous 2D implicit function using neural fields, termed NDFs. This is achieved by approximating the continuous 2D implicit function using a multiresolution grid and the MLPs network
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}
with activation functions, defined as

\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}:\mathbf{x}\rightarrow{d(\mathbf{x})},

(1)

where the network takes 2D coordinate positions
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
as input and outputs the disparity
{d(\mathbf{x})}
at that point. By optimizing the network weights
\mathbf{\Theta}
, this representation maps each input 2D coordinate
\mathbf{x}
to its corresponding disparity
{d(\mathbf{x})}
.

Forward problem modeling

The light field is regularly sampled on the viewpoint plane, ensuring that the pixels in the central view image are visible in at least half of the viewpoints, resulting in a stronger coupling between the central view and other views. Therefore, this study employs the disparity propagation process from other views to the central view in the light field to model disparity reconstruction. This process effectively utilizes the constraints of regular sampling on the viewpoint plane, while preserving the characteristics of the light field. This provides a robust forward problem model for disparity reconstruction.

To account for occlusion and camera noise in real scenes with a light field, a forward problem model for disparity reconstruction under angular consistency was established as follows:

L(d(\mathbf{x}))=\frac{\sum_{(u_{i},v_{j})\in\phi}\left(m_{(u_{i},v_{j})}(\mathbf{x})\cdot L_{(u_{i},v_{j})}\left(\mathbf{x}+\Delta\cdot d(\mathbf{x})\right)\right)}{\sum_{(u_{i},v_{j})\in\phi}m_{(u_{i},v_{j})}(\mathbf{x})}.

\phi

Previous studies explored different approaches for storing latent features, such as 3D grids

To address this issue and reduce memory and training data requirements, prior work has explored the use of tree-like data structures similar to those of Acorn

Considering the disparity characteristics, adjacent pixels often have similar values, reflecting the smoothness of scene depth variations. This necessitates the precise capture of subtle changes using the NDF model. Additionally, the distribution of gradients in the disparity exhibits sparsity, with most pixels having gradients close to zero, which is significant only at the scene or object edges. This sparsity implies that the local pixels share the same features, obviating the need to learn unique feature descriptions for each independent pixel, thereby reducing the training time. V
Discussion

We conducted comparative experiments on widely used light-field datasets to validate the performance of the proposed method in disparity estimation. The results demonstrate the exceptional proficiency of the proposed method in addressing inclined surfaces, smooth textureless regions, and occlusions in intricate scenes. When confronted with the weakly textured tail area of the butterfly and occluded leaf boundaries in the Papillon scene, the proposed method delivered a higher accuracy and smoother disparity estimates than MV, LF_OCC, SPO, and AMN. Further validation of the method’s resilience and adaptability was sought through tailored challenges in scenes such as backgammons, dots, pyramids, and stripes, which tested its ability to handle fine structures, noise interference, convex-concave geometries, and low-texture contrast scenarios. Despite a slight decline in the performance under high-noise conditions, the proposed method achieved effective disparity reconstruction and detail preservation in most cases. Additionally, experiments on the Stanford Lytro light field dataset demonstrated the success of the proposed method in accurately discerning disparity structures, even in highly noisy and complex occluded real-world scenes, particularly in suppressing background noise and reconstructing foreground edge details relative to contrastive algorithms. Moreover, by extracting disparities at varying resolutions directly from a pre-trained NDF, the proposed approach transcends the conventional limitations imposed by the reliance on discrete matrix-based disparity representations. Finally, the ablation studies shed light on the influence of combination strategies and selected viewpoints on the loss function. The MSSIM loss curbed the spread of local pixel errors. In contrast, including total variation regularization enforces constraints on the disparity gradient domain, ensuring smooth and coherent estimation outcomes. The capability to propagate with only half of the viewpoints bolsters the resistance to occlusion and noise.

Although the proposed method demonstrated excellent performance in the above experiments, the disparity map exhibited higher frequencies than the natural images, particularly in edge regions with disparity differences, resulting in oscillations in the predicted disparities. Thus, further research is required to investigate parameterized network models for disparities to enhance algorithm performance and applicability.

In summary, the proposed compact network model demonstrates efficiency and robustness in handling weak textures, varying noise levels, and occlusion scenarios. This could potentially provide more precise and reliable solutions for light field depth estimation in practical applications.

VI
Conclusions

We proposed a novel iterative approach to reconstruct the NDF that utilized neural network parameterization to achieve an implicit, continuous disparity function. The essential feature of this method is the implicit parameterization of the disparity by neural fields, which overcomes the resolution limitations inherent in traditional light-field disparity estimation. Compared to conventional methods based on discrete disparity maps, the proposed approach significantly reduced computational complexity while maintaining high precision and demonstrating superior robustness, particularly in handling complex scenes where occlusion and noise affect accurate disparity reconstruction. The proposed method employed a differentiable framework to construct a neural network model capable of modeling disparity continuously, thereby effectively avoiding the loss of detail caused by discrete representations. During the backpropagation optimization process, only a subset of rays was selected for propagation computation in the viewpoint direction, helping to mitigate occlusion effects and noise interference in the disparity estimation. Experimental validation indicates that the reconstructed NDF achieved accurate disparity on both synthetic and real datasets, enabling the effective recovery of high-resolution disparity.
