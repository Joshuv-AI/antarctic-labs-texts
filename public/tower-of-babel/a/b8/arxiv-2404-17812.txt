High-Dimensional Single-Index Models: Link Estimation and Marginal Inference
Kazuma Sawaya 1 , Yoshimasa Uematsu 2 , Masaaki Imaizumi 1,3,4

Abstract
. This study proposes a novel method for estimation and hypothesis testing in high-dimensional single-index models. We address a common scenario where the sample size and the dimension of regression coefficients are large and comparable. Unlike previous approaches, which often overlook the estimation of the unknown link function, we introduce a new method for link function estimation. Leveraging the information from the estimated link function, we propose more efficient estimators that are better aligned with the underlying model. Furthermore, we rigorously establish the asymptotic normality of each coordinate of the estimator. This provides a valid construction of confidence intervals and p p -values for any finite collection of coordinates. Numerical experiments validate our theoretical results.



1.
Introduction

We consider
n
i.i.d. observations
\{(\bm{X}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with a
p
-dimensional Gaussian feature vector
\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}),\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and each scalar response
y_{i}
belongs to a set
\mathcal{Y}
(e.g.,
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}_{+},\{0,1\},\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
), following the single-index model:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}=\bm{x}]=g(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{x}),

where

\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an unknown deterministic coefficient vector, and

g(\cdot)
is an unknown deterministic function, referred to as the link function, with
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{x}
being the index.
To identify the scale of
\bm{\beta}
, we assume
\mathrm{Var}(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}=1
.
The model includes common scenarios such as:

•

Linear regression
:
y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})

with
\sigma_{\varepsilon}>0
by setting
g(t)=t
.

•

Poisson regression
:
y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\exp(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}))
by setting
g(t)=\exp(t)
.

•

Binary choice models
:
y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(g(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}))
with
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
. This includes logistic regression for
g(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))
and the probit model by setting
g(\cdot)
as the cumulative distribution function of the standard Gaussian distribution.

We are interested in a high-dimensional setting, where both the sample size
n
and the coefficient dimension
p:=p(n)
are large and comparable.
Specifically, this study examines the proportionally high-dimensional regime defined by:

(2)

\displaystyle n,p(n)\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad p(n)/n=:\kappa\to\bar{\kappa},

where
\bar{\kappa}
is a positive constant.

The single-index model (
1
) possesses several practically important properties. First, it mitigates concerns about model misspecification, as it eliminates the need to specify
g(\cdot)
. Second, this model bypasses the curse of dimensionality associated with function estimation since the input index
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}
is a scalar. This advantage is particularly notable in comparison with nonparametric regression models, such as
y_{i}=\check{g}(\bm{X}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
, where
\check{g}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
remains unspecified. Third, the model facilitates the analysis of the contribution of each covariate,
X_{ij}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
, to the response
y_{i}
by testing
\beta_{j}=0
against
\beta_{j}\neq 0
.
Owing to these advantages, single-index models have been actively researched for decades
(
Powell et al., 1989
;
Härdle and Stoker, 1989
;
Li and Duan, 1989
;
Ichimura, 1993
;
Klein and Spady, 1993
;
Härdle et al., 1997
;
Hristache et al., 2001
;
Nishiyama and Robinson, 2005
;
Dalalyan et al., 2008
;
Alquier and Biau, 2013
;
Eftekhari et al., 2021
;
Bietti et al., 2022
;
Kuchibhotla et al., 2023
;
Fan et al., 2023
)
, attracting interest across a broad spectrum of fields, particularly in econometrics
(
Horowitz, 2009
;
Li and Racine, 2023
)
.

In the proportionally high-dimensional regime as defined in (
2
), the single-index model and its variants have been extensively studied. For logistic regression, which is a particular instance of the single-index model,
Sur et al. (2019)
;
Salehi et al. (2019)
have investigated the estimation and classification errors of the regression coefficient estimators
\bm{\beta}
. Furthermore,
Sur and Candès (2019)
;
Zhao et al. (2022)
;
Yadlowsky et al. (2021)
have developed methods for asymptotically valid statistical inference. In the case of generalized linear models with a known link function
g(\cdot)
,
Rangan (2011)
;
Barbier et al. (2019)
have characterized the asymptotic behavior of the coefficient estimator, while
Sawaya et al. (2023)
have derived the coordinate-wise marginal asymptotic normality of an adjusted estimator of
\beta_{j}
. For the single-index model with an unknown link function
g(\cdot)
, the seminal work by
Bellec (2022)
establishes the (non-marginal) asymptotic normality of estimators, even when there is link misspecification. However, the construction of an estimator for the link function
g(\cdot)
and the marginal asymptotic normality of the coefficient estimator are issues that have not yet been fully resolved.

Inspired by these seminal works, the following questions naturally arise:

(1)

Can we consistently estimate the unknown link function
g(\cdot)
?

(2)

Can we rigorously establish marginal statistical inference for each coordinate of
\bm{\beta}
?

(3)

Can we improve the estimation efficiency by utilizing the estimated link function?

This paper aims to provide affirmative answers to these questions. Specifically, we propose a novel estimation methodology comprising three steps. First, we construct an estimator for the index
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}
.
Second, we develop an estimator for the link function
g(\cdot)
.
Third, we design a new estimator for
\bm{\beta}
with the estimated link function.
To conduct statistical inference, we investigate the estimation problem of inferential parameters necessary for establishing coordinate-wise asymptotic normality in high-dimensional settings.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

-

Link estimation
: We propose a consistent estimator for the link function
g(\cdot)
, which is of practical significance as well as estimating coefficients. This aids in interpreting the model via the link function and mitigates negative impacts on coefficient estimation due to link misspecification.

-

Marginal inference
: We establish the asymptotic normality for any finite subset of the coordinates of our estimator, facilitating coordinate-wise inference of
\bm{\beta}
. This approach allows us not only to test each variable’s contribution to the response but also to conduct variable selection based on importance statistics for each feature.

-

Efficiency improvement
:
By utilizing the consistently estimated link function, we anticipate that our estimator of
\bm{\beta}
will be more efficient than previous estimators that rely on potentially misspecified link functions. We predominantly validate this efficiency through numerical simulations.

From a technical perspective,
we leverage the proof strategy in
Zhao et al. (2022)

to demonstrate the marginal asymptotic normality of our estimator for
\bm{\beta}
.
Specifically, we extend the arguments to a broader class of unregularized M-estimators, whereas
Zhao et al. (2022)
originally considered the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for logistic regression.

1.1.
Marginal Inference in High Dimensions

We review key technical aspects of statistical inference for each coordinate
\beta_{j}
in the proportionally high-dimensional regime (
2
). We maintain
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}
of constant order by considering the setting
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}=\Theta(1)
. We define
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
as the precision matrix for the distribution of
\bm{X}_{i}
and set
\tau_{j}^{2}:=\Theta_{jj}^{-1}>0
. An unbiased estimator of
\tau_{j}
can be constructed using nodewise regression (cf. Section 5.1 in
Zhao et al. (2022)
). For simplicity, we assume
\tau_{j}
is known, following prior studies.

In the high-dimensional regime (
2
), statistical inference must address two components: the asymptotic distribution and the
inferential parameters
of an estimator. We review the asymptotic distribution of the MLE
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\mathrm{m}}
for logistic regression. According to
Zhao et al. (2022)
, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that
\sqrt{p}\tau_{j}\beta_{j}=O(1)
as
n\to\infty
, the estimator achieves the following asymptotic normality:

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{p}(\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\mathrm{m}}-\mu_{*}\beta_{j})}{\sigma_{*}/\tau_{j}}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Here, we define
\mu_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{*}>0
as the asymptotic bias and variance, respectively, ensuring the convergence (
3
). It is crucial to note that both the estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{\mathrm{m}}
and the target
\beta_{j}
scale as
O_{p}(1/\sqrt{p})
here.

To perform statistical inference based on (
3
), it is necessary to estimate the inferential parameters
\mu_{*}
and
\sigma_{*}
. Several studies including
El Karoui et al. (2013)
;
Thrampoulidis et al. (2018)
;
Sur and Candès (2019)
;
Loureiro et al. (2021)
theoretically characterize these parameters as solutions to a system of nonlinear equations that depend on the data-generating process and the loss function. Additionally, various approaches have been developed to practically solve the equations by determining their hyperparameter
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}
under different conditions. Specifically,
Sur and Candès (2019)
introduces
ProbeFrontier
for estimating
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}
based on the asymptotic existence/non-existence boundary of the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) in logistic regression.
SLOE
, proposed by
Yadlowsky et al. (2021)
, enhances this estimation using a leave-one-out technique. Moreover,
Sawaya et al. (2023)
takes a different moment-based approach to estimate
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}
for generalized linear models.

For single-index models,
Bellec (2022)
introduces
observable adjustments
that estimate the inferential parameters directly under the identification condition
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}=1
(as in Assumption
1
) irrespective of link misspecification, bypassing the system of equations. In our study, we develop an estimator for the single-index model satisfying the asymptotic normality (
3
), with corresponding estimators for the inferential parameters using observable adjustments.

2.
Statistical Estimation Procedure

In this section, we introduce a novel statistical estimation method for single-index models as defined in (
1
). To give an overview, our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is constructed through the following steps:

(i)

Construct an index estimator
W_{i}
for
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}
using the ridge regression estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
, referred to as a pilot estimator.

(ii)

Develop a function estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
for the link function
g(\cdot)
, based on the distributional characteristics of the index estimator
W_{i}
.

(iii)

Construct our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
for the coefficients
\bm{\beta}
, using the estimated link
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
function.

Furthermore, statistical inference additionally involves a fourth step:

(iv)

Estimate the inferential parameters
\mu_{*}
and
\sigma_{*}
, conditional on the estimated link function
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
.

In our estimation procedure, we divide the dataset
{(\bm{X}_{i},y_{i})}_{i=1}^{n}
into two disjoint subsets
{(\bm{X}_{i},y_{i})}_{i\in I_{1}}
and
{(\bm{X}_{i},y_{i})}_{i\in I_{2}}
, where
I_{1},I_{2}\subset[n]
are index sets such that
I_{1}\cap I_{2}=\emptyset
and
I_{1}\cup I_{2}=[n]
. Additionally, for
k=1,2
, let
\bm{X}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times p}
and
\bm{y}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
denote the design matrix and response vector of subset
I_{k}
, respectively. We utilize the first subset to estimate the link function (Steps (i) and (ii)), and the second subset to estimate the regression coefficients (Step (iii)) and inference parameters (Step (iv)). From a theoretical perspective, this division helps to manage the complicated dependency structure caused by data reuse. Nonetheless, for practical applications, we recommend employing all observations in each step to maximize the utilization of the data’s inherent signal strength. Here,
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are the sample sizes for each partition, satisfying
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
such that
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are the same asymptotic order, i.e., we define
\kappa_{1}=p/n_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}=p/n_{2}
and there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
with
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
independent of
n
such that
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
holds.

2.1.
Index Estimation

In this step, we use the first subset
(\bm{X}^{(1)},\bm{y}^{(1)})
. We define the pilot estimator as the ridge estimator,

\tilde{\bm{\beta}}=(({\bm{X}^{(1)}})^{\top}\bm{X}^{(1)}+n_{1}\lambda\bm{I}_{p})^{-1}({\bm{X}^{(1)}})^{\top}\bm{y}^{(1)}
where
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter. Further, we consider inferential parameters
(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1})
of
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
, which satisfy

\sqrt{p}\tau_{j}(\tilde{\beta}_{j}-\mu_{1}\beta_{j})/\sigma_{1}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for
j\in[p]
such that
\sqrt{p}\tau_{j}\beta_{j}=O(1)
.
Using these parameters, we develop an estimator
W_{i}
for the index
\bm{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}
as follows:

(4)

\displaystyle W_{i}:=\tilde{\mu}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}-\tilde{\mu}^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}\left(y_{i}^{(1)}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}\right)

for each
i\in[n_{1}]
. Here,
\tilde{\mu}
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}
are estimators of
\mu_{1}
and
\sigma_{1}
, defined as

(5)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}=\left\lvert\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}-\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\right\rvert^{1/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{n_{1}^{-1}\|\bm{y}^{(1)}-\bm{X}^{(1)}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}}{(\tilde{v}+\lambda)^{2}/\kappa_{1}},

where
\tilde{\gamma}:=\kappa_{1}/(\tilde{v}+\lambda)
and
\tilde{v}=n_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{X}^{(1)}({(\bm{X}^{(1)}})^{\top}\bm{X}^{(1)}+n_{1}\lambda\bm{I}_{p})^{-1}{(\bm{X}^{(1)}})^{\top})
.
These estimators are obtained by the observable adjustment technique described in
Bellec (2022)
.

This index estimator
W_{i}
is approximately unbiased for the index
\bm{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}
, yielding the following asymptotic result.

(6)

\displaystyle W_{i}\approx\bm{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}+\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\sigma}^{2}/\tilde{\mu}^{2}).

We will provide its rigorous statement in Proposition
5
in Section
5.1
.

There are other options for the pilot estimator besides the ridge estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
.
If
\kappa_{1}\leq 1
holds, the least squares estimator can be an alternative.
If
y_{i}
is a binary or non-negative integer, the MLE of logistic or Poisson regression can be a natural candidate, respectively, although the ridge estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
is valid regardless of the form that
y_{i}
takes.
In each case, the estimated inferential parameters
(\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\sigma}^{2})
should be updated accordingly.
Details are presented in Section
6
.

2.2.
Link Estimation

We develop an estimator of the link function
g(\cdot)
using
W_{i}
in (
4
).
If we could observe the true index
\bm{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}
, it would be possible to estimate
g(x)=\mathbb{E}[y_{1}\mid\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{1}=x]
by applying standard nonparametric methods to the pairs of responses and true indices
(\bm{y}^{(1)},\bm{X}^{(1)}\bm{\beta})
. However, as the true index is unobservable, we must estimate
g(\cdot)
using given pairs of responses and contaminated indices
{(y_{i}^{(1)},W_{i})}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}
, where
W_{i}\approx\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}+\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\varsigma}^{2})
with
\tilde{\varsigma}^{2}=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}/\tilde{\mu}^{2}
being a ratio of the inferential parameters. The type of error
\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\varsigma}^{2})
involving the regressor leads to an attenuation bias in the estimation of
g(\cdot)
, known as the
errors-in-variables
problem. To address this issue, we utilize a deconvolution technique
(
Stefanski and Carroll, 1990
)
to remove the bias asymptotically. Further details of the deconvolution are provided in Appendix
D
.

We define an estimator of
g(\cdot)
.
In preparation, we specify a kernel function
K:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and define a deconvolution kernel
K_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

(7)

\displaystyle K_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\mathrm{i}tx)\frac{\phi_{K}(t)}{\phi_{\tilde{\varsigma}}(t/h_{n})}dt,

where
h_{n}>0
is a bandwidth,
\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}
is an imaginary unit, and
\phi_{K}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\phi_{\tilde{\varsigma}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are the Fourier transform of
K(\cdot)
and the density function of
\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\varsigma}^{2})
, respectively.
We then define our estimator of
g(\cdot)
as

(8)

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(x):=\mathcal{R}[\breve{g}](x)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\breve{g}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}y_{i}^{(1)}K_{n}\left((x-W_{i})/h_{n}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}K_{n}\left((x-W_{i})/h_{n}\right)},

where
\mathcal{R}[\cdot]
is a monotonization operator, specified later, which maps any measurable function to a monotonic function, and
\breve{g}(\cdot)
is a Nadaraya-Watson estimator obtained by the deconvolution kernel. We will prove the consistency of this estimator in Theorem
1
in Section
3
.

The monotonization operation
\mathcal{R}[\cdot]
on
\breve{g}(\cdot)
is justifiable because the true link function
g(\cdot)
is assumed to be monotonic.
One simple choice for
\mathcal{R}[\cdot]
, applicable to any measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, is

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{naive}}[f](x)=\sup_{x^{\prime}\leq x}f(x^{\prime}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

This definition holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Another effective alternative is the rearrangement operator
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2009
)
. This operator monotonizes a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
within a compact interval
[\underline{x},\overline{x}]\subset\mathbb{R}
:

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{r}}[f](x)=\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\int_{[0,1]}1\left\{f\left(\frac{u-\underline{x}}{\overline{x}-\underline{x}}\right)\leq t\right\}du\geq\frac{x-\underline{x}}{\overline{x}-\underline{x}}\right\},~x\in[\underline{x},\overline{x}].

This operator, which sorts the values of
f(\cdot)
in increasing order, is robust against local fluctuations such as function bumps. Thus, it effectively addresses bumps in
\breve{g}(\cdot)
arising from kernel-based methods.

Remark 1

(Motivation for Nadaraya-Watson estimator)
.

We employ the Nadaraya-Watson-type estimator in (
8
) to achieve a consistent nonparametric estimator in the error-in-variables setup.
In the presence of errors in variables, while several approaches based on deconvolution combined with B-spline
(
Koo and Lee, 1998
)
or wavelet regression
(
Chichignoud et al., 2017
)
have been proposed, these methods do not cover the case in which the error in variables follows a Gaussian distribution.
There also exist studies
using neural networks
(
Kohler and Mehnert, 2011
)
, but their consistency results rely on the assumption that the error in variables is asymptotically negligible, which is not realistic in practice.

2.3.
Coefficient Estimation

We next propose our estimator of
\bm{\beta}
using
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
obtained in (
8
). In this step, we consider the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
from
\bm{X}^{(1)}
as given, and estimate
\bm{\beta}
using
\bm{X}^{(2)}
. To facilitate this, we introduce the
surrogate loss function
for
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and any measurable function
\bar{g}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
:

(11)

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{b};\bm{x},y,\bar{g}):=\bar{G}(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{b})-y\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{b},

where
\bar{G}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function such that
\bar{G}^{\prime}(t)=\bar{g}(t)
. This function can be viewed as a natural extension of the matching loss
(
Auer et al., 1995
)
used in generalized linear models.
If
\bar{g}(\cdot)
is strictly increasing, then the loss is strictly convex in
\bm{b}
. Moreover, the surrogate loss is justified by the characteristics of the true parameter as follows
(
Agarwal et al., 2014
)
:

(12)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}=\argmin_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bm{b};{\bm{X}_{1}},{y_{1}},{g})|\bm{X}_{1}\right],

provided that
G(\cdot)
is integrable.
The surrogate loss aligns with the negative log-likelihood when
g(\cdot)
is known and serves as a canonical link function, thereby making the surrogate loss minimizer a generalization of the MLEs in generalized linear models.

Using the second dataset
(\bm{X}^{(2)},\bm{y}^{(2)})
with any given function
\bar{g}(\cdot)
, we define our estimator of
\bm{\beta}
as

(13)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g})=\argmin_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\ell(\bm{b};\bm{X}_{i}^{(2)},y_{i}^{(2)},\bar{g})+J(\bm{b}),

where
J:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex regularization function.
Finally, we substitute the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
into (
13
) to obtain our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
.
The use of a nonzero regularization term,
J(\cdot)
, is beneficial in cases where the minimizer (
13
) is not unique or does not exist; see, for example,
Candès and Sur (2020)
for the logistic regression case.

Remark 2

(Motivation of sample splitting)
.

We adopt sample splitting because, when the same data are used both for estimating the link function and for estimating the regression coefficients, the resulting dependence introduces a complex bias structure, whose impact on the asymptotic normality with observable adjustments is technically challenging to analyze.
On the other hand, from a conceptual perspective, classical arguments based on Neyman orthogonality
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2022
)
for single-index models suggest that the first-order effect of the link-function estimation error on the asymptotic distribution of the coefficient estimator vanishes. As a consequence, even without sample splitting, one may expect the asymptotic normality of the coefficient estimator to remain valid, despite reusing the same data for both stages.

2.4.
Inferential Parameter Estimation

We finally study estimators for the inferential parameters of our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
, which are essential for statistical inference as discussed in Section
1.1
. As established in (
3
), it is necessary to estimate the asymptotic bias
\mu_{*}(\widehat{g})
and variance
\sigma_{*}^{2}(\widehat{g})
that satisfy the following relationship:

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{p}(\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})-\mu_{*}(\widehat{g})\beta_{j})}{\sigma_{*}(\widehat{g})}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad j\in[p],

conditional on
(\bm{X}^{(1)},\bm{y}^{(1)})
and consequently on
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
.

We develop estimators for these inferential parameters using observable adjustments as suggested by
Bellec (2022)
, in accordance with the estimator (
13
).
For any measurable function
\bar{g}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define
\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\bar{g}^{\prime}(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g})))
and
\widehat{v}_{\lambda}=n_{2}^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{D}-\bm{D}\bm{X}^{(2)}(({\bm{X}^{(2)}})^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{X}^{(2)}+n_{2}\lambda\bm{I}_{p})^{-1}({\bm{X}^{(2)}})^{\top}\bm{D})
for
\lambda\geq 0
.
When incorporating
J(\bm{b})=\lambda\|\bm{b}\|^{2}/2
into (
13
) with
\lambda>0
, we propose the following estimators:

(15)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(\bar{g})

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g})\|^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\bar{g})\right\rvert^{1/2}\mbox{~~and~~}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\bar{g})=\frac{\|\bm{y}^{(2)}-\bar{g}(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g}))\|^{2}}{n_{2}(\widehat{v}_{\lambda}+\lambda)^{2}/\kappa_{2}}.

In the case where
J(\cdot)\equiv\bm{0}
holds, we define

(16)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\bar{g})=\left\lvert\frac{{\|\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g})\|^{2}}}{n_{2}}-(1-\kappa_{2})\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}(\bar{g})\right\rvert^{1/2}\mbox{~~and~~}\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}(\bar{g})=\frac{\|\bm{y}^{(2)}-\bar{g}(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g}))\|^{2}}{n_{2}\widehat{v}_{0}^{2}/\kappa_{2}}.

A theoretical justification for the asymptotic normality with these estimators and their application in inference is provided in Section
3.2
.

3.
Main Theoretical Results of Proposed Estimators

This section presents theoretical results for our estimation framework. Specifically, we prove the consistency of the estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
for the link function
g(\cdot)
, and the asymptotic normality of the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
for the coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}
. Outlines of the proofs for each assertion will be provided in Section
5
.

Assumption 1

(Gaussian covariates and identification)
.

Each row of the matrix
\bm{X}
independently follows
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
with
\bm{\Sigma}
obeying
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}=1
and
0<c_{\Sigma}^{-1}\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq c_{\Sigma}<\infty
for some constant
c_{\Sigma}
.

It is common to assume Gaussianity of covariates in the proportionally high-dimensional regime, as mentioned in Section
1.2
. The condition
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}=1
is necessary to identify the scale of
\bm{\beta}
, which ensures the uniqueness of the estimator in the single-index model with an unknown link function
g(\cdot)
. For example, without this condition, it would be impossible to distinguish between
g(\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{b})
and
f(2\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{b})
, where
f(t)=g(t/2)
, for any
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Furthermore, the assumption of upper and lower bounds on the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}
implies that
\|\bm{\beta}\|=\Theta(1)
.

Assumption 2

(Coherency of the single-index model)
.

A distribution of
(y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
, which follows the model (
1
), satisfies that there exist an (unknown) deterministic function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that we have

(17)

\displaystyle y_{i}=F(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i},U_{i}),

where

U_{i}
is some random variable independent of
\bm{X}_{i}
.

This assumption ensures the coherency of the single-index model (
1
).
The description has been commonly used
Li and Duan (1989)
;
Bellec (2022)
and includes a wide class of models, e.g., the linear regression with
F(v,u)=v+u
and
U_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{U}^{2})
with
\sigma_{U}^{2}>0
, and the logistic regression model with
F(v,u)=\textbf{1}\{u\leq 1/(1+e^{-v})\}
and
U_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
.
Specifically, this assumption excludes a case where
\bm{X}_{i}
affects
y_{i}
in a way that is independent of the index
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}
, such as
y_{i}=g(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i})+\bm{\gamma}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}\epsilon_{i}
, where
\epsilon_{i}
is a mean zero random variable independent of
\bm{X}_{i}
and
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an additional coefficient.
A property of
F
is indirectly constrained by assumptions about the link
g
that follow.

Assumption 3

(Monotone and smooth link function)
.

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
and constants
a<b
such that
g^{(\ell)}(x)
exists for every
\ell=0,1\ldots,m
and
x\in[a,b]
.
Also, there exists a constant
B>0
such that
\max_{\ell=0,1,...,m}\max_{x\in[a,b]}|g^{(\ell)}(x)|\leq B
holds.
Furthermore, there exists
c_{g}\in(0,\infty)
such that
c_{g}^{-1}\leq\min_{x\in[a,b]}g^{\prime}(x)
holds.

Assumption
3
restricts the class of link functions to those that are monotonic. This class has been extensively reviewed in the literature, with
Balabdaoui et al. (2019)
providing a comprehensive discussion. It encompasses a wide range of applications, including utility functions, growth curves, and dose-response models
(
Matzkin, 1991
;
Wan et al., 2017
;
Foster et al., 2013
)
. Furthermore, under a monotonically increasing link function, the sign of
\bm{\beta}
is identified, so that we can identify
\bm{\beta}
only by the scale condition
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}=1
.

The lower boundedness of
g^{\prime}(\cdot)
on the closed interval implies that the loss function (
13
) for the coefficient estimation is strictly convex on the interval. This assumption holds for the negative log-likelihood of the logistic regression that is not strictly convex on the real line.

Assumption 4

(Moment conditions of
\bm{y}
)
.

\mathbb{E}[y_{1}^{2}]<\infty
holds.
Further,
m_{2}(x):=\mathbb{E}[y_{1}^{2}\mid\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\beta}=x]
is continuous in
x\in[a,b]
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
defined in Assumption
3
.

The continuity of
m_{2}(\cdot)
is maintained in many commonly used models, particularly when
g(\cdot)
is continuous. For instance, the Poisson regression model defines
m_{2}(x)=\exp(x)(1+\exp(x))
, and binary choice models specify
m_{2}(x)=g(x)
.

3.1.
Consistency of Link Estimation

We demonstrate the uniform consistency of the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
in (
8
) over closed intervals. We consider the
m\mathrm{th}
-order kernel
K(\cdot)
that satisfies

(18)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(t)dt=1,~~~\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{m}K(t)dt\neq 0,~\mbox{and}~\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{\ell}K(t)dt=0,

for
\ell\in[m-1]
.
We then obtain the following:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
4
hold and the Fourier transform
\phi_{K}(t)
of the kernel
K(\cdot)
has a bounded support in
[-M_{0},M_{0}]
with some
M_{0}>0
, and the bandwidth
h_{n}=(c_{h}\log n_{1})^{-1/2}
satisfies
2M_{0}^{2}(\sigma_{1}/\mu_{1})^{2}c_{h}<1
.
Also, for
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\ i\in[n]
defined in Proposition
5
, suppose that
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{n})
holds.
Then, we have the following as
n_{1}\to\infty
:

(19)

\displaystyle\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}|\widehat{g}(x)-g(x)|=O_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\frac{1}{(\log n_{1})^{m/2}}\right).

This result shows the consistency of the link estimator.
About the convergence rate, according to
Fan and Truong (1993)
, the logarithmic rate
O_{\mathrm{p}}(1/(\log n_{1})^{m/2})
reaches a lower bound, indicating that this rate cannot be improved.

Regarding the condition on
(Z_{1},...,Z_{n})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}_{n}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{n})
, it ensures that the index estimator
W_{i}
can be regarded as asymptotically i.i.d. and is necessary for constructing a consistent link function estimator. We numerically discuss the plausibility of the condition using the statistical testing of independence in Appendix
C
.

3.2.
Marginal Asymptotic Normality of Coefficient Estimators

This section demonstrates the marginal asymptotic normality of our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
for
\bm{\beta}
, facilitated by the estimators of the inferential parameters,
\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g})
and
\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})
. These results are directly applicable to hypothesis testing and the construction of confidence intervals for any finite subset of the
\beta_{j}
’s.

3.2.1.
Unit Covariance and
p>n
Case

As previously noted, the inferential parameters vary depending on the estimator considered. In this section, we focus on the ridge regularized estimator with unit covariance
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}_{p}
. We will also present additional results for generalized covariance matrices and the ridgeless scenario later.

Theorem 2
.

We consider the coefficient estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
with
J(\bm{b})=\lambda\|\bm{b}\|^{2}/2
, and the inferential estimators
(\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g}))
, associated with the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
.
Suppose that
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{I}_{p}
,
\|\bm{\beta}\|=1
, and Assumptions
2
-
3
hold.
Then, a conditional distribution of
(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g}))
with a fixed event on
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
satisfies the following: for any coordinate
j\in[p]
satisfying

\sqrt{p}\beta_{j}=O(1)
,
we have

(20)

\displaystyle T_{j}:=\frac{\sqrt{p}({\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g})\beta_{j}})}{\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

as
n,p\to\infty
with the regime (
2
).
Moreover, for any finite set of coordinates
\mathcal{S}\subset[p]
satisfying
\sqrt{p}\|\bm{\beta}_{\mathcal{S}}\|=O(1)
, we have, as
n,p\to\infty
,

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{p}({\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{g})-\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g})\bm{\beta}_{\mathcal{S}})}}{\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{|\mathcal{S}|}).

This result also implies that
\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})/\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g})
is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of
\beta_{j}
. Note that the convergence of the conditional distribution is ensured by the non-degeneracy property of the conditional event, as defined by
({\bm{X}}^{(1)},{\bm{y}}^{(1)})
; see
Goggin (1994)
for details.

We can improve the condition
\sqrt{p}\beta_{j}=O(1)
to
\beta_{j}=o(1)
under the Assumption E in
Bellec (2022)
which derives the explicit rate of convergence for the estimation error of
\widehat{\mu}(\cdot)
.

We highlight two key contributions of Theorem
2
.
First, it is the first result in the literature of a single-index model to demonstrate the marginal asymptotic normality of the coefficient estimator that leverages the link estimator.
Second, it remains valid even when the ratio
\kappa=p/n
exceeds one, a notable distinction from a similar marginal asymptotic result (Theorem 5.2 in
Bellec (2022)
), which holds only when
\kappa
is less than one.
Although Section 4 of
Bellec (2022)
addresses the case
\kappa>1
and considers many penalty functions, its results pertain not to marginal asymptotic normality, but rather to the average behavior of the estimator.

Application to Statistical Inference
:
From Theorem
2
, we construct a confidence interval
\mathrm{CI}_{{1-\alpha}}^{j}
for each
\beta_{j}
with a confidence level
(1-\alpha)
as follows:

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CI}_{{1-\alpha}}^{j}:=\frac{1}{\widehat{\mu}(\widehat{g})}\left[\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})-z_{(1-\alpha/2)}\frac{\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})}{\sqrt{p}},~\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})+z_{(1-\alpha/2)}\frac{\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})}{\sqrt{p}}\right],

where
j\in[p]
and
z_{(1-\alpha/2)}
is the
(1-\alpha/2)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution. This construction ensures the coverage probability adheres to the specified confidence level asymptotically.

Corollary 3
.

Under the settings of Theorem
2
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have the following as
n,p\to\infty
with the regime (
2
):

(23)

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq j\leq p}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{j}\in\mathrm{CI}_{1-\alpha}^{j}\right)-(1-\alpha)\right\rvert\to 0.

Hence, for testing the hypothesis
H_{0}^{j}:\beta_{j}=0
against
H_{1}^{j}:\beta_{j}\neq 0
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we can use the corrected
t
-statistics in (
20
). The test that rejects the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{j}
if

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})z_{(1-\alpha/2)}}{\sqrt{p}\tau_{j}}\leq|\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})|

controls the asymptotic size of the test at the level
\alpha
.
Additionally, it is feasible to develop a variable selection procedure that identifies variables related to the response. Specifically, the
p
-value associated with
H_{0}^{j}
and the statistic
\sqrt{p}\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})/\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{g})
can serve as importance statistics for the
j
th covariate. This approach facilitates variable selection procedures that control the false discovery rate, as detailed in sources such as
Benjamini and Hochberg (1995)
;
Candès et al. (2018)
;
Xing et al. (2023)
;
Dai et al. (2023)
.

3.2.2.
General Covariance and
p<n
Case

We extend Theorem
2
to scenarios with a general covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
in unregularized settings. To this end, we utilize the estimators
(\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\widehat{g}))
, which are defined for inferential parameters in Section
2.4
. Recall that the precision matrix
\bm{\Theta}
is defined as
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
.

Theorem 4
.

We consider the coefficient estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
with
J(\bm{b})\equiv 0
, and the inferential estimators
(\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\widehat{g}))
, associated with the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
.
Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
3
hold.
Then, a conditional distribution of
(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\widehat{g}),\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\widehat{g}))
with a fixed event on
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
satisfies the following: for any coordinate
j\in[p]
satisfying

\sqrt{p}\tau_{j}\beta_{j}=O(1)
,
we have

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{p}({\widehat{\beta}_{j}(\widehat{g})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\widehat{g})\beta_{j}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\widehat{g})/\tau_{j}}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

as
n,p\to\infty
with the regime (
2
) with
\bar{\kappa}\in(0,1)
.
Moreover, for a finite set of coordinates
\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\ldots,p\}
, we have

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{p}\bm{\Theta}_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1/2}({\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{g})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\widehat{g})\bm{\beta}_{\mathcal{S}})}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(\widehat{g})}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{|\mathcal{S}|}),

where the submatrix

We can also improve the condition

4.4.
Efficiency Comparison

Finally, we compare the estimation efficiency of the proposed estimator with several pilot estimators.
We use the effective asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}_{*}/\mu_{*}^{2}
as an efficiency measure, which is the inverse of the effective signal-to-noise ratio as described in
Feng et al. (2022)

{\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}}/({\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{\beta}})-1
for an estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
. This statistic is a reasonable approximation of the asymptotic variance of the debiased version of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

5.1.
Consistency of Link Estimation (Theorem
1
)

We provide an overview of the proof for Theorem
1
, which comprises two primary steps: (i) the asymptotic characteristics of the index estimator
W_{i}
discussed in Section
2.1
, and (ii) demonstrating the consistency of the estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
in Section
2.2
, related to
W_{i}
.

5.1.1.
Error of Index Estimator

We consider the distributional approximation (
6
) for the index estimator
W_{i}
, established through observable adjustments by
Bellec (2022)
. Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 in
Bellec (2022)
support Proposition
5
, i.e., under suitable assumptions we have the following statement.
Recall that we defined

(27)

\displaystyle W_{i}=\tilde{\mu}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}-\tilde{\mu}^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}\left(y_{i}^{(1)}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}\right).

Proposition 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
\mathbb{E}[y_{1}^{2}]<\infty
, there exists
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

independent of
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

such that, for each
i\in[n_{1}]
, as
n_{1}\to\infty
, it holds that

(28)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tilde{\mu}W_{i}-\tilde{\mu}\bm{\beta}^{\top}{\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}}-\tilde{\sigma}Z_{i}\right\rvert\overset{{p}}{\to}0.

This asserts that each
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{{\bm{X}}_{i}^{(1)}}
for
i\in[n_{1}]
is approximately equal to the sum of the biased true index
\tilde{\mu}\bm{\beta}^{\top}{{\bm{X}}_{i}^{(1)}}
, a Gaussian error, and an additive bias term.
We can see that the estimation error of the index estimator
W_{i}
is asymptotically represented by the Gaussian term as shown in Equation (
6
).

5.1.2.
Error of Link Estimator

Next, we prove the consistency of the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
using the index estimator
W_{i}
.
To this aim, we define a noise-contaminated index

(29)

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{i}:=\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}^{(1)}+\tilde{Z}_{i},

where
\tilde{Z_{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}/\mu_{1}^{2})
is an independent Gaussian variable.
If
W_{i}
were exactly equivalent to
\tilde{W}_{i}
, we could apply the classical result of nonparametric error-in-variables regression
(
Fan and Truong, 1993
)
to demonstrate the uniform consistency of
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
. However, this equivalence is only asymptotic as shown in (
28
). Therefore, we establish that the error due to this asymptotic equivalence is negligibly small in the estimation of
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
to complete the proof.

Specifically, we take the following steps.
First, we decompose the error of
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
into two terms.
In preparation, we define
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
as a deconvolution estimator using a deconvolution kernel
\tilde{K}_{n}(x)=({2\pi})^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\mathrm{i}tx){\phi_{K}(t)}/{\phi_{\varsigma}(t/h_{n})}dt
using the true inferential parameters as
\varsigma=\sigma_{*}/\mu_{*}
(its precise definition is given in Supplementary Material).
This estimator corresponds to the estimator for the error-in-variable setup developed by
Fan and Truong (1993)
.
Then, from the effect of the monotonization operator, we obtain the following decomposition:

(30)

\displaystyle\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}\left\lvert\widehat{g}(x)-g(x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}\left\lvert\breve{g}(x)-{g}(x)\right\rvert

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}\left\lvert\breve{g}(x)-\tilde{g}(x)\right\rvert+\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}\left\lvert\tilde{g}(x)-{g}(x)\right\rvert.

The second term
\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}\left\lvert\tilde{g}(x)-{g}(x)\right\rvert
in (
31
) is the estimation error by the deconvolution estimator
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
, which is proven to be
o_{\mathrm{p}}(1)
according to the result of
Fan and Truong (1993)
.

On the other hand, the first term
\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}\left\lvert\breve{g}(x)-\tilde{g}(x)\right\rvert
in (
31
) represents how our pre-monotonized estimator
\breve{g}(\cdot)
in (
8
) approximates the estimator
\tilde{g}(\cdot)
.
Rigorously, we obtain

(32)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\breve{g}(x)-\tilde{g}(x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{1}h_{n}}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}{K}_{n}\left(\frac{\tilde{W}_{i}-x}{h_{n}}\right)-{K}_{n}\left(\frac{{W}_{i}-x}{h_{n}}\right)\right\rvert}_{=:T_{1}}

(33)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{1}h_{n}}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}{K}_{n}\left(\frac{{W}_{i}-x}{h_{n}}\right)-\tilde{K}_{n}\left(\frac{{W}_{i}-x}{h_{n}}\right)\right\rvert}_{=:T_{2}},

where
\lesssim
is an inequality up to some universal constant.
The first term
T_{1}
describes the discrepancy between the estimator with

the index estimator
{W}_{i}

and the contaminated index
\tilde{W}_{i}
.

We develop an upper bound on
T_{1}
by using the result of Proposition
5
.
The second term
T_{2}
represents the discrepancy between the convolution kernels
K_{n}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{K}_{n}(\cdot)
.
Note that
K_{n}(\cdot)
depends on the estimator
\tilde{\varsigma}^{2}=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}/\tilde{\mu}^{2}
of the inferential parameter, and
\tilde{K}_{n}(\cdot)
depends on the true value of the inferential parameter
\varsigma=\sigma_{*}/\mu_{*}
.
We derive its upper bound by evaluating the error of the estimators
\tilde{K}_{n}(\cdot)
.

By integrating these results into (
31
), we prove that the estimation error of
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
is
O_{p}((\log n_{1})^{-m/2})

5.2.
Marginal Asymptotic Normality (Theorem
4
)

This section provides a proof sketch of Theorem
4
. We specifically present a general theorem that characterizes the asymptotic normality of each coordinate of the unregularized estimator in high-dimensional settings. This discussion extends the proof provided by
Zhao et al. (2022)
for logistic regression.

Consider the single-index model given by (
17
) and an arbitrary loss function
\bar{\ell}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
. We define an M-estimator
\bar{\bm{\beta}}
, based on the loss function
\bar{\ell}(\cdot)
, as follows:

(34)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\beta}}\in\argmin_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\ell}(\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i};y_{i}).

With this general setup, we establish the following statement:

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
Also, suppose that the M-estimator
\bar{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
in (
34
) is uniquely determined and there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\|\bar{\bm{\beta}}\|<C)\geq 1-o(1)
holds.
With the true parameter
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, define

(35)

\displaystyle\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}=\frac{\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}}{\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}^{2}=\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta}}^{\perp}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bar{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}=\|\bar{\bm{\beta}}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\bm{\beta}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2},

where
\bm{P}_{\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta}}^{\perp}=\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}/\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}
.
Then, for any coordinates
j\in[p]
with
\sqrt{p}\tau_{j}\beta_{j}=O(1)
, we obtain

(36)

\displaystyle T_{j}:=\frac{\sqrt{p}(\bar{\beta}_{j}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\beta_{j})}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}/\tau_{j}}\overset{{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

as
n,p\to\infty
with
p/n\to\bar{\kappa}<1
.

This theorem establishes the marginal asymptotic normality for a broad class of estimators defined by the minimization of convex loss functions. Additionally, it demonstrates that the limiting distributional behavior of
\bar{\bm{\beta}}
is characterized by
\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}
and
\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}^{2}
in the high-dimensional setting (
2
).
Intuitively,
\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}
is a scaled inner product of
\bar{\bm{\beta}}
and
\bm{\beta}
, and
\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}^{2}
denotes the magnitude of the orthogonal component of
\bar{\bm{\beta}}
to
\bm{\beta}
.

The rigorous proof in Supplementary Material is conducted in the following steps:

(i)

Since we have
\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}=(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{X}_{i})^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta})
, we achieve the replacements
\bm{X}_{i}
to
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{X}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p})
,
\bm{\beta}
to
\bm{\eta}=\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta}
, and
\bar{\bm{\beta}}
to
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bar{\bm{\beta}}
. From the Cholesky factorization of
\bm{\Sigma}
, we have

(37)

\displaystyle T_{j}=\frac{\sqrt{p}(\bar{\beta}_{j}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\beta_{j})}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}/\tau_{j}}=\frac{\sqrt{p}(\widehat{\eta}_{j}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\eta_{j})}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}.

(ii)

Considering the rotation
\bm{U}
around
\bm{\eta}
(i.e.,
\bm{U}\bm{\eta}=\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}=\bm{I}_{p}
), several calculations give, for
\bm{T}:=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{p})^{\top}/\sqrt{p}
,

(38)

\displaystyle\bm{T}=\frac{\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}}{\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\|}\overset{\rm d}{=}\frac{\bm{U}\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}}{\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\|}.

This means that
\bm{T}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere in
\bm{\eta}^{\perp}
(See Figure
4
).

(iii)

Drawing on the analogy to the asymptotic equivalence between the
p
-dimensional standard normal distribution and
\mathrm{Unif}(\sqrt{p}\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
, we obtain the asymptotic normality of
T_{j}
.

We apply this general theorem to obtain Theorem
4
.
A similar argument implies Theorem
2
for the regularized estimator.

The proof strategy for the marginal asymptotic normality developed here is fundamentally different from Bellec’s approach

7.
Conclusion and Discussion

This study establishes a novel statistical inference procedure for high-dimensional single-index models. Specifically, we develop a consistent estimation method for the link function. Furthermore, using the estimated link function, we formulate an efficient estimator and confirm its marginal asymptotic normality. This verification allows for the accurate construction of confidence intervals and
p
-values for any finite collection of coordinates.

We identify several avenues for future research: (a) extending these results to cases where the covariate distribution is non-Gaussian, (b) generalizing our findings to multi-index models, and (c) confirming the marginal asymptotic normality of our proposed estimators under any form of regularization and covariance. These prospects offer intriguing possibilities for further exploration.

Particularly, (c) can be achieved by imposing the exchangeability condition on the true coefficient
\bm{\beta}
and employing a technique similar to the proof of Corollary 3.8 in
Li and Sur (2023)
.

Appendix A
Effect of Link Estimation on Inferential Parameters

The following theorem reveals that the estimation error of the link function is asymptotically negligible with respect to the observable adjustments.

Specifically, we consider a slightly modified version of the inferential estimator.
In preparation, we define a censoring operator
\iota:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
on a interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
as
\iota(z)=\max(a,\min(b,z))
.
Then, for any
\bar{g}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define a truncation version of
\bm{D}
as
\bm{D}_{c}=\mathrm{diag}(\bar{g}^{\prime}(\iota(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g}))))
, and
\widehat{v}_{0c}=n_{2}^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{D}_{c}-\bm{D}_{c}\bm{X}^{(2)}(({\bm{X}^{(2)}})^{\top}\bm{D}_{c}\bm{X}^{(2)})^{-1}({\bm{X}^{(2)}})^{\top}\bm{D}_{c})
.
Further, in the case of
J(\cdot)\equiv\bm{0}
, we define the modified estimator as

(46)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{0c}(\bar{g})

\displaystyle=\left\lvert{\|\iota(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g}))\|^{2}}/n_{2}-(1-\kappa_{2})\widehat{\sigma}_{0c}^{2}(\bar{g})\right\rvert^{1/2},\mbox{~~and}

(47)

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{0c}^{2}(\bar{g})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\bm{y}^{(2)}-\bar{g}(\iota(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\bar{g})))\|^{2}}{n_{2}\widehat{v}_{0c}^{2}/\kappa_{2}}.

Using the modified definition, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 9
.

Suppose that
J(\cdot)\equiv\bm{0}
holds and the estimator (
13
) exists.
Further, suppose that Assumptions
1
-
4
hold.
Then, we have the following as
n_{1}\to\infty
:

(48)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\widehat{\mu}_{0c}(\widehat{g})-\widehat{\mu}_{0c}(g)\right\rvert\overset{{p}}{\to}0,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\left\lvert\widehat{\sigma}_{0c}^{2}(\widehat{g})-\widehat{\sigma}_{0c}^{2}(g)\right\rvert\overset{{p}}{\to}0.

This result indicates that, since the link estimator
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
is consistent, we can estimate the inferential parameters under the true link
g(\cdot)
.

The difficulty in this proof arises from the dependence between the elements of the estimator, which cannot be handled by the triangle inequality or Hölder’s inequality,

Appendix B
Theoretical Efficiency Comparison

We compare the efficiency of our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
with that of the ridge estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
as the pilot.
As shown in
Bellec (2022)
, the ridge estimator is a valid estimator for the single-index model in the high-dimensional scheme (
2
) even without estimating the link function
g(\cdot)
.

To the aim, we define the
effective asymptotic variance
based on inferential parameters, which is a ratio of the asymptotic bias and the asymptotic variance. That is, our estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
has its effective asymptotic variance
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g})/\widehat{\mu}^{2}(\widehat{g})
, and the ridge estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
has
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}/\tilde{\mu}^{2}
.
The effective asymptotic variance corresponds to the asymptotic variance of each coordinate of the estimators with bias correction.

We give the following result for necessary and sufficient conditions for the proposed estimator to be more efficient than the least squares estimator and the ridge estimator.

Proposition 10
.

We consider the coefficient estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})
with
J(\bm{b})\equiv\lambda\|\bm{b}\|^{2}
and the setup
n_{1}=n_{2}
.
We use the regularization parameter
\lambda_{1}>0
for the pilot estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
.
Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
3
are fulfilled.
Then,
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g})/\widehat{\mu}^{2}(\widehat{g})<\tilde{\sigma}^{2}/\tilde{\mu}^{2}
holds if and only if we have

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|}{\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\widehat{v}_{\lambda}+\lambda\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\tilde{v}+\lambda_{1}\right\rvert}\cdot\frac{\|\bm{y}^{(1)}-\bm{X}^{(1)}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|}{\|\bm{y}^{(2)}-\widehat{g}(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}))\|}>1.

This necessary and sufficient condition suggests that our estimator may have an advantage by exploiting the nonlinearity of the link function
g(\cdot)
.
The first reason is that, when
\bm{y}
has nonlinearity in
\bm{X}\bm{\beta}
, the residual
\|\bm{y}-\widehat{g}(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\|^{2}
of the proposed method is expected to be asymptotically smaller than
\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}
.
The second reason is that
\widehat{v}_{\lambda}
approximates the gradient mean
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{g}^{\prime}(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}))
, so this element increases when
g(\cdot)
has a large gradient. Using these facts, the proposed method incorporates the nonlinearity of
g(\cdot)
and helps improve efficiency.

Proposition 11
.

If
J(\cdot)\equiv\bm{0}
,
n_{1}=n_{2}
, and are fulfilled, then
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}(\widehat{g})/\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{2}(\widehat{g})<\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{LS}}^{2}/\tilde{\mu}_{\mathrm{LS}}^{2}

\displaystyle{\frac{\|\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|}{\|\bm{X}^{(1)}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\widehat{v}_{0}\right\rvert}{1-\kappa_{1}}\cdot\frac{\|\bm{y}^{(1)}-\bm{X}^{(1)}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|}{\|\bm{y}^{(2)}-\widehat{g}(\bm{X}^{(2)}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}))\|}}>1.

This result does not imply that the estimator

Appendix D
Nonparametric Regression with Deconvolution

In this section, we review the concept of nonparametric regression with deconvolution to address the errors-in-variable problem. To begin with, we redefine the notation only for this section. For a pair of random variables

(X,Y,Z)
, suppose that the model is

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x]=m(x),

and that we can only observe
n
i.i.d. realizations of
Y=X+\varepsilon
and
Z
. Here,
\varepsilon
is a random variable called measurement error or error in variables. For the identification, we assume that the distribution of
\varepsilon
is known.
Let the joint distribution of
(X,Z)
be
f(x,z)
. By the definition of the conditional expectations,
m(x)={r(x)}/{f(x)}
with

(52)

\displaystyle r(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}zf(x,z)dz,\quad f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,z)dz,

for the continuous random variables. The goal of the problem is to estimate the function
m(\cdot)
.

If we could observe
X
, a popular estimator of
m(x)
is Nadaraya-Watson estimator
{\tilde{r}(x)}/{\tilde{f}(x)}
with

(53)

\displaystyle\tilde{r}(x)=\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}K\left(\frac{x-X_{i}}{h_{n}}\right),\quad\tilde{f}(x)=\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{x-X_{i}}{h_{n}}\right),

where
K(\cdot)
is a kernel function and
h_{n}
is the bandwidth. Since
X
is unobservable, we alternatively construct the
deconvolution
estimator
(
Stefanski and Carroll, 1990
)
. Let the characteristic function of
X
,
Y
and
\varepsilon
be

\phi_{X}(\cdot)
,
\phi_{Y}(\cdot)
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
, respectively. Since the density of
Y
is the convolution of that of
X
and
\varepsilon
, and the convolution in the frequency domain is just a multiplication, we have
\phi_{X}(t)=\phi_{Y}(t)/\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)
. Thus, the inverse Fourier transform of
\phi_{Y}(t)/\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)
gives the density of
X
. Since we know the distribution of
\varepsilon
and we can approximate
\phi_{Y}(t)
by the characteristic function of the kernel density estimator of
Y
, we can construct an estimator of
f(x)
as

(54)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\mathrm{i}tx)\phi_{K}(th_{n})\frac{\widehat{\phi}_{Y}(t)}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}dt,

where we use the fact that the Fourier transform of
\tilde{f}_{Y}(y)=(nh_{n})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K((y-Y_{i})/h_{n})
is
\phi_{K}(th_{n})\widehat{\phi}_{Y}(t)
, which approximates
\phi_{Y}(\cdot)
.
Here,
\widehat{\phi}_{Y}(t)
is the empirical characteristic function:

(55)

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{Y}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(\mathrm{i}tY_{i}).

We can rewrite (
54

(56)

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{n}\left(\frac{x-Y_{i}}{h_{n}}\right),

with

(57)

\displaystyle K_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\mathrm{i}tx)\frac{\phi_{K}(t)}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t/h_{n})}dt.

Using this,
Fan and Truong (1993)
proposes a kernel regression estimator
\widehat{m}(x)=\widehat{r}(x)/\widehat{f}(x)
involving errors in variables with

(58)

\displaystyle\widehat{r}(x)=\frac{1}{nh_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}K_{n}\left(\frac{x-Y_{i}}{h_{n}}\right).

To establish the theoretical guarantee, we impose the following assumptions:

(N1)

For a convex function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a constant
\gamma>0
, define the proximal operator
\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma f}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(125)

\displaystyle\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma f}(x)=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\gamma f(z)+\frac{1}{2}(x-z)^{2}\right\}.

E.1.
Proof of Master Theorem

First, we define the notation used in the proof. We consider an invertible matrix
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
that satisfies
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}
. Define, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(126)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}=\bm{L}^{-1}\bm{X}_{i},\quad\bm{\eta}=\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{\beta},\quad\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{L}^{\top}\bar{\bm{\beta}}.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

We consider the following three steps.

Step 1: Reduction to standard Gaussian features
.
Note that the single-index model
y_{i}=g(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})+\varepsilon_{i}
is equivalent to
y_{i}=g(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\eta})+\varepsilon_{i}
.
Since
\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b}=\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{b})
holds, we have
\bar{\ell}(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b};y_{i})=\bar{\ell}(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\top}\bm{b};y_{i})
. Hence,
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\in\argmin_{\tilde{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\ell}(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{b}};y_{i})
is the estimator corresponding to the true parameter
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and features
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p})
.

We can choose
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}
to be a Cholesky factorization so that
\eta_{p}=\tau_{p}\beta_{p}
and
\widehat{\eta}_{p}=\tau_{p}\bar{\beta}_{p}
with
\tau_{p}=(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})_{pp}^{-1/2}
by (
126
). This follows from the fact that
L_{pp}=\tau_{p}
since
\tau_{p}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(X_{ip}\mid\bm{X}_{i\setminus p})=\mathrm{Var}(X_{ip}\mid\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i\setminus p})
, where
\bm{X}_{i\setminus p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
denotes the vector
\bm{X}_{i}
without
p
th coordinate. Since we can generalize this to any coordinate by permutation, we obtain

(127)

\displaystyle\tau_{j}\frac{\bar{\beta}_{j}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\beta_{j}}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}=\frac{\widehat{\eta}_{j}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\eta_{j}}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}},

for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and any pair
(\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}},\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}})
.

Step 2: Reduction to uniform distribution on sphere
.
Define an orthogonal projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}/\left\lVert\bm{\eta}\right\rVert^{2}
onto
\bm{\eta}
, and an orthogonal projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}=\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}
onto the orthogonal complement of
\bm{\eta}
. Let
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be any orthogonal matrix obeying
\bm{U}\bm{\eta}=\bm{\eta}
, namely, any rotation operator about
\bm{\eta}
. Then, since
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}+\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
, we have

(128)

\displaystyle\bm{U}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{U}\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}+\bm{U}\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}+\bm{U}\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}.

Using this, we obtain

(129)

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{U}\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}}{\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\|}\overset{\rm d}{=}\frac{\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}}{\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\|}=\frac{\widehat{\bm{\eta}}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\bm{\eta}}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}},

where the first identity follows from the fact that
\bm{U}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\overset{\rm d}{=}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
since
\bm{U}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
is the estimator with a true coefficient
\bm{U}\bm{\eta}=\bm{\eta}
and features drawn iid from
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p})
, by
\bar{\ell}(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{b}};y_{i})=\bar{\ell}((\bm{U}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{U}\tilde{\bm{b}};y_{i})
and
\bm{U}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}\overset{\rm d}{=}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{i}
.
(
129
) reveals that
(\widehat{\bm{\eta}}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\bm{\eta})/\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}
is rotationally invariant about
\bm{\eta}
, lies in
\bm{\eta}^{\perp}
, and has a unit norm. This means
(\widehat{\bm{\eta}}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\bm{\eta})/\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere lying in
\bm{\eta}^{\perp}
.

Step 3: Deriving asymptotic normality
.
The result of the previous step gives us

(130)

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\bm{\eta}}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\bm{\eta}}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}\overset{\rm d}{=}\frac{\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\bm{Z}}{\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\bm{Z}\|},

where
\bm{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p})
. Triangle inequalities yield that

(131)

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert\bm{Z}\right\rVert}{\sqrt{p}}-\frac{|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\bm{Z}|}{\sqrt{p}\left\lVert\bm{\eta}\right\rVert}\leq\frac{\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\bm{Z}\|}{\sqrt{p}}\leq\frac{\left\lVert\bm{Z}\right\rVert}{\sqrt{p}}+\frac{|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\bm{Z}|}{\sqrt{p}\left\lVert\bm{\eta}\right\rVert}.

Since
|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\bm{Z}|/(\sqrt{p}\left\lVert\bm{\eta}\right\rVert)\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0
and
\left\lVert\bm{Z}\right\rVert/\sqrt{p}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}1
, we obtain
\|\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\bm{Z}\|/\sqrt{p}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}1
. Therefore, this fact and (
130
) imply that

(132)

\displaystyle\sqrt{p}\frac{\widehat{\eta}_{j}-\mu_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}\eta_{j}}{\sigma_{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}\overset{\rm d}{=}\check{\sigma}_{j}Q+o_{\mathrm{p}}(1),\quad\check{\sigma}_{j}^{2}=1-\frac{\eta_{j}^{2}}{\left\lVert\bm{\eta}\right\rVert^{2}},

where
Q\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Here we use the fact that the covariance matrix of
\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\bm{Z}
is
\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}\bm{P}_{\bm{\eta}}^{\perp}=\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}/\|\bm{\eta}\|^{2}
. The assumptions
\eta_{j}=o(1)
and
\|\bm{\eta}\|=1
complete the proof.
∎

\displaystyle=n_{1}^{-1}\|{\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|}^{2}-(1-\kappa_{1})\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{LS}}^{2},\quad\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{LS}}^{2}=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{n_{1}(1-\kappa_{1})^{2}}\|{\bm{y}-\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|}^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{2}(\widehat{g})

\displaystyle=n_{2}^{-1}\|\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|^{2}-(1-\kappa_{2})\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g}),\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g})=\frac{\kappa_{2}}{n_{2}\widehat{v}_{\lambda}^{2}}{\|\bm{y}-\widehat{g}(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}))\|^{2}}.

Since we have

(267)

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{\mathrm{LS}}^{2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{LS}}^{2}}=\frac{\|\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|^{2}}{\frac{\kappa_{1}}{(1-\kappa_{1})^{2}}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|^{2}}-(1-\kappa_{1}),

(268)

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{2}(\widehat{g})}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g})}=\frac{\|\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|^{2}}{\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\widehat{v}_{\lambda}^{2}}\|\bm{y}-\widehat{g}(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}))\|^{2}}-(1-\kappa_{2}),

(269)

\displaystyle{\frac{\|\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|}{\|\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\widehat{v}_{\lambda}\right\rvert}{1-\kappa_{1}}\cdot\frac{\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|}{\|\bm{y}-\widehat{g}(\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g}))\|}}>1.

, recall that

(270)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}-\tilde{\sigma}^{2},\quad\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\kappa_{1}n_{1}^{-1}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|^{2}(\tilde{v}^{2}+\lambda_{1})^{-2}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{2}(\widehat{g})

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g}),\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\widehat{g})=\kappa_{2}n_{1}^{-1}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{g})\|^{2}(\widehat{v}_{\lambda}^{2}+\lambda)^{-2}.

Thus, in a similar way as when
