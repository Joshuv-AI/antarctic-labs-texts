On regularity of solutions for certain linear Boltzmann transport equations
Jouko Tervo

Abstract
. The paper considers a class of linear Boltzmann transport equations which models a charged particle transport. The equation is an approximation of the original exact transport equation which involves hyper-singular integrals in their collision terms. Hyper-singular integrals can be approximated by partial differential operators together with partial integral operators which leads to an approximation under consideration. This type of approximation is applied, for example in the dose calculation of radiation therapy. The related transport problem is a characteristic initial inflow boundary value problem. Regularity results of solutions are verified utilizing the scales of relevant anisotropic Sobolev spaces.



Keywords:
linear Boltzmann transport equation, continuous slowing down approximation,

regularity of solutions, characteristic boundary value problems, charged particle transport

1.
Introduction

We consider the existence and regularity of solutions of the
linear Boltzmann transport continuous slowing down equation

(1)

\displaystyle T\psi:=a(x,E){\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial E}}+c(x,E)\Delta_{S}\psi+d(x,\omega,E)\cdot\nabla_{S}\psi+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+\Sigma(x,\omega,E)\psi-K_{r}\psi=f

in
G\times S\times I
.
Here
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a spatial domain,
S:=S_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the unit sphere (velocity direction domain) and
I=[E_{0},E_{\rm m}]
is the energy interval.
The solution satisfies the inflow boundary condition

(2)

\displaystyle{\psi}_{|\Gamma_{-}}=g

where
\Gamma_{-}
is ”the inflow boundary”
\{(y,\omega,E)\in(\partial G)\times S\times I|\ \omega\cdot\nu(y)<0\}
(see section
2
below).
In addition, the solution obeys the initial condition

(3)

\displaystyle\psi(.,.,E_{\rm m})=0

where
E_{\rm m}
is the co-called
cut-off energy
.
The conditions (
2
), (
3
) guarantee that the overall initial inflow boundary value problem (
1
), (
2
), (
3
) is well-posed.
The
restricted collision operator

K_{r}
is a partial integral type operator (see section
3.1
below) of the form

\displaystyle K_{r}\psi=\int_{S^{\prime}}\int_{I^{\prime}}\sigma^{1}(x,\omega^{\prime},\omega,E^{\prime},E)\psi(x,\omega^{\prime},E^{\prime})dE^{\prime}d\omega^{\prime}+\int_{S^{\prime}}\sigma^{2}(x,\omega^{\prime},\omega,E)\psi(x,\omega^{\prime},E)d\omega^{\prime}

(4)

\displaystyle+\int_{I^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sigma^{3}(x,E^{\prime},E)\psi(x,\gamma(E^{\prime},E,\omega)(s),E^{\prime})dsdE^{\prime}.

The function
f=f(x,\omega,E)
in (
1
) represents the internal source.
and
g=g(y,\omega,E)
in (
2
) represents the boundary source.
The solution

of the problem (
1
), (
1
), (
3
)
describes the fluence of the considered particle.

In

The starting point is that differential cross sections for charged particles may contain hyper-singularities with respect to energy variable and hence the corresponding exact (original) collision operator is a partial
hyper-singular integral operator

2.1.
Basic function spaces

All function spaces below are real valued.
Define the space
W^{2}(G\times S\times I)
by

(9)

\displaystyle W^{2}(G\times S\times I)=\{\psi\in L^{2}(G\times S\times I)\ |\ \omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi\in L^{2}(G\times S\times I)\}.

The space
W^{2}(G\times S\times I)
is equipped with the inner product

(10)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\psi},v\right\rangle_{W^{2}(G\times S\times I)}=\left\langle{\psi},v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(G\times S\times I)}+\left\langle\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi,\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(G\times S\times I)}.

Then
W^{2}(G\times S\times I)
is a Hilbert space.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space.
The space

denotes the standard

\displaystyle H^{k}(I,X)=\{\psi|\ {{\partial^{l}\psi}\over{\partial E^{l}}}\in X,\ l\leq k\}

and
H^{k}(I,X)
is equipped with the standard inner product.
Let for
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle C^{k}(\overline{G}\times S\times I):=\{\psi\in C^{k}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})|\ \psi=f_{|G\times S\times I^{\circ}},\ f\in C_{0}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times S\times\mathbb{R})\}.

The space
C^{1}(\overline{G}\times S\times I)

is a dense subspace of
W^{2}(G\times S\times I)
and

.

The space of

-functions on

with respect to the measure

|\omega\cdot\nu|\ d\sigma d\omega dE
is denoted by

that is,
T^{2}(\Gamma_{-})=L^{2}(\Gamma_{-},|\omega\cdot\nu|\ d\sigma d\omega dE)
.

T^{2}(\Gamma_{-})
is a Hilbert space and its natural inner product is

(11)

\displaystyle\left\langle g_{1},g_{2}\right\rangle_{T^{2}(\Gamma_{-})}=\int_{\Gamma_{-}}g_{1}(y,\omega,E)g_{2}(y,\omega,E)|\omega\cdot\nu|\ d\sigma(y)d\omega dE.

In patches

the inner product (

\displaystyle\left\langle g_{1},g_{2}\right\rangle_{T^{2}(\Gamma_{-}^{i})}

\displaystyle=\int_{S\times I}\int_{U_{i,-,\omega}}g_{1}(h_{i}(z),\omega,E)g_{2}(h_{i}(z),\omega,E)|\omega\cdot\nu(h_{i}(z))\left\|(\partial_{z_{1}}h_{i}\times\partial_{z_{2}}h_{i})(z)\right\|dzd\omega dE

and

when
(U_{i},\varphi_{i})
is the corresponding chart of
(\partial G)_{i}
.

The spaces
T^{2}(\Gamma_{+})
and

and their inner products are similarly defined.
In addition, we define the (Hilbert) space

\displaystyle T_{\tau_{\pm}}^{2}(\Gamma_{\pm})=L^{2}(\Gamma_{\pm},\tau_{\pm}(.,.)|\omega\cdot\nu|\ d\sigma d\omega dE)

where the canonical inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle g_{1},g_{2}\right\rangle_{T_{\tau_{\pm}}^{2}(\Gamma_{\pm})}=\int_{\Gamma_{\pm}}g_{1}(y,\omega,E)g_{2}(y,\omega,E)\tau_{\pm}(y,\omega)|\omega\cdot\nu|\ d\sigma(y)d\omega dE

is used.

Since
\tau_{\pm}(y,\omega)\leq{\rm diam}(G)=d<\infty
we obtain
from
[

]
, p. 252,
[

]

]
, Theorem 2.16

Theorem 2.1

The inflow trace operators

\displaystyle\gamma_{\pm}:{W}^{2}(G\times S\times I)\to T_{\tau_{\pm}}^{2}(\Gamma_{\pm})\ {\rm defined\ by}\ \gamma_{\pm}(\psi):=\psi_{|\Gamma_{\pm}}

are (well-defined) bounded surjective operators and they have bounded right inverses

that is,

Remark 2.2

The lift

can be given explicitly, for instance by

\displaystyle(L_{-}g)(x,\omega,E)=e^{-\lambda t(x,\omega)}g(x-t(x,\omega)\omega,\omega,E)

where

. For

Because for any compact

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{2}(G\times S\times I)=\{\psi\in{W}^{2}(G\times S\times I)|\ \gamma(\psi)\in T^{2}(\Gamma)\}.

The space

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi,v\right\rangle_{\widetilde{W}^{2}(G\times S\times I)}=\left\langle\psi,v\right\rangle_{{W}^{2}(G\times S\times I)}+\left\langle\gamma(\psi),\gamma(v)\right\rangle_{T^{2}(\Gamma)}.

For

\displaystyle\int_{G\times S\times I}(\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi)v\ dxd\omega dE+\int_{G\times S\times I}(\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}v)\psi\ dxd\omega dE=\int_{\partial G\times S\times I}(\omega\cdot\nu)v\ \psi\ d\sigma d\omega dE

2.2.
Anisotropic Sobolev spaces

Let
m=(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
be a multi-index (for simplicity we restrict ourselves here to integer multi-indexes but the definitions below can be generalized for
s=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\in[0,\infty[^{3}
in the standard way).
Define an
anisotropic ( or mixed-norm) Sobolev spaces

H^{m}(G\times S\times I^{\circ}):=H^{m,2}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
by

\displaystyle H^{m}(G\times S\times I^{\circ}):=\Big\{\psi\in L^{2}(G\times S\times I)\ |\ \partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{{\omega}}^{\beta}\partial_{E}^{l}\psi\in L^{2}(G\times S\times I),

(15)

\displaystyle{\rm for\ all}\ |\alpha|\leq m_{1},\ |\beta|\leq m_{2},\ l\leq m_{3}\Big\}.

Here we denote more shortly
{{\partial^{\alpha}}\over{\partial x^{\alpha}}}=\partial_{x}^{\alpha}

and similarly
\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}=\partial_{\omega_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\partial_{\omega_{2}}^{\beta_{2}}

where
\{\partial_{\omega_{j}},\ j=1,2\}
is a local basis of the tangent space
T(S)
.
We recall that for sufficiently smooth functions
f:S\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\omega_{j}}}_{\Big|\omega}={\partial\over{\partial w_{j}}}(f\circ h)_{\Big|u=h^{-1}(\omega)},\ j=1,2

where
h:W\to S\setminus S_{0}
is a parametrization of
S\setminus S_{0}
where
S_{0}
has the surface measure zero.
Moreover, recall that
f\in L^{2}(S)
if and only if
f\circ h\in L^{2}(W,\left\|\partial_{1}h\times\partial_{2}h\right\|du)
.
For
f\in L^{2}(S)
we define the inner product

(16)

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(S)}=\int_{S}f_{1}f_{2}d\omega=\int_{W}(f_{1}\circ h)(f_{2}\circ h)\left\|\partial_{1}h\times\partial_{2}h\right\|du.

The space
H^{m}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
is a Hilbert space when equipped with the inner product

(17)

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi,v\right\rangle_{H^{m}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m_{1},|\beta|\leq m_{2},l\leq m_{3}}\left\langle\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}\partial_{E}^{l}\psi,\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}\partial_{E}^{l}v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(G\times S\times I)}.

The corresponding norm is

\displaystyle\left\|\psi\right\|_{H^{m}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})}=\Big(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m_{1}}\sum_{|\beta|\leq m_{2}}\sum_{l\leq m_{3}}\left\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\omega}^{\beta}\partial_{E}^{l}\psi\right\|_{L^{2}(G\times S\times I)}^{2}\Big)^{{1\over 2}}.

In
L^{2}(\partial G)
we define an inner product analogously to (
16
)
that is,

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial G)}=\int_{\partial G}f_{1}f_{2}d\sigma.

The definition of spaces
H^{m}((\partial G)\times S\times I^{\circ})
and their
inner products are analogous to that of
H^{m}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
.

Note that for
m^{\prime}\geq m

5.3.
A note on regularity based on the smoothing property of restricted collision operator

The so called smoothing property of restricted collision operator can be applied in regularity analysis. We outline shortly the idea below.

Let

,

and let

. Recall that for an open set

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{H^{s}(G)}^{2}=\left\|u\right\|_{H^{m}(G)}^{2}+\sum_{|\alpha|=m}\int_{G}\int_{G}{{|(\partial^{\alpha}u)(x)-(\partial^{\alpha}u)(y)|^{2}}\over{|x-y|^{3+2\kappa}}}dxdy.

\displaystyle+\sum_{|\alpha|=m}\int_{I}\int_{S}\int_{G}\int_{G}{{|(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\psi)(x,\omega,E)-(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\psi)(y,\omega,E)|^{2}}\over{|x-y|^{3+2\kappa}}}dxdyd\omega dE<\infty

In some cases the transport problem in the so called rectified coordinates is useful.

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(z,\omega,E):=g(h^{-1}(z),\omega,E).

We see that the problem is again of variable multiplicity.

6.2.
On tangential regularity

The solutions of the characteristic initial boundary value problems do not have necessarily full Sobolev regularity (e.g.
[
39
, Examples 2.1, 2.2]
). However, the problems whose boundary matrix has a constant rank (problems with constant multiplicity) the so called tangential (or co-normal) Sobolev regularity can be shown under relevant assumptions (e.g.
[
43
]
,
[
38
]
). For the problems with variable multiplicity even the tangential regularity need not be valid but for some problems it may be possible.
In the sequel we formulate some details of tangential regularity concerning for transport problems under consideration.

A smooth vector field
F=\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}:\overline{G}\times S\times I\to T(\mathbb{R}^{3})=\mathbb{R}^{3}
is
tangential (or co-normal) with respect to
\Gamma=(\partial G)\times S\times I
if (here
a:=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
)

\displaystyle\left\langle a,\nu\right\rangle=0\ {\rm on}\ \Gamma.

Similarly, we define tangential (or co-normal) vector fields with respect to
\Gamma_{\pm}
and
tangential (or co-normal) vector fields
F:\overline{D}_{+,a,b}\times S\times I\to T(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with respect to
D_{0,a,b}\times S\times I
.

We find that

\{\partial_{z_{1}},\partial_{z_{2}},z_{3}\partial_{z_{3}}\}
forms a basis for the space of tangential vector fields
F:\eta\in\overline{D}_{+,a,b}\times S\times I\to T(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with respect to
D_{0,a,b}\times S\times I
.
This follows from the fact that for any smooth
\eta\in\overline{D}_{+,a,b}\times S\times I
obeying
\eta(z_{1},z_{2},0,\omega,E)=0

we have

\displaystyle\eta(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\omega,E)=z_{3}\int_{0}^{1}{\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial z_{3}}}(z_{1},z_{2},tz_{3},\omega,E)dt.

This implies that the space of tangential vector fields with respect to
\Gamma
has a natural local basis (near
\Gamma
) as well which can be seen as follows.
We find that for
\phi=\psi\circ h^{-1}
and for
j=1,2

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z_{j}}}=\left\langle(\nabla_{x}\psi)\circ h^{-1},{\frac{\partial(h^{-1})}{\partial z_{j}}}\right\rangle.

Let
A_{h}
and
A_{h^{-1}}
be the Jacobian matrices of
h
and
h^{-1}
, respectively.
Since

\displaystyle A_{h^{-1}}=\left(\begin{matrix}{\frac{\partial(h^{-1})}{\partial z_{1}}}&{\frac{\partial(h^{-1})}{\partial z_{2}}}&{\frac{\partial(h^{-1})}{\partial z_{3}}}\\
\end{matrix}\right)=A_{h}^{-1}\circ h^{-1}=(({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1}\circ h^{-1})\left(\begin{matrix}A_{1}&A_{2}&A_{3}\\
\end{matrix}\right)\circ h^{-1}

where

\displaystyle A_{1}=\left(\begin{matrix}\partial_{2}h_{2}\partial_{3}h_{3}-\partial_{2}h_{3}\partial_{3}h_{2}\\
\partial_{1}h_{2}\partial_{3}h_{3}-\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{3}h_{2}\\
\partial_{1}h_{2}\partial_{2}h_{3}-\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{2}h_{2}\\
\end{matrix}\right),A_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}\partial_{3}h_{1}\partial_{2}h_{3}-\partial_{3}h_{3}\partial_{2}h_{1}\\
\partial_{1}h_{1}\partial_{3}h_{3}-\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{3}h_{1}\\
\partial_{2}h_{1}\partial_{1}h_{3}-\partial_{2}h_{3}\partial_{1}h_{1}\\
\end{matrix}\right),A_{3}=\left(\begin{matrix}\partial_{2}h_{1}\partial_{3}h_{2}-\partial_{2}h_{2}\partial_{3}h_{1}\\
\partial_{3}h_{1}\partial_{1}h_{2}-\partial_{3}h_{2}\partial_{1}h_{1}\\
\partial_{1}h_{1}\partial_{2}h_{2}-\partial_{1}h_{2}\partial_{2}h_{1}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

we see that

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial(h^{-1})}{\partial z_{j}}}=(({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1}\circ h^{-1})A_{j}\circ h^{-1}.

Hence

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z_{j}}}=(({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1}\circ h^{-1})\left\langle(\nabla_{x}\psi)\circ h^{-1},A_{j}\circ h^{-1}\right\rangle

(223)

\displaystyle=(({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1}\circ h^{-1})\left\langle\nabla_{x}\psi,A_{j}\right\rangle\circ h^{-1}.

Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\nu=-{{\nabla_{x}h_{3}}\over{\left\|\nabla_{x}h_{3}\right\|}}

we see that on
\Gamma

\displaystyle-\left\|\nabla_{x}h_{3}\right\|\left\langle\nu,A_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\big(\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{2}h_{2}\partial_{3}h_{3}-\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{2}h_{3}\partial_{3}h_{2}+\partial_{2}h_{3}\partial_{1}h_{2}\partial_{3}h_{3}-\partial_{2}h_{3}\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{3}h_{2}

(224)

\displaystyle+\partial_{3}h_{3}\partial_{1}h_{2}\partial_{2}h_{3}-\partial_{3}h_{3}\partial_{1}h_{3}\partial_{2}h_{2}\big)=0.

Similarly

(225)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{x}h_{3}\right\|\left\langle\nu,A_{2}\right\rangle=0

on
\Gamma

and so
F_{j}:=({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1}\left\langle A_{j},\nabla_{x}\right\rangle,\ j=1,2
are tangential vector fields with respect to
\Gamma
.
Finally, we analogously find that

(226)

\displaystyle z_{3}{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z_{3}}}=(h_{3}(({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1})\circ h^{-1})\left\langle\nabla_{x}\psi,A_{3}\right\rangle\circ h^{-1}.

Since
h_{3}(y)=0
on
\Gamma
we see that
also
F_{3}:=h_{3}({\rm det}(A_{h}))^{-1}\left\langle A_{3},\nabla_{x}\right\rangle
is a tangential vector field with respect to
\Gamma
.
The above computations show that
\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\}:=\{\left\langle A_{1},\nabla_{x}\right\rangle,\ \left\langle A_{2},\nabla_{x}\right\rangle,\ h_{3}\left\langle A_{3},\nabla_{x}\right\rangle\}
forms (near
\Gamma
) a local basis of tangential vector fields with respect to
\Gamma
.

We define

\displaystyle H^{(m,0,0)}(G\times S\times I^{\circ};\Gamma):=L^{2}(S\times I,H^{m}(G;\Gamma))

\displaystyle:=\{\psi\in L^{2}(G\times S\times I)|\ F^{\alpha}\psi\in L^{2}(G\times S\times I)\ {\rm for}\ |\alpha|\leq m\}

where
F\alpha:=F_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}F_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}F_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}
and
\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\}
is a local basis of tangential vector fields with respect to
\Gamma
.
Similarly we define tangential Sobolev spaces
H^{(m,0,0)}(G\times S\times I^{\circ};\Gamma_{\pm})
and the tangential Sobolev spaces
H^{(m,0,0)}(D_{+,a,b,}\times S\times I^{\circ};D_{a,b,0}\times S\times I)
.
Note that
H^{(m,0,0)}(G\times S\times I^{\circ};\Gamma)\subset L^{2}(S\times I,H^{m}_{\rm loc}(G))
.
Moreover, we find that
\psi\in H^{(m,0,0)}(G\times S\times I^{\circ};\Gamma)

if and only if locally

(227)

\displaystyle\left\|Z^{\alpha}(\psi\circ h^{-1})\right\|_{L^{2}(D_{+,a,b}\times S\times I)}<\infty\ {\rm for\ all}\ |\alpha|\leq m

where
Z^{\alpha}:=\partial_{z_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{z_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}(z_{3}\partial_{z_{3}})^{\alpha_{3}}
for
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
.
The condition (
227
)means that locally
\phi=\psi\circ h^{-1}\in H^{(m,0,0)}(D_{+,a,b,}\times S\times I^{\circ};D_{0,a,b}\times S\times I)
.

H^{(m,0,0)}(G\times S\times I^{\circ};\Gamma)
can be equipped with a natural norm by glueing together local patches. Actually, it can be equipped with an inner product and it is a Hilbert space.
We omit details.

Lemma 6.3

Let
G=B(0,1)
and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
q>0
. The integral

(228)

\displaystyle\int_{G\times S}{{(\omega\cdot x)^{k}}\over{((\omega\cdot x)^{2}+1-|x|^{2})^{q}}}d\omega dx

convergences if and only if
k-2q>-3
.

Proof.

Noting that
\nu(y)=y,\ \tau_{-}(y,\omega)=2|\omega\cdot y|
and that on
\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\omega\cdot(y+t\omega))^{2}+1-|y+t\omega|^{2}=(\omega\cdot y)^{2}

we have by Lemma
4.1

\displaystyle\int_{G\times S}{{(\omega\cdot x)^{k}}\over{((\omega\cdot x)^{2}+1-|x|^{2})^{q}}}d\omega dx=\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{2|\omega\cdot y|}{{(\omega\cdot(y+t\omega))^{k}}\over{((\omega\cdot(y+t\omega))^{2}+1-|y+t\omega|^{2})^{q}}}|\omega\cdot y|dtd\sigma(y)d\omega

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{2|\omega\cdot y|}{{(\omega\cdot y+t)^{k}}\over{(\omega\cdot y)^{2q}}}|\omega\cdot y|dtd\sigma(y)d\omega

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{2|\omega\cdot y|}{{(\omega\cdot y)^{j}t^{k-j}}\over{(\omega\cdot y)^{2q}}}|\omega\cdot y|dtd\sigma(y)d\omega

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}{1\over{k-j+1}}\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}{{(\omega\cdot y)^{j}(2|\omega\cdot y|)^{k-j+1}}\over{(\omega\cdot y)^{2q}}}|\omega\cdot y|d\sigma(y)d\omega

(229)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}{1\over{k-j+1}}2^{k-j+1}(-1)^{j-2q}\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}|\omega\cdot y|^{k-2q+2}d\sigma(y)d\omega

where we additionally used that
|\omega\cdot y|=-\omega\cdot y
on
\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}
.

Consider the integral of the form
\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}|\omega\cdot y|^{s}d\sigma(y)d\omega
.
Let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-,\omega}^{\prime}:=\{y\in\partial G|\ \omega\cdot y<0\},\ S_{\tau,\omega}:=\{y\in\partial G|\ \omega\cdot y=\tau\}.

Note that each
\Gamma_{-,\omega}
is a hemisphere and
S_{\tau,\omega}
is a circle whose radius is
\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}
. Moreover, let
d\ell_{\tau,\omega}
be the differential path length on
S_{\tau,\omega}
.
In virtue of the Fubin’s Theorem
(see
[
48
]
, section 3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}|\omega\cdot y|^{s}d\sigma(y)d\omega=\int_{S}\int_{\Gamma_{-,\omega}}|\omega\cdot y|^{s}d\sigma(y)d\omega=\int_{S}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{S_{\tau,\omega}}|\omega\cdot y|^{s}d\ell_{\tau,\omega}(y){1\over{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}}d\tau d\omega

(230)

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{S_{\tau,\omega}}\tau^{s}{1\over{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}}d\ell_{\tau,\omega}(y)d\tau d\omega=2\pi\int_{S}\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{s}\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}{1\over{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}}d\tau d\omega=8\pi\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{s}d\tau

and so the integral
\int_{\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}}|\omega\cdot y|^{s}d\sigma(y)d\omega
convergences if and only if
s>-1
.
Hence the integral (
6.2
) convergences if and only if

\displaystyle k-2q+2>-1\Leftrightarrow k-2q>-3

as desired.
∎

Remark 6.4

In the previous lemma it is not essential that
k\in\mathbb{N}
since the integral

\int_{0}^{2|\omega\cdot y|}(\omega\cdot y+t)^{k}dt
can be computed for any

. However, the computations above are sufficient for our needs. Especially we find that
the integral
\int_{G\times S}{1\over{((\omega\cdot x)^{2}+1-|x|^{2})^{q}}}d\omega dx
convergences if and only if

. Since
|\omega\cdot y+t|\leq|\omega\cdot y|+t
we also see by the above proof that the integral

\int_{G\times S}{{|\omega\cdot x|^{k}}\over{((\omega\cdot x)^{2}+1-|x|^{2})^{q}}}d\omega dx

convergences if
k-2q>-3
.

We need the following spaces. Using the notations of

]

Consider the following homogeneous inflow boundary values transport problem

\displaystyle\omega^{*}\ ({\rm Hess}\ r(x))\ \omega\ {\rm is\ negative}\ {\rm for}\ (x,\omega)\in\Gamma_{0}.

Using again the notations by

\displaystyle\Omega:=G\times S,\ r(x,\omega):=r(x),\ h(x,\omega)=h_{\pm}(x,\omega):=(\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}r)(x),

\displaystyle M(x,\omega):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}&{\rm on}\ \Gamma_{+}\cup\Gamma_{0}\\

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\omega_{k}\partial_{k}(\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}r)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\omega_{k}\omega_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{j}r=\omega^{*}\ ({\rm Hess}\ r(x))\ \omega.

These methods require further study but we conjecture the following regularity result:

Assume that

(239)

\displaystyle-a(x,E_{0})\geq q_{3}>0,\ -a(x,E_{m})\geq q_{3}>0,\ {\rm a.e.}

7.
Remarks on transport operators from microlocal perspective

Transport operator interpreted as a pseudo-differential operator
.
Consider the equation

\displaystyle T\psi:=-a(x,E){\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial E}}

\displaystyle+c(x,E)\Delta_{S}\psi+d(x,\omega,E)\cdot\nabla_{S}\psi

(243)

\displaystyle+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+\Sigma(x,\omega,E)\psi-K_{r}\psi=f

where we assume (for simplicity) that the restricted collision operator
K_{r}
is of the form

\displaystyle(K_{r}\psi)(x,\omega,E)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{\prime}}\int_{I^{\prime}}\sigma^{1}(x,\omega^{\prime},\omega,E^{\prime},E)\psi(x,\omega^{\prime},E^{\prime})dE^{\prime}d\omega^{\prime}

(244)

\displaystyle+\int_{S^{\prime}}\sigma^{2}(x,\omega^{\prime},\omega,E)\psi(x,\omega^{\prime},E)d\omega^{\prime}=:(K_{r}^{1}\psi)(x,\omega,E)+(K_{r}^{2}\psi)(x,\omega,E).

Denote more shortly
U:=G\times S\times I^{\circ},\ z:=(x,\omega,E)\in U
. Let
\zeta:=(\xi,\eta,\tau)
be the cotangent variable on
T_{z}^{*}(U)
.
Assuming that the cross-sections

\sigma^{1},\ \sigma^{2}
obey certain criteria the partial integral operator
K_{r}
can be expressed as a sum
K_{r}=K_{r}(z,D)+R
where
K_{r}(z,D)
is a pseudo-differential operator
whose symbol, say
k_{r}(z,\zeta)
, belongs to the
classical symbol class

S_{1,0}^{0}(U)
and
R
is a smoothing operator (cf.
[
27
, Chapter 7]
). In the sequel the operator
R
is meaningless and so we omit it.
Hence assuming that the coefficients are smooth the operator
T
is a pseudo-differential operator
T(z,D)
on
U
whose symbol

\displaystyle t_{0}(z,\zeta)={\rm i}\ a(x,E)\tau-c(x,E)\sum_{i,j}^{2}g^{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}-{\rm i}\ d(z)\cdot\eta-{\rm i}\ (\omega\cdot\xi)+\Sigma(z)-k_{r}(z,\zeta)

belongs to the class
S_{1,0}^{2}(U)
where

g^{ij}
are the elements of the inverse matrix

\big(\left\langle{{\partial}\over{\partial\omega_{i}}}_{|\omega},{{\partial}\over{\partial\omega_{j}}}_{|\omega}\right\rangle\big)^{-1}.

The principal symbol of

T(z,D)
is

\displaystyle t_{0}(z,\zeta)=-c(x,E)\sum_{i,j}^{2}g^{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}

or

\displaystyle t_{0}(z,\zeta)={\rm i}\ a(x,E)\tau-{\rm i}\ d(z)\cdot\eta-{\rm i}\ (\omega\cdot\xi)

depending on whether
c\equiv 0
or not.
We remark that the principal symbol
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
is well-defined on the cotangent bundle

T^{*}(U)
(that is, it is defined in the coordinate free way). (Above
\xi_{j}=(\xi\cdot dx)(\partial_{x_{j}}),\ \eta_{j}=(\eta\cdot d\omega)(\partial_{\omega_{j}}),\ \tau=(\tau\cdot dE)(\partial_{E})
for
\zeta\cdot dz=\xi\cdot dx+\eta\cdot d\omega+\tau dE\in T^{*}(U)
).
Denote the differential part by
P(z,D\psi:=-a{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial E}}+c\Delta_{S}\psi+d\cdot\nabla_{S}\psi+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+\Sigma\psi
. Then
T(z,D)=P(z,D-K_{r}(z,D)
. We find that
P(z,D)
is properly supported (as a partial differential operator). Moreover,
K_{r}(z.D)
is properly supported if
K_{r}^{1}=0
.

Wavefront set in the interior
.
The transport problems have a limited regularity of solutions (in spite of regularity of data and domains). There are some general methods along which one is able to study the formation and location of singularities. One of them is the microlocal study of distributional solutions. The basic concepts of microlocal analysis are the wavefront sets and propagation of singularities along bicharacteristic curves. We shortly recall some basics of them.

To start with we define the wavefront set in the case where the domain, say
V
, is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}(V)\sim V\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\ \xi_{0}\not=0
.
An open neighbourhood
\mathcal{C}_{\xi_{0}}\subset T_{x_{0}}^{*}(V)
of
\xi_{0}
is called a
conical neighbourhood
if
\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\xi_{0}}
implies
that
t\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\xi_{0}}
for all
t>0
.
The
C^{\infty}

wavefront set
WF(u)
of a distribution
u\in D^{\prime}(V)
is the closed subset of
T^{*}(V)
defined (via its complement) as follows (e.g.
[
24
]
,

[
42
]
and
[
15
]
(where a lot of results regarding to microlocal analysis and its consequences are exposed mainly without proofs):

Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}(V),\ \xi_{0}\not=0
. Then
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
is not in
WF(u)

if and only if there exists a function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(V)
for which
\varphi(x_{0})\not=0
and a conical neighbourhood
\mathcal{C}_{\xi_{0}}
of
\xi_{0}
, such that for any
N>0
there exists
C_{N}>0
such that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(\varphi u)(\xi)|\leq C_{N}(1+|\xi|)^{-N},\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\xi_{0}}

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform.

One of the equivalent definitions of wavefront sets is:

(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}(V),\ \xi_{0}\not=0
is not in
WF(u)
if and only if there exists
a symbol
a(x,\xi)\in S_{1,0}^{m}(V)
such that
a_{0}(x,\xi)\not=0
and
a(x,D)(\varphi u)\in C^{\infty}(V)
where

a_{0}(x,\xi)
is the principal symbol of
a(x,\xi)
and
\varphi
is as in the above definition.

A distribution
on a manifold (like on
U
) can be defined in various (coordinate free) ways.
The definition of the wavefront set
WF(\psi)\subset T^{*}(U)
in the case where the domain is the manifold

U=G\times S\times I^{\circ}
can be reverted to the above definition by using pullbacks along coordinate charts. The other possibility is to employ the equivalent definition noted above after the definition of
WF(u)\subset T^{*}(V)
.
We omit exact definitions.

It can be shown that

(245)

\displaystyle\pi({WF}(\psi))={\rm sing\ supp}(\psi)

where
\pi
is the natural projection
\pi:T^{*}(U)\to U
and
{\rm sing\ supp}(\psi)
is the singular support of
\psi
(that is, the complement of the open set where
\psi
is
C^{\infty}
).

The
characteristic set (or variety)
of
T:=T(z,D)
is

\displaystyle{\rm char}(T):=\{(z,\zeta\cdot dz)\in T^{*}(U)|\ t_{0}(z,\zeta)=0\}.

In the case where
T(z,D)
is properly supported
T(z,D)\psi
is defined for any
\psi\in D^{\prime}(U)
(recall that
T(z,D)
is properly supported if e.g.
K_{r}^{1}=0
).
Assuming that
T(z,D)
is properly supported or
\psi\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(U)
(here
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(U)
is the space of distributions with compact support) the basic result is

(246)

\displaystyle WF(T\psi)\subset WF(\psi)\subset WF(f)\cup{\rm char}(T)

where
f:=T\psi
.
The inclusions (
246
) tell us especially that possible wavefront points (that is, microlocal singularities) of the distributional solutions
\psi
of the equation
T\psi=f
lie on
{\rm char}(T)
when
f
is smooth.

The
bicharacteristic curve
of
T(z,D)
emanating from
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})
is the solution
\alpha=(z,\zeta):\Delta\to{\rm char}(T)
of the Hamiltonian system of differential equations which in local coordinates is (here
z^{\prime}={{dz}\over{ds}},\ \zeta^{\prime}={{d\zeta}\over{ds}}
)

(247)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&z^{\prime}=\nabla_{\zeta}t_{0}(z,\zeta)\\
&\zeta^{\prime}=-\nabla_{z}t_{0}(z,\zeta),\\
&(z(0),\zeta(0))=(z_{0},\zeta_{0})\end{cases}.

The solutions curves
\alpha(s)=(z(s),\zeta(s)),\ s\in\Delta
of (
247
) give the Hamiltonian flows that is, integral curves of the Hamiltonian vector field

H_{t_{0}}:=\nabla_{\zeta}t_{0}\cdot{{\partial}\over{\partial z}}-\nabla_{z}t_{0}\cdot{{\partial}\over{\partial\zeta}}
with
t_{0}
.
The projection curves
\tilde{\alpha}=\pi\circ\alpha:\Delta\to U
are called
characteristic curves
.

The operator
T(z,D)
is of
principal type
if its principal symbol
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
is homogeneous in
\zeta
and
\nabla_{\zeta}(z,\zeta)\not=0,\ \zeta\not=0
on
{\rm char}(T)
.

Suppose that
T(z,D)
is properly supported, principal type and that
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
is real valued. Then
the bicharacteristic curve preserves microlocal singularities in the following sense:

Suppose
that
\psi\in D^{\prime}(U)
is a distributional solution of

T(z,D)\psi=f.

Furthermore, assume that
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})\in WF(\psi)\setminus WF(f)
. Then the whole bicharacteristic curve
\alpha(s)=(z(s),\zeta(s)),\ s\in\Delta
through
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})
is included in

WF(\psi)\setminus WF(f)
(that is, possible the singularity
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})
propagates along the bicharacteristic curve when
f
is smooth).
This result holds without the assumption ’
T(z,D)
is properly supported’ if
\psi
has a compact support in
U
.

The above definition gives the notion of
C^{\infty}
wave front set.
There exists also other type of wavefront sets such as
Sobolev wave front sets
.
Relevant function spaces in our case would be the anisotropic Sobolev spaces

H^{(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
(given in section
2.2
above).
Basic results for Sobolev wavefront sets have counterparts to the above formulations.

Wavefront set up to the boundary
.
Consider the initial boundary value problem

(248)

\displaystyle T(z,D)\psi=f,\ {B\psi}_{|\partial U}=g,\ \psi(.,.,E_{\rm m})=0

where
B
is a partial differential boundary operator.
We assume that
\partial U
is non-characteristic for
T(z,D)
that is,

t_{0}(z,n(z))\not=0
for
z\in(\partial U)_{r}
where
n(z)
is the unit normal vector on the regular part
(\partial U)_{r}
of
\partial U
.
When the boundary values are included the micro-localization must be studied on the so called
(compressed)
b
-cotangent bundle
(e.g.

[
33
]
,

[
57
, sections 1 and 2]
,
[
42
]
).
Omitting all details we only mention that the
b
-cotangent bundle
{}^{b}T^{*}(\overline{U})
is the dual bundle of the bundle, say
{}^{b}T(\overline{U})
, formed by the tangential vector fields.

A distribution
\psi\in D^{\prime}(U)
is
extendable
if it can be extended to
a distribution
\tilde{\psi}\in D^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times S\times\mathbb{R})
and if
\tilde{\psi}_{|\partial U}\in D^{\prime}(\partial U)
is well-defined.
The relevant microlocal concept to analyse boundary value problems
is the so called
C^{\infty}

(compressed) b-wavefront set

{}^{b}WF(\psi)
of an extendable distribution
\psi\in D^{\prime}(U)
. We omit its definition but remark that

{}^{b}WF(\psi)\subset(T^{*}(U)\setminus\{0\})\cup(T^{*}(\partial U)\setminus\{0\}),\ T^{*}(\overline{U})\subset\ ^{b}T^{*}(\overline{U}).

Let
\iota
be the natural imbedding
T^{*}(\overline{U})\to\ ^{b}T^{*}(\overline{U})
. Suppose that
T(z,D)
is properly supported.
The basic result is that

{}^{b}WF(\psi)\subset\ ^{b}WF(f)\cup WF(g)\cup\widetilde{\rm char}(T)

where

\widetilde{\rm char}(T):=\iota({\rm char}(T))

and
f=T\psi
.

Suppose that
T
is properly supported, principal type,
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
is real valued
and that data is smooth. Then wavefront singularities propagate along the so called
broken bicharacteristic curve
.
The broken bicharacteristic curve
\alpha:\Delta\to\widetilde{\rm char}(T)
obeys a kind of broken Hamiltonian system.
In the interior
U
the requirement for the broken bicharacteristic curve reduces to (
247
). When the bicharacteristic curve encounters the boundary
\partial U
it may reflect or retract back to
U
or it may glide on the boundary.
For example, the geometric nature of reflection and gliding are different and so one needs the concept of a broken bicharacteristic curve.

The above
standard assumptions concerning for microlocal analysis are not necessarily valid for all transport problems. For example,

A. The boundary
\Gamma=(\partial G)\times S\times I^{\circ}
(and hence either
\partial U
) is not generally non-characteristic for the transport operator
T(z,D)
. This implies (among others) that
\iota({\rm char}(T))\not\subset\ ^{b}T^{*}(U)

where
\iota
is the imbedding given above.
That is why the
b
-cotangent bundles
{}^{b}T^{*}(U)

are not necessarily sufficient structures to analyse inflow boundary value problems. For example,
\Gamma
is not non-characteristic for

T(z,D)=\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\Sigma-K_{r}
since
t_{0}(z,\nu(x))=-{\rm i}\ \omega\cdot\nu(x)=0
when
\omega\perp\nu(x)
. Because
{\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\xi_{j}}}={\rm i}\omega_{j}
and
\left\|\omega\right\|=1
we see that
T(z,D)
is principal type.
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
is not real-valued.

B.
The transport operators are not necessarily principal type. This implies that the principal part operator, say
T_{0}(z,D)
, does not dominate lower order pseudo-differential operators. In addition, the Hamiltonian flow may trap in a compact set
A\subset U
.
For example,
the principal part of the operator
T(z,D)=-a{{\partial}\over{\partial E}}+c\Delta_{S}+d\cdot\nabla_{S}+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\Sigma-K_{r}
is
t_{0}(z,\zeta)=-c\sum_{i,j}^{2}g^{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}
. So,
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
is real-valued. Furthermore,
{\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\xi_{j}}}={\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\tau}}=0,\ j=1,2,3
and
({\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\eta_{1}}},({\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\eta_{2}}})=-c(2g^{11}\eta_{1}+g^{21}\eta_{2}+g^{12}\eta_{2},2g^{22}\eta_{2}+g^{12}\eta_{1}+g^{21}\eta_{1})
. Hence
T(z,D)
is not principal type since
{\rm char}(T)=\{(\xi,\eta,\tau)|\ \eta=0\}
and so
{\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\xi_{j}}}={\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\tau}}={\frac{\partial t_{0}}{\partial\eta_{j}}}=0
on

{\rm char}(T)
.
There exists variant conditions instead of principal type property. For instance, they assume that the differential
dt_{0}=(\nabla_{z}t_{0})\cdot dz+(\nabla_{\zeta}t_{0})\cdot d\zeta
and the fundamental 1-form
\zeta\cdot dz=\xi\cdot dx+\eta\cdot d\omega+\tau dE
are linearly independent on
{\rm char}(T)
.
They are not necessarily valid for transport problems as well.

The assumption that
t_{0}(z,\zeta)
must be real-valued can be in some cases avoided by considering
-{\rm i}T(z,D)
instead of
T(z,D)
.
For example, the principal symbol of

T(z,D)\psi=-{{\partial}\over{\partial E}}+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\Sigma
is
t_{0}(z,\zeta)={\rm i}\tau-{\rm i}(\omega\cdot\xi)
. The principal symbol of
-{\rm i}T(z,D)
is
t_{0}(z,\zeta)=-\tau+\omega\cdot\xi
which is real-valued.
By computing a bicharacteristic curve of the operator
-{\rm i}T(z,D)

and its projection along
U

we see that the corresponding characteristic curve is

\tilde{\alpha}(s)=(x_{0}-\omega_{0}s,\omega_{0},E_{0}+s),

as wellknown.

Finally we remark that emerging and propagation of singularities can be studied by using different methods. For example, in
[
30
]
one has considered (without microlocal methods) the
propagation of discontinuities
along the characteristic curves in non-convex domains. Moreover, one shows therein that the discontinuities emanate at the non-convex part of the (grazing) boundary
\Gamma
.

8.
Discussion

In the global case
G=\mathbb{R}^{3}
the regularity of solutions of initial value transport problems may increase according to data (see
[
53
]
). In the case where
G
is not the whole space the relevant transport problems contain the inflow boundary condition. The corresponding initial boundary value problem is characteristic. It is well-known from the general viewpoint of the first order PDE-systems that solutions of these kind of initial boundary value problems may posses only limited regularity. In the case of initial inflow boundary value transport problems the limit of regularity with respect to
x
(and
\omega
-variables) seems to be
3/2
(see Example
4.11
above). In the case where the transport equation does not contain the slowing down term
a{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial E}}
the regularity with respect to
E
-variable is not necessarily limited (see section
4.1.3
above).

The regularity results exposed in this paper may be largely generalized for fractional indexes. The relevant spaces within which the results can be formulated are then fractional anisotropic Sobolev-Slobodevskij spaces
H^{(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
(cf. Remark
4.10
above).
Under relevant assumptions the solution of the problem
(
1
), (
2
), (
3
)
lies in
H^{(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
for certain indexes
s_{j}>1
. The restricted collision operator
K_{r}
may be more general than that of (
161
) and the assumption (
167
) is superfluous.
The analysis requires further study and techniques but we think that the scales
H^{(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})}(G\times S\times I^{\circ})
are relevant for more general formulations.

In the section
5.1
the decomposition of the solution as
\psi=u+L_{-}g,\ u\in D(\widetilde{P}_{C,0})
is not necessary for the use of Neumann series in regularity analysis. For instance, consider the situation given in section
4.2
and assume that the coefficient
a
is a positive constant. After exponential shift the problem therein is (here
f_{C}
