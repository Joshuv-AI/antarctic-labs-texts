K. Mitra, M. Vohralík

Abstract
The Richards equation is commonly used to model the flow of water and air through soil, and it serves as a gateway equation for multiphase flows through porous media. It is a nonlinear advection–reaction–diffusion equation that exhibits both parabolic–hyperbolic and parabolic–elliptic kinds of degeneracies. In this study, we provide reliable, fully computable, and locally space–time efficient a posteriori error bounds for numerical approximations of the fully degenerate Richards equation. For showing global reliability, a nonlocal-in-time error estimate is derived individually for the time-integrated H 1 ​ ( H − 1 ) H^{1}(H^{-1}) , L 2 ​ ( L 2 ) L^{2}(L^{2}) , and the L 2 ​ ( H 1 ) L^{2}(H^{1}) errors. A maximum principle and a degeneracy estimator are employed for the last one. Global and local space–time efficiency error bounds are then obtained in a standard H 1 ​ ( H − 1 ) ∩ L 2 ​ ( H 1 ) H^{1}(H^{-1})\cap L^{2}(H^{1}) norm. The reliability and efficiency norms employed coincide when there is no nonlinearity. Moreover, error contributors such as flux nonconformity, time discretization, quadrature, linearization, and data oscillation are identified and separated. The estimates are also valid in a setting where iterative linearization with inexact solvers is considered. Numerical tests are conducted for nondegenerate and degenerate cases having exact solutions, as well as for a realistic case. It is shown that the estimators correctly identify the errors up to a factor of the order of unity. Keywords— Richards equation, a-posteriori error estimates, nonlinear degenerate problems, flow through porous media, finite element method

Introduction

The Richards equation models flow of water through porous medium (e.g., soil) partially filled with air
[
20
,
6
]
. For a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and final time
T>0
, with water saturation
s
and pressure
p
being the primary unknowns, it equates

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s-\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{\bar{K}}(\bm{x})\,\kappa(s)\,(\nabla p+\bm{g})]=f(s,\bm{x},t)\text{ in }\Omega\times[0,T].

(1.1a)

Here, space and time variables are denoted by
\bm{x}
and
t
, respectively. The source term
f(s,\bm{x},t)
represents contribution due to reaction/absorption. The gravity is represented by the constant vector
-\bm{g}
. The absolute permeability tensor
\mathbf{\bar{K}}(\bm{x})
and the relative permeability function
\kappa:[0,1]\to[0,1]
are properties of the medium. Initial condition is provided for the saturation
s
, and homogeneous Dirichtlet boundary condition is provided for the pressure
p
, i.e.,

s(\bm{x},0)=s_{0}(\bm{x})\text{ for }\bm{x}\in\Omega\text{ and }p=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T].

(1.1b)

Dirichlet–Neumann mixed boundary conditions are also considered in the numerical
Section

6
.
To close (
1.1a
)–(
1.1b
), it is usually assumed that saturation and pressure are related algebraically (commonly referred to as the capillary pressure relationship
[
20
]
), i.e., for a function
S:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
one has

\displaystyle s=S(p).

(1.1c)

Here, we assume that the saturation
s
is bounded in the closed interval
[0,1]
.
Equation (
1.1a
) is obtained by combining the constitutive relation for the flux, stated by the Darcy law

\bm{\sigma}:=-\mathbf{\bar{K}}(\bm{x})\kappa(s)(\nabla p+\bm{g}),

with the mass balance equation
\partial_{t}s+\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}=f(s,\bm{x},t)
.
The Richards equation is important in modelling groundwater flow and various chemical and biological processes. It is a nonlinear advection–reaction–diffusion equation which degenerates into an elliptic equation if
S^{\prime}(p)=0
at some point of the domain. On the other hand, if
\kappa(s)=0
, then the equation becomes a first order ordinary differential equation (hyperbolic) with the loss of regularity of the solution. Nonlinearity and degeneracy are the two main challenges in analysing the system (
1.1
).

Existence of solutions for the Richards equation was shown in
[
2
,
3
]
. However, in the degenerate case when
\kappa(s)=0
, only the existence of a weak limit can be shown
[
3
]
. Consequently, the pair
(s,p)
might not satisfy (
1.1
) in a weak sense. We give appropriate details in
Section

2.4
. Uniqueness of solutions is proven in
[
32
]
using the
L^{1}
-contraction method.

Different spatial discretization methods have been designed for the Richards equation. Some notable examples are
[
19
]
for finite volumes,
[
30
]
for finite elements,
[
4
,
35
]
for mixed finite elements,
[
26
]
for the discontinuous Galerkin method, and
[
22
]
for multi-point flux approximations. Iterative linearization methods such as the Newton, Picard, Jäger–Kačur, and the L-schemes have been investigated in
[
7
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
21
]
, and
[
27
,
28
]
, respectively, see also the references therein. An improvement of the Newton method was proposed in
[
9
]
by parametrizing both the saturation and the pressure as functions of a separate primary variable. A comprehensive review of numerical methods for the Richards equation can be found in
[
41
]
.

The theory of a posteriori estimates for elliptic differential equations is well studied, see, e.g.
[
1
,
36
,
40
]
.
A posteriori upper error bounds for the heat equation in the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap L^{\infty}(L^{2})
norm were derived in
[
34
]
. In
[
39
]
, global efficiency in space on every time step together with reliability are proven for the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap L^{\infty}(L^{2})\cap H^{1}(H^{-1})
norm. In
[
17
]
, a local efficiency estimate in space and in time is established for the norm further enriched by time jumps.
A general framework for obtaining rigorous a posteriori estimates for nonlinear problems has been laid out in
[
37
,
38
]
.
However, the Lipschitz continuity and invertibility of the operators associated with the differential equations are assumed, which limits the scope of the estimates.
A more specific result for the
p
-Laplacian problem is given in
[
23
]
. Using a formulation relying on the
N
-functions, the coercivity and Lipschitz-continuity of the flux function are shown with respect to the gradient. This makes it possible to derive a posteriori estimates for the problem.
Estimators for nonlinear advection–diffusion equations were proposed in
[
14
]
.
Both upper and lower bounds (reliability and efficiency) were established, robust with respect to the nonlinearities and advection dominance, but for a weaker space–time mesh-dependent norm. Moreover, it was also assumed that the solutions belong to
H^{1}(L^{2})
, which may not be the case for degenerate problems and/or if the initial condition is discontinuous.
Using entropy methods, error estimates in the
L^{1}
-norm were derived in
[
31
]
for singularly perturbed nonlinear advection–diffusion problems. Degenerate parabolic equations were considered in
[
29
]
. An
L^{\infty}(H^{-1})
estimate was derived using dual equations of the diffusion problem. For problems having parabolic–hyperbolic degeneracy, a posteriori upper bounds on the
L^{2}(H^{-1})\cap L^{\infty}(H^{-1})
norm combined with the time-integrated
L^{2}(L^{2})
norm of error were derived using Green’s function in
[
13
]
for a Stefan problem and on the
L^{2}(H^{-1})\cap L^{2}(H^{1})\cap L^{2}(L^{2})
norm in
[
10
]
for two-phase flow through porous media.
For the Richards equation, a posteriori error upper bounds in the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap H^{1}(L^{2})
norm were derived in
[
8
]
. A regularization term was introduced to avoid degeneracy and to obtain
H^{1}(L^{2})
estimates.

In the present paper, we provide a posteriori error estimates for the Richards equation (
1.1a
). The main improvements in this study are: (a)
Rigorous derivation of the upper as well as lower bounds of error
by the equivalence of the dual norm of the residual with an error metric that reduces to the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap H^{1}(H^{-1})\cap L^{\infty}(L^{2})
norm in the linear case. (b) Equivalence of the dual norm of the residual with
fully computable
and
locally space–time efficient estimates
. (c)
No higher-order regularity assumptions
such as the pressure in
L^{2}(H^{2})\cap H^{1}(L^{2})
or that the initial condition is in
H^{1}
. (d)
Inclusion of both the parabolic–hyperbolic and the parabolic–elliptic type of degeneracies
. This requires relaxing the assumptions on the associated functions such as
S^{\prime}(p),\,\kappa(s)>0
, assumed for instance in
[
8
,
5
,
10
]
in order to avoid the blow-up due to degeneracy. It poses a challenge particularly since the parabolic–hyperbolic degeneracy, stemming from
\kappa(s)=0
, causes a loss of regularity of the solutions. To circumvent this issue, we assume instead that the initial saturation
s_{0}
is bounded away from the degenerate value at
0
. With this assumption, a function
S_{\mathrm{m}}:[0,T]\to(0,1]
is computed using maximum principle such that
S_{\mathrm{m}}(t)\leq s(\bm{x},t)\leq 1
for all
(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
. For the parabolic–elliptic degeneracy, a degeneracy estimator is introduced to provide an upper bound on the
L^{2}(H^{1})
norm of the error. (e)
Rigorous inclusion
of
linearization errors due to inexact solvers
, space and time
adaptive meshes
, and implementation of
adaptive linearization
. (f) It is shown numerically that despite nonlinearities, degeneracies, and heterogeneities, the effectivity index of the estimators lies between 1 and 3 in most cases, even locally.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section

2
serves as a mathematical prologue to the Richards equation. The associated functions, relevant transformations, well-posedness results, and maximum principles are discussed in detail. In
Section

3
, lower and upper bounds on error by the dual norm of the residual are derived. The upper bound is provided separately for the
H^{1}(H^{-1})
,
L^{2}(L^{2})
, and the
L^{2}(H^{1})
errors in a time-smoothened fashion, see
Theorem

3.3
. In
Section

4
, a finite element approximation to the Richards problem (
1.1
) is considered, and some time-interpolations are discussed. These are used in
Section

5
to compute the equilibrated flux and the a posteriori estimators. Reliability and local space–time efficiency bounds are proven for the estimators. Finally, numerical results are presented in
Section

6
. The theoretical findings are verified and the corresponding effectivity indices are obtained using a nondegenerate as well as a degenerate case with known exact solutions. To demonstrate the prowess of the estimators, a realistic degenerate problem is analyzed in a heterogeneous, anisotropic domain, with discontinuous initial condition and mixed boundary condition. To conclude, it is shown in Appendix
A
how to take into account the additional errors from iterative linearization, whereas Appendix
B
collects some technical proofs.

2
The Richards equation

Here, we give a brief introduction to the Richards equation and state some of its properties important for our analysis.

2.1
Basic notation

Spaces:
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open polytope with a Lipschitz-continuous boundary.
Let
(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\|\cdot\|
represent respectively the
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product and norm;
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\omega}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\omega}
stand for the
L^{2}
-inner product and norm with respect to any Lipschitz subdomain
\omega\subset\Omega
. The Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega)
contains all functions
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that the weak derivative
\nabla u\in\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is the subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega)
containing functions vanishing at the boundary
\partial\Omega
in the trace sense. The space
H^{-1}(\Omega)
stands for the dual of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the corresponding duality pairing.
With final time
T>0
and
L^{2}(0,T;V)
denoting the
L^{2}
Bochner space for a Banach space
V
, we introduce the Hilbert spaces

\displaystyle{\bf\mathcal{X}}:=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\text{ and }{\bf\mathcal{Y}}:=\{u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)):\;\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))\}.

(2.1)

Inequalities:
For a Lipschitz subdomain
\omega\subseteq\Omega
with diameter
h_{\omega}
, let
u\in H^{1}(\omega)
be such that either
\int_{\omega}u=0
or the trace of
u
is zero on a section of
\partial\omega
of nonzero measure. Then the Poincaré–Friedrichs inequality states that there exists a constant
C_{\mathrm{P},\omega}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\omega}\leq C_{\mathrm{P},\omega}h_{\omega}\,\|\nabla u\|_{\omega}.

(2.2)

For a convex
\omega
in the zero mean-value case,
C_{\mathrm{P},\omega}
can be taken as
\pi^{-1}
.

Notation:
Let
[\cdot]_{+}=\max(\cdot,0)
and
[\cdot]_{-}=\min(\cdot,0)
denote the positive and negative part functions respectively. In our notation,
a\lesssim b
will refer to the inequality
a\leq Cb
, where
C>0
is a constant that depends solely on the shape-regularity of the spatial meshes in the space dimension
d
, and on the ratio
K_{\mathrm{m}}/\penalty K_{\mathrm{M}}
(see
(P3)
below). In particular, it is independent of mesh-size, time-step size, the functions
\kappa(\cdot),\,S(\cdot)
,
f
, and the polynomial degrees associated with the numerical scheme.

2.2
Assumptions on the data

We assume the following properties for the data in (
1.1
):

(P1)

The relative permeability function
\kappa
is of the class
C^{1}([0,1])
with
\kappa(0)\geq 0
,
\kappa(1)=1
, and
\kappa(0)<\kappa(s)<\kappa(1)
for all
s\in(0,1)
.

(P2)

The saturation function
S
is of the class
\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{R})
with Lipschitz constant
L_{S}>0
. It is either linear, or there exists a constant
p_{\mathrm{M}}\in(0,\infty]
such that
\lim\limits_{p\searrow-\infty}S(p)=0
, and

(a)

S|_{(-\infty,p_{\mathrm{M}}]}\in C^{2}((-\infty,p_{\mathrm{M}}])
,
S^{\prime}(p)>0\text{ for all }p<p_{\mathrm{M}}
, and
\lim\limits_{p\nearrow p_{\mathrm{M}}}S^{\prime}(p)>0
;

(b)

S(p)=1\text{ and consequently }S^{\prime}(p)=0\text{ for all }p>p_{\mathrm{M}}.

(P3)

The absolute permeability tensor
\mathbf{\bar{K}}:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is piecewise constant in
\Omega
, bounded, and satisfies the ellipticity condition, i.e., there exists positive constants
K_{\mathrm{m}},K_{\mathrm{M}}
such that for any
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

K_{\mathrm{m}}|\bm{\zeta}|^{2}\leq\bm{\zeta}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{\bar{K}}(\bm{x})\bm{\zeta}\leq K_{\mathrm{M}}|\bm{\zeta}|^{2}\quad\text{ for almost all }\bm{x}\in\Omega,

where
|\bm{\zeta}|
is the Euclidean norm of
\bm{\zeta}
, i.e.,
|\bm{\zeta}|=(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\zeta_{j}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Consequently, there exist unique positive-definite tensor-valued functions
\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, and
\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{-1}
.

(P4)

The source term
f\in C^{1}([0,1]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R})
and there exists a function
f_{\mathrm{m}}\in C^{1}([0,1])
such that
f_{\mathrm{m}}(\cdot)\leq\inf_{\bm{x}\in\Omega,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(\cdot,\bm{x},t)
.

(P5)

The initial condition
s_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
satisfies

{0<\mathrm{ess}\inf_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\{s_{0}(\bm{x})\}\leq\mathrm{ess}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\{s_{0}(\bm{x})\}\leq 1}.

These assumptions are consistent with experiments, see e.g.
[
20
]
.

where

2.3.2
Diffusivity and total pressure functions

We further introduce the diffusivity function

D(s):=\kappa(s)\,{p_{\mathrm{c}}}^{\prime}(s),

2.4
Weak formulations

We give below two equivalent weak formulations of the problem (
1.1
) discussing their strong and weak points. They will both be used to derive the a posteriori error estimates.

2.4.1
The pressure formulation

In the pressure formulation of (
1.1
), the main unknown is the pressure
p
. It reads:
solve for
p\in{\bf\mathcal{X}}
and
s=S(p)\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
such that
s(0)=s_{0}
and for all
\varphi\in{\bf\mathcal{X}}
,

\displaystyle\int^{T}_{0}\langle\partial_{t}S(p),\varphi\rangle+\int^{T}_{0}(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\kappa(S(p))(\nabla p+\bm{g}),\nabla\varphi)=\int^{T}_{0}(f(S(p),\bm{x},t),\varphi).

(2.15)

This formulation has the advantage of generalizing to heterogeneous porous media, where the functions
S
and
\kappa
are defined differently in different subdomains of
\Omega
. In particular, since
p
is a physical quantity that remains continuous across the interfaces of such subdomains, formulation (
2.15
) has a conforming nature also in such circumstances.

2.4.2
The total pressure formulation

In the total pressure formulation of (
1.1
), the main unknown is the total pressure
\mathcal{K}(p)
which will henceforth be denoted by
\Psi
. It reads: solve for

2.5
Maximum principle

In the case of the Richards equation, the saturation

is bounded in
[0,1]
, and

causes parabolic–hyperbolic degeneracy to occur. In this section, we use the maximum principle to obtain computable lower bounds for

, bounding it away from

For a positive initial saturation, the function

is a lower bound function of

, if

0<S_{\mathrm{m}}(t)\leq s(\bm{x},t)\text{ for almost all }(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times[0,T].

To ensure that a lower bound function satisfying (
2.17
) exists for
S(p)\in{\bf\mathcal{Y}}
when

solves (
2.15
), additional restrictions have to be imposed on the source term function
f
.
In particular, note that if
\kappa(0)=0
and for some
(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
we have
s=0
, then from (
1.1a
)
\partial_{t}s=f(0,\bm{x},t)
. Since
s<0
is unphysical, this forces

f(0,\bm{x},t)\geq 0\text{ for all }(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times[0,T].

(2.18)

This constraint will be imposed below to obtain computable maximum principle estimates.
If

is independent of

, then (
2.18
) simply implies that

.
In comparison, in the context of the heat equation,

is not bounded in
[0,1]
, and hence conditions such as (

3.1

\displaystyle\Psi_{h\tau}\in{\bf\mathcal{X}}\text{ with }s_{h\tau}:=\theta(\Psi_{h\tau})\in{\bf\mathcal{Y}},

the residual

with

3.2
Norms

On a Lipschitz subdomain
\omega\subseteq\Omega
, we introduce
equivalent (semi)norms on
H^{1}(\omega)
,
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
and
H^{-1}(\omega)
:

\displaystyle\|\varrho\|_{{H^{1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}:=\|\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\varrho\|_{\omega}\text{ for }\varrho\in H^{1}(\omega),

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\|\varrho\|_{{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}:=\sup_{\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)}\{\langle\varrho,\varphi\rangle_{H^{-1}(\omega),H^{1}_{0}(\omega)}/\penalty\|\varphi\|_{{H^{1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}\}\text{ for }\varrho\in H^{-1}(\omega).

(3.3b)

From the properties of
\mathbf{\bar{K}}
stated in
(P3)
, it is immediate that

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{m}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\varrho\|_{\omega}\leq\|\varrho\|_{{H^{1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}\leq K_{\mathrm{M}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\varrho\|_{\omega}\;\text{ and }\;K_{\mathrm{M}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\varrho\|_{H^{-1}(\omega)}\leq\|\varrho\|_{{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}\leq K_{\mathrm{m}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\varrho\|_{H^{-1}(\omega)}.

(3.4)

Let
\alpha:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
denote a bounded non-negative function. For a subdomain
\omega\subseteq\Omega
, and an interval
I\subseteq[0,T]
, we introduce the distance measure
{\mathrm{dist}}^{\alpha}_{\omega,I}
on the set
\{\psi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\omega)):\theta(\psi)\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\omega))\}
as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{dist}}^{\alpha}_{\omega,I}(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}):=

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}(\theta(\Psi_{1})-\theta(\Psi_{2}))\|_{L^{2}(I;{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega))}

\displaystyle+\|\alpha(\theta(\Psi_{1})-\theta(\Psi_{2}))\|_{L^{2}(\omega\times I)}+\|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}(I,{H^{1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega))}.

(3.5)

The distance measure combines the
L^{2}(I;{H^{1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega))
-norm of
\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}
with the
H^{1}(I;{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega))\cap L^{2}(\omega\times I)
norms of
\theta(\Psi_{1})-\theta(\Psi_{2})
. Note that for
\alpha=0
, the middle term disappears.

We also introduce the class of time-integration functionals
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}:L^{2}([0,T])\to[0,\infty)
as: for
\varrho\in L^{2}([0,T])
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(\varrho):=\left[\exp\left(-\smallint_{0}^{T}\alpha\right)\int_{0}^{T}\left(\varrho^{2}(t)+\alpha(t)\exp\left(\smallint_{t}^{T}\alpha\right)\int_{0}^{t}\varrho^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.6)

The operator
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}
defines a norm and satisfies the triangle inequality. It is actually equivalent to the
L^{2}([0,T])
-norm, since the inequality
0\leq\int_{0}^{t}\varrho^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq\|\varrho\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T])}
for
t\in[0,T]
, and
\int_{0}^{T}\alpha\exp(\int_{t}^{T}\alpha)\mathrm{d}t=\exp(\int_{0}^{T}\alpha)-1
directly gives

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\smallint_{0}^{T}\alpha\right)\|\varrho\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}\leq\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(\varrho)\leq\|\varrho\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}.

(3.7)

Consequently, it is equal to the
L^{2}([0,T])
norm if
\alpha=0
.

4
Finite element discretization

We describe in this section the discretization of the Richards problem (
1.1
) by the finite element method.

4.1
Time steps

For the time-interval
(0,T)
, we introduce
N+1
discrete times
\bm{t}_{N}=(t_{n})_{n=0}^{N}
where
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
. Let
I_{n}:=(t_{n-1},t_{n}]
denote the time intervals and
\tau_{n}:=t_{n}-t_{n-1}
the lengths of the time steps for
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Note that, we allow nonuniform time stepping. Further, for a vector space
V
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}\left(I_{n};V\right)
denotes the space of
V
-valued affine functions over the time-step interval
I_{n}
.

4.2
Space meshes

For the time sequence
\bm{t}_{N}
, let
\{\mathcal{T}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
denote the sequence of matching and uniformly shape regular simplical meshes for the domain
\Omega
. The meshes are allowed to undergo refinement or coarsening between time steps. Henceforth, discontinuities of
\mathbf{\bar{K}}
are only allowed to happen along internal edges of the mesh.
For each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let
h_{K}:=\mathrm{diam}\{K\}
denote the diameter of
K
and let
\mathfrak{p}_{n}\geq 1
denote the spatial polynomial degree associated with
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Our results are generalizable to polynomial degrees depending locally on
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
. However, to keep the notation simple, we only consider
\mathfrak{p}_{n}
changing between time steps here. For full
hp
-adaptive algorithm, we refer to
[
17
]
.

4.3
Approximation spaces

On a time step
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, we define the
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-conforming
hp
-finite element space
V_{n,h}
as:

V_{n,h}:=\left\{u_{h}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\;\left.u_{h}\right|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(K)\quad\forall\,K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\right\},

(4.1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(K)
denotes the polynomial space of degree
\mathfrak{p}_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
on
K
.
Further, let
\Pi_{n,h}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to V_{n,h}
and
{\Lambda}_{n,h}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
represent the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection operator with respect to the spaces
V_{n,h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pi_{n,h}u\in V_{n,h}\text{ for }u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\text{ is such that }(\Pi_{n,h}u,\varphi_{h})=(u,\varphi_{h}),\text{ for all }\varphi_{h}\in V_{n,h};

(4.2a)

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{n,h}u\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\text{ for }u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\text{ is such that }({\Lambda}_{n,h}u,\varphi_{h})=(u,\varphi_{h}),\text{ for all }\varphi_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}).

(4.2b)

4.4
Finite element discretization

In
Section

3
, formulation (
2.16
) is used to derive the estimates. However, since (
2.15
) is the most general and commonly used formulation, we propose the finite element scheme for (
2.15
). We will still be able to apply the analysis of
Section

3
.
For time discretization, we consider the backward Euler scheme. The problem for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and a given
S_{n-1,h}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is to find
p_{n,h}\in V_{n,h}
which satisfies for all
\varphi_{h}\in V_{n,h}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\tau_{n}}(S(p_{n,h})-S_{n-1,h},\varphi_{h})+(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\kappa(S(p_{n,h}))[\nabla p_{n,h}+\bm{g}],\nabla\varphi_{h})=(f(S(p_{n,h})),\bm{x},t_{n}),\varphi_{h}).

(4.3)

For
n=1
, we set
S_{n-1,h}:=\Pi_{1,h}s_{0}
, whereas, for
n>1
,
S_{n-1,h}:=S(p_{n-1,h})
. The existence of
p_{n,h}
solving (
4.3
) is discussed in
[
15
]
for the nondegenerate case (
\kappa(0)>0
). The degenerate case is covered in
[
33
]
for the control volume finite element method. In practice, since the problem (
4.3
) is nonlinear, the exact
p_{n,h}
is generally not known, and linear iterations have to be used to approximate
p_{n,h}
. This is discussed at length in Appendix
A
.

From the sequence
\{p_{n,h}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, we define the space–time discrete total pressure and saturation for all
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
as

\displaystyle\Psi_{n,h}:=\mathcal{K}(p_{n,h})\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\text{ and }S_{n,h}:=\theta(\Psi_{n,h})\overset{\eqref{eq:ConnectionVariables}}{=}S(p_{n,h})\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(4.4)

The choice
\Psi_{0,h}=P_{\mathrm{c}}(S_{0,h})=P_{\mathrm{c}}(\Pi_{1,h}s_{0})
is used for extending the definition of
\Psi_{n,h}
to
n=0
.

4.5
Time-continuous solutions

There are multiple ways to define a time-continuous total pressure
\Psi_{h\tau}\in{\bf\mathcal{X}}
and saturation
s_{h\tau}\in{\bf\mathcal{Y}}
, satisfying the requirements of
Theorems

3.3
and
3.1
, starting from
\{S_{n,h}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and
\{\Psi_{n,h}\}_{n=1}^{N}
introduced in (
4.4
). Here, for
t\in I_{n}
, we choose

\displaystyle\Psi_{h\tau}(t):=

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\tfrac{t-t_{n-1}}{\tau_{n}}S_{n,h}+\tfrac{t_{n}-t}{\tau_{n}}S_{n-1,h}\right)+\left[\tfrac{t-t_{n-1}}{\tau_{n}}\Psi_{n,h}+\tfrac{t_{n}-t}{\tau_{n}}\Psi_{n-1,h}-P_{\mathrm{M}}\right]_{+},

(4.5a)

\displaystyle s_{h\tau}(t):=

\displaystyle\theta(\Psi_{h\tau}(t)).

(4.5b)

Observe that,
\Psi_{h\tau}
and
s_{h\tau}
defined this way satisfy

\displaystyle\Psi_{h\tau}\in C(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\subset{\bf\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle\text{ and }s_{h\tau}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\subset{\bf\mathcal{Y}};

(4.6a)

\displaystyle\Psi_{h\tau}(t_{n})=\Psi_{n,h},

\displaystyle\text{ and }s_{h\tau}(t_{n})=S_{n,h},\quad\forall\,n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

(4.6b)

The relation (
4.6b
) even holds when
\Psi_{n,h}>P_{\mathrm{M}}
since
P_{\mathrm{c}}(S_{n,h})=P_{\mathrm{M}}
in this case and the other contribution from (
4.5a
) adds
[\Psi_{n,h}-P_{\mathrm{M}}]_{+}
.
Another advantage of this interpolation is that, using (
2.10
), if both
\Psi_{n,h},\,\Psi_{n-1,h}\leq P_{\mathrm{M}}
(nondegenerate case), or
\Psi_{n,h},\,\Psi_{n-1,h}\geq P_{\mathrm{M}}
(degenerate case), i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{ if either }\Psi_{h\tau}\leq P_{\mathrm{M}}\text{ or }\Psi_{h\tau}\geq P_{\mathrm{M}}\text{ in }I_{n},\text{ then }\partial_{t}s_{h\tau}=\tfrac{1}{\tau_{n}}(S_{n,h}-S_{n-1,h}).

(4.7)

5
A posteriori error estimates

We apply here the developments of
Section

3
to perform a posteriori error analysis of the finite element discretization of
Section

4
.

5.1
Equilibrated flux

The objective of this section is to design an equilibrated flux
\bm{\sigma}_{n,h}\in\bm{H}(\mathrm{div},\Omega)
that satisfies the mass balance property

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left[\tfrac{1}{\tau_{n}}(S_{n,h}-S_{n-1,h})+\nabla{\cdot}\bm{\sigma}_{n,h}-f(S_{n,h},\bm{x},t_{n})\right]=0\text{ for all }K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}.

(5.1)

5.1.1
Local mixed finite element spaces

For the construction of
\bm{\sigma}_{n,h}
, we introduce some standard mixed finite element spaces.
For each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, let
\mathcal{V}_{n}
denote the set of vertices of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, where we distinguish the set of interior vertices
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n}
and the set of boundary vertices
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n}
. For
K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
,
\mathcal{V}_{K}\subset\mathcal{V}_{n}
denotes the set of vertices of
K
. For each
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
, let
\psi_{\mathbf{a}}
denote the hat function associated with
\mathbf{a}
and
{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}
the interior of the support of
\psi_{\mathbf{a}}
, with the associated diameter
h_{{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}}
.
Furthermore, let
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n}
denote the restriction of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{n}
to
{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}
.

For a polynomial degree
\mathfrak{p}\geq 0
, the local spaces
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n})
and
\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n})
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\{u_{h}\in L^{2}({\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}),\quad u_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(K)\quad\forall\,K\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n})

\displaystyle:=\{\bm{v}_{h}\in\bm{L}^{2}({\omega_{\mathbf{a}}};\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\bm{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}}(K)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n}\},

where
\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}}(K):=\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(K;\mathbb{R}^{d})+\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(K)\bm{x}
denotes the Raviart–Thomas–Nédélec space of order
\mathfrak{p}
on
K
. We use a similar notation on the whole mesh
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and introduce the local mixed finite element spaces
\bm{V}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n,h}
and

as

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n,h}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}&\left\{\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}+1}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n}),\;\bm{v}_{h}\in\bm{H}(\mathrm{div},{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}),\;\bm{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{n}=0\text{ on }\partial{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}\right\}\;\text{if }\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n},\\
&\left\{\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}+1}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n}),\;\bm{v}_{h}\in\bm{H}(\mathrm{div},{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}),\;\bm{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{n}=0\text{ on }\partial{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}\setminus\partial\Omega\right\}\;\text{if }\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n},\end{cases}

\displaystyle Q_{n,h}^{\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\left\{u_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}+1}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n}),\quad(u_{h},1)_{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}=0\right\}&\hskip 117.51001pt\text{if }\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n},\\
\;\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}+1}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n})&\hskip 117.51001pt\text{if }\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n}.\end{cases}

(5.2)

The projector
\Pi_{n,h}^{{\mathrm{RT}}}:\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is then defined as:

\displaystyle\text{ for }\bm{u}\in\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\Pi_{n,h}^{{\mathrm{RT}}}\bm{u},\bm{v}_{h})=(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\,\bm{u},\bm{v}_{h}),\;\text{ for all }\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{RTN}_{\mathfrak{p}_{n}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}).

(5.3)

Note that it is computed elementwise.

5.1.2
Flux reconstruction

For each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}

) in

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n,h}

\displaystyle:=f(S_{n,h},\bm{x},t_{n})-\tfrac{1}{\tau_{n}}(S_{n,h}-S_{n-1,h}),\quad\bm{F}_{n,h}:=\nabla\Psi_{n,h}+\bm{g}\kappa(S_{n,h}).

Recalling the projection operators
\Pi_{n,h}
,
{\Lambda}_{n,h}
, and
\Pi_{n,h}^{{\mathrm{RT}}}
from (

\displaystyle g^{\mathbf{a}}_{n,h}:=(\psi_{\mathbf{a}}\,{\Lambda}_{n,h}\mathcal{G}_{n,h}-\nabla\psi_{\mathbf{a}}\cdot\,\mathbf{\bar{K}}\,\Pi_{n,h}^{{\mathrm{RT}}}\,\bm{F}_{n,h})|_{{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}},\quad\bm{\tau}^{\mathbf{a}}_{n,h}:=-(\psi_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{\bar{K}}\,\Pi_{n,h}^{{\mathrm{RT}}}\,\bm{F}_{n,h})|_{{\omega_{\mathbf{a}}}}.

Definition 5.1

For a given time-step

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{n,h}\overset{\eqref{eq:flux_reconstruction_1}}{=}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{n,h}^{\mathbf{a}}\overset{\eqref{eq:tau_g_def}}{=}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}}[(\psi_{\mathbf{a}}\,{\Lambda}_{n,h}\mathcal{G}_{n,h}-\nabla\psi_{\mathbf{a}}\cdot\,\mathbf{\bar{K}}\,\Pi_{n,h}^{{\mathrm{RT}}}\,\bm{F}_{n,h})]={\Lambda}_{n,h}\mathcal{G}_{n,h},

A posterori error estimators

Recalling the definition of time-continuous solutions
(\Psi_{h\tau},s_{h\tau})
from

, we introduce the following a posteriori error estimators: Take
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, an open polytope
\omega\subseteq\Omega
, and

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{F}}_{n,h,\omega}(t)

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\sigma}_{n,h}+\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\Psi_{h\tau}+\bm{g}\,\kappa(s_{h\tau}))(t)\|_{\omega}

(5.9a)

measures the lack of

-conformity of the numerical flux

. The quadrature error estimator arising from

not being polynomial (see (

(5.9b)

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{qd},t}_{n,h,\omega}(t):=\|\partial_{t}s_{h\tau}-\tfrac{1}{\tau_{n}}(S_{n,h}-S_{n-1,h})\|_{{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}.

, and it vanishes in both purely degenerate and nondegenerate regimes due to (

).
The temporal oscillation in data

is measured by

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{osc}}_{n,\omega}(t)

\displaystyle:=\|f(s_{h\tau}(t_{n}),\bm{x},t_{n})-f(s_{h\tau}(t),\bm{x},t)\|_{{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\omega)}.

(5.9d)

The errors in the approximation of the initial condition

are accounted by

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{ini},L^{2}}

\displaystyle:=\|s_{0}-\Pi_{1,h}s_{0}\|,\quad\eta^{\mathrm{ini},H^{-1}}:=\|s_{0}-\Pi_{1,h}s_{0}\|_{{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\Omega)}.

(5.9e)

The projectors
\Pi_{n,h}
and
{\Lambda}_{n,h}
were defined in (
4.2
), and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{{H^{-1}_{\!{}_{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}}}(\cdot)}
was introduced in (

). With the above definitions, the total estimator is computed as

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathcal{R}}(t):=\left[\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}[\eta^{\mathrm{F}}_{n,h,K}(t)+\eta^{\mathrm{qd},\mathcal{G}}_{n,h,K}]^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\eta^{\mathrm{qd},t}_{n,h,\Omega}(t)+\eta^{\mathrm{osc}}_{n,\Omega}(t).

(5.9f)

(5.12a)

(5.12c)

We collect here the proofs of the statements of

.

Proof of
Proposition

2.3
.

We claim that
(S_{\mathrm{m}},p_{\mathrm{c}}(S_{\mathrm{m}}))
serves as a subsolution of
(s,p)
for a constant
\mathbf{\bar{K}}
. From (
2.19
) we have that
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{m}}(t)>S(0)
for some
t>0
only if
f_{\mathrm{m}}(S(0))\geq 0
or
f(S(0),\bm{x},t)\geq 0
a.e. in
(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Hence, if
S_{\mathrm{m}}(t)=\min(\bar{S}_{\mathrm{m}}(t),S(0))=S(0)
in some interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
then
\partial_{t}S_{\mathrm{m}}-f(S_{\mathrm{m}},\bm{x},t)\leq 0-f_{\mathrm{m}}(S(0))\leq 0
. On the other hand, if
S_{\mathrm{m}}(t)=\bar{S}_{\mathrm{m}}(t)
then
\partial_{t}S_{\mathrm{m}}-f(S_{\mathrm{m}},\bm{x},t)\leq\partial_{t}\bar{S}_{\mathrm{m}}-f_{\mathrm{m}}(\bar{S}_{\mathrm{m}})=0
. Hence,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{\mathrm{m}}-\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{\bar{K}}\,\kappa(S_{\mathrm{m}})\,(\nabla p_{\mathrm{c}}(S_{\mathrm{m}})+\bm{g})]-f(S_{\mathrm{m}},\bm{x},t)=\partial_{t}S_{\mathrm{m}}-f(S_{\mathrm{m}},\bm{x},t)\leq 0.

Moreover,
p_{\mathrm{c}}(S_{\mathrm{m}})\leq p_{\mathrm{c}}(S(0))=0
in relation to the boundary.
Thus, invoking the comparison principle
[
32
]
, we conclude that
(S_{\mathrm{m}},p_{\mathrm{c}}(S_{\mathrm{m}}))
is a subsolution of
(s,p)
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

2.4
.

Let
J_{1}:=\min(J,p_{l})<0
. For the sake of simplicity, let the space coordinate be translated such that
\min\{\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}\}=0
. To show the lower bound of
\varsigma
we use
\nabla(\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x})=\bm{g}
, and rewrite (
2.20
) as

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\kappa(S(\varsigma))\nabla[\varsigma+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}],\nabla\varphi)=\left(\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}[f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},t)]_{-},\varphi\right).

(B.1)

Selecting the test function
\varphi=[\varsigma-J_{1}+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}]_{-}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
(observe that
\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
since
\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}-J_{1}\geq 0
for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
) one then obtains in the left hand side of (
B.1
),

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\kappa(S(\varsigma))\nabla[\varsigma+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}],\nabla[\varsigma-J_{1}+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}]_{-})\geq\int_{\Omega}\kappa(S(\varsigma))\left|\mathbf{\bar{K}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla[\varsigma-J_{1}+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}]_{-}\right|^{2}.

Observe that
\varphi
is nonzero only when
\varsigma\leq J_{1}-\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}\leq p_{l}
, implying
f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},t)\geq 0
. Hence, the right hand side of (
B.1
) yields

\displaystyle\left(\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}[f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},t)]_{-},[\varsigma-J_{1}+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}]_{-}\right)=0.

Hence, from (
B.1
), one obtains
\varsigma\geq J_{1}-\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}
. We obtain the upper bound by testing with
\varphi=[\varsigma-J+\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}-\max\{\bm{g}\cdot\bm{x}\}]_{+}
and following the arguments as before.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

2.5
.

Observe that the choice of
J
implies from
Proposition

2.4
that
\varsigma\leq 0
and
S(\varsigma)\leq s_{0}
a.e. in
\Omega
. Moreover, from (
2.20
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varsigma-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\kappa(S(\varsigma))[\nabla\varsigma+\bm{g}])-f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{\bar{K}}\kappa(S(\varsigma))[\nabla\varsigma+\bm{g}])-\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}[f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},\zeta)]_{-}=0,

since
f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},t)\geq\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}[f(S(\varsigma),\bm{x},\zeta)]_{-}
. Hence, similar to the proof of
Proposition

2.3
, the result follows from applying the comparison principle.
∎
