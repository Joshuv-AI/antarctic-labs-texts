Abstract
. The neural rings and ideals as an algebraic tool for analyzing the intrinsic structure of neural codes were introduced by C. Curto et al. in 2013. Since then they were investigated in several papers, including the 2017 paper by Güntürkün et al., in which the notion of polarization of neural ideals was introduced. In this paper we extend their ideas by introducing the notions of polarization of motifs and neural codes. We show that the notions that we introduced have very nice properties which could allow the studying of the intrinsic structure of neural codes of length n n via the square free monomial ideals in 2 ​ n 2n variables and interpreting the results back in the original neural code ambient space. In the last section of the paper we introduce the notions of inactive neurons, partial neural codes, and partial motifs, as well as the notions of polarization of these codes and motifs. We use these notions to give a new proof of a theorem from the paper by Güntürkün et al. that we mentioned above.

(

Let

Remark 1.22

(about definitions and notation related to
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2n}
vs those related to
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
)
.

Square-free monomial ideals are the ideals generated by square-free monomials and they are easier to deal with than the pseudo-monomial ideals. The previous two definitions show that, in order to get some conclusions about the pseudo-monomial ideals in
n
variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, we can consider some related square-free monomial ideals in
2n
variables, which, however, are not denoted by
X_{1},\dots,X_{2n}
, but by
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
. Because of this difference in the notation for variables, we should be aware that, for example, a
pseudomonomial
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}]
is a polynomial of the form

f=\prod_{i\in\sigma}X_{i}\,\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-X_{j})\prod_{k\in\mu}Y_{k}\,\prod_{l\in\nu}(1-Y_{l}),

where
\sigma,\tau,\mu,\nu\subseteq[n]
,
\sigma\cap\tau=\emptyset
,
\mu\cap\nu=\emptyset
. Similarly, we have, for example, that for a motif
{\mathbf{a}}=b_{1}\dots b_{n}\,c_{1}\dots c_{n}\in\mathrm{Mot}(2n)
, the
Lagrange polynomial

L_{\mathbf{a}}
of
{\mathbf{a}}
and the
prime ideal

{\mathfrak{p}}_{\mathbf{a}}
of
{\mathbf{a}}
are respectively given in the following way:

(2)

L_{\mathbf{a}}=\prod_{a_{i}=1}X_{i}\,\prod_{a_{j}=0}(1-X_{j})\prod_{b_{i}=1}Y_{i}\,\prod_{b_{j}=0}(1-Y_{j}),

(3)

{\mathfrak{p}}_{\mathbf{a}}=(\{X_{i}\,:\,b_{i}=0\}\cup\{1-X_{j}\,:\,b_{j}=1\}\cup\{Y_{i}\,:\,c_{i}=0\}\cup\{1-Y_{j}\,:\,c_{j}=1\}).

So the definitions of these notions with respect to
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2n}
are the same as the ones with respect to
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
, we just need to take into account the notation for the variables. This works for other notions as well (like, for example,
minimal pseudo-monomials
in an ideal, the
neural ideal
of a code, the
canonical form
of a pseudo-monomial ideal, etc.), while some notions (like, for example,
minimal primes
of an ideal, etc.) can be given in the form that does not depend on the notation for the variables.

From now on we have the following convention: if the length of motifs and codes is denoted by
n
, then the associated rings and ideals will always be in
n
variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, while for the length denoted by
2n
the associated rings and ideals will always be in
2n
variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
. For lengths given by concrete numbers it will always be clear from the context if the number is
n
or
2n
.

2.
Definitions of the polarizations of motifs and codes

We would like to define the motif
{\mathbf{a}}^{p}
which is the polarization of a motif
{\mathbf{a}}=a_{1}\dots a_{n}\in{\mathbb{M}}^{n}
. Since for the motifs from
{\mathbb{M}}^{n}
we have that the Lagrange polynomials and the prime ideals of motifs are in
n
variables, and the polarizations of those Lagrange polynomials and prime ideals of motifs are in
2n
variables, it is natural to try to define
{\mathbf{a}}^{p}
to be an element of
{\mathbb{M}}^{2n}
. After defining the polarization of the motifs, we would define the polarization of a neural code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
in the following way:

{\mathcal{C}}^{p}=\cup\,\{V_{{\mathbf{a}}^{p}}\;:\;{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}})\}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2n}.

This would imply that

(4)

\mathrm{MaxMot}^{p}({\mathcal{C}})=\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p}).

Here, and in the rest of the paper, we use the notation

M^{p}=\{{\mathbf{a}}^{p}\;:\;{\mathbf{a}}\in M\}

for any
M\subseteq{\mathbb{M}}^{n}
. We would also like that the formula

(5)

\mathrm{Min}(J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{p}})=\mathrm{Min}^{p}(J_{\mathcal{C}})

holds.
We will first try to find the formula for
{\mathbf{a}}^{p}
in an example. So we will assume that the relations (
4
), and (
5
) hold. In addition to that, we will be using the relation (
1
):

\mathrm{Min}(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{\mathbf{a}}\;:\;{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}})\}.

Example 2.1
.

Suppose that the relations (
1
), (
4
) and (
5
) hold. Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{10\}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2}
. Then

\text{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}})=\{10\}.

By (
5
) we have

\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})=\{10^{p}\}.

By (
1
),

\mathrm{Min}(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\{(1-X_{1},X_{2})\}={\mathfrak{p}}_{10},

hence by (
5
),

\mathrm{Min}(J_{{\mathcal{C}}^{p}})=\{(1-X_{1},X_{2})^{p}\}=\{X_{2},Y_{1}\}={\mathfrak{p}}_{*00*}.

Hence by (
1
),

\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})=\{*00*\}=\mathrm{MaxMot}^{p}({\mathcal{C}})=\{10^{p}\}.

Thus

(6)

10^{p}=*00\!*.

Now
{\mathcal{D}}=\,^{c}{\mathcal{C}}=\{00,01,11\}
, so that

\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{D}})=\{0*,*1\}.

Hence by (
4
),

\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{D}}^{p})=\{0*^{p},*1^{p}\}.

By (
1
),

\mathrm{Min}(J_{\mathcal{D}})=\{(X_{1}),(1-X_{2})\},

hence by (
5
),

\mathrm{Min}(J_{{\mathcal{D}}^{p}})=\{(X_{1}),(1-X_{2})\}^{p}=\{(X_{1}),(Y_{2})\}=\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{0***},{\mathfrak{p}}_{***0}\}.

Hence by (
1
),

\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{D}}^{p})=\{0\!*\!**,\,*\!*\!*0\}=\mathrm{MaxMot}^{p}({\mathcal{D}})=\{0*^{p},*1^{p}\}.

Hence we have either

(7)

0*^{p}=0\!*\!**,\hskip 10.00002pt*1^{p}=*\!*\!*0,

or

(8)

*1^{p}=*\!*\!*0,\hskip 10.00002pt0*^{p}=0\!*\!**.

Now if we have

(a_{1}a_{2})^{p}=b_{1}b_{2}|c_{1}c_{2},

then the formula (
6
) would suggest that

\displaystyle a_{1}=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{1}=*,\,c_{1}=0,

\displaystyle a_{2}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{2}=0,\,c_{2}=*,

and the formula (
7
) would suggest that

\displaystyle a_{1}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{1}=0,\,\,c_{1}=*,

\displaystyle a_{2}=*

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{2}=*,\,\,c_{2}=*.

This would suggest that if

(a_{1}\dots a_{n})^{p}=b_{1}\dots b_{n}|c_{1}\dots c_{n},

then

\displaystyle a_{i}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{i}=0,\,\,c_{i}=*,

\displaystyle a_{i}=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{i}=*,\,\,c_{i}=0,

\displaystyle a_{i}=*

\displaystyle\Rightarrow b_{i}=*,\,\,c_{i}=*.

(This, in particular, suggests that the formulas (
7
) are the correct way to polarize and not the formulas (
8
).)

Having completed this example, based on the reasoning in it, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{a}}=a_{1}\dots a_{n}\in{\mathbb{M}}^{n}
. We define its
polarization

{\mathbf{a}}^{p}=b_{1}\dots b_{n}|c_{1}\dots c_{n}

in the following way:

\displaystyle\text{if }a_{i}=0\text{ in }{\mathbf{a}},

Theorem 3.3
.

For any code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
we have

\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})=\mathrm{MaxMot}^{p}({\mathcal{C}}).

Proof.

Claim 1.
\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})\subseteq\{0,*\}^{2n}
.

Proof of Claim 1. Suppose to the contrary, i.e., that
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})
has a component
b_{\alpha}=1
for some
\alpha\in[2n]
. Let
{\mathbf{w}}\in V_{\mathbf{b}}
. Then
w_{\alpha}=1
. We have
{\mathbf{w}}\in V_{{\mathbf{a}}^{p}}
for some
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}})

({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{\alpha}=*
, hence the word
{\mathbf{w}}^{\prime}
obtained by replacing

in

{\mathcal{C}}

{\mathbf{b}}^{\prime}
obtained by replacing

by

{\mathcal{C}}^{p}

{\mathbf{b}}

Claim 2. Let

. Then there is no
i\in[n]
such that
b_{i}=b_{n+i}=0
.

Proof of Claim 2. Suppose to the contrary. Let

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{j\in[n]\;:\;b_{j}=b_{n+j}=*\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\{j\in[n]\;:\;b_{j}=0,\,b_{n+j}=*\},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\{j\in[n]\;:\;b_{j}=*,\,b_{n+j}=0\},

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\{j\in[n]\;:\;b_{j}=b_{n+j}=0\}.

Then the sets
A,B,C,D
form a partition of
[n]
and
i\in D
. Let
{\mathbf{w}}\in V_{\mathbf{b}}
be defined in the following way:

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in A)\;

\displaystyle w_{j}=w_{n+j}=1;

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in B)\;

\displaystyle w_{j}=0,\,w_{n+j}=1;

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in C)\;

\displaystyle w_{j}=1,\,w_{n+j}=0;

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in D)\;

\displaystyle w_{j}=w_{n+j}=0.

Since
{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{p}
, there is an
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}})
such that
{\mathbf{w}}\in V_{{\mathbf{a}}^{p}}
. Since
{\mathbf{a}}^{p}
is a polar motif, we have:

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in A)\;

\displaystyle({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{j}=({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{n+j}=*;

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in B)\;

\displaystyle({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{j}=0\text{ or }*,\,({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{n+j}=*;

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in C)\;

\displaystyle({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{j}=*,\,({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{n+j}=0\text{ or }*;

\displaystyle(\forall\,j\in D)\;

\displaystyle\text{at least one of $({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{j},\,({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{n+j}$ is $*$, the other one is $0$ or $*$}.

Since
D
contains at least one element, these relations imply
{\mathbf{a}}^{p}>{\mathbf{b}}
, contradicting to the maximality of
{\mathbf{b}}
. Claim 2 is proved.

Claim 3.
\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})\subseteq\mathrm{MaxMot}^{p}({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof of Claim 3. Let
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}}^{p})
. By the claims 1 and 2, for each
i\in[n]
we have one the following three cases:
b_{i}=b_{n+i}=*
, or,
b_{i}=0
,
b_{n+i}=*
, or,
b_{i}=*
,
b_{n+i}=0
. Let
{\mathbf{w}}\in V_{\mathbf{b}}
be a word defined in the following way: if
b_{i}=b_{n+i}=*
, then
w_{i}=w_{n+i}=1
; if
b_{i}=0
and
b_{n+i}=*
, then
w_{i}=0
,
w_{n+i}=1
; if
b_{i}=*
and
b_{n+i}=0
, then
w_{i}=1
,
w_{n+i}=0
. This word belongs to some

, where
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{MaxMot}({\mathcal{C}})
. Since
{\mathbf{a}}^{p}
is a polar motif, we have the following cases: when
w_{i}=w_{n+i}=1
, then
({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{i}=({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{n+i}=*

w_{i}=0
and
w_{n+i}=1
, then
({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{i}=0

({\mathbf{a}}^{p})_{n+i}=*
