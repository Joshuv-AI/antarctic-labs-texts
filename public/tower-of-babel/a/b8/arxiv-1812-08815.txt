Abstract
Context: Formal methods have been around for a while, still being unclear how to leverage their benefits, overcome their challenges, and set new directions for their improvement towards a more successful transfer into practice. Objective: We study the use of formal methods in mission-critical software domains, examining industrial and academic views. Method: We perform a cross-sectional on-line survey. Results: Our results indicate an increased intent to apply formal methods in industry, suggesting a positively perceived usefulness. But the results also indicate a negatively perceived ease of use. Scalability, skills, and education seem to be among the key challenges to support this intent. Conclusions: We present the largest study of this kind so far ( N = 216 N=216 ), and our observations provide valuable insights, highlighting directions for future theoretical and empirical research of formal methods. Our findings are strongly coherent with earlier observations by Austin & Parkin 1993 . Background and Terminology

By
formal methods
, we refer to
explicit
mathematical
models and
sound
logical reasoning about
critical
properties

(
Rushby 1994
)
—such as reliability, safety,
security, more generally, dependability and performance—of
electrical, electronic, and programmable electronic or software
systems in mission- or property-critical application domains.
Model checking, theorem proving, abstract interpretation, assertion
checking, and formal contracts are examples of formal methods.
By
use of formal methods
, we refer to their application in the
development and analysis of critical systems and to substantially
integrating formal methods with the used programming methodologies (e.g. structured development, model-based engineering, assertion-based programming,
test-driven development), notations (e.g. UML, SysML), and tools.

Tool and Method Evaluation.

In the following, we give an overview of several evaluation approaches
and explain in
Section

4.2
which approach we take.

The widely used
technology acceptance model
(TAM;

Davis 1989
) is a psychological test
that allows the assessment of end-user IT based on the two constructs

perceived ease of use
(PEOU, i.e., positive and negative experiences
while using an IT system) and
perceived usefulness
(PU, i.e., positive
experiences of accomplishing a task using an IT system compared to not
using this system for accomplishing the same task).

Complementary to technology acceptance model,

Basili 1985
proposes the
goal-question-metric approach to method and tool evaluation.
While goal-question-metric serves as a good basis for quantitative follow-up studies,
we follow the user-focused technology acceptance model.
Maturity models according to the
Capability Maturity Model Integration
(
SEI 2010
)
do not fit our purposes
because they focus on engineering process improvement beyond
particular development techniques.

Poston & Sexton 1992
present tool survey
guidelines based on technology-focused classification and selection
criteria with a very limited view on tool usefulness and usability.

Miyoshi & Azuma 1993
evaluate
ease of
use
of development environments (i.e., specification and modelling
tools) using metrics from the ISO/IEC 9126 quality model.

From comparing two models
of predicting an individual’s intention to use a tool,

Mathieson 1991
supports technology acceptance model’s validity
and convenience but indicates its limits in providing enough
information on users’ opinions.
For software methods and programming techniques,

Murphy et al. 1999
show how surveys, case
studies, and experiments can be used to compensate for this lack of
information about usefulness and usability.

Because formal methods are by definition based on a formal language and usually
supported by tools, it is reasonable to adopt the technology acceptance model for the
assessment of formal methods.
Unfortunately, the body of literature on the evaluation of formal methods in technology acceptance model
style is very small.
However,
Riemenschneider et al. 2002
apply
technology acceptance model to methods (e.g. UML-based architecture design), concluding that “if
a methodology is not regarded as useful by developers, its prospects
for successful deployment may be severely undermined.”
According to their approach, formal method usage intentions would be driven by

(1)

an organisational mandate to use formal methods,

(2)

the compatibility of formal methods with how engineers perform their work,
and

(3)

the opinions of developers’ coworkers and supervisors toward
using formal methods.

Overall, the application of technology acceptance model to formal methods allows causal
reasoning from formal method user acceptance towards intention of formal method use.

Specialising the approach in

Riemenschneider et al. 2002
,

ease of use
of a formal method characterises the type and amount of
effort a user is likely to spend to learn, adopt, and apply this formal method.

Usefulness
(U) determines how fit a formal method is for its purpose, that is, how well it supports the engineer to accomplish an appropriate task.
If ease of use and U are measured by a survey whose data points are user
perceptions then we talk of
perceived ease of use (PEOU)
and
perceived usefulness (PU)
.
Together, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness form the
user acceptance of a
formal method
and, by support of

Mathieson 1991
;
Riemenschneider et al. 2002
,
can predict the intention to use this formal method.

Whereas technology acceptance model is a model based on the two user-focused constructs
perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness,

Kitchenham et al. 1997
propose a
meta-evaluation approach called DESMET for tools and methods based on
multiple performance indicators (e.g. with technology acceptance model as one of the
indicators).

Most of these works present personal experiences, opinions, case

practitioners and experts. However, we found four similar studies.

Austin & Parkin 1993
sought to explain the low
acceptance of formal methods in industry around 1992. Using a questionnaire
similar to ours with only open questions, they evaluated 111 responses
from a sample of size 444, using a sampling method similar to
ours (then using different channels). Responses from formal method users
are distinguished from general responses. Their questions examine
benefits, limitations, barriers, suggestions to overcome those
barriers, personal reasons for or against the use of formal methods, and
ways of assessing formal methods.

In a second study in 2001,
Snook & Harrison 2001
conduct interviews with
representatives of five companies to discover the main issues involved
in formal method use, in particular, issues of understandability and the
difficulty of creating and utilising formal specifications.

A similar, though more comprehensive interview study was performed by

Woodcock et al. 2009
in 2009. They assess the state of the art of the
application of formal methods, using questionnaires to collect data on 62
industrial projects.

Liebel et al. 2016
assess effects
on and shortcomings of the adoption of model-based engineering in embedded software engineering
including a discussion of formal method adoption. The authors observe a
lack of tool support, bad reputation, and rigid development processes
as obstacles to formal method adoption. Their data suggests a need of
formal method adoption. 30% of the responses from industry declare the
need for formal methods as a reason to adopt model-based engineering. Moreover, responses
indicate that model-based engineering adoption has a positive effect on formal method adoption.
One limitation of their study is the small number of responses from
formal method users.

While these studies focus on the elicitation of the state of the art
and the state of practice, the main focus of our study is to compare
the current formal method adoption or use with the intention to adopt and
use formal methods in the future. To the best of our knowledge, our study
offers the largest set of data points investigating the use of
formal methods in software engineering, so far. In
Section

6.3
, we provide a
further discussion of how our findings relate to the findings of these
studies, particularly to the works of

Austin & Parkin 1993
;
Woodcock et al. 2009
.

Discussion

In this section, we discuss and interpret our findings, relate them to
existing evidence, outline general feedback on the questionnaire, and
critically assess the validity of our study.

6.1
Findings and their Interpretation

The following (F)indings are based on the data summarised and analysed
in the

,
5.3
and
5.2
.
All findings are then collected in

Findings for RQ 1.

(F1)

Regulatory authorities
with their norms, codes, or policies
represent only a minor motivating factor to use formal methods.

Intrinsic motivation
(maybe market-triggered) seems to be
stronger. This finding is consistent with what we know from the
literature survey in

Gleirscher et al. 2019
: formal methods are not
formally required by corresponding standards today, not even for the
highest safety integrity levels. If regulatory authorities change
their recommendations to requirements, then this might spike as a
motivating factor.

(F2)
The low fraction of respondents with no experience in

Figure

3
may have been caused

(1)

by our choice of expert channels in
Table

5
where
the likelihood of encountering formal method users is probably higher than in more generic software engineering channels (e.g. Stack
Overflow) and

(2)

by the fact that software engineering students will usually have an formal method
course or some lectures about formal methods such that they would choose
“1–3 years” in Q
3
and “studied in course”
in Q
3
.

(F3)
We observe the least use of formal methods in computational engineering and for
reasoning about dynamical systems, for example, reasoning about the correctness
of algorithms, and their implementation in embedded software,
controlling such systems. One explanation for this is that our sample
mainly comprises software and systems engineers who will work less
intensively with such formal methods than, for example, mechanical or control engineers.
Another explanation is that such formal methods are still less widely known, less
well developed, or less well supported by tools than formal methods focusing on
the reasoning about pure software.

Findings for RQ 2.

(F4)
It seems that in
all given
domains
(
Figure

9
, except for
other
)
respondents intend to
increase
their future use of formal methods.
Moreover, we observe that this tendency is
independent
of the
particular
formal method class
(except process calculi) or

purpose
. The data also suggest that the use of formal methods by
teachers and researchers is saturated. This saturation indicates a
stable intent to teach formal methods, to perform research in formal methods, or
to otherwise use formal methods in teaching or research. However, there is an
increased intent to apply formal methods in
industrial contexts
in
the future. One explanation could be that engineers have already
wanted to use formal methods but have not had the opportunity or were not
told or permitted to do so. Another explanation for an increased
intent of formal method non-users could be due to some bias when answering
questions about whether someone would do (e.g. try out) something.

(F5)
Our data suggest that experience in using a certain formal method class is
positively associated with the intent to use this formal method class in the
future. To investigate this suspicion, we analysed the intended use
of a formal method class based on the experience of participants in using
this class (also by association analysis as described in

Section

4.6
). We observe that the
more experience

one has with using a specific formal method class, the
more likely
they will apply it in the future
(see the two charts in the

Sections

0.A.3
and
0.A.5
). No experience with a specific formal method class correlates with a

low intent
to use that class. Participants not having used
formal methods and, hence, unfamiliar with them might not have had the need
in the first place. Only little experience with a certain formal method
class
significantly increases the intent to apply it again in
the future
. Similar observations can be made for the use of
formal methods in general for a specific purpose.

(F6)
The differences in past and intended use between code- and model-based
formal methods (
Section

5.4
), for example, when looking at inspection
and assurance, are marginal. Moreover, we cannot find a significant
difference or a trend between these two categories of formal methods when
considering different purposes, experience levels, and usage
frequencies.

(F7)
The approximation in the

6.2
Relationship to TAM for Methods (answering RQ 4)

In analogy to the reasoning in

Davis 1989
, an increased positive
experience with practically applying formal methods forms a high degree of
perceived usefulness (
Section

2
).

Davis 1989
observed that
current and intended usage are significantly correlated with perceived usefulness, less
with perceived ease of use.
In fact, F
6.1
suggests an increased intent to use
formal methods in the future. Moreover, F
6.1

suggests a positive association of the degree of experience with

\mathit{UFM}_{i}
, that is, more experience increases the intent.

(F15)
Because the use of formal methods is not mandatory for most respondents, a
likely explanation for an increased intent (
\mathit{UFM}_{i}
) is that our
respondents perceive the
usefulness of formal methods
to be more
positive than negative.

Inspired by
Riemenschneider et al. 2002
,
in the last paragraph of
Section

4.2
, we justify the use
of challenge scales to collect data for PEOU and PU.
We justify the validity of the formal method-specific challenge scale
using the studies in
Table

1
. The column
“supported by” in
Table

6
indicates studies discussing
the corresponding challenges. From these discussions, we infer that
tackling these challenges contributes to an increased EOU
and U.
First, the studies suggest that formal methods are
easier to use
if
users have sufficient skills and education, if the methods scale to
large systems, if mature tools and automation are available, and if
proofs are easily maintainable and reusable.
Second, the studies suggest that formal methods are
more useful
if
they are compatible with the process, if their cost-benefit ratio is
low, if their abstractions are correct and expressive, and if proofs
can be correctly transferred to reality.
Hence, these challenges represent formal method-specific
substrata
(
Davis 1989
, p. 325)
of
EOU and U for formal methods.
Moreover, a high degree of perceived ease of use corresponds to an increased positive user
experience with formal methods which translates to a low proportion of tough
ratings for the obstacles measured in Q
3
.
However, from F
6.1
, we observe that respondents
rate most challenges as moderate to tough, largely independent of
other variables (F
6.1
).

(F16)
Overall, it thus seems that our respondents perceive the
ease of
use of formal methods
to be more negative than positive. According to

Table

6
, many of the surveyed studies discuss

skills & education
(12 studies) and
tools &
automation
(16) as important challenges. Moreover,

Figure

16
suggests that conceptual difficulties (possibly,
from a lack of education and training, from difficulties in formal method
teaching, from a lack of formal method students) seem to be at least as
responsible for the negative ease of use as the lower ranked tool
deficiencies. Indeed, in a recent discussion of “push-button
verifiers”,
O’Hearn 2018
highlights that both conceptual
expertise and tool deficiencies are still significant bottlenecks.
However, an investigation of respondents’ experiences with formal method
tools in comparison to their experiences with formal method concepts goes
beyond the possibilities of the data collected for this study.

6.3
Relationship to Existing Evidence

Our systematic map shows that our list of challenges is completely
backed by substantial literature (see
Table

6
) raising
and discussing these challenges.

(F17)
However, the fact that maintainability and reusability were least
covered by our literature is, on the one hand, in line with
F
6.1
but, on the other hand, not with
F
6.1
and typical cultures of reuse in practice.

Beyond the general findings about formal method benefits in

Austin & Parkin 1993
, we steered our half-open
questionnaire towards a refined classification of responses, comparing
past with intended use, and interrogating recently perceived obstacles
among a methodologically and geographically more diverse sample.
Their sample mainly covers Z and VDM users in the UK. Our
questionnaire has less focus on representation and methodology and
excludes both questions on benefits and on suggestions to overcome
obstacles. Regarding the latter,

Austin & Parkin 1993
mention the improvement of
education and standardisation, the preparation of case studies, and
the definition of formal method effectiveness metrics.

(F18)
The report of
Austin & Parkin 1993
from the National
Physical Laboratory archive was unfortunately no more available
to us. We finally managed to get access to a paper copy provided by a
friendly colleague. This, however, only happened after conducting
this survey. Anyway, we found that our conclusions are nearly
identical to
Austin & Parkin 1993
’s. The data
from
Figure

16
and
Table

6
confirms that many
of the obstacles (i.e., limitations and barriers) they identified back
in 1991/2
remain (e.g. understanding the notation and the underlying
mathematics, resistance to process changes),
some have been lightly addressed (e.g. lack of cost/benefit evidence)
and
some have been more strongly addressed (e.g. lack of expressiveness,
lack of appropriate tools).
Not mentioned in
Austin & Parkin 1993
is scalability,
rated by our respondents to be the toughest obstacle.

F
6.1
is in line with other observations in

Woodcock et al. 2009
;
Bicarregui et al. 2009
that the repeated use of a formal method
results in lower overheads (i.e., an experienced effort or cost
reduction and improved error removal), up to an order of magnitude
less than its first use
(
Miller et al. 2010
)
.
Finally, our study generalises the main findings about barriers in

Davis et al. 2013
to several geographies and
application domains, however, using an on-line questionnaire instead
of interviews and not asking for barrier mitigations.

7
Conclusions

We conducted an on-line survey of mission-critical software
engineering practitioners and researchers to examine how formal
methods have been used, how these professionals intend to use them,
and how they perceive challenges in using them. This study
aims to contribute to the body of knowledge of the software
engineering and formal methods communities.

Overall Findings.

From the evidence we gathered for the use of formal methods, we make
the following observations:

•

Intrinsic motivation
is stronger than the regulatory one.

•

Despite the challenges, our respondents show an
increased
intent
to use formal methods in industrial contexts.

•

Past experience is
correlated
with usage intent.

•

All challenges were rated
either moderately or highly

difficult, with
scalability, skills, and education leading.
Experienced respondents rate challenges as highly difficult more
often than less experienced respondents.

•

From the literature and the responses, we identified three
additional challenges:
sufficient resources
,
process
compatibility
,
good practicality/reputation
.

•

The negative responses to the questions about obstacles to FM
effectiveness suggest that the
ease of use of formal methods
is
perceived more negative than positive.

•

Gaining experience and confidence in the application of a formal method seems
to play a role in developing a
positive perception of usefulness
of that formal method
.

Barroca & McDermid 1992
present evidence to show that formal methods can be used
in industry effectively and more widely. Their observation from 1992
is that formal method use had been limited, benefits were clear but
limitations were subtle. In response to
Barroca & McDermid 1992
’s
finding “formal methods are both oversold and under-used”, our insights
from the analysis of RQ 2 and 3 lead us to conclude that today
formal methods are probably more underused than oversold. However, our data
also suggests that these methods still need substantial improvement
and support in several areas in order for their benefits to be better
utilised.

General Feedback on the Survey.

The questionnaire seems to be well-received by the
participants. One of them found it an “interesting set of
questions.” This impression is confirmed by another
participant:

“Well chosen questions which do not leave me guessing. Relevant to
future formal method research and practice.”

Another respondent noted:

“Thank you very much for this survey. It is very constructive and
important. It handles most of the issues encountered by any
practitioner and user of formal methods.”

Only one participant found the questionnaire difficult for formal method beginners.

Implications Towards a Research Agenda.

In the spirit of
Jeffery et al. 2015
and complementing the suggestions
from the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats analysis in

Gleirscher et al. 2019
, we want to make
another step in setting out an agenda for future formal method research.

To address
scalability
, we need more research on how
compositional methods (e.g. automated assume-guarantee reasoning,

Cofer et al. 2012
; automated assertion checking,

Leino 2017
) can be better
leveraged in practical settings.
To address
skills and education
, we need an enhanced and
up-to-date
formal method body of knowledge
(FMBoK;
Oliveira et al. 2018
).
From his survey of “formal methods courses in European higher education”,

Oliveira 2004
observes that

(i)

“model-oriented specification”, “formalising distribution,
concurrency and mobility”, and “logical foundations of formal
methods” showed to be the topic areas most frequently taught by formal method
lecturers, and

(ii)

Z, B, SML, CSP, and Haskell showed to be the most popular formal
notations and languages taught in these courses.

A comparison of the current state with
Oliveira 2004
’s
observations can help to evaluate and revise current formal method
curricula (e.g. for undergraduate software engineering as suggested in

Davis et al. 2013
) and to derive
recommendations for improved formal method courses fostering good modelling,
composition, and refinement skills in software engineering practice.
To address
controllable abstractions
,
we need semantics workbenches for underpinning domain-specific
languages with formal semantics.
We believe that further steps in
theory integration and
unification

(
Gleirscher et al. 2019
)
can
help establish proof hierarchies and, hence,
reusability
and

proof transfer
.

To address
process compatibility
, we need more research in

continuous reasoning
(e.g.
O’Hearn 2018
;
Chudnov et al. 2018
), a
revival of activities, possibly even regulations, in tool integration
and model data interchange, and guidance on how to update engineering
development processes.
To address
reputation
, we need to provide more incentives for
practitioners to use formal methods and take recent progress in formal method
research into account when changing current software processes,
policies, regulations, and standards.
This includes convincing practitioners to invest in the support of
large-scale studies for monitoring formal method use in industry.
Cost-savings analyses of formal method applications (e.g.
Jeffery et al. 2015
) supported by strong
empirical designs (i.e., controlled field experiments) can help to
collect the necessary evidence for decision making, successful
knowledge transfer, and for implementing this vision.

This survey underpins and enhances the analysis of strengths and
weaknesses of formal methods in

Gleirscher et al. 2019
and can be a guide

(1)

for consulting and managing practitioners when considering the
introduction of formal methods into a engineering organisation,

(2)

for research managers when shaping a grant programme for formal method
experimentation and transfer, and

(3)

for associate editors when organising a journal special section
on applied formal method research.

Future Work.

Our survey is another important step in the research of effectively
applying FM-based technologies in practice. To put it with the words
of one of our participants: “[A] closed questionnaire is just a
start.”

Hence, we aim at a follow-up study

(i)

to find out which particular formal method (and tool) is used in which
domain for which particular purpose and role (e.g. was SMT
solving used for model checking in certification or for task
scheduling at run-time?),

(ii)

to measure where particular techniques work well (e.g. which
types of formal contracts work well in control software requirements
management in a DO-178C context?),

(iii)

to measure key indicators for successful use of formal methods,

(iv)

to identify management techniques needed to accommodate the
changes in working practices, and, finally,

(v)

to provide guidance to future projects wishing to adopt formal methods.

In a next survey, we like to ask about typical formal method benefits,
about suggestions for barrier
mitigation
(
Davis et al. 2013
)
, pose more
specific questions on scalability and useful abstraction, the
geographical
9
9

9

According to

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_geoscheme
.
and
educational background, and for conceptual alignment. Further
analysis of obstacles, benefits, and usage intent could also benefit
from a more fine-grained distinction between formal methods directly applied
to program code and formal methods focusing on more abstract models. We
would also like to change from 3-level to 5-level Likert-type
scales to receive fine-granular responses. Our research design
accounts for repeatability, hence, allowing us to go for a
longitudinal study.

The research design, and even our current data set, allows the
derivation of the usage intent (
\mathit{UFM}_{i}
) for each formal method class,
application domain, and obstacle. These
\mathit{UFM}_{i}
values could be used
to analyse whether a particular formal method might be

(1)

underused (i.e., domains with an increased usage intent indicate
a potential for more applicability) or

(2)

oversold (i.e., domains with a lower usage intent and were
obstacles are perceived as being particularly tough and, hence,
formal methods as being less effective).
