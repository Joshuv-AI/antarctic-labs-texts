Online And Lightweight Kernel-Based Approximate Policy Iteration for Dynamic p-Norm Linear Adaptive Filtering


Abstract
This paper introduces a solution to the problem of selecting dynamically (online) the “optimal” p-norm to combat outliers in linear adaptive filtering without any knowledge on the probability density function of the outliers. The proposed online and data-driven framework is built on kernel-based reinforcement learning (KBRL). To this end, novel Bellman mappings on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) are introduced. These mappings do not require any knowledge on transition probabilities of Markov decision processes, and are nonexpansive with respect to the underlying Hilbertian norm. The fixed-point sets of the proposed Bellman mappings are utilized to build an approximate policy-iteration (API) framework for the problem at hand. To address the “curse of dimensionality” in RKHSs, random Fourier features are utilized to bound the computational complexity of the API. Numerical tests on synthetic data for several outlier scenarios demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed API framework over several non-RL and KBRL schemes.

Introduction

The least-squares (LS) error/loss (between an observed value and its predicted one) plays a
pivotal role in signal processing, e.g., adaptive filtering
[
1
]
, and
machine learning
[
2
]
. For example, the least-mean squares (LMS) and
recursive (R)LS
[
1
]
are two celebrated algorithms in adaptive filtering
and stochastic approximation based on the LS-error criterion. Notwithstanding, LS methods are
notoriously sensitive to the presence of outliers within data
[
3
]
, where
outliers are defined as (sparsely) contaminating data that do not adhere to a nominal data
generation model, and are often modeled as random variables (RVs) with non-Gaussian heavy
tailed distributions, e.g.,
\alpha
-stable ones
[
4
]
. To combat outliers,
several non-LS criteria, such as least mean p-power (LMP)
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
and
maximum correntropy (MC)
[

]
, have been studied. This work focuses on the LMP
criterion, owing to the well-documented robustness of LMP against outliers
[
12
]
,
while results on MC will be reported elsewhere.

This study is built on the classical data-generation model

y_{n}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{n}+o_{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
denotes discrete
time (
\mathbb{N}
is the set of all non-negative integers),
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}
is the

L\times 1
vector whose entries are the system parameters that need to be identified,
o_{n}
is
the RV which models outliers/noise,
(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})
stands for the input-output pair of
available data, where
\mathbf{x}_{n}
is an
L\times 1
vector and
y_{n}
is real-valued, and

\intercal
denotes vector/matrix transposition. For an arbitrarily fixed
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
,
the LMP algorithm
[
5
]
generates estimates

(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}
according to the following
recursion:

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n+1}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}+\rho p\lvert e_{n}\rvert^{p-2}e_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}\,,

(1)

where
e_{n}\coloneqq y_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}
,

is the learning

is a

user-defined real-valued number within the
interval
[1,2]
to ensure that the
p
-norm loss

\lvert y_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}\rvert^{p}
is a convex function of

\boldsymbol{\theta}

[
5
]
. Notice that if
p=1
and
2
, then (
1
) boils

[
1
]
.

Intuition suggests that the choice of

should be based on the probability density
function (PDF) of the RV

. For example, if
o_{n}
obeys a Gaussian PDF, then
p=2
should
be chosen (recall the maximum-likelihood criterion). To enhance robustness against outliers,
combination of adaptive filters with different forgetting factors, but with the same fixed

p
-norm, have been also introduced
[
8
]
. Nevertheless, it seems that an

online
and
data-driven
solution to the problem of
dynamically

,
without
any prior knowledge on the PDF of
o_{n}
, is yet to be found.

This work offers a solution to the aforementioned open problem via reinforcement learning
(RL)
[
13
]
; a machine-learning paradigm where an “agent”
interacts with the surrounding environment to identify iteratively the policy which minimizes
the cost of its “actions.” More specifically, the well-known policy-iteration (PI)
framework
[
13
]
of RL is adopted, because of its well-documented
merits (e.g.,
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
) over the alternative
RL frameworks of temporal-difference (TD) and Q-learning
[
13
]
,
especially for continuous and high-dimensional state spaces. PI comprises two stages at every
iteration

:
policy evaluation
and
policy improvement
. At policy
evaluation, the current policy is evaluated by a

Q
-function
[
13
]
, which represents, loosely speaking, the
long-term cost that the agent would suffer had the current policy been chosen to determine the
next state, whereas at the policy-improvement stage, the agent uses the
Q
-function value to
update the policy. The underlying state space is considered to be continuous, due to the nature
of
(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})
, while the action space is considered to be discrete: an action is a
value of
p

[1,2]

Deep neural networks offer approximating spaces for Q-functions, e.g.,
[
17
]
, but they
may require processing of batch data (even re-training) during online-mode operation, since
they may face test data generated by PDFs different from those of the training ones (dynamic
environments). Such batch processing inflicts large computational times and complexity,
discouraging the application of deep neural networks to online modes of operation where a small
computational footprint is desired. To meet such computational complexity requirements,
this study builds an approximate (A)PI framework for
online
RL along the lines of
kernel-based (KB)RL
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

Central to the proposed API is the construction of novel Bellman mappings
[
26
,
13
]
. The proposed Bellman mappings are defined on a reproducing
kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}

[
27
,
28
]
, which
serves as the approximating space for the
Q
-functions. Unlike the classical Bellman
operators, where information on transition probabilities in a Markov decision process is
needed
[
13
]
, the proposed Bellman mappings make no use of such
information, and need neither training/offline data nor past policies, but sample and average
the sample space on the fly, at each iteration

, to perform
exploration
of the
surrounding environment. This suits the current adaptive-filtering setting, where the presence
of outliers, with a possibly time-varying PDF, may render the information obtained offline or
from past policies outdated. As such, the proposed Bellman mappings fall closer
to
[
15
]
than to studies which use training data collected beforehand
(offline), e.g.,
[
29
,
16
,
30
]
.

Further, in contrast to the prevailing route in KBRL
[

,
15
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, which views Bellman mappings as

-norm Banach spaces (by definition, no inner product
available), this study introduces nonexpansive
[
31
]
Bellman operators on

\mathcal{H}
to capitalize on the reproducing property of the inner product of

[
27
,
28
]
, and to open the door to powerful

[
31
]
. A byproduct of this path is the additional flexibility
offered to the user by the fact that the fixed-point set of a nonexpansive mapping is
non-singleton in general, as opposed to the case of a contraction mapping which is known to
have a unique fixed point. Supersets of those fixed-point sets are designed to build the
proposed API framework.

It is worth stressing here that the proposed API framework, together with its complementary
study
[
32
]
, appear to be the first attempts to apply RL arguments to robust
adaptive filtering. In contrast to
[
32
]
, where the state space is the

\mathbb{R}^{2L+1}
(
\mathbb{R}
is the set of all real numbers), this study confines

\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Moreover, this study constructs potentially
infinite-dimensional hyperplanes as supersets of the fixed-point sets of a proposed Bellman
mappings, as opposed to
[
32
]
where finite-dimensional affine sets are
designed. To address the “curse of dimensionality,” which arises naturally in online learning
in RKHSs (
\mathcal{H}
may be infinite dimensional), the proposed framework uses random
Fourier features (RFF)
[
33
,
34
]
to bound the computational
complexity of the proposed API, while the approximate-linear-dependency
criterion
[
35
]
, which does not ensure a bounded computational complexity, is
used in
[
32
]
. Finally, to robustify the proposed scheme, experience
replay
[
36
]
is applied, whereas
[
32
]
employs

[
13
]
.

Numerical tests on synthetic data showcase the promising performance of the the advocated
framework, which outperforms several RL and non-RL schemes. Due to space limitations, long
proofs, the convergence analysis of the proposed framework, and further numerical tests will be
reported in the journal version of this paper.

Nonexpansive Bellman Mappings on RKHSs

2.1
State-Action Space

Following the setting of (
1
), the state space
\mathfrak{S}
is assumed to be
continuous. In contrast to
[

]
, where the state space is the high dimensional

\mathbb{R}^{2L+1}

\displaystyle s_{1}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\log_{10}\lvert y_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{n}\rvert\,,

(2a)

\displaystyle s_{2}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{M_{\textnormal{av}}}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{M_{\textnormal{av}}}\log_{10}\frac{\lvert y_{n-k}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{n-k}\rvert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{n-k}\rVert_{2}}\,,

(2b)

\displaystyle s_{3}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\log_{10}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{n}\rVert_{2}\,,

(2c)

\displaystyle s_{4}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varpi s_{4}^{(n-1)}+(1-\varpi)\log_{10}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n-1}\rVert_{2}}{\rho}\,,

(2d)

[

]

-length

[

]

),
and a smoothing auto-regressive process in (

(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The reason for including

\rho
in (

) is to remove

’s effect from

. Owing to (

),
the initial value

in (

\log_{10}[(1/\rho)\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\rVert_{2}]=\log_{10}{p_{0}}+(p_{0}-1)s_{1}^{(0)}+s_{3}^{(0)}
. The

function is employed to decrease the dynamic

).

The action space
\mathfrak{A}
is defined as any finite grid of the interval
[1,2]
, so that

becomes any value of

taken from that finite grid. The

, and

Along the lines of the general notation in

]
, consider now the
set of all mappings

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\{\mu(\cdot)\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mu(\cdot):\mathfrak{S}\to\mathfrak{A}:\mathbf{s}\mapsto\mu(\mathbf{s})\}
. In other words, given a
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
,

\mu(\mathbf{s})
denotes the action that the “system” may take at state
\mathbf{s}
to “move

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}
. The one-step loss for this transition is

g:\mathfrak{Z}\to\mathbb{R}:(\mathbf{s},a)\mapsto g(\mathbf{s},a)
. The set
\Pi

\Pi\coloneqq\mathcal{M}^{\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq\{(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n},\ldots)\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. A policy will be denoted by

. Given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, the stationary policy

\pi_{\mu}\coloneqq(\mu,\mu,\ldots,\mu,\ldots)
. It is customary for

to denote

. Function

2.2
Novel Bellman Mappings

Central to dynamic programming and RL
[
13
]
is the concept of

\forall(\mathbf{s},a)\in\mathfrak{Z}

\displaystyle(T_{\mu}^{\diamond}Q)(\mathbf{s},a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq g(\mathbf{s},a)+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\{Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mu(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}))\}\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle(T^{\diamond}Q)(\mathbf{s},a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq g(\mathbf{s},a)+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}(\mathbf{s},a)}\{\min_{a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\prime},a^{\prime})\}\,,

[0,1)

is

-norm

Nevertheless, in most cases of practical interest, there is not sufficient information on the

3

, and the reproducing

,

\forall Q\in\mathcal{H}

28
]

(4a)

\displaystyle TQ

\displaystyle\coloneqq g+\alpha\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}}\inf\nolimits_{a_{j}\in\mathfrak{A}}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j},a_{j}))\cdot\psi_{j}\,,

The following proposition constructs a superset for the fixed-point set of

Proposition 1

The fixed-point set

\Fix T_{\mu_{n}}^{(n)}\coloneqq\{Q\in\mathcal{H}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}T_{\mu_{n}}^{(n)}Q=Q\}

{H}_{n}\coloneqq\{Q\in\mathcal{H}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})=\langle Q\mathop{}|\mathop{}h_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\}

h_{n}\coloneqq\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\alpha({1}/{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]})\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}\varphi^{\textnormal{av}}_{\mu_{n},j}[n]

Proof.

Consider any

,
where
\mathbf{s}_{n}
and
a_{n}
are the current state vector and action at iteration
n
,
respectively, the reproducing property of the inner product of
\mathcal{H}
yields

0=\langle Q-T_{\mu_{n}}^{(n)}Q\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, or via
(
4a
):

\displaystyle 0=\langle Q-g_{n}-\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}[n],\mu_{n}(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}[n]))\psi_{j}^{(n)}\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle{}g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})=Q(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}[n],\mu_{n}(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}[n]))\psi_{j}^{(n)}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\,.

(6)

Vectors
\{\psi_{j}^{(n)}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}
can be designed as follows:

\psi_{j}^{(n)}\in\{\psi\in\mathcal{H}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\langle\psi\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\psi(\mathbf{z}_{n})=1/N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]\}
,

\forall j\in N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]
. Notice that the set from where
\psi_{j}^{(n)}
is chosen
from is a nonempty hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}
. Under this choice, (
6
)
becomes:

\displaystyle g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})=Q(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\alpha\tfrac{1}{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}Q(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}[n],\mu_{n}(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{av}}_{j}[n]))

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow{}

\displaystyle{}g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})=\langle Q\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\alpha\tfrac{1}{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}\varphi^{\textnormal{av}}_{\mu_{n},j}[n]\,\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,,

(7)

which completes the proof.
∎

The one-step loss function
g_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
is chosen here such that

\displaystyle g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})=\tfrac{1}{M_{\textnormal{av}}}\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{M_{\textnormal{av}}-1}\log_{10}\frac{\lvert y_{n-k}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n+1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{n-k}\rvert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{n-k}\rVert_{2}}\,,

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n+1}
is provided by (
1
). Recall by (
2b
) that the
right-hand-side of (
8
) is nothing but
s_{2}^{(n+1)}
. There always exists

g_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
such that (
8
) is satisfied, since one can choose any

g_{n}
from the nonempty hyperplane:

\{g\in\mathcal{H}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\langle g\mathop{}|\mathop{}\varphi(\mathbf{z}_{n})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=g(\mathbf{z}_{n})=s_{2}^{(n+1)}\}
.

Given the current estimate
Q_{n}
of the Q-function, there are several ways to update to

Q_{n+1}
from the hyperplane
{H}_{n}
. For example,

Q_{n+1}\coloneqq P_{{H}_{n}}(Q_{n})
, where
P_{{H}_{n}}(\cdot)
stands for the
(metric) projection mapping onto
{H}_{n}

[
31
]
. Or, via the minimum-norm
solution
Q_{n+1}\coloneqq P_{{H}_{n}}(0)
. Nevertheless, to offer even a more
standard approach, the classical steepest-descent methodology on the quadratic loss

{L}_{n}(Q)\coloneqq(1/2)[\langle Q\mathop{}|\mathop{}h_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})]^{2}
:

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q_{n}-\eta\nabla{L}_{n}(Q_{n})

\displaystyle=Q_{n}-\eta[\langle Q_{n}\mathop{}|\mathop{}h_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-g_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{n})]h_{n}\,,

(9)

is provided here, where
\eta
is the learning rate (step size).

Although there are many ways to generate samples

\{\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\textnormal{av}}[n]\}_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]}
, see for
example
[
32
]
, a different approach than
[
32
]
is followed
here. In short, past data are re-used, as in

\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\textnormal{av}}[n]\coloneqq[\log_{10}|y_{n+1-j}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n+1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{n+1-j}|,s_{2}^{(n)},\log_{10}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{n+1-j}\rVert_{2},s_{4}^{(n)}]^{\intercal}
,

j\in\{1,\ldots,N_{\textnormal{av}}[n]\}
, to capitalize on the fact that RVs

(\mathbf{x}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are IID in
Section

4
.

To robustify the proposed API, (prioritized) experience replay
[
36
]
is
utilized to allow re-use of past data. To this end, an experience-replay (ER) buffer is
constructed to comprise information

\{\mathbf{s}_{\nu},a_{\nu},g_{\nu},\{\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\textnormal{av}}[\nu]\}_{j=1}^{N_{\textnormal{av}}[\nu]}\}
which is collected at instances
\nu
taken from

\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Whenever experience replay is applied, data from the ER buffer are
utilized. In short, the following route is followed at each
n
:

Q_{n}\to\eqref{Q.update}\to[\text{Re-use past data from the ER buffer}]\to\eqref{Q.update}\to Q_{n+1}
. Details on how to select information for the ER buffer and to utilize that
information in the proposed API will be reported in the journal version of the paper.

A direct application of (
9
) may lead to memory and computational complications,
since at each
n
, (
9
) potentially adds new kernel functions into the
representation of
Q_{n+1}
via
h_{n}
. This unpleasant phenomenon is fueled by the potential
infinite dimensionality of
\mathcal{H}
; see for example the case where the kernel of

\mathcal{H}
is the Gaussian
[
28
]

\kappa_{\textnormal{G}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})
,

(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
, as in
Section

4
. To address this
“curse of dimensionality,” this work employs the methodology of RFF
[
33
]
. Avoiding most
of the details due to space limitations,

\kappa_{\textnormal{G}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})
is approximated by the following inner
product
\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{z})^{\intercal}\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})
, where the
Euclidean feature vector

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq(\tfrac{2}{D})^{1/2}[\cos{(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}+b_{1})},\ldots,\cos{\mathbf{(v}_{D}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}+b_{D})}]^{\intercal}\,,

(10)

with
D\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
being a user-defined dimension, while
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
and

\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{D}
are RVs following the Gaussian and uniform distributions, respectively. The
feature mapping (
10
) is used instead of
\varphi(\cdot)
throughout this work to
transfer learning from the infinite dimensional
(\mathcal{H},\kappa_{\textnormal{G}})
to the

D
-dimensional
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Mapping (
10
) together with the low-complexity
iteration (
9
) yield an API with bounded computational complexity.
