Well-posedness and stationary states for a crowded active Brownian system with size-exclusion
Martin Burger, Simon Schulz

Abstract
. We prove the existence of solutions to a non-linear, non-local, degenerate equation which was previously derived as the formal hydrodynamic limit of an active Brownian particle system, where the particles are endowed with a position and an orientation. This equation incorporates diffusion in both the spatial and angular coordinates, as well as a non-linear non-local drift term, which depends on the angle-independent density. The spatial diffusion is non-linear degenerate and also comprises diffusion of the angle-independent density, which one may interpret as cross-diffusion with infinitely many species. Our proof relies on interpreting the equation as the perturbation of a gradient flow in a Wasserstein-type space. It generalizes the boundedness-by-entropy method to this setting and makes use of a gain of integrability due to the angular diffusion. For this latter step, we adapt a classical interpolation lemma for function spaces depending on time. We also prove uniqueness in the particular case where the non-local drift term is null, and provide existence and uniqueness results for stationary equilibrium solutions. Keywords: Boundedness-by-entropy; active particles; cross-diffusion; gradient flow. AMS Subject Classification: 35K65; 35K55; 76M30; 35Q92.



1.
Introduction

We study the following non-local non-linear degenerate equation, which was derived in
[
6
]
as the formal hydrodynamic limit of a many-particle system modeling the interaction of active Brownian particles endowed with a two-dimensional position
x\in\Omega=(0,2\pi)^{2}
and orientation
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\mathrm{Pe}\dv\big(f(1-\rho){\bf e}(\theta)\big)=D_{e}\dv\big((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho\big)+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f,

where
\rho(t,x)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(t,x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
is the
angle-independent density
,

{\bf e}(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
, the real constant parameters
\mathrm{Pe}
and
D_{e}>0
are respectively the Péclet number and spatial diffusion coefficient, supplemented with initial data
f_{0}
and periodic boundary conditions in both the angle and space variables; the periodic cell for the space-angle coordinates is denoted by
\Upsilon:=\Omega\times(0,2\pi)
. We prove the existence of weak solutions to (
1.1
), show uniqueness of such solutions in the case of zero Péclet number, and provide existence and uniqueness results for stationary states under various assumptions on the Péclet number.

Active Brownian systems model the motion of self-propelled particles and have an abundance of applications; for instance in biology, pedestrian and traffic flows, and the modeling of collective behaviour in general (
cf. e.g.
[
24
,
10
,
23
,
11
,
26
,
27
,
25
]
). Much like kinetic models, active Brownian systems depict the
mesoscopic
dynamics of a group of interacting agents and, indeed, equation (
1.1
) presents similarities with kinetic Fokker–Planck equations as well as non-linear degenerate parabolic systems. At first glance, (
1.1
) appears similar to the cross-diffusion system studied in
[
8
]
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r=\dv\big((1-\sigma)\nabla r+r\nabla\sigma+r(1-\sigma)\nabla V\big),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}b=\dv\big((1-\sigma)\nabla b+b\nabla\sigma+b(1-\sigma)\nabla W\big),

where
\sigma=r+b
and
V,W
are given external potentials. Equation (
1.1
) may be interpreted as the analog of this system with infinitely-many species; the diffusive term
\dv f\nabla\rho
encodes the diffusion of the uncountable family of angles in the interval
(0,2\pi)
. However, unlike the system in
[
8
]
, the equation (
1.1
) is non-local; both in its diffusive and drift terms. This non-locality is a feature shared by certain kinetic models, in particular the Vlasov–Benney equation
[
4
,
16
]
and the recent contribution
[
5
]
. Moreover, the angle-independent density, which formally satisfies the strongly parabolic drift-diffusion equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\dv\big((1-\rho){\bf p}\big)=\Delta\rho,

where
{\bf p}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f{\bf e}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, is expected to be more regular than
f
as its governing equation is no longer degenerate. This mechanism is evocative of velocity averaging lemmas (see,
e.g.
,
[
22
]
), illustrating another parallel between kinetic theory and active Brownian systems. The zones of degeneracy
\{\rho=1\}
and
\{f=0\}
comprised in (
1.1
) make its analysis delicate as one expects the formation of discontinuities and other losses of regularity along these interfaces; this first degenerate region is all-the-more troublesome because one can only infer information about the local behaviour of
\rho
, not
f
. In turn, there exists relatively few known results in the existing literature for such equations.

To the authors’ knowledge, there exists only one prior contribution studying equations similar to (
1.1
) (except the authors’ own work
[
7
]
, which has a different diffusion model and thus less degeneracy), namely the result of Erignoux
[
13
, Theorem 2.3.3]
. Therein, the author relies solely on probabilistic techniques to compute the hydrodynamic limit of the underlying particle system, thereby establishing the existence of a very weak solution; defined only as a measure. The present contribution provides an entirely deterministic PDE-theoretic proof, and we show the existence of a Sobolev weak solution with stronger regularity properties by means of the boundedness-by-entropy principle and a vanishing diffusivity method. Our strategy relies on using the linear diffusion in angle to upgrade the integrability of
f
and obtain improved compactness properties; for this we adapt a classical interpolation lemma (
cf.
Appendix
A
) . We also prove uniqueness of our solution within the stronger class of admissible weak solutions when the Péclet number is assumed to be null, and provide existence and uniqueness results for stationary solutions.

At the core of the aforementioned boundedness-by-entropy method (originally developed in
[
8
]
) is the notion that equation (
1.1
) may be interpreted as a perturbation of a Wasserstein gradient flow associated to the entropy functional

E[f]=\int_{\Upsilon}\big(f\log f+(1-\rho)\log(1-\rho)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi};

see §
2
for further details. One is able to obtain additional
a priori
estimates on the equation by considering the natural dual problem from the point of view of the underlying variational evolution. That is to say, we consider the first variation
u=E^{\prime}[f]
and rewrite (
1.1
) as an equation on
u
, which reads as a perturbation of a gradient flow associated to the convex conjugate functional
E^{*}[u]
. By rewriting the original unknown
f
in terms of
u
, one is able to immediately determine the non-negativity of
f
and the
a priori
bounds
0\leq\rho\leq 1
. This generalizes the boundedness-by-entropy method developed for the related cross-diffusion system (
1.2
) in
[
8
]
and later generalized in
[
17
]
. The underlying philosophy is analogous to the reformulation of Lagrangian to Hamiltonian mechanics; now written in the context of Wasserstein gradient flows with nontrivial mobility.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In §
1.1
we state our main results precisely, and outline our strategy in §
1.2
. §
2
introduces the boundedness-by-entropy method; which we use to construct a sequence of suitable transformed regularised problems related to (
1.1
). In §
3
, we prove the existence of the Galerkin approximation to the aforementioned transformed regularised problems and obtain uniform estimates. In §
4
, we give the proof of our main existence result. In §
5
, we prove uniqueness of the solution obtained in §
4
under the assumption that the Péclet number is null. Finally, in §
6
, we prove the existence and uniqueness of stationary states under particular assumptions on the Péclet number. Appendix
A
contains an interpolation lemma pertaining to the uniform estimates of §
3.2
, while Appendix
B
contains technical results related to the sequence of regularised initial data used for the existence proof.

C
will denote a positive constant independent of the parameters
\varepsilon,n
, which may change from line to line. Unless stated explicitly otherwise, the symbols
\dv,\nabla,\Delta
denote divergences, gradients, and Laplacians taken with respect to the space variable

, while the symbols
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}},\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}

\bm{\xi}=(x,\theta)
. The domain on which the equation is posed will be denoted by
\Upsilon_{T}:=(0,T)\times\Upsilon
, the closure of which we denote by
\overline{\Upsilon}_{T}:=[0,T]\times\overline{\Upsilon}
; the sets
\Omega_{T}
and
\overline{\Omega}_{T}
are defined analogously. In later estimates and weak formulations, we shall make use of the families of admissible test functions
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}_{s}
, defined by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=\big\{\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}):\,\psi(t,\cdot)\text{ triply }2\pi\text{-periodic in }x,\theta,\,\psi(0,\cdot)=\psi(T,\cdot)=0\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{s}:=\big\{\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}):\,\psi(t,\cdot)\text{ doubly }2\pi\text{-periodic in }x,\,\psi(0,\cdot)=\psi(T,\cdot)=0\big\},

and test functions will always be denoted by the letter
\psi
. We define the function spaces

\displaystyle X:=L^{6}(0,T;W^{1,6}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)),\quad Y:=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)),\quad Z:=W^{1,6}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{X}:=\Big\{f\in&L^{3}(0,T;L^{3}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)):f\geq 0\text{ a.e.},\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f}\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T}),\partial_{t}f\in X^{\prime},\\
&\rho={\int_{0}^{2\pi}}f\,\mathrm{d}\theta\in[0,1]\text{ a.e.},\partial_{t}\rho\in Y^{\prime},\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T}),\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\sqrt{f}\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})\Big\},\end{aligned}

and, in what follows, the notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
will always refer to the obvious underlying duality product where there is no confusion, unless stated explicitly otherwise. In the previous definitions and in what follows, the functions spaces denoted by
Z_{\mathrm{per}}(S)
for
S\in\{(0,2\pi)^{d}\}_{d=1}^{3}
are understood to mean

Z_{\mathrm{per}}(S):=\big\{g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}:\,\|g\|_{Z(S)}<\infty,\text{ and }g(y+2\pi{\bf e}_{i})=g(y)\,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\big\},

where
\{{\bf e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The case
d=2
corresponds to functions depending on
x
and
d=3
to functions depending on
x,\theta
.

1.1.
Main theorems

In order to state our main existence result, we begin by introducing our notion of solution.

Definition 1.1

(Weak solution)
.

We say that
f\in\mathcal{X}
is a
weak solution
of (
1.1
) with periodic boundary conditions if, for all
\psi\in X
, there holds

(1.4)

\displaystyle-\langle\partial_{t}f,\psi\rangle+\mathrm{Pe}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho)

\displaystyle f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\Big(D_{e}\big((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\psi+\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\psi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

We note that all integrals in the above weak formulation are well-defined since, using the Jensen and Hölder inequalities,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\psi=2\underbrace{\sqrt{f}}_{\in L^{6}}\underbrace{\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f}}_{\in L^{2}}\underbrace{\partial_{\theta}\psi}_{\in L^{6}}\in L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon_{T})\subset L^{1}(\Upsilon_{T})

\displaystyle f\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\psi=-2\underbrace{f}_{\in L^{3}}\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\rho}}_{\in L^{\infty}}\underbrace{\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}}_{L^{2}}\cdot\underbrace{\nabla\psi}_{L^{6}}\in L^{1}(\Upsilon_{T})

\displaystyle(1-\rho)\nabla f\cdot\nabla\psi=2\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\rho}}_{\in L^{\infty}}\underbrace{\sqrt{f}}_{\in L^{6}}\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\sqrt{f}}_{\in L^{2}}\cdot\underbrace{\nabla\psi}_{\in L^{6}}\in L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon_{T})\subset L^{1}(\Upsilon_{T}).

Note that one may identify the duality product with

\langle\partial_{t}f,\psi\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}f(t,\cdot),\psi(t,\cdot)\rangle_{Z^{\prime}\times Z}\,\mathrm{d}t,

whence a standard argument (
cf.

[
3
]
) shows that the weak formulation of Definition
1.1
is equivalent to requiring that, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{A}
, there holds

(1.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}f\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{Pe}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho)

\displaystyle f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\Big(D_{e}\big((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\psi+\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\psi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Our main existence result is the following.

Theorem 1

(Existence of weak solution)
.

Let
T>0
be arbitrary. Let
f_{0}
be measurable non-negative initial data such that
f_{0}\in L^{p}(\Upsilon)
for some
p>1
and
\rho_{0}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\in[0,1]
a.e. in
\Omega
. Then, there exists
f\in\mathcal{X}
a weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
. Furthermore, the initial data is attained in the sense
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|f(t,\cdot)-f_{0}\|_{Z^{\prime}}=0
.

The essential requirement on
f_{0}
in the above theorem is the finiteness of the entropy functional
E[f_{0}]
. This is of course satisfied by imposing the stronger condition
f_{0}\in L^{p}(\Upsilon)
for some
p>1
. For clarity of exposition, we choose this latter requirement in order to frame the problem in Lebesgue spaces instead of Orlicz spaces. We note in passing that the weak solution obtained in Theorem
1
also satisfies an alternative notion of weak solution which is more convenient when studying regularity (
cf.

[
1
]
); this alternative weak formulation is written at the end of §
4
.

Remark 1.2

(Attainment of initial data)
.

Note that
\mathcal{X}\subset W^{1,\frac{6}{5}}(0,T;Z^{\prime})
, whence (see,
e.g.
,
[
14
, Theorem 2, §5.9.2]
)
f\in C([0,T];Z^{\prime})
and
\|f(t,\cdot)-f(s,\cdot)\|_{Z^{\prime}}\leq|t-s|^{1/6}C(\|\partial_{t}f\|_{Y^{\prime}})
for a.e.
0<s<t<T
. Hence, the attainment of the initial data is phrased in the sense of
Z^{\prime}
.

Our main uniqueness result is the following, and focuses on the case of zero Péclet number.

Theorem 2

(Uniqueness for null Péclet number)
.

If
\mathrm{Pe}=0
, then the weak solution provided by Theorem
1
is unique in
\mathcal{X}
.

Finally, we study the existence and uniqueness of stationary states of (
1.1
) in §
6
. We mostly concentrate on uniqueness, which we divide into two parts: uniqueness for zero Péclet number, and uniqueness of strong solutions near
constant
stationary states for general Péclet number. We delay the statements of these results to §
6
.

1.2.
Strategy and set-up

In this section, we describe our strategy of proof for the main existence result Theorem
1
in §
1.2.1
, and then describe our mollification procedure for regularising the initial data in §
1.2.2
.

1.2.1.
Strategy

We briefly outline our strategy of proof. As already mentioned, the equation (
1.1
) may be interpreted as a perturbation of a Wasserstein gradient flow associated to the entropy functional
E
. It is therefore natural to reframe the problem in terms of the first variation of
E[f]
, which we denote by
u=E^{\prime}[f]
; see §
2
for more details. The resulting equation for
u
is again non-linear, non-local, and degenerate-parabolic. We therefore add the regularisation term
\varepsilon\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}u
to this equation, and compute Galerkin approximations of the solution of this equation, which we denote by
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
; where
n
denotes the
n
-th Galerkin approximation. These approximations give rise to approximations
f^{\varepsilon}_{n}
for the solution of (
1.1
) by a simple explicit formula. After proving the required uniform estimates for the families of functions
\{u^{\varepsilon}_{n},f^{\varepsilon}_{n},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}\}_{n,\varepsilon}
, which are independent of
n
and
\varepsilon
, we perform a diagonal procedure so as to simultaneously take the limit
n\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
, and we obtain a weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
.

1.2.2.
Set-up for initial data

We shall approximate the initial data with smooth strictly positive functions. We consider the product mollifier sequence

\eta_{\varepsilon}(x,\theta):=\alpha_{\varepsilon}(x)\beta_{\varepsilon}(\theta),

where
\alpha,\beta
are compactly supported smooth non-negative bump functions with unit integral;
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-2}\alpha(x/\varepsilon)
,
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=\varepsilon^{-\gamma}\beta(\theta/\varepsilon^{\gamma})
for
\gamma>2
. Define, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the strictly positive periodic functions

\displaystyle f_{0}^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\pi}+(1-\varepsilon)\eta_{\varepsilon}*f_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\Upsilon}),

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{\varepsilon}*\rho_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\Omega}),

where the convolutions are understood in the sense
\eta_{\varepsilon}*f_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\eta_{\varepsilon}(\cdot-\bm{\xi})f_{0}(\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}
and
\alpha_{\varepsilon}*\rho_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\cdot-x)\rho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. A straightforward computation (
cf.
Appendix
B
) shows that
0<f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
and
0<\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}<1
, whence the function

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\varepsilon}:=\log\Big(\frac{f^{\varepsilon}_{0}}{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}}\Big)\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\Upsilon})

is also well-defined. The next lemma summarises the key properties of the regularised initial data. The proof is delayed to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 1.3

(Properties of the regularised initial data)
.

There exists a subsequence of the regularised initial data sequence
\{f^{\varepsilon}_{0},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0},u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\}_{\varepsilon}
, which we do not relabel, such that

f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\to f_{0}\text{ strongly in }L^{p}(\Upsilon),\quad\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\to\rho_{0}\text{ strongly in }L^{q}(\Omega)\text{ for all }q\in[1,\infty),

and for which there exists a positive constant
C=C(p,\Upsilon)
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

\displaystyle E[f^{\varepsilon}_{0}]+\varepsilon\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\leq C\big(1+\|f_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\big).

2.
Entropy Variable and Transformed Problem

In the following we will perform a transformation of the problem (
1.1
) into entropy variables, which diagonalizes the diffusion part at the cost of coupling the time derivatives of
f
and
\rho
. This transformation allows for a suitable approximation via the Galerkin method, as it did for the cross-diffusion system (
1.2
) considered in
[
8
]
, and an application of the boundedness-by-entropy principle; developed again in
[
8
]
and subsequently generalised in
[
17
]
.

For this sake we consider the aforementioned
entropy functional
of (
1
),
i.e.
,

E[f]=\int_{\Upsilon}\big(f\log f+(1-\rho)\log(1-\rho)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

where
\rho=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\,\mathrm{d}\theta
. Note that
E[f]
is bounded from below by a fixed constant for all
f
, provided
0\leq\rho\leq 1
and
f\geq 0
a.e. We can define
E
on a dense subspace of
L^{p}(\Upsilon)
for any
p>1
,
e.g.
, smooth functions, and extend it uniquely to the whole space. Note that
E
is a convex functional, and we may define its convex conjugate, or equivalently its Legendre transform,
E^{*}:L^{p^{\prime}}(\Upsilon)\to\mathbb{R}

via

E^{*}[u]:=\sup\big\{\langle u,f\rangle-E[f]:f\in L^{p}(\Upsilon)\big\},

which is again a convex functional. In fact, we may compute
E^{*}
explicitly as

E^{*}[u]=\int_{\Omega}\log\left(1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

As usual we define the
entropy variable
(or dual variable) related to
f
via the functional derivative

(2.1)

u:=E^{\prime}[f]=\log f-\log(1-\rho).

We therefore write
f
and
\rho
as functions of
u:\Upsilon_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
via the expressions:

\displaystyle f=(1-\rho)e^{u},\qquad\rho=(1-\rho)\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

It follows that

(2.2)

\rho(t,x)=\frac{\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u(t,x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u(t,x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}

automatically satisfies the bound
0\leq\rho\leq 1
. In turn, we obtain

(2.3)

f(t,x,\theta)=\frac{e^{u(t,x,\theta)}}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u(t,x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}.

Note that this latter formula makes the relation
(E^{*})^{\prime}=(E^{\prime})^{-1}
explicit,
i.e.
,

u=E^{\prime}[f],\quad f=(E^{*})^{\prime}[u].

The main idea to analyze the system is as follows: the original problem (
1.1
) is considered as a perturbation of the gradient flow

\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\left(M(f,\rho)(\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}E^{\prime}[f]+V)\right)

with mobility
M
and perturbative vector field
V
, independent of
f
, given by

M(f,\rho)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}D_{e}f(1-\rho)I&0\\
0&f\end{array}\right),\qquad V(x,\theta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\mathrm{Pe}~{\bf e}(\theta)\\
0\end{array}\right).

Transforming to the entropy variable we arrive at

(2.4)

\partial_{t}((E^{*})^{\prime}[u])=\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\left(\tilde{M}(u)(\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u+V)\right),

where
\tilde{M}(u)=M(f[u],\rho[u])
is given by

(2.5)

\tilde{M}(u)=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{e^{u}}{(1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u}\,\mathrm{d}\theta)^{2}}D_{e}I&0\\
0&\frac{e^{u}}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\end{matrix}\right).

This dual formulation has several advantages. First of all, the boundedness-by-entropy principle applies,
i.e.
if we find a weak solution
u
of the above, it is apparent that after transformation
f\geq 0
and
0\leq\rho\leq 1
hold almost everywhere.
Moreover, the transformed system can be approximated more easily,
e.g.
by regularising with a multiple of the heat operator. This yields a problem of the form

(2.6)

(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}))[\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}]=\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\left((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon})V\right),

where, for all
u\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\Upsilon)
, the quantity
A(u)
is a linear operator which corresponds to the second functional derivative of
E^{*}
evaluated on the element
u
; furthermore, since
E^{*}
is convex, this quantity is a positive semi-definite linear operator. In fact, one can explicitly write

(2.7)

\displaystyle A(u^{\varepsilon})[\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}]=\frac{e^{u^{\varepsilon}}}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-\frac{e^{u^{\varepsilon}}}{\big(1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}}\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

In principle, one can now write a proof of existence and uniqueness including some regularity for the regularised problem (
2.7
); this is the content of §
3
. Moreover, testing the equation with
u
preserves
a priori
estimates for the entropy, noticing that

(2.8)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E[f]=\langle E^{\prime}(f),\partial_{t}f\rangle_{\Upsilon}=\langle u,\partial_{t}((E^{*})^{\prime}[u])\rangle_{\Upsilon}.

3.
Galerkin Approximation for the Transformed Regularised System

The main result of this section is as follows. We shall prove it in several steps: first, we obtain the existence of the Galerkin approximations to the regularised problem (
2.6
) in §
3.1
, and then we obtain the desired uniform estimates in §
3.2
.

Proposition 3.1

(Existence and uniform estimates for Galerkin approximations)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists functions
u^{\varepsilon}_{n},f^{\varepsilon}_{n},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Upsilon}_{T})
satisfying the relations

(3.1)

f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\theta)=\frac{e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\theta)}}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta},\qquad\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\in[0,1]

in
\Upsilon_{T}
, such that, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{A}
, there holds

(3.2)

\displaystyle-\langle\partial_{t}(\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}_{n}),\psi\rangle+\mathrm{Pe}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\Big(\varepsilon\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\psi+D_{e}\big((1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla f^{\varepsilon}_{n}+f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}\big)\cdot\nabla\psi+\partial_{\theta}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\partial_{\theta}\psi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t,

Furthermore there exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
\varepsilon
and
n
, such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](t)+\esssup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Upsilon}|\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C,

as well as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{3}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{\frac{10}{3}}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(0,T;W^{1,\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon))}\leq C,

and the time-derivative bounds

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}(\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+f^{\varepsilon}_{n})\|_{X^{\prime}}\leq C,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}(\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\rho^{\varepsilon})\|_{Y^{\prime}}\leq C,

where
U^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
also satisfies

(3.9)

\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}U^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}U^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C.

3.1.
Existence of Galerkin approximants

In what follows, we use that the biharmonic operator
\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}^{2}
, considered with periodic boundary conditions in space and angle on
\Upsilon
, is a coercive, bounded, linear self-adjoint operator with respect to the inner product of
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
, where

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)}=\int_{\Upsilon}\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}f\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}g\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

It follows from the classical spectral theorem on Hilbert spaces that
\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}^{2}
admits a discrete spectrum
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\dots
with eigenfunctions
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that there holds
\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}^{2}\varphi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\varphi_{j}
for all
j
; a classical bootstrapping procedure ensures that
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\Upsilon})
for all
j
,
cf.

e.g.

[
2
, Chapitre 7]
. Furthermore,
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis of
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
.

We note in passing that our choice of functional setting
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
is motivated by the fact that we must control the
L^{\infty}
norm of the sequence of Galerkin approximations
\{u_{n}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\}_{n}
in order to estimate the approximations
\{f_{n}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\}_{n}
via the entropy variable formula (
3.1
); no
L^{p}
control for
p<\infty
suffices. This is made possible by approximating
u^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)
in
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
and using Morrey’s inequality over the three-dimensional open domain
\Upsilon
; see Step 1 of the proof of Lemma
3.3
for details.

For each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define the closed subspace
\mathcal{V}_{n}:=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. Note from our previous discussion that
\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{V}_{n}
is dense in
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
,
\mathcal{V}_{n}\subset\mathcal{V}_{n+1}
for all
n
, and, for each
n
, the finite subset
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}
. We now define the
n
-th approximant to the entropy variable
u^{\varepsilon}
, to be given by the explicit formula

(3.10)

u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\theta):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{\varepsilon,n}(t)\varphi_{j}(x,\theta),

where the coefficients
\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}
are to be determined. This construction is such that, for large
n
, the function
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
ought to be a suitable approximation of
u^{\varepsilon}
; a weak solution in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
of (
2.6
).

One can see, by plugging in
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
in place of
u^{\varepsilon}
in (
2.6
), that the coefficients
\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}
will be determined from requiring

(3.11)

(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}\varphi_{j}\Big]=\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\Big((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\varphi_{j}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\Big)

in the weak sense in
\mathcal{V}_{n}
,
i.e.
, for all
\phi\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
, there holds

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+

\displaystyle A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\varphi_{j}]\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}\int_{\Upsilon}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\phi\cdot\left((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\varphi_{j}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

for all
t\in(0,T)
, where we used linearity and
A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\alpha_{j}^{\varepsilon,n}(t)\varphi_{j}]=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\alpha_{j}^{\varepsilon,n}(t)A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})[\varphi_{j}]
, which can be seen directly from the formula (
2.7
). Using the decomposition of
\phi
into the basis functions
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, the previous relation is equivalent to requiring: for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there holds

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+

\displaystyle A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\varphi_{j}]\varphi_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}\int_{\Upsilon}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\varphi_{k}\cdot\left((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\varphi_{j}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

for all
t\in(0,T)
. Note that, provided the coefficients
\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}
belong to
C^{1}([0,T])
, all terms in the previous equality are well-defined; in particular, the integrals are finite due to the boundedness of the functions
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\Upsilon})
. The above can be recast as a quasilinear system of ODEs of the form

(3.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{M}(\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}(t))\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathcal{R}(\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}(t)),\\
&\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}(0)=\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where we have defined the vector function
\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}=(\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{n})
and the entries of the matrices are given explicitly by

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}(t))_{kj}=\int_{\Upsilon}(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\varphi_{j}]\varphi_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}(t))_{kj}=-\int_{\Upsilon}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\varphi_{k}\cdot\left((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\varphi_{j}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

where
k,j
range over all possible values in
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and the initial data is given explicitly by

(3.14)

\alpha_{j}^{\varepsilon,n}(0)=\langle u^{\varepsilon}_{0},\varphi_{j}\rangle_{H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)},

such that, with
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)=u^{\varepsilon}_{n,0}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}(0)\varphi_{j}
, there holds the strong convergence

(3.15)

u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\to u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\quad\text{in }H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon).

It follows from the positivity
\varepsilon>0
and the positive semidefiniteness of the operator
A
that the matrix
\mathcal{M}
is invertible, and furthermore the product
\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{R}
is locally Lipschitz; indeed, this quantity and its derivatives involve only exponential functions of the
\alpha^{\varepsilon,n}_{j}
and their products, weighted by the reciprocal of a determinant which is bounded strictly away from zero for each
\varepsilon>0
. In turn, the Cauchy–Lipschitz Theorem for ODE systems implies the existence and uniqueness of
\gamma^{\varepsilon,n}\in C^{1}([0,T])
solving (
3.12
).

It follows that we have proved the following result, which concludes this subsection.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
u^{\varepsilon}_{n,0}\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
be given by

u^{\varepsilon}_{n,0}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi_{j}\langle u^{\varepsilon}_{0},\varphi_{j}\rangle_{H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)}.

There exists
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Upsilon}_{T})
such that
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)=u^{\varepsilon}_{n,0}
and, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
, there holds for all
t\in(0,T)

(3.16)

\int_{\Upsilon}(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}]\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=-\int_{\Upsilon}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\psi\cdot\left((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

i.e., in the weak sense with respect to
\mathcal{V}_{n}
,

(3.17)

(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}]=\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\left((\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\right).

We then define
f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{\Upsilon})
and its angle-independent density
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
by

(3.18)

f^{\varepsilon}_{n}:=(E^{*})^{\prime}[u^{\varepsilon}_{n}]=\frac{e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}}}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta},\quad\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta},

whence

(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}]=\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\partial_{t}((E^{*})^{\prime}[u^{\varepsilon}_{n}])=\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}_{n},

and from which we obtain, using also (
2.8
),

(3.19)

\int_{\Upsilon}(\varepsilon\mathrm{id}+A(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))[\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}]u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}].

At this point, we record the relations

(3.20)

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\partial_{\theta}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}=\partial_{\theta}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}=2\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}},

\displaystyle(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{n}=(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla f^{\varepsilon}_{n}+f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}=2\sqrt{(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\nabla\sqrt{(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}+2f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n},

whence the equation (
3.17
) may be rewritten as

(3.21)

\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\mathrm{Pe}\dv((1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}{\bf e}(\theta))=\varepsilon\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+D_{e}\dv((1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla f^{\varepsilon}_{n}+f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}.

It follows from an integration by parts that (
3.2
) is verified. We have therefore proved the existence part of Proposition
3.1
. It remains to obtain the uniform estimates, which is the focus of the next section.

3.2.
Uniform estimates for the Galerkin approximations

In this section, we perform the main entropy-dissipation estimate, and obtain bounds uniform in
n
and
\varepsilon
for both the entropy variable and the original unknown. After this, we establish similar estimates for their respective time derivatives.

We first remark that, for all fixed
t,x
there holds
\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}((0,2\pi))}\leq 1
as well as
f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\geq 0
and
0\leq\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}\leq 1
,
from which we get

(3.22)

\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T};L^{1}(0,2\pi))}\leq 1.

This bound will be used in the proof of the main result of this subsection, which we now state.

Lemma 3.3

(Uniform estimates for Galerkin approximation)
.

Let
\varepsilon,n,u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
be as in Lemma
3.2
and
f^{\varepsilon}_{n},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
be as in (
3.18
). There exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
\varepsilon,n
such that there holds the uniform estimates:

(3.23)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](t)+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Upsilon}|\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C,

and

(3.24)

\displaystyle\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{3}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C

(3.25)

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{\frac{10}{3}}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C

(3.26)

\displaystyle\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(0,T;W^{1,\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon))}\leq C.

Proof.

1.
Entropy dissipation
: By testing (
3.17
) with
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
, which is an admissible test function since one can directly verify
u^{\varepsilon}_{n},\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
, we compute, using (
3.19
), the entropy dissipation

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}]+\int_{\Upsilon}(\varepsilon I+\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n}))\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=-\int_{\Upsilon}\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

Again, we emphasise that, since
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\Upsilon})
for all
j
, the integrands in the previous line are composed of functions belonging to
C^{1}(\overline{\Upsilon}_{T})
, whence the integrals are well-defined. Recall that the matrix
\tilde{M}
is positive semi-definite and symmetric. It follows that there exists a unique square-root matrix
\tilde{N}
of the same dimension such that
\tilde{N}^{T}\tilde{N}=\tilde{M}
, namely

\tilde{N}(u)=N(f[u])=\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{D_{e}f(1-\rho)}I&0\\
0&\sqrt{f}\end{matrix}\right).

In turn, there holds, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

(3.27)

\displaystyle E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](t)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Upsilon}

\displaystyle|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}\underbrace{\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}}_{\geq 0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](0)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}\tilde{N}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\tilde{N}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

and observe that the final term on the right-hand side may be bounded by means of the Young inequality as follows

(3.28)

\displaystyle|\tilde{N}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\tilde{N}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{N}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{N}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})V\cdot V

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon_{T})}^{2}|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}|,

where we used the boundedness of
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
;
cf.
(
3.22
). Recall from (
3.15
) that
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\to u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
strongly in
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
, and hence almost everywhere for a subsequence; which, with slight abuse of notation, we still label as
\{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\}_{n}
. In view of the Morrey embedding
H^{2}(\Upsilon)\hookrightarrow C^{0,1/2}(\Upsilon)
, we deduce

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)-u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}\to 0,

and hence

\sup_{n}\|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}<\infty.

It follows from the continuity of the exponential and the relations (
3.18
) that
f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\to f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
almost everywhere, and

0<f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\leq\exp\big(\sup_{n}{\|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}}\big)<\infty,

and we remark that
\exp(\sup_{n}{\|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}})
is constant and is therefore an integrable dominating function on the bounded domain
\Upsilon
. Using the previous estimate, an application of the Dominated Convergence Theorem yields, for all
q\in[1,\infty)
,

(3.29)

f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\to f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(\Upsilon)\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and a similar argument using also (
3.22
),
cf.

e.g.
Appendix
B
, yields
\lim_{n\to\infty}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)]=E[f^{\varepsilon}_{0}]
in the sense of real numbers. It therefore follows, using also (
3.22
), (
3.27
), and (
3.28
), that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](t)+

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\tilde{M}(u^{\varepsilon}_{n})\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\bigg(1+E[f^{\varepsilon}_{0}]+\varepsilon\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\bigg),

for some positive constant
C=C(\mathrm{Pe},D_{e},T,\Upsilon)
independent of
\varepsilon,n
. Our choice of regularised initial data (
cf.
Lemma
1.3
) implies that the entire right-hand side of the above is bounded independently of
\varepsilon,n
. By rewriting the final term on the left-hand side in terms of
f^{\varepsilon}_{n},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
using (
3.20
), we get

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](t)+

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Upsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\Big(D_{e}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})\big|\nabla\log\big(\frac{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\big)\big|^{2}+f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\big|\partial_{\theta}\log\big(\frac{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\big)\big|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

i.e.
,

(3.30)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E[f^{\varepsilon}_{n}](t)+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\Upsilon}|\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})|\nabla\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C,

where we used the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem and the commutativity of the derivative in
x
and integral in
\theta
to rewrite
\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\nabla f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\cdot\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t
for the final term on the left-hand side of (
3.30
). Observe that, using the bounds in the previous estimate,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

and similarly

(3.31)

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})|\nabla\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C.

Collating these latter two bounds with (
3.30
) yields the estimate (
3.23
).

2.
Interpolated bounds on
\sqrt{f}
and
\sqrt{(1-\rho)f}
: Using the product rule and the estimates (
3.31
) and (
3.30
), we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n})f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

Our objective is then to make use of the

Appendix

A direct application of Lemma

\|\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{6}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C,

and (

3.
Estimate on
f\sqrt{1-\rho}
: We write

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}=2\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}},

3.23
) and (

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\big(f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\big)\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon_{T})}+\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\Upsilon_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Upsilon_{T})}+\|f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{3}(\Upsilon_{T})}\|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C,

where we again used (
3.23
) and (
3.24
). Therefore, using again Jensen’s inequality and (
3.24
) to bound

uniformly in

∎

In what follows, we estimate the time derivatives of the transformed variable and original unknowns; these bounds will then permit us to apply the Aubin–Lions Lemma in the proof of Theorem

(3.35)

4.
Proof of the Main Existence Theorem

In this section, we study the convergence of the approximate solutions and obtain the existence of a weak solution to (
1.1
). We shall essentially take the double-limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
in the regularisation parameter and
n\to\infty
in the Galerkin approximation simultaneously, which will entail the use of a diagonal argument. We explain why we proceed in this manner in the next paragraph.

The reason for employing a double-limit strategy is that the time-derivative estimates of Corollary
3.4
do not decouple into separate estimates on
\partial_{t}\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
\partial_{t}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
individually. This means that the Aubin–Lions Lemma yields the strong convergence of only the sum
\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
as
n\to\infty
for fixed
\varepsilon
. It is then, to the authors’ knowledge, not possible to obtain the strong convergence
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}\to\rho^{\varepsilon}
for fixed
\varepsilon
without having already determined that
\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}_{n}
converges strongly. While the classical theory of
[
20
]
implies that the linear heat term
\varepsilon(\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}-\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}_{n})
ought to yield
H^{2}
-regularity of
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
and hence
L^{2}
-boundedness of
\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
(from which one could infer strong convergence of
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}
by another application of Aubin–Lions), the presence of the non-local operator
A
and the non-local matrix
\tilde{M}
in the equation (
3.17
) makes
quantitative estimates
rather intractable. In turn, we are not able to deduce that
U^{\varepsilon}_{n}
converges strongly as
n\to\infty
while keeping
\varepsilon
fixed. Instead, we rely on the uniform bound on
\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}U^{\varepsilon}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}
to deduce that
\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}_{n}\to 0
strongly as
\varepsilon\to 0
; whence the aforementioned strong convergence of the sum
\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}_{n}+\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
yields the desired strong convergence of
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}
.

The main difficulty in implementing this strategy lies in making sure that the sequence of regularised initial data converges appropriately. This is all-the-more subtle since the initial functions
\{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0),\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\}_{\varepsilon,n}
are determined via the usual non-linear transformation from
u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)
, the
n
-th Galerkin approximation of the initial data
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
,
i.e.
,

(4.1)

u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle u^{\varepsilon}_{0},\varphi_{j}\rangle_{H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)}\varphi_{j},\quad f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)=\frac{e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)}}{1+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{u^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta},\quad\rho^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(0)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

where we recall that
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j}
is the orthonormal basis of
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
used in §
3.1
.

The next lemma shows that there exists a choice of diagonal subsequence from the array
\{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\}_{\varepsilon,n}
which preserves the desired convergence to the initial data
f_{0}
. The proof is delayed to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 4.1

(Diagonal convergence of initial data)
.

There exists a diagonal subsequence of the array
\{f^{\varepsilon}_{n}\}_{\varepsilon,n}
, which, with slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\{f^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
, such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|f^{\varepsilon}(0)-f_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}=0.

We are now ready to give the proof of the main existence theorem; Theorem
1
. We will show that the sequence
\{f^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
constructed in the previous lemma admits a subsequential limit which is indeed a weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
. We emphasise that
\{f^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the uniform estimates of Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We assume for the time being that the test function
\psi
belongs to the stronger space
\mathcal{A}
. We shall relax this assumption at the end of the proof.

1.
Convergence of time-derivative
: The uniform bound (
3.7
) implies

\partial_{t}(\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}+f^{\varepsilon})\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\zeta\quad\text{ weakly-* in }X^{\prime},

for some unkown
\zeta\in X^{\prime}
. The estimate (
3.4
) implies
f^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
L^{3}(\Upsilon_{T})
whence, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\langle\zeta,\psi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle\partial_{t}(\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}+f^{\varepsilon}),\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}f^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}f\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where we used that
\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C
from (

\zeta=\partial_{t}f
and
\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{t}f
weakly-* in

. An analogous argument shows
\partial_{t}\rho^{\varepsilon}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{t}\rho

Y^{\prime}

2.
Strong convergence of

In turn, the time-derivative bound (
3.8
) and the Aubin–Lions Lemma imply that, up to a subsequence which we do not relabel,

\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}+\rho^{\varepsilon}\to A\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega_{T}).

for some unknown

. Note that the triangle inequality and the uniform estimate on

from Proposition

imply

\displaystyle\|\rho^{\varepsilon}-A\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon U^{\varepsilon}+\rho^{\varepsilon}-A\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\underbrace{\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}U^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}}_{\leq C}\to 0,

and thus by the uniqueness of limits,

,
i.e.
,

(4.2)

\rho^{\varepsilon}\to\rho\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega_{T}),

and

to establish an admissible constant majorant function,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}\big|\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{1-\rho}\big|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0,

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}}\dv\big(f^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{1-\rho^{\varepsilon}}\nabla\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}^{\varepsilon}+I_{2}^{\varepsilon},

The weak convergence from (

from which we deduce

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{1}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\big(f\sqrt{1-\rho}\big)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{2}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\sqrt{1-\rho}\big(f\sqrt{1-\rho}\big)\Delta\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using the computation in (
4.11
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}f^{\varepsilon}\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\sqrt{1-\rho}\dv\big(f\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\psi\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}f\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t,

where we used that the above quantities are regular enough to perform the integration by parts;
cf.
(
4.8
). By collating the previous limit with (
4.10
) and using (
4.9
), we get

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\big((1-\rho^{\varepsilon})\nabla f^{\varepsilon}+f^{\varepsilon}\nabla\rho^{\varepsilon}\big)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\big((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

5.
Convergence of angular diffusion, non-local drift, and
\varepsilon
-error term
: For the angular diffusion, the weak convergence of
\partial_{\theta}f^{\varepsilon}
in (
4.7
) implies directly

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\partial_{\theta}f^{\varepsilon}\partial_{\theta}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

For the non-local drift, the strong convergence of
\rho^{\varepsilon}
from (
4.3
) and the weak convergence of
f^{\varepsilon}
implies
(1-\rho^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup(1-\rho)f
weakly in
L^{1}(\Upsilon_{T})
, whence

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Similarly, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Upsilon}\varepsilon\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\underbrace{\|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}}_{\leq C}\|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}=0,

where we used the uniform estimate (
3.3
).

6.
Admissible test functions and attainment of initial data
: It therefore follows that the weak formulation of Definition
1.1
holds for all test functions
\psi\in\mathcal{A}
. Using the density of this latter space in
X
and the fact that
f,\rho
and their derivatives are bounded in suitable spaces,
cf.
equation (
1.5
), the weak formulation extends to test functions belonging to
X
.

Finally, the attainment of the initial data in the dual space
Z^{\prime}
is clear from Remark
1.2
and Lemma
4.1
. The proof is complete.
∎

also satisfies an alternative notion of weak solution, which does not require the test functions of the weak formulation to be periodic with respect to the space-angle variable
\bm{\xi}
; this is the content of the next lemma. This formulation is convenient when implementing the method of De Giorgi to study the regularity of the solution (
cf
.
[

]
), which will be the subject of future works. The proof is identical to that of Theorem
1
, using that integrating by parts over all of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
against an arbitrary non-periodic test function
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
yields no boundary terms, as was also the case when testing against periodic test functions
\psi\in X
and integrating over one periodic cell.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{X}
be the weak solution obtained in Theorem
1
. Then, for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pe}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big(D_{e}\big((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\psi+\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\psi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t.

5.
Uniqueness for Null Péclet Number

In the case
\mathrm{Pe}=0
, the equation reads

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f=D_{e}\dv((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho)+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f,\\
&\partial_{t}\rho=D_{e}\Delta\rho.\end{aligned}\right.

The main observation is that, as
\rho
satisfies the heat equation, uniqueness for
\rho
is trivial. With this information, we employ the method of Gajewski
[
15
]
to deduce that the solution of (
5.1
) is unique within the class
\mathcal{X}
; this approach had been used for a similar uniqueness argument by Jüngel and Zamponi in
[
19
]
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
be two weak solutions of (
5.1
) with identical admissible initial data, and define
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
accordingly. As
\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}
satisfies the heat equation with zero initial data, the standard theory implies
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
; we therefore denote this common function by
\rho
, which satisfies the usual estimate
0\leq\rho\leq 1
. It therefore follows that

(5.2)

\partial_{t}f_{i}=D_{e}\dv((1-\rho)\nabla f_{i}+f_{i}\nabla\rho)+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f_{i}

for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Consider the distance

(5.3)

d(u,v):=\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(\zeta(u)+\zeta(v)-2\zeta\big(\frac{u+v}{2}\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

where
\zeta(s)=s(\log s-1)+1
for
s\geq 0
. Note that the convexity of
\zeta
implies that
d(u,v)\geq 0
. In fact we shall consider the regularised variant for
\delta>0
:

(5.4)

d_{\delta}(u,v):=\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(\zeta_{\delta}(u)+\zeta_{\delta}(v)-2\zeta_{\delta}\big(\frac{u+v}{2}\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

where
\zeta_{\delta}(s)=\zeta(s+\delta)
, and again
d_{\delta}
is non-negative because
\zeta_{\delta}
is convex. Note that
\zeta^{\prime}(s)=\log(s)
for
s>0
and that, by a standard argument using Taylor’s Theorem, there holds for all
\delta>0
:

(5.5)

\zeta_{\delta}(u)+\zeta_{\delta}(v)-2\zeta_{\delta}\big(\frac{u+v}{2}\big)\geq\frac{1}{8}|u-v|^{2};

we refer the reader to
[
19
, §6]
for further details.

Our goal is to show that
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}
a.e. in
\Upsilon_{T}
. With this in mind, we compute

(5.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}d_{\delta}(f_{1},f_{2})=

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}f_{1},\log(f_{1}+\delta)\rangle+\langle\partial_{t}f_{2},\log(f_{2}+\delta)\rangle-\langle\partial_{t}f_{1}+\partial_{t}f_{2},\log\big(\frac{f_{1}+f_{2}}{2}+\delta\big)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-4D_{e}\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2}|\nabla\sqrt{f_{i}+\delta}|^{2}-|\nabla\sqrt{f_{1}+f_{2}+2\delta}|^{2}\Big)(1-\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle-4\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2}|\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f_{i}+\delta}|^{2}-|\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f_{1}+f_{2}+2\delta}|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle-D_{e}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(\frac{f_{i}}{f_{1}+\delta}-\frac{f_{1}+f_{2}}{f_{1}+f_{2}+2\delta}\Big)\nabla f_{i}\cdot\nabla\rho\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

The first two integrals above are non-negative by virtue of the subadditivity of the Fisher information
[
19
, Lemma 9]
;
cf.

[
21
, §3.6]
. Our strategy will be to show that the final term on the right-hand side of the previous equation vanishes in the limit as
\delta\to 0
. It will suffice to first show that the term
\nabla f_{i}\cdot\nabla\rho
is integrable, and to then apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem. We rewrite this product as

\nabla f_{i}\cdot\nabla\rho=-4\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\sqrt{f_{i}}\cdot\sqrt{f_{i}}\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho},

from which it follows, using the Young inequality, that

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}|\nabla f_{i}\cdot\nabla\rho|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho)|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{f_{i}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}|^{2}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}_{=\rho}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}(1-\rho)|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\sqrt{f_{i}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

and thus
\nabla f_{i}\cdot\nabla\rho\in L^{1}(\Upsilon_{T})
; the boundedness of the right-hand side follows from the definition of
\mathcal{X}
. It follows that, by integrating (
5.6
) in time, there holds, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

d_{\delta}(f_{1},f_{2})(t)-\underbrace{d_{\delta}(f_{1},f_{2})(0)}_{=0}\leq D_{e}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(\frac{f_{i}}{f_{1}+\delta}-\frac{f_{1}+f_{2}}{f_{1}+f_{2}+2\delta}\Big)\nabla f_{i}\cdot\nabla\rho\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

whence, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem and using
d_{\delta}\geq 0
, we deduce that, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}d_{\delta}(f_{1},f_{2})(t)=0.

It follows from Fatou’s Lemma that

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\Big(\zeta_{\delta}(f_{1})+\zeta_{\delta}(f_{1})-2\zeta_{\delta}\big(\frac{f_{1}+f_{2}}{2}\big)\Big)=0\quad\text{a.e.~in }\Upsilon_{T},

whence the estimate (
5.5
) implies that
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}
a.e. in
\Upsilon_{T}
, as required.
∎

6.
Existence and Uniqueness of Stationary States

We consider the existence and uniqueness of stationary solutions
f_{\infty}:\Upsilon\to\mathbb{R}
which satisfy, with
\rho_{\infty}(x)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{\infty}(x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta
,

(6.1)

\mathrm{Pe}\dv\big((1-\rho_{\infty})f_{\infty}{\bf e}(\theta)\big)=D_{e}\dv\big((1-\rho_{\infty})\nabla f_{\infty}+f_{\infty}\nabla\rho_{\infty}\big)+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f_{\infty}.

The question of existence of stationary states is straightforward, with no restriction on the Péclet number. Indeed, given any
\mathrm{Pe}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists at least one non-trivial constant stationary solution; namely
f_{\infty}=1/2\pi
, which gives rise to
\rho_{\infty}=1
. In fact, by writing the drift term as

\dv\big((1-\rho_{\infty})f_{\infty}{\bf e}(\theta)\big)={\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\big((1-\rho_{\infty})f_{\infty}\big),

for sufficiently regular
f_{\infty}
, we deduce that all constant solutions satisfy (
6.1
).

We therefore focus entirely on uniqueness henceforth, and divide our results into two subsections; §
6.1
focused exclusively on the case
\mathrm{Pe}=0
by means of a simple variational approach, and §
6.2
concerned with uniqueness of strong solutions near constant stationary states for general
\mathrm{Pe}
, which requires a more involved analysis.

6.1.
Uniqueness of stationary solutions for null Péclet number

The case
\mathrm{Pe}=0
is particularly simple since, in this case, the equation (
1.1
) admits an exact gradient flow structure. We shall show that stationary states are unique up to a mass constraint via a variational approach. The proof does not readily generalise to
\mathrm{Pe}\neq 0
; this is essentially because the perturbative term
V
in (
2.4
) does not admit a primitive, whence
\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\cdot(\tilde{M}(u)(\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u+V))
cannot be rewritten as the dissipation of a suitable energy (
cf.

[
8
, §3.3]
).

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathrm{Pe}=0
and
m>0
. Then, the stationary solution
f_{\infty}
of (
6.1
) is unique within the class
\int_{\Upsilon}f_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=m
.

Further results regarding the convergence to stationary states can be obtained using the theory of Bakry–Emery or log-Sobolev inequalities applied to equations endowed with an exact Wasserstein gradient flow structure (
cf.

e.g.

[
3
,
9
,
18
]
). This is well-known, and we do not include the details here. The study of the convergence to stationary states by means of hypocoercivity methods for the case of non-zero Péclet number, for which one does not have an exact gradient flow structure, will be the subject of future investigation.

Proof.

1.
Variational consequence of stationary equation
: As per §
2
, we introduce the transformed variable
u_{\infty}

(6.2)

u_{\infty}:=\log\big(\frac{f_{\infty}}{1-\rho_{\infty}}\big).

For clarity of presentation, we assume that the above is well-defined and perform formal computations; these may easily be rendered rigorous by standard approximation arguments. We show that any stationary solution satisfies

(6.3)

\int_{\Upsilon}f_{\infty}(1-\rho_{\infty})|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u_{\infty}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=0.

Indeed, define the functional

F[f]:=\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(D_{e}(1-\rho)f|\nabla u|^{2}+f|\partial_{\theta}u|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=\int_{\Upsilon}\tilde{M}(u)\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}u\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\geq 0,

where we used that
\tilde{M}(u)
is positive semidefinite, and observe that testing the equation (
6.1
) with
u_{\infty}
yields
F[f_{\infty}]=0
. The equality (
6.3
) follows from the non-negativity of the integrand in the functional
F
and basic manipulations.

2.
Uniqueness from mass constraint
: The relation (
6.3
) implies that all stationary solutions with
\mathrm{Pe}=0
are constant. The mass constraint
\int_{\Upsilon}f_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=m
then uniquely determines the stationary solution, as required.
∎

6.2.
Uniqueness of strong solutions near constant stationary states

Our aim in this section is to prove that, provided a strong solution is sufficiently near a constant stationary state (in a sense to be made precise), then it is the only such solution. We proceed by means of a fixed-point argument employing the Contraction Mapping Theorem, similar to
[
8
, §3.5]
, for which we define the spaces

(6.4)

\displaystyle\Xi_{t}:=L^{\infty}(t,T;H^{2}(\Upsilon))\cap L^{2}(t,T;H^{3}(\Upsilon))\cap H^{1}(t,T;H^{1}(\Upsilon)),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}:=L^{\infty}(t,T;H^{1}(\Upsilon))\cap L^{2}(t,T;H^{2}(\Upsilon))\cap H^{1}(t,T;L^{2}(\Upsilon)),

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}:=L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))\cap L^{2}(t,T;H^{1}(\Upsilon)).

Where no confusion arises, we shall omit the
t
subscript and simply write
\Xi,\Lambda,\Theta
. The main result of this section is as follows.

Theorem 4
.

Let
D_{e}>0
,
\mathrm{Pe}\in\mathbb{R}
,
f_{\infty}\in(0,1/2\pi)
be a given constant stationary state, and
f
be a solution of (
1.1
). Assume there exists
t\in(0,T)
such that
f(t,\cdot)\in H^{2}(\Upsilon)
with

\|f(t,\cdot)-f_{\infty}\|_{H^{2}(\Upsilon)}\leq R^{2}

for
R
sufficiently small, depending only on
f_{\infty},D_{e}
and
\mathrm{Pe}
. Then, there exists a unique solution of (
1.1
) in

B_{R,t}:=\big\{f\in\Xi:\,\|f-f_{\infty}\|_{\Xi_{t}}\leq R\big\}.

The proof of Theorem
4
entails studying the time-evolution of the error term

w:=f-f_{\infty}
, with
W:=\rho-\rho_{\infty}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w-\partial^{2}_{\theta}w-D_{e}\big[(1-\rho_{\infty})\Delta w

\displaystyle+f_{\infty}\Delta W\big]+\mathrm{Pe}\big[(1-\rho_{\infty})\nabla w-f_{\infty}\nabla W\big]\cdot{\bf e}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{e}\big[w\Delta W-W\Delta w\big]+\mathrm{Pe}\big[w\nabla W+W\nabla w\big]\cdot{\bf e}(\theta).

We therefore construct the operator
\Gamma
to be the solution map
\Gamma:w\mapsto z
, where
z
solves the linear problem

(6.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}z-\partial^{2}_{\theta}z-D_{e}\big[(1-\rho_{\infty})&&\Delta z+f_{\infty}\Delta Z\big]+\mathrm{Pe}\big[(1-\rho_{\infty})\nabla z-f_{\infty}\nabla Z\big]\cdot{\bf e}(\theta)\\
&&&=\underbrace{D_{e}\big(w\Delta W-W\Delta w\big)+\mathrm{Pe}\big(w\nabla W+W\nabla w\big)\cdot{\bf e}(\theta)}_{=:G(w)},\\
&z(0,\cdot)=z_{0},&&\end{aligned}\right.

and
Z:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}z\,\mathrm{d}\theta
; we write
\Gamma=S\circ G
, where
S
is the solution mapping to the above equation for given right-hand side and initial data
z_{0}
, and
G
maps precisely to this desired right-hand source term. Our goal is to show that
\Gamma
admits a fixed point.

Note that, provided the above is supplemented with adequate initial data and the right-hand term
G(w)
is sufficiently regular, the solution map
\Gamma
is well-defined in a suitable space using a standard application of the Galerkin method/Fourier series. Indeed, writing the ansatz

z(t,\bm{\xi})=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}(t)e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{n}\cdot\bm{\xi}},\quad Z(t,x)=2\pi\sum_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\alpha_{(\mathbf{n}^{\prime},0)}(t)e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\cdot x},

where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
,
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=(n_{1},n_{2})
, the equation (
6.5
) reads

(6.6)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}(t)+n_{3}^{2}\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}(t)+D_{e}(n_{1}^{2}+n_{2}^{2})\big[(1-\rho_{\infty})\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}(t)

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{\{n_{3}=0\}}f_{\infty}2\pi\alpha_{(\mathbf{n}^{\prime},0)}(t)\big]

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Pe}(1-\rho_{\infty})2\pi\mathrm{i}n_{1}\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}(t)=\hat{G}_{\mathbf{n}}(t),

where

\hat{G}_{\mathbf{n}}(t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(w)(t,\bm{\xi})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{n}\cdot\bm{\xi}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

It follows that, provided
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{n}}(w)
is sufficiently well-behaved, the ODE system (
6.6
) is solvable by standard methods for all values of
f_{\infty}\geq 0
and
0\leq\rho_{\infty}\leq 1
. This existence construction is standard; we therefore deliberately omit further details for clarity of presentation, and henceforth focus exclusively on the
a priori
estimates required for the fixed-point strategy. Our results are encapsulated in the following two lemmas, which will be used to prove that
\Gamma
admits a unique fixed point.

Lemma 6.1

(Estimates for the operator
S
)
.

Assume
f_{\infty}\in(0,1/2\pi)
, i.e.,
\rho_{\infty}\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a positive constant
C_{\infty,D_{e}}=C_{\infty,D_{e}}(f_{\infty},D_{e})
such that

(6.7)

\displaystyle\|Sv\|_{\Theta}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{0}^{1/2}\Big(\|z_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}\Big),

\displaystyle\|\nabla Sv\|_{\Theta}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{0}\Big(\|\nabla z_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}\Big),

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\theta}Sv\|_{\Theta}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{0}^{3/2}\Big(\|z_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\partial_{\theta}z_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}\Big),

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}Sv\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{1}\Big(\|z_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}\Big),

where

(6.8)

\displaystyle C_{0}=e^{T(1+\mathrm{Pe}^{2}{D_{e}^{-1}})},\qquad C_{1}=(1+\mathrm{Pe}^{2}D_{e}^{-1})^{1/2}C_{0}^{3/2},

and

(6.9)

\|Sv\|_{\Xi}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{1}\Big(\|z_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|v\|_{\Theta}\Big).

Lemma 6.2

(Estimates for the operator
G
)
.

There exists a positive constant
C_{D_{e}}
, depending solely on
D_{e}
and
\Upsilon
, such that, for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Xi
, there holds

(6.10)

\|G(w_{1})-G(w_{2})\|_{\Theta}\leq C_{D_{e}}(1+\mathrm{Pe}D_{e}^{-1})\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\Xi}\big(\|w_{1}\|_{\Xi}+\|w_{2}\|_{\Xi}\big),

and, for all
w\in\Xi
,

(6.11)

\|G(w)\|_{\Theta}\leq C_{D_{e}}(1+\mathrm{Pe}D_{e}^{-1})\|w\|_{\Xi}^{2}.

Before proving Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, we provide the proof of Theorem
4
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Suppose for the time being that
T\leq 1
. This assumption will be removed in the third step of the proof.

1.
\Gamma
is self-mapping
: Suppose
f\in B_{R}
. Using the estimates of Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, we find that

\|\Gamma w\|_{\Xi}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{1}\Big(1+C_{D_{e}}(1+\mathrm{Pe}D_{e}^{-1})\Big)R^{2}.

Since we have restricted
T\leq 1
, it follows that there exists
R
sufficiently small, depending only on
D_{e},f_{\infty},\mathrm{Pe}
such that there holds

\|\Gamma w\|_{\Xi}\leq R,

whence
\Gamma
is self-mapping from
B_{R}
to itself.

2.
\Gamma
is contractive
: Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in B_{R}
. Observe that the linearity of
S
implies
\Gamma w_{1}-\Gamma w_{2}=S(Gw_{1}-Gw_{2})
, where the initial data
z_{0}
in (
6.5
) is null. It follows from Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
that there holds

\|\Gamma w_{1}-\Gamma w_{2}\|_{\Xi}\leq C_{\infty,D_{e}}C_{1}C_{D_{e}}(1+\mathrm{Pe}D_{e}^{-1})R\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\Xi}.

As per the first part of the proof, it follows that one may select
R
sufficiently small, depending only on
f_{\infty},D_{e},\mathrm{Pe}
such that
\Gamma
is a contraction. An application of Banach’s Contraction Mapping Theorem implies that there exists a unique
f
solving (
1.1
) and such that
\|f-f_{\infty}\|_{\Xi}
over the time interval
t\in(0,1)
.

3.
Extension to arbitrary time intervals
: We apply Step 2 of the proof repeatedly over overlapping time intervals
(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})
for Lebesgue points
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}
chosen such that
\beta_{j-1}>\alpha_{j}
for all
j
and such that
\bigcup_{j}(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})\supset(t,T)
. In turn, obtain that there exists a unique
f\in B_{R}
over the entire time interval
(0,T)
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

By testing (
6.5
) with
z
, we obtain the classical parabolic estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon}

\displaystyle|\partial_{\theta}z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+D_{e}\Big((1-\rho_{\infty})\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+f_{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla Z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pe}\int_{\Upsilon}\big[(1-\rho_{\infty})|z||\nabla z|+f_{\infty}|z||\nabla Z|\big]\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\Upsilon}G(w)z\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}

Appendix A
Interpolation Lemma

In the present appendix, we derive a variant of a classical interpolation estimate for spaces of functions depending on time and space; our function spaces include dependence on time, space, and angle.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
m,p\geq 1
and define the Banach spaces

\displaystyle V^{m,p}:=L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T};L^{m}(0,2\pi)))\cap L^{p}(\Omega_{T};W^{1,p}(0,2\pi)),

\displaystyle V^{m,p}_{0}:=L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T};L^{m}(0,2\pi))\cap L^{p}(\Omega_{T};W^{1,p}_{0}(0,2\pi)),

both equipped with the norm

\|v\|_{V^{m,p}}:=\esssup_{(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}}\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)}+\|\partial_{\theta}v\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon_{T})}.

There exists a positive constant
C
depending only on
p,m,\Upsilon,T
such that, for all
v\in V^{m,p}
, there holds

\|v\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C\|v\|_{V^{m,p}},

where
q=p(m+1)
.

Proof.

1.
Inequality for
V_{0}^{m,p}
: Assume for the time being that
v\in V^{m,p}_{0}
. Observe that
p\geq 1>\frac{m}{m+1}
,
i.e.

p-m+mp>0
. The Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality
[
12
, §1, Theorem 2.1, (2.2-i)]
yields, for a.e.
(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
,

\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(0,2\pi)}\leq C\|\partial_{\theta}v(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(0,2\pi)}^{\frac{p}{q}}\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|^{1-\frac{p}{q}}_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)},

where
q=p(m+1)
. In turn, raising the previous inequality to the power
q
and integrating over
\Omega_{T}
yields

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}^{q}\leq C\bigg(\int_{\Omega_{T}}\|\partial_{\theta}v(t,x,\cdot)\|^{p}_{L^{p}(0,2\pi)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg)\esssup_{(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}}\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|^{q-p}_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)},

and thus

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C\|\partial_{\theta}v\|^{\frac{p}{q}}_{L^{p}(\Upsilon_{T})}\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|^{1-\frac{p}{q}}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T};L^{m}(0,2\pi))}.

Applying the Young inequality to the previous right-hand side with exponent
q/p>1
and its Hölder conjugate yields

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C\|v\|_{V^{m,p}}.

A classical argument shows that the bound above still holds for functions
v\in V^{m,p}
such that
\int_{0}^{2\pi}v\,\mathrm{d}\theta=0
a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
;
cf.

[
12
, §1 Remark 2.1]
.

2.
Relaxation to
V^{m,p}
: Let
v\in V^{m,p}
and define, for a.e.
(t,x,\theta)
, its mean-zero counterpart

w(t,x,\theta):=v(t,x,\theta)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}v(t,x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

In view of the remark at the end of Step 1, we deduce that there holds

\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq C\|w\|_{V^{m,p}}.

Notice that
\partial_{\theta}w=\partial_{\theta}v
and
\|w(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)}\leq 2\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)}
a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
, and that Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{2\pi}v(\cdot,\cdot,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq

\displaystyle C\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|v(t,x,\theta^{\prime})|^{m}\,\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\bigg)^{\frac{q}{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\esssup_{(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}}\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)}.

It therefore follows from the Minkowski inequality that

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}\leq

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big\|\int_{0}^{2\pi}v\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big\|_{L^{q}(\Upsilon_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\bigg(\|\underbrace{\partial_{\theta}w}_{=\partial_{\theta}v}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon_{T})}+\esssup_{(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}}\|v(t,x,\cdot)\|_{L^{m}(0,2\pi)}\bigg),

and the result follows.
∎

(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0})\log(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0})
, whence the Dominated Convergence Theorem implies

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0})\log(1-\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho_{0})\log(1-\rho_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C,

for
C
depending only on the maximum value of
s\log s
over the interval
[0,1]
and
\Omega
.

Similarly, we decompose

\int_{\Upsilon}f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\log f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=\int_{\{0<f^{\varepsilon}_{0}<1\}}f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\log f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+\int_{\{f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\geq 1\}}f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\log f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

and the local boundedness of
s\log s
and the Dominated Convergence Theorem that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\{0<f^{\varepsilon}_{0}<1\}}f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\log f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=\int_{\{0<f_{0}<1\}}f_{0}\log f_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\leq C,

where the right-hand side of the above is bounded by virtue of the function
s\log s
being bounded over the interval
s\in(0,1)
. Meanwhile, the assumption
f_{0}\in L^{p}(\Upsilon)
for
p>1
and the estimate
|\log s|\mathds{1}_{\{s\geq 1\}}\leq C_{p}|s|^{p-1}
imply

\int_{\{f^{\varepsilon}_{0}>1\}}f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\log f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\leq C_{p}\int_{\{f^{\varepsilon}_{0}>1\}}|f^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\leq C_{p}(1+\|f_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}^{p}),

and the right-hand side is bounded independently of
\varepsilon
.
∎

The next proof consists in a quantitative diagonal approach; the argument is classical, but it is nevertheless useful as it helps identify exactly which diagonal subsequence we select. We recall that for
\{\sigma^{\prime}(n)\}_{n}
to be a subsequence of
\{\sigma(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, it is sufficient that there holds:
\{\sigma^{\prime}(n)\}_{n}\subset\{\sigma(n)\}_{n}
,
\sigma^{\prime}(n)\geq\sigma(n)
, and
\sigma^{\prime}(n+1)>\sigma^{\prime}(n)
for all
n
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

For the purposes of the argument that follows, it is crucial that we replace the continuum
\{\varepsilon\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
with a countable set. For this reason, we select
1>\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>\dots>0
and define

f^{m}_{n}(0):=f^{\varepsilon_{m}}_{n}(0),\qquad f^{m}_{0}:=f^{\varepsilon_{m}}_{0},

so that the limit
m\to\infty
morally corresponds to
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Recall from equation (
3.29
) of §
3.1
that for each fixed
m
, we have

\|f^{m}_{n}(0)-f^{m}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

In turn, for
m=1
, there exists a subsequence
\{\sigma_{1}(n)\}_{n}
of
\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n
,

(B.3)

\|f^{1}_{\sigma_{1}(n)}(0)-f^{1}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Note in particular that the above implies, for all
\ell\geq n
,

(B.4)

\|f^{1}_{\sigma_{1}(\ell)}(0)-f^{1}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Then, for
m=2
, since we also know that
\|f^{2}_{n}(0)-f^{2}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we may construct a subsequence
\{\sigma_{2}(n)\}_{n}
of
\{\sigma_{1}(n)\}_{n}
such that, for all
n
,

\|f^{2}_{\sigma_{2}(n)}(0)-f^{2}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Furthermore, since
\{\sigma_{2}(n)\}_{n}
is a subsequence of
\{\sigma_{1}(n)\}_{n}
, the bound (
B.4
) implies, for all
n
,

\|f^{1}_{\sigma_{2}(n)}(0)-f^{1}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

We continue iteratively, and construct for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
a subsequence
\{\sigma_{m}(n)\}_{n}
of all previous
m-1
subsequences, such that there holds, for all
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and all
n
,

(B.5)

\|f^{j}_{\sigma_{m}(n)}(0)-f^{j}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

In turn, we define the diagonal sequence

(B.6)

f^{m}:=f^{m}_{\sigma_{m}(m)},

and note that (
B.5
) implies, for all
m
,

\|f^{m}(0)-f^{m}_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{m}.

Using the above and the strong convergence of
f^{m}_{0}\to f_{0}
in
L^{p}(\Upsilon)
of Lemma
1.3
, the triangle inequality yields

\|f^{m}(0)-f_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}\leq\underbrace{\|f^{m}(0)-f_{0}^{m}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}}_{\leq 1/m}+\underbrace{\|f_{0}^{m}-f_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Upsilon)}}_{\to 0}\to 0

as
m\to\infty
, and the proof is complete.
∎
