Masked Completion via Structured Diffusion with White-Box Transformers
Druv Pai, Ziyang Wu, Sam Buchanan, Yaodong Yu, Yi Ma

Abstract
Modern learning frameworks often train deep neural networks with massive amounts of unlabeled data to learn representations by solving simple pretext tasks, then use the representations as foundations for downstream tasks. These networks are empirically designed; as such, they are usually not interpretable, their representations are not structured, and their designs are potentially redundant. White-box deep networks, in which each layer explicitly identifies and transforms structures in the data, present a promising alternative. However, existing white-box architectures have only been shown to work at scale in supervised settings with labeled data, such as classification. In this work, we provide the first instantiation of the white-box design paradigm that can be applied to large-scale unsupervised representation learning. We do this by exploiting a fundamental connection between diffusion, compression, and (masked) completion, deriving a deep transformer-like masked autoencoder architecture, called crate-mae , in which the role of each layer is mathematically fully interpretable: they transform the data distribution to and from a structured representation. Extensive empirical evaluations confirm our analytical insights. crate-mae demonstrates highly promising performance on large-scale imagery datasets while using only ∼ \sim 30% of the parameters compared to the standard masked autoencoder with the same model configuration. The representations learned by crate-mae have explicit structure and also contain semantic meaning. Code is available on GitHub .

Introduction

In recent years, deep learning has been called upon to process continually larger quantities of high-dimensional, noisy, and unlabeled data. A key property which makes these ever-larger tasks tractable is that the high-dimensional data tends to have
low-dimensional geometric and statistical structure
. Modern deep networks tend to learn (implicit or explicit) representations of this structure, which are then used to efficiently perform downstream tasks. Learning these representations is thus of central importance in machine learning, and there are so far several common methodologies for this task.
We focus our attention below on approaches that incrementally transform the data towards the end representation with
simple, mathematically-interpretable primitives
. Discussion of popular alternatives is postponed to
Appendix

A
.

Denoising-diffusion models for high-dimensional data.
A popular method for learning implicit representations of high-dimensional data is
learning to denoise
: a model that can
denoise
, i.e., remove noise from a corrupted
observation from the data distribution (to the extent information-theoretically possible), can be chained across noise levels to transform the data distribution to and from certain highly structured distributions, such as an isotropic Gaussian,
enabling efficient sampling

(
Hyvärinen, 2005
;
Vincent, 2011
;
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Kadkhodaie & Simoncelli, 2021
;
Song et al., 2021
;
Song et al., 2023
)
.
Crucially, in the case of data with low-dimensional structure—including the highly nonlinear structure characteristic of natural images—these models can be learned efficiently

(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Oko et al., 2023
;
Moitra & Risteski, 2020
)
, and as a result this
framework has significant practical impact
(
Rombach et al., 2022
)
. Despite this
progress, these techniques have been largely limited to use in generative
modeling; a key reason is the unstructured nature of the final ‘noisy’ state of
the diffusion process, which makes it challenging to control and interpret the
model’s learned implicit representation of the data.

White-box models and structured representation learning.
In contrast,

white-box
models are designed to produce explicit and structured
representations of the data distribution according to a desired parsimonious
configuration, such as sparsity
(
Gregor & LeCun, 2010
;
Zhai et al., 2020
)
or
(piecewise) linearity
(
Chan et al., 2022
)
. Recent work

(
Chan et al., 2022
;
Yu et al., 2023a
)
has built white-box deep networks via unrolled
optimization: namely, to obtain representations with a desired set of
properties, one constructs an objective function which encourages these
desiderata, then constructs a deep network where each layer is designed to
iteratively optimize the objective. This builds deep networks as a chain of
operators, representing well-understood optimization primitives, which
iteratively transform the representations to the desired structure. For example,

Yu et al. (2023a)
uses an information-theoretic objective promoting lossy
compression of the data towards a fixed statistical structure to build
a transformer-like architecture named
crate
in the above manner. However,
such-obtained deep networks have yet to be constructed for most unsupervised
contexts. The fundamental difficulty here is that decoder networks must map from
representations to data, and hence
invert
(in a distributional sense)
the transformations made to the data distribution by the encoder. This renders
the unrolled optimization approach used to construct white-box encoders such as

crate
infeasible for constructing decoders, and instead demands
a fine-grained understanding of the operators that implement the encoder, and
their (distributional) inverses.

Our contributions.
To overcome this difficulty and extend
the applicability of white-box models to unsupervised settings, we demonstrate
in this work that these two
paradigms have more in common than previously appreciated. First, we show quantitatively that under certain natural regimes,
denoising
and
compression
are highly similar primitive data processing operations: when the target distribution has low-dimensional structure, both operations implement a projection operation onto this structure. Second, using this insight, we demonstrate a quantitative connection between unrolled discretized diffusion models and unrolled optimization-constructed deep networks.
This leads to a significant expansion of the existing conceptual toolkit for developing white-box neural network architectures, which we use to derive white-box transformer-like encoder and decoder architectures that together form an autoencoding model that we call
crate-mae
, illustrated in Fig.
1
. We evaluate
crate-mae
on the challenging masked autoencoding task
(
He et al., 2022
)
and demonstrate promising performance with large parameter savings over traditional masked autoencoders, along with many side benefits such as emergence of semantic meaning in the representations.

Approach

2.1
Setup and Notation

We use the same notation and basic problem setup as in
Yu et al. (2023a)
. Namely, we have some matrix-valued random variable
\bm{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
representing the data, where the
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
are called “tokens” and may be arbitrarily correlated. To obtain representations of the input, we learn an
encoder

f\colon\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
; our representations are denoted by the random variable
\bm{Z}=f(\bm{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
, where the token representations are
\bm{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the autoencoding setup, we also learn a
decoder

g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
, such that
\bm{X}\approx\widehat{\bm{X}}=[\widehat{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\bm{x}}_{N}]\doteq g(\bm{Z})
.

Our encoder and decoder will be deep neural networks, and as such they will be composed of several, say
L
,
layers
each. Write
f=f^{L}\circ\cdots\circ f^{1}\circ f^{\mathrm{pre}}
and
g=g^{\mathrm{post}}\circ g^{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ g^{0}
, where
f^{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
and
g^{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
are the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
layer of the encoder and decoder respectively, and
f^{\mathrm{pre}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
and
g^{\mathrm{post}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
are the pre- and post-processing layers respectively. The
input
to the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
layer of the encoder is denoted
\bm{Z}^{\ell}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{1}^{\ell},\dots,\bm{z}_{N}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
, and the
input
to the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
layer of the decoder is denoted
\bm{Y}^{\ell}\doteq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}_{1}^{\ell},\dots,\bm{y}_{N}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
.

crate-mae

. Furthermore, such representations are transformed back to the original image by the decoder layers

. Each encoder layer

.

2.2
Desiderata, Objective, and Optimization

Our goal is to use the encoder
f
and decoder
g
to learn
representations

\bm{Z}
which are
parsimonious

(
Ma et al., 2022
)
and
invertible
; namely, they have
low-dimensional, sparse, (piecewise) linear
geometric and statistical structure, and are (approximately) bijective with the original data
\bm{X}
.

Yu et al. (2023a)
proposes to implement these desiderata by positing a
signal model
for the representations:

Low-Dimensional Gaussian Mixture Codebook
.

Let
\bm{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
be a random matrix. We impose the following statistical model on
{\bm{Z}}
, parameterized by orthonormal bases
\bm{U}_{[K]}=(\bm{U}_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d\times p})^{K}
: each token
{\bm{z}}_{i}
has marginal distribution given by

\bm{z}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bm{U}_{s_{i}}\bm{\alpha}_{i},\quad\forall i\in[N]

(2.1)

where
(s_{i})_{i\in[N]}\in[K]^{N}
are random variables corresponding to the subspace indices, and
(\bm{\alpha}_{i})_{i\in[N]}\in(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{N}
are zero-mean Gaussian variables. If we optionally specify a noise parameter
\sigma\geq 0
, we mean that we “diffuse” the tokens with Gaussian noise: by an abuse of notation, each token
{\bm{z}}_{i}
has marginal distribution given by

\bm{z}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bm{U}_{s_{i}}\bm{\alpha}_{i}+\sigma\bm{w}_{i},\quad\forall i\in[N]

(2.2)

where
(\bm{w}_{i})_{i\in[N]}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian variables, independent of
s_{i}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{i}
.

If the
\bm{U}_{k}
are sufficiently incoherent and axis-aligned, we expect such representations to maximize the
sparse rate reduction
objective function
(
Yu et al., 2023a
)
:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Z}}[\Delta R(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})-\lambda\lVert\bm{Z}\rVert_{0}]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Z}}[R(\bm{Z})-R^{c}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})-\lambda\lVert\bm{Z}\rVert_{0}],

(2.3)

where
R
and
R^{c}
are
lossy coding rates
, or
rate distortions

(
Cover, 1999
)
, which are estimates for the number of bits required to encode the sample up to precision
\epsilon>0
using a Gaussian codebook, both unconditionally (for
R
), and conditioned on the samples being drawn from
\bm{U}_{k}
summed over all
k
(for
R^{c}
). Closed-form estimates
(
Ma et al., 2007
;
Yu et al., 2023a
)
for such rate distortions are:

\displaystyle R(\bm{Z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\left(\bm{I}_{N}+\alpha\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{Z}\right),\qquad\alpha\doteq\frac{d}{N\epsilon^{2}}

(2.4)

\displaystyle R^{c}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\det\left(\bm{I}_{N}+\beta(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z})^{\top}(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z})\right),\qquad\beta\doteq\frac{p}{N\epsilon^{2}}.

(2.5)

Notably,
R^{c}
is a measure of
compression against our statistical
structure
— it measures how closely the overall distribution of tokens in

\bm{Z}
fit a Gaussian mixture on
\bm{U}_{[K]}
. Meanwhile, the other two terms

R
and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{0}
ensure non-collapse and sparsity of the representations, respectively.

Following
Yu et al. (2023a)
, one then constructs a deep network that

incrementally optimizes the sparse rate reduction
in order to transform
the data distribution towards the desired parsimonious configuration

Equation

2.1
.
Specifically,
Yu et al. (2023a)
proposed to construct the deep neural network
f

as a two-step alternating optimization procedure which compresses the input against the (learned) local signal model
\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell}
at layer
\ell
, by taking a step of gradient descent on
R^{c}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell})
, and subsequently taking a step of proximal gradient descent on a LASSO objective
(
Tibshirani, 1996
;
Wright & Ma, 2022
)
to sparsify the data in a (learned) dictionary
\bm{D}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
:

\displaystyle\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}

\displaystyle=\bm{Z}^{\ell}+\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell})\approx\bm{Z}^{\ell}-\kappa\nabla_{\bm{Z}}R^{c}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell})

(2.6)

\displaystyle\bm{Z}^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}\mid\bm{D}^{\ell})\approx\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\bm{Z}\geq\bm{0}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}-\bm{D}^{\ell}\bm{Z}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\bm{Z}\rVert_{1}\right],

(2.7)

where
\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\cdot)
, the
M
ulti-head
S
ubspace
S
elf-
A
ttention block
(
Yu et al., 2023a
)
, is defined as

\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})\doteq\frac{p}{N\epsilon^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{U}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}\bm{Z})\operatorname{softmax}((\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}\bm{Z})^{\top}(\bm{U}_{1}^{\top}\bm{Z}))\\
\vdots\\
(\bm{U}_{K}^{\top}\bm{Z})\operatorname{softmax}((\bm{U}_{K}^{\top}\bm{Z})^{\top}(\bm{U}_{K}^{\top}\bm{Z}))\end{bmatrix},

(2.8)

and
\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\cdot)
, the
I
terative
S
hrinkage-
T
hresholding
A
lgorithm block
(
Yu et al., 2023a
)
, is defined as

\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{D})\doteq\operatorname{ReLU}(\bm{Z}-\eta\bm{D}^{\top}(\bm{D}\bm{Z}-\bm{Z})-\eta\lambda\bm{1}).

(2.9)

The
\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}
block is exactly the same as a multi-head self-attention block in
a transformer, with the changes that the

\bm{Q}_{k}
/
\bm{K}_{k}
/
\bm{V}_{k}
blocks are replaced by a single
matrix
\bm{U}_{k}
in each head
k
. The resulting layer
f^{\ell}
thus
bears significant resemblance to a transformer-like block, and so the

crate
model is a white-box transformer-like architecture constructed via
unrolled optimization. Such
crate
models obtain competitive performance on
standard tasks while enjoying many side benefits

(
Yu et al., 2023a
;
Yu et al., 2023b
)
, yet they have so far only been trained for
supervised learning. In the sequel, we introduce a paradigm to obtain fully white-box
networks for unsupervised learning, such as autoencoding, through
a novel understanding of the
crate
model’s distributional layerwise
inverse.

, and each encoder layer of

.
The compression step, implemented by the
\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}
operator, projects the tokens
\bm{Z}^{\ell}
towards the subspace model
\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell}
to form
\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}
. The sparsification step, implemented by the

\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}
towards the coordinate axes, using the sparsifying dictionary
\bm{D}^{\ell}
, to get
\bm{Z}^{\ell+1}
. The steps are performed in sequence and comprise a single of the
crate-mae
encoder.

2.3
Unifying Compression and Denoising

To transform our representations to the idealized signal model given by

Equation

2.1
, we seek to iteratively remove the disturbances or
deviations of each sample from this signal model. One way to perform this task
is to perform
lossy data compression

(
Ma et al., 2007
;
Yu et al., 2020
;
Psenka et al., 2023
;
Yu et al., 2023a
)
:
compressed versions of the data, without ancillary disturbances, form the
representations. This approach has been favored in the construction of previous
white-box deep networks, such as
crate
described above, due to the
existence of explicit information-theoretic criteria for compression. In this
case, the term
R^{c}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})
, defined in
Equation

2.5
,
measures the lossy compression of the representations
\bm{Z}
against the class
of statistical models given by
Equation

2.1
. Thus, an operation
to minimize
R^{c}
, such as
Equation

2.6
, implements a step of
compression to learn better representations.

Another way to remove disturbances from the signal model

Equation

2.1
, especially if the perturbed model has the noisy
structure given in
Equation

2.2
, is to
denoise
.
When the data is highly structured or low-dimensional, one-step denoising
becomes statistically and computationally difficult

(
Pedregosa, 2023
)
. Hence the modern solution to this problem is
via
denoising diffusion models
, which take many small denoising steps
towards the data distribution at progressively decreasing noise levels

(
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
;
Karras et al., 2022
)
.
Such models use estimates of the so-called
score function

\nabla\log p_{\sigma}

(
Hyvärinen, 2005
)
, where
p_{\sigma}
is the
probability density function of the noised input when the noise has standard
deviation
\sigma>0
. At all sufficiently small values of
\sigma
, the score function
\nabla\log p_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{Z}})
for a particular noised input
\widetilde{\bm{Z}}

points towards the closest point to
\widetilde{\bm{Z}}
on
the data distribution support

(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Lu et al., 2023
;
Yu et al., 2023a
)
, or more generally the modes
of the true data distribution, which guides the denoising diffusion model to
project
\bm{Z}
onto the support of the data distribution and diffuse it within
this support.

. In the sequel, we use this connection to understand the

.

2.4
Constructing a Distributionally-Invertible Transformer Layer

In
Section

2.1
, we described a method to construct a white-box transformer-like encoder network via unrolled optimization meant to compress the data against learned geometric and statistical structures, say against a distribution of tokens where each token is marginally distributed as a Gaussian mixture supported on
\bm{U}_{[K]}
. In
Section

2.3
, we described in general terms an approach that relates denoising and compression to yield a conceptually similar network using the formalism of diffusion models, this time trainable via autoencoding. In this section, we carry out this procedure concretely to obtain an encoder and decoder layer with similarly interpretable operational characteristics.

To measure compression, we use the
R^{c}
function defined in
Equation

2.5
. By using a standard (reverse-time) diffusion process with a scaling of
R^{c}
as a drop-in replacement for the score (see
Section

A.3
for details), we obtain that such a denoising diffusion process may be described by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE)
(
Song et al., 2021
)
.

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{Z}(t)=-\frac{1}{T-t}\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Z}(t)\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{B}(t),\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

(2.10)

where
(\bm{B}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a Brownian motion.
As a design choice
, we wish to assert that our encoder and decoder ought to be deterministic, in particular preferring that our encoder-decoder architecture achieves
sample-wise autoencoding
as opposed to
distribution-wise autoencoding
or
generation
. Thus we need to construct some ordinary differential equation (ODE) which transports the input probability distribution in the same way as
Equation

2.10
. Such an equation is readily obtained as the
probability flow ODE

(
Song et al., 2021
)
, which itself is commonly used for denoising and sampling
(
Song et al., 2021
;
Lu et al., 2022
;
Song et al., 2023
)
and has the form

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{Z}(t)=-\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Z}(t)\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(2.11)

In particular, the
\bm{Z}(t)
generated by
Equations

2.10
and
2.11
have the same law. A first-order discretization (see
Section

A.3
) of
Equation

2.11
with step size
\kappa
obtains the iteration:

\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}=\bm{Z}^{\ell}+\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell})\approx\bm{Z}^{\ell}-\kappa\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell}),

(2.12)

where
\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\cdot)
was defined in
Equation

2.8
. Similar to
Yu et al. (2023a)
, in order to optimize the sparse rate reduction of the features, and in particular to sparsify them, we instantiate a learnable dictionary
\bm{D}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and sparsify against it, obtaining

\bm{Z}^{\ell+1}=\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}\mid\bm{D}^{\ell}),

(2.13)

where
\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\cdot)
was defined in
Equation

2.9
. Thus, we obtain a two step iteration for the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
encoder layer
f^{\ell}
, where
\bm{Z}^{\ell+1}=f^{\ell}(\bm{Z}^{\ell})
:

\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}=\bm{Z}^{\ell}+\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell}),\qquad\bm{Z}^{\ell+1}=\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell+1/2}\mid\bm{D}^{\ell}).

(2.14)

This is the same layer as in
crate
, whose conceptual behavior is illustrated in
Figure

2
. This equivalence stems from the fact that the diffusion probability flow
Equation

2.11
is conceptually and mechanically similar to gradient flow on the compression objective in certain regimes, and so it demonstrates a useful conceptual connection between discretized diffusion and unrolled optimization as iteratively compressing or denoising the signal against the learned data structures.

Note that we parameterized a
different
local signal model
\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell}
and dictionary
\bm{D}^{\ell}
at each layer, despite the continuous-time flows in
Equation

2.11
using only one (i.e., the final) local signal model. This is because the sparsification step
Equation

2.13
transforms the data distribution, and so we require a different local signal model at each layer to model the new (more sparse) data distribution; see
Figure

1

Now that we have shown how the structured diffusion approach can recover the original
crate
architecture
(
Yu et al., 2023a
)
as an encoder in our autoencoding problem, we use our new approach to construct a novel matching decoder. The time reversal of the ODE
Equation

2.11
is:

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{Y}(t)=\frac{1}{2t}\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Y}(t)\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

(2.15)

in the sense that the
\bm{Y}(T-t)
generated by
Equation

2.15
has the same law as the
\bm{Z}(t)
generated by
Equation

2.11
, assuming compatible initial conditions. A first-order discretization of
Equation

2.15
obtains the iteration:

\bm{Y}^{\ell+1}=\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}-\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}\mid\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell})\approx\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}+\kappa\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}\mid\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell}),

(2.16)

where
\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell}=(\bm{V}_{1}^{\ell},\dots,\bm{V}_{K}^{\ell})
and each
\bm{V}_{k}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
are the bases of the subspaces to “anti-compress” against. In our work, we treat them as
different
from the corresponding
\bm{U}_{k}^{L-\ell}
, because the discretization of
Equations

2.11
and
2.15
is imperfect, and thus we should not expect a 1-1 correspondence between local signal models in the encoder and decoder.
To invert the effect of a sparsifying
\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}
step, which our mental model in
Figure

2
portrays as a rotation of the subspace supports to a more incoherent configuration, we multiply by another learnable dictionary
\bm{E}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, obtaining

\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}=\bm{E}^{\ell}\bm{Y}^{\ell},\qquad\bm{Y}^{\ell+1}=\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}-\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Y}^{\ell+1/2}\mid\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell}).

(2.17)

This constructs the
(\ell+1)^{\mathrm{st}}
layer
g^{\ell}
of our decoder.
In the implementation, we add layer normalizations to ensure that the features are roughly constant-size so that the above approximations hold.

2.5
A Complete White-Box Transformer-Like Architecture for Autoencoding

As previously discussed, the encoder is the concatenation of a preprocessing map
f^{\mathrm{pre}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
, which has learnable parameters
\bm{W}^{\mathrm{pre}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
and
\bm{E}^{\mathrm{pos}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
, and has the form:

f^{\mathrm{pre}}(\bm{X})\doteq\bm{W}^{\mathrm{pre}}\bm{X}+\bm{E}^{\mathrm{pos}},

(2.18)

and
L
transformer-like layers
f^{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
given by

f^{\ell}(\bm{Z}^{\ell})\doteq\operatorname{\texttt{ISTA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}+\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{Z}^{\ell}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell})\mid\bm{D}^{\ell}),\qquad\forall\ell\in[L],

(2.19)

omitting normalization for simplicity. The decoder is the concatenation of
L
transformer-like layers
g^{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
given by

g^{\ell}(\bm{Y}^{\ell})\doteq\bm{E}^{\ell}\bm{Y}^{\ell}-\operatorname{\texttt{MSSA}}(\bm{E}^{\ell}\bm{Y}^{\ell}\mid\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell}),\qquad\forall\ell\in[L]-1,

(2.20)

with a postprocessing map
g^{\mathrm{post}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
which is a learnable linear map
\bm{W}^{\mathrm{post}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
:

g^{\mathrm{post}}(\bm{Y}^{L})\doteq\bm{W}^{\mathrm{post}}\bm{Y}^{L}.

(2.21)

A full diagram of the autoencoding procedure is given in

Figure

1
.

Our training procedure seeks to learn and represent the structures in the data distribution. For this, we use a pretext task that measures the degree to which these structures have been learned:
masked autoencoding

(
He et al., 2022
)
, which “masks out” a large percentage of randomly selected tokens in the input
\bm{X}
and then attempts to reconstruct the whole image, measuring success by the resulting autoencoding performance.
Conceptually, masked autoencoding can be seen as a nonlinear generalization of
the classical
matrix completion
task, which exploits low-dimensional
structure to impute missing entries in incomplete data; classical matrix completion can be solved efficiently if and only if the data have low-dimensional structure
(
Candès & Recht, 2009
;
Amelunxen et al., 2014
;
Wright & Ma, 2022
)
.
The masked autoencoding loss writes

L_{\mathrm{MAE}}(f,g)\doteq\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert(g\circ f)(\operatorname{\texttt{Mask}}(\bm{X}))-\bm{X}\rVert_{2}^{2}].

(2.22)

Further implementation details of this architecture are discussed in
Sections

B.1
and
B.2
.

4
Conclusion

In this work, we uncover a quantitative connection between denoising and compression, and use it to design a conceptual framework for building white-box (mathematically interpretable) transformer-like deep neural networks which can learn using unsupervised pretext tasks, such as masked autoencoding. We show that such models are more parameter-efficient over their empirically designed cousins, achieve promising performance on large-scale real-world imagery datasets, and learn structured representations that contain semantic meaning. This work demonstrates the potential and practicality of white-box networks derived from first principles for tasks outside supervised classification. We thus believe that this work helps to bridge the theory and practice of deep learning, by unifying on both the conceptual and technical level many previously separated approaches including, but not limited to, diffusion and denoising, compression and rate reduction, transformers, and (masked) autoencoding.

Appendix A
Other Related Work

In
Section

1
, we described the approaches to unsupervised learning of low-dimensional structures in the data that were most relevant to the rest of the work. Here, we discuss some other popular alternatives for completeness.

Black-box unsupervised representation learning.

On the other end from white-box models, which learn representations from data that have
a priori
desired structures, are black-box unsupervised learning methods which learn fully data-driven representations. One implementation of this principle includes contrastive learning, which learns representations from computing the statistics of multiple augmentations of the same data point
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Bardes et al., 2022
)
. Another angle is to seek a representation with desirable characteristics for a specific task, such as classification; prior works have considered diffusion models as “representation learners” from this angle
(
Chen et al., 2024
;
Xiang et al., 2023
)
. The notion of representation learning we are interested in in this work, namely the transformation of the data distribution towards a structured form that preserves its essential information content, is different from the notion in this latter group of works. Still another implementation is that of
autoencoding
models, the most recently popular of which is the
masked autoencoder
(MAE)
(
He et al., 2022
)
. Autoencoders attempt to build low-dimensional representations of the data and use them to reconstruct input data
(
Tishby & Zaslavsky, 2015
;
Rezende et al., 2014
;
Kingma & Welling, 2014
;
Hinton & Zemel, 1993
)
; masked autoencoders specifically mask the input data in training and attempt to impute the missing entries through reconstruction.

The common point in all such unsupervised learning methods so far is that they use
black-box
neural networks, such as ResNets
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
or transformers
(
Caron et al., 2021
)
, as their back-end. Thus, although they sometimes develop semantically meaningful representations of the data
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Caron et al., 2021
;
Bardes et al., 2022
)
, they are uninterpretable, and their training procedures and internal mechanisms are opaque.

Deep networks and stochastic dynamics.

There are many quantitative rapprochements of deep learning and stochastic dynamics. The most well-known of these is diffusion models, which can be modeled as discretizations of Itö diffusion processes
(
Song et al., 2021
)
. The neural network is usually trained to estimate the so-called
score function
. Diffusion models can be thought of as implementing a particular approximation to optimal transport between a template distribution and the true data distribution
(
Khrulkov et al., 2023
)
. Different types of stochastic dynamics useful for generative modeling may be derived from optimal transport between the data distribution and a pre-specified template
(
De Bortoli et al., 2021
;
Albergo et al., 2023
)
. However, diffusion models are unique among these methods in that they have an
iterative denoising
interpretation
(
Karras et al., 2022
)
, which this work draws on. Such an interpretation has previously been used to construct deep denoising networks from unrolled diffusion processes
(
Mei & Wu, 2023
)
, instead of just using the deep networks to do black-box estimation of the score function. Similar studies have interpreted deep networks as discretizations of diffusion processes without this particular denoising interpretation
(
Li et al., 2022
)
, but the aforementioned unrolled iterative denoising strategy is what we draw upon in this work.

Other related work.

Here we also discuss some related work with regards to different modifications of the transformer architecture and training procedures which interface well with our white-box design. For example,
Kitaev et al. (2020)
suggests that sharing the
Q
and
K
matrices in the regular transformer is a mechanism to make the transformer more efficient at no performance cost. This choice is a heuristic, whereas our white-box design suggests that
Q
,
K
, and
V
should be set equal, and as we see in the paper this comes with some small tradeoffs. Also, since white-box models are derived such that each layer has a defined and understood role, it is natural to ask if such models can be trained layer-wise, i.e., one layer at a time
(
Bengio et al., 2006
)
. While this is also possible, we leave it to future work; our experiments show that with end-to-end training, a vastly more common method to train deep networks, each layer still follows the role it was designed for.

A.2
Companion to
Section

In this section, we prove a formal version of the result
Theorem

1
stated in
Section

2.3
. That is, we examine a basic yet representative instantiation of the signal model
Equation

2.2
, and show that under this model, in a
natural regime of parameter scales motivated by the architecture of
crate-mae

applied to standard image classification benchmarks, the operation implemented
by taking a gradient step on the compression term of the sparse rate reduction
objective
Equation

2.3
corresponds to a projection operation at
quantization scales
\varepsilon^{2}
proportional to the size of the deviation.
This leads us in particular to a formal version of the result

Theorem

1
.

Signal model.

We consider an instantiation of the model
Equation

2.2
, elaborated here. That is, we fix a distribution over tokens
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
induced by
the following natural signal model: each token
\bm{z}_{i}
is drawn independently from the
normalized isotropic Gaussian measure on one of
K

p
-dimensional
subspaces with orthonormal bases
\bm{U}_{1},\dots,\bm{U}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
,
2
2

2

More precisely,
\bm{z}_{i}
is distributed according to the
pushforward of the normalized isotropic Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\tfrac{1}{p}\bm{I})
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
by the bases
\bm{U}_{k}
.

which comprise the low-dimensional structure in the observed tokens, then
corrupted with i.i.d. Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\bm{I})
;
the subspace each token is drawn from is selected uniformly at random,
independently of all other randomness in the problem.
This signal model therefore corresponds to the setting of uncorrelated tokens,
with maximum entropy coordinate distributions within subspaces.
It is natural to first develop our
theoretical understanding of the connection between compression and the score
function in the uncorrelated setting, although in general, the ability of
crate-mae
to capture correlations in the
data through the MSSA block is essential. In connection with the latter issue, we note that our proofs will generalize straightforwardly to the setting of “well-dispersed” correlated tokens: see the discussion in
Remark

5
.

We make the further following assumptions within this model:

1.

Inspired by an ablation in
Yu et al. (2023a)
, which suggests that the learned

crate-mae
model on supervised classification on ImageNet has signal models
\bm{U}_{k}

which are near-incoherent, we will assume that the subspaces
\bm{U}_{k}
have pairwise orthogonal column spaces.
Our proofs will generalize straightforwardly to the setting where the subspaces are merely incoherent: see the discussion in
Remark

5
.

2.

We assume that the relative scales of these parameters conform to the

crate-mae
-Base settings, trained on ImageNet: from

Table

1
, these parameters are

(a)

d=768
;

(b)

N=196
;

(c)

K=12
;

(d)

p=d/K=64
.

In particular,
d\gg N\gg p
and
Kp=d
.

These precise parameter values will not play a role in our analysis. We merely require the following quantitative relationships between the parameter values, which are more general than the above precise settings.

Assumption 2
.

We have
\varepsilon\leq 1
,
\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}={\mathds{1}}_{k=k^{\prime}}\bm{I}
for all
k\neq k^{\prime}
, and the following parameter settings and scales:

•

d\geq N\geq p\geq K\geq 2
;

•

Kp=d
;

•

C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}\leq\frac{1}{2}N/K
, where
C_{1}
is the same as the universal constant
C_{1}
in the statement of
Proposition

12
;

•

6C_{2}^{2}N\leq d
, where
C_{2}
is the same as the universal constant
C_{3}
in the statement of
Proposition

15
;

•

2C_{4}^{2}N\leq d
, where
C_{4}
is the same as the universal constant
C_{1}
in
Proposition

11
;

Note:
there is no self-reference, as the third inequality is not used to prove
Proposition

12
, the fourth is not used to prove
Proposition

15
, and the fifth is not used to prove
Proposition

11
.

The first and second inequalities together imply in particular that
p\geq N/K
. The third inequality implies that
C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}<N/K
. The first, second, and and third inequalities together imply that
p>C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}
, and that
0<N/K-C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}<N/K<N/K+C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}<N
.

These inequalities are verifiable in practice if one wishes to explicitly compute the absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
, and indeed they hold for our
crate-mae
-Base model.

Formally, let
\mu(K,p,\sigma^{2})
denote the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}

corresponding to the noisy Gaussian mixture distribution specified above.
We let
\bm{Z}_{\natural}\sim\mu
denote an observation distributed according to
this signal model: formally, there exists a (random) map
i\mapsto s_{i}
, for
i\in[N]
and
s_{i}\in[K]
, such that

\bm{z}_{\natural i}=\bm{U}_{s_{i}}\bm{\alpha}_{i}+\bm{\delta}_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(A.13)

where
\bm{\Delta}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\delta}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\bm{I})
, and (independently)
\bm{\alpha}_{i}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\tfrac{1}{p}\bm{I})
.
It is convenient to write this observation model in block form.
To this end, let
K_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathds{1}}_{s_{i}=k}
for
k\in[K]

denote the number of times the
k
-th subspace is represented amongst the
columns of
\bm{Z}_{\natural}
(a random variable).
Then by rotational invariance of the Gaussian distribution, we have

\bm{Z}_{\natural}\overset{d}{=}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{1}\bm{A}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{U}_{K}\bm{A}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\bm{\Pi}+\bm{\Delta},

(A.14)

where
\overset{d}{=}
denotes equality in distribution,
\bm{\Pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is
a uniformly random permutation matrix, and each
\bm{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times K_{k}}
. We also define
\bm{X}_{\natural}
to be the noise-free version of
\bm{Z}_{\natural}
.

Because of this equality in distribution, we will commit the mild abuse of
notation of identifying the block representation

Equation

A.14
with the observation model

Equation

A.13
that follows the distribution
\mu
.

Denoising in the uncorrelated tokens model.

In the uncorrelated tokens model
Equation

A.14
, the marginal
distribution of each column of
\bm{Z}_{\natural}
is identical, and equal to an
equiproportional mixture of (normalized) isotropic Gaussians on the subspaces

\bm{U}_{1},\dots\bm{U}_{k}
, convolved with the noise distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{d}\bm{I})
.
This marginal distribution was studied in
Yu et al. (2023a)
, where it was shown that
when the perturbation level
\sigma^{2}\to 0
, the score function for this
marginal distribution approximately implements a projection operation onto the
nearest subspace
\bm{U}_{k}
.

Hence, we can connect compression, as implemented in the MSSA block of the

crate-mae
architecture, to denoising in the uncorrelated tokens model by showing
that at similar local scales, and for suitable settings of the model parameters,
the compression operation implements a projection onto the low-dimensional
structure of the distribution, as well.

Compression operation.

The MSSA block of the
crate-mae
architecture arises from taking an (approximate)
gradient step on the
R^{c}
term of the sparse rate reduction objective

Equation

2.3
. This term writes

R^{c}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\det\left(\bm{I}+\beta(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}\right),

(A.15)

where

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{N\varepsilon^{2}},

(A.16)

and
\varepsilon>0
is the quantization error.
Calculating the gradient, we have

\nabla_{\bm{Z}}R^{c}(\bm{Z}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}\right)^{-1}.

(A.17)

Minimizing the sparse rate reduction objective corresponds to taking a gradient
descent step on
R^{c}(\,\cdot\,\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})
. Performing this operation at the
observation from the uncorrelated tokens model
\bm{Z}_{\natural}
, the output can be
written as

\bm{Z}^{+}=\bm{Z}_{\natural}-\eta\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Z}_{\natural}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}),

(A.18)

where
\eta>0
is the step size.

Main result on projection.

We will see shortly that the behavior of
the compression output
Equation

A.18
depends on the relative
scales of the perturbation about the low-dimensional structure
\sigma^{2}
and
the target quantization error
\varepsilon^{2}
.

Theorem 3
.

There are universal constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}>0
such that the following holds.
Suppose
Assumption

2
holds, and moreover suppose that

\sigma\leq 1
and
C_{1}\beta\sigma\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Then with probability at least
1-KC_{2}\bigl(e^{-C_{3}d}+e^{-C_{4}N/K}+N^{-2}\bigr)
,
it holds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{Z}^{+}-\left[\left(\bm{\Delta}-\eta\mathcal{P}_{\bm{U}_{[K]}}(\beta\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})\bm{\Pi}\right)+\frac{1+\beta^{-1}-\eta}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{X}_{\natural}\right]\right\rVert

(A.19)

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}K\eta\left(\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}+\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{K}\beta\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{N/d}\right).

(A.20)

Here,

\mathcal{P}_{\bm{U}_{[K]}}
implements a projection onto the relevant subspaces for
each token in the limiting case as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and is precisely defined
in
Equations

A.101
and
A.102
.

We give the proof of
Theorem

3
below. First, we make three remarks
on interpreting the result, our technical assumptions, and our analysis.

Remark 4
.

Theorem

3
admits the following interesting interpretation in
an asymptotic setting, where we can identify the leading-order behavior of
the gradient and confirm our hypothesis about the connection between
compression and score-following.
Choose
\eta=\beta^{-1}
, so that the guarantee in
Theorem

3

incurs some cancellation, and moreover delineate more precise dependencies
on the RHS of the guarantee:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{Z}^{+}-\left[\left(\bm{\Delta}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{U}_{[K]}}(\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})\bm{\Pi}\right)+\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{X}_{\natural}\right]\right\rVert

(A.21)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\frac{NK^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{d}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}d^{2}}{N^{2}K^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}+\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{N\sqrt{K}\varepsilon^{2}}(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{N/d}\right)

(A.22)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim K^{3/2}\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{N\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{NK^{2}}{d}\left(\sigma+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}\right)\varepsilon^{2},

(A.23)

where we used
Assumption

2
, which implies
p=d/K
and

N/d\leq 1
.
We will check in due course whether we have satisfied the hypotheses of

Theorem

3
, so that this guarantee indeed applies.
To this end, we optimize this bound as a function of
\varepsilon>0
, since this is a
parameter of the compression model.
The optimal
\varepsilon
is straightforward to compute using calculus: it
satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left.\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{N}\right/\frac{K^{2}N}{d}\left(\sigma+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}\right)}

(A.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma d}{NK\sqrt{\sigma+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}}},

(A.25)

and the value of the residual arising from
Theorem

3
with this
choice of
\varepsilon
is no larger than an absolute constant multiple of

K^{3/2}\sigma^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{K^{2}\sigma^{2}d}{N}\left(\frac{N\sigma}{d}+\left(\frac{N}{d}\right)^{3/2}\right)}=K\sigma\left(\sqrt{K}\sigma+\sqrt{\sigma+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}}\right).

(A.26)

Moreover, with this choice of
\varepsilon
,
\beta
satisfies

\beta^{-1}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}NK}{d}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d\sigma^{2}}}}}.

(A.27)

In particular, the condition
\beta\sigma\lesssim 1
in

Theorem

3
demands

\sqrt{\sigma+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}}\lesssim 1,

(A.28)

which holds for sufficiently small
\sigma
and sufficiently large
d\geq N
, showing that
Theorem

3
can be nontrivially applied in this
setting.
If we consider a simplifying limiting regime where
N,d\to+\infty
such
that
N/d\to 0
and
N/K\to+\infty
, we observe the following asymptotic
behavior of the guarantee of
Theorem

3
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{Z}^{+}-\left[\left(\bm{\Delta}-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{U}_{[K]}}(\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})\bm{\Pi}\right)+\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\sigma}}\bm{X}_{\natural}\right]\right\rVert\lesssim K\sigma^{3/2}\left(1+\sqrt{K\sigma}\right).

(A.29)

This demonstrates that a gradient step on
R^{c}
performs denoising: there is
a noise-level-dependent shrinkage effect applied to the signal

\bm{X}_{\natural}
, which vanishes as
\sigma\to 0
, and meanwhile the noise
term
\bm{\Delta}
is reduced.

Moreover, as
\sigma\to 0
, we can express the limiting form of

\mathcal{P}_{\bm{U}_{[K]}}
exactly as an orthogonal projection, since this drives

\beta^{-1}\to 0
:
following
Equations

A.101
and
A.102
, we have here

\mathcal{P}_{U_{[K]}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{1}&\ldots&\mathcal{P}_{K}\end{bmatrix},

(A.30)

where

\mathcal{P}_{k}\to\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}\operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{im}(\bm{A}_{k^{\prime}})^{\perp}}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}.

(A.31)

This shows that, in an asymptotic sense, a gradient step on
R^{c}
serves to suppress
the effect of the perturbation applied to the observations
\bm{Z}_{\natural}

about the local signal model
\bm{X}_{\natural}
. This verifies our claim
previously that in this setting, there is a correspondence between a
score-following algorithm and a compression-based approach: locally, both
project the observations onto the structures of the signal model.

It can be shown moreover that the shrinkage effect on
\bm{X}_{\natural}

demonstrated here appears as a consequence of using the
R^{c}

“compression” term for the gradient step in
crate-mae
; when the gradient
step is taken instead on the full
\Delta R
rate reduction objective (which
is computationally prohibitive, of course), there is zero shrinkage, and
perfect denoising is performed for a wider variety of step sizes
\eta
than the choice made here. We see the introduction of this shrinkage
effect this as the price of constructing an efficient and interpretable
network architecture. In practice, the ISTA block of
crate-mae
counteracts
this shrinkage effect, which is anyways minor at reasonable parameter scales.

Remark 5
.

We have made two assumptions which may not hold exactly in practice: namely, we have assumed that the
\bm{U}_{k}
’s have orthogonal columns, namely
\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}={\mathds{1}}_{k=k^{\prime}}\bm{I}
, and we have assumed that the linear combination coefficients
\bm{A}_{k}
that form the matrix
\bm{X}_{\natural}
are i.i.d. samples from Gaussian distributions. Both these assumptions can be made more realistic, at the cost of additional (non-instructive) complexity in the analysis; we briefly go over how.

Relaxing the orthogonality condition
\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}={\mathds{1}}_{k=k^{\prime}}\bm{I}
to near-orthogonality, namely
\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}-{\mathds{1}}_{k=k^{\prime}}\bm{I}\rVert\leq\nu
for a small
\nu
, as observed in practice
(
Yu et al., 2023a
)
would introduce additional small error terms in the proof, say polynomial in
\nu
. The magnitudes of these errors could in principle be precisely tracked, whence one could obtain a similar result to
Theorem

3
.

Secondly, we have assumed that the
\bm{A}_{k}
’s have independent columns which are sampled from (the same) Gaussian distribution. However, in the conceptual framework for
crate-mae
, we exploit the joint distribution (and in particular the correlations) between the tokens in order to obtain good performance for our model. Our analysis is not completely agnostic to this fact; as we will see, the proof of
Theorem

3
only leverages the independence of the columns of each
\bm{A}_{k}
’s in order to obtain high-probability upper bounds on the smallest and largest singular value of the token matrices. If these bounds were ensured by some other method, such as appropriate normalization and incoherence, a similar conclusion to
Theorem

3
could hold in the more realistic correlated tokens model. Going beyond well-conditioned token matrices for each subspace would require additional modeling assumptions, and additional investigative experimental work to determine a realistic basis for such assumptions.

Remark 6
.

We have not attempted to optimize constants or rates of concentration in the
proof of
Theorem

3
, preferring instead to pursue a
straightforward analysis that leads to a qualitative interpretation of the
behavior of the rate reduction gradient in our model problem. Minor
improvements to the concentration analysis would enable the parameter
scaling requirements in
Assumption

2
to be relaxed slightly,
and the probability bound in
Theorem

3
that scales as
K/N^{2}
can easily be improved to any positive power of
1/N
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

We start by noticing that, by orthonormality of the subspaces
\bm{U}_{k}
,
we have by
Equation

A.14

\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\bm{\Pi}+\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta},

(A.32)

so that

\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)^{-1}=\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\left(\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\beta^{-1}\bm{I}&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&\\
&&\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&\beta^{-1}\bm{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{D}_{k}}+\bm{\Xi}_{k}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Pi},

(A.33)

because permutation matrices are orthogonal matrices, and where the
perturbation
\bm{\Xi}_{k}
is defined by

\bm{\Xi}_{k}=\bm{\Pi}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{K}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(A.34)

and where we have defined (implicitly) in addition

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}.

(A.35)

The matrix
\bm{D}_{k}\succ\bm{0}
, so we can write

\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)^{-1}=\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\left(\bm{I}+\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Pi},

(A.36)

from which it follows

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)^{-1}

(A.37)

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)\left(\bm{I}+\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Pi}.

(A.38)

The task before us is therefore to control
\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert<1
, in order to apply the Neumann series to further simplify this
expression. We will do this in stages: first, we invoke several
auxiliary lemmas to construct a high-probability event on which the
random quantities in the preceding expression are controlled about their
nominal values; next, we show that the Neumann series can be applied on
this event and a main term extracted; finally, we simplify this main
term further in order to establish the claimed expression.

High-probability event construction.

In order to achieve the appropriate control on all random quantities, we would like to construct a high-probability event on which the random quantities are not too large. By
Propositions

9
,
10
and
11
and union bound, there exist universal constants
C_{i}>0
for which

\mathbb{P}\left[\begin{array}[]{rl}&\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert\leq\sigma(C_{1}+\sqrt{N/d})\\
\forall k\in[K]\colon&\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert\leq 1+C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}\\
\forall k\in[K]\colon&\lVert\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{I}\rVert\leq C_{3}\sqrt{N/d}\end{array}\right]\geq 1-C_{4}K(e^{-C_{5}d}+e^{-C_{6}N/K}+N^{-2}).

The event we compute the probability of, which we denote
E^{\star}
, is precisely the good event that we want. Formally,

E^{\star}\doteq\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert\leq\sigma(C_{1}+\sqrt{N/d})\\
\forall k\in[K]\colon&\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert\leq 1+C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}\\
\forall k\in[K]\colon&\lVert\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{I}\rVert\leq C_{3}\sqrt{N/d}\end{array}\right\}.

We know that
E^{\star}
occurs with high probability, and are able to strongly control the random quantities to the degree desired.

Main term extraction.

By
Lemma

8
and our hypotheses on the problem parameters, we have on
E^{\star}
that

\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert\leq C\beta\sigma<1.

(A.47)

We can therefore apply the Neumann series to obtain

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)^{-1}

(A.48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.8}{$\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)\left(\bm{I}-\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\left(\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)^{j}\right)\bm{\Pi}$}.

(A.49)

Again on
E^{\star}
, we have

\left\lVert\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\left(\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)^{j}\right\rVert\leq\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right\rVert^{j}\leq C(\beta\sigma)^{2}\frac{1}{1-C\beta\sigma}\leq C^{\prime}(\beta\sigma)^{2}.

(A.50)

Moreover, as in the proof of
Lemma

8
, we have on the previous event
that

\left\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right\rVert\leq C\beta\sigma.

(A.51)

Thus, if we define a “main term”

\bm{M}_{k}=\left[\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right)+\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right]\bm{\Pi},

(A.52)

we have on the same event as previously

\left\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)^{-1}-\bm{M}_{k}\right\rVert\leq C(\beta\sigma)^{2}.

(A.53)

To conclude, we need only study this main term, since
\bm{U}_{k}
has operator norm
1
.

Simplifying the main term.

Our approach will be to control the main term
\bm{M}_{k}
around a simpler
expression, using basic perturbation theory; by the triangle inequality
for the operator norm, this will give control of the desired gradient
term.
After distributing,
\bm{M}_{k}
is a sum of three terms; we will start with
the simplest term. We first compute

\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}=\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots\bm{\Delta}_{k}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.54)

We are going to argue that the residual

\left\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}-\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert

(A.55)

is small. To this end, note that by the fact that
\bm{U}_{k}
has unit
operator norm,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}-\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert

(A.56)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{k}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert

(A.57)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

(A.58)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert.

(A.59)

By
Section

A.2
and
Equation

A.105
from

Lemma

7
, the second term
here is controlled on
E^{\star}
.
For the first term, we note that by definition and the fact that
the unit sphere is invariant to rotations (and permutations are
rotations),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert=\sup_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{2}

(A.60)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{K}\bm{\Delta}_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\right\rVert_{2},

(A.61)

where
\bm{u}_{i}
are coordinate-subset-induced partitions of the vector

\bm{u}
induced by those of
\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
.
This yields immediately

\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert\leq\sup_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(\max_{k\in[K]}\,\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}\rVert\right)\sup_{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lVert\bm{u}_{i}\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{K}\left(\max_{k\in[K]}\,\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}\rVert\right),

(A.62)

by the triangle inequality and inequalities for
\ell^{p}
norms.
Similarly, choosing a specific
\bm{u}
in the operator norm expression,
namely one that is supported entirely on one of the coordinate
partitions
\bm{u}_{i}
, shows that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}

(A.63)

for any
i
, whence

\max_{k\in[K]}\,\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}\rVert\leq\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}\right\rVert.

(A.64)

It follows that we control the first term above on
E^{\star}
.
Combining this reasoning, we conclude from the above

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}-\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert

(A.65)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma(C+\sqrt{N/d})\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}+\frac{C^{\prime}\sqrt{N/d}}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)

(A.66)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sigma(1+C\sqrt{N/d}),

(A.67)

where the second line uses
Assumption

2
to
remove the higher-order residual.

Next, we recall that
\bm{\Xi}_{k}
is a sum of two terms; we will do one term
at a time for concision. We have first

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\bm{\Pi}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}

(A.68)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\Delta}_{K}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\Delta}_{K}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

(A.69)

\displaystyle=\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.70)

We then multiply this term by
\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}
on the right to get the term
that appears in
\bm{M}_{k}
(ignoring the multiplication on the right by

\bm{\Pi}
, because it does not change operator norms).
In particular, we will control

\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right\rVert,

(A.71)

showing that this term is small.
We will accomplish this with the block diagonal structure of
\bm{D}_{k}
:
indeed, this gives that
\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}
is obtained by blockwise inversion,
and hence

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right\rVert

(A.72)

\displaystyle\quad=\scalebox{0.9}{$\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{k}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert$}

(A.73)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{K}\max\Bigl\{\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\right\rVert,

(A.74)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\max_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\,\beta\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k^{\prime}}\right\rVert\Bigr\},

(A.75)

where the second line uses
Equation

A.62
.
We will give a coarse control of this term—the error could be improved
further by exploiting more thoroughly independence of the blocks

\bm{\Delta}_{k}
to show that the maximum over
k^{\prime}\neq k
in the last line
of the preceding expression is smaller.
We have by submultiplicativity of the operator norm
and the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\right\rVert

(A.76)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}+\frac{C\sqrt{N/d}}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert

(A.77)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+C\sqrt{N/d}\right)\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert,

(A.78)

where the first line uses
Lemma

7
, and the second line
uses
Assumption

2
to simplify the residual as above.
We have meanwhile
from the definition of
E^{\star}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\lesssim\sigma^{2}\left(1+\sqrt{N/d}\right),

(A.79)

because
\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}
is an orthogonal projection, and again using

Assumption

2
to simplify the residual.
We can argue analogously to simplify the other term in the maximum
appearing above, and this yields

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right\rVert

(A.80)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sqrt{K}\beta\sigma^{2}\left(1+C\sqrt{N/d}\right)\left(1+\sqrt{N/d}\right),

(A.81)

where we used the fact that
\varepsilon\leq 1
and the rest of

Assumption

2
implies that
\beta\geq 1
.
This residual simplifies using
Assumption

2
to

\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\right\rVert\lesssim\sqrt{K}\beta\sigma^{2}\left(1+C\sqrt{N/d}\right).

(A.82)

Next, we examine the last term, which is the other summand arising in
the definition of
\bm{\Xi}_{k}
. We have

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{K}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{\Delta}_{K}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(A.83)

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.95}{$\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\left(\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right)&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}$}.

(A.84)

Now multiplying on the right by
\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}
gives the term (again
ignoring the right-multiplication by
\bm{\Pi}
, which does not affect the
operator norm)

\scalebox{0.8}{$\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\left(\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right)\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}&\ldots&\beta\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}$}.

(A.85)

We will argue that this term is close to the term

\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\ldots&\beta\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}

(A.86)

in operator norm. The argument is similar to the preceding arguments:
for this, it suffices to bound

\scalebox{0.9}{$\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\left(\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right)\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert$},

(A.87)

which is the same as controlling the operator norm of the nonzero block.
Using submultiplicativity and
Lemma

7
along with the
simplifications we have done above leveraging

Assumption

2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\left(\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right)\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

(A.88)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(1+C\sqrt{N/d}\right)\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert.

(A.89)

Meanwhile, on
E^{\star}
we have the operator norm of
\bm{\Delta}_{k}
and

\bm{A}_{k}
controlled, using again

Equation

A.64
. Applying then
the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity, we obtain

\left\lVert\bm{\Delta}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}_{k}\right\rVert\lesssim\sigma\left(1+\sqrt{N/d}\right),

(A.90)

again simplifying with
Assumption

2
.
This shows that
Equation

A.85
is close to

Equation

A.86

with deviations of the order
\lesssim\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})
.

Aggregating the previous results.

Combining our perturbation analysis above, we have established control

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\bm{M}_{k}-\Bigl[\left(\bm{I}-\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\right)\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}

(A.91)

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\Bigr]\bm{\Pi}\Bigr\|

(A.92)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{K}\beta\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{N/d}).

(A.93)

It is convenient to include one additional stage of simplification here:
namely, we use
Lemma

7
once more to simplify the
second term in the nominal value of
\bm{M}_{k}
appearing here.
Namely, we have (arguing as we have above, once again)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert

(A.94)

\displaystyle\quad=\left\lVert\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{A}_{k}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

(A.95)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sqrt{N/d},

(A.96)

from which it follows

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\bm{M}_{k}-\Bigl[\left(\bm{I}-\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\right)\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{\Delta}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\ldots&\beta\bm{\Delta}_{K}\end{bmatrix}

(A.97)

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\Bigr]\bm{\Pi}\Bigr\|

(A.98)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{K}\beta\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{N/d}.

(A.99)

Meanwhile, recall the residual of scale
\lesssim(\sigma\beta)^{2}

arising when we controlled the gradient term around
\bm{M}_{k}
:

\left\lVert\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\left(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+(\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right)^{-1}-\bm{M}_{k}\right\rVert\leq C(\beta\sigma)^{2}.

(A.100)

Combining these two bounds with the triangle inequality controls the
gradient term around its nominal value. Now, we sum these errors over

k
(again with the triangle inequality) to obtain control of the
aggregate gradient around its nominal value.
We introduce notation to concisely capture the
accumulations of the (approximate) orthogonal projections arising in the
nominal value of the main term: for each
k\in[K]
, define

\mathcal{P}_{k}=\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{A}_{k^{\prime}}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k^{\prime}})^{-1}\bm{A}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}\right)\bm{U}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top},

(A.101)

and define an overall (approximate) projection operator (which acts on block matrices
partitioned compatibly with the class sizes
N_{k}
, as in

Equation

A.14
) by

\mathcal{P}_{U_{[K]}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{1}&\ldots&\mathcal{P}_{K}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.102)

Then the above argument implies

\displaystyle\;\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{Z}}R^{c}(\bm{Z}_{\natural}\mid\bm{U}_{[K]})-\mathcal{P}_{\bm{U}_{[K]}}(\beta\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})\bm{\Pi}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{X}_{\natural}\right\rVert

(A.103)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\;K\left(\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}+\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{K}\beta\sigma^{2}(1+\sqrt{N/d})+\sqrt{N/d}\right),

(A.104)

which is enough to conclude.

∎

A.2.1
Key Auxiliary Lemmas

In this section we state and prove two key concentration inequalities that are used in the proof of the main theorem. They rely on simpler results which will be conveyed in subsequent subsections.

Lemma 7
.

There exist universal constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
such that the following holds. Let
d,p,N,K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
Assumption

2
holds. Let
\bm{A}_{k}
,
k\in[K]
, be defined as above. Let
E^{\star}
be the good event defined in
Section

A.2
. If
E^{\star}
occurs, then for
k\in[K]
we have

\left\lVert(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{I}\right\rVert\leq\frac{C\sqrt{N/d}}{(1+\beta^{-1})}.

(A.105)

and in addition

\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{A}_{k}\right\rVert\leq\frac{C^{\prime}\sqrt{N/d}}{(1+\beta^{-1})}.

(A.106)

Proof.

Since
E^{\star}
holds, for all
k\in[K]
we have

\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert\leq 1+C_{1}\sqrt{N/d},\qquad\lVert\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{I}\rVert\leq C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}.

(A.107)

By
Assumption

2
, we have
\lVert\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{I}\rVert<1
, so
\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}
is well-conditioned. Write

\bm{\Xi}\doteq\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}-\bm{I},

(A.108)

so that

\displaystyle(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}

\displaystyle=((1+\beta^{-1})\bm{I}+\bm{\Xi})^{-1}

(A.109)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\left(\bm{I}+\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{\Xi}\right)^{-1}

(A.110)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)^{j}\bm{\Xi}^{j}

(A.111)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{I}+\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)^{j}\bm{\Xi}^{j}

(A.112)

by the Neumann series. This gives us

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{I}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)^{j}\bm{\Xi}^{j}\right\rVert

(A.113)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)^{j}\lVert\bm{\Xi}\rVert^{j}

(A.114)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}}{1+\beta^{-1}}\right)^{j}

(A.115)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}}{(1+\beta^{-1})(1+\beta^{-1}-C_{2}\sqrt{N/d})}.

(A.116)

Meanwhile, by
Assumption

2
, it holds

\displaystyle C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}\leq\sqrt{1/6},

(A.117)

so it follows

\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}}{1+\beta^{-1}-C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}}\leq 2C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}.

(A.118)

By the submultiplicativity of the operator norm, we thus have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{A}_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert\left\lVert(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{I}\right\rVert

(A.119)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{[1+C_{1}\sqrt{N/d}]C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}}{(1+\beta^{-1})(1+\beta^{-1}-C_{2}\sqrt{N/d})}

(A.120)

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{[1+C_{1}\sqrt{N/d}]C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}}{1+\beta^{-1}}

(A.121)

\displaystyle=2\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}+C_{1}C_{2}N/d}{1+\beta^{-1}}.

(A.122)

By
Assumption

2
, we have that there exists some absolute constant
C_{3}>0
with
C_{3}\cdot N/d\leq\sqrt{N/d}
, which gives

\left\lVert\bm{A}_{k}(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}-\frac{1}{1+\beta^{-1}}\bm{A}_{k}\right\rVert\leq 2\frac{(C_{2}+C_{1}C_{2}C_{3}^{-1})\sqrt{N/d}}{1+\beta^{-1}},

(A.123)

as desired.
∎

Lemma 8
.

There exist universal constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that the following holds. Let
d,p,N,K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
Assumption

2
holds. Let
\bm{A}_{k}
,
k\in[K]
, be defined as above. Let
\bm{D}_{k}
be defined as in
Equation

A.33
. Let
\bm{\Xi}_{k}
be defined as in
Equation

A.34
. Let
E^{\star}
be the good event defined in
Section

A.2
. If
E^{\star}
occurs, then for
k\in[K]
we have

\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}\rVert^{-1}\leq C_{1}\beta[\sigma^{2}+\sigma(C_{2}+\sqrt{N/d})].

(A.124)

Proof.

Since we have

\bm{D}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta^{-1}\bm{I}&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&\\
&&\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&\beta^{-1}\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}

(A.125)

it holds that

\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta\bm{I}&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&\\
&&(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&\beta\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.126)

We will use the straightforward estimate
\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert\leq\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\rVert\lVert\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert
and bound the two matrices’ operator norms individually. By the previous expression,

\lVert\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert=\max\{\beta,\lVert(\beta^{-1}\bm{I}+\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\rVert\}\leq\beta,

(A.127)

because
\bm{A}_{k}^{\top}\bm{A}_{k}\succeq\bm{0}
, so we need only control the operator norm of
\bm{\Xi}_{k}
.
To this end, note the convenient expression

\bm{\Xi}_{k}=\bm{\Pi}\bm{\Delta}^{\top}\bm{U}_{k}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}+2\operatorname{sym}\left((\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(A.128)

where
\operatorname{sym}(\,\cdot\,)
denotes the symmetric part operator.
By the triangle inequality, the operator norm of
\bm{\Xi}_{k}
is no larger
than the sum of the operator norms of each term in the previous
expression.
The operator norm of the first term is no larger than

\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert^{2}
, because
\bm{\Pi}
is a permutation matrix and
\bm{U}\bm{U}_{k}^{\top}
is an orthogonal projection.
Meanwhile, using that the symmetric part operator is the orthogonal
projection onto the space of symmetric matrices, it follows

\scalebox{0.9}{$\left\lVert 2\operatorname{sym}\left((\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right)\right\rVert\leq 2\left\lVert(\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{U}_{k}\bm{A}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert$},

(A.129)

and then we find as above that the RHS is no larger than
2\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert
. Since the good event
E^{\star}
defined in
Section

A.2
holds by assumption, we have that there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\left(C_{1}+\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}\right)

(A.130)

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 1+C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}.

(A.131)

By
Assumption

2
we have
d\geq N
, so that
\sqrt{N/d}\leq 1
. Therefore we have

\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert\leq\sigma\left(C_{1}+1\right)=C_{3}\sigma

(A.132)

for
C_{3}\doteq C_{1}+1
another universal constant. Thus on this good event we have

2\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert\leq C_{3}\sigma\left(1+C_{2}\sqrt{N/d}\right).

(A.133)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert^{2}+2\lVert\bm{\Delta}\rVert\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert

(A.134)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}^{2}\sigma^{2}+C_{3}\sigma(1+C_{2}\sqrt{N/d})

(A.135)

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}[\sigma^{2}+\sigma(1+C_{2}\sqrt{N/d})]

(A.136)

where
C_{4}=\max\{C_{3},C_{3}^{2}\}
is another universal constant. Thus on
E^{\star}
we have

\lVert\bm{\Xi}_{k}\bm{D}_{k}^{-1}\rVert\leq C_{4}\beta[\sigma^{2}+\sigma(1+C_{2}\sqrt{N/d})]\leq C_{5}\beta[\sigma^{2}+\sigma(1+\sqrt{N/d})]

(A.137)

for
C_{5}>0
another obvious universal constant.
∎

A.2.2

Proposition 9

Assumption

2

Proposition

14
with the parameters
q=d
,
n=N
, and
x=\sigma/\sqrt{d}
, which obtains

Notice that we have

(A.142)

∎

Proposition 10
.

There are universal constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}>0
such that the following holds. Let
p,N,K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
Assumption

2
holds. Let
\bm{A}_{k}
,
k\in[K]
, be defined as above. Then

\mathbb{P}\left[\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert>1+C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}\right]\leq C_{2}\exp\left(-C_{3}\frac{N}{K}\right)+\frac{C_{4}}{N^{2}}

(A.143)

Proof.

By
Propositions

12
and
14
with parameters
n=n
,
k=K
,
q=p
, and
x=1/\sqrt{p}

N_{\min}\doteq\left\lfloor\frac{N}{K}-C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}\right\rfloor,\qquad N_{\max}\doteq\left\lceil\frac{N}{K}+C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}\right\rceil

(A.144)

then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert>s]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N_{\min}}^{N_{\max}}\mathbb{P}[\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert>s\mid K_{k}=n]\mathbb{P}[K_{k}=n]+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.145)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N_{\min}}^{N_{\max}}C_{3}\exp\left(-n\left\{\frac{s\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}}{C_{4}\sqrt{4}}-1\right\}^{2}\right)\mathbb{P}[K_{k}=n]+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}},

(A.146)

for all
s
obeying

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\left(\sqrt{p}+C_{4}\sqrt{N_{\max}}\right)

(A.147)

\displaystyle=1+C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N_{\max}}{p}}.

(A.148)

Thus we have that the concentration holds for all
s
obeying

s\geq 1+C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N_{\max}}{p}}.

(A.149)

In order to cancel out the most interior terms, we choose

s=1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N_{\max}}{p}}.

(A.150)

This choice obtains

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\lVert\bm{A}_{k}\rVert>s]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N_{\min}}^{N_{\max}}C_{3}\exp\left(-n\left\{\frac{s\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}}{C_{4}\sqrt{n}}-1\right\}^{2}\right)\mathbb{P}[K_{k}=n]+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.151)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=N_{\min}}^{N_{\max}}C_{3}\exp\left(-n\underbrace{\left\{2\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{N_{\max}}{n}}}_{\geq 1}-1\right\}^{2}}_{\geq 1}\right)\mathbb{P}[K_{k}=n]+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.152)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N_{\min}}^{N_{\max}}C_{3}\exp\left(-n\right)\mathbb{P}[K_{k}=n]+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.153)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N_{\min}}^{N_{\max}}C_{3}\exp\left(-N_{\min}\right)\mathbb{P}[K_{k}=n]+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.154)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\exp(-N_{\min})+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.155)

\displaystyle=C_{3}\exp\left(-\frac{N}{K}+C_{1}\sqrt{N\log N}\right)+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.156)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\exp\left(-\frac{N}{K}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{N\log N}\right)+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}

(A.157)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{N}{K}\right)+\frac{C_{2}}{N^{2}}.

(A.158)

To obtain the conclusion of the theorem, note that any
s
such that

s\geq 1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N_{\max}}{p}}=1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N/K}{p}+C_{1}\frac{\sqrt{N\log N}}{p}}=1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}+C_{1}\frac{\sqrt{N\log N}}{p}}

(A.159)

enjoys the same high-probability bound. By
Assumption

2
, we have

\displaystyle\;1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}+C_{1}\frac{\sqrt{N\log N}}{p}}\leq 1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{N/K}{p}}

(A.160)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{N}{d}}=1+2C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{N}{d}}

(A.161)

whence the ultimate conclusion is obtained by combining constants.
∎

A.2.3

Binomial concentration.

Proposition 12
.

There exist universal constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that the following holds. Let
n,k\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
i\in[k]
, let
B_{i}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/k)
, such that the
B_{i}
are identically (marginally) distributed but not necessarily independent binomial random variables. Let
E
be an event. Then for any
i\in[k]
, we have

\mathbb{P}[E]\leq\sum_{b=\left\lfloor n/k-C_{1}\sqrt{n\log n}\right\rfloor}^{\left\lceil n/k+C_{1}\sqrt{n\log n}\right\rceil}\mathbb{P}[E\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]+\frac{C_{2}}{n^{2}}.

(A.179)

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{P}[E]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[E\mid B_{i}]]=\sum_{b=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b].

(A.180)

Each
B_{i}
is unconditionally distributed as
\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/k)
. By union bound and Hoeffding’s inequality
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Theorem 2.2.6)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\lvert B_{i}-n/k\rvert\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{n}\right).

(A.181)

Inverting this inequality obtains that there exists some (simple) universal constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\lvert B_{i}-n/k\rvert\geq C_{1}\sqrt{n\log n}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}}{n^{3}}.

(A.182)

Thus, if we define

b_{\min}\doteq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k}-C_{1}\sqrt{n\log n}\right\rfloor,\qquad b_{\max}\doteq\left\lceil\frac{n}{k}+C_{1}\sqrt{n\log n}\right\rceil,

(A.183)

then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[E]

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]

(A.184)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{b_{\min}-1}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]}_{\leq 1}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]}_{\leq C_{2}/n^{3}}+\sum_{b=b_{\max}+1}^{n}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b_{1}]}_{\leq 1}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]}_{\leq C_{2}/n^{3}}

(A.185)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{b=b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]

(A.186)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b=0}^{b_{\min}-1}\frac{C_{2}}{n^{3}}+\sum_{b=b_{\max}+1}^{n}\frac{C_{2}}{n^{3}}+\sum_{b=b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]

(A.187)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b=0}^{n}\frac{C_{2}}{n^{3}}+\sum_{b=b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]

(A.188)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{2}}{n^{2}}+\frac{C_{2}}{n^{3}}+\sum_{b=b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b]

(A.189)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{2}}{n^{2}}+\sum_{b=b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\mathbb{P}[E\mid B_{i}=b]\mathbb{P}[B_{i}=b].

(A.190)

.

\lVert\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\rVert-\sqrt{q}\leq C_{3}\left(\sqrt{n}+t\right).

\displaystyle\lVert\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\rVert-\sqrt{q}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\left(\sqrt{n}+t\right)

(A.196)

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{M}\rVert-x\sqrt{q}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}x\left(\sqrt{n}+t\right)

(A.197)

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{M}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq x\left\{\sqrt{q}+C_{3}\left(\sqrt{n}+t\right)\right\}.

(A.198)

s>0

(A.199)

(A.200)

\mathbb{P}[\lVert\bm{M}\rVert>s]\leq 2\exp\left(-\left\{\frac{s/x-\sqrt{q}}{C_{3}}-\sqrt{n}\right\}^{2}\right)=2\exp\left(-n\left\{\frac{s/x-\sqrt{q}}{C_{3}\sqrt{n}}-1\right\}^{2}\right).

Proposition 15
.

There are universal constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
such that the following holds. Let
n,q\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
be such that
M_{ij}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,x^{2})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{M}-qx^{2}\bm{I}\right\rVert>s\right]

(A.202)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}C_{1}\exp\left(-n\left\{\frac{1}{C_{2}^{2}C_{3}\sqrt{nq}x^{2}}s-1\right\}^{2}\right),&\text{if}\ C_{2}^{2}C_{3}\sqrt{nq}x^{2}\leq s\leq C_{2}^{2}qx^{2}\\

Proof.

Define
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\doteq\frac{1}{x}\bm{M}

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{ij}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

Vershynin (2018, Example 2.5.8, Lemma 3.4.2)

\lVert\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}

C_{1}>0

By
Vershynin (2018, Eq. 4.22)

C_{2}>0

\left\lVert\frac{1}{q}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\top}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\bm{I}\right\rVert\leq C_{1}^{2}\max\{\delta,\delta^{2}\}\quad\text{where}\quad\delta\doteq C_{2}\frac{\sqrt{n}+t}{\sqrt{q}}.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{q}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\top}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{M}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\bm{I}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}q\max\{\delta,\delta^{2}\}

In this section, we justify the scaling applied to

in

and supply the discretization scheme.

First, suppose that

satisfies
Equation

\bm{Z}_{t}\doteq\bm{Z}_{\natural}^{\ell}+\sigma_{t}\bm{W}

\bm{Z}_{t}

. Let

be the density of
\bm{Z}_{t}
. Theoretical analysis from
(
Lu et al., 2023
)
and empirical analysis from
(
Song & Ermon, 2019
)
demonstrates that under generic conditions, we have that

\lVert\nabla q_{t}(\bm{Z}_{t})\rVert_{2}\propto\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},

(A.220)

ignoring all terms in the right-hand side except for those involving
\sigma_{t}
. On the other hand, from the proof of
Theorem

3
, we obtain that
-\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Z}_{t})
has constant (in
\sigma_{t}
) magnitude with high probability. Thus, in order to have them be the same magnitude, we need to divide
-\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Z}_{t})
by
\sigma_{t}^{2}
to have it be a drop-in replacement for the score function, as alluded to in
Sections

2.3
and
2.4
.

Second, we wish to explicitly state our discretization scheme given in
Section

2.4
. To wit, we provide a discretization scheme that turns the structured diffusion ODE
Equation

2.15
into its discretized analogue; the other discretization of the structured denoising ODE
Equation

2.11
occurs similarly. To begin with, define the shorthand notation

f(t,\bm{Y}(t))\doteq\nabla R^{c}(\bm{Y}(t)\mid\bm{U}_{[K]}(T-t)),

(A.221)

so that we have

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{Y}(t)=\frac{1}{2t}f(t,\bm{Y}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(

)

Fix

, and let

, such that

Thus it remains to set

B.1
Experiment Details and Clarifications

In all setups, as is standard practice
(
He et al., 2022
)
, we append a trainable class token
\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
after masking and linear projection, namely,

f^{\mathrm{pre}}(\bm{X})=[\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{1},\bm{W}^{\mathrm{pre}}\bm{X}+\bm{E}^{\mathrm{pos}}].

(B.1)

Everything else goes through with
Z
having
N+1
instead of
N
tokens, indexing the
N
image tokens’ intermediate representations as
\bm{z}_{i}^{\ell}
, etc., and indexing the class token intermediate representation as
\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{\ell}
. At the end, the post-processing map is

g^{\mathrm{post}}(\bm{Y}^{L})=\bm{W}^{\mathrm{post}}\bm{Y}_{1:N}^{L}

(B.2)

where
\bm{Y}_{1:N}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
is the matrix whose columns are the columns of
\bm{Y}^{L}
corresponding to image tokens, i.e., the second through last columns of
\bm{Y}^{L}
. Thus, the class token
\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{1}
(and its representation
\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}
, i.e., the first column of
\bm{Z}
) are neither masked or reconstructed.

For training using masked autoencoding, we follow a modified recipe of
(
He et al., 2022
)
. We mask a fixed percentage
\mu\in[0,1]
of randomly selected tokens in
\bm{X}
; that is, we randomly set
(1-\mu)N
image tokens
\bm{x}_{i}
to
\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, obtaining
\overline{\bm{X}}
. Then
\overline{\bm{X}}
is the input to the encoder. Unlike in
(
He et al., 2022
)
, the decoder receives no special treatment, and operates on all token representations. The loss is computed only on the masked image patches. Refer to
He et al. (2022)
for more MAE implementation details.

For fine-tuning using supervised classification, we use the representation
\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}
of the so-far-vestigial class token as the feature used for classification. Namely, we obtain the unnormalized log-probabilities for the classes as
\bm{u}\doteq\bm{W}^{\mathrm{head}}\operatorname{LN}(\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}})
, where
\operatorname{LN}
is a trainable layer-norm and
\bm{W}^{\mathrm{head}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d}
is a trainable weight matrix, where
C
is the number of classes. The output
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
is the input to the softmax cross-entropy loss. All model parameters are trainable during fine-tuning, while in linear probing only the weight matrix
\bm{W}^{\mathrm{head}}
is trainable (and in fact learned via full-batch logistic regression).

In all training setups, we average the loss over all samples in the batch.

We pre-train
crate-mae
on ImageNet-1K
(
Deng et al., 2009
)
. We employ the AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019
)
. We configure the learning rate as
3\times 10^{-5}
, weight decay as
0.1
, and batch size as
4,096
.

We fine-tune and linear probe our pre-trained
crate-mae
on the following target datasets: CIFAR10/CIFAR100
(
Krizhevsky et al., 2009
)
, Oxford Flowers-102
(
Nilsback & Zisserman, 2008
)
, Oxford-IIIT-Pets
(
Parkhi et al., 2012
)
.
For each fine-tuning task, we employ the AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019
)
.
We configure the learning rate as
5\times 10^{-5}
, weight decay as
0.01
, and batch size as
256
.
For each linear probing task, we use the linear probing functionality in Scikit-Learn
(
Pedregosa et al., 2011
)
. For each evaluation we choose several regularizers

, train a logistic regression model on features from the whole dataset, and choose the logistic regression model with the best performance. All numbers are reported on the test sets.

B.3
Visualization Methodology

In this subsection we formally describe the procedures we used to generate the visualizations used to evaluate the segmentation property of
crate-mae
in
Figure

7
. Much of this evaluation is the same as in
Yu et al. (2023b)
, which initially demonstrates the emergent segmentation properties of white-box architectures.

B.3.1
PCA Visualizations

We recapitulate the method to visualize the patch representations in
Figure

7(a)
using PCA from
Amir et al. (2021)
;
Oquab et al. (2023)
;
Yu et al. (2023b)
.

We first select
J
images that belong to the same class,
\{\bm{X}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, and extract the token representations for each image at layer
\ell
, i.e.,
\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{z}}_{j,1}^{\ell},\dots,\bm{z}_{j,N}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}
for
j\in[J]
. In particular,
\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}
represents the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
token representation at the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
layer for the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
image. We then compute the first principal components of
\widehat{\bm{Z}}^{\ell}=\{\widehat{\bm{z}}_{1,1}^{\ell},\dots,\widehat{\bm{z}}_{1,N}^{\ell},\dots,\widehat{\bm{z}}_{J,1}^{\ell},\dots,\widehat{\bm{z}}_{J,N}^{\ell}\}
, and use
\widehat{\bm{z}}_{j,i}^{\ell}
to denote the aggregated token representation for the
i
-th token of
\bm{X}_{j}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\bm{z}}_{j,i}^{\ell}=[(\bm{U}_{1}^{*}\widehat{\bm{z}}_{j,i}^{\ell})^{\top},\dots,(\bm{U}_{K}^{*}\widehat{\bm{z}}_{j,i}^{\ell})^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p\cdot K)\times 1}
.
We denote the first eigenvector of the matrix
\widehat{\bm{Z}}^{*}\widehat{\bm{Z}}
by
\bm{u}_{0}
and compute the projection values as
\{\sigma_{\lambda}(\langle\bm{u}_{0},\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}\rangle)\}_{i,j}
,
where
\sigma_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}x,&\lvert x\rvert\geq\lambda\\
0,&\lvert x\rvert<\lambda\end{cases}
is the hard-thresholding function.
We then select a subset of token representations from
\widehat{\bm{Z}}
with
\sigma_{\lambda}(\langle\bm{u}_{0},\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}\rangle)>0
.
which correspond to non-zero projection values after thresholding, and we denote this subset as
\widehat{\bm{Z}}_{s}\subseteq\widehat{\bm{Z}}
.
This selection step is used to remove the background
(
Oquab et al., 2023
)
.
We then compute the first three right singular vectors of
\widehat{\bm{Z}}_{s}
with the first three eigenvectors of the matrix
\widehat{\bm{Z}}_{s}^{*}\widehat{\bm{Z}}_{s}
denoted as
\{\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\bm{u}_{3}\}
. We define the RGB tuple for each token as:

[r_{j,i},g_{j,i},b_{j,i}]=[\langle\bm{u}_{1},\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}\rangle,\langle\bm{u}_{2},\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}\rangle,\langle\bm{u}_{3},\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}\rangle],\quad i\in[N],\,j\in[J],\bm{z}_{j,i}^{\ell}\in\widehat{\bm{Z}}_{s}.

(B.3)

Next, for each image
\bm{X}_{j}
we compute
\bm{R}_{j},\bm{G}_{j},\bm{B}_{j}
, where
\bm{R}_{j}=[r_{j,1},\dots,r_{j,N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
(similar for
\bm{G}_{j}
and
\bm{B}_{j}
).
Then we reshape the three matrices into
\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{N}
and visualize the “principal components” of image
\bm{X}_{j}
via the RGB image
(\bm{R}_{j},\bm{G}_{j},\bm{B}_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{N}}
.

B.3.2
Visualizing Attention Maps

We recapitulate the method to visualize attention maps in
Abnar & Zuidema (2020)
;
Caron et al. (2021)
.

For the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
head at the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
layer of the encoder of
crate-mae
, we compute the
self-attention map

\bm{A}_{k}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined as follows:

\bm{A}_{k}^{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{k,1}^{\ell}\\
\vdots\\
A_{k,N}^{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\text{where}\quad A_{k,i}^{\ell}=\frac{\exp(\langle\bm{U}_{k}^{\ell*}\bm{z}_{i}^{\ell},\bm{U}_{k}^{\ell*}\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{\ell}\rangle)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\langle\bm{U}_{k}^{\ell*}\bm{z}_{j}^{\ell},\bm{U}_{k}^{\ell*}\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{\ell}\rangle)}.

(B.4)

where
\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{\ell}
is the
\ell^{\mathrm{th}}
layer representation of the class token.

For each image, we reshape the attention matrix
\bm{A}_{k}^{L-1}
for the penultimate layer
L-1
into a
\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{N}
matrix and visualize the heatmaps as shown in
Figure

7(b)
. For example, the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
row and the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
column element of each heatmap in
Figure

7(b)
corresponds to the
m^{\mathrm{th}}
component of
\bm{A}_{k}^{\ell}
, where
m=(i-1)\cdot\sqrt{N}+j
.
In
Figure

7(b)
, for each image we select one attention head
k
of
crate-mae
and visualize the attention matrix
\bm{A}_{k}^{L-1}
.

A_{k,i}^{\ell}=\frac{\exp(\langle\bm{K}_{k}^{\ell}\bm{z}_{i}^{\ell},\bm{Q}_{k}^{\ell}\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{\ell}\rangle)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\langle\bm{K}_{k}^{\ell}\bm{z}_{j}^{\ell},\bm{Q}_{k}^{\ell}\bm{z}_{\mathrm{cls}}^{\ell}\rangle)}.

(B.15)

Overall,
Figure

7
and
Figure

11
demonstrate that, at least empirically, the attention semantics in
crate-mae
-Base are significantly better and clearer than ViT-MAE-Base. The reason that
crate-mae
models have semantically meaningful attention maps may be due to our white-box design, namely the fact that in
crate-mae
we have
\bm{Q}_{[K]}^{\ell}=\bm{K}_{[K]}^{\ell}=\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell}=\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell*}
; indeed, the fact that setting
\bm{Q}_{[K]}^{\ell}=\bm{K}_{[K]}^{\ell}=\bm{V}_{[K]}^{\ell}=\bm{U}_{[K]}^{\ell*}
yields semantically meaningful attention maps has been shown in other work, albeit in a different setting
(
Yu et al., 2023b
)
. The reason that the ViT-MAE has semantically meaningful attention maps, albeit not as clear and worse than
crate-mae
, may be due to several different factors such as using the class token as a register
(
Darcet et al., 2023
)
. Nevertheless, the features in both models have linear structures, or at least each class’ representations have three principal components which correlate strongly with the semantics of the class.
