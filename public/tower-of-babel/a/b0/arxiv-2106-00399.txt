Computing Least and Greatest Fixed Points in Absorptive Semirings
Matthias Naaf

Abstract
We present two methods to algorithmically compute both least and greatest solutions of polynomial equation systems over absorptive semirings (with certain completeness and continuity assumptions), such as the tropical semiring. Both methods require a polynomial number of semiring operations, including semiring addition, multiplication and an infinitary power operation. Our main result is a closed-form solution for least and greatest fixed points based on the fixed-point iteration. The proof builds on the notion of (possibly infinite) derivation trees; a careful analysis of the shape of these trees allows us to collapse the fixed-point iteration to a linear number of steps. The second method is an iterative symbolic computation in the semiring of generalized absorptive polynomials, largely based on results on Kleene algebras.

A recent line of research on semiring provenance analysis for databases

The underlying idea is to replace the Boolean evaluation of formulae by computations in certain semirings.
From this point of view, a formula is essentially a polynomial expression over some semiring, and fixed-point formulae evaluate to least or greatest solutions of polynomial equation systems.
To guarantee the existence and meaningfulness (when interpreted as provenance information) of these fixed points, one assumes that the semiring is equipped with a natural order that is a complete lattice (for the existence) and that the semiring is absorptive, that is,

The textbook approach is the fixed-point iteration: start by setting all indeterminates to the smallest (or greatest) semiring value, then repeatedly evaluate the equations to obtain new values for all indeterminates.

2.1
Polynomial Equation Systems

Throughout the paper, we always fix a finite set
{\bm{X}}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\}
of
\ell
pairwise different indeterminates.
A monomial over
{\bm{X}}
is a product of powers of indeterminates, represented as mapping
m\colon{\bm{X}}\to\mathbb{N}
assigning exponents to the indeterminates.

We use bold symbols to denote tuples:
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})
.
In particular,
\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)
and
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)
.
To simplify the presentation, we often avoid numbered indices.
Given
{\bm{X}}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\}
, we instead index tuples by these indeterminates.
That is, for a tuple
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})
and an indeterminate
X\in{\bm{X}}
, we write
\mathbf{a}_{X}
for the entry
a_{i}
such that
X_{i}=X
.

Definition 1

A
polynomial

P
over a semiring
(K,+,\cdot,0,1)
and indeterminates
{\bm{X}}
is a finite formal sum of the form
P=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\cdot m_{i}
, where the
m_{i}
are pairwise different monomials over
{\bm{X}}
and
c_{i}\in K\setminus\{0\}
are arbitrary coefficients.

Abusing notation, we write
m\in P
if there is an
i
with
m=m_{i}
, and
c\cdot m\in P
if additionally
c=c_{i}
.
We may write
P(X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})
to make the indeterminates explicit.
Then,
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})\in K
is the semiring value obtained by instantiating each indeterminate
X_{i}
by
a_{i}\in K
and evaluating the resulting expression in
K
.

Definition 2

A
polynomial equation system

\mathcal{E}
over a semiring
K
and indeterminates
{\bm{X}}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\}
is a family of equations
\mathcal{E}\colon\big(X_{i}=P_{i}(X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})\big)_{1\leq i\leq\ell}

with polynomials
P_{i}
over
{\bm{X}}
and
K
.

We associate with
\mathcal{E}
the operator
F_{\mathcal{E}}\colon K^{\ell}\to K^{\ell}
defined by
F_{\mathcal{E}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})_{X}=P_{X}(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})
, for
X\in{\bm{X}}
.
The least (greatest) solution to
\mathcal{E}
is thus the least (greatest) fixed point of
F_{\mathcal{E}}
.
We drop the index if
\mathcal{E}
is clear from the context.

Notice that these are quadratic systems, with the number of equations equal to the number of indeterminates.
We recall the example from the introduction in the tropical semiring (where semiring addition is
\min
and semiring multiplication is
+_{\mathbb{R}}
).
Using
{\bm{X}}=\{X_{a},X_{b},X_{c}\}
, we refer to the polynomial equation system as
(X=P_{X})_{X\in{\bm{X}}}
.
For example,
P_{X_{b}}
is the polynomial
\min(1+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{a},20+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{c})
consisting of the two coefficient-monomial pairs
1+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{a}
and
20+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{c}
.

2.2
Semirings

Definition 3

A (commutative)
semiring
is an algebraic structure

(K,+,\cdot,0,1)
, with
0\neq 1
, such that
(K,+,0)

and
(K,\cdot,1)
are commutative monoids,
\cdot

distributes over
+
, and
0\cdot a=a\cdot 0=0
.
It is
idempotent
if
a+a=a
and
absorptive
if
1+a=1
, for all
a\in K
.

In an idempotent semiring
K
, the
natural order

\leq_{K}
is the partial order
with
a\leq_{K}b
if
a+b=b
, for
a,b\in K
.
We drop the index if
K
is clear from the context.

All semirings considered in this paper are commutative and absorptive (except for
{\mathbb{N}}^{\infty}
below).
Absorption (also called
0-closed
or
bounded

[
18
]
)
implies idempotence and is equivalent to
1
being the
\leq_{K}
-maximal element and to multiplication being decreasing, i.e.,
ab\leq_{K}a
for all
a,b\in K
(dually to increasing addition).

To guarantee the existence of fixed points, we further require that the natural order is a complete lattice so that suprema
\bigsqcup
and infima
\bigsqcap
always exist (with respect to
\leq_{K}
).
In addition, we make a continuity assumption stating that the semiring operations commute with the lattice operations on chains (a chain is a totally ordered set).
This is crucial for most of our proofs, but does not seem to be a strong restriction in practice: all natural examples of complete-lattice semirings we are aware of are in fact also fully continuous (a notable exception are binary relations with union and composition, but the latter is not commutative).

Definition 4

An idempotent semiring
K
is
fully continuous
if
\leq_{K}
is a complete lattice and for all
a\in K
, all nonempty chains
C\subseteq K
and
\circ\in\{+,\cdot\}
,

\bigsqcup(a\circ C)=a\circ\bigsqcup C\quad\text{and}\quad\bigsqcap(a\circ C)=a\circ\bigsqcap C.

A homomorphism
h\colon K_{1}\to K_{2}
on fully-continuous semirings is
fully continuous
if
h(\bigsqcup C)=\bigsqcup h(C)
and
h(\bigsqcap C)=\bigsqcap h(C)
, for all nonempty chains
C\subseteq K_{1}
.

Remark 1

We note that requiring suprema and infima of chains in fact suffices to guarantee the existence of fixed points.
However, in idempotent semirings this already implies the existence of arbitrary suprema and infima
[
4
]
and is thus equivalent to our assumption of a complete lattice.
For continuity, the situation is more complicated:
compatibility of
+
and
\cdot
with suprema of chains implies compatibility with arbitrary suprema (see
Appendix

0.A
), but the same does, in general, not hold for infima.

Following the above remark, we see that fully-continuous semirings are similar to quantales.
Indeed, since multiplication is compatible with suprema of arbitrary sets, every absorptive, fully-continuous semiring
K
induces a quantale
(K,\bigsqcup,\cdot)
with the top element
1
as unit.
The main difference to quantales is that we additionally require compatibility of semiring operations with infima (but only of chains).
Another related concept is that of topological dioids in
[
8
]
which requires that both operations are compatible with suprema of countable chains.

Since multiplication is decreasing in absorptive semirings,
powers of an element
a
form a descending chain
1\geq a\geq a^{2}\geq\dots
whose infimum we denote by
a^{\infty}
.

Definition 5

In an absorptive, fully-continuous semiring
K
, the
infinitary power
operation is defined by

a^{\infty}\coloneqq\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a^{n}
, for each
a\in K
,
and
\mathbf{a}^{\infty}\coloneqq(a_{1}^{\infty},\dots,a_{\ell}^{\infty})
for tuples
\mathbf{a}\in K^{\ell}
.

Using continuity of multiplication, one can easily verify the properties
(ab)^{\infty}=a^{\infty}b^{\infty}
,
(a^{n})^{\infty}=a^{\infty}
and
(a+b)^{\infty}=a^{\infty}+b^{\infty}
(see
[
3
]
for details).
We remark that it is usually quite easy to compute the infinitary power.
One can further define infinite sum and product operations on families
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
over
K
with arbitrary index set
I
.
Summation is simply defined as supremum, products can be defined as infimum over finite subproducts (see
[
3
, Appendix]
).
Here we only need infinite products over finite domain
\{a_{i}\mid i\in I\}
(as the polynomials we consider have finitely many coefficients), which are commutative, associative and commute with fully-continuous homomorphisms and the infinitary power.

Fully-continuous homomorphisms further preserve fixed points of monotone functions, in particular least and greatest solutions of polynomial systems:

Lemma 1

Let
h\colon K_{1}\to K_{2}
be a fully-continuous homomorphism on absorptive, fully-continuous semirings.
Let
\mathcal{E}\colon(X_{i}=P_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be a polynomial equation system over
K_{1}
.
Let
h(\mathcal{E})\colon(X_{i}=h(P_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n}
result from
\mathcal{E}
by applying
h
to all coefficients.
Then,
\lfp(F_{h(\mathcal{E})})=h(\lfp(F_{\mathcal{E}}))
and
\gfp(F_{h(\mathcal{E})})=h(\gfp(F_{\mathcal{E}}))
.

Proof

Recall that the semiring operations are fully continuous in
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
.
Hence
F_{\mathcal{E}}
and
F_{h(\mathcal{E})}
are fully continuous as well, and by Kleene’s fixed-point Theorem and the continuity of
h
, we get

h(\lfp(F_{\mathcal{E}}))=h\Big(\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}F_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(\mathbf{0})\Big)=\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}h\big(F_{\mathcal{E}}^{n}(\mathbf{0})\big)\overset{\clap{\scriptsize(*)}}{=}\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big(F_{h(\mathcal{E})}^{n}(\mathbf{0})\big)=\lfp(F_{h(\mathcal{E})}),

where
(*)
is easy to see by induction, since
h
is a homomorphism and the operators are defined by polynomials. The proof for greatest solutions is symmetric.
∎

2.3
Derivation Trees

Inspired by the analysis of Newton’s method
[
6
]
, we use derivation trees to describe the behaviour of polynomial equation systems.
For the intuition behind this notion, think of a polynomial system as a formal grammar:
The indeterminates are the nonterminal symbols, coefficients the terminal symbols, and each monomial in
P_{X}
gives rise to a production rule for
X
.
We essentially consider derivation trees of this grammar in the usual sense, except that we ignore the order of children (we use commutative semirings) and allow infinite derivations.

While our notion of derivation trees is conceptually identical to
[
6
]
, we should note that the definition of the yield labeling is slightly different.

Definition 7

A
derivation tree

T=(V,E,\mathsf{var},\mathsf{yd})
over a semiring
K
and indeterminates
{\bm{X}}
is a (possibly infinite) tree
(V,E)
with node labelings

\mathsf{var}\colon V\to{\bm{X}}

and

\mathsf{yd}\colon V\to K
, the
yield
of
v
.
We say that
T
is
from
X
if for the root
\varepsilon
, we have
\mathsf{var}(\varepsilon)=X
.
For convenience, we often write
v\in T
instead of
v\in V

and refer to
v
with
\mathsf{var}(v)=X
as an occurrence of
X
in
T
.

We associate with each node the monomial
\mathsf{mon}(v)=\prod_{w\in vE}\mathsf{var}(w)
composed of its children’s indeterminates.
We say that
T
is
compatible with the system
(X=P_{X})_{X\in{\bm{X}}}
if for each node,

\mathsf{yd}(v)\cdot\mathsf{mon}(v)\in P_{\mathsf{var}(v)}
.
The set of all derivation trees from
X
that are compatible with the system
\mathcal{E}
is denoted
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
.

Given an equation system
\mathcal{E}\colon(X=P_{X})_{X\in{\bm{X}}}
and an indeterminate

, a derivation tree
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
first chooses from the equation

a monomial
\mathsf{mon}(\varepsilon)
together with its coefficient
\mathsf{yd}(\varepsilon)
.
On the next level, it then makes analogous choices for all indeterminates occurring in
\mathsf{mon}(\varepsilon)
, where the exponent specifies how often an indeterminate occurs.
The leaves

of such a derivation tree (if they exist) have

and correspond to absolute coefficients in one of the equations.
See

We define the yield of an entire tree as the combined yield of all nodes (recall that we assume

to be absorptive and fully-continuous, so infinite products are well-defined):

Definition 8

The

of a derivation tree
T=(V,E,\mathsf{var},\mathsf{yd})
over

is the (possibly infinite) product

\mathsf{yd}(T)=\prod_{v\in V}\mathsf{yd}(v)\in K
.

We compare the yields of two trees by counting occurrences of coefficients or, equivalently, of monomials from the polynomial equation system.

Definition 9

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an equation system over
{\bm{X}}
, let
Y\in{\bm{X}}
and
m\in P_{Y}
.
For derivation trees

, we define

|{T}|_{m,Y}=\big|\{v\in T\mid\text{$\mathsf{mon}(v)=m$, $\mathsf{var}(v)=Y$}\}\big|\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\infty}

as the number of occurrences of
m\in P_{Y}
in
T
.
Notice that we use pairs
(m,Y)
to unambiguously refer to
m\in P_{Y}
, as
m
may also occur in other polynomials of
\mathcal{E}
.

Lemma 4
(yield comparison)

Given a polynomial system
\mathcal{E}\colon(X=P_{X})_{X\in{\bm{X}}}
over an absorptive, fully-continuous semiring and trees
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
,

•

if
|{T}|_{m,Y}\geq|{T^{\prime}}|_{m,Y}
for all
m\in P_{Y}
,
Y\in{\bm{X}}
, then
\mathsf{yd}(T)\leq\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime})
,

•

if
|T|_{m,Y}=0
implies
|T^{\prime}|_{m,Y}=0
for all
m,Y
, then
\mathsf{yd}(T)^{\infty}\leq\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime})^{\infty}
.

Proof

We use the properties of infinite products to group the yields by monomials.
For each
m\in P_{Y}
, let
c_{m,Y}\in K
be its coefficient, such that
c\cdot m\in P_{Y}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathsf{yd}(T)=\prod_{v\in T}\mathsf{yd}(v)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in{\bm{X}},\\
m\in P_{Y}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in T,\\
\mathsf{var}(v)=Y,\\
\mathsf{mon}(v)=m\end{subarray}}\mathsf{yd}(v)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in{\bm{X}},\\
m\in P_{Y}\end{subarray}}(c_{m,Y})^{|{T}|_{m,Y}},

and the same applies to

The product on the right is finite and by absorption, larger exponents lead to smaller values,
hence
\mathsf{yd}(T)\leq\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime})
.
If we apply the infinitary power, we similarly get

\mathsf{yd}(T)^{\infty}=\prod_{m,Y}(c_{m,Y})^{\infty\cdot|{T}|_{m,Y}},

where

3
Derivation Trees and the Fixed-Point Iteration

As a first step towards our main result, this section shows that we can express least and greatest solutions in terms of the yields of derivation trees.
Notice that a single derivation tree does not correspond to a solution of the equation system, but only to (the derivation of) a single term in the solution.
We thus consider the sum over all derivation trees.

For least solutions, this was already shown (for a slightly different notion of derivation trees) in
[
6
]
.
Here we are mostly concerned with the proof for greatest solutions, as this is much more involved due to the trees being infinite.

Theorem 3.1

Let
K
be an absorptive, fully-continuous semiring.
Let

be a polynomial equation system over

We recall that summation is equivalent to supremum (in idempotent semirings).
Here and in the following, we use summation in reminiscence of the general, non-idempotent case (cf.
[
6
]
) and only switch to supremum as needed.
Towards a proof, we first observe that it suffices to prove

The following, mostly technical lemma establishes the general connection between truncations of derivation trees and the fixed-point iteration.

Lemma 5
(tree iteration)

Let
K={\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
and
\mathbf{b}\in K^{\ell}
. Then,

F^{n}(\mathbf{b})_{X}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{b}})
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X\in{\bm{X}}
.

Proof

Induction on
n
.
For
n=0

F^{n}(\mathbf{b})_{X}=\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{0}^{\mathbf{b}})=\mathbf{b}_{X}
for all derivation trees
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
.
For the induction step, assume that
F^{n}(\mathbf{b})_{X}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{b}})
for all
X\in{\bm{X}}
.
We have to show that

\displaystyle F^{n+1}(\mathbf{b})_{X}=P_{X}(F^{n}(\mathbf{b}))\overset{\clap{\scriptsize!}}{=}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n+1}^{\mathbf{b}}).

To simplify notation, let
\mathbf{a}
be the tuple with
\mathbf{a}_{X}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{b}})
.
We can rewrite the left-hand side as follows (recall that
m(X)
denotes the exponent of
X
in

\displaystyle P_{X}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\cdot m\in P_{X}}(c\cdot m)(\mathbf{a})=\sum_{c\cdot m\in P_{X}}\Big(c\cdot\prod_{X\in{\bm{X}}}\mathbf{a}_{X}^{m(X)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\cdot m\in P_{X}}c\cdot\big(\underbrace{\mathbf{a}_{X_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{X_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{a}_{X_{1}}}_{m(X_{1})\text{ times}}\;\cdot\;\dots\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\mathbf{a}_{X_{\ell}}\cdots\mathbf{a}_{X_{\ell}}}_{m(X_{\ell})\text{ times}}\big)=\,\dots

Notice that the unfolded product is finite, since

only has finite exponents.
By
Lemma

2
, multiplication distributes over the (infinite) sums
\mathbf{a}_{X_{1}},\mathbf{a}_{X_{2}},\dots
.
The product
\mathbf{a}_{X_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{X_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{a}_{X_{\ell}}
can thus be rewritten as sum:

\displaystyle=\sum\bigg\{c\cdot\;\prod_{\mathclap{}}^{\mathclap{}}\,{}_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\mathsf{yd}(T_{i,1}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{b}})\cdots\mathsf{yd}(T_{i,m(X_{i})}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{b}})\;\bigg|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\text{$c\cdot m\in P_{X}$ and all possible}\\
\text{choices of trees $T_{i,j}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X_{i})$}\end{array}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum\Big\{\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n+1}^{\mathbf{b}})\;\big|\;\text{$c\cdot m\in P_{X}$, $T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)$ with $\mathsf{mon}(\varepsilon)=m$, $\mathsf{yd}(\varepsilon)=c$ }\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum\Big\{\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n+1}^{\mathbf{b}})\;\big|\;\text{$T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)$}\Big\}.

For these last three steps, recall that derivation trees from
X
first choose a monomial (and corresponding coefficient)

.
The root

then has yield

and the children are derivation trees from the indeterminates occurring in

By commutativity of the infinite product
\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n+1}^{\mathbf{b}})

we can group together the yields of the child subtrees,
thereby obtaining a one-to-one correspondence with the product terms in the first step. ∎

To prove
Theorem

3.1
, all that is left to do is to consider the supremum of the iteration
F^{n}(\mathbf{0})
and the corresponding tree truncations, and dually the infimum of
F^{n}(\mathbf{1})
.
For the infimum, one last obstacle needs to be resolved:
We must show that whenever we pick for each

some
n

n
.
A similar observation has been used for strategy trees of model-checking games in
[
4
]
, where it was called

(due to a more involved construction of the infinite tree).

Lemma 6
(puzzle lemma
[
4
]
)

Let
X\in{\bm{X}}
and
K={\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
.
Let
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of trees
T_{n}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)

such that their yields
\mathsf{yd}(T_{n}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{1}})
form a descending chain.
Then there is a tree
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
with
\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime})\geq\bigsqcap_{n}\mathsf{yd}(T_{n}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{1}})
.

The proof in our setting is quite similar, so we refer to
[
4
]
or
Appendix

0.A
for a complete proof.
Essentially, the finite number of indeterminates in
{\bm{A}}
allows us to choose a sufficiently large

such that the truncation
T_{n}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{1}}
contains a
“nice”
part that we can repeat to obtain the infinite tree

.
Here,
“nice”
means that no matter how often we repeat this part, the yield does not fall below
\bigsqcap_{n}\mathsf{yd}(T_{n}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{1}})
.

With this taken care of, we can prove that the sum of all (finite) derivation trees gives the least and greatest solutions.

Proof
(of
Theorem

3.1
)

Recall that
F
is fully continuous, as it is defined by polynomial expressions over a fully-continuous semiring.
By Kleene’s fixed-point theorem, we can thus express its least (or greatest) fixed point as supremum of
F^{n}(\mathbf{0})
(or infimum of
F^{n}(\mathbf{1})
) over
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By idempotence, sums coincide with suprema, so for the least solution we immediately obtain:

\displaystyle\lfp(F)_{X}=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}F^{n}(\mathbf{0})_{X}

\displaystyle\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize(\ref{lemTreeIteration})}}{=}\;\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\sum\,_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{0}})\Big)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\Big(\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{0}})\Big).

Now observe that
\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{0}})=\mathsf{yd}(T)
if
T
has height
<n
,
otherwise
\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{0}})=0
.
Hence
\bigsqcup_{n}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{0}})=\mathsf{yd}(T)
if
T
is finite and
0
otherwise.

It remains to consider the greatest solution.
We apply
Lemma

3
to express the infimum in
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
as a supremum:

\displaystyle\gfp(F)_{X}

\displaystyle=\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}F^{n}(\mathbf{1})_{X}\,\overset{\clap{\scriptsize(\ref{lemTreeIteration})}}{=}\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\sum\,_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{1}})\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\clap{\scriptsize(\ref{lemSinfInfima})}}{=}\;\bigsqcup\Big\{\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}y_{n}\;\Big|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\text{\small$(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a descending chain of monomials}\\
\text{\small with $y_{n}=\mathsf{yd}(T_{n}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{n}^{\mathbf{1}})$ for some $T_{n}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)$}\end{array}\Big\}

\displaystyle\overset{\clap{\scriptsize(\ref{lemTreesPuzzle})}}{=}\;\bigsqcup\Big\{\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime})\;\big|\;T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)\Big\}=\sum\,_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T).

In the last line, we apply the puzzle lemma.
This gives us for each monomial chain

with
\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime})\geq\bigsqcap_{n}y_{n}

Conversely, each tree

induces the monomial chain defined by

It is easy to see that this chain has infimum

T
.

Let
W
be the set of earliest occurrences of
{\bm{X}}_{\infty}
in
T
(cf.
Fig.

2a
). Formally,

W=\{v\in T\mid\mathsf{var}(v)\in{\bm{X}}_{\infty}
, there is no
v^{\prime}\sqsubset v
with
\mathsf{var}(v^{\prime})\in{\bm{X}}_{\infty}\}
.
Now let
S
be the tree that results from
T
by replacing the subtree at each
v\in W
with the tree
T^{\infty}_{\mathsf{var}(v)}
(cf.
Fig.

2b
).
The tree
S
is almost of the desired shape, but the trees
T^{\infty}_{X}
may be rooted at depth
>\ell
.
To fix this, we consider the prefix up to the subtrees
T^{\infty}_{X}
and eliminate all repetitions of indeterminates within the prefix.
As all indeterminates in the prefix occur only finitely often, we can eliminate repetitions by replacing each first occurrence of an indeterminate
Z
by a last occurrence of
Z
within the prefix (cf.
Fig.

2c
).

More formally, call a path
v_{0}v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{k}
from the root of
S

unresolved
if

k\geq\ell
and no node on the path is contained in one of the deterministic subtrees
T_{X}^{\infty}
.
Given an unresolved path, there must be an indeterminate
Z
that occurs twice on the path.
Further,
Z\notin{\bm{X}}_{\infty}
, as otherwise the nodes labeled
Z
would lie within one of the deterministic subtrees by construction of
S
and
W
.
Let
i<j
be indices such that
v_{i}
is the first and
v_{j}
the last occurrence of
Z
on the path, so
\mathsf{var}(v_{i})=\mathsf{var}(v_{j})=Z
.
Now let
S^{\prime}
result from
S
by replacing the subtree
S_{v_{i}}
rooted at
v_{i}
with the subtree
S_{v_{j}}
rooted at
v_{j}
, thereby removing at least one occurrence of
Z
from the tree (
Fig.

2c
).

Apply this elimination step exhaustively, until there are no more unresolved paths.
As all indeterminates in the prefix of
S
occur only finitely often, this process terminates.
Let
T^{\prime}
be the resulting tree (notice that
T^{\prime}
is not uniquely determined, but this does not affect our argument).
Then
T^{\prime}
has the desired shape: when no unresolved path exists, then all nodes at depth
\ell
(if any) must be contained in one of deterministic subtrees
T_{X}^{\infty}
.

Moreover, the elimination step only removes nodes of
S
, but neither adds nodes nor modifies any node labels.
It follows that
|{T^{\prime}}|_{m,Y}\leq|{S}|_{m,Y}
for all
m\in P_{Y},Y\in{\bm{X}}
.
As the trees
T_{X}^{\infty}
only use monomials that occur infinitely often in
T
, we further have
|{S}|_{m,Y}\leq|{T}|_{m,Y}
for all
m,Y
, closing the proof.∎

4.3
Proof of the Main Result

We relate infinite trees of this shape to the expression
F^{\ell}(\,F^{\ell}(\mathbf{1})^{\infty}\,)
.
The deterministic trees rooted at depth
\ell
correspond to the inner term
F^{\ell}(\mathbf{1})^{\infty}
, relying on
Lemma

8
to ensure that
\ell
applications of
F
suffice.
The outer applications of
F
correspond to the prefix on which we impose no further restrictions (except that it has height at most
\ell
).
The following lemma formalizes this intuition.

Lemma 10

Let
\mathbf{b}
be the tuple with
\mathbf{b}_{X}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)}\mathsf{yd}(T\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}})^{\infty}
for
X\in{\bm{X}}
.
For each
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
, there is a tree

such that
\mathsf{yd}(T)\leq\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{b}})
.

Proof

Let
T\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{E},X)
.
Using
Lemma

9
, we obtain a tree
T^{\prime}
of a certain shape:
Let
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
be the subtrees of
T^{\prime}
rooted at depth
\ell
.
These subtrees are deterministic and all monomials occurring in

occur infinitely often in

and moreover,
|{T^{\prime}}|_{m,Y}\leq|{T}|_{m,Y}
for all
m\in P_{Y},Y\in{\bm{X}}
.
We claim that

\mathsf{yd}(T)\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize!}}{\leq}\;\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{yd}(S_{i})^{\infty}

To see this, we expand the definition of
\mathsf{yd}
and rearrange terms.
Borrowing the notation

for the coefficient of

from the proof of

4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in{\bm{X}},\\
m\in P_{Y}\end{subarray}}(c_{m,Y})^{|{T}|_{m,Y}}\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize!}}{\leq}\;\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in{\bm{X}},\\
m\in P_{Y}\end{subarray}}(c_{m,Y})^{|{T^{\prime}\|_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}}}|_{m,Y}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\infty\cdot|{S_{i}}|_{m,Y}},

To prove the inequality, it suffices to show that
|{T}|_{m,Y}\geq|{T^{\prime}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}}}|_{m,Y}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\infty\cdot|{S_{i}}|_{m,Y}
for all

.
This holds by construction of

:
If

for some

, then
m
occurs infinitely often in

and hence

.
Otherwise, the right-hand side is equal to

.
This proves our claim.

Now let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in T^{\prime}
be the root nodes of the deterministic subtrees
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
.
By

8
,

\mathsf{yd}(S_{i})^{\infty}=\mathsf{yd}(S_{i}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}})^{\infty}\leq\mathbf{b}_{\mathsf{var}(v_{i})}
, and thus

\mathsf{yd}(T)\leq\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{yd}(S_{i})^{\infty}\leq\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{b}_{\mathsf{var}(v_{i})}=\mathsf{yd}(T^{\prime}\scalebox{1}[1.3]{$\|$}\vphantom{|}_{\ell}^{\mathbf{b}}).

We are now ready to prove our main result.
The statement on the least solution follows rather directly from our earlier considerations.
For greatest fixed points, the previous lemma already proves the difficult direction.

(of

)

Remark 2

One can generalize our main result to polynomial equation systems that allow
\infty
as exponent (similar to absorptive polynomials).
We have chosen to spare the reader from the additional complications that arise from the corresponding derivation trees with infinite degree, in particular infinite products on the semiring level, as these are not relevant for the main ideas.
Alternatively, the symbolic approach of
Section

5
can be used in this setting.

5
Symbolic Computation

This section complements our main results by a second approach focused specifically on polynomial equation systems over the semiring
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
.
To this end, we adapt results of Hopkins and Kozen on Kleene algebras
[
15
]
since we can view absorptive semirings as a special case of Kleene algebras (by setting
a^{*}=1
for all elements
a
).
These results are based on symbolic derivatives of polynomials (which is also the basis for Newton’s method
[
6
]
) to express least solutions.
Here, we generalize this approach to include the infinitary power operation, eventually leading to a similar statement also for greatest solutions.

Remark 3

Extensions of Kleene algebra by an operation similar to infinitary power have already been studied in other contexts.
For instance, Cohen
[
2
]
introduces
\omega
-algebras by axiomatizing a unary operation
a^{\omega}
, which can be interpreted as infinite repetition in
\omega
-words or infinite iteration in the relational model of program analysis.
However, Cohen’s axioms seem too weak for the proofs below; we discuss an alternative axiomatization of
a^{\infty}
in
Appendix

0.B
.

5.1
Setting and Derivatives

It is convenient to slightly reformulate our problem setting:
Instead of a system
\mathcal{E}\colon(X=P_{X})_{X\in{\bm{X}}}
with polynomials
P_{X}
over
{\bm{X}}
and coefficients
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
, we now regard
P_{X}
as an absorptive polynomial
P_{X}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}\cup{\bm{X}}]
(so we no longer distinguish between indeterminates of the polynomial system and indeterminates occurring in coefficients).
This allows a more uniform treatment when we eliminate indeterminates one by one, and it is easy to see that it does not affect the solutions.

To simplify notation, we write
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}},X]
for
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}\cup\{X\}]
.
Recall that we write
P(X)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}},X]
to make explicit that
X
may occur in
P
;
then
P(a)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
denotes the polynomial that results from
P(X)
by replacing
X
with
a\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
.
In the following, we use
{\bm{X}}
to denote an arbitrary (finite) indeterminate set.

Definition 12

Let
X\in{\bm{X}}
and
P(X)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
.
We denote the
partial derivative
of
P
with respect to
X
as
P^{\prime}
(leaving
X
implicit) and define it inductively by

\displaystyle X^{\prime}=1,\quad Y^{\prime}=0\text{ for $X\neq Y\in{\bm{X}}$},

\displaystyle(PQ)^{\prime}=P^{\prime}\cdot Q+P\cdot Q^{\prime},\quad(P+Q)^{\prime}=P^{\prime}+Q^{\prime},\quad\big({P^{\infty}}\big)^{\prime}=P^{\infty}\cdot P^{\prime},

where
P(X),Q(X)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
.

5.2
Solutions in One Dimension

We first show how least and greatest solutions of a single equation
X=P(X)
can be expressed using derivatives, following the proof in
[
15
]
.
Notice that
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
, and in fact any absorptive semiring, can be regarded as a Kleene algebra in the sense of
[
15
]
by setting
a^{*}=1
for all
a\in K
.
Hence, most of the lemmas require no modifications, except for our addition of the infinitary power.

Lemma 11

Let
P(X),Q(X)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
and further
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
. Then,

1.

P(Q)^{\prime}=P^{\prime}(Q)\cdot Q^{\prime}
, (chain rule, cf.
[
15
]
)

2.

P(a+b)=P(a)+P^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b
(Taylor’s theorem, cf.
[
15
]
),

3.

ac\leq bc\;\implies\;P(a)c\leq P(b)c
(cf.
[
15
]
).

Proof

By structural induction on
P
.
The proof of
[
15
]
also applies to
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
, so we only have to consider the case
P=H^{\infty}
.
For the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\big(H^{\infty}(Q)\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\big(H(Q)^{\infty}\big)^{\prime}=H(Q)^{\infty}\cdot H(Q)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=H(Q)^{\infty}\cdot H^{\prime}(Q)\cdot H^{\prime}=\big(H(Q)^{\infty}\big)^{\prime}\cdot Q^{\prime}=\big(H^{\infty}(Q)\big)^{\prime}\cdot Q^{\prime},

and for Taylor’s theorem,

\displaystyle H^{\infty}(a+b)

\displaystyle=H(a+b)^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\big(H(a)+H^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b\big)^{\infty}

\displaystyle\overset{\clap{\scriptsize$(*)$}}{=}H(a)^{\infty}+\big(H(a)+H^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b\big)^{\infty}\cdot H^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b

\displaystyle=H(a)^{\infty}+H(a+b)^{\infty}\cdot H^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b

\displaystyle=H^{\infty}(a)+H^{\infty}(a+b)\cdot H^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b

\displaystyle=H^{\infty}(a)+(H^{\infty})^{\prime}(a+b)\cdot b.

In
(*)
, we use the fact that
(a+b)^{\infty}=a^{\infty}+(a+b)^{\infty}\cdot b
for elements
a,b
of any absorptive, fully-continuous semiring.
For (3), we apply full continuity:

\displaystyle H^{\infty}(a)c=\Big(\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}H^{n}(a)\Big)\cdot c=\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big(H^{n}(a)c\big)\leq\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big(H^{n}(b)c\big)=H^{\infty}(b)c.

∎

For the result about the greatest solution, we need an additional observation about the infinitary power operation:

Lemma 12

Let
K
be an absorptive, fully-continuous semiring.
Then all elements
a,b,c\in K
satisfy:

c+ab\geq b\quad\implies\quad c+a^{\infty}b\geq b.

Proof

We first show by induction that
c+a^{n}b\geq b
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
n=0
, trivially
c+a^{0}b=c+b\geq b
, and for
n=1
this holds by assumption.
The induction step follows from absorption:

c+a^{n+1}b\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize abs}}{=}\;c+ac+a^{n+1}b=c+a(c+a^{n}b)\geq c+ab\geq b.

The claim then follows by full continuity:

c+a^{\infty}b=c+\Big(\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a^{n}\Big)b=\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(c+a^{n}b)\geq\bigsqcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b=b.

Using the observations in
Lemma

11
, Hopkins and Kozen prove that the least solution is
P^{\prime}(P(0))^{*}\cdot P(0)
, which in our setting is equal to
P(0)
and can in fact be derived directly from absorption, without derivatives.
However, using derivatives allows us to also express greatest solutions:

Theorem 5.1

Let
P(X)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}},X]
.
Then
X=P(X)
has the least solution
P(0)
and
the greatest solution
P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
.

Proof

See
[
15
]
for a proof of the least solution.
Alternatively, let
a_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
be the absolute coefficient of
P
(i.e., the sum of all monomials not containing
X
) so that
P(0)=a_{0}
.
Since we have
m(a_{0})\leq a_{0}
for every monomial
m
containing
X
, it follows that
P(P(0))=P(a_{0})=a_{0}
by absorption, so
P(0)
is the least solution.

For the greatest solution, we first prove that
P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}
is a solution to the inequality
X\leq P(X)
:

\displaystyle P\big(P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}\big)\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize$(\ref{lemChainTaylor}.2)$}}{=}\;{}

\displaystyle P(0)+P^{\prime}\big(P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}\big)\cdot\big(P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle\geq{}\;

\displaystyle P(0)+P^{\prime}(P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty})\cdot P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}

\displaystyle\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize$(\ref{lemChainTaylor}.3)$}}{\geq}\;{}

\displaystyle P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)\cdot P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}=P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}.

We next show that this is the greatest solution to
X\leq P(X)
.
To this end, let
a\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
be a solution, i.e.,
a\leq P(a)
.
As
1\geq a
, we get

P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)\cdot a\geq P(0)+P^{\prime}(a)\cdot a\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize(\ref{lemChainTaylor}.2)}}{=}\;P(a)\geq a.

Using
Lemma

12
, we can conclude

P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}\;\overset{\clap{\scriptsize abs}}{\geq}\;P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}\cdot a\geq a.

Finally, note that the greatest solution to
X\leq P(X)
is also the greatest solution to
X=P(X)
by the well-known Knaster-Tarski theorem. ∎

5.3
Solutions of Larger Systems

To solve systems with more than one equation, we eliminate indeterminates one by one, in each step applying
Theorem

5.1
.
The main theoretical underpinning is the uniformity of the solutions in one indeterminate.
In
[
15
]
, this uniformity follows from the axiomatic proofs and the fact that instantiations preserve the axioms of Kleene algebra.
Here, we instead appeal to the universal property of
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
and the fact that fully-continuous semiring homomorphisms preserve least and greatest fixed points.

For the sake of simplicity, we only consider systems of two equations; we can inductively apply the same approach to larger systems.
Moreover, we only state the result for greatest solutions, as least solutions are symmetric.
We use the notation
\gfp(X\mapsto P(X,Y))
to refer to the greatest solution of the equation
X=P(X,Y)
using
Theorem

5.1
(where we treat the additional indeterminate
Y
as a coefficient, i.e., we apply the theorem with
Y\in{\bm{A}}
).
With this notation, we can formulate the solution of a system in two indeterminates as follows:

Theorem 5.2

Consider the equation system
\mathcal{E}\colon X=P(X,Y),Y=Q(X,Y)
with
P,Q\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}},X,Y]
.
Let further

\displaystyle H(Y)

\displaystyle=\gfp(X\mapsto P(X,Y))

\displaystyle\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}},Y],

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\gfp(Y\mapsto Q(H(Y),Y))

\displaystyle\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}].

Then
(H(b),b)
is the greatest solution of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof

It is easy to see that
(H(b),b)
is a solution:
By definition of
b
, we have
Q(H(b),b)=b
.
By definition of
H
, we further have
P(H(Y),Y)=H(Y)
, and by applying the instantiation
Y\mapsto b
we get
P(H(b),b)=H(b)
.

To prove that
(H(b),b)
is the greatest solution, we make use of the universal property.
Let
(c,d)
be any solution with
c,d\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
.
We claim that
H(d)=\gfp(X\mapsto P(X,d))
.
To see this, consider the definition of
H(Y)
and apply the instantiation
Y\mapsto d
.
By the universal property, this instantiation is fully continuous and thus preserves greatest fixed points.

Now, since
(c,d)
is a solution, we have
P(c,d)=c
, and since
H(d)
is the greatest solution to
X=P(X,d)
, we must have
H(d)\geq c
.
Then also
Q(H(d),d)\geq Q(c,d)=d
.
Since
b
is the greatest solution to
Q(H(Y),Y)=Y
and hence also to
Q(H(Y),Y)\geq Y
, we have
b\geq d
.
Finally,
H(b)\geq H(d)\geq c
, so
(H(b),b)
is indeed the greatest solution. ∎

We can apply this second technique to semirings other than
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
by first performing a symbolic abstraction.
That is, we replace all coefficients by pairwise different indeterminates from
{\bm{A}}
, then compute the solution and apply the reverse instantiation (which preserves solutions).

Example 3

Recall our example in the tropical semiring
\mathbb{T}
.
By replacing coefficients with indeterminates
a,b,c
, we obtain the equation system on the right.

\displaystyle X_{a}

\displaystyle=1+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{a}

\displaystyle X_{a}

\displaystyle=a\cdot X_{a}

\displaystyle X_{b}

\displaystyle=\min(1+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{a},20+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{c})\mathrlap{\qquad\rightsquigarrow}

\displaystyle X_{b}

\displaystyle=a\cdot X_{a}+b\cdot X_{c}

\displaystyle X_{c}

\displaystyle=0+_{\mathbb{R}}X_{c}

\displaystyle X_{c}

\displaystyle=c\cdot X_{c}

We solve the system over
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[X_{a},X_{b},X_{c},a,b,c]
by the symbolic approach:

•

\gfp(X_{a}\mapsto a\cdot X_{a})=0+a^{\infty}=a^{\infty}
(by
Theorem

5.1
),

•

\gfp(X_{b}\mapsto a\cdot a^{\infty}+b\cdot X_{c})=a^{\infty}+b\cdot X_{c}
(we first instantiate
X_{a}
by
a^{\infty}
)

•

\gfp(X_{c}\mapsto c\cdot X_{c})=c^{\infty}

The greatest solution is thus
X_{a}=a^{\infty}
,
X_{b}=a^{\infty}+bc^{\infty}
,
X_{c}=c^{\infty}
.
Applying the reverse substitution, we get the expected solution
(\infty,20,0)
in
\mathbb{T}
.

\lrcorner

Usually, the closed-form solution in
Theorem

4.1
is preferable, as we can work directly in the target semiring.
The symbolic technique is best suited to compute solutions in
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{A}}]
, which is of interest for semiring provenance analysis.
Compared to the closed-form solution, we need slightly fewer computation steps and can often avoid an intermediate blowup in the size of the polynomials.

Example 4

Recall the equation system
X_{1}=b\cdot X_{2}
,
X_{2}=(b+c)\cdot X_{2}X_{3}
,
X_{3}=a\cdot X_{1}
of

. Eliminating indeterminates one by one, we get

•

\gfp(X_{1}\mapsto b\cdot X_{2})=b\cdot X_{2}

•

\gfp(X_{2}\mapsto(b+c)\cdot X_{2}X_{3})=\big((b+c)X_{3}\big)^{\infty}=b^{\infty}X_{3}^{\infty}+c^{\infty}X_{3}^{\infty}

•

\gfp(X_{3}\mapsto ab\cdot(b^{\infty}X_{3}^{\infty}+c^{\infty}X_{3}^{\infty}))=a^{\infty}b^{\infty}+a^{\infty}b^{\infty}c^{\infty}=a^{\infty}b^{\infty}

and by substituting the results backwards, we obtain
X_{1}=X_{2}=X_{3}=a^{\infty}b^{\infty}
.

Remark 4

An attentive reader may have noticed that we have phrased all results in this section for the semiring
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
of absorptive polynomials, whereas the results in
[
15
]
apply to polynomials
K[{\bm{X}}]
over an arbitrary Kleene algebra
K
.
The reason for this restrictive choice is that the iterative lifting to multivariate equation systems in
Theorem

5.2
requires some form of compositionality:
in
[
15
]
,
K[X,Y]
is viewed as
K[X][Y]
.
Unfortunately, the usual notion of polynomials
K[X]
over an absorptive semiring
K
, as in
Definition

1
, is itself not absorptive, and it seems not obvious how one would define an absorptive version of
K[X]
(for instance, if
a,b\in K
are incomparable we want
(aX+aX^{2})+bX^{2}=aX+bX^{2}
by absorption, but also
aX+(aX^{2}+bX^{2})=aX+(a+b)X^{2}
, violating associativity).
As our proofs rely on absorption, they do not apply to
K[X][Y]
.

Since our main result already provides a direct computation in any absorptive, fully-continuous semiring, we have here restricted our interest to
{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
.
In fact, it was this limitation that motivated our search for a closed form solution.

Remark 5

For least solutions, the one-dimensional solution in
Theorem

5.1
in fact implies the solution in
Theorem

4.1
, as shown in
[
7
, Prop. 28]
.
It seems an interesting question if a similar connection holds for greatest solutions, i.e., can the solution
F^{\ell}(F^{\ell}(\mathbf{1})^{\infty})
for
\ell
equations be derived from the solution
P(0)+P^{\prime}(1)^{\infty}
of a single equation by algebraic methods, without derivation trees?

6
Conclusion

We have presented two methods to compute least and, most importantly, greatest solutions of polynomial equation systems over absorptive, fully-continuous semirings.
Both methods require only polynomially many applications of the semiring operations and the infinitary power, in terms of the number of equations.

While we assume full continuity mostly to guarantee the existence of both kinds of solutions, absorption is a strong assumption that leads to a particularly simple way of computing solutions.
Our motivation to consider absorptive semirings comes from semiring provenance of fixed-point logics, where our methods can directly be applied to compute provenance information, for example of Büchi games or formulae in fixed-point logics such as
L_{\mu}
or LFP.

The first method, and our main result, is a closed-form solution that works in any absorptive, fully-continuous semiring and is as easy as computing the standard fixed-point iteration with an added application of the infinitary power.
To prove the correctness for greatest solutions, we extended the notion of derivation trees used in the analysis of Newton’s method
[
6
]
to infinite trees.
Derivation trees provide an intuitive tool to understand the fixed-point iteration, but require somewhat involved arguments and constructions to properly handle infinite trees.
Our main technical contribution is that it suffices to consider trees of a particular shape resembling the solution term
F^{\ell}(F^{\ell}(\mathbf{1})^{\infty})
, intuitively corresponding to a reachability prefix with infinitely repeating deterministic subtrees.
For the second method, we applied results on least solutions over Kleene algebras
[
15
]
specifically to the semiring of generalized absorptive polynomials, and extended these results by similar observations for greatest solutions.

Comparing the two proofs, we see that the symbolic approach has a simpler algebraic proof, raising the question whether we can avoid the constructions of infinite trees in our main proof in favor of algebraic arguments.
A further direction for future work is to study systems of nested fixed points over absorptive semirings.
Recently, quasipolynomial-time algorithms have been developed to solve such systems in the Boolean case
[
1
]
or over finite lattices
[
14
]
.
With the simple computation based on the fixed-point iteration, absorptive semirings might be a candidate to further increase the applicability of these algorithms.

In
Remark

1
, we mentioned the observation in

that the existence of suprema of chains implies the existence of arbitrary surpema (and thus also arbitrary infima so that
\leq_{K}
is a complete lattice).
This follows from arguments of Markowsky
[

on chain-complete posets:
first observe that due to idempotence, the supremum of finitely many elements is simply their sum; suprema of finite sets and of chains then suffice to guarantee suprema of arbitrary sets, as shown in
[

Following Markowsky’s arguments, one can further show the following relation between suprema of chains and sets:

Lemma 0.A0

Let
K_{1},K_{2}
be fully-continuous semirings.
If
f\colon K_{1}\to K_{2}
preserves suprema of finite sets and of nonempty chains, then
f
also preserves suprema of arbitrary sets.

This applies in particular to addition and multiplication with a fixed element, as both preserve addition and hence suprema of finite sets.
It follows that in every fully-continuous semiring
K
, it holds that
a\cdot\bigsqcup S=\bigsqcup aS
for every
a\in K
and every set
S\subseteq K
.
In other words,
(K,\bigsqcup,\cdot)
is a quantale.

Lemma
2

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
and
P\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\infty}[{\bm{X}}]
. Then,

1.

P\cdot\sum S=\sum\{P\cdot Q\mid Q\in S\}
, and

2.

(\sum S)^{\infty}=\sum\{Q^{\infty}\mid Q\in S\}
, and

3.

h(\sum S)=\sum h(S)
, if

Proof

We recall that summation is the same as supremum in idempotent semirings.
Statement (2) was shown in

3

and the other statements follow by the same argument: since

For (1), we then have

P\cdot\sum S=Pm_{1}+\dots+Pm_{k}\leq PQ_{1}+\dots+PQ_{k}\leq\sum\{PQ\mid Q\in S\}

and for (3),

h(\sum S)=h(m_{1}+\dots+m_{k})=h(m_{1})+\dots+h(m_{k})\leq h(Q_{1})+\dots+h(Q_{k})\leq\sum h(S)

The other direction follows by monotonicity in both cases, since

Appendix 0.B
On an Axiomatization of the Infinitary Power

This appendix discusses an axiomatization of the infinitary power operation
a^{\infty}
in absorptive semirings, similar to Cohen’s
\omega
-algebras
[
2
]
.
To justify the new axioms, we derive the common properties of the infinitary power and provide a completeness result.

Definition 13

An
∞
-algebra
is an algebraic structure
(K,+,\cdot,{}^{\infty},0,1)
such that
(K,+,\cdot,0,1)
is an absorptive semiring and the following axioms hold:

\displaystyle a^{\infty}

\displaystyle=aa^{\infty}

(
\infty
abs)

\displaystyle b\leq c+ab\;

\displaystyle\to\;b\leq c+a^{\infty}b

(
\infty
ind)

where
\leq
denotes the natural order.

We remark that every
∞
-algebra is in particular a Kleene algebra (see e.g.
[
16
]
) by setting
a^{*}=1
for all
a\in K
.
For a complete axiomatization, we can thus use axioms (3) – (13) of
[
16
]
together with commutativity
ab=ba
, absorption
1+a=1
and the
∞
-axioms.
Moreover, every
∞
-algebra is also an
\omega
-algebra by setting
a^{\omega}=a^{\infty}
,
since (
\infty
ind) and absorption imply Cohen’s axiom

b\leq c+ab\;\to\;b\leq a^{*}c+a^{\infty}.

Clearly, every absorptive, fully-continuous semiring is an
∞
-algebra (by defining
a^{\infty}
as in
Definition

5
), since (
\infty
ind) holds by
Lemma

12
and (
\infty
abs) follows from continuity:
aa^{\infty}=a\cdot\bigsqcap_{n}a^{n}=\bigsqcap_{n}a^{n+1}=a^{\infty}
.
Conversely, we show how to derive the main properties of the infinitary power in such semirings from the axioms in
Definition

13
.
The first lemma provides an alternative proof of
Lemma

11
(3) and implies that
∞
is monotone w.r.t.
\leq
(using
c=1
).

Lemma 0.B0

Every
∞
-algebra satisfies the implication

ac\leq bc\;\to\;a^{\infty}c\leq b^{\infty}c.

Proof

Using (
\infty
abs) and the assumption, we get
a^{\infty}c=a^{\infty}ac\leq a^{\infty}bc
.
By absorption, we thus have
a^{\infty}c\leq b(a^{\infty}c)\leq b(a^{\infty}c)+b^{\infty}c
.
Applying (
\infty
ind) and absorption then gives
a^{\infty}c\leq b^{\infty}(a^{\infty}c)+b^{\infty}c=b^{\infty}c
as claimed. ∎

Lemma 0.B0

In every
∞
-algebra,
∞
has the following properties:

1.

a^{n}\geq a^{\infty}
and
a^{n}a^{\infty}=a^{\infty}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

2.

a^{\infty}a^{\infty}=a^{\infty}
,

3.

(a^{\infty})^{\infty}=a^{\infty}
,

4.

(ab)^{\infty}=a^{\infty}b^{\infty}
,

5.

(a+b)^{\infty}=a^{\infty}+b^{\infty}
.

Proof

1.

Follows inductively from (
\infty
abs) and absorption.

2.

Trivially
a^{\infty}a^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}
by absorption.
For the other direction, we have
a^{\infty}\leq aa^{\infty}
by (
\infty
abs) and thus
a^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}a^{\infty}
by (
\infty
ind).

3.

By (
\infty
abs) and absorption,
(a^{\infty})^{\infty}=a^{\infty}\cdot(a^{\infty})^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}
.
Conversely,
a^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}a^{\infty}
by (2) and thus
a^{\infty}\leq(a^{\infty})^{\infty}a^{\infty}\leq(a^{\infty})^{\infty}
by (
\infty
ind) and absorption.

4.

We have
a^{\infty}b^{\infty}\leq(ab)a^{\infty}b^{\infty}
by (
\infty
abs) and thus
a^{\infty}b^{\infty}\leq(ab)^{\infty}a^{\infty}b^{\infty}\leq(ab)^{\infty}
by (
\infty

For the other direction, we observe that
(ab)^{\infty}=ab(ab)^{\infty}\leq a(ab)^{\infty}
by (
\infty
abs) and absorption.
Hence
(ab)^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}(ab)^{\infty}
by (
\infty

(ab)^{\infty}\leq b^{\infty}(ab)^{\infty}

Combining both, we get
(ab)^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}b^{\infty}(ab)^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}b^{\infty}

5.

By monotonicity,
a+b\geq a,b
implies
(a+b)^{\infty}\geq a^{\infty},b^{\infty}

(a+b)^{\infty}\geq a^{\infty}+b^{\infty}

Conversely, we have
(a+b)^{\infty}=a(a+b)^{\infty}+b(a+b)^{\infty}

\infty

Thus
(a+b)^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}(a+b)^{\infty}+b^{\infty}(a+b)^{\infty}\leq a^{\infty}+b^{\infty}
by applying (
\infty

The above lemmas provide a sanity check for our axiomatization.
It is further easy to see that we cannot omit (
\infty
abs) or (
\infty

or
a^{\infty}=0

we want to axiomatize.
To further justify our axioms, we observe the following straightforward completeness result.

Proposition 0.B0

be two terms constructed from variables, the constants
0
and
1

,
\cdot
and
∞
.

\alpha=\beta

One implication is trivial, since every absorptive, fully-continuous semiring is an

For the converse, assume that

We show that we can derive

Using

We can thus derive equalities of the form

Let
