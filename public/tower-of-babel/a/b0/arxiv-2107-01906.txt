THE LAST-ITERATE CONVERGENCE RATE OF OPTIMISTIC MIRROR DESCENT IN STOCHASTIC VARIATIONAL INEQUALITIES
Waïss Azizian ∗, Franck Iutzeler †, Jérôme Malick ‡, Panayotis Mertikopoulos ♯

Abstract
. In this paper, we analyze the local convergence rate of OMD methods in stochastic VI , a class of optimization problems with important applications to learning theory and machine learning. Our analysis reveals an intricate relation between the algorithm’s rate of convergence and the local geometry induced by the method’s underlying Bregman function. We quantify this relation by means of the Legendre exponent , a notion that we introduce to measure the growth rate of the Bregman divergence relative to the ambient norm near a solution. We show that this exponent determines both the optimal step-size policy of the algorithm and the optimal rates attained, explaining in this way the differences observed for some popular Bregman functions (Euclidean projection, negative entropy, fractional power, etc.).

1.
Introduction

Variational inequalities – and, in particular,
stochastic

VI
– have recently attracted considerable attention in machine learning and learning theory as a flexible paradigm for “optimization beyond minimization” – i.e., for problems where finding an optimal solution does not necessarily involve minimizing a loss function.
In this context, our paper examines the local rate of convergence of
OMD
(
OMD
), a state-of-the-art algorithmic template for solving
VI
that incorporates an “optimistic” look-ahead step with a “mirror descent” apparatus relying on a suitably chosen Bregman kernel.
Our contributions focus exclusively on the stochastic case, which is of central interest to learning theory.
To put them in context, we begin with a general overview below and discuss more specialized references in
Section

5
.

General overview.

Algorithms for solving
VI
have a very rich history in optimization that goes back at least to the original proximal point algorithm of
Martinet 1970
and
Rockafellar 1976
;
for a survey, see
Facchinei & Pang 2003
.
At a high level, if the vector field defining the problem is bounded and strictly monotone, simple forward-backward schemes are known to converge – and if combined with a Polyak–Ruppert averaging scheme, they achieve an
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t})
rate of convergence without the strictness caveat
(
Bruck Jr. 1977
,
Passty 1979
,
Nesterov 2009
,
Bauschke & Combettes 2017
)
.
If, in addition, the problem’s defining vector field is Lipschitz continuous, the
EG
(
EG
) algorithm of
Korpelevich 1976
achieves trajectory convergence without strict monotonicity requirements, while the method’s ergodic average converges at a
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
rate
(
Nemirovski 2004
,
Nesterov 2007
)
.
Finally, if the problem is
strongly
monotone, forward-backward methods
achieve an
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
convergence speed;
and if the operator is also Lipschitz continuous, classical results in operator theory show that simple forward-backward methods suffice to achieve a linear convergence rate
(
Facchinei & Pang 2003
,
Bauschke & Combettes 2017
)
.

The stochastic version of the problem arises in two closely related and interconnected ways:
the defining operator could have itself a stochastic structure, or the optimizer could only be able to access a stochastic estimate thereof.
Here, the landscape is considerably different:
For
VI
problems with a monotone operator, the stochastic version of the
EG
algorithm achieves an ergodic
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t})
convergence rate
(
Juditsky et al. 2011
,
Gidel et al. 2019
)
.
On the other hand, if the problem is
strongly
monotone, this rate improves to
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
and it is achieved by simple forward-backward methods, with or without averaging
(
Nemirovski et al. 2009
)
.

In this context, the
OMD
algorithm has been designed to meet two complementing objectives:

(1)

improve the dependence of the above rates on the problem’s dimensionality in cases with a favorable geometry;
and

(2)

minimize the number of oracle queries per iteration.

The first of these objectives is achieved by the “
MD
” component:
by employing a suitable
DGF
(
DGF
) – like the negative entropy on the simplex or the log-barrier on the orthant) –
MD
achieves convergence rates that are (almost) dimension-free in problems with a favorable geometry.
This idea dates back to
Nemirovski & Yudin 1983
, and it is also the main building block of the
MP
(
MP
) algorithm which achieves order-optimal rates with two oracle queries per iteration
(
Nemirovski 2004
)
.

The “optimistic” module then clicks on top of the
MD
/
MP
template by replacing one of the two operator queries by already observed information.
This “information reuse” idea was originally due to
Popov 1980
, and it has recently resurfaced several times in learning theory, cf.
Chiang et al. 2012
,
Rakhlin & Sridharan 2013b
,
Rakhlin & Sridharan 2013a
,
Daskalakis et al. 2018
,
Gidel et al. 2019
,
Hsieh et al. 2019
,
Hsieh et al. 2021
, and references therein.
However, the quantitative behavior of the last-iterate in such mirror methods still remains an open question.

Our contributions.

In view of the aove, the aim of our paper is to examine the local convergence rate of
OMD
(
OMD
) in stochastic
VI
problems.
For generality, we focus on non-monotone
VI
, and we investigate the algorithm’s convergence to local solutions that satisfy a second-order sufficient condition.

In this regard, our first finding is that the algorithm’s rate of convergence depends sharply on the local geometry induced by the method’s underlying Bregman function.
We formalize this by introducing the notion of the
Legendre exponent
, which can roughly be described as the logarithmic ratio of the volume of a regular ball w.r.t. the ambient norm centered at the solution under study to that of a Bregman ball of the same radius.
For example, the ordinary Euclidean version of
OMD
has a Legendre exponent of
\beta=0
;
on the other hand, the entropic variant has a Legendre exponent of
\beta=1/2
on boundary points.

We then obtain the following rates as a function of
\beta
:

(1)

\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-(1-\beta)}\right)~\text{ if }~0\leq\beta<1/2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2\beta}(1-\beta)}\right)~\text{ if }~1/2\leq\beta<1

for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
.
Interestingly, these guarantees undergo a first-order phase transition between the Euclidean-like phase (
0\leq\beta<1/2
) and the entropy-like phase (
1/2\leq\beta<1
).
This coincides with the dichotomy between steep and non-steep
DGF
(i.e.,
DGF
that are differentiable only on the interior of the problem’s domain versus those that are differentiable throughout). Moreover, these rate guarantees are reached for different step-size schedules, respectively
\gamma_{t}=\Theta(1/t^{1-\beta})
and
\Theta(1/t^{(1-\varepsilon)/2})
, also depending on the Legendre exponent.

To the best of our knowledge, the only comparable result in the literature is the recent work of

Hsieh et al. 2019
that derives an
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
convergence rate for the Euclidean case (i.e., when
\beta=0
).
Along with some other recent works on optimistic gradient methods, we discuss this in detail in
Section

5
, after the precise statement of our results.

2.
Problem setup and preliminaries

Problem formulation, examples, and blanket assumptions.

Throughout what follows, we will focus on solving (Stampacchia)
VI
of the general form:

(VI)

\text{Find $x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{K}$ such that}\;\;\langle v(x^{\ast}),x-x^{\ast}\rangle\geq 0\;\;\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{K}$}.

In the above,

\mathcal{K}
is a closed convex subset of a
d
-dimensional real space
\mathcal{X}
with norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
,

\langle y,x\rangle
denotes the canonical pairing between
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
and
the
defining vector field

v\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{Y}
of the problem is a single-valued operator with values in the dual space
\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
of
\mathcal{X}
.
Letting
\lVert y\rVert_{\ast}\coloneqq\max\{\langle y,x\rangle:\lVert x\rVert\leq 1\}
denote the induced dual norm on
\mathcal{Y}
, we will make the following blanket assumption for
v
.

Assumption 1

(Lipschitz continuity)
.

The vector field
v
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous
, i.e.,

(LC)

\lVert v(x^{\prime})-v(x)\rVert_{\ast}\leq L\lVert x^{\prime}-x\rVert\quad\text{for all $x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}$}.

For concreteness, we provide below some archetypal examples of
VI
problems.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the minimization problem

(Min)

\displaystyle\textrm{minimize}_{x\in\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle f(x)

with
f\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
assumed
C^{1}
-smooth.
Then, letting
v(x)=\nabla f(x)
, the solutions of (
VI
) are precisely the
KKT
(
KKT
) points of (
Min
), cf.
Facchinei & Pang 2003
.

\lozenge

Example 2.2
.

A
saddle-point
– or
min-max
– problem can be stated, in normal form,
as

(SP)

\min_{x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}}\max_{x_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}}\,\Phi(x_{1},x_{2})

where
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are convex and closed, and the problem’s objective function
\Phi\colon\mathcal{K}_{1}\times\mathcal{K}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is again assumed to be smooth.
In the game-theoretic interpretation of
von Neumann 1928
,

x_{1}
is controlled by a player seeking to minimize
\Phi(\cdot,x_{2})
,
whereas
x_{2}
is controlled by a player seeking to maximize
\Phi(x_{1},\cdot)
.
Accordingly, solving (
SP
) consists of finding a
min-max point

(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{2}^{\ast})\in\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{1}\times\mathcal{K}_{2}
such that

(MinMax)

\Phi(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{2})\leq\Phi(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{2}^{\ast})\leq\Phi(x_{1},x_{2}^{\ast})\quad\text{for all $x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}$, $x_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}$}.

Min-max points may not exist if
\Phi
is not convex-concave.
In this case, one looks instead for
FOS
points of
\Phi
, i.e., action profiles
(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{2}^{\ast})\in\mathcal{K}_{1}\times\mathcal{K}_{2}
such that
x_{1}^{\ast}
is a
KKT
point of
\Phi(\cdot,x_{2}^{\ast})
and
x_{2}^{\ast}
is a
KKT
point of
-\Phi(x_{1}^{\ast},\cdot)
.
Letting
x=(x_{1},x_{2})

and
v=(\nabla_{x_{1}}\Phi,-\nabla_{x_{2}}\Phi)
,
we see that the solutions of (
VI
) are precisely the
FOS
points of
\Phi
.

\lozenge

The above examples show that not all solutions of (
VI
) are desirable:
for example, such a solution could be a local
maximum
of
f
in the case of (
Min
) or a max-min point in the case of (
MinMax
).
For this reason, we will concentrate on solutions of (
VI
) that satisfy the following second-order condition:

Assumption 2

(Second-order sufficiency)
.

For a solution
x^{\ast}
of (
VI
), there exists
a neighborhood
\mathcal{B}

and
a positive constant
\mu>0

such that

(SOS)

\langle v(x),x-x^{\ast}\rangle\geq\mu\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{B}$}.

In the context of (
Min
),
2
implies that
f
grows (at least) quadratically along every ray emanating from
x^{\ast}
;
in particular, we have
f(x)-f(x^{\ast})\geq\langle\nabla f(x^{\ast}),x-x^{\ast}\rangle+(\mu/2)\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}=\Omega(\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2})
for all
x\in\mathcal{B}
(though this does not mean that
f
is strongly convex in
\mathcal{B}
).
Likewise, in the context of (
MinMax
),
2
gives
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2}^{\ast})=\Omega(\lVert x_{1}-x_{1}^{\ast}\rVert^{2})
and
\Phi(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{2})=-\Omega(\lVert x_{2}-x_{2}^{\ast}\rVert^{2})
, so
x^{\ast}
is a local
NE
of
\Phi
.
In general,
2
guarantees that
x^{\ast}
is the unique solution of (
VI
) in
\mathcal{B}
.
(Indeed, any other solution
\hat{x}\neq x^{\ast}
of (
VI
) would satisfy
0\geq\langle v(\hat{x}),\hat{x}-x^{\ast}\rangle\geq\mu\lVert\hat{x}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}>0
, a contradiction.)

3.
The
OMD
algorithm

In this section, we recall the
OMD
(
OMD
) method for solving (
VI
).
To streamline the flow of our paper, we first discuss the type of feedback available to the optimizer.

3.1.
The oracle model

OMD
is a first-order method that only requires access to the problem’s defining vector field via a “black-box oracle” that returns a (possibly imperfect) version of
v(x)
at the selected query point
x\in\mathcal{K}
.
Concretely, we focus on
SFO
of the form

(SFO)

\displaystyle\orcl(x;\theta)

\displaystyle=v(x)+\err(x;\theta)

where

\theta
is a random variable taking values in some abstract measure space
\Theta

and

\err(x;\theta)
is an umbrella error term capturing all sources of uncertainty in the model.

In practice, the oracle is called repeatedly at a sequence of query points
X_{t}\in\mathcal{K}
with a different random seed
\theta_{t}
at each time.
1
1

1

In the sequel, we will allow the index
t
to take both integer and half-integer values.

The sequence of oracle signals
V_{t}=\orcl(X_{t};\theta_{t})
may then be written as

(2)

V_{t}=v(X_{t})+U_{t}

where
U_{t}=\err(X_{t};\theta_{t})
denotes the error of the oracle model at stage
t
.
To keep track of this sequence of events, we will treat
X_{t}
as a stochastic process on some complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
, and we will write
\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{F}
for the
history of play
up to stage
t
(inclusive).
Since the randomness entering the oracle is triggered only
after
a query point has been selected, we will posit throughout that
\theta_{t}
(and hence
U_{t}
and
V_{t}
) is
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-measurable – but not necessarily
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable.
We will also make the blanket assumption below for the oracle.

Assumption 3

(Oracle signal)
.

The error term
U_{t}
in (
2
) satisfies the following properties:

(3a)

\displaystyle a)\quad

Zero-mean:

\displaystyle\ex[U_{t}\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0.

(3b)

\displaystyle b)\quad

Finite variance:

\displaystyle\ex[\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{2}\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\sigma^{2}.\hskip 170.00026pt

Both assumptions are standard in the literature on stochastic methods in optimization, cf.
Polyak 1987
,
Hazan 2012
,
Bubeck 2015
, and references therein.

3.2.
OMD

With all this in hand, the
OMD
algorithm is defined in recursive form as follows:

(OMD)

\displaystyle X_{t+1/2}

\displaystyle=P_{#1}(-\gamma_{t}V_{t-1/2})

\displaystyle X_{t+1}

\displaystyle=P_{#1}(-\gamma_{t}V_{t+1/2})

In the above,

t=1,2,\dotsc
, is the algorithm’s iteration counter,

X_{t}
and
X_{t+1/2}
respectively denote the algorithm’s
base
and
leading
states at stage
t
,

V_{t+1/2}
is an oracle signal obtained by querying (
SFO
) at
X_{t+1/2}
,
and

P_{#1}(y)
denotes the method’s so-called “prox-mapping”.
In terms of initialization, we also take
X_{1}=X_{1/2}\in\relint\mathcal{K}
for simplicity.
All these elements are defined in detail below.

At a high level, (
OMD
) seeks to leverage past feedback to anticipate the landscape of
v
and perform more informed steps in subsequent iterations.
In more detail, starting at some base state
X_{t}
,
t=1,2,\dotsc
, the algorithm first generates an intermediate, leading state
X_{t+1/2}
, and then updates the base state with oracle input from
X_{t+1/2}
– that is,
V_{t+1/2}
.
This is also the main idea behind the
EG
algorithm of
Korpelevich 1976
;
the key difference here is that
OMD
avoids making two oracle queries per iteration by using the oracle input
V_{t-1/2}
received at the previous leading state
X_{t-1/2}
as a proxy for
V_{t}
(which is never requested or received by the optimizer).

The second basic component of the method is the prox-mapping
P
, which, loosely speaking, can be viewed as a generalized, proximal/projection operator adapted to the geometry of the problem’s primitives.
To define it formally, we will need the notion of a
DGF
on
\mathcal{K}
:

Definition 1
.

A convex
lsc
function
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
(with
\dom h=\mathcal{K}
) is a
DGF
(
DGF
) on
\mathcal{K}
if

(1)

The subdifferential of
h
admits a
continuous selection
, i.e., there exists a continuous mapping
\nabla h\colon\dom\partial h\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
\nabla h(x)\in\partial h(x)
for all
x\in\dom\partial h
.

(2)

h
is continuous and
1
-strongly convex on
\mathcal{K}
, i.e., for all
x\in\dom\partial h,x^{\prime}\in\dom h
, we have:

(4)

h(x^{\prime})\geq h(x)+\langle\nabla h(x),x^{\prime}-x\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert x^{\prime}-x\rVert^{2}.

For posterity, the set
\mathcal{K}_{h}\coloneqq\dom\partial h
will be referred to as the
prox-domain
of
h
.
We also define the
Bregman divergence
of
h
as

(5)

\displaystyle D(p,x)

\displaystyle=h(p)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),p-x\rangle,

for all
p\in\mathcal{K}
,
x\in\mathcal{K}_{h}

and the induced
prox-mapping

P\colon\mathcal{K}_{h}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{K}_{h}
as

(6)

\displaystyle P_{#1}(y)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}}\{\langle y,x-x^{\prime}\rangle+D(x^{\prime},x)\}

\displaystyle\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{K}_{h}$, $y\in\mathcal{Y}$}.

In the rest of this section we take a closer look at some commonly used
DGF
and the corresponding prox-mappings;
for simplicity, we focus on one-dimensional problems on
\mathcal{K}=[0,1]
.

Example 3.1

(Euclidean projection)
.

For the quadratic
DGF

h(x)=x^{2}/2
for
x\in\mathcal{K}
,
the Bregman divergence is
D(p,x)=(p-x)^{2}/2
and the induced prox-mapping is the Euclidean projector
\displaystyle P_{#1}(y)=[x+y]_{0}^{1}
.
The prox-domain of
h
is the entire feasible region, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{h}=\mathcal{K}=[0,1]
.

\lozenge

Example 3.2

(Negative entropy)
.

Another popular example is the entropic
DGF

h(x)=x\log x+(1-x)\log(1-x)
.
The corresponding Bregman divergence is the relative entropy
D(p,x)=p\log\frac{p}{x}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-x}
, and a standard calculation shows that
P_{#1}(y)=xe^{y}/[(1-x)+xe^{y}]
.
In contrast to
Example

3.1
, the prox-domain of
h
is
\mathcal{K}_{h}=(0,1)
.
For a (highly incomplete) list of references on the use of this
DGF
in learning theory and optimization, cf.
Auer et al. 1995
,
Beck & Teboulle 2003
,
Shalev-Shwartz 2011
,
Arora et al. 2012
,
Coucheney et al. 2015
,
Bomze et al. 2019
,
Lattimore & Szepesvári 2020
.

\lozenge

Example 3.3

(Tsallis entropy)
.

An alternative to the Gibbs–Shannon negentropy is the
Tsallis
/
fractional power

DGF

h(x)=-\frac{1}{q(1-q)}\cdot[x^{q}+(1-x)^{q}]
for
q>0

(
Tsallis 1988
,
Alvarez et al. 2004
,
Mertikopoulos & Sandholm 2016
,
Mertikopoulos & Sandholm 2018
)
.
Formally, to define
h
for
q\leftarrow 1
, we will employ the continuity convention
t^{q}/(q-1)\leftarrow\log t
, which yields the entropic
DGF
of
Example

3.2
.
Instead, for
q\leftarrow 2
, we readily get the Euclidean
DGF
of
Example

3.1
.
The corresponding prox-mapping does not admit a closed-form expression for all
p
, but it can always be calculated in logarithmic time with a simple binary search.
Finally, we note that the prox-domain
\mathcal{K}_{h}
of
h
is the entire space
\mathcal{K}
for
q>1
;
on the other hand, for
q\in(0,1]
, we have
\mathcal{K}_{h}=(0,1)
.

\lozenge

The above examples will play a key role in illustrating the analysis to come and we will use them as running examples throughout.

4.
The geometry of
DGF

The Bregman topology.

To proceed with our analysis and the statement of our results for (
OMD
), we will need to take a closer look at the geometry induced on
\mathcal{K}
by the choice of
h
.
The first observation is that, by the strong convexity requirement for
h
, we have:

(7)

D(p,x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert p-x\rVert^{2}\quad\text{for all $p\in\mathcal{K}$, $x\in\mathcal{K}_{h}$}.

Topologically, this means that the convergence topology induced by the Bregman divergence of
h
on
\mathcal{K}
is
at least as fine
as the corresponding norm topology:
if a sequence
x_{t}
converges to
p\in\mathcal{K}
in the Bregman sense (
D(p,x_{t})\to 0
), it also converges in the ordinary norm topology (
\lVert x_{t}-p\rVert\to 0
).

On the other hand, the converse of this statement is, in general, false:
specifically, the norm topology could be
strictly coarser
than the Bregman topology. To see this, let
\mathcal{K}
be the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert x\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
.
For this space, a popular choice of
DGF
is the
Hellinger regularizer

h(x)=-\sqrt{1-\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}
, which has
\mathcal{K}_{h}=\relint\mathcal{K}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert x\rVert_{2}<1\}

(
Alvarez et al. 2004
,
Bauschke et al. 2017
)
.
Then, for all
p
on the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
(i.e.,
\lVert p\rVert_{2}=1
), the Bregman divergence is

(8)

D(p,x)=\frac{1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}x_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}}.

Shadowing the Euclidean definition, a “Hellinger sphere” of radius
r
centered at
p
is defined as the corresponding level set of
D(p,x)
, i.e.,
S_{p}(r)=\{x\in\mathcal{K}_{h}:D(p,x)=r\}
.
Hence, by (
8
), this means that a point
x\in\mathcal{K}_{h}
is at “Hellinger distance”
r
relative to
p
if

(9)

1-\sum\nolimits_{i}p_{i}x_{i}=r\sqrt{1-\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

Now, if we take a sequence
x_{t}
converging to
p
in the ordinary Euclidean sense (i.e.,
\lVert x_{t}-p\rVert_{2}\to 0
), both sides of (
9
) converge to
0
.
This has several counterintuitive consequences:

(1)

S_{r}(p)
is
not closed
in the Euclidean topology;
and

(2)

the “Hellinger center”
p
of
S_{r}(p)
actually belongs to the Euclidean closure of
S_{r}(p)
.

As a result, we can have a sequence
x_{t}
with
\lVert x_{t}-p\rVert_{2}\to 0
, but which remains at
constant
Hellinger divergence relative to
p
.

From a qualitative standpoint, this discrepancy means that
D(p,x)
is not a reliable measure of convergence of
x
to
p
:
if
D(p,x)
is large, this does not necessarily mean that
p
and
x
are topologically “far”.
For this reason, in the analysis of non-Euclidean instances of Bregman proximal methods, it is common to make the so-called “reciprocity” assumption

(10)

D(p,x_{t})\to 0\quad\text{whenever}\quad x_{t}\to p.

A straightforward verification shows that the
DGF
of
Examples

3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
all satisfy (
10
);

The Legendre exponent.

From a
quantitative
standpoint, even if (
10
) is satisfied, the local geometry induced by a Bregman
DGF
could still be significantly different from the base, Euclidean case.
A clear example of this is provided by contrasting the Euclidean and entropic
DGF
of
Examples

3.1
and
3.2
.
Concretely, if we focus on the base point
p=0
, we have:

(1)

In the Euclidean case (
Example

3.1
):

D(0,x)=x^{2}/2=\Theta(x^{2})
for small
x\geq 0
;

(2)

In the entropic case (
Example

3.2
):

D(0,x)=-\log(1-x)=\Theta(x)
for small
x\geq 0
.

Consequently, the rate of convergence of
x_{t}
to
p=0
is drastically different if it is measured relative to the Euclidean divergence of
Example

3.1
or the Kullback-Leibler divergence of
Example

3.2
.
As an example, if we take the sequence
x_{t}=1/t
, we get
D(0,x_{t})=\Theta(1/t^{2})
in terms of the Euclidean divergence, but
D(0,x_{t})=\Theta(1/t)
in terms of the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

This disparity is due to the fact that it might be impossible to invert the inequality (
7
), even to leading order.
To account for this, we introduce below the notion of the
Legendre exponent
of
h
.

Definition 2
.

Let
h
be a
DGF
on
\mathcal{K}
.
Then the
Legendre exponent
of
h
at
p\in\mathcal{K}
is defined to be the smallest
\beta\in[0,1)
such that there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}
of
p

and some
\kappa\geq 0
such that

(11)

D(p,x)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa\lVert p-x\rVert^{2(1-\beta)}\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h}$}.

Heuristically, the Legendre exponent measures the difference in relative size between ordinary “norm neighborhoods” in
\mathcal{K}
and the corresponding “Bregman neighborhoods” induced by the sublevel sets of the Bregman divergence.
It is straightforward to see that the requirement
\beta\geq 0
is imposed by the strong convexity of
h
:
since
D(p,x)=\Omega(\lVert p-x\rVert^{2})
for
x
close to
p
, we cannot also have
D(p,x)=\mathcal{O}(\lVert p-x\rVert^{2+\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Note also that the notion of a Legendre exponent is only relevant if
h
satisfies the reciprocity requirement (
10
):
otherwise, we could have
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}}D(p,x)=\infty
for any neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
p
in
\mathcal{K}_{h}
, in which case we say that the Legendre exponent is
1
by convention.

To see the Legendre exponents in action, we compute them for our running examples:

(1)

Euclidean projection

(
Example

3.1
):

D(p,x)=(p-x)^{2}/2

for all
p,x\in\mathcal{K}
, so
\beta=0
for all
p
.

(2)

Negative entropy

(
Example

3.2
):
If
p\in\mathcal{K}_{h}=(0,1)
, a Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder gives

D(p,x)=h(p)-h(x)-h^{\prime}(x)(p-x)=\mathcal{O}((p-x)^{2})
and thus
\beta=0
.
Now if
p=0
,
D(0,x)=-\log(1-x)=\Theta(x)
which implies
\beta=1/2
. Symmetrically, if
p=1
,
\beta=1/2
too.

(3)

Tsallis entropy

(
Example

3.3
):
Assume that
q\neq 1
(the case
q=1
has been treated above).
We then have two cases:
if
p\in\mathcal{K}_{h}=(0,1)
,
h
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of
p
so that, as previously, by the Taylor formula,

D(p,x)

On the other hand, if
p=0
,

(12)

D(0,x)=\frac{x^{q}}{q}-\frac{1}{q(1-q)}[1-(1-x)^{q}-q(1-x)^{q-1}x]\,,

so
D(0,x)=\Theta(x^{\min(q,2)})
when
x
goes to zero.
By symmetry, the situation is the same at
p=1
, so the Legendre exponent of the Tsallis entropy is
\beta=\max(0,1-q/2)
.

(4)

Hellinger regularizer:

As we saw at the beginning of section, the Hellinger divergence (
8
) does not satisfy (
10
), so
\beta=1
by convention.

Of course, beyond our running examples, the same rationale applies
when
\mathcal{K}
is a subset of a

If

(see

for a precise statement), while if
p
lies at the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
, the exponent may be positive;
we illustrate this below for the important case of the simplex with the entropic

Example 4.1

(Entropy on the simplex)
.

Consider the

\mathcal{K}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\}
and the entropic regularizer

The associated divergence (also called Kullback-Leibler divergence) can be decomposed as

\displaystyle D(p,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:p_{i}=0}(\log x_{i}+1)x_{i}-x_{i}\log x_{i}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i:p_{i}\neq 0}p_{i}\log p_{i}-x_{i}\log x_{i}-(\log x_{i}+1)(p_{i}-x_{i}).

The first sum is simply

\displaystyle D(p,x)

\displaystyle\textstyle=\sum_{i:p_{i}=0}x_{i}+\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i:p_{i}\neq 0}(p_{i}-x_{i})^{2}\right).

If

Related work.

The rate guarantees of
Theorem

1
should be compared and contrasted to a series of recent results on the convergence speed of
OMD
and its variants. Most of these results concern the deterministic case, i.e., when
U_{t}=0
for all
t
.
In this regime, and focusing on the last iterate of the method,
Golowich et al. 2020a
,
Golowich et al. 2020b
showed that the convergence speed of the (optimistic) extra-gradient algorithm in unconstrained, smooth, monotone variational inequalities is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t})
in terms of
\lVert v(X_{t})\rVert
.
If, in addition,
v
is
strongly
monotone, it is well known that this rate becomes linear;
for a recent take on different (Euclidean) variants of (
OMD
), see
Malitsky 2015
,
Gidel et al. 2019
,
Hsieh et al. 2019
,
Mokhtari et al. 2019a
,
Mokhtari et al. 2019b
, and references therein.

Unsurprisingly, the stochastic regime is fundamentally different.
In the merely monotone case (i.e., neither strongly nor strictly monotone), we are not aware of any result on the method’s last-iterate convergence speed:
existing results in the literature either cover the algorithm’s ergodic average
(
Juditsky et al. 2011
,
Gidel et al. 2019
,
Cui & Shanbhag 2016
)
or are asymptotic in nature
(
Iusem et al. 2017
)
.
Because of the lack of deterministic correlation between gradients at the base and leading states in stochastic problems, smoothness does not help to achieve better rates in this case – except for mollifying the requirement for bounded gradient signals
(
Juditsky et al. 2011
)
.
By contrast, strong monotonicity – local or global –
does
help:
as was shown by
Hsieh et al. 2019
, the iterates of the
Euclidean
version of (
OMD
) run with a
1/t
step-size schedule converge locally to solutions satisfying (
SOS
) at a
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
rate in terms of the mean square distance to the solution.
This result is a special instance of Case II of
Theorem

1
:
in particular, for
\beta=0
,
\eta=1
, (
19
) indicates that the
1/t
step-size schedule examined by
Hsieh et al. 2019
is optimal for the Euclidean case;
however, as we explain below, it is
not
optimal for non-Euclidean
DGF
.

Step-size tuning.

A key take-away from
Theorem

1
is that the step-size schedule that optimizes the rate guarantees in
Eqs.

17
and
18
is either
\gamma_{t}=\Theta(1/t^{1-\beta})
when
0\leq\beta<1/2
, or as close as possible to
\Theta(t^{1/2})
when
1/2\leq\beta<1
;
in both cases, the optimal tuning of the algorithm’s step-size
depends on the Legendre exponent of
x^{\ast}
. We feel that this is an important parameter to keep in mind when deploying non-Euclidean versions of (
OMD
) in a stochastic setting – e.g., as in the
OMWU
(
OMWU
) algorithm of
Daskalakis & Panageas 2019
.

Theorem

1
suggests that the “best practices” of the Euclidean setting do not carry over to non-Euclidean ones:
Euclidean step-size policy
1/t
would lead to a catastrophic drop from
1/\sqrt{t}
to
1/\log t
in the rate guarantees for (
OMD
).

Geometry-dependent rates.

One final observation for
Theorem

1
is that the convergence speed of (
OMD
) is measured in terms of the Bregman divergence
D(x^{\ast},X_{t})
.
Since
\lVert X_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{D(x^{\ast},X_{t})})
, this rate immediately translates to a rate on
\lVert X_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert
.
However, this rate is
not
tight, again because of the disparity between the Bregman and norm geometries discussed in
Section

4
.
Specifically, in many cases, a non-zero Legendre exponent implies a sharper lower bound on the Bregman divergence of the form
D(x^{\ast},X_{t})=\Omega(\lVert X_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2-2\beta})
.
Thus, for
\beta\in[0,1/2)
, Jensen’s inequality applied to the optimized bound (
17
) gives
\ex[\lVert X_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert]=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t})
.
Importantly, this rate guarantee is the same as in the Euclidean case (
\beta=0
), though it requires a completely different step-size policy to achieve it.
Determining when the Legendre exponent also provides a lower bound is a very interesting direction for future research, but one that lies beyond the scope of this work.

(1)

a_{t}
based on this recursive inequality.
The necessary groundwork is provided by
Lemmas

A.11
and
A.12
, and hinges on whether
\eta\geq\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}
or not (and is also where the value of
c(\eta,\alpha)
comes in).

Putting everything together, we obtain the conditional rates in the statement of the proposition.
∎

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the rate of convergence of
OMD
for solving variational inequalities with stochastic oracle feedback.
Our results highlight the relationship between the rate of convergence of the last iterate and the local geometry of the
DGF
at the solution.
To capture this local geometry, we introduced
the regularity exponent of the divergence relative to the norm that we dubbed the Legendre exponent.
This quantity plays a central role in our results:
the less regular the Bregman divergence around the solution, the slower the convergence of the last iterate.
Furthermore, we show that that the step-size policy that guarantees the best rates depends on the

DGF
through the associated Legendre exponent.

This work opens the door to various refinements and extensions diving deeper into the geometry of the method’s
DGF
.
A key remark is that the method’s Legendre exponent seems to depend crucially on which constraints of the problem are active at a given solution.
Deriving a precise characterization of the method’s convergence rate in these cases is a fruitful direction for future research.

.

.

For

.

If
2
holds,

Lemma A.7

(Trivial Legendre exponent on the interior)
.

Assume that
\nabla h
is locally Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{K}_{h}
. Then, for any
p\in\mathcal{K}_{h}
, the Legendre exponent of
h
at
p
is
\beta=0
.

Proof.

Take
p\in\mathcal{K}_{h}
. As
\nabla h
is assumed to be locally Lipschitz, there exists
\mathcal{V}
a neighborhood of
p
and
\kappa>0
such that,
for any
x\in\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h}
,

\lVert\nabla h(p)-\nabla h(x)\rVert_{\ast}\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\lVert p-x\rVert\,.

Now, as

\displaystyle D(p,x)

\displaystyle=h(p)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),p-x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla h(p)-\nabla h(x),p-x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\nabla h(p)-\nabla h(x)\rVert_{\ast}\lVert p-x\rVert\,.

Using the local Lipschitz continuity of

, we get, for any

which reads

∎

Lemma A.8

(

)
.

Let

,

Lemma A.9

(
Chung 1954
)
.

Let
(a_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of non-negative scalars,
q,q^{\prime}>0
,
t_{0}\geq 0
,
1>\eta>0
.
If, for any
t\geq 1
,

a_{t+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{q}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}\right)a_{t}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\,,

Lemma A.10

(
Polyak 1987
)
.

Let
(a_{t})_{1\leq t\leq T}
be a sequence of non-negative scalars,
(\gamma_{t})_{1\leq t\leq T}
be sequence of positive scalars and
\alpha>0
.
If, for any

Though the next two lemmas allow for step-sizes of the form
\gamma_{t}=\frac{\gamma}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}
, they do not exploit the term
t_{0}
.

Lemma A.11
.

Let
(a_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of non-negative scalars,
(\gamma_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be sequence of positive scalars of the form
\gamma_{t}=\frac{q}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}
with
q,\eta>0
,
t_{0}\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
,
q^{\prime}>0
such that,

a_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-\gamma_{t}a_{t}^{1+\alpha}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\,.

If,

\displaystyle 1\geq

\displaystyle\eta\geq\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}>\frac{1}{2}\,,

\displaystyle q^{\prime}q^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq c(\eta,\alpha)\coloneqq\alpha\left(\frac{1-2^{-(1+\alpha)}}{1+\alpha}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\,,

then, for any
T\geq 1
,

a_{T}\leq\frac{a_{1}+b}{\left(1+\alpha(a_{1}+b)^{\alpha}2^{-\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_{t}\right)^{1/\alpha}}\,,

where
b=\left(\frac{1-2^{1-2\eta}}{(1+\alpha)q}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
.

Proof.

First, define
p=1+\alpha>1
and note that
\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}=\frac{p}{2p-1}=\frac{1}{2-p^{-1}}\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
so, in particular, the condition on
\eta
is not absurd.
Moreover, this means that
\beta\coloneqq 2\eta-1
belongs to
(0,1]
. Then, we have
b=\left(\frac{1-2^{-\beta}}{pq}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}>0
and, define, for
t\geq 1
,
b_{t}\coloneqq\frac{b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta}}
.

The first part of the proof consists in showing, that, for
t\geq 1
,

\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\leq b_{t}-b_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}\,.

For this, we will need the following remark. As
\beta\leq 1
,
x\mapsto(1+x)^{\beta}
is concave. Hence, it is above its chords, and in particular above its chord going from
0
to
1
. Thus, for
0\leq x\leq 1
,
(1+x)^{\beta}\geq 1+x(2^{\beta}-1)
.

We use this remark to lower bound
b_{t}-b_{t+1}
for
t\geq 1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle b_{t}-b_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta}}-\frac{b}{(t+1+t_{0})^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{(t+1+t_{0})^{\beta}}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{t+t_{0}}\right)^{\beta}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{b}{(t+1+t_{0})^{\beta}}\frac{2^{\beta}-1}{t+t_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta+1}}\frac{2^{\beta}-1}{2^{\beta}}\,.

Therefore,

b_{t}-b_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}\geq\frac{b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta+1}}(1-2^{-\beta})-\frac{qb^{p}}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta+p\beta}}\,.

Now, by the definition of
\beta
,
\beta+1=2\eta
and
\eta+p\beta=(2p+1)\eta-p=2\eta+(2p-1)\eta-p\geq 2\eta
by the assumption that
\eta\geq\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}=\frac{p}{2p-1}
. Hence,

b_{t}-b_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}\geq\frac{1}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}(b(1-2^{-\beta})-qb^{p})\,,

so that we only need to show that
q^{\prime}\leq b(1-2^{-\beta})-qb^{p}
.

Rearranging and replacing
b
by its expression gives,

\displaystyle b(1-2^{-\beta})-qb^{p}

\displaystyle=b((1-2^{-\beta})-qb^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=b\left((1-2^{-\beta})-\frac{1-2^{-\beta}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=b(1-2^{-\beta})\frac{p-1}{p}

\displaystyle=(1-2^{-\beta})^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{p-1}{p^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}q^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\,.

Therefore, with
c(\eta,\alpha)=\alpha\left(\frac{1-2^{-(1+\alpha)}}{1+\alpha}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}=(1-2^{-\beta})^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\frac{p-1}{p^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}>0
and
q^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}q^{\prime}\leq c(\eta,\alpha)
, we finally get that
q^{\prime}\leq b(1-2^{-\beta})-qb^{p}
and, for
t\geq 1
,

\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\leq b_{t}-b_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}\,.

Recall that
(a_{t})_{t\geq 1}
satisfies, for
t\geq 1
,

a_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-\gamma_{t}a_{t}^{p}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\,.

Therefore, putting these two inequalities together gives,

a_{t+1}+b_{t+1}\leq a_{t}+b_{t}-\gamma_{t}(a_{t}^{p}+b_{t}^{p})\,.

Finally, by convexity of
x\mapsto x^{p}
,

a_{t+1}+b_{t+1}\leq a_{t}+b_{t}-\gamma_{t}2^{1-p}(a_{t}+b_{t})^{p}\,.

Now, we can apply
Lemma

A.10
with
a_{t}\leftarrow a_{t}+b_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}\leftarrow\gamma_{t}2^{1-p}
. For any
T\geq 1
,

a_{T}\leq a_{T}+b_{T}\leq\frac{1}{\left((a_{1}+b_{1})^{-\alpha}+\alpha 2^{-\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_{t}\right)^{1/\alpha}}\,.

As the right-hand side is non-decreasing in
b_{1}
and
b_{1}\leq b
, we get the result of the statement.
∎

Lemma A.12
.

Let
(a_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of non-negative scalars,
(\gamma_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be sequence of positive scalars of the form
\gamma_{t}=\frac{q}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}
with
q,\eta>0
,
b\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
,
q^{\prime}>0
such that,

a_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-\gamma_{t}a_{t}^{1+\alpha}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\,.

If,

\displaystyle 1>\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\eta>\frac{1}{2}\,,

\displaystyle q^{\prime}q^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq c(\eta,\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{1-\eta}{(1+\alpha)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}+1}}\,,

then, for any

,

a_{T}\leq\frac{a_{1}}{\left(1+\alpha a_{1}^{\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_{t}\right)^{1/\alpha}}+\frac{1}{((1+\alpha)q)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}{(T+t_{0})}^{\eta}}\,.

Proof.

This proof is the “mirror” of the proof of the previous lemma.
As before, define
p=1+\alpha>1
and note that
\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}=\frac{p}{2p-1}=\frac{1}{2-p^{-1}}\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
so the condition on
\eta
is not absurd.
Moreover, this means that
\beta\coloneqq\frac{\eta}{p}
belongs to
(0,1)
. Then, define
b\lx@nobreakspace\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{pq}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}>0
and, define, for
t\geq 1
,
b_{t}\coloneqq\frac{b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta}}
.

Opposite to the proof of the previous lemma, the first part of the proof consists in showing, that, for
t\geq 1
,

\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\leq b_{t+1}+\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}-b_{t}\,.

For this, we use the concavity of
x\mapsto(1+x)^{\beta}
, as
\beta\leq 1
, so that, for
x\geq 0
,
(1+x)^{\beta}\leq 1+\beta x
.

This remark enables us to upper bound
b_{t}-b_{t+1}
for
t\geq 1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle b_{t}-b_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta}}-\frac{b}{(t+1+t_{0})^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{(t+1+t_{0})^{\beta}}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{t+t_{0}}\right)^{\beta}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{b}{(t+1+t_{0})^{\beta}}\frac{\beta}{t+t_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta+1}}\,.

Therefore,

b_{t}-b_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}\leq\frac{\beta b}{(t+t_{0})^{\beta+1}}-\frac{qb^{p}}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta+p\beta}}\,.

Now, by the definition of
\beta=\frac{\eta}{p}
,
\eta+p\beta=2\eta
and
\beta+1=2\eta+\frac{\eta}{p}+1-2\eta=2\eta+\frac{p-(2p-1)\eta}{p}\geq 2\eta
by the assumption that
\eta\leq\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}=\frac{p}{2p-1}
. Hence,

b_{t}-b_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}\leq\frac{1}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}(\beta b-qb^{p})\,,

so that,

b_{t+1}+\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}-b_{t}\geq\frac{1}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}(qb^{p}-\beta b)\,.

Again, we only need to show that
q^{\prime}\leq qb^{p}-\beta b
.

Rearranging and replacing
b
by its expression gives,

\displaystyle qb^{p}-b\beta

\displaystyle=b(qb^{\alpha}-\beta)

\displaystyle=b\left(\frac{1}{p}-\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}p^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\beta\right)\,.

Therefore, with
c(\eta,\alpha)=\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\beta\right)>0
and
q^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}q^{\prime}\leq c(\eta,\alpha)
, we finally get that
q^{\prime}\leq qb^{p}-\beta b
and, for
t\geq 1
,

\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\leq b_{t+1}+\gamma_{t}b_{t}^{p}-b_{t}\,.

Putting this inequality together with the one on the sequence
(a_{t})_{t\geq 1}
gives,

a_{t+1}-b_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-b_{t}-\gamma_{t}(a_{t}^{p}-b_{t}^{p})\,.

Now, let us discuss separately the case when
a_{t}>b_{t}
and when
a_{t}\leq b_{t}
.

More precisely, define
T_{0}=\min\{t\geq 1:a_{t}\leq b_{t}\}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\cup\{+\infty\}
, so that, for any
1\leq t<T_{0}
,
a_{t}>b_{t}>0
.
Note that, for
x,y>0
, as
p>1
,
x^{p}+y^{p}\leq(x+y)^{p}
. For
1\leq t<T_{0}
, apply this with
x\leftarrow a_{t}-b_{t}
,

gives
(a_{t}-b_{t})^{p}\leq a_{t}^{p}-b_{t}^{p}
so that,

a_{t+1}-b_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-b_{t}-\gamma_{t}(a_{t}-b_{t})^{p}\,.

Lemma

A.10
with
a_{t}\leftarrow a_{t}-b_{t}
gives, for
1\leq T<T_{0}
,

\displaystyle a_{T}

\displaystyle\leq b_{T}+\frac{1}{((a_{1}-b_{1})^{-\alpha}+\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_{t})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\leq b_{T}+\frac{1}{(a_{1}^{-\alpha}+\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_{t})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\,,

so the statement holds in this case.

We now handle the case of

. At
t=T_{0}
, we have
a_{T_{0}}\leq b_{T_{0}}
. We show, by induction, that, for any

a_{t}\leq b_{t}
. The initialization at

is trivial. So, now, assume that
a_{t}\leq b_{t}
for some

Recall that we have,

a_{t+1}-b_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-b_{t}-\gamma_{t}(a_{t}^{p}-b_{t}^{p})\,,

so we only need to show that,

Define
\varphi_{t}:x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto x-\gamma_{t}x^{p}

But

by definition of

Therefore, we have shown that, for all

Descent and stability of optimistic mirror descent

B.1.
Descent inequality

The lemmas we present here link the divergence between two consecutive iterates and the solution.

In particular, the first one only rely on i) the definition of one iteration of
OMD
; and ii) the core properties of the Bregman regularizer i.e., the
1
-strong convexity of
h
(through
Lemma

A.1
) and the non-expansivity of the proximal mapping (
Lemma

A.2
). It does not require any assumption on the vector field
v
, on the noise in
V
, or additional properties of the Bregman divergence (such as a Legendre exponent).

Lemma B.1
.

If the stepsizes verify

\mu\gamma_{t}+4L^{2}\gamma_{t}^{2}\leq 1\,,

then, the iterates of

initialized with
X_{1/2}\in\mathcal{K}
,
X_{1}\in\dom\partial h
verify for all

,

\displaystyle D(x^{\ast},X_{t+1})+\phi_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+(1-\gamma_{t}\mu)\phi_{t}

\displaystyle~~~~~-\gamma_{t}\langle V_{t+1/2},X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle+\left(2\gamma_{t}^{2}L^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\|X_{t+1/2}-X_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~+\gamma_{t}^{2}\Delta_{t+1}^{2}-\gamma_{t}^{2}\tau^{2}\|X_{t}-X_{t-1/2}\|^{2}\,,

where
\Delta_{t+1}^{2}=(\|V_{t+1/2}-V_{t-1/2}\|^{2}_{*}-L^{2}\|X_{t+1/2}-X_{t-1/2}\|^{2})_{+}
,

\phi_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{t-1}^{2}}{2}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t-3/2}\|^{2}
for all

,
and
\phi_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\|X_{1}-X_{1/2}\|^{2}
.

Proof.

First, apply
Lemma

A.4
with
(x,y_{1},y_{2})\leftarrow(X_{t},-\gamma_{t}V_{t-1/2},-\gamma_{t}V_{t+1/2})
and

(B.1)

\displaystyle D(x^{\ast},X_{t+1})\leq D(x^{\ast},X_{t})-\gamma_{t}\langle V_{t+1/2},X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle+\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t+1/2}\|-\frac{1}{2}\|X_{t+1/2}-X_{t}\|^{2}\,.

\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t+1/2}\|-\frac{1}{2}\|X_{t+1/2}-X_{t}\|^{2}
, we use the definition of

to see that

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t+1/2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\gamma_{t}^{2}}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t+1/2}\|^{2}-\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t+1/2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\gamma_{t}^{2}}\|V_{t-1/2}-V_{t+1/2}\|^{2}-\phi_{t+1}\,,

and the definition of
\Delta_{t+1}

Eq.

C.1
, and the fact that
\kappa\geq 1
,

\displaystyle(D(x^{\ast},X_{t+1})+\phi_{t+1})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+(1-\frac{\gamma_{t}\mu}{\kappa})\phi_{t})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}-\gamma_{t}\langle U_{t+1/2},X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}

\displaystyle~~~~~+\left(4\gamma_{t}^{2}L^{2}+\mu\gamma_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\|X_{t+1/2}-X_{t}\|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}+4\gamma_{t}^{2}(\|U_{t+1/2}\|_{*}^{2}+\|U_{t-1/2}\|_{*}^{2})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\,.

Now, the sequence of events
(\mathcal{E}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is non-increasing, so
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\leq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\leq 1
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+(1-\frac{\gamma_{t}\mu}{\kappa})\phi_{t}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+(1-\gamma_{t}\mu)\phi_{t}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\,.

Note that the term between parenthesis is always non-negative even when
\alpha>0
.

Moreover, by the choice of
\gamma
,
4\gamma_{t}^{2}L^{2}+\mu\gamma_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\leq 0
.
Therefore, the descent inequality can be simplified to give,

\displaystyle\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t+1})+\phi_{t+1}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+(1-\frac{\gamma_{t}\mu}{\kappa})\phi_{t}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}-\gamma_{t}\langle U_{t+1/2},X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}

\displaystyle~~~~+4\gamma_{t}^{2}(\|U_{t+1/2}\|_{*}^{2}+\|U_{t-1/2}\|_{*}^{2})\,.

We now take the expectation of this inequality. For this, note that, as
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+1/2}
measurable,

\displaystyle\ex\left[\langle U_{t+1/2},X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\ex\left[\ex\left[\left.\langle U_{t+1/2},X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\,\right|\,\mathcal{F}_{t+1/2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\ex\left[\langle\ex\left[\left.U_{t+1/2}\,\right|\,\mathcal{F}_{t+1/2}\right],X_{t+1/2}-x^{\ast}\rangle\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=0\,.

As a consequence, and using the assumption on the noise,

(C.3)

\displaystyle\ex\left[(D(x^{\ast},X_{t+1})+\phi_{t+1})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{t}\mu}{\kappa}\right)\phi_{t}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]+8\gamma_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}\,.

(5)

The final step will be to study the sequence
(a_{t})
defined by
a_{t}=\ex\left[(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]
for all
t\geq 1
.
Indeed, a bound on
a_{t}=\ex\left((D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right)
implies a bound on the quantity of interest,

\ex\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\right)\,.

As
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\subset\mathcal{E}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{U}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\ex\left[(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]}{\prob[\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{U}}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ex\left[(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t}}\right]}{\prob[\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{U}}]}\,

Now, the inequality
Eq.

C.3
above can be rewritten as follows,

(C.4)

\displaystyle a_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}\ex\left[\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+\phi_{t}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]+8\gamma_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}\,.

(6)

Now, the behavior of this sequence depends heavily on the Legendre exponent
\alpha
:

•

If
\alpha=0
, (
C.4
) becomes

\displaystyle a_{t+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu\gamma}{\kappa(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}\right)a_{t}+\frac{8\gamma^{2}\sigma^{2}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\,.

Then, for
\eta=1
,
Lemma

A.8
can be applied with the additional assumption that
\gamma>\frac{\kappa}{\mu}
to get that for any
T\geq 1
,
a_{T}=\mathcal{O}(1/T)
.

For
\frac{1}{2}<\eta<1
,
Lemma

A.9
can be directly apply to obtain
a_{T}=\mathcal{O}(1/T^{\eta})
.

•

If
\alpha>0
, a little more work has to be done before on (
C.4
) before concluding. First, (
C.4
) implies that for any
t\geq 1
,
a_{t}\leq a_{0}+8\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{t}^{2}
and since
we assume
D(x^{\ast},X_{1})+\phi_{1}=D(x^{\ast},X_{1})\leq\frac{r^{2}}{12}

we have that

\displaystyle\ex\left[\phi_{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]=a_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[(D(x^{\ast},X_{1})+\phi_{1})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}\right]+8\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{r^{2}}{12}+\frac{8\sigma^{2}\gamma^{2}}{(2\eta-1)t_{0}^{2\eta-1}}}_{:=\Phi}.

As a consequence,
-\ex\left[\phi_{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]\leq-\ex\left[\phi_{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]^{1+\alpha}/\Phi^{\alpha}
, and thus (
C.4
) gives us

\displaystyle a_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq a_{t}-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{\kappa}\ex\left[\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})^{1+\alpha}+\frac{1}{\Phi^{\alpha}}\phi_{t}^{1+\alpha}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]+8\gamma_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a_{t}-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{2^{\alpha}\max(1,\Phi^{\alpha})\kappa}\ex\left[\left(D(x^{\ast},X_{t})+\phi_{t}\right)^{1+\alpha}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{t-1}}\right]+8\gamma_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a_{t}-\frac{\mu\gamma_{t}}{2^{\alpha}\max(1,\Phi^{\alpha})\kappa}a_{t}^{1+\alpha}+8\gamma_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}

where the second inequality comes from the fact that
(x+y)^{1+\alpha}/2^{\alpha}\leq x^{1+\alpha}+y^{1+\alpha}
for positive
x,y
and the last one from Jensen inequality applied to the convex function
x\mapsto x^{1+\alpha}
.

We will now apply one of
Lemmas

A.11
and
A.12
to the sequence
(a_{t})
. To make this step clear, let us introduce the same notations as these lemmas. Define

\displaystyle q\coloneqq\frac{\mu\gamma}{2^{\alpha}\max(1,\Phi^{\alpha})\kappa}\quad\text{and}\quad q^{\prime}\coloneqq 8\sigma^{2}\gamma^{2}\,.

With these notations, the descent inequality can be rewritten as,

a_{t+1}\leq a_{t}-\frac{q}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}a_{t}^{1+\alpha}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{(t+t_{0})^{2\eta}}\,.

Both
Lemmas

A.11
and
A.12
require that,

q^{\prime}q^{1/\alpha}\leq c(\eta,\alpha)\iff\gamma^{2+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq\frac{c(1,\alpha)}{4\sigma^{2}(\mu/\kappa)^{{1/\alpha}}}\max(1,\Phi)\,.

Finally, we distinguish two cases.

–

If
\eta\geq\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}
, we apply
Lemma

A.11
to get that,
for any
T\geq 1
,

a_{T}\leq\frac{a_{1}+b}{\left(1+\alpha(a_{1}+b)^{\alpha}2^{-\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{q}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}\right)^{1/\alpha}}\,,

where
b=\left(\frac{1-2^{1-2\eta}}{(1+\alpha)q}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
.
Using asymptotic notations, this simply means that,

a_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}\right)^{-1/\alpha}\right)\,.

The final bound on
a_{T}
now comes from the fact that,

\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{1}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}=\begin{cases}\Theta\left(T^{1-\eta}\right)&\text{ if }\eta<1\\
\Theta\left(\log T\right)&\text{ if }\eta=1\,.\end{cases}

–

If
\eta\leq\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}
, we apply
Lemma

A.12
to get that,
for any
T\geq 1
,

a_{T}\leq\frac{a_{1}}{\left(1+\alpha a_{1}^{\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{q}{(t+t_{0})^{\eta}}\right)^{1/\alpha}}+\frac{1}{((1+\alpha)q)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}{(t+t_{0})}^{\eta}}\,.

In other words, the sequence
(a_{t})
satisfies, as
\eta<1
,

a_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T^{(1-\eta)/\alpha}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T^{\eta}}\right)\,.

∎
