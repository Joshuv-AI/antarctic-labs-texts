Exploring the Evidence-Based Beliefs and Behaviors of LLM-Based Programming Assistants
Chris Brown, Jason Cusati

Abstract
. Recent innovations in artificial intelligence (AI), primarily powered by large language models (LLMs), have transformed how programmers develop and maintain software—leading to new frontiers in software engineering (SE). The advanced capabilities of LLM-based programming assistants to support software development tasks have led to a rise in the adoption of LLMs in SE. However, little is known about the evidenced-based practices, tools and processes verified by research findings, supported and adopted by AI programming assistants. To this end, our work conducts a preliminary evaluation exploring the beliefs and behaviors of LLM used to support software development tasks. We investigate 17 evidence-based claims posited by empirical SE research across five LLM-based programming assistants. Our findings show that LLM-based programming assistants have ambiguous beliefs regarding research claims, lack credible evidence to support responses, and are incapable of adopting practices demonstrated by empirical SE research to support development tasks. Based on our results, we provide implications for practitioners adopting LLM-based programming assistants in development contexts and shed light on future research directions to enhance the reliability and trustworthiness of LLMs—aiming to increase awareness and adoption of evidence-based SE research findings in practice.

2.1.1.
LLM-Based Programming Assistants

Large language models are deep learning models trained on large amounts of data, such as books, articles, websites, and source code
(
Ozkaya 2023
)

(

as tools powered by LLMs that have capabilities to support assistance in programming and software development tasks. For this project, we explore the capabilities of a variety of LLM-based programming assistants (see Section
2.2

2.1.2.

We define

(

beliefs
and
behaviors

2.1.3.
LLM-Generated Belief

We define an LLM-generated belief as an assumption made in natural language responses from systems powered by generative AI with regard to empirically verified research-based concepts. While there are numerous distinct uses of the concept of ‘belief’ in AI across many philosophical and cognitive science domains
(
Hadley 1991
)
, we take the syntactically defined logic-based “belief as ‘what one could rapidly discover”’ approach, presented by Hadley as follows:

Agent
X
believes [sentence]
S
if and only if
S
is in
X
’s belief base, or
X
could prove
S
, using the belief base and a logic
L
, within time
T
belief base.
(
Hadley 1991
, p. 66)

This definition incorporates the capabilities of modern LLMs—which are trained on information provided in a large belief base, but have the ability to generate new knowledge in a fixed amount of time—as opposed to other approaches (
i.e.,
belief as “degrees of confidence”). Further, this definition of belief regarding AI can impact human reasoning
(
Hadley 1991
)
.

2.1.4.
LLM-Generated Behavior

We define an LLM-generated behavior as the capability of generative AI-based systems to adopt or neglect beneficial practices related to empirical SE claims. Prior work suggests LLMs exhibit social behavior during interactions with humans
(
Leng and Yuan 2023
)
. However, most LLM-based assistants are
black box
systems, based on opaque or hidden information and algorithms
(
von Eschenbach 2021
)
. In particular for SE-related tasks, the methods by which output is generated is unknown—leading to major consequences such as insecure
(
Khoury et al. 2023
)
or incorrect
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
code. In this work, we aim to understand if LLM-based programming assistants have capability to behave in accordance with empirically supported research practices. To observe this, we ask the systems to provide insight on their ability to adopt evidence-based SE tools and processes when generating and analyzing provided code to support software development tasks.

3.
Methodology

To answer our research questions, we analyzed AI-driven programming assistants to observe their “beliefs” and “behaviors” regarding various claims related to software development practices—inspecting LLM-generated output against empirically validated research findings.

3.1.
Data Collection

To observe the beliefs and behaviors of LLM-based programming assistants, we prompted each AI tool presented in Section
2.2
. Prompting is a key feature of conversational LLMs where users submit natural language-based queries, or prompts, and receive generated responses in a natural language format
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)

(
White et al. 2023
)
. To gain insight into LLM-based programming assistants’ beliefs and behaviors regarding empirical SE research claims, we used zero-shot prompting to prompt each system once without providing examples or additional clarification
(
DAIR.AI 2024
)
. All LLM-based programming assistant data was collected between April and June 2023. The complete list of prompts and LLM-generated responses for this research are available in an online repository
(
Brown and Cusati 2024
)
.

3.1.1.
RQ1: Collecting LLM-Generated Beliefs

We observed natural language responses to prompts regarding empirical SE findings from the LLM-based programming assistants used in our evaluation to determine their beliefs. To prompt each AI programming assistant, the claims (see Table
1
) were transformed into questions to ask each AI programming assistant platform to provide a response for each item.For example, “Code quality (defect occurrence) depends on which programming language is used” was input as “Does code quality (defect occurrence) depend on which programming language is used?” (C1). We initially prompted AI systems to provide a 5-point Likert response from one (Strongly Disagree) to five (Strongly Agree) for each claim–however, after several attempts of using this approach we observed the AI programming assistants would generate arguments for each Likert rating without selecting a level of agreement. Thus, we transitioned to prompting with yes or no questions to observe LLM-generated beliefs. This approach still provided ambiguous responses, which we incorporate into our analysis (see Section
3.2.1
).

3.1.2.
RQ2: Deriving Evidence for LLM-Generated Beliefs

As software developers are adept to blindly accept recommendations from LLMs
(
Kammerath 2024
)
, examining the evidence for the beliefs of LLM-based programming assistants is crucial to assess generated output for SE activities. To examine how LLMs evince their beliefs, we analyzed the responses for LLM-generated beliefs and prompted AI assistants to understand how they came up with responses to claims about beliefs. Following each prompt to derive LLM-generated beliefs on the empirical research claims studied (see Section
3.1.1
and Table
1
), we prompted “How did you arrive at your answer?” to gain further insight into how the LLMs explored in this study form the basis of their responses to questions regarding empirical SE research claims.

3.1.3.
RQ3: Collecting LLM-generated Behaviors

To understand the capabilities of LLM-based programming assistants with regard to empirically supported behaviors, we prompted systems to understand whether they adopt practices based on SE research claims in their code generation and analysis processes. In particular, we used C5 (commenting code), C9 (unit testing), C13 (asserts), C14 (static analysis tools), C15 (coding standards), C16 (code reviews), C17a (debugging tools), and C17b (print-statement debugging). This consisted of asking the agents if they adopt certain behaviors when generating code or performing tasks on provided code. Examples include “Can you use static analysis tools to improve generated code?” (C14) and “Can you use debugging tools to debug provided code?” (C17). In certain instances, we only modified the prompt to specify the behavior was directed toward the LLM when responses were not applicable (
e.g.,
for C14, changing “Can you use static analysis tools to improve generated code?” to “Can ChatGPT use static analysis tools to improve generated code?”). These responses were analyzed to understand if LLMs can adopt evidence-based practices while supporting software development tasks.

3.2.
Data Analysis

To analyze the collected LLM-generated beliefs, evidence for beliefs, and behaviors, we use a mixed methods approach.

3.2.1.
RQ1: Beliefs

To analyze the overall empirical beliefs of LLM-based programming assistants, an initial inspection was performed on generated responses from each LLM observed in our study. The goal of this process was to produce a high-level categorization of “Yes” (Y), “No” (N), “Ambiguous” (A), or “Not Applicable” (N/A)—indicating whether the LLM agreed with, disagreed with, or provided a noncommittal (
i.e.,
did not make a clear stance by integrating both yes and no into responses) response. For example, a
yes
categorization for C14 with respect to belief signifies the text output from an LLM agreed that static analysis tools improve code quality. Sample categorized responses are provided in Appendix
A
. We measured
ambiguous
responses since we do not re-prompt LLMs to obtain a definitive answer, maintaining a consistent zero-shot approach for LLMs in our evaluation. For our initial evaluation, the authors performed a closed coding of the 85 responses to categorize LLM-generated beliefs—individually rating responses based on the above criteria, then meeting to discuss respective ratings and resolve disagreements. We use Cohen’s Kappa (
\kappa
) to measure inter-rater agreement, observing “fair agreement” (
\kappa=0.398
)
(
Landis and Koch 1977
)
. Most disagreements arose coding A with Y (
n=12/26
) and N (
n=10/26
) responses,
e.g.,
as LLM replies often began positively but ended vaguely.

To further investigate the evidence-based beliefs of LLMs, we observed several quantitative metrics. We calculate the rate of correct responses (
✓%
) for beliefs (belief = Y) to determine if LLM-based programming assistant beliefs align with empirical SE findings. We measure the rate of correctness across generative AI systems to investigate the beliefs of each LLM and across each SE research claim to observe which findings are most upheld by LLMs. We also calculate the variance for each empirical SE claim observed in our study to determine which beliefs and behaviors are the most controversial among the evaluated LLM-based programming assistants. To calculate the variance, the initial categorizations were coded to numerical values, reclassifying
yes
to 1,
noncommittal
to 2, and
no
to 3
(
Kaplan 2020
, Chapter 3.2)
. Then, we calculate the variance of responses (
\sigma^{2}
) using the formula shown in Equation

3.2.2.
RQ2: Evidence

To answer RQ2, we inspected the
original response
from LLMs based on given prompts to gain insight on generated statements regarding beliefs. We were particularly interested in whether or not LLMs provided evidence to support their responses. To measure this, we examined the LLM-generated belief responses to examine the text for evidence—or resources and content to support their answers. The first author scanned responses for links to and clear mentions of sources of evidence. For links, this involved observing the content of the provided source and the goals of the hosting website. Based on this analysis, the evidence was then categorized into one the following types: (1)
research papers
published in academic venues; (2) opinionated
blogs
by individuals or groups; (3) posts shared on
social media
; (4)
training resources
such as tutorials, documentation, and materials for learning; (5) posts on
Q&A
platforms; (6)
tech reports
summarizing non-academic and unpublished studies from industry; and (7) LLM-generated
code examples
.

Further, we further prompted LLM-based programming assistants to understand how they arrived at their responses to questions regarding empirical SE research claims (see Section
3.1.2
). The follow-up questions allowed us to gain insight into the evidence used to support responses for each LLM-generated belief from a
prompted response
. We leveraged an open coding thematic analysis approach to inspect the responses provided by LLMs
(
Braun and Clarke 2012
)
. The responses to the 17 follow-up questions focusing on evidence for each research claim were examined by the two authors, with each independently evaluating the output to investigate sources of evidence for responses. The concepts used to label the data were derived directly from the content of responses. After developing initial codes, the researchers segmented and grouped responses into categories based on common patterns and recurring themes. We observed four themes described as sources of evidence for LLMs: research, training data, it’s own reasoning, and previous conversations in the chat. We report the evidence from original and prompted responses in Section
4.2
with our results.

3.2.3.
RQ3: Behaviors

To analyze the behavioral capabilities of LLM-based programming assistants, we used a similar closed coding approach described in Section
3.2.1
. We categorized responses regarding behavior as Y, N, A, or N/A to indicate whether or not LLMs are able to adopt practices supported by empirical SE research. For instance, a
no
categorization for C14 with respect to behavior indicates an LLM cannot use static analysis tools to examine generated code. Example categorizations are presented in Table
5
in Appendix
A
. The authors performed a closed coding on the 40 responses regarding the LLM-generated behaviors, individually rating responses before resolving disagreements. We observed “substantial agreement” (
\kappa=0.780
) between the raters’ categorizations
(
Landis and Koch 1977
)
.

We also conducted quantitative analyses to understand the capabilities of LLM-based programming assistants. We calculated the rate of correctness (
✓%
) to indicate LLMs are able to adopt the observed behaviors (behavior = Y). We report the LLM able to adopt the most evidence-based behaviors, and the behavior that is most capable of being adopted by LLMs. For behavior, we exclude C17b concerning print-statement debugging. While we are interested in if LLMs are capable of using print statements to support debugging efforts, this is not considered best practice and was removed from the measurement of correct behavior. Further, we observe the rate inconsistency (
I
) for empirical SE research claims between the beliefs of LLM-based programming assistants. That is, instances where the output from LLMs supports empirical SE research claims (belief = Y), but the model itself reports not adopting the corresponding practice when automating SE-related tasks (behavior = N). Finally, we calculate the variance of responses (
\sigma^{2}
) by translating the behavioral categories (Y, N, and NC) to numbers as described in Section
3.2.1
and using Equation
1
.

(1)

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}

x_{i}

3.3.
Threats to Validity

There are several threats to the validity of our research findings.

Threats to External Validity:

Our study is based on only five AI-based programming assistants. We attempted to mitigate this threat by analyzing systems based on generative AI models trained on varying data and offered by different organizations (
i.e.,
OpenAI, Google, Blackbox AI, and Anthropic). We also utilize different access methods, obtaining LLM-generated output through IDE plugins and online chat interfaces. In addition, we examined a limited number (
n=17
) of empirical SE research claims. The claims we analyzed, except for C17, were motivated by prior work exploring the software engineer beliefs of empirically verified research
(
Devanbu et al. 2016
)
. To enhance the generalizability of this project, future work can explore using additional LLM-driven systems (
i.e.,
Devin
(
Wu 2024
)
or watsonx
(
wat [n. d.]
)
), incorporating advanced versions of the models used in this evaluation (
i.e.,
GPT-4o), and investigating beliefs and behaviors for more claims supported by findings from the empirical SE research community.

Threats to Construct Validity:

Our constructs of an “LLM-generated belief” and “LLM programming assistant behaviors” are flawed, as AI systems in and of themselves cannot believe and behave—as their output is based on the data the models are trained on. While our methods for collecting the beliefs and behaviors of LLMs have yet to be supported by experimental results, we use these terms for “technical convenience, and not as an analysis of ‘real belief”’
(
Hadley 1991
, p. 66)
. In particular for behavior, since we do not have knowledge of the inner workings of LLM-based programming assistants, our analysis is solely focused on the self-reported behaviors derived through analyzing natural language output from the systems. Further, to analyze behaviors we only analyze the output from LLMs in responses to prompts. In addition, further inspection and more in-depth knowledge of specific models is needed to understand whether LLM-based programming assistants do actually adopt these practices in support of development tasks. For RQ2, the types of evidence categories for the original responses have opportunities for overlap (
i.e.,
Q&A posts on social media, blogs summarizing research papers, etc.). We inspected the evidence content and goals of hosting website, when applicable, to assign unique categories to each resource.

Threats to Internal Validity:

Our data collection and analysis methods for LLM-generated beliefs, evidence, and behaviors have limitations. LLM-based chatbots, such as ChatGPT and Gemini, can perform differently in different chat sessions and have biased results based on previous discussions
(
Morris 2024
)
. Similarly, studies show re-prompting LLMs with the same prompt can generate different responses
(
Ouyang et al. 2023
)
. In this preliminary evaluation, we use zero-shot prompting for each LLM—observing initial responses by prompting once without follow-up. This can limit our results, as pre-trained models may not be specifically trained for our task
(
Ramlochan 2024
)
. Additional techniques such as one- or few-shot prompting
(
Kadam and Vaidya 2020
)
, Chain-of-Thoughts prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, other finetuning of instructions
(
Wei et al. 2021
)
, and changing temperature values
(
Renze and Guven 2024
)
could impact our results. LLMs also have the ability to hallucinate, or confidently provide false information in responses
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
. For instance, we observed Copilot note “As an AI, I don’t execute or run code”—when in fact, it can
(
cop [n. d.]
)
.

The manual qualitative analysis used to categorize the beliefs, evidence, and behaviors of LLMs can incorporate bias. Converting categorical data to numerical values for variable calculations can limit our results
(
Kaplan 2020
)
. One researcher categorizing types of sources for evidence can also be biased. We also only report if evidence exists, but do not assess the quality of evidential sources. The closed coding analyses inhibit our evaluation, as open coding thematic analysis approach could provide more insight on the LLM-generated beliefs and behaviors. Our analysis approach was used due to the exploratory and preliminary nature of this work, and future work is needed to further analyze the beliefs and behaviors of LLMs regarding empirical SE research claims. Further, our collected and analyzed data are available in a public repository to support the verifiability and replicability of our work
(
Brown and Cusati 2024
)
.

4.
Results

This section presents our findings on the evidence-based beliefs, evidence, and behaviors of five LLM-based programming assistants observed in our methodology to answer our research questions.

4.1.
RQ1: LLM-Generated Beliefs

Our results regarding the beliefs of LLM-based programming assistants are presented in Table
2
. The majority of responses (
n=55
, 64.7%) supported the evidence-based claims explored in our study. We observed a limited number (
n=9
, 10.6%) of negative responses disagreeing with research claims and noted 22 ambiguous responses (25.9%). We provide further insights based on the most positive, negative, and controversial beliefs as well as the accuracy of LLMs.

Most Supported Beliefs.

All of the LLM-based programming assistants agreed that evidence-based practices such as unit testing (C9), assert statements (C13), static analysis tools (C14), coding standards (C15), and code reviews (C16) enhance code quality and benefit software projects. In addition, each LLM agreed with the claims that more complex code leads to more defects (C11) and quality varies based on project context (C12). These indicate evidence-based claims supported by empirical SE research findings that LLMs agree are valuable for enhancing software development in practice.

Least Supported Beliefs.

Several claims supported by empirical SE research had no positive claims. For instance, all LLMs provided ambiguous responses for the claims that statically typed languages have fewer bugs (C6) and stronger code ownership leads to higher quality software (C8). In addition, no LLM agreed that merge commits are buggier than other types of commits (C8). Moreover, we observed C8 and C2, the claim that fixing bugs is riskier than adding new features, received the most negative responses from LLM-generated output (
n=3
). Thus, despite research evidence in support of these claims, they are not promoted by LLMs commonly used for software development tasks.

Most Controversial Belief

We define the most controversial belief as the research claim with the highest variance, indicating the highest disagreement between the LLM-based programming assistants evaluated in our study. Our results show the claim that fixing defects
is more likely to cause defects than adding new features (C2) was the most controversial (
\sigma^{2}=0.96
), incurring three negative responses (ChatGPT, Copilot, and Gemini) and two positive responses (Blackbox AI, Claude). The negative responses directly opposed this claim—stating, for instance, “in general, fixing defects is often perceived as less risky than adding new features” (ChatGPT) and “Fixing defects is generally considered less risky because you’re dealing with existing code…Adding new features inherently has more risk due to the introduction of entirely new code and its interaction with the existing system” (Gemini). However, research suggests the opposite trend
(
Shihab et al. 2012
;
Mockus and Weiss 2000
)
. For instance, prior work suggests bug fixing changes introduced on Fridays in source code for Mozilla and Eclipse had a higher chance of introducing new bugs
(
Śliwerski et al. 2005
)
. On the other hand, positive responses provided reasons for bug fixing being riskier, such as unintended consequences in defect repair (
i.e.,
“Defects are often interconnected, and fixing one defect can have a ripple effect, causing other defects to emerge. This can lead to a cascade of fixes, each with its own set of risks”, Blackbox AI), and arguments for adding new features being less risky (
i.e.,
“ adding new features typically involves working with a more isolated and well-defined scope, which can be easier to design, implement, and test thoroughly”, Claude).

Most Accurate LLMs

Overall, we found Blackbox AI and Claude were the most accurate LLMs with regard to the empirical SE research findings evaluated in our study—providing positive responses for 13 of the 17 claims (76%). Both models also did not respond negatively to any evidence-based claims, while providing ambiguous responses to four. The models were in agreement on all claims except two. Claude was the only LLM to clearly state well-commented code can reduce defect occurrence (C5). Blackbox AI responded positively to experienced programmers produce code with fewer defects (C10), but Claude was ambiguous. To understand the rationale for these responses, more information is needed on the training data and AI models powering these LLM-based programming assistants.

Least Accurate LLMs

Alternatively, we observed ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot were the least accurate LLMs observed in our study, responding positively to only nine claims (53%). Copilot was the most incorrect model, responding negatively to the most evidence-based claims (
n=4
, 23.5%), disagreeing that code quality depends on programming language (C1), fixing defects is more likely to cause bugs than adding new features (C2), specific project experience decreases bugs compared to general programming experience (C4), and merge commits are buggier than other types of commits (C8). ChatGPT also responded negatively to C2, C4, and C8, but provided a positive response to C1. However, we found ChatGPT, along with Gemini, were the most noncommittal LLMs in responses (
n=5
, 29.4%). These models consistently provided ambiguous responses by sharing both reasons to agree with and disagree with empirical research claims, not taking a clear stance in either direction. The inaccuracies of ChatGPT and Copilot could point to a limitation of the underlying model, as both are powered by OpenAI’s GPT.

4.3.
RQ3: LLM-Generated Behaviors

Our findings on the behaviors of LLM-based programming assistants are presented in Table
4
. Overall, we found LLMs do not inherently adopt evidence-based practices (
n=25
, 71.4%), only receiving eight positive responses (22.9%). Google Gemini was the only system to provide ambiguous responses for behaviors (
n=2
, 5.7%), claiming it did not know whether the specific behavior could be adopted or not. For example, in response to C14 Gemini replied “There’s a good chance Gemini leverages static analysis tools in some way to improve the quality of generated code for the end user, but we can’t say for sure without official confirmation from Google”. We provide further observations on the most and least adopted practices, as well as the LLMs most capable and incapable of adopting the studied research-based claims.

Most Adopted Behavior

We found most LLMs observed in our study can apply coding standards to generated code. For example, GitHub Copilot responded “I follow a set of coding standards and best practices to generate high-quality code”. Output from ChatGPT noted specific coding standards, stating “I follow language-specific coding standards and guidelines, such as Python’s PEP 8, JavaScript’s ESLint rules, or Java’s coding conventions, to ensure consistency and compatibility with the ecosystem”. Claude was the only LLM not able to use coding standards. Prior work emphasizes the value of coding standards, noting their ability to reduce errors and improve software quality
(
Boogerd and Moonen 2009
;
Popić et al. 2018
)
.

Least Adopted Behaviors

No LLMs were intrinsically able to adopt code reviews and debugging tools to improve AI-generated code quality. Code review processes usually involve human input, as noted in LLM responses stating, “Code reviews are a human-centric process where developers examine and discuss code for functionality, efficiency, and adherence to best practices” (Gemini) and “as an AI, I do not actually have the capability to generate code, conduct code reviews, or directly participate in software development activities myself” (Claude). Regarding debugging tools, there were varied responses provided across the LLMs. Gemini reported its lack of development environment and limited code execution prevent it from using debuggers “in the same way a programmer would”. Claude also reported “As an AI assistant without access to an actual programming environment, I cannot execute, inspect or step through code using debugging utilities”. Thus, future work is needed to examine how these processes can be incorporated into LLM-generated output for software development tasks.

Most Capable LLM

We found ChatGPT was the most capable LLM-based programming assistant, reporting the ability to adopt three out of the seven (42.9%) observed evidence-based behaviors: commenting code (C5), using assert statements (C13), and adhering to coding standards (C15). The system also reported being able to use print-statements for debugging purposes (C17b), which was not considered an evidence-based practice in this work. Regarding the behavioral prompt for C5, ChatGPT replied “Yes, I can add code comments to reduce defects in generated code…By incorporating comments effectively into generated code, I can help improve its quality, maintainability, and understandability”. However, as we only inspected output from LLMs to assess their behavior, further work is needed to investigate if LLM-based programming assistants, in particular ChatGPT, do in fact adopt these practices while generating code for users.

Least Capable LLM

We observed Claude was not able to adopt any of the evidence-based behaviors observed in our study. Every response in Claude’s output included a similar version of the templated response: “As an AI assistant, I do not have the ability to generate, write, or manage any code directly” and “My role is limited to providing information to you based on my training data”. Similar responses occurred across other LLMs, noting an inability to adopt particular behaviors because they are an AI—however, Claude was the only LLM to always reply in this manner. As such, Claude was also the most inconsistent regarding discrepancies between beliefs and behaviors (
n=6
).

5.
Implications

AI programming assistants have shown to be effective for generating code, however our results show that LLM-based programming assistants lack beliefs and behaviors in accordance with empirical SE research. We found most LLMs have ambiguous beliefs about research findings, lack evidence to support their claims, and are unable to adopt evidence-based behaviors to support software development in practice. Based on these findings, we provide implications for software developers adopting LLM-based programming assistants in their development workflows.

LLMs have mostly positive perceptions of evidence-based practices, but can be noncommittal.

We observed the majority of responses from LLMs agreed with the SE research claims in our study (see Table
2
). Over half of the LLM-generated beliefs were positive, with unanimous agreement with the findings more complex code results in more defects (C11), factors affecting code quality vary between projects (C12), static analysis tools improve end user quality (C14), coding standards improve software quality (C15), and code reviews improve software quality (C16). However, developers utilizing these systems for SE tasks should note they can be noncommittal on important development concepts. For instance, two LLM-based programming assistants provided ambiguous responses on the claim that well-commented code has fewer defects (C5), stating “this correlation is not absolute” (ChatGPT) and “components with more unit tests do not necessarily have fewer customer-found defects” (Blackbox AI). Thus additional measures should be taken to mitigate ambiguous responses from LLMs. For example, prior work suggests vague responses from ChatGPT can be overcome by refining prompts, increased understanding of the model’s reasoning, providing feedback and examples, and using external tools or plugins to enhance communication with ChatGPT
(
jen 2023
)
.

LLM-generated responses lack credible evidence

Moreover, for claims that LLM-based programming assistants do agree with, the large majority do not provide evidence to support their beliefs. We found most LLMs claim to use research, but do not provide specific research findings which they base their responses on. Further, LLMs frequently use their own reasoning and training data to inform responses (see Table
3
), but these are often unknown to users and can lead to unsubstantiated and misleading responses
(
jen 2023
;
bla [n. d.]
)
. For instance, every prompted response from GitHub Copilot indicated its responses are generated based on the data it was trained on. Only one LLM-based programming assistant (Blackbox AI) consistently provided evidence to support claims made by the generative AI model—but, these were largely from non-scientific sources (see Figure
1
). While the evidential support for responses in Blackbox AI is a valuable feature, we observed several complications including duplicated references, missing citations, and broken links. The evidence provided by Blackbox AI and Claude could contribute to their accuracy in LLM-generated beliefs. However, the discovered issues and lack of evidence for other LLMs assistants should caution practitioners using their output to support development tasks and motivate future work to enhance evidenced-based responses from generative AI in SE contexts.

LLMs can adhere to coding standards

We found most LLM-based programming assistants observed in our study can apply coding standards (C15) to generated code. This aligns with prior work suggesting AI is effective for teaching elegant coding styles to students
(
Woodrow et al. 2024
)
. For instance, responses included “By incorporating coding standards into the generation process, I strive to produce high-quality code that meets industry best practices and standards, ultimately improving the overall software quality and developer experience” (ChatGPT), “As an AI developed by OpenAI, I follow a set of coding standards and best practices to generate high-quality code” (Copilot), “By being influenced by coding standards, Gemini can generate code that is more consistent, easier to understand, and potentially less error-prone” (Gemini), and “Yes, Blackbox AI uses coding standards to improve the software quality of generated code…Blackbox AI’s use of coding standards is essential in improving the software quality of generated code” (Blackbox AI). In addition, the LLMs we observed also unanimously agreed that using coding standards is a beneficial practice for improving software quality. Thus, while we observed inconsistent adoption of behaviors across the LLMs observed in our study, our findings show they can be useful for formatting generated code to adhere to specific coding standards.

LLMs cannot adopt evidence-based behaviors, but can be used to support them

LLM-based programming assistants are increasingly used in software development activities
(
Russo 2024
)
. However, our findings indicate these systems are not able to adopt many practices supported by empirical SE research—even when they perceive these practices as beneficial to software development (see Table
4
). While these systems cannot adopt evidence-based practices, many generative AI models reported being able to assist with useful practices supported by empirical SE research. For example, ChatGPT does not use static analysis tools to assess generated code (C14), but can “help you understand how they work and how you can use them to improve the quality of generated code”. Similarly, multiple LLMs reported they cannot add comments to generated code (C5), but stated, “If you’d like to explore ways to improve the readability of your generated code, I can provide some tips on best practices for code structure and naming conventions” (Gemini) and “While Blackbox AI can generate code, it does not directly add code comments to reduce defects in the generated code. However, Blackbox AI can provide suggestions for comments based on the code’s functionality and structure” (Blackbox AI). Similarly, while LLMs cannot actively conduct code reviews and use debugging tools, all five reported having the ability to assist developers in completing code review and debugging tasks in some capacity. Thus, LLM-based programming assistants are capable of providing insight and resources to help developers improve evidence-based practices.

LLMs cannot adopt evidence-based behaviors, but can be instructed to do so

In our approach, we used zero-shot prompting with no examples or clarifications provided to understand the beliefs and behaviors of LLM-based programming assistants. However, the output of some tools reports incorporating specific instructions and details into input, or finetuning instructions, can enhance the abilities of LLMs to incorporate empirically-supported practices. For example, in response to several behaviors (C5, C9, and C13), GitHub Copilot responded that it does not inherently apply these practices to generated code, yet added “However, I can certainly [include comments, help create unit tests, include asserts] if instructed to do so”. Similarly, Gemini replied “While Gemini itself might not use asserts directly, it can generate code that facilitates their use during the development process” (C13) and ChatGPT noted “If you have specific coding standards or guidelines you’d like me to adhere to, feel free to specify them, and I’ll ensure that the generated code complies with them” (C15). Thus, providing specific instructions for LLMs to incorporate useful behaviors in prompts can improve the behavior of these systems, enhancing the credibility of the generated output.

LLM-generated beliefs are similar to human beliefs

The evidence-based claims used for our study were derived from previous work exploring the beliefs of human software engineers at Microsoft
(
Devanbu et al. 2016
)
. We do not directly compare human and LLM beliefs due to differences in the rating scales used (Y/N/A vs. 5-point scale). However, at a glance we observe similar trends in the beliefs of LLMs and humans. For instance, the four least controversial beliefs for humans—code reviews (C16), coding standards (C15), static analysis tools (C14), and assert statements (C13)—were all unanimously agreed on by the LLMs observed in our study. Likewise, four of the claims with the most disparity in disagreement between humans—code quality depends on language (C1), fixing defects is riskier than adding new features (C2), and specific project experience matters more than general programming experience (C4)—also were the most varied among the LLM-based programming assistants. Thus, this motivates the need for future research to enhance evidence-based software development beliefs and practices for LLMs and human software engineers.

8.
Conclusion

Software engineering is a complex activity that relies on practitioners to develop, maintain, and innovate software-based systems. To support software development, empirical research has offered a variety of tools and practices supported by scientific evidence to enhance the development and maintenance of software. More recently, large language models (LLMs) have transformed the development and maintenance of software—receiving rapid attention and adoption for their ability to automatically support a wide variety of SE-related tasks. However, little is known about these AI-driven systems with regard to evidence-based practices purported by empirical software engineering research. This project analyzes the beliefs and behaviors of popular LLM-based programming assistants regarding empirically validated research claims. Our findings show that LLM-generated beliefs are ambiguous, not providing a clear stance on the benefits or detriment of evidence-based practices. Moreover, we show LLM-based programming assistants are unable to adopt useful practices supported by research into code generation and analysis tasks in software development. In light of these findings, this work provides implications for software engineers using LLM-based programming assistants in software development contexts and provides future research directions to enhance the beliefs and behaviors of evidence-based practices in software engineering.
