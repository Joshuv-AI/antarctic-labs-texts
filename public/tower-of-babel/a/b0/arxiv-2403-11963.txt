Abstract
. We study the fundamental problem of transfer learning where a learning algorithm collects data from some source distribution P P but needs to perform well with respect to a different target distribution Q Q . A standard change of measure argument implies that transfer learning happens when the density ratio d ​ Q / d ​ P dQ/dP is bounded. Yet, prior thought-provoking works by Kpotufe and Martinet (COLT, 2018) and Hanneke and Kpotufe (NeurIPS, 2019) demonstrate cases where the ratio d ​ Q / d ​ P dQ/dP is unbounded, but transfer learning is possible. In this work, we focus on transfer learning over the class of low-degree polynomial estimators . Our main result is a general transfer inequality over the domain ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , proving that non-trivial transfer learning for low-degree polynomials is possible under very mild assumptions , going well beyond the classical assumption that d ​ Q / d ​ P dQ/dP is bounded. For instance, it always applies if Q Q is a log-concave measure and the inverse ratio d ​ P / d ​ Q dP/dQ is bounded. To demonstrate the applicability of our inequality, we obtain new results in the settings of: (1) the classical truncated regression setting, where d ​ Q / d ​ P dQ/dP equals infinity, and (2) the more recent out-of-distribution generalization setting for in-context learning linear functions with transformers. We also provide a discrete analogue of our transfer inequality on the Boolean Hypercube { − 1 , 1 } n \{-1,1\}^{n} , and study its connections with the recent problem of Generalization on the Unseen of Abbe, Bengio, Lotfi and Rizk (ICML, 2023). Our main conceptual contribution is that the maximum influence of the error of the estimator f ^ − f ⋆ \hat{f}-f^{\star} under Q Q , I max ​ ( f ^ − f ⋆ ) \mathrm{I}_{\max}(\hat{f}-f^{\star}) , acts as a sufficient condition for transferability; when I max ​ ( f ^ − f ⋆ ) \mathrm{I}_{\max}(\hat{f}-f^{\star}) is appropriately bounded, transfer is possible over the Boolean domain. The main techniques for proving our results are the Carbery-Wright anti-concentration inequality and the invariance principle of Mossel, O’Donnell and Oleszkiewicz (Annals of Mathematics, 2010).

Introduction

Transfer learning is a pivotal concept in Statistics and Machine Learning that addresses the challenge of applying knowledge gained from one domain (the
source
) to improve performance in a different, yet related domain (the
target
). This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where the distribution of data in the target domain, denoted as
Q
, differs from that of the source domain, characterized by distribution
P
. A rich body of research, including the works by
[
HK19
,
HKM23
,
YHC13
,
RMH+20
,
TJJ20
,
BDBC+10
,
BDBCP06
,
PMW22
]
, and others, has extensively explored transfer learning from various angles, underscoring its theoretical and practical significance. This concept, also known as Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization or Domain Adaptation, is crucial for understanding how algorithms can be trained on data from the source domain and then adeptly applied to the target domain. The quest to pinpoint the exact nature of distributional similarity that facilitates successful transfer remains a central, yet not well-understood, inquiry in this domain.

The concept of transfer learning can be encapsulated within the framework of a standard regression problem. Here, our goal is to identify a
regressor

f
that aims to minimize the mean squared error
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]
, using samples in the form
(x,y)
, where
x
is drawn from distribution
P
and
\mathbb{E}[y|x]=f^{\star}(x)
. The twist in transfer learning emerges when the objective shifts towards minimizing the expected error
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]
, despite the fact that our available samples are drawn from
P
. This raises the pivotal question: is it possible to establish a relationship between the expected errors
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]
? In essence, this inquiry delves into the capability of the regressor
f
to
extrapolate
effectively to samples that are out-of-distribution, originating from the target distribution
Q
, based on its training with the source distribution
P
.

f\approx f^{*}
with probability
1-o(1)

This simple and intuitive idea has inspired a line of work that explores transfer learning assuming an upper bound on some norm of the likelihood ratio

This naturally leads us to question the validity of the assumption that the ratio
\|dQ/dP\|_{\infty}
remains within a bounded range. Unfortunately, this ratio can potentially be unbounded in many relevant scenarios such as: (i) truncation and censoring, which are fundamental challenges in Statistics, with historical roots tracing back to the works of Galton
[
Gal98
]
and Pearson
[

]
; (ii) the innovative Generalization On The Unseen framework proposed by
[

]
; (iii) straightforward distribution shifts like translation, exemplified by the transition from a uniform distribution

Due to the many instances where the ratio

Is transfer learning possible when

This question is further inspired by insightful findings from Kpotufe and Martinet
[

]

[

]
, within the framework of nonparametric classification. These studies highlight scenarios in which the ratio

1.1.
Summary of Contributions

In this paper, we consider the expressive and well-studied class of
low-degree polynomials
. Our main results are general transfer inequalities, for functions in this class, under mild assumptions on the distribution pair
P
and
Q
, going well beyond the boundedness of the ratio
dQ/dP
. In fact, we can almost always bound the error with respect to
Q
as a function of the error with respect to
P
.

Transferability of Low-degree Polynomials in the Euclidean Domain.

When the domain of the covariates
x
is the
n

Informal Theorem 1

(See

For any pair of degree-

(1.2)

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]\leq C_{d}\cdot\left\lVert\frac{dP}{dQ}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]\,,

where
C_{d}>0

(1.3)

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]\leq C_{d}\cdot\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}\left\lVert\frac{dQ}{d\mu}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert\frac{dP}{d\mu}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}]\,,

where the infimum is over the set
\mathcal{L}
of log-concave distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
P
,
Q
are arbitrary

Let us give some explanation of the results of the Theorem above.

Inequality (
1.2
) (The importance of
dP/dQ
):

If we compare (
1.2
) with (
1.1
) we observe the surprising fact that in (
1.2
) the
“inverse” ratio

dP/dQ

arises. Comparing the boundedness assumption on

with the naive assumption that

is bounded, our inequality proves something perhaps surprising. Even in cases where (i) the support of the source

does not contain that of

is bounded,
the error with respect to

Q
for low-degree polynomials.

Inequality (
1.3
):

Combining (
1.2
) with the naive bound of (
1.1
), we get inequality (
1.3
). This inequality allows us to show transferability even between distributions

and

completely disjoint supports

Furthermore, the term

defines a novel quantitative notion of “transfer difference” between

and

Transferability of Low-degree Polynomials in the Boolean Domain.

Next we focus on discrete domains and in particular on the Boolean domain
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. In this setting, we need some additional assumptions than just that
f
,
f^{\star}
being polynomials. The main reason is that any function over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
can be expressed as a polynomial in the Fourier(-Walsh) basis and transferability can be easily shown not to hold for all functions.

Given our inequality in the continuous domain, one may wonder if transfer holds as long as we focus on low-degree Boolean functions.
However, it is not difficult to see that the low-degree assumption is not anymore sufficient for transfer learning in discrete domains. For instance, let us consider the (shifted) dictator (in direction
i=1
) function
f(x)=x_{1}+1
, which is a low-degree non-negative polynomial,
Q
be the uniform distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
and
P
be the uniform distribution on the subset of the Boolean hypercube with
\{x_{1}=-1\}.
It is evident that
\mathbb{E}_{P}f=0,
while
\mathbb{E}_{Q}f=1
,
which means that transfer from samples of
P
to samples of
Q
does not hold, i.e., no inequality of the form
\mathbb{E}_{Q}f^{2}=O(\mathbb{E}_{P}f^{2})
is possible.

A reader familiar with Boolean Fourier analysis may realize that a potential underlying issue with the dictator function is its
high influence
4
4

4

Recall that for a function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
the influence
\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)
in direction

i

the value of
f
is flipped as well. For instance, for
f(x_{1},...,x_{n})=x_{i}
, we have that
\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)=1
and
\mathrm{Inf}_{j}(f)=0,j\neq i
.
This notion of sensitivity naturally extends to functions

Section

3
.
(in direction
i=1)
; it is in fact easy to construct more counterexamples with other Boolean functions of high influence in some direction.
This perhaps suggests that the maximum influence of low-degree functions over all directions

could be an important parameter for general transfer. Focusing on low-degree Boolean functions, we actually show that having a high influence in some direction is the

for transferability in the Boolean domain.

2.
Transfer Learning for Low-Degree Polynomials in the Euclidean Domain

2.1.
Notation & Definitions

We start with some definitions that we need in order to state our main Theorem in this section.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
P,Q
be two probability distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
P(x)
(resp.
Q(x)
) be the probability density function of
P
(resp.
Q)
evaluated at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define

\left\|\frac{dP}{dQ}\right\|_{\infty}\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\in[1,\infty]\,,

and, for real
\alpha\geq 1
,

D_{\alpha}(P\parallel Q)\triangleq\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\,.

Definition 2.2
.

A distribution is called log-concave if its density is of the form
\propto\exp(-f)
, where
f
is convex. Let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of all log-concave distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.2.
Main Inequality

We are now ready to state our main result for the Euclidean domain.

Theorem 2.3

(Transferability of Polynomials)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the space of log-concave probability distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Consider two probability distributions
P,Q
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a degree-
d
polynomial.
There exists an absolute constant
C
such that for
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty]
with
1/\alpha+1/\beta=1
, it holds that

(2.1)

\mathbb{E}_{Q}|f|\leq(Cd)^{d}2^{d\beta}\cdot\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}D_{\alpha}(Q\parallel\mu)D_{\alpha}(P\parallel\mu)^{\beta d}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{P}|f|^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}\,.

In particular, if
\alpha=\infty
, we get that

(2.2)

\mathbb{E}_{Q}|f|\leq(2Cd)^{d}\cdot\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}\left\|\frac{dQ}{d\mu}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\frac{dP}{d\mu}\right\|_{\infty}^{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}|f|\,.

If
Q
is log-concave, then the above simplifies to the following.

Corollary 2.4
.

Consider two probability distributions
P,Q
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
Q
is log-concave. Assume that
\mathrm{supp}(P)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(Q)
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a degree-
d
polynomial. Then, for any
\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty]
with
1/\alpha+1/\beta=1
, it holds that

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}_{Q}|f|\leq(Cd)^{d}2^{d\beta}\cdot D_{\alpha}(P\parallel Q)^{\beta d}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{P}|f|^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}\,.

In particular, if
\alpha=\infty
, we get that

(2.4)

\mathbb{E}_{Q}|f|\leq(2Cd)^{d}\cdot\left\|\frac{dP}{dQ}\right\|_{\infty}^{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}|f|\,.

Our
Theorem

2.3
, and in particular
Corollary

2.4
, perhaps unintuitively, propose that considering the inverse ratio
\|dP/dQ\|_{\infty}
allows for transfering when working with
low-degree polynomials
. For the proof of
Theorem

2.3
, we refer to
Section

7.1
and for
Corollary

2.4
to
Section

7.2
.

Theorem

2.3
allows us to transfer even in situations where both ratios are infinity.
Consider, for instance, the simple example of distribution shift from source
P=\mathcal{U}([0,1])
to target
Q=\mathcal{U}([2,3])
. Our
Theorem

2.3
proposes that we can use an intermediate log-concave measure
\mu
that “covers” both
P
and
Q
(e.g.,
\mu=\mathcal{U}([0,3])

and obtain transfer results at a cost that scales with
\|dQ/d\mu\|_{\infty}\cdot\|dP/d\mu\|_{\infty}^{d}
, where
d
is the degree of the polynomial. This, perhaps counter-intuitive, phenomenon follows from the property of polynomials
f
in the Euclidean space, that the probability density of the random variable
f(x)
does not contain point masses when
x
is drawn from a log-concave distribution.

In
Appendix

B
, we compute such transfer ratios for various
(P,Q)
pairs.
An interesting example is
the setting where both
P
and
Q
are standard Gaussians with different means in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(translation). One can show that the ratio
\|dQ/dP\|_{\infty}
scales exponentially with the distance of the two means but, in the contrary, for
\|dQ/d\mu\|_{\infty}\|dP/d\mu\|_{\infty}
there exists a log-concave distribution
\mu
so that the product scales polynomially with the means’ distance.

3.
Transfer Learning for Low-Degree Polynomials in the Boolean Domain

In this section, we will provide a similar transfer
inequality for discrete domains.
For simplicity, we will work in the Boolean domain
\{-1,1\}^{n}
.

3.1.
Quick Boolean Analysis Background

We start with some necessary background. For a function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we will write
\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}c_{S}\chi_{S}
for its Fourier decomposition, where
\chi_{S}
(resp.
c_{S})
is the parity function (resp. the Fourier coefficient) on
S\subseteq[n]
.
We will say that
f
is of degree-
d
if
c_{S}=0
for any
|S|>d
.
Recall also that for
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
, the influence of
f
in direction
i\in[n]
under
\mathcal{U}(\{-1,1\}^{n})
is defined as

\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)=\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{U}(\{-1,1\}^{n})}[f(x)\neq f(x^{\sim i})],

where
x^{\sim i}
is
x
with
x_{i}
flipped. This definition can be extended when the image of
f
is not Boolean and when the underlying measure is product but potentially not uniform.
For general
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and product measure
P=\otimes_{i\in[n]}P_{i}
over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, we have

\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[\mathrm{Var}_{x_{i}\sim P_{i}}(f(x))].

In the special case where
P=\mathcal{U}(\{-1,1\}^{n})
, we also have

\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)=\sum_{S\ni i}c_{S}^{2}
.
For further details on Boolean functions, see
[
O’D14
]
.

One of our main tools in this section is the Invariance Principle of
[
MOO05
]
. This result is a generalization of the Berry–Esseen CLT (in fact, the invariance principle is an analogue of the Berry–Esseen theorem for low-degree polynomials) and, informally, states that the CDF of a low-degree Boolean
function with small influences can be approximated by that of the same function when replacing the input variable by a Gaussian vector.

Proposition 3.1

(Invariance Principle
[
MOO05
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},...,X_{n}
be independent random variables satisfying that

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
,

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]=1
and

\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{3}]\leq\beta
.
Let
f(x)=\sum_{S}c_{S}\chi_{S}
be a degree-
d
multilinear function such that

\sum_{|S|>0}c_{S}^{2}=1
and

\max_{i}\sum_{S\ni i}c_{S}^{2}\leq\tau
. Then

\sup_{t}\left|\Pr[f(X_{1},...,X_{n})\leq t]-\Pr[f(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})\leq t]\right|\leq O(d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})\,,

where
Y_{i}
are independent standard Gaussians.

We will refer to the additive approximation factor

c\cdot d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d}

as the
invariance principle gap
, where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

3.2.
Main Inequality

We proceed with our main transfer inequality for the Boolean domain.
Recall that in the Euclidean setting, the structure of the function class (polynomials of low-degree) was crucial for the argument. In the Boolean domain,
any
function is essentially a polynomial in the Fourier basis.
Hence, we cannot expect that every function with low-degree Fourier spectrum will transfer. To this end, we give a sufficient condition for transfer. This condition essentially requires that the function has also small
influence
in any direction.
In particular, the maximum influence of the function should be roughly upper bounded by the inverse of the mass of the seen part of the Boolean hypercube.

Theorem 3.2

(Transferability of Boolean functions)
.

Let

Q
be a product probability distribution supported on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[x_{i}]=0,\mathbb{E}_{Q}[x_{i}^{2}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[|x_{i}|^{3}]\leq\beta
for any
i\in[n]
.
Let
S\subseteq\{-1,1\}^{n}
be the seen part of the Boolean hypercube and
let
P=Q_{S}
be the distribution
Q
conditioned on the seen subset
S
. Let
f(x)=\sum_{S}c_{S}\chi_{S}

be a degree-
d
multilinear function
with bounded maximum influence, i.e.,
\max_{i}\sum_{S\ni i}c_{S}^{2}\leq\tau
and unit variance, i.e.,
\sum_{S:|S|>0}c_{S}^{2}=1.

For some universal constant
c
, assume that
Q(S)
is larger than the invariance principle gap, i.e.,

(3.1)

Q(S)\geq c\cdot d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d}\,.

Then, it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f^{2}]\leq d^{O(d)}Q(S)^{-2d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}[f^{2}]\,.

For the proof, we refer to
Section

8
.
In the domain
\{-1,1\}^{n}
,
\tau
corresponds to the maximum influence of
f,
i.e.,
\tau=\max_{i}\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)
. Our condition states that
Q(S)
should be at least of order
d\tau^{1/d}
, where
d
is the degree of
f
. For any measure
Q
, this reveals an interesting connection between the complexity of
f
(degree and max-
Q
-influence) and the structure of the seen part (mass under
Q
). For instance, for
Q
being the uniform distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, we have that dictator functions require that
Q(S)=1
(anything must be seen).
We refer the reader to
Section

5
for an extensive discussion on GOTU in Boolean domains and connections with the work of
[
ABLR23
]
.

Applications in the Euclidean Domain

We next employ our
Theorem

2.3
in two application domains. In
Section

4.1
we study truncated statistics and in
Section

4.2
we study in-context transfer learning.

4.1.
Truncated Statistics

Our first example comes from the literature of truncated statistics
[
Gal98
,
Coh91
,
DGTZ18
]
.
We can view learning from truncated samples as a transfer learning problem with source and target distributions defined as follows.

4.1.1.
General Transfer Learning for Truncated Gaussians

One of the most canonical examples in truncated statistics is learning Gaussians in
n
dimensions from truncated examples. In more detail, let us consider the target distribution
Q=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
but assume only sample access to the source probability distribution

P=\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu,\Sigma)
, where

\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu,\Sigma;x)=\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma;x)1\{x\in S\}}{\int_{S}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma;y)dy}\,,

where
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
corresponds to the truncation set. This is a standard example where
the ratio
\frac{dQ(x)}{dP(x)}
can be infinite, i.e., for
x\not\in S,

\frac{dQ(x)}{dP(x)}=\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma;x)}{\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu,\Sigma;x)}=\infty
. On the other side, the opposite ratio

\frac{dP(x)}{dQ(x)}
, on which our main inequality is based, can be easily proven to be always finite when
S
is “full-dimensional”, in the sense
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma;S)=\int_{S}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma;y)dy>0
; more precisely, it holds
\|\frac{dP}{dQ}\|_{\infty}=O(1/\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma;S))
.

Assume sample access to a truncated normal
\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})

and let

\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star};S)\geq\alpha
for some
\alpha>0
. Consider any estimator
\mu
of
\mu^{\star}
which is trained using arbitrarily many samples from
\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})
. It is natural to expect that in many settings,
\mu
can achieve a small mean-square error (MSE) performance (also known as prediction performance) against a fresh sample from the truncated Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})
.
Using
Theorem

2.3
, we can derive that this
automatically
translates to a non-trivial upper bound on the MSE against a fresh sample from the
non-truncated
Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})
.

To be more precise, the MSE of any estimate
\mu
of
\mu^{\star}
with respect to the truncated density is equal to

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})}[\|y-\mu\|^{2}]\,.

Since
f(y)=\|y-\mu\|^{2}
is a non-negative degree-2 polynomial in the variables of
y
and the (non-truncated) normal distribution is a log-concave measure, we can apply our
Theorem

2.3
to show that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})}[\|y-\mu\|^{2}]\leq O(1/\alpha^{2})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})}[\|y-\mu\|^{2}]\,.

Additionally, using a vanilla change of measure we have that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})}[\|y-\mu\|^{2}]\leq(1/\alpha)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})}[\|y-\mu\|^{2}]\,.

The above expression relates multiplicatively the MSE of
\mu
with respect to the target
Q=\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})
to that of the source
P=\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},\Sigma^{\star})
. The term
1/\alpha^{2}
appears since we transfer a degree-2 polynomial and the density ratio, as discussed above, is
O(1/\alpha)
. We highlight that (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) hold
for all truncations

S
with mass at least
\alpha,
and
for all measurable estimators

\mu
.

Remark 4.1
.

We now discuss how our equations (
4.1
), (
4.2
) compare with the recent relevant works on learning a Gaussian distribution in truncated statistics
[
DGTZ18
,
KTZ19
]
. To simplify this comparison we are going to assume that
\Sigma^{\star}=I
. In
[
DGTZ18
,
KTZ19
]
the authors construct estimators
\hat{\mu}
from “truncated” samples drawn from
\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},I)
that have the property that the distance
\|\mu^{\star}-\hat{\mu}\|
is small, and in particular goes to zero as the number of samples goes to infinity, i.e.,
\hat{\mu}
is consistent. This in turn implies that the non-truncated error satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},I)}[\|y-\hat{\mu}\|^{2}]\leq\mathrm{OPT}+o(1),

where
\mathrm{OPT}
is the minimum achievable MSE with respect to
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},I)
. We note that the estimators from
[
DGTZ18
,
KTZ19
]
achieving this error guarantee are quite sophisticated and carefully exploit the knowledge of the truncation
S
.

Our equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) show that one can control the “non-truncated” MSE with respect to
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},I)
in an immediate way: as long as one has any bound on the “truncated” MSE with respect to
\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},I)
(so any control on the “standard” testing error for the samples received), the “non-truncated” MSE is then provably within a multiplicative factor of
O(1/\alpha^{2})
from the “truncated” MSE. Notably these all hold for
any estimator
\mu
.

For example, as a corollary, if we choose
\tilde{\mu}
to minimize the MSE with respect to the truncated data this already implies a non-trivial upper bound on the non-truncated MSE; in particular we have that
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\star},I)}[\|y-\tilde{\mu}\|^{2}]\leq O(1/\alpha^{2})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(\mu^{\star},I)}[\|y-\tilde{\mu}\|^{2}]\leq O(1/\alpha^{3})\cdot\mathrm{OPT}
(here we used (
4.1
) in the first inequality and (
4.2
) together with the optimality of
\widetilde{\mu}
in the second). This in turn can be translated to a guarantee of the form
\|\mu^{\star}-\tilde{\mu}\|\leq\mathrm{poly}(1/\alpha)
. Although this estimator is not consistent, it achieves a non-trivial error
without the need to know
S
at all
. Similar estimators
\tilde{\mu}
with
\mathrm{poly}(1/\alpha)
error (without knowing
S)
are already found useful as a warm start to build more sophisticated estimators, as in
[
DGTZ18
,
KTZ19
]
, and we can upper bound the error of such
\tilde{\mu}
by a simple application of our transfer inequality.

4.1.2.
General Transfer Learning for Truncated Regression

Our next goal is to employ the idea that we used to derive (
4.1
) to bound the error of regression problems from truncated samples.

Definition 4.2
.

Consider the setting where we observe
arbitrary covariates
x^{(1)},...,x^{(N)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and labels
y_{i}=f^{\star}(x^{(i)})+\varepsilon_{i}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for some model
f^{\star}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
belonging to some class of functions
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a measurable subset of the real line.
In the truncated setting, we observe the pair
(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})
only if
y^{(i)}\in S
, by sampling
y^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1).

Remark 4.3
.

We note that the special case of
Definition

4.2
where
f^{\star}
is a linear function
f_{w}^{\star}(x)=w^{\top}x
with parameters
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has been recently studied in
[
DGTZ19
]
.

Let us assume that we have sample access to a truncated regression problem, as in
Definition

4.2
, with true model
f^{\star}
and truncation set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We will show that transfer learning between the truncated samples and the non-truncated samples is statistically possible under very mild assumptions. To do so, we will use our main transfer inequality to relate the truncated MSE of some model
f
, aiming to estimate
f^{\star}
, to the non-truncated MSE of
f
.

Let us now rephrase the problem in the transfer learning terminology.
Consider target distribution
Q=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathcal{N}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1)
and source distribution
P=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathcal{N}_{S}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1)
.
The “truncated” MSE of a model
f
is defined as

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1)}[(y-f(x^{(i)}))^{2}]\,

and the “non-truncated” MSE of
f
is similarly

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1)}[(y-f(x^{(i)}))^{2}]\,.

Using our
Theorem

2.3
, we can obtain the following result.

Corollary 4.4

(Transfer in Truncated Regression)
.

Consider the truncated regression setting
y_{i}=f^{\star}(x^{(i)})+\varepsilon_{i},i\in[N]
of
Definition

4.2
with true model
f^{\star}
and truncation set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Let us set
\mu_{i}\triangleq f^{\star}(x^{(i)})
.
Assume that the truncation set
S
has mass at least
\alpha>0
with respect to all
\mu_{i},i=1,\ldots,N
i.e.,
\min_{i\in[N]}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},1;S)\geq\alpha
.

Then, for any model
f,
it holds that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1)}[(y-f(x^{(i)}))^{2}]\leq\left(\frac{C}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}_{S}(f^{\star}(x^{(i)}),1)}[(y-f(x^{(i)}))^{2}]\,,

for some absolute constant
C>0.

The above inequality allows us to
relate multiplicatively the “truncated” MS’ of an arbitrary prediction model
f
of the true model
f^{\star}
to the MSE of
f
over the whole non-truncated measure.

For the proof, we refer to
Section

A.1
.
We mention that
[
DGTZ19
]
established a similar inequality to show that transfer learning is possible under the assumption that
f^{\star}
is linear
5
5

5

Also, the assumption that the set
S
has mass with respect to any Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},1)
can be weakened under some structural properties for the family
\mathcal{F}
. For instance, for linear functions it suffices to consider a lower bound on the average of the masses (see Assumption 1 in
[
DGTZ19
]
).
. The above result applies much more generally and, in particular, note that the exact details of the function
f^{\star}
do not play a role: if
f^{\star}
is linear or a degree-100 polynomial, the “transfer coefficient” is still simply
C/\alpha^{2}.

Remark 4.5
.

We should mention that the above result, does not discuss the (non-trivial) details on how to efficiently find an estimator that has in fact small MSE under the truncated measure. While this is beyond the scope of this work, we would like to mention that for many natural families of parameterized regression functions
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{w}:w\in\mathcal{W}\}
, the truncated log-likelihood is concave with respect to
w
and, under some mild assumptions, one can design a first-order method to optimize it efficiently and obtain a small truncated MSE. For instance, such an analysis is provided by
[
DGTZ19
]
for the case of linear functions
f_{w}(x)=w^{\top}x
.

4.2.
In-Context Transfer Learning

Transformers
[
VSP+17
]
and in-context learning (ICL) have been extensively explored and studied recently
[
VONR+23
,
ASA+22
,
GTLV22
,
LIPO23
,
BCW+23
,
ACDS23
,
SIS21
]
, mainly because of their applications in text generation via large language models (LLMs).
In-context learning
[
GTLV22
]
corresponds to the ability of a model to condition on a prompt sequence consisting of
in-context examples (input-output pairs corresponding to some task) along with a new query input, and
generate the corresponding output.

4.2.1.
Background on ICL

We give the formal setting below.

Definition 4.6

(Class and Distributions)
.

We consider a feature space
\mathcal{X}
and a label space
\mathcal{Y}
. We let
H\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a concept class of functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
. We let
P_{X}
be a distribution over the feature space
\mathcal{X}

and
P_{H}
be a distribution over functions in
H
.

For our application, we will consider
H
to be the set of linear functions
\{h(x)=w^{\top}x:w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. As an example,
P_{X}
could be the standard normal distribution and
P_{H}
could generate a random linear function
x\mapsto w^{\top}x
by drawing
w
from some probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 4.7

(Training/Testing In-Context Samples)
.

A realizable training sample or
training prompt
of length
M\in\mathbb{N}
from
(P_{X},P_{H})
corresponds to a random sequence
(x_{1},y_{1},...,x_{M},y_{M})\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{M}
generated as follows.

(1)

We first draw a concept
h\sim P_{H}
.

(2)

Independently of
h
, we draw
x_{1},...x_{M}
i.i.d. from
P_{X}
.

(3)

We set
y_{i}=h(x_{i})
for any
i\in[M]
and return
(x_{1},y_{1},...,x_{M},y_{M})
.

A realizable testing sample or
testing prompt
of length
N\in\mathbb{N}
from
(P_{X},P_{H})
corresponds to a random sequence
(x_{1},y_{1},...,x_{N},y_{N},x_{\mathrm{query}})\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{N}\times\mathcal{X}
generated as follows.

(1)

We first draw
h\sim P_{H}
.

(2)

Independently of
h
, we draw
x_{1},...x_{N},x_{\mathrm{query}}
i.i.d. from
P_{X}
.

5.

In this section, we discuss an application of

Let us first formally introduce the GOTU setup of

Let us consider

5.1.
A General Transfer Inequality from Seen to Unseen

Let

be uniformly supported on the seen part

Our

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}[f^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{U}(S)^{O(d)}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{S}}[f^{2}]

One may wonder how (

Analysis of Results

Let us now comment on

Observe that polynomial regression learns everything inside the dotted box, which is completely observable and

Let us now explain the connection between our Inequality (

) with the extrapolation properties of polynomial regression in

Hence, our main inequality (

\mathbb{E}_{Q}(f-p)^{2}\leq C_{d}\cdot\|dP/dQ\|_{\infty}^{2d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}(f-p)^{2}.

The true function
f^{\star}
generating
Figure

1
(and
Figure

2
) is not a polynomial so it is perhaps not immediately clear that doing polynomial regression on samples from

should be enjoying nice transfer learning properties. Yet, thanks to

being smooth on a bounded domain transfer is still happening. To see this, notice that
f^{\star}
can be well approximated by a polynomial

there is a

be the output of gradient descent on the polynomial regression task. If

is a

-degree polynomial. Therefore (as long as no

Now the trained ReLU network greatly fails to enjoy such nice extrapolation properties in our example. We note that this observed inability of ReLU networks to extrapolate, and in particular, the absence of a similar transfer inequality like (

Proof of

(i)

for all

(iii)

corresponds to the distribution

of the Boolean hypercube, i.e.,

for some set

We have that

\mathbb{E}_{P}f^{2}\geq\gamma\cdot\left(1-\Pr_{P}[f^{2}\leq\gamma]\right)=\gamma\cdot\left(1-\Pr_{P}\left[\left(\sum_{S}c_{S}\chi_{S}\right)^{2}\leq\gamma\right]\right)\,.

Hence, it suffices to upper bound the above right-hand side probability term. Since
Q
is supported on the Boolean hypercube, the ratio
\|dP/dQ\|_{\infty}<\infty
and we can proceed as follows:

\Pr_{P}\left[\left(\sum_{S}c_{S}\chi_{S}\right)^{2}\leq\gamma\right]\leq\left\|\frac{dP}{dQ}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\Pr_{Q}\left[\left|\sum_{S}c_{S}\chi_{S}\right|\leq\sqrt{\gamma}\right]\,.

Next we remark that over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, we have that

g=|f|=\sum_{S}|c_{S}|\chi_{S}
is a multilinear function of degree
d
and so, by the invariance principle (cf.
Proposition

3.1
) applied on

and

\Pr_{x\sim Q}\left[g(x)\leq\sqrt{\gamma}\right]\leq\Pr_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left[g(y)\leq\sqrt{\gamma}\right]+O(d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})\,.

This means that

\Pr_{x\sim Q}\left[f(x)^{2}\leq\gamma\right]\leq\Pr_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left[f(y)^{2}\leq\gamma\right]+O(d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})\,.

Next, we can apply the Carbery-Wright inequality (cf.
Proposition

7.1
) and get that

\Pr_{x\sim Q}\left[f(x)^{2}\leq{\gamma}\right]\leq\frac{d\gamma^{1/2d}}{(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}f(y)^{2})^{1/2d}}+O(d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})\,.

Observe that

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}[f(y)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\left[\sum_{|S|\leq d,|T|\leq d}c_{S}c_{T}\chi_{S}(y)\chi_{T}(y)\right]=c_{\emptyset}^{2}+\sum_{|S|\leq d}c_{S}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[f(x)^{2}]\,,

and so

\Pr_{x\sim Q}\left[f(x)^{2}\leq{\gamma}\right]\leq\frac{d\gamma^{1/2d}}{(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}f(x)^{2})^{1/2d}}+O(d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})\,.

We now have to optimize over
\gamma:
we pick

\gamma=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[f(x)^{2}]/d^{O(d)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\|dP/dQ\|_{\infty}}-O(d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})\right)^{2d}.

Note that
(i) since
P
is the seen part of

, we have that
\|dP/dQ\|_{\infty}=1/Q(S)
, and
(ii) we have, by assumption, that

is at least the invariance gap.
These two properties imply that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f^{2}]\leq\frac{d^{O(d)}}{(Q(S)-c_{1}d\beta^{1/3}\tau^{1/8d})^{2d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}[f^{2}]\,,

for some constant
c_{1}>0
,
which completes the proof.
∎

9.
Conclusion

In this work, we show general transfer inequalities for low-degree polynomials in the Euclidean domain. We apply this inequality in order to obtain new results in truncated statistics and in-context transfer learning for linear attention layers. We also provide an extension of this inequality to the Boolean domain and study its connections with the GOTU setup of
[
ABLR23
]
.

So far, the theory of statistics and statistical learning theory have been traditionally focusing on three axes:
sample complexity
(how many examples are required to learn the class),
expressivity
(what type of functions can be expressed by the class) and
training time
(how much computation is required to learn the class). In all three axes, multiple beautiful and structural results have been proven. We believe that
transferability
(i.e., which distribution shifts do not deteriorate generalization) is also a crucial criterion for the statistician. Our work demonstrates that general and structural statistical results can also be proven in this axis, paving the way for hopefully more to come. For instance, we present a few intriguing future directions.

(1)

Whether transferability holds or not is a property of (a) the class of estimators used, (b) the pair of source and target distributions
P
and
Q
,
and
, (c) the training algorithm. While our work focus solely on (1) an (2), understanding the ability
of transfer learning as an
algorithmic property
(i.e., focusing on (3) apart from solely (1) and (2)) is an interesting direction for future work.

(2)

A natural question is whether similar inequalities can be obtained beyond regression, for
classification settings
. It is rather clear that the answer is, in general, no; consider the simple example of learning a threshold function
h^{\star}(x)=\mathbb{1}\{x>t^{\star}\},x\in\mathbb{R}
with source distribution
P
and target
Q
; if
\mathrm{supp}(Q)=[t^{\star}-1,t^{\star}+1]
and
\mathrm{supp}(P)=[t^{\star}+c_{1},t^{\star}+2c_{1}]
for
c_{1}>1
then
any algorithm cannot distinguish
h^{\star}
from the function
h=1
using the source labeled examples and hence
cannot
do better than random guessing in the target distribution. Yet, it would be interesting if, for instance, one could use our inequalities in the context of polynomial regression in agnostic PAC learning by leveraging the connection between regression and classification. As an example of this connection, we refer to the work of
[
KSV23
]
and specifically their “transfer lemma” (Lemma 2.8).

(3)

We establish in
Theorem

2.3
that general transfer is possible in the continuous domain for polynomial functions. Can we generalize our result beyond polynomial functions? Interestingly, our experiments suggest ReLU neural networks fail to enjoy such transfer properties, but is this a structural truth or does something else in our experiment lead to this failure? From a mathematical standpoint this quest is also non-trivial, as our proof crucially uses the Carbery-Wright inequality which is proven solely for polynomial functions. We believe exploring more this direction is interesting both from a mathematical and applied point of view.

(4)

Finally, our
Theorem

3.2
deepens the connections between transferability and key notions from Boolean Fourier analysis. We prove that as long as the maximum influence of the error of an estimator is sufficiently small, the estimator enjoys general transfer properties. The result naturally arises from the invariance principle from Boolean Fourier analysis. From a mathematical standpoint, the maximum influence condition is also necessary for transfer inequalities to hold (e.g., the Dictator function provides a counterexample). Yet, is the maximum influence the key measure that explains in practical scenaria when a given estimator will enjoy desired transfer properties or not? Our results do not answer this instance-specific question and we believe this is a very interesting future direction to explore.

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)~\Big|f(x,w)\geq\gamma\Big]\Pr_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)\geq\gamma\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\gamma\Pr_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)\geq\gamma\Big]\,.

\mathbb{E}_{(x,w)\sim P}[f(x,w)]\geq\gamma\left(1-\Pr_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)\leq\gamma\Big]\right)\,.

It suffices to obtain an upper bound on the probability mass of the event
\{(x,w)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\otimes N+2}:f(x,w)\leq\gamma\}
under
P
.
Under distribution shift from
P_{H}
to
\mu
, we have that

\Pr_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)\leq\gamma\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1}}\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mu}\left[\frac{P_{H}(w)}{\mu(w)}~1\{f(x,w)\leq\gamma\}\right]\,.

We can upper bound this quantity as follows:

(A.4)

\Pr_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)\leq\gamma\Big]\leq\|dP_{H}/d\mu\|_{\infty}\left(\Pr_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1},w\sim\mu}\Big[f(x,w)\leq\gamma\Big]\right)\,.

Since
x
and
w
are independent and the product of two log-concave densities is log-concave, we can apply
Proposition

7.1
in (
A.4
), by picking
q=d
(the degree of

, and get that

(A.5)

\Pr_{(x,w)\sim P}\Big[f(x,w)\leq\gamma\Big]\leq\|dP_{H}/d\mu\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{Cd\gamma^{1/d}}{\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1},w\sim\mu}[f(x,w)]\right)^{1/{d}}}\right)\,.

We choose
\gamma
so that

\|dP_{H}/d\mu\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{Cd\gamma^{1/d}}{\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1},w\sim\mu}[f(x,w)]\right)^{1/d}}\right)=1/2\,,

which implies, since
d
is some absolute constant, that there exist some universal positive constants

so that

\gamma=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1},w\sim\mu}[f(x,w)]}{C\|dP_{H}/d\mu\|_{\infty}^{c}}\,.

Using this choice we can return to (
A.3
) and get that, for some absolute constants

,

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1},w\sim P_{H}}[f(x,w)]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{X}^{\otimes N+1},w\sim\mu}[f(x,w)]}{C\|dP_{H}/d\mu\|_{\infty}^{c}}\,.

We can now complete the proof using (
A.2
), which gives that

\mathbb{E}_{(x,w)\sim Q}[f(x,w)]\leq C\cdot\left\|\frac{dQ_{H}}{d\mu}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\frac{dP_{H}}{d\mu}\right\|_{\infty}^{c}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(x,w)\sim P}[f(x,w)]\,.

By minimizing over
\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
, the proof for the task shift (Item (2)) is complete. The proof for Item (3) is similar.
∎

Appendix B
Transfer Coefficient Examples

In this section, we give a couple of examples to better understand the transfer coefficient of
Theorem

2.3
.

We first consider the setting where both
P
and
Q
are standard Gaussians with means
0
and
\mu
respectively in the single-dimensional setting. We show that the ratio
\|dQ/dP\|_{\infty}
scales exponentially with the distance of the two means. In the contrary when we consider the ratio
\inf_{\nu}\|dQ/d\nu\|_{\infty}\|dP/d\nu\|_{\infty}
the scaling drops to polynomial, where the infimum runs over log-concave measures.

Lemma B.1

(Transfer Coefficient for Gaussians in 1D)
.

Consider target distribution
Q=\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
and source distribution
P=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
where
\mu\neq 0
.
Then it holds that

\|dQ/dP\|_{\infty}=\Omega(\exp(\mu^{2}))

but
there exists a log-concave probability distribution
\nu
such that

\|dQ/d\nu\|_{\infty}\cdot\|dP/d\nu\|_{\infty}=1+O(\mu^{2}).

Proof.

We have that

\|dQ/dP\|_{\infty}=\Omega(\exp(\mu^{2}/2))
by setting
x=\mu
.
Without loss, assume that
\mu>0.

Consider the function

f(x)=1\{x\leq 0\}\mathcal{N}(0,1;x)+1\{x\in(0,\mu)\}\mathcal{N}(0,1;0)+1\{x\geq\mu\}\mathcal{N}(\mu,1;x)\,,

where
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1;x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^{2}/2}
.
Clearly,
f
is not a density and, in order define the desired log-concave distribution
\nu
so we have to compute the value

Z_{\nu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)dx=1+\int_{0}^{\mu}\mathcal{N}(0,1;0)dx=1+\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,.

Let us consider the measure
\nu
with density
f/Z_{\nu}
.
It remains to argue about the value of each density ratio.
We start with the case
d\mathcal{N}(0,1)/d\nu.

•

If
x\leq 0
, we have that

\frac{d\mathcal{N}(0,1)}{d\nu}(x)=Z_{\nu}=1+\mu/\sqrt{2\pi}\,.

•

If
x\geq\mu
, we have that

\frac{d\mathcal{N}(0,1)}{d\nu}(x)=Z_{\nu}\cdot\frac{\exp(-x^{2}/2)}{\exp(-(x-\mu)^{2}/2)}\,.

Since we take the supremum over
x\geq\mu
, we observe that the numerator
\exp(-x^{2}/2)
decays much faster than the denominator
\exp(-(x-\mu)^{2})
in the interval
(\mu,\infty)
and so the density ratio in this regime is upper bounded by
1+\mu/\sqrt{2\pi}.

•

Finally, if
x\in(0,\mu)
, then

\frac{d\mathcal{N}(0,1)}{d\nu}(x)=Z_{\nu}\cdot\exp(-x^{2}/2)\,.

This means that taking the supremum over
(0,\mu)
we get a density ratio upper bounded by
1+\mu/\sqrt{2\pi}
.

The cases for
\mathcal{N}(\mu,0)
are symmetric. Hence in total we have that the transfer coefficient

\|d\mathcal{N}(0,1)/d\nu\|_{\infty}\|d\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)/d\nu\|_{\infty}=O((1+\mu)^{2})\,.

We remark that the function
\phi(x)=1\{x\leq 0\}x^{2}+1\{x\geq\mu\}(x-\mu)^{2}
is continuous and convex and so
\nu
is log-concave.
∎

We can also extend this construction in high dimensions.

Lemma B.2

(Transfer Coefficient for Gaussians in High Dimensions)
.

Consider target distribution
Q=\mathcal{N}(\mu,I)
and source distribution
P=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|\mu\|_{2}>0
.
Then
there exists a log-concave probability distribution
\nu
such that

\|dQ/d\nu\|_{\infty}\cdot\|dP/d\nu\|_{\infty}=O((1+\|\mu\|_{2})^{2}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality let us assume that
\mu=\gamma e_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma>0.

Let us define the Gaussian density
\mathcal{N}(\mu,I;x)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\exp(-\|x-\mu\|_{2}^{2}/2)
.
We consider the mapping

f(x)=1\{x_{1}<0\}\mathcal{N}(0,I;x)+1\{x_{1}>\gamma\}\mathcal{N}(\mu,I;x)+1\{x_{1}\in(0,\gamma)\}\mathcal{N}(0,I;(0,x_{-1}))\,,

where
(0,x_{-1})
corresponds to the
n
-dimensional vector
x
with its first coordinate fixed to 0. In order to make
f
a density function that corresponds to a distribution
\nu
, we have to normalize with the integral

Z_{\nu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f=1+\int_{x_{1}=0}^{\gamma}\int_{x_{-1}}\mathcal{N}(0,I;(0,x_{-1}))dx=1+\gamma/\sqrt{2\pi}\,.

Let us take
\nu
be the log-concave distribution with density
f/Z_{\nu}
.
Then using a similar case analysis as in the 1-dimensional proof of
Lemma

B.1
, we get that the transfer coefficient is of order

\|d\mathcal{N}(0,I)/d\nu\|_{\infty}\|d\mathcal{N}(\mu,I)/d\nu\|_{\infty}=O((1+\|\mu\|_{2})^{2})
.
∎

We remark that the way we design the distribution
\nu
in the above lemma can be adapted to more general log-concave distributions
P
and
Q
where
Q
is a translated version of
P
and
P
is a product measure.

Lemma B.3

(Transfer Coefficient for Translated Log-concave Products)
.

Consider a source distribution
P
with density
P(x)=\prod_{i\in[n]}\phi_{i}(x_{i})
, where each
\phi_{i}
is log-concave with mode at 0 and
\phi_{i}(0)=\Theta(1)
and let
Q
be a translation of
P
in direction
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then there exists a log-concave probability distribution
\nu
such that

\|dP/d\nu\|_{\infty}\|dQ/d\nu\|_{\infty}=O((1+\|\mu\|_{2})^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\mu=\gamma e_{1},\gamma>0
. Then define

f(x)=1\{x_{1}<0\}P(x)+1\{x_{1}>\gamma\}P(x-\gamma e_{1})+1\{x_{1}\in(0,\gamma)\}P(0,x_{-1})
, where
(0,x_{-1})
corresponds to the
n
-dimensional vector
x
with its first coordinate fixed to 0. In order to make
f
a density function we have to normalize with the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f=1+\int_{x_{1}=0}^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(0)\int_{x_{2}}\phi_{2}(x_{2})\cdots\int_{x_{n}}\phi_{n}(x_{n})=1+\int_{x_{1}=0}^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(0)=1+\Theta(\gamma)
. The result follows by a similar case analysis as in
Lemma

B.1
.
∎

We mention that the above results can be extended for translations of log-concave densities over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are not necessarily products. As a simple ilustration, consider
P=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
and
Q=\mathcal{N}(\gamma e_{1},\Sigma)
for some arbitrary full-rank
\Sigma
and
\gamma>0
. Then, we can consider

f(x)=1\{x_{1}<0\}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma;x)+1\{x_{1}>\gamma\}\mathcal{N}(\gamma e_{1},\Sigma;x)+1\{x_{1}\in(0,\gamma)\}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma;(0,x_{-1}))
, where
(0,x_{-1})
corresponds to the
n
-dimensional vector
x
with its first coordinate fixed to 0.
In order to make
f
a density function we have to normalize with the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f=1+\int_{x_{1}=0}^{\gamma}\int_{x_{-1}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma;(0,x_{-1}))dx
. Note that

\ \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma;(0,x_{-1}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\mathrm{det}(\Sigma)}}e^{-x_{-1}^{\top}(\Sigma^{\prime})^{-1}x_{-1}}\,,

where
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
is obtained from
\Sigma
by deleting the first row and column. This matrix is still symmetric and PSD and so induces an
(n-1)
-dimensional Gaussian distribution. Hence, we have that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f=1+\gamma\cdot\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{n-1}\mathrm{det}(\Sigma^{\prime})}{(2\pi)^{n}\mathrm{det}(\Sigma)}}=1+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{det}(\Sigma^{\prime})}{\mathrm{det}(\Sigma)}}\,.

Let
\lambda_{i}
(resp.
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}
) be the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\Sigma
(resp.
\Sigma^{\prime}
) and
\lambda_{1}>0
.
By the Eigenvalue Interlacing Theorem (since
\Sigma^{\prime}
is a principal submatrix of
\Sigma
), we know that

\frac{\mathrm{det}(\Sigma^{\prime})}{\mathrm{det}(\Sigma)}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{i+1}}\leq 1/\lambda_{1}
. Hence, for non-degenerate Gaussian distributions, we get that the partition function
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f=1+O(\gamma)
. Similar analysis as in the above lemmata gives that the log-concave distribution with density
f/\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f
satisfies
\|d\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)/d\nu\|_{\infty}\|d\mathcal{N}(\gamma e_{1},\Sigma)/d\nu\|_{\infty}
scales as
(1+c\gamma)^{2}
for some constant
c
, which depends on the covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

A similar results can be established for translations of general log-concave distributions.

Remark B.4

(Transfer for Families of Target Distributions)
.

It is interesting to study scenarios where the target distribution
Q
is not fixed but belongs to some class of distributions. For instance,
consider
a family of target distributions
\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,I):\mu\in\mathcal{W}\}
for some
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and source distribution
P=\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},I)
for some
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then it holds that
the cost of transfer learning from
P
to the family
\mathcal{Q}
scales as
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{W}}(1+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{2})^{2}
, since for any distribution in
\mathcal{Q}
, there exists a (potentially different) log-concave distribution that achieves a transfer coefficient of order at most
\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{W}}(1+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{2})^{2}
.
