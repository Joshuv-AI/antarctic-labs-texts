Evaluating the Performance of Large Language Models for SDG Mapping (Technical Report)
Hui Yin, Amir Aryani, Nakul Nambiar

Abstract
The use of large language models (LLMs) is expanding rapidly, and open-source versions are becoming available, offering users safer and more adaptable options. These models enable users to protect data privacy by eliminating the need to provide data to third parties and can be customized for specific tasks. In this study, we compare the performance of various language models on the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) mapping task, using the output of GPT-4o as the baseline. The selected open-source models for comparison include Mixtral, LLaMA 2, LLaMA 3, Gemma, and Qwen2. Additionally, GPT-4o-mini, a more specialized version of GPT-4o, was included to extend the comparison. Given the multi-label nature of the SDG mapping task, we employed metrics such as F1 score, precision, and recall with micro-averaging to evaluate different aspects of the models’ performance. These metrics are derived from the confusion matrix to ensure a comprehensive evaluation. We provide a clear observation and analysis of each model’s performance by plotting curves based on F1 score, precision, and recall at different thresholds. According to the results of this experiment, LLaMA 2 and Gemma still have significant room for improvement. The other four models do not exhibit particularly large differences in performance. The outputs from all seven models are available on Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.12789375 . Keywords: Large language models (LLMs), Open-source models, Sustainable Development Goal (SDG), SDG mapping

Introduction

LLMs are AI models that aim to generate human-like text by learning from vast amounts of existing data, such as Wikipedia, books, articles, and web pages. These models are based on deep neural network architectures, particularly the transformer structure, which enables them to handle large datasets and complex language tasks effectively. LLMs have been known as game-changers in Natural Language Processing (NLP) because they allow machines to perform tasks that require the comprehension and generation of highly fluent and accurate human language.
Some well-known companies’ large language models are not publicly available or require subscription fees. For example, OpenAI’s ChatGPT (Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer)

OpenAI’s ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) offers limited functionality for ordinary users, while those who pay a subscription fee can access more advanced models with richer features. For example, ChatGPT (GPT-4.0) can directly upload pictures and documents for enhanced outputs.
Google’s LaMDA is primarily used in Google’s internal projects and products, and Microsoft’s Turing-NLG is used within Microsoft’s suite of products and services; it is not available as an open-source model.

Uploading data to external companies presents significant security risks for confidential materials related to scientific research institutions, businesses, the military, and government entities. Therefore, open-source large language models that can run locally without exposing data to third parties have become urgent. This approach ensures data security and allows users to tailor language models for specific tasks. The excellent performance of these locally run models underscores their potential for a wide range of applications while maintaining user control and privacy.
How do free, open-source large language models (LLMs) compare to paid models like GPT-4o? In this study, we conducted a case study using 1,000 publications from the Swinburne University of Technology research bank. Each model was given the same prompt to perform the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) mapping task, which involved assigning each publication to different goals and providing a confidence level and an explanation for each assignment. The open-source LLMs evaluated in this experiment were Mixtral, LLaMA 2, LLaMA 3, Gemma, and Qwen2. The paid models were GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini. Given the lack of ground truth labels in the experimental data, the output of GPT-4o was used as the baseline.
We hope the results of this experiment will help other researchers select suitable open-source LLMs for their projects.

Result Comparison

Table
1
shows that the output of the large language model for SDG mapping is presented as a whole paragraph of text. While some models also provide results in the JSON format specified by the prompt, this format is not conducive to our performance comparison between models. Therefore, we processed the results to extract only the goals and confidence scores for comparison.
The processed output, such as “Goal 9: 0.85, Goal 11: 0.8, Goal 12: 0.6, Goal 3: 0.3, Goal 7: 0.2”, allows us to compare model outputs, each goal corresponds to a confidence level.
To evaluate the models, we set thresholds at intervals of 0.1 (e.g., 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, etc.).
We use the output of GPT-4o (with a threshold of 0.5) as the baseline to assess the performance of the other models.
This method enables us to evaluate and compare the models’ performance based on different confidence levels, helping to identify which models provide more consistent and reliable mappings to the SDGs.
We employed the F1 score, precision, and recall from the confusion matrix to measure the models’ performance because SDG mapping is a multi-label classification task where a publication can be assigned to multiple goals.
Micro-averaged metrics were chosen to provide an overall performance metric that reflects the effectiveness of the models across all instances. This approach helps understand the general robustness of the models.
To compare each model’s performance against GPT-4o, we plotted curves based on the F1 score, precision, and recall values obtained at different thresholds. These visualizations allow for more precise observation and analysis of how each model performs across various confidence levels, providing insights into their effectiveness and reliability in the SDG mapping task.

4
Conclusion

This study compares the performance of six large language models with GPT-4o on the SDG mapping task. The models evaluated are Mixtral, LLaMA 2, LLaMA 2, Gemma 2, Qwen2, and GPT-4o-mini. Since the dataset lacks ground truth labels, the output of GPT-4o (with a threshold of 0.5) is used as the baseline for comparison.
Generally, the F1 score, precision, and recall curves of the four models, GPT-4o-mini, Mixtral, LLaMA 3, and Qwen2, exhibit similar trends across thresholds ranging from 0.1 to 0.9. Their performances in the SDG mapping task are comparable. Conversely, the performances of LLaMA 2 and Gemma 2 are notably less satisfactory.
Among these, GPT-4o-mini performs slightly better, LLaMA 3 and Qwen2 are also reliable models.
Depending on the task requirements, selecting an appropriate threshold can optimize the balance between precision and recall. In this study, setting the threshold to 0.5 or 0.6 is advisable, as all the models’ F1 scores and precision start to drop off beyond this point.
For tasks requiring high recall and balanced performance, GPT-4o-mini is recommended. Consider fine-tuning models like Mixtral, Qwen2, and LLaMA 3 for applications where precision is more critical.
