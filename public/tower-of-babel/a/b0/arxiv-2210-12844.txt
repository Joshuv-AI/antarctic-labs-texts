Assorted inequalities for pattern occurrences
Reza Rastegar

Abstract
In this note, we present several inequalities in the context of pattern containment, utilizing elementary applications of the Fortuin-Kasteleyn-Ginibre (FKG) inequality and Shearer’s lemma.

Introduction

In this manuscript, we establish several inequalities concerning pattern containment in permutations that has been the subject of intense research over the last three decades, and many extensions and related results can be found in
[
2
,
4
]
. While some of our results have a broader scope, we limit our discussion to permutations for ease of presentation.

To present our findings, a few notations and definitions will be given in the next several paragraphs. We use the notation
{\mathbb{N}}:=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
to denote the set of natural numbers and the set of non-negative integers, respectively; that is,
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}.
We also define
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
as the set of all non-negative real numbers. Given a set
A,
we denote its cardinality as
\#A.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we set
[n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
, and for
d\leq n,
we define
[n]_{d}
to be the set of all
d
-subsets of
[n]
. Moreover, we define
S_{n}
as the set of all permutations of length
n
. We will interchangeably interpret any permutation
\pi
as either a sequence or a vector. In the context of the latter, we use
\pi(A)
to denote the sequence
(\pi_{i})_{i\in A},
where
A\subset[n]
.

A
pattern
of length
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
is any distinguished permutation chosen from
S_{d}
. Recall that any word of length
d
with
d
distinct letters can be naturally reduced to a permutation in
S_{d}
, preserving the relative order of the values. For instance, the word
284
reduces to the permutation
132
. Given an arbitrary permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
with
n\geq d
, an occurrence of the pattern
v\in S_{d}
in
\pi
is a sequence of
d
indices
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{d}\leq n
such that the
subsequence

\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{d}}
is
order-isomorphic
to the pattern
v
. In other words,
\pi({j_{1},\cdots,j_{d}})
can be naturally reduced to
v
, as defined by

\displaystyle\pi_{j_{p}}<\pi_{j_{q}}\Longleftrightarrow v_{p}<v_{q}\qquad\forall,1\leq p,q\leq d.

For any permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and any pattern
v\in S_{d}
with
d\leq n
, we define
{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v)
as the subset of
[n]_{d}
at which
v
occurs in
\pi
, that is,
\pi(B)
is order-isomorphic to
v
if and only if
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v)\subset[n]_{d}
. We use
\occ_{v}(\pi)
to denote the number of occurrences of
v
in
\pi
, that is,
\occ_{v}(\pi):=\#{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v)
. For any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we denote by
F_{r}^{v}(S_{n})
the set of permutations in
S_{n}
containing
v
exactly
r
times, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{r}^{v}(S_{n})=\{\pi\in S_{n}:\ \occ_{v}(\pi)=r\}.

We define
f_{r}^{v}(S_{n})
to be the cardinality of
F_{r}^{v}(S_{n})
.
For instance, if
v
is the inversion
21
and
\pi=12435
, then
{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v)=\{34\}
,
\occ_{v}(\pi)=1
, and
\pi\in F_{1}^{21}(S_{5})
.

For any permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we denote by
C_{d}(\pi)
the set of all patterns
v\in S_{d}
that occurs in the permutation
\pi
. For instance:

\displaystyle C_{3}(1324)=\{132,123,213\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad C_{3}(1234)=\{123\}.

In addition,
C_{1}(\pi)=\{1\}
for any permutation
\pi.
Set
c_{d}(\pi):=\#C_{d}(\pi)
.

For our first result, we seek to express
c_{d}(\pi)
and
\occ_{\pi}(v)
in terms of simpler structures of shorter length for a fixed permutation
\pi
. It states

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a fixed permutation, then

(a)

for any
1\leq\ell<d<n
,

\displaystyle c_{d}(\pi)\leq\left(c_{\ell}(\pi)\binom{d}{\ell}\right)^{\frac{d}{\ell}}.

(b)

for any
v\in S_{d}
and
1<\ell<d

\displaystyle\occ_{\pi}(v)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{w\in C_{\ell}(v)}\occ_{\pi}(w)^{\frac{\occ_{v}(w)}{\binom{d-1}{l-1}}}.

As a simple trivial example for part (a), when
\pi
is the identity permutation and
v
is the pattern
1\cdots d
, we have
C_{d-1}(v)=\{1\cdots(d-1)\}
, and the inequality reduces to

\displaystyle\binom{n}{d}\leq\binom{n}{d-1}^{d/(d-1)}.

With regard to part (b), to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no known simple relation between
\occ_{\pi}(v)
and
\occ_{\pi}(w)
for
w\in C_{\ell}(v)
. Therefore, Theorem
1.1
-(b) offers some new insight in this direction. Figure
1
displays a simulation of the left-hand side and the right-hand side of the inequality.

Figure 1:
Simulation results comparing the exact value of
\occ_{\pi}(v)
(blue plot) with the upper bound predicted by Theorem
1.1
-(b) (red plot) for 200 randomly generated permutations in
S_{20}
. The pattern
v
is taken to be
5274316
on the left and
1234765
on the right.

The proofs of both parts of the theorem are obtained through entropy argument and an application of Shearer’s lemma and are presented in the section
2
.

Our second result has a different flavor. In order to state it, we first provide an additional definition. Let
B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}
denote
r
distinct sets in
[n]d
, and let
F^{v}_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}(S_{n})
denote the set of permutations in
S_{n}
where order-isomorphic copies of
v
appear precisely at sets
B_{i}
. That is,
\pi\in F^{v}_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}(S_{n})
if and only if
{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v)=\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}
. Set
f^{v}_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}(S_{n}):=\#F^{v}_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}(S_{n})
.

Furthermore, we recall that in the realm of order theory, a function
\mu:L\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is log-supermodular for any finite distributive lattice
L
if it satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mu(x)\mu(y)\leq\mu(x\wedge y)\mu(x\vee y),\quad x,y\in L,

where
x\wedge y
and
x\vee y
denote the infimum and supremum of
x
and
y
, respectively, as defined by the order on
L
.Let
\mathcal{P}(n)
denote the set of all subsets of
[n]
. This set forms a distributive lattice. We define a log-supermodular function on
\mathcal{P}(n)
using the probability measure
\mu(A):=p^{|A|}(1-p)^{n-|A|}
for
A\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, where
p\in(0,1)
is a constant. Our result indicate that it is feasible to derive a correlation inequality between the sizes of sets of permutations categorized based on their fixed containment locations.

Theorem 1.2
.

(a)

Let
\mu
be any log-supermodular probability measure on
{\mathcal{P}}(n)
. Define

\nu_{{\mathcal{B}}}:=\mu\left(A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)|\forall B\in{\mathcal{B}},B\not\subset A\right),\quad\mbox{for }{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(n).

Then, for each pattern
v\in S_{d}
, we have

\prod_{r=1}^{\binom{n}{d}}\prod_{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\in[n]_{d}}\nu_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}^{f_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}^{v}(S_{n})}\leq\sum_{A\subset[n],|A|<d}\mu(A).

(b)

Choose a chain of subsets
\{\}=A_{0}\subsetneq A_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{d}=[d].
Let
\mu
be any probability measure on this chain. For any pattern
v\in S_{d},
and for each
i
, let the pattern
v^{i}
to be the reduced form of
v(A_{i}).
Then, for any
0<x_{d}\leq\cdots\leq x_{2}<1
we have

\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{d}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{i-1}\mu(A_{\ell})+\sum_{\ell=i}^{d}x_{\ell}\mu(A_{\ell})\right)^{f_{0}^{v^{i}|v^{i-1}}(S_{n})}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}\mu(A_{i})\prod_{\ell=2}^{i}x_{\ell}^{f_{0}^{v^{\ell}|v^{\ell-1}}(S_{n})},

where
f^{v^{i}|v^{i-1}}(S_{n})
is the number of permutations in
S_{n}
avoiding
v^{i}
while containing
v^{i-1}.

We use the FKG inequality (Theorem 6.2.1,
[
1
]
) to prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
3
. It states that increasing events on these lattices are positively correlated, while an increasing and a decreasing event are negatively correlated.

We point out that the scope of this theorem extends beyond pattern occurrences in permutations to a broader class of sequences. Additionally, we note that certain sets
B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}
may have
f_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{r}\}}^{v}(S_{n})=0
, which can be inferred from the structure of the pattern
v
. Notably, for highly ordered patterns such as
v=1\cdots d
, the inequality (a) may not yield much information. However, we conjecture that the inequality holds stronger for highly unordered patterns, although we currently lack proof for this hypothesis.

As an illustration of Theorem
1.2
-(b), consider the pattern
v=143265
in
S_{6}
. We choose the subset chain

\{\}\subset\{2\}\subset\{2,3\}\subset\{2,3,5\}\subset\{2,3,5,6\}\subset\{2,3,4,5,6\}\subset\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}.

The corresponding patterns
v^{i}
are
v^{0}=\epsilon
,
v^{1}=1
,
v^{2}=21
,
v^{3}=213
,
v^{4}=2143
,
v^{5}=32154
, and
v=v^{6}=143265
, where
\epsilon
denotes the null permutation. See also the discussion immediately following the proof in the Section
3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

In combinatorics, entropy based arguments have been extensively used to provide simple yet elegant proof of nontrivial results. See
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
and the references within for a review of the method and several interesting examples. In this section, we use entropy to prove Theorem
1.1
. To that goal, let
X
be a random variable sampled from the set
\Omega=\{x_{1},...,x_{m}\}
according to the probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}(.)
. Define the entropy of the random variable
X
as

\displaystyle H(X):=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i})\log{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{i}).

Entropy has many elegant properties, two of which we will use in the rest of this note: boundedness and sub-additivity. The boundedness property for the entropy of a random variable
X
is the inequality
H(X)\leq\log\#\supp(X)
, where
\supp X
is the range of the variable
X
(see
[
1
]
- Lemma 15.7.1-(i).) With respect to the latter, a simple generalization of sub-additivity property (Shearer’s lemma - see
[
1
]
- Proposition 15.7.4) is the main ingredient of our proof. Let
X:=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
be any random vector. Shearer’s lemma states that for a family of subsets of
[n]
possibly with repeats, namely
{\mathcal{A}}
, with each
i\in[n]
included in at least
t
members of
{\mathcal{A}}
,

\displaystyle tH(X)\leq\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{A}}}H(X(A)).

We use these two properties along with the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means (AM-GM) to bound the quantity that we would like to enumerate.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
-(a).

Let
1\leq\ell<d<n
be fixed. Fix a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}.

Let
v=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{d})
be a uniformly random pattern sampled from
C_{d}(\pi)
. Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}=[d]_{\ell}
and use
H_{\pi}(v)
(resp.
H_{\pi}(v(A)
) to refer to the entropy of
v
(resp.
v(A)
). Then, Shearer’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}H_{\pi}(v)\leq\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{A}}}H_{\pi}(v(A)).

(1)

Since
v
is uniformly chosen from
C_{d}(\pi)
, then by the definition of entropy

\displaystyle H_{\pi}(v)=\log c_{d}(\pi).

(2)

In addition, by the boundedness property we write

\displaystyle H_{\pi}(v(A))\leq\log\#\supp{v(A)}.

(3)

Plugging (
2
) and (
3
) into (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\log c_{d}(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}}\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{A}}}\log\#\supp{v(A)}=\log\left(\prod_{A\in[d]_{\ell}}\#\supp{v(A)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}}}

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{A\in[d]_{\ell}}c_{d}(\pi;A)\right)^{\frac{1}{\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}}},

with
c_{d}(\pi;A)=\#C_{d}(\pi;A)
. Here
C_{d}(\pi;A)
is a subset of
C_{d}(\pi)
such that for any two distinct patterns
v
and
w
in
S_{d}

\displaystyle v,w\in C_{d}(\pi;A)\mbox{ if and only if }v(A)\neq w(A).

Now, for any
\eta\in S_{\ell}
we define
C_{d}^{\eta}(\pi;A)
to be the subset of
C_{d}(\pi;A)
such that for any two distinct patterns
v
and
w
;
v,w\in C^{\eta}_{d}(\pi;A)
if and only if

\displaystyle v(A)\neq w(A),\mbox{ and }v(A),w(A)\mbox{ are order-isomorphic to }\eta.

Next, we write

\displaystyle C_{d}(\pi;A)=\cup_{\eta\in C_{\ell}(\pi)}C_{d}^{\eta}(\pi;A).

(5)

For any
\eta\in C_{\ell}(\pi),
there are
\binom{d}{\ell}
distinct choices for the value of
v(A)
and hence
\#C_{d}^{\eta}(\pi;A)\leq\binom{d}{\ell}
and

\displaystyle c_{d}(\pi;A)\leq\sum_{\eta\in C_{\ell}(\pi)}\#C_{d}^{\eta}(\pi;A)\leq\binom{d}{\ell}c_{\ell}(\pi).

Therefore, (
4
) yields

\displaystyle c_{d}(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\max_{A\in[d]_{\ell}}c_{d}(\pi;A)\right)^{\frac{\binom{d}{\ell}}{\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(c_{\ell}(\pi)\binom{d}{\ell}\right)^{\frac{\binom{d}{\ell}}{\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}}}=\left(c_{\ell}(\pi)\binom{d}{\ell}\right)^{\frac{d}{\ell}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
-(b).

Fix a permutation
\pi\in S_{n},
and a pattern
v\in S_{d}
. Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{d})
be a uniformly random element chosen from
{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v).
This implies

\displaystyle H_{\pi}(\sigma)=\log\occ_{\pi}(v).

(6)

We then write

\displaystyle\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}\log\occ_{\pi}(v)=\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}H_{\pi}(\sigma)\qquad\qquad\textrm{By \eqref{eq1_3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{B\in[d]_{\ell}}H_{\pi}(\sigma(B))\qquad\qquad\qquad\textrm{By Shearer's Lemma}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{B\in[d]_{\ell}}\log(\#\supp\sigma(B))\qquad\quad\textrm{By boundedness property}

\displaystyle\qquad=\log\prod_{B\in[d]_{\ell}}\#\supp\sigma(B)

\displaystyle\qquad=\log\prod_{w\in C_{\ell}(v)}\prod_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{v}(w)}\#\supp\sigma(B)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\log\prod_{w\in C_{\ell}(v)}\left(\frac{1}{\#{\mathcal{B}}_{v}(w)}\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{v}(w)}\#\supp\sigma(B)\right)^{\#{\mathcal{B}}_{v}(w)}\ \textrm{By AM-GM}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\log\prod_{w\in C_{\ell}(v)}\occ_{\pi}(w)^{\occ_{v}(w)},

where for the last line we observe that, for any
w\in C_{\ell}(v),
if
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{v}(w)
, then
\pi(\sigma(B))
is an occurrence instance of
w
in
\pi,
and hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{v}(w)}\#\supp\sigma(B)\leq\occ_{v}(w)\occ_{\pi}(w).

(7)

Divide both sides by
\binom{d-1}{\ell-1}
and simplify. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2

The Fortuin–Kasteleyn–Ginibre (FKG) inequality is a fundamental correlation inequality in statistical physics and percolation, expressed in terms of log-supermodular probability measures on distributive lattices. In this section we use it to prove Theorem
1.2
. We first review the inequality FKG.

Suppose
L
is a distributive lattice and
\mu
is a log-supermodular probability measure on
L.
A non-negative function
g:L\to{\mathbb{R}}
is increasing (resp. decreasing) on
L
if for every
x\leq y
we have
g(x)\leq g(y)
(resp.
g(x)\geq g(y)
). The FKG inequality (See
[
1
]
, Theorem 6.2.1) states that for family
{\mathcal{G}}
of increasing functions,

\displaystyle\prod_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\sum_{x\in L}\mu(x)g(x)\leq\sum_{x\in L}\mu(x)\prod_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}g(x).

(8)

The same inequality holds for a family of decreasing functions. Now, we give the proof of Theorem
1.2
by choosing appropriate
L
and
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
-(a).

Fix a pattern
v\in S_{d}
. For each permutation
\pi\in S_{n},
we define a function
g_{\pi,v}(.):{\mathcal{P}}(n)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
as

\displaystyle g_{\pi,v}(A)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if }\pi(A)\mbox{ avoids }v\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

For each
\pi\in S_{n},

g_{\pi,v}(A)
is an decreasing function on the distributive lattice
L={\mathcal{P}}(n).
This is clear from the fact that for any
A\subset B\subset[n]
, we have

\displaystyle g_{\pi,v}(A)=[\pi(A)\mbox{ avoids }v]\geq[\pi(B)\mbox{ avoids }v]=g_{\pi,v}(B),

where
[h]
is one if
h
holds true, and is zero otherwise. Let
{\mathcal{G}}=\{g_{\pi,v}\ |\ \pi\in S_{n}\},
and apply the FKG inequality (
8
). To that goal, choose any
A\subset[n].
If
|A|\geq d,
one can find
\pi\in S_{n}
where
\pi(A)
contains
v
and hence
\prod_{\pi\in S_{n}}g_{\pi,v}(A)=0.
If
|A|\leq d-1,
then for any
\pi\in S_{n},

\pi(A)
avoids
v
and hence
\prod_{\pi\in S_{n}}g_{\pi,v}(A)=1.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\prod_{\pi\in S_{n}}g_{\pi,v}(A)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if}\quad|A|\leq d-1\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(9)

Next, recall
S_{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle S_{n}=F_{0}^{v}(S_{n})\cup\left(\cup_{\ell\geq 1}\cup_{\mbox{distinct }B_{1},\cdots,B_{\ell}\in[n]_{d}}F_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{\ell}\}}^{v}(S_{n})\right).

(10)

Observe that if
\pi
avoids
v
, then
g_{\pi,v}(A)=1
for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)
and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)}\mu(A)g_{\pi,v}(A)=\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)}\mu(A)=1.

(11)

Similarly, given any
\ell
distinct
B_{1},\cdots,B_{\ell}\in[n]_{d}
, and for any
\pi\in F_{\{B_{1},\cdots,B_{\ell}\}}^{v}(S_{n})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)}\mu(A)g_{\pi,v}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)|\pi(A)\mbox{ avoids }v\right)

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)|B_{i}\not\subset A,1\leq i\leq\ell\right).

Finally, we plug (
11
) and (
12
) into the LHS and (
9
) into the RHS of (
8
), and use (
10
) to group the terms. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

-(b).

Set

\displaystyle\mu(A_{i})\mu(A_{j})=\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j})\mu(A_{i}\cup A_{j}).

we define the function

as

\displaystyle g_{\pi,v}(A_{i})=\begin{cases}1&\quad i<\ell_{\pi,v,T_{d}}\\
x_{i}&\quad i\geq\ell_{\pi,v,T_{d}}\end{cases},

for any

where

is the minimal value between

and

where
\pi
avoids

\ell_{\pi,v,T_{d}}

Inserting (

\displaystyle\prod_{v\in S_{d}}g_{\pi,v}(A)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if}\quad|A|\leq d-1\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

(15)

Next, observe that if
\pi
avoids
v

g_{\pi,v}(A)=1

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)}\mu(A)g_{\pi,v}(A)=\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)}\mu(A)=1.

(16)

However, for

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)}\mu(A)g_{\pi,v}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)|\pi(A)\mbox{ avoids }v\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(A\in{\mathcal{P}}(n)|\forall B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(v),\ B\not\subset A\right).

)-(
17

) completes the following result

Lemma 3.1

For each fixed
