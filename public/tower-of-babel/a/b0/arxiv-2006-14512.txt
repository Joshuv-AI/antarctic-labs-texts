Uncovering the Connections Between Adversarial Transferability and Knowledge Transferability
Kaizhao Liang, Jacky Y. Zhang, Boxin Wang, Zhuolin Yang, Oluwasanmi Koyejo, Bo Li, Kaizhao Liang, Jacky Y. Zhang, Boxin Wang, Zhuolin Yang, Oluwasanmi Koyejo, Bo Li

Abstract
Knowledge transferability, or transfer learning, has been widely adopted to allow a pre-trained model in the source domain to be effectively adapted to downstream tasks in the target domain. It is thus important to explore and understand the factors affecting knowledge transferability. In this paper, as the first work, we analyze and demonstrate the connections between knowledge transferability and another important phenomenon–adversarial transferability, i.e. , adversarial examples generated against one model can be transferred to attack other models. Our theoretical studies show that adversarial transferability indicates knowledge transferability, and vice versa. Moreover, based on the theoretical insights, we propose two practical adversarial transferability metrics to characterize this process, serving as bidirectional indicators between adversarial and knowledge transferability. We conduct extensive experiments for different scenarios on diverse datasets, showing a positive correlation between adversarial transferability and knowledge transferability. Our findings will shed light on future research about effective knowledge transfer learning and adversarial transferability analyses. All code and data are available here .

Introduction

Knowledge transfer is quickly becoming the standard approach for fast learning adaptation across domains. Also known as transfer learning or learning transfer, knowledge transfer has been a critical technology for enabling several real-world applications, including object detection
(
Zhang et al. 2014
)
, image segmentation
(
Kendall et al. 2018
)
, multi-lingual machine translation
(
Dong et al. 2015
)
, and language understanding evaluation
(
Wang et al. 2019a
)
, among others.
For example, since the release of ImageNet
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
, pretrained ImageNet models (e.g., on TensorFlow Hub or PyTorch-Hub) have become the default option for the knowledge transfer source due to its broad coverage of visual concepts and compatibility with various visual tasks
(
Huh et al. 2016
)
. Motivated by its importance, many studies have explored the factors associated with knowledge transferability. Most recently,
Salman et al. 2020
showed that more robust pretrained ImageNet models transfer better to downstream tasks, which reveals that
adversarial training
helps to improve knowledge transferability.

In the meantime,
adversarial transferability
has been extensively studied—a phenomenon that an adversarial instance generated against one model has high probability attack another one without additional modification
(
Papernot et al. 2016
;
Goodfellow et al. 2014
;
Joon Oh et al. 2017
)
.
Hence, adversarial transferability is widely exploited in black-box attacks
(
Ilyas et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2016
;
Naseer et al. 2019
)
.
A line of work has been conducted to bound the adversarial transferability based on model (gradient) similarity
(
Tramèr et al. 2017b
)
.
Given that both
adversarial transferability
and
knowledge transferability
are impacted by certain model similarity and adversarial ML properties, in this work, we aim to conduct the
first
study to analyze the connections between them and ask,

What is the fundamental connection between knowledge transferability and adversarial transferability? Can we measure one and indicate the other?

Technical Contributions.

In this paper, we take the
first
step
towards exploring the fundamental relation between adversarial transferability and knowledge transferability.
We make contributions on both theoretical
and empirical fronts.

•

We formally define the adversarial transferability for the
first
time by considering all potential adversarial perturbation vectors. We then conduct thorough and novel theoretical analysis to characterize the precise connection between adversarial transferability and knowledge transferability based on our definition.

•

In particular, we prove that high adversarial transferability will indicate high knowledge transferability, which can be represented as the distance in an inner product space defined by the Hessian of the adversarial loss.
In the meantime, we prove that high knowledge transferability will indicate high adversarial transferability.

•

Based on our theoretical insights, we propose two practical adversarial transferability metrics that quantitatively measure the adversarial transferability in practice. We then provide simulational results to verify how these metrics connect with the knowledge transferability in a bidirectional way.

•

Extensive experiments justify our theoretical insights and the proposed adversarial transferability metrics, leading to our discussion on potential applications and future research.

Related Work
There is a line of research studying different factors that affect knowledge transferability
(
Yosinski et al. 2014
;
Long et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2019b
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
Shinya et al. 2019
)
. Further, empirical observations show that the correlation between learning tasks
(
Achille et al. 2019
;
Zamir et al. 2018
)
, the similarity of model architectures, and data distribution are all correlated with different knowledge transfer abilities. Interestingly, recent empirical evidence suggests that adversarially-trained models transfer better than non-robust models
(
Salman et al. 2020
;
Utrera et al. 2020
)
, suggesting a connection between the adversarial properties and knowledge transferability. On the other hand, several approaches have been proposed to boost the adversarial transferability
(
Zhou et al. 2018
;
Demontis et al. 2019
;
Dong et al. 2019
;
Xie et al. 2019
)
. Beyond the above empirical studies, there are a few existing analyses of adversarial transferability, which explore different conditions that may enhance adversarial transferability
(
Athalye et al. 2018
;
Tramèr et al. 2017b
;
Ma et al. 2018
;
Demontis et al. 2019
)
.
In this work, we aim to bridge the connection between adversarial and knowledge transferability, both of which reveal interesting properties of ML model similarities from different perspectives.

Adversarial Transferability and Knowledge Transferability

This section introduces the preliminaries and the formal definitions of the knowledge and adversarial transferability, and formally defines our problem of interest.

Notation.
Sets are denoted in blackboard bold, e.g.,
{\mathbb{R}}
, and the set of integers
\{1\dots n\}
is denoted as
[n]
. Distributions are denoted in calligraphy, e.g.,
{\mathcal{D}}
, and the support of a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
is denoted as
\text{supp}({\mathcal{D}})
. Vectors are denoted as bold lower case letters, e.g.,
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and matrices are denoted as bold uppercase letters, e.g.,
{\bm{W}}
. We denote the entry-wise product operator between vectors or matrices as
\odot
. The Moore–Penrose inverse of a matrix
{\bm{W}}
is denoted as
{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}
.
We use
\|\cdot\|_{2}
to denote Euclidean norm induced by Euclidean inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
The standard inner product of two matrices is defined as
\langle{\bm{W}},{\bm{M}}\rangle=\tr({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{M}})
, where
\tr(\cdot)
is the trace of a matrix. The Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is induced by the standard matrix inner product.
Moreover, in the (semi-)inner product space defined by a positive (semi-)definite matrix
{\bm{S}}
, the (semi-)inner product of two vectors or matrices is defined by
\langle{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}\rangle_{\bm{S}}={\bm{v}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{S}}{\bm{v}}_{2}
or
\langle{\bm{W}},{\bm{M}}\rangle_{\bm{S}}=\tr({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}{\bm{M}})
, respectively. Given a vector
{\bm{v}}
, we define its normalization as
\widehat{{\bm{v}}}={\bm{v}}/\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}
. When using a denominator
\|\cdot\|_{*}
other than Euclidean norm, we denote the normalization as
\widehat{{\bm{v}}}|_{*}
.

Given a (vector-valued) function
f
, we denote
f({\bm{x}})
as its evaluated value at
{\bm{x}}
, and
f
represents the function itself in the corresponding Hilbert space.
Composition of functions is denoted as
g\circ f({\bm{x}})=g(f({\bm{x}}))
. We use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}
to denote the inner product induced by distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
and inherited from Euclidean inner product, i.e.,
\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\langle f_{1}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}})\rangle
.
Accordingly, we use
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
to denote the norm induced by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}
, i.e.,
\|f\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}}
. When the inherited inner product is defined by
{\bm{S}}
, we denote
\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{S}}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\langle f_{1}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}})\rangle_{\bm{S}}
, and similarly for
\|f\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{S}}}
.

Knowledge Transferability

Given a pre-trained
source
model
f_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and a
target
domain
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with data distribution
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
and
target
labels
y({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
knowledge transferability
is defined as the performance of fine-tuning
f_{S}
on
{\mathcal{D}}
to predict
y
. Concretely, knowledge transferability can be represented as a loss
\mathcal{L}(\ \cdot\ ,y,{\mathcal{D}})
after fine-tuning by composing the fixed source model with a trainable function
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, typically from a small function class
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\quad\mathcal{L}(g\circ f_{S},y,{\mathcal{D}}),

(2)

where the loss function
\mathcal{L}
measures the error between
g\circ f_{S}
and the ground truth
y
under the
target
data distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
. For example, for neural networks it is usual to stack on and fine-tune a linear layer; here
{\mathbb{G}}
is the affine function class. We will focus on the affine setting in this paper.

For our purposes, a more useful measure of transfer is to compare the quality of the fine-tuned model to a model trained directly on the target domain
f_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Thus, we study the following surrogate of knowledge transferability, where the ground truth target is replaced by a reference target model
f_{T}
:

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\quad\mathcal{L}(g\circ f_{S},f_{T},{\mathcal{D}}).

(3)

. Adversarial Attacks.

For simplicity we consider untargeted attacks that seeks to maximize the deviation of model output
as measured by a given adversarial loss function
\ell_{adv}(\cdot,\cdot)
. The targeted attack can be viewed as a special case.
Without loss of generality, we assume the adversarial loss is
non-negative
.
Given a datapoint
\bm{x}
and model
f
, an adversarial example of magnitude
\epsilon
is denoted by
\bm{\delta}_{f,\epsilon}(\bm{x})
, computed as:

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{f,\epsilon}(\bm{x})\ =\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|\leq\epsilon}\ \ell_{adv}(f({\bm{x}}),f({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta})).

(4)

We note that in theory
\bm{\delta}_{f,\epsilon}(\bm{x})
may not be unique, and its generalized definition and its discussion are provided in our theoretical analysis (Section
3
).

Adversarial Transferability.
The process of adversarial transfer involves applying the adversarial example generated against a model
f_{1}
to another model
f_{2}
. Thus, adversarial transferability from
f_{1}
to
f_{2}
measures how well
\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}
attacks
f_{2}
. We propose two metrics, namely,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
that characterize adversarial transferability from complementary perspectives. To provide a visual overview of our definitions for the proposed adversarial transferability metrics, we present an illustration in Figure
1
(a).

Definition 1

(The First Adversarial Transferability)
.

The first adversarial transferability from
f_{1}
to
f_{2}
at data sample
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})=\frac{\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}(\bm{x})))}{\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{2},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})))}.

(5)

Taking the expectation, the first adversarial transferability is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\alpha^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})\right].

(6)

Observe that the first adversarial transferability characterize how well the adversarial attacks
\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}
generated against
f_{1}
perform on
f_{2}
, compared to
f_{2}
’s whitebox adversarial attacks
\bm{\delta}_{f_{2},\epsilon}
. Thus, high
\alpha_{1}
indicates high adversarial transferability. Note that the two attacks use the same magnitude constraint
\epsilon
.

Recall that
\ell_{adv}(f({\bm{x}}),f({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}))
measures the effect of the attack
\bm{\delta}
on the model output
f({\bm{x}})
.

\alpha_{1}
characterizes the relative magnitude of this deviation. However, this magnitude information is incomplete, as the direction of the deviation also encodes information about the adversarial transfer process. To this end, we propose the second adverserial metric, inspired by our theoretical analysis, which characterizes adversarial transferability from the directional perspective.

Definition 2

(The Second Adversarial Transferability)
.

The second adversarial transferability from
f_{1}
to
f_{2}
, under data distribution
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}})}\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})}^{\top}]\|_{F},

(7)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=f_{1}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}({\bm{x}}))-f_{1}({\bm{x}})

(8)

\displaystyle\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}({\bm{x}}))-f_{2}({\bm{x}})

(9)

are deviations in model output given the adversarial attack
\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})
generated against
f_{1}
, and
\widehat{\ \cdot\ }
denotes the corresponding unit-length vector.

To further clarify the second adversarial transferability metric, consider the following alternative form of
\alpha_{2}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The
\alpha_{2}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
can be reformulated as

\displaystyle(\alpha_{2}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}})^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}}\left[\theta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})\theta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})\right],

(10)

where
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}
, and

\displaystyle\theta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{1})},\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\rangle

(11)

\displaystyle\theta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{1})},\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\rangle

(12)

We can see that high
\alpha_{2}
indicates that it is more likely for the two inner products (
i.e.
,
\theta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}
and
\theta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
) to have the same sign. Given that the direction of
f_{1}
’s output deviation indicates its attack
\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}
, and the direction of
f_{2}
’s output deviation indicates the transferred attack
\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}
, high
\alpha_{2}
implies that the two directions will rotate by a similar angle as the data changes.

\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
represent complementary aspects of the adversarial transferability:

\alpha_{1}
can be understood as how often the adversarial attack transfers, while
\alpha_{2}
encodes directional information of the output deviation caused by adversarial attacks. An example is provided in the appendix section
A
to illustrate the necessity of both the metrics in characterizing the relation between adversarial transferability and knowledge transferability. To jointly take the two adversarial transferability metrics into consideration, we propose the following metric as the combined value of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1}*\alpha_{2})^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}=

(13)

\displaystyle\qquad\big\|\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}})}\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})}^{\top}]\big\|_{F}.

(14)

We defer the justification for the combined adversarial transferability metric in the next section, and move on to state a useful proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

The adversarial transferabililty metrics
\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
,
\alpha_{2}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
and
(\alpha_{1}*\alpha_{2})^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
are in
[0,1]
.

So far, we have defined knowledge transferability, and two adversarial trasferability metrics. We can now analyze their connections more precisely.

Problem of Interest.
Given a
source
model
f_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the
target
data distribution
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, the ground truth target
y:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and a
target
reference model
f_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we aim to study how the adversarial transferability between
f_{S}
and
f_{T}
, characterized by the two proposed adversarial transferability metrics, connects to the knowledge transfer loss
\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\mathcal{L}(g\circ f_{S},y,{\mathcal{D}})
with affine functions
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
(equation
2
).

Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we present the theoretical analysis on how the adversarial transferability and the knowledge transfer process are tied together.
To simplify the discussion, as the objects studied in this section are specifically focused on the source domain
S
and the target domain
T
, we can use
\star
or
\diamond
as a placeholder for either
S
or
T
throughout this section.

Theoretical Analysis Overview.

In subsection
3.1
, we define the two
generalized adversarial transferabilities
, (
i.e.
,
{\bm{A}}_{1}
,
{\bm{A}}_{2}
), and present Theorem
3.1
showing that
{\bm{A}}_{1}
,
{\bm{A}}_{2}
together determine a gradient matching distance
\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\|\nabla f_{\star}-\nabla g\circ f_{\diamond}\|
, between the Jacobian matrices of the source and target models in an inner product space defined by the Hessian of the adversarial loss function. In the same subsection, we also show that
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
represent the most influential factors in
{\bm{A}}_{1}
and
{\bm{A}}_{2}
, respectively. Next, we explore the connection to knowledge transferability in subsection
3.2
via Theorem
3.2
which shows the gradient matching distance approximates the function matching distance,
i.e.
,
\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\|f_{\star}-g\circ f_{\diamond}\|
, with a distribution shift up to a Wasserstein distance. Finally, in subsection
3.3
we complete the analysis by outlining the connection between the function matching distance and the knowledge transfer loss. A visual overview is shown in Figure
1
(b).

Setting.
As adversarial perturbations are constrained in a small
\epsilon
-ball, it is reasonable to approximate the deviation of model outputs by its first-order Taylor approximation. Specifically, in this section we consider the Euclidean
\epsilon
-ball. Therefore, the output deviation of a function
f
at
{\bm{x}}
given a small perturbation
\|\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
can be approximated by

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon})-f({\bm{x}})\approx\nabla f({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon},

(15)

where
\nabla f({\bm{x}})
is the Jacobian matrix of
f
at
{\bm{x}}
.

We consider a convex and twice-differentiable adversarial loss function
\ell^{\star}_{adv}(\cdot)
that measures the deviation of model output
f_{\star}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon})-f_{\star}({\bm{x}})
, with minimum
\ell^{\star}_{adv}(\bm{0})=0
, for
\star\in\{S,T\}
. We note that we should treat the adversarial loss on
f_{S}
and
f_{T}
differently, as they may have different output dimensions. Accordingly, the adversarial attack (equation
4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})\ =\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\ \ell^{\star}_{adv}(\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}).

(16)

Another justification of the small-
\epsilon
approximation follows the literature; since the ideal attack defined in equation
4
is often intractable to compute, much of the literature uses the proposed formulation (
16
) in practice,
e.g.
, see
(
Miyato et al. 2018
)
, with experimental results suggesting similar behaviour as the standard definition.

The Small-
\epsilon
Regime.

Recall that the adversarial loss
\ell^{\star}_{adv}(\cdot)
studied in this section is convex, twice-differentiable, and achieves its minimum at
\bm{0}
, thus in the small
\epsilon
regime:

\displaystyle\ell^{\star}_{adv}(\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon}^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{H}}_{\star}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}

(17)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{\epsilon}\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(18)

which is the norm of
f_{\star}
’s output deviation in the inner product space defined by the Hessian
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
of the squared adversarial loss
(\ell_{adv}^{\star})^{2}
.

Accordingly, the adversarial attacks (
16
) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})\ =\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\ \|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(19)

and we can measure the output deviation of
f_{\diamond}
’s caused by
f_{\star}
’s adversarial attack
\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})
, denoted as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})=\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star},\epsilon}({\bm{x}}).

(20)

Note that in the small-
\epsilon
regime, the actual value of
\epsilon
becomes trivial (
e.g.,
\alpha_{1}
), consequently we will omit the
\epsilon
for notational ease:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\Delta_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}{\|\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}.

(21)

Similarly,
\alpha_{2}
can be computed using (
20
) in Definition
2
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[\widehat{\Delta_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})}\widehat{\Delta_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})}^{\top}]\|_{F}.

(22)

With these insights, next we will derive our first theorem.

3.1
Adversarial Transfer Indicates the Gradient Matching Distance, and Vice Versa

We present an interesting finding in this subsection,
i.e.
, the generalized adversarial transferabilities
{\bm{A}}_{1},{\bm{A}}_{2}
have a direct connection to the gradient matching distance between the source model
f_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and target model
f_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The gradient matching distance is defined as the smallest distance an affine transformation can achieve between their Jacobians
\nabla f_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
and
\nabla f_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
in the inner product space defined by
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
and data sample distribution
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, as shown below.

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\quad\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}-\nabla(g\circ f_{\diamond})^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(23)

where
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
are affine transformations. Note that
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if
(\star,\diamond)=(T,S)
, and
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
if
(\star,\diamond)=(S,T)
.
We defer the analysis of how the gradient matching distance approximates the knowledge transfer loss, and focus on its connection to adversarial transfer.

A Full Picture of Adversarial Transferability.
A key observation is that the adversarial attack (equation
19
) is the singular vector corresponding to the largest singular value of the Jacobian
\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})
in the
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
inner product space. Thus, information regarding other singular values that are not revealed by the adversarial attack. Therefore, we can consider other singular values, corresponding to smaller signals than the one revealed by adversarial attacks, to complete the analysis.
We denote
\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as the descending (in absolute value) singular values of the Jacobian
\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\cdot\times n}
in the
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
inner product space. In other words, we denote
\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as the square root of the descending eigenvalues of
\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{H}}_{\star}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}({\bm{x}})=[\sigma_{f_{\star}}^{(1)}({\bm{x}}),\dots,\sigma_{f_{\star}}^{(n)}({\bm{x}})]^{\top}.

(24)

Note that the number of non-zero singular values may be less than
n
, in which case we fill the rest with zeros such that vector is
n
-dimensional.

Since the adversarial attack
\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})
corresponds to the largest singular value
\sigma_{f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})^{(1)}
, we can also generalize the adversarial attack by including all the singular vectors.
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star}}^{(i)}({\bm{x}})\quad\text{ corresponds to }\quad\sigma_{f_{\star}}^{(i)}({\bm{x}}),\quad\forall i\in[n].

(25)

Loosely speaking, one could think
\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star}}^{(i)}({\bm{x}})
as the adversarial attack of
f_{\star}({\bm{x}})
in the subspace orthogonal to all the previous attacks,
i.e.
,
\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star}}^{(j)}({\bm{x}})
for
\forall j\in[i-1]
.

Accordingly, for
\forall i\in[i]
we denote the output deviation as

\displaystyle\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})=\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star}}^{(i)}({\bm{x}}).

(26)

As a consequence, we
generalize the first adversarial transferability
to be a
n
-dimensional vector
\bm{A}_{1}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}})
including the adversarial losses of all of the generalized adversarial attacks, where the
i^{th}
element in the vector is

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{1}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{(i)}=\frac{\|\Delta_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}^{(i)}({\bm{x}})\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}{\|\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}.

(27)

Note that the first entry of
\bm{A}_{1}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}})
is the original adversarial transferability,
i.e.
,
{{\bm{A}}}_{1}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{(1)}
is the same as the
{\alpha}_{1}^{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})
in Definition
1
.

With the above generalization that captures the full picture of the adversarial transfer process, we able to derive the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given the target and source models
f_{\star},f_{\diamond}
, where
(\star,\diamond)\in\{(S,T),(T,S)\}
, the gradient matching distance (equation
23
) can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\quad\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}-\nabla(g\circ f_{\diamond})^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}=

(28)

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\bm{v}}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{{\bm{A}}}_{2}^{\star,\diamond}({{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}}){\bm{v}}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}}_{2})]}{\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}^{2}\cdot\|{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}\|^{-1}_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}}\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(29)

where the expectation is taken over
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}
, and

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(1)}_{f_{\diamond},{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}({\bm{x}})\odot{\bm{A}}_{1}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}})

(30)

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})].

(31)

Moreover,
{{\bm{A}}}_{2}^{\star,\diamond}({{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}})
is a matrix, and its element in the
i^{th}
row and
j^{th}
column is

\displaystyle{{\bm{A}}}_{2}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}

\displaystyle)^{(i,j)}=\langle\widehat{\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\big|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}},\widehat{\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\big|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}\rangle

(32)

\displaystyle\cdot\langle\widehat{\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{1})}\big|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}},\widehat{\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\big|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}\rangle_{\widehat{{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}}|_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}.

(33)

Recall the alternative representation of the second adversarial transferability
\alpha_{2}
, and we can immediately observe that
\alpha_{2}
is determined by
{\bm{A}}_{2}
. Therefore, both
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
appear in this relation. Let us interpret the theorem, and justify the two proposed adversarial transferability metrics.

Interpretation of Theorem
3.1
.
First, we consider components that are not directly related to the adversarial transfer in the RHS of (
28
). The
\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}
outside represents the overall magnitude of the loss. In the fraction, the
\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}
in the denominator normalizes the
\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star}}
in the numerator. Similarly, though more complicated, the
\|{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}\|^{-1}_{2}
in the denominator corresponds to the
\sigma_{f_{\diamond}}^{(1)}
in the numerator. We note that these are properties of
f_{\star},f_{\diamond}
.

Next, observe that the components directly related to the adversarial transfer process are the
generalized adversarial transferability

{\bm{A}}_{1}
and
{\bm{A}}_{2}
. Let us neglect the superscript
(i)
or
(j)
for now, so we can see that their interpretations are the same as we introduced for
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
in section
2
. That is,
{\bm{A}}_{1}
captures the magnitude of the deviation in model outputs caused by adversarial attacks, while
{\bm{A}}_{2}
captures the direction of the deviation. A minor difference between
\alpha_{2}
and
{\bm{A}}_{2}
is that the second inner product in the elements of
{\bm{A}}_{2}
is defined by a positive semi-definite matrix
\widehat{{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}}
. For practical implementation, we choose to neglect this term, and use the standard Euclidean inner product in
\alpha_{2}
, which can be understood as a stretched version of the
\widehat{{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}}
inner product space.

Moreover, as the singular vector
\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star}}
has descending entries, we can see that in the vector
{\bm{A}}_{1}
and the matrix
{\bm{A}}_{2}
, the elements with superscript
(1)
have the most influence in the relations. In other words, the two proposed adversarial transferability metrics,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, are the most influential factors in equation
28
. We can also see that the combined metric
(\alpha_{1}*\alpha_{2})
also stems from here by only considering the components with the first superscript.

To interpret the relation between the gradient matching distance and the adversarial transferabilities, we introduce the following proposition. This shows that, in general,
{\bm{A}}_{1}
and
{\bm{A}}_{2}
with their elements closer to
1
can serve as a bidirectional indicator of a smaller gradient matching distance.

Proposition 3.1
.

In Theorem
3.1
,

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\bm{v}}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{2}^{\star,\diamond}({{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}}){\bm{v}}^{\star,\diamond}({\bm{x}}_{2})]}{\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}^{2}\cdot\|{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}\|^{-1}_{{\bm{H}}_{\diamond}}}\leq 1.

(34)

In conclusion, Theorem
3.1
reveals a bidirectional relation between the adversarial transfer process and the gradient matching distance, where the adversarial transfer process can be encoded by the generalized adversarial transferabilities,
i.e.
,
{\bm{A}}_{1}
and
{\bm{A}}_{2}
. Moreover,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
play the most influential role in their generalization,
i.e.,

{\bm{A}}_{1}
and
{\bm{A}}_{2}
.

3.2
The Gradient Matching Distance indicates the Function Matching Distance, and Vice Versa

To bridge the gradient matching distance to the knowledge transfer loss, an immediate step is to connect the gradient distance to the function distance which directly serves as a surrogate knowledge transfer loss as defined in (equation
3
).
Specifically, in this subsection, we present a connection between the function matching distance,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|f_{\star}-g\circ f_{\diamond}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(35)

and the gradient matching distance,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}-\nabla(g\circ f_{\diamond})^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(36)

where
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
are affine transformations.

For intuition, consider a point
{\bm{x}}_{0}
in the input space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, a path
\gamma_{\bm{x}}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
\gamma_{\bm{x}}(0)={\bm{x}}_{0}
and
\gamma_{\bm{x}}(1)={\bm{x}}
. Then, denoting
\gamma
as the function of
{\bm{x}}
, we can write the difference between the two functions as

\displaystyle f_{\star}-{g}\circ f_{\diamond}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\nabla f_{\star}(\gamma(t))-\nabla(g\circ f_{\diamond}(\gamma(t))))^{\top}\dot{\gamma}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

(37)

\displaystyle+(f_{\star}({\bm{x}}_{0})-g\circ f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}}_{0})).

(38)

Noting that the function difference is a path integral of the gradient difference, we should expect a distribution shift when characterizing their connection,
i.e.
, the integral path affects the original data distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
. Accordingly, as the integral path may leave the support of
{\mathcal{D}}
, it is necessary to assume the smoothness of the function, as shown below.

Denoting the optimal
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
in (
35
) as
\tilde{g}
, and one of the optimal
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
in (
36
) as
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
, we define

\displaystyle h_{\star,\diamond}:=f_{\star}-\tilde{g}\circ f_{\diamond}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\star,\diamond}^{\prime}:=f_{\star}-\tilde{g}^{\prime}\circ f_{\diamond},

(39)

and we can see that the gradient matching distance and the function matching distance can be written as

\displaystyle(\ref{def:function-matching-dist})=\|h_{\star,\diamond}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\ref{def:gradient-matching-dist-new})=\|\nabla{h^{\prime}_{\star,\diamond}}^{\top}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}.

(40)

Assumption 1

(
\beta
-smoothness)
.

We assume
h_{\star,\diamond}
and
h^{\prime}_{\star,\diamond}
are both
\beta
-smooth,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla h_{\star,\diamond}^{\top}({\bm{x}}_{1})-\nabla h_{\star,\diamond}^{\top}({\bm{x}}_{2})\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}\leq\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|_{2},

(41)

and similarly for
h^{\prime}_{\star,\diamond}
.

With this assumption, we can prove that the gradient matching distance and the function matching distance can bound each other.

Theorem 3.2
.

With the notation defined in equation
39
, assume the
\beta
-smoothness assumption holds. Given a data distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
and
\tau>0
, there exist distributions
{\mathcal{D}}_{1},{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
such that the type-1 Wasserstein distance
W_{1}({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{D}}_{1})\leq\tau
and
W_{1}({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\leq\tau
satisfying

\displaystyle{\tfrac{1}{2B^{2}}}\|h_{\star,\diamond}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla h_{\star,\diamond}^{\prime\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{1},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\beta^{2}(B-\tau)_{+}^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{3n}\|\nabla h_{\star,\diamond}^{\prime\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{2}{\tau^{2}}\|h_{\star,\diamond}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{2},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\beta^{2}\tau^{2},

(43)

where
n
is the dimension of
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, and
B=\inf_{{\bm{x}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\text{supp}({\mathcal{D}})}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{0}\|_{2}
is the radius of
\text{supp}({\mathcal{D}})
.

We note that the above theorem compromises some tightness in exchange for a cleaner presentation without losing its core message, which is discussed in the proof of the theorem.

Interpretation of Theorem
3.2
.
The theorem shows that under the smoothness assumption, the gradient matching distance indicates the function matching distance, and vice versa, with a distribution shift bounded in Wasserstein distance. As the distribution shift is in general necessary, we conjecture that using different data distributions for adversarial transfer and knowledge transfer can also be applicable.

3.3
The Function Matching Distance Indicates Knowledge Transferability, and Vice Versa

To complete the story, it remains to connect the function matching distance to knowledge transferability. As the adversarial transfer is symmetric (
i.e.
, either from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
or
f_{T}\to f_{S}
), we are able to use the placeholders
\star,\diamond\in\{S,T\}
all the way through. However, as the knowledge transfer is asymmetric (
i.e.
,
f_{S}\to y
to the target ground truth), we need to instantiate the direction of adversarial transfer to further our discussion.

Adversarial Transfer from
f_{T}\to f_{S}
.
As we can see from the
{\bm{A}}_{1}^{\star,\diamond}
in Theorem
3.1
, this direction corresponds to
(\star,\diamond)=(T,S)
. Accordingly, the function matching distance (equation
35
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|f_{T}-g\circ f_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}.

(44)

We can see that equation
44
directly translates to the surrogate knowledge transfer loss that uses the “pseudo ground truth” from the target reference model
f_{T}
.

In other words, the function matching distance serves as an approximation of the knowledge transfer loss defined as their distance in the inner product space of
{\bm{H}}_{T}
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|y-g\circ f_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}.

(45)

The accuracy of the approximation depends on the performance of
f_{T}
, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

The surrogate transfer loss (
44
) and the true transfer loss (
45
) are close, with an error of
\|f_{T}-y\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}
.

\displaystyle-\|f_{T}-y\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}\leq(\ref{def:know-transfer-dist})-(\ref{eq:function-distance-S-T})\leq\|f_{T}-y\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}

(46)

Adversarial Transfer from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
.
This direction corresponds to
(\star,\diamond)=(S,T)
. Accordingly, the function matching distance (equation
35
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|f_{S}-g\circ f_{T}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{S}}.

(47)

Since the affine transformation
g
acts on the target reference model, it can not be directly viewed as a surrogate transfer loss. However, interesting interpretations can be found in this direction, depending on the output dimension of
f_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
f_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

That is, when the direction of adversarial transfer is from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
, the indicating relation between it and knowledge transferability would possibly be unidirectional, depending on the dimensions. More discussion is included in the appendix section
B
due to space limitation.

Conclusion

We theoretically analyze the relation between adversarial transferability and knowledge transferability. We provide empirical experimental justifications in pratical settings. Both our theoretical and empirical results show that adversarial transferability can indicate knowledge transferability and vice versa. We expect our work will inspire future work on further exploring other factors that impact knowledge transferability and adversarial transferability.

Appendix A
An Example Illustrating the Necessity of both
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
in Characterizing the Relation Between Adversarial Transferability and Knowledge Transferability

\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
(Definition
1
&
2
) represent complementary aspects of the adversarial transferability:

\alpha_{1}
can be understood as how often the adversarial attack transfers, while
\alpha_{2}
encodes directional information of the output deviation caused by adversarial attacks. Recall that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]
(higher values indicate better adversarial transferability). As we show in our theoretical results reveal that high
\alpha_{1}
alone is not enough,
i.e.
, both the proposed metrics are necessary to characterize adversarial transferability and the relation between adversarial and knowledge transferabilities.

We provide a one-dimensional example showing that large
\alpha_{1}
only is not enough to indicate high knowledge transferability. Suppose the ground truth target function
f_{T}(x)=x^{2}
, and the source function
f_{S}(x)=\texttt{sgn}(x)\cdot x^{2}
where
\texttt{sgn}(\cdot)
denotes the sign function. Let the adversarial loss be the deviation in function output, and the data distribution be the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]
. As we can see, the direction that makes either
f_{T}
or
f_{S}
deviates the most is always the same,
i.e.
,
in this example even with
\alpha_{1}=1
achieves its maximum and adversarial attacks always transfer, regardless of the choice of
f_{1}\to f_{2}
or
f_{2}\to f_{1}
. However,
there does not exist an affine function
g
(
i.e.
, fine-tuning) making
g\circ f_{S}
close to
f_{T}
on
[-1,1]
. Indeed, one can verify that
\alpha_{2}=0
in this case (either
f_{1}\to f_{2}
or
f_{2}\to f_{1}
), which contributes to the low knowledge transferability.
However, if we move the data distribution to
[0,2]
, we can have
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=1
(either
f_{1}\to f_{2}
or
f_{2}\to f_{1}
) indicating high adversarial transferability, and indeed it achieves
f_{S}=f_{T}
showing perfect knowledge transferability.

Detailed Discussion About the Direction of Adversarial Transfer From
f_{S}\to f_{T}
in Subsection

In this section, we present a detailed discussion, in addition to subsection
3.3
, about the connection between function matching distance and knowledge transfer distance when the direction of adversarial transfer is from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
.

Recall that, to complete the story, it remains to connect the function matching distance to knowledge transferability. As the adversarial transfer is symmetric (
i.e.
, either from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
or
f_{T}\to f_{S}
), we are able to use the placeholders
\star,\diamond\in\{S,T\}
all the way through. However, as the knowledge transfer is asymmetric (
i.e.
,
f_{S}\to y
to the target ground truth), we need to instantiate the direction of adversarial transfer to further our discussion. We have discussed the direction of adversarial transfer from
f_{T}\to f_{S}
in the main paper, where we show that the function matching distance of this direction,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|f_{T}-g\circ f_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}},

(
44
)

can both upper and lower bound the knowledge transfer distance,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|y-g\circ f_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}.

(
45
)

The direction of adversarial transfer from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
corresponds to
(\star,\diamond)=(S,T)
. Accordingly, the function matching distance (equation
35
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\ \|f_{S}-g\circ f_{T}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{S}}.

(
47
)

Since the affine transformation
g
acts on the target reference model, it can not be directly viewed as a surrogate transfer loss. However, interesting interpretations can be found in this direction, depending on the output dimension of
f_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
f_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

In this subsection in the appendix we provide detailed discussion on the connection between the function matching distance of the direction of adversarial transfer from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
(equation
47
)
and the knowledge transfer distance (equation
45
). We build this connection by providing the relationships between the two directions of function matching distance,
i.e.
, equation
44
and equation
47
. That is being said, since we know equation
45
and equation
44
are tied together, we only need to provide relationships between equation
44
and equation
47
to show the connection between equation
47
and equation
45
.

Suppose
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is full rank, and loosely speaking we can derive the following intuitions.

•

If
d<m
, then
g
is injective and there exists
g^{-1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
g^{-1}\circ g
is the identity function. That is, if
g
can map
f_{T}
to closely track
f_{S}
, then reversely
g^{-1}
can map
f_{S}
to
f_{T}
, showing equation
47
upper bounds equation
44
in some sense.

•

If
d>m
, then
g
is surjective. By symmetry, equation
44
upper bounds equation
47
in some sense.

•

It is when

that equation

and equation

coincide.

Formally, we have the following theorem.

Theorem B.1
.

Denote
\tilde{g}_{T,S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as the optimal solution of equation

, and
\tilde{g}_{S,T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
as the optimal solution of equation

\tilde{g}_{T,S},\tilde{g}_{S,T}
are both full-rank. For

\tilde{g}_{T,S}
as

. Similarly, for
{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, denote the matrix representation of
\tilde{g}_{S,T}
as
\tilde{g}_{S,T}({\bm{w}})=\tilde{{\bm{W}}}_{S,T}{\bm{w}}+\tilde{{\bm{b}}}_{S,T}
. We have the following statements.

If
d<m
, then
\tilde{g}_{S,T}
is injective, and we have:

\displaystyle\|f_{T}-\tilde{g}_{T,S}\circ f_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{T}}\leq\sqrt{\|(\tilde{{\bm{W}}}_{S,T}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}_{S,T})^{-1}\|_{F}\cdot\|{\bm{H}}_{T}\|_{F}}\cdot\|f_{S}-\tilde{g}_{S,T}\circ f_{T}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}.

(48)

If
d>m
, then
\tilde{g}_{T,S}
is injective, and we have:

\displaystyle\|f_{S}-\tilde{g}_{S,T}\circ f_{T}\|_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{S}}\leq\sqrt{\|(\tilde{{\bm{W}}}_{T,S}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}_{T,S})^{-1}\|_{F}\cdot\|{\bm{H}}_{S}\|_{F}}\cdot\|f_{T}-\tilde{g}_{T,S}\circ f_{S}\|_{{\mathcal{D}}}.

(49)

If
d=m
, then both
\tilde{g}_{S,T}
and
\tilde{g}_{T,S}
are bijective, and we have both (
48
) and (
49
) stand.

That is, when the direction of adversarial transfer is from
f_{S}\to f_{T}
, the indicating relation between the function matching distance if this direction (equation
47
) and knowledge transferability would possibly be unidirectional, depending on the dimensions.

Proposition C.2

(Proposition
2.2
Restated)
.

The adversarial transferability metrics
\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
,
\alpha_{2}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}
and

are in
[0,1]

Proof.

Let us begin with

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})=\frac{\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}(\bm{x})))}{\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{2},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})))}.

(64)

Recall that
\ell_{adv}(\cdot)\geq 0
, and the definition of adversarial attack:

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{f,\epsilon}(\bm{x})\ =\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|\leq\epsilon}\ \ell_{adv}(f({\bm{x}}),f({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta})),

(65)

and we can see that by definition,

\displaystyle 0\leq\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}(\bm{x})))\leq\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{2},\epsilon}({\bm{x}}))).

(66)

Therefore,

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{1},\epsilon}(\bm{x})))}{\ell_{adv}(f_{2}({\bm{x}}),f_{2}({\bm{x}}+\bm{\delta}_{f_{2},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})))}\leq 1,

(67)

where we define
0/0=0
if necessary.

Hence,
\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[\alpha_{1}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}})]

.

2.1
to prove the same property for

\displaystyle(\alpha_{2}^{f_{1}\to f_{2}})^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}}\left[\langle\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{1})},\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\rangle\cdot\langle\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{1})},\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}}\left[\langle\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{1})},\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{1}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\rangle\cdot\langle\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{1})},\widehat{\Delta_{f_{1}\to f_{2}}({\bm{x}}_{2})}\rangle\right]\in[-1,1].

D.1
Proof of Theorem

Lemma D.1
.

The square of the gradient matching distance is

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\quad\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}-\nabla(g\circ f_{\diamond})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}=\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}-\langle{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}},{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}\rangle,

(77)

where
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
are affine transformations, and

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right],\qquad{\bm{J}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right].

(78)

Proof.

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\quad\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}-\nabla(g\circ f_{\diamond})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}

\displaystyle=\min_{{\bm{W}}}\quad\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}-{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}

(79)

\displaystyle=\min_{{\bm{W}}}\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}-{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}},

(80)

where
{\bm{W}}
is a matrix.

We can see that (
80
) is a convex program, where the optimal solution exists in a closed-form form, as shown in the following. Denote
l({\bm{W}})=\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}-{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}
, we have

\displaystyle l({\bm{W}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\|{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}-2\langle\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top},{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\rangle_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}\right]

(81)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\tr\left(\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}}){\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\right)-2\tr\left(\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\right)\right]

(82)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}}){\bm{W}}^{\top}\right)-2\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})\right)\right].

(83)

Taking the derivative of
l(\cdot)
w.r.t.
{\bm{W}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial l}{\partial{\bm{W}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[2{\bm{H}}_{\star}\left({\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}-\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\right)\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]

(84)

\displaystyle=2{\bm{H}}_{\star}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\bm{W}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})-\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]

(85)

\displaystyle=2{\bm{H}}_{\star}\left({\bm{W}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]\right).

(86)

Since
l(\cdot)
is convex, if there exists a
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}
such that
\frac{\partial l}{\partial{\bm{W}}}\big|_{{\bm{W}}=\tilde{{\bm{W}}}}=\bm{0}
then we know that
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}
is an optimal solution. Luckily, we can find such solution easily by using pseudo inverse,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{W}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]\right)^{\dagger}

(87)

\displaystyle={\bm{P}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger},

(88)

where we denote
{\bm{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]
and
{\bm{J}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]
.

We can verify that such
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}
indeed make the partial derivative (equation
86
) zero. In equation
86
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{W}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]={\bm{P}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}{\bm{J}}-{\bm{P}}.

(89)

To continue, we can see from Lemma
E.2
that
\ker({\bm{J}})\subseteq\ker({\bm{P}})
which means
\rowsp({\bm{P}})\subseteq\rowsp({\bm{J}})
, where
\ker(\cdot)
denotes the kernel of a matrix, and
\rowsp(\cdot)
denotes the row space of a matrix. Therefore, by definition of the pseudo-inverse, we can see that
{\bm{P}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}{\bm{J}}={\bm{P}}
,
i.e.
,
(\ref{eq:1-1-1-nn})=\bm{0}
, and hence
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}
is indeed the optimal solution.

Plugging (
88
) into (
80
), we have the optimal value as

\displaystyle(\ref{eq:1-1-1})

\displaystyle=l(\tilde{{\bm{W}}})

(90)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}\right)-2\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})\right)\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{J}}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{\top}-2{\bm{H}}_{\star}\tilde{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}\right)

(92)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}+\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}{\bm{J}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}{\bm{P}}^{\top}-2{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}{\bm{P}}^{\top}\right)

(93)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}-\tr\left({\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}{\bm{P}}^{\top}\right)

(94)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}-\langle{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}},{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}\rangle.

(95)

∎

Next, we present another lemma to analyze the term
{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}
.

Lemma D.2
.

In this lemma, we break down the matrix representation of
{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}
into pieces relating to the output deviation caused by the generalized adversarial attacks (defined in equation
26
)

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})\right)\cdot\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{1})\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{2})^{\top}\right).

(96)

Proof.

Denote a symmetric decomposition of the positive semi-definitive matrix
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
as

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{\star}={\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}},

(97)

where
{\bm{T}}
is of the same dimension of
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
. We note that the choice of decomposition does not matter.

Then, plugging in the definition of
{\bm{P}}
, we can see that

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}{\bm{P}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})\right]\cdot{\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right]

(98)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{T}}^{\top}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\bm{T}}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})\right].

(99)

A key observation to connect the above equation to the adversarial attack (equation
19
) is that,

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star},\epsilon}({\bm{x}})\

\displaystyle=\quad\argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\ \|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}

(100)

\displaystyle=\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\quad\|\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}\|_{{\bm{H}}_{\star}}^{2}

(101)

\displaystyle=\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\quad\bm{\delta}^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{H}}_{\star}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}

(102)

\displaystyle=\ \argmax_{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\quad\|{\bm{T}}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}.

(103)

That is being said, the adversarial attack is the right singular vector corresponding to the largest singular value (in absolute value) of
{\bm{T}}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}
.

Similarly, we can see the singular values
\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, defined as the descending (in absolute value) singular values of the Jacobian
\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\cdot\times n}
in the
{\bm{H}}_{\star}
inner product space (equation
24
), are the singular values of
{\bm{T}}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}
.

With this perspective, if we write down the singular value decomposition of
{\bm{T}}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}})^{\top}={\bm{U}}_{\star}({\bm{x}})\Sigma_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{V}}_{\star}^{\top}({\bm{x}}),

(104)

we can observe that:

1.

\Sigma_{\star}({\bm{x}})
is diagonalized singular values
\bm{\sigma}_{f_{\star},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}({\bm{x}})
;

2.

The
i^{th}
column of
{\bm{V}}_{\star}({\bm{x}})
is the
i^{th}
generalized attack
\bm{\delta}_{f_{\star}}^{(i)}({\bm{x}})
(defined in equation
25
);

3.

The
i^{th}
column of
{\bm{U}}_{\star}({\bm{x}})\Sigma({\bm{x}})
is
{\bm{T}}\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})
where
\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})
is the output deviation (defined in equation
26
);

4.

The
i^{th}
column of
\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{V}}_{\star}({\bm{x}})
is the output deviation
\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})
(defined in equation
26
).

With the four key observations, we can break down the Jacobian matrices as

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\star}({\bm{x}}){\bm{T}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta^{(1)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})\dots\Delta^{(n)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta^{(1)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\Delta^{(n)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}

(105)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}.

(106)

Therefore, plugging it into the equation
99
, we have

\displaystyle(\ref{eq:1-2-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{T}}\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}})\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\right]

(107)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{1})\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({\bm{T}}\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{2})^{\top}\right)\right]

(108)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{1})\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{2})^{\top}\right)

(109)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})\right)\cdot\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{1})\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{2})^{\top}\right),

(110)

where the last equality is due to that
\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})
is a scalar value.

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla f_{\diamond}({\bm{x}})].

Proof.

Combining the result from Lemma
D.1

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{\star}^{\top}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{H}}_{\star}}-\bigg\langle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{H}}_{\star}\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\star}}({\bm{x}}_{2})\right)\cdot\left(\Delta^{(i)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{1})\Delta^{(j)}_{f_{\star}\to f_{\diamond}}({\bm{x}}_{2})^{\top}\right),{\bm{J}}^{\dagger}\bigg\rangle

As the generalized first adversarial transferability

Lemma E.1

(Compatibility of
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{H}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
)
.

Let
{\bm{H}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
be a positive semi-definite matrix, and denote
{\bm{H}}={\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}
as its symmetric decomposition with
{\bm{T}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
. For
{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
and
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}\|_{\bm{H}}\leq\|{\bm{W}}\|_{\bm{H}}\cdot\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}.

(205)

Proof.

\displaystyle\|{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}\|^{2}_{\bm{H}}

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}=\|{\bm{T}}{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}\|^{2}_{2}

(206)

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bm{T}}{\bm{W}}\|^{2}_{2}\cdot\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|{\bm{T}}{\bm{W}}\|^{2}_{F}\cdot\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}_{2},

(207)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.
Then, we can continue as

\displaystyle\|{\bm{T}}{\bm{W}}\|^{2}_{F}=\tr({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{W}})=\tr({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}{\bm{W}})=\|{\bm{W}}\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}.

(208)

Combining the above two parts, we have the lemma proved.
∎

Lemma E.2

(Expectation Preserves the Inclusion Relationship Between Linear Spaces)
.

Given a distribution
{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we denote the associated probability measure as
\mu
. Given linear maps
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{x}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
{\bm{N}}_{\bm{x}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, noting that they are both functions of
{\bm{x}}
, we have the following statement.

\displaystyle\ker\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{\bm{M}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}\right)\subseteq\ker\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}\right),

(209)

where
\ker(\cdot)
denotes the kernel space of a given liner map.

Proof.

It suffice to show for
\forall{\bm{v}}\in\ker\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{\bm{M}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}\right)
, we also have
{\bm{v}}\in\ker\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}\right)
.

Denote
{\bm{P}}:=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{\bm{M}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}
, and let
{\bm{v}}\in\ker({\bm{P}})
, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}{\bm{v}}=\bm{0}.

(210)

Noting that
{\bm{P}}
is positive semi-definite, we have the following equivalent statements.

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}\in\ker({\bm{P}})\quad\iff\quad{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}{\bm{v}}=0,

(211)

where the ’
\implies
’ direction is trivial, and the ’
\impliedby
’ direction can be proved by decomposing
{\bm{P}}={\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}
as two matrices and noting that

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{v}}=0\quad\implies\quad\|{\bm{T}}{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}=0\quad\implies\quad{\bm{T}}{\bm{v}}=\bm{0}\quad\implies\quad{\bm{T}}^{\top}{\bm{T}}{\bm{v}}=\bm{0}\quad\implies{\bm{P}}{\bm{v}}=\bm{0}.

(212)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=0

(213)

\displaystyle\implies\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}{\bm{v}}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(214)

\displaystyle\implies\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\|{\bm{M}}_{x}{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(215)

\displaystyle\implies\quad\int\|{\bm{M}}_{x}{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=0,

(216)

which implies
{\bm{M}}_{x}{\bm{v}}=\bm{0}
almost everywhere w.r.t.
\mu
.

Therefore, applying
{\bm{v}}
to
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}]
and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}\right]{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\int{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}{\bm{v}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

(217)

\displaystyle=\int_{a.e.}{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}\bm{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

(218)

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(219)

which means
{\bm{v}}\in\ker\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}{\bm{N}}_{x}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{x}\right)
.

∎

Lemma E.3

(Inverse an Injective Linear Map)
.

Given a full-rank injective affine transformation
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we denote its matrix representation as
g({\bm{v}})={\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}+{\bm{b}}
where
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times m},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The inverse of
g
is
g^{-1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
defined by
g^{-1}({\bm{w}}):={\bm{W}}^{\dagger}{\bm{w}}-{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}{\bm{b}}
for
{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
i.e.
,
g^{-1}\circ g
is the identity function. Moreover, given a positive semi-definite matrix
{\bm{H}}
, for
\forall{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\forall{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\|({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}\|_{F}\cdot\|{\bm{H}}\|_{F}}\cdot\|{\bm{w}}-g({\bm{v}})\|_{2}\geq\|{\bm{v}}-g^{-1}({\bm{w}})\|_{\bm{H}}.

(220)

Proof.

First, let us verify that
g^{-1}\circ g
is the identity function. The conditions of
g
being full-rank and injective are equivalent to
{\bm{W}}
being full-rank and
d\geq m
. That is being said,
{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}
is invertible and
{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}=({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{W}}^{\top}
. Therefore, for
\forall{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we have

\displaystyle g^{-1}\circ g({\bm{v}})

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}^{\dagger}({\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}+{\bm{b}})-{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}{\bm{b}}={\bm{W}}^{\dagger}{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}

(221)

\displaystyle=({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}.

(222)

That is,
g^{-1}\circ g
is indeed the identity function.

Next, to prove the inequality, let us start from the right-hand-side of the inequality.

\displaystyle\|{\bm{v}}-g^{-1}({\bm{w}})\|_{\bm{H}}

\displaystyle=\|g^{-1}\circ g({\bm{v}})-g^{-1}({\bm{w}})\|_{\bm{H}}

(223)

\displaystyle=\|{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}(g({\bm{v}})-{\bm{w}})\|_{\bm{H}}

(224)

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}\|_{\bm{H}}\cdot\|g({\bm{v}})-{\bm{w}}\|_{2},

(225)

where the inequality is done by applying Lemma
E.1
.

To complete the prove, we can see that

\displaystyle\|{\bm{W}}^{\dagger}\|^{2}_{\bm{H}}

\displaystyle=\|({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{W}}^{\top}\|^{2}_{\bm{H}}=\tr({\bm{W}}({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{H}}({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{W}}^{\top})

(226)

\displaystyle=\tr(({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{H}}({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})=\tr(({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}{\bm{H}})

(227)

\displaystyle=\langle({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1},{\bm{H}}\rangle

(228)

\displaystyle\leq\|({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{W}})^{-1}\|_{F}\cdot\|{\bm{H}}\|_{F}.

(229)

Plugging the square root of equation
229
into equation
225
, we have the lemma proved.
∎
