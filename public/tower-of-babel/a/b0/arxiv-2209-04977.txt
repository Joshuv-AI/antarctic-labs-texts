Semi-supervised Triply Robust Inductive Transfer Learning
Tianxi Cai, Mengyan Li, Molei Liu

Abstract
In this work, we propose a S emi-supervised T riply R obust I nductive trans F er LE arning (STRIFLE) approach, which integrates heterogeneous data from a label-rich source population and a label-scarce target population and utilizes a large amount of unlabeled data simultaneously to improve the learning accuracy in the target population. Specifically, we consider a high dimensional covariate shift setting and employ two nuisance models, a density ratio model and an imputation model, to combine transfer learning and surrogate-assisted semi-supervised learning strategies effectively and achieve triple robustness. While the STRIFLE approach assumes the target and source populations to share the same conditional distribution of outcome Y Y given both the surrogate features 𝐒 {\bf S} and predictors 𝐗 {\bf X} , it allows the true underlying model of Y | 𝐗 Y\mid{\bf X} to differ between the two populations due to the potential covariate shift in 𝐒 {\bf S} and 𝐗 {\bf X} . Different from double robustness, even if both nuisance models are misspecified or the distribution of Y | 𝐒 , 𝐗 Y\mid{\bf S},{\bf X} is not the same between the two populations, the triply robust STRIFLE estimator can still partially use the source population when the shifted source population and the target population share enough similarities. Moreover, it is guaranteed to be no worse than the target-only surrogate-assisted semi-supervised estimator with an additional error term from transferability detection. These desirable properties of our estimator are established theoretically and verified in finite samples via extensive simulation studies. We utilize the STRIFLE estimator to train a Type II diabetes polygenic risk prediction model for the African American target population by transferring knowledge from electronic health records linked genomic data observed in a larger European source population.



Keywords:
Covariate shift, surrogate-assisted semi-supervised learning, high dimensional data, transfer learning, model misspecification, robustness.

1
Introduction

1.1
Background

A precision medicine approach is vitally important for clinical care
(
Ashley 2016
)
.
The reliable and precise personalized prediction of clinical outcomes and treatment responses is fundamental for successful disease management.
More aggressive treatments should be considered for those unlikely to respond to standard therapy while less toxic options may be considered for patients with a favorable prognosis.
Advances in genetics and the growing availability of electronic health records (EHR) present increased opportunities to develop accurate personalized risk prediction models. Incorporating such models at the bedside makes precise personalized patient care a clinical reality
(
Hodson 2016
)
.

Personalized risk prediction modeling, however, faces several challenges in practice. The first challenge is the heterogeneity and imbalance across sub-populations.
Current prevalent predictive modeling schemes often exhibit biases when applied to heterogeneous and noisy EHR and genomic data, tending to prioritize overall accuracy at the expense of representing under-represented groups.
This leads to performance disparities caused by varying data completeness, distribution discrepancies, and coding biases.
For instance, about 79% of GWAS participants are of European descent.
This imbalance, together with genetic differences across ancestral populations, results in polygenic risk scores being several times more accurate for the European group than others
(
West et al. 2017
;
Ferryman & Pitcan 2018
;
Martin et al. 2019
)
.
The presence of heterogeneity and the relatively low sample size of the under-represented groups highlight the need for robust transfer learning techniques to effectively improve prediction accuracy in the underrepresented sub-populations and achieve fairness in precision medicine.

The second challenge arises from the lack of direct observation of the clinical outcomes needed for training supervised prediction models.
Precise clinical outcome data often require extensive manual chart review, which is not scalable. Alternative sources like diagnosis codes are readily available but lack accuracy. To efficiently leverage both the limited labeled data and the imperfect surrogates, it is critical to incorporate
semi-supervised (SS) learning
strategies to combine information from both labeled and unlabeled observations. The third challenge comes from the high dimensionality of the feature space. Both the number of candidate predictors, such as genetic markers, and the number of candidate surrogates from EHR can be large.

Much progress has been made in transfer learning and SS learning in recent years, both in low and high dimensional settings. However, there is a paucity of literature on robust transfer learning under SS settings to leverage the large amount of unlabeled source and target data while the amount of target labeled data is small. We aim to address this gap by developing a
S
emi-supervised
T
riply
R
obust
I
nductive trans
F
er
LE
arning (STRIFLE) method that effectively leverages such data structure to improve the estimation and prediction accuracy of risk modeling in the underrepresented target population. Specifically, we consider a covariate shift setting, which allows the predictors,
{\bf X}
, and the surrogates of the labels,
{\bf S}
, to have different distributions in the target and the source population. We assume that the two populations share the same conditional distribution of the outcome
Y
given
{\bf X}
and
{\bf S}
, but allow the conditional distribution of the outcome
Y
given
{\bf X}
to differ between the two populations. In EHR applications, since
{\bf S}
can be highly predictive of the response,
Y
given
{\bf X}
and
{\bf S}
tends to be more consistent across populations compared with
Y
given
{\bf X}
.
In both populations, we have a small number of labeled observations and a large number of unlabeled observations, respectively. Further, the number of the candidate predictors and the surrogates can be much larger than the number of labeled observations.

1.2
Related Literature and Our Contributions

Our work is closely related to several tracks of recent literature in statistical learning. In this section, we shall introduce them separately and summarize our methodological novelty and contribution in view of the existing literature.

Surrogate-assisted semi-supervised learning.

In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on SS learning that makes use of the large amount of unlabeled data to improve the estimation and prediction accuracy with the small amount of labeled data
(
Zhu 2005
;
Chakrabortty & Cai 2018
;
Gronsbell et al. 2022
, e.g.)
. Moreover, in biomedical and EHR studies, highly predictive surrogates of the disease status, such as the diagnostic codes, medication prescriptions, and laboratory tests are readily available for the unlabeled observations. To improve the learning accuracy,
Hou et al. 2021
proposed a robust surrogate-assisted semi-supervised (SAS) method that imputes the unobserved outcome (e.g. the disease status) by utilizing the candidate predictors (e.g. genetic variants) as well as the surrogate EHR features via a sparse working imputation model.

Zhang et al. 2022
proposed an SS method that adaptively incorporates prior knowledge from EHR surrogates by shrinking the high dimensional sparse estimator towards a direction derived from the unlabeled data with surrogates. However, none of these existing methods can handle covariate shift between the source population and the target population, which could lead to biased or inefficient estimation of the target parameters.

Covariate shift correction.

Distributional shift of the covariates between the training (source) and testing (target) data has been frequently studied in recent years. While importance weighting
(
Huang et al. 2006
, e.g.)

is not robust to misspecified models or excessive estimation errors of the density ratio model. In recent years, methods have been developed for doubly robust covariate shift correction
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2022
, e.g.)
. Motivated by semiparametric theory, they introduce an imputation model for
Y
to augment the importance weighted estimating equations. The resulting estimator is valid when either the density ratio or the imputation model is correctly specified. The main limitation of this strategy is that it purely relies on labels from the source population and fails to give a valid estimation when both models are misspecified.
The limitation is further exacerbated if the conditional model of
Y
varies between the source and target populations.
Under the scenario considered in our paper, there is a space to overcome this limitation due to the availability of a small amount of labeled data from the target population.

Knowledge transferring regression.

Recently,
Bastani 2021
,
Li et al. 2022
,
Tian & Feng 2022
, and
Li et al. 2023
proposed trans-Lasso, a transfer learning method for high dimensional linear models and generalized linear models (GLM) with Lasso penalty
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
to improve the learning accuracy on target population by adaptively borrowing information from potentially transferable source data.
Their core idea is to shrink source estimators learned using source labeled data towards target parameters through Lasso penalties. Based on this trans-Lasso framework,
Li et al. 2021
proposed a federated transfer learning approach that integrates heterogeneous data from diverse populations and multiple healthcare institutions.
Different from us, existing methods on trans-Lasso neither adjust for covariate shift between the source and target data nor incorporate unlabeled target observations with surrogates. As will be verified in our theoretical and numerical studies, this could cause a large efficiency loss in our setting.

Semi-supervised transfer learning

Our proposed STRIFLE method also relates to the SS transfer learning literature under a setting where the target data has abundant unlabeled samples to assist the transfer learning, sometimes as well as the source. For example,
Abuduweili et al. 2021
proposed an adaptive consistency regularization method for deep learning that involves two penalization terms ensuring adaptive knowledge transfer and representation consistency between the labeled and unlabeled target samples, respectively.
Jakubovitz et al. 2023
improved the
transferability of neural networks (NN) by incorporating regularization terms on the large unlabeled target data based on mutual (or Lautum) information. In the context of statistical learning closer to us,
Zhang et al. 2023a
developed a doubly robust SS method for the mean estimation on a target population with covariate shift.

Zhang et al. 2023b
further extended this framework to the SS transfer learning of casual effects. However, this track of work did not address certain main challenges in transfer learning, including model shift and high-dimensionality of the target models. Neither did they handle potential misspecification of the parametric nuisance models adequately.

Our contribution.

The STRIFLE method we propose stands out for being both surrogate-assisted and model-assisted.
Assisted by predictive surrogates, we employ a working and nuisance imputation model under a moment constraint to impute the outcomes for the unlabeled target observations. To integrate the label-rich source population, we introduce a working and nuisance density ratio model embedded in a penalized transfer learning procedure when estimating the imputation model.
The novel embedded density ratio model, estimated using unlabeled observations from the two populations, can potentially increase the transferability of the source population.

Unlike the naive combination of transfer learning and SS learning, which is vulnerable to model misspecification and may lead to erroneous results practically, STRIFLE unifies transfer learning and SAS techniques using the two nuisance models as bridges, and achieves ‘triple robustness’.
Building upon the concept of ‘double robustness’ in the literature, where robustness is achieved if at least one of two nuisance models is correctly specified, our approach introduces a third layer of robustness, augmented by a bias correction step and a transferability detection procedure, benefiting from the availability of a small amount of labeled target data. The concept of triple robustness is characterized by three key features.
First, it extends beyond double robustness by being more resilient to misspecification of nuisance models.
Even if both nuisance models are misspecified, STRIFLE can still partially utilize source labeled data to boost statistical efficiency, provided there are sufficient similarities between the source population shifted based on the working density ratio model and the target population.
Second, it is robust to potential violation of the conditional distributions of
Y
given
{\bf S}
and
{\bf X}
being identical between populations, as long as
the shifted source imputation model and the target imputation model are similar enough. Third,
STRIFLE is an adaptive transferring approach in the sense that it is guaranteed to be no worse than the target-only SAS estimator with an additional error term from transferability detection.
In addition, our framework is not restricted to Lasso penalty, and allows the use of non-convex penalties such as SCAD
(
Fan & Li 2001
)
and MCP
(
Zhang 2010
)
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that leverages information from the source population and simultaneously utilizes unlabeled observations from both populations, achieving a higher level of robustness.

2.1
Problem Setup

For the
i
-th observation,
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the outcome variable,
{\bf X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the
p
-dimensional candidate predictors including the intercept term as its first element, and
{\bf S}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
denotes the
q
-dimensional surrogates for
Y_{i}
.
We let
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
denote the target and source populations;
L
and
U
index the labeled and unlabeled subsets; and
\mathcal{I}_{\iota,\jmath}
denote the index set of observations from target/source labeled/unlabeled populations with
\iota\in\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}\}
and
\jmath\in\{L,U\}
. We assume that
Y
is only observed in the labeled subsets while
{\bf Z}=({\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T},{\bf S}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
is observed in both labeled and unlabeled data. We let

\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}=\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}\cup\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},U}
and
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}=\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S},L}\cup\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S},U}
denote the observed data from the target and the source populations with

\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\iota,L}=\{(Y_{i},{\bf Z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T},i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\iota,L}\}
,
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\iota,U}=\{{\bf Z}_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\iota,U}\}
,

\iota=\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}
.
Let
|\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}|=n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
,
|\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},U}|=N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
,
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S},L}|=n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}
,
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S},U}|=N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}
,
d=p+q
, and
n=n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
.
We consider the high dimensional setting where
p
and
q
grow with
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
and can be much larger than
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
and
n
, and allow
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}
and
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
to vary freely. While this work is primarily motivated by an SS context, our theoretical results also accommodate scenarios where

N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\leq n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
.

Let
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\iota)}_{\bf Z}
,
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\iota)}_{Y\mid{\bf Z}}
,
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\iota)}_{{\bf S}\mid{\bf X}}
, and
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\iota)}_{Y\mid{\bf X}}
denote the probability density of
{\bf Z}_{i}
, the conditional density of
Y_{i}
given
{\bf Z}_{i}
, the conditional density of
{\bf S}_{i}
given
{\bf X}_{i}
, and the conditional density of
Y_{i}
given
{\bf X}_{i}
, respectively, in the population
\iota
, for
\iota\in\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}\}
.
We consider covariate shift structure in this work, that is, we assume
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{S})}_{Y\mid{\bf Z}}(y,{\bf z})=p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}_{Y\mid{\bf Z}}(y,{\bf z})

while allowing
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{S})}_{\bf Z}({\bf z})
to be different from
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}_{\bf Z}({\bf z})
.
Specifically, we allow
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{S})}_{{\bf S}\mid{\bf X}}\neq p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}_{{\bf S}\mid{\bf X}}
and consequently,
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{S})}_{Y\mid{\bf X}}
can be different from
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}_{Y\mid{\bf X}}
.
We let
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\iota}(\cdot)
denote the expectation taken over the distribution in the population
\iota\in\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}\}
.

We aim to develop a prediction model for
Y\mid{\bf X}
in
\mathcal{T}
under a
working model

E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}(Y_{i}\mid{\bf X}_{i})=g({\bf X}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}})
, where
g(\cdot)
is a known inverse link function. Specifically, our goal is to estimate
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the solution to

\displaystyle E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}[{\bf X}_{i}\{Y_{i}-g({\bf X}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}})\}]={\bf 0}.

(1)

We allow
g({\bf X}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}})
to be misspecified for
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}(Y_{i}\mid{\bf X}_{i})
and
allow
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
to be dense.
Although
{\bf S}_{i}
can be strongly informative of
Y_{i}
, the outcome prediction model in (
1
) does not include
{\bf S}_{i}
.
In the motivating example of developing a polygenic risk prediction model for T2D,
Y
is the T2D status,
{\bf S}
comprises EHR features like Hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) test mentions and metformin prescriptions, and
{\bf X}
includes demographic variables and genetic markers.
Though the surrogates represent noisy proxies of the disease status and can be used to impute the missing
Y
, they are not suitable as predictors when the prediction is performed at some baseline time prior to the ascertainment of
Y
or its noisy proxy
{\bf S}
.

2.2
Overview of the STRIFLE Algorithm

We first give an overview of the STRIFLE procedure. To leverage the unlabeled data and the surrogates, STRIFLE employs a series of robust imputation strategies under a
working
imputation model for
Y\mid{\bf Z}
:

m({\bf Z})=E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}(Y\mid{\bf Z})=E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}(Y\mid{\bf Z})=g({\bf Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}})
.
For any fitted imputation model
\widehat{m}({\bf Z})=g({\bf Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\widehat{\bm{\theta}})
, let
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
be the limit of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
when the sample size goes to infinity, and
\bar{m}({\bf Z})=g({\bf Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bar{\bm{\theta}})
be the limiting imputation model.
We construct an SS estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
utilizing the fitted imputation model as

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})=\arg\min_{{\bm{\beta}}\in\Omega_{\bm{\beta}}}\mathcal{L}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}^{{\bm{\beta}}}({\bm{\beta}};\widehat{\bm{\theta}})+p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\beta}}}}({\bm{\beta}})
,
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}^{{\bm{\beta}}}({\bm{\beta}};\widehat{\bm{\theta}})=-\frac{1}{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},U}}Q\{g({\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}}),g({\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}}Q\{g({\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}}),y_{i}\}\right],

(2)

Q(\cdot,\cdot)
is the log-(quasi-)likelihood function (after dropping any dispersion parameter), i.e.,

Q\{g({\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}}),\widehat{m}({\bf z}_{i})\}=\widehat{m}({\bf z}_{i}){\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}}-G({\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}})
,
Q\{g({\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}}),y_{i}\}=y_{i}{\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}}-G({\bf x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\beta}})
,

G(u)=\int_{0}^{u}g(u^{\prime})du^{\prime}
,
p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\beta}}}}({\bm{\beta}})
is a penalty function on
{\bm{\beta}}
with a tuning parameter
\lambda_{\bm{\beta}}
, and
\Omega_{\bm{\beta}}
is the
p
-dimensional parameter space. Detailed definition of
\Omega_{\bm{\beta}}
is given in Section
3
.

By the definition of
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
in (
1
), it is straightforward to show that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is consistent for
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
provided that

E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}[{\bf X}_{i}\{Y_{i}-\bar{m}({\bf Z}_{i})\}]={\bf 0}.

(3)

A straightforward way to ensure the consistency of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

is to solely rely on the target labeled data for estimating
{\bm{\theta}}
as in
Hou et al. 2021
.
However, this approach misses the opportunity to benefit from the abundant labeled data in
\mathcal{S}
.
In covariate shift setting where
p_{Y\mid{\bf Z}}^{(\mathcal{S})}=p_{Y\mid{\bf Z}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
but
p_{Y\mid{\bf X}}^{(\mathcal{S})}
can
differ significantly from
p_{Y\mid{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
, the learning accuracy of
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
can be improved if we can borrow useful information from
\mathcal{S}
when estimating
m({\bf z})
.
Thus, our STRIFLE algorithm centers its strategy on robust and efficient estimation of
{\bm{\theta}}
by leveraging useful information from
\mathcal{S}
.

Specifically, we employ a
working
density ratio model to improve the transferability of the source data in a double-robust manner.
Note that when both
working
imputation and density ratio models are misspecified, the estimation of
{\bm{\theta}}
can be inconsistent.
To address this, we use the limited labeled target data for bias correction. However, if the
working
models are severely flawed,
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
may be insufficient for full bias correction.
To avoid negative transfer, we include a transferability detection step.
If the discrepancy between the shifted source and target imputation models is too large, we opt to not use the source data.
The STRIFLE estimation of
{\bm{\theta}}
consists of three key components:

1.

fitting a
working
density ratio model to estimate
w({\bf z})=p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}_{\bf Z}({\bf z})/p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{S})}_{\bf Z}({\bf z})
as
\widehat{w}({\bf z})
;

2.

constructing an initial target-only estimator of the imputation parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
as
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
as well as a
meta-learning estimator,
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}
, using
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\cup\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}
and
\widehat{w}({\bf z})
;

3.

constructing the STRIFLE estimator of
{\bm{\theta}}
,
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
, as a convex combination of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
and
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}
to overcome potential negative transfer.

The final STRIFLE estimator for
{\bm{\beta}}
is then obtained as

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)})
.
We detail each of the components below.

2.3
Estimation of the Density Ratio Model

To potentially increase the transferability of the source population and efficiently leverage the source data, we fit a
working
density ratio model utilizing the large number of unlabeled observations from both populations for
p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}_{\bf Z}({\bf z})/p^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{S})}_{\bf Z}({\bf z})
:

w({\bf z})=\exp\{{\bf f}({\bf z})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\zeta}}\},

(4)

where
{\bf f}(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
is a vector of basis functions that can be used to capture non-linear effects.
Equation (
4
) encapsulates a wide range of models.
{\bf f}(\cdot)
can be derived from prior knowledge related to covariate shifts or estimated directly from unlabeled data.
The methodology with estimated basis functions
\widehat{\bf f}({\bf z})
aligns well with various machine learning (ML) techniques.
For example, in multi-layer NN, the final layer acts as a simple linear classifier
(
Papyan et al. 2020
)
,
and
\widehat{\bf f}({\bf z})
can be obtained by fitting the NN (excluding the last layer) with the unlabeled data.
However, it’s important to recognize that even when employing ML methods for density ratio estimation, the challenges of model misspecification and excessive error persist, as discussed in
Dukes et al. 2021
. Hence, the bias correction and the transferability detection given in Section
2.4
and
2.5
are still necessary. This issue largely hinges on the potential non-convexity of ML methods
, complicated tuning procedures, and the balance between model complexity and the size of unlabeled data.
In Section
3
, the theoretical results are derived assuming the known nature of
{\bf f}(\cdot)
. Further discussions on
\widehat{\bf f}({\bf z})
and ML methods are presented in Remark
1
.

One key observation related to estimating
{\bm{\zeta}}
is that
if
w({\bf z})
is correctly specified, for any measurable function
{\bf h}({\bf z})
, we have
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\{w({\bf Z}_{i}){\bf h}({\bf Z}_{i})\}=E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\{{\bf h}({\bf Z}_{i})\}
.
Here we let
{\bf h}({\bf z})={\bf f}({\bf z})
, as also adopted in
Tan et al. 2020
and

Imai & Ratkovic 2014
.
Other
{\bf h}({\bf z})
can be used to construct the loss function for
{\bm{\zeta}}
as discussed in Section S.1 of Supplementary Materials.
We define the population parameter
{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}
as the minimizer of

E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\left[\exp\left\{{\bf f}({\bf Z}_{i})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\zeta}}\right\}\right]-E_{\mathcal{T}}\left\{{\bf f}({\bf Z}_{i})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\zeta}}\right\}
.
Many standard estimators can be used to estimate
{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}
.
In our theoretical derivations, we do not restrict to any specific estimator provided that its convergence rates satisfy Assumption
1
given in Section
3
.

One such estimator for
{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}
can be constructed as
\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\zeta}}\in\Omega_{{\bm{\zeta}}}}\mathcal{L}_{N}^{w}({\bm{\zeta}})+p_{\lambda_{\bm{\zeta}}}({\bm{\zeta}})
,
where

\mathcal{L}_{N}^{w}({\bm{\zeta}})=N_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S},U}}\exp\{{\bf f}({\bf z}_{i})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\zeta}}\}-N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},U}}{\bf f}({\bf z}_{i})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\zeta}}
,
p_{\lambda_{\bm{\zeta}}}({\bm{\zeta}})
is a penalty function with a tuning parameter
\lambda_{\bm{\zeta}}
, and
\Omega_{\bm{\zeta}}
is the feasible parameter space of
{\bm{\zeta}}
.
One may choose
p_{\lambda_{\bm{\zeta}}}({\bm{\zeta}})
as commonly used penalty functions including the ridge, Lasso, SCAD, etc.
Here and after, with a slight abuse of notation, we use
p_{\lambda}(\cdot)
to denote different types of penalty functions with a tuning parameter
\lambda
.
We show in Section S.2 of Supplementary Materials that
\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}
obtained with a Lasso penalty and
\Omega_{\bm{\zeta}}=\mathbb{R}^{L}
satisfies Assumption
1
.

2.4
Target-only and Meta-learning of the Imputation Model

We next construct two estimators for
{\bm{\theta}}
in
m({\bf z})
, one using
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}
only and one via meta-learning combining
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
and
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}
. These estimators are derived to achieve both robustness and efficiency of the estimation of the target parameter
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
in the presence of potential model misspecifications. To this end, we first provide two key observations about the estimation of
m({\bf z})
.
First, the target unlabeled observations can be utilized to consistently estimate
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
with missing labels imputed by any consistent estimator of
\bar{m}({\bf z})
satisfying the moment condition (
3
).
We define

\displaystyle\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}=\arg\max_{{\bm{\theta}}}E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\left[Q\left\{g({\bf Z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}),Y_{i}\right\}\right],

(5)

where
Q\{g({\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}),y_{i}\}=y_{i}{\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}-G({\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}})
,
and the resulting
\bar{m}({\bf z})=g({\bf z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bar{{\bm{\theta}}})
satisfies the moment condition (
3
), even if the imputation model is misspecified.
With target labeled data, one straightforward estimator of
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
is

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\Omega_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}}\left[-{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}}Q\left\{g({\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}),y_{i}\right\}+p_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}})\right]

where
p_{\lambda}({\bm{\theta}})
is a penalty function on
{\bm{\theta}}
with a tuning parameter

\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}

{\bm{\theta}}

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}

\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}

Using

The second key observation is that, under our covariate shift setting and the moment condition (

E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}[w_{0}({\bf Z}_{i}){\bf Z}_{i}\{Y_{i}-\bar{m}({\bf Z}_{i})\}]={\bf 0}

We further define

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\arg\max_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left\{\frac{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}}{n}E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}\left[w_{0}({\bf Z}_{i})Q\left\{g({\bf Z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}),Y_{i}\right\}\right]+\frac{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}{n}E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\left[Q\left\{g({\bf Z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}),Y_{i}\right\}\right]\right\},

(6)

and
m^{*}({\bf z})=g({\bf z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}^{*})
.
When either
m({\bf z})
or
w({\bf z})

{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
and one may estimate
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}

\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
.
However, when both models are misspecified or
p_{Y|{\bf Z}}
differs between populations,
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\neq\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
and such an approach could lead to a biased estimator for
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
.

Inspired by the idea of oracle Trans-Lasso
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Tian & Feng 2022
)
,
we propose a two-step density-ratio-embedded
meta-learning
algorithm to estimate
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
by correcting the potential bias
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}=\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
, as summarized in Algorithm
1
.
Different from oracle Trans-Lasso, we deal with a more complex loss function with an estimated
working
density ratio embedded in it.

1. Using
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S},L}\cup\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}
along with
\widehat{w}({\bf z})=\exp({\bf z}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\widehat{\bm{\zeta}})
, we estimate
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
as
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}_{n}({\bm{\theta}};\widehat{\bm{\zeta}})+p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bm{\theta}})
,
where

\mathcal{L}_{n}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{\zeta}})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S},L}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}}w({\bf z}_{i};{\bm{\zeta}})^{I(i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S},L})}Q\left\{g({\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bf z}_{i}),y_{i}\right\},

(7)

I(i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S},L})
is an indicator function,
p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bm{\theta}})

is a penalty function on
{\bm{\theta}}
with a tuning parameter
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
, and
\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}
is the feasible parameter space with the detailed definition given in Section
3
.

2. Estimate
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
using
\mathscr{D}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}
with
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
as

\widehat{\bm{\delta}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\delta}}\in\Omega_{{\bm{\delta}}}}\mathcal{L}_{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}})+p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\delta}}}}({\bm{\delta}})
,
to construct the meta-learning estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}=\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}+\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
, where

\mathcal{L}_{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}}):=-\frac{1}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}}Q[g\{{\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\delta}})\},y_{i}],

(8)

p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\delta}}}}({\bm{\delta}})
is a penalty function on
{\bm{\delta}}
with a tuning parameter
\lambda_{\bm{\delta}}
, and
\Omega_{\bm{\delta}}
is the feasible parameter space with the detailed definition given in Section
3
.

* With a slight abuse of notation,
p_{\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}}
and
p_{\lambda_{{\bm{\delta}}}}
can be different penalty functions with tuning parameter
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
and
\lambda_{{\bm{\delta}}}
, respectively.

Algorithm 1

Robust Meta-Learning of
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}

In the first step, we estimate
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
by minimizing the empirical version of negative of (
6
) with a penalty function.
Under covariate shift setting,
when at least one of the two working models is correctly specified,
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}={\bf 0}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
is consistent for
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
under standard high dimensional conditions. In this case, source labeled data can be fully utilized.
When
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\neq\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
yet
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
is relatively close to zero,
with the second bias correction step,
the source labeled data can still be partially utilized and hence the meta-learning estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}
can still outperform the target-only estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
.
However, the second step is unable to fully correct the bias in
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
when
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
is too dense to be fully recovered with limited labeled target data. This negative transfer phenomenon is verified in both our theoretical analyses in Section
3
and numerical studies in Section
4
. We overcome this in
our final step of the STRIFLE estimation as
detailed below.

2.5
STRIFLE Estimator of the Imputation Model

To overcome negative transfer, we define our STRIFLE estimator for
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
as a convex combination of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
and
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}
, and estimate the combination weight via data splitting. Specifically, we randomly split data in
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}
into two halves,
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
.
We use data in
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}
to construct the target-only estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T},k)}
, and use data in
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S},L}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}
to construct the meta-learning estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta,k)}
based on Algorithm
1
. The remaining data in
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}
are used to estimate the coefficient in the convex combination. Inspired by
Tian & Feng 2022
,
we consider a binary coefficient
\rho\in\{0,1\}
, inferring whether
\mathcal{S}
is transferable, which is estimated by comparing the loss
\mathcal{L}^{\rho^{\scriptscriptstyle(-k)}}_{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}/2}({\bm{\theta}})=2/n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}}G({\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}})-y_{i}{\bf z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\theta}}
at
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T},k)}
and
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta,k)}
. To mitigate the efficiency loss of data splitting, cross-fitting can be employed and

\widehat{\rho}=I\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{\rho^{\scriptscriptstyle(-k)}}_{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}/2}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta,k)})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{\rho^{\scriptscriptstyle(-k)}}_{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}/2}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T},k)})-\epsilon_{0}\right\},

(9)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is a non-negative constant chosen to achieve theoretical guarantee.
See Section S.4
of Supplementary Materials for more details of the choice of
\epsilon_{0}
.

The final STRIFLE estimator for
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
is defined as

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}=\widehat{\rho}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}+(1-\widehat{\rho})\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
,
which is no worse than
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
with an additional error from estimating
\rho
even if
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
significantly deviates from the zero vector. Consequently,
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
is no worse than the target-only SAS estimator accompanied by an extra error resulting from transferability detection. When
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
is equal to or close to zero
and
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}
is much larger than
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
,
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
can be substantially more efficient than SAS estimator.

3
Theoretical Results

We start by introducing some notations. For a vector
{\bf v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we
define
\|{\bf v}\|_{0}=|\rm{supp}({\bf v})|
, where
\rm{supp}({\bf v})=\{j:v_{j}\neq 0\}

and
|A|
is the cardinality of a set
A
. For
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}
, let
{\bf v}_{S}=\{v_{j}:j\in S\}
and
S^{c}
be the complement of
S
.
Denote
\|{\bf v}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|v_{j}|
, and
\|{\bf v}\|_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}|v_{j}|^{r}
, for
r\in(0,1]
.
Define
\mathbb{B}_{r}^{p}(R)=\{{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|{\bf v}\|_{r}\leq R\}
, for
r\in[0,1]
.
For two positive sequences
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
, we use
a_{n}\precsim b_{n}

to denote
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
for some constant
C>0
,
and use
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
to denote
C\leq a_{n}/b_{n}\leq C^{\prime}
for
some constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
.

We then introduce an assumption on the convergence rates of density ratio estimation, essential for the theoretical basis of our STRIFLE estimator.

Assumption 1
.

Denote by
\Omega_{0}
the event that

\|\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}-{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}\|_{1}\leq C_{{\bm{\zeta}}}
, and
\|\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}-{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=o(1)
,
where
C_{{\bm{\zeta}}}
is a positive constant, and
\hbox{pr}(\Omega_{0})
goes to 1 when the sample size goes to infinity.

Remark 1
.

As mentioned in Section
2.3
, there is great flexibility in estimating the density ratio model. Here, we focus on the parametric model
\exp\{{\bf f}({\bf z})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}{\bm{\zeta}}\}
with pre-specified
{\bf f}(\cdot)
, which is allowed to be misspecified, to simplify theoretical derivations.
As detailed in Section S.1
of Supplementary Materials, the density ratio estimation can be reformulated as a binary classification task, enabling the use of ML techniques.
In this case, Assumption
1
can be modified to
‘
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}[\{\widehat{w}({\bf Z})-w_{0}({\bf Z})\}^{2}]=o(1)
, and
\sup_{\bf Z}w_{0}({\bf Z})/\widehat{w}({\bf Z})
is bounded with probability goes to 1 when the sample size goes to infinity.’
Given sufficient unlabeled data from both populations, recent advances in convergence rates of ML methods, such as deep NN
(
Farrell et al. 2020
;
Farrell et al. 2021
)
and generalized random forests
(
Athey et al. 2019
)
, ensure that these methods do not compromise the theoretical performance of our STRIFLE estimator under corresponding conditions.

Regarding sparsity assumptions, similar to
Li et al. 2022
, we allow
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
to be soft sparse.
For
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
, we consider two sparsity scenarios.
In scenario 1, like existing work on TransLasso
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Tian & Feng 2022
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
)
, we assume
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
is
l_{0}
sparse.
While in scenario 2, we assume
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
and
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
are both
l_{r}
sparse, and consequently,
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\bar{\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
is also
l_{r}
sparse for a fixed
r\in[0,1]
.
In this section, we only present the detailed theoretical results for

l_{0}
sparse
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
and
l_{1}
sparse
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
, driven by the simplicity and ease of interpretation inherent in this specific scenario.
We provide a brief explanation of the theoretical findings in other scenarios in Remark
3
. The detailed theoretical results in general settings are deferred to Sections S.5-S.8 of Supplementary Materials.

We defer other Assumptions S1-S5 on the design matrices, models, penalty functions, sparsity of
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
,
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
and
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
to Section S.3
of Supplementary Materials.
In short, in the scenario where
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
is
l_{0}
sparse and
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
is
l_{1}
sparse,
we assume

{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\in\mathbb{B}_{1}^{d}(R_{{\bm{\delta}},1})
,

R_{{\bm{\delta}},1}\precsim\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}/\hbox{log}~d}
,

\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}^{d}(R_{\bar{\bm{\theta}},r})
,
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}\in\mathbb{B}^{p}_{r}(R_{{\bm{\beta}},r})
,
R_{\bar{\bm{\theta}},r}\precsim(n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}/\hbox{log}~d)^{1-r/2}
, and

R_{{\bm{\beta}},r}\precsim\{(N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}})/\hbox{log}~d\}^{1-r/2}

for
r=0,1
. With a slight abuse of notation, we define the true supports of
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
and
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
as
S_{\bm{\theta}}
and
S_{\bm{\beta}}
with cardinality
s_{\bm{\theta}}
and
s_{\bm{\beta}}
, respectively.
We also assume
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}\asymp\sqrt{n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}
,
\lambda_{\bm{\delta}}\asymp\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}
, and

\lambda_{\bm{\beta}}\asymp\sqrt{(N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}})^{-1}\hbox{log}~p}
.
We establish theoretical results under the framework of
Negahban et al. 2012
and
Loh & Wainwright 2015
allowing for nonconvex penalties like SCAD and MCP.
To facilitate theoretical derivations, similar to
Loh & Wainwright 2015
, we define the feasible parameter spaces as

\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}=\{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|{\bm{\theta}}\|_{1}\leq R_{\bar{\bm{\theta}},1}\}
,

\Omega_{\bm{\delta}}=\{{\bm{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|{\bm{\delta}}\|_{1}\leq R_{{\bm{\delta}},1}\}
, and

\Omega_{\bm{\beta}}=\{{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}\leq R_{{\bm{\beta}},1}\}
. Note that if the loss functions with penalties are convex, the feasible parameter spaces extend to

\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}=\Omega_{\bm{\delta}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Omega_{\bm{\beta}}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

3.1
Theoretical Properties of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}

We first establish the error bounds of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}
in the following Theorem with proofs given in Section S.6 of Supplementary Materials.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
, and S1-S4 hold.
We have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{1}=O_{p}

\displaystyle\left\{s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n}}+\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{\hbox{log}~d}}\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\left(1\vee\sqrt{\frac{n}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}}\rm{Err}_{{\bm{\zeta}}}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{2}^{2}=O_{p}

\displaystyle\left\{s_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}}\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}\wedge\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}^{2}+\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\left(1\vee\frac{n}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\right)\right\},

where
{\rm Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}=n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}^{2}n^{-2}(\|\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}-{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d\|\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}-{\bm{\zeta}}_{0}\|_{1}^{2})
.

Remark 2
.

As one referee pointed out, the
l_{1}
error rate in Theorem
1
is no better than the rate of
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\|_{1}+\|{\bm{\theta}}^{*}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\|_{1}
, which implies that the second bias correction step in Algorithm
1
does not improve the
l_{1}
error rate when
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
is
l_{1}
sparse.
This is because of the intrinsic properties of applying
l_{1}
type penalties to
l_{1}
sparse parameters, and it agrees with the minimax result proved in
Li et al. 2022
and
Li et al. 2023
.

Negahban et al. 2012
also shows that for high dimensional linear models, the
l_{1}
error of a penalized estimator with a decomposable penalty for a
l_{1}
sparse parameter is the
l_{1}
norm of the parameter itself, which is also the minimax rate
(
Raskutti et al. 2011
)
.
However, this bias correction step can help improve the
l_{2}
error.
Moreover, when we consider
l_{r}
sparse
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
,
r\in[0,1)
, the rate of

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\|_{1}
can be better than that of
\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\|_{1}+\|{\bm{\theta}}^{*}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\|_{1}
. Detailed results are given in Corollaries S1 and S2 in Supplementary Materials.
For example, when both
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
and
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
are
l_{0}
sparse and
\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{0}\precsim s_{\bm{\theta}}
,
we have

\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\|_{1}=O_{p}\left(s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}+\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\sqrt{n/\hbox{log}~d}\right)

and

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}\|_{1}=O_{p}\left\{s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}+\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{0}\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}+\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\sqrt{n/\hbox{log}~d}(1\vee\sqrt{n/n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}{\rm Err}_{\bm{\zeta}})\right\}.

When
\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{0}\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}=o(\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1})
, the bias correction step helps.

In typical SS settings, we have
\max(n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}},n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}})=o\{\min(N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{S}},N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}})\}
, resulting higher order estimation error for
\widehat{\bm{\zeta}}
, and simplified error bounds

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{1}=O_{p}\left(s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}+\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}\right)
, and

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{2}^{2}=O_{p}\left(s_{\bm{\theta}}n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d+\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}\wedge\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}^{2}\right).

When
w({\bf z})
or
m({\bf z})
is correctly specified, then
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}={\bf 0}
,

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{1}=O_{p}\left(s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}\right)
,

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf meta)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{2}^{2}=O_{p}\left(s_{\bm{\theta}}n^{-1}\hbox{log}~d\right)
,
which are the standard high dimensional error rates with a sample size of
n=n_{\mathcal{S}}+n_{\mathcal{T}}
. In other words, the source labeled data have been fully utilized to improve the estimation accuracy of
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
.
When
\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}=o\left(s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}\right)
, even if both of the working models are misspecified, we can still gain estimation accuracy by partially utilizing the source labeled observations.
When
s_{\bm{\theta}}\sqrt{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d}=o(\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1})
,
the error rates are worse than those of the target-only estimator and we begin to have the negative transfer.

Remark 3
.

In scenario 2, where both
{\bm{\delta}}_{0}
and
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
are
l_{r}
sparse for a fixed
r\in[0,1]
, by Corollary S2 in the Supplementary Materials, we begin to have negative transfer
when
\|\bar{\bm{\theta}}\|_{r}=o(\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{r})
. It agrees with the intuition that when the difference between the shifted source parameter and the target parameter is denser than the target parameter, the second step in Algorithm
1
using target labeled data could not fully correct the bias in
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}
, and hence the resulting meta-learning estimator is worse than the target-only estimator.

We present in Theorem
2
the upper error bound of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
, which overcomes negative transfer when
\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}
is large. The proof is given in Section S.7 of Supplementary Materials.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
, and S1-S4 hold.
We have

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{2}^{2}=O_{p}\left[\left\{\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n}s_{\bm{\theta}}+\sqrt{\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}}\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}\wedge\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}^{2}+\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\left(1\vee\frac{n}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\right)\right\}\wedge\left(\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}s_{\bm{\theta}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1/2}\right)\right].

We can see that when
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{1/2}/\hbox{log}~d\precsim s_{\bm{\theta}}
,

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}\|_{2}^{2}
is guaranteed to be no worse than that of the target-only estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}
. While when
s_{\bm{\theta}}=o(n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{1/2}/\hbox{log}~d)
, there is indeed a price to pay for the transferability detection step.
Similar results have been proved for general
l_{r}
sparsity. See Theorem S2 in Supplementary Materials.

3.2
Theoretical Properties of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}

With triply robust estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
, we can leverage target unlabeled data to robustly improve the learning accuracy of
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
.
Similar to the SAS estimator, we allow
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
to be much denser than
\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}
when
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}=o(N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}})
.
The upper error bound of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
is established in the following Theorem with its proofs given in Section S8 of Supplementary Materials.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
, and S1 - S5, let
\varrho=n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}/(n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}})
, and we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}-{\bm{\beta}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=O_{p}

\displaystyle\left((1-\varrho)\left[\left\{s_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}}\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}\wedge\|{\bm{\delta}}_{0}\|_{1}^{2}+\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\left(1\vee\frac{n}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}\rm{Err}_{\bm{\zeta}}\right)\right\}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\wedge\left(\frac{\hbox{log}~d}{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}s_{\bm{\theta}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1/2}\right)\right]+s_{\bm{\beta}}\frac{\hbox{log}~p}{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}+n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}}\right).

Remark 4
.

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\sf STRIFLE)}
is guaranteed to be no worse than the target-only supervised estimator
when
(1-\varrho)s_{{\bm{\theta}}}n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~d\precsim s_{{\bm{\beta}}}n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\hbox{log}~p
and
(1-\varrho)n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}^{1/2}/\hbox{log}~p\precsim s_{\bm{\beta}}
even if
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}\precsim n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T}}
.
These conditions are typically met in EHR applications with highly predictive surrogates, where

Simulation Studies

In this section, we evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed STRIFLE estimator for both estimation and prediction accuracy. We compare the STRIFLE estimator with four benchmark methods: (i) the supervised estimator (SUP) obtained by estimating
{\bm{\beta}}_{0}
directly using data in
\mathcal{I}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{T},L}

m({\bf z})
using data in

by minimizing (

In this section, we apply the proposed STRIFLE method to derive a T2D polygenic risk prediction model for the AA population, using SNP and EHR data from Mass General Brigham’s (MGB) Biobank.

Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we have proposed an adaptive transfer learning method in the SS learning framework.
Different from other existing methods, our method is both model-assisted and surrogate-assisted.
Compared with existing non-model-assisted transfer learning methods,
when a large number of unlabeled observations from both populations are available, which is common in large-scale biomedical studies, the density ratio model can be estimated relatively well, which consequently can improve the transferability of the source population.
By employing the imputation model with predictive surrogates, the target unlabeled observations can be effectively leveraged and the sparsity assumption on the final outcome model can be relaxed, which is important in practice. For example, in polygenic risk prediction models, usually the signals of SNPs are relatively weak and dense.
Instead of combining these two strategies scrappily, we construct our method, specifically the estimating equations, carefully so that it is robust against severe model misspecifications, distributional assumption violation, and negative transfer.

The proposed triply robust transferring procedure focuses on the estimation for the imputation model under the covariate shift setting.
In fact, the source unlabeled observations can only be transferred to improve the estimation rate of
{\bm{\beta}}
when the density ratio model is correctly specified, since
p_{Y\mid{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{T})}

.
How to robustly and efficiently conduct transfer learning directly on the outcome model, i.e., on
