Sequential optimal experimental design for vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling
Martin Bubel 1, Jochen Schmid 1, Volodymyr Kozachynskyi 2, Erik Esche 2, Michael Bortz 1 1 Fraunhofer Institute for Industrial Mathematics (ITWM), 67663 Kaiserslautern, Germany 2 Technische Universität Berlin, Process Dynamics and Operations Group, 10623 Berlin, Germany jochen.schmid@itwm.fraunhofer.de

Abstract
We propose a general methodology of sequential locally optimal design of experiments for explicit or implicit nonlinear models, as they abound in chemical engineering and, in particular, in vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling. As a sequential design method, our method iteratively alternates between performing experiments, updating parameter estimates, and computing new experiments. Specifically, our sequential design method computes a whole batch of new experiments in each iteration and this batch of new experiments is designed in a two-stage locally optimal manner. In essence, this means that in every iteration the combined information content of the newly proposed experiments and of the already performed experiments is maximized. In order to solve these two-stage locally optimal design problems, a recent and efficient adaptive discretization algorithm is used. We demonstrate the benefits of the proposed methodology on the example of of the parameter estimation for the non-random two-liquid model for narrow azeotropic vapor-liquid equilibria. As it turns out, our sequential optimal design method requires substantially fewer experiments than traditional factorial design to achieve the same model precision and prediction quality. Consequently, our method can contribute to a substantially reduced experimental effort in vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling and beyond.



1
Introduction

In order to reliably simulate and optimize engineering processes, one needs sufficiently precise mathematical models describing the functional relationships between relevant input and output quantities of the considered process. In many cases, these models come in the form of parametric models, that is, their predictions
f(x,\theta)
for the output quantity
y
of interest not only depend on the input quantity values
x
but also on additional model parameters
\theta
. A priori, the true value of these model parameters is unknown and therefore has to be estimated from appropriate experimental data. Specifically, at suitably selected input points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
one measures the values
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
of the output quantity and then adjusts the model parameters
\theta
in such a way that the discrepancy between the model’s predictions
f(x_{i},\theta)
and the measurements
y_{i}
becomes minimal in the least-squares sense.

Intuitively, it is clear that in general the quality of the thus obtained parameter estimate strongly depends on the selected experimental points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
or, in other words, on where one measures and on how often one measures. It is also clear that in practice one can often perform only a fairly limited number
n
of experiments, just because experiments in a lab are typically time- and cost-intensive. Consequently, it is of crucial importance to carefully plan and select the actually performed experiments. In particular, it is important that (i) the parameter estimate and the resulting predictions based on the selected experiments
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are sufficiently precise and that (ii) the number
n
of experiments is affordably small. Coming up with experimental plans that meet these criteria is the core task of experimental design.

In the extensive literature on experimental design, two main approaches can be distinguished, namely factorial experimental design on the one hand and optimal experimental design on the other hand. In factorial design, one spreads the experiments in a uniform and grid-like manner in the entire input space and, as a consequence, ends up with a relatively large number of experiments already for moderate input space dimensions. In optimal design, by constrast, one tries to make experiments only at places in the input space where enough information can be gained about the model parameters to be estimated. Specifically, one tries to distribute the experimental points such that the information content – and hence the precision – of the corresponding parameter estimate becomes optimal. As a consequence, with optimal design one can expect to obtain the same parameter and prediction quality as with factorial design, but with substantially fewer experiments. And this effect can be expected to be all the more pronounced, the more nonlinear the considered model
f
is, as a function of the inputs
x
or of the model parameters
\theta
. In light of this, optimal experimental design is a promising approach especially for vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling with its intrinsically nonlinear models for activity coefficients, for instance.

In the present paper, we propose a general methodology of sequential locally optimal experimental design
[
10
,
39
,
15
,
30
]
for general nonlinear models, and these nonlinear models can be defined either by explicit or by implicit algebraic equations. As a sequential design method, our method iteratively alternates between carrying out experiments, estimating the parameters, and designing new experiments. Specifically, the sequential design method proposed here is characterized by the following key features, which also distinguish it from the sequential design method recently proposed in
[
12
]
.

A first essential feature of our method is that, in every iteration, it computes a whole batch of new experiments, the maximal batch size being specified by the user. In contrast, the design methodology of
[
12
]
computes new experiments one by one (except for the initial iteration). We decided for batches of new experiments because this will generally lead to fewer alternations between the experimental and the computational phase of the procedure and thus to a smoother workflow in practice. In particular, this is beneficial when each experimental phase requires extensive set-up or ramp-up times or when the experiments and the computations are performed by different people at different institutions.

A second important feature of our method is that, in every iteration, the batch of new experiments is computed in a locally optimal manner, namely by solving a two-stage locally optimal experimental design problem. Specifically, the new experiments are designed such that the combined information content of the already performed experiments (stage one) and of the new experiments to be performed next (stage two) becomes maximal. As usual in two-stage locally optimal design, the combined information content is measured in terms of a suitable design criterion
\Psi
applied to the information matrix of a suitably linearized version of the model
f
at the combined experimental design. Also, the linearization of
f
, in every iteration, is performed about the current best parameter estimate, namely, about the least-squares parameter estimate based on all experimental data collected until the current iteration. As the design criterion
\Psi
, in turn, one can choose each of the usual standard criteria provided only it is differentiable. In particular, this includes the A- and the D-criterion, while it excludes essentially only the E-criterion.

A third distinctive feature of our method is that it solves the occurring two-stage locally optimal experimental design problems by means of the recent adaptive discretization algorithm from
[
48
]
, which is specifically tailored to such design problems. And, in fact, this algorithm exhibits very good convergence properties, both from a theoretical and from a practical point of view. We terminate our iterative alternation between experiments, parameter estimation, and locally optimal experimental design, if the total number of already performed and of newly proposed experiments exceeds a user-specified upper bound or if the newly proposed experiments do not differ substantially anymore from already performed ones.

In order to demonstrate the benefits of the proposed sequential design methodology for practical use, we apply it to the estimation of non-random two-liquid parameters. As is well-known, these parameters describe the binary interactions in the liquid phase of a multi-component mixture and therefore they play an essential role in vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling. It is also well-known that the estimation of these parameters is generally a challenging task, especially for azeotropic systems. In fact, the corresponding sum of squared errors often features flat local minima, multiple local minima, or even multiple global minima
[
41
,
22
,
46
]
.

In the present paper, we specifically consider the binary system consisting of propanol and propyl acetate and we measure the vapor composition and the temperature of this system at vapor-liquid equilibrium for different liquid compositions and pressures. It is known that this system exhibits narrow azeotropic behavior and therefore the estimation of its non-random two-liquid parameters is already a rather challenging task. Applying our sequential design method to this system, we end up with a locally optimal experimental design consisting of
15
experiments. And, as it turns out, these
15
optimally designed experiments lead to the same prediction quality as
27
experiments from a traditional factorial experimental design. So, compared to traditional factorial design, our sequential locally optimal design method substantially reduces the experimental effort – and thus helps the experimenter to save substantial amounts of time and money – without impairing the model prediction quality. Additionally, our method also helps the experimenter in deciding what to do in case there still is some experimental budget left, after all the experiments proposed by a first run of our sequential design method have been performed. Indeed, by its very definition, our method always indicates where making a batch of further experiments is most promising and where it is not.

Apart from the papers
[
11
,
12
]
, we are not aware of any other works that apply optimal design techniques
to the estimation of thermodynamic property parameters. It should be noticed that in the mentioned papers
[
11
,
12
]
no real lab experiments are performed, but simulated data are used instead, based on literature values for the non-random two-liquid parameters to be estimated. In sharp contrast to that, we perform actual lab experiments and only rely on the corresponding real experimental data. In particular, we do not rely on literature data or on literature values for the considered parameters at all.

2
Setting and preliminaries

In this section, we specify the setting and the basic terminology used throughout the paper. We also collect those facts from parameter estimation and experimental design that are relevant for the sequential locally optimal experimental design method proposed in this paper.

2.1
Setting and basic terminology

In the entire paper, we assume that the considered functional relationship between input quantities
x
and output quantities
y
can be described by the predictions
f(x,\theta)
of a parametric model

\displaystyle f:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\mathcal{Y},

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{x}}}
and
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
are the sets of admissible input values and of admissible model parameter values respectively, while
\mathcal{Y}:=\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{y}}}
is a superset of the set of possibly observable output values.

Such a parametric model
f
can be linear or nonlinear and, moreover, it can be explicit or implicit.
As usual,
f
is called
linear
iff it is affine-linear w.r.t.
\theta
, that is,

\displaystyle f(x,\theta)=c(x)+J(x)\theta\qquad((x,\theta)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}})

(2.2)

for some arbitrary functions
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{y}}}
and
J:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{y}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
. In particular, a linear model
f
can be arbitrarily nonlinear w.r.t. the inputs
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Simple examples of linear models are furnished by the polynomial functions in
x
with the monomial coefficients making up the model parameter vector
\theta
.
Additionally,
f
is called
explicit
iff it is explicitly defined by a closed-form expression for
f(x,\theta)
for every
(x,\theta)\in\mathcal{X}\times\Theta
. And correspondingly,
f
is called
implicit
iff one has no closed-form expression for
f(x,\theta)
but instead
f(x,\theta)
for every
(x,\theta)\in\mathcal{X}\times\Theta
is implicitly defined as the solution
y=h(s(x,\theta),x,\theta)
of a system of algebraic equations of the form

\displaystyle g(s,x,\theta)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=h(s,x,\theta)

(2.3)

with explicitly defined functions
g
and
h
. In this system of equations, the first equation (
2.3
.a) determines the state
s=s(x,\theta)
as a function of
(x,\theta)
, while the second equation (
2.3
.b) determines the output quantity
y=h(s(x,\theta),x,\theta)
as a function of the state.

Simple examples of explicit nonlinear models from chemical engineering are given by the Antoine model for the saturation vapor pressure of a pure substance or by the non-random two-liquid model for the activity coefficients of a mixture of substances. Similarly, a simple chemical engineering example of an implicit nonlinear model arises in the prediction of the bubble-point temperature of a mixture of substances. With this particular example of an implicit nonlinear model, we will illustrate the general methodology proposed in this paper. See Section
4
below.

We point out, though, that the proposed general methodology is by no means restricted to thermodynamic models but rather can be applied in a wide range of circumstances, to explicit and implicit nonlinear models alike. All that needs to be satisfied for the methodology to apply are a few rather mild regularity and representability assumptions on the considered model
f
. Specifically, the model
f
needs to be sufficiently regular (Assumption
1
) and sufficiently representative (Assumption
2
) in the sense that, up to additive random measurement errors, all possible output measurement values are explained by the model’s predictions with one specific model parameter value
\theta^{*}
. Spelled out in detail, these assumptions take the following form.

Assumption 1
.

(i)

\mathcal{X}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{x}}}
and
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
is a compact subset of
\mathcal{X}

(ii)

\Theta
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
that is equal to the closure of its interior, in short,
\overline{\Theta^{\circ}}=\Theta

(iii)

\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto f(x,\theta)
is continuously differentiable for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}\ni x\mapsto D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})
is continuous for every
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
. As usual,
D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})
denotes the derivative (Jacobi matrix) of
f(x,\cdot)
at
\overline{\theta}
.

Conventionally, the subset
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
of the input space
\mathcal{X}
from the assumption above is called the
experimental design space
. It consists of all potential experiments, that is, all input points
x\in\mathcal{X}
at which the experimenter can measure the output quantity. We point out that the experimental design space
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
can be discrete (finite) or continuous (infinite). In particular,
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
can be equal to
\mathcal{X}
, but in most experimental design algorithms
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
is chosen to be finite for the sake of compuational feasibility
[
15
,
30
,
48
,
12
,
12
]
. We also point out that the assumed regularity conditions are very mild and usually easy to verify. Indeed, for an explicit model one has only to investigate the explicit model parametrization
f
, and for an implicit model one has only to investigate the implicit model-defining functions
g
and
h
and invoke the implicit function theorem.

Assumption 2
.

A parameter value
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
exists such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every set of input values
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
, the corresponding measured values
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathcal{Y}
of the output quantity are given by the predictions
f(x_{1},\theta^{*}),\dots,f(x_{n},\theta^{*})
of the model
f(\cdot,\theta^{*})
up to independent normally distributed measurement errors
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}
, that is,

\displaystyle y_{i}=f(x_{i},\theta^{*})+\epsilon_{i}\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,n\})

(2.4)

where
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}
are realizations of independent and normally distributed measurement errors
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
having mean
0
and a known positive definite covariance matrix
\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{y}}\times d_{\mathrm{y}}}_{\mathrm{pd}}
.

Any value
\theta^{*}
as in the above assumption is called (the)
true value of the model parameter
and, accordingly, the corresponding model
f(\cdot,\theta^{*})
is called (the)
true model
. It is important to notice that the assumption does not require such a true parameter value to be known to the modeler. In fact, quite to the contrary, it is always unknown in practice and therefore has to be estimated. All the assumption requires is that such a true (but unknown) parameter value exists and that the additive random measurement errors are independent and normally distributed. See
[
6
]
(Section 1.3.1) for a critical discussion and justification of these standard assumptions in parameter estimation
[
6
,
36
]
and in experimental design
[
15
,
26
,
30
]
.

With the basic assumptions set out, we now formally introduce the notion of (unweighted and weighted) experimental designs along with the pertinent notation that will be continually used throughout the paper.
An
experimental design
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
simply is a tuple

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n}

(2.5)

consisting of a natural number
n
of input points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
which are called the
experimental points of
\tilde{x}
. Also,
n
is called the
size of the experimental design
\tilde{x}
. It should be noticed that the experimental points of a given experimental design
\tilde{x}
are not required to be pairwise distinct. If they are, then
\tilde{x}
is called an
experimental design without replications (repetitions)
, and otherwise
\tilde{x}
is called an
experimental design with replications (repetitions)
. Since performing actual experiments in a lab is typically expensive, it is often important to take into account experiments
\tilde{x}^{-}=(x^{-}_{1},\dots,x^{-}_{n^{-}})
that have already be performed at an earlier stage and combine them with newly performed experiments
\tilde{x}^{+}=(x^{+}_{1},\dots,x^{+}_{n^{+}})
. We will denote the resulting
combined (or refined) experimental design
by

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{-}\,\&\,\tilde{x}^{+}:=(x^{-}_{1},\dots,x^{-}_{n^{-}},x^{+}_{1},\dots,x^{+}_{n^{+}})\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n^{-}+n^{+}}.

(2.6)

In order to distinguish experimental designs in the sense of (
2.5
) from weighted experimental designs defined next, we will sometimes refer to the former as
unweighted experimental designs
.

If
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a given unweighted experimental design, it is often useful to aggregate the repeated experimental points, to indicate the number of replications, and then to normalize by the size
n
of the design. In this manner, one can associate to every unweighted experimental design
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
the corresponding weighted experimental design

\displaystyle\xi_{\tilde{x}}:=\big((w_{x},x))_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}\qquad\text{with}\qquad w_{x}:=r_{x}/n,

(2.7)

where
r_{x}\in\{0,\dots,n\}
for every potential experiment
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
indicates how often it occurs in the design
\tilde{x}
. Sometimes, it is further useful to allow for general real weights
w_{x}\in[0,1]
instead of the specific rational weights in (
2.7
). Specifically, for a finite design space
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
, it is sometimes useful to consider tuples
((w_{x},x))_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}
of general weight-experiment pairs. Such tuples are called
weighted experimental designs
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
and are usually denoted by the letter
\xi
in the literature. Accordingly, the set of all weighted experimental designs on a finite design space
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
is denoted by

\displaystyle\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}}):=\bigg\{\big((w_{x},x))_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}:w_{x}\in[0,1]\text{ for every }x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}\text{ and }\sum_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}w_{x}=1\bigg\}.

(2.8)

In parameter estimation and in experimental design alike, information matrices play a fundamental role. As usual, the
one-point information matrix of
f
at a design point
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
for the reference parameter
\overline{\theta}
is defined as

\displaystyle m_{f}(x,\overline{\theta}):=D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})^{\top}\varsigma^{-1}D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}},

(2.9)

where
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
denotes the set of all positive semidefinite
d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}
matrices. Analogously, the
information matrix of an experimental design
– either an unweighted experimental design
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
or a weighted experimental design
\xi=((w_{x},x))_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})
– is defined as the sum or, respectively, as the weighted sum of the corresponding one-point information matrices. In formulas,

\displaystyle M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\theta}f(x_{i},\overline{\theta})^{\top}\varsigma^{-1}D_{\theta}f(x_{i},\overline{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}},

(2.10)

\displaystyle M_{f}(\xi,\overline{\theta}):=\sum_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}w_{x}D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})^{\top}\varsigma^{-1}D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}.

(2.11)

An extension of the tilde notation (
2.5
) for experimental designs to vector- or matrix-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{X}\times\Theta
is very convenient both in parameter estimation and in experimental design. Specifically, if
a:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{V}
and
b:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\mathcal{V}
are given functions from
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{X}\times\Theta
to some space
\mathcal{V}
of real column vectors or of real matrices and if
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a given experimental design of size
n
, then we will repeatedly use the shorthand notations

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(\tilde{x}):=\begin{pmatrix}a(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
a(x_{n})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{b}(\tilde{x},\theta):=\begin{pmatrix}b(x_{1},\theta)\\
\vdots\\
b(x_{n},\theta)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}

(2.12)

to denote the outcome of applying
a
or
b
simultaneously to all experimental points of
\tilde{x}
. With this notation, Assumption
2
can be concisely reformulated as follows: for every experimental design
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
, the corresponding experimental results
\tilde{y}=(y_{1}^{\top},\dots,y_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
are realizations of the
nd_{\mathrm{y}}
-dimensional random variable

\displaystyle\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}):=\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},\theta^{*})+\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{x}),

(2.13)

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{x})=(\varepsilon_{1}^{\top},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
is an
nd_{\mathrm{y}}
-dimensional normally distributed random variable with expectation value
0
and block-diagonal covariance matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{\varsigma}:=\operatorname{diag}(\varsigma,\dots,\varsigma)=\begin{pmatrix}\varsigma&\dotsb&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dotsb&\varsigma\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{pd}}^{nd_{\mathrm{y}}\times nd_{\mathrm{y}}}.

(2.14)

Similarly, applying the above tilde notation to the function
J:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{y}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
defined by
J(x,\overline{\theta}):=D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})
, the information matrix of an experimental design
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
can be rewritten in the form

\displaystyle M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})=\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta}).

(2.15)

It is clear from this representation that the information matrix
M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
is positive definite if and only if the matrix
\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{nd_{\mathrm{y}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
has a trivial nullspace. In particular, for the information matrix
M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
of
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
to be invertible, it is necessary that

\displaystyle ld_{\mathrm{y}}\geq d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\qquad(l:=\#\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\})

(2.16)

or, in other words, that the number
l
of different experimental points in
\tilde{x}
is at least as large as
d_{\mathrm{\theta}}/d_{\mathrm{y}}
, the number of model parameters divided by the number of outputs. As is well-known, the invertibility of
M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
plays an essential role in the estimability of the model parameters from the experimental design
\tilde{x}
, see (
2.18
) and (
2.20
) below and
[
30
]
(Section 7.2).

2.2
Central facts from parameter estimation

Since the true value
\theta^{*}
of the model parameter (Assumption
2
) is unknown, it needs to be estimated. A standard way of doing so is least-squares estimation, that is, one measures the values
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathcal{Y}
of the output quantity at certain experimental points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
and then chooses the model parameters
\theta
such that the model’s predictions
f(x_{1},\theta),\dots,f(x_{n},\theta)
deviate as little as possible from the observed measurements
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
. Specifically, one chooses the model parameter
\theta\in\Theta
as a solution to the least-squares estimation problem

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(x_{i},\theta)-y_{i}\big)^{\top}\varsigma^{-1}\big(f(x_{i},\theta)-y_{i}\big),

(2.17)

where
\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{pd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
is the covariance matrix of the measurement errors (Assumption
2
). Any solution to (
2.17
) is called a
least-squares estimate for
f
based on the experimental design
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with corresponding experimental results
\tilde{y}=(y_{1}^{\top},\dots,y_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
. Since
\Theta
is assumed to be compact and
f(x_{i},\cdot)
is continuous (Assumption
1
), the existence of a least-squares estimate is guaranteed for arbitrary experimental data
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
. If the model is further assumed to be globally least-squares estimable for a given experimental design
\tilde{x}
, then uniqueness of the least-squares estimate for
\tilde{x}
can also be established (Section 7.2 of
[
30
]
). We do not need such global estimability assumptions, however, and will simply write
\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
for any of the potentially multiple least-squares estimates for
f
based on
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
.

2.2.1
Computing least-squares estimates

In the special case of linear models, one has a closed-form expression for the least-squares estimates. Specifically, for
f
as in (
2.2
), the least-squares estimate is unique and given by the formula

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\big(\tilde{J}(\tilde{x})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x})\big)^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\big(\tilde{y}-\tilde{c}(\tilde{x})\big),

(2.18)

provided that the occurring inverse of
\tilde{J}(\tilde{x})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x})
exists, see (
2.16
). If it does not, then one can replace it in (
2.18
) by the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse to get the unique minimal-norm least-squares estimate of
f
for
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
. As is well-known, the pseudoinverse can be computed by means of the singular value decomposition or by various regularizations. See
[
29
]
(Section 10.2) and
[
18
]
(Chapter 5) for an overview of concrete methods to compute least-squares estimates for linear models.

In the general case of nonlinear models, by contrast, one usually has no closed-form expression for the least-squares estimates anymore and therefore one has to resort to suitable approximations. A standard approximation is based on linearizing
f
around a suitable reference parameter value
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
. Specifically, in the least-squares problem (
2.17
) one replaces the general nonlinear model
f
by the linearized model
f_{\overline{\theta}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
defined by

\displaystyle f_{\overline{\theta}}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\theta):=f(x,\overline{\theta})+J(x,\overline{\theta})(\theta-\overline{\theta})\qquad((x,\theta)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}})

(2.19)

with
J(x,\overline{\theta}):=D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})
. Applying then (
2.18
), one obtains the approximate formula

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\approx\widehat{\theta}_{f_{\overline{\theta}}^{\mathrm{lin}}}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\overline{\theta}+\big(\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})\big)^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\big(\tilde{y}-\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})\big)

(2.20)

provided that the occurring inverse of
\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})=M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
exists, see (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) above. If one iterates this approximation (
2.20
) starting from an initial
\overline{\theta}^{0}
and if in each iteration one chooses an appropriate step size, then one arrives at the Gauß-Newton method. As is well-known, this iterative method converges to a critical point of the sum-of-squares objective function in (
2.17
), provided that the matrices
\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\theta)^{\top}\tilde{\varsigma}^{-1}\tilde{J}(\tilde{x},\theta)
are invertible for all
\theta
in the region of interest (Theorem 10.1 of
[
29
]
). If this uniform invertibility is not satisfied, one can use the Levenberg-Marquardt method instead (Theorem 10.3 of
[
29
]
). We refer to
[
29
]
(Section 10.3) and to
[
36
]
(Chapter 14) for a an extensive overview of methods to compute least-squares estimates for nonlinear models.

2.2.2
Covariance of least-squares estimates

Since the experimental results
\tilde{y}
for any given experimental design
\tilde{x}
are subject to random measurement errors, the same is true for the corresponding least-squares parameter estimates
\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
. In other words, the least-squares estimates for any given experimental design
\tilde{x}
are uncertain. A standard measure to quantify this uncertainty is the covariance matrix

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}))\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}

(2.21)

of the least-squares estimate for
\tilde{x}
and the random observations
\tilde{y}(\tilde{x})
from (
2.13
) (Assumption
2
).
In the case of linear models, by (
2.18
) and (
2.15
) one has the exact identity

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}))=M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{-1}

(2.22)

for arbitrarily chosen reference parameter values
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
. Indeed, the information matrix
M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
actually does not at all depend on
\overline{\theta}
for linear models (
2.2
).
In the case of nonlinear models, by (
2.20
) and (
2.15
) one has at least the approximate identity

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}))\approx M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{-1}

(2.23)

for suitably chosen reference parameter values
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
. As opposed to linear models, the information matrix
M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
of nonlinear models generally strongly depends on the reference parameter value
\overline{\theta}
. In general, the approximation (
2.23
) will be the better, the closer
\overline{\theta}
is to true parameter value
\theta^{*}
. A rigorous asymptotic justification of this is provided by the classical asymptotic normality results for least-squares estimation (Section 12.2.3 of
[
36
]
or Section 3.1.3 of
[
30
]
).

2.3
Central facts from experimental design

Since by (
2.22
) and (
2.23
) the uncertainty of the least-squares estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}))
depends on the underlying experimental design
\tilde{x}
, the prediction uncertainty of the estimated model
f(\cdot,\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x})))
depends on the experimental design as well. And so, in order to get a sufficiently precise estimated model that is sufficiently certain about its predictions, one has to carefully choose the experimental design
\tilde{x}
on which the estimation is based. In experimental design, one tries to come up with experimental designs
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
that on the one hand have a fesible size
n
and on the other hand lead to an estimated model that is sufficiently certain about its predictions. Commonly, this uncertainty is quantified in terms of a suitable scalar function

\displaystyle\Psi(\operatorname{Cov}\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}))^{-1})

(2.24)

of the inverse of the estimate’s covariance. Such functions
\Psi
are called
design criteria
and for the proposed methodology to work, we have to impose only the following assumptions on
\Psi
which are standard in convex design theory
[
24
,
39
,
32
,
2
,
15
,
30
]
. A reader not familiar with that theory can simply always think of the particularly important special case where
\Psi
is the D- or the A-criterion, see (
2.25
) below.

Assumption 3
.

\Psi:\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is convex, antitonic, lower semicontinuos function whose restriction to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{pd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
takes only finite values and is continuously differentiable.

All standard design criteria
\Psi_{p}
except for the E-criterion
\Psi_{\infty}
satisfy the above assumptions and thus the methodology proposed here can be applied with all standard design criteria except for the E-criterion. In particular, the assumptions are satisfied for the D-criterion
\Psi_{0}
and for the A-criterion
\Psi_{1}
, which are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(M):=\ln\det(M^{-1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{1}(M):=\operatorname{tr}(M^{-1})

(2.25)

for
M\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{pd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
and
\Psi_{p}(M):=\infty
for
M\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{pd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
.
See
[
35
]
for precise definitions of the notions used in Assumption
3
and of the standard design criteria
\Psi_{p}
as well as for detailed verifications.

In the extensive literature on experimental design, two main approaches can be distinguished, namely factorial design
[
16
,
8
,
26
]
on the one hand and optimal design
[
23
,
14
,
24
,
39
,
32
,
2
,
15
,
30
]
on the other hand. In essence, these approaches differ in their primary goal and in their generality and scope of applicability.

2.3.1
Factorial experimental design

In factorial experimental design, the primary goal is to find a factorial experimental design
\tilde{x}^{*}
, that is, a geometrically regular design whose experimental points constitute either a full grid in
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
or a regularly formed fraction of a full grid in
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
. Accordingly, one speaks of full factorial designs in the first case and of fractional factorial designs in the second case. In contrast to optimal experimental design, the optimality of
\tilde{x}^{*}
plays only
a secondary role here.
As it turns out, for certain fairly special models
f
and for cuboidal design spaces

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{X}}=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\dotsb\times[a_{d_{\mathrm{x}}},b_{d_{\mathrm{x}}}],

(2.26)

suitably chosen factorial designs are also optimal (w.r.t. suitable design criteria
\Psi
). Specifically, for linear models
f
that are also linear or at least multilinear in the inputs
x
, the standard full factorial designs consisting of the corner points of (
2.26
) are A-, D-, and E-optimal (Section 3.3.4 of
[
4
]
). Similarly, for linear models
f
that are quadratic in the inputs
x
, fractional factorial designs can be found
[
25
]
that are D-optimal. It should be noted, however, that these designs are weighted designs and their support is fairly large even for small input dimensions (Section 2.2.2 of
[
4
]
). It should also be noted that, unlike the specialized designs from
[
25
]
, the standard factorial designs for linear models that are quadratic in the inputs are not D-optimal (
[
28
]
or Section 3.4.7 of
[
4
]
).
As soon as the model
f
becomes more general (higher-order in
x
or nonlinear in
\theta
), factorial designs will no longer be optimal, however. In fact, this can already be seen from third-order polynomial regression in one input dimension (Section 2.2.1 of
[
4
]
or Section 2.6 of
[
15
]
) or in two input dimensions (Section 2.2.2 of
[
4
]
). So, for applications described by higher-order linear models or by nonlinear models, factorial design is not the method of choice and optimal experimental design is recommended instead (Section 11.4.4 of
[
26
]
). Similarly, optimal experimental design is recommended for models on non-cuboidal input spaces
\mathcal{X}
(Section 11.4.4 of
[
26
]
).

2.3.2
Optimal experimental design

In optimal experimental design, the primary goal is to find an optimal experimental design
\tilde{x}^{*}
w.r.t. the chosen design criterion
\Psi
, that is, a solution to the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{x}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\Psi(\operatorname{Cov}\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}))^{-1}),

(2.27)

where the design size
n
can be fixed or allowed to vary in some given range. In contrast to factorial design, the geometric regularity of
\tilde{x}^{*}
plays no role here. And in fact, by the remarks in the previous paragraph, optimal experimental designs are generally not factorial.
As has been pointed out above, except for linear models
f
, one usually has no exact expressions for the estimate’s covariance and hence for the objective function in (
2.27
). So, for nonlinear models
f
, one has to work with appropriate approximations of the covariance. In many cases, the linearization-based approximation (
2.23
) is used, which turns the original optimal experimental design problem (
2.27
) into the substitute problem

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{x}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\Psi(M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta}))

(2.28)

with some appropriate reference parameter value
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
and with a fixed or a variable design size
n
. It should be noticed that (
2.28
) is a nonlinear optimization problem which is discrete, continuous, or discrete-continuous depending on whether the design space
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
is discrete or continuous and on whether
n
is fixed or not. Conventionally, problems like (
2.28
) are called locally optimal experimental design problems
[
9
]
and, accordingly, any approach based on substituting (
2.27
) by (
2.28
) is referred to as locally optimal design. It is important to notice that the linearization-based approximation (
2.23
) is by no means restricted to locally optimal design. In fact, quite to the contrary, the vast majority of approaches to optimal experimental design for nonlinear models relies, in one way or another, on the approximation (
2.23
).
In particular, this is true for average optimal design and minimax optimal design,
which are defined by the problems

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{x}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\int_{\Theta}\Psi(M_{f}(\tilde{x},\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\min_{\tilde{x}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\Psi(M_{f}(\tilde{x},\theta))

(2.29)

with some prior probability distribution
\pi
on
\Theta
. Additionally, this is true for the class of sequential design methods
[
10
,
39
,
15
,
30
]
,
to which the method proposed here belongs. We refer to
[
15
,
30
]
for comprehensive expositions of these linearization-based approaches to optimal experimental design for nonlinear models and to
[
17
]
for a concise overview of locally optimal experimental design specifically tailored to dynamic nonlinear models. We also refer to
[
19
,
43
,
27
]
for approaches that – unlike the first-order approaches mentioned so far – propose higher-order approximations of the covariance or of the related confidence regions of the least-squares estimate.

3
Sequential locally optimal experimental design

In this section, we formally introduce our sequential locally optimal experimental design method. In rough terms, the method repeatedly solves locally optimal experimental design problems of the kind

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{x}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{n}}\Psi(M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta}))

(3.1)

with successively improved reference parameter values
\overline{\theta}
. Specifically, in every iteration,
\overline{\theta}
is chosen to be the current best parameter estimate, based on all experiments performed until then, and the locally optimal experimental design problem with that value of
\overline{\theta}
is solved by means of the recent adaptive discretization algorithm from
[
48
]
. We continue this iteration until the total number of performed experiments reaches some upper bound or the newly proposed experiments do not differ substantially anymore from already performed experiments. As is usual
[
15
,
30
,
48
]
for concrete experimental design algorithms, we choose the design space
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
to be a finite set and work with weighted designs for the sake of computational feasibility.

Assumption 4
.

\underline{\mathcal{X}}
is a finite set and for every given reference parameter
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
all the one-point information matrices
m_{f}(x,\overline{\theta})=D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})^{\top}\varsigma^{-1}D_{\theta}f(x,\overline{\theta})
for
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
can be computed sufficiently fast.

In the case of an explicit model
f
, the computability assumption above poses no restriction. In the case of an implicit model
f
, in turn, it boils down to requiring a sufficiently fast numerical solvability of the equation (
2.3
.a) for
s=s(x,\overline{\theta})
along with a sufficiently fast numerical invertibility of the Jacobi matrices
D_{s}g(s(x,\overline{\theta}),x,\overline{\theta})
for all
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
.

3.1
Computing locally optimal experimental designs

At the heart of our sequential experimental design method is the solution of suitable variants of the locally optimal experimental design problem (
3.1
). In essence, these variants are obtained by means of two modifications.

As a first modification to (
3.1
), we extend the search space from the unweighted experimental designs of a given size
n
to all weighted experimental designs on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
. In this manner, we arrive at the one-stage locally optimal experimental design problem

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})}\Psi\big(M_{f}(\xi,\overline{\theta})\big).

(3.2)

Since the weighted designs
\xi\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})
are uniquely determined by their weights
w_{x}
for
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
, the design problem (
3.2
) is actually a weight optimization problem. Since, moreover, the design criterion
\Psi
is convex (Assumption
3
), the weight optimization problem (
3.2
) is a convex nonlinear optimization problem and therefore has a much simpler structure than the original general nonlinear design problem (
3.1
). It is precisely this substantial structural simplification that makes the introduction of weighted experimental designs standard in optimal experimental design. In the literature, problems like (
3.2
) are referred to as one-stage experimental design problems because their design criterion does not explicitly take into account experiments that have already been performed at an earlier stage. Such preliminary experiments, however, are quite often available in experimental practice.

As a further modification to (
3.1
), we therefore explicitly incorporate such previous experiments into the design criterion. Specifically, we consider the two-stage locally optimal experimental design problem

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})}\Psi\big(\alpha M_{f}(\xi_{\tilde{x}^{-}},\overline{\theta})+(1-\alpha)M_{f}(\xi,\overline{\theta})\big),

(3.3)

where
\tilde{x}^{-}=(x_{1}^{-},\dots,x_{n^{-}}^{-})
are experiments that have already been performed at a previous stage,
\xi_{\tilde{x}^{-}}
is the corresponding weighted design (
2.7
), and
\alpha\in[0,1]
is an importance factor for those previously performed experiments. In the limiting case
\alpha=0
, the information contained in the already performed experiments is completely ignored in the design of the new experiments, and thus (
3.3
) reduces to (
3.2
) then. In the limiting case
\alpha=1
, by contrast, the information contained in the new experiments is completely ignored which makes the optimization (
3.3
) pointless, of course. If one chooses
\alpha=0.5
, then the uncertainty induced by the previous experiments and the uncertainty induced by the prospective experiments are both taken into account in equal measure. Just like (
3.2
), the two-stage experimental design problem (
3.3
) is actually a weight optimization problem and, by the convexity of
\Psi
(Assumption
3
), it is a convex continuous optimization problem.

In order to solve one- or two-stage locally optimal design problems of the kind (
3.2
) or (
3.3
), one can resort to a large variety of tailor-made iterative algorithms from the literature like the algorithms from
[
14
,
47
,
3
,
38
,
7
,
49
]
or
[
20
]
, for instance. See also
[
2
,
15
,
30
]
for a good overview of the pertinent algorithms known until 2013. In this paper, we use the more recent adaptive discretization algorithm from
[
48
]
because of its very good convergence properties, both from a theoretical and from a practical point of view
[
48
,
35
,
37
,
42
]
. Instead of solving (
3.3
) in one shot, this algorithm repeatedly solves discretized versions of (
3.3
) with successively refined discretizations. And since these discretizations are refined adaptively, the discretized problems to be solved in the course of the algorithm remain fairly small, typically. Specifically, we use the following version of the algorithm
[
35
]
.

Algorithm 1
.

Input:
an unweighted design
\tilde{x}^{-}
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
with an associated importance factor
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
a reference parameter value
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
,
the one-point information matrices
m_{f}(x,\overline{\theta})
for all
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
,
a small finite subset
\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
of
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\alpha M_{f}(\xi_{\tilde{x}^{-}},\overline{\theta})+(1-\alpha)M_{f}(\xi,\overline{\theta})
is invertible for some design
\xi
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
,
and an optimality tolerance
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
.
Initialize
k=0
. With these inputs, proceed in the following steps.

1.

Compute a solution
\xi^{k}\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k})
of the discretized version

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k})}\Psi^{(\alpha)}(M_{f}(\xi,\overline{\theta}))

(3.4)

of the two-stage locally optimal design problem (
3.3
), where
\Psi^{(\alpha)}
is the two-stage design criterion defined by
\Psi^{(\alpha)}(M):=\Psi(\alpha M_{f}(\xi_{\tilde{x}^{-}},\overline{\theta})+(1-\alpha)M)
for all
M\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{psd}}^{d_{\mathrm{\theta}}\times d_{\mathrm{\theta}}}
.

2.

Check how far the computed solution
\xi^{k}
of the discretized problem (
3.4
) is from being optimal for the original problem (
3.3
). In order to do so, compute a solution
x^{k}\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
of the strongest optimality violator problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}\psi^{(\alpha)}(M_{f}(\xi^{k},\overline{\theta}),x),

(3.5)

where
\psi^{(\alpha)}
is the sensitivity function of
\Psi^{(\alpha)}
w.r.t.
M_{f}(\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}}),\overline{\theta})
(which is a certain directional derivative of
\Psi^{(\alpha)}
).

–

In case
\psi^{(\alpha)}(M_{f}(\xi^{k},\overline{\theta}),x^{k})<-\varepsilon
, refine the discretization by setting
\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k+1}:=\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k}\cup\{x^{k}\}
and return to Step 1 with
k
replaced by
k+1
.

–

In the opposite case, return
\xi^{k}
and terminate.

As is shown in
[
35
]
, the algorithm above terminates after finitely many iterations with an approximately optimal design
\xi^{+}\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})
in the sense that

\displaystyle\Psi\big(\alpha M_{f}(\xi_{\tilde{x}^{-}},\overline{\theta})+(1-\alpha)M_{f}(\xi^{+},\overline{\theta})\big)\leq\min_{\xi\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})}\Psi\big(\alpha M_{f}(\xi_{\tilde{x}^{-}},\overline{\theta})+(1-\alpha)M_{f}(\xi,\overline{\theta})\big)+\varepsilon.

(3.6)

In words, this relation says that the design criterion value for
\xi^{+}
is guaranteed to exceed the optimal design criterion value by at most
\varepsilon
. It is important to notice that this approximation error bound
\varepsilon
can be freely set by the user. In the following, we will refer to a design
\xi^{+}\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})
that satisfies (
3.6
) as a
locally
\varepsilon
-optimal weighted design for
f
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
around
\overline{\theta}
with
\tilde{x}^{-}
as the previous-stage design of importance
\alpha
. Algorithm
1
is an adaptive discretization algorithm because the discretization
\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k+1}
is obtained from the previous discretization
\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k}
by an adaptive refinement, namely by adding a strongest violator
x^{k}
of the optimality condition for the solution
\xi^{k}
of the previous discretized design problem (
3.4
). It is because of this adaptive refinement that Algorithm
1
typically does not need many iterations until termination. In particular, the discretized design problems (
3.4
) to be solved in the course of the algorithm are typically fairly small and hence easy to solve. After all, these discretized design problems (
3.4
) are convex weight optimization problems with the number of weight variables being equal to the size of the discretization
\underline{\mathcal{X}}^{k}
. Also, by the finiteness of
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
(Assumption
4
), the strongest violator problems (
3.5
) can simply be solved by means of enumeration, using the explicit formulas for the sensitivity function
\psi^{(\alpha)}
of two-stage design criteria
\Psi^{(\alpha)}
provided in
[
35
]
, for instance.

As is indicated already by their name, the locally
\varepsilon
-optimal weighted designs computed by Algorithm
1
are weighted experimental designs
\xi^{+}=((w_{x}^{+},x))_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}\in\Xi(\underline{\mathcal{X}})
. And in most cases, the weights
w_{x}^{+}
will not be of the form
w_{x}^{+}=r_{x}^{+}/n^{+}
with a practically realizable number
n^{+}
of new experiments and replication numbers
r_{x}^{+}\in\{0,\dots,n^{+}\}
indicating how often the experimenter should measure at the points
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
. In other words, the weighted experimental designs
\xi^{+}
computed by Algorithm
1
usually do not directly correspond to an unweighted experimental design
\tilde{x}^{+}=(x_{1}^{+},\dots,x_{n^{+}}^{+})
of a practically realizable size
n^{+}
. Instead, they first have to be converted to such a practically realizable unweighted design. Standard methods of doing so are based on suitable rounding procedures for the (pseudo)quotas
n^{+}w_{x}^{+}

[
15
]
(Section 3.3),
(n^{+}-l^{+})w_{x}^{+}

[
14
]
(Section 3.1), or
(n^{+}-l^{+}/2)w_{x}^{+}

[
33
]
(Section 6), where
l^{+}
is the number of experimental points in
\xi^{+}
. In the case where the affordable number
n^{+}
of new experiments is large, these rounding procedures produce unweighted designs
\tilde{x}^{+}
that are efficient in the sense that the design criterion value of
\xi_{\tilde{x}^{+}}
exceeds that of
\xi^{+}
only by a little bit. Indeed, for large experimental budgets
n^{+}
, the efficiency bounds from
[
14
,
15
,
33
]
become tight. In the practically relevant case of small experimental budgets
n^{+}
, however, the aforementioned efficiency bounds do not say much about the actually achievable efficiency. Additionally, the mentioned efficiency bounds are valid only for homogeneous design criteria
[
15
]
, but two-stage design criteria – as the criteria
\Psi^{(\alpha)}
used here – are not homogeneous. We therefore propose a different conversion procedure in this paper, namely one that is not based on rounding but instead directly aims at producing efficient unweighted designs
\tilde{x}^{+}
from
\xi^{+}
. Specifically, among the experimental points of
\xi^{+}
with non-negligible weights, we simply select
n^{+}
points such that the design criterion value becomes as small as possible.

Summarizing, we arrive at the following two-stage locally optimal experimental design algorithm. Starting from an unweighted design
\tilde{x}^{-}
containing experiments from a previous stage, it computes an unweighted experimental design
\tilde{x}^{+}
containing the experiments for the next stage.

Algorithm 2
.

Input: an unweighted experimental design
\tilde{x}^{-}
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}

with an associated importance
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
the one-point information matrices
m_{f}(x,\overline{\theta})
for all
x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}
,
a reference parameter value
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
, an optimality tolerance
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
a minimal total weight
\underline{w}^{+}\in(0,1]
,
and a maximal number
\overline{n}^{+}
of new experiments.
With these inputs, proceed in the following steps.

1.

Compute a locally
\varepsilon
-optimal weighted design

\displaystyle\xi^{+}=((w_{x}^{+},x))_{x\in\underline{\mathcal{X}}}

(3.7)

for
f
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
around
\overline{\theta}
with
\tilde{x}^{-}
as the previous-stage design of importance
\alpha
. In order to do so, use Algorithm
1
.

2.

Convert the computed weighted design
\xi^{+}
to an unweighted design
\tilde{x}^{+}
consisting of at most
\overline{n}^{+}
experiments. In order to do so, successively sieve out the least important experiment from
\xi^{+}
until the total weight of the remaining experiments only just remains above the prescribed threshold
\underline{w}^{+}
. Write

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{x}}^{+}=(\underline{x}_{1}^{+},\dots,\underline{x}_{\underline{n}^{+}}^{+})

(3.8)

for the remaining unweighted design and then compare the number
\underline{n}^{+}
of experiments in
\tilde{\underline{x}}^{+}
with the prescribed upper bound
\overline{n}^{+}
.

–

In case
\underline{n}^{+}>\overline{n}^{+}
, choose an appropriate subdesign
\tilde{x}^{+}
of the intermediate design
\tilde{\underline{x}}^{+}
, consisting of exactly
\overline{n}^{+}
experiments. Specifically, among all subdesigns
\tilde{z}^{+}
of
\tilde{\underline{x}}^{+}
consisting of exactly
\overline{n}^{+}
experiments, choose one with minimal design criterion value

\displaystyle\Psi\big(\alpha M_{f}(\xi^{-},\overline{\theta})+(1-\alpha)M_{f}(\xi_{\tilde{z}^{+}},\overline{\theta})\big)

(3.9)

–

In the opposite case, simply choose
\tilde{x}^{+}
to be equal to the intermediate design
\tilde{\underline{x}}^{+}
.

Clearly, the output
\tilde{x}^{+}
of the above algorithm is an unweighted experimental design without replications consisting of at most
\overline{n}^{+}
experiments in
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
. In the following, we will refer to it as a
locally
\varepsilon
-optimal design for
f
on
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
around
\overline{\theta}
consisting of at most
\overline{n}^{+}
experiments with total weight at least
\underline{w}^{+}
and with
\tilde{x}^{-}
as the previous-stage design of importance
\alpha
.

3.2
Iterating experiments, parameter estimation, and locally optimal experimental design

As has been pointed out above, the quality of the approximation (
2.23
) generally strongly depends on the specific choice of the reference parameter
\overline{\theta}
about which the model is linearized. So, as the locally optimal experimental design problems (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are built precisely on this approximation, it is essential to come up with good choices for the reference parameter
\overline{\theta}
. A very natural and established strategy here is sequential design
[
10
,
39
,
15
,
30
]
where
\overline{\theta}
is simply always chosen to be the current best parameter estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{-},\tilde{y}^{-})
, based on all experimental data
\tilde{x}^{-}
and
\tilde{y}^{-}
that are available at the current stage. With this estimate
\overline{\theta}
, we then apply our locally optimal experimental design algorithm (Algorithm
2
) to get the most informative batch
\tilde{x}^{+}
of new experiments. We repeat this three-step procedure of performing experiments, estimating the parameters, and computing locally optimal experimental designs until the total number of experiments exceeds a user-specified upper bound
\overline{n}
or each of the newly proposed experiments differs from an already performed experiment by less than a user-specified progress tolerance
\delta
. We measure this distance between two experimental points
x=(x_{j})_{j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathrm{x}}\}}
and
x^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathrm{x}}\}}
in terms of a suitably scaled maximum norm defined by

\displaystyle\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}:=\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathrm{x}}\}}|x_{j}-x^{\prime}_{j}|/\lambda_{j},

(3.10)

where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d_{\mathrm{x}}}
are the side lengths of the smallest cuboid (axis-aligned box) containing the design space
\underline{\mathcal{X}}
. (In other words,
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d_{\mathrm{x}}}
describe the extension of the design space in the individual input directions.)
In detail, our sequential design procedure is described in Algorithm
3
. A schematic illustration of the procedure is given in Figure
1
.

3.3
Assessing the quality of experimental designs

In this section, we introduce the criteria that will be used to assess the quality of a given experimental design. We use three different criteria, and all of them express the quality of an experimental design
\tilde{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
in terms of the prediction quality of the corresponding estimated model
f(\cdot,\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x})))
, that is, the model that has been trained based on
\tilde{x}
.

As a first quality measure of a given experimental design
\tilde{x}
(with associated experimental results
\tilde{y}
), we take the sum of squared prediction errors that the trained model
f(\cdot,\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}))
makes on all available experimental data
(\tilde{x}^{\mathrm{tot}},\tilde{y}^{\mathrm{tot}})
. Specifically, we take the root mean squared errors

\displaystyle\rho_{j}(\tilde{x}):=\rho_{j}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}):=\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{\mathrm{tot}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\mathrm{tot}}}\big(f_{j}(x^{\mathrm{tot}}_{i},\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}))-y^{\mathrm{tot}}_{ij}\big)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\qquad(j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathrm{y}}\})

(3.14)

of the trained model’s components on the totality of available data.
As our second and third quality measures for a given experimental design
\tilde{x}
, we take two different approximations of the prediction variance

\displaystyle V_{j}(x,\tilde{x}):=\operatorname{Var}f_{j}(x,\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x})))

(3.15)

of the estimated model’s components based on
\tilde{x}
, namely a linearization-based approximation
V_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}
on the one hand and a sampling-based approximation
V_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}
on the other hand.
Specifically, the linearization-based prediction variance
V_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x})
is defined as the variance of the estimated linearized model at
x
, that is,

\displaystyle V_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x}):=\operatorname{Var}f^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\overline{\theta},j}(x,\widehat{\theta}_{f^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\overline{\theta}}}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}(\tilde{x})))\qquad(j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathrm{y}}\}),

(3.16)

where
x\in\mathcal{X}
is an arbitrary input point,
\tilde{y}(\tilde{x}):=\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},\theta^{*})+\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{x})
is the random observation model (
2.13
), and the model is linearized around
\overline{\theta}:=\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{\mathrm{tot}},\tilde{y}^{\mathrm{tot}})
, which is the best parameter estimate based on the totality of available data. In view of (
2.19
) and (
2.22
),

\displaystyle V_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x})=\nabla_{\theta}f_{j}(x,\overline{\theta})^{\top}M_{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}f_{j}(x,\overline{\theta}).

(3.17)

Additionally, the sampling-based prediction variance
V_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x})
is defined as the sample variance of the estimated nonlinear model at
x
, that is,

\displaystyle V_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x}):=\frac{1}{n^{\mathrm{sam}}}\sum_{s=1}^{n^{\mathrm{sam}}}\big(f_{j}(x,\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}^{(s)}))-\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x})\big)^{2}\qquad(j\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathrm{y}}\}),

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x}):=\frac{1}{n^{\mathrm{sam}}}\sum_{s=1}^{n^{\mathrm{sam}}}f_{j}(x,\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}^{(s)})),

(3.19)

where
x\in\mathcal{X}
is an arbitrary input point,
\tilde{y}^{(s)}
are
n^{\mathrm{sam}}
samples independently drawn from the random observation model
\tilde{y}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta}):=\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})+\tilde{\varepsilon}(\tilde{x})
with
\overline{\theta}:=\widehat{\theta}_{f}(\tilde{x}^{\mathrm{tot}},\tilde{y}^{\mathrm{tot}})
. Since this
\overline{\theta}
is the best parameter estimate based on the totality of available data, the employed observation model
\tilde{y}(\tilde{x},\overline{\theta})
is the best available substitute for the true observation model (
2.13
).
Similarly to (
3.14
), we will use the square roots

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x}):=\big(V_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x})\big)^{1/2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x}):=\big(V_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x})\big)^{1/2}

(3.20)

of the prediction variances (
3.16
) and (
3.18
) at given input points
x\in\mathcal{X}
. In fact, in in our comparisons of different designs, we will actually use the
x
-independent worst-case uncertainties

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\tilde{x}):=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(\tilde{x}):=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x}).

(3.21)

We will refer to (
3.21
) as the worst-case linearization- and the worst-case sampling-based prediction uncertainties, respectively.

It is clear that for substantially nonlinear models
f
, the sampling-based prediction uncertainty
\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{sam}}(x,\tilde{x})
is a better approximation to the exact prediction uncertainty than the linearization-based prediction uncertainty
\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{lin}}(x,\tilde{x})
, just because the former does not rely on any linearization. It is also clear by the very definitions (
3.14
) and (
3.20
) of our three quality measures for experimental designs that they all depend not only on the experimental design
\tilde{x}
alone but also on the total set of gathered experimental data
(\tilde{x}^{\mathrm{tot}},\tilde{y}^{\mathrm{tot}})
. It should be noticed that this dependence on
(\tilde{x}^{\mathrm{tot}},\tilde{y}^{\mathrm{tot}})
is strongest for the root mean squared errors (
3.14
), while it is only indirect for the linearization-based prediction uncertainty (
3.20
.a) and even more so for the sampling-based prediction unctertainty (
3.20
.b). In order to keep the notation simple, we suppress this additional dependence on
(\tilde{x}^{\mathrm{tot}},\tilde{y}^{\mathrm{tot}})
in the following.

4
Application to vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling

In this section, we apply the proposed general sequential design method to the estimation of non-random two-liquid parameters. Specifically, we consider the binary system consisting of propanol and propyl acetate. It is known that this system exhibits narrow azeotropic behavior and therefore the estimation of its non-random two-liquid parameters is already a challenging task. With our sequential locally optimal design method, we end up with a locally optimal design consisting of
15
experiments, four of which are repeated experiments. As we will see, traditional factorial design, by contrast, already requires
27
experiments to achieve roughly the same prediction quality.

4.1
Considered system

As explained above, the system considered here is the narrow azeotropic binary mixture consisting of propanol and propyl acetate. In the following, propanol will always be considered as component
1
and propyl acetate as component
2
.
In order to estimate the binary interaction parameters of this two-component mixture, we observe it at different liquid compositions
(l,1-l)
and at different pressures
P
and, for all these conditions, we measure the corresponding vapor composition
(v,1-v)
and the temperature
T
at vapor-liquid equilibrium. In our general terminology, this means that the input quantities
x
are the liquid mole fraction
l
of propanol along with the system pressure
P
, while the output quantities
y
are the vapor mole fraction
v
of propanol along with the system temperature
T
at vapor-liquid equilibrium. In short,

\displaystyle x:=(l,P)\in\mathcal{X}:=[0,1]\times[\underline{P},\overline{P}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad y:=(v,T)\in\mathcal{Y}:=[0,1]\times[\underline{T},\overline{T}],

(4.1)

where
\underline{P},\overline{P}
and
\underline{T},\overline{T}
determine the pressure and temperature ranges of interest. In our case, pressure and temperature are consistently measured, respectively, in units of Pascal (Pa) and Kelvin (K), and mole fractions are measured in the unit mol/mol, of course. Also,
\underline{P}:=1\cdot 10^{5}
and
\overline{P}:=3\cdot 10^{5}
in our case.

In order to model the functional relationship between the input and output quantities (
4.1
), we assume Raoult’s law in its extended version without Poynting correction. In particular, we assume non-ideal mixture behavior in the liquid phase but an ideal vapor phase (Section 13.3 of
[
40
]
). Spelled out, this means that for every given input condition
x=(l,P)\in\mathcal{X}
, the equilibrium temperature
T
is implicitly defined by the equation

\displaystyle\frac{P_{1}(T)}{P}\cdot\gamma_{1}(l,T)l+\frac{P_{2}(T)}{P}\cdot\gamma_{2}(l,T)(1-l)=1,

(4.2)

and the equilibrium vapor mole fraction
v
of propanol, in turn, is defined by the equation

\displaystyle v=\frac{P_{1}(T)}{P}\cdot\gamma_{1}(l,T)l.

(4.3)

In the above equations,
P_{i}(T)
denotes the saturation vapor pressure of pure component
i
at temperature
T
and
\gamma_{i}(l,T)
denotes the activity coefficient of component
i
in the mixture with liquid composition
(l,1-l)
at temperature
T
.
Concerning the saturation vapor pressure, we assume it to be given by the Antoine model (Section 6.5 of
[
40
]
), that is,

\displaystyle P_{i}(T):=10^{5}\cdot 10^{A_{i}-B_{i}/(T+C_{i})}\qquad(i\in\{1,2\}),

(4.4)

and for the Antoine parameters
A_{i},B_{i},C_{i}
we take the estimated values from Table
1
.
Concerning the activity coefficients, we assume them to be given by the non-random two-liquid model of Renon and Prausnitz (Section 13.4 of
[
40
]
or Section 6.15 of
[
31
]
),
that is,

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(l,T):=\exp\bigg(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{2}l_{k}\tau_{ki}(T)G_{ki}(T)}{\sum_{k=1}^{2}l_{k}G_{ki}(T)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{l_{j}G_{ij}(T)}{\sum_{k=1}^{2}l_{k}G_{kj}(T)}\bigg(\tau_{ij}(T)-\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{2}l_{k}\tau_{kj}(T)G_{kj}(T)}{\sum_{k=1}^{2}l_{k}G_{kj}(T)}\bigg)\bigg),

(4.5)

where
(l_{1},l_{2}):=(l,1-l)
and where
G_{ij}
and
\tau_{ij}
are defined by

\displaystyle G_{ij}(T):=\exp(-\alpha_{ij}(T)\tau_{ij}(T))\qquad(i,j\in\{1,2\}),

(4.6)

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(T):=a_{ij}+\frac{b_{ij}}{T}\qquad(i\neq j)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau_{ii}(T):=0\qquad(i\in\{1,2\})

(4.7)

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}(T):=c_{ij}+d_{ij}T\qquad(i\neq j)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{ii}(T):=0\qquad(i\in\{1,2\}).

(4.8)

As in the original model proposed in
[
34
]
, we further assume the non-randomness terms
\alpha_{ij}
to be symmetric and temperature-independent, that is,

\displaystyle c_{ij}=c_{ji}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d_{ij}=0\qquad(i,j\in\{1,2\}).

(4.9)

Since the Antoine parameters have already been estimated by a prior parameter estimation based on pure-component measurements, the parameters that remain to be estimated for our two-component mixture are the five non-random two-liquid parameters

\displaystyle\theta:=(a_{12},a_{21},b_{12},b_{21},c_{12})\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{5}.

(4.10)

In contrast to
[
34
]
and
[
12
]
, we explicitly incorporate the non-randomness parameter
c_{12}=c_{21}
into the least-squares parameter estimation. In
[
34
]
, instead, this parameter is recommended to be manually selected as one of the four values from the set
\{0.2,0.3,0.4,0.47\}
, based on the characteristics of the mixture’s vapor-liquid equilibrium diagram.

All vapor-liquid equilibrium experiments for the considered two-component system were carried out in an ILUDEST

(4.12)

4.3
Application-specific settings for parameter estimation and optimal design

As a general modeling and optimization framework supporting automatic differentiation, we choose the software package Casadi
[
1
]
. Additionally, in the present application example, we use the following specific settings for the occurring parameter estimation and optimal design problems.
In keeping with the accuracies (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) of the employed measurement devices, we take the covariance matrix of the measurement errors to be

\displaystyle\varsigma:=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{v}^{2}&0\\
0&\sigma_{T}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2.25\cdot 10^{-6}&0\\
0&9\cdot 10^{-4}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{pd}}^{d_{\mathrm{y}}\times d_{\mathrm{y}}}.

(4.15)

In particular, by the diagonality of this matrix, we implicitly assume the measurement errors for vapor composition and the measurement errors for temperature to be independent of each other. It should be recalled from (
2.10
) and (
2.17
) that the covariance of the measurement errors enters all least-squares estimation problems and – via the information matrices – also all locally optimal experimental design problems. And so the above covariance matrix (
4.15
) features in all optimization problems of our application example.

4.3.1
Settings for parameter estimation

Since the covariance matrix (
4.15
) of the measurement errors is diagonal, all least-squares estimation problems in our case study take the special form

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(f_{j}(x_{i},\theta)-y_{ij})^{2}/\sigma_{j}^{2}

(4.16)

of weighted least-squares problems. In order to solve these problems, we use the interior-point optimizer Ipopt
[

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we have proposed a general method of sequential locally optimal experimental design for nonlinear models. It can be applied to estimate the parameters of nonlinear models as they abound in vapor-liquid equilibrium modeling, for instance. And these nonlinear models can be defined either by explicit or by implicit algebraic equations.
In every iteration of our method, a whole batch of new experiments is computed in a two-stage locally optimal manner. In other words, a two-stage locally optimal experimental design problem is solved in every iteration of our method, based on the previously collected experimental data and on the corresponding least-squares estimate. As the underlying design criterion, any of the standard differentiable design criteria can be chosen, in particular, the A- and the D-criterion. Also, the number of new experiments computed in each iteration can be freely specified by the user in such a way that it fits the concrete experimental circumstances best.

We have demonstrated the benefits of the general method by applying it to the estimation of non-random two-liquid parameters of a narrow azeotropic system. In particular, we have found that the proposed sequential locally optimal experimental design method requires substantially fewer experiments than traditional factorial experimental design to achieve the same model precision and prediction quality. Specifically, compared to factorial design, our sequential design method required only about half the experiments and thus helps to considerably reduce the experimental effort for the non-random two-liquid parameter estimation.

In future work, we plan to demonstrate the benefits of the proposed sequential design methodolgy also on other application examples, especially, on systems with liquid-liquid equilibria and on systems with chemical reactions.
We also plan to extend the proposed sequential design methodolgy to experimental design problems with constraints on the designs, like cost or location constraints. In practice, such constraints are highly relevant, but are rarely taken into account systematically. And finally, we will investigate how experimental design can further be improved with the help of other design criteria than the linearization-based design criteria used in this work. In particular, it will be interesting to see how and to what extent experimental design for nonlinear models improves when the designs are computed using suitable sampling-based approximations to the parameter uncertainties or to the prediction uncertainties, as opposed to the successive linearization-based approximations used in this work.
