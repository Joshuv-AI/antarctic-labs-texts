1
Introduction

Graph spanners are sparse subgraphs that approximately preserve pairwise shortest-path distances. Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected unweighted graph on
n
vertices and let
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a function. We say that a subgraph
H\subseteq G
is a spanner with
stretch function

f
if for every pair
s,t\in V
,
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H}(s,t)\leq f(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t))
. The research on spanners focuses on the optimal trade-offs between the stretch function
f
and the sparsity (the number of edges) of the spanner
H
.

One typical case is that we allow
f(d)
to be significantly greater than
d
, and such spanners are known as
multiplicative
spanners. It was shown that for every integer
k\geq 1
, there always exists a subgraph
H
with
O(n^{1+1/k})
edges and stretch function
f(d)=(2k-1)d

[
ADD+93
]
. Furthermore, this sparsity bound is tight under the Girth Conjecture of Erdös
[
Erd63
]
.

Another typical case is that we restrict
f
to be very close to
d
. In particular,
f(d)=d+O(1)
. There has been a line of previous work studying the sparsity of spanners with such stretch functions. When
f(d)=d+2
, it was shown that graph
G
always has a spanner with
O(n^{3/2})
edges
[
ACIM99
]
; when
f(d)=d+4
, a construction of spanner with
\tilde{O}(n^{7/5})
edges was proposed in
[
Che13
]
; when
f(d)=d+6
, spanners with
O(n^{4/3})
edges were known to exist by
[
BKMP10
]
. For the lower bound side, in a recent breakthrough
[
AB17
]
, it was proved that, for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there are graphs such that any spanner with
O(n^{4/3-\varepsilon})
edges has stretch
n^{\Omega(1)}
. Hence, we already have an almost complete understanding of the spanner sparsity when
f(d)=d+O(1)
.

Besides the two typical cases mentioned above, much less is known when
f
lies in intermediate regimes. Two notable regimes studied in previous works are the
sublinear additive
regime where
f(d)=d+o(d)
, and the
additive
regime where
f(d)=d+o(n)
.

As for sublinear additive spanners, Thorup and Zwick
[
TZ06
,
HP19
]
were the first to design a nontrivial construction of sublinear additive spanners when
f(d)=d+O(d^{1-1/k})
where
k\geq 2
is a constant integer, and the number of edges in the spanner is bounded by
O(n^{1+1/k})
. This sparsity bound was later improved to
O\bigg(n^{1+\frac{(3/4)^{k-2}}{7-2\cdot(3/4)^{k-2}}}\bigg)
in
[
Pet09
]
. The sparsity bound for the special case where
k=2
was subsequently improved to
\tilde{O}(n^{20/17})
by
[
Che13
]
. These algorithms also work for a non-constant
k
, but here we only focus on the case where
k
is a constant, as we are mainly interested in the stretch/size dependency on
d
and
n
. On the lower bound side, Abboud, Bodwin, and Pettie
[
ABP18
]
proved that any distance-reporting data structure (including spanners, emulators) achieving stretch
f(d)=d+O(d^{1-1/k})
must have size at least
\Omega\bigg(n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}-o(1)}\bigg)
for each constant
k\geq 2
, which is also the best known lower bound for spanners. Thus, there still exists a large gap between sparsity upper and lower bounds for sublinear additive spanners.

For the additive regime where
f(d)=d+o(n)
, the tail term
f(d)-d
is usually called the
additive stretch
. A natural question is to study the best additive stretch that can be achieved by spanners with
\tilde{O}(n)
edges. The first nontrivial construction was given by
[
Pet09
]
with an additive stretch of
O(n^{9/16})
, which was improved subsequently by
[
BW15
,
BW21
]
to
O(n^{3/7+\varepsilon})
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
. On the negative side, the first stretch lower bound of
\Omega(n^{1/22})
was proved in
[
AB17
]
, and later on raised to
\Omega(n^{1/7})
by a sequence of works
[
HP21
,
LWWX22
,
BH22
]
.

1.1
Our results

Sublinear additive spanners.

Our primary result is the following almost optimal bound on sublinear additive spanners.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any undirected unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices, given any constant
\delta>0
and any constant integer
k\geq 2
, there exists a sufficiently large constant
C=C(k,\delta)
, such that
every undirected unweighted graph
G
on
n
vertices admits a spanner
H\subseteq G
with stretch function
f(d)=d+C\cdot d^{1-1/k}
and
O\bigg(n^{1+\frac{1+\delta}{2^{k+1}-1}}\bigg)
edges.

This sparsity upper bound almost matches the previous lower bound of
\Omega\left(n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}-o(1)}\right)
proved in
[
ABP18
]
. Formally, the authors of
[
ABP18
]
showed that there exists a constant
c=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\right)
, such that in some hard examples of input graph
G
, any spanner with stretch
f(d)=d+c\cdot d^{1-1/(k+1)}
must have
\Omega\bigg(n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}-o(1)}\bigg)
edges. When the distance value
d
is larger than
\left(C/c\right)^{k(k+1)}
which is a constant only dependent on
k
, the stretch function
f(d)=d+C\cdot d^{1-1/k}
of our spanner construction is always better than the stretch
d+c\cdot d^{1-1/(k+1)}
in the lower bound construction of
[
ABP18
]
. Since our sparsity upper bound almost matches the lower bound of
\Omega\left(n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}-o(1)}\right)
, our construction is almost optimal.

The same lower bound in
[
ABP18
]
also extends to the more relaxed notion of emulators which are graphs defined on the vertex set
V
but not required to be a subgraph of
G
. For the sublinear additive stretch function
f(d)=d+O(d^{1-1/k})
, there are known emulator constructions of size
O\left(kn^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}}\right)
by
[
TZ06
,
HP19
]
which matches the lower bound in
[
ABP18
]
and ties our new bound for spanners. In fact, the emulator lower bound in
[
ABP18
]
holds for any form of graph metric compression schemes, in the sense that any data structure that answers approximate distance queries (regardless of query time) with stretch
f(d)=d+c\cdot d^{1-1/(k+1)}
for some constant
c<2k^{1-1/(k+1)}
must occupy at least
n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}-o(1)}
bits of space. Therefore, since our spanner construction matches the lower bound against any graph metric compression scheme, as our take-home message, it means that although spanners are the most restrictive form of compression compared to emulators and distance oracles, they are already the most powerful ones.

Our construction also directly translates to
(1+\varepsilon,\beta)
-mixed spanners with stretch function
f(d)=(1+\varepsilon)d+\beta
when
\beta=\frac{(k-1)^{k-1}C^{k}}{k^{k}\varepsilon^{k-1}}
by AM-GM inequality. Previous works on mixed spanners include
[
EP04
]
where the authors showed that for every integer
\kappa
and
\varepsilon>0
, every
n
-vertex graph admits a
(1+\varepsilon,O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\kappa)^{\log\kappa})
-spanner of size
O(n^{1+1/\kappa}\cdot O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\kappa)^{\log\kappa})
, and
[
TZ06
]
where the authors constructed
\left(1+\varepsilon,O(k/\varepsilon)^{k-1}\right)
-spanner of size
O\left(k\cdot n^{1+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}}\right)
for any integer
k
, and
[
Pet09
]
where the author showed the construction of
(1+\varepsilon,O(\log\log n/\varepsilon)^{\log\log n})
-spanner of size
O(n\log\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\log n))
. Among these previous results, the result by
[
TZ06
]
and ours provide the same form of stretch
\left(1+\varepsilon,O(1/\varepsilon^{k-1})\right)
and sparsity
n^{1+\frac{1+\delta}{2^{k+1}-1}}
when
k
is a constant integer, which is proved to be almost optimal by
[
ABP18
]
.

Additive spanners.

Our second result is a slightly improved bound on near-linear size additive spanners upon the bound
O(n^{3/7+\varepsilon})
of
[
BW21
]
. In addition, we show that such a spanner can be computed in subquadratic time, while previous constructions in
[
BW21
,
BW15
]
need the computation of all-pairs shortest paths in
G
which takes time
O(\min\{mn,n^{2.373}\})
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any undirected unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices and
m
egdges, there exists a spanner with
O(n^{0.403})
additive stretch and
\tilde{O}(n)
edges. Moreover, such a spanner can be computed in sub-quadratic time
\tilde{O}\big(m+n^{13/7}\big)
.

Remark 1.3
.

In the above theorem, if we could tolerate an
O(mn)
construction time of the additive spanner, then the size can be made purely linear
O(n)
with the same additive stretch
O(n^{0.403})
.

1.2
Technical overview

The basic tool of our algorithms is a clustering algorithm in
[
BW21
]
. Let
G=(V,E)
be the input undirected unweighted graph. Roughly speaking, for any radius parameter
R
, we can decompose the graph into a set
{\mathcal{B}}
of balls that may share vertices, with the following properties.

•

(Radius) The radius of each ball in
{\mathcal{B}}
is roughly
R
.

•

(Coverage) The union of all balls in
{\mathcal{B}}
covers the whole graph
G
.

•

(Disjointness) The total size of the balls in
{\mathcal{B}}
is linear in
n
.

Next, we will describe how to utilize the above clustering to construct new sublinear additive spanners and additive spanners, respectively.

1.2.2
Pairwise sublinear additive spanners

The only missing component towards a sublinear additive spanner with stretch
f(d)=d+O(d^{1-1/k})
for general
k\geq 2
turns out to be a pairwise spanner with stretch
d+O(d^{1-1/(k-1)})
. As the previous work on pairwise spanners
[
CGK13
,
Kav17
]
only considered stretch function
f(d)=d+\tilde{O}(1)
, we need to generalize their result for general
k
. Assume we are given an undirected unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices and a set
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq V\times V
of pairs, and the goal is to find a spanner
H\subseteq G
with at most
\tilde{O}(n|{\mathcal{P}}|^{1/2^{k+1}})
edges, such that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H}(s,t)\leq f(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t))
for all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{P}}
. For the purpose of this overview, we assume for simplicity that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)=D
for all
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{P}}
.

The construction of
H
is inductive on
k\geq 1
. Assume we know how to construct such spanners for
k-1
. First, apply the clustering algorithm from
[
BW21
]
with radius
R=D^{1-1/k}
to graph
G
, and obtain a set
{\mathcal{B}}
of balls.

Uniform size.

We start by considering a special case, where all balls in
{\mathcal{B}}
have the same size
|\mathsf{B}(c,r)|=L
. By the disjointness property, the number of balls in
\mathcal{B}
is approximately
n/L
.
For each pair
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{P}}
, we compute a sequence of
D^{1/k}
balls and partition the shortest path connecting
s,t
, in a similar way as illustrated in
Figure

1
.
We wish to inductively build inside each ball
G[\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})]
a pairwise spanner with a better stretch
d+O(d^{1-1/(k-1)})
; if this is done, then the cumulative error along the shortest path connecting
s
and
t
is bounded by
D^{1/k}\cdot R^{1-1/(k-1)}=D^{1-1/k}
.

In order to apply the inductive hypothesis, we will construct, for each ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in{\mathcal{B}}
, a set
{\mathcal{P}}_{c}
of demand pairs. But if we add, for all
i
, the pair
(w_{i},w_{i+1})
to
{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{i}}
, then the sets
|{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{i}}|
may become too large (as large as
|{\mathcal{P}}|
) to construct a spanner for. To circumvent this, we use the approach in
[
Kav17
]
. Observe that, if we add each pair
(w_{i},w_{i+1})
to
{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{i}}
, then not only the distance between
s,t
is approximately preserved (in this case we call the pair
(s,t)

settled
), but the distances between all pairs of ball centers
c_{i},c_{j}
are also approximately preserved. Now there are two cases.
Let
\beta
be a parameter to be determined later.

•

Case 1. There are at least
\beta\cdot(l+1)
center pairs that were unsettled in
H
; recall that
l+1
refers to the number of clusters as in
Figure

1
.

Then after adding each
(w_{i},w_{i+1})
as a demand pair to
{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{i}}
, the total number of such center pairs would drop by
\beta\cdot(l+1)
. In other words, each new demand pair
(w_{i},w_{i+1})
contributes
\beta
to the decrease of the number of unsettled center pairs on average.

•

Case 2. There are fewer than
\beta\cdot(l+1)
center pairs that were unsettled in
H
.

In this case, we show that there is always a “bridge” that helps settle the pair
(s,t)
. Specifically, we can prove there are indices
x\in[1,\beta],z\in[\beta+1,l+1-\beta],y\in[l-\beta+2,l+1]
such that center pairs
(c_{x},c_{z})
and
(c_{z},c_{y})
were already settled. In this case, adding demand pairs
(w_{i},w_{i+1})
to
{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{i}}
only for
i\in[1,\beta]\cup[l-\beta+2,l+1]
would be enough. See Figure
3
for an illustration.

for some
1\leq i<j\leq l
. Then, by the algorithm description we have
t_{j}\in\mathsf{B}(c_{j},2r_{j})=\mathsf{B}(c_{i},2r_{i})
, which contradicts the choice of
t_{i}
which is the closest-to-
t
vertex in
\mathsf{B}(c_{i},2r_{i})
on
\pi
.
∎

Observation 3.3
.

For each
1\leq i\leq l
, the subpath
\alpha_{i}
is entirely contained in the ball
\mathsf{B}(c_{i},4r_{i})
.

Proof.

From the description of the subroutine, for each
1\leq i\leq l
,
s_{i}\in\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})
and
t_{i}\in\mathsf{B}(c_{i},2r_{i})
. Therefore, for every vertex
u\in\pi[s_{i},t_{i}]
, by triangle inequality:

\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},u)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},s_{i})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{i},t_{i})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},s_{i})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},s_{i})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},t_{i})\leq 4r_{i},

which implies that
u\in\mathsf{B}(c_{i},4r_{i})
.
∎

Observation 3.4
.

Let
R
be the minimum radius of the balls in
\mathcal{B}
. Then
l\leq|\pi|/R+1
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that, for each
1\leq i<l
,
|\alpha_{i}|\geq R
.
From the algorithm, vertex
s_{i}
belongs to the ball
\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})
, and vertex
v_{i}
is the last vertex on
\pi
that lies in the ball
\mathsf{B}(c_{i},2r_{i})
, so if
t_{i}\neq t
then
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(t_{i},c_{i})=2r_{i}
must hold. Therefore,

|\pi[s_{i},t_{i}]|\geq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},t_{i})-\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},s_{i})\geq 2r_{i}-r_{i}=r_{i}\geq R
.
∎

Level of balls.

Before we describe the algorithm for constructing the sets
\left\{{\mathcal{P}}_{c}\right\}
, we first classify all clusters according to their sizes as follows.
Recall that we are initially given a set
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq V\times V
of pairs in
G
.
For each integer
1\leq i\leq\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil
, we say that a ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
is
at level
i
if

n^{1-i\varepsilon}/\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}<|\mathsf{B}(c,r)|\leq n^{1-(i-1)\varepsilon}/\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}.

Denote by
\mathcal{B}_{i}
the set of all level-
i
balls in
\mathcal{B}
.
We define the set of level-
0
balls as
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{\mathsf{B}(c,r)\mid|\mathsf{B}(c,r)|>n/\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}\}
, so
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{0}\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{B}_{\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil}
. A ball from
\mathcal{B}
is called
large
if it is in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, and
small
otherwise. Here is a simple observation.

Observation 3.5
.

For each
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil
,
|\mathcal{B}_{i}|\leq O(n^{(i+1)\varepsilon}\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}/\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
,
\sum_{\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}|\mathsf{B}(c,r)|\leq\sum_{\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|\leq O(n^{1+\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)
, and since each ball in
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
has size at least
n^{1-i\varepsilon}/\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}
,
|\mathcal{B}_{i}|\leq O(n^{(i+1)\varepsilon}\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}/\varepsilon)
.
∎

3.2
Step 1. Handling large balls

We first take a uniformly random subset
S\subseteq V
of size
\left\lceil 10\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}\log n\right\rceil
. Since each ball in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
contains at least
n/\sqrt{|{\mathcal{P}}|}
vertices, with high probability,
S
intersects all balls in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.

We now proceed to iteratively construct the sets
\left\{{\mathcal{P}}_{c}\right\}
of pairs. Throughout, we maintain, for each ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in{\mathcal{B}}
, a set
{\mathcal{P}}_{c}
of pairs of vertices in
\mathsf{B}(c,2r)
, and another set
U_{c}\subseteq S
of vertices in
V
, which are initially empty sets. Intuitively,
U_{c}
collects all vertices in
S
whose distances from the ball center

are already preserved by the spanner
H_{D}
during the algorithm.

For every pair
s,t
of vertices in
S
, we denote by

an arbitrary shortest path connecting them in
G
, and we compute the set

\Pi=\{\pi_{s,t}\mid s,t\in S,\text{ }\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)<2D+4\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}\}.

We then process all paths in
\Pi
sequentially in an arbitrary order. For each path
\pi_{s,t}\in\Pi
, we first apply the subroutine
PathPartition
to it and the collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of balls, and obtain a partition
\pi_{s,t}=\alpha_{1}\circ\alpha_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\alpha_{l}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq l
, we denote by
\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})
the ball that hosts the subpath
\alpha_{i}
.
If there exists a ball
\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})
such that

. Otherwise, for each

to the set

(s_{i},t_{i})
to the set

of pairs.

Stretch analysis of Step 1.

We make use of the following simple observation.

Observation 3.6
.

At the end of Step 1, for each ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}
,
|{\mathcal{P}}_{c}|\leq|S|=\left\lceil 10|{\mathcal{P}}|^{1/2}\log n\right\rceil

Proof.

By the algorithm description along with

, every time a new pair is added to

, a new vertex from

is also added to

. Since no more pair will be added to

as long as

contains all vertices of

, we get that

.
∎

We now show in the following claims that, after the first step, all pairwise distances (in

are well-preserved in

. Therefore, as the set

hits all balls in

was added to the collection

∎

Claim 3.8
.

For any

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+100\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}.

For any such pair of vertices

•

:

In this case, for each

3.3
Step 2. Handling small balls

We now process all pairs
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{P}}
with
D\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)<2D
in an arbitrary order. Take any such pair
(s,t)
, and compute a shortest path
\pi_{s,t}
between them in
G
. We then apply the subroutine
PathPartition
to it and the collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of balls, and obtain a partitioning
\pi_{s,t}=\alpha_{1}\circ\alpha_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\alpha_{l}
together with the balls
\left\{\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})\right\}_{1\leq i\leq l}
that host them. However, as the number of pairs
(s,t)\in{\mathcal{P}}
with

can be very large, we cannot afford to add the pair
(s_{i},t_{i})

Basic notations.

We will now describe how to choose these indices. This is again done via an iterative process. Throughout, we will gradually construct a binary tree
\mathcal{T}
, which initially contains a single node, which is the root of
\mathcal{T}
.
Each node of the tree is labeled by a subinterval of
[1,l]

\mathcal{T}
is labeled with
[1,l]
. Each index
i\in[1,l]
is either marked

or

\mathcal{T}

(s_{i},t_{i})
to the set
{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{i}}

We say that an index

is

(that is, if

Path-buying with tree structure
\mathcal{T}

As a pre-processing step, we first focus on level-
0

be the smallest and the largest level-
0

\mathcal{T}

[1,i_{1}-1]

[i_{2}+1,l]

[1,i_{1}-1]
and
[i_{2}+1,l]
are at least
1
. Here we do not modify the active/inactive status of the indices.

We now describe an iteration. We take an arbitrary leaf of the tree
\mathcal{T}
whose associated interval contains an inactive index, and denote by
I
the interval associated with this leaf node. We can assume that all levels of
I
are at least
1
.
For each

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{L}_{a}},c_{i^{L}_{b}})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{L}_{a}},c_{i^{L}_{b}})+\left(3\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}+10\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}\right)\cdot D^{1-1/k}.

Case 1. For all

\mathcal{T}

Case 2. There exists some

In this case,
we activate the

In order to describe the modification of

Lemma 3.9

There exists three level-

Lemma 3.11

For every pair

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+2^{30k/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}

Proof.

We will utilize the tree structure of

\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}
rooted at

Claim 3.12
.

For each non-root tree node
N
with height

and associated interval

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})+30\cdot(2^{h+1}-1)\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}.

3.12
.

We prove the claim by induction on

. The base case is when

and
N
is a leaf in
\mathcal{T}
. From the algorithm, for each
i\in[i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}]

in

\displaystyle|\phi_{j}|\leq|\alpha_{j}|+2^{30(k-1)/\varepsilon}\cdot|\alpha_{j}|^{1-1/(k-1)}

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{j}|+2^{30(k-1)/\varepsilon}\cdot\left(4\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}\right)^{1-1/(k-1)}

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{j}|+4\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-2/k}.

Taking a summation and using
3.4
(which implies that

),

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s_{i^{\prime}},t_{i^{\prime\prime}})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{i^{\prime}},t_{i^{\prime\prime}})+12\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}.

By triangle inequality, we have:

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s_{i^{\prime}},t_{i^{\prime\prime}})\geq\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})-\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{\prime}},s_{i^{\prime}})-\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{\prime\prime}},t_{i^{\prime\prime}})\geq\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})-3\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k},

\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{i^{\prime}},t_{i^{\prime\prime}})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime}},s_{i^{\prime}})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime\prime}},t_{i^{\prime\prime}})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})+3\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

Combining all three inequalities, we have:

\displaystyle\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})+\left(12\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}+6\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}\right)\cdot D^{1-1/k}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i^{\prime}},c_{i^{\prime\prime}})+18\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}.

Assume now that the claim is true for
1,\ldots,h-1
. Consider a node
N

with two children

associated with intervals
[i^{\prime},i_{x}]
and
[i_{y},i^{\prime\prime}]

Symmetrically, we also have

.
The base case is when

4.1
Algorithm description

We now describe the construction of graph

to

with parameters

and

. Let

Similar to the algorithm in

for each ball

for each small ball

for each ball

.
We then compute

-

We now proceed to iteratively construct the sets

\mathsf{B}(c,2r)

We then process all paths in

of balls, and obtain a partitioning

If either all the balls

\mathsf{B}(c,4r)

|\phi_{i}|\leq 4r\leq 4\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
. Therefore,
\rho=\phi_{1}\circ\phi_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{i}
is a path in
H_{D}
that connects
s
and
c
, and moreover,

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,c)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}|\phi_{j}|\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,c)+50\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

∎

Claim 4.4
.

For any pair of vertices
s,t\in S
such that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)<2D+4\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
,

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+101\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

Proof.

For any such pair of vertices
s,t\in S
, consider the moment when the shortest path
\pi_{s,t}\in\Pi
was processed and partitioned into
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}
. We distinguish between the following two cases.

•

There existed an index
1\leq i\leq l
such that
s,t\in U_{c_{i}}
at the moment.

In this case, by
4.3
,
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,c_{i})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,c_{i})+50\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
, and
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i},t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},t)+50\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
. By triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,c_{i})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{i},t)

\displaystyle\leq(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,s_{i})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{i},c_{i}))+(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{i},t)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{i},c_{i}))

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+2\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

Therefore,
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,c_{i})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(c_{i},t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+101\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
.

•

There did not exist any
i
such that
s,t\in U_{c_{i}}
at the moment.

In this case, for each
1\leq j\leq l
, the pair
(s_{j},t_{j})
is added to the set
{\mathcal{P}}_{c_{j}}
after this iteration. According to the algorithm, in the resulting graph
H_{D}
, for each
1\leq i\leq l
, there is a path
\phi_{i}
in
{H_{D}}
between
s_{i},t_{i}
such that
|\phi_{i}|\leq|\alpha_{i}|+4\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}D^{1-2/k}
. By
3.4
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}|\phi_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\left(|\alpha_{i}|+4\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}D^{1-2/k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+48\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

∎

In the following lemma, we complete the analysis of stretch of the graph
H_{D}
on pairs of vertices in
G
at distance at most
2D
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any pair of vertices
s,t\in V
such that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)<2D
, we have:

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+2^{30k/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be an
s
-
t
shortest path. We apply the subroutine
PathPartition
to path
\pi
and the set
\mathcal{B}
of balls and obtain a partition
\pi=\alpha_{1}\circ\alpha_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\alpha_{l}
and balls
\mathsf{B}(c_{1},r_{1}),\mathsf{B}(c_{2},r_{2}),\ldots,\mathsf{B}(c_{l},r_{l})
. If all these balls are small, note that for each
1\leq i\leq l
, the subpath
\alpha_{i}
lies entirely in
G[\mathsf{B}(r_{i},r_{i})]
, so there exists a path
\phi_{i}
in
H_{D}
, such that

\displaystyle|\phi_{i}|\leq|\alpha_{i}|+2^{30(k-1)/\varepsilon}\cdot|\alpha_{i}|^{1-1/(k-1)}

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{i}|+2^{30(k-1)/\varepsilon}\cdot\left(4\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}\cdot D^{1-1/k}\right)^{1-1/(k-1)}

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{i}|+4\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}D^{1-2/k}.

Since
l\leq\left\lceil\frac{|\pi|}{D^{1-1/k}}\right\rceil<2D^{1/k}+1<3D^{1/k}
, we get that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+12\cdot 2^{(30k-20)\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
.

Assume from now on that, some ball among
\mathsf{B}(c_{1},r_{1}),\mathsf{B}(c_{2},r_{2}),\ldots,\mathsf{B}(c_{l},r_{l})
is large. Let
1\leq x\leq l
be the smallest index of a large ball, and let
1\leq y\leq l
be the largest index of a large ball. By the hitting set property, there exist
u,v\in S
such that
u\in\mathsf{B}(c_{x},r_{x}),v\in\mathsf{B}(c_{y},r_{y})
. By triangle inequality,

\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u,v)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{x},c_{y})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{x},u)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c_{y},v)<2D+4\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

From
4.4
,
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(u,v)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u,v)+101\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}
.

For each
j\in[1,x)\cup(y,l]
, since
\mathsf{B}(c_{j},r_{j})
is small, there is a path
\phi_{j}
in
H_{D}
connecting
s_{j}
to
t_{j}
, such that

|\phi_{j}|\leq|\alpha_{j}|+2^{30(k-1)/\varepsilon}\cdot|\alpha_{j}|^{1-1/(k-1)}\leq|\alpha_{j}|+4\cdot 2^{(30k-20)/\varepsilon}D^{1-2/k}.

Then,

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,u)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(v,t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,u)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(v,t)+12\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

Finally, by triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{dist}}_{H_{D}}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,u)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u,v)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(v,t)+113\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}

\displaystyle\leq(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,s_{x})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{x},u))

\displaystyle+(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{x},s_{y})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{x},u)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{y},v))

\displaystyle+(\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{y},t)+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(v,s_{y}))+113\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+8\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}+113\cdot 2^{(30k-10)/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,t)+2^{30k/\varepsilon}D^{1-1/k}.

∎

4.3
Size analysis

From the algorithm, the edge set of
H_{D}
contains three types of edges that are calculated below.

1.

For each ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}
, graph
H_{D}
contains a BFS tree
T_{c}
that is rooted at
c
and spans all vertices in
\mathsf{B}(c,4r)
. From
Lemma

2.2
,
\sum|E(T_{c})|=\sum|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|=O(n^{1+\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)
.

2.

For each small ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}
, graph
H_{D}
contains a sublinear additive spanner of the subgraph
G[\mathsf{B}(c,4r)]
with stretch function
f_{k-1,\varepsilon}
, which contains
O(|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|^{1+10(k-1)\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2^{k}-1}})
edges by inductive hypothesis. Summing over all small balls, the number of edges in these spanners is at most (ignoring constant factors):

\sum_{\mathsf{B}(c,r)\text{ is small}}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|^{1+10(k-1)\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2^{k}-1}}\leq n^{1+(10k-5)\varepsilon}\cdot L_{k}^{\frac{1}{2^{k}-1}}.

3.

For each large ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}
, graph
H_{D}
contains a pairwise sublinear additive spanner of the induced subgraph
G[\mathsf{B}(c,4r)]
with respect to the set
{\mathcal{P}}_{c}
of pairs, with stretch function
f_{k-1,\varepsilon}
. By
Lemma

3.1
and
4.2
, the number of edges in such a spanner is at most

\tilde{O}\left(2^{2k/\varepsilon}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|^{1+10(k-1)\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{L_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\right).

Summing over all large balls, the number of edges in all these spanners is at most

\tilde{O}\left(\sum_{\mathsf{B}(c,r)\text{ is large}}2^{2k/\varepsilon}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|^{1+10(k-1)\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{L_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\right)\leq 2^{2k/\varepsilon}n^{1+(10k-5)\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{L_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}.

Setting
L_{k}=n^{\frac{2^{k}-1}{2^{k+1}-1}}
, the total number of edges over all the above types is at most
O(2^{2k/\varepsilon}\cdot n^{1+(10k-5)\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}})
. Summing over all
D\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,2^{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}\right\}
, we can conclude that
|E(H)|\leq O(n^{1+10k\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}-1}})
.

5
Subset Additive Spanners

In this section, we prove the following lemma, which will serve as a building block for
Theorem

1.2
.
For a graph
G=(V,E)
, a subgraph
H\subseteq G
and a subset
U\subseteq V
, we say that
H
is a
subset spanner of
G
on
U
with additive error
f(n)
, if for every pair
u,u^{\prime}\in U
,
\textnormal{{dist}}_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u,u^{\prime})+f(n)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there is an algorithm that, given an unweighted undirected graph
G
on
n
vertices and
m
edges, and a subset
U\subseteq V(G)
, in time
O\big(m\big(|U|+2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\big)\big)
, computes a subset spanner
H
of
G
on
U
with additive stretch
O(|U|^{3/2}\cdot n^{\varepsilon})
, such that
|E(H)|=O\left(2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot n\log n\right)
.

Remark 5.2
.

If we do not care about the runtime constraint, then the size of
E(H)
can be made
2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n
by replacing
Lemma

2.2
with the original Lemma 13 from
[
BW21
]
.

5.1
Algorithm description

We now describe the algorithm for
Lemma

5.1
.
We first slightly perturb the (unit) weight of each edge, such that in the resulting graph, there is a
unique shortest path
between every pair of vertices. When calculating their distances, we ignore this perturbation. It is easy to verify that any subset spanner of this resulting graph immediately gives a subset spanner of the original graph with the same stretch function. We rename the resulting graph by
G
.

We now describe the algorithm for constructing
H
.
We first apply the algorithm from
Lemma

2.2
to
G
with parameter
R=\left\lceil|U|^{3/2}\right\rceil
while replacing
\varepsilon
in
Lemma

2.2
with
\frac{10}{\varepsilon\log_{2}n}
, and obtain a set
\mathcal{B}
of balls in time
O\left(2^{10/\varepsilon}\cdot m\log n\right)
. Classify balls in
{\mathcal{B}}
as two categories: (Small)
|\mathsf{B}(c,r)|\leq|U|^{2}
, (Large)
|\mathsf{B}(c,r)|>|U|^{2}

For each ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}

\sum_{c}|E(T_{c})|=\sum_{c}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|=O\left(2^{10/\varepsilon}n\log n\right)
.

Handling small balls.

Consider a small ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}
. From Lemma
2.1
, we can compute in time
O(\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r)))
an integer

such that

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\{\mathsf{B}(c,d)\mid\mathsf{B}(c,r)\in\mathcal{B}\text{ is small}\}.

We then apply the algorithm from
Corollary

2.5
to the induced subgraph
G[\mathsf{B}(c,4r)]
with

, and obtain a collection
\Pi_{c}
of shortest paths. We define

.
From
Corollary

2.5
and
Lemma

2.2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{c}|E(L_{c})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}O\left(|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|+\sqrt{|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|}\cdot\left(\frac{|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|}{R}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}O(2^{15/\varepsilon}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|)=O(2^{25/\varepsilon}n\log n).

Handling large balls.

We first apply the algorithm from
Corollary

2.5
to
G
with

, and obtain a collection

of shortest paths connecting pairs of

.
We will then iteratively construct, for each large ball

We then process all paths in

from

to

. Then, the algorithm consists of the following steps.

1.

Recall that a vertex is covered by

3.

Otherwise, add all paths in

s_{1}\in\mathsf{B}(c_{x},r_{x})\cap S
and
s_{2}\in\mathsf{B}(c_{y},r_{y})\cap S
. Since
H
contains a subset spanner
\hat{H}
on
S
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{dist}}_{H}(s_{1},s_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{1},s_{2})+\tilde{O}(|S|^{3/2}n^{\varepsilon})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s_{1},s_{2})+\tilde{O}(Rn^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u_{x},u_{y})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u_{x},s_{1})+\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u_{y},s_{2})+\tilde{O}(Rn^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u_{x},u_{y})+\tilde{O}(Rn^{\varepsilon}).

Using similar arguments in the proof of
6.4
, we can show that:

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H}(s,u_{x})\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(s,u_{x})+15\cdot Rn^{\varepsilon}+\left(2^{15/\varepsilon}\cdot n^{\varepsilon}\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{x-1}\frac{|\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})|}{R^{2/3}}\right),

\textnormal{{dist}}_{H}(u_{y},t)\leq\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(u_{y},t)+15\cdot Rn^{\varepsilon}+\left(2^{15/\varepsilon}\cdot n^{\varepsilon}\cdot\sum_{i=y+1}^{l-1}\frac{|\mathsf{B}(c_{i},r_{i})|}{R^{2/3}}\right).

Summing over all three inequalities completes the proof.
∎

Setting
R=\left\lceil n^{3/7}\right\rceil
, the above claim implies that the additive error of
H
is
\tilde{O}(n^{3/7+\varepsilon})
.

6.1.2
Size and runtime analysis

Claim 6.8

(spanner size)
.

|E(H)|=2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot n\log n
.

Proof.

From
Lemma

5.1
, each subset spanner
L_{c}
within
G[\mathsf{B}(c,4r)]
contains
2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|
edges, and so
\sum_{c}|E(L_{c})|=2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot 2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n\log n=2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n\log n
. Similarly, the global subset spanner
\hat{H}
satisfies that
|E(\hat{H})|=2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n\log n
.
Additionally,
\sum_{c}|E(T_{c})|=\sum_{c}|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|=2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n\log n
edges. Altogether, we get that
|E(H)|=2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot n\log n
.
∎

Claim 6.9

(runtime)
.

The runtime of the algorithm is
\tilde{O}(m+|E(G^{\prime})|\cdot 2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot R^{2/3})
.

Proof.

The runtime for computing
G^{\prime}
is
\tilde{O}(m)
.
From
Lemma

2.2
, the set
\mathcal{B}
of balls can be computed in time
2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot|E(G^{\prime})|
.
The runtime for computing BFS trees within balls in
\mathcal{B}
is
2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot|E(G^{\prime})|
.
From
Lemma

5.1
, the runtime for computing subset spanners within small balls is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{c}O\bigg(\operatorname{vol}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))\cdot\bigg(2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}+\frac{2|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|}{R}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c}O\bigg(\operatorname{vol}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))\cdot 2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot R^{2/3}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot 2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot R^{2/3}.

The claim now follows.
∎

Since we set
R=\left\lceil n^{3/7}\right\rceil
, the above claim implies that the runtime of the algorithm for computing
H
is
\tilde{O}(m+2^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot n^{13/7})
, as
|E(G^{\prime})|=O(n^{11/7})
.

.

Let

Remark 6.11

If we do not care about the runtime constraint, then the spanner size of

(that is, without the logarithmic factor) by replacing

]

We now use

to prove

1.2
.
We set
\varepsilon>0
as a small enough constant.
Note that

\mathsf{Alg}=\mathsf{Alg}_{0}

Proof Sketch of

.

The algorithm for

Step 1.

Sparsify

Sample a random subset

The output graph

Stretch analysis.

Appendix A
Proof of
Lemma

2.2

Throughout, we use the parameter
\beta=n^{\varepsilon}
.

The algorithm iteratively builds the collection
\mathcal{B}
. Initially,
\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
. The algorithm continues to be executed as long as there exists a vertex that is not covered by the collection
\mathcal{B}
.
We now describe an iteration. First, we pick an arbitrary vertex
c
that is not covered by the current collection
\mathcal{B}
,
and add a new ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
to
{\mathcal{B}}
centered at
c
. Its radius
r
is determined by the following process:

1.

Start with
r=R
.

2.

Perform breadth-first search from
c
in
G
to compute the ball
\mathsf{B}(c,4r)
.

3.

If
|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|\leq\beta\cdot|\mathsf{B}(c,r/2)|
and
\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))\leq\beta\cdot\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,r/2))
, then add the ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
to
\mathcal{B}
, and terminate the iteration. Otherwise, update
r\leftarrow 4r
and repeat Steps (2) and (3).

We now proceed to analyze the algorithm.
First, it is easy to see that the radius of every ball in
{\mathcal{B}}
at the end of the algorithm is at least
R
, as the process of determining the radius of each new ball start with
r=R
and only increases
r
afterwards.
Second, from the algorithm, when a new ball is added to
{\mathcal{B}}
, its radius
r
is determined by an iterative process, where in each round,
r
is increased by a factor of
4
whenever
|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|>\beta\cdot|\mathsf{B}(c,r/2)|
or
\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))>\beta\cdot|\mathsf{B}(c,r/2)|
. As
|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|
and
\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))
are bounded by
n
and
m
respectively, the number of times that the radius
r
is increased is at most
\left\lceil\log_{\beta}n\right\rceil+\left\lceil\log_{\beta}m\right\rceil<5/\varepsilon
. Therefore, in the end,
r\leq R\cdot 4^{5/\varepsilon}=R\cdot 2^{10/\varepsilon}
.

We next prove the following observation.

Observation A.1
.

At the end of the algorithm, for every vertex
v\in V
, there are at most
5/\varepsilon
balls
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
in
{\mathcal{B}}
, such that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c,v)\leq r/2
.

Proof.

We first prove the following observation.

Observation A.2
.

When a new ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
is added to the collection
{\mathcal{B}}
, for any other ball
\mathsf{B}(c^{\prime},r^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{B}
with
\mathsf{B}(c^{\prime},r^{\prime}/2)\cap\mathsf{B}(c,r/2)\neq\emptyset
,
r^{\prime}\leq r/4
must hold.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise that
r^{\prime}>r/4
. As all radius are integral powers of
4
,
r^{\prime}\geq r
. Therefore,
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c,c^{\prime})\leq r^{\prime}/2+r/2\leq r^{\prime}
, and so
c\in\mathsf{B}(c^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, which means that
c
was covered by the collection
{\mathcal{B}}
before the ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
is added, a contradiction.
∎

We say that a vertex
v
is
captured
by a ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
if
\textnormal{{dist}}_{G}(c,v)\leq r/2
.
From
A.2
, when
v
is captured by a new ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
, its radius
r
is at least
4
times the radius of any other ball in
{\mathcal{B}}
that captures
v
. As we have shown that the radius of every ball in
{\mathcal{B}}
is at least
R
and at most
R\cdot 4^{5/\varepsilon}
, the number of balls in
{\mathcal{B}}
that captures
v
is at most
5/\varepsilon
.
∎

From
A.1
, at the end of the algorithm, each vertex in
G
is occupied by at most
O(1/\varepsilon)
balls in
\mathcal{B}
. Therefore,
\sum|\mathsf{B}(c,r/2)|\leq O(n/\varepsilon)
; and

\sum|\mathsf{B}(c,4r)|\leq\beta\cdot\sum|\mathsf{B}(c,r/2)|\leq O(n^{1+\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)
.
Similarly,
\sum\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))\leq\beta\cdot\sum\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,r/2))\leq\beta\cdot O(m/\varepsilon)=O(m\cdot n^{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)
.

Finally, note that when we add a ball
\mathsf{B}(c,r)
to
{\mathcal{B}}
, the running time of the algorithm in that iteration is
O(\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r)))
. Therefore, algorithm terminates in time
O\big(\sum\operatorname{vol}(\mathsf{B}(c,4r))\big)\leq O(m\cdot n^{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)
.
