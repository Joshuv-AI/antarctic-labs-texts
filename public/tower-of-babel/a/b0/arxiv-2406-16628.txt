Effective atypical intersections and applications to orbit closures
Gregorio Baldi, David Urbanik

Abstract
We propose a new unifying setting for dealing with monodromically atypical intersections that goes beyond the usual Zilber-Pink conjecture. In particular, we give a new proof of finiteness of the maximal atypical orbit closures in each stratum of translation surfaces Ω ​ ℳ g ​ ( κ ) \Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa) , as given by Eskin, Filip, and Wright. We also describe a concrete algorithm, implementable in principle on a computer, which provably computes all maximal orbit closures which are “atypical” in a sense described by Filip. The same methods also give a general algorithm for computing atypical special loci associated to systems of differential equations, and in particular give an effective and o-minimal free proof of the geometric Zilber-Pink conjecture for variations of mixed Hodge structures.

Introduction

In the last 15 years, moduli spaces of translation surfaces
(X,\omega)
have attracted a lot of attention, thanks to the work of Eskin, Filip, McMullen, Mirzakhani, Mohammadi, Möller, Wright, and many others. A variety of tools have been used to try to grasp them, mostly from dynamics, analysis, and algebraic geometry. In particular, the work of the aforementioned authors gives striking results on so-called
orbit closures

\mathcal{N}=\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{+}\cdot(X,\omega)}
. The aim of this paper is to push the Hodge-theoretic and algebro-geometric understanding of such loci
\mathcal{N}
one step further. Using tools from differential geometry and differential algebra we will show that one can understand the distribution of orbit closures just by knowing whether they are
typical or atypical intersections
.

This new perspective will ultimately come from a new understanding of the (geometric) Zilber–Pink philosophy in the following setting. We start with
S
a smooth complex algebraic variety,
H
an algebraic group (which is a semidirect product of a unipotent and a semisimple group), and
\pi:P\to S
an algebraic
H
-torsor. The torsor
P
will carry a
H
-principal flat connection
\nabla
with Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(\nabla)=H
. We will then study the behaviour of
atypical special subvarieties

W
of
S
(e.g. in the sense of Hodge or Teichmüller theory) by pulling back to
P
certain algebraic data from an extended period domain. Our work will crucially use recent breakthrough work of Blázquez-Sanz, Casale, Freitag, and Nagloo
[
25
]
. Thanks to an observation of Filip
[
47
, §5]
(partially motivated by recent progress in Hodge theory regarding the distribution of the Hodge locus
[
16
]
), such a setting recovers the atypical orbit closures. Indeed the main motivation behind our work is
Question

1.4
, recalled below. Our result on the orbit closures rests on the deep fact that orbit closures are algebraic and admit a purely algebro-geometric characterization (see e.g.
[
47
, Thm. 2]
).

1.1
Special subvarieties in Teichmüller and Hodge theory, after Eskin-Filip-Wright, and Baldi-Klingler-Ullmo

We start by recalling the main protagonists of the theory of moduli spaces of translation surfaces, mostly following the recent notes
[
97
,
47
]
. Let
X
be an irreducible smooth projective algebraic curve over
\mathbb{C}
, or simply a compact
Riemann surface
. A
translation surface
is a pair
(X,\omega)
, where
X
is a compact Riemann surface and
\omega
is a non-zero global section of the cotangent bundle of
X
(also known as an
abelian differential
). If
g\geq 1
is the genus of
X
, then
\omega
has
2g-2
zeros, counted with multiplicities. Let
\mathcal{M}_{g}
denote the moduli space of genus
g
compact Riemann surfaces and
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
the bundle whose fibre over
[X]\in\mathcal{M}_{g}
is the space of all holomorphic 1-forms on
X
. It is a rank
g
(algebraic) vector bundle on
\mathcal{M}_{g}
, naturally equipped with a stratification by smooth (not necessarily irreducible) algebraic subvarieties
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, where
\kappa=(\kappa_{0},\dots,\kappa_{n})
satisfies
\sum_{i}\kappa_{i}=2g-2
and denotes the multiplicities of zeros of
\omega
. We will also write
n=\operatorname{length}(\kappa)-1
. (In fact, to avoid orbifold issues, we will later work with finite covers of such moduli spaces).

A holomorphic 1-form
\omega
on
X
can be thought of as giving a collection of ‘charts’ on
X
to
\mathbb{C}
, such that:

•

the transition maps are translations,

•

the charts are allowed to be ramified at finitely many points corresponding to the zeros of
\omega
,

•

they are locally given by
z\mapsto\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\omega
.

Such charts induce the so-called
period coordinates
on
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
. We therefore obtain an action of
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, locally given by the diagonal action on a product of copies of
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Such an action is a priori outside the realm of algebraic geometry, but it turns out to be closely related to Hodge theory.

We will study the distribution of strict orbit closures
\mathcal{N}:=\overline{\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cdot(X,\omega)}\subset\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, where
\overline{(\cdot)}
denotes the topological closure, also known in the literature as
affine invariant submanifolds
. (For terminology and notations we try to follow
[
47
]
.) Associated to an orbit closure there are (at least) two natural numbers that measure its complexity, namely the degree and rank; see §
5.2
for precise definitions.

We start by recalling a theorem of Eskin, Filip, and Wright. Related methods were earlier used by Matheus-Wright
[
68
]
and Lanneau-Nguyen-Wright
[
65

to prove finiteness results in the special case of Teichmüller curves.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
43

)
.

In each (irreducible component of each) stratum
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, all but finitely many orbit closures have rank 1 and degree at most 2. In each genus there is a finite union of orbit closures
\mathcal{N}
of rank 2 and degree 1 such that all but finitely many of the orbit closures of rank 1 and degree 2 are a codimension 2 subvariety of one of these
\mathcal{N}
.

In fact, it will be better to work with an equivalent formulation of the above. We will see that in the above result, the orbit closures which arise in finite families are
atypical intersections
, and the others are typical ones. Already in the introduction of
[
43
]
a link with the André–Oort conjecture is mentioned. But it is only in the recent notes of Filip
[

where this is made more precise. The following, again due to Eskin, Filip, and Wright
[

1.1
(see also
[

, Thm. 3]
and §

Theorem 1.2

Each orbit closure
\mathcal{M}
contains only finitely many maximal atypical suborbit closures. If an orbit closure

admits relatively typical suborbit closures, then those are dense in

With the above formulation, the result looks similar to what happens in Hodge theory, as recently shown by Baldi, Klingler, and Ullmo

(see §

Theorem 1.3

(
[

)

Let

be a

1.

The positive period dimensional atypical Hodge locus is a strict algebraic subvariety of

2.

The positive period dimensional typical Hodge locus is either empty or analytically (therefore Zariski) dense in

.

One expects the same results to be true without the

1.2
The abstract/unified setting

Special Manifolds and Intersections:

Certain manifolds
M
come naturally with a distinguished class of submanifolds
\{M_{i}\}_{i\in I}
exhibiting a
special
behaviour. Examples include abelian varieties with sub-abelian varieties and their torsion translates; Shimura varieties and more general period domains with sub-Shimura varieties and sub-Mumford-Tate domains; and locally homogeneous spaces with totally geodesic sub-spaces. Given a submanifold
S\subset M
, one then obtains from
\{M_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a distinguished class of
special intersections

S\cap M_{i}
. Here the examples of interest are (mixed) Hodge loci, and the case of particular interest to us: orbit closures within strata of abelian differentials (cf. (
6.1
) and the corresponding discussion). Zilber-Pink-type results in such settings suggest distribution behaviour of the special intersections can often be understood by simple dimension criteria. In particular, one says that a component
Y
of
S\cap M_{i}
is
atypical
if

\operatorname{codim}_{M}(Y)<\operatorname{codim}_{M}(S)+\operatorname{codim}_{M}(M_{i}),

(1.1)

and
typical
otherwise. One then expects that:

(1)

S
contains only finitely many maximal atypical intersections.

(2)

The following are equivalent:

(i)

S
contains one typical intersection;

(ii)

the collection of typical intersections is dense in
S
; and

(iii)

there exists an
i\in I
such that:

\dim S-\operatorname{codim}_{M}(M_{i})\geq 0.

Our goal is to discuss two concrete instances of such viewpoint. More precisely we will describe a general strategy to tackle (1) in both the setting of orbit closures and Hodge loci of variations of mixed Hodge structures. The setting we work with might look indirect at first, so we explain here the main protagonists, and how they relate to the above picture.

The Differential Viewpoint:

First of all, it will be crucial for us that
S
is algebraic, since the main tools come from algebraic geometry — in particular we will no longer speak of manifolds, but only of algebraic varieties
S
and complex analytic period domains
D
, and their quotients by lattices
\Gamma
. (With reference to the above viewpoint, one can think of
M
as some quotient
\Gamma\backslash D
of
D
into which
S
embeds.) We consider normal (pointed) complex varieties
S
, supporting a local system
\mathbb{V}
with algebraic monodromy group
\mathbf{H}_{S}
— for the applications,
\mathbb{V}
will underline a
\mathbb{Z}
-variation of mixed Hodge structures. Inspired by an idea of Bakker and Tsimerman
[
13
]
, in §
2.2
we introduce
P=P(s_{0})
, an algebraic principal
\mathbf{H}_{S}
-subbundle of the algebraic bundle
\sheafhom(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{O}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}})
associated to
\sheafhom(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{V}_{s_{0}}\times S)
(via the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence), where
\mathcal{V}
is the algebraic bundle associated to
\mathbb{V}
. The bundle
P
has an equivariant flat algebraic connection induced from the connection on
\mathbb{V}
, which foliates
P
by flat leaves
\mathcal{L}
. Through each point
x\in P
, there is a unique leaf
\mathcal{L}_{x}
. (Similar constructions appear elsewhere in the literature.)

The gist of our approach is the following. In mixed Hodge theory, the special intersections in
S
are particular cases of
weakly special subvarieties
, i.e. the maximal subvarieties of
S
with a given monodromy group. A recent spectacular result of Blázquez-Sanz, Casale, Freitag, and Nagloo
[
25
]
, known as the
Ax-Schanuel theorem
asserts that, if an algebraic subvariety
V\subset P
has an
atypical intersection

U\subset V\cap\mathcal{L}
with some leaf
\mathcal{L}
then the projection of
U
to the base lies in a strict weakly special subvariety. Our strategy now proceeds as follows:

I.

Relate the atypicality inequality (
1.1
) to an analogous one appearing in the torsor
P\to S
constructed in §
2.2
.

II.

Construct an algebraic family
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}
of subvarieties of
P
such that all subvarieties of
P
which can give rise to atypical intersections when intersected with
\mathcal{L}
appear as fibers of
f
.

III.

Use a suitable version of the Ax-Schanuel theorem
in families
§
3.3
to show that all atypical intersections with leaves parameterized by
\mathcal{Y}
project into the fibres of finitely many families of weakly special subvarieties of
S
(not necessarily atypical themselves); and

IV.

Conclude by either using some rigidity or maximality property.

Remark 1.5
.

We often refer to Step II as the “over-parameterization”, since there are usually extra varieties parameterized by
f
that need not give rise to atypical intersections inside
P
when intersected with
\mathcal{L}
. For more details regarding what we mean by a
suitable version
of the Ax-Schanuel Theorem in families in Step III, we refer to the end of
Section

3.3
.

To implement the above strategy, Hodge theory (essentially through the
fixed part theorem
) is needed at two steps. It is used to guarantee, a priori, that the atypical intersections we are interested in can be over-parametrized by algebraic families (of subvarieties of
P
), as well as to say that the weakly special subvarieties one obtains after applying the Ax-Schanuel theorem fibre-by-fibre can be assembled in countably many families (of subvarieties of
S
).

Arguments of this kind are not new, see for example
[
16
, §3.6]
for a history of successful applications of Ax-Schanuel-type theorems to Zilber-Pink. Also, in the case of subvarieties of Shimura varieties, a “differential-geometric” approach (rather than the o-minimal one of Baldi-Klingler-Ullmo, cf. also
[
20
]
) to the geometric Zilber-Pink theorem can already be found in
[
22
]
, and the generalization to arbitrary VMHS does not pose particular difficulties. There is however a key difference between earlier approaches and ours: namely, we use (an opportune) Ax-Schanuel result in
P
rather than the Hodge-theoretic one associated to some period domain
D
. At first sight, this might look like a small and technical difference but, in reality, it makes the scope of the Zilber-Pink theory much broader. The reason is that the Hodge-theoretic Ax theorem considers only atypical intersections inside
S\times D
, which typically has much smaller dimension than
P
. This means that there can be more atypical intersections in
P
, since there is more freedom to impose algebraic conditions on leaves
\mathcal{L}\subset P
than on the graph of some period map. This difference is crucial for treating the case of orbit closures: the space
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
supports a
\mathbb{Z}
VMHS, but the atypicality of the orbit closure
\mathcal{N}
can be detected only in an opportune automorphic bundle above the mixed Shimura variety associated to the
\mathbb{Z}
VMHS. Said in more plain terms: the conditions defining orbit closures include algebraic conditions on periods of the one-form
\omega
which do not come from conditions on Hodge structures, but can be imposed inside
P
. In particular our main results are evidence in favour of a deeper Zilber-Pink philosophy that enriches already the case of varieties mapping to mixed Shimura varieties, but also includes mixed Hodge theory.

1.3
Applications of the main result to Teichmüller and Hodge theory

1.3.1
What do we mean by effective?

When we use the term “effective” to describe a proof or a result, we will mean in the sense that the proof describes an explicit algorithm, implementable in principle on a computer, to compute the object whose existence is described by the proof; in particular, if we say that the proof of the finiteness of a set of orbit closures is effective, we mean that the proof gives an algorithm to compute this set. Note that our use of the term “algorithm” is always understood in the sense of “algorithm that is provably guaranteed to terminate with the correct output” — we generally do not speak of “non-terminating algorithms,” nor does the word “algorithm” refer to a kind of heuristic procedure or method.

To make such statements truly precise in an algebro-geometric context, it is important to specify computational models. More precisely, one should specify precisely in what terms the data involved in the proof — whether it be an algebraic variety, a connection, or an algebraic map — is being presented, both when viewed as “input data” and when viewed as “output data”. To do this carefully, we will largely follow the conventions of
[
91
, §2]
. We give additional details in §
9
.

1.5
Related works

Our paper is not the first one to use ideas from functional transcendence to study orbit closures, although the previous ones had different motivations. To conclude the introduction, we mention here the history, to the best of our knowledge, of results on translation surfaces/abelian differentials linked to the Zilber-Pink viewpoint. A first link between Zilber-Pink in
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}
and orbit closures was observed by Bainbridge, Habegger, and Möller
[
7
]
. Klingler and Lerer
[
61
]
started analysing strata of abelian differentials, through the lens of
bi-algebraic geometry
(however the
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action does not enter in the picture). In
[
13
]
, Bakker and Tsimerman proved
[
61
, Conj. 2.15]
, giving an
Ax–Lindemann
type of statement for abelian differentials. See also the related
[
41
]
, where the authors construct a non-linear bi-algebraic curve, resp. surface, of translation surfaces of genus 7, resp. 10. Even more recently, Benirschke
[
21
]
investigated some special cases of Ax-Schanuel results for translation surfaces. In a different but related direction, we also mention the work of Pila and Tsimerman, regarding an Ax-Schanuel theorem for complex curves and differentials
[
81
, Thm. 3.2]
. We hope the framework we consider will have more applications to this emerging viewpoint.

On the technical side, as we discussed above, the novelty of our approach is to work in the period torsor
P
. Its construction and the link between monodromy and differential Galois group is certainly not new and was noticed by several people in the nineties (but certainly known even before, since it is a generalization of Picard-Vessiot theory), and inspired by the work of Deligne
[
39
]
. However we weren’t able to find a reference that accommodates our construction. For example O. Gabber pointed out to us
[
29
, §5]
(in particular point b) of §5.4) and various papers and preprints of B. Malgrange (like
[
67
]
, where a more complicated setting is studied). One of the oldest references we are aware of is due to Schlesinger
[
84
]
. The role of the Gauss-Manin connection (and related foliated bundles) for the study of some Hodge loci is also highlighted in the work of Movasati, see for example
[
75
]
. With the aim of obtaining effective versions of the geometric Zilber-Pink for Shimura varieties (first obtained in
[
36
]
), Binyamini and Daw
[
22
]
also noticed the advantage of the differential algebraic geometry viewpoint for atypical intersection problems (regarding the arithmetic side of the story, we refer also to Binyamini’s work
[
23
]
). The framework considered by Binyamini and Binyamini-Daw, together with the very general Ax-Schanuel theorem of Blázquez-Sanz, Casale, Freitag, and Nagloo discussed above, and its Hodge theoretic incarnation first explored by Bakker and Tsimerman
[
13
]
were the main source of inspiration for our work. We remark here that, very recently, also the approach using o-minimality turned out to be effective
[
24
]
. Finally we mention also the work of Pila and Scanlon
[
80
]
, who used some interesting constructibility and model theoretic arguments related to algebraic differential equations as well as
[
60
]
(especially Thm. 6.1 in
op. cit.
).

Since the first draft of this paper appeared online, it has found intersecting applications
[
59
,
51
]
to the study of Ceresa cycles in families. Moreover, we recently proved, following the strategy developed in this article, a
uniform version
of the general mixed geometric Zilber-Pink theorem; see indeed
[
19
, Thm. 4.4]
.

Before starting our discussion on weakly special subvarieties and the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, we recall a well-known theorem of Chevalley (see e.g. Prop. 3.1 in Deligne’s article in the collection
[

]
).

Let

be an affine algebraic group over a field

K

.

Theorem 2.1

(Chevalley’s Theorem)
.

For any algebraic subgroup
H\subset G
, there exists a representation

constructed from

by standard tensor operations (i.e. taking direct sums, duals, or tensor products, in any order and any finite number of times), and a line

, such that

is the stabilizer of

Moreover if

2.1
Preliminary definitions

We start with a pointed complex irreducible smooth variety
(S,s_{0})
and a complex local system
\mathbb{V}
on
S
, corresponding to a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(S,s_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{V}_{s_{0}})
, where we identify
S
with its analytification.

Definition 2.2
.

Given an irreducible (not necessarily smooth) subvariety
(Y,s_{0})\subset(S,s_{0})
, we define its
algebraic monodromy group

\mathbf{H}_{Y}=\mathbf{H}_{Y,\mathbb{V}}=\mathbf{H}_{Y,\mathbb{V},s_{0}}
as the identity component of the Zariski closure of the induced monodromy representation
\pi_{1}(Y^{\textrm{nor}},s_{0})\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathbb{V}_{s_{0}})
, where
Y^{\textrm{nor}}\to Y
denotes the normalization. When
\mathbb{V}
and the point
s_{0}
are understood, we will just write
\mathbf{H}_{Y}
.

Note that algebraic monodromy groups are unchanged by passing to an open subvariety of
Y
, or replacing

with a smooth resolution — this is because if
X
is an irreducible complex normal variety and
U\subset X
is an open subvariety,
\pi_{1}(U)\to\pi_{1}(X)
is surjective, cf.
[
49
, (0.7) (B)]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A geometrically irreducible variety

is said to be
weakly special (for
\mathbb{V}
)
if it is maximal under inclusion among geometrically irreducible subvarieties of
S
for the dimension of
\mathbf{H}_{Y}
. In other words, any

strictly containing

satisfies
\mathbf{H}_{Y}\varsubsetneq\mathbf{H}_{Y^{\prime}}
.

Directly from the definition

is a weakly special subvariety, and in cases of interest — for instance when
\mathbb{V}
comes from a family of cohomology groups of algebraic varieties inducing a quasi-finite period map — every closed point

will also be weakly special. We will primarily interest ourselves in other weakly specials, not equal to either
S
or a closed point. The former we call
strict
weakly special subvarieties.

2.2
A recap of Riemann-Hilbert

Given a smooth irreducible algebraic variety
S
and complex local system
\mathbb{V}\to S
, there is the
Riemann-Hilbert correspondence
functor

\displaystyle\tau:\textrm{LocSys}(S^{\textrm{an}})

\displaystyle\to\textrm{MIC}_{\textrm{reg}}(S)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle\mapsto\tau(\mathbb{V})=(\mathcal{V},\nabla).

Here
\textrm{MIC}_{\textrm{reg}}
denotes the category of locally free
\mathcal{O}_{S}
-modules
\mathcal{V}
on
S
with regular-singular integrable connection
\nabla
.
2
2

2

We note that it is part of the theory of Deligne’s Riemann-Hilbert correspondence
[
39
]
that the connection
\nabla
is
algebraic
, i.e., after fixing local algebraic sections for
\mathcal{V}
and a basis of algebraic differentials on

S

The functor has the property that the space of
\nabla^{\textrm{an}}

up to natural isomorphism. Moreover, the analytic vector bundle
\mathbb{V}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{O}^{\textrm{an}}

with regular singularities. The functor

is an equivalence of (Tannakian) categories
[
39
]
.

Given a fixed such
(\mathcal{V},\nabla)
, the tensor powers
\mathcal{V}^{a,b}:=\mathcal{V}^{\otimes a}\otimes(\mathcal{V}^{*})^{\otimes b}
also carry natural algebraic connections, where we use
(-)^{*}
to denote the dual vector bundle. Correspondingly, write
\mathbb{V}^{a,b}=\mathbb{V}^{\otimes a}\otimes(\mathbb{V}^{*})^{\otimes b}
. Now let
\mathbf{H}_{S}
be the algebraic monodromy group of

. Using Chevalley’s
Theorem

2.1
on invariants, we may understand the group
\mathbf{H}_{S}
as the identity component of the stabilizer of a one-dimensional subsystem
\mathbb{L}\subset\bigoplus_{a,b}\mathbb{V}^{a,b}
, and hence it admits an algebraic realization
\mathbf{H}_{S,s}
in each fibre
\operatorname{GL}(\mathcal{V}_{s})
as the identity component of the stabilizer of
\tau(\mathbb{L})_{s}
.

Associated to this data we may construct a certain algebraic subbundle
P(s_{0})
of the principal left
\operatorname{GL}(\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}})

\sheafper(\mathcal{V}):=\sheafiso(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{O}_{S}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}})
; here the fibres
\sheafper(\mathcal{V})_{s}
above points

are identified with the set of isomorphisms
\textrm{Iso}(\mathcal{V}_{s},\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}})
. We write points of
\sheafper(\mathcal{V})
as pairs

, where
\beta:\mathcal{V}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}}
. To construct

we define

P(s_{0}):=\{(\beta,s)\in\sheafper(\mathcal{V}):\beta(\tau(\mathbb{L})_{s})=\tau(\mathbb{L})_{s_{0}}\}

(2.1)

where
\mathbb{L}\subset\bigoplus_{a,b}\mathbb{V}^{a,b}
is as above. This defines an algebraic subbundle of
\sheafper(\mathcal{V})
, and using flat frames we may observe that each

is naturally a left torsor for the Zariski closure
\mathbf{H}^{\textrm{full}}_{S}
of the monodromy representation at
s_{0}
.

The torsors

contain a canonical analytic subvariety
\mathcal{L}\subset P(s_{0})
, which we also call the
canonical leaf
, constructed as follows. The identity transformation
\textrm{id}_{s_{0}}\in\textrm{Hom}(\mathbb{V}_{s_{0}},\mathbb{V}_{s_{0}})
is a point of the fibre of
P(s_{0})
over

. If we analytically continue

\textrm{id}_{s_{0}}

\beta(\tau(\mathbb{L}))_{s}=\tau(\mathbb{L})_{s_{0}}

\mathbb{L}

We will usually work instead with the component

2.3
Torsor preliminaries

In this section we first introduce the formalism of
H
-torsors (also known as
principal bundles
) and principal connections. The reader may refer for example to
[
25
, §2]
for more details, although we will try to be as self contained as possible. Throughout we work over the complex numbers.

Let
S
be an (irreducible) smooth variety, and
H
a linear algebraic group. Let
\pi:P\to S
be an
H
-
torsor
; that is
H
acts on
P
(on the right, with the action denoted by
\cdot
) and such that the action induces an isomorphism

P\times H\simeq P\times_{S}P,\ (p,g)\mapsto(p,p\cdot g).

In particular the fibres of
\pi
are principal homogeneous spaces, and the choice of a point
p\in P_{s}:=\pi^{-1}(s)
induces an isomorphism
H\cong P_{s}
, given by
g\mapsto p\cdot g
. We have the following exact sequence of
S
-bundles

0\to\textrm{ker}\,d\pi\to TP\to TS\times_{S}P\to 0,

(2.2)

where
\textrm{ker}\,d\pi
is the so called
vertical bundle
. A
connection

\nabla
is a section of (
2.2
). We say that
\nabla
is
H
-
principal
if it is
H
-equivariant. We additionally say that
\nabla
is
flat
if it lifts to a morphism of Lie algebras: the equation
\nabla_{[v,w]}=[\nabla_{v},\nabla_{w}]
holds on the level of maps of
\mathbb{C}
-schemes. From now on all connections are
H
-principal and flat.

Since
\nabla
is flat, the space of rational
\nabla
-horizontal vector field is a singular foliation on
P
. Moreover, if
\nabla
is an algebraic flat connection we obtain a regular foliation
\mathcal{F}
, cf.
[
25
, §2.1 and 2.2]
. Let
m
be the rank of the foliation
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 2.4
.

An
integral submanifold
of
\mathcal{F}
is an
m
-dimensional immersed analytic submanifold
S\subset P

(not necessarily embedded in
P
) whose tangent space at each point is generated by the values
of vector fields in
\mathcal{F}
(i.e. the image of
\nabla
). Maximal connected integral submanifolds are called
leaves
.

Frobenius theorem ensures that through any regular point passes a unique leaf. Finally, we notice here that
\mathcal{F}
has also
vertical
leaves included
in the fibers of
P
at non regular points of
\nabla
, but we will never consider such case. (In particular when speaking of leaves, we always mean
horizontal
leaves, cf.
[
25
, end of §2.2]
.)

Example 2.5
.

The component
P
containing
(\textrm{id}_{s_{0}},s_{0})
of the bundle
P(s_{0})
introduced in §
2.2
is a principal
\mathbf{H}_{S}
-bundle that naturally comes with an algebraic flat connection. We start by constructing an analytic map

\nabla:TS\times_{S}\sheafper(\mathcal{V})\to T\sheafper(\mathcal{V})

of bundles over
S
which sends a pair
(v,(\beta,s))
to the tangent vector at
(\beta,s)
which corresponds to the unique lift of

to the leaf through
(\beta,s)
. Using the algebraicity of the connection on
\mathcal{V}
, one may show that this map is algebraic by working in explicit coordinates. For simplicity we may identify the fixed fibre
\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}}
with
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, with
m
the rank of
\mathcal{V}
. Working on some Zariski open
U\subset S
, we may choose a frame
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}
which trivializes
\mathcal{V}
over

, and a basis of differentials

for sheaf
\Omega^{1}_{U}
. We obtain a corresponding trivialization
\sheafper(\mathcal{V})\simeq U\times\operatorname{GL}_{m}
. Letting
[f_{tu}]_{1\leq t,u\leq m}
denote the coordinates on

, we wish to show that the resulting map

TU\times\operatorname{GL}_{m}\to TU\times T\operatorname{GL}_{m}

is an algebraic map. Note that the component of this map mapping to

is just
(v,(\beta,s))\mapsto v
, so it suffices to determine the second component. This means expressing the differentials

as algebraic functions of
ds_{i}
, the
s_{j}
, and the
f_{tu}
. How to do this is explained in
[

.

Note that, by construction, the map
\nabla
restricts to give a map
TS\times_{S}P(s_{0})\to TP(s_{0})
, which gives the desired algebraic flat connection on the principal
\mathbf{H}_{S}
-bundle

-equivariant.

.

We remark here that similar considerations appeared (at least in the VHS setting) in

Definition 3.1

3.2
A constructibility fact

A useful fact which we will use repeatedly is the following:

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
(P,\nabla)
be an algebraic
H
-torsor with flat connection on an algebraic variety
S
and let
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a family of subvarieties of
P
(in the sense of
Definition

1.9
). Then the locus

\mathcal{Y}(f,e):=\{(x,y)\in P\times\mathcal{Y}:\dim_{x}(\mathcal{L}_{x}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{y})\geq e\}

(3.1)

is algebraically constructible.

Above and in the rest of the text, by
\dim_{x}
we denote the local analytic dimension at the point
x
.

Proof.

We begin with some preliminary reductions.

-

By stratifying the base
\mathcal{Y}
, we may assume that
\mathcal{Y}
is smooth, irreducible, and use
[
89
,
Lemma 05F9
]
to ensure all fibres of
f
have the same dimension.

-

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}
be the irreducible components of
\mathcal{Z}
, and write
f_{i}
for the associated families. Then

\mathcal{Y}(f,e)=\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{Y}(f_{i},e)

so it suffices to assume
\mathcal{Z}
is irreducible.

-

Consider the torsor
(Q,\delta)
obtained from
(P,\nabla)
by pulling back along the natural projection
\pi:S\times\mathcal{Y}\to S
. From the definition of the fibre product one obtains an embedding
\mathcal{Z}\subset Q
. Then
\mathcal{Z}\subset Q
is foliated by the intersections
\mathcal{L}_{z}\cap f^{-1}(y)
where
\mathcal{L}_{z}\subset Q
is an analytic leaf passing through
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. The locus
\mathcal{Y}(f,e)
is just the image of

\mathcal{Z}(f,e):=\{z\in\mathcal{Z}:\dim_{z}\mathcal{L}_{z}\cap f^{-1}(f(z))\geq e\},

so it suffices to show the constructibility of
\mathcal{Z}(f,e)
.

Now we argue by induction on
\dim\mathcal{Z}
. Note that we have

\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}(f,0)\supset\mathcal{Z}(f,1)\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{Z}(f,e)\supset\mathcal{Z}(f,e+1)\supset\cdots

so there is some first integer
e
, call it
e_{f}
, such that
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)
is strictly contained in
\mathcal{Z}
. Suppose we can produce some closed algebraic locus
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
containing
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)
and strictly contained in
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f})
. Then because
\mathcal{Z}
is irreducible, this implies
\dim\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}<\dim\mathcal{Z}
. If we then consider the family
f^{\prime}:\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{Y}
obtained by restriction, we will have as a consequence of
\mathcal{Z}\supset\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)
that

\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}(f^{\prime},e):=\{z\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}:\dim_{z}\mathcal{L}_{z}\cap f^{\prime-1}(f^{\prime}(z))\geq e\}=\mathcal{Z}(f,e)

for all
e\geq e_{f}+1
, so replacing
\mathcal{Z}
with
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
and
f
with
f^{\prime}
we will be done by the induction hypothesis on
\dim\mathcal{Z}
.

Now consider the locus

\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)^{1}:=\{z\in\mathcal{Z}:\dim T_{z}\mathcal{L}_{z}\cap T_{z}f^{-1}(f(z))\geq e_{f}+1\}.

Because
\nabla
is algebraic, so is the bundle
\bigcup_{\mathcal{L}\subset P}T\mathcal{L}
, hence the locus
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)^{1}
is defined by algebraically constructible conditions. Let us take
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
to be the Zariski closure of
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)^{1}
; since
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)^{1}
is algebraically constructible, this is the same as the topological closure. We observe that
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
is strictly contained in
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f})
: it suffices to produce an open analytic neighbourhood of
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f})
which does not intersect
\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)^{1}
, which we can do since the locus where the intersections
\mathcal{L}_{z}\cap f(f^{-1}(z))
are smooth of dimension
e_{f}
is open in
z
. But
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{Z}(f,e_{f}+1)
by construction, so this completes the proof.
∎

Once we have an algorithmic way of working with torsors and their connections, the above proof gives an algorithm for computing the loci
\mathcal{Z}(f,e)
and
\mathcal{Y}(f,e)
. This will be the essential input in making our results effective in §
9
.

3.2.1
A priori degree bounds

Although it is not our main focus, let us explain how one can use the above proof to obtain an a priori bound on the degree of the closure of
\mathcal{Y}(f,e)
, and therefore also the closure of its image in
S
. Since in applications one will use such
\mathcal{Y}(f,e)
to construct atypical (weakly) special loci, including atypical orbit closures, we believe this can be of interest in situations where computing the orbit closure itself is computationally difficult but computing the degree estimates is more tractable.

The basic input is the following elementary fact (see for e.g.
[
1
]
):

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
f:\mathbb{A}^{m}\to\mathbb{A}^{m}
be a polynomial map. Suppose
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
, and let
\kappa=\operatorname{max}_{i}\{\deg f_{i}\}
(where

denotes the total degree). Then for an algebraic subvariety

of dimension

, one has
\deg\overline{f(W)}^{\textrm{Zar}}\leq[\deg W]\kappa^{r}
. ∎

If one then runs the argument of

, then this fact can be used to bound the degree of
\overline{\mathcal{Y}(f,e)}^{\textrm{Zar}}
and its image in
S
after choosing explicit coordinates for
S,P,\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{Y}
, etc, and that we have an explicit presentation for
\nabla
as a section of (
2.2
). Indeed, since
\mathcal{Y}(f,e)
is the image of
\mathcal{Z}(f,e)
, this reduces to bounding the degree of
\mathcal{Z}(f,e)
. Then
\mathcal{Z}(f,e)
is constructed as the closure of loci of the form
\mathcal{Z}(f^{\prime},e^{\prime})^{1}
for appropriate
(f^{\prime},e^{\prime})
determined by
(f,e)
. These loci, in turn, are projections of loci in the tangent bundle

defined by explicit equations, so we may bound their degrees as well. The number of such loci needed in the proof can be bounded by bounding the number of steps needed in the Noetherian induction, which can be done by using the degree bound on each
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
appearing in the proof to bound the number of its components as well.

We conclude this section explaining some subtleties of the general strategy outlined in

4.6
Relation with period torsors associated to
\mathbb{V}

The relationship between

and appears also in the work of Bakker-Tsimerman

Let
(\mathbf{H}_{S},D^{0}_{S})
be the weak Hodge datum associated to
\mathbb{V}
. We may fix a basepoint
s_{0}\in S
, and regard this data as being associated to the fibre
\mathbb{V}_{s_{0}}
. Then in particular,

is a variety parameterizing mixed Hodge data at

. Recall that

was constructed as subbundle of the total space of the vector bundle
\sheafhom(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{O}_{S}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}})

is the algebraic bundle underlying

[\eta\in\textrm{Hom}(\mathcal{V}_{s},\mathcal{V}_{s_{0}})]\mapsto\eta(W_{\bullet,s},F^{\bullet}_{s}),

where we use that both the Hodge flag and the filtration by weight are given by algebraic subbundles of

is given by local subsystems to which we can apply the Riemann-Hilbert functor, together with the corresponding result for the Hodge filtration on the pure graded quotients, which is a consequence of work of Schmid

rather than some larger flag variety), it suffices by

To recover

4.7

In our applications of the results of §

Proposition 4.18

Let

be a

Let

be a weakly special subvariety with associated monodromy/Hodge datum
(\mathbf{H}_{Y}\subset\mathbf{G}_{Y},D_{Y})
. Let

be a special subvariety containing

defined by

. Then we can construct a finite cover
e:\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}\to Y^{\textrm{sp}}
and a period map
\Xi:\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}\to\Gamma_{\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}/Y}\backslash D_{\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}/Y}
associated to the quotient datum
(\mathbf{G}_{\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}/Y},D_{\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}/Y})=(\mathbf{G}_{Y},D_{Y})/\mathbf{H}_{Y}
(in the sense of
[
50
, §5]
). From
[
50
, Prop. 5.1 (ii)]
, the irreducible components
\widetilde{Z}
of the fibres of
\Xi
are weakly special subvarieties of
\widetilde{Y}^{\textrm{sp}}
for the period map

, and their images

in

are weakly special subvarieties of

for
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathbb{V}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{Y^{\textrm{sp}}}}
(use that the algebraic monodromy group is unchanged by passing to a finite covering), and in fact weakly special subvarieties of

for
\mathbb{V}
.

Although a priori depending on

, the construction above in fact depends only on the choice of

and a
\mathbb{Q}

Lemma 4.19

Let
e:N\to M
be a quasi-finite map, and

We may assume that

is dominant, and that

and

are irreducible. By stratifying

with respect to the dimension of its fibres, we may assume that all fibres have the same dimension. By removing a closed locus from

, one can further assume that the fibres of

are equidimensional.

We observe that there exists some finite-type family
h:C\to B
of subvarieties of
M
, all of whose fibres are irreducible, and such that all components of varieties of the form
\overline{e(g^{-1}(t))}^{\textrm{Zar}}
occur as fibres. This can be deduced by observing that, with respect to a fixed line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on some projective compactification

. We then argue that the locus

5
Recap on orbit closures

In this section we survey some known results on the orbit closures, expanding the discussion from the beginning of
Section

, and recall how they relate to the map

\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)\to\Omega\mathcal{A}_{g,n}.

We mainly follow
[
97
,
47
]
, but see also
[
99
]
for more background about the ergodic viewpoint.

In the sequel,
\mathcal{M}_{g}
denotes the moduli space of genus
g
compact Riemann surfaces and
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
the bundle whose fibre over
[X]\in\mathcal{M}_{g}
is the space of all (non-zero) holomorphic 1-forms on
X
(in particular it has dimension
4g-3
). It is a rank
g
(algebraic) vector bundle on
\mathcal{M}_{g}
(with the zero section removed), naturally equipped with a stratification by smooth (not necessarily irreducible) algebraic subvarieties
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, where
\kappa=(\kappa_{0},\dots,\kappa_{n})
satisfies
\sum_{i}\kappa_{i}=2g-2
and denotes the multiplicities of zeros of
\omega
. Notice that
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
has dimension
2g+n
, where
n=\textrm{length}(\kappa)-1
. For example the only open stratum is
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(1,...,1)
(where the 1 is repeated
2g-2
times).

Below we use some rudiments of variational (mixed) Hodge theory. We have already discussed such theory in greater generality in §
4
, but here we only deal with variation of Hodge structures (VMHS) whose weight filtration has two steps. In fact in the orbit closure setting all of our period spaces will belong to the theory of mixed Shimura varieties of
Kuga type
; see
[
53
]
for an introduction to this notion.

Remark 5.1
.

In what follows, we often secretly view
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}
and its strata as orbifolds or Deligne-Mumford stacks. To avoid this, one can replace
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}
by a fixed covering over which the pairs
(X,\omega)
glue together into a universal algebraic family and always work just with algebraic varieties and variations of Hodge structure on this covering. This will be harmless for our considerations (the finiteness/abundance of orbit closures in a stratum can equivalently be detected on a finite covering).

5.1
Recollection and first properties

We first recall some basic facts about
relative cohomology
. Let
(X,Z)
be a pair of a Riemann surface (i.e. an irreducible smooth projective algebraic curve over
\mathbb{C}
) and
n+1
points
Z=\{z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}\subset X
(that will usually be given by the zeros of some (non-zero)
1
-form
\omega
on
X
). We have a short exact sequence:

0\to\tilde{H}^{0}(Z;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{1}(X,Z;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{1}(X;\mathbb{Z})\to 0.

(5.1)

Above,
\tilde{H}^{0}(Z;\mathbb{Z})
is the
reduced cohomology
: it denotes the group of all formal linear combinations of points in
Z
, modulo the element which takes each point in
Z
with coefficient
1
(it is the dual of the reduced homology group).
We will always refer to the middle term as
relative cohomology
and the last term as
absolute cohomology
. Note that the form
\omega
naturally induces a class inside both
H^{1}(X,Z(\omega);\mathbb{Z})
and
H^{1}(X;\mathbb{Z})
by integrating over homological cycles and using homology-cohomology duality.

We have a version of (
5.1
) for flat bundles above
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
(see also
[
47
, 3.1.13]
):

0\to W_{0}\to H^{1}_{rel}\to H^{1}\to 0.

(5.2)

The notation is justified by the fact that
W_{0}
denotes the weight-zero local subsystem for the mixed VHS structure on
H^{1}_{rel}
.

The
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action on
H^{1}(X,Z(\omega);\mathbb{C})
is given by identifying
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and considering

H^{1}(X,Z(\omega);\mathbb{C})\cong H^{1}(X,Z(\omega);\mathbb{R})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The action is now the natural
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
at the very end of the equation (it is less straightforward to describe in a more global way the
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
action on the moduli space of translation surfaces).

We now recall the concept of developing map and monodromy. To do so, let
\operatorname{Mod}(X,Z)
be the mapping class group of diffeomorphisms of
X
preserving the subset
Z
. We fix a base point
s:=(X,\omega)\in\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, and look at the developing map/period coordinates on its universal covering

\operatorname{Dev}:\widetilde{\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)}\to H^{1}(X,Z(\omega);\mathbb{C}).

(5.3)

Concretely, the map sends
(X^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
to
\omega^{\prime}\in H^{1}(X^{\prime},Z(\omega^{\prime});\mathbb{C})
and then applies the flat transport to obtain an element of
H^{1}(X,Z(\omega);\mathbb{C})
.
By a theorem of Veech
[
93
, Thm. 7.5]
, locally
\operatorname{Dev}
is biholomorphic. Moreover
\operatorname{Dev}
is equivariant for a representation of

\pi_{1}(\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa))\to\operatorname{Mod}(X,Z),

where the mapping class group acts on cohomology by

L:\operatorname{Mod}(X,Z)\to\operatorname{Sp}(H^{1}(X,Z))\cong\operatorname{Sp}(H^{1}(X))\ltimes\operatorname{Hom}(H^{1}(X),\tilde{H}^{0}(Z;\mathbb{C})).

(5.4)

All monodromy groups presented so far are naturally subgroups of
\operatorname{Aut}(H^{1}_{rel,s})
: this is the group of automorphisms of
H^{1}_{rel,s}
that act as the identity on
W_{0,s}
and act symplectically on
H^{1}_{s}
. If we pick a splitting
H^{1}_{s}\to H^{1}_{rel,s}
, we obtain a semidirect product decomposition
\operatorname{Sp}(H^{1}_{s})\ltimes\textrm{Hom}(H^{1}_{s},W_{0,s})
. Indeed we have

0\to\textrm{Hom}(H^{1}_{s},W_{0,s})\to\operatorname{Aut}(H^{1}_{rel,s})\to\operatorname{Sp}(H^{1}_{s})\to 0.

Recall that an element
\xi\in\textrm{Hom}(H^{1}_{s},W_{0,s})
acts as a unipotent automorphism of
H^{1}_{rel,s}
by
v\mapsto v+\xi(p(v))
(where
p:H^{1}_{rel}\to H^{1}
is the projection to absolute cohomology). All automorphisms of
H^{1}_{rel,s}
that act as the identity on
W_{0,s}
and symplectically on
H^{1}_{s}
are in fact of this form.

The bundle
H^{1}_{rel}
carries naturally the structure of an admissible, graded-polarized, variation of
\mathbb{Z}
-mixed Hodge structures on (each irreducible component of)
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
. The weight filtration on
\mathbb{V}
has two steps (for facts and definitions about mixed VHS, we refer to
Section

4
):

0\to W_{0}(\mathbb{V})\to\mathbb{V}\to\operatorname{Gr}_{1}^{W}(\mathbb{V})\cong p^{*}R^{1}f_{*}\mathbb{Z}\to 0.

(5.5)

Here
p:\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
is just the projection, and
f:\mathcal{C}_{g}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
is the universal family of genus
g
curves. The local system
W_{0}(\mathbb{V})
becomes isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}(0)^{n}
after passing to a finite covering. See also
[
61
, §3.3 and §3.4]
for a related discussion (in
op. cit.
the authors find
n-1
copies of
\mathbb{Z}(0)
since they started with
n
points, whereas we had
n+1
-points; this is coherent with our choice of denoting the multiplicities of the zeros of
\omega
by
(\kappa_{0},\dots,\kappa_{n})
).

Finally, we refer to
[
64
]
(see also
[
47
, 3.1.16]
) for a description of the connected components of
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, that we will implicitly use when speaking of atypical orbit closures.

5.2
Structure of orbit closures

We recall a special case of a theorem of Eskin, Mirzakhani, and Mohammadi
[
44
, Thm. 2.1]
see also
[
47
, Thm. 1]
:

Theorem 5.2

(Linearity/topological rigidity)
.

Each orbit closure
\mathcal{N}\subset\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
is locally in period coordinates a
linear manifold
, i.e. any sufficiently small open
U\subset\mathcal{N}
is such that
\operatorname{Dev}(U)
is an open set inside a linear subspace (see (
5.3
) for the definition of the developing map).

Let
\mathcal{N}\subset\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
be an orbit closure, and denote by
T\mathcal{N}
its tangent bundle. The linearity of orbit closures, as recalled in
Theorem

5.2
, allows one to realize
T\mathcal{N}
as a local subsystem of
H^{1}_{rel}
. As a consequence we have another short exact sequence of real bundles above
\mathcal{N}
:

0\to W_{0}(T\mathcal{N})\to T\mathcal{N}\xrightarrow{p}H^{1}(T\mathcal{N})\to 0.

(5.6)

To explain the notation:
H^{1}(T\mathcal{N})
is just the image of
T\mathcal{N}\subset H^{1}_{rel}
in
H^{1}
(the absolute cohomology), and
W_{0}(T\mathcal{N})=T\mathcal{N}\cap W_{0}
.

Let
T\subset\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
denote the upper triangular group.

Theorem 5.3

(Isolation property,
[
44
, Thm. 2.3]
, see also
[
47
, Thm. 4.1.8.]
)
.

For every sequence of orbit closures
(\mathcal{N}_{i})
there are
T
-invariant measures
\mu_{i}
, and after passing to a subsequence
(\mathcal{N}_{i},\mu_{i})
there is another linear immersed submanifold
\mathcal{M}
, with a finite
T
-invariant measure
\mu
such that
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subset\mathcal{M}
and
\mu_{i}\to\mu
.

Note that the above result implies the same statement with
T
replaced by
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which is how we will use it; see
[
47
, Rem. 4.1.9 (ii)]
.

We can now recall the following theorem of Filip
[
46
]
(which builds on the earlier work
[
45
]
, as well as earlier work of Wright
[
96
]
and Möller
[
73
]
), see also
[
47
, Thm. 2 and Thm. 4.4.2]
. We denote by
K=K_{\mathcal{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
the smallest field over which
\mathcal{N}
is
K
-linear in the sense of
Theorem

5.2
. Let
\mathcal{J}
be the abelian scheme over
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
whose fiber at
(X,\omega)
is the Jacobian of
X
. We have:

Theorem 5.4

(S. Filip)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}\subset\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
be an orbit closure. There exists, up to isogeny, a non-trivial factor
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{J}_{|\mathcal{N}}
and a subgroup
\mathcal{S}
of the free abelian group on the zeros of
1
-forms, such that:

1.

\mathcal{F}
admits real multiplication by an order of the ring of integers of
K
, which is, in particular, a totally real number field
4
4

4

i.e.
K
is equal to the rational endomorphism ring of
\mathcal{F}
over the generic point of
\mathcal{N}
, and see
[
47
, 4.3.18]
for more about
orders
.
;

2.

The Abel-Jacobi map, possibly twisted by real multiplication,
AJ:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{F}
assumes torsion values;

3.

For each
(X,\omega)\in\mathcal{N}
,
\omega
is an eigenform for the real multiplication on the fibre of
\mathcal{F}
above
X
(see also
[
47
, §4.2.17]
).

Furthermore, these conditions, together with a dimension bound (cf.
Remark

5.8
), characterize
\mathcal{N}
.

(See
[
47
, §4.5]
for more details on the
twisted torsion
condition.)

Remark 5.5
.

Notice also that in
[
47
, Thm. 2 and Thm. 4.4.2]
, the factor
\mathcal{F}
is implicitly understood as a
\mathbb{Q}
-
factor
of the associated pure
\mathbb{Z}
VHS. See also
[
73
, Thm. 2.7]
, for a related statement highlighting the possibility of the splitting up to isogeny.

Corollary 5.6
.

Each orbit closure is a closed algebraic subvariety of
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}
. It is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and its Galois conjugates are also orbit closures.

From
Theorem

5.4
, one may introduce
real multiplication, torsion and eigenform
invariants (see
Section

6.1
, for a geometric description). In particular, we write

•

r=r_{\mathcal{N}}
, the
(cylinder) rank

\frac{1}{2}\dim H^{1}(T\mathcal{N})
(which is in fact an integer, since
T\mathcal{N}
is known to be symplectic thanks to
[
4
]
);

•

d=d_{\mathcal{N}}
, to denote the degree of the number field defining the linear equations of
\mathcal{N}
(that is
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
);

•

Finally
t=t_{\mathcal{N}}
the
torsion corank
is
\operatorname{rank}W_{0}(T\mathcal{N})
.

With these notations, the dimension bound appearing at the end of
Theorem

5.4
is just

\dim\mathcal{N}=2r_{\mathcal{N}}+t_{\mathcal{N}}.

Remark 5.7
.

Combining
Corollary

5.6
and
Theorem

5.3
, we observe that the Zariski closure of a collection of orbit closures is a finite union of orbit closures. Cf. the beginning of the proof of
[
47
, Thm. 5.4.5.]
.

Remark 5.8
.

Let us comment on the “dimension bound” appearing in
5.4
(cf.
[
46
, Rem. 5.5]
and the discussion in the proof of
[
46
, Cor. 5.7]
). Any locus
L
satisfying the first three conditions in
5.4
will always have dimension
at most

2r+t
, where
r
and
t
are integers naturally associated to the data. This occurs for the following reasons.

(1)

The third condition in
5.4
implies that, over
L
, the form
\omega
lies in an eigensubspace of
H^{1}_{\textrm{abs}}
which has dimension
2r
, and hence imposes
2g-2r
linear conditions.

(2)

For holomorphic forms over
L
lying in such an eigensubspace, the second condition in
5.4
implies linear conditions on the relative periods (cf.
[
46
, Rem. 1.5]
). As explained in
[
47
, §4.5.4]
, the number of such conditions imposed on periods of
\omega
is
n-t
, where
n
is the number of marked points associated to the ambient stratum
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
.

(3)

The conditions are independent, so since
\textrm{rank}\,H^{1}_{\textrm{rel}}=2g+n
one finds in flat coordinates over
L
that the form
\omega
lies in a subspace of dimension
2r+t
.

(4)

The restriction of the developing map (
5.3
) to
L
is locally injective, so this implies
\dim L\leq 2r+t
.

5.3
Orbit closures and relative dynamical (a)typicality

In this subsection we first recall the definition of (a)typicality appearing in
Theorem

1.2
. Actually, rather than giving the intrinsic definition as in
[
43
,
47
]
in terms of
algebraic hulls
, we give one that captures the essential property of algebraic hull computations which will be relevant to us.

Definition 5.9
.

At each
p=(X,\omega)\in\mathcal{N}
, we have the
tautological 2-dimensional subspace

T(p):=\operatorname{Span}(Re(\omega),Im(\omega))\subset(H^{1}_{rel})_{p}
(and analogously for
H^{1}_{abs}
).

We remark here that the above construction gives a
\mathbf{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant subbundle, which is not flat. See also
[
47
, §5.3.7.]
.

Definition 5.10
.

Given an orbit closure
\mathcal{N}
, the
algebraic hull
for the
\mathbf{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action on
H^{1}_{rel,\mathcal{N}}
is the subgroup of the monodromy group (at a fixed point
p\in\mathcal{M}
)
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{N},\mathbb{R}}
stabilizing the tautological plane
T=T(p)
, introduced in
Definition

5.9
.

The following is implicit in
[
43
, Thm. 1.3]
, and the (a)typical terminology can be found, explicitly, in
[
47
, §5.1.13]
(see also §5.1.16 and Thm. 5.4.5 in
op. cit.
):

Definition 5.11

(Eskin-Filip-Wright)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{M}
be an orbit closure. We say that
\mathcal{N}
is
(dynamically) typical relatively to
\mathcal{M}
if the algebraic hull of
\mathcal{N}
is equal, up to compact factors, to the algebraic hull of
\mathcal{M}
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{N}
is said
(dynamically) atypical relatively to
\mathcal{M}
.

We are finally ready to recall the theorem of Eskin, Filip, and Wright stated in the introduction (to ease the notation, we omit the parenthetical “dynamically”):
See
1.2

Our goal is to introduce an a priori different notion of (a)typicality defined directly from their Hodge-theoretic description, rather than a dynamical description. Even this Hodge-theoretic viewpoint is strongly influenced by Filip’s results described above, and it is essentially taken from
[
47
]
. As we will see below, some extra care is needed when
\mathcal{M}
is different from the ambient stratum of
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}
. (In particular our proof will use the results from
Section

5.2
but not the ones on equidistribution of algebraic hulls from
[
43
]
.)

5.4
Local Systems and Monodromy

We recall several facts about the natural local systems appearing over an orbit closure
\mathcal{M}
. We let
(\mathcal{V},\nabla)
be the algebraic vector bundle with integral connection associated to
\mathbb{V}=H^{1}_{\operatorname{rel}}
. We denote by
\mathcal{V}_{\operatorname{abs}}
the graded quotient of
\mathcal{V}
which corresponds to
H^{1}_{\operatorname{abs}}
. Applying
[
96
, Thm. 1.5]
we have a direct sum decomposition

\mathbb{V}_{\textrm{abs}}=\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{\iota:K_{\mathcal{M}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{U}_{\iota}\right)}_{\mathbb{U}}\oplus\mathbb{W}

(5.7)

of variations of pure Hodge structures
[
45
, Thm. 8.2]
, where
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{W}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-summands and the
\mathbb{U}_{i}
are
\mathbb{R}
-summands. Moreover the summands
\mathbb{U}_{i}
and
\mathbb{U}
are isotypic, again as a consequence of
[
96
, Thm. 1.5]
. Applying the Riemann-Hilbert functor we obtain a corresponding decomposition

\mathcal{V}_{\textrm{abs}}=\underbrace{\left(\bigoplus_{\iota:K_{\mathcal{M}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{U}_{\iota}\right)}_{\mathcal{U}}\oplus\mathcal{W}

of filtered vector bundles with regular singular connections.

As explained in §
5.2
, the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{M}
is naturally a flat subbundle of
\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and by
[
96
, Thm. 1.5]
its projection
p(T\mathcal{M})
to
\mathbb{V}_{\operatorname{abs},\mathbb{C}}
can be identified with one of the
\mathbb{U}_{\operatorname{id},\mathbb{C}}
. The algebraic monodromy group of
T\mathcal{M}
, viewed as a local system, is known to be the full semi-direct product
U\ltimes\operatorname{Sp}(T\mathcal{M}))
, where
U
is a unipotent group isomorphic to
\textrm{Hom}(p(T\mathcal{M}),W_{0}(T\mathcal{M}))
. This is explained in
[
43
, Prop. 4.7]
, and uses Wright’s cylinder deformations
[
96
]
. The same results are also true for the Galois conjugates of
T\mathcal{M}
with respect to the rational structure of
\mathbb{V}
, one learns that the algebraic monodromy groups of each
\mathbb{U}_{\iota}
are the full symplectic groups
\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{U}_{\iota,s_{0}})
, where
s_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is any fixed point. For the case where
\mathcal{M}
is a component of
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)
, these monodromy results can also be deduced from the work of Calderon-Salter
[
28
]
.

6
Finiteness of atypical orbit closures and density of the typical ones

In this section we study orbit closures via various
period maps
. Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{g}
the moduli space of principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension
g
, and by
j
the Torelli morphism
j:\mathcal{M}_{g}\to\mathcal{A}_{g},\ X\mapsto(J(X),\theta_{X})
which associates to a genus
g
curve
X
its Jacobian
J(X)
(naturally equipped with its principal polarization
\theta_{X}
). For every
n
, let
\mathcal{A}_{g,n}\to\mathcal{A}_{g}
denote the fibration whose fibre over a point
[A]\in\mathcal{A}_{g}
is
\operatorname{Sym}^{[n]}A
, the unordered
n
-tuples of points of
A
.

The varieties
\mathcal{A}_{g}
and
\mathcal{A}_{g,n}
can be interpreted as mixed period spaces (or mixed Shimura varieties). For
\mathcal{A}_{g}
this is the standard Siegel space description, and a detailed construction of
\mathcal{A}_{g,1}
as a mixed Shimura variety appears in
[
53
, §2]
. In general one has a natural bijection

\mathcal{A}_{g,n}\simeq\{(H,E):H\in\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}_{g},\hskip 5.0ptE\in\textrm{Ext}^{1}_{\textrm{MHS}}(H,\mathbb{Z}^{n}))\},

where we view
\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}_{g}\simeq\mathcal{A}_{g}
as the moduli space for principally polarized pure weight one
\mathbb{Z}
-Hodge structures, and we view
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as a pure weight zero Hodge structure; see
[
47
, 4.3.14]
. Using (
5.2
) and applying this bijection, one obtains an algebraic mixed period map
\varphi:\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)\to\mathcal{A}_{g,n}
which sends
(X,\omega)
to the isomorphism class of the extension described by (
5.1
). The data of
\varphi
is equivalent to the data of the mixed
\mathbb{Z}
VHS
H^{1}_{\textrm{rel}}
(at least when viewed as a map of orbifolds). Note that
\varphi
is quasi-finite, cf. the discussion at the end of
[
61
, §3.4]
. We will typically work with the factorization

\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g}(\kappa)\xrightarrow{\Omega\varphi}\Omega\mathcal{A}_{g,n}\to\mathcal{A}_{g,n},

of
\varphi
, where the first arrow is obtained by seeing the differential
\omega
on the Jacobian of
X
.

Remark 6.1
.

With the language of (mixed) Shimura varieties one can equivalently describe
\mathcal{A}_{g,n}
as follows. The classifying space for the extensions of a weight one
\mathbb{Z}
VHS of dimension 2g by
\mathbb{Z}(0)
is the mixed Shimura variety

\mathbb{A}_{g}
. It is the universal principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
g
over
\mathcal{A}_{g}
. Finally

\mathcal{A}_{g,n}\cong\mathbb{A}_{g}\times_{\mathcal{A}_{g}}\mathbb{A}_{g}\times_{\mathcal{A}_{g}}\dots\times_{\mathcal{A}_{g}}\mathbb{A}_{g}/S_{n},

product of
n
factors, modulo the action of
S_{n}
(the symmetric group on
n
letters). In fact,
\mathcal{A}_{g,n}
is a
mixed Shimura variety of Kuga type
, see
[
53
, §2]
for more details on this. This means that, with the notation of §
4
,
W_{-2}=0
and
W_{-1}
is the unipotent radical of its generic Mumford–Tate group, which is given by
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{2g\cdot n}
.

Lemma 6.7

The map

-orbit which factors

We recall the construction of the canonical leaf
\mathcal{L}\subset P

\mathbb{V}

-eigenspace

is an orbit of
\mathbf{H}(\mathbb{C})

Now

acts on the absolute cohomology

through its reductive quotient

. Note that the stabilizer of

in

surjects onto the stabilizer of

in

: it suffices to check the corresponding statement on the level of Lie algebras, where the corresponding map is identified with

(cf. the proof following
[
50

), with

is induced by the one on

. To determine this latter stabilizer subgroup of

obtained as the pure quotient of
\mathbb{V}
. From our discussion in §
5.4
, we have subspaces

As explained in §

∎

Given a suborbit closure

We have

, then we have the following inequality of formal codimensions:

The equality

of dimensions is formal. More precisely, the variety

of

Theorem 7.1

(Geometric mixed ZP)
.

There is a finite set
\Sigma=\Sigma_{(S,\mathbb{V})}
of triples
(\mathbf{H},D_{H},\mathbf{N})
, where
(\mathbf{H},D_{H})
is some sub-Hodge datum of the generic Hodge datum
(\mathbf{G}_{S},D_{S})

is a normal subgroup of

such that, up to the action of
\Gamma

is the image of

7.1

Let
(S,\mathbb{V})

\widecheck{{D}}=\widecheck{{D}}_{S}^{0}

\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}_{S}

Lemma 7.2

Consider the set

\mathcal{Y}

\widecheck{{D}}

\mathbf{G}

\mathbf{G}

properly. Let

to

. Moreover by the irreducibility of

) this

: if we had some other point

.

Property (i) holds by construction. More specifically the definition of
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(f,e)
dictates that the intersection

It therefore suffices to show

9
Algorithmic setup

In this section we explain how the determination of the maximal atypical orbit closures (
Theorem

1.6
) and maximal totally geodesic subvarieties of non-arithmetic ball quotients (
Corollary

1.14
) can be made effective. In what follows by the term
algorithm
we always understand
terminating algorithm
.

9.1
Computational models

First we say a word about the computational models we use, following
[
91
, §2]
. We will assume that all fields we work over have computable field operations; for applications it will even suffice to assume all fields are number fields. An affine variety
V
over a field
K
is then a finitely-presented
K
-algebra, which we represent computationally through explicit polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}
in a polynomial ring
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
such that the ideal
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j})
defines the variety of interest. For modelling a more general variety
V
, we use a finite collection
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of affine varieties with
V_{i}=\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptR_{i}\subset\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptK[x_{i1},\ldots,x_{in_{i}}]=:\mathbb{A}^{n_{i}}
, and additionally assume that, for each pair
(i_{1},i_{2})
, we have polynomials
g_{i_{1}i_{2}}
in the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{A}^{n_{i_{1}}}\times\mathbb{A}^{n_{i_{2}}}
which define the intersection of the diagonal
\Delta\subset V\times V
with
V_{i_{1}}\times V_{i_{2}}
. Note that this gives us a natural finite presentation of the intersections
V_{i_{1}}\cap V_{i_{2}}=(V_{i_{1}}\times V_{i_{2}})\cap\Delta
.

We will model
\mathbb{P}^{N}=\textrm{Proj}\,[x_{0},\ldots,x_{N}]
in the usual way with affine opens
\{\{x_{i}\neq 0\}\}_{i=0}^{n}
. A morphism of varieties
f:V\to W
is then represented computationally using:

(i)

covers
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
such that for each
j
, we have
f^{-1}(W_{j})=\bigcup_{i\in I_{j}}V_{i}
for some
I_{j}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
; and

(ii)

morphisms
f_{ij}:V_{i}\to W_{j}
for each
i\in I_{j}
with
I_{j}
as above.

To compose morphisms one in general has to refine the representing covers.

A coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
V
is given by module data compatible with a fixed cover
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In particular, it consists of:

•

a finitely-presented
R_{i}
-module
M_{i}
for each
V_{i}=\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptR_{i}
in the cover;

•

for each intersection
V_{i}\cap V_{j}=\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptR_{ij}
, a finitely presented
R_{ij}
-module
N_{ij}
, together with a restriction morphism
\textrm{res}_{ij}:M_{i}\to N_{ij}
of
R_{i}
-modules;

•

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
a map
\xi_{ij}:N_{ij}\xrightarrow{\sim}N_{ji}
of
R_{ij}
-modules such that the collection
\{\xi_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
defines a gluing datum.

All the morphisms above are encoded using the images of the generators coming from the fixed presentations. In the case where
\mathcal{F}
is a vector bundle of rank
r
we will always assume that the modules
M_{i}
and
N_{ij}
are free of rank
r
and come with an explicit basis. A morphism of coherent sheaves can then be represented via morphisms of the underlying modules with the obvious compatibilities.

Given a model for
V
we have a standard model for
\Omega^{1}_{V}
whose constituent modules
M_{i}
are quotients of the standard differential module
\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{n_{i}}}
by
df_{1},\ldots,df_{j}
, where
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{j})
is the ideal defining
V_{i}
. Given two coherent sheaves
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{V}
modelled as above, we also obtain models for the tensor and hom-sheaf constructions associated to these sheaves; see
[
91
, Prop. 2.1]
.

Given a locally free sheaf
\mathcal{F}
over
V
, there is a natural algebraic vector bundle
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to V
whose sheaf of sections recovers
\mathcal{F}
up to isomorphism. We will wish to use both perspectives in a constructive way, for which we require the following simple fact.

Lemma 9.1
.

The functor
\mathbb{B}:\textrm{LocFree}(V)\to\textrm{VecBun}(V)
from the category of locally free sheaves on
V
to the category of vector bundles on
V
is computable. More precisely, after fixing a computational model
(\{\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptR_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{g_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n})
for
V
as above, there exists

(i)

an algorithm which, given a computational model
(\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{N_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n},\{\xi_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n})
for
\mathcal{F}
compatible with the one for
V
, outputs a model
(\{F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{h_{ij}\}_{i=1}^{n})
for
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})
, a model
\{\pi_{i}:F_{i}\to V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
\pi:\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to V
, and models for the maps
(+),(-),(\cdot)
of bundles corresponding to addition, inversion and scalar multiplication; and

(ii)

an algorithm which, given in addition a computational model

(\{M^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{N^{\prime}_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n},\{\xi^{\prime}_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n})

for
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and a model
(\{s_{i}:M_{i}\to M^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{t_{ij}:N_{ij}\to N^{\prime}_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n})
for a morphism
\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, outputs a model for the map
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
compatible with the models produced by (i).

Proof.

(i)

The standard construction of
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})
as in
[
89
,
Section 01M1
]
associates to the affine open
\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptR_{i}
the symmetric algebra
\textrm{Sym}\,M_{i}
; because
\mathcal{F}
is locally free this can be presented as a polynomial ring
P_{i}
over
R_{i}
whose generators are given by some basis
m_{i1},\ldots,m_{ir}
for
M_{i}
. The diagonal ideal in
\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptP_{i}\times\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptP_{j}
is then generated by the elements
\{g_{ij}\}^{n}_{i,j=1}
and the elements
m_{ik}-\textrm{res}_{ji}^{-1}(\xi_{ij}(\textrm{res}_{ij}(m_{ik}))
for
1\leq k\leq r
, so it suffices to check that we can invert the map
\textrm{res}_{ji}
. This is just a matter of inverting a matrix over
R_{ji}
, which can be done using Cramer’s rule.

That one has a model for the projection is immediate as we may send the fixed generators for
R_{i}
coming from the presentation to the same elements of
P_{i}
.

The maps
(+):\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\times_{S}\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})
,
(-):\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})
and
(\cdot):\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{O}_{S})\times_{S}\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})
are constructed in the natural way; we give just the argument for
(+)
. In this case the construction above associates to
M_{i}\oplus M_{i}
symmetric algebra generated by
\{m^{(1)}_{ik}\}_{k=1}^{r}\sqcup\{m^{(2)}_{ik}\}_{k=1}^{r}
to the affine set
V_{i}
, with an analogous construction for the diagonal ideals. Then the morphism is just defined on generators by fixing the generators of
R_{i}
and the usual map
m_{ik}\mapsto m^{(1)}_{ik}+m^{(2)}_{ik}
.

(ii)

The morphisms of modules
s_{i}
induce natural maps of the associated symmetric algebras and given that our presentation of the modules
\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptP_{i}
(resp.
\textrm{Spec}\hskip 1.49994ptP^{\prime}_{i}
) is relative to a basis for
M_{i}
(resp.
M^{\prime}_{i}
) it is easily seen that this gives a morphism
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
by, for each
i
, fixing the generators of
R_{i}
and acting with the maps
s_{i}
on the basis elements.

∎

Remark 9.2
.

It is less obvious how one computes the inverse of this functor; that is, how given an algebraic map
\pi:\mathcal{B}\to V
and maps
(+),(-)
and
(\cdot)
one computes the associated trivializations. As we will want to use both perspectives in our computations, it will always be important for us that we start with objects on the locally free sheaf side before passing to the bundle picture.

Finally let us mention that we can represent constructible subsets of an algebraic variety
V
as a union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\cap D_{i}
of finitely many intersections between an open and closed subvariety; more details are given in
[
91
, §2]
. Then preimages and images of constructible sets are also computable, as detailed in
[
91
, Prop. 2.1]
.

and

. It then follows from a diagram chase that the row involving

More precisely, one begins with a local section

in the kernel of

which lifts to a section

of

then has a unique lift to the relative cohomology sheaf obtained as the quotient of the first two non-zero entries in the middle column, which from commutativity of the diagram necessarily comes from a section of the relative cohomology sheaf

arises from an element of

What remains to be shown is that we can compute the vector bundle
\mathcal{V}
, its filtered subbundle

, and the map
\delta
. Note that as a consequence of
[
91
, Prop. 2.1]
, direct sums, tensor products, quotients, and pullbacks of finite rank locally free sheaves on algebraic varieties are all computable, in the sense that one can compute explicit models of the resulting locally free sheaves from models of the input data; see
[

, §2.1]
. Thus it is possible to compute all the objects in the diagram (
9.2
) of vector bundles on

. This allows us to construct, as in
[

, Thm. 2.4]

computing the cohomology of the complex

However the terms of the double complex

have infinite rank over

and

the essential difficulty, as in
[

[

, Lem. 2.6]

has rank

rank

has rank

[

, Thm. 2.4]

∎

A basic observation that we will use is the following:

The proof of

Computing weakly specials

Atypical orbit closures

10.2.2

In this section we establish an important fact about the differential conditions that define atypical orbit closures. This will say, roughly, that atypical orbit closures

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to the proof of the following proposition.

Proposition 10.11
.

Let

be an irreducible algebraic subvariety containing an atypical orbit closure

. Suppose that
\mathbf{N}\subset\mathbf{H}_{S}

, construct the locus

consisting of points whose germs agree (upon projecting to
S
) with germs of some fibre of
g_{i}
. (This can be done by replacing the families
f
appearing in loci like
\mathcal{Y}(f,e)
with families like
f\cap g_{i}
, cf. the proof of
3.12
.) Applying
3.13
one will have
\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{G}(g_{i})=\mathcal{Y}(e)\setminus\mathcal{K}(e)
for some finite set
I
, and at this point one terminates with the output
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.
∎

Remark 10.22
.

Analogously to
10.8
, one expects to be able to avoid the search procedure in the proof of
10.21
with more detailed analysis. In the simplest case, when one happens to know that the families
g_{i}
have only a single fibre (this is true in the next example in §
10.4
), one just takes the image of each component of
\mathcal{Y}(e)\setminus\mathcal{K}(e)
in
S
.

10.4
Example: totally geodesic subvarieties of ball quotients

In this section we prove
1.14
announced in the introduction. By
10.21
and
9.3
, it suffices to construct a geometric variation of pure Hodge structures for which the maximal totally geodesic subvarieties are maximal atypical weakly special subvarieties. Essentially what one is doing is making the strategy appearing in
[
18
]
effective.

First of all we recall that in
\operatorname{PU}(1,2)
, by the work of Deligne, Mostow and Deraux, Parker, Paupert, there are 22 known commensurability classes of non-arithmetic lattices (NA from now on). In
\operatorname{PU}(1,3)
only 2 (thanks to Deligne, Mostow and Deraux). For details and precise references we refer for example to
[
18
, §2.5]
. Parker
[
78
]
showed that all the currently known non-arithmetic lattices in
\operatorname{PU}(1,2)
are monodromy groups of higher hypergeometric functions. Here a totally geodesic subvariety is said to be
maximal
if there are no other totally geodesic subvarieties trivially containing it. For
n>3
non-arithmetic lattices are currently not known to exist.

For each non-arithmetic lattice
\Gamma
from
[
38
, Thm. 12.9 and 12.11]
, there is an algebraic family defined over
\mathbb{Q}
of algebraic curves
X
whose monodromy group
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(H^{1}(X_{0},\mathbb{Z}))
which is not of finite covolume in its Zariski-closure. (A conjecture of Simpson
[
86
, Page 9]
predicts that every NA lattice comes from the monodromy of a ‘motivic’ VHS, possibly defined over a number field; this is not known in general, but it is true, by construction, in the DM cases). We recall below more explicitly such families of curves. See also the viewpoint offered in
[
71
]
.

We start with
n+3

weights

\mu=\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=0\dots n+2}
. They are rational numbers such that each
\mu_{i}\in(0,1)
and they add up to
2
. We set
N
to be the least
common denominator of the
\mu_{i}
. We look at the quasi-projective variety over
\mathbb{Q}

Q_{n}=\{x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n+2})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n+3}:x_{j}=x_{k}\text{ iff }k=j\}/Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}),

where
Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1})
acts diagonally and freely.
The Appell-Lauricella hypergeometric functions are solutions
H_{\mu}
of a system of linear partial differential equations in
n
variables
x_{i},i=2,\dots,n+1
with regular singularities along
x_{i}=0,1,\infty
,
x_{i}=x_{j}
(for
i\neq j
). The system
H_{\mu}
has an
n+1
dimensional solution space. We denote its monodromy group by
\Gamma_{\mu}
; it is a subgroup of
\operatorname{PU}(1,n)
.

Over
Q_{n}
, we have a family of smooth projective curves, with singular affine model given by

v^{N}=u^{N\mu_{0}}(u-1)^{N\mu_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n+1}(u-x_{i})^{N\mu_{i}}.

The period map associated to the
\mathbb{Z}
VHS in question is given by the Prym variety of the above family of covers of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Example 10.23
.

The example 15 from
[
38
, §14.3]
, which gives a non-arithmetic lattice
\Gamma
in
\operatorname{PU}(1,2)
, is given by the family of curves (with parameters
x_{2},x_{3}
)

v^{12}=u^{6}(u-1)^{4}(u-x_{2})^{5}(u-x_{3})^{4}.

It is a cyclic covering of order 12 of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Proof of (
1.14
):

Let
\Gamma
be a non-arithmetic lattice in
\operatorname{PU}(1,n)
given as the monodromy of a smooth projective family of varieties (whose corresponding
\mathbb{Z}
VHS is denoted by
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}
). By the results of
[
18
, §5]
, the (complex) totally geodesic subvarieties
S_{\Gamma}=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{B}^{n}
are the weakly special subvarieties for
(S_{\Gamma},\widehat{\mathbb{V}})
and are in fact atypical intersections (that’s how the finiteness of the maximal ones is proved in
op. cit.
). Now using
9.3
to compute the data
(\mathcal{V},F^{\bullet},\nabla)
associated to the smooth projective family, the result follows from
10.21
.
∎
