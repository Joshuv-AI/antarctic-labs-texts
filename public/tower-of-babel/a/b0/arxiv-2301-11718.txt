Sampling without replacement from a high-dimensional finite population
Jiang Hulabel=e1]huj156@nenu.edu.cn, Shaochen Wanglabel=e2]mascwang@scut.edu.cn, Yangchun Zhanglabel=e3]zycstatis@shu.edu.cn, Wang Zhoulabel=e4]stazw@nus.edu.sglabel=u1, url]

Abstract
It is well known that most of the existing theoretical results in statistics are based on the assumption that the sample is generated with replacement from an infinite population. However, in practice, available samples are almost always collected without replacement. If the population is a finite set of real numbers, whether we can still safely use the results from samples drawn without replacement becomes an important problem. In this paper, we focus on the eigenvalues of high-dimensional sample covariance matrices generated without replacement from finite populations. Specifically, we derive the Tracy-Widom laws for their largest eigenvalues and apply these results to parallel analysis. We provide new insight into the permutation methods proposed by Buja and Eyuboglu in [Multivar Behav Res. 27(4) (1992) 509–540]. Simulation and real data studies are conducted to demonstrate our results.

Introduction

Let

{\mathbf{U}}_{p\times N}=({\bm{u}}^{\top}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}^{\top}_{p})^{\top}
be a finite population matrix of dimension
p
and size
N
,
where
{\bm{u}}_{i}=(u_{i1},\ldots,u_{iN})
,
1\leq i\leq p
, is the i-th row of
{\mathbf{U}}_{p\times N}
.
Suppose that for each
i=1,\ldots,p
,
{{\bm{w}}}_{i}=(w_{i1},\ldots,w_{in})
is generated by sampling without replacement from the
N
coordinates of
{\bm{u}}_{i}
with equal probability.
Then, the sample covariance matrix from the finite population is given by

\displaystyle{{\bm{S}}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{W}}_{n}{\bm{W}}_{n}^{\top},

(1.1)

where
{\bm{W}}_{n}=({{\bm{w}}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,{{\bm{w}}}_{p}^{\top})^{\top}
. In this paper, our aim is to derive the asymptotic properties of the eigenvalues of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
in the large dimensional framework, that is, cases where the dimension and sample size proportionally tend to infinity.

Sample covariance matrices have been extensively investigated since proposed by
Wishart 1928
. For fixed sample dimension cases, one can see any multivariate statistical analysis (MSA) textbook (e.g.,
(
Anderson 1984
)
). However, when the dimension of the sample is large or even larger than the sample size, these classical theories may no longer be applicable. Since Marcenko and Pastur discovered the well-known global spectral distribution (MP law) in their seminal work
Marcenko and Pastur 1967
, there has been much literature devoted to the spectral property of the large-dimensional sample covariance matrix and its variants. For the global properties of eigenvalues of sample covariance matrices, see
Bai and Silverstein 2010
, where one can also find many references related to this topic. For the individual eigenvalue, the most interesting
one is the largest eigenvalue, since it accounts for as much of the variability in data
as possible. The limiting distribution of the largest eigenvalue of the large-dimensional sample covariance matrix was first studied and shown to follow the Tracy-Widom (TW) distribution by
Johansson 2000
(complex Gaussian populations)
and
Johnstone 2001
(real Gaussian populations)
under the assumption that all components of the population are independent and
identically distributed
(i.i.d.).
For the follow-up work, see
El Karoui 2007
;
Onatski 2008
;
Bao et al. 2015
;
Lee and Schnelli 2016
.

The coordinates of the population are linear combinations of independent random variables, which is a common condition in the above results. To relax this condition, many researchers have considered more general cases.
For example,
Bai and Zhou 2008
considered the global spectral distribution of the sample covariance matrix without an independence structure in columns,
Bao et al. 2012
and
Pillai and Yin 2012
considered the TW law of the largest eigenvalue of the sample correlation matrix,
Gao et al. 2017
considered the linear spectral statistics of the sample correlation matrix,
Bao 2019
considered the TW law of the largest eigenvalue of the Kendall rank correlation matrix,
Hu et al. 2019
and
Wen et al. 2020
considered the linear spectral statistics and the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix, respectively, in elliptical distributions, and
Tian et al. 2021
considered the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix generated by VARMA.

It is worth noting that the sample covariance matrix from finite population
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
, which is a classical statistic in MSA, is a kind of sample covariance matrix without an independence structure. However, it does not belong to any model presented in the above references. This is because (i) the entries of each row of
{\bm{W}}_{n}
are correlated and (ii) the populations of different rows of
{\bm{W}}_{n}
could be different. Here we introduce Spearman’s rank correlation matrix and the permutation matrix in parallel analysis (PA) as two examples of our matrix model.

(I):

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient is a classical nonparametric measure of correlation between two random variables. Suppose that we have
n
i.i.d. samples
{\bm{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{Y}}_{n}
, where
{\bm{Y}}_{k}
is a
p
-vector consisting of i.i.d. random variables
Y_{1k},\ldots,Y_{pk},k=1,\ldots,n.
Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{p}=\left(\mathfrak{r}_{kl}\right)_{1\leq k,l\leq p},

(1.2)

where
\mathfrak{r}_{kl}
is Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between the
k
-th and
l
-th rows of
\left[{\bm{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{Y}}_{n}\right]
.
Actually,
\mathfrak{R}_{p}
can be expressed in the form of (
1.1
) with
u_{ij}=\frac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{n^{2}-1}}(j-\frac{n+1}{2})
,
i=1,\dots,p
,
j=1,\dots,N
and
n=N
. See
Bai and Zhou 2008
;
Bao et al. 2015
;
Bao 2017
for more details.

(II):

PA is a popular method that is used to select the number
of factors in the factor model. PA is an applicable method because the largest singular value of the permuted data matrix decreases compared with that of the original value if there is indeed at least one factor (see
Dobriban and Owen 2019
;
Dobriban 2020
for further explanation).
Suppose the sample matrix
{\bm{B}}=(b_{ij})_{p\times n}
satisfies the signal-plus-noise model, i.e.,
{\bm{B}}={\bm{S}}+\bm{\Psi}
, where
{\bm{S}}
is a
p\times n
signal matrix of
low rank and
\bm{\Psi}
is a
p\times n
noise matrix.
In this case, the permutation matrix in
Dobriban and Owen 2019
;
Dobriban 2020
turns into
{\bm{W}}
of (
1.1
) with
N=n
.

We highlight two main contributions of the present paper.
First, we theoretically investigate some spectral properties of high-dimensional covariance matrices from finite populations, which have not attracted much attention in modern statistics. Second,
we practically characterize the asymptotic distribution of the largest eigenvalue of the high-dimensional standard permutation matrix in PA, which provides a direct procedure without permutation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the necessary notations and state the main results, which include the global law, the edge universality and the fluctuation of the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
.
In Section
3
, we introduce
some applications of our main results in high-dimensional statistical inference to parallel analysis.
Related simulations and real data analyses are also conducted to demonstrate our results.
Proofs of the main theorems and some preparatory lemmas are given in Section
4
.

2
Notations and statements of main results

2.1
Main results

To easily achieve our goal, we write
{\mathbf{U}}_{p\times N}
as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}_{p\times N}={\bm{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{V}}_{p\times N},

where
{\bm{T}}=\text{{\rm diag}}\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}\}
is a positive definite
p
-dimensional diagonal matrix and
{\mathbf{V}}_{p\times N}=(v_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq p,~1\leq k\leq N}
is a
p\times N
matrix.
{\bm{T}}
is chosen such that the rows of
{\mathbf{V}}_{p\times N}
are standardized. The rows of
{\mathbf{U}}_{p\times N}
and
{\mathbf{V}}_{p\times N}
are denoted as
{\bm{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}_{p}
and
{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{p}
, respectively.
After scaling the observations
{{\bm{y}}}_{i}={{\bm{w}}}_{i}/\sqrt{n},~{{\bm{x}}}_{i}={{\bm{y}}}_{i}/\sqrt{t_{i}},~i=1,\ldots,p
and setting
{\bm{X}}_{n}=({{\bm{x}}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,{{\bm{x}}}_{p}^{\top})^{\top}=(x_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq p,~1\leq k\leq n}
,
{\bm{Y}}_{n}=({{\bm{y}}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,{{\bm{y}}}_{p}^{\top})^{\top}=(y_{jk})_{1\leq j\leq p,~1\leq k\leq n}
, we can rewrite (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle{{\bm{S}}}_{n}={\bm{Y}}_{n}{\bm{Y}}_{n}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bm{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}_{n}{\bm{X}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.1)

Henceforth, we denote
\lambda_{p}({\bm{A}})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{1}({\bm{A}})
as the ordered eigenvalues of
a
p\times p
Hermitian matrix
{\bm{A}}
. The empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of
{\bm{T}}
is

\displaystyle H_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\mathbb{I}}_{\{\lambda_{j}({\bm{T}})\leq\lambda\}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

(2.2)

and that of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
is

\displaystyle F_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\mathbb{I}}_{\{\lambda_{j}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})\leq\lambda\}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Here and throughout the paper,
{\mathbb{I}}_{A}
represents the indicator function of event
A
. The Stieltjes transform of
F_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle m_{n}(z)=\int\frac{1}{x-z}{\rm d}F_{n}(x),

where
z=E+i\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

In addition, we need a crucial parameter
\xi_{+}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{\lambda\xi_{+}}{1-\lambda\xi_{+}}\right)^{2}{\rm d}H_{n}(\lambda)=p/n.

(2.3)

It is easy to check that the solution to (
2.3
) in
[0,1/\lambda_{1}({\bm{T}}))
is unique. See
El Karoui 2007
for more discussion of
\xi_{+}
.
Now, we state our main assumptions as follows.

Assumption 2.1
.

(i)

(On dimensionality) We assume

\displaystyle{c_{n}:=p/n\rightarrow c_{0}\in(0,\infty),~~y_{n}:=n/N\rightarrow y\in[0,1]}

(2.4)

as
n\to\infty
. For simplicity, we assume that there are
two positive constants
c_{1}
,
C_{1}
such that
c_{1}<c_{n}<C_{1}
for all
n\geq 1
.

(ii)

(On
{\bm{T}}
) We assume that
{\bm{T}}
is diagonal with
{\liminf}_{n}\lambda_{p}({\bm{T}})>0
,
{\limsup}_{n}\lambda_{1}({\bm{T}})<\infty
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\lambda_{1}({\bm{T}})\xi_{+}<1,

(2.5)

and there exists a deterministic distribution
H
such that
H_{n}\to H
as
n\to\infty
.

(iii)

(On
{\bm{V}}
) We assume that the elements in
{\mathbf{V}}_{p\times N}
satisfy for each
j=1,\ldots,p
,
\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{jk}=0
,
N^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{jk}^{2}=1
. In addition, for any positive integer
m
, there is a constant

C_{m}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|v_{jk}|^{m}\leq C_{m},\ j=1,\ldots,p.

(2.6)

Remark 2.1
.

We remark that the aim of Assumption (
2.6
) is to make moments of
\sqrt{n}x_{ij}
bounded, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}|\sqrt{n}x_{ij}|^{m}\leq C_{m}
, which justifies the large deviation of the strong MP law and the moments’ comparison procedure. Details can be found in Section
4
.

Our main results on the sample covariance matrix
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
from a finite population are formulated as follows.
First, we present the global law of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,
F_{n}
almost surely converges to a probability distribution
F_{c_{0},H}
, whose Stieltjes transform
m_{c_{0},H}=m_{c_{0},H}(z)
is determined by

\displaystyle m_{c_{0},H}=\int\frac{1}{t(1-c_{0}-c_{0}zm_{c_{0},H})-z}{\rm d}H(t).

(2.7)

We further denote by
{\bm{Q}}:=n^{-1}{\bm{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{X}\mathcal{X}^{\top}{\bm{T}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a
p\times n
data matrix with i.i.d.
N(0,1)
variables. Our main theorem on the edge universality of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
can be formulated as follows, and its proof is deferred to Section
4
. Let

\displaystyle E_{+}=\frac{1}{\xi_{+}}\left(1+c_{n}^{-1}\int\frac{\lambda\xi_{+}}{1-\lambda\xi_{+}}{\rm d}H_{n}(\lambda)\right).

(2.8)

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds, and
N=n
.
Then, there exist positive constants
\varepsilon
and
\delta
such that for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s-n^{-\varepsilon}\right)-n^{-\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})-E_{+})\leq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s+n^{-\varepsilon}\right)+n^{-\delta},

holds for sufficiently large
n
.

One typical application of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
lies in Spearman’s correlation matrix (
1.2
). In this case,
{\bm{T}}
is identity, and it is straightforward to verify that Assumption
2.1
holds. Thus, we have the following corollary, which was first derived in
Bao 2017
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose that
c_{n}:=p/n\rightarrow c_{0}\in(0,\infty)
. Then, the ESD of Spearman’s rank correlation matrix

\mathfrak{R}_{p}
almost surely converges to the standard MP law, and

\displaystyle n^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathfrak{R}_{p})-\underline{E}_{+}}{\underline{\gamma}_{0}}\right)\Rightarrow TW_{1},

where
\underline{E}_{+}=(1+\sqrt{c_{n}})^{2}
and
\underline{\gamma}_{0}=c_{n}^{-\frac{1}{6}}\underline{E}_{+}^{\frac{2}{3}}
. Here and
in the sequel
,
TW_{1}
is the type-1 TW distribution,
``\Rightarrow"
means convergence in distribution.

However, one cannot apply Theorem
2.3
directly in PA because in PA, the data matrix is random, and consequently, the condition
\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{jk}=0
is not satisfied. To overcome this problem, we consider modified versions of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
, which fortunately work for all cases of
n\leq N
.

Now, we consider the general case of
n\leq N
. We first introduce the spatial sign matrix. Let
\bar{{\bm{Y}}}={\rm diag}\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}y_{1l},\dots,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}y_{pl}\}{\bm{e}}_{p}{\bm{e}}_{p}^{\top}
,
{\bm{S}}_{P}=({\bm{Y}}_{n}-\bar{{\bm{Y}}})({\bm{Y}}_{n}-\bar{{\bm{Y}}})^{\top}
and

{\bm{D}}_{2}^{(n)}={\rm diag}\{({\bm{S}}_{P})_{11},\dots,({\bm{S}}_{P})_{pp}\}
. Here
{\bm{e}}_{p}
represents the
p
-dimensional vector with elements all 1. Then, the spatial sign matrix is defined as

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}_{n}=({\bm{D}}_{2}^{(n)})^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{S}}_{P}({\bm{D}}_{2}^{(n)})^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.9)

In addition, we provide an alternative assumption for (iii) of Assumption
2.1
; namely,

(iii’)

Assume that for any positive integer
m
, there is a constant
C_{m}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|v_{jk}|^{m}\leq C_{m},~~~~j=1,\dots,p.

(2.10)

Furthermore, we assume

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big(v_{ij}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{ik}\Big)^{2}\geq c>0,

(2.11)

for some constant
c
.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{jk}=0
and
N^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{jk}^{2}=1
in (iii) are no longer needed because of the standardization
in the spatial-sign matrix. The aim of the new condition (
2.11
) is to bound the moments of
\sqrt{n}\frac{y_{ij}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{ik}}{\sqrt{({\bm{S}}_{P})_{ii}}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\sqrt{n}\frac{y_{ij}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{ik}}{\sqrt{({\bm{S}}_{P})_{ii}}}\Big|^{m}\leq C_{m},

which will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.6
.

Theorem 2.6
.

In addition to (i) and (ii) of Assumption
2.1
, we assume (iii’) holds. We write
{\bm{Q}}_{1}:=\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{X}\mathcal{X}^{\top}
; then, there exist positive constants
\varepsilon
and
\delta
such that for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}}_{1})-\underline{E}_{+})\leq s-n^{-\varepsilon}\right)-n^{-\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{P}}_{n})-\underline{E}_{+})\leq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}}_{1})-\underline{E}_{+})\leq s+n^{-\varepsilon}\right)+n^{-\delta}

holds for sufficiently large
n
. Here,
\underline{E}_{+}
is defined in Corollary
2.4
.

Remark 2.7
.

The above results can be generalized to the joint distribution of the largest several eigenvalues. More specifically, under the assumptions in Theorem
2.3
, there exist positive constants
\varepsilon
and
\delta
such that for any
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s_{1}-n^{-\varepsilon},\ldots,n^{2/3}(\lambda_{k}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s_{k}-n^{-\varepsilon}\right)-n^{-\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})-E_{+})\leq s_{1},\ldots,n^{2/3}(\lambda_{k}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})-E_{+})\leq s_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s_{1}+n^{-\varepsilon},\ldots,n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s_{k}+n^{-\varepsilon}\right)+n^{-\delta}

holds for sufficiently large
n
. Correspondingly,
\lambda_{1}({\bm{P}}_{n}),\ldots,\lambda_{k}({\bm{P}}_{n})
have analogous properties under the conditions of Theorem
2.6
.

From Theorems
2.3
and
2.6
, we have the following corollary on the largest eigenvalues of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n},{\bm{P}}_{n}
.

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds. Denoting

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{3}=\frac{1}{\xi_{+}^{3}}\left(1+c_{n}^{-1}\int\left(\frac{\lambda\xi_{+}}{1-\lambda\xi_{+}}\right)^{3}{\rm d}H_{n}(\lambda)\right),

(2.12)

we have

\displaystyle n^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})-E_{+}}{\gamma_{0}}\right)\Rightarrow TW_{1}.

Additionally, under the same assumptions in Theorem
2.6
, we have

\displaystyle n^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}({\bm{P}}_{n})-\underline{E}_{+}}{\underline{\gamma}_{0}}\right)\Rightarrow TW_{1}.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is deferred to Section
4
. The proofs of Theorem
2.6
and Corollary
2.8
are deferred to Section A.3 of Supplementary Material (
Hu et al. 2022
).

2.2
Basic notions

What truly pertains to our discussion in the sequel is the nonasymptotic version of
F_{c_{0},H}
(i.e.,
F_{c_{n},H_{n}}
), which can be obtained by replacing
c_{0}
and
H
with
c_{n}
and
H_{n}
in
F_{c_{0},H}
, respectively. More precisely,
F_{c_{n},H_{n}}
is the corresponding distribution function of the Stieltjes transform
m_{c_{n},H_{n}}(z):=m_{c_{n},H_{n}}\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
satisfying the following self-consistent equation:

\displaystyle m_{c_{n},H_{n}}(z)=\int\frac{1}{t(1-c_{n}-c_{n}zm_{c_{n},H_{n}}(z))-z}{\rm d}H_{n}(t).

(2.13)

Define an
n\times n
matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}={\bm{Y}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{Y}}_{n},

(2.14)

which shares the same nonzero eigenvalues with
{\bm{S}}_{n}
. Denoting the ESD of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
as
\underline{F}_{n}
, we have

\underline{F}_{n}=c_{n}F_{n}+(1-c_{n})\mathbb{I}_{[0,\infty)},

which converges almost surely to

\displaystyle\underline{F}_{c_{0},H}=c_{0}F_{c_{0},H}+(1-c_{0})\mathbb{I}_{[0,\infty)}.

(2.15)

Then, the n-dependent version of (
2.15
) is

\displaystyle\underline{F}_{c_{n},H_{n}}=c_{n}F_{c_{n},H_{n}}+(1-c_{n})\mathbb{I}_{[0,\infty)}.

(2.16)

We also obtain

\displaystyle\underline{m}_{c_{n},H_{n}}=c_{n}m_{c_{n},H_{n}}-\frac{1-c_{n}}{z},

(2.17)

which satisfies the following self-consistent equation:

\displaystyle z=-\frac{1}{\underline{m}_{c_{n},H_{n}}}+c_{n}\int\frac{t}{1+t\underline{m}_{c_{n},H_{n}}}\text{d}H_{n}(t).

(2.18)

For simplicity, we briefly use the notation

\displaystyle\tilde{m}(z):=m_{c_{n},H_{n}}(z),~~\tilde{F}:=F_{c_{n},H_{n}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{m}}(z):=\underline{m}_{c_{n},H_{n}}(z),~~\underline{\tilde{F}}:=\underline{F}_{c_{n},H_{n}}.

From the discussions in
Silverstein and Choi 1995
, we know that
\underline{F}_{c_{n},H_{n}}
has a continuous derivative
\underline{\rho}_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. The inequality (
2.5
) guarantees that the density
\underline{\rho}_{0}
exhibits
a square-root-type behavior at the rightmost endpoint of its support; see Theorem 3.1 in
Bao et al. 2015
.
The rightmost boundary of the support of
\underline{\rho}_{0}
is
E_{+}
defined in (
2.8
), that is,
E_{+}=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\underline{\tilde{F}}(x)=1\}
. Moreover, the parameter
\xi_{+}
defined by (
2.3
) satisfies
\xi_{+}=-\lim_{\eta\to 0}\tilde{\underline{m}}(E_{+}+i\eta)=-\tilde{\underline{m}}(E_{+})
.

3
Applications and numerical studies

3.1
High-dimensional PA

As Spearman’s rank correlation matrix has been discussed in the unpublished paper by
Bao 2017
, in this section, we only focus on the high-dimensional PA. Recall the signal-plus-noise matrix
{\bm{B}}={\bm{S}}+\bm{\Psi}
, and recall that

\bm{\Psi}
has the following structure:

\bm{\Psi}=\tilde{{\bm{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Phi},

where
\tilde{{\bm{T}}}={\rm diag}\{\tilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{t}_{p}\}
is a diagonal matrix with
\|\tilde{{\bm{T}}}\|
and
\|\tilde{{\bm{T}}}^{-1}\|
bounded in
p
,
\bm{\Phi}
is a
p\times n
collection of i.i.d. noise
\phi_{ij}
satisfying

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\phi_{ij}=0,~~{\mathbb{E}}\phi_{ij}^{2}=1.

(3.1)

Moreover, we assume that every
\phi_{ij}
has a uniform subexponential decay, that is, there exists a constant
\vartheta>0
such that for
u>1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\phi_{ij}|>u)\leq\vartheta^{-1}\exp(-u^{\vartheta}).

(3.2)

Denote
\bm{S}=(s_{ij})_{p\times n}
, and assume that for any positive integer
m
, there exists a constant

C_{m}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|s_{ij}|^{m}\leq C_{m},~~1\leq i\leq p,~1\leq j\leq n.

(3.3)

Recall the standard factor model
{\bm{B}}=\bm{H}\bm{\Lambda}^{\top}+\bm{\Psi}
, where

\bm{H}
is the
p\times r
(
r
is fixed) matrix containing the factor values
h_{ij}
and
\bm{\Lambda}
is the

n\times r
factor loading matrix with entries
\lambda_{jk}
.
The first term is the signal due to the factor component, whose rank is at most
r
. Thus, the factor model falls into
the signal-plus-noise framework.

We start with a
p\times n
data matrix
{\bm{B}}
with measurements
given by
b_{ij}
,
i=1,\ldots,p,~j=1,\ldots,n
.
We generate a matrix
{\bm{B}}_{\pi}
by separately permuting the entries
in each row of
{\bm{B}}
. Here
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{p})
is a permutation

4
Technical proofs

4.1
Proof strategy

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is completed with the aid of a general strategy presented in
Knowles and Yin 2017
for the covariance type matrices, which itself is an adaptation of the method originally described in
Erdös et al. 2012
for Wigner matrices. Generally, to prove the TW law for the largest eigenvalue, first, one needs to prove a local law for the spectral distribution, which controls the location of the eigenvalues on an optimal local scale. Second, with the aid of the local law, one needs to perform Green function comparison between the matrix of interest and a certain reference matrix ensemble, whose edge spectral behavior is already known. In
Erdös et al. 2012
, an extended criterion of the local law for the covariance type of matrices with independent columns (or rows) was given, see Theorem 3.6 in
Pillai and Yin 2014
. It allows one to relax the independence assumption on the entries within columns (or rows) to a certain extent, as long as some large deviation estimates
hold for certain linear and quadratic forms of each column (or row) of the data matrix; see Lemma 3.4 in
Pillai and Yin 2014
.

Recall the sample covariance matrix
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
defined in (
1.1
). Our main task is to show a large deviation estimate for each row of
{\bm{X}}_{n}
. Once the large deviation estimate is established, we can use the criterion in Proposition 5.1 in
Wen et al. 2020
to conclude the weak local law of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
and Lemma 5.6 in
Knowles and Yin 2017
for strong local law. In fact, Green function comparison can be completed similarly to the approach in
Erdös et al. 2012
by choosing an appropriate reference matrix ensemble. The reference matrix that is chosen in this paper is the traditional unbiased estimator of the population covariance matrix.

4.2
Notations

For two positive quantities
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
that depend on
n
, we use the notation
A_{n}\asymp B_{n}
to mean that
C^{-1}A_{n}\leq B_{n}\leq CA_{n}
for some positive constant
C>1
. We need the following probability comparison definition from
Erdös et al. 2013
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\equiv\mathcal{X}^{(N)}
and
\mathcal{Y}\equiv\mathcal{Y}^{(N)}
be two sequences of nonnegative random variables. We say that
\mathcal{Y}
stochastically dominates
\mathcal{X}
if, for all (small)
\epsilon>0
and (large)
D>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{(N)}>N^{\epsilon}\mathcal{Y}^{(N)})\leq N^{-D},

(4.1)

for sufficiently large
N>N_{0}(\epsilon,D)
, and we write
\mathcal{X}\prec\mathcal{Y}
or
\mathcal{X}=O_{\prec}(\mathcal{Y})
. When
\mathcal{X}^{(N)}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{(N)}
depend on a parameter
v\in\mathcal{V}
(typically an index label or a spectral parameter), then
\mathcal{X}\prec\mathcal{Y}
uniformly in
v\in\mathcal{V}
, which means that the threshold
N_{0}(\epsilon,D)
can be chosen independently of
v
.
We use the symbols
O(\cdot)
and
o(\cdot)
for the standard big-O and little-o notations, respectively. We use
c
and
C
to denote strictly positive constants that do not depend on
N
. Their values may change from line to line. For any matrix
{\bm{A}}
, we denote
\|{\bm{A}}\|
as its operator norm, while for any vector
\bm{v}
, we use
\|\bm{v}\|
to denote its
L_{2}
-norm. In addition, we use
{\bm{a}}_{i}
to represent the
i
-th row of matrix
{\bm{A}}
.

Proof of Theorem

When

is asymptotically given by the standard MP law. For general

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}w_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=nt_{i}\mathbb{E}x_{ij}^{2}=t_{i},~~\mathbb{E}w_{i1}w_{i2}=nt_{i}\mathbb{E}x_{i1}x_{i2}=-\frac{t_{i}}{N-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}w_{i1}^{2}w_{i2}w_{i3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(N-1)(N-2)}\sum_{1\leq s\neq t\neq l\leq N}u_{is}^{2}u_{it}u_{il}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(N-1)(N-2)}(\sum_{s=1}^{N}u_{is}^{4}/N)-\frac{N}{(N-1)(N-2)}t_{i}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}w_{i1}w_{i2}w_{i3}w_{i4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}\sum_{1\leq s\neq t\neq l\neq m\leq N}u_{is}u_{it}u_{il}u_{im}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}(-6(\sum_{s=1}^{N}u_{is}^{4}/N)+3Nt_{i}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(w_{i1}^{2}-\mathbb{E}w_{i1}^{2})(w_{i2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}w_{i2}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}(t_{i}^{2}-\sum_{s=1}^{N}u_{is}^{4}/N).

Therefore, (

4.4

In this section, our goal is to prove a strong

, which are rows of the matrix

We now introduce a lemma on the stochastic domination of high-order moments.

Lemma 4.1
.

(Lemma 7.1 in
Benaych-Georges and Knowles 2016
)
Suppose that the deterministic control parameter
\Phi
satisfies
\Phi\geq n^{-C}
for some constant
C>0
and that for all

, there is a constant
C_{p}
such that the nonnegative random variable
X
satisfies
\mathbb{E}X^{p}\leq n^{C_{p}}
. Then, we have the following equivalence

X\prec\Phi\Longleftrightarrow\mathbb{E}X^{n}\prec\Phi^{n}

for any fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}.

For simplicity, we set
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{v}}_{i}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
and
{\bm{x}}_{i}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
as the
i
-th row of

and

.

4.4.1
Large deviation estimates

Lemma 4.2
.

(Large deviation lemma).
For any deterministic vector
{\bm{A}}={A_{i}}\in\mathbb{C}_{n}
and deterministic matrix
{\bm{B}}={B_{ij}}\in\mathbb{C}_{n\times n}
, we have

\displaystyle|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}A_{i}|\prec(\|{\bm{A}}\|^{2}/n)^{1/2},

(4.2)

\displaystyle|\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{ii}x_{i}^{2}-n^{-1}{\rm Tr}{\bm{B}}|\prec n^{-1}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|B_{ii}|^{2})^{1/2},

(4.3)

\displaystyle|\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}x_{i}B_{ij}x_{j}+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}B_{ij}|\prec n^{-1}(\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}.

(4.4)

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

The proof of Lemma
4.2
is very similar to the proof of Proposition 2.1 in
Bao 2017
.

By Chebyshev’s inequality

\mathbb{P}(|x_{i}|\geq n^{\epsilon}n^{-\frac{1}{2}})\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{|\sqrt{n}x_{i}|^{q}}{n^{q\epsilon}},

and (
2.10
), we have
|x_{i}|=O_{\prec}(n^{-1/2})
.

For (
4.2
), we first construct a sequence of martingale differences. Define the filtration

\mathcal{F}_{0}=\varnothing,~~~\mathcal{F}_{l}:=\sigma\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}\},~~~l\in[1,n],

and set

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}[\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l})-\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l-1})].

(4.5)

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{l}
,
x_{i}
is uniformly distributed for all
x_{i}\in\{x_{l+1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
by sampling without replacement. Hence, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l})=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}x_{i})/(n-l)=-\sum_{i=1}^{l}x_{i}/(n-l),~~~j\geq l+1,

(4.6)

where we used the fact that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}=0
.
Furthermore, because
\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l})=x_{j},~j\in[1,l]
and
\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l-1})=x_{j},~j\in[1,l-1]
,
\mathcal{M}_{l}
can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{l}=x_{l}A_{l}+\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}A_{j}\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l})-\sum_{j=l}^{n}A_{j}\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{l}x_{l}-x_{l}\frac{\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}A_{j}}{n-l}+A_{l}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}x_{k}}{n-l+1}+\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}A_{j}\frac{-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}x_{k}}{n-l}-\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}A_{j}\frac{-\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}x_{k}}{n-l+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A_{l}-\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}A_{j})(x_{l}+\frac{1}{n-l+1}\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}x_{k}).

(4.7)

Using the bound
|x_{k}|=O_{\prec}(n^{-1/2})
and the fact that

\displaystyle|\sum_{k=1}^{l-1}x_{k}|\prec n^{-1/2}\min(l-1,n-l+1),

(4.8)

we deduce that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}_{l}|\prec n^{-1/2}(|A_{l}|+\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|A_{j}|).

(4.9)

By the Burkholder inequality, we derive that for any fixed positive integer
q>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{l}|^{q}\leq K_{q}\mathbb{E}(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{l}^{2})^{q/2},

(4.10)

where

is finite depending on

. Now, we estimate

. According to (
4.9
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{l}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{M}_{l}^{2}\prec n^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}[\frac{1}{(n-l)^{2}}(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|A_{j}|)^{2}+|A_{l}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}[\frac{1}{(n-l)^{2}}(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|A_{j}|)^{2}+|A_{l}|^{2}]\prec n^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|A_{j}|^{2}+|A_{l}|^{2})

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle n^{-1}\log n\|A\|^{2}\prec n^{-1}\|A\|^{2}.

(4.11)

Plugging (
4.4.1
) into (
4.10
), using Lemma
4.1
and Markov’s inequality, we can find (
4.2
).

For (
4.3

\mathcal{N}_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{jj}[\mathbb{E}(x_{j}^{2}|F_{l})-\mathbb{E}(x_{j}^{2}|F_{l-1})].

Applying (

The remaining proof of (

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l1}|^{q}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}B_{il}x_{i}|^{q}|x_{l}-\mathbb{E}(x_{l}|\mathcal{F}_{l-1})|^{q})

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle n^{-q/2}\mathbb{E}|\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}B_{il}x_{i}|^{q}\prec n^{-q}(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{il}|^{2})^{q/2},

(4.24)

which is due to (
4.2
) and
x_{i}=O_{\prec}(n^{-1/2})
.

For
\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l2}|^{q}
, from (
4.6
) and (
4.8
), when
l\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l})-\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l-1})|=\frac{1}{n-l}|x_{l}+\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}x_{j}}{n-l+1}|\prec\frac{1}{(n-l)\sqrt{n}}.

(4.25)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{l2}|\prec

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-l)\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}B_{ij}x_{i}|\prec\frac{1}{(n-l)n}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n-l}}{(n-l)n}(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}\prec\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n-l}}(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l2}|^{q}\prec n^{-q}(n-l)^{-q/2}(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{q/2}.

(4.26)

Next, we estimate
\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l3}|^{q}
. Obviously, according to (
4.17
) and (
4.21
),

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{l3}|\prec\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|B_{lj}|/(n\sqrt{n-l})\prec(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|B_{lj}|^{2})^{1/2}/n,

In other words,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l3}|^{q}\prec(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|B_{lj}|^{2})^{q/2}/n^{q}.

(4.27)

Now, we estimate
\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l4}|^{q}
. From (
4.17
), (
4.4.1
) and (
4.20
), we derive that for
i,j>l
,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}(x_{i}x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l})-\mathbb{E}(x_{i}x_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{l-1})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\frac{1}{(n-l-1)(n-l)(n-l+1)}(\{(\sum_{i\leq l-1}x_{i})^{2}-(1-\sum_{i\leq l-1}x_{i}^{2})\})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{(n-l)(n-l-1)}x_{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}x_{j}|\prec\frac{1}{n(n-l)^{3/2}}.

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{l4}\prec\frac{1}{n(n-l)^{3/2}}\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}|B_{ij}|\prec\frac{1}{n(n-l)^{1/2}}(\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}.

(4.28)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l4}|^{q}\prec\frac{1}{n^{q}(n-l)^{q/2}}(\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{q/2}.

(4.29)

Finally, from (
4.4.1
), (
4.26
), (
4.27
) and (
4.29
), we conclude that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l}|^{q})^{2/q}=

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}(|\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\mathcal{R}_{l\alpha}|^{2})^{q/2})^{2/q}\leq 4(\mathbb{E}(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}\mathcal{R}_{l\alpha}^{2})^{q/2})^{2/q}\leq 4\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4}(\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{R}_{l\alpha}^{q}|)^{2/q}

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle n^{-2}(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{il}|^{2}+\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|B_{lj}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{ij}|^{2}+\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{i=l+1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}|B_{ij}|^{2})

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle n^{-2}(\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}|B_{il}|^{2}+\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}|B_{lj}|^{2}+\frac{1}{n-l}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}|B_{ij}|^{2}).

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{j=l+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{n-l}|B_{ij}|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{n-l})|B_{ij}|^{2}\leq C\log n(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1})|B_{ij}|^{2}\prec\sum_{i<j}|B_{ij}|^{2},

we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}_{l}|^{q}\prec n^{-q}(\sum_{i<j}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{q/2}.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle|\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}B_{ij}x_{j}-\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}B_{ij}x_{j})|\prec n^{-1}(\sum_{i\neq j}|B_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}.

(4.30)

By the fact that
\mathbb{E}x_{i}x_{j}=-\frac{1}{n(n-1)},~i\neq j
, Lemma
4.2
is concluded.
∎

4.5
Edge universality for
S_{n}

In this section, we prove the edge universality of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
by the Green function comparison.

Recall the intermediate matrices defined in (
4.36
) and the notation introduced in (
4.37
). We aim to show the following
lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix any
\gamma\in[1,\ldots,p]
, and choose
\epsilon
as any sufficiently small constant. Let
E,E_{1},E_{2}\in R
satisfy
E_{1}<E_{2}
and

\displaystyle|E-E_{+}|,~|E_{1}-E_{+}|,~|E_{2}-E_{+}|\leq n^{-\frac{2}{3}+\epsilon},

(4.46)

and set
\eta_{0}=n^{-\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon}
. Define
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as a smooth function satisfying

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F^{(l)}(x)|(|x|+1)^{-C}\leq C,~~~l=1,2,3,4

for some positive constant
C
. Then, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}F(n\eta_{0}{\rm Im}m_{\gamma}(z))-\mathbb{E}F(n\eta_{0}{\rm Im}m_{\gamma+1}(z))|\prec n^{-1-\delta},~~~~~~~z=E+i\eta_{0},

(4.47)

and

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}F(n\int_{E_{1}}^{E_{2}}{\rm Im}m_{\gamma}(x+i\eta_{0}){\rm d}x)-\mathbb{E}F(n\int_{E_{1}}^{E_{2}}{\rm Im}m_{\gamma+1}(x+i\eta_{0}){\rm d}x)|\prec n^{-1-\delta}.

(4.48)

The proof of Lemma
4.5
can be found in Section A.2 of the Supplementary Material (
Hu et al. 2022
). Using Lemma
4.5
, we can now prove Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Since the proof of Theorem
2.3
is similar to that of Theorem 3.3 in
Wen et al. 2020
, we only outline the general strategy here. First, we attempt to convert the problem to a comparison of the Stieltjes transform. As a result,
for any
\eta>0
, we define

\vartheta_{\eta}(x)=\frac{\eta}{\pi(x^{2}+\eta^{2})}=\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{x-i\eta}.

We notice that for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\leq b
, the convolution
of
\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}
and
\vartheta_{\eta}
applied to the eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,p
yields that

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}*\vartheta_{\eta}(\lambda_{i})=\frac{p}{\pi}\int_{a}^{b}\operatorname{Im}m_{n}(x+i\eta){\rm d}x.

In terms of functional calculus notation, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(\lambda_{i})={\rm Tr}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}({{\bm{S}}}_{n}),\quad\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}*\vartheta_{\eta}(\lambda_{i})={\rm Tr}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}*\vartheta_{\eta}({{\bm{S}}}_{n}).

For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
, define
{\cal N}(a,b)=\#(a\leq\lambda_{j}\leq b)
as the number of eigenvalues of
{{\bm{S}}}_{n}
in
[a,b]
. Then, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

(Lemma 4.1 in
Pillai and Yin 2014
)
Let
\varepsilon>0

be an arbitrarily small number. Set
E_{\varepsilon}=E_{+}+n^{-2/3+\varepsilon}
,

\ell_{1}=n^{-2/3-3\varepsilon}
and
\eta_{1}=n^{-2/3-9\varepsilon}
.
Then, for any
E
satisfying
|E-E_{+}|\leq\frac{3}{2}n^{-2/3+\varepsilon}
, it holds with high probability that

|{\rm Tr}\mathbf{1}_{[E,E_{\varepsilon}]}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})-{\rm Tr}\mathbf{1}_{[E,E_{\varepsilon}]}*\vartheta_{\eta}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})|\leq C(n^{-2\varepsilon}+{\cal N}(E-\ell_{1},E+\ell_{1})).

Lemma
4.6
shows that
{\rm Tr}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})
can be well approximated by its smoothed version
{\rm Tr}\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}*\vartheta_{\eta}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})
for
a,b
around edge
E_{+}
so that the problem can be converted to a comparison of the Stieltjes transform.

Then, from Lemma
4.5
, Lemma
4.6
, the square root behavior of
\underline{\rho}_{0}
(see Section
2.2
), and the rigidity on the right edge (see Lemma
4.4
), one can see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}(\tilde{{\bm{S}}})-E_{+})\leq s-n^{-\varepsilon}\right)-n^{-\delta}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({{\bm{S}}}_{n})-E_{+})\leq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}(\tilde{{\bm{S}}})-E_{+})\leq s+n^{-\varepsilon}\right)+n^{-\delta},

(4.49)

where
\tilde{{\bm{S}}}
is defined in (

It is known from the eigenvalue sticking result (Theorem 2.7 of
Bloemendal et al. 2016

and Section 11 in

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s-n^{-\varepsilon}\right)-n^{-\delta}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}(\tilde{{\bm{S}}})-E_{+})\leq s\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2/3}(\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}})-E_{+})\leq s+n^{-\varepsilon}\right)+n^{-\delta},

(4.50)

where

5
Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we focus on the eigenvalues of high-dimensional sample covariance matrices generated without replacement from finite populations. The largest eigenvalues are proven to follow the TW law. Based on the permutation method introduced by
Dobriban 2020
, we consider an analogous strategy to compare the top singular
values of
{\bm{B}}
and a certain percentile of the top singular values of its permuted matrices after centralization, where the latter can be attributed to our theoretical results.

Technically, for the PA, one might be more interested in the asymptotic distribution of
\lambda_{1}(\frac{1}{n}({\bm{B}})_{\pi}({\bm{B}})_{\pi}^{\top})
, that is the largest eigenvalue of permuted sample covariance matrices without centralization. We believe that it would still tend to the TW law as
n,~p\to\infty
by adding some more restrictions on
{\bm{S}}
. However, it requires more technical work, thus we decide to leave it for future work. Besides,
Dobriban and Owen 2019
proposed derandomized PA (DPA), deflated DPA (DDPA) and DDPA+ methods, which
are faster and more reproducibly than the PA method.
Essentially, our theoretical results can explain why the DPA method is a kind of parallel analysis from another perspective. For DDPA and DDPA+ methods, both counter the shadowing problem of DPA by removing some largest factors one by one. We think that the idea of removing the poorly estimated singular vectors can also improve the accuracy of the quantiles of the TW distribution in practice. We will also leave the rigorous discussion of this property for future work.
