Higher Braidings of Diagonal Type
Michael CUNTZ and Tobias OHRMANN

Abstract
Heckenberger introduced the Weyl groupoid of a finite-dimensional Nichols algebra of diagonal type. We replace the matrix of its braiding by a higher tensor and present a construction which yields further Weyl groupoids. Abelian cohomology theory gives evidence for the existence of a higher braiding associated to such a tensor.

Introduction

To a braided vector space
(V,c)
, i.e., a vector space
V
and a linear isomorphism

c\colon\ V\otimes V\rightarrow V\otimes V\qquad\text{with}\quad(c\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes c)(c\otimes\mathrm{id})=(\mathrm{id}\otimes c)(c\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes c)

one can associate a Hopf algebra
\mathfrak{B}(V)=T(V)/\mathfrak{I}(V)
, where the ideal
\mathfrak{I}(V)
is the kernel of the symmetrizer of the braiding. This algebra
\mathfrak{B}(V)
is called the Nichols algebra of
(V,c)
. For example, given a generalized Cartan matrix
A
of finite type and a parameter
q\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, it is well-known that the positive part of the Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum enveloping algebra

U_{q}(A)
is a Nichols algebra. This is a special case of a Nichols algebra of diagonal type.

The classification of finite-dimensional Nichols algebras of diagonal type
[
10
]
was achieved using certain root systems. In classical Lie theory, these are orbits under the action of a Weyl group. For Nichols algebras, Heckenberger
[
9
]
noticed that one has to consider Weyl groupoids, i.e., categories whose morphisms are compositions of reflections acting on a lattice.

Unfortunately, associating a Weyl groupoid to a Nichols algebra appears to be neither a surjective nor an injective map (up to isomorphisms). At least in the case when the braiding defining the Nichols algebra is of diagonal type, one does not obtain all finite Weyl groupoids as classified in
[
4
]
. To realize a much larger class of Weyl groupoids, Cuntz and Lentner
[
5
]
constructed Nichols algebras associated to restrictions of crystallographic arrangements. However, these are almost never Nichols algebras of Yetter–Drinfeld modules of finite groups. Moreover, we do not expect to obtain too many further Weyl groupoids of rank two in this way: in rank two, there are infinitely many finite Weyl groupoids, but only a very small finite set is associated to Nichols algebras of diagonal type. Restrictions of Weyl arrangements won’t produce enough algebras using the construction by Cuntz and Lentner.

A braiding
c
of diagonal type is completely determined by a matrix
{\bf q}=(q_{ij})
(when
c(x_{i}\otimes x_{j})=q_{ij}x_{j}\otimes x_{i}
for a basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
). To understand the action of a reflection
\sigma
on a Nichols algebra, it is convenient to consider the bicharacter
\chi
associated to
{\bf q}
. The map
\sigma
acts on the exponent vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of the generators of the Nichols algebra. But to obtain the new Nichols algebra
\sigma(\mathfrak{B}(V,c))
, one has to apply
\sigma
on
\chi
. In an additive notation, this turns out to be the action of
\sigma\otimes\sigma
on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, see Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

Conversely, given a Weyl groupoid
\mathcal{W}
, the tensor squares of its reflections define the variety of braidings
c
such that
\mathcal{W}
is the Weyl groupoid of
\mathfrak{B}(V,c)
. In additive notation it is very natural to generalize this machinery to higher tensor powers of reflections.
For example, fourth tensor powers would produce a variety of “tensors”
{\bf q}=(q_{i,j,k,l})
.

To compute this variety, we need a rule to determine the reflections associated to such a tensor. In the classical theory of Nichols algebras of diagonal type, this rule is given by a formula that we call
Rosso’s condition
:
the Cartan entry
c_{\ell,j}
is minus the smallest
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\big(1-q_{\ell,\ell}^{m}q_{\ell,j}q_{j,\ell}\big)\sum_{\nu=0}^{m}q_{\ell,\ell}^{\nu}=0.

In Section
3.3
, we compute in a natural way a generalized Rosso condition for
d
-th tensor powers when
d
is even.
In general, the Cartan entry
c_{\ell,j}
is minus the smallest
m
such that

\left(1-\prod_{\nu=1}^{d}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}((m+1)^{\nu}-m^{\nu}+(-1)^{\nu+1})}\right)\sum_{\mu=0}^{m}\prod_{\nu=1}^{d}q_{d-\nu}^{\mu\frac{1}{2}\left((m+1)^{\nu-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1}(-1)^{\nu-k}m^{k}\right)}=0,

where

q_{\nu}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in\{\ell,j\}\\
|\{k\mid i_{k}=j\}|=\nu\end{subarray}}q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}.

We also obtain a recursion for this condition (Theorem
3.2
) which is similar to the formula obtained in the classical case for the Nichols algebra.
We prove that our generalized Rosso condition produces Cartan matrices such that all axioms of a Weyl groupoid are satisfied (Theorem
3.10
). In examples, we obtain finite Weyl groupoids which do not occur as invariants of Nichols algebras of diagonal type. We conjecture that all finite Weyl groupoids are attained asymptotically when the tensor power (i.e.,
d
) increases.

In Section
4
, we give a precise definition of higher braidings in terms of abelian cohomology theory.
For an abelian group
G
, it is well-known that monoidal structures on the category of finite-dimensional
G
-graded vector spaces are (up to braided monoidal equivalence) classified by the third abelian cohomology
H_{ab}^{3}(G,\mathbb{K}^{\times})
.
If the associator is trivial, the defining abelian
3
-cocycle will simply be a bicharacter on
G
. In this situation every object naturally becomes a diagonally braided vector space.
In particular, if we set
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, a braiding on
V=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{K}\cdot x_{i}
is uniquely determined by a matrix
{\bf q}=(q_{ij})\in(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n\times n}
.
Two such braidings are equivalent if the numbers
q_{ii}
and
q_{ij}q_{ji}
coincide.
Consistently, we define a
d
-dimensional braiding to be an abelian
(2d-1)
-cocycle. Again, if the (higher) associative structure is trivial this will simply be a
d
-character on
G
. For
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
the
d
-dimensional braiding is then
uniquely determined by a tensor
{\bf q}=(q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}})
. In analogy to the classical case we can associate the numbers
q_{\nu}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
(see above) to such a
d
-dimensional diagonal braiding and see that these numbers only depend on the cohomology class of the abelian
(2d-1)
-cocycle.

We also notice that, as in the classical case (
d=2
), the higher
Rosso condition
as defined before only depends on the numbers
q_{\nu}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
and hence only on the cohomology class of the
d
-dimensional braiding
{\bf q}
(see Example
4.12
). Moreover, we conjecture that the numbers
q_{\nu}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
are defined by multi-dimensional symmetric forms
\theta_{\lambda}\in H^{2d-1}(G,\mathbb{K}^{\times})
indexed by partitions
\lambda
of
d
. This is Conjecture
4.10
and Remark
4.11
. For the case
d=2
, we obtain the well-known quadratic form
\theta_{2}
and symmetric bilinear form
\theta_{(1,1)}
, which define the numbers
q_{ii}
and
q_{ij}q_{ji}
in the diagonal case.

The computations in Section
4
require explicit knowledge of low-dimensional generators and boundaries of the abelian chain complex. In Table
1
, we list these up to
n=6
, as we believe this can be helpful to other people dealing with abelian chain complexes independently from our motivation.

2
Nichols algebras of diagonal type

2.1
Nichols algebras

Let
G
be an abelian group and
V
a finite-dimensional representation of the Drinfeld double
D(G)
over the field
\mathbb{K}
. Since
G
is abelian,
V
decomposes into a direct sum of one-dimensional modules

V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}V_{g_{i},\xi_{i}},

where
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in G
,
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\hat{G}=\Irr(G)
. Choose
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in V
such that
V_{g_{i},\xi_{i}}=\langle x_{i}\rangle
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then
G
acts on
V
via

g\cdot x_{i}=\xi_{i}(g)x_{i},\qquad g\in G,

and
\hat{G}
acts on
V
via

\xi\cdot x_{i}=\xi(g_{i})x_{i},\qquad\xi\in\hat{G}.

In this section, the relevant information about
V
is encoded in the matrix

{\bf q}=(q_{i,j})_{i,j}:=(\xi_{j}(g_{i}))_{i,j}.

There are many equivalent definitions of a Nichols algebra. For our purpose, the definition via skew derivations will be the most convenient (cf.
[
9
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Define (left and right)
skew derivations

\partial^{L}_{i}\colon V^{\otimes k}\rightarrow V^{\otimes k-1}
,

\partial^{R}_{i}\colon V^{\otimes k}\rightarrow V^{\otimes k-1}
,

i=1,\dots,n
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
inductively by

\displaystyle\partial^{L}_{i}(1):=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\partial^{L}_{i}(x_{j}):=\delta_{i,j},\qquad

\displaystyle\partial^{L}_{i}(xy):=\partial^{L}_{i}(x)y+(\xi_{i}\cdot x)\partial^{L}_{i}(y),

\displaystyle\partial^{R}_{i}(1):=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\partial^{R}_{i}(x_{j}):=\delta_{i,j},\qquad

\displaystyle\partial^{R}_{i}(xy):=x\partial^{R}_{i}(y)+\partial^{R}_{i}(x)(g_{i}\cdot y),

for
x
,
y
of degree
\geq 1
.

Let
\varepsilon\colon T(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
be the homomorphism of algebras with
\varepsilon(v)=0
for all
v\in V
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{I}(V)
be the largest ideal among all ideals
I
of
T(V)
such that
\varepsilon(I)=0
and

\partial^{L}_{i}(I)\subset I
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then
\mathfrak{B}(V):=T(V)/\mathfrak{I}(V)
is called the
Nichols algebra
(
of diagonal type
)
of
V
.

Using methods similar to
[
11
, Lemma 2.2]
, one can prove the following result:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\mathcal{K}_{i}:=\ker\partial^{R}_{i}
and

h_{i}:=\min\left\{m\geq 1\mid\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}q_{i,i}^{k}=0\right\}\cup\{\infty\}.

Then

\mathfrak{B}(V)\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{K}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{K}[x_{i}],&h_{i}=\infty,\\
\mathcal{K}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{K}[x_{i}]/\big(x_{i}^{h_{i}}\big),&h_{i}\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

as
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-graded vector spaces over
\mathbb{K}
.

2.2
Adjoint action

Following
[
9
]
, we will construct a (generalized) root system associated to a Nichols algebra of diagonal type.
An essential tool is the adjoint action:

Definition 2.4
.

With the above notation, for
i=1,\dots,n
and
y\in T(V)
, let

(\ad x_{i})(y):=x_{i}y-(g_{i}\cdot y)x_{i}.

In
[
9
, Proposition 1]
, it is shown that the algebra
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{B}(V)
generated by the elements
(\ad x_{i})^{m}(x_{j})
,
m\geq 0
,
j\neq i
. This implies:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is finitely generated if and only if: for all
j\neq i
, there exists
m_{i,j}\geq 0
such that

(\ad x_{i})^{m_{i,j}+1}(x_{j})\in\mathfrak{I}(V).

The following proposition will be the key for our generalization, hence we sketch a proof:

Proposition 2.6
.

In the situation of Proposition
2.5
, we have

m_{i,j}=\min\left\{m\geq 0\mid\sum_{k=0}^{m}q_{i,i}^{k}=0\ \text{or}\ q_{i,i}^{m}q_{i,j}q_{j,i}=1\right\}.

(2.1)

Proof.

Let
y\in T(V)
. Since
g_{i}\partial^{L}_{i}(y)=q_{i,i}^{-1}\partial^{L}_{i}(g_{i}y)
,

\displaystyle\partial^{L}_{i}((\ad x_{i})(y))

\displaystyle=y+q_{i,i}x_{i}\partial^{L}_{i}(y)-\partial^{L}_{i}(g_{i}y)x_{i}-(\xi_{i}g_{i}y)

\displaystyle=y-(\xi_{i}g_{i}y)+q_{i,i}(\ad x_{i})\partial^{L}_{i}(y).

In particular, for
y=(\ad x_{i})^{k}(x_{j})
,
k\geq 0
we get

\partial^{L}_{i}\big((\ad x_{i})^{k+1}(x_{j})\big)=\big(1-q_{i,i}^{2k}q_{i,j}q_{j,i}\big)(\ad x_{i})^{k}(x_{j})+q_{i,i}(\ad x_{i})\partial^{L}_{i}\big((\ad x_{i})^{k}(x_{j})\big).

With

R_{0}:=1-q_{i,j}q_{j,i},\qquad R_{k}:=1-q_{i,i}^{2k}q_{i,j}q_{j,i}+q_{i,i}R_{k-1}

(2.2)

for
k>0
, we obtain

\partial^{L}_{i}\big((\ad x_{i})^{k}(x_{j})\big)=R_{k-1}(\ad x_{i})^{k-1}(x_{j}).

Using induction we see that

R_{k}=\big(1-q_{i,i}^{k}q_{i,j}q_{j,i}\big)\sum_{\nu=0}^{k}q_{i,i}^{\nu}.

(2.3)

Thus
(\ad x_{i})^{k}(x_{j})\in\mathfrak{I}(V)
if and only if
R_{k-1}=0
.
∎

Let us call (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) the
Rosso condition
because it goes back to Rosso
[
15
]
.

2.3
The Weyl groupoid of a Nichols algebra

Recall that the Nichols algebra of diagonal type is completely determined by the matrix
{\bf q}
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
Cartan matrix

\big(c^{\bf q}_{i,j}\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
of
{\bf q}
is given by the formulas
c^{\bf q}_{i,i}=2
and (
2.1
):

\displaystyle c^{\bf q}_{i,j}=-\min\big\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid 1+q_{ii}+q_{ii}^{2}+\cdots+q_{ii}^{m}=0\ \text{or}\ q_{ii}^{m}q_{ij}q_{ji}=1\big\},\qquad i\neq j.

(2.4)

Notice that depending on
{\bf q}
, some of these entries may not be defined since an
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
satisfying one of the two conditions possibly does not exist. In this case, we will say that the Nichols algebra has no corresponding Weyl groupoid.
Proposition
2.5
ensures that a Cartan matrix exists in the special case that the Nichols algebra is finite dimensional.

Definition 2.8

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\langle\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\rangle
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be the standard basis.
Let
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\sigma_{\ell}\in\Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
be the reflection given by

\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{j}):=\alpha_{j}-c^{\bf q}_{\ell,j}\alpha_{\ell}

for
\ell=1,\dots,n
. Using
\sigma_{\ell}
we obtain a new matrix
\sigma_{\ell}({\bf q})
via

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell}({\bf q})_{i,j}:=\prod_{k,s=1}^{n}q_{k,s}^{\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{i})_{k}\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{j})_{s}}.

(2.5)

We can compute a Cartan matrix to
\sigma_{\ell}({\bf q})
instead of
{\bf q}
using Rosso’s formula and apply another reflection and so on.
This way we obtain a groupoid called the
Weyl groupoid
whose objects are the matrix
{\bf q}
and its images and the morphisms are compositions of the reflections between these matrices.
We recall the precise definition of a Weyl groupoid in the following subsection.

The
Dynkin diagram
of
{\bf q}
is a graph with vertices
1,\dots,n
and edges
(i,j)
when
q_{ij}q_{ji}\neq 1
and labeled by
q_{ij}q_{ji}
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
n=3
,
\zeta
be a primitive third root of unity, and

{\bf q}=(q_{ij})_{i,j}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&\hphantom{-}\zeta&\hphantom{-}\zeta\\
\hphantom{-}1&-1&\hphantom{-}\zeta\\
\hphantom{-}1&\hphantom{-}1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Dynkin diagrams in the Weyl groupoid of Example
2.9
.

Figure
1
displays the Weyl groupoid obtained from
{\bf q}
: the Dynkin diagrams are the objects, reflections are indicated by separating lines.

2.4
Definition of a Weyl groupoid

2.4.1
Cartan graphs

The collection of Cartan matrices produced in Example
2.9
is an example of what is known as a Cartan graph.

In early papers these were called Cartan schemes.

To define the corresponding Weyl groupoid, we need several notions (compare
[
2
,
3
]
).

Definition 2.10

(
[
12
, Section 1.1]
)
.

Let
I
be a non-empty finite set and
\{\alpha_{i}\mid i\in I\}
the standard basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{I}
.
A
generalized Cartan matrix

C=(c_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
is a matrix in
\mathbb{Z}^{I\times I}
such that

(M1)

c_{ii}=2
and
c_{jk}\leq 0
for all
i,j,k\in I
with
j\not=k
,

(M2)

if
i,j\in I
and
c_{ij}=0
, then
c_{ji}=0
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
A
be a non-empty set,
\rho_{i}\colon A\to A
a map for all
i\in I
, and
C^{a}=\big(c^{a}_{jk}\big)_{j,k\in I}
a generalized Cartan matrix in
\mathbb{Z}^{I\times I}
for all
a\in A
. The quadruple

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\big(I,A,(\rho_{i})_{i\in I},\big(C^{a}\big)_{a\in A}\big)

is called a
Cartan graph
if

(C1)

\rho_{i}^{2}=\mathrm{id}
for all
i\in I
,

(C2)

c^{a}_{ij}=c^{\rho_{i}(a)}_{ij}
for all
a\in A
and
i,j\in I
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\big(I,A,(\rho_{i})_{i\in I},\big(C^{a}\big)_{a\in A}\big)
be a Cartan graph.
For all
i\in I
and
a\in A
define
\sigma_{i}^{a}\in\Aut\big(\mathbb{Z}^{I}\big)
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{a}\big(\alpha_{j}\big)=\alpha_{j}-c_{ij}^{a}\alpha_{i}\qquad\text{for all $j\in I$.}

The
Weyl groupoid of

\mathcal{C}
is the category
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C})
such that
\Obj(\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C}))=A
and
the morphisms are compositions of maps
\sigma_{i}^{a}
with
i\in I
and
a\in A
,
where
\sigma_{i}^{a}
is considered as an element in
\Hom(a,\rho_{i}(a))
.
The category
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C})
is a groupoid in the sense that all morphisms are isomorphisms.
The cardinality of
I
is the
rank of

\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{C})
.

Since most upper indices referring to elements of
A
are determined by the context, we will often omit them to improve readability.

2.4.2
Root systems

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Cartan graph. For all
a\in A
let

(R^{\mathrm{re}})^{a}=\big\{\mathrm{id}^{a}\sigma_{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{i_{k}}(\alpha_{j})\,|\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,i_{1},\dots,i_{k},j\in I\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{I}.

The elements of the set
(R^{\mathrm{re}})^{a}
are called
real roots
(at
a
).
The pair
\big(\mathcal{C},\big((R^{\mathrm{re}})^{a}\big)_{a\in A}\big)
is denoted by
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{re}}(\mathcal{C})
.
A real root
\alpha\in(R^{\mathrm{re}})^{a}
, where
a\in A
, is called positive
(resp. negative) if
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{I}
(resp.
\alpha\in-\mathbb{N}_{0}^{I}
).

The Weyl groupoids which are relevant for the study of Nichols algebras produce sets of real roots which satisfies additional properties:

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\big(I,A,(\rho_{i})_{i\in I},\big(C^{a}\big)_{a\in A}\big)
be a Cartan graph.
For all
a\in A
let
R^{a}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{I}
, and define

m_{i,j}^{a}=\big|R^{a}\cap\big(\mathbb{N}_{0}\alpha_{i}+\mathbb{N}_{0}\alpha_{j}\big)\big|
for all
i,j\in I
and
a\in A
. We say that

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}\big(\mathcal{C},\big(R^{a}\big)_{a\in A}\big)

is a
root system of type

\mathcal{C}
, if it satisfies the following axioms.

(R1)

R^{a}=R^{a}_{+}\cup-R^{a}_{+}
, where
R^{a}_{+}=R^{a}\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}^{I}
, for all
a\in A
.

(R2)

R^{a}\cap\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{i}=\{\alpha_{i},-\alpha_{i}\}
for all
i\in I
,
a\in A
.

(R3)

\sigma_{i}^{a}\big(R^{a}\big)=R^{\rho_{i}(a)}
for all
i\in I
,
a\in A
.

(R4)

If
i,j\in I
and
a\in A
such that
i\not=j
and
m_{i,j}^{a}
is finite, then

(\rho_{i}\rho_{j})^{m_{i,j}^{a}}(a)=a
.

The axioms (R2) and (R3) are always fulfilled for
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{re}}
.
The root system
\mathcal{R}
is called
finite
if for all
a\in A
the set
R^{a}
is finite.
By
[
3
, Proposition 2.12]
, if
\mathcal{R}
is a finite root system
of type
\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{re}}
, and hence
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{re}}
is a root
system of type
\mathcal{C}
in that case.

3
Tensor powers of reflections

3.1
Additive notation

Let us take a closer look to Rosso’s formula (
2.4
). For
i\neq j
, we are looking for a smallest
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

1+q_{ii}+q_{ii}^{2}+\cdots+q_{ii}^{m}=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad q_{ii}^{m}q_{ij}q_{ji}=1,

or equivalently,

\big(q_{ii}^{m+1}=1\quad\text{and}\quad q_{ii}\neq 1\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad q_{ii}^{m}q_{ij}q_{ji}=1.

(3.1)

This shows that in the whole procedure computing the Weyl groupoid, only integral powers of
q_{ij}
are involved (remember (
2.5
)).
This allows us to write everything in an additive notation:
Let
W:=\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}=\langle e_{ij}\mid i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\rangle
and
\chi_{\bf q}
be the group homomorphism

\chi_{\bf q}\colon\ (W,+)\rightarrow U:=\langle q_{ij}\mid i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\rangle\leq\mathbb{K}^{\times},\qquad e_{ij}\mapsto q_{ij}.

Then we obtain an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules

\varepsilon\colon\ U\rightarrow M_{\bf q}:=W/\ker(\chi_{\bf q}).

Using the map
\varepsilon
, Rosso’s condition (
3.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\big((m+1)\varepsilon(q_{ii})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon(q_{ii})\neq 0\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad m\varepsilon(q_{ii})+\varepsilon(q_{ij})+\varepsilon(q_{ji})=0

or with
\overline{e}_{ij}:=e_{ij}+\ker(\chi_{\bf q})
for
i,j=1,\dots,n
in
M_{\bf q}
,

\displaystyle c^{\bf q}_{\ell,j}=c^{\sigma_{\ell}({\bf q})}_{\ell,j}

to the eigenvalues
\pm 1
for

Because of this fact, the conditions will remain the same after reflecting.

and

u_{m}:=((m+1)\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j})^{\otimes 2}-(m\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j})^{\otimes 2}=(2m+1)\alpha_{\ell}\otimes\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\otimes\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j}\otimes\alpha_{\ell}.

\displaystyle v_{m}:=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes 2}(u_{m})+u_{m}\big)=(m+1)\alpha_{\ell}^{\otimes 2},

\displaystyle w_{m}:=-\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes 2}(u_{m})-u_{m}\big)=m\alpha_{\ell}^{\otimes 2}+\alpha_{\ell}\otimes\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j}\otimes\alpha_{\ell}.

We observe that Rosso’s condition (

(1-q_{\ell,\ell})R_{m}=(1-\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m}))(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})).

3.3
Higher tensor powers

We now generalize the additive decomposition into eigenvectors (
3.2
) to higher tensor powers.
Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and

{\bf q}=(q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\in\mathbb{K}^{\{1,\dots,n\}^{d}}.

Let
W:=\big(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\big)^{\otimes d}
and denote by
e_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}:=\alpha_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\alpha_{i_{d}}
the elements of its standard basis.
Let
\chi_{\bf q}
be the group homomorphism

\chi_{\bf q}\colon\ (W,+)\rightarrow U:=\big\langle q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\mid i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in\{1,\dots,n\}\big\rangle\leq\mathbb{K}^{\times},\qquad e_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\mapsto q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}.

As before, we compare the
d
-th tensor powers of presumed degrees:

u_{m}:=((m+1)\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j})^{\otimes d}-(m\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j})^{\otimes d}.

We decompose
u_{m}
as a sum of eigenvectors of
\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes d}

u_{m}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes d}(u_{m})+u_{m}\big)}_{v_{m}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes d}(u_{m})-u_{m}\big)}_{w_{m}}.

We do not know yet what will be the analog for
d>2
of the factor
(1-q_{\ell,\ell})
, so let us first set

\tilde{R}_{m}:=\big(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})\big)\big(1-\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m})\big).

In the following Theorem
3.2
, we find a recursion for
\tilde{R}_{m}
similar to equation (
2.2
). We need some notation for its formulation:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\ell,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For
k\in\{0,\dots,d\}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we set

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in\{\ell,j\}\\
|\{\nu\>\mid\>i_{\nu}=j\}|=k\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\alpha_{i_{d}},\qquad q_{k}:=\chi_{\bf q}(\gamma_{k})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in\{\ell,j\}\\
|\{\nu\>\mid\>i_{\nu}=j\}|=k\end{subarray}}q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}},

\displaystyle e_{m,k}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu=0\\
(-1)^{\nu}=(-1)^{k}\end{subarray}}^{k-2}\binom{k}{\nu}m^{\nu},\qquad r_{m}:=\prod_{i=0}^{d-2}q_{i}^{e_{m,d-i}},

\displaystyle f_{m,k}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu=0\\
(-1)^{\nu}=(-1)^{k+1}\end{subarray}}^{k-1}\binom{k}{\nu}m^{\nu},\qquad z_{m}:=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}q_{i}^{f_{m,d-i}}.

Theorem 3.2
.

With the above notation,
\tilde{R}_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy the following recursions:

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{0}=(1-r_{0})(1-z_{0}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{m}=r_{m}\tilde{R}_{m-1}+(1-r_{m})(1-z_{m}).

(3.4)

Proof.

For equation (
3.3
), we compute

\displaystyle v_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes d}(u_{0})+u_{0}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big((-\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j})^{\otimes d}-\alpha_{j}^{\otimes d}+(\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j})^{\otimes d}-\alpha_{j}^{\otimes d}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-1}\big(1+(-1)^{d-\nu}\big)\gamma_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-2}e_{0,d-\nu}\gamma_{\nu}

because
e_{0,k}=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+(-1)^{k}\big)
; hence
\chi_{\bf q}(v_{0})=r_{0}
.
We check in the same way that
\chi_{\bf q}(w_{0})=z_{0}
, thus
\tilde{R}_{0}=(1-r_{0})(1-z_{0})
.
For the recursions, observe first that using

e_{m,k}+f_{m,k}=(m+1)^{k}-m^{k}

we obtain

e_{m,k}-f_{m,k}=(m-1)^{k}-m^{k}=-e_{m-1,k}-f_{m-1,k}.

(3.5)

This implies the relations

\displaystyle\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{f_{m,d-\nu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{efef}}}{{=}}\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-2}q_{\nu}^{e_{m,d-\nu}}\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{e_{m-1,d-\nu}+f_{m-1,d-\nu}},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-2}q_{\nu}^{e_{m,d-\nu}}\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}(e_{m-1,d-\nu}+f_{m-1,d-\nu})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{efef}}}{{=}}\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}(e_{m,d-\nu}+f_{m,d-\nu})}.

(3.7)

We now compute
v_{m}
and

\displaystyle v_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\ell}^{\otimes d}(u_{m})+u_{m}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\big(-\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j}\big)^{\otimes d}-\alpha_{j}^{\otimes d}+\big((m+1)\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j}\big)^{\otimes d}-\big(m\alpha_{\ell}+\alpha_{j}\big)^{\otimes d}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-1}\big(e_{m,d-\nu}+f_{m,d-\nu}+(-1)^{d-\nu}\big)\gamma_{\nu}

and similarly

w_{m}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-1}\big(e_{m,d-\nu}+f_{m,d-\nu}+(-1)^{d-\nu+1}\big)\gamma_{\nu}.

Thus we get

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{m}=(1-\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m}))(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m}))

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{R}_m }{}=1-\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{m,d-\nu}+f_{m,d-\nu}+(-1)^{d-\nu}\big)}-\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{m,d-\nu}+f_{m,d-\nu}+(-1)^{d-\nu+1}\big)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{R}_m=}{}+\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{e_{m,d-\nu}+f_{m,d-\nu}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{ef1}, \eqref{ef2}}}}{{=}}\left(\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-2}q_{\nu}^{e_{m,d-\nu}}\right)\left(1-\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{m-1,d-\nu}+f_{m-1,d-\nu}+(-1)^{d-\nu}\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{R}_m=}{}\times\left(1-\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{m-1,d-\nu}+f_{m-1,d-\nu}+(-1)^{d-\nu+1}\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{R}_m=}{}+\left(1-\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-2}q_{\nu}^{e_{m,d-\nu}}\right)\left(1-\prod_{\nu=0}^{d-1}q_{\nu}^{f_{m,d-\nu}}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{R}_m}{}=r_{m}\tilde{R}_{m-1}+(1-r_{m})(1-z_{m}).

3.4
Higher Rosso conditions

Remember that the original formula by Rosso (
2.4
) in multiplicative notation includes a geometric series which we replaced by the condition that
q_{\ell,\ell}^{m+1}=1
and
q_{\ell,\ell}\neq 1
. In other words, when
d=2
, we have to divide
\tilde{R}_{m}
by
1-q_{\ell,\ell}
. Moreover, this division is necessary to ensure Axiom (M2) (see Proposition
3.8
) if we aim to get a Weyl groupoid.

So we need to find some polynomial
\tilde{g}_{m}
in
q_{0},\dots,q_{d}
such that
\tilde{R}_{m}/\tilde{g}_{m}
is a condition that produces a Weyl groupoid. In particular, to obtain a Cartan matrix and a Cartan graph (which will be done in Definition
3.7
and Proposition
3.9
),

\tilde{R}_{m}/\tilde{g}_{m}
should not be rational functions with non trivial denominators.

In the classical case of Nichols algebras,
\tilde{g}_{m}
is a divisor of
(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m}))
. For
d>2
, the polynomial
(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m}))
in
q_{0},\dots,q_{d}
does not factorize in a nice way. However, we notice that the greatest common divisor of the coordinates of
v_{m}
(as polynomials in
m
) is
m+1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle v_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\big((m+1)^{\nu}-m^{\nu}+(-1)^{\nu}\big)\gamma_{d-\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}(m+1)\left((m+1)^{\nu-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1}(-1)^{\nu-k}m^{k}\right)\gamma_{d-\nu}.

This suggests the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

With the above notation and
m\in\mathbb{N}
we set

\displaystyle s_{m}:=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\left((m+1)^{\nu-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1}(-1)^{\nu-k}m^{k}\right)\gamma_{d-\nu},

\displaystyle g_{m}:=\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})=\prod_{\nu=1}^{d}q_{d-\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}\left((m+1)^{\nu-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1}(-1)^{\nu-k}m^{k}\right)},

\displaystyle R_{m}:=\frac{\tilde{R}_{m}}{1-g_{m}}=\frac{(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m}))(1-\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m}))}{1-\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})}.

(3.8)

Unfortunately, some of the coordinates of
s_{m}
are in
\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
(the exponents of
q_{i}
for
m
odd,
(i+d)
odd, and
i<d-1
) and we possibly get square roots of
q_{0},\dots,q_{d}
in
g_{m}
. In order to obtain a consistent condition and definition, we may fix square roots
\sqrt{q_{0}},\dots,\sqrt{q_{d}}
first and define the
q_{i}
to be their squares. This corresponds to enlarging the
\mathbb{Z}
-module considered in additive notation. We will see below that in most cases, the choice of square roots in fact does not affect the condition
R_{m}=0
.

Corollary 3.5
.

The conditions
R_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
satisfy:

\displaystyle R_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}\big[\sqrt{q_{0}},\dots,\sqrt{q_{d}}\big],

\displaystyle R_{0}=1-z_{0},

\displaystyle R_{m}=\frac{r_{m}(1-g_{m-1})}{1-g_{m}}R_{m-1}+\frac{(1-r_{m})(1-z_{m})}{1-g_{m}}.

Proof.

Since
\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})=g_{m}^{m+1}
, the polynomial
1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})
is divisible by
1-g_{m}
, the quotient being a geometric series. However, these are integral polynomials in
\sqrt{q_{i}}
,
i=0,\dots,d
by the definition of
g_{m}
.
One can check that

g_{0}=\prod_{\nu=1}^{d}q_{d-\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}(1+(-1)^{\nu})}=r_{0},

thus

R_{0}=(1-r_{0})(1-z_{0})/(1-g_{0})=1-z_{0}
.
The recursion is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The condition
R_{m}=0
may be written as

\displaystyle\big(\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})=1\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})\neq 1\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m})=1,

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\big(\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})^{m+1}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})\neq 1\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m})=1.

Note that square roots of
q_{i}
only appear in
\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})
for odd
m
since
v_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[q_{0},\dots,q_{d}]
and
s_{m}=v_{m}/(m+1)
.

Now assume that
\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})^{2}=1
. Then the first condition cannot be satisfied for even
m
.
If
m
is odd,
then
\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})^{m+1}=1^{(m+1)/2}=1
and in this case the first condition is satisfied if and only if
\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})=-1
. Hence
R_{m}=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\big(\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})=1\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\bf q}(2s_{m})\neq 1\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad(\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})=-1\quad\text{and}\quad m\text{ odd})

\displaystyle\text{or}\qquad\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m})=1.

It may happen that
\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})=-1
with
m
odd never occurs in the construction of the Weyl groupoid of a specific tensor. In this case, the general Rosso condition would be

\displaystyle\big(\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m})=1\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\bf q}(2s_{m})\neq 1\big)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m})=1,

and it would avoid the square roots.
However, in the classical case (
d=2
) one often has
q_{\ell,\ell}=-1
, and we expect that this
condition would exclude many cases where
s_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m})=-1
.

3.5
Weyl groupoids from higher tensor powers

We may now use the Rosso condition
R_{m}
for

to compute Weyl groupoids for a given
{\bf q}
in the same way as for
d=2
.
For
\ell,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we write
R^{\ell,j}_{m}:=R_{m}
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}

{\bf q}=(q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\in\mathbb{K}^{\{1,\dots,n\}^{d}}

For each tuple
(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
, we fix a square root

\sqrt{q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}}\in\mathbb{K}
of

and
by abuse of notation we write

\sqrt{{\bf q}}:=\big(\sqrt{q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}}\big)_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}.

Then using equation (
3.8

c^{\bf q}_{\ell,j}=\begin{cases}-\min\big\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid R^{\ell,j}_{m}=0\big\},&\ell\neq j,\\
2,&\ell=j,\end{cases}

and obtain a matrix

if such an
m
exists for all
\ell\neq j
.
In this case, we call

the

of
{\bf q}
.
It defines maps
\sigma_{\ell}
via

\sigma_{\ell}({\bf q})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}:=\prod_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}=1}^{n}q_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}}^{\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{i_{1}})_{k_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{i_{d}})_{k_{d}}}.

These maps act on the square roots in a compatible way since additively,
{\bf q}
is just twice the vector

and

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be even and

{\bf q}=(q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\in\mathbb{K}^{\{1,\dots,n\}^{d}}
.
If a Cartan matrix

is defined, then it is a generalized Cartan matrix.

Proof.

Axiom (M1) is satisfied by definition.
For (M2), remember that
R_{0}=1-z_{0}
. Since

z_{0}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-1}\frac{1}{2}\big(1-(-1)^{\nu}\big)\gamma_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-1}\frac{1}{2}\big(1-(-1)^{d-\nu}\big)\gamma_{\nu},

the condition
R_{0}
is the same for
c^{\bf q}_{\ell,j}
as for
c^{\bf q}_{j,\ell}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be even,
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
, and

A
be a set of tensors
{\bf q}\in\mathbb{K}^{I^{d}}
with defined Cartan matrix

.
Assume that for all
{\bf q}\in A
and
\ell\in I

\rho_{\ell}({\bf q}):=\sigma^{{\bf q}}_{\ell}({\bf q})\in A.

Then
(I,A,(\rho_{i})_{i\in I},(c^{\bf q})_{{\bf q}\in A})
is a Cartan graph.

Proof.

Let
{\bf q}\in A
and
\ell,j\in I
.
The matrices

are generalized Cartan matrices by Proposition
3.8
.
If Axiom (C2) is satisfied, then (C1) is satisfied as well because
\rho_{\ell}
is defined using a reflection which has order two and
\sigma^{{\bf q}}_{\ell}=\sigma^{\rho_{\ell}({\bf q})}_{\ell}
since the relevant Cartan entries are equal by (C2).

For (C2), we need to compare
c^{\bf q}_{\ell,j}
and
c^{\rho_{\ell}({\bf q})}_{\ell,j}
for
\ell\neq j
.
With
m:=-c^{\bf q}_{\ell,j}

and
R_{k}\neq 0
for
k<m
.
Now
R_{m}=(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m}))(1-\chi_{\bf q}(w_{m}))/(1-\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m}))

v_{m}
and

are eigenvectors, i.e.,
\big(\sigma^{{\bf q}}_{\ell}\big)^{\otimes d}(v_{m})=v_{m}

\big(\sigma^{{\bf q}}_{\ell}\big)^{\otimes d}(w_{m})=-w_{m}

also,
\big(\sigma^{{\bf q}}_{\ell}\big)^{\otimes d}(s_{m})=s_{m}
because
(m+1)s_{m}=v_{m}
.
Hence replacing
{\bf q}
by
\sigma^{{\bf q}}_{\ell}({\bf q})
will map
R_{m}

(1-\chi_{\bf q}(v_{m}))(1-\chi_{\bf q}(-w_{m}))/(1-\chi_{\bf q}(s_{m}))

But
1-\chi_{\bf q}(-w_{m})=0
if and only if
-w_{m}=0
or equivalently
w_{m}=0
.
Therefore, via (

) the new condition produces the same Cartan entry

for
\ell\neq j
.

4.1
Nichols algebras as graded vector spaces

Let
G
be an abelian group and let
\mathsf{Vect}_{G}
denote the category of finite-dimensional
G
-graded vector spaces over a field
\mathbb{K}
.

It is well-known
[
8
, Example 8.4.8]
that braided monoidal structures on
\mathsf{Vect}_{G}
are classified (up to braided monoidal equivalence) by the third abelian cohomology
H^{3}_{1}\big(G,\mathbb{K}^{\times}\big)
. The construction goes as follows. Let
(\omega,\theta)\in Z^{3}_{1}\big(G,\mathbb{K}^{\times}\big)
be an abelian 3-cocycle. For
g,h,k\in G
, let
V_{g}
,
V_{h}
and
V_{k}
be the corresponding simple objects in
\mathsf{Vect}_{G}
. Then we can define an associator
a
and a braiding
c
on
\mathsf{Vect}_{G}
as follows:

\displaystyle a_{g,h,k}\colon\ (V_{g}\otimes V_{h})\otimes V_{k}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow V_{g}\otimes(V_{h}\otimes V_{k}),

\displaystyle\qquad e_{g}\otimes e_{h}\otimes e_{k}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\omega(g,h,k)\,e_{g}\otimes e_{h}\otimes e_{k},

\displaystyle c_{g,h}\colon\ V_{g}\otimes V_{h}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow V_{h}\otimes V_{g},

\displaystyle e_{g}\otimes e_{h}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\theta(g,h)\,e_{h}\otimes e_{g}.

Here, the tensor product of simple objects
V_{g}
and
V_{h}
is simply given by
V_{g}\otimes V_{h}:=V_{gh}
. We denote the resulting braided monoidal category by
\mathsf{Vect}_{G}^{(\omega,\theta)}
.

If the (ordinary) 3-cocycle
\omega
is trivial, then we can read off Table
1
(
n=4
,
k=1
) that
\theta
must be a bicharacter. Hence, we can define a

-action on every simple object

by setting

. For this reason, any object in

can be regarded as a Yetter–Drinfeld module over

. In particular, for

the object

\displaystyle V=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\,V_{g_{i}}\in\mathsf{Vect}_{G}^{(1,\theta)}

is a diagonally braided vector space with braid matrix

\displaystyle{\bf q}=(q_{i,j})_{i,j}:=(\theta(g_{i},g_{j}))_{i,j}

and we can define the Nichols algebra

of

as a Hopf algebra inside the braided monoidal category

because its construction (including the operators

and

As pointed out by a referee, this has also been done recently in

4.2
Abelian cohomology theory

In this subsection, we recapitulate the cohomology theory of abelian groups first introduced by Eilenberg and MacLane in
[
6
,
7
,
14
]
. They realized that the homology theory appropriate to an
abelian
group
3
3

3

We write
\Pi
instead of
G
here because this is the usual symbol for abelian cohomology.

\Pi
is not given by the ordinary bar complex
A_{\bullet}(\Pi)
of the group ring
\mathbb{Z}[\Pi]
, as the proof of the condition
\partial\partial=0
only uses associativity of
\Pi
but not commutativity. In order to resolve this issue, they defined further cell complexes
A^{k}_{\bullet}(\Pi)
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
can be thought of as the
level of commutativity
which is taken into account. In particular, we have
A_{\bullet}(\Pi)=A^{0}_{\bullet}(\Pi)
.

We will reproduce the inductive definition of the cell complexes
A^{k}_{\bullet}(\Pi)
from
[
7
]
:

Let
A_{\bullet}(\Pi)
be the ordinary bar complex of the abelian group
\Pi
.

More precisely,
A_{n}(\Pi)
is the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by
n
-tuples
[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of elements
x_{i}\in\Pi
. The boundary operators
\partial_{n}\colon A_{n}(\Pi)\to A_{n-1}(\Pi)
are given by

\displaystyle\partial_{n}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]:=[x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\,(-1)^{i}[x_{1},\dots,x_{i}x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}]+(-1)^{n}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}].

Since this is the basis of our induction, we set

\displaystyle A^{0}_{\bullet}(\Pi)=A_{\bullet}(\Pi),\qquad\partial^{0}_{n}=\partial_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[x_{1}\,|_{0}\dots|_{0}\,x_{n}]=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}].

As a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module,
A^{k}_{n}(\Pi)
is generated by
p
-tuples
[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}]
, where the
\alpha_{i}
are generators of
A^{k-1}_{n_{i}}(\Pi)
, such that

\displaystyle n=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\,n_{i}+(p-1)k.

Hence, we can think of the symbol
|_{k}
as a placeholder for
k

\displaystyle A^{0}_{n}(\Pi)\leq A^{1}_{n}(\Pi)\leq\dots\leq A^{n-2}_{n}(\Pi)=A^{n-1}_{n}(\Pi)=\cdots

(4.1)

of free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules.

The graded
\mathbb{Z}
-module
A^{k}_{\bullet}(\Pi)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\,A^{k}_{n}(\Pi)
is endowed with a so-called
k
-shuffle product

\displaystyle\ast_{k}\colon\ A^{k}_{n}(\Pi)\times A^{k}_{m}(\Pi)\to A^{k}_{n+m+k}(\Pi).

For
\alpha=[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}]\in A^{k}_{n}(\Pi)

-tuple

\displaystyle[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}\,|_{k}\,\beta_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\beta_{q}]

in a way that preserves the order of the
\alpha_{i}

\beta_{j}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\gamma}:=\sum_{\text{$(i,j)$ such that $\alpha_{i}$ is after $\beta_{j}$ in $\gamma$}}\,(n_{i}+k)(n_{j}+k).

The
k
-shuffle product on
A^{k}_{\bullet}(\Pi)
is then defined as follows:

\displaystyle[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}]\ast_{k}[\beta_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\beta_{q}]:=\sum_{\text{shuffles $\gamma$ of $\alpha$ and $\beta$}}\,(-1)^{\epsilon_{\gamma}}[\gamma_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\gamma_{p+q}].

Having introduced the
k
-shuffle product, we are now ready to define the boundary operators
\partial^{k}_{n}\colon A^{k}_{n}(\Pi)\to A^{k}_{n-1}(\Pi)

\displaystyle\partial^{k}_{n}[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}]:=

\displaystyle{}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\,(-1)^{a_{i-1}}[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\partial^{k-1}\alpha_{i}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}]

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\,(-1)^{a_{i}}[\alpha_{1}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{i-1}\,|_{k}\,\alpha_{i}\ast_{k-1}\alpha_{i+1}\,|_{k}\,\alpha_{i+2}\,|_{k}\dots|_{k}\,\alpha_{p}],

where

For the proof that
\partial^{k}_{n-1}\partial^{k}_{n}=0
holds, we refer to
[

\Pi

\{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{p}\}_{\lambda}\in A_{2d-1}^{1}(\Pi)
is a cycle, i.e.,

\displaystyle\partial\{a_{1};\dots;a_{p}\}_{\lambda}=0.

Proof.

Applying Definition
4.2
, we obtain the general formula

\displaystyle\partial[\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{d}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\,(-1)^{a_{i}}[\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{i}\ast_{0}\beta_{i+1}|\dots|\beta_{d}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\big([\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{i},\beta_{i+1}|\dots|\beta_{d}]-[\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{i+1},\beta_{i}|\dots|\beta_{d}]\big).

Note that
a_{i}=2i
is always even in the considered case.

Looking at this formula, we notice that the
i
th summand of
\partial[\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{d}]
vanishes iff
\beta_{i}=\beta_{i+1}
. If
\beta_{i}\neq\beta_{i+1}
, then
[\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{i+1}|\beta_{i}|\dots|\beta_{d}]
is the unique summand of
\{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{p}\}_{\lambda}
such that the
i
th summand of its boundary cancels out the
i
th summand of
\partial[\beta_{1}|\dots|\beta_{d}]
. Since this is a one-to-one correspondence between non-vanishing summands of
\partial\{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{p}\}_{\lambda}
, the statement follows.
∎

In the following, the elements
\{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{p}\}_{\lambda}\in Z_{2d-1}^{1}(\Pi)
will be referred to as
symmetrized cycles
.

We will now explore how symmetrized cycles are related to each other. We start by proving identities for small
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Pi
we have the following identities in
H_{3}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\beta\}_{(2)}\big]=\big[\{\alpha\}_{(2)}\big]+\big[\{\beta\}_{(2)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(1,1)}\big],

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\beta;\gamma\}_{(1,1)}\big]=\big[\{\alpha;\gamma\}_{(1,1)}\big]+\big[\{\beta;\gamma\}_{(1,1)}\big],

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\}_{(2)}\big]=\big[\big\{\alpha^{-1}\big\}_{(2)}\big].

Proof.

Using the definition of the boundary operator, one can easily verify that the element

\displaystyle-\{\alpha\beta\}_{(2)}+\{\alpha\}_{(2)}+\{\beta\}_{(2)}+\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(1,1)}

is the boundary of the following element in
A_{4}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle[\alpha,\beta|\alpha\beta]+[\alpha|\beta,\alpha]+[\beta|\alpha,\beta]-[\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta].

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\partial\big([\alpha,\beta|\gamma]+[\gamma|\alpha,\beta]\big)=-\{\alpha\beta;\gamma\}_{(1,1)}+\{\alpha;\gamma\}_{(1,1)}+\{\beta;\gamma\}_{(1,1)}

and

\displaystyle\partial\big(\big[\alpha|\alpha^{-1},\alpha\big]-\big[\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\alpha^{-1}\big]+\big[\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\big]\big)=\{\alpha\}_{(2)}-\big\{\alpha^{-1}\big\}_{(2)}.

∎

Using the symmetry from Remark
4.3
, the following corollary follows immediately from the previous lemma.

Corollary 4.7
.

For
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\}_{(2)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\}_{(2)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}\}_{(2)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\}_{(2)}\big]

\displaystyle{}+\big[\{\alpha_{1}\}_{(2)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{2}\}_{(2)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{3}\}_{(2)}\big].

Lemma 4.8
.

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\Pi
, we have the following identities in
H_{5}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\beta\}_{(3)}\big]=\big[\{\alpha\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\beta\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(2,1)}\big]+\big[\{\beta;\alpha\}_{(2,1)}\big],

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\beta;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}\big]=\big[\{\alpha;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}\big]+\big[\{\beta;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha;\beta;\gamma\}_{(1,1,1)}\big],

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha;\beta\gamma\}_{(2,1)}\big]=\big[\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(2,1)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}\big],

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\beta;\gamma;\delta\}_{(1,1,1)}\big]=\big[\{\alpha;\gamma;\delta\}_{(1,1,1)}\big]+\big[\{\beta;\gamma;\delta\}_{(1,1,1)}\big]

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha\}_{(3)}\big]=-\big[\big\{\alpha^{-1}\big\}_{(3)}\big]

\displaystyle\big[\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(2,1)}\big]=\big[\big\{\alpha^{-1};\beta\big\}_{(2,1)}\big].

Proof.

A long and tedious calculation shows that

\displaystyle-\{\alpha\beta\}_{(3)}+\{\alpha\}_{(3)}+\{\beta\}_{(3)}+\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(2,1)}+\{\beta;\alpha\}_{(2,1)}

is the boundary of the following element in
A_{6}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle[\alpha,\beta|\alpha\beta|\alpha\beta]+[\beta|\alpha,\beta|\alpha\beta]+[\alpha|\beta,\alpha|\beta\alpha]+[\beta|\alpha|\alpha,\beta]+[\alpha|\beta|\alpha,\beta]+[\alpha|\alpha|\alpha,\beta]+[\beta|\beta|\alpha,\beta]

\displaystyle\qquad{}-[\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta|\alpha\beta]-[\beta|\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta]-[\alpha|\beta,\alpha,\beta,\alpha]+[\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta].

Another calculation shows that

\displaystyle-\{\alpha\beta;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}+\{\alpha;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}+\{\beta;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}+\{\alpha;\beta;\gamma\}_{(1,1,1)}

is the boundary of the following element in
A_{6}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle[\alpha,\beta|\alpha\beta|\gamma]+[\alpha,\beta|\gamma|\alpha\beta]+[\gamma|\alpha,\beta|\alpha\beta]+[\gamma|\beta|\alpha,\beta]+[\beta|\gamma|\alpha,\beta]+[\gamma|\alpha|\beta,\alpha]+[\alpha|\gamma|\beta,\alpha]

\displaystyle\qquad{}+[\alpha|\beta,\alpha|\gamma]+[\beta|\alpha,\beta|\gamma]-[\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta|\gamma]-[\gamma|\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta].

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\partial\big([\alpha|\alpha|\beta,\gamma]+[\alpha|\beta,\gamma|\alpha]+[\beta,\gamma|\alpha|\alpha]\big)=-\{\alpha;\beta\gamma\}_{(2,1)}+\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(2,1)}+\{\alpha;\gamma\}_{(2,1)}.

The fourth identity follows from the element

\displaystyle-\{\alpha\beta;\gamma;\delta\}_{(1,1,1)}+\{\alpha;\gamma;\delta\}_{(1,1,1)}+\{\beta;\gamma;\delta\}_{(1,1,1)}

being the boundary of the following element in
A_{6}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle[\alpha,\beta|\gamma|\delta]+[\gamma|\alpha,\beta|\delta]+[\gamma|\delta|\alpha,\beta]+[\alpha,\beta|\delta|\gamma]+[\delta|\alpha,\beta|\gamma]+[\delta|\gamma|\alpha,\beta].

Furthermore, the element

\displaystyle\{\alpha\}_{(3)}+\big\{\alpha^{-1}\big\}_{(3)}

is the boundary of the following element in
A_{6}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle\big[\alpha^{-1}|\alpha^{-1}|\alpha^{-1},\alpha\big]-\big[\alpha^{-1}|\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\alpha\big]+\big[\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\alpha|\alpha\big]-\big[\alpha^{-1}|\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\big[\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1}|\alpha\big]+\big[\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\big].

Finally, the element

\displaystyle\{\alpha;\beta\}_{(2,1)}-\big\{\alpha^{-1};\beta\big\}_{(2,1)}

is the boundary of the following element in
A_{6}^{1}(\Pi)
:

\displaystyle\big[\alpha^{-1}|\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\beta\big]-\big[\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\alpha|\beta\big]+\big[\alpha^{-1}|\beta|\alpha^{-1},\alpha\big]-\big[\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\beta|\alpha\big]

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\big[\beta|\alpha^{-1}|\alpha^{-1},\alpha\big]-\big[\beta|\alpha^{-1},\alpha|\alpha\big]-\big[\beta|\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\big]-\big[\alpha,\alpha^{-1},\alpha,\alpha^{-1}|\beta\big].

∎

Again, using the symmetry from Remark
4.3
, the following corollary follows immediately from the previous lemma.

Corollary 4.9
.

For
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}\in\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]

\displaystyle\phantom{0=}{}-\big[\{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\}_{(3)}\big]

\displaystyle\phantom{0=}{}+\big[\{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{2}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{3}\}_{(3)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{4}\}_{(3)}\big],

\displaystyle 0=\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big]-\big[\{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big]

\displaystyle\phantom{0=}{}+\big[\{\alpha_{1};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{2};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big]+\big[\{\alpha_{3};\alpha_{4}\}_{(2,1)}\big].

.

Let

be a
p

and

\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\lambda_{i}+1},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{p}\}\subseteq\Pi.

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq\lambda_{i}+1}\,\big[\{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{i-1};\beta_{1}\dots\hat{\beta}_{k}\dots\hat{\beta}_{l}\dots\beta_{\lambda_{i}+1};\alpha_{i+1};\dots;\alpha_{p}\}_{\lambda}\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\vdots

Remark 4.11
.

Let
A
be an abelian group and let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})
be a
p
-composition of
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, let
\theta\in A^{2d-1}_{1}(\Pi,A)
be a cochain. We can define a map
\theta_{\lambda}\colon\Pi^{\times p}\to A
by setting

\displaystyle\theta_{\lambda}(\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{p}):=\theta\big(\{\alpha_{1};\dots;\alpha_{p}\}_{\lambda}\big).

Now, we can reinterpret Conjecture
4.10
as follows: If
\theta
is a cocycle, then
\theta_{\lambda}
is a
\lambda_{i}
-form in the
i
th argument. Moreover, from Lemma
4.5
we know that in this case
\theta_{\lambda}
only depends on the cohomology class of
\theta\in Z^{2d-1}_{1}(\Pi,A)
.

Example 4.12
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field and
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
\Pi=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
A=\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. Let
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We are now in the setting of Definition
3.1
.

Let
\theta\in Z^{2d-1}_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{K}^{\times})
be a
(2d-1)
-cocycle. For
i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we define

\displaystyle q_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}:=\theta([\alpha_{i_{1}}|\dots|\alpha_{i_{d}}])\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}.

Then, for
\ell,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
k\in\{0,\dots,d\}
, we have

\displaystyle q_{k}=\theta_{(d-k,k)}(\alpha_{\ell},\alpha_{j}).

In particular, by the previous remark the Rosso condition
\tilde{R}_{m}=0
only depends on the cohomology class
[\theta]\in H^{2d-1}_{1}\big(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{K}^{\times}\big)
.
