Abstract
There are four non-isomorphic configurations of triples that can form a triangle in a 3 3 -uniform hypergraph. Forbidding different combinations of these four configurations, fifteen extremal problems can be defined, several of which already appeared in the literature in some different context. Here we systematically study all of these problems solving the new cases exactly or asymptotically. In many cases we also characterize the extremal constructions.



1
Introduction

1.1
Notations

A
hypergraph

{\mathcal{F}}
is a pair
(V({\mathcal{F}}),E({\mathcal{F}}))

where the set of
edges
,
E({\mathcal{F}})
, is a family of subsets of
the set of
vertices

V({\mathcal{F}})
(see Berge
[
1
]
, Lovász
[
20
]
).
To simplify notations we frequently identify
{\mathcal{F}}
with
E({\mathcal{F}})
(and omit parentheses, etc.) if it does not cause ambiguity.
If all edges of
{\mathcal{F}}
have the same size, say
r
, then we speak of an
r
-graph, or
r
-
uniform
hypergraph.
With this terminology
2
-graphs are simple graphs without loops and 3-uniform hypergraphs are also called
triple systems
.
We usually identify
V=V({\mathcal{F}})
with the set of first
n
positive integers,
[n]:=\{1,2,3,\dots,n\}
. Then
{V\choose r}
stands for the complete
r
-graph with vertex set
V
, it is
\{F:|F|=r,F\subseteq V\}
.

We write
\partial{\mathcal{F}}
for
\{F\setminus\{x\}:F\in{\mathcal{F}},x\in F\}
, it is called the
shadow
of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
If
{\mathcal{F}}
is a triple system then its shadow is a simple graph.
The
degree
of
S\subseteq V({\mathcal{F}})
is the number of edges containing
S
, in notation

\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(S):=|\{F:F\in{\mathcal{F}},S\subseteq F\}|.

Given a triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
we write
{\mathcal{F}}[x]
for the
link
of
x
in
{\mathcal{F}}
, i.e., the graph on
V({\mathcal{F}})\setminus\{x\}
whose edges are those pairs that form with
x
a triple in
{\mathcal{F}}
. If
F
is an edge of a
3
-uniform hypergraph
{{\mathcal{F}}}
such that
x,y\in F
,
x\neq y
,
and there is no other
F^{\prime}\in{{\mathcal{F}}}
containing both
x
and
y
, then we say that
the pair
xy
is an
own pair
of
F
. With our previous notation,
\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(xy)=1
.

1.2
Cycles in hypergraphs

In hypergraph theory
a cycle of length
\ell
is usually defined as a sequence of

distinct

vertices
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{\ell}
together with a sequence of

distinct
edges

E_{1},\dots,E_{\ell}
such that
E_{i}
contains
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}

for
1\leq i<\ell
and
E_{\ell}
contains
x_{\ell}
and
x_{1}
.
We refer to a cycle of
\ell
edges as an
\ell
-cycle.
For ordinary graphs, the above definition coincides with the definition
of a cycle
C_{\ell}
in graphs.
We call a 3-cycle a
triangle
.

One of the oldest problems in extremal graph theory is to
investigate the maximum number of edges of graphs without given short
cycles, e. g., the Turán-Mantel theorem says that a triangle-free
graph on
n
vertices has at most

\lfloor\frac{1}{4}n^{2}\rfloor
edges and the only extremal graph is the
complete bipartite graph with parts of sizes

\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
and
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
, respectively.
For further graph results see the book of Bollobás
[
4
]
.

Hypergraph problems are more complicated, but there are some results, e. g.,
that of Lazebnik and Verstraëte
[
19
]

saying that if
{\mathcal{F}}
is
3
-uniform, has
n
vertices, girth five
(i.e., no cycles of length less than five) and a maximum
number of edges, then
|E({\mathcal{F}})|=\frac{1}{6}n^{3/2}+o(n^{3/2})
.

In this paper we deal with 3-uniform hypergraphs containing no triangles of certain types.

1.3
Triangles in triple systems

One can easily realize that
there are
altogether four non-isomorphic configurations of three triples that create a
triangle. We will call these configurations
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}
, and
{\mathbf{D}}
(see Figure 1 below). The main goal of this paper is to study the maximum number of triples one can have on
n
vertices while excluding certain combinations of the four triangle creating
configurations.

Configuration
{\mathbf{A}}
is the one needing six vertices, that is, it is isomorphic to
the hypergraph with
V=\{1,\dots,6\},E=\{124,135,236\}.

Configuration
{\mathbf{B}}
is on five vertices, where two of the three triples involved
intersect in two vertices and each of them meets the third triple in a different vertex.
Thus
{\mathbf{B}}
is
isomorphic to the hypergraph with
V=\{1,\dots,5\},E=\{125,134,234\}.

Configuration
{\mathbf{C}}
is the one on four vertices, that is, it is isomorphic to
the hypergraph with
V=\{1,\dots,4\},E=\{124,134,234\}.

Configuration
{\mathbf{D}}
is on five vertices again, with one triple sharing with each of the other two a different pair of vertices. It is isomorphic to the hypergraph
with
V=\{1,\dots,5\},E=\{123,134,235\}.

Figure 1:
The four configurations of three triples forming a triangle

Our starting point is a result (first proved by Győri
[
16

) giving the maximum number of edges in a

3

{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}

and

are forbidden subconfigurations.
In Section
2
we reprove it and also describe the extremal families.
Then we consider the fourteen other cases when some proper subset of

\{{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{D}}\}
is forbidden. Five of these subsets define
problems of long history, most of them are solved, but not all. Then we go on to
investigate the other nine. For all but one of those nine combinations, we obtain an exact solution of the extremal problem (though sometimes only for large enough
n
), often along with a characterization of the extremal constructions. In the one exceptional case we give an asymptotic solution.

for

It denotes the maximum number of triples in a
3
-uniform hypergraph on

.

2
The starting case: no triangle at all

How large can a triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
on
n

vertices be, if it contains no triangle at all? Here is a construction originally suggested by
András Gyárfás as a conjectured optimum. Let
n\geq 3
be given, and let
k
be a positive integer such that
2k<n
. Partition the set
V
of
n
vertices into
k
pairs
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
and a set
B
of size
n-2k
. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{n,k}
consist of all
k(n-2k)
triples of the form
A_{i}\cup\{b\}
where
1\leq i\leq k
and
b\in B
. Then
{\mathcal{F}}_{n,k}
contains no triangles. To maximize the size
k(n-2k)
for a given
n
, we choose
k
to be as close as possible to
\frac{n}{4}
(this choice is unique unless
n\equiv 2\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
, in which case the two choices
k=\frac{n\pm 2}{4}
are equally good). With this optimal choice of
k
we have
|{\mathcal{F}}_{n,k}|=\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor
.

The optimality of this construction among all triangle-free triple systems was proved by Győri
[
16
]
. Here we reproduce the proof and extend the analysis to obtain also a characterization of the extremal constructions.

Theorem 1
.

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}})=\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor

and equality is attained by a triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
if and only if it is of the form
{\mathcal{F}}_{n,k}
with
k
chosen optimally as indicated above.

Proof.

Let
{{\mathcal{F}}}
be a triple system not containing any of
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{D}}.

First observe that not containing
{\mathbf{C}}
and
{\mathbf{D}}
implies (in fact, is
equivalent to) that every triple contains at least two pairs which are its own.
For those triples containing three own pairs choose two that we single out as
special. Consider the graph
G
on
V({{\mathcal{F}}})
formed by the own pairs and
the special own pairs of the triples containing two or three own pairs,
respectively. We claim that
G
is triangle-free. Indeed, if a triangle were
formed by own pairs of three different triples, then we would have a triangle
in
{{\mathcal{F}}}
in the hypergraph sense, that is, one of our forbidden
configurations would appear. Thus at least two edges of a triangle in
G

should be own pairs of the same triple
F
. However, in that case the third
edge of the triangle is also contained in
F
, thus it cannot be the own pair
of another triple
F^{\prime}
. But it also cannot be an edge of
G
as a third own
pair of
F
since every triple in
{{\mathcal{F}}}
is represented by exactly two pairs
in
G
. Therefore a triangle in
G
cannot occur.
This implies by Turán’s theorem that
|E(G)|\leq\frac{1}{4}n^{2}
. Since

|E(G)|=2|{\mathcal{F}}|
, we got
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor.

It remains to show that if
|{\mathcal{F}}|=\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor
then
{\mathcal{F}}
must be of the form
{\mathcal{F}}_{n,k}
. Note that
|E(G)|=2\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor
, and this number equals
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}n^{2}\rfloor
unless
n\equiv 2\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
, in which case it equals
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}n^{2}\rfloor-1
. By known characterizations
(see
[
7
]
)
of the extremal and next-to-extremal cases for Turán’s theorem,
G
must be bipartite with parts
A
and
B
satisfying one of the following:

(a)

n\not\equiv 2\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
,
|A|
and
|B|
differ by at most
1
and
G
is complete bipartite.

(b)

n\equiv 2\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
,
|A|=|B|
and
G
is complete bipartite minus one edge.

(c)

n\equiv 2\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
,
|A|
and
|B|
differ by
2
and
G
is complete bipartite.

Each triple in
{\mathcal{F}}
contributes two own pairs to
G
, hence contains two vertices in
A
and one in
B
or vice versa. We claim that all triples in
{\mathcal{F}}
must be of the same kind, i.e., the same part contains two vertices from each triple. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there are triples
F_{A},F_{B}\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
F_{A}\cap A=\{x,x^{\prime}\}
and
F_{B}\cap B=\{y,y^{\prime}\}
. Out of the four pairs in
\{x,x^{\prime}\}\times\{y,y^{\prime}\}
, at most one is a non-edge of
G
. Hence w.l.o.g.
x^{\prime}y,yx,xy^{\prime}
form a path in
G
. Say these edges of
G
represent the triples
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
, respectively. Clearly,
F_{1},F_{2}
and
F_{3}
cannot all be the same triple, so w.l.o.g.
F_{1}\neq F_{2}
, and neither
F_{1}
nor
F_{2}
contains
\{x,x^{\prime}\}
. But then
F_{A},F_{1},F_{2}
are three distinct triples in
{\mathcal{F}}
which form a forbidden triangle.

Thus, we assume w.l.o.g. that every triple in
{\mathcal{F}}
contains two vertices in
A
and one in
B
. For each vertex
b\in B
, its link in
{\mathcal{F}}
consists of disjoint pairs in
A
(otherwise, we would have an edge of
G
that belongs to two triples in
{\mathcal{F}}
). This gives the upper bound

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lfloor\frac{|A|}{2}\rfloor\cdot|B|.

If
|A|
is odd, this bound is strictly smaller than
\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor
. Thus
|A|
must be even, ruling out case (b) above. Moreover, the bound must hold as an equality, so the link of each
b\in B
is a partition of
A
into
k
pairs
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
, where
k=\frac{|A|}{2}
. Furthermore, this partition must be the same for all
b\in B
, otherwise there would be a forbidden triangle. This shows that
{\mathcal{F}}
is of the form
{\mathcal{F}}_{n,k}
, and the conditions on the difference between
|A|
and
|B|
in cases (a), (c) above imply the optimal choice of
k
.
∎

3
Earlier results

The four cases where we exclude just one type of triangle creating
configurations
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}
, or
{\mathbf{D}}
, were already investigated
in some different context.
In particular,
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}})={{n-1}\choose 2}
for large enough
n
is proven in
[
12
]

(as Theorem 3.3). This result also determines that all extremal
constructions are
full stars
,
3
-uniform hypergraphs containing all edges through a fixed vertex.

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}})={n-1\choose 2}\text{ for }n\geq n_{0}.

Observing that this construction also does not contain
configuration
{\mathbf{B}}
, we have that the same holds for

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}
-free hypergraphs, too.
Here (in Section
4
) we prove that
n_{0}=5
for
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}})
.

An early attempt to generalize Turán’s theorem to hypergraphs
(for triple systems) was proposed by Erdős and
Katona, who conjectured that the largest triple system on

n
vertices such that no three distinct triples

A,B,C
satisfy
A\bigtriangleup B\subset C
is the complete
3
-partite hypergraph with part sizes as equal as possible.
Here
\bigtriangleup
is the symmetric difference,

A\bigtriangleup B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.
One can see that (for
A\neq B
)
A\bigtriangleup B\subset C

can only occur when
A
and
B
have two common vertices and
the three triples form either a configuration
{\mathbf{B}}
or
{\mathbf{C}}
.
The conjecture was verified by Bollobás
[
3
]
thus showing

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{C}})=\lfloor{n\over 3}\rfloor\lfloor{{n+1}\over 3}\rfloor\lfloor{{n+2}\over 3}\rfloor.

Frankl and Füredi (in
[
10
]
as Theorem 4) sharpened
Bollobás’s theorem
proving that for
n\geq 3000
it is sufficient to exclude only

{\mathbf{B}}
.
Subsequently, a new proof was given by Keevash and Mubayi
[
18
]

who also significantly lowered the constraint for
n
:

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{B}})=\lfloor{n\over 3}\rfloor\lfloor{{n+1}\over 3}\rfloor\lfloor{{n+2}\over 3}\rfloor\quad{\rm for\,\,}n\geq 33,

with equality only for the balanced complete 3-partite hypergraph.
They also proved that the extremal family is rather stable, namely
for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0

such that if
{\mathcal{H}}
is a
{\mathbf{B}}
-free triple system on
n
vertices
with at least
(1-\delta)\frac{1}{27}n^{3}
edges, then there exists a
partition of the vertex set of
\mathcal{H}
as

V({\mathcal{H}})=V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}
so that all but at most

\varepsilon n^{3}
edges of
\mathcal{H}
have one vertex in each
V_{i}
.

The value of
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{C}})
is not known, it is a famous problem
proposed by Erdős and Sós
[
8
]
that is thought to be
difficult. Frankl and Füredi
[
11
]
gave a recursive
construction based on the design
S_{2}(6,3,2)
, yielding the best known lower bound
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{C}})\geq\frac{2}{7}{n\choose 3}\bigl(1-o(1)\bigr)
. An upper bound with
\frac{1}{3}
instead of
\frac{2}{7}
was proved by de Caen
[
5
]
and Sidorenko
[
21
]
. A sequence of small improvements of the coefficient
\frac{1}{3}
followed. The current record, obtained by Falgas-Ravry and Vaughan
[
9
]
using semi-definite programming, is slightly less than
0.287
(close to but still larger than
\frac{2}{7}
). Thus

\frac{2}{7}{n\choose 3}\bigl(1-o(1)\bigr)\leq{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{C}})\leq 0.287{n\choose 3}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

Configuration
{\mathbf{D}}
is the most restrictive in terms of the largest size of a triple system excluding it: the upper bound
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{D}})\leq\frac{n(n-1)}{3}
follows from Theorem 3.8 of Frankl and Füredi
[
12
]
.
Determining the exact value of
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{D}})
takes more work, which we carry out below in Section
5
.

4
Excluding
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}

Concerning
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}})
we prove a stronger statement.

Define configuration
{\mathbf{A}}^{+}
as a six vertex triple system of four edges
with
V=\{1,\dots,6\},E=\{123,124,135,236\}.

This is the same as
{\mathbf{A}}
with the middle triangle
123
as an additional triple.
Similarly, configuration
{\mathbf{B}}^{+}
on five vertices
is the same as
{\mathbf{B}}
with one more central triple
123
, i.e.,

V=\{1,\dots,5\},E=\{123,125,134,234\}.

We obviously have
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}})\leq{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}^{+}{\mathbf{B}}^{+})
. Since the full star contains neither configuration
{\mathbf{A}}
nor configuration
{\mathbf{B}}
, the following theorem implies the similar statement for
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}})
in place of
{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}^{+}{\mathbf{B}}^{+})
.

Theorem 2
.

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{A}}^{+}{\mathbf{B}}^{+})={n-1\choose 2}\text{ for }n\geq 5

and the only extremal family is the full star.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{[n]\choose 3}
be an optimal
{\mathbf{A}}^{+}{\mathbf{B}}^{+}
-free family.
Define
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
as the family of triples with an own pair,

{\mathcal{F}}_{1}:=\left\{xyz\in{\mathcal{F}}:\min\{\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(xy),\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(yz),\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(zx)\}=1\right\}
, and let
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}:={\mathcal{F}}\setminus{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
.
Our main observation is that for every
F=xyz\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
we have
\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(xy)=\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(yz)=\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(zx)=2
. Indeed,
by definition, all these three pairs have degrees at least
2
, so one can find triples

xya
,
yzb
,
zxc\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\{x,y,z\}\cap\{a,b,c\}=\emptyset
.
These three triples together with
xyz
form a configuration
{\mathbf{A}}^{+}
or
{\mathbf{B}}^{+}
unless
a=b=c
. We obtain that
a
is unique so
\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(xy)=2
, and similarly for
b
and
c
.

Define a nonnegative function
w(e,F):{[n]\choose 2}\times{[n]\choose 3}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows.
It is always
0
except when
e\subset F
,
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
, when it is
\frac{1}{\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(e)}
.
Note that here
\frac{1}{\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(e)}\geq\frac{1}{n-2}
.
For every pair
e\in\partial{\mathcal{F}}
we have

w(e):=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}}w(e,F)=\sum_{F:e\subset F\in{\mathcal{F}}}\frac{1}{\deg_{{\mathcal{F}}}(e)}=1.

For every
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
,
F=xyz
we have

w(F):=\sum_{e\subset F,|e|=2}w(e,F)=\frac{1}{\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(xy)}+\frac{1}{\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(yz)}+\frac{1}{\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(zx)}\geq 1+\frac{2}{n-2}=\frac{n}{n-2}.

For each
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
we have
w(F)=\frac{3}{2}
. Assuming that
n\geq 6
, this is at least
\frac{n}{n-2}
, hence

In case of

If

5
Excluding
{\mathbf{D}}
(or
{\mathbf{B}}
and
{\mathbf{D}}
)

As mentioned, the upper bound

follows from

]
. Here we conduct a careful analysis showing that this bound is either tight or nearly tight, depending on the residue of

modulo

. The same analysis also determines

. The following theorem summarizes our findings.

Theorem 3
.

There exists
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have:

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{D}})={\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{D}})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n(n-1)}{3}&\text{if }n\equiv 1,4\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)\\
\dfrac{n(n-1)}{3}-4&\text{if }n\equiv 7,10\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)\\
\dfrac{n(n-2)}{3}&\text{if }n\equiv 0,2,3,8\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)\\
\dfrac{n(n-2)}{3}-1&\text{if }n\equiv 5,11\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)\end{cases}

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{D}})=\frac{n(n-2)}{3}\text{ and }{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{D}})=\frac{n(n-2)}{3}-1\text{ if }n\equiv 6,9\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12).

Without the requirement
n\geq n_{0}
, the indicated quantities are always valid as upper bounds, and as exact values for
n\equiv 0,1,3,4\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
.

5.1
Preparations for the proof

In preparation for the proof, we develop or recall some tools that will be used in it. Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a triple system on a vertex set
V
,
|V|=n
. We call two triples in
{\mathcal{F}}

contiguous
if they share two vertices. The contiguity relation defines a graph on
{\mathcal{F}}
, which allows us to partition
{\mathcal{F}}
uniquely into its connected components with respect to contiguity:

{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}{\mathcal{F}}_{i}

Let us denote by
\partial{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
the shadow of
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
, i.e., the set of those pairs contained in some triple in
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
. Viewing the
\partial{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
as graphs, they are edge-disjoint.

Two special types of
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
serve as building blocks of
{\mathbf{D}}
-free triple systems. One, that we call a
cluster
, consists of
3
or
4
triples on
4
vertices; its shadow is a
K_{4}
graph. The other, that we call a
k
-
crown
, consists of some number
k\geq 1
of triples, all sharing a fixed pair of vertices
x
and
y
; its shadow is a book graph
B_{k}
on
k+2
vertices, consisting of
k
triangles sharing an edge. In the case
k=1
we get a single triple (triangle). The following easy-to-check fact is recorded here for later reference.

Observation 4
.

A triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
is
{\mathbf{D}}
-free if and only if each of its connected components
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
is either a cluster or a
k
-crown for some
k\geq 1
.

Thus, constructing a
{\mathbf{D}}
-free triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
on
V
amounts to packing edge-disjoint copies of
K_{4}
and some
B_{k}
’s in the complete graph
K_{n}
. As we are interested in the largest possible
{\mathcal{F}}
, we may assume that each copy of
K_{4}
contributes
4
triples to
{\mathcal{F}}
. The ratio
\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}|}{|E(\partial{\mathcal{F}}_{i})|}
is
\frac{2}{3}
in each cluster and less than
\frac{1}{2}
in each crown. Hence we would like to pack in
K_{n}
as many copies of
K_{4}
as we can, and use the leftover edges, if any, to add copies of some
B_{k}
’s if possible. The maximum number of copies of a fixed graph

that can be packed in
K_{n}
was determined for large enough

by Caro and Yuster
[
6
]
. Their main tool, which will also be ours, was the following decomposition theorem of Gustavsson, which extends Wilson’s theorem (see Theorem
23
below) from complete graphs to sufficiently dense ones.

Theorem 5

(Gustavsson
[
15
]
, Glock et al.
[
14
]
)
.

For every fixed graph

there exist
n_{0}=n_{0}(H)
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(H)>0
such that the following holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
and every graph
G
on
n
vertices with all vertex degrees greater than
(1-\varepsilon)n

There exists a decomposition of the edge set
E(G)
into copies of
H
if and only if the number of edges of
H
divides that of
G
, and the greatest common divisor of the vertex degrees in
H
divides that of the vertex degrees in
G
.

We will apply this theorem to

. In this case, the divisibility conditions become:
6||E(G)|
and
3|\deg_{G}(v)
for all
v\in V
. These conditions are satisfied by
G=K_{n}
if and only if
n\equiv 1,4\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
. For all (not just large enough)
n\equiv 1,4\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
, a full packing of copies of
K_{4}
in
K_{n}
is known to exist (Hanani
[
17
]
). When such a full packing does not exist, we will use a decomposition into
K_{4}
’s of a large subgraph
G
of
K_{n}
, specified by describing its complement
\overline{G}
. In terms of
\overline{G}
the divisibility conditions become:

|E(\overline{G})|\equiv{n\choose 2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,6)\text{ and }\deg_{\overline{G}}(v)\equiv n-1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)\text{ for all }v\in V.

The graphs
\overline{G}
that we will specify have constant maximum degree, which ensures that all vertex degrees in
G
will be greater than
(1-\varepsilon)n
for large enough

. The
K_{4}
-decomposition of

provided by Theorem
5
, plus some edge-disjoint

when available, will naturally define a large

-free triple system

. In most cases this

-free; when this is not the case we will modify

to get a slightly smaller triple system which is

Another useful way to analyze triple systems

is by describing their links. Recall that the link

of a vertex

is the graph on

whose edges are those pairs of vertices that form with

{\mathcal{F}}
. Here is another simple characterization of

Observation 6

A triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
is

-free if and only if each of its links

is a vertex-disjoint union of triangles and stars (including the cases of an isolated vertex or edge). For such

|{\mathcal{F}}|=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{x\in V}\bigl(n-1-\mathrm{st}({\mathcal{F}}[x])\bigr).

Note that this immediately implies the following upper bound:

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{D}})\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{n(n-1)}{3}&\text{if }n\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)\\
\dfrac{n(n-2)}{3}&\text{if }n\not\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)\end{cases}

In our detailed analysis, depending on the residue of
n
modulo
12
, we will construct
{\mathbf{D}}
(and even

8.2
The structure of nearly extremal triangle-free graphs

We will need the following lemma about triangle-free graphs on
n
vertices having at least
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
edges. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, two vertices
x,y\in V
are called
remote
if the distance between them in
G
is greater than
2
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a triangle-free graph with
|V|=n
and
|E|\geq\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
. Then either

1.

G
is bipartite, and if
|E|>\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
then no two vertices of the same part are remote, or

2.

G
is non-bipartite, and there are at most two pairs of remote vertices (none if
|E|>\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
).

Proof.

Our proof extends a classical one by Andrásfai, Erdős and Gallai (see
[
7
]
), who showed that a triangle-free graph with at least
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor+2
edges must be bipartite.

Suppose first that
G
is bipartite with parts
A
and
B
of cardinality
a
and
b
, respectively, where
a+b=n
,
a\leq b
. If some two vertices of the same part are remote, then at least
a
pairs in
A\times B
are non-edges in
G
. This implies that
|E|\leq a(b-1)\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
, proving the assertion in the first part of the lemma.

Next, suppose that
G
is non-bipartite, and let
C_{\ell}
be a shortest odd cycle in
G
, of length
\ell\geq 5
. To avoid a shorter odd cycle,
C_{\ell}
must be induced, and every vertex in
V\setminus V(C_{\ell})
can have at most two neighbors in
V(C_{\ell})
. Using these facts, and Turán’s theorem for the graph induced on
V\setminus V(C_{\ell})
, we get the upper bound

|E|\leq\ell+2(n-\ell)+\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-\ell)^{2}\rfloor.

This bound is a decreasing function of
\ell
, and for
\ell=7
it is strictly less than
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
. Hence we must have
\ell=5
, for which the bound becomes
|E|\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor+1
.

If
|E|=\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor+1
then
G
has the following structure: a
5
-cycle
C_{5}
, a complete bipartite graph
K_{\lfloor\frac{n-5}{2}\rfloor,\lceil\frac{n-5}{2}\rceil}
on
V\setminus V(C_{5})
, and every vertex of the latter is joined by edges to two non-adjacent vertices of the former. This graph is easily seen to have diameter
2
, i.e., no pair of remote vertices. If
|E|=\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor
then one of the two terms
2(n-5)
and
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-5)^{2}\rfloor
in the upper bound on
|E|
was off by
1
. If it was the former, then there is a unique vertex
x
in
V\setminus V(C_{5})
that has a single neighbor
y
in
V(C_{5})
. Denoting the two vertices of
C_{5}
at distance
2
from
y
by
z
and
w
, we see that there are at most two pairs of remote vertices,
\{x,z\}
and
\{x,w\}
. If it was the latter, then the triangle-free graph induced on
V\setminus V(C_{5})
has
\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-5)^{2}\rfloor-1
edges, one less than the Turán bound. This induced graph can only be of one of the following forms: the Turán graph minus an edge, or (when
n-5
is even) a
K_{\frac{n-5}{2}-1,\frac{n-5}{2}+1}
, or (when
n-5=5
) another copy of
C_{5}
. In each of these cases, one can check that there are at most two pairs of remote vertices.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be an
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{C}}
-free triple system on
n

10.1
Non-uniform triangle-free hypergraphs

Győri
[
16
]
proved his upper bound in the following more general form.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a triangle-free multi-hypergraph on
n\geq 100
vertices. Then

\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{H}}}\bigl(|E|-2\bigr)\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor.

(13)

In this formulation,
{\mathcal{H}}
may contain edges of different sizes, and may contain multiple copies of the same set of vertices. Such multiple copies are considered distinct for the purpose of forming a triangle, and are counted with multiplicity in the summation in (
13
).

Given an
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{D}}
-free triple system
{\mathcal{F}}
one can prove
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor
for
n\geq 100
using (
13
) as follows. Let
{\mathcal{Q}}:=\{Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{q}\}
be all the vertex sets of
{\mathbf{C}}
configurations in
{\mathcal{F}}
, i.e.,
|Q_{i}|=4
and at least three triples within
Q_{i}
are in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that distinct
Q_{i}
can share at most one vertex, otherwise we get a
{\mathbf{D}}
configuration. Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the multi-hypergraph having the following edges:
two copies of each
Q_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,q
and one copy of each
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
F\not\subseteq Q_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,q
.
Note that

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq 4|{\mathcal{Q}}|+|{\mathcal{F}}\setminus(\partial{\mathcal{Q}})|=\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{H}}}\bigl(|E|-2\bigr).

(14)

Indeed, the inequality holds because each
Q_{i}
contains at most
4
triples in
{\mathcal{F}}
. The equality holds because each
Q_{i}
contributes
\bigl(4-2\bigr)+\bigl(4-2\bigr)=4
to the sum, and every other triple in
{\mathcal{F}}
contributes

. Upon checking that
{\mathcal{H}}
is triangle-free, (
13
) implies the desired bound
\lfloor\frac{1}{8}n^{2}\rfloor
for the right-hand side of (

).

The above proof does not yield the extremal systems and only works for
n\geq 100
.

Definition 20
.

A (simple) hypergraph

is called a

-family on

if there is a partition

–

,
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{[n]\choose 3}
,
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{[n]\choose 2}
,

–

{\mathcal{H}}
is triangle-free, and

–

|H\cap H^{\prime}|\leq 1
for any two distinct members

.

Let

Proof.

Let

,
and

\sigma_{3}(n):=\max\{\sigma({\mathcal{H}}):{\mathcal{H}}

-family on
[n]
with
{\mathcal{Q}}=\emptyset\}
.
Since a complete bipartite graph can be considered as a

-family we have

.

(16)

Indeed,
\sigma_{3}(0)=\sigma_{3}(1)=0

,
\sigma_{3}(3)=\frac{7}{2}=\frac{1}{4}(3^{2}+5)

Define weights

if

,

if

, and finally

for edges in

.

So every triangle

and receives at least

Hence

\begin{split}\frac{7}{2}|{\mathcal{T}}|+|{\mathcal{E}}|&\leq\frac{7}{6}\left(3\ell+3{\ell\choose 2}\right)+\frac{5}{4}\ell(n-3\ell)+\frac{1}{4}(n-3\ell)^{2}\\

,

For

-free families we know the upper bound

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}})\leq{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}})=\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n-1)^{2}\rfloor.

The extremal construction for

– of the form
{\mathcal{F}}_{T}
for some tournament

– does contain
{\mathbf{B}}
configurations, so this bound cannot be exactly matched. But we do have an asymptotically matching construction.

Theorem 22
.

{\rm ex}(n,{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}})=\frac{1}{4}n^{2}\bigl(1-o(1)\bigr).

Our construction uses Wilson’s well-known decomposition theorem, which we recall now.

Theorem 23

(Wilson
[
22
]
)
.

For every fixed graph
H
there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(H)
such that the following holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
:

There exists a decomposition of the edge set
E(K_{n})
of the complete graph on
n
vertices into copies of
H
if and only if the number of edges of
H
divides
{n\choose 2}
, and the greatest common divisor of the vertex degrees in
H
divides
n-1
.

Proof of Theorem
22
.
For every integer
m\geq 2
we construct a graph
H_{m}
to which we will apply Theorem
23
. It has
m^{2}
vertices, partitioned into
{m\choose 2}
pairs
A_{1},\ldots,A_{{m\choose 2}}
and a set
B
of size
m
. The graph induced on each
A_{i}\cup B
,
1\leq i\leq{m\choose 2}
, is complete, and these are all the edges of
H_{m}
. Thus

•

|E(H_{m})|={m\choose 2}(2m+1)+{m\choose 2}={m\choose 2}(2m+2)=m(m-1)(m+1)
.

•

\gcd\bigl(\{\deg_{H_{m}}(v)\colon v\in V(H_{m})\}\bigr)=\gcd(m+1,m^{2}-1)=m+1
.

Let
n\geq n_{0}(H_{m})
be such that
2m(m-1)(m+1)
divides
n-1
. Then both divisibility conditions in Theorem
23
are satisfied, so we can find a decomposition of
E(K_{n})
into
\frac{1}{m(m-1)(m+1)}{n\choose 2}
copies of
H_{m}
.

In each copy of
H_{m}
in the decomposition, we place
{m\choose 2}m
triples of the form
A_{i}\cup\{b\}
where
1\leq i\leq{m\choose 2}
and
b\in B
. Within each copy, this triple system is triangle-free: it actually follows the construction presented before Theorem
1
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{m}
be the system formed by all these triples in all copies of
H_{m}
. Because the triples in a given copy use only edges of that copy of
H_{m}
, and distinct copies are edge-disjoint, triples in distinct copies can share at most one vertex. This immediately implies that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{m}
is
{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}}
-free. Suppose there is a
{\mathbf{B}}
configuration in
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{m}
. Then the two triples in it that share an edge must come from the same copy of
H_{m}
and be of the form
A_{i}\cup\{b\}
,
A_{i}\cup\{b^{\prime}\}
for some
b\neq b^{\prime}\in B
. The third triple contains
\{b,b^{\prime}\}
, which is an edge of the same copy of
H_{m}
. Thus the third triple could only come from this same copy, but no triple in the construction contains two vertices of
B
. We have shown that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{m}
is
{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}}
-free. The number of triples in it is

|{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{m}|={m\choose 2}m\cdot\frac{1}{m(m-1)(m+1)}{n\choose 2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{m}{m+1}{n\choose 2}.

When
m
and
n
both go to infinity, with
n
satisfying the above conditions with respect to
m
, namely
n\geq n_{0}(H_{m})
and
2m(m-1)(m+1)|n-1
, we have
|{\mathcal{H}}^{n}_{m}|=\frac{1}{4}n^{2}\bigl(1-o(1)\bigr)
. For sufficiently large
n
that does not satisfy the divisibility condition, we can find an

that does,

, and take the triple system
{\mathcal{H}}^{n^{\prime}}_{m}
, still of the required asymptotic size. ∎
