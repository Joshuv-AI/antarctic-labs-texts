From Kernel Methods to Neural Networks: A Unifying Variational Formulation
Michael Unser

Abstract
The minimization of a data-fidelity term and an additive regularization functional gives rise to a powerful framework for supervised learning. In this paper, we present a unifying regularization functional that depends on an operator L {\rm L} and on a generic Radon-domain norm. We establish the existence of a minimizer and give the parametric form of the solution(s) under very mild assumptions. When the norm is Hilbertian, the proposed formulation yields a solution that involves radial-basis functions and is compatible with the classical methods of machine learning. By contrast, for the total-variation norm, the solution takes the form of a two-layer neural network with an activation function that is determined by the regularization operator. In particular, we retrieve the popular ReLU networks by letting the operator be the Laplacian. We also characterize the solution for the intermediate regularization norms ∥ ⋅ ∥ = ∥ ⋅ ∥ L p \|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}} with p ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] p\in(1,2] . Our framework offers guarantees of universal approximation for a broad family of regularization operators or, equivalently, for a wide variety of shallow neural networks, including the cases (such as ReLU) where the activation function is increasing polynomially. It also explains the favorable role of bias and skip connections in neural architectures.

Mathematical Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

We shall consider multidimensional functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are indexed by the variable
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To describe their partial derivatives, we use the multi-index

{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
(where
\mathbb{N}
includes

) with the notational conventions
{\boldsymbol{k}}!=\prod_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}!
,
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
,

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{k_{i}}
for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\partial^{\boldsymbol{k}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\partial^{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})}{\partial^{k_{1}}_{x_{1}}\cdots\partial^{k_{d}}_{x_{d}}}
. This allows us to write the multidimensional Taylor expansion around
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
of an analytical function

explicitly as

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|=n}\frac{\partial^{\boldsymbol{k}}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})^{\boldsymbol{k}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!\lx@nobreakspace}

(1)

where the internal summation is over all multi-indices
{\boldsymbol{k}}
such that
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}=n
.

Schwartz’ space of smooth and rapidly decreasing test functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the usual Fréchet-Schwartz topology is denoted by
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Its continuous dual is the space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of tempered distributions. In this setting, the Lebesgue spaces
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
can be specified as the completion of

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the
L_{p}
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}}
, denoted as
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}})}
.
For the endpoint

, we have
\overline{({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}})}=C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\varphi\|_{L_{\infty}}=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})|
, which is the space of continuous functions that vanish at infinity. The continuous dual of
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}<\infty\}
of bounded
Radon measures with

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sup_{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|\varphi\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 1}\langle f,\varphi\rangle.

(2)

The latter is a superset of
L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is isometrically embedded in it, in the sense that
\|f\|_{L_{1}}=\|f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}
for all
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The Fourier transform of a function
\varphi\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}\{\varphi\}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle}{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(3)

Since the Fourier operator
\mathcal{F}
continuously maps
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into itself, the transform can be extended by duality to the whole space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of tempered distribution. Specifically,
\widehat{f}=\mathcal{F}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the (unique)
generalized Fourier transform
of
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if and only if
\langle\widehat{f},\varphi\rangle=\langle f,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle
for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\widehat{\varphi}=\mathcal{F}\{\varphi\}
is the “classical” Fourier transform of

defined by (
3
).

To control the minimal order
\alpha\geq 0
of decay (resp., the maximal rate of growth) of functions, we use the dual pair of spaces

L_{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
L_{\infty,-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\big(L_{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)^{\prime}
. These are the Banach spaces associated with the weighted norms

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{1,\alpha}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{\alpha}|f({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}

(4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{\infty,-\alpha}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\esssup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}}(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{-\alpha}|f({\boldsymbol{x}})|,

(5)

respectively.
Specifically, the inclusion

2.2
Admissible Regularization Operators

The regularization operators
{\rm L}
that are of interest to us are linear, shift-invariant (LSI), and isotropic. Consequently, they are characterized by a frequency response

\widehat{L}
that is purely radial, with
\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|)
, where the radial profile
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous, symmetric function.
Our condition for admissibility is that
{\rm L}
be invertible in an appropriate sense.

Definition 1

(Spline-admissible operators with trivial null space)
.

An isotropic LSI operator
{\rm L}
has a trivial null space if its radial frequency profile
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}
does not vanish over
\mathbb{R}
. We then say that it is spline-admissible if

1/\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\rho_{\rm rad}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{1/\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}\}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
where the operator
\mathcal{F}^{-1}:L_{1}(\mathbb{R})\to C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is the classical inverse Fourier transform.

The typical scenario is
\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=(1+\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{2})^{\alpha/2}
with
\alpha\geq 1
, which results in a stable inverse operator
{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}
whose radially symmetric impulse response is the Bessel potential of order
\alpha
. These operators play a central in the theory of Sobolev spaces
[
21
]
.

Distribution theory allows us to go further and to invert operators with nontrivial null spaces, but only if the zeros of the frequency response are located at isolated points. When the operator is isotropic, this
reduces the options to the cases where
\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
has a (multiple) zero at
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
.
Specifically, we shall say that
{\rm L}
is of order
\gamma_{0}
if

|\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})|/\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{\gamma_{0}}=C_{0}
as
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|\to 0
. The second important parameter is the asymptotic growth exponent of
\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
. This is the largest index
\gamma_{1}
such that
|\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})|\geq C_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{\gamma_{1}},
for all
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|>R
. It determines the smoothness of the Green’s function of the operator.

Definition 2

(Spline-admissible operators with nontrivial null space)
.

An isotropic LSI operator
{\rm L}
with radial frequency profile
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}
is said to be spline-admissible with a polynomial null space of degree
n_{0}
if the following conditions are satisfied.

1.

The profile
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}
does not vanish over
\mathbb{R}
, except for a zero of order
\gamma_{0}\in(n_{0},n_{0}+1]
at the origin; that is,
|\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)|/|\omega|^{\gamma_{0}}=C_{0}
as
\omega\to 0
.

2.

There exists an order
\gamma_{1}>1
, a constant
C_{1}>0
, and a radius
R_{1}>0
such that
|\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)|\geq C_{1}|\omega|^{\gamma_{1}}
for all
|\omega|>R_{1}
(ellipticity).

3.

For all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
{\rm L}^{\ast}\{\varphi\}\in L_{1,n_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The connection between Condition 1 and the null space of
{\rm L}
will be explained in Section
2.3
.
Conditions 1 and 2 with
\gamma_{1}>1
ensure that
\rho_{\rm rad}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{1/\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}\}
, which is the generalized inverse Fourier transform of the distribution
1/\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}
, is identifiable as a continuous function
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. The order
\gamma_{1}
actually controls the degree of differentiability (Sobolev smoothness) of
\rho_{\rm rad}
.
Condition 3 is a mild technical constraint on the decay of
{\rm L}^{\ast}\varphi
; this constraint has not appeared to be a practical limitation so far. For instance, if
{\rm L}
is an ordinary differential operator (an arbitrary polynomial of the Laplace operator
\Delta
) then
{\rm L}^{\ast}\{\varphi\}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is included in
L_{1,m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. We use this third condition for the handling of fractional operators whose impulse response decays slowly.

An attractive class of admissible operators with
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}=\alpha
and
n_{0}=\lceil\alpha-1\rceil
are
the fractional Laplacians
(-\Delta)^{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}
with
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
whose frequency response is
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{\alpha}
. The inverse of the fractional Laplacian of order
\alpha
, which corresponds to a frequency-domain multiplication by
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{-\alpha}
, is denoted by
(-\Delta)^{-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}
. Both operators are part of the same family (isotropic LSI and scale-invariant), their distributional impulse response being given by

\displaystyle k_{\alpha,d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{\alpha}}\right\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\begin{cases}A_{\alpha,d}\;\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{\alpha-d},&\alpha-d,-\alpha\notin 2\mathbb{N}\\
B_{n,d}\;\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2n}\log(\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|),&\alpha-d=2n\in 2\mathbb{N}\\

(6)

with constants

A_{d,\alpha}=\frac{\Gamma\big(\tfrac{d-\alpha}{2}\big)}{2^{\alpha}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\big(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\big)}

and
B_{d,n}=\frac{(-1)^{1+n}}{2^{2n+d-1}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma\big(n+\tfrac{d}{2}\big)n!}

[
19

44
]
. The
kernel

can also be interpreted as the Green’s function of

.
In view of (
6

We note that the impulse response of the filtering operator

, which tells us that it decays asymptotically like

Functionally, this means that

2.3
Nontrivial Null Space and Related Projectors

A sufficient
condition for an LSI operator
{\rm L}
to annihilate the polynomials of degree
n_{0}
is
[
56

\displaystyle\partial^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{0}})=0,\mbox{ for all }{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\mbox{ with }|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}.

(7)

We also recall that the directional derivative of a function along the direction
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|=1

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}f={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}f=\xi_{1}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}f+\cdots+\xi_{d}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{d}}f.

(8)

The operator
{\rm D}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
is LSI with frequency response
\widehat{{\rm D}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=(\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}})
.
The
n
th iterate of
{\rm D}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
yields the
n
th derivative along
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
whose explicit expression in terms of partial derivatives is

\displaystyle{\rm D}^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{(\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}})^{n}\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|=n}\frac{n!}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!\lx@nobreakspace}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\partial^{\boldsymbol{k}}f({\boldsymbol{x}}),

(9)

where the right-hand side follows from the application of the multinomial expansion to

(\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}})^{n}=(\xi_{1}\mathrm{i}\omega_{1}+\dots+\xi_{d}\mathrm{i}\omega_{d})^{n}
.

For isotropic operators, the directional derivatives
{\mathrm{D}}^{n}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{0}})
do not dependent on the direction

and coincide with the radial derivatives
\widehat{L}^{(n)}_{\rm rad}(0)
.
In view of (

, (

) then has the radial equivalent

\displaystyle\widehat{L}^{(n)}_{\rm rad}(0)=\frac{{\rm d}^{n}\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(0)}{{\rm d}\omega^{n}}=0,\mbox{ for }n=0,1,\dots,n_{0},

(10)

which is much simpler to test. It follows that an operator whose radial frequency profile is
such that
|\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)|/|\omega|^{\gamma_{0}}=C_{0}
as

will annihilate all polynomials up to degree

.

The null space of a spline-admissible operator
{\rm L}
of order
\gamma_{0}
therefore consists of the polynomials of degree

when
\gamma_{0}
is an integer and
n_{0}=\lfloor\gamma_{0}\rfloor

. We shall represent these polynomials by expanding them in the monomial/Taylor basis

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}

with

We also add a topological structure by equipping the space with the

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}=\big\{p_{0}=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:\|p_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}}<\infty\big\}\mbox{ with }\|p_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\|(b_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\|_{2}.

(12)

To avoid a notational overload, we shall often denote this null space by

, with the convention that
{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}=\{0\}
when

(for the operators
{\rm L}
whose null space is trivial).
The important point here is that (
12
) specifies a finite-dimensional Banach subspace of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Its continuous dual
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
is finite-dimensional as well, although it is composed of “abstract” elements
p^{\ast}_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
that are, in fact, equivalence classes in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Yet, it is possible to
identify every dual element
p^{\ast}_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
as a true function by selecting a particular dual basis
\{m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\ast}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}
such that
\langle m^{\ast}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
(Kroneker delta). Our specific choice is

\displaystyle m^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\kappa_{\rm iso}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(13)

with
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, where
\kappa_{\rm iso}
is the isotropic function described in Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1

(adapted from
[
52
]
)
.

There exists an isotropic window
\kappa_{\rm iso}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that

\displaystyle\langle m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{n}}|}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}\kappa_{\rm iso}\rangle=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{n}}}

(14)

for all
{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, subject to the spectral constraints
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=1
for
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|<\frac{1}{2}
,
1\geq\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\geq 0
for
\frac{1}{2}<\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|<1
, and
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=0
for
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|\geq 1
.

This allows us to describe the dual space explicitly as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}_{n_{0}}=\big\{p_{0}^{\ast}=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}b^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}}m^{\ast}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:\|p_{0}^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}<\infty\big\}\mbox{ with }\|p_{0}^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\|(b^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\|_{2}

(15)

where each elements
p_{0}^{\ast}
has a unique representation in terms of its coefficients
(b^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}
.
We use the dual basis
\{m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\}
to specify the projection operator

\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}\{f\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\langle f,m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\rangle\;m_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(16)

which is well-defined for any
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
since
m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The “transpose” of this operator is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}\{\nu\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\langle m_{\boldsymbol{k}},\nu\rangle\;m^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(17)

which returns the projection of
\nu
onto
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}_{n_{0}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
under the implicit assumption that
\nu
has sufficient decay for
\nu\mapsto\langle m_{\boldsymbol{k}},\nu\rangle
to be well-defined—for instance,
\nu\in L_{1,n_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Correspondingly, we also have that

\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}\{{\rm L}^{\ast}\varphi\}=0
for all
\varphi
such that
{\rm L}^{\ast}\varphi\in L_{1,n_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
since
\langle m_{\boldsymbol{k}},{\rm L}^{\ast}\varphi\rangle=\langle{\rm L}m_{\boldsymbol{k}},\varphi\rangle=0
for
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}
. The latter manipulation of the duality product is legitimate in reason of the inclusion
{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}\subset L_{\infty,-n_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\big(L_{1,n_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)^{\prime}
.

Even though the null space of an admissible operator
{\rm L}
may be non-trivial, its intersection with
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is always
\{0\}
.
This implies that
{\rm L}^{\ast}={\rm L}
is injective on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
{\rm L}^{-1\ast}{\rm L}^{\ast}\varphi=\varphi
for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

where
{\rm L}^{\ast-1}={\rm L}^{-1}
is the LSI operator whose frequency response is
1/|\widehat{L}|
.

3
Radon Transform

The Radon transform extracts the integrals of a function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
over all
hyperplanes of dimension
(d-1)
. These hyperplanes are indexed over

\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{2}=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The coordinates of a hyperplane associated with an offset
t\in\mathbb{R}
and a normal vector
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
satisfy

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\xi_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\xi_{d}x_{d}=t.

Here, we first review the classical theory of the Radon transform
[
26
]
, starting with the case of test functions (Section
3.1
), and extending it by duality to tempered distributions (Section
3.2
).
Then, in Section
3.3
, we specify the Radon transform and its inverse on an appropriate class of intermediate Banach spaces
{\mathcal{Y}}
with
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm d.}}{\mathcal{Y}}\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm d.}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(Theorem
3
). Finally, in Section
3.4
, we provide the (filtered) Radon transforms of the dictionary elements—isotropic kernels and ridges—that are relevant to our investigation.

3.1
Classical Integral Formulation

The Radon transform of the function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\delta(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}},\quad(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(18)

The adjoint of
{\mathrm{R}}
is the backprojection operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
.
Its action on
g:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
yields the function

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g(\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(19)

Given the
d
-dimensional Fourier transform
\hat{f}
of
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one can calculate
{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
through the relation

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}{\rm d}\omega=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\hat{f}(\cdot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\}\{t\},

(20)

In other words, the restriction of
\hat{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
along the ray
\{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}:\omega\in\mathbb{R}\}
coincides with the 1D Fourier transform of
{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
, a property that is referred to as the
Fourier-slice theorem
.

To describe the functional properties of the Radon transform, one needs the
(hyper)spherical (or Radon-domain) counterparts of the spaces described in Section
2.1
. There, the Euclidean indexing with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
must be replaced by
(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
The spherical counterpart of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Correspondingly, an element
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is a continuous linear functional on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
whose action on the test function
\phi
is represented by the duality product
g:\phi\mapsto\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}
. When
g
can be identified as an ordinary function
g:(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mapsto g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}
, one can write that

\displaystyle\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

(21)

where
{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
stands for the surface element on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

The key property for analysis is that the Radon transform is continuous on
{\mathcal{S}}
and invertible
[
26
,
23
,
41
]
.

Theorem 1

(Continuity and invertibility of the Radon transform on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

The Radon operator
{\mathrm{R}}
continuously maps
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Moreover,
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{K}}=\mathrm{Id}\mbox{ on\lx@nobreakspace}{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where
{\mathrm{K}}=({\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}})^{-1}=c_{d}(-\Delta)^{\tfrac{d-1}{2}}
with
c_{d}=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d-1}}
is the so-called “filtering” operator, and
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}
its one-dimensional radial counterpart that acts along the Radon-domain variable
t
. These operators are characterized by their frequency response

\widehat{K}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=c_{d}\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{d-1}
and

\widehat{K}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=c_{d}|\omega|^{d-1}
, respectively.

It should be noted, however, that the operator
{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is not surjective, which means that not very hyper-spherical test function
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
can be written as
\phi={\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}
with
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. A necessary condition is that

\phi
be even, but this is not sufficient
[
20
,
26
,
23
]
. The good news, however,
is that the range of
{\mathrm{R}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is closed
in
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}={\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)
equipped with the nuclear topology of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

[
23
, p. 60]
. Since the underlying spaces are both Fréchet, this translates into the transform
\varphi\mapsto{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}
being a homeomorphism of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
onto
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
.

Corollary 1
.

The operator
{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
is a continuous bijection, with a continuous inverse given by
{\mathrm{R}}^{-1}=({\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}):{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

3.2
Distributional Extension

To extend the framework to distributions, one proceeds by duality. By invoking the property that

{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{Id}

on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we make the manipulation

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\langle f,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathrm{R}}\{f\},{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle{\mathrm{R}}\{f\},\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad},

(22)

with
\phi={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\in{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\subset{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\varphi={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{\phi\}
. Relation (
22
), which is valid in the classical sense for
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is then used as definition to extend the scope of
{\mathrm{R}}
for
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 3
.

The distribution
g={\mathrm{R}}\{f\}\in\big({\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}})\big)^{\prime}

is the (formal) Radon transform of
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big):\quad\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle f,{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{\phi\}\rangle.

(23)

Likewise,
\tilde{g}={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}^{\prime}
is the (formal) filtered projection of
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}:\quad\langle\tilde{g},\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle f,{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}\{\phi\}\rangle.

(24)

Finally,
f={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the backprojection of
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
if

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\quad\langle{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\},\varphi\rangle=\langle g,{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}.

(25)

While (
25
) identifies
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}
as a single, unique distribution in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, this is not so for (
24
) (resp., (
23
)), as
the members of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
(resp., of
\big({\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}})\big)^{\prime}
) are equivalence classes in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.
To make this explicit, we take advantage of the equivalence
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}=0\Leftrightarrow\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=0
to identity the null space of the backprojection operator as being

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}}=\{g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}):\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=0,\forall\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}\},

(26)

which is a closed subspace of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. It is then possible to describe
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
as the abstract quotient space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})/{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}}
. In other words, if we find a hyper-spherical distribution
g_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
such that
(
24
) is met for a given
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then, strictly speaking,
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
is the equivalence class (or coset) given by

\displaystyle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}=[g_{0}]=\{g_{0}+h:h\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}}\}.

(27)

Since
[g_{0}]=[g]
for any
g\in{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}
, we refer to the members of
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}
as “formal” filtered projections of
f
to remind us of this lack of unicity.

Based on those definitions, one obtains the classical result on the invertibility of the (filtered) Radon transform on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
26
]
, which is the dual of
Corollary
1
.

Theorem 2

(Invertibility of the Radon transform on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

It holds that

{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{Id}
on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
More precisely, the filtered Radon transform
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
is a continuous bijection, with a continuous inverse given by
({\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}})^{-1}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

To illustrate the fact that (
24
) does not identify a single distribution,
we consider the Dirac ridge
\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and refer to the definition (
18
) of the Radon transform to deduce that, for all
\phi={\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
with
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\langle\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot-t_{0}),{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}\{\phi\}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot-t_{0}),\overbrace{{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}}^{\mathrm{Id}}\{\varphi\}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}={\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(-t_{0},-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\big(\cdot+(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\big),{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle\delta\big(\cdot+(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\big),\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad},

which shows that
\delta\big(\cdot+{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}\big)
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}=(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
is a formal filtered projection of
\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})
. Moreover, since

\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})=\delta(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}+t_{0})
, the same holds true for
\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})
, as well as for

\delta_{{\rm Rad},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\big(\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})+\delta(\cdot+{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})\big)
, which has the advantage of being symmetric. While the general solution in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot-t_{0})\}=[\delta\big(\cdot\pm{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}\big)]
, we shall see that there also exists a way to specify a representer that is unique (i.e.,
\delta_{{\rm Rad},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}})
by restricting the
range of
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}
to a suitable space of measures.

The distributional extension of the Radon transform inherits most of the properties of the “classical” operator defined in (
18
).
Of special relevance to us is the quasi-commutativity of
{\mathrm{R}}
with convolution, also known as the
intertwining property
. Specifically, let
h,f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two distributions whose convolution
h\ast f
is well-defined in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{h\ast f\}={\mathrm{R}}\{h\}\circledast{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}

(28)

where the symbol “
\circledast
” denotes the 1D convolution along the radial variable
t\in\mathbb{R}
with
(u\circledast g)(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\langle u(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),g(t-\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\rangle
. In particular, when
h={\rm L}\{\delta\}
is the (isotropic) impulse response of an LSI operator whose frequency response
\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|)
is purely radial, we get that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{h\ast f\}={\mathrm{R}}{\rm L}\{f\}={\rm L}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\},

(29)

where
{\rm L}_{\rm rad}
is the radial convolution operator whose 1D frequency response is
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}
. Likewise, by duality, for
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
we have that

\displaystyle{\rm L}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\rm L}_{\rm rad}\{g\},

(30)

under the implicit assumption that
{\rm L}\{{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}g\}
and
{\rm L}_{\rm rad}\{g\}
are well-defined distributions.
By taking inspiration from Theorem 1, we can then use these relations for
{\rm L}={\mathrm{K}}=({\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}})^{-1}
to show that
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{K}}\{f\}={\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}=f
for a broad class of distributions. The first form is valid for all
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(Theorem
2
), but there is a slight restriction with the
second form (resp., third form), which requires that
{\mathrm{K}}\{f\}

(
resp.,
{\mathrm{K}}\{g\}
with
g={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
be well-defined in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. While the latter condition is always met when
d
is odd, it may fail
2
2

2

For
d=2n
even,
\widehat{K}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\propto\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{2n-1}
which is
C^{\infty}
everywhere, except at the origin where it is only
C^{2n-2}
. This means that
{\mathrm{K}}
can properly handle (and annihilate) polynomials only up to degree
2n-2
.
in even dimensions with distributions (e.g., polynomials) whose Fourier transform is singular at the origin.
The good news for our regularization framework is that these problematic distributions are either excluded from the native space or annihilated by
{\rm L}
, so that it is legitimate to write
that
{\rm L}_{\rm R}={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}{\rm L}={\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{K}}{\rm L}
, where the second form has the advantage that
{\mathrm{K}}
and
{\rm L}
can be pooled into a single augmented operator
({\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{L}})
. An alternative form is
{\rm L}_{\rm R}={\mathrm{Q}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}
, where
{\mathrm{Q}}_{\rm rad}={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}
is the radial Radon-domain operator whose frequency response is
\widehat{Q}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=c_{d}|\omega|^{d-1}\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)
.

3.3
Radon-Compatible Banach Spaces

Our formulation requires the identification of Radon-domain Banach spaces over which the backprojection operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
is invertible. This is a non-trivial point because the extended operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in Definition
3
is not injective. In fact, it has the highly non-trivial null space

{\rm ker}({\mathrm{R}}^{\ast})={\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}_{\rm Rad}
, which is a superset of the odd Radon-domain distributions
[
20
]
. Yet,
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
is invertible on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
and surjective on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(Theorem
2
).

To ensure invertibility, we therefore need to restrict ourselves to Banach spaces that are embedded in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
.
To identify such objects, we consider a generic Banach space

{\mathcal{X}}=({\mathcal{X}},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})
such that

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm d.}}{\mathcal{X}}\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm d.}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.
This dense-embedding hypothesis has several implications:

1.

The space
{\mathcal{X}}
is the completion of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in the
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
norm; i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}=\overline{\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\big)}.

(31)

2.

The dual space
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\xhookrightarrow{}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\sup_{\phi\in{\mathcal{X}}:\;\|\phi\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\leq 1}\langle g,\phi\rangle=\sup_{\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}):\;\|\phi\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\leq 1}\langle g,\phi\rangle,

(32)

where the restriction of
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
on the right-hand side of (
32
) is justified by the denseness of

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in
{\mathcal{X}}
.

3.

The definition of
\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
given by the right-hand side of (
32
) is valid for
any distribution
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\infty
for
g\notin{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
. Accordingly, we can specify the topological dual of

{\mathrm{L}}^{\dagger}_{\rm R}=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}}){\mathrm{L}}^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle e_{k}=\pm\rho_{\rm rad}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{k})\mp p_{0,k},

The two cases in Theorem

that are of direct practical relevance to machine learning are Items 1 and 3. The first scenario
yields a learning architecture that is
a linear expansion of RBFs, which also has a classical RKHS interpretation, as explained in Section

4.2
.

The form of the solution in Item 3 is equivalent to a shallow network with the weights (

It actually turns out that this normalization is inconsequential when the activation is a homogeneous function. This happens for instance when the regularization operator

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-b_{k}|^{\gamma_{0}-1}=\|{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|^{-\gamma_{0}+1}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{k}|^{\gamma_{0}-1}

(a_{k})
of the output layer. The case

{\rm L}=\Delta
(Laplacian) is particularly attractive, as it nicely maps into a ReLU network with one hidden layer and a skip connection to implement the affine component
[
36

.

The form of the solution in Item 2 is more involved, but still useful to get insight into the transition with
p
from RBFs to neural nets. The equivalence with Item 1 for

4.2

4.3
Universal Approximation Properties

The universal-approximation properties of the supervised-learning scheme specified by (

4.4
Regularization Operators for Antisymmetric Activations

While the framework that has been discussed so far is very powerful, it has one shortcoming: it yields canonical activation functions
\rho_{\rm rad}
that are necessarily symmetric. In some cases such as
{\rm L}_{\rm R}={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}(-\Delta)
, these can be converted into one-sided functions such as the ReLU by doctoring the polynomial term.
That said, the original scheme that is described by Theorem
5
does not allow for sigmoids, which are frequently used for classification
[
8
]
. This is the reason why we now introduce a variant that systematically produces anti-symmetric activations, including sigmoids for
n_{0}=0
.

The idea is to substitute the (symmetric) filtering operator
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}
by its anti-symmetric counterpart
\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}_{\rm rad}
, which includes an additional Hilbert transform. Specifically,
\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}_{\rm rad}
is the hyper-spherical radial filter whose frequency response is

\widehat{\tilde{K}}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=-\mathrm{i}\,{\rm sign}(\omega)c_{p}|\omega|^{-d-1}
and whose adjoint is
\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}^{\ast}_{\rm rad}=-\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}_{\rm rad}
. Since the action of the (radial) Hilbert transform
\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rm rad}:\phi(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mapsto\phi\circledast 1/(\pi t)
is well-defined on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

with
\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}^{\ast}_{\rm rad}=-\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rm rad}=\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}^{-1}_{\rm rad}
, we have the identity
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rm rad}^{\ast}\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}_{\rm rad}^{\ast}\tilde{{\mathrm{R}}}=\mathrm{Id}
with
\tilde{{\mathrm{R}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}
. Accordingly, we can essentially replicate the whole mechanism of construction of spaces in Section
3.3
by subtituting
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
by
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\rm Rad}=\tilde{{\mathrm{R}}}({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rm rad}({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad})
, which is a space of odd functions that are smooth (
C^{\infty}
) and included in
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
for all
p\geq 1
. While the members of
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\rm Rad}
do not necessarily decay rapidly, the mapping
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}^{\ast}_{\rm rad}:\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is still guaranted to be an isomorphism (see
[
41
, Theorem 3.3.1, p. 83]
where similar arguments are used).
Under the assumption that the hyper-spherical norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is continuous on
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\rm Rad}
, we can readily adapt the proof
of Theorem
3
to establish the following.

The map

with

The underlying definition of the “oddified” backprojection operator

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{{\mathrm{R}}}^{\ast}\{g\},\varphi\rangle=\langle g,\tilde{{\mathrm{R}}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle\tilde{{\mathrm{H}}}^{\ast}_{\rm rad}\{g\},{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}

(63)

Likewise, by using the property that the Hilbert transform is a homeomorphism from

The bottom line is that the whole argumentation, including the critical Fourier-based proofs of Section

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathrm{L}}}_{\rm R}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{L}}=\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}},

(64)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\rm rad}(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{i}\,{\rm sign}(\omega)}{\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)}\right\}(t),

(65)

To answer the fundamental issue of the existence of a solution in Theorem

5.1
Kernel and Stability of Generalized Inverse Operators

Let
\nu:f\mapsto\langle\nu,f\rangle
be a linear functional that is acting on some Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
. We recall that
\nu
is weak*-continuous if and only if

\nu\in{\mathcal{X}}
, where
{\mathcal{X}}
is the predual of
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}

[
42
]
. The condition that
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is reflexive (i.e.,
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}={\mathcal{X}}
) is equivalent to the continuity of
\nu
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, which is the standard condition for analysis. However, when
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is not reflexive (e.g.,
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{M}}=(C_{0})^{\prime}
), the constraint of weak* continuity is stronger than continuity, contrary to what could be suggested by the qualifier “weak.” In that case, the predual space
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}
, which is continuously embedded in
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}
, turns out to be smaller than
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore, in order to establish the weak* continuity of the Dirac functionals
\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
for the scenarios in Theorem
5
, we need to show that
\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm R}}
, which can be reduced to proving that

{\rm L}_{\rm R}^{\ast\dagger}\{\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
.

Theorem 7

(Properties of the impulse response of
{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm R}^{\ast\dagger}={\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{L}}_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1\ast}
)
.

Let
{\rm L}
be an isotropic operator such that

\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|)
where
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and
\rho_{\rm rad}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{1/\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}\}(t)
. We consider two cases:

1.

Trivial null space: If
{\rm L}
satisfies the admissibility conditions in Definition
1
,
then
{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm R}^{\ast\dagger}={\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{L}}^{-1\ast}
and

\displaystyle\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{L}}^{-1\ast}\{\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\rho_{\rm rad}(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})

(66)

with
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Moreover,
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
for
{\mathcal{X}}=C_{0}
as well as
{\mathcal{X}}=L_{q}
with
q\in[1,\infty]
.

2.

Nontrivial null space: If
{\rm L}
satisfies the admissibility conditions in Definition
2
with a polynomial null space of degree
n_{0}
,
then

\displaystyle\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle={\rm L}_{\rm R}^{\ast\dagger}\{\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\rho_{\rm rad}(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})-\sum_{n=0}^{n_{0}}\frac{(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})^{n}}{n!}\big(\kappa_{\rm rad}\ast\partial^{n}\rho_{\rm rad}\big)(t)

(67)

with
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
for the same spaces as in Item 1, but with
q\in[2,\infty]
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\sup_{({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(1+|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}|)^{-n_{0}}\|\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty

(68)

for any

.

Proof.

To show that
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
, it is sufficient to prove that
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
since
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}
is in the range of the Radon transform by construction.

When the null space of
{\rm L}
is trivial,
{\rm L}
has a stable convolution inverse so that it suffices to show that
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in L_{q}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\cap C_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. To that end, we formally identify the isotropic distribution
\rho_{\rm iso}={\mathrm{L}}^{-1\ast}\{\delta\}={\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\{\delta\}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{1/\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|)\}
and apply Proposition
1
, which yields

\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})={\mathrm{R}}\{\rho_{\rm iso}(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\rho_{\rm rad}(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}).

Due to our assumptions, this
function is such that
\|\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{L_{1}}=\|\rho_{\rm rad}\|_{L_{1}}<\infty
for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Moreover, since
1/\hat{L}_{\rm rad}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
\rho_{\rm rad}
is bounded, continuous, and vanishing at infinity (by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma), which gives
\rho_{\rm rad}(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Since

\rho_{\rm rad}\in L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we readily deduce that
\rho_{\rm rad}(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in L_{q}(\mathbb{R})
for all intermediate
q\geq 1
, which then also yields
\rho_{\rm rad}(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in L_{q}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
because the surface of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is bounded.
Finally, since
\rho_{\rm rad}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and vanishing at infinity,
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is jointly continuous in

(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
and vanishing at
t\to\pm\infty
for all
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, which implies that
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.

For the more difficult case of a nontrivial null space, we invoke the Fourier-slice theorem to evaluate the 1D Fourier transform of
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
with
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
fixed as

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(\omega,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}\{\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})-\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\langle m_{\boldsymbol{k}},\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\rangle\;m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\}(\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}-\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{k}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}\;\widehat{m}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}(\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}-\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{k}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}\;(-\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}(\omega)}{\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}-\sum_{n=0}^{n_{0}}\frac{(-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})^{n}}{n!}(\mathrm{i}\omega)^{n}\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}(\omega)}{\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)},

(69)

where the simplification of the summation over
{\boldsymbol{k}}
results from the multinomial expansion

(-\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})^{n}=(y_{1}+\cdots+y_{d})^{n}=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|=n}\frac{n!}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
with

{\boldsymbol{y}}={\big(-\mathrm{i}\omega\xi_{i}x_{0,i}\big)_{i=1}^{d}}
. The delicate point here is that (
69

\gamma_{0}
at
\omega=

\gamma_{0}\leq n_{0}+1
ensures that there is a proper pole-zero cancellation: By recalling that

for
\omega\in\Omega_{0}=[-R_{0},R_{0}]
with

and setting

, which is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t_{0}}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{(-\mathrm{i}t_{0})^{n}}{n!}\omega^{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\left|(-\mathrm{i}t_{0})^{N+1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t_{0}}\right|\;\frac{|\omega|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|t_{0}|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\;|\omega|^{N+1}.

(70)

This then yields the estimate

\displaystyle|\hat{\nu}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(\omega,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}|^{n_{0}+1}}{C_{0}(n_{0}+1)!}|\omega|^{\epsilon}\quad\mbox{ as\lx@nobreakspace}\omega\to 0

\displaystyle\mbox{ with }\epsilon=n_{0}+1-\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\\
1-(\gamma_{0}-\lfloor\gamma_{0}\rfloor)\in(0,1),&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(71)

Since the denominator
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}
in (
69
) is continuous and non-vanishing away from the origin,

\hat{\nu}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is bounded on
\Omega_{0}
, and, by extension, on any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, since

|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}|=1
and
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}
is rapidly decreasing, there exists a constant

such that

for

First, if

\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}\in L_{q,\rm Rad}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
for
all

provided that

\gamma_{1}>1
. The explicit Radon-domain formula (

) with

is obtained by taking the 1D inverse transform of (

\tau_{0}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, which, in the non-trivial scenario, typically grows as
O(|t|^{\gamma_{0}-1})
. Our analysis shows that the second correction term in (
67
), which is unbounded at infinity as well, essentially neutralizes this growth. It is tempting to call this a miraculous cancellation.

The bottom line is that, for any
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the function

is continuous, bounded with a maximum that is proportional to
|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}|^{n_{0}}
(see (
81

p=\infty
), and vanishing at infinity.
This is a remarkable property that also guarantees the boundedness of
{\rm L}_{\rm R}^{\ast\dagger}:L_{1,n_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
, which is not obvious a priori.
The enabling ingredient there is (
68
), which ensures that the corresponding bounding constant in Theorem
8
is finite.
Indeed, since
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\geq|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}|
with equality if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}
are colinear, we have that

\displaystyle\|{\rm L}_{\rm R}^{\ast\dagger}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{-n_{0}}\|\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(1+|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}|)^{-n_{0}}\|\nu_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty.

Theorem 8

(Stability of Cartesian-to-Radon-domain mappings)
.

Let
h_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})={\mathrm{T}}\{\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\}
denote the generalized impulse response of the operator
{\mathrm{T}}:L_{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and let

\displaystyle C_{p,\alpha}=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{-\alpha}\|h_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{R})}.

(82)

1.

{\mathcal{X}}=C_{0}
: If
C_{\infty,\alpha}<\infty
, then
{\mathrm{T}}:L_{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C_{0,\rm Rad}
is bounded with
\|{\mathrm{T}}\|\leq C_{\infty,\alpha}
.

2.

{\mathcal{X}}=L_{p}
with

, then
{\mathrm{T}}:L_{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{p,\rm Rad}
is bounded with

\|{\mathrm{T}}\|\leq\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}C_{p,\alpha}
.

The same holds true for the adjoint
{\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}\to L_{\infty,-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|{\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}\|=\|{\mathrm{T}}\|
.

Proof.

The function
\big((t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),{\boldsymbol{x}}\big)\mapsto h_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is the Schwartz kernel of the operator
{\mathrm{T}}
, so that

\displaystyle|g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle=\big|{\mathrm{T}}\{f\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\big|=\big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\;f({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left((1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{-\alpha}\|h_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{L_{\infty}}\right)\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{\alpha}|f({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L_{\infty}}=\sup_{(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|

\displaystyle=C_{\infty,\alpha}\|f\|_{L_{1,\alpha}},

which is the desired bound for
{\mathcal{X}}=C_{0}
.

To handle the reflexive case
{\mathcal{X}}=L_{p}
, we consider the adjoint operator

{\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}
whose Schwartz kernel

since

is isometrically embedded in its bidual

The action of the adjoint operator is described as

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}\{g\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}h_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\;g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\rm d}t

which, with the help of Hölder’s inequality, yields the bound

\displaystyle\big|(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)^{-\alpha}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq S_{d}\;C_{p,\alpha}\;\|g\|_{L_{q}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})},

where

is the surface of the unit hypersphere

.
This proves the desired result with

Appendix A
Appendix: Duality Maps

The generalization of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for any dual pair
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})
of Banach spaces is
the so-called
duality inequality

\displaystyle\forall(f,x)\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}:\langle f,x\rangle_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\leq\|f\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\|x\|_{{\mathcal{X}}},

(86)

which is tightly linked to the definition of the dual norm given by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\|x\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\leq 1}\langle f,x\rangle_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}}.

(87)

Definition 4

(Strict convexity)
.

A Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
is said to be strictly convex if, for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\|x_{1}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\|x_{2}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}=1

and
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, one has that
\|\theta x_{1}+(1-\theta)x_{2}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}<1
for all

\theta\in(0,1)
.

It is obvious from (
87
) that (
86
) is sharp. Moreover, when
{\mathcal{X}}

is reflexive and strictly convex, there is a single element
x^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
(the Banach conjugate of
x
)
such that
\|x^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\|x\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
(isometry) and

\langle x^{\ast},x\rangle_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}}=\|x^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\|x\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
(sharp duality bound)
[
13
]
. This leads to the definition of the corresponding duality map
{\mathrm{J}}_{\mathcal{X}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\{x\}=x^{\ast}.

(88)

Since the dual of
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is strictly convex as well, we have that
{\mathrm{J}}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}={\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}:{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{X}}
with
{\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\{x^{\ast}\}=x
, where
(x^{\ast})^{\ast}=x\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime\prime}={\mathcal{X}}

is the unique Banach conjugate of
x^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
.

A relevant example of reflexive and strictly convex Banach space is
{\mathcal{X}}=L_{q}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
for
q\in(1,\infty)
.
Its topological dual is

{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=L_{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
p=q/(q-1)
being the conjugate exponent of
q
.
For that particular pair,
(
86
) reduces to the Hölder inequality for hyper-spherical functions.
From
[
13
, Chapter 4]
, the corresponding duality map
{\mathrm{J}}_{q}:L_{q}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to L_{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

is

\displaystyle{\mathrm{J}}_{q}\{\nu\}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\nu^{\ast}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\frac{\left|\nu({\boldsymbol{z}})\right|^{q-1}}{\|\nu\|^{q-2}_{L_{q}}}{\rm sign}\big(\nu({\boldsymbol{z}})\big),

(89)

which establishes a one-to-one isometric mapping between the spaces
L_{q}
and

L_{p}=(L_{q})^{\prime}
with the property that
{\mathrm{J}}^{-1}_{q}={\mathrm{J}}_{p}
.

Proposition 4

(Banach isometries)
.

Let
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})
be a dual pair of reflexive and strictly convex Banach spaces with corresponding duality map
{\mathrm{J}}_{\mathcal{X}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
. We consider two generic types of linear isometries.

1) One-to-one map: Let
{\mathrm{T}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathrm{T}}({\mathcal{X}})
be an injective operator whose inverse is denoted by
{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}
with
{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}{\mathrm{T}}=\mathrm{Id}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then,
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathrm{T}}({\mathcal{X}})=\{y={\mathrm{T}}\{x\}:x\in{\mathcal{X}}\}
equipped with the norm
\|y\|_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\|{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}\{y\}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is a Banach space. Its
continuous dual is the Banach space
{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}={\mathrm{T}}^{-1\ast}({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})
with

\|y^{\prime}\|_{{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}}=\|{\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}\{y^{\prime}\}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
, while the
corresponding duality map is

{\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}=({\mathrm{T}}^{\ast})^{-1}\circ{\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\circ{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}:{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}
.

2) Projection: Let
{\mathrm{P}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{U}}\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm iso.}}{\mathcal{X}}
be a continuous projection operator on
{\mathcal{X}}
with
\|{\mathrm{P}}\|=1
. Then,
\big({\mathrm{P}}({\mathcal{X}}),{\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})\big)=\big({\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\big)
is a dual pair of Banach subspaces with corresponding duality map

{\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{U}}}={\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}\circ{\mathrm{J}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\circ{\mathrm{P}}:{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We first recall that the dual of a reflexive and strictly convex Banach space is reflexive (by definition) and strictly convex as well.

1) Injective operator:
For the first property, we refer to
[
53
, Proposition 1]
. The key observation is that the operators
{\mathrm{T}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
and

{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}:{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
, as well as their adjoints, are isometries with

({\mathrm{T}}^{\ast})^{-1}={\mathrm{T}}^{-1\ast}
. The argument then primarily rests upon the duality inequality

\displaystyle\langle y^{\prime},y\rangle_{{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{Y}}}=\langle y^{\prime},{\mathrm{T}}{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}\{y\}\rangle_{{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}\{y^{\prime}\},{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}\{y\}\rangle_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\mathrm{T}}^{\ast}\{y^{\prime}\}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\;\|{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}\{y\}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}},

(90)

which is sharp if and only if

For the second part, let
u\in{\mathcal{U}}\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm iso.}}{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\|u^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\langle u^{\ast},u\rangle_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle=\langle u^{\ast},{\mathrm{P}}^{2}u\rangle_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{X}}}=\langle{\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}u^{\ast},{\mathrm{P}}{\mathrm{u}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{U}}}
