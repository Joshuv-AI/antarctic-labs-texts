Uniformly distributed sequences generated by a greedy minimization of the L 2 L_{2} discrepancy
Ralph Kritzinger

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to develop greedy algorithms which generate uniformly distributed sequences in the d d -dimensional unit cube [ 0 , 1 ] d [0,1]^{d} . The figures of merit are three different variants of L 2 L_{2} discrepancy. Theoretical results along with numerical experiments suggest that the resulting sequences have excellent distribution properties. The approach we follow here is motivated by recent work of Steinerberger and Pausinger who consider similar greedy algorithms, where they minimize functionals that can be related to the star discrepancy or energy of point sets. In contrast to many greedy algorithms where the resulting elements of the sequence can only be given numerically, we will find that in the one-dimensional case our algorithms yield rational numbers which we can describe precisely. In particular, we will observe that any initial segment of a sequence in [ 0 , 1 ) [0,1) can be naturally extended to a uniformly distributed sequence where all subsequent elements are of the form x N = 2 ​ n − 1 2 ​ N x_{N}=\frac{2n-1}{2N} for some n ∈ { 1 , … , N } n\in\{1,\dots,N\} . We will also investigate the dependence of the L 2 L_{2} discrepancy of the resulting sequences on the dimension d d .

uniform distribution modulo 1,
L_{2}
discrepancy, diaphony, van der Corput sequence, greedy algorithm

Introduction

The theory of uniform distribution modulo 1 dates back to the seminal work of Weyl
[
31
]
in 1916 and is concerned with the following elementary question: How does one construct a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of real numbers in the unit interval
[0,1)
such that for all numbers
N\geq 1
and all intervals
J\subseteq[0,1]
the difference

|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:x_{n}\in J\}-N|J||

is small or even bounded in
N
? For a fixed number
N
, it is not difficult to see that

\sup_{J\subseteq[0,1]}|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:x_{n}\in J\}-N|J||\lesssim 1

(
J
is always an interval) if we choose an equispaced set of points
x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{N}
; i.e.

|x_{n+1}-x_{n}|=\frac{1}{N}\text{\, for all \,}n=2,\dots,N.

However, as we are asked to build an infinite sequence, the number
N
increases and we cannot use equispaced point sets. In fact it is known from a celebrated result by Schmidt
[
25
]
that any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in the unit interval satisfies

\sup_{J\subseteq[0,1]}|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:x_{n}\in J\}-N|J||\gtrsim\log{N}

(1)

for infinitely many
N
. Schmidt’s result implies that it is not possible to construct a sequence in the unit interval which is distributed too regularly for all
N
simultaneously. Therefore, one speaks of irregularities of distribution.

It is known that Schmidt’s lower bound is sharp. Two classical and well-known constructions which attain the
(\log{N})
-rate in (
1
) are:

•

The
(n\alpha)
-sequence or Kronecker sequence, where
x_{n}=n\alpha\pmod{1}
for an irrational number
\alpha
with a bounded continued fraction expansion.

•

The van-der-Corput sequence as defined in Section
2
.

Analogue questions can be asked for sequences in the
d
-dimensional unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
too. By a famous result of Roth in 1954 it is known that for any sequence
(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
[0,1]^{d}
we have

\sup_{J\subseteq[0,1]^{d}}|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in J\}-N|J||\gtrsim(\log{N})^{\frac{d}{2}}

(2)

for infinitely many
N
. Roth’s lower bound has since then been improved by Beck
[
1
]
in dimension 2 and Bilyk, Lacey and Vagharshakyan
[
3
]
for
d\geq 2
, but the correct exponent of the
(\log{N})
-term is still unknown. The best known constructions of infinite sequences
(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfy upper bounds of the form

\sup_{J\subseteq[0,1]^{d}}|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in J\}-N|J||\lesssim(\log{N})^{d},

(3)

where all these constructions are based on number-theoretic methods like digit expansions. Many researchers in the theory of uniform distribution believe that the exponent
d
is best possible, while others conjecture that the exponent
\frac{d}{2}
in Roth’s lower bound should be replaced by
\frac{d+1}{2}
to get a sharp lower bound. Both conjectures are in accordance with Schmidt’s result for
d=1
. If we assume the exact exponent to be
\frac{d+1}{2}
rather than
d
(or at least some number smaller than
d
), then we need new constructions of extremely well distributed sequences in
[0,1]^{d}
.

Since the known algorithms based on digit expansions are not strong enough to yield such sequences, Steinerberger
[
26
,
27
]
recently proposed a novel method to construct sequences dynamically: Given an initial set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N-1}\}
of points in
[0,1)
, he constructs
x_{N}
in a greedy manner

x_{N}=\argmin_{x\in[0,1]}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}f(|x-x_{n}|)

(4)

for suitable functions
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, every subsequent element of the sequence is chosen dynamically such that a certain functional which depends on the already constructed points is minimized. Steinerberger proved that the resulting sequences are uniformly distributed and he performed numerical calculations which suggest that the sequences are very well distributed in the unit interval. He showed analogous results on higher dimensional sequences as well. Pausinger
[
21
]
recently proved that under certain conditions on the function
f
in (
4
) the resulting sequences are generalized van-der-Corput sequences, which demonstrates that the dynamically defined sequences have the potential to be very well distributed indeed.

We will adopt Steinerberger’s and Pausinger’s ideas and construct sequences in a greedy manner. We will present here one of our new results in dimension 1. We construct a sequence such that

x_{1}=\frac{1}{2};\qquad x_{N+1}:=\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\left(-2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\min\{x_{n},x\}+(N+1)x^{2}-x\right).

The motivation behind this construction is based on an
L_{2}
approach and will be explained in Sections
3
and
4
. The algorithm generates a sequence starting with the points

\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4},\frac{5}{6},\frac{1}{8},\frac{7}{10},\frac{5}{12},\frac{13}{14},\frac{1}{16},\frac{11}{18},\frac{7}{20},\dots

(5)

We will prove that the elements of this sequence are of the form

x_{N}=\frac{2n-1}{2N}\text{\, for some \,}n=1,\dots,N\text{\, for all \,}N\geq 1.

More generally, we find that independently of the points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
we start the algorithm with the subsequent elements of the sequence are all rational numbers of the above form. We will observe numerically that for this particular sequence the quantity

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:x_{n}\in[0,x)\}-Nx|

is very small for all
N
and seems to be even better behaved than for the van-der-Corput sequence. We view these results as an indication that the greedy approach is very effective.

We will prove that our sequence (
5
) satisfies

\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\#\{1\leq n\leq N:x_{n}\in[0,x)\}-Nx|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\sqrt{N},

which implies uniform distribution of the sequence in particular. We will further establish the same result for higher-dimensional sequences generated by new greedy algorithms.

Summarizing, the sequence (
5
) seems to have remarkable properties. It seems to improve upon classical constructions such as the van-der-Corput sequence with respect to uniform distribution and the established bound
\sqrt{N}
seems far from the truth, which is probably
\sqrt{\log{N}}
. In order to prove the latter bound (if it is true), it is necessary to understand why the sequence (
5
) works so well. Perhaps it is possible to find an explicit formula for the elements of the sequence or a simpler recursion, which enables a thorough analysis of the distribution properties of the sequence.

The rest of this paper will be structured as follows. In Section
2
we will give the formal definitions of the quantities of interest and state known results which are relevant in the context of the paper. In Section
3
, we explain how our greedy algorithms work. We construct sequences in
[0,1)^{d}
such that the inclusion of a new point
\boldsymbol{x}_{N+1}
always minimizes the
L_{2}
discrepancy of the initial segment
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{x}_{N+1}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}
are already constructed.
We will show that our greedy algorithms yield sequences that are uniformly distributed modulo 1. We analyze the one-dimensional case in more detail in Section
4
, where we are able to prove precise results on the structure of the resulting sequences. We investigate the dependence of the
L_{2}
discrepancy of our sequences on the dimension
d
in Section
5
and propose another greedy algorithm based on the weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy. We close with a conclusion in Section
6
.

2
Definitions and background

Now we give the necessary definitions and state results which are important in the context of the paper.

We consider an infinite sequence
\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{3},\dots\}
of points in the
d
-dimensional unit cube. Let
B
be a measurable subset of
[0,1]^{d}
. For a uniformly distributed sequence the number of points among the first
N
elements of
\mathcal{S}
which lie in
B
should be close to the Lebesgue measure
|B|
of
B
, multiplied with the total number of points. This motivates the definition of the local discrepancy of the first
N
elements of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to
B
by

\Delta_{N}(B,\mathcal{S}):=\#\{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}:\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in B\}-N|B|.

In the introduction we considered the supremum of the absolute value of the local discrepancy over all intervals
J\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
. In literature, this quantity is often called the (star) discrepancy of the sequence
\mathcal{S}
, which is formally given by

D_{N}(\mathcal{S}):=\sup_{J\subset[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{N}(J,\mathcal{S})|.

It is well known that is suffices to only consider intervals
J
which are anchored in the origin; i.e. intervals of the form
J=[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t})
,
where for
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
we set
[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t})=[0,t_{1})\times[0,t_{2})\times\dots\times[0,t_{d})
. In this case, we write
D_{N}^{*}
instead of
D_{N}
.

Clearly, it makes sense to measure the average behaviour of the local discrepancy by calculating its
L_{2}
norm. The corresponding quantity is called (star)
L_{2}
discrepancy and is formally defined as

L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S})=L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{star}}(\mathcal{S}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{N}([\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t}),\mathcal{S})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In the case of
L_{2}
discrepancy, it turns out that (in constrast to the case of supremum norm) we obtain two non-equivalent measures of distribution of sequences if we choose arbitrary subintervals
J\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
as test sets instead of intervals anchored in the origin. We therefore introduce the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy, where we use arbitrary subintervals
[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
of
[0,1]^{d}
as test sets. Here for
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
with
\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}
, i.e.
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
, we define
[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=[x_{1},y_{1})\times\dots\times[x_{d},y_{d})
. The extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy is then defined as

L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{S}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d},\,\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}}|\Delta_{N}([\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),\mathcal{S})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy uses periodic boxes as test sets, which can be introduced as follows: For
x,y\in[0,1]
set

I(x,y)=\begin{cases}[x,y)&\text{if $x\leq y$},\\
[0,y)\cup[x,1)&\text{if $x>y$,}\end{cases}

and for
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
as above define
B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=I(x_{1},y_{1})\times\dots\times I(x_{d},y_{d})
.
We define the periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy of
\mathcal{S}
as

L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{S}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{N}(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),\mathcal{S})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

It is known that in dimension 1 we have the relation
L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{S})^{2}=2L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{S})^{2}
for every sequence
\mathcal{S}\subset[0,1]
(see
[
10
, Theorem 7]
). Further, the periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy divided by
N
is (up to a
multiplicative factor) exactly the diaphony. The diaphony is a well-known measure for
the irregularity of the distribution of point sets and was introduced by Zinterhof
[
32
]
.

A sequence
\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{3},\dots\}\subset[0,1)^{d}
is called uniformly distributed modulo 1 if and only if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}:\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\}}{N}=|[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})|

for all intervals
[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
. It is well-known that a sequence
\mathcal{S}
is uniformly distributed if and only if

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}L_{2,N}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{S})=0\text{\, for any \,}\bullet\in\{\mathrm{star},\mathrm{extr},\mathrm{per}\}.

For the star
L_{2}
discrepancy see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 1.6, Theorem 1.8]
). For the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy we refer to
[
17
]
and for the periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy or diaphony consult
[
32
]
.

The
L_{2}
discrepancy of a sequence cannot be arbitrarily small. For any sequence
\mathcal{S}
in
[0,1]^{d}
we have

L_{2,N}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{S})\gtrsim(\log{N})^{\frac{d}{2}}\text{\, for infinitely many \,}N,

where
\bullet\in\{\mathrm{star},\mathrm{extr},\mathrm{per}\}
and where the implied constant depends only on
d
. We refer to
[
22
]
and
[
12
, Theorem 3, Theorem 5]
for the star and periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy. The claim for the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy can be found in the recent note
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, from which the lower bounds on the star and periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy follow as well, which both dominate the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy
[
10
, Equ. (1) and Theorem 6]
. For
\bullet\in\{\mathrm{star},\mathrm{extr}\}
it is known that there exist sequences which match these bounds, e.g. higher order digital sequences (see
[
6
]
) or Halton sequences for
d\geq 2
(see
[
16
]
).

We survey the case
d=1
in more detail. The van der Corput sequence
[
28
,
29
]
is a classical example of a so-called low-discrepancy sequence. It is defined as follows: If
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
has the dyadic expansion
n=\sum_{j=0}^{m}n_{j}2^{j}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and digits
n_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for
j=0,\dots,m
, set
\varphi(n):=\sum_{j=0}^{m}n_{j}2^{-j-1}
. Then the van der Corput sequence (in base
2
) is defined as
\mathcal{V}:=(\varphi(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. It is known (see
[
4
,
24
]
) that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{L_{2,N}(\mathcal{V})}{\log{N}}=\frac{1}{6\log{2}},

i.e.
L_{2,N}(\mathcal{V})=\mathcal{O}(\log{N})
, which is not best possible in
N
. However, a simple modification of the van der Corput sequence matches the optimal
L_{2}
discrepancy bound. For the symmetrized van der Corput sequence

\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}:=\{\varphi(0),1-\varphi(0),\varphi(1),1-\varphi(1),\varphi(2),1-\varphi(2),\dots\}

we have
L_{2,N}(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}})\lesssim\sqrt{\log{N}}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
(see
[
9
,
15
,
23
]
). Since the normalized periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy is up to multiplicative constants the diaphony, it holds
L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{V})=\sqrt{2}L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{V})\lesssim\sqrt{\log{N}}
for all
N\geq 1
(see
[
4
]
). A proof of the optimal extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy bound of the symmetrized van der Corput sequence is also possible via Haar functions; see
[
13
, Remark 39]
. Hence, the extreme and periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy do not require a symmetrization of the van der Corput sequence in order to achieve the best possible order in
N
.

As already mentioned in the introduction, the van-der-Corput sequence attains the best possible order of star discrepancy in
N
. More precisely, it is known (see
[
2
]
) that

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{D_{N}^{*}(\mathcal{V})}{\log N}=\frac{1}{3\log 2}.

3
Greedy algorithms and general upper bounds on the
L_{2}
discrepancy

In this section we will explain our new greedy algorithms and establish the general
L_{2}
discrepancy bound of order
\sqrt{N}
, which we announced in the introduction.

The idea is the following. We construct a sequence
\mathcal{S}
element by element such that the inclusion of the next element of
\mathcal{S}
always minimizes the
L_{2}
discrepancy. In the following, for sets
X
,
Y
(the latter totally ordered) and
M\subseteq X
and a function
f:X\to Y
we set

\argmin_{x\in M}f(x):=\{x\in M:f(x)\leq f(y)\text{\, for all \,}y\in M\}.

By
x^{*}\in\argmin_{x\in M}f(x)
we express that
x^{*}
may be chosen as any number in the set
\argmin_{x\in M}f(x)
.

Algorithm 1

For
\bullet\in\{\mathrm{star},\mathrm{extr},\mathrm{per}\}
let
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\bullet}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},...\}\subset[0,1)^{d}
be generated as follows:

1.

For some arbitrary integer
k\geq 1
choose
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}\subset[0,1)^{d}
arbitrarily.

2.

For
N\geq k
let
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\}
already be given. Choose

\boldsymbol{x}_{N+1}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}}L_{2,N+1}^{\bullet}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{y}\}).

(6)

Now we prove the following upper bound on the
L_{2}
discrepancy.

Theorem 1

For
\bullet\in\{\mathrm{star},\mathrm{extr},\mathrm{per}\}
a sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\bullet}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies

L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{*})^{2}\leq\max\{c_{d}^{\bullet},L_{2,k}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{P}_{k})^{2}\}\cdot(N-k+1),

(7)

for all
N\geq k
, where

c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}=c_{d}^{\mathrm{per}}:=\frac{1}{2^{d}}-\frac{1}{3^{d}}\text{\,\, and \,\,}c_{d}^{\mathrm{extr}}:=\frac{1}{6^{d}}-\frac{1}{12^{d}}.

In particular, the sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\bullet}
is uniformly distributed modulo 1.

We only show the result for
\bullet=\mathrm{star}
, since the proof of the other two cases follows similar lines, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots\}
be a sequence in
[0,1)^{d}
, where we write
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=(x_{n,1},\dots,x_{n,d})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we have

\displaystyle L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S})^{2}=\frac{N^{2}}{3^{d}}-\frac{N}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})+\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},x_{m,i}\}\right).

(8)

This formula follows by simple integration from the definition of the star
L_{2}
discrepancy and can also be found in
[
30
]
. It is often referred to as Warnock’s formula. Using (
8
) for
L_{2,N+1}(\mathcal{S})^{2}
, we can derive the recursive formula

\displaystyle L_{2,N+1}(\mathcal{S})^{2}=

\displaystyle L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S})^{2}+\frac{2N+1}{3^{d}}-\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})-\frac{N+1}{2^{d-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{N+1,i}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},x_{N+1,i}\}\right)+\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{N+1,i}),

(9)

which holds for all
N\geq 1
. The first expression in the right-hand-side of (
8
) for
L_{2,N+1}(\mathcal{S})^{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{(N+1)^{2}}{3^{d}}=\frac{N^{2}}{3^{d}}+\frac{2N+1}{3^{d}}.

Further we have

\displaystyle-\frac{N+1}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N+1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{N}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})-\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})-\frac{N+1}{2^{d-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{N+1,i}^{2})

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n,m=1}^{N+1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},x_{m,i}\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},x_{m,i}\}\right)+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},x_{N+1,i}\}\right)+\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{N+1,i}).

Inserting these expressions into Warnock’s formula (
8
) yields (
3
).

Now we prove (
7
) for
\bullet=\mathrm{star}
.
The assertion is trivially true for
N=k
. Assume it is true for some fixed
N\geq k
. For
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}

define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}):=

\displaystyle L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{*})^{2}+\frac{2N+1}{3^{d}}-\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})-\frac{N+1}{2^{d-1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-y_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},y_{i}\}\right)+\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-y_{i}).

By (
3
) we get that
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0
for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1]^{d}
. By definition of the sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}
and the induction hypothesis we deduce

\displaystyle L_{2,N+1}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\mathrm{star}})^{2}=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y})\leq\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\mathrm{star}})^{2}+\frac{1}{2^{d}}-\frac{1}{3^{d}}\leq\max\{c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}},L_{2,k}(\mathcal{P}_{k})^{2}\}\cdot(N-k+2).

We used
\int_{0}^{1}(1-y_{i}^{2})\,\mathrm{d}y_{i}=\frac{2}{3}
,
\int_{0}^{1}(1-y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}y_{i}=\frac{1}{2}
and
\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},y_{i}\}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-x_{n,i}^{2}\right)
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
in the second to last step. The proof is complete.

\hfill\Box

Warnock type formulas exist for the extreme and periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy too (see e.g.
[
10
,
11
,
17
,
20
]
), which can be used to show Theorem
1
for
\bullet\in\{\mathrm{extr},\mathrm{per}\}
. As a side result from the proof of Theorem
1
, we obtain the following relations:

\displaystyle\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}}L_{2,N+1}^{\bullet}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{y}\})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}}f_{N,d}^{\bullet},

where for
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in[0,1)^{d}
we define
f_{N,d}^{\bullet}:[0,1)^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle f_{N,d}^{\mathrm{star}}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{N+1}{2^{d-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-y_{i}^{2})+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1-\max\{x_{n,i},y_{i}\}\right)+\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-y_{i}),

\displaystyle f_{N,d}^{\mathrm{extr}}

\displaystyle:=\left(1-\frac{N+1}{2^{d-1}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}(1-y_{i})+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\min\{x_{n,i},y_{i}\}-x_{n,i}y_{i}\right),

\displaystyle f_{N,d}^{\mathrm{per}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x_{n,i}-y_{i}|+(x_{n,i}-y_{i})^{2}\right).

4.1
Greedy minimization of the star

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
be a point set in
[0,1)

y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq...\leq y_{N}

(10)

This formula can be derived directly from (
8
) for
d=1
and can also be found in
[
18
, Corollary 1.1]
.
We immediately conclude that for a fixed
N
the unique
N
-element point set in
[0,1)
with minimal
L_{2}
discrepancy is the centred regular grid; i.e. the point set

\Gamma_{N}:=\left\{\frac{2n-1}{2N}:n=1,2,\dots,N\right\}.

Therefore, for a fixed number
N
of points the best point distribution with respect to
L_{2}
discrepancy is known. However, constructing an infinite sequence such that the segment of the first
N

elements does have low discrepancy for all
N\geq 1
is a much more difficult problem. Therefore we utilize the greedy algorithm from the previous chapter. For
d=1
, Algorithm
1
can be written in a simplified form:
Let
\mathcal{S}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an infinite sequence in
[0,1)
. Then for every

L_{2,N+1}(\mathcal{S})^{2}=L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S})^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{2}-2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\max\{x_{n},x_{N+1}\}+(N+1)x_{N+1}^{2}-x_{N+1}+\frac{2N+1}{3}.

Clearly,

\argmin_{x_{N+1}\in[0,1)}L_{2,N}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N+1}\})^{2}=\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}f_{N}(x),

where
f_{N}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

f_{N}(x):=-2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\max\{x_{n},x\}+(N+1)x^{2}-x.

(11)

It is reasonable to choose
x_{1}
such that the
L_{2}
discrepancy of the one-element point set
\{x_{1}\}
is minimal. This is the case for
x_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
, as we see from equation (
10
), which leads to the following construction algorithm.

Algorithm 2

We construct a sequence

in

in the following way:

1.

Set

.

2.

For
N\geq 1
: Assume that the elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
are already constructed. Set

, where
f_{N}
as defined in (

).

Theorem 2

Let
\mathcal{S}^{*}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
2
. Then we have
x_{N}\in\Gamma_{N}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Further,
x_{N}
is different from all previous elements of
\mathcal{S}
.

The assertion is obviously true for
N=1
. Let
N\geq 1
. From the induction hypothesis we have that the elements
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
are pairwise distinct and nonzero.
Define the intervals
I_{k}=[y_{k-1},y_{k}]
for
k\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
. By
I_{k}^{\prime}
we denote the interior of
I_{k}
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
. There clearly exists an index
l\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
such that
\gamma_{l}\in I_{l}^{\prime}
, since
\gamma_{k}\notin I_{k}^{\prime}
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
induces
\gamma_{N+1}\in I_{N+1}^{\prime}
.
For every
k=1,\dots,N+1
the function
\mathfrak{f}_{k}(x)
is differentiable on
I_{k}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{\prime}(x)=-(2k-1)+2(N+1)x
. Since
\mathfrak{f}_{k}\big|_{x\in I_{k}}
is a quadratic function defined on a closed interval and its graph is part of an upwardly open parabola, it can have its only global minimum either at
y_{k-1}
, at
y_{k}
or at
\gamma_{k}
in case that
\gamma_{k}\in I_{k}^{\prime}
, since
\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{\prime}(\gamma_{k})=0
. To be more precise, for
k\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
the minimum of
\mathfrak{f}_{k}\big|_{x\in I_{k}}
is at

\begin{cases}y_{k-1},&\text{if \,}\gamma_{k}\leq y_{k-1},\\
\gamma_{k},&\text{if \,}\gamma_{k}\in(y_{k-1},y_{k}),\\
y_{k}&\text{if \,}\gamma_{k}\geq y_{k}.\end{cases}

For
k=1
we can rule out the first case, whereas for
k=N+1
the third case cannot occur.
As a conclusion, we state that the arguments of the global minima of
f_{N}
are elements of the set
\Gamma_{N+1}\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
. Now we will rule out the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
as possible candidates for arguments of global minima. Assume that
\mathfrak{f}_{k}\big|_{x\in I_{k}}
(for some

) takes its minimum at
y_{k-1}
. Then
\gamma_{k-1}<\gamma_{k}\leq y_{k-1}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{f}_{k-1}\big|_{x\in I_{k-1}}
takes its minimum either at
\gamma_{k-1}
or at
y_{k-2}
and

cannot be the argument of a global minimum of
f_{N}
. If
\mathfrak{f}_{k}\big|_{x\in I_{k}}
for some

takes its minimum at
y_{k}
, then
\gamma_{k+1}>\gamma_{k}\geq y_{k}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{f}_{k+1}\big|_{x\in I_{k+1}}
takes its minimum either at
\gamma_{k+1}
or at
y_{k+1}
and
y_{k}
cannot be the argument of a global minimum of
f_{N}
. Hence,
f_{N}
takes its global minimum at an element
\gamma_{l}\in\Gamma_{N+1}
such that
\gamma_{l}\in I_{l}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\gamma_{l}
is also different from all previous points of the sequence.

Remark 1

Let us consider a modified version of Algorithm
2
, where we start the algorithm with
k\geq 2
points. Choose an arbitrary set
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset[0,1]
and for

as in Algorithm

. Then with the very same arguments as in the proof of Theorem

we can prove that

for all

. It is easy to see that a situation where elements of
\mathcal{P}_{k}
are equal does not cause any problems. The crucial observation in the proof of Theorem

is that the function

is differentiable for every

such that

and that its derivative does not depend on the already generated points. Therefore, regardless of which curious set of initial elements one likes to choose as input for the greedy algorithm, the subsequent elements
x_{N}
for
N>k
are all rational numbers of the form
x_{N}=\frac{2l-1}{2N}
for some
l\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. This fact is remarkable, since usually similar greedy algorithms tend to produce numbers which can only be given numerically and which depend heavily on the first
k
elements we provide as input for the algorithm.

We would like to learn more about the structure of the sequence
\mathcal{S}^{*}
. We prove that two consecutive elements of the first
N
elements of the sequence generated by Algorithm 1 can never be too close to each other (which demonstrates that there cannot occur clusters in initial segments of the sequence).

Theorem 3

Let
\mathcal{S}^{*}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm 1 and let the first
N\geq 1
elements
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
already be generated. Then we have
\min_{0\leq k\leq N}(y_{k+1}-y_{k})\geq\frac{1}{2N}
.

The assertion is clearly true for

. Assume that it is true for
M_{N}:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
.
It is easy to show that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{k-1}(x)=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{k}\left(x+\frac{1}{N+1}\right)+2\left(\frac{k-1}{N+1}-y_{k-1}\right)\text{\, for \,}k=2,\dots,N+1,\text{\, and}

(13)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{k+1}(x)=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{k}\left(x-\frac{1}{N+1}\right)+2\left(y_{k}-\frac{k}{N+1}\right)\text{\, for \,}k=1,\dots,N.

(14)

Now assume that
x_{N+1}=\gamma_{l}=\frac{2l-1}{2(N+1)}
for some

. Then
\gamma_{l}\in(y_{l-1},y_{l})
and

. We will show that

, which implies the assertion of the theorem. Assume that

(which is not possible if

lying in

\displaystyle=

Greedy minimization of the extreme

For

for
\bullet\in\{\mathrm{extr},\mathrm{per}\}
has the following form. Here we make the particular choice

. Note that

for every

Algorithm 3

We construct a sequence
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
[0,1)
in the following way:

1.

Set
x_{1}=0
.

2.

For
N\geq 1

x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
are already constructed. Set

g_{N}(x):=-Nx(1-x)+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\min\{x_{n},x\}-x_{n}x)

and

h_{N}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left((x_{n}-x)^{2}-|x_{n}-x|\right).

Note that the functional
g_{N}
stems from the greedy minimization of the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy, whereas
h_{N}
comes from the periodic

discrepancy. The equality

\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}g_{N}(x)=\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}h_{N}(x)

L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{S})^{2}=2L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{S})^{2}
for every sequence in
[0,1)
. Alternatively, it is not difficult to show directly that
g_{N}
and
h_{N}
share the same arguments of global minima by regarding the fact that
2\min\{x_{n},x\}=x_{n}+x-|x_{n}-x|
. A beautiful result by Pausinger
[

, Theorem 2.1]
immediately implies the following theorem.

Theorem 4

3
generates the van-der-Corput sequence in base 2, i.e.

for all

.

The sequence
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is defined by
x_{1}=0
and

x_{N+1}=\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}h_{N}(x)=\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(|x_{n}-x|)

with

Since
f(x)=f(1-x)
for all

,

is twice differentiable on
(0,1)
and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,1)
we can apply
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
and the result follows. Note that Pausinger’s theorem tells us even more: if we do not always choose the smallest argument of a global minimum of
h_{N}
, Algorithm 2 still produces a generalized van der Corput sequence. We refer to
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
for more details.

\hfill\Box

We will give a direct proof of this result, based on Pausinger’s ideas, in the Appendix.

Remark 3

Theorem
1
yields
L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\sqrt{2}L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\leq\sqrt{N/6}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, whereas Theorem
4
implies the optimal upper bound
L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\sqrt{2}L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{extr}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})\lesssim\sqrt{\log{N}}
. We can understand this result as an indication that the upper bounds in Theorem
1
are far from best possible and the
L_{2}
discrepancy is much smaller than the given bounds. However, in order to prove the better bounds one needs to know the structure of the sequences resulting from the greedy algorithms, which is probably a very difficult task to investigate; especially in dimension
d\geq 2
. Further, due the fact that Algorithm 2 leads to a sequence with the optimal order of extreme and periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy, we can conjecture that Algorithm
2
generates a sequence in
[0,1)
with the optimal order of star
L_{2}
discrepancy; i.e.
L_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}^{*})\lesssim\sqrt{\log{N}}
. From this point of view it is clear why Algorithm
2
does not generate the van der Corput sequence which fails to have the optimal order of
L_{2}
discrepancy.

Remark 4

Algorithm
3
does not have the nice property of Algorithm
2
that the resulting elements of the sequence are all rational numbers independently of the set of points we start the algorithm with, because the derivative of
g_{N}\big|_{x\in(y_{l-1},y_{l})}
depends on the already constructed points for
l\in\{2,\dots,N+1\}
.

5
Tractability

In this section we would like to mention connections of our results to the theory of information based complexity. In this field, one is for instance interested in the dependence of the discrepancy on the dimension
d
. In this section we would like to know the minimal number
N(d,\varepsilon)
such that there exists an
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
such that its normalized
L_{2}
discrepancy
N^{-1}L_{2,N}(\mathcal{P})
is smaller than a given
\varepsilon>0
. If
N(d,\varepsilon)
depends only polynomially on
d
and on
\varepsilon^{-1}
, one speaks of polynomial tractability. If there is no dependence on the dimension
d
at all, one speaks of strong polynomial tractability.

On average, the squared normalized
L_{2}
discrepancy of an
N
-element point set is

\int_{[0,1]^{Nd}}\left(N^{-1}L_{2,N}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{N}=\left(\frac{1}{2^{d}}-\frac{1}{3^{d}}\right)N^{-1}=c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}N^{-1}

(15)

(see e.g.
[
17
]
), which proves the existence of an
N
-element point set in
[0,1)^{d}
such that its normalized
L_{2}
discrepancy is bounded by
\sqrt{c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}}/\sqrt{N}
. Note that Algorithm
1
generates an infinite sequence where the segment
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\}
of its first
N
elements satisfies this bound for every
N\geq 1
(provided that we start the algorithm with a single element
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in[0,1]^{d}
such that
L_{2,1}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\})\leq\sqrt{c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}}
). Similar statements are true for the extreme and periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy.

The dependence on
d
in (
15
) seems to be very good, as
c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}
decreases exponentially in
d
. However, this positive interpretation of the formula is deceptive. As pointed out by Novak and Woźniakowski in
[
19
]
, the
L_{2}
discrepancy is not properly normalized in the following sense: the normalized
L_{2}
discrepancy of the empty set is given by

\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|0-|[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t})||^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}t_{1}^{2}\cdot\dots\cdot t_{d}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=3^{-d/2}.

Hence, it makes more sense to consider the quantity

N_{2}(d,\varepsilon):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}:\inf_{\mathcal{P}\in[0,1]^{d},\,\#\mathcal{P}=N}L_{2,N}(\mathcal{P})<\varepsilon 3^{-d/2}\},

which is often referred to as the inverse of the
L_{2}
discrepancy.
Since
\sqrt{c_{d}^{\mathrm{star}}}/\sqrt{N}<\varepsilon 3^{-d/2}
is equivalent to
N>\left(1.5^{d}-1\right)\varepsilon^{-2}
, it is impossible to derive polynomial tractability from the discrepancy bound in (
15
) with respect to the normalized error criterion
N_{2}(d,\varepsilon)
. It is known (see e.g.
[
5
, Proposition 3.58]
) that the normalized
L_{2}
discrepancy is not tractable at all; in particular we have

N_{2}(d,\varepsilon)\geq\left(\frac{9}{8}\right)^{d}(1-\varepsilon^{2}).

To overcome the bad dependency on
d
, Novak and Woźniakowski
[
19
]
introduced the concept of weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy, where groups of variables are weighted differently. For every subset
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1,\dots,d\}
one chooses a positive weight
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}
. For the definition of the weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy we refer to
[
5
,
14
,
19
,
20
]
. Here, we only remark that the choice
\gamma_{\{1,\dots,d\}}=1
and
\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}=0
for all
\mathfrak{u}\neq\{1,\dots,d\}
yields the standard star
L_{2}
discrepancy. A popular choice of weights are product weights, where one selects a non-increasing sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of positive weights and puts

\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}=\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\gamma_{j}.

If one chooses product weights, the weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy
L_{2,N}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathcal{S})
of the first
N
elements of a sequence
\mathcal{S}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=(x_{n,1},\dots,x_{n,d})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
is given explicitly by the Warnock-type formula (see
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
)

\displaystyle L_{2,N}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathcal{S})=

\displaystyle N^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{3}\right)-2N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{2}(1-x_{n,i}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\gamma_{i}(1-\max\{x_{n,i},x_{m,i}\})\right).

We formulate a greedy algorithm in the style of Algorithm
1
which is based on the weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy, and state a corresponding discrepancy result in the following theorem. The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 5

Let
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},...\}\subset[0,1)^{d}
be generated as follows:

1.

Choose
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in[0,1]^{d}
such that
L_{2,1}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})^{2}\leq c_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, where

c_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{2}\right)-\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{3}\right).

2.

For
N\geq 1
let
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\}
already be given. Choose

\boldsymbol{x}_{N+1}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}}L_{2,N+1}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{y}\})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}}f_{N,d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{y}),

(16)

where
f_{N,d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle f_{N,d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{y})=

\displaystyle-2(N+1)\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{2}(1-y_{i}^{2})\right)+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\gamma_{i}(1-\max\{x_{n,i},y_{i}\})\right)

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\gamma_{i}(1-y_{i})\right)

for
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
.

Then
L_{2,N}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})^{2}\leq c_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}N
for all
N\geq 1
.

Note that it is possible to find a point
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in[0,1]^{d}
such that
L_{2,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})^{2}\leq c_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, since the average of the squared weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy over all
N
-element point sets in
[0,1]^{d}
is exactly
c_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}N
(see e.g.
[
5
, Section 3.6]
). Since

c_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}<\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{2}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log{\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{2}\right)}}<\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}};

hence we have
L_{2,N}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})^{2}\leq c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}N
with a positive constant
c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
independent of
d
if

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}<\infty.

We have
L_{2,N}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})^{2}\leq c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime}d^{a}N
with positive constants
c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime}
and
a
independent of
d
if

\limsup_{d\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}}{\log{d}}<\infty.

Therefore, under suitable conditions on the weights we achieve (strong) polynomial tractability. Note that under the same conditions on the weights we obtain the same tractability notions for the normalized error criterion; i.e. the situation where we demand
L_{2,N}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\leq\varepsilon\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{3}\right)^{1/2}
, where
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\gamma_{i}}{3}\right)^{1/2}
is the normalized weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy of the empty set (see
[
19
, Section 7]
or
[
20
, Section 9.3]
).

6
Conclusion

We considered greedy algorithms where we choose
k\geq 1
elements in
[0,1)^{d}
; i.e. an initial set of points
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}\subset[0,1)^{d}
. The point
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
is then chosen such that a certain variant of
L_{2}
discrepancy of the point set
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}\}
is minimized. All subsequent elements of the sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are selected in the same way. We proved that all sequences we can generate with this method are uniformly distributed modulo 1 and satisfy
L_{2,N}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{S}_{d})\leq C_{d}\sqrt{N}
for a suitable notion of
L_{2}
discrepancy, where the positive constant
C_{d}
depends only on the dimension
d
and on the initial set of points
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
.

We proved precise results on the resulting sequences in the one-dimensional case, where we put most attention on cases where we start the algorithms with a single element
x_{1}\in[0,1)
. A greedy minimization of the star
L_{2}
discrepancy yields a natural extension of any initial segment
\mathcal{P}_{k}:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset[0,1)
to a uniformly distributed sequence, where
x_{N}=\frac{2n-1}{2N}
with some
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
for all
N\geq k+1
. We analysed the situation where
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{\frac{1}{2}\}
in more detail, where already for the first element the star
L_{2}
discrepancy is minimized. We proved that two consecutive elements in the first
N
elements of this sequence must always have a distance of at least
\frac{1}{2N}
. We also found numerically that the resulting sequence is likely to be low-discrepancy and might have significantly lower star discrepancy than the classical van der Corput sequence.

If we consider an algorithm based on a greedy minimization of the one-dimensional extreme or periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy, a result by Pausinger immediately yields that for the initial set
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{0\}
we obtain the van der Corput sequence or a permuted variant thereof. Therefore, this greedy algorithm indeed generates a low-discrepancy sequence. However, the situation for general
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is less clear than in case of a minimization of the star
L_{2}
discrepancy, since a greedy algorithm based on the extreme
L_{2}
discrepancy does not produce all rational points in general.

Besides the rough discrepancy bound
L_{2,N}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{S}_{d})\leq C_{d}\sqrt{N}
we were not yet able to prove better and more precise results on higher-dimensional sequences generated by our greedy algorithms in the order of
N
. We assume that they might be low-discrepancy as well. However, if we consider a greedy algorithm based on the weighted
L_{2}
discrepancy, under certain conditions on the weights the resulting sequence satisfies excellent
L_{2}
discrepancy bounds with respect to the dimension
d
for all
N\geq 1
simultaneously.

Summarizing, there remain many interesting open problems related to the present work, where we would like to point out Conjectures
1
and
2
from Section 3 in particular.

Appendix - A direct proof of Theorem
4

Although the proof of Theorem
4
is complete by simply citing Pausinger’s result, we would like to give a detailed and direct proof based on his arguments to give insight why exactly the van der Corput sequence is the output of Algorithm
3
. For a fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the function
G_{N}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

G_{N}(x):=-Nx(1-x)+2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\min\{\varphi(n),x\}-\varphi(n)x).

It is clear that we have to show that for all
N\geq 1
we have

\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}G_{N}(x)=\varphi(N)

(17)

in order to prove Theorem 2. We show (
17
) in Corollary
1
, Proposition
1
and Corollary
2
, which will conclude the proof. First, we prove two crucial properties of the function
G_{N}
:

Lemma 1

Let be
N\geq 1
and
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
maximal such that
2^{r}
divides
N
; i.e.
N=2^{r}m
for some odd integer
m\geq 1
.

1.

Then
G_{N}
is
2^{-r}
-periodic; i.e. for every
x\in[0,2^{-r})
we have
G_{N}(x+2^{-r}l)=G_{N}(x)
for all
l\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{r}-1\}
.

2.

We have
G_{m}(2^{r}x)=2^{r}G_{N}(x)
for all
x\in[0,2^{-r})
.

Consider the set
M_{N}:=\{\varphi(0),\varphi(1),\dots,\varphi(N-1)\}=\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{N}\}
, where
y_{1}<y_{2}<\dots<y_{N}
. Let
y_{i}\in[0,2^{-r})
; then
y_{i}=\varphi(2^{r}t)
for some
t\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\{\varphi(2^{r}t+s):s\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{r}-1\}\right\}=

\displaystyle\left\{\varphi(2^{r}t)+\varphi(s):s\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{r}-1\}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\varphi(2^{r}t)+\frac{w}{2^{r}}:w\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{r}-1\}\right\},

which implies that for every element
y_{i}\in M_{N}\cap[0,2^{-r})
also
y_{i}+2^{-r}w\in M_{N}
for all
w\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{r}-1\}
and that every element of
M_{N}
can be expressed that way. We use this fact to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\varphi(n)=

\displaystyle\sum_{w=0}^{2^{r}-1}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\left(y_{n}+2^{-r}w\right)=2^{r}\sum_{n=1}^{m}y_{n}+m\left(2^{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

and therefore

\sum_{n=1}^{m}y_{n}=2^{-r}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\varphi(n)-m\left(2^{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right).

(18)

Now choose an arbitrary
x\in[0,2^{-r})
. Let
k
be maximal such that
y_{k}\leq x
. Then we have

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\min\{\varphi(n),x\}=\sum_{n=1}^{k}y_{n}+(N-k)x

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}

\displaystyle\min\{\varphi(n),x+2^{-r}l\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w=0}^{l-1}\sum_{n=1}^{m}(y_{n}+2^{-r}w)+\sum_{n=1}^{k}(y_{n}+2^{-r}l)+(N-lm-k)(x+2^{-r}l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle l\sum_{n=1}^{m}y_{n}+m2^{-r-1}l(l-1)+2^{-r}kl+(N-k)2^{-r}l-lm(x+2^{-r}l)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\min\{\varphi(n),x\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-r}l\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\varphi(N)-\frac{lm}{2}+N2^{-r}l-lmx-2^{-r-1}l^{2}m+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\min\{\varphi(n),x\},

where we used (
18
) in the last line. From the last line follows immediately

\displaystyle G_{N}(x+2^{-r}l)=

\displaystyle-N(x+2^{-r}l)(1-x-2^{-r}l)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\min\{\varphi(n),x+2^{-r}l\}-\varphi(n)(x+2^{-r}l))=G_{N}(x),

as all terms that do not belong to
G_{N}(x)
cancel out.

We prove the second item. With the arguments and notations from above we have

\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\min\{\varphi(n),2^{r}x\}=\sum_{n=1}^{k}(2^{r}y_{n})+(m-k)2^{r}x=2^{r}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\min\{\varphi(n),x\}+(m-N)x\right)

and

\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\varphi(n)=\sum_{n=1}^{m}(2^{r}y_{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\varphi(n)-m\left(2^{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

by equation (
18
). That yields

\displaystyle G_{m}(2^{r}x)=

\displaystyle-m2^{r}x(1-2^{r}x)+2\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\min\{\varphi(n),2^{r}x\}-2^{r+1}x\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\varphi(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{r}\left(G_{N}(x)+Nx(1-x)-mx(1-2^{r}x)+2(m-N)x+2xm\left(2^{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{r}G_{N}(x),

and the proof is complete.

\hfill\Box

We immediately conclude

Corollary 1

For all
r\geq 0
we have
\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}G_{2^{r}}(x)=\Gamma_{2^{r}}.

We use Lemma
1
to obtain
G_{2^{r}}(x)=2^{-r}G_{1}(2^{r}x)=-x(1-2^{r}x)
for all
x\in[0,2^{-r})
. Hence,
x^{*}=2^{-r-1}
is the argument of the only global minimum of
G_{N}
in
[0,2^{-r})
. The rest follows by the periodicity property of
G_{N}
given in Lemma
1
.

\hfill\Box

We note that
\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}G_{2^{r}}(x)=2^{-r-1}=\varphi(2^{r})
, which proves (
17
) for powers of 2. Next, we show the result for odd integers
N\geq 1
. For
N=1
it is obvious. In the next proof, we employ the reasoning of Pausinger.

Proposition 1

For an odd integer
N\geq 3
we have (
17
).

Write
N=\sum_{j=1}^{k}2^{m_{j}}
, where
m_{k}>m_{k-1}>\dots>m_{1}=0
are integers. Set
N_{i}:=\sum_{j=i}^{k}2^{m_{j}}
for
i=1,\dots,k
and
N_{k+1}:=0
.

Now we can write
G_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{i}}}
,
where we set

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{i}}}(x):=

\displaystyle-2^{m_{i}}x(1-x)+2\sum_{n=N_{i+1}}^{N_{i}-1}(\min\{\varphi(n),x\}-\varphi(n)x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2^{m_{i}}x(1-x)+2\sum_{n=0}^{2^{m_{i}}-1}(\min\{\varphi(n+N_{i+1}),x\}-\varphi(n+N_{i+1})x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{2^{m_{i}}}(x-\varphi(N_{i+1}))+\underbrace{2^{m_{i}}\varphi(N_{i+1})(1-\varphi(N_{i+1}))-2\varphi(N_{i+1})\sum_{n=0}^{2^{m_{i}}-1}\varphi(n)}_{\text{independent of $x$}},

where in the last step we regarded
\varphi(n+N_{i+1})=\varphi(n)+\varphi(N_{i+1})
and applied some elementary algebra.
From the last equality together with Corollary
1
we conclude that

\displaystyle\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{i}}}(x)=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2^{m_{i}}}+\varphi(N_{i+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{l}{2^{m_{i}}}+\frac{1}{2^{m_{i}+1}}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{m_{j}+1}}:l=0,1,\dots,2^{m_{i}}-1\right\}.

It is now obvious that we have

\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{i}}}(x)\subset\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{i+1}}}(x)

for all
i=1,\dots,k-1
, which implies

\displaystyle\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}G_{N}(x)=

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{i}}}(x)=\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}\widetilde{G}_{2^{m_{1}}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{2^{m_{1}+1}}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{m_{j}+1}}\right\}=\{\varphi(N)\}.

Hence,
\min\argmin_{x\in[0,1)}G_{N}(x)=\varphi(N)
as claimed.

\hfill\Box

Corollary 2

Let
N=2^{r}m
with an integer
r\geq 1
and an odd integer
m\geq 3
. Then for
N
we have (
17
).

Since
G_{N}(x)=2^{-r}G_{m}(2^{r}x)
for
x\in[0,2^{-r})
by Lemma
1
, we derive from Proposition
1
that
G_{N}(x)
takes a unique global minimum in
[0,2^{-r})
at
x^{*}=\varphi(m)/2^{r}=\varphi(N)
, which proves the corollary.
