A Liouville theorem for the Neumann problem of Monge-Ampère equations Thanks: This work was supported by NSFC 11771237.


Abstract
. In this paper, we study the Neumann problem of Monge-Ampère equations in Semi-space. For two dimensional case, we prove that its viscosity convex solutions must be a quadratic polynomial. When the space dimension n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , we show that the conclusion still holds if either the boundary value is zero or the viscosity convex solutions restricted on some n − 2 n-2 dimensional subspace is bounded from above by a quadratic function.

1.
Introduction

The Bernstein theorem for Monge-Ampère equation
\operatorname{det}D^{2}u(x)=1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
concludes that its solution must be a
quadratic polynomial, which was proved in

[
14
,
8
,
21
,
22
,
9
]
. This type of theorems was extended to the Hessian equation
[
2
]
, special Lagrangian equation
[
25
]
, affine maximal hypersurface
equation
[
23
]
and more general Monge-Ampére equation
\operatorname{det}D^{2}u(x)=f(x)

[
6
,
7
,
10
,
18
]
, and it is called Liouville theorem if additional
assumption on the solution is necessary, just as the Liouville theorem for harmonic functions.
The Liouville theorem for Monge-Ampère equations in semi-space
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
with Dirichlet boundary value on
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
was studied in
[
17
,
20
]
.

In this paper, we study the Liouville theorem for the Neumann
problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}u(x)=1&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\\
D_{n}u(x^{\prime},0)=ax_{1}&\text{ on }\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.\end{cases}

Here and below,
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}:=\{(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;x_{n}>0\}
,

D_{n}u=D_{e_{n}}u=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}
,
a\in R
is a constant, and
e_{n}

is the standard unit vector on
x_{n}
-axis.

When
n\geq 3
, we need to assume that
u
is quadratic growth in the auxiliary subspace at the infinite, i.e.,

(1.2)

\lim_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n-2},z\to\infty}\frac{u(0,z,0)}{|z|^{2}}<\infty.

We use
\Theta
to denote the family of functions satisfying (
1.2
)
.

We will use the standard concept for (Alesandrov) generalized solutions and viscosity solutions to the first equation of (
1.1
). See the book
[
12
]
for example.
Caffarelli
[
3
]
indicated that the generalized solution and viscosity solution to the first equation of (
1.1
) are equivalent, which is proved in detail
in
[
12
]
. This fact will be used again and again. To describe the Neumann boundary value condition in a weak sense, we choose the viscosity solution.

We say that
u
is a viscosity subsolution ( or supersolution) of problem (
1.1
) if
u\in C(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}})
is a convex function,
and for any
x_{0}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}
and any convex function
\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}})
, the condition that
u-\varphi
has
a local maximun ( or minimum)
at
x_{0}
, implies

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}\varphi(x_{0})\geq(or\leq)1&if\;x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\\
D_{n}\varphi(x_{0})\geq(or\leq)\phi(x_{0})&if\;x_{0}\in\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.\end{cases}

If
u\in C(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}})
is both viscosity subsolution and viscosity supersolution, we call it a viscosity solution.

The main result of this paper is the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u
be a viscosity solution to problem (
1.1
). If either
n=2
, or when
n\geq 3
,
u\in\Theta
or
a=0
,
then
u
is a quadratic polynomial.

In a future paper
[
15
]
, we will use Theorem
1.1
and perturbation method to prove the global Schauder estimate for the viscosity solution to the Neumann problem of
Monge-Ampère equation
\operatorname{det}D^{2}u(x)=f(x)
in a bounded convex domain. Such estimate was obtained in
[
19
,
24
]
for Dirichlet boundary value problem.

Observe that the condition
(
1.2
) does not depend on the base point
0
, this is because

2u(a,b+z^{\prime\prime},0)\leq u(0,2z^{\prime\prime},0)+u(2a,2b,0)

by the convexity, and (
1.2
) is invariant after subtracting any linear functions.
Therefore, under a suitable linear transformation, our theorem actually applies to the following problem:

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}u(x)=c&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\\
D_{\alpha}u(x^{\prime},0)=\beta\cdot x^{\prime}+b&\text{ on }\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\end{cases}

where
c>0
and
b\in R
are constants, while
\alpha
and
\beta
are constant vectors such that
\alpha\cdot e_{n}>0
and
\beta\cdot e_{n}=0
.
In this situation, the assumption (
1.2
) changes to

(1.4)

\lim_{z^{\prime}\in H^{\prime\prime},z^{\prime}\to\infty}\frac{u(z^{\prime},0)}{|z^{\prime}|^{2}}<\infty

for some
n-2
dimensional subspace
H^{\prime\prime}\subset R^{n}
which is not parallel to

\beta
. And Theorem 1.1 is turned to

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in C(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}})
be a continuous, convex viscosity solution to problem (
1.3
). Suppose that either
n=2
, or when
n\geq 3
,
u
satisfies (
1.4
) or
\beta=0
,
then
u
is a quadratic polynomial.

Our start point is to consider the level set at boundary point. First, we need to prove that
\nabla u(0)
is well defined for a viscosity solution. Then the main task is
to study the geometry of the height section

(1.5)

S_{h}(0):=\{x\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}|\,u(x)<u(0)+\nabla u(0)\cdot x+h\}

by blowing down
u
at the infinite point of auxiliary subplane and then blowing up at origin. As a result, we will finally prove that
D_{n}u(x)

is a linear function.
The case
n=2
or
a=0
is much more easy, which will be proved in Remark
2.16
and Remark 8.2 respectively.

This paper is arranged as follows.
In section 2, we will show the basic properties for the viscosity solutions of problem (
1.1
)
and list a few well-known Lemmas to be used.
In section 3, we will prove
the strict convexity of solution to (
1.1
) away from the boundary and then introduce the linearized problem for
D_{n}u(x)

to obtain a boundary estimate of the normal derivatives of the solutions.
In section 4, we will study a general global strict convexity lemma and provide an example showing that the growth assumption for the strict convexity is optimal.
In section 5, we will prove the height section of solution to (
1.1
) has a good shape. In section 6, we will use the result for case
a=0
to prove a stability result for small
a
.
In section 7, we will prove the global uniformly convexity of solution to (
1.1
).
In the last section, we will improve the famous
C^{1,\alpha}
estimate techniques
[
3
,
5
]
,
so as to prove that the blow down-up limit has certain
C^{2}
regularity on a cone with vertex
0
,
which implies that
u
is a quadratic polynomial.

2.
Preliminaries

(or

The oscillation function of a function

(i=0,1,2)

Definition 2.1

(Good Shape)
.

We say that a section

is of good shape if it satisfies the following two property:

c\mathcal{P}S_{h}^{u}({0})\subset\mathcal{P}G_{h}^{u}(0)\cap(-\mathcal{P}G_{h}^{u}(0)).

We say that a closed set

Lemma 2.4
.

Given any convex set
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose the line
l(t):=x+te_{n}
intersects
E
at the points

. Then

(2.7)

|p_{n}-q_{n}|\cdot\operatorname{Vol}_{n-1}(\mathcal{P}E)\leq C\operatorname{Vol}(E).

In addition, if
\mathcal{P}E
is balance about
\mathcal{P}q
up to a constant
\kappa
, then we have the reverse inequality

(2.8)

|p_{n}-q_{n}|\cdot\operatorname{Vol}_{n-1}(\mathcal{P}E)\geq c\kappa\operatorname{Vol}(E).

Proof.

Consider the non-negative concave function defined on
\mathcal{P}E

L(x^{\prime})=\sup\{(y-z)\cdot e_{n}|\,y,z\in E,\ \text{ such that }\mathcal{P}y=\mathcal{P}z=x^{\prime}\}.

Then

\operatorname{Vol}(E)\sim||L||_{L^{\infty}}\operatorname{Vol}_{n-1}(\mathcal{P}E),

which yields (2.7). Now, suppose that
\mathcal{P}E
is balance about
\mathcal{P}q
up to the constant
\kappa
. By concavity
L(\mathcal{P}q)\geq c\kappa||L||_{L^{\infty}}
, and (

) follows.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
u\in C(\overline{B_{1}^{+}(0)})
be a convex function. Suppose that

0\leq u\leq\sigma_{1}(|x|)\text{ in }B_{c}^{+}(0)

and

\varlimsup_{t\to 0^{+}}D_{n}u(x^{\prime},t)\geq-\sigma_{0}(|x^{\prime}|),\forall(x^{\prime},0)\in\overline{B_{c}^{+}(0)}.

Then

(2.9)

|\nabla u(y)|\leq C\left(\inf_{|y|\leq t\leq c}\frac{2\sigma_{1}(1+t)}{t}+\sigma_{0}(|y^{\prime}|)\right)\text{ in }B_{c}^{+}(0).

Here we do not require
\sigma_{i}(0)=0\;(i=0,1).

Proof.

Given
y\in B_{c}^{+}(0)
,
c\leq\frac{1}{2}
, let
p\in\partial u(y)
. The assumption and the convexity implies that

p_{n}\geq-\sigma_{0}(|y^{\prime}|).

Suppose that
|p^{\prime}|>0
. Note that for every constant
a\in[0,1]
, the point
z=y+a|y|(\frac{p^{\prime}}{|p^{\prime}|},1)
is in
B_{1}^{+}(0)
.
Hence, by the convexity we have

a|y|(p_{n}+|p^{\prime}|)=p\cdot(z-y)\leq u(z)-u(y)\leq 2\sigma_{1}((1+a)|y|).

Letting
t=a|y|
, we obtain the desired (
2.9
). We may
ignore the estimation for
y\in\partial\Omega
, because
\nabla u(y)
in this paper is determined by taking the internal limit. (See
the above definition for
D_{n}u(y)
and Corollary
3.3
).

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be small constant,
u\in C(\overline{B_{1}^{+}(0)})
and
v\in C^{2}(\overline{B_{1}^{+}(0)})
be two convex functions. Suppose that

||u-v||_{L^{\infty}}\leq\epsilon

and

\varlimsup_{t\to 0^{+}}D_{n}u(x^{\prime},t)\geq D_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}},\forall(x,0)\overline{B_{1}^{+}(0)}.

Then

(2.10)

|\nabla u(y)-\nabla v(y)|\leq C_{v}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{ in }B_{c}^{+}(0),

where
C_{v}
is aconstant which may depends on
||v||_{C^{2}}
.

Proof.

Given point
y\in B_{c}^{+}(0)
. Consider the function

U^{y}(x)=u(x)+2\epsilon-v(y)-\nabla v(y)\cdot(x-y).

Then by the assumption we have

0\leq U^{y}(x)\leq 2\epsilon+v(x)+2\epsilon-v(y)-\nabla v(y)\cdot(x-y)\leq C_{v}|x-y|^{2}+4\epsilon,

and

\varlimsup_{t\to 0^{+}}D_{n}U^{y}(x^{\prime},t)\geq D_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}-D_{n}v(y^{\prime},0)\geq-C_{v}|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Regarding

as origin and applying Lemmas

and

Lemma 2.10

( Comparison principle for mixed problem)
.

Supposed that
\Omega
is a bounded convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
G_{1}\subset\partial\Omega

and
G_{2}=\partial\Omega\backslash G_{1}
are piecewise
C^{1}
smooth, and
\beta
is
inward vector fieled on
\partial\Omega_{2}
.
If

are continuous, convex function defined on
\overline{\Omega}
such that either

or

is
C^{2}
smooth, satisfying

(2.11)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}v<\operatorname{det}D^{2}u<\infty&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u<v&\text{ on }G_{1}\\
D_{\beta}v<D_{\beta}u&\text{ on }G_{2}\end{cases}

in the viscosity sense, then
u<v
on
\overline{\Omega}
.

Proof.

On the contrary to the conclusion, we suppose that
w=u-v
archives positive maximum value

at a point
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
. According to the assumption and
the first two inequalities in (
2.11
) we can take

such that

.
This means that
u-[v(x_{0})+\nabla v(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})+m]
archives
the maximum at
x_{0}
when

is
C^{2}
smooth, or
v-[u(x_{0})+\nabla u(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})-m]

archives the minimum at
x_{0}
when

is
C^{2}
smooth, which contradicts the third inequality in (
2.6
) by the definition of viscosity solution.

3.
Interior strict convexity and normal derivative estimate

In this section, we first prove that a viscosity solution to problem (

)

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
u
is a viscosity solution to problem (
1.1
).
Then the graph of
u
can’t contains any ray, and
u
is strictly convex away from the boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.

Proof.

If the graph of
u
contains a ray
l
and take

, the function

u^{y}(x)=u(x)-u(y)-\nabla u(y)\cdot(y-x)

is bounded in the tubular domain

and

\operatorname{det}D^{2}u^{y}=1\text{ in }E,

which contradicts Lemma
2.11
since

has infinite volume.

Suppose
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Let

(3.1)

h_{y}^{u}:=\sup\{h|\,S_{t}^{u}(y)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\ \forall t<h\}.

Since
u
is strictly convex in the interior of sections
[
3
]
,
it is enough for the strict convexity of

away from the boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
to show
h_{y}^{u}>0
for
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.

Otherwise, if

, the convex set

\Gamma:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}|\,u(x)=u(y)+\nabla u(y)\cdot(x-y)\}

should contain more than one point. Lemma
2.14
implies that the extremal point of
\Gamma
is not in interior.
There are two cases: either
\Gamma
contains a line
l
parallel to
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, or
\Gamma
contains a ray
l
starting from point
z=(z^{\prime},0)
on
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.
Both cases contradicts the previous fact that the graph of
u
can’t contains any ray.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
u
is a viscosity solution to problem (
1.1
) and
y\in\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Then

D_{n}u(y):=\overline{\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}}D_{n}u(y^{\prime},t)
is well-defined. Moreover,

(3.2)

D_{n}u(y)=ay_{1}

and

(3.3)

u(y+te_{n})\geq u(y)+tay_{1},\ \ \forall t>0.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume
y=0
. By Lemma
3.1
and the convexity of
u
,
D_{n}(te_{n})
are well-definend and continuous for

. Hence,

D_{n}u(0):=\overline{\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}}D_{n}u(te_{n})=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}D_{n}u(te_{n})

is well-defined.
Notice that
ay_{1}|_{y=0}=0
. Were if (
3.2
) false, then

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}D_{n}u(te_{n})<-2\epsilon

for some
\epsilon>0
. Consider the tubular domain

\Gamma=\{(x^{\prime},0)+st_{0}e_{n}|\,x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}_{r}(0),\ s\in(0,1)\}

and choose the function

where
t_{0}
and

are positive and so small that

D_{n}u(t_{0}e_{n})<-2\epsilon t_{0},\ \ 2\operatorname{Osc}_{B_{r}^{+}(t_{0}e_{n})}u\leq\epsilon t_{0}\text{ and }|a|r<\epsilon,

which implies

Notice that
v
is linear along the

direction and
u
is convex, we find that

is strictly greater than

on the boundary

will touch

by above at some point

.
Moreover,

which contradicts the definition of viscosity supersolution. Thus, (

∎

4.
Strict Convexity Lemma and Examples

Recalling that if
u\in C(\overline{B_{1}^{+}(0)})
is a convex function and satisfies

(4.1)

\lambda\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}u\leq\Lambda\text{ in }B_{1}^{+}(0),

we can naturally
define

D_{n}u(x^{\prime},0)=\varlimsup_{t\to 0+}D_{n}u(x^{\prime},t),\ \ \forall x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}_{1}(0).

See Lemma 3.2 for the proof.
In this section, we first study the strict convexity for such functions. The we provide an example showing that the growth assumption
for the strict convexity is optimal.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given positive constants
H,M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Suppose that
u\in C(\overline{B_{1}^{+}(0)})
is a convex function satisfying (
4.1
) and

D_{n}u(x^{\prime},0)\geq-M_{2}\text{ in }B^{\prime}_{1}(0).

There exists
\sigma=\sigma(H,M_{1},M_{2},\lambda,\Lambda,n)>0
such that if
u
satisfies

0\leq u(x_{1},x^{\prime\prime},0)\leq\sigma+M_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\text{ in }B_{1}^{\prime}(0),

then

\sup_{B_{1}^{+}(0)}u\geq H.

Proof.

Assume by way of contradiction that there exists
\sigma_{k}\to 0^{+}
and convex functions
u_{k}
defined on
B_{1}^{+}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfying

0<\lambda\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}u_{k}\leq\Lambda\text{ in }B_{1}^{+}(0),\ D_{n}u_{k}(x^{\prime},0)\geq-M_{2}\text{ in }B^{\prime}_{1}(0),

0\leq u_{k}(x_{1},x^{\prime\prime},0)\leq\sigma_{k}+M_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\text{ in }B^{\prime}_{1}(0),

and

\sup_{B_{1}^{+}(0)}u_{k}\leq H.

Applying Lemma
2.5
we see that
u_{k}
is locally Lipschitz with

||u_{k}||_{Lip(\overline{B_{4c}^{+}(0)})}\leq C(H+M_{2}).

Hence,

u_{k}(x)\leq\sigma_{k}+M_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+C(H+M_{2})|x_{n}|\text{ in }B_{4c}^{+}(0).

Let
h_{k}=\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\operatorname{diag}(1,h_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime},h_{k})
. Consider the functions

v_{k}(x)=\frac{u_{k}(\mathcal{D}_{k}x)}{h_{k}},\ x\in\Omega_{k}:=\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}B_{4c}^{+}(0).

Then
0<\lambda\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}v_{k}\leq\Lambda
in
\Omega_{k}
,

v_{k}(x)\leq\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}+M_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+C(H+M_{2})|x_{n}|\text{ in }\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}B_{1}^{+}(0)

and

D_{n}v_{k}(x^{\prime},0)\geq-M_{2}\text{ on }\mathcal{D}_{k}^{-1}B^{\prime}_{1}(0).

Similar to Lemma
2.5
, for any given
R>0
, we can prove that

||v_{k}||_{Lip(B_{R}(0)\cap\Omega_{k})}\leq C(H+M_{1}+M_{2})R^{2}.

Therefore,
v_{k}
is locally uniformly Lipschitz, and there is a subsequence that locally uniformly converges to
a convex function
v
defined on
\Omega:=[-4c,4c]\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Moreover, we have

(4.2)

0<\lambda\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}v\leq\Lambda,

and

(4.3)

v(x)\leq M_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+C(H+M_{2})|x_{n}|\text{ in }B_{4c}^{+}(0),\ D_{n}v(x^{\prime},0)\geq-M_{2}\text{ on }B_{R}^{\prime}(0).

Furthermore, (
4.3
) implies that

(4.4)

a(x_{1})=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{v(x_{1},0,t)}{t}

is well-defined, and

-M_{2}\leq a(x_{1})\leq C(H+M_{2}).

Again, we can consider the blow-up of
v
along axis
e_{1}
by taking

\bar{h}_{k}=2^{k},\;\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{k}:=\operatorname{diag}(1,\bar{h}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime},\bar{h}_{k}),\;\bar{v}_{k}(x)=\frac{v(\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{k}x)}{\bar{h}_{k}},\;x\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{-1}\Omega.

The limit
\bar{v}
still satisfies (
4.2
), (
4.3
). And (
4.4
) gives

\bar{v}(x_{1},0,x_{n})=a(x_{1})x_{n}.

Obviously,
\nabla\bar{v}(e_{n})\cdot e_{n}=a(x_{1})
. The non-negative function

w(x)=\bar{v}(x)-\bar{v}(e_{n})-\nabla\bar{v}(e_{n})\cdot x,

is zero on the line
te_{n}
.
This is,
w
is bounded on a convex domain with infinite volume. However,
0<\lambda\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}w\leq\Lambda
, which contradicts with Lemma 2.11.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Given positive constants
H
and
M_{1}
. Suppose
u\in C(\overline{B_{1}(0)})
is a convex function satisfying

\lambda\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}u\leq\Lambda\text{ in }B_{1}(0).

There exists
\sigma=\sigma(H,M_{1},M_{2},\lambda,\Lambda,n)>0
such that if
u
satisfies

0\leq u(x_{1},x^{\prime\prime},0)\leq\sigma+M_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\text{ on }B_{1}^{\prime}(0),

then

\sup_{B_{1}(0)}u\geq H.

Proof.

Assume by way of contradiction that
\sup_{B_{1}(0)}u<H
. Then, the assumption of Lemma
4.2
immediately implies that

u\in Lip(B_{c}(0))
and
|\nabla^{\prime}u(0)|\leq C(\sigma+\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}})
( See Lemma 2.6). After subtracting the supporting function

u(0)+\nabla u(0)\cdot x
at
0
,
u
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
4.1
with
M_{2}=\sigma+\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Applying Lemma
4.1
we
obtain a contradiction, completing the proof.
∎

We point that the constant
\sigma
in the growth assumption of Lemma 4.1 and 4.2 can not be allowed to be zero. Otherwise, the constant
H
may tend to
\infty
, yielding a contradiction.
Moreover, the following example shows that the growth exponent
2
is optimal.

Example 4.3
.

Suppose
n\geq 3
, constants
1<a,b<\infty
and
\delta>0
is small such that

\frac{1}{a}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n-2}\delta,\;1-\delta=\frac{1}{b}.

Let

E_{1}:=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:\;|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a}\geq|x_{1}|^{b}\},\ \ E_{2}:=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:\;|x_{1}|^{b}\geq|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a}\}.

Consider the function

(4.5)

W_{a,b}(x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a}+|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a-\frac{2a}{b}}|x_{1}|^{2},&x\in E_{1}\\
\frac{2b+a-ab}{b}|x_{1}|^{b}+\frac{ab-a}{b}|x_{1}|^{b-\frac{2b}{a}}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2},&x\in E_{2}\end{cases}

and

(4.6)

W(x)=(1+x_{n}^{2})W_{a,b}(x^{\prime}).

We will show that there is a
\rho=\rho(\delta)>0
such that
W
is a convex function in

R^{n-1}\times(-\rho,\rho))
satisfying

(4.7)

c(\delta)\leq\operatorname{det}D^{2}W\leq C(\delta),\ D_{n}W(x^{\prime},0)=0\;\text{in}\;R^{n-1}\times(-\rho,\rho).

Proof.

Notice that
W\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x^{\prime}=0\})
,
\nabla W(\{x^{\prime}=0\})\subset\{x^{\prime}=0\}
and the n-dim Hausdroff measure
of the set
\nabla W(\{x^{\prime}=0\})
is zero. The second equation in (
4.7
) is obvious.
Hence, it is enough to show that
W
is convex and satisfies the first inequality in (
4.7
) in the sense of (Alexandrov) generalized solution.
For this purpose, we need to calculate
\operatorname{det}D^{2}W
at any point
x^{\prime}\neq 0
.

It is easy to see that

D^{2}W=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(1+x_{n}^{2})D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}&2x_{n}D^{\prime}W_{a,b}\\
2x_{n}D^{\prime}W_{a,b}&2W_{a,b}\end{array}\right].

So the convexity of
W
and
\det D^{2}W
is reduced to the study of the matrix

\mathcal{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(1+x_{n}^{2})D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}-2W_{a,b}^{-2}x_{n}^{2}D^{\prime}W_{a,b}\otimes D^{\prime}W_{a,b}&0\\
0&2W_{a,b}\end{array}\right].

First, we assume
x^{\prime}\in E_{1}
. Then

|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a}\leq W_{a,b}\leq 2|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a},\ \ \forall x^{\prime}\in E_{1}.

Set the matrix functions

\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime}(x^{\prime\prime})=\operatorname{diag}\{|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a-\frac{2a}{b}},|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a-2}\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}\},\ \ \mathcal{K}_{1}(x^{\prime\prime})=\operatorname{diag}\{\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime},|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a}\}.

Then by the assumption on
a
and
b
we have

(4.8)

\operatorname{det}\mathcal{K}_{1}=|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2a(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{b}-\frac{n-2}{a})}\equiv 1.

Observing that
C(a,b)
can be written as
C(\delta)
, we have

0\leq 2W_{a,b}^{-2}D^{\prime}W_{a,b}\otimes D^{\prime}W_{a,b}\leq C(\delta)\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime},\ \ D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}\leq C(\delta)\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime},\ \ \forall x^{\prime}\in E_{1}.

Hence, in
E_{1}
we have

(4.9)

C(\delta)\mathcal{K}_{1}\geq\mathcal{A}\geq\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(1+x_{n}^{2})D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}-x_{n}^{2}C(\delta)\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime}&0\\
0&C(\delta)|x^{\prime\prime}|^{a}\end{array}\right].

Since
|x_{n}|\leq\rho
will be small, by (
4.8
)-(
4.9
) we can conclude the convexity of
W
and
the first inequality in (
4.7
) after we prove

(4.10)

D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}\geq\epsilon(\delta)\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime},\ \ \forall x^{\prime}\in E_{1}.

Denote

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{1}^{\prime}},\ X=|x^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}x^{\prime\prime},\ y=|x_{1}||x^{\prime\prime}|^{-\frac{a}{b}}

and

m=a[\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}+(a-2)X\otimes X]+(a-\frac{2a}{b})[\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}+(a-\frac{2a}{b}-2)X\otimes X]y.

By a direct calculation we have

(4.11)

\mathcal{B}_{1}^{-1}D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}\mathcal{B}_{1}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}2&2(a-\frac{2a}{b})yX\\
2(a-\frac{2a}{b})yX^{T}&m\end{array}\right].

Observe that

(4.12)

m-2(a-\frac{2a}{b})^{2}|y|^{2}X\otimes X=f_{1}\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}-f_{2}X\otimes X

where,
f_{1}=2a-\frac{2a}{b}
and

f_{2}=2(a-\frac{2a}{b})^{2}y^{2}-(a-\frac{2a}{b})(a-\frac{2a}{b}-2)y^{2}-a(a-2).

Notice that

\displaystyle 2(a-\frac{2a}{b})^{2}-(a-\frac{2a}{b})(a-\frac{2a}{b}-2)

\displaystyle=(a-\frac{2a}{b})(a-\frac{2a}{b}+2)

\displaystyle=a(a-\frac{2a}{b})(\frac{1}{n-2}-1)\delta

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Obviously,
|y|\leq 1
in
E_{1}
. Recall
\delta
is small,
a\geq\frac{2a}{b}-a\geq\frac{7}{4}>\frac{3}{2}
, thus we get

f_{1}-f_{2}\geq(a-\frac{1}{2})^{2}-(\frac{2a}{b}-a-\frac{1}{2})^{2}\geq c[a-(\frac{2a}{b}-a)]\geq c\delta a.

Since

\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}-X\otimes X\geq 0,

we obtain

m-2(a-\frac{2a}{b})^{2}|y|^{2}X\otimes X\geq ca\delta\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}.

Replacing this in (
4.12
) and using (
4.10
)-(
4.11
), we finally obtain (
4.10
).

Second, we assume
x\in E_{2}
. Then we have

c(\delta)|x_{1}|^{b}\leq W_{a,b}\leq C(\delta)|x_{1}|^{b}.

Denote

\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}\{|x_{1}|^{b-2},|x_{1}|^{b-\frac{2b}{a}}\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}\}\;\text{ and}\;\mathcal{K}_{2}=\operatorname{diag}\{\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\prime},|x_{1}|^{b}\}.

Then

\operatorname{det}\mathcal{K}_{2}=|x_{1}|^{2b(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{b}-\frac{n-2}{a})}\equiv 1.

Similar to the first case in
E_{1}
, it is enough to show that

(4.13)

D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}\geq\epsilon(\delta)\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\prime}\ \ inE_{2}.

Denote

\mathcal{B}_{2}=\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\prime}},\ Y=|x^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}x^{\prime\prime},\ y\ =|x_{1}|^{-\frac{b}{a}}|x^{\prime\prime}|

and

d=(2-a\delta)b(b-1)+a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})(b-\frac{2b}{a}-1)y^{2}.

By a direct calculation we obtain

(4.14)

\mathcal{B}_{2}^{-1}D^{\prime 2}W_{a,b}\mathcal{B}_{2}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d&2a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})yY\\
2a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})yY&2a\delta\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}\end{array}\right].

Write

d-2a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})^{2}y^{2}|Y|^{2}=g_{1}-g_{2}y^{2},

where

\displaystyle g_{2}

\displaystyle=a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})(b-\frac{2b}{a}-1)-2a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta b^{2}(2-a)(\frac{2}{a}+\frac{1}{b}-1)

\displaystyle\geq 0

Obviously,
|y|\leq 1
in
E_{1}
. If
\delta>0
is small, we have

\displaystyle d-2a\delta(b-\frac{2b}{a})^{2}y^{2}|Y|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq b^{2}\delta[(2-a\delta)+a\frac{2\delta}{n-2}(1-\frac{2}{a}-\frac{1}{b})-2a(\frac{2\delta}{n-2})^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq cb^{2}\delta,

which, together with (
4.14
), implies the desired (
4.13
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Suppose that
R>0
,
W
is given by (
4.5
)-(
4.6
), and consider the Dirichlet problem

(4.15)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}W_{R}^{+}=c(\delta)&\text{ in }B_{R}^{\prime}(0)\times[-\rho,\rho]\\
W_{R}^{+}=W&\text{ on }\partial(B_{R}^{\prime}(0)\times[-\rho,\rho]).\end{cases}.

It is known that there exists a convex solution
W_{R}^{+}
, which is still symmetric about
x_{n}=0
since so is
W
. This is to say

D_{n}W_{R}^{+}(x^{\prime},0)=0.

Comparison principle (Lemma
2.9
) implies

W_{R}^{+}\geq W\geq 0

if
c(\delta)>0
is small. The convexity means

W_{R}^{+}(x^{\prime},t)\leq\max\{W_{R}^{+}(x^{\prime},\rho),W_{R}^{+}(x^{\prime},-\rho)\}\leq 4W(x^{\prime},0),\ \ x\in B_{R}^{\prime}(0),t\in[-\rho,\rho].

and it follows that

W_{R}^{+}(te_{n})=0.

This mean
W_{R}^{+}
has the same asymptotic behavior around
0
as
W
.

5.
Good Shape Lemma

In this section, we assume that
u
is a viscosity solution to problem (
1.1
), satisfying (
2.6
) and the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
.
We are going to prove that its height section
S_{h}:=S_{h}^{u}(0)
(defined by (
2.4
)) is of good shape for large
h
,
then we will introduce the normalization family
(u_{h},S_{1}^{h})
of
(u,S_{h})
.

At first, we mention here that the good shape property is invariant under any linear transform of the form
\mathcal{A}=\operatorname{diag}\{\mathcal{A}^{\prime},a_{n}\}

with
c\leq a_{n},\;\operatorname{det}\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\leq C
.

Let
p_{h}
be the mass center of
S_{h}
, and consider the
sliding transformation

\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}x=x-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{p_{h}\cdot e_{i}}{p_{h}\cdot e_{n}}x_{n}e_{i}.

Take
y_{h}\in\overline{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}S_{h})}
such that

|y_{h}\cdot e_{1}|=\sup\{x\cdot e_{1}|\,x\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}S_{h})\}

and consider another sliding transformation

\mathcal{A}_{h}x=x-\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\frac{y_{h}\cdot e_{i}}{y_{h}\cdot e_{1}}x_{1}e_{i}.

Apply John’s lemma (Lemma
2.8
) to
\{x^{\prime\prime}\in R^{n-2}|\,(x_{1},x^{\prime\prime},0)\in\mathcal{A}_{h}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}S_{h})\}
,
we can find a new coordinates
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
which keeps
e_{1}
and
e_{n}
(or only
e_{n}
for
\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}
) invariant such that

\mathcal{D}_{h}B_{1}(q_{h})\subset\mathcal{A}_{h}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}S_{h}\subset\mathcal{D}_{h}B_{C(n)}(q_{h})\subset\mathcal{D}_{h}B_{2C(n)}^{+}(0),

where
q_{h}=\mathcal{A}_{h}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}p_{h}
,
\mathcal{D}_{h}:=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}(h),\cdots,d_{n}(h))
is the diagonal matrix satisfying

\Pi_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}(h)=\operatorname{det}\mathcal{D}_{h}=Vol(S_{h}).

It follows from Lemma
2.11
that
\operatorname{Vol}(S_{h})\leq Ch^{\frac{n}{2}}
. Denote

\tau_{h}=\frac{(\operatorname{det}\mathcal{D}_{h})^{\frac{1}{n}}}{h^{\frac{1}{2}}},\;\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}=\tau_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{h}:=\operatorname{diag}(\bar{d}_{1}(h),\cdots,\bar{d}_{n}(h)).

We first consider the rescaling
(u_{h},S_{1}^{h})
of
(u,S_{h})
given by

(5.1)

{u}_{h}(x)=\frac{u({\mathcal{A}}_{h}^{-1}\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}x)}{h},\ \ x\in S_{1}^{h}:=\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{h}S_{h},

then we have

(5.2)

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}^{*}B_{\tau_{h}}(x_{h})\subset S_{1}^{h}\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{h}^{*}B_{C(n)\tau_{h}}(x_{h})\subset\{x_{n}\leq 2\tau_{n}C(n)\}

for some
x_{h}
, where

A_{h}^{*}=\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{h}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{c}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{-1}\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}

is still a sliding transformation of the form
A_{h}^{*}x=x-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\beta_{h}^{i}x_{n}e_{i}
.

We use the notation
G_{1}^{h}:=S_{1}^{h}\cap\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
similar to (
2.5
). And we will simplify
{S}_{t}^{h},{G}_{t}^{h}
by
{S}_{t},{G}_{t}

when the dependence on
h
and
{u}_{h}

is self-evident from the context.
In Section 7, we will use the corresponding notations
S_{t}^{R},G_{t}^{R}
for the
(u_{R},S_{1}^{R})
as we just defined
S_{1}^{h},G_{1}^{h}
for
(u,S_{1})
.

Now, the Neumann problem (
1.1
) becomes

(5.3)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}{u}_{h}=1&\text{ in }{S}_{1}^{h}\\
D_{n}{u}_{h}=a_{h}x_{1}&\text{ on }{G}_{1}^{h}\\
{u}_{h}=1&\text{ on }\partial{S}_{1}^{h}\setminus{G}_{1}^{h}\end{cases}

where

(5.4)

a_{h}=\frac{\bar{d}_{1}(h)\bar{d}_{n}(h)}{h}a.

Recall that if
n\geq 3,

u\in\Theta
and (
2.2
) is satisfied. Let

\delta_{h}=\frac{K}{h},\ \bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}=\frac{C\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}^{\prime\prime T}I^{\prime\prime}\bar{\mathcal{D}_{h}}^{\prime\prime}}{h}.

We have

(5.5)

u_{h}(0,z,0)\leq z^{T}\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}z+\delta_{h},\;\forall z\in R^{n-2}

and

(5.6)

a_{h}^{2}\operatorname{det}\mathcal{Q}_{h}=C^{n}\tau_{h}^{-2n}\frac{(\operatorname{det}\mathcal{D}_{h})^{2}}{h^{n}}a^{2}\leq C.

If
h\geq 2K
, (
2.2
) also implies
d_{i}(h)\geq ch^{\frac{1}{2}}
for
2\leq i\leq n-1
. More precisely,

\Pi_{i=2}^{n-1}\bar{d}_{i}(h)\geq C\tau_{h}^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\operatorname{Vol}_{n-2}(S_{h}(0)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\geq Ch^{\frac{n-2}{2}}.

Therefore
\operatorname{det}\mathcal{Q}_{h}\geq c
and
0\leq a_{h}\leq C
. To conclude, we have obtained that

(5.7)

0\leq a_{h}\leq C,\ \ u_{h}\in\Theta(\delta_{h}),\ \ \forall h\geq 2K.

The Good Shape Lemma is stated as follows.

Theorem 5.1

(Good Shape)
.

For large
h
, the normalization
(u_{h},S_{1}^{h})
given by (
5.1
) satisfies

(5.8)

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{P}S_{1}^{h}(0)\subset C\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}(0)\text{ and }S_{1}^{h}(0)\subset B_{C(n)\tau_{h}}(0),

(5.9)

\displaystyle\ c\leq\operatorname{Vol}(S_{1}^{h}(0))\leq C\;\text{ and }\;c\leq\tau_{h}\leq C,

(5.10)

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}(0)\subset-C\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}(0).

We are going to prove the theorem by the coming three lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

The normalization
(u_{h},S_{1}^{h})
given by (
5.1
) satisfies (
5.8
) for large
h
. More precisely,

\mathcal{P}S_{1}^{h}(0)\subset(1+C|a_{h}|)\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}(0)\subset C\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}(0).

Proof.

If there exists an unit direction
e,e\bot e_{n}
such that

\frac{\sup\{s|\,se\in\mathcal{P}S_{1}^{h}(0)\}}{\sup\{s|\,te\in\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}(0),\ \forall t\in(0,s)\}}\geq M>1.

We assume that the maximum values are attained at points
y
and
z
respectively:

y=y_{e}e+y_{n}e_{n}\in\partial\overline{S_{1}^{h}(0)},\ \ z=z_{e}e\in\partial\overline{G_{1}^{h}(0)}.

It is clear that

(5.11)

u(z)=u(y)=1,\ \ y_{e}\geq Mz_{e},\ \ 0<y_{n}\leq C,

where we have used (
5.2
) for the last inequality.

Since the transforms
\mathcal{A}_{h}^{*}
leaves
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
invariant, we have
|z|\leq C
. Let

l_{y,0}=\{sy,\ s\in(0,1)\},

l_{z}^{e_{n}}=\{z+te_{n},\ t\in[0,\infty)\}.

There exists a point
p=sy=z+ty_{n}e_{n}\in l_{y,0}\cap l_{z}^{e_{n}}
with
s,t\in(0,\frac{1}{M}]
. By convexity, (
2.6
) and (
5.11
) we have

u(p)\leq su(y)+(1-s)u(0)\leq\frac{1}{M}.

However, Lemma
3.2
, (
5.11
), the convexity again, and the fact
|z|\leq C
implies that

u(p)\geq u(z)+ty_{n}D_{n}u(z)\geq 1+ta_{h}y_{n}z\cdot e_{1}\geq 1-Ct|a_{h}|y_{n}\geq 1-\frac{C|a_{h}|}{M}.

These two inequalities mean that
M\leq 1+C|a_{h}|\leq C
by (
5.7
),
which implies
S_{1}^{h}(0)\subset B_{C(n)\tau_{h}}(0)
.
Combining this and (5.2) we have

S_{1}^{h}\subset\{x_{n}\leq C\tau_{h}\}\cap\{CG_{1}^{h}\times[0,\infty]\}\subset B_{C\tau_{h}}(0).

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

The normalization
(u_{h},S_{1}^{h})
given by (
5.1
) satisfies (
5.9
) for large
h
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{det}\mathcal{D}_{h}=Vol(S_{h})\leq Ch^{\frac{n}{2}}
by Lemma 2.11, we see that
\tau_{h}\leq C
by its definition. Notice that

\tau_{h}^{n}\sim\operatorname{Vol}(S_{1}^{h}(0))=\tau_{h}^{n}\operatorname{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\sim\tau_{h}^{n}.

It is enough for (5.9) to prove that
\tau_{h}\geq c
.

Consider the function
\upsilon
defined by

\upsilon(x)=\tau_{h}^{-1}\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{8C_{n}^{2}}+\frac{x_{n}}{2C_{n}}\right).

If
\tau_{h}>0
is small, by (
5.3
), (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) we have

\upsilon\leq\tau_{h}^{-1}\left(\frac{\tau_{h}^{2}}{8}+\frac{\tau_{h}}{2}\right)<1\leq u_{h}\;\text{ on }\partial S_{1}^{h}\setminus G_{1}^{h},

\operatorname{det}D^{2}\upsilon\geq c\tau_{h}^{-n}>1=\operatorname{det}D^{2}u_{h}\;\text{ in }S_{1}^{h},

and

D_{n}v\geq\frac{1}{2C_{n}\tau_{h}}>C\geq D_{n}u_{h}\;\text{ on }G_{1}^{h}.

By comparison principle (Lemma
2.10
)
v<u_{h}
in
S_{1}^{h}
, which contradicts
v(0)=0=u_{h}(0)
. Therefore
\tau_{h}\geq c
.

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

The normalization
(u_{h},S_{1}^{h})
given by (
5.1
) satisfies (
5.10
) for large
h
.

Proof.

Lemmas 5.2 and 5.3 mean that

\tau_{h}
is between two positive universal constants, and

(5.12)

\mathcal{P}S_{1}^{h}\subset C\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}\text{ and }B_{c}(x_{h})\subset S_{1}^{h}\subset B_{C}(0)

for some
x_{h}\in R_{+}^{n}
.
Therefore, we only need to show
\mathcal{P}G_{1}^{h}
is balance about
0
, this is

(5.13)

\mathcal{P}{G}_{1}^{h}\supset B_{c}^{\prime}(0).

Let
M\geq 4n^{4n}C^{4n}
is large universal,
\theta=M^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}
,
m=M^{-1}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{M^{3}}
is small enough.
On the contrary to (5.13), there would exist a
p=(p_{1},p^{\prime\prime})\in\partial\mathcal{P}{G}_{1}^{h}
such that

(5.14)

|p|=\operatorname{dist}(0,p)=\operatorname{dist}(0,\partial\mathcal{P}{G}_{1}^{h})\leq\delta.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
p_{1}\geq 0
. There are two cases:

Case 1:
|a_{h}|\geq CM^{-1}
. Then we have

\max\{d_{2}(h),\cdots,d_{n-1}(h)\}\leq CMh^{\frac{1}{2}},

which means that

B_{cM^{-1}}^{\prime\prime}(0)\subset\mathcal{P}{S}_{1}^{h}(0)\subset B_{C}^{\prime}(0).

By convexity and (
5.14
), we get

\sup\{x_{1}|x\in{G}_{1}^{h}\}\leq CM\delta\leq\frac{C}{M}.

Therefore, (
5.7
) and the Neumann condition in (
5.3
) implies

|D_{n}u_{h}(x^{\prime},0)|\leq\frac{C}{M},\text{ on }x_{1}\geq-\frac{1}{M}.

Case 2:
|a_{h}|\leq CM^{-1}
. Then

|D_{n}u_{h}(x^{\prime},0)|\leq\frac{C}{M},\text{ on }{G}_{1}^{h}.

Next, we consider a new coordinate with zero as based point, axis
{e}_{1}=p/|p|
and
e_{i}(i\geq 2)
unchanged. For simplicity, we still use the notation
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
.

In both cases, consider the domain
E=\{x\in S_{1}^{h}|x_{1}>-\frac{1}{M}\}
. Then
\partial E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{3}
,
where

\begin{array}[]{l}E_{1}=\overline{E}\cap\{x_{1}=-\frac{1}{M}\},\\
E_{2}=\overline{E}\cap G_{1}^{h}\cap\{x_{1}>-\frac{1}{M}\},\\
E_{3}=\overline{E}\cap(\partial S_{1}^{h}\setminus G_{1}^{h}).\end{array}

For the function

\upsilon(x)=\left[Q(Mx_{1})+\theta\sum_{i=2}^{n}Q(\frac{x_{i}}{C})\right]

where
Q(t)=\frac{1}{4nC^{2}}(\frac{t^{2}}{2}+2t),
we
claim that

(5.15)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{det}D^{2}{u_{h}}<\operatorname{det}D^{2}\upsilon&\text{ in }E\\
D_{-x}{u_{h}}<D_{-x}\upsilon&\text{ on }E_{1}\\
D_{n}{u_{h}}<D_{n}\upsilon&\text{ on }E_{2}\\
\upsilon<{u_{h}}&\text{ on }E_{3}\\
\end{cases}.

By comparison principle (Lemma
2.10
) we have
\upsilon<u_{h}
in
E
. This would contradict
\upsilon(0)=u_{h}(0)
and hence (
5.13
) is proved.

Finally, let us verify (
5.15
). First, by convexity and (
2.6
) we see that

D_{-x}{u_{h}}=-D{u_{h}}\cdot x\leq u_{h}(0)-u_{h}(x)\leq 0

holds in viscosity sense. Applying this, the results of Case 1 and 2, and the largeness of
M
,
we have:

in
E
,
\operatorname{det}D^{2}\upsilon\geq CM^{2}\theta^{n-1}>1=\operatorname{det}D^{2}{u_{h}}
;

on
E_{1}
,
D_{-x}\upsilon\geq c-\theta\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}C>0\geq D_{-x}u_{h}
;

on
E_{2}
,
D_{n}\upsilon\geq c\theta>\frac{C}{M}\geq D_{n}u_{h}
;

on
E_{3}
,
\upsilon\leq\frac{3}{4}<1={u_{h}}
.
∎

In this way, we have proved Theorem 5.1. Since
c\leq\tau_{h}\leq C
, we can still take

\mathcal{D}_{h}=\operatorname{diag}\{d_{1}(h),\cdots,d_{n}(h)\}

in (
5.1

Remark 8.3
.

In the case
n=2
, there are two other proofs for Theorem
1.1
, which depends on the strict convex Lemma
4.1
and
4.2
.
With the two lemmas, one can show the solution is
C^{1}
up to boundary and is of super-linearity growth at infinite. This fact, together
with partial Legendre transform and the Liouville theorem for harmonic functions, will gives us the first proof. As for the second proof, we
need to use Wang’s function
[
26
]
to construct supersolution/subsolution and so prove that the
u
is
C^{1,1}
up to boundary, then
by blowing up at infinity to obtain
D^{2}u=c\mathcal{I}
.

As the end of this paper, we give the two proofs in more details.

Proof 1.

We write a point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
(x,y)
and use the classical partial Legendre transform in the
x
-variable. For any
p\in R
, define

u^{*}\left(p,y\right):=\sup_{x\in R}\{px-u\left(x,y\right)\}.

The uniformly strict convexity of
u
at boundary and the smoothness of
u
at interior also implies the superlinearity at infinite, which guarantees
the uniqueness and existence of
x=X\left(p,y\right)
such that the above supremum is attained.

By a calculation the two dimensional case of problem (
1.1
),

\operatorname{det}D^{2}u=1\;in\;\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2},\ \ D_{2}u\left(x,0\right)=ax\;on\;R

is turned to the problem for
u^{*}
:

\begin{cases}\Delta u^{*}=1&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\\
D_{v^{*}}u^{*}=0&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\end{cases},

where
v^{*}=e_{2}+ae_{1}
. Differentiating
u^{*}
with respect to
v^{*}
and letting
V=D_{v^{*}}u^{*}
we obtain

\begin{cases}\Delta V=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\\
V=0&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\end{cases}.

Note that

V=D_{v^{*}}u^{*}\left(p,y\right)=aX\left(p,y\right)-u_{2}\left(X,y\right)=u_{2}\left(X,0\right)-u_{2}\left(X,y\right)\leq 0.

We can use the Liuoville theorem for harmonic functions to see that the only solutions to above problem are

V(x,y)=D_{v^{*}}u^{*}\left(x,y\right)\equiv A

for some constant
A
. Then

u^{*}\left(p,y\right)=g\left(p-ay\right)+Ay

for some function
g:[0,\infty)\to R
. Recalling
\Delta u^{*}=1,D_{v^{*}}u^{*}=0
, we have

g\left(0\right)=0\ and\ \left(1+a^{2}\right)g^{\prime\prime}=1,

which implies that
u^{*}
is a quadratic polynomial, and so is the
u
.

Proof 2.

As we have said, when
n=2
, one could use strict convexity Lemma
4.1
and
4.2
to show
that the solution
u
to problem (
1.1
) is uniformly
C^{1,\alpha}
up to boundary
[
5
]
.

We consider Wang’s singular function with bounded positive Monge-Ampépre measure for large
a>>2
:

W_{a,b}\left(p,q\right)=\begin{cases}p^{a}+\frac{a^{2}-1}{a(a-2)}p^{a-\frac{2a}{b}}q^{2},\ |p|^{a}\geq|q|^{b}\\
\frac{4a-5}{2(a-2)}q^{b}+\frac{1}{2a}q^{b-\frac{2b}{a}}p^{2},\ |q|^{b}\geq|p|^{a}\end{cases}.

Let

W\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=CaW_{a,b}\left(\tau x_{1},x_{2}/\tau\right),

where
\tau>0
is small and satisfies
a\tau^{a}\leq c
.
One can use this function to construct a supersolution
(W^{+})
/subsolution
(W^{-})
at boundary point:

\begin{cases}w^{+}\left(x\right)=u\left(x\right)-W\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)+Mx_{2}\\
w^{-}\left(x\right)=\epsilon_{1}\left(W\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)-u\left(x\right)+\epsilon_{2}x_{2}\right)\end{cases}.

This will give the uniform estimate
c\leq D_{nn}u\leq C
on the boundary. Therefore
u
is
C^{1,1}
and then in
C^{2,\alpha}
.
After blowing up at infinite, this together with the superlinearity will imply
D^{2}u=c\mathcal{I}
and the proof is completed.
