Zeta-regularized Lattice Field Theory with Lorentzian background metrics
Tobias Hartung, Karl Jansen, Chiara Sarti

Abstract
. Lattice field theory is a very powerful tool to study Feynman’s path integral non-perturbatively. However, it usually requires Euclidean background metrics to be well-defined. On the other hand, a recently developed regularization scheme based on Fourier integral operator ζ \zeta -functions can treat Feynman’s path integral non-pertubatively in Lorentzian background metrics. In this article, we formally ζ \zeta -regularize lattice theories with Lorentzian backgrounds and identify conditions for the Fourier integral operator ζ \zeta -function regularization to be applicable. Furthermore, we show that the classical limit of the ζ \zeta -regularized theory is independent of the regularization. Finally, we consider the harmonic oscillator as an explicit example. We discuss multiple options for the regularization and analytically show that they all reproduce the correct ground state energy on the lattice and in the continuum limit. Additionally, we solve the harmonic oscillator on the lattice in Minkowski background numerically.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Lattice Field Theory

Feynman’s path integral
19
;
18
approach to
quantum mechanics and quantum field theory has become
an indispensable conceptual tool to evaluate physical observables, in
particular in high energy physics. One very fascinating aspect
of the path integral formulation is that it allows in principle
to compute also non-perturbative phenomena and thus reaches out
to problems that can not be addressed in perturbation theory.

The major obstacle to employ the path integral for studying
non-perturbative effects is, however, that it is in general
ill defined, if we take it at face value, i.e., using a Lorentzian
background metric. An elegant way out is to analytically
continue to imaginary – Euclidean – time and discretize the system
either on a time lattice in quantum mechanics or on a space-time
grid in quantum field theory. The so introduced lattice spacing
acts then as an ultraviolet regulator, whereas
a finite time or box length serves
as an infrared regulator resulting thus in completely regularized
version of the path integral, see
20
;
54

for introductions to the
so obtained
lattice field theories
.

Another important advantage of the Euclidean lattice regulated path integral
is its resemblance to systems in statistical physics where it
corresponds to the partition function. This allows in particular
to resort
to numerical calculations using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to evaluate
the path integral.
This approach has been employed very successfully in the last years
to obtain many non-perturbative results mainly for quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the
strong interaction between quarks and gluons.
In particular, it became possible to perform an ab initio calculation of the
low-lying baryon spectrum using only QCD as the underlying theory
12
.
Furthermore, lattice QCD calculations led to a detailed insight into the
structure of hadrons
7
;
6
and
they have provided non-perturbative contributions to electroweak
processes
42
and flavor physics
35
,
see e.g.
1
;
38
for overviews.
They were also very successful to determine thermodynamic properties
10
.
The remarkable progress in lattice QCD simulations can be seen by the fact
that nowadays
calculations are performed on large lattices – presently of the order of
around
100^{3}\times 200
lattice points – and directly in physical conditions.

Despite this impressive success, such Euclidean time lattice simulations can not
be employed in important and so far open questions such as the matter
anti-matter asymmetry, topological theories, the discrepancy of the observed
and theoretically predicted amount of charge-parity (CP) violation and
non-equilibrium physics.
The reason is that in these cases the integrand in the path integral
becomes complex such that MCMC can not be used.

It would therefore be extremely valuable to have an alternative regularization
of the path integral which allows, on one hand, to address non-perturbative physics and,
on the other hand, to use a Minkowski background metric or even more general Lorentzian
metrics. It is precisely at this point where the proposed
\zeta
-regularization
of the path integral
29
;
30
;
25
;
34
is filling this so far missing gap.
By “gauging” the path integral (cf.
subsection 1.2
),
it allows for a fully non-perturbative regularization
for very general metrics.
In this way, physical observables can be computed
even in cases where
the Euclidean lattice techniques such as MCMC simulations fail and even
on quantum computers
29
;
30
;
25
;
34
.

In this article, we aim to extend the mathematical formulation of
\zeta
-regularization within the context of lattice theories and to illustrate its application at
the example of the quantum mechanical harmonic oscillator. In particular, we will test different ways
of gauging the path integral and demonstrate that
all the here employed choices lead to correct physical results. In addition,
we will show that the
\zeta
-regularization gives the
correct classical limit.
Our work will therefore not only provide a rather simple example of the

\zeta
-regularization on the lattice with Lorentzian background metric, but it also discusses different classes of
gauges providing thus suitable choices when applied to a particular physical model
of interest.

1.2.
\zeta
-regularization

Operator
\zeta
-functions are a means of constructing traces on certain operator algebras. Given an algebra
\mathcal{A}
of operators, a trace
\tau
that is defined on a sub-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, and an operator
A\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0}
, the aim of operator
\zeta
-functions is to define
\tau(A)
. To this end, we construct a holomorphic family
\varphi:\ \mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi
maps an open connected subset of
\mathbb{C}
into
\mathcal{A}_{0}
and
\varphi(0)=A
. Given this setup and sufficient regularity of the operator algebra and construction of
\varphi
, we can study the operator
\zeta
-function
\zeta(\varphi)
which is defined via analytic continuation of
z\mapsto\tau(\varphi(z))
on the domain that is mapped to
\mathcal{A}_{0}
under
\varphi
. If this analytic extension exists in a neighborhood of
0
and the value
\zeta(\varphi)(0)
is independent of the choices made in the construction of
\varphi
, then we can use
\zeta(\varphi)(0)
as our definition of
\tau(\varphi(0))
, i.e.,
\tau(A)
.

For example, if we consider the absolute value of the differential operator
A:=\left\lvert{\partial}\right\lvert
on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is the algebra of classical pseudo-differential operators on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
and
\tau
the trace on trace-class operators, i.e., the sum of eigenvalues counting multiplicities. Furthermore, we may construct
\varphi(z):=A^{z+1}
. Formally evaluating the trace on
\varphi(z)
thus yields
\tau(\varphi(z))=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert{n}\right\lvert^{z+1}=2\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}n^{z+1}
.
This makes sense for
\Re(z)<-2
and implies that the corresponding operator
\zeta
-function is
\zeta(\varphi)(z)=2\zeta_{R}(-z-1)
where
\zeta_{R}
denotes the Riemann
\zeta
-function. In particular, the
\zeta
-regularized trace of
\left\lvert{\partial}\right\lvert
is given by
\zeta(\varphi)(0)=2\zeta_{R}(-1)=-\frac{1}{6}
.

Furthermore, the construction is formally applicable to lattice formulations in Lorentzian space-time as well. Applying a lattice discretization to

\int\omega(\Phi)\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)e^{iS(\Phi)}\mathcal{D}(\Phi)

What kind of gauge families

\zeta

\zeta

The first question therefore addresses the freedom we have in choosing gauge families most suitable for a given lattice simulation whereas answering the second question allows us to prove equivalence with the Fourier integral operator
\zeta

\zeta

Aims of this article

Since

Gauging the transfer matrix and time evolution operator

In

The transfer matrix
U_{\Delta}
is given by its kernel
\kappa_{\Delta}(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi)=e^{iS_{\Delta}(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi)}

\displaystyle\forall\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(X):\ \langle\varphi,U_{\Delta}\psi\rangle=\int_{X}\int_{X}\varphi(\Phi^{\prime})^{*}e^{iS_{\Delta}(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi)}\psi(\Phi)d\mu(\Phi^{\prime})d\mu(\Phi),

This directly yields the gauge family
\mathfrak{G}_{\Delta}
in the operator point of view via

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{G}_{\Delta}(z)\psi\right)(\Phi^{\prime\prime})=

\displaystyle\left(U_{\Delta}^{*}\tilde{U}_{\Delta}(z)\psi\right)(\Phi^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\int_{X}\int_{X}e^{-iS_{\Delta}(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime\prime})}e^{iS_{\Delta}(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi)\psi(\Phi)d\mu(\Phi)d\mu(\Phi^{\prime})

4.
The classical limit

In the construction of
\zeta
-regularized vacuum expectation values, we consider a quotient of
\zeta
-functions
\frac{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}\Omega)}{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G})}
which in the lattice setting takes the form
\frac{\int e^{iS}\mathfrak{g}\omega d\mu}{\int e^{iS}\mathfrak{g}d\mu}
. Although the general theory of operator
\zeta
-functions would allow for different choices of
\mathfrak{G}
(and thus different choices of
\mathfrak{g}
) in the numerator and denominator, the definition of
\zeta
-regularized vacuum expectation values
29
implicitly requires us to make the same choice. This was introduced to handle the problem that
\frac{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}_{1}\Omega)}{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}_{2})}
can depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
if
\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}_{1}\Omega)(0)=\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}_{2})(0)=0
. While enforcing
\mathfrak{G}_{1}=\mathfrak{G}_{2}=\mathfrak{G}
does not obviously solve the problem of
\frac{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}\Omega)}{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G})}(0)
being dependent on
\mathfrak{G}
if
\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}\Omega)(0)=\zeta(U\mathfrak{G})(0)=0
– in fact, independence of
\mathfrak{G}
is a consequence of the physicality proof for
\zeta
-regularized vacuum expectation values
25
– there is a physical reason to hope for this to be the correct requirement. In a very loose sense, we can interpret the denominator
\zeta(U\mathfrak{G})(z)
to be the partition function of a quantum field theory
\mathrm{QFT}(z)
with “time evolution”
U\mathfrak{G}(z)
. For
\Re(z)\ll 0
the vacuum expectation
\langle\Omega\rangle(z)
of
\Omega
is then well-defined in
\mathrm{QFT}(z)
using Feynman’s construction and satisfies
\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=\frac{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G}\Omega)(z)}{\zeta(U\mathfrak{G})(z)}
. The
\zeta
-regularized vacuum expectation value
\langle\Omega\rangle(0)
can therefore be loosely understood as the analytic continuation of well-defined vacuum expectation values in Feynman’s sense with respect to the holomorphic family of quantum field theories
\mathrm{QFT}
.

Of course, this interpretation requires many philosophical concessions because in the lattice picture,
\mathrm{QFT}(z)
corresponds to the “action”
S(z)=S(0)-i\ln(\mathfrak{g}(z))
where
S(0)
is the action of
\mathrm{QFT}(0)
, i.e., the action of the QFT we wish to study. However, if we accept this interpretation, then we can ask the question regarding the classical limit. In general, interpreting
S(z)
as a classical action is likely to be difficult due to
-i\ln(\mathfrak{g}(z))
being complex. The closest classical action we can extract from
S(z)
is the original action
S(0)
which leads to the hypothesis that the classical limit should always be the classical limit of
\mathrm{QFT}(0)
independent of the value of
z
.
Theorem 5
is precisely this surprising result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
S:\ \mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-degenerate action with a unique minimum, that is, there is exactly one point
C\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
S^{\prime}(C)=0
which furthermore satisfies
\det S^{\prime\prime}(C)\neq 0
. Let
\omega:\ \mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be an observable and
\mathfrak{g}(z)
a gauge function with
\forall z\in\mathbb{C}:\ \mathfrak{g}(z)(C)\neq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\forall z\in\mathbb{C}:\ \lim_{\hbar\searrow 0}\langle\omega\rangle(z)=\lim_{\hbar\searrow 0}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)\omega(\Phi)d\Phi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)d\Phi}=\omega(C).

In particular,
\lim_{\hbar\searrow 0}\langle\omega\rangle(0)=\omega(C)

Proof.

Since we are interested in the classical limit
\hbar\searrow 0
, we will expand the integrals in

\displaystyle\langle\omega\rangle(z)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)\omega(\Phi)d\Phi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)d\Phi}.

using stationary phase approximation and obtain

\displaystyle\langle\omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(C)}\left\lvert{\det S^{\prime\prime}(C)}\right\lvert^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}\mathrm{sgn}S^{\prime\prime}(C)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(C)\omega(C)\left(2\pi\hbar\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}+o\left(\hbar^{\frac{N}{2}}\right)}{e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(C)}\left\lvert{\det S^{\prime\prime}(C)}\right\lvert^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}\mathrm{sgn}S^{\prime\prime}(C)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(C)\left(2\pi\hbar\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}+o\left(\hbar^{\frac{N}{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega(C)+o(1)}{1+o(1)}.

Hence, we observe

\displaystyle\forall z\in\mathbb{C}:\ \lim_{\hbar\searrow 0}\langle\omega\rangle(z)=\omega(C).

∎

Consider the harmonic oscillator on the lattice with action

\displaystyle S:\ \mathbb{R}^{\tau}\to\mathbb{R};\ x\mapsto a\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}\frac{m}{2}\frac{(x_{j+1}-x_{j})^{2}}{a^{2}}+\mu^{2}x_{j}^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{k}S(x)=

\displaystyle m\frac{x_{k}-x_{k-1}}{a}-m\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{a}+2a\mu^{2}x_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\frac{2x_{k}-x_{k-1}-x_{k+1}}{a}+2a\mu^{2}x_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(Mx)_{k}

with
M_{j,k}=\left(\frac{2m}{a}+2a\mu^{2}\right)\delta_{j,k}-\frac{m}{a}\delta_{j+1,k}-\frac{m}{a}\delta_{j-1,k}

\displaystyle p(\lambda_{k})=\frac{2m}{a}+2a\mu^{2}-\frac{m}{a}e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{\tau}}-\frac{m}{a}e^{-\frac{2\pi ik}{\tau}}=2a\mu^{2}+\frac{2m}{a}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{\tau}\right)\right).

5.1.
The Wick rotation

5.2.
The global distributional gauge

The first type of gauge will be global in time and distributionally regularizing. These are generally of the form

\displaystyle\forall r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\ \forall\Phi\in\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}:\ \mathfrak{g}(z)(r,\Phi)=r^{z}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)(\Phi)=r^{z}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right).

Using such a gauge, we can evaluate the integral with respect to

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle i^{\frac{\tau+z+\alpha}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau+z+\alpha}{2}\right)\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi).

Hence,

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{i^{\frac{\tau+z+\alpha}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau+z+\alpha}{2}\right)\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)}{i^{\frac{\tau+z}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau+z}{2}\right)\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)}

\displaystyle\frac{i^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau+z+\alpha}{2}\right)\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau+z}{2}\right)\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\partial}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)}

and

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(0)=

\displaystyle\frac{i^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau+\alpha}{2}\right)\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}})}.

If we choose the eigenbasis of
\Sigma

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\ldots\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(\varphi_{1})^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{\tau-2}\sin(\varphi_{j})^{\tau-1-j}d\varphi_{\tau-1}\ldots d\varphi_{1}

The global absolutely integrable gauge

5.4.
The local distributional gauge

With the third approach we want to gauge the transfer matrix (as opposed to “only” gauging the entire time evolution operator). This is often useful because it retains locality in time – a property frequently exploited in many lattice methods; e.g., when computing correlation functions. In terms of the following computation, this choice of gauge allows us to use Fubini as in the Wick rotated computation. Let us start with a generic
\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)=\prod_{j=0}^{\tau-1}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(\Phi_{j})
. Then we observe

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}e^{i\left\lVert{\Psi}\right\lVert^{2}}\mathfrak{g}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\Psi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}e^{i\left\lVert{\Psi}\right\lVert^{2}}\mathfrak{g}(z)\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\Psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}\prod_{j=0}^{\tau-1}e^{i\Psi_{j}^{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{j}\Psi_{j}\right)\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}(\lambda_{k}\Psi_{k})^{\alpha}d\Psi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}\prod_{j=0}^{\tau-1}e^{i\Psi_{j}^{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{j}\Psi_{j}\right)d\Psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\Psi_{k}^{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\Psi_{k}\right)(\lambda_{k}\Psi_{k})^{\alpha}d\Psi_{k}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\Psi_{k}^{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\Psi_{k}\right)d\Psi_{k}}

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}=\left(\frac{am\omega^{2}}{2}+\frac{m}{a}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{\tau}\right)\right)\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

If we choose
\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\Psi_{k}\right)=\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\left\lvert{\Psi_{k}}\right\lvert\right)
, we further obtain

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{i\psi}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\sqrt{\Psi_{k}}\right)\Psi_{k}^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}d\Psi_{k}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{i\psi}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\sqrt{\Psi_{k}}\right)\Psi_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}d\Psi_{k}}.

Again, we have the choice between a distributionally regularizing gauge or an absolutely integrable gauge. In this subsection, we will proceed with a distributionally regularizing gauge
\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)(\psi)=\left\lvert{\psi}\right\lvert^{z}
which implies

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{i\psi}\psi^{\frac{\alpha+z-1}{2}}d\psi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{i\psi}\psi^{\frac{z-1}{2}}d\psi}=\frac{i^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+z+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}

and hence the required

\displaystyle\langle\Phi^{2}\rangle(0)=

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{i\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{2}=\frac{i}{2\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{2}.

5.5.
The local absolutely integrable gauge

The fourth and final choice of gauge would be an absolutely integrable gauge for the transfer matrix. For example, we may consider

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)(\psi)=\begin{cases}1&,\ \left\lvert{\psi}\right\lvert<1\\
\left\lvert{\psi}\right\lvert^{z}&,\ \left\lvert{\psi}\right\lvert\geq 1\end{cases}.

In this case, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{i\psi}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\sqrt{\psi}\right)\psi^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}d\psi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{i\psi}\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)\left(\lambda_{k}\sqrt{\psi}\right)\psi^{-\frac{1}{2}}d\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{\int_{0}^{\lambda_{k}^{-1}}e^{i\psi}\psi^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}d\psi+\lambda_{k}^{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>{\lambda_{k}^{-1}}}}e^{i\psi}\psi^{\frac{\alpha+z-1}{2}}d\psi}{\int_{0}^{\lambda_{k}^{-1}}e^{i\psi}\psi^{-\frac{1}{2}}d\psi+\lambda_{k}^{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>{\lambda_{k}^{-1}}}}e^{i\psi}\psi^{\frac{z-1}{2}}d\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{i^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)+\lambda_{k}^{z}i^{\frac{\alpha+z+1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+z+1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)}{i^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\left(\frac{1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)+\lambda_{k}^{z}i^{\frac{z+1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)}

which in the limit
z\to 0
yields

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(0)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{i^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)+i^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)}{\gamma\left(\frac{1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)+\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2},\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right)}=\frac{i^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}

and thus

\displaystyle\langle\Phi^{2}\rangle(0)=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\lambda_{k}^{2}.

6.1.
Solving the spherical integrals

The numerical difficulty of computing

\displaystyle E_{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{m\omega^{2}\tau}{2}\frac{\int_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right)d\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\Psi)}{\mathrm{vol}_{\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}}(\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}})}

is the quadrature of the high-dimensional spherical integrals. We are using “Sobol’ points on the sphere
\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}
” which can be generated from a “standard” Sobol’ sequence
57
in three steps.

Step 1.

Generate a
\tau
-dimensional Sobol’ sequence (uniform distribution on
(0,1)^{\tau}
).

Step 2.

Apply the standard normal (
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
) percent point function
\mathrm{ppf}
in each coordinate (standard normal distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
).

Step 3.

Normalize each sample (uniform distribution on
\partial B_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}
).

In order to make full use of the properties of the Sobol’ sequence, we generate
2^{n}
Sobol’ points for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and skip the first point since the first (non-randomized) Sobol’ point is always
\left(\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2}\right)^{T}
which gets mapped to the origin under
\mathrm{ppf}
and thus cannot be normalized.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of generated Sobol’ points. Then we can approximate
E_{0}
via

\displaystyle E_{0}\approx\frac{m\omega^{2}\tau}{2}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\Psi\in\mathcal{S}}\Omega\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}^{-1}\Psi\right).

The convergence in terms of the number of (randomized) Sobol’ samples is shown in
Figure 1
. The standard deviations, which were extracted from
100
simulations each, indicate an error scaling proportional to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\#\mathcal{S}}}
. In other words, the
\zeta
-regularized lattice simulation of the harmonic oscillator with Lorentzian background is comparable to a Markov Chain Monte-Carlo simulation in Euclidean background in terms of numerical effort.

6.2.
Extrapolation from
\Re(z)\ll 0

In more complicated situations, it may not always be possible to compute the dependence on the gauge parameter
z
explicitly, or computing the spherical integrals may be more difficult than computing the
\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
integrals

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle(z)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}e^{iS(\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)\Omega(\Phi)d\Phi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\tau}}e^{iS(\Phi)}\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)d\Phi}.

In such cases, it may be advantageous to compute
\langle\Omega\rangle(z)
at
\Re(z)\ll 0
where the integrals are well-defined and numerically well-posed. In order to guide the extrapolation to

z

\log

are known. Thus, by combining those constituent terms, we obtain a parametric expression
\Delta(z,p)
such that

p^{*}

\Delta(z,p)

\log

p^{*}

\Delta(z,p)

Consider an action

7.
Conclusion

In this article, we have studied the
\zeta
-regularization of lattice field theories with Lorentzian background metrics. In particular, we have discussed the connection between lattice field theories with Euclidean backgrounds and Lorentzian backgrounds in
section 2
, in order to understand how the
\zeta
-regularization arises as a naïve means of regularizing lattice expectation values when it is not possible to analytically continue the theory to Euclidean time. In
section 3
, we have then made the connection from the naïvely
\zeta
-regularized lattice theory to the Hamiltonian formulation so far discussed in the literature
30
;
29
;
25
;
26
;
34
. This allowed us to gain insights into the two main questions that need considering when extending the
\zeta
-regularization in the Hamiltonian formulation to the Lagrangian formulation commonly used to describe lattice field theories.

Q1:

What kind of gauge families
\mathfrak{g}
can be used to
\zeta
-regularize lattice field theories in a Lorentzian background?

Q2:

Under what conditions is it possible to construct a
\zeta
-regularized Hamiltonian theory starting from a “naïvely”
\zeta
-regularized lattice field theory?

Question Q1 is at least partially answered by
Theorem 1
and
Theorem 2
. In order to
\zeta
-regularize a lattice field theory in a Lorentzian background,
\mathfrak{g}(z)
must be chosen in such a way, that the corresponding integrals are well-defined for
\Re(z)\ll 0
. This is generally satisfied if
\mathfrak{g}(z)
has an asymptotic behavior of
\left\lvert{\mathfrak{g}(z)(\Phi)}\right\lvert\leq c(1+\left\lVert{\Phi}\right\lVert^{2})^{\delta\Re(z)}
or exhibits even faster convergence to zero as
\left\lVert{\Phi}\right\lVert\to\infty
. Furthermore, for an increasing, exhaustive sequence of compacta
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the compactified vacuum expectation values
\left(\frac{\langle 1_{K_{n}^{T}}\Omega\mathfrak{g}\rangle_{L}}{\langle 1_{K_{n}^{T}}\mathfrak{g}\rangle_{L}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
need to be locally bounded in
C(D)
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is open, connected, and contains
0
as well as a half-space with
\Re(z)\ll 0
. This local boundedness condition is likely to be the most intricate assumption to satisfy, as this is the likely point at which renormalization and modeling decisions can have a major impact.

It is important to note that the answer to Q1 merely implies the
existence of a
\zeta
-regularized lattice theory. Without further information, there is no reason to assume that this
\zeta
-regularized lattice theory is at all physical. In order to conclude physicality of the expectation values extracted from the
\zeta
-regularized lattice theory, we need to connect the Lagrangian formulation of the
\zeta
-regularized lattice theory to the Hamiltonian formulation studied to date
30
;
29
;
25
;
26
;
34
which includes a proof of physicality. As such, answering Q2 allows us to make physical sense of the
\zeta
-regularized lattice vacuum expectation values.

Question Q2 is partially answered by
Theorem 3
and
Theorem 4
. In essence, we need to ensure that the resulting gauged transfer matrix or gauged time evolution operator is a suitable family of Fourier integral operators; namely a gauged family in the sense of
30
;
29
;
25
;
26
;
34
. This can be ensured by choosing
\mathfrak{g}(z)
or
\mathfrak{g}_{\Delta}(z)
to be in a Hörmander class
S^{\delta\Re(z)}
or (poly-)homogeneous. While this restricts the class of gauge families
\mathfrak{g}
sufficient to construct the
\zeta
-regularized lattice theory as per question Q1, computationally this is not a bad class of functions as many analytic results are known and, thus, allowing for computational simplifications in numerical simulations of
\zeta
-regularized lattice theories.

In
section 4
, we then considered the classical limit of
\zeta
-regularized lattice theories and observed the remarkable fact that the classical limit is independent of the value for
z
. If we consider the impact of the gauge on a classical action, this can be rationalized since the gauge adds an imaginary component to a classical action. Thus, if we expect for only the real-part of the gauged action to be retained in the classical limit, then
Theorem 5
confirms this point of view.

Finally, we illustrated the theory of
\zeta
-regularized lattice theories developed in this article at the example of the harmonic oscillator. First, we treated it analytically in
section 5
; second, we considered it numerically in
section 6
. Both the analytic and numeric studies of the harmonic oscillator focus on the ground state energy.

In
section 5
, we computed the ground state energy of the harmonic oscillator on the lattice five times. Initially, we computed the ground state energy by using a Wick rotation, i.e., using the canonical approach of analytic continuation to imaginary (Euclidean) time. This served as a comparison for the four choices of gauge families that follow, as well as to show that the lattice theory has the correct continuum limit. The four gauge families that were used to compute the
\zeta
-regularized harmonic oscillator on the lattice with Minkowski background highlight the four cases arising from two binary choices we can make. On one hand, we have the choice of gauging local in time vs. global in time, that is, gauging the transfer matrix or the entire time evolution operator. On the other hand, we can choose between absolutely integrable gauge families and distributionally regularizing gauge families, where the former is more directly applicable to numerical simulations but the latter allows for analytic shortcuts via the Laplace transform. Of course, all four gauge families reproduced the correct ground state energy, as is to be expected since we have gauge-independence.

The numerical study of the
\zeta
-regularized harmonic oscillator on the lattice with Minkowski background in
section 6
focused on the numerical obstacles arising in classical simulations of
\zeta
-regularized lattice theories as opposed to quantum simulations which are a special case of the quantum simulations discussed in
25
;
34
. In particular, we noted that distributionally regularizing gauge families allow for numerical shortcuts via Laplace transform and can make for simpler functional dependence on the complex parameter
z
, which needs to be fitted against simulation data for extrapolation if the extrapolation cannot be done analytically to begin with. However, distributionally regularizing gauge families require numerical quadratures over high-dimensional manifolds (a sphere for the harmonic oscillator). Absolutely integrable gauge families, on the other hand, “only” require quadratures in high-dimensional vector spaces
\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
. As more powerful integration techniques exist for integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
than codimension
1
submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
, the absolutely integrable gauge families might be preferable from a numerical point of view. This question may be exacerbated further when considering gauge fields in lattice field theories. Unfortunately, the cost of choosing absolutely integrable gauge families is a more complicated dependence on the complex parameter
z
which may make extrapolation to
z=0
more difficult.
