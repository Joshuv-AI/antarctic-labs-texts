New second-order optimality conditions in sub-Riemannian Geometry
Michał Jóźwikowski

Abstract
We study the geometry of the second-order expansion of the extended end-point map for the sub-Riemannian geodesic problem. Translating the geometric reality into equations we derive new second-order necessary optimality conditions in sub-Riemannian Geometry. In particular, we find an ODE for velocity of an abnormal sub-Riemannian geodesics. It allows to divide abnormal minimizers into two classes, which we propose to call 2-normal and 2-abnormal extremals. In the 2-normal case the above ODE completely determines the velocity of a curve, while in the 2-abnormal case the velocity is undetermined at some, or at all points. With some enhancement of the presented results it should be possible to prove the regularity of all 2-normal extremals (the 2-abnormal case seems to require study of higher-order conditions) thus making a step towards solving the problem of smoothness of sub-Riemannian abnormal geodesics. As a by-product we present a new derivation of Goh conditions. We also prove that the assumptions weaker than these used in [Boarotto, Monti, Palmurella, 2020] to derive third-order Goh conditions, imply piece-wise- C 2 C^{2} regularity of an abnormal extremal.

Supplementary results.

The question whether the set

Preliminaries

Regularity of curves.

Throughout this work we will talk about curves on smooth manifolds of various classes of regularity. (Some emphasis will be put on improving the regularity of a curve by means of a bootstrap technique.) At the bottom level we have

L^{\infty}
-curves, i.e.
measurable and essentially bounded
. A step higher are
ACB
curves, i.e.
absolutely continuous with
L^{\infty}
derivatives
. The relation within these two is that if

satisfies an ODE

\dot{\gamma}(t)\overset{\text{a.e.}}{=}f(t)
, where

is of class

then
\gamma(t)
is ACB. This resembles the standard relation between
C^{r}
and
C^{r+1}
curves. Therefore it should not be very abusive to the standard mathematical jargon to denote the class
L^{\infty}
as
C^{0_{-}}
, and the class ACB as
C^{1_{-}}
(so that
C^{0_{-}+1}=C^{1_{-}}
and also
C^{1_{-}}\subset C^{0}
).

2.2
On jets

In the paper we speak about second-order optimality conditions in SR geometry. The term
second-order
means that we will consider the second-order Taylor expansions of various objects. Geometrically, we rather speak about
2-jets
. Below we recall some basic definitions and constructions from jet theory
[
Sau89
,
KMS93
]
.

Jets of curves.

We say that two
C^{2}
-curves
\gamma,\widetilde{\gamma}:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\longrightarrow M
are
tangent of order two
at
p=\gamma(0)=\widetilde{\gamma}(0)
if in some (and thus any) local coordinate system around
p
on
M
we have

\gamma^{\prime}(0)=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma^{\prime\prime}(0)=\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(0)\ .

In other words, maps
s\mapsto\gamma(s)
and
s\mapsto\widetilde{\gamma}(s)
have the same order-two Taylor expansions at
s=0
. The relation
\sim_{2}
of being tangent of order two is an equivalence relation and its equivalence classes
[\gamma]_{\sim_{2}}=[\widetilde{\gamma}]_{\sim_{2}}
are called
2-jets
at
p
. We denote the set of all 2-jets at
p
by
\mathrm{T}^{2}_{p}M
, and call it the
second tangent space of
M
at
p
. In fact the collection
\mathrm{T}^{2}M:=\bigcup_{p\in M}\mathrm{T}^{2}_{p}M
is a locally trivial bundle over
M
. We call it the
second tangent bundle of
M
. It is an example of a
graded bundle
in the sense of
[
GR12
]
, with the canonical multiplicative
\mathbb{R}
-action defined on representatives by
\lambda\mapsto\left(s\mapsto\gamma(\lambda\cdot s)\right)
.

Given a curve
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow M
and its (local) second-order Taylor expansion

\gamma(s)\overset{loc}{=}p+s\cdot\beta^{(1)}+{s^{2}}\cdot\beta^{(2)}+o(s^{2})\ ,

we may thus identify its 2-jet with a pair
(\beta^{(1)},\beta^{(2)})\simeq[\gamma]_{\sim_{2}}
. Although one should remember, that only the first term
\beta^{(1)}
is a geometric object, representing a tangent vector at
p
, while the term
\beta^{(2)}
on its own (without the presence of a particular
\beta^{(1)}
) has no intrinsic geometric meaning. In a particular situation when
M=V
is a vector space we may, however, canonically identify
\mathrm{T}^{2}_{p}V\simeq V\times V
, by considering the above Taylor expansion in any linear coordinates on
V
. Thus for a vector space, the bare term
\beta^{(2)}
has a geometric interpretation of an element of
V
.

Jets of maps.

Let
F:M\rightarrow N
be a smooth map between manifolds. For a
C^{2}
-curve
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\rightarrow M
passing through
\gamma(0)=p\in M
consider a (local) second-order Taylor expansion

F(\gamma(s))\overset{loc}{=}F(p)+s\cdot b^{(1)}+s^{2}\cdot b^{(2)}+o(s^{2})\ .

Then the pair
(b^{(1)},b^{(2)})
depends only on the 2-jet
[\gamma]_{\sim_{2}}
. Thus we may define the 2-jet of map
F

\mathrm{T}^{2}F:\mathrm{T}^{2}M\longrightarrow\mathrm{T}^{2}N\qquad\text{via}\qquad\mathrm{T}^{2}_{p}M\ni[\gamma]_{\sim_{2}}\longmapsto[F(\gamma)]_{\sim_{2}}\in\mathrm{T}^{2}_{F(p)}N\ .

\mathrm{T}^{2}F
is a bundle map over
F:M\longrightarrow N
.

Pairings on jets.

As a particular example of the above consider a smooth function
f:M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a
C^{2}
-curve
\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\longrightarrow M
passing through
\gamma(0)=p\in M
. The order-two Taylor expansion of the composition

and it depends only on the 2-jet

. Further, the resulting 2-jet

is valued in the second tangent space of a vector space

, and so the term of degree two

has an intrinsic geometric meaning.

Note that for a fixed 2-jet

, the value of (

) depends only on the first and second derivatives

and
\operatorname{D}^{2}_{p}f
, i.e. the second-order Taylor expansion of

a natural (and geometrically meaningful) pairing between a 2-jet of a curve

and a pair

\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[u]

We would like to study the following second-order expansion of this map (written in some local coordinate system)

\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]\overset{loc}{=}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[u]+s\cdot b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)+s^{2}\cdot b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u)+o(s^{2})\ ,

(2.3)

where
\Delta u\in\mathrm{T}_{u}L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})\simeq L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a control, and

is a real parameter. Of course

To calculate

Obviously

\dot{b^{(1)}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)\cdot\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}\left[b^{(1)}(t)\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot X_{i}\Big|_{q(t)}

\dot{b^{(2)}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)\left\{\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}\left[b^{(2)}(t)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{D}^{2}_{q(t)}X_{i}\left[b^{(1)}(t),b^{(1)}(t)\right]\right\}+2\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}\left[b^{(1)}(t)\right]

The extended end-point map.

Now we would like to add the energy (
E
) to the picture. We define the

extended end-point map

{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}:L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})\longrightarrow M\times\mathbb{R}\ ,

simply as
{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[u]:=(\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[u],E^{[t_{0},t]}[u])
. For any
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
the second expansion of the energy term is just

E^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]=E^{[t_{0},t]}(u)+s\cdot c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)+s^{2}\cdot c^{(2)}(t,\Delta u)\ ,

(2.7)

where
c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sum_{i}u_{i}(\tau)\cdot\Delta u_{i}(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau
and
c^{(2)}(t,\Delta u)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(\tau)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau

\begin{split}\dot{c^{(1)}}(t)&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)\cdot\Delta u_{i}(t)\\
\dot{c^{(2)}}(t)&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta u_{i}(t)^{2}\end{split}

(2.8)

with the initial conditions
c^{(1)}(t_{0})=0
and
c^{(2)}(t_{0})=0
.

Normal and abnormal extremals

Note that if for some
t
the vector space

\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}=\{(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))\ |\ \Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\}

equals
\mathrm{T}_{(q(t),E^{[t_{0},t]}[u])}(M\times\mathbb{R})
then, by the open-mapping theorem, the map
{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
is open at
u
, and hence the related trajectory cannot be a minimizing sub-Riemannian geodesic. In this way we obtained a necessary condition that a SR geodesics should satisfy. Curves that satisfy this conditions are known as
extremals
. We can further classify these according to the geometric properties of
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Consider a sub-Riemannian trajectory
q(t)
with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
corresponding to the control
u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Curve
q(t)
is called an
extremal
if
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
is a proper subspace of
\mathrm{T}_{(q(t_{1}),E^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}[u])}(M\times\mathbb{R})
. An extremal is called
normal
if the space
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
does not contain the subspace
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}
. If the image
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
is contained in a space
V\times\mathbb{R}
, where
V\subset\mathrm{T}_{q(t_{1})}M
is some proper subspace,
q(t)
is called
abnormal
. We will say an abnormal extremal has
co-rank
r
if
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
is a co-rank
r
-subspace of
\mathrm{T}_{(q(t_{1}),E^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}[u])}(M\times\mathbb{R})
.

Strictly abnormal
extremals are abnormal extremals which are not normal, that is
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}=V\times\mathbb{R}
for some proper subspace
V\subset\mathrm{T}_{q(t_{1})}M
.

Normal extremals are known to be
C^{\infty}
-smooth and energy-minimizing on small distances
[
LS95
,
Mon06
]
.

First-order abnormal optimality conditions

Let us recall the following standard fact
[
Mon06
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let a SR trajectory
q(t)
corresponding to the control
u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
be an abnormal extremal. Then there exists a nowhere-vanishing covector curve
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(t)}M
satisfying the following conditions:

\begin{split}&\big\langle X_{i}\big|_{q(t)},\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0\qquad\text{for every $i=1,2,\ldots,k$}\\
&\big\langle b,\dot{\varphi}(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\sum_{i}u_{i}(t)\cdot\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b],\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0\quad\text{for every $b\in\mathrm{T}_{q(t)}M$. }\end{split}

(2.9)

We shall call such a
\varphi(t)
a
Pontryagin covector
.

It is instructive to see that conditions (
2.9
) can be easily derived from the following one

\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}\subset\ker(\varphi(t),0)\quad\text{(equivalently}\quad\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0\text{ for every $t$ and $\Delta u$)}\ ,

which express the fact that
\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
is contained in a proper subspace
V\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathrm{T}_{q(t)}M\times\mathrm{T}_{E^{[t_{0}]}{t}[u]}\mathbb{R}
for every
t
.

To see this it is enough to differentiate the above condition with respect to
t
to get:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\big\langle\dot{b^{(1)}}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t,\Delta u)\big\rangle+\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\dot{\varphi}(t)\big\rangle\overset{\eqref{eqn:b1}}{=}

\displaystyle\big\langle\sum_{i}u_{i}(t)\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]\big\rangle+\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(t)\big\langle X_{i}\big|_{q(t)},\varphi(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\dot{\varphi}(t)\big\rangle\ .

This should hold for every possible choice of the control
\Delta u
. Thus the part linear in
\Delta u
should vanish leading to
\big\langle X_{i}\big|_{q(t)},\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0
for every
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Consequently we are left with

\displaystyle 0=\big\langle\sum_{i}u_{i}(t)\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]\big\rangle+\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\dot{\varphi}(t)\big\rangle\ .

It is instructive to see that the notions from Definition
2.3
give the well-known first order optimality condition. For example if
q(t)
is an abnormal extremal then there exists a non-zero covector
\varphi_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}
such that
\varphi_{0}+0\cdot\mathrm{d}r\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{\ast}
annihilates the reachable set
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{(\Lambda_{1})}(t_{0},t_{1})
. In more concrete terms

\big\langle q^{(1)}(t_{1},\Delta u),\varphi_{0}\big\rangle=0
for every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. By (
4.3
) this gives us

\displaystyle 0=\big\langle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(1)}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t,\varphi_{0}\big\rangle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\varphi_{0}\big\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t

and so for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and every
i=1,2\ldots,k

\displaystyle 0=\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\varphi_{0}\big\rangle\overset{\text{Lem.~\ref{lem:adapted}}}{=}\big\langle\Phi^{(1)}(t)\left[X_{i}(q(t))\right],\varphi_{0}\big\rangle=\big\langle X_{i}(q(t)),\Phi^{(1)}(t)^{\ast}\varphi_{0}\big\rangle\ .

As we see
\varphi(t):=\Phi^{(1)}(t)^{\ast}\varphi_{0}\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(t)}M
annihilates the whole distribution
\mathcal{D}
along the trajectory

defined in such a way is a

At the end of the day we arrived at the standard conditions for an abnormal extremal

3
The main result and its discussion

In this section we will formulate our main result – Theorem
3.3
– and discuss its consequences. We would like to present our result as an extension of the classical Agrachev-Sarychev theory. However, for the sake of generalization, we prefer to use the language of 2-jets rather then the one of first and second derivatives originally used in
[
AS96
]
. For this reason our notation will be somehow non-standard.

3.1
The Agrachev-Sarychev Index Lemma

Statement of the Index Lemma

Recall that by a
negative index
of a quadratic form we understand the maximal dimension of a subspace on which this form is negatively-defined. We have the following classical result.

Lemma 3.1

(Index Lemma
[
AS96
]
)
.

Let
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, be an abnormal minimizing SR geodesic of co-rank
r
, corresponding to the control
u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Then there exists a non-vanishing Pontryagin covector
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(t)}M
such that for each
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
we have

(i)

\operatorname{Im}{\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}}\subset\ker\varphi(t)
, and

(ii)

the negative index of the quadratic form (the
Agrachev-Sarychev index
,
AS index
in short)

\big\langle\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[\cdot,\cdot],\varphi(t)\big\rangle:\ker\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}\times\ker\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

is smaller than
r
.

Above we identify
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M
with
\varphi(t)+0\cdot\mathrm{d}r\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}M\times\mathrm{T}^{\ast}\mathbb{R}
, so that the pairing between
\varphi(t)
and the element
\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u]
makes sense.

For a concise proof we refer to the recent monograph
[
Rif14
, Appendix B.]
. Let us remark, that although the second derivative
\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}
has only a local sense, the above map
\big\langle\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[\cdot,\cdot],\varphi(t)\big\rangle
is well-defined, since our attention is restricted to the kernel
\ker\operatorname{D}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}
– cf. our discussion of pairing of 2-jets preceding formula (
2.2
).

A simple corollary

We are most interested in the situation when the Agrachev-Sarychev index is actually zero, i.e. the respective quadratic map is non-negatively defined. It turns out that this is not so difficult to achieve by the following argument.

As we shall prove later (see Lemma
4.5
), function
t\longmapsto\operatorname{ind}_{-}\big\langle\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}[\cdot,\cdot],\varphi(t)\big\rangle
, assigning to each
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
the respective AS index, is non-decreasing. On the other hand, by the result of Lemma
3.1
this function is bounded. It follows that there are at most
r-1
points of growth on the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Inbetween these points the index must be zero. Therefore for the price of excluding a finite number of points from the trajectory (or, to see it differently, of dividing the trajectory into a finite number of pieces), we may set the AS index to zero. Now we have

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
, there exists a non-vanishing Pontryagin covector
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(t)}M
such that

\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0

for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Further, there exist at most
r+1
points
\tau_{0}=t_{0}\leq\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\tau_{r^{\prime}}=t_{1}
such that for each subinterval
[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]\subset(\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
the following inequality

\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle\geq 0

(3.1)

holds for every
\Delta u\in\ker\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]}\subset L^{\infty}([t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
and every
t\in[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]
.

3.2
Optimality conditions of degree 2

The main result

Our main result – Theorem
3.3
– may be seen as a twofold generalization of Lemma
3.2
. First of all, we generalize inequality (
3.1
) by adding a quadratic part to it. Thanks to that controls
\Delta u
can be arbitrary, not just form the kernel of
\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
as was previously the case. Since we are no longer restricted in the choice of controls
\Delta u
, we will be able to add the monotonicity condition (
3.4
) related with the discussed inequality. This is the part that was completely absent in the original Agrachev-Sarychev theory. It has, however, important consequences, allowing us to derive new equations for abnormal geodesic in
Theorem
3.4
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, be a strictly abnormal minimizing SR geodesic of co-rank
r
, corresponding to the control
u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. For
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
consider the second-order expansion of the extended end-point map in the form

\displaystyle{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}

\displaystyle[u+s\cdot\Delta u]=\left(\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u],E^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]\right)\overset{\text{loc.}}{=}

\displaystyle\left(q(t),E^{[t_{0},t]}[u]\right)+s\left(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)\right)+s^{2}\left(b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(2)}(t,\Delta u)\right)+o(s^{2})\ .

Then there exists a non-vanishing Pontryagin covector
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(t)}M
such that

\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0

(3.2)

for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Further there exists a division of the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
by at most
r(n-r-k+1)+1
points
\tau_{0}=t_{0}<\tau_{1}<\ldots<\tau_{N}=t_{1}
such that on each sub-interval
[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]\subset(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})
there exists a family of symmetric bi-linear maps
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t):\mathrm{T}_{{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0}^{\prime},t]}[u]}(M\times\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{T}_{{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0}^{\prime},t]}[u]}(M\times\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
parameterized by
t\in[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]
satisfying the following conditions:

•

-\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[(b^{(1)},c^{(1)}),(b^{(1)},c^{(1)})]
equals to the infimum

\inf\{\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle\ |\ \Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k}),b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=b^{(1)},c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=c^{(1)}\}\ .

We conclude that for every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
and
t\in[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]
the following inequality holds

\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle+\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)),(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))]\geq 0;

(3.3)

•

the assignment
t\mapsto\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
is a map of bounded variation, hence differentiable almost everywhere,

•

moreover, for every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
the assignment

t\longmapsto\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle+\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)),(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))]

(3.4)

is non-decreasing.

The proof is rather long and technical, and will be given in Section
4
.

Explanation of the main result

Let us briefly discuss the above result. At the fundamental level it says that there is a relation between optimality of an abnormal curve and the geometry of the second-order expansion of the extended end-point map
{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}
for each time
t
. This relation is expressed by (
3.2
) – an equality involving the first derivatives of this map (the standard first-order optimality condition) – and (
3.3
) – an inequality involving 2-jets of
s\mapsto{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
. The latter inequality is stated by means of a pairing of a 2-jet and 2-covector
((\phi(t),0),\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t))
on
M\times\mathbb{R}
– cf. Remark
2.2
. To understand the meaning of these conditions let
f:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function realizing this 2-covector, i.e such that
\operatorname{D}f[\cdot]=\big\langle\cdot,(\varphi(t),0)\big\rangle
and
\operatorname{D}^{2}f[\cdot,\cdot]=\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[\cdot,\cdot]
. Then conditions (
3.2
)–(
3.3
) imply that, up to the terms of order higher than 2, the curves
s\mapsto\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
lie on a one side of a level set of
f
– cf. our considerations on page
2.1
. To put it differently: there is a quadratic hypersurface which bounds the 2-jets of curves
s\mapsto\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
for every possible choice of control
\Delta u
. Since
\operatorname{D}f=(\varphi(t),0)
is trivial on
\mathbb{R}
-component, this hypersurface is 1-tangent to the energy direction
\mathbb{R}
in
M\times\mathbb{R}
. The question whether we have also 2-tangency of these objects will be a basis of a distinction between
2-normal
and
2-abnormal
curves, which we shall introduce later in this section.

At this point it is worth to explain a presumable contradiction, pointed out by one of the reviewers of this paper. Namely, in the Agrachew-Sarychew Lemma
3.1
, since our attention is restricted to controls
\Delta u
in the kernel of
\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}
, we can state conditions on the second derivative of the end-point map
\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}
– which now becomes the dominant term of the expansion of
{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
. On the contrary, assumptions of Theorem
3.3
do not require
\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u]
to vanish, hence the access to the second derivative
\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u,\Delta u]
does not seem so obvious. Note, however, that we do not study the end-point map
{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
directly, but rather through the composition
f\circ{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
. The latter expands as (cf. the last paragraph of Subsection
2.2
)

\displaystyle f\circ

\displaystyle{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]=f\circ{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[u]+s\cdot\operatorname{D}f\left[\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u]\right]+

\displaystyle s^{2}\cdot\left(\operatorname{D}f\left[\operatorname{D}^{2}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u,\Delta u]\right]+\operatorname{D}^{2}f\left[\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u],\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u]\right]\right)+o(s^{2})

Now
\operatorname{D}f
is the Pontryagin covector which annihilates
\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}[\Delta u]
, making the quadratic part the dominant term in the expansion. Theorem
3.3
gives us precisely information about this quadratic term, for
\operatorname{D}f=(\varphi(t),0)
and
\operatorname{D}^{2}f[\cdot,\cdot]=\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[\cdot,\cdot]
.

The important part of the assertion is that we have a direct description of what this quadratic map
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
is. Namely,
-\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[(b^{(1)},c^{(1)}),(b^{(1)},c^{(1)})]
is defined as a solution of the (optimal control) problem of minimizing the value
\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle
in the class of controls
\Delta u
that give fixed values
b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=b^{(1)}
and
c^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=c^{(1)}
. In other words, we cannot further improve inequality (
3.3
) – it describes the envelope of the space of all 2-jets of
s\mapsto{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t]}[u+s\cdot\Delta u]
. This description allows to calculate
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
in concrete examples. Let us stress, that neither the finiteness of the solution of the above optimal control problem, nor the property it is actually quadratic in
(b^{(1)},c^{(1)})
is a trivial fact.

There is a caveat to what we said above. Namely the described properties of the expansion of the extended end-point map may not be true on the whole geodesic, but only on its sub-intervals obtained after a removal of a finite number of points. These points play essentially the same role as the points at which the AS index grows (cf. Lemma
3.1
)
– their removal guarantees the positive-definiteness of the quadratic maps considered in the discussed results. In principle, it should be possible to reformulate the conditions (
3.3
) of Theorem
3.3
as a finite-index condition for some global map quadratic in
\Delta u
(as it is done in the original Agrachev-Sarychev Lemma
3.1
). However, in such a formulation one would not be able to state condition (
3.4
). As we shall see shortly, the latter condition is responsible for the derivation of evolution equations, hence possibly also proving regularity of the trajectory. Therefore, we see no satisfactory way of getting rid of the point-removal procedure. On the other hand, isolated point singularities may be treated by the methods of
[
HLD16
]
.

Equations of an abnormal geodesic

3.3
Discussion of the main result

Let us now discuss some particular consequences of Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
.

Problem of regularity

Observe that if
\xi(t)
and/or
a_{2}(t)
are non-zero (we shall call
q(t)
a
2-normal extremal
in such a situation – see the next paragraph), then at least one of equations (
3.6
)–(
3.7
) gives us an algebraic equation for the controls
u_{i}(t)
(and thus an ODE for the trajectory) expressed by means of the data of Theorem
3.3
.

Since all
a_{2}(t)
,
\xi(t)
, and
\Phi_{2}(t)
are maps of bounded variation, and
X_{i}\big|_{q(t)}
shares the same regularity as
q(t)
, we can only conclude that in such a situation
u_{i}(t)
’s are functions of bounded variation.
If, however, the time-regularity of quadratic map
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
could be improved, we could also improve the regularity of the controls, and thus the SR geodesic.
Recall that maps
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
are defined as solutions of a certain optimization problem (Bellmann value function to be more precise – we shall discuss that in detail in Subsection
4.1
). Therefore the question whether 2-normal extremals are smooth boils down to an other regularity question in optimal control theory. I was personally unable to get any general results in this direction (yet some more specific ones are collected in Section
5
), but perhaps someone more experienced in optimal control theory could solve this problem.

2-normality and 2-abnormality

Observe that if
a_{2}(t)\neq 0
, equation (
3.6
) provides information about the controls
u_{i}(t)
. Basing on this fact we propose the following definition:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
be a strictly abnormal sub-Riemannian trajectory such that the pair
(\varphi(t),\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t))
described by Thm
3.3
exists for every
t\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
. If for every
t\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
we have
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)[(0,1),(0,1)]=a_{2}(t)\neq 0
we call
q(t)
a
2-normal extremal
.
Otherwise we say that
q(t)
is an
2-abnormal extremal
.

By the results of Thm
3.3
every strictly abnormal SR trajectory is either non-minimizing, or can be divided into a finite number of pieces, each of them being either 2-normal or 2-abnormal extremal.

The division of abnormal trajectories into the classes of 2-normal and 2-abnormal extremals proposed above shares some natural similarities with an analogous distinction between normal and abnormal trajectories in degree one. Namely, 2-normal extremals are characterized by a property that the second-order approximation of their extended end-point map can be strictly separated from the direction of the decreasing cost (see fig. on page
1
). Further they satisfy an ODE (although we were not able to prove their regularity from this property). On the other hand, for 2-abnormal extremals this separation may not be strict, and equation (
3.6
) gives us no information about the control
u(t)
at the points where
a_{2}(t)=0
.

Note that if
a_{2}(t)\equiv 0
, equations (
3.8
) and (
3.6
) are the same as equations for an abnormal extremal given at page
2.3
. Thus we have two abnormal Pontryagin covectors
\varphi(t)
and
\xi(t)
in that case (although the latter may be trivial).

Finally, note that the classes of 2-normal and 2-abnormal extremals are, in general, not disjoint as an abnormal trajectory of co-rank greater than one may admit several distinct Pontryagin covectors.

Derivation of Goh conditions

Equations (
3.6
)-(
3.7
) allow for an easy derivation of
Goh conditions
. Put
b^{(1)}=X_{j}
in (
3.7
) to get

\displaystyle 2\big\langle\operatorname{D}X_{i}[X_{j}],\varphi\big\rangle\overset{\eqref{eqn:Phi_b_1}}{=}-2\Phi_{2}\left[X_{i},X_{j}\right]-\big\langle X_{j},\xi\big\rangle u_{i}(t)\overset{\eqref{eqn:xi_u_1}}{=}-2\Phi_{2}\left[X_{i},X_{j}\right]+a_{2}\cdot u_{j}(t)u_{i}(t)\ .

The right-hand side of the above expression is symmetric in
i
and
j
, hence

\big\langle[X_{i},X_{j}],\varphi\big\rangle=\big\langle\operatorname{D}X_{i}[X_{j}],\varphi\big\rangle-\big\langle\operatorname{D}X_{j}[X_{i}],\varphi\big\rangle=0\ .

The Goh condition may be seen as a vanishing of a non-symmetric part of the derivatives (
3.6
)–(
3.7
) due to the symmetric nature of the quadratic form
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
.

Other observations

If
a_{2}(t)\equiv 0
(or more generally if
\dot{a}_{2}(t)=0
), then the bi-linear form (
3.5
) contains a bare term linear in
c^{(1)}
. This term should vanish for every possible choice of
b^{(1)}
, since otherwise the form would not be positively-defined. This reasoning gives the evolution equation for
\xi(t)
:

\big\langle b^{(1)},\dot{\xi}(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)\cdot\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)}],\xi(t)\big\rangle=0\qquad\text{for every $b^{(1)}\in\operatorname{Im}\left(\operatorname{D}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}\right)$,}

(3.8)

nd so,
\xi(t)
is a (possibly trivial)
Pontryagin covector
.

The question whether the bi-linear form (
3.5
) is strictly positive or has some null-directions is closely related to the question whether the infimum value of the quadratic map
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
is actually achieved by some control. We will discuss it in more detail in Section
5
. At this point note that if the bi-linear form (
3.5
) is constantly zero, and if the objects
\Phi_{2}(t)
,
\xi(t)
and
a_{2}(t)
are absolutely continuous w.r.t.
t
then, in addition to the above equation (
3.8
), we get
\dot{a_{2}}(t)\equiv 0
and, at the end of the day, also evolution equation for
\Phi_{2}(2)
:

\big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)\operatorname{D}^{2}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)},b^{(1)}],\varphi\big\rangle-\dot{\Phi}_{2}[b^{(1)},b^{(1)}]-2\Phi_{2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(t)\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)}],b^{(1)}\right]=0

(3.9)

which should hold for every
b^{(1)}\in\operatorname{Im}\left(\operatorname{D}_{u}\mathrm{End}^{[t_{0},t]}_{q_{0}}\right)
. Informally speaking, these equations mean that the pair
(\varphi(t),\Phi_{2}(t))
has a natural evolution compatible with the flow of the control vector field
q\mapsto\sum_{i}u_{i}(t)X_{i}(q)
. For more details the Reader is directed to

]
.

3.4
Examples

Example - Martinet system

Consider
M=\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni(x,y,z)
and a distribution
\mathcal{D}
spanned by orthonormal vector fields
X_{1}=\partial_{x}
, and
X_{2}=(1-x)\partial_{y}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\partial_{z}
. Let
q(t)=(0,t,0)
be a trajectory corresponding to the control
(u_{1}(t)\equiv 0,u_{2}(t)\equiv 1)
. It is well-known that
q(t)
is an abnormal minimizing geodesic, and that
\phi(t)=\mathrm{d}z
is the related adjoint curve
[
Mon06
]
. We want to see how Thm.
3.3
works in this case.

First we would like to derive equations (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) for the considered situation. Let us decompose

b^{(1)}(t)=b^{(1)}_{x}(t)\cdot\partial_{x}+b^{(1)}_{y}(t)\cdot\partial_{y}+b^{(1)}_{z}(t)\cdot\partial_{z}\quad\text{and}\quad b^{(2)}(t)=b^{(2)}_{x}(t)\cdot\partial_{x}+b^{(2)}_{y}(t)\cdot\partial_{y}+b^{(2)}_{z}(t)\cdot\partial_{z}\ .

Now note that

\sum_{i=1}^{2}u_{i}(t)\operatorname{D}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)}]=-\mathrm{d}x[b^{(1)}]\cdot\partial_{y}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{2}u_{i}(t)\operatorname{D}^{2}_{q(t)}X_{i}[b^{(1)},b^{(1)}]=\mathrm{d}x\otimes\mathrm{d}x[b^{(1)},b^{(1)}]\cdot\partial_{z}\ .

From the above observation equation (
2.5
) reads as

\dot{b^{(1)}_{x}}(t)=\Delta u_{1}(t),\quad\dot{b^{(1)}_{y}}(t)=-b^{(1)}_{x}(t)+\Delta u_{2}(t),\quad\dot{b^{(1)}_{z}}(t)=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{c^{(1)}}(t)=\Delta u_{2}(t)\ .

In particular,

b^{(1)}_{y}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}b^{(1)}_{x}(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\Delta u_{2}(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau=-\int_{0}^{t}b^{(1)}_{x}(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau+c^{(1)}(t)\ .

(3.10)

Similarly, (
2.6
) gives us

\dot{b^{(2)}_{x}}(t)=0,\quad\dot{b^{(2)}_{y}}(t)=-b^{(2)}_{x}(t)-\Delta u_{2}(t)b^{(1)}_{x}(t),\quad\dot{b^{(2)}_{z}}(t)=b^{(1)}_{x}(t)^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{c^{(2)}}(t)=\Delta u_{1}(t)^{2}+\Delta u_{2}(t)^{2}\ .

In consequence, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we can estimate

\displaystyle\big\langle b^{(2)}(t),\phi(t)\big\rangle=b^{(2)}_{z}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}b^{(1)}_{x}(\tau)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\tau\overset{\text{C-S}}{\geq}\frac{1}{t}\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{t}b^{(1)}_{x}(\tau)\ \mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{t}\cdot\left[c^{(1)}(t)-b^{(1)}_{y}(t)\right]^{2}\ .

It is easy to see that the above inequality is always strict, yet on the other hand, it can be satisfied with an arbitrary accuracy. It follows that every minimal 2-jet is finite, but the minimum is never attained. We conclude that
\widehat{Q}_{0,t}(b^{(1)},c^{(1)})=\frac{1}{t}\cdot\left[c^{(1)}(t)-b^{(1)}_{y}(t)\right]^{2}
and, consequently,

\Phi_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{d}y\otimes\mathrm{d}y,\quad\xi(t)=-\frac{2}{t}\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{and}\quad a_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\ .

Since
a_{2}(t)\neq 0
the considered trajectory is a
2-normal extremal
in the sense of Definition
3.5
. We leave to the Reader checking that conditions (
3.6
)-(
3.7
) hold for the above data. Further, condition (
3.5
) reads as

\left(b^{(1)}_{x}(t)+\frac{1}{t}b^{(1)}_{y}(t)\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{2}{t^{2}}b^{(1)}_{y}(t)+\frac{2}{t}b^{(1)}_{x}(t)\right)c^{(1)}(t)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}c^{(1)}(t)^{2}=\left(b^{(1)}_{x}(t)+\frac{1}{t}b^{(1)}_{y}(t)-\frac{1}{t}c^{(1)}(t)\right)^{2}\geq 0

and of course holds.

Example – a corner on a free Carnot group

The example below was studied by Hakavouri and Le Donne as communicated to us by
[
LD21
]
. It turns out to be a 2-abnormal extremal.

Consider a free Carnot group
G
or rank 2 and step 4. That is, the Lie algebra of left-invariant vector fields on
G
is generated by two fields
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
, whose Lie brackets of order higher than 4 vanish, and Lie brackets of order less or equal 4 have no relations apart from the obvious ones arising either from the skew-symmetry, or from the Jacobi identity. It follows that all left-invariant vector fields on
G
are spanned (over
\mathbb{R}
) by fields:
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,

Y:=[X_{1},X_{2}]
,
Z_{1}:=[X_{1},Y]=[X_{1},[X_{1},X_{2}]]
,
Z_{2}:=[X_{2},-Y]=[X_{2},[X_{2}.X_{1}]]
,
W_{1}:=[X_{1},Z_{1}]=[X_{1},[X_{1},[X_{1},X_{2}]]]
,
W_{2}:=[X_{2},Z_{2}]=[X_{2},[X_{2},[X_{2},X_{1}]]]
, and
W_{3}:=[X_{2},Z_{1}]=[X_{1},-Z_{2}]=[X_{1},[X_{2},[X_{1},X_{2}]]]
.Let us consider a left-invariant SR geodesic problem on
G
, constituted by setting
\mathcal{D}_{q}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}\big|_{q}
and the assumption that the fields
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are orthonormal.

In such a setting let
q:[-1,1]\rightarrow G
be the
corner
controlled by
u(t)=(\chi_{[-1,0]}(t),\chi_{[0,1]}(t))
, i.e.

q(t)=\begin{cases}\exp(t\cdot X_{1})&\text{for $t\in[-1,0]$}\\
\exp(t\cdot X_{2})&\text{for $t\in[0,1]$}\end{cases}\ .

It turns out that
q(t)
is a strictly abnormal SR extremal, with

\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[-1,1]}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{2},Y,Z_{1},Z_{2},W_{1},W_{2}\}\big|_{q(1)}\oplus\mathbb{R}\ .

In consequence, there is only one (up to rescaling) candidate for the Pontryagin covector
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(t)}G
uniquely defined by conditions

\big\langle W_{3}\big|_{q(t)},\varphi(t)\big\rangle=1,\ \text{and}\ \big\langle\cdot,\varphi(t)\big\rangle=0\ \text{on the space}\ \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{2},Y,Z_{1},Z_{2},W_{1},W_{2}\}\big|_{q(t)}\subset\mathrm{T}_{q(t)}G\ .

In this particular situation we were able to calculate
\big\langle\bm{b}^{(2)}(1,\Delta u),\varphi(1)\big\rangle
for every control
\Delta u=(\Delta u_{1},\Delta u_{2})\in L^{\infty}([-1,1],\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We skip the details of this computation, as it is rather long and would require introduction of some additional theory regarding invariant SR structures on Lie groups. To state our result we need to introduce the function
f:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

f\left(\exp(w_{3}W_{3})\exp(w_{2}W_{2})\exp(w_{1}W_{1})\exp(z_{2}Z_{2})\exp(z_{1}Z_{1})\exp(yY)\exp(x_{2}X_{2})\exp(x_{1}X_{1})q(1)\right):=w_{3}\

(obviously
\operatorname{D}_{q(1)}f=\varphi(1)
), and a covector
\rho\in\mathrm{T}^{\ast}_{q(1)}G
defined by conditions

\big\langle X_{1}\big|_{q(1)},\rho\big\rangle=1,\ \text{and}\ \big\langle\cdot,\rho\big\rangle=0\ \text{on the space}\ \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},Y,Z_{1},Z_{2},W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}\}\big|_{q(1)}\ ,

i.e.
\big\langle b^{(1)}(1,\Delta u),\rho\big\rangle
is the
X_{1}
-component of the vector
b^{(1)}(1,\Delta u)\in\mathrm{T}_{q(1)}G
.

It turns out that for every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([-1,1],\mathbb{R}^{2})
the pair
(\bm{b}^{(1)}(1,\Delta u),\bm{b}^{(2)}(1,\Delta u))
is subject to the following equality

\displaystyle\big\langle\bm{b}^{(2)}(1,\Delta u),\varphi(1)\big\rangle+\operatorname{D}^{2}_{q(1)}f[b^{(1)}(1,\Delta u),b^{(1)}(1,\Delta u)]+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle b^{(1)}(1,\Delta u),\rho\big\rangle^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\Delta u_{1}(s)\ \mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t-\int_{-1}^{0}t\left(\int_{-1}^{t}\Delta u_{2}(s)\ \mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t\ .

Clearly the right-hand side is positively defined. We conclude that in the considered case inequality (

) holds with
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(1)[(b^{(1)},c^{(1)}),(b^{(1)},c^{(1)})]=-\operatorname{D}^{2}_{q(1)}f[b^{(1)},b^{(1)}]-\frac{1}{2}\big\langle b^{(1)},\rho\big\rangle^{2}
. As the the dependence on
c^{(1)}
is trivial,

, and hence we deal with a

in the sense of Definition
3.5
. It is worth to mention that, by the results of
[
HLD18
]
, a corner curve cannot be a SR geodesic.

Technical preparations (adapted coordinates)

In the proof of Theorem

]

Pontryagin covector in adapted coordinates

Observe that equation (

Proposition 4.3
.

A curve

such that

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=\Phi^{(1)}(t)^{\ast}\psi_{0}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi_{0}\big\rangle=0\quad\text{for every $i=1,2,\ldots,k$ and $t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]$.}

Further if
\varphi(t)
is as above and if the pairs
(b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u)

\displaystyle\big\langle b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle=

\displaystyle\big\langle q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle=

\displaystyle\big\langle q^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\psi_{0}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\Phi^{(2)}(t)[b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),b^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)],\Phi^{(1)}(t)^{\ast}\psi_{0}\big\rangle=

\displaystyle\big\langle q^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\psi_{0}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle A(t)\Phi^{(2)}(t)[A(t)q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),A(t)q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)],\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ ,

\Phi^{(1)}(t)
.

Recall that in Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2

The characteristic optimal control problem

Motivated by Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
and our observations from the previous paragraph we define the following optimization problem for trajectories of the characteristic control system (

1

).

Definition 4.4
.

Let
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, be a SR trajectory corresponding to the control
u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Choose
\psi_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}
,
\tau\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{(\Lambda_{1})}(t_{0},\tau)
. By a
characteristic optimal control problem
(
characteristic OCP
, in short) of the trajectory
q(t)
we understand a problem of minimizing the functional

Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u]:=\big\langle q^{(2)}(\tau,\Delta u),\psi_{0}\big\rangle

in the class of controls
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},\tau],\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t_{0},\Delta u)=\bm{0}
and
\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u)=\bm{v}
. (In other words,
\Delta u
steers (

Λ
1

) from
\bm{0}
to
\bm{v}
on
[t_{0},\tau]
.)

Note that by (

)

Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u]=\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\ ,

hence we are actually solving a Larange-type optimal control problem.

Putting for the moment the question of the existence of solutions aside, define
the minimal 2-jet
to be the Bellman value function related with this problem

\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v}):=\inf\{Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u]\ |\ \text{$\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},\tau],\mathbb{R}^{k})$ is a control steering \eqref{eqn:CS_deg_1} from $\bm{0}$ to $\bm{v}$ on $[t_{0},\tau]$}\}\ .

We admit a possibility that
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
equals to
-\infty
.

Finally, note that the cost functional

is quadratic, in the sense that
Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u]=B_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u,\Delta u]
, where
B_{\tau}[\cdot,\cdot]:L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\times L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a symmetric bilinear map defined by

\displaystyle 2\ B_{\tau}[\Delta u,\Delta v]:=\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}\sum_{i}\big\langle\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t,\Delta v))+\Delta v_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ \mathrm{d}t\ .

Strategy of the proof

In the remaining part of this section, we are going to investigate properties of the minimal 2-jet

•

Secondly, in Lemma

Lastly, in Lemma

With all the above points to conclude the proof it is enough to prove Theorem

Step 1 – setting the AS index to zero

\mathcal{Q}_{[t_{0},t]}:\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{[t_{0},t]}:\Delta u\longmapsto\big\langle b^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\varphi(t)\big\rangle\overset{\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:pontryagin_in_adapted}}}{=}\big\langle q^{(2)}(t,\Delta u),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ ,

Step 2 – Bellman-like inequalities

The minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\cdot)
is defined as the Bellman’s value function for an optimal control problem. Thus it should not be surprising that it shares its basic properties.
Namely, it turns out, that for a fixed control
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, the distance between
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]
and the infimum value
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v}=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))
is a non-decreasing function of time
t
. This property will be of crucial importance later.

Lemma 4.8

(Bellman’s inequality)
.

For every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, function

t\longmapsto Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))

is non-decreasing.

Proof.

Fix a control
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and assume for simplicity that
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))=5
, while
Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\Delta u]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t^{\prime},\Delta u))=1
for some
t_{0}\leq t<t^{\prime}\leq t_{1}
. Since
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\cdot)
is defined as the infimum, there is some control
\Delta u^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
such that
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u^{\prime})
, while
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u^{\prime}]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))<5-1=4
. Thus

Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u^{\prime}]-Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]<-1\ .

(4.5)

Now consider a concatenated control
\widetilde{\Delta u}:=\Delta u^{\prime}|_{[t_{0},t]}\circ\Delta u|_{[t,t^{\prime}]}\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Obviously,
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\widetilde{\Delta u})=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u^{\prime})=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)
; and
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t^{\prime},\widetilde{\Delta u})=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t^{\prime},\Delta u)
as both controls
\widetilde{\Delta u}
and
\Delta u
equal on
[t,t^{\prime}]
and share the same initial point
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\widetilde{\Delta u}]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u^{\prime}]+\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\sum\Delta u_{i}(\tau)\cdot\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(\tau,q^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ \mathrm{d}\tau=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u^{\prime}]+Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\Delta u]-Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]\overset{\eqref{eqn:inequality}}{<}Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\Delta u]-1\ .

It follows that

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\widetilde{\Delta u}]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t^{\prime},\widetilde{\Delta u}))=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\widetilde{\Delta u}]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t^{\prime},\Delta u))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}[\Delta u]-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t^{\prime},\Delta u))-1=0\ .

This is a contradiction, with the definition of
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t^{\prime}}(\cdot)
as the infimum.
∎

Now we have all the pieces ready to conclude the proof of Theorem

5
Supplementary results – on solutions of the characteristic OCP

In Ssec.
4.2
we were able to describe the shape of the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})
, i.e the infimum of the set of values of
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]
under the condition that
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{v}
. This, in turn, allowed us to prove Thm
3.3
describing sub-Riemannian optimality conditions of degree two. So far, however, we didn’t address the question whether these infimum values are realized by some controls, i.e. if the characteristic OCP actually has solutions. In general this will not happen, as may be seen in the examples discussed in Subsection
3.4
. The reason is that
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})
is defined as an infimum taken over elements
\Delta u
belonging to an affine subspace of finite codimension in
L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. And we lack any sort of universal (weakly) compactness results in such situations. Below we will discuss some conditions that can guarantee the existence of the infimum.

First of all, in Subsection
5.1
, we study the characteristic OCP from the algebraic point of view, exploiting mostly the fact that the problem is quadratic in controls. It turns out that an important role for the problem in general is played by
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(\bm{0})}
– the space of solutions of the characteristic OCP with the end-point
\bm{0}
. We identify that one of the assumptions made in
[
BMP20
]
implies that this space has finite codimension in the space of all controls
L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. We formalize this as
Monti’s condition
and prove, in Lemma
5.5
, that this finite codimension assumption implies that the characteristic OCP has solutions for an arbitrary end-point.

If the existence of solutions of the characteristic OCP can be somehow assured, we can apply to them the Pontryagin Maximum Principle to get additional information about the underlying SR trajectory
q(t)
. Precisely this situation is considered in Subsection
5.2
. In particular, by combining our main result with the conclusions of the PMP, we prove Lemma
5.9
stating that if Monti’s conditions are assumed, then the abnormal SR geodesics consist of a finite number of pieces which are either
C^{2}
or 2-abnormal.

5.1
Algebraic approach to the characteristic OCP, Monti’s condition

Algebraic criteria for the existence of solutions

We begin our discussion with a simple algebraic criteria for a control to be a solution of the characteristic OCP.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
be such that
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{v}
. Then
\Delta u
is a solution of the characteristic OCP if and only if

(i)

B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]=0
for every
\Delta v\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)
and

(ii)

\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})
is finite (hence equal to zero by Prop.
4.7
).

Proof.

First let us prove that conditions (
i
)–(
ii
) imply optimality of the control
\Delta u
.
Note that controls
\Delta u,\Delta u^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
are such that
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u^{\prime})=\bm{v}
if and only if
\Delta v:=\Delta u^{\prime}-\Delta u
belongs to
\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)
. Now by our assumptions
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]\geq\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})=0
and
B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]=0
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u^{\prime}]=Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u+\Delta v]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]+2B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]+Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]+Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]\geq Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]\ ,

Since
\Delta u^{\prime}
was arbitrary,
\Delta u
is indeed a solution of the characteristic OCP.

Assume now that
\Delta u
is a solution of the characteristic OCP. This implies, in particular, that
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})
is finite for
\bm{v}=\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)
. The latter condition suffices to prove (
ii
). Indeed, take any
\Delta v\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u+\lambda\Delta v]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]+2B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\lambda\Delta v]+Q_{t_{0},t}[\lambda\Delta v]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]+2\lambda\cdot B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]+\lambda^{2}\cdot Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]\ .

Thus if
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]
would be negative, we could make
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u+\lambda\Delta v]
as low as possible by taking
\lambda
big enough. Therefore the assumption of finteness of
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})
requires

for every
\Delta v\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)
. In consequence,
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})\geq 0
and thus
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})=0
by Prop.
4.7
.

To prove point (
i
) take
\Delta v\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)
. Then for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the control
\Delta u+\lambda\Delta v
belongs to
L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and ends at
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u+\lambda\cdot\Delta v)=\bm{v}
. Thus the map

\mathbb{R}\ni\lambda\longmapsto Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u+\lambda\Delta v]=Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]+2\lambda\cdot B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]+\lambda^{2}\cdot Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]

has a minimum at
\lambda=0
, which implies
B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]=0
.
∎

Spaces of solution of the characteristic OCP

We conclude that the space of solutions of the characteristic OCP has a simple structure.

Proposition 5.2
.

The set of solutions of the characteristic OCP

\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{}:=\{\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\ |\ Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]=\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))\}

is a vector space. Further the space of solutions satisfying
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{v}

\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(\bm{v})}:=\{\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\ |\ Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]=\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v}),\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{v}\}

is an affine space modeled on

\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(\bm{0})}=\{\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\ |\ Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]=\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0}),\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)=\bm{0}\}\ .

Proof.

By Prop.
5.1
, each solution
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
is characterized by the condition that the map

B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\cdot]:\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

is null. Note that
B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\cdot]
is linear in
\Delta u
. This ends the first part of the proof.

Let now
\Delta u,\Delta u^{\prime}
be two elements of
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(}\bm{v})
. Then, of course
\Delta v:=\Delta u^{\prime}-\Delta u
satisfies
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta v)=\bm{0}
. Further

\displaystyle 0=\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})-\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})=Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u^{\prime}]-Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u+\Delta v]-Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]=

\displaystyle 2B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]+Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]\ .

By the results of Prop.
5.1
the bilinear term vanishes, and so
Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]=0=\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})
, i.e.
\Delta u^{\prime}-\Delta u\in\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(\bm{0})}
. Finally if put
\bm{v}=\bm{0}
in the above calculation, we would get that a difference of two elements of
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(\bm{0})}
lies in
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t}{(\bm{0})}
. We conclude that the latter is a vector space. This ends the proof.
∎

The space
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},\tau}{(\bm{0})}
has some interesting properties.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume that the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
is finite for every
\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{(\Lambda_{1})}(t_{0},\tau)
. Then for every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and every
\Delta v\in\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},\tau}{(\bm{0})}
we have

B_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u,\Delta v]=0\ .

Proof.

Take any such
\Delta u
and
\Delta v
. Since
Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta v]=\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{0})=0
, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u+\lambda\cdot\Delta v]=Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u]+2\lambda B_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u,\Delta v]+\lambda^{2}Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta v]=Q_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u]+2\lambda B_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u,\Delta v]

Note that
\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u+\lambda\Delta v)=\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u)
, as
\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta v)=0
. If
B_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u,\Delta v]\neq 0
, then by taking
\lambda
big or small enough we would find a control steering (

Λ
1

) from
\bm{0}
to
\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u)
with arbitrarily small cost. This contradicts the finiteness of
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u))
.

∎

Monti’s condition

Prop.
5.1
shows that solutions of the characteristic OCP are related with the kernel of the linear maps
B_{t_{0},\tau}[\Delta u,\cdot]:\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},\tau)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, the previous Prop.
5.3
shows that the space
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},\tau}{(\bm{0})}
is contained in that kernel. It turns out that if the space
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},\tau}{(\bm{0})}
is big enough it may guarantee the existence of solutions of the characteristic OCP. To formalize this property let us propose the following definition.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
q(t)
with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
be a sub-Riemannian trajectory and consider the related characteristic OCP. We say that the trajectory satisfies
Monti’s condition
at time
\tau\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
if the space
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},\tau}{(\bm{0})}
is of finite codimension in
L^{\infty}([t_{0},\tau],\mathbb{R}^{k})
(equivalently: of finite codimension in
\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},\tau)
, as the latter is a space of finite codimension in
L^{\infty}([t_{0},\tau],\mathbb{R}^{k})
being the kernel of the linear map
\bm{q}^{(1)}:L^{\infty}([t_{0},\tau],\mathbb{R}^{k})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
).

We propose the name Monti’s condition as this is one of the assumptions used in
[
BMP20
]
to derive the third-order optimality conditions.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
q(t)
with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
be a sub-Riemannian trajectory. Consider the related characteristic OCP and assume that the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
is finite for every
\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{(\Lambda_{1})}(t_{0},\tau)
. If the trajectory satisfies Monti’s condition at time

\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{(\Lambda_{1})}(t_{0},\tau)
.

Proof.

We will show that Monti’s condition actually reduces the characteristic OCP to a finite-dimensional optimization problem.

Consider a control
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})

\bm{q}^{(1)}(\tau,\Delta u)=\bm{v}
, and let us introduce any splitting
\mathcal{U}_{0}{(t,\tau)}=V\oplus\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},\tau}{(\bm{0})}

.

5.2
Solutions of the characteristic OCP in light of the Pontryagin Maximum Principle

Once the existence of solutions of the characteristic OCP is established (for example by assuming Monti’s condition), we may use the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP, in short)
[
PMBG62
]
to derive necessary conditions for optimality. They will give us a lot of information on the trajectory. When discussing solutions of the characteristic OCP we will always speak about normal or abnormal
extremals of the characteristic OCP
, not to be confused with the
sub-Riemannian extremal
that constitutes the characteristic OCP itself.

The PMP for the characteristic OCP

For the characteristic OCP the
Pontryagin Hamiltonian

H:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{\ast}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle H(t,(q^{(1)},c^{(1)}),

\displaystyle(\psi,a),\Delta v)=H(t,q^{(1)},(\psi,a),\Delta v):=

\displaystyle\big\langle\sum_{i}\Delta v_{i}\cdot Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi\big\rangle+a\sum_{i}\Delta v_{i}\cdot u_{i}(t)+p_{0}\big\langle\sum_{i}\Delta v_{i}\cdot Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ .

Here,
(\psi,a)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{\ast}
is a covector dual to the pair
(q^{(1)},c^{(1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
p_{0}
is either -1 (in the normal case) or
0
(in the abnormal case). Actually, we may assume that the pair
(\psi,a)
belongs to the dual of the reachable set
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{(\Lambda_{1})}(t_{0},t_{1})
. Note that since
H
does not depends on
c^{(1)}
, the dual object
a
will always be constant. The following result is immediate.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let the control
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
together with a covector curve
(\psi(t),a(t))
be an extremal of the the characteristic OCP related with the SR trajectory
q(t)
, corresponding to the control
u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Then in the abnormal case (
p_{0}=0
) both
\psi(t)
and
a(t)
are constant. In the normal case (
p_{0}=-1
)
a(t)
is constant, while
\psi(t)
is subject to the evolution equation

\big\langle b,\dot{\psi}(t)\big\rangle=\big\langle\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,b),\psi_{0}\big\rangle

(5.1)

where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is arbitrary.

Further, as the Hamiltonian
H(t,q^{(1)},(\psi,a),\Delta v)
is a linear function of
\Delta v
, and we have no bounds on the control, the Maximum Principle gives us the following conditions

\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle+a\cdot u_{i}(t)+p_{0}\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}),\psi_{0}\big\rangle=0

(5.2)

for every
i=1,2,\ldots,k
.

Existence of extremals implies regularity of the SR trajectory

The existence of certain extremals of the PMP for the characteristic OCP allows to deduce regularity of the underlying SR trajectory
q(t)
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Under the assumptions of Prop.
5.6

•

if
\Delta u
is an abnormal extremal of the characteristic OCP with
a\neq 0
, the SR trajectory
q(t)
has to be normal and thus
C^{\infty}
-smooth;

•

if
\Delta u
is a normal extremal of the characteristic OCP with
a\neq 0
, then the SR trajectory
q(t)
is
C^{1}
,

•

if
\Delta u
is a normal extremal of the characteristic OCP with
a\neq 0
, and the SR trajectory
q(t)
additionally satisfies Goh conditions, then
q(t)
is
C^{2}
.

Proof.

To prove the first part, let
(\psi,a)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{\ast}
be the abnormal covector curve of the characteristic OCP (it is constant by Prop.
5.6
). The Maximum Principle (
5.2
) gives us in this case

\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi\big\rangle+a\cdot u_{i}(t)=0\quad\text{for $i=1,2,\ldots,k$}.

These are precisely the equations of the normal SR trajectory. In particular we get
u_{i}(t)=-\frac{1}{a}\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi\big\rangle
. Now note that on the left-hand side we have something of class
C^{r}
(with
r=0_{-},1_{-},1,2,\ldots
) while, by the results of Lemma
4.1
, on the right-hand side something of class
C^{r+1}
. Hence
u_{i}(t)
must be
C^{\infty}
-smooth by the bootstrap argument.

A similar argument works also for a normal extremal of the characteristic OCP. In that case, by (
5.2
) we have

u_{i}(t)=-\frac{1}{a}\left(\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle-\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\right)\ .

Now note that, if
u(t)
was of class
L^{\infty}
, then, by Lemma
4.1
,
Y_{i}^{(1)}(t)
and
Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,\cdot)
are absolutely continuous with respect to
t
. Further the covector curve
\psi(t)
, as well as the trajectory
q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)
are Lipschitz. Since
Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)})
depends linearly on
q^{(1)}
, we conclude that
u_{i}(t)
is absolutely continuous and, consequently,
q(t)
is
C^{1}
. This ends the proof of the second part.

For the last part we differentiate (
5.2
) (which we now know is possible almost everywhere) to get

\displaystyle a\dot{u_{i}}(t)=

\displaystyle-\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle-\big\langle Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\dot{\psi}(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle+\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,\dot{q^{(1)}}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\overset{\eqref{eqn:ev_PMP},\eqref{eqn:CS_deg_1}}{=}

\displaystyle-\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle-\big\langle\sum_{j}\Delta u_{j}(t)\cdot Y_{j}^{(2)}(t,Y_{i}^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle+\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,\sum_{j}\Delta u_{j}(t)\cdot Y_{j}^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle=

\displaystyle-\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j}\Delta u_{j}\big\langle Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,Y_{j}^{(1)}(t))-Y_{j}^{(2)}(t,Y_{i}^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\overset{\text{Lem.~\ref{lem:adapted}}}{=}

\displaystyle-\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\partial_{t}Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle+\sum_{j}\Delta u_{j}\big\langle\Phi^{(1)}(t)[X_{i},X_{j}],\psi_{0}\big\rangle\ ,

By Lemma
4.1
the
t
-dependence of
Y_{i}^{(1)}(t)
and
Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,\cdot)
is
C^{1}
(as
u_{i}(t)
’s are now absolutely continuous), and the term involving the Lie bracket vanishes because of the Goh condition. By repeating our reasoning from the previous point,
\dot{u_{i}}(t)

is absolutely continuous and hence
q(t)
is actually
C^{2}
.
∎

With a little more effort, combining the information provided by the PMP with our previous results about optimality conditions of degree two, we can prove that actually solutions of the characteristic OCP are precisely the normal extremals of the PMP. Further, the existence of particular solutions of the characteristic OCP guarantees the
C^{2}
-regularity of the underlining SR trajectory.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
q(t)
with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
be a strictly abnormal SR extremal.

(i)

Assume that the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})
is finite. Then the control
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a solution of the characteristic OCP if and only if it is a normal extremal of the characteristic OCP.

(ii)

Assume that the assertion of Thm
4.13
holds. In particular, let
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})=\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t)[\bm{v},\bm{v}]
for a quadratic map
\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t):\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then if the control
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t],\mathbb{R}^{k})
is such a solution of the characteristic OCP that
\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t)[(0,1),\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]\neq 0
, the SR trajectory
q(t)
is of class
C^{2}
.

Proof.

If
\Delta u
is a solution of the characteristic OCP it must be either a normal, or an abnormal extremal of the PMP. The abnormal case can be easily excluded. Indeed, let
\Delta u
be an abnormal extremal corresponding to a covector
(\psi,a)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{\ast}
. By the results of previous Lemma
5.7
, if
a\neq 0
, then
q(t)
must be a normal SR extremal, which is impossible since it is assumed to be strictly abnormal.

In the case
a=0
, by (
5.2
) the covector
\psi
annihilates all control fields
Y_{i}^{(1)}(t)
and, in consequence, the whole reachable set of (

Λ
1

), so
\psi=0
and
a=0
which is impossible, as the covector defined by the PMP has to be nowhere-vanishing.

Now we can concentrate on the case where
\Delta u
is a normal extremal of the characteristic OCP. Let
(\psi(t),a)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{\ast}
be the related covector curve. For any other control
\Delta v\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
we would like to calculate the bi-linear form
B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]
. Note that

\displaystyle\big\langle\sum_{i}\Delta v_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\overset{\eqref{eqn:max_princip}}{=}

\displaystyle\big\langle\sum_{i}\Delta v_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(1)}(t),\psi(t)\big\rangle+a\cdot\sum_{i}\Delta v_{i}(t)\cdot u_{i}(t)\overset{\eqref{eqn:CS_deg_1}}{=}

\displaystyle\big\langle{\dot{q}}^{(1)}(t,\Delta v),\psi(t)\big\rangle+a\cdot\dot{c^{(1)}}(t,\Delta v)\ .

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\big\langle\sum_{i}\Delta u_{i}(t)\cdot Y_{i}^{(2)}(t,q^{(1)}(t,\Delta v)),\psi_{0}\big\rangle\overset{\eqref{eqn:ev_PMP}}{=}

\displaystyle\big\langle q^{(1)}(t,\Delta v),\dot{\psi}(t)\big\rangle

By adding the two above equalities and integrating over
[t_{0},t]
we get

B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]=\big\langle q^{(1)}(t,\Delta v),\psi(t)\big\rangle+a\cdot c^{(1)}(t,\Delta v)\ .

(5.3)

Our first conclusion is that whenever
\Delta v\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(t_{0},t)
then
q^{(1)}(t,\Delta v)=0
and
c^{(1)}(t,\Delta v)=0
, and thus
B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]=0
. By Prop.
5.1
this (together with the finiteness of
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{0})
) implies that
\Delta u
is optimal, proving point (
i
).

To prove (
ii
), take any
\Delta v\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
,
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, and denote
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta v)=\bm{v}=(v,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
. We have

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u+\varepsilon\cdot\Delta v]=

\displaystyle Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u]+\varepsilon B_{t_{0},t}[\Delta u,\Delta v]+\varepsilon^{2}Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]=

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))+\varepsilon\big\langle\bm{v},(\psi(t),a)\big\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}Q_{t_{0},t}[\Delta v]\geq

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))+\varepsilon\big\langle\bm{v},(\psi(t),a)\big\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})\ ,

and thus

\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)+\varepsilon\bm{v})\geq\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))+\varepsilon\big\langle\bm{v},(\psi(t),a)\big\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})\ .

(5.4)

On the other hand, by Thm
4.13
we know that the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\cdot)
is a quadratic function and hence

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)+\varepsilon\bm{v})=

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)+\varepsilon\bm{v},\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)+\varepsilon\bm{v}]=

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u),\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]+2\varepsilon\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{v},\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{v},\bm{v}]=

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))+2\varepsilon\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{v},\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]+\varepsilon^{2}\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v})\overset{\eqref{eqn:ineq_Q}}{\geq}

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u))+\varepsilon\big\langle\bm{v},(\psi(t),a)\big\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},t}(\bm{v}),

implying

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{v},\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]\geq\varepsilon\big\langle\bm{v},(\psi(t),a)\big\rangle\ .

As
\varepsilon
and
\bm{v}=(v,c)
were arbitrary, the above inequality may hold if and only if

\displaystyle\big\langle v,\psi(t)\big\rangle+c\cdot a=

\displaystyle\big\langle\bm{v},(\psi(t),a)\big\rangle=2\bm{\Psi}_{2}[\bm{v},\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]\ .

In particular, taking
\bm{v}=(0,1)
we get
a=2\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t)[(0,1),\bm{q}^{(1)}(t,\Delta u)]
. By Lemma
5.7
if the latter number is different than zero and the Goh conditions are satisfied, the trajectory
q(t)
is of class
C^{2}
. Note however, that if the assertion of Theorem
4.13
holds, then also the assertion of Theorem
3.3
is true (with a single piece). To show it suffices to repeat the reasoning from the last paragraph of Subsection
4.2
. But then Goh conditions are true by the results of Subsection
3.3
. This ends the proof.
∎

Regularity under Monit’s condition

We end our consideration by stating the following consequences of Monti’s condition.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, be a minimizing strictly abnormal SR geodesic. Assume additionally that it satisfies Monti’s condition at time
t_{1}
(in the sense of Definition
5.4
).

Consider the division of
q(t)
into a finite number of pieces provided by Thm
3.4
. Then on each of these pieces
q(t)
is either a 2-abnormal extremal in the sense of Definition
3.5
, or of class
C^{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the division of
q(t)
into pieces
[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
provided by Theorem
3.4
. Take any
[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]\subset(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})
. Let
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
be the related quadratic map and
a_{2}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
its
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
-component. Now we have two possibilities, either the piece of
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}^{\prime}]
, is a 2-abnormal extremal, or
a_{2}(t)\neq 0
for every
t
(the 2-normal case). Let us assume that we have the latter situation.

Observe that if the Monti’s condition holds at
t_{1}
then also it holds at
t_{1}^{\prime}
on the shorter trajectory as all we have to do is to take the finite-codimensional extension
\operatorname{Sol}_{t_{0},t_{1}}{(}\bm{0})\subset L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
and intersect it with
L^{\infty}([t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.
In particular, by Lemma
5.5
, the characteristic OCP on
[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]
has solutions for every possible end-point condition. Let
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
be such a solution with
\bm{q}^{(1)}(t_{1}^{\prime},\Delta u)=(0,1)
. Recall that by the argument form the last paragraph of Subsection
4.2
the quadratic maps
\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t)
and
\bm{\Phi}_{2}(t)
have the same
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
-component, hence

\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t_{1}^{\prime})[(0,1),\bm{q}^{(1)}(t_{1}^{\prime},\Delta u)]=\bm{\Psi}_{2}(t_{1}^{\prime})[(0,1),(0,1)]=\bm{\Phi}_{2}[(0,1),(0,1)]=a_{2}(t)\neq 0\ .

Now Lemma
5.8
point (
ii
) guarantees that
q(t)
is of class
C^{2}
on
[t_{0}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}]
. This ends the proof.
∎

6
Further study

The presented material provokes a few interesting questions:

•

Is it possible to improve the time-regularity of the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
in Thm.
4.13
? Answering this question would be an important step in solving the regularity problem of SR geodesics. Namely, if the
\tau
-regularity of
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
could be improved to, say being of class
C^{k}
, then in the 2-normal case equation (
3.6
) would guarantee that the control
u(t)
is also of class
C^{k}
, and thus the trajectory
q(t)
be of class
C^{k+1}
on each piece from a finite collection.

We
hypothesize
that it is possible to prove that if the control
u(t)
is of class
C^{r}
(where
r=0_{-},1_{-},1,2,\ldots
) then the dependence
\tau\mapsto\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
is of class
C^{r+1}
. This, by the standard bootstrap argument, will imply that every 2-normal trajectory
q(t)
is piece-wise smooth (with a finite number of pieces), thus smooth by the results of
[
HLD16
]
.

•

For 2-abnormal trajectories, as equation (
3.6
) does not determines the control
u(t)
, the question of regularity remains open regardless of the regularity properties of the minimal 2-jet
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0},\tau}(\bm{v})
. It seems that for such curves, one needs to understand the geometry of third-order (and higher) order expansion of the extended end-point map. Some research in this direction was initiated in
[
BMP20
]
, yet a fully satisfactory theory is yet to be developed.

•

Sub-Riemannian normal extremals are known to be locally minimizing. It is natural to ask whether the same is true for 2-normal extremals. We
hypothesize
that the answer to this question is positive, and could be provided by extending the methods from
[
JR16
]
.

•

Our proof is based on Agrachev-Sarychev Index Lemma (Lem.
3.1
). It seems to us that is is possible to (slightly) modify the proof of this result to guarantee the following result.

Hypothesis
. Let
q(t)
, with
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
be a minimizing strictly abnormal SR geodesic of co-rank
r
. Then there exists a covector
\varphi_{0}:\mathrm{T}_{q(t_{1})}M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and a symmetric 2-form
\bm{\Phi}_{2}:\mathrm{T}_{q(t_{1})}(M\times\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{T}_{q(t_{1})}(M\times\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that:

–

\big\langle\bm{b}^{(1)}(t_{1},\Delta u),\varphi_{0}\big\rangle=0
for every
\Delta u\in L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})

–

the negative index of the 2-form

L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})\ni\Delta u\longmapsto\big\langle\bm{b}^{(2)}(t_{1},\Delta u),\varphi_{0}\big\rangle-\bm{\Phi}_{2}[\bm{b}^{(1)}(t_{1},\Delta u),\bm{b}^{(1)}(t_{1},\Delta u)]

is at most
r
.

In the standard version the bi-linear term
\bm{\Phi}_{2}
is not present, as the 2-form is restricted to a subspace
\ker\operatorname{D}_{u}{\mathbf{End}}^{[t_{0},t_{1}]}_{q_{0}}\subset L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

It should be also possible to prove that necessarily
\bm{\Phi}_{2}[(0,1),(0,1)]\geq 0
, and so
a_{2}(t)
described by Thm
3.3
is always non-negative.

Such an improvement on Agrachev-Sarychev Lemma will allow to simplify the cutting procedure that we used in order to guarantee the finiteness of all minimal 2-jets
\widehat{Q}_{t_{0}^{\prime},\tau}(\bm{v})
, and thus reduce the estimate on the maximal number of divisions in Thm
3.3
.

We plan to address these questions in a future series of publications.
