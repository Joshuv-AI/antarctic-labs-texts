Expansion for a Fundamental Solution of Laplace’s Equation in Flat-Ring Cyclide Coordinates
Lijuan BI a, Howard S. COHL b and Hans VOLKMER c

Abstract
We derive an expansion for the fundamental solution of Laplace’s equation in flat-ring coordinates in three-dimensional Euclidean space. This expansion is a double series of products of functions that are harmonic in the interior and exterior of “flat rings”. These internal and external flat-ring harmonic functions are expressed in terms of simply-periodic Lamé functions. In a limiting case we obtain the expansion of the fundamental solution in toroidal coordinates.

Introduction

In 1875 Albert Wangerin
[
23
]
introduced a coordinate system
s
,
t
in the
xy
-plane by setting

x+{\rm i}y=\dn(s+{\rm i}t,k)
, where
\dn

[
18
, formula (22.2.6)]
is one of the Jacobian elliptic functions. By rotating the system about the
y
-axis
he obtained coordinates
s
,
t
,
\phi
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that are called flat-ring
coordinates by Moon and Spencer
[
16
, p. 126]
. Actually, Moon and Spencer use
x+{\rm i}y=\sn(s+{\rm i}t,k)

[
18
, formula (22.2.4)]
but this leads to the same coordinate system.
Wangerin showed that the Laplace equation

\Delta u=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z^{2}}=0

(1.1)

can be solved by functions of the form

u(x,y,z)={\mathcal{R}}(x,y,z)u_{1}(s)u_{2}(t)u_{3}(\phi),

(1.2)

where
{\mathcal{R}}
is a known elementary function called
the modulation factor (following Morse and Feshbach), and
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
u_{3}
solve
ordinary differential equations.
In our case

{\mathcal{R}}=R^{-1/2}
, where
R
is the distance to the axis of rotation.
This is referred to as
{\mathcal{R}}
-separation of variables
and we express this by saying that
the Laplace equation is
{\mathcal{R}}
-separable in flat-ring coordinates.
Wangerin won a prize for his original work but only for the first part of his paper. In the second part
Wangerin tried to expand the fundamental solution of the Laplace equation (given as the inverse distance to a fixed point),
in terms of the separated solutions (
1.2
). Such an expansion is needed in physical applications.
However, there were some obvious mistakes in this part of the paper. It is the purpose of the present paper to
derive the desired expansion of the fundamental solution in flat-ring coordinates. It is a little bit surprising, but, as far as we know, this work has not been carried out in all those years since 1875.

Flat-ring coordinates are a special instance of so-called cyclidic coordinate systems. In these systems the coordinate surfaces
are of order 4 (they are zero sets of polynomials of degree
4
in the Cartesian coordinates
x
,
y
,
z
). This is in contrast to quadric coordinate systems in which the coordinate surfaces have order 2 (like spherical coordinates).
Bôcher
[
3
]
listed quadric and cyclidic coordinate systems in which Laplace’s equation can be solved using separation of variables in his book “Über die Reihenentwicklungen der Potentialtheorie” published in 1894.
We can also find these quadric and cyclidic coordinates systems in Miller’s book
[
15
, Tables 14 on p. 164 and 17 on p. 210]
. The quadric coordinates are Cartesian, cylindrical, parabolic cylindrical, elliptic cylindrical, spherical,
prolate spheroidal, oblate spheroidal, parabolic, paraboloidal, sphero-conal, and ellipsoidal coordinates. The cyclidic coordinates are flat-ring cyclide, flat-disk cyclide, bi-cyclide, cap-cyclide, and 5-cyclide coordinates.
In the quadric systems we can choose
R=1
in (
1.2
) but this is not possible in the cyclidic systems.
In several of these coordinate systems the expansion of the fundamental solution is known; see
[
2
,
7
,
11
,
17
]
.

Let us consider perhaps the simplest rotationally-invariant cyclidic coordinate system which
{\mathcal{R}}
-separates Laplace’s equation in three-dimensions, the toroidal coordinate system.
As we show in Section
7.1
flat-ring coordinates reduce to toroidal coordinates in a limiting case.
Toroidal coordinates
[
13
, Section 8.10]
are given by

\displaystyle x=\frac{\sinh\tau\cos\phi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi},\qquad y=\frac{\sinh\tau\sin\phi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi},\qquad z=\frac{\sin\psi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi},

(1.3)

where
\tau>0
,
-\pi<\phi,\psi\leq\pi
. The coordinate surfaces
\tau={\rm constant}
are tori, i.e.,

\big(1+x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\big)^{2}=4\big(x^{2}+y^{2}\big)\coth^{2}\tau.

The coordinate surfaces
\psi={\rm constant}
are spherical bowls, i.e.,

(z-\cot\psi)^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\psi}.

The coordinate surfaces
\phi={\rm constant}
are half-planes, i.e.,

x\sin\phi=y\cos\phi.

Laplace’s equation (
1.1
) has solutions of the form

u(x,y,z)=(\cosh\tau-\cos\psi)^{1/2}u_{1}(\tau)u_{2}(\psi)u_{3}(\phi),

where
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
u_{3}
satisfy the following ordinary differential equations, respectively:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sinh\tau}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\bigg(\sinh\tau\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{1}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\bigg)-\bigg(n^{2}-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{m^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\tau}\bigg)u_{1}=0,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}u_{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}\psi^{2}}+n^{2}u_{2}=0,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}u_{3}}{{\mathrm{d}}\phi^{2}}+m^{2}u_{3}=0,

(1.6)

where
m
,
n
are separation parameters. The general solution of (
1.4
) is

u_{1}(\tau)=c_{1}P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\tau)+c_{2}Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\tau),

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants, and
P_{\nu}^{\mu}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,1]\to\mathbb{C}
and
Q_{\nu}^{\mu}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,1]{\to\mathbb{C}}
are the associated Legendre functions of the first and second kind respectively
defined in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function by
[
18
, functions (14.3.6) and (14.3.7)]

\displaystyle P_{\nu}^{\mu}(z):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\mu)}\bigg(\frac{z+1}{z-1}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}\mu}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-\nu,\nu+1}{1-\mu};\frac{1-z}{2}\right)\!,

(1.7)

and

\displaystyle Q_{\nu}^{\mu}(z):=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}\mu\pi}\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\nu+\mu+1)\big(z^{2}-1\big)^{\frac{1}{2}\mu}}{2^{\nu+1}\Gamma\big(\nu+\frac{3}{2}\big)z^{\nu+\mu+1}}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\frac{\nu+\mu+1}{2},\frac{\nu+\mu+2}{2}}{\nu+\frac 32};\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\!,

(1.8)

where
\nu+\mu\not\in-\mathbb{N}
.

The internal toroidal harmonics
[
13
, equation (8.11.8)]

G_{m,n}(x,y,z){:=}(\cosh\tau-\cos\psi)^{1/2}Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\tau){\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}n\psi}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}m\phi}\qquad\text{for}\quad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}

are harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
except for the
z
-axis. The external toroidal harmonics

[
13
, equation (8.11.9)]

H_{m,n}(x,y,z){:=}(\cosh\tau-\cos\psi)^{1/2}\,P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\tau){\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}n\psi}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}m\phi}\qquad\text{for}\quad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}

are harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
except for the unit circle
z=0
,
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
.
The desired expansion of the fundamental solution of Laplace’s equation in internal and external toroidal harmonics is

\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\ast}\|}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{m}\frac{\Gamma\big(n-m+\frac{1}{2}\big)}{\Gamma\big(n+m+\frac{1}{2}\big)}G_{m,n}(\mathbf{r})\overline{H_{m,n}(\mathbf{r}^{\ast})}

(1.9)

provided
\tau>\tau^{\ast}
, where
\tau
,
\tau^{\ast}
are toroidal coordinates of
\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)
and
\mathbf{r}^{\ast}=(x^{\ast},y^{\ast},z^{\ast})
, respectively.
Formula (
1.9
) follows by inserting
[
10
, Theorem 8.795.2]

Q_{\nu}(\cosh\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon_{k}(-1)^{k}P_{\nu}^{-k}(\cosh\tau)Q_{\nu}^{k}(\cosh\tau^{\ast})\cos(k(\psi-\psi^{\ast})),

where
\epsilon_{k}:=2-\delta_{k,0}
is the Neumann factor (commonly occurring in Fourier cosine series),
\cosh\alpha:=\cosh\tau\cosh\tau^{\ast}-\sinh\tau\sinh\tau^{\ast}\cos(\psi-\psi^{\ast})
,
with
\nu=n-\frac{1}{2}
, into the single summation
expression for the reciprocal distance between two points in toroidal coordinates given by Bateman

[
1
, Section 10.3, equation (26)]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\ast}\|}=\frac{\sqrt{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi}\sqrt{\cosh\tau^{\ast}-\cos\psi^{\ast}}}{\sqrt{2}\,a\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos(\psi-\psi^{\ast})}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\ast}\|}}{}=\frac{\sqrt{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi}\sqrt{\cosh\tau^{\ast}-\cos\psi^{\ast}}}{a\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\alpha){\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}n(\psi-\psi^{\ast})},

which is a direct consequence of Heine’s reciprocal square root identity

[
5
, formula (A5)]

\frac{1}{\sqrt{z-x}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\rm i}n\theta}Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(z),

where
x=\cos\theta
and
z>1
.

In this work we carry out the corresponding analysis in flat-ring coordinates. “Flat-rings” now play the role of
tori.
In Section
2
we consider flat-ring coordinates in the form used by Poole

[
19
,
20
]
. This form is the most convenient for our purposes.
In Section
3
we solve the Laplace equation by
{\mathcal{R}}
-separation of variables. The differential equation for
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
is the Lamé equation. In Section
4

5.1
), we find an integral representation of external flat-ring harmonics in terms of internal

In Sections

we compare the corresponding expansions of the fundamental solution.

2.1
Flat-ring coordinates in algebraic from

Flat-ring coordinates
\mu
,
\rho
,
\phi
form an orthogonal coordinate system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with rotational symmetry.
We define these coordinates by

x=\frac{\cos\phi}{T},\qquad y=\frac{\sin\phi}{T},\qquad z=\frac{1}{T}\bigg(\frac{-\mu\rho}{a}\bigg)^{1/2},

(2.1)

where

T=\bigg(\frac{(a-\mu)(a-\rho{)}}{a(a-1)}\bigg)^{1/2}+\bigg(\frac{(1-\mu)(1-\rho)}{a-1}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(2.2)

The parameter
a>1
is given. The coordinates
\mu
,
\rho
vary
according to

-\infty<\mu<0<\rho<1.

The roots appearing in (
2.1
), (
2.2
) are understood as positive roots.
Miller
[
15
, system (15), p. 210]
considers flat-ring coordinates
\mu^{\prime}
,
\rho^{\prime}
,
\phi
depending on a parameter
a^{\prime}
in a slightly different form. The connection is given by

a^{\prime}=\frac{a}{a-1},\qquad\mu=a-(a-1)\mu^{\prime},\qquad\rho=a-(a-1)\rho^{\prime},

where

1<\rho^{\prime}<\frac{a}{a-1}<\mu^{\prime}<\infty.

For
\phi=0
we obtain a planar coordinate system

x=\frac{1}{T},\qquad z=\frac{1}{T}\bigg(\frac{-\mu\rho}{a}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(2.3)

This defines a function
g
by setting
(x,z)=g(\mu,\rho)
. We introduce the sets

A=(-\infty,0)\times(0,1),\qquad Q=\big\{(x,z)\colon x,z>0,\,x^{2}+z^{2}<1\big\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

g
maps
A
bijectively onto
Q
. If
(x,z)=g(\mu,\rho)
then

\frac{\big(x^{2}+z^{2}+1\big)^{2}}{\tau-a}-\frac{\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2}}{\tau-1}-\frac{4z^{2}}{\tau}=0\qquad\text{for}\quad\tau=\mu,\rho,

(2.4)

so the inverse map
(\mu,\rho)=g^{-1}(x,z)
is given by

\mu=\frac{1}{8x^{2}}\big({-}\omega-\big(\omega^{2}+64ax^{2}z^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big),\qquad\rho=\frac{1}{8x^{2}}\big({-}\omega+(\omega^{2}+64ax^{2}z^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big),

(2.5)

where

\omega=4(1+a)z^{2}+a\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2}-\big(x^{2}+z^{2}+1\big)^{2}.

The map
g
and its inverse map are real-analytic.

Proof.

Let
(\mu,\rho)\in A
and
(x,z)=g(\mu,\rho)
. Then

\frac{1}{T}\bigg(1-\frac{\mu\rho}{a}\bigg)=\bigg(\frac{(a-\mu)(a-\rho)}{a(a-1)}\bigg)^{1/2}-\bigg(\frac{(1-\mu)(1-\rho)}{a-1}\bigg)^{1/2},

which leads to

\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}-1}{2x}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mu\rho}{a}-T\bigg)=-\bigg(\frac{(1-\mu)(1-\rho)}{a-1}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(2.6)

By a similar calculation we obtain

\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}+1}{2x}=\bigg(\frac{(a-\mu)(a-\rho)}{a(a-1)}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(2.7)

By (
2.6
) and (
2.7
),

\frac{\big(x^{2}+z^{2}+1\big)^{2}}{\mu-a}-\frac{\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2}}{\mu-1}-\frac{4z^{2}}{\mu}=4x^{2}\bigg(\frac{\rho-a}{a(a-1)}-\frac{\rho-1}{a-1}+\frac{\rho}{a}\bigg)=0.

With the same calculation we show that (
2.4
) also holds for
\tau=\rho
.

If
(x,z)=g(\mu,\rho)
with
(\mu,\rho)\in A
then it is clear that
x,z>0
, and (
2.6
) shows that
(x,z)\in Q
.
Conversely, suppose that
(x,z)\in Q
. We consider the quadratic polynomial

F(\tau)=\tau(\tau-1)(\tau-a)\bigg(\frac{\big(x^{2}+z^{2}+1\big)^{2}}{\tau-a}-\frac{\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2}}{\tau-1}-\frac{4z^{2}}{\tau}\bigg).

(2.8)

The factor of
\tau^{2}
is

\big(x^{2}+z^{2}+1\big)^{2}-\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2}-4z^{2}=4x^{2}>0.

Moreover,

\displaystyle F(0)=-4az^{2}<0,

\displaystyle F(1)=(a-1)\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2}>0.

It follows that there is a unique
(\mu,\rho)\in A
such that

F(\tau)=4x^{2}(\tau-\mu)(\tau-\rho)\qquad\text{for}\quad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.9)

By setting
\tau=0,1,a
in (
2.9
) it follows that

\displaystyle 4x^{2}\mu\rho=-4az^{2},

\displaystyle 4x^{2}(1-\mu)(1-\rho)=(a-1)\big(x^{2}+z^{2}-1\big)^{2},

\displaystyle 4x^{2}(a-\mu)(a-\rho)=a(a-1)\big(x^{2}+z^{2}+1\big)^{2}.

Then we obtain

\displaystyle T=\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}+1}{2x}-\frac{x^{2}+z^{2}-1}{2x}=\frac{1}{x}\qquad\text{and}\qquad z^{2}=x^{2}\frac{-\mu\rho}{a}=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\frac{-\mu\rho}{a}.

It follows that
g(\mu,\rho)=(x,z)
and so we have shown that
g
is surjective.

Suppose that
g(\mu,\rho)=g(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\rho})=(x,z)
. By (
2.4
),
\mu
,
\rho
,
\tilde{\mu}
,
\tilde{\rho}
are all roots of
F(\mu)=0
with
F
defined in (
2.8
).
Since
(\mu,\rho),(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\rho})\in A
, this implies
(\mu,\rho)=(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\rho})
. Thus
g
is injective.
It is clear that
g
and
g^{-1}
are analytic maps.

Flat-ring coordinates in transcendental form

We write
a=k^{-2}
with

, and set

\rho=\sn^{2}(s,k),\qquad\mu=\sn^{2}({\rm i}t,k),

(2.10)

where
\sn(u,k)
denotes the Jacobian elliptic function of modulus
k
(see
[

, Chapter XXII]
,

\cn(z,k)
,
\dn(z,k)
,

K=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}},\qquad K^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^{\prime 2}\sin^{2}\theta}}.

By (

),

(2.12)

When

\displaystyle x+{\rm i}z=f(s+{\rm i}t),\qquad f(w)=\frac{1}{k^{\prime}}(\dn(w,k)-k\,\cn(w,k)).

Each of the systems

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{4K^{2}}\leq a_{\nu}^{n}(k)\leq\nu(\nu+1)k^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{4K^{2}}\qquad\text{if}\quad\nu\geq 0,

\displaystyle\nu(\nu+1)k^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{4K^{2}}\leq a_{\nu}^{n}(k)\leq\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{4K^{2}}\qquad\text{if}\quad-\frac{1}{2}\leq\nu<0.

The same estimates hold with
b_{\nu}^{n+1}
in place of
a_{\nu}^{n}
.

Proof 4.2
.

The stated inequalities follow from

\displaystyle 0\leq\nu(\nu+1)k^{2}\sn^{2}(s,k)\leq\nu(\nu+1)k^{2}\qquad\text{if}\quad\nu\geq 0,

\displaystyle\nu(\nu+1)k^{2}\leq\nu(\nu+1)k^{2}\sn^{2}(s,k)\leq 0\qquad\text{if}\quad-\frac{1}{2}\leq\nu<0,

using a comparison theorem for eigenvalues
[
22
, Section 27.IX]
.

The next lemma is a refinement of a result in
[
8
, p. 288]

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
q\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function,
\lambda
a real number, and
y\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
a solution of the
differential equation

-y^{\prime\prime}+q(s)y=\lambda y

(4.2)

such that
y(a)y^{\prime}(a)=y(b)y^{\prime}(b)=0
, and
\int_{a}^{b}y(s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=1
.
Then

y(s)^{2}\leq 2(b-a)^{-1}+2(R(q))^{1/2}\qquad\text{for}\quad s\in[a,b],

where

R(q):=\max\{q(s)\colon a\leq s\leq b\}-\min\{q(s)\colon a\leq s\leq b\}.

Proof 4.4
.

We set

u=\min\{q(s)\colon a\leq s\leq b\},\qquad v=R(q).

Obviously, the lemma is true when
v=0
so we assume that
v>0
.
Since we may add the same constant to
q
and
\lambda
, we also assume that
u>0
.
Multiplying (
4.2
) by
y
and integrating from
a
to
b
we obtain

\int_{a}^{b}y^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s+\int_{a}^{b}q(s)y(s)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s=\lambda.

It follows that

\int_{a}^{b}y^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\lambda-u\leq\lambda.

(4.3)

Multiplying (
4.2
) by
y^{\prime}
and integrating from
s
to
t
, we obtain

y^{\prime}(t)^{2}+\lambda y(t)^{2}=2f(s,t)+y^{\prime}(s)^{2}+\lambda y(s)^{2},

(4.4)

where

f(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}q(r)y(r)y^{\prime}(r)\,{\mathrm{d}}r.

Integrating (
4.4
) from
s=a
to
s=b
, we find

y^{\prime}(t)^{2}+\lambda y(t)^{2}=(b-a)^{-1}\bigg(2\int_{a}^{b}f(s,t)\,{\mathrm{d}}s+\int_{a}^{b}y^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\lambda\bigg).

(4.5)

Using (
4.3
) we have

|f(s,t)|\leq(u+v)\bigg(\int_{a}^{b}y^{\prime}(s)^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\bigg)^{1/2}\leq(u+v)(\lambda-u)^{1/2}.

Therefore, (
4.3
) and (
4.5
) give

\lambda y(t)^{2}\leq y^{\prime}(t)^{2}+\lambda y(t)^{2}\leq 2(u+v)(\lambda-u)^{1/2}+(b-a)^{-1}2\lambda.

By (
4.3
),
\lambda\geq u>0
, so we can divide by
\lambda

y(t)^{2}\leq 2(b-a)^{-1}+2(u+v)\lambda^{-1}(\lambda-u)^{1/2}.

Now

\lambda^{-1}(\lambda-u)^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}u^{-1/2}\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda\geq u,

so we get

y(t)^{2}\leq 2(b-a)^{-1}+(u+v)u^{-1/2}.

Our
v>0
is given but we can choose
u>0
as we like.
Taking
u=v
we get the desired estimate.

Theorem 0
.

The internal flat-ring harmonics

Proof 5.1
.

From our discussion at the beginning of this section we know that

and

is harmonic on

except for along the

The inversion

5.2
The Dirichlet problem

Theorem
4.1
implies the following theorem.

Theorem 0
.

The system of functions

is an orthonormal basis in the Hilbert space

We use internal flat-ring harmonics to solve the following Dirichlet problem.
For a fixed
t_{0}\in(0,K^{\prime})
consider the region
D_{1}
defined in (
5.1
).
For a given function

defined on the boundary
\partial D_{1}
of
D_{1}
we find the function
u

which is harmonic on
D_{1}
and attains the boundary values
f
on
\partial D_{1}
in the weak sense.
The latter means that
\big(x^{2}+y^{2}\big)^{1/4}u
(expressed in terms of flat-ring coordinates

,

,

converges to

as

.
Since

has a positive distance to the

can be omitted in this definition.

We note that the solution of this Dirichlet problem is unique (if it exists).
To see this, assume that

is a harmonic function in
D_{1}
that attains the boundary values
0
in the weak sense.
Let

be the function obtained from

by expressing

).
This function is analytic for

and

For

,

,

define

By differentiation under the integral sign, integration by parts and using (

4.1
) with

Since

By assumption,

Theorem 0
.

Let

be a function defined on the boundary
\partial D_{1}
of the region

for some

.
Suppose that

is represented in the first variant of flat-ring coordinates as

Proof 5.2

Using flat-ring coordinates, we can write surface integrals over

As we show in Section

corresponding special functions found by separation of the Laplace equation in toroidal coordinates. In order to

Lemma 6.1
.

(a)

Let

, and let

be a sequence of real numbers such that

as

(b)

For

For every

be a nontrivial solution of the differential equation

y_{n}^{\prime\prime}+p_{n}(x)y_{n}^{\prime}+q_{n}(x)y_{n}=0

such that
y_{n}(b_{n})=0
.

(c)

Let
p_{\infty},q_{\infty}\colon[a,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous functions such that

p_{n}(x)\to p_{\infty}(x)
and
q_{n}(x)\to q_{\infty}(x)
as

uniformly on each compact interval
[a,b]
. Suppose that the differential equation

\displaystyle y_{\infty}^{\prime\prime}+p_{\infty}(x)y^{\prime}_{\infty}+q_{\infty}(x)y_{\infty}=0

admits a bounded nontrivial solution
y_{\infty}\colon[a,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, and that every solution of (
6.1

is unbounded as

.

Under assumptions
(a)
,
(b)
,

, we have

\frac{y_{n}(x)}{y_{n}(a)}\to\frac{y_{\infty}(x)}{y_{\infty}(a)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{y_{n}^{\prime}(x)}{y_{n}(a)}\to\frac{y^{\prime}_{\infty}(x)}{y_{\infty}(a)}

(6.2)

as

uniformly on every compact interval
[a,b]
.
The same result is true if the condition

is replaced by
y_{n}^{\prime}(b_{n})=0
.

Proof 6.2

Without loss of generality we assume that
y_{n}^{\prime}(b_{n})<0

Consider
y_{n}
for a fixed

This implies the contradiction

Therefore,

y_{n}(x)>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{n}^{\prime}(x)<0\qquad\text{for all}\quad x\in[a,b_{n}).

Suppose that the sequence

converges to

for all

.
Then

as

.
By assumption

for all

7.1
Flat-ring coordinates in the limit
\boldsymbol{k\to 0}

In the following we will frequently use that

K(k)\to\frac{1}{2}\pi,\qquad K^{\prime}(k)\to\infty\qquad\text{as}\quad k\to 0.

Moreover,

\sn(\zeta,k)\to\sin\zeta,\qquad\cn(\zeta,k)\to\cos\zeta,\qquad\dn(\zeta,k)\to 1\qquad\text{as}\quad k\to 0

(7.1)

locally uniformly on
\mathbb{C}

[
18
, Table 22.5.3]
and

\sn(\zeta,k)\to\tanh\zeta,\qquad\cn(\zeta,k)\to\sech\zeta,\qquad\dn(\zeta,k)\to\sech\zeta\qquad\text{as}\quad k\to 1

(7.2)

locally uniformly at all points
\zeta
such that
\cosh\zeta\neq 0

[
18
, Table 22.5.4]
.

We use the first variant of flat-ring coordinates
s\in(-2K,2K)
,
t\in(0,K^{\prime})
, and set
\tau=K^{\prime}-t
,
\psi=2K-s
.
Then

T=\frac{\dn(\psi,k)-\cn(\psi,k)\dn(\tau,k^{\prime})}{k^{\prime}\sn(\tau,k^{\prime})}.

Therefore,

\lim_{k\to 0}T=\frac{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi}{\sinh\tau}.

It follows that

\lim_{k\to 0}x=\frac{\sinh\tau\cos\phi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi},\qquad\lim_{k\to 0}y=\frac{\sinh\tau\sin\phi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi}.

Moreover,

z=\frac{k^{\prime}\sn(\psi,k)\cn(\tau,k^{\prime})}{\dn(\psi,k)-\cn(\psi,k)\dn(\tau,k^{\prime})}

gives

\lim_{k\to 0}z=\frac{\sin\psi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\psi}.

These limits can be taken for
\tau>0
and
\psi\in(0,2\pi)
.
Therefore, flat-ring coordinates become toroidal coordinates (

) as

.

\displaystyle\Ec_{\nu}^{0}(s,k)\to\bigg(\frac{2}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2},

Proof 7.1
.

The limits of the eigenvalues follow from

6.3

In the following theorem,

Theorem 2
.

Let
\tau_{0}>0
,
\nu\geq-\frac{1}{2}
,

\frac{\Ec_{\nu}^{n}({\rm i}(K^{\prime}-\tau),k)}{\Ec_{\nu}^{n}({\rm i}(K^{\prime}-\tau_{0}),k)}\to\frac{(\sinh\tau)^{1/2}\,Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\tau)}{(\sinh\tau_{0})^{1/2}\,Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\tau_{0})}

as

locally uniformly for
\operatorname{Re}\tau>0

The same results holds with
\Es_{\nu}^{n}
in place of
\Ec_{\nu}^{n}
for

Proof 7.2
.

The function
w(\tau)=\Ec_{\nu}^{n}({\rm i}(K^{\prime}-\tau),k)
,
0<\tau<2K^{\prime}
, satisfies the differential equation

w^{\prime\prime}+\left(\nu(\nu+1)-a_{\nu}^{n}(k)-\nu(\nu+1)\ns^{2}(\tau,k^{\prime})\right)w=0.

(7.3)

We would like to apply Lemma
6.1
to this differential equation but we cannot show that the coefficient of
w
is negative if
-\frac{1}{2}\leq\nu<0
.
Instead of
w
we consider the function

v(\tau):=\sn^{-1/2}(\tau,k^{\prime})w(\tau).

It satisfies the differential equation

v^{\prime\prime}+p(\tau,k)v^{\prime}+q(\tau,k)v=0,

(7.4)

where

\displaystyle p(\tau,k):=\cn(\tau,k^{\prime})\ds(\tau,k^{\prime}),

\displaystyle q(\tau,k):=\frac{1}{4}k^{2}-a_{\nu}^{n}(k)-\frac{3}{4}\dn^{2}(\tau,k^{\prime})-\bigg(\nu+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)^{2}\cs^{2}(\tau,k^{\prime}).

By Lemma

As

\displaystyle p(\tau,k)\to p(\tau):=(\cosh\tau\sinh\tau)^{-1},

\displaystyle q(\tau,k)\to q(\tau):=-n^{2}-\frac{3}{4}\cosh^{-2}\tau-\bigg(\nu+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)^{2}\sinh^{-2}\tau

locally uniformly for
\operatorname{Re}\tau>0
.
Up to a constant factor, the function

u(\tau)=(\cosh\tau)^{1/2}\,Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\tau)

is the only solution of the differential equation

u^{\prime\prime}+p(\tau)u^{\prime}+q(\tau)u=0,

which is bounded on the interval
[\tau_{0},\infty)
.
Now we apply Lemma
6.1
with
a=\tau_{0}
to the differential equation (
7.4
) as

, and we obtain the statement of the theorem
with uniform convergence on
[\tau_{0},b]

. The extension to locally uniform convergence for

follows from Lemma
6.3
applied to differential equation (
7.3
). The proof for
\Es
is very similar.

Let
\F_{\nu}(s,h,k)

be the solution of Lamé’s equation belonging to the exponent

at

, that is,

\F_{\nu}(s,h,k)=\tau^{\nu+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\tau^{j},\qquad\tau=K^{\prime}+{\rm i}s,\quad c_{0}=1.

By analytic continuation, this function is well-defined in the strip

Theorem 2

Proof 7.3
.

The function

\tau^{2}w^{\prime\prime}=p(\tau,h,k)w,\qquad p(\tau,h,k):=\tau^{2}\big(h-\nu(\nu+1)+\nu(\nu+1)\ns^{2}(\tau,k^{\prime})\big).

Now (

\tau\ns(\tau,k^{\prime})\to\tau\coth\tau\qquad\text{as}\quad k\to 0\quad\text{locally uniformly for}\quad|\operatorname{Im}\tau|<\pi.

Using Theorem

\displaystyle p(\tau,a_{\nu}^{n}(k),k)\to p_{\infty}(\tau):=\tau^{2}\big(n^{2}-\nu(\nu+1)+\nu(\nu+1)\coth^{2}\tau\big)

\displaystyle\phantom{p(\tau,a_{\nu}^{n}(k),k)\to p_{\infty}(\tau)\,}{}=\tau^{2}\big(n^{2}+\nu(\nu+1)\sinh^{-2}\tau\big)

as

Let
