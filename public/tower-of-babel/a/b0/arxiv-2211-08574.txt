Solutions to the SU( 𝒩 \mathcal{N} ) self-dual Yang–Mills equation
Shangshuai Li, Changzheng Qu, Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
In this paper we aim to derive solutions for the SU( 𝒩 \mathcal{N} ) self-dual Yang–Mills (SDYM) equation with arbitrary 𝒩 \mathcal{N} . A set of noncommutative relations are introduced to construct a matrix equation that can be reduced to the SDYM equation. It is shown that these relations can be generated from two different Sylvester equations, which correspond to the two Cauchy matrix schemes for the (matrix) Kadomtsev–Petviashvili hierarchy and the (matrix) Ablowitz–Kaup–Newell–Segur hierarchy, respectively. In each Cauchy matrix scheme we investigate the possible reductions that can lead to the SU ( 𝒩 ) (\mathcal{N}) SDYM equation and also analyze the physical significance of some solutions, i.e. being Hermitian, positive-definite and of determinant being one. Keywords: self-dual Yang–Mills equation, Cauchy matrix approach, Sylvester equation, exact solution, integrable system PACS numbers: 02.30.Ik, 02.30.Ks, 05.45.Yv

Introduction

The SU
(\mathcal{N})
self-dual Yang–Mills (SDYM) equation takes a form
[
1
]

(J_{\bar{y}}J^{-1})_{y}+(J_{\bar{z}}J^{-1})_{z}=0,

(1.1)

where
J
is a
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrix function of

(y,\bar{y},z,\bar{z})\in\mathbb{C}^{4}
.
To agree with the metric in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, it requires that
\bar{y}
and
\bar{z}

are complex conjugates of
y
and
z
, and
J
is a
positive-definite and Hermitian matrix with
|J|=1
(see Yang’s formulation
[
16
]
).
Note that the original Yang–Mills equation
[
17
]
is not integrable in general
but the self-dual gauge field equations are integrable, e.g., in the sense of Painlevé property
[
5
,
12
]
.
One can also refer to an early review
[
10
]
or a recent paper
[
6
]

and the references therein, or a recent theses
[
4
]
, for more details.

In the recent paper
[
6
]
we developed an approach to construct solutions to the
SU
(2)
SDYM equation by means of the Cauchy matrix method.
It is based on a framework of the Cauchy matrix approach
to the Ablowitz–Kaup–Newell–Segur (AKNS) equations.
Through introducing an independent variable
x_{0}
,
we were able to establish a set of noncommutative recursive and evolution relations
for master functions
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}

(see equations (22) and (23) in
[
6
]
),
which enable us to construct solutions to the SU
(2)
SDYM equation.
It is possible to extend the approach developed in
[
6
]
to
the SU
(\mathcal{N})
SDYM equation for
\mathcal{N}
being even, however, not for arbitrary
\mathcal{N}
.

In the present paper
we aim to construct solutions for the SU
(\mathcal{N})
SDYM equation with arbitrary
\mathcal{N}
.
Our plan is the following.
We will begin by
recalling the Cauchy matrix approach used in
[
6
]
for the SU
(2)
SDYM equation and
presenting a set of derivative and difference relations
for the master functions
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
(see (
2.4
) and (
2.5
)).
We naively assume these relations can be extended from
2\times 2
to

\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrices with arbitrary
\mathcal{N}
.
Then we will look for
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
that can match these relations.
It turns out that we can have two Cauchy matrix schemes to define such
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
,
which are associated with two different Sylvester equations,
corresponding to the (matrix) Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy
and the (matrix) AKNS hierarchy, respectively.
We will investigate how solutions of the SU
(\mathcal{N})
SDYM equations
are constructed from these two Cauchy matrix schemes
by imposing constraints.
As examples, we will discuss some explicit solutions of the SU
(2)
SDYM equation
with respect to their physical significance.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

we recall the Cauchy matrix approach for the SU
(2)
SDYM equation
and introduce a set of noncommutative evolution relations and a recursive relation
for the master matrix functions
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
,
and then we give two different Sylvester equations that can be used to define the master functions
subject to these relations. In Section
3
and
4

we investigate the two Sylvester equations and their related
SU
(\mathcal{N})
SDYM equations. At this stage the investigation of solutions will be
elaborated about their determinants and the Hermitian property.
We also analyze some solutions for their positive-definiteness.
Finally in Section
5
concluding remarks are given.
There are two appendices where in Appendix
A
we present
an alternative proof to obtain equation (
2.11
),
and in Appendix
B
we provide an example of dimension reduction
to a 3-dimensional system.

2
Cauchy matrix schemes

2.1
Cauchy matrix approach to the SU
(2)
SDYM equation

Let us briefly recall the Cauchy matrix approach used in
[
6
]

for deriving solutions to the SU
(2)
SDYM equation.
The Cauchy matrix approach is a method to construct and study integrable equations by
means of the Sylvester-type equations. In this approach integrable equations are presented as
closed forms of some functions (i.e.
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
) and their shifts (or derivatives).
It is first systematically used in
[
8
]
to
investigate ABS equations and later developed in
[
15
,
18
]
to more general cases.

Consider the Sylvester equation

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{M}-\bm{M}\bm{K}=\bm{r}\bm{s}^{T},

(2.1)

assigned with general dispersion relations

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{x_{n}}=\bm{AK}^{n}\bm{r},~~\bm{s}_{x_{n}}=\bm{A}(\bm{K}^{T})^{n}\bm{s},~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}),

(2.2)

where
\{x_{n}\}
are infinitely many complex independent variables,

\bm{K},\bm{M},\bm{A},\bm{r},\bm{s}
are block matrices

\displaystyle\bm{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}_{1}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{K}_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{M}_{1}\\
\bm{M}_{2}&\bm{0}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{N_{1}}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&-\bm{I}_{N_{2}}\end{pmatrix},~\bm{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{r}_{1}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{r}_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\bm{s}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{s}_{1}\\
\bm{s}_{2}&\bm{0}\\
\end{pmatrix},

with
\bm{K}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{i}\times N_{i}},\bm{M}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{1}\times N_{2}}[{\mathbf{x}}],\bm{M}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{2}\times N_{1}}[\mathbf{x}],\bm{r}_{i},\bm{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{N_{i}\times 1}[\mathbf{x}]
,

\bm{I}_{N_{i}}
being the
N_{i}
-th order identity matrix,

\mathbf{x}=(\cdots,x_{-1},x_{0},x_{1},\cdots)
, and
N_{1}+N_{2}=2N
.
It is also assumed that
\bm{K}_{1}
and
\bm{K}_{2}
are invertible and do not share any eigenvalues
so that for given
\bm{K},\bm{r},\bm{s}

the Sylvester equation (
2.1
) has a unique solution
\bm{M}

[
11
]
.
Define
2\times 2
matrix functions

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=\bm{s}^{T}\bm{K}^{j}(\bm{I}_{2N}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r},~~(i,j\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.3)

Using the Sylvester equation (
2.1
) one can derive a recursive relation for
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}

(cf.
[
15
,
20
]
):

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(0,j)}\bm{S}^{(i,0)}=\bm{S}^{(i+1,j)}-\bm{S}^{(i,j+1)},

(2.4)

which is independent of the dispersion relation (
2.2
).
By differentiate Sylvester equation (
2.1
) with respect to
x_{n}
and making use of (
2.2
),
one can derive evolution relations of
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}

(see
[
6
]
):

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i+n,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+n)}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},

\displaystyle(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}),

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=[\bm{S}^{(i,j)},\bm{a}],

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i+n,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+n)}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},

\displaystyle(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-}),

(2.5c)

where
\bm{a}=\mathbf{diag}(1,-1)
.
We call
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
master functions in the Cauchy matrix approach
since nonlinear equations arise as closed forms of them (see
[
8
,
15
,
18
]
).
These relations enabled us to obtain the equations
[
6
]

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}\bm{v}^{-1}=-\bm{u}_{x_{n}},~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}),

(2.6)

where
\bm{u}=\bm{S}^{(0,0)}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}
,
and consequently,

\displaystyle(\bm{v}_{x_{n+1}}\bm{v}^{-1})_{x_{m}}-(\bm{v}_{x_{m+1}}\bm{v}^{-1})_{x_{n}}=0,~~(n,m\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.7)

By properly imposing constraints on the coordinates
\{x_{n}\}
we were able to recover the SU
(2)
SDYM equation
(
1.1
) from (
2.7
).
More details can be referred from
[
6
]
.

2.2
General assumptions of derivative and difference relation

The relations (
2.4
) and (
2.5
),
which hold for the
2\times 2
matrix case,
are crucial and important to obtaining equation (
2.6
)
that finally leads us to the SU
(2)
SDYM equation.
However, the scheme used in
[
6
]
can only be
extended to the case of
\mathcal{N}
being even.
In this paper, we naively assume that such a set of relations hold as well for

\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrix functions
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}

with arbitrary
\mathcal{N}
.
This will immediately give rise to the equation (

)

are

SDYM equation (

Let us begin by stating our assumption.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose there exist matrix functions
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}}[\mathbf{x}]

that satisfy the evolution relations

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i+n,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+n)}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},

\displaystyle(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}),

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=[\bm{S}^{(i,j)},\bm{a}],

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j)}_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{(i+n,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+n)}+\sum_{l=-1}^{n}\bm{S}^{(n-1-l,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,l)},

\displaystyle(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{-})

(2.8c)

and the difference relation

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i+1,j)}-\bm{S}^{(i,j+1)}=\bm{S}^{(0,j)}\bm{S}^{(i,0)},

(2.9)

where
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\mathbf{x}\doteq(\cdots,x_{-1},x_{0},x_{1},\cdots)
,

\bm{a}=\mathrm{diag}(a^{(1)},\cdots,a^{(\mathcal{N})})
with
a^{(i)}\in\mathbb{C}
,
and
[A,B]=AB-BA
.
Define

\bm{U}\doteq\bm{S}^{(0,0)},~~\bm{V}\doteq\bm{I}_{\mathcal{N}}-\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}.

(2.10)

Then we have

\bm{V}_{x_{n+1}}\bm{V}^{-1}=-\bm{U}_{x_{n}},~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}),

(2.11)

and consequently,

\displaystyle(\bm{V}_{x_{n+1}}\bm{V}^{-1})_{x_{m}}-(\bm{V}_{x_{m+1}}\bm{V}^{-1})_{x_{n}}=0,~~(n,m\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.12)

The proof is the same as the one for Theorem 2 in
[
6
]
for the
2\times 2
case. We skip it.

2.3
Sylvester equations for equation (
2.12
)

Next, we need to look for well-defined
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrix functions

that can meet the assumed relations (

) and (

To achieve that, we introduce a general Sylvester equation

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{M}-\bm{M}\bm{L}=\bm{r}\bm{s}^{T},

where
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{C}_{N\times N}[\mathbf{x}]
,
\bm{K},\bm{L}\in\mathbb{C}_{N\times N}

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{x_{n}}=\bm{K}^{n}\bm{r}\bm{a},~~\bm{s}_{x_{n}}=-(\bm{L}^{T})^{n}\bm{s}\bm{a},~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}),

1

The above equations allow solutions

\displaystyle\bm{r}=(\bm{r}^{(1)},\bm{r}^{(2)},\cdots,\bm{r}^{(\mathcal{N})}),~~\bm{s}=(\bm{s}^{(1)},\bm{s}^{(2)},\cdots,\bm{s}^{(\mathcal{N})}),

\overset{\circ}{\bm{r}}{}^{(i)}

It is important that

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{x_{n}}=\sum^{n-1}_{l=0}\bm{K}^{n-1-l}\bm{r}\bm{a}\bm{s}^{T}\bm{L}^{l},~~~~(n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}).

Concluding remarks

In this paper we have established two Cauchy matrix schemes for the SU(
\mathcal{N}
) SDYM equation (
1.1
).
These schemes are based on two different Sylvester equations (asymmetric and symmetric cases),
both of which can generate the noncommutative relations (
2.8
) and (
2.9
)
that are used to obtain the unreduced equation (
2.12
).
This equation was then reduced to the SU(
\mathcal{N}
) SDYM equation
(in Yang’s formulation
[
16
,
1
]
) in Sec.
3
and Sec.
4
,
for the asymmetric and symmetric Sylvester equation cases, respectively.
As solutions, in both cases
\bm{V}
is Hermitian and
|\bm{V}|
is a constant.
The property of positive-definiteness of
\bm{V}

was investigated.
For the SU(2) SDYM equation and the obtained one-soliton solutions and two-soliton solutions,
we have worked out a way to obtain a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
where
\bm{V}
is positive-definite.
Note that our solutions are different from those of the SU(
\mathcal{N}
) SDYM equation
obtained using Darboux transformation method
[
9
]
.
In the two Cauchy matrix schemes, the dispersion relation of solutions can be characterized
by
\mathfrak{L}_{j}(\bm{x})
defined in (
3.24
),
which contains an arbitrary “
n
”. Such an arbitrariness allows us to have dimension reductions
with respect of
\bm{x}
, for example, to get solutions to a 3-dimensional
relativistic-invariant system studied by Manakov and Zakharov
[
7
]
.
We will present the example with more details in Appendix
B
.

To derive equation (
2.12
),
we also provided an alternative approach in Appendix
A
,
where we made use of matrix equation
\bm{\Phi}_{x_{n}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{A}\bm{P}_{n}

and the compatibility with
\bm{\Phi}_{x_{n+1}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{A}\bm{P}_{n+1}
.
Since
n
is arbitrary, one can also consider the compatibility between

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{x_{n}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{A}\bm{P}_{n},~~\bm{\Phi}_{x_{n+l}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{A}\bm{P}_{n+l},~~~l=2,\cdots,

and derive new equations. For the case
l
greater than one, more

\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
than
\bm{S}^{(0,0)}
and
\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}
will be needed.
In addition, it is worthy to mention that the two Cauchy matrix schemes that are involved
are respectively based on the Sylvester equation for the (matrix)
KP system (cf.
[
2
]
)
and the Sylvester equation for the (matrix) AKNS system (cf.
[
20
]
).
Since
\bm{V}
is defined via
\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}
, this implies possible links
between the SDYM equation and the equations related to
\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}
element
in the matrix KP hierarchy and matrix AKNS hierarchy.
In addition, in our paper, we reduce equation (
2.12
) to the SU(
\mathcal{N}
) SDYM equation in

\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
It is also possible to consider other reductions in different metric spaces.
All these will be the topics for the future investigation.

(2+1)

(see
[
13

14
]

),

to be large enough and all

subject to

3.24b
