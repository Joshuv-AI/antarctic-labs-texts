Introduction

A chemotaxis model with indirect signal production.
Chemotaxis, the biased movement of cells along
concentration gradients of a chemical signal, is known to play a significant role
in numerous biological circumstances such as
bacterial aggregation, spatial pattern formation, embryonic
morphogenesis, cell sorting, immune response, wounding healing,
tumor-induced angiogenesis, and also tumor invasion
(see
[
35
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
28
]
,

[
11
]
,
[
1
]
,

[
10
]
,
[
6
]
and

[
7
]
, for instance). The renowned Keller-Segel
model (cf. (
1.4
) below), describing the collective behavior of cells in response to a
signal produced by the cells themselves, has been well-studied
with regard to biological implications, but beyond this, during the last decades
quite a thorough comprehension of its mathematical features has grown
in various directions (
[
35
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
2
]
).

In contrast to this well-understood paradigmatic case, the theoretical understanding
is much less developed in situations when a chemotactic cue is not released by the cells themselves.
Typical examples for such mechanisms include cases when the signal is not produced at all, such as in
oxygenotaxis processes of swimming aerobic bacteria
which preferably move toward higher concentrations of externally provided oxygen as their nutrient
(
[
36
]
),
and also cases in which signal production occurs within more complex processes, possibly involving chemical
reactions or even cascades thereof, such as e.g. in the glycolysis reaction
(
[
9
]
,
[
29
]
;
cf. also
[
23
]
and
[
5
]
for further extensions of chemotaxis models involving
additional couplings.

It is the purpose of the present work to
achieve some insight into possible features of chemotaxis models
accounting for the latter type of more complex signal production mechanisms.
Specifically, we shall be concerned with the prototypical parabolic-elliptic-ODE system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v),&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
0=\Delta v-\mu(t)+w,&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
\tau w_{t}+\delta w=u,&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\,t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad w(x,0)=w_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

in the unit disk
\Omega:=B_{1}:=B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
where
\delta\geq 0
and
\tau>0
are fixed parameters and

\mu(t):=-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{\Omega}w(x,t)dx,\qquad t>0.

(1.2)

In a concrete biological framework,
this model arises as a simplification of the chemotaxis model recently proposed
by Strohm, Tyson and Powell in
[
32
]
to describe the spread
and aggregative behavior of the Mountain Pine Beetle (MPB) in a forest
habitat
considered negligibly thin in its vertical dimension.
Their model involves three variables: the density of flying
MPB, denoted by
u
, the density of nesting MPB, represented by
w
,
and the concentration
v
of beetle pheromone, the latter being secreted only
by those MBP which are nested in trees.
Besides random diffusive motion, the flying MPB can partially orient their
movement according to concentration gradients of MPB pheromone. Once MPB
nest they do not move any longer, thus meaning that apart from the
increase of
w
through transition from the flying to the nested state,
the only further quantity relevant to their evolution remains their death rate
\delta
.
For more details on the physical background, we refer the reader to
[
32
, Section 2]
.

From a mathematical point of view, (
1.1
) can be viewed as a variant of the Keller-Segel model
associated with the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v),&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
0=\Delta v-\widetilde{\mu}+u,&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

where
\widetilde{\mu}:=-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{\Omega}u\equiv-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
. which can formally be obtained from (
1.1
) upon taking

\tau\searrow 0
. In the case when
\Omega
coincides with the
entire space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the corresponding limit system of the
latter arises in the modeling of self-gravitating particles
(
[
4
]
), and furthermore it was introduced
in
[
16
]
as a simplification of the well-known classical
Keller-Segel model (
[
17
]
) of chemotaxis, the original version
of which being

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v),&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\[2.84526pt]
v_{t}=\Delta v-v+u,&x\in\Omega,\,t>0.\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

Here the hypotheses justifying the reduction of (
1.4
) to (
1.3
), namely the
physically meaningful assumptions that chemicals diffuse much faster than cells, and that the
particular signal substance in question degrades sufficiently slowly, have been used in various related
contexts and are also part of the simplification of the original model in
[
32
]
to (
1.1
)
(cf. also the review paper
[
14
]
).

Let us emphasize here the evident difference between (
1.1
) for
\tau>0
on the one hand and the two-component
Keller-Segel systems (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) on the other:
In both of the latter, the quantity
u

directly
produces the quantity
v
governing its cross-diffusion, whereas the corresponding signal production in (
1.1
)
occurs in an
indirect
process, with first
u
producing the
third quantity
w
, and with the latter being exclusively
responsible for the release of
v
.

Blow-up and critical mass phenomena.
It is known that chemotactic cross-diffusion terms, constituting
the apparently most characteristic model ingredient in all systems (
1.1
), (
1.3
) and (
1.4
),
may have a strong destabilizing potential and even enforce the formation of singularities.
Correspondingly, a striking feature of both Keller-Segel systems (
1.3
) and (
1.4
)
appears to be the occurrence of some solutions blowing up in finite time, which is commonly
viewed as mathematically expressing numerous processes of spontaneous cell aggregation which can be observed in
experiments (see
[
13
]
and also
[
2
]
for a survey).
Indeed, in the spatially two-dimensional framework considered here, the appearance of
such explosion phenomena is closely related to the initially present total mass
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
of cells.
For instance, it was shown in
[
16
]
and
[
3
]
that in
the spatially radial setting, the system (
1.3
) possesses some
solutions which blow up in finite time provided that this mass
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
is large enough, whereas solutions remain bounded whenever
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
is small; as a precise value distinguishing the respective mass
regimes either allowing for or suppressing explosions the
critical mass

m_{c}=8\pi
could be identified (cf.
[
3
]
,

[
26
]
and
[
30
]
for (
1.3
) and closely
related variants thereof).

As for the fully parabolic chemotaxis system (
1.4
), an analogous critical mass phenomenon is known to occur,
the respective threshold value again being
m_{c}=8\pi
in the radially symmetric situation.
For corresponding results on boundedness in the subcritical regime we refer to
[
25
]
;
some quite particular blow-up solutions with
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}>8\pi
have been detected in
[
12
]
,
whereas recently in
[
22
]
it was shown that such a singularity formation indeed occurs
within a considerably large set of supercritical-mass initial data, which can even be viewed generic in an
appropriate sense.

In the nonradial setting, corresponding critical mass phenomena seem to be present, with
a reduced value of
m_{c}=4\pi
. For parabolic-elliptic Keller-Segel systems, rigorous proofs for this can be found in,
or easily adapted from
[
26
]
and
[
25
]
; in the parabolic-parabolic case,
only a respective boundedness result is available in the case
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}<4\pi
(
[
25
]
),
whereas the occurrence of any nonradial finite-time blow-up solution to (
1.4
) appears to be a challenging
open problem (cf.
[
15
]
for a partial result on unboundedness).

Let us mention that in the spatially one-dimensional versions of
both (
1.3
) and (
1.4
), all solutions emanating from
conveniently smooth initial data are global in time and remain
uniformly bounded (
[
27
]
), while in three- or
higher-dimensional balls, for arbitrarily small values of
m>0
one
can find smooth initial data fulfilling
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}=m
, for which the
corresponding solution will blow up in finite time (see

[
24
]
for a parabolic-elliptic and
[
37
]

for the fully parabolic case). A critical mass phenomenon thus
occurs only in the two-dimensional situation.

Main results. A novel type of critical mass phenomenon.
It is the purpose of the present paper to rigorously
investigate the qualitative features of the system (
1.1
) with regard to its original intention to model
processes of aggregation.
Here our focus will be on the question in how far the indirect signal production mechanism in (
1.1
)
can enforce singularity formation in the first solution component
u
.
Our main results in this direction
show that actually also (
1.1
) exhibits a type of critical mass phenomenon, but that the latter appears to be
novel in the context of chemotaxis problems: Surprisingly, namely,
unlike that for (
1.3
) and (
1.4
), the mass threshold property we shall identify here will
refer to blow-up
in infinite time
rather than in finite time.

Indeed, by deriving energy-type estimates through rather straightforward testing procedures
we can first show that for all reasonably regular initial data with arbitrary mass
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
,
(
1.1
) is globally classically solvable:

Proposition 1.1

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
\tau>0
, and
suppose that
u_{0}\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
and
w_{0}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})

are nonnegative.
Then there exists a unique triple
(u,v,w)
of nonnegative functions

\displaystyle u\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\infty))\cap C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times(0,\infty)),

\displaystyle v\in C^{2,0}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\infty)),

\displaystyle w\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\infty)),

which solves (
1.1
) in the classical sense.

We shall next detect the number

\displaystyle m_{c}:=8\pi\delta

to be critical with regard to boundedness of radial solutions.
The first part of this characterization is contained in the following.

Theorem 1.2

Let
\delta>0
and
\tau>0
, and suppose that
u_{0}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
and
w_{0}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})

are radially symmetric and nonnegative, and that
m:=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle m<8\pi\delta.

Then the solution of (
1.1
) is bounded in
\Omega\times(0,\infty)
; that is, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle u(x,t)\leq C,\quad v(x,t)\leq C\quad\mbox{and}\quad w(x,t)\leq C\qquad\mbox{for all $x\in\Omega$ and }t>0.

Secondly, the above picture is completed by our final statement: In fact, for any
m>8\pi\delta

we shall derive an essentially explicit condition
on the radial initial data
u_{0}
and
w_{0}
which under the assumption
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}=m
ensures that in the large time limit,
the solution diverges exponentially in its first component when measured in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
:

Theorem 1.3

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
\tau>0
. Then for any

\displaystyle m>8\pi\delta

there exist
R\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>0
such that for each

\eta>0
one can find positive constants
\Gamma_{u}(m,\eta),\gamma(m,\eta)
and
\Gamma_{w}(m,\eta)

with the property
that for all radially symmetric nonnegative functions
u_{0}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
and

w_{0}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})

satisfying

\int_{\Omega}u_{0}=m>8\pi\delta

(1.5)

and

-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{r}}u_{0}\geq\Gamma_{u}(m,\eta)\qquad\mbox{for all }r\in(0,R)

(1.6)

and

-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}u_{0}\leq\gamma\qquad\mbox{for all }r\in(R,1)

(1.7)

as well as

-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{r}}w_{0}\geq-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}+\Gamma_{w}(m,\eta)\qquad\mbox{for all }r\in(0,R)

(1.8)

and

-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r}}w_{0}\leq-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}-\eta\qquad\mbox{for all }r\in(R,1),

(1.9)

the corresponding solution
(u,v,w)
of (
1.1
) is unbounded in the sense that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\geq ce^{\alpha t}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0

with some
c=c(m,\eta,\delta,\tau,\|w_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)})>0
.

As a particular consequence, this provides some quantitative information on the damping role of the death rate

\delta
in (
1.1
).
For instance, it follows from Theorem
1.2
that for any given initial data
(u_{0},w_{0})
the associated
solution will remain bounded whenever
\delta>0
is suitably large.
On the other hand, if
\delta
vanishes then unbounded solutions can be found for arbitrarily small values of the
initial mass
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}
.

Moreover, the criticality of
m_{c}=8\pi\delta
, as thus detected to predict the possibility or impossibility
of aggregation phenomena in (
1.1
) for positive values of
\tau
and
\delta
, appears to be consistent with the above
mass threshold properties of (
1.3
): Indeed, in the limit case
\tau=0
, in which in (
1.1
) clearly any
initial condition on
w
becomes obsolete, we will have
w\equiv\frac{1}{\delta}u
.
Hence, upon substituting
\tilde{u}:=\frac{1}{\delta}u
we see that we may assume that
\delta=1
, and that
(
1.1
) reduces to (
1.3
), having critical mass
m_{c}=8\pi=8\pi\delta
;
the fact that
m_{c}
is then related to finite-time blow-up, rather than to inifinite-time aggregation,
may be viewed as a consequence of the lacking relaxation mechanism reflected in the ODE for
w
in (
1.1
)
when
\tau>0
.
In summary, varying
\tau
over the interval
[0,\infty)
does not change the
value
of the critical mass,
but it significantly affects its precise
role
when passing from positive
\tau
to the case
\tau=0
.

Main ideas underlying our approach.
Let us briefly outline the methods we pursue in the derivation of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.
Our approach to both of these will be based on a transformation reducing (
1.1
) to an initial-boundary value problem
for a scalar degenerate parabolic equation.
Though well-established in related contexts, this transformation results in an equation which,
unlike the corresponding situation in the standard
Keller-Segel system (
1.3
) (
[
16
]
), now contains a nonlinear production term that is nonlocal in time.
More precisely, we shall see that the mass distribution function
U
associated with a given radial solution

u=u(r,t)
of (
1.1
), that is, the function defined by

\displaystyle U(\xi,t):=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}ru(r,t)dr,\qquad\xi\in[0,1],\ t\geq 0,

satisfies the single equation

U_{t}=4\xi U_{\xi\xi}+\frac{2}{\tau}\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\Big(U(\xi,s)-\frac{m}{2\pi}\xi\Big)\,ds\Bigg\}\cdot U_{\xi}+2\Big(W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi\Big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}U_{\xi},

(1.10)

for
\xi\in(0,1)
and
t>0
, where
W_{0}(\xi):=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}rw_{0}(r)dr
,
\xi\in[0,1]
, and
K_{0}:=W_{0}(1)

(cf. Lemma
4.1
). Clearly,
u
is bounded if and only if the spatial gradient
U_{\xi}
is bounded.
Fortunately, the corresponding parabolic operator allows for a comparison principle
(Lemma
4.2
), and thus enables us to focus our subsequent analysis on the construction
of appropriate super- and subsolutions.

Based on such a comparison argument, under the subcriticality assumption
m<8\pi\delta
from Theorem
1.2

we shall first obtain an estimate of the form
U(\xi,t)\leq C\xi
for all
(\xi,t)\in(0,1)\times(0,\infty)
and some
C>0

(Lemma
5.2
).
This means that given
\varepsilon>0
, adjusting
r_{0}\in(0,1)
suitably we can achieve that
the mass which the original solution accumulates in the ball
B_{r_{0}}(0)
satisfies
\int_{B_{r_{0}}(0)}u(x,t)dx<\varepsilon

for all
t>0
. In conjunction with a corresponding
\varepsilon
-regularity result (Section
5.3
) this will yield the
desired boundedness property of such solutions.

In the case
m>8\pi\delta
addressed in Theorem
1.3
, we will construct subsolutions exhibiting
gradient grow-up at the origin; that is, we shall find a family of adequate
subsolutions
\underline{U}
to (
1.10
) with the properties
\underline{U}(0,t)=0
for all
t>0
and

\underline{U}_{\xi}(0,t)\to+\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
Proving Theorem
1.3
then amounts to finding sufficient conditions for

u_{0}
and
w_{0}
ensuring that
U(\xi,0)\geq\underline{U}(\xi,0)
for all
\xi\in(0,1)
.

We find it worthwhile to underline here that the structure near the origin of the latter
comparison functions, to be explicitly constructed and analyzed in detail in Section
6
, will be given by

\underline{U}(\xi,t):=\frac{a(t)\xi}{b(t)+\xi},\qquad\xi\in[0,\xi_{0}),\ t\geq 0,

(1.11)

with
b(t)=b_{0}e^{-\alpha t}
,

, and appropriately chosen

,
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
,
b_{0}>0

and
\alpha>0
.
The idea for this construction originates from standard knowledge on equilibria for
the classical parabolic-elliptic Keller-Segel system obtained from (
1.3
) in the limit case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Indeed, choosing
a\equiv 4
and
b\equiv const.
in (
1.11
) one would rediscover a well-known
family of explicit radial steady states for the corrseponding version of (
1.3
) (
[
19
]

Local existence

The following basic result on local existence of solutions to (
1.1
)
can be proved by adapting approaches that are well-established in the context of
parabolic-elliptic models for taxis mechanisms involving both cross-diffusion terms and
ODE dynamics (cf.
[
34
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
18
]

and
[
8
]
, for instance).
Here we note that our assumption that
w_{0}
belong to
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
enables us to
use standard elliptic Schauder theory to gain appropriate knowledge on
the spatial regularity of
v
. Indeed, expressing
w
via the formula

w(x,t)=w_{0}(x)e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}u(x,s)ds,\qquad x\in\Omega,\ t>0,

(2.1)

we see that
v(\cdot,t)
actually solves the Poisson equation with a temporally nonlocal inhomogeneity
which thanks to the inclusion
w_{0}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})

will be Hölder continuous in
\overline{\Omega}
provided that
u(\cdot,t)
is sufficiently regular, where the
latter can be guaranteed by standard arguments involving appropriate smoothing properties of the Neumann heat
semigroup in
\Omega
.

Lemma 2.1

Let
\delta\geq 0
, and suppose that

u_{0}\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
and
w_{0}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
are nonnegative.
Then there exist
T_{max}\in(0,\infty]
and uniquely determined nonnegative functions

\displaystyle u\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{max}))\cap C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T_{max})),

\displaystyle v\in C^{2,0}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{max})),

\displaystyle w\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{max})),

which solve (
1.1
) classically in
\Omega\times(0,T_{max})
and which are such that

\mbox{if $T_{max}<\infty$, then}~\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow\infty\qquad\mbox{as $t\nearrow T_{max}$}.

(2.2)

The following identities describing the evolution of the total masses of the first and third components in (
1.1
)
can easily be checked.

Lemma 2.2

Let
\delta\geq 0
. Then the solution
(u,v,w)
of (

) satisfies

\int_{\Omega}u(\cdot,t)=m:=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\qquad\mbox{for all }t\in(0,T_{max}),

(2.3)

and for all

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w(\cdot,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\int_{\Omega}w_{0}+\frac{m}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}ds

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\int_{\Omega}w_{0}+\frac{m}{\delta}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t})&\mbox{if }\delta>0,\\[2.84526pt]

Integrating the first equation in (

) with respect

\Box

3
Global existence

The following basic statement on the time evolution of the functional
\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}

will be the starting point for our derivation of bounds for
u
, and also for
w
, in spaces of the form

L^{\infty}((0,T_{max});L^{p}(\Omega))
with
p>1
. Besides in Lemma
3.2
, it will be referred to in Lemma
5.4

below.

Lemma 3.1

Let
\delta\geq 0
. Then for all
p>1
, the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\{\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\bigg\}+\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+\delta\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\leq\frac{p-1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}w+\int_{\Omega}uw^{p}

(3.1)

for all
t\in(0,T_{max})
.

Proof.
We multiply the first equation in (
1.1
) by
u^{p-1}
and
integrate by parts using the identity
\Delta v=\mu(t)-w
to find
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p-1)\int_{\Omega}u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v

(3.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{p-1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\Delta v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{p-1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\Big(\mu(t)-w\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p-1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}w\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T_{max})$},

because
\mu(t)\geq 0
by Corollary
2.3
. On the other
hand, multiplying the third equation in (
1.1
) by
w^{p}
and
integrating with respect to
x\in\Omega
we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{p+1}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}+\delta\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}=\int_{\Omega}uw^{p}\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T_{max})$}.

Adding this to (
3.2
) proves (
3.1
).

\Box

Further estimating the terms on the right of (
3.1
)
shows that the functional in question actually satisfies the following autonomous differential inequality.

Lemma 3.2

Let
\delta\geq 0
. Then
for any
p>1
there exists
C(p)>0
such that the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\{\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\bigg\}\leq C(p)\cdot\bigg\{\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\bigg\}\qquad\mbox{for all }t\in(0,T_{max}).

(3.3)

Proof.
Let us first invoke the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality to fix
c_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{p+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\varphi^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{L^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}\|\nabla\varphi^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\cdot\|\varphi^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{\frac{2}{p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{p}}+c_{1}\|\varphi^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{\frac{2}{p}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}\|\nabla\varphi^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\cdot\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+c_{1}\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{p+1}\qquad\mbox{for all nonnegative $\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)$.}

We now let
\varepsilon:=\frac{2(p-1)}{mc_{1}p^{2}}

and use the Young inequality to estimate the two terms on the right of (
3.1
) according to

\frac{p-1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}w+\int_{\Omega}uw^{p}\leq 2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}+(\varepsilon^{-p}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{p}})\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T_{max})$.}

(3.5)

Here since
\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u=m
for all
t\in(0,T_{max})
due to Lemma
2.2
,
by the Hölder inequality and (
3.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon c_{1}m\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+2\varepsilon c_{1}m\bigg(\int_{\Omega}u\bigg)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon c_{1}m\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+2\varepsilon c_{1}m\cdot|\Omega|^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\cdot|\Omega|^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\qquad\mbox{for all }t\in(0,T_{max}).

Inserting this into (
3.5
) and recalling (
3.1
) proves (
3.3
).

\Box

We are now in the position to assert our global existence result for (
1.1
).

Proof
of Proposition
1.1
. For any given
T\in(0,T_{max})
, the ODI (
3.3
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\leq c_{1}(p,T)\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T)$}

with
c_{1}(p,T):=\Big(\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w_{0}^{p+1}\Big)\cdot e^{C(p)\cdot T}
, where
C(p)>0
is as defined by
Lemma
3.2
. Since
\tau>0
, this immediately yields

\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\leq pc_{2}(p,T)\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T)$}

(3.6)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\leq c_{2}(p,T)\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T)$}

with
c_{2}(p,T):=\max\{p,\frac{p+1}{\tau}\}\cdot c_{1}(p,T)
. From the latter and standard
elliptic regularity theory we obtain a bound for
v
in all spaces

L^{\infty}((0,T);W^{2,p}(\Omega))
for any
p\in(1,\infty)
,
whence in particular there exists
c_{3}(p,T)>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c_{3}(p,T)\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T)$.}

Along with (
3.6
), this ensures
that Lemma 4.1 in
[
33
]
becomes applicable so as to assert
via a Moser-type iteration that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c_{4}(p,T)\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(0,T)$}

holds for some
c_{4}(p,T)>0
. Finally, Proposition
1.1
is an evident consequence of this and the
extensibility criterion in Lemma
2.1
.

\Box

4
Radial solutions. A comparison principle

Throughout the sequel we shall assume that the initial data
u_{0}
and
w_{0}
, and hence clearly also all components of
the solution
(u,v,w)
, are radially symmetric with respect to the spatial origin, and unless stated otherwise we fix

m:=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}.

(4.1)

Then without danger of confusion we may and will switch to the usual radial notation and write
u=u(r,t)
for

r=|x|\in[0,1]
whenever this appears convenient.

Lemma 4.1

Suppose that
\delta\geq 0
, and given a radial solution
(u,v,w)
of (
1.1
), let

U(\xi,t):=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}ru(r,t)dr,\qquad\xi\in[0,1],\ t\geq 0.

(4.2)

Then

U(0,t)=0\quad\mbox{as well as}\quad U(1,t)=\frac{m}{2\pi}\qquad\mbox{for all }t\geq 0

(4.3)

and

U_{\xi}(\xi,t)\geq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>0.

(4.4)

Moreover,

{\cal P}U(\xi,t)=0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

(4.5)

where the operator
{\cal P}
is defined by setting

\displaystyle\hskip-8.53581pt{\cal P}\widetilde{U}(\xi,t):=\widetilde{U}_{t}-4\xi\widetilde{U}_{\xi\xi}-\frac{2}{\tau}\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\Big(\widetilde{U}(\xi,s)-\frac{m}{2\pi}\xi\Big)\,ds\Bigg\}\cdot\widetilde{U}_{\xi}-2\Big(W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi\Big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\widetilde{U}_{\xi}

(4.6)

for
\xi\in(0,1),t>0
and
\widetilde{U}\in C^{1}((0,1)\times(0,\infty))\cap C^{0}((0,\infty);W^{2,\infty}((0,1)))
, with

W_{0}(\xi):=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}rw_{0}(r)dr,\quad\xi\in[0,1],\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad K_{0}:=W_{0}(1)=\int_{0}^{1}rw_{0}(r)dr.

(4.7)

Proof.
The boundary properties in (
4.3
) are immediate from (
4.2
) and (
2.3
), whereas the monotonicity
statement in (
4.4
) is equivalent to the nonnegativity of
u
.
Moreover, upon differentiation in (
1.1
) we see that for
\xi\in(0,1)
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle U_{t}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}r\cdot\Big\{\frac{1}{r}(ru_{r})_{r}-\frac{1}{r}(ruv_{r})_{r}\Big\}\,dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\xi}u_{r}(\sqrt{\xi},t)-\sqrt{\xi}u(\sqrt{\xi},t)v_{r}(\sqrt{\xi},t),

where by (
4.2
) we have

\displaystyle u(\sqrt{\xi},t)=2U_{\xi}(\xi,t)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad u_{r}(\sqrt{\xi},t)=4\sqrt{\xi}U_{\xi\xi}(\xi,t).

Since the second equation in (
1.1
) implies that

\displaystyle rv_{r}(r,t)=-\int_{0}^{r}\rho w(\rho,t)d\rho+\frac{\mu(t)r^{2}}{2}\qquad\mbox{for all $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

we thus obtain

U_{t}=4\xi U_{\xi\xi}+2U_{\xi}W-\mu(t)\xi U_{\xi}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

(4.8)

with
W(\xi,t):=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}rw(r,t)dr
,
\xi\in[0,1],t\geq 0
.
Now by (
4.7
) and (
2.1
),

\displaystyle W(\xi,t)=W_{0}(\xi)e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}U(\xi,s)ds\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

whereas

\displaystyle\mu(t)=2K_{0}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}+\frac{m}{\pi\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}ds\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0

according to (
2.7
) and (
4.7
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle 2U_{\xi}W-\mu(t)\xi U_{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\tau}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}U(\xi,s)ds\bigg\}\cdot U_{\xi}(\xi,t)+2W_{0}(\xi)e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}U_{\xi}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle-2K_{0}\xi\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}U_{\xi}(\xi,t)-\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\frac{m}{\pi\tau}\xi ds\bigg\}\cdot U_{\xi}(\xi,t)

for all
\xi\in(0,1)
and
t>0
, which along with (
4.8
) proves (
4.5
).

\Box

Fortunately, the parabolic operator
{\cal P}
introduced above falls into a class of operators allowing for
a comparison principle.
To see this, for functions
A,B
and
D
to be specified below, let us consider

{\cal Q}\widetilde{U}(\xi,t):=\widetilde{U}_{t}(\xi,t)-A(\xi,t)\widetilde{U}_{\xi\xi}(\xi,t)-\bigg\{B(\xi,t)+\int_{0}^{t}D(\xi,t,s)\widetilde{U}(\xi,s)ds\bigg\}\cdot\widetilde{U}_{\xi}(\xi,t),\quad\xi\in(0,1),\ t\in(t_{0},T),

(4.9)

for
0\leq t_{0}<T
and sufficiently regular
\widetilde{U}:(0,1)\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then assuming, besides parabolicity, that the memory term has a favorable sign, we can indeed derive the following
comparison principle for spatially nondecreasing functions.

Lemma 4.2

Let
t_{0}\geq 0
and
T>t_{0}
, and suppose that
A\in C^{0}((0,1)\times(t_{0},T))
,
B\in C^{0}((0,1)\times(t_{0},T))
and

D\in C^{0}([0,1]\times[0,T]\times[0,T])
satisfy

A\geq 0\quad\mbox{in }(0,1)\times(t_{0},T)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad D\geq 0\quad\mbox{in }[0,1]\times[0,T]\times[0,T].

(4.10)

Moreover, assume that
\underline{U}
and
\overline{U}
are nonnegative functions belonging to

C^{0}([0,1]\times[0,T])\cap C^{1}((0,1)\times(t_{0},T))\cap C^{0}((t_{0},T);W^{2,\infty}((0,1))),

(4.11)

which are such that

0\leq\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi,t)\leq M\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t\in(t_{0},T)

(4.12)

with some
M>0
, and such that with
{\cal Q}
as defined in (
4.9
) we have

{\cal Q}\underline{U}(\cdot,t)\leq{\cal Q}\overline{U}(\cdot,t)\qquad\mbox{a.e.~in $(0,1)$ for all }t\in(t_{0},T).

(4.13)

Then if

\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq\overline{U}(\xi,t)\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in[0,1]$ and }t\in[0,t_{0}]

(4.14)

as well as

\underline{U}(0,t)\leq\overline{U}(0,t)\quad\mbox{for all }t\in[t_{0},T]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\underline{U}(1,t)\leq\overline{U}(1,t)\quad\mbox{for all }t\in[t_{0},T],

(4.15)

we have the global ordering property

\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq\overline{U}(\xi,t)\quad\mbox{for all $\xi\in[0,1]$ and }t\in[0,T].

(4.16)

Proof.
We let
c_{1}:=\|D\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)\times(0,T)\times(0,T))}
and
\alpha:=\sqrt{c_{1}M}
with
M
as in (
4.12
),
and for arbitrary
\varepsilon_{0}>0
we let

\varepsilon(t):=\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t},\qquad t\geq 0,

(4.17)

and

\displaystyle d(\xi,t):=\underline{U}(\xi,t)-\overline{U}(\xi,t)-\varepsilon(t)\qquad\mbox{for $\xi\in[0,1]$ and }t\in[0,T].

Then
d
is continuous in
[0,1]\times[0,T]
with

\displaystyle d(\xi,t)\leq-\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t}<0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in[0,1]$ and }t\in[0,t_{0}]

by (
4.14
) and

\displaystyle d(\xi,t)\leq-\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t}<0\qquad\mbox{for $\xi\in\{0,1\}$ and }t\in[t_{0},T]

according to (
4.15
).
Thus,

\displaystyle t_{\star}:=\sup\Big\{t\in(0,T)\ \Big|\ d<0\mbox{ in }[0,1]\times[0,t]\Big\}

is well-defined and satisfies
t_{\star}\in(t_{0},T]
, and if we had
t_{\star}<T
, then there would exist

\xi_{\star}\in(0,1)
such that

d(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})=\max_{\xi\in[0,1]}d(\xi,t_{\star})=0,

(4.18)

whence evidently

d_{t}(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})\geq 0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad d_{\xi}(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})=0,

(4.19)

because
d\in C^{1}((0,1)\times(t_{0},T))
by (
4.11
).
Now by (
4.13
) we know that there exists a null set
N\subset(0,1)
such that
d_{\xi\xi}(\xi,t_{\star})
exists
for all
\xi\in(0,1)\setminus N
and

\displaystyle d_{t}(\xi,t_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle A(\xi,t_{\star})d_{\xi\xi}(\xi,t_{\star})+\bigg\{B(\xi,t_{\star})+\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}d(\xi,t_{\star},s)\overline{U}(\xi,s)ds\bigg\}\cdot d_{\xi}(\xi,t_{\star})

(4.20)

\displaystyle+\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi,t_{\star})\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}D(\xi,t_{\star},s)d(\xi,s)ds

\displaystyle+\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi,t_{\star})\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}D(\xi,t_{\star},s)\cdot\varepsilon(s)ds-\varepsilon^{\prime}(t_{\star})\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,1)\setminus N.

In order to make appropriate use of (
4.19
) and the maximality property in (
4.18
), we observe that (
4.18
)
necessarily implies that there exists
(\xi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1)\setminus N
such that

\xi_{j}\to\xi_{\star}
as
j\to\infty
and

\displaystyle d_{\xi\xi}(\xi_{j},t_{\star})\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all }j\in\mathbb{N},

for otherwise we would have
\rm{essliminf}_{\xi\to\xi_{\star}}d_{\xi\xi}(\xi,t_{\star})>0
, contradicting (
4.18
).
Choosing
\xi=\xi_{j}
in (
4.20
) and using that (
4.19
) and (
4.10
) entail that

\displaystyle\limsup_{j\to\infty}d_{t}(\xi_{j},t_{\star})\geq 0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad d_{\xi}(\xi_{j},t_{\star})\to 0\qquad\mbox{as }j\to\infty,

we obtain on taking
j\to\infty
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}D(\xi_{\star},t_{\star},s)d(\xi_{\star},s)ds+\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}D(\xi_{\star},t_{\star},s)\cdot\varepsilon(s)ds-\varepsilon^{\prime}(t_{\star}).

(4.21)

Here since
d(\xi_{\star},s)<0
for all
s\in[0,t_{\star})
by definition of
t_{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}D(\xi_{\star},t_{\star},s)d(\xi_{\star},s)ds\leq 0,

because
D\geq 0
and
\underline{U}_{\xi}\geq 0
by (
4.10
) and (
4.12
).
Furthermore, (
4.12
) and our choice of
c_{1}
ensure that

\displaystyle\underline{U}_{\xi}(\xi_{\star},t_{\star})\cdot\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}D(\xi_{\star},t_{\star},s)\cdot\varepsilon(s)ds\leq Mc_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}\varepsilon(s)ds,

so that recalling (
4.17
), from (
4.21
) we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Mc_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{\star}}\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha s}ds-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t_{\star}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Mc_{1}\varepsilon_{0}}{\alpha}(e^{\alpha t_{\star}}-1)-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t_{\star}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{Mc_{1}}{\alpha^{2}}-1\Big)\cdot\alpha\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t_{\star}}.

Since
\frac{Mc_{1}}{\alpha^{2}}=1
according to our definition of
\alpha
, this absurd conclusion shows that actually

t_{\star}=T
and hence
\underline{U}\leq\overline{U}+\varepsilon_{0}e^{\alpha t}
throughout
[0,1]\times[0,T]
. On taking

\varepsilon_{0}\searrow 0
we finally arrive at the desired inequality.

\Box

5
Boundedness for
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}<8\pi\delta
. Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we shall make sure that small-mass solutions remain bounded in the sense of Theorem
1.2
.

5.1
A pointwise upper bound for
U

As a preliminary, let us prove the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 5.1

Let
m>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, and suppose that
\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1))
is such that
\varphi(0)=0
and

\varphi(\xi)\leq\frac{m}{2\pi}
for all
\xi\in(0,1)
. Then there exists
b\in(0,1)
such that

\varphi(\xi)\leq\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{(b+1+\varepsilon)\xi}{b+\xi}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,1).

(5.1)

Proof.
Since
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi_{\xi}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we can find
c_{1}>0
such that

\varphi(\xi)\leq c_{1}\xi
for all
\xi\in(0,1)
, where we may assume that
c_{1}>\frac{m}{2\pi}
.
Therefore, our assumption warrants that

\varphi(\xi)\leq\hat{\varphi}(\xi):=\min\Big\{c_{1}\xi,\frac{m}{2\pi}\Big\}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,1).

(5.2)

Now writing
\varphi_{b}(\xi):=\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{(b+1+\varepsilon)\xi}{b+\xi}
for
\xi\in[0,1]
and

b\in(0,1)
, we see that
the quotient
\frac{\hat{\varphi}}{\varphi_{b}}
admits a continuous extension
Q_{b}
to all of
[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle Q_{b}(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2\pi}{m}\cdot\frac{c_{1}(b+\xi)}{b+1+\varepsilon}&\mbox{if }\xi\in[0,\xi_{1}],\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{b+\xi}{(b+1+\varepsilon)\xi}&\mbox{if }\xi\in(\xi_{1},1],\end{array}\right.

where
\xi_{1}:=\frac{m}{2\pi c_{1}}\in(0,1)
thanks to our choice of
c_{1}
.
Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\frac{b+\xi}{b+1+\varepsilon}=\frac{1+\varepsilon-\xi}{(b+1+\varepsilon)^{2}}>0\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in[0,1],

it follows that as
b\searrow 0
we have

\displaystyle Q_{b}(\xi)\searrow Q(\xi):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2\pi}{m}\cdot\frac{c_{1}\xi}{1+\varepsilon}&\mbox{if }\xi\in[0,\xi_{1}],\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}&\mbox{if }\xi\in(\xi_{1},1].\end{array}\right.

As
Q
is continuous in
[0,1]
, Dini’s theorem asserts that the convergence
Q_{b}\to Q
is actually uniform in
[0,1]
.
Since
Q(\xi)\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}<1
for all
\xi\in[0,1]
, we can therefore pick some sufficiently small
b\in(0,1)

such that
Q_{b}(\xi)\leq 1
for all
\xi\in[0,1]
, which in view of (
5.2
) implies (
5.1
).

\Box

By means of a comparison argument, we can now prove that under the assumption
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}<8\pi\delta
, it is possible
to control the mass concentrating in small balls around the origin uniformly with respect to
t\in(0,\infty)

in the following sense.

Lemma 5.2

Let
\delta\geq 0
, and assume that
u_{0}
has the property that

m\equiv\int_{\Omega}u_{0}<8\pi\delta.

(5.5)

Then there exists
C>0
such that the function
U
defined in (

) satisfies

U(\xi,t)\leq C\xi\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>0.

(5.6)

Proof.
Since
m<8\pi\delta
, we can find
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle 8>\frac{m}{\pi\delta}(1+\varepsilon),

and thereupon choose
t_{0}>0
large fulfilling

8\geq\frac{m}{\pi\delta}(1+\varepsilon)+4c_{1}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t_{0}},

(5.7)

where

c_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

(5.8)

With these values of
\varepsilon
and
t_{0}
fixed, using that for all
t\in[0,t_{0}]
we have
U(0,t)=0
and

U(\xi,t)\leq U(1,t)=\frac{m}{2\pi}
for all
\xi\in[0,1]
by (

) and (
4.4
),
we can apply Lemma
5.1
to find
b\in(0,1)
satisfying

\max_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}U(\xi,t)\leq\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{(b+1+\varepsilon)\xi}{b+\xi}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in[0,1].

(5.9)

This means that if we let

\displaystyle\overline{U}(\xi,t):=\frac{a\xi}{b+\xi}\qquad\mbox{for $\xi\in[0,1]$ and }t\geq 0,

with

\displaystyle a:=\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot(b+1+\varepsilon),

then
U(\xi,t)\leq\overline{U}(\xi,t)
for all
\xi\in[0,1]
and
t\in[0,t_{0}]
. Moreover, clearly

0=U(0,t)\leq\overline{U}(0,t)=0
and
U(1,t)=\frac{m}{2\pi}<\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{b+1+\varepsilon}{b+1}=\overline{U}(1,t)
for all

t\geq t_{0}
.
Computing

\displaystyle\overline{U}_{t}=0,\quad\overline{U}_{\xi}=\frac{ab}{(b+\xi)^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{U}_{\xi\xi}=-\frac{2ab}{(b+\xi)^{3}}\qquad\mbox{for $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>t_{0},

we see that with
{\cal P}
as defined in (
4.6
) we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}\overline{U}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8ab\xi}{(b+\xi)^{3}}-\bigg\{\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\Big(\frac{a\xi}{b+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\xi\Big)ds\bigg\}\cdot\frac{ab}{(b+\xi)^{2}}

(5.10)

\displaystyle-2\Big(W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi\Big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\cdot\frac{ab}{(b+\xi)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ab\xi}{(b+\xi)^{2}}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{8}{b+\xi}-\frac{2}{\delta}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t})\cdot\Big(\frac{a}{b+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\Big)-2\Big(\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}\Big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\bigg\}

for all
\xi\in(0,1)
and
t>t_{0}
.
Here we use the definition of
a
and the nonnegativity of
e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}
to estimate

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\delta}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t})\cdot\Big(\frac{a}{b+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\delta}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t})\cdot\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\Big(\frac{b+1+\varepsilon}{b+\xi}-1\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\delta}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t})\cdot\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1+\varepsilon-\xi}{b+\xi}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\delta}\cdot\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1+\varepsilon}{b+\xi}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>t_{0}.

Since by (
4.7
) and (
5.8
) we have

\displaystyle W_{0}(\xi)\leq\|w_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\cdot\frac{(\sqrt{\xi})^{2}}{2}=c_{1}\xi\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,1),

we moreover see that

\displaystyle 2\Big(\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}\Big)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2c_{1}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\leq 2c_{1}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t_{0}}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>t_{0}.

According to (
5.7
), the identity (
5.10
) thus shows that

\displaystyle{\cal P}\overline{U}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{ab\xi}{(b+\xi)^{2}}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{8}{b+\xi}-\frac{2}{\delta}\cdot\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1+\varepsilon}{b+\xi}-2c_{1}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t_{0}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ab\xi}{(b+\xi)^{3}}\cdot\bigg\{8-\frac{m}{\pi\delta}(1+\varepsilon)-2c_{1}(b+\xi)e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t_{0}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{ab\xi}{(b+\xi)^{3}}\cdot\bigg\{8-\frac{m}{\pi\delta}(1+\varepsilon)-4c_{1}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t_{0}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>t_{0},

where we have used that
b+\xi\leq b+1\leq 2
, because
b<1
.
By comparison on the basis of Lemma
4.2
, we thereby conclude that
\overline{U}\geq U
in
(0,1)\times(0,\infty)
,
which in particular shows that

\displaystyle U(\xi,t)\leq\frac{m(b+1+\varepsilon)}{2\pi b}\cdot\xi\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

and thereby completes the proof.

\Box

5.2
Boundedness away from the origin

In the case
\delta>0

when the third equation in (
1.1
) contains an absorption term, radial solutions can become unbounded in their
first component
u
only near the spatial origin.
This is contained in the following lemma, the outcome of which will be an essential ingredient to our
\varepsilon
-regularity
result in Section
5.3
, and hence in establishing Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 5.3

Let
\delta>0
. Then for all
r_{0}\in(0,1)
there exists
C(r_{0})>0
such that the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

u(x,t)\leq C(r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all $x\in\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}$ and }t>0.

(5.11)

Proof.
We evidently only need to consider the case when
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
, in which we proceed in six setps.

Step 1.
We first claim that there exists
c_{1}>0
such that

|v_{r}(r,t)|\leq\frac{c_{1}}{r}\qquad\mbox{for all $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0.

(5.12)

To verify this, we write the second equation in (
1.1
) in the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}(rv_{r})_{r}=\mu(t)-w\qquad\mbox{for $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

multiply this by
r
and integrate using
v_{r}(0,t)=0
for all
t>0
to see that

\displaystyle rv_{r}(r,t)=\frac{\mu(t)r^{2}}{2}-\int_{0}^{r}\rho w(\rho,t)d\rho\qquad\mbox{for all $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0.

Since
w\geq 0
and
\int_{0}^{1}\rho w(\rho,t)d\rho=\frac{\mu(t)}{2}
for all
t>0
by (
1.2
), from this we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu(t)}{2}\leq rv_{r}(r,t)\leq\frac{\mu(t)r^{2}}{2}\qquad\mbox{for all $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0.

This implies (
5.12
) if we choose
c_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\infty))}
which is finite according to
(
2.7
).

Step 2.
We next assert that for all
p\in(0,1)
and each
r_{0}\in(0,1)
we can find
c_{2}(p,r_{0})>0
fulfilling

\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}\leq c_{2}(p,r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

(5.13)

To this end, we fix a radially symmetric
\zeta\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
in
\Omega
,

\zeta\equiv 1
in
\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}
and
\zeta\equiv 0
in
B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}
, and multiply the first equation
in (
1.1
) by
\zeta^{2}u^{p-1}
to see upon integrating by parts that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-p)\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}-2\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta

(5.14)

\displaystyle-(1-p)\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+2\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{p}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0,

where we note that
u
, and hence also
u^{p-1}
and
u^{p-2}
, is smooth and positive in
\overline{\Omega}\times(0,\infty)

thanks to our assumption that
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
and the strong maximum principle.
Now by Young’s inequality, the Hölder inequality and (
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\bigg|-2\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{1-p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{m^{p}}{1-p}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\zeta|^{\frac{2}{1-p}}\bigg)^{1-p}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

By the same token combined with (
5.12
),

\displaystyle\bigg|-(1-p)\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+(1-p)\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p}|\nabla v|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+(1-p)m^{p}\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}}|\nabla v|^{\frac{2}{1-p}}\bigg)^{1-p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+c_{3}(p,r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0

with
c_{3}(p,r_{0}):=c_{1}^{2}\cdot(1-p)m^{p}\cdot\Big(2\pi\int_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}^{1}r^{1-\frac{2}{1-p}}dr\Big)^{1-p}
.
Similarly, we find
c_{4}(p,r_{0})>0
fulfilling

\displaystyle\bigg|2\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{p}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta\bigg|\leq 2m^{p}\bigg(\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}}|\nabla v|^{\frac{1}{1-p}}\cdot|\nabla\zeta|^{\frac{1}{1-p}}\bigg)^{1-p}\leq c_{4}(p,r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0,

whence (
5.14
) altogether yields
c_{5}(p,r_{0})>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p}\geq\frac{1-p}{4}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}-c_{5}(p,r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

After a time integration and another application of the Hölder inequality and (
2.3
), we thus obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{4}\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}u^{p}(\cdot,t+1)+c_{5}(p,r_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{m^{p}\cdot\pi^{1-p}}{p}+c_{5}(p,r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0,

which entails (
5.13
) in view of the fact that
\zeta\equiv 1
in
\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}
.

Step 3.
We now make sure that for all
r_{0}\in(0,1)
we can find
c_{6}(r_{0})>0
satisfying

\int_{t}^{t+1}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}})}ds\leq c_{6}(r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

(5.15)

To this end, let us fix an arbitrary
p\in(0,1)
. Then again by radial symmetry we may combine the one-dimensional
version of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality with the outcome of Step 2 and (
2.3
) to fix positive constants

c_{7}(r_{0})
,
c_{8}(r_{0})
and
c_{9}(r_{0})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+1}\|u^{\frac{p}{2}}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}((r_{0},1))}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{7}(r_{0})\int_{t}^{t+1}\bigg\{\|(u^{\frac{p}{2}})_{r}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\cdot\|u^{\frac{p}{2}}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\frac{2}{p}}((r_{0},1))}^{\frac{2}{p}}

\displaystyle\hskip 65.44133pt+\|u^{\frac{p}{2}}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\frac{2}{p}}((r_{0},1))}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}\bigg\}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{8}(r_{0})\int_{t}^{t+1}\Big\{\|(u^{\frac{p}{2}})_{r}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}((r_{0},1))}^{2}+1\Big\}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{9}(r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

Since
\|u^{\frac{p}{2}}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}((r_{0},1))}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}=\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}((r_{0},1))}^{p+1}
,
an application of the Hölder inequality thereupon yields (
5.15
).

Step 4.
We proceed to show that for any
r_{0}\in(0,1)
there exists
c_{10}(r_{0})>0
such that

\|w(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}})}\leq c_{10}(r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

(5.16)

Indeed, given
t>0
we write
I_{j}:=(t-j-1,t-j)\cap(0,\infty)
for nonnegative integers
j
, and representing

w(\cdot,t)
according to
w(\cdot,t)=e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}w_{0}+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}u(\cdot,s)ds
we can estimate

\displaystyle\|w(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{I_{j}}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}})}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}j}\int_{I_{j}}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}})}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+c_{6}(r_{0})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\delta j}{\tau}}.

Since the rightmost series converges thanks to our assumption
\delta>0
, this proves (
5.16
).

Step 5.
Next, we prove that for each
r_{0}\in(0,1)
we can fix
c_{11}(r_{0})>0
such that

\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{r_{0}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq c_{11}(r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t\geq 1.

(5.17)

Since
t\geq 1
, Step 3 allows us to pick
t_{0}\in(t-1,t)
such that

\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}})}\leq c_{6}\Big(\frac{r_{0}}{2}\Big).

(5.18)

Then using
\zeta
as introduced in Step 2, by a straightforward testing procedure we infer that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{2}(\cdot,s)+\int_{\Omega}\zeta|\nabla u(\cdot,s)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta+\int_{\Omega}\zeta u\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\Delta\zeta-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{2}\Delta v+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\Big(\Delta\zeta+\zeta w+\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta\Big)\qquad\mbox{for all }s\in(t_{0},t+1).

Thus, by Step 4, Step 1 and (
2.3
), we can find
c_{12}(r_{0})>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{2}(\cdot,s)+\int_{\Omega}\zeta|\nabla u(\cdot,s)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{12}(r_{0})\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}}u^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{12}(r_{0})\cdot m\cdot\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}})}\qquad\mbox{for all }s\in(t_{0},t+1),

whence integrating and using (
5.18
) and Step 3 shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{2}(\cdot,t+1)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega}\zeta|\nabla u|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\zeta u^{2}(\cdot,t_{0})+c_{12}(r_{0})\cdot m\cdot\int_{t_{0}}^{t+1}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}})}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}c_{6}^{2}\Big(\frac{r_{0}}{2}\Big)+c_{12}(r_{0})\cdot m\cdot 2c_{6}\Big(\frac{r_{0}}{2}\Big).

As
t_{0}<t
, this implies (
5.17
).

Step 6.
Conclusion. Again with
\zeta
as in Step 2, we let
\widetilde{u}(r,t):=\zeta(r)u(r,t)
for
r\in[0,1]
and
t\geq 0
.
Then

\widetilde{u}_{t}=\widetilde{u}_{rr}+f(r,t)\qquad\mbox{for all $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0,

(5.19)

with

\displaystyle f(r,t):=\frac{1}{r}\zeta u_{r}-2\zeta_{r}u_{r}-\zeta_{rr}u-\zeta u_{r}v_{r}-\mu(t)\zeta u+\zeta uw\qquad\mbox{for $r\in(0,1)$ and }t>0.

By the outcome of Step 1, Step 3, Step 4 and Step 5, for some
c_{13}(r_{0})>0
we have

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+2}\|f(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}ds\leq c_{13}(r_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }t_{0}\geq 1.

(5.20)

Now given
t\geq 2
, once more by Step 3 we can fix
t_{0}\in(t-1,t)
fulfilling

\|\widetilde{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1))}\leq c_{6}\Big(\frac{r_{0}}{2}\Big).

(5.21)

Since
\widetilde{u}_{r}=0
on
\partial(0,1)
, the variation-of-constants representation of
\widetilde{u}
in terms of the one-dimensional
Neumann heat semigroup
(e^{\tau\Delta})_{\tau\geq 0}
on the interval
(0,1)
shows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}(\cdot,t)=e^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}\widetilde{u}(\cdot,t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(\cdot,s)ds.

Therefore, using standard smoothing estimates (
[
31
]
) along with (
5.21
), the Hölder inequality
and (
5.20
) we can find
c_{14}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{u}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1))}+c_{14}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\|f(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{6}\Big(\frac{r_{0}}{2}\Big)+c_{14}\bigg(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}ds\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\bigg(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|f(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}ds\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{6}\Big(\frac{r_{0}}{2}\Big)+c_{14}\cdot(2\sqrt{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\big(c_{13}(r_{0})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
\widetilde{u}(r,t)=u(r,t)
for all
r>r_{0}
, this establishes (
5.11
).

5.3
An
\varepsilon
-regularity result. Proof of Theorem
1.2

In deriving Theorem
1.2
from Lemma
5.2
, we shall
make use of a regularity statement which says that solutions already
must remain bounded if only their mass concentrating in an
arbitrarily small ball centered at the origin is sufficiently small.
A first step toward this is achieved in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4

Let
\delta>0
. Then for all
p>1
there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(p)>0
such that if for some
r_{0}\in(0,1)
,
a radial solution of (
1.1
) satisfies

\int_{B_{r_{0}}}u(x,t)dx<\varepsilon\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0,

(5.22)

then

\sup_{t>0}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}(x,t)dx<\infty.

(5.23)

Proof.
Using Young’s inequality, given
p>1
we can find
c_{1}=c_{1}(p)>0
such that

\frac{p-1}{p}A^{p}B+AB^{p}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}B^{p+1}+c_{1}A^{p+1}\qquad\mbox{for all $A\geq 0$ and }B\geq 0.

(5.24)

Moreover, the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality says that with some
c_{2}=c_{2}(p)>0
we have

\|\varphi\|_{L^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}\leq c_{2}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\frac{2}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{p}}+c_{2}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\frac{2}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}\qquad\mbox{for all }\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega).

(5.25)

We claim that if (
5.22
) holds with some
r_{0}\in(0,1)
and

\varepsilon:=\frac{p-1}{c_{1}c_{2}p^{2}},

(5.26)

then (
5.23
) must be valid.
To see this, we apply Lemma
3.1
and estimate the terms on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) by means of
(
5.24
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg\{\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}+\frac{\tau}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\bigg\}+\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+\delta\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\int_{\Omega}w^{p+1}+c_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}

(5.27)

for all
t>0
.
We now fix
\zeta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
0\leq\zeta\leq 1
in
\Omega
,
\zeta\equiv 1
in
B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}

and
{\rm supp}\,\zeta\subset B_{r_{0}}
, and split

c_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p+1}=c_{1}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}u^{p+1}+c_{1}\int_{\Omega}(1-\zeta^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}})u^{p+1},

(5.28)

where according to Lemma
5.3
we can find
c_{3}=c_{3}(p,r_{0})>0
such that

c_{1}\int_{\Omega}(1-\zeta^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}})u^{p+1}\leq c_{1}\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}}u^{p+1}\leq c_{3}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

(5.29)

The first term on the right of (
5.28
) can be estimated using (
5.25
) according to

\displaystyle c_{1}\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}u^{p+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}\|\zeta u^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{L^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}

(5.30)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}c_{2}\|\nabla(\zeta u^{\frac{p}{2}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\zeta u^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{L^{\frac{2}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2}{p}}+c_{1}c_{2}\|\zeta u^{\frac{p}{2}}\|_{L^{\frac{2}{p}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2(p+1)}{p}}.

Here since
\nabla(\zeta u^{\frac{p}{2}})=\zeta\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}+u^{\frac{p}{2}}\nabla\zeta
, again by Lemma
5.3

we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla(\zeta u^{\frac{p}{2}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\zeta^{2}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+2\int_{\Omega}u^{p}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}|^{2}+c_{4}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0

with some
c_{4}=c_{4}(p,r_{0})>0
, because
{\rm supp}\,\nabla\zeta\subset\Omega\setminus B_{\frac{r_{0}}{2}}
.

.

The technical key toward our proof of infinite-time blow-up in
the supercritical case is contained in the following lemma which
says that in the supercritical mass case we can achieve that
\underline{U}

is a subsolution in the inner region for suitably large times upon
an appropriate choice of the parameters.

Lemma 6.5

Let
\delta\geq 0
and

m>8\pi\delta,

(6.16)

and suppose that taking
W_{0}
and
K_{0}
from (
4.7
), we have

W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi\geq 0\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,1).

(6.17)

Then there exist
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{\star}>0
with the property that for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{\star})

one can find
b_{0}\in(0,\xi_{0}^{2})
and
t_{0}>0
such that with

b(t):=b_{0}e^{-\alpha t}\qquad\mbox{for }t\geq 0

(6.18)

and
a\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
as given by (
6.7
), the function
\underline{U}
in (
6.1
) satisfies

{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\geq t_{0},

(6.19)

where
{\cal P}
is given by (
4.6
).

Proof.
We detail the proof for the case when
\delta
is positive, leaving the minor modifications necessary for
the limit case
\delta=0
to the reader.
Since
m>8\pi\delta
, we can pick
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small enough such that

c_{1}:=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{3}m}{(1+\varepsilon)\pi\delta}-8>0,

(6.20)

and thereafter fix
\alpha_{\star}>0
and
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
small fulfilling

\alpha_{\star}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{4}

(6.21)

and

\alpha_{\star}\leq\frac{\delta\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}{\tau\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

(6.22)

as well as

\xi_{0}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(6.23)

Given
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{\star})
, we then choose
b_{0}>0
suitably small and
t_{0}>0
sufficiently large such that

b_{0}\leq\varepsilon\xi_{0}^{2}

(6.24)

and

t_{0}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon},

(6.25)

and thereupon let
b,a
and
\underline{U}
be defined by (
6.18
), (
6.7
) and (
6.1
).
Then by (
6.17
), Lemma
6.1
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{(b(t)+\xi)^{2}}{a(t)b(t)\xi}\cdot{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq J(\xi,t):=\frac{a^{\prime}(b+\xi)}{ab}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{b}+\frac{8}{b+\xi}-\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{a(s)}{b(s)+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\bigg\}ds

(6.26)

for all
\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})
and
t>0
.
Here by Lemma
6.4
, we can estimate

\displaystyle J_{1}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{\prime}(b+\xi)}{ab}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{b}

(6.27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}+1\bigg)\cdot\frac{b^{\prime}}{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}+1\bigg)\cdot\alpha

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\xi_{0}}\cdot\alpha\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

Next, to estimate the integral in (
6.26
) we first note that (
6.23
) guarantees that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\cdot\frac{a(t)}{b(t)+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\bigg\{\varepsilon\cdot\frac{b+\xi_{0}}{b+\xi}\cdot\frac{b+\xi_{0}}{b+\xi_{0}^{2}}-1\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\bigg\{\varepsilon\cdot 1\cdot\frac{\xi_{0}}{2\xi_{0}^{2}}-1\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t>0,

and that

\displaystyle a(t)\geq\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{\xi_{0}^{2}}{b+\xi_{0}^{2}}\geq\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{\xi_{0}^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)\xi_{0}^{2}}=\frac{m}{2(1+\varepsilon)\pi}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0

by (
6.24
). Therefore,

\displaystyle J_{2}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{b(t)+\xi}-\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{a(s)}{b(s)+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\bigg\}ds

(6.28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8}{b(t)+\xi}-\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\frac{a(s)}{b(s)+\xi}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8}{b(t)+\xi}-\frac{(1-\varepsilon)m}{(1+\varepsilon)\pi\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\frac{1}{b(s)+\xi}ds\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t>0.

Now whenever
t\geq s\geq t-\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{b(t)}{b(s)}=e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\geq e^{-\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}=1-\varepsilon,

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{b(t)+\xi}{b(s)+\xi}\geq\frac{b(t)+(1-\varepsilon)\xi}{b(s)+\xi}\geq 1-\varepsilon

for all
\xi>0
and any such
t
and
s
.
By means of (
6.25
), we can hence estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\frac{1}{b(s)+\xi}ds

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1-\varepsilon}{b(t)+\xi}\cdot\int_{t-\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\varepsilon}{b(t)+\xi}\cdot\frac{\tau}{\delta}\bigg(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha\tau}\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\tau}{(b(t)+\xi)\cdot\delta}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\geq t_{0},

because (
6.22
) ensures that

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha\tau}\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{\delta}{\tau}\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\tau\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}{\delta\ln\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}\bigg)=\varepsilon.

Accordingly, (
6.28
) and (
6.20
) yield

\displaystyle J_{2}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b(t)+\xi}\cdot\bigg\{8-\frac{(1-\varepsilon)m}{(1+\varepsilon)\pi\tau}\cdot\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\tau}{\delta}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{1}}{b(t)+\xi}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\geq t_{0},

which since by (
6.24
) we have

\displaystyle b(t)+\xi\leq b_{0}+\xi_{0}\leq\varepsilon\xi_{0}^{2}+\xi_{0}\leq 2\xi_{0}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t>0

guarantees that

\displaystyle J_{2}(\xi,t)\leq-\frac{c_{1}}{2\xi_{0}}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\geq t_{0}.

In conjunction with (
6.27
), (
6.26
) and (
6.21
), this shows that

\displaystyle J(\xi,t)\leq\frac{2}{\xi_{0}}\cdot\alpha-\frac{c_{1}}{2\xi_{0}}\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\geq t_{0}

and thereby proves (
6.19
).

\Box

Now for small times, we can also achieve that
{\cal P}\underline{U}\leq 0
if we suppose
w_{0}
to be sufficiently strongly
concentrated near the origin.

Lemma 6.6

Let
\delta\geq 0
and
m>0
, and suppose that
\alpha>0,b_{0}>0
and
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
, that

b(t)=b_{0}e^{-\alpha t}\qquad\mbox{for }t\geq 0,

(6.29)

and that
a\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
is as given by (
6.7
).
Then for all
t_{0}>0
there exists
\Gamma_{0}(\alpha,b_{0},\xi_{0},t_{0})>0

such that whenever
W_{0}
and
K_{0}
as introduced in (
4.7
) satisfy

\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}\geq\Gamma_{0}(\alpha,b_{0},\xi_{0},t_{0})\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,\xi_{0}),

(6.30)

the function
\underline{U}
defined in (
6.1
) has the property that with
{\cal P}
as in (
4.6
) we have

{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and $t\in(0,t_{0})$.}

(6.31)

Proof.
By (
6.2
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{(b+\xi)^{2}}{ab\xi}\cdot{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a^{\prime}(b+\xi)}{ab}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{b}+\frac{8}{b+\xi}-\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{a(s)}{b(s)+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\bigg\}ds

(6.32)

\displaystyle-2\bigg(\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}\bigg)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t>0,

where Lemma
6.4
ensures that

\frac{a^{\prime}(b+\xi)}{ab}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{b}\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}+1\bigg)\cdot\frac{-b^{\prime}}{b}=\bigg(\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}+1\bigg)\cdot\alpha,

(6.33)

and where

\frac{8}{b+\xi}\leq\frac{8}{b_{0}}e^{\alpha t_{0}}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\in(0,t_{0})

(6.34)

according to (
6.29
).
Moreover, due to (
6.7
) we see that

\frac{a}{b+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}=\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{b+\xi_{0}}{b+\xi}\cdot\frac{b+\xi_{0}}{b+\xi_{0}^{2}}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\geq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t>0,

(6.35)

because for any choice of
\xi<\xi_{0}
we have
b+\xi_{0}\geq b+\xi
and also
b+\xi_{0}\geq b+\xi_{0}^{2}
due to the fact
that
\xi_{0}<1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}(t-s)}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{a(s)}{b(s)+\xi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\bigg\}ds\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t>0,

which combined with (
6.32
)-(
6.35
) shows that

\frac{ab\xi}{(b+\xi)^{2}}\cdot{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq\bigg(\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}+1\bigg)\cdot\alpha+\frac{8}{b_{0}}e^{\alpha t_{0}}-2\bigg(\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}\bigg)\cdot e^{-\frac{\delta}{\tau}t}\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\in(0,t_{0}).

(6.36)

Thus, if

\displaystyle\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}\geq\Gamma_{0}(\alpha,b_{0},\xi_{0},t_{0}):=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\bigg\{\Big(\frac{1}{\xi_{0}}+1\Big)\cdot\alpha+\frac{8}{b_{0}}e^{\alpha t_{0}}\bigg\}\cdot e^{\frac{\delta}{\tau}t_{0}}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,\xi_{0}),

then (
6.31
) results from (
6.36
).

\Box

By a careful selection of the parameters in (
6.1
) we can finally combine Lemma
6.3
, Lemma
6.5

and Lemma
6.6
to establish our main result on infinite-time blow-up of supercritical-mass solutions.

Proof
of Theorem
1.3
.  We first take
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{\star}>0
as provided by Lemma
6.5
and let

R:=\sqrt{\xi_{0}}.

(6.37)

Again invoking Lemma
6.5
, we then find
b_{0}\in(0,\xi_{0}^{2})
and
t_{0}>0
with the properties listed there, and
thereupon pick
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{\star})
as given by Lemma
6.3
when applied to
\eta_{0}:=\frac{\eta}{2}
.
Having thus fixed
\alpha,b_{0},\xi_{0}
and
t_{0}
, we finally fix
\Gamma_{0}:=\Gamma_{0}(\alpha,b_{0},\xi_{0},t_{0})
as yielded by
Lemma
6.6
, and claim that thereupon the conclusion of Theorem
1.3
holds if we let

\Gamma_{u}(m,\eta):=\frac{m}{\pi}\cdot\frac{(b_{0}+\xi_{0})^{2}}{b_{0}(b_{0}+\xi_{0}^{2})}

(6.38)

and

\gamma(m,\eta):=\frac{m}{\pi}\cdot\frac{b_{0}}{b_{0}+\xi_{0}^{2}}

(6.39)

as well as

\Gamma_{w}(m,\eta):=2\Gamma_{0}.

(6.40)

To verify this, given
u_{0}
and
w_{0}
with the assumed properties we let
W_{0}
,
K_{0}
and
U
be defined by
(
4.7
) and (
4.2
), respectively, and fix
\underline{U}
as in (
6.1
), with
b(t):=b_{0}e^{-\alpha t}
and

a\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
given by (
6.7
).
Then (
4.7
) and (
1.9
) imply that

\displaystyle W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}\rho w_{0}(\rho)d\rho-K_{0}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg\{K_{0}-\int_{\sqrt{\xi}}^{1}\rho w_{0}(\rho)d\rho\bigg\}-K_{0}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\xi)K_{0}-\frac{1-\xi}{2}-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\sqrt{\xi}}}w_{0}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-\xi)K_{0}-\frac{1-\xi}{2}\cdot\bigg\{-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}-\eta\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\xi)\cdot\bigg\{K_{0}-\frac{1}{2}-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}+\frac{\eta}{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\xi)\cdot\frac{\eta}{2}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(\xi_{0},1),

because
\xi_{0}=R^{2}
by (
6.37
).
Therefore,

\frac{W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi}{1-\xi}\geq\frac{\eta}{2}=\eta_{0}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(\xi_{0},1),

(6.41)

so that Lemma
6.3
applies to show that according to our choice of
\alpha
and the fact that

b_{0}\in(0,\xi_{0}^{2})
, taking
{\cal P}
as in (
4.6
) we have

{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(\xi_{0},1)$ and }t>0.

(6.42)

We next combine (
4.7
) with (
1.8
) and (
6.40
) to see that

\displaystyle\frac{W_{0}(\xi)}{\xi}-K_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}\rho w_{0}(\rho)d\rho-\int_{0}^{1}\rho w_{0}(\rho)d\rho

(6.43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg\{-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{\sqrt{\xi}}}w_{0}--\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}}w_{0}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Gamma_{w}(m,\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,\xi_{0}),

again because
\xi_{0}=R^{2}
by (
6.37
).
Consequently, Lemma
6.6
asserts that

{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\in(0,t_{0}).

(6.44)

Moreover, since (
6.41
) together with (
6.43
) clearly implies that
W_{0}(\xi)-K_{0}\xi\geq 0
for all

\xi\in(0,1)
, thanks to our choice of
b_{0}
and
t_{0}
and the fact that
\alpha<\alpha_{\star}
we may employ
Lemma
6.5
to infer that

{\cal P}\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq 0\qquad\mbox{for all $\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})$ and }t\geq t_{0}.

(6.45)

Now from the definition of
\underline{U}
and Lemma
4.1
it is clear that

\underline{U}(0,t)=U(0,t)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\underline{U}(1,t)=U(1,t)=\frac{m}{2\pi}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0.

(6.46)

In order to show that furthermore

\underline{U}(\xi,0)\leq U(\xi,0)\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,1),

(6.47)

we first consider small values of
\xi
, for which from (
1.6
) and (
6.37
) we gain the inequality

\displaystyle U(\xi,0)=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\xi}}\rho u_{0}(\rho)d\rho=\frac{\xi}{2}-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{\sqrt{\xi}}}u_{0}\geq\frac{\xi}{2}\cdot\Gamma_{u}(m,\eta)\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,\xi_{0}).

On the other hand, (
6.1
), (
6.7
) and (
6.38
) show that

\displaystyle\underline{U}(\xi,0)=\frac{a(0)\xi}{b_{0}+\xi}\leq\frac{a(0)\xi}{b_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{u}(m,\eta)\cdot\xi\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(0,\xi_{0}),

and that hence (
6.47
) is valid for any such
\xi
.
For large
\xi
, by (
6.7
) we have

\displaystyle\underline{U}(\xi,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a(0)\cdot(b_{0}\xi ü+\xi_{0}^{2})}{(b_{0}+\xi_{0}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{b_{0}\xi+\xi_{0}^{2}}{b_{0}+\xi_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\bigg\{1-\frac{b_{0}}{b_{0}+\xi_{0}^{2}}\cdot(1-\xi)\bigg\}\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(\xi_{0},1),

whereas (
1.7
), (
6.39
) and again (
6.37
) yield

\displaystyle U(\xi,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\rho u_{0}(\rho)d\rho-\int_{\sqrt{\xi}}^{1}\rho u_{0}(\rho)d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}-\frac{1-\xi}{2}\cdot-\hskip-11.38109pt\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\sqrt{\xi}}}u_{0}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}-\frac{1-\xi}{2}\cdot\gamma(m,\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{2\pi}-\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{b_{0}}{b_{0}+\xi_{0}^{2}}\cdot(1-\xi)\qquad\mbox{for all }\xi\in(\xi_{0},1).

We thereby conclude that the claimed ordering in (
6.47
) indeed holds, so that on the basis of
(
6.42
), (
6.44
), (
6.45
) and (
6.46
) we may invoke the comparison principle
in Lemma
4.2
to infer that
\underline{U}(\xi,t)\leq U(\xi,t)
for all
\xi\in[0,1]
and
t\geq 0
.
In particular, this entails that for each fixed
t>0
we must have

\displaystyle U_{\xi}(0,t)=\lim_{\xi\searrow 0}\frac{U(\xi,t)}{\xi}\geq\lim_{\xi\searrow 0}\frac{\underline{U}(\xi,t)}{\xi}=\lim_{\xi\searrow 0}\frac{a(t)}{b(t)+\xi}=\frac{a(t)}{b(t)}=\frac{m}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{(b(t)+\xi_{0})^{2}}{b(t)+\xi_{0}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{b(t)}.

Since
b(t)=b_{0}e^{-\alpha t}\leq b_{0}<\xi_{0}^{2}
for all
t>0
, this ensures that

\displaystyle u(0,t)=2U_{\xi}(0,t)\geq\frac{m}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\xi_{0}^{2}}{2\xi_{0}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{b(t)}=\frac{m}{2\pi b_{0}}e^{\alpha t}\qquad\mbox{for all }t>0

and hence completes the proof.
