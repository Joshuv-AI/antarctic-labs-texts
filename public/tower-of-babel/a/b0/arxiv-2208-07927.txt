Semi-supervised Transfer Learning for Evaluation of Model Classification Performance
Linshanshan Wang, Xuan Wang, Katherine P. Liao, Tianxi Cai

Abstract
In many modern machine learning applications, frequent encounters of covariate shift and label scarcity have posed challenges to robust model training and evaluation. Numerous transfer learning methods have been developed to robustly adapt the model itself to some unlabeled target populations using existing labeled data in a source population. However, there is a paucity of literature on transferring performance metrics of a trained model. In this paper, we aim to evaluate the performance of a trained binary classifier on unlabeled target population based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. We proposed S emi-supervised T ransfer l E arning of A ccuracy M easures (STEAM), an efficient three-step estimation procedure that employs 1) double-index modeling to construct calibrated density ratio weights and 2) robust imputation to leverage the large amount of unlabeled data to improve estimation efficiency. We establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the proposed estimator under correct specification of either the density ratio model or the outcome model. We also correct for potential overfitting bias in the estimators in finite samples with cross-validation. We compare our proposed estimators to existing methods and show reductions in bias and gains in efficiency through simulations. We illustrate the practical utility of the proposed method on evaluating prediction performance of a phenotyping model for Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) on a temporally evolving EHR cohort.

1
Introduction

The increasing adoption of electronic health records (EHR) for clinical care has enabled many opportunities for translational and clinical research (
Hripcsak & Albers 2013a
,
Miotto et al. 2016a
). However, a major limitation of EHR data is the lack of precise information on disease phenotypes. Readily available EHR features such as diagnosis codes often exhibit poor specificity that can bias or depower the downstream study (
Liao et al. 2010a
,
Cipparone et al. 2015a
). Meanwhile, manual annotation of phenotypes via chart review is laborious and unscalable. To overcome these challenges, numerous supervised phenotyping classification algorithms have been developed for prediction of disease status of individual patients (
Carroll et al. 2012a
,
Xia et al. 2013a
,
Liao et al. 2015c
,
Liao et al. 2015b
).

To build a supervised phenotyping model, researchers often manually annotate outcome information for a randomly chosen subset of the large EHR dataset, and then use these labeled observations for model training and validation. Once a model is trained and validated in one population, it is often then used for other populations such as a similar population from a different healthcare system (
Carroll et al. 2012a
) or the same EHR cohort updated over time (
Huang et al. 2020a
). Generalizing a classification model trained in a
source
population to a
target
population, however, requires caution due to the shift in the covariate distribution owing to the heterogeneity in the underlying patient populations. It is well-known that such shifts in the distribution of the covariates, often referred to as
covariate shift
, may have a large impact on the performance of a prediction algorithm trained in source cohort on target cohort (
Rasmy et al. 2018a
). It is thus important to assess the portability of the algorithm in face of such changes in datasets before its usage in the target sites.

In these scenarios, methods to accurately evaluate the the performance of these algorithms in target cohort is especially valuable. If outcome information is available for some patients in target cohort, for instance, via chart review, then the standard methods can be applied to estimate the prediction performance parameters on target dataset. Recently, robust semi-supervised methods have also been proposed to improve the estimation efficiency for prediction performance measures (
Gronsbell & Cai 2018c
). The issue, however, is that typically, performing chart review for every new target population is infeasible due to time and resource constraints. It is thus highly desirable to robustly and efficiently estimate the prediction performance parameters in target cohort without requiring any outcome information in target dataset.

Although the problem of covariate shift has been extensively studied in recent literature of transfer learning, most existing work focused on robust adaptation of the model itself to the target population (
Chen et al. 2016a
,
Wen et al. 2014a
,
Rotnitzky et al. 2012a
,
Reddi et al. 2015a
,
Liu et al. 2020a
). There is a paucity of literature on transferring performance metrics of a trained model based on the commonly used ROC analysis (
Pepe 2003a
).
Inoue & Uno 2018a
used density ratio weighting methods to assess model performance in the target population. However, their estimator do not protect against the mis-specification of the density ratio model.
Xu & Tibshirani 2022a
proposed a method based on parametric bootstrap which does not require estimation of the density ratio model. However, their focus was on the out-of-sample error of the prediction model, not estimating the accuracy meausures based on the ROC curve. Moreover, their method does not protect against mis-specification of the prediction model. Doubly robust methods for estimating accuracy measures have been proposed in verification bias literature (
Alonzo & Pepe 2005a
,
Rotnitzky et al. 2006a
,
Fluss et al. 2009a
), and recently in the context of estimating the AUC in a target population that has a different data distribution compared to the source population (
Li et al. 2022a
). Nevertheless, no existing method leveraged both unlabeled source and target data to improve estimation efficiency. To address this gap in methodology, we propose a
S
emi-supervised
T
ransfer l
E
arning of
A
ccuracy
M
easures (STEAM) in the unlabeled target cohort. We employ a double-index modeling approach to construct calibrated density ratio weights similar to
Cheng et al. 2020a
, incorporating both the covariate shift model and the outcome imputation model. Although also taking a re-weighting form, the STEAM estimators are doubly robust with consistency attained when either the density ratio model or the classification model is correctly specified. The large amount of unlabeled source and target data enabled us to efficiently estimate the density ratio and the imputation model.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: We formalize the problem of interest in Section 2. In section 3, we detail the STEAM estimation procedure along with a perturbation resampling procedure for inference. We conduct simulation studies to evaluate the
finite sample robustness and efficiency of the proposed method in Section 4. We then illustrate the practical utility of the proposed method on evaluating prediction performance of a phenotyping model for Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) on temporally evolving EHR cohorts in Section 5.

2.3
Accuracy Measures of interest

With a given
\bm{\bar{\beta}}
trained by source data, we may classify subjects in the target population with the highest scores of
\bm{\bar{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}
, i.e.
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\equiv F_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(\bm{\bar{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X})\geq c
for some fraction
c

F_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(a)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\bm{\bar{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}\leq a)
.
To identify a desirable threshold
c
and evaluate the classification accuracy in the unlabeled target population, we consider the commonly employed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (
Pepe 2003a
). Specifically, the true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) of the binary classification rule
I({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)
in the target population are defined as

\displaystyle\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c|Y=1),\quad\rm{FPR}_{0}(c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c|Y=0)

The ROC curve,
\rm{ROC}_{0}=\rm{TPR}_{0}\{\rm{FPR}_{0}^{-1}(u)\}
, summarizes the trade-off between the TPR and FPR functions.
The area under the ROC curve,
\rm{AUC}_{0}=\int_{0}^{1}\rm{ROC}_{0}(u)du
, summarizes the overall prediction performance of
\mathcal{P}_{\hat{\beta}}
. Additionally, a threshold value
c_{0}
for classifying an individual as having the phenotype, namely
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c_{0}
, is often chosen to achieve a desired FPR level
u_{0}
. Once the threshold value
c_{0}
is determined, one may summarize the predictive performance of
I({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c_{0})
, based on positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) defined as

\displaystyle\rm{PPV}_{0}(c_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y=1|{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c_{0}),\quad\rm{NPV}_{0}(c_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y=0|{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}<c_{0})

Directly estimating theses accuracy parameters using the source data will result in inconsistency due to the covariate shift as well as potential model mis-specification of the model for
P(Y=1|\bm{X})
(
Liu et al. 2021a
). To correct for the bias, it is natural to incorporate importance sampling weighting. For example, with
\bm{\bar{\beta}}
estimated as
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
, one may estimate
\rm{TPR}_{0}
as

\widehat{\rm{TPR}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf wgt}(c)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widehat{w}(\bm{X}_{i})\cdot Y_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}\widehat{w}(\bm{X}_{i})\cdot Y_{i}}

(3)

where
\widehat{w}(\bm{X})
is an estimate for the density ratio

w(\bm{X})=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\bm{X})/\mathbb{P}_{1}(\bm{X})=\{1-\pi(\bm{X})\}/\pi(\bm{X})\cdot P(S=1)/P(S=0),\quad\pi(\bm{X})=P(S=1|\bm{X}),

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}=\widehat{F}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i})
, and
\widehat{F}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(a)=N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}\leq a)
. However, the validity of this re-weighting estimator heavily relies on the consistency of
\widehat{w}(\bm{X})
for

and can perform poorly when the density ratio model is not well estimated. Moreover, the estimator suffers from low precision when
n
is small, which is often the case in EHR settings. To improve the robustness and efficiency of the estimation for the accuracy parameters, we next propose the STEAM estimators that leverage the unlabeled data.

3
Methods

3.1
Estimation Procedure

To motivate the STEAM estimation procedure, we first note that

\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{0}\{Y\cdot I({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)\}}{\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{0}\{m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\cdot I({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)\}}{\mathbb{E}_{0}\{m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{0}\{\int_{c}^{1}m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(q)dq\}}{\mathbb{E}_{0}\{\int_{0}^{1}m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(q)dq\}}

where
m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(q)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y=1|{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}=q)
. Even though
\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y=1\mid\bm{X})=\mathbb{P}_{1}(Y=1\mid\bm{X})
,
\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y=1\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\neq\mathbb{P}_{1}(Y=1\mid{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})
under covariate shift and possible mis-specification of the outcome model (
1
).
To enable robust transfer of knowledge from the source to target while leveraging unlabeled data, the STEAM estimation procedure consists of three key steps:
(I) construct calibrated density ratio weight
\widehat{w}_{i}
via double index modeling; (II) derive non-parametric calibrated estimator of
m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(c)
; and (III) obtain the final STEAM estimator by projecting to the unlabeled target data using the estimated
m_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(\cdot)
.

The calibrated density ratio weight estimator involves both an initial estimator for
\pi(\bm{X})=P(S=1\mid\bm{X})
and the estimated outcome model. To estimate
\pi(\bm{X})
, we consider a parametric working model,

P(S=1|\bm{X})=g_{\pi}(\bm{\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{\Psi}),

(4)

where

is a known link function,
\bm{\Psi}
is a
q
-dimensional vector of transformed
\bm{X}
that may include non-linear bases of
\bm{X}
with the first element being 1, and
\bm{\alpha}
is the
unknown parameter. Since
\pi(\cdot)
doesn’t involve the outcome, we may use the unlabeled data to obtain a regularized estimator for
\bm{\bar{\alpha}}=\argmax_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\{\ell_{g_{\pi}}(\bm{\alpha};S_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\}

\bm{\widehat{\alpha}}=\argmax_{\bm{\alpha}}\left\{(N+N_{t})^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{U}\cup\mathcal{I}_{T}}\ell_{g_{\pi}}(\bm{\alpha};S_{i},\bm{\Psi}_{i})-\lambda_{\pi,N}\|\bm{\alpha}_{-1}/\bm{\widetilde{\alpha}}_{-1}\|_{1}\right\}

with properly chosen tuning parameters

,

for

, where the expectation is taken over the pooled source and target population.
Similar to the outcome model, one may show that

is a regular estimator for
\bm{\bar{\alpha}}
.
We then obtain a non-parametrically calibrated estimate of

over the two scores

\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{U}\cup\mathcal{I}_{T}}K_{\pi,h_{1}}\left\{(\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})\right\}S_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{U}\cup\mathcal{I}_{T}}K_{\pi,h_{1}}\left\{(\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})\right\}}

\widehat{\pi}_{i}\equiv\widehat{w}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})=\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})}{\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})},

\xi(c,\bm{\beta})=\xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\alpha}}}(c,\bm{\beta})
. We show that

n^{1/2}\{\widehat{\xi}(c,\bm{\widehat{\beta}})-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\}=n^{-1/2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}\left[\bar{w}_{j}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}>c)\{Y_{j}-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\}+\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\right]+o_{p}(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}
is the influence function of
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
, and
\mathbf{C}_{2}
is defined in Appendix B. The weak convergence of
n^{1/2}\{\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)-\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)\}
thus follows similar as Appendix A of
Gronsbell & Cai 2018c
.

Even though the asymptotic variance can be obtained through influence function, a direct estimate is difficult because it involves unknown conditional density functions which are difficult to estimate explicitly, particularly under model mis-specification. We instead propose the use of a simple resampling procedure for inference based on
Jin et al. 2001a
in the next section.

3.3
Perturbation Resampling Procedure for Inference

Now we outline the perturbation resampling procedure for inference. Let
\mathcal{G}=\{G_{i}:i=1,\dots,N\}
be non-negative independent and identically distributed random variables with unit mean and variance that are independent of the observed data. We first obtain perturbed estimates
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}
as:

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}=\argmax_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}\ell_{g}(\bm{\beta};Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})G_{i}-\lambda_{\mu,n}\|(\bm{\beta}_{-1}/\bm{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\ast}_{-1})^{\gamma}\|_{1}\right\}

where
\bm{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\ast}
is perturbed initial estimates obtained from analogously perturbing its estimating equations. This leads to the perturbed estimates of the density ratio weights:

\widehat{w}_{i}^{*}\equiv\widehat{w}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*})=\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*})}{\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*})}

where

\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast})=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{U}\cup\mathcal{I}_{T}}K_{\pi,h_{1}}\left\{(\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})\right\}S_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{U}\cup\mathcal{I}_{T}}K_{\pi,h_{1}}\left\{(\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X})\right\}}

Since
\bm{\widehat{\alpha}}
and
\widehat{\pi}(\cdot)
are estimated based
\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{T}
, their contribution to the asymptotic variance is of higher order when
N\gg n
.
We then obtain the perturbed estimate of conditional risk function as

\widehat{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}(q)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}K_{m,h_{2}}\{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}(\bm{X}_{i})-q\}\widehat{w}^{\ast}_{i}Y_{i}G_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}K_{m.h_{2}}\{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}(\bm{X}_{i})-q\}\widehat{w}^{\ast}_{i}G_{i}}

Finally we calculate the perturbed
\widehat{\rm{TPR}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}
as

\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}_{0}(c)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}\geq c)\widehat{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}})}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\widehat{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}})}

It can be shown using arguments from
Jin et al. 2001a
that the asymptotic distribution of
n^{1/2}\left\{\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)-\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)\right\}
coincides with that of
n^{1/2}\left\{\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}_{0}(c)-\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)\right\}|\mathcal{D}
. This allows us to consistently approximate the SE of
\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}(c)
based on the empirical standard deviation of resamples
\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}(c)
. The confidence intervals of
\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}
an be constructed by using the empirical percentiles of the resamples. We can obtain perturbed counterparts of
\widehat{\rm{FPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{ROC}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{PPV}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{NPV}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}(c)
respectively denoted as
\widehat{\rm{FPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{ROC}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{PPV}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{NPV}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}(c)
, and obtain SE estimates and construct confidence intervals accordingly.

For practical use, an issue of this perturbation resampling procedure is that calculating
\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast})
requires two-dimensional smoothing, which can be very time-consuming. To overcome this difficulty, we propose an approximated resampling procedure that is more computationally feasible. We first generate
\{\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{[b]}:(G_{1}^{[b]},\cdots,G_{N}^{[b]})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T},b=1,\cdots,B\}
where
\bm{\mathcal{G}}
has dimension
N\times B
, followed by the perturbed
\bm{\beta}
estimates
\{\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*}:(\bm{\widehat{\beta}}_{1}^{*[b]},\cdots,\bm{\widehat{\beta}}_{p}^{*[b]}),b=1,\cdots,B\}
where
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*}
has dimension
p\times B
. We then approximate the perturbed estimates
\widehat{\pi}
by

[\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*})]_{N\times B}=[\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})]_{N\times B}+[\nabla\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})]_{N\times p}[\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{*}-\bm{\widehat{\beta}}]_{p\times B},

where

\nabla\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\beta})=\frac{\partial\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\beta})}{\partial\bm{\beta}}.

The perturbed estimate of conditional risk can then be obtained as
[\widehat{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\ast}})]_{N\times B}
, and the perturbed
\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM*}_{0}(c)
thus follows. We evaluate the performance of the perturbation resampling procedure with and without approximation through simulations.

4
Simulation Studies

We performed extensive simulations to assess the finite sample bias, standard error (SE), root mean square error (RMSE) of our proposed estimators (STEAM) compared to alternative estimators. In separate simulations we also examined the performance of the perturbation procedure, with and without approximation, for inference based on STEAM estimators. Across all numerical studies including the data application in section 5, we used the adaptive LASSO to estimate
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
, where we choose
\lambda_{\mu,{n}}
and
\lambda_{\pi,N}
using a modified BIC criteria (
Minnier et al. 2011a
). For the bandwidth in the two-dimensional smoothing for
\pi(\cdot)
, we use a Gaussian product kernel of order with a plug-in bandwidth
h_{1}=\widehat{\sigma}N^{-1/6}
, where
\widehat{\sigma}
is the sample standard deviation of either
\bm{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}
,
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}
. Prior to smoothing,
\bm{\widehat{\alpha}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}
and
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}
were standardized and transformed by a probability integral transform based on the normal cumulative distribution function to induce approximately uniformly distributed inputs, which can improve finite-sample performance (
Wand et al. 1991a
). For the non-parametric smoothing for
m(\cdot)
, we used the Gaussian kernel with
h_{2}=n^{-0.4}\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathcal{P}}
, where
\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathcal{P}}
is the empirical standard deviation of
\{\widehat{\mathcal{P}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. As we focused on binary outcomes, we specified logistic link functions
g_{\mu}(u)=g_{\pi}(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})
.

For comparison, we considered alternative estimators for performance accuracy parameters: (1) the naive estimators, where the performance accuracy parameters are estimated using cross-validation on the labeled source dataset (
source
), (2) estimation based on
100
randomly labeled validation samples in target dataset (
target_labeled
), (3) the standard weighted estimators based on cross-validation (
weighted
), (4) the doubly robust estimator proposed by
Alonzo & Pepe 2005a
with cross-validation (
DR-aug
). This estimator is a special case of
Rotnitzky et al. 2006a
and
Fluss et al. 2009a
when
Y
is independent of
S
conditional on
\bm{X}
.

Throughout, we generated
\bm{X}
from
MVN\{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho)\mathbb{I}_{p\times p}+\sigma^{2}\rho\}
,
S=1|\bm{X}\sim Bernoulli\{\pi(\bm{X})\}
, and
Y=1|\bm{X}\sim Bernoulli\{\mu(\bm{X})\}
. We considered simulating data that roughly resembled the EHR data example in terms of model parameters but were simple enough to be more broadly relevant. To this end, we considered
p=10
covariates with variance
\sigma^{2}=1
, and correlations
\rho=0.2
. These simulations were varied over different model specifications, predictive strength of
\bm{X}
to
S
, and sample sizes. We considered the following data generating mechanisms:

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{X})

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}expit(0.1X_{1}+0.05X_{2}-0.1X_{5}-0.05X_{6}+0.05X_{1}X_{2})&\text{(Weak)}\\
expit(0.2X_{1}+0.1X_{2}-0.2X_{5}-0.1X_{6}+0.1X_{1}X_{2})&\text{(Moderate)}\\
expit(0.6X_{1}+0.3X_{2}-0.6X_{5}-0.3X_{6}+0.3X_{1}X_{2})&\text{(Strong)}\end{cases}

or

Fitting

.

where

,

. The outcome prevalence was approximately

, and the sample size in source and target are roughly equal. We considered

5
Application to Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) Phenotyping model

To illustrate the performance of our proposed estimators, we applied our procedures to evaluate a phenotyping algorithm for classifying RA disease, using EHR data from Partner’s Healthcare (
Liao et al. 2010a
). The source dataset from 2009 consists of 20,451 total patients, of which
n=267
were labeled with the true disease status through chart review by an rheumatologist. Both narrative and codified data were available to develop the prediction model (
p=21
). The narrative variables, including disease diagnoses and medications, were obtained with natural language processing. The codified data included ICD-9 codes, electronic prescriptions, and laboratory values. Healthcare utility was also included. The transformation
u\mapsto\log(1+u)
was applied to all count variables to mitigate instability in the estimation due to skewness in their distributions. An RA phenotyping model, named as
ALASSO-2009
, was trained on labeled data from 2009. We are interested in assessing its performance in classifying the disease status of patients in the same EHR database across different time windows, in 2011 (
N_{t}=25,405,n_{t}=100
), 2013 (
N_{t}=30,804,n_{t}=100
), 2015 (
N_{t}=36,095,n_{t}=150
) and 2017 (
N_{t}=39,550,n_{t}=200
). Each of these consists of a large number of unlabeled data with the same set of covariates available. A random subset of each dataset was labeled with true RA status, and can serve as the validation sets to verify our results.

For comparison, we considered
source
,
target_labeled
,
weighted
and
DR-aug
estimators as in Section 4. We also
considered semi-supervised estimation using labeled and unlabeled data in target as proposed by
Gronsbell & Cai 2018c
(
target_SSL
). The estimated AUC of the model on the target datasets are presented in Figure
2
. The AUC estimates for the target datasets obtained using the validation sets (
target_labeled
and
target_SSL
) are noticeably different from the ones estimated from the source data (
source
), especially in 2015 and 2017. This is not too surprising since many changes in the database have occurred over the years: for instance, patient characteristics, such as age, have undergo changes. Moreover, the EHR system was switched to Epic (Epic System Corporation) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system was changed from version 9 to version 10 around 2015-2016, which may also explain the lower AUC in 2015 and 2017. The point estimates based on our proposed STEAM method are similar to
target_labeled
, suggesting the stability of the proposed procedure in a real data setting. Moreover, we observe substantial efficiency gains of STEAM over
target_labeled
,
weighted
and
DR-aug
. As a result, we have a more precise estimate of the prediction performance of the phenotype algorithm using the STEAM method. The estimated ROC curves along with 95% point-wise confidence bands can be found in Appendix D.

The estimates of cutoff values and the corresponding
TPR
,
PPV
and
NPV
estimates of the binary classification rule for
u_{0}=0.05
and
0.10
as well as their
95\%
CIs are presented in Figure
3
. For choosing the cutoff values, STEAM is
\sim 4.2-7.2
times as efficient as
target_labeled
, and
\sim 1.1-2.1
times as efficient as
DR-aug
. For each accuracy measure, STEAM estimators are
3.1-5.8
times as efficient as
target_labeled
, and the efficiency gain is at least
14\%
with gains as high as
245\%
compared with
DR-aug
.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we proposed a robust and efficient transfer learning procedure for model evaluation rather than model fitting. Our estimators are doubly robust, i.e. they are consistent when either the prediction model
\mu(\bm{X})
or the weighting model
\pi(\bm{X})
is correct. We addressed potential overfitting bias in our SS estimators with CV and also developed a perturbation resampling procedure for making inference. We illustrate the performance and practical utility of the proposed estimators through simulations and application on an RA phenotying model.

We have assumed that the true outcomes are labeled completely at random in source population, which may be reasonable if investigators control the labeling. However, this assumption could be restrictive if labeling was stratified by some known factors or if some records that are available were not labeled for research purposes. Further work is needed to extend our results to the missing at random (MAR) setting (
Rubin 1976a
) to allow the labeling process to depend on
\bm{X}
. Other refinements and extensions to the proposed approach is possible. For instance, when the prediction model is clearly mis-specified, we may incorporate an additional outcome model in the estimation procedure to preserve the doubly robust property, i.e. the estimator are consistent if either the new outcome model or the weighting model is correct.

Throughout, we focused on the setting with fixed
p
but accommodated settings in which
p
is not small relative to
n
in finite sample with regularized estimation. However, when
p
is large so that
\nu
is large, a large power parameter
\gamma
would be required to maintain the oracle properties, leading to an unstable penalty and poor finite sample performance. Estimation under the setting with
p\gg n
would require different theoretical justifications and warrant additional research.

Appendix

In the Appendix, we outline theoretical derivations to establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of our proposed estimator. Let
\mathbb{P}_{s}
and
\mathbb{E}_{s}
denote the probability measure and expectations in the population
S=s
; let
\{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}},\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}\}
and
\{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{U}},\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{U}}\}
denote the corresponding empirical measures in the labeled and unlabeled set; and let
\mathbb{G}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}-\mathbb{P}_{t})
and
\mathbb{G}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{U}}=N_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{U}}-\mathbb{P}_{t})
.
As defined in the main text, let
w(\bm{X})=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\bm{X})/\mathbb{P}_{1}(\bm{X})=\{1-\pi(\bm{X})\}/\pi(\bm{X})\cdot P(S=1)/P(S=0)
,
\pi(\bm{X})=P(S=1|\bm{X})
. Let
w(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})=\{1-\pi(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})\}/\pi(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})\cdot P(S=1)/P(S=0)
,
\pi(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})=P(S=1\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X},\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X})
and
\widehat{w}(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})=\{1-\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})\}/\widehat{\pi}(\bm{X};\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})
. Besides the assumptions made in Section 2.1, we also assume the following:

1.

\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
belongs to compact parameter spaces, and the covariates
\bm{X}
are bounded.

2.

\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}
and
\bm{\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}
are continuous with continuously differentiable density functions.
P(Y=1|\bm{X})
and
P(S=1|\bm{X})
are twice continuously differentiable.

3.

The kernel
K
is a symmetric, bounded and continuously differentiable kernel. We set the bandwidths
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
to
h_{1}=O(N^{-\nu_{1}})
and
h_{2}=O(n^{-\nu_{2}})
and
N\gtrsim n^{r_{0}}
with
\nu_{1}\in[1/6,1/2)
,
\nu_{2}\in(1/4,1/2)
,
\nu_{1}<\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{r_{0}})
,
r_{0}>\max(4\nu_{2},1+2\nu_{2})=1+2\nu_{2}
.

4.

At least one of the following two conditions holds, (i) The weighting model is is correct,
w(\bm{X}_{i};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})=w(\bm{\bm{X}}_{i})
. (ii) The outcome model is correct,
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}|\bar{\bm{\alpha}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i},\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i})=\mu(\bm{X}_{i})
.

Remark 2
.

If we choose undersmoothing for outcome regression with
\nu_{2}=1/3
, then we need
r_{0}>5/3
and can choose
\nu_{1}<\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{r_{0}})<\frac{1}{5}
.

A. Consistency

First, we establish the consistency of
\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)
when either the outcome model (
1
) or the density ratio model (
4
) is correctly specified. From the weak convergence of
{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})=\widehat{F}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{x})
to
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})=F_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{x})
as a process in
\bm{x}
and
\bm{\beta}

(
Wellner 2013a
)
,

\sup_{\bm{\beta},\bm{X}}\left|{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X})-\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{X})\right|=O_{p}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})

(5)

and
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{x})=N^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\{{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})\right\}

converges weakly as a process in
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{x}
. This implies stochastic continuity of
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{X})
in
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{X}
. Thus

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}},\bm{x})-\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{x})\to 0
in probability.

With the convergence of
\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
to
\bar{\bm{\alpha}},\bar{\bm{\beta}}
, by
(
Zou & Zhang 2009c
)
, the uniform convergence rates of kernel estimators
(
Ullah & Pagan 1999a
)
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{i}|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}|=O_{p}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\quad\sup_{\bm{x},\bm{\beta}}|\widehat{w}(\bm{x};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\beta})-w(\bm{x},\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\beta})|=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{Nh_{1}^{2}}}\right),

(6)

\displaystyle\max_{i}|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}-{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}|=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\max_{i}|\widehat{w}_{i}-\bar{w}_{i}|=O_{p}\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{Nh_{1}^{2}}}\right),

(7)

where
\bar{w}_{i}=w(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}})
. It then follows from the uniform consistency of the kernel estimators
(
Ullah & Pagan 1999a
)
and arguments similar to those given in
(
Gronsbell & Cai 2018c
)
that

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{0}(q,\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})):=\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}K_{m,h_{2}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}-q)Y_{i}\widehat{w}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})}{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}K_{m,h_{2}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}-q)\widehat{w}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}})}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{p}

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{0}(q):=\bar{m}_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{1}\{Y_{i}\bar{w}_{i}K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)\}}{\mathbb{E}_{1}\{\bar{w}_{i}K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)\}},

where
\rightarrow_{p}
means converges in probability.
Next, we will show that under assumption
4
, i.e. if either model (
1
) or model (
4
) is correct,

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{0}(q)

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{p}

\displaystyle m_{0}(q):=m_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}})=\frac{m_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}})f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}(q)}{f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\beta}}}(q)},

(8)

where
m_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\beta}})=\mathbb{E}_{0}(Y|{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\beta}}=q)
and
f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}(\cdot)
is the density function of
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}
. If model (
4
) is valid,
\bar{w}_{i}=w(\bm{\bm{X}}_{i})
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)Y_{i}\bar{w}_{i}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)Y_{i}\}\rightarrow_{p}m_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}})f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}(q),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)\bar{w}_{i}\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{p}

\displaystyle f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}(q),

so (
8
) follows.
On the other hand, if model (
1
) is correct, then
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}\mid\bm{X}_{i})=g(\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i})
and
Y_{i}\perp S_{i}\mid\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}
, and thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)Y_{i}\bar{w}_{i}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)g(\bm{\bar{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i})\bar{w}_{i}|\bar{\bm{\alpha}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i},\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)g(\bm{\bar{\beta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{X}_{i})\}\rightarrow_{p}m_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}})f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}(q),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\{K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-q)\bar{w}_{i}\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{p}

\displaystyle f_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bar{\bm{\beta}}}}(q),

which implies (
8
).
Combining the consistency of
\widehat{m}_{0}(q,\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))
to
m_{0}(q,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\bm{\bar{\beta}}})
with the convergence of
\max_{i}|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}-{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}|\rightarrow_{p}0
, we have

\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)\equiv\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widehat{m}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\widehat{m}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))}\rightarrow_{p}\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)\equiv\frac{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\bar{\beta}}\geq c,Y=1)}{\mathbb{P}_{0}(Y=1)}

when either model (
1
) or (
4
) is correct.

B. Asymptotic normality

Next, we establish the asymptotic normality of
n^{\frac{1}{2}}\{\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)-\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)\}
. From
Zou & Zhang 2009c
, we have the asymptotic normality and oracle properties for
\bm{\widehat{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
such that

\displaystyle N^{\frac{1}{2}}({\bm{\widehat{\alpha}}}-\bm{\bar{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\alpha}}}+o_{p}(1),

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}}-\bm{\bar{\beta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}+o_{p}(1),

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\alpha}}},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}
are respective influence functions. Due to oracle properties, the elements of
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\alpha}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}
corresponding to the zero components of
\bm{\bar{\alpha}}
and
\bm{\bar{\beta}}
also take value zero. Let

\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)=\frac{N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widehat{m}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))}{N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\widehat{m}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))}=\frac{\widehat{\xi}(c,\bm{\widehat{\beta}})}{\widehat{\xi}(0,\bm{\widehat{\beta}})},

\xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\alpha}}(c,\bm{\beta})=E\{I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\geq c)m_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},w(\cdot,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta})\}
, and
\xi(c,\bm{\beta})=\xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\alpha}}}(c,\bm{\beta})
. We write

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\{\widehat{\xi}(c,\bm{\widehat{\beta}})-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widehat{m}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))-n^{\frac{1}{2}}\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{1}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}})+\widehat{W}_{2}+\widehat{W}_{3}+\widehat{W}_{4}

where

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{1}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\left\{I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\geq c)\widehat{M}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})-I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\geq c)\widehat{M}_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\left\{I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widehat{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})-I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)\widetilde{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\bm{\bar{\alpha}})\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)\left\{\widehat{m}_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))-m_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\left\{I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)m_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\right\},

\widehat{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(u;\mathcal{P},\alpha)=\widehat{m}_{0}(u,\mathcal{P},\widehat{w}(\cdot,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}))
and
\widetilde{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(u;\mathcal{P},\alpha)=\widehat{m}_{0}(u,\mathcal{P},w(\cdot,\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}))
. It is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle W_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\{N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)m_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\right\}=O_{p}(n^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{2}})=o_{p}(1).

We next approximate each of the remaining three terms.

To approximate
\widehat{W}_{1}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}})
, we write
\widehat{W}_{1}(\bm{\beta})=\widehat{W}_{11}(\bm{\beta})+\widehat{W}_{12}(\bm{\beta})
with

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{11}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\{I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\geq c)-I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\geq c)\}\widehat{M}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{12}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\geq c)\{\widehat{M}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})-\widehat{M}_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})\}

For
\widehat{W}_{11}(\bm{\beta})
, we write

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{11}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{c}^{1}\widehat{M}_{0}(u;{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathcal{P}}(du,\bm{\beta})

where
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathcal{P}}(u,\bm{\beta})=N_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\{I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\leq c)-I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\leq c)\}
can be shown as convergent to a Gaussian process in
u
and
\bm{\beta}
. It then follows similar arguments given in Appendix A of
Gronsbell & Cai 2018c
, we have

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{q,\bm{\beta}}\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{m,h_{2}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-q)Y_{i}\widehat{w}_{i}Y_{i}^{a}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{m,h_{2}}(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-q)Y_{i}\bar{w}_{i}Y_{i}^{a}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n\log N}{Nh_{1}^{2}}}+\left|\int K_{m,h_{2}}(v-q)d\mathbb{G}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}\left[I\{\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{x}\leq\widehat{F}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}^{-1}}(v)\}y^{a}\bar{w}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-I\{\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{x}\leq F_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}^{-1}}(v)\}y^{a}\bar{w}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n\log N}{Nh_{1}^{2}}}\right)+h_{2}^{-1}\|\mathbb{G}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}}\lesssim O_{p}\left\{(n^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{2}+\nu_{1}}+n^{\nu_{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{4}})\log(N)\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle O_{p}\left[\{N^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-1/r_{0}-2\nu_{1})}+N^{-\frac{1}{4}+\nu_{2}/r_{0}}\}\log(N)\right]

where
\bar{w}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}=w(\bm{x},\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\beta})
,

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}=\{I(\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{x}\leq s)y^{a}\bar{w}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-I(\bm{\beta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bm{x}\leq s^{\prime})y^{a}\bar{w}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}:\bm{\beta},|s-s^{\prime}|<\delta\}
and
\|\mathbb{G}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}}\lesssim O_{p}\{N^{-1/4}\log(N)\}
.
For
\widehat{W}_{12}(\bm{\beta})
, we note that

\max_{i,j}|(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}})-(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}-\mathcal{P}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}})|=O_{p}(N_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. By a Taylor series expansion, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\beta}}\left|\widehat{W}_{12}(\bm{\beta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{\bm{\beta}}\left|N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}|\widehat{M}_{0}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})-\widehat{M}_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}};\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})|\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim O_{p}(h_{2}^{-1}n^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{2}})=O_{p}\left(N^{-\frac{1-1/r_{0}-2\nu_{2}/r_{0}}{2}}\right)

It follows from Assumption 3 that
|\widehat{W}_{1}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}})|\leq\sup_{\bm{\beta}}|\widehat{W}_{11}(\bm{\beta})|+\sup_{\bm{\beta}}|\widehat{W}_{12}(\bm{\beta})|=o_{p}(1)
.

For the second term, we write
\widehat{W}_{2}=\widehat{W}_{21}+\widehat{W}_{22}
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{21}

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\left\{\widehat{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\bm{\widehat{\alpha}})-\widetilde{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\bm{\bar{\alpha}})\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{22}

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\left\{I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widetilde{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},\bm{\bar{\alpha}})-I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)\widetilde{M}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\bm{\bar{\alpha}})\right\}

From (
6
), we have
|\widehat{W}_{21}|\lesssim O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n\log N}{Nh_{1}^{2}}}\right)=o_{p}(1)
. Next, we expand
\widehat{W}_{22}
as
\widehat{W}_{22}=\widehat{W}_{22,1}+\widehat{W}_{22,2}+\widehat{W}_{22,3}
, where

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{22,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{c}^{1}\left\{\widehat{m}_{0}(u,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))-m_{0}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))\right\}d\left\{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}(u)-\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{22,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{c}^{1}m_{0}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))d\left\{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}(u)-\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{22,3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{c}^{1}\left\{\widehat{m}_{0}(u,{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}))-\widehat{m}_{0}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))\right\}d\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(c)=N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\leq c)
and

\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(c)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\leq c)
. From Lemma A.2 and A.3 of
Chakrabortty & Cai 2018a
, we have
\widehat{W}_{22,1}=o_{p}(1)
. Since
\mathbb{G}_{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{U}}\{I(\mathcal{P}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\leq c)\}
converges weakly to a Gaussian process in
\bm{\beta}
and
c
, we have

\widehat{W}_{22,2}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}}-\bm{\bar{\beta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\int_{c}^{\infty}m_{0}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))d\dot{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)+o_{p}(1)

where
\dot{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(u)=\partial\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(u)/\partial\bm{\beta}
.
For
\widehat{W}_{22,3}
, we take a Taylor series expansion to obtain

\widehat{W}_{22,3}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}}-\bm{\bar{\beta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\int_{c}^{\infty}\dot{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}})d\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)+o_{p}(1)

where
\dot{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\beta})=\partial m_{0}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\beta}))/\partial\bm{\beta}
. This, together with other terms associated with
\widehat{W}_{2}
, we have

\widehat{W}_{2}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\widehat{\beta}}-\bm{\bar{\beta}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{C}_{2}+o_{p}(1).

where
\mathbf{C}_{2}=\int_{c}^{\infty}m_{0}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))d\dot{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)+\int_{c}^{\infty}\dot{m}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}})d\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(u)
.

For
\widehat{W}_{3}
, we again use Lemma A.2 of
Chakrabortty & Cai 2018a
and write

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}N_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}\geq c)\{\widehat{m}_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},w(\cdot,\bm{\bar{\alpha}},\bm{\bar{\beta}}))-\bar{m}_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{c}^{1}\frac{n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-s)\bar{w}_{j}\{Y_{j}-m_{0}(s,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\}}{n^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-s)\bar{w}_{j}}d\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(s)+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}\int_{c}^{1}K_{m,h_{2}}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}-s)\bar{w}_{j}\{Y_{j}-m_{0}(s,\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\}d\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}(s)+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}\bar{w}_{j}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}>c)\{Y_{j}-m_{0}({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}},\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}})\}+o_{p}(1).

Combining expansions for
\widehat{W}_{2}
and
\widehat{W}_{3}
, we have

n^{\frac{1}{2}}\{\widehat{\xi}(c,\bm{\widehat{\beta}})-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{L}}\left[\bar{w}_{j}I({\mathcal{P}}_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\bar{\beta}}}>c)\{Y_{j}-\xi(c,\bm{\bar{\beta}})\}+\mathbf{C}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\beta}}\right]+o_{p}(1),

which converges in distribution to a normal. The asymptotic normality of
n^{\frac{1}{2}}\{\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0}(c)-\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)\}
thus follows, and similarly, we can establish the other accuracy parameters.

C. Cross-validation procedure

It is important to note that the proposed estimators are subject to overfitting because the data in
\mathcal{L}
is used to estimate both the risk score for classification and the conditional risk functions
m(\cdot)
, which are used for estimation of the accuracy parameters. To reduce the over-fitting bias in the proposed estimators, we propose a
K
-fold cross-validation procedure. CV in this setting involves partitioning
\mathcal{L}
for the estimation of
\bm{\beta}
and
m_{0}(\cdot)
, whereas use all
\mathcal{T}
to estimate the performance measures. Denote each fold of
\mathcal{L}
as
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and the corresponding indices as
\mathcal{I}_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,K
. For a given
k
, we fit the regression model with
\mathcal{L}\backslash\mathcal{L}_{k}
to obtain an estimator for
\bm{\beta}
denoted as
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)}
. The observations in the left-out set
\mathcal{L}_{k}
are used to estimate
m_{0}(\cdot)
with the local constant smoother as

\widehat{m}_{0,CV}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}(q)=\frac{\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}K_{m,h_{2}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)}}-q)\widehat{w}_{i}^{(-k)}Y_{i}}{\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}K_{m,h_{2}}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0,i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)}}-q)\widehat{w}_{i}^{(-k)}}

where

\widehat{w}_{i}^{(-k)}=\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}^{(-k)}_{i}}{\widehat{\pi}^{(-k)}_{i}}

with

\widehat{\pi}^{(-k)}_{i}=\widehat{\pi}^{(-k)}(\bm{X}_{i};\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)})=\frac{\sum_{j}K_{h}\{(\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\bm{X}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i})\}S_{i}}{\sum_{j}K_{h}\{(\bm{\widehat{\alpha}},\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(\bm{X}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i})\}}

where

\bm{\widehat{\beta}}^{(-k)}=\argmax_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{L}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{k}}\ell(\bm{\beta};Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})-\lambda_{\mu,n}\|(\bm{\beta}_{-1}/\bm{\widetilde{\beta}}^{\ast}_{-1})^{\gamma}\|_{1}\right\}

We then estimate
\rm{TPR}_{0}(c)
as

\widehat{\rm{TPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0,CV}(c)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}I(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}}\geq c)\widehat{m}_{0,CV}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}})}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{T}}\widehat{m}_{0,CV}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\widehat{\beta}}})}

Similarly, we may construct CV estimators for
\rm{FPR}_{0}(c)
,
\rm{ROC}_{0}(c)
,
\rm{PPV}_{0}(c)
and
\rm{NPV}_{0}(c)
, respective denoted as
\widehat{\rm{FPR}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0,CV}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{ROC}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0,CV}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{PPV}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0,CV}(c)
,
\widehat{\rm{NPV}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf STEAM}_{0,CV}(c)
.
