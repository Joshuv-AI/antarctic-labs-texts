Abstract
. We study the existence and uniqueness of direct sum decompositions in additive bicategories. We find a simple definition of Krull–Schmidt bicategories, for which we prove the uniqueness of decompositions into indecomposable objects as well as a characterization in terms of splitting of idempotents and properties of 2-cell endomorphism rings. Examples of Krull–Schmidt bicategories abound, with many arising from the various flavors of 2-dimensional linear representation theory.

1.
Introduction and results

A Krull–Schmidt category is an additive category in which every object admits a decomposition into a direct sum of finitely many objects which are strongly indecomposable,
i.e.
whose endomorphism rings are local.
In fact, in a Krull–Schmidt category strongly indecomposable objects coincide with those which are indecomposable for direct sums.
Most importantly, every object is a direct sum of indecomposable ones in a unique way, up to isomorphism and permutation of the factors.
There is a nice characterization of Krull–Schmidt categories: They are precisely those additive categories in which all idempotent morphisms split and all endomorphism rings are semiperfect in the sense of Bass. See
e.g.

[
Kra15
]
.

The goal of this note is to prove versions of the above eminently useful facts in the setting of additive
bi
categories.
Perhaps surprisingly, the new bicategorical theory is arguably more elegant and easier to use than its classical counterpart.

The usual 1-categorical Krull–Schmidt property holds, in particular, for categories of finite dimensional representations of groups and algebras. It is a cornerstone of representation theory which helps alleviate the pain of leaving the semisimple case for the modular case, where it becomes imperative to understand the global structure of categories of representations.
The Krull–Schmidt property is also surprisingly subtle (
e.g.
it holds for finite length modules over any ring but fails for artinian modules already over certain semilocal noetherian commutative rings
[
FHLV95
]

[
Fac03
]
), and it has benefited from some serious investigation.
Similarly, as
2-representation theory
(in its various directions) is beginning to venture beyond the semisimple case too, it seems wise to prepare a suitable Krull–Schmidt theory.
We do this in Sections
2
-
6
.

The final
Section

7
discusses examples of Krull–Schmidt bicategories.
They include various bimodule bicategories of rings (such as rings with noetherian center), bicategories of Mackey 2-motives in the sense of Balmer–Dell’Ambrogio (
7.9
– our original motivating example),
semisimple 2-categories such as that of finite dimensional 2-vector spaces à la Kapranov–Voevodsky–Neuchl (
7.10
) or 2-Hilbert spaces à la Baez (
7.11
),
finite module categories over finite tensor categories à la Etingof–Ostrik (
7.18
),
the 2-category of finite dimensional 2-representations of any 2-group over any field (
7.21
),
and the 2-category of 2-representations of a finitary linear 2-category à la Mazorchuk–Miemietz (
7.24
).
Underlying the representation-theoretic examples is
7.14
, which categorifies the classical fact that finite dimensional representations of any algebra form a Krull–Schmidt category.
In view of all this, we expect Krull–Schmidt bicategories to enter the basic toolkit of any future modular (
i.e.
nonsemisimple) linear 2-representation theory, where global structural questions should gain in importance relative to combinatorial ones.

We now present our theoretical results in details.

We say a bicategory is
additive
if it is locally additive,
i.e.
its Hom categories are additive and its composition functors are additive in both variables, and if it admits all finite direct sums of its objects; see
Section

2
for details. For instance additive categories, additive functors and natural transformations form a very large additive bicategory.
A nonzero object
X
in an additive bicategory is
indecomposable
if a direct sum decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
necessarily implies that
X_{1}\simeq 0
or
X_{2}\simeq 0
(where
\simeq
denotes equivalence).
We say
X
is
strongly indecomposable
if its 2-cell endomorphism ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}(X,X)}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
is a connected commutative ring (it is always commutative by the Eckmann–Hilton argument).
Here is our main result:

Theorem

(See
4.3
)
.

Suppose that an object
X
of an additive bicategory admits two direct sum decompositions
Y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus Y_{n}
and
Z_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus Z_{m}
into strongly indecomposable objects.
Then
n=m
and there are equivalences
Y_{k}\simeq Z_{\sigma(k)}
for all
k
after some permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
of the factors.

This suggests the following definition: An additive bicategory is
Krull–Schmidt
if every object is equivalent to a direct sum of finitely many strongly indecomposable objects, in the above sense. We easily deduce (
4.6
) that indecomposable and strongly indecomposable objects coincide in a Krull–Schmidt bicategory, hence that every object admits a unique decomposition into indecomposable ones.

We also obtain a very satisfying characterization of Krull–Schmidt bicategories.
Let us call a commutative ring
semiconnected
if it is a direct product of finitely many connected rings,
i.e.
if its Zariski spectrum has finitely many topological connected components.
We also say an additive bicategory is
weakly block complete

if every decomposition
1_{X}=e_{1}+e_{2}
of the identity 2-cell
1_{X}\in\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
as a sum of two orthogonal idempotent 2-cells is induced by a direct sum decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
at the level of objects (see details in
2.10
).
Then:

Theorem

(See
4.8
)
.

An additive bicategory is Krull–Schmidt if and only if it is weakly block complete and the 2-cell endomorphism ring
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
of every object
X
is semiconnected.

Some comments are warranted.

As far as we can see, our theory does not recover its classical counterpart because we cannot usefully view an additive category as an additive bicategory. Thus our definitions and results are just analogs rather than generalizations.

On its face, our 2-categorical definition of a strongly indecomposable object
X
may appear to be unrelated to the original 1-categorical notion.
A direct 2-categorical analog of “
\End(X)
is local” would be to require that whenever
F
and
G
are two 1-cells
X\to X
which are not equivalences, then
F\oplus G\colon X\to X
is not an equivalence either. But it turns out that the latter property is a consequence of our definition, and as soon as the category
\End(X)
is idempotent complete (a mild hypothesis) the two become equivalent.
In fact, all reasonable options we could think of become equivalent under the same hypothesis (
5.4
).

Obviously, in an additive 1-category endomorphism rings are rarely commutative and there are no 2-cell endomorphism rings.
This might explain why passing to a higher-categorical setting actually appears to make indecomposables and the uniqueness of decompositions
easier
to handle—insofar as semiconnected rings are easier than semiperfect ones.
It also makes examples easier to recognize,
e.g.
we do not feel any pressing need to discuss higher versions of bi-chain conditions, finite length objects, etc. In order to recognize a Krull–Schmidt bicategory using our characterization, it suffices to ensure weak block completeness (which can always be implemented by
[
BD20
, Thm. A.7.23]
) and to have a reasonable grasp of the 2-cell Homs (
e.g.
it suffices that they are not unreasonably large).

We thought it instructive to give two different proofs of the uniqueness theorem.
The first proof (in
Section

4
), which seems not to have any 1-categorical analog, easily follows from two facts. Firstly, that a direct summand
Y
of
X
is determined up to equivalence by the idempotent element of
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
corresponding to
1_{Y}
; note that, by definition, the summand is strongly indecomposable precisely when the idempotent is primitive. Secondly, that in a commutative ring there cannot be two different decompositions of
1
as a sum of orthogonal primitive idempotents.
The second proof (in
Section

6
) is a calque of the usual 1-categorical proof, and proceeds by recursively comparing summands of two decompositions of the same object via the given equivalences.
Both proofs are easy in principle but require a close inspection of the bicategorical notion of “direct summand”, which we carry out in
Section

3
.
To wit, in weakly block complete bicategories we characterize a direct summand
Y
of
X
in a few equivalent ways: as a special kind of ambijunction
Y\leftrightarrows X
, or (forgetting the object
Y
) as a special kind of Frobenius monad on
X
, or quite magically (forgetting the 1-cell
X\to X
) as an idempotent in
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
.

2.
Preliminaries on direct sums

Most of these preliminaries are also covered in
[
BD20
, §A.7]
, where the reader may find more details and context.

2.1 Convention
.

We will use the standard terminology for bicategories, as recalled
e.g.
in
[
BD20
, App. A]
.
However, whenever convenient and without further mention we will pretend our bicategories are strict 2-categories in order to simplify calculations and to more easily talk about (internal) monads; this is justified by the coherence theorem for bicategories (
[
JY21
, Ch. 8]
).
If
\mathcal{B}
is a bicategory, we write
\mathcal{B}(X,Y)
for its Hom categories and
\End_{\mathcal{B}(X,Y)}(F)
or just
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(F)
or
\End(F)
for the 2-cell endomorphism set of a 1-cell
F\colon X\to Y
. Internally to a bicategory,
\cong
and
\simeq
stand for isomorphism and equivalence, respectively;
\mathrm{Id}_{X}
and
\mathrm{id}_{F}
for identity 1-cells and identity 2-cells, respectively; and also
1_{X}
or just
1
is short for
\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{Id}_{X}}
.
Products, coproducts, initial and final objects in a bicategory are understood in the sense of
pseudo
(co)limits (a.k.a. bilimits
[
JY21
, §5]
), rather than strict ones; in particular, they are only unique up to equivalence rather than isomorphism.
We mostly ignore set-theoretical size issues as irrelevant to this topic.

2.2 Definition
.

A bicategory
\mathcal{B}
is
locally additive
if each Hom category
\mathcal{B}(X,Y)
is additive and each composition functor
\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)\times\mathcal{B}(X,Y)\to\mathcal{B}(X,Z)
is additive in both variables.

2.3 Definition
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a locally additive bicategory.
A
direct sum
of two objects
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is a diagram of 1-cells of
\mathcal{B}
of the form

(2.4)

\textstyle{X_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{I_{1}}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{P_{1}}
\scriptstyle{P_{2}}
\textstyle{X_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{I_{2}}

such that there exist isomorphisms

P_{i}I_{i}\cong\mathrm{Id}_{X_{i}}
and
P_{i}I_{j}\cong 0
(for
i\neq j
) and
I_{1}P_{1}\oplus I_{2}P_{2}\cong\mathrm{Id}_{X}
, the latter two being a zero object and a direct sum in the additive categories
\Hom_{\mathcal{B}}(X_{j},X_{i})
and
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, respectively.
As usual, we will write
X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
to indicate the object
X
or even the whole diagram;
direct sums of
n\geq 2
objects are defined similarly; and the direct sum of no objects is by definition a zero objet of
\mathcal{B}
,
i.e.
one which is both initial and final.

2.5 Definition
.

We say that a bicategory is
additive
if it is locally additive and if it admits arbitrary finite direct sums of its objects.

2.6 Example
.

The (very large) 2-category
\mathsf{ADD}
of (non-necessarily small) additive categories, additive functors and natural transformations is an additive bicategory.
Direct sums are provided by the (strict) finite Cartesian products of categories, with their projection functors
P_{i}\colon\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}\to\mathcal{A}_{i}
and with inclusion functors defined by
I_{1}\colon A_{1}\mapsto(A_{1},0)
and
I_{2}\colon A_{2}\mapsto(0,A_{2})
.

2.7 Definition
.

An object
X
of an additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
is
indecomposable
if whenever there is an equivalence
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
then
X_{1}\simeq 0
or
X_{2}\simeq 0
.

2.8 Remark
.

One easily checks that any direct sum as in
2.3
is also a product
(X,P_{1},P_{2})
and a coproduct
(X,I_{1},I_{2})
of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
(and similarly with
n\geq 2
factors).
It follows that direct sums are unique up to equivalence.
Also, every direct sum decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
induces equivalences of additive categories

(2.9)

\mathcal{B}(Y,X)\simeq\mathcal{B}(Y,X_{1})\times\mathcal{B}(Y,X_{2})\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathcal{B}(X,Y)\simeq\mathcal{B}(X_{1},Y)\times\mathcal{B}(X_{2},Y)

for all objects
Y
.
It is therefore possible to use matrix notation in the usual way for 1-cells as well as 2-cells between two directs sums, just as one does inside each additive Hom category.
In particular (
cf.

[
BD20
, Rem. A.7.15]
), we may decompose the category
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(X)
into four factors.
Then
\mathrm{Id}_{X}
corresponds to the diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{Id}_{X_{1}},\mathrm{Id}_{X_{2}})
and its endomorphism ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}(X,X)}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
is also diagonal (
cf.

5.1
if necessary), so that we get an isomorphism of commutative rings

\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})\cong\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X_{1}})\times\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X_{2}}).

The latter is equivalent to a decomposition of
1_{X}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{Id}_{X}}
as a sum
e_{1}+e_{2}
of two orthogonal idempotents, with
e_{i}
corresponding to
1_{X_{i}}
.
Given such a decomposition of
1_{X}
, however, nothing guarantees that it arises as above from a direct sum decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
. Whence:

2.10 Definition
.

An additive bicategory is
weakly block complete
if for every object
X
and every idempotent element
e=e^{2}
of the ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
there exists a direct sum decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
under which the 2-cells
e
and
1_{X}-e
correspond to
[\,{}^{1}_{0}\,{}^{0}_{0}\,]
and
[\,{}^{0}_{0}\,{}^{0}_{1}\,]
, respectively, as in
2.8
.
As in
[
BD20
]
, we say that
\mathcal{B}
is
block complete
if moreover every Hom category of
\mathcal{B}
is idempotent complete.

2.11 Theorem

(See
[
BD20
, A.7.23–24]
)
.

For every additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
, there exists a block complete bicategory
\mathcal{B}^{\flat}
and a 2-fully faithful pseudofunctor
\mathcal{B}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\flat}
which is 2-universal among additive pseudofunctors to block complete bicategories.

2.12 Remark
.

Using
2.11
, we can also produce a similarly universal
weak block completion

\mathcal{B}^{w\flat}
for any additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
:
It suffices to take the smallest 2-full sub-bicategory of
\mathcal{B}^{\flat}
which contains (the image of)
\mathcal{B}
and is weakly block complete. It will have the same objects as
\mathcal{B}^{\flat}
but may lack some of its 1-cells.

In
Section

7
we will need the fact that block completeness and weak block completeness are inherited pointwise, in the sense that bicategories of pseudofunctors inherit these properties from their target bicategory. More precisely:

2.13 Terminology
.

Let
\Bbbk
be a commutative ring.
A bicategory is
\Bbbk
-linear
if its Hom categories and composition functors are
\Bbbk
-linear (
i.e.
enriched in
\Bbbk
-modules).
For instance, a locally additive bicategory is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear bicategory which also admits finite direct sums of parallel 1-cells.
A pseudofunctor
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
between
\Bbbk
-linear bicategories is
\Bbbk
-linear
if each functor
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{B}(X,Y)\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}X,\mathcal{F}Y)
is
\Bbbk
-linear,
i.e.
preserves
\Bbbk
-linear combinations of 2-cells.
Note that a
\Bbbk
-linear pseudofunctor preserves whatever direct sums of 1-cells and objects exist in its source, as the latter are equationally defined (
cf.

[
BD20
, Prop. A.7.14]
).
We will denote by

\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})

the bicategory of
\Bbbk
-linear pseudofunctors
\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
, pseudonatural transformations between them, and modifications;
see
e.g.

[
BD20
, §A.1]
for details.

2.14 Proposition
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is (weakly) block complete then so is
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

First note that
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
inherits a pointwise
\Bbbk
-linear structure. Namely, suppose
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
are pseudofunctors,
t,s\colon\mathcal{F}_{1}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{2}
pseudonatural transformations,
M,M^{\prime}\colon t\Rrightarrow s
modifications, and
\lambda\in\Bbbk
a scalar.
Then there is a modification
M+\lambda M^{\prime}\colon t\Rrightarrow s
whose component at each object
X\in\mathcal{B}
is defined by setting
(M+\lambda M^{\prime})_{X}:=M_{X}+\lambda M^{\prime}_{X}
in the
\Bbbk
-module
\Hom_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{1}X,\mathcal{F}_{2}X)}(t_{X},s_{X})
.
Similarly,
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
inherits direct sums of 1-cells and objects from
\mathcal{C}
:
The components in
\mathcal{C}
of the transformation
t\oplus s\colon\mathcal{F}_{1}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{2}
are

(t\oplus s)_{X}:=t_{X}\oplus s_{X}\colon\mathcal{F}_{1}X\to\mathcal{F}_{2}X

for all objects
X\in\mathcal{B}
and the diagonal

(t\oplus s)_{F}:=t_{F}\oplus s_{F}=\left(\,{}^{t_{F}}_{0}\,\;{}^{0}_{s_{F}}\right)\colon\mathcal{F}_{2}F\circ(t\oplus s)_{X}\overset{\sim}{\Longrightarrow}(t\oplus s)_{Y}\circ\mathcal{F}_{1}F

for all 1-cells
F\colon X\to Y
of
\mathcal{B}
(for the latter, we use that horizontal composition in
\mathcal{C}
preserves direct sums of 1-cells), with the evident direct sum structure maps.

For the direct sum
\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2}
of pseudofunctors, set
(\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2})(X):=\mathcal{F}_{1}X\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2}X
on objects, with diagonal component
\Bbbk
-linear functors (at each pair
X,Y\in\Obj\mathcal{B}
)

\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2}:=\left(\,{}^{\mathcal{F}_{1}}_{0}\,\;{}^{0}_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}\right)\colon\mathcal{B}(X,Y)\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}_{1}X\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2}X,\mathcal{F}_{1}Y\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2}Y),

where the right-hand side is decomposed into four categories as in
2.8
.

It remains to see that
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
also inherits the splitting of 1-cells and objects.
Suppose
M=M^{2}\colon t\Rrightarrow t
is an idempotent modification.
Then each component
M_{X}\colon t_{X}\Rightarrow t_{X}
is an idempotent 2-cell of
\mathcal{C}
, giving rise to a decomposition
t_{X}\cong\img(M_{X})\oplus\img(\mathrm{id}_{t_{X}}-M_{X})
as soon as that idempotent splits in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}_{1}X,\mathcal{F}_{2}X)
.
We can then assemble these splittings into an isomorphism
t\cong\img(M)\oplus\img(\mathrm{id}_{t}-M)
in the additive category
\PsNat(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2})
of transformations and modifications.
Thus if
\mathcal{C}
is locally idempotent complete so is
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
.
If
\mathcal{C}
is weakly block complete, in the special case when
t=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{F}}
is the identity transformation of
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{2}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
we also have decompositions
\mathcal{F}X\simeq Y_{X}\oplus Z_{X}
of objects of
\mathcal{C}
with
1_{Y_{X}}
and
1_{Z_{X}}
corresponding under these equivalences to the identity 2-cells of
\img(M_{X})
and
\img(1_{\mathcal{F}X}-M_{X})
, respectively.
Any choice of such adjoint equivalences assembles into the required decomposition
\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{F}}\simeq\img(M)\oplus\img(1_{\mathcal{F}}-M)
in
\PsNat(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2})
. (If necessary, to see why the latter assertion is true one may use the characterizations of direct summands treated in the next section.)

2.15 Remark
.

In
[

]

3.
Characterizations of direct summands

We work throughout in a locally additive bicategory.
Note that, when referring to a direct sum
X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
as in (
2.4
), we were implicitly assuming a choice was made for its four projection and injection 1-cells, but not for the 2-isomorphisms implementing their relations.
We can do better:

3.1 Proposition
.

Consider any direct sum
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
as in (
2.4
).
Then the 2-cells implementing the direct sum relations can be chosen so that they simultaneously form four adjunctions
P_{i}\dashv I_{i}
and
I_{i}\dashv P_{i}
(
i=1,2
).
More precisely,
write

\textstyle{P_{i}I_{i}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varepsilon_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Id}_{X_{i}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overline{\eta}_{i}}

and

\textstyle{I_{1}P_{1}\oplus I_{2}P_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\!\!\!\scriptstyle{\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}}&\!\!\scriptstyle{\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}}\end{array}\right]}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Id}_{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces,}
\scriptstyle{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\!\!\!\scriptstyle{\eta_{1}}\\
\!\!\!\scriptstyle{\eta_{2}}\end{array}\right]}

for the 2-cell components; they can be chosen so that, besides satisfying the relations

(3.2)

\varepsilon_{i}=(\overline{\eta}_{i})^{-1}\quad\quad\quad\eta_{i}\overline{\varepsilon}_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{id}_{I_{i}P_{i}}&\textrm{ if }i=j\\
0&\textrm{ if }i\neq j\end{array}\right.\quad\quad\quad\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\eta_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\eta_{2}=1_{X}

they also partake in the adjunctions (for
i=1,2
)

(P_{i}\dashv I_{i},\eta_{i},\varepsilon_{i})\quad\textrm{ and }\quad(I_{i}\dashv P_{i},\overline{\eta}_{i},\overline{\varepsilon}_{i})

as units or counits.
The analogous statement holds for sums of
n\geq 2
objects.

3.3 Definition
.

An
adjoint direct sum
in a locally additive bicategory is the data of a direct sum diagram together with a choice of 2-cells as in
3.1
.

3.4 Remark
.

Consider any direct sum diagram (
2.4
), say with 2-cell components
\beta_{i}\colon P_{i}I_{i}\overset{\sim}{\Rightarrow}\mathrm{Id}_{X_{i}}
whose inverse we will write
\overline{\alpha}_{i}
, and
[\overline{\beta}_{1}\;\;\overline{\beta}_{2}]\colon I_{1}P_{1}\oplus I_{2}P_{2}\overset{\sim}{\Rightarrow}\mathrm{Id}_{X}
with inverse
{}^{t}[\alpha_{1}\;\;\alpha_{2}]
.
These 2-cells will satisfy the direct sum relations as in (
3.2
), of course.
Moreover, not only are
\beta_{i}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{i}
mutually inverse, but we also have

P_{i}\overline{\beta}_{i}=(P_{i}\alpha_{i})^{-1}\colon P_{i}\overset{\sim}{\Rightarrow}P_{i}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\overline{\beta}_{i}I_{i}=(\alpha_{i}I_{i})^{-1}\colon I_{i}\overset{\sim}{\Rightarrow}I_{i}.

Indeed, these 2-cell pairs are already mutual inverses on one side (even before applying
P_{i}
or
I_{i}
) by one of the defining relations.
To see that they are inverses on the other side too, it suffices to whisker the relation
\overline{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{1}+\overline{\beta}_{2}\alpha_{2}=1_{X}
by
P_{i}
or

3.5 Remark
.

There is a far less elementary but perhaps more suggestive proof of
3.1
, which goes as follows.
Firstly note that the 2-category
\mathsf{ADD}
of additive categories has a canonical choice of
adjoint
direct sums: Just take Cartesian products, with the projections
P_{i}
and injections
I_{i}
as in
2.6
and the evident 2-cells (involving solely identity and zero natural transformations). Indeed, in this case we even have
P_{i}I_{i}=\mathrm{Id}
and all required triangular identities are trivially true.
Secondly, for any locally additive (or just
\mathbb{Z}
-linear) bicategory
\mathcal{B}
, the bicategory
\PsFun_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathsf{ADD})
of additive pseudofunctors (

\mathsf{ADD}
, by the very same level-wise construction as in the proof of
2.14
.
Thirdly, observe that adjoint direct sums (or even just their 2-cells) can be transported along (adjoint) equivalences.
Finally, recall (
e.g.
from
[
BD20
, Rem. A.7.25]
) that for any locally additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
there is a Yoneda embedding
\mathcal{B}\to\PsFun_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathsf{ADD})
which is an additive pseudofunctor and a biequivalence

onto its full image

.
Using

and a pseudoinverse

, any direct sum diagram that exists in
\mathcal{B}
can therefore be corrected into an adjoint sum by the previous remarks.

In the following, we will tacitly assume all our direct sums to be adjoint in the sense of
3.3
.
Let us now study a single summand at a time.

3.6 Definition
.

In a locally additive bicategory, a

of an object

consists of an object
Y
, two 1-cells

and

and two adjunctions

(P\dashv I,\eta,\varepsilon)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad(I\dashv P,\overline{\eta},\overline{\varepsilon})

such that
\varepsilon=\overline{\eta}^{-1}
and
\eta\overline{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{id}_{IP}
.
A splitting datum for
X
is a
direct summand of
X
if it appears as one of the ambijunctions

for some decomposition into an adjoint direct sum

as in

A
morphism
of splitting data
(Y,I,P,\eta,\varepsilon,\overline{\eta},\overline{\varepsilon})
and
(Y^{\prime},I^{\prime},P^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime},\overline{\eta}^{\prime},\overline{\varepsilon}^{\prime})

(In other words, forgetting some structure, here we are considering splitting data as objects of the comma bicategory of
\mathcal{B}
under

of splitting data for

3.9 Remark
.

An idempotent monad
(T\colon X\to X,\mu\colon T^{2}\overset{\sim}{\Rightarrow}T,\iota\colon\mathrm{Id}_{X}\Rightarrow T)
on an object
X
is the same thing as a
localization
of
X

(T,\iota\colon\mathrm{Id}_{X}\Rightarrow T)
such that
T\eta
and
\eta T\colon T\Rightarrow T^{2}
are equal and invertible (their common inverse then recovers the multiplication

Dually, an idempotent comonad is the same as a
colocalization
.
Thus

says that a splitting datum amounts to a pair

(E,\eta\colon\mathrm{Id}_{X}\Rightarrow E)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad(E,\overline{\varepsilon}\colon E\Rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{X})

is a section of
\eta

3.10 Remark
.

The monad and comonad of

form an

(T,\mu,\iota,\delta,\epsilon)
on

such that
\iota\epsilon=\mathrm{id}_{T}

Indeed, the latter equation is

“idempotent” refers to multiplication

3.11 Definition
.

For any splitting datum
(Y,I,P,\eta,\varepsilon,\overline{\eta},\overline{\varepsilon})

to the given splitting datum.

4.
Krull–Schmidt bicategories

We are now ready to prove our main results. Indeed, it will be a very easy task because most of the work is already done.

4.1 Definition
.

We say that a nonzero object
X
of a locally additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
is
strongly indecomposable
if the commutative ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}(X,X)}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
is connected.

Let us justify the use of “strongly”:

4.2 Remark
.

Strongly indecomposable objects are indecomposable.
Indeed, nontrivial decompositions
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
produce (as in
2.8
) nontrivial decompositions
1_{X}=e_{1}+e_{2}
in orthogonal idempotents of
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
, where
e_{i}
is the idempotent associated to the direct summand
X_{i}
as in
3.11
.

4.3 Theorem
.

Let
X
be an object of an additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
which admits
two direct sum decompositions

X\simeq Y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus Y_{n}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad X\simeq Z_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus Z_{m}

such that all
Y_{k}
and
Z_{\ell}
are strongly indecomposable (
4.1
).
Then
n=m
and, after renumbering the factors, there are equivalences
Y_{k}\simeq Z_{k}
for all
k
.

Proof.

By construction, the identities
1_{Y_{k}}
(
k=1,\ldots,n
) correspond under the given equivalences to a complete family of orthogonal primitive idempotents
e_{k}
in the commutative ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
.
Here each
e_{k}
is the idempotent associated with the direct summand
Y_{k}
.
Similarly, the direct summands
Z_{\ell}
yield a complete family of orthogonal primitive idempotents
f_{\ell}
.
But then clearly, this being a commutative ring, each of the
e_{k}
must be equal to exactly one of the
f_{\ell}
.
Thus
n=m
and we find a permutation
\sigma
such that
e_{k}=f_{\sigma(k)}
for all
k
.
It follows by
3.13
that
Y_{k}
and
Z_{\sigma(k)}
are equivalent, even as direct summands (
i.e.
splitting data) of
X
.
∎

4.4 Definition
.

We say an additive bicategory is
Krull–Schmidt
if every nonzero object admits a direct sum decomposition into strongly indecomposable objects.

4.5 Remark
.

In a weakly block complete bicategory (
2.10
), indecomposable objects and strongly indecomposable objects coincide. Indeed, by definition, each nontrivial decomposition of the 2-cell
1_{X}
as a sum of orthogonal idempotents arises from a nontrivial direct sum decomposition of the object
X
.

4.6 Corollary
.

In a Krull–Schmidt bicategory (
4.4
), indecomposable and strongly indecomposable objects coincide, and every object decomposes in an essentially unique way into a direct sum of indecomposable objects.

Proof.

Strongly indecomposable objects are indecomposable by
4.2
.
By hypothesis every object
X
admits a decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{n}
into strongly indecomposables. If
X
is indecomposable,
n=1
hence
X\simeq X_{1}
is strongly indecomposable.
The remaining statement now follows from
4.3
.
∎

4.7 Definition
.

As in the introduction, we say a commutative ring is
semiconnected
if it is a direct product of finitely many connected rings.

4.8 Theorem
.

An additive bicategory is Krull–Schmidt if and only if it is weakly block complete and every 2-cell endomorphism ring is semiconnected.

Proof.

Consider an idempotent 2-cell
e\colon\mathrm{Id}_{X}\Rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{X}
on an object
X
of a Krull–Schmidt bicategory
\mathcal{B}
.
Let
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{n}
be a decomposition in strongly indecomposable objects
X_{k}
.
This equivalence induces a decomposition
1_{X}=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{n}
in orthogonal primitive idempotents, where
e_{k}\in\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
is the idempotent associated to
X_{k}
.
It follows that
e=\sum_{k\in J}e_{k}
for some subset
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
By
3.13
,
X\simeq\big(\bigoplus_{k\in J}X_{k}\big)\oplus\big(\bigoplus_{k\not\in J}X_{k}\big)

realizes the decomposition
1_{X}=e+(1_{X}-e)
at the level of objects.
This shows that
\mathcal{B}
is weakly block complete.
Also, each ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X_{k}})\cong e_{k}\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
is connected as
e_{k}
is primitive.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is weakly block complete and that for each object
X
there is a decomposition
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})\cong R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{n}
in finitely many connected rings
R_{k}
.
Then
1_{X}
is a sum of
n
primitive idempotents and, by weak block completeness, there follows a decomposition
X\simeq X_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{n}
with
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{X_{k}})\cong R_{k}
.
In particular, each
X_{k}
is strongly indecomposable.
This shows that
\mathcal{B}
is Krull–Schmidt.
∎

5.
Notions of strong indecomposability

In this section we compare various candidates for the notion of strong indecomposable objects in an additive bicategory (
5.4
).
This further justifies our choice in
4.1
, if needed, and relates it to its 1-categorical analog.

5.1 Lemma
.

Suppose
A
and
B
are two objects in an additive category such that the endomorphism ring
\End(A\oplus B)
of their sum is commutative.
Then
A
and
B
are Hom-orthogonal and therefore
\End(A\oplus B)=\End(A)\times\End(B)
.

Proof.

For
x\in\Hom(A,B)
and
y\in\Hom(B,A)
, consider the elements
u=[\,{}^{1}_{0}\,{}^{0}_{0}\,]
,
v=[\,{}^{0}_{0}\,{}^{0}_{1}\,]
,
f=[\,{}^{0}_{x}\,{}^{0}_{0}\,]
and
g=[\,{}^{0}_{0}\,{}^{y}_{0}\,]
of

\End(A\oplus B)
. By commutativity, the calculations
vf=f
and
fv=0
imply
x=0
, whereas
ug=g
and
gu=0
imply
y=0
.
∎

5.2 Proposition
.

Consider the following properties of a (nonzero) additive monoidal category
\mathcal{A}
,
i.e.
a category which is additive and monoidal and whose tensor functor
\otimes\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
is additive in both variables:

(1)

The commutative endomorphism ring
\End_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{1})
of the tensor unit
\mathbb{1}
is connected.

(2)

The tensor unit object
\mathbb{1}
is indecomposable in
\mathcal{A}
for the direct sum.

(3)

If a sum of two objects of
\mathcal{A}
is tensor-invertible, one of them must be too.

(4)

If
\mathcal{A}
is equivalent as a monoidal category to a product
\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
of two additive monoidal categories, then
\mathcal{A}_{1}\simeq 0
or
\mathcal{A}_{2}\simeq 0
.

Then (
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
4
), and if
\mathcal{A}
is idempotent complete all four are equivalent.

Proof.

(
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
):
Suppose that
\mathbb{1}\cong E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Since
\End(\mathbb{1})
is commutative, we have
\End(\mathbb{1})\cong\End(E_{1})\times\End(E_{2})
by
5.1
.
Assuming
\End(\mathbb{1})
is connected, we must therefore have
\End(E_{i})=0
and thus
E_{i}\cong 0
for
i=1
or
i=2
.

(
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
):
Suppose
A\oplus B
has a two-sided tensor inverse
C
, with
A,B,C\in\mathcal{A}
. Then
\mathbb{1}\cong(A\oplus B)\otimes C\cong(A\otimes C)\oplus(B\otimes C)
, hence if
\mathbb{1}
is indecomposable we must have
\mathbb{1}\cong A\otimes C
or
\mathbb{1}\cong B\otimes C
. Since
C
is invertible, either
A
or
B
must be too.

(
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
4
):
Given an equivalence
\mathcal{A}\simeq\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
of additive monoidal categories, there follows in
\mathcal{A}
a decomposition
\mathbb{1}\cong E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
, where
E_{i}
corresponds to the tensor unit of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.
Since
\mathbb{1}
is tensor invertible in
\mathcal{A}
, then by (
3
) either
E_{1}
or
E_{2}
must be as well.
Say
E_{1}
is, with inverse
F
.
Since
E_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}\simeq 0
, it follows that
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}=F\otimes E_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}\simeq 0
.
Thus (
4
) holds.

Finally, we show (
4
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
) assuming that
\mathcal{A}
is idempotent complete.
Let
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{1}}=e_{1}+e_{2}
for two orthogonal idempotents
e_{1},e_{2}\colon\mathbb{1}\to\mathbb{1}
.
For every object
A\in\mathcal{A}
, write
e_{i}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A}
for the endomorphism of
A
corresponding to
e_{i}\otimes A
under the structural isomorphism
A\cong\mathbb{1}\otimes A
.
Note that
e_{1}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A}
and
e_{2}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A}
are orthogonal idempotents on
A
with
\mathrm{id}_{A}=e_{1}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A}+\;e_{2}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A}
.
By idempotent completion, we get a decomposition
A\cong A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
where
A_{i}=\img(e_{i}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A})
.
One easily checks that this direct sum decomposition is functorial in
A
and gives rise to a decomposition
\mathcal{A}\simeq\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
of
\mathcal{A}
as an additive monoidal category, where
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the full subcategory
\{A\in\mathcal{A}\mid e_{i}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A}=\mathrm{id}_{A}\}
of
\mathcal{A}
.
By (
4
), we must have for either
i
that
\mathcal{A}_{i}\simeq 0
, that is that
\img(e_{i}\!\!\downharpoonright_{A})\cong 0
for all
A
and thus
e_{i}=0
.
This shows that the ring
\End_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbb{1})
is connected.
∎

5.3 Remark
.

In the situation of
5.2
, we could also consider the following two properties of the category
\mathcal{A}
which do not depend on its monoidal structure:

(1)
′

The commutative ring
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}})
of natural transformations
\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}}\Rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}}
,
i.e.
the categorical “center” of
\mathcal{A}
, is connected.

(4)
′

If
\mathcal{A}\simeq\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
as an additive category then
\mathcal{A}_{1}\simeq 0
or
\mathcal{A}_{2}\simeq 0
.

One can verify that (
1
)
′
\Rightarrow
(
1
), (
4
)
′
\Rightarrow
(
4
) and (
1
)
′
\Rightarrow
(
4
)
′
in general, and that (
1
)
′
\Leftrightarrow
(
4
)
′
if
\mathcal{A}
is idempotent complete.
But (
1
)
′
and (
4
)
′
do not seem otherwise relevant here.

The endomorphism category of any object in a locally additive bicategory can be viewed as an additive monoidal category, with tensor product given by horizontal composition. In this case,
5.2
translates as follows:

5.4 Corollary
.

For any (nonzero) object
X
of a locally additive bicategory
\mathcal{B}
, we have implications (
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
4
) between the following properties, and all four are in fact equivalent if the additive category
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(X)
is idempotent complete:

(1)

The commutative endomorphism ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
is connected.

(2)

The identity 1-cell
\mathrm{Id}_{X}
is indecomposable in the additive category
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(X)
.

(3)

If a sum of two 1-cells
X\to X
is an equivalence, then one of them must be too.

(4)

\End_{\mathcal{B}}(X)
is indecomposable as an additive monoidal category.
∎

5.5 Remark
.

Property (
3
) is the evident 2-categorical analog of being a
strongly indecomposable
object
A
of an additive (1-)category
\mathcal{A}
. Recall the latter means that
\End_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
is a local ring, that is: If a sum
f+g
of two morphisms
f,g\colon A\to A
is invertible then either
f
or
g
is invertible.
Since we do not wish to assume idempotent completion of our Hom categories, we are left with the choice of at least four reasonable 2-categorical analogs of strong indecomposability.
In
4.1
, we chose the strongest and most useful one.
It is also the only one which always implies the usual indecomposability and which is stable under (weak) block completion.

6.
Direct comparison of direct summands

In this section we explain how to categorify the usual proof of the uniqueness of decompositions in strongly indecomposables.
This does not use the characterization of direct summands via idempotents (
3.13
), but instead requires us to understand how to “cut down” a direct summand by another (
6.2
).
It is a considerably harder task than its 1-categorical analog, which is a triviality.

6.1 Lemma
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is weakly block complete, and that a 1-cell
E\colon X\to X
is part of an idempotent monad
(E,\mu,\iota)
and of an idempotent comonad
(E,\delta,\epsilon)
such that
\iota\epsilon=\mathrm{id}_{E}
.
Then there is a decomposition
X\simeq Y\oplus Z
such that the direct summand
Y
induces the given monad and comonad as in
3.8
.

Proof.

Since
\iota\epsilon=\mathrm{id}_{E}
, the 2-cell
e:=\epsilon\iota\colon\mathrm{Id}_{X}\Rightarrow E\Rightarrow\mathrm{Id}_{X}
is an idempotent element of the ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
, and so is
f:=1_{X}-e
.
By weak block completion, we deduce a direct sum decomposition
X\simeq Y\oplus Z
such that
\mathrm{Id}_{Y}
and
\mathrm{Id}_{Z}
correspond to
\img(e)
and
\img(f)
respectively.
Moreover, since
\mathrm{Id}_{X}\cong\img(e)\oplus\img(f)
and since
\epsilon
is a split mono by hypothesis, we have
\img(e)=\img(\iota)\cong E
in the category
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, compatibly with the respective structure 2-cells. We can now easily conclude.
∎

7.
Examples

We finally consider some examples of Krull–Schmidt bicategories, concentrating on various kinds of “2-representation theory”.
We begin with a few generalities:

7.1 Proposition
.

Suppose
\mathcal{B}
is an additive bicategory where the 2-cell endomorphism ring
\End_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Id}_{X})
of every object
X
is semiconnected (
4.7
).
Then both its block completion
\mathcal{B}^{\flat}
(
2.11
) and its weak block completion
\mathcal{B}^{w\flat}
(
2.12
) are Krull–Schmidt bicategories.

Proof.

The block completion and weak block completion are both weakly block complete bicategories.
Moreover, the canonical embeddings
\mathcal{B}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\flat}
and
\mathcal{B}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{w\flat}
are 2-fully faithful pseudofunctors, and by construction the 2-cell endomorphism ring of every object in either completion is a direct factor of that of some object of
\mathcal{B}
(
cf.

2.8
). Since direct factors of semiconnected rings are semiconnected, we conclude with the characterization of
2.11
.
∎

Clearly not every commutative ring is semiconnected (consider
R=\prod_{\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}
), hence we need some criteria.
The following two lemmas already go a long way:

7.2 Lemma
.

Every noetherian commutative ring is semiconnected.

Proof.

This can be deduced from the fact that the spectrum of a noetherian ring is a finite union of irreducible closed subsets (
[
Har77
, Prop. I.1.5 and Ex. II.2.13]
): The latter being connected, there are only finitely many connected components.
∎

7.3 Lemma
.

Unital subrings of semiconnected rings are semiconnected.

Proof.

This boils down to injective morphisms of commutative rings inducing maps with dense image between Zariski spectra (
[
AM69
, Ch. 1 Ex. 21(v)]
): In particular, they induce surjective maps between sets of connected components.
∎

The following criterion follows immediately from
7.2
and
4.8
.

7.4 Corollary
.

Suppose
\mathcal{B}
is a weakly block complete bicategory which is
\Bbbk
-linear over some noetherian commutative ring
\Bbbk
and such that every 2-cell Hom space is finitely generated as a
\Bbbk
-module.
Then
\mathcal{B}
is a Krull–Schmidt bicategory.
∎

We now turn to more concrete situations, beginning with a non-example:

7.5 Example
.

Recall from
2.6
the additive 2-category
\mathsf{ADD}
of additive categories.
Consider its 2- and 1-full additive sub-bicategory
\mathsf{ADD}^{\mathsf{ic}}
of idempotent complete categories:
It is block complete (see
[
BD20
, A.7.18]
) but not Krull–Schmidt, because it contains
\mathcal{A}=\Mod R
for rings
R
whose center
\mathrm{Z}(R)\cong\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}})
is not semiconnected. Here, and again below, we use the well-known identification of the center of a ring with the center
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\Mod R})
of its module category.

7.6 Example
.

Consider the full 2-subcategory
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathsf{ADD}^{\mathsf{ic}}
whose objects are the idempotent complete additive categories
\mathcal{A}
such that
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}})
is semiconnected.
Then
\mathcal{B}
is Krull–Schmidt.
Indeed, if
1_{\mathcal{A}}=e_{1}+e_{2}\in\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}})
for such a category
\mathcal{A}
with
e_{1},e_{2}
two orthogonal idempotents, we get a corresponding decomposition
\mathcal{A}\simeq\mathcal{A}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{2}
in
\mathsf{ADD}^{\mathsf{ic}}
, as the latter is weakly block complete.
But then
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}})=R_{1}\times R_{2}
with
R_{k}\cong\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}})
, hence the categories
\mathcal{A}_{k}
are again in
\mathcal{B}
since direct factors of semiconnected rings are semiconnected.
Thus
\mathcal{B}
is weakly block complete and we conclude with
4.8
.

7.7 Example
.

Many variations of the bicategory
\mathcal{B}
in
7.6
are possible.
For instance, we may consider the full subbicategory
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}
of module categories
\mathcal{A}=\Mod R
over rings
R
whose center is semiconnected.
(Indeed, if
\Mod R\cong\mathcal{A}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{2}
in
\mathsf{ADD}^{\mathsf{ic}}
for idempotents

, we have
\mathcal{A}_{k}\simeq\Mod R_{k}
for the rings

(

). Moreover, each
\mathrm{Z}(R_{k})
is semiconnected since
\mathrm{Z}(R)
is and
\mathrm{Z}(R)\cong\mathrm{Z}(R_{1})\times\mathrm{Z}(R_{2})
.)
Similarly, we may further restrict to rings

with noetherian center (by
7.2
) and/or replace
\Mod R
with the category of finitely presented/generated modules, or projective modules, finitely generated projectives, etc., indeed any option for which the previous splitting argument still goes through. We may also take module categories over finite dimensional algebras

over a fixed field
\Bbbk
and
\Bbbk
-linear functors between them, since the center of a finite dimensional algebra is again finite dimensional hence noetherian.

7.8 Example
.

Consider the bicategory of rings, bimodules and bimodules maps, and its sub-bicategory
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
of rings whose center is semiconnected.
Then
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
is Krull–Schmidt.
To see this, just recall that by the Eilenberg-Watts theorem we may identify
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
with the 2-full sub-bicategory of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathsf{ADD}^{\mathsf{ic}}
as in

having the same objects but where the only 1-cells are the colimit-preserving functors.
Since all functors involved in direct sum decompositions are colimit-preserving (they all have two-sided adjoints), the same object decompositions of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
equally work in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}

\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
is also weakly block complete.
As before, we may replace “semiconnected” with “noetherian”, or we may work over a fixed
\Bbbk
.

Our original motivation for this article is the following application:

7.9 Corollary
.

Let
\Bbbk
be any noetherian commutative ring.
Every bicategory of
\Bbbk
-linear
Mackey 2-motives
in the sense of
[
BD20
]
is Krull–Schmidt.
Similarly, every bicategory of
cohomological
Mackey 2-motives as in
[
BD21
]
is Krull–Schmidt.
By
4.3
, we deduce the uniqueness of the motivic decomposition of every finite group in each of these bicategories.

Proof.

These bicategories are constructed as block completions, hence in particular they are weakly block complete.
Moreover their 2-cell Hom spaces are easily seen to be finitely generated free
\Bbbk
-modules.
We may thus apply
7.1
or
7.4
. (The cohomological case is also a variant of
7.8
.)
∎

*\;*\;*

From now on we look at various flavors of linear 2-representation theory, starting with some commonly used target 2-categories for said 2-representations:

7.10 Example
.

Let
\Bbbk
be any field. Consider the 2-category
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
of
finite dimensional 2-vector spaces
(over
\Bbbk
) in the sense of Kapranov–Voevodsky
[
KV94
]
and Neuchl
[
Neu
]
.
Recall that
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
can be defined as the
\Bbbk
-linear 2-category of finite semisimple
\Bbbk
-linear categories,
\Bbbk
-linear functors and natural transformations.
Here a
\Bbbk
-linear category is
finite semisimple
if it is abelian and there is a finite set of absolutely simple objects (objects with one-dimensional endomorphism algebra) such that every other object is a direct sum of these in an essentially unique way. (Each finite semisimple category is in fact equivalent to
\mathrm{vect}_{\Bbbk}^{n}
for some
n
.)
One easily checks that
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
is block complete and all its 2-cell Hom
\Bbbk
-vector spaces are finite dimensional. In particular it is Krull–Schmidt by
7.4
.

7.11 Example
.

Consider the 2-category
2FHilb
of
finite-dimensional 2-Hilbert spaces
in the sense of Baez
[
Bae97
]
(see also
[
HV19
]
).
Recall that a 2-Hilbert space is a dagger category which is enriched on finite dimensional Hilbert spaces (compatibly with adjoints and inner products) and has finite direct sums and split idempotents; it is finite dimensional if it admits a finite set of simple objects generating all objects under finite direct sums.
Then
2FHilb
is Krull–Schmidt.
Indeed, there is a 2-functor
\textsf{2FHilb}\overset{\sim}{\to}\textsf{2FVect}_{\mathbb{C}}
forgetting daggers and inner products which is a biequivalence of bicategories (this is analogous to the equivalence between the 1-categories of finite dimensional Hilbert spaces and finite dimensional complex vector spaces).

7.12 Example
.

Let
\Bbbk
be a field.
A
\Bbbk
-linear category is
finite
if it is equivalent to that of finite dimensional representations of some finite dimensional associative unital
\Bbbk
-algebra.
The 2-category
\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}^{\mathsf{fin}}
of finite
\Bbbk
-linear categories,
\Bbbk
-linear functors and natural transformations is Krull–Schmidt; it is indeed a variation of
7.7
.

7.13 Example
.

For a field
\Bbbk
, consider the 2-category
\mathsf{TRI}_{\Bbbk}^{\mathsf{fin}}
whose objects are triangulated categories equivalent to the bounded derived category of some finite
\Bbbk
-linear category (
7.12
), triangulated functors and triangulated natural transformations. We leave it as an exercise to verify that
\mathsf{TRI}_{\Bbbk}^{\mathsf{fin}}
is Krull–Schmidt.

The next result will essentially take care of all our remaining examples:

7.14 Theorem
.

Let
\Bbbk
be any commutative ring (for instance
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Z}
or a field).
Consider the
\Bbbk
-linear bicategory
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
of
\Bbbk
-linear pseudofunctors between two
\Bbbk
-linear bicategories
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
(
2.13
).
Then
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
is Krull–Schmidt if
\mathcal{C}
is Krull–Schmidt and
\mathcal{B}
has finitely many equivalence classes of objects.

Proof.

By replacing
\mathcal{B}
with a biequivalent sub-bicategory if necessary, we may assume it has finitely many objects.
If
\mathcal{C}
is weakly block complete then so is
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
by
2.14
.
Now recall that for (non-necessarily
\Bbbk
-linear) pseudofunctors
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
and transformations
t,s\colon\mathcal{F}_{1}\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{2}
, a modification
M\colon t\Rrightarrow s
consists of a family
\{M_{X}\}_{X}
of 2-cells
M_{X}\colon t_{X}\Rightarrow s_{X}
of
\mathcal{C}
indexed by the objects
X
of
\mathcal{B}
, satisfying some relations.
In particular, for every
\Bbbk
-linear pseudofunctor
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
the ring
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{F}})
of self-modifications of the identity transformation is a unital subring of the product
\prod_{X\in\Obj\mathcal{B}}\End_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{F}X,\mathcal{F}X)}(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{F}X})
.
Being a product of finitely many semiconnected rings, the latter is semiconnected.
Hence so is
\End(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{F}})
by
7.3
.
Once again, we conclude with
4.8
.
∎

7.15 Remark
.

If
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
are 2-categories with
\mathcal{C}
Krull–Schmidt, the same proof (including that of
2.14
) shows that the 2-category
\2Fun^{\mathsf{str}}_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
of
strict
2-functors,
strict
transformations (
i.e.
2-natural transformations) and modifications is also Krull–Schmidt.
In fact, if the 2-category
\mathcal{B}
is small (which we had better assume anyway!), it should be possible to show that the 2-full inclusion
\2Fun^{\mathsf{str}}_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})\subset\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
is a biequivalence by adapting to the
\Bbbk
-linear case the coherence results for 2-monads due to Power and Lack; see
[
Pow89
]

[
Lac02
]

[
Lac07
]
.

A
\Bbbk
-linear tensor category
is a monoidal categories whose tensor product is a
\Bbbk
-linear functor of both variables. For a fixed such tensor category
\mathcal{A}
, we may consider the 2-category
\PsMod\mathcal{A}
whose objects are
(
\Bbbk
-linear left
)
module categories over
\mathcal{A}
, whose 1-morphisms are
module functors
and 2-morphisms
module natural transformations
; see
e.g.

[
Gre10
]
for detailed definitions.
(These notions specialize those of pseudomonoids and their left pseudomodules
[
DS97
]

[
McC00
]
to the symmetric monoidal 2-category
\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}
of
\Bbbk
-linear categories,
\Bbbk
-linear functors and natural transformations.)
In fact:

7.16 Lemma
.

For any
\Bbbk
-linear monoidal category
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathrm{B}\mathcal{A}
denote its delooping,
i.e.
the bicategory with a single object having
\mathcal{A}
as monoidal endo-category.
Then there is a canonical biequivalence (actually an isomorphism) of
\Bbbk
-linear 2-categories between
\PsMod\mathcal{A}
as defined above and
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathrm{B}\mathcal{A},\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk})
(
2.13
).

Proof.

The exponential law yields a bijection between objects of the 2-categories, with a (coherently associative and unital) pseudoaction
-\odot-\colon\mathcal{A}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
corresponding to a strong monoidal functor
\mathcal{A}\to\End_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{M})
, that is to a pseudofunctor
\mathrm{B}\mathcal{A}\to\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}
sending the unique object of
\mathrm{B}\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{M}
.
It is immediate from the definitions that module functors
F\colon\mathcal{M}_{1}\to\mathcal{M}_{2}
and module natural transformations
\varphi\colon F\Rightarrow F^{\prime}
correspond to pseudonatural transformations and modifications.
∎

7.17 Remark
.

By replacing
\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}
with a full 2-subcategory
\mathsf{ADD}_{\Bbbk}
,
\mathsf{ADD}^{\mathsf{ic}}_{\Bbbk}
,
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
etc. in the above, we can restrict attention to those tensor categories and their module categories which are additive, idempotent complete etc., as appropriate.

We conclude by specializing
7.14
to some examples of interest.

7.18 Corollary
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any finite (multi-) tensor category in the sense of Etingof–Ostrik
[
Ost03
]

[
EO04
]
, over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
.
Then the 2-category of finite module categories over
\mathcal{A}
is Krull–Schmidt, as well as its 2-subcategory of exact module categories.

Proof.

A finite tensor (or “multi-tensor”) category
\mathcal{A}
is in particular a
\Bbbk
-linear tensor category in our sense above; it is one which is
finite
as in
7.12
.
Since the 2-subcategory
\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}^{\mathsf{fin}}\subset\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}
of finite
\Bbbk
-linear categories is Krull–Schmidt, so is
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathrm{B}\mathcal{A},\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}^{\mathsf{fin}})
by
7.14
.
We conclude with
7.16
and
7.17
that finite
\mathcal{A}
-module categories form a Krull–Schmidt 2-category.

By definition, a module category
\mathcal{M}
is
exact
if
P\odot M
is a projective object in
\mathcal{M}
for every
M\in\mathcal{M}
and every projective
P\in\mathcal{A}
. Over a finite
\mathcal{A}
, exact module categories are finite (
[
Ost03
, Lemma 3.4]
) and are clearly closed under taking direct summands, hence they form a full 2-subcategory which is also Krull–Schmidt.
∎

7.19 Corollary
.

Every finite semisimple 2-category in the sense of Douglas–Reutter
[
DR18
]
is Krull–Schmidt, and its indecomposable and simple objects coincide.

Proof.

As in
[
DR18
]
, here we work over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero.
Then by
[
DR18
, Thms. 1.4.8-9]
, the 2-category of finite (left) semisimple module categories over a multifusion category
\mathcal{A}
is an example of a finite semisimple 2-category, and the latter are all of this form up to biequivalence. (The cited result is formulated for right module categories, but it holds equally well for left ones because multifusion categories are stable under reversing their tensor product).
By definition, a multifusion category is just a finite semisimple tensor category, thus in particular a
\Bbbk
-linear tensor category
\mathcal{A}
as above.
By
7.16
, with
\mathsf{CAT}_{\Bbbk}
replaced by
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
(
7.17
), we see that the 2-category of finite semisimple module categories over
\mathcal{A}
is biequivalent to
\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\mathrm{B}\mathcal{A},\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk})
.
Since
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
is Krull–Schmidt (
7.10
), we can therefore conclude with
7.14
.

It is easily checked that simples and indecomposables agree.
∎

7.20 Remark
.

We found it satisfying to derive
7.19
from
7.14
, but this is overkill: More directly, and more generally, it follows from the results in
[
DR18
]
that all
locally finite
semisimple 2-categories are also Krull–Schmidt.

Recall now that a
2-groupoid
is a bicategory in which every 1-cell is an equivalence and every 2-cell an isomorphism. It is a
2-group
if it has a single object,
i.e.
if it is the delooping of a monoidal groupoid whose objects are tensor-invertible.

7.21 Corollary
.

Let
\Bbbk
be any field.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a 2-groupoid with only finitely many equivalence classes of objects (
e.g.
a 2-group).
Then the bicategory

\mathsf{2FRep}_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{(}\mathcal{G}):=\PsFun(\mathcal{G},\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk})

of finite dimensional
\Bbbk
-linear 2-representations of
\mathcal{G}
is Krull–Schmidt.

Proof.

Consider the (easily constructed) free
\Bbbk
-linearization of
\mathcal{G}
, which is a
\Bbbk
-linear bicategory
\Bbbk\mathcal{G}
with the same objects as
\mathcal{G}
and equipped with a pseudofunctor
\mathcal{G}\to\Bbbk\mathcal{G}
inducing a biequivalence

\PsFun_{\Bbbk}(\Bbbk\mathcal{G},\mathcal{C})\overset{\sim}{\to}\PsFun(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{C})

for every
\Bbbk
-linear bicategory
\mathcal{C}
.
In particular, for
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
we obtain a biequivalence between
\mathsf{2FRep}_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{(}\mathcal{G})
and a Krull–Schmidt bicategory as in
7.14
.
∎

7.22 Remark
.

By varying the 2-groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, we can specialize
\mathsf{2FRep}_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{G})
to an impressive variety of families of interesting 2-categories already over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero,
i.e.
when we are at the semisimple intersection of
7.21
and
7.19
.
See the detailed bestiary in
[
DR18
, §1.4.5]
.

7.23 Remark
.

By replacing
\mathsf{2FVect}_{\Bbbk}
with
\mathsf{2FHilb}
(
7.11
) we obtain the Krull–Schmidt property for
unitary
finite dimensional representations of 2-groups.

7.24 Corollary
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be any
\Bbbk
-finitary 2-category in the sense of Mazorchuk–Miemietz
[
MM11
]
, over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
.
Then the 2-category
\mathcal{B}\mathfrak{-mod}
of
\Bbbk
-linear 2-representations of
\mathcal{B}
is Krull–Schmidt.

Proof.

By definition
\mathcal{B}\mathfrak{-mod}:=\2Fun^{\mathsf{str}}_{\Bbbk}(\mathcal{B},\mathsf{CAT}^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Bbbk})
as in
7.15
and
\mathcal{B}
has finitely many objects, hence we may once again apply
7.14
.
∎

Many further variations are possible, for instance one may wish to study finite “derived” rather than abelian or semisimple 2-representations by using the Krull–Schmidt 2-category
\mathsf{TRI}^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Bbbk}
as target (
7.13
).
As sampled above, most directions of 2-representation theory found in the literature impose strong finiteness conditions (often motivated by combinatorial goals) which ensure the Krull–Schmidt property.
We trust interested readers to adapt our proofs to any such situations.
