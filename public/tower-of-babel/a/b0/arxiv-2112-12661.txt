Capacity Bounds under Imperfect Polarization Tracking
Mohammad Farsi, Magnus Karlsson, Erik Agrell

Abstract
In optical fiber communication, due to the random variation of the environment, the state of polarization (SOP) fluctuates randomly with time leading to distortion and performance degradation. The memory-less SOP fluctuations can be regarded as a two-by-two random unitary matrix. In this paper, for what we believe to be the first time, the capacity of the polarization drift channel under an average power constraint with imperfect channel knowledge is characterized. An achievable information rate (AIR) is derived when imperfect channel knowledge is available and is shown to be highly dependent on the channel estimation technique. It is also shown that a tighter lower bound can be achieved when a unitary estimation of the channel is available. However, the conventional estimation algorithms do not guarantee a unitary channel estimation. Therefore, by considering the unitary constraint of the channel, a data-aided channel estimator based on the Kabsch algorithm is proposed, and its performance is numerically evaluated in terms of AIR. Monte Carlo simulations show that Kabsch outperforms the least-square error algorithm. In particular, with complex, Gaussian inputs and eight pilot symbols per block, Kabsch improves the AIR by 0.2 0.2 to 0.35 0.35 bits/symbol throughout the range of studied signal-to-noise ratios.



I
Introduction

The growing demand for reliable long-distance communication makes it essential to determine the capacity limits of optical links
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. The emergence of coherent optical communication systems enabled digital signal processing and polarization-division multiplexing to achieve higher spectral efficiencies. However, higher data rates come with increased sensitivity to impairments such as polarization-mode dispersion and state of polarization fluctuations, which must be tracked dynamically in the receiver
[
4
]
. Due to the random variation of both internal and environmental impairments, the state of polarization fluctuates randomly with time
[
5
]
. Previous long-term measurements showed that the state of polarization drift might vary from days and hours in buried fibers
[
6
,
7
]
to microseconds in aerial fibers
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

The conventional digital signal processing solutions for state of polarization tracking are replete with both blind and data-aided algorithms. The constant modulus algorithm
[
11
]
, thanks to its low complexity and tolerance to phase noise, has been widely considered for blind polarization tracking
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Many studies have been conducted to overcome the so-called “singularity” problem of constant modulus algorithm
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
, where the two estimated channels converge twice to the same polarization. The radially directed equalizer and its variants were proposed to account for higher-order quadrature amplitude modulation in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. The multi-modulus algorithm
[
21
]
is also applicable to higher-order modulations.
More reliable convergence can be obtained using data-aided algorithms such as least mean square, which was adopted for state of polarization estimation by
[
13
,
22
]
, or standard least-square error, which has been extensively studied for both wireless and optical applications
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. While the least mean square algorithm performs real-time continuous equalization, least-square error applies to block-based estimation.

While the dual-polarization channel can be represented by a unitary matrix, the majority of the available estimation techniques including least-square error and least mean square have no unitary constraint on the estimated channel, leading to sub-optimal solutions. This makes room for algorithms that provide a unitary estimation of the dual-polarization channel. Louchet
et al.

[
27
]
proposed the Kabsch algorithm
[
28
]
as a joint blind phase noise and polarization-mode dispersion estimation method. In
[
29
]
, a blind modulation-format-independent joint phase noise and polarization tracking algorithm was proposed, and its performance was compared with the blind Kabsch algorithm.

Depending on the estimation algorithm, the speed of the fluctuations, or the additive noise, the polarization tracking might be imperfect. This makes it relevant to ask how the capacity is affected by such an estimation error? While there are no capacity studies regarding the polarization drift channels, to the best of our knowledge, the literature is replete with fiber capacity results. The first finite capacity limit for the single-wavelength optical channel based on a sort of Gaussian Noise model was introduced in
[
30
,
31
]
, showing a lower bound that increases with the power until it reaches a maximum, and then it decreases due to the nonlinear impairments. Although different nonlinear models were applied, similar results were reported in
[
32
,
33
]
. Since the Gaussian Noise model cannot truly describe the nonlinear impairments
[
34
]
, some modeled the nonlinear impairments as a linear time-variant distortion highly correlated over time
[
35
,
36
]
. This made it possible to counteract nonlinear impairments using the techniques that were conventionally used for linear impairments (e.g., phase noise, chromatic dispersion, polarization-mode dispersion, and state of polarization
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
). For example, in
[
40
]
, a tighter lower bound for single-polarization, dispersion-unmanaged systems was achieved by considering the phase noise.

In this paper, for the first time, we study the capacity of the polarization drift channel in the presence of imperfect knowledge of the channel (e.g., imperfect polarization tracking).
Using the mismatched decoding method, we derive an AIR, which is a lower bound on the capacity, in the presence of a channel estimation error. We show that a unitary estimation of the channel leads to a tighter lower bound, which makes it reasonable to seek unitary estimators. A data-aided version of the Kabsch algorithm is proposed for dual-polarization channel estimation, for the first time to the best of our knowledge. We compare Kabsch with an least-square error algorithm in terms of achievable information rate and show that Kabsch outperforms least-square error throughout the range of considered signal-to-noise ratios. For instance, with only eight pilot symbols per channel and block, Kabsch improves the achievable information rate by at least
0.2
bits per symbol compared to least-square error.

II
Channel Model and Mutual Information

II-A

Channel Model

We consider transmission over
n
channels in the presence of amplified spontaneous emission noise at the receiver. The channel is assumed to be constant during a transmission block of length
N
, and changes randomly and independently between the blocks. The assumption that the channel does not change within a block is consistent with the fact that the state of polarization drifts at a much slower rate than typical transmission rates in optical links
[
6
,
7
]
and is well established in optical communications literature
[
38
,
40
]
.
The block length
N
is chosen based on the application and the drift speed of the channel.
In most optical communication systems, there is no feedback channel between the transmitter and the receiver. Therefore, the problem of input distribution optimization, corresponding to the case that the channel is known at the transmitter, is not investigated.
It is assumed that polarization-mode dispersion is negligible and all channel impairments, including nonlinearities and chromatic dispersion are ideally compensated, with the exception of polarization fluctuation and amplified spontaneous emission noise, which are modeled by a unitary matrix and additive white Gaussian noise, respectively.

Since different blocks are independent, we just model the symbols in one transmission block in the following. The transmitted signal in each channel at time
\smash{k=0,\ldots,N-1}
is an
n
-dimensional random vector
\mathbf{\underline{s}}_{k}
that takes on values from a set
\mathcal{S}
of zero-mean constellation points. After filtering and resampling the received signals into one sample per symbol, the vector of received samples
\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{k}
can be expressed as

\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{k}=\mathrm{H}\mathbf{\underline{s}}_{k}+\mathbf{\underline{z}}_{k}

(1)

where the
n\times n
matrix
\mathrm{H}
represents a multiple-input multiple-output channel and
\mathbf{\underline{z}}_{k}
denotes the complex amplified spontaneous emission noise samples at time
k
, which is assumed to be
\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n})
and independent of
\mathbf{\underline{s}}_{k}
.
In the remainder of this paper, we will omit the time index
k
explicitly for notational convenience. This is possible because the input and noise are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) over time.
We define the covariance matrix of the input vector as

\Lambda_{\underline{s}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}}}[\mathbf{\underline{s}}\mathbf{\underline{s}}^{\dagger}].

(2)

In order to maintain the generality of the results, they are given for an arbitrary number of channels
n
whenever possible. Thus, the multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel (
1
) can describe a wide range of applications and impairments. However, for the purpose of dual-polarization optical channel modeling, we are particularly interested in the special cases of
\smash{n=2}
and
\mathrm{H}
being unitary, denoted by
\mathrm{H_{u}}
.

II-B

Mutual Information with Perfect Knowledge of Channel at the Receiver

The conditional mutual information between two random vectors
\mathbf{\underline{s}}
,
\mathbf{\underline{x}}
, when the channel
\smash{\mathbf{H}=\mathrm{H}}
is given, is defined as
[
41
, Eq. 2.61]

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{\underline{s}};\mathbf{\underline{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}},\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{p(\mathbf{\underline{x}}|\mathbf{\underline{s}},\mathrm{H})}{p(\mathbf{\underline{x}}|\mathrm{H})}\right)\right].

(3)

The capacity of this channel under an average power constraint is
[
42
]

\displaystyle C=\max\limits_{p(\mathbf{\underline{s}})}{I}(\mathbf{\underline{s}};\mathbf{\underline{x}})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\tr(\Lambda_{\underline{s}})\leq P,

(4)

where
P
is the transmission power constraint. For the multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel in (
1
), the channel law given
\mathrm{H}
and
\underline{s}
is characterized by the probability density function

p(\underline{x}|\underline{s},\mathrm{H})=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n}\sigma_{z}^{2n}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\lVert\underline{x}-\mathrm{H}\underline{s}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}}\right).

(5)

Then, the probability density function of the channel output can be calculated as

\displaystyle p(\underline{x}|\mathrm{H})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}}}\left[p\right(\underline{x}|\mathbf{\underline{s}},\mathrm{H})].

(6)

Given
\mathrm{H}
, the capacity-achieving distribution of the multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel law (
5
) is
\mathcal{CN}

[
43
]
. Therefore, assuming
\mathcal{CN}(0,\Lambda_{\underline{s}})
inputs,

\displaystyle p(\underline{x}|\mathrm{H})=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n}|\Lambda_{\underline{x}}|}\exp\left(-\underline{x}^{{\dagger}}\Lambda_{\underline{x}}^{-1}\underline{x}\right),

(7)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\underline{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\mathbf{\underline{x}}\mathbf{\underline{x}}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{H}\Lambda_{\underline{s}}\mathrm{H}^{\dagger}+\sigma_{z}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n}

(8)

is the covariance matrix of the received samples when the channel is given.
The mutual information of a general multiple-input multiple-output system for a given channel is

[
43
]

\centering I(\mathbf{\underline{s}};\mathbf{\underline{x}})=\log\left|\mathrm{I}_{n}+\mathrm{H}^{{\dagger}}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{Q}\right|,\@add@centering

(9)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}=\Lambda_{\underline{s}}/\sigma_{z}^{2},

(10)

and hence
\smash{\tr(\mathrm{Q})\leq n\eta}
, where
\smash{\eta=P/(n\sigma_{z}^{2})}
is the signal-to-noise ratio of each channel. If the channel matrix
\mathrm{H}
is confined to the set of unitary matrices (i.e.,
\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{H_{u}}
), (
9
) gives

I(\mathbf{\underline{s}};\mathbf{\underline{x}})=\log\left|\mathrm{I}_{n}+\mathrm{Q}\right|.

(11)

The capacity of a unitary multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel is the supremum of (
11
) for all possible
\mathrm{Q}
. In general,
\mathrm{Q}
needs to be optimized for each realization of the channel
\mathrm{H}
, if it is known at the transmitter; however, based on (
11
), the mutual information is independent of
\mathrm{H}
. Since maximizing (
11
) is equivalent to maximizing
|\smash{\mathrm{I}_{n}+\mathrm{Q}}|
and
\mathrm{Q}
is positive definite, the nondiagonal elements of
\mathrm{Q}
must be zero, yielding
\mathrm{Q}
to be a diagonal positive definite matrix. From the well-known theorem that the geometric mean is always upper-bounded by the arithmetic mean, it is straight-forward to show that uniform power distribution at the transmitter (i.e.,
\smash{\mathrm{Q}=\eta\mathrm{I}_{n}}
) maximizes (
11
). Thus, the capacity of an
n
-dimensional unitary multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel, still assuming that the channel is perfectly known at the receiver, is

\centering C=n\log\left(1+\eta\right).\@add@centering

(12)

The capacity of the dual-polarization channel is given by simply setting
\smash{n=2}
in (
12
).

For a uniform input distribution over a given constellation
\mathcal{S}
, the integral in (
6
) must be replaced by a summation over all the constellation points, i.e.,

\displaystyle p(\underline{x}|\mathrm{H})=\sum_{\underline{s}\in\mathcal{S}}{P(\underline{s})p(\underline{x}|\underline{s},\mathrm{H})},

(13)

where
P(\underline{s})
denotes the probability mass function of the input vector and
p(\underline{x}|\underline{s},\mathrm{H})
is defined in (
5
). Using (
3
) and (
13
), the mutual information of the channel for a uniformly distributed discrete input can be expressed as

\displaystyle{I}(\mathbf{\underline{s}};\mathbf{\underline{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}},\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\exp(-\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{\underline{x}}-\mathrm{H}\mathbf{\underline{s}}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}})}{\underset{\underline{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\sum}\exp(-\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{\underline{x}}-\mathrm{H}\underline{s}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}})}\right)\right],

(14)

where the expectations can be estimated numerically.

III
the Mutual information in presence of Channel Estimation Error

We derived the capacity of the dual-polarization channel with the assumption of perfect channel knowledge at the receiver in (
12
).
In this section, we derive a lower bound on the mutual information of the channel in the presence of an estimation error. As already shown in
[
43
]
, when the channel is known, the capacity-achieving distribution is a zero-mean
\mathcal{CN}
input with a power constraint. Thus, keeping the capacity-achieving distribution, i.e.,
\smash{\mathbf{\underline{s}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\Lambda_{\underline{s}})}
seems reasonable for the imperfectly estimated channel as well.
To derive the achievable information rate

, which is a lower bound on the mutual information (
3
) between
\mathbf{\underline{s}}
and
\mathbf{\underline{x}}

[

I(\mathbf{\underline{s}};\mathbf{\underline{x}})\geq I_{q}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{x}},\mathbf{\underline{s}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{q(\mathbf{\underline{x}}|\mathbf{\underline{s}})}{q(\mathbf{\underline{x}})}\right)\right]

(15)

is used, where
q(\cdot)
stands for the probability density function of an auxiliary channel. Note that this inequality holds for an arbitrary distribution of
q(\cdot)
. The mismatched channel law is here assumed to be

q(\underline{x}|\underline{s})=p(\underline{x}|\underline{s},\mathrm{\hat{H}}),

(16)

which is obtained by replacing
\mathrm{H}
in (
5
) with the estimated channel
\mathrm{\hat{H}}

\displaystyle q(\underline{x})=p(\underline{x}|\mathrm{\hat{H}}).

(17)

Also, the covariance matrix of the auxiliary channel’s output is obtained by replacing
\mathrm{H}
with
\mathrm{\hat{H}}
in (
II-B
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}=\hat{\mathrm{H}}\Lambda_{\underline{s}}\hat{\mathrm{H}}^{\dagger}+\sigma_{z}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n}.

(18)

The average achievable information rate when the estimated channel is random can be written as

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{q}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\hat{H}}}[I_{q}]

(19)

In the following, we first derive an achievable information rate for the multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel model with a fixed channel
\mathrm{H}
and estimated channel
\mathrm{\hat{H}}
. Then, we extend the derived achievable information rate to
n
-dimensional unitary channels. By assuming a unitary estimate of the channel (i.e.,
\smash{\mathrm{\hat{H}}\mathrm{\hat{H}}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{I}_{n}}
), a tighter achievable information rate is derived. Finally, we use (

Theorem 1

Consider an arbitrary complex multiple-input multiple-output channel matrix
\mathrm{H}

\displaystyle I_{q}

\displaystyle=\log\left|\mathrm{I}_{n}+\mathrm{\hat{H}}\mathrm{Q}\mathrm{\hat{H}}^{\dagger}\right|-\frac{1}{\ln{2}}\tr\left(\mathrm{Q}\mathrm{E}^{\dagger}\mathrm{E}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\ln{2}}\tr\left(\mathrm{I}_{n}-\Lambda_{\underline{x}}\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}^{-1}\right),

(20)

where the definitions of

,

), and (

Proof:

One may rewrite (

\displaystyle I_{q}

\displaystyle=\overbrace{-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\log q(\mathbf{\underline{x}})\right]}^{A}+\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}},\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\log q(\mathbf{\underline{x}}|\mathbf{\underline{s}})\right]}^{B}.

(21)

Using (
7
) and (
17
), given
\mathrm{H}
, the first term

can be calculated as

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n}|\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}|}\exp\left(-\mathbf{\underline{x}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}^{-1}\mathbf{\underline{x}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left(\pi^{n}|\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}|\right)+\frac{1}{\ln{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\mathbf{\underline{x}}^{\dagger}\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}^{-1}\mathbf{\underline{x}}\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left(\pi^{n}|\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}|\right)+\frac{1}{\ln{2}}\tr\left(\Lambda_{\underline{x}}\hat{\Lambda}_{\underline{x}}^{-1}\right),

(22)

where the last step follows the cyclic permutation rule of the trace. For the second term,

\displaystyle B=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}},\mathbf{\underline{x}}}\left[\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n}\sigma_{z}^{2n}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{\underline{x}}-\mathrm{\hat{H}}\mathbf{\underline{s}}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\left(\pi^{n}\sigma_{z}^{2n}\right)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{z}},\mathbf{\underline{s}}}\left[\left\lVert(\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{\hat{H}})\mathbf{\underline{s}}+\mathbf{\underline{z}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]}{\sigma_{z}^{2}\ln{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\left(\pi^{n}\sigma_{z}^{2n}\right)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{s}}}\left[\left\lVert\mathrm{E}\mathbf{\underline{s}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\underline{z}}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{\underline{z}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]}{\sigma_{z}^{2}\ln{2}}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\left(\pi^{n}\sigma_{z}^{2n}\right)-\frac{\tr\left(\sigma_{z}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n}+\mathrm{E}\Lambda_{\underline{s}}\mathrm{E}^{\dagger}\right)}{\sigma_{z}^{2}\ln{2}}

\displaystyle=

where (

For the sake of keeping Theorem
as general as possible, no assumption is made about the transmitter knowledge of the estimated channel. However, in the context of the optical communications, we are more interested in the case that the transmitter has no channel knowledge. Assume that the channel is unitary (i.e.,
\smash{\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{H_{u}}}

The uniform power distribution

Transmission block model

In this section, first, the well-established least-square error estimation algorithm is presented. Then, a unitary estimation method is proposed for unitary channels. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, to make data-aided channel estimation possible, the first
L
symbols of the transmission block are pilot symbols
\smash{\mathrm{D}=[\underline{s}_{0},...,\underline{s}_{L-1}]}
, which are assumed to be known at both transmitter and receiver. The optimal pilot assignment
\mathrm{D}
should have the following properties
[
45
]
:

•

\smash{L\geq n}

•

\smash{\mathrm{D}\mathrm{D}^{\dagger}=\frac{PL}{n}\mathrm{I}_{n}}

The complex matrix
\smash{\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{0},...,\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{L-1}]}
of received symbols is

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm{D}+\mathbf{Z},

(33)

where
\smash{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n})}
is an
\smash{n\times L}
matrix of i.i.d. noise samples and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{u}}
is an
\smash{n\times n}
random unitary channel matrix, which is assumed to remain constant during a transmission block.

Note that the channel estimation problem can be translated to finding a certain number of independent real values, often regarded as the degrees of freedom and denoted by
\nu
.

IV-A

least-square error Algorithm

Conventional optical transmission systems often use least mean square for a real-time estimate of the channel, because least mean square tracks the channel change with each received symbol. However, in this paper it is assumed that the channel is constant during a transmission block and the least-square error method is well adapted to block based transmissions. The least-square error estimator estimates the channel by minimizing the squared error between the desired and received signal. For a multiple-input multiple-output-additive white Gaussian noise channel, the least-square error optimization problem can be expressed as
[
46
]

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{\hat{H}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{H}}\mathrm{D}\right\rVert^{2}

(34)

Knowing
\mathrm{D}
and
\mathbf{X}
, the solution of (
34
) is
[
46
]
,
[
47
, Ch. 8]

\mathbf{\hat{H}}_{\text{LS}}=\mathbf{X}\mathrm{D}^{\dagger}(\mathrm{D}\mathrm{D}^{\dagger})^{-1}

(35)

However, the pilots are chosen in such a way that
\smash{\mathrm{D}\mathrm{D}^{\dagger}=\frac{PL}{n}\mathrm{I}_{n}}
. Therefore, we can write

\mathbf{\hat{H}}_{\text{LS}}=\frac{n}{PL}\mathbf{X}\mathrm{D}^{\dagger}.

(36)

It can be shown that
\mathrm{R_{E}}
for the LS algorithm is
[
46
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{R_{E}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}\left[\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}\right]=\frac{n\sigma_{z}^{2}}{PL}\mathrm{I}_{n}=\frac{1}{\eta L}\mathrm{I}_{n}

(37)

showing that the estimation error of the least-square error algorithm is inversely proportional to the signal-to-noise ratio and the pilot length
L
.

It is worth mentioning that the least-square error algorithm has to find
\nu=2n^{2}
independent real values to make an estimate.

IV-B

Kabsch Algorithm

The problem with both blind estimation algorithms (e.g., constant modulus algorithm, radially directed equalizer, and multi-modulus algorithm) and pilot-aided estimation algorithms (e.g., least-square error and least mean square) is that their optimization problems are not adopted for unitary channel estimation, making their solutions suboptimal if
\mathbf{H}
is known to be unitary. Thus, in this part, we apply the unitary constraint of the channel to the estimation problem of (
34
) and write

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{\hat{H}}}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{\hat{H}}\mathrm{D}\right\rVert^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{\hat{H}}\mathbf{\hat{H}}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{I}_{n}.

(38)

The optimal solution to this problem is given by the Kabsch algorithm
[
28
]
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{H}}_{\text{Kabsch}}=\mathbf{UV}^{\dagger},

(39)

where
\smash{\mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}}
is the singular value decomposition function of
\mathbf{X}\mathrm{D}^{\dagger}
. As can be seen, unlike the blind conventional estimators, Kabsch is independent of the modulation format of the transmission. The Kabsch algorithm was proposed for optical communication by Louchet
et al.

[
27
]
as a blind polarization tracking algorithm, where decision-directed symbols were used instead of pilots.

In contrast to least-square error, no analytical result is known for
\mathrm{R_{E}}
of the Kabsch algorithm. Although we cannot analytically prove it, we can make an intuitive prediction by considering that
\smash{\nu=n^{2}}
for a unitary estimation of the channel while for a general estimation of the channel
\smash{\nu=2n^{2}}
. Since the channel estimation problem is equivalent to finding
\nu
independent real-valued quantities, one can predict that the estimation error of Kabsch would be half of least-square error. More interestingly, for special unitary channels where
\smash{|\mathrm{H_{u}}|=1}
, the degrees of freedom is
\smash{n^{2}-1}
and the gain by the unitary algorithm can be even higher; however, this gain vanishes for large
n
.

Note that in the case of the dual-polarization channel, the singular value decomposition is deployed only on a two-by-two matrix, making it less computationally complex than for higher
n
.

VI
Conclusion

The capacity of unitary MIMO-AWGN channels and specifically dual-polarization was investigated. With perfect channel knowledge, the capacity of unitary channels is the same as for the regular additive white Gaussian noise channel. An achievable information rate with imperfect channel knowledge was derived and showed that the achievable information rate is highly dependent on the estimation algorithm. In the case of unitary channels, higher achievable information rates are obtained with a unitary estimation of the channel. The bounds are derived for any
\smash{n\geq 2}
dimensions, meaning that the results can be applied to other optical channels. In particular, ?THM?
can be directly applied to space-division multiplexed channels which are impaired with polarization- and mode-dependent loss. In contrast to the conventional estimation algorithms, the proposed data-aided Kabsch algorithm ensures a unitary estimate of the channel. Numerical results showed that for various input distributions, Kabsch outperforms least-square error in terms of achievable information rate. Also, like least-square error, Kabsch can perform very well with only a few pilot symbols, making the transmission rate loss due to the pilots negligible.
