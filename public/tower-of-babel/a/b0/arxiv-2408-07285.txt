DDIM Redux : Mathematical Foundation and Some Extension
Manhyung Han

Abstract
This note provides a critical review of the mathematical concepts underlying the generalized diffusion denoising implicit model (gDDIM) and the exponential integrator (EI) scheme. We present enhanced mathematical results, including an exact expression for the reverse trajectory in the probability flow ODE and an exact expression for the covariance matrix in the gDDIM scheme. Furthermore, we offer an improved understanding of the EI scheme’s efficiency in terms of the change of variables. The noising process in DDIM is analyzed from the perspective of non-equilibrium statistical physics. Additionally, we propose a new scheme for DDIM, called the principal-axis DDIM (paDDIM).

This note reviews the general DPM as a diffusion process governed by two arbitrary matrices,

{\bf f}

{\bf g}

Finally, we propose a potentially useful scheme called the principal-axis DDIM (paDDIM) as a generalization of the well-known plain-vanilla DDIM.

II
Backgrounds

Some Mathematical Discussion

The backbone of diffusion models consists of two steps: forward and reverse diffusion, or noising and de-noising. The forward diffusion takes a data
{\bf x}
in the form of a
d
-dimensional vector and adds noise to it until it becomes a random variable with zero mean. The forward process is described by SDE of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type, or linear SDE:

\displaystyle d{\bf x}={\bf f}(t){\bf x}dt+{\bf g}(t)d{\bf w}.

(1)

The drift term
{\bf f}(t)
and the stochastic term
{\bf g}(t)
are both
d\times d
dimensional, time-dependent but
{\bf x}
-independent matrices, and
d{\bf w}
is the standard Wiener process with each component
w_{i}
of the
d
-dimensional
{\bf w}
vector following the unit normal distribution:
w_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
. The same relation can be expressed in the differential equation form,

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf x}(t)}{dt}={\bf f}(t){\bf x}(t)+{\bf g}(t){\bm{\eta}}(t),

(2)

where
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\rangle=0
and
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.

The initial data
{\bf x}(0)
evolves in the data space according to

\displaystyle{\bf x}(t)=K(t,0){\bf x}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K(t,t^{\prime}){\bf g}(t^{\prime}){\bm{\eta}}(t^{\prime}).

(3)

The transition matrix
K(t,t^{\prime})=U(t)U^{-1}(t^{\prime})
satisfies the composition rule
K(t_{1},t_{2})K(t_{2},t_{3})=K(t_{1},t_{3})
. The evolution matrix
U(t)
is the solution of

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}={\bf f}(t)U(t)

(4)

with the initial condition
U(0)=\mathbf{I}
. See Appendix
A
for derivation of (
3
).

The expression for
{\bf x}(t)
in (
3
) neatly breaks up into the mean
\langle{\bf x}(t)\rangle=K(t,0){\bf x}(0)
and the fluctuation
\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K(t,t^{\prime}){\bf g}(t^{\prime}){\bm{\eta}}(t^{\prime})
. From the latter part we can deduce the covariance of
{\bf x}(t)
as

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K(t,t^{\prime}){\bf D}(t^{\prime})K^{T}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=U(t)\left(\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}U^{-1}(t^{\prime}){\bf D}(t^{\prime})U^{-T}(t^{\prime})\right)U^{T}(t).

(5)

The diffusion matrix
{\bf D}(t)
is defined in terms of the fluctuation matrix
{\bf g}(t)
by

\displaystyle{\bf D}(t)={\bf g}(t){\bf g}^{T}(t).

(6)

The solution for
{\bf x}(t)
in (
2
) suggests that a trajectory that began at
{\bf x}(0)
evolves into the probability distribution for
{\bf x}(t)
at time
t
which is normal:

\displaystyle p({\bf x}(t))={\cal N}\Bigl({\bf x}(t);K(t,0){\bf x}(0),{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)\Bigr).

(7)

At the initial time we have
{\bm{\Sigma}}(0)=0
and the probability reduces to a delta function sharply peaked at
{\bf x}={\bf x}(0)
. The exact score function for the forward process is given accordingly:

\displaystyle{\bm{\nabla}}\log p({\bf x}(t))=-{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}(t)\Bigl({\bf x}(t)-K(t,0){\bf x}(0)\Bigr).

(8)

This summarizes the forward diffusion process governed by the time-dependent, matrix-valued drift term
{\bf f}(t)
and the stochastic term
{\bf g}(t)
, for the trajectory that started at the exactly known location
{\bf x}(0)
.

Exact Solution for the Reverse Trajectory

The corresponding reverse-diffusion process can be formulated as an SDE
Anderson 1982
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Zhang and Chen 2021

\displaystyle d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\left({\bf f}(t){\bf x}-\frac{1}{2}(1+\lambda^{2}){\bf D}(t){\bm{\nabla}}\log p({\bf x}(t))\right)dt+\lambda{\bf g}(t)d{\bf w}

(9)

with a family of
\lambda>0
values and
\log p({\bf x}(t))
given by (
8
). Given the final distribution
p({\bf x}(T))
, (
9
) predicts the same marginal distribution
p({\bf x}(t))
for
0<t<T
regardless of
\lambda>0
. The proof, first given in
Zhang and Chen 2021
, is reproduced in Appendix
B
for completeness.

In particular, the case
\lambda=0
is called the probability flow ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bf x}}{dt}={\bf f}(t){\bf x}+\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}(t){\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}(t)\Bigl({\bf x}-K(t,0){\bf x}(0)\Bigr)

(10)

where we have inserted the exact expression for the score given in (
8
). This equation admits a simple solution once the following change of variables is implemented:

\displaystyle{\bf x}(t)=K(t,0){\bf x}(0)+V(t){\bf y}(t).

(11)

The first term is the mean position at time
t
, and
V(t)
is the matrix that decomposes the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
through

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)=V(t)V^{T}(t).

(12)

The two matrices
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
and
V(t)
are governed respectively by

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{\Sigma}}}{dt}

\displaystyle={\bf D}+{\bf f}{\bm{\Sigma}}+{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf f}^{T}

\displaystyle\frac{dV}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}V^{-T}+{\bf f}V.

(13)

See Appendix
C
for derivation of the two equations.

In general the choice of
V(t)
is not unique as any orthogonal matrix
R(t)
implementing the change
V(t)\rightarrow V(t)R(t)
leaves the decomposition (
12
) intact. The ambiguity is removed by treating
V(t)
as the solution of (
13
) with the initial condition
V(0)=0
. Such
V(t)
satisfying both (
13
) and the correct initial condition is the solution of the integral equation

\displaystyle V(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K(t,t^{\prime}){\bf D}(t^{\prime})V^{-T}(t^{\prime}).

(14)

Differentiating both sides with respect to
t
gives the differential equation for
V(t)
in (
13
), and the correct boundary condition
V(0)=0
follows from the expression (
14
). Alternatively, (
14
) is written

\displaystyle V(t)U^{-1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}U^{-1}(t^{\prime}){\bf D}(t^{\prime})V^{-T}(t^{\prime}).

(15)

This form will become useful when we write down the formula governing the generation process in DPM.

Remarkably, as shown in Appendix
C
,
d{\bf y}/dt=0
and one can write
{\bf y}=\epsilon
as a
d
-dimensional constant vector, admitting an exact solution

\displaystyle{\bf x}(t)=K(t,0){\bf x}(0)+V(t)\epsilon.

(16)

This formula, while formally exact, requires a careful interpretation. It is said that the reverse-diffusion process (
9
) admits the same distribution
p({\bf x}(t))
regardless of
\lambda
, including
\lambda=0
. The deterministic trajectory (
16
) implies the mean
\langle{\bf x}(t)\rangle=K(t,0){\bf x}(0)
and the covariance
V_{i\alpha}(t)V_{j\beta}(t)\langle\epsilon_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\beta}\rangle
which, by definition, must match the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{ij}(t)
. For this relation to work, we must assume the white-noise distribution
\langle\epsilon_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
for
\bf\epsilon
. By treating
{\bf\epsilon}
as a random variable with zero mean and unit covariance, we successfully recover the correct probability distribution for
{\bf x}(t)
. This is one interpretation of the formula (
16
).

The other interpretation of it is as a deterministic path going from a fixed
{\bf x}(t)
to
{\bf x}(0)
in reverse time. In particular, since at large
T
the mean position
\langle{\bf x}(T)\rangle\approx 0
, we can write

\displaystyle{\bf x}(T)\approx V(T){\bf\epsilon}~\rightarrow~{\bf\epsilon}=V^{-1}(T){\bf x}(T).

(17)

To a very good approximation we can write the solution

\displaystyle{\bf x}(t)=U(t)U^{-1}(0){\bf x}(0)+V(t)V^{-1}(T){\bf x}(T).

(18)

We have deliberately written
K(t,0)=U(t)=U(t)U^{-1}(0)
to emphasize the symmetry of the expressions involved. This formula provides a completely general expression for the backward trajectory connecting the initial position
{\bf x}(T)
back to the original position
{\bf x}(0)
in
any diffusion process
characterized by forward SDE (
1
) and backward ODE (
10
), including DDIM as a special case [see Sec.
III
]. The only assumption required for (
18
) to be valid is that a
single position

{\bf x}(0)
be selected to start off the diffusion process.

The formula (
18
) allows for a neat interpretation of the trajectory evolving from
{\bf x}(T)
to
{\bf x}(0)
as a “straight line” connecting the two points, with relative weights given by
U(t)U^{-1}(0)
and
V(t)V^{-1}(T)
, respectively. It is not a straight line in the literal sense of Euclidean geometry because the coefficients multiplying the two extremal positions are matrices rather than numbers.

Exponential Integrator Scheme

Instead of writing the backward trajectory as in (
18
), we can write it in a more general form

\displaystyle{\bf x}(t)

\displaystyle=K(t,t_{0}){\bf x}(t_{0})+\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}K(t,t^{\prime}){\bf D}(t^{\prime})V^{-T}(t^{\prime})\right)\epsilon

\displaystyle=K(t,t_{0}){\bf x}(t_{0})+U(t)\left(V(t)U^{-1}(t)-V(t_{0})U^{-1}(t_{0})\right){\bf\epsilon}

(19)

where
0<t_{0}<T
stands for an
arbitrary
initial time. (See Appendix
D
for proof.) The second line follows from the first by utilizing the defining relation for
V(t)
given in (
15
). This expression is very simple as it no longer involves any integrals (though the original publication
Zhang and Chen 2022
termed it “exponential integrator”), provided that we have prior knowledge of
U(t),V(t)
.

For a small time interval
|t-t_{0}|\ll T
, (
19
) can be used to generate the coordinate
{\bf x}(t)
iteratively from the previous one at
{\bf x}(t_{0})
. Indeed, motivated by the above exact result, (
19
), a scheme
Zhang and Chen 2022
was proposed to generate new data over small time steps, i.e.
t=t_{s-1},t_{0}=t_{s}
, and
t_{s}-t_{s-1}\ll T
:

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{s-1}

\displaystyle=K(t_{s-1},t_{s}){\bf x}_{s}

\displaystyle+U(t_{s-1})\left(V(t_{s-1})U^{-1}(t_{s-1})-V(t_{s})U^{-1}(t_{s})\right){\bf\epsilon}({\bf x}_{s}).

(20)

Instead of constant
\epsilon
, an intricate function
\epsilon({\bf x}_{s})
depending on the data coordinate
{\bf x}_{s}
is assumed. All the quantities on the right-hand side of the equation are a priori known so that the integration procedure can proceed efficiently.

Origin of the Efficiency

The initial state
{\bf x}(0)
evolving according to the SDE (
1
) ends up at
{\bf x}(t)
at time
t
with probability
p({\bf x}(t))={\cal N}({\bf x}(t);K(t,0){\bf x}(0),{\bm{\Sigma}}(t))
. At time
t=0
,
{\bm{\Sigma}}(0)=0
renders a delta function for
p({\bf x}(0))
. The change of variables from
{\bf x}(t)
to
{\bf y}(t)
implied by (
11
) amounts to rewriting this probability distribution to

\displaystyle p({\bf y}(t))

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\bf y}(t);0,\mathbf{I})

(21)

with zero mean and unit covariance. The change of variables has the effect of “smoothing out” whatever irregularities and sharp fluctuations there may have been in
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
, turning it into a unit normal distribution at all times. It then makes sense to keep track of the data evolution in terms of
{\bf y}(t)
rather than
{\bf x}(t)
, and re-express the score accordingly:

\displaystyle{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bf y}\log p({\bf y})=V^{T}{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bf x}\log p({\bf x}).

(22)

The variational function
{\bf\epsilon}({\bf x}_{s})
in (
20
) is nothing but the approximation to the ground-truth score in terms of
{\bf y}
:

\displaystyle{\bf\epsilon}({\bf x})\approx-{\bm{\nabla}}_{\bf y}\log p({\bf y}).

(23)

It was noted
Zhang and Chen 2022
that using
{\bf\epsilon}({\bf x})
rather than the bare score
{\bf s}({\bf x})
led to improved stability in the generation process, precisely because the change of variables involved in going from
{\bf x}
to
{\bf y}
has the effect of removing fluctuations that might be present in
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
.

When It Fails and How to Make It Work Again

The objective of the score function
{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bf x)
in the diffusion model is to minimize the cost function

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\int d{\bf x}_{s}p({\bf x}_{s})||{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x}_{s})-{\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s})||^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\int d{\bf x}_{s}d{\bf x}_{i}p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{i})p({\bf x}_{i})||{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x}_{s})-{\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{i})||^{2}+C.

(24)

The sum
\sum_{s}
is over all intermediate times
1\leq s\leq N
, and the second line follows from Vincent’s identity with a constant
C
which is independent of the hyperparameter
\bm{\theta}

Vincent 2011
.

When the initial distribution is confined to a single point,
{\bf x}_{i}={\bf x}_{0}
, we can write
p({\bf x}_{i})=\delta({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf x}_{0})
, and turn the second line in (
24
) into

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\int d{\bf x}_{s}p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0})||{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x}_{s})-{\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0})||^{2}.

(25)

The conditional probability
p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0})
and its gradient are exactly known - see (
7
) and (
8
) - and we can solve for the score function exactly without having to invoke the hyperparameters
{\bm{\theta}}
.

If instead the initial distribution consists of
n
discrete points
{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)}
, possibly with uneven weights
w_{i}
,

\displaystyle p({\bf x}_{0})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta({\bf x}_{i}-{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)})}{\sum_{i}w_{i}},

(26)

then the cost function becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i}w_{i}\sum_{s}\int d{\bf x}_{s}p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)})||{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x}_{s})-{\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)})||^{2}.

(27)

Though each
{\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)})
for
1\leq i\leq n
is known exactly, no single score function
{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x}_{s})
can fit all of them simultaneously. A reasonable guess for the score function would be

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf x}_{s})={\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log\left(\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)})}{\sum_{i}w_{i}}\right).

(28)

This formula reduces to
{\bf s}({\bf x}_{s})={\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0})
for a single initial point
{\bf x}_{0}
. In the other extreme of having performed an infinite number of sampling over
{\bf x}_{0}
, on the other hand, the expression inside the logarithm becomes
\int d{\bf x}_{0}p({\bf x}_{0})p({\bf x}_{s}|{\bf x}_{0})=p({\bf x}_{s})
, and we recover the ground truth result
{\bf s}({\bf x}_{s})={\bm{\nabla}}_{{\bf x}_{s}}\log p({\bf x}_{s})
. The variational score function (
28
) thus works well in the two extreme cases of a single, and an infinite number of data points, and might work well in between.

III
Understanding DDIM

Definition of DDIM

So far we have placed no constraint on the structure of
{\bf f}(t),{\bf g}(t)
and treated them as arbitrary time-dependent vectors. The DDIM is a subset of diffusion models in which the two matrices are related by

\displaystyle{\bf f}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}{\bf g}(t){\bf g}^{T}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}(t).

(29)

This includes the well-known DDIM parameterized by

\displaystyle{\bf f}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\log\alpha}{dt}\mathbf{I},~~{\bf g}=\sqrt{-\frac{d\log\alpha}{dt}}\mathbf{I},

(30)

which we refer to as the
plain-vanilla
DDIM. The formal results we derive below are, however, valid for general DDIM defined by (
29
).

Derivation of the Covariance Matrix

In DDIM, the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
in (
5
) is exactly given by

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)=\mathbf{I}-U(t)U^{T}(t)

(31)

in terms of the matrix
U(t)
introduced in (
4
). For proof, see Appendix
E
. At long times
U(T)\rightarrow 0
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}(T)\rightarrow\mathbf{I}
.

The positive semi-definite matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
can be decomposed as in (
12
), and together with (
31
) we have

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)=\mathbf{I}-U(t)U^{T}(t)=V(t)V^{T}(t).

(32)

One can find
U(t)
as the solution of
dU/dt={\bf f}U=-({\bf D}/2)U
with
U(0)=\mathbf{I}
, and
V(t)
as the solution of (
14
) with
V(0)=0
. Once
U(t)
and
V(t)
are solved for, (
18
) and (
20
) can be used to generate the backward trajectory for
{\bf x}(t)
.

Plain-vanilla DDIM

The plain-vanilla DDIM given in (
30
) with the monotonic function
\alpha(t)
satisfying
\alpha(0)=1
and
\alpha(T)=0
can be solved for
U(t)
in (
4
):

\displaystyle U(t)=\sqrt{\alpha(t)},~~K(t,t^{\prime})=\sqrt{\alpha(t)/\alpha(t^{\prime})},

(33)

and

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)=1-\alpha(t),~~V(t)=\sqrt{1-\alpha(t)}.

(34)

Well-known generative relations for
{\bf x}_{s}
in DDIM are obtained by feeding
U(t),V(t)
in (
34
) into (
20
):

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf x}_{s-1}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{s-1}}}=\frac{{\bf x}_{s}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}}+\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{s-1}^{-1}-1}-\sqrt{\alpha_{s}^{-1}-1}\right){\bf\epsilon}({\bf x}_{s}).

(35)

IV
Approach to equilibrium in DDIM

The evolution of the data
{\bf x}
through the noising process is an example of an ensemble approaching the equilibrium, and one can understood from the perspective of non-equilibrium statistical physics
Kwon et al. 2005
;
Han et al. 2021
.

At equilibrium
d{\bm{\Sigma}}/dt=0
and (
13
) dictates

{\bf f}{\bm{\Sigma}}+{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf f}^{T}=-{\bf D}
. One can accordingly make the decomposition of the
{\bf f}{\bm{\Sigma}}
matrix into symmetric and anti-symmetric parts:

\displaystyle{\bf f}{\bm{\Sigma}}=-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}-{\bf Q},~~{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf f}^{T}=-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}+{\bf Q}.

(36)

The anti-symmetric part
{\bf Q}^{T}=-{\bf Q}
is determined by solving
Kwon et al. 2005

\displaystyle{\bf f}{\bf Q}+{\bf Q}{\bf f}^{T}=\frac{1}{2}({\bf f}{\bf D}-{\bf D}{\bf f}^{T}).

(37)

Once
{\bf Q}
is known, one can relate the
\bf f
matrix to the covariance matrix by

\displaystyle{\bf f}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}+{\bf Q}\right){\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}.

(38)

The probability current governing the Fokker-Planck equation
\partial_{t}\rho+{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf J}=0
is

\displaystyle{\bf J}=\rho{\bf f}{\bf x}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}\rho=\rho\left({\bf f}{\bf x}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}\log p\right).

(39)

Inserting
{\bf f}=-{\bf D}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}/2
gives
{\bf J}=0
provided the distribution is Boltzmann-like:
p({\bf x})\propto\exp(-{\bf x}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bf x}/2)
. When
{\bf Q}\neq 0
, the same equilibrium distribution holds, but now there is nonzero steady-state current

\displaystyle{\bf J}^{c}=-{\bf Q}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bf x}\rho({\bf x})={\bf Q}{\bm{\nabla}}\rho({\bf x}).

(40)

Though this current is nonzero even at equilibrium, it is divergence-free, i.e.
\partial_{i}J_{i}^{c}=Q_{ik}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\rho=0
, and represents a
circulating current
that does not affect the continuity equation. From the perspective of convergence to the steady state, nonzero
{\bf J}^{c}
means a particle may be trapped in a circulating pattern about the equilibrium position
{\bf x}=0
without being able to reach it. For efficient equilibration, it will be desirable if the circulating current was absent:
{\bf J}^{c}=0
.

When
{\bf f}
is a function of
{\bf D}
as in DDIM, the term on the right side of (
37
) vanishes, hence
{\bf Q}=0
and
{\bf f}{\bm{\Sigma}}=-{\bf D}/2
. In particular,
{\bf f}=-{\bf D}/2
gives

Appendix B
Proof of (

We give a self-contained discussion of the Fokker-Planck (forward Kolmogorov) equation and the reverse-time Kolmogorov equation. Anderson’s formula for reverse diffusion is derived, along with the generalization made in
Zhang and Chen 2021
. For this section we write the forward diffusion process as

\displaystyle d{\bf x}={\bf f}dt+{\bf g}d{\bf w}

(59)

without assuming the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck form for the drift
{\bf f}
.

The stochastic evolution of the trajectory
{\bf x}
according to (
59
) results in the conditional probability

\displaystyle p({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\bf x}_{t};{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t}+{\bf f}(t)\Delta t,{\bf D}(t)\Delta t)

between a pair of trajectories
({\bf x}_{t-\Delta t},{\bf x}_{t})
at closed spaced times
t
and
t-\Delta t
, and
{\bf D}={\bf g}{\bf g}^{T}
. The distribution
p({\bf x}_{t},t)
at time
t
follows

\displaystyle p({\bf x}_{t},t)=\int d{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t}p({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t})p({\bf x}_{t-\Delta t},t-\Delta t).

(60)

Explicit time dependence of the probability
p({\bf x}_{t},t)
follows from the time-varying
{\bf f}(t)
and
{\bf g}(t)
in the SDE. The integration over
{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t}
will be performed after making the change of variables to
{\bf y}={\bf x}_{t-\Delta t}+{\bf f}\Delta t-{\bf x}_{t}
, which results in a new integration measure
d{\bf y}=[1+({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf f})\Delta t]d{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t}
, or

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t-\Delta t}=[1-({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf f})\Delta t]d{\bf y}.

With the new variable
{\bf y}
, (
60
) becomes

\displaystyle p({\bf x}_{t},t)

\displaystyle=[1-({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf f})\Delta t]

\displaystyle~~\times\int d{\bf y}{\cal N}({\bf y};0,{\bf D}\Delta t)p({\bf x}_{t}+{\bf y}-{\bf f}\Delta t,t-\Delta t).

The Gaussian kernel is narrowly peaked around
{\bf y}=0
, and in the
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
limit one can expand the probability function inside the integral up to first order in
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle p({\bf x}+{\bf y}-{\bf f}\Delta t,t-\Delta t)

\displaystyle\approx p({\bf x},t)-[({\bf f}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}})p+\partial_{t}p]\Delta t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}y_{i}y_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}.

(61)

Terms that are linear in
{\bf y}
and vanish after integration are not shown. Replacing
y_{i}y_{j}
by its average
\langle y_{i}y_{j}\rangle=D_{ij}(t)\Delta t
gives

\displaystyle p=[1-({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf f})\Delta t]\left[p-(\partial_{t}p+{\bf f}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}p)\Delta t+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf D}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}p\right]

where
p=p({\bf x}_{t},t)
, and
{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf D}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}=D_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}
. It is assumed that the diffusion matrix
{\bf D}
depends on time but not on the coordinate
{\bf x}
. Collecting terms of order
\Delta t
and setting them to zero,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p+({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf f})p+({\bf f}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}})p-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf D}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}p=0~~\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p+{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot\left(p{\bf f}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}p\right)=0.

(62)

This is a continuity equation for the probability density
p
known as the Fokker-Planck equation, with the current density
{\bf J}
given by

\displaystyle{\bf J}=p{\bf f}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}p=p\left({\bf f}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}\log p\right).

(63)

Physically, the probability current consists of the drift term
p{\bf f}
and the diffusive term
-p{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}\log p/2
.

The probability
p({\bf x},t)
and the transition probability
p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})
are related by

\displaystyle p({\bf x},t)=\int d{\bf x}_{i}p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})p({\bf x}_{i},t_{i}).

(64)

The transition probability
p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})
obeys the same Fokker-Planck equation as the probability
p({\bf x},t)
itself, with respect to
({\bf x},t)
.

The differential equation for the initial time and position
({\bf x}_{i},t_{i})
of the transition probability known as the reverse Kolmogorov equation can be derived as well. One starts off with the composition rule:

\displaystyle\int d{\bf x}p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x},t)p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})=p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i}).

(65)

Differentiating it with respect to
t
gives

\displaystyle\int d{\bf x}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x},t))\right)p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})

\displaystyle+\int d{\bf x}p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x},t)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})\right)=0.

(66)

We can replace
\partial_{t}p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})
by
-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot(p{\bf f}-{\bf D}{\bm{\nabla}}p/2)
in the second integral, and perform integration by parts to rewrite (
66

\displaystyle\int d{\bf x}\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x},t)+({\bf f}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}})p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x},t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}({\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf D}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}})p({\bf x}_{f},t_{f}|{\bf x},t)\Bigr]p({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{i},t_{i})=0.

(67)

The expression inside the bracket must vanish, yielding the backward Kolmogorov equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}p(x_{2}|x_{1})=

\displaystyle-{\bf f}(x_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{2}|x_{1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}({\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot{\bf D}(t_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1})p(x_{2}|x_{1}),

(68)

where we have expressed the space and time coordinates by
x=({\bf x},t)
, and
t_{2}>t_{1}
.

Now we are prepared to derive Anderson’s reverse diffusion equation and its generalization. The probability distribution

itself follows the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}p(x_{1})=

\displaystyle-{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot\left({\bf f}(x_{1})p(x_{1})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot{\bf D}(t_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{1}).

(69)

Multiplying

to obtain the joint probability

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}p(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-p(x_{1}){\bf f}(x_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{2}|x_{1})-\frac{1}{2}p(x_{1}){\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot{\bf D}(t_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{2}|x_{1})-p(x_{2}|x_{1}){\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot\left({\bf f}(x_{1})p(x_{1})\right)+\frac{1}{2}p(x_{2}|x_{1}){\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot{\bf D}(t_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{1})

\displaystyle-{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot\left({\bf f}(x_{1})p(x_{2}|x_{1})p(x_{1})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[p(x_{2}|x_{1}){\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot{\bf D}(t_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{1})-p(x_{1}){\bm{\nabla}}_{1}\cdot{\bf D}(t_{1})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}_{1}p(x_{2}|x_{1})\Bigr].

We replace

\displaystyle-p(x_{1})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\frac{p(x_{1},x_{2})}{p(x_{1})}=

in DDIM. This implies

\displaystyle\frac{dU^{-1}}{dt}=\frac{1}{2}U^{-1}{\bf D},\quad\frac{dU^{-T}}{dt}=\frac{1}{2}{\bf D}U^{-T}.

(86)

Then the term inside the parenthesis of (
5
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}U^{-1}(t^{\prime}){\bf D}(t^{\prime})U^{-T}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\frac{dU^{-1}(t^{\prime})}{dt^{\prime}}U^{-T}(t^{\prime})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}U^{-1}(t^{\prime})\frac{dU^{-T}(t^{\prime})}{dt^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}\left[U^{-1}(t^{\prime})U^{-T}(t^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=U^{-1}(t)U^{-T}(t)-1,

(87)

yielding (
31
).

Appendix F
Covariance matrix in the rotating basis

If we could ignore the rotation of the basis vectors,
du_{\alpha\beta}/dt=-(1/2)\lambda_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}
is solved readily by

\displaystyle u^{0}_{\alpha\beta}=u^{0}_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha\beta},~~u_{\alpha}^{0}(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\lambda_{\alpha}(t^{\prime})\right).

(88)

The initial condition
U(0)=1
is translated into
u^{0}_{\alpha}(0)=1
for the coefficients, since the completeness condition of the basis vectors guarantees

\displaystyle U(0)=\sum_{\alpha}{\bf d}_{\alpha}(0){\bf d}^{T}_{\alpha}(0)=1.

(89)

The zeroth-order solution is incomplete due to the rotation of the basis vectors over time, and one must write the solution in the form
u_{\alpha\beta}(t)=u_{\alpha\beta}^{0}(t)+u_{\alpha\beta}^{1}(t)
with the zeroth-order solution given already in (
88
). The perturbative part
u_{\alpha\beta}^{1}(t)
is obtained by rewriting (
43
) in terms of
u^{0}
and
u^{1}
, and then isolating terms that occur at the same level of perturbation:

\displaystyle\frac{du^{1}_{\alpha\beta}}{dt}=(u^{0}_{\beta}-u^{0}_{\alpha})e_{\alpha\beta}.

(90)

Its solution is

\displaystyle u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}(u^{0}_{\beta}(t^{\prime})-u^{0}_{\alpha}(t^{\prime}))e_{\alpha\beta}(t^{\prime})=u^{1}_{\beta\alpha}(t)

(91)

and satisfies the correct initial condition
u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(0)=0
. The
U
matrix is symmetric at this level of approximation since
u_{\alpha\beta}=u^{0}_{\alpha\beta}+u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}=u_{\beta\alpha}
.

Using
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)=1-U(t)U^{T}(t)=1-U^{2}(t)
, matrix elements of
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t)
are

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\left(1-(u_{\alpha}^{0}(t))^{2}\right)\delta_{\alpha\beta}-(u^{0}_{\alpha}(t)+u^{0}_{\beta}(t))u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(t),

(92)

hence

\displaystyle({\bf D}{\bm{\Sigma}})_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda_{\alpha}(1-(u^{0}_{\alpha})^{2})\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\lambda_{\alpha}(u^{0}_{\alpha}+u^{0}_{\beta})u^{1}_{\alpha\beta},

\displaystyle({\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf D})_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda_{\alpha}(1-(u^{0}_{\alpha})^{2})\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}(u^{0}_{\alpha}+u^{0}_{\beta})u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}.

(93)

The anti-symmetric part

\displaystyle({\bf D}{\bm{\Sigma}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf D})_{\alpha\beta}=(\lambda_{\beta}-\lambda_{\alpha})(u^{0}_{\alpha}+u^{0}_{\beta})u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}

(94)

arises as a result of the rotation of the basis vectors. Without the rotation
{\bf D}{\bm{\Sigma}}
remains symmetric at all times.

\displaystyle({\bm{\Sigma}})_{\alpha\beta}(t)\approx\left(1-(u_{\alpha}^{0}(t))^{2}\right)\delta_{\alpha\beta}-(u^{0}_{\alpha}(t)+u^{0}_{\beta}(t))u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(t),

(95)

\displaystyle({\bm{\Sigma}})_{\alpha\beta}(t)\approx\left(1-(u_{\alpha}^{0}(t))^{2}\right)\delta_{\alpha\beta}-(u^{0}_{\alpha}(t)+u^{0}_{\beta}(t))u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(t),

(96)

and

\displaystyle({\bf D}{\bm{\Sigma}})_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda_{\alpha}(1-(u^{0}_{\alpha})^{2})\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\lambda_{\alpha}(u^{0}_{\alpha}+u^{0}_{\beta})u^{1}_{\alpha\beta},

\displaystyle({\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf D})_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda_{\alpha}(1-(u^{0}_{\alpha})^{2})\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\lambda_{\beta}(u^{0}_{\alpha}+u^{0}_{\beta})u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}

(97)

where

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}^{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\lambda_{\alpha}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle u^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}(u^{0}_{\beta}(t^{\prime})-u^{0}_{\alpha}(t^{\prime}))e_{\alpha\beta}(t^{\prime})=u^{1}_{\beta\alpha}(t).

(98)

Indeed, the anti-symmetric part is nonzero,

\displaystyle({\bf D}{\bm{\Sigma}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bf D})_{\alpha\beta}=(\lambda_{\beta}-\lambda_{\alpha})(u^{0}_{\alpha}+u^{0}_{\beta})u^{1}_{\alpha\beta},

(99)

and arises as a result of the rotation of the basis vectors.
