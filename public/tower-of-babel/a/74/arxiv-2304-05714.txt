Norm of matrix-valued polynomials in random unitaries and permutations
Charles Bordenave, Benoît Collins

Abstract
We consider a non-commutative polynomial in several independent N N -dimensional random unitary matrices, uniformly distributed over the unitary, orthogonal or symmetric groups, and assume that the coefficients are n n -dimensional matrices. The main purpose of this paper is to study the operator norm of this random non-commutative polynomial. We compare it with its counterpart, where the random unitary matrices are replaced by the unitary generators of the free group von Neumann algebra. Our first result concerns the upper bound of the sequence: it is bounded above by an arbitrarily small inflation of its free counterpart with an overwhelming probability in the large N N limit, and this estimate is uniform over all matrix coefficients as long as n ≤ exp ⁡ ( N α ) n\leq\exp(N^{\alpha}) for some explicit α > 0 \alpha>0 . Such results had been obtained by very different techniques for various regimes, all falling in the category n ≪ N n\ll N . Our result provides a new proof of the Peterson-Thom conjecture. Our second result is a universal quantitative lower bound for the operator norm of polynomials in independent N N -dimensional random unitary and permutation matrices with coefficients in an arbitrary C ∗ C^{*} -algebra. A variant of this result for permutation matrices generalizes the Alon-Boppana lower bound in two directions: firstly, it applies to arbitrary polynomials and not only linear polynomials, and secondly, it applies to coefficients of an arbitrary C ∗ C^{*} -algebra with non-negative joint moments and not only for non-negative real numbers.

1.1
Motivation

Fix an integer
d\geq 1
and consider a sequence of
d

(X_{N,1},\ldots X_{N,d})

is stable by conjugation, i.e.,
X_{i}^{*}\in X
for all

. To understand the algebra generated by these matrices
in the large
N
limit, it is necessary
to understand for any non-commutative polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{F}_{d}]
, the operator norm of

P_{N}=P(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}),

(1)

as the dimension
N
goes to infinity (here and below

d

In operator algebraic random matrix theory, one says that
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})

-algebra

Thanks to a linearization trick, establishing the strong convergence of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
is
equivalent to proving that for any integer

(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})
in
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, setting
X_{0}=1_{N}
, the operator norm of

A_{N}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}\otimes X_{i},

(2)

converges toward the operator norm of

[

[

Establishing strong convergence in its form (

-algebra

To our knowledge, no known non-trivial examples of strongly convergent deterministic sequences of matrices exist. Toward the objective of reducing the role of randomness and capturing some ‘intrinsic freeness’ (to quote

]

,

,

Let us give a partial account on this body of works for the matrix

in (

From this list of recent results on independent GUE matrices and Haar unitaries of dimension

, one might guess that

in

! However, Pisier also shows that for any

cannot be smaller that

for a carefully designed family of matrices
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})
of dimension

. Hence from this perspective, the regime

with

is the pertinent scaling.

The first objective of this paper is to fill this huge gap between the regimes
n=O(N)
where strong asymptotic freeness has been proven for very specific choices of
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})
and the regime

identified by Pisier. Theorem
1.1
proves strong asymptotic freeness in a very quantitative way for independent Haar unitaries for all
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})
in

with

and an explicit constant

. Among other consequences, this result gives an alternative proof of Hayes criterion
[
36
]
for the Peterson-Thom conjecture, see Subsection
9.3
. In the more challenging case of independent random permutations, Theorem
1.4
proves a similar quantitative result when

which is already much larger than what was available for GUE or Haar unitary matrices. This result gives the first quantitative version of the main result in
[
13
]
and has an interesting consequence for random actions of the Cartesian product of free groups on finite sets, see Subsection
9.3
.

The second objective of this paper is to provide also matching lower bounds of the form
\|A_{N}\|\geq(1-o(1))\|A\|
. As pointed out in
[
5
]
, even if the community has, with reason, focused on upper bounds on

, it is not straightforward to prove lower bounds when
n,N
goes to infinity. Our main finding is that there is a universal lower bound of the form

which is uniform in

, see Theorem
1.8
. As we shall see, this is ultimately a consequence of the exactness of the free group reduced

-algebra. There is, thus, a sharp contrast between lower and upper bounds in this perspective. In the specific case of permutations, this lower bound can be interpreted as a universal Alon-Boppana lower bound, see Theorem
1.7
, which is of independent interest.

Finally, in Section
8
, we will also explain how to transfer all these estimates on
\|A_{N}\|
into estimates on
\|P_{N}\|
with

as in (
1
) by a quantitative version of the linearization trick. We also recall how we can easily deduce from this the convergence of the spectrum of

to the spectrum of
P
quantitatively when

and

are self-adjoint.

1.2
Models and main results

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
be unital
C^{*}
-algebras equipped with faithful normal states
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
. We denote by
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
the minimal tensor product of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. The state
\tau=\tau_{1}\otimes\tau_{2}
is a faithful normal state on
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

We are mainly interested in two specific examples for
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. The first is
\mathcal{A}_{2}=M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
,
N\geq 1
integer and
\tau_{2}={\rm tr}_{N}
is the normalized trace
{\rm tr}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}{\rm Tr}
. The second example is
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{{\mathrm{F}}}
the left group algebra of the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}
with free generators
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
and their inverses. The algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
is the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the convolution operators
\lambda(g)
on
\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d})
defined for
g,h\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
by

\lambda(g)\delta_{h}=\delta_{gh},

where
\delta_{g}
is the unit coordinate vector at
g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
. Note that
\lambda(g)
is unitary and
\lambda(g^{-1})=\lambda(g)^{*}
. For integer
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}=\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we set
i^{*}=i+d
and for
d+1\leq i\leq 2d
, we set
i^{*}=i-d
and
g_{i}=g_{i-d}^{-1}
. By construction,
i\mapsto i^{*}
is an involution of
{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
.

In
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
, we define

A_{\mathrm{F}}=a_{0}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes\lambda(g_{i}),

(3)

with
a_{0},a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
for all
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
. The operator
A_{\mathrm{F}}
is self-adjoint if the
a_{i}
’s are such that for all
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
,

a_{i}^{*}=a_{i^{*}}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad a_{0}^{*}=a_{0}.

(4)

Next, for any integer
N\geq 1
, let
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
be independent and Haar-distributed elements on
\mathrm{U}_{N}
or
\mathrm{O}_{N}
, we define the operator in
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
,

A_{N}=a_{0}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes U_{i},

(5)

where for
d+1\leq i\leq 2d
, we have set
U_{i}=U_{i^{*}}^{*}
and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
are as above. Under the condition (
4
), the operator
A_{N}
is self-adjoint. We will also consider below the case when
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
are independent uniform permutation matrices of size
N
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the central objective of this paper is to establish that, under rather weak conditions, on
\mathcal{A}_{1}
, the norm of

is close to the norm of

\|T\|\leq(nN)^{1/p}\|T\|_{p}.

(6)

\|A_{N}\Pi_{N}\|\geq\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|{{\left(1-c\frac{\log(2d)}{\sqrt{\log N}}\right)}}.

For Haar unitaries, we will prove the following universal lower bound.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{2d})

\mathcal{A}_{1}
and

,

be as in (

We note that the random variable

Comparing this manuscript with
[
13
,
14
]

This paper is a continuation of a joint effort of the authors to understand the operator norm of matrix models build with independent unitary matrices in large dimension through non-backtracking techniques.
While some results of this paper solve important problems in operator algebras (the Peterson Thom conjecture and Pisier’s exponential estimates), as well as in graph theory (a universal Alon Boppana bound), we stress that from the technological point of view, this paper is not an incremental refinement of
[
13
,
14
]
. Rather, many new theoretical tools are developed, mostly on the operator algebraic side, which we enumerate below:

1.

The non-backtracking equation (see section
3
) is completely new. Unlike the previous versions, it is completely combinatorial and relies on little to no complex analysis, and therefore allows for very efficient estimates.

2.

A new tensor version of a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is needed to overcome problems intrinsic to the high non-commutativity of high dimensional coefficients.

3.

A completely new summation method is introduced to control the moments

4.

A new effective version of the linearization trick is introduced.

5.

An effective operator theoretic characterization of exactness in the case of the reduced group
C*
-algebra is introduced and used in a crucial way to ensure control on the lower bound of the matrix models.

We expect that these new tools will again prove useful in subsequent studies of the spectrum of matrices with tensor structure

2
Overview of the proofs for the upper bounds

2.1
Overview of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3

By a standard linearization trick, it is sufficient to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.3
under the symmetry assumption (
4
). Under this assumption,
A_{N}
and
A_{\mathrm{F}}
are self-adjoint and thus for any even integer
\ell\geq 2
,

(\mathbf{E}\|A_{N}\|_{\ell})^{\ell}\leq\mathbf{E}(\|A_{N}\|^{\ell}_{\ell})=\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(A_{N}^{\ell}\right)}}.

The goal is then to show that for
\ell
as large as possible,

\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(A_{N}^{\ell}\right)}}\leq(1+o(1))^{\ell}\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|^{\ell},

(8)

where
o(1)
is an explicit vanishing bound as
N\to\infty
. This is basically the expected high trace method introduced by Fűredi and Komlós
[
27
]
. For lighter notation, we now remove the explicit dependency in
N
and write
A
instead of
A_{N}
.

Step 1: a new non-backtracking decomposition.

Non-backtracking operators have proven to be a very powerful tool in conjunction with the expected trace method; see
[
30
,
57
,
26
,
10
,
16
,
31
,
12
]
to cite a few related references. Roughly speaking, non-backtracking operators can be thought as discrete geodesic flows and, as in Selberg’s trace formulas, there are spectral correspondences between diffusive operators and non-backtracking operators. This correspondence is usually called Ihara-Bass identity. Operator-valued non-backtracking operators were introduced in
[
13
,
14
]
to study operators of the form (
5
). These operators have, however, some important drawbacks: they are not self-adjoint, the spectral correspondence is established only for
\mathcal{A}_{1}=M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and it is difficult to extract quantitative information from this correspondence.

In Section
3
of the present paper, we overcome these limitations and introduce a new family of non-backtracking operators with two integer parameters
B^{(\ell,m)}
which are self-adjoint and such that

A^{\ell}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}B^{(\ell,m)}.

(9)

The parameter
m
encodes the number of non-backtracking steps. Indeed, specified to the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}
and on the operator
A_{\mathrm{F}}
defined in (
3
) this decomposition simply amounts to writing

A_{\mathrm{F}}^{\ell}=\sum_{g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}a(\ell,g)\otimes\lambda(g)=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}{{\left(\sum_{g\in S_{m}}a(\ell,g)\otimes\lambda(g)\right)}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}B_{\mathrm{F}}^{(\ell,m)},

(10)

where
a(\ell,g)\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
,
S_{0}=\{\o\}
is the unit of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
, and for
m\geq 1
,
S_{m}
is the set of elements
g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
written in reduced form as
g=g_{i_{m}}g_{i_{m-1}}\cdots g_{i_{1}}
with
i_{t+1}^{*}\neq i_{t}
(that is the set of elements at word distance exactly
m
from the unit). Viewing
A_{\mathrm{F}}
as an
\mathcal{A}_{1}
-operator-valued operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d})
,
a(\ell,g)
is equal to the entry
(g,\o)
of
A_{\mathrm{F}}^{\ell}
.

As a byproduct, using the linearity of the trace and the expectation, we find in (
8
),

\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(A^{\ell}\right)}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(B^{(\ell,m)}\right)}}.

(11)

We have thus reduced our task to controlling from above the trace of an operator-valued self-adjoint non-backtracking operator.

Step 2: expected high trace method.

In Section
5
, after expanding the trace
\tau=\tau_{1}\otimes{\rm tr}_{N}
of
B^{(\ell,m)}

\tau=\tau_{1}\otimes{\rm tr}_{N}
over
{\rm tr}_{N}
, and some manipulations, we encounter the expression, for
1\leq m\leq\ell
,

\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(B^{(\ell,m)}\right)}}=\frac{1}{N}\tau_{1}{{\left(\sum_{\gamma=(g,x)}a(\ell,g)w(\gamma)\right)}},

where the sum is over
\gamma=(g,x)\in S_{m}\times{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[N\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{m+1}
,
a(\ell,g)\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
as in (
10
) and, if
g=(g_{i_{m}}g_{i_{m-1}}\cdots g_{i_{1}})
and
x=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})
,
x_{0}=x_{m}
,

w(\gamma)=\mathbf{E}\prod_{t=1}^{m}(U_{i_{t}})_{x_{t-1},x_{t}}

is an expected product of entries of the random unitary matrices. An element
\gamma
can be thought of as a closed non-backtracking path.

We partition the elements
\gamma
in terms of equivalence classes
\pi
on which
w(\gamma)
is constant.
Calling
p(\pi)
this common value, we get the upper bound:

\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(B^{(\ell,m)}\right)}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\pi}|p(\pi)|{{\left\|\sum_{\gamma\in\pi}a(\ell,g)\right\|}},

where
{{\left\|\cdot\right\|}}
is the norm on
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and the first sum is over all equivalence classes
\pi
.
The probabilistic weight
w(\gamma)
is estimated using Weingarten calculus. This work was already done in
[
14
]
. Our central technical contribution (Lemma
5.9
) is an upper bound of the form for some explicit multiplicative factor
L(\pi)
,

{{\left\|\sum_{\gamma\in\pi}a(\ell,g)\right\|}}\leq L(\pi)\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|^{\ell}.

(12)

We have thus obtained the upper bound

\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left(B^{(\ell,m)}\right)}}\leq{{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\pi}|p(\pi)|L(\pi)\right)}}\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|^{\ell}.

We are then able to get an estimate of the form (
8
). This final estimate depends on the number of equivalence classes with a given upper bound on
L(\pi)
and
p(\pi)
. In all these bounds, it is very useful to see the elements
\gamma=(g,x)
as paths of length
m
on the graph spanned by the vertices
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})
and where the edges are decorated by the generators of the free group.

The proof of (
12
) differs very significantly from previous related lemmas established in
[
13
,
14
]
. The major difference is that in these references,
the quantity to be controlled is

\sum_{\gamma\in\pi}{{\left\|a(\ell,g)\right\|}}.

With the norm inside the sum, this last expression can be much larger than the left-hand side of (

). For example, if
\mathcal{A}_{1}=M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, there could be a factor as worth as
\sqrt{n}
between the two expressions. This turns out to be crucial. By keeping the norm outside the sum in (

C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Step 3: a non-commutative Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

At the core of the proof of (
12
) there is a non-commutative inequality proven in Section
4
. This inequality is a non-commutative version of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality written as:

{{\left|\sum_{\vec{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[n\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{k}}x_{\vec{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}y_{i,j_{i}}z_{i,j_{i}}\right|}}\leq\max_{\vec{j}}|x_{\vec{j}}|\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\sum_{j}|y_{i,j}|^{2}\cdot\sum_{j}|z_{i,j}|^{2}},

(13)

with

in
\mathbb{C}
. In Section
4
, the variables
y_{i,j}
,

are now in an arbitrary
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
, the variables

are replaced by a finite collection
(x_{1,\vec{j}},\ldots,x_{l,\vec{j}})
of elements in
\mathcal{A}
, we take a product of all these elements according to some ordering, and finally we replace
{{\left|\cdot\right|}}
by a norm. Under an assumption on the variables

and their positions in the product, we prove in Theorem
4.1
that the analog of (
13
) holds. In Subsection
4.2
, we identify situations where a form of (

. This improvement will ultimately lead to the improvement given by Theorem
1.3
on Theorem

when the

Interestingly, this non-commutative Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies the non-commutative Kintchine inequality due to Lust-Piquard and Pisier
[
43
]
with the optimal constant found by Buchholz
[
20
]
.

2.2
Overview of Theorem
1.4

The proof of Theorem
1.4
follows
the same strategy as the proof of Theorem
1.1
. There is however the well-known caveat that
\mathbf{E}\|A_{N}\Pi_{N}\|_{p}^{p}
cannot be
(1+o(1))^{p}
close to

for

even when

(at least for non-trivial choices of the

’s). This obstruction is due to what Friedman
[
29
]
called

. We refer to
[
13
, Subsection 4.1]
for a more detailed discussion. To overcome this difficulty, we follow a strategy similar to the one developed among others, in

[
44
,
16
,
11
,
13
]
and then combine it to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let us describe the steps of proof more precisely. We remove the dependency in
N
and set
\Pi=\Pi_{N}
.

Step 1: Powers of non-backtracking decompositions.

We may assume that the symmetry condition (
4
) holds. We compute an estimate on
\|A\Pi\|_{p}
by composing
p/\ell
steps of
\ell
: using (
9
) and
\Pi A\Pi=A\Pi=\Pi A
, we get for integers
\ell,q\geq 1
,
q
even,

\|A\Pi\|_{\ell q}^{\ell}=\|A^{\ell}\Pi\|_{q}^{\ell}={{\left\|\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}B^{(\ell,m)}\Pi\right\|}}_{q}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\|B^{(\ell,m)}\Pi\|_{q}.

The parameter
\ell
will be of order
\log_{2d-1}N
and

as large as
\log N/(\log d\log\log N)
. The goal is then to upper-bound individually each
\|B^{(\ell,m)}\Pi\|_{q}
.
In contrast to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we consider the powers of the non-backtracking operators here.

Step 2: remove the tangles.

In Subsection
6.2
, the next step is to identify an operator
\widetilde{B}^{(\ell,m)}
which coincides with
B^{(\ell,m)}
on an event
E
with high probability and such that
\widetilde{B}^{(\ell,m)}
when written as a sum over non-backtracking paths of length

cancels on paths which visits more than one cycle (tangle-free paths). To enjoy such property
m\leq\ell
cannot be of size larger than
O(\log_{2d-1}(N))
.

Step 3: orthogonal projection.

We then compute an expression for
\widetilde{B}^{(\ell,m)}\Pi
in terms of the centered matrices

\underline{U}_{i}=U_{i}-\mathbf{E}U_{i}=U_{i}-1\!\!{\sf I}_{N}\otimes 1\!\!{\sf I}_{N}.

This is straightforward for

, but since we have removed the tangled paths in

, this is now more delicate to do it efficiently. Nevertheless, this step proves that
\widetilde{B}^{(\ell,m)}\Pi
is close to the operator that we would have obtained from

after removing the tangled paths (even though the formal operations “removing the tangles” and “projecting by
\Pi
” do not commute). This is achieved with Lemma
6.4
in Subsection
6.2
. A similar step was already done in
[
44
,
16
,
11
,
13
]
.

Step 4: expected high trace methods.

Up to an extra control of some remainder terms, we are now
in a position to apply the strategy of proof developed for Theorem
1.1
and compute an upper bound for
\mathbf{E}[\|\widetilde{B}^{(\ell,m)}\Pi\|_{q}^{q}]
. The necessary estimates on random permutations were already available from
[
11
,
13
]
.

Similarly, the first identity is proved by decomposing a path of length

to

along its last passage times at

,

, … . Assuming

for

∎

We conclude this section with a bound on the norm of

in terms of the norm

in the self-adjoint case.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that the symmetry condition (

) holds. Then, for any integer

,

and

, we have

\|(C_{\mathrm{F}}^{(k,i)})_{g\o}\|\leq\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|^{k}\and\|((C_{\mathrm{F}}^{(k,i)})^{*}C_{\mathrm{F}}^{(k,i)})_{\o\o}\|\leq\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|^{2k}.

Moreover,

\|C_{\mathrm{F}}^{(k,i)}\|\leq k\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|^{k}.

Proof.

We remove the subscript
{\mathrm{F}}
for ease of notation. We start from the first inequality. We may assume
g\in V_{i}
otherwise this is trivial since
C^{(k,i)}_{g\o}=0
. By Lemma
3.2
, since the operator norm is sub-multiplicative, we have for

,

\|C^{(k,i)}_{g\o}\|\leq\|((A^{\o})^{k-1})_{gg_{i}}\|\|a_{i}\|\leq\|A^{\o}\|^{k-1}\|a_{i}\|.

Next, since
A
and
A^{\o}
are self-adjoint and
A^{\o}
is the restriction of

to

, we also have

. The bound

follows.

Similarly, from Lemma
3.2
,

((C^{(k,i)})^{*}C^{(k,i)})_{\o\o}=\sum_{g\in F_{d}}((C^{(k,i)})^{*})_{\o g}C^{(k,i)}_{g\o}=\sum_{g\in V_{i}}a_{i}^{*}((A^{\o})^{k-1})^{*}_{gg_{i}}((A^{\o})^{k-1})_{gg_{i}}a_{i},

where we have use we have use that

for

.
Hence,

\displaystyle\|((C^{(k,i)})^{*}C^{(k,i)})_{\o\o}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|a_{i}\|^{2}\|\sum_{g\in V_{i}}((A^{\o})^{k-1})^{*}_{gg_{i}}((A^{\o})^{k-1})_{gg_{i}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|a_{i}\|^{2}\|(((A^{\o})^{k-1})^{*}(A^{\o})^{k-1})_{g_{i}g_{i}}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|a_{i}\|^{2}\|A^{\o}\|^{2(k-1)}.

Using again

, we obtain the second inequality.
For the last inequality, we find the recursion, for any integer

,

AC^{(k,i)}=C^{(k+1,i)}+\sum_{w\in W_{k-1}}1\!\!{\sf I}([w]=\o)1\!\!{\sf I}(\hbox{for all }t\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[k-1\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}},[w]_{t}g_{i}\neq\o)\,a_{i}^{*}a(w)a_{i}\otimes 1_{\mathrm{F}_{d}}.

Indeed, if
w\in W_{k-1}
,
g_{j}[w]g_{i}=\o
and for all for all
t\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[k-1\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}},[w]_{t}g_{i}\neq\o
then
j=i^{*}
and thus
[w]=\o
. We get

AC^{(k,i)}=C^{(k+1,i)}+a^{*}_{i}C^{(k,i)}_{g_{i}\o}\otimes 1_{\mathrm{F}_{d}}.

Similarly, for

, we have
C^{(1,i)}=a_{i}\otimes\lambda(g_{i})
. We deduce that
\|C^{(1,i)}\|=\|a_{i}\|\leq\|A\|
and

\displaystyle\|C^{(k+1,i)}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A\|\|C^{(k,i)}\|+\|a_{i}^{*}C^{(k,i)}_{g_{i}\o}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A\|\|C^{(k,i)}\|+\|A\|\|C^{(k,i)}_{g_{i}\o}\|.

Now, from first inequality,

as requested (we note that this bound improves on Haagerup’s inequality

∎

A non-commutative Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

4.1
Main inequality

Let
r\geq k\geq 1
be integers and
\pi
be a partition of a subset

, and
T={{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[r\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}\backslash S
. We assume that
\pi
partitions

into
k

and each block is either a singleton or a pair of elements in

. For

, let

be the index of the block containing

.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer and

be a collection of elements in a

-algebra

and

(i,j_{l_{i}})
(more precisely, for

and

With these notations, we define

X_{\pi}=\sum_{\vec{j}\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{k}}X_{1,\vec{j}}\cdots X_{r,\vec{j}}.

For example, if

,

and

X_{\pi}=\sum_{1\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\leq m}X_{1,j_{1}}X_{2,j_{2}}X_{3,(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}X_{4,j_{3}}X_{5,j_{1}}X_{6,(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}X_{7,j_{2}}X_{8,j_{3}}.

(19)

Our goal is to estimate the norm of
X_{\pi}
. Our bound will require a compatibility assumption between
X
and the partition
\pi
, that we first describe. For
i\in T
and

, we say that the block
B_{l}
is

at

if
\min(i^{\prime}\in B_{l})<i<\max(i^{\prime}\in B_{l})
(thinking of a block

as the extremal points of a discrete interval of
{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[r\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
,
B_{l}
is open at

, if
i
is inside this interval). We say
X
is
open
for
\pi
if for all
i\in T
, the map
\vec{j}\to X_{i,\vec{j}}
depends only on the blocks open at
i
(that is, if
\vec{j}
and
\vec{j}^{\prime}
are equal on the coordinates
j_{l}
with
B_{l}
open at
i
then
X_{i,\vec{j}}=X_{i,\vec{j}^{\prime}}
). For example, in (
19
),
X
will be open for
\pi
, if
X_{3,(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}
depends only on
(j_{1},j_{2})
and if
X_{6,(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}
depends only on
(j_{2},j_{3})
.

To estimate the norm of
X_{\pi}
, we introduce a last notation.
For
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[r\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
, we define
Q_{\pi,i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
as follows:

1.

If

,

Q_{\pi,i}=\max_{\vec{j}\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{k}}{{\left\|X_{i,\vec{j}}\right\|}}.

2.

If
i\in S
is the leftmost element of a block with two elements,

Q_{\pi,i}={{\left\|\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}X_{i,j}X_{i,j}^{*}\right\|}}^{1/2}.

3.

If
i\in S
is the rightmost element of a block with two elements,

Q_{\pi,i}={{\left\|\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}X_{i,j}^{*}X_{i,j}\right\|}}^{1/2}.

4.

If

is an element of a singleton block, then

Q_{\pi,i}={{\left\|\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}\sqrt{X_{i,j}X_{i,j}^{*}}\right\|}}^{1/2}\cdot{{\left\|\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}\sqrt{X_{i,j}^{*}X_{i,j}}\right\|}}^{1/2}.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
X
is open for
\pi
then we have

{{\left\|X_{\pi}\right\|}}\leq\prod_{i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[r\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}Q_{\pi,i}.

Proof.

We first treat the case where
\pi
has no singletons.
As usual, for
j,j^{\prime}\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
,
E_{jj^{\prime}}\in M_{m}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the matrix with all entries equal to
0
except entry
(j,j^{\prime})
set to
1
. In
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes k}\otimes\mathcal{A}
, we introduce the elements

\tilde{X}_{i}
as follows

1.

If
i\in S
is the leftmost element of its block,

\tilde{X}_{i}=\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}1_{m}^{\otimes l_{i}-1}\otimes E_{1j}\otimes 1_{m}^{\otimes k-l_{i}}\otimes X_{i,j}.

2.

If
i\in S
is the rightmost element of its block,

\tilde{X}_{i}=\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}1_{m}^{\otimes l_{i}-1}\otimes E_{j1}\otimes 1_{m}^{\otimes k-l_{i}}\otimes X_{i,j}.

3.

If
i\in T
and
L_{i}\subset{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[k\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
are the indices of blocks open at
i
,

\tilde{X}_{i}=\sum_{\vec{j}\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{L_{i}}}K_{p_{1,i}(\vec{j})}\otimes\cdots\otimes K_{p_{r,i}(\vec{j})}\otimes X_{i,\vec{j}},

where
K_{p_{l,i}(\vec{j})}\in\{1_{m},E_{j_{l}j_{l}}\}
is

if

and is

otherwise. Also,

is a slight abuse of notation (since
X_{i,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})}
depends only on
(j_{l})_{l\in L_{i}}
).

The key computation is the following simple verification:

E_{11}^{\otimes k}\otimes X_{\pi}=\prod_{i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[r\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}\tilde{X}_{i}.

(20)

Let us check that this formula is correct in the example (
19
) assuming that
X
is open for
\pi
: for shorter notation, we simply write
X_{3,(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}=X_{3,(j_{1},j_{2})}
and
X_{6,(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}=X_{6,(j_{2},j_{3})}
. We have

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[r\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}\tilde{X}_{i}=

\displaystyle{{\left(\sum_{j_{1}}E_{1j_{1}}\otimes 1\otimes 1\otimes X_{1,j_{1}}\right)}}{{\left(\sum_{j_{2}}1\otimes E_{1j_{2}}\otimes 1\otimes X_{2,j_{2}}\right)}}{{\left(\sum_{j_{3},j_{4}}E_{j_{3}j_{3}}\otimes E_{j_{4}j_{4}}\otimes 1\otimes X_{3,(j_{3},j_{4})}\right)}}

\displaystyle\cdot{{\left(\sum_{j_{5}}1\otimes 1\otimes E_{1j_{5}}\otimes X_{4,j_{5}}\right)}}{{\left(\sum_{j_{6}}E_{j_{6}1}\otimes 1\otimes 1\otimes X_{5,j_{6}}\right)}}{{\left(\sum_{j_{7},j_{8}}1\otimes E_{j_{7}j_{7}}\otimes E_{j_{8}j_{8}}\otimes X_{6,(j_{7},j_{8})}\right)}}

\displaystyle\cdot{{\left(\sum_{j_{9}}1\otimes 1\otimes E_{1j_{9}}\otimes 1\otimes X_{7,j_{9}}\right)}}{{\left(\sum_{j_{10}}1\otimes 1\otimes E_{j_{10}1}X_{8,j_{10}}\right)}}.

Expanding the products, this forces
j_{1}=j_{3}=j_{6}
,
j_{2}=j_{4}=j_{7}=j_{9}
and
j_{5}=j_{8}=j_{10}
. We obtain the claimed formula (
20
).

Next, the conclusion follows from the sub-multiplicativity of the operator norm on
M_{m}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes k}\otimes\mathcal{A}
.
Indeed:

(1) Since
E_{11}^{\otimes k}
is a selfadjoint projection, it is of operator norm
1
and it follows that

\|E_{11}^{\otimes k}\otimes X_{\pi}\|=\|X_{\pi}\|.

(2) It follows from the
C^{*}
norm axiom that

\|\tilde{X}_{i}\|=Q_{\pi,i}.

This concludes the proof when
\pi
has no singletons. In the general case, we can handle singletons by doubling them.
For
i\in S
in a singleton block, we write a polar decomposition
X_{ij}=H_{ij}U_{ij}
where
H_{ij}
positive,
U_{ij}
unitary.
Then we write
X_{ij}=(\sqrt{H_{ij}})(\sqrt{H_{ij}}U_{ij})
and we are back to the case without singletons by increasing by the value of
r
by the number of singletons of
\pi
. It remains to observe that
H_{ij}H_{ij}^{*}=H_{ij}^{2}=\sqrt{X_{ij}X_{ij}^{*}}
and
(\sqrt{H_{ij}}U_{ij})^{*}(\sqrt{H_{ij}}U_{ij})=\sqrt{X_{ij}^{*}X_{ij}}
. ∎

Remark 4.2
.

To apply Theorem
4.1
in concrete applications, we observe that it is possible to consider the following (seemingly) more general setting: the vectors
\vec{j}
take values in

where for

The definitions

and

remain unchanged up to the modification of the index set

by

. In this setting, the conclusion of Theorem

still holds. Indeed, we may assume without loss of generality that

4.2
Stronger forms of Theorem
4.1

We do not know whether the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
remains true without the assumption that
X
is open for
\pi
(we are not aware of a counterexample). In this subsection, we will explore a few cases where a potential improvement of this inequality can be proved. To that end, we introduce two definitions.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\theta\geq 1
.
We say
(X,\pi)
is
\theta
-controlled, if for any
\delta=(\delta_{\vec{j}})\in[-1,1]^{{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{k}}

{{\left\|\sum_{\vec{j}\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{k}}\delta_{\vec{j}}X_{1,\vec{j}}\cdots X_{r,\vec{j}}\right\|}}\leq\theta^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{r}Q_{\pi,i}.

We say
(X,\pi)
is strongly
\theta
-controlled on the left (respectively on the right) if

{{\left\|\sum_{\vec{j}\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}^{k}}|X_{1,\vec{j}}\cdots X_{r,\vec{j}}|\right\|}}\leq\theta^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{r}Q_{\pi,i},

where
|A|=\sqrt{AA^{*}}
(respectively
|A|=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
).

Note that as its name suggests, strong
\theta
-control implies
\theta
-control. In the remainder of this section, we give necessary conditions for
(X,\pi)
to be strongly
\theta
-controlled

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and assume that
\mathcal{A}=M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathcal{A}_{0}
where
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra. We assume that for all
i\in S
,
X_{i,j}=x_{i,j}\otimes u_{i,j}
where
x_{i,j}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
u_{i,j}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
is unitary. Then
(X,\pi)
is strongly
n
-controlled on the left and on the right.

Proof.

From the triangle inequality for the operator norm and its sub-multiplicativity, we get

\|X_{\pi}\|\leq\sum_{\vec{j}}\|X_{1,\vec{j}}\|\cdots\|X_{r,\vec{j}}\|.

We deduce from the classical Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\|X_{\pi}\|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i},

where for
i\in T
,
\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}=Q_{\pi,i}
and for
i\in S
in a block of two elements,

\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}={{\left(\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}\|X_{i,j}\|^{2}\right)}}^{1/2},

and for for
i\in S
in a singleton block,

\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}=\sum_{j\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}\|X_{i,j}\|,

Then, the lemma follows from the claim
\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}\leq\sqrt{n}Q_{\pi,i}
for
i
in a block of two elements and
\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}\leq nQ_{\pi,i}
for
i
in a singleton block (recall that
k
is the number of blocks of
\pi
).

We check the claim. From our assumption,
\|X_{i,j}\|=\|x_{i,j}\|\leq\sqrt{{\rm Tr}(x_{i,j}x_{i,j}^{*}})
. From the linearity of the trace, it follows that, for
i
in a block of two elements,

\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}\leq{{\left({\rm Tr}\sum_{j}x_{i,j}^{*}x_{i,j}\right)}}^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{n}{{\left\|\sum_{j}x_{i,j}^{*}x_{i,j}\right\|}}^{1/2}=\sqrt{n}{{\left\|\sum_{j}X_{i,j}^{*}X_{i,j}\right\|}}^{1/2},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
u^{*}_{ij}u_{ij}=1
and
\|T\otimes 1\|=\|T\|
. The same bound holds with
X_{i,j}^{*}X_{i,j}
. We deduce that
\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}\leq\sqrt{n}Q_{\pi,i}
, as claimed. For a singleton block, the proof is identical.
∎

The bistochastic matrices give another simple example of matrices that can be
\theta
-controlled.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}=M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and that there exists a unit vector

such that for all

and

,

is strongly

-controlled on the left and on the right.

Proof.

With
\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}
as in the proof of Lemma
4.4
, it is sufficient to check that
\tilde{Q}_{\pi,i}\leq Q_{\pi,i}
(in fact there will be equal). Let us check the claim for

in a block of two elements. Using the assumption, we have

{{\left\|\sum_{j}X_{i,j}^{*}X_{i,j}\right\|}}\geq{{\left\|{{\left(\sum_{j}X_{i,j}^{*}X_{i,j}\right)}}f\right\|}}_{2}=\tilde{Q}^{2}_{\pi,i},

and similarly with

.
For a singleton block, the proof is identical.∎

4.3
Relation to non-commutative Kintchine inequalities

(This subsection is a digression independent of the rest of the article). Theorem
4.1
is connected to the non-commutative Kintchine inequalities. Indeed, as a corollary of the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we obtain the following version of a classical non-commutative Kintchine-inequality due to Lust-Piquard and Pisier
[
43
]
with the optimal constant found by Buchholz
[
20
]
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{m})
be independent standard Gaussian variables and
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
be elements in a
C^{*}
-algebra with faithful state
\tau
. For any integer
k\geq 1
, we have

\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left\{{{\left|\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}X_{j}\right|}}^{2k}\right\}}}\leq\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}\max{{\left(\tau{{\left\{{{\left(\sum_{j}X_{j}X_{j}^{*}\right)}}^{k}\right\}}},\tau{{\left\{{{\left(\sum_{j}X_{j}^{*}X_{j}\right)}}^{k}\right\}}}\right)}},

where
|x|=\sqrt{xx^{*}}
or
|x|=\sqrt{x^{*}x}
.

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left|\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}X_{j}\right|}}^{2k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left({{\left(\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}X_{j}\right)}}{{\left(\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}X^{*}_{j}\right)}}\right)}}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{2k})}\tau{{\left\{X_{j_{1}}X_{j_{2}}^{*}\cdots X_{j_{2k-1}}X_{j_{2k}}^{*}\right\}}}\mathbf{E}\prod_{i=1}^{2k}\gamma_{j_{i}}.

From Wick’s formula, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\tau{{\left|\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}X_{j}\right|}}^{2k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\tau{{\left\{X_{\pi}\right\}}},

where the sum is over all pair partitions
\pi
of
{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2k\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
and
X_{\pi}
is as in the previous subsections with
r=2k
,
X=(X_{1,\vec{j}},\ldots,X_{2k,\vec{j}})
and
X_{2i-1,\vec{j}}=X_{j_{l_{2i-1}}}
,

7.1

7.1.1
Haagerup’s inequality

In this paragraph, we recall how Haagerup’s inequality
[

, Lemma 1.4]
classically allows to upper bound the ratio

with its normalized tracial state

Lemma 7.1
.

\|T\|\leq(l+1)\sqrt{\sum_{g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}\|a(g)\|^{2}}.

In particular, if

\|T\|\leq(l+1)\sqrt{n}\|T\|_{2}
.

Proof.

The classical Haagerup’s inequality
[
33
, Lemma 1.4]
is for
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathbb{C}
but his proof extends verbatim to this setting. We also refer to Buchholz
[
19
, Theorem 2.8]
for a refinement.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain the following.

(60)

Next, we write, if

Corollary

to bypass this limitation. We refer to the monograph of Brown and Ozawa
[

for background on exactness. One key is the following quantitative exactness theorem:

Theorem 7.3
.

Let

and

be integers,

a Hilbert space and
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathcal{B}(H)

of rank at most

\|(1_{H}\otimes p)\cdot A_{\mathrm{F}}\cdot(1_{H}\otimes p)\|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|.

(61)

In addition, there exists a self-adjoint projection

of rank at most

such that

\|(q\otimes 1_{\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d})})\cdot A_{\mathrm{F}}\cdot(q\otimes 1_{\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d})})\|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|.

We are indebted to Narutaka Ozawa for suggesting us to use the exactness of the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra of the free group
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
and for outlining the proof of the theorem.

Proof of Theorem

Let
|\cdot|
be the reduced word length on
\mathrm{F}_{d}
with free generators

if

and

. In all other cases, we set

This function satisfies the following three properties:

(i)

(ii)

For all

(iii)

For

\eta_{i}(x)=1-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}\sqrt{\mu(g_{i}x,g_{i}y)\mu(x,y)}
. We have

|x|=l-1

|g_{i}x|=l-2
}, and

The first two properties follow directly from the definition.
As for the third property, for

, we write

in reduced form. Observe that

and

or

, and (2) if

and

or if

and

. In all theses cases,
\{\mu(g_{i}x,g_{i}y),\mu(x,y)\}=\{0,1/l\}
. Property (

The function
\mu
can be seen as a mass transport map on
\mathrm{F}_{d}
. The map
x\to\mu(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure on
\mathrm{F}_{d}
with support on
B_{l-1}
and the action of the canonical generators of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
on these measures is
(1/l)
-invariant with respect to the total variation distance. We refer to Brown and Ozawa
[
18
, Proposition 5.1.8]
for more context.

We next consider the operator

\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(B_{l-1}))\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))
given by

\Phi(e_{x,y})=\sum_{g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}\sqrt{\mu(xg,x)\mu(yg,y)}e_{xg,yg},

where
e_{x,y}=\delta_{x}\otimes\delta_{y}
. This function is well-defined since the sum can be expressed as a finite sum from property (
i
). We first claim that this function is completely positive. Indeed, for any
g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
, consider the operator
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))
given
by

e_{x,y}\mapsto\sqrt{\mu(xg,x)\mu(yg,y)}e_{x,y}.

It is the Schur multiplier with the rank one self-adjoint projection
(\sqrt{\mu(xg,x)\mu(yg,y)})_{x,y}
. It is thus completely positive.
It follows, after conjugating by the appropriate unitary translation, that
the operator

\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))
defined by

e_{x,y}\mapsto\sqrt{\mu(xg,x)\mu(yg,y)}e_{xg,yg}

is also completely positive.
Therefore its restriction
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(B_{l-1}))\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))
is again completely positive. Finally, since
\Phi
is a finite sum of such operators, it is completely positive.
Moreover, one checks directly from property (
ii
) that
\Phi(1_{B_{l-1}})=1_{\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d})}
. Therefore
\Phi
is a completely positive unital map, and thus, it is completely contractive.

Next, let
\Psi:\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))
be the corner map defined by, for
T\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathrm{F}_{d}))
,

\Psi(T)=p\cdot T\cdot p,

where
p
is the self-adjoint projection onto
B_{l-1}
. This map is also completely contractive.

We now claim that for
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
, we have

\|(1_{H}\otimes(\Phi\circ\Psi))A_{\mathrm{F}}-A_{\mathrm{F}}\|\leq\frac{2}{l}\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|.

(62)

Indeed, since
\lambda(g_{i})=\sum_{y}e_{g_{i}y,y}
, we have, setting
S=B_{l-1}\cap g_{i}^{-1}B_{l-1}
,

\displaystyle(\Phi\circ\Psi)\lambda(g_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in S}\Phi(e_{g_{i}y,y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathrm{F}_{d},g\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}\sqrt{\mu(g_{i}yg,g_{i}y)\mu(yg,y)}e_{g_{i}yg,yg}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}e_{g_{i}x,x}{{\left(\sum_{y\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}\sqrt{\mu(g_{i}x,g_{i}y)\mu(x,y)}\right)}},

where at the second line we have used property (
i
) (the summand is

for all

Next, recalling the definition of

in property (

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{F}}-(1_{H}\otimes(\Phi\circ\Psi))A_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2d}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{F}_{d}}a_{i}\otimes e_{g_{i}x,x}\eta_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{l}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{F}_{d}\backslash\{\o\}}\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes e_{g_{i}x,x}+\frac{1}{l}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{F}_{d}\backslash B_{l-2}}\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes e_{g_{i}x,x}(1-1\!\!{\sf I}_{|x|=l-1,x_{1}=i^{*}})

\displaystyle\frac{T_{1}+T_{2}}{l},

where for

written in reduced form as

and we deduce that (

) holds.

By the triangle inequality, (

To prove the second assertion, let

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{2d})

,

Proof.

We introduce the Hilbert-Schmidt norm on

(since

). Moreover, for any matrix

{{\left({\rm Tr}|M|^{p}\right)}}^{1/p}={{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{p}\right)}}^{1/p}\leq{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{2}\right)}}^{1/2}=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}(MM^{*})}.

Since

It remains to apply
[

(we can take

\tau_{1}((T_{N})_{xx})=\sum_{g}\tau_{1}(a(g))U(g)_{xx}\geq\tau_{1}(a(\o))=\tau(T).

The conclusion follows by applying this to
T_{N}=A_{N}^{2h}
and
T=A_{\mathrm{F}}^{2h}
.
∎

8
Polynomial of unitaries

8.1
Linearization trick for unitaries

In this subsection, we consider an extension of our main model defined in Section
1.2
. Recall that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
is the left group algebra of the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}
with free generators
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
and their inverses. We denote by
B_{l}\subset\mathrm{F}_{d}
the set of elements of word length at most
l
. Let
(a_{g})_{g\in B_{l}}
be a collection of operators in a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}(H_{1})
. We consider the operator in
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
:

P_{\mathrm{F}}=\sum_{g\in B_{l}}a_{g}\otimes\lambda(g).

Let
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
be unitaries in
\mathrm{U}_{N}
, its corresponding operator in
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
,

P_{N}=\sum_{g\in B_{l}}a_{g}\otimes U(g),

where
U:\mathrm{F}_{d}\to\mathrm{U}_{N}
is the group homomorphism defined by
U(g_{i})=U_{i}
. The degree of
P_{\mathrm{F}}
and
P_{N}
is the minimal
l
such that they can be expressed in this form. The operators
A_{\mathrm{F}}
and
A_{N}
in (
3
)-(
5
) are operators of degree (at most)
l=1
.

The goal of this subsection is to leverage estimates on
\|A_{N}\|/\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|
into estimates on
\|P_{N}\|/\|P_{\mathrm{F}}\|
. This can be done thanks to the linearization trick. In a nutshell, it is possible to reduce the degree of
P_{N}
iteratively at the cost of replacing
\mathcal{A}_{1}
by
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes M_{k}(\mathbb{C})
for some integer
k\geq 1
. For ease of exposition, we generalize the framework. Let
\Gamma
be a finite generated group and let
S
be a finite symmetric set of generators of
\Gamma
. We denote by
B_{l}
the ball of radius
l
for the word metric associated to
S
. Let
H_{2}
be an Hilbert space and let

P_{\Gamma}=P_{\Gamma}((a_{g})_{g\in B_{l}})=\sum_{g\in B_{l}}a_{g}\otimes\lambda(g)\and P=P((a_{g})_{g\in B_{l}})=\sum_{g\in B_{l}}a_{g}\otimes u(g),

(65)

where
\lambda
is the left-regular representation
\Gamma
and
g\mapsto u(g)
is a group homomorphism from
\Gamma
to
\mathrm{U}(H_{2})
.
We have the following lemma, which is a quantitative version of the linearization trick of Pisier for unitaries
[
52
, Proposition 6]
; see also Lehner
[
41
, Section 5]
and the appendix in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
l\geq 2
be an even integer and a collection
(a_{g})_{g\in B_{l}}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. There exist
(b_{g})_{g\in B_{l/2}}
with
b_{g}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes M_{2|B_{l/2}|}(\mathbb{C})
and
\theta\geq 0
such that, if
P=P((a_{g})_{g\in B_{l}})
and
Q=P((b_{g})_{g\in B_{l/2}})
for some group homomorphism
g\mapsto u(g)
from
\Gamma
to
\mathrm{U}(H_{2})
, we have

\|P\|=\|Q\|^{2}-\theta.

Moreover, if
P_{\Gamma}
is as in (
65
), we have
\theta\leq|B_{l/2}|\|P_{\Gamma}\|
.

Proof.

By the standard linearization trick (see proof of Theorem
1.1
), we can assume that the symmetry condition is met at the cost of replacing
\mathcal{A}_{1}
by
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and also that
\|P\|
is equal to the rightmost point in the spectrum of
P
. We repeat the proof of
[
14
, Lemma 29]
with the present notation. Recall
\mathcal{A}_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}(H_{1})
.

Consider the self-adjoint element
\tilde{a}\in M_{B_{l/2}}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathcal{A}_{1}
defined in matrix form as

\tilde{a}=(\tilde{a}_{g,h})_{g,h\in B_{l/2}}

with

\tilde{a}_{g,h}=a_{g^{-1}h}/|\{(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in B_{l/2},(g^{\prime})^{-1}h^{\prime}=g^{-1}h\}|.

In particular, for all
w\in B_{l}
,

\sum_{g,h\in B_{l/2},g^{-1}h=w}\tilde{a}_{g,h}=a_{w}.

We claim that
\|\tilde{a}\|\leq\|P_{\Gamma}\|
. Indeed, if
{\rm Tr}
denotes the partial trace on
M_{B_{l/2}}(\mathbb{C})
(non-normalized), we have

\|\tilde{a}\|^{2}\leq\|{\rm Tr}(\tilde{a}\tilde{a}^{*})\|={{\left\|\sum_{g,h\in B_{l/2}}\tilde{a}_{g,h}\tilde{a}_{g,h}^{*}\right\|}}\leq{{\left\|\sum_{w\in B_{l}}\tilde{a}_{w}\tilde{a}_{w}^{*}\right\|}}={{\left\|(P_{\Gamma}P_{\Gamma}^{*})_{\o\o}\right\|}}\leq\|P_{\Gamma}\|^{2},

where
\o
is the unit of
\Gamma
. The operator
\tilde{a}+\|\tilde{a}\|1
is positive semi-definite and let

\tilde{b}\in M_{B_{l/2}}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathcal{A}_{1}
be a self-adjoint square root of
\tilde{a}+\|\tilde{a}\|1
.
For
g\in B_{l/2}
, we then set

b_{g}=\tilde{b}\cdot(e_{g,\o}\otimes 1_{H_{1}})\in M_{B_{l/2}}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathcal{A}_{1},

where for
g,h\in B_{l/2}
,
e_{g,h}=\delta_{g}\otimes\delta_{h}\in M_{B_{l/2}}(\mathbb{C})
and
\o
is the unit of
\Gamma
. Then, the operator
Q=\sum_{g\in B_{l/2}}b_{g}\otimes u(g)
satisfies

\displaystyle Q^{*}Q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g,h\in B_{l/2}}(e_{\o,g}\otimes 1_{H_{1}})\tilde{b}^{2}(e_{h,\o}\otimes 1_{H_{1}})\otimes u(g^{-1}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g,h\in B_{l/2}}e_{\o,\o}\otimes(\tilde{a}_{g,h}+\|\tilde{a}\|1\!\!{\sf I}_{g=h}\cdot 1_{H_{1}})\otimes u(g^{-1}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{\o,\o}\otimes(P+\theta 1),

where we have set
\theta=\|\tilde{a}\||B_{l/2}|
.
Finally, since
\theta\geq 0
and the rightmost point in the spectrum of
P
is equal to
\|P\|
, we find

\|Q\|^{2}=\|P+\theta 1\|=\|P\|+\theta
.
∎

We note that
|B_{l}|\leq(2d)^{l}
and thus
\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}(2|B_{2^{k}}|)\leq 2^{m}(2d)^{2^{m}}
.
By iteration, we deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
l\geq 2
be an integer,
m=\lceil\log_{2}l\rceil
and
P
as above. For each
k\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
, there is an integer
n_{k}\geq 1
, elements
(a^{k}_{g})_{g\in B_{2^{m-k}}}
with
a^{k}_{g}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})
and
\theta_{k}\geq 0
such that
n_{m}\leq 2l(2d)^{2l}
and, setting
Q_{0}=P
and
Q_{k}=P((a^{k}_{g})_{g\in B_{2^{m-k}}})
, we have

\|Q_{k-1}\|=\|Q_{k}\|^{2}-\theta_{k}.

Moreover,
\theta_{k}\leq\|P_{\Gamma}((a^{k-1}_{g})_{g\in B_{2^{m-k+1}}})\|(2d)^{2^{m-k}}
(for
k=1
,
P_{\Gamma}
is as in (
65
)).

The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
l\geq 2
be an integer,
m=\lceil\log_{2}l\rceil
and
P_{\Gamma},P
be as in (
65
). Set
a_{0}=a^{m}_{\o}
and for
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
,
a_{i}=a^{m}_{g_{i}}
where
(a^{m}_{g})_{g\in B_{1}}
is as in Corollary
8.2
. Let
A_{\Gamma},A
be as in (
65
) with
l=1
. For
\varepsilon>0
, if
\|A\|\leq\|A_{\Gamma}\|(1+\varepsilon)
and
\varepsilon\leq(2d)^{-l}l^{-2}
then

\|P\|\leq\|P_{\Gamma}\|(1+4l^{2}(2d)^{2l}\varepsilon).

Conversely, if
\|A\|\geq\|A_{\Gamma}\|(1-\varepsilon)
then

\|P\|\geq\|P_{\Gamma}\|(1-4l^{2}(2d)^{2l}\varepsilon).

Proof.

For
k\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
, let
(a^{k}_{g})_{g\in B_{2^{m-k}}}
be as in Corollary
8.2
. Let
Q_{k}=P((a^{k}_{g})_{g\in B_{2^{m-k}}})
and
Q_{\Gamma,k}=P_{\Gamma}((a^{k}_{g})_{g\in B_{2^{m-k}}}))
be the corresponding operators. We set
Q_{0}=P
and
Q_{\Gamma,0}=P_{\Gamma}
. With this notation, we have
Q_{m}=A
and
Q_{\Gamma,m}=A_{\Gamma}
.

For some
k\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[m\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
, assume that
\|Q_{k}\|\leq\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|(1+\varepsilon_{k})
with
0\leq\varepsilon_{k}\leq 1
. Then, applying Corollary
8.2
twice, we get

\displaystyle\|Q_{k-1}\|-\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|=\|Q_{k}\|^{2}-\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{k}(1+\varepsilon_{k})(\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|+\theta_{k})\leq\varepsilon_{k-1}\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|,

with
\varepsilon_{k-1}=4\varepsilon_{k}(2d)^{2^{m-k}}
. It follows that

\varepsilon_{0}\leq\varepsilon_{m}\prod_{k=1}^{m}4(2d)^{2^{m-k}}\leq 4^{m}(2d)^{2^{m}}\varepsilon\leq 4l^{2}(2d)^{2l}\varepsilon,

provided that
4^{m-1}(2d)^{2^{m-1}}\varepsilon\leq l^{2}(2d)^{l}\varepsilon\leq 1
.

Conversely, assume
\|Q_{k}\|\geq\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|(1-\varepsilon_{k})

\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|-\|Q_{k-1}\|=\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|^{2}-\|Q_{k}\|^{2}.

It follows that if
\|Q_{k}\|\geq\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|
then
\|Q_{k-1}\|\geq\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|
. Otherwise,
\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|(1-\varepsilon_{k})\leq\|Q_{k}\|\leq\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|
and we get

\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|-\|Q_{k-1}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|(\|Q_{\Gamma,k}\|+\|Q_{k}\|)\leq 2\varepsilon_{k}(\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|+\theta_{k})\leq\varepsilon_{k-1}\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|,

with
\varepsilon_{k-1}=4\varepsilon_{k}(2d)^{2^{m-k}}
as above. Hence, in both cases,
\|Q_{k-1}\|\geq\|Q_{\Gamma,k-1}\|(1-\varepsilon_{k-1})
.
∎

In conjunction with Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.8
, Theorem

gives quantitative estimates on

for
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
independent Haar unitaries on

or
\mathrm{O}_{N}
. For example, in the case where

and

, we obtain

with high probability as soon as

for some constant

. Conversely, we get
\|P_{N}\|\geq(1-o(1))\|P_{\mathrm{F}}\|
with high probability if

. Similarly, if
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
are independent random permutation matrices, we may use Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.6
. If

and

, we find
\|P_{N}\Pi_{N}\|=(1+o(1))\|P_{\mathrm{F}}\|
with high probability as soon as

for some constant

.

8.2
Hausdorff distance for spectra

Let
P
and
P_{\Gamma}
be as in (
65
)-(
65
). Assume that further that
a(g)^{*}=a(g^{-1})
for all
g\in B_{l}\subset\mathrm{F}_{d}
. Then
P
and
P_{\Gamma}
are self-adjoint, and it turns out that it is straightforward to compare the spectrum (as a set) of
P
and
P_{\Gamma}
in terms of norm estimates of polynomials with degrees doubled, which in turn can be linearized thanks to Theorem
8.1
.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
l\geq 1
be an integer and
P_{\Gamma},P
be as in (
65
) such that
a(g^{-1})=a(g)^{*}
for all
g\in B_{l}
. Let
x\leq y
be real numbers. There exists
(b_{g})_{g\in B_{2l}}
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
such that if
Q=P((b_{g})_{g\in B_{2l}})
and
Q_{\Gamma}=P_{\Gamma}((b_{g})_{g\in B_{2l}})
the following holds.

For
\varepsilon>0
, set
\eta=4\varepsilon\|P_{\Gamma}\|^{2}/(y-x)
if
\|Q\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|Q_{\Gamma}\|
,
(x,y)\subset\sigma(P_{\Gamma})^{c}
and
y>x+2\eta
, then
(x-\eta,y-\eta)\subset\sigma(P)^{c}
.

Conversely, if
0<\varepsilon<1
,
\|Q\|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|Q_{\Gamma}\|
and
[x,y]\in\sigma(P)
then
\mathrm{dist}([x,y],\sigma(P))\leq\eta
. If
x=y
we have
\mathrm{dist}(x,\sigma(P))\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon\|P_{\Gamma}\|}
.

Proof.

Set
c=\|P_{\Gamma}\|
. We consider the quadratic function
f(\lambda)=\theta+(y-\lambda)(x-a)
and set
Q=f(P)
and
Q_{\Gamma}=f(P_{\Gamma})
, these operators are of the required form. If
\theta=2c^{2}
, for all
\lambda\in[-c,c]\backslash(a,b)
,
|f(\lambda)|\leq f(a)=f(b)=\theta
. Hence, by functional calculus,
\|Q_{\mathrm{F}}\|=\theta
and
\|Q\|=\max(|f(\lambda)|,\lambda\in\sigma(P))
. Moreover, by concavity, if
\lambda\in[a,(a+b)/2]
then
f(\lambda)\geq\theta+(\lambda-a)(b-a)/2
. This gives the first claim. For the second claim, this is similar (with the same function
f
).
∎

9
Application to strong convergence

9.1
Strong convergence

Fix an integer
d\geq 1
and let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
be elements of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
with faithful normal state
\tau
and associated operator norm
\|\cdot\|
. For integer
N\geq 1
, let
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})
be elements of
(\mathcal{A}_{N},\tau_{N})
a sequence of unital
C^{*}
-algebra with faithful normal states
\tau_{N}
and operator norm also denoted by
\|\cdot\|
.

We shall say that
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})

converges strongly
to
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
if for any non-commutative
*
-polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{F}_{d}]
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\|P(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})\|=\|P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\|.

(66)

In fact, provided that
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
is simple and
\tau
is the unique tracial state of
\mathcal{A}
, it is sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\|P(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})\|\leq\|P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\|,

(67)

(see the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
42
]
). In fact, in this last case,
by a compactness argument,
it also implies that for any
P\in\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{F}_{d}]
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\tau_{N}(P(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d}))=\tau(P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})).

If
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})
are random elements of
(\mathcal{A}_{N},\tau_{N})
, we will say that
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})
converge strongly toward
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
in probability, if for any
P\in\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{F}_{d}]
, the convergence (
66
) holds in probability.
Note that if
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,d})
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
are unitaries, then Lemma
8.1
implies that it is sufficient to prove that for any integer
n\geq 1
and any
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{2d})\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with the symmetry condition (
4
), we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}{{\left\|a_{0}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes X_{N,i}\right\|}}={{\left\|a_{0}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes x_{i}\right\|}},

where for
i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}
, we have set
X_{N,i+d}=X_{N,i}^{*}
and
x_{i+d}=x_{i}^{*}
.
For unitaries, we observe finally that, by Lemma
8.1
, to prove that (
67
) holds (in probability), it is sufficient to prove that for any integer
n\geq 1
and
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{2d})\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, (in probability)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}{{\left\|a_{0}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes X_{N,i}\right\|}}\leq{{\left\|a_{0}\otimes 1+\sum_{i=1}^{2d}a_{i}\otimes x_{i}\right\|}}.

(68)

Strong convergence of unitaries is particularly natural and important for unitary representations of a group. More precisely, let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group and let
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}\subset\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
be its reduced
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the left-regular representation,
\lambda(g)
,
g\in\Gamma
. Let
\rho_{N}
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
of finite dimension
k_{N}
, say on
\mathbb{C}^{k_{N}}
. Let
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})
be finite generating set of
\Gamma
. Along a sequence
N\to\infty
, we say that
\rho_{N}
converges strongly toward the regular representations of
\Gamma
if
(\rho_{N}(g_{1}),\ldots,\rho_{N}(g_{d}))\in\mathrm{U}_{k_{N}}
converges strongly toward
(\lambda(g_{1}),\ldots,\lambda(g_{d}))
. Note that this notion does not depend on the particular choice of the generating set. For example, in this language,
[
24
]
implies that the random unitary representation of the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}
defined by
\rho_{N}(g_{i})=U_{i}
with
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
independent Haar distributed on
\mathrm{U}_{N}
converge strongly in probability toward the regular representations of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
. Similarly,
[
13
]
implies that the random action of the free group defined by
\rho_{N}(g_{i})=U_{i}
with
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
independent uniformly distributed on
\mathrm{S}_{N}
converges strongly on
1\!\!{\sf I}_{N}^{\perp}
toward the regular representations of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
.

9.2
Absorption principle

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
be unital
C^{*}
-algebra equipped with tracial faithful normal states. A well-known property of free Haar unitaries
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})
in
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is that for any unitaries
(v_{1},\ldots v_{d})
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
,
(v_{1}\otimes u_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\otimes u_{d})
remain free Haar unitaries in
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
. Here, we say
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})
are free Haar unitaries if they have the same distribution as
(\lambda(g_{1}),\ldots,\lambda(g_{d}))
in the free group reduced
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
.
This property implies immediately that
(v_{1}\otimes U_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\otimes U_{N})
are asymptotically free as
N\to\infty
if
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
are independent Haar unitaries on
\mathrm{U}_{N}
.
For more details about this property (a variant of an absorption principle), we refer to
[
53
,
21
,
23
]
.

Theorem
1.3
for independent Haar unitaries on
\mathrm{U}_{N}
or
\mathrm{O}_{N}
and Theorem
1.4
for independent random permutations matrices on
\mathrm{S}_{N}
allow to extend quantitatively this to strong convergence. We obtain, for example, the following result.

Theorem 9.1
.

Fix
d\geq 2
and let
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d})
be unitaries in
\mathrm{U}_{n}
and let
N=N_{n}
be a diverging integers sequence. We assume that either (i)
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
are independent Haar unitaries on
\mathrm{U}_{N}
or
\mathrm{O}_{N}
and
N\gg(\log n)^{80}
or (ii)
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
are the restriction to
1\!\!{\sf I}_{N}^{\perp}
of independent and uniform permutation matrices in
\mathrm{S}_{N}
and
\log N\gg\sqrt{(\log\log n)\log n}
.
Then
(U_{1}\otimes V_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\otimes V_{d})
converges strongly in probability as
n\to\infty
toward free Haar unitaries.

Proof.

We start with the upper bound (
68
). For
\mathrm{O}_{N}
and
\mathrm{U}_{N}
, we apply Theorem
1.3
to
\mathcal{A}_{1}=M_{k}(\mathbb{C})\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for fixed
k
. For
\mathrm{S}_{N}
, we apply Theorem
1.4
. Thanks to (
6
), it implies (
68
). For the converse lower bound, it is guaranteed (as soon as
N\gg 1
) by Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.6
. Note that since
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
is simple
[
55
]
, the upper bound was, in fact, sufficient.
∎

9.3
Random representations of
\mathrm{F}_{d}\times\cdots\times\mathrm{F}_{d}
and a new proof of the Hayes’ criterion

The Peterson-Thom conjecture
[
51
]
says that any diffuse, amenable subalgebra of the free group factor
L(F_{2})
is contained in a unique maximal amenable subalgebra. Hayes proved in
[
36
]
that the following property implies this conjecture:
given a sequence of
2d
-tuples
(U_{i})_{i\in{{\left[\hskip-1.4pt\left[2d\right]\hskip-1.4pt\right]}}}
of independent and Haar distributed random unitary in
\mathrm{U}_{N}
,
the family
(U_{1}\otimes 1_{N},\ldots,U_{d}\otimes 1_{N},1_{N}\otimes U_{d+1},\ldots,1_{N}\otimes U_{2d})
in
M_{N^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
converges strongly in probability, as
N\to\infty
, to
(\lambda(g_{1})\otimes 1,\ldots,\lambda(g_{d})\otimes 1,1\otimes\lambda(g_{d+1}),\ldots,1\otimes\lambda(g_{d+1}))
elements of the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra of
\mathrm{F}_{d}\times\mathrm{F}_{d}
where
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})
(resp.
(g_{d+1},\ldots,g_{2d})
are the free generators of the first copy (resp. second) copy of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
. In other words, Hayes’s criterion amounts to prove that a random unitary representation of dimension
N^{2}
of
\mathrm{F}_{d}\times\mathrm{F}_{d}
converges strongly in probability toward the regular representations of
\mathrm{F}_{d}\times\mathrm{F}_{d}
.

As mentioned in the introduction, Hayes found equivalent reformulations of this criterion, and the proof of
one reformulation involving GUEs was recently done by Belinschi and Capitaine
[
7
]
.
