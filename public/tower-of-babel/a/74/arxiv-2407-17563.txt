Quantum Algorithms for Realizing Symmetric, Asymmetric, and Antisymmetric Projectors
Margarite L. LaBorde, Soorya Rethinasamy, Mark M. Wilde

Abstract
In quantum computing, knowing the symmetries a given system or state obeys or disobeys is often useful. For example, Hamiltonian symmetries may limit allowed state transitions or simplify learning parameters in machine learning applications, and certain asymmetric quantum states are known to be resourceful in various applications. Symmetry testing algorithms provide a means to identify and quantify these properties with respect to a representation of a group. In this paper, we present a collection of quantum algorithms that realize projections onto the symmetric subspace, as well as the asymmetric subspace, of quantum systems. We describe how this can be modified to realize an antisymmetric projection as well, and we show how projectors can be combined in a systematic way to effectively measure various projections in a single quantum circuit. Using these constructions, we demonstrate applications such as testing for Werner-state symmetry and estimating Schmidt ranks of bipartite states, supported by experimental data from IBM Quantum systems. This work underscores the pivotal role of symmetry in simplifying quantum calculations and advancing quantum information tasks.



I
Introduction

Symmetry has been prevalent throughout the field of physics since the introduction of Noether’s theorem
[
1
]
, and perhaps even earlier through its prominence in mathematics and observation in crystalline materials
[
2
]
. A recent direction of scholarship focuses on combining symmetry with quantum information theory, demonstrating how the mathematical beauty of symmetry can give rise to physically interesting phenomena in this regime. For instance, the concept of Noether’s theorem, an integral link between physical symmetries and conserved quantities, was expanded upon in
[
3
]
, which investigated the resourcefulness of quantum states under free channels. Similarly, the resource theory of asymmetry
[
4
]
illustrates a connection between quantum computational resources and symmetric properties of said quantum resources. Finally, Schur–Weyl duality and its links to permutation symmetry enable quantum de Finetti theorems
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, which in turn provide the backbone for many tests of entanglement, particularly in the case of
k
-extendibility
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

In quantum computing, symmetry can in principle be used to simplify calculations, speed up computational tasks, or tackle classically intractable problems.
For instance, fully permutationally invariant Hamiltonians have been shown to be efficiently simulatable on classical hardware
[
11
]
. Quantum algorithms for testing symmetry of states
[
12
]
, Hamiltonians
[
13
]
, and open quantum systems
[
14
]
have been investigated in recent work, and some were shown to delineate a hierarchy of quantum complexity classes
[
15
]
. Furthermore, incorporating symmetry into quantum machine learning can reduce parameter search space and speed up calculations
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

In physics, a natural complement to symmetry is antisymmetry. Antisymmetry is of course one particular type of symmetry, observable in nature in fermionic systems. Antisymmetric properties are pertinent for a variety of physical phenomena. Time reversal and spatial inversion antisymmetries occur in magnetic crystallography, where some can be used to classify physical properties (see
[
19
]
and references therein). Additionally, the antisymmetry inherent in the quantum marginal problem for fermionic systems
[
20
]
implies generalized Pauli constraints for these systems. Particularly relevant to quantum information tasks, antisymmetric projections have been employed in relation to the Schmidt rank
[
21
]
of quantum states—indeed, we will use this result later in this work.

In this paper, we propose various quantum algorithms for testing symmetry, antisymmetry, and the asymmetry of states. In more detail, we delineate algorithms for projecting quantum states onto the symmetric and antisymmetric subspaces of the standard representation of a permutation group and then generalize this procedure to implement projectors in sequence, a process we term projector concatenation. In combining these mathematical projectors, we demonstrate how our tests enable us to examine relevant quantum information tasks, such as testing for Werner-state symmetry and estimating the Schmidt rank of a pure bipartite state. For these applications, we include example data from experiments run on the IBM Quantum ibm_kyoto and ibm_osaka computers. We also show how our algorithms generalize to more generic projectors. Finally, we examine how a similar framework can realize a quantum switch that estimates commutators of observables.

The structure of our paper is as follows. In Section
II
, we review previously established formalism for symmetry tests and introduce the symmetric and antisymmetric projectors. In Section
III
, we show how additional uncomputing steps allow for realizing both projectors in a single quantum circuit. We then show how this method can be conducted in sequence to test a state’s projection onto various subspaces by means of a single circuit. We give examples for symmetric subspaces and realize the difference of two or more projectors. In Section
IV
, we discuss applications of these techniques. In particular, we show how an antisymmetry test can be used to estimate Schmidt rank and how the difference of two projectors can be used as a test for Werner-state symmetry. We also show how an antisymmetrized circuit can be used to estimate commutators. Finally, we conclude in Section
V
with a summary of our results and comments on remaining open problems.

II
Symmetric and Antisymmetric Projections

II.1
Review of Symmetric Projections

This work naturally rests on a foundation of symmetry tests; we will begin there and then build upon it recursively, generalizing more in each step. Let us begin by defining precisely what we mean by symmetry and, in turn, antisymmetry.

Consider a finite group
G
with a unitary representation
U:G\to\mathcal{U}(V)
on a vector space
V
. For such a representation
U
of a group
G
, we define the
G
-symmetric subspace by

\mathcal{S}^{G}\coloneqq\{|\psi\rangle\in V\ |\ U(g)|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle,\ \forall g\in G\}.

(1)

Vectors that belong to
\mathcal{S}^{G}
are called
G
-symmetric vectors. If
V=\mathcal{H}
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then the set of
G
-symmetric density matrices is given by

\mathcal{S}^{G}_{\rho}\coloneqq\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\ |\ U(g)\rho U^{\dagger}(g),\ \forall g\in G\right\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the set of density operators acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

A stronger notion of symmetry for density operators, called
G
-Bose symmetry, is discussed at length in
[
12
]
, wherein a mixed state
\rho
is said to be
G
-Bose symmetric if

\rho=\Pi_{G}\rho\Pi_{G},

(3)

with the projector onto the
G
-Bose symmetric subspace defined as

\Pi_{G}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}U(g)\,.

(4)

The projection onto the symmetric subspace has been well studied and is of interest for many applications (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
). It should be noted for clarity that in this work we use the term “projector” to refer to the mathematical object above and “projection” to refer to the image of a quantum state after being acted on by such a projector.

It was shown that a test for
G
-Bose symmetry could be achieved using a generalized phase estimation algorithm, as proposed in
[
25
, Chapter 8]
, and Figure
1
depicts this algorithm. Since
G
-Bose symmetry is a strictly stronger notion than
G
-symmetry, any state exhibiting the former symmetry naturally demonstrates the latter symmetry as well.

Figure 1:
Figure demonstrating the generalized phase estimation applied to test for
G
-Bose-symmetry of a state, as in (

.

For the case where
G
is the symmetric group
S_{k}
on
k
letters, there is a natural representation
U(\sigma)
whose action on the tensor-power Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
is given by permuting the Hilbert spaces according to the permutation elements
\sigma
of
S_{k}
. The space of states that is invariant under this action is known as the symmetric subspace:

\mathcal{S}^{S_{k}}\coloneqq\{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}\ |\ U(\sigma)|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle,\ \forall\sigma\in S_{k}\}.

(5)

The projection onto
\mathcal{S}^{S_{k}}
is given by acting on a state with the projector

\Pi_{S_{k}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}U(\sigma)\,,

(6)

where, unless stated otherwise, the representation
\{U(\sigma)\}_{\sigma\in S_{k}}
of the symmetric group is generally taken to be the standard representation. Explicitly, we consider permutations
\sigma\in S_{k}
of the Hilbert space in such a manner that the action of
U(\sigma)
on a state
|\phi\rangle_{A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k}}
is realized as

U(\sigma)|\phi\rangle_{A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k}}=|\phi\rangle_{A_{\sigma(1)}A_{\sigma(2)}\cdots A_{\sigma(k)}}\,,

(7)

where
\{U(\sigma)\}_{\sigma\in S_{k}}
is a unitary representation of
S_{k}
in the same manner as those given in
[
26
]
. Equivalently,

U(\sigma)=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[d]}|i_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\ldots,i_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}\rangle\!\langle i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}|,

(8)

as defined in
[
24
, page 3]
.

Given the projector onto the symmetric subspace, Ref.
[
26
]
gave a construction to test whether an input state
\rho
obeys the
S_{k}
-Bose symmetry condition

\rho=\Pi_{S_{k}}\rho\Pi_{S_{k}}\,.

(9)

Using the fact that the symmetric group
S_{k}
can be generated by acting on the group
S_{k-1}
with all transpositions of the type
(i\ k)
, the entire symmetry test can be achieved using only transpositions and particular choices of ancilla qubits.

We will now briefly review the construction, but we urge readers to review the more detailed explanations given in
[
26
]
and
[
27
]
. The circuit for
S_{k}
is generated recursively given a circuit for
S_{k-1}
, and
S_{2}
can be constructed explicitly. Define a control state

|+_{S_{k}}\rangle_{C}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}|0\rangle^{\otimes j-1}\,,

(10)

where
A_{j}
is a unitary with the following action:

\displaystyle A_{j}|0\rangle^{\otimes j-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}|0\rangle^{\otimes j-1}+\sqrt{\frac{j-1}{j}}|W_{j-1}\rangle,

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}\left(|0\rangle^{\otimes j-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}|2^{i-1}\rangle\right),

(12)

and
|2^{i-1}\rangle
is the computational basis state with a one in the
i
-th position and zeros everywhere else. The
j
-th iteration controls an array of
j-1
C-SWAP gates corresponding to transpositions of the form
(i\ j)
, where
i
runs from
1
to
j-1
.

To see this, consider generating the test for
S_{3}
. Begin with the circuit for
S_{2}
, the base case, which is equivalent to the SWAP test between Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. The control state is then given by one qubit in the state

|+_{S_{2}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle+|1\rangle\right).

(13)

To create the test for
S_{3}
, we append to this a layer where
j-1=2
. Thus two ancilla qubits are necessary to control two new C-SWAP gates, and they are initialized in the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle\right),

(14)

for an overall control state of

|+_{S_{3}}\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle\right)\otimes|+_{S_{2}}\rangle\,.

(15)

Controlling from (
15
), if the additional two ancilla qubits are in the state
|01\rangle
, the second and third Hilbert spaces are swapped, realizing the transposition
(2\ 3)
. If two new ancilla qubits are in the state
|10\rangle
, the first and third Hilbert spaces are swapped, realizing
(1\ 3)
. As before,
|1\rangle
on the original ancilla realizes a swap between the first and second Hilbert spaces, or the transposition
(1\ 2)
. There is some freedom in choosing which transposition corresponds to which control states, but this general procedure reflects the construction given in Figure
2
and is discussed at length in
[
27
]
. With this recipe, the projector
\Pi_{S_{k}}
can be realized for every
k
using circuit depth
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
.

A_{j}
is given in (

). The symbol
\rho

qubits or systems.

II.2
Antisymmetric Projection

Let us now consider antisymmetry in terms of the standard representation of a group—or rather, in terms of permutations. Cayley’s theorem
[
28
]
states that any finite group
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of a permutation group, and so we can safely restrict ourselves to considering group elements as being isomorphic to permutations without loss of generality. An element
|\psi\rangle
in the appropriate vector space
V
is antisymmetric with respect to a representation
U
of a group
G
if, for every transposition
\sigma^{\prime}\in G
, we have that

U(\sigma^{\prime})|\psi\rangle=(-1)|\psi\rangle\,.

(16)

Equivalently, an antisymmetric vector
|\psi\rangle
is defined by

U(\sigma)|\psi\rangle=\text{sgn}(\sigma)|\psi\rangle\,,

(17)

where
\text{sgn}(\sigma)
denotes the sign of the permutation
\sigma
.

To model this action on quantum states, let us consider permutations of systems acting as in (
7
), and define single transpositions
\sigma_{ij}
that swap the
i
-th and
j
-th systems, and some multipartite state
\rho_{A_{1}\cdots A_{k}}
,
which is defined on a tensor-product Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
. Then we propose a test analogous to (
9
), wherein we define the antisymmetric projector

\Pi_{\textrm{anti}_{k}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)U(\sigma)\,,

(18)

where
\{U(\sigma)\}_{\sigma\in S_{k}}
is a unitary representation of
S_{k}
in the same manner as that given in
[
26
]
; specifically, we choose a unitary representation such that it obeys the desired group action described in (
7
).

Given this definition of antisymmetry, a corresponding algorithmic test can be distilled from that in
[
26
,
27
]
. The circuit construction is depicted in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Figure demonstrating how to systematically generate a test for antisymmetry. The definition of the unitaries
A_{j}
is given in (
11
), and
\overline{Z}_{n}
is defined in (

refers to a state on

qubits or systems.

As a reminder, the control state for that algorithm is

|+_{S_{k}}\rangle_{C}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}|0\rangle^{\otimes j-1}\,,

(19)

where the action of the unitary
A_{j}
is given in (
11
). Here we take the same approach shown in Figure
2
, but additionally perform the operation
\overline{Z}_{k-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\overline{Z}_{1}
after the C-SWAP gates.
The operation
\overline{Z}_{n}
is defined as follows:

\overline{Z}_{n}\coloneqq Z^{\otimes n},

(20)

where
n
is the number of qubits in the register. After this change, the effective controlled unitary in each instance is given by

\operatorname*{C-antiSWAP}({i,j})\coloneqq|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{C}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{i,j}\\
+|1\rangle\!\langle 1|_{C}\otimes(-1)U(\sigma_{i,j}),

(21)

where
C
is the specific qubit controlling the swap and
i,j
are the spaces to be swapped. Additionally,
U(\sigma_{i,j})
is a SWAP gate acting on the
i
th and
j
th registers. Replacing all of the controlled-SWAPs in the algorithm with
\operatorname*{C-antiSWAP}_{i,j}
gives an overall implementation of the antisymmetric projector in (
18
):

\langle+_{S_{k}}|_{C}\ \prod_{j=2}^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}\operatorname*{C-antiSWAP}(i,j)|+_{S_{k}}\rangle_{C}=\Pi_{\textrm{anti}_{k}}\,.

(22)

Therefore, a test of antisymmetry can be easily generated from a corresponding test of symmetry.

III
Concatenating Projectors

III.1
Uncomputing to Expand upon a Symmetry Test

The approach in Figure
1
can be expanded upon by means of uncomputation. Uncomputation refers to a technique commonly employed in quantum algorithms wherein a computation is performed in reverse after the desired output has been coherently stored
[
29
,
30
]
. Of course, as with many quantum algorithms, the control state is already undergoing some uncomputation. However, by reversing the action on the remaining systems, the outcome can be used to measure not only the projection onto the symmetric subspace but also the projection onto the asymmetric space as well.

The rough sketch of the algorithm goes as thus: perform a symmetry testing circuit on the state in question. Then, instead of performing the usual measurement, store the acceptance outcome in an additional ancilla by performing a CNOT controlled off of the all-zeros outcome. After this, uncompute the symmetry test by reversing the order of the gates in the original test and taking their conjugate transposes. If the new ancilla qubit is measured, the probability that we measure one is
\left\|\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}
and the probability to measure zero is
\left\|\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Furthermore, the post-measurement state will be proportional to
\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle
in the first case and to
\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle
in the second case. Therefore, this algorithm can measure the symmetric and asymmetric projections of a state using a single circuit and in a coherent manner.

We will now give a more thorough, mathematical description of the algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Given a unitary representation
\left\{U(g)\right\}_{g\in G}
of a group
G
, we can implement the group projection from (
4
) with the correct output probability distribution and post-measurement states, by performing the following steps:

1.

Prepare the state
|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|G\right|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}
.

2.

Perform the controlled unitary
\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}\otimes U(g)
from the control register to the data register.

3.

Prepare a control qubit in the state
|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}
and perform the controlled unitary

X_{C^{\prime}}\otimes|+_{G}\rangle\!\langle+_{G}|_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{C^{\prime}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{I}-|+_{G}\rangle\!\langle+_{G}|\right)_{C}.

(23)

4.

Perform the controlled unitary
\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}(g)
from the first control register
C
to the data register.

5.

Optional: discard the control register
C
.

6.

Measure the register
C^{\prime}
in the computational basis.

Figure 4:
Figure demonstrating the use of uncomputation to generalize the previous symmetry test. The gate
P
acts on the all-zeros state to create a superposition
|+_{G}\rangle
, and
\{U(g)\}_{g\in G}
denotes the unitary representation of
G
. The target qubit is measured to be in the
|0\rangle
state with probability
\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{G}\rho]
or in the
|1\rangle
state with probability
\operatorname{Tr}[(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G})\rho]
. Furthemore, the post-measurement state is proportional to
\Pi_{G}\rho\Pi_{G}
or
(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G})\rho(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G})
, depending on the measurement outcome.

Figure
4
depicts the approach. Following the procedure above, the initial state of the algorithm after step one is
|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle
where

|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}\,,

(24)

a choice of control state we will employ often. After step two, the overall state of the system is

\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|g\rangle_{C}U(g)|\psi\rangle\,.

(25)

Figure 5:
For convenience, we define a plus or minus controlled gate. The gate
P
prepares the state
|+_{G}\rangle
on
n
qubits; by convention, we say that applying a
Z
gate to each qubit of the
|+_{G}\rangle
state generates the
|-_{G}\rangle
state. The controlled gate flips the target qubit if a) the control is in the
|+_{G}\rangle
state or b) the control is in the
|-_{G}\rangle
state, respectively. The operation
\overline{Z}_{n}
is defined in (
20
).

Here, we depart from the procedure followed in
[
12
]
. Instead of measuring the control qubit after uncomputing, perform a controlled operation using the
|+_{G}\rangle
as a control state instead of a
|0\rangle
or
|1\rangle
instance. This change of control is shown in Figure
5
a), where we define a circuit diagram for use in future algorithms.
After the controlled unitary in step three, the state of the system is

\left[X_{C^{\prime}}\otimes|+_{G}\rangle\!\langle+_{G}|_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{C^{\prime}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{I}-|+_{G}\rangle\!\langle+_{G}|\right)_{C}\right]\ \times\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|g\rangle_{C}U(g)|\psi\rangle\,.

(26)

Before continuing, let us observe a few facts. First, building from the logic of the symmetry test depicted in Figure
1
, we know that

(\langle+_{G}|_{C}\otimes\mathbb{I})\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}\otimes U(g)=\Pi_{G}\,.

(27)

Indeed, the algorithm presented in
[
12
]
depends on this fact. Furthermore, since
\Pi_{G}
is a projector formed from the sum over the unitary representation, observe that

U(g)\Pi_{G}=U^{\dagger}(g)\Pi_{G}=\Pi_{G}\,.

(28)

Equipped with these facts, we can rewrite (
26
) as

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle-|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle\\
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|g\rangle_{C}U(g)|\psi\rangle\,.

(29)

From here, we progress to step four. Using the property in (
28
), the state of the system after step four is

\displaystyle|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle-|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}(\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle)+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G})|\psi\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle=|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\otimes\big[|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}(\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle)+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G})|\psi\rangle\big]\,.

(31)

Since the
C
register is in tensor product with the other registers, it can be discarded. We will elect to do so, in order to clean up notation. This gives the final state of the system:

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle\,.

(32)

From (
32
), we can clearly see that, if we measure register
C^{\prime}
in the standard basis, then we will detect
C^{\prime}
to be in the state
|1\rangle
with probability

\Pr(|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}})=\left\|\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(33)

with post-measurement state

\frac{\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle}{\left\|\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}}\,.

(34)

Furthermore, we will detect the state
|0\rangle
with probability

\Pr(|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}})=\left\|\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(35)

with post-measurement state

\frac{\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle}{\left\|\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}}\,.

(36)

The result in (
35
)–(
36
) can be understood as realizing the projection onto the asymmetric subspace. Thus, we have accomplished our task of generalizing the symmetric test to realize both projections simply by performing additional uncomputation. Previous implementations
[
12
]
of this type of symmetry test gave an estimate of the projection onto the symmetric subspace by measuring the ancilla qubit. By adding additional ancilla qubits to the control register and then acting on the data register with the Hermitian conjugate of the symmetry testing algorithms, we have created a way to realize either the symmetric and asymmetric projections probabilistically. Furthermore, we know that the post-measurement state will either be symmetric or asymmetric according to the outcome of the measurement.

III.2
Realizing the Antisymmetric Projection

A similar method can be employed for the antisymmetric test given in Section
II.2
, which gives analogous results. Define the antisymmetric projector for a group
G
as

\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{sgn}(g)U(g).

(37)

This can be understood as a specific symmetry where the representation is defined as
\{\operatorname{sgn}(g)U(g)\}_{g\in G}
, that is, a representation identical to that of the symmetric group up to the sign of the group element. While the sign of the group element may seem nebulously defined, for finite groups there is a clear meaning, as previously stated. Cayley’s theorem states that all groups are isomorphic to a subgroup of the full permutation group. The elements of a permutation group have a well-defined sign, as even permutations have sign
+1
and odd-order permutations have sign
-1
. Thus, to determine the sign of a group element, it suffices to determine the sign of its corresponding permutation under Cayley’s theorem.

For completeness, we now detail the procedure.

Algorithm 2

The algorithm consists of the following steps:

1.

Prepare the state
|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|G\right|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}
.

2.

Perform the controlled unitary
\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}\otimes U(g)
from the control register to the data register.

3.

Act on every control qubit, changing the sign of all odd-parity basis states to
-1
. This is equivalent to applying the unitary
\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{sgn}(g)|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}
.

4.

Prepare another control qubit in the state

|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}
and perform the controlled unitary

X_{C^{\prime}}\otimes|+_{G}\rangle\!\langle+_{G}|_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{C^{\prime}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{I}-|+_{G}\rangle\!\langle+_{G}|\right)_{C}.

(38)

5.

Act on the control qubits once more to change the sign of all odd-parity basis states (equivalent to applying the unitary
\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{sgn}(g)|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}
).

6.

Perform the controlled unitary
\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}(g)
from the control register to the data register.

7.

Optional: discard the control register
C
.

8.

Measure the register
C^{\prime}
in the computational basis.

The first two steps are identical to those used for the symmetric projection, and the state of the system afterward is given by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|g\rangle_{C}U(g)|\psi\rangle\,,

(39)

as before. Now, before moving on to the third step, define the state

|-_{G}\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{sgn}(g)|g\rangle\,.

(40)

It is easily checked that, for permutation groups,
\overline{Z}_{n}|+_{G}\rangle=|-_{G}\rangle
when the control state on
n
qubits is constructed in such a way that odd-parity basis states in the control are used to represent odd-parity permutations. As alluded to in Section
II.2
, any finite group can be thought of as a subgroup of a permutation group, and so this manner of creating
|-_{G}\rangle
from
|+_{G}\rangle
generalizes in principle. Indeed, this is a natural way of constructing the control register. If the identity group element
g=e
is associated to
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
for
n
ancilla qubits, then the first transposition
g=(1\ 2)
in
S_{2}
can be associated to
|0\rangle^{\otimes n-1}\otimes|1\rangle
, and so on for each additional transposition. This exactly follows the construction in Figure
2
.
Thus we can combine steps 3-5 into a single step where the effective controlled unitary is instead

X_{C^{\prime}}\otimes|-_{G}\rangle\!\langle-_{G}|_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{C^{\prime}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{I}-|-_{G}\rangle\!\langle-_{G}|\right)_{C}.

(41)

Note that this is completely equivalent to the
\operatorname*{C-antiSWAP}
construction used previously in Section
II.2
for the antisymmetry test; the Pauli-
Z
gates have merely been absorbed into the control state instead of the controlled unitary. Similar to how the
|+_{G}\rangle
state was used to alter a controlled-NOT gate, this state will also be used in that manner. This is depicted in Figure
5
b).

After applying the new controlled unitary as described above, the state of the system is given by

\left[X_{C^{\prime}}\otimes|-_{G}\rangle\!\langle-_{G}|_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{C^{\prime}}\otimes\left(\mathbb{I}-|-_{G}\rangle\!\langle-_{G}|\right)_{C}\right]\ \times\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|g\rangle_{C}U(g)|\psi\rangle,

(42)

which in turn simplifies to

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|-_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle-|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|-_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle+\\
|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}U(g)|\psi\rangle\,.

(43)

This simplification can be computed directly from the definition of the antisymmetric projector given in (
18
) and an analogous version of (
27
) with respect to the antisymmetric projector:

(\langle-_{G}|_{C}\otimes\mathbb{I})\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}\otimes U(g)=\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\,.

(44)

Next, we uncompute the data register via the controlled unitary
\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle\!\langle g|_{C}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}(g)
. The state is then given by

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{sgn}(g)|g\rangle U^{\dagger}(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle\\
-|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{sgn}(g)|g\rangle U^{\dagger}(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle\\
+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}|g\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle.

(45)

Now, using the fact that

\operatorname{sgn}(g)U(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}=\operatorname{sgn}(g)U^{\dagger}(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}=\Pi_{\mathcal{A}},

(46)

the state simplifies to

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}|+_{G}\rangle_{C}(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}})|\psi\rangle.

(47)

After this, we discard the control register
C
. Then the state of the system is

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}})|\psi\rangle\,.

(48)

We can now see that, if we measure register
C^{\prime}
in the standard basis, then we will detect
C^{\prime}
to be in the state
|1\rangle
with probability

\Pr(|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}})=\left\|\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(49)

and with post-measurement state

\frac{\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle}{\left\|\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

(50)

Furthermore, we detect the state
|0\rangle
with probability

\Pr(|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}})=\left\|\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\right)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(51)

and with post-measurement state

\frac{\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\right)|\psi\rangle}{\left\|\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\right)|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

(52)

III.3
Generalizing the Construction

Let us now attempt to further generalize our previous constructions. In the prior sections, we were able to realize projections of a state onto a symmetric subspace by uncomputing the circuit used to realize the projector; however, we can imagine using the same procedure to combine the action of two different group projectors. We will refer to this procedure throughout as concatenating the projectors, in reference to how the projectors are essentially performed in series within a single algorithm. The projectors should act on the same number of qubits, but a projector that acts on a smaller system can essentially be padded to a larger one via tensoring with the identity.

Suppose that we have a unitary representation
\{V(h)\}_{h\in H}
of another group
H
. We can then follow the exact procedure from Section
III.1
only replacing the superposition state with

|+_{H}\rangle_{C}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|H|}}\sum_{h\in H}|h\rangle_{C}\,.

(53)

Then the first controlled unitary, used in step two, becomes

\sum_{h\in H}|h\rangle\!\langle h|_{C}\otimes V(h)\,,

(54)

and the final controlled unitary, used in step four, becomes

\sum_{h\in H}|h\rangle\!\langle h|_{C}\otimes V^{\dagger}(h)\,.

(55)

Implementing these substitutions, the resultant state before measurement is

|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\Pi_{H}|\psi\rangle+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{H}\right)|\psi\rangle\,,

(56)

where the projection
\Pi_{H}
is defined as

\Pi_{H}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}V(h)\,.

(57)

Let us now consider cascading the two procedures. In this construction, depicted in Figure
6
, we first perform the approach for the group
G
and then follow it with the circuit corresponding to
H
. To achieve this, two ancilla qubits are necessary, which we denote
C^{\prime}_{1}
and
C^{\prime}_{2}
, respectively. (A note of caution: the group representations should act on Hilbert spaces of the same size, and if
|G|\neq|H|
, the control state on
C
will have to be discarded after the first algorithm and cannot be reused.) The transformation realized by this concatenation is

|\psi\rangle\to|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{1}}|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{2}}\Pi_{H}\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{1}}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{2}}\Pi_{H}\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle\\
+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{1}}|1\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{2}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{H}\right)\Pi_{G}|\psi\rangle\\
+|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{1}}|0\rangle_{C^{\prime}_{2}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{H}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{G}\right)|\psi\rangle\,.

(58)

Figure 6:
Quantum circuit demonstrating the concatenation of two projectors in sequence.
\{U(g)\}
is a unitary representation of one group and
\{V(h)\}
is a unitary representation of another. Both unitary representations should act on spaces of the same dimension.

III.4.3
Difference of Two Projectors

Consider a unitary that can be written as the difference of two projectors,
U=P-Q

P
and
Q

\mathbb{I}=P+Q

P=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+U)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[P\rho Q\rho]=\operatorname{Tr}[Q\rho P\rho],

(87)

\displaystyle p(011)-p(111)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[P\rho P\rho].

(88)

Next, we show that this is simply related to the quantity of interest:
\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Consider that

\left\|\operatorname{Re}(X)\right\|^{2}_{2}=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Tr}[X^{2}+XX^{\dagger}+X^{\dagger}X+(X^{\dagger})^{2}].

(89)

Substituting for
X
with
P\rho Q
, we find that

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big[\operatorname{Tr}[QP\rho QP\rho]+\operatorname{Tr}[PQ\rho PQ\rho]+2\operatorname{Tr}[P\rho Q\rho]\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[P\rho Q\rho],

(90)

where the second equality results from the fact that
PQ=QP=0
.
Combining both results, we see that

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(p(001)-p(101))

(91)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(p(010)-p(110)).

(92)

Thus, by repeating the circuit multiple times and building measurement statistics, we can estimate
\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Additionally, by collecting both the measurement results from (
86
) and (
87
), we can converge to the desired quantity of
\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2}
faster than if we were sampling only a single measurement outcome. In more detail, for all
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, let
Y_{t}
be a random variable that is set to
0
after obtaining the results 000, 100, 011, or 111,
+1
after obtaining the results 001 or 010, and
-1
after obtaining the results 101 or 110. Then, it follows from (
85
)–(
88
) and (
90
) that

\mu\equiv\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=2\operatorname{Tr}[P\rho Q\rho]=4\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(93)

so that
\overline{Y_{T}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}
is an unbiased estimator of
4\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P\rho Q)\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Set
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. The Hoeffding bound (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Lemma 1]
) implies that, if
T\geq\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
, then
\Pr[|\overline{Y_{T}}-\mu|\leq\varepsilon]\geq 1-\delta
.

Resolution of Identity

Consider the
r
-term resolution of identity, given by

\mathbb{I}=P_{0}+\cdots+P_{r-1},

(94)

where each
P_{i}
is a projector.
In this example, we provide an algorithm, and an antisymmetry perspective, to estimate terms of the form

\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P_{i}\rho P_{j})\right\|^{2}_{2}.

(95)

For
i\neq j
, this quantity measures the component of the state
\rho
in the off-block-diagonal subspaces. For example, using projectors onto subspaces of fixed Hamming weight, it was shown that quantitites of this form can be used to test if a state is invariant under collective phase rotations

[

.

Using a similar argument as in the previous section, it follows that, when

,

\left\|\operatorname{Re}(P_{i}\rho P_{j})\right\|^{2}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}\rho P_{j}\rho].

(96)

Define the following unitary
U

U\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\exp{\left(\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}j}{r}\right)}P_{j}.

(97)

We also define the following operation
\tilde{Z}

\tilde{Z}\coloneqq\sum_{c=0}^{r-1}\exp{\left(\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}c}{r}\right)}|c\rangle\!\langle c|.

(98)

IV.1
Estimating Schmidt Rank

The Schmidt rank of a pure state can be estimated by utilizing a projection onto the antisymmetric subspace
[
21
]
. In this section, we will briefly review Schmidt rank and the aforementioned result before giving example computations of this application.

The Schmidt decomposition of a bipartite pure state is a superposition of coordinated orthonormal states. We state the theorem more formally as follows.

Theorem IV.1
(Schmidt Decomposition)

Suppose that we have a bipartite pure state,

|\psi\rangle_{RS}\in\mathcal{H}_{R}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S},

(101)

where
\mathcal{H}_{R}
and
\mathcal{H}_{S}
are finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, not necessarily of the same dimension, and
\left\||\psi\rangle_{RS}\right\|_{2}=1
. Then it is possible to express this state as follows:

|\psi\rangle_{RS}=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}|v_{i}\rangle_{R}|w_{i}\rangle_{S},

(102)

where the amplitudes
\lambda_{i}
are real, strictly positive, and normalized so that
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=1
, the states
\{|v_{i}\rangle_{R}\}_{i=0}^{r-1}
form an orthonormal basis in system
R
, and the states
\{|w_{i}\rangle_{S}\}_{i=0}^{r-1}
form an orthonormal basis in system
S
. The Schmidt rank
r
of a bipartite state is equal to the number of Schmidt coefficients in its Schmidt decomposition; i.e.,
r=|\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{r-1}|
.

The Schmidt decomposition theorem applies to any bipartite cut of a pure quantum state, and the Schmidt rank
r
obeys the following inequality

r\leq\min\{\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{R}),\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\}.

(103)

It is well known that the Schmidt rank can be used to decide if a state is entangled. The Schmidt rank
r>1
if and only if the state is entangled. Another application of estimating the Schmidt rank, in the context of quantum machine learning, can found in
[
35
]
.

The result from
[
21
]
is as follows:

Theorem IV.2
(
[
21
]
)

Suppose
|\psi\rangle_{RS}\in\mathcal{H}_{R}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}
is a bipartite state vector. Then
\operatorname{SR}(|\psi\rangle)\leq r
if and only if

\left(\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}_{R,r+1}\otimes\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}_{S,r+1}\right)(|\psi\rangle_{RS}^{\otimes r+1})=0,

(104)

where
\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}_{R,r+1}
is the projection onto the antisymmetric subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes(r+1)}_{R}
(and similarly for the
S
system).

Using the antisymmetry test from Section
II.2
, we can construct a test for estimating the Schmidt rank by performing two antisymmetry tests in tandem. This process is shown in the schematic in Figure
10
, and we give a concrete construction for
r=2
in Figure

. For these algorithms, a non-zero probability of measuring the all-zeros (
|\vec{0}\rangle_{C}
) outcome indicates that the Schmidt rank of the state is strictly greater than
r
, i.e.,
\operatorname{SR}(|\psi\rangle)>r
.
Note that in both figures, the registers
R
and
S
can be multi-qubit registers. In that case, each qubit of the multi-qubit registers is individually swapped. The count of the number of qubits, two-qubit gates, and CSWAPs to compare the Schmidt rank of a bipartite

IV.2
Alternate Test for Werner States

A Werner state
[
36
]
exhibits a particular kind of symmetry called Werner invariance. Consider a state
\rho
on
N
qudits, and then define
\overline{\rho}
by

\overline{\rho}=\int(U)^{\otimes N}\rho(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes N}dU\,.

(105)

If a state
\rho
exhibits symmetry under a collective unitary action, then
\rho=\overline{\rho}
is called a Werner state. For instance, this is the case for a two-party state that satisfies the following:

\overline{\rho}=\int(U\otimes U)\rho_{AB}(U\otimes U)^{{\dagger}}dU\,.

(106)

As a special case of Schur–Weyl duality, we know that
\rho_{AB}=\overline{\rho}
in (
106
) if and only if

\rho_{AB}=p\frac{\Pi_{S}}{d(d+1)/2}+(1-p)\frac{\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}}{d(d-1)/2}\,,

(107)

where
p\in[0,1]
,
\Pi_{S}
is the projection onto the symmetric subspace, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is the projection onto the antisymmetric subspace. Specifically, we define
\Pi_{S}
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}
to be

\displaystyle\Pi_{S}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{I}_{AB}+F_{AB}}{2}\,,

(108)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{I}_{AB}-F_{AB}}{2}\,,

(109)

where
F_{AB}
is the unitary swap operator for systems
A
and
B
.

Since
\Pi_{S}+\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathbb{I}_{AB}
, it follows that a general state can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{AB}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{S}+\Pi_{\mathcal{A}})\sigma_{AB}(\Pi_{S}+\Pi_{\mathcal{A}})

(110)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{S}\sigma_{AB}\Pi_{S}+\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma_{AB}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma_{AB}\Pi_{S}+\Pi_{S}\sigma_{AB}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\,.

(111)

By comparison with (
107
), we see that a state
\sigma_{AB}
is a Werner state if and only if both of the cross terms
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma_{AB}\Pi_{S}
and
\Pi_{S}\sigma_{AB}\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}
are zero. Thus, it suffices to develop a quantum algorithm for estimating the norm of these matrix elements in order to perform Werner-state symmetry tests.

We can perform such a test by using the general approach developed in Example
III.4.3
. First, notice that
\operatorname{SWAP}=(\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}})
. Then let
U=\operatorname{SWAP}
. By measuring the control state as described therein, we obtain an estimate of
\left\|\operatorname{Re}(\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}\rho\Pi_{\mathcal{A}})\right\|_{2}^{2}
. This is depicted in Figure
15
.

A simple example of a Werner state is the singlet state

|\psi^{-}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|01\rangle-|10\rangle\right)\,.

(112)

Indeed, every pure Werner state is equal to a linear combination of singlets
[
37
, Theorem 1]
.
For example, consider the “pizza” state for six qubits given in
[
38
, Figure 1]
:

|P\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{8}}\Big(|000111\rangle-|111000\rangle-|001110\rangle\\
+|110001\rangle-|010101\rangle+|101010\rangle\\
+|011100\rangle-|100011\rangle\Big)\,.

(113)

Another example of a two-qubit Werner state is achieved via a convex combination of the maximally-mixed state and a Bell state,

W(p)=\frac{p}{6}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{p}{3}&0&0&0\\

IV.3
Estimating Commutators

Commutators play an important role in quantum mechanics, especially in quantifying uncertainty when measuring two observables. We present here two different methods for estimating a commutator and a nested commutator. We show how the antisymmetric circuit structure can be easily adapted to realize a quantum switch between operators, as described in
[
39
,
40
,
41
]
.

We begin by considering the commutator between two unitaries
A
and
B
, which for now we suppose have the same dimensions. Figure
16
depicts a quantum circuit to obtain this quantity. First, consider an input state
|0\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle
. After acting on the control qubit with a Hadamard and then a Pauli-
Z
gate, the state of the system is

|-\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle-|1\rangle_{C}|\psi\rangle)\,.

(115)

Next, conditioned on the control qubit, act on the system with the operators
AB
if in the state
|0\rangle_{C}
or with operators
BA
is the control is in the state
|1\rangle
. Performing this sequence of controls leads to the overall state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle_{C}AB|\psi\rangle-|1\rangle_{C}BA|\psi\rangle)\,.

(116)

After a second Hadamard on the control register, this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle_{C}AB|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle_{C}AB|\psi\rangle-|0\rangle_{C}BA|\psi\rangle)+|1\rangle_{C}BA|\psi\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[|0\rangle_{C}\otimes(AB-BA)|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle_{C}\otimes(AB+BA)|\psi\rangle)]

(117)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[|0\rangle_{C}[A,B]|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle_{C}\{A,B\}|\psi\rangle]\,,

(118)

where
\{A,B\}
denotes the anticommutator of
A
and
B
. Given this final state, we measure the control qubit to be in the state
|0\rangle_{C}
with probability

P(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{C})=\frac{1}{4}\left\|[A,B]|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(119)

In this manner, we have effectively reproduced Equations (5)–(8) of
[
41
]
.

Figure 16:
Figure demonstrating how to measure the commutator of two unitary operators
A
and
B
. The probability to measure
|0\rangle
on the control state is
\frac{1}{4}\left\|[A,B]|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}

However, in principle there could be higher order, or nested, commutators to consider. We can determine the sum of the nested commutators between two operators
A
and
B
via the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula. For some elements
X
and
Y
in the Lie algebra of a Lie group, BCH (specifically, an 1897 lemma of the famous theorem) tells us that

e^{X}Ye^{-X}=e^{\operatorname{ad}_{X}}Y=Y+[X,Y]+\frac{1}{2!}[X,[X,Y]]+\cdots\,,

(120)

where
\operatorname{ad}_{X}
is the adjoint endomorphism defined to act as
\operatorname{ad}_{X}Y=[X,Y]
.

Figure 17:
Figure demonstrating how to measure the commutator of two operators
A
and
B
. This circuit uses the BCH formula to calculate the full sum of nested commutations.

Suppose now that we have an exponentiated unitary
U=e^{iA}
. In this context,
A
is essentially a Hamiltonian, and the unitary
U
can be realized via Hamiltonian simulation methods
[
42
]
. For particular choices of the Hamiltonian
A
, the unitary
U
can be efficiently realized on a quantum computer.
While every unitary can be written in this form via Stone’s theorem
[
43
]
, for practical purposes, it is best to choose
A
such that
U
can be implemented efficiently on a quantum computer. We can use (
120
) to calculate the sum of the nested commutators. Observe that

\displaystyle e^{iA}Be^{-iA}-B

\displaystyle=i[A,B]+\frac{-1}{2!}[A,[A,B]]+\cdots

(121)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\operatorname{ad}_{iA})^{n}B.

Realizing this quantity merely employs a rewriting of the previous algorithm used for the commutator. Replace the operator
AB
with
e^{iA}Be^{-iA}
and
BA
with
B
, as shown in Figure
17
. Then, follow the same procedure as below; only now, the final state of the system before the measurement is

\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle_{C}(e^{iA}Be^{-iA}-B)|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle_{C}(e^{iA}Be^{-iA}+B)|\psi\rangle)\\
=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|0\rangle_{C}(\operatorname{ad}_{iA})^{n}B|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle_{C}(e^{iA}Be^{-iA}+B)|\psi\rangle\right)\,.

(122)

From this final state, we measure the control qubit to be in the state
|0\rangle_{C}
with probability

P(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{C})=\frac{1}{4}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\operatorname{ad}_{iA})^{n}B|\psi\rangle\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(123)

Both the probabilities in (
119
) and (
123
) above depend on the input state
|\psi\rangle
. By choosing to replace
|\psi\rangle
with the maximally-mixed state, we can obtain an estimation of both the commutator and the sum of nested commutators respectively as

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{4d}\left\|[A,B]\right\|_{2}^{2}\,

(124)

and

P=\frac{1}{4d}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\operatorname{ad}_{iA})^{n}B\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(125)

where
d
is the dimension of the input state. However, by instead maximizing all possible input states
|\psi\rangle
, we instead obtain

P=\frac{1}{4}\left\|[A,B]\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\,

(126)

for the single commutator estimation and

P=\frac{1}{4}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\operatorname{ad}_{iA})^{n}B\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\,,

(127)

for the algorithm in which we consider the sum of the nested commutators of
iA
and
B
.

Thus, via this simple application of an asymmetric circuit we have demonstrated methods by which the commutator of two operators can be computed.

IV.4
Brief Comments on Schur Sampling

A pointed observation of our procedure for concatenating projectors, particularly projectors onto irreps of the symmetric group as in Section
III.4.2
, might naturally lead to the question of how this procedure compares to Schur sampling. Schur sampling refers to the task of measuring the Schur basis labels of a
n
-qubit (or, in principle, qudit) system
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
. This task is often accomplished by first implementing the quantum Schur transform
[
47
,
25
]
. The Schur basis labels are typically given in a triple
(\lambda,p_{\lambda},q_{\lambda})
, which denote the Young label and bases of the irreducible representations given by Schur–Weyl duality. Determining the values of each of these labels is called strong Schur sampling, while the task of only determining the Young label is called weak Schur sampling
[
46
]
. For more information on the quantum Schur transform and the related tasks of Schur sampling, see Refs.
[
47
,
25
]
.

Much previous literature has been devoted to implementing the quantum Schur transform and measuring Young labels.
The seminal work of
[
47
]
realized the quantum Schur transform in
O(\operatorname{poly}(n,d,\log(1/\varepsilon)))
operations, with
d
being the dimension of a qudit,
\varepsilon
the allowable error, and
n
is index of the symmetric group
S_{n}
. Similarly, more recent scholarship affirms that high-dimensional quantum Schur transforms can be implemented in
O(\operatorname{poly}(n,\log(1/\varepsilon))
operations, and an explicit gate count of
O(n^{4}\log(n/\varepsilon))
was given in
[
44
]
for the inverse Schur transform. These approaches all use
O(n)
qubits. A technique for weak Schur sampling can estimate Young labels using logarithmically many qubits and
O(n^{3}\log(n/\varepsilon))
gates
[
46
]
. So how would an approach based on our concatenated circuits fair in comparison?

The natural procedure for Schur sampling based on the concatenated projector procedure given in Section
III
would be to implement a projector onto each of the
r
irreps of
S_{n}
in turn and measure the projection of a state on that system. The number of qubits necessary for this task would require at least
n
from the data register and an additional
n^{2}+r
ancillary qubits, based on the circuit construction for the symmetric group given in
[
12
]
, and
r-1
qubits to store each projection measurement outcome. The reliance on ancillary qubits could in principle be reduced, as the optimal implementation based on group size would be
\log_{d}(|S_{n}|)=\log_{d}(n!)
, where
d
is again the dimension of a qudit. For sufficiently large
n
, the Stirling approximation gives that this scales asymptotically as
O(n\log(n))
with the size of the group. As such, the number of qubits required goes at best as
O(n\log(n)+n+r)
.
For the construction used in this work, each transposition is realized by either a controlled-SWAP gate or such a gate with an additional Pauli-
Z
acting on the ancilla. Each of these gates requires five to seven elementary gates from the Clifford+
T
gate set to be realized
[
46
]
if not native. The symmetric test for
S_{n}
uses
O(n^{2})
such gates. Assuming each irrep projector scales similarly, a procedure for testing each of these projections in sequence for
r
projectors would scale as
O(5rn^{2})
elementary gates. The number of irreps of
S^{n}
corresponds directly to the partitions of
n
, which itself grows exponentially with respect to
\sqrt{n}

[
48
,
49
]
. As such, alternate approaches would likely be preferable to reduce necessary resources. Indeed, the authors of
[
47
]
predicted just such an inefficiency, and instead achieve better results by employing Clebsch–Gordon transforms recursively.

However, our procedure does offer a benefit in an important regard. If only the projections onto the symmetric and antisymmetric spaces are desired, all other projections can be grouped together into a collective result. In this case, the algorithm described in Example
III.4.2
will suffice. That procedure would scale primarily with the size of the symmetric and antisymmetric tests, thus requiring around
O(2n^{2})
operations to implement.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have provided a variety of procedures for realizing projectors in a quantum circuit, with a view towards combining symmetry, asymmetry, and antisymmetry. We have introduced an algorithm to realize the antisymmetric projector and demonstrated how an antisymmetrized circuit can be used to test for relevant physical phenomenon such as Schmidt rank, Werner invariance, or to realize a quantum switch. For the Schmidt-rank and Werner-state tests, we have given experimental results for various examples with small-dimensional systems, using currently available quantum hardware.

Some open questions remain. First and foremost, it remains to be seen whether this general procedure for concatenating projectors can be used to realize a Schur transform or a Schur sampling algorithm in the manner of
[
47
,
25
,
46
]
. The naïve approach presented here would scale poorly with the size of the symmetric group. Previous literature utilized a recursive structure to simplify the implementation of this task to scale reasonably on a quantum computer. A natural next step would be to combine the recursive structure used in
[
47
]
with the concatenation procedure given here to see if a less resource-intensive algorithm would result.

Another open question is whether these algorithms can be utilized to efficiently prepare the antisymmetric state. The natural approach of utilizing the maximally-mixed state as input to an antisymmetry test and then post-selecting for successful states would result in an exponentially low acceptance probability with respect to the number of qubits in said state. If such an algorithm cannot be devised to run efficiently, the natural alternative question is whether there exists an efficiently preparable state such that the acceptance probability is appreciably large.

Both of these questions rely on reasonable scaling of resources in terms of gates and qubits and are each worthy of further study.
