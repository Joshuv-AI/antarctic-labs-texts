Revisited Containment for Graph Patterns
Houari Mahfoud

Abstract
We consider the class of conditional graph patterns ( CGPs ) that allow user to query data graphs with complex patterns that contain negation and predicates. To overcome the prohibitive cost of subgraph isomorphism, we consider matching of CGPs under simulation semantics which can be conducted in quadratic time. In emerging applications, one would like to reduce more this matching time, and the static analysis of patterns may allow ensuring part of this reduction. We study the containment problem of CGPs that aims to check whether the matches of some pattern P 1 P_{1} , over any data graph, are contained in those of another pattern P 2 P_{2} (written P 1 ⊑ P 2 P_{1}\sqsubseteq P_{2} ). The optimization process consists to extract matches of P 1 P_{1} only from those of P 2 P_{2} without querying the (possibly large) data graph. We show that the traditional semantics of containment is decidable in quadratic time, but it fails to meet the optimization goal in the presence of negation and predicates. To overcome this limit, we propose a new semantics of containment, called strong containment , that is more suitable for CGPs and allows to reduce their matching time. We show that strong containment can be decided in cubic time by providing such an algorithm. We are planing to use results of this paper to answer CGPs using views. This paper is part of an ongoing project that aims to design a caching system for complex graph patterns.

Introduction

Given a data graph
G
and a graph pattern
P
, graph pattern matching (
GPM
) is to find all subgraphs of
G
that match
P
. Matching is traditionally expressed in terms of subgraph isomorphism which is cost prohibitive. To avoid this cost, graph simulation
[
16
]
and its extensions
[
3
,
2
,
11
]
have been proposed that allow
GPM
to be approximately conducted in polynomial time. However, existing simulation-based
GPM
consider very simple patterns which do not meet requirements of real-life applications. To overcome this limit, we proposed in
[
14
]
conditional graph patterns (
CGPs
) which allow to query data graphs with complex features like quantifications, predicates and negation.
Our goal is to study the containment problem of
CGPs
in order to reduce their matching time. Given two
CGPs

C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, it is to decide whether the matches of
C_{1}
over any data graph are all contained in those of
C_{2}
. If such is the case, then one can reduce matching time of
C_{1}
by extracting its matches from those of
C_{2}
. The containment problem has been widely studied for different query languages (e.g. relational queries
[
18
]
, XPath queries
[
17
]
, reachability queries
[
10
]
). When it comes to graph patterns however, the containment problem has not received sufficient attention since it has been studied by only few works that have considered only simple patterns (e.g.
[
2
]
). Moreover, its decision problem has been studied under a traditional semantics which does not apply for complex patterns and also hinders the realization of some tasks. We explain these limits by the next example.

Related Work.
The pattern containment is a classical and fundamental problem for any query language. It has been well studied for relational queries (e.g.
[
18
]
) and XML queries (e.g.,
[
8
,
17
]
).
For graph patterns however, it is striking how little attention has been paid for this problem. The containment problem has been studied in
[
2
]
for graph patterns without neither predicates nor negation, which makes the task less intriguing.
Moreover, the problem has been studied in
[
10
]
for graph reachability queries with memory which is not closer to our work. Apart from these works, we are not aware of other ones that study the containment problem in case of complex patterns.

Preliminaries

2.1
Data Graphs and Graph Patterns

Data Graphs.

A data graph is a directed graph
G
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A}
) where:
1)
V
is a finite set of nodes; 2)
E\subseteq V\times V
is a finite set of edges in which
(v,v^{{}^{\prime}})
denotes an edge from node
v
to
v^{{}^{\prime}}
; 3)
\mathcal{L}
is a function that assigns a label
\mathcal{L}(v)
(resp.
\mathcal{L}(e)
) to each node
v\in V
(resp. edge
e\in E
); and 4) for each node
v\in V
,
\mathcal{A}(v)
is a tuple
(A_{1}=c_{1},\dots,A_{n}=c_{n})
where:
A_{i}
is an attribute of
v
,
c_{i}
is a constant value,
n\geq 0
, and
A_{i}\neq A_{j}
if
i\neq j
. We denote by
v.A_{i}
the value of attribute
A_{i}
on node
v
(i.e. value
c_{i}
).

Intuitively, the label of a node represents an entity (e.g.
Movie
,
Person
) while the label of an edge represents a relationship (e.g.
Person\xrightarrow{producedBy}Movie
,
Person\xrightarrow{isFriend}Person
). Moreover, the function
\mathcal{A}
defines properties over nodes (e.g.
title
and
release date
of movies).

Subgraphs.

Given a data graph
G
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A}
), a subgraph
G_{s}
=(
V_{s},E_{s},\mathcal{L}_{s},\mathcal{A}_{s}
) of
G
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A}
) must satisfy: 1)
V_{s}\!\subseteq\!V
; 2)
E_{s}\!\subseteq\!E
; 3)
\mathcal{L}_{s}(x)\!=\!\mathcal{L}(x)
for each
x\in E_{s}\!\cup\!V_{s}
; and 4)
\mathcal{A}_{s}(v)\!=\!\mathcal{A}(v)
for each
v\in V_{s}
.

Conventional Graphs Patterns.

A graph pattern is a directed connected graph
P
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A}
) where: 1)
V
,
E
, and
\mathcal{L}
are defined as for data graphs; and 2) for each node
u\in V
,
\mathcal{A}(u)
is a predicate defined as a conjunction of atomic formulas of the form “
A

op

c
” where:
A
is an attribute of
u
,
c
is a constant, and
op\in\{\geq,\leq,=,\neq\}
. Intuitively,
\mathcal{A}(u)
specifies a search condition on a node
u
.

Paths.
An
undirected path

\textlangle e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\textrangle
in
P
is a sequence of edges of
E
where: for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
e_{i}
intersects with
e_{i+1}
in some node. For instance,
\textlangle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\textrangle
with
e_{1}=a\rightarrow b
,
e_{2}=c\rightarrow b
and
e_{3}=d\rightarrow c
is an undirected path.

2.2
Conventional Graph Pattern Matching

We refer hereafter to the data graph
G
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A}
) and the conventional graph pattern
P
=(
V_{{}_{P}},E_{{}_{P}},\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}},\mathcal{A}_{{}_{P}}
). We next revise definitions of these three graph pattern matching models.

Attribute Constraints Matching.

Given a pattern node
u\in V_{P}
and a data node
v\in V
, we say that attributes values of
v
match attributes constraints of
u
, written
\mathcal{A}(v)\sim\mathcal{A}_{P}(u)
, if and only if: for each atomic formula “
A~op~c
” in
\mathcal{A}_{{}_{P}}(u)
, there exists
A=c^{{}^{\prime}}
in
\mathcal{A}(v)
where
c^{{}^{\prime}}~op~c
holds.

Subgraph Isomorphism.

A subgraph
G_{s}
=(
V_{s},E_{s},\mathcal{L}_{s},\mathcal{A}_{s}
) of
G
matches
P
via
subgraph isomorphism
if there exists a
bijective function

f
:
V_{{}_{P}}\rightarrow V_{s}
s.t.:
1
) for each
u\in V_{{}_{P}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}}(u)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(f(u))
and
\mathcal{A}_{s}(f(u))\sim\mathcal{A}_{{}_{P}}(u)
;
2
) for each
e_{u}=(u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
in
E_{P}
, there exists an edge
e_{s}=(f(u),f(u^{{}^{\prime}}))
in
G_{s}
with
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}}(e_{u})=\mathcal{L}_{s}(e_{s})
.

Graph Simulation.

G
matches
P
via
graph simulation
if there exists a
binary match relation

S\subseteq V_{{}_{P}}\times V
s.t.:

1
) for each
(u,v)\in S
,
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}}(u)=\mathcal{L}(v)
and
\mathcal{A}(v)\sim\mathcal{A}_{{}_{P}}(u)
;
2
) for each
(u,v)\in S
and each edge
e_{u}=(u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
in
E_{{}_{P}}
, there exists an edge
e_{v}=(v,v^{{}^{\prime}})
in
E
with
(u^{{}^{\prime}},v^{{}^{\prime}})\in S
and
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}}(e_{u})=\mathcal{L}(e_{v})
; and
3
) for each
u\in V_{{}_{P}}
, there is at least one node
v\in V
with
(u,v)\in S
.

By condition (2), graph simulation preserves only child relationships.

Dual Simulation.

G
matches
P
via
dual simulation
if there exists a
binary match relation

S_{D}\subseteq V_{{}_{P}}\times V
s.t.:

1
) for each
(u,v)\in S
,
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}}(u)=\mathcal{L}(v)
and
\mathcal{A}(v)\sim\mathcal{A}_{{}_{P}}(u)
;
2
) for each
(u,v)\in S
and each edge
e_{u}=(u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
(resp.
e_{u}=(u^{{}^{\prime}},u)
) in
E_{{}_{P}}
, there exists an edge
e_{v}=(v,v^{{}^{\prime}})
(resp.
e_{v}=(v^{{}^{\prime}},v)
) in
E
with
(u^{{}^{\prime}},v^{{}^{\prime}})\in S
and
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{P}}(e_{u})=\mathcal{L}(e_{v})
; and
3
) for each node
u\in V_{{}_{P}}
, there exists at least one node
v\in V
with
(u,v)\in S
.
2
2

2

By condition (2), dual simulation preserves both child and parent relationships.

Conditional Graph Patterns (
CGPs
)

3.1
Definition of
CGPs

We start by extending conventional patterns with simple
counting quantifiers (CQs)
, which leads to
quantified graph patterns (QGPs)
.

Definition 1

A
QGP
is a connected directed graph
Q
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}
) where: 1)
V
,
E
,
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{A}
are
defined as for conventional graph patterns; and 2) for each edge
e\in E
,
\mathcal{C}(e)
is a
CQ
given by an integer
p
(
p\geq 1
).
\Square

Intuitively, for any data graph
G
and any edge
e=(u,u^{\prime})
in
Q
with a
CQ

\mathcal{C}(e)=p
, a data node
v
in
G
matches
u
if it has at least
p

u^{\prime}
, and moreover, these children must be reached from
v
via an edge labeled
\mathcal{L}(e)
. We omit
CQs
that are equal to
1
for more readability. Matching
Q
over
G
consists to find all subgraphs of
G
that match the structure (i.e. nodes and edges relationships) and constraints (i.e. labeling, attributes and
CQs
) of
Q
. We denote this match result by
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{Q}}
and we report its definition to the next subsection.

Along the same lines as
[
5
]
, we define a special form of
QGPs
by considering the
query focus
.

Definition 2

We denote by
Q(u)
a QGP
Q
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}
) with a
query focus

u\in V
used for search intent. Indeed, the result of
Q(u)
over any data graph
G
is a nodes set, extracted from
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{Q}}
, that contains all data nodes in
G
that match
u
.
\Square

Using
QGPs
(general and special forms) as building blocks, we next define a new class of graph patterns called
conditional graph patterns (CGPs)
.

Definition 3

A
CGP
is a connected directed graph
C
=(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}^{+},\mathcal{P}^{-}
) where:

1.

(
V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C}
) is a QGP called the
core
of
C
; and

2.

\mathcal{P}^{+}
(resp.
\mathcal{P}^{-}
) is a set of positive (resp. negative) predicates defined over nodes in
V
such that:

(a)

each
p^{+}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
(resp.
p^{-}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{-}
) is a QGP that defines a positive (resp. negative) predicate with the query focus
u\in V
; and

(b)

p^{+}(u)
(resp.
p^{-}(u)
) intersects with
V
only on the node
u
.
3
3

3

This may not reduce the practicability of our approach since many query languages (e.g. XPath, SQL) adopt this syntax of predicates.

\Square

Remark that
CGPs
extend
QGPs
by incorporating two (possibly empty) sets of positive and negative predicates,
\mathcal{P}^{+}
and
\mathcal{P}^{-}
. Contrary to conventional patterns where conditions are expressed only in terms of attributes, our syntax allows the definition of a
quantified and attributed graph-based condition
. A
core node
(resp.
core edge
) is any node (resp. edge) that belongs to the core of
C
, i.e. the set
V
(resp.
E
). Moreover, a
predicate node
(resp.
predicate edge
) is any node (resp. edge) that belongs to some predicate in
\mathcal{P}^{+}\cup\mathcal{P}^{-}
.

The semantic of a
CGP

C
is stated as follows. A data graph
G
matches
C
if and only if: 1) it has a subgraph
G_{s}
that matches the core of
C
; and 2) for any node
u\in V
and any predicate
p^{+}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
(resp.
p^{-}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{-}
), a node
v
in
G_{s}
is a match of
u
if it belongs (resp. does not belong) to the nodes set returned by
p^{+}(u)
(resp.
p^{-}(u)
) over
G
.

Inspired by well-known conditional languages (e.g. SQL, XPath), the core of a
CGP

C
represents the structure of the match result that will be returned to the user, while predicates in
\mathcal{P}^{+}\cup\mathcal{P}^{-}
are used only during the matching process to refine this result. To our knowledge, the expressivity given by our
CGPs
is not covered by any approach in the literature (e.g
[
11
,
2
,
5
,
3
]
).

3.2
Conditional Graph Pattern Matching

In order to overcomes the prohibitive cost of isomorphism, we propose to match
CGPs
via an extension of graph simulation, called
conditional simulation
.

Definition 5

A data graph
G=(V,E,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A})
matches a
CGP

C=(V_{{}_{C}},E_{{}_{C}},\mathcal{L}_{{}_{C}},\mathcal{A}_{{}_{C}},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}^{+},\mathcal{P}^{-})
via
conditional simulation
, denoted by
C\prec_{{}_{c}}G
, if there exists a binary match relation
S^{G}_{{}_{C}}\subseteq V_{{}_{C}}\times V
s.t.:

1.

For each
(u,v)\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
:
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{C}}(u)
=
\mathcal{L}(v)
and
\mathcal{A}(v)\sim\mathcal{A}_{{}_{C}}(u)
.

2.

For each
(u,v)\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
and each
e_{u}=(u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\in E_{{}_{C}}
with
\mathcal{C}(e_{u})
=
n
, there are at least
n
edges
e_{1}
=
(v,v_{1}),\dots,e_{n}
=
(v,v_{n})
in
E
s.t:
\mathcal{L}(e_{i})
=
\mathcal{L}_{{}_{C}}(e_{u})
and
(u^{{}^{\prime}},v_{i})\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
for
i\in[1,n]
.

3.

For each
(u,v)\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
and each edge
e_{u}=(u^{{}^{\prime}},u)
in
E_{{}_{C}}
, there is at least one edge
e_{v}=(v^{{}^{\prime}},v)
in
E
s.t:
\mathcal{L}(e_{v})=\mathcal{L}_{{}_{C}}(e_{u})
and
(u^{{}^{\prime}},v^{{}^{\prime}})\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
.

4.

For each
(u,v)\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
and each positive predicate
p^{+}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
, there is a subgraph
G_{s}\subseteq G
s.t.:
p^{+}(u)\prec_{{}_{c}}G_{s}
with a match relation
S
; and
(u,v)\in S
.

5.

For each
(u,v)\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
and each
p^{-}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{-}
, there is no subgraph
G_{s}\subseteq G
s.t.:
p^{-}(u)\prec_{{}_{c}}G_{s}
with a match relation
S
; and
(u,v)\in S
.

6.

Each node
u\!\in\!V_{{}_{C}}
has at least one match
(u,v)\!\in\!S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
.
\Square

Conditional simulation extends dual simulation by condition (2), in order to consider simple
CQs
on core edges of
C
; and by conditions (4-5) to deal with predicates defined over core nodes of
C
. Since a predicate
p^{+}(u)
(resp.
p^{-}(u)
) is a
QGP
, i.e. a
CGP
with no predicate, then one can check whether
p^{+}(u)\prec_{{}_{c}}G_{s}
(resp.
p^{-}(u)\prec_{{}_{c}}G_{s}
) by considering only conditions (1–3,6) of Def.
5
.

When
C\prec_{{}_{c}}G
, there exists a unique maximum match relation
S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
in

G
for
C

[
14
]
. We derive from
S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
the function
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C}}
, called the
match result
of
C
in
G
, where: for any core node
u\in V_{{}_{C}}
,
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C}}(u)=\{v\in V\backslash(u,v)\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}}\}
; and moreover, for any core edge
e_{u}=(u,u^{\prime})\in E_{{}_{C}}
,
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C}}(e_{u})=\{e_{v}=(v,v^{\prime})\in E\backslash\{(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\}\in S^{G}_{{}_{C}},~and~\mathcal{L}(e_{v})=\mathcal{L}_{{}_{C}}(e_{u})\}
.

We have shown in
[
14
]
that the checking of
C\prec_{{}_{c}}G
as well as the computation of the maximum match relation
S^{G}_{{}_{C}}
can be done in quadratic time. We proposed in
[
15
]
two techniques to reduce matching time of
CGPs
.

4
Pattern-Only Matching

We introduce the notion of
pattern-only matching
that aims to check, for two graph patterns
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, whether
Q_{1}
matches all constraints of
Q_{2}
.

Definition 6

Given two QGPs

6

The main result of this section is a new semantics for containment, called

6.1
Predicates Evaluability

Given two
CGPs

C_{i\in[1,2]}
such that
C_{{}_{1}}\sqsubseteq C_{{}_{2}}
via the mapping

for any core node

and any data graph

in presence of predicates. To decide statically whether such extraction is possible, we introduce the notion of

Definition 10

Given two CGPs

if, for any data graph
G
and any data node

v
satisfies

.

The intersection is due to the result of Proposition
1
. Obviously, the

aims to check whether the potential match set of

.

The semantics of

is quite simple, but its implementation is not trivial and requires to introduce two more notions,
refinement
and

Based on the notion of

Definition 12

For any CGPs

extends the traditional containment by condition (2) to check whether

can be extracted from

over any data graph
G
.

7
Matching
CGPs
via
Strong Containment

The main result of this Section is as follows:

Theorem 7.1

For any two CGPs
C_{i\in[1,2]}
and any data graph
G
, if
C_{{}_{1}}\sqsubseteq_{s}C_{{}_{2}}
then
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{1}}}}
can be computed in
O(|C_{{}_{1}}|+|\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{2}}}}|)
time without accessing
G
at all.
\Square

When a direct matching of
C_{{}_{1}}
over
G
is in
O(|C_{{}_{1}}|.|G|)
time
[
14
,
15
]
,
strong containment
allows to reduce this time, if
C_{{}_{1}}\sqsubseteq_{s}C_{{}_{2}}
, by accessing only the match result of
C_{{}_{2}}
over
G
which is much smaller than
G
. We denote by
|\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{2}}}}|
the number of data nodes and data edges in
G
that match nodes and edges of
C_{{}_{2}}
respectively. Indeed,
|\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{2}}}}|
is at most
|V_{{}_{2}}|.|V|+|E_{{}_{2}}|.|E|
but it is much smaller than
|G|
(i.e.
|V|+|E|
) in practice.

One can provide a special matching algorithm to prove this Theorem. The idea of this algorithm is quite simple. Consider that
C_{{}_{1}}\sqsubseteq_{s}C_{{}_{2}}
via the mapping
\lambda
. For any core node
u\in V_{{}_{1}}
, a set of its potential matches
S(u)
is initialized to be
\bigcap_{u^{\prime}\in\lambda(u)}\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{2}}}}(u^{\prime})
. This set is refined later using the constraints in
C_{{}_{1}}
(i.e. attributes constraints, labeling and
CQs
) as well as the relations
\mathcal{R}^{+}
and
\mathcal{R}^{-}
. For instance, for any
v\in S(u)
and any negative predicate
p^{-}(u)\in\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{1}}
that is covered by
\mathcal{R}^{-}
(i.e. evaluable by refinement), if there exists a subgraph
G_{s}(v)
in
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{2}}}}
that matches
p^{-}(u)
then
S(u)
is refined by removing
v
from it. Moreover, for any
v\in S(u)
and any part of a positive predicate, let’s be
p^{+}(u)
, that is covered by
\mathcal{R}^{+}
(i.e. evaluable by refinement), if there is no subgraph
G_{s}(v)
in
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{2}}}}
that matches
p^{+}(u)
then
v
is removed from
S(u)
. After the refinement process, if for some core node
u
in
V_{{}_{1}}
, we get
S(u)=\emptyset
then
C_{{}_{1}}\not\prec_{{}_{c}}G
and
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{1}}}}
is empty. Otherwise,
\mathcal{M}^{G}_{{}_{C_{{}_{1}}}}
can be extracted easily from the refined version of
S
.

8
Conclusion

We started by studying the application of the traditional containment for
CGPs
, we discussed its complexity time, and we showed that its classical semantics allows to do
sub-matching/over-matching
of
CGPs
but not necessarily exact matching. To overcome this limit, we proposed
strong containment
that takes in account the semantics of predicates and negation and allows to optimize well matching time of
CGPs
. We showed that the new semantics does not come with a higher price since it is decidable in cubic time.
We are conducting an experimental study using real-life data graphs to check the effectiveness and efficiency of
strong containment
. We are investigating the use of
strong containment
for answering
CGPs
using views. We notice that the presence of attributes and negation on nodes of
CGPs
makes the problem very difficult compared to
[
4
]
since the results of some
CGP
can be obtained, not only by merging results of some views (as in
[
4
]
), but also by applying some operations like intersection and difference.

Appendix 0.C
Proof of Theorem
5.1

We prove Theorem
5.1
by providing the algorithm
TContained
(shown in Fig.
2
). Given two
CGPs

C_{{}_{1}}
and
C_{{}_{2}}
in input, it returns
\emptyset
if
C_{{}_{1}}\not\sqsubseteq C_{{}_{2}}
, or the mapping
\lambda
that allows
C_{{}_{1}}
to be traditionally contained in
C_{{}_{2}}
as well as the match relation
S
that realizes
\lambda
. Notice that the relation
S
is an intermediate data that allows to compute
\lambda
. However, we return it since both
\lambda
and
S
are necessary for strong containment checking. First of all, the algorithm checks whether
C^{+}_{{}_{1}}
matches
C^{+}_{{}_{2}}
(line 1) using a
pattern-only-matching
algorithm
pom
(see Appendix). Next, the resulting match relation
S
is refined by eliminating each pair (
u_{1},u_{2}
) from it if:
a
) the node
u_{1}
of
C_{{}_{1}}
does not satisfy negative predicates defined over
u_{2}
of
C_{{}_{2}}
(lines 2–4); or
b
)
u_{1}
is a core node while
u_{2}
is a predicate node (lines 5–6), which is due to the fact that the traditional containment maps core nodes (resp. edges) of
C_{{}_{1}}
to core nodes (resp. edges) of
C_{{}_{2}}
. The procedure
pom
is called over the refined version of
S
to check whether this later still allows
C^{+}_{{}_{1}}
to match
C^{+}_{{}_{2}}
(line 7). If this is the case, then the set
\lambda(u)
(resp.
\lambda(e)
) is computed based on
S
for any core node
u
(resp. core edge
e
) in
C_{{}_{1}}
(lines 9–16). Finally, if some set
\lambda(u)
(resp.
\lambda(e)
) is empty then
C_{{}_{1}}\not\sqsubseteq C_{{}_{2}}
and the algorithm returns (
\emptyset,\emptyset
), otherwise,
C_{{}_{1}}\sqsubseteq C_{{}_{2}}
and the pair (

) is returned (lines 17–20).

Based on Lemma
1
, it takes
O(|C^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|C^{+}_{{}_{2}}|)

C^{+}_{{}_{1}}
matches
C^{+}_{{}_{2}}
(line 1). Next, it takes

(u_{1},u_{2})\in S

u_{1}
satisfies all negative predicates of
u_{2}
(lines 2–4). Moreover, it takes

\lambda
(lines 9–16). One can conclude that
|C_{{}_{1}}|=|C^{+}_{{}_{1}}|+|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{1}|
(resp.
|C_{{}_{2}}|=|C^{+}_{{}_{2}}|+|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{2}|
). Therefore, the overall cost of algorithm
TContained
is bounded by
O(|C_{{}_{1}}|.|C_{{}_{2}}|)

5.1
.

Appendix 0.D
Procedure
ExtractRels

The procedure
ExtractRels
is given in Fig.
6
. Given two
CGPs

C_{{}_{1}}
and
C_{{}_{2}}
and a match relation
S
that allows
C_{{}_{1}}
to be traditionally contained in
C_{{}_{2}}
(Lemma
2
).
ExtractRels
computes and returns the four relations
\mathcal{R}^{+}
,
\mathcal{R}^{-}
,
\mathcal{E}^{+}
and
\mathcal{E}^{-}
. The computation of
\mathcal{R}^{+}
(lines 2–4),
\mathcal{R}^{-}
(lines 30–34) and
\mathcal{E}^{-}
(lines 35–37) is quite simple and follows from Def.
11
. The computation of
\mathcal{E}^{+}
is not a trivial task as we show hereafter. For each positive node
u
in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{1}
, we compute the sets
\overrightarrow{E_{{}_{1}}}(u)
(resp.
\overleftarrow{E_{{}_{1}}}(u)
) of all positive edges that start (resp. end) at
u
(line 5). Similar sets are computed for each positive node
w
in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{2}
(line 6). For each positive predicate
p^{+}_{2}(w)
in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{2}
and each pair
(u,w)
in
S
, the goal is to check whether there exists a part of a positive predicate in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{1}
, denoted by
p^{+}_{1}(u)
, that is centered at
u
and eliminable over
w
: i.e. there exists a part of
p^{+}_{2}(w)
that is equivalent to
p^{+}_{1}(u)
. To check that, the procedure firstly computes a set
PEE_{u}
that includes all edges in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{1}
that are reachable from
u
and equivalent to some edges in
p^{+}_{2}(w)
via
S
. A
Breadth-First Search

[
1
]
is applied (lines 8–15) to compute the set
PEE_{u}
. The edges in
PEE_{u}
are concerned by an elimination over
w
only if they satisfy condition (2b) of Def.
11
. Thus, the procedure refines the set
PEE(u)
(lines 16–26) by eliminating all edges that do not satisfy this condition. This refinement is done in terms of nodes rather than edges in order to reduce the time complexity. In a nutshell, the set
V_{u}
is extracted from
PEE_{u}
to include all positive nodes in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{1}
that play a role in
PEE_{u}
(line 16). Next, for each node
u^{\prime}
in
V_{u}
, if some adjacent edges to
u^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{1}
do not belong to
PEE_{u}
(line 19), then all edges that start/end at
u^{\prime}
in
PEE_{u}
do not satisfy the aforementioned condition (2b) and must be eliminated from
PEE_{u}
. Once eliminated from
PEE_{u}
, their adjacent edges will no longer satisfy condition (2b) and so on. For this reason, all edges in
PEE_{u}
that connect
u
to
u^{\prime}
are eliminated from
PEE_{u}
in a recursive manner (lines 20–26).
Finally, if the refined version of
PEE_{u}
is not empty then there exists a part of a positive predicate in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{1}
, denoted by
p^{+}_{1}(u)
, that is eliminable over
w
and induced by the nodes set
V_{u}
and the edges set
PEE_{u}
(lines 27–28). Hence, the pair
(p^{+}_{1}(u),w)
is added to the relation
\mathcal{E}^{+}
(line 29).

Appendix 0.E
Complexity Analysis of
SContained

Consider first the procedure
ExtractRels
. On can verify that the relation
\mathcal{R}{+}
can be computed in
O(|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|)
time (lines 2–4), while
\mathcal{R}{-}
can be computed in
O(|E^{-}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|)
time (lines 30–34). Since the equivalence between two
QGPs
can be checked via a bidirectional containment, then for each pair of negative predicates
p^{-}_{1}(u_{1})
and
p^{-}_{2}(u_{2})
, it takes
O(|p^{-}_{1}(u_{1})|.|p^{-}_{2}(u_{2})|)
time (from Theorem
5.1
) to check whether
p^{-}_{1}(u_{1})\equiv p^{-}_{2}(u_{2})
. Thus, the relation
\mathcal{E}^{-}
can be computed in
O(|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{2}}|)
time by considering all possible pairs (lines 35–37). The cost required to compute
\mathcal{E}^{+}
is detailed as follows. The different sets
\overrightarrow{E_{{}_{1}}}
and
\overleftarrow{E_{{}_{1}}}
are computed for all positive nodes of
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}
in
O(|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|+|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
time (line 5), which is equivalent to
O(|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
. Similarly, the different sets
\overrightarrow{E_{{}_{2}}}
and
\overleftarrow{E_{{}_{2}}}
are computed for all positive nodes of
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}
in
O(|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}|)
time (line 6).
For each positive predicate
p^{+}_{2}(w)
in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}
, let
E_{w}
(resp.
V_{w}
) be the edges set (resp. nodes set) of this predicate. For each node
u\in V^{+}_{{}_{1}}
, the set
PEE_{u}
can be computed in
O(|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{w}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|V_{w}|)
time (lines 8–15). This set is refined later (lines 16–26) in
O(|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|+|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
time, i.e. in
O(|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
time. If the refined version of
PEE_{u}
is not empty, then the corresponding subpredicate
p^{+}_{1}(u)
is computed and added to
\mathcal{E}^{+}
in
O(|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
time (lines 27–29). By considering all positive predicates in
p^{+}_{2}(w)
in
\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}
and all possible pairs
(u,w)\in S
, all possible sets
PEE_{u}
can be computed in
O(|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.(|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|E^{+}_{{}_{2}}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|V^{+}_{{}_{2}}|))
time and refined in
O(|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|V^{+}_{{}_{2}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
time. Therefore, the overall cost related to the computation of the relation
\mathcal{E}^{+}
is bounded by
O(|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}|)
.

Given the above, the overall cost of procedure
ExtractRels
is bounded by
O(|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|+|E^{-}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|+|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{2}}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}|)
. Notice that the cost
O(|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{-}_{{}_{2}}|)
time (resp.
O(|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}|)
time) is due to the evaluability checking of negative (resp. positive) predicates of
C_{{}_{1}}
over
C_{{}_{2}}
.

The complexity of algorithm
SContained
is stated as follows. From Theorem
5.1
, it requires
O(|C_{{}_{1}}|.|C_{{}_{2}}|)
time to check whether
C_{{}_{1}}\sqsubseteq C_{{}_{2}}
(line 1). Next, the aforementioned cost of procedure
ExtractRels
is required to compute the relations
\mathcal{R}^{+}
,
\mathcal{R}^{-}
,
\mathcal{E}^{+}
and
\mathcal{E}^{-}
(line 3). The set
CE^{+}
is computed in
O(|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|V^{+}_{{}_{2}}|.|E^{+}_{{}_{1}}|)
time (lines 5–6) while the set
CE^{-}
takes
O(|E^{-}_{{}_{1}}|.|E_{{}_{2}}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{2}}|.|E^{-}_{{}_{1}}|)
time (lines 7–8). Hence, the overall cost of
SContained
is bounded by
O(|C_{{}_{1}}|.|C_{{}_{2}}|+|V^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{1}}|.|\mathcal{P}^{+}_{{}_{2}}|)
time, which completes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.
