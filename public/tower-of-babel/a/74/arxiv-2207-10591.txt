FDD Massive MIMO Channel Training: Optimal Rate-Distortion Bounds and the Efficiency of “one-shot” Schemes
Mahdi Barzegar Khalilsarai, Yi Song, Tianyu Yang, Giuseppe Caire

Abstract
We study the problem of providing channel state information (CSI) at the transmitter in multi-user “massive” MIMO systems operating in frequency division duplexing (FDD). The wideband MIMO channel is a vector-valued random process correlated in time, space (antennas), and frequency (subcarriers). The base station (BS) broadcasts periodically β tr \beta_{\rm tr} pilot symbols from its M M antenna ports to K K single-antenna users (UEs). Correspondingly, the K K UEs send feedback messages about their channel state using β fb \beta_{\rm fb} symbols in the uplink (UL). Using results from remote rate-distortion theory, we show that, as 𝗌𝗇𝗋 → ∞ {\sf snr}\to\infty , the optimal feedback strategy achieves a channel state estimation mean squared error (MSE) that behaves as Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \Theta(1) if β tr < r \beta_{\rm tr}<r and as Θ ⁡ ( 𝗌𝗇𝗋 − α ) \Theta\left({\sf snr}^{-\alpha}\right) when β tr ≥ r \beta_{\rm tr}\geq r , where α = min ⁡ ( β fb / r , 1 ) \alpha=\min(\beta_{\rm fb}/r,1) , where r r is the rank of the channel covariance matrix. The MSE-optimal rate-distortion strategy implies encoding of long sequences of channel states, which would yield completely stale CSI and therefore poor multiuser precoding performance. Hence, we consider three practical “one-shot” CSI strategies with minimum one-slot delay and analyze their large-SNR channel estimation MSE behavior. These are: (1) digital feedback via entropy-coded scalar quantization (ECSQ), (2) analog feedback (AF), and (3) local channel estimation at the UEs and digital feedback. These schemes have different requirements in terms of knowledge of the channel statistics at the UE and at the BS. In particular, the latter strategy requires no statistical knowledge and is closely inspired by a CSI feedback scheme currently proposed in 3GPP standardization. It is shown that ECSQ achieves optimal MSE at the price of a slight increase in feedback rate which vanishes for large SNR. AF achieves the optimal MSE decay rate of Θ ⁡ ( 𝗌𝗇𝗋 − 1 ) \Theta({\sf snr}^{-1}) whenever β tr , β fb ≥ r \beta_{\rm tr},\beta_{\rm fb}\geq r but is sub-optimal if β ≥ r \beta\geq r and β fb < r \beta_{\rm fb}<r . The 3GPP-inspired scheme is shown, via numerical simulations, to achieves performance similar to ECSQ and AF when the multipath channel is sufficiently sparse in the angle-delay domain, but suffers from a large performance gap if this requirement is not met.

Multiuser (massive) MIMO consists of serving

Achieving the remarkable benefits of massive MIMO relies at large on the availability of accurate channel state information (CSI) at the BS.
In particular, the downlink (DL) requires that the BS computes precoding vectors as a function of the users’ DL CSI. In time division duplexing (TDD)

Training with finite-rate feedback results in imperfect CSI estimates, which translates into a loss in DL spectral efficiency, the proportions of which depends on the CSI estimate quality. It is for instance well-known that when the mean squared error (MSE) between true and estimated CSI of the users decreases as

The feedback strategy that achieves the optimal rate-distortion trade-off involves knowledge of the channel statistics both at the BS and the UE side,
as well as employment of high-dimensional vector quantizers operating on infinite-dimensional sequences of channel observations.
While achieving the best CSI MSE, this strategy is totally impractical since it incurs a large feedback delay, thus providing completely stale CSI, which is therefore useless for computing the multi-user MIMO precoding vectors on each coherence block.

1.

IV
Practical Feedback Schemes

The rate-distortion bound of Section
III
is achieved by encoding infinite-dimensional blocks of MMSE channel estimates
\{{\bf u}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{S},\,S\to\infty
given in (
7
). As already remarked in Section

although minimizing the channel
estimation MSE at the BS, it would yield
stale
channel estimates that cannot be used for DL precoding.
In this section we examine practical one-shot estimation and feedback schemes where the feedback message at each frame
i
is a function of the

{\bf y}^{{\rm tr}\,(i)}
in the current frame only. Interestingly, as we will show, such schemes achieve CSIT estimation errors similar to that of the optimal rate-distortion scheme at the cost of a tolerable increase in the feedback rate.

IV-A

Entropy-Coded Scalar Quantization

The feedback method based on entropy-coded scalar quantization (ECSQ) assumes channel covariance knowledge at both the BS and the UE side and computes the feedback message as follows. In any frame, the UE computes the MMSE channel estimate
{\bf u}={\mathbb{E}}[{\bf h}|{\bf y}^{\rm tr}]
from the pilot signal
{\bf y}^{\rm tr}
, where we have dropped the superscript
i
from the variables for simplicity. The Karhunen-Loève (KL) expansion of
{\bf u}
is given by

{\bf u}=\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}+{\bf F}{\bf w},

(12)

where
{\bf F}=[{\bf f}_{1},\ldots,{\bf f}_{MN}]

\{{\bf f}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{MN}

\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{u}
as its columns,
{\bf w}=[w_{1},\ldots,w_{MN}]^{\sf T}

w_{\ell}\sim{{\cal C}{\cal N}}(0,\lambda_{\ell}^{u})

coefficients, and

\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{u}

Since
\mu

{\bf F}

Define the dithering random variable
Z_{q}
statistically independent of
{\bf w}
and uniformly distributed over the interval

This dithering variable is known to both the BS and the UE.
12
12

12

common randomness, which can be approached in practice by suitably synchronized
pseudo-random number generation, analogous to random-spreading CDMA, frequency hopping, and many other schemes that require
some form of common randomness. Furthermore, the dithering is needed to obtain a tractable rate-distortion distortion bound, but in practice it is well-known that a slightly better performance can be achieved without dithering. Therefore, the assumption of common randomness is not at all a limiting factor in this scheme.

Analog Feedback

Feedback based on ECSQ requires full knowledge of the DL channel statistics at the UE in order to compute
the MMSE estimate in (
7
) and its KL expansion in (
12
). While the DL channel covariance
can be estimated at the BS side from the UL pilots sent by the UE to the BS followed by a suitable UL-DL covariance transformation (e.g., see
[
25
,
26
]
),
providing the DL channel statistics to the UEs is significantly harder.
The reason is that, as explained before, in massive MIMO
we aim at minimizing the DL training dimension
\beta_{\rm tr}
such that it is significantly less than
M
.
Methods for covariance estimation from low dimensional projections (i.e.,
\beta^{\rm tr}<M
) have been studied (e.g., see
[
27
]
). Nevertheless, they are computationally quite intensive.
Therefore, it is preferable not to rely on the availability of DL channel covariance at the user side.

The analog feedback (AF) strategy presented in this section is an example of a method that does not rely on such assumption. In AF, the UE extracts its
\beta_{\rm tr}
received DL pilot symbols from the DL training and feeds them back to the BS via Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) symbols with unquantized I and Q components via
\beta_{\rm fb}=\zeta\beta_{\rm tr}
channel symbols (e.g., see
[
28

10
]

In particular, the received training vector
{\bf y}^{\rm tr}
is modulated by a full-rank “spreading” matrix
\Psi
of dimension
\beta_{\rm tr}\times\beta_{\rm fb}

{\bf y}^{\rm af}={\bf y}^{\rm tr}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+{\bf z}^{\rm ul}={\bf h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+{\bf z}^{\rm af},

(15)

{\bf z}^{\rm ul}\sim{{\cal C}{\cal N}}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

The AF transmitted signal in (
15

\beta_{\rm fb}
channel uses with symbols

\hbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{i}
is the
i

\Psi
, for
i=1,\ldots,\beta_{\rm fb}

From the UL per-user capacity scaling given in Section

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|{\bf y}^{\rm tr}\hbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{i}|^{2}]=\hbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{i}^{{\sf H}}{\bf R}_{{\bf y}^{\rm tr}}\hbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{i}=M{\sf snr}_{\rm ul},

(16)

for all

is of rank
\beta_{\rm tr}

IV-C

3GPP-Inspired Feedback Scheme

Release 16 of the the 3GPP new radio suggests a new wideband CSI feedback method that reduces overhead by sending only the strongest coefficients of the channel decomposition in terms of space-frequency basis vectors (see, e.g.
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
). We describe the concept of this highly popular scheme in the industry, and based on it, introduce the
3GPP-inspired
feedback method studied in this work.
Let
{\bf H}=[{\bf h}[1],\ldots,{\bf h}[N]]
denote the
M\times N
(antenna-subcarriers) channel matrix for a given UE, such that
{\bf h}={\rm vec}({\bf H})
. The scheme consists of approximating

{\bf H}\approx{\bf A}{\bf W}{\bf B}^{\sf T}=\sum_{j=1}^{G_{a}}\sum_{i=1}^{G_{d}}w_{i,j}{\bf a}(\hbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}){\bf b}(\tau_{i})^{\sf T},

(20)

for fixed codebooks
{\bf A}=[{\bf a}(\hbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{1}),\ldots,{\bf a}(\hbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{G_{a}})]
and
{\bf B}=[{\bf b}(\tau_{1}),\ldots,{\bf b}(\tau_{G_{d}})]
, where
G_{a}
is proportional to the number of antennas (typically
G_{a}=2M
) and
G_{d}
is proportional to the number of subcarriers (typically
G_{d}=2N
). The columns of
{\bf A}
are the array steering vectors for a discrete grid of angle-of-departures (AoDs) and the columns
{\bf b}(\tau_{i})
of
{\bf B}
form a Fourier basis for a discrete grid of delays.
The main idea of the scheme is that the channel is “sparse” in the angle-delay domain. Therefore, the matrix
{\bf W}
contains only a few dominant coefficients. Hence, the scheme sends the quantization of such dominant coefficients and the indices of the corresponding codebook vectors as a representation of the channel. Reformulating (
20
) we have

\displaystyle{\bf h}

\displaystyle\approx{\rm vec}{({\bf A}{\bf W}{\bf B}^{\sf T})}=({\bf B}\otimes{\bf A}){\bf w},

(21)

where
{\bf w}={\rm vec}{({\bf W})}
is the sparse vector containing the angle-delay channel coefficients and
\otimes
is the Kronecker product. From (
2
) and (
21
) we can write the training measurements as

{\bf y}^{\rm tr}\approx{\bf w}^{{\sf H}}({\bf B}\otimes{\bf A})^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}+{\bf z}^{\rm tr}={\bf w}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}}+{\bf z}^{\rm tr},

(22)

where we have defined
\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}}\triangleq({\bf B}\otimes{\bf A})^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
as the
sensing matrix
. The idea is to quantize and feedback the
s_{0}
largest coefficients in
{\bf w}
. But in order to send the dominant coefficients, first an estimate of
{\bf w}
must be obtained at the UE from the DL training

{\bf y}^{\rm tr}
in (
2
). Interestingly, the 3GPP standard does not specify how such estimate should be obtained.
Typical Least-Squares estimation in the form (e.g., see
[
2
]
)

\widehat{{\bf w}}=(\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}}({\bf y}^{\rm tr})^{{\sf H}}

(23)

requires that the
MN\times MN
matrix
\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}
is invertible, which in turns requires

\beta_{\rm tr}\geq MN
, which is completely impractical in massive MIMO. Heuristic approaches consist of limiting the estimation only on the pilot subcarriers in
{\cal N}_{p}
(typically one per resource block) and then interpolating over the subcarriers (e.g., see
[
18
]
). In this case, the invertibility conditions yields
\beta_{\rm tr}\geq MN_{p}
, which yields

T_{p}\geq M
pilot symbols in time per pilot subcarrier. For a typical resource block of about 200 symbols and number of antennas ranging from 32 to 128 it is clear that the DL pilot overhead is still too large. For this reason, several works have focused on
exploiting the inherent sparsity of
{\bf w}
to estimate it at the UE when
\beta_{\rm tr}
is smaller than
MN
. These approaches use
some form of CS, and are referred to in general as “compressed DL pilot” schemes (e.g., see
[
32
,
15
,
13
]
). Now, there exist plenty of sparse recovery methods in the CS literature to estimate
{\bf w}
from the noisy measurements
{\bf y}^{\rm tr}
. Among these, we consider
the well-known orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)
[
33
]
as a representative of sparse recovery methods, first because it is shown to be highly successful in a variety of settings and second because it is much less computationally complex in comparison to the convex optimization-based alternatives such as
[
34
]
. We omit the details of OMP to save space and refer the reader to
[
33
]
.

To perform OMP, the user is assumed to know the sensing matrix
\Phi
(composed of the training matrix and the codebooks) and the sparsity level
s_{0}
. The estimator outputs an estimate
\widehat{{\bf w}}
containing
s_{0}
non-zero coefficients. Following
[
29
]
, scalar quantization is performed independently on the normalized
amplitude and phase of each coefficient. The amplitudes are first normalized to the largest one and then each quantized with a codebook of
2^{b}
uniformly spaced quantization levels in the interval
[0,1]
as
{\cal Q}_{\rm amp}=\{2^{-b}\ell\,:\,\ell=1,\ldots,2^{b}\}
, for some positive integer
b
. The phase is quantized with a codebook of
2^{b}
uniformly spaced quantization levels in the interval
[0,2\pi]
as
{\cal Q}_{\rm phase}=\{2\pi\times 2^{-b}\ell\,:\,\ell=1,\ldots,2^{b}\}
. Therefore
2b
bits are spent to quantize a single coefficient. Finally, the quantization bits and the indices of the codebook columns whose corresponding coefficients are non-zero (known as the support set) are transmitted to the BS.

The resulting total number of feedback bits in this case is given by

B_{\rm cs}=2b(s_{0}-1)+\lceil\log s_{0}\rceil+s_{0}\lceil\log(G_{a}G_{d})\rceil.

(24)

Here the first, second and third terms represents the number of bits spent on quantizing amplitudes, the number of bits spent to report the number of non-zeros and the number of bits necessary to encode the support set, respectively. From this, we can compute the feedback dimension necessary with the OMP method and scalar
quantization as
\beta_{\rm fb}=B_{\rm cs}/C^{\rm ul}
.

The channel estimation error at the BS for CS-based feedback does
not
go to zero even in high SNR, because of the approximation error inherent in (
21
), which is independent of SNR. The point is that, except in rare cases, the channel
{\bf h}
cannot be exactly described by a linear combination of
s_{0}
columns of
{\bf B}\otimes{\bf A}
, since the multipath angle-delay parameters do not lie on the assumed discrete grid but are rather arbitrarily distributed over the continuum. This mismatch between the exact “off-grid” sparse representation of a vector and its on-grid sparse approximation is well-known in the CS literature (see, e.g.
[
35
]
). The mismatch results in an error in sparse estimation of the channel, which does not vanish with SNR. Therefore, not only OMP, but any CS-based feedback method based on approximation of the channel with
s_{0}
mismatched angle-delay vectors (with
s_{0}\leq MN
) results in a QSE of
\alpha_{\rm cs}=0
for all
\beta_{\rm tr}<MN
and all
\beta_{\rm fb}
.
13
13

13

If
\beta_{\rm tr}\geq MN
, the UE can simply
compute the Least-Squares estimate (
23
) with an error that goes to zero with
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}\to\infty
.

However, as we shall see in the next section, the
3GPP-inspired
scheme can achieve a DL spectral efficiency in line with the other schemes when the channel is sufficiently sparse.

1.

Synthetic Multipath Channel Model:
The synthetic multipath wideband channel model for an arbitrary user is given by

{\bf h}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c_{\ell}{\bf b}(\tau_{\ell})\otimes{\bf a}(\theta_{\ell}),

where

\theta_{\ell}
and
{\bf b}(\tau_{\ell})

\tau_{\ell}

[{\bf b}(\tau_{\ell})]_{n}=e^{-j2\pi nf_{s}\tau_{\ell}}

), except that we assume each angle
\theta_{\ell}
to be generated uniformly at random over
[-\pi/2,\pi/d]
and each delay
\tau_{\ell}

[0,\tau_{\max}]

\{c_{\ell}\}_{\ell}

{{\cal C}{\cal N}}(0,1)
. The number of paths
L
is the same for all UEs. This results in a correlated Gaussian channel with mean
\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=0
(which is intended by construction for simplicity) and covariance
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}({\bf b}(\tau_{\ell}){\bf b}(\tau_{\ell})^{{\sf H}})\otimes({\bf a}(\tau_{\ell}){\bf a}(\tau_{\ell})^{{\sf H}})
. This multipath channel model is considered extensively in the literature
[
36
,
32
,
37

38
]
and ensures a correlated Gaussian distribution for the channel,

2.

CDL Channel Model:
The clustered delay line (CDL) channel types are proposed by 3GPP as standard link-level MIMO fading channel models
[
39
,
40
]
. In these models the channel is the contribution of a number of multipath “clusters”, parametrized by their average gain power, AoDs and delays.
In order to generate CDL model, we use the MATLAB system object
nrCDLChannel

[
41
]
. For each user, we generate
r
clusters with angle and delay parameters chosen uniformly at random and independently across users.
The system object produces random realizations of the fading channel, which we normalize such that
\|{\bf h}\|^{2}=MN
. We compute an approximation of the channel covariance by computing the sample covariance matrix over 2000 realizations.
14
14

14

Note that this number of samples is sufficient for the sample covariance matrix to reasonably converge to the true covariance, given the small covariance rank in these simulations.
Note that in this model the channel does not follow a Gaussian distribution, except perhaps in an approximate sense due to the summation of many random multipath gains as a consequence of the Central Limit Theorem. However, the simulations will show that the ranking of the discussed feedback strategies and the theoretical results of this work not only hold for correlated Gaussian channels but also empirically carry over to the more realistic CDL model.

Performance Metrics

We study the CSI estimation MSE and the multiuser system total spectral efficiency (sum-rate) with ZF precoding for all the feedback strategies. To compute these, we first fix a channel distribution according to one of the models described above and generate a set of random, wideband channel realizations based on that distribution. Then we generate a single random training matrix according to the design explained in Section

\displaystyle\text{MSE}_{\text{avg}}=\frac{1}{MN}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf h}-\widehat{{\bf h}}\|^{2}],

(25)

where the expected value is computed empirically by averaging over
the randomly generated training matrices, channel distributions and random channels realizations. To compare achievable sum-rates in DL, we consider ZF precoding where the transmit data vector over subcarrier
n
is given by
{\bf d}[n]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{P_{k}}s_{k}[n]{\bf v}_{k}[n]

(26)

VI
Conclusion

We provided optimal rate-distortion bounds for the problem of CSIT feedback in wideband massive MIMO systems, demonstrating an upper-bound on the MSE decay rate in high SNR for any feedback scheme when the BS broadcasts random Gaussian training pilots to the users. We then discussed three one-shot feedback methods, each assuming various levels of channel statistics knowledge either at the BS or the users. In particular, we showed that the low-complexity analog feedback yields a near-optimal high-SNR channel estimation error decay with no channel statistics knowledge at the user side and no quantization quantization and channel coding. We also studied a 3GPP-inspired feedback method based on compressed sensing estimation of the channel sparse coefficients at the user side, where we showed that the method entails a residual CSIT estimation error even in high SNR, due to a mismatch between the true space-delay domain of sparsity and the one assumed by the estimator. However, the method can yield decent results for sufficiently sparse channels. The findings were supported by numerical simulations comparing the normalized channel estimation MSE and achievable DL spectral efficiency with various feedback methods.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
1

We start by stating a few standard results regarding the (remote) rate-distortion function. It is well-known that the rate-distortion function of an i.i.d source represented by the random variable
{\bf u}
with distribution
p_{{\bf u}}
can be computed as (see
[
thomas2006elements
]
Theorem 10.2.1)

R_{{\bf u}}(D)=\min_{p_{\widebar{{\bf u}}|{\bf u}}:d(\widebar{{\bf u}},{\bf u})\leq D}I(\widebar{{\bf u}};{\bf u}),

(28)

where
\widebar{{\bf u}}
is the quantization of
{\bf u}
,
I(\widebar{{\bf u}};{\bf u})
is the mutual information between
{\bf u}
and
\widebar{{\bf u}}
,
d(\widebar{{\bf u}},{\bf u})
is the distortion between
{\bf u}
and
\widebar{{\bf u}}
(see (
4
)) and the minimum is taken over all conditional distributions for which the joint distribution
p_{{\bf u},\widebar{{\bf u}}}
satisfies the distortion constraint. It is also known that the remote rate distortion function of a source represented by the random variable
{\bf h}
, and encoded given its observations denoted by the random variable
{\bf y}^{\rm tr}
is given by (see
[
8
]
)

R_{{\bf h}}^{r}(D)=\min_{p_{\widebar{\mathbb{h}}|\mathbb{y}^{\rm tr}}:d(\widebar{{\bf h}},{\bf h})\leq D}~I(\widebar{{\bf h}};{\bf y}^{\rm tr})

(29)

where
\widebar{{\bf h}}
is the quantization of
{\bf h}
,
I(\widebar{{\bf h}};{\bf y}^{\rm tr})
is the mutual information between
\widebar{{\bf h}}
and
{\bf y}^{\rm tr}
, and the minimum is taken over all conditional distributions
p_{\widebar{{\bf h}}|{\bf y}^{\rm tr}}
for which the joint distribution
p_{{\bf h},\widebar{{\bf h}}}=p_{{\bf h}}p_{{\bf y}^{\rm tr}|{\bf h}}p_{\widebar{{\bf h}}|{\bf y}^{\rm tr}}
satisfies the distortion constraint. Note that since all sources are i.i.d, we have removed realization index superscripts from the variables (hence
{\bf h}
instead of
{\bf h}^{(i)}
). From the premise of the lemma,
{\bf u}
is the MMSE estimate of the channel given the training measurements, i.e.
{\bf u}={\mathbb{E}}[{\bf h}|{\bf y}^{\rm tr}]
. Using the same technique employed to prove inequality (15) of
[
24
]
(see Appendix A in
[
24
]
), we can verify that the remote rate-distortion function of
{\bf h}
is related to the rate distortion function of
{\bf u}
by

R_{{\bf h}}^{r}(D)=R_{{\bf u}}(D-D_{\rm mmse}),

(30)

for
D\geq D_{\rm mmse}
, where
D_{\rm mmse}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\bf h}-{\bf u}\|^{2}\right]
is the MMSE of estimating the channel at the UE.

On the other hand, the rate-distortion function of a correlated vector Gaussian source is given by reverse water-filling over its covariance eigenvalues
[
23
]
. If we denote the eigenvalues of
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{u}
by
\{\lambda_{\ell}^{u}\}_{\ell=1}^{MN}
, then we have
R_{{\bf u}}(D)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{MN}\left[\log\frac{\lambda_{\ell}^{u}}{\gamma}\right]_{+},
where
\gamma
is chosen such that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{MN}\min\{\gamma,\lambda_{\ell}^{u}\}=D
. Plugging this in (
30
) we get

R_{{\bf h}}^{r}(D)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{MN}\left[\log\frac{\lambda_{\ell}^{u}}{\gamma}\right]_{+},

(31)

where
\gamma
is chosen such that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{MN}\min\{\gamma,\lambda_{u,\ell}\}=D-D_{\rm mmse}.
The proof is complete.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
1

We divide the proof to two parts. First, we show that if
\beta_{\rm tr}<r
, then the achievable error behaves as
\Theta(1)
for all realizations of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
. Second, we show that if
\beta_{\rm tr}\geq r
, then an error decaying as
\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\min(\beta_{\rm fb}/r,1)})
is achievable with probability 1
over the realizations of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
.

Part I.
To prove part I, we first bound the minimum mean squared error (MMSE) of estimating the channel given the training measurements at the user, namely the variable
D_{\rm mmse}
. From
{\bf u}={\mathbb{E}}[{\bf h}|{\bf y}^{\rm tr}]
we have

\displaystyle D_{\rm mmse}={\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf h}-{\bf u}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left({\mathbb{E}}[{\bf h}{\bf h}^{{\sf H}}]-{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf h}{\bf y}^{\rm tr}]{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf y}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}{\bf y}^{\rm tr}]^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}[{\bf h}{\bf y}^{\rm tr}]^{{\sf H}}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left(\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}-\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\left({\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}\right)

Let us define the eigendecomposition of
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}
as
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}={\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}
, where
{\bf U}_{h}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{MN\times r}
is a tall unitary matrix and
\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}={\rm diag}(\hbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix of positive eigenvalues represented by the vector
\hbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}=[\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}]^{\sf T}
where we assume
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{r}
without loss of generality. Using this decomposition and applying the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury matrix identity to
\left({\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\left({\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}={\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf G}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}-{\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf G}\left(\mathbf{I}+{\bf G}\right)^{-1}{\bf G}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}},

(33)

where we have defined

\displaystyle{\bf G}\triangleq\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}{\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}.

(34)

Plugging this into (
32
) we have

D_{\rm mmse}={\rm Tr}\left(\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bf G}+{\bf G}(\mathbf{I}+{\bf G})^{-1}{\bf G}\right)\right).

(35)

Using a simple trace inequality, one can show that

\lambda_{r}\,g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})\leq D_{\rm mmse}\leq\lambda_{1}\,g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}),

(36)

where we have defined
g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})={\rm Tr}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bf G}+{\bf G}(\mathbf{I}+{\bf G})^{-1}{\bf G}\right)
, to explicitly denote the dependence of this term on
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
. Note that this dependence emerges from the dependence of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
and therefore
{\bf G}
on
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
.
We now show how
g(\cdot)
behaves for large
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
.
For a given realization of the training matrix
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
, denote the eigenvalues of
{\bf G}
by
\mu_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,r
. It follows that

\displaystyle g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})

\displaystyle=r-\sum_{i}\mu_{i}+\sum_{i}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\mu_{i}+1}=r-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+1}

(37)

Also note that the training matrix can be written as
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}=\sqrt{{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}}{\bf X}_{0}^{\rm tr}
, where
{\bf X}_{0}^{\rm tr}
is randomly generated and independent from
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
. From this and the definition (
34
), we have
\mu_{i}=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})
for all
\mu_{i}\neq 0
. Using this and (
37
), we deduce that if
{\bf G}
is full-rank (
\mu_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
) then
g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
and using (
36
) we have
D_{\rm mmse}=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
. Conversely, if
{\bf G}
has at least one zero eigenvalue (
\mu_{i}=0
for some
i
) then from (
37
) we have
g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})>1
for all
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
and from (
51
) we have
D_{\rm mmse}=\Theta(1)
.

Now, the rank of
{\bf G}
depends on the specific realization of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
. When
\beta_{\rm tr}\geq r
and
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
consists of Gaussian isotropic pilot vectors,
{\bf G}
is full-rank with probability one because of the following. The product
{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}}
consists of
\beta_{\rm tr}
independent Gaussian columns, each of dimension
r
. The event that these vectors span a space of dimension less than
r
has probability zero. Therefore,
{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}{\bf U}_{h}
has rank
r
with probability one, and since
\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}
has positive diagonal elements, by definition (
34
)
{\bf G}
also has rank
r
with probability one and
\mu_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. Conversely, if
\beta_{\rm tr}<r
,
{\bf G}
has rank at most equal to
\beta_{\rm tr}
for any realization of the training matrix, leading to
\mu_{i}=0
for some
i
. This results in
D_{\rm mmse}=\Theta(1)
. In short, we have proved
D_{\rm mmse}=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
for
\beta_{\rm tr}\geq r
and
D_{\rm mmse}=\Theta(1)
for
\beta_{\rm tr}<r
with probability one over the realizations of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
. In addition, Lemma
1
states that only errors
D\geq D_{\rm mmse}
are achievable. It follows that, if
\beta_{\rm tr}<r
, then the minimum achievable error in estimating the CSIT behaves as
\Theta(1)
. This completes the first part of the proof.

Part II.
To prove the second part, first note that if
\beta_{\rm tr}\geq r
, then the covariance of the MMSE channel estimate
{\bf u}
at the UE, given as

\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{u}=\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\left({\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}-\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}^{{\sf H}}

(38)

has rank
r
with probability one over the realizations of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
. Without loss of generality assume the eigenvalues of
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{u}
to be ordered as
\lambda_{1}^{u}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{r}^{u}>0
. Next, consider the remote rate-distortion function in Lemma
1
, given as

R_{{\bf h}}^{r}(D)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{MN}\left[\log\frac{\lambda_{\ell}^{u}}{\gamma}\right]_{+},~\text{for}~D\geq D_{\rm mmse}

(39)

where
\gamma
is chosen such that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{MN}\min\{\gamma,\lambda_{\ell}^{u}\}=D-D_{\rm mmse}.
Consider an interval of error values
D
for which
D-D_{\rm mmse}<\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon
. For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
we have
\gamma=(D-D_{\rm mmse})/r
and the remote rate-distortion function is given by

R_{{\bf h}}^{r}(D)=f(r)-r\log(D-D_{\rm mmse}),~~\text{for }D-D_{\rm mmse}<\varepsilon,

(40)

where
f(r)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\log\lambda_{\ell}^{u}+r\log r
is a value independent of
D
. This implies that for all rates

we can write the remote

D_{{\bf h}}^{r}(R)=2^{\frac{f(r)-R}{r}}+D_{\rm mmse}.

(41)

Now let
R=\beta_{\rm fb}C^{\rm ul}
. Replacing the MIMO-MAC capacity formula
C^{\rm ul}=\log(1+M\kappa{\sf snr}_{\rm dl})
, we notice that there exists some
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{\varepsilon}
such that
\beta_{\rm fb}\log(1+M\kappa{\sf snr}_{\rm dl})>R_{\varepsilon}

{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}>{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{\varepsilon}
. Therefore we can write

\log(D_{{\bf h}}^{r}(\beta_{\rm fb}C^{\rm ul})-D_{\rm mmse})=\log r+\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\log\lambda_{\ell}^{u}/r-\frac{\beta_{\rm fb}}{r}\log(1+M\kappa{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}),~\text{for }{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}>{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{\varepsilon}.

(42)

From (
38
) we have that the non-zero eigenvalues of
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{u}
scale as
\Theta(1)
, i.e.
\lambda_{\ell}^{u}=\Theta(1),\,\ell=1,\ldots,r
. Therefore, the right-hand-side of (

\Theta(\log({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\beta_{\rm fb}/r}))

{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}

\beta_{\rm tr}\geq r
we have

\displaystyle D_{{\bf h}}^{r}(\beta_{\rm fb}C^{\rm ul})=D_{\rm mmse}+\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\beta_{\rm fb}/r})

\displaystyle=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})+\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\beta_{\rm fb}/r})

\displaystyle=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\min(\beta_{\rm fb}/r,1)})

with a feedback dimension of
\beta_{\rm fb}

Combining Parts I and II of the proof, we have that rate-distortion feedback achieves a CSIT estimation error of
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf h}-\widehat{{\bf h}}\|^{2}]=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\alpha_{\rm rd}})

\alpha_{\rm rd}=\min(\beta_{\rm fb}/r,1)\mathbf{1}\{\beta_{\rm tr}\geq r\}
.
\blacksquare

), and applying the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury matrix identity we have

\displaystyle{\bf R}_{y,\,{\rm af}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}-(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}{\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}\times

(47)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}+\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}{\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}.

It follows that the second term appearing within the
{\rm Tr}(\cdot)
in (
45
) can be written as

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}{\bf R}_{y,\,{\rm af}}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}

\displaystyle={\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf G}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}-{\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf G}\left(\mathbf{I}+{\bf G}\right)^{-1}{\bf G}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}},

(48)

where we define

\displaystyle{\bf G}

\displaystyle\triangleq\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}{\bf U}_{h}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}{\bf U}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}^{1/2}

(49)

Plugging (
48
) into (
45
) we have

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf h}-\widehat{{\bf h}}\|^{2}]={\rm Tr}\left(\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bf G}+{\bf G}(\mathbf{I}+{\bf G})^{-1}{\bf G}\right)\right).

(50)

This formula is exactly the same as (
35
) except for the definition of
{\bf G}
. Therefore the same trace inequality as in (
36
) holds here for the CSIT estimation error, i.e. we have

\lambda_{r}\,g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf h}-\widehat{{\bf h}}\|^{2}]\leq\lambda_{1}\,g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}),

(51)

where
g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})={\rm Tr}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\bf G}+{\bf G}(\mathbf{I}+{\bf G})^{-1}{\bf G}\right)
. We now show how
g(\cdot)
behaves for large
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
. For a given realization of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
, denote the eigenvalues of
{\bf G}
by
\mu_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,r
. We can write

\displaystyle g({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})

\displaystyle=r-\sum_{i}\mu_{i}+\sum_{i}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\mu_{i}+1}=r-\sum_{i}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+1}

(52)

From the definition in (
49
), the constituents of
{\bf G}
depend on
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
as follows:

(a)

We can represent the training matrix as
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}=\sqrt{{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}}{\bf X}_{0}^{\rm tr}
, where
{\bf X}_{0}^{\rm tr}
is generated randomly independent from
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
. Hence,
the non-identically zero elements of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
scale with
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
as
\Theta(\sqrt{{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}})
with probability 1.

(b)

From constraint (
16
), we have that each column of
\Psi
can be written as
\hbox{\boldmath$\psi$}_{i}=\sqrt{a_{i}}\hbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{i}
, where
a_{i}=\frac{M\kappa{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}}{c_{i}{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}+1}
with
c_{i}=\hbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{i}^{{\sf H}}{\bf X}_{0}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}_{0}\hbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{i}
. Here
\hbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{i},\,i\in\{1,\ldots,\beta_{\rm fb}\}
are a set of unit-norm vectors that contain a subset of
\min(\beta_{\rm tr},\beta_{\rm fb})
linearly independent vectors, and are independent of
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
. The existence of this set is guaranteed because
{\bf X}_{0}^{{\rm tr}\,{{\sf H}}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{h}{\bf X}^{\rm tr}_{0}
is full-rank with probability one. It follows that

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}=\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}+{\bf S})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{{\sf H}},

(53)

where
\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}=[\hbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{1},\ldots,\hbox{\boldmath$\phi$}_{\beta_{\rm fb}}]
is independent of
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
and
{\bf S}
is a diagonal matrix whose
(i,i)
element is given by
S_{i,i}=\frac{c_{i}}{M\kappa}+\frac{1}{M\kappa{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}}
. The matrix
{\bf S}
is the only variable dependent on
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
, and its diagonal elements scale as
\Theta(1)
.

(c)

The matrices
{\bf U}_{h}
and
\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}_{h}
are independent of
{\sf snr}_{\rm dl}
.

From these we conclude that the non-zero eigenvalues of
{\bf G}
scale as
\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})
, i.e.
\mu_{i}=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl})
for all
\mu_{i}\neq 0
. Now, the rank of
{\bf G}
depends on the specific realization of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
. When
\min(\beta_{\rm tr},\beta_{\rm fb})\geq r
and
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
contains isotropic Gaussian pilot vectors,
{\bf G}
is full-rank with probability one because of the same argument as used in Part I of the proof of Theorem
1
and considering the fact that the constituent matrix
\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}(\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}^{{\sf H}}
is positive semi-definite with rank
\min(\beta_{\rm tr},\beta_{\rm fb})
. In this case we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf h}-\widehat{{\bf h}}\|^{2}]=\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
and therefore an error of
\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-1})
is achievable. Conversely, if
r>\beta_{\rm tr}
or
r>\beta_{\rm fb}
,
{\bf G}
has rank at most
\min(\beta_{\rm tr},\beta_{\rm fb})<r
for any design of pilot matrices, leading to
\mu_{i}=0
for some
i
and from (
51
), the error is bounded from below and above by constants, i.e. we have an error of
\Theta(1)
. Therefore analog feedback achieves an error of
\Theta({\sf snr}_{\rm dl}^{-\alpha_{\rm af}})
with probability one over the realizations of
{\bf X}^{\rm tr}
where
\alpha_{\rm af}=\mathbf{1}\{\min(\beta_{\rm tr},\beta_{\rm fb})\geq r\}
. This completes the proof.
\blacksquare
