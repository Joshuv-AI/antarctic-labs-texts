Moving higher-order Taylor approximations method for smooth constrained minimization problems Thanks: Submitted to the editors
Yassine Nabou, Ion Necoara

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a higher-order method for solving composite (non)convex minimization problems with smooth (non)convex functional constraints. At each iteration, our method approximates the smooth part of the objective function and of the constraints by higher-order Taylor approximations, leading to a moving Taylor approximation method (MTA). We present convergence guarantees for the MTA algorithm for both, nonconvex and convex problems. In particular, when the objective and the constraints are nonconvex functions, we prove that the sequence generated by MTA algorithm converges globally to a KKT point. Moreover, we derive convergence rates in the iterates when the problem’s data satisfy the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property. Further, when the objective function is (uniformly) convex and the constraints are also convex, we provide (linear/superlinear) sublinear convergence rates for our algorithm. Finally, we present an efficient implementation of the proposed algorithm and compare it with existing methods from the literature.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the following composite optimization problem with functional constraints:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{E}}F(x)

\displaystyle:=F_{0}(x)+h(x)

(1)

s.t.

\displaystyle:\;F_{i}(x)\leq 0\quad\forall i=1:m,

where
F_{i}:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{R}
, for
i=0:m
, are continuous differentiable functions and
h:\mathbb{E}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is proper and convex function (e.g., the indicator function of some convex set). Here,
\mathbb{E}
is a finite-dimensional real vector space. Nonlinear programming problems have a long and rich history (see, for example, the monograph
[
6
]
), because they model many practical applications. Some of the most common are power systems, engineering design, control, signal and image processing, machine learning and statistics, see e.g.
[
4

17
,
32

34
,
35

36

. Several algorithms with complexity guarantees have been developed for solving (
1

[
9
,
23
,
40
]
are based on solving a sequence of quadratic regularized subproblems over a set of linearized approximations of
the constraints (known as SCP/SQP type algorithms). Furthermore, the augmented Lagrangian (AL) methods reformulate the nonlinear problem (
1
) via unconstrained minimization problems (known as methods of multipliers), see e.g.,
[
5
,
25
]
. For the particular class of constrained optimization problems with smooth data,
[
3
]
introduces a moving ball approximation method (MBA) that approximates the objective function with a quadratic and the feasible set by a sequence of appropriately defined balls. The authors provide asymptotic convergence guarantees for the sequence generated by MBA when the data is (non)convex, and linear convergence if the objective function is strongly convex. Later, several papers considered variants of the MBA algorithm; see
[
11
,
12
,
38
]
. For example, in
[
38
]
the authors present a line search MBA algorithm for difference-of-convex minimization problems and derive asymptotic convergence in the nonconvex settings and local convergence in the iterates when a special potential function related to the objective satisfies the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property. In
[
11
]
, the authors consider convex composite minimization problems that cover, in particular, problems of the form (
1
), and use a similar MBA type scheme for solving such problems, deriving a sublinear convergence rate for it. Note that all these previous methods are
first-order methods
, and despite their empirical success in solving difficult optimization problems, their convergence speed is known to be slow.

A natural way to ensure faster convergence rates is to increase the power of the oracle, i.e., to use higher-order information (derivatives) to build a higher-order (Taylor) model (see
[
16
]
for a detailed exposition). For example,
[
30
]
derives the first global convergence rate of cubic regularization of Newton method for unconstrained smooth minimization problems with the hessian Lipschitz continuous (i.e., using a second-order oracle). Paper
[
30
]
derives global convergence guarantees (in function value) in the convex case of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
. Higher-order methods are recently popular due to their performance in dealing with ill conditioning and fast rates of convergence. However, the main obstacle in the implementation of these (higher-order) methods lies in the complexity of the corresponding model approximation formed by a high-order multidimensional polynomial, which may be difficult to handle and minimize (see, for example,
[
8
,
14
]
). Nevertheless, for convex unconstrained smooth problems,
[
29
]
proved that a regularized Taylor approximation becomes convex when the corresponding regularization parameter is sufficiently large. This observation opened the door for using higher-order Taylor approximations to different structured problems (see, for example,
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
26
]
). Several papers have already proposed higher-order methods for solving composite optimization problems of the form (
1
) with complexity guarantees, see e.g.,
[
7
,
24
,
19
,
16
]
. For example, in a recent paper
[
19
]
, the authors consider fully composite problems (which cover, as a particular case, (
1
)), and when the data are
p
times continuously differentiable with the
p
th derivative Lipschitz and uniformly convex, they derive linear convergence in function values. In
[
7
]
, the authors derive worst-case complexity bounds for computing approximate first-order critical points using higher-order derivatives for problem (
1
) with nonlinear equality constraints. Paper
[
24
]
also considers problem (
1
) with nonlinear equality constraints and employs an approach wherein the objective is approximated with a model of arbitrarily high-order. Each iteration requires the computation of an approximate KKT point for the subproblem, devoiding of any constraint qualification condition. The authors show that their scheme converges to an approximate KKT point within
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-\frac{p+\beta}{p+1-\beta}}\right)
iterations, where
\beta\in[0,1]

In this paper, we develop a higher-order method for solving composite (non)convex minimization problems with smooth (non)convex functional constraints (
1

Contributions.
Our main contributions are as follows:

(i) We introduce a new moving Taylor approximation (MTA) method for solving problem (
1
)
that requires starting from an initial feasible point
. Our framework is flexible in the sense that we can approximate the objective function and the constraints with higher-order Taylor approximations of different degrees (i.e., we can approximate the smooth part of the objective function with a Taylor approximation of degree
p
and the constraints with a Taylor approximation of degree
q
).

(ii) We derive convergence guarantees for MTA algorithm for (non)convex problems with smooth (non)convex functional constraints. More precisely, when the data is nonconvex, we show that the iterates generated by MTA converge to a KKT point and the convergence rate is of order
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\min\left(\frac{p}{p+1},\frac{q}{q+1}\right)}\right)
, where
k
is the iteration counter and
p
and
q
are the degrees of the Taylor approximations for objective and constraints, respectively. When the data of the problem are semialgebraic, we derive (linear) sublinear convergence rates in the iterates (depending on the parameter of the KL property). Moreover, for convex problems, we derive a global sublinear convergence rate of order
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\min(p,q)}\right)
in function values, and additionally, if the objective function is uniformly convex, we derive a (superlinear) linear convergence rate (depending on the degree of the uniform convexity). The convergence rates obtained in this paper are summarized in Table
1
.

(iii) Note that the subproblem we need to solve at each iteration of MTA is usually nonconvex and it can have local minima. However, we show for
p,q\leq 2
that our approach is implementable, since this subproblem is equivalent to minimizing an explicitly written convex function over a convex set that can be solve using efficient convex optimization tools. We believe that this is an additional step towards practical implementation of higher-order (tensor) methods in smooth nonconvex optimization problems with smooth nonconvex functional constraints.

Besides providing a unifying analysis of higher-order methods, in special cases, where complexity bounds are known for some particular algorithms, our convergence results recover the existing bounds. For example, for
p=q=1
, we recover the convergence results obtained in
[
3
,
38
]
in the nonconvex setting. We also recover the sublinear convergence rate in the convex case derived in
[
11
]
for
p=q=1
, as well as the linear convergence rate in function values obtained in
[
19
]
, but we only assume uniform convexity on the objective and not on the constraints.

We denote a finite-dimensional real vector space with
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{*}
its dual space composed of linear functions on
\mathbb{E}
. For

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert=\langle Bx,x\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{E},\qquad\lVert g\rVert_{*}=\langle g,B^{-1}g\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}.

For a twice differentiable function

and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)

\nabla\phi(x)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)h\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
for all
x\in\text{dom}\;\phi
,

. Throughout the paper, we consider
p,q
positive integers. In what follows, we often work with directional derivatives of function
\phi
at
x
along directions

of order

D^{p}\phi(x)[h_{1},\cdots,h_{p}]
, with
i=1:p
. If all the directions
h_{1},\cdots,h_{p}
are the same, we use the notation

D^{p}\phi(x)[h]
, for
h\in\mathbb{E}.

Note that if
\phi
is
p
times differentiable, then
D^{p}\phi(x)
is a symmetric
p
-linear form. Then, its norm is defined as
[
29
]
:

\displaystyle\lVert D^{p}\phi(x)\rVert=\max\limits_{h\in\mathbb{E}}\left\{D^{p}\phi(x)[h]^{p}:\lVert h\rVert\leq 1\right\}.

Further, the Taylor approximation of order
p
of
\phi
at
x\in\text{dom}\;\phi
is denoted with:

\displaystyle T_{p}^{\phi}(y;x)=\phi(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{i!}D^{i}\phi(x)[y-x]^{i}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{E}.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a
p
differentiable function on
\mathbb{E}
. Then, the
p
derivative is Lipschitz continuous if there exist a constant
L_{p}^{\phi}>0
such that:

\|D^{p}\phi(x)-D^{p}\phi(y)\|\leq L_{p}^{\phi}\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{E}.

(2)

It is known that if (
2
) holds, then the residual between the function and its Taylor approximation can be bounded
[
29
]
:

|\phi(y)-T_{p}^{\phi}(y;x)|\leq\frac{L_{p}^{\phi}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x\rVert^{p+1}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{E}.

(3)

If
p\geq 2
, we also have the following inequalities valid for all
x,y\in\mathbb{E}
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla T_{p}^{\phi}(y;x)\|_{*}\leq\frac{L_{p}^{\phi}}{p!}\|y-x\|^{p},

(4)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\phi(y)-\nabla^{2}T_{p}^{\phi}(y;x)\|\leq\frac{L_{p}^{\phi}}{(p-1)!}\|y-x\|^{p-1}.

(5)

For the Hessian, the norm defined in (
5
) corresponds to the spectral norm of self-adjoint linear operator (maximal module of all eigenvalues computed w.r.t.
B
). We say that
\phi
is uniformly convex of degree
\theta
with constant
\sigma
if:

\displaystyle\phi(y)\geq\phi(x)+\langle\nabla\phi(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\sigma}{\theta}\|y-x\|^{\theta}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{E}.

For a convex function
h:\mathbb{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\partial h(x)
its subdifferential at
x
that is defined as
\partial h(x):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{E}^{*}:h(y)\geq h(x)+\langle\lambda,y-x\rangle\;\forall y\in\mathbb{E}\}
. Denote
S_{f}(x):=\text{dist}(0,\partial f(x))
. Let us also recall the definition of a function satisfying the
Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL)
property (see
[
10
]
for more details).

Definition 1
.

A proper lower semicontinuous function
f:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
satisfies Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property on the compact set
\Omega\subseteq\text{dom}\;f
on which
f
takes a constant value

if there exist
\delta,\epsilon>0
such that one has:

\kappa^{\prime}(f(x)-f_{*})\cdot S_{f}(x)\geq 1\quad\forall x\!:\emph{dist}(x,\Omega)\leq\delta,\;f_{*}<f(x)<f_{*}+\epsilon,

(6)

where
\kappa:[0,\epsilon]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is concave differentiable function satisfying
\kappa(0)=0
and
\kappa^{\prime}>0
.

This definition is satisfied by a large class of functions, for example, functions that are semialgebric (e.g., real polynomial functions), vector or matrix (semi)norms (e.g.,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
with

rational number), see
[
10
]
for a comprehensive list.

A moving Taylor approximation method

In this section, we introduce a new higher-order algorithm, which we call
Moving Taylor Approximation
(MTA) algorithm, for solving the constrained optimization problem (
1
). We denote the feasible set of (
1
) by
{\cal F}=\{x\in\mathbb{E}:F_{i}(x)\leq 0\;\;\forall i=1:m\}
. In this paper, we consider the following assumptions for the objective and the constraints:

Assumption 1
.

We have the following assumptions for problem (
1
):

1.

The (possibly nonconvex) function
F_{0}
is
p
-times continuously differentiable (with
p\geq 1
) and its
p
th derivative satisfy the Lipschitz condition:

\displaystyle\|D^{p}F_{0}(x)-D^{p}F_{0}(y)\|\leq L_{p}\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{E}.

2.

The (possibly nonconvex) constraints
F_{i}
are
q
-times continuously differentiable (with
q\geq 1
) and their
q
th derivatives satisfy the Lipschitz condition:

\|DF_{i}^{q}(x)-DF_{i}^{q}(y)\|\leq L_{q}^{i}\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{E},\;i=1:m.

3.

h
is simple, proper, convex and locally Lipschitz continuous function.

Next, we assume that our problem is feasible and has bounded level sets:

Assumption 2
.

Problem (
1
) is feasible, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset
and the set
\mathcal{A}(x_{0}):=\{x\in\mathbb{E}:x\in{\cal F}\;\text{and}\;F(x)\leq F(x_{0})\}
is bounded for any fixed
x_{0}\in{\cal F}
.

Finally, we assume that the Mangasarian-Fromovitch constraint qualification (MFCQ) holds for the problem (
1
):

Assumption 3
.

The MFCQ holds for the optimization problem (
1
):

\forall x\in{\cal F}\;\;\exists d\in\mathbb{E}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\langle\nabla F_{i}(x),d\rangle<0\;\;\forall i\in I(x),

where
I(x):=\{i\in[m],\;F_{i}(x)=0\}.

Note that Assumptions
2
and
3
are standard in the context of nonlinear programming. In particular, the MFCQ guarantees the existence of bounded Lagrange multipliers satisfying the KKT optimality conditions. Note that in general, for an optimization algorithm, if one wants to prove only local convergence rates around a local minimum

3

[
31

22

\displaystyle|F_{0}(y)-T_{p}^{F_{0}}(y;x)|\leq\frac{L_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|y-x\|^{p+1},

\displaystyle|F_{i}(y)-T_{q}^{F_{i}}(y;x)|\leq\frac{L_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|y-x\|^{q+1},\;i=1:m,

\displaystyle s_{M_{q}^{i}}(y;x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}T_{q}^{F_{i}}(y;x)+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|y-x\|^{q+1},

4
Nonconvex convergence analysis

In this section we assume that the constrained optimization problem (
1
) is nonconvex, i.e.,
F_{i}
’s, for
i=0:m
, are (possibly) nonconvex functions. Then, the subproblem (
9
) is usually also nonconvex. Consequently, in this section we assume that
x_{k+1}
satisfies the descent (
10
), and, additionally, is an
approximate stationary point
of the subproblem (
9
) as defined in the following assumption:

Assumption 4
.

Given
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}>0
, there exist Lagrange multipliers
u^{k+1}=(u_{1}^{k+1},\cdots,u_{m}^{k+1})\geq 0
and
\Lambda_{k+1}\in\partial h(x_{k+1})
such that for all

, the following approximate KKT (A-KKT) conditions hold for

corresponding to the subproblem (
9
) in MTA algorithm:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla s_{M_{p}}^{M}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+\Lambda_{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{k+1}\nabla s_{M_{q}^{i}}(x_{k+1};x_{k})\right\|\leq\eta_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{\min(p,q)},

\displaystyle|u_{i}^{k+1}s_{M_{q}^{i}}(x_{k+1};x_{k})|\leq\frac{\eta_{2}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}\!-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\quad\forall i=1:m,

(11)

\displaystyle\left(\!s_{M_{q}^{i}}(x_{k+1};x_{k})\!\right)_{+}\!\!\leq\frac{\eta_{3}}{(q\!+\!1)!}\|x_{k+1}\!-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\quad\forall i=1:m,\;\text{ where }(a)_{+}=\max(0,a).

Similar approximate KKT conditions have been studied in
[

[
1

\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=\eta_{3}=0

x_{k+1}

3

\zeta\in\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle F_{i}(x)+\langle\nabla F_{i}(x),\zeta-x\rangle+\sum_{j=2}^{q}\frac{1}{j!}\nabla^{j}F_{i}(x)[\zeta-x]^{j}+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|\zeta-x\|^{q+1}<0,

for all

and

such that

, satisfies strictly these inequalities provided that

is sufficiently small. Indeed, for an inactive constraint
F_{i}(x)<0
, we have:

\displaystyle F_{i}(x)+\langle\nabla F_{i}(x),\zeta-x\rangle+\sum_{j=2}^{q}\frac{1}{j!}\nabla^{j}F_{i}(x)[\zeta-x]^{j}+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|\zeta-x\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle=F_{i}(x)+t\langle\nabla F_{i}(x),d\rangle+\sum_{j=2}^{q}t^{j}\frac{1}{j!}\nabla^{j}F_{i}(x)[d]^{j}+t^{q+1}\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|d\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle F_{i}(x)+\langle\nabla F_{i}(x),\zeta-x\rangle+\sum_{j=2}^{q}\frac{1}{j!}\nabla^{j}F_{i}(x)[\zeta-x]^{j}+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|\zeta-x\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle=t\langle\nabla F_{i}(x),d\rangle+\sum_{j=2}^{q}t^{j}\frac{1}{j!}\|\nabla^{j}F_{i}(x)\|+t^{q+1}\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}<0,

provided that

4.1
Convergence rate to KKT points

In the general nonconvex case we want to see how fast we can satisfy (approximately) the KKT optimality conditions for the problem (
1
). We consider points satisfying the first order local necessary optimality conditions for problem (
1
), i.e., points which belong to
{\cal S}
:

\displaystyle{\cal S}=

\displaystyle\left\{x\in{\cal F}:\exists\;u_{i}\geq 0,\;\Lambda\in\partial h(x)\;\text{s.t.}:\nabla F_{0}(x)+\Lambda\!+\!\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x)\!=\!0,\right.

(12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.u_{i}F_{i}(x)\!=\!0,\;i=1\!:\!m\right\}.

Hence, an appropriate measure of optimality is optimality and complementary violations of KKT conditions. Therefore, for
\Lambda_{k+1}\in\partial h(x_{k+1})
we define the map:

\displaystyle{\cal M}(x_{k+1})=\max

\displaystyle\left\{\left\|\nabla F_{0}(x_{k+1})+\Lambda_{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u^{k+1}_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k+1})\right\|,\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\Big(-u^{k+1}_{i}F_{i}(x_{k+1})\Big)^{\frac{q}{q+1}},\;i=1:m\right\}.

Recall that
M_{p}>L_{p}
. For simplicity, let us introduce the following constants
C_{1}=\frac{L_{p}+M_{p}}{p!}
,
C_{2}=\left(\frac{C_{u}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})+M}{q!}+\left(C_{u}\left(\max_{i=1:m}\frac{M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\right)+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{2}}}{(q+1)!}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\right)
and

\displaystyle C=\max

\displaystyle\left(\frac{((q+1)!)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\left(C_{1}(2D)^{p-q}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}\right)(F(x_{0})-F_{\infty})^{\frac{q}{q+1}}}{M^{\frac{q}{q+1}}},\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.\frac{((p+1)!)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}\left(C_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}(2D)^{q-p}\right)(F(x_{0})-F_{\infty})^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}{(M_{p}-L_{p})^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}\right).

Then, we have the following convergence rate for the measure of optimality
\mathcal{M}(x_{k})
:

Theorem 4
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
2
hold and
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be generated by MTA algorithm. Then, there exists
\Lambda_{k+1}\in\partial h(x_{k+1})
such that:

(i)

The following bound holds:

\displaystyle{\cal M}(x_{k+1})\leq C_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{\min(p,q)}}+C_{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}.

Consequently, if
x_{k+1}=x_{k}
, then
x_{k}
is a KKT point of the original nonconvex problem (
1
).

(ii)

The sequence
\left(\mathcal{M}(x_{k})\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges to
0
with the following sublinear rate:

\displaystyle\min_{j=1:k}{\cal M}(x_{j})\leq\frac{C}{k^{\min\left(\frac{q}{q+1},\frac{p}{p+1}\right)}}.

Proof.

Let
u^{k+1}_{i}\geq 0
be given in (
4
), then from (
8
) we get for all
i=1:m

\displaystyle-u^{k+1}_{i}\frac{L_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\leq u^{k+1}_{i}\Big(F_{i}(x_{k+1})-T^{F_{i}}_{q}(x_{k+1};x_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=u^{k+1}_{i}F_{i}(x_{k+1})-u^{k+1}_{i}\left(T^{F_{i}}_{q}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad+u^{k+1}_{i}\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=u^{k+1}_{i}F_{i}(x_{k+1})-u^{k+1}_{i}s_{M_{q}^{i}}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+u^{k+1}_{i}\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq u^{k+1}_{i}F_{i}(x_{k+1})+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\eta_{2}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}}+u^{k+1}_{i}\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1},

where the the last inequality follows from (
4
)
. Since the multipliers are bounded, taking the maximum, we get:

\displaystyle\max_{i=1:m}\left\{\left(-u^{k+1}_{i}F_{i}(x_{k+1})\right)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\right\}

(13)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\max_{i=1:m}\left\{\left(u^{k+1}_{i}\left(\frac{M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\right)+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{2}}}{(q+1)!}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(C_{u}\left(\max_{i=1:m}\frac{M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\right)+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{2}}}{(q+1)!}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}.

Further, let
\Lambda_{k+1}\in\partial h(x_{k+1})
, then we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla F_{0}(x_{k+1})+\Lambda_{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u^{k+1}_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k+1})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla F_{0}(x_{k+1})-\nabla T^{F_{0}}_{p}(x_{k+1};x_{k})\|+\left\|\nabla T^{F_{0}}_{p}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+\Lambda_{k+1}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\frac{M_{p}}{p!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p-1}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})+\frac{M}{q!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q-1}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u^{k+1}_{i}\left(\nabla T^{F_{i}}_{q}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{q!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q-1}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|-\frac{M_{p}}{p!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p-1}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\quad-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u^{k+1}_{i}M_{q}^{i}+M}{q!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q-1}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}u^{k+1}_{i}\left(\nabla F_{i}(x_{k+1})-\nabla T^{F_{i}}_{q}(x_{k+1};x_{k})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{p}+M_{p}}{p!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{\min(p,q)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u^{k+1}_{i}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})+M}{q!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{p}+M_{p}}{p!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{\min(p,q)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{C_{u}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})+M}{q!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q},

where the second inequality follows from (
7
), (
8
), and Assumption
4
,
i.e., the relations (
4
)
. The last inequality follows from Lemma
3
. Combining this inequality with (

\displaystyle{\cal M}(x_{k+1})\leq\frac{L_{p}+M_{p}}{p!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{\min(p,q)}}

\displaystyle\;+\!\left(\!\frac{C_{u}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})\!+\!M}{q!}+\!\left(C_{u}\!\left(\!\max_{i=1:m}\frac{M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\right)\!+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{2}}}{(q+1)!}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}\!\right)\!\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}

\displaystyle=C_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{\min(p,q)}}+C_{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}.

Hence, the first assertion follows. Further, if

, then it follows that:

\displaystyle{\cal M}(x_{k+1})\leq\left(C_{1}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p-q}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}\right)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x_{k+1})\leq\left(C_{1}(2D)^{p-q}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}\right)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q}.

Combining this inequality with the descent from Lemma

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x_{k+1})^{\frac{q+1}{q}}\leq\frac{\left(C_{1}(2D)^{p-q}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}\right)^{\frac{q+1}{q}}(q+1)!}{M}\big(F(x_{k})-F(x_{k+1})\big).

\displaystyle\min_{j=1:k}{\cal M}(x_{j})\leq\frac{((q+1)!)^{\frac{q}{q+1}}(C_{1}(2D)^{p-q}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2})(F(x_{0})-F_{\infty})^{\frac{q}{q+1}}}{M^{\frac{q}{q+1}}k^{\frac{q}{q+1}}}.

\displaystyle{\cal M}(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\left(C_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}(2D)^{q-p}\right)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p}.

Combining this inequality with the descent from Lemma

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x_{k+1})^{\frac{p+1}{p}}\leq\frac{\left(C_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}(2D)^{q-p}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}(p+1)!}{M_{p}-L_{p}}\left(F(x_{k})-F(x_{k+1})\right).

Summing up this inequality and taking the minimum, we obtain:

\displaystyle\min_{j=1:k}{\cal M}(x_{j})\leq\frac{((p+1)!)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}(C_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+C_{2}(2D)^{q-p})(F(x_{0})-F_{\infty})^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}{(M_{p}-L_{p})^{\frac{p}{p+1}}k^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}.

(15)

Hence, combining inequalities (
14
) and (
15
), our assertion follows.

Remark 5
.

Theorem
4
, shows that there exist a subsequence of the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, generated by MTA algorithm, which converges to a KKT point of the original problem (
1
).
Moreover, from Theorem
4
(i) it follows that any fixed point of MTA algorithm is a KKT point of the original nonconvex problem (
1
).
If
p=q
, then the convergence rate is of order
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{p}{p+1}}\right)
, which is the usual convergence rate for higher-order algorithms for (unconstrained) nonconvex problems
[
24
,
27
,
15
,
16
,
26
]
. If
M=0
(in this case (
14
) is replaced with an inequality similar to the one in (
15
)), then the convergence rate in the minimum of the optimality map
\mathcal{M}(x_{k})
is of order
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\min\left(\frac{q}{p+1},\frac{p}{p+1}\right)}\right)
. Thus, if
q\leq p
we have
\frac{q}{p+1}\leq\frac{p}{p+1}
, and hence the convergence rate becomes
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{q}{p+1}}\right)
, which is worse than the rate
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{q}{q+1}}\right)
. For a better understanding of this situation, consider a particular case:
p=2
and
q=1
. Then, for
M=0
, the rate is
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{1}{3}}\right)
, while for
M>0
the rate is
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
. In conclusion, it is beneficial to have additionally the regularization of order
q+1
in the objective function since it leads to faster convergence rates.

for

.Initialize:

and

.

2:

while

do

3:

Step 1:
Compute
x_{k+1}
for (

) satisfying (

) and (

) with parameters:

and

. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\leq\rho\max\left(\kappa(S_{k}),S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}\right).

(19)

Let us assume now that
\kappa(s)=s^{1-\nu}
, where
\nu\in[0,1)
. Then, it follows that:

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\leq\rho\max\left(S_{k}^{1-\nu},S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}\right).

(20)

Further, from the KL property (
6
) and Lemma
10
, we have:

\displaystyle S_{k}^{\nu}\leq(1-\nu)\|G_{k}\|\leq(\beta_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+\beta_{2}D^{q-p})\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{p}.

Hence, combining this inequality with inequality (
18
), we further get:

\displaystyle S_{k}^{\nu}\leq(\beta_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+\beta_{2}D^{q-p})\left(\frac{2(p+1)!}{M_{p}-L_{p}}(S_{k-1}-S_{k})\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}.

Recall that
\Gamma=\left(\beta_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{1}}+B_{2}D^{q-p}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}\frac{2(p+1)!}{M_{p}-L_{p}}
, then we have the following recurrence:

\displaystyle S_{k}^{\frac{\nu(p+1)}{p}}\leq\Gamma(S_{k-1}-S_{k}).

(21)

1.

Let
\nu=0
. If
S_{k}>0
for all
k\geq k_{1}
, then we have
\frac{1}{\Gamma}\leq S_{k-1}-S_{k}
. Letting
k\mapsto\infty
we get
0<\frac{1}{\Gamma}\leq 0
which is a contradiction. Hence, there exist
k>k_{1}
such that
S_{k}=0
and finally
S_{k}\mapsto 0
in a finite number of steps and from (
20
),
x_{k}\mapsto x^{*}
in a finite number of iterations.

2.

Let
\nu\in(0,\frac{p}{p+1}]
, then
\frac{\nu(p+1)}{p}\leq 1
and
1-\nu\geq\frac{1}{p+1}
. Thus using Lemma 3 in
[
26
]
, for
\theta=\frac{\nu(p+1)}{p}
, we get:

\displaystyle S_{k}\leq\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\frac{p}{\nu(p+1)}}}{1+\Gamma^{\frac{p}{\nu(p+1)}}}\right)^{k-k_{1}}S_{0}.

Since we have
S_{k}<1
for all
k>k_{1}
and
S_{k}
is nonincreasing, then we have
\max\left(S_{k}^{1-\nu},S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}\right)=S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}
and thus:

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\leq\rho\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\frac{p}{\nu(p+1)^{2}}}}{(1+\Gamma^{\frac{p}{\nu(p+1)}})^{\frac{1}{p+1}}}\right)^{k-(1+k_{1})}S_{0}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}.

3.

Let
\frac{p}{p+1}<\nu<1
, then
\frac{\nu(p+1)}{p}>1
and thus using Lemma 3 in
[
26
]
for
\theta=\frac{\nu(p+1)}{p}
, there exists
\alpha>0
such that:

\displaystyle S_{k}\leq\frac{\alpha}{(k-k_{1})^{\frac{p}{\nu(p+1)-p}}}.

In this case, we have
\max\left(S_{k}^{1-\nu},S_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}\right)=S_{k-1}^{1-\nu}
and thus we have:

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|\leq\frac{\rho\alpha^{1-\nu}}{(k-k_{1})^{\frac{p(1-\nu)}{\nu(p+1)-p}}}.

Hence, our assertions follow.

Remark 12
.

In this section, we derived global convergence rates using higher-order information for solving nonconvex problems with functional constraints provided that the Lyapunov function
\xi_{p}
satisfies the KL property. Note that if
F
and
F_{i}
’s, for
i=1:m
, satisfy the KL property, then
\xi_{p}
also satisfies the KL property. For
p=q=1
, we recover the convergence results from
[
38
,
12
]
.

5
Convex convergence analysis

In this section we assume that the functions
F_{i}
’s, for
i=0:m
, are convex functions. Then, the subproblem (
9
) is also convex, provided that
M_{p}\geq pL_{p}
and
M_{q}^{i}\geq qL_{q}^{i}
for
i=1:m
(see Theorem 2 in
[
29
]
). Hence, in this section we assume that
x_{k+1}
is a minimum point of the convex subproblem (
9
). Note that since the Lagrangian function (see Section
4.3
)
x\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{sp}(y;x;u)
,
is convex (provided that
M_{p}\geq pL_{p}
and
M_{q}^{i}\geq qL_{q}^{i}
for
i=1:m
), then from the optimality condition of
x_{k+1}
it follows that
x_{k+1}
is the global minimizer of the function
\mathcal{L}_{sp}(y;x_{k};u^{k+1})
. Let us introduce the following constants
D_{1}=\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}}{(p+1)!}
and
D_{2}=\left(\frac{M+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{u}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})}{(q+1)!}\right)
, then we have the following sublinear convergence rate:

Theorem 13
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold and, additionally,
F_{i}
’s, for
i=0:m
, be convex functions. Let also the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be generated by MTA algorithm with
x_{0}\in\cal F
,
M_{p}\geq pL_{p},M>0
and
M_{q}^{i}\geq qL_{q}^{i}
for all
i=1:m
. Then, we have the following sublinear convergence rate:

\displaystyle F(x_{k})-F^{*}\leq\frac{2\max\Big((p\!+\!1)^{p+1},(q\!+\!1)^{q+1}\Big)\Big(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\Big)}{k^{\min(p,q)}}\;\;\forall k\geq 1.

Proof.

If the subproblem (
9
) is convex and Assumption
3
holds, then we proved on page 7 that the Slater’s condition holds for this subproblem and consequently A-KKT conditions (
4
) are valid with
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=\eta_{3}=0
.
Then, we have:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:fineq},\eqref{eq:CAKKT}}}{{\leq}}T_{p}^{F_{0}}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+\frac{M_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{p+1}+\frac{M}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\quad+h(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{k+1}\left(T^{F_{i}}_{q}(x_{k+1};x_{k})+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{x}T_{p}^{F_{0}}(x;x_{k})+\frac{M_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|x-x_{k}\|^{p+1}+\frac{M}{(q+1)!}\|x-x_{k}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\quad+h(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{k+1}\left(T^{F_{i}}_{q}(x;x_{k})+\frac{M_{q}^{i}}{(q+1)!}\|x-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:fineq}}}{{\leq}}\min_{x}F(x)+\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|x-x_{k}\|^{p+1}+\frac{M}{(q+1)!}\|x-x_{k}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{k+1}\left(F_{i}(x)+\frac{(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})}{(q+1)!}\|x-x_{k}\|^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1-\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha^{q+1}M}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{k+1}\Big(\alpha F_{i}(x^{*})+(1-\alpha)F_{i}(x_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{q+1}\frac{u_{i}^{k+1}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1-\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha^{q+1}M}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha^{q+1}\frac{u_{i}^{k+1}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1},

where the first equality follows from
x_{k+1}
being the global minimum of the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}_{sp}(y;x_{k};u^{k+1})
, the last inequality follows from the fact that
x_{k}
and
x^{*}
are feasible for the problem (
1
). Since the multipliers are bounded, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1-\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}}{(p+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{q+1}\left(\frac{M+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{u}(M_{q}^{i}+L_{q}^{i})}{(q+1)!}\right)\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}.

Hence, we get the following relation:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}\!+\!(1\!-\!\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}D_{1}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}+\alpha^{q+1}D_{2}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1},

(22)

which implies that:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1\!-\!\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+\alpha^{q+1}D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}.

(23)

If
q\leq p
, then from inequality (
23
), we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1\!-\!\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right).

Since the previous inequality holds for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
, then we consider:

\displaystyle A_{k}:=k(k+1)\cdots(k+q),\;\;a_{k+1}:=A_{k+1}-A_{k}=\frac{q+1}{k+q+1}A_{k+1},\;\;\alpha_{k}:=\frac{a_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}.

Therefor, we obtain:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}F^{*}+\frac{A_{k}}{A_{k+1}}F(x_{k})+\left(\frac{a_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}\right)^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a_{k+1}}{A_{k+1}}F^{*}+\frac{A_{k}}{A_{k+1}}F(x_{k})+\left(\frac{q+1}{k+q+1}\right)^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right).

Multiplying both sides with
A_{k+1}
, we get:

\displaystyle A_{k+1}(F(x_{k+1})-F^{*})

\displaystyle\leq A_{k}(F(x_{k})-F^{*})

\displaystyle\quad+A_{k+1}\left(\frac{q+1}{k+q+1}\right)^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq A_{k}(F(x_{k})-F^{*})+(q+1)^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right).

Summing up this inequality, we get for all
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle F(x_{k})-F^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{A_{k}}k(q+1)^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(q+1)^{q+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right)}{k^{q}}.

Further, if
p\leq q
, then from inequality (
23
), we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1-\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right).

Following the same procedure as before, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k})-F^{*}\leq\frac{(p+1)^{p+1}\left(D_{1}(2D)^{p+1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q+1}\right)}{k^{p}}.

Hence, our assertion follows.

Remark 14
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first convergence rate using higher-order information for a convex problem with smooth functional constraints. Note that for
p=1
and
q=1
, we recover the convergence rate in
[
11
]
. If
m=0
, then we recover the convergence rate in
[
29
]
.

5.1
Uniform convex convergence analysis

In this section, we derive (super)linear convergence in function value for the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by MTA algorithm, provided that the objective function
F
is uniformly convex of degree
\theta
with constant
\sigma
and the constraints
F_{i}
’s are only convex functions. For simplicity, let us introduce the following constants
U=\frac{(D_{1}(2D)^{p-q}+D_{2})(q+1)!}{\sigma}
and
\bar{U}=\frac{(D_{1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q-p})(p+1)!}{\sigma}
, where
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are defined in Section
5
. Then, we can establish the following convergence rate in function values:

Theorem 15
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
13
hold. Additionally, assume that
F
is uniformly convex of order
\theta
with constant
\sigma
. Then, the following hold:

(i) If
\theta=\min(p+1,q+1)
, then:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left(\left(1-\frac{q}{U^{\frac{1}{q}}(q\!+\!1)^{1+\frac{1}{q}}}\right)\!,\!\left(1-\frac{p}{\bar{U}^{\frac{1}{p}}(p\!+\!1)^{1+\frac{1}{p}}}\right)\right)(F(x_{k})-F^{*}).

(ii) If
\theta<\min(p+1,q+1)
, then:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}\leq

\displaystyle\max\left(\frac{(D_{1}D^{p-q}+D_{2})\theta^{\frac{q+1}{\theta}}}{\sigma^{\frac{q+1}{\theta}}},\frac{(D_{1}+D_{2}D^{q-p})\theta^{\frac{p+1}{\theta}}}{\sigma^{\frac{p+1}{\theta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\left(F(x_{k})-F^{*}\right)^{\frac{\min(p+1,q+1)}{\theta}}.

Proof.

If
q\leq p
, then, from inequality (
22
), we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq\alpha F^{*}+(1-\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{q+1}(D_{1}(2D)^{p-q}+D_{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}.

Let
\Lambda^{*}\in h(x^{*})
. Since
F
is uniformly convex, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k})

\displaystyle\geq F^{*}+\langle\nabla F_{0}(x^{*})+\Lambda^{*},x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\frac{\sigma}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}

\displaystyle\geq F^{*}+\frac{\sigma}{(q+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
x^{*}
satisfies the optimality condition:
\langle\nabla F(x^{*})+\Lambda^{*},x-x^{*}\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall x\in\mathcal{F}
and that the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is feasible, i.e.,
x_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
for all
k\geq 0
.
Combining the last two inequalities, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\alpha F^{*}+(1\!-\!\alpha)F(x_{k})+\alpha^{q+1}\frac{(D_{1}(2D)^{p-q}\!+\!D_{2})(q\!+\!1)!}{\sigma}(F(x_{k})\!-\!F^{*}).

Hence, it follows that:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left(1\!-\!\alpha\!+\!\alpha^{q+1}\frac{(D_{1}(2D)^{p\!-\!q}\!+\!D_{2})(q\!+\!1)!}{\sigma}\right)(F(x_{k})\!-\!F^{*}).

By minimizing the right-hand side over
\alpha
, the optimal choice is:

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha=\frac{1}{(q+1)^{\frac{1}{q}}U^{\frac{1}{q}}}\leq 1.

Replacing this choice in the last inequality, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{(q+1)^{\frac{1}{q}}U^{\frac{1}{q}}}+\frac{U}{(q+1)^{\frac{q+1}{q}}U^{\frac{q+1}{q}}}\right)(F(x_{k})-F^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{q}{U^{\frac{1}{q}}(q+1)^{1+\frac{1}{q}}}\right)(F(x_{k})-F^{*}).

Further, if
p\leq q
, then we have:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq\alpha_{k}F^{*}+(1-\alpha_{k})F(x_{k})+\alpha^{p+1}(D_{1}+D_{2}(2D)^{q-p})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}.

Since
F
is uniformly convex of degree
p+1
, we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k})\geq F^{*}+\frac{\sigma}{(p+1)!}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}.

Combining the last two inequalities and following the same procedure as in the first case, we get the following statement:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}\leq\left(1-\frac{p}{\bar{U}^{\frac{1}{p}}(p+1)^{1+\frac{1}{p}}}\right)(F(x_{k})-F^{*}).

Hence, our first assertion holds. Further, we have:

\displaystyle F(x_{k})\geq F^{*}+\frac{\sigma}{\theta}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{\theta}.

(24)

Taking
\alpha=1
in inequality (
22
) we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}\leq D_{1}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{p+1}+D_{2}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}.

Assume
q\leq p
. Since the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is bounded, then we further get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}\leq(D_{1}D^{p-q}+D_{2})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{q+1}.

Combining this inequality with (
24
), we get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}\leq\frac{(D_{1}D^{p-q}+D_{2})\theta^{\frac{q+1}{\theta}}}{\sigma^{\frac{q+1}{\theta}}}\left(F(x_{k})-f^{*}\right)^{\frac{q+1}{\theta}}.

If
p\leq q
, then we also get:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F^{*}\leq\frac{(D_{1}+D_{2}D^{q-p})\theta^{\frac{p+1}{\theta}}}{\sigma^{\frac{p+1}{\theta}}}\left(F(x_{k})-f^{*}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{\theta}},

which proves the second statement of the theorem.

Remark 16
.

In
[
19
]
the authors propose an algorithm, different from MTA, for solving problem (
1
) based on lower approximations of the functions
F_{i}
, for
i=0:m
. Moreover, in
[
19
]
both the objective and the constraints functions
F_{i}
’s are assumed uniformly convex. Under these settings,
[
19
]
derives linear convergence in function values for their algorithm. Our result is less conservative since we require only the objective function to be uniformly convex and the functional constraints are assumed convex. Note also that if
p=q=1
and
h=0
we recover the convergence rate in
[
3
]
.

6
Efficient solution of subproblem for (non)convex problems

For
p=q=1
it has been shown that the subproblem (
9
) can be solved efficiently, see
[
3
]
. In this section, we show that the subproblem (
9
) for
p=q=2
(or
p=2
and
q=1
) can be also solved efficiently using efficient convex optimization tools. To this end, for
p=q=2
and
h=0
, in order to compute
x_{k+1}
in subproblem (
9
), one needs to solve the following problem (here
M_{0}=M_{p}+M
):

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F_{0}(x_{k})+\langle\nabla F_{0}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}F_{0}(x_{k})(x-x_{k}),(x-x_{k})\right\rangle+\frac{M_{0}}{6}\|x-x_{k}\|^{3}

(25)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}:F_{i}(x_{k})+\langle\nabla F_{i}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})(x-x_{k}),(x-x_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{M_{i}}{6}\|x-x_{k}\|^{3}\leq 0\;\;i=1\!:\!m.

Denote
u=(u_{0},u_{1},\cdots,u_{m})
. Then, this problem is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}_{+}\\
u_{0}=1\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}F_{i}(x_{k})+\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})(x-x_{k}),(x-x_{k})\right\rangle+\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}M_{i}}{6}\|x-x_{k}\|^{3}.

Further, we get:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}_{+}\\
u_{0}=1\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}F_{i}(x_{k})+\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k}),x\!-\!x_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})(x\!-\!x_{k}),(x\!-\!x_{k})\right\rangle\!+\max_{w\geq 0}\left(\frac{w}{4}\|x\!-\!x_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{12(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}M_{i})^{2}}w^{3}\right).

Denote for simplicity
H(u,w)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})+\frac{w}{2}I
,
g(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k})
,
l(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}F_{i}(x_{k})
and
\tilde{M}(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}M_{i}
.
Then, the dual formulation of this problem takes the form:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}_{+}\\
u_{0}=1\end{subarray}}\;

\displaystyle l(u)\!+\!\left\langle g(u),x\!-\!x_{k}\right\rangle\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)(x\!-\!x_{k}),(x\!-\!x_{k})\right\rangle\!-\!\frac{w^{3}}{12(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}M_{i})^{2}}.

Consider the following notations:

\displaystyle\theta(x,u)\!=l(u)\!+\!\langle g(u),x\!-\!x_{k}\rangle\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\!\left\langle\!\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})\right)\!(x\!-\!x_{k}),x\!-\!x_{k}\!\!\right\rangle\!+\!\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{6}\|x\!-\!x_{k}\|^{3},

\displaystyle\beta(u,w)=l(u)-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)^{-1}g(u),g(u)\right\rangle-\frac{1}{12(\tilde{M}(u))^{2}}w^{3},

\displaystyle D=\left\{(u_{0},u_{1},\cdots,u_{m},w)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}_{+}:\;u_{0}=1\;\text{and}\;\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})+\frac{w}{2}I\succ 0\right\}.

Then, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 17
.

If there exists an
M_{i}>0
, for some
i=0:m
, then we have the following relation:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}:=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\max_{u\geq 0}\theta(x,u)=\max_{(u,w)\in D}\beta(u,w)=\beta^{*}.

Moreover, for any
(u,w)\in D
the direction
x(u,w)=x_{k}-H(u,w)^{-1}g(u)
satisfies:

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta(x(u,w),u)-\beta(u,w)=\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{12}\left(\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}+2r_{k}\right)\left(r_{k}-\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}\right)^{2},

(26)

where
r_{k}=\|x(u,w)-x_{k}\|
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\theta^{*}\geq\beta^{*}
. Indeed, using a similar reasoning as in
[
30
]
, we have:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}_{+}\\
u_{0}=1\end{subarray}}l(u)+\left\langle g(u),x-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)(x-x_{k}),(x-x_{k})\right\rangle\!-\!\frac{w^{3}}{12\tilde{M}(u)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}_{+}\\
u_{0}=1\end{subarray}}\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}l(u)+\left\langle g(u),x-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)(x-x_{k}),(x-x_{k})\right\rangle\!-\!\frac{w^{3}}{12\tilde{M}(u)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{(u,w)\in D}\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}l(u)+\left\langle g(u),x-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)(x-x_{k}),(x-x_{k})\right\rangle-\frac{w^{3}}{12\tilde{M}(u)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\max_{(u,w)\in D}l(u)-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)^{-1}g(u),g(u)\right\rangle-\frac{1}{12(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}M_{i})^{2}}w^{3}=\beta^{*}.

Let
(u,w)\in D
. Then, we have

g(u)=-H(u,w)(x(u,w)-x_{k})
and thus:

\displaystyle\theta(x(u,w),u)=l(u)+\langle g(u),x(u,w)-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{6}r_{k}^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})\right)(x(u,w)-x_{k}),x(u,w)-x_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=l(u)-\left\langle H(u,w)(x(u,w)-x),x(u,w)-x\right\rangle+\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{6}r_{k}^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})\right)(x(u,w)-x_{k}),x(u,w)-x_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=l(u)\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\!\!\left\langle\!\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})+\frac{w}{2}I\right)(x(u,w)-x_{k}),x(u,w)-x_{k}\right\rangle\!-\!\frac{w}{4}r_{k}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{6}r_{k}^{3}

\displaystyle=\beta(u,w)+\frac{1}{12\tilde{M}(u)^{2}}w^{3}-\frac{w}{4}r_{k}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{6}r_{k}^{3}

\displaystyle=\beta(u,w)+\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{12}\!\left(\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}\right)^{3}\!\!\!-\!\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{4}\!\!\left(\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}\right)\!r_{k}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{6}r_{k}^{3}

\displaystyle=\beta(u,w)+\frac{\tilde{M}(u)}{12}\left(\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}+2r_{k}\right)\left(r_{k}-\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}\right)^{2},

which proves (
26
). Note that we have:

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}\beta(u,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\|x(u,w)-x_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\tilde{M}(u)^{2}}w^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left(r_{k}+\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}\right)\left(r_{k}-\frac{w}{\tilde{M}(u)}\right).

Therefore if
\beta^{*}
is attained at some
(u^{*},w^{*})>0
from
D
, then we have
\nabla\beta(u^{*},w^{*})=0
. This implies
\frac{w^{*}}{\tilde{M}(u^{*})}=r_{k}(u^{*},w^{*})
and by (
26
) we conclude that
\theta^{*}=\beta^{*}
.

Remark 18
.

Note that in nondegenerate situations the global minimum of nonconvex cubic problem over nonconvex cubic constraints (
25
) can be computed by:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-H(u,w)^{-1}g(u),

where recall that
H(u,w)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})+\frac{w}{2}I
,
g(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k})
and
l(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}F_{i}(x_{k})
, with
(u,w)
the solution of the following dual problem:

\displaystyle\max_{(u,w)\in D}\;l(u)-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H(u,w)^{-1}g(u),g(u)\right\rangle-\frac{1}{12(\sum_{i=0}^{m}u_{i}M_{i})^{2}}w^{3},

(27)

i.e., a maximization of a concave function over a convex set
D
. Hence, if
m
is not too large, this dual problem can be solved very efficiently by interior point methods
[
28
]
.

Corollary 19
.

If there exist
M_{i}>0
, then the set
D
is nonempty and convex. If the problem (
27
) has solution, then strong duality holds for the subproblem (
25
).

In conclusion, MTA algorithm can be implementable for
p=q=2
even for nonconvex problems, since we can effectively compute the global minimum
x_{k+1}
of subproblem (
9
) using the powerful tools from convex optimization. Note that a similar analysis can be derived for
p=2
and
q=1
. Next, we show the efficiency of the MTA algorithm numerically and compare it with existing methods from literature.

7
Numerical illustrations

In this section we present numerical experiments illustrating the performance of MTA algorithm and compare it with existing algorithms from the literature. We consider an optimization problem that is based on the convex function
x\mapsto\log\left(1+\exp(a_{0}^{T}x)\right)
having gradient Lipschitz with constant
\|a_{0}\|^{2}
and hessian Lipschitz with constant
2\|a_{0}\|^{3}
, and on the nonconvex function
x\mapsto\log\left(\frac{(c^{T}x+e)^{2}}{2}+1\right)
having also gradient Lipschitz with constant
2\|c\|^{2}
and hessian Lipschitz with constant
4\|c\|^{3}
. These functions appear frequently in machine learning applications
[
39
]
. In order to make our problem nontrivial and highly nonconvex, we add quadratic regularizers, i.e., we consider the problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x}F(x)=\log\left(1+\exp(a_{0}^{T}x)\right)+\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Q_{0}x+c_{0}^{T}x+d_{0}

(28)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}:\;F_{i}(x)=\log\left(\frac{(a_{i}^{T}x+b_{i})^{2}}{2}+1\right)+\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Q_{i}x+c_{i}^{T}x+d_{i}\leq 0,\;i=1:m.

We generate the data
a_{i},b_{i},Q_{i},c_{i},d_{i}
, for
i=0:m
, randomly, where
Q_{i}
’s are symmetric indefinite matrices such that the problem is strictly feasible (this is ensured by the choice
d_{i}<\log\left(\frac{b_{i}^{2}}{2}\right)
for
i=1:m
). Hence, the problem (
28
) is nonconvex, i.e., both the objective and the constraints are nonconvex functions. Our numerical simulation are performed as follows: for given problem data and an initial feasible point
x_{0}
we compute an approximate
F^{*}
and
x^{*}
solution of (
28
) using IPOPT
[
37
]
; then, we implement our algorithm MTA(2,1) (i.e.,
p=2,q=1
), MTA(2,2) (i.e.,
p=q=2
), with the regularization parameters chosen to satisfy
M_{p}>L_{p}
and
M_{q}^{i}>L_{q}^{i}
for all
i=1:m
, and compare with SCP
[
23
]
and MBA algorithm proposed in
[
3
]
.
Recall that SCP linearizes only the nonconvex functions in the objective and constraints at the current iterate and (possibly) adds some quadratic regularizations, keeping the other convex functions unchanged, see
[
23
]
for mode details
. Note also that MBA is similar to MTA for
p=q=1
. The stopping criterion are:
F(x_{k})-F^{*}\leq 10^{-3}
and
\max_{i=1:m}(0,F_{i}(x_{k}))\leq 10^{-3}
and each subproblem is solved using IPOPT (i.e., for MTA(2,1) and MTA(2,2) we use IPOPT to solve the corresponding dual subproblem (
27
) at each iteration). The results are given in Table
2
for different choices of the problem dimension (
n
) and number of constraints (
m
). In the table we report the cpu time and number of iterations for each method. From our numerical simulations, one can observe that our MTA(2,1) performs better than MBA and SCP methods, although the theoretical convergence rates are the same for all three methods (see
[
3
,
38
]
). Moreover, increasing
p
and
q
, e.g., algorithm MTA(2,2), leads to even much better performance for our algorithm, i.e., MTA(2,2) is superior to MTA(2,1), MBA and SCP, which is expected from our convergence theory. Figure 1 also shows that increasing the approximation orders
p
and
q
is beneficial in our MTA algorithm, leading to better performance than first order methods (e.g. MBA and SCP) or than MTA(2,1).

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed a higher-order algorithm for solving composite problems with smooth functional constraints, called MTA. Our method uses higher-order derivatives to build a model that approximates the objective and the functional constraints. We have proven global convergence guarantees in both nonconvex and convex cases. We have also shown that our algorithm MTA is implementable and efficient in numerical simulations.
Our approach requires starting from a feasible point, hence it will be important to explore strategies allowing to relax this assumption.
