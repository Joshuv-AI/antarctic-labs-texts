Existence and decay of traveling waves for the nonlocal Gross–Pitaevskii equation
André de Laire, Salvador López-Martínez

Abstract
We consider the nonlocal Gross–Pitaevskii equation that models a Bose gas with general nonlocal interactions between particles in one spatial dimension, with constant density far away. We address the problem of the existence of traveling waves with nonvanishing conditions at infinity, i.e. dark solitons. Under general conditions on the interactions, we prove existence of dark solitons for almost every subsonic speed. Moreover, we show existence in the whole subsonic regime for a family of potentials. The proofs rely on a Mountain Pass argument combined with the so-called “monotonicity trick”, as well as on a priori estimates for the Palais–Smale sequences. Finally, we establish properties of the solitons such as exponential decay at infinity and analyticity.



Keywords:

Nonlocal Schrödinger equation, Gross–Pitaevskii equation, traveling waves, dark solitons, nonzero conditions at infinity, decay, analyticity.

Introduction

1.1
The problem

In order to describe the dynamics of a weakly interacting Bose gas,
Gross
[
30
]
and
Pitaevskii
[
51
]
found
that the wavefunction
\Psi
governing the condensate satisfies
a Schrödinger equation, that in dimension one and in its dimensionless form, is given by

i\partial_{t}\Psi=-\partial_{xx}\Psi+\Psi\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\Psi(y,t)|^{2}V(x-y)\,dy,\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.

(1.1)

Here,
\Psi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
and
V
describes the interaction between bosons. In their works, they are interested in a function
\Psi
satisfying the nonzero condition at infinity:

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|\Psi(x,\cdot)|=1,

(1.2)

representing the fact that the density is constant far away.

Equation (
1.1
) also appears as the model for the evolution of a one-dimensional optical beam of intensity
|\Psi|^{2}
in a self-defocusing nonlocal Kerr-like medium, where
V
characterizes the nonlocal response of the medium
[
49
,
41
]
.
In this case, the condition (
1.2
) is natural when studying dark optical solitons.
In all of these physical situations,
V
is assumed to be real-valued and symmetric. Moreover, in the most typical first approximation,
V
is considered as a Dirac delta function, which leads to the standard Gross–Pitaevskii equation with nonvanishing condition at infinity, that has been intensively investigated (see e.g.
[
35
,
34
,
39
,
17
]
).

To provide a clear mathematical context to the problem, it is useful to perform the change of variables
\Psi\to e^{-it}\overline{\Psi}
, which leads to the equation

i\partial_{t}\Psi={\partial_{xx}\Psi}+\Psi(\mathcal{W}*(1-|\Psi|^{2}))\quad\text{ in }~\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},

(1.3)

where we assumed that
V*1=1
and denoted by
\mathcal{W}
the potential to make our following assumptions more clear.
Here
*
denotes the convolution in
\mathbb{R}
.
We assume from now on that
\mathcal{W}
is a real-valued even tempered distribution. In this manner, (
1.3
)
is Hamiltonian and its energy

E(\Psi(t))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}\Psi(t)|^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}*(1-|\Psi(t)|^{2}))(1-|\Psi(t)|^{2})\,dx,

is formally conserved. The (renormalized) momentum

p(\Psi(t))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle i\partial_{x}\Psi(t),\Psi(t)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\left(1-\frac{1}{|\Psi(t)|^{2}}\right)dx,

is formally conserved too whenever
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\Psi(x,t)|>0
, where
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}\bar{z}_{2})
, for
z_{1}
,
z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
(see
[
19

).

We will be interested in special solutions to (
1.3
) with boundary condition (
1.2
), the so-called dark solitons. Roughly speaking, these are localized density notches that propagate without spreading
[
38
]
. They have been observed for example in Bose–Einstein condensates
[
23
,
5
]
. More precisely, dark solitons in our context will be nontrivial finite energy solutions to (

) of the form

\Psi_{c}(x,t)=u(x-ct),

which represents a traveling wave with profile

propagating at speed
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the soliton
u
satisfies

icu^{\prime}+u^{\prime\prime}+u(\mathcal{W}\ast(1-|u|^{2}))=0\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}.

(
S({\mathcal{W},c})
)

Notice that taking the complex conjugate of

in equation (

S

(
W
,
c
)

), we are reduced to the case

By finite energy solution to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

) we mean a solution belonging to the energy space

\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})=\{v\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}):1-|v|^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\ v^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\}.

This is justified by assuming that the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{W}
is bounded, i.e. that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, by Plancherel’s identity,

E(u)\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\lVert\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\lVert 1-|u|^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

We point out that any function in the energy space satisfies (

[
26
]
).

The simplest case for (

S

(
W
,

)

) that one may consider corresponds to the

(1.4)

Thus there is a family of dark solitons belonging to the nonvanishing energy space

There is also one stationary soliton that vanishes at exactly one point, associated with the speed

Notice also that the values of

In the case of spatial dimension equal to two or three, the study of traveling waves for the contact interaction

]

, Ruiz and Bellazzini

Despite the physical interest of the most realistic case where

is a more general distribution, there are very few mathematical results concerning nonlocal interactions with nonzero conditions at infinity. To our knowledge, most of the mathematical results concerning the existence of traveling waves deal with functions vanishing at infinity (see e.g.
[
42

16
,
3
,
6
,
46

) and
the techniques used in these works cannot be adapted to include solutions satisfying (

Recently, de Laire and Mennuni

proved the existence of a branch of solutions to (

(
W
,
c
)

),
by using a minimization approach.
For

, they consider the (nondecreasing) minimization curve

E_{\text{min}}(\mathfrak{q}):=\inf\{E(v):v\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}),\ p(v)=\mathfrak{q}\},

(1.5)

and set

\mathfrak{q}_{*}=\sup\{\mathfrak{q}>0~|~\forall v\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}),\ E(v)\leq E_{\min}(\mathfrak{q})\Rightarrow\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\inf}|v|>0\}.

(1.6)

Under certain technical conditions on

, they show that

and that for any

,
the minimum associated with

and the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equation

is exactly (

S

(
W
,
c
)

), where

appears as a Lagrange multiplier.
In addition, their solutions are orbitally stable. Therefore, their result establishes the existence of a family of solutions to (

S

(
W
,

)

) parametrized by the momentum.
This theorem applies for instance to the potential

\mathcal{W}_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{\beta}{\beta-2\alpha}(\delta_{0}-\alpha e^{-\beta|x|})
,
for
\beta>2\alpha>0
, which describes a strong repulsion force when two particles are in the same place, but an attractive force otherwise. However, the results in
[

]
do not apply

, since its Fourier transform grows exponentially in the complex plane.
The first goal of this paper is to provide simple conditions on

that guarantee the existence of nontrivial finite energy solutions to (

(
W
,
c
)

), covering a large variety of relevant nonlocal interactions, such as the Gaussian potential. The second one is to determine an optimal range for

(depending on

) for which there exist finite energy traveling waves. Finally, a third goal is to establish the regularity and the decay of these solutions, and their nonexistence at critical speed.

1.2
Main results

From now on we assume that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
satisfies the following minimal regularity assumption:

(H0)

\mathcal{W}
is an even tempered distribution such that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let us remark that the condition
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is equivalent to the continuity of the application
\eta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto\mathcal{W}*\eta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[

]

As explained in
[

]
, the Bogoliubov dispersion relation

]

w(\xi)=\sqrt{\xi^{4}+2\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)\xi^{2}}.

We formally get

S

(
W
,
c
)

) with finite energy when

Observe also that if

fulfills the normalization condition (see
[
22
]

Here and in what follows we use the convention that the Fourier transform of an integrable function is

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ix\xi}f(x)dx.

In particular, the Fourier transform of the Dirac delta is

,
so that

and the nonexistence conjecture holds when

.

In order to prove existence in the whole subsonic regime, we have to restrict the potentials we work with; in particular, they will satisfy

2.13
).

We turn now to the second part of the result. Indeed, let
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
\rho>0
on
\mathbb{R}
. Assume that
\rho
satisfies (
2.13
) and consider
\theta
defined by (
2.14
). Then, one may immediately check that the equations (
2.15
) are satisfied. Hence,
u=\rho e^{i\theta}
is a solution to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

).
It only remains to verify that
u\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
if
1-\rho\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Indeed, by the Sobolev embedding theorem, we get
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
with
\rho(\pm\infty)=1
and
\rho\geq\delta
on
\mathbb{R}
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Thus

1-|u|^{2}=1-\rho^{2}=(1-\rho)(1+\rho)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, by definition of
\theta
,

|u^{\prime}|^{2}=(\rho^{\prime})^{2}+\rho^{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}=(\rho^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{4\rho^{2}}(1-\rho^{2})^{2},

which also belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, since
(1-\rho^{2})^{2}=(1-\rho)^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}
and
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

∎

In view of Proposition
2.4
, the problem of existence of vortexless finite energy solution to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

) is reduced to the existence of positive solution to (
2.13
) with
1-\rho\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Abusing of the concept of energy, we will say that a solution to (
2.13
) has finite if
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

3
The variational formulation

In this section we introduce a variational formulation that will lead to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Formally speaking, it is showed in
[
22
]
that critical points of the functional
E(u)-cp(u)
are (complex-valued) solutions to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

). Thanks to Proposition
2.4
, we may simplify the setting and work in a space of
real-valued
functions. More precisely, we will find solutions to (
2.13
) as critical points of the functional

J_{c}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
formally defined by

J_{c}(1-\rho)=\mathcal{A}(1-\rho)-c^{2}\mathcal{B}(1-\rho),\quad\text{ for }\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R}),

where

\mathcal{A}(1-\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}\ast(1-\rho^{2}))(1-\rho^{2})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{B}(1-\rho)=\frac{1}{8}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}{\rho^{2}}.

It is easy to see, thanks to Remark
2.3
, that for every solution
u\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

), the equality
J_{c}(1-\rho)=E(u)-cp(u)
holds, where
\rho=|u|
.

Notice that if
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\rho\geq 0
, then
1-\rho\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
iff
1-\rho^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by (
2.2
).
To avoid ambiguities in the definition, we will restrict
J_{c}
to
the nonvanishing set

\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})=\{v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}):\,\,1-v>0\ \text{ in }\mathbb{R}\},

which is an open in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
due to the continuous embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus
J_{c}
is defined in the variable
v=1-\rho\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
by

J_{c}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(v^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}\ast(v(2-v)))v(2-v)-\frac{c^{2}}{8}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{v^{2}(2-v)^{2}}{(1-v)^{2}}.

It is not difficult to show that the functional satisfies a mountain pass geometry (see Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
). However, it is not clear at all that the Palais–Smale sequences are bounded. In order to overcome this issue, we take advantage of the “monotonicity trick” of Struwe
[
54
]
. More precisely, we are deeply inspired by the work of Jeanjean
[
33
]
. We adapt some of his results, since several nontrivial modifications are needed due to the singular behavior of
J_{c}
. This way, we are able to obtain
bounded
Palais–Smale sequences for
almost every
speed
c\in(0,\sqrt{2})
.

We start by showing that
J_{c}
is smooth on
\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
and that the critical points provide solutions to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
c>0
. The functional
J_{c}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R}))
. Moreover, for any
v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
,
its Fréchet derivatives are given by

\displaystyle J_{c}^{\prime}(v)(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}\ast f(v))(1-v)\phi-{c^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(v)\phi,

(3.1)

\displaystyle J_{c}^{\prime\prime}(v)(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}-c^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{\prime}(v)\phi\psi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\mathcal{W}\ast(f^{\prime}(v)\psi)\big)(1-v)\phi-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\mathcal{W}\ast f(v)\big)\phi\psi,

(3.2)

for all
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, where

f(s)=s(2-s)
and
h(s)=\frac{s(2-s)(s^{2}-2s+2)}{4(1-s)^{3}}
for all
s<1
.

Remark 3.2
.

Observe that if
v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}
satisfies
J_{c}^{\prime}(v)=0
, then

-v^{\prime\prime}+\big(\mathcal{W}*f(v)\big)(1-v)-{c^{2}}h(v)=0\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}.

(3.3)

Hence, setting
\rho=1-v
and noticing that
h(1-\rho)=(1-\rho^{4})/(4\rho^{3})

and that
f(1-\rho)=1-\rho^{2}
, we conclude that
\rho
is a nontrivial solution
to (
2.13
). Therefore, by
Proposition
2.4
, this provides a nontrivial finite energy solution
u
to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

).

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

First, we recall that since
\mathcal{W}
is even, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}*g_{1})g_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}*g_{2})g_{1},\quad\text{ for all }g_{1},g_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

(3.4)

In order to differentiate the nonlocal term of
J_{c}
, by using the dominated convergence theorem, we conclude that
the functional
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{W}\ast f(v)\right)f(v)\in\mathbb{R}
admits a Fréchet derivative, given by

2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{W}\ast f(v)\right)f^{\prime}(v)\phi,\quad\text{for all }\phi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

Therefore, we easily deduce (
3.1
). Computing (
3.2
) is also straightforward.

In order to prove the continuity of
J^{\prime\prime}_{c}
in
\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
, let
v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
and consider a sequence
\{v_{n}\}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
v_{n}\to v
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, since there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
v\leq 1-\delta
on
\mathbb{R}
, it follows that
v_{n}\leq 1-{\delta}/{2}
on
\mathbb{R}
, for
n
large enough. Thus,
h^{\prime}(v_{n})\to h^{\prime}(v)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by using the dominated convergence theorem. The continuity of the other terms is standard.
∎

To apply a mountain pass argument, we need to invoke a deformation lemma.
Although there are many versions of this classical lemma, we did not find one that fits in our framework since our functional is well-defined only in the open set
\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
, and not in the whole space. For this reason, we give here a modification of Lemma 2.3 in
[
59
]

that can be applied to our purposes. Furthermore, such a version does not require any Palais–Smale condition. For the sake of completeness, we also include its proof in the appendix.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
c>0
. For some
R>0
and for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, let us consider the set

Z_{\delta}=\{v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R}):\|v\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq R+1-\delta,\ v\leq 1-\delta\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\}.

(3.5)

Assume that there exist constants
0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\delta_{3}<1
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\|J^{\prime}_{c}(v)\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})}\geq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\delta_{3}-\delta_{2}},\quad\text{ for all }v\in J_{c}^{-1}([\gamma-2\varepsilon,\gamma+2\varepsilon])\cap Z_{\delta_{1}}.

Then there exists a continuous function
h:[0,1]\times\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(i)

h(0,v)=v,\quad\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R}),

(ii)

h(t,v)=v,\quad\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\left(J_{c}^{-1}([\gamma-2\varepsilon,\gamma+2\varepsilon])\cap Z_{\delta_{1}}\right),\text{ for all }t\in[0,1],

(iii)

h(t,Z_{\delta_{3}})\subset Z_{\delta_{2}},\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,1]
,

(iv)

J_{c}(h(t,v))\leq J_{c}(v),\quad\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,1],

(v)

h(1,J_{c}^{\gamma+\varepsilon}\cap Z_{\delta_{3}})\subset J_{c}^{\gamma-\varepsilon}\cap Z_{\delta_{2}},

where
J_{c}^{d}=J_{c}^{-1}((-\infty,d])
for every
d\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.4
.

To simplify the statement and the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we used the sharp constant in the Sobolev embedding (see
[
15
, p. 138, Theorem 4]
), so that

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\lVert v^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})})\leq\sqrt{\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\lVert v^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}=\|v\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})},

(3.6)

for all
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

The following two lemmas provide the mountain pass geometry of
J_{c}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
(H1)
, and let
c\in(0,\sqrt{2\sigma})
. Then, there is a constant
r_{c}>0
such that, for every
r\in(0,r_{c}]
, there exists
l_{r}>0
,
depending on
\kappa
,
\sigma
,
c
and
r
, such that
J_{c}(1-\rho)\geq l_{r}
for every
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|1-\rho\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=r
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
\|1-\rho\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq r_{c}
for some
r_{c}\in(0,1)
to be chosen later, and let
\eta=1-\rho^{2}.
By applying Plancherel’s identity and
(H1)
, we deduce that

J_{c}(1-\rho)\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma-\kappa\xi^{2})|\widehat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi-\frac{c^{2}}{8}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\eta^{2}}{\rho^{2}}.

Using that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma-\kappa\xi^{2})|\widehat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi=\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta^{2}-\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\eta^{\prime})^{2},

and that
\eta^{\prime}=-2\rho\rho^{\prime}
, we get the lower bound

\displaystyle J_{c}(1-\rho)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}(1-2\kappa\rho^{2})+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sigma-\frac{c^{2}}{2\rho^{2}}\right)\eta^{2}.

By invoking (
3.6
), we see that
1-r_{c}\leq\rho\leq 1+r_{c}
on
\mathbb{R}
,
Thus, recalling that
2\kappa<1
and
c^{2}<\sigma
, we choose
r_{c}>0
small enough so that
1-2\kappa(1+r_{c})^{2}>0
and
\sigma-\frac{c^{2}}{2(1-r_{c})^{2}}>0
.
Consequently, using that
\eta^{2}=(1-\rho)^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}\geq(1-\rho)^{2}
, we finally obtain

J_{c}(1-\rho)\geq\ell_{r}\|1-\rho\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})},\quad\text{with }\ell_{r}=\min\left\{\frac{1-2\kappa(1+r_{c})^{2}}{2},\frac{1}{4}\left(\sigma-\frac{c^{2}}{2(1-r_{c})^{2}}\right)\right\}.

The result follows by choosing
l_{r}=\ell_{r}r^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For every
\mathfrak{c}>0
, there exists
\phi_{\mathfrak{c}}\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
J_{c}(1-\phi_{\mathfrak{c}})\in(-\infty,0)
for every
c\geq\mathfrak{c}
.

Proof.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
r>0
to be chosen later, let us consider a nonnegative even function
\phi
, with
0\leq 1-\phi^{2}\leq 1-\delta\text{ in }\mathbb{R},
satisfying the following properties:

\phi^{2}=\delta\text{ in }[0,r],\qquad\phi=1\text{ in }[r+1,\infty),\qquad\phi(x+r)=\psi(x),\text{ for all }x\in[0,1],

where
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function independent of
r
that we choose such that

\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
In particular,
1-\phi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, by Plancherel’s identity, we
get for all
c\geq\mathfrak{c}
,

\displaystyle J_{c}(1-\phi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{\|\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\phi^{2})^{2}-\frac{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}{8}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(1-\phi^{2})^{2}}{\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}}{2}\left(\|\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}-\frac{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}{2\delta}\right)r+\int_{r}^{r+1}\left[(\phi^{\prime})^{2}+\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}}{2}-\frac{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}{4\phi^{2}}\right)(1-\phi^{2})\right].

Let us choose
\delta\in(0,1)
so that
\|\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}-{\mathfrak{c}^{2}}/{2\delta}<0
.
Notice that the last integral depends on
\delta
and
\mathfrak{c}
, but not on
r
, since
\phi(x+r)=\psi(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Therefore we may take
r>0
large enough so that
J_{c}(1-\phi)<0
. In this way,
\delta
and
r
depend on only
\mathfrak{c}
and
\|\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. The proof concludes by taking
\phi_{\mathfrak{c}}=\phi
.
∎

In the rest of this section we assume that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
(H1)
, we fix
\mathfrak{c}\in(0,\sqrt{2\sigma})
and we focus on speeds
c
on the interval
(\mathfrak{c},\sqrt{2\sigma})
.
We consider the paths connecting the origin
with
1-\phi_{\mathfrak{c}}
given by Lemma
3.6
, as follows

\Gamma(\mathfrak{c})=\{g\in\mathcal{C}([0,1],\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})):\,\,g(0)=0,\,\,g(1)=1-\phi_{\mathfrak{c}}\}.

Thanks to the mountain pass geometry (Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
) and to the continuity of
J_{c}
in
\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
, the so-called mountain pass level is well-defined and is positive:

\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}(c)\coloneqq\inf_{g\in\Gamma(\mathfrak{c})}\max_{t\in[0,1]}J_{c}(g(t))>0,\quad\text{ for all }c\in[\mathfrak{c},\sqrt{2\sigma}).

Notice that the
\Gamma(\mathfrak{c})
and
\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}(c)
are not standard since the paths take values on the set
\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
(and not on a vector space). We recall that the advantage of working only on
\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
is that
J_{c}
is smooth. However, the drawback of this setting if that one needs to control
J_{c}
near
\partial\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
in some sense. In fact, in principle there might exist a sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}\subset 1-\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\inf_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}
tends to zero but
J_{c}(1-\rho_{n})
remains finite for all
n
. The next result provides some properties of the functional
\mathcal{B}
that prevent undesirable phenomena to happen, so we can deal with the singular behavior near
\partial\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Given
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, it holds that

\mathcal{B}(1-\rho)<+\infty\iff 1-\rho\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R}).

More precisely, if
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\|1-\rho\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\mathcal{B}(1-\rho)\leq R.

(3.7)

for some
R>0
, then there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\rho\geq\delta
on
\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

We first prove the equivalence. Let
1-\rho\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
, so that
\rho
is continuous,
\rho>0
on
\mathbb{R}
and
\rho(\pm\infty)=1
. Thus, taking
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho(x_{0})=\min_{\mathbb{R}}\rho>0
, we have

8\mathcal{B}(1-\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}{\rho^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\leq\frac{\|1-\rho\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\|1+\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}{\rho(x_{0})^{2}}<+\infty.

For the converse implication, we consider

\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})

such that
\min_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\leq 0
. Since
\rho(\pm\infty)=1
, we deduce that there is
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho(x_{0})=0
and
\rho(x)>0
for all
x>x_{0}
.
Thus, the continuous embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{C}^{0,{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R})
implies that

\rho(x)^{2}=(\rho(x)-\rho(x_{0}))^{2}\leq\|1-\rho\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})}(x-x_{0}),\quad\text{for all }x>x_{0},

so that
\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}{1}/{\rho^{2}}=+\infty
, for every
\delta>0
. Now we choose
\delta>0
such that
\min_{[x_{0},x_{0}+\delta]}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}>0
. Hence,

8\mathcal{B}(1-\rho)\geq\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}\frac{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\geq\min_{[x_{0},x_{0}+\delta]}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}=+\infty.

Thus the equivalence
\mathcal{B}(1-\rho)<+\infty\iff 1-\rho\in\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
holds true.

We turn now to the proof of the fact that
\rho\geq\delta
provided that (
3.7
) holds. First, we have already proved that any
\rho\in 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying (
3.7
) belongs to
1-\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
. We argue now by contradiction and assume that there exist
R>0
and a sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}\subset 1-\mathcal{NV}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\rho_{n}
satisfies (
3.7
) for all
n
but
\min_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Since
\{\rho_{n}(\cdot+x_{n})\}\subset 1+H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
still satisfies (
3.7
) for any sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
, then we may assume without loss of generality that
\rho_{n}(0)=\min_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
By the embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{C}^{0,{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R})
, we deduce that there is a constant
C>0
such that

\|1-\rho_{n}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|1-\rho_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq CR
for all
n
, so that

\rho_{n}(x)\leq\rho_{n}(0)+CR\sqrt{x},\quad\text{ for all }x>0,\ \text{for all }n.

We conclude as before that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\delta}{1}/{\rho_{n}^{2}}=+\infty
, for every
\delta>0
. If there exists
\delta>0
such that the sequence
\{\min_{[0,\delta]}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}\}
is bounded away from zero, then we may argue as above to conclude that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{B}(1-\rho_{n})=+\infty
, a contradiction. Otherwise, for every
\delta>0
, up a subsequence (that depends on
\delta
),
\lim_{n\to\infty}\min_{[0,\delta]}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}=0
. For some fixed
\delta>0
to be chosen below, we consider the mentioned subsequence, which we do not relabel, and we take
\{x_{n}\}\subset[0,\delta]
such that
\rho_{n}(x_{n})=\max_{[0,\delta]}\rho_{n}
. Observe that
\rho_{n}(x_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
and

\rho_{n}(x_{n})-\rho_{n}(0)\leq CR\sqrt{x_{n}}\leq CR\sqrt{\delta},\quad\text{ for all }n.

As the left hand side of the previous inequality tends to one as
n\to\infty
, choosing
0<\delta<1/(CR)^{2}
leads one more time to a contradiction. The proof is now concluded.
∎

Following the ideas of
[
33
]
, we observe that
\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}(c)
is a nonincreasing function of
c
. Therefore, its derivative
\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}^{\prime}(c)
exists for almost every
c\in[\mathfrak{c},\sqrt{2\sigma}]
. The points of differentiability of
\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}
will be crucial in our arguments. For this reason
we introduce the set

\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{c}}=\{c\in(\mathfrak{c},\sqrt{2\sigma}):\ \gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}\text{ is differentiable at }c\}.

As we have pointed out,

|\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{c}}|=|(\mathfrak{c},\sqrt{2\sigma})|=\sqrt{2\sigma}-\mathfrak{c}.

(3.8)

Now we can state the following result due to Jeanjean
[
33
]
adapted to our setting.

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
(H1)
. Let
c\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{c}}

and let
\{c_{n}\}
be an increasing sequence such that
c_{n}\to c
. Then there exist a sequence
\{g_{n}\}\subset\Gamma(\mathfrak{c})
and a constant
R=R(\gamma^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{c}}(c))>0
such that the following holds:

(i)

For any
t\in[0,1]
such that
J_{c}(g_{n}(t))\geq\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}(c)-(c-c_{n})
, we have the estimate

\|g_{n}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\mathcal{B}(g_{n}(t))\leq R.

(ii)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}J_{c}(g_{n}(t))\leq\gamma_{\mathfrak{c}}(c)+(-\gamma^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{c}}(c)+2)(c-c_{n}).

Proof.

The proof is exactly as the one of
[
33
, Proposition 2.1]
. We only point out that, in our case, we conclude using the coerciveness of
\mathcal{A}
. We also stress that the estimate
\mathcal{B}(g_{n}(t))\leq R
follows directly from the proof of
[

4.2
Refined study of Palais–Smale sequences

We start by recalling a classical result of profile decomposition of a bounded sequence,
that is a refinement of the Banach–Alaoglu theorem. We use here the version given in Theorem 4.6.5 in
[
55
]
(see also
[
31
,
4
]
).

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\{v_{n}\}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be a bounded sequence. Then
there exist a family of concentration profiles
\{w_{j}\}\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R})

and points
\{y_{n,j}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that, on a renumbered subsequence,

y_{n,1}=0,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}|y_{n,i}-y_{n,j}|\to\infty,\text{ if }i\neq j,

v_{n}(\cdot+y_{n,j})\rightharpoonup w_{j}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
v_{n}(\cdot+y_{n,j})\to w_{j}
in
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
,

v_{n}-S_{n}\to 0\text{ in }L^{q}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\text{ where }S_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{j}(\cdot-y_{n,j}),

(4.17)

for all
q\in(2,\infty)
. Moreover, the series
S_{n}
converges in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
unconditionally and uniformly in
n
, and for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
\{\alpha_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
q\in(2,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle v_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{n,j}+r_{n,k},\text{ with }\lim_{k\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}r_{n,k}(\cdot-\alpha_{n})\varphi\right|=\lim_{k\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lVert r_{n,k}\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}=0,

(4.18)

where
w_{n,j}=w_{j}(\cdot-y_{n,j})
. In addition,

\lVert D^{m}v_{n}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lVert D^{m}w_{j}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\lVert D^{m}r_{n,k}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+o_{n}(1),\quad\text{for }m\in\{0,1\}.

(4.19)

In Theorem
4.9
and for the rest of the article, the notation
o_{n}(1)
stands for a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
such that
o_{n}(1)\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Besides, from now on
o_{n}(1;L^{1})
will denote a function such that
\lVert o_{n}(1;L^{1})\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\to 0
,
as
n\to\infty
.

Notice also that we added to the statement in
[
55
]
that
v_{n}(\cdot+y_{n,j})
converges to
w_{j}
in
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, by invoking the Rellich theorem.

We recall now a version of the Brezis–Lieb lemma given in

[
57
]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume that
G\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
, with
G(0)=0
, and that there exist
a>0
and

such that

|G^{\prime}(t)|\leq a(|t|+|t|^{q}),\quad\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.20)

If the sequence
\{v_{n}\}
is bounded in

and converges a.e. to

, then

G(v_{n})=G(v_{n}-v)+G(v)+o_{n}(1;L^{1}).

(4.21)

Moreover, using the notations in Theorem
4.9
, if the profile decomposition
is finite, i.e. there exists
k\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle v_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{n,j}+r_{n},\text{ with }r_{n}\rightharpoonup 0\text{ in }H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and }r_{n}\to 0\text{ in }L^{q}(\mathbb{R})\text{ for all }q\in(2,\infty),

(4.22)

then

\displaystyle G(v_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}G(w_{n,j})+G(r_{n})+o_{n}(1;L^{1}).

(4.23)

Proof.

The decomposition in (
4.21
) corresponds to Lemma 2.3 in
[
57
]
.
To show (
4.23
),
let us denote by
\tau_{n,j}

the translation by
y_{n,j}
, i.e.
\tau_{n,j}v=v(\cdot+y_{n,j})
.
Using that
y_{n,1}=0
, we have by Theorem
4.9
that
v_{n}\rightharpoonup w_{1}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, by (
4.21
) we obtain

G(v_{n})=G(w_{1})+G(v_{n}-w_{1})+o_{n}(1;L^{1}).

Now, again by Theorem

and using that

as

a.e. on

Therefore, by a change of variables,

G(v_{n}-w_{1})-G(w_{n,2})-G(v_{n}-w_{1}-w_{n,2})=o_{n}(1;L^{1}).

Combining with (

By repeating the same argument

In the following lemma we deal with the splitting of the singular term

Lemma 4.11

Let

(4.26)

Moreover, if the profile decomposition
is finite
as in (

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\delta,\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R},\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,k,

\displaystyle r_{n}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{2},\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R},\quad\text{for all }n\geq N,

\mathcal{B}(v_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{B}(w_{j})+\mathcal{B}(r_{n})+o_{n}(1).

We first prove (

We turn now to proving (

We remark that we can easily construct a bounded function

|J_{c}^{\prime}(r_{n})(r_{n})|\lesssim\lVert\mathcal{W}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert r_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\lVert o_{n}(1;L^{1})\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

This completes the proof of (

).
∎

The property (

can be seen as an a priori estimate for solutions obtained via splitting of Palais–Smale sequences. However, it is not clear how to use this property if

Lemma 4.15
.

Let

and let

S

(
W
,
c
)

). Then we have the following estimates
in terms of

(i)

Proof.

Combining (

Writing the left-hand side in terms of

Observe now that, by Plancherel’s identity,

4.3
Passing to the limit

For any

, Corollary

provides a sequence of nontrivial finite energy solutions

to

with

consists of passing to the limit in

[
7
]
in a similar context.

We start with a lemma that provides a sufficient condition for the boundedness of the sequence of energies.

Lemma 4.17
.

Assume that the hypotheses in Corollary
4.16
hold,
and let
\{\rho_{n}\}
be given by Corollary
4.16
.
Set

S_{n}^{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\rho_{n}(x)<r\}.

If for some
r\in({c}/{\sqrt{2}},1)
the sequence
\{|S_{n}^{r}|\}
is bounded, then
\{E(u_{n})\}
is also bounded.

Proof.

Let
\{c_{n}\}
and
\delta_{c}\in(0,1)
be given by Corollary
4.16
, and let us take some
r\in(0,1)
. Since
mV_{0}(c_{n})^{2}<1
for all
n
large, we have
1-m\rho_{n}^{2}
, so by invoking (
4.60
), we obtain

\displaystyle C\geq J_{c_{n}}(1-\rho_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\int_{S_{n}^{r}}\left(1-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{2\rho_{n}^{2}}\right)(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{(S_{n}^{r})^{c}}\left(1-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{2\rho_{n}^{2}}\right)(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{2r^{2}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}-\frac{c_{n}^{2}(1-\delta_{c}^{2})^{2}}{8\delta_{c}^{2}}|S^{r}_{n}|.

Since
c_{n}\to c
, choosing

, we infer that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}\leq C_{1}(1+|S_{n}^{r}|),\quad\text{ for all }n,

for some constant
C_{1}>0
independent of
n
. Thus, if
\{|S_{n}^{r}|\}
is bounded, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}\leq C_{2},

for some
C_{2}>0
.

Recall that
J_{c_{n}}(1-\rho_{n})=E(u_{n})-c_{n}p(u_{n})
(see Remark
2.3
). Then, using (
4.63
) and (
4.64
) again, we conclude

E(u_{n})=J_{c_{n}}(1-\rho_{n})+\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}}{\rho_{n}^{2}}\leq C+\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{4\delta_{c}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}\leq C_{3},

for some
C_{3}>0
.
∎

Next result is proved as Lemma 6.6 in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be a bounded sequence, and consider a sequence
\{S_{n}\}
of measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
such that
|S_{n}|\to\infty
. Then, for every
n
, there exist
x_{n}\in S_{n}
and
R_{n}>0
with
R_{n}\to\infty
such that

\int_{B(x_{n},R_{n})}|f_{n}|\to 0.

So far, we have only used the condition
(H4)
for
p=2
or
p=\infty
.
In the next lemma we will use it also for
p=1
in order to handle the weak star convergence, denoted by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}
, in
L^{\infty}
.

Lemma 4.19
.

Assume that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
(H4)
and let
\{\eta_{n}\}
be a bounded sequence in
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists
\eta\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that, up to a subsequence,

\eta_{n}\to\eta
in
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{W}\ast\eta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mathcal{W}\ast\eta
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

In addition, for any sequence
\{f_{n}\}\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

f_{n}\to f
in
L^{\infty}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
,
we have the following convergence in the sense of distributions,

f_{n}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta_{n})\to f(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta)\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}).

(4.65)

Proof.

First, a standard diagonal argument together with Ascoli–Arzela’s theorem imply that there exists
\eta\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that, up to a subsequence,

\eta_{n}\to\eta\quad\text{ in }L^{\infty}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}).

(4.66)

On the other hand, we also deduce that there is

such that, up to a subsequence,

\eta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}g
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. By (
4.66
), we get

.
Let us now fix a function

. By

, we have

and, using that

is even, we deduce that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}*\eta_{n})\varphi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta_{n}(\mathcal{W}*\varphi)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta(\mathcal{W}*\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}*\eta)\varphi.

\mathcal{W}\ast\eta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mathcal{W}\ast\eta\quad\text{ in }L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

To prove (

), we consider

,
with

, for some compact set

and notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta_{n})f_{n}\phi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta_{n})f\phi+\int_{K}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta_{n})(f_{n}-f)\eta\phi.

(4.68)

The second term in the right-hand side can be bounded by
\lVert\mathcal{W}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\eta_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\lVert f_{n}-f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K)}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}(K)},

which goes to zero by hypothesis. Since

using (

we can thus pass to the limit in (

∎

(

5
Nonexistence and properties of solitons

This section is devoted to the study of the Fourier transform of equation (
2.4
), that is

M_{c}(\xi)\widehat{\eta}(\xi)=\widehat{F}(\xi),\quad\text{with }\ M_{c}(\xi)=\xi^{2}+2\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)-c^{2},

(5.1)

where

F=2K+2\eta(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta),\quad\eta=1-|u|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad K=|u^{\prime}|^{2}.

We keep this notation for
F
and
\eta
for the rest of the Section,
and we assume, as always, that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
(H0)
.
If
M_{c}>0
a.e., we can recast (
5.1
) as

\widehat{\eta}(\xi)=L_{c}(\xi)\widehat{F}(\xi),\quad\text{with }\ L_{c}(\xi)=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{c}(\xi)=\frac{1}{M_{c}(\xi)}.

(5.2)

We will see that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{c}
plays an essential role in order to study the regularity and asymptotic behavior at infinity of the solitons given by Theorems
1.1
and
4.1
.

We also stress that
(H1)
is sufficient condition for
\mathcal{L}_{c}
to be well defined, for
c\in[0,\sqrt{2\sigma})
. Indeed, we have
M_{c}(\xi)\geq(1-2\kappa)\xi^{2}+2\sigma-c^{2}>0
for a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, so that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{c}(\xi)|d\xi\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\xi}{(1-2\kappa)\xi^{2}+2\sigma-c^{2}}<\infty.

(5.3)

Thus
L_{c}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{L}_{c}
is a bounded continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
.

We can now establish our nonexistence result for solitons with critical speed.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\geq 0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
and that there exists
\delta>0
such that one of the following holds:

(i)

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)=1-\xi^{2}/2
, for all
\xi\in(-\delta,\delta)
.

(ii)

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
is differentiable on
(-\delta,\delta)
,
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(0)=1
and
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)\not=1-\xi^{2}/2
for a.e.
\xi\in(-\delta,\delta)
.

Then
(S(\mathcal{W},\sqrt{2}))
admits no nontrivial solution in
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, assume that there exists a nontrivial solution
u\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
to
(S(\mathcal{W},\sqrt{2}))
. Then, Proposition
2.2
implies that (
5.1
) holds, i.e.

M(\xi)\widehat{\eta}(\xi)=\widehat{F}(\xi),\quad\text{with }\ M(\xi)=\xi^{2}+2\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)-2.

(5.4)

On the other hand, by virtue of Lemma
2.1
,
\eta
and
K
belong to
W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all
p\in[2,\infty]
.

Let us show that
\widehat{F}
is continuous and
\widehat{F}(0)>0
. Indeed, from (
2.6
) and from the fact that
\eta(\pm\infty)=0
, we deduce the existence of constants
R,C>0
such that

|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\leq C(\eta(x)^{2}+\eta^{\prime}(x)^{2})\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R].

Hence
K\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and, in turn,
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\widehat{F}
is continuous.
Also, it follows from (
5.4
) that we can assume that
M\widehat{\eta}
is also continuous.
Moreover, since
u
is not trivial and
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\geq 0
a.e., Plancherel’s identity yields

\widehat{F}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)dx=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)|\widehat{\eta}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi>0.

(5.5)

If assumption
(i)
holds, we deduce that
M\widehat{\eta}=0
on
(-\delta,\delta)
, so that, by (
5.4
),
\widehat{F}(0)=0
, which contradicts (
5.5
).

If assumption
(ii)
holds, then

is differentiable on
(-\delta,\delta)
,
M(0)=0
and
M(\xi)\not=0
for a.e.

. Therefore,

\widehat{\eta}(\xi)=L(\xi)\widehat{F}(\xi)\quad\text{a.e. }\xi\in(-\delta,\delta),

(5.6)

where
L=1/M
. Let us show now that
L\not\in L^{1}((-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}))
for every
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,\delta)
small enough. Expanding

around zero leads to

M(\xi)=M^{\prime}(0)\xi+o(\xi^{2}),\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in(-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}),

for some
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,\delta)
. Thus, taking
\tilde{\delta}
even smaller if necessary,

|M(\xi)|\leq(|M^{\prime}(0)|+1)|\xi|,\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in(-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}).

In consequence,

\int_{-\tilde{\delta}}^{\tilde{\delta}}L(\xi)d\xi\geq\frac{1}{|M^{\prime}(0)|+1}\int_{-\tilde{\delta}}^{\tilde{\delta}}\frac{d\xi}{|\xi|}=\infty.

In particular,
L\not\in L^{2}((-\tilde{\delta},\tilde{\delta}))
. Hence, taking (
5.6
) into account, in order
\widehat{\eta}
to belong to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, it is necessary that
\widehat{F}(0)=0
. This is again a contradiction with (
5.5
).
∎

We can prove now the nonexistence result stated in the Introduction.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\delta>0
be such that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}((-\delta,\delta))
.
Recall that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(0)=1
and
(\widehat{\mathcal{W}})^{\prime}(0)=0
.
If
(\widehat{\mathcal{W}})^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=-1
for all

\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)=1-\xi^{2}/2
,
for

, so that we are in the case (ii) of Theorem

.

Assume now that
(\widehat{\mathcal{W}})^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq-1

\delta
if necessary, we deduce by continuity that

(\widehat{\mathcal{W}})^{\prime\prime}>-1
on
(-\delta,\delta)
, or
(\widehat{\mathcal{W}})^{\prime\prime}<-1
on
(-\delta,\delta)
.
On the other hand,
by Taylor’s theorem, and using that

is even, we deduce that
for any

, there exists

such that

Thus we are in the case (ii) of Theorem
5.1
, and the conclusion follows.
∎

Proposition 5.3

On the one hand, we recall that Lemma

Lemma 5.5

(

For any

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{\ell|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{m}e^{m|x-y|}}dx\leq B\frac{e^{\ell|y|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|y|})^{m}},\quad\text{ for all }y\in\mathbb{R},

(5.10)

where

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

First, we point out that the result holds true for
\ell=0
. Indeed, (
1.14
) and Hölder’s inequality yield
\mathcal{L}_{c}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, by virtue of Lemmas

and

. We will then focus only on the case

.

From (

where
C_{1}=\|e^{m|\cdot|}\mathcal{L}_{c}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}^{q}
.
We will prove next that
e^{\ell|\cdot|}\eta\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
and
e^{\ell|\cdot|}\eta^{\prime}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\ell\in(0,m)
. In order to do so, let
\ell\in(0,m)
and, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, let us consider the functions

Since
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\ell<m

and

) and Hölder’s inequality with exponents

and

\displaystyle\int_{\{|x|>R\}}

\displaystyle|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx=\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q-r}\frac{e^{\ell r|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{rm}}|\eta(x)|^{r}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{\frac{r}{q}}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q-r}\frac{e^{\ell r|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{rm}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|F(y)|^{q}}{e^{qm|x-y|}}dy\right)^{\frac{r}{q}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{\frac{r}{q}}\left(\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx\right)^{\frac{q-r}{q}}\left(\int_{\{|x|>R\}}\frac{e^{\ell q|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{qm}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|F(y)|^{q}}{e^{qm|x-y|}}dy\right)dx\right)^{\frac{r}{q}}.

\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx\leq C_{1}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}\frac{e^{\ell q|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{qm}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|F(y)|^{q}}{e^{qm|x-y|}}dy\right)dx.

Now, by Fubini’s theorem and Lemma
5.5
, we derive

\displaystyle\int_{\{|x|>R\}}

\displaystyle|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx\leq C_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(y)|^{q}\left(\int_{\{|x|>R\}}\frac{e^{\ell q|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{qm}e^{qm|x-y|}}dx\right)dy

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}

\displaystyle\int_{\{|y|>R\}}|F(y)|^{q}\frac{Be^{\ell q|y|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|y|})^{qm}}dy

\displaystyle+C_{1}

\displaystyle\int_{\{|y|\leq R\}}|F(y)|^{q}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}\frac{e^{\ell q|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{qm}e^{qm|x-y|}}dxdy.

We will now estimate the last two integrals. On the one hand, using the inequality
||x|-|y||\leq|x-y|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\{|y|\leq R\}}

\displaystyle|F(y)|^{q}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}\frac{e^{\ell q|x|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|x|})^{qm}e^{qm|x-y|}}dxdy\leq\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{q}\int_{\{|y|\leq R\}}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}\frac{e^{\ell q|x|}}{e^{qm|x-y|}}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{q}\left(\int_{\{|y|\leq R\}}e^{qm|y|}dy\right)\left(\int_{\{|x|>R\}}e^{-(m-\ell)q|x|}dx\right)\coloneqq C_{2}/C_{1}.

On the other hand, recall that, by Lemma
2.1
,

\eta(\pm\infty)=\eta^{\prime}(\pm\infty)=(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta)(\pm\infty)=0.

Hence, equation (
2.6
) in Proposition

large enough so that

(5.12)

Therefore,

\int_{\{|y|>R\}}|F(y)|^{q}\frac{Be^{\ell q|y|}}{(1+\varepsilon e^{|y|})^{qm}}dy\leq\delta B\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx+\delta B\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx.

In sum,

\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx\leq\delta BC_{1}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx+\delta BC_{1}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx+C_{2}.

) with

\eta^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}_{c}\ast\left(4\eta^{\prime}\mathcal{W}\ast\eta+2\eta\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime}\right).

Notice that
(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime})(\pm\infty)=0

\left|4\eta^{\prime}(y)\mathcal{W}\ast\eta(y)+2\eta(y)\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime}(y)\right|^{q}\leq\delta|\eta(y)|^{q}+\delta|\eta^{\prime}(y)|^{q},\quad\text{ for all }|y|>R.

This estimate allows us to follow the same arguments as we did for

in order to deduce

\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx\leq\delta BC_{1}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx+\delta BC_{1}\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx+C_{3},

(5.14)

where

\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx+\int_{\{|x|>R\}}|\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{q}dx\leq C_{4},

.
By virtue of Fatou’s lemma, we take limits as

\int_{\{|x|>R\}}e^{\ell q|x|}|\eta(x)|^{q}dx+\int_{\{|x|>R\}}e^{\ell q|x|}|\eta^{\prime}(x)|^{q}dx\leq C_{4}.

In conclusion,
e^{\ell|\cdot|}\eta\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
,
e^{\ell|\cdot|}\eta^{\prime}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
.

Notice that both

Hence, the Sobolev’s embedding theorem implies that

and

.

We have just proved the result for

. Taking

in (

, where

5.2
Analyticity

Let us recall that for
H\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, the associated multiplier operator
\mathcal{H}
is defined by

\widehat{\mathcal{H}(\varphi)}(\xi)=H(\xi)\widehat{\varphi}(\xi),\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).

We say that
H
is an
L^{p}
-
multiplier
, with
p\in[1,\infty]
, if there exists
\alpha>0
such that

\|\mathcal{H}(\varphi)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\alpha\|\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})},\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}).

The smallest
\alpha>0
for which the previous inequality holds is the norm of the multiplier, and it is denoted by
\|\mathcal{H}\|_{p}
.
For instance, by assumption
(H0)
,
\mathcal{W}
is an
L^{2}
-multiplier and, by
(
1.23
),

\|\mathcal{W}\|_{2}=\|\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
.

We recall the so-called Hörmander–Mikhlin multiplier theorem in
[
32
,
48
]
(see also
[
44
]
) adapted to our one-dimensional setting, as follows.

Theorem 5.10

(
[
32
,
48
]
)
.

Let
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a weakly differentiable function and suppose that there exists
M>0
such that

\sup\{|\xi^{k}D^{k}H(\xi)|\ :\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\ k\in\{0,1\}\}\leq M.

(5.20)

Then
H
is an
L^{p}
-multiplier for every
p\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover,
there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
, depending only on
p
, such that

\lVert\mathcal{H}\rVert_{p}\leq C_{p}M.

Assume that
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
(H1)
, and including also the limit case
\kappa=1/2,
and that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
is (weakly) differentiable.
We will apply this theorem to
the function

H_{c}(\xi)=\frac{-\xi^{2}}{\xi^{2}+2\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)-c^{2}},\quad\text{ for }c\in[0,\sqrt{2\sigma}).

Observe that then
H_{c}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and that

\xi H^{\prime}_{c}(\xi)=\frac{2\xi^{3}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{W}}\big)^{\prime}(\xi)-4\xi^{2}\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)+2c^{2}\xi^{2}}{(\xi^{2}+2\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)-c^{2})^{2}}.

Therefore,
\xi\mapsto\xi H^{\prime}_{c}(\xi)

(5.21)

for some

. In this case, using Theorem

we conclude that

is an
L^{p}
-multiplier for every

. More precisely, for every

, there exists a constant

such that

\|\mathcal{H}_{c}(\varphi)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\alpha_{p}\|\varphi\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})},\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}),

(5.22)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{c}=H_{c}
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
be a solution to (

S

(
W
,
c
)

).
We will exploit (
5.7
) in order to prove that
\eta
is a real analytic function.
First, we prove a technical lemma.

Lemma 5.11
.

Assume that there exist
\sigma\in(0,1]
and
\kappa\in[0,1/2]
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}(\xi)\geq\sigma-\kappa\xi^{2}
a.e. on

.
Assume in addition that
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
is weakly differentiable and that there exists
C>0
such that (
5.21
) holds. Let

S

(
W
,
c
)

Then there exist

only through

(5.24)

Proof.

We start by proving (

Let us take

and

By (

(5.26)

By using also (

Moreover, by invoking the Sobolev’s embedding (see Remark
3.4

\displaystyle\|D^{j}\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|D^{j}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|D^{j+1}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}\left(\|D^{j-3}F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|D^{j-2}F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)\leq\alpha_{2}\mu_{k}.

Therefore, we take

As far as (

\tilde{k}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}

, taking (

\displaystyle\|D^{k+1}

\displaystyle F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|D^{k+1}\left(2\eta(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime})+4\eta^{\prime}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta)\right)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\|2D^{k}\left(\eta^{\prime}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime})+\eta(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime\prime})\right)+4D^{k}\left(\eta^{\prime}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime})+\eta^{\prime}(\mathcal{W}\ast\eta^{\prime\prime})\right)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}(\|D^{j+1}\eta(\mathcal{W}\ast D^{k-j+1}\eta)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|D^{j}\eta(\mathcal{W}\ast D^{k-j+2}\eta)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})})

\displaystyle+4\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}(\|(\mathcal{W}\ast D^{j+1}\eta)D^{k-j+1}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|(\mathcal{W}\ast D^{j}\eta)D^{k-j+2}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}).

\displaystyle\|D^{k+1}

\displaystyle F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 2\omega\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}(\|D^{j+1}\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\|D^{k-j+1}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|D^{j}\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\|D^{k-j+2}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})})

\displaystyle+4\omega\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}(\|D^{j+1}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|D^{k-j+1}\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}+\|D^{j}\eta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|D^{k-j+2}\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})})

\displaystyle\leq 12\omega\beta^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}\mu_{j}\mu_{k-j}.

The induction hypothesis leads to

\displaystyle\|D^{k+1}

\displaystyle F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 12\omega\beta^{2}\Bigg(2\mu_{0}\mu_{k}+\gamma^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{k\choose j}j^{j-1}(k-j)^{k-j-1}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=12\omega\beta^{2}\Bigg(2(\mu_{0}\mu_{k}-\gamma^{k}k^{k-1})+\gamma^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{k!}{j!(k-j)!}j^{(j-1)^{+}}(k-j)^{(k-j-1)^{+}}\Bigg),

where we adopt the convention

. Now, a combinatorial lemma due to Kahane
[
37
]
implies that

\|D^{k+1}F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 12\omega\beta^{2}\left(2(\mu_{0}\mu_{k}-\gamma^{k}k^{k-1})+4\gamma^{k}k^{k-1}\right).

Using the induction hypothesis again and choosing
\gamma>0
large enough so that

, we deduce that

\|D^{k+1}F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 24\omega\beta^{2}(\mu_{0}+1)\gamma^{k}k^{k-1}\leq\gamma^{k+1}k^{k-1}.

In conclusion,

\mu_{k+1}=\max\{\mu_{k},\|D^{k+1}F^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\}\leq\max\{\gamma^{k}k^{k-1},\gamma^{k+1}k^{k-1}\}=\gamma^{k+1}k^{k-1}\leq\gamma^{k+1}(k+1)^{k},

which completes the proof.

Theorem 5.12
.

Under the hypotheses of Lemma
5.11
, for every solution

to (

(

Proof.

We need to prove that the Taylor series expansion
about any point

converges with radius of convergence

. Indeed, let
