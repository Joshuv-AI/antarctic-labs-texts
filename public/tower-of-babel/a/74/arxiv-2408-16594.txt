Continuous Gaussian mixture solution for linear Bayesian inversion with application to Laplace priors
R Flock, Y Dong, F Uribe, O Zahm

Abstract
We focus on Bayesian inverse problems with Gaussian likelihood, linear forward model, and priors that can be formulated as a Gaussian mixture. Such a mixture is expressed as an integral of Gaussian density functions weighted by a mixing density over the mixing variables. Within this framework, the corresponding posterior distribution also takes the form of a Gaussian mixture, and we derive the closed-form expression for its posterior mixing density. To sample from the posterior Gaussian mixture, we propose a two-step sampling method. First, we sample the mixture variables from the posterior mixing density, and then we sample the variables of interest from Gaussian densities conditioned on the sampled mixing variables. However, the posterior mixing density is relatively difficult to sample from, especially in high dimensions. Therefore, we propose to replace the posterior mixing density by a dimension-reduced approximation, and we provide a bound in the Hellinger distance for the resulting approximate posterior. We apply the proposed approach to a posterior with Laplace prior, where we introduce two dimension-reduced approximations for the posterior mixing density. Our numerical experiments indicate that samples generated via the proposed approximations have very low correlation and are close to the exact posterior.

Introduction

In Bayesian inverse problems, the posterior density on the unknown parameter
x
, given the data
y
, is formulated using Bayes’ theorem as the product of the likelihood function
x\mapsto\pi(y|x)
and the prior density
\pi(x)
as

\pi(x|y)\propto\pi(y|x)\pi(x).

The likelihood function incorporates the forward model, the observed data and the assumed noise model. In this paper, we focus on likelihood functions with linear forward model and additive Gaussian noise (linear-Gaussian likelihood). In this setting, it is well-known that if the prior is Gaussian, the posterior is also Gaussian, e.g.,
[
23
, p.78]
. For other priors, oftentimes no closed-form expressions for the posterior exist.

We express our non-Gaussian prior as a
mixture model

[
29
]
of the form

\pi(x)=\int\pi(x|w)\pi(w)\mathrm{d}w,

where
w
is a
mixing variable
with density
\pi(w)
. Gaussian mixture models correspond to the case where
\pi(x|w)
is Gaussian, and they encompass the
finite Gaussian mixture model
(known as GMM, e.g.,
[
18
]
) and
Gaussian scale mixtures
, e.g.,
[
1
,
38
,
19
,
10
]
.
In the Bayesian context, GMMs are a valuable tool to model multimodal priors and are successfully applied in practice, for example in geophysics to model properties of the subsurface
[
20
]
and in patch-based image denoising to model a prior on image patches
[
22
]
. Gaussian scale mixtures are used in Bayesian regression analysis to model hierarchical heavy-tailed error models and priors
[
39
,
2
]
. In particular, using a Gaussian scale mixture representation for the Laplace prior leads to the well-known Bayesian LASSO
[
31
]
. Hierarchical models based on Gaussian scale mixtures are also frequently used in connection with image reconstruction
[
8
,
9
]
. More recently,
[
35
]
employed a Gaussian scale mixture to propose the extended horseshoe prior model, and
[
34
]
used a Gaussian scale mixture representation of the Student’s t-distribution to construct a hierarchical Markov random field prior.

In the first part of this paper, we derive a general closed-form expression of the posterior as a continuous Gaussian mixture of the form

\pi(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\pi(w|y)\mathrm{d}w,

under the assumptions that the prior
\pi(x)
is a Gaussian mixture, and the likelihood
\pi(y|x)
is linear-Gaussian.
The Gaussian posterior mixture naturally gives rise to a simple algorithm to obtain samples from the posterior, which consists of two sampling steps. In the first step, one samples the mixing variable
w
from the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
, and in the second step one samples the Gaussian
\pi(x|w,y)
conditioned on the previously sampled mixing variable. We propose to replace the posterior mixing density by an easy-to-sample and dimension-reduced approximation
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
and show that the Hellinger distance between
\pi(x|y)
and the approximation

\tilde{\pi}_{r}(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)\mathrm{d}w

is bounded by the Hellinger distance between
\pi(w|y)
and
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
.

In the second part of this paper, we focus on applying our framework to the case of Laplace prior, which can be formulated as a Gaussian scale mixture. Besides its usage in Bayesian regression analysis, the Laplace prior can be employed in Bayesian inference for signal processing. This is because natural signals can be effectively represented in a sparse manner using adapted bases, such as point source bases and wavelets bases
[
7
]
. Then, one can use the so-called
synthesis formulation

s=Wx
to expand a signal
s
in a suitable basis
W
for which
x
is sparse
[
15
]
. In this case, the heavy-tailed Laplace distribution is a typical prior choice to enforce sparsity in
x
.

For the case of Laplace prior, we formulate two dimension-reduced approximations
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
.
The first one is obtained by employing the certified coordinate selection (CCS) method
[
16
]
, and the second one is based on a maximum a posteriori probability (MAP) estimate similar to the Laplace approximation, see, e.g.,
[
30
]
.
We illustrate with two numerical experiments that both approximations lead to computationally efficient and nearly uncorrelated samples from the approximate posterior
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(x|y)
.
Moreover, the estimates are close to our reference solutions.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are as follows.

•

We derive a closed-form expression for continuous Gaussian posterior mixtures resulting from posteriors with a linear-Gaussian likelihood and a Gaussian prior mixture.

•

Leveraging the Gaussian posterior mixture formulation, we describe two easy-to-sample approximations to a posterior with Laplace prior, where we replace the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
with a dimension-reduced approximation
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
.

•

We numerically illustrate the benefits of the Gaussian mixture formulation by applying our dimension-reduced posterior approximations to a 1D deblurring example and a 2D high-dimensional super-resolution example with Laplace priors.

Outline.

In Section
2
, we derive the closed-form expression of the Gaussian posterior mixture for posteriors with a linear-Gaussian likelihood and a Gaussian prior mixture. In Section
3

\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
for the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
. In Section
4
, we test our approximations from the previous section on two synthetic examples: A 1D deblurring example and a 2D high-dimensional super-resolution example. We draw our conclusions in Section
5
.

2
General framework

In this section, under a certain assumption, we first introduce the general posterior mixture, which can be formulated given any likelihood function and prior mixture. Subsequently, we derive the closed-form expression of the Gaussian posterior mixture resulting from a linear-Gaussian likelihood and a Gaussian prior mixture. We end the section with a simple algorithm for sampling the Gaussian posterior mixture.

2.1
Posterior mixture

Bayesian inverse problems are formulated via the posterior probability density

\pi(x|y)=\frac{\pi(y|x)\pi(x)}{\pi(y)},

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the vector of unknown parameters and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the observed data. As discussed in the introduction,
x\mapsto\pi(y|x)
is the likelihood function of observing the data
y
,
\pi(x)
is the prior density, and
\pi(y)
is the data density, which for some fixed
y
is sometimes called the
(model) evidence
.

Definition 1

(Mixture
[
29
]
)
.

A density
\pi(x)
defined as

\displaystyle\pi(x)=\int\pi(x|w)\pi(w)\mathrm{d}w,

(2)

is a
mixture
with
component density

\pi(x|w)
,
mixing density

\pi(w)
and
mixing variable

w
.

Before we state our general posterior mixture, we need the assumption that
w
and
y
are conditionally independent given
x
.

Assumption 1
.

The data
y
and the mixing variable
w
are conditionally independent given the parameter
x
, meaning

\pi(y,w|x)=\pi(y|x)\pi(w|x).

(3)

The random variable
w
can be considered to be a hyperparameter, and thus only a property of the prior density on
x
.
Therefore, it is natural to assume that given a realization of
x
, the data
y
and
w
should be independent.
Under Assumption
1
, the joint density of
(x,y,w)
is properly defined as the product
\pi(x,y,w)=\pi(y|x)\pi(x|w)\pi(w)
.
In this case, we can represent the posterior
\pi(x|y)
as a mixture as follows.

Proposition 1

(Posterior mixture)
.

Under Assumption
1
, we can formulate the posterior mixture

\pi(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\pi(w|y)\mathrm{d}w,

(4)

where

\displaystyle\pi(x|w,y)

\displaystyle\propto\pi(y|x)\pi(x|w)

(5)

\displaystyle\pi(w|y)

\displaystyle\propto\pi(w)\int\pi(y|x^{\prime})\pi(x^{\prime}|w)\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

(6)

are respectively called posterior component density and posterior mixing density.

Proof.

See
A.1
.
∎

The posterior mixture formulation (
4
) can be used to sample from
\pi(x|y)
by first drawing
w^{(i)}\sim\pi(w|y)
and then
x^{(i)}\sim\pi(x|w^{(i)},y)
. While sampling from
\pi(x|w^{(i)},y)
in (
5
) is straightforward when the likelihood
\pi(y|x)
and the prior component
\pi(x|w)
are
conjugate distributions
, sampling from
\pi(w|y)
can be difficult in general. Therefore, we consider replacing
\pi(w|y)
with an easy-to-sample approximation
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
. The following proposition shows that we can control the resulting approximate posterior
\tilde{\pi}(x|y)
if we can control
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
in the Hellinger distance.

Proposition 2

(Certified approximate posterior mixture)
.

Let
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
be an approximation to
\pi(w|y)
in (
6
), and let
\tilde{\pi}(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}(w|y)\mathrm{d}w
be the resulting approximate posterior. By controlling the Hellinger distance

H\!\left({\pi(w|y)},{\tilde{\pi}(w|y)}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{\pi(w|y)}-\sqrt{\tilde{\pi}(w|y)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}w\leq\epsilon,

for a given
\epsilon>0
, we can ensure

H\!\left({\pi(x|y)},{\tilde{\pi}(x|y)}\right)^{2}\leq\epsilon.

(7)

Proof.

See
A.2
.
∎

2.2
Gaussian posterior mixture

We now consider the case where the component density
\pi(x|w)
is Gaussian.

Definition 2

(Gaussian mixture)
.

A Gaussian mixture
\pi(x)
is a mixture as in Definition
1
with Gaussian component density
\pi(x|w)
, meaning

\pi(x|w)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|x-\mu_{0}(w)\|_{\Sigma_{0}(w)^{-1}}^{2}\right).

(8)

Here
\mu_{0}(w)
is the component mean vector and
\Sigma_{0}(w)
is the component covariance matrix. Specifically, if
\mu_{0}(w)=0
, the mixture is called a Gaussian scale mixture
[
1
,
19
]
.

Example 1

(Gaussian mixture model (GMM))
.

In the literature, the term Gaussian mixture model (GMM) is commonly used for Gaussian mixtures with a finite number of Gaussian component densities, e.g.,
[
30
]
.
In this case, the mixing density
\pi(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}(w)p_{i}
reduces to a sum of Dirac masses with weights
p_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=1
, such that

\pi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\det\Sigma_{i}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|x-\mu_{i}\|_{\Sigma_{i}^{-1}}^{2}\right)p_{i}.

Example 2

(Laplace distribution)
.

The univariate probability density function of the Laplace distribution
\pi(x)=\frac{\delta}{2}\exp\left(-\delta|x|\right)
with zero mean and rate parameter
\delta>0
can be written as Gaussian scale mixture
[
1
]

\pi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi w}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{x^{2}}{w}\right)\pi(w)\mathrm{d}w,

(9)

with the exponential mixing density
\pi(w)=\lambda\exp(-\lambda w)
with rate parameter
\lambda=\delta^{2}/2
.

Example 3

(Student’s t-distribution)
.

The univariate probability density function of the Student’s t-distribution
\pi(x)\propto(1+\frac{t^{2}}{\nu})^{-(\nu+1)/2}
with
\nu
degrees of freedom can also be written as Gaussian scale mixture (
9
).
It has an inverse-gamma mixing density
\pi(w)\propto w^{-\alpha-1}\exp(-\frac{\beta}{w})
with shape parameter
\alpha=\nu/2
and rate parameter
\beta=\nu/2

[
1
]
.

Now let the likelihood
\pi(y|x)
be linear-Gaussian and given by

\pi(y|x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-y\|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}}^{2}\right),

(10)

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and a positive definite covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.
This likelihood corresponds to the data-generating model
y=Ax+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}})
is Gaussian noise.
A posterior with the linear-Gaussian likelihood (
10
) and a prior formulated as Gaussian mixture (
8
) can be formulated as Gaussian posterior mixture. We give the explicit expressions for the posterior component density and the posterior mixing density in the following proposition.

Proposition 3

(Gaussian posterior mixture)
.

Assume a linear-Gaussian likelihood
\pi(y|x)
as in (
10
) and a Gaussian prior mixture
\pi(x)
as in Definition
2
. Under Assumption
1
, the posterior
\pi(x|y)
is a Gaussian mixture
\pi(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\pi(w|y)\mathrm{d}w
with Gaussian component density

\pi(x|w,y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|x-\mu(w,y)\|_{\Sigma(w)^{-1}}^{2}\right),

(11)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma(w)^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}A+\Sigma_{0}(w)^{-1}

(12)

\displaystyle\mu(w,y)

\displaystyle=\Sigma(w)\left(A^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}y+\Sigma_{0}(w)^{-1}\mu_{0}(w)\right),

(13)

and with the mixing density

\pi(w|y)\propto\pi(y|w)\pi(w),

(14)

where

\pi(y|w)\propto\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma(w)}{\det\Sigma_{0}(w)}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\mu(w,y)\|_{\Sigma(w)^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{0}(w)\|_{\Sigma_{0}(w)^{-1}}^{2}\right).

(15)

Proof.

See
A.3
.
∎

We notice that
\pi(y|w)
in (
15
) does not depend on the mixing density
\pi(w)
.
This means that we can easily adapt the posterior mixture to any choice of Gaussian prior mixture by replacing
\pi(w)
in (
14
), e.g., using the Gaussian mixtures in Examples
2
and
3
.

2.3
General sampling algorithm

We can draw samples
\{x^{(i)}\}_{i\geq 1}
from the posterior mixture (
4
) as shown in Algorithm
1
.
This is because Algorithm
1
yields samples
\{(w^{(i)},x^{(i)})\}_{i\geq 1}
from the joint probability density
\pi(w,x|y)=\pi(x|w,y)\pi(w|y)
, whose marginal with respect to
x
is precisely
\pi(x|y)
.

Algorithm 1

General algorithm to sample the posterior mixture density (
4
)

1

for

i=1

to

N

do

2

Sample
w^{(i)}
from the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
.

3

Sample
x^{(i)}
from the Gaussian posterior component density
\pi(x|w^{(i)},y)
.

4

end for

5

Return samples
\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Sampling from the posterior component density
\pi(x|w,y)
in the second step is straightforward since it is Gaussian, see Proposition
3
. Moreover, once samples
w^{(i)}\sim\pi(w|y)
are available, the second sampling step can be parallelized. In fact, efficient sampling from the Gaussian posterior mixture hinges on the ability of sampling from the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
in (
14
) in the first step. In the following, we discuss the two sampling steps in detail.

2.3.1
Dimension reduction in the posterior mixing density.

Sampling the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
can be computationally expensive, since its evaluation requires the computation of determinants and solutions to linear systems of equations.
This is further exacerbated when the dimensions of the parameter
x
and the mixing component
w
are high.
For these reasons, we propose to replace
\pi(w|y)
by an approximation
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
obtained via the certified dimension reduction (CDR) method introduced in
[
40
]
, see also
[
11
,
28
]
.
Note that other approximations can be used, for example, a Laplace approximation, see, e.g.,
[
30
]
, or the prior mixing density
\pi(w)
.

Applying the CDR method to the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
in (
14
) consists in replacing
\pi(y|w)
in (
15
) by a
ridge
approximation
w\mapsto\tilde{\pi}(y|U_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}w)
.
The matrix
U_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
is pre-determined and has
r\ll d
orthogonal columns, where
d
is the dimension of the mixing variable
w
(in our experiments, both
w
and
x
are of dimension
d
).
This way, the effective dimension
r
of the approximation is much smaller than the original, which reduces the computational cost significantly.
Consequently, the approximated posterior mixing density takes the form of

\tilde{\pi}(w|y)\propto\tilde{\pi}_{r}(y|U_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}w)\pi(w),

where
U_{r}
is constructed by minimizing an upper-bound of the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence of
\pi(w|y)
from
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
.
This procedure has been generalized to the broader class of
\alpha
-divergences in
[
28
]
.

However, the theoretical foundations of the CDR method requires the mixing density
\pi(w)
to satisfy a logarithmic Sobolev inequality, which is trivially satisfied by the Gaussian
\pi(w)\propto\exp(-\|w\|^{2}/2)
, but not by, e.g., the exponential density
\pi(w)\propto\exp(-\lambda w)
associated with the Laplace prior, see Example
2
. One way to circumvent this issue is to consider a transformation
T
, which pushes forward
\pi(w)
to a standard normal random variable
u
and to apply the CDR method on the transformed component
u=T(w)
, see
[
12
,
36
]
.

We note that the Hellinger distance squared is bounded by the KL divergence, and thus, Proposition
2
can be used to bound the approximated posterior mixture
\tilde{\pi}(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}(w|y)\mathrm{d}w
, where
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
is obtained through the CDR method.
In Section
3.2.1
, we apply a specialized version of the CDR method, the certified coordinate selection (CCS)
[
16
]
, to the case where the prior is Laplace and thus can be written as a Gaussian scale mixture.

We conclude this section with a practical result from
[
16
]
.
Regardless of the approximation method, we can estimate the Hellinger distance based on samples
w^{(i)}\sim\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
as follows:

H\!\left({\pi(w|y)},{\tilde{\pi}(w|y)}\right)^{2}\leq 2\int\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{u}(w|y)}{\tilde{\pi}_{u}(w|y)}}-1\right)^{2}\tilde{\pi}(w|y)\mathrm{d}w\approx\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{u}(w^{(i)}|y)}{\tilde{\pi}_{u}(w^{(i)}|y)}}-1\right)^{2}.

Here we use a subscript “
u
” to denote unnormalized densities, such that
\pi_{u}(w|y)\propto\pi(w|y)
, and
\tilde{\pi}_{u}(w|y)\propto\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
.
This way, the above bound does not require the computation of normalizing constants.
We refer to Appendix A.3 of
[
16
]
for a proof of the above inequality.

2.3.2
Sampling the posterior component density.

Sampling the posterior component density
\pi(x|w^{(i)},y)
in (
11
) consists in drawing samples from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu(w^{(i)},y),\Sigma(w^{(i)}))
.
The standard way for exact sampling is via the Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix.
However, if
\Sigma(w^{(i)})
in (
12
) is high-dimensional, it is numerically expensive to perform a single Cholesky decomposition for each sample
w^{(i)}
.
Conversely, the Cholesky decomposition of the prior component covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}(w)
is oftentimes relatively easy to compute, e.g., when it has diagonal structure.
In this case, the linear randomize-then-optimize (RTO) method proposed in
[
3
,
4
]
can be used to obtain samples by computing the Cholesky decomposition of
\Sigma_{0}(w^{(i)})
and solving linear systems of equations with the system matrix
\Sigma(w^{(i)})^{-1}
.
Moreover, the linear system can be reformulated as a linear least squares problem, which is better conditioned.
We describe the methodology in detail in
B
.

In fact, sampling from a high-dimensional Gaussian distribution is a well explored field of research. Many methods which are tailored to specific problem structures are available.
We refer to
[
37
]
for an overview and references therein.
In particular, taking into account the structure of
\Sigma(w)^{-1}
, a Gibbs sampler based on exact (GEDA) or approximate data augmentation (Approx. DA) can be used as an alternative to linear RTO.

3
Application to Laplace prior

In this section, we employ Algorithm
1
to sample from a posterior density defined by a linear-Gaussian likelihood and a Laplace prior. We obtain the Gaussian posterior mixture immediately by applying Proposition
3
.
Moreover, by employing the CCS method
[
16
]
, we formulate two easy-to-sample and dimension-reduced approximate posterior mixing densities
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(w|y)
, which can be used to replace the relatively complicated density
\pi(w|y)
in step
1
of Algorithm
1
.

3.1
Problem formulation

We aim at sampling from a posterior distribution defined by a linear-Gaussian likelihood (
10
) and a product-form Laplace prior:

\pi(x)\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}|x_{i}|\right),

(16)

where
\delta_{i}>0
for
i=1,\cdots,d
are the rate parameters. The posterior reads

\pi(x|y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-y\|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}|x_{i}|\right).

(17)

Recall from Example
2
that the mixing density of a univariate Laplace distribution is an exponential density. Thus, the Gaussian scale mixture representation of (
16
) has the Gaussian component density
\pi(x|w)
in (
8
) and the mixing density

\pi(w)\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}w_{i}\right)

(18)

with
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
and rate parameters
\lambda_{i}=\delta_{i}^{2}/2
.
Since (
16
) has product-form, we have
\Sigma_{0}(w)=\Lambda_{w}
, which denotes a diagonal matrix with
w
on the main diagonal.
Note that
\mu_{0}(w)=0
.
By (
14
) we can directly formulate
\pi(w|y)
, where
\pi(w)
is given by (
18
).

3.2
Dimension-reduced posterior mixing density

As discussed in Section
2.3.1
, direct sampling from
\pi(w|y)
in (
14
) is a difficult task.
One option for a dimension-reduced and controlled approximation
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
is to apply the CDR method combined with a transformation of random variables as described in Section
2.3.2
.
However since the prior mixing density
\pi(w)
is exponential and has product-form, it is convenient to apply the CCS method
[
16
]
, a variant of the CDR method.
In the following sections, we first apply the CCS method to find a controlled and easy-to-sample approximation to
\pi(w|y)
.
Then, following the principles of the CCS method, we propose a second dimension-reduced approximation, which is based on a MAP estimate of
\pi(w|y)
.

3.2.1
CCS-approximated posterior mixing density.

The CCS method is a special variant of the CDR method as it is developed for the particular case of product-form Laplace or exponential priors.
Here, we employ it to obtain a dimension-reduced approximation
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{CCS}}_{r}(w|y)
for
\pi(w|y)
.
Compared to the CDR method, which we briefly introduced in Section
2.3.1
, in the CCS method the projection matrix
U_{r}
is restricted to be a coordinate selection operator, so that
U_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}w=w_{\mathcal{I}}
, where
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,...,d\}
is the index set of the
r=|\mathcal{I}|

selected coordinates
.
Consequently, we denote the set of
not selected coordinates
by
w_{\mathcal{J}}
with
\mathcal{J}=\{1,\dots,d\}\backslash\mathcal{I}
.
We define such a coordinate splitting by a permutation matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
:

Pw=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{\mathcal{I}}\\
w_{\mathcal{J}}\end{array}\right].

(19)

CCS approximates a posterior density with Laplace or exponential prior by replacing its likelihood with a dimension-reduced ridge approximation on the
r(\ll d)
selected coordinates.
This allows the approximation of the posterior mixing density
\pi(w|y)
in (
14
) by

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w|y)\propto\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\pi(w_{\mathcal{J}}),

(20)

where
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
is an approximate marginal posterior mixing density given by

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\propto\tilde{\pi}_{r}(y|w_{\mathcal{I}})\pi(w_{\mathcal{I}})

(21)

with

\tilde{\pi}_{r}(y|w_{\mathcal{I}})=\left(\int\sqrt{\pi(y|w_{\mathcal{I}},w_{\mathcal{J}})}\pi(w_{\mathcal{J}})\mathrm{d}w_{\mathcal{J}}\right)^{2}.

(22)

We denote the posterior approximation which results from replacing
\pi(w|y)
by
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w|y)
by

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w|y)\mathrm{d}w.

(23)

According to
[
16
]
, the choice of
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(y|w_{\mathcal{I}})
in (
22
) is optimal with respect to the Hellinger distance.
In particular, we can bound the approximation error as

H\!\left({\pi(w|y)},{\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w|y)}\right)^{2}\leq\epsilon(r)=2\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}h_{i},

(24)

where
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
diagnostic vector
defined by

h_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\int\left(\frac{\partial\log\pi(y|w)}{\partial w_{i}}\right)^{2}\pi(w|y)\mathrm{d}w.

(25)

Here
\lambda_{i}
is the rate parameter of the exponential prior mixing density (
18
).
Recall from (
7
) that the control in (
24
) ensures

H\!\left({\pi(x|y)},{\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(x|y)}\right)^{2}\leq\epsilon(r).

It is clear that the diagnostic
h
in (
25
) cannot be computed exactly, since it requires the posterior mixing density to be known.
Instead, we consider the Monte Carlo estimate

\tilde{h}_{i}=\frac{1}{N\lambda_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\partial\log\pi(y|w^{(j)})}{\partial w_{i}}\right)^{2},

(26)

where
\{w^{(j)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are iid samples from either
\pi(w)
or some other approximation
\tilde{\pi}(w|y)
, e.g.,
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
, see the next section.

Once
h
is estimated, the coordinate splitting can be performed according to (
24
), which suggests that
\mathcal{I}
should contain the indices associated with the largest components in
h
.
Hence, for a desired precision
\tau
on the Hellinger distance, we can attempt to find
\mathcal{I}
satisfying
\epsilon(r)\leq\tau
.
However, the resulting number of selected coordinates
r(\tau)
can be abnormally large, especially if the bound (
24
) is loose.
In this case, we set
r=\min(r(\tau),r_{\max})
with a pre-given
r_{\max}
and let
\mathcal{I}
contain the indices of the
r
largest components in
h
.

To allow for the application of standard Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, we suggest the following two reformulations in
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
.
The resulting expressions of the log target density and its gradient, and an efficient implementation are presented in
C.3
.

(1) Transformation of random variables.

The domain of
w
, which is the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
, prohibits the application of most standard MCMC methods.
To circumvent this problem, we apply the element-wise transformation of random variables given by
f:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

f(w)=\log(w).

(27)

Then, standard MCMC algorithms can be run on the variable
f(w)
, which now lives in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(2) Approximation of optimal
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(y|w_{\mathcal{I}})
.

A practical way to approximate
\tilde{\pi}_{r}(y|w_{\mathcal{I}})
in (
22
) is to fix the coordinates
w_{\mathcal{J}}
at their prior mean as suggested in
[
40
,
16
]
, i.e.,

w_{\mathcal{J}}=\left[\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}},\cdots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathcal{J}_{d-r}}}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}.

In fact, this approximation allows for further simplifications in
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w|y)
, which reduce the computations of determinants and inverses of
d\times d
to
r\times r
matrices, see
C.3
.

Note that the main computational effort is reduced to sampling from the low-dimensional approximate marginal posterior mixing density

in line

This is because we can sample from
\pi(w_{\mathcal{J}})
in line

independently and in a closed form.

3.2.2
MAP-approximated posterior mixing density.

The following approximation is inspired by the classical Laplace approximation for posterior densities.
Such an approximation corresponds to a Gaussian whose mean is given by the MAP estimate and whose covariance is given by the inverse of the Hessian of the negative log posterior density evaluated at the MAP estimate
[
30
]
.
In the following approximation to
\pi(w|y)
, we use this concept to construct an approximate marginal posterior density
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
as an alternative to
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
in (
20
).
We denote the resulting approximate posterior mixing density by

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)\propto\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\pi(w_{\mathcal{J}}).

(28)

We start the construction of
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
by computing a MAP estimate

w^{\mathrm{MAP}}\in\arg\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\,-\log\pi(w|y).

(29)

Extending the domain of
w
from
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
allows us to perform a coordinate splitting (
19
) analogous to the previous section based on the following index set:

\mathcal{I}=\{i\in\{1,\dots,d\}|w^{\mathrm{MAP}}_{i}>0\}.

(30)

We note that it is not clear if
\pi(w|y)
is uni-modal, nor if
w^{\mathrm{MAP}}
is unique.
In our numerical experiments, we always initialize the minimization solver for (
29
) at
w_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\dots,d
, and the obtained stationary point facilitates good approximations.

After finding
w^{\mathrm{MAP}}
, we define the
r
-dimensional marginal approximate posterior mixing density
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
in (
28
) as

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{\mathcal{I}}-w_{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{MAP}}\|^{2}_{{\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{MAP}}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}\left(w_{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{>0}\right),

(31)

where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{MAP}}^{-1}=[-\nabla^{2}\log\pi(w^{\mathrm{MAP}}|y)]_{[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]}
and
\mathbbm{(}1)
is the indicator function.
Here, the subscript
[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]
refers to the selection of rows and columns with indices
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\Sigma_{\mathrm{MAP}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, and the formula for
\nabla^{2}\log\pi(w|y)
can be found in
C.1
.

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
is essentially a truncated Gaussian density on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}
centered at
w^{\mathrm{MAP}}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Sampling from the low-dimensional yet truncated Gaussian density is not straightforward, but special methods, e.g.,
[
5
]
, are available.
We denote the posterior approximation which results from replacing
\pi(w|y)
by
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
by

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(x|y)=\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)\mathrm{d}w.

(32)

Recall that the MAP of
\pi(w)
is
w_{i}=0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,d
.
Therefore, the coordinate splitting (
30
) can be justified by arguing that the coordinates that are most affected by the update from the mixing prior
\pi(w)
to the mixing posterior
\pi(w|y)
are those whose MAP estimate changes from
w_{i}=0
to
w_{i}>0
.
Moreover, we observe in our numerical experiments that if the mixing prior differs significantly from the mixing posterior only on a few coordinates, we have
r\ll d
.
This has two favorable consequences.
First, we can efficiently compute the minimization program (
29
), see
C.2
for details.
Second, sampling from
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
becomes computationally cheap, since the main sampling effort is reduced to sampling from the truncated
r
-dimensional Gaussian density
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
.

in Algorithm

3.3
Comparison to the Bayesian LASSO

The Bayesian LASSO
[
31
]
is designed to handle the LASSO estimate of linear regression parameters in the Bayesian framework. The LASSO posterior density equates to our posterior in (
17
) if one (i) lets
A
be a matrix of standardized regressors, (ii) assumes the variance of the noise to be constant across parameters, i.e.,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}=\sigma^{2}I
,
\sigma^{2}>0
, and (iii) fixes the rate parameter globally, i.e.,
\delta_{i}=\delta>0
for all
i
.

Our sampling method is different from the Bayesian LASSO, since the latter employs a hierarchical framework by modeling the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
as hyper-parameter and conditioning the Laplace prior of
x
on
\sigma^{2}
. This modeling choice is made to ensure uni-modality of the posterior density. Moreover, the global rate parameter
\delta
can be fixed in advance via empirical Bayes or also included in the hierarchical framework as hyper-parameter. In our notation, the hierarchical model of the Bayesian LASSO reads

\displaystyle\eqalign{y|x,\sigma^{2}&\sim\mathcal{N}(Ax,\sigma^{2}I)\\
x|\sigma^{2},w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{d}&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\Lambda_{w})\\
\sigma^{2},w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{d}&\sim\pi(\sigma^{2})\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}w_{i}^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}w_{i}}

The parameters in the hierarchical model (
3.3
) can be sampled with a Gibbs sampler in closed form by exploiting conjugacy relationships. Sampling the full conditional
x|\sigma^{2},\delta,w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{d}
is similar to sampling our Gaussian posterior component density but with the slightly different posterior component precision matrix
\Sigma(w)^{-1}=\sigma^{-1}[A^{\mathsf{T}}A+\Lambda_{w}^{-1}]
, which is due to the conditioning of
x
on
\sigma^{2}
.

Another difference worth mentioning from a sampling perspective is the way in which the vector of variances
w
is sampled. While the Bayesian LASSO can sample all full conditionals
w_{i}|x_{i},\sigma^{2},\delta^{2}
independently, we must sample all
w_{i}
from
\pi(w|y)
at once. Hence, it is challenging to apply our general sampling Algorithm
1
for the exact posterior in high-dimensional problems, but it also motivates the use of the dimension-reduced approximations
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w|y)
in (
20
) or
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
in (
28
).
A clear advantage of our method is that as soon as
w
is sampled, sampling
x
from the posterior component density
\pi(x|w,y)
can be performed in parallel.

4
Numerical experiments

In the following numerical experiments, we test our methods from Section
3
on posterior densities with Laplace prior.
Besides showing the feasibility of our approximations (
23
) and (
32
), we aim to highlight the benefits of approximating
\pi(w|y)
instead of directly approximating
\pi(x|y)
.

Specifically, in the first experiment we compare two CCS-approximations of
\pi(x|y)
: one obtained via CCS on
\pi(w|y)
, see Section
3.2.1
, and the other one obtained by applying CCS directly on
\pi(x|y)
.
To this end, we write CCS(W) or CCS(X), depending on whether we apply the CCS method to
\pi(w|y)
or
\pi(x|y)
.
We differentiate the resulting posterior approximations by using the notations

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{CCS(W)}}_{r}(x|y)\propto\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\pi(w_{\mathcal{J}})\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{CCS(X)}}_{r}(x|y)\propto\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(x_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\pi(x_{\mathcal{J}}).

Here
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(x_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
is obtained analogously to
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{CCS}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)
by replacing
w
by
x
in (
21
) and (
22
).
Moreover, the diagnostic for CCS(X) is computed as in (
25
) with
w
replaced by
x
, and
\lambda_{i}
replaced by
\delta_{i}
.

In the second experiment, we compare two MAP-approximations of
\pi(x|y)
: one obtained via a Laplace approximation on the selected coordinates
w_{\mathcal{I}}
of
\pi(w|y)
, see Section
3.2.2
, and the other one obtained via a Laplace approximation directly on
\pi(x|y)
.
To differentiate between the resulting posterior approximations, we denote them by

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(W)}}_{r}(x|y)\propto\int\pi(x|w,y)\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)\pi(w_{\mathcal{J}})\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(X)}}(x|y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\mathrm{MAP}}\|^{2}_{\tilde{H}(x^{\mathrm{MAP}})}\right),

where
x^{\mathrm{MAP}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the MAP estimate of
\pi(x|y)
, and
H(x^{\mathrm{MAP}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is an approximation to
-\nabla^{2}\log\pi(x^{\mathrm{MAP}}|y)
, see Section
3.2.2
.

In some experiments, we apply the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA)
[
33
]
for sampling.
MALA is derived by discretizing a Langevin diffusion equation and steers the sampling process by using gradient information of the log-target density
[
32
]
.
Whenever we use MALA, we compute 5 independent chains and check their convergence by means of the estimated potential scale reduction (EPSR) statistic
[
17
]
.
In brief, the EPSR compares the within-variance of the sample chains with their in-between-variance, and chains with an EPSR of less than
1.1
are considered to have converged.
We use the Python package arviz
[
25
]
to compute the ESPR, effective sample sizes (ESS) and credibility intervals (CI) (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
for definitions).

4.2
2D super-resolution microscopy

In this experiment, we illustrate the feasibility of our approximated posterior mixing density
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
in a high-dimensional problem.
The test problem is inspired by the application of stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) in

A similar example was considered in the Bayesian context in

STORM is a super-resolution microscopy technique based on single-molecule stochastic switching, where the goal is to detect molecule positions in live cell imaging.
The images are obtained by a microscope detecting the photon count of the (fluorescence) photoactivated molecules.

Problem description.

We consider a microscopic image

Here, we set

In this example, we set the oversampling ratio

d=mk^{2}=16,384

Based on the kernel from the optical measurement instrument given in
[
41
]

A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
.
The data
y
is obtained via

y=Ax_{\mathrm{true}}+e,

(39)

where

is simulated from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2})

Similar as in
[

x_{\mathrm{true}}
for the high photon count case with
50
uniformly distributed molecules on a field of the size
4\mu\mathrm{m}\times 4\mu\mathrm{m}
.
The intensity of each molecule is simulated from a log-normal distribution with the mode

\pi(x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2}}\|y-Ax\|_{2}^{2}-\delta\|x\|_{1}\right).

4.2.2
MAP-based approximation of the posterior mixing density.

In the following, we compare our posterior approximation
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(W)}}_{r}(x|y)
in (
32
) with a Laplace approximation of
\pi(x|y)
, which we denote by
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(X)}}(x|y)
.
Recall that we write MAP(W) and MAP(X) to differentiate between the two posterior approximation methods.

We first compute approximate posterior samples via MAP(X).
We start by constructing the Laplace approximation
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(X)}}(x|y)
by computing the MAP estimate of
\pi(x|y)
.
This is achieved by solving
x^{\mathrm{MAP}}=\mathrm{arg\,min}_{x}-\log\pi(x|y)
, which is a convex optimization problem, and we use the convex optimization toolbox cvxpy
[
13
]
for the computation.
The MAP estimate
x^{\mathrm{MAP}}
is shown in Figure
7
, and we observe that it recovers the true molecule positions quite well.

) for CCS(W) and CCS(X), estimated via the approximate diagnostics

We cannot directly proceed with the construction of
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(X)}}(x|y)
, since the Laplace approximation requires the log posterior density to be two times differentiable at the MAP estimate.
This is not the case for the present posterior density, and we approximate the Laplace prior as

\pi(x)\propto\exp(-\delta\|x\|_{1})\approx\exp(-\delta\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{x_{i}^{2}+\gamma})
for some
0<\gamma\ll 1
.
We follow the heuristic rule in
[
16
]
and fix
\gamma=\delta^{2}/4
.
The requirement of this approximation is a disadvantage compared to MAP(W), where we employ the Laplace approximation on the selected coordinates
w_{\mathcal{I}}
of the smooth density
\pi(w|y)
.
With the modified prior density, the approximated Hessian of the negative log posterior density at
x^{\mathrm{MAP}}
reads

\tilde{H}(x^{\mathrm{MAP}})=1/\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2}A^{\mathsf{T}}A+\Lambda_{z}
,
where
\Lambda_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a diagonal matrix with the vector

z=\delta\gamma/\sqrt{(x^{\mathrm{MAP}})^{2}+\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
on its diagonal.
Thus,
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(X)}}(x|y)=\mathcal{N}(x^{\mathrm{MAP}},\tilde{H}(x^{\mathrm{MAP}})^{-1})
, and we draw
10,000
samples
x^{(i)}\sim\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(X)}}(x|y)
using linear RTO.

We now compute approximate posterior samples via MAP(W).
Recall that sampling from
\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{MAP(W)}}_{r}(x|y)
consists of sampling
w^{(i)}\sim\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
and then sampling
x^{(i)}\sim\pi(x|w^{(i)},y)
.
To obtain
\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)
, we first compute
w^{\mathrm{MAP}}
via the formulations in
C.2
.
As in the experiment before, we use the L-BFGS-B algorithm
[
6
]
with the initial guess
w_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\dots,d
.
We obtain
\|w^{\ast}\|_{0}=138
, which is very sparse considering the total dimension of

.
Using Algorithm
3
, we then compute
10,000
samples of
w^{(i)}\sim\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w|y)

[
5

to sample from the
138

\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{\mathrm{MAP}}(w_{\mathcal{I}}|y)

Subsequently, we draw
10,000
samples
x^{(i)}\sim\pi(x|w^{(i)},y)
via linear RTO.

With the samples

5
Conclusions

We derived the closed form expressions of the posterior component density and posterior mixing density in the case of a linear-Gaussian likelihood and a Gaussian mixture prior.
Our proposed sampling strategy for such a posterior includes two steps. First, sampling the mixing variables from the posterior mixing density, and second, sampling the component densities conditioned on the sampled mixing variables.
Since direct sampling from the posterior mixing density is often not possible, alternative methods like MCMC methods must be used.
However, exact sampling via such methods is relatively expensive due to the computational complexity of the mixing density. Therefore, we propose to replace the posterior mixing density by a dimension-reduced approximation and provide a bound in the Hellinger distance for the resulting posterior approximation.

Furthermore, we considered the Laplace prior, which can be written as a continuous Gaussian mixture, and described two methods using the CCS method to approximate the posterior mixing density.
In our numerical results, we showed that approximating the posterior mixing density via these methods, instead of directly approximating the posterior density, yields samples closer to the exact posterior.
Moreover, due to the two-step sampling scheme, the correlation among posterior samples can be significantly reduced.

As a future research direction, we aim to investigate whether the numerical improvements observed on problems with the Laplace prior can also be achieved with other continuous Gaussian mixture priors, such as the Student’s-t or horseshoe prior.
