Learning Lipschitz Functions by GD-trained Shallow Overparameterized ReLU Neural Networks
Ilja Kuzborskij, Csaba Szepesvári

Abstract
We explore the ability of overparameterized shallow ReLU neural networks to learn Lipschitz, non-differentiable, bounded functions with additive noise when trained by GD ( GD ). To avoid the problem that in the presence of noise, neural networks trained to nearly zero training error are inconsistent in this class, we focus on the early-stopped GD which allows us to show consistency and optimal rates. In particular, we explore this problem from the viewpoint of the NTK ( NTK ) approximation of a GD -trained finite-width neural network. We show that whenever some early stopping rule is guaranteed to give an optimal rate (of excess risk) on the Hilbert space of the kernel induced by the ReLU activation function, the same rule can be used to achieve minimax optimal rate for learning on the class of considered Lipschitz functions by neural networks. We discuss several data-free and data-dependent practically appealing stopping rules that yield optimal rates.

Prior work.

Understanding learning ability of algorithms for training neural networks often concerns behavior of the
statistical risk
(a.k.a. the risk) defined as

L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}]
,
which is closely related to the excess risk studied here,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\|f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-f^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}]=L(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\sigma^{2}~.

Study of the risk in neural network learning is a long-lived topic of interest
(
Devroye et al. 1996
;
Anthony and Bartlett 1999
)
.
The classical approach to this problem considers
uniform bounds
on the risk, which hold simultaneously over all members of a given class of neural networks for all data distributions.
In such an algorithm-free analysis, bounds are controlled by some notion of the capacity of the class, such as VC-dimension, Rademacher complexity, or metric entropy
(
Anthony and Bartlett 1999
;
Bartlett and Mendelson 2002
;
Bartlett et al. 2017
;
Golowich et al. 2018
)
.
Clearly, these bounds due to their generality can be used to argue about the risk of the predictor generated by a concrete algorithm, such as
GD
we consider here.
To this end, this gives us high-probability bounds on the risk which capture the trade-off between how well the algorithm fits the sample and complexity of the solution (which is measured by some norm of the parameters, such as
\ell^{2}
):

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})\leq\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})+c\,\frac{\text{network-complexity}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})}{\sqrt{n}}~.

Thus, if one could minimize the empirical risk while keeping network complexity under control, one could keep the risk close to the noise rate
\sigma^{2}
.
Unfortunately, minimization of such an objective is theoretically challenging because of its non-convex nature, and so it is non-obvious whether
GD
can minimize it till the required precision in general.
On the other hand, nowadays it is a common knowledge that
overparameterized
neural networks (when the width
m
by far exceeds the sample size
n
) are able to achieve arbitrarily small training error on any training sample.
From a statistical perspective, driving
\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})\to 0
should lead to fitting the noise, or
overfitting
, however
Zhang et al. 2021
and others concluded that training neural networks by
GD
-type algorithms to near-zero empirical risk results only in a mild overfitting.
As such, these algorithms are often able to perform well on the unseen sample despite of the absence of explicit complexity control.

To this end, from the optimization viewpoint, some tangible progress was recently made by
Du et al. 2019
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019b
;
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi 2020
;
Zou et al. 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2021
;
Song et al. 2021
and others, who proposed an explanation why

GD
is able to minimize the empirical risk in the overparameterized regime.
In particular, these works show an exponential convergence rate with high probability:
2
2

2

Throughout this paper, we use
f\lesssim g
to say that there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that
f\leq c\,\mathrm{polylog}(g)\,g
holds uniformly over all arguments.

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})\lesssim\left(1-c\eta\,\frac{d}{n}\right)^{T}~.

Roughly speaking, these proofs are based on the observation that under sufficient overparameterization (
m=\mathrm{poly}(n,d)
), the predictor
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}}
behaves similarly to the linear predictor defined on a large feature space constructed from the gradient of the network around its (randomized) initialization, a so-called
NTF
(
NTF
) space.
At the same time, considering the setting when
m\to\infty
, one can show
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
that under appropriate conditions such a feature space gains approximation power of a
RKHS
(
RKHS
)
(
Cucker and Zhou 2007
)
, giving rise to the
NTK
theory.
To some extent, this connection should not come as a surprise since
NTF
is a particular case of the
RF
(
RF
) framework well-studied over decades
(
Rahimi and Recht 2007
;
Rudi and Rosasco 2017
)
.

The
NTK
theory established a bridge between neural network learning and the
RKHS
literature, and as such led to many interesting results (see review by
Bartlett et al. 2021
).
Going back to the question of the risk behavior,

NTK
theory might also be used as a tool to understand complexity of a resulting predictor by looking at it through the lens of learning with kernels.
A number of works put forward possible explanations for the complexity control in
GD
-trained neural networks suggesting that the
GD
algorithm is responsible for an
implicit
complexity control (or implicit bias), and analyzed it through the
RKHS
viewpoint
(
Arora et al. 2019
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019a
;
Ji and Telgarsky 2019
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
.
For example,
Arora et al. 2019
showed that in the
noise-free
setting (
\sigma=0
) the risk is controlled by the norm of a minimal-norm
\ell^{2}
kernel interpolant (predictor perfectly fitting the sample),

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})=\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\frac{\|\text{minimal-norm-interpolant}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty~.

Moreover,
Arora et al. 2019
demonstrated several examples when the norm above can be bounded when the regression function
f^{\star}
is a shallow polynomial neural network of infinite width.
Results in a similar spirit were also obtained for a hard-margin classification setting
(
Ji and Telgarsky 2019
)
.

At the same time, for
\sigma>0
, it is known that
interpolating
neural networks (achieving zero empirical risk) are in general
inconsistent

(
Köhler and Krzyżak 2019
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
.
To this end, Corollary 1 of
Köhler and Krzyżak 2019
implies that as long as
\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})=o(1/n)
, for any large enough
n

there exists a distribution
P
with, say,
\sigma^{2}=1/4
, such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})-\sigma^{2}\geq c
for some universal constant
c>0
, where the distribution can even be chosen to be ‘sufficiently regular’, though the marginal
P_{X}
with respect to the inputs will be an atomic distribution.

1.1
Our contribution

In this work we focus on the case
\sigma>0
and ‘complex’ regression functions
f^{\star}
, that is, non-differentiable, Lipschitz, and bounded.
The setting of learning such functions is called
nonparametric
, where in general the number of parameters (here, width
m
), has to increase with the number of observations for us to be able to estimate
f^{\star}
well.
Recall that the minimax-optimal rate of the excess risk in this setting is
\Theta_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}(n^{-\frac{2}{2+d}})

(
Györfi et al. 2006
)
.
In particular, we show that early-stopped
GD
without explicit penalization (
Algorithm

1
) used for training overparameterized shallow ReLU neural networks is
consistent
in the considered setting
and
matches
this optimal rate.

Our main result is a ‘reduction’ theorem, which allows us to convert any excess risk bound for the early-stopped
GD
learning in the
RKHS

\mathcal{H}
(induced by the
NTK
of ReLU), to the excess risk bound for
GD
using the same stopping rule and learning Lipschitz functions by neural networks.
More formally, suppose that given any function
h\in\mathcal{H}

with
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq R
,
3
3

3

is
\ell^{2}

2.1

we can guarantee that

(1+R)\,\epsilon(n,\widehat{T})
.
Then, in
Theorem

1

\displaystyle\|f_{\widehat{T}}-f^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\Lambda^{2}(\epsilon(n,\widehat{T}))^{\frac{2}{d}}+\epsilon(n,\widehat{T})+\frac{\mathrm{poly}_{4}(n/d)}{\sqrt{m}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\qquad n\to\infty~.

(1)

Here, term
\Lambda^{2}(\epsilon(n,\widehat{T}))^{\frac{2}{d}}
is a price we pay for approximation of a Lipschitz function

is a cost of learning such a function in

and term

For now suppose that we chose an early-stopping rule for

\epsilon(n,\widehat{T})\lesssim n^{-\frac{d}{2+d}}

Then, it is clear that for a sufficiently (polynomially) overparameterized neural network and for

\displaystyle\|f_{\widehat{T}}-f^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\Lambda^{2}\,n^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\qquad n\to\infty~.

(2)

Polynomial overparameterization considered here is a standard assumption in the literature on training of shallow networks by

(
Du et al. 2019
;
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi 2020
)
.
The rate of
Eq.

2
is optimal in the sample size and input dimension, however it is suboptimal in the Lipschitz constant
\Lambda

\Lambda^{\frac{2}{2+d}}
in place of
\Lambda^{2}
.
This worse dependence comes at the expense of an agnostic algorithm, which does not require any knowledge of
\Lambda

A similar bound also holds for the

In contrast to

\Theta(n^{-\frac{d}{d+2}})

(
Steinwart et al. 2009
;
Bach 2017
)
— a much faster rate, however
such balls only contain
highly smooth functions
:

Bietti and Mairal 2019
identified that these are
at least

\left\lceil d/2\right\rceil
times differentiable even functions on a sphere with bounded derivatives. Thus, the rate shown here suggest that
GD
-trained neural networks can potentially learn much more complex functions than those within fixed

On the other hand,

Bach 2017
observed that such
RKHS

and that the approximation error decreases as we consider approximators of increasing norm.
In this work we exploit this fact together with observation that an early-stopped
GD

Non-empirical stopping rules.

Now we turn our attention to a concrete example of a stopping rule which yields optimal rate of
Eq.

2
.
In particular, we consider the rule of
Dieuleveut and Bach 2016
, which
requires some knowledge about the regularity of the
RKHS
.
One such standard regularity condition is the eigenvalue decay rate of the kernel function (given by the Mercer’s theorem, see
Section

2.1
), which roughly speaking captures how smooth functions belonging to the
RKHS
can be.
In our case we consider a polynomial decay rate of order
k^{-\beta}
for some
\beta>0
:
RKHS
of the
NTK
derived from the
ReLU
activation is known to have decay rate of order
k^{-\frac{d}{2}}

(
Bach 2017
)
.
(
Dieuleveut and Bach 2016
, Corollary 3)
showed that by using
GD
the step size
\eta=\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-\frac{1}{1+\beta}}
and
\widehat{T}=n
,
GD
enjoys excess risk of order

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\epsilon(n,\widehat{T})]\lesssim n^{-\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}~.

Combining this result (for
\beta=d/2
) with
Eq.

1
(by using Markov’s inequality) we indeed recover optimal rate of
Eq.

2
with the loss of a high probability guarantee.

It is interesting to ask which decay rate of eigenvalues is required to get optimal rates
(
Pillaud-Vivien et al. 2018
)
.
For instance, we can consider a classical analysis of early-stopped
GD
of
Yao et al. 2007
which states that for the number of steps chosen as
\widehat{T}=n^{\frac{1}{3}}
, with high probability we have excess risk of order
n^{-\frac{1}{3}}
for learning in
RKHS
.
This results in a suboptimal excess risk

\|f_{\widehat{T}}-f^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}\left(\Lambda^{2}\,n^{-\frac{2}{3d}}\right)~.

Empirical stopping rule.

The stopping rule of
Dieuleveut and Bach 2016
already gives us optimal rate for a known non-empirical tuning of the algorithm.
To be clear, this is possible because we know the decay rate of kernel spectrum in the case of learning with shallow
ReLU
neural networks.
On the other hand, consider going beyond the setting of our paper where we might want to extrapolate to the situation where a more complex neural network is used (for instance, a multilayer one), while we might still believe that
NTK
approximation is a reasonable model to capture learning.
In such case the decay rate of eigenvalues is unknown and we might want to have an adaptive stopping rule which still learns at the best possible rate.

Here we discuss one of the many data-dependent stopping rules designed for this purpose (see
(
Celisse and Wahl 2021
)
for a comprehensive overview).
Specifically, we consider the rule of
RWY
(
RWY
)
(
Raskutti et al. 2014
)
, which empirically controls the complexity of a predictor class within the
RKHS
.
5
5

5

Technically, the rule controls an upper bound on the empirical localized Rademacher complexity of the ball within the
RKHS

(
Bartlett et al. 2002
;
Wainwright 2019
)
.

Computationally, this boils down to having access to the eigenvalues of the
NTK
matrix (or say its empirical approximation, the
NTF
Gram matrix)
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}
.
In particular, the rule suggests stopping
GD
after
\widehat{T}
steps where

\displaystyle\widehat{T}=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}\;\middle|\;\hat{\mathcal{R}}(1/\sqrt{\eta t})>(2e\sigma\eta t)^{-1}\right\}-1~,\quad\hat{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x^{2}\vee(\lambda_{i}/n))\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}~.

(3)

Intuitively, in the above
\hat{\mathcal{R}}(x)
captures the complexity of the ball within the
RKHS
: The smaller is the argument
x
, the simpler is the class of considered functions.
6
6

6

Since functions on the
RKHS
are defined in the span of the kernel eigenbasis (see
Section

2.1
), simpler functions lie in the low-dimensional subspace spanned by the largest eigenvalues. Note that parameter
x
in
\hat{\mathcal{R}}(x)
determines the cut-off for eigenvalues.

Thus, the number of steps is selected to restrict the solution of
GD
to the simplest possible functions given the data, where the simplicity is chosen with respect to the noise rate
\sigma^{2}
.
Note that
\widehat{T}\to\infty
as
\sigma\to 0
, which suggests that in the noise-free regime
GD
interpolates on the training sample.
This rule requires the knowledge of
\sigma^{2}
, which is not unreasonable and can be estimated on a hold-out set.
As a sanity check, we verify that this rule is still able to recover the optimal rate in our Lipschitz setting.

Raskutti et al. 2014
(see also
Section

5.2
) showed that
GD
using this rule is guaranteed with high probability to achieve optimal excess risk with

\epsilon(n,\widehat{T})\lesssim n^{-\frac{d}{d+2}}
, and
so we recover the optimal rate for Lipschitz functions of
Eq.

2
as well.

1.2
Related work

It is well known that the penalized
ERM
(
ERM
) procedure defined with respect to shallow neural networks,
that is
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mathrm{pen}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}
, matches nonparametric rate
\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}(n^{-\frac{2}{2+d}})
for the appropriate choice of penalization
(
Devroye et al. 1996
;
Györfi et al. 2006
)
.
Here, a standard penalty function is a norm of parameters (usually
\ell^{2},\ell^{1}
or
\ell^{\infty}
) with a carefully tuned magnitude factor depending on
(f^{\star},\sigma^{2},S)
.
In this work we focus on
GD
rather than
ERM
, and show that
GD
used to minimize empirical risk can achieve an optimal rate without knowledge of unknown parameters.
Several other recent works have also focused on excess risk of
GD
for training shallow neural networks.

Hu et al. 2021
showed that for the regression functions within
RKHS
induced by
NTK
,
GD
with a carefully tuned
\ell^{2}
penalization
can achieve rate of excess risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}}\left(n^{-\frac{d}{2d-1}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\qquad n\to\infty~.

Note that the class of regression functions (
\ell^{2}
-ball on
RKHS
) considered by
Hu et al. 2021
is
much smaller
than the class of
f^{\star}
we analyze here, hence the rate has a much better exponential dependence on
d
.
It is also known that the minimax optimal rate for learning in such classes
is
\Theta(n^{-\frac{d}{d+2}})

(
Steinwart et al. 2009
;
Bach 2017
)
.

Several recent papers went beyond
RKHS
setting and considered less regular regression functions.
To this end,
Bietti et al. 2022
analyzed a so-called
single-index model
: Here, the regression function is given as
f^{\star}_{\mathrm{si}}(\mathbf{x})=g(\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle)
, where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a twice-differentiable Lipschitz ‘link’ function and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
are unknown parameters.
Instead of
GD
they focused on the early-stopped
Gradient Flow
algorithm minimizing
\ell^{2}
-penalized empirical risk, and showed that the rate of the excess risk is at best
n^{-\frac{1}{4}}
.
In the single-index model, thanks to the additional structure imposed on
f^{\star}
, the optimal rate of excess risk is much faster than the rate for the general case we consider in this paper.
In such case, it is known that the constrained
ERM
procedure attains a minimax optimal rate of order
n^{-\frac{2p}{2p+1}}
when link function is
p
-times differentiable
(
Györfi et al. 2006
, Chap. 22)
, which suggests that the rate of
Bietti et al. 2022
is potentially improvable till
n^{-\frac{4}{5}}
.

In similar line of work,
Damian et al. 2022
considered a generalization of the above known as the
multi-index model
, where the regression function is

f^{\star}_{\mathrm{mi}}(\mathbf{x})=g(\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{\star},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle+\cdots+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{\star},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle)
and
g
is a polynomial of degree
p
.
Similarly to
Bietti et al. 2022
they analyzed minimization of
\ell^{2}
-penalized empirical risk, but this time, by the early-stopped
GD
.
They showed excess risk of order
\sqrt{\frac{dr^{p}}{n}}
(for a network of width
m\gtrsim r^{p}
),
which is near-optimal for the considered polynomial link functions.
Surprisingly, the rate appears to be much faster than one would expect in the nonparametric setting, however it is worth noting that the polynomial regularity assumption might be essential here.
At the same time,
it is known that for
p
-times differentiable link functions in multi-index models, the minimax optimal rate is of order
n^{-\frac{2p}{2p+r}}

(
Köhler and Krzyżak 2016
)
, i.e., exhibiting exponential dependence on
r
.

All these works employ
\ell^{2}
penalization (sometimes in conjunction with early stopping), however in a practical setting neural networks are rarely trained with it and yet while trained without penalization they still demonstrate ability to perform well on the unseen sample even in the presence of noise
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
.
At the same time practitioners often do not run
GD
methods until
nearly
-zero empirical risk, but rather track the performance on a held-out validation sample and stop the training
early
when an approximate minimum on the validation sample has been reached.

1.3
Additional discussion, limitations, and future directions

Learnability of
f^{\star}
using smooth activation functions.

Regression functions we consider here are non-differentiable, Lipschitz, and bounded.
Numerous works in nonparametric statistics showed that the penalized (or constrained)
ERM
procedure defined with respect to shallow and deep neural networks with
smooth
sigmoidal activation functions, such as sigmoid
x\mapsto e^{x}/(1+e^{x})
, is able to achieve consistency with minimax optimal rates
(
Györfi et al. 2006
;
Köhler and Krzyżak 2005
;
Köhler and Krzyżak 2016
)
.
Note that these results are approximation-theoretic, and it is interesting to ask whether optimal rates can also be achieved by an efficient algorithm such as
GD
while employing such smooth activation functions.
Approaching this question from the viewpoint of
NTK
, we need to understand the regularity of the resulting
RKHS
to be able to say how well can be approximate
f^{\star}
.
To this end,
Wu and Long 2022
showed that the
NTK
corresponding to any fixed smooth activation function with bounded derivatives has an
exponentially
decaying spectrum.
Now, consider the approximation error of
s
-Sobolev classes by such
RKHS
:
Namely, it was shown
(
Cucker and Zhou 2007
, Corollary 6.9)
that for the
RKHS
with exponential spectral decay of order
\exp(-k^{\alpha})
, we have
logarithmically
decaying approximation error ,

\inf_{\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq R}\|h-f^{\star}\|_{2}\lesssim(\ln R)^{-c(\alpha)\,s}

with
R\geq 1
and constant
c(\alpha)
depending only on
\alpha
.
In other words
GD
would have to find a function with exponential
\ell^{2}
-norm in order to reach a reasonable approximation error, while this would essentially prevent us from controlling the estimation error (see also
Wainwright 2019
for a related discussion).
This suggests that
GD
is either unable to consistently learn Lipschitz functions with smooth sigmoidal activations or the
NTK
theory is too weak to capture such a learning scenario.

Relationship to analysis of Sobolev spaces.

The space of Lipschitz non-differentiable and bounded functions considered here can be regarded as an extreme case of Sobolev spaces, namely it is equivalent to
W^{1,\infty}
, see for instance
(
Adams and Fournier 2003
;
Zadorozhnyi et al. 2021
)
.
Such Sobolev spaces cannot be embedded into
RKHS
and one needs to control the approximation error, which leads to the excess risk of order
n^{-\frac{2}{2+d}+\varepsilon}
for an arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
.
Here to show
Eq.

1
, we avoid the Sobolev space argument and instead we control the approximation error using theorem of
Bach 2017
(see
Lemma

7
).

Improved rates for
f^{\star}
with more structure.

Despite being minimax optimal, the rate
n^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}
is extremely slow for high-dimensional problems: the manifestation of the
curse of dimensionality
is unavoidable unless we impose additional structure on the regression function.
In this work we focus on the general Lipschitz non-differentiable regression functions.
The nonparametric literature often considers some notion of additional regularity imposed on
f^{\star}
, such as
p
-times differentiability (or a closely related Hölder condition), which allows to establish minimax optimal rates
n^{-\frac{2p}{2p+d}}
, notably a much faster rate than derived here when
p
is large.
To this end,
Köhler and Krzyżak 2005
shows that such rates are indeed possible when neural networks are fitted by the
ERM
procedure with carefully chosen constraints on the parameters.
It is an interesting possibility to show this kind of adaptivity to the higher-order smoothness of the regression function for a practical algorithm such as
GD
and its stochastic variants.

Another possible way to beat the curse of dimensionality is to assume that the regression function is only sensitive to the
r
-dimensional subspace of the input space as done in the multi-index model.
As discussed before,
(
Damian et al. 2022
)
established an interesting, essentially parametric rate, for polynomial link functions.
At the same time, the literature
(
Bauer and Köhler 2019
;
Köhler and Langer 2021
)
considering minimizers of the empirical risk constrained to some class of neural networks, shows that the rate
n^{-\frac{2}{2+r}}
is achievable for Lipschitz link functions.

Establishing
Eq.

2
for smaller networks.

The main result of
Eq.

2
holds for networks of a polynomial width, that is
m\geq\mathrm{poly}_{6}\left((1+1/d)n\right)
and in addition the width has to scale at least as
\mathrm{poly}_{8}(n/d)
in order to control the gap we pay for predictions by the neural network instead of learning on
RKHS
directly.
The requirement on the overparameterization we have here matches the one of
(
Du et al. 2019
)
.
Several other optimization-themed results have established convergence of
\hat{L}(\cdot)
for shallow neural networks under smaller overparameterization requirement, for instance
\mathrm{poly}_{2}(\cdot)

(
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi 2020
)
(for smooth activations),
\mathrm{poly}_{3/2}(\cdot)

(
Song et al. 2021
)
(for smooth activations), and linear order
(
Nguyen 2021
;
Razborov 2022
)
.
All of these works are concerned with convergence to global minima of the empirical risk, while in our work we require to control the aforementioned prediction gap.
Moreover these works sometimes require sample-size-dependent tuning of the step size, initialization scale, and normalization of targets, while here these are constant.
At the same time, some other works have looked at the prediction gap in the context of generalization
(
Arora et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
, however they where essentially built on the proof of
(
Du et al. 2019
)
and arrived at orders similar to ours.
While the goal of the current work is tangential to the optimization-themed results discussed above, it is definitely an interesting open problem whether optimal nonparametric rates can be achieved by
GD
with much smaller width requirement.
To this end, some approximation-theoretic results have demonstrated that the constrained
ERM
procedure fitting
deep
neural networks can indeed achieve small approximation error with relatively small number of parameters (sometimes linear in the sample size)
(
Bauer and Köhler 2019
;
Köhler and Langer 2021
;
Shen et al. 2022
)
.
However, it is unclear whether the same can be done for
GD
-trained deep neural networks.

Throughout the paper

Concatenation of vectors is denoted by parentheses, that is
(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m})=[\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}

2.1
Basics of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

We recall some basics of
RKHS
(
RKHS
).
A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\subset L^{2}(P_{X})
is a family of functions
f:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
for which
\|f\|_{2}<\infty
and for which we have an associated inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
under which
\mathcal{H}
is complete.

A function
\kappa:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called a Mercer kernel if it is continuous, symmetric, and
PSD
in the sense that
\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\geq 0
for any
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Without loss of generality we will assume that
\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\leq 1
.
Given a Mercer kernel, one can construct an associated
RKHS

such that for each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the function
\kappa(\mathbf{x},\cdot)
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
and a
reproducing
relation holds, that is for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
f(\mathbf{x})=\left\langle f,\kappa(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Mercer’s theorem
(
Cucker and Zhou 2007
)
claims that under suitable conditions on
\kappa
, we have a spectral decomposition

\displaystyle\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\tilde{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}})\qquad(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\tilde{x}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

where
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\ldots\geq 0
are eigenvalues of a kernel and
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\ldots
are eigenfunctions which form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(P_{X})
.

6.1
Some facts about shallow ReLU neural networks

Proposition 3
.

For any any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{dm}
and any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we have

(i)

f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}
,

(ii)

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we have
\|\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\leq 1
,

(iii)

\|\nabla\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}\leq 4\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=(u_{1}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\right\}\mathbf{x},\ldots,u_{m}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbf{w}_{m}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\right\}\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{dm}~.

Fact
(i)
comes by observing that

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x})_{+}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\right\}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}_{k}=\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}~.

Fact
(ii)
comes simply by computing the squared norm:

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\right\}^{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\leq 1~.

Now, consider the gradient of the loss

\nabla(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2}=2(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-y)\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
,
and so

\displaystyle\|\nabla(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2}\|^{2}\leq 4(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2}\|\nabla f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\leq 4(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2}~.

The above combined with Jensen’s inequality gives us

\displaystyle\|\nabla\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-y_{i}|\leq 2\sqrt{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}

and squaring this shows fact
(iii)
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

For all
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\tilde{w}},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\right\}-\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbf{\tilde{w}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\right\}\neq 0

\Longrightarrow

|\mathbf{\tilde{w}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|\leq|(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{\tilde{w}})^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|
.

Proposition 5

(Activation patterns)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{u}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=(\mathbf{w}_{0,1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{0,m})
are chosen as in
Algorithm

1
.
Consider the set of indices of neurons that changed their activation patterns on input
\mathbf{x}

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
is replaced by some parameters
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}=(\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{m})
:

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\mathbf{x})=\left\{k\in[m]\;\middle|\;\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\}-\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{w}_{0,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\}\neq 0\right\}\qquad(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dm},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1})~.

Then, for
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}
whose components satisfy
\max_{k}\|\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{0,k}\|\leq\rho
for some fixed
\rho\geq 0
, and

, the following facts hold:

(i)

For all
k\in P(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\mathbf{x})

|\mathbf{w}_{0,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|\leq\|\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{0,k}\|

(ii)

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|P(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\mathbf{x})|\leq m\rho

(iii)

With probability at least

Proof.

Fact
(i)
is immediate from
Proposition

4
and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

Proof of
(ii)
.
Now,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|P(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{\tilde{w}}_{k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\}-\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{w}_{0,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}>0\}\neq 0\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathbb{I}\left\{|\mathbf{w}_{0,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|\leq\rho\right\}

\displaystyle=m\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\mathbf{w}_{0,1}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}|\leq\rho\right)

Proof of

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\hat{K}}-\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{\hat{K}}-\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}|(\mathbf{\hat{K}})_{i,j}-(\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t})_{i,j}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{0,k,i}\mathbb{I}_{0,k,j}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\mathbb{I}_{t,k,j}\right|\left|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|(\mathbb{I}_{0,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i})\mathbb{I}_{0,k,j}+\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}(\mathbb{I}_{0,k,j}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,j})\right|\left|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{0,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right|+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{0,k,j}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{2}\,\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}|P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i})|}{m}

(with
P(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in
Proposition

5
)

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{2}\left(\rho+\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{m}}\right)

(By fact
(iii)
of
Proposition

5
w.p. at least
1-2e^{-\nu}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)~.

(By
Lemma

2
)

Finally, combining the above with Weyl’s inequality, namely

|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t})-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}})|\leq\|\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t}-\mathbf{\hat{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 7

(Concentration of empirical Gram matrix)
.

Assume that the initial parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
are sampled as in
Algorithm

1
and let the failure probability over
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
be
\delta=2ne^{-\nu}
for any
\nu>0
.
Assume that the width obeys
m\geq\frac{64n^{2}\nu}{\lambda_{0}^{2}}
.
Then,
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}})\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0})\geq 1-\delta
.

Proof.

By Weyl’s inequality

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K})-\|\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{\hat{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}~.

On the other hand the fact
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{F}
, Hoeffding’s inequality, and the union bound over entries give (see
(
Du et al. 2019
, Lemma 3.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\left(\|\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{\hat{K}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 4n\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{m}}\right)\geq 1-2ne^{-\nu}\qquad(\nu>0)~.

(4)

Then requiring

\lambda_{0}-4n\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{m}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}

completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

Proposition

6
and
Proposition

7
imply that given a sufficient overparameterization, with high probability, the smallest eigenvalue of any empirical Gram matrix
\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t}
for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
is strictly positive assuming that
\lambda_{0}>0
.

The proof of the following theorem essentially follows ideas of
(
Du et al. 2019
)
.

Theorem 4

(Convergence rate of
GD
)
.

Assume that the initial parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
are sampled as in
Algorithm

1
and
let the failure probability over
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
be
\delta=2(1+n)e^{-\nu}
for any
\nu>0
.
Assume that the step size obeys
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Assume that the network width satisfies

\displaystyle m\geq 64\left(2\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)^{2}+\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)(2+n)\right)^{2}\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}~.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T})\leq B_{y}^{2}\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{2n}\right)^{T}~.

Proof.

The proof works by induction.
Assumption of
Lemma

2
is the induction hypothesis.
The base case is immediate (see
Remark

1
).
Thus, we need to establish a
t+1
case

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1})\leq B_{y}^{2}\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{2n}\right)^{t+1}~.

By the fact
(i)
of
Proposition

5
the vector of predictions of
f_{t}
on the training sample can be written as
\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
.
Consequently the
GD
update can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=-\frac{2\eta}{n}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\mathbf{y})~.

Now, consider a decomposition of the empirical risk

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{t+1}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{t+1}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\|(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{t+1})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|^{2}}_{(i)}+\frac{2}{n}\|(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{t+1})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{y}\|+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}_{(ii)}~.

Here, term
(i)
can be regarded as the ‘feature change’ vector, which is essentially controlled by the number of pattern changes in a feature vector.
Using
Proposition

5
we will show that
(i)
is small whenever width
m
is large.

Throughout the proof it will be convenient to abbreviate

\widetilde{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}~.

Controlling
(ii)
.

To analyze
(ii)
we essentially use the usual
GD
dynamics update

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\mathbf{y}-\frac{2\eta}{n}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{2\eta}{n}\,\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t}\right)(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\mathbf{y})~.

Observe that
\mathbf{I}-\frac{2\eta}{n}\,\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t}
is a
PSD
matrix if we ensure that

\eta\leq\frac{1}{2n}\,\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Thus taking
\|\cdot\|^{2}/n
, using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, and the fact that
x^{2}\leq x
for
x\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{2\eta}{n}\,\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t})\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{2\eta}{n}\,\left(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}})-\frac{2n^{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\widetilde{n}\right)\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

(By
Proposition

6
)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{n}+\frac{4\eta n\widetilde{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

(6)

where
Eq.

6
holds with probability at least
1-2ne^{-\nu}
thanks to
Proposition

7
.

Controlling
(i)
.

Now it remains to show that the ‘feature change’ term is small.
Abbreviate
\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}=\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{w}_{t,k}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{i}>0\}
.
Namely,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{t+1})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}_{t+1,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}_{t+1,k}\right)^{2}

and now consider an inner summand for any
i\in[n]
:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}_{t+1,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}_{t+1,k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{t+1,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right|\left|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}_{t+1,k}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{t+1,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right|\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1,k}-\mathbf{w}_{t,k}\|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}2\eta\sqrt{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{t+1,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\eta\sqrt{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}}{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{t+1,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{0,k,i}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|\mathbb{I}_{0,k,i}-\mathbb{I}_{t,k,i}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\eta\sqrt{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}}{m}\left(|P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1},\mathbf{x}_{i})|+|P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i})|\right)~.

where
P(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in
Proposition

5
.
In the above chain of inequalities,
(a)
comes by
Proposition

4
and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

(b)
is a basic consequence of
GD
update, namely:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1,k}-\mathbf{w}_{t,k}\|=\frac{2\eta}{n}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-y_{i})\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{2\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-y_{i}|\leq 2\eta\sqrt{\frac{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}{m}}~,

while
(c)
is due to triangle inequality.
Now using fact
(iii)
of
Proposition

5
and

Lemma

2
,

\displaystyle|P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1},\mathbf{x}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq m\rho+\sqrt{\nu m}

\displaystyle\leq 4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n\sqrt{m}}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu m}

(7)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{n}\sqrt{m}

where
Eq.

7
holds by
Proposition

5
with probability at least
1-2e^{-\nu}
.
Similarly we get a bound on
|P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i})|
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|(\mathbf{\Phi}_{t}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{t+1})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|^{2}\leq\frac{16\eta^{2}\widetilde{n}^{2}}{m}\,\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})~.

(8)

Putting all together.

Finally, putting obtained bounds into
Eq.

5
we get

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16\eta^{2}\widetilde{n}^{2}}{m}\,\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+2\,\frac{4\eta\widetilde{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(1-\frac{\eta}{n}\,\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}}_{t})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{n}+\frac{4\eta n\widetilde{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{n}+\frac{16(\eta\widetilde{n})^{2}}{m}+\frac{8\eta\widetilde{n}}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{4\eta n\widetilde{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{2n}\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

by ensuring that the width is chosen to satisfy:

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{n}+\frac{16(\eta\widetilde{n})^{2}}{m}+\frac{4\eta\widetilde{n}(2+n)}{\sqrt{m}}\leq-\frac{\eta\lambda_{0}}{2n}

\displaystyle\Longleftarrow\quad-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{n}+\frac{8\eta\widetilde{n}^{2}}{m}+\frac{4\widetilde{n}(2+n)}{\sqrt{m}}\leq-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2n}

(Using
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2}
)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{8\widetilde{n}^{2}}{m}+\frac{4\widetilde{n}(2+n)}{\sqrt{m}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2n}

\displaystyle\Longleftarrow\quad\left(16\widetilde{n}^{2}+8\widetilde{n}(2+n)\right)\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}\leq\sqrt{m}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\left((4\widetilde{n})^{2}+8\widetilde{n}(2+n)\right)^{2}\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}\leq m~.

Thus, the induction step is completed. We complete the proof by applying a union bound for high probability bounds of
Eq.

6
and
Eq.

7
.
∎

The main result of this section is the following ‘coupling’ theorem, shown in

6.3.2
, which gives a bound on the
\ell^{\infty}
-gap between the prediction of a GD-trained shallow neural network and that of the
GD
-trained
KLS

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}\geq 1
.
Under conditions of
Theorem

4
, for any

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(f_{t}(\mathbf{x})-f^{\mathrm{\kappa}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\leq\frac{64}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)^{2}\left(256\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+9\right)^{2}+\frac{\nu}{m}\,B_{y}^{2}\,\left(24\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4}~.

6.3.1
Lemmata for the proof of
Theorem

5

In this section we establish a number of coupling results which will eventually lead to
Theorem

5
.
In the following proofs we will occasionally use a recursive relationship:

\displaystyle x_{s+1}=a_{s}x_{s}+b_{s}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{0}=0

(For
((a_{s},b_{s},x_{s}))_{s=0}^{t}
with
(a_{s},b_{s},x_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\qquad x_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}b_{s}\prod_{k=s+1}^{t}a_{k}~.

(9)

We will also use a vector notation for predictions of
f_{s},f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s},f^{\mathrm{\kappa}}_{s}
on inputs
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
.
For any
s\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{s}

\displaystyle=(f_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,f_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{n}))~,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}

\displaystyle=(f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{n}))~,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{\kappa}}_{s}

\displaystyle=(f^{\mathrm{\kappa}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,f^{\mathrm{\kappa}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{n}))~.

Lemma 3

(Neural network –
RF
predictor coupling on the sample)
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{N},\nu\geq 1
, under conditions of
Theorem

4
,

\displaystyle\|f_{t}-f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{t}\|_{n}^{2}\leq\frac{(32B_{y})^{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\left(\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)\,\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2n}\,\lambda_{0}\right)^{t}~.

Proof.

For now consider the following decomposition on an arbitrary input
\mathbf{x}
for step
s\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle f_{s+1}(\mathbf{x})-f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s+1}(\mathbf{x})

(10)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s+1}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}-\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{s+1}

(Note that
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=0
)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}+(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s+1}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}-\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{s+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}-\eta\nabla\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})\right)+(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s+1}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}-\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\left(\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{s}-\eta\nabla\hat{L}^{\mathrm{rf}}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=f_{s}(\mathbf{x})-f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}(\mathbf{x})+\eta\Big(\underbrace{\nabla\hat{L}^{\mathrm{rf}}(\boldsymbol{\bar{\theta}}_{s})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x})}_{\Delta_{s}(\mathbf{x})}\Big)+\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s+1}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}}_{\beta_{s}(\mathbf{x})}~.

(11)

Now we express
\Delta_{s}(\mathbf{x})
in terms of
f_{s}(\mathbf{x})-f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}(\mathbf{x})
.
Introduce residual terms
r_{s,j}=(f_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-y_{j})
and
\bar{r}_{s,j}=(f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-y_{j})
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\Delta_{s}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bar{r}_{s,j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-r_{s,j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left((\bar{r}_{s,j}-r_{s,j})\,\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-r_{s,j}\,(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j}))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\bar{r}_{s,j}-r_{s,j})\,\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j}))+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-f^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right)\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))~.

Now, it is convenient to we write
Eq.

11
in a vector form over inputs
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
, for which we introduce some vector abbreviations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,\beta_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{n}))~,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,\gamma_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{n}))~,\qquad\gamma_{s}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{2\eta}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right)~,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,\gamma_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{n}))~,\qquad\gamma^{\prime}_{s}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{2\eta}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))~.

Namely,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{s+1}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s+1}=(\mathbf{I}-\tfrac{2\eta}{n}\mathbf{\hat{K}})(\boldsymbol{f}_{s}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{rf}}_{s})+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}~.

Unrolling the recursion for

by applying

9

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{t}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{rf}}_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\mathbf{I}-\tfrac{2\eta}{n}\mathbf{\hat{K}})^{t-s}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}\right)~.

In particular, taking
\ell^{2}

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{f}_{t}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{rf}}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\|(\mathbf{I}-\tfrac{2\eta}{n}\,\mathbf{\hat{K}})^{t-s}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}\|

(12)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1-\tfrac{2\eta}{n}\,\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{\hat{K}}))^{t-s}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1-\tfrac{\eta}{n}\,\lambda_{0})^{t-s}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}\|\right)~.

(By
Proposition

7
)

Now we turn our attention to
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}\|
,
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}\|
, and
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}\|
.
The bound on the Euclidean norm of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}
comes by

, namely

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|(\mathbf{\Phi}_{s+1}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{s})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)\sqrt{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})}

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)\,B_{y}(1-\tfrac{\eta}{2n}\,\lambda_{0})^{\frac{s}{2}}~.

(By

)

Now we give an upper bound on

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\eta)^{2}}\,\gamma_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{\intercal}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\|^{2}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{s,j}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s})

(By
Corollary

4
w.p. at least

)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)B_{y}^{2}(1-\tfrac{\eta}{2n}\,\lambda_{0})^{s}~.

(By
Theorem

4
)

Note that
\gamma^{\prime}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}
can be handled in the same way as above.
Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}\|\leq 2\eta\,\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{m}}\,\left(4B_{y}^{2}\,\frac{n}{\lambda_{0}}+\sqrt{\nu}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{y}(1-\tfrac{\eta}{2n}\,\lambda_{0})^{\frac{s}{2}}~,

and the same bound holds on
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s}^{\prime}\|
.
Now, turning back to
Eq.

12
and assuming that
\nu\geq 1
