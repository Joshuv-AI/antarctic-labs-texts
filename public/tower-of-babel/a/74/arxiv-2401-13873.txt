Abstract
. In this paper we investigate the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for kinetic stochastic differential equations with Hölder diffusion and unbounded singular drifts in Kato’s class. Moreover, we also establish sharp two-sided estimates for the density of the solution. In particular, the drift b b can be in the mixed L t q ​ L x 1 p 1 ​ L x 2 p 2 L^{q}_{t}L^{p_{1}}_{x_{1}}L^{p_{2}}_{x_{2}} space with 2 q + d p 1 + 3 ​ d p 2 < 1 \frac{2}{q}+\frac{d}{p_{1}}+\frac{3d}{p_{2}}<1 . As an application, we show the existence and uniqueness of weak solution to a second order singular interacting particle system in ℝ d ​ N {\mathbb{R}}^{dN} .



1.
Introduction

In this paper we aim to prove the Aronson-like bounds for the transition probability density of the kinetic stochastic differential equations
(abbreviated as kinetic SDEs) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
with Hölder diffusion and unbounded singular drifts,
which are in certain Kato’s class:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{1}_{t}=b{\big(}t,X^{1}_{t},X^{2}_{t}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\sigma{\big(}t,X^{1}_{t},X^{2}_{t}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\\
&{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{2}_{t}=X^{1}_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion on some stochastic basis
\mathfrak{F}:=(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},{\mathbf{P}})
,
and
(b,\sigma):{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
are Borel measurable functions.
For simplicity of notations, we shall denote
{\boldsymbol{X}}:=(X^{1},X^{2})
and for
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,

\displaystyle B(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=(b(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}),x_{1})^{*},\ \ \Sigma(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=(\sqrt{2}\sigma(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}),0_{d\times d})^{*},

(1.2)

where
*
stands for the transpose of a row vector.
By this, we can rewrite SDE
(
1.1
)
as

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}=B(t,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\Sigma(t,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},

(1.3)

and its generator is given by

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{t}:=\mathrm{tr}((\sigma\sigma^{*})(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\nabla^{2}_{x_{1}})+x_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{2}}+b(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}},

(1.4)

where
\mathrm{tr}
denotes the trace of a matrix, and
\nabla_{x_{i}}
denotes the gradient with respect to variable
x_{i}
.

The SDE (
1.1
), also called stochastic Hamiltonian/kinetic system,
represents essential frameworks for studying the dynamic behavior of physical, chemical, and biological systems under the influence of both deterministic laws and stochastic forces.
These systems integrate the principles of Hamiltonian mechanics with the inherent randomness captured by stochastic processes, providing a comprehensive understanding of complex phenomena across various disciplines. We refer the reader to
[
25
,
12
,
16
]
for a general background introduction, to
[
11
,
15
,
28
]
for the ergodicity of the system, and to
[
22
,
27
]
for Euler approximations of the invariant measures.

In recent years, there has been a great interest in the study of the well posedness for SDE (
1.1
) with irregular drifts and non-degenerate diffusion coefficients.
For the strong well posedness of degenerate SDE (
1.1
), Chaudru de Raynal in
[
3
]
obtained the first result under the assumptions that
b
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous in
x_{1}
and
\beta
-Hölder continuous in
x_{2}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},1)
. This result was then extended by Wang and Zhang
[
29
]
under similar Hölder-Dini conditions.
When
b
is in some anisotropic Sobolev space, Zhang in
[
35
]
established the strong well-posedness of (
1.1
) by solving the associated Kolmogorov equation and
Zvonkin’s transformation (see also
[
10
]
for similar result with constant diffusion coefficients but stronger drift condition than
[
35
]
).
For the weak well-posedness of SDE (
1.1
), to the best of our knowledge, the weakest result is obtained by Chaudru de Raynal and Menozzi in
[
6
, Theorem 1]
under the assumptions that

\sigma
is bounded measurable and uniformly elliptic and Hölder continuous in
x
uniformly in
t
, and

b\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}))\ \mbox{ with }\tfrac{2}{q}+\tfrac{4d}{p}<1.

In fact, they considered more general models of SDEs that can be seen as perturbed ODEs for which a noise acting on the first component will transmit to the whole chain
of ODEs through a weak Hörmander like condition. It is noted that they also provide counterexamples in
[
6
, Theorem 2]
to illustrate the almost sharpness of condition
\tfrac{2}{q}+\tfrac{4d}{p}<1
.
We mention that kinetic SDEs driven by
\alpha
-stable processes were studied in
[
14
]
.

Now we consider a second order singular interacting particle system in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\ddot{X}^{N,i}_{t}=\sum_{j\not=i}^{N}\frac{\gamma_{j}(X^{N,i}-X^{N,j})}{|X^{N,i}-X^{N,j}|^{\alpha}}-\gamma\dot{X}^{N,i}_{t}+\sqrt{2\nu}\dot{W}^{N,i}_{t},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

(1.5)

where
\gamma_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\alpha\in[1,d+1)
,
\gamma\geqslant 0
represents the damping force, and
\nu>0
is the intensity of the noise,
(W^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
is a sequence of i.i.d. Brownian motions.
Note that if we let
\dot{X}^{N,i}_{t}=:V^{N,i}_{t}
, then the above second order system can be written as the following first order system:

\dot{X}^{N,i}_{t}=V^{N,i}_{t},\ \ \dot{V}^{N,i}_{t}=\sum_{j\not=i}^{N}\frac{\gamma_{j}(X^{N,i}-X^{N,j})}{|X^{N,i}-X^{N,j}|^{\alpha}}-\gamma V^{N,i}_{t}+\sqrt{2\nu}\dot{W}^{N,i}_{t}.

In particular,
Z^{N}_{t}:=(V^{N,i}_{t},X^{N,i}_{t})_{i=1,\cdots,N}
satisfies a degenerate SDE in phase space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2dN}
.
It is easy to see that
x\mapsto x/|x|^{\alpha}\in L^{p}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for any
p<d/(\alpha-1)
. Thus, to apply the result in
[
6
]
to SDE (
1.5
),
one needs to assume
\alpha\in[1,1+1/(4N))
due to the restriction
\tfrac{2}{q}+\tfrac{4Nd}{p}<1
. A natural question is that for a fixed
\alpha>1
, is it possible to establish the well-posedness of SDE (
1.5
)
for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
? We shall give an affirmative answer that it is true for any
\alpha\in(1,\frac{4}{3})
by considering the drift
b
being in some Kato’s class. In the non-degenerate case, this problem has been tackled in
[
21
,
13
]
. Therein, the strong well-posedness are considered.

Another aim of this paper is to show the existence and two-sided estimates of the density or heat kernel of SDE (
1.1
) with singular drifts.
Let us first consider a special case
b=0
and
\sigma={\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}
:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{1}=\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ \ {\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{2}=X_{t}^{1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\ \ t>0.

(1.6)

In
[
18
]
, Kolmogorov first wrote down the density of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
starting from
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
:

\displaystyle p(0,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\right)^{d}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\left|K_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-\theta_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\right|^{2}\right),

(1.7)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})=(x_{1},x_{2}+x_{1}t),\ \ K_{t}=\left(\begin{matrix}2t{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d},&t^{2}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}\\
t^{2}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d},&\frac{2t^{3}}{3}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}\end{matrix}\right).

(1.8)

From the expression, it is easy to derive the following sharp two-sided estimates:

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\Big)^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{4+\sqrt{13}}{4}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{y}}-\theta_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))|^{2}}\leqslant p(0,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\leqslant\Big(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\Big)^{d}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{4-\sqrt{13}}{4}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{y}}-\theta_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}))|^{2}},

(1.9)

where for
t>0
,
{\mathbb{T}}_{t}
is the scale matrix given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}_{t}=\left(\begin{matrix}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}&0_{d\times d}\\
0_{d\times d}&t^{-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}\end{matrix}\right).

(1.10)

Indeed, noting that

K^{-1}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}2t^{-1}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}&-3t^{-2}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}\\
-3t^{-2}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}&6t^{-3}{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d},\end{pmatrix}

the two-sided estimates (
1.9
) follow by

-(2+\sqrt{13})t|x_{1}|^{2}+(2-\sqrt{13})t^{-1}|x_{2}|^{2}\leqslant 6\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\leqslant-(2-\sqrt{13})t|x_{1}|^{2}+(2+\sqrt{13})t^{-1}|x_{2}|^{2}.

Since the seminal work of
[
18
]
, there are many works concentrated on the heat kernel estimates of SDE (
1.1
). In
[
19
]
, the global upper and lower diagonal bounds for the heat kernel were obtained when the drift is uniformly Lipschitz continuous and bounded. The result was extended to more general models of SDEs that can be seen a full chain of perturbed ODEs. We refer to the works
[
8
,
23
]
for related discussions. The authors in
[
5
]
proved the two-sided heat kernel estimates of SDE (
1.1
) with unbounded and Hölder drifts. However, the drift term of the SDE discussed in
[
19
,
8
,
23
,
5
]
does not include a singular drift.

Before stating our main result, we first introduce the following notion of weak solutions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{F}:=(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},{\mathbf{P}})
be a stochastic basis.
We call a pair of
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
-adapted processes
({\boldsymbol{X}},W)
on
\mathfrak{F}
a weak solution of SDE (
1.1
) starting from point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
if

(i)

{\mathbf{P}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{x}})=1
;

(ii)

W
is a
d
-dimensional
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
-Brownian motion;

(iii)

For Lebesgue almost all
t>0
, the law of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure;

(iv)

For any
t>0
, it holds that
\int^{t}_{0}|b(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s<\infty
a.s. and

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{x}}+\int^{t}_{0}B(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int^{t}_{0}\Sigma(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s},\ \ a.s.

Remark 1.2
.

In the above definition, we require (iii) that is used to show the apriori Krylov estimate.
Without (iii), one can not use the mollifying technique to take limits (see Lemma
4.1
below). It is an open question to show (iii) priorly under (i) (ii) and (iv). It is noted that in the non-degenerate case, we can drop it (see
[
30
]
).

Now we introduce the following main assumptions used below:

(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)

There is a
\kappa_{0}\geqslant 1
such that for all
t\geqslant 0
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\sigma(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\xi|^{2}\leqslant\kappa_{0}|\xi|^{2},

(1.11)

and for some
\gamma_{0}\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle|\sigma(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})-\sigma(t,{\boldsymbol{y}})|\leqslant\kappa_{0}\big(|({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})_{1}|^{\gamma_{0}}+|({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})_{2}|^{\gamma_{0}/3}\big).

(1.12)

Moreover,
b=b_{0}+b_{1}
, where
b_{0}
satisfies that for all
j=1,2,\cdots
,

\displaystyle|b_{0}(t,0)|\leqslant\kappa_{0},\ \ \|\nabla^{j}b_{0}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\kappa_{j},

(1.13)

and
|b_{1}|
belongs to Kato’s class
{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{1}
(see Definition
2.2
below).

Remark 1.3
.

If
|b_{1}|
belongs to Kato’s class
{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{1}
and
b_{0}
only satisfies that for some
\beta\in(0,1]
,

|b_{0}(t,0)|\leqslant\kappa_{0},\ \ |b_{0}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})-b_{0}(t,{\boldsymbol{y}})|\leqslant\kappa_{0}|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{\beta},

then
b
still satisfies the assumption in
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
. In fact, we can let
\tilde{b}_{0}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=b_{0}(t,\cdot)*\phi_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\tilde{b}_{1}:=b_{1}+b_{0}-\tilde{b}_{0}
, where
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n}
is a family of modifiers. Then
\tilde{b}_{0}
satisfies (
1.13
), and
b_{0}-\tilde{b}_{0}
is bounded measurable, and thus
\tilde{b}_{1}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{1}
.

Let
B_{0}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=(b_{0}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}),x_{1})^{*}
and
\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})
be the unique solution of ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})=B_{0}(t,\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})),\ \ t,s\geqslant 0,\ \ {\theta}_{s,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{x}}.

(1.14)

Note that
\{\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}}),t\geqslant s\geqslant 0\}
stands for the forward flow and

\{\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}}),0\leqslant t\leqslant s\}
stands for the backward flow.
For
T\in(0,\infty)
we introduce the following notation:

{\mathbb{D}}_{T}:=\{(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}):0\leqslant s<t\leqslant T,{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\}.

The aim of this paper is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

Under
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
, for each
s>0
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, there is a unique weak solution
({\mathbf{X}}_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}}))_{t\geqslant s}
to SDE (
1.1
) starting from
{\boldsymbol{x}}
at time
s
in the sense of Definition (
1.1
).
Moreover, if
|b_{1}|^{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{K}}_{1}
for some
\gamma>1
, then
{\mathbf{X}}_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})
admits a density
p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})
enjoying the following two-sided estimates: for any
T>0
and all
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
,

\displaystyle C_{0}(t-s)^{-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}}\leqslant p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\leqslant C_{1}(t-s)^{-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}},

(1.15)

where
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}
depend on
T,d,\gamma_{0},\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1}
, and
C_{0},C_{1}
depend on
T,d,\gamma_{0},\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1}
and function
|b_{1}|^{\gamma}
.

Remark 1.5
.

By Remark
2.4
below and the above theorem, one sees that for any
\alpha\in(1,\frac{4}{3})
, SDE (
1.5
) admits a unique weak solution and a density for any starting point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we give the definition of Kato’s class in kinetic case and prove a crucial estimate in Lemma
2.9
related to the function in Kato’s class.
Roughly to say, let
p_{s,t}(x,\cdot)

be a family of time-inhomoegenous transition kernels. For a function
b:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
, we show an estimate in Lemma
2.9
as

\int^{t}_{s}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p_{s,r}(x,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)b(r,z)p_{r,t}(z,A){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\leqslant K_{b}\cdot p_{s,t}(x,A),

where
K_{b}
is some functional of
b
that is used to define the Kato class. Such an estimate plays a crucial role in establishing the two-sided estimates of heat kernel.

In Section 3, we solve the kinetic PDEs with drifts being unbounded and in Kato’s class. The main result is Theorem
3.5
. To figure out the main idea of the proof, we consider the following kinetic SDE:

\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{x_{1}}u+x_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{2}}u+b\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u+f=0,\ \ u(T)=0,

where
b=b_{0}+b_{1}
satisfies the assumptions in
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
.
Since
b_{0}
is an unbounded vector field, one can not directly solve it by considering the following integral equation

u(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})=\int^{T}_{s}P_{r-s}(b\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u+f)(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,

where
P_{t}
is the kinetic semigroup associated with
\Delta_{x_{1}}+x_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{2}}
.
Instead of this, we consider the following perturbation integral equation

\displaystyle u(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})=\int^{T}_{s}P^{(0)}_{r,s}(b_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u+f)(r,{\boldsymbol{x}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,

(1.16)

where
P^{(0)}_{t,s}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}):={\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
and
X_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the unique solution of SDE (
1.1
) with
\sigma={\mathbb{I}}
and
b=b_{0}
,
and starting from
{\boldsymbol{x}}
at time
s
. To solve (
1.16
), we shall use the heat kernel estimates obtained in
[
5
]
.

In Section 4, we establish the weak well-posedness of SDE (
1.1
) under
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
based on the solvability of the kinetic PDE. In Section 5, we prove the two-sided estimates of the density following the same line as in

2.
Preliminaries

The Kato class was first introduced by Kato in
[
17
]
to prove the essential self-adjointness of Schrödinger operator. It plays important roles in the analysis of partial differential equations and mathematical physics. A measurable function
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is said to be of Kato class
{\mathbb{K}}_{d}
if

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{|x-y|\leqslant r}|f(y)|K(x-y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=0,

where

K(x)=|x|^{2-d}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{\{d\geqslant 3\}}+\log|x|^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{\{d=2\}}+|x|\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{\{d=1\}}.

In
[
1
]
, Aizenman and Simon provided an equivalent characterization for
{\mathbb{K}}_{d}
by

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(2\pi s)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2s}}|f(y)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=0.

We refer to
[
7
, Theorem 3.6]
for more information of this result. For general time-dependent case, Q.S.Zhang in
[
32
]
introduce the following definition of Kato’s class:

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{t>0}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}s^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\lambda|y|^{2}}{s}}|f(t\pm s,x\pm y)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=0,\qquad\forall\lambda>0,

(2.1)

and showed two-sided Gaussian estimates for second order operators with drift satisfying (
2.1
) in
[
31
]
.

In this section we introduce the Kato class of space-time functions on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,
which extends the classical definition of Kato’s class in
[
32
]
to kinetic case. Then we present some basic properties of functions in Kato’s class
used in this paper. Moreover, we show a crucial estimate in Lemma
2.9
that shall be used to prove the two-sided estimates of heat kernel.

To introduce the Kato class, we need the following Gaussian kernel function as used in (
1.9
): for
\beta\geqslant 0
and
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=t^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|{\mathbb{T}}_{t}{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}},\ \ (t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},

(2.2)

where
{\mathbb{T}}_{t}
is defined by (
1.10
).
Clearly,

\displaystyle\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(1,\cdot)\in\cap_{p\in[1,\infty]}L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}).

(2.3)

For
\lambda,\delta>0
and a space-time function
f(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we define

\displaystyle K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta):=\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{\delta}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(r,{\boldsymbol{y}})|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\bigg\},

(2.4)

where

|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|:=\sum_{i,j=0,1}|f(t+(-1)^{i}r,{\boldsymbol{x}}+(-1)^{j}{\boldsymbol{y}})|.

It is easy to see that
\delta\mapsto K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta)
is monotone increasing and

\lambda\mapsto K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta)
is monotone decreasing, and for
0\leqslant\beta^{\prime}\leqslant\beta
,

\displaystyle K^{(\beta^{\prime})}_{\lambda}(f;\delta)\leqslant\delta^{\frac{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}{2}}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta),\ \ \forall\delta>0.

(2.5)

We introduce an anisotropic distance in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
by

|{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{\rm d}:=\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|^{\frac{1}{3}}\},\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

The following lemma tells us that for
\beta,\lambda>0
,

K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;1)<\infty\Rightarrow f\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\beta>0
, there is a constant
C=C(d,\beta)>0
such that for all
\lambda,\delta\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}}\int^{\delta}_{0}\!\!\!\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\leqslant C\mathrm{e}^{10\lambda}\delta^{\frac{\beta}{2}+2d}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta).

Proof.

By definition we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(r,{\boldsymbol{y}})|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant\int^{\delta}_{\delta/2}\!\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}}r^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|{\mathbb{T}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad\geqslant\delta^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-10\lambda}\int^{\delta}_{\delta/2}\!\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Hence,

\sup_{(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}}\int^{\delta}_{\delta/2}\!\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{10\lambda}\delta^{\frac{\beta}{2}+2d}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta).

On the other hand, in view that there exist
M_{n}:=d^{2d}2^{2nd}
points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
so that

\big\{{\boldsymbol{y}}:|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}\big\}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{M_{n}}\big\{{\boldsymbol{y}}:|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{2^{-n}\delta}\big\},

by the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\int^{\delta}_{0}\!\!\!\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int^{2^{-n}\delta}_{2^{-(n+1)}\delta}\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{n}}\int^{2^{-n}\delta}_{2^{-(n+1)}\delta}\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{2^{-n}\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{n}\sup_{i}\int^{2^{-n}\delta}_{2^{-(n+1)}\delta}\int_{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant\sqrt{2^{-n}\delta}}|f(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{y}}))|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d^{2d}2^{2nd}\mathrm{e}^{10\lambda}(2^{-n}\delta)^{\frac{\beta}{2}+2d}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;2^{-n}\delta)=\frac{d^{2d}\delta^{\frac{\beta}{2}+2d}\mathrm{e}^{10\lambda}}{1-2^{-\beta/2}}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;2^{-n}\delta).

The proof is complete.
∎

Now we introduce the definition of Kato’s class in the kinetic case used below.

Definition 2.2

(Kato’s class)
.

For any
\beta\geqslant 0
, we define

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}_{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}):K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;1)<\infty,\mbox{ $\forall\lambda>0$}\Big\},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{f\in{\mathbb{K}}_{\beta}:\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta)=0,\mbox{ $\forall\lambda>0$}\bigg\},

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm p}_{\beta}:=\bigg\{f\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{\beta}:\mbox{ $\forall p,\lambda>0$, $\exists N\in{\mathbb{N}}$ s.t. }\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;r))^{N}}{r^{p}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r<\infty\bigg\}.

Remark 2.3
.

If
f(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})=f(t,x_{1})
only depends on the first variable

, then the definition of

coincides with the classical definition (
2.1
).
By (

), one sees that for

,

For a function

, we shall extend it to
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
by putting

for

.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
f(x)=|x_{2}|^{1-\alpha}
. It is easy to see that for

,

. Indeed, by definition and the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}r^{-\frac{1}{2}-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|{\mathbb{T}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}}|x_{2}-y_{2}|^{1-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}r^{-\frac{1+3d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|y_{2}|^{2}/r^{3}}|x_{2}-y_{2}|^{1-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\delta^{2-\frac{3\alpha}{2}}}{4-3\alpha}\left(\int_{|y|\leqslant 1}|y|^{1-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|y|^{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y\right).

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

f_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=(f*\phi_{n})(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}f(t-s,{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})\phi_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}):=n^{1+2d}\phi(ns,n{\boldsymbol{x}}).

(2.6)

By the definition of convolution, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f_{n};\delta)=K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(|f_{n}|;\delta)\leqslant K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(|f|;\delta)=K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;\delta).

(2.7)

Moreover, if
\beta>0

The following lemma is useful.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\beta\geqslant 0
. For any

and

, it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{1}}_{D_{R}}|f_{n}-f|;T)=0,

where

and
D_{R}:=\{(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}:|t|\leqslant R,\ |{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{\rm d}\leqslant R\}

Proof.

For simplicity of notations, we write
g^{R}_{n}:={\mathbf{1}}_{D_{R}}|f_{n}-f|
.
For any
\delta\in(0,T)
, we have

\displaystyle K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(g^{R}_{n};T)

\displaystyle\leqslant K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(g^{R}_{n};\delta)+\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\bigg\{\int^{T}_{\delta}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(r,{\boldsymbol{y}})g^{R}_{n}(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leqslant K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(g^{R}_{n};\delta)+\delta^{-\frac{\beta}{2}-2d}\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\bigg\{\int^{T}_{\delta}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}g^{R}_{n}(t\pm r,{\boldsymbol{x}}\pm{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\bigg\}

The following lemma asserts that the mixed
L^{q}_{t}L^{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
-space is contained in
{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm p}_{\beta}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
q\in[1,\infty]
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{2d})\in[1,\infty]^{2d}
satisfy

\kappa:=2-(\tfrac{1}{p_{1}}+\cdots\tfrac{1}{p_{d}}+\tfrac{3}{p_{d+1}}+\cdots+\tfrac{3}{p_{2d}}+\tfrac{2}{q})>0.

For any
\beta\in[0,\kappa)
,
there is a constant
C=C(d,{\boldsymbol{p}},q,\beta)>0
such that for all

K^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(f;T)\lesssim_{C}T^{(\kappa-\beta)/2}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}})},

where

\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\cdots\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|f(s,x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(2d)})|^{p_{1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x^{(1)}\right)^{\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}}\cdots{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x^{(2d)}\right)^{\frac{q}{p_{2d}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

In particular, for any
\beta\in[0,\kappa)
,

{\mathbb{L}}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\subset{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm p}_{\beta}.

Proof.

Let
\bar{q}:=\frac{q}{q-1}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}=(\bar{p}_{1},\cdots,\bar{p}_{2d})
with
\bar{p}_{i}:=\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}
.
By Hölder’s inequality, we have

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(s,{\boldsymbol{y}})|f(t+s,{\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant\|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\bar{q}}_{T}({\mathbb{L}}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}})}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{q}({\mathbb{L}}^{{\boldsymbol{p}}})},

where

\|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\bar{q}}({\mathbb{L}}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}})}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\cdots\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(s,x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(2d)})|^{\bar{p}_{1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x^{(1)}\right)^{\frac{\bar{p}_{2}}{\bar{p}_{1}}}\cdots{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x^{(2d)}\right)^{\frac{\bar{q}}{\bar{p}_{2d}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{\frac{1}{\bar{q}}}.

By the change of variables, it is easy to see that for each
s\in(0,1)
,

\|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(s)\|_{\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}}=s^{\gamma}\|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(1)\|_{\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}},

where
\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(s)=\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(s,\cdot)
and

\gamma:=\tfrac{1}{2\bar{p}_{1}}+\cdots+\tfrac{1}{2\bar{p}_{d}}+\tfrac{3}{2\bar{p}_{d+1}}+\cdots+\tfrac{3}{2\bar{p}_{2d}}-2d-\tfrac{\beta}{2}.

Hence,

\|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\bar{q}}_{T}({\mathbb{L}}^{\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}})}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}s^{\gamma\bar{q}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{\frac{1}{\bar{q}}}\|\eta^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}(1)\|_{\bar{\boldsymbol{p}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{214}}}{{\lesssim}}T^{\gamma+\frac{1}{\bar{q}}}=T^{\frac{\kappa-\beta}{2}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Let

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
E
be an index set and
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
a bounded open set.
Let
f_{n}(t,x):E\times D\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a sequence of differentiable functions in
x
. Assume that for some
f:E\times D\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(t,x)\in E\times D}|f_{n}(t,x)-f(t,x)|=0,

(3.14)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in E\times D}\sup_{n}|\nabla f_{n}(t,x)|<\infty,\ \ \lim_{|x-y|\to 0}\sup_{t\in E}\sup_{n}|\nabla f_{n}(t,x)-\nabla f_{n}(t,y)|=0.

(3.15)

Then
x\mapsto\nabla f(t,x)
is uniformly continuous in
t\in E
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(t,x)\in E\times D}|\nabla f_{n}(t,x)-\nabla f(t,x)|=0.

(3.16)

Proof.

By (
3.15
) and Ascoli-Arzela’s theorem, there is a subsequence
n_{k}
and an
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
-valued function
H:E\times D\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which is continuous in
x
and uniformly in
t\in E
, so that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{(t,x)\in E\times D}|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(t,x)-H(t,x)|=0,

which together with (
3.14
) yields

H(t,x)=\nabla f(t,x).

Now we prove (
3.16
).
For simplicity of notation, we write
g_{n}:=f_{n}-f
. Fix
i=1,\cdots,m
,
x\in D
and
r<{\rm dis}(x,\partial D)
. Let
e_{i}=(0,\cdots,1,\cdots,0)
be the unit vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
By the mean valued theorem, there is a point
y\in B_{r}(x)
such that

\displaystyle g_{n}(t,x)-g_{n}(t,x+re_{i})=r\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,y).

(3.17)

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, by the assumption, we can choose
r
small enough so that

\sup_{|x-y|\leqslant r}\sup_{t\in E}\sup_{n}|\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,x)-\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,y)|\leqslant\varepsilon,

and by (
3.17
),

|\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,x)|\leqslant|\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,x)-\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,y)|+|\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}(t,y)|\leqslant\varepsilon+2\sup_{z\in B_{r}(x)}|g_{n}(t,z)|/r.

From these, we derive the desired limit by letting
n\to\infty
and (
3.14
).
∎

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})-\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}|_{\bf d}^{\gamma_{1}}\delta^{-{\gamma_{1}}}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f_{n}+b^{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n};1)+K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f_{n}+b^{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n};\delta)

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}|_{\bf d}^{\gamma_{1}}\delta^{-{\gamma_{1}}}\Big(K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|f_{n}|;1)+\|\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}\|_{\infty}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b^{n}_{1}|;1)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|f_{n}|;\delta)+\|\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}\|_{\infty}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b^{n}_{1}|;\delta)

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}|_{\bf d}^{\gamma_{1}}\delta^{-{\gamma_{1}}}+K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|f|;\delta)+K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;\delta),

(3.25)

where the implicit constant does not depend on
n,\delta
and
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}
. Thus, by Lemma
3.3
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{s\in[0,t],{\boldsymbol{x}}\in D}|\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{m_{k}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})-\nabla_{x_{1}}u(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})|=0.

Now, by Lemma
3.4
and taking limits for (
3.24
), one sees that
u
is a solution of (
3.20
). Finally, by the uniqueness, the whole sequence converges.
∎

4.
Well-posedness for SDE with singular drift

In this section, we aim to demonstrate the well-posedness of SDE with drift
b_{1}
belonging to the Kato class and provide a proof for the first part of Theorem
1.4
. Moreover, throughout the following discussion, we still fix
T
as defined in (
3.19
).

We first establish the following crucial Krylov estimate based on (
3.10
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}}
be a weak solution of SDE (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
.
Under
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
, there are constants
C_{3}>0
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
depending only on
\Theta
such that for any
0\leqslant f\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{1}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f;t),\ \ \forall t\in(0,T].

(4.1)

Proof.

Fix
t\in(0,T]
and the starting point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
of SDE (
1.3
).
Firstly we consider
0\leqslant f\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
and let
u(s,\cdot):={\mathscr{I}}^{t}_{f}(s,\cdot)
.
By (
3.8
), Itô’s formula and (
3.7
), we have for any
r\in[0,t]
,

\displaystyle u(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r})

\displaystyle=u(0,{\boldsymbol{x}})+\int_{0}^{r}(\partial_{s}u+{\mathscr{L}}_{s}u)(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{r}(\sigma^{*}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u)(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}

\displaystyle=u(0,{\boldsymbol{x}})+\int_{0}^{r}(b_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u-f)(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{r}(\sigma^{*}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u)(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}.

Let
R>|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
and define a stopping time

\tau_{R}:=\inf\bigg\{r>0:\int^{r}_{0}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\geqslant R\mbox{ or }|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}|\geqslant R\bigg\}.

By BDG’s inequality, it is easy to see that for some
C_{2}=C_{2}(\Theta)>0
,

{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant 2\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{t}}+\|\nabla_{x_{1}}u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{t}}\left[{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+C_{2}\right].

By (
3.10
), we further have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(f;t)+C_{1}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f;t)\left[{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+C_{2}\right].

(4.2)

Let
b^{n}_{1}:=b_{1}*\phi_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
. Then for Lebesgue almost all
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})=b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}).

Since the law of
(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})=b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}),\ \ a.s.

Hence, by Fatou’s lemma and (
4.2
) with
f=b^{n}_{1}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant\varliminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sup_{n}2C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(|b^{n}_{1}|;t)+\sup_{n}C_{1}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b^{n}_{1}|;t)\left[{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+C_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 2C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;t)+\frac{1}{2}\left[{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+C_{2}\right],

where the last step is due to (
2.7
) and (
3.19
).
This implies that for any
R>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant 4C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;t)+C_{2}.

(4.3)

Substituting this into (
4.2
) and letting
R\to\infty
, we obtain the desired estimate for
f\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
.

Next, we consider the general case
0\leqslant f\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\rm o}_{1}
. Let

f_{n}:=f*\phi_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}).

By Fatou’s lemma and what we have proven, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant\varliminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}f_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant C_{3}\sup_{n}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f_{n};t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{CC1}}}{{\leqslant}}C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f;t).

The proof is complete.
∎

Next we consider the approximating SDE with smooth coefficients:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}=(B_{0}+B_{1}^{n})(t,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\Sigma_{n}{\big(}t,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{n}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{0}={\boldsymbol{x}},

(4.4)

where

\displaystyle B_{1}^{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})=(b_{1}*\phi_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}),0)^{*},\ \ \Sigma_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})=(\sqrt{2}(\sigma*\phi_{n})(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}),0_{d\times d})^{*}.

(4.5)

Since the coefficients are global Lipschitz, there is a unique strong solution to the above SDE.
Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
be the law of
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}
in the space
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
. We have

Theorem 4.2
.

Under
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
, for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, there is a unique weak solution
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})\}_{t\in[0,T]}
to SDE (
1.3
) starting from
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Moreover,

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
weakly converges to the law of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\cdot}
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
.

Proof.

(
Uniqueness
)
Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
. Consider the following backward PDE with mollifying
b^{n}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}u_{n}+{\mathscr{L}}_{s}^{(0)}u_{n}+b_{1}^{n}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}+f=0,\ \ u_{n}(T,{\boldsymbol{x}})=0.

(4.6)

By the Schauder theory (see
[
4
]
), there is a unique solution
u_{n}
with the regularity

\nabla^{2}_{x_{1}}u_{n},\nabla_{x_{2}}u_{n}\in{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{T}C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}).

Let
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}:={\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})
be a solution of SDE (
1.3
) starting from
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
By Itô’s formula and (
4.6
), we have

u_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})=u_{n}(0,{\boldsymbol{x}})-\int_{0}^{t}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{0}^{t}((b_{1}-b_{1}^{n})\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n})(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\mbox{ a martingale}.

Let
R>|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
and define a stopping time

\tau_{R}:=\inf\big\{t>0:|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}|\geqslant R\big\}.

By the optional stopping theorem, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}u_{n}(T\wedge\tau_{R},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{T\wedge\tau_{R}})=u_{n}(0,{\boldsymbol{x}})+{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau_{R}}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+J^{R}_{n},

(4.7)

where

J^{R}_{n}:={\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau_{R}}((b_{1}-b_{1}^{n})\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n})(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right).

For
J^{R}_{n}
, by (
2.7
) and (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle|J_{n}^{R}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau_{R}}|b_{1}-b_{1}^{n}|(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbf{1}}_{|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}|\leqslant R}|b_{1}-b_{1}^{n}|(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim K^{(1)}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{1}}_{[0,T]\times B_{R}}|b_{1}-b^{n}_{1}|;T),

which implies by Lemma
2.5
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}|J_{n}^{R}|=0.

By Duhamel’s formula,
u_{n}
also satisfies the following integral equation:

u_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathscr{I}}^{T}_{f}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})+{\mathscr{I}}^{T}_{b^{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u_{n}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}),

where
{\mathscr{I}}^{T}_{f}
is defined in (
3.9
).
Let
u\in C_{b}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
be the unique solution of integral equation (
3.20
).
By Theorem
3.5
, we have
for each
t,{\boldsymbol{x}}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})=u(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}).

(4.8)

Thus by the dominated convergence theorem and taking limits
n\to\infty
for (
4.7
), we get

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}u(T\wedge\tau_{R},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{T\wedge\tau_{R}})=u(0,{\boldsymbol{x}})-{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau_{R}}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right).

Finally, letting
R\to\infty
and noting
u(T,{\boldsymbol{x}})=0
, we obtain

u(0,{\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right).

From this, it is now standard to derive the uniqueness (see
[
9
, Theorem 4.4.3]
).

(
Existence
) We use the standard weak convergence method. Note that

{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}={\boldsymbol{x}}+\int_{0}^{t}B_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{0}^{t}B^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}.

We first show the tightness of
U^{n}_{t}:=\int^{t}_{0}B^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\Big(\int^{t}_{0}b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,0\Big)^{*},n\in{\mathbb{N}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
.
For any stopping time
\tau\leqslant T
, by the strong Markov property and (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}|U^{n}_{\tau+\delta}-U^{n}_{\tau}|

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\delta}|b_{1}^{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}({\boldsymbol{x}}))|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{t,{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{t}^{t+\delta}|b_{1}^{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}({\boldsymbol{y}}))|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}^{n}|;\delta)\leqslant C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;\delta).

By
[
33
, Lemma 2.7]
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|U^{n}_{t+\delta}-U^{n}_{t}|^{1/2}\right)\lesssim(K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;\delta))^{1/2},

which implies that the law of
U^{n}_{\cdot}
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
is tight.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{n}|\leqslant|{\boldsymbol{x}}|+\int_{0}^{t}|B_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{0}^{t}|b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}\right|.

Since
B_{0}
is linear growth and
\Sigma_{n}
is bounded, by BDG’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant|{\boldsymbol{x}}|+C\int_{0}^{t}(1+{\mathbf{E}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+C\sqrt{t},

which implies by Gronwall’s inequality and (
4.1
) that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}|\right)\lesssim 1+|{\boldsymbol{x}}|+{\mathbf{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b^{n}_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\lesssim 1+|{\boldsymbol{x}}|.

(4.9)

Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int^{t+\delta}_{t}|B_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant C\delta{\mathbf{E}}\left(1+\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{n}|\right)\leqslant C(1+|{\boldsymbol{x}}|)\delta.

Consequently, the law of
(\int^{\cdot}_{0}B_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
is tight.
Moreover, by BDG’s inequality, it is also easy to see that the law of
(\int^{\cdot}_{0}\Sigma_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
is tight.
Combining these tightness, we obtain the tightness of
({\mathbb{Q}}^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}:=({\mathbf{P}}\circ({\boldsymbol{X}}^{n},W)^{-1})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}\times{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
.
Hence, there is a subsequence still denoted by
n
so that
{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
weakly converges to some probability measure
{\mathbb{Q}}
. By Skorokhod’s representation theorem, there are probablity space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{{\mathscr{F}}},\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}})
and
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}\times{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
-valued random variables
(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n},\tilde{W}^{n})
and
(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}},\tilde{W})
defined on it such that

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}\circ(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n},\tilde{W}^{n})^{-1}={\mathbb{Q}}^{n},\ \ \tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}\circ(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}},\tilde{W})^{-1}={\mathbb{Q}},

(4.10)

\displaystyle(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n},\tilde{W}^{n})\to(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}},\tilde{W}),\ \ \tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}-a.s.

(4.11)

and

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}=(B_{0}+B_{1}^{n})(t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\Sigma_{n}{\big(}t,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t}^{n}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\tilde{W}^{n}_{t}.

(4.12)

To prove that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\cdot}
is a weak solution, it suffices to show that for each
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{n}(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\tilde{W}^{n}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\to}}\int^{t}_{0}\Sigma(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\tilde{W}_{s}\mbox{ in probability},

(4.13)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}b_{1}^{n}(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\to}}\int^{t}_{0}b_{1}(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\mbox{ in probability}.

(4.14)

For limit (
4.13
), it follows by (
4.11
) and
[
24
, p.32]
, we omit the details.
Next we look at (
4.14
).
For fixed
m
, by the dominated convergence theorem, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}|b^{m}_{1}(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n}_{s})-b^{m}_{1}(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{s})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)=0.

For
R>|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define a stopping time

\tau^{n}_{R}:=\inf\big\{t>0:|\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}|\geqslant R\big\}.

By (
4.1
), we have

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau^{n}_{R}}|b_{1}^{m}-b_{1}|(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)\leqslant C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{1}}_{[0,T]\times B_{R}}|b_{1}^{m}-b_{1}|;T),

which implies by Lemma

that for fixed

,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup_{n}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau^{n}_{R}}|b_{1}^{m}-b_{1}|(s,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)=0.

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{n}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\tau^{n}_{R}>T)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{n}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}|\geqslant R\right)\leqslant\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{n}{\mathbf{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t}|\right)/R=0.

5.
Two-sided estimates of heat kernel

In this section we devote to proving the existence of density and two-sided estimates (

).
Our strategy is as follows: We first prove (
1.15
) for the smooth approximation SDE (

). Then by taking weak limits, we immediately obtain the desired estimates.
More precisely, consider the approximation SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t,s}=B_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t,s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\Sigma_{n}{\big(}t,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t,s}^{n}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ \ t\geqslant s,

(5.1)

where

and

are given in (

By
[
8
]
or
[
5

that enjoys the following two-sided estimates:

C_{0}(t-s)^{-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}}\leqslant p_{n}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\leqslant C_{1}(t-s)^{-2d}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}},

where, in
[
8
]
and
[
5
]

depend on

in
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
and the Lipschitz or Hölder constants of

.

The aim below is to show that the above constants only depend on
\Theta
in (
3.3
) and the quantity
K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|^{\gamma},1)
, where
\gamma>1
and for

,
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(\Theta)>0
.
Once we show this, then noting that the above two-sided estimates are equivalent to that for any

,

\displaystyle C_{0}(t-s)^{-2d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}}f({\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant{\mathbf{E}}f({\boldsymbol{X}}^{n}_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\leqslant C_{1}(t-s)^{-2d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}}f({\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{y}},

by Theorem
4.2
and taking weak limits

).
In the following we always assume
(H
{}^{\sigma}_{b}
)
and

\displaystyle b_{1},\sigma\in\cap_{T>0}{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{T}C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}).

(5.2)

We will use the convention that all the constants appearing below only depend on
\Theta
in (
3.3
) and the quantity

.
We shall fix

as in (

).

Let

be the heat kernel of operator
{\mathscr{L}}^{(0)}
with regular drift
b_{0}
and

be the heat kernel of operator
{\mathscr{L}}
.
By the Duhamel representation (see
[
5
, Subsection 3.1]
), we have

\displaystyle p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})=p_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})+(p\otimes H)(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}),

(5.3)

where

p\otimes H(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})=\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})H(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,

and

For
N\geqslant 2
, iterating equation (
5.3
)
N-1
times, we obtain

\displaystyle p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})=p_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(p_{0}\otimes H^{\otimes j})(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})+(p\otimes H^{\otimes N})(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

(5.4)

For simplicity of notations, we write

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\lambda}(t):=K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;t).

(5.5)

The following lemma provides an estimate for the summation.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
T\in(0,1]
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there are constants
C_{N}>0,\lambda_{N}\in(0,1)
depending only on
\Theta
and
N
such that for all
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}|p_{0}\otimes H^{\otimes j}|(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\leqslant C_{N}\Lambda^{N}_{\lambda_{N}}(t-s)g_{\lambda_{N}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}),

(5.6)

where
\lambda_{N}\downarrow 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

First of all, we use induction to show that

\displaystyle|H^{\otimes N}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})|\lesssim\Lambda^{N-1}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(t-s)|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}),

(5.7)

where
\lambda_{N}=\kappa^{N-1}\lambda
, and
\lambda,\kappa\in(0,1)
are from (
3.5
) and (
2.10
).
For
N=1
,
by (
3.5
) we have

|H|(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\lesssim\left|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})\right|g^{(1)}_{\lambda}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

For general
N\geqslant 2
, by induction, (
2.10
) and (
3.5
), it is readily seen that

\displaystyle|H^{\otimes N}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}H^{\otimes(N-1)}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})\cdot\nabla_{z_{1}}p_{0}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-2}}^{N-2}(t-s)|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})|\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-2}}^{N-2}(t-s)|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})|\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-2}}^{N-2}(t-s)|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})|\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}(t-s)g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}^{N-1}(t-s)|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

Thus, we have for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle|p_{0}\otimes H^{\otimes N}|(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}^{N-1}(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}g^{(0)}_{\lambda}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}^{N-1}(t-s)\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}g^{(0)}_{\lambda_{N}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}^{N-1}(t-s)\Lambda_{\kappa\lambda_{N}}(t-s)g^{(0)}_{\kappa\lambda_{N}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

This completes the proof.
∎

To treat the remainder term
p\otimes H^{\otimes N}
, we use the same argument as in
[
5
]
.
We recall the following variational representation formula due to Boué and Dupuis (see
[
2
]
and
[
34
]
).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
F
be a bounded Wiener functional on the classical Wiener space
({\mathbb{C}}_{T},{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}_{T}),\mu)
, where
\mu
is the classical Wiener measure.
Then we have

\displaystyle-\ln{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu}\mathrm{e}^{F}=\inf_{h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{b}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{h}(s)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s-F(\cdot+h)\right),

(5.8)

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{b}
denotes the set of all
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
-adapted and absolutely continuous process with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{h}(s)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant N,\ \ N>0.

(5.9)

Let
h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{b}
be a control process satisfying (
5.9
).
Consider the following controlled SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{t}=B{\big(}t,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{t}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+(\dot{h}(t),0)^{*}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\Sigma{\big(}t,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{t}{\big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ \ {\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{0}={\boldsymbol{x}},

(5.10)

where
W
is the Brownian motion on the classical Wiener space
({\mathbb{C}}_{T},{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}_{T}),\mu)
. All the expectations
{\mathbb{E}}
below will be taken with respect to the classical Wiener measure
\mu
.
Since
B
and
\Sigma
are Lipschitz continuous, there is a unique solution
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}
to the above controlled SDE.
Still, we fix
T
as in (
3.19
).
We need the following conditional version of Krylov’s estimate for controlled process
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}
.

Lemma 5.3

(Krylov type estimate)
.

For any
h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{b}
, there exist two constants
C=C(\Theta)>0
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that for all
0\leqslant f\in{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{T}C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
and
0\leqslant s<t\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}f(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)\lesssim_{C}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f;T)\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)+1\right].

(5.11)

Proof.

Let
u(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}):={\mathscr{I}}^{T}_{f}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
By (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle u(t,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h})

\displaystyle=u(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})+\int_{s}^{t}(\partial_{r}+{\mathscr{L}}_{r}+\dot{h}(r)\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}})u(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\int_{s}^{t}(\Sigma^{*}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u)(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{r}

\displaystyle=u(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})+\int_{s}^{t}[(b_{1}+\dot{h}(r))\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})-f(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\int_{s}^{t}(\Sigma^{*}\cdot\nabla_{x_{1}}u)(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{r}.

By taking conditional expectation and (
3.10
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}f(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)\leqslant 2\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{T}}+\|\nabla_{x_{1}}u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{T}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{r})|+|\dot{h}(r)|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(f;T)+C_{1}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(f;T){\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{r})|+|\dot{h}(r)|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right).

(5.12)

Taking
f=|b_{1}|
in (
5.12
) and by (
3.19
), we get

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)\leqslant 2C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;T)+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{r})|+|\dot{h}(r)|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right),

which implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 4C_{0}K^{(0)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;T)+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{AA1},\eqref{13}}}{{\leqslant}}C+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right).

(5.13)

Substituting this into (
5.12
) and by (
2.5
), we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Now we can show the following variational estimate that is crucial for the upper bound estimate.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\ell:{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\to(0,\infty)
be a measurable function satisfying that for some
\kappa>1
,

\kappa^{-1}\leqslant\ell({\boldsymbol{x}})\leqslant\kappa,\ \ {\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

For the given
T>0
from (
3.19
), there is a constant
\lambda=\lambda(\Theta)>0
such that for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\leqslant\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{\ln\ell({\boldsymbol{z}})-\lambda|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
s=0
and write

{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}:={\boldsymbol{X}}_{t,s}(x),\,\theta_{t}=\theta_{t,0}({\boldsymbol{x}}).

By the variational representation (
5.8
) with
F=\ln(\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}))
, we have

\displaystyle-\ln{\mathbb{E}}\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})=\inf_{h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{b}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\dot{h}(s)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s-\ln\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h})\right),

(5.14)

where
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}
solves SDE
(
5.10
)
with starting point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Then by
(
5.13
)
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})|{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{s}|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\Big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})|{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\big|{\mathscr{F}}_{s}+C\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+C\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b_{1}(s,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{2}+2{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+C\right)^{2},

where the last step is due to Young’s inequality. This implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{2}\leqslant 4{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+C\right)^{2}.

(5.15)

On the other hand, by (
1.14
), (
5.10
) and Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h}-\theta_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{t}\langle B(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})+(\dot{h}(r),0)^{*}-B_{0}(r,\theta_{r}),{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r}\rangle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad+2\int^{t}_{0}\langle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r},\Sigma(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}^{h}_{r}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{r}\rangle+\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma\Sigma^{*})(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Noting that by (
1.13
),

\displaystyle|B(r,{\boldsymbol{x}})-B_{0}(r,{\boldsymbol{y}})|\leqslant|b_{0}(r,{\boldsymbol{x}})-b_{0}(r,{\boldsymbol{y}})|+|x_{2}-y_{2}|+|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{x}})|\leqslant\kappa_{1}|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}|+|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{x}})|,

by BDG’s inequality and Young’s inequality, we have for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h}-\theta_{s}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa_{1}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r}|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|+|\dot{h}(r)|)\cdot|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r}|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+C{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r}|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{1/2}+Ct

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa_{1}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r}|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)+\varepsilon{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h}-\theta_{r}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|+|\dot{h}(r)|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}t.

Hence,
by Gronwall’s inequality and (
5.15
), we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{s}^{h}-\theta_{s}|^{2}\right)\lesssim_{C_{1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}^{h})|+|\dot{h}(r)|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{2}+t\lesssim_{C_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\dot{h}(r)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)+1.

Substituting this into (
5.14
), we derive

\displaystyle-\ln{\mathbb{E}}\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{b}}\left(\frac{1}{2C_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h}-\theta_{t}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}-{\mathbb{E}}\ln\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h})\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{b}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{1}{2C_{2}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h}-\theta_{t}|^{2}-\ln\ell({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}^{h})\right)-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\left(\frac{1}{2C_{2}}|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{t}|^{2}-\ln\ell({\boldsymbol{z}})\right)-\frac{1}{2}.

From this we immediately obtain the desired estimate.
∎

5.1.
Proof of the upper bound

Now we use the previous lemmas to show the following Gaussian upper-bound estimate.

Proposition 5.5

(Control of the remainder)
.

Suppose that

|b_{1}|^{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{K}}_{1}
for some
\gamma>1
. Then
there exist constants
C_{3},\lambda>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
N\geqslant N_{0}
and
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
,

(p\otimes H^{\otimes N})(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\leqslant C_{3}g_{\lambda}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

Proof.

By a standard scaling technique, it suffices to consider the case
s=0,t=1
(see
[
5
, Proposition 3.7]
for more details about the scaling).
By
(
5.7
)
and Hölder’s inqualiity we have

\displaystyle|(p\otimes H^{\otimes N})(0,{\boldsymbol{x}};1,{\boldsymbol{y}})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda^{N-1}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(1-r)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}p(0,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};1,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda^{N-1}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(1-r){\mathbb{E}}\left(|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}}))|g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}});1,{\boldsymbol{y}})\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda^{\gamma^{\prime}(N-1)}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(1-r){\mathbb{E}}g_{\lambda_{N}}^{(1)}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}});1,{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\gamma^{\prime}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left({\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{1}|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}}))|^{\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}},

where
{\frac{1}{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}}=1
. By Lemma
5.3
with
h=0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}}))|^{\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\lesssim K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|^{\gamma};1).

On the other hand, for fixed
\varepsilon,r\in(0,1)
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, let

\ell_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\varepsilon+g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(r,{\boldsymbol{x}};1,{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\gamma^{\prime}}.

Clearly,

\varepsilon\leqslant\ell_{\varepsilon}\leqslant\varepsilon+(1-r)^{-\gamma^{\prime}(2d+1/2)}.

By Lemma
5.4
with
\ell({\boldsymbol{x}})=\ell_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and (
2.2
), (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\ell_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{\ln\ell_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\lambda|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\left(\ell_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{z}})\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}\left(\varepsilon+(1-r)^{-\gamma^{\prime}(2d+1/2)}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{\prime}\lambda_{N}|{\mathbb{T}}_{1-r}(\theta_{1,r}({\boldsymbol{z}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}-\lambda|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}}\right).

Noting that by (
2.9
),

\displaystyle\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{\prime}\lambda_{N}|{\mathbb{T}}_{1-r}(\theta_{1,r}({\boldsymbol{z}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}-\lambda|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{\prime}\lambda_{N}|\theta_{1,r}({\boldsymbol{z}})-{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}-\lambda|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda^{\prime}_{N}|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,1}({\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}-\lambda_{N}^{\prime}|{\boldsymbol{z}}-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}_{N}}{2}|\theta_{r,1}({\boldsymbol{y}})-\theta_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}}\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{N}^{\prime\prime}|\theta_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}},

we further have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}g^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N}}(r,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}});1,{\boldsymbol{y}})^{\gamma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}\ell_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,0}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\lesssim(1-r)^{-(2d+1/2)\gamma^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{N}^{\prime\prime}|\theta_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle|(p\otimes H^{\otimes N})(0,{\boldsymbol{x}};1,{\boldsymbol{y}})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}(r)^{(N-1)\gamma^{\prime}}}{r^{(2d+1/2)\gamma^{\prime}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{N}^{\prime\prime}|\theta_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}}.

By (
5.5
) and Lemma
2.7
, there exists an
N=N(\gamma,d)
large enough so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{\lambda_{N-1}}(r)^{(N-1)\gamma^{\prime}}}{r^{(2d+1/2)\gamma^{\prime}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{K^{(1)}_{\lambda_{N-1}}(|b_{1}|;r)^{(N-1)\gamma^{\prime}}}{r^{(2d+1/2)\gamma^{\prime}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r<\infty.

Thus, we have

|(p\otimes H^{\otimes N})(0,{\boldsymbol{x}};1,{\boldsymbol{y}})|\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{N}^{\prime\prime}|\theta_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}}|^{2}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

5.2.
Proof of the lower bound

The proof is almost the same as in
[
5
, Subsection 3.3]
. For the readers’ convenience, we provide the detailed proof here.
For fixed
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
,
consider the following deterministic control problem:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{r,s}=B_{0}(r,\phi_{r,s})+(\varphi_{r},0)^{*},\ r\in[s,t],\ \phi_{s,s}={\boldsymbol{x}},\ \phi_{t,s}={\boldsymbol{y}},

(5.16)

where
\varphi:[s,t]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a square integrable control function. Let
I(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})
be the associated energy functional

\displaystyle I(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})=\inf_{\varphi\in L^{2}([s,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\left\{\left(\int_{s}^{t}|\varphi_{r}|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{1/2},\ \phi_{s,s}={\boldsymbol{x}},\ \phi_{t,s}={\boldsymbol{y}}\right\}.

We recall the following lemma proven in
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
about the energy functional which is crucial for proving the lower bound estimate.

Lemma 5.6
.

There exists constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(T,d,\kappa_{1})\geqslant 1
such that for all
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
,

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-1}(|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|-1)\leqslant I(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\leqslant c_{1}(|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|+1).

(5.17)

Moreover, one can find a control
\varphi\in L^{2}([s,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and a solution
\phi_{r,s}
to ODE (
5.16
) such that

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in[s,t]}|\varphi_{r}|\leqslant c_{2}(|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|+1)/\sqrt{t-s},

(5.18)

where
c_{2}=c_{2}(T,d,\kappa_{1})>0
.

Now we can show the following lower bound estimate.

Proposition 5.7
.

Suppose that

|b_{1}|^{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{K}}_{1}
for some
\gamma>1
. Then there exist constants
C,\lambda>0
such that for all
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
,

p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\geqslant Cg_{\lambda}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

Proof.

Let

c_{3}=2c_{1}c_{2}+2c_{0}+2,

where
c_{0},c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are from (
2.9
), (
5.17
) and (
5.18
).
Since
\lim_{\delta\to 0}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;\delta)=0
, we choose
T_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;T_{0})\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-c^{2}_{3}/\lambda}/(2C_{0}C_{3}).

(5.19)

We first derive the lower bound on
{\mathbb{D}}_{T_{0}}
. By (
3.4
), (
3.5
), (
2.10
) and the upper bound for
p
, we have

\displaystyle p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=p_{0}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})+(p\otimes H)(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}^{-1}g^{(0)}_{\lambda^{-1}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})-\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|H(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}^{-1}g^{(0)}_{\lambda^{-1}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})-C_{2}\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}g^{(0)}_{\lambda}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|b_{1}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}})|g^{(1)}_{\lambda}(r,{\boldsymbol{z}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\geqslant C^{-1}_{0}g^{(0)}_{\lambda^{-1}_{0}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})-C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;t-s)g^{(0)}_{\lambda_{4}}(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=(t-s)^{-2d}\Big(C^{-1}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}/\lambda}-C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;t-s)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{4}|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}}\Big).

If
(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})\in{\mathbb{D}}_{T}
satisfies

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|\leqslant c_{3},

then by (
5.19
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})&\geqslant(t-s)^{-2d}\Big(C^{-1}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-c^{2}_{3}/\lambda}-C_{3}K^{(1)}_{\lambda}(|b_{1}|;T_{0})\Big)\\
&\geqslant\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-c_{3}^{2}/\lambda}}{2C_{0}}(t-s)^{-2d}=\hat{C}(t-s)^{-2d}.\end{split}

(5.20)

Next we consider the case that

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|\geqslant c_{3}.

Let
M
be the smallest integer such that

\displaystyle M-1\leqslant|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}\leqslant M.

(5.21)

Define

\delta:=\frac{t-s}{M},\;t_{j}=s+j\delta,\;j=0,1,\cdots,M

Let
\phi_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})
be the optimal curve in Lemma
5.6
and
\varphi
is the corresponding control with

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in[s,t]}|\varphi_{r}|\leqslant c_{2}(|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|+1)/\sqrt{t-s}\leqslant c_{2}(\sqrt{M}+1)/\sqrt{t-s}.

Define

\xi_{j}:=\phi_{t_{j},s}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\;j=0,1,\cdots,M.

For any
j=0,\cdots,M-1
, by Lemma
5.6
, we have

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\theta_{t_{j+1},t_{j}}(\xi_{j}))-\xi_{j+1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}I(t_{j},\xi_{j};t_{j+1},\xi_{j+1})+1\leqslant c_{1}\left(\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}|\varphi_{r}|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{1/2}+1

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{1/2}\sup_{r\in[s,t]}|\varphi_{r}|+1\leqslant 2c_{1}c_{2}+1.

(5.22)

Now set

\Sigma_{0}:=\{\xi_{0}\}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\},\;\;\Sigma_{M}:=\{\xi_{M}\}=\{{\boldsymbol{y}}\},

and for
j=1,\cdots,M-1
,

\Sigma_{j}=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}:|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}({\boldsymbol{z}}-\xi_{j})|\leqslant 1\}.

By (
5.2
) and (
2.9
), for any
j=0,\cdots,M-1
, we have that for
z_{j}\in\Sigma_{j}
and
z_{j+1}\in\Sigma_{j+1}
,

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\theta_{t_{j+1},t_{j}}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j+1})|\leqslant

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\theta_{t_{j+1},t_{j}}(\xi_{j})-\xi_{j+1})|+|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\xi_{j+1}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j+1})|

\displaystyle+|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\theta_{t_{j+1},t_{j}}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})-\theta_{t_{j+1},t_{j}}(\xi_{j}))|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\theta_{t_{j+1},t_{j}}(\xi_{j})-\xi_{j+1})|+|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\xi_{j+1}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j+1})|

\displaystyle+c_{0}(|{\mathbb{T}}_{\delta}(\xi_{j}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})|+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2c_{1}c_{2}+2c_{0}+2=c_{3}.

Thus, by the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation and
(
5.20
), we have

\displaystyle p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}}:t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\cdots\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}p(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}};t_{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})\cdots p(t_{M-1},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{M-1};t_{M},{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\cdots{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{M-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\cdots\int_{\Sigma_{M-1}}p(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}};t_{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})\cdots p(t_{M-1},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{M-1};t_{M},{\boldsymbol{y}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\cdots{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{M-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant(\hat{C}\delta^{-2d})^{M}\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\cdots\int_{\Sigma_{M-1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\cdots{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{M-1}=(\hat{C}\delta^{-2d})^{M}(C\delta^{2d})^{M-1},

where
\hat{C}
is given in (
5.20
), and the last equality is due to
|\Sigma_{j}|=C\delta^{2d}
for some
C
depending on
d
. Recalling
\delta=(t-s)/M
and
M
given in (
5.21
), we finally have

\displaystyle p(s,{\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\hat{C}^{M}C^{M-1}\delta^{-2d}=(t-s)^{-2d}M^{2d}\exp\{M\log(\hat{C}C)\}/C

\displaystyle\gtrsim(t-s)^{-2d}\exp\{-\lambda|{\mathbb{T}}_{t-s}(\theta_{t,s}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}\}.

The lower bound is thus obtained for
t-s\leqslant T_{0}
and all
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
. For general
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T
, it again follows from the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation.
∎
