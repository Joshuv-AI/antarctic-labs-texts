Abstract
. This paper offers a unified approach to determining when two generalized Toeplitz operators on L 2 L^{2} are “equivalent”. This will be done through multipliers between closed subspaces of L 2 L^{2} . Our discussion will include Toeplitz operators (and their duals) on the Hardy space, Hankel operators, asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators, and dual asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators. Along the way, there will be a discussion of “equivalence” of functions and kernels of generalized Toeplitz operators and a generalization of the Brown–Halmos theorem for this class of operators.

1.
Introduction

This paper relates multipliers between subspaces of
L^{2}
and equivalence of generalized Toeplitz operators (defined below in (
1.2
)). To give some context to our results, we begin with some comments on what we mean by, and the significance of, the term equivalence of operators. For two complex Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
, we let
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
denote the bounded linear transformations from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{K}
. When
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}
, we let
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
.
There are various ways to identify
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
with
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2})
.
For example, when
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{K}
, and there is an isometric isomorphism
U:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfying
A=UBU^{*}
, then
A
and
B
are
unitarily equivalent
. Such operators
A
and
B
share the same norm, spectrum, invariant subspace structure, as well as any assumed properties of normality, subnormality, or hyponormality. When the isometric assumption on
U
is weakened and
U
is just a bounded invertible operator, then
A
and
B
are
similar
(
A=UBU^{-1}
) and thus share the same spectral properties and invariant subspace structure.

The focus of this paper is the following, even weaker, but very useful identification of
A
and
B
. Indeed,
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
and
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2})
are
equivalent
if there are bounded
invertible
operators
E:\mathcal{K}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
F:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{K}_{1}
such that

(1.1)

A=EBF.

The above notion of equivalence has been previously used to study spectral properties of operators and the solvability of singular integral equations
[
2
,
12
,
30
,
31
,
37
,
41
]
. For instance, the main motivation behind Wiener–Hopf factorization, and its varied applications, relies on the fact that it provides a useful equivalence between a Toeplitz operator (on the Hardy space) with a nonvanishing continuous symbol and a Toeplitz operator with a more easily understood monomial symbol.

One can show that equivalent operators are simultaneously Fredholm (with the same Fredholm defect numbers) and the range, kernel, and inverse of one operator is uniquely determined by the other. For example, a straightforward argument using the singular value decomposition will show that two
n\times n
matrices, thought of as linear transformations on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, are equivalent if and only if they have the same rank (see Proposition
6.9
).

This paper studies the above notion of equivalence for the class of generalized Toeplitz operators defined as follows. Let
L^{2}
denote the standard Lebesgue space of the circle and
L^{\infty}
the essentially bounded functions in
L^{2}
. For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, let
M_{\varphi}:L^{2}\to L^{2}
denote the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
. For two closed subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
of
L^{2}
, the
generalized Toeplitz operator
from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
with
symbol

\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, is defined by

(1.2)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2},\quad T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}=P_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}M_{\varphi}|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}},

where
P_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}
onto the closed subspace
\mathcal{H}
.
We will use the notation

\mathscr{T}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}):=\{T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}:\varphi\in L^{\infty}\}
and

\mathscr{T}(\mathcal{H}):=\mathscr{T}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}).

These generalized Toeplitz operators are particular cases of
general Wiener-Hopf operators

[
15
,
41
]
. When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=H^{2}
, the standard Hardy space of the disk
[
21
,
34
]
, the set
\mathscr{T}(H^{2})
is the set of
Toeplitz operators

[
4
]
. When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=(H^{2})^{\perp}=L^{2}\ominus H^{2}
,
\mathscr{T}((H^{2})^{\perp})
is the set of
dual Toeplitz operators

[
42
]
.
When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=H^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}=(H^{2})^{\perp}
, the set
\mathscr{T}(H^{2},(H^{2})^{\perp})
becomes the set of
Hankel operators

[
38
,
39
]
. For inner functions
\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}
and their corresponding model spaces
\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{j}}=H^{2}\ominus\Theta_{j}H^{2}
,
j=1,2
, the operators in
\mathscr{T}(\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{1}},\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{2}})
are the
asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators

[
6
,
11
,
40
]
. When
\mathcal{H}_{j}=(\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{j}})^{\perp}=L^{2}\ominus\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{j}}=\Theta_{j}H^{2}\oplus(H^{2})^{\perp}
,
j=1,2
, then
\mathscr{T}((\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{1}})^{\perp},(\mathcal{K}_{\Theta_{2}})^{\perp})
is the set of
asymmetric dual truncated Toeplitz operators

[
7
,
8
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. We will discuss the above classes of operators throughout this paper as motivating examples of generalized Toeplitz operators.

Our path towards an equivalence of generalized Toeplitz operators as in (
1.1
) will be through the bounded invertible
multipliers
between the ambient subspaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
of
L^{2}
. To this end, let

\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}

denote the set of invertible elements of the algebra
L^{\infty}
(i.e.,
a,\frac{1}{a}\in L^{\infty}
). If
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
satisfies

\mathcal{H}=a\mathcal{K},

i.e., is a bounded multiplier from
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{H}
, then the generalized Toeplitz operator
T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}
as in (
1.2
) becomes the (surjective) multiplication operator

T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=M_{a}|_{\mathcal{K}}.

Then, if
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
satisfy

\mathcal{H}_{1}=a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}=a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2},
Proposition
2.9
shows that the operators
E:\mathcal{K}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
F:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{K}_{1}
defined by

E=T_{\overline{a}_{2}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F=T_{a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{1}}

are invertible.
Finally, if the functions
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
are related by the identity

(1.3)

\psi=\bar{a}_{2}\varphi\,a_{1},

we will show in Theorem
2.17
that

(1.4)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}=ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}F,

and thus the generalized Toeplitz operators
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}
are equivalent in the sense of (
1.1
). Moreover, they are equivalent by means of the operators
E
and
F
which, in themselves, are generalized Toeplitz operators. Although the result above has a converse in the case of the classical Toeplitz operators (and connects to the well-known Brown–Halmos theorem
[
5
, Thm. 8]
), this does not extend to generalized Toeplitz operators since these operators can be the zero operator without the symbol being zero. We attempt to overcome this difficulty and formulate a sort of converse to this result in Proposition
3.16
.

When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{K}
, and
a_{1}=a_{2}=a
, the identity from (
1.4
) becomes

(1.5)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}}=F^{*}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}}F

and it is natural to ask whether
F^{*}=F^{-1}
, making
F=T^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}_{a^{-1}}
a unitary operator, and hence making the generalized Toeplitz operators
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}}
unitarily equivalent. We formulate conditions for when this occurs in Proposition
2.24
and Corollary
2.26
.

The equality (
1.3
) defines an equivalence relation between the functions
\varphi
and
\psi
. In §
5
we will see plenty of examples of equivalent functions amongst the inner functions and unimodular functions. For example, any Frostman shift of an inner function
\Theta
is equivalent to
\Theta
, and any two finite Blaschke products of the same degree are equivalent. It is also the case that any two Lipschitz continuous functions with the same winding number about the origin are equivalent.

These equivalences reduce the study of numerous properties (Fredholmness, invertibility, kernel, range) of a given generalized Toeplitz operator to those of another operator of the same type, but with a simpler symbol. This happens, for instance, when studying Toeplitz operators by using a Wiener-Hopf factorization of their symbols.
Alternatively, one can use the equivalence (
1.4
) to reduce the study of an operator between two spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
to that of an operator acting on “simpler” spaces
\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}
. We will cover this in §
6
and §
7
.

The skeptical reader might ask why we are exploring a notion of equivalence of generalized Toeplitz operators when there are the readily available and stronger notions of similarity and unitary equivalence.
The issue is that one might not be able to relate generalized Toeplitz operators using similarity or unitary equivalence (see Proposition
2.24
and Corollary
2.26
), but it is often possible using this weaker notion of equivalence. Sometimes this is all that is needed to relate the Fredholm properties of the two operators. There is also the obvious reason for the need for an equivalence beyond unitary equivalence and similarity due to the simple fact that the operators
A
and
B
in (
1.1
) each act between two
different
spaces which, in a sense, almost forces the need for the operators
E
and
F
to appear the way they do.

There is often an antilinear unitary mapping
C_{\mathcal{K}}
on
\mathcal{K}
for which the generalized Toeplitz operator
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}}
is
complex selfadjoint
in that

C_{\mathcal{K}}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}=(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}})^{*}.

When the generalized Toeplitz operators
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{H}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}}
are related by (
1.5
), we will show in §
4
that we can define an “equivalent” antilinear unitary operator
C_{\mathcal{H}}
such that complex selfadjointness of
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{K}}
with respect to
C_{\mathcal{K}}
implies complex selfadjointness with respect to
C_{\mathcal{H}}
and vice-versa. We will explore this phenomenon for certain classes of truncated Toeplitz operators in §
4
.

2.
Multipliers, projections, and equivalent operators

Let
L^{2}:=L^{2}(m)
, where
m=d\theta/2\pi
represents the standard normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
, and
L^{\infty}=L^{\infty}(m)
denotes the set of essentially bounded functions on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. For two closed subspaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
of
L^{2}
, the function
a\in L^{\infty}
is a
multiplier
from
\mathcal{K}
to
\mathcal{H}
if
a\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. In this section we will focus our attention on the case when
a\in\mathscr{G\!}L^{\infty}
, where

\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}:=\Big\{g\in L^{\infty}:\frac{1}{g}\in L^{\infty}\Big\}

(the invertible elements of the algebra
L^{\infty}
) and
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
. In this case, the multiplication operator
M_{a}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
, defined by
M_{a}f=af
, is bounded and invertible.

An important class of multipliers arises when
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
are both equal to
H^{2}
, the classical Hardy space

H^{2}=\{f\in L^{2}:\widehat{f}(n)=0\mbox{ for all }n<0\}.

In the above,
(\widehat{f}(n))_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
is the sequence of Fourier coefficients associated with an
f\in L^{2}
. By means of radial boundary values on
\mathbb{T}
, we can also regard
H^{2}
as the space of analytic functions on the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
whose Taylor coefficients belong to the sequence space
\ell^{2}
. Standard facts about
H^{2}
(inner-outer factorization
[
21
, Ch. 2]
) say that
\varphi H^{2}\subseteq H^{2}
if and only if
\varphi\in H^{\infty}:=H^{2}\cap L^{\infty}
and
\varphi H^{2}=H^{2}
if and only if
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, where

\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}:=\Big\{g\in H^{\infty}:\frac{1}{g}\in H^{\infty}\Big\}

(the invertible elements of the algebra
H^{\infty}
).

Another important example of multipliers appears when
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
are model spaces. A
model space
corresponding to an
inner function

I
(
I\in H^{\infty}
and
|I|=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
) is

\mathcal{K}_{I}:=H^{2}\cap(IH^{2})^{\perp}.

See
[
24
]
for the basics of model spaces.
Multipliers between model spaces were initially explored by Crofoot
[
14
]
and then later in
[
22
]
. If
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and
a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
(
\alpha
and
\theta
are inner), results from
[
14
]
show that
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
. In fact, there is a complete description of these types of multipliers
[
14
, Cor. 18]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Crofoot)
.

For
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and inner functions
\theta
and
\alpha
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
;

(b)

a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and

\alpha\bar{a}/(\theta a)
is a unimodular constant function on
\mathbb{T}
.

There can be unbounded multipliers from one model space onto another
[
22
, Thm. 6.9]
but in this paper we will only consider the
bounded
ones. The multipliers from one model space to
itself
, are just the non-zero constant functions
[
14
, Prop. 12]
. Finally, there may not be
any
multiplier between two given model spaces (e.g., when
\theta
and
\alpha
are finite Blaschke products of different degrees as this will correspond to finite dimensional model spaces of different dimensions
[
24
, p. 116]
). However, when
a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
, the multiplier
a
is unique up to a nonzero constant factor
[
14
, Cor. 13]
.

Model spaces
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
are kernels of Toeplitz operators, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\theta}}

[
24
, p. 108]
. For a
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, recall the
Toeplitz operator

T_{\varphi}
with symbol
\varphi
is the bounded operator on
H^{2}
defined by

(2.2)

T_{\varphi}f=P_{+}(\varphi f),

where
P_{+}
denotes the
Riesz projection

[
4
,
20
]
. Recall that
P_{+}
is the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto
H^{2}
and is given via Fourier series as

P_{+}\Big(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(n)\xi^{n}\Big)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(n)\xi^{n},\quad f\in L^{2}.

This allows us to formulate an equivalent version of Proposition
2.1
which will be useful in a moment.

Proposition 2.3
.

For
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and inner functions
\theta
and
\alpha
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
;

(b)

a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
a=\bar{\theta}\alpha\bar{h}
for some
h\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
.

Proof.

If
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and
a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
, then
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
. Using
[
12
, Prop. 2.16]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}=a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\iff\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\alpha}}=a\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\theta}}

\displaystyle\iff\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\alpha}}=\operatorname{ker}T_{a^{-1}\bar{\theta}}

\displaystyle\iff\bar{\alpha}=a^{-1}\bar{\theta}\bar{h}\quad\mbox{for some $h\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}$}.

Thus,
a=\bar{\theta}\alpha\bar{h}
with
h\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
. Conversely, if
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
a=\bar{\theta}\alpha\bar{h}
with
h\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, then

\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\alpha}}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\theta}a^{-1}\bar{h}}=a\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\theta}}.

Hence,
a\mathcal{K}_{\theta}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
.
∎

Returning to our general discussion of multipliers from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{K}
, we have the following useful results for the multipliers between the annihilators
\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

(a)

For
a\in L^{\infty}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

a\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
;

(ii)

\bar{a}\mathcal{H}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
.

(b)

For
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
;

(ii)

\bar{a}\mathcal{H}^{\perp}=\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Suppose
a\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. If
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
\widetilde{h}\in\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
, then

\langle\bar{a}\widetilde{h},k\rangle=\langle\widetilde{h},ak\rangle=0
and so
\bar{a}\mathcal{H}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
. The argument above can be reversed.
∎

Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
yield the following.

Proposition 2.5
.

For
b\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and inner functions
\alpha,\theta
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

b\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\perp}=\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{\perp}
;

(b)

b\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
and
\alpha b/(\theta\bar{b})
is a unimodular constant function on
\mathbb{T}
;

(c)

b\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
and
b=\theta\bar{\alpha}h
for some
h\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
.

In what follows,
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}
will be closed subspaces of
L^{2}
and, for a closed subspace
\mathcal{H}
of
L^{2}
,
P_{\mathcal{H}}
will denote the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto
\mathcal{H}
(and of course
P_{\mathcal{H}^{\perp}}=I_{\operatorname{d}}-P_{\mathcal{H}}
).

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\psi,\varphi\in L^{\infty}
. If either
\psi\mathcal{H}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\perp}
or
\varphi\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, then

T_{\psi\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}=T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2}}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}}.

Proof.

If
\psi\mathcal{H}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\perp}
we have

\displaystyle T_{\psi\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}(\psi\varphi)P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\psi(P_{\mathcal{H}}+P_{\mathcal{H}^{\perp}})\varphi P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\psi P_{\mathcal{H}}\varphi P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}

\displaystyle=T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2}}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}}.

If
\varphi\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, then

T_{\psi\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}=P_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\psi\varphi P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=P_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\psi P_{\mathcal{H}}\varphi P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2}}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}}.\qed

Remark 2.7
.

A theorem of Brown and Halmos
[
5
, Thm. 8]
says that for the classical Toeplitz operators on the Hardy space
H^{2}
we have, for
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
,

(2.8)

T_{\varphi}T_{\psi}=T_{\varphi\psi}\iff\mbox{$\varphi\in\overline{H^{\infty}}$ or $\psi\in H^{\infty}$.}

Since the condition
\varphi\in\overline{H^{\infty}}
is equivalent to
\varphi(H^{2})^{\perp}\subseteq(H^{2})^{\perp}
and the condition
\psi\in H^{\infty}
is equivalent to
\psi H^{2}\subseteq H^{2}
, the formula in (
2.8
) can be interpreted in terms of multipliers as saying that
T_{\varphi}T_{\psi}=T_{\varphi\psi}
if and only if either
\varphi
is a multiplier from
(H^{2})^{\perp}
into
(H^{2})^{\perp}
or
\psi
is a multiplier from
H^{2}
into
H^{2}
.

For generalized Toeplitz operators the “only if” part of the theorem cannot be always be generalized since the symbols
\varphi
and
\psi
are not unique (see (
2.33
) and (
2.34
) below). Thus, Theorem
2.6
appears to be the natural generalization of the Brown–Halmos theorem for generalized Toeplitz operators. We will discuss this further in the next section.

As we approach the main theorem of this section, we note that it follows from Theorem
2.6
that each
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
for which
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
determines two closely related bounded invertible operators from
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
. Then the operators

(2.10)

T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=M_{a}|_{\mathcal{K}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=P_{\mathcal{H}}\bar{a}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{K}}|_{\mathcal{K}}

are invertible with

(2.11)

(T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}})^{-1}=T_{a^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(T_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}})^{-1}=T_{\bar{a}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}

which are generalized Toeplitz operators. Moreover,

(2.12)

T_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=[(T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}})^{*}]^{-1}.

Proof.

The invertibility and the two equalities in (
2.11
) follow from Theorem
2.6
and the identities

\displaystyle T_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=(T_{a^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}})^{*}=[(T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}})^{-1}]^{*}=[(T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}})^{*}]^{-1}.\qed

The next two results relate the orthogonal projections
P_{\mathcal{H}}
and
P_{\mathcal{K}}
when
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
are related by multipliers.

Proposition 2.13
.

If
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with
\mathcal{H}\subseteq a\mathcal{K}
, then

(2.14)

P_{\mathcal{H}}f=aP_{\mathcal{K}}a^{-1}P_{\mathcal{H}}f=P_{\mathcal{H}}\bar{a}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{K}}\bar{a}f\quad\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}$}.

Proof.

Since
a^{-1}\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
we have that

P_{\mathcal{H}}f=aa^{-1}P_{\mathcal{H}}f=aP_{\mathcal{K}}a^{-1}P_{\mathcal{H}}f\quad\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}$}.

On the other hand, by Proposition
2.4
,
a^{-1}\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{K}\implies\bar{a}^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
, and so

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{H}}f

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{H}}\bar{a}^{-1}\bar{a}P_{\mathcal{H}}f=P_{\mathcal{H}}\bar{a}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{K}}\bar{a}P_{\mathcal{H}}f\quad\mbox{for every $f\in L^{2}$.}\qed

Corollary 2.15
.

If
a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
, then

(2.16)

P_{\mathcal{H}}=\widetilde{E}(P_{\mathcal{K}}\bar{a}I_{\operatorname{d}})=(aP_{\mathcal{K}})\widetilde{F},

where

\widetilde{E}=P_{\mathcal{H}}\bar{a}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\widetilde{F}=P_{\mathcal{K}}a^{-1}P_{\mathcal{H}}

are such that

T_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=\widetilde{E}|_{\mathcal{K}}
and
T_{a^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}=\widetilde{F}|_{\mathcal{H}}.

We now formulate our main theorem regarding equivalence of generalized Toeplitz operators. Various reformulations of this will be provided below (for Toeplitz, Hankel, and truncated Toeplitz operators). In §6 we will see an application of this to the inverse of a truncated Toeplitz operator, and in §7 we reformulate this for dual truncated Toeplitz operators.

Theorem 2.17
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}
be closed subspaces of
L^{2}
and
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
. If
\varphi,\psi\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with
\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
, then

(2.18)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}=ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}F,

where

(2.19)

E=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F=T_{a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1}}=M_{a_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}.

Thus, the generalized Toeplitz operators
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
are equivalent.

Proof.

The equivalence in (
2.18
) follows from Corollary
2.15
since we have

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}

\displaystyle=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}P_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}I_{L_{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=a_{1}^{-1}P_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}a_{1}P_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=a_{1}^{-1}T_{a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1}}.\qed

Remark 2.20
.

By Theorem
2.6
, the identity

(2.21)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}=ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}F,

where
\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
,
E=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}
, and
F=T_{a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1}},

holds if we impose the weaker conditions
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}
. However, with these weaker conditions, the operators
E
and
F
may not be invertible and so (
2.21
) does not provide an equivalence between
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
as in (
1.1
).

When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{K}
, Theorem
2.17
becomes the following.

Corollary 2.22
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
such that
a_{i}\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
,
i=1,2
. Then if
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
with

\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
, we have

(2.23)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}=ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}F,

with

E=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}
and
F=T_{a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=M_{a_{1}}|_{\mathcal{K}}.

At this point, it makes sense to ask under what conditions does (
2.23
) define a similarity or a unitary equivalence. Regarding similarity, we have the following.

Proposition 2.24
.

With the assumptions and notation as in Corollary
2.22
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

the operators
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
are similar via (
2.23
);

(b)

E=F^{-1}
;

(c)

T_{\bar{a}_{2}-a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}=0
;

(d)

T_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}-a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=0
;

(e)

T_{1-\bar{a}_{2}a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0
;

(f)

T_{1-\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H}}=0
.

Proof.

Observe that Proposition
2.9
yields

\displaystyle E=F^{-1}

\displaystyle\iff T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}=T_{a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}\iff T_{\bar{a}_{2}-a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}=0

\displaystyle\iff P_{\mathcal{K}}\bar{a}_{2}\big(1-a_{1}^{-1}\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}\big)P_{\mathcal{H}}|_{\mathcal{H}}=0

\displaystyle\iff(P_{\mathcal{K}}\bar{a}_{2}P_{\mathcal{H}})\Big(P_{\mathcal{H}}(1-a_{1}^{-1}\bar{a}_{2}^{-1})P_{\mathcal{H}}\Big)|_{\mathcal{H}}=0

\displaystyle\iff ET_{1-\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H}}=0\iff T_{1-\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}a_{1}^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H}}=0,

where we have taken into account that, by Corollary
2.4
,
\bar{a}_{2}\mathcal{H}^{\perp}=\mathcal{K}^{\perp}
and
E
is invertible. Analogously, Proposition
2.9
yields

\displaystyle E^{-1}=F\iff T_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}-a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=0\iff E^{-1}T_{1-a_{1}\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0\iff T_{1-a_{1}\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0,

where we took into account that
\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
.
∎

Remark 2.25
.

Naturally, the operator
F^{-1}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}F:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
is similar to
T^{\mathcal{H}}_{\psi}
for any invertible
F
on
\mathcal{K}
. However, in general, it is not a generalized Toeplitz operator of the form
T^{\mathcal{K}}_{\psi}
(see Example
6.4
).

Regarding the possibility that (
2.23
) defines a unitary equivalence between the operators
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
, which can happen only when the multiplication operator
M_{a_{1}}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
is unitary, we have the following.

Corollary 2.26
.

With the same assumptions as in Corollary
2.22
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

the identity in (
2.23
) is a unitary equivalence;

(b)

E=F^{-1}=F^{*}
;

(c)

T_{1-\bar{a}_{2}a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0
and
T_{1-|a_{1}|^{2}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0
.

Proof.

The condition
T_{1-\bar{a}_{2}a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0
is equivalent to
E=F^{-1}
while the condition
T_{1-|a_{1}|^{2}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0
is equivalent to
F^{-1}=F^{*}
.
∎

Corollary 2.27
.

When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{K}
,
a_{1}=a_{2}=a\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
, and
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
, the identity in (
2.23
) takes the form

(2.28)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}=F^{*}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}F,

with
F=T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}}=M_{a}|_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\psi=|a|^{-2}\varphi
,
and
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}
is unitarily equivalent to
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if
T_{1-|a|^{2}}^{\mathcal{K}}=0,

or equivalently,
T_{\bar{a}-a^{-1}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}=0.

In this case,
F^{*}=F^{-1}
, making
M_{a}|_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
an isometric isomorphism.

The question of similarity or unitary equivalence is therefore related to the delicate question of characterizing the functions
\eta\in L^{\infty}
for which
T_{\eta}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}=0
. For certain
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
, this takes place only when the symbol is zero. For other choices of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
, however, there is often a rich variety of symbols
\eta
corresponding to the zero generalized Toeplitz operator. We will explore this in the next several sections. Let us now discuss Theorem
2.17
in the case of the classical Toeplitz and Hankel operators as well as truncated Toeplitz operators.

Classical Toeplitz operators

Consider the classical Toeplitz operators
T_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}
, where
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
(recall the definition in (
2.2
)). Setting
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=H^{2}
in Theorem
2.17
, we have the following equivalence.

Theorem 2.29
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
with
\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
. Then

(2.30)

T_{\varphi}=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}T_{\psi}T_{a_{1}}

and thus
T_{\varphi}
is equivalent to
T_{\psi}
.

The alert reader will recognize an alternative proof of Theorem
2.29
from the well known Brown–Halmos theorem
[
5
, Thm. 8]
. One will also recognize a converse to Theorem
2.29
in that if
T_{\varphi}=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}T_{\psi}T_{a_{1}}
with
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, then
T_{\varphi}=T_{\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}}
and, by the uniqueness of symbols for Toeplitz operators
[
20
, p. 179]
,
\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
. In the following section we will recast this in terms of equivalence the symbols of Toeplitz operators. The uniqueness of these symbols, and the fact that
H^{\infty}\cap\overline{H^{\infty}}=\mathbb{C}
, also shows that (
2.30
)
never
provides a similarity between two Toeplitz operators unless we are in the degenerate case where
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are constant functions.

Classical Hankel operators

For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
recall the classical
Hankel operator

H_{\varphi}:H^{2}\to(H^{2})^{\perp}
defined by
H_{\varphi}=P_{-}\varphi P_{+}|_{H^{2}}

[
38
,
39
]
. Taking
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{1}=H^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=(H^{2})^{\perp}
in Theorem
2.17
, we have the following.

Theorem 2.31
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
with
\varphi=a_{2}\psi a_{1}
. Then

H_{\varphi}=S_{a_{2}}H_{\psi}T_{a_{1}},

where
S_{a_{2}}=P_{-}a_{2}P_{-}|_{(H^{2})^{\perp}}
is an invertible dual Toeplitz operator and
T_{a_{1}}
is an invertible Toeplitz operator. Thus,
H_{\varphi}
and
H_{\psi}
are equivalent Hankel operators.

See
[
19
,
42
]
for more on the dual of a Toeplitz operator. The non-uniqueness of symbols of Hankel operators (indeed
H_{\varphi}\equiv 0
if and only of
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
) prevents us from formulating a converse of Theorem
2.31
as we did for Toeplitz operators. See §
3
where we address this issue further.

Truncated Toeplitz operators

This section gives a version of Theorem
2.17
in the special case where the generalized Toeplitz operator becomes a truncated Toeplitz operator.
For an inner function
\alpha
, we let
P_{\alpha}
denote the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
.
For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
and inner functions
\alpha
and
\gamma
, let

(2.32)

A_{\varphi}^{\gamma,\alpha}:\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\to\mathcal{K}_{\alpha},\quad A^{\gamma,\alpha}_{\varphi}f:=P_{\alpha}(\varphi f)

be the
asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operator
from
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
to
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
with symbol
\varphi

[
6
,
11
]
. When
\gamma=\alpha
we write
A^{\gamma,\gamma}_{\varphi}=A^{\gamma}_{\varphi}
, which is called the
truncated Toeplitz operator
on
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
with symbol
\varphi

[
40
]
. Under the right circumstances, one can define
A^{\gamma,\alpha}_{\varphi}
when
\varphi\in L^{2}
(define it densely on bounded functions in
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
and consider if it has a bounded extension to
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
). Surprisingly, not every one of these operators can be represented by an
L^{\infty}
symbol
\varphi

[
1
]
. However, since we will be relating equivalence of symbols with equivalence of these operators, we will focus our attention solely on
bounded
symbols
\varphi
. The symbol
\varphi
for
A^{\gamma}_{\varphi}
is not unique. Indeed
[
40
]
,

(2.33)

A^{\gamma}_{\varphi}\equiv 0\iff\varphi\in\overline{\gamma}\overline{H^{2}}+\gamma H^{2}.

In a similar way
[
35
]
,

(2.34)

A^{\gamma,\alpha}_{\varphi}\equiv 0\iff\varphi\in\overline{\gamma}\overline{H^{2}}+\alpha H^{2}.

We now apply our main equivalence theorem (Theorem
2.17
) to these types of operators. To this end, let

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{\eta}
,
\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma},

where
\theta,\alpha,\eta
, and
\gamma
are inner functions. Recall from Proposition
2.3
that for inner functions
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
we have that
a\mathcal{K}_{\theta_{2}}=\mathcal{K}_{\theta_{1}}
with
a\in L^{\infty}
if and only if
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and

\theta_{1}=h_{-}\theta_{2}a
and
h_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
.
From the identity

1=\theta_{1}\bar{\theta}_{1}=h_{-}\theta_{2}a\bar{h}_{-}\bar{\theta}_{2}\bar{a}=h_{-}a\bar{h}_{-}\bar{a}

we see that
h_{-}\bar{a}=\bar{h}_{-}^{-1}a^{-1}=k\in\mathbb{C}
with
|k|=1
and so
h_{-}=k\bar{a}^{-1}
and we can assume without loss of generality that

\mathcal{K}_{\theta_{1}}=a\mathcal{K}_{\theta_{2}}\iff\theta_{1}=\bar{a}^{-1}\theta_{2}a\quad\mbox{with $a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}$}.

With this in mind, a version of Theorem
2.17
for truncated Toeplitz operators becomes the following.

Theorem 2.35
.

Let
\theta,\alpha,\eta,\gamma
be inner functions such that

\theta=\bar{a}_{1}^{-1}\eta a_{1},\quad\alpha=\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}\gamma a_{2}\quad\mbox{with $a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}$}.

Then for any
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
,
A_{\varphi}^{\theta,\alpha}
is equivalent to
A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\eta,\gamma}
with
\widetilde{\varphi}=\bar{a}_{2}\varphi a_{1}
, and we have

(2.36)

A_{\varphi}^{\theta,\alpha}=A_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\eta,\gamma}A_{a_{1}^{-1}}^{\theta,\eta}=A_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\eta,\gamma}M_{a_{1}^{-1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}.

In particular, if
\theta=\alpha
,
\eta=\gamma
, and
a_{1}=a_{2}=a
, the identity in (
2.36
) becomes

(2.37)

A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}=A_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\gamma}A_{a^{-1}}^{\alpha,\gamma}=A_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\gamma}M_{a^{-1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}},

with
\widetilde{\varphi}=\varphi|a|^{2}
.

We will have more to say about truncated Toeplitz operators in §
6
and we will give another reformulation of Theorem
2.17
involving dual truncated Toeplitz operators in sections §
7
.

3.
Multipliers and generalized Toeplitz kernels and ranges

In this section, we focus on how the equivalence of generalized Toeplitz operators, induced by multipliers, relates to the kernels and ranges of these operators.

Let us keep the notation from before:
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}
are closed subspaces of
L^{2}
and
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
. One of the main motivations for studying equivalence of operators of the form (
1.2
), with symbols
\varphi
and
\psi
related by a factorization
\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
as in Theorem
2.17
, stems from the study of Toeplitz operators and singular integral equations. In fact, the characterization of Toeplitz kernels and ranges, invertibility, and Fredholm properties is strongly related to the existence of an appropriate factorization of their symbols (for instance a Wiener–Hopf factorization or an almost periodic factorization). This allows us to establish an equivalence with Toeplitz operators with monomial symbols or simple exponential symbols – which are much easier to understand.

Note that when
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
with
|\varphi|=1
on
\mathbb{T}
, we have

(3.1)

\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}\not=\{0\}\iff\varphi=\bar{z}\bar{I}\bar{F}F^{-1},

where
I
is an inner function and
F\in H^{2}
is outer
[
12
,
36
]
. If
F\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, then for
\theta=zI
we have that
\varphi=\bar{F}\bar{\theta}F^{-1}
and, by Theorem
2.17
,
T_{\varphi}
is equivalent to
T_{\bar{\theta}}
, with

\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}=F\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{\theta}}=F\mathcal{K}_{\theta}.

This is true because if
g_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
g_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
, then for any
\widetilde{\varphi}\in L^{\infty}
,

(3.2)

\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}g_{+}}=g_{+}^{-1}\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}g_{-}}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}}.

One may ask about the relationship between the kernels of two Toeplitz operators when the symbol of one of them is multiplied by another function in
\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
which is not necessarily in
\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
or
\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
. If that function is a non-constant inner function, which also belongs to
H^{\infty}\setminus\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, we have that

(3.3)

\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}\theta}\subsetneq\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\theta\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}\theta}\subsetneq\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}}.

If we now consider Hankel operators instead, we see that with the same notation, if
g_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
\theta
is inner,

(3.4)

\operatorname{ker}H_{\widetilde{\varphi}g_{+}}=\operatorname{ker}H_{\widetilde{\varphi}},

(3.5)

\operatorname{ker}H_{\widetilde{\varphi}\theta}\supseteq\operatorname{ker}H_{\widetilde{\varphi}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\theta\operatorname{ker}H_{\widetilde{\varphi}\theta}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}H_{\widetilde{\varphi}},

while no simple relation corresponds to the second equality in (
3.2
) for Hankel operators.

In this section we show that by interpreting the relations (
3.2
) through (
3.5
) in terms of multipliers, we can put them in a general context and, along the way, get a better understanding of them.

If two Hilbert space operators
A
and
B
are equivalent via (
1.1
), with
A=EBF
, where
E
and
F
are invertible operators, it is clear that their kernels and ranges are isomorphic with

\operatorname{ker}A=F^{-1}\operatorname{ker}B
and
\operatorname{Ran}A=E\operatorname{Ran}B.

More generally we have the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2})
and
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
with
A=EBF
, where
E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2})
and
F\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
.

(a)

If
E
is invertible, then

F\operatorname{ker}A\subseteq\operatorname{ker}B
and
E\operatorname{Ran}B\supseteq\operatorname{Ran}A.

(b)

If
F
is invertible, then

\operatorname{ker}B\subseteq F\operatorname{ker}A
and
E\operatorname{Ran}B\subseteq\operatorname{Ran}A.

Proof.

For the proof of (a), note that

Af=0\iff EBFf=0\iff BFf=0\implies Ff\in\operatorname{ker}B.

Moreover,

Af=g\iff EBFf=g\iff BFf=E^{-1}g\iff E^{-1}g\in\operatorname{Ran}B.

For the proof of (b), note that

\displaystyle Bf=0

\displaystyle\iff BF(F^{-1}f)=0

\displaystyle\implies EBF(F^{-1}f)=0

\displaystyle\iff A(F^{-1}f)=0

\displaystyle\implies F^{-1}f\in\operatorname{ker}A.

Moreover,

Bf=g\implies EBf=Eg\iff EBF(F^{-1}f)=Eg

∎

Concerning the case where the operators
A
and
B
are generalized Toeplitz operators, one can take into account Theorem
2.6
, Remark
2.20
, and Proposition
3.6
to formulate the following.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{\infty}
with
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and let
\varphi=\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}
.

(a)

If
a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
, then

a_{1}\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}})\supseteq\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}).

(b)

If
a_{1}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}
, then

\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq a_{1}\operatorname{ker}_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}})\subseteq\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}).

Some immediate consequences of Proposition
3.7
now follow.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{\infty}
.

(a)

If
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}
we have

(3.9)

a_{1}\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}})\supseteq\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\psi a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}).

(b)

If
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}
we have

(3.10)

\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{a}_{2}\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}})\subseteq\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\bar{a}_{2}\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}).

(c)

If
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{H}_{2}

, then

(3.11)

a_{1}\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}\;\mbox{and}\;\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}})=T_{\bar{a}_{2}}^{\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2}}\operatorname{Ran}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}).

Corollary 3.12
.

If
a_{1}\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}
, then for any
\mathcal{H}_{2}

(3.13)

a_{1}^{-1}\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}.

If
a_{2}\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
, then for any
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,

(3.14)

\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{a}_{2}\psi}^{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}.

Remark 3.15
.

(a)

For Toeplitz operators on

) can be seen as particular cases of (

) and (

), while the inclusion in (

) can be see as a particular case of (

) with
a_{2}=\theta
and

.

(b)

For Hankel operators, the equality (

and

(c)

From the previous results we see that if
a_{1}
is a multiplier from
\mathcal{K}_{1}
into (onto)
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, then
a_{1}
is also a multiplier from
\operatorname{ker}T_{\bar{a}_{2}\psi a_{1}}^{\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2}}

if

by

, we do have the following.

.

Let
a_{1}\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
with

. If

then there exists a

such that

and

for any

.

Note how this gives somewhat of a converse to Theorem

.

4.
Complex selfadjointness

For a closed subspace
\mathcal{K}
of
L^{2}
, consider a mapping
C_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
for which

C_{\mathcal{K}}(f+ag)=C_{\mathcal{K}}f+\bar{a}C_{\mathcal{K}}g
for all
f,g,\in\mathcal{K}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
. Such a mapping is
antilinear
. For an antilinear mapping
C_{\mathcal{K}}
, one can define an antilinear adjoint
C_{\mathcal{K}}^{*}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
which satisfies

\langle C_{\mathcal{K}}f,g\rangle=\overline{\langle f,C^{*}_{\mathcal{K}}g\rangle}
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{K}
. The mapping
C_{\mathcal{K}}
is called an
antilinear unitary mapping
if
C_{\mathcal{K}}^{*}=C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}
. We will give some examples in a moment.

Remark 4.1
.

In many settings, one imposes the additional condition that
C_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}=I_{\operatorname{d}}
and such
involutive
and antilinear unitary mappings are called
conjugations
. Many of the
C_{\mathcal{K}}
we will present below will be involutive. However, this extra hypothesis is not necessary for our results and so we leave it out. The version without the involutive assumption was explored in
[
9
]
while the version with the involutive assumption was explored in
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

Let
C_{\mathcal{K}}
denote an antilinear unitary mapping on
\mathcal{K}
. For
a\in\mathscr{G}L^{\infty}
with
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
, this next result shows that, under certain conditions, the operators

F=M_{a}|_{\mathcal{K}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad E=F^{*}=P_{\mathcal{K}}\bar{a}P_{\mathcal{H}}|_{\mathcal{H}}

from Proposition
2.9
induce a corresponding antilinear unitary mapping
C_{\mathcal{H}}
on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
be closed subspaces of
L^{2}
and
C_{\mathcal{K}}
be an antilinear unitary mapping on
\mathcal{K}
,
F
be an invertible operator from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{K}
, and
E=F^{*}
. Then for

(4.3)

C_{\mathcal{H}}=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{K}}F,

the following are equivalent:

(a)

C_{\mathcal{H}}
is an antilinear unitary mapping on
\mathcal{H}
;

(b)

C_{\mathcal{K}}(FE)=(FE)C_{\mathcal{K}}
.

(c)

C_{\mathcal{H}}=EC_{\mathcal{K}}E^{-1}
.

Proof.

With
C_{\mathcal{H}}
defined to be
F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{K}}F
, we see that
C_{\mathcal{H}}
is antilinear and invertible with

C_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}=F^{*}C^{*}_{\mathcal{K}}(F^{-1})^{*}=F^{*}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(F^{-1})^{*}

and
C_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}F
. Moreover,

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}=C_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\iff F^{*}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(F^{-1})^{*}=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}F

\displaystyle\iff FF^{*}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(FF^{*})^{-1}=C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\iff(FF^{*})C_{\mathcal{K}}=C_{\mathcal{K}}(FF^{*})

\displaystyle\iff(FE)C_{\mathcal{K}}=C_{\mathcal{K}}(FE)

\displaystyle\iff EC_{\mathcal{K}}E^{-1}=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{K}}F.\qed

Recall from Remark
4.1
that our antilinear isometry
C_{\mathcal{K}}
is called a conjugation if
C_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}=I_{\operatorname{d}}
. Note that a conjugation satisfies
C=C^{-1}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
C_{\mathcal{H}}
be defined as in (
4.3
). Then
C_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=I_{\operatorname{d}}\iff C_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}=I_{\operatorname{d}}
.

An operator
T
on
\mathcal{H}
is
complex selfadjoint
with respect to an antilinear isometry
C
on
\mathcal{H}
, if
CTC^{-1}=T^{*}

[
9
]
. If the antilinear unitary mapping also satisfies the involutive criterion
C^{2}=I_{d}
, then
T
is called a
C
-
symmetric operator
. These types of operators were explored in great detail in
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

As we will see shortly, some generalized Toeplitz operators are complex self adjoint. The main theorem of this section relates the complex self adjointness of two equivalent generalized Toeplitz operators.

Theorem 4.5
.

For closed subspaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
of
L^{2}
and
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
, suppose that
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}
and
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
are equivalent via

E=T_{\bar{a}}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F=T_{a}^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}},

where
a\in\mathscr{G}L^{\infty}
with
a\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
. Also suppose that
C_{\mathcal{H}}
is an antilinear unitary mapping on
\mathcal{H}
such that
F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{H}}F=EC_{\mathcal{H}}E^{-1}
. Then
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
is complex selfadjoint with respect to
C_{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}
is complex selfadjoint with respect to the antilinear unitary mapping
C_{\mathcal{K}}=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{H}}F
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{K}}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}C_{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}=(T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}})^{*}

\displaystyle\iff(EC_{\mathcal{H}}E^{-1})(ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}F)(F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}F)=E(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}})^{*}F

\displaystyle\iff C_{\mathcal{H}}T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}C_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}=(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}})^{*}\qed

It is natural to also consider a conjugation on
\mathcal{K}
which is equivalent, via the operators
E
and
F
defined in Theorem
4.5
, to the antilinear unitary mapping
C_{\mathcal{H}}
on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

With the same assumptions as in Proposition
4.2
, and with

(4.7)

\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}=EC_{\mathcal{H}}F,

the following are equivalent:

(a)

\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}
is an antilinear unitary mapping on
\mathcal{K}
;

(b)

(FE)C_{\mathcal{H}}(FE)=C_{\mathcal{H}}
;

(c)

EC_{\mathcal{H}}F=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{H}}E^{-1}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}
be defined as in Proposition
4.6
and assume that it is an anti-linear unitary mapping.
Then
C_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=I_{\operatorname{d}}
if and only if
\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}=I_{\operatorname{d}}
.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle=EC_{\mathcal{H}}(FEC_{\mathcal{H}})F=EC_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}E^{-1}F^{-1}F=I_{\operatorname{d}}\qed.

Note that, even if
\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}
, defined by Proposition
4.7
, is an antilinear unitary mapping, we are unable to conclude, in general, that
T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}
is complex selfadjoint with respect to
\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}
when
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
is complex selfadjoint with respect to
C_{\mathcal{H}}
. Indeed, in general, if
F
is not a unitary operator, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}\widetilde{C}^{-1}_{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle=F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{H}}E^{-1}(ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}F)F^{-1}C_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}E^{-1}

\displaystyle=F^{-1}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}})^{*}E^{-1}

\displaystyle\not=F^{*}(T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}})^{*}E^{*}=(T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}})^{*}.

When
F
is a unitary operator, we have the following.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
C_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}
be defined as in Propositions
4.2
and
4.6
, respectively. If
F
is unitary then

(a)

C_{\mathcal{K}}
is an antilinear unitary mapping.

(b)

C_{\mathcal{K}}=\widetilde{C}_{\mathcal{K}}
,

(c)

T_{\varphi}^{\mathcal{K}}=ET_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}F
is complex selfadjoint with respect to
C_{\mathcal{K}}
if and only if
T_{\psi}^{\mathcal{H}}
is complex selfadjoint with respect to
C_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Example 4.10
.

For an inner function
\gamma
, the truncated Toeplitz operator
A_{\varphi}^{\gamma}
(defined in (
2.32
)) is complex selfadjoint
[
27
]
(see also
[
24
, p. 291]
) with respect to the conjugation
C_{\gamma}
on the model space
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
defined by

(C_{\gamma}f)(\xi)=\gamma(\xi)\overline{\xi f(\xi)}.

Now let
\alpha
be any inner function and
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
such that
a\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
, i.e.,
a=\bar{\alpha}\gamma\bar{h}
for some
g\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
. Since
\alpha,\gamma
are inner, we have

|a|=|h|
and that
a\bar{a}=h\bar{h}
if and only if
ah^{-1}=\bar{h}\bar{a}^{-1}
. Since the left hand side of the last equality belongs to
H^{\infty}
while the right hand side belongs to
\overline{H^{\infty}}
, we conclude that
ah^{-1}=\bar{h}\bar{a}^{-1}
is a constant and therefore
h=ca
. We can assume that
c=1
and it follows that

(4.11)

\gamma=\bar{a}^{-1}\alpha a.

Therefore,
Theorem
2.35
says that

A_{|a|^{2}\psi}^{\alpha}=A_{\bar{a}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\psi}^{\gamma}A_{a}^{\alpha,\gamma}.

Theorem
4.5
now says that
A_{|a|^{2}\psi}^{\alpha}
is complex symmetric with respect to the conjugation
\widetilde{C}
defined on
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
by
\widetilde{C}f=a^{-1}C_{\gamma}af
. Note that
\widetilde{C}
satisfies the equivalent conditions of Proposition
4.2
. Indeed, using
4.11
, we see that for any
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle a^{-1}C_{\gamma}af

\displaystyle=a^{-1}\gamma\bar{z}\bar{a}\bar{f}

\displaystyle=a^{-1}(\bar{a}^{-1}\alpha a)\bar{z}\bar{a}\bar{f}

\displaystyle=\alpha\bar{z}\bar{f}

and

\displaystyle A_{\bar{a}}^{\gamma,\alpha}C_{\gamma}A_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\alpha,\gamma}f

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\bar{a}P_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}P_{\gamma}\bar{a}^{-1}P_{\alpha}f

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\bar{a}P_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}\bar{a}^{-1}f

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\bar{a}P_{\gamma}\gamma\bar{z}a^{-1}\bar{f}

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\bar{a}P_{\gamma}\bar{a}^{-1}\alpha\bar{z}\bar{f}

\displaystyle=P_{\alpha}\bar{a}\bar{a}^{-1}\alpha\bar{z}\bar{f}

\displaystyle=\alpha\bar{z}\bar{f}

and we verify that
\widetilde{C}
turns out to be the usual conjugation on
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
.

5.
Kernels of Toeplitz operators

When
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=\mathcal{K}_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{2}=H^{2}
, then
aH^{2}=H^{2}
if and only if
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
. We say that
\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}
are
equivalent
, and write
\varphi\sim\psi
, if and only if there are
a_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
a_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
such that

(5.1)

\varphi=a_{-}\psi a_{+}.

In this case, the Toeplitz operators
T_{\varphi}
and
T_{\psi}
are equivalent (see Theorem
2.29
). Conversely, if
T_{\varphi}
and
T_{\psi}
are equivalent via (
2.18
), then by Corollary
3.8
there exists an
a_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
such that
\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}=a_{+}\operatorname{ker}T_{\psi}
. It follows that
\psi
and
\varphi
satisfy a relation of the form (
5.1
), so that
\varphi\sim\psi
(see the remarks before Proposition
3.16
).

It is often the case that the function
\psi
in (
5.1
) is the conjugate of an inner function
\theta
, i.e.,
\varphi\in\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and

(5.2)

\varphi=a_{-}\bar{\theta}a_{+}.

This is the case when the function
\varphi
is an invertible Hölder continuous function on
\mathbb{T}
with exponent
\mu\in(0,1)
which has a negative index with respect to the origin
[
37
, Ch. 3, Cor. 5,2]
and we take
\theta=z^{n}
.
It is also the case for certain classes of almost periodic functions
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
, where the inner function
\theta
is an exponential of the form
\theta(x)=e^{iax}
,
a\in(0,\infty)

[
3
]
.

Let us give a few examples of equivalent functions.

Example 5.3

(Finite Blaschke products)
.

Suppose
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are points in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
(repetitions allowed) and

\alpha(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{z-a_{j}}{1-\bar{a}_{j}z}

is a finite Blaschke product
[
25
]
. Note that
\alpha
is an inner function. For any
z\in\mathbb{T}
we have the identity

(5.4)

\alpha(z)=\frac{1}{z^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-a_{j})\cdot z^{n}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-\bar{a}_{j}z}=\alpha_{-}(z)\cdot z^{n}\cdot\alpha_{+}(z),

where
\alpha_{+},\bar{\alpha}_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, and hence
\alpha\sim z^{n}
. As an immediate consequence, we see that all finite Blaschke products of the same degree (same number of zeros – counting multiplicity) are equivalent.

Example 5.5

(Generalized Frostman shifts)
.

Let
h\in H^{\infty}
with
\|h\|_{\infty}<1
. For any inner function
\theta
we define

(5.6)

\theta_{\bar{h}}:=\frac{\theta-\overline{h}}{1-h\theta}.

Using the fact that
\theta
is inner, and thus
\theta\bar{\theta}=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
, it follows that

\theta_{\bar{h}}\in L^{\infty}
and
|\theta_{\bar{h}}|=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
.
Furthermore, a calculation shows that

\theta_{\bar{h}}=a_{-}\theta a_{+},

where

a_{-}=1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad a_{+}=(1-h\theta)^{-1}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}.

Thus
\theta\sim\theta_{\bar{h}}
. Observe that
\theta
is inner while
\theta_{\bar{h}}
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
, but not necessarily inner (since it may not be the boundary values of a bounded analytic function on
\mathbb{D}
).
An interesting case arises when
\bar{h}
is a constant function equal to
a\in\mathbb{D}
. In this case, the function
\theta_{\bar{h}}
from (
5.6
) becomes

(5.7)

\theta_{a}(z)=\frac{\theta(z)-a}{1-\bar{a}\theta(z)},

which is a
Frostman shift
of
\theta
(and also an inner function). Thus
\theta
and
\theta_{a}
are equivalent inner functions for every
a\in\mathbb{D}
.
A classical result of Frostman
[
23
]
(see also
[
29
, p. 75]
) says that
\theta_{a}
is a Blaschke product for “most”
a\in\mathbb{D}
.

It follows from (
5.2
) that
a_{+}^{-1}
is a multiplier from
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
onto
\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}
, thus yielding two isomorphisms between
\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
as in Proposition
2.9
. This gives us the following (taking Proposition
2.4
and Corollary
3.8
into account).

Proposition 5.8
.

If
\varphi=a_{-}\bar{\theta}a_{+}
, where
a_{+},\bar{a}_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
, then

(a)

\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}=a_{+}^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
.

(b)

(\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi})^{\perp}=\bar{a}_{+}\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{\perp}
.

Moreover, if (
5.2
) holds, when studying
\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}
we can assume, without loss of generality, that
|\varphi|=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
because
\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}=\operatorname{ker}T_{\widetilde{\varphi}}
with
\widetilde{\varphi}=\bar{a}_{+}\bar{\theta}a_{+}
(since
a_{-},\bar{a}_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}

[
12
]
). On the other hand, if
|\varphi|=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
and
\varphi=a_{-}\bar{\theta}a_{+}
, it is not difficult to see that
a_{-}
and
\bar{a}_{+}^{-1}
must differ by a multiplicative constant. Thus, if
|\varphi|=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
and (
5.2
) holds, then

(\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi})^{\perp}=a_{-}^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{\perp}.

The equality in Proposition
5.8
has a connection with Hayashi’s representation of Toeplitz kernels. In
[
32
]
, Hayashi proved that the kernel
K
of a Toeplitz operator with symbol
\varphi
can be written as
K=f_{+}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
, where
f_{+}
is outer,
\alpha
is inner with
\alpha(0)=0
, and
f_{+}
multiplies the model space
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
isometrically onto
K
. When
f_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, we must have
\varphi=f_{-}\bar{\alpha}f_{+}^{-1}
for some
f_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}
(see §
3
).
In general, neither the outer function
f_{+}
nor the inner function
\alpha
in Hayashi’s representation of a Toeplitz kernel are explicitly known. However, the representation in Proposition
5.8
is explicit whenever one can factor
\varphi
as in (
5.2
). This can be done for a wide class of functions and provides an isomorphism between
\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
through an invertible bounded multiplier. From here, one might ask when is this isomorphism isometric, as in Hayashi’s representation. The answer lies in the relationship between the two isomorphisms from
\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}
onto
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, namely

(5.9)

I_{1}:=M_{a_{+}}|_{\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad I_{2}:=P_{\theta}\bar{a}_{+}^{-1}I_{\operatorname{d}}|_{\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}}=(I_{1}^{-1})^{*},

and, by Corollary
2.27
, can be given in terms of a truncated Toeplitz operator as follows.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\varphi=a_{-}\bar{\theta}a_{+}
, where
a_{+},\bar{a}_{-}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
and
\theta
is inner. Then

M_{a_{+}^{-1}}:\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{\varphi}
is an isometric isomorphism if and only if

(5.11)

A^{\theta}_{1-|a_{+}|^{-2}}=0

or, equivalently,

(5.12)

1-|a_{+}|^{-2}\in\overline{\theta H^{2}}+\theta H^{2}.

Example 5.13

(Generalized Crofoot transform)
.

For
h\in H^{\infty}
with
\|h\|_{\infty}<1
and
\theta
inner, recall

\theta_{\bar{h}}=\frac{\theta-\bar{h}}{1-h\theta},

the generalized Frostman shift from Example
5.5
. For any
k\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,

\overline{\theta_{\bar{h}}}=a_{-}\bar{\theta}a_{+},

where

a_{+}:=k^{-1}(1-h\theta)\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}\quad\mbox{and}\quad a_{-}=k(1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta})^{-1}\in\mathscr{G}\!\overline{H^{\infty}}.

From Proposition
5.8
we know that

J_{\bar{h}}:=\frac{k}{1-h\theta}

is a multiplier from
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
onto
\operatorname{ker}T_{\overline{\theta_{\bar{h}}}}
. We call the operator of multiplication by
J_{\bar{h}}
a
generalization of the Crofoot transform
, for it includes, as a special case, the Crofoot transform as defined in
[
40
]
– as we will now show.

From Proposition
5.10
,
M_{J_{\bar{h}}}
will be an isometric isomorphism from
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
onto
\operatorname{ker}T_{\overline{\theta_{\bar{h}}}}
if and only if

(5.14)

1-\frac{|k|^{2}}{|1-h\theta|^{2}}\in\overline{\theta H^{2}}+\theta H^{2}.

Observe that

\displaystyle 1-\frac{|k|^{2}}{|1-h\theta|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-h\theta)(1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta})-|k|^{2}}{(1-h\theta)(1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta})}

\displaystyle=\frac{h(\bar{h}-\theta)+\bar{h}(h-\bar{\theta})+1-h\bar{h}-|k|^{2}}{(1-h\theta)(1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h}{1-h\theta}\theta-\frac{\bar{h}}{1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta}}\bar{\theta}+\frac{1-|h|^{2}-|k|^{2}}{(1-h\theta)(1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta})}

and that

\frac{h}{1-h\theta}\in H^{\infty}\subseteq H^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\bar{h}}{1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta}}\in\overline{H^{\infty}}\subseteq\overline{H^{2}}.

Therefore, (
5.14
) is satisfied if and only if

(5.15)

\frac{1-|h|^{2}-|k|^{2}}{(1-h\theta)(1-\bar{h}\bar{\theta})}\in\overline{\theta H^{2}}+\theta H^{2}.

This last condition is certainly satisfied when
h=\bar{p}\in\mathbb{D}
is a constant function, and then choosing the constant
k
such that
|k|^{2}=1-|h|^{2}
. In this case,
\theta_{\bar{h}}=\theta_{p}
is an inner function (a Frostman shift of
\theta
from (
5.7
)),
\operatorname{ker}T_{\overline{\theta_{\bar{h}}}}=\mathcal{K}_{\theta_{p}}
is a model space, and
M_{J_{\bar{h}}}=M_{J_{p}}
is the
Crofoot transform
which maps
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
isometrically onto
K_{\theta_{p}}
(considered in
[
40
]
). Whether (
5.15
) can be satisfied for some
k\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
when
h
is not a constant function is an open question worthy of further investigation.

6.
Truncated Toeplitz operators

In this section, we continue (and refine) our discussion of truncated Toeplitz operators begun in §
2
.
From Theorem
2.35
, with
\theta=\alpha,\eta=\gamma
, and
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, and from Corollary
2.27
, we have the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

If
\alpha
and
\gamma
are inner functions,
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, and
\alpha=\bar{a}^{-1}\gamma a
, with
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, then
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}=a\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
and

(6.2)

A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}=A_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\gamma}A_{a^{-1}}^{\alpha,\gamma}=A_{\bar{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\gamma}M_{a^{-1}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}},

where
\widetilde{\varphi}=\varphi|a|^{2}
. The relation in (
6.2
) defines a unitary equivalence between
A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}
and
A_{\varphi|a|^{2}}^{\gamma}
if and only if

1-|a|^{2}\in\overline{\gamma H^{2}}+\gamma H^{2},

or, equivalently,

1-|a|^{-2}\in\overline{\alpha H^{2}}+\alpha H^{2},

and, in this case,

(6.3)

A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}=aA_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\gamma}a^{-1}I_{\operatorname{d}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}}.

An example where (
6.3
) holds is given by the Crofoot transform acting on a truncated Toeplitz operator, as shown in
[
40
]
(see also Example
5.13
).

Note that if we consider an operator on
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
by replacing
A_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}
in (
2.36
) by the inverse of
A_{a_{1}^{-1}}^{\theta,\eta}
(which is equal to multiplication by
a_{1}
on
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
), we do not, in general, obtain a truncated Toeplitz operator. This can be seen with the following example.

Example 6.4
.

For any
\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and any finite Blaschke product
\theta
, define

(6.5)

k_{\lambda}^{\theta}(z)=\frac{1-\overline{\theta(\lambda)}\theta(z)}{1-\bar{\lambda}z}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\theta}(z)=\frac{\theta(z)-\theta(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}.

Now let
\alpha
be the simple degree two Blaschke product defined by

\alpha(z)=\frac{(z-\tfrac{1}{2})(z-\tfrac{1}{3})}{(1-\tfrac{1}{2}z)(1-\tfrac{1}{3}z)},

which can be factored as
\alpha=\bar{a}^{-1}z^{2}a,

where

a(z)=\frac{1}{(1-\tfrac{1}{2}z)(1-\tfrac{1}{3}z)}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty},

and let
\varphi(z)=\alpha(z)/z
. Then defining

A:=A_{a^{-1}}^{\alpha,z^{2}}A_{\alpha/z}^{\alpha}A_{a}^{z^{2},\alpha}=a^{-1}A_{\alpha/z}^{\alpha}aI_{\operatorname{d}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z^{2}}},

which is an operator from
\mathcal{K}_{z^{2}}
to itself, we see that for any
f\in\mathcal{K}_{z^{2}}=\operatorname{span}\{1,z\}
,

(6.6)

Af=a(0)f(0)a^{-1}\widetilde{k}_{0}^{\alpha}=a(0)a^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{k}_{0}^{\alpha}\otimes k_{0}^{z^{2}}(f).

Thus,
A
is a rank-one operator. If
A
were a truncated Toeplitz operator, it would have be a scalar multiple of one of the following types:

k_{\lambda}^{z^{2}}\otimes\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{z^{2}}
,
\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{z^{2}}\otimes k_{\lambda}^{z^{2}}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
or
k_{\zeta}^{z^{2}}\otimes k_{\zeta}^{z^{2}}
, where
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}

[
40
]
. Comparing this with the form of
A
in (
6.6
), we see that
a^{-1}\widetilde{k}_{0}^{\alpha}
should be a constant multiple of
\widetilde{k}_{0}^{z^{2}}
, which it is not.

Theorem
6.1
allows us to reduce the study of truncated Toeplitz operators on arbitrary model spaces of dimension
n
to that of a truncated Toeplitz operator on
\mathcal{K}_{z^{n}}
, as in the following example.

Example 6.7
.

Let
\alpha
be the degree two Blaschke product from Example
6.4
and consider the question of invertibility of the operator
A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}
, where

\varphi(z)=\frac{(z-\tfrac{1}{2})(z-\tfrac{1}{3})(z^{2}+1)}{z^{2}}.

Since
\alpha=\bar{a}^{-1}z^{2}a
with
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
as in Example
6.4
, we can use Theorem
6.1
to conclude that
A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}
is equivalent to
A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{2}}
with

\widetilde{\varphi}(z)=1+\tfrac{5}{6}z+z^{2}h_{+}

for some
h_{+}\in H^{\infty}
. Moreover

A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{2}}=A_{1+\frac{5}{6}z}^{z^{2}}

which is invertible
[
10
, Thm. 4.1]
. Thus,
A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}
is invertible.

Also note that (
2.37
) provides an equivalence between the inverses of the two operators
A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}
and
A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\gamma}
, if they exist. We have the following result which can be obtained from
[
10
, Thm. 2.4]
.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
\theta
be an inner function and let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
be such that

G=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\theta}&0\\
\varphi&\theta\end{bmatrix}

admits a Wiener–Hopf factorization of the form
G=G_{-}G_{+}
, with
\overline{G}_{-}^{\,\pm},G_{+}^{\pm}\in(H^{2})_{2\times 2}

[
10
,
37
]
. Moreover, denote

G_{+}^{-1}=[g_{ij}^{+}]
and
G_{-}^{-1}=[g_{ij}^{-}]
,
i,j=1,2.

Then, for all
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
,

(A_{\varphi}^{\theta})^{-1}f=P_{\theta}(g_{11}^{+}P_{+}g_{12}^{-}+g_{12}^{+}P_{+}g_{22}^{-})f.

When
\theta=z^{n}
, necessary and sufficient conditions for
A_{\varphi}^{\theta}
to be invertible were obtained in
[
10
]
and the factorization of
G
itself can be similarly determined in explicit form by solving the relatively simple Riemann–Hilbert problem
GG_{+}^{-1}=G_{-}
(
G
is a
2\times 2
triangular matrix of polynomials with diagonal entries
\bar{z}^{n}
and
z^{n}
). Proposition
6.8
provides an explicit and simple expression for the inverse of
A_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{n}}
and from (
2.37
) we can then obtain an expression for the inverse of
A_{\varphi}^{\alpha}
(which does not depend on the basis that one chooses for
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
).

As we have seen in Example
5.3
, for two finite Blaschke products
\alpha
and
\gamma
of equal degree, there is an
a\in\mathscr{G}H^{\infty}
such that
a\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
. This allows us to prove the following.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\alpha
and
\gamma
be two finite Blaschke products of equal degree. Then two truncated Toeplitz operators
A^{\alpha}_{\varphi}
and
A^{\gamma}_{\psi}
are equivalent if and only if they have the same rank.

Proof.

This result will follow from the following linear algebra result. Two
n\times n
matrices
A
and
B
are equivalent (i.e., there are
n\times n
invertible matrices
E
and
F
with
A=EBF
) if and only if they have the same rank. Indeed, if
A
is equivalent to
B
then clearly
A
and
B
have the same rank. Conversely, if
A
and
B
have the same rank
r\leqslant n
then by the singular value decomposition there are
n\times n
unitary matrices
U,V,W,Q
such that

A=U\Sigma_{A}V
and
B=W\Sigma_{B}Q,

where
\Sigma_{A},\Sigma_{B}
are the
n\times n
diagonal matrices

\Sigma_{A}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{r},0,0,\ldots 0),

\Sigma_{B}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r},0,0,\ldots,0).

Of course,
\sigma_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
are nonzero for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
.
If

D=\operatorname{diag}\Big(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\lambda_{1}},\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\lambda_{2}},\ldots,\frac{\sigma_{r}}{\lambda_{r}},1,1,\ldots,1\Big),

then
D
is invertible and

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=U\Sigma_{A}V

\displaystyle=UD\Sigma_{B}V

\displaystyle=UDW^{*}BQ^{*}V

\displaystyle=(UDW^{*})B(Q^{*}V)

\displaystyle=EBF

and so
A
is equivalent to
B
.
∎

The proof of Proposition
6.9
says that
any
matrix is equivalent to a Toeplitz matrix of equal rank. In fact, one can say more. A result from
[
13
, Thm. 6.2]
says that any square matrix is similar to some truncated Toeplitz operator on some finite dimensional model space. One can only go so far with these type of results since small matrices are similar to Toeplitz matrices while large ones are generally not
[
33
]
.

We end with an application to the essential spectrum of two equivalent truncated Toeplitz operators. For a bounded operator
T
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, recall that the
essential spectrum
of
T
, denoted by
\sigma_{e}(T)
, is the set of all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that the operator
T-\lambda I
is not Fredholm.

For an inner function
\alpha
, let

\Sigma(\alpha):=\Big\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}:\varliminf_{z\to\xi}|\alpha(z)|=0\Big\}

denote the
boundary spectrum
of the inner function
\alpha
. One can show that
\Sigma(\alpha)
consists of the accumulation points of the zeros of
\alpha
along with the support of the associated singular measure for
\alpha
in its Riesz factorization
[
24
, p. 152]
. Another known result
[
24
, p. 204]
is that

(6.10)

\Sigma(\alpha)=\sigma_{e}(A^{\alpha}_{z}).

Let
\mathscr{R}
denote the set of rational functions whose poles are not in
\mathbb{T}
. Also define

\mathscr{R}_{+}:=P_{+}\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{R}\cap H^{\infty}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathscr{R}_{-}:=P_{-}\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{R}\cap\overline{zH^{\infty}}.

As used before,
P_{+}
is the Riesz projection from
L^{2}
onto
H^{2}
and
P_{-}=I-P_{+}
.
If
\gamma
is inner and also satisfies
\gamma=\bar{a}^{-1}\alpha a
for some
a\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}
, then by Theorem
2.35
, the operators

A^{\alpha}_{R-\lambda}
and
A^{\gamma}_{|a|^{2}(R-\lambda)}
are equivalent for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
Thus,
A^{\alpha}_{R-\lambda}
is Fredholm if and only if the operator
A^{\gamma}_{|a|^{2}(R-\lambda)}
is Fredholm. Using the notation
P_{\gamma H^{2}}
for the orthogonal projection of

L^{2}

onto
\gamma H^{2}
, we see that

\displaystyle A^{\gamma}_{|a|^{2}(R-\lambda)}

\displaystyle=P_{\gamma}|a|^{2}(R-\lambda)P_{\gamma}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\Big(P_{\gamma}(R-\lambda)P_{\gamma}\bar{a}P_{\alpha}aP_{\gamma}+P_{\gamma}(R-\lambda)P_{-}(\bar{a}P_{\alpha}aP_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{\gamma}(R-\lambda)P_{\gamma H^{2}}(\bar{a}P_{\alpha}aP_{\gamma})\Big)|_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=A_{R-\lambda}^{\gamma}A_{\bar{a}}^{\alpha,\gamma}A_{a}^{\gamma,\alpha}+P_{\gamma}(R-\lambda)P_{-}(\bar{a}P_{\alpha}aP_{\gamma})|_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{\gamma}(R-\lambda)P_{\gamma H^{2}}(\bar{\alpha}P_{\alpha}\alpha P_{\gamma})|_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}},

where the last two operators above are finite rank (see Lemma
6.12
below). Since the operators
A_{\bar{a}}^{\alpha,\gamma}
and
A_{a}^{\gamma,\alpha}
are invertible, we obtain the following.

Theorem 6.11
.

With the assumptions above,

\sigma_{e}(A^{\alpha}_{R})=\sigma_{e}(A^{\gamma}_{R})
.

As mentioned above, the needed ingredient to complete the proof of Theorem
6.11
is the following lemma.

Lemma 6.12
.

If
R\in\mathscr{R}
, then
P_{\gamma}RP_{-}
and
P_{\gamma}RP_{\gamma H^{2}}
are finite rank operators.

Proof.

We have
R=R_{+}+R_{-}
with
R_{\pm}\in\mathscr{R}_{\pm}
. Then

P_{\gamma}RP_{-}=P_{\gamma}(R_{-}+R_{+})P_{-}=P_{\gamma}(P_{+}R_{+}P_{-})

and

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}RP_{\gamma H^{2}}

\displaystyle=P_{\gamma}(R_{-}+R_{+})\gamma P_{+}\bar{\gamma}P_{+}

\displaystyle=P_{\gamma}R\gamma P_{+}\bar{\gamma}P_{+}

\displaystyle=P_{\gamma}\gamma(P_{-}R_{-}P_{+})\bar{\gamma}P_{+}.

By Kronecker’s theorem on finite rank Hankel operators
[
39
]
, it follows that
P_{\gamma}R_{+}P_{-}
and
P_{\gamma}R_{-}P_{\gamma H^{2}}
are finite rank operators.
∎

Theorem
6.11
along with (
6.10
) gives another proof of an observation of Crofoot
[
14
, Thm. 14]
:
A necessary condition for
\alpha
to be equivalent to
\gamma
is that
\Sigma(\alpha)=\Sigma(\gamma)
.

7.
Dual truncated Toeplitz operators

This section formulates a version of Theorem
2.17
when the generalized Toeplitz operator becomes a dual truncated Toeplitz operator. If
\alpha
is inner, recall from our discussion at the end of §
2
that
P_{\alpha}
denotes the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto the model space
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
. Then
Q_{\alpha}:=I-P_{\alpha}
is the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}
onto

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\perp}=\alpha H^{2}\oplus(H^{2})^{\perp}.

For
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, define

D_{\varphi}^{\gamma,\alpha}:\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\perp},\quad D_{\varphi}^{\gamma,\alpha}f:=Q_{\alpha}(\varphi f).

When
\gamma=\alpha
we write
D^{\gamma,\gamma}_{\varphi}=D_{\varphi}^{\gamma}
. The operator
D_{\varphi}^{\gamma}
is a
dual truncated Toeplitz operator
on
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
with symbol
\varphi
, while
D_{\varphi}^{\gamma,\alpha}
is an
asymmetric dual truncated Toeplitz operator
from
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
to
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
with symbol
\varphi
. These operators have received considerable attention recently
[
8
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. Unlike the criterion in (
2.33
) and (
2.34
) for an asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operator to be the zero operator, we have the following (see
[
16
, Prop. 2.1]
)

(7.1)

D^{\gamma,\alpha}_{\varphi}\equiv 0\iff\varphi=0.

Thus, the symbol of a dual truncated Toeplitz operator is unique.

Some results concerning the equivalence of dual truncated Toeplitz operators are known when
\varphi(z)=z
and thus
D_{z}^{\alpha}
is the dual of the compressed shift
[
7
]
. For example,
D_{z}^{\alpha}
is unitarily equivalent to
D_{z}^{\gamma}
if and only if
|\alpha(0)|=|\gamma(0)|
. When
\alpha(0)\not=0
and
\gamma(0)\not=0
, then
D_{z}^{\alpha}
is similar to
D_{z}^{\gamma}
and they are both similar to the bilateral shift on
L^{2}
(i.e., the operator
f(\xi)\mapsto\xi f(\xi)
). When
\alpha(0)=0
,
D_{z}^{\alpha}
is unitarily equivalent to
S\oplus S^{*}
on
H^{2}\oplus H^{2}
, where
S
is the unilateral shift. From here, one can characterize the invariant subspaces of
D_{z}^{\alpha}

[
43
]
.

A version of Theorem
2.17
for dual truncated Toeplitz operators is the following.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\theta
,
\eta,\alpha,\gamma
be inner functions and
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathscr{G}\overline{H^{\infty}}
with

\theta=a_{1}\eta\bar{a}_{1}^{-1}
and
\alpha=a_{2}\gamma\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}
.
Then for any
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
, the operator

D_{\varphi}^{\theta,\alpha}
is equivalent to
D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\eta,\gamma}
, where

\widetilde{\varphi}=\bar{a}_{2}\varphi a_{1}
. Moreover,

D_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}
and
D_{a_{1}^{-1}}^{\theta,\eta}
are bounded invertible operators such that

D_{\varphi}^{\theta,\alpha}=D_{\bar{a}_{2}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\eta,\gamma}D_{a_{1}^{-1}}^{\theta,\eta}.

Corollary 7.3
.

For inner functions
\gamma
,
\alpha
and
a\in\mathscr{G}\overline{H^{\infty}}
with

\alpha=a\gamma\bar{a}^{-1}
,
let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}=|a|^{2}\varphi
. Then

D^{\alpha}_{\varphi}=(D^{\alpha,\gamma}_{a^{-1}})^{*}D^{\gamma}_{\widetilde{\varphi}}D_{a^{-1}}^{\alpha,\gamma}.

One can wonder whether the equivalence in the previous result can be an isometric isomorphism, that is to say,
D_{a}^{\gamma,\alpha}=D_{\overline{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}
? Unlike the case with truncated Toeplitz operators discussed earlier (Theorem
6.1
), similarity in this case can only happen in a trivial way. Indeed,
D_{a}^{\gamma,\alpha}=D_{\overline{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}
implies that

D_{a-\overline{a}^{-1}}^{\gamma,\alpha}\equiv 0
and so, by (
7.1
),
a=\overline{a}^{-1}
. Since
H^{\infty}\cap\overline{H^{\infty}}=\mathbb{C}
, this would imply that
a=\overline{a}^{-1}
and is a unimodular constant function.

It follows from Corollary
7.3
that

(7.4)

\operatorname{ker}D^{\theta}_{\varphi}=\bar{a}\operatorname{ker}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{\eta}.

As an application of this result we study the kernel of a dual truncated Toeplitz operator whose symbol
\varphi
does not belong to
\mathscr{G}\!L^{\infty}
and thus whose study cannot be reduced to that of a truncated Toeplitz operator with symbol
\varphi^{-1}

[
8
, §6]
.

We have that

(7.5)

\operatorname{ker}D^{\theta}_{\varphi}=\{f_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{\perp}:\varphi f_{1}=f_{2+}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\}.

Noting that
f_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{\perp}
if and only if
f_{1}=f_{1-}+\theta f_{1+}
, where
f_{1-}\in(H^{2})^{\perp}
and
f_{1+}\in H^{2}
; and
f_{2+}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
if and only if
f_{2+}\in H^{2}
and
\bar{\theta}f_{2+}=f_{2-}\in(H^{2})^{\perp}
, the equation defining
\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}
is equivalent to the matricial Riemann–Hilbert problem

(7.6)

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\theta}&0\\
-1&\varphi\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f_{2+}\\
f_{1+}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
0&\varphi\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f_{2-}\\
f_{1-}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Now let
\theta
be a finite Blaschke product of degree
n
and, for any inner function
\alpha
, define
\varphi(z)=\alpha(z)(z-1)
. From Example
5.3
we see that

\theta=\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}z^{n}B_{+}\quad\mbox{with $B_{+}\in\mathscr{G}\!H^{\infty}$}.

Therefore, by Corollary
7.3
,

(7.7)

D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=D_{B_{+}}^{z^{n},\theta}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{n}}D_{\overline{B_{+}}}^{\theta,z^{n}},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\widetilde{\varphi}=\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}\varphi B_{+}^{-1},

and, by (
7.4
),

(7.8)

\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=D^{z^{n},\theta}_{\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}}\operatorname{ker}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{n}}=\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}\operatorname{ker}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{n}}.

Thus we have reduced the problem of determining
\operatorname{ker}D^{\theta}_{\varphi}
to that of determining
\operatorname{ker}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{n}}
. For this problem, we use (
7.5
) with
\theta
and
\varphi
replaced by
z^{n}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}
respectively to obtain

(7.9)

\bar{z}^{n}f_{2+}=f_{2-}

and

(7.10)

f_{2+}-B_{+}^{-1}\alpha(z-1)\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}z^{n}f_{1+}=B_{+}^{-1}\alpha(z-1)\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}f_{1-}.

From (
7.9
) we have that
f_{2+}=p_{n-1}
, where
p_{n-1}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-1}
(the polynomials of degree at most
n-1
). Substituting this into the equation in (
7.10
) we see that

p_{n-1}-B_{+}^{-1}\alpha(z-1)\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}z^{n}f_{1+}=B_{+}^{-1}\alpha(z-1)\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}f_{1-}

if and only if

B_{+}^{-1}(z-1)f_{1+}=\bar{\alpha}\bar{z}^{n}p_{n-1}\overline{B_{+}}-\bar{\theta}\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}(z-1)f_{1-}.

Moreover, since the left hand side of the last equality belongs to
H^{2}
while the right hand side belongs to
\overline{H^{2}}
, both sides are equal to a constant. Due to the factor of
z-1
on the left hand side, this constant must be zero. This means that
f_{1+}=0
and

(7.11)

\bar{\alpha}\bar{z}^{n}p_{n-1}\overline{B_{+}}=\bar{\theta}\overline{B_{+}^{-1}}(z-1)f_{1-}.

It follows from (
7.11
) that

\frac{p_{n-1}}{z-1}=\bar{\theta}\alpha z^{n}\overline{B_{+}^{-2}}f_{1-}\in L^{2}

and so
p_{n-1}(1)=0
. Therefore, if
n=1
we have that
p_{n-1}\equiv 0
and thus
\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=\{0\}
.
When
n>1
, we have

(7.12)

\bar{\alpha}\bar{z}^{n}\bar{B}_{+}^{2}p_{n-2}=\bar{\theta}f_{1-}\quad\mbox{with $p_{n-2}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2}$}.

Let
\gamma=\operatorname{gcd}(\theta,z\alpha)
(which is necessarily a finite Blaschke product). Then, from (
7.12
),

\frac{z^{2}\alpha}{\gamma}B_{+}^{2}z^{n-2}\overline{p_{n-2}}=\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\overline{f_{1-}}.

From the fact that
z^{n-2}\overline{p_{n-2}}=\widetilde{p}_{n-2}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2}
, we see that

\frac{z\alpha}{\gamma}B_{+}^{2}\widetilde{p}_{n-2}=\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\bar{z}\overline{f_{1-}}\quad\mbox{with $\bar{z}\overline{f_{1-}}\in H^{2}$}.

and thus,

\widetilde{p}_{n-2}\in\frac{\theta}{\gamma}H^{2}.

Let
k=\operatorname{deg}(\theta/\gamma)
. When
n-2<k
, we have
\widetilde{p}_{n-2}=0
and so
\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=\{0\}
. When
n-2\geqslant k
we have

\widetilde{p}_{n-2}=p_{\theta/\gamma}\widetilde{p}_{n-2-k},

where
p_{\theta/\gamma}
is the numerator of
\theta/\gamma
and
\widetilde{p}_{n-2-k}\in\mathscr{P}_{n-2-k}
. Hence,

f_{1-}=\theta\overline{p_{\theta/\gamma}}\bar{\alpha}\bar{z}^{2}\overline{B_{+}^{2}}\overline{\widetilde{p}_{n-2-k}}.

Conversely, if
f_{1}=f_{1-}
(note that
f_{1+}=0
), then
f_{1}\in\operatorname{ker}D_{\widetilde{\varphi}}^{z^{n}}
and so
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=n-1-k
.

Summarizing these results we obtain the following.

Proposition 7.13
.

Let
\theta
be a Blaschke product of degree
n
and, for any inner function
\alpha
, let
\varphi(z)=\alpha(z)(z-1).
Furthermore, let

\gamma=\operatorname{gcd}(\theta,z\alpha),\quad k=\operatorname{deg}\frac{\theta}{\gamma},

and

d(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-\overline{w_{j}}z),

where
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
are the zeros of
\theta/\gamma
(counting multiplicity). Then

(a)

if
n\leqslant k+1
,
\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=\{0\}
.

(b)

if
n>k+1
,

\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=a\big(\overline{d}\cdot\overline{\big(\frac{\alpha z}{\gamma}\big)}\cdot\bar{z}\cdot\overline{\mathscr{P}_{n-k-2}})\subseteq(H^{2})^{\perp}

and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}D_{\varphi}^{\theta}=n-1-k
.
