Abstract
. We study properties of the convex hull of a set S S described by quadratic inequalities. A simple way of generating inequalities valid on S S is to take nonnegative linear combinations of the defining inequalities of S S . We call such inequalities aggregations . Special aggregations naturally contain the convex hull of S S , and we give sufficient conditions for intersection of such aggregations to define the convex hull. We introduce the notion of hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC), which is related to the classical notion of hidden convexity of quadratic maps. We show that if the quadratic map associated with S S satisfies HHC, then the convex hull of S S is defined by special aggregations. To the best of our knowledge, this result generalizes all known results regarding aggregations defining convex hulls. Using this sufficient condition, we are able to recognize previously unknown classes of sets where aggregations lead to convex hull. We show that the condition known as positive definite linear combination for every triple of inequalities, together with hidden hyperplane convexity is sufficient for finitely many aggregations to define the convex hull, answering a question raised in [ 8 ] . All the above results are for sets defined using open quadratic inequalities. For closed quadratic inequalities, we prove a new result regarding aggregations giving the convex hull, without topological assumptions on S S , which were needed in [ 14 , 8 ] .



1.
Introduction

The well-known Farkas lemma in linear programming states that any implied linear inequality for a non-empty set defined by finitely many linear inequalities, can be obtained by taking a nonnegative weighted combination of the original inequalities. We call the procedure of obtaining implied inequalities for a given set by rescaling the defining constraints by nonnegative weights and then adding the scaled constraints together as
aggregation
. Aggregations have also been studied in the context of integer linear programming (for example,
[
2
]
) and mixed-integer nonlinear programming (for example,
[
10
]
) to obtain better cutting-planes or improved dual bounds. In this paper, we extend the study of aggregation
[
22
,
4
,
14
,
8
]
in the context of quadratic constraints. While sets defined by linear inequalities are always convex, sets defined by quadratic inequalities are usually not, and we address the question of when the convex hull can be found via aggregation.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set defined by finitely many quadratic constraints. Since we are interested in finding the convex hull of
S
, it makes sense to consider only “good aggregations”, which have at most one negative eigenvalue, so that the set defined by the aggregated constraint has at most two connected components that are both convex, and furthermore contains the convex hull in one of its connected components. It is known that the convex hull of
S
is always described by intersection of these “good aggregations” in the case where
S
is defined by two quadratic constraints
[
22
]
. In fact, the convex hull of a set defined by two quadratic inequalities can be obtained as the intersection of two good aggregations. Henceforth, for simplicity, if it is clear from context we will drop the term “good” and refer to good aggregation as aggregation.

The paper
[
8
]
extended the result of
[
22
]
to the case of a set
S
defined using three quadratic constraints, by showing that under an additional condition called
positive definite linear combination
(PDLC), the convex hull of
S
is obtained as the intersection of good aggregations. They also show via examples that if PDLC does not hold, then the convex hull of
S
may not be given by good aggregations.

A key ingredient of the result in
[
22
]
is the S-lemma
[
15
]
. The main use of the PDLC condition in
[
8
]
is also to prove a version of the homogeneous S-lemma for three quadratics under PDLC. The result is an application of Calabi’s convexity theorem
[
6
,
16
]
which states that under PDLC the image of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by three quadratic forms is a closed convex cone in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Convexity of the image of quadratic maps is a classical mathematical problem in convex geometry and real algebraic geometry, dating back to Dines’ theorem
[
9
]
and Brickman’s theorem
[
3
]
. Yakubovich’s S-lemma (also called S-procedure)
[
20
]
connects this problem to the realm of polynomial optimization. Since then the notion of hidden convexity has become a powerful tool to study quadratic programming
[
16
,
11
]
. See
[
19
]
for a mathematical treatment of convexity of quadratic maps and
[
15
]
for a survey of the S-lemma.

The main goals of this paper are three-fold:

•

General sufficient conditions for aggregations to yield convex hull: We establish a new sufficient condition for aggregations to define the convex hull, which we call
hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC).
To the best of our knowledge, hidden hyperplane convexity gives the most general result on convex hull of a region defined by quadratic inequalities being given by aggregations. In particular, we simultaneously generalize the results of
[
22
]
and
[
8
]
, which deal with two and three quadratic inequalities respectively. Furthermore, we give new examples of sets described by more than
3
quadratic inequalities where convex hull is given by aggregations. We show that hidden hyperplane convexity is a stronger requirement than hidden convexity, and in order for the convex hull of a set defined by quadratic inequalities to be given by aggregations, hidden convexity is not sufficient while HHC is not necessary.

•

Finiteness of aggregations: While
[
22
]
shows that only two good aggregations suffice to define the convex hull in the case of sets described by two quadratic inequalities, the paper
[
8
]
only shows that the intersection of good aggregations yields the convex hull –leaving the question of whether only a finite number of good aggregations are sufficient to obtain the convex hull for three quadratic constraints satisfying PDLC, as an open problem. We answer this question in the affirmative in this paper, and we show that six aggregations suffice to describe the convex hull for three quadratic constraints satisfying PDLC. Furthermore, we establish a more general sufficient condition for finiteness of aggregations in Theorem
2.18
.

•

Closed quadratic inequalities: All of the above results are for the case of open quadratic inequalities, whereas typically in mathematical programming we are interested in sets defined by closed quadratic inequalities. The situation with closed inequalities is much more delicate, as we illustrate in Example
2.23
. Much of the difficulty comes from the fact that sets defined by closed inequalities can have low-dimensional connected components. Previously known results make topological assumptions to avoid this situation. In particular, it was shown in
[
14
]
that if the set defined by closed inequalities has no lower-dimensional connected components, then the closures of convex hulls of sets defined by closed or open inequalities are the same; this allows us to transfer results from the open case to the closed case, under a topological assumption. Unfortunately, this assumption is hard to check computationally, and it does not hold in some interesting cases. We show that if hidden hyperplane convexity holds, and the zero matrix is not a non-trivial aggregation, then the interior of the convex hull of the set given by closed quadratic inequalities is equal to the interior of the intersection of aggregations (Theorem
2.24
). While these are restrictive conditions, they do not make topological assumptions on the set defined by closed inequalities.

2.2.
Hidden hyperplane convexity

We call a map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
a quadratic map, if there exist
m
symmetric matrices
Q_{1},\dots Q_{m}
such that:

\varphi(x)=\left(x^{\top}Q_{1}x,\dots,x^{\top}Q_{m}x\right)\ \textup{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A quadratic map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies
hidden convexity
if
\textup{image}\left(\varphi\right)=\{\varphi(x):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is convex. We say that
n\times n
symmetric matrices
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy hidden convexity if the map
x\mapsto(x^{\top}Q_{1}x,\ldots,x^{\top}Q_{m}x)
satisfies hidden convexity.

We now introduce a new notion of
hidden hyperplane convexity
of quadratic maps, which will be our key assumption in proving that the convex hull of a set defined by quadratic inequalities is given by aggregations of these inequalities. We say
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear hyperplane if
H
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\textup{dim}(H)=n-1
.

Definition 2.1

(Hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC))
.

A quadratic map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC) if for all linear hyperplanes
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\textup{image}\left(\varphi|_{H}\right)=\{\varphi(x):x\in H\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a convex set. Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
be
n\times n
symmetric matrices. We say
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy HHC if the map
x\mapsto(x^{\top}Q_{1}x,\ldots,x^{\top}Q_{m}x)
satisfies HHC.

We present some properties of hidden hyperplane convexity.

Remark 2.2
.

If the matrices
Q_{i}
are linearly independent, then we must have
n\geq m
for hidden convexity, and
n-1\geq m
for hidden hyperplane convexity. For hidden convexity, suppose
n<m
, and consider the span of the image of the quadratic map
\varphi
. Since the image is convex, the span has the same dimension as the image, and it is at most
n
. This means for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have there exist
\lambda_{i}
, not all zero, such that
\sum\lambda_{i}x^{\top}Q_{i}x=\sum x^{\top}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}x=0
, and therefore
Q_{i}
are linearly dependent. Contradiction.

For hidden hyperplane convexity, suppose
n-1<m
, and consider the span of the image of
\varphi
restricted to a hyperplane. For a general hyperplane
H
the restrictions of
Q_{i}
to
H
will be linearly independent and then the argument is same as for hyperplane convexity.

More generally, we must have
\dim(\operatorname{span}\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}\})\leq n
(resp.
n-1
) for hidden convexity (resp. hyperplane hidden convexity) to hold. The proofs are the same as the ones outlined above.

We now observe that hidden hyperplane convexity implies hidden convexity.

Observation 2.3
.

Hidden hyperplane convexity implies the usual hidden convexity as long as
n\geq 3
. Given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we may pick some hyperplane
H
containing both
x
and
y
, and the segment between
\varphi(x)
and
\varphi(y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is then contained in
\textup{image}(\varphi|_{H})\subseteq\textup{image }\varphi
.

On the other hand, HHC is a strictly stronger condition than hidden convexity as the next example illustrates.

Example 2.4

(Hidden convexity does not imply hidden hyperplane convexity)
.

Consider a diagonal quadratic map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\varphi(x)=\left(x^{\top}D_{1}x,\dots,x^{\top}D_{m}x\right),

where
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
are diagonal matrices; such a map is also sometimes referred to as a separable quadratic map. Any diagonal quadratic map
\varphi
is known to satisfy hidden convexity (see Proposition 3.7 in
[
16
]
), and we include a quick proof here. Given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let

be defined as

z_{j}=\sqrt{\lambda x_{j}^{2}+(1-\lambda)y_{j}^{2}},\ j\in[n].

Then it is straightforward to verify that

\lambda\varphi(x)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(y)=\varphi(z),

that is,
\textup{image}\left(\varphi\right)
is convex.

On the other hand, we show that a diagonal quadratic map may not satisfy hidden hyperplane convexity:
Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be given by
f_{1}=x_{1}^{2}
,
f_{2}=x_{2}^{2}
and
f_{3}=x_{3}^{2}
. The image of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is the non-negative orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Now let’s consider restrictions of
\varphi
to a
linear
hyperplane
H
.
It is clear that in this specific example,
\{\varphi(x):x\in H\}=\{\varphi(x):x\in\pi(H)\}
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\pi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)
is the linear projection that forgets the last coordinate.
If
H
does not contain the vector
(0,0,0,1)
,
then
\pi(H)=\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\{\varphi(x):x\in H\}
is the non-negative orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and hidden hyperplane convexity on

holds. If
H
does contain
(0,0,0,1)
, then
the image of
H
under

may not be convex. For instance, let
H=\textup{span}\{(1,1,0,0),(1,0,1,0),(0,0,0,1)\}=\{(s+t,s,t,u)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{4}:s,t,u\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then
A=\textup{image }\varphi|_{H}=\{((s+t)^{2},s^{2},t^{2})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}:s,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is not convex, since
(4,1,1),(0,1,1)\in A
but
(2,1,1)\notin A
.
Thus we see that
\varphi
satisfies hidden convexity, but does not satisfy hidden hyperplane convexity.
This example is interesting in the sense that for a dense subset of linear hyperplanes, the image of this quadratic map restricted to these hyperplanes is convex, and yet this convexity does not hold for all linear hyperplanes.

We now show that hidden hyperplane convexity is preserved under the following two different operations.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy HHC. Then the following matrices also satisfy HHC:

(1)

P^{\top}Q_{1}P,\ldots,P^{\top}Q_{m}P
where
P
is any invertible matrix.

(2)

Q^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{k}
where
\textup{span}(Q^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{k})\subseteq\textup{span}(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m})
. (Equivalently, there exists a
k\times m
matrix
\Lambda
such that
Q^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Lambda_{ij}Q_{j}
for all
i\in[k]
.)

Proof.

Let
H
be any hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For the first statement, we have

U:=\{(x^{\top}P^{\top}Q_{1}Px,\ldots,x^{\top}P^{\top}Q_{m}Px):x\in H\}=\\
\{(x^{\top}Q_{1}x,\ldots,x^{\top}Q_{m}x):x\in H^{\prime}\},

where
H^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{Px:x\in H\}=PH}
is also a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, by HHC of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
the set
U
is also convex.

For the second statement, since
\textup{span}(Q^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{k})\subseteq\textup{span}(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m})
, there exists a
k\times m
matrix
\Lambda
such that
Q^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Lambda_{ij}Q_{j}
for all
i\in[k]
. Then we have
\{(x^{\top}Q^{\prime}_{1}x,\ldots,x^{\top}Q^{\prime}_{k}x):x\in H\}=\Lambda\{(x^{\top}Q_{1}x,\ldots,x^{\top}Q_{m}x):x\in H\}
, which is convex since convexity is preserved under linear transformations.
∎

The above result is important, especially (2), since it shows that hidden
hyperplane
convexity is a property of linear subspaces of the space of symmetric matrices rather than a property that holds for some arbitrary subset of quadratic maps.

Our next observation is that hidden hyperplane convexity can be formulated with matrices. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a quadratic map. Let

\displaystyle\textup{image}(\varphi|_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(x^{\top}Q_{1}x,\dots,x^{\top}Q_{m}x{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}:x\in H\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(y^{\top}W_{H}^{\top}Q_{1}W_{H}y,\dots,y^{\top}W_{H}^{\top}Q_{m}W_{H}y):y\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{image}(\varphi(H)),

where
\varphi(H):{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}}

. On the other hand, the columns of any full rank

Observation 2.6
.

Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy HHC if and only if for all full-rank matrix

,

W^{\top}Q_{1}W,\ldots,W^{\top}Q_{m}W
satisfy hidden convexity.

Corollary 2.7

(
m=2
, or

.

(1)

Let

be symmetric matrices of dimension

. Then

(2)

Let

Proof.

(1)

By Observation

.

(2)

∎

.

. Then

\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega}S_{\lambda}.

Our proof follows the same road-map as the proof in

for the case of three quadratics satisfying PDLC. One of the main ingredients of their proof is the homogeneous S-lemma, and we prove a similar result using the hidden hyperplane convexity assumption. See Section

for a proof of Theorem
2.9

Corollary

states that HHC is always satisfied if

or
m=3

together with Corollary

recovers the main results of

and

. In fact, by using Lemma

it is straightforward to see that we can obtain the following slightly more general result than presented in

Theorem 2.10
.

Suppose that
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}
satisfy the following:

•

There exists two indices

such that

belong to the span of

, or,

•

and

satisfy PDLC.

If
\emptyset\subsetneq\textup{conv}(S)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\textup{conv}(S)
is given by aggregations, i.e.,

We next evaluate the “tightness” of Theorem
2.9
vis-á-vis the hidden hyperplane convexity condition. First via the following example, we show that the weaker condition of hidden convexity is not sufficient for convex hull to be given by aggregations.

Example 2.11

(Hidden convexity is not sufficient)
.

This example is in part inspired by Example 3.4 in
[
16
]
. Let
n\geq 3
and consider the following three quadratic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

f_{1}(x)=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2},\,\,\,f_{2}(x)=x_{1}x_{2},\,\,\,f_{3}(x)=-1-(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})-x_{1}x_{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n}x_{i}^{2}.

It is straightforward to verify that
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
satisfy hidden convexity, as the image of the associated homogeneous quadratic map is
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Observe that PDLC does not hold as the coefficients of
x_{1}^{2}
and
x_{2}^{2}
either have different signs, or are both zero in any linear combination of
f_{i}
.

We now show that any good aggregation must be a scalar multiple of
f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}
, and the set defined by all good aggregations is
\{x:\sum_{i=3}^{n}x_{i}^{2}<1\}
, which has no restrictions on
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. Observe that
(-0.1,0.9,0,\ldots,0),(0.5,-0.7,0,\ldots,0)\in S
and hence their midpoint
x^{*}=(0.2,0.1,0,\ldots,0)
lies in
\textup{conv}(S)
. Let
\lambda\geq 0
be any good aggregation, i.e.,
Q_{\lambda}
has at most one negative eigenvalue and
\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq S_{\lambda}
. In particular
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(x^{*})<0
as
x^{*}\in\textup{conv}(S)
. Since
f_{1}(x^{*})>0,f_{2}(x^{*})>0,f_{3}(x^{*})<0
we must have
\lambda_{3}>0
,
which means the bottom right diagonal element of
Q_{\lambda}
is negative.
Since
Q_{\lambda}
has at most one negative eigenvalue
and the last row and column are always zero except for the diagonal element (there are no linear terms), the leading
n\times n
principal submatrix of
Q_{\lambda}
must be PSD, which means

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
.

On the other hand, the actual convex hull is given by
\{x:x_{2}^{2}<1-\sum_{i=3}^{n}x_{i}^{2},(2x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}<1-\sum_{i=3}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\}
. Geometrically for any fixed
x_{3},\ldots,x_{n}
such that
\sum_{i=3}^{n}x_{i}^{2}<1
, the set of feasible
(x_{1},x_{2})
lies inside an open parallelogram, with vertices
(-a,a),(0,a),(a,-a),(0,-a)
where
a=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i=3}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}
.

We next ask whether hidden hyperplane convexity is a necessary condition for obtaining the convex hull of a set defined by quadratic inequalities using aggregations. As shown in Example
2.4
diagonal quadratic functions may not satisfy HHC. However, in the next result we show that if
S
is defined by diagonal quadratic inequalities then
\textup{conv}(S)
is always given by aggregations. See
[
5
]
for a study of semidefinite relaxations of related sets.

Theorem 2.12
.

(HHC not necessary; Separable quadratic maps) Let
n\geq 2
and
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the functions
f_{i}(x)=x^{\top}A_{i}x+2b_{i}^{\top}x+c_{i},i\in[m]
. Let
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{i}(x)<0,i\in[m]\}
. Assume
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}
are diagonal. Then:

(1)

S=\emptyset
if and only if there exists nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}\succeq 0
.

(2)

If
S\neq\emptyset
, then
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if there exists
\lambda\geq 0
such that the leading
n\times n
principal submatrix of
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
is nonzero and positive semidefinite, i.e. there exists
\lambda\geq 0
such that the set
\{x:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(x)<0\}
is convex
and not
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(3)

If
\emptyset\subsetneq\textup{conv}(S)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
conv(S)
is described by finitely many aggregations, where the leading
n\times n
principal submatrix of each aggregation is positive semidefinite.

A proof of Theorem
2.12
is provided in Section
8
.

In Theorem
2.9
we assume
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and it is natural to ask what happens if either assumption fails. We show that non-emptiness of
S
can be certified using aggregations under the weaker assumption of hidden convexity (without requiring HHC). The situation for
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is more nuanced, and aggregation certificates suffice except for one case.

Proposition 2.13

(Hidden convexity certifies non-emptiness of
S
)
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
be quadratic functions where the image of the associated homogeneous quadratic map
f^{h}=(f^{h}_{1},\ldots,f^{h}_{m}):\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is convex. Then
S=\emptyset
if and only if
Q_{\lambda}\succeq 0
for some nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

A proof of Proposition
2.13
is given in Section
6
.

Note that if there exists
a nonzero

\lambda\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
and furthermore
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(x)
is not a negative constant
function
, then
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We now show the partial converse that if no nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
then
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
if we assume hidden convexity on a particular hyperplane. The unresolved case is where
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(x)
is a negative constant for all nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{(x,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{n+1}=0\}
and assume
\textup{image }f^{h}|_{\mathcal{E}}
is convex. Assume there does not exist nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
. Then
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

It is clear that this assumption is weaker than hidden hyperplane convexity, which requires
\textup{image }f^{h}|_{H}
to be convex for any hyperplane
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. A proof of Proposition
2.14
is presented in Section
6
.

2.4.
Convex hull of sets defined by linear and sphere constraints

In Theorem
2.8
we prove hidden hyperplane convexity of a special class of quadratic maps. This results leads to the following theorem on sets defined by linear
and sphere inequalities.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
f_{i}(x)=x^{\top}A_{i}x+2b_{i}^{\top}x+c_{i},1\leq i\leq m
be quadratic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
A_{i}
is either
I_{n}
for
i\in P\subseteq[m]
,
-I_{n}
for
i\in N\subseteq[m]
or
0
for
i\in Z\subseteq[m]
. Let
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{i}(x)<0,i\in[m]\}
.

Then:

•

S=\emptyset
if and only there exists some nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
such that
Q_{\lambda}\succeq 0
(which can be checked using an SDP.)

•

\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if either
P\neq\emptyset
or there exists
i\in Z
such that
b_{i}\neq 0
or
b_{i}=0
and
c_{i}\geq 0
.

•

If
\emptyset\subsetneq\textup{conv}(S)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and either
m\leq n-1
or
m\leq n
and PDLC condition holds, then
\textup{conv}(S)
can be described by at most
|P||N|+|P|+|Z|
aggregations.

A proof of Theorem
2.15
is presented in Section
7
. Here is an example where
m=n=3
and PDLC holds.

Example 2.16
.

Consider the following three quadratics in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

f_{1}(x)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-2x_{3}-1,\,\,f_{2}(x)=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+2x_{3}-4,\,\,f_{3}(x)=-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}+1

PDLC is satisfied with
\theta=(-1,-1,-3)
, and in this case
|P|=2,|N|=1,|Z|=0
. Thus by Theorem
2.15
the convex hull is given by at most four aggregations. In fact, three aggregations suffice for this example. A plot of this region is given in Figure

.

Note each
f_{i}
defines a region that is either a ball if
A_{i}=I
, a linear halfspace if
A_{i}=0
, or the complement of a ball if
A_{i}=-I
. Thus Theorem
2.15
applies to sets defined by linear and sphere inequalities. Such sets appear in the context of trust region subproblems, and have been studied in papers such as
[

. We make no assumptions on the constraints, as opposed to
[

]
which requires the outside-the-ball constraints (
A=-I
) to be non-intersecting. On the other hand we only study
\textup{conv}(S)
instead of the convex hull in lifted space
\{(x,xx^{\top}):x\in S\}
or algorithms to solve the trust region problem as in
[

]
. If
|P|+|N|=1
, then the set in Theorem

is related to the set studied in
[

.
We also note that in this case every good aggregation is in fact convex, i.e., each good aggregation defines a single convex set, and the set of good aggregations
\Omega
is polyhedral. Therefore the results in
[

apply to this case.

The following example shows that convex hull may not be given by aggregations when there are
n+1
(linearly independent) linear and sphere constraints and PDLC does not hold. We do not know whether an example with
n
linearly independent constraints (without PDLC) exists.

Example 2.17
.

Let

f_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptf_{i}=-x_{i},i\in[n].

We claim that
\textup{conv}(S)=\{x>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}>1\}
. It is clear
\varepsilon\bar{1}+\alpha e_{i}\in S
for all
\varepsilon>0,\alpha>1,i\in[n]
, and therefore
\{x>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x>1\}\subseteq\textup{conv}(S)
. To show
\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq\{x>0:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x>1\}
, it suffices to show
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}>1
holds for all
x\in S
. Suppose this does not hold, then there exists
y>0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\leq 1
, then
0\leq y_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\leq 1
, which means
f_{0}(y)\geq 0
and
y\notin S
.

We now show that the set defined by all good aggregations is the positive orthant, which is different from the actual convex hull. Let
\lambda\geq 0
be any good aggregation. Since
Q_{\lambda}
has at most one negative eigenvalue, we must have
\lambda_{0}=0
. Thus all good aggregations are nonnegative linear combinations of
f_{i}
.

2.5.
Together hidden hyperplane convexity and PDLC for every triple lead to finite number of good aggregations defining the convex hull

In the case where convex hull can be described by aggregations, a natural question is whether finitely many aggregations suffice. When
m=2
, this is already shown to be true
[
22
]
. Note that we also verified this for the special cases as stated in Theorem
2.15
and Theorem
2.12
.

This question was raised in
[
8
]
for three quadratics under PDLC condition. Here we give an affirmative answer for three quadratics, and consider the question in the more general setting of
m
quadratics under hidden hyperplane convexity assumption, where every triple of quadratics satisfies PDLC condition.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the functions
f_{i}(x)=x^{\top}A_{i}x+2b_{i}^{\top}x+c_{i},i\in[m]
. Let
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{i}(x)<0,i\in[m]\}
. Assume
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and HHC holds for the associated homogeneous quadratic map
f^{h}
, so that Theorem
2.9
holds and

\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{1}}S_{\lambda},

where
\Omega_{1}=\Omega\setminus\{\lambda\in\Omega:S_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
.
Furthermore assume for all distinct
i,j,k\in[m]
there exist scalars
p_{ijk},q_{ijk},r_{ijk}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{ijk}Q_{i}+q_{ijk}Q_{j}+r_{ijk}Q_{k}\succ 0
. Then there exist
\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(r)}\in\Omega_{2}
such that

\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}S_{\lambda^{(i)}},

where
\Omega_{2}=\{\lambda\in\Omega_{1}:|\{i:\lambda_{i}>0\}|\leq 2\}
and
r\leq m^{2}-m
.

Moreover, given any
u,v\in[m]
,
u\neq v
, there are at most two
\lambda^{(i)}
s with support
u
,
v
. Furthermore, these
\lambda^{(i)}
s can be written as
\alpha^{\prime}e_{u}+(1-\alpha^{\prime})e_{v},\alpha^{\prime\prime}e_{u}+(1-\alpha^{\prime\prime})e_{v}
, where
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}
are roots of
\det(\alpha Q_{u}+(1-\alpha)Q_{v})=0
.

A proof of Theorem
2.18
is presented in Section
9
.
The key ideas to prove Theorem
2.18
are the following:

•

Given an aggregation
S_{\lambda}
, one can obtain an improved aggregation
S_{\tilde{\lambda}}
, i.e.
S_{\tilde{\lambda}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
, such that
\tilde{\lambda}\in\Omega_{1}
. This is obtained as
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda+\theta
where
Q_{\theta}\succeq 0
. (Proposition
9.1
)

•

The idea is to repeatedly improve along such positive definite linear combinations so as to reduce the support of aggregations that are required to obtain the convex hull to at most
2
. (Proposition
9.2
)

•

Now among aggregations that have support of fixed two indices, say
i
and
j
, it is shown that at most two aggregations are sufficient. (Proposition
9.6
)

Remark 2.19
.

As discussed in
[
7
]
, the closure of each component defined by a good aggregation is second-order cone representable (SOCr). Thus finiteness of good aggregations implies that the closure of convex hull is SOCr, since it is given by intersection of finitely many components which are all SOCr.

For the case of
m=3
, note that PDLC implies hidden hyperplane convexity, so PDLC is sufficient to guarantee that no more than
3^{2}-3=6
aggregations are sufficient to obtain the convex hull, answering a question raised in
[
8
]
.

Corollary 2.20
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
be three quadratic functions such that there exist
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\theta_{i}Q_{i}\succ 0
. Suppose
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then there exists
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega,|\Omega|\leq 6
such that
\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega^{\prime}}S_{\lambda}
.

The following example for
m=3
case requires
4
aggregations to describe the convex hull.

Example 2.21
.

Consider the set
S
described by the following functions:

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=-x_{1}^{2}+1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}+5x_{1}-4+\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=-x_{1}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

, corresponding to a positive definite matrix. Thus PDLC holds and therefore
\textup{conv}(S)
is given by at most 6 aggregations. In fact,

\textup{conv}(S)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{1}(x)<0,f_{2}(x)<0,f_{1}(x)+f_{3}(x)<0,f_{2}(x)+f_{3}(x)<0\}.

The bound of
m^{2}-m
can be improved in the special case where the quadratics
Q_{i}
that defined

span a linear space of dimension at most three. We have already shown in Theorem
2.10
that PDLC (or less, when the dimension of the span of the associated quadratic map is
2
)
is sufficient for the convex hull to be given by aggregations. We separate our discussion into two cases based on the dimension of the span of
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}
.

Span of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
is two dimensional

Consider the conical hull of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
, which is a closed polyhedral cone of dimension two. If this cone is a linear subspace or a linear halfspace, i.e., there exists nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
where
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}=0
, then
S=\emptyset
. If
S\neq\emptyset
, then the conical hull of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
is a closed pointed polyhedral cone of dimension two, which has exactly two extreme rays. Say the two extreme rays are generated by
Q_{i},Q_{j}
respectively, then all other
Q_{k}
can be written as nonnegative combinations of
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
. In this case
\textup{conv}(S)=\textup{conv}(S_{i}\cap S_{j})
, reducing to the two quadratics case which is described in Theorem 1 of
[
22
]
.

Span of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
is three dimensional

We make the following observations: if
S\neq\emptyset
, then the conical hull of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}
, must be a pointed closed polyhedral cone of dimension three, and we may assume without loss of generality that every
Q_{i}
spans an extreme ray of
\mathcal{C}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
has exactly
m
facets, and each facet is generated by exactly two
Q_{i}
’s. Given an aggregation that lies in the intersection of
\Omega_{1}
and interior of
\mathcal{C}
, by Proposition
9.1
, we may improve it using a positive definite combination until it touches the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
. Proposition
9.6
implies that intersection of all aggregations in
\Omega
on a facet can be described by two endpoints. Since there are exactly
m
facets, it follows that
2m
aggregations suffice to describe the convex hull for the case of dependent quadratics. Therefore we obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
be quadratic functions such that span of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
is three dimensional, and there exists
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}Q_{i}\succ 0
. Suppose
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then there exists
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega,|\Omega|\leq 2m
such that
\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega^{\prime}}S_{\lambda}
.

2.6.
Closed Inequalities

Given quadratic functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
, let
S=\{x:f_{i}(x)<0,i\in[m]\}
be the set defined by open inequalities, and let
T=\{x:f_{i}(x)\leq 0,i\in[m]\}
be the one with closed inequalities. As usual let
Q_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&b_{i}\\
b_{i}^{\top}&c_{i}\end{bmatrix}
. Note
\textup{conv}(S)
is always open but
\textup{conv}(T)
may not be closed. In this section we study
G
, the interior of
\overline{\textup{conv}(T)}
. It is clear
G
is convex, open and
\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq G
.

In
[
8
]
it was shown that when
G=\textup{conv}(S)
and
\textup{conv}(S)
is given by aggregations, then
\overline{\textup{conv}(T)}
is given by the same aggregations after changing all open inequalities to closed. The original proof is only for the case of three quadratics with PDLC, but the same proof works for arbitrary number of quadratics with hidden hyperplane convexity.

We do not make the assumption that
G=\textup{conv}(S)
, and show that

G
is still given by aggregations of open inequalities, under
HHC and
an additional technical assumption. We now give an example where
G\not=\textup{conv}(S)
, which illustrates the delicate nature of closed inequalities.

Example 2.23

(
G\not=\textup{conv}(S)
)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and consider the following two quadratic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=-x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

Then
\textup{conv}(S)=S=\{x:f_{1}(x)<0,f_{2}(x)<0\}=\{x:x_{1}<0,\|x\|_{2}<1\}
. Note
T=\overline{S}\cup\{e_{1}\}
, and
G
strictly contains
\textup{conv}(S)
. It turns out that
G
is also given by aggregations
G=\{x:f_{2}(x)<0,2f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)<0\}
, which is different from the ones defining
\textup{conv}(S)
.

2.23

We now state our main theorem for closed inequalities.

Theorem 2.24
.

Given quadratic functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
, let
T=\{x:f_{i}(x)\leq 0,i\in[m]\}
and
G=\textup{int}(\overline{\textup{conv}(T)})
. Assume
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy hidden hyperplane convexity
\emptyset\subsetneq G\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and furthermore
Q_{\lambda}\neq 0
for all nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
. Then
G=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{T}}S_{\lambda}
, where

and

is the set of

has at most one negative eigenvalue and

The condition that

A proof of Proposition

3.
Conclusions and open questions

We showed that for a set described by any number of quadratics, hidden hyperplane convexity is a sufficient condition for convex hull to be given by good aggregations, assuming
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\neq}\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore Theorem
2.12
and Example
2.11
together show that hidden hyperplane convexity is not necessary while hidden convexity is not sufficient.

We conjecture that even with hidden hyperplane convexity there exist sets
S
defined by quadratic inequalities where infinitely many good aggregations are needed to define the convex hull.

Conjecture 3.1
.

There exists a set
S
described by quadratic inequalities satisfying hidden hyperplane convexity, such that
\textup{conv}(S)
cannot be described by finitely many good aggregations.

In Theorem
2.18
we showed that
m^{2}-m
good aggregations describe the convex hull under hidden hyperplane convexity and every triple PDLC assumption. In particular when
m=3
and PDLC holds, six aggregations suffice, and we gave an example where four good aggregations are needed. We have not discovered an example which requires more than four good aggregations to describe the convex hull, but we conjecture that such examples exist.

Conjecture 3.2
.

There exists a set
S
described by three quadratic inequalities satisfying PDLC, such that
\textup{conv}(S)
is described by using exactly six aggregations.

We showed that aggregations always certify when
S=\emptyset
, and almost always certify when
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, except for one unresolved case, where
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
is a negative constant for all nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
. We conjecture that under hidden hyperplane convexity assumption, in this case
\textup{conv}(S)
is also
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In other words we have the following conjecture, which states aggregations always provide certificates when
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

Conjecture 3.3
.

Let
f_{i}(x)=x^{\top}A_{i}x+2b_{i}^{\top}x+c_{i},i\in[m]
be quadratic functions where the associated homogeneous quadratic map
f^{h}=(f^{h}_{1},\ldots,f^{h}_{m}):\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
has hidden hyperplane convexity. Assume
S=\{x:f_{i}(x)<0,x\in[m]\}
is nonempty. Then
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if there exists nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
is not a negative constant.

4.
HHC for a special class of quadratic forms

Our goal in this section is to prove that a special class of quadratic maps satisfies hidden hyperplane convexity. We first study the image of one particular quadratic map.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
f_{0}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
,
f_{i}(x)=x_{1}x_{i},1\leq i\leq n
be

quadratic forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\{(f_{0}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x)):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}=\{(y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}):y_{0}y_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2},y_{0}\geq 0,y_{1}\geq 0\},

which is linearly isomorphic to the boundary of second-order cone (Lorentz cone) in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, given by
\{(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}):z_{0}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{2},z_{0}\geq 0\}
.

Proof.

“
\subseteq
”: This is clear since
f_{0}(x)f_{1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)^{2}
and
f_{0},f_{1}
are sum of squares.

“
\supseteq
”: Given

satisfying
y_{0}y_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2},y_{0}\geq 0,y_{1}\geq 0
, we construct
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
y_{i}=f_{i}(x)
. We divide this into two cases based on whether

is zero or positive:

•

If

, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}=y_{0}y_{1}=0
implies

for all

. In this case we let
x_{2}=\sqrt{y_{0}}
and

otherwise.

•

If

x_{1}=\sqrt{y_{1}}
and

for all

In both cases one can directly verify

for all

.

To see that the image is linearly isomorphic to boundary of Lorentz cone, let

for all

and with this substitution one can verify that
\{(y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}):y_{0}y_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2},y_{0}\geq 0,y_{1}\geq 0\}=\{(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}):z_{0}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{2},z_{0}\geq 0\}

∎

Proposition
4.1
shows that the image of this quadratic map is the boundary of a full-dimensional closed pointed convex cone. We now show that the image of such a set under any linear map with nontrivial kernel must be convex.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a full-dimensional closed convex set which does not contain lines. Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be any linear mapping with nontrivial kernel. Then
\pi(\partial C)=\pi(C)
, and therefore
\pi(\partial C)
is convex.

Proof.

Let

be a point in the interior of

, as
\pi(x)=\pi(z)
for all

. Let
\ell
be any one-dimensional linear subspace of
\ker\pi

does not contain lines, there exists

that is not contained in

. Thus there exists

on the segment

that is on boundary of

∎

Remark 4.3
.

This statement may not be true when

contains a line. For example let

and

be projection onto the first coordinate. Then

.

\displaystyle\subseteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\lambda^{\top}y\leq\mu\}.

Rest of the proof is exactly the same (other than here we have
\textup{image }f^{h}
instead of
\textup{image }f^{h}|_{H}
) as the step in proof of Theorem

where we show

and
\mu=0
, which means
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f^{h}_{i}(x,x_{n+1})\geq 0
for all
(x,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and

. Namely:

•

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
. Otherwise suppose there exists some

where
\lambda_{j}<0
for contradiction. Then for any

let

, where

is the

standard basis vector. Then

for any

, and
\lambda^{\top}v_{M}=-M\lambda_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i}
. Since
\lambda_{j}<0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i},\mu
is constant, for sufficiently large

, which is contradiction.

•

\mu=0
. Since

∎

We now examine the case
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and prove Proposition

.

Proof of Proposition
2.14
.

Let
g_{i}(x)=x^{\top}A_{i}x,i\in[m]
be the quadratic parts of
f_{i}
. Then
g_{i}
are homogeneous, and we have
\hat{g}_{i}=g_{i}
and
\textup{image }\hat{g}=\textup{image }f^{h}|_{\mathcal{E}}
. Using Proposition
2.13
for

we have
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g_{i}(x)<0,i\in[m]\}=\emptyset
if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
for some nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
.

Thus if such
\lambda
does not exist, then there exists some

where
v^{\top}A_{i}v=g_{i}(v)<0
for all

. We now show that for any fixed

,

for some
M>0
, which then implies

and hence
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We have

\displaystyle f_{i}(x+Mv)

\displaystyle=M^{2}(v^{\top}A_{i}v)+2M(x^{\top}A_{i}v+b_{i}^{\top}v)+f_{i}(x),

\displaystyle f_{i}(x-Mv)

\displaystyle=M^{2}(v^{\top}A_{i}v)-2M(x^{\top}A_{i}v+b_{i}^{\top}v)+f_{i}(x).

Since

for all

, the leading coefficient is negative, and the function values become negative for sufficiently large

. To be more precise, in order for

for all

∎

7.
Application to sets defined by spheres and halfspaces

Using results from Section
4
we now study sets defined by linear and sphere constraints: consider quadratic functions
f_{i}(x)=x^{\top}A_{i}x+2b_{i}^{\top}x+c_{i},\,\,1\leq i\leq m
where each
A_{i}
is either
I,-I
or zero, and let
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{i}(x)<0,1\leq i\leq m\}
. We call such a set

defined by spheres and halfspaces, since each
f_{i}
is either an affine linear function, or it defines the interior or exterior of a sphere. Let
P,Z,N
be the index sets of interior of the sphere constraints, exterior of the sphere constraints and affine linear constraints respectively:
P=\{i\in[m]:A_{i}=I\}
,
Z=\{i\in[m]:A_{i}=0\}
,
N=\{i\in[m]:A_{i}=-I\}
.

In terms of matrices, after taking
\ell(x)=x_{1}
Theorem
2.8
can be restated as follows. Note we may use Lemma
2.5
to change basis for the matrices, corresponding to choosing different linear function
\ell(x)
.

Corollary 7.1
.

Fix integers
n>m+1,m\geq 2
and let
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{m}
be
n\times n
symmetric matrices such that
Q_{0}
is positive definite, and
Q_{i}
has nonzero entries only in first row and column for all
1\leq i\leq m
. Then
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy hidden hyperplane convexity.

We can use Theorem
2.9
to show that the convex hull of a set defined by constraints described above is always given by good aggregations.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
f_{i},i\in[m]
be linear or sphere constraints such that one of the two following conditions holds:

•

\dim\textup{span}(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m})\leq n-1
.

•

\dim\textup{span}(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m})=n
and PDLC condition holds.

Suppose
\emptyset\subsetneq\textup{conv}(S)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
\textup{conv}(S)
is defined by good aggregations, i.e.,
\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega}S_{\lambda}
, where
\Omega=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}\setminus\{0\}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0\}
and
S_{\lambda}=\{x:\sum_{i=1}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(x)<0\}
.

Note that given the special structure of the constraints, the description of
\Omega
can be greatly simplified compared to the general case. This is due to the fact that each
A_{i}
is either
I,-I
or 0, and therefore
Q_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&b_{i}\\
b_{i}^{\top}&c_{i}\end{bmatrix}
has at most one negative eigenvalue if and only if
\lambda_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0
. In this case
S_{\lambda}
is automatically convex. In fact, the set
\Omega\cup\{0\}
has a polyhedral description:
\Omega\cup\{0\}=\{\lambda\geq 0:\sum_{i\in P}\lambda_{i}\geq\sum_{i\in N}\lambda_{i}\}
.

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

When PDLC condition does not hold and there are
m\leq n-1
constraints, we can always add a trivial constraint
f_{0}=-1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
with
Q_{0}=-I
, which does not change
S
. Thus from now on we assume
\dim\textup{span}(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m})\leq n
and PDLC holds. We show that there exist symmetric matrices
Q^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{m-1}
such that
\textup{span}(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m})=\textup{span}(Q^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{m-1})
,
Q^{\prime}_{0}\succ 0
and
A^{\prime}_{i}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq m-1
. Then
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
satisfy HHC by Corollary
7.1
and Lemma
2.5
.

Such
Q^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{m-1}
can be chosen as follows:
Q^{\prime}_{0}
is the linear combination of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
that is positive definite. Since
A_{i}=I,-I
or 0, upon rescaling we may assume
A^{\prime}_{0}=I
. Upon relabeling assume coefficient of
Q_{m}
in the linear combination of
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}
that produces
Q^{\prime}_{0}
is nonzero. Now the
Q^{\prime}_{i}
s (other than
Q^{\prime}_{0}
) can be chosen to be

Q^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}Q_{i}&\textup{ if }A_{i}=0\\
Q_{i}-Q^{\prime}_{0}&\textup{ if }A_{i}=I\\
Q_{i}+Q^{\prime}_{0}&\textup{ if }A_{i}=-I\end{cases},1\leq i\leq m-1

Then clearly
A^{\prime}_{i}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, and each
Q^{\prime}_{i}
is a linear combination of
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
. Conversely, all
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m-1}
are linear combinations of
Q^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{m-1}
, and
Q_{m}
is a linear combination of
Q^{\prime}_{0},Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m-1}
and hence
Q^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{m-1}
.
∎

Recall from Proposition
2.13
that
S=\emptyset
if and only if there exists nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
such that
Q_{\lambda}\succeq 0
, which can be checked using an semidefinite program. In general, we do not have a necessary and sufficient condition for
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the special case of sphere and linear constraints, it is easy to determine whether
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
by checking the types of sphere constraints. Recall
P,Z,N
denote index sets where

respectively.

Lemma 7.3
.

\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if either
P\neq\emptyset
or there exists
i\in Z
such that
b_{i}\neq 0
or
b_{i}=0
and
c_{i}\geq 0
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
We show the contrapositive. Suppose
P=\emptyset
and for all

we have
b_{i}=0
and
c_{i}<0
. Then complement of each
S_{i}
is bounded, and thus complement of

is also bounded, which means
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(\Leftarrow)
If such

∎

7.1.
Finiteness for number of good aggregations

Unlike in the general case where we need further conditions to ensure that the convex hull is given by finitely many good aggregations, for linear and sphere constraints if the convex hull is given by good aggregations, then finitely many will always suffice. The number of aggregations needed will depend on size of
P,Z,N
, which count the number of constraints where
A_{i}=I,0,-I
respectively.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
S
be defined using linear and sphere constraints such that
\textup{conv}(S)
is given by good aggregations. Then
\textup{conv}(S)
can be described by at most
|P||N|+|P|+|Z|
aggregations. Furthermore each good aggregation is either of the form
f_{i}
for some
i\in P\cup Z
or
f_{i}+f_{j}
for some
i\in P,j\in N
.

Proof.

First observe that
f_{i},i\in P\cup Z
and
f_{i}+f_{j},i\in P,j\in N
are good aggregations, as their leading
n\times n
principal submatrices are PSD. Let
f_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
be any good aggregation. We claim that there exist nonnegative coefficients
\{\gamma_{ij},i\in P,j\in N\},\{\alpha_{i},i\in P\},\{\beta_{k},k\in Z\}
such that

(2)

f_{\lambda}=\sum_{i\in P,j\in N}\gamma_{ij}(f_{i}+f_{j})+\sum_{i\in P}\alpha_{i}f_{i}+\sum_{k\in Z}\beta_{k}f_{k}.

The above equation would show that
f_{\lambda}
is an aggregation of
f_{i},i\in P\cup Z
and
f_{i}+f_{j},i\in P,j\in N
. This implies that the constraint
f_{\lambda}<0
is dominated by the intersection of the constraints
f_{i}+f_{j}<0,

(i,j)\in P\times N
,
f_{i},i\in P
, and
f_{k}<0
,
k\in Z
, giving us the upper bound of
|P||N|+|P|+|Z|
.

Now we prove the claim about (
2
). Since
f_{\lambda}
is a good aggregation, we must have
\lambda\geq 0
and
\sum_{i\in P}\lambda_{i}\geq\sum_{j\in N}\lambda_{j}
. If
\sum_{j\in N}\lambda_{j}=0
then
\lambda_{j}=0
for all
j\in N
, and we may choose
\gamma_{ij}=0
for all
i\in P,j\in N
,
\alpha_{i}=\lambda_{i}
for all
i\in P
and
\beta_{k}=\lambda_{k}
for all
k\in Z
.

From now on assume
\sum_{j\in N}\lambda_{j}>0
. For all
i\in P
we let

\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i}\frac{\sum_{j\in N}\lambda_{j}}{\sum_{i\in P}\lambda_{i}},

which satisfies
0\leq\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
for all
i\in P
and
\sum_{i\in P}\mu_{i}=\sum_{j\in N}\lambda_{j}
. Then (
2
) holds true if we let

\gamma_{ij}=\frac{\mu_{i}\lambda_{j}}{\sum_{j\in N}\lambda_{j}},i\in P,j\in N,\quad\alpha_{i}=\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i},i\in P,\quad\beta_{k}=\lambda_{k},k\in Z.

∎

8.
Diagonal Inequalities

For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
x\circ y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the element-wise product
(x_{1}y_{1},...,x_{n}y_{n})
. Given a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we let
\textup{Diag}(u)
be the
k\times k
diagonal matrix with
(i,i)
diagonal entry equal to
u_{i}
.

Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{m}
be diagonal
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrices, and consider the set

S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\begin{bmatrix}x\\
1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}Q_{i}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
1\end{bmatrix}<0,1\leq i\leq m\}.

Our goal is to describe
\textup{conv}(S)
, and it is natural to consider the following “open-polyhedron”: let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be defined such that
Q_{i}=\textup{Diag}(a_{i1},...,a_{in},-b_{i})
, then clearly
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\circ x\in\mathcal{P}\}
where
\mathcal{P}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax<b\}
is a open-polyhedron, and in particular it is open in the topological sense, since it is given by intersection of finitely many open halfspaces. The usual notion of polyhedron given by closed linear inequalities is sometimes referred to as closed polyhedron.

Now we define a new set
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists x,Ax<b,y<x\}
. We first show that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
describe the element-wise squares of the convex hull.

Proposition 8.1
.

\textup{conv}(S)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\circ x\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the all-one vector.
First observe that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
: If
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{P}
, then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\hat{x}+\epsilon\cdot\bar{1}\in\mathcal{P}
, since
\mathcal{P}
is open. Since
\hat{x}<\hat{x}+\epsilon\cdot\bar{1}
, this implies
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Since
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
we have
S\subseteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y\circ y\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}
. Also observe that if
x\in S
, then
u\in S
where
|u_{i}|=|x_{i}|
for all
i\in[n]
. Thus, given
x\in S
, if
|y_{i}|<|x_{i}|
for all
i\in[n]
(or equivalently
y\circ y<x\circ x
), then
y\in\textup{conv}(S)
. This shows that
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y\circ y\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}\subseteq\textup{conv}(S)
, i.e. ,

S\subseteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y\circ y\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}\subseteq\textup{conv}(S).

It remains to show that
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y\circ y\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}
is convex. Let
u,v
be such that
u\circ u,v\circ v\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. This means there exists
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
such that
u\circ u<u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime},v\circ v<v^{\prime}\circ v^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\circ v^{\prime}\in\ P
. Note that
u\circ u<u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime},v\circ v<v^{\prime}\circ v^{\prime}
is equivalent to
|u_{i}|<|u^{\prime}_{i}|,|v_{i}|<|v^{\prime}_{i}|
for all
i\in[n]
.

Fix any
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, and let
w=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v
. We now show
w\circ w\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which then completes the proof. Since
f(t)=t^{2}
is a convex function, we have

\displaystyle(w\circ w)_{i}

\displaystyle=(\lambda u_{i}+(1-\lambda)v_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda u_{i}^{2}+(1-\lambda)v_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle<\lambda(u^{\prime}_{i})^{2}+(1-\lambda)(v^{\prime}_{i})^{2},

which shows
w\circ w<\lambda(u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime})+(1-\lambda)(v^{\prime}\circ v^{\prime})
. Since
u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\circ v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}
is convex, we have
\lambda(u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime})+(1-\lambda)(v^{\prime}\circ v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}
which shows
w\circ w\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
∎

The following observation about
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

is
useful in further analysis.

Observation 8.2
.

\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are always open. If
\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset
then its closure is given by

\overline{\mathcal{P}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax\leq b\}
, in which case
\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists x,Ax\leq b,y\leq x\}
is a closed polyhedron given by
\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Gx\leq h\}
for some
G\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n},h\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
k
is some positive integer. Then
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\text{int}(\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Gx<h\}
is an open-polyhedron.

Proof.

\mathcal{P}
is clearly open since it is intersection of finitely many open halfspaces.
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is open since it is the projection of an open set.

From now on assume
\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset
. Clearly
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax\leq b\}
is closed and contains
\mathcal{P}
and hence
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
. For the reverse inclusion, Let
\hat{x}
satisfies
A\hat{x}\leq b
and fix arbitrary
u\in\mathcal{P}
. Then note that for
\lambda\in(0,1)
we have
\lambda\hat{x}+(1-\lambda)u\in\mathcal{P}
. By selecting
\lambda
arbitrarily close to
1
, we can obtain a point arbitrarily close to
\hat{x}
, which means it is a limit point of
\mathcal{P}
and hence belongs to its closure.

We now study closure of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Clearly
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists x,Ax\leq b,y\leq x\}
contains
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and is a closed polyhedron since it is the projection of a closed polyhedron
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:Ax\leq b,y\leq x\}
onto the

coordinates. To show
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists x,Ax\leq b,y\leq x\}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
, take any
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
A\hat{x}\leq b,\hat{y}\leq\hat{x}
. Since
\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset
there exists
u\in\mathcal{P}
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is open there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

, which satisfies

. Therefore for all
0<\lambda<1
we have

A(\lambda\hat{x}+(1-\lambda)v)=\lambda A\hat{x}+(1-\lambda)Av<b,\lambda\hat{y}+(1-\lambda)u<\lambda\hat{x}+(1-\lambda)v.

Therefore
\lambda\hat{y}+(1-\lambda)u\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Letting
\lambda\to 1
we get
\hat{y}\in\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
.

Now
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset
is open, and its closure
\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
, say
\{x:Gx\leq h\}
, is a closed polyhedron with nonempty interior. First half of observation states that closure of
\{x:Gx<h\}
is
\{x:Gx\leq h\}
if

. Furthermore
\{x:Gx<h\}
cannot be empty otherwise
\{x:Gx\leq h\}
is not full-dimensional. Since both
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\{x:Gx<h\}
are (topologically) open with same closure, they must coincide.
∎

Now we state the necessary and sufficient condition for
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. Geometrically, the condition states that the recession cone of
\mathcal{P}
, same as the recession cone of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
, contains a strictly positive vector.

Proposition 8.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\neq\emptyset
. Then
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
if and only if
Ax\leq 0,x>0
is feasible, or equivalently there does not
exist

u\geq 0
such that
u^{\top}A\geq 0
and is nonzero.

Proof.

Observe
Ax\leq 0,x>0
is infeasible if and only if

is infeasible. By Farkas lemma, this happens if and only if there exist
u,v\geq 0
such that
u^{\top}A-v^{\top}=0,-v^{\top}\bar{1}<0
, or equivalently there exists
u\geq 0
such that
u^{\top}A\geq 0
and is nonzero.

(\Rightarrow)
We show the contrapositive and assume there exists
u\geq 0
such that
u^{\top}A\geq 0
and is nonzero. Fix index

where
i^{th}
coordinate of
u^{\top}A
is strictly positive. Then for sufficiently large
\lambda

\lambda u^{\top}Ae_{i}>u^{\top}b
. We now claim
\lambda e_{i}\notin\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
which completes the proof of this direction. By definition of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
it suffices to show for any

we have

. Suppose for contradiction that
w\in\mathcal{P}
for some

. Then we would have

since

, and

since

. But then we would also have

since

and
u^{\top}A\geq 0
, contradicting
\lambda u^{\top}Ae_{i}>u^{\top}b
.

(\Leftarrow)
Choose
w\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
v>0
such that
Av\leq 0
. Then for all
\lambda>0
,
A(w+\lambda v)<0
, i.e.,

. Observe for any

we have

for sufficiently large

, which shows

.
∎

Observation
8.2
states that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is an open polyhedron when
\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset
. The following proposition states that when
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\neq\emptyset
, then the defining inequalities of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are precisely aggregations of defining inequalities of
\mathcal{P}
, where all coefficients in the aggregation are nonnegative.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let

\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\neq\emptyset
. Then
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{y:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\alpha^{(i)})^{\top}}y<\beta^{i},i\in[k]\}
for some integer
k
and

•

\alpha^{(i)}\geq 0
and
b^{(i)}\geq 0
for all
i\in[k]
.

•

There exists
\lambda^{(i)}\geq 0
, such that
(\alpha^{(i)})^{\top}=(\lambda^{(i)})^{\top}A
and
\beta^{(i)}=(\lambda^{(i)})^{\top}b
.

Proof.

Consider the lifted open-polyhedron
Q
and its closure given by Observation
8.2
:

Q:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:Ax<b,y<x\},\overline{Q}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:Ax\leq b,y\leq x\}.

Observation
8.2
states that
\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists x:Ax\leq b,y\leq x\}
is the projection of
\overline{Q}
onto the
y
coordinates, which means
\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
has description
\overline{P^{\prime}}=\{y:(\alpha^{(i))^{\top}}y\leq\beta^{i},i\in[k]\}
for some integer
k
, where each inequality is facet defining. Observation
8.2
also shows that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is given by the corresponding open inequalities
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{y:(\alpha^{(i))^{\top}}y<\beta^{i},i\in[k]\}
.

Now it remains to show these inequalities have the desired form. Consider a facet defining inequality
\alpha^{\top}x\leq\beta
for
\overline{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}=\textup{proj}_{y}(\overline{Q})
. We can consider the following associated LP and its dual:

max

\displaystyle\alpha^{\top}y

s.t.

\displaystyle Ax\leq b

\displaystyle y-x\leq 0.

min

\displaystyle\lambda^{\top}b

s.t.

\displaystyle\lambda^{\top}A-\mu^{\top}I=0

\displaystyle\mu^{\top}I=\alpha^{\top}

\displaystyle\lambda\geq 0,\mu\geq 0.

Note that since the primal is feasible and bounded, this implies the dual is feasible and bounded. In particular, let
(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
be the optimal dual solution. Then, from the second constraint of the dual
\alpha=\mu^{*}\geq 0
. Since
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\neq\emptyset
, we have that
\beta\geq 0
.

Also note the first constraint of the dual now shows that
(\lambda^{*})^{\top}A=\mu^{*}=\alpha
and the objective of the dual optimal solution shows that
(\lambda^{*})^{\top}b=\beta
.
∎

Now we are ready to describe the convex hull when all quadratics are diagonal.

Proof of Theorem
2.12
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be defined such that
Q_{i}=\textup{diag}(a_{i1},...,a_{in},-b_{i})
. In this definition
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\circ x\in\mathcal{P}\}
where
\mathcal{P}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax<b\}
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists x\in\mathcal{P},y<x\}
. As shown in Proposition
8.1
,
\textup{conv}(S)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\circ x\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\}
. We have three cases

•

S=\emptyset
. Since
x\circ x\geq 0
for all
x
,
S=\emptyset
if and only if
Ax<b,x\geq 0
is infeasible. By Motzkin’s transposition theorem
[
18
]
, this happens if and only if there exists
u,v\geq 0,u^{\top}A-v^{\top}=0
such that
u\neq 0
and
u^{\top}b\leq 0
, i.e. there exists nonzero
u\geq 0
such that
u^{\top}A\geq 0,u^{\top}b\leq 0
. Note if
u=0
then
u^{\top}b\geq 0
is automatically true. Now observe
u^{\top}A\geq 0,u^{\top}b\leq 0
is equivalent to
\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}Q_{i}\succeq 0
.

•

\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose
S\neq\emptyset
, or equivalently
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\neq\emptyset
. Then
\textup{conv}(S)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, and by Proposition
8.3
this is equivalent to existence of
u\geq 0
such that
u^{\top}A\geq 0
and is nonzero.

•

\emptyset\subsetneq\textup{conv}(S)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
:
In this case

\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\neq\emptyset
. Therefore, using Proposition
8.1
and Proposition
8.4
, we obtain that
\textup{conv}(S)
is given by finitely many aggregations where the aggregated constraint is convex, as each defining inequality of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
has the form
(\lambda^{(j)})^{\top}Ay<(\lambda^{(j)})^{\top}b
, for some
\lambda^{(j)}\geq 0
, corresponding to the following aggregation

S_{\lambda^{(j)}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\begin{bmatrix}x\\
1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{i}^{(j)}Q_{i}\right)\begin{bmatrix}x\\
1\end{bmatrix}<0\}.

The leading
n\times n
principal submatrix of
Q_{\lambda^{(j)}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{i}^{(j)}Q_{i}
is PSD as
(\lambda^{(j)})^{\top}A\geq 0
from Proposition
8.4
, so
Q_{\lambda^{(j)}}
has at most one negative eigenvalue and
S_{\lambda^{(j)}}
is convex, which means
\lambda^{(j)}\in\Omega
.

∎

9.
Finite number of aggregations sufficient to obtain the convex hull

We let
\Theta=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}Q_{i}\succeq 0\}
denote the set of linear combinations that gives rise to a PSD matrix. Our first observation is that one may always improve an aggregation
\lambda\in\Omega
(where
S_{\lambda}\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
) by elements in
\Theta
as long as it still stays inside nonnegative orthant.
Recall that
\Omega=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\setminus\{0\}:\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq S_{\lambda}\textup{ and }Q_{\lambda}\textup{ has at most one negative eigenvalue}\}
.

Proposition 9.1
.

Assume
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\Omega_{1}=\Omega\setminus\{\lambda\in\Omega:S_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. Let
\lambda\in\Omega_{1}
and
\theta\in\Theta
so that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda+\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Omega_{1}
and
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
.

Note that Proposition
9.1
is for arbitrary quadratics, and in particular does not assume HHC.

Proof.

Since
Q_{\lambda^{\prime}}\succeq Q_{\lambda}
we have
f_{\lambda^{\prime}}\geq f_{\lambda}
and
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq S
, which also implies
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
since
S_{\lambda}\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The next step is to show
Q_{\lambda^{\prime}}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue. Since
Q_{\lambda^{\prime}}\succeq Q_{\lambda}
,
Q_{\lambda^{\prime}}
cannot have more negative eigenvalues than
Q_{\lambda}
, due to Weyl’s inequality on eigenvalues
[
13
]
. Since
Q_{\lambda}
has at most one negative eigenvalue, same must be true for
Q_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. On the other hand
Q_{\lambda^{\prime}}
is not PSD since
S\neq\emptyset
and
S\subseteq S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. Thus it has exactly one negative eigenvalue.

It remains to show
\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. We consider several cases based on whether
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
and
S_{\lambda}
are convex or unions of two disjoint convex sets.

•

S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
is convex then we are done since
S\subseteq S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
.

•

S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
is union of two disjoint convex sets, i.e.,
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}=C_{1}^{\prime}\cup C_{2}^{\prime}
where
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}
are disjoint and convex. There are two subcases about
S_{\lambda}
.

–

S_{\lambda}
is convex. From Lemma
5.3

\textup{conv}(S_{\lambda^{\prime}})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
we must have
S_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, contradicting our assumption that
\lambda\in\Omega_{1}
.

–

S_{\lambda}
is union of two disjoint convex sets, i.e.,
S_{\lambda}=C_{1}\cup C_{2}
where
C_{1},C_{2}
are disjoint and convex. Since
\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq S_{\lambda}
,

\textup{conv}(S)
must lie entirely in one of

C_{1}
or
C_{2}
, say
C_{1}
upon relabeling, which means
\textup{conv}(S)\cap C_{2}=\emptyset
since
C_{1},C_{2}
are disjoint. Since
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
, upon relabeling assume
C_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq C_{1}
,
C_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq C_{2}
. This means
\textup{conv}(S)\cap C_{2}^{\prime}=\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\subseteq C_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. Hence
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Omega_{1}
.

∎

We now prove
under the assumption that every triple of quadratics satisfies the PDLC condition, elements in
\Omega_{1}
with support at most 2 describe the same set as all elements in
\Omega_{1}
.
The idea is to repeatedly improve along positive definite linear combinations to reduce the support of a good aggregation.

Proposition 9.2
.

Assume
S\neq\emptyset
and
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let

\Omega_{1}=\Omega\setminus\{\lambda\in\Omega:S_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. Furthermore assume for all distinct
i,j,k\in[m]
there exist scalars
p_{ijk},q_{ijk},r_{ijk}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{ijk}Q_{i}+q_{ijk}Q_{j}+r_{ijk}Q_{k}\succ 0
. Let
\Omega_{2}=\{\lambda\in\Omega_{1}:|\{i:\lambda_{i}>0\}|\leq 2\}
. Then
\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{2}}S_{\lambda}=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{1}}S_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Clearly
\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{1}}S_{\lambda}\subseteq\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{2}}S_{\lambda}
since
\Omega_{2}\subseteq\Omega_{1}
. For reverse inclusion we show that for any
\lambda\in\Omega_{1}
with
|\{i:\lambda_{i}>0\}|\geq 3
there exists
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Omega_{1}
with
|\{i:\lambda^{\prime}_{i}>0\}|<|\{i:\lambda_{i}>0\}|
with
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
. Then repeatedly applying this subroutine whenever possible, we eventually get
\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega_{1}
with
|\{i:\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{i}>0\}|\leq 2
and
S_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
.

Fix
i,j,k\in\{l:\lambda_{l}>0\}
and
p_{ijk},q_{ijk},r_{ijk}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p_{ijk}Q_{i}+q_{ijk}Q_{j}+r_{ijk}Q_{k}\succ 0
.
If
\lambda
is a multiple of
v=p_{ijk}e_{i}+q_{ijk}e_{j}+r_{ijk}e_{k}
, we can perturb
(p_{ijk},q_{ijk},r_{ijk})
so that
\lambda
is not a multiple of
v=p_{ijk}e_{i}+q_{ijk}e_{j}+r_{ijk}e_{k}
and
p_{ijk}Q_{i}+q_{ijk}Q_{j}+r_{ijk}Q_{k}\succ 0
.
Also note that
p_{ijk},q_{ijk},r_{ijk}
cannot be all nonnegative, since otherwise we have that
S\subseteq S_{v}=\emptyset
.
Now let
\alpha_{0}=\max\{\alpha>0:\lambda+\alpha v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\}
and
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda+\alpha_{0}v
. More explicitly
\alpha_{0}
is the minimum between
\frac{\lambda_{i}}{-p_{ijk}},\frac{\lambda_{j}}{-q_{ijk}},\frac{\lambda_{k}}{-r_{ijk}}
where we only consider the terms where denominator is positive. Then clearly
|\{i:\lambda^{\prime}_{i}>0\}|<|\{i:\lambda_{i}>0\}|
, and
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Omega_{1},S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
due to Proposition
9.1
.
∎

Now we study the structure of aggregations with fixed support of size two
that contain
\textup{conv}(S)
, and show they are either empty or form one or two intervals, whose endpoints are the same as the intervals described in
[
22
]
. Thus
for the description of the convex hull it suffices to take either the two outermost endpoints, or two endpoints of the same interval. The key ingredient in our proof is the geometry of the set defined by two quadratic inequalities, which was studied in
[
22
]
. Here we list the results that are needed for our proof, and describe their implications. Note that the versions stated in our paper differ by a sign compared to
[
22
]
, as we study the set of points where the quadratic inequalities are negative (instead of positive).

In words, these results show that convex combinations of any two quadratics contain at most two intervals of matrices that have at most one negative eigenvalue. The endpoints of the intervals can be recognized as points where the rank drops. Furthermore, homogenized good aggregations lying in the same interval have an additional geometric property described in Lemma
9.4
.

Now we formally state these results. Let
\nu(M)
denote the number of negative eigenvalues of a matrix
M
.

Theorem 9.3

(Lemma 2 of
[
22
]
)
.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}
be two symmetric matrices, and let
\Lambda=\{0\leq\alpha\leq 1:\nu(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})=1\}
. If
\Lambda\neq\emptyset
, then there exists
n_{c}\in\{1,2\}
and
\Lambda=\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq n_{c}}\{\mathcal{I}_{j}\}
, where each
\mathcal{I}_{j}
is a closed interval of
[0,1]
and
\mathcal{I}_{j},\mathcal{I}_{k}
are disjoint if
j\neq k
. Furthermore, the endpoints of
\mathcal{I}_{j}
are real roots of
\det(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})=0
, known as generalized eigenvalues (GEVs) of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
.

Lemma 9.4

(Lemma 7 of
[
22
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be one interval in the previous theorem. Then there exists a linear hyperplane
L
that does not intersect
\{x:x^{\top}(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})x<0\}
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
.

Using these results we prove the following proposition about aggregations of homogeneous quadratics with fixed support of size two: if all aggregations contain a given set
S_{0}
and one aggregation with one negative eigenvalue contains
\textup{conv}(S_{0})
(so that this aggregation is good), then all aggregations in the same interval must also contain
\textup{conv}(S_{0})
, i.e. all aggregations in the same interval are good.
The main idea is to use the hyperplane
L
as promised by Lemma
9.4
, and show that
\textup{conv}(S_{0})
must lie entirely in one of the halfspaces separated by
L
, and be disjoint from the other halfspace.

Proposition 9.5
.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}
be
(n+1)\times(n+1)
symmetric matrices. Let
S_{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and assume
\emptyset\subsetneq\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Let
\Lambda=\{0\leq\alpha\leq 1:\nu(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})=1\}
. Suppose
\Lambda\neq\emptyset
and
S_{0}\subseteq\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}
for all
\alpha\in\Lambda
.

Based on Theorem
9.3
we have
\Lambda=\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq n_{c}}\{\mathcal{I}_{j}\}
,
n_{c}\in\{1,2\}
, where each
\mathcal{I}_{j}
is a closed interval of
[0,1]
and
\mathcal{I}_{j},\mathcal{I}_{k}
are disjoint if
j\neq k
. Fix
1\leq j\leq n_{c}
and assume

\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subseteq\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha_{0}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha_{0})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}

for some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
. Then

\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subseteq\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}

for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
5.1
, the set
\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha_{0}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha_{0})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}
is an SCC, i.e., a union of two disjoint open convex cones that are symmetric reflections of each other across the origin. Let
\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha_{0}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha_{0})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}=C_{\alpha_{0}}^{+}\cup C_{\alpha_{0}}^{-}
, where
C_{\alpha_{0}}^{+},C_{\alpha_{0}}^{-}
are the disjoint open convex cones. Since
\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subseteq\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha_{0}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha_{0})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}
, it is fully contained in one of the convex cones and disjoint from the other. Upon relabeling we may assume
\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subseteq C_{\alpha_{0}}^{+}
and
\textup{conv}(S_{0})\cap C_{\alpha_{0}}^{-}=\emptyset
.

From Lemma
9.4
, there exists linear hyperplane
L
that does not intersect
\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha Q_{1}+(1-\alpha)Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
. Let
L^{+},L^{-}
be the two open halfspaces separated by
L
. Upon relabeling we may assume
C_{\alpha_{0}}^{+}\subseteq L^{+},C_{\alpha_{0}}^{-}\subseteq L^{-}
.

Now fix arbitrary
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
. The set
\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha^{\prime}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha^{\prime})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}
is an SCC. Let
\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha^{\prime}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha^{\prime})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}=C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+}\cup C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{-}
, where
C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+},C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{-}
are the disjoint open convex cones. Note
L\cap\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha^{\prime}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha^{\prime})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}=\emptyset
, which means each of
C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+},C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{-}
is fully contained in one of the open halfspaces separated by
L
. Upon relabeling we may assume
C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+}\subseteq L^{+},C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{-}\subseteq L^{-}
.

Since
\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subseteq C_{\alpha_{0}}^{+}\subseteq L^{+}
and
L^{+}\cap L^{-}=\emptyset
, we have
\textup{conv}(S_{0})\cap L^{-}=\emptyset
and hence
S_{0}\cap L^{-}=\emptyset
. Therefore
S_{0}\cap C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{-}=\emptyset
as
C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{-}\subseteq L^{-}
, and
S_{0}\subseteq C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+}
. Since
C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+}
is convex, we have
\textup{conv}(S_{0})\subseteq C_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{+}\subseteq\{\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\hat{x}^{\top}(\alpha^{\prime}Q_{1}+(1-\alpha^{\prime})Q_{2})\hat{x}<0\}
as desired.
∎

Recall that
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{\top}A_{i}x+2b_{i}^{\top}x+c_{i}<0,i\in[m]\}
and
\Omega
is the set of good aggregations which have at most one negative eigenvalue and contain
\textup{conv}(S)
. Proposition
9.5
implies the following result about pairwise aggregations that contain the convex hull.

Proposition 9.6
.

Assume
S\neq\emptyset
,
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and hidden hyperplane convexity holds for the associated quadratic map
f^{h}
, so that Theorem
2.9
holds and
\textup{conv}(S)=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega}S_{\lambda}
. Fix
i,j\in[m]
and let

\Omega_{ij}=\{\lambda\in\Omega:\lambda_{k}=0,\forall k\notin\{i,j\}\}

be aggregations in
\Omega
that have support in
\{i,j\}
. Then either
\Omega_{ij}=\emptyset
, or there exists
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega_{ij}
such that
\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{ij}}S_{\lambda}=S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cap S_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
, where
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}
can be written as
\lambda^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}e_{i}+(1-\alpha^{\prime})e_{j},\lambda^{\prime\prime}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}e_{i}+(1-\alpha^{\prime\prime})e_{j}
, where
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}
are roots of
\det(\alpha Q_{i}+(1-\alpha)Q_{j})=0
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we reparametrize aggregations with support
\{i,j\}
by the unit length interval
[0,1]
, and view each
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
as the aggregation
\alpha e_{i}+(1-\alpha)e_{j}
.

Assume
\Omega_{ij}\neq\emptyset
. Clearly
\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{ij}}S_{\lambda}\subseteq S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cap S_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
since
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega_{ij}
. For reverse inclusion, from Theorem
9.3
,
\{\lambda:\lambda_{k}=0,\forall k\notin\{i,j\}\}
where
Q_{\lambda}
has at most one negative eigenvalues forms one or two closed intervals
(contained in the reparametrized
[0,1]
interval)
. Furthermore, each interval either lies entirely in
\Omega_{ij}
or is disjoint from it, by applying Proposition
9.5
to
S_{0}=\{(x,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x\in S\}
and observing that
S_{0}\subseteq S_{\lambda}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\times\{1\}}
for all nonzero
\lambda\geq 0
. Thus
\Omega_{ij}
is also one or two closed intervals whose endpoints are GEVs of
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
. We let
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}
be the two outermost endpoints of
\Omega_{ij}
, and observe that any
\lambda\in\Omega_{ij}
is a nonnegative combination of
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
and therefore

S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cap S_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq S_{\lambda}
, which means
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cap S_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Omega_{ij}}S_{\lambda}
.
∎

.

.

. Then from Proposition

,

. Therefore Theorem

to all

10.
Results for closed inequalities

Proof of Theorem
2.24
.

Recall that
T=\{x:f_{i}(x)\leq 0,i\in[m]\}
is the set defined by closed inequalities and
G=\textup{int}(\overline{\textup{conv}(T)})
. We remind the reader that
S\subseteq G
and we assume
G\neq\emptyset
.

Take any
y\notin G
. Since
G
is convex and open, there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
G\subseteq\{x:\alpha^{\top}x>\alpha^{\top}y\}
. Let
H=\{x:\alpha^{\top}x=\alpha^{\top}y\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the separating affine hyperplane and
\hat{H}=\{(x,x_{n+1}):\alpha^{\top}x=(\alpha^{\top}y)x_{n+1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be its homogenization. Let
\hat{H}_{+}=\{(x,x_{n+1}):\alpha^{\top}x\geq(\alpha^{\top}y)x_{n+1}\},\hat{H}_{-}=\{(x,x_{n+1}):\alpha^{\top}x\leq(\alpha^{\top}y)x_{n+1}\}
be the closed halfspaces created by
\hat{H}
. Then we have
\overline{\textup{conv}(T)}\times\{1\}\subseteq\hat{H}_{+}
.

Note that Lemma
5.4
still applies since
\textup{conv}(S)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, since
y\notin S
(because
S\subseteq G
) we have that
S^{h}\cap\hat{H}=\emptyset
, i.e.,

\{\hat{x}\in\hat{H}:\hat{x}^{\top}Q_{i}\hat{x}<0,i\in[m]\}=\emptyset
.
Proceeding in the same way
as
in the proof
of
Theorem
2.9
, we use the fact that
Q_{i}
’s
satisfy hidden hyperplane convexity, to show that there exists a nonzero vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
such that
Q_{\lambda}
is PSD on
\hat{H}
. By our assumption we also have
Q_{\lambda}\neq 0
.

By the Interlacing Theorem,
Q_{\lambda}
has at most one negative eigenvalue, and it cannot be PSD otherwise
T\times\{1\}\subseteq\ker Q_{\lambda}
, which would imply
G=\emptyset
. This means
Q_{\lambda}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue. Therefore
S_{\lambda}
consists of one or two disjoint open convex sets. Let
T_{\lambda}=\{x:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(x)\leq 0\}
denote the set defined by the same aggregation with closed inequalities. It is clear that
T_{\lambda}
is closed,
T\subseteq T_{\lambda}
and
S_{\lambda}=\textup{int}(T_{\lambda})
.

We now show that
\overline{\textup{conv}(T)}\subseteq T_{\lambda}
which then implies that
G\subseteq S_{\lambda}
. If
S_{\lambda}
is convex then we are done as in this case
T_{\lambda}
is convex and closed. Suppose
S_{\lambda}
consists of two disjoint open convex connected components. We write
S_{\lambda}=(S_{\lambda})_{+}\cup(S_{\lambda})_{-}
where
(S_{\lambda})_{+},(S_{\lambda})_{-}
are disjoint open convex sets. Since
Q_{\lambda}
is PSD on
\hat{H}
, these two sets lie in different sides of
\hat{H}
, and upon relabeling we assume
(S_{\lambda})_{+}\subseteq\hat{H}_{+},(S_{\lambda})_{-}\subseteq\hat{H}_{-}
.

Let
(T_{\lambda})_{+}=\overline{(S_{\lambda})_{+}},(T_{\lambda})_{-}=\overline{(S_{\lambda})_{-}}
. Then it is clear that
T_{\lambda}=(T_{\lambda})_{+}\cup(T_{\lambda})_{-},(T_{\lambda})_{+}\cap(T_{\lambda})_{-}\subseteq\hat{H}
, and
(T_{\lambda})_{+}\subseteq\hat{H}_{+},(T_{\lambda})_{-}\subseteq\hat{H}_{-}
.

Recall
T\times\{1\}\subseteq\hat{H}_{+}
. We claim this implies
T\times\{1\}\subseteq(T_{\lambda})_{+}
, which completes the proof as
(T_{\lambda})_{+}
is closed and convex. Suppose otherwise, then
(T\times\{1\})\cap((T_{\lambda})_{-}\setminus(T_{\lambda})_{+})\neq\emptyset
, but
(T_{\lambda})_{-}\setminus(T_{\lambda})_{+}\subseteq\hat{H}_{-}\setminus\hat{H}
which is disjoint from
\hat{H}_{+}
. ∎
