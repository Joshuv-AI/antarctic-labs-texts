Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the class containing functions that are analytic in the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,|z|<1\}
and are normalized such that

f(0)=0=f^{\prime}(0)-1
, i.e.,

(1)

f(z)=z+a_{2}z^{2}+a_{3}z^{3}+\cdots.

Further, let class
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
,
0<\lambda\leq 1
, consists of all functions
f\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that

\left|\left(\frac{z}{f(z)}\right)^{2}f^{\prime}(z)-1\right|<\lambda\quad(z\in{\mathbb{D}}).

The functions from the class
{\mathcal{U}}\equiv{\mathcal{U}}(1)
are univalent (analytic and one-on-one), but are not starlike (doesn’t map the unit disk onto a starlike region). The last is rare since the class of univalent starlike functions os very wide, and is main reason that class
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
attracts significant attention in past decades.

Studying the behaviour of the coefficients of normalized univalent functions is one of the main directions for research in the theory of univalent functions. A problem that recently rediscovered, is to find upper bound (preferably sharp) of the modulus of the Hankel determinant
H_{q}(n)(f)
of a given function
f
, for
q\geq 1
and
n\geq 1
, defined by

H_{q}(n)(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\ldots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n+2}&\ldots&a_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q}&\ldots&a_{n+2q-2}\\
\end{array}\right|.

The general Hankel determinant is hard to deal with, so the second and the third ones,

H_{2}(2)(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{array}\right|=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2}

and

H_{3}(1)(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}&a_{5}\\
\end{array}\right|=a_{3}(a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2})-a_{4}(a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3})+a_{5}(a_{3}-a_{2}^{2}),

respectively, are studied instead. The research is focused on the subclasses of univalent functions (starlike, convex,
\alpha
-convex, close-to-convex, spirallike,…) since the general class of normalised univalent functions is also hard to deal with. Some of the more significant results can be found in

In this paper we will give the sharp upper bound of the modulus of the second and third Hankel determinant for the inverse functions for the functions in the class
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
. Since functions
f
from
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
are univalent, they have their inverse at least on the disk with radius 1/4 (due to the famous Koebe’s 1/4 theorem). If the inverse has an expansion

(2)

f^{-1}(w)=w+A_{2}w^{2}+A_{3}w^{3}+\cdots,

then, by using the identity
f(f^{-1}(w))=w
, from (
1
) and (
2
) we receive

(3)

\begin{array}[]{l}A_{2}=-a_{2},\\
A_{3}=-a_{3}+2a_{2}^{2},\\
A_{4}=-a_{4}+5a_{2}a_{3}-5a_{2}^{3},\\
A_{5}=-a_{5}+6a_{2}a_{4}-21a_{2}^{2}a_{3}+3a_{3}^{2}+14a_{2}^{4}.\end{array}

For our consideration we need the next lemma proven in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 1
.

For each function
f
in
\mathcal{U}(\lambda),

0<\lambda\leq 1,
there exists function
\omega_{1},
analytic in
\mathbb{D},
such that
|\omega_{1}(z)|\leq|z|<1,
and
|\omega^{\prime}_{1}(z)|\leq 1,

for all
z\in\mathbb{D},
with

(4)

\frac{z}{f(z)}=1-a_{2}z-\lambda z\omega_{1}(z).

Additionally, for

(5)

\omega_{1}(z)=c_{1}z+c_{2}z^{2}+\cdots,

we have

(6)

|c_{1}|\leq 1,\quad|c_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-|c_{1}|^{2})\quad and\quad|c_{3}|\leq\frac{1}{3}\left[1-|c_{1}|^{2}-\frac{4|c_{2}|^{2}}{1+|c_{1}|}\right].

Using (
4
) and (
5
) we have

z=[1-a_{2}z-\lambda z\omega_{1}(z)]f(z),

and after equating the coefficients,

\displaystyle a_{3}=\lambda c_{1}+a^{2}_{2},

(7)

\displaystyle a_{4}=\lambda c_{2}+2\lambda a_{2}c_{1}+a_{2}^{3},

\displaystyle a_{5}=\lambda c_{3}+2\lambda a_{2}c_{2}+\lambda^{2}c_{1}^{2}+3\lambda a_{2}^{2}c_{1}+a_{2}^{4},

that we will use later on.

From (
3
) and (

), after some calculations, we derive

(8)

\begin{array}[]{l}A_{2}=-a_{2},\\
A_{3}=-\lambda c_{1}+a_{2}^{2},\\
A_{4}=-\lambda c_{2}+3\lambda a_{2}c_{1}-a_{2}^{3},\\
A_{5}=-\lambda c_{3}+4\lambda a_{2}c_{2}-6\lambda a_{2}^{2}c_{1}+2\lambda^{2}c_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{4}.\end{array}

We also need the next results from
[
16
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
for
0<\lambda\leq 1
, and be given by
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
. Then

(9)

|a_{2}|\leq 1+\lambda.

If
|a_{2}|=1+\lambda
, then
f
must be of the form

(10)

f(z)=\frac{z}{1-(1+\lambda)e^{i\phi}z+\lambda e^{2i\phi}z^{2}}

for some
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
.

In the same paper (
[
16
]
) it was conjectured that for functions in
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
,
|a_{n}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lambda^{i}
holds sharply, and was claimed to be proven in the case
n=3
:

(11)

|a_{3}|\leq 1+\lambda+\lambda^{2}.

The proof rely on another claim, that for all functions
f
from
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
,

(12)

\frac{f(z)}{z}\prec\frac{1}{(1+z)(1+\lambda z)}.

Recently, in
[
8
]
, the second claim, and consequently the first one also, was proven to be wrong by giving a counterexample. Still, the subset of
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
when the inequality (
11
) and subordination (
12
) hold is nonempty, as the function

f_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)(1-\lambda z)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1-\lambda^{n}}{1-\lambda}z^{n}=z+(1+\lambda)z^{2}+(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2})z^{2}+\cdots

shows. Here
\left.\frac{1-\lambda^{n}}{1-\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}=n
for all
n=1,2,3,\ldots
.

Now we will give the sharp upper bound of the modulus of the second and the third Hankel determinant for the inverse functions of the functions from the class
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
,
0<\lambda\leq 1
, and let its inverse is
f^{-1}
. Then

(
i
)

|H_{2}(2)(f^{-1})|\leq\lambda(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2})
if the third coefficient of
f
satisfies inequality (
11
);

(
ii
)

|H_{3}(1)(f^{-1})|\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4},&0<\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4},\\
\lambda^{3},&\frac{1}{4}\leq\lambda\leq 1.\end{array}\right.

Both results are sharp.

Proof.

(
i
) Using the definition of the second Hankel determinant, together with (
2
) and (
8
) we have

H_{2}(2)(f^{-1})=A_{2}A_{4}-A_{3}^{2}=\lambda(a_{2}c_{2}-a_{2}^{2}c_{1}-\lambda c_{1}^{2}),

which gives

|H_{2}(2)(f^{-1})|\leq\lambda\left(|a_{2}||c_{2}|+|c_{1}||\lambda c_{1}+a_{2}^{2}|\right).

From (
11
) we have
|\lambda c_{1}+a_{2}^{2}|=|a_{3}|\leq 1+\lambda+\lambda^{2}
, from (
6
),
|c_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-|c_{1}|^{2})
, and so,

(13)

\begin{split}|H_{2}(2)(f^{-1})|&\leq\lambda\left[\frac{|a_{2}|}{2}(1-|c_{1}|^{2})+(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2})|c_{1}|\right]\\
&=\lambda\left[-\frac{|a_{2}|}{2}|c_{1}|^{2}+(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2})|c_{1}|+\frac{|a_{2}|}{2}\right]\\
&:=\lambda\phi_{1}(|c_{1}|),\end{split}

where
\phi_{1}(t)=\lambda\left[-\frac{|a_{2}|}{2}t^{2}+(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2})t+\frac{|a_{2}|}{2}\right]
,
0\leq t\leq 1
. Since for
f\in{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
we have
|a_{2}|\leq 1+\lambda
(see (
9
)), then we conclude that
\phi_{1}
is an increasing function on [0,1], i.e., it attains its maximum for
t=|c_{1}|=1
, which is equal to
1+\lambda+\lambda^{2}
. Using this and (
13
) we have the statement of part (i) of this theorem. The result is sharp. Namely, for the function
f_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)(1-\lambda z)}=z+(1+\lambda)z^{2}+(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2})z^{3}+(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{3})z^{4}+\cdots
, but using (
3
) we have that

f^{-1}_{\lambda}(w)=w-(1+\lambda)w^{2}+(1+3\lambda+\lambda^{2})w^{3}-(1+6\lambda+6\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{3})w^{4}+\cdots

and

H_{2}(2)(f^{-1})=(1+\lambda)(1+6\lambda+6\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{3})-(1+3\lambda+\lambda^{2})^{2}=\lambda(1+\lambda+\lambda^{2}).

(
ii
) Using (
2
) and the definition of the third Hankel determinant, we have

H_{3}(1)(f^{-1})=A_{3}(A_{2}A_{4}-A_{3}^{2})-A_{4}(A_{4}-A_{2}A_{3})+A_{5}(A_{3}-A_{2}^{2}).

From this and (
8
), after some calculations we have

\left|H_{3}(1)(f^{-1})\right|=\left|\lambda^{2}c_{1}c_{3}-\lambda^{2}c_{2}^{2}-\lambda^{3}c_{1}^{3}\right|\leq\lambda^{2}\left(|c_{1}||c_{3}|+|c_{2}|^{2}+\lambda|c_{1}|^{3}\right),

and, by using the relations for
|c_{2}|
and
|c_{3}|
given in (
6
):

(14)

\begin{split}\left|H_{3}(1)(f^{-1})\right|&\leq\lambda^{2}\left[|c_{1}|\frac{1}{3}\left(1-|c_{1}|^{2}-\frac{4|c_{2}|^{2}}{1+|c_{1}|}\right)+|c_{2}|^{2}+\lambda|c_{1}|^{3}\right]\\
&=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3}\cdot\left[|c_{1}|-|c_{1}|^{3}+\frac{3-|c_{1}|}{1+|c_{1}|}|c_{2}|^{2}+3\lambda|c_{1}|^{3}\right]\\
&\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3}\cdot\left[|c_{1}|+(3\lambda-1)|c_{1}|^{3}+\frac{3-|c_{1}|}{1+|c_{1}|}\frac{1}{4}(1-|c_{1}|^{2})^{2}\right]\\
&=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{12}\cdot\left[3-2|c_{1}|^{2}+12\lambda|c_{1}|^{3}-|c_{1}|^{4}\right]\\
&:=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{12}\cdot\phi_{2}(t),\end{split}

where
\phi_{2}(t)=3-2t^{2}+12\lambda t^{3}-t^{4},

0\leq t=|c_{1}|\leq 1
.

Since
\phi^{\prime}_{2}(t)=-4t(t^{2}-9\lambda t+1)
, then if
0<\lambda\leq\frac{2}{9}
, we have
\phi^{\prime}_{2}(t)\leq 0
for
0\leq t\leq 1
, and
\phi_{2}(t)\leq\phi_{2}(0)=3
, which by (
14
) implies
\left|H_{3}(1)(f^{-1})\right|\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}.

If
\frac{2}{9}\leq\lambda\leq 1
, then

\begin{split}\max\{\phi_{2}(t):0\leq t\leq 1\}&=\max\{\phi_{2}(0),\phi_{2}(1)\}=\max\{3,12\lambda\}\\
&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}3,&\frac{2}{9}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4},\\
12\lambda,&\frac{1}{4}\leq\lambda\leq 1.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Combining all this facts we receive the estimate (
ii
) of the theorem.

The estimate (
ii
) is also sharp due to the functions
f_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1-\frac{\lambda}{2}z^{3}}=z+\frac{\lambda}{2}z^{4}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}z^{7}+\cdots
and
f_{2}(z)=\frac{z}{1-\lambda z^{2}}=z+\lambda z^{3}+\lambda^{2}z^{5}+\cdots
. Indeed, it is easy to check that both functions are in
{\mathcal{U}}(\lambda)
. Further, for
f_{1}
we have
a_{2}=a_{3}=a_{5}=0
,
a_{4}=\frac{\lambda}{2}
, and by (
3
),
A_{2}=A_{3}=A_{5}=0
,
A_{4}=-\frac{\lambda}{2}
, i.e.,
H_{3}(1)(f_{1}^{-1})=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}
and
\left|H_{3}(1)(f_{1}^{-1})\right|=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}
. Similarly, for the function
f_{2}
,
a_{2}=0
,
a_{3}=\lambda
,
a_{4}=0
,
a_{5}=\lambda^{2}
, and
A_{2}=0
,
A_{3}=-\lambda
,
A_{4}=0
.
A_{5}=2\lambda^{2}
, i.e.,
\left|H_{3}(1)(f_{2}^{-1})\right|=|-\lambda^{3}|=\lambda^{3}
.

∎
