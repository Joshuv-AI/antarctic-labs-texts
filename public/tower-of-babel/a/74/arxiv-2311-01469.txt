Leveraging Language Models to Detect Greenwashing
Margaret Capetz Christina Chance Rebecca Pattichis, Avalon Vinella Reshmi Ghosh Kai-Wei Chang

Abstract
In recent years, climate change repercussions have increasingly captured public interest. Consequently, corporations are emphasizing their environmental efforts in sustainability reports to bolster their public image. Yet, the absence of stringent regulations in review of such reports allows potential greenwashing. In this study, we introduce a novel preliminary methodology to train a language model on generated labels for greenwashing risk. Our primary contributions encompass: developing a preliminary mathematical formulation to quantify greenwashing risk, a fine-tuned ClimateBERT model for this problem, and a comparative analysis of results. On a test set comprising of sustainability reports, our best model achieved an average accuracy score of 86.34% and F1 score of 0.67, demonstrating that our proof-of-concept methodology shows a promising direction of exploration for this task.



1
Introduction

As climate change has moved further to the forefront of social and political discussions, corporations have an increased stake in presenting themselves as environmentally conscious to investors, government agencies, and consumers. Greenwashing is broadly defined as when an entity creates an overly positive corporate image through selective disclosure
(
Bowen and Aragon-Correa 2014
)
. In other words, authors of corporate impact reports may present misleading information about their sustainability efforts in order to maintain public favor and profit, rather than being transparent about their potentially negative environmental impact. Natural Language Processing (NLP) can aid with greenwashing detection in the dense language of corporate sustainability reports, and this potential has been recently explored in previous studies. However, a major obstacle is the relative subjectivity of a definition for greenwashing, which is difficult to quantify. In this work, we present methodology for generating ground truth labels for greenwashing risk based on climate-specific attributes. We validate our results with gathered metrics of reported corporate carbon emissions from our collaborators.

2
Related Work

We build our work off of previous studies and models that have addressed climate-related issues in an NLP context and defined greenwashing.

NLP for Climate Work.

Many works utilize NLP methods to assess environmental risk and impact. For example,
Kang and Kim 2022
and
Polignano et al. 2022
employ sentence similarity, sentiment classification, and text extraction to evaluate corporate sustainability reports. While
Kim et al. 2023
created a search service for greenwashing pattern detection, our work goes beyond pattern detection by developing a novel quantitative definition of greenwashing risk and fine-tuning a specialized language model (ClimateBERT) for this task. Additionally, we validate our results against real-world corporate emissions data.

ClimateBERT.

ClimateBERT is a BERT architecture-based model, more specifically based on Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach (RoBERTa), pre-trained on a corpus of climate-related texts, which has improved performance on various climate-related downstream tasks
(
Webersinke et al. 2022
)
. ClimateBERT has been fine-tuned for climate-related tasks such as text detection, sentiment classification, etc
(
Bingler et al. 2023
)
. We build off of this model in our work.

Definition of Greenwashing.

Delmas and Burbano 2011
define greenwashing as a combination of a firm’s positive communication about environmental performance despite true poor environmental performance. Additionally,
Lyon and Maxwell 2011-03
define corporate greenwashing as "the selective disclosure of positive information about a company’s environmental or social performance while withholding negative information on these dimensions." This suggests that companies tend to view sustainability information as optional to disclose based on whether or not it will positively impact them.

3
Methodology

3.1
Data

ClimateBERT Data.

The ClimateBERT model was pre-trained on 2 million samples of news articles, research abstracts, and corporate climate reports (2015 to 2020)
(
Webersinke et al. 2022
)
. The public ClimateBERT dataset includes paragraphs sourced from firm’s financial disclosures
(
Bingler et al. 2023
)
. We use this data for fine-tuning ClimateBERT for greenwashing risk classification by extracting text from the corpus that was identified as climate-related text from all dataset splits. Then, we re-split into training and validation sets of 80/20 percent (1,056/264 examples), respectively.

Open for Good Data.
Open for Good is a project at the Center for Impact at the University of California, Los Angeles Anderson School of Management tackling transparent reporting of corporate sustainability
(
Delmas et al. 2023
)
. We use their carbon emissions environmental impact data, which is classified into Scope 1, Scope 2, and Scope 3 by the US Department of Energy. Scope 1 emissions are company’s direct production of a good, Scope 2 includes indirect emissions for purchased electricity, and Scope 3 includes emissions from the value chain of the product’s life-cycle. We use these sustainability reports as our test set.

3.2
Generating Ground Truth for Greenwashing Risk

While greenwashing has consistent implications, defining its qualities can be subjective and context-dependent. We have identified the following characteristics typical of greenwashing language in corporate sustainability reports: 1. Absence of explicit climate-related commitments and actions, 2. Use of non-specific language, 3. overly optimistic sentiment, and 4. lack of evasive or hedging terms. This characterization was developed through a comprehensive literature review and corroborated by domain experts from the UCLA Anderson School of Management, ensuring alignment with both academic research and industry insights. Given the legal significance of sustainability reports, there are often instances of legal hedging, or use of evasive language. For example, “The Group is
not aware
of any noise pollution that could negatively impact the environment.” These cases are not necessarily greenwashing, as they are not trying to positively re-frame some adverse action, but more so legal hedging
(
Vass 2017
)
.

Lueg and Lueg 2020
propose a method to model the relationship between a company’s market risk and its sustainability performance, including a linear combination method to assess various types of risk based on the company’s attributes. We therefore adopt a similar approach of using a linear combination of specific metrics to determine greenwashing risk in sustainability-related text.

To quantify the attributes outlined above, we utilize binary labeling, as it is more aligned with the standard sentiments of "positive" and "negative." We utilized pre-trained ClimateBERT models fine-tuned for climate commitment and specificity tasks. These models output probabilities, which we binarized using a threshold of 0.5. For sentiment, while ClimateBERT has been fine-tuned for climate-specific sentiment, the model employs a ternary labeling scheme. To align with our binary labeling, we instead use the pre-trained SiBERT model, which is trained for sentiment analysis
(
Hartmann et al. 2023
)
. The model’s output was binarized, with positive sentiment assigned a value of 1 and negative sentiment a value of 0. We developed a custom function to detect hedging language using a predefined list of hedging phrases, see Appendix
A.1
. The function returns 1 if any hedging phrase is found in the text, and 0 otherwise.

To establish a mathematical formulation for greenwashing, we sourced 10 representative exemplars from the ClimateBERT dataset. Subject-matter experts from the UCLA Anderson School of Management, with extensive background in corporate sustainability and environmental reporting, rigorously annotated and categorized the exemplars, determining whether each exhibited characteristics indicative of greenwashing risk. While this sample size is limited, our work aims to provide a preliminary approach to quantifying greenwashing risk, serving as a proof of concept for future research. The small sample size was due to the time-intensive nature of human-validation. Future work should focus on expanding the ground truth dataset significantly, potentially through data augmentation techniques or other ideas to overcome the challenge of lack of ground truth data.

We used sklearn’s least squares linear regression module to fit a linear model to our data. The input features were the four metrics (sentiment, commitment, specificity, and hedging), and the target variable was the expert-annotated label of greenwashing risk. Additionally, utilizing insights around definitions of greenwashing and the characteristics we identified, we iteratively developed another linear combination for comparison. We also provided a threshold to fit the binary nature of the task.

The second formulation, developed from our understanding of greenwashing and metric definitions, is described below. The negative coefficient for sentiment indicates that more positive sentiment increases the risk of greenwashing, which is consistent with the idea that greenwashing often involves overly positive language regarding environmental performance. The positive coefficients for commitment and specificity reflect that companies that are genuinely engaged in sustainability efforts often include explicit commitments and specific language in their reports. The positive coefficient for hedging language may seem counter intuitive, but is based on that fact that companies making genuine environmental claims often use cautious language to avoid over-promising. This formulation aims to capture the essence of greenwashing: overly positive language without specific commitments or details, while also accounting for the nuanced use of hedging in corporate communications.

Fine-Tuning ClimateBERT for Greenwashing Risk.
We fine-tuned the ClimateBERT model (125M parameters) for greenwashing risk detection on data labeled using the first equation, and experimented with freezing all but the classification head vs. fine-tuning all layers. Our hyperparameter search included learning rates (1e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4) and batch sizes (16, 32), with the best performance achieved using a learning rate of 5e-5 and a batch size of 32. We report the mean and standard deviation of validation accuracy and F1 scores across the 10 runs for each configuration. We used the Adam Optimizer, 0 warm up steps, and 70 epochs. Each experiment ran a total of 10 times with different random seeds to report mean and standard deviation (see Appendix
A.2
for plots of these results).

We found that not freezing the RoBERTa layers resulted in higher mean validation accuracy (70.78% ± 2.03%) and F1 scores (0.70 ± 0.02) as well as a smaller deviation, compared to the frozen configuration (accuracy: 66.52% ± 2.39%, F1: 0.68 ± 0.01). This suggests that the non-frozen model may be slightly more robust than the frozen configuration. It also indicates that our task is specific enough to benefit from training all layers in order to improve the accuracy of ClimateBERT, and that this approach of fine-tuning would be appropriate for determining greenwashing risk.

Out of Distribution (OOD) Evaluation.
We select the run that falls in the mode of accuracy (validation accuracy appears at least twice) and has the highest F1 score within that subset (see Appendix
A.2
). Our OOD test set (from the Open for Good Data) come from APA Corporation, Devon Energy, and Diamondback Energy in the oil and gas sector and Autodesk, NVIDIA, and ServiceNow in the tech sector. The sections relevant to environmental impact were manually identified and extracted. To accommodate the maximum input length of the models, these sections were split into paragraph-aligned chunks determined by a character count upper limit before being tokenized. Finally, the report’s overall label comes from a majority voting of its intermediate climate-related paragraphs.

Both models correctly predicted a negative greenwashing risk for each report with significantly higher accuracy than the validation set; however, the F1 variation is much higher (Table
2
). It is likely that this is due to an imbalance of positive versus negative samples in each report and how they were chunked (see Discussion).

Discussion

There are potential improvements in our ground truth generation. For example, the ClimateBERT models are preliminary, and our ground truth for our data is dependent on the accuracy of these models. During human annotation, we noticed the following examples had matching attributes (from ClimateBERT) but were given opposite labels by our domain-experts, see Table

.

During annotation, experts noted that while the first example was greenwashing due to obfuscation, the second was not because they are admitting to bad performance. It is these types of nuances that the intermediate attributes may fail to capture. Ultimately, we would prefer to have the ground truth dataset extensively expert-validated.

Another indicator of greenwashing to consider in our test set is the reported carbon emissions (Scope 1 and 2) for each company relative to its industry’s average. If a company has a high risk of greenwashing, their emissions may be higher than average in their domain relative to company size, which we approximate using annual revenue (as in Appendix
A.3
). Given this data, there might be reason to intuit that APA, whose emissions are incredibly high relative to similar companies, has a risk of using greenwashing language, but neither our ground truth equation nor model reflect this. While it is not definitive evidence, this discrepancy could suggest some error in our formulations.

6
Conclusion

We present a novel method of quantitatively defining greenwashing and utilize it to fine-tune ClimateBERT, a BERT model trained on climate-related textual data, to identify risks of greenwashing in corporate sustainability reports. Our best model achieved an average accuracy score of 86.34% and F1 score of 0.67 on our test set of sustainability reports, which indicates further room for research and improvement in both the ground truth label formulation and training of the language model.

Limitations

To avoid using test data that ClimateBERT had seen in its training, we only used sustainability reports from 2021 and beyond. Because of this, we were limited by the amount of data in the Open For Good data set that met this requirement, skewing our distribution of labels. Table
2
indicates a potential dataset imbalance, with both models performing well on one label but underperforming on the other. In future work, a balanced dataset can be assembled to provide more valid results.

Lastly, we are limited to analyzing the sustainability reports paragraph-by-paragraph given the length of the reports. This may impact the final score in both the ground truth generation and model results due to the possible loss of context. Labels may also be impacted depending on text parsing.

Ethics Statement

Ethical Concerns.
Due to the limitations of our work as well as the inconsistent definition of greenwashing, we utilize the language of potential risk of greenwashing rather than categorizing something as greenwashing itself. With this understanding, our work has no clear ethical concerns as we are not producing data that can be mistreated and have no potential negative applications to the field.

Broader Impact.
Our work provides potential benefits for researchers, policymakers, and corporate stakeholders. For researchers, our proof-of-concept provides a foundation for more sophisticated NLP-based greenwashing detection. This can lead to downstream benefits for policymakers in informing industry environmental regulations and corporate stakeholders like investors, auditors, and corporate sustainability officers in evaluating and improving environmental disclosures. By providing preliminary results for this line of research, we aim to contribute to more transparent, accountable, and impactful corporate sustainability practices and promote environmental responsibility.
