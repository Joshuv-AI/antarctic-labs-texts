Rényi entropies of the free Fermi gas in multi-dimensional space at high temperature
Hajo Leschke, Alexander V. Sobolev, Wolfgang Spitzer

Abstract
We study the local and (bipartite) entanglement Rényi entropies of the free Fermi gas in multi-dimensional Euclidean space ℝ d \mathbbm{R}^{d} in thermal equilibrium. We prove positivity of the entanglement entropies with Rényi index γ ≤ 1 \gamma\leq 1 for all temperatures T > 0 T>0 . Furthermore, for general γ > 0 \gamma>0 we establish the asymptotics of the entropies for large T T and large scaling parameter α > 0 \alpha>0 for two different regimes – for fixed chemical potential μ ∈ ℝ \mu\in\mathbbm{R} and also for fixed particle density ρ > 0 \rho>0 . In particular, we thereby provide the last remaining building block for complete proofs of our low- and high-temperature results presented (for γ = 1 \gamma=1 ) in J. Phys. A: Math. Theor. 49 , 30LT04 (2016) [Corrigendum: 50 , 129501 (2017)], but being supported there only by the basic proof ideas.

Introduction

This section briefly describes the physical background, introduces the most important mathematical definitions, and provides a summary of the main results.

1.1
Physical background

Over the last decades the so-called entanglement entropy (EE) has become a useful single-number quantifier of non-classical correlations between subsystems of a composite quantum-mechanical system
Amico08
;
HHHH09
. For example, one may imagine a macroscopically large system consisting of a huge number of particles in the state of thermal equilibrium at some (absolute) temperature
T\geq 0
. All the particles inside some bounded spatial region
\Lambda
may then be considered to constitute one subsystem and the particles outside of
\Lambda
another one. The corresponding EE, more precisely the spatially bipartite thermal EE, now quantifies, to some extent, how strongly these two subsystems are correlated “across the interface” between
\Lambda
and its complement.

In the simplified situation where the particles do not dynamically interact with each other, such as in the ideal gas or, slightly more general, in the free gas, all possible correlations are entirely due to either the Bose–Einstein or the
(Pauli–)
Fermi–Dirac statistics by the assumed indistinguishability of the (point-like and spinless) particles.The present study is devoted to the latter case. Accordingly, we consider the free Fermi gas
Fermi26
;
Balian92
;
Bratteli97
infinitely extended in the Euclidean space
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
of an arbitrary dimension
d\geq 1
. Although the fermions neither interact with each other nor with any externally applied field, their EE remains a complicated function of the region
\Lambda\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
which is difficult to study by analytical methods. In general one can only hope for estimates and asymptotic results for its (physically interesting) growth when
\Lambda
is replaced with
\alpha\Lambda
where the scaling parameter
\alpha>0
becomes large. A decisive progress towards the understanding of the growth of the EE at
T=0
, in other words of the ground-state EE, is due to Gioev and Klich
G06
;
GK06
. They observed, remarkably enough, that this growth is related to a conjecture of Harold Widom
W82
;
W90
about the quasi-classical Szegő-type asymptotics for traces of (smooth) functions of multi-dimensional versions of truncated Wiener–Hopf operators with discontinuous symbols. After Widom’s conjecture had been proved by one of us
Sobolev2013
;
S15
the gate stood open to confirm
LSS14
the precise (leading) large-scale growth conjectured in
GK06
and, in addition, to establish its extension from the von Neumann EE to the whole one-parameter family of (quantum) Rényi EE’s.

In the present study we only consider the case of a true thermal state characterized by a
strictly
positive temperature
T>0
(and a chemical potential
\mu\in\mathbbm{R}
or, equivalently, a spatial particle-number density
\rho>0
). On the one hand, the case
T>0
is simpler, because the
Fermi function

E\mapsto 1/\big(1+\exp(E/T)\big)
on the real line
\mathbbm{R}
is smooth in contrast to its zero-temperature limit, the Heaviside unit-step function. On the other hand, a reasonable definition of the EE is more complicated, because the thermal state is not a pure state, see (
1.8
) below. Nevertheless, due to the presence of the “smoothing parameter”
T>0
the leading asymptotic growth of the EE as
\alpha\to\infty
, is determined by an asymptotic coefficient again going back to Widom
W60
;
Widom1985
, see also
Rocca84
;
BuBu91
. We introduce it in (
2.4
) below and denote it by
\mathcal{B}
.

From a physical point of view it is interesting to study the scaling asymptotics
\alpha\to\infty
as the temperature
T
varies. The emerging double asymptotics of the EE and of the coefficient
\mathcal{B}
are not simple to analyze and hard to guess by heuristic arguments.
For low temperatures, that is, small
T>0
, this analysis has been performed in
LeSpSo3
for
d=1
and in
S17
for
d\geq 2
yielding a result consistent with that for
T=0
in
GK06
;
LSS14
.

At high temperatures quantum effects become weaker and the free Fermi gas should exhibit properties of the corresponding classical free gas without correlations (for fixed particle density). In particular, the ideal Fermi gas
Fermi26
;
Balian92
should behave like the Maxwell–Boltzmann gas, the time-honored “germ cell” of modern statistical mechanics. Hence the main purpose of our study is to determine the precise two-parameter asymptotics of the EE as
\alpha\to\infty
and
T\to\infty
.

1.2
Pseudo-differential operators and entropies

At first we introduce the translation invariant pseudo-differential operator

LeSpSo3

\bigl(\op_{\alpha}(a)u\bigr)(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{\alpha^{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}\times\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\alpha\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})}a(\boldsymbol{\xi})u(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\,.

Here the smooth real-valued function

is its underlying

,

is an arbitrary complex-valued Schwartz function,
{\mathrm{i}}
denotes the imaginary unit, and
\alpha>0
is the scaling parameter. Informally, one may think of

as the function

of the gradient operator
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\coloneqq(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}})
, that is, the vector of partial derivatives with respect to
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
.

The main role will be played by the
truncated Wiener–Hopf operator

W_{\alpha}(a,\Lambda)\coloneqq\chi_{\Lambda}\op_{\alpha}(a)\chi_{\Lambda}\,,

where
\chi_{\Lambda}
is the (multiplication operator corresponding to the) indicator function of the “truncating” open set
\Lambda\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
.
Clearly, for a bounded symbol
a
the operators
\op_{\alpha}(a)
and
W_{\alpha}(a;\Lambda)
are bounded on the Hilbert space
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
. Given a
test function

f:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}
, we are interested in the operator
f\big(W_{\alpha}(a,\Lambda)\big)
and in the operator difference

D_{\alpha}(a,\Lambda;f)\coloneqq\chi_{\Lambda}f\big(W_{\alpha}(a,\Lambda)\big)\chi_{\Lambda}-W_{\alpha}(f\circ a,\Lambda)\,,

(1.2)

where the symbol
f\circ a
is the composition of
f
and
a
defined by
(f\circ a)(\boldsymbol{\xi})\coloneqq f\big(a(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)
.

For a bounded
\Lambda
and suitable
a
and
f
both operators on the right-hand side of (
1.2
) belong individually to the trace class. Remarkably, its difference does so even for a large class of unbounded
\Lambda
, see Condition
2.1
and Proposition
2.6
below. Our analysis of the scaling behavior of the entropies will be based on the asymptotics for the trace of
D_{\alpha}(a,\Lambda;f)
as
\alpha\to\infty
. The reciprocal parameter
\alpha^{-1}
can be naturally viewed as the Planck constant, and hence the limit
\alpha\to\infty
can be regarded as the quasi-classical limit. By a straightforward change of variables the operator (
1.2
) is seen to be unitarily equivalent to
D_{1}(a,\alpha\Lambda;f)
, so that
\alpha\to\infty
can be interpreted also as a spatial large-scale limit. In our large-scale applications it is either
\alpha
itself or a certain combination of
\alpha
with the temperature
T
that will become large.

The macroscopic thermal equilibrium state of the free Fermi gas depends, first of all, on its (classical) single-particle Hamiltonian
h:\mathbbm{R}^{d}\to\mathbbm{R}
, sometimes also called the energy-momentum (dispersion) relation. The minimal conditions that we impose on
h
are as follows. We assume that
h
is smooth in the sense that
h\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
and that it satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{n}h(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big|\lesssim|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2m}\quad\text{for all}\,\,n\in\mathbbm{N}_{0}^{d}\,,\textup{ and }\,h(\boldsymbol{\xi})\gtrsim|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2m}\,\,\textup{for}\,\,|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\gtrsim 1\,,

(1.3)

for some constant
m>0
. Here
\mathbbm{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbbm{N}\cup\{0\}
and the notations
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{n}
,
\lesssim
,
\gtrsim
are defined at the end of the Introduction.

For given
h
, temperature
T>0
, and chemical potential
\mu\in\mathbbm{R}
we introduce the
Fermi symbol
as the composition of the Fermi function and the “shifted” Hamiltonian
h-\mu
by

a_{T,\mu}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\coloneqq\frac{1}{1+\exp{\big(\frac{h(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\mu}{T}\big)}}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\,.

(1.4)

Next, we introduce the (bounded and continuous)
entropy function

\eta_{\gamma}:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathopen{[}0,\ln(2)\mathclose{]}
for each
Rényi index

\gamma>0
. If
t\not\in{]0,1[}
, we set
\eta_{\gamma}(t)\coloneqq 0
for any
\gamma>0
. If
t\in{]0,1[)}
and
\gamma\not=1
we define

\eta_{\gamma}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\ln\big[t^{\gamma}+(1-t)^{\gamma}\big]\,.

(1.5)

The von Neumann case,
\gamma=1
, is then given by the point-wise limit

\eta_{1}(t)\coloneqq\lim_{\gamma\to 1}\eta_{\gamma}(t)=-t\ln(t)-(1-t)\ln(1-t)\,.

(1.6)

Finally, we define the (Rényi)
local entropy
associated with a
bounded
region
\Lambda
as the trace

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\Lambda)\coloneqq\tr\eta_{\gamma}\big(W_{1}(a_{T,\mu},\Lambda)\big)\geq 0\,,

(1.7)

and the (Rényi)
entanglement entropy
(EE) for the bipartition
\mathbbm{R}^{d}=\Lambda\cup(\mathbbm{R}^{d}\setminus\Lambda)
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\Lambda)\coloneqq\tr D_{1}(a_{T,\mu},\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma})+\tr D_{1}(a_{T,\mu},\mathbbm{R}^{d}\setminus\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma})\,.

(1.8)

This definition is motivated by the notion of mutual information, see e.g.
(
Amico08
, Eq. (9))
. The conditions (
1.3
) guarantee that these entropies are well-defined, see the paragraph after Proposition
2.6
. In formula (
1.8
) either
\Lambda
or its complement
\mathbbm{R}^{d}\setminus\Lambda
is assumed to be bounded, see Section
2
for details. It is useful to observe, as in
(
LeSpSo2
, Eq. (26))
, that the local entropy (
1.7
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\Lambda)=s_{\gamma}(T,\mu)\,|\Lambda|+\tr D_{1}(a_{T,\mu},\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma})\,.

(1.9)

Here
|\Lambda|
is the volume (Lebesgue measure) of the bounded region
\Lambda
and

\displaystyle s_{\gamma}(T,\mu)\coloneqq\frac{\tr W_{1}(\eta_{\gamma}\circ a_{T,\mu},\Lambda)}{|\Lambda|}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\eta_{\gamma}\big(a_{T,\mu}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

(1.10)

is the spatial
entropy density
.
For
\gamma=1
it is the usual thermal entropy density
Balian92
;
Bratteli97
.

1.3
Summary of the main results

Our first result is of non-asymptotic nature. In Section
3
we explore concavity properties of the function
\eta_{\gamma}
. First we notice that
\eta_{\gamma}
is concave on the unit interval
{[0,1]}
for all
\gamma\in{]0,2]}
, so that for a bounded
\Lambda
one can use a Jensen-type trace inequality to establish a lower bound for the local entropy (
1.7
) in terms of the entropy density (
1.10
), see Theorem
3.3
. For the EE (
1.8
) this argument is not applicable, but we observe that
\eta_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in{]0,1]}
is even
operator concave
so that the Davis operator inequality (
3.4
) implies the positivity of the EE for
\gamma\in{]0,1]}
(including the most important case
\gamma=1
corresponding to the von Neumann EE), see Theorem
3.5
. We do not know whether the EE is positive for
\gamma\in{]1,2]}
. Later on however, we will see that the EE for
\gamma\in{]1,2]}
is positive at least for large
T
, see Remarks
2
and
5
in Subsection
6.3
.

The other main objective of the present paper is to study the asymptotics of
{\mathrm{H}}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\alpha\Lambda)
as
\alpha\to\infty
and
T\to\infty
. For this, we have to impose conditions on the Hamiltonian
h
stronger than those in (
1.3
), see Subsection
5.1
. In particular,
h
should be asymptotically homogeneous as
|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\to\infty
. We distinguish between two high-temperature cases: the case of a constant chemical potential
\mu\in\mathbbm{R}
and the case where the mean particle density

\displaystyle\varrho(T,\mu)\coloneqq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}a_{T,\mu}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,,

(1.11)

being finite by (
1.3
), is (asymptotically) fixed to a prescribed constant
\rho>0
, as
T
becomes large. The latter case implies that the chemical potential effectively becomes a function of
T
(and
\rho
) in the sense that

\displaystyle\varrho(T,\mu_{\rho}(T))\to\rho\quad\textup{as}\quad T\to\infty\,.

(1.12)

This corresponds to the quasi-classical limit of the free Fermi gas at fixed particle density. Indeed, since the so-called integrated density of states

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T)\coloneqq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{h(\boldsymbol{\xi})<T}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

(1.13)

of the single-particle Hamiltonian
h
, evaluated at
T
, tends to infinity as
T\to\infty
, one has
\rho/\mathcal{N}(T)\to 0
. In physical terms, the spatial density of the number of particles is much smaller than that of the number of occupiable single-particle states with (eigen)energies below
T
, when
T
is sufficiently large. Therefore the restrictions by the Pauli exclusion principle are negligible.

Our results on the high-temperature scaling asymptotics are presented in Theorem
6.1
(for constant
\mu
) and in Theorem
6.3
(for constant
\rho
). We postpone the discussion of these theorems until Section
6
. Here we only mention two important facts: a) in both regimes the asymptotics still hold if
T\to\infty
and the scaling parameter
\alpha
is fixed, e.g.
\alpha=1
, b) the EE with Rényi index
\gamma>2
becomes
negative
for fixed particle density at high temperature; the same happens for fixed chemical potential at large
\gamma
. This suggests that values
\gamma>2
are only of limited physical interest.

The main asymptotic orders as
\alpha\to\infty
and
T\to\infty
have been presented (without precise pre-factors and proofs) in
LeSpSo2
, in the cases of fixed
\mu
and fixed
\rho
, for
\gamma=1
and all
d\geq 1
. Here we provide complete proofs for all
\gamma>0
, but concentrate on the multi-dimensional case
d\geq 2
. The case
d=1
is not considered for lack of space.

Basic definitions and basic facts

In this section we collect some definitions and facts from
Sobolev2019
concerning the trace of (
1.2
) and its asymptotic evaluation. They are instrumental in the proof of our main asymptotic results corresponding to
a=a_{T,\mu}
and
f=\eta_{\gamma}
. Throughout the rest of the paper we always assume
d\geq 2
for the spatial dimension.

2.1
Conditions on the truncating region
\Lambda

We call an open and connected set
\Lambda\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
a Lipschitz domain, if it can be described locally as the epigraph of a Lipschitz function, see
Sobolev2014
;
S15
for details. We call
\Lambda
a
Lipschitz region
, if it is a union of finitely many Lipschitz domains such that their closures are pair-wise disjoint. From now on we always assume that
\Lambda
satisfies the following condition. Nevertheless, for convenience we will often mention it.

Condition 2.1

1.

The set
\Lambda\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
is a Lipschitz region, and either
\Lambda
or
\mathbbm{R}^{d}\setminus\Lambda
is bounded.

2.

The boundary (surface)
\partial\Lambda
is piece-wise
\textup{{{C}}}^{1}
-smooth.

We note that
\Lambda
and
\mathbbm{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Lambda}
satisfy Condition
2.1
simultaneously.

2.2
The asymptotic coefficient
\mathcal{B}
and its basic properties

We assume the real-valued symbol to be smooth in the sense that
a\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{d})
and satisfies the decay condition

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{n}a(\boldsymbol{\xi})\big|\lesssim\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle^{-\beta}\quad\text{ with some constant }\quad\beta>d\,,

(2.1)

for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
and all
n\in\mathbbm{N}_{0}^{d}
with some implicit constants that may depend on
n
.

Before stating the leading asymptotic formula for
\tr D_{\alpha}(a,\Lambda;f)
as
\alpha\to\infty
, we need to introduce the corresponding asymptotic coefficient. For a function
f:\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}
and any
u,v\in\mathbbm{R}
we consider the integral

U(u,v;f)\,\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f\bigl(tu+(1-t)v\bigr)-\big[tf(u)+(1-t)f(v)\big]}{t(1-t)}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

(2.2)

It is well-defined for any Hölder continuous
f
. And it is positive if
f
is also concave.

For every unit vector
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
we define a functional of the symbol
a
by the principal-value integral:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(a,\mathbf{e};f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\int_{|t|>\varepsilon}\frac{U\bigl(a(\boldsymbol{\xi}),a(\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\mathbf{e});f\bigr)}{t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(2.3)

Finally we define the main asymptotic coefficient by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(a,\partial\Lambda;f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\int_{\partial\Lambda}\mathcal{A}(a,\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{x}};f)\,\sigma(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\,,

(2.4)

where
\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{x}}
is the (unit outward) normal vector at the point
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Lambda
and
\sigma
is the canonical
(d-1)
-dimensional area measure on the boundary surface
\partial\Lambda
.

For a function
f\in\textup{{{C}}}^{2}(\mathbbm{R})
with bounded second derivative and for a symbol
a
obeying the condition (
2.1
) the integral (
2.3
) exists in the usual sense and is bounded uniformly in
\mathbf{e}
. Hence (
2.4
) is also finite, see
(
Sobolev2019
, Section 3)
. However, in order to accommodate the entropy function
\eta_{\gamma}
we allow for test functions being non-smooth in the sense of the following condition.

Condition 2.2

The function
f
is in
\textup{{{C}}}^{2}(\mathbbm{R}\setminus\mathcal{T})\cap\textup{{{C}}}(\mathbbm{R})
, where
\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{N}\}
is a finite set of its singular points. Moreover, for some
\delta>0
and all
R>0
the function
f\eqqcolon f^{(0)}
and its first two derivatives satisfy the bounds

\big|f^{(k)}(t)\big|\lesssim\sum_{u\in\mathcal{T}}|t-u|^{\delta-k}\,,\quad k\in\{0,1,2\}\,,

(2.5)

for all
t\in[-R,R]\setminus\mathcal{T}
with an implicit constant that may depend on
R
.

Under this condition
\mathcal{B}(a,\partial\Lambda;f)
is finite:

Proposition 2.3

(
Sobolev2019
, Corollary 3.4)

Let the set
\Lambda
satisfy Condition
2.1
and let the function
f
satisfy Condition
2.2
with
\delta>0
. Finally, let the symbol
a
satisfy (
2.1
), but this time with some
\beta>d\,\max\{1,1/\delta\}
. Then the coefficient
\mathcal{B}(a,\partial\Lambda;f)
is finite.

We point out a few simple properties of this coefficient.

Remark 2.4

1.

Since
\mathcal{A}(a,\mathbf{e};f)=\mathcal{A}(a,-\mathbf{e};f)
, the coefficients
\mathcal{B}
for the regions
\Lambda

\|\,\cdot\,\|_{p}

3
The positivity of certain entanglement entropies

Given the organization of the paper, this section is a kind of interlude. It turns out that the property given by its title is present if the underlying entropy function
\eta_{\gamma}
, as defined in (
1.5

1.6

\mathbbm{R}
to its unit interval
{[0,1]}
). Since this and related results are not of asymptotic character, we assume in this section
\alpha=1
for the scaling parameter.

3.1
Concavity of the entropy function
\eta_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\leq 2

We prove the property given by the title of this subsection and then establish consequences for the corresponding local and entanglement entropies. The next lemma is elementary.

Lemma 3.1

The entropy function
\eta_{\gamma}
is concave on the interval
{[0,1]}
if
\gamma\in{]0,2]}
, and neither convave nor convex if
\gamma>2
.

Proof

By the continuity of
\eta_{\gamma}
on
{[0,1]}
its enough to check the sign of the second derivative of
\eta_{\gamma}
on the open interval
{]0,1[}
. For
\gamma=1
we simply have
\eta_{1}^{\prime\prime}(t)=-t^{-1}(1-t)^{-1}<0
so that
\eta_{1}
is concave. For
\gamma\not=1
we use the formula

\displaystyle\eta_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(t)[t^{\gamma}+(1-t)^{\gamma}\big]^{2}=-\gamma[t(1-t)]^{\gamma-2}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}[t^{\gamma-1}-(1-t)^{\gamma-1}]^{2}\,.

(3.1)

For
\gamma<1
the right-hand side is obviously negative for all
t\in{]0,1[}
. For
\gamma=2
it simply equals
-8t(1-t)<0
. If
\gamma>2
, then (
3.1
) implies
\eta_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\gamma/(\gamma-1)>0
and
\eta_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(1/2)=-4\gamma<0
. Hence
\eta_{\gamma}
is neither concave nor convex.

It remains to consider the case
\gamma\in{]1,2[}
. We rewrite (
3.1
) as

\displaystyle\eta_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(t)[t^{\gamma}+(1-t)^{\gamma}]^{2}=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}g_{\gamma-1}(t)\,,

\displaystyle g_{p}(t)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ p[t(1-t)]^{p-1}-[t^{p}-(1-t)^{p}]^{2}\,,

for any
p\coloneqq\gamma-1\in{]0,1[}
. Our claim
g_{p}(t)\geq 0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle[t(1-t)]^{1-p}[t^{2p}+(1-t)^{2p}]\leq 2t(1-t)+p\,.

(3.2)

Using the abbreviation

\displaystyle M\coloneqq 2^{p-1}\max_{t\in[0,1]}[t^{2p}+(1-t)^{2p}]=\begin{cases}2^{-p}&\quad\textup{if}\quad 0<p<1/2\\
2^{p-1}&\quad\textup{if}\quad 1/2\leq p<1\end{cases}\,,

the (elementary example of the) Young inequality

\displaystyle ab\leq\frac{a^{u}}{u}+\frac{b^{v}}{v}\,,\quad a,b\geq 0\,,\quad u,v>1\,,\,\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}=1

for
a=[t(1-t)]^{1-p}\,,u=(1-p)^{-1}
and
b=1,v=p^{-1}
yields

\displaystyle[(t(1-t)]^{1-p}[t^{2p}+(1-t)^{2p}]\leq

\displaystyle\ M[2t(1-t)]^{1-p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ M(1-p)[2t(1-t)]+Mp\leq 2t(1-t)+p\,.

Since this coincides with (
3.2
), the proof is complete.

The just established concavity is useful to find a lower bound on the local entropy (
1.7
) with
\gamma\leq 2
, which is larger than the obvious bound
0
. To this end, we recall a formulation
(
LapSaf1996
, Theorem A.1)
of an abstract Jensen-type trace inequality dating back to Berezin
Ber72
.

Proposition 3.2

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex separable Hilbert space,
P
an orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{H}
,
A
a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
with its spectrum contained in the interval
I\subset\mathbbm{R}
, and
f:I\to\mathbbm{R}
a concave function. Finally, let
\Delta\coloneqq Pf(PAP)P-Pf(A)P
be of trace class and PAP compact. Then
\tr\Delta\geq 0
. If
\Delta
and
PAP
are of trace class, than also the following trace inequality is valid:

\displaystyle\tr(Pf(PAP)P)\geq\tr(Pf(A)P)\,.

(If
0\notin I
, then the operator
f(PAP)
is understood to act on the subspace
P\mathcal{H}
).

The following result is a corollary to Proposition
3.2

be bounded and satisfy Condition

satisfies (

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\Lambda)\geq s_{\gamma}(T,\mu)|\Lambda|\,,

(3.3)

where

is the entropy density (

Proof

We use Proposition
3.2
with
A=\op_{1}(a_{T,\mu})
,

, and the concave function

3.2
Operator concavity of the entropy function
\eta_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\leq 1

For the general background of this genre we recommend Simon’s comprehensive book
Simon2019
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex separable Hilbert space of infinite dimension and
\{A,B\}
an arbitrary pair of bounded self-adjoint operators on
\mathcal{H}
with spectra in an interval
I\subset\mathbbm{R}
. A continuous function
f:I\to\mathbbm{R}
is called (decreasing)
operator monotone
if the (operator) inequality
f(A)\geq f(B)
holds whenever
A\geq B
. Likewise it is called
operator concave
if
f\big(qA+(1-q)B\big)\geq qf(A)+(1-q)f(B)
holds for all
q\in{[0,1]}
. It is called
operator convex
if
-f
is operator concave. Of course, every operator monotone (operator concave)
function is monotone (concave). We are going to use the following standard examples, see
And
:

1.

The function
t\mapsto t^{p}
,
t\in{[0,\infty[},
is operator monotone and operator concave if
p\in{]0,1]}
.

2.

The function
t\mapsto\ln(t)
,
t\in{]0,\infty[}
, is operator monotone and operator concave.

3.

The function
t\mapsto-t\ln(t)
,
t\in{[0,\infty[}
, is operator concave.

Any operator concave function
f
satisfies
Dav
;
HP03
the following
Davis operator inequality
for all bounded self-adjoint operators
A
with spectrum in
I
and all orthogonal projections
P
on
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle Pf\big(PAP\big)P\geq Pf(A)P\,.

(3.4)

If
0\notin I
, then the operator
f(PAP)
is understood to act on the subspace
P\mathcal{H}
. If
0\in I
and
f(0)=0
, then (
3.4
) may be shortened to
f\big(PAP\big)\geq Pf(A)P
.

Lemma 3.4

If
\gamma\in{]0,1]}
, then
\eta_{\gamma}
is operator concave on the interval
{[0,1]}
.

Proof

It suffices to consider self-adjoint operators
A
and
B
with
0\leq A,B\leq\mathbbm{1}
. Assume first that
\gamma<1
. The function
g_{\gamma}(t)\coloneqq t^{\gamma}+(1-t)^{\gamma}
is operator concave on
{[0,1]}
(by example 1 above) and the logarithm is operator monotone on
{]0,1]}
(example 2). Thus for all
q\in{[0,1]}
we have

\displaystyle\eta_{\gamma}\big(qA+(1-q)B\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\ln\big[g_{\gamma}\big(qA+(1-q)B\big)\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\ln\big[qg_{\gamma}\big(A\big)+(1-q)g_{\gamma}\big(B\big)\big]\,.

Now, since the logarithm is also operator concave on
{]0,1]}
(again example 2), the right-hand side is larger than or equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\big[q\ln(g_{\gamma}(A))+(1-q)\ln(g_{\gamma}(B))\big]=q\eta_{\gamma}(A)+(1-q)\eta_{\gamma}(B)\,.

Hence
\eta_{\gamma}
, for
\gamma<1
, is operator concave on
{]0,1]}
and, by continuity, on
{[0,1]}
. For
\gamma=1
we proceed more directly and use that
g(t)\coloneqq-t\ln(t)
is operator concave on
{[0,1]}
(example 3).This immediately implies that
\eta_{1}(t)=g(t)+g(1-t)
is also operator concave on
{[0,1]}
.

Now we can use the inequality (
3.4
) with
f=\eta_{\gamma}
(for
\gamma\in{]0,1]}
),
P=\chi_{\Lambda}
, and
A=\op_{1}(a_{T,\mu})
. Combining this with Proposition
2.6
yields the following result.

Theorem 3.5

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
satisfy Condition
2.1
. Assume that the Hamiltonian
h
is as in (
1.3
) and that
\gamma\in{]0,1]}
. Then both operators

This method cannot be used for the EE with
\gamma>1
because of the following negative result.

Theorem 3.6

If
\gamma>1
, then
\eta_{\gamma}
is not operator concave on the interval
{[0,1]}
.

Proof

For convenience, instead of
\eta_{\gamma}
we consider the function

\displaystyle g_{\gamma}(u)\coloneqq-\eta_{\gamma}\big(u+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\big)=\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\,\ln\big[({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}-u)^{\gamma}+({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}+u)^{\gamma}\big]\,,\quad u\in\big[-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}},{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\big]\,.

Our objective is to show that
g_{\gamma}
is not operator convex on
[-1/2,1/2]
. If
g_{\gamma}
were operator convex, then by
(
BeSh
, Corollary 1)
,
g_{\gamma}
would be analytic on the complex plane with cuts along the half-lines
]-\infty,-1/2[
and
]1/2,\infty[
. Let us prove that such an analytic continuation of

is impossible. To this end, let
u={\mathrm{i}}y/2
with

. Then

{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\pm u={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1+y^{2}}\,\exp[\pm{\mathrm{i}}\tan^{-1}(y)]

so that

\displaystyle({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}-u)^{\gamma}+({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}+u)^{\gamma}=2^{1-\gamma}(1+y^{2})^{\gamma/2}\,\cos[\gamma\tan^{-1}(y)]\,.

(3.5)

Since

has a branching point at

is not operator convex, as claimed.

4

In this section we collect some bounds for the Schatten–von Neumann classes
\mathfrak{S}_{p}
,

\mathcal{H}
, see e.g.
(

, Chapter 11)
. As mentioned at the end of the Introduction, the functional
\|A\|_{p}\coloneqq(\tr(A^{*}A)^{p/2})^{1/p},\ A\in\mathfrak{S}_{p},
defines a norm for

\displaystyle\|A+B\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|A\|_{p}^{p}+\|B\|_{p}^{p}\,,\quad 0<p\leq 1.

(4.1)

This inequality is used systematically in what follows. The main part is played by estimates for

f(A)J-Jf(B)
with bounded

and bounded self-adjoint

. The following fact is adapted from
(
Sobolev2016
, Theorem 2.4)
.

Proposition 4.1

Suppose that the function
f
satisfies Condition
2.2
with some

. Let
A,B
be two bounded self-adjoint operators and let

be a bounded operator. Suppose that

where

satisfies

\|f(A)J-Jf(B)\|_{1}\lesssim\|J\|^{1-q}\big(1+\|A\|^{\delta-q}+\|B\|^{\delta-q}\big)\|AJ-JB\|_{q}^{q}\,,

(4.2)

with a constant independent of
A,B
, and
J
. This constant may depend on the set
\mathcal{T}
in Condition
2.2
, and is uniform in the set of functions
f
satisfying (
2.5
) with the same implicit constants.

Actually,
(
Sobolev2016
, Theorem 2.4)
provides bounds of the type (

) is sufficient.

All subsequent bounds involving the function

are uniform in

in the sense specified in Proposition
4.1

Corollary 4.2

Let

, and

\displaystyle[A,J]=[B,J]=0\,,\quad(A-B)J=0\,.

(4.3)

Then

,

Proof

For the proof of (

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A,P;f)J=

\displaystyle\ P\big(f(PAP)PJ-PJf(A)\big)-P[f(A),PJ]\,.

\displaystyle\|Pf(PAP)

\displaystyle PJ-\ PJf(A)\|_{1}\leq\|f(PAP)PJ-PJf(A)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\ \|PAPJ-PJA\|_{q}^{q}\lesssim\ \|P(AJ-JA)P\|_{q}^{q}+\|[P,J]\|_{q}^{q}+\|[JA,P]\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\ \|[P,J]\|_{q}^{q}+\|[JA,P]\|_{q}^{q}\,.

Adding up these two estimates we arrive at (

For the proof of (

\displaystyle Pf(PAP)PJ-JPf(PBP)P

\displaystyle=\ P\big(f(PAP)J-Jf(PBP)\big)P

\displaystyle\ +Pf(PAP)[P,J]-[J,P]f(PBP)P\,.

\displaystyle\|P\big(f(PAP)J-Jf(PBP)\big)P\|_{1}\lesssim\|PAPJ-JPBP\|_{q}^{q}\leq\|[J,P]\|_{q}^{q}\,.

To estimate the last two terms on the right-hand side of (
Proof

We are going to derive such a bound also for the difference analogous to (

\displaystyle Pf(A)PJ-JPf(B)P=

\displaystyle\ P\big(f(A)J-Jf(B)\big)P

\delta=1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(p_{T};\tilde{f}_{T})-D_{\alpha}(w_{rT}\,p_{T};\tilde{f}_{T})=

\displaystyle\ \big[D_{\alpha}(p_{T};\tilde{\eta}_{T})-D_{\alpha}(w_{rT}\,p_{T};\tilde{\eta}_{T})\big]

\displaystyle\ \quad+\big[D_{\alpha}(p_{T};\tilde{g}_{T})-D_{\alpha}(w_{rT}\,p_{T};\tilde{g}_{T})\big]\,,

where
\tilde{\eta}_{T}(t)\coloneqq\lambda_{T}^{-1}\eta(\lambda_{T}\,t)
and
\tilde{g}_{T}(t)\coloneqq\lambda_{T}^{-1}g(\lambda_{T}\,t)
. As in the previous calculation, the second term is estimated with the help of (
5.11
) by
r^{\frac{d-1}{2m}}
. Since
\tilde{\eta}_{T}(t)=\eta(t)-t\ln(\lambda_{T})
and the operator difference (
1.2
) vanishes for linear functions
f
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(p_{T};\tilde{\eta}_{T})-D_{\alpha}(w_{rT}\,p_{T};\tilde{\eta}_{T})=D_{\alpha}(p_{T};\eta)-D_{\alpha}(w_{rT}\,p_{T};\eta)\,.

The function
\eta
from (
2.9
) satisfies (
5.17
) for all
\delta<1
, and hence by (
5.11
) the above difference is again estimated by
r^{\frac{d-1}{2m}}
. This entails (
5.9
) and the proof of Theorem
5.2
is complete.

6
Main results on the high-temperature asymptotics

In this section we adapt Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
to two different asymptotic regimes of the entanglement entropy (

Bratteli97

6.1
Case of a fixed chemical potential
\mu

Since the Fermi symbol

Let the truncating region

\displaystyle\lim\big(\alpha T^{\textstyle\frac{1}{2m}}\big)^{1-d}\,\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\alpha\Lambda)=2\,\mathcal{B}\big((1+\mathrm{e}^{h_{\infty}})^{-1},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma}\big)

, as

\displaystyle a_{T,\mu_{\rho}(T)}=\lambda_{T}p_{T}\,,

6.3
Concluding remarks

1.

We have proved the positivity of the EE for Rényi index
\gamma\leq 1
, see Theorem
3.5
. For bounded
\Lambda
we have actually the stronger statement:

0\leq\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\Lambda)-s_{\gamma}(T,\mu)|\Lambda|\leq\mathrm{H}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\Lambda)\,,\quad\gamma\leq 1\,.{}

Here the first inequality for the local entropy is due to Theorem
3.3
and holds even for
\gamma\leq 2
. The second one follows by combining (
1.8
), (
1.9
), and Theorem
3.5
. Although Theorem
3.5
ensures the positivity of the EE only for
\gamma\leq 1
, its asymptotic coefficient
\mathcal{B}(a_{T,\mu},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma})
is positive for all
\gamma\leq 2
(and all
\mu\in\mathbbm{R}
,
T>0
), see Remark
2.4
(2). It is an open question whether the positivity of the EE itself extends to all
\gamma\leq 2
.

2.

In case of a fixed
\rho
(Theorem
6.3
) and
\gamma\leq 2
, the function
\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}
in Table
1
is strictly concave so that, by
h_{\infty}\geq 0
and Remark
2.4
, the coefficient
\mathcal{B}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-h_{\infty}},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}\big)
is strictly positive. On the other hand, if
\gamma>2
, then
\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}
is strictly convex so that this coefficient is strictly
negative
. This change of sign, unexpected by us, suggests that our definition (
1.8
) of the EE is somewhat unphysical for
\gamma>2
.
In passing we note that
\mathcal{B}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-h_{\infty}},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}\big)
for
\gamma>2
can be computed rather explicitly. Indeed, by Table
1
the
integral (
2.2
) becomes simply:

U\big(u,v;\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}\big)=-\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma-1)}(u-v)^{2}\,,\quad\gamma>2\,.{}

This observation and the use of Parseval’s identity, as in the proof of
(
Widom1982
, Proposition 1)
, enables us to determine the resulting quantities (
2.3
) and hence (
2.4
) in terms of the Fourier transform of
\mathrm{e}^{-h_{\infty}}
.
In particular, for the quadratic “asymptotic” Hamiltonian
h_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}/2
(which includes the Hamiltonian of the ideal Fermi gas) we obtain the formula

\mathcal{B}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-h_{\infty}},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}\big)=-\frac{1}{4}(4\pi)^{-(d+1)/2}\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}|\partial\Lambda|\,,\quad\gamma>2\,.

3.

For
\gamma<2
the function
\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}
is exactly the classical entropy function of the Maxwell–Boltzmann gas. This confirms our expectations stated in the Introduction. This conclusion does not hold for
\gamma\geq 2
.

4.

In case of a fixed
\mu
(Theorem
6.1
) and
\gamma\leq 2
we know that the coefficient
\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}:=\mathcal{B}(p_{\infty},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma})
, with

p_{\infty}=1/(1+\mathrm{e}^{h_{\infty}})
, on the right-hand side of (
6.1
) is positive by the strict concavity of
\eta_{\gamma}
. Here we want to indicate that
\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}
should become negative for large
\gamma
. To this end, we consider the point-wise limit:

\eta_{\infty}(t)\coloneqq\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\eta_{\gamma}(t)=\min\big\{-\ln(1-t),-\ln(t)\big\}\,,\quad t\in[0,1]\,.

This function is (strictly) convex on
{[0,1/2]}
and also on
{[1/2,1]}
, but not on the whole interval
{[0,1]}
. Fortunately however, since
p_{\infty}\leq 1/2
by our assumptions in Subsection
5.1
, the integral
U\big(u,v;\eta_{\infty}\big)
from (
2.2
) underlying
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
enters only for
u,v\in{[0,1/2]}
so that

\mathcal{B}_{\infty}=\mathcal{B}\big(p_{\infty},\partial\Lambda;-\ln(1-\cdot)\big)<0\,.

To get more specific about the value of
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
we observe that for
u,v\in[0,1/2]
:

\displaystyle U\big(u,v;\eta_{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle=-U\big(u,v;\ln(1-\cdot)\big)=-U\big(1-u,1-v;\ln(\cdot)\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\big(\ln(1-u)-\ln(1-v)\big)^{2}\,,

where the last equality is an elementary calculation, see again
Widom1982
. It reconfirms the negativity of
U
. Introducing the symbol
q_{\infty}\coloneqq\ln(1+\mathrm{e}^{-h_{\infty}})
we get

U\big(p_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),p_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\mathbf{e});\eta_{\infty}\big)=-\frac{1}{2}\big(q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\xi}+t\mathbf{e})\big)^{2}\,.

Similarly to Remark
2
, the coefficient
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
can now be found in terms of the Fourier transform of (the Taylor series of)
q_{\infty}
. In particular, for
h_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{2}/2
the coefficient
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
can be computed explicitly:

\mathcal{B}\big((1+\mathrm{e}^{h_{\infty}})^{-1},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\infty}\big)=-\frac{1}{2}(2\pi)^{-(d+1)/2}\Sigma(d)|\partial\Lambda|\,,

where
\Sigma(d)\coloneqq\sum_{n,m\geq 1}(-1)^{n+m}(nm)^{-1/2}(n+m)^{-(d+1)/2}
. We omit the details. [Numerically we find e.g.
\Sigma(2)\approx 0.19798
and
\Sigma(3)\approx 0.15419
.]
Thus, assuming continuity of
\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}
as a function of
\gamma>0
, we can claim the existence of a finite value
\gamma_{0}>2
such that
\mathcal{B}_{\gamma_{0}}=0
and
\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}<0
for certain finite
\gamma>\gamma_{0}
.

5.

In both high-temperature regimes (fixed
\mu
or fixed
\rho
) the scaling parameter
\alpha
may be fixed, to
\alpha=1
, say. Then the truncating set
\alpha\Lambda
is fixed and only the temperature
T
tends to infinity.

6.

Using the relation (
1.9
) and Theorem
5.1
one can also derive appropriate asymptotic formulas for the local entropy. For example, assuming that
\mu
is fixed, as in Theorem
6.1
, we easily obtain the asymptotic relation

\displaystyle\lim\big(\alpha T^{\textstyle\frac{1}{2m}}\big)^{1-d}\,\big[\mathrm{S}_{\gamma}(T,\mu;\alpha\Lambda)-\alpha^{d}s_{\gamma}(T,\mu)\,|\Lambda|\big]=\mathcal{B}\big((1+\mathrm{e}^{h_{\infty}})^{-1},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma}\big)\,,

as
\alpha T^{\frac{1}{2m}}\,\to\infty
and
T\to\infty
. However, in order to obtain from this formula a proper asymptotic expansion for the local entropy, one needs to find an expansion for the entropy density
s_{\gamma}(T,\mu)
as
T\to\infty
. A convenient starting point for its derivation could be, for example, the representation (10.8) in
LeSpSo3
.

An analogous formula can be written down for the case of a fixed
\rho
. The inequality
\mathcal{B}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-h_{\infty}},\partial\Lambda;\eta_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{eff}}\big)<0
for
\gamma>2
would then imply that the local entropy obeys for large
T
the bound

{\mathrm{S}}_{\gamma}\big(T,\mu_{\rho}(T);\Lambda\big)<s_{\gamma}\big(T,\mu_{\rho}(T)\big)|\Lambda|
, instead of (
3.3
) which is valid for all
\gamma\leq 2
and all
T>0
.

Our main aim is to derive formula (
