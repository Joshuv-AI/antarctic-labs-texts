Unrestricted Red Size and Sign-Coherence
Tucker J. Ervin

Abstract
. The unrestricted red size of a quiver is the maximal number of red vertices in its framed quiver after any given mutation sequence. In a 2023 paper by E. Bucher and J. Machacek, it was shown that connected, mutation-finite quivers either have an unrestricted red size of n − 1 n-1 or n n , where n n is the number of vertices in the quiver. We prove here that the same holds for the connected, mutation-infinite case using forks. As such, the unrestricted red size for any quiver equals n − c n-c , where c c is the number of connected components of the quiver that do not admit a reddening sequence. Additionally, we prove a result on the c c -vectors of forks that allows us to show that the c c -vectors of both abundant acyclic quivers on any number of vertices and mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices are sign-coherent with only elementary methods.



1.
Introduction

Maximal green sequences were formally dubbed such in a 2010 talk by B. Keller, which was later compiled into a series of lecture notes
[
12
]
.
Both maximal green sequences and reddening sequences are special sequences of mutations that end in quivers with all red vertices, and they have powerful implications for the cluster algebras and Donald-Thompson invariants associated to quivers that admit them
[
5
]
.
Not every quiver admits a maximal green sequence or a reddening sequence, and in 2020 Ahmad and Li created a measure, the red size
Red
, which counts the maximal number of red vertices in a quiver after mutating only at a green vertices
[
1
]
.
In 2023, Bucher and Machacek generalized this to reddening sequences with the unrestricted red size
uRed
, which counts the maximal number of red vertices after any given mutation sequence
[
3
]
.
Naturally, for a quiver
Q
on
n
vertices that admits a reddening sequence, we have that
\text{uRed}(Q)=n
.
The question then becomes: what is the unrestricted red size of
Q
when it does not admit a reddening sequence?

Bucher and Machacek conjectured the following for the case of connected quivers.

Conjecture 1.1
.

[
3
]

For any connected quiver
Q
on
n
vertices which does not admit a reddening sequence

(1)

We have
\text{Red}(Q)=n-1
.

(2)

We have
\text{Red}(Q)=n-1
and every vertex of
Q
is the last remaining green vertex after the application of some general maximal green sequence.

(3)

We have
\text{uRed}(Q)=n-1
.

(4)

We have
\text{uRed}(Q)=n-1
and every vertex of
Q
is the last remaining green vertex after the application of some general reddening sequence.

Answering any part of Conjecture
1.1
affirmatively would naturally imply their other conjecture —a generalization of the mutation-invariance of admitting a reddening sequence.

Conjecture 1.2
.

[
3
]

The unrestricted red size is a mutation-invariant.

Conjecture
1.1
was proven for the connected, mutation-finite case.
Using a special type of quiver called a fork, we extend their results to the connected, mutation-infinite case.
This proves part (3) of Conjecture
1.1
; however, the stronger variations of Conjecture
1.1
remain unresolved.

Corollary 1.3
.

Every connected, mutation-infinite quiver
Q
on
n
vertices has
uRed(Q)\geq n-1
.
Furthermore, if
Q
is any quiver on
n
vertices, then
\text{uRed}(Q)=n-c
, where
c
is the number of connected components of
Q
that do not admit reddening sequences.

To tackle this question, we explore a generalization of sign-coherence.
Instead of depending on the signs of the
c
-vectors arising from principal coefficients, we allow a broader class of starting coefficients.
Those ice quivers which still have their
c
-vectors satisfy the sign-coherence property under any mutation sequence are of particular interest.
We take the name
uniformly sign-coherent
for these ice quivers from a paper by Cao and Li
[
4
]
.

As sign-coherence for
c
-vectors is already a well-known and well-studied property of framed quivers
[
6
,
11
]
and all connected and mutation-infinite quivers are mutation-equivalent to a fork
[
15
]
, we demonstrate our result on the unrestricted red size by finding a sequence ending in
n-1
red vertices for arbitrary
strictly sign-coherent
—which disallows the zero vector as a
c
-vector—forks with
n
mutable vertices.
While exploring this topic, we found Proposition
5.2
: a way to describe the arrows between frozen and mutable vertices after mutating in a fork-preserving way.
This proposition has applications in a future work of the author and his collaborators, whose extended abstract was recently showcased at the 2024 FPSAC conference
[
8
]
.
Additionally, this argument can be modified slightly, culminating in Corollaries
5.6
and
5.7
.
The two corollaries prove that the
c
-vectors of both abundant acyclic quivers on any number of vertices and mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices are sign-coherent with only elementary methods.
Similarly, we expand slightly on Proposition
5.2
to show that mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices do not admit reddening sequences with only elementary methods.

As for the structure of the paper, we list the necessary preliminary material in Section
2
.
Section
3
develops the information on acyclic orderings that we need for Section
4
, where we prove our main result.
Finally, Section
5
gives some results on the general
c
-vectors of ice quivers.

3.
Acyclic Orderings

In order to keep track of the color of vertices in a fork, we will first need some results on the acyclic orderings arising from abundant acyclic quivers and forks.

Definition 3.1
.

[
2
]

Let
Q
be a quiver on
n
vertices.
Then we say that an ordering
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}
on the vertices is acyclic if whenever there exists an arrow
v_{i}\to v_{j}
then
v_{i}\prec v_{j}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

[
2
, Proposition 3.1.2]

Let
Q
be an acyclic quiver.
Then there exists an acyclic ordering of its vertices.

Naturally, if a quiver is abundant and acyclic, its acyclic ordering must be unique.
We can then track the acyclic orderings of every acyclic quiver in its source mutation cycle.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Q
be an abundant acyclic quiver, where
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}
is its unique acyclic ordering.
Then the mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}=[v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{j}]
is a source mutation sequence such that
Q^{j}=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}}(Q)
has the unique acyclic ordering

v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}\prec v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j}

for all
j\leq n
.

Proof.

Mutating
Q
at
v_{1}
will give us the quiver
Q^{1}
with unique acyclic ordering
v_{2}\prec v_{3}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}\prec v_{1}
, as the source of
Q
will become the sink of
Q^{1}
, affecting no other arrows.
As such, if we keep mutating along
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}
, we cycle through the acyclic ordering.
Thus
Q^{i}=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}}(Q)
has the unique acyclic ordering
v_{i+1}\prec v_{i+2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}\prec v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{i-1}\prec v_{i}
for all
i<n
.
If
i=n
, then we have arrived back at our original
Q
.
∎

As removing the point of a return from a fork produces an abundant acyclic quiver, we can also examine the acyclic ordering produced by subquivers of a fork after mutation not at the point of return.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
F
be a fork with point of return
r
, where
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}
is the unique acyclic ordering on
F\setminus\{r\}
.
For some vertex
v_{j}
, let
F^{\prime}=\mu_{v_{j}}(F)
.
Then
F^{\prime}\setminus\{v_{j}\}
has unique acyclic ordering

r\prec v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}

if
r\to v_{j}
in
F
.
If instead
v_{j}\to r
in
F
, we have the unique acyclic ordering

v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}\prec r

Proof.

First, note that the direction of arrows in
F\setminus\{r,v_{j}\}
is completely unchanged after mutation at
v_{j}
, as
F\setminus\{r\}
is abundant acyclic.
Thus the unique acyclic ordering of
F^{\prime}\setminus\{r,v_{j}\}
is
v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}
.
As
F^{\prime}\setminus\{v_{j}\}
is abundant acyclic, we need only add
r
into the ordering.

Before doing so, note that
r\to v_{1}
and
v_{n}\to r
in
F
, as forks do not have sources or sinks.
Now suppose that
r\to v_{j}
in
F
.
Then
r\to v_{1}
in
F^{\prime}
, and the unique acyclic ordering of
F^{\prime}\setminus\{v_{j}\}
is
r\prec v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}
.
If instead
v_{j}\to r
in
F
, then
v_{n}\to r
in
F^{\prime}
, and the unique acyclic ordering of
F^{\prime}\setminus\{v_{j}\}
is
v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}\prec r
.
∎

Lemmata
3.3
and
3.4
combine to give us a thorough understanding of the two acyclic orderings that can be found by performing source mutations on
F\setminus\{r\}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
F
be a fork with point of return
r
, where
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}
is the unique acyclic ordering on
F\setminus\{r\}
.
If
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}=[v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{j}]
for
j\leq n-1
, let
F^{j}=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}}(F)
.
Then
F^{j}\setminus\{v_{j}\}
has unique acyclic ordering

r\prec v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}\prec v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}

and
F^{j}\setminus\{r\}
has unique acyclic ordering

v_{j+1}\prec\dots\prec v_{n-1}\prec v_{1}\prec\dots\prec v_{j-1}\prec v_{j}.

Proof.

Lemma
3.3
proves the desired result for the acyclic ordering on

, as

is abundant acyclic and
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}
is a source mutation sequence.
We will then show that

by induction.
As
r\to v_{1}
in
F
, we know that
F^{1}\setminus\{v_{1}\}
must have acyclic ordering

4.
Sign-coherent Ice Quivers

We now give a definition of two different types of quivers with sign-coherent
c
-vectors, where the name uniformly sign-coherent comes from the term “uniform column sign-coherent” given by Cao and Li
[
4
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

An ice quiver is said to be
uniformly sign-coherent
if every quiver in its mutation class has every vertex red, green, or blue.
Equivalently, every
c
-vector has either negative sign, has positive sign, or is the zero vector.
In this paper, we assume that not every vertex of a uniformly sign-coherent quiver is blue.
If we do use such a quiver, we will refer to it as a
trivially sign-coherent
quiver.
Finally, if every quiver in a mutation class has every vertex exclusively red or green, we say it is
strictly sign-coherent
.
Equivalently, every
c
-vector has either negative or positive sign.

Introducing strictly sign-coherent quivers allows us to speak about ice quivers that are mutation equivalent to a framed or coframed quiver.
The sign-coherence theorem forces all such quivers to be strictly sign-coherent.
We will prove some of our results for the more general uniformly sign-coherent case, but the strictly sign-coherent case is all we need, beginning with a result that handles how colors change after mutation in relation to the acyclic ordering of an abundant acyclic quiver.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
Q
be an abundant acyclic and uniformly sign-coherent quiver, where
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}
is its unique acyclic ordering.
Pick a vertex
v_{j}
of
Q
for
j\in Q_{0}
, and let
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{v_{j}}(Q)
.

•

If
v_{j}
is blue in
Q
, then the color of every vertex is unchanged after mutation.

•

If
v_{j}
is green in
Q
, then the color of every vertex
v_{i}
of
Q
such that
v_{j}\prec v_{i}
is unchanged after mutation and
v_{j}
is red in
Q^{\prime}
.

•

If
v_{j}
is red in
Q
, then the color of every vertex
v_{i}
of
Q
such that
v_{i}\prec v_{j}
is unchanged after mutation and
v_{j}
is green in
Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

If
v_{j}
is blue in
Q
, then there are no arrows between
v_{j}
and frozen vertices in
Q
.
As such, mutation at
v_{j}
will neither add or subtract arrows between mutable and frozen vertices in
Q^{\prime}
.
Thus, the color of every vertex in
Q
is unchanged after mutation at
v_{j}
.

If
v_{j}
is green in
Q
, then there are arrows going from
v_{j}
to frozen vertices.
Thus, for any vertex
v_{i}
of
Q
such that
v_{j}\prec v_{i}
, we know that there is no path of length 2 from a frozen vertex to
v_{i}
or a path of length 2 from
v_{i}
to a frozen vertex passing through
v_{j}
.
This follows from the fact that
v_{j}\prec v_{i}
if and only if there exists and arrow
v_{j}\to v_{i}
.
Naturally, this means that color of the vertices
v_{i}
such that
v_{j}\prec v_{i}
is unchanged after mutation at
v_{j}
, and the color of
v_{j}
must be red in
Q^{\prime}
.
A similar argument proves our result for the case where
v_{j}
is red in
Q
.
∎

5.
Controlling c-vectors

While observing strictly sign-coherent forks, a pattern began to emerge in the number and direction of arrows between mutable and frozen vertices.
As such, we looked at the
c
-vectors arising from mutation sequences that never mutated at a fork’s point of return.
Proposition
5.2
is the result, and it controls the signs and magnitudes of the
c
-vectors as long as certain conditions on the starting ice quiver are met.
A future work will make use of this result
[
8
]
.
However, we first demonstrate that a broad class of ice quivers satisfy the assumptions necessary for Proposition
5.2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver with all red or all green vertices.
If
v
is a vertex of
Q
, then

•

Every mutable vertex of
\mu_{v}(Q)
is either red or green;

•

If
b_{vj}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\geq 0
and
b_{vj}^{[v]}\neq 0
for some vertex
j\neq v
, then either
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})=\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})
or
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})c_{j}^{[v]}\geq\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})c_{v}^{[v]}
;

•

If
b_{vj}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\leq 0
for some vertex
j\neq v
, then
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})=-\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})
;

•

If
b_{ij}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\geq 0
for distinct vertices
i
and
j
that are neither
v
, then
\sgn(c_{i}^{[v]})=\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})
.

In particular, the framing, coframing, and any triangular extension
[
7
]
of a mutable quiver with frozen vertices satisfy the above.

Proof.

First, every mutable vertex other than
v
of
\mu_{v}(Q)
retains their color, as mutating at
v
can only add arrows between frozen and mutable vertices in the same direction as they were already pointing.
As mutating at
v
only reverses the direction of the arrows at
v
, it is now the opposite color of what it was originally, proving the first result.
In terms of
c
-vectors, we have that
c_{v}^{[v]}=-c_{v}
and
\sgn(c_{i}^{[v]})=\sgn(c_{i})
for all vertices
i\neq v
.

Thus
\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})
and
\sgn(c_{i}^{[v]})=\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})
for all distinct vertices
i
and
j
with
v\notin\{i,j\}
, demonstrating the latter two points.
Finally, if
b_{vj}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\geq 0
and
b_{vj}^{[v]}\neq 0
for some vertex
j\neq v
, then
b_{jv}^{[v]}c_{v}^{[v]}\geq 0
as
\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})
.
Thus
b_{jv}c_{v}\geq 0
and

c_{j}^{[v]}=c_{j}+|b_{jv}|c_{v}.

As
c_{j}
and
c_{v}
share the same sign and
b_{jv}\neq 0
, we naturally have that
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})c_{j}^{[v]}\geq\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})c_{v}^{[v]}
, completing our proof.
∎

As many ice quivers satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
5.2
, we may now proceed with the inductive step of the proof.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver such that the mutable subquiver of
\mu_{v}(Q)
is a fork with point of return
v
for some vertex
v
.
Further, let
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be any reduced mutation sequence beginning with
v
and suppose that

•

Every mutable vertex of
\mu_{v}(Q)
is either red or green;

•

If
b_{vj}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\geq 0
for some vertex
j\neq v
, then either
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})=\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})
or
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})c_{j}^{[v]}\geq\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})c_{v}^{[v]}
;

•

If
b_{vj}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\leq 0
for some vertex
j\neq v
, then
\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})=-\sgn(c_{v}^{[v]})
;

•

If
b_{ij}^{[v]}c_{j}^{[v]}\geq 0
for distinct vertices
i
and
j
that are neither
v
, then
\sgn(c_{i}^{[v]})=\sgn(c_{j}^{[v]})
.

Then, if
r
is the last mutation of
{\boldsymbol{w}}
, we have the following:

•

Every mutable vertex of
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)
is either red or green;

•

If
b_{rj}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
for some vertex
j\neq r
, then either
\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})
or
\sgn(c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}})c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
;

•

If
b_{rj}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\leq 0
for some vertex
j\neq r
, then
\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=-\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})
;

•

If
b_{ij}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
for distinct vertices
i
and
j
that are neither
r
, then
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}})
.

Proof.

Take for our inductive hypothesis that the four conclusions hold for a reduced mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}
beginning with
v
of length
m\geq 1
.
Our base case was managed by our assumptions on the mutation sequence
[v]
and
\mu_{v}(Q)
.
If
{\boldsymbol{w}}={\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}[k]
for some mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}
of length
m
ending in
r
and some vertex
k\neq r
, then we will show that the same conclusions hold for
{\boldsymbol{w}}
and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)
.

Assume for now that
i\notin\{r,k\}
.
By the inductive hypothesis, we know
b_{ik}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq 0
implies
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
.
Then
c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+|b_{ik}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
, which gives us

\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}).

The magnitude of each entry of
c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
is then greater than or equal to the magnitude of each entry of
c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=-c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
as
|b_{ik}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|>0
from
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}(Q)
being a fork.
Thus
b_{ki}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
and
\sgn(c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}})c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq\sgn(c_{k}^{\boldsymbol{w}})c_{k}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
, as desired.

If instead
b_{ik}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\leq 0
, we know that
c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
.
As such, if
b_{ki}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
, then
b_{ki}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\leq 0
.
This forces

\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}).

If
b_{ki}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\leq 0
, then
b_{ki}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq 0
.
Thus the inductive hypothesis gives

\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}).

Both of the above situations are the anticipated outcomes.
Additionally, they combine to show that
\sgn(c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}})>0
or
\sgn(c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}})<0
for all
i\neq r
.
This is equivalent to every vertex other than possibly
r
being red or green.

Now we tackle the case where
i=r
.
If
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\leq 0
, then
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
by the inductive hypothesis.
As such, we have that
c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
,
\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})
, and
b_{kr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
, as desired.
If
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq 0
, then either
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
or
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
by the inductive hypothesis.
In the first case, we know that
c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+|b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
has the same sign as
c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
and thus the opposite sign of
c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
.
This forces
b_{kr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
and
\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
.

When
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
and
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
, this again forces
c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+|b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
to have the opposite sign of
c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}
.
Furthermore, our assumption gives

\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})[c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+|b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}]

=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})(|b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|-1)c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}

\geq\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}+\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})(|b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|-1)c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}

=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})(|b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}|-1)c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}

\geq\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}

\geq\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}

with the last inequality coming from the abundant property of forks.
This completes inductive step for the first two sign conditions, and it shows that every vertex is either red or green.
We only need to demonstrate the last sign condition.
Assume then that
b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
for distinct vertices
i
and
j
that are neither
k
or
r
.
The case where
r\in\{i,j\}
will be treated as a special case at the end of the proof.

First, note that
\sgn(b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
if
r\notin\{i,j\}
, as
B^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\setminus\{r\}
is an acyclic quiver and we mutated at
k\notin\{i,j,r\}
.
Thus, if
b_{jk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\leq 0
, we have that
b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq 0
as
c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
.
This forces
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
by the inductive hypothesis.
If
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
, then

\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}).

If
\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
, then
b_{ik}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\leq 0
.
Otherwise, the inductive hypothesis would imply that
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
, a contradiction.
Thus

\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}).

In both cases, we have
b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
and
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})
.

Now, if
b_{jk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq 0
, the inductive hypothesis gives that
\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
.
Since
\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
and
\sgn(b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
, we again have
b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\geq 0
.
The inductive hypothesis then gives that
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
.
Thus

\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}),

completing the last sign condition for the case where
r\notin\{i,j\}
.

Finally, if
b_{ij}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
and
\sgn(c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})
for distinct vertices
i
and
j
that are neither
k
with
r\in\{i,j\}
, we will soon arrive at a contradiction.
As this forces
b_{ji}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
, we can assume without loss of generality that
i=r
, i.e.,
b_{rj}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
and
b_{jr}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
.
Then

-\sgn(b_{jr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{rj}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(b_{kr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})

as mutation at
k
will force
b_{rv}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
to have the sign of
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
for all vertices
v\notin\{k,r\}
.
Thus
b_{kr}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\leq 0
, forcing
\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})=-\sgn(c_{k}^{\boldsymbol{w}})
as shown previously.
Then both
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\leq 0
and
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}\leq 0
, forcing
c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
.
By the inductive hypothesis, we know that
\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})
.
However, this creates a contradiction as

\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})=\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(c_{k}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}).

Therefore, we cannot have both
b_{rj}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq 0
and
\sgn(c_{r}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})
for some vertex
j\notin\{k,r\}
, completing the inductive step of our proof.
Thus the four conclusions hold for any reduced mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
beginning with
v
.
∎

We then have an immediate corollary on the case where
b_{ir}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver such that the mutable subquiver of
\mu_{v}(Q)
is a fork with point of return
v
for some vertex
v
satisfying the assumptions of Proposition
5.2
.
If
r
is the last mutation of
{\boldsymbol{w}}
—hence the point of return of
Q^{\boldsymbol{w}}
—and
b_{ir}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
for some vertex

, then
\sgn(c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}})=-\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})

\sgn(c_{i})^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}c_{i}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\geq\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
.

Additionally, we can easily generalize Proposition
5.2
to abundant acyclic quivers.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver such that the mutable subquiver of
\mu_{v}(Q)
is either a fork with point of return
v
or an abundant acyclic quiver with
v
a sink or source.
Further, let
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be any reduced sequence beginning with
v
.
Then, if the assumptions of Proposition
5.2
hold, so do the conclusions.

Proof.

As abundant acyclic quivers are mutation-abundant, we only need to check the last part of the proof, where we arrived at our contradiction.
To do so, we need to show that mutation at
k
will force
b_{rv}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
to have the sign of
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
for all vertices
v\notin\{k,r\}
.
If
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)
is abundant acyclic, then both
r
and
k
were source or sink mutations.
As
{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a reduced mutation sequence, to do otherwise would force
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)
to be a fork.
Thus, the vertex
r
was a sink or source in
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}(Q)
.
As such,

-\sgn(b_{jr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{rj}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{rj}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(b_{kr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}),

and the proof goes through as before.

Assume then that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)
is a fork.
In the case that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}(Q)
is a fork, then the proof works without modification.
In the case that
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}(Q)
is abundant acyclic, then
r
is either a source or a sink.
The above argument then finishes our generalization.
∎

If we reduce to the case where we have three mutable vertices, we can further generalize to mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver such that the mutable subquiver of
\mu_{v}(Q)
is a mutation-cyclic quiver on three vertices.
Further, let
v
be any vertex and
{\boldsymbol{w}}
be any reduced mutation sequence beginning with
v
.
Then, if the assumptions of Proposition
5.2
hold, so do the conclusions.

Proof.

As mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices are mutation-abundant, we need only show that mutation at
k
will force
b_{rj}^{\boldsymbol{w}}
to have the sign of
b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}
for all vertices
j\notin\{k,r\}
.
Since our quiver is a cycle and mutating produces a cycle with opposite orientation, we have

-\sgn(b_{jr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{rj}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=-\sgn(b_{rj}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=\sgn(b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}})=-\sgn(b_{rk}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}})=\sgn(b_{kr}^{{\boldsymbol{w}}}),

and the proof follows as before.
∎

Notably, any framing or coframing of a fork
F
will have
\mu_{v}(F)
satisfy the constraints of Proposition
5.2
for all vertices
v
that are not the point of return by Lemma
5.1
.
If instead we look at a framing or coframing of
Q
for arbitrary abundant acyclic quivers or for mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices, we see that
\mu_{v}(Q)
satisfies the constraints of Proposition
5.2
for all vertices
v
.
We can then use this information to prove that the latter two types of quivers are strictly sign-coherent.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver such that the mutable subquiver of
Q
is an abundant acyclic quiver.
If
\mu_{v}(Q)
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
5.4
for all mutable vertices
v
, then
Q
is strictly sign-coherent.
In particular, any framing or coframing of an abundant acyclic quiver is strictly sign-coherent.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
Q
be any ice quiver such that the mutable subquiver of
Q
is a mutation-cyclic quiver on three vertices.
If
\mu_{v}(Q)
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
5.4
for all mutable vertices
v
, then
Q
is strictly sign-coherent.
In particular, any framing or coframing of a mutation-cyclic quiver on three vertices is strictly sign-coherent.

Finally, both the Markov quiver—the cycle on three vertices with two arrows between each pair of vertices—and arbitrary mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices do not admit a maximal green sequence
[
14
]
.
It was additionally shown that the Markov quiver does not admit a reddening sequence
[
13
]
.
Further, it is well-known that arbitrary mutation-cyclic quivers on three vertices do not admit a reddening sequence; however, we are unaware of where this was first proven in the literature.
We give an elementary proof of this fact using Lemma
5.5
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
Q
be any mutation-cyclic quiver on three vertices.
Then the framing of
Q
is not mutation-equivalent to any quiver with all red vertices.
As such, any mutation-cyclic
Q
on three vertices does not admit a reddening sequence.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction.
Assume that we may mutate the framing of
Q
to an ice quiver
P
where all the vertices are red.
Let
r
be the last mutation of the sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}
that takes
\widehat{Q}
to
P
.
Since
Q
is mutation-cyclic and every vertex of
P
is red, we know that
b_{ri}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{i}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq 0
and
b_{rj}^{\boldsymbol{w}}c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}}\leq 0
for mutable vertices
i
and
j
in
P
.
However, this would imply that
\sgn(c_{r}^{\boldsymbol{w}})=-\sgn(c_{j}^{\boldsymbol{w}})
by Lemma
5.5
, contradicting all vertices of
P
being red.
Therefore, the framing
\widehat{Q}
cannot be mutated to any ice quiver with all red vertices, forcing
Q
to not admit a reddening sequence.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

This argument can also be applied to abundant acyclic quivers on an arbitrary number of vertices.
Since the vertex
r
can be a source or sink, the contradiction reached in Lemma
5.8
can be avoided.
As such, the only reddening sequences available to abundant acyclic quivers is those that reduce to source mutation sequences.
Similar exploration of possible reddening sequences for more arbitrary quivers will be presented in an upcoming work with Scott Neville
[
9
]
.
