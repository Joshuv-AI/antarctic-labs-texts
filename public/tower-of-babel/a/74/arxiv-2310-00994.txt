A Uniform One-Dimensional Fragment with Alternation of Quantifiers
Emanuel Kieroński

Abstract
The uniform one-dimensional fragment of first-order logic was introduced a few years ago as a generalization of the two-variable fragment of first-order logic to contexts involving relations of arity greater than two. Quantifiers in this logic are used in blocks, each block consisting only of existential quantifiers or only of universal quantifiers. In this paper we consider the possibility of mixing quantifiers in blocks. We identify a non-trivial variation of the logic with mixed blocks of quantifiers which retains some good properties of the two-variable fragment and of the uniform one-dimensional fragment: it has the finite (exponential) model property and hence decidable, NExpTime -complete satisfiability problem.

Introduction

In this paper we are going to push forward the research on the uniform one-dimensional fragment of first-order logic. To
set up the scene and locate our results in a broader context let us first recall some facts about the two-variable fragment,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
.
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, obtained just by restricting first-order logic so
that its formulas may use only variables
x
and
y
, is one of the most important decidable fragments of first-order logic identified so far.
The decidability of its satisfiability problem was shown by Scott
[
35
]
in the case without equality, and
by Mortimer
[
27
]
in the case with equality. In
[
27
]
it is proved that the logic has the finite model property, that is, its
every satisfiable formula has a finite model. Later, Grädel, Kolaitis and Vardi
[
12
]
strengthened that result, by showing that every satisfiable formula has a model of size bounded exponentially in its length. This exponential model property led to the
NExpTime
upper bound on the complexity of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
satisfiability. The matching lower bound follows from the earlier work by Lewis
[
25
]
.

An important motivation for studying
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
is the fact that it embeds, via the so-called standard translation, basic modal logic and many standard description logics.
Thus
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
constitutes an elegant first-order framework for those formalisms. However, its simplicity and naturalness make it also
an attractive logic in itself, inheriting potential applications in knowledge representation, artificial intelligence, or verification of
hardware and software from modal and description logics. Plenty of results on
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, its extensions and variations
have been obtained in the last few decades, e.g., decidability was shown for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with counting quantifiers
[
13
,
28
,
29
]
, one or two equivalence relations
[
20
,
19
]
, counting quantifiers and equivalence relation
[
30
]
, betweenness relations
[
23
]
, its complexity was established on words and trees, in various scenarios including the presence of data or counting
[
6
,
5
,
9
,
10
,
4
]
, to mention just a few of them.

However, further applications, e.g., in database theory are limited by the fact that
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
and its extensions mentioned above
can speak non-trivially only about
relations of arity at most two. This is in contrast to some other decidable fragments studied because of their potential applications in computer science,
like the guarded fragment,
GF

[
2
]
,
the unary negation fragment,
UNFO

[
8
]
, the guarded negation fragment,
GNFO

[
3
]
, or the fluted fragment,
FF

[
33
,
32
]
.

A natural question is whether there is an elegant decidable formalism which retains full expressivity of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, but additionally, allows one to speak
non-trivially about relations of arity bigger than two. In the recent literature we
can find a few such formalisms.

An interesting idea is for example to combine
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with
GF
. The idea can be traced back already in Kazakov’s PhD thesis
[
16
]
, was present in the work
by Bourish, Morak and Pieris
[
7
]
, and found a more systematic treatment in the paper by
Rudolph and Šimkus
[
34
]
, who formally introduced the triguarded fragment,
TGF
.
TGF
is obtained from

GF
by allowing quantification for subformulas with at most two free variables to be unguarded.
What we get this way is a logic in which one can speak freely about pairs of elements, and in a local, guarded way about
tuples of bigger arity.

TGF
turns out to be undecidable with equality, but becomes decidable when equality is forbidden. The satisfiability problem
is then 2
-ExpTime
- or 2
-NExpTime
-complete, depending on whether constants are allowed in signatures
[
34
]
; the finite model property is
retained
[
22
]
.
A variation of the idea above is the one-dimensional triguarded fragment
[
21
]
, still containing
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, which becomes decidable even in the presence of equality.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(or, actually, even its extension with counting quantifiers, C
2
) was also combined with
FF
by
Pratt-Hartmann
[
31
]
. This logic was shown decidable but the complexity of its satisfiability problem is non-elementary,
as already
FF
alone has non-elementary complexity

[
32
]
.

Finally, probably the most canonical extension of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
to contexts with relations of arity bigger than two, capturing the spirit of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
more closely than
the logics discussed above, is the
uniform one-dimensional fragment
,
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, proposed by Hella and Kuusisto
[
14
]
.
In this fragment quantifiers are used in blocks and a single block is built out only of existential or only of universal quantifiers and leaves
at most one variable free; a fragment meeting this condition is called
one-dimensional
. Imposing one-dimensionality alone is not sufficient for ensuring the
decidability of the satisfiability problem and thus another restriction,
uniformity
, is applied which, roughly speaking,
allows boolean combinations of atoms only if the atoms use precisely the same set of variables or use just one variable.
In effect, just as
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
contains modal logic (or even Boolean modal logic),
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
contains
polyadic
modal logic (even with negations of the accessibility relations) (cf.
[
24
]
).
In
[
14
]
it is shown that

\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
without equality is decidable and has the finite model property. In
[
17
]
this result is improved by showing that the decidability
is retained even if free use of equalities is
allowed (by
free use
of equalities we mean that they need not obey the uniformity restriction) and that the logic has exponential model property and
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability problem, exactly as
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
.

A question arises whether the requirement that the blocks of quantifiers from the definition of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
must consist of quantifiers of the same type (all universal or all existential) is necessary
for decidability, that is what happens if we allow one to mix quantifiers as, e.g., in the formula
\forall x\exists y\forall zR(x,y,z,t)
.
Let us denote the
extension of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
allowing to alternate quantifiers in blocks
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.
The motivations behind studying
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
are multifarious.
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
lies very close to the borderlines between the decidable and undecidable, so,
firstly and most importantly, analysing its expressive extensions may enhance our understanding of these borderlines which may be also useful in different scenarios.
Secondly, the logics
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
can be useful themselves, offering extensions of modal and description logics
to contexts with relations of arity greater than two, such as databases, orthogonal to other proposals. Thirdly, though it is of course a matter of
taste, we believe that
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
is just quite an elegant formalism, which can be justified by a relative simplicity of its definition and a nice game-theoretic characterization of its expressivity—natural Ehrenfeucht-style games for
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
were introduced in
[
17
]
; shifting to
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
would probably allow for an even nicer game characterizations
(though this topic is not formally studied in this paper).

The first step to understand
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
was done in the companion paper
[
11
]
, where we show the decidability and the finite model property of the three variable restriction of this logic,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
; in that paper
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
is then made a basis for obtaining a rich decidable subclass of the three-variable fragment,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
.

Turning now to our current contribution, we first remark that in this paper we still do not answer the question whether the whole
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
has decidable satisfiability. We however make another step towards understanding
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
by identifying its fragment,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, which contains full
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
without equality, allows for mixed blocks of quantifiers of unbounded length,
has
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability
problem, and has the exponential model property. Additionally, we observe that if we allow for a free use of equality in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
then we lose the
finite model property.

The main restriction of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, compared to full
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
, is that it admits only blocks of quantifiers that are purely universal or end with the existential quantifier. Additionally, mostly for the clarity of presentation, we will define
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
not as an extension of the version of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
originally defined in
[
14
]
,
but rather as an extension of the
strongly
uniform one-dimensional fragment,
\mbox{\rm sUF}_{1}
, introduced in
[
18
]
.
The definition of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
is inspired by the definition of the Maslov class
\overline{\mbox{\rm K}}

[
26
]
and, as we will see in a moment, the
decidability of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
can be shown by a reduction to conjunctions of sentences in
\overline{\mbox{\rm K}}
, whose decidability was
shown by the resolution method by Hustadt and Schmidt
[
15
]
.
However, this reduction does not allow us to establish the precise compleixty of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, since, to the best of our knowledge, the precise complexity of the Maslov class has not been established. It is also not known whether
\overline{\mbox{\rm K}}
has the finite model property.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation and terminology

We assume that the reader is familiar with first-order logic.
We work with purely relational signatures with no constants nor function symbols.
We refer to structures using Fraktur capital letters, and to their domains using
the corresponding Roman capitals. Given a structure

and some

we
denote by

to its subdomain

We usually use

For a tuple of variables

\overline{x}

In the context of uniform logics it is convenient to speak about some partially defined (sub)structures which we will call

We will use Fraktur letters decorated with

Similarly, if

An (atomic)
1
-

is a
maximal consistent set of atomic or negated atomic formulas over

. We often identify a

We will usually be interested in

-types over signatures

, and hence also in the length

Let

be a structure, and let

.

2.2
Satisfiability and finite model property

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a class of first-order formulas (a logic). The
(finite) satisfiability problem
for
\mathcal{L}

takes as its input a sentence from
\mathcal{L}
and verifies if it has a (finite) model.

\mathcal{L}
has the
finite model property
if every satisfiable sentence in
\mathcal{L}
has a finite model;
\mathcal{L}
has the
exponential model property
if there is a fixed exponential function

such that
every satisfiable sentence
\varphi
has a finite model over a domain whose size if bounded by
f(|\varphi|)

(where the length of
\varphi
,
|\varphi|
, is measured in any reasonable fashion).

Logics

2.4
Normal forms and basic decidability result

We introduce normal form for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
formulas, generalizing Scott’s normal form for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(cf.
[
35
,
12
]
). We start with a version
involving
0
-ary predicates, called
weak
normal form, and then explain how to remove them. In our normal form as well as
in some intermediate formulas
we allow ourselves to use implications which are usually not allowed in NNF formulas, but here they are very natural (note
that converting them to disjunctions using the basic law
p\rightarrow q\equiv\neg p\vee q
will not affect the blocks
of quantifiers).
We say that a
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence is in

if it is a conjunction of formulas having one of the following shapes.

\displaystyle\mbox{Q}_{1}x_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}x_{k}\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),

(1)

\displaystyle E\rightarrow\mbox{Q}_{1}x_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}x_{k}\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),

(2)

where

•

k\geqslant 0
is a natural number

•

the
x_{i}
are distinct variables

•

\psi
is a quantifier-free
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
formula (Boolean combination of atoms, each of them containing all the variables

x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
or at most one of them)

•

every
\mbox{Q}_{i}
is a quantifier (universal or existential) and either all the
\mbox{Q}_{i}
are universal (
universal conjunct
) or
\mbox{Q}_{k}
is existential (
existential conjunct
)

•

E
is a
0
-ary relation symbol

In particular, in a formula of type (
1
),
k
may be equal to
0
; in this case
\psi
is a Boolean combination of
0

Lemma 1
.

Let
\varphi
be a
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence. Then there exists a polynomially computable
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence
\varphi^{\prime}
in weak normal form over a signature extending the
signature of
\varphi
by some fresh unary and
0
-ary relation symbols, such that (i) every model of
\varphi
can be expanded to a model of
\varphi^{\prime}
and (ii) every
model of
\varphi^{\prime}
is a model of
\varphi
.

Proof.

(Sketch)
Assume that
\varphi
is in NNF.
Take an innermost subformula
\psi_{0}
starting with a maximal block of quantifiers. If it has a free variable, that is, is of the form

\mbox{Q}_{1}x_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}x_{k}\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y)

replace it by
P(y)
, for a fresh unary symbol
P
, and add the following normal form conjunct
\varphi_{\psi_{0}}
(partially) axiomatising
P
.

\forall y\mbox{Q}_{1}x_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}x_{k}(P(y)\rightarrow\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y)).

In other words,
\varphi
is replaced by
\varphi(P(y)/\psi_{0})\wedge\varphi_{\psi_{0}}
.

If
\psi_{0}
is a proper subsentence, that is it is of the form

\mbox{Q}_{1}x_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}x_{k}\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),

then replace it by
E
, for a fresh
0
-ary symbol
E
and add the conjunct

E\rightarrow\mbox{Q}_{1}x_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}x_{k}\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}).

Repeat this process as long as possible. Note that we indeed append conjuncts belonging
to
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
.

The above process is similar to Scott’s reduction of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
formulas to their normal form. Besides the natural modifications needed to deal with
sequences of quantifiers rather than with single quantifiers, the main difference is that in the appended conjuncts
axiomatizing the freshly introduced unary and
0
-ary predicates, we write
implications in only one direction.
This is sound as our initial formula is assumed to be in NNF. Indeed, consider a single step of the reduction, assuming that the case with a free variable in
\psi_{0}
applies. In this step
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi(P(y)/\psi_{0})\wedge\varphi_{\psi_{0}}
is produced from
\varphi
. Assuming that

we obtain a model
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
of

by making the unary relation

such that

.
This makes the appended conjunct
\varphi_{\psi_{0}}
true; obviously, also
\varphi(P(y)/\psi_{0})

In the opposite direction assume
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\models\varphi^{\prime}
. It may happen that the subformula
\psi_{0}(y)
of

is true in
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
in more points than
P(y)
is. However, to guarantee that
\varphi
is true it suffices that it is true
at least
at those
points where
P(y)
is, which is ensured by the appended conjunct
\varphi_{\psi_{0}}
; this is because
\varphi
is assumed to be in NNF and thus

\psi_{0}
appears in

in the scope of no negation symbol. We reason similarly for the case when

∎

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}}\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}\wedge\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}}\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i},

The discussion above justifies the following.

Lemma 2

(i)
The satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}

and every atom of

x_{i}
- or

-variable, (ii) it
contains all the

-variables and no

-variables, or (iii) it contains an existentially quantified variable

and no
z_{i}
-variables

Now, one easily observes that every
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
-normal form conjunct belongs to
\overline{\text{K}}
. Indeed, every
\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i}
-conjunct is of the form (
4
) with

and its every atom satisfies either condition (i) or (ii); every
\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}
-conjunct is of the form (
4
) with

and every atom satisfying (i) or (iii).

Hence any normal form formula belongs to

\overline{\text{DK}}
, the class of conjunctions of formulas in
\overline{\text{K}}
. The satisfiability problem for

\overline{\text{K}}
was shown to be decidable in
[

]
. This result was extended to the class
\overline{\text{DK}}
in
[
15
]
. This gives us the basic decidability result.

Theorem 3
.

The satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
is decidable.

We recall that the precise complexity of
\overline{\text{DK}}
has not been established. It is also not known if

\overline{\text{DK}}
has the finite model property and if its finite satisfiability is decidable.
The same questions for
\overline{\text{K}}
are also open.

3
Finite model property

The following theorem is the main results of this paper. Besides just proving the finite model property for

Theorem 4

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
has the exponential model property.

The rest of this section is devoted to a proof of the above theorem.

3.1
Satisfaction forests

In this subsection we introduce

3.1.2
Satisfaction forests and the existence of finite models

Let
\varphi
be a normal form
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence (we do not assume that a model of
\varphi
is known). Formally, a

for

\varphi

over a domain

is a sequence of

\mathcal{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}}
together with a labelling function

, assigning elements of

to the nodes of the

(with the exception of their roots to which the special empty label is assigned) and pre-structures to their branches, such that each of the trees
\mathcal{T}_{i}
satisfies conditions
(T1)-(T5) and the whole sequence satisfies conditions (F1)-(F4).

Lemma 6
.

A normal form
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence
\varphi
has a finite model over a domain
B
iff it has a satisfaction forest over
B
.

Proof.

Left-to-right implication is justified by the extraction of a satisfaction forest from a given finite model of
\varphi

described in Section
3.1.1
, and in particular by Claim
5
.

In the opposite
direction assume that a satisfaction forest over a finite domain
B
for
\varphi
is given. We construct a model
{\mathfrak{B}}
of
\varphi

over the domain
B
. The construction is natural:

Step 1:
1
-types.
The
1
-type of an element

is defined as the
1
-type of
b
in the structure

\mathcal{L}(\flat)
for an arbitrarily chosen branch
\flat

\mathcal{T}_{i}
, for which

Step 2: Witnesses.

For every tree
\mathcal{T}_{i}
and its every branch

of
\mathcal{T}_{i}

in accordance with
\mathcal{L}(\flat)

Step 3: Completion.

For any set of distinct elements
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}

-compatible pre-structure which retains the already defined

Properties (T3), (F2), (T4) and (F3) guarantee that Step 1 and Step 2 can be performed without conflicts

It remains to see that

Consider any existential conjunct of

\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}=\mbox{Q}_{1}^{i}x_{1}\mbox{Q}_{2}^{i}x_{2}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k_{i}{-}1}^{i}x_{k_{i}{-}1}\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}

3.2
From a model to a satisfaction forest over a small domain

We are ready to present the main construction of this paper in which we show that every satisfiable formula has a satisfaction forest over a small domain.

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a (possibly infinite) model of a normal form sentence
\varphi

3.2.1
Domain

Let

\varphi
) realized in
{\mathfrak{A}}
, and let these types be enumerated as
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{L}

We define the domain

For convenience let us split

3.2.2
Some simple combinatorics: Extension functions

During the construction of the satisfaction forest we will design a special strategy for assigning
labels to the leaves. To this end we introduce an auxiliary combinatorial tool, which we will call

extension functions
.

Let us recall a well known Hall’s marriage theorem.
A
matching
in a bipartite graph
(G_{1},G_{2},E
) is a partial injective function
f:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}
such that if
f(a)=b
then

(a,b)\in E
.

Theorem 7

(Hall)
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2},E)
be a bipartite graph. There exists a matching covering
G_{1}
iff for any set
W\subseteq G_{1}
the number of vertices of

G_{2}
incident to the edges emitted from
W
is greater or equal to
|W|
.

For a natural number
n
, let
[n]
denote the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and for
1\leqslant l\leqslant n
let
[n]^{l}
denote the set of all subsets of
[n]
of cardinality
l
.

Lemma 8
.

For every
0<l<K
there exists a
1{-}1
function
ext_{l}:[2K]^{l}\rightarrow[2K]^{l+1}
such that for any
S\in[2K]^{l}
we have that

S\subseteq ext_{l}(S)
.

Proof.

Consider the bipartite graph
([2K]^{l},[2K]^{l+1},E)
such that
(S,S^{\prime})\in E
iff
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
. To show that a desired
ext_{l}
exists it
suffices to show the existence of a matching covering entirely the set
[2K]^{l}
. To this end we apply Hall’s marriage theorem.
In our graph every
node from
[2K]^{l}
has degree
2K-l
(given an
l
-element subset of
[2K]
it can be expanded to an
l+1
subset just by adding to it
precisely one of the remaining
2K-l
elements) and every node from
[2K]^{l+1}
has degree
l+1
(to obtain an
l
-element subset of a
l+1
-subset
one just removes one of the elements of the latter). Take a subset
W
of
[2K]^{l}
.
The nodes of this subset are incident to
|W|\cdot(2K-l)
edges in total. Let us see that the number of nodes in
[2K]^{l+1}
incident to a node from
W
is greater than or equal
to
|W|
. Indeed, assume to the contrary that it is not. Then at most
|W|-1
nodes absorb
|W|\cdot(2K-l)
edges emitted by

,
but this means that
|W|\cdot(2K-l)\leqslant(|W|-1)\cdot(l+1)
. Rearranging this inequality we get that

|W|(2K-2l-1)+l+1\leqslant 0
. But using the assumption that

we have that

and hence
the whole left-hand side of the last inequality must be greater than

. Contradiction. Thus
our graph satisfies the Hall’s theorem assumptions which guarantee the existence of a matching from
[2K]^{l}
to
[2K]^{l+1}
, covering entirely
[2K]^{l}
.
This matching can be taken as
ext_{l}
.
∎

Choose an extension function
ext_{l}
for every

and let

, that is,

is a function which takes a non-empty subset of
[2K]
of size at most

and returns a superset
containing precisely one new element. Obviously

remains an injective function.

4
Infinity axiom with free use of equality

In this section we note that allowing for free use of equality in our logic changes the situation significantly: we lose the finite model property. We recall that in the case of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
free use of equality does not spoil the decidability and even does not change the complexity.

In the recent paper
[
11
]
we note that the fragment with arbitrary blocks of quantifiers
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
and with free use of equality contains infinity axioms (satisfiable formulas without finite models), by constructing the following three-variable formula:

\exists xS(x)\wedge\forall x\exists y\forall z(\neg S(y)\wedge R(x,y,z)\wedge(x=z\vee\neg R(z,y,x))),

which has no finite models but is satisfied in the model whose universe is the set of natural numbers,
S
is true only at
0
and
Rxyz
is
true iff
y=x+1
.

The above example can be simply adapted to the case of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
with free use of equality. We just add a dummy existentially quantified variable
t
and
require it to be equal to the previous, universally quantified variable
z
. To accommodate all the variables we increase the arity of
R
by
2
(one can think
that the first and the last position of
R
from the previous example have been doubled):

\exists xS(x)\wedge\forall x\exists y\forall z\exists t.(t=z\wedge\neg S(y)\wedge R(x,x,y,z,t)\wedge(x=z\vee\neg R(z,t,y,x,x))).

5
Conclusions

We identified a non-trivial uniform one-dimensional logic in which a use of mixed blocks of quantifiers is allowed,
strictly extending the two-variable fragment
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
without equality and the previously defined fragment
\mbox{\rm sUF}_{1}
without equality.
We proved that, similarly to
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
and s
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, this logic has the finite, exponential model property and
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability problem.

There are two interesting directions, orthogonal to each other, in which it would be
valuable to extend our work. The first is investigating the decidability, complexity
and the status of the finite model property for full
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
without equality,
that is to see what happens to our logic if arbitrary blocks of quantifiers, possibly ending with the universal quantifier,
are allowed. As already mentioned, in our recent work
[
11
]
we answered this question for the three variable restriction,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
by
showing the exponential model property and
NExpTime
-completeness of its satisfiability problem.

The second idea is to revive the research on Maslov Class
\overline{\text{K}}
, by attempting to determine the precise complexity of its satisfiability problem
and investigating whether it has the finite model property. When designing the fragment
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
we took some inspiration from
the definition of
\overline{\text{K}}
, and indeed we were able to reduce satisfiability of the former to the latter. We believe that
what we have learned working on
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
will prove useful in the case of
\overline{\text{K}}
.

There are also some probably slightly less attractive, but still interesting, a bit more technical questions that one can try to answer. For example,
what happens to our logic if a use of equalities/inequalities (free or uniform) or constants is allowed.
