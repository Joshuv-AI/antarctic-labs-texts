Duality of Hoffman constants
Javier F. Peña, Juan C. Vera, Luis F. Zuluaga

Abstract
Suppose A ∈ ℝ m × n A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} , and R ⊆ ℝ n R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} and S ⊆ ℝ m S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m} are reference polyhedral cones with dual cones R ∗ ⊆ ℝ n , S ∗ ⊆ ℝ m R^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\;S^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m} . We show that a suitable Slater condition implies a duality inequality between the Hoffman constants of the feasibility problems A ​ x − b ∈ S x ∈ R and c − A T ​ y ∈ R ∗ y ∈ S ∗ . \begin{array}[]{r}Ax-b\in S\\ x\in R\end{array}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\begin{array}[]{r}c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in R^{*}\\ y\in S^{*}.\end{array} As an interesting application, we show a striking identity between the Hoffman constants of box-constrained feasibility problems with a similar primal-dual format, but where one of the reference sets is a box and the other is a linear subspace. We also establish a surprising identity between Hoffman constants of box-constrained feasibility problems and the chi condition measures for weighted least-squares problems.

Introduction

Several recent papers show a surge of interest in the central role that Hoffman bounds, and more general error bounds, play in mathematical optimization. In particular, Hoffman constants play a central role in the modern development of first-order algorithms for linear programming
[
1
,
20
,
29
]
and in recent advances on the conditional gradient method
[
19
,
35
,
53
]
. Error bounds are instrumental for many other results on the convergence of a variety of optimization algorithms
[
1
,
3
,
12
,
16
,
26
,
27
,
30
,
51
,
48
,
50
]
.

The now vast literature on error bounds (see
[
2
,
7
,
18
,
28
,
31
,
37
,
39
,
52
,
54
]
and the many references therein) started with the seminal paper of Hoffman
[
22
]
, who showed that the distance from a point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

to a nonempty polyhedron of the form
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:A^{\text{\sf T}}y\leq c\}
can be bounded above in terms of a constant that depends only on
A
and the magnitude of the violation of the constraints
A^{\text{\sf T}}v\leq c
.

The Hoffman error bound property extends to the following more general context. Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are
reference
polyhedra such as a polyhedral cone, a box, or a simplex.
Hoffman’s bound implies that the distance from a point
u\in R
to a nonempty polyhedron of the form
\{x\in R:Ax-b\in S\}
can also be bounded in terms of a
Hoffman constant
that depends only on the triple
(A,R,S)
and the magnitude of the violation of the constraints
Ax-b\in S
.

The renewed interest in Hoffman constants and the central role that duality plays in optimization have inspired us to investigate the duality properties of Hoffman constants. More precisely, this paper is concerned with the following natural duality question concerning Hoffman constants:

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are reference polyhedral cones with dual cones
R^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\;S^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. What is the relationship between the Hoffman constants of the feasibility problems

\begin{array}[]{r}Ax-b\in S\\
x\in R\end{array}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\begin{array}[]{r}c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in R^{*}\\
y\in S^{*}?\end{array}

We show a strong relationship between these Hoffman constants. More precisely, we show that a suitable
Slater
condition implies a
duality inequality
between the Hoffman constants of the above feasibility problems (Corollary
1
). The direction of the inequality is determined by the Slater condition that holds. As an interesting application, in the special case when
R=\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
box
and
S=L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a
linear subspace
with orthogonal complement
L^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we show that the Hoffman constants of the following box-constrained feasibility problems are identical (Theorem
3
):

\begin{array}[]{r}Ax-b\in L\\
x\in\mathbb{B}\end{array}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\begin{array}[]{r}c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in\mathbb{B}\\
y\in L^{\perp}.\end{array}

We also show that if
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are endowed with Euclidean norms, then for suitable choices of
A
and
L
the Hoffman constants of these box-constrained feasibility problems are identical to the
chi
condition measures
\chi(A)
and
\overline{\chi}(A)
(Theorem
4
).

The crux of our duality developments is a
Hoffman duality inequality
for solution mappings of general polyhedral feasibility problems (Theorem
2
).
The key building block for our technical results is a refinement of a characterization of Hoffman constants developed in our previous work
[
37
]
(Theorem
1
).

Our duality results rely on characterizations of Hoffman constants such as those presented in
[
2
,
18
,
25
,
52
,
54
]
. Although these characterizations have been known for several decades, to the best of our knowledge, there are no duality results of the kind we develop in this paper. An obstacle for that kind of development so far has been the absence of
exact
characterizations of Hoffman constants for general feasibility problems of the form
Ax-b\in S,\;x\in R
where
S
and
R
are reference polyhedral sets.
Indeed, most of the existing characterizations on Hoffman constants, including those in
[
2
,
18
,
25
,
52
,
54
]
, consider only feasibility problems in
standard inequality-equality format

Ax\leq b,\,Cx=d
or the more restricted
standard inequality-only format

Ax\leq b
. In principle, any feasibility problem of the form
Ax-b\in S,\;x\in R
for reference polyhedra
S
and
R
can be recast in standard inequality-equality or inequality-only format. Therefore,

upper bounds
on its Hoffman constant readily follow from the Hoffman constant of its standard inequality-equality reformulation. However, such an approach can only yield loose upper bounds since the recasting in inequality-equality format would inevitably introduce spurious dependencies on the descriptions of the sets
R
and
S
. This type of shortcoming applies, e.g, to
[
50
, Prop. 3]
. Without a characterization of the
exact
Hoffman constants for general feasibility problems
of the form
Ax-b\in S,\;x\in R
, it would be impossible to state a meaningful statement comparing Hoffman constants of different problem instances. As we formally state in Theorem
1
, the more recent article
[
37
]
can be leveraged to
exactly
characterize Hoffman constants for the general class of feasibility problems considered in this paper. This is a key component of our duality developments.

Beyond the scope of this paper, we should note that Hoffman constants for feasibility problems of more general form
Ax-b\in S,\;x\in R
are a key component to establish the linear convergence of various first-order algorithmic schemes in each of the recent papers
[

,
29
]
. Similarly, a key ingredient underlying the results in the recent paper
[
50

is a technique, based on the characterization of Hoffman constants in
[
37
]
, to construct tight integer programming formulations of complementarity constraints. Characterizations of Hoffman constants have been recently used in
[
36
,

to obtain novel approaches to characterize the linear convergence of well-known optimization algorithms such as the Douglas-Rachford or the closely related Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers. Also, as evident in
[
50
, Prop. 3]
, better characterization of Hoffman constants can lead to substantial improvements in the solution of reformulations of indefinite quadratic optimization problems.

As we show in Section
3
below, there are some interesting identities among Hoffman constants for box-constrained systems of linear inequalities and the chi condition measures introduced in the seminal papers
[

]
(Theorem

Our duality developments are similar in spirit to the duality properties of the chi condition measures

Similar duality properties also hold for the

The chi condition measure plays a prominent role in weighted least squares problems

. The novel connection in Theorem

1.1
Overview of main results

We will now summarize our main results and introduce some of the notation and terminology used throughout this paper. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and let
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be some
reference polyhedra.
Consider the feasibility problem defined by the triple
(A,R,S)
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

\begin{array}[]{r}Ax-b\in S\\
x\in R.\end{array}

(1)

This format encompasses a broad class of feasibility problems. Our main duality results focus on the specific case where both
R
and
S
are polyhedral cones, such as the non-negative orthant, a linear subspace, or a linear transformation of a Cartesian product of these cones.

Since our interest lies in the behavior of the
solution set
for feasibility problems, we will utilize standard
set-valued mappings
terminology and notation, as discussed in the popular textbooks by Rockafellar and Wets
[
40
]
, Kahn et al.
[
24
]
, Ioffe
[
23
]
, and Mordukhovich
[
33
]
. We briefly recall the terminology and notation of set-valued mappings that suffice for our purposes in Section
2
.
Set-valued mappings frequently arise in optimization, nonsmooth analysis, variational inequalities, and equilibrium models. In contrast to point-to-point mappings, set-valued mappings map points to sets. This feature allows for more complex and flexible relationships. Set-valued mappings are a natural tool for studying generalized constraints, subdifferentials, and optimality conditions where it is critical to get a grasp on the entire solution set to a problem.
Set-valued mappings are particularly prevalent in the literature on Hoffman constants and error bounds, as highlighted in
[
8
,
9
,
37
]
.

Define the
solution mapping

P_{A,R,S}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
corresponding to the feasibility problem (
1
) as follows

P_{A,R,S}(b):=\{x\in R:Ax-b\in S\}.

(2)

In other words,
P_{A,R,S}
maps each
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to the (possibly empty) set of solutions
P_{A,R,S}(b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the feasibility problem (
1
).

The role of norms and their duals is central to our developments. To that end,
suppose
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are endowed with some (primal) norms
\|\cdot\|
. Let
{\mathrm{dist}}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the point-to-set distance defined by the norm
\|\cdot\|
.
Let
\|\cdot\|^{*}
denote the dual norms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
{\mathrm{dist}}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the point-to-set distance defined by the norm
\|\cdot\|^{*}
. Also, given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and sets
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\,S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we will write
A(R)-S
to denote the set
\{Ax-s:x\in R,\;s\in S\}
.

Hoffman’s Lemma
[
22
]
implies that there exists a finite constant
H
that depends only on
(A,R,S)
such that for all

x\in R
and
b\in A(R)-S

{\mathrm{dist}}(x,P_{A,R,S}(b))\leq H\cdot{\mathrm{dist}}(Ax-b,S).

(3)

Throughout the paper we will write
\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})
to denote the sharpest constant
H
satisfying (
3
), that is,

\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})=\sup_{b\in A(R)-S\atop x\in R\setminus P_{A,R,S}(b)}\frac{{\mathrm{dist}}(x,P_{A,R,S}(b))}{{\mathrm{dist}}(Ax-b,S)}.

(4)

In the special case when
R
and
S
are polyhedral cones, there is a natural dual counterpart of (
1
), namely, the feasibility problem

\begin{array}[]{r}c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in R^{*}\\
y\in S^{*},\end{array}

(5)

where
R^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the dual cones of
R
and
S
respectively.

Observe that the problem (
5
) has the same format as (
1
) but is defined by the triple
(A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*})
in lieu of
(A,R,S)
. In particular, the solution mapping
P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
to (
5
) is

P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}(c)=\{y\in S^{*}:A^{\text{\sf T}}y-c\in-R^{*}\}=\{y\in S^{*}:c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in R^{*}\}.

(6)

We show the following duality result (Corollary
1
): If the
primal
Slater condition
Ax\in{\mathrm{ri}}(S),\,x\in{\mathrm{ri}}(R)
holds for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})\leq\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}})
. In natural symmetric fashion,
if the
dual
Slater condition
-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in{\mathrm{ri}}(R^{*}),\,y\in{\mathrm{ri}}(S^{*})
holds for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
then
\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}})\leq\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})
.

The above duality result applies to the case when
R
and
S
are cones but it has an interesting application to the following non-conic case. Suppose
R=\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a box and
S=L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear subspace with orthogonal complement
L^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In Theorem
3
we show the
identity

\mathcal{H}(P_{A,\mathbb{B},L})=\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}})
between the Hoffman constant of the solution mapping
P_{A,\mathbb{B},L}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the feasibility problem

\begin{array}[]{r}Ax-b\in L\\
x\in\mathbb{B}\end{array}

(7)

and the Hoffman constant of the solution mapping
P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
to the feasibility problem

\begin{array}[]{r}c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in\mathbb{B}\\
y\in L^{\perp}.\end{array}

(8)

It is important to notice that (
7
) and (
8
) are not dual to each other.

In addition to the above duality properties, we also show a striking identity (Theorem
4
) between the Hoffman constants for box-constrained feasibility problems (
7
) and the chi condition measures for weighted least squares problems
[
5
,
14
,
15
,
34
,
42
]
when the underlying norms are Euclidean. The identity of these quantities is particularly tantalizing because the Hoffman constant and the chi measures are constructed and intended to capture conditioning properties of fundamentally different problems. Hoffman constants are concerned with error bound properties for linear inequalities and are oblivious to scaled projections. By contrast, the chi measures are concerned with norms of scaled projections and is oblivious to inequalities. Our identity is surprising and rectifies a long-standing misconception in the literature on the relation between condition measures and Hoffman bounds
[
21
]
. More precisely, some of our results (Theorem
4
and Lemma
1
) readily show that, contrary to the explicit statement at the end of
[
21
, Section 4]
, the inequality between the two quantities in
[
21
, Theorem 4.6]
can be strengthened to equality.

2
Hoffman duality inequality

This section outlines the foundational elements of our developments, namely a key characterization of Hoffman constants (Theorem
1
) and the
Hoffman duality inequality
(Theorem
2
). The latter in turn yields Corollary
1
which establishes our main duality result concerning the feasibility problems (
1
) and (
5
)

To introduce and motivate our notation, we describe a general format for formulating feasibility problems. Suppose the triple
(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathbb{R}^{q},G)
is such that
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
and consider the following generic feasibility problem:

\text{ given }u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\text{ find }v\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\text{ such that }(u,v)\in G.

The natural
solution mapping

\Phi_{G}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
for this feasibility problem is as follows

\Phi_{G}(u):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\text{ such that }\;(u,v)\in G\}.

The following example illustrates how the solution mapping
P_{A,R,S}
defined via (
2
) in Section
1.1
is an instance of the solution mapping
\Phi_{G}
for a suitable chosen
G
.

Example 1
.

Suppose
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are reference polyhedra and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. For

G=\{(Ax-s,x):x\in R,s\in S\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
the solution mapping
\Phi_{G}
is precisely
P_{A,R,S}
, that is, the solution mapping defined in (
2
).

The above construction highlights the natural connection between feasibility problems and set-valued mappings and introduces a key concept: the set
G
corresponds to the
graph
of the set-valued mapping
\Phi_{G}
. In what follows, we will rely on the graph and a few other basic concepts and notation of
set-valued mappings
as described in the popular textbooks by Rockafellar and Wets
[
40
]
, Kahn et al.
[
24
]
, Ioffe
[
23
]
, or Mordukhovich
[
33
]
. In particular, the definitions of graph, domain, and image of a set-valued mapping below are taken from
[
40
, Chapter 5]
and
[
24
, Chapter 1]
.

Suppose
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a set-valued mapping, that is,
\Phi(u)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
for each
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The
graph
of
\Phi
is

{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}:v\in\Phi(u)\}.

Observe that
\Phi
is precisely the solution mapping of the following feasibility problem,

\text{ given }u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\text{ find }v\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\text{ such that }(u,v)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi).

The domain
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and image
\Image(\Phi)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, of
\Phi
are as follows

{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\Phi(u)\neq\emptyset\},\;\Image(\Phi):=\bigcup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}\Phi(u).

It is easy to see that the
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)
and
\Image(\Phi)
are the projections of
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
onto
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
respectively.

The inverse mapping
\Phi^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}
is defined via
u\in\Phi^{-1}(v)\Leftrightarrow v\in\Phi(u)
. Observe that

{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi^{-1})=\{(v,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:(u,v)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)\},

and consequently
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)=\Image(\Phi^{-1})
and
\Image(\Phi)={\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi^{-1})
.

A set-valued mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is
polyhedral
if
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a polyhedron. Suppose
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}

is a polyhedral mapping and each of the spaces
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
is endowed with some norm
\|\cdot\|
.
Define the
Hoffman constant

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)
as the sharpest
H
such that for all
u\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)=\Image(\Phi^{-1})
and all
v\in\Image(\Phi)={\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi^{-1})
the following inequality holds

{\mathrm{dist}}(v,\Phi(u))\leq H\cdot{\mathrm{dist}}(u,\Phi^{-1}(v)).

In other words, the Hoffman constant
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)
is

\mathcal{H}(\Phi):=\sup_{u\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)\atop v\in\Image(\Phi)\setminus\Phi(u)}\frac{{\mathrm{dist}}(v,\Phi(u))}{{\mathrm{dist}}(u,\Phi^{-1}(v))}.

(9)

Note that the right-hand side expression in (
9
) is well-defined. Indeed, since
\Phi
is polyhedral, it follows that
\Phi^{-1}(v)
is nonempty and closed for all
v\in\Image(\Phi)
. Thus
{\mathrm{dist}}(u,\Phi^{-1}(v))>0
whenever
u\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)
and
v\in\Image(\Phi)\setminus\Phi(u)
.

A straightforward verification shows that for
\Phi=P_{A,R,S}
the definition of the Hoffman constant
\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})
in (
9
) indeed matches the one in (
4
).

We should clarify a minor but important detail concerning the notation
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)
of the Hoffman constant (
9
). In our previous article
[
37
]
, this constant was denoted
\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{-1})
. Thus our references to
[
37
]
, such as Theorem
1
below, have been adjusted accordingly. Although this is only a choice of notation, and thus of no fundamental relevance, the notation in (
9
) is more convenient as it emphasizes the role of the solution mapping
\Phi
. It is also consistent with the notation used in other recent articles
[
8
,
9
]
.

We next recall some key concepts that lie at the heart of our duality developments, namely
sublinear mappings
and the
upper adjoint
.
A set-valued mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is
sublinear
if
0\in\Phi(0)
and
\lambda\Phi(x)+\lambda^{\prime}\Phi(x^{\prime})\subseteq\Phi(\lambda x+\lambda^{\prime}x^{\prime})
for all
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\geq 0
and
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In other words,
a set-valued mapping is sublinear if and only if
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
is a convex cone. Set-valued sublinear mappings are also known as convex processes
[
6
,
38
]
.

Suppose
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is sublinear. The
upper adjoint

\Phi^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}
is defined as

z\in\Phi^{*}(w)\Leftrightarrow\langle z,u\rangle\leq\langle w,v\rangle\text{ for all }(u,v)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi).

(10)

Observe that

{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi^{*})=\{(w,z):(-z,w)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)^{*}\}.

(11)

The following example illustrates the upper adjoint in a particularly relevant case for our developments.

Example 2
.

Suppose
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are reference polyhedral cones and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Then the mappings
P_{A,R,S}
and
P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}
defined via (
2
) and (
6
) are sublinear and

(P_{A,R,S})^{*}=P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}.

To ease notation, in the special case when
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},R=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},
and
S=\{0\}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we shall write
P_{A}
as shorthand for
P_{A,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\{0\}}
and
P_{A}^{*}
as shorthand for its upper adjoint
(P_{A,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\{0\}})^{*}=P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},\mathbb{R}^{m},-\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}
. In other words,
P_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the mapping defined via

P_{A}(b)=\{x\geq 0:Ax=b\}

(12)

and
P_{A}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the mapping
defined via

P_{A}^{*}(c)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:A^{\text{\sf T}}y\leq c\}.

(13)

Our main developments hinge on the characterization of Hoffman constants in Theorem
1
below. This characterization is crucial for this article and has broader implications that extend beyond our specific context. In fact, the code available in the following repository utilizes Theorem
1
to compute Hoffman constants:

https://github.com/javi-pena/HoffmanCode

This code has been used in
[
50
]
to obtain integer programming formulations of quadratic programs.

Theorem
1
relies on specific collections of tangent cones associated with a polyhedral set-valued mapping. We will also use these collections of tangent cones in some of our developments. Suppose
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}

is a polyhedral mapping. For
(u,v)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
, or equivalently for
v\in\Phi(u)
, let
T_{(u,v)}(\Phi)
denote the tangent cone to
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
at
(u,v)
, that is,

T_{(u,v)}(\Phi)=\{(w,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:(u,v)+t(w,z)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)\text{ for some }t>0\}.

Let
\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
denote the collection of all tangent cones to
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
, that is,

\mathcal{T}(\Phi):=\{T_{(u,v)}(\Phi):(u,v)\in{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)\}.

For
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
let
\Phi_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the sublinear mapping defined via

z\in\Phi_{T}(w)\Leftrightarrow(w,z)\in T.

In other words,
\Phi_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is the sublinear mapping that satisfies
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi_{T})=T
.
Let
{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
be the following smaller collection of tangent cones

{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi):=\{T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi):{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{T})\text{ is a linear subspace}\}.

The following example illustrates the sets
\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
and

{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)
for the solution mappings
P_{A}
and
P_{A}^{*}
.

Example 3
.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and consider the solution mappings
P_{A}
and
P_{A}^{*}
defined in (
12
) and (
13
).

(a)

Suppose
\Phi=P_{A}
. Then each member of
\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
is of the form
T_{I}:=\{(Ax,x):x_{I}\geq 0\}
for some
I\subseteq[n]
. Furthermore,
T_{I}\in{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)
if and only if the following Slater condition holds:
Ax=0,x_{I}>0
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(b)

Suppose
\Phi=P_{A}^{*}
. Then each member of
\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
is of the form
T^{I}:=\{(A^{\text{\sf T}}y+s,y):y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\;s_{I}\geq 0\}
for some
I\subseteq[n]
. Furthermore,
T^{I}\in{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)
if and only if the following Slater condition holds:
(A^{\text{\sf T}}y)_{I}<0
for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Define the
norm
of a sublinear mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
as follows

\|\Phi\|=\max_{u\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)\atop\|u\|\leq 1}\min_{v\in\Phi(u)}\|v\|.

(14)

The upper adjoint
\Phi^{*}
is a mapping between the dual spaces of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. According to (
11
) the norm of
\Phi^{*}
is as follows

\|\Phi^{*}\|^{*}=\max_{w\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi^{*})\atop\|w\|^{*}\leq 1}\min_{z\in\Phi^{*}(w)}\|z\|^{*}.

(15)

Observe that when
\Phi
is a linear mapping, the norm
\|\Phi\|
defined via (
14
) is precisely the usual operator norm of
\Phi
. For general sublinear mappings, we should note that the norm defined via (
14
) is due to Robinson
[
38
]
and is slightly different from the popular
inner
norm discussed and used in
[
33
,
40
]
, namely,

\|\Phi\|^{-}=\max_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\atop\|u\|\leq 1}\min_{v\in\Phi(u)}\|v\|.

It is evident that
\|\Phi\|=\|\Phi\|^{-}

when
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)=\mathbb{R}^{p}
. On the other hand, when
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a polyhedral sublinear mapping and
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)\neq\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it holds that

\|\Phi\|^{-}=+\infty
whereas
\|\Phi\|<+\infty
. The latter finiteness property is crucial for the characterization of Hoffman constants presented next, which is a key foundational block for our developments.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
be a polyhedral mapping.

(a)

Suppose
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
satisfies the following bounding property: for all
T\in{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)
there exists
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\|\Phi_{T}\|\leq\|\Phi_{T^{\prime}}\|.
Then

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\max_{T\in\mathcal{S}}\|\Phi_{T}\|.

(16)

In particular, if
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)
is a linear subspace then
\mathcal{S}:=\{{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)\}
satisfies the bounding property and thus

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\|\Phi\|.

(17)

(b)

For all
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
it holds that
\mathcal{H}(\Phi_{T})\leq\mathcal{H}(\Phi)
.

Proof.

(a)

From
[
37
, Theorem 1 and Lemma 1]
it follows that

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\max_{T\in{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)}\|\Phi_{T}\|=\max_{T\in\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi)}\|\Phi_{T}\|.

(18)

The bounding property and (
18
) imply that

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\max_{T\in{\mathfrak{S}}(\Phi)}\|\Phi_{T}\|\leq\max_{T\in\mathcal{S}}\|\Phi_{T}\|\leq\max_{T\in\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi)}\|\Phi_{T}\|=\mathcal{H}(\Phi).

The first and last steps above follow from (
18
). The first inequality follows from the bounding property of
\mathcal{S}
and the second one follows from the assumption that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi)
.
Thus (
16
) follows.

When
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)
is a linear subspace, it readily follows that
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)={\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{T})
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
and hence
\mathcal{S}:=\{{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)\}
satisfies the bounding property. The latter fact and (
16
) imply (
17
).

(b)

Since
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
is a polyhedron, it follows that
\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{T})\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\Phi).
Therefore

\mathcal{H}(\Phi_{T})=\max_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{T})}\|\Phi_{T^{\prime}}\|\leq\max_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)}\|\Phi_{T^{\prime}}\|=\mathcal{H}(\Phi).

The first and last steps above follow from the second identity in (
18
) applied to
\Phi_{T}
and
\Phi
.
The inequality in the middle step follows because
\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{T})\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\Phi).

∎

The following example illustrates Theorem
1
for the solution mappings
P_{A}
and
P_{A}^{*}
. This example relies on the description of the tangent cones detailed in Example
3
.

Example 4
.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and consider the solution mappings
P_{A}
and
P_{A}^{*}
defined in (
12
) and (
13
).

(a)

If the primal Slater condition
Ax=0,x>0
holds for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
{\mathrm{dom}}(P_{A})=A(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a linear subspace and thus Theorem
1
implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(P_{A})

\displaystyle=\|P_{A}\|

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\atop\|Ay\|\leq 1}\min_{x\geq 0\atop Ax=Ay}\|x\|

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\atop Ay\neq 0}\min_{x\geq 0\atop Ax=Ay}\frac{\|x\|}{\|Ay\|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\inf\{\|Ay\|:Ay\not\in\{Ax:x\geq 0,\|x\|\leq 1\}}.

The denominator in the last expression has the following geometric interpretation: it is the radius of the largest ball in the space
A(\mathbb{R}^{n})
centered at the origin and contained in the set
\{Ax:x\geq 0,\|x\|\leq 1\}
. The primal Slater condition ensures that this radius is strictly positive and thus
\mathcal{H}(P_{A})
is finite, as it should be.

More generally, let
\mathcal{I}
denote the collection of maximal sets
I\subseteq[n]
(with respect to inclusion) such that the Slater condition
Ax=0,x_{I}>0
holds for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the collection of tangent cones
\{T_{I}:I\in\mathcal{I}\}\subseteq{\mathrm{graph}}(P_{A})
where
T_{I}=\{(Ax,x):x_{I}\geq 0\}
satisfies the bounding property and consequently

\mathcal{H}(P_{A})=\max_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{1}{\inf\{\|Ay\|:Ay\not\in\{Ax:x_{I}\geq 0,\|x\|\leq 1\}}.

(b)

If the dual Slater condition
A^{\text{\sf T}}y<0
holds for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
then
{\mathrm{dom}}(P_{A}^{*})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear subspace and thus Theorem
1
implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})

\displaystyle=\|P_{A}^{*}\|^{*}

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\atop\|z\|^{*}\leq 1}\min_{A^{\text{\sf T}}y\leq z}\|y\|^{*}

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\atop\|z\|^{*}\leq 1}\max_{x\geq 0\atop\|Ax\|\leq 1}-\langle z,x\rangle

\displaystyle=\max\{\|x\|:x\geq 0,\|Ax\|\leq 1\}.

The last expression has the following evident geometric interpretation: it is the largest element in the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\geq 0,\|Ax\|\leq 1\}
. Observe that this quantity is finite and thus so is
\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})
, as it should be, because the dual Slater condition implies that
Ax\neq 0
for any
x\geq 0,\,x\neq 0
.

More generally, let
\mathcal{I}
be the collection of maximal sets
I\subseteq[n]
(with respect to inclusion) such that the Slater condition
(A^{\text{\sf T}}y)_{I}<0
holds for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then the collection of tangent cones
\{T^{I}:I\in\mathcal{I}\}\subseteq{\mathrm{graph}}(Q_{A,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}})
where
T^{I}=\{(A^{\text{\sf T}}y+s,y):s_{I}\geq 0\}
satisfies the bounding property and consequently

\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})=\max_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\max\{\|x\|:x\in\mathbb{R}^{I},\,x\geq 0,\|A_{I}x\|\leq 1\}.

In the special case where
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a linear mapping, it is well known that
\|\Phi\|=\|\Phi^{*}\|^{*}
. Furthermore, in this special case it also holds that
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{*})

since Theorem
1
implies that

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\|\Phi\|
and
\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{*})=\|\Phi^{*}\|^{*}
.
Although neither
\|\Phi\|=\|\Phi^{*}\|^{*}
nor
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{*})
necessarily holds when
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a general polyhedral sublinear mapping, the following theorem shows that a suitable combination of these two identities, namely
\|\Phi\|\leq\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{*})
, holds.
As we show later, Theorem
2
automatically unveils several interesting duality properties of Hoffman constants.

Theorem 2

(Hoffman duality inequality)
.

Suppose
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a polyhedral sublinear mapping. Then

\|\Phi\|\leq\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{*}).

In particular, if
{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)
is a linear subspace then

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)\leq\mathcal{H}(\Phi^{*}).

Proof.

By Theorem
1
, it suffices to show that there exists
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi^{*})
such that
\|\Phi^{*}_{T}\|^{*}\geq\|\Phi\|
. To that end, let
\bar{u}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi)
with
\|\bar{u}\|=1
be such that

\|\Phi\|=\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\|v\|.

The existence of such
\bar{u}
follows from (
14
).
We will show that there exists
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi^{*})
and
\hat{w}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi^{*}_{T})
with
\|\hat{w}\|^{*}\leq 1
such that

z\in(\Phi^{*}_{T})(\hat{w})\Rightarrow\|z\|^{*}\geq\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\|v\|.

(19)

This will finish the proof because (
14
) and (
19
) imply that

\|\Phi^{*}_{T}\|^{*}=\max_{w\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{T}^{*})\atop\|w\|^{*}\leq 1}\min_{z\in(\Phi^{*}_{T})(w)}\|z\|^{*}\geq\min_{z\in(\Phi^{*}_{T})(\hat{w})}\|z\|^{*}\geq\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\|v\|=\|\Phi\|.

We next construct
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi^{*})
and
\hat{w}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi^{*}_{T})
with
\|\hat{w}\|^{*}\leq 1
such that (
19
) holds.
To that end, first observe that

\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\|v\|=\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\max_{\|w\|^{*}\leq 1}\langle w,v\rangle.

(20)

Second, apply Sion’s minimax theorem
[
41
]
to get

\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\max_{\|w\|^{*}\leq 1}\langle w,v\rangle=\max_{\|w\|^{*}\leq 1}\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\langle w,v\rangle.

(21)

Third, since
\Phi
is a polyhedral sublinear mapping,

K:={\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)
is a polyhedral convex cone and thus convex duality implies that for and each
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\langle w,v\rangle=\min_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\atop(\bar{u},v)\in K}\langle w,v\rangle=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\atop(-z,w)\in K^{*}}\langle z,\bar{u}\rangle=\max_{z\in\Phi^{*}(w)}\langle z,\bar{u}\rangle.

(22)

Putting together (
20
), (
21
), and (
22
) we get

\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\|v\|=\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\max_{\|w\|^{*}\leq 1}\langle w,v\rangle=\max_{\|w\|^{*}\leq 1\atop z\in\Phi^{*}(w)}\langle z,\bar{u}\rangle.

(23)

Let
\bar{v}
and
(\bar{z},\bar{w})
be optimal solutions to the left-most and right-most problems in (
23
). Then
\bar{v}\in\Phi(\bar{u}),\;\bar{z}\in\Phi^{*}(\bar{w}),
and

\|\bar{v}\|=\langle\bar{w},\bar{v}\rangle=\langle\bar{z},\bar{u}\rangle.

(24)

Let
T:=T_{(\bar{w},\bar{z})}(\Phi^{*})\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi^{*})
. Observe that

(-\bar{w},-\bar{z})\in T
. In addition, for all
(-\bar{w},z)\in T
we have
\bar{z}+tz\in\Phi^{*}(\bar{w}-t\bar{w})
for
t>0
sufficiently small and hence the construction of the upper adjoint
\Phi^{*}
and (
24
) yield

\langle\bar{z}+tz,\bar{u}\rangle\leq\langle\bar{w}-t\bar{w},\bar{v}\rangle\Rightarrow\langle z,\bar{u}\rangle\leq-\langle\bar{w},\bar{v}\rangle=-\|\bar{v}\|.

In other words, if
z\in\Phi_{T}^{*}(-\bar{w})
then
\langle z,\bar{u}\rangle\leq-\|\bar{v}\|
.
Thus
\hat{w}:=-\bar{w}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{T}^{*})
satisfies
\|\hat{w}\|^{*}=\|\bar{w}\|^{*}\leq 1
, and for all
z\in\Phi_{T}^{*}(\hat{w})
we have

\|z\|^{*}\geq-\langle z,\bar{u}\rangle\geq\|\bar{v}\|=\min_{v\in\Phi(\bar{u})}\|v\|.

Therefore (
19
) holds.

∎

Applying Theorem
2
to the mappings
P_{A,R,S}
and
P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}
we obtain the following relationship between the Hoffman constants of the primal-dual pair of feasibility problems (
1
) and (
5
).

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are polyhedral cones and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Consider the solution mappings
P_{A,R,S}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined via (
2
) and (
6
).

(a)

If the primal Slater condition
Ax\in{\mathrm{ri}}(S),x\in{\mathrm{ri}}(R)
holds for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})\leq\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}})
.

(b)

If the dual Slater condition
-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in{\mathrm{ri}}(R^{*}),\;y\in{\mathrm{ri}}(S^{*})
holds for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
then
\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}})\leq\mathcal{H}(P_{A,R,S})
.

Proof.

Part (a) follows by applying Theorem
2
to
\Phi:=P_{A,R,S}
and observing that
{\mathrm{dom}}(P_{A,R,S})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear subspace if the primal Slater condition holds. Likewise, part (b) follows by applying Theorem
2
to
\Phi:=P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}}
and observing that
{\mathrm{dom}}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},S^{*},-R^{*}})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear subspace if the dual Slater condition holds.
∎

It is easy to see that the primal and dual Slater conditions in parts (a) and (b) of Corollary
1
cannot both hold simultaneously. Therefore, there is an inherent dual asymmetry between the Hoffman constants of the feasibility problems (
1
) and (
5
). Interestingly, in Section
3
we show that there is a perfect symmetry between the Hoffman constants of the box-constrained feasibility problems (
7
) and (
8
).

The two examples below show that the right-hand side of each of the inequalities in Corollary
1
can be arbitrarily larger than the left-hand side. For ease of computation, the following examples assume that
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are endowed with the
\ell_{2}
-norm and the
\ell_{1}
-norm respectively and thus the dual spaces

(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{*}
and
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
are endowed with the
\ell_{2}
-norm and the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm respectively.

Example 5
.

Suppose
m=2,n=4,\theta\in(0,\pi/6)
and

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cos(\theta)&-\cos(\theta)\\
1&-1&-\sin(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\end{bmatrix}.

In this case the primal Slater condition
Ax=0,x>0
holds at the following point

\bar{x}:=\begin{bmatrix}1+2\sin(\theta)&1&1&1\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}.

(25)

Thus identity (
17
) in Theorem
1
and some simple calculations show that

\mathcal{H}(P_{A})=\|P_{A}\|=\frac{1}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}<\frac{1}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)}.

On the other hand,
\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})\geq 1/\sin(\theta)
because for
c=\begin{bmatrix}1/\sin(\theta)&0&-1&-1\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}
and
v=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}
we have
{\mathrm{dist}}^{*}(c-A^{\text{\sf T}}v,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})=\|(A^{\text{\sf T}}v-c)_{+}\|_{\infty}=1
and

y\in P_{A}^{*}(c)\Rightarrow y=\begin{bmatrix}0&1/\sin(\theta\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}.
Thus

y\in P_{A}^{*}(c)\Rightarrow\|v-y\|_{2}=\|y\|_{2}=y_{2}\geq\frac{1}{\sin(\theta)}

and so

{\mathrm{dist}}(v,P_{A}^{*}(c))\geq\frac{1}{\sin(\theta)}=\frac{1}{\sin(\theta)}\cdot{\mathrm{dist}}^{*}(c-A^{\text{\sf T}}v,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}).

Since this holds for any
\theta\in(0,\pi/6)
, it follows that
\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})
can be arbitrarily larger than
\mathcal{H}(P_{A})
by choosing
\theta
sufficiently small.

Example 6
.

Suppose
m=2,n=3,\theta\in(0,\pi/6)
and

A=\begin{bmatrix}\sin(\theta)&0&1\\
1&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

In this case
the dual Slater condition
A^{\text{\sf T}}y<0
holds for
\bar{y}=-\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}
. Thus
identity (
17
) in Theorem
1

and some simple calculations show that

\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})=\|P_{A}^{*}\|^{*}=\sqrt{2}.

On the other hand,
\mathcal{H}(P_{A})\geq 2/\sin(\theta)
because for
b=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}
and
u=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}
we have
\|Au-b\|_{2}=\sin(\theta)
and
x\in P_{A}(b)\Rightarrow x=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\text{\sf T}}
. Thus

x\in P_{A}(b)\Rightarrow\|x-u\|_{1}=2

and so

{\mathrm{dist}}(u,P_{A}(b))=2=\frac{2}{\sin(\theta)}\cdot\|Au-b\|_{2}.

Since this holds for any
\theta\in(0,\pi/6)
, it follows that
\mathcal{H}(P_{A})
can be arbitrarily larger than
\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})
by choosing
\theta
sufficiently small.

3
Box-constrained feasibility problems

This section details an interesting application of our duality results. We show that the Hoffman constants of the box-constrained feasibility problems (
7
) and (
8
) are identical. We also establish a striking identity between these Hoffman constants and the chi condition measures
[
34
,
42
]
for suitable choices of
A
and
L
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear subspace, and
\mathbb{B}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ell\leq x\leq u\}
for some
\ell,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\ell<u.
Let
P_{A,\mathbb{B},L}
and
P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}}
be the solution mappings to the box-constrained
systems of linear inequalities (
7
) and (
8
) respectively. Then
\mathcal{H}(P_{A,\mathbb{B},L})=\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}})
.

Proof.

To ease notation, let
\Phi:=P_{A,\mathbb{B},L}
and
\Psi:=P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}}
throughout this proof. Before proving the identity
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\mathcal{H}(\Psi)
, we establish the convenient expressions (
26
) and (
27
) below for
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)
and
\mathcal{H}(\Psi)
.

Observe that
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Phi)=\{(Ax-s,x):x\in\mathbb{B},s\in L\}
. Thus it follows that every
T\in\mathcal{T}(\Phi)
is of the form
T=T_{I,J}=\{(Ax-s,x):x_{I}\geq 0,x_{J}\leq 0,s\in L\}
for some
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
I\cap J=\emptyset
. For simplicity, we shall write

\Phi_{I,J}
as shorthand for
\Phi_{T_{I,J}}
and

\Phi_{I}
as shorthand for
\Phi_{T_{I,I^{c}}}
.

We claim that the collection
\{T_{I,I^{c}}:I\subseteq[n]\}
satisfies the bounding property and thus Theorem
1
implies that

\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\max_{I\subseteq[n]}\|\Phi_{I}\|=\max_{I\subseteq[n]}\;\mathcal{H}(\Phi_{I}).

(26)

We next prove the above claim that
\{T_{I,I^{c}}:I\subseteq[n]\}
satisfies the bounding property. To that end, suppose
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
I\cap J=\emptyset
and let
b\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{I,J})
be such that
\|b\|=1
and

\|\Phi_{I,J}\|=\min_{x\in\Phi_{I,J}(b)}\|x\|=\min\{\|x\|:Ax-b\in L,x_{I}\geq 0,x_{J}\leq 0\}.

Suppose
\bar{x}
is a minimizer of the last expression and let

I^{\prime}:=I\cup\{i:\bar{x}_{i}>0\}.

This choice of
I^{\prime}
guarantees that
b\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Phi_{I^{\prime}})
because

(b,\bar{x})\in T_{I^{\prime},(I^{\prime})^{c}}
. The choice of
I^{\prime}
also guarantees
that
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
and
J\subseteq(I^{\prime})^{c}
because if
j\in J
then
j\not\in I
and
\bar{x}_{j}\leq 0
so
j\not\in I^{\prime}
.
Therefore
T_{I^{\prime},(I^{\prime})^{c}}\subseteq T_{I,J}
and

\|\Phi_{I^{\prime}}\|\geq\min_{x\in\Phi_{I^{\prime}}(b)}\|x\|\geq\min_{x\in\Phi_{I,J}(b)}\|x\|=\|\Phi_{I,J}\|.

Since this holds for all
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
I\cap J=\emptyset
, it follows that
\{T_{I,I^{c}}:I\subseteq[n]\}
satisfies the bounding property.

We can proceed in a similar fashion for
\Psi
. Observe that
{\mathrm{graph}}(\Psi)=\{(A^{\text{\sf T}}y+s,y):y\in L^{\perp},s\in\mathbb{B}\}
and thus it follows that every
K\in\mathcal{T}(\Psi)
is of the form
K=K_{I,J}=\{(A^{\text{\sf T}}y+s,y):y\in L^{\perp},s_{I}\geq 0,s_{J}\leq 0\}
for some
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
I\cap J=\emptyset
. For simplicity, we shall write

\Psi_{I}
as shorthand for
\Psi_{K_{I,I^{c}}}
.

Again the collection
\{K_{I,I^{c}}:I\subseteq[n]\}
satisfies the bounding property and thus Theorem
1
implies that

\mathcal{H}(\Psi)=\max_{I\subseteq[n]}\|\Psi_{I}\|^{*}=\max_{I\subseteq[n]}\;\mathcal{H}(\Psi_{I}).

(27)

Next, observe that for each
I\subseteq[n]
the mappings
\Phi_{I}
and
\Psi_{I}
can be defined via

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\mapsto\Phi_{I}(b)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax-b\in L,x_{I}\geq 0,x_{I^{c}}\leq 0\}

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\mapsto\Psi_{I}(c)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:A^{\text{\sf T}}y+s=c,y\in L^{\perp},s_{I}\geq 0,s_{I^{c}}\leq 0\}.

Thus by applying Example
2
with
S=L
and
R=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{I}\geq 0,\,x_{I^{c}}\leq 0\}
, it follows that
\Phi_{I}^{*}=\Psi_{I}
for all

I\subseteq[n]
. Theorem
2
in turn implies that

\|\Phi_{I}\|\leq\mathcal{H}(\Psi_{I})\text{ and }\|\Psi_{I}\|^{*}\leq\mathcal{H}(\Phi_{I})

for all
I\subseteq[n]
. The latter inequalities together with (
26
) and (
27
) imply that
\mathcal{H}(\Phi)=\mathcal{H}(\Psi)
.
∎

We conclude this section by establishing a striking connection between the Hoffman constants of box-constrained feasibility problems and the chi condition measures introduced in the seminal papers
[
34
,
42
]
that we next recall.
Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is full row-rank,
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are endowed with Euclidean norms, and
\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the set of diagonal matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with positive diagonal entries. The condition measures
\chi(A)
and
\overline{\chi}(A)
are defined as follows (see
[
34
,
42
]
):

\chi(A):=\max_{D\in\mathscr{D}}\|(ADA^{\text{\sf T}})^{-1}AD\|_{2}

and

\overline{\chi}(A):=\max_{D\in\mathscr{D}}\|A^{\text{\sf T}}(ADA^{\text{\sf T}})^{-1}AD\|_{2}.

Although it is not immediately evident, the constants
\chi(A)
and
\overline{\chi}(A)
are finite for any full row-rank matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. This fact was independently shown by Ben-Tal and Teboulle
[
4
]
,
Dikin
[
13
]
, Stewart
[
42
]
, and Todd
[
43
]
. The constants
\chi(A)
and
\overline{\chi}(A)

arise in and
play a key role in weighted least-squares problems
[
5
,
14
,
15
]
and in linear
programming
[
21
,
44
,
45
,
47
]
.
We write
\|\cdot\|_{2}
in the above expressions for
\chi(A)
and
\overline{\chi}(A)
to highlight that the operator norms in both cases are those induced by the Euclidean norms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We note that the quantity
\overline{\chi}(A)
only depends on the space
\Image(A^{\text{\sf T}})
but not on the specific matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. In other words,
\overline{\chi}(A)=\overline{\chi}(\tilde{A})
for any other full row-rank matrix
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
\Image(A^{\text{\sf T}})=\Image(\tilde{A}^{\text{\sf T}})
, or equivalently

\overline{\chi}(A)=\overline{\chi}(RA)
for any non-singular
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. By contrast,
\chi(A)
does depend on the specific matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Furthermore, since
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are endowed with Euclidean norms, when the rows of
A
are orthonormal we have
\|A^{\text{\sf T}}y\|_{2}=\|y\|_{2}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Thus when the rows of
A
are orthonormal, the following identity holds

\overline{\chi}(A)=\chi(A).

(28)

Theorem
4
below formalizes an interesting and surprising connection between Hoffman constants of box-constrained feasibility problems and the chi condition measures
\chi(A)
and
\overline{\chi}(A)
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is full row-rank and both
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are endowed with Euclidean norms. Then for any
\ell,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\ell<u
and
\mathbb{B}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ell\leq x\leq u\}
the following identities hold. First, the chi condition measure
\chi(A)
is identical to the Hoffman constants of the following box-constrained feasibility problems

\begin{array}[]{r}Ax-b=0\\
x\in\mathbb{B}\end{array}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\begin{array}[]{r}c-A^{\text{\sf T}}y\in\mathbb{B}\\
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.\end{array}

In other words,

\mathcal{H}(P_{A,\mathbb{B},\{0\}})=\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},\mathbb{R}^{m},-\mathbb{B}})=\chi(A).

(29)

Second, for
L_{A}:=\ker(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
the chi condition measure
\overline{\chi}(A)
is identical to the Hoffman constants of the following box-constrained feasibility problems

\begin{array}[]{r}x-b\in L_{A}\\
x\in\mathbb{B}\end{array}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\begin{array}[]{r}c-y\in\mathbb{B}\\
y\in L_{A}^{\perp}.\end{array}

In other words,

\mathcal{H}(P_{I_{n},\mathbb{B},L_{A}})=\mathcal{H}(P_{I_{n},L_{A}^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}})=\overline{\chi}(A),

(30)

where
I_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix.

The proof of Theorem
4
relies on Lemma
1
which is of independent interest. The statement of Lemma
1
relies on the following set of
signature matrices

{\mathscr{S}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
:

{\mathscr{S}}:=\{\operatorname{Diag}(d):d\in\{-1,1\}^{n}\}.

(31)

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear subspace, and
\mathbb{B}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ell\leq x\leq u\}
for some
\ell,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\ell<u.
In addition, suppose
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are endowed with Euclidean norms.
Then

\mathcal{H}(P_{A,\mathbb{B},L})=\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{AD,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},L})\;\text{ and }\;\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}})=\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{DA^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}).

(32)

In particular,

\mathcal{H}(P_{A,\mathbb{B},\{0\}})=\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{AD})\;\text{ and }\;\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},\mathbb{R}^{m},-\mathbb{B}})=\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{AD}^{*}),

(33)

where
P_{A}
and
P_{A}^{*}
are as
defined in (
12
) and (
13
) respectively.

Proof.

There is a natural one-to-one correspondence between the subsets of
[n]
and the set of signature matrices
{\mathscr{S}}
, namely

I\subseteq[n]\leftrightarrow D_{I}=\{\operatorname{Diag}(d_{I}):d_{i}=1\text{ for }i\in I\text{ and }d_{i}=-1\text{ for }i\not\in I\}.

This correspondence and the fact that
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is endowed with the Euclidean norm allow us to restate the identities (
26
), (
27
) in the proof of Theorem
3
as follows

\mathcal{H}(P_{A,\mathbb{B},L})=\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{AD,\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},L})

and

\mathcal{H}(P_{A^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{B}})=\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{DA^{\text{\sf T}},L^{\perp},-\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}).

Therefore (
32
) follows. Identity (
33
) in turn follows from (
32
) and the definition (
12
) and (
13
) of
P_{A}
and
P_{A}^{*}
respectively.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4
relies on the following notation. For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
I\subseteq[n]
let
A_{I}
denote the
m\times|I|
submatrix of
A
defined by the columns in
I
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

The first identity (
29
) is an immediate consequence of Theorem
3
, identity (
33
) in Lemma
1
and the following two facts.

Fact 1.
The quantity
\chi(A)
can be characterized as follows
[
14
, Corollary 2.2]
:

\chi(A)=\max_{I\subseteq[n],|I|=m\atop A_{I}\text{nonsingular}}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{I}\atop\|A_{I}x\|_{2}\leq 1}\|x\|_{2}.

(34)

Fact 2.
The quantity
\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})
can be characterized as follows (see, e.g.
[
18
,
25
,
37
,
48
]
):

\mathcal{H}(P_{A}^{*})=\max_{I\subseteq[n],|I|=m\atop A_{I}\text{nonsingular}}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{I}_{+}\atop\|A_{I}x\|_{2}\leq 1}\|x\|_{2}.

(35)

Indeed, (
34
) and (
35
) imply that

\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{AD}^{*})\leq\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\chi(AD)=\chi(A).

(36)

On the other hand, suppose
\bar{I}
and
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{I}}
attain
\chi(A)
in (
34
). Choose
\hat{D}\in{\mathscr{S}}
such that
\hat{D}_{ii}=\text{sign}(\bar{x}_{ii})
for each
i\in\bar{I}
and let
\hat{x}:=\hat{D}_{I}\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{I}}_{+}
. Observe that
(A\hat{D})_{\bar{I}}=A_{\bar{I}}\hat{D}_{\bar{I}}
is nonsingular and

\|(A\hat{D})_{\bar{I}}\hat{x}\|_{2}=\|A_{\bar{I}}\hat{D}_{\bar{I}}\hat{x}\|_{2}=\|A_{\bar{I}}\bar{x}\|_{2}=1.

Thus by (
35
)

\max_{D\in{\mathscr{S}}}\mathcal{H}(P_{AD}^{*})\geq\mathcal{H}(P_{A\hat{D}}^{*})\geq\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{I}}_{+}\atop\|(A\hat{D})_{\bar{I}}x\|_{2}\leq 1}\|x\|_{2}\geq\|\hat{x}\|_{2}=\|\bar{x}\|_{2}=\chi(A).

(37)

Identity (
29
) thus follows by putting together (
36
), (
37
), (
33
), and Theorem
3
.

To prove the second identity (
30
), let
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a matrix with othonormal rows such that
\ker(\tilde{A})=L_{A}
. Then by (
29
)
and Theorem
3

\overline{\chi}(A)=\chi(\tilde{A})=\mathcal{H}(P_{\tilde{A}^{\text{\sf T}},\mathbb{R}^{m},-\mathbb{B}}).

(38)

Since the rows of
\tilde{A}
are orthonormal, it holds that
\|\tilde{A}^{\text{\sf T}}y\|_{2}=\|y\|_{2}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Hence
\mathcal{H}(P_{\tilde{A}^{\text{\sf T}},\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{B}})=\mathcal{H}(P_{I_{n},L_{A}^{\perp},\mathbb{B}})
and thus (
30
) follows from (
38
).
∎

4
Conclusions

Theorem
2
provides a duality inequality between the norm of a polyhedral sublinear mapping and the Hoffman constant of its upper adjoint. As a consequence, we obtain a novel duality relationship between the Hoffman constants of the feasibility problems (
1
) and (
5
) whenever
R,S
are polyhedral cones and either a primal or a dual Slater condition holds. We also obtain an interesting identity between the Hoffman constants of the box-constrained feasibility problems (
7
) and (
8
).

For the special case when the underlying spaces are endowed with Euclidean norms, we also establish a surprising identity between the Hoffman constants of the box-constrained feasibility problems (
7
) and (
8
) and the chi condition measures from
[
34
,
42
]
. This identity can be extended to the kappa measure of
[
10
]
and could be used to shed new light on the various
proximity results
that underlie important developments for linear programming such as those documented in
[
1
,
11
,
32
,
47
]
. We will pursue this line of research in some future work.

The duality results developed in this paper also set the stage for more general duality developments. In particular, the recent paper
[
9
]
extends our characterizations of the Hoffman constant
[
37
]
to a more general context concerning the
calmness modulus
of solution mappings for semi-infinite systems of linear inequalities. It is thus natural to explore what duality relationships hold in that more general context. The recent work of Wirth et al.
[
49
]
on the Frank-Wolfe algorithm relies heavily on the
inner facial distance
and
outer facial distance
of a polytope, both of which have natural geometric interpretations as discussed in
[
49
]
. The inner facial distance was initially introduced in
[
35
]
and shown to be a Hoffman constant in
[
19
]
.
We conjecture that the outer facial distance is a Hoffman constant as well but that has not been formally shown as of the time of this writing. We also conjecture that there is a duality relationship between the inner and outer facial distances. Positive answers to these conjectures would in turn have interesting implications on the convergence properties of first-order algorithms in light of the recent developments in
[
1
,
19
,
20
,
26
,
35
,
49
]
.
