The Lieb-Robinson correlation function for the quantum transverse field Ising model
Brendan J. Mahoney, Craig S. Lent

Abstract
The Lieb-Robinson correlation function is the norm of a commutator between local operators acting on separate subsystems at different times. This provides a useful state-independent measure for characterizing the specifically quantum interaction between spatially separated qubits. The finite propagation velocity for this correlator defines a “light-cone” of quantum influence. We calculate the Lieb-Robinson correlation function for one-dimensional qubit arrays described by the transverse field Ising model. Direct calculations of this correlation function have been limited by the exponential increase in the size of the state space with the number of qubits. We introduce a new technique that avoids this barrier by transforming the calculation to a sum over Pauli walks which results in linear scaling with system size. We can then explore propagation in arrays of hundreds of qubits and observe the effects of the quantum phase transition in the system. We observe the emergence of two distinct velocities of propagation: a correlation front velocity, which is affected by the phase transition, and the Lieb-Robinson velocity which is not. The correlation front velocity is equal to the maximum group velocity of single quasiparticle excitations. The Lieb-Robinson velocity describes the extreme leading edge of correlations when the value of the correlation function itself is still very small. For the semi-infinite chain of qubits at the quantum critical point, we derive an analytical result for the correlation function.



I
Introduction

How does quantum information or influence spread? A significant advance in understanding this important question was famously provided in 1972 by Lieb and Robinson
Lieb and Robinson 1972
. They focused on many-body systems composed of localized but interacting sub-systems.
They considered the norm of the commutator

C_{A,B}(t)=\left\|\left[\hat{A}_{k},\hat{B}_{m}(t)\right]\right\|

(1)

where
\hat{A}_{k}
operates on subsystem
k
and

\hat{B}_{m}(t)=e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\hat{B}_{m}(0)e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}

(2)

is a Heisenberg operator which acts on subsystem
m
at time
t
. They showed that under very general conditions, the norm of the commutator in Eq. (
1
) is bounded,

C_{A,B}(t)\leq Ke^{-\mu(d_{k,m}-v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t)},

(3)

where
d_{k,m}
is an appropriate distance between the two subsystems. The Lieb-Robinson velocity
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}
is understood to be an effective speed limit on the propagation of quantum information or influence. This bound holds even for non-relativistic quantum mechanics and is often envisioned as a sort of effective “light-cone” of possible interaction outside of which influence is exponentially suppressed.

There is by now a large literature calculating and improving the values of
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}
in the Lieb-Robinson bound for many different systems and including the effects of finite temperatures and disorder
Wang and Hazzard 2020
;
Tran et al. 2020
;
Chen and Lucas 2021
;
Chen and Lucas 2021
;
Baldwin et al. 2023
. The Lieb-Robinson result has been shown to imply finite-length correlations in the ground state of any gapped many-body system
Hastings and Koma 2006
;
Nachtergaele et al. 2006
.
Considerable work has been done on specific lattice spin systems, with near-neighbor coupling and those with power-law interactions
Foss-Feig et al. 2015
;
Colmenarez and Luitz 2020
. Work on propagation in such spin models is particularly relevant to current developments in quantum computing and quantum information processing. Recent reviews of the Lieb-Robinson bound and its connection to fundamental questions of operator locality and quantum information are given in references
Chen et al. 2023
and
Xu and Swingle 2024
. The bounds have been explored experimentally
Them 2014
; a review is in
Cheneau 2022
.

The Lieb-Robinson analysis is related to issues of quantum entanglement
Horodecki et al. 2009
. Equations (
1
) and (
2
) involve only
\hat{A}_{k}
,
\hat{B}_{m}
, and the system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. Entanglement by contrast is a feature of the quantum
state
of a composite system, which develops precisely because of the interaction between subsystems. The Lieb-Robinson commutator captures an aspect of the effective, possibly mediated, interaction between the two subsystems which is the precondition for them becoming entangled with one another. As emphasized by Chen, Lucas, and Yin
Chen et al. 2023
, the quantity
C_{A,B}(t)
represents a fundamental constraint on purely quantum non-locality and entanglement.

Here we focus not on the bound in Eq. (
3
) in the general case but on one specific model–the quantum transverse field Ising (QTFIM) model for a one-dimensional chain of qubits (or equivalently, spins).
Following the work of Colmenarez and Luitz
Colmenarez and Luitz 2020
, we define the Lieb-Robinson correlation function by

C_{k}(t)\equiv\left\|\left[\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{1}(t)\;,\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k}\right]\right\|,

(4)

where the operators are the usual Pauli operators on qubits
1
and
k
.

The QTFIM is the simplest model that includes both intrinsic local dynamics and coupling between subsystems, and it has other advantages as well. There is a well-known quantum phase transition that occurs when the strength of the local dynamics is equal to the inter-qubit coupling. A disordered system for small coupling becomes ferromagnetically ordered at a critical value of the coupling. The Hamiltonian can be diagonalized using now-standard methods which map the many-spin system onto free fermion quasiparticles
Lieb et al. 1961
;
Sachdev 2011
. Though simple in form, the QTFIM is also experimentally and practically relevant; it is used in D-Wave quantum-annealing based computers. Recent results have been reported for one-dimensional Ising chains consisting of 2000 qubits
King et al. 2022
and higher-dimensional configurations with 5000 qubits
King et al. 2023
. Quantum-dot cellular automata dynamics have been mapped to the QTFIM
Tougaw and Lent 1996
.

We study the behavior of
C_{k}(t)
as a function of space (qubit index
k
) and time. Our goals are to understand in detail the propagation of these quantum correlations down the chain and in particular (a) the effects of varying the Hamiltonian parameters, (b) the fingerprints of the quantum phase transition, and (c) the relation of the propagation speed to the Lieb-Robinson velocity.

Directly evaluating
C_{k}(t)
has been practically challenging for all but relatively short chains even for the QTFIM. The reasons are familiar: the size of the state space grows exponentially with the length of the chain, and the matrix exponentials in Eq. (
2
) are costly to compute. Heroic methods may be required for chains of 22 qubits
Colmenarez and Luitz 2020
. For the QTFIM, we devise an operator Pauli walk method which scales only linearly with the system size, and so allows evaluation of Eq. (
4
) for chains of hundreds of qubits and long times. This is sufficient to establish key features of the propagation for chains of any length. For the special case of the QTFIM at the quantum critical point, the method is extended to yield a closed form expression for the semi-infinite chain.

The term
correlation function
often denotes an expectation value, for example of the form

\left<\hat{A}(0)\hat{B}(t)\right>-\left<\hat{A}(0)\right>\left<\hat{B}(t)\right>,

(5)

and so depends on the system state
Schweigler et al. 2017
. By contrast, the Lieb-Robinson correlation function defined in Eq. (
4
) is a correlation between operators independent of state. Moreover, because of the commutator, it captures only explicitly quantum mechanical effects not classical correlations. It is also fairly associated with bounding the spread of
information
in a quantum channel
Chessa et al. 2019
, though it is not defined in Shannon information theory terms. The quantity
C_{k}(t)
could also be conceived as quantifying
influence
, or even
potential
influence of one system on another (and reciprocally). We will refer to it as a correlation function; its precise meaning is clear from the definition.

We consider the dynamics of the QTFIM in the sense of calculating
C_{k}(t)
using the time-dependent Heisenberg operators. The Hamiltonian itself is time-independent. We are not addressing the situation of the time-dependent state of the system when the Hamiltonian changes abruptly—a so-called “quench.”

The organization of the paper is as follows. Section
II
describes the QTFIM and the corresponding operator space. Section
III
presents the operator Pauli walk method which is at the heart of our calculations. The special case of evaluating
C_{k}(t)
at the quantum critical point is described in Section
IV
. At the leading edge of the correlation front which propagates down the chain, a simpler analysis is possible. This was described in
Mahoney and Lent 2022
in one, two, and three dimensions. We review the main results for one-dimensional chains in Section
V
in order to connect them with the present calculations. In Section
VI
, we examine the effect of the inter-qubit coupling strength on
C_{k}(t)
, its saturation value, and propagation speed. A discussion of the main results follows.

II
Model

II.1
System Hamiltonian

We consider a linear array of
N_{q}
qubits (or spins) with near-neighbor interactions. The system is described by the transverse-field Ising model with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=-\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k}^{x\mathstrut}-J\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}-1}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k}^{z\mathstrut}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k+1}^{z\mathstrut}.

(6)

where the operators
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k}^{\{x,y,z\}\mathstrut}
are the usual Pauli operators on site
k
. The first term in Eq. (
6
) represents the internal dynamics of each qubit and produces a characteristic time for these dynamics given by

\tau\equiv\frac{\pi\hbar}{\gamma}.

(7)

The second term represents the interaction between adjacent qubits and results in an energetic cost for neighboring opposite spins. We can re-write Eq. (
6
) in dimensionless form as

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}/\gamma=-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k}^{x\mathstrut}-J^{\prime}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}-1}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k}^{z\mathstrut}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathstrut k+1}^{z\mathstrut},

(8)

with
J^{\prime}=J/\gamma
now characterizing the system. The quantum phase transition between the ordered and disordered phase occurs at
J^{\prime}=1
.

II.2
Operator space

The set of operators on the qubit array form a Hilbert space with the inner product

\Braket{\hat{A}|\hat{B}}\equiv\frac{1}{\cal{N}}\Tr\left(\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{B}\right).

(9)

with
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{N_{q}}
being the dimension of the operator state space.
The norm induced by this inner product is

\|\hat{A}\|=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cal{N}}\Tr\left(\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\right)}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cal{N}}\Tr\left|\hat{A}\right|^{2}},

(10)

known as the normalized Frobenius norm. The operator norm, which is equal to the maximum modulus of the singular values, is frequently used in defining the Lieb-Robinson bound. Different norms can in general produce somewhat different results
Tran et al. 2020
, but for the problem at hand the two norms

yield identical values (see Section
III.6
below and the Supplementary Material
SupplementalMaterial
).

Pauli operators on the same site
k
obey the commutation relations

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{k},\hat{\sigma}^{y}_{k}\right]=2i\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k},\;\left[\hat{\sigma}^{y}_{k},\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k}\right]=2i\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{k},\;\left[\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k},\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{k}\right]=2i\hat{\sigma}^{y}_{k},

(11)

and Pauli operators on different sites commute. It is sometimes convenient to use the common alternative notation

X_{k}=\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{k},\qquad Y_{k}=\hat{\sigma}^{y}_{k},\qquad Z_{k}=\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k}.

(12)

We consider Pauli strings of the form

\hat{\sigma}_{s}=\prod_{k=1}^{N_{q}}\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha_{k}}_{k},\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha_{k}}_{k}\in[\hat{I}_{k},\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{k},\hat{\sigma}^{y}_{k},\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k}].

(13)

For example, using the simpler notation,

\hat{\sigma}_{s}=X_{1}X_{2}Z_{3}I_{4}\ldots

(14)

or

\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}=Z_{1}I_{2}Y_{3}Z_{4}\ldots.

(15)

The set of all
4^{N_{q}}
Pauli strings we denote
\cal{P}
. These Pauli strings form an orthonormal basis for Hermitian operators on the system such that

\Braket{\hat{\sigma}_{s}|\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\Tr\left(\hat{\sigma}_{s}\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\right)=\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}.

(16)

III
Calculating the Lieb-Robinson correlation function

III.1
Heisenberg time dependence

In the Heisenberg picture, the time dependence of any operator can be written

\hat{Q}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{it}{\hbar}\right)^{n}\left[\left(\hat{H}\right)^{n},\;\hat{Q}\right]

(17)

where
\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}(0)
, and we use the usual notation for
n

iterated commutators

\left[\left(A\right)^{n},B\right]\equiv[\underbrace{A,\dots[A,[A}_{\text{n times}},B]\dots]]

(18)

Using Eq. (
7
), the time dependence of
\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{1}
can then be written

\sigma^{z}_{1}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\pi^{n}i^{n}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\sigma_{1}^{z}\right].

(19)

The Lieb Robinson correlation function is defined by

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z}(t)\right]\|=\|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\;e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\right]\right\|

(20)

and we take this as the most direct method of computing it.
Making explicit use of the norm defined in Eq. (
10
) we have

\displaystyle C_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Tr\left(\frac{\left|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]\right|^{2}}{\cal{N}}\right)}.

(21)

From the definition, we see
C_{k}(0)=0
because
\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}
commutes with itself and with the Pauli operators on all other sites. The time dependence of
\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{1}(t)
can be envisioned as the operator spreading out from the first qubit progressively down the array, incorporating components of Pauli operators on other qubits. As the expanding operator encounters site
k
, the quantum correlation between the qubit
1
and qubit
k
, as quantified by
C_{k}(t)
, increases. It is this spread and growth that we want to understand in detail.

We focus first on the operator formed by the commutator

\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]
and using Eq. (
19
) obtain

\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\pi^{n}i^{n}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\sigma_{1}^{z}\right]\right].

(22)

Because Pauli strings span the operator space, the commutator can be expanded as a weighted sum of Pauli strings as

\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{1}\right]=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\cal{P}}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\hat{\sigma}_{s}.

(23)

To solve for the coefficients
C_{n,\hat{\sigma}s}
, we multiply both sides of (
23
) by
\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{1}\right]\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}=\left(\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\cal{P}}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\hat{\sigma}_{s}\right)\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}},

(24)

and take the trace

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{1}\right]\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\right)=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\cal{P}}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\Tr\left(\hat{\sigma}_{s}\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\right).

(25)

Now using Eq. (
16
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}=\frac{\Tr\left(\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\sigma_{1}^{z}\right]\hat{\sigma}_{s}\right)}{\mathcal{N}}

(26)

or

\displaystyle C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}=\Braket{\left[\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)^{n},\sigma_{1}^{z}\right]|\hat{\sigma}_{s}}.

(27)

By substituting Eq. (
23
) into Eq. (
22
), we have

\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\cal{P}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\pi^{n}\left(i\right)^{n}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{s}\right].

(28)

We define

\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{k},\hat{\sigma}_{s}\right].

(29)

By virtue of Eq. (
16
), one can show that the operators
\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}_{k,s}
also have an orthogonality relationship,

\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\Tr\left(\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}_{k,s}\hat{\sigma}_{k,s^{\prime}}\right)=\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\Tr\left(\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}_{k,s}\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}\right).

(30)

From Eq. (
28

\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\cal{P}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n!}\pi^{n}\left(i\right)^{n}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}.

(31)

It is necessary to calculate the trace of the absolute value square of this commutator so that we can evaluate the Lieb Robinson correlation function using Eq. (
21
). We proceed by defining two quantities that will be temporarily useful,

R_{n}\left(t\right)\equiv\frac{2}{n!}\pi^{n}\left(i\right)^{n}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n},

(32)

and

B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}(t)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}R_{n}\left(t\right)C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}.

(33)

Note that both the quantities
R_{n}(t)
and
B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}(t)
are numbers rather than operators. Using Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
), we can rewrite Eq. (
31

\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\in\cal{P}}B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{\prime}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{k,s^{\prime}}.

(34)

The absolute value squared of the commutator is then

\displaystyle\left|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\cal{P}}B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}\right)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\in\cal{P}}B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{k,s^{\prime}}\right)

(35)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s},\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\in\cal{P}}B^{*}_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\left(t\right)\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}^{\dagger}B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}}\left(t\right)\hat{\sigma}_{k,s^{\prime}}.

(36)

so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\Tr\left(\left|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s},\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}}B^{*}_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}(t)B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s^{\prime}}}(t)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Tr\left(\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{k,s^{\prime}}\right)}{\mathcal{N}}

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}}\left|B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}(t)\right|^{2}D_{k,\sigma_{s}}

(38)

where we have used Eq. (
30
) and define

\displaystyle D_{k,\sigma_{s}}\equiv\Braket{\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}|\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr\left(\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{k,s}\right)}{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{s}\right]\right\|^{2}.

(39)

Re-inserting the expressions for
B_{\hat{\sigma}_{s}}(t)
and
R_{n}(t)
from Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) yields

\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\Tr\left(\left|\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]\right|^{2}\right)\\
=\sum_{\sigma_{s}\in\cal{P}}\left|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n!}\pi^{n}\left(i\right)^{n}\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\right)\right|^{2}D_{k,\sigma_{s}},

(40)

and so from the definition for the correlation function in Eq. (
21
), we have

\displaystyle{C_{k}(t)=\sqrt{\displaystyle{\sum_{\sigma_{s}\in\cal{P}}}\left|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n!}\left(i\pi\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}\right)\right|^{2}D_{k,\sigma_{s}}}}

(41)

To calculate this correlation function we need to determine the quantity
C_{n,\hat{\sigma}_{s}}
from Eq. (
27
) and

from Eq. (

III.4
Calculating

We have seen that for the 4-qubit line, only the

, after which all the succeeding
\sigma_{m}

is zero for any Pauli string which includes

or

because both commute with

and 1 otherwise. Therefore the effect of

is to limit the sum over

in Eq. (

, so we can write

C_{k}(t)=\sqrt{\sum_{m=2k}^{8}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n!}\left(-2\pi\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}\left[(\bm{A}^{^{\prime}})^{n}\right]_{1,m}\right|^{2}}.

(85)

III.5
Generalizing the method of operator Pauli walks

Generalizing from the case of

to any number of qubits is now straightforward.
In place of the 8 equations relations in Eqs. (
46
)-(

to

. Specifically, we have

\left[{H}^{\prime},\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}X_{k}\right)Z_{j}\right]=2i\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}X_{k}\right)Y_{j}\\
-2iJ^{\prime}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-2}X_{k}\right)Y_{j-1},\quad 1\leq j\leq N_{q}\\

(86)

and

\left[{H}^{\prime},\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}X_{k}\right)Y_{j}\right]=\hfill\\
\qquad\qquad\begin{cases}{-2i\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{j-1}X_{k}\right)Z_{j}}\\
\quad+2iJ^{\prime}\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{j}X_{k}\right)Z_{j+1},\;1\leq j<N_{q}\\
{-2i\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{j-1}X_{k}\right)Z_{j}},\hfill j=N_{q}.\end{cases}\hfill

(87)

These connect the Pauli strings that contribute to Eq. (

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=Y_{1}

\displaystyle\sigma_{2j-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{(j-2)/2}X_{k}\right)Y_{(j/2)},\quad

\displaystyle j\text{ even, }j\geq 2

\displaystyle\sigma_{2j}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{(j-1)/2}X_{i}\right)Z_{(j+1)/2},\quad

\displaystyle j\text{ odd, }j\geq 2

The method of enumerating Pauli walks on the nodes labeled by

as described in the previous section is naturally extended to any length chain.
The adjacency matrix

\bm{A}=2i\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\

III.6
Choosing a norm

We can now revisit the question of which norm to use in computing the Lieb-Robinson correlation function.

The operator norm and Frobenius norm of an operator
Q
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\|Q\|_{\text{Op}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\hat{Q}_{\mathstrut}^{\mathstrut}\hat{Q}_{\mathstrut}^{\dagger\mathstrut}\right)}

(92)

\displaystyle\|Q\|_{\text{Frob}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cal{N}}\Tr\left(\hat{Q}_{\mathstrut}^{\mathstrut}\hat{Q}_{\mathstrut}^{\dagger\mathstrut}\right)}.

(93)

The operator norm is the square root of the maximum eigenvalue of
QQ^{\dagger}
and the Frobenius norm is the square root of the average of the eigenvalues of
QQ^{\dagger}
.
In general these are of course not equal to each other.

For the QTFIM, we have shown above that the operator
Q=\left[\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{z},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}(t)\right]
can be written as a linear combination of just the
2N_{q}
Pauli strings in Eq. (
88
). One can show that therefore in this case, for all times
QQ^{\dagger}
is a (time-dependent) multiple of the identity operator. For the identity operator
I
, the eigenvalues are all
1
, so the largest eigenvalue and the average eigenvalue are identical.
The two norms then yield the same result.
The detailed proof is included as Supplemental Material
SupplementalMaterial
. As a consequence of this identity, the Lieb-Robinson correlation function defined by Eq. (
4
) can be calculated with either definition of the norm; we have chosen to use the Frobenius norm.

\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{y}

IV
The semi-infinite chain at the phase transition

The results for
C_{k}(t)
given by Eq. (
91
) can be simplified even further if we consider the case of a semi-infinite chain at the critical inter-qubit coupling
J^{\prime}=1
. The adjacency matrix in Eq. (
89
) becomes

\bm{A}^{\prime}_{c}=\begin{bmatrix}[r]0&1&0&0&0&\cdots\\
-1&0&1&0&0&\cdots\\
0&-1&0&1&0&\cdots\\
0&0&-1&0&1&\cdots\\
0&0&0&-1&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

(94)

It will prove helpful in this context to renumber the operator nodes in Eq. (
88
) (not the qubits) so the first node has the index
0
. We then define

G_{n,m}\equiv\left[(\bm{A}_{c})^{n}\right]_{1,m+1},

(95)

and write the correlation function

C_{k}(t)=\sqrt{\sum_{m=2k-1}^{2N_{q}-1}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n!}\left(-2\pi\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}G_{n,m}\right|^{2}}

(96)

The quantity
G_{n,m}
is the sum of the weight products for all walks of length
n
from node 0 to node
m
. The weight product for each of the walks will be
\pm 1
.
Each negative step (a step to the left) in the walk generates a negative sign in the weight product. The overall sign of the weight product for a walk is determined by the number of negative steps. All of the walks of length
n
from node
0
to node
m
must have the same number of negative steps, and thus the same overall sign. One corollary is that in computing
G
there is no interference between walks to
m
that have the same length.

Consider a walk from node 0 to node
m
of length
n
. Let
n_{p}
be the number of positive steps and
n_{n}
be the number of negative steps. The weight product for the walk will be
(+1)^{n_{p}}(-1)^{n_{n}}
. We have
n=n_{p}+n_{n}
and
m=n_{p}-n_{n}
,
so

n_{n}=\frac{n-m}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad n_{p}=\frac{n+m}{2}.

(97)

Since
n_{n}
is an integer, we must have
n-m\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, i.e.,
n
and
m
must have the same parity. A walk with an even (odd) number of steps cannot end on an odd (even) node.

Let
N_{w}(n,m)
be the
number
of walks from node
0
to node
m
of length
n
. A well established result in combinatorics (Bertand’s ballot problem) is that in the case of a line of nodes unbounded to the right the number of such walks is

N_{w}(n,m)=\begin{cases}\frac{m+1}{1+(n+m)/2}\binom{n}{(n+m)/2},\\
\qquad\qquad\text{if }(n-m)=0\pmod{2}\\
0,\quad\qquad\text{if }(n-m)=1\pmod{2}.\end{cases}

(See, for example, the expression in reference
Renault 2008
with
a=n_{p}+1
and
b=n_{n}
.) Note that for
m>n,N_{w}(n,m)=0
.

Combining the information about the number of walks and the weight product for each walk, we can write

G_{n,m}=(-1)^{(n-m)/2}\;N_{w}(n,m).

(98)

So, if we consider a semi-infinite line of qubits (
N_{q}\rightarrow\infty
) we can write for the correlation function

C^{2}_{k}(t)=\sum_{m=2k-1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n!}\left(-2\pi\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{n}(-1)^{(n-m)/2}\;N_{w}(n,m)\right|^{2}.

(99)

Because only values of
n
with the same parity as
m
contribute to the sum in Eq. (
99
), we make the change of variables
n^{\prime}\equiv(n-m)/2
,
n=2n^{\prime}+m
, with the result

C^{2}_{k}(t)=4\sum_{m=2k-1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right)^{2m}(m+1)^{2}\\
\times\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+m+1)!n!}\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right)^{2n}\right|^{2},

(100)

where we have then substituted the symbol
n
for
n^{\prime}
.
The sum over
n
can be connected to a Bessel function of the first kind through the relation (8.440 in
Gradshteyn and I.M. 1980
)

J_{m+1}(2x)=x^{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+m+1)!n!}\;x^{2n}.

(101)

Substituting Eq. (
101
) into Eq. (
100
)
yields

C^{2}_{k}(t)=4\sum_{m=2k-1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right)^{2m}(m+1)^{2}\\
\times\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right)^{-(m+1)}J_{m+1}\left(\frac{4\pi t}{\tau}\right)\right|^{2}

(102)

\displaystyle C^{2}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right)^{2}}\sum_{m=2k-1}^{\infty}(m+1)^{2}J_{m+1}^{2}\left(\frac{4\pi t}{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(z/2\right)^{2}}\underbrace{\sum_{m=2k-1}^{\infty}(m+1)^{2}J_{m+1}^{2}(z)}_{S},

(103)

where we let

z\equiv\frac{4\pi t}{\tau}.

(104)

We divide the sum
S
into two parts

S=\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(m+1)^{2}J_{m+1}^{2}(z)}_{S_{1}}\\
\quad-\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{m=2k-2}(m+1)^{2}J_{m+1}^{2}(z)}_{S_{2}}.

(105)

The first term can be written

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}J_{m}^{2}(z)-J_{1}^{2}(z).

(106)

Now we can use the relations (11.4.7 in
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
)

J_{m}^{2}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}J_{2m}(2x\cos{\theta})d\theta

(107)

and

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}J_{2m}(u)=\frac{u^{2}}{8}

(108)

(8.513.4 in
Gradshteyn and I.M. 1980
) to write Eq. (
106
) as

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2}J_{2m}(2z\cos{\theta})d\theta-J_{1}^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{z^{2}}{\pi}\cos^{2}{\theta}d\theta-J_{1}^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}z^{2}-J_{1}^{2}(z).

(109)

Substituting Eq. (
109
) and Eq. (
105
) into Eq. (
103
), we obtain

\displaystyle C^{2}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{4}z^{2}-J_{1}^{2}(z)-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{2k-2}(m+1)^{2}J^{2}_{m+1}(z)}{\left(z/2\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{4}z^{2}-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{2k-1}m^{2}J^{2}_{m+1}(z)}{\left(z/2\right)^{2}}.

(110)

Substituting in Eq. (
104
), we arrive at a closed-form solution for the Lieb-Robinson correlation function for the
J^{\prime}=1
case,

C_{k}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{4\pi t}{\tau}\right)^{2}-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{2k-1}m^{2}J^{2}_{m}\left(\frac{4\pi t}{\tau}\right)}}{\pi t/\tau}.

(111)

) and black dots are the results of the Pauli walk method given by Eq. (

).

The complicated behavior of the Lieb-Robinson correleation function shown in Figure
2
is much simpler if we focus on the the early turn-on of correlation in each successive bit—the leading edge of the correlation. Figure
3
shows
C_{k}(t)
for
J^{\prime}=1/2
as correlations first start to grow and propagate along the qubit chain. The solid lines show the results computed directly from Eq. (
20
) and the dots indicate the results from the Pauli walk method of Eq. (
91
). For each qubit
k

is still small, the growth follows a power-law behavior in time
\sim t^{2k-1}

C_{k}(t)\quad\underset{\text{\small leading edge}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\quad\frac{{{2^{2k}}{\pi^{2k-1}}}}{{(2k-1)!}}{\left(J^{\prime}\right)^{k-1}}{\left({\frac{t}{\tau}}\right)^{2k-1}}.

(112)

Only odd orders contribute to the initial growth so the next most important term is proportional to
t^{2k+1}

The leading edge correlation response quantifies the way in which the correlation between qubit
k
and qubit
1
initially turns on. How fast does this leading edge of correlation propagate down the chain? For qubits far enough down the chain that we can replace the factorial in the denominator of Eq. (
112
) with Stirling’s approximation, we obtain

C_{k}(t)\underset{\underset{\text{\tiny leading edge}}{\text{\tiny k large}}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi J^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\left(\frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{(k-1/2)}\ \right)^{2k-1},

(113)

where the value of
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}
is given by

v_{\text{\tiny LR}}\tau=e\pi\sqrt{J^{\prime}},

(114)

which we identify as the Lieb-Robinson velocity.

A comparison of the leading edge expression of Eq. (
114
) with the results of the operator Pauli walk method is shown in Figure

Note that the magnitude of
C
here is very small.

To see something more like a simple exponential decay in front of the correlation front, one must go much further down the chain. In that limit, where
k
is quite large and in the region of the leading edge, where
k\approx v_{LR}t

C_{k}(t)\underset{\underset{\text{\tiny leading edge}}{\text{\tiny large k}}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\;\frac{e}{\sqrt{\pi J^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}e^{-2\left(k-v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t\right)}.

(115)

The details of the connection between Eq. (
113
) and Eq. (
115
) are in Appendix A.

We recognize that this nearly-exponential leading edge of correlation corresponds to the classic results of Lieb and Robinson. For the TFIM, we have here the additional information of the specific prefactor and its dependence on
J^{\prime}
, and the modification of the wavefront by the factor
1/\sqrt{k}
(also noted in
Chessa and Giovannetti 2019

VI.2
Saturation value

For a long chain, where reflection from the end can be neglected, we see that
C_{k}(t)
for any qubit eventually reaches a saturation value which we denote
C_{\text{sat}}
.
When the correlation front that is moving down the line is well passed a particular qubit
k
, the correlation function
C_{k}
for that qubit will approach arbitrarily closely to
C_{\text{sat}}
.

The calculated values of
C_{\text{sat}}
for different values of
J^{\prime}
are shown in Figure
13
. For
J^{\prime}\leq 1
, corresponding to the weakly-coupled disordered phase,
C_{\text{sat}}=2
independent of
J^{\prime}
. We observe that for
J^{\prime}\geq 1
, corresponding to the ordered phase, the value of
C_{\text{sat}}
decreases inversely with increasing
J^{\prime}

\displaystyle C_{\text{sat}}=\begin{cases}2,&\quad J^{\prime}\leq 1\\
2/J^{\prime},&\quad J^{\prime}>1.\end{cases}

(116)

The agreement between the numerical calculations using the Pauli walk method and this conjectured, though unproven, analytical expression is excellent.

VI.3
The velocity of correlation propagation

We have seen in Figures
7
–
12
that the Lieb-Robinson correlation front propagates down the chain of qubits. How fast does this front travel? We quantify this in the obvious way by picking an arbitrary threshold level
C_{\text{threshold}}
and finding the time
t_{k}
when
C_{k}(t)=C_{\text{threshold}}
. Using finite differences, the velocity of the front can then be calculated. Near the beginning of the chain, the velocity varies, but subsequently saturates to a stable value, as is clear in the figures. We find that this value
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
is

\displaystyle v_{\text{\tiny front}}\tau=\begin{cases}2\pi J^{\prime}&\quad J^{\prime}\leq 1\\
2\pi&\quad J^{\prime}>1.\end{cases}

(117)

This front velocity can be connected to a well-established result for the QTFIM. For the infinite qubit chain, or one with periodic boundary conditions, the Hamilonian of Eq. (
6
) can be mapped onto an equivalent fermion problem using the Jordan-Wigner transformation
Lieb et al. 1961
;
Sachdev 2011
;
Iglói and Tóth 2023
. A subsequent Bogoliubov transformation rotates the fermion creation and annihilation operators to yield a free (non-interacting) fermion Hamiltonian with quasiparticle excitation spectrum

E(q)=2J\sqrt{g^{2}+1-2g\cos{q}}.

(118)

Here
q
is the wavenumber of the harmonic excitation and
g\equiv 1/J^{\prime}
.
The corresponding group velocity can be calculated via the usual expression

v_{g}(q)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial q}E(q).

(119)

The maximum value of this group velocity
v^{\text{\tiny max}}_{g}
is given by precisely the expression for
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
in Eq. (
117
). The derivation is included for completeness in

Appendix B.
Thus, the correlation front quantified by
C_{k}(t)
and depicted in Figures
7
–
12
travels down the qubit chain at the speed of the maximum quasiparticle group velocity.

There is another velocity in the problem, as we have already described in Section
V
, the Lieb-Robinson velocity in Eqs. (
113
) and (
114
). The front velocity
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
and the Lieb-Robinson velocity
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}
are plotted as a function of coupling strength
J^{\prime}
in Figure
14
. The points are obtained from numerical calculations using the operator Pauli walk method. The solid lines are from the analytic expressions in Eqs. (
114
) and (
117
). The phase transition at
J^{\prime}=1
is clearly evident in
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
, but is not manifested in
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}
.
For any value of
J^{\prime}>0
,

v_{\text{\tiny LR}}>v_{\text{\tiny front}},

(120)

so the exponential leading edge of correlation is traveling down the chain considerably ahead of, and faster than, the main correlation front seen in Figures
7
–
12
.

Figure 14:
Correlation front velocity and the Lieb-Robinson velocity for the leading edge. Points are from numerical solutions using the operator Pauli walk method. Solid lines are from Eqs. (
114
) and (
117
).

The relationship of the two velocities in the problem can also be seen in the light-cone plot of
C_{k}(t)
in Figure

. The color in the figure represents the logarithm of the correlation function for a 200-qubit chain with

). Isocontours for the correlation function are shown in white (slight oscillations are an artifact of the contouring algorithm and the discreteness of the grid). The black line segment corresponds to the velocity
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
, and the red line segment corresponds to
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}
.
Because time is on the vertical axis, a lower slope on the graph corresponds to a higher velocity.
The main correlation front expands with a linear light cone given by
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
. The velocity of the leading edge is initially faster than that given by Eq. (
114
), and saturates at the Lieb-Robinson velocity from above
Mahoney and Lent 2022
. As the primary front expands down the chain, the slope of the isocontours at a particular qubit shift to correspond to
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
. The region that is sufficiently far enough ahead of the primary front that it is well-described by
C_{k}\sim e^{-2(k-vt)}
is more than 100 qubits down the chain and has an extremely small value of the correlation function.

A note about terminology is perhaps in order here. The original Lieb-Robinson paper
Lieb and Robinson 1972
used the term “group velocity” to describe the velocity that appears in Eq. (
3
). Their result was very general and did not depend on a specific energy dispersion relationship, or indeed on the precise Hamiltonian. The meaning of this velocity is provided by the context in which it appeared—a bound on correlations that depends on the quantity
(d-vt)
. Here we use the term “group velocity” in the standard sense provided by Eq. (
119
). In this case, the group velocity
v_{g}
is understood as the speed of a wavepacket composed of a superposition of single-quasiparticle Hamiltonian eigenstates with some distribution of wavenumbers centered about
q
. We identify our observed (calculated) front velocity
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
with the maximum group velocity so defined.

VII
Discussion

The primary result of this investigation is to highlight the fact that for the 1D QTFIM two different velocities characterize the propagation of the Lieb-Robinson correlation function. A correlation front moves down the qubit chain with velocity
v_{\text{\tiny front}}
, ahead of which
C_{k}(t)
is exponentially smaller. This velocity increases linearly with coupling strength in the disordered Ising phase (
J^{\prime}<1
) and is independent of coupling strength in the ordered phase. The velocity is given by the maximum group velocity of the single quasiparticle excitation band. Behind the propagating front,
C_{k}(t)
saturates to a value of 2 in the disordered phase, and
2/J^{\prime}
in the ordered phase.

Well ahead of the correlation front, the exponential leading edge of the correlation front moves faster. This velocity saturates from above to the Lieb-Robinson velocity
v_{\text{\tiny LR}}=e\sqrt{J\gamma}/\hbar
(equivalent to Eq. (
114
)). This velocity is unaffected by the phase transition at
J^{\prime}=1
. The Lieb-Robinson velocity describes a region well out ahead of the main correlation front in which the value of
C_{k}(t)
is extremely small.

It is not clear that the operator Pauli walk method developed here for the QTFIM can be extended to other models. The key feature that enabled this method to work was that the number of Pauli strings that were required to support the spreading Heisenberg operator was reduced from
4^{N_{q}}
to
2N_{q}
. For longer-range interactions, that may well not be the case. Further investigation will be required to reveal whether the general features of the propagation of
C_{k}(t)
as described here hold for such systems.

Appendix A
Derivation of Equation Eq. (
115
)

We begin with Eq. (
113
) and consider the limit in which the qubit index
k
is quite large and focus on the region ahead of the leading edge of correlations defined by
k\approx v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t
. Equation (
113
) can be written

C_{k}(t)\underset{\underset{\text{\tiny leading edge}}{\text{\tiny k large}}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi J^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\left[v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t\right]^{2k-1}\left[\frac{1}{(k-1/2)}\ \right]^{2k-1}.

(121)

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{k-\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{k(1-\frac{1}{2k})}\right]^{2k-1}

(122)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2k-1}\left[\frac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{2k})}\right]^{2k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2k-1}\left[\frac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{2k})}\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{1}{2k}\right)}\right]^{2k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2k-1}\left[1-\frac{1}{2k}\right]\left[\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{1}{2k})\right)}\right]^{2k}

In the large
k
limit, the first term in square brackets in Eq. (
122
) becomes 1. For the second term in square brackets, we can use the fact that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left[\frac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{x})}\right]^{x}=e

(123)

to obtain

C_{k}(t)\underset{\underset{\text{\tiny leading edge}}{\text{\tiny k large}}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\;\frac{e}{\sqrt{\pi J^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\left[\frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k}\right]^{2k-1}.

(124)

The term in square brackets in Eq. (
124
) can be written

\left[\frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k}\right]^{2k-1}=e^{(2k-1)\log{ \left( \frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k} \right)}}.

(125)

We define the index at the leading edge of correlation at time
t
to be

k_{t}\equiv v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t

(126)

and consider a region of width
\Delta k
out in front of this point so

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{t}+\Delta k

(127)

\displaystyle\Delta k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k-k_{t}=k-v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t.

(128)

We are interested in the limit when
k_{t}\rightarrow\infty
and therefore
\Delta k/k_{t}\rightarrow 0
. In that case

\frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k}=\frac{k_{t}}{k_{t}+\Delta k}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\Delta k}{k_{t}}}

(129)

so the logarithm in Eq. (
125
) can be written

\displaystyle\log{ \left( \frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k} \right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log{ \left( 1 + \frac{\Delta k}{k_t}\right) }

(130)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta k}{k_{t}}.

(131)

The argument of the exponential in Eq. (
125
) can therefore be written

\displaystyle(2k-1)\log{ \left( \frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k} \right)}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-2k\frac{\Delta k}{k_{t}}+\frac{\Delta k}{k_{t}}.

(132)

The second term on the right can be dropped to first order, but the first term must be treated more carefully. Using Eq. (
127
), we have

\displaystyle(2k-1)\log{ \left( \frac{v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t}{k} \right)}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-2(k_{t}+\Delta k)\frac{\Delta k}{k_{t}}

(133)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-2\Delta k

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-2(k-v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t).

Combining this result with Eq. (
125
) and Eq. (
124
) we obtain

C_{k}(t)\underset{\underset{\text{\tiny leading edge}}{\text{\tiny large k}}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\;\frac{e}{\sqrt{\pi J^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}e^{-2\left(k-v_{\text{\tiny LR}}t\right)}

(134)

which is identical to Eq. (
115
). The validity of this expression is confirmed by the close match shown in Figure 6. Of course, if the width of
\Delta k
is extended too far, then
C_{k}(t)
will drop faster. The exponential given by Eq. (
134
) bounds this very advanced leading edge of the correlation function in a region where its magnitude is extremely small.

Appendix B
Maximum quasi-particle group velocity for the transverse Ising chain

We start with the dispersion relation for single quasiparticle excitations for the QTFIM,

E(q)=2J\sqrt{g^{2}+1-2g\cos{q}},

(135)

where
g=1/J^{\prime}=\gamma/J

Sachdev 2011
.

The group velocity is given by

v_{g}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{dE(q)}{dq},

(136)

and

\displaystyle\frac{dE(q)}{dq}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Jg\sin(q)}{\sqrt{(g-\cos(q))^{2}+\sin^{2}(q)}}.

(137)

We consider two cases:

Case I

g\leq 1,\qquad J^{\prime}\geq 1

If
g\leq 1
, (
137
) can be maximize by making

(g-\cos(q)
in the denominator
0
, so
q=\arccos(g)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle v_{g}^{\text{\tiny max}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\frac{dE(q)}{dq}\right)_{\text{max}}=\frac{2Jg}{\hbar}

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}.

(139)

Therefore

\displaystyle v_{g}^{\text{\tiny max}}\tau=2\pi\quad\quad\text{for }\;g\leq 1,\quad J^{\prime}\geq 1.

(140)

Case II

g\geq 1,\qquad J^{\prime}\leq 1

We write Eq. (
137
) as:

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{(}q)}{dq}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Jg}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{g}{\sin(q)}-\cot(q)\right)}^{2}}

(141)

Maximizing (
141
) is equivalent to minimizing

\beta(q)=\frac{g}{\sin{q}}-\cot{q}.

(142)

Taking the derivative of
\beta
with respect to
k
and setting it to zero, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{d\beta}{dq}

\displaystyle=-g\cot(q)\csc(q)+\csc^{2}(q)=0,

(143)

so at the value
q_{0}
which minimizes
\beta

\displaystyle g\cot(q_0)=\csc(q_0),\quad\cos(q_0)=\frac{1}{g},

(144)

and

\displaystyle\sin(q_0)=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{g^{2}}}.

(145)

Substituting (
144
) and (
145
) into (
137
) we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dE(q)}{dq}\right|_{q_{0}}=2J.

(146)

The maximum group velocity in this case is then

\displaystyle v_{g}^{\text{\tiny max}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2J}{\hbar}

(147)

or

\displaystyle v_{g}^{\text{\tiny max}}\tau

\displaystyle=2\pi J^{\prime}.

(148)

Summarizing the two cases therefore we have,

v_{g}^{\text{\tiny max}}\tau=\begin{cases}2\pi J^{\prime},&J^{\prime}\leq 1,\quad g\geq 1\\
2\pi,&J^{\prime}\geq 1,\quad g\leq 1.\end{cases}

(149)
