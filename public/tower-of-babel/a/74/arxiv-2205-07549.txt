Magnetic neutron scattering from spherical nanoparticles with Néel surface anisotropy: Analytical treatment
Michael P. Adams, Andreas Michels, Hamid Kachkachi

Abstract
The magnetization profile and the related magnetic small-angle neutron scattering cross section of a single spherical nanoparticle with Néel surface anisotropy is analytically investigated. We employ a Hamiltonian that comprises the isotropic exchange interaction, an external magnetic field, a uniaxial magnetocrystalline anisotropy in the core of the particle, and the Néel anisotropy at the surface. Using a perturbation approach, the determination of the magnetization profile can be reduced to a Helmholtz equation with Neumann boundary condition, whose solution is represented by an infinite series in terms of spherical harmonics and spherical Bessel functions. From the resulting infinite series expansion, we analytically calculate the Fourier transform, which is algebraically related to the magnetic small-angle neutron scattering cross section. The approximate analytical solution is compared to the numerical solution using the Landau-Lifshitz equation, which accounts for the full nonlinearity of the problem.

Introduction

Magnetic small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) is a powerful technique for investigating spin structures on the mesoscopic length scale (
\sim 1
-
100\,\mathrm{nm}
) and inside the volume of magnetic materials
Mühlbauer et al. 2019
;
Michels 2021
. Recent SANS studies on magnetic nanoparticles, in particular employing spin-polarized neutrons, unanimously demonstrate that their spin textures are highly complex and exhibit a variety of nonuniform, canted, or core-shell-type configurations (see,
e.g.
Refs.
Disch et al. 2012
;
Krycka et al. 2014
;
Hasz et al. 2014
;
Günther et al. 2014
;
Maurer et al. 2014
;
Dennis et al. 2015
;
Grutter et al. 2017
;
Oberdick et al. 2018
;
Ijiri et al. 2019
;
Bender et al. 2019
;
Bersweiler et al. 2019
;
Zákutná et al. 2020
;
Honecker et al. 2022
and references therein). The magnetic SANS data analysis largely relies on structural form-factor-models for the cross section, borrowed from nuclear SANS, which do not properly account for the existing spin inhomogeneity inside magnetic nanoparticles or nanomagnets (NM).

Progress in magnetic SANS theory
Honecker and Michels 2013
;
Michels et al. 2014
;
Mettus and Michels 2015
;
Erokhin et al. 2015
;
Metlov and Michels 2015
;
Metlov and Michels 2016
;
Michels et al. 2016
;
Michels et al. 2019
;
Mistonov et al. 2019
;
Zaporozhets et al. 2022
strongly suggests that for the analysis of experimental magnetic SANS data the spatial nanometer scale variation of the orientation and magnitude of the magnetization vector field must be taken into account, and that macrospin-based models—assuming a
uniform
magnetization—are not adequate. The starting point for a proper analysis of the scattering problem is a micromagnetic continuum expression for the magnetic energy of the system. In the static case, this then leads to the so-called Brown’s equations, a set of nonlinear partial differential equations for the magnetization along with complex boundary conditions on the surface of the magnet. From these equations the Fourier image and the magnetic SANS cross section may be obtained.

In this paper, we present an
analytical
treatment of the magnetic SANS cross section of a spherical NM with Néel’s surface anisotropy
Néel 1954
. The manuscript is organized as follows: In Section
II
, we calculate the real-space spin structure of the spherical NM using classical micromagnetic theory within the second-order perturbation approach. In Section
III
, we compute the three-dimensional Fourier transform of the real-space spin structure, which directly yields the magnetic neutron scattering cross section and the pair-distance distribution function. The analytical results are benchmarked by comparing them to numerical finite-difference simulations using the Landau-Lifshitz equation of motion. Finally, Section
IV
summarizes the main findings of this study.

We also make reference to our numerical study
Adams et al. 2022
, where in contrast to the present analytical work the full nonlinearity of the problem is considered.

II
Micromagnetic Theory

In the static micromagnetic approach
Brown Jr. 1963
, the magnetic configuration of a system is described by the continuous magnetization vector field
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})
, which is subject to a constant magnitude
\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})\|=M_{0}
. The saturation magnetization
M_{0}
is only a function of temperature. The normalized magnetization vector field is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})/M_{0}=[m_{x}(\mathbf{r}),m_{y}(\mathbf{r}),m_{z}(\mathbf{r})].

(1)

Our Hamiltonian for the NM includes the isotropic exchange interaction, the Zeeman energy, a uniaxial magnetic anisotropy for spins in the core and Néel’s surface anisotropy for those on the surface. In the continuum approach, it reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-A\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\int_{V}m_{\alpha}\Delta m_{\alpha}\;d^{3}r

\displaystyle-M_{0}\mathbf{B}_{0}\int_{V}\mathbf{m}\;d^{3}r-K_{c}\int_{V}\left(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}}\right)^{2}\;d^{3}r

\displaystyle+A\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\oint_{\partial V}m_{\alpha}\nabla m_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;d^{2}r

\displaystyle-\frac{K_{s}}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\oint_{\partial V}\left|n_{\alpha}\right|m_{\alpha}^{2}\;d^{2}r

(2)

where
A
is the exchange-stiffness constant,
\nabla
is the Del operator,
\Delta
is the Laplace operator,
\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0}
is a constant applied magnetic field,
K_{c}>0
denotes the uniaxial core anisotropy constant,
\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}}
is a unit vector specifying the arbitrary core anisotropy axis,
K_{s}>0
is the Néel surface anisotropy constant
Néel 1954
with

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}=[\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta]

(3)

being the surface normal to the boundary of the NM
Garanin and Kachkachi 2003
;
H. Kachkachi 2007
. In (
2
), the two surface integrals take into account the boundary conditions for the magnetization on the surface (
\partial V
) of the NM of volume
V
, which result from the exchange interaction and the Néel term.

For small deviations from the homogeneous magnetization state, a perturbation approach is applicable. Let
\mathbf{m}_{0}
be the principal unit vector (average direction) associated with
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})
and let the vector function
\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{r})\perp\mathbf{m}_{0}
describe the spin misalignment. One can then write:

\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{m}_{0}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}}+\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{r}),\quad\|\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})\|=1.

(4)

Assuming that
\psi_{x},\psi_{y},\psi_{z}\ll 1
, the following second-order Maclaurin expansion in
\bm{\psi}
is used to find an approximate closed-form solution for
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})
:

\mathbf{m}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)\cong\mathbf{m}_{0}+\bm{\psi}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{r})\|^{2}\mathbf{m}_{0},

(5)

where
\mathbf{m}_{0}
is taken as a known constant vector in subsequent calculations. By choosing the orthonormal vector base
Garanin and Kachkachi 2009

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{0}=\mathbf{m}_{0},\\
\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{1}=\frac{\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{0}\times\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}}}{\displaystyle\|\mathbf{m}_{0}\times\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}}\|},\\
\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{2}=\frac{\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}})\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}}}{\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}})\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}}\|},\end{cases}

(6)

the parametrization

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{r})=\psi_{1}(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{g}_{1}+\psi_{2}(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{g}_{2},

(7)

and by introducing the dimensionless coordinates
\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{r}/R
(with
\xi=\|\bm{\xi}\|=r/R
), where
\mathbf{r}
is the position vector,

\displaystyle\bm{r}=[r\sin\theta\cos\phi,r\sin\theta\sin\phi,r\cos\theta],

(8)

and
R
denotes the radius of the NM, the minimization of the Hamiltonian (
2
) leads to the well-known Helmholtz equation with Neumann boundary conditions on the unit sphere
H. Kachkachi 2007
;
Garanin and Kachkachi 2003
:

\displaystyle[\Delta_{\bm{\xi}}-\kappa_{\beta}^{2}]\psi_{\beta}

\displaystyle=0\;,\quad\beta\in\{1,2\}

(9)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\psi_{\beta}}{d\xi}\right|_{\xi=1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\chi_{\alpha}^{\beta}|n_{\alpha}|,

(10)

where the constants are defined as:

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{0}+2k_{c}(\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}})^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{0}+2k_{c}\left[2(\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{A}})^{2}-1\right],

(12)

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=k_{s}(\mathbf{m}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\alpha})(\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}),

(13)

with the dimensionless quantities

\displaystyle k_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}K_{c}}{A},

\displaystyle k_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{RK_{s}}{A},

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}M_{0}}{A}\mathbf{B}_{0}.

(14)

The
\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}
(with
\alpha=x,y,z
) in (
13
) denote the unit vectors of the Cartesian laboratory coordinate frame (in which
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{r}
are defined). We emphasize that there are only two independent differential equations for
\bm{\psi}
, which is a consequence of the constraint
\|\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})\|=1
.

In our graphical representations, we will frequently use the following values:
k_{c}=0.1
and
k_{s}=3.0
, which (using
R=5\,\mathrm{nm}
and
A=10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J/m}
) correspond to
K_{c}=40\,\mathrm{kJ/m^{3}}
and
K_{s}=6\,\mathrm{mJ/m^{2}}

Batlle et al. 2022
;
O’Handley 2000
. For
M_{0}=1.7\times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{A/m}
, the relation between
b_{0}
(dimensionless) and the external field is
B_{0}=4/17b_{0}\times 1\mathrm{T}
.

The fundamental solution of the homogeneous Helmholtz equation (
9
) is well known
Weber and Arfken 2003
;
Riley et al. 2006
. Its nonsingular part can be expressed in spherical coordinates as an infinite series in terms of spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)
and spherical Bessel functions of the first kind
j_{n}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)
,

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}c_{\ell m}^{\beta}j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi).

(15)

The imaginary number ‘
\mathrm{i}
’ in the argument of the spherical Bessel function is due to the negative sign in the Helmholtz equation (
9
). The expansion coefficients
c_{\ell m}^{\beta}
are obtained from the Neumann boundary condition (
10
) using the method of least squares (see Appendix
A
). From there it is seen that the zero-order term with
\ell=0
vanishes, which physically makes sense, since the spin misalignment in our model is caused by the Néel surface anisotropy and, thus, due to symmetry reasons there is no misalignment at the center of the NM,
i.e.

\psi_{\beta}(\xi=0,\theta,\phi)\equiv 0
. By contrast, the largest spin misalignment is found at the boundary of the NM,
i.e.

\xi=1
. Further, we find that the coefficients
c_{\ell m}^{\beta}
vanish in the case of odd
\ell
and
m
, they are real-valued, and even with respect to the index
m
,
i.e.

c_{\ell m}^{\beta}=c_{\ell,-m}^{\beta}
. Taking these properties into account, one can conveniently express the solution in terms of the associated Legendre polynomials
P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)
with
\ell=2\nu
and
m=2\mu
[note that we use the convention that
Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)=N_{\ell m}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}
(p. 378 (14.30.1) in
Olver et al. 2010
)]:

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mu=0}^{\nu}a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)P_{2\nu}^{2\mu}(\cos\theta)\cos(2\mu\phi),

(16)

where we define (compare p. 624–626 in
Weber and Arfken 2003
)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=j_{2\nu}(\mathrm{i}\tau)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\nu}(\tau/2)^{2(s+\nu)}}{s!\Gamma(2\nu+s+3/2)},

(17)

and the expansion coefficients are given by

\displaystyle a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k_{s}N_{2\nu,2\mu}}{1+\delta_{\mu,0}}\frac{\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\cdot\operatorname{diag}\left[I_{2\nu,2\mu}^{x},I_{2\nu,2\mu}^{y},I_{2\nu,2\mu}^{z}\right]\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0}}{\kappa_{\beta}\Upsilon_{\nu}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}

(18)

with

\displaystyle I_{\ell m}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}Y_{\ell,m}^{*}(\theta,\phi)|n_{\alpha}|\sin\theta\;d\theta d\phi

(19)

and

\displaystyle N_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}.

(20)

In (
18
),
\delta_{\mu,0}
is the Kronecker delta function,
\operatorname{diag}[...]
denotes a
3\times 3
diagonal matrix, and
\Upsilon_{\nu}^{\prime}(\tau)
is the first-order derivative of (
17
) with respect to
\tau
. For some small values of
\ell
and
m
, the exact solutions of the integrals
I_{\ell m}^{\alpha}
are listed in Appendix
B
.

From (
18
) it is seen that the functions
\psi_{\beta}
depend linearly on
k_{s}
, such that for
k_{s}=0
the magnetization of the NM is homogeneous (as expected). Since we assume that
\psi_{x},\psi_{y},\psi_{z}\ll 1
, it is clear that the validity of our solution is restricted to a finite range
0\leq k_{s}\leq k_{s,\mathrm{max}}
. Taking only the terms with
\nu=1
into account (corresponding to
\ell=2
), the remaining (second-order) expression reads:

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}(\xi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{15k_{s}}{32}\frac{\Upsilon_{1}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)}{\kappa_{\beta}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{V}}(\theta,\phi)\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0},

(21)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{V}}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}\begin{bmatrix}\cos^{2}\theta-1/3-\sin^{2}\theta\cos(2\phi)\\
\cos^{2}\theta-1/3+\sin^{2}\theta\cos(2\phi)\\
-2(\cos^{2}\theta-1/3)\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

A reasonable approximation for small
\kappa_{\beta}
in (
21
) is obtained by taking into account the first two terms in the infinite series (
17
) for
\Upsilon_{\nu}(\tau)
. This results in the following expression [compare (
21
)]:

\displaystyle\frac{\Upsilon_{1}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)}{\kappa_{\beta}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}\approx\frac{1}{4}\frac{\kappa_{\beta}^{2}\xi^{4}+14\xi^{2}}{\kappa_{\beta}^{2}+7}.

(23)

In the limit
\kappa_{\beta}\rightarrow 0
, this expression reduces to a quadratic function in
\xi

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa_{\beta}\rightarrow 0}\left\{\frac{\Upsilon_{1}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)}{\kappa_{\beta}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}\right\}=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}.

(24)

The case of an infinite applied magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{0}
, or of a strong uniaxial core anisotropy [compare (
11
) and (
12
)], corresponds to the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa_{\beta}\rightarrow\infty}\left\{\frac{\Upsilon_{1}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)}{\kappa_{\beta}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}\right\}=0,

(25)

which recovers the expected result of zero spin misalignment. Note that the limit
\kappa_{\beta}\rightarrow\infty
is only obtained using all terms of the infinite series (
17
).

Of particular interest is the behavior of
\psi_{\beta}
as a function of the radius
R
of the NM. Inspecting the Hamiltonian (
2

, while the uniaxial core anisotropy energy scales as

21

\mathbf{m}_{0}
into the minimization of the Hamiltonian. Generally,

is a function of

,

III
Magnetic SANS cross section

The quantity of interest in experimental SANS studies is the elastic magnetic differential scattering cross section
d\Sigma_{M}/d\Omega
, which is usually recorded on a two-dimensional position-sensitive detector. For the most commonly used scattering geometry in magnetic SANS experiments, where the applied magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{0}\parallel\mathbf{e}_{z}
is perpendicular to the wave vector
\mathbf{k}_{0}\parallel\mathbf{e}_{x}
of the incident neutrons (see Fig.
4
),
d\Sigma_{M}/d\Omega
(for unpolarized neutrons) can be written as
Mühlbauer et al. 2019
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\Sigma_{M}}{d\Omega}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{8\pi^{3}}{V}b_{H}^{2}\left(|\widetilde{M}_{x}|^{2}+|\widetilde{M}_{y}|^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{q}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+|\widetilde{M}_{z}|^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{q}-(\widetilde{M}_{y}\widetilde{M}_{z}^{\ast}+\widetilde{M}_{y}^{\ast}\widetilde{M}_{z})\sin\theta_{q}\cos\theta_{q}\right),

(31)

where
V
is the scattering volume and
b_{H}=2.91\times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{A}^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-1}
the magnetic scattering length in the small-angle regime (the atomic magnetic form factor is approximated by
1
, since we are dealing with forward scattering);
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{q})=[\widetilde{M}_{x}(\mathbf{q}),\widetilde{M}_{y}(\mathbf{q}),\widetilde{M}_{z}(\mathbf{q})]
represents the magnetization vector field
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})
in Fourier space,
\theta_{q}
denotes the angle between
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{B}_{0}
, and the asterisk ‘
*
’ stands for the complex conjugate. Note that in the perpendicular scattering geometry, the Fourier components are evaluated in the plane
q_{x}=0
.

The Fourier transform of the three-dimensional magnetization vector field (with a tilde above the symbol) is defined as follows

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{V}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}\right)d^{3}r,

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{V}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{q})\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}\right)d^{3}q.

(33)

For subsequent calculations, we introduce the following dimensionless quantities

\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}R,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{4\pi R^{3}M_{0}}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}},

(34)

and we express the dimensionless scattering vector in spherical coordinates as

\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}=[\upsilon\sin\theta_{q}\cos\phi_{q},\upsilon\sin\theta_{q}\sin\phi_{q},\upsilon\cos\theta_{q}].

(35)

Next, in (
32
) we use the following first-order approximation for the real-space magnetization vector
\mathbf{m}(\bm{\xi})
[see (
5
) and (
7
)]

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{m}_{0}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\psi_{\beta}(\bm{\xi}).

(36)

As shown in Appendix
C
, the final expression for the Fourier transform of the magnetization is then given by:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})=\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\mathbf{m}_{0}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mu=0}^{\nu}(-1)^{\nu}a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}\rho_{\nu}^{\beta}(\upsilon)P_{2\nu}^{2\mu}(\cos\theta_{q})\cos(2\mu\phi_{q}),

(37)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{\nu}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=-\frac{\upsilon j_{2\nu-1}(\upsilon)\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta})-\kappa_{\beta}\mathcal{\aleph}_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta})j_{2\nu}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}},

(38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\aleph}_{\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\nu}(\tau/2)^{2(s+\nu)-1}}{s!\Gamma(2\nu+s+1/2)},

(39)

and
\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta})
is given by (
17
). The zero-order term
\propto j_{1}(\upsilon)/\upsilon
in (
37
) represents the form factor of a homogeneously magnetized sphere
Michels 2021
. In the limiting case of an infinite applied magnetic field, which is equivalent to the limit
\kappa_{\beta}\rightarrow\infty
, the additional terms [second line in (
37
)] vanish [compare (
25
)] and the spherical form factor remains. On the other hand, if
k_{s}=0
, the additional terms also vanish because from the physical point of view, the Néel surface anisotropy cancels and from (
18
) we know that the coefficients
a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}
are linear in
k_{s}
. Taking only the terms with
\nu=1
into account and setting
\phi_{q}=\pi/2
(
\upsilon_{x}=0
), corresponding to the scattering geometry where the applied magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{0}\parallel\mathbf{e}_{z}
is perpendicular to the wave vector
\mathbf{k}_{0}\parallel\mathbf{e}_{x}
of the incident neutrons (Fig.
4
), the expression for
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})
can be written as [compare with (
21
)]:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\mathbf{m}_{0}

\displaystyle-\frac{15k_{s}}{32}\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(\upsilon)\left(\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{V}}(\theta_{q},\pi/2)\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0}\right)\mathbf{g}_{\beta},

(40)

where the radial function is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{\upsilon j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}\frac{\Upsilon_{1}(\kappa_{\beta})}{\kappa_{\beta}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}-\frac{j_{2}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}\frac{\aleph_{1}(\kappa_{\beta})}{\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa_{\beta})}.

(41)

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(\upsilon)
can be approximated for small
\kappa_{\beta}
and when only terms up to
s=1
in the infinite series (
17
) and (
39
) are kept:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\upsilon j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}\frac{\kappa_{\beta}^{2}+14}{\kappa_{\beta}^{2}+7}-\frac{7}{4}\frac{j_{2}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}\frac{\kappa_{\beta}^{2}+10}{\kappa_{\beta}^{2}+7}.

(42)

For small
\upsilon
-values, one finds the following limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{\upsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(\upsilon)=0,

(43)

which is consistent with

\displaystyle\int_{V}\bm{\psi}(\mathbf{r})\;d^{3}r=\bm{0}.

(44)

This can be seen by inspecting the definition of the Fourier transform in (
32
). Note that for

the Fourier transform is proportional to the average of the magnetization vector field
\mathbf{M}
and the maximum of this average is given by the homogeneous magnetization state. Using this result, the
\upsilon\rightarrow 0
limit for the first-order approximation in
\bm{\psi}
of the Fourier transform of the magnetization yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{\upsilon\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\upsilon,\theta_{q},\phi_{q})=\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{m}_{0}.

(45)

Beyond the linear approximation in
\bm{\psi}
, a nonvanishing term appears in
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}
in the limit
\upsilon\rightarrow 0
, which reduces the Fourier components relative to the homogeneous magnetization state. In the second order in
\bm{\psi}
, the result is [compare (
5
)]:

\displaystyle\lim_{\upsilon\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\upsilon,\theta_{q},\phi_{q})=\left[\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{V}\|\bm{\psi}(\bm{\xi})\|^{2}\;d^{3}\xi\right]\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0}

(46)

Using (

\displaystyle\frac{d\Sigma_{M}}{d\Omega}=\frac{16\pi^{2}R^{6}M_{0}^{2}b_{H}^{2}}{V}\mathcal{S}_{M},

(47)

the dimensionless two-dimensional magnetic SANS cross section

can be straightforwardly obtained as [compare (

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{M}(\upsilon,\theta_{q})

\displaystyle=|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{x}|^{2}+|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}|^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{q}+|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{z}|^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{q}

\displaystyle-(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{z}^{\ast}+\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{y}^{\ast}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{z})\sin\theta_{q}\cos\theta_{q}.

In limit
k_{s}\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\lim_{k_{s}\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{S}_{M}(\upsilon,\theta_{q})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\right)^{2}\left(m_{0,x}^{2}+m_{0,y}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{q}\right.

\displaystyle+m_{0,z}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{q}-\left.2m_{0,y}m_{0,z}\sin\theta_{q}\cos\theta_{q}\right).

The relation (

The analytical calculations presented here provide a general framework for future studies of more complicated (anisotropic) magnetic interactions.

IV
Conclusion

We have analytically computed the magnetization distribution and the ensuing magnetic small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) cross section of a spherical nanoparticle. Our micromagnetic Hamiltonian takes into account the isotropic exchange interaction, an external magnetic field, a uniaxial anisotropy for the particle’s core, and Néel anisotropy on its boundary. The resulting Helmholtz equation has been solved by expanding the real-space magnetization in terms of spherical Bessel functions and spherical harmonics. The central results are the infinite series (
16
) and its second-order expansion (
21
) for the real-space magnetization, and the corresponding Fourier transforms (
37
) and (
40
). Using these expressions, the two-dimensional magnetic SANS cross section
\mathcal{S}_{M}(\upsilon,\theta_{q})
, the azimuthally-averaged SANS signal
\mathcal{I}(\upsilon)
, and the correlation functions
\mathcal{P}(\xi)
and
\mathcal{C}(\xi)
were obtained and compared to the case of a homogeneous spin configuration (uniform magnetization vector field). The signature of Néel’s surface anisotropy (of constant
k_{s}
) has been identified in all of these functions. However, its effect is relatively small, even for large values of
k_{s}
. Taking into account the magnetodipolar and/or the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction, or shape asymmetry, will likely result in configurations with stronger spin misalignment (
e.g.
in vortex-type textures or skyrmions) and thereby in more prominent signatures in the SANS cross section and correlation function. These interactions are beyond the scope of the current analytical approach and will be considered in our future (numerical) works
Adams et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Solution of the Boundary Value Problem of the Helmholtz Equation

The coefficients
c_{\ell m}^{\beta}
in the fundamental solution (
15
) of the Helmholtz equation (
9
) must be determined such that the Neumann boundary condition (
10
) is satisfied. For this purpose, we use the method of least squares, where we make use of the orthogonality properties of the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)
. The normal derivative of (
15
) at the surface of the NM (
\xi=1
) is:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d\psi_{\beta}}{d\xi}\right|_{\xi=1}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}c_{\ell m}^{\beta}[j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)]_{\xi=1}^{\prime}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi),

(58)

where

\displaystyle[j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)]_{\xi=1}^{\prime}=\left.\frac{d}{d\xi}j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)\right|_{\xi=1}.

(59)

Our goal is now to minimize the following error functional with respect to the coefficients
c_{\ell m}^{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon[c_{\ell m}^{\beta}]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\chi_{\alpha}^{\beta}|n_{\alpha}|\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}c_{\ell m}^{\beta}[j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)]_{\xi=1}^{\prime}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\right|^{2}\sin\theta\;d\theta d\phi.

(60)

The minimum of this error functional is found from the condition that the partial derivatives of
\varepsilon[c_{\ell m}^{\beta}]
with respect to the
(c_{ij}^{\beta})^{\ast}
vanish:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varepsilon[c_{\ell m}^{\beta}]}{\partial(c_{ij}^{\beta})^{\ast}}=0,

(61)

where ‘
*
’ denotes the complex conjugate. Using the orthogonality relation (
62
) (p. 378 (14.30.8) in
Olver et al. 2010
) and by defining the integral
I_{ij}^{\alpha}
by (
63
),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)Y_{ij}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta\;d\theta d\phi

\displaystyle=\delta_{\ell i}\delta_{mj},

(62)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}Y_{ij}^{*}(\theta,\phi)|n_{\alpha}|\sin\theta\;d\theta d\phi

\displaystyle=I_{ij}^{\alpha},

(63)

the solution of (
61
) can be written as:

\displaystyle c_{ij}^{\beta}=\frac{1}{[j_{j}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)]_{\xi=1}^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\chi_{\alpha}^{\beta}I_{ij}^{\alpha}.

(64)

Alternatively, using again the indices
\ell
and
m
, expressing the coefficient
\chi_{\alpha}^{\beta}
as in (
13
), and by using the matrix-vector product, the coefficients

\displaystyle c_{\ell m}^{\beta}=\frac{k_{s}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\cdot\operatorname{diag}\left[I_{\ell m}^{x},I_{\ell m}^{y},I_{\ell m}^{z}\right]\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0}}{[j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)]_{\xi=1}^{\prime}}.

(65)

For some low-orders of
\ell
ans
m

are presented in Appendix
B
.

From (
65
) several conclusions can be drawn. First, the zero-order term (
\ell=m=0
) in (

c_{00}^{\beta}
as:

\displaystyle c_{00}^{\beta}=\frac{k_{s}\sqrt{\pi}(\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{0})}{[j_{0}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)]_{\xi=1}^{\prime}}=0,

(66)

which is due to the orthogonality of
\mathbf{m}_{0}
and
\mathbf{g}_{\beta}
with
\beta\in\{1,2\}
[see (
6
)]. Moreover, we see that

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}(\xi=0,\theta,\phi)=0,

which is a consequence of the behavior of the spherical Bessel functions of the first kind at the origin. Since the

due to

it is seen that the coefficients

vanish for odd

\displaystyle c_{\ell,m}^{\beta}=c_{\ell,-m}^{\beta}.

(68)

Taking these properties into account, one can express the solution (

and

[note that we use the convention that

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mu=0}^{\nu}a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)P_{2\nu}^{2\mu}(\cos\theta)\cos(2\mu\phi),

\displaystyle j_{l}(\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\tau}}J_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\tau),

\displaystyle J_{l}(\tau)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{s}(\tau/2)^{2s+l}}{s!\Gamma(l+s+1)}.

Appendix B
Integral Coefficients

The integrals

can be simplified, since both

are separable functions in
\theta

\displaystyle I_{\ell m}^{x}

\displaystyle=N_{\ell m}K_{\ell m}^{(1)}U_{m}^{(1)},

(72)

\displaystyle I_{\ell m}^{y}

\displaystyle=N_{\ell m}K_{\ell m}^{(1)}U_{m}^{(2)},

(73)

\displaystyle I_{\ell m}^{z}

\displaystyle=N_{\ell m}K_{\ell m}^{(2)}U_{m}^{(3)},

(74)

where

\displaystyle K_{\ell m}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)|\sin\theta|\sin\theta d\theta,

(75)

\displaystyle K_{\ell m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)|\cos\theta|\sin\theta d\theta,

(76)

\displaystyle U_{m}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}|\cos\phi|\;d\phi=(-1)^{|m|/2}\frac{2(1+(-1)^{|m|})}{1-m^{2}+\delta_{|m|,1}},

(77)

\displaystyle U_{m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}|\sin\phi|\;d\phi=\frac{2(1+(-1)^{|m|})}{1-m^{2}+\delta_{|m|,1}},

(78)

\displaystyle U_{m}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}\;d\phi=2\pi\delta_{0,m},

(79)

\displaystyle N_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}.

(80)

The integrals
U_{m}^{(1)}
,
U_{m}^{(2)}
, and
U_{m}^{(3)}

.
It is common to express the integrals

and
K_{\ell m}^{(2)}
by the substitution

(

\displaystyle K_{\ell m}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell}^{m}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\;dx,

(81)

\displaystyle K_{\ell m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell}^{m}(x)|x|\;dx.

(82)

Using
U_{m}^{(3)}=2\pi\delta_{0,m}
, we need only to compute
K_{\ell,0}^{(2)}
such that the associated Legendre polynomials in
K_{\ell,m}^{(2)}
are reduced to the Legendre polynomials [with one index only (p. 352 in
Olver et al. 2010
)]. By considering the symmetry properties of the Legendre polynomials it becomes clear that the integral must vanish for odd
\ell
and can be simplified for even
\ell
in the following way:

\displaystyle K_{\ell,0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=(1+(-1)^{\ell})\int_{0}^{1}P_{\ell}(x)x\;dx.

(83)

The closed-form of
K_{\ell,0}^{(2)}
is then found in terms of the Gamma function (p. 771 (7.126.1) in
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
):

\displaystyle K_{\ell,0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(1+(-1)^{\ell})}{4\Gamma(3/2-\ell/2)\Gamma(\ell/2+2)}.

(84)

The overall solution for
I_{\ell m}^{z}
is then written as:

\displaystyle I_{\ell m}^{z}=\frac{\pi(1+(-1)^{\ell})\sqrt{2\ell+1}}{4\Gamma(3/2-\ell/2)\Gamma(\ell/2+2)}\delta_{0,m}.

(85)

Since we only have to calculate
K_{\ell m}^{(1)}
for even
m
(since
U_{m}^{(1)}
and
U_{m}^{(2)}
include the term
1+(-1)^{|m|}
), we may use the index
m=2\mu
, and by exploiting the parity of the associated Legendre polynomials
P_{\ell}^{m}(-x)=(-1)^{\ell+m}P_{\ell}^{m}(x)
, we find:

\displaystyle K_{\ell,2\mu}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(1+(-1)^{\ell})\int_{0}^{1}P_{\ell}^{2\mu}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\;dx.

(86)

From these results it is seen that the integrals
I_{\ell m}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}
vanish for odd
\ell
and
m
(note that in (
86
) this is only the case for
m=2\mu
). For the remaining integrals
K_{2\nu,2\mu}^{(1)}
in (
86
), where
\ell=2\nu
, we do not give an expression in closed form, but there must exist one in terms of the Gamma function or the Beta function, since (p. 324 (3.251.2) in
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{s}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\;dx=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{s+1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right),

(87)

where
\mathrm{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Beta function (Euler integral), and the associated Legendre functions
P_{2\nu}^{2\mu}
of even order and degree are true polynomials, as seen for example from the related Rodrigues formula (p. 360 (14.7.14) in
Olver et al. 2010
). Since the remaining integrals are numerically easy to compute, we used Mathematica for the reconstruction of the analytically exact results, which we present in the Table below for some low orders of

Appendix C
Derivation of the Fourier Transform of the Magnetization

The Fourier transform of the magnetization vector field
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})
is written as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{V}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}\right)d^{3}r.

(88)

In the sequel, we will use dimensionless quantities. For this purpose, we define the dimensionless scattering vector
\bm{\upsilon}=\mathbf{q}R
, where
R
is the radius of the nanomagnet, the dimensionless position vector
\bm{\xi}=\mathbf{r}/R
, and the dimensionless magnetization vector
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{M}/M_{0}
, where
M_{0}
is the saturation magnetization. Substituting in (
88
) results in

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}(\bm{\upsilon})=\frac{R^{3}M_{0}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{V}\mathbf{m}(\bm{\xi})\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\bm{\upsilon}\cdot\bm{\xi}\right)\;d^{3}\xi.

(89)

The dimensionless Fourier transform
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}
is then defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{V}\mathbf{m}(\bm{\xi})\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\bm{\upsilon}\cdot\bm{\xi}\right)\;d^{3}\xi,

(90)

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}=\frac{4\pi R^{3}M_{0}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}.

(91)

The next step consists in calculating the Fourier integral of the first-order approximation (
36
) of the magnetization vector
\mathbf{m}
. Since (
36
) is formulated in dimensionless spherical coordinates
\xi,\theta,\phi
, it is convenient to express the scattering vector
\bm{\upsilon}
in spherical coordinates as well:

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=[\xi\sin\theta\cos\phi,\xi\sin\theta\sin\phi,\xi\cos\theta],

(92)

\displaystyle\bm{\upsilon}

\displaystyle=[\upsilon\sin\theta_{q}\cos\phi_{q},\upsilon\sin\theta_{q}\sin\phi_{q},\upsilon\cos\theta_{q}],

(93)

so that the plane-wave expansion of the complex exponential can be used
Jackson 1999

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\text{i}\bm{\upsilon}\cdot\bm{\xi}\right)=4\pi\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-k}^{k}(-\text{i})^{k}j_{k}(\upsilon\xi)Y_{kn}^{*}(\theta,\phi)Y_{kn}(\theta_{q},\phi_{q}).

(94)

The Fourier integral (
90
) is then expanded into the following infinite series:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-k}^{k}(-\text{i})^{k}\hat{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{kn}(\upsilon)Y_{kn}(\theta_{q},\phi_{q}),

(95)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{kn}(\upsilon)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{m}(\bm{\xi})j_{k}(\upsilon\xi)Y_{kn}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\xi^{2}\sin\theta\;d\xi d\theta d\phi.

(96)

We now use the infinite series (
15
) for
\psi_{\beta}
to express the first-order approximation of the magnetization, which leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{m}_{0}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}c_{\ell m}^{\beta}j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi).

(97)

Since the integral transform (
96
) is linear, each term in (
97
) can be separately transformed. For the zero-order term, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{m}_{0}j_{k}(\upsilon\xi)Y_{kn}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\xi^{2}\sin\theta\;d\xi d\theta d\phi=\sqrt{4\pi}\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\mathbf{m}_{0}\delta_{k,0}\delta_{n,0}.

(98)

This result is well known to the neutron-scattering community as the spherical form factor, corresponding to a uniformly-magnetized spherical particle
Michels 2021
. In the second step, we carry out the integration of the higher-order terms from (
97
). The radial and the angular parts in the higher-order terms of (
97
) are multiplicative, such that the volume integral (
96
) is separable into:

\displaystyle A_{\ell k}^{\beta}(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}j_{\ell}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)j_{k}(\upsilon\xi)\xi^{2}d\xi,

(99)

\displaystyle B_{\ell m}^{kn}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)Y_{kn}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta d\theta d\phi.

(100)

As an intermediate result, the integral (
96
) is then rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}_{kn}(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi}\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\mathbf{m}_{0}\delta_{k,0}\delta_{n,0}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}c_{\ell m}^{\beta}A_{\ell k}^{\beta}(\upsilon)B_{\ell m}^{kn}.

(101)

The integral (
100
) directly corresponds to the orthogonality relation of the spherical harmonics, and thereby we have
B_{\ell m}^{kn}=\delta_{\ell k}\delta_{mn}
(p. 378 (14.30.8) in
Olver et al. 2010
). Due to the term
\delta_{\ell k}
in
B_{\ell m}^{kn}
, and since
B_{\ell m}^{kn}
and
A_{\ell k}^{\beta}
are multiplicative in (
101
), the following spherical Hankel transform results:

\displaystyle A_{kk}^{\beta}(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}j_{k}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)j_{k}(\upsilon\xi)\xi^{2}d\xi.

(102)

In order to calculate these integrals, we replace [using (
70
)] the spherical Bessel functions of the first kind
j_{k}(\cdot)
with the ordinary Bessel functions of the first kind
J_{\iota}(\cdot)
. This yields:

\displaystyle A_{kk}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\upsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}}}W_{k}^{\beta}(\upsilon),

(103)

where

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=\int_{0}^{1}J_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}\xi)J_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\upsilon\xi)\xi d\xi.

(104)

is the Hommel integral. The result for the integral
W_{k}^{\beta}
can be found in the textbook by Gradshteyn and Ryzhik (p. 664 (6.521.1) in
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
) and is written as

\displaystyle W_{k}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=-\frac{\upsilon J_{k-\frac{1}{2}}(\upsilon)J_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta})-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}J_{k-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta})J_{k+\frac{1}{2}}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}.

(105)

Again, upon applying the relation (
70
), we write the solution for
A_{kk}^{\beta}(\upsilon)
more convenient in terms of spherical Bessel functions of the first kind as

\displaystyle A_{kk}^{\beta}(\upsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\upsilon j_{k-1}(\upsilon)j_{k}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta})-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}j_{k-1}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta})j_{k}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}.

(106)

Now that the integrals are obtained, we have to substitute (
101
) in (
95
). The term on the first line of (
101
) only accounts for
k=0
and
n=0
in (
95
). Therefore, the contribution of this term to
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}
is
j_{1}(\upsilon)/\upsilon\mathbf{m}_{0}
, since
Y_{00}(\theta,\phi)=1/\sqrt{4\pi}
. Since
B_{\ell m}^{kn}=\delta_{\ell k}\delta_{mn}
, the final result is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\mathbf{m}_{0}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-k}^{k}(-\text{i})^{k}c_{kn}^{\beta}A_{kk}^{\beta}(\upsilon)Y_{kn}(\theta_{q},\phi_{q}).

(107)

Using the properties of the coefficients
c_{kn}^{\beta}
studied in Appendix
A
, we reformulate (
107
) in terms of the associated Legendre polynomials, the indices
k=2\nu
and
n=2\mu
, and the coefficients
a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})=\frac{j_{1}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\mathbf{m}_{0}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{\beta}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mu=0}^{\nu}(-1)^{\nu}a_{\nu\mu}^{\beta}\rho_{\nu}^{\beta}(\upsilon)P_{2\nu}^{2\mu}(\cos\theta_{q})\cos(2\mu\phi_{q}),

(108)

where the radial function is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\nu}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=-\frac{\upsilon j_{2\nu-1}(\upsilon)j_{2\nu}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta})-\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta}j_{2\nu-1}(\mathrm{i}\kappa_{\beta})j_{2\nu}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}.

(109)

Using once again (
70
) and the well-known series (
71
) for the Bessel functions of the first kind, we redefine the spherical Bessel functions of imaginary arguments [as they appear in (
109
)] as:

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=j_{2\nu}(\mathrm{i}\tau)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\nu}(\tau/2)^{2(s+\nu)}}{s!\Gamma(2\nu+s+3/2)},

(110)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\aleph}_{\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}j_{2\nu-1}(\mathrm{i}\tau)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\nu}(\tau/2)^{2(s+\nu)-1}}{s!\Gamma(2\nu+s+1/2)},

(111)

such that it becomes clear that
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}(\bm{\upsilon})
is a purely real-valued function. The radial function is then rewritten as

\displaystyle\rho_{\nu}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=-\frac{\upsilon j_{2\nu-1}(\upsilon)\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta})-\kappa_{\beta}\mathcal{\aleph}_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta})j_{2\nu}(\upsilon)}{\upsilon^{2}+\kappa_{\beta}^{2}}.

(112)

By comparing this result to (
102
), we find the following pair of spherical Hankel transforms

\displaystyle\rho_{\nu}^{\beta}(\upsilon)=\int_{0}^{1}\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)j_{\nu}(\upsilon\xi)\xi^{2}d\xi.

(113)

By comparing the result (
108
) to (
16
) it becomes clear that the angular part is (due to the orthogonality relation of the spherical harmonics) shape invariant under Fourier transformation, while the spherical Hankel transform (
113
) of the radial function
\Upsilon_{\nu}(\kappa_{\beta}\xi)
remains.
