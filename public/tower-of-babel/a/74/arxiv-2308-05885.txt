1.
Introduction

We investigate the oscillation properties of a class of second-order half-linear difference equations

\Delta(r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha})+q(\zeta)x^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma)=0,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{o},

(1.1)

and

\Delta(r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha})+q(\zeta)x^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma+1)=0,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{o},

(
1.1^{\prime}
)

where the difference operator (forward)
\Delta
is given by
\Delta x(\zeta)=x(\zeta+1)-x(\zeta)
,
\sigma\geq 0
is an integer for (
1.1
) and
\sigma\geq 1
for (

1.1
′

), and
\alpha=\frac{m}{n}
for relatively prime odd positive integers
m
and
n
. Assume that the real sequences
\{r(\zeta)\}^{\infty}_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}
and
\{q(\zeta)\}^{\infty}_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}
meet the following requirements:

(H_{1})

\{r(\zeta)\}^{\infty}_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}
is a positive sequence;

(H_{2})

q(\zeta)\geq 0
and
q(\zeta)\not\equiv 0
for infinitely many values of
\zeta\in\mathbb{N}(\zeta_{0})=\{\zeta_{0},\zeta_{0}+1,\zeta_{0}+2,\cdots\}
.

“A solution of (
1.1
) is a non-trivial real sequence
\{x(\zeta)\}
that is defined for
\zeta\geq\zeta_{0}-\sigma
and satisfies (
1.1
) for all
\zeta\geq\zeta_{0}
. Obviously, if
x(\zeta)=A_{\zeta}
for
\zeta=\zeta_{0}-\sigma
,
\zeta_{0}-\sigma+1,\ldots,\zeta_{0}-1
are given, then (
1.1
) has a unique solution satisfying the above initial conditions. (
1.1
) is said to be oscillatory if a solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
of (
1.1
) is neither eventually positive nor eventually negative, and if it is not, it is said to be non-oscillatory. If all the solutions of (
1.1
) are non-oscillatory, then it is called non-oscillatory”
[
20
]
.

The equation (
1.1
) is said to be in canonical form if

R(\zeta):=\sum_{s=\zeta_{0}}^{\zeta-1}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}\rightarrow\infty\quad as\quad\zeta\rightarrow\infty.

(1.2)

The equation (
1.1
) is in non-canonical form if

\theta(\zeta):=\sum_{s=\zeta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}<\infty.

(1.3)

In recent years, the oscillation conditions for difference equations have been examined for first and higher orders (see
[
4
,

,
8
,

,
13
,
14
,

,
17
,
20
]
). We recommend
[

,

,

,
15
]
and the nearly 500 sources listed therein for a general theory of oscillation of difference equations. Second-order difference equations have considered far less attention in the study than first-order difference equations, despite the fact that they occur in mathematical biology, economics, and other fields (see
[
5
]
). Many papers
[
9
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
18
]
have some recent results on second-order difference equations. On the other hand, it looks that much less is known about the oscillation properties of (
1.1
). Most of the papers
[
5
,
6
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
16
,
21
,
24
]
deal with the qualitative properties of solutions to differential and difference equations.

The structure of non-oscillatory solutions differ tremendously between non-canonical and canonical difference equations. Generally, the first order difference of a positive solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
of the equation (
1.1
) is of constant sign eventually, but Eq. (
1.2
) assures that the solution is eventually increasing. The equation (
1.1
) has been analyzed in canonical form a number of times.

The goal of our study is to analyze the oscillation condition and asymptotic properties of the equation (
1.1
) in the form of non-canonical. As a result, we’ll assume that (
1.3
) holds in the next sections. We review at a few key oscillation outcomes for second-order non-canonical equations that inspired us to do this research.

In
[
25
]
, Zhang
et al.
obtained oscillation conditions for the following equation

\Delta(p(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha})+q(\zeta+1)f(x(\zeta+1))=0,\quad\zeta=0,1,2,\ldots.

In
[
19
]
, Li examined and established oscillatory criteria for the solutions of the following equation

\Delta(p(\zeta)g(\Delta x(\zeta)))+q(\zeta+1)f(x(\zeta+1))=0,\quad\zeta=0,1,2,\ldots.

Saker
[
23
]
investigated the following equation

\Delta(p(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta))+q(\zeta)f(x(\zeta-\sigma))=0,\quad\zeta=0,1,2,3,\ldots.

(1.4)

and established conditions for oscillation of (
1.4
).

Dinakar
et al.

[
7
]
established sufficient conditions for the oscillation of the following equation

\Delta(a(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta)^{\alpha}))+q(\zeta)x^{\alpha}(\sigma(\zeta))=0,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{0},

(1.5)

and proved that every solutions of (
1.5
) is oscillatory if

\sum_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{a(\zeta)}\sum_{s=\zeta_{0}}^{\zeta-1}A^{\alpha}(\sigma(s))q(s)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=\infty,

where
A(\zeta)=\sum_{s=\zeta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{a^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}
.

In
[
9
]
, Grace and Alzabut established new oscillations criteria for non-linear second-order equation

\Delta(a(\zeta)(\Delta y(\zeta))^{\alpha})+q(\zeta)x^{\gamma}(\zeta-m+1)+c(\zeta)x^{\mu}(\zeta+m^{*}+1)=0,

by comparison with the oscillation results of first order difference equation.

In
[
20
]
, Murugesan and Jayakuma derived oscillatory criteria for the difference equation in non-canonical form of advanced type

\Delta(r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha})+q(\zeta)x^{\alpha}(\zeta+\sigma)=0,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{o}

For the purpose of simplicity and generality, we can only deal with eventually positive solutions of (
1.1
). Any functional inequality is assumed to hold eventually.

2.
Main results

Theorem 2.1
.

If

\sum_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{r(\zeta)}\sum_{s=\zeta_{0}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=\infty,

(2.1)

then every solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
of (
1.1
) oscillates or
\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow\infty}x(\zeta)=0
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that (
1.1
) has an eventually positive solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
. Then either
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
or
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
holds eventually, say,
\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{1}\geq\zeta_{0}
.

If
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
, then we have
\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow\infty}x(\zeta)=c\geq 0
exists. Let
c>0
. Summing (
1.1
) from
\zeta_{1}
to
\zeta-1
, we have

r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}-r(\zeta_{1})(\Delta x(\zeta_{1}))^{\alpha}+\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)x^{\alpha}(s-\sigma)=0,

which gives that

r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}\leq-\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)x^{\alpha}(s-\sigma)

and hence

\Delta x(\zeta)\leq-\frac{c}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)}\left(\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Summing the above from
\zeta_{1}
to
\zeta-1
, we have

x(\zeta)-x(\zeta_{1})\leq-c\sum_{u=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(u)}\left(\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{u-1}q(s)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Taking
\zeta\rightarrow\infty
and using (
2.1
), we see that
x(\zeta)\rightarrow-\infty
as
\zeta\rightarrow\infty
. This is a contradiction and hence
c=0
.

Suppose that
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
. Let us define a sequence
\{w(\zeta)\}
given by

w(\zeta):=\frac{r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma)}>0.

Clearly,
\{w(\zeta)\}
satisfies

\Delta w(\zeta)=-q(\zeta)-\frac{\alpha w(\zeta+1)\Delta x(\zeta-\sigma)}{x(\zeta-\sigma)}\leq-q(\zeta).

(2.2)

Summing (
2.2
) from
\zeta_{1}
to
\zeta-1
, we obtain

w(\zeta)\leq w(\zeta_{1})-\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s).

In view of (
2.1
) and (
1.3
), we get that
\{\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\}
is an unbounded sequence, which is a contradiction. Hence the case
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
is not possible and hence we complete the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

If one of the following conditions

\sum_{s=\zeta_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{r(\zeta)}\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\theta^{\alpha}(s-\sigma)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=\infty

(2.3)

and

\sum_{s=\zeta_{0}}^{\infty}q(s)\theta^{\alpha+1}(s+1)=\infty

(2.4)

holds, then (
1.1
) oscillates.

Proof.

On the contrary, let us suppose that (
1.1
) has an eventually positive solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
. Then, either
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
or
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
eventually,
\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{1}\geq\zeta_{0}
.

Case I:

Assume that
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
and (
2.3
) holds. Now, for
l>\zeta
, we have

\displaystyle x(l)-x(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=\zeta}^{l-1}\Delta x(s)

\displaystyle\leq\left(r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sum_{s=\zeta}^{l-1}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}.

Thus, as
\zeta\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain

-x(\zeta)\leq\left(r(\zeta_{1})(\Delta x(\zeta_{1}))^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\theta(\zeta).

(2.5)

Using (
2.5
) in (
1.1
), we have

\Delta(r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha})\leq L^{\alpha}q(s)\theta^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma),

where
L=\left(r(\zeta_{1})(\Delta x(\zeta_{1}))^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}<0.

Summing the above from
\zeta_{1}
to
\zeta-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta_{1}))^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq r(\zeta_{1})\Delta x(\zeta_{1})+L^{\alpha}\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\theta^{\alpha}(s-\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq L^{\alpha}\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\theta^{\alpha}(s-\sigma).

It follows from the last inequality that

x(\zeta)-x(\zeta_{1})\leq L\left(\sum_{u=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}\frac{1}{r(u)}\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{u-1}q(s)\theta^{\alpha}(s-\sigma)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

By using (
2.3
), we see that
\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow}x(\zeta)=-\infty
which is a contradiction on the assumption of
\{x(\zeta)\}
.

Case II:

Assume that
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
and (
2.4
) holds.
Set

u(\zeta)=\frac{r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma)}.

(2.6)

Obviously,
u(\zeta)<0
,
\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}
. Since
\{r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}\}
is nonincreasing, we obtain

r(s)(\Delta x(s))^{\alpha}\leq r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha},\quad s\geq\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}.

Summing the above from
\zeta
to
l-1
, we have

x(l)\leq x(\zeta)+r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\sum_{s=\zeta}^{l-1}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}.

Letting
l\rightarrow\infty
in the above inequality, we have

0\leq x(\zeta)+r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\theta(\zeta),\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}.

So we get

\frac{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)}{x(\zeta)}\theta(\zeta)\geq-1,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}

or

-1\leq\theta(\zeta)u^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)<0,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta-1.

From (
2.6
), we have

\Delta u(\zeta)+q(\zeta)+\frac{\alpha u^{\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}}(\zeta)}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta-\sigma)}\leq 0,\quad\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}.

(2.7)

Multiplying (
2.7
) by
\theta^{\alpha+1}(\zeta)
and summing from
\zeta_{1}
to
l-1
, we have

\theta^{\alpha+1}(l)u(l)-\theta^{\alpha+1}(\zeta_{1})u(\zeta_{1})+\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{l-1}q(s)\theta^{\alpha+1}(s+1)\\
-(\alpha+1)\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{l-1}\frac{u(s)\theta^{\alpha}(s)}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}+\alpha\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{l-1}\frac{\theta^{\alpha+1}(s+1)u^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}}(s)}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s-\sigma)}\leq 0.

(2.8)

Now

\left|\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{\alpha}(s)u(s)}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}\right|\leq\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}|\theta^{\alpha}(s)u(s)|\leq\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}<\infty

and

\left|\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{\alpha+1}(s+1)u^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}}(s)}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s-\sigma)}\right|\leq\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s-\sigma)}\left|\theta^{\alpha}(s)u(s)\right|^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}}<\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s-\sigma)}<\infty.

By applying the above inequalities, letting
l\rightarrow\infty
in (
2.8
), we see that

\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\infty}q(s)\theta^{\alpha+1}(s+1)<\infty,

which contradicts (
2.4
).

So in either case
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
is impossible.

If
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
, then we repeat the same procedure as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
and so we omitt the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

[
7
]

Suppose that

\sum_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}^{\infty}q(\zeta)=\infty.

(2.9)

If
\{x(\zeta)\}^{\infty}_{\zeta=\zeta_{0}}
is an eventually positive solution of (
1.1
), then
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If

\limsup_{\zeta\rightarrow\infty}\left(\theta^{\alpha}(\zeta)\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)\right)>1,

(2.10)

then (
1.1
) oscillates.

Proof.

Let us assume on the contrary, without loss of generality, that (
1.1
) has an eventually positive solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
. Then, either
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
or
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
eventually, for
\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{1}\geq\zeta_{0}
.
First now, we suppose that
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
. Since
\{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\}
is decreasing, we get

\displaystyle x(\zeta-\sigma)\geq x(\zeta)\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\zeta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}\left(-r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)\Delta x(s)\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\theta(\zeta)r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\geq 0.

(2.11)

Summing (
1.1
) from
\zeta_{1}
to
\zeta-1
and using (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle-r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)x^{\alpha}(s-\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq x^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma)\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s)

\displaystyle\geq-r(\zeta)(\Delta x(\zeta))^{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha}(\zeta)\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-1}q(s).

Taking the limsup, we have a contradiction to (
2.10
).

Now suppose that
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
. Then by (
1.3
) and (
2.10
), we have (
2.9
) holds. Then by Lemma
2.5
, we have a contradiction and the proof is now complete.
∎

(2.12)

1.1
′

).

.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
\alpha\geq 1
and
\{x(\zeta)\}
is an eventually positive solution of (

1.1
′

), then

\left(\frac{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)}{x(\zeta-\sigma+1)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\leq\theta^{1-\alpha}(\zeta).

(2.13)

Proof.

Suppose that (

1.1
′

) has an eventually positive solution
\{x(\zeta)\}
. Then we get
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
or
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
eventually for
\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{1}\geq\zeta_{0}
.

Let us assume first that
\Delta x(\zeta)<0
. Since
\alpha\geq 1
, repeatting the procedure as in the proof of Theorem
2.4
, we obtain

x(\zeta-\sigma+1)\geq-\theta(\zeta)r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\geq 0,

which implies (
2.13
).

Assume now that
\Delta x(\zeta)>0
. Note that
R(\zeta-\sigma)+\theta(\zeta-\sigma)=\theta(\zeta_{0})>0
together with (
1.3
) implies
R(\zeta-\sigma)\geq\theta(\zeta)
. Now

\displaystyle x(\zeta-\sigma+1)\geq x(\zeta-\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-\sigma-1}\Delta x(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=\zeta_{1}}^{\zeta-\sigma-1}\frac{1}{r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)}r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(s)\Delta x(s)

\displaystyle\geq R(\zeta-\sigma)r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)

\displaystyle\geq\theta(\zeta)r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta),

which is obviously the same as (
2.13
) and this completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
. Suppose that Eq. (
2.12
) with

\widetilde{r}(\zeta)=\theta(\zeta)\theta(\zeta+1)r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta),

\widetilde{q}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\theta(\zeta+1)\theta^{\alpha-1}(\zeta)\theta(\zeta-\sigma+1)q(\zeta)

oscillates. Then (

1.1
′

) oscillates.

Proof.

Let us assume on the contrary that
\{x(\zeta)\}
is an eventually positive solution of (

1.1
′

). Now the equation (

1.1
′

) can be rewritten as

\Delta\left(r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\right)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\right)^{1-\alpha}q(\zeta)x^{\alpha}(\zeta-\sigma+1)\leq 0.

By Lemma
2.6
, we conclude that
\{x(\zeta)\}
is a solution of the second-order inequality

\Delta\left(r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta x(\zeta)\right)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha-1}(\zeta)q(\zeta)x(\zeta-\sigma+1)\leq 0,

for
\zeta\geq\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta\geq\zeta_{0}
. Now the inequality can be modified into

\frac{1}{\theta(\zeta+1)}\Delta\left(\theta(\zeta)\theta(\zeta+1)r^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\zeta)\Delta\left(\frac{x(\zeta)}{\theta(\zeta)}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\theta^{\alpha-1}(\zeta)q(\zeta)x(\zeta-\sigma+1)\leq 0.

(2.14)

Put

u(\zeta-1)=\frac{x(\zeta)}{\theta(\zeta)}.

Then the inequality (
2.14
) reduces to

\Delta(\widetilde{r}(\zeta)\Delta u(\zeta-1))+\widetilde{q}(\zeta)u(\zeta-\sigma)\leq 0.

(2.15)

This shows that
\{u(\zeta)\}
is an eventually positive solution of (
2.15
). Then by Lemma
2.5
, (
2.12
) has an eventually positive solution which is a contradiction and hence the proof is completed.
∎

3.
Conclusion

The results of this study are unique and also have a high degree of generality. In contrast to most previous studies, our oscillation result of the investigated equation (
1.1
) is obtained with only one condition. Also, we have derived oscillation criteria for (

1.1
′

) by comparison with the second-order linear delay difference equation in canonical form. To illustrate our results, three examples are provided.
