Convergence Analysis of Blurring Mean Shift
Ryoya Yamasaki, Toshiyuki Tanaka

Abstract
Blurring mean shift (BMS) algorithm, a variant of the mean shift algorithm, is a kernel-based iterative method for data clustering, where data points are clustered according to their convergent points via iterative blurring. In this paper, we analyze convergence properties of the BMS algorithm by leveraging its interpretation as an optimization procedure, which is known but has been underutilized in existing convergence studies. Whereas existing results on convergence properties applicable to multi-dimensional data only cover the case where all the blurred data point sequences converge to a single point, this study provides a convergence guarantee even when those sequences can converge to multiple points, yielding multiple clusters. This study also shows that the convergence of the BMS algorithm is fast by further leveraging geometrical characterization of the convergent points.

III-C

One-Dimensional Convergence Guarantee

There is a convergence guarantee that is only applicable
to the one-dimensional case (
d=1
), while covering situations where
the blurred data point sequences converge to multiple points:

[
3
, Theorem 14.2]
states that
the BMS algorithm based on the Epanechnikov kernel
K

(or the truncated-flat function
G
)
converges in a finite number of iterations.

In the one-dimensional case with the Epanechnikov kernel,
they proved that the blurred data point sequences
do not change their order through iterations.
In the same way as in Theorem
1
, one can prove
that the blurred data point sequences starting from the outermost points
are bounded and monotone.
Also, they proved that the distance with which each of the outermost points moves
in each iteration is either zero or larger than a positive constant
that is independent of the number of iterations so far.
These results constitute the proof of the finite-time convergence of the outermost points.
By repeating the same considerations for
the inner points except for the converged outermost points,
one can prove the convergence of all the blurring data point sequences.

However, their proof strategy is restricted to
the one-dimensional case with the Epanechnikov kernel.
In particular, it should be difficult to extend the argument
based on the order-preserving property to the multi-dimensional case.

III-E

Optimization View in Configuration Space

The work
[

{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}

for (locally) maximizing the objective function

\displaystyle L({\bm{u}})\coloneq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}K\biggl(\frac{{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}}{h}\biggr)

(17)

defined in terms of the configuration

{\bm{u}}\coloneq({\bm{u}}^{\top}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}^{\top}_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}
.
We here describe this optimization view.

Assume for a moment that the kernel
K
is differentiable
for the simplicity of the description.
The BMS update rule (
5
) is then rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bm{y}}_{t+1,i}&={\bm{y}}_{t,i}+\frac{h^{2}}{2\sum_{j=1}^{n}G_{t,i,j}}\biggl(-\frac{2}{h^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t,j})G_{t,i,j}\biggr)\\

(18)

In the configuration space, it can further be represented as

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t+1}={\bm{y}}_{t}+\frac{h^{2}}{2}{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{-1}\nabla L({\bm{y}}_{t})

with the abbreviation

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{t}\coloneq\diag\biggl(\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}G_{t,i,j}\biggr)_{i\in[n]}\biggr)\otimes{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd\times nd}.

(20)

It shows that the BMS algorithm can be viewed

{\bm{y}}_{1}=({\bm{x}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}

determined by (
6

The matrix
\frac{h^{2}}{2}{\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{-1}
in (
19
)
corresponds to the step size,
and the BMS algorithm is interpretable as employing

{\bm{y}}_{t}
-dependent step sizes
for the coordinates corresponding to each data point.

The existing studies
[

,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5

6

have not leveraged the above optimization view

({\bm{y}}_{t,1})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}},\ldots,({\bm{y}}_{t,n})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}

or that of the configuration sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
However, as will be shown in the following section,

IV
Convergence Analysis

IV-A

Properties of Objective Function

In this subsection

in preparation for the convergence analysis of the BMS algorithm
in this paper.

First, the function

has the following invariance:

Proposition 4

(Invariance of the objective function)
.

It holds that

{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}

Also, for a permutation

Assuming that

Proposition 5

Assume Assumption

exploit the Łojasiewicz inequality to prove
the convergence of various optimization algorithms.
Similarly to those studies, by applying abstract convergence theorems,

[
43
, Theorem 3.2]

and
[
36
, Theorem 3.1]
,
to the BMS algorithm, we obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 4

(Conditional convergence guarantee for smooth kernels)
.

Assume Assumption
1
,
and, for the closure
{\mathcal{T}}=\cl(\Conv(\{{\bm{y}}_{t}\}_{t\geq\tau}))
of
the convex hull
\Conv(\{{\bm{y}}_{t}\}_{t\geq\tau})
of

\{{\bm{y}}_{t}\}_{t\geq\tau}
with some
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}

(b1
)

the function
L
is differentiable and has a Lipschitz-continuous gradient on
{\mathcal{T}}

(i.e., there exists a constant

such that

for any

);

(b2
)

the function
L
has the Łojasiewicz property on
{\mathcal{T}}
.

Then the configuration sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges
to a stationary point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}
of the function
L
.

We next argue sufficient conditions on the kernel
K

which make the function
L
to satisfy the conditions (
b1
) and (
b2
).
The conditions (
b1
) and (
b2
) require
the function
L
to have a Lipschitz-continuous gradient
(smoothness) and the Łojasiewicz property around
the trajectory of the sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\geq\tau}
.
Simply because the summation retains the smoothness in the above-mentioned sense,
the function
L
satisfies the condition (
b1
)
if the kernel
K
satisfies the following assumption:

Assumption 2
.

The kernel
K
is differentiable with a Lipschitz-continuous gradient.

This assumption implies

.
Thus, when the kernel

is analytic or globally subanalytic,
Propositions
6
and
7
show
that the function
L
has the Łojasiewicz property.
However, in order to have the function
L
to satisfy the condition (
b2
),
one finds that
requiring only the subanalyticity, not the global subanalyticity,
for the kernel

is sufficient due to
a characteristic behavior of the BMS algorithm, as explained below:
Since Theorem
1
under Assumption
1

shows
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\Conv(\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]})^{n}
for any

,
one can restrict the domain of every kernel

as a function of
{\bm{u}}
to

without any problems.
Also, Assumption

ensures the boundedness of

.
Thus, for a subanalytic kernel

,
the restriction of

to

is a subanalytic function with a bounded graph

,
and hence the restriction of the corresponding function

is

and

and Assumption

Accordingly, under Assumptions

and
2
,
we can employ the following assumption instead of the condition (

Assumption 3
.

The kernel
K
is analytic or subanalytic.

Thus, the following holds as a corollary of Theorem
4
:

Theorem 5

(Convergence guarantee for smooth kernels;
corollary of Theorem
4
)
.

Assume Assumptions

,
2
, and

.
Then the configuration sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to
a stationary point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}

The main significance of Theorem

that it establishes the convergence of the BMS

.
For these truncated kernels,

IV-C
3
Convergence Rate Bounds

On the basis of an abstract convergence theorem

[
36
, Theorem 3.5]
,
which leverages the Łojasiewicz property,
we can obtain the following convergence rate bounds.

Theorem 6

(Convergence rate bounds for smooth kernels)
.

Assume assumptions of Theorem
4
or
5
,
and that the function
L
has the Łojasiewicz exponent
\theta
at
\bar{{\bm{y}}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{y}}_{t}
.
Then one has that

(c1
)

if
\theta\in[0,\frac{1}{2})

then the BMS algorithm converges in a finite number of iterations
(i.e., there exist
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
M=M_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}}
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

{\bm{y}}_{t}=\bar{{\bm{y}}}
,

{\bm{y}}_{t,i}={\bm{z}}_{m}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L({\bm{y}}_{t})=L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})
for any
t\geq\tau
);

(c2
)

if
\theta=\frac{1}{2}

then the BMS algorithm achieves the exponential rate convergence
(i.e., there exist
q\in(0,1)
and
M=M_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|=O(q^{t})
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|=O(q^{t})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})=O(q^{2t})
);

(c3
)

if
\theta\in(\frac{1}{2},1)

then the BMS algorithm achieves the polynomial rate convergence
(i.e., there exist
M=M_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|=O(t^{-\frac{1-\theta}{2\theta-1}})
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|=O(t^{-\frac{1-\theta}{2\theta-1}})

for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})=O(t^{-\frac{1}{2\theta-1}})
).

Calculation of the Łojasiewicz exponent
\theta
at
a convergent stationary point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
is difficult in general,
but the followings are known about
\theta
:
When
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
is a local minimizer of
L
,
\theta
becomes 0.
Also, when
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
is a critical point of
L

that is not a local minimizer, if
L
is twice differentiable and the Hessian of
L
is non-degenerate at
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
,
one has
\theta=1/2
, which is the typical value
of the Łojasiewicz exponent.
Furthermore, when the BMS algorithm uses a
C^{1}
piecewise polynomial kernel,
we can apply known upper bounds of the Łojasiewicz exponent for
polynomial functions
[
46
,
47
]
.
One of such bounds show
\theta\leq 1-1/\max\{l(3l-4)^{nd-1},2l(3l-3)^{nd-2}\}

when the kernel
K
is a
C^{1}
piecewise polynomial
kernel with maximum degree
l\geq 2

(e.g.,
l=4
for the Biweight kernel);
see these studies and a similar discussion for
the MS algorithm in
[
17
]
.

It should be noted that the convergence rate bounds given
in Theorem
6
may be loose,
since they are based on
generic theoretical guarantees for gradient-based algorithms,
and do not reflect the structures specific to the BMS algorithm.

IV-D

Refined Convergence Rate Bounds for Smooth Kernels

Besides the convergence rate bounds
of the BMS algorithm with a smooth kernel that we have just obtained via
the Łojasiewicz property in Theorem
6
,
we also reveal a case where refined convergence rate bounds
can be obtained from a discussion specific to the BMS algorithm.
In this subsection we describe those refined convergence rate bounds.

According to the discussion in the previous subsection, we find that,
under the assumptions of Theorem
4
or
5
,
a convergent point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
of the configuration sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is a stationary point of the function
L
.
Stationary points of the function
L
are fully characterized in terms of the BMS graph,
as in the following theorem.

Theorem 7

(Geometrical characterization of a stationary point)
.

Assume Assumption
1
.
Then the following two statements are equivalent:

(d1
)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j})G(\frac{{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}}{h})=\bm{0}_{d}
for all
i\in[n]
.

(d2
)

The BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bm{u}}
is singular
(i.e., either
{\bm{u}}_{i}={\bm{u}}_{j}
or
G(\frac{{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}}{h})=0
holds for every pair
\{i,j\}\in[n]^{2}
).

The statement (
d1
) implies that
{\bm{u}}
is a fixed point of the BMS update
(i.e., (
6
) with
{\bm{y}}_{t}={\bm{u}}
gives
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}={\bm{u}}
).
It further implies that
\nabla L({\bm{u}})=\bm{0}_{nd}
holds
if
K(\frac{{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}}{h})
for all
\{i,j\}\in[n]^{2}
are differentiable at
{\bm{u}}
.

This geometrical characterization of the fixed point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
,
to which the configuration sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges
under the assumptions of Theorem
4
or
5
,
indicates that the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}

of the convergent point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
is singular.
Taking this observation into account,
the convergent point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
satisfies either of the two conditions:

(e1
)

the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
of
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
is singular and stable;

(e2
)

the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
of
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
is singular and unstable,

as exemplified
in Figure
1
(
i
) and (
iv
).

From our experience, we conjecture
that a convergent point with an unstable BMS graph
would not have a finite-volume basin of attraction in the configuration space,
and that the instability of the BMS graph of a convergent point
will not happen with a generic initial configuration.
As we have so far not been successful in proving or disproving this conjecture,
we would leave the study of this conjecture for future research.
For the case (
e2
)
we can only rely on Theorem
6

to obtain convergence rate bounds,
while for the case (
e1
) we can give refined convergence rate bounds
by leveraging geometrical properties of the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}

of the convergent point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
.

For the case (
e1
),
a convergence guarantee of the sequence
({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}

(Theorem
4
or
5
)
shows that there exists
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

{\bm{y}}_{\tau},{\bm{y}}_{\tau+1},\ldots
belong to an open neighborhood
of the convergent point
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
so that
the BMS graphs
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}},{\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau+1}},\ldots

are the same as the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
,
which is singular and hence closed.
Therefore, the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}}
is closed with some
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
and Theorem
2
is applicable to the case (
e1
)
to prove the exponential rate convergence.

Looking into the proof of Theorem
2

in the supplementary material,
one observes that, among properties of
g
available to us,
only the non-negativity and monotonicity of
g
are used there.
Under the geometrical information (
e1
) of the stationary point,
if one is allowed to assume stronger regularity of
g(u)

in the vicinity of
u=0
,
as in Assumption
4
below,
then one can prove an improved convergence rate bound.

Assumption 4
.

The kernel
K
has the profile
k
that is differentiable with
a Lipschitz-continuous derivative on some neighborhood of the origin,

(0,v]
with some
v>0

(so that, under Assumption
1
,
there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

g
in (
7
) satisfies
|g(u)-g(0)|\leq cu
for any
u\in[0,v]
).

It should be noted that Assumption
4

holds under Assumption
2
.

This assumption allows us to apply a local approximation of
the function
G
in the BMS algorithm (
5
)
around the convergent point that satisfies (
e1
).
This approximation yields a tighter evaluation of the time evolution of
the diameter for components of the BMS graph,
defined similarly to (
13
),
and then leads to a tighter convergence rate bound, for the case (
e1
).

These arguments
are summarized as follows:

Theorem 8

(Convergence rate bounds for smooth kernels)
.

Assume the assumptions of Theorem
4

and
(
e1
)
with
\bar{{\bm{y}}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{y}}_{t}
.
Then the BMS algorithm achieves the exponential rate convergence:
There exists
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
all the BMS graphs
({\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{t}})_{t\geq\tau}
are the same as
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
,
and there exist
q\in[\frac{3}{4},1)
and

M=M_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}}
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|=O(q^{t})
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|=O(q^{t})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})=O(q^{2t})
.
Assume further Assumption
4
.
Then the BMS algorithm achieves the cubic convergence:
Take
\tau
and define
M
as above.
Then there exist
\tau^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
p\in(0,\infty)
,
q\in(0,1)
, and

M
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|\leq p\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|^{3}
for any
t\geq\tau^{\prime}
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|=O(q^{3^{t}})
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|=O(q^{3^{t}})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})=O(q^{2\cdot 3^{t}})
.

Note that the cubic convergence
\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|\leq p\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|^{3}

leads to all the remaining convergence rate bounds described above, but we could not prove

\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|\leq p\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|^{3}
.

Theorem
8
clarifies that
the cubic convergence shown by
[
4
,
5
]

in a certain limited situation (see Section
III-D
) should rather be regarded
as the typical convergence rate for the BMS algorithm for a variety of kernels
in the finite-sample setting.
Indeed, one can empirically observe that,
once the BMS graph becomes closed,
one would only need a few more iterations
for the blurred data point sequences, represented in a
floating-point format on a computer, to converge;
see Sections S4 and S5 of the supplementary material.

IV-E

Convergence Guarantee and Rate Bounds
for Non-Smoothly Truncated Kernels

Unlike the arguments in Section
IV-C
on smooth kernels,
one cannot apply the abstract convergence theorems

[
43
,
36
]

to ensure the convergence of the configuration sequence

({\bm{y}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by the BMS algorithm with a non-smooth kernel.
It turns out, however, that convergence properties of
the BMS algorithm with a non-smoothly truncated kernel,
such as the cosine and Epanechnikov kernels, can still be analyzed
by leveraging geometrical structures inherent in the algorithm.

The non-smooth truncation of a kernel
K
implies that
the function
g
has discontinuity at the truncation point.
In other words,

\alpha\coloneq\inf\{g(u)/g(0),u\in[0,\infty)\mid g(u)\neq 0\}>0

(26)

holds.
On the basis of this property and
the convergence guarantee of the objective sequence

(L({\bm{y}}_{t}))_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
(Theorem
3
), one can prove that
the BMS graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}}
becomes closed with a sufficiently large
\tau
.
This allows us to employ the proof strategy of
Theorems
2
and
8

to derive the following convergence theorem:

Theorem 9

(Convergence guarantee and rate bounds for non-smoothly truncated kernels)
.

Assume Assumption
1

and that the kernel
K
is a non-smoothly truncated kernel.
Then the BMS algorithm converges and achieves the exponential rate convergence:
There exist
\bar{{\bm{y}}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{y}}_{t}
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
all the BMS graphs
({\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{t}})_{t\geq\tau}
are the same as
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
,
and there exist
q\in[\frac{3}{4},1)
and
M=M_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}}
different points

{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|=O(q^{t})
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|=O(q^{t})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})=O(q^{2t})
.
Assume further Assumption
4
.
Then the BMS algorithm achieves the cubic convergence:
For
\bar{{\bm{y}}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{y}}_{t}
, take
\tau
and define
M
as above.
Then there exist
\tau^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
p\in(0,\infty)
,
q\in(0,1)
, and

M
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|\leq p\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|^{3}
for any
t\geq\tau^{\prime}
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{y}}}\|=O(q^{3^{t}})
,

\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{z}}_{m}\|=O(q^{3^{t}})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})=O(q^{2\cdot 3^{t}})
.

Additionally, under this convergence guarantee,
we can further ensure the finite-time convergence
of the BMS algorithm for certain non-smoothly
truncated kernels including the Epanechnikov kernel:

Theorem 10

(Convergence rate bound for, e.g., Epanechnikov kernel)
.

Assume assumptions of Theorem
9

and that the profile
k
of the kernel
K
satisfies

k(u^{2}/2)=k(0)+(k^{\prime}(0+)/2)u^{2}
for any
u\in[0,\beta^{\prime}]
with
some
\beta^{\prime}\in(0,\beta]
for the truncation point
\beta
of
K

(for example, the Epanechnikov kernel).
Then the BMS algorithm converges in a finite number of iterations:
There exist
\bar{{\bm{y}}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{y}}_{t}
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
all the BMS graphs
({\mathcal{G}}_{{\bm{y}}_{t}})_{t\geq\tau}
are the same as
{\mathcal{G}}_{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}
,
and there exist
M=M_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau}}
different points
{\bm{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

{\bm{y}}_{t}=\bar{{\bm{y}}}
,

{\bm{y}}_{t,i}={\bm{z}}_{m}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{\tau},m}
for all
m\in[M]
,
and
L({\bm{y}}_{t})=L(\bar{{\bm{y}}})
for any
t\geq\tau
.

The ‘finite number of iterations’ in this theorem is
practically a few dozen as far as we have experienced;
refer to Section S4 of the supplementary material.
This theorem serves as a multi-dimensional
generalization of
[
3
, Theorem 14.2]

that we reviewed in Section
III-C
.

Lemma 1
.

Assume Assumption
1
.
Then there exists
\bar{a}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}L({\bm{y}}_{t+1})-L({\bm{y}}_{t})&\geq R({\bm{y}}_{t+1}|{\bm{y}}_{t})-R({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t})\\
&\geq\bar{a}\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1}-{\bm{y}}_{t}\|^{2}\end{split}

(27)

holds for all
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Let us introduce the abbreviation

{\mathbf{G}}_{t}\coloneq(G_{t,i,j})_{i,j\in[n]}\otimes{\mathbf{I}}_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd\times nd}
.
The
{\bm{y}}
-dependent part of the minorizer

R({\bm{y}}|{\bm{y}}_{t})
times
-h^{2}
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{G_{t,i,j}}{2}\|{\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{y}}_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}G_{t,i,j}\|{\bm{y}}_{i}\|^{2}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}G_{t,i,j}{\bm{y}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{j}

\displaystyle={\bm{y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{S}}_{t}-{\mathbf{G}}_{t}){\bm{y}}.

(28)

Also, the update rule (
5
) of the BMS algorithm is expressed as

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t+1}={\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{-1}{\mathbf{G}}_{t}{\bm{y}}_{t}.

(29)

Therefore, one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}&h^{2}\{R({\bm{y}}_{t+1}|{\bm{y}}_{t})-R({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t})\}\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{G_{t,i,j}}{2}\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t,j}\|^{2}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{G_{t,i,j}}{2}\|{\bm{y}}_{t+1,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t+1,j}\|^{2}\\
&={{\bm{y}}_{t}}^{\top}({\mathbf{S}}_{t}-{\mathbf{G}}_{t}){\bm{y}}_{t}-({\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{-1}{\mathbf{G}}_{t}{\bm{y}}_{t})^{\top}({\mathbf{S}}_{t}-{\mathbf{G}}_{t})({\mathbf{S}}_{t}^{-1}{\mathbf{G}}_{t}{\bm{y}}_{t}).\end{split}

(30)

It generally holds that, for matrices

\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\bm{y}}_{t},*}
, for some
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
.
Let

be an arbitrary unit vector,
and assume that there exist

VI
Conclusion

In this study, we analyzed convergence properties of the BMS algorithm by utilizing
its interpretation as an optimization procedure for the objective function
L
,
which is known but has been underutilized in existing convergence studies.
Consequently, we presented a convergence guarantee
when the blurred data point sequences can
converge to multiple points and yield multiple clusters,
when the algorithm is based on a
C^{1}
subanalytic kernel
(which includes the biweight and triweight kernels), or a non-smoothly
truncated kernel (which includes the cosine and Epanechnikov kernels).
We also showed that the algorithm for these kernels
typically achieves the cubic convergence,
and the finite-time convergence for the Epanechnikov kernel.
On the other hand, it is unanswered whether the case (
e2
) actually
occurs, for which the algorithm has only a weaker convergence rate bound.

\bar{{\mathcal{P}}}_{1}\coloneq\{{\bm{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}_{n_{1}}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}_{1}

and
\bar{{\mathcal{P}}}_{2}\coloneq\{{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{v}}_{n_{2}}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}_{2}

be the sets of these points, where
n_{1},n_{2}\in[d+1]
denote
the numbers of the points in
\bar{{\mathcal{P}}}_{1}
and
\bar{{\mathcal{P}}}_{2}
, respectively.
One can then let

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mu_{j}{\bm{v}}_{j}

(S6)

with
\lambda_{i},\mu_{j}>0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mu_{j}=1
.

By assumption, we have
\|{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}\|^{2}\leq L_{1}^{2}

for
{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{u}}_{j}\in\bar{{\mathcal{P}}}_{1}
. Multiplying it with
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}>0
and summing up that result over

i,j\in[n_{1}]
, one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}\|{\bm{u}}_{i}\|^{2}-2\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\|{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}\|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}\sum_{j\not=i}\lambda_{j}\|{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}\|^{2}\\
&\leq L_{1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})\\
&=L_{1}^{2}(1-\|\bm{\lambda}\|^{2}),\end{split}

(S7)

where
\bm{\lambda}\coloneq(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n_{1}})^{\top}
.
Similarly, since
\|{\bm{v}}_{i}-{\bm{v}}_{j}\|^{2}\leq L_{2}^{2}

for
{\bm{v}}_{i},{\bm{v}}_{j}\in\bar{{\mathcal{P}}}_{2}
, one has

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mu_{j}\|{\bm{v}}_{j}\|^{2}-2\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}\leq L_{2}^{2}(1-\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}),

(S8)

where
\bm{\mu}\coloneq(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n_{2}})^{\top}
.

Assume to the contrary that for any
i\in[n_{1}]
and

j\in[n_{2}]
one had
\|{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{v}}_{j}\|^{2}\geq\frac{L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2}}{2}
.
Multiplying it with
\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}>0
and summing up that result over

i\in[n_{1}]
and
j\in[n_{2}]
, one would have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{i}\|{\bm{u}}_{i}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mu_{j}\|{\bm{v}}_{j}\|^{2}-2\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\|{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{v}}_{j}\|^{2}\geq\frac{L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2}}{2}.

(S9)

Combining it with (
S7
) and (
S8
),
one would obtain
L_{1}^{2}\|\bm{\lambda}\|^{2}+L_{2}^{2}\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}\leq 0
,
which is a contradiction
since
L_{1}>0
or
L_{2}>0
and
\|\bm{\lambda}\|^{2},\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}>0
.
∎

Preliminaries on Łojasiewicz inequality:

Let

The concavity of

The Łojasiewicz inequality (

Also, it is known that the Łojasiewicz inequality (

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(f({\bm{u}}^{\prime})-f({\bm{u}}))\|\nabla f({\bm{u}})\|\geq 1\text{ at }({\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}})\text{ such that }{\bm{u}}\in\bar{{\mathcal{U}}}({\bm{u}}^{\prime},f,{\mathcal{T}},\epsilon,\delta)

S3 Review of Previous Work

S3.1 Theorem 4 of
[
2
]

[
2
, Theorem 4]
claimed finite-time convergence
of the BMS algorithm with an additional assumption.
The claim is reproduced in the following:

Claim S3

(Theorem 4 of
[

]
)
.

If the blurred data points cannot move arbitrarily close to each other,
and

is either zero or larger than a fixed positive constant,

One may be able to criticize this claim from at least two aspects.
First, as for the assumption “the blurred data points cannot move

the finite-precision nature of the floating-point representations
on computers is supposed to be the cause that makes the assumption valid
(see the second paragraph of
[
2

If it is the case, then one has to modify the BMS update rule
in such a way that it incorporates the rounding
due to the finite-precision representation.
The argument in
[
2
]
, however, does not include
such a modification of the BMS algorithm.
Second, one might alternatively be able to regard the claim
as being applicable to the original BMS algorithm without any
modifications with regard to rounding.
If it is the case, then one should argue under what conditions
the assumption holds true.
However,
[
2
]
does not provide any argument about this,
which would make the significance of this claim quite obscure.

S5 Demonstration of Convergence Rate

In this section, we demonstrate the cubic convergence of the BMS algorithm
under a specific finite-sample setting.
The whole process of the BMS algorithm can be described explicitly
when data points are located at the vertices of a regular simplex.
In this case, the blurred data points move toward the center
of the simplex, and every BMS update shrinks the simplex
by a certain scale factor.
Assume that
n
blurred data point
{\bm{y}}_{t,1},\ldots,{\bm{y}}_{t,n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

with
n\leq d+1

are vertices of a
(n-1)
-dimensional regular simplex
centered at the origin,
and
{\bm{y}}_{t,1}=(r_{t}/\sqrt{n},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
with
r_{t}>0

so that
\|{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t,j}\|=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n-1}}r_{t}
for all
i,j\in[n]
such that
i\neq j

and
\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\bm{0}_{n(n-1)}\|=r_{t}
.
Under this setting, the update of
{\bm{y}}_{t,i}
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bm{y}}_{t+1,i}&=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}G\bigl(\frac{{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t,j}}{h}\bigr){\bm{y}}_{t,j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}G\bigl(\frac{{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t,j}}{h}\bigr)}\\
&=\frac{g(0){\bm{y}}_{t,i}+g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))\sum_{j\not=i}{\bm{y}}_{t,j}}{g(0)+(n-1)g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}\quad(\because\text{$\|\tfrac{{\bm{y}}_{t,i}-{\bm{y}}_{t,j}}{h}\|^{2}/2=(r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1)$ for $j\neq i$})\\
&=\frac{g(0)-g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}{g(0)+(n-1)g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}{\bm{y}}_{t,i}\quad(\because{\textstyle\sum_{j\not=i}{\bm{y}}_{t,j}=-{\bm{y}}_{t,i}}).\end{split}

(S42)

This shows that the BMS update shrinks the
n
blurred data points
toward the origin by the scale factor

\frac{g(0)-g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}{g(0)+(n-1)g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}.

(S43)

It furthermore shows that when the data points are located
at the vertices of a regular simplex,
the blurred data points at any
t
are also located at the vertices
of a simplex shrunk by a certain factor.
Then, one has

\displaystyle r_{t+1}=\frac{g(0)-g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}{g(0)+(n-1)g((r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1))}r_{t}\text{ for all $t\in{\mathbb{N}}$}.

(S44)

When

consists of
n

When

one can apply Theorem

to show the exponential rate convergence.
Furthermore, under Assumption
4

(there exists a constant

such that

satisfies

if

,

\displaystyle r_{t+1}\leq\frac{g(0)-\{g(0)-c(r_{t}/h)^{2}/(n-1)\}}{g(0)}r_{t}\leq\frac{c}{(n-1)g(0)h^{2}}r_{t}^{3}

which implies the cubic convergence when
