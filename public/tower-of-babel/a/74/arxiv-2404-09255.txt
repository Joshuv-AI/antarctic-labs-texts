Quiver matroids Matroid morphisms, quiver Grassmannians, their Euler characteristics and 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} -points
Manoel Jarra, Oliver Lorscheid, Eduardo Vital

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce morphisms for matroids with coefficients (in the sense of Baker and Bowler) and quiver matroids. We investigate their basic properties, such as functoriality, duality, minors and cryptomorphic characterizations in terms of vectors, circuits and bases (a.k.a. Grassmann-Plücker functions). We generalize quiver matroids to quiver matroid bundles and construct their moduli space, which is an 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} -analogue of a complex quiver Grassmannian. Eventually we introduce a suitable interpretation of 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} -points for these moduli spaces, so that in ‘nice’ cases their number is equal to the Euler characteristic of the associated complex quiver Grassmannian.

Matroid quotients and flag matroids

A matroid quotient is a strong map that is the identity on the respective underlying sets. Matroid quotients have been generalized to oriented matroids in

Flag matroids have been introduced in

Quiver representations and quiver Grassmannians

A quiver representation over a field is a collection of vector spaces, indexed by the vertices of the quiver, together with a collection of linear maps, indexed by the arrows of the quiver. Quiver representations have been widely studied because of their central relevance for the representation theory of finite dimensional algebras; for instance, see
[
ASS06
]
.

The quiver Grassmannian of a quiver representation is the collection of all subrepresentations with a given dimension vector. The Caldero-Chapoton formula (
[
CC06
]
) expresses the structure constants of cluster algebras in terms of the Euler characteristics of complex quiver Grassmannians, which turns an intricate combinatorial problem into a geometric problem. While the quiver Grassmannians of a wild quiver assume every isomorphism type of a projective scheme (
[
Rei13
]
,
[
Rin18
]
), quiver Grassmannians of tame quivers are varieties that have a Schubert decomposition into affine spaces (
[
LW19b
]
). In particular, the Euler characteristic corresponds to the number of Schubert cells in the latter case.

Flag varieties resurface as a particular type of quiver Grassmannians: the underlying quiver is of (equi-oriented) type
A_{n}
and the quiver representation consists of a sequence of identity self-maps of a linear
n
-space. A point of this flag variety corresponds to a flag of linear subspaces, which is the same thing as a flag matroid over the base field.

Morphisms of matroids

For the purpose of this introduction, we assume that the reader is familiar with Baker-Bowler theory (as in
[
BB19
]
); we refer to
Section 1.3
for a summary. An
idyll
is a tract whose nullset is closed under addition. An idyll is
perfect
if vectors are orthogonal to covectors for all matroids over the idyll, which is an important property for our purposes; in particular, the notions of weak and strong matroids agree for perfect idylls (
[
BB19
, Thm. 3.46]
). Examples of perfect idylls are fields and partial fields, the Krasner hyperfield
{\mathbb{K}}
, the tropical hyperfield
{\mathbb{T}}
, the sign hyperfield
{\mathbb{S}}
and, more generally, stringent hyperfields (
[
BP19
]
). More generally, every idyll can be completed to a perfect idyll (
[
BZ23
]
).

Let
F
be a perfect idyll. A matrix
\Phi
with entries
\Phi_{i,j}\in F
is
submonomial
if every row and every column contains at most one nonzero entry. If
\Phi
is indexed by
T\times S
, then it defines a map
\Phi:F^{S}\to F^{T}
by the rule
(\Phi\cdot X)_{i}=\sum_{j\in S}\Phi_{i,j}X_{j}
, which defines an element of
F
since the sum contains at most one nonzero term. Note that the composition of such maps is well-defined since the product
\Psi\cdot\Phi
of submonomial matrices
\Phi
and
\Psi
is again submonomial.

Consider
F
-matroids
N
and
M
with respective underlying sets
S
and
T
and respective vector sets
{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\subseteq F^{S}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}\subseteq F^{T}
. A
morphism
\Phi:N\to M
of
F
-matroids
(or, for short, an
F
-morphism
) is a submonomial matrix
\Phi
indexed by
T\times S
such that
\Phi\cdot{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
. It is clear from the definition that morphisms of
F
-matroids are composable, which yields the category
\Mat_{F}
of
F
-matroids.

Push-forward along an idyll morphism

Let
f:F\to G
be a morphism of perfect idylls and
\Phi
a submonomial matrix with coefficients in
F
. The
push-forward of
\Phi
along
f
is the matrix
f_{\ast}\Phi
with coefficients
(f_{\ast}\Phi)_{i,j}=f(\Phi_{i,j})
, which are elements of
G
. The following is
Section 2.6
.

Proposition A

(functoriality)
.

The push-forward of an
F
-morphism
\Phi:N\to M
along a morphism
f:F\to G
of perfect idylls is a
G
-morphism
f_{\ast}\Phi:f_{\ast}N\to f_{\ast}M
. This defines a functor
f_{\ast}:\Mat_{F}\to\Mat_{G}
.

Duality

We denote the transpose of a submonomial matrix
\Phi
by
\Phi^{t}
, which is also a submonomial matrix.

Proposition B

(duality)
.

The transpose of an
F
-morphism
\Phi:N\to M
is an
F
-morphism
\Phi^{t}:M^{\ast}\to N^{\ast}
. This defines a contravariant endofunctor on
\Mat_{F}
whose square is the identity.

Relation to strong maps

The
augmentation
of a set
S
is the pointed set
\underline{S}=S\sqcup\{0\}
with base point
0
. Let
\Phi
be a submonomial matrix with coefficients in
F
and indexed by
T\times S
. The
underlying map
of
\Phi
is the map
{\underline{\Phi}}:{\underline{S}}\to{\underline{T}}
with
\underline{\Phi}(j)=i
if
\Phi_{i,j}\neq 0
and
\underline{\Phi}(j)=0
if
j=0
or if
\Phi_{i,j}=0
for all
i
. Note that
{\underline{\Phi}}:{\underline{S}}\to{\underline{T}}
is
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-linear
, in the sense that it preserves base points and that for every
i\in T
, there is at most one
j\in S
with
{\underline{\Phi}}(j)=i
.

We denote the underlying matroid of an
F
-matroid
M
by
\underline{M}
. Note that
{\underline{M}}
has the same ground set as
M
, and that strong maps of matroids are, by definition, maps between augmented ground sets.

Proposition C

(relation to strong maps)
.

Let
N
and
M
be
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids with respective underlying sets
S
and
T
. A submonomial matrix
\Phi
with entries in
{\mathbb{K}}
is a morphism
\Phi:N\to M
of
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids if and only if
{\underline{\Phi}}:{\underline{N}}\to{\underline{M}}
is a strong map. In particular, the underlying map
\underline{\Phi}:\underline{S}\to\underline{T}
of an
F
-morphism
\Phi:N\to M
(where
F
is any perfect idyll) is a strong map
{\underline{\Phi}}:{\underline{N}}\to{\underline{M}}
between the underlying matroids.

Remark
.

Since
1
is the unique nonzero element of
{\mathbb{K}}
, a submonomial matrix
\Phi
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{K}}
is uniquely determined by its underlying map
{\underline{\Phi}}
. For the same reason, a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid
M
is uniquely determined by its underlying matroid
{\underline{M}}
. This means that
Relation to strong maps
provides a faithful embedding of
\Mat_{\mathbb{K}}
into the category of usual matroids together with strong maps.

While every matroid is the underlying matroid
{\underline{M}}
of a (uniquely determined)
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid
M
, not every strong map comes from a
{\mathbb{K}}
-morphism. More precisely, a strong map
\mysigma:{\underline{N}}\to{\underline{M}}
comes from a
{\mathbb{K}}
-morphism if and only if it is
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-linear.

This means that isomorphisms, restrictions, contractions, quotients, and compositions thereof, come from
{\mathbb{K}}
-morphism. Strong maps that identify distinct parallel elements do not come from
{\mathbb{K}}
-morphisms.

Further examples

If

{\mathcal{V}}_{N}
is a linear subspace of

. A

A

Warning
.

There is a tension between linear algebra and matroid theory, which stems from the fact for a field
k
, the vector set of a
k
-matroid represents the dual of its underlying matroid. This culminates in the most confusing fact that a (submonomial) linear map represents a strong map in the opposite direction.

To explain, the rank of a
k
-matroid
M
is equal to the
codimension
of its vector set
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}\subseteq k^{T}
. More to the point,
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
represents the dual
\underline{M}^{\ast}
of the underlying matroid
\underline{M}
of
M
. The
dimension
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
equals the rank of
\underline{M}^{\ast}
.

A
k
-morphism
\Phi:N\to M
between
k
-matroids
N
and
M
defines a strong map
\underline{\Phi}:\underline{N}\to\underline{M}
between the respective underlying matroids
in the same direction
. The matroids
represented by
{\mathcal{V}}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
are, however, the duals of
\underline{N}
and
\underline{M}
, respectively. Therefore
\Phi
induces the strong map
\underline{\Phi}^{t}:\underline{M}^{\ast}\to\underline{N}^{\ast}
between the matroids represented by
{\mathcal{V}}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}

in the opposite direction
.

Let

-matroid on

Proposition D

(pre-image)

There is a unique

Cryptomorphisms

Let
N
and

be
F
-matroids with respective underlying sets

and

Theorem E

.

Let

(1)

(4)

\sum_{k=0}^{w}\ (-1)^{k}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{w})\cdot\Phi_{{\underline{\Phi}}(y_{k}),y_{k}}\cdot\mymu\big(\underline{\Phi}(y_{k}),x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r}\big)\quad\in\quad N_{F},

Minors

Let

and

be

-matroids with respective ground sets

-morphism. For subsets

Proposition F

(minors)
.

If

Relation to
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representations of quivers

An
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation of
Q
is a tuple
\Lambda=\big(({\Lambda}_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}},(\Lambda_{\myalpha})_{\myalpha\in Q_{1}}\big)
where
\Lambda_{v}
is a finite pointed set with base point
0
(for
v\in Q_{0}
) and
\Lambda_{\myalpha}:{\Lambda}_{s(\myalpha)}\to{\Lambda}_{t(\myalpha)}
(for
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
) is an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-linear map
, i.e.
\Lambda_{\myalpha}(0)=0
and
\#\Lambda_{\myalpha}^{-1}(e)\leqslant 1
for
e\neq 0
.

It is a general phenomenon that (postulated) objects in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry surface as certain types of matroids, which are usually of a particularly simple shape. Quiver representations form a first such instance (for this paper): an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation
\Lambda
defines a
(Q,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid
\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)
whose matroids
\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)_{v}
(for
v\in Q_{0}
) are characterized by their vector sets
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)_{v}}={\mathbb{K}}^{\Lambda_{v}-\{0\}}
and whose submonomial matrices
\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)_{\myalpha}
(for
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
) have coefficients

\big(\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda_{\myalpha})\big)_{i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}1&\text{if }\Lambda_{\myalpha}(j)=i,\\
0&\text{if }\Lambda_{\myalpha}(j)\neq i.\end{cases}

In other words,
\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)_{\myalpha}
is the unique submonomial matrix with coefficients in
{\mathbb{K}}
such that
\underline{\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)_{\myalpha}}=\Lambda_{\myalpha}
.

Conversely, every
(Q,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid
M
has an
underlying
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation

\mathsf{U}_{Q}(M)
with pointed sets
\mathsf{U}_{Q}(M)_{v}=\underline{E_{v}}
(for
v\in Q_{0}
), where
E_{v}
is the ground set of
M_{v}
, and
\mathsf{U}_{Q}(M)_{\myalpha}=\underline{M_{\myalpha}}
. We have
\mathsf{U}_{Q}\circ\mathsf{M}_{Q}(\Lambda)=\Lambda
for every
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation
\Lambda
of
Q
, which, in fact, extends to an adjunction between the category of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representations of

and
(Q,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroids with respect to a suitable notion of morphisms (see
Section 3.3
).

\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)(F)\ =\ \big\{\text{$(Q,F)$-matroids $M$ of rank $\textbf{r}$ with $\mathsf{U}_{Q}(M)=\Lambda$}\big\}

The quiver Grassmannian as a moduli space

The push-forward along an perfect idyll morphism

]

More accurately, we first extend the functor

to band schemes in the following way. A

\Lambda
-matroid bundles on a band scheme

Theorem G
.

The functor
\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)
has the structure of a band scheme. In other words, the band scheme

is the fine moduli space of
\Lambda
-matroid bundles.

Euler characteristic and
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-rational points

The following heuristic reasoning leads to the expectation that the number of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-points of a sufficiently nice
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-variety
X
is equal to the (topological) Euler characteristic of its
{\mathbb{C}}
-points
X({\mathbb{C}})
(with respect to the complex topology).

Namely, assume that
X
is an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-model of a smooth projective scheme
X_{\mathbb{Z}}
for which the number
N(q)=\#X_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-rational points (for prime powers
q
) is a polynomial in
q
. As a consequence of the comparison result of singular cohomology with
l
-adic cohomology and of Deligne’s proof of the Weil conjectures, this polynomial is

N(q)\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{\dim X}b_{2i}\cdot q^{i}

where
b_{i}
are the Betti numbers of the complex manifold
X({\mathbb{C}})
, with
b_{2i+1}=0
for odd indices (see
[
Lor16
, Section 7]
for more details). Thus the number of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-points of
X
equals the Euler characteristic of
X({\mathbb{C}})
:

\mychi\big(X({\mathbb{C}})\big)\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{2\dim X}(-1)^{i}\cdot b_{i}\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{\dim X}b_{2i}\cdot 1^{i}\ =\ N(1).

This heuristic is reflected by the following expectation on the Grassmannian over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
: an
r
-dimensional subspace of an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-vector space
\Lambda
(which is a pointed set) is a pointed subset

with
\#(N-\{0\})=r
. In other words,
N-\{0\}
is an
r
-subset of
\Lambda-\{0\}
. This leads to the expectation that

\Gr(r,n)({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\ =\ \big\{\text{$r$-subsets of $\{1,\dotsc,n\}$}\big\}

has
\binom{n}{r}
elements, which equals the Euler characteristic of
\Gr(r,n)({\mathbb{C}})
.

These expectations are not met, however, if we interpret the notion of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-points of
X
in the usual way, i.e. as morphisms
\Spec{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}\to X
. For example,
X={\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
has
2^{n+1}-1
such morphisms (say, when considered as monoid schemes
2
2

2

Note that finite type monoid schemes form a full subcategory of virtually any other approach to
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry, so the number of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-rational points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
does not depend on the notion of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry that we use.
), but the Euler characteristic of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is
n+1
.

In many instances, our expectations on
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-points are met by certain
{\mathbb{K}}
-points of a suitable
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-model. To explain, a band scheme

comes with a set

-points in the following examples (cf.

LT23
]
,
[
BJL24
, Section 3.4]

•

•

The Weyl group

The closed

We call the set of closed

-representations. This includes the following cases (see

Theorem H
.

Let

be a finite quiver and
\Lambda
an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation. Assume that one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

The coefficient quiver
\Gamma
of
\Lambda

Section 7.5
for the definition of
\Gamma

(2)

Then

\mychi\big(\Gr_{{\mathbf{r}}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{C}})\big)\ =\ \#\Gr_{{\mathbf{r}}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}.

for every dimension vector
{{\mathbf{r}}}
for
Q
.

Geometric sketch of proof

Every dimension
{\textbf{r}^{*}}
subrepresentation of
\Lambda
, which is a collection of pointed subsets
\Omega_{v}
of
\Lambda_{v}
(for
v\in Q_{0}
) that is stable under the maps
\Lambda_{\myalpha}
(for
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
), determines a so-called
coordinate matroid

M
, which has
B_{v}=\Lambda_{v}-\Omega_{v}
as its unique basis at

. By
Theorem G
,
M
corresponds to a point

of
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{K}})
. Since

has a unique nonzero coordinate at
B_{v}
for each

\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{K}})
, which establishes an injection

\big\{\text{dimension ${\textbf{r}^{*}}$ subrepresentations of $\Lambda$}\big\}\ \longrightarrow\ \Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem H
, there is a sequence of torus actions that allows us to establish an equality between these sets and between their cardinalities and the Euler characteristic of
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{C}})
.

Namely, Jun and Sistko’s work
[
JS21
]
provides a
nice sequence of gradings of
\Lambda
that distinguishes elements
. These gradings determine the weights of actions

\mytheta_{i}:\ {\mathbb{G}}_{m}\times X_{i}\ \longrightarrow\ X_{i}

(for
i=1,\dotsc,n
) of the multiplicative group scheme
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
on

and on the subspace

of fixed points of
\mytheta_{i}
(for

By a theorem of Białynicki-Birula (

is equal to the Euler characteristic of the

-fixed points

of

, and thus, after repeating the argument

Since the torus actions are defined in terms of gradings of
\Lambda

{\textbf{r}^{*}}

\Lambda
.

In order to show that every point of

does not correspond to a point in

(as multiplicative group), we can consider the limit

which is a point of

with strictly more zero coefficients than

Remark on generalizations

While the definitions and concepts that we develop in this paper are sufficient to illustrate the interplay of matroid theory,
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry and quiver representations, they lean themselves to generalizations in different directions, which might be of independent interest for a broader framework for matroid theory and quiver Grassmannians. At this point, we restrict ourselves to the following brief comments.

Idylls with involutions

Baker and Bowler introduce
F
-matroids for idylls (and more generally, tracts)
F
together with an involution
i:F\to F
, which allows, for instance, to take Hermitian complements of complex matroids, opposed to orthogonal complements. All results of this paper generalize readily to perfect idylls and tracts with involution; also cf.
Section 2.7
.

Morphisms of matroid bundles

The notion of morphisms of matroid bundles over band schemes has to be seen as a provisional concept. The problem is that the composition of two such morphisms are in general not a morphism in the sense of this text anymore. This effect occurs already for non-perfect idylls; cf.
Section 5.3
.

The failure of composability can be fixed in terms of the so-called
perfection
of an idyll
F
, which is a perfect idyll that has the same class of (weak)
F
-matroids, but more morphisms. In particular, the composition of
F
-morphisms is defined over the perfection of
F
. A similar, but more subtle, line of thought leads to a satisfactory notion of morphisms for matroid bundles. All this is part of follow-up work.

Arbitrary matrices as morphisms

Even for perfect idylls
F
, there is a more ample class of morphisms of
F
-matroids, which are given by
F
-matrices that are not necessarily submonomial. This notion of morphism is required to preserve certain
F^{+}
-semimodules
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}^{+}\subseteq(F^{+})^{E}
of “additive” vectors for an
F
-matroid
M
.

This larger class of morphisms contains all strong maps (and more) in the case of the Krasner hyperfield
F={\mathbb{K}}
. In case of a field
F
, it is equal to the class of all linear maps that preserve the given subspaces of vectors. Also this is part of follow-up work.

Quiver Grassmannians for
F
-representations

In the definition of quiver Grassmannians, it is not necessary that the chosen ambient representation
\Lambda
is
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-linear. For a fixed quiver
Q
and a perfect idyll
F
, any choice of a rank vector
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
, of a collection
(\Lambda_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
of finite ground sets
\Lambda_{v}
and of a collection
(\Lambda_{\myalpha})_{\myalpha\in Q_{1}}
of
\Lambda_{t(\myalpha)}\times\Lambda_{s(\myalpha)}
-matrices
\Lambda_{\myalpha}
with coefficients in
F
leads to the definition of a quiver Grassmannian
\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)
in terms of the quiver Plücker relations (cf.
Section 6.1
), which is a band scheme defined over
F
.

If the matrices
\Lambda_{\myalpha}
are submonomial, then for every perfect idyll
P
together with a structure map
F\to P
, the rational point set
\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)(P)
consists of all
(Q,P)
-matroids
M
with rank vector
\underline{\rk}(M)={\mathbf{r}}
and with
M_{\myalpha}=\Lambda_{\myalpha}
for all
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
.

Since there is a unique morphism
F\to{\mathbb{K}}
, this leads to the definition of the
Tits space

\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}
as the subspace of closed points of
\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{K}})
, which should be thought of as the
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-points of
\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)
.

This more ample notion of quiver Grassmannians over
F={\mathbb{C}}
applies, in particular, to irreducible representations
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
of tame quiver (of extended Dynkin type
ADE
). A
basis for
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
is a collection of bases
\Lambda_{v}
for
\Lambda_{{\mathbb{C}},v}
(for all
v\in Q_{0}
). A basis for
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
identifies
\Lambda_{{\mathbb{C}},\myalpha}
with a matrix
\Lambda_{\myalpha}
(for all
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
). The verification of sample cases leads us to expect the following generalization of
Theorem H
.

Conjecture I
.

Let
Q
be a tame quiver,
{\mathbf{r}}
a rank vector for
Q
and
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
an irreducible representation of
Q
. Then there exists a basis for
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\mychi\big(\Gr_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\ast}}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}})\big)\ =\ \#\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}.

1.
Background

1.1.
Vector spaces over
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}

A
vector space over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
is a pointed set, whose base point we denote typically by
0
. An
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-linear map
between
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-vector spaces
V
and
W
is a base point preserving map
f:V\to W
such that
\#f^{-1}(w)\leqslant 1
for all
w\neq 0
. This defines the category
\Vect_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
.

The
adjoint
of an
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map
f:V\to W
is the
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map
f^{t}:W\to V
given by

f^{t}(w)\ =\ \begin{cases}v&\text{if}\;\;w\neq 0\text{ and }f(v)=w\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

1.2.
Strong maps of classical matroids

Let
M
be a matroid with ground set
E
. We denote the sets of circuits of
M
by
{\mathcal{C}}_{M}
and its set of cocircuits by
{\mathcal{C}}_{M}^{\ast}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A
vector
of
M
is a union of circuits of
M
. A
covector
of
M
is a vector of the dual matroid
M^{*}
. We denote the set of vectors of
M
by
\mathcal{V}_{M}
and its set of covectors by
\mathcal{V}^{*}_{M}
.

Since a circuit is the same as the complement of a cohyperplane (
[
Oxl11
, Prop. 2.1.6]
), a vector is nothing else than the complement of a coflat. For our comparison of usual matroid theory with linear algebra and Baker-Bowler theory (cf.
Section 1.3
), it is more convenient to work with vectors than with coflats.

An alternative characterization of vectors is as follows. Two subsets
A
and
B
of
E
are
orthogonal
, denoted by
A\perp B
, if
\#(A\cap B)\neq 1
. Two collections
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
of subsets of
E
are
orthogonal
, denoted by
\mathcal{X}\perp\mathcal{Y}
, if
X\perp Y
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. For
\mathcal{X}\subseteq 2^{E}
, we define
\mathcal{X}^{\perp}:=\{Z\subseteq E\mid X\perp Z\text{ for all }X\in\mathcal{X}\}
. Since circuits are orthogonal to cocircuits (
[
Oxl11
, Prop. 2.1.11]
), we have that

\mathcal{V}_{M}^{\ast}\ =\ \mathcal{C}_{M}^{\perp}\ =\ {\mathcal{V}}_{M}^{\perp}.

Notation 1.2
.

We denote the closure operator of
M
by
\textup{cl}_{M}
and define the matroid
\widetilde{M}:=M\oplus U_{1}^{0}
on the set
\underline{E}
. In other words,
\widetilde{M}
is obtained from

by adding a loop at
0
.

Let
N
and

be classical matroids with respective underlying sets

and
T
. A

\mysigma
from

to
M
, denoted by
\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
, is a base point preserving map
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
such that
\mysigma^{-1}(F)
is a flat of

for any flat

(see
[
Kun86
, Def. 8.1.1]
). If

and

is a strong map,

is called a

of

. By

, Prop. 8.1.3]
, a base point preserving map
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
is a strong map if, and only if,
\mysigma\big(\textup{cl}_{N}(A)\big)\subseteq\textup{cl}_{\widetilde{M}}\big(\mysigma(A)\big)
for every

.

Definition 1.3
.

An
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear strong map

\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
is a strong map
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
that is
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear.

Examples of strong maps are isomorphisms of matroids, restrictions
M|A\to M
, contractions

, quotient maps and the identification of parallel elements. Every strong map is the composition of these elementary types of strong maps (see
[
Kun86
, pp. 227-228]
). Among these types of strong maps, isomorphisms, restrictions, contractions and quotient maps are
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-linear. Nontrivial identifications of parallel elements are not.

In fact, the factorization theorem for strong maps (
[
Kun86
, Thm 8.2.7]
) asserts that every quotient map is the composition of a restriction followed by a contraction. This fact does not generalize to other idylls (see
[

, Cor. 3.5]
), which means that quotient maps play a central role as a building block of matroid morphisms; also cf.
Section 1.2
and
Theorem 2.19
.

Definition 1.4

(
[

, p. 228]
)
.

Let
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
be a base point preserving map and

\rk_{\mysigma^{-1}(M)}(A):=\rk_{\widetilde{M}}\big(\mysigma(A)\big).

Remark 1.5
.

Note that

is obtained from
\mysigma^{-1}(M)
by identifying parallel elements, deleting loops and relabeling elements.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
be a base point preserving map. Then
\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
is a strong map if, and only if,

is a quotient of

Proof.

Using the fact that

\big\{\text{flats of }\widetilde{\big(\mysigma^{-1}(M)\big)}\big\}=\big\{\mysigma^{-1}(F)\;\big|\;F\text{ is a flat of }\widetilde{M}\big\},

the Proposition follows.

Remark 1.7
.

By

, a strong map
\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
is
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear if, and only if, it does not identify parallel elements.

The following result generalizes
[
Kun86
, Prop. 8.1.6]
from matroid quotients to all strong maps.

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
be a base point preserving map. Then
\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
is a strong map if, and only if,
\mysigma^{-1}:2^{T}\rightarrow 2^{S}
sends cocircuits of
M
to covectors of
N
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
is a strong map. Let
Z
be a cocircuit of
M
. Next we show that
\mysigma^{-1}(Z)\in\mathcal{V}^{*}_{N}
. Suppose that there exists
C\in\mathcal{C}_{N}
such that
C\cap\mysigma^{-1}(Z)=\{x\}
is a singleton. As
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{M}=\mathcal{C}^{*}_{\widetilde{M}}
, the set
H:=\underline{T}-Z
is a hyperplane of
\widetilde{M}
that contains
\mysigma(C-\{x\})
. Thus

\mysigma(x)\in\mysigma(C)\subseteq\mysigma\big(\textup{cl}_{N}(C-\{x\})\big)\subseteq\textup{cl}_{\widetilde{M}}\big(\mysigma(C-\{x\})\big)\subseteq H,

which is a contradiction, because
\mysigma(x)\in Z
.

Assume
\mysigma^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{*}_{M})\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{*}_{N}
. Let
A\subseteq S
. Next we prove that
\mysigma\big(\textup{cl}_{N}(A)\big)\subseteq\textup{cl}_{\widetilde{M}}\big(\mysigma(A)\big)
. Suppose that there exists
x\in\textup{cl}_{N}(A)
such that
\mysigma(x)\in\underline{T}-\textup{cl}_{\widetilde{M}}\big(\mysigma(A)\big)
. As
\textup{cl}_{\widetilde{M}}\big(\mysigma(A)\big)
is the intersection of all hyperplanes of
\widetilde{M}
that contain
\mysigma(A)
, there exists a hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{M}
such that
\mysigma(A)\subseteq H
and
\mysigma(x)\notin H
. Thus
Z:=\underline{T}-H
is a cocircuit of
\widetilde{M}
with
\mysigma(x)\in Z
and
\mysigma(A)\cap Z=\emptyset
.

As
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{\widetilde{M}}=\mathcal{C}^{*}_{M}
, it follows that
\mysigma^{-1}(Z)\in\mathcal{V}^{*}_{N}
, which implies that there exists a cocircuit
Y
of
N
such that
x\in Y\subseteq\mysigma^{-1}(Z)
. Thus
L:=S-Y
is a hyperplane of
N
with

. Because
A\subseteq L
, it follows that
\textup{cl}_{N}(A)\subseteq L
and, consequently,
x\notin\textup{cl}_{N}(A)
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
\mysigma:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mysigma:N\rightarrow M
is a strong map;

(2)

\mysigma^{t}:M^{*}\rightarrow N^{*}
is a strong map;

(3)

\mysigma({\mathcal{C}}_{N})\perp{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{M}
.

Proof.

As
\mysigma
is
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear

\#\big(X\cap\mysigma^{-1}(Y)\big)=\#\big((\mysigma^{t})^{-1}(X)\cap Y\big)

for all
X\subseteq S
and
Y\subseteq T
. In particular,
\mysigma^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{*}_{M})\perp\mathcal{C}_{N}
if, and only if,
\mathcal{C}_{M^{*}}\perp(\mysigma^{\mathfrak{t}})^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{*}_{N^{*}})
. Thus by
Section 1.2
, we conclude that (
1
) is equivalent to (
2
).

Now, note that
\mysigma(X)-\{0_{T}\}=(\mysigma^{\mathfrak{t}})^{-1}(X)
for each
X\subseteq S
. Therefore the equivalence between (
2
) and (
3
) follows from
Section 1.2
.
∎

1.3.
Baker-Bowler theory

We recall the theory of matroids with coefficients introduced by Baker and Bowler in
[
BB19
]
.

Idylls.

A
pointed abelian group
is a (multiplicatively written) commutative monoid
F
with a distinguished element
0
such that
F^{\times}=F-\{0\}
and
0\cdot a=0
for all
a\in F
. An
idyll
is a pointed abelian group
F
together with a subset
N_{F}
(called the
nullset
) of the group semiring
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
that satisfies the following axioms:

(I1)

N_{F}
is a proper ideal of
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
, i.e.
0\in N_{F}
,
1\notin N_{F}
,
a+b\in N_{F}
and
ac\in N_{F}
for all
a,b\in N_{F}
and
c\in{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
.

(I2)

There is a unique element
-1\in F^{\times}
with
1+(-1)\in N_{F}
;

Note that these axioms imply that every element
a\in F
has an
additive inverse

-a=(-1)\cdot a
, which is the unique element with
a+(-a)\in N_{F}
. This further implies that
(-1)^{2}=1
and
a\in N_{F}
only if
a=0
.

A
morphism of idylls
is a multiplicative map
f:F\rightarrow G
with
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
such that
\sum f(a_{i})\in N_{G}
for all
\sum a_{i}\in N_{F}
. This defines the category
\Idylls
.

Example 1.10

(Fields as idylls)
.

A field
k
is naturally an idyll if we replace its addition by the nullset

\textstyle N_{k}:=\left\{\sum a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}[k^{\times}]\mid\sum a_{i}=0\text{ in }k\right\}.

By abuse of notation, we denote this idyll by the same symbol
k
. More to the point, the category of fields embeds as a full subcategory into
\Idylls
, which allows us to consider fields as particular types of idylls.

Example 1.11
.

The
regular partial field
is the multiplicative pointed group

{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}=\{0,1,-1\}\quad\text{ with nullset }\quad N_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{m\cdot 1+n\cdot(-1)\mid m=n\}.

This idyll is the initial object of the category
\Idylls
. Given an idyll
F
, the unique morphism
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}\rightarrow F
is determined by
1_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\mapsto 1_{F}
and
-1_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\mapsto-1_{F}
.

Example 1.12

(Hyperfields as idylls)
.

Roughly speaking, a
hyperfield
is a field where the standard addition is replaced by a multivaluated
hyperaddition
, see
[
DL07
]
for details. Given a hyperfield
H
with hyperaddition
\boxplus:H\times H\rightarrow 2^{H}
, we consider
H
as an idyll with respect to the nullset
N_{H}:=\big\{\sum a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}[H^{\times}]\mid 0\in\hypersum a_{i}\big\}
.

Some examples of interest are:

•

The
Krasner hyperfield
is
{\mathbb{K}}=\{0,1\}
with hyperaddition given by
a\boxplus 0=\{a\}
and
1\boxplus 1=\{0,1\}
. As an idyll,
-1_{{\mathbb{K}}}=1
and its nullset is
{\mathbb{N}}-\{1\}
. This hyperfield is the final object in the category
\Idylls
.

•

The
tropical hyperfield
is
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
with the usual multiplication, whose hyperaddition is given by
a\boxplus b=\textup{max}\{a,b\}
for
b\neq a
, and
a\boxplus a=[0,a]
. As an idyll,
-1_{{\mathbb{T}}}=1
and its nullset is

\textstyle N_{\mathbb{T}}=\big\{\sum a_{i}\,\big|\,\text{ the maximum of }\{a_{i}\}\text{ appears at least twice}\big\}.

•

The
phase hyperfield
is
{\mathbb{P}}=S^{1}\cup\{0\}
, where
S^{1}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid z\text{ has norm }1\}
, with usual multiplication and hyperaddition given by

a\boxplus b=\left\{\frac{\myalpha a+\mybeta b}{||\myalpha a+\mybeta b||}\;\bigg|\;\myalpha,\mybeta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\right\}

for
b\neq-a
, and
a\boxplus-a=\{-a,0,a\}
. As an idyll,
-1_{{\mathbb{P}}}=-1
and its nullset is

\textstyle N_{{\mathbb{P}}}=\left\{\sum x_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}[S^{1}]\;\big|\;\sum\myalpha_{i}x_{i}=0\text{ in }{\mathbb{C}},\text{ for some }\myalpha_{i}\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\right\}.

Remark 1.13
.

Historically, the notion of
hypergroup
was introduced by Marty in 1934
[
Mar34
]
. Around 1940, more general aspects of
hyperstructure
were considered, when Krasner defined the notion of
hyperring
and
hyperfield
. For instance, Jun develop algebraic geometry over hyperrings in
[
Jun18
]
, and the second author studies tropical geometry over the tropical hyperfield in
[
Lor22
]
.

Notation 1.14
.

For an idyll
F
, a set
E
, an integer
r
, a tuple
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\in E^{r}
and a permutation
\mysigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
, we define:

•

|{\bf x}|:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subseteq E
;

•

the sign
\sign(\mysigma)\in\{\pm 1\}
, considered as an element of
F
;

•

{\bf x}^{\mysigma}:=(x_{\mysigma(1)},\dots,x_{\mysigma(r)})
;

•

{\bf x}_{\widehat{k}}:=(x_{1},\dots,\widehat{x}_{k},\dots,x_{r})\in E^{r-1}
.

Matroids over idylls.
Let
F
be an idyll,
E
a finite set and
r
an integer. A
Grassmann-Plücker function
of rank
r
on
E
with coefficients in
F
is a nonzero function
\myvarphi:E^{r}\rightarrow F
satisfying the following two properties:

(GP1)

\myvarphi
is
alternating
, i.e.
\myvarphi({\bf x})=\sign(\mysigma)\cdot\myvarphi({\bf x}^{\mysigma})
for all
\mysigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
and
\myvarphi({\bf x})=0
whenever
\#|{\mathbf{x}}|<r
;

(GP2)

for all
x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r},y_{0},\dotsc,y_{r}\in E
,

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\ (-1)^{k}\cdot\myvarphi(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r})\cdot\myvarphi(y_{k},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})\ \in\ N_{F}.

The relations in
(GP2)
are called
Plücker relations
. Two Grassmann-Plücker functions
\myvarphi_{1}
and
\myvarphi_{2}
are
equivalent
if

\myvarphi_{1}=a\cdot\myvarphi_{2}
for some
a
in
F^{\times}
. An
F
-matroid
of rank
r
on
E
over
F
is an equivalence class
[\myvarphi]
of a Grassmann-Plücker function
\myvarphi:E^{r}\to F
.

Example 1.15
.

Given a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid
M=[\myvarphi]
of rank
r
on the set
E
, the collection

{\mathcal{B}}=\big\{\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}\subseteq E\mid\myvarphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\neq 0\big\}

is the set of bases of a classical matroid, called the
underlying matroid
of
M
and denoted by
\underline{M}
. By
[
BB19
, Remark 3.19]
, the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\{{\mathbb{K}}\text{-matroids on }E\}&\longrightarrow&\{\text{classical matroids on }E\}\\
M&\longmapsto&\underline{M}\end{array}

is a bijection. This shows that matroids over idylls are generalizations of classical matroids.

Functoriality.

Given a morphism of idylls
f:F\rightarrow G
and a Grassmann-Plücker function
\myvarphi:E^{r}\rightarrow F
with coefficients in
F
, the composition
f\circ\myvarphi:E^{r}\rightarrow G
is a Grassmann-Plücker function with coefficients in
G
. For an
F
-matroid
M=[\myvarphi]
, we define the
push-forward
of
M

along

f
as the
G
-matroid
f_{*}(M):=[f\circ\myvarphi]
.

Definition 1.16
.

Given an
F
-matroid
M
, its
underlying matroid
is the classical matroid
\underline{M}:=\underline{f_{*}(M)}
, where
f:F\to{\mathbb{K}}
the unique idyll morphism into
{\mathbb{K}}
.

Duality.
Let
F
be an idyll and
M=[\myvarphi]
an
F
-matroid of rank
r
on the set
E
. Fix a total order on
E
. For
{\bf y}\in E^{n}
with
|\mathbf{y}|=E
, let
\mysigma
be the permutation for which
y_{\mysigma(1)}<\cdots<y_{\mysigma(n)}
, and define
\sign({\bf y}):=\sign(\mysigma)\in\{1,-1\}
.

The
dual
of
M
is the
F
-matroid
M^{*}:=[\myvarphi^{*}]
, where
\myvarphi^{*}:E^{n-r}\rightarrow F
is the Grassmann-Plücker function given by

\myvarphi^{*}({\bf x})\ :=\ \begin{cases}0,\text{ if there exists }i\neq j\text{ with }x_{i}=x_{j}\\
\sign({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime})\cdot\myvarphi({\bf x}^{\prime}),\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where
{\bf x}^{\prime}\in E^{r}
is such that
E=|{\bf x}|\sqcup|{\bf x}^{\prime}|
. The dual of
M
is independent of the order on
E
and satisfies
(M^{*})^{*}=M
.

Circuits and vectors.

Let
X=(X_{e})_{e\in E}
be an element of
F^{E}
. The
support
of
X
is the set
\underline{X}:=\{e\in E\mid X_{e}\neq 0\}
. More generally, for a subset
{\mathcal{S}}
of
F^{E}
, its support is
\underline{{\mathcal{S}}}:=\{\underline{X}\mid X\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
.

The
inner product
of two elements
X
and
Y
of
F^{E}
is defined as

X\cdot Y\ =\ \sum X_{e}\cdot Y_{e}\in{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}].

We say that
X
is
orthogonal
to
Y
, denoted by
X\perp Y
, if
X\cdot Y
is in
N_{F}
. Two subsets
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
of
F^{E}
are
orthogonal
, written as
{\mathcal{X}}\perp{\mathcal{Y}}
, if
X\perp Y
for each
X
in
{\mathcal{X}}
and
Y
in
{\mathcal{Y}}
. The
orthogonal complement
of a subset
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq F^{E}
is
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}:=\big\{X\in F^{E}\mid\{X\}\perp{\mathcal{S}}\big\}
.

Let
M=[\myvarphi:E^{r}\to F]
be an
F
-matroid and define
\Xi_{M}
as the collection of all
{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{0},\dotsc,y_{r})\in E^{r+1}
for which
\#|{\mathbf{y}}|=r+1
and for which
\myvarphi(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r})\neq 0
for some
k=0,\dotsc,r
. We define the
fundamental circuit of
{\mathbf{y}}\in\Xi_{M}
with respect to
\myvarphi
as

X_{{\mathbf{y}}}(x)\ =\ \begin{cases}(-1)^{i}\cdot\myvarphi(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{i}},\dotsc,y_{r})&\text{if }x=y_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The set of
F
-circuits
of
M
is

{\mathcal{C}}_{M}\ =\ \big\{a\cdot X_{{\mathbf{y}}}\,\big|\,a\in F^{\times},\ {\mathbf{y}}\in\Xi_{M}\big\}.

The set of
F
-cocircuits
of
M
is
{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{M}:={\mathcal{C}}_{M^{*}}
. We define the set of
F
-vectors
and of
F
-covectors
of
M
as

{\mathcal{V}}_{M}:=({\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{M})^{\perp}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{V}}^{*}_{M}:={\mathcal{C}}_{M}^{\perp},

respectively. The circuits of
\underline{M}
are exactly the supports

\underline{X}\ =\ \{e\in E\mid X_{e}\neq 0\}

of the
F
-circuits
X\in{\mathcal{C}}_{M}
of

, i.e. the elements of
\underline{{\mathcal{C}}_{M}}
.

An idyll

is

if
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}\perp{\mathcal{V}}^{*}_{M}
for all
F
-matroids

. Examples of perfect idylls are: fields, the regular partial field
{\mathbb{F}}^{\pm}_{1}
, and the hyperfields
{\mathbb{K}}
and
{\mathbb{T}}
. The phase hyperfield
{\mathbb{P}}
is not perfect. For more details, see
[
BB19
]
.

2.
Category of
F
-matroids

2.1.
Submonomial matrices

Let
F
be a perfect idyll and
T
and
S
finite sets.

Definition 2.1
.

An
F
-matrix
(indexed by
T\times S
) is a matrix
\Phi=(\Phi_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in T\times S}
with entries
\Phi_{i,j}\in F
. An
F
-matrix
\Phi
is
submonomial
if every row and column of
\Phi
contains at most one nonzero entry. We denote the
transpose
of
\Phi
by
\Phi^{t}
, which has entries
\Phi^{t}_{i,j}:=\Phi_{j,i}
.

Note that the product
\Phi\cdot X
of a submonomial
F
-matrix
\Phi
indexed by
T\times S
with
X\in F^{S}
is a well-defined element of
F^{T}
, since the sum in the definition of
(\Phi\cdot X)_{i}=\sum_{j\in S}\Phi_{i,j}X_{j}
contains at most one nonzero term. Due to this, we also consider a submonomial
F
-matrix
\Phi
as a map
\Phi:F^{S}\to F^{T}
. For a subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq F^{S}
, we use
\Phi\cdot\mathcal{X}
to denote the image
\{\Phi\cdot X\mid X\in\mathcal{X}\}\subseteq F^{T}
.

Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be submonomial
F
-matrices indexed by
T\times S
and
W\times T
, respectively. The product
\Psi\cdot\Phi
is well-defined and corresponds to the composition
\Psi\circ\Phi:F^{S}\to F^{W}
of the maps
\Phi:F^{S}\to F^{T}
and
\Psi:F^{T}\to F^{W}
. The transpose
\Phi^{t}
of
\Phi
is also submonomial, and thus defines a map
\Phi^{t}:F^{T}\to F^{S}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
underlying map
of a submonomial
F
-matrix
\Phi
indexed by
T\times S
is the
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map
\uline{\Phi}:\uline{S}\to\uline{T}
given by

\uline{\Phi}(j)\ :=\ \begin{cases}i&\text{if $j\neq 0$ and $\Phi_{i,j}\neq 0$;}\\
0&\text{if $j=0$, or if $j\neq 0$ and $\Phi_{i,j}=0$ for all $i\in T$.}\end{cases}

which satisfies
(\underline{\Phi})^{t}=\underline{(\Phi^{t})}
(see
Section 1.1
). For
s\in\underline{S}
, we define

\Phi_{s}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\Phi_{\underline{\Phi}(s),s}&\text{if $s\neq 0$ and $\Phi_{\underline{\Phi}(s),s}\neq 0$;}\\
0&\text{if $s=0$, or if $s\neq 0$ and $\Phi_{i,s}=0$ for all $i\in T$.}\end{cases}

Remark 2.3
.

Note that since
\Phi
is submonomial, there is at most one
i
for every given
j
such that
\Phi_{i,j}\neq 0
. Note further that
(\Phi^{t}\cdot Z)_{s}=\Phi_{s}\cdot Z_{\underline{\Phi}(s)}
for
Z\in F^{T}
and
s\in S
.

2.2.
Morphisms of matroids with coefficients

Definition 2.4

(Category of
F
-matroids)
.

Let
N
and
M
be
F
-matroids with respective underlying sets
S
and
T
. A
morphism
of
F
-matroids from
N
to
M
is a submonomial
F
-matrix
\Phi
indexed by
T\times S
such that
\Phi\cdot{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
. We write
\Phi:N\to M
for a morphism
\Phi
from
N
to
M
. This defines the category
\Mat_{F}
of
F
-matroids.

Example 2.5
.

A morphism of
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids is a particular type of strong map, which includes isomorphisms, restrictions, contractions and quotients, but not the identification of parallel elements. A rigorous discussion of this relation can be found in
Section 2.3
.

In the case of a field
k
, a submonomial matrix
\Phi
describes a linear map
\Phi:k^{S}\rightarrow k^{T}
that sends coordinate vectors of
k^{S}
(i.e. vectors with only one nonzero coefficient) to coordinate vectors of
k^{T}
or
0
. Therefore a morphism
\Phi:N\to M
of
k
-matroids (with respective ground sets
S
and
T
) is a submonomial linear map
\Phi:k^{S}\rightarrow k^{T}
with
\Phi\cdot{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
, where
{\mathcal{V}}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
are the vector sets of
M
and
N
, respectively.

Similarly, a morphism
\Phi:N\to M
of
{\mathbb{T}}
-matroids (with respective ground sets
S
and
T
) is a submonomial linear map
{\mathbb{T}}^{S}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{T}
such that
\Phi\cdot{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
.

A quotient
M
of an oriented matroid
N
is the same thing as an
{\mathbb{S}}
-morphism
\Phi:N\to M
that is given by a square identity matrix
\Phi
. Examples of other
{\mathbb{S}}
-morphisms are

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big]:M\to M^{\prime}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\big]:M\to N

where
M
,
M^{\prime}
and
N
are the
{\mathbb{S}}
-matroids with respective vector sets

\textstyle{\mathcal{V}}_{M}\ =\ \big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}-1\\
-1\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\},\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{M^{\prime}}\ =\ \big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
-1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}-1\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\},\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\ =\ \big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}-1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\}.

Thus the underlying matroid of both

and

is
U^{1}_{2}
and the underlying matroid of

is

.

Recall that the inner product of two tuples

is defined as the element

of
{\mathbb{N}}[F^{\times}]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Phi
be a submonomial
F
-matrix indexed by
T\times S
. Let
X\in F^{S}
and

. Then
(\Phi\cdot X)\cdot Y=X\cdot(\Phi^{t}\cdot Y)
.

Proof.

This follows from a direct computation:

(\Phi\cdot X)\cdot Y\ =\ \sum_{e\in T}\Phi_{e,\underline{\Phi}^{t}(e)}\cdot X_{\underline{\Phi}^{t}(e)}\cdot Y_{e}\ =\ \sum_{e\in S}X_{e}\cdot\Phi^{t}_{e,\underline{\Phi}(e)}\cdot Y_{\underline{\Phi}(e)}\ =\ X\cdot(\Phi^{t}\cdot Y).\qed

Definition 2.7
.

Given a function
\mymu:E^{r}\rightarrow F

\begin{array}[]{clll}\widetilde{\mymu}:&\underline{E}^{r}&\longrightarrow&\quad\;F\\
&{\bf x}&\longmapsto&\begin{cases}\mymu({\bf x})&\text{if }x_{i}\neq 0\text{ for all }i\\

If

is an

-matroid on

is an

-matroid on

Remark 2.22

The deletion of a morphism is the restriction of its transpose to the complementary subsets. More explicitly, consider a submonomial

2.7.
Remark on morphisms for idylls with involution

Baker and Bowler formulate their theory of matroids with coefficients for idylls
F
with an involution
x\mapsto\overline{x}
(which is an automorphism
F\to F
of order
2
). The most prominent example for such an involution are the complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
together with the complex conjugate function
z\mapsto\overline{z}
. In this case, the dual of a
{\mathbb{C}}
-matroid
M
with vectors
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{T}
is the
{\mathbb{C}}
-matroid
M^{\ast}
whose vectors
{\mathcal{V}}_{M^{\ast}}
is the Hermitian complement of
{\mathcal{V}}_{M}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{T}
.

The presence of an involution changes the notion of orthogonality and therefore the notion of the dual of an
F
-matroid. This extends to morphisms of
F
-matroids: the dual of a morphism
\Phi:N\to M
of
F
-matroids (with respect to an involution
x\mapsto\overline{x}
) is described by the
conjugate transpose

\overline{\Phi}^{t}:M^{\ast}\to N^{\ast}
of
\Phi
.

It is readily verified that the conjugate transpose is indeed a morphism of
F
-matroids, and that the cryptomorphic descriptions of morphisms and minor constructions extend to matroids over idylls with involution. We omit further details.

4.
Bands and band schemes

The vessel of our approach to moduli spaces of quiver matroids are band schemes, which form a streamlined approach to
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry that is developed in
[
BJL24
]
. We review this theory in brevity in this section.

In particular, we tailor certain results about ordered blueprints to bands, which is possible since bands are ordered blueprints of a particularly well-behaved type (see
Section 4.1.7
)—morally speaking, a band is the part of an ordered blueprint that is sensitive to matroid theory. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
BJL24
]
.

4.1.
Bands

A band is a generalization of a ring, in the same way that an idyll is a generalization of a field.

A
pointed monoid

B
is a commutative monoid
B
together with an element
0
such that
0\cdot a=0
for all
a\in B
. The
ambient semiring
of a pointed monoid
B
is the semiring

\textstyle B^{+}\ :=\ {\mathbb{N}}[B]\,/\,\langle 0\rangle\ =\ \big\{\sum a_{i}\,\big|\,a_{i}\in B-\{0\}\big\}.

Note that
B
embeds as a submonoid of
B^{+}
. An
ideal
of
B^{+}
is a subset
I
such that
0\in I
,
I+I=I
and
B\cdot I=I
.

A
band
is a pointed monoid
B
together with an ideal
N_{B}\subseteq B^{+}
(the
null set of
B
) such that for every
a\in B
, there exists a unique element
b\in B
such that
a+b\in N_{B}
, which we call the
additive inverse of
a
and which we denote by
-a
. We write
a-b
for
a+(-b)
. A
band morphism
is a multiplicative map
f:B\to C
of bands that preserves
0
and
1
such that
\sum a_{i}\in N_{B}
implies
\sum f(a_{i})\in N_{C}
. We denote the category of bands by
\Bands
.

The null set
N_{B}
of a band
B
is
generated by a subset
S
of
B^{+}
if it is generated by
S
and
1-1
as an ideal of
B^{+}
. We write
N_{B}=\langle S\rangle_{B^{+}}
in this case.

Example 4.1
.

The main examples of interest to us are the following:

(1)

Every idyll is naturally a band. More to the point, an idyll is the same as a band
F
with
F^{\times}=F-\{0\}
.

(2)

A ring
R
is a band with null set
N_{R}=\{\sum a_{i}\mid\sum a_{i}=0\text{ in }R\}
.

4.1.1.
Free algebras

Let
k
be a band. A
k
-algebra
is a band
B
together with a band morphism
\myalpha_{B}:k\to B
(called the
structure map
). A
k
-linear morphism of
k
-algebras
from
B
to
C
is a band morphism
f:B\to C
such that
\myalpha_{C}=f\circ\myalpha_{B}
.

The
free
k
-algebra in
T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}
is the multiplicative submonoid

k[T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}]\ =\ \big\{a\cdot T_{1}^{e_{1}}\dotsb T_{n}^{e_{n}}\,\big|\,a\in k,\,e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}\big\}

of the polynomial semiring
k[T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}]^{+}
over
k^{+}
. It contains
k
as a submonoid and gains the structure of a band with respect to the null set generated by
N_{k}
. The free algebra
k[T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}]
in
T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}
satisfies the expected universal property: every map
f_{0}:\{T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}\}\to B
into a
k
-algebra
B
extends uniquely to a
k
-linear morphism
f:k[T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}]\to B
.

4.1.2.
Quotients

A
null ideal
of a band
B
is an ideal
I
of the ambient semiring
B^{+}
that contains the null set
N_{B}
and satisfies the substitution rule:

(SR)

if
a-c\in I
and
c+\sum b_{j}\in I
, then
a+\sum b_{j}\in I
.

Given a subset
S
of
B^{+}
, we denote the smallest null ideal that contains
S
by
\langle S\rangle
.

The
quotient of
B
by a null ideal
I
is the following band
B\!\sslash\!I
: as a set,
B\!\sslash\!I
is the quotient
B/\sim
by the equivalence relation given by
a\sim b
if and only if
a-b\in I
. It is a pointed monoid with product
[a]\cdot[b]=[ab]
. The null set of
B\!\sslash\!I
is defined as

\textstyle N_{B\!\sslash\!I}\ =\ \big\{\sum[a_{i}]\,\big|\,\sum a_{i}\in I\big\}.

The association
a\mapsto[a]
defines a band morphism
\mypi_{I}:B\to B\!\sslash\!I
, which satisfies the following universal property: every band morphism
f:B\to C
with
\sum f(a_{i})\in N_{C}
for every
\sum a_{i}\in I
factors into
f=\bar{f}\circ\mypi_{I}
for a uniquely determined morphism
\bar{f}:B\!\sslash\!I\to C
.

4.1.3.
Localizations

A
multiplicative subset
of a band
B
is a subset
S
that contains
1
and is closed under multiplication. Given such
S
, we define the
localization of
B
at
S
as

S^{-1}B\ =\ (S\times B)\,/\,\sim

where
(s,a)\sim(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
if and only if
tsa^{\prime}=ts^{\prime}a
for some
t\in S
. We denote by
\frac{a}{s}
the class of
(s,a)
in
S^{-1}B
. The product
\frac{a}{s}\cdot\frac{b}{t}=\frac{ab}{st}
turns
S^{-1}B
into a pointed monoid, and the null set

\textstyle N_{S^{-1}B}\ =\ \big\{\sum\frac{a_{i}}{s}\,\big|\,s\in S,\ \sum a_{i}\in N_{B}\big\}

endows
S^{-1}B
with the structure of a band.

The association
a\mapsto\frac{a}{1}
defines a band morphism
\myiota_{S}:B\to S^{-1}B
, which satisfies the following universal property: every band morphism
f:B\to C
with
f(S)\subseteq C^{\times}
factors into
f=f_{S}\circ\myiota_{S}
for a uniquely determined morphism
f_{S}:S^{-1}B\to C
.

A
finite localization of
B
is a localization of the form
B[h^{-1}]=S^{-1}B
where
h\in B
and
S=\{h^{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

4.1.4.
Ideals

An
m
-ideal
of a band
B
is a subset
I
of
B
that contains
0
such that
IB=I
. An
m
-ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}
is
prime
if
S=B-{\mathfrak{p}}
is a multiplicative subset of
B
. The
localization of
B
at
{\mathfrak{p}}
is
B_{\mathfrak{p}}=S^{-1}B
.

Every nonzero band
B
has a unique maximal
m
-ideal, which is
{\mathfrak{m}}_{B}=B-B^{\times}
and which is prime.

4.1.5.
Limits and colimits

The category of a bands contains all limits and colimits. The regular partial field
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
is initial and the zero band
\mathbf{0}=\{0\}
is terminal in
\Bands
. The product of two bands
B
and
C
is the Cartesian product
B\times C
together with the inherited structure as a band. The tensor product
B\otimes_{k}C
of two
k
-algebras
B
and
C
is defined as the colimit of
B\leftarrow k\to C
.

4.1.6.
The universal ring

The
universal ring
of a band
B
is the ring

B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ :=\ {\mathbb{Z}}[B]/\langle N_{B}\rangle

where
\langle N_{B}\rangle
is the ideal of
{\mathbb{Z}}[B]
generated by all expression in
N_{B}
, interpreted as a subset of the monoid algebra
{\mathbb{Z}}[B]
. If
R
is a ring with associated band
B
, then
B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}=R
.

The universal ring comes together with a map
B\to B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is universal among all maps
f:B\to R
into a ring
R
with the property that
\sum f(a_{i})=0
for all
\sum a_{i}\in N_{B}
. In other words,
\Rings
form a reflective subcategory of
\Bands
.

Note that the canonical map
B\to B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is in general not injective. For example,
{\mathbb{K}}^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is the trivial ring
\mathbf{0}=\{0\}
.

4.1.7.
Bands as ordered blueprints

For the sake of this section, we assume that the reader is familiar with the notion of an ordered blueprint. We employ the notation from
[
BL21
]
and
[
BJL24
]
.

A band
B
can be viewed as the ordered blueprint
B^{\textup{oblpr}}
with underlying monoid
B
, ambient semiring
B^{+}
and partial order generated by the relations
0\leqslant\sum a_{i}
for which
\sum a_{i}\in N_{B}
. This association defines a fully faithful embedding
\Bands\to\OBlpr
. In fact, this embedding is a coreflection: every ordered blueprint
B=(B^{\bullet},B^{+},\leqslant)
has an associated band
B^{\textup{band}}=B^{\bullet}
whose nullset is

\textstyle N_{B^{\textup{band}}}\ =\ \big\{\sum a_{i}\,\big|\,0\leqslant\sum a_{i}\text{ in }B\big\},

and the canonical map
(B^{\textup{band}})^{\textup{oblpr}}\to B
establishes an adjunction

\Hom_{\Bands}(B,\ C^{\textup{band}})\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad\Hom_{\OBlpr}(B^{\textup{oblpr}},\ C).

This tight connection between bands and ordered blueprints makes it possible to specialize and transfer many results from ordered blueprints to bands.

4.2.
Band schemes

The spectrum of a band is a topological space that comes with a structure sheaf of coordinate bands. A band scheme is a space that locally looks like the spectrum of a band. We review these definitions in the following.

4.2.1.
Band spaces

A
band space
is a topological space
X
together with a sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
in
\Bands
. Every point
x\in X
comes with a
stalk
, which is
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}=\colim{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)
where
U
ranges over all open neighbourhoods
U
of
x
. A
morphism of band spaces
is a continuous map
\myvarphi:X\to Y
together with a morphism
\myvarphi^{\#}:{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}\to\myvarphi_{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of sheaves that is
local
in the sense that for every
x\in X
and
y=\myvarphi(x)
, the induced morphism of stalks
\myvarphi^{\#}_{x}:{\mathcal{O}}_{Y,y}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}
maps the maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}_{y}
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{Y,y}
to the maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}_{x}
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}
. This defines the category
\BandSpaces
of band spaces.

Every open subset
U
of a band space
X
is naturally a band space with respect to the restriction of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
to
U
.

4.2.2.
The spectrum

Let
B
be a band. The
spectrum of
B
is the set
X=\Spec B
of all prime
m
-ideals
{\mathfrak{p}}
of
B
, endowed with the topology generated by the
principal opens

U_{h}\ =\ \{{\mathfrak{p}}\in X\mid h\notin{\mathfrak{p}}\}

for
h\in B
and with the sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
with values
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U_{h})=B[h^{-1}]
and stalks
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,{\mathfrak{p}}}=B_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Note that
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is uniquely determined by its values on principal opens since the
U_{h}
form a basis for the topology of
\Spec B
. The proof of the existence of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is easier than in usual algebraic geometry since every non-empty spectrum
\Spec B
contains a unique closed point, which is the maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}_{B}=B-B^{\times}
of
B
.

A band morphism
f:B\to C
induces a morphism
\myvarphi=f^{\ast}:\Spec C\to\Spec B
with
f^{\ast}({\mathfrak{q}})=f^{-1}({\mathfrak{q}})
and the sheaf morphism
\myvarphi^{\#}
determined by

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\myvarphi^{\#}(U_{h}):&B[h^{-1}]&\longrightarrow&C[f(h)^{-1}].\\[5.0pt]
&\frac{a}{h^{i}}&\longmapsto&\frac{f(a)}{f(h)^{i}}\end{array}

This defines a contravariant functor
\Spec:\Bands\to\BandSpaces
, which is fully faithful.

Example 4.2
.

The following are first examples of spectra:

(1)

An idyll
F
has a unique prime
m
-ideal, which is
\myeta=\{0\}
. Thus
\Spec F=\{\myeta\}
is a singleton.

(2)

The
affine space
over a band
B
is
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}=\Spec B[T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}]
. For an idyll
F
, the prime
m
-ideals of
{\mathfrak{p}}
of
F[T_{1},\dotsc,T_{n}]
are of the form
{\mathfrak{p}}_{I}=\langle T_{i}\mid i\in I\rangle
for subsets
I
of
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. Thus
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{F}=\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{I}\mid I\subseteq E\}
as a set, and
{\mathfrak{p}}_{I}
is in the closure of
{\mathfrak{p}}_{J}
if and only if
J\subseteq I
.

4.2.3.
Band schemes

An
affine band scheme
is a band space that is isomorphic to the spectrum of a band. A
band scheme
is a band space that has an open covering by
affine opens
, which are open subsets that are affine band schemes. This defines the categories
\BAff
of affine band schemes and
\BSch
of band schemes.

Taking global sections defines a (contravariant) functor
\Gamma:\BSch\to\Bands
that is adjoint to
\Spec
, i.e.
\Hom(B,\Gamma X)=\Hom(X,\Spec B)
for all bands
B
and band schemes
X
. The restrictions
\Gamma:\BAff\to\Bands
and
\Spec:\Bands\to\BAff
are mutually inverse anti-equivalences of categories.

Remark 4.3

(Local nature of a band scheme)
.

Since every band has a unique maximal ideal, every affine band scheme has a unique closed point. This fact has strong consequences:

(1)

Every closed point
x
of a band scheme
X
is contained in a unique affine open neighbourhood, which is
U_{x}=\Spec{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}
. In particular, if
X
is finite, then the affine opens of the form
U_{x}
(with
x\in X
closed) form the unique minimal affine open covering of
X
.

(2)

If a band morphism
f:B\to C
induces an open immersion
f^{\ast}:\Spec C\to\Spec B
of affine band scheme, then
f
is a finite localization, i.e.
C=B[h^{-1}]
for some
h\in B
.

(3)

Let
\myvarphi:X\to Y
be a morphism of band schemes and
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
and
Y=\bigcup_{j\in J}V_{j}
affine open coverings. Then there is a map
\myvarphi_{0}:I\to J
such that
\myvarphi(U_{i})\subseteq V_{\myvarphi_{0}(i)}
for every
i\in I
.

4.2.4.
Base extension to
{\mathbb{Z}}

A band scheme
X
comes with a universal scheme
X^{+}
and a canonical inclusion of
R
-rational point sets
X(R)\subseteq X^{+}(R)
for every ring
R
, which is a bijection if
R
is local.

The universal scheme
X^{+}
is defined as follows. If
X=\Spec B
is affine, we define
X^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}:=\Spec B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. For arbitrary
X
, we let
{\mathcal{U}}
be the diagram of all affine opens
U
of
X
together with all inclusions
\myiota_{V,U}:V\hookrightarrow U
as subsets of
X
, which are open immersions of affine band schemes. Since
(-)^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is functorial, this yields a diagram
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
of affine schemes. We define

X^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ =\ \colim{\mathcal{U}}^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}.

As a matter of fact, all morphisms of
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
are principal open immersions and the canonical inclusions
U^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to X^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
are open immersions for
U\in{\mathcal{U}}
.

The functoriality of this construction defines an injection

X(R)\ =\ \Hom(\Spec R,X)\ \longrightarrow\ \Hom(\Spec R,X^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}})\ =\ X^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}(R)

for every ring
R
, which is bijective if
R
is local since in this case every morphism
\Spec R\to X^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
factors through some affine open of the form
U^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
with
U\in{\mathcal{U}}
.

4.2.5.
Fibre products

The categorical fibre product of two morphisms
\myvarphi_{X}:X\to Z
and
\myvarphi_{Y}:Y\to Z
is represented by a band scheme
X\times_{Z}Y
, which comes with two projections
\mypi_{X}:X\times_{Z}Y\to X
and
\mypi_{Y}:X\times_{Z}Y\to Y
. In the affine case
X=\Spec B
,
Y=\Spec C
and
Z=\Spec k
, we have
X\times_{Z}Y=\Spec(B\otimes_{k}C)
. In general, the fibre product
X\times_{Z}Y
is covered by affine opens of this form.

4.2.6.
Open and closed subschemes

An
open subscheme
of a band scheme
X
is an open subset
U
together with the restriction of the structure sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of
X
to
U
. An
open immersion
is a morphism of band schemes
\myvarphi:Y\to X
that factors through an isomorphism
Y\to U
with
U
an open subscheme of
X
.

An
affine morphism
is a morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
of band schemes such that for every affine open
U
of
X
, the inverse image
\myvarphi^{-1}(U)=Y\times_{X}U
is affine. A
closed immersion
is an affine morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
such that for every affine open
U
of
X
and
V=\myvarphi^{-1}(U)
, the map
\myvarphi^{\#}(U):\Gamma U\to\Gamma V
is surjective. A
closed subscheme of
X
is an isomorphism class of closed immersions
\myvarphi:Y\to X
.

4.3.
Projective geometry

Similar to usual schemes, projective geometry for band schemes is governed by homogeneous coordinate algebras and the
\Proj
-construction. We introduce all relevant notions in the following.

4.3.1.
Projective space

Let
k
be a band. The free algebra
k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]
is graded by
\deg T_{i}=1
for each
i
, and this grading is inherited by all of its localizations at elements
4
4

4

Note that all elements of
k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]
are homogeneous since they are monomials in
T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}
.

h\in k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]
.

The
projective
n
-space over
k
is the band scheme
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
that consists of all prime
m
-ideals of
k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]
that do not contain all of
T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}
, endowed with the topology generated by the
principal opens

U_{h}\ =\ \{{\mathfrak{p}}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}\mid h\notin{\mathfrak{p}}\}

for
h\in k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]
, and whose structure sheaf is characterized by the values

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}}(U_{h})\ =\ \big(k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}][h^{-1}]\big)_{0},

on principal opens, where
(-)_{0}
stands for the degree
0
part. The projective
n
-space is covered by the canonical affine opens

U_{i}\ =\ U_{T_{i}}\ =\ \{{\mathfrak{p}}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}\mid T_{i}\notin{\mathfrak{p}}\},

which are isomorphic to the affine space
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{k}
via the morphism associated to the band isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{k})&=&k[T_{0}/T_{i},\dotsc,T_{n}/T_{i}]&\longrightarrow&\big(k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}][T_{i}^{-1}]\big)_{0}\\
&&T_{j}/T_{i}&\longmapsto&T_{j}/T_{i}.\end{array}

4.3.2.
Projective band schemes

A
homogeneous element of
B=k[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]^{+}
of degree
d
is a polynomial
\sum a_{\underline{e}}T_{1}^{e_{1}}\dotsb T_{n}^{e_{n}}
such that
e_{1}+\dotsb+e_{n}=d
for all indices
\underline{e}=(e_{i})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
with
a_{\underline{e}}\neq 0
. A
homogeneous null ideal of
B
is a null ideal
I
that is generated by homogeneous elements (of possibly different degrees).

A homogeneous null ideal
I
of
B
defines the closed subscheme
X=\Proj(B\!\sslash\!I)
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
whose underlying space is the subspace

X\ =\ \big\{{\mathfrak{p}}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}\,\big|\,\text{if $a-b\in I$ and $a\in{\mathfrak{p}}$, then also $b\in{\mathfrak{p}}$}\big\}

of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
and whose structure sheaf is determined by its values

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\big(U_{h}\cap X\big)\ =\ \big((B\!\sslash\!I)[h^{-1}]\big)_{0}

on the intersections with principal opens
U_{h}
. The inclusion
\myiota:X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
together with the surjection
\myiota^{\#}:{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of sheaves is a closed immersion. Conversely, every closed subscheme of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
is of this form.

Example 4.4

(Grassmannian)
.

An example of primary interest are Grassmannians
\Gr(r,E)
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
for
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
r
an integer. The ambient projective space

{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r}-1}\ =\ \Proj\big({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{\textbf{x}}\mid\textbf{x}\in E^{r}]\big)

has homogeneous coordinates
T_{\textbf{x}}
, indexed by
r
-tuples
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})
of elements of
E
. The homogeneous null ideal
I
that defines
\Gr(r,E)
as a closed subscheme of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r}-1}
is generated by the set of
Plücker relations

\mathpzc{Pl}(r,E)
, which consists of the following homogeneous elements (cf.
(GP1)
and
(GP2)
):

\displaystyle T_{\textbf{x}}

\displaystyle\text{whenever }\#|\textbf{x}|<r;

\displaystyle T_{\textbf{x}^{\mysigma}}\ -\ \sign(\mysigma)\cdot T_{\textbf{x}}

\displaystyle\text{for every $\mysigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}$};

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r+1}(-1)^{k}\penalty\ T_{\textbf{y}_{\widehat{k}}}\cdot T_{(y_{k},\textbf{x})}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\textbf{y}\in E^{r+1}\text{ and }\textbf{x}\in E^{r-1}.

We call the closed immersion
\myiota:\Gr(r,E)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r}-1}
the
Plücker embedding
in analogy with the classical theory.

Note that
\Gr(r,E)(k)
corresponds to the classical Grassmann variety in the case of a field
k
(cf.
Section 4.2.4
).

4.3.3.
Multigraded bands

Let
S
be a finite set and
n_{v}\in{\mathbb{N}}
for
v\in S
. The band
B=k[T_{v,i}\mid v\in S,i=0,\dotsc,n_{v}]
is graded in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{S}
with multidegree
\deg T_{v,i}=(\mydelta_{v,w})_{w\in S}
. A
homogeneous element of
B^{+}
with multidegree
(d_{v})_{v\in S}
is a polynomial
\sum a_{e}\prod T_{v,i}^{e_{v,i}}
with
e_{v,0}+\dotsb+e_{v,n_{v}}=d_{v}
for all indices
e=(e_{v,i})
with
a_{e}\neq 0
. A
multi-homogeneous null ideal of
B
is a null ideal
I
of
B
that is generated by multi-homogeneous elements.

A multi-homogeneous null ideal
I
of
B
defines the following closed subscheme
X=\Proj(B\!\sslash\!I)
of
\prod_{v\in S}{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}
. As a subspace of
\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}
,

\textstyle X\ =\ \big\{{\mathfrak{p}}\in\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}\,\big|\,\text{if $a-b\in I$ and $a\in{\mathfrak{p}}$, then also $b\in{\mathfrak{p}}$}\big\}.

Its structure sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is determined by its values

{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U_{(h_{v})})\ =\ \big((B\!\sslash\!I)[h_{v}^{-1}\mid v\in S]\big)_{0}

on principal opens
U_{(h_{v})}=\prod U_{h_{v}}
of
\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}
where
(-)_{0}
stands for the subband of multidegree
0
elements. The inclusion
\myiota:X\to\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}
together with the canonical projection
\myiota^{\#}:{\mathcal{O}}_{\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
of sheaves is a closed immersion and turns

into a closed subscheme of
\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{v}}_{k}
.

Remark 4.5
.

The Segre embedding of usual algebraic geometry generalizes to band schemes and defines a closed immersion
\myiota:{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{k}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{nm+n+m}_{k}
, which identifies
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{k}
with a closed subscheme of
{\mathbb{P}}^{nm+n+m}_{k}
. To be more precise, the Segre embedding
\myiota
is induced by the band morphism
k[T_{i,j}]\to k[T_{i},T_{j}]
that sends
T_{i,j}
to
T_{i}\cdot T_{j}
and taking
C
-points (for a
k
-algebra
C
), yields the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\myiota_{C}:&{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}(C)\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m}_{k}(C)&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{P}}^{nm+n+m}_{k}(C)\\
&\big([a_{0}:\dotsc:a_{n}],[b_{0}:\dotsc:b_{m}]\big)&\longmapsto&[a_{0}b_{0}:\dotsc:a_{n}b_{m}].\end{array}

The homogeneous null ideal of the image of
\myiota
is generated by the quadratic polynomials
T_{i,j}T_{k,l}-T_{i,l}T_{k,j}
for
i,k\in\{0,\dotsc,n\}
and
j,l\in\{0,\dotsc,m\}
.

In particular, this shows that closed subvarieties of products of projective spaces are themselves projective varieties.

Example 4.6

(Flag varieties)
.

Flag varieties
\Fl(\textbf{r},E)
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
(where
\textbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{s})
is the type of the flag) were introduced in
[
JL24
]
as closed subvarieties of
\prod_{i=1}^{s}\Gr(r_{i},E)
. Quiver Grassmannians, as defined in
Section 6.3
are a vast generalization of flag varieties.

We exemplify this in the simplest nontrivial example, which is the flag variety
\Fl(\textbf{r},E)
for
E=\{1,2,3\}
and
\textbf{r}=(1,2)
. The ambient Grassmannians
\Gr(1,E)
and
\Gr(2,E)
are both projective planes
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, with respective homogeneous coordinates
T_{i}
(with
i\in E
for
\Gr(1,E)
) and
T_{k,l}
(with
k,l\in E
and
k\neq l
for
\Gr(2,E)
). The flag variety
\Fl(\textbf{r},E)
is the closed subvariety of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
defined by (the multi-homogeneous null ideal generated by) the single polynomial

T_{1}\cdot T_{2,3}\ -\ T_{2}\cdot T_{1,3}\ +\ T_{3}\cdot T_{1,2}.

4.4.
Line bundles and maps to projective space

In this section, we introduce
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
-modules and line bundles, and we explain that global sections that generate a line bundle define a map to a projective space.

4.4.1.
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
-modules

Let
B
be a band. A
B
-module is a pointed set
M
, with base point
0_{M}
, together with a subset
N_{M}
of the free semigroup
M^{+}={\mathbb{N}}[M-\{0_{M}\}]
(called the
null set of
M
) and a map
\mytheta:B\times M\to M
, written as
a.m=\mytheta(a,m)
, such that for all
a,a_{i},b\in B
and
m,m_{j},n\in M
(where we identify
0_{M}
with the empty sum in
M^{+}
):

(1)

1.m=m
,
0.m=0_{M}
,
and

a.0_{M}=0_{M}
;

(2)

(ab).m=a.(b.m)
;

(3)

\sum a_{i}.m_{j}\in N_{M}
whenever
\sum a_{i}\in N_{B}
or
\sum m_{j}\in N_{M}
;

(4)

m+n\in N_{M}
if and only if
n=-m:=(-1).m
.

A
B
-linear morphism between
B
-modules
M
and
M^{\prime}
is a map
f:M\to M^{\prime}
such that
f(a.m)=a.f(m)
and
\sum f(m_{j})\in N_{M^{\prime}}
whenever
\sum m_{j}\in N_{M}
. We denote the category of
B
-modules by
\Mod_{B}
. Note that an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-module is the same as a pointed set
M
together with a subset
N_{M}
of
M^{+}
that contains
0_{M}
and such that for every
m\in M
, there is a unique
n\in M
such that
m+n\in N_{M}
.

Let
X
be a band scheme. An
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
-module
is a sheaf
{\mathcal{M}}
in
\Mod_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
together with a morphism
\mytheta:{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{M}}
of sheaves such that
\mytheta(U):{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)\times{\mathcal{M}}(U)\to{\mathcal{M}}(U)
endows
{\mathcal{M}}(U)
with the structure of an
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)
-module and such that the restriction maps
\res:{\mathcal{M}}(U)\to{\mathcal{M}}(V)
for opens
V\subseteq U
are
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(U)
-linear.

4.4.2.
Line bundles

A
line bundle
is an
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
-module
{\mathcal{L}}
on
X
that is locally isomorphic to
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
, i.e. there is an open covering
X=\bigcup U_{i}
such that
{\mathcal{L}}|_{U_{i}}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{X}|_{U_{i}}
as
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}|_{U_{i}}
-modules for all
i
. We write
\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}
for the
\Gamma X
-module
\Gamma(X,{\mathcal{L}})
.

The sheaf pullback of a line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}
over
X
along a morphism
\myvarphi:Y\to X
is a line bundle
\myvarphi^{\ast}{\mathcal{L}}
over
Y
. The pullback of global sections defines a
\Gamma X
-linear map
\Gamma\myvarphi:\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}\to\Gamma(\myvarphi^{\ast}{\mathcal{L}})
.

Example 4.7

(Line bundles on points)
.

Let
F
be an idyll and
X=\Spec F
its spectrum, whose unique point is the trivial ideal
\myeta=\{0\}
of
F
. A line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}
over
X
is characterized by its global sections
\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}(\{\myeta\})
, which are isomorphic to
\Gamma X=F
by the definition of a line bundle. In particular, every line bundle over
X
is isomorphic to the trivial line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
.

Example 4.8

(Twisted sheaves on projective space)
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The
twisted line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}(d)
over
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}=\Proj{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]
is defined as the
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}
-module with values

{\mathcal{O}}(d)(U_{h})\ =\ \big({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}][h^{-1}]\big)_{d}

for homogeneous
h\in{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}]^{+}
, where
(-)_{d}
refers to the
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}(U_{h})
-submodule of degree
d
elements. For example, the
canonical sections

T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
are global sections of the
twisting sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

4.4.3.
Morphisms to projective space

A subset
\{s_{i}\}\subseteq\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}
of global sections is said to
generate
{\mathcal{L}}
if for every
x\in X
, there is an
i
such that the class of
s_{i}
is a unit in
{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}
. Let
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
be two line bundles together with respective ordered sets of global sections
(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{n})
and
(s_{0}^{\prime},\dotsc,s_{n}^{\prime})
. We say that
\big({\mathcal{L}},(s_{i})\big)
and
\big({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},(s_{i}^{\prime})\big)
are equivalent if there is an isomorphism
\mytheta:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
of line bundles such that
\mytheta(s_{i})=s_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
. We denote by
\mathpzc{M}_{n}(X)
the set of all equivalence classes of such
({\mathcal{L}},(s_{i}))
for which
\{s_{0},\dotsc,s_{n}\}
generates
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Let
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
be the twisting sheaf on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}=\Proj({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}])
and
T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}\in\Gamma{\mathcal{O}}(1)
the canonical sections (see
Section 4.4.2
). The following result is
[
BL21
, Thm. 4.20]
, specialized from ordered blue schemes to band schemes.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
X
be a band scheme and
T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}
the canonical global sections of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. Then pulling back
T_{0},\dotsc,T_{n}
along a morphism
\mychi:X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
yields a functorial bijection

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Hom(X,{\mathbb{P}}^{n})&\longrightarrow&\mathpzc{M}_{n}(X)\\
\mychi:X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}&\longmapsto&\big[\mychi^{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}(1),(\Gamma\mychi(T_{i}))\big].\end{array}

5.
Matroid bundles

Matroid bundles for ordered blue schemes are introduced in
[
BL21
]
. In this section, we transfer this definition to band schemes, reformulate the moduli property of Grassmannians in this setting and introduce morphisms for matroid bundles, which is a novel concept.

5.1.
Definition

Let
X
be a band scheme,
r
an integer and
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. A
Grassmann-Plücker function over
X
(of rank
r
on
E
)
is a line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}
over
X
together with a function

\Delta:\ E^{r}\ \longrightarrow\ \Gamma{\mathcal{L}}

that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\{\Delta(\textbf{x})\mid\textbf{x}\in E^{r}\}
generates
{\mathcal{L}}
;

(2)

\Delta(\textbf{x})=0
if
\#|\textbf{x}|<r
;

(3)

\Delta(\textbf{x})=\sign(\mysigma)\cdot\Delta(\textbf{x}^{\mysigma})
for every
\mysigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
;

(4)

for all
x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r},y_{0},\dotsc,y_{r}\in E
,

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\ (-1)^{k}\cdot\Delta(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{r})\otimes\Delta(y_{k},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r})\quad\in\quad N_{\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}^{\otimes 2}}.

Two Grassmann-Plücker functions
\mymu:E^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mymu^{\prime}:E^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
(on the same set
E
, over the same band scheme
X
and with same rank
r
) are
equivalent
if there exists an isomorphism
\myvarphi:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
such that
\mymu^{\prime}=\Gamma\myvarphi\circ\mymu
. A
matroid bundle over
X
is an equivalence class
{\mathcal{M}}=[\mymu:E^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}]
of a Grassmann-Plücker function
\mymu
over
X
.

Given a matroid bundle
{\mathcal{M}}=[\mymu:E^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}]
over
X
and a morphism of band schemes
\mytheta:Y\rightarrow X
, we define the
pullback
of
{\mathcal{M}}
along
\mytheta
as the matroid bundle
\mytheta^{*}{\mathcal{M}}
over
Y
given by the equivalence class of the composition

E^{r}\overset{\mymu}{\longrightarrow}\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}\overset{\Gamma\mytheta}{\longrightarrow}\Gamma(\mytheta^{*}{\mathcal{L}}).

Example 5.1

(Matroid bundles on points)
.

Let
F
be an idyll,
X=\Spec F
its spectrum and
{\mathcal{M}}=[\mymu:E^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}]
a matroid bundle over
X
. As discussed in
Section 4.4.2
, every line bundle over
X
is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
. Thus we may assume that
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{O}}_{X}
and
\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}=F
, which identifies
\mymu:E^{r}\to F
as a Grassmann-Plücker function over
F
(see
Section 1.3
). Since every automorphism of
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
is given by multiplication with an element in
F^{\times}
, the class of
\mymu
as a matroid bundle over
X
and that as an
F
-matroid coincide.

This shows that matroid bundles over
\Spec F
correspond bijectively and in a canonical way to
F
-matroids.

5.2.
The moduli property of the Grassmannian

Let
r
be an integer,
E:=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
\Gr(r,E)
the Grassmannian over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which comes with the Plücker embedding
\myiota:\Gr(r,E)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r}-1}
into projective space (see
Section 4.3.2
). Let
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
be the twisting sheaf on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r}-1}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\textup{univ}}=\myiota^{\ast}{\mathcal{O}}(1)
its pullback to
\Gr(r,E)
, which we call the
universal line bundle of
\Gr(r,E)
. The
canonical sections of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\textup{univ}}
are the global sections
s_{\textbf{x}}=\Gamma\myiota(T_{\textbf{x}})
of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\textup{univ}}
for
{\textbf{x}}\in E^{r}
.

The following result identifies the Grassmannian
\Gr(r,E)
with the fine moduli space of matroid bundles of rank
r
on
E
(cf.
[
BL21
, Thm. 5.5]
).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
X
be a band scheme. Sending a morphism
\mychi:X\to\Gr(r,E)
to the matroid bundle
\big[\mymu_{\mychi}:E^{r}\to\Gamma\mychi^{\ast}{\mathcal{L}}^{\textup{univ}}\big]
over
X
, given by
\mymu_{\mychi}({\mathbf{x}})=\Gamma\mychi(s_{\mathbf{x}})
, defines a bijection

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Phi:&\Hom\big(X,\,\Gr(r,E)\big)&\longrightarrow&\Big\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{matroid bundles of rank $r$}\\
\text{with ground set $E$ over $X$}\end{array}\Big\}\end{array}

that is functorial in
X
. If
\Phi(\mychi)={\mathcal{M}}
, then we call
\mychi
the
characteristic morphism of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Remark 5.3
.

Let
k
be a field, considered as an idyll, and
X
a band
k
-scheme. By the functoriality of the base extension
(-)^{+}
from band
k
-schemes to (usual)
k
-schemes, we have a canonical injection

\Hom_{\mathsf{BSch}}\big(X,\,\Gr(r,E)\big)\ \longrightarrow\ \Hom_{k}\big(X^{+},\,\Gr(r,E)^{+}_{k}\big).

where
\Gr(r,E)^{+}_{k}
is the usual Grassmann variety over
k
. Together with
Theorem 5.2
, this identifies rank
r
matroid bundles over
X
on
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
with rank
r
subbundles of the trivial rank
n
vector bundle
{\mathcal{O}}^{n}_{X^{+}}
over
X^{+}
.

5.3.
Morphisms of matroid bundles

Since the notion of vectors for matroid bundles is intricate (see
Section 5.3
), it is better for our purposes to describe morphism of matroid bundles in terms of Grassmann-Plücker functions (cf.
Theorem 2.8
).

In analogy to the case of perfect idylls, we consider submonomial matrices with entries in a band
B
in the following. Each submonomial
B
-matrix
\Phi
induces an
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map
\underline{\Phi}
with
{\underline{\Phi}}(j)=i
if
\Phi_{i,j}\neq 0
and
{\underline{\Phi}}(j)=0
if
j=0
or if
{\underline{\Phi}}_{i,j}=0
for all
i
. Conversely, if
B\neq 0
, each
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map
f
induces a unique submonomial
B
-matrix
f_{B}
with entries in
\{0,1\}
that satisfies
\underline{f_{B}}=f
.

Let
{\mathcal{N}}=[\mynu:S^{w}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{N}}]
and
{\mathcal{M}}=[\mymu:T^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{M}}]
be matroid bundles over
X
. A
morphism
\Phi:{\mathcal{N}}\to{\mathcal{M}}
of matroid bundles
is a submonomial
\Gamma X
-matrix
\Phi
indexed by
T\times S
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{w}(-1)^{k}\cdot\Phi_{{\underline{\Phi}}(y_{k}),y_{k}}\cdot\mynu(y_{0},\dotsc,\widehat{y_{k}},\dotsc,y_{w})\otimes\widetilde{\mymu}\big(\underline{\Phi}(y_{k}),x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r}\big)\quad\in\quad N_{\Gamma({\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{N}}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{M}})}

for all
y_{0},\dotsc,y_{w}\in S
and
x_{2},\dotsc,x_{r}\in T
, where
\widetilde{\mymu}:\underline{T}^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{M}}}
is given by
\widetilde{\mymu}(\textbf{z}):=\mymu(\textbf{z})
if
\textbf{z}\in T^{r}
and
\widetilde{\mymu}(\textbf{z}):=0
if
0\in|\textbf{z}|
(cf.
Section 2.2
).

Example 5.4

(Morphisms over points)
.

Let
F
be a perfect idyll,
X=\Spec F
and
{\mathcal{N}}=[\mynu:S^{w}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{O}}_{X}]
and
{\mathcal{M}}=[\mymu:T^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{O}}_{X}]
two matroid bundles over
X
(recall from
Section 5.1
that every line bundle over

is trivial). Since
\Gamma{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=F
, the Grassmann-Plücker function
\mynu:S^{w}\to F
defines an
F
-matroid

and
\mymu:T^{r}\to F
defines an
F
-matroid
M
. By the very definition of morphisms for
F
-matroids and for matroid bundles, a submonomial matrix
\Phi
indexed by
T\times S
is a morphism of matroid bundles
\Phi:{\mathcal{N}}\to{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if it is a morphism of
F
-matroids
\Phi:N\to M
.

Remark 5.5

(Composition of morphisms)
.

Morphisms of matroid bundles are in general not composable. This fails already for matroids over idylls that are not perfect (see
[
JL24
, Ex. 2.19]
).

This failure goes back to the fact that vectors are in general not orthogonal to covectors. This issue can be resolved by an intricate categorical construction, which is the theme of a forthcoming work. In particular, this leads to a richer class of morphisms, which are composable and which contain morphisms as defined in this text as a subclass.

5.4.
The dual of a matroid bundle

Let
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
r
an integer. For each
\textbf{x}\in E^{r^{*}}
with
\#|\textbf{x}|=r^{*}:=n-r
, we fix
\textbf{x}^{\prime}\in E^{r}
such that
|\textbf{x}|\sqcup|\textbf{x}^{\prime}|=E
. Let
X
be a band scheme and
{\mathcal{M}}=[\mymu:E^{r}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}]
a matroid bundle over
X
. The dual of
{\mathcal{M}}
is the matroid bundle
{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}:=[\mymu^{\vee}:E^{r^{\ast}}\to\Gamma{\mathcal{L}}]
represented by the Grassmann-Plücker function
\mymu^{\vee}
given by

\mymu^{\vee}({\bf x})\ :=\ \begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\#|\textbf{x}|<r^{*}\\
\sign({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime})\cdot\myvarphi({\bf x}^{\prime})&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

where, for
\textbf{z}\in E^{n}
,
\sign(\textbf{z})
is the sign of the unique permutation
\mysigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
z_{\mysigma(1)}<\dots<z_{\mysigma(n)}
if
|\textbf{z}|=E
, and
0
if
\#|\textbf{z}|<n
.

Duality of matroid bundles is induced by an isomorphism between Grassmannians. The isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{ccc}{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{\bf x}\mid{\bf x}\in E^{r^{\ast}}]\!\sslash\!\langle\mathpzc{Pl}(r^{\ast},E)\rangle&\longrightarrow&{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{\bf x}\mid{\bf x}\in E^{r}]\!\sslash\!\langle\mathpzc{Pl}(r,E)\rangle\\
&&\\
T_{{\mathbf{x}}}&\longmapsto&\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\#|{\mathbf{x}}|<r^{\ast}\\
\sign({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\cdot T_{{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{array}

of graded bands induces an isomorphism
\mydelta_{r,E}:\Gr(r,E)\to\Gr(r^{\ast},E)
of band schemes. The following is
[
BL21
, Prop. 5.8]
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
X
be a band scheme and
{\mathcal{M}}
a matroid bundle with characteristic morphism
\mychi_{\mathcal{M}}:X\to\Gr(r,E)
. Let
{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}
be the dual of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Then the characteristic morphism of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}
is
\mychi_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}}=\mydelta_{r,E}\circ\mychi_{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e. the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gr(r,E)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gr(r^{\ast},E)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mydelta_{r,E}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mychi_{\mathcal{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mychi_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}}}

commutes.

5.5.
The dual of a morphism

Duality for matroid bundles extends to a duality on morphisms.

6.
The moduli space of quiver matroids

In
[
JL24
, Section 3]
, the first and second authors construct the moduli space for flag matroids with coefficients. Here we generalize this construction to the case of
quiver matroids
. In this section we assume that every quiver is finite, i.e. it has a finite set of vertices and arrows.

6.1.
Classical quiver Grassmannians

Quiver Grassmannians appeared for the first time in a paper of Schofield (
[
Sch92
]
), but they did not gain much attention until Caldero and Chapoton exhibit in
[
CC06
]
an explicit formula for cluster variables in terms of the Euler characteristics of quiver Grassmannians, which turned quiver Grassmannians and their Euler characteristics into objects of central interest for representation theory.

Let
k
be a field,
Q
be a quiver and
\Lambda
a finite dimensional representation of
Q
over
k
. Let
\textbf{e}=(e_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
be a dimension vector for
Q
. As a set, the
quiver Grassmannian

\Gr_{\textbf{e}}(\Lambda)(k)
is defined as the collection of all subrepresentations
M
of
\Lambda
with dimension vector
e
. It gains its structure as a projective variety from the embedding

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gr_{\textbf{e}}(\Lambda)(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\displaystyle\prod_{v\in Q_{0}}\Gr(e_{v},\Lambda_{v})(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(M_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(M_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}}

as a closed subset of the product of classical Grassmann varieties
\Gr(e_{v},\Lambda_{v})(k)
over
k
.

The quiver Grassmannian gains the structure of a
k
-scheme in terms of the following set of defining equations (see
[
LW19a
]
). For each vertex
v
, let
{\mathcal{B}}_{v}
be an ordered basis of
\Lambda_{v}
, which yields homogeneous coordinates
[\dotsc:\Delta_{I}:\dotsc]
for the Grassmannian
\Gr(e_{v},\Lambda_{v})
, where
I
is an
e_{v}
-subset of
{\mathcal{B}}_{v}
. For each arrow
\myalpha
, let
[a_{i,j}]
be the matrix which represents
\Lambda_{\myalpha}
with respect to the bases
{\mathcal{B}}_{s(\myalpha)}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{t(\myalpha)}
. For
I\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{v}
and
i\in{\mathcal{B}}_{v}
, define

\myepsilon(i,I)\ =\ \#\,\{i^{\prime}\in I\mid i^{\prime}\leq i\}.

The
quiver Plücker relations
are the equations

E(\myalpha,I,J):\quad\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}_{v}-I,\,j\in J}(-1)^{\myepsilon(i,I)+\myepsilon(j,J)}\penalty\ a_{i,j}\penalty\ \Delta_{I\cup\{i\}}\penalty\ \Delta_{J-\{j\}}\;=\;0,

where
\myalpha
varies through
Q_{1}
,
I
varies through all
(e_{s(\myalpha)}-1)
-subsets of
{\mathcal{B}}_{s(\myalpha)}
and
J
varies through all
(e_{t(\myalpha)}+1)
-subsets of
{\mathcal{B}}_{t(\myalpha)}
. These equations define
\Gr_{\textbf{e}}(\Lambda)
as a closed subscheme of the product of Grassmannians
\prod\Gr(e_{v},\Lambda_{v})
.

6.2.
The moduli functor of quiver matroids

In the following, we introduce quiver matroid bundles and show that the quiver Plücker relations define a band model of the quiver Grassmannian, which turns out to be the moduli space of quiver matroid bundles.

For the remainder of this section, we fix a finite quiver
Q
and a representation
\Lambda
of
Q
over
\mathsf{Vect}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
. We assume that
E_{v}:=\Lambda_{v}-\{0\}
is finite for all
v\in Q_{0}
.

Let
B
be a band. We denote by
\Lambda_{\myalpha,B}
the submonomial
B
-matrix with coefficients

(\Lambda_{\myalpha,B})_{i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}1&\text{if }\Lambda(j)=i\neq 0,\\
0&\text{if there is no $i\in E_{t(\myalpha)}$ such that $\Lambda(j)=i$},\end{cases}

where
(i,j)
ranges through
E_{t(\myalpha)}\times E_{s(\myalpha)}
; cf.

.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
X
be a band scheme. A
\Lambda
-matroid bundle over
X
, or

-matroid bundle for short, is a tuple

such that the submonomial

is the tuple
\big(\rk({\mathcal{M}}_{v})\big)_{v\in Q_{0}}

Let

be a dimension vector for

as follows: for a band scheme

\mathpzc{Gr}_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)(X)\;:=\;\{(\Lambda,X)\text{-matroid bundles of rank }\textbf{r}\},

and for a morphism of band schemes

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathpzc{Gr}_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)(\myvarphi):&\mathpzc{Gr}_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)(Y)&\longrightarrow&\mathpzc{Gr}_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)(X)\\

7.
Euler characteristics of quiver Grassmannians

In this section, we extend previous comparison results between the complex Euler characteristic and the number of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-rational points from flag varieties to a larger class of quiver Grassmannians. Throughout this section we assume that every quiver is finite, i.e. it has a finite set of vertices and arrows.

More precisely, let
X
be a band scheme and
X({\mathbb{C}})=X^{+}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathbb{C}})
be the complex analytic space of
{\mathbb{C}}
-rational points. The Tits space of
X
is a certain subspace of
X({\mathbb{K}})
. In general the Euler characteristic of
X({\mathbb{C}})
differs from the cardinality of
X^{\textup{Tits}}
, but in nice situations they agree, e.g. for flag varieties over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
; cf.
[
LT23
]
and
[
BJL24
, Section 3.4]
.

In this section, we extend this result to a larger class of quiver Grassmannians
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)
. Our method of proof uses a torus action on
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)
whose fixed points in
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{C}})
are coordinate vectors. By a result of Białynicki-Birula (
[
BB73
]
), the Euler characteristic of
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{C}})
agrees with the number of these coordinate vectors. An analysis of the combinatorics of
\Lambda
(in terms of its coefficient quiver) eventually leads to an identification of the coordinate vectors with the points of
X^{\textup{Tits}}
. This analysis relies on some basic matroid theoretic constructions.

7.1.
The fine topology

Let
F
be an idyll with a topology for which the multiplication of
F
and the inversion of
F^{\times}
are continuous and such that
0
is a closed point. The examples of main interest are the complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
with the usual topology and the Krasner hyperfield
{\mathbb{K}}
with the topology for which
0
is closed, but not
1
.

Then we can topologize the sets of
F
-rational points
X(F)
of a band scheme
X
in the following way (cf.
[
BJL24
, Thm. 3.5]
).

Theorem 7.1
.

There is a unique enrichment of
h_{F}=\Hom(\Spec F,-)
to a functor
h_{F}:\BSch\to\Top
such that the following properties hold (where we write
X(F)
for
h_{F}(X)
):

(1)

The canonical bijection
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}(F)\to F^{n}
is a homeomorphism.

(2)

If
\myiota:Y\to X
is an open (closed) immersion, then
\myiota_{F}:Y(F)\to X(F)
is an open (closed) topological embedding.

(3)

If

is an open covering, then
X(F)=\bigcup U_{i}(F)
is an open topological covering.

(4)

If
X=\Spec B
, then the canonical bijection

is a homeomorphism where
\Hom(B,F)
is endowed with the compact-open topology with respect to the discrete topology for
B
.

This topology for
X(F)
is called the

.

In particular, the topology of
X({\mathbb{C}})=X^{+}_{\mathbb{C}}({\mathbb{C}})
coincides with the usual complex topology for every band scheme
X
. The space of
{\mathbb{K}}
-rational points

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{K}})\ =\ \big\{[a_{0}:\dotsc:a_{n}]\,\big|\,a_{0},\dotsc,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{K}}\big\}

is finite and its topology is determined by the rule that
[a_{0}:\dotsc:a_{n}]
is in the closure of
[b_{0}:\dotsc:b_{n}]
if and only if
b_{i}=0
implies
a_{i}=0
for all

. According to
Theorem 7.1
, the product
(\prod{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}})({\mathbb{K}})=\prod\big({\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}}({\mathbb{K}})\big)
of projective spaces carries the product topology and the
{\mathbb{K}}
-points
X({\mathbb{K}})
of a closed subscheme

carry the subspace topology. This applies to all quiver Grassmannians.

7.2.
The Tits space

The
Tits space
X^{\textup{Tits}}
of a band scheme
X
is defined as the subset of closed points of
X({\mathbb{K}})
.

For example, the Tits space of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is the subspace

({\mathbb{P}}^{n})^{\textup{Tits}}\ =\ \big\{[1:0:\dotsc:0],\dotsc,[0:\dotsc:0:1]\big\}

of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{K}})=\{[a_{0}:\dotsc:a_{n}]\mid a_{0},\dotsc,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{K}}\}
. More generally,

\Gr(r,E)^{\textup{Tits}}\ =\ \big\{[\mymu_{\textbf{x}}]_{{\textbf{x}}\in E^{r}}\,\big|\,\text{there is an $r$-subset $I\subseteq E$ such that }\mymu_{\textbf{x}}=0\text{ if }|{\textbf{x}}|\neq I\big\}

as a subspace of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n^{r}-1}({\mathbb{K}})
. Since for given
I\subseteq E
, there is a unique point
[\mymu_{\textbf{x}}]\in\Gr(r,E)({\mathbb{K}})
with
\mymu_{\textbf{x}}=0
for
|{\textbf{x}}|\neq I
, the Tits space of
\Gr(r,E)
has
\binom{n}{r}
points, which is equal to the Euler characteristic of the complex Grassmann variety
\Gr(r,E)({\mathbb{C}})
.

7.3.
The initial matroid

The initial matroid of a valuated matroid was introduced in Dress-Wenzel’s seminal paper on valuated matroids (see
[
DW92
, Prop. 2.9]
). The term “initial matroid” does not appear in this paper, however, but makes its first appearance in
[
MS15
, Def. 4.2.7]
, to the best of our knowledge; also see
[
BS22
]
.

In this section, we consider only particular types of initial matroids, cf.
Section 7.3
for details on the relation to valuated matroids.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
M
be a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid on
E
and
\partial:E\to{\mathbb{Z}}
a map. For

, define

and

\mathcal{B}\ =\ \{B\in\mathcal{B}(M)\mid\partial(B)=m\}

is the set of bases of a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid.

Proof.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
and
x\in B_{1}-B_{2}
. As
M
is a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid, there exists

such that

and
A_{2}:=B_{2}\cup\{x\}-\{y\}
are bases of

. Note that

and

, thus

, i.e.

.
∎

(\partial_{0},\dotsc,\partial_{n})
be a finite sequence of maps
\partial_{i}:E\to{\mathbb{Z}}
. The
initial matroid with respect to
(\partial_{0},\dotsc,\partial_{n})
is the
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid

\textstyle\min_{\partial_{n},\dotsc,\partial_{0}}(M)=\min_{\partial_{n}}\circ\dots\circ\min_{\partial_{0}}(M).

Remark 7.4
.

The relation to initial matroids in the sense of
[
DW92
]
and
[
MS15
]
is as follows. Let
M
be a matroid with Grassmann-Plücker function
\Delta:E^{r}\to{\mathbb{K}}
and
v=\myiota\circ\Delta:R^{r}\to{\mathbb{T}}
the trivial valuation where
\myiota:{\mathbb{K}}\to{\mathbb{T}}
is the natural inclusion. The choice of a function
\partial:E\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and a
q>1
defines a rescaling
v_{q,\partial}:E^{r}\to{\mathbb{T}}
of
v
, defined by
v_{q,\partial}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})=q^{\partial(x_{1})+\dotsb+\partial(x_{r})}\cdot v(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r})
. The initial matroid
\min_{\partial}(M)
(in our sense) is the same as the initial matroid of the valuated matroid
[v_{q,\partial}]
in the sense of
[
DW92
]
and
[
MS15
]
(which does not depend on the choice of
q>1
).

Since
\mathcal{B}\big(\min_{\partial}(M)\big)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(M)
, we have
\rk(M)=\rk\big(\min_{\partial}(M)\big)
. The identity map on
{\underline{E}}
is a weak map (in the sense of
[
KN86
]
) between the underlying matroids of
M
and
\min_{\partial}(M)
.

If we interpret
M
and
\min_{\partial}(M)
as points of
\Gr(r,E)({\mathbb{K}})
via
Theorem 5.2
, then this means that
\min_{\partial}(M)
is contained in the closure of
M
with respect to the natural topology from
Section 7.1
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
M
be a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid on
E
and
\partial_{0},\dotsc,\partial_{n}:E\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
maps such that for any
a,b\in E
there is an
i
with
\partial_{i}(a)\neq\partial_{i}(b)
. Then
\min_{\partial_{n},\dotsc,\partial_{0}}(M)=M
if, and only if,
M
has only one basis.

Proof.

Assume
\min_{\partial_{n},\dotsc,\partial_{0}}(M)=M
and let
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(M)
. Note that
\partial_{i}
is constant in
\mathcal{B}(M)
for every
i
. If
B_{1}\neq B_{2}
, fix
x\in B_{1}-B_{2}
. Then there exists
y\in B_{2}-B_{1}
such that
B_{1}\cup\{y\}-\{x\}
is a basis of
M
.

Let
\ell
such that
\partial_{\ell}(x)\neq\partial_{\ell}(y)
. Then

\partial_{\ell}(B_{1})\;=\;\partial_{\ell}(B_{1}-\{x\})+\partial_{\ell}(x)\;\neq\;\partial_{\ell}(B_{1}-\{x\})+\partial_{\ell}(y)\;=\;\partial_{\ell}(B_{1}\cup\{y\}-\{x\}).

Since
\partial_{\ell}
is constant on
\mathcal{B}(M)
, one has a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
N
and
M
be two
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids with respective ground sets
S
and
T
. Let
\Phi:N\rightarrow M
be a morphism and
\partial:S\sqcup T\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
a map such that

\partial(s_{1})-\partial\big(\underline{\Phi}(s_{1})\big)\ =\ \partial(s_{2})-\partial\big(\underline{\Phi}(s_{2})\big)

for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in\underline{\Phi}^{-1}(T)
. Then
\Phi:\min_{\partial|_{S}}(N)\rightarrow\min_{\partial|_{T}}(M)
is a morphism.

Proof.

Let
\mynu
and
\mymu
be Grassmann-Plücker functions for
N
and
M
, respectively. Write
\min_{\partial|_{S}}(N)=[\mynu^{\prime}]
,
\min_{\partial|_{T}}(M)=[\mymu^{\prime}]
,
w:=\rk(N)
and
r:=\rk(M)
. Let
\mathbf{y}\in S^{w+1}
and
\mathbf{x}\in T^{r-1}
. Suppose that there exists
\ell\in\{1,\dotsc,w+1\}
such that

\mynu^{\prime}(\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{\ell}})\ \Phi_{y_{\ell}}\ \mymu^{\prime}\big(\underline{\Phi}(y_{\ell}),\mathbf{x}\big)=1.

In particular,
|\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{\ell}}|
is a basis of
N
,
\{\underline{\Phi}(y_{\ell})\}\cup|\mathbf{x}|
is a basis of
M
and
\Phi_{y_{\ell}}=1
, i.e.

\mynu(\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{\ell}})\ \Phi_{y_{\ell}}\ \mymu\big(\underline{\Phi}(y_{\ell}),\mathbf{x}\big)=1.

As
\Phi:N\rightarrow M
is a morphism and
N_{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{N}}-\{1\}
, there exists
m\neq\ell
such that

\mynu(\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{m}})\ \Phi_{y_{m}}\ \mymu\big(\underline{\Phi}(y_{m}),\mathbf{x}\big)=1.

Next, we prove that
|\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{m}}|
is basis of
\min_{\partial|_{S}}(N)
and
\{\underline{\Phi}(y_{\ell})\}\cup|\mathbf{x}|
is a basis of
\min_{\partial|_{T}}(M)
. Let
m_{N}:=\min\{\partial(B)\mid B\in\mathcal{B}(N)\}
and
m_{M}:=\min\{\partial(B)\mid B\in\mathcal{B}(M)\}
. Note that

m_{N}+m_{M}\;=\;\partial(|\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{\ell}}|)+\partial(\{\underline{\Phi}(y_{\ell})\}\cup|\mathbf{x}|)\;=\;\partial(|\mathbf{y}|\cup|\mathbf{x}|)\;=\;\partial(|\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{m}}|)+\partial(\{\underline{\Phi}(y_{m})\}\cup|\mathbf{x}|).

As
m_{N}\leqslant\partial(|\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{m}}|)
and
m_{M}\leqslant\partial(\{\underline{\Phi}(y_{m})\}\cup|\mathbf{x}|)
, these must be equalities, thus
|\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{m}}|
is basis of
\min_{\partial|_{S}}(N)
and
\{\underline{\Phi}(y_{\ell})\}\cup|\mathbf{x}|
is a basis of
\min_{\partial|_{T}}(M)
, i.e.

\mynu^{\prime}(\mathbf{y}_{\widehat{m}})\ \Phi_{y_{m}}\ \mymu^{\prime}\big(\underline{\Phi}(y_{m}),\mathbf{x}\big)=1.

By
Theorem 2.8
,
\Phi
is a morphism from
\min_{\partial|_{S}}(N)
to
\min_{\partial|_{T}}(M)
.
∎

7.4.
Nice gradings

Nice gradings of quiver representations are a tool introduced by Cerulli Irelli (
[
CI11
]
) to compute the Euler characteristic of quiver Grassmannians in terms of subquivers of the coefficient quivers. They were further refined by Haupt (
[
Hau12
]
) and by Jun-Sistko (
[
JS21
]
). The latter paper links coefficient quivers that are windings (as they appear in
[
Hau12
]
) to
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representations; cf.
Section 7.4
.

For the remainder of this section, we fix a finite quiver
Q
and an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation
\Lambda
of
Q
. We write
E_{v}
for the (unpointed) set
\Lambda_{v}-\{0\}
and
n_{v}
for its cardinality. For a dimension vector
{\textbf{r}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
of
Q
, we define
{\textbf{r}}^{*}=(r_{v}^{*})_{v\in Q_{0}}
with
r^{\ast}=n_{v}-r_{v}
.

Definition 7.7

(
[
JS21
, Def. 4.1]
)
.

A
grading of
\Lambda
is a map
\partial:{\bigsqcup}_{v\in Q_{0}}E_{v}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
. A grading
\partial
of
\Lambda
is
nice
if

\partial(a)-\partial\big(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\big)=\partial(b)-\partial\big(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(b)\big)

for all
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
and
a,b\in\Lambda_{s(\myalpha)}
with
\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\neq 0
and
\Lambda_{\myalpha}(b)\neq 0
.

A nice grading
\partial
of
\Lambda
extends to
Q_{1}
via the rule

\partial(\myalpha):=\begin{cases}\partial(a)-\partial\big(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\big)&\text{for }a\in\Lambda_{s(\myalpha)}\text{ with }\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\neq 0,\\
{}0&\text{if there is no }a\in\Lambda_{s(\myalpha)}\text{ with }\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\neq 0.\end{cases}

Note that the value
\partial(\myalpha)
does not depend on the choice of
a\in\Lambda_{s(\myalpha)}
since
\partial
is nice.

Definition 7.8
.

Let
M
be a
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid and
\partial:{\bigsqcup}_{v\in Q_{0}}E_{v}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
a grading. The
initial matroid of
M
with respect to
\partial
is the tuple
\min_{\partial}(M):=\big(\min_{\partial|_{E_{v}}}(M_{v})\big)_{v\in Q_{0}}
of
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroids.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
M
be a
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid. If
\partial:{\bigsqcup}_{v\in Q_{0}}E_{v}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
a nice grading, then
\min_{\partial}(M)
is a
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid.

Proof.

This follows at once from
Section 7.3
.
∎

Definition 7.10
.

A
coordinate matroid
is a
{\mathbb{K}}
-matroid
M
with a unique basis. For
A\subseteq E
, we denote by
\textup{Co}(A,E)
the coordinate matroid on
E
that has
A
as its unique basis.

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
N=\textup{Co}(A,S)
and
M=\textup{Co}(B,T)
and
f:\underline{S}\rightarrow\underline{T}
an
{\mathbb{F}}_{1}
-linear map. Then
f
is a strong map from
N
to
M
if, and only if,
f(\underline{A^{c}})\subseteq\underline{B^{c}}
.

Proof.

Since the circuits of
N
are the elements of
S-A
and the cocircuits of
M
are the elements of
B
, the result follows from
Section 1.2
.
∎

Definition 7.12
.

A
subrepresentation of
\Lambda
is a tuple
\Omega=(\Omega_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
of pointed subsets
\Omega_{v}\subseteq\Lambda_{v}
such that
\Lambda_{\myalpha}({\Omega_{s(\myalpha)}})\subseteq\Omega_{t(\myalpha)}
for all
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
(cf.
Section 3.1
). The
dimension vector of
(\Omega_{v})
is the tuple

\mathbf{\dim}(\Omega)\ =\ (\#\Omega_{v}-1)_{v\in Q_{0}}\quad\in\quad{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}.

Proposition 7.13
.

Let
\mathbf{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
be a dimension vector for
Q
and
\mathbf{r}^{*}:=(\#E_{v}-r_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
. Then the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{subrepresentations of }\Lambda\\
\text{of dimension }\mathbf{r}^{*}\end{array}\bigg\}&\longrightarrow&\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{coordinate $(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})$-matroids}\\
\text{of rank }\mathbf{r}\end{array}\bigg\}\\[10.0pt]
({\Omega_{v}})_{v\in Q_{0}}&\longmapsto&\big(\textup{Co}(\Omega_{v}^{c},E_{v})\big)_{v\in Q_{0}}\end{array}

is a bijection, where
\Omega_{v}^{c}:=\Lambda_{v}-\Omega_{v}
.

Proof.

This follows from
Theorem 2.13
and
Section 7.4
.
∎

Remark 7.14
.

By
Theorem 6.9
, a
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid
M
of rank
r
corresponds to a
{\mathbb{K}}
-rational point of the quiver Grassmannian
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)
. If
M
is a coordinate
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid, then each matroid
M_{v}
has a unique basis and is thus a closed point of
\Gr(r_{v},E_{v})({\mathbb{K}})
. This defines an injection

\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{coordinate $(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})$-matroids}\\
\text{of rank }{\textbf{r}}\end{array}\bigg\}\ \longrightarrow\ \Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}

In the remainder of this section, we exhibit conditions that guarantee that this map is a bijection.

Definition 7.15

(
[
JS21
, Def. 4.1 and 4.6]
)
.

Let
\partial_{1},\dotsc,\partial_{n}
be gradings of
\Lambda
. A
nice
(\partial_{1},\dotsc,\partial_{n})
-grading
of
\Lambda
is a grading
\partial
such that for all
\myalpha\in Q_{1}
and
a,b\in E_{s(\myalpha)}
with
\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a),\Lambda_{\myalpha}(b)\neq 0
and with

\partial_{i}(a)=\partial_{i}(b)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{i}(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\big)=\partial_{i}(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(b)\big)\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,\dotsc,n,

it satisfies

\partial\big(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(a)\big)-\partial\big(\Lambda_{\myalpha}(b)\big)\ =\ \partial(a)-\partial(b).

A
nice sequence
for
\Lambda
is a sequence
\underline{\partial}=\{\partial_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
of gradings of
\Lambda
such that
\partial_{0}
is nice and
\partial_{i}
is a nice
(\partial_{0},\dotsc,\partial_{i-1})
-grading for all
i>0
. We say that
\underline{\partial}

distinguishes elements
if for any
a,b\in{\bigsqcup}_{v\in Q_{0}}E_{v}
with
a\neq b
, we have
\partial_{i}(a)\neq\partial_{i}(b)
for some
i
.

Theorem 7.16
.

If there exists a nice sequence of gradings for
\Lambda
that distinguishes elements, then

\mychi\big(\Gr_{\mathbf{r}^{*}}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}})\big)\ =\ \#\,\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}

for every
{\mathbf{r}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
.

Proof.

Since nice sequences of gradings of
\Lambda
in the sense of
Section 7.4
are nice sequences of gradings for the winding associated to the coefficient quiver of
\Lambda
in the sense of
[
JS21
, Def. 4.1]
,
\mychi\big(\Gr_{\textbf{r}^{*}}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}})\big)
is equal to the number of subrepresentations of
\Lambda
of dimension
{\textbf{r}^{*}}
by
[
JS21
, Prop. 5.2]
, which is the same as the number of coordinate
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroids of rank
r
by
Section 7.4
.

Let
\underline{\partial}=\{\partial_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a nice sequence of gradings for
\Lambda
that distinguishes elements. Since
Q_{0}
is finite, there exists an
n>0
such that for all distinct
a,b\in{\bigsqcup}\;E_{v}
, we have
\partial_{i}(a)\neq\partial_{i}(b)
for some
0\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Consider a
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid
M\in\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{K}})
. By
Section 7.3
and
Section 7.4
, the initial
(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})
-matroid
\min_{\partial_{n},\dotsc,\partial_{0}}(M)
is a coordinate matroid and thus lies in the closure of
M
. This shows that the injection

\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{coordinate $(\Lambda,{\mathbb{K}})$-matroids}\\
\text{of rank }{\textbf{r}}\end{array}\bigg\}\ \longrightarrow\ \Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}

from
Section 7.4
is a bijection under the assumption of the theorem.

Combining these identifications shows that the Euler characteristic of
\Gr_{\textbf{r}^{*}}(\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}})
is equal to the cardinality of
\Gr_{\textbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}
.
∎

Remark 7.17
.

In
[
CI11
]
and
[
Hau12
]
, one finds the condition that the coefficient quiver of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
is a
winding
, which means that the matrices
\Lambda_{{\mathbb{C}},\myalpha}
are submonomial. Since the matrices stemming from
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representations are by definition submonomial, the condition of a winding does not appear in our context.

7.5.
A formula for the Euler characteristic

Let
Q
be a finite quiver and
\Lambda
an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-representation of
Q
with
E_{v}=\Lambda_{v}-\{0\}
finite for all
v\in Q_{0}
. For a dimension vector
r
, we denote by
{\textbf{r}}^{\ast}=(\#\,E_{v}-r_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
its codimension vector.

The
coefficient quiver of
\Lambda
is the quiver
\Gamma
with vertex set

\Gamma_{0}\ =\ \bigsqcup_{v\in Q_{0}}\ E_{v}

and arrow set

\Gamma_{1}\ =\ \big\{(\myalpha,i,j)\,\big|\,\myalpha\in Q_{1},\,j\in\Lambda_{s(\myalpha)},\,i=\Lambda_{\myalpha}(j)\neq 0\big\}.

We say that
\Gamma
is a
tree
if its underlying graph is a tree and that
\Gamma
is a
primitive cycle
if its underlying graph is a single cycle and if
\#\,\Lambda_{v}\leqslant 2
for all
v\in Q_{0}
.

Theorem 7.18
.

If the coefficient quiver of
\Lambda
is a tree or a primitive cycle, then

\mychi\big(\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{C}})\big)\ =\ \#\,\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}

for any dimension vector
{\mathbf{r}}
.

Proof.

This follows at once from
Theorem 7.16
and
[
JS21
, Cor. 5.18, Thm. 5.20]
.
∎

Recall that
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
is the complex representation of
Q
with
\Lambda_{{\mathbb{C}},v}={\mathbb{C}}^{E_{v}}
and whose matrices
\Lambda_{{\mathbb{C}},\myalpha}
have coefficients

(\Lambda_{{\mathbb{C}},\myalpha})_{i,j}\ =\ \begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=\Lambda_{\myalpha}(j)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for
(i,j)\in E_{t(\myalpha)}\times E_{s(\myalpha)}
.

Corollary 7.19
.

If
Q
is a simply laced (extended) Dynkin quiver and
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
is irreducible, then

\mychi\big(\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)({\mathbb{C}})\big)\ =\ \#\,\Gr_{\mathbf{r}}(\Lambda)^{\textup{Tits}}

for any dimension vector
{\mathbf{r}}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be the coefficient quiver of
\Lambda
, and therefore of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
with respect to the canonical basis of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
\Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}
is irreducible,
\Gamma
is connected.

If
Q
is a tree, then also
\Gamma
is a tree, thus the result follows from
Theorem 7.18
in this case. If
Q
is not a tree, then
Q
must be of extended Dynkin type
\widetilde{A}_{n}
, i.e. the underlying graph of
Q
is a single cycle. Then every irreducible and finite dimensional representation of
Q
is either a string (which is a tree) or a primitive cycle. Thus also in this case, the result follows from
Theorem 7.18
.
∎
