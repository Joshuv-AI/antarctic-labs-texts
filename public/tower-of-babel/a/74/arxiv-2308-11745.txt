1.
Introduction

We consider the higher order Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\psi_{t}=(-\Delta)^{m}\psi+V\psi,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

with a real-valued, decaying potential
V
in
n
spatial dimensions when
2m<n<4m
.

We denote the free higher order Schrödinger operator as
H_{0}=(-\Delta)^{m}
and the perturbed operator by
H=(-\Delta)^{m}+V(x)
.
The free solution operator
e^{-itH_{0}}
satisfies the dispersive estimate

\|e^{-itH_{0}}\|_{1\to\infty}\leq C_{n,m}|t|^{-\frac{n}{2m}}.

We show, with
P_{ac}(H)
the projection onto the absolutely continuous subspace of
H
, that the solution operator
e^{-itH}P_{ac}(H)
satisfies the same bound for a class of scaling-critical potentials. That is, due to commutation relations between powers of the Laplacian and dilations, perturbing
H_{0}
by a re-scaled potential of the form
V_{r}(x)=r^{2m}V(rx)
obeys the same dispersive bounds as perturbing by
V
. The admissible class of potentials in our argument is invariant under the scaling from
V
to
V_{r}
.

A natural way to capture scaling properties is through the use of Kato-type norms. For
0\leq\alpha\leq n
, we define the
\alpha
-Kato norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be

(1)

\displaystyle\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|V(x)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\,dx.

When
\alpha=0
this is the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm. The Kato norm with
\alpha=n-2m
has critical scaling with respect to the higher order Laplacian in the sense above, and naturally arises in that
V(-\Delta)^{-m}
is a bounded operator on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
precisely when
V\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}
. In our main results we consider potentials in the closure of
C_{0}
with respect to the
{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}
norm and we denote this subspace
{\mathcal{K}}
.

To state our main theorem, we recall that a resonance at energy
\lambda
is a distributional solution to
H\psi=\lambda\psi
with
\psi\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, but in a slightly larger space depending on the dimension and order of the Laplacian. For
H=(-\Delta)^{m}+V
, a resonance occurs when
(1+|x|)^{-\sigma}\psi\in L^{2}
for some
\sigma>2m-\frac{n}{2}
. It may similarly be characterized in terms of non-invertibility of certain operators related to the free resolvent. Here, and throughout the paper, we write
A\lesssim B
to mean there exists a constant
C>0
so that
A\leq CB
. Our main result is

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
2m<n<4m
. If
V\in{\mathcal{K}}
, the closure of
C_{0}
functions in the
{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}
norm, is real-valued such that the operator
H=(-\Delta)^{m}+V
has no eigenvalues on
[0,\infty)
and no resonance at zero energy, then

\|e^{-itH}P_{ac}(H)\|_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-\frac{n}{2m}}.

In particular as part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
we obtain the fact that the operator
H
has no resonances in
(0,\infty)
under the assumption that there are no eigenvalues in
(0,\infty)
, see Corollary
5.3
below.

To motivate our second main result we note that the Fourier transform of the function
e^{i|\xi|^{2m}}|\xi|^{2m\alpha}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded provided that
-\frac{n}{2m}<\alpha\leq\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2m}
; see Lemma
4.3
below for a proof of this when
n=1
. By scaling and letting
\beta=\alpha+\frac{n}{2m}
, this implies the following family of dispersive estimates for
H_{0}=(-\Delta)^{m}
:

\||H_{0}|^{\beta-\frac{n}{2m}}e^{-itH_{0}}\|_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-\beta},\,\,\,\,0<\beta\leq\frac{n}{2}.

Dispersive estimates of this type (with
\beta=\frac{n}{2}
) were used by the authors in
[
10
]
in dimensions
n\geq 4m
to construct counterexamples to the
L^{p}
boundedness of the wave operators for insufficiently smooth potentials. The obstruction in
[
10
]
is not present in lower dimensions.
Here we prove

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
2m<n<4m
.
If
V\in{\mathcal{K}}
is real-valued such that the operator
H=(-\Delta)^{m}+V
has no eigenvalues on
[0,\infty)
and no resonance at zero energy, then

\||H|^{\beta-\frac{n}{2m}}e^{-itH}P_{ac}(H)\|_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-\beta},

provided that
0<\beta\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

Note that Theorem
1.1
is a special case of this when
\beta=\frac{n}{2m}\in(1,2)
.

Dispersive estimates in the case of
m=1
are well-studied in all dimensions. See, for example, the recent survey paper
[
23
]
, and the references therein.
When
m=1
and
n=3
, dispersive bounds for scaling critical potentials were proven by Beceanu and the second author,
[
5
]
, by applying an operator-valued Wiener inversion theorem. This type of inversion was first used by Beceanu in
[
3
]
. The operator-valued Wiener inversion theorem was later extended by Beceanu and the second author in
[
6
]
to study a three-dimensional wave equation, and by Beceanu in
[
4
]
to study the wave operators in three dimensions. Bui, Duong and Hong employed the Wiener inversion results in a further study of a three-dimensional wave equation. Beceanu and Schlag further refined results on the structure of the wave operators in three dimensions,
[
7
,
8
]
. Hill used the Wiener inversion method to study the case of
n=1
and
m=1,2
,
[
20
]
. We note that this is the first time this method has been used in even dimensions, or on higher order operators for scaling-critical potentials. We extend the Wiener inversion method in several directions to obtain these results. Namely, we utilize mappings between more generalized Kato spaces to accommodate the more complicated resolvent expansions in both even and odd dimensions for the polyharmonic case
m>1
. This also allows us to control higher order derivatives of the resolvents and to develop the necessary spectral theory.

The literature on dispersive estimates for
m>1
has blossomed recently. First, local dispersive bounds were considered as maps between weighted
L^{2}
spaces. Feng, Soffer and Yao proved local dispersive bounds for fourth order Schrödinger operators, when
m=2
and
n=3
or
n>4
in
[
14
]
. The third author and Toprak considered global,
L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
estimates in the case of
n=4
, and the case of
n=3
with the first author,
[
19
,
13
]
. Global bounds were proven by Soffer, Wu and Yao when
n=1
,
[
24
]
, and by Li, Soffer and Yao when
n=2
,
[
21
]
.

Feng, Soffer, Wu and Yao proved local dispersive bounds when
n>2m
in
[
15
]
assuming that
|V(x)|\lesssim(1+|x|)^{-\delta}
for some
\delta>n
. Global dispersive bounds are established as a consequence of the first and third author’s work on the
L^{p}
boundedness of the wave operators provided
\delta>n+3
when
n
is odd,
[
11
]
, and
\delta>n+4
for
n
even,
[
12
]
. When
n\geq 4m
some measure of smoothness is needed to ensure global bounds hold, see
[
11
,
10
]
. In all cases, there is a substantial gap to the scaling-critical decay, which roughly corresponds to
\delta>2m
. We also note that while earlier results require bounded potentials, our results allow for local singularities and establish the uniform global bounds.

The paper is organized as follows. First, in Section
2
, we collect necessary facts about the higher order Schrödinger resolvent operators and Fourier transforms of related functions. In Section
3
we recall the framework for an operator-valued Wiener theorem that we wish to use to establish the dispersive estimates. In Section

which includes Theorem

as a special case. Finally, in Section

we prove Proposition
3.2
to ensure that the Wiener algebra machinery applies to the higher order Schrödinger operators and as a consequence establish facts about the spectral theory of

with

.

2.
Properties of the Resolvent

Our analysis relies on a careful analysis of resolvent operators. In this section we collect some needed facts about the resolvent operators and use them to prove results used in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Our work relies on understanding properties of the perturbed resolvents
\mathcal{R}_{V}^{\pm}(z)=((-\Delta)^{m}+V-z)^{-1}
where the ‘
+
’ and ‘
-
’, denote the limiting values of the resolvent as
z

[0,\infty)
from above and below respectively.

We have the splitting identity for

, (c.f.
[
15
]
)

(2)

\mathcal{R}_{0}(z)(x,y):=((-\Delta)^{m}-z)^{-1}(x,y)=\frac{1}{mz^{1-\frac{1}{m}}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\omega_{\ell}R_{0}(\omega_{\ell}z^{\frac{1}{m}})(x,y)

where

Using the change of variables

(3)

\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda^{2m})(x,y):=((-\Delta)^{m}-\lambda^{2m})^{-1}(x,y)=\frac{1}{m\lambda^{2m-2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\omega_{\ell}R_{0}(\omega_{\ell}\lambda^{2})(x,y).

By the well-known Bessel function expansions, for

(4)

R_{0}(z^{2})(x,y)=\frac{e^{iz|x-y|}}{|x-y|^{n-2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{n-3}{2}}c_{n,j}|x-y|^{j}z^{j},\qquad\Im(z)>0.

Even dimensions are more complicated due to the appearance of logarithmic terms. We need detailed information on the limiting resolvent operators
\mathcal{R}_{V}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})
as

In what follows we need to extend the resolvent to

\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}}(\lambda)(x,y)=\frac{1}{m\lambda^{2m-2}}\bigg[R_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2})(x,y)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\eta(\lambda|x-y|)\omega_{\ell}R_{0}(\omega_{\ell}\lambda^{2})(x,y)\bigg].

Proposition 2.1
.

In dimensions
2m<n<4m
, we have the representation

\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})(x,y)=\frac{G^{\pm}(\lambda|x-y|)}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}=\frac{e^{\pm i\lambda|x-y|}F^{\pm}(\lambda|x-y|)}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}.

When

G^{\pm},F^{\pm}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

|(F^{\pm})^{(N)}(r)|\lesssim\langle r\rangle^{\frac{n+1}{2}-2m-N}
,
|(G^{\pm})^{(N)}(r)|\lesssim\langle r\rangle^{\frac{n+1}{2}-2m}
,

In addition, the Taylor expansion of
G^{\pm}
at zero is of the form
a_{0}+c_{\pm}r^{n-2m}+\cdots

Moreover, when

When

G^{\pm}(r)=a_{0}+d_{0}^{\pm}r^{n-2m}+\cdots+d_{2m-2}^{\pm}r^{2m-2}+d_{2m}r^{2m}\log(|r|)+E^{\pm}(r),

Proof.

We drop the

This representation is a refinement of Lemmas 3.2 and 6.2

[

For odd

\displaystyle G(\lambda|x-y|)

\displaystyle=|x-y|^{n-2m}\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda^{2m})(x,y)=\frac{|x-y|^{n-2m}}{m\lambda^{2m-2}}\big[R_{0}(\lambda^{2})(x,y)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\omega_{\ell}R_{0}(\omega_{\ell}\lambda^{2})(x,y)\big]

Lemma 2.2
.

For any

if

if

is even, and any

Moreover, the Fourier transform is a finite measure if

Proof.

We first consider the case when

is odd. Note that for
|r|\lesssim 1
,
G^{(k)}(r)=r^{-\min(0,k+2m-n)}E_{k}(r),
for some

. Therefore, the Fourier transform of

r^{\gamma}G^{(k)}(r)\chi(r)

For

r^{\gamma}G^{(k)}(r)=e^{ir}\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{j,k}r^{\gamma}F^{(j)}(r).

By Proposition

\Big|\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{j,k}r^{\gamma}F^{(j)}(r)\Big)^{(N)}\Big|\lesssim\langle r\rangle^{\frac{n+1}{2}-2m-N+\gamma}.

When

When

When

By the splitting identity, (

Hence we may write

G(r)=\frac{C_{n}}{r^{2m-\frac{n+2}{2}}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\omega_{\ell}H^{(1)}_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\omega_{\ell}^{\frac{1}{2}}r)

for all

. By the exponential decay when

r^{\gamma}G_{0}^{(k)}(r)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{c_{n,k}}{r^{j-\frac{1}{2}}}e^{i(r-\frac{n+1}{4})}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi r}}\bigg(1+\mathcal{E}(r)\bigg)

For

Lemma 2.3
.

If
f
is
C^{1}
, supported on
\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)
, and
|f^{(j)}(r)|\lesssim|r|^{-j-\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
and
j=0,1
, then
\widehat{f}
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Furthermore, if
f
is continuous and compactly supported in
(-2,2)
and its distributional derivative is a function satisfying
|f^{\prime}(r)|\lesssim|r|^{-\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon<1
, then
\widehat{f}
is in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Since
f\in\dot{H}^{1}
,
\widehat{f}(\rho)=|\rho|^{-1}g(\rho)
, where
g\in L^{2}
. Therefore
\widehat{f}
is integrable on
|\rho|\gtrsim 1
. It remains to prove that
\widehat{f}
is integrable as
|\rho|\to 0
. Note that by an integration by parts (taking
\rho,r>0
)

|\widehat{f}(\rho)|=\Big|\int_{1}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}f(r)e^{-ir\rho}dr+\frac{1}{ie^{i}\rho}f(1/\rho)-\int_{\frac{1}{\rho}}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(r)\frac{e^{-ir\rho}}{-i\rho}dr\Big|

\lesssim\rho^{\epsilon-1}+\int_{1}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}r^{-\epsilon}dr+\int_{\frac{1}{\rho}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{1+\epsilon}\rho}dr\lesssim\rho^{\epsilon-1}.

This yields the first claim.

For the second claim, we first note that
\widehat{f}\in L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}
by the first assumption. If
\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, then
f\in H^{1}
and hence
\widehat{f}\in L^{1}
. We now assume that
\frac{1}{2}\leq\epsilon<1
. Fix

such that
1<p<\frac{1}{\epsilon}\leq 2
, then
f^{\prime}\in L^{p}
. By Hausdorff-Young, we have
\widehat{f^{\prime}}\in L^{p^{\prime}}
. Further, since
\widehat{f}
is also bounded,
\langle\xi\rangle\widehat{f}(\xi)\in L^{p^{\prime}}
. Now, we note

\int|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq\int|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\frac{\langle\xi\rangle}{\langle\xi\rangle}\,d\xi\lesssim\|\widehat{f}(\xi)\langle\xi\rangle\|_{p^{\prime}}\|\langle\xi\rangle^{-1}\|_{p}<\infty.

∎

We make some elementary observations about how the Kato norms are related.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\gamma
,

\frac{1}{|x-z|^{\beta}|z-y|^{\gamma+\alpha-\beta}}\lesssim\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\Big(\frac{1}{|x-z|^{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{|z-y|^{\gamma}}\Big).

As a consequence, for any
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq n-2m
and
V\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}
,

(6)

\Big\|V(\cdot)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{f(y)}{|\,\cdot\,-y|^{n-2m+\alpha-\beta}}dy\Big\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\beta}}\lesssim\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}}\|f\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}.

The first inequality can be verified by examining the regions where
|x-z|<\frac{1}{2}|x-y|
, where
|z-y|<\frac{1}{2}|x-y|
and where
|x-z|,|z-y|\geq\frac{1}{2}|x-y|
. The norm bound then follows by applying Fubini’s theorem and using the pointwise inequality to dominate
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{V(z)}{|x-z|^{\beta}|z-y|^{n-2m+\alpha-\beta}}dz
by
|x-y|^{-\alpha}
.

Our goal is to understand the perturbed resolvent operators
\mathcal{R}_{V}^{\pm}
. We do this by relating the perturbed resolvent to the free resolvents, specifically by applying the resolvent identity, we may write

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{V}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})

\displaystyle=(I+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})V)^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}.

To employ the Wiener inversion machinery for the operators
(I+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})V)
and
(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m}))
, we need another extension of
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})
to the real line in
\lambda
. We cannot use
\breve{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\lambda)
here since the spectral assumptions are not necessarily valid for
I+V\breve{\mathcal{R}}_{0}(\lambda)
when
\lambda<0
.
To this end, we extend the free resolvents as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{\pm}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\pm}(\lambda^{2m})&\lambda\geq 0\\
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\mp}((-\lambda)^{2m})&\lambda<0\end{array}\right.

With this extension the spectral assumptions of Theorem
1.2
can be used to show that
(I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{\pm}(\lambda))
and
(I+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{\pm}(\lambda)V)
are invertible for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. For notational convenience, we often omit the
\lambda
dependence of these operators as well as the
\pm
signs.
To keep the different extensions used clear, we also introduce the notation

(10)

M_{\ell}:=(I+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}V)^{-1},\quad\quad M_{r}:=(I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{-1}.

To establish the results in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we show that the inverse operators above are well-defined in an appropriate sense. The next section explores these operators in depth.

3.
An Abstract Wiener Theorem

The operator-valued Wiener inversion theorem we will use is an application of the
one proved in
[
5
]
. In particular,
I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{\pm}(\lambda)
is a function of
\lambda
taking values in
{\mathcal{B}}(L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, the set of bounded linear operators on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Its Fourier transform with respect to
\lambda
(and with
\rho
as the dual variable) will be integrable in the sense described below.

Given a Banach lattice
X
of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let the space
{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
consist of Borel measures
T
supported on
\mathbb{R}^{1+2n}
for which the marginal distribution of
|T|
, formally written as

M(T)(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|T(\rho,x,y)|\,d\rho

and more precisely defined by

M(T)(E):=|T|(\mathbb{R}\times E)\text{ for all Borel subsets $E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n}$,}

defines a bounded integral operator on
X
. The natural norm on this space is

\|T\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X}}:=\|M(T)\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(X)}.

With
\mathcal{M}
denoting the finite complex-valued measures on
\mathbb{R}
, we note that
elements of
{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
also act as bounded operators on
X_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
. Here
X_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
is the compound space of measures
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
M(x)=\|\nu(\cdot,x)\|_{\mathcal{M}}
is finite for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and belongs to the Banach space
X
. Elements of
{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
act on this space
through the formal convolution

TF(\rho,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}T(\rho-\sigma,x,y)F(\sigma,y)\,d\sigma dy.

This equips
{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
with the structure of a unital Banach Algebra whose identity element
\mathbbm{1}
is given by the measure

\delta_{\rho=0}\otimes\delta_{x=y}
.

One may define the Fourier transform of
T\in{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
with respect to the
\rho

variable by

\widehat{T}(\lambda,x,y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\lambda\rho}T(\rho,x,y)\,d\rho.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the kernel
\widehat{T}(\lambda,x,y)
is dominated pointwise by

M(T)
, hence
\|\widehat{T}(\lambda)\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(X)}\leq\|T\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X}}
.

Given two distinct Banach lattices
X
and
Y
of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
{\mathcal{U}}_{X,Y}
as in
[
5
]
to be the set of Borel measures
T
supported on
\mathbb{R}^{1+2n}
such that

M(T)
defines a bounded integral operator from
Y
to
X
. For the same reason as above,

\|\widehat{T}(\lambda)\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(Y,X)}\leq\|T\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X,Y}}
. It is straightforward to show that the expected algebraic relations,

(11)

\|ST\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X,Z}}\leq\|S\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X,Y}}\|T\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{Y,Z}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\widehat{ST})(\lambda)=\widehat{S}(\lambda)\widehat{T}(\lambda),

both hold in this degree of generality.
We also write
\widehat{T}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{X,Y}
if
T\in{\mathcal{U}}_{X,Y}
.

A key step in our proof of the dispersive bounds is to show that
(I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{\pm}(\lambda))^{-1}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{1}}
, that is it has a Fourier transform in
{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{1}}
. It follows by duality that

(I+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{\pm}(\lambda)V)^{-1}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty}}
.
Control of the operator inverses will follow from the following result once we verify that all the conditions are satisfied for
X=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
T=\mathcal{F}(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\cdot))
.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
5
, Proposition 3.1]

Suppose
T\in{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
is such that

a)

For some
N>0
,
\lim\limits_{\delta\to 0}\|T^{N}(\rho,x,y)-T^{N}(\rho-\delta,x,y)\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X}}=0
.

b)

\lim\limits_{R\to\infty}\|\chi_{|\rho|\geq R}T\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{X}}=0
.

If
I+\widehat{T}(\lambda)
is an invertible element of
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
for every

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\mathbbm{1}+T
is invertible in

{\mathcal{U}}_{X}
.

Our argument to handle the full range of cases
2m<n<4m
makes use of some intermediate Kato norms as defined in (
1
). We will need to verify that the conditions of Theorem
3.1
are also satisfied for
X={\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
,
0\leq\alpha\leq n-2m
. In particular, in Section
5
, we will prove

Proposition 3.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\|M_{r}\|_{\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}<\infty\text{ for $0\leq\alpha\leq n-2m$},\quad\text{and}\quad\|M_{\ell}\|_{\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty}}}<\infty,

where
M_{r}=(I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{-1}
and
M_{\ell}=(I+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}V)^{-1}
.

The proof of this proposition appears in Section
5
below.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we use Proposition
3.2
to prove Theorem
1.2
, from which Theorem
1.1
follows as a special case. By the well-known Stone formula, we may write:

(12)

\displaystyle|H|^{\beta-\frac{n}{2m}}e^{-itH}P_{ac}(H)f=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\lambda}\lambda^{\beta-\frac{n}{2m}}[\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}-\mathcal{R}_{V}^{-}](\lambda)f\,d\lambda.

Inserting a smooth, even cut-off function
\chi
with
\chi(y)=1
on
[-1,1]
and
\chi(y)=0
if
|y|\geq 2
, with
L\geq 1
along with the convenient change of variables

(13)

\displaystyle|H|^{\beta-\frac{n}{2m}}e^{-itH}P_{ac}(H)\chi(H/L)=\frac{m}{\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\lambda^{2m}}\lambda^{2m\beta-n+2m-1}\chi(\lambda/L)E(\lambda)\,d\lambda,

where

Our key observation is

Theorem 4.1
.

For any

,
a suitable extension of
\frac{\lambda^{k}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}E(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}

\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty},L^{1}}

Proof.

We start with the case

as

E(\lambda)=(I+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})V)^{-1}[\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}-\mathcal{R}_{0}^{-}](\lambda^{2m})(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{-}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}.

We extend the boundary operators to
\mathbb{R}
as in (
2
) introducing the operators
M_{r},M_{\ell}
, (
10
). By Proposition
3.2
, we have
M_{r}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{1}}
and
M_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty}}.

From the splitting identity, (
3
), since
\omega_{\ell}^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{2}\notin[0,\infty)
for

, we see that

\frac{[\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}-\mathcal{R}_{0}^{-}](\lambda^{2m})(x,y)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}=\frac{[R_{0}^{+}-R_{0}^{-}](\lambda^{2})(x,y)}{\lambda^{n-2}},

where
R_{0}^{\pm}
is the usual second order resolvent. By
[
17
]
in odd dimensions and Lemma 3.5 in
[
18
]

\frac{[R_{0}^{+}-R_{0}^{-}](\lambda^{2})(x,y)}{\lambda^{n-2}}=g(\lambda|x-y|)

for some

. (When
n
is odd

, while
g\in C^{\frac{n}{2}-1}
when

is even.) Moreover, for
|r|>1
, we can write

as a linear combination of
e^{\pm ir}f(r)
, with
|f^{(N)}(r)|\lesssim\langle r\rangle^{\frac{1-n}{2}-N}
. This implies that
\widehat{g}\in L^{1}
, and hence the Fourier transform of
\frac{[\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}-\mathcal{R}_{0}^{-}](\lambda^{2m})(x,y)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}
is in
{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty},L^{1}}
.
The claim for
k=0
now follows from (
11
).

Now we consider
\frac{\lambda\partial_{\lambda}E(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}=\frac{\lambda\partial_{\lambda}[\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}-\mathcal{R}_{V}^{-}](\lambda^{2m})}{\lambda^{n-2m}}
. We restrict our attention to the contribution of
\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}
, the argument for
\mathcal{R}_{V}^{-}
follows analogously.
By the resolvent identity, (
7
), we may write
\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}\,(\lambda^{2m})
in terms of the free resolvents as follows:

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})=(I+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})V)^{-1}\big[\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})\big](I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}

We extend the boundary operators to
\mathbb{R}
as above introducing
M_{r},M_{\ell}
, (
10
). The middle resolvent in the above expression must be handled in a different manner since it’s differentiablity to multiple orders at zero is crucial for our analysis.
We use the operator
\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)(x,y)
(see (
5
)) for this, and hence extend
\frac{\lambda\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})}{\lambda^{n-2m}}
to

M_{\ell}\frac{\lambda\partial_{\lambda}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}^{+}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}M_{r}=M_{\ell}\frac{G^{\prime}(\lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}{(\lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|)^{n-2m-1}}M_{r}.

By Lemma
2.2
with
\gamma=1+2m-n
, the Fourier transform of

is a finite measure, and hence the operator in the middle is in
\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty},L^{1}}
, which again suffices by (
11
).

For higher derivatives, we only consider the contribution of
\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}
, and we extend the operators to
\mathbb{R}
as above. The following identities are helpful for differentiating (
16

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}(I+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})V)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})V)^{-1}\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}V,

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}V\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})(I+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m}))^{-1}.

With that,
\frac{\lambda^{k}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}\mathcal{R}_{V}^{+}}{\lambda^{n-2m}}
can be extended to
\mathbb{R}
as a linear combination of the degenerate term,
M_{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{k}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}\breve{\mathcal{R}}_{0}^{+}}{\lambda^{n-2m}}M_{r}
where all derivatives act on the inner resolvent, and terms where at least one of
M_{\ell}
or
M_{r}
are differentiated which are of the form

\frac{\lambda^{k}}{\lambda^{n-2m}}M_{\ell}\big[\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{0}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)\big]\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}M_{r}V\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)\Big]M_{r},

where
k_{0},k_{i}
are all
\geq 1
and their sum is

.

Proposition
3.2
states that
M_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty}}
and
M_{r}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}
for

0\leq\alpha\leq n-2m
. Recall that
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})={\mathcal{K}}^{0}
. We will distribute the powers of
\lambda
into the product as follows.

M_{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{0}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{0}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}M_{r}V\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{i}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)\Big]M_{r},

where
\tilde{k}_{i}=\min(k_{i},\lfloor 2m-\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor)
for each

and
\tilde{k}_{0}=k-\sum_{i}\tilde{k}_{i}
.

Note that the kernel of
\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{i}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)
has the form

\frac{(\lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|)^{\tilde{k}_{i}}G^{(k_{i})}(\lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{n-2m-(k_{i}-\tilde{k}_{i})}}

2.2

function or finite measure since
\tilde{k}_{i}\leq\lfloor 2m-\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
and

\tilde{k}_{i}\geq k_{i}+2m-n

k_{i}\leq\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
ensures that the upper and lower bounds are compatible with one another.

As a consequence, Proposition
2.4
implies that
V\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{i}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)
belongs to
\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha_{i}},{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha_{i-1}}}
with
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}=k_{i}-\tilde{k}_{i}
provided
\alpha_{i-1}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{i}\leq n-2m
. Similarly, Proposition
3.2
gives us
M_{r}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha_{i}}}
for any
\alpha_{i}
in that range.

Start with
\alpha_{0}=0
so that
{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha_{0}}=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This gives a sequence

where the maximal value of

is achieved when

Putting all these pieces together with (

\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}M_{r}V\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{i}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)\Big]M_{r}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha_{I}},L^{1}}

with
\alpha_{I}=\sum_{i}(k_{i}-\tilde{k}_{i})
.

Now we consider the leftmost operator
M_{\ell}\frac{\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{0}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{0}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}
. Recall that
k_{0}=k-\sum_{i}k_{i}
and
\tilde{k}_{0}=k-\sum_{i}\tilde{k}_{i}

\tilde{k}_{0}-k_{0}=\sum_{i}(k_{i}-\tilde{k}_{i})=\alpha_{I}

\frac{\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{0}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{0}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}=\frac{G^{(k_{0})}(\lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}{(\lambda|x_{1}-x_{2}|)^{n-2m-\tilde{k}_{0}}}\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha_{I}}}.

\tilde{k_{0}}\leq k\leq\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor

\tilde{k}_{0}\geq k_{0}

\frac{\lambda^{\tilde{k}_{0}}\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{0}}\breve{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{n-2m}}\in\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty},{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha_{I}}}

Here
\gamma=2m+k-n=2m-\frac{n+1}{2}

applies to show that the Fourier transform is a finite measure when

and

suffices to prove the claim.

Remark 4.2
.

In the proof above, consider the case
k=\frac{n}{2}-1
when

is even. We claim that every term, except the degenerate one where no derivatives act on

or

, can handle one more derivative. Namely, that

\frac{\lambda^{k+1}}{\lambda^{n-2m}}\partial_{\lambda}\Big[M_{\ell}\big[\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{0}}\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda)\big]\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{I}M_{r}V\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda)\Big]M_{r}\Big]

has an extension to
\mathbb{R}
which is in
\mathcal{F}{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty},L^{1}}
provided that
I\geq 1
(nondegenerate),
k_{0},k_{i}
are all

and their sum is
k=\frac{n}{2}-1
. This is because the derivative will be a linear combination of products of similar form with at least two factors
\partial_{\lambda}^{k_{i}}\mathcal{R}_{0}
,
i=0,1,..,J
,
J\geq 1
, and with
\frac{n}{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{J}k_{i}

\widetilde{k}_{i}=\min(k_{i},2m-\frac{n+1}{2})
(as opposed to having the floor of the second term in the minimum). And let
\widetilde{k}_{0}=\frac{n}{2}-\sum\widetilde{k}_{i}\leq\frac{n-1}{2}
since each
\widetilde{k}_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and there is at least one of them. Therefore we can use Lemma
2.2
with
\gamma=\widetilde{k}_{0}+2m-n\leq 2m-\frac{n+1}{2}
. All other conditions are satisfied as in the proof above.

The following lemma will be useful in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
m\geq 1
and
-1<\alpha\leq m-1
, we have the bound

\bigg\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigg(e^{-it\lambda^{2m}}\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda>0}\lambda^{\alpha}\bigg)\bigg\|_{\infty}\lesssim|t|^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2m}}.

Here the inverse Fourier transform is understood in the sense of distributions.

Proof.

This follows by scaling from the fact that
e^{-i\lambda^{2m}}\lambda^{\alpha}\mathbbm{1}_{\lambda>0}
has a bounded Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}
provided that
m\geq 1
and
-1<\alpha\leq m-1
. To see this, first note that we can take
\lambda\gtrsim 1
and consider

\int e^{-i\lambda^{2m}+i\lambda\rho}\lambda^{\alpha}\chi(\lambda/2^{j})d\lambda,

where
\chi
is a smooth cutoff for
\lambda\approx 1
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

By van der Corput lemma, noting that the magnitude of the second derivative of the phase is
\approx 2^{j(2m-2)}
, we can bound the integral by

2^{-j(m-1)}\|\partial_{\lambda}[\lambda^{\alpha}\chi(\lambda/2^{j})]\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim 2^{-j(m-1-\alpha)}.

This implies the claim for
\alpha<m-1
by summing in

. When
\alpha=m-1
and
|\rho|\not\approx 2^{j(2m-1)}
, we instead use the nonstationary phase estimate

2^{j(\alpha-2m+1)}=2^{-jm}
, which allows us to sum in
j
for each fixed
\rho
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem

which includes Theorem
1.1
as a special case:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We divide the integral in (

that solves
\phi+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)\phi=0
. Without loss of generality, we take
\lambda\geq 0
and
\phi
solves
\phi+V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})\phi=0
by (
2
). If
\lambda<0
, the argument follows through by replacing
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}
with
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{-}
. This implies that
\psi:=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi
solves the equation
\psi+\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}V\psi=0
. By Proposition
2.1
, for each
\lambda
, the kernel of
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}
is pointwise dominated by the fractional integral operator
I_{2m}
.

We note that
I_{2m}
also maps
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
L^{2,\frac{n}{2}-2m-}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The mapping bound follows by duality noting that for
2m<n<4m

\sup_{y}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2n-4m}\langle x\rangle^{4m-n+}}dx\lesssim 1.

This means that
\psi=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi
belongs to
L^{2,\frac{n}{2}-2m-}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and is a distributional solution to
H\psi=\lambda\psi
, hence it is a resonance. The assumed lack of eigenvalues and resonances (when
\lambda=0
) prohibits such a
\psi
. For
\lambda>0
, we need to conclude that
\psi\in L^{2}
in order to rule it out by the assumed absence of embedded eigenvalues. We show this with an argument similar to the limiting absorption principle.

Note that since
V
is real-valued we have that
\langle\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi,V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi\rangle
is real-valued. Further, since
\phi=-V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi
, that makes
\langle\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi,V\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi\rangle=-\langle\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi,\phi\rangle
and causes the imaginary part of
\langle\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}\phi,\phi\rangle
to vanish.
Since we may also identify the imaginary part of
\langle\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2}m)\phi,\phi\rangle
as a multiple of
\int_{|\xi|=\lambda}|\widehat{\phi}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi
, this implies that
\widehat{\phi}
vanishes on
\lambda S^{n-1}
.

Also note that by factoring
|\xi|^{2m}-\lambda^{2m}
on the Fourier side, one has

(20)

\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda^{2m})\phi=R_{0}(\lambda^{2})\big[\prod_{\ell=1}^{m-1}R_{0}(\omega_{\ell}\lambda^{2})\big]\phi

The operator in brackets is Fourier multiplication by
(\lambda^{2m-2}+\lambda^{2m-4}|\xi|^{2}+\ldots+|\xi|^{2m-2})^{-1}
, which is real-analytic and behaves like
|\xi|^{-(2m-2)}
for large
\xi
. We can therefore bound the kernel of that operator by
\frac{e^{-c|x-y|}}{|x-y|^{n-2m+2}}
for some
c>0
.
This maps
L^{1}
to
L^{1}\cap L^{q}
for
q<\frac{n}{n-2m+2}
.
Furthermore,
\big[\prod_{\ell=1}^{m-1}R_{0}(\lambda^{2}\omega_{\ell})\big]\phi
still has vanishing Fourier transform on the sphere
\lambda S^{n-1}
. We observe that
\frac{2n}{n+4}<\frac{n}{n-2m+2}
when
2m<n<4m
, so that we may apply Theorem 2 in
[
16
]
to conclude that
\psi=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{+}(\lambda^{2m})\phi
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Now consider the general case
\phi\in{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
. Split
V=V_{1}+V_{2}
, where
V_{1}\in C_{0}
and
\|V_{2}\|_{\mathcal{K}}<\varepsilon
for a small
\varepsilon>0
to be determined in a moment.
Then
\phi+V_{2}\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda^{2m})\phi=-V_{1}\mathcal{R}_{0}(\lambda^{2m})\phi
. The pointwise bounds for the free resolvent in Proposition
2.1
allow an estimate

\|V_{1}\mathcal{R}_{0}\phi\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|V_{1}(x)|}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}|\phi(y)|dxdy\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\phi(y)|}{(1+|y|)^{n-2m}}dy<\infty.

The constant depends on the size and support of
V_{1}
, however what is important is that
V_{1}\mathcal{R}_{0}\phi\in L^{1}
. Then the
\alpha=0
case of Proposition
2.4
implies that for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,
I+V_{2}\mathcal{R}_{0}
will be a small perturbation of the identity in
{\mathcal{B}}(L^{1})
. Thus

\phi=-(I+V_{2}\mathcal{R}_{0})^{-1}V_{1}\mathcal{R}_{0}\phi\in L^{1},

which cannot occur without a resonance or eigenvalue if
\lambda=0
, or an embedded eigenvalue if
\lambda>0
, by the argument just above.
∎

The proof of Lemma
5.1
also yields the following corollary.

Corollary 5.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, the operator
H=(-\Delta)^{m}+V
has no resonances in
(0,\infty)
.

It remains to check that the operators
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})
satisfy the conditions a) and b) of Theorem
3.1
. Note that

(21)

[V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}](\lambda,x,y)=\frac{V(x)}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}e^{i\lambda|x-y|}\widetilde{F}(\lambda|x-y|),

where
\widetilde{F}(r)=F^{+}(r)
for
r\geq 0
and
F^{-}(-r)
for
r<0
. An application of Proposition
2.4
shows that that the integral kernel
\frac{V(x)}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}
defines a bounded operator on
{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha
in the range
0\leq\alpha\leq n-2m
.

We first prove that
\mathcal{F}\widetilde{F}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
when
2m<n<4m-1
, and that it is a finite measure (a Dirac-
\delta
plus an
L^{1}
function) when
n=4m-1
. Let
\chi
be a smooth cutoff for
[-1,1]
. Note that, by Proposition
2.1
, the compactly supported function
\widetilde{F}(r)\chi(r)
is continuous and its derivative is bounded, therefore the second claim in Lemma
2.3
applies. When
2m<n<4m-1
, the
j^{th}
derivative of the function
\widetilde{F}(r)(1-\chi(r))
decays at least as fast as
\langle r\rangle^{-1/2-j}
,
j=0,1
. Therefore, its Fourier transform is in
L^{1}
by the first claim in Lemma
2.3
.

The case
n=4m-1
also follows from Lemma
2.3
since
\widetilde{F}(r)-c
decays like
\frac{1}{|r|}
and derivatives decay faster as above. Therefore
\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{F})-c\delta_{0}\in L^{1}
. The lemma below completes the proof of Proposition
3.2
when
0\leq\alpha<n-2m
. The case of
\alpha=n-2m
will be deduced afterwards with a different argument.

Lemma 5.4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.1
, the operator
T:=\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}

satisfies conditions a) and b) in Theorem
3.1
with respect to spaces

{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}
provided
0\leq\alpha<n-2m
.

Proof.

We first consider the case
2m<n<4m-1
. By (
21
), we have

\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}(\rho,x,y)=\frac{V(x)}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\widehat{\widetilde{F}}\big(\frac{\rho}{|x-y|}-1\big)
. Integrating the absolute value with respect to
\rho
yields

M(\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}})(x,y)=\frac{|V(x)|}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}\|_{L^{1}}.

Then Proposition
2.4
with
\alpha=\beta
indicates that

\|M(\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}})\|_{B({\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha})}\lesssim\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}}\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}\|_{L^{1}}
for all
0\leq\alpha\leq n-2m
. In other words,

(22)

\|\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}\lesssim\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}}\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}\|_{L^{1}}.

We now check the conditions a, b in the statement of Theorem
3.1
when
2m<n<4m-1
.
By a similar calculation as above, we have (with
N=1
)

\|[\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}](\rho,x,y)-[\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}](\rho-\delta,x,y)\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}\leq\|V\|_{\mathcal{K}^{n-2m}}\big\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\cdot)-\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\cdot-\delta)\big\|_{L^{1}}\to 0

as
\delta
goes to zero by norm continuity in
L^{1}
. The condition b follows similarly. Take
V\in{\mathcal{K}}
, the
{\mathcal{K}}
-norm closure of compactly supported continuous functions,
C_{0}
. Using the bound (
22
), it suffices to prove the claim assuming that
V\in C_{0}
. We have

M(\chi_{|\rho|\geq R}\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}})(x,y)=\frac{|V(x)|}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}\int_{|\rho|>R/|x-y|}|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\rho-1)|d\rho.

Everywhere inside the domain
|\rho|>R/|x-y|
, at least one of
|\rho|
or
|x-y|
must be greater than
\sqrt{R}
. Thus

M(\chi_{|\rho|\geq R}\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}})(x,y)\leq\frac{|V(x)|}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}\int_{|\rho|>\sqrt{R}}|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\rho-1)|d\rho+\frac{|V(x)|\chi_{|x-y|>\sqrt{R}}}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}\|_{L^{1}}.

The first term on the right defines a bounded operator on
{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
by Proposition
2.4
, with norm controlled by
\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}}\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\rho-1)\|_{L^{1}(|\rho|>\sqrt{R})}
. To determine the
B({\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha})
norm of the second term, we observe that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|V(x)|\chi_{|x-y|>\sqrt{R}}}{|z-x|^{\alpha}|x-y|^{n-2m}}dx\leq R^{\frac{\alpha+2m-n}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|V(x)|}{|z-x|^{\alpha}|x-y|^{\alpha}}dx\lesssim\frac{R^{\frac{\alpha+2m-n}{2}}\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}{|z-y|^{\alpha}},

hence

\sup_{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|V(x)|\chi_{|x-y|>\sqrt{R}}}{|z-x|^{\alpha}|x-y|^{n-2m}}f(y)dxdy\lesssim R^{\frac{\alpha+2m-n}{2}}\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}\,\sup_{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|f(y)|}{|z-y|^{\alpha}}dy.

Combining the two terms yields

\|\chi_{|\rho|\geq R}\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{K^{\alpha}}}\lesssim\|V\|_{\mathcal{K}}\|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\rho-1)\|_{L^{1}(|\rho|>\sqrt{R})}+R^{\frac{\alpha+2m-n}{2}}\|V\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}},

which decreases to zero as
R\to\infty
since
\alpha<n-2m
.

We now turn to the case
n=4m-1
. It still suffices to verify conditions a and b for all potentials
V\in C_{0}
and use (
22
) to extend the property to
V\in{\mathcal{K}}
. The argument for condition b is largely unchanged when
n=4m-1
, even though
\widehat{\widetilde{F}}
now contains a point mass
c\delta_{0}
in addition to a function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. The essential properties in that argument were

\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{|\rho|>\sqrt{R}}|\widehat{\widetilde{F}}(\rho-1)|d\rho=0

and that
\widehat{\widetilde{F}}
has finite total mass, both of which are still true.

It remains to check condition a when
n=4m-1
assuming that
V\in C_{0}
. This condition does not hold for
N=1
because the total mass of measures
\delta(\,\cdot\,)-\delta(\,\cdot\,-\varepsilon)
remains large as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We will show that it holds for
N=3
instead.

We may assume that
V
is supported in the ball of radius
M
centered at the origin. It follows from the boundedness of
\widetilde{F}(r)
in (
21
), which is guaranteed by Proposition
2.1
, that
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda,x,y)
is dominated poinwise by
|x-y|^{2m-n}
uniformly in
\lambda
. It will be useful to note that for
\sigma\geq 0
,

\int_{|x|<M}\frac{\langle x\rangle^{2\sigma}}{|x-y|^{2n-4m}}\,dx\lesssim\frac{1}{\langle y\rangle^{2n-4m}}

with constants depending on
M
and
\sigma
. We are taking advantage of the fact that
4m>n
here. Then since
V
is a bounded function,

\|V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda,\,\cdot\,,y)\|_{L^{2,\sigma}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\langle y\rangle^{n-2m}}.

This estimate is sufficient to show that
\|V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2,\sigma})}\lesssim 1
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and any choice of
\sigma\geq 0
. For
|\lambda|\gtrsim 1
and larger
\sigma
, a stronger estimate is possible.

We refer back to the factorization of the free resolvent in (
20
). The operator in brackets is
\lambda^{2-2m}
times Fourier multiplication with a symbol
(1+(|\xi|/\lambda)^{2}+\ldots+(|\xi|/\lambda)^{2m-2})^{-1}
whose derivatives up to order
\sigma+1
are uniformly bounded. Hence this is a bounded operator on
L^{2,\sigma}
with norm controlled by
\lambda^{2-2m}
. It is a well-known property of the Schrödinger resolvent that
R_{0}(\lambda^{2})
maps
L^{2,\sigma}
to
L^{2,-\sigma}
with norm controlled by
\lambda^{-1}
provided
\sigma>\frac{1}{2}
. Multiplication by
V\in C_{0}
maps this back to
L^{2,\sigma}
. Put together, these estimates show that

\|V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)f\|_{L^{2,\sigma}}\lesssim\langle\lambda\rangle^{1-2m}\|f\|_{L^{2,\sigma}}.

Choose a fixed
\sigma>2m-\frac{n}{2}
. Pointwise domination of
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda,x,y)
by
|x-y|^{2m-n}
also shows that
V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\lambda)
maps
L^{2,\sigma}
to
{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
with a bound independent of
\lambda
. Applying all these mapping bounds in order shows that

(23)

\|(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}(\lambda,\,\cdot\,,y)\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}\lesssim\frac{\langle\lambda\rangle^{1-2m}}{\langle y\rangle^{n-2m}}.

Note that the difference of Fourier transforms can be written

\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho)-\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho-\delta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\rho\lambda}(1-e^{i\delta\lambda})(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}(\lambda)\,d\lambda.

Then (
23
) and the crude bound
|1-e^{i\delta\lambda}|\leq\delta|\lambda|
show that

\big\|\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho,\,\cdot\,,y)-\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho-\delta,\,\cdot\,,y)\big\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\langle y\rangle^{n-2m}}.

The
{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
norm is not changed by taking absolute values, so we can use the Minkowski inequality to bound

\Big\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{|\rho|>R}\big|\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho,\,\cdot\,,y)-\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho-\delta,\,\cdot\,,y)\big|d\rho\Big\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}\lesssim\frac{\delta R}{\langle y\rangle^{n-2m}}

for any
R<\infty
. We note that
\langle y\rangle^{2m-n}
belongs to the dual space of
{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}
for
0\leq\alpha\leq n-2m
. Thus

\Big\|\chi_{|\rho|>R}\big[\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho)-\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho-\delta)\big]\Big\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}\lesssim\delta R,

which vanishes as
\delta\to 0
. It follows that for any
R<\infty
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho-\delta)-\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho)\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\|\chi_{|\rho|>R}[\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho-\delta)-\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho)]\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\chi_{|\rho|>R}\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho)\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}.

We did not directly verify condition b) for the iterated operator
(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}
, but thanks to the convolution structure in
\rho
,

\|\chi_{|\rho|>R}\widehat{(V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{3}}(\rho)\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}\lesssim C(V)\|\chi_{|\rho|>R/3}\widehat{V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}(\rho)\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha}}}.

Taking
R\to\infty
and invoking condition b) for
V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}
completes the verification of condition a).
∎

Curiously, the edge case
\alpha=n-2m
cannot be proved as a direct application of Theorem
3.1
with any choice of
N>1
.
Instead we write

(I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{-1}=I-V(I+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}V)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}

We already proved that
(I+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}V)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty}}
. It follows from (
21
) and Lemma
2.2
with
\gamma=k=0
that for any
f\in{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}
we have (for some
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
)

\|\widehat{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}f\|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty}}}\leq\|g(\rho)\|_{L^{1}_{\rho}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|f(x)|}{|x-y|^{n-2m}}\,dx\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}}.

In particular, this shows that

\widehat{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{L^{\infty},{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}}
. Finally, multiplying by
V
on the left brings us from
L^{\infty}
back to
{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}
.
From here we may conclude that
(I+V\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{0}})^{-1}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathcal{K}}^{n-2m}}
. This completes the final case in the proof of Proposition
3.2
.
