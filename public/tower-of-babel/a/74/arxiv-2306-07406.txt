1.
Introduction

Throughout the paper, we use
f*g
to denote the convolution of
f
and
g
, and use
\widehat{f}
and
f^{\vee}
to denote the
Fourier transform
and
inverse Fourier transform
of
f
, respectively. Moreover, we will use
\mathcal{M}f
to denote the
centered Hardy-Littlewood maximal function
. Some special functions like
Bessel function
will be denoted by
J_{\alpha}(x)
, and the
Hypergeometric function
will be denoted by
{}_{p}F_{q}
.

1.1.
Fourier and inverse Fourier transform

Roughly speaking, a function is
Schwartz
if it is smooth and all of its derivatives decay faster than the reciprocal of any polynomial at infinity. For the precise definition we refer to
[
6
, Definition 2.2.1]
.

Given a Schwartz function
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\langle x,\,\xi\rangle}\mathrm{d}x,

where
\langle x,\,\xi\rangle

is the Euclidean inner product of
x
and
\xi
.
We call
\widehat{f}
the
Fourier transform
of
f
(
[
6
, Definition 2.2.8]
).

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define

f^{\vee}(x)=\widehat{f}(-x).

The operator
f\mapsto f^{\vee}
is called the
inverse Fourier transform
(
[
6
, Definition 2.2.13]
).

1.2.
The maximal truncated Riesz transform

To define the Riesz transform, we first introduce tempered distributions
\mathbf{W}_{j}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
1\leq j\leq d
, as follows: For
\varphi\in S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let

\langle\mathbf{W}_{j},\varphi\rangle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{|y|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{y_{j}}{|y|^{d+1}}\varphi(y)\mathrm{d}y.

The
j
th
Riesz transform
of
f
is given by the convolution with the distribution
\mathbf{W}_{j}
, that is,

R_{j}f(x)=(f*\mathbf{W}_{j})(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{|x-y|>t}\frac{x_{j}-y_{j}}{|x-y|^{d+1}}f(y)\mathrm{d}y,

for all
f\in S(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The above definition makes sense for any integrable function
f
that has the property that for all
x
there exist
C_{x}>0
,
\varepsilon_{x}>0
, and
\delta_{x}>0
such that
for
y
satisfying
|y-x|<\delta_{x}
, we have
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C_{x}|x-y|^{\varepsilon_{x}}
. Moreover, one can show the following
L^{p}
estimate:

\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}},\ \ \ 1<p<\infty,

for some positive constant
C
independent of
f
. Therefore, by the obvious density argument,
R_{j}
can be defined on
L^{p}
space (see
[
4
]
,
[
15
]
, and
[
21
]
).

The
truncated Riesz transform
, denoted by
R_{j}^{t}f
, is given as follows:

(1)

R_{j}^{t}f(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\int_{|x-y|>t}\frac{x_{j}-y_{j}}{|x-y|^{d+1}}f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

It is easy to see that
R_{j}f(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}R_{j}^{t}f(x)
.

The
maximal truncated Riesz transform
, denoted by
R_{j}^{*}f
, is the maximal function of the singular integral (
1
). Namely, for
j=1,\ldots,d
, we set

R_{j}^{*}f(x)=\sup_{t>0}\left|R_{j}^{t}f(x)\right|.

1.3.
Norm estimate of the maximal truncated Riesz transform

Clearly,
R_{j}^{*}f(x)\geq R_{j}f(x)
for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. On the other hand, Mateu and Verdera proved in
[
15
, Theorem 1]
that, up to a multiplicative constant, also the reverse inequality holds in the
L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
norm. More precisely, they showed that there exists a constant
C_{p,d}
depending on
p
and the dimension
d
, such that

(2)

\|R_{j}^{*}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p,d}\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}},

for all
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
1<p<\infty
.
The above norm estimate (
2
) was later generalized to much broader classes of singular integral operators with even kernels
[
14
]
and with odd kernels
[
13
]
.

In 2022, Kucharski and Wróbel showed in
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
that for the case
p=2
, one may take a universal constant in (
2
), i.e. the
L^{2}
norm of
R^{*}_{j}
is dimension-free. More precisely, they proved the following result: For every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\|R^{*}_{j}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2\cdot 10^{8}\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Very recently, they further improved the above estimate with Zienkiewicz, and obtain the dimension-free
L^{p}
estimate for the vector of
the maximal Riesz transforms of odd order in terms of the Riesz transforms (see
[
10
, Theorem 2.3]
) and then the same for all higher order Riesz transforms (see
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
).

1.4.
Two operators
M^{t}
and
M^{*}

Let
M^{t}
,
t>0
, be defined by (see
[
9
, (3.1) and (3.5)]
)

\widehat{M^{t}f}(\xi)=m(t|\xi|)\widehat{f}(\xi),\ \ \ f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ \ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

(3)

m(t|\xi|)=\frac{2^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{2\pi t|\xi|}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{d}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(r)\mathrm{d}r,

and set

M^{*}f(\xi)=\sup_{t>0}|M^{t}f(\xi)|.

Then for each
t>0
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the truncated Riesz transform factorizes as (
[
9
, (3.10)]
)

(4)

R^{t}_{j}f=M^{t}(R_{j}f),\ \ \ j=1,\ldots,d.

Moreover, the maximal operator
M^{*}
is bounded on all
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
spaces,
1<p<\infty
, and the optimal constant
C_{p}
in the inequality

(5)

\|R^{*}_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p}\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}

equals
\|M^{*}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}
(
[
9
, Corollary 3.3]
).

We will invoke (
4
) and (
5
) and the operators
M^{t}
and
M^{*}
in the rest of this paper. We remark here that in
[
10
, Theorem 2.3]
, Kucharski, Wróbel and Zienkiewicz showed there exists a constant
C>0
, such that
C_{p}\leq C\cdot(p^{*})^{2+1/2}
, where
p^{*}=\max\{p,(p-1)^{-1}\}
.

1.5.
Motivation

It would be interesting to investigate further the asymptotics of
C_{p}
in (
5
) and more accurate order of its magnitude. Estimating maximal operators is often a difficult task and the more usual approach is using Littlewood-Paley decomposition and finding estimates of pieces whose Fourier transforms are well localized. Kucharski and Wrobel obtained their
L^{2}
estimate using
M^{*}
and following the Lemma 3 from
[
2
]
with some subtle inequalities for Bessel functions.

Let us say that the exact norms of maximal operators are even more delicate problems and are known only in a very limited number of cases. For example, weak type estimate for the centered Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator on
\mathbb{R}
(see
[
16
]
) or
L^{p}
inequality for uncentered Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator on
\mathbb{R}
(see
[
7
]
).

In this paper, we first give a sharp estimate of the function
m
given by (
3
), and then, by using some identities for Bessel and hypergeometric functions, we find its inverse Fourier transform. This enables us to give the explicit formula of
M^{t}
. In fact, we split
M^{t}f
into the sum of two convolution integrals
\varphi_{1}^{t}*f+\varphi_{2}^{t}*f
, where the kernel of
\varphi_{1}^{t}
is supported away from the origin in
\{|x|\geq t\}
, while the kernel of
\varphi_{2}^{t}
is supported near the origin in
\{|x|<t\}
. After precise calculations, we use the methods developed in
[
1
]
. This approach gives better asymptotics for
C_{p}
at infinity and considerably shorter proof. However, we were not been able to prove the same for
1<p<2.

As an application of the obtained results, we show that
\|R_{j}^{t}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}
, for all
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
1<p<\infty
.
Moreover, using the same arguments developed in
[
1
]
but for the concrete positive and radial kernel, we find a better dimension-free
L^{p}
norm estimates for
R_{j}^{*}
in terms of
R_{j}
.

Our main results are given in the next subsection. First we will give the accurate calculations for
m
and its (inverse) Fourier transform, and then the
L^{p}
estimates for truncated Riesz transforms and maximal operator
M^{*}.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce two kind of special functions and recall two theorems which will be used in the proof of our main results. We start with the definition of the following Bessel function and hypergeometric function.

2.1.
Bessel functions

Assume that
\alpha
is a real number. The
Bessel function of first kind

J_{\alpha}(x)
is defined as follows (
[
3
, Page 200]
or
[
25
, Page 15]
):

(7)

J_{\alpha}(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)k!}\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{2k+\alpha}.

It follows from
[
3
, (4.7.5)-(4.7.6)]
that
J_{\alpha}(t)
also has the following integral form:

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{\alpha}}{2^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{its}(1-s^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}s

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{(t/2)^{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(t\cos\theta)\sin^{2\alpha}\theta\mathrm{d}\theta.

2.2.
Hypergeometric functions

The hypergeometric function

{}_{p}F_{q}[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{q};x]

is defined by the series (
[
3
, (2.1.2)]
):

(9)

{}_{p}F_{q}[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{q};x]=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}\cdots(a_{p})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}\cdots(b_{q})_{n}}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

for all
|x|<1
and by continuation elsewhere.

Here
(q)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol which is defined as follows

(q)_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{\ }l}1,&\mbox{if}\ \ \ n=0;\\
\\
q(q+1)\cdots(q+n-1),&\mbox{if}\ \ \ n>0.\end{array}\right.

Further we will use some formulas for hypergeometric and Bessel functions that can be found in
[
18
]
.

After making precise calculations, we will use the following general maximal estimate for radial multipliers:

Theorem 5
.

(
[
1
, Theorem 11 and Lemma 8]
)

Let
g
be a radial kernel in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, for
d\geq 4
and
p\geq 2
,

\left\|\sup_{t>0}|g_{t}*f|\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\left(C(2)+\sqrt{\frac{d-2}{d-3}}\right)^{2/p}\|g\|_{L^{1}}\|f\|_{L^{p}},

where
C(p)=p/(p-1).

3.1.

It follows from

\displaystyle m(x)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{2\pi x}r^{-\frac{d}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(r)\mathrm{d}r

Then, by letting

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sin^{d-1}\theta\frac{\sin(2\pi x\cos\theta)}{2\pi x\cos\theta}2\pi x\sin\theta\mathrm{d}\theta

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi x}\left(1-\left(\frac{t}{2\pi x}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{\sin t}{t}\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle I(x)

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
3

According to the Young’s inequality, we see from (
4
) and Theorem
2
that:

\|R^{t}_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}=\|M^{t}(R_{j}f)\|_{L^{p}}=\|\varphi_{t}\ast R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\varphi_{t}\|_{L^{1}}\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}=\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}},

where
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
1<p<\infty
.

Let
f_{n}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\displaystyle\|R_{j}^{t}f\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{j}^{t}f_{n}\|_{L^{p}}+\|R_{j}^{t}(f-f_{n})\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{j}f_{n}\|_{L^{p}}+\|R_{j}^{*}(f-f_{n})\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}+\|R_{j}(f-f_{n})\|_{L^{p}}+C_{p,d}\|R_{j}(f-f_{n})\|_{L^{p}},

where
C_{p,d}
is a constant depending on
p
and
d
(see (
2
)).
Letting
n\to\infty
, and noticing that
\|R_{j}(f-f_{n})\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{p}}
, we have

\|R_{j}^{t}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}},\ \ \ 1<p<\infty.

It follows from
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
that
\|R_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq H_{p}\|f\|_{L^{p}}
,
where

H_{p}=\begin{cases}\tan\frac{\pi}{2p},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<p\leq 2,\\
\cot\frac{\pi}{2p},\quad\text{for}\quad 2\leq p<\infty.\end{cases}

Therefore, one can easily get
\|R^{t}_{j}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq H_{p}\|f\|_{L^{p}}
, and thus,

\|R^{t}_{j}\|_{L^{p}}\leq H_{p}

for all
t>0
.

On the other hand, recall that

R^{t}_{j}f(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\int_{|x-y|>t}\frac{x_{j}-y_{j}}{|x-y|^{d+1}}f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

By substitution
x=tu
and

, we get

R^{t}_{j}f(tu)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{|u-z|>1}\frac{u_{j}-z_{j}}{|u-z|^{d+1}}f(tz)\mathrm{d}z=R^{1}_{j}f_{t}(u),

where
f_{t}(u):=f(tu).

Hence, the norm of
R^{t}_{j}
does not depend on
t
, and therefore, by
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}R^{t}_{j}f=R_{j}f
and Fatou’s lemma we have:

\|R^{t}_{j}\|_{L^{p}}\geq\left\|\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}R^{t}_{j}\right\|_{L^{p}}=\|R_{j}\|_{L^{p}}=H_{p}.

Based on the above discussions, we have

\|R^{t}_{j}\|_{L^{p}}=H_{p}

and the proof is completed. ∎

\displaystyle M^{t}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1+d}{2}\right)^{2}}{\pi^{1+\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{d}{2}\right)}\int_{|y-x|>t}\frac{t}{|y-x|^{1+d}}\ _{2}F_{1}\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1+d}{2};\frac{2+d}{2};\frac{t^{2}}{|y-x|^{2}}\right]f(y)\mathrm{d}y

m\left(\frac{t\rho}{2\pi|x|}\right)=\frac{2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi}\int_{\frac{t}{|x|}\rho}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\left(\cos\left(r-\frac{d+1}{4}\pi\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}r.

The second part of the above integral can be estimated as follows:

\int_{\frac{t}{|x|}\rho}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}O\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}r=O\left(\int_{\frac{t}{|x|}\rho}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{d+3}{2}}\mathrm{d}r\right)=O\left(\rho^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\right),\ \ \ \mbox{as}\ \ \rho\to\infty.

For the first part of the above integral, we can use integration by parts and obtain the following:

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{t}{|x|}\rho}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\cos\left(r-\frac{d+1}{4}\pi\right)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{t}{|x|}\rho\right)^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\sin\left(\frac{t}{|x|}\rho-\frac{d+1}{4}\pi\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\frac{d+1}{2}\int_{\frac{t}{|x|}\rho}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{d+3}{2}}\sin\left(r-\frac{d+1}{4}\pi\right)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=O\left(\rho^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\right),\ \ \ \mbox{as}\ \ \ \rho\to\infty.

Based on the above discussions, we have

(25)

m\left(\frac{t\rho}{2\pi|x|}\right)=O\left(\rho^{-\frac{1+d}{2}}\right),\ \ \ \mbox{as}\ \ \ \rho\to\infty.

Now, by using (
24
) and (
25
), one can easily obtain

\lim_{\rho\to\infty}m\left(\frac{t\rho}{2\pi|x|}\right)\rho^{\frac{d}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}}(\rho)=0,

which completes the proof. ∎

4.2.
Another computational proof of

Let

Then

.

Notice that
\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}}=\|\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}=\|\varphi_{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\|\varphi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}

According to the definition of hypergeometric functions, we see that

\varphi_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{1+d}\pi^{1+\frac{d}{2}}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(n+\frac{1+d}{2})}{\Gamma(n+1+\frac{d}{2})n!}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)^{2n},\ \mbox{for}\ |x|>1.

[

, by letting

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\pi^{1+\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(n+\frac{1+d}{2})}{\Gamma(n+1+\frac{d}{2})n!}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)^{2n}\mathrm{d}r

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{1}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+d}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\pi^{1+\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(n+\frac{1+d}{2})}{\Gamma(n+1+\frac{d}{2})n!(2n+1)}

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{1+d}{2})^{2}}{\pi\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})\Gamma(1+\frac{d}{2})}\ _{3}F_{2}\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1+d}{2};\frac{3}{2},1+\frac{d}{2};1\right].
