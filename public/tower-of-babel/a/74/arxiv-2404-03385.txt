Signed eigenvalue/vector distribution of complex order-three random tensor
Naoki Sasakura

Abstract
We compute the signed distribution of the eigenvalues/vectors of the complex order-three random tensor by computing a partition function of a four-fermi theory, where signs are from a Hessian determinant associated to each eigenvector. The issue of the presence of a continuous degeneracy of the eigenvectors is properly treated by a gauge-fixing. The final expression is compactly represented by a generating function, which has an expansion whose powers are the dimensions of the tensor index spaces. A crosscheck is performed by Monte Carlo simulations. By taking the large-​ N N limit we obtain a critical point where the behavior of the signed distribution qualitatively changes, and also the end of the signed distribution. The expected agreement of the end of the signed distribution with that of the genuine distribution provides a few applications, such as the largest eigenvalue, the geometric measure of entanglement, and the best rank-one approximation in the large-​ N N limit.

Quantum field theory expressions of distributions

In fact this strategy is widely applicable to various problems, so we will explain it in general forms in this section.
Suppose that there is a set of real equations
f_{i}(x,C)=0\ (i=1,2,\cdots,\bar{N})

It is also assumed that

In the present case of Section

The probability distribution of the solutions

\displaystyle\rho(x,C)=\sum_{k=1}^{\#{\rm sol}(C)}\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{N}}\delta\left(x_{i}-x^{(k)}_{i}(C)\right),

(11)

where

When

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\langle\rho(x,C)\rangle_{C},

Since

\displaystyle\rho(x,C)=\left|\det M(x,C)\right|\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{N}}\delta\left(f_{i}(x,C)\right),

\displaystyle M(x,C)_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_{j}(x,C)}{\partial x_{i}},\ (i,j=1,2,\ldots,\bar{N}).

The Jacobian factor with the absolute value in (

On the other hand, we can also consider a distribution without taking the

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{\rm signed}(x,C)&:=\det M(x,C)\prod_{i=1}^{\bar{N}}\delta\left(f_{i}(x,C)\right)\\

(15)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm signed}(x)=\left\langle\rho_{\rm signed}(x,C)\right\rangle_{C}.

(16)

There are a few reasons to make this signed distribution important, though it is different from the genuine distribution.
One is the technical reason that the signed distribution is much easier to compute.
Without taking the absolute value, the determinant factor can straightforwardly be represented by using a formula

\det M=\int d\bar{\psi}d\psi\,e^{\bar{\psi}M\psi}

Then, as we will see below, the signed distribution is expressed as a partition function

are essentially taking derivatives,

Another reason is that a signed distribution may coincide with a genuine distribution

N

though they are generally different in the other regions.
This can generally be expected in the case that an eigenvalue/vector equation is a stationary
point equation of a potential of an optimization problem.
In such a case, the matrix

is a Hessian on each stationary point of a potential, and it takes
a common signature, all positive (or negative), over most of the stationary points
in the vicinity of an end of a distribution, since
most of the stationary points near an end are stable (or maximally unstable). In particular
the ends of a signed and a genuine distributions should agree.
Since such an end represents the typical optimized value of an optimization problem,
a signed distribution can be used to compute it, though it is different from
the genuine distribution.

By considering the case that

6
Gauge invariant distribution

The signed distribution (

An obstacle for this purpose is that the Jacobian matrix

determining the quadratic terms of the fermions in (

\displaystyle{\rm Sign}\left(\det M(v,C,\beta)\right)={\rm Sign}\left(v_{(2)}\cdot v_{(2)}\right){\rm Sign}\left(v_{(3)}\cdot v_{(3)}\right)\,{\rm Sign}\left({\rm det}_{\neq 0}M(v,C,\beta=0)\right),

Therefore, by simply multiplying

The delta functions in (

Picking up the relevant part in (

In the large-​

The transverse part of (

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\perp}&=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\bar{\psi}_{(i)}\cdot\psi_{(i)}+\bar{\varphi}_{(i)}\cdot\varphi_{(i)}\right)+g\sum_{i,j=1,i>j}^{3}\left(\bar{\varphi}_{(i)}\psi_{(j)}+\bar{\varphi}_{(j)}\psi_{(i)}\right)\cdot\left(\bar{\psi}_{(i)}\varphi_{(j)}+\bar{\psi}_{(j)}\varphi_{(i)}\right)\\

The starting point of the SD method is to assume two-fermion expectation values as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle\bar{\psi}_{(i)a}\psi_{(i)}^{b}\rangle=Q^{i}_{1}\delta_{a}^{b},\\

&\langle\varphi_{(i)a}\psi_{(i)}^{b}\rangle=Q^{i}_{3}\delta_{a}^{b},\\
&\langle\varphi_{(i)a}\bar{\varphi}_{(i)}^{b}\rangle=Q^{i}_{4}\delta_{a}^{b},\\

9

9

9
Summary and future prospects

In this paper we have computed the signed eigenvalue/vector distribution of the complex order-three random tensor
by computing a partition function of a four-fermi theory. The final result
has a compact expression in terms of a generating function, which has an expansion
whose powers are the dimensions of the index spaces.
We have studied the large-​
N
limit both by the Schwinger-Dyson equation and by a saddle point analysis,
and have obtained the critical point where the behavior of the signed distribution qualitatively changes
and the end of the signed distribution in the limit.
Under plausible assumptions, we applied the location of the end to compute
the largest eigenvalue, the geometric measure of entanglement, and
the best rank-one approximation in the large-​
N
limit.

A new challenge compared to the previous cases was the presence of a continuous degeneracy of the solutions
to the eigenvalue/vector equations, which is generated by a two-dimensional Abelian symmetry.
To apply the procedure to rewrite the distribution as a partition function of a quantum field theory,
we modified the eigenvalue/vector equations so that they included the gauge fixing condition to remove the degeneracy,
and have extracted the gauge invariant distribution at the final stage.
This procedure we took, however, appears to be specific to this particular case.
It would be better to have a more systematic general procedure
as in the gauge fixing procedure in gauge theories.

The applications in Section
8
are based on the following two assumptions.
One is that the signed and the genuine distributions have a common end in the large-​
N
limit.
The other is that the distribution and the mean of the distribution
coincide in the large-​
N
limit. Both assumptions have been proven for the case of the real symmetric
random tensor
[
41
,
42
,
14
,
28
]
, but not for the complex random tensor to our knowledge.
We hope future study will support our results by proving these assumptions.

This paper has added another example which shows the powerfulness of the quantum field theoretical approach
to the eigenvalue/vector distribution. We hope we will extend it further to other various cases
which have not yet been covered. It is also interesting to extend it for the quantities other than means.
Computing the second moment is particularly interesting, since this proves the latter assumption
above
[
28
]
.

An identity

In this appendix we will prove an identity used in the text. Consider

&xH_{m}x=x_{i}(H_{m})^{ij}x_{j},\end{split}

(105)

Appendix E

In this appendix, to make this paper self-contained,
we explain the method using the SD equation.
The contents are rather standard, but would be useful for unfamiliar readers.

The general form of a four-fermi theory is given by

\displaystyle S=\bar{\psi}_{a}K_{ab}\psi_{b}+gJ_{abcd}\bar{\psi}_{a}\bar{\psi}_{b}\psi_{c}\psi_{d},

(129)

where
g
is a coupling,
J,K
are constants, and
J
satisfies

\displaystyle J_{abcd}=-J_{bacd}=-J_{abdc},

(130)

to be consistent with the anti-commutativity of the fermions.
The partition function of the system is defined by

\displaystyle Z=\int d\bar{\psi}d\psi\,e^{S},

(131)

where the integration measure

is defined so that
[
43

\displaystyle\int d\bar{\psi}d\psi\,e^{\bar{\psi}_{a}K_{ab}\psi_{b}}=\det K.

(132)

The system is invariant under a scaling transformation,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{a}^{\prime}=s\bar{\psi}_{a},\ \psi_{a}^{\prime}=s^{-1}\psi_{a},

(133)

where
s
is arbitrary.

The fermion integration vanishes when the integrand is a total derivative. So, we have an identity,

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\int d\bar{\psi}d\psi\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\psi}_{a}}(\bar{\psi}_{b}\,e^{S})\\

(134)

By dividing this by
Z
, we obtain a Schwinger-Dyson equation,

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{ac}\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\phi_{c}\rangle+2gJ_{acde}\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{d}\psi_{e}\rangle=\delta_{ab},\end{split}

(135)

where correlation functions are defined by

\displaystyle\langle{\cal O}\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\int d\bar{\psi}d\psi\,{\cal O}e^{S}.

(136)

The four-fermion correlation function in (
135
) may be expressed in terms of connected
two-fermion

Since one-fermion correlation functions of fermions vanish, any two-fermion correlation functions are connected.

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{d}\psi_{e}\rangle=-\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\psi_{d}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{e}\rangle+\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\psi_{e}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{d}\rangle+\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{d}\psi_{e}\rangle_{c},

(137)

137
) lead to an equation for the two-fermion correlation functions,

\displaystyle K_{ac}\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\phi_{c}\rangle+4gJ_{acde}\langle\bar{\psi}_{b}\psi_{e}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{d}\rangle=\delta_{ab}.

(138)

In fact, the equation (
138
) can be written in another way. Let us denote
G_{ab}=\langle\bar{\psi}_{a}\psi_{b}\rangle
.
Then, by applying
G^{-1}
on both sides of (
138
), we obtain

\displaystyle K_{ab}+4gJ_{acdb}G_{cd}=G^{-1}_{ba}.

(139)

Once a solution of (
139
) for
G
is obtained, the partition function
Z
can be computed
in the following manner. We first note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dg}\log Z&=J_{abcd}\langle\bar{\psi}_{a}\bar{\psi}_{b}\psi_{c}\psi_{d}\rangle\\
&=2J_{abcd}G_{ad}G_{bc}\end{split}

(140)

by applying the same approximation as above expressing the four-fermion correlation functions in terms of two-fermion ones.
This first-order differential equation can be solved with the initial condition,

\displaystyle Z(g=0)=\det K.

(141)

The procedure above can be summarized as a more intuitive process.
Let us define

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\rm eff}&=\langle S\rangle-\log\det G\\
&=K_{ab}G_{ab}+2gJ_{abcd}G_{ad}G_{bc}-\log\det G,\end{split}

(142)

where
\langle S\rangle
in (
142
) has been computed with the similar approximation above.
In this expression, depending on the sign of
\det G
, the term
\log\det G
may be replaced by
\log(-\det G)
.

The stationary condition of
S_{\rm eff}
with respect to
G
leads to (
139
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\frac{\partial}{\partial G_{ab}}S_{\rm eff}\\
&=K_{ab}+4gJ_{acdb}G_{cd}-G^{-1}_{ba}.\end{split}

(143)

Moreover, let us define

\displaystyle Z^{0}_{\rm eff}=\det K\,e^{S^{0}_{\rm eff}-S_{\rm eff}^{0}(g=0)},

(144)

where
S^{0}_{\rm eff}
is the value of
S_{\rm eff}
for the solution
G=G^{0}
of (
143
), and

S_{\rm eff}^{0}(g=0)
is that for
g=0
. Then
Z^{0}_{\rm eff}
gives the partition function in this approximation.
This is because, by using the stationary condition (
143
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dg}\log Z^{0}_{\rm eff}=\frac{\partial S^{0}_{\rm eff}}{\partial g}+\frac{\partial S^{0}_{\rm eff}}{\partial G^{0}_{ab}}\frac{dG^{0}_{ab}}{dg}=2J_{abcd}G^{0}_{ad}G^{0}_{bc},

(145)

which agrees with (
140
), and
Z^{0}_{\rm eff}(g=0)=\det K
.

Appendix F
Stationary conditions of
S_{\rm eff}

By taking
\partial S_{\rm eff}/\partial Q^{i}_{j}=0
one obtains

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-1+\sum_{j\neq i}Q^{j}_{1}g_{j}-\frac{Q^{i}_{1}}{\det Q_{(i)}}=0,\\
&\sum_{j\neq i}Q^{j}_{2}g_{j}-\frac{Q^{i}_{2}}{\det Q_{(i)}}=0,\\
&\sum_{j\neq i}Q^{j}_{3}g_{j}-\frac{Q^{i}_{3}}{\det Q_{(i)}}=0,\\
&1+\sum_{j\neq i}Q^{j}_{4}g_{j}-\frac{Q^{i}_{4}}{\det Q_{(i)}}=0,\end{split}

(146)

where
g_{i}=N_{i}g
. By defining new parameters,
A_{i}=(\det Q_{(i)})^{-1}
and
B_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}Q_{j}^{i}g_{i}
, one can
solve (
146
) as

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{i}=\frac{B_{1}-1}{g_{i}+A_{i}},\ Q_{2}^{i}=\frac{B_{2}}{g_{i}+A_{i}},\ Q_{3}^{i}=\frac{B_{3}}{g_{i}+A_{i}},\ Q_{4}^{i}=\frac{B_{4}+1}{g_{i}+A_{i}},

(147)

with consistency conditions,

\displaystyle\det Q_{(i)}=\frac{(-1+B_{1})(1+B_{4})-B_{2}B_{3}}{(g_{i}+A_{i})^{2}}=\frac{1}{A_{i}},

(148)

\displaystyle(-1+B_{1})p=B_{1},\ B_{2}p=B_{2},\ B_{3}p=B_{3},\ (1+B_{4})p=B_{4},

(149)

where

\displaystyle p=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{g_{i}}{g_{i}+A_{i}}.

(150)

From the equations (
149
), we immediately obtain
p\neq 1
,
and so
B_{2}=B_{3}=0
and
B_{1}=-B_{4}=p/(p-1)
. Putting these into (
148
), we obtain

\displaystyle A_{i}+(p-1)^{2}(g_{i}+A_{i})^{2}=0.

(151)

Let us summarize: For given
g,N_{i}
(note that
g_{i}=gN_{i}
), the four equations in (
150
) and (
151
) determine
the four variables
p
and
A_{i}
, and therefore all
Q_{j}^{i}
.

The quadratic equations in (
151
) have two solutions for
A_{i}
. Which solution should be taken for small
g

can be determined by the following argument. When
g\rightarrow 0
, the four-fermi interactions vanish, and therefore

Q_{1}^{i}=-Q_{4}^{i}=1,Q_{2}^{i}=Q^{i}_{3}=0
. This is satisfied by the solution,

\displaystyle A_{i}=\frac{-1-2g_{i}(p-1)^{2}-\sqrt{1+4g_{i}(p-1)^{2}}}{2(p-1)^{2}}.

(152)

Putting these into (
150
) and reparameterize
p=1-q
, we obtain (
58
).
