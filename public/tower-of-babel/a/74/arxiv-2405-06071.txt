Exact solution for heat transfer across the Sakiadis boundary layer
W. Cade Reinberger, Nathaniel S. Barlow, Mohamed A. Samaha, Steven J. Weinstein

Abstract
We consider the problem of convective heat transfer across the laminar boundary-layer induced by an isothermal moving surface in a Newtonian fluid. In previous work (Barlow, Reinberger, and Weinstein, 2024, Physics of Fluids , 36 (031703), 1-3) an exact power series solution was provided for the hydrodynamic flow, often referred to as the Sakiadis boundary layer. Here, we utilize this expression to develop an exact solution for the associated thermal boundary layer as characterized by the Prandtl number ( Pr \operatorname{Pr} ) and local Reynolds number along the surface. To extract the location-dependent heat-transfer coefficient (expressed in dimensionless form as the Nusselt number), the dimensionless temperature gradient at the wall is required; this gradient is solely a function of Pr \operatorname{Pr} , and is expressed as an integral of the exact boundary layer flow solution. We find that the exact solution for the temperature gradient is computationally unstable at large Pr \operatorname{Pr} , and a large Pr \operatorname{Pr} expansion for the temperature gradient is obtained using Laplace’s method. A composite solution is obtained that is accurate to O ⁡ ( 10 − 10 ) O(10^{-10}) . Although divergent, the classical power series solution for the Sakiadis boundary layer—expanded about the wall—may be used to obtain all higher-order corrections in the asymptotic expansion. We show that this result is connected to the physics of large Prandtl number flows where the thickness of the hydrodynamic boundary layer is much larger than that of the thermal boundary layer. The present model is valid for all Prandtl numbers and attractive for ease of use.

Introduction

The laminar boundary layer flow induced by a moving flat surface through an incompressible fluid has significant relevance to coating flows, and forms the basis for many studies involving moving elastic sheets with simultaneous heat and mass transport. The boundary layer flow was first analyzed in 1961 by

III
The equivalence of Laplace’s Method for
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
and approximations employing polynomial velocity profiles

Significant physical insights and mathematical simplifications may be obtained by considering an engineering approach to solving for
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
for large
\operatorname{Pr}
. In Section 3.1, we follow the Polhausen approach of assuming a polynomial profile for the dimensionless stream function
f
in the evaluation of
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
in (
8
). We examine the implications of these results to the evaluation of
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
in Section 3.2 via Laplace’s method, and propose an alternative simple method to do so.

III.1
Determining
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
via low order polynomial expression for velocity

At large
\operatorname{Pr}
, the thickness of the thermal boundary layer (
\delta_{t}
) is thinner than that of the hydrodynamic boundary layer (
\delta
); see Figure 1 for a definition sketch. Consequently, the thermal boundary layer is confined in a zone where the velocity,
u_{x}=U_{w}f^{\prime}(\eta)
, is linear with the dimensionless distance from the wall
\eta
. A similar assumption was first introduced by
Lévêque [41]
for the Blasius boundary-layer flow and can be considered as analogous to the polynomial profiles used in the Polhausen approximation for the wall shear (although as we will show, the boundary conditions used in the classical Polhausen approximation must be modified from analogy with the Polhausen approximation in order to obtain the correct asymptotics for large
\operatorname{Pr}
flows).

The stream function
f
is parabolic for a linear velocity approximation; the constraints in (
1b
) at
\eta=0
as well as
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\kappa
are applied to yield

f=\kappa\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}+\eta,\hphantom{\sum}\text{ for }\eta\leq\eta_{t}

(16a)

where
\eta_{t}
is a representation of the nondimensionalized distance to the edge of the boundary. Note that the locus of points,
\eta=\eta_{t}
, maps out the location of the thermal boundary layer,
\delta_{t}
, when mapped back to physical
x
-
y
coordinates, and is given by

y=\delta_{t}=\eta_{t}\sqrt{\dfrac{\nu x}{U_{w}}}.

(16b)

Substituting (
16a
) in the Pohlhausen ODE, (
2a
), gives

\dfrac{\theta^{\prime\prime}}{\theta^{\prime}}=-\operatorname{Pr}\left(\kappa\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{4}+\dfrac{\eta}{2}\right),\hphantom{\sum}\text{ for }\eta<\eta_{t}.

(17)

Integrating (
17
) and employing the boundary conditions, Eqs. (
2b
), gives

\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}(0)\exp\left(-\operatorname{Pr}\left(\kappa\dfrac{\eta^{3}}{12}+\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{4}\right)\right),\hphantom{\sum}\text{ for }\eta<\eta_{t}.

(18a)

Note that
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
is an unknown quantity at this point, so is in fact an integration constant. At the edge of the thermal boundary layer (
\eta=\eta_{t}
), we expect
\theta^{\prime}
to be zero. However, the functional form of (
18a
) does not allow that choice. Instead, we choose the minimum of
\theta^{\prime}
to be zero at the edge of the boundary layer, i.e.
\theta^{\prime\prime}=0
, and thus from (
17
) we have

\eta_{t}=-\dfrac{2}{\kappa}.

(18b)

In order to find
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
, (
18a
) is integrated across the boundary layer to yield

\theta(\eta_{t})-\theta(0)=\theta^{\prime}(0)\int_{0}^{\eta_{t}}\exp\left(-\operatorname{Pr}\left(\kappa\dfrac{\eta^{3}}{12}+\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{4}\right)\right)\text{d}\eta.

(19)

Consistent with the approximations made thus far, we assume that
\theta(\eta_{t})=1
, which is the infinite boundary condition in (
2b
) located at the boundary layer edge. Using this constraint we obtain

{\theta^{\prime}(0)}=\left[\int_{0}^{\eta_{t}}\exp\left(-\operatorname{Pr}\left(\kappa\dfrac{\eta^{3}}{12}+\dfrac{\eta^{2}}{4}\right)\right)\text{d}\eta\right]^{-1}.

(20)

Let us now summarize the approximations embedded in (
20
). First, we have assumed a velocity profile to obtain
f
. Second, we used the fact that
\theta^{\prime\prime}
is zero at the edge of the boundary layer, but
\theta^{\prime}(\eta)\neq 0
there. Finally, we have displaced the boundary condition on theta from infinity down to
\eta=\eta_{t}
. Considering the approximations made at the edge of the boundary layer, there is no reason to evaluate (
20
) exactly. Instead, we utilize Laplace’s method on (
20
), which uses the fact that the maximum of the argument of the exponential occurs at
\eta=0
, and this dominates the behavior of the integral as
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
. An intuitive advantage of this method is that the integral is “weighted" away from the most approximated location at
\eta=\eta_{t}
. Laplace’s method may be implemented by expanding

\exp\left(-\operatorname{Pr}\left(\kappa\dfrac{\eta^{3}}{12}\right)\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-\kappa/12)^{n}}{n!}\operatorname{Pr}^{n}\eta^{3n},

in (
20
) and moving the upper limit of the integral to infinity; this step only incurs asymptotically small errors. The resulting expression
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}
can be expressed in terms of particular values of the Gamma function as

{\theta^{\prime}(0)}\approx\left[\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\dfrac{3n+1}{2}\right)}{n!}\left(\dfrac{-2\kappa}{3}\right)^{n}\operatorname{Pr}^{-(n+1)/2}\right]^{-1}.

(21)

Remarkably, the first three terms of (
21
) are identical to the correct asymptotic expansion of
{\theta^{\prime}(0)}^{-1}
in the
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
limit given by (
14
). Recall that this result utilized the exact solution for
f
coupled with Laplace’s method. In fact, in the next Section, we show that the full Laplace method expansion may be obtained via a mathematical approach motivated by this observation.

III.2
Full Laplace Expansion as
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
using Divergent Infinite Series Solution

The fact that a low-order polynomial approximation was used to recover the first few terms in the asymptotic expansion (
13
) motivates the examination of higher-order polynomials to generate higher-order corrections. We now discuss such an analysis. In Appendix A.1, Laplace’s method is used to determine the large
\operatorname{Pr}
behavior of the integral (
8
); this integral is embedded in the desired Nusselt number expression (
5
) by (
7
). Laplace’s method is based on the exponential character of the integrand in the large
\operatorname{Pr}
limit. As
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
, the magnitude of the integrand becomes concentrated near the location
u=1
, which corresponds to the maximum of the exponential argument. This justifies the use of the Taylor series about
u=1
in the method (see (
22a
)) and in subsequent simplifications. We recall here that
u=\exp(-C\eta/2)
(see text just above (
7
), or equivalently Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
)), and thus the limit as
u\to 1
corresponds to that of
\eta\to 0
in (
1
). Thus, we hypothesize—and will go on to demonstrate—that the full Taylor series expansion of the solution in
\eta
about
\eta=0
may be used directly to obtain the same large Pr expansion (
13
) and coefficients in Appendix B.

The power series solution of the Sakiadis problem (
1
) about
\eta=0
is well established, and in fact, the recursion for the coefficients was first given by
Blasius [3]
for flow over a stationary plate. That solution was modified slightly by
Sakiadis [2]
to satisfy the different boundary conditions in the Sakiadis flow, and is given in Appendix C by (
43
). The solution has a finite radius of convergence,
R
—the solution converges only when
|\eta|<R
, where
R\approx 4.0722
, and is divergent for larger
\eta
values. Hence, the series solution cannot bridge the semi-infinite domain in the Sakiadis problem (
1
), and is thus deficient. It is for precisely this reason that
Naghshineh et al. [31]
developed the alternative convergent expansion given by (
3
). Nevertheless, in Appendix C, we show that, in fact, the divergent infinite series expansion (
43
) may be used to extract the
full
asymptotic expansion (
13
) with identical coefficients shown in Appendix A. The methodology using the divergent power series solution takes less steps (compare work in Appendices B and C) and satisfies a slightly simpler recurrence relation than that using the exact convergent solution.

Thus, we call out this interesting element, namely that it is not necessary to have a convergent solution for the full flow field to obtain the full
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
asymptotic behavior. Only the flow field near the wall needs to be represented accurately, and the series divergence away from the wall does not impact Laplace’s method—as the method focuses on the dominant behavior of the solution as
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
near the wall. The fact that the divergent series expansion may be used in the
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
limit is a physically appealing result, since the characteristic thickness of the thermal boundary layer is small compared with that of the hydrodynamic boundary layer for large
\operatorname{Pr}
. This is precisely as discussed in the approximate analysis shown in Section (3.1). In Section 2, recall that we mentioned it was surprising that the asymptotic constant
C
, where
f\sim C
as
\eta\to\infty
, did not appear in the
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
asymptotic behavior when the convergent expansion (
3
) was employed. We see now why that is the case—the use of the divergent expansion makes clear that it is only the behavior of the flow solution near the wall that matters to the asymptotic solution, and thus, only the shear parameter
\kappa
appears in the asymptotic coefficients (see Appendix B).

Finally, we comment on the use of polynomial approximations to the velocity field used to predict convective heat transfer coefficients in boundary layer flows. In order to reproduce the correct Laplace’s method expansion as
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
, it is necessary that a power series be generated as an expansion about the wall (
\eta=0
) in accordance with the differential equation itself, as the power series solution to (1). It is commonplace for zero slope, curvature, etc. to be applied at the edge of the boundary layer when using a Polhausen approximation to predict wall shear stresses; see, for example
Schlichting [33]
(page 206). However, one cannot utilize such an approach when coupling the polynomial approximation with a thermal boundary layer problem if one wants to recover the correct asymptotic behavior as
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
. It is only if the power series is generated algorithmically as described above, using information at the wall for higher derivatives, that the correct polynomials can be generated with a finite number of terms.

III.3
Importance of the wall shear stress

The presented exact and approximate models in Sections

are the closest to those predicted by the present analytical model (with an error as low as

) as indicated in Figure

[

IV
Conclusions

We have utilized a known exact solution for the Sakiadis boundary layer to develop a similarly exact solution for the associated thermal boundary layer. Although exact, we find that the solution for the temperature gradient at the wall, needed to extract the convective heat transfer coefficient, is computationally unstable at large
\operatorname{Pr}
. To compensate, we developed a large
\operatorname{Pr}
expansion for the temperature gradient using Laplace’s method. By combining the exact and asymptotic solutions, an analytical expression for the temperature gradient is obtained that is accurate to
O(10^{-10})
for all
\operatorname{Pr}
when compared with machine-precision numerical results. The large
\operatorname{Pr}
asymptotic analysis shows that a divergent power series solution for the fluid flow—that is itself expanded about the location of the moving surface—may be used instead of the convergent power series solution to obtain all higher order coefficients in the asymptotic expansion. We show that this result is connected to the physics of large Prandtl number flows where the thickness of the hydrodynamic boundary layer is much larger than that of the thermal boundary layer. The latter approach must be used when trying to estimate the wall shear stress using the Polhausen approximation, but is not the appropriate approximation to use for the heat transfer asymptotics. Additionally, to remain consistent with the
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
asymptotic behavior, we have shown that any higher-order constraints on an assumed polynomial velocity field used to approximate heat transfer must be consistent with the boundary-layer equations evaluated at the wall, as opposed to imposing higher-order constraints at the edge of the boundary layer itself. We also demonstrate that a divergent infinite power series expanded about the wall location may be used in place of an exact convergent series expansion to examine the large
\operatorname{Pr}
limit via Laplace’s method. By the well-known analogy between heat and mass transfer in boundary layer flows, the results of our study may also be used to describe the convective mass transport of a dilute solute into the fluid from the wall. The present model is highly accurate and attractive for ease of use in engineering applications.

Cuq^{\prime}(u)+\tilde{a}_{0}
has the same Taylor expansion as
g
, and hence
Cuq^{\prime}(u)+\tilde{a}_{0}=g(u)
. Since
\tilde{a}_{0}=C
this differential equation for
q
can be integrated to yield

q(u)=q(1)+\int_{1}^{u}\frac{g(\tau)-C}{C\tau}\ \text{d}\tau.

(33)

At this point we can evaluate the integral (
33
) using the Taylor expansion of
g(u)
about
u=1
. This is also derived by Naghshineh et al.
[
31
]
as

g(u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{a}_{n}(u-1)^{n}

(34a)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{a}_{n+3}&=-\frac{2n+3}{n+3}\hat{a}_{n+2}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{(n+2)(n+3)}\hat{a}_{n+1}\\
&+\frac{1}{C(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}\biggl\{\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(k+1)(k+2)\hat{a}_{k+2}\hat{a}_{n-k-1}\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+1)\left[(k+2)\hat{a}_{k+2}+\hat{a}_{k+1}\right]\hat{a}_{n-k}\biggr\},\\
\hat{a}_{0}&=0,\hphantom{\sum}\hat{a}_{1}=-\frac{2}{C},\hphantom{\sum}\hat{a}_{2}=\frac{2\kappa}{C^{2}}+\frac{1}{C}.\end{split}

(34b)

We can thus use standard series manipulations of the integrand to derive the Taylor coefficients of
q(u)
about
u=1
; this provides us with a method to compute the desired coefficients
\phi_{k}
in (
11b
) as follows. For the first term,
q(1)=\phi(1)=\phi_{0}
in (
11b
); we cannot compute this value without summing an infinite series (although, as stated before, it converges to double precision in
35
terms). However, its value is not needed as it does not appear in (
14
) after cancellation of the exponential that appears in the reciprocal form (
13
). All of the higher order terms, though, can be computed by Taylor expanding
g(\tau)-C
about
\tau=1
in the integrand, expanding the
\tau^{-1}
as a geometric series in
\tau-1
, computing the Cauchy product of these series (which simply gives the Taylor expansion of the product of two functions with known expansions), and integrating term-by-term. In particular, if we first write

\frac{g(\tau)-\tau}{C}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{\hat{a}}(\tau-1)^{n},

(35)

we then have
\hat{\hat{a}}_{0}=-1
and
\hat{\hat{a}}_{n}=\hat{a}_{n}/C
. If we further write

\frac{g(\tau)-C}{C\tau}=\frac{g(\tau)-C}{C(1+(\tau-1))}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}(\tau-1)^{n},

(36)

we can simply compute the series product of the sum of the
\hat{\hat{a}}_{n}
and the geometric series from the denominator as

\tilde{\alpha}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\hat{\hat{a}}_{k}(-1)^{n-k}.

(37)

We subsequently integrate this result term-by-term to obtain

q(u)=q(1)+\int_{1}^{u}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}(\tau-1)^{n}\ \text{d}\tau=q(1)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{n}}{n+1}(u-1)^{n+1}.

(38)

Rearranging (
30
), we have

\phi(u)=q(u)+\ln(u).

(39)

To extract the Taylor coefficients
\phi_{n}
according to (
22a
), then, we require the Taylor expansion of
\ln(u)
as

\ln(u)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}(u-1)^{n}.

(40)

Inserting Eqs. (
38
) and (
40
) into (
39
), and comparing with the form (
22a
) term by term, we obtain the desired expression

\phi_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{n-1}+(-1)^{n+1}\right),n\geq 1.

(41)

\phi_{n}
as follows

Appendix C
Alternative Approach: Large
\operatorname{Pr}
asymptotic expansion via divergent Sakiadis Power Series Solution

In Appendix
A.1
, Laplace’s method was used to determine the large
\operatorname{Pr}
behavior of the integral (
11a
); this integral is embedded in the desired Nusselt number expression (
5
) by (
8
). The purpose of this appendix is to show that one may utilize the Taylor series expansion in
\eta
about
\eta=0
directly to obtain the same large
\operatorname{Pr}
expansion (
13
) and coefficients in Appendix
B
.

To proceed, we make use of the well-established power series solution to (
2
), with a recursion for the coefficients given first by
Blasius [3]
for flow over a stationary plate, but here written for the Sakiadis flow as

\displaystyle f(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\eta^{n},

(43a)

\displaystyle a_{n+3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)(k+2)a_{k+1}a_{n-k}}{2(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)},\hphantom{\sum}n\geq 0

(43b)

\displaystyle a_{0}=0,\hphantom{\sum}a_{1}

\displaystyle=1,\hphantom{\sum}a_{2}=\kappa/2.

(43c)

As noted by
Naghshineh et al. [31]
, the series (
43
) is convergent in the range
\eta<R
, where
R\approx 4.0722
. Since the series (
43
) for
f
diverges for
\eta>R
, the series solution is deficient in that it cannot satisfy the boundary condition
f^{\prime}\to 0
as
\eta\to\infty
in (
2
). In fact, this was the motivation for the gauge transformation that yields the convergent solution (
3
) (see
[
31
]
). Nevertheless, the series (
43
)
is
sufficient to extract the large
\operatorname{Pr}
behavior in accordance with Laplace’s method as follows.

For Large
\operatorname{Pr}
, the integral is dominated by its integrand’s behavior near
\eta=0
; thus, we first replace the upper bound in (
7
) with a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
which justifies the use of the expansion (
43a
) in the evaluation of the integral.

{\theta^{\prime}(0)}^{-1}\sim\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Pr}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n+1}\eta^{n+1}\right)\ \text{d}\eta.

(44)

In accordance with Laplace’s method, we rewrite the integral (
44
) as

{\theta^{\prime}(0)}^{-1}\sim\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Pr}\eta^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Pr}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n+1}\eta^{n+1}\right)\ \text{d}\eta\\
\sim\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{2}}\exp\left(\operatorname{Pr}\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\bar{a}_{n}\eta^{n}\right)\ \text{d}\eta

(45)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{0}

\displaystyle=\bar{a}_{1}=\bar{a}_{2}=0,

(46a)

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\displaystyle a_{n-1}}{2n},n\geq 3.

(46b)

Next, we use the Taylor series of the exponential in (
45
) to obtain

\displaystyle\exp\left(\operatorname{Pr}\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\bar{a}_{n}\eta^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{Pr^{n}}{n!}\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bar{a}_{n}\eta^{n}\right\}^{n}

(47a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Pr}^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=3n}^{\infty}\lambda_{n,k}\eta^{k},

(47b)

where we have used the following

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bar{a}_{j}\eta^{j}\right\}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n,k}\eta^{k},

(48a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{0,0}

\displaystyle=1,\hphantom{\sum\sum}\lambda_{0,k}=0,k\geq 1,

(48b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{n,j}\bar{a}_{k-j}.

(48c)

Eqs. (
48
) are obtained by taking the powers of the relevant series recursively using Cauchy’s product rule. Combining this expansion with (
44
) and noting that the leading term in the LHS of (
48a
) is of order
\eta^{3}
,
\lambda_{n,k}=0
when
k<3n
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\theta^{\prime}(0)}^{-1}

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Pr}\eta^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Pr}^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=3n}^{\infty}\lambda_{n,k}\eta^{k}\ \text{d}\eta

(49a)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=3n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\operatorname{Pr}^{n}\lambda_{n,k}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Pr}\eta^{2}}\eta^{k}\ \text{d}\eta

(49b)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=3n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\operatorname{Pr}^{n}\lambda_{n,k}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Pr}\eta^{2}}\eta^{k}\ \text{d}\eta

(49c)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=3n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\operatorname{Pr}^{n}\lambda_{n,k}\Gamma\left(\frac{k+1}{2}\right)2^{k}\operatorname{Pr}^{(2n-k-1)/2}

(49d)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{n!}\lambda_{n,\ell+2n-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+2n}{2}\right)2^{\ell+2n-1}\right]\operatorname{Pr}^{-\ell/2},

(49e)

yielding the result

\gamma_{\ell}=\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{n!}\lambda_{n,\ell+2n-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+2n}{2}\right)2^{\ell+2n-1}.

(50)

In comparing (
49b
) with (
49c
), note that we have extended the upper bound on the integral from
\varepsilon
to infinity, which only adds subdominant terms as
\operatorname{Pr}\to\infty
, as can be established with integration by parts. (
49e
) follows from (
49d
) by rewriting the sum for fixed order in
\operatorname{Pr}
. The resulting (
50
) indeed produces exactly the values
\gamma_{n}
from Appendix
B
, while satisfying a slightly simpler recurrence relation than (
29a
), and makes clear that the coefficients
\gamma_{n}
depend only on
\kappa
.
