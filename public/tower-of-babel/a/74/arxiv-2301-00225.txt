Abstract
. We construct simple geometric operations on faces of the Cayley sum of two polytopes. These operations can be thought of as convex geometric counterparts of divided difference operators in Schubert calculus. We show that these operations give a uniform construction of Knutson–Miller mitosis (in type A A ) and (simplified) Fujita mitosis (in type C C ) on Kogan faces of Gelfand–Zetlin polytopes.



1.
Introduction

Mitosis as a mathematical notion was introduced by Knutson and Miller to generate
combinatorial objects (reduced pipe dreams or rc-graphs) from a single object
[
KnM
,
M
]
.
Originally, pipe dreams were used in Fomin–Kirillov theorem to enumerate the coefficients of Schubert polynomials in type
A
.
Recall that Schubert polynomials are defined inductively by applying divided difference operators to a single Schubert polynomial.
Hence, mitosis operations can be thought of as combinatorial counterparts of divided difference operators from Schubert calculus.
A convex geometric version of mitosis was defined in
[
K16
]
: pipe dreams were replaced by faces of polytopes from representation theory (such as Nakashima–Zelevinsky polytopes) and divided difference operators were replaced by Demazure operators.
In the present paper, we introduce a different version of geometric mitosis (simple geometric mitosis).
It is more general and can be applied to classical Gelfand–Zetlin polytopes in all types.
The starting point for this paper was the desire to understand the results of
[
F
]
from a geometric rather than representation theoretic viewpoint.

Simple geometric mitosis is defined on a convex polytope
\Delta
such that
\Delta
can be represented as the Cayley sum of two of its facets.
Let
v
be a vertex of
\Delta
.
Faces of
\Delta
that contain
v
play the role of pipe dreams.
Mitosis operation acts only on faces that contain
v
and assigns to a face a collection of faces of dimension one greater.
In applications to Schubert calculus, there are several different mitosis operations associated with
\Delta
.
This is because Gelfand–Zetlin polytopes (and other polytopes from representation theory) admit several decompositions into Cayley sums of facets.
In this case, mitosis operations generate faces (that contain
v
) from the single vertex
v
.
The faces generated this way can be used to represent Schubert cycles as sums of faces.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we give a definition of simple geometric mitosis.
In Section
3
, we recall the combinatorial definition of Knutson–Miller mitosis on pipe dreams in type
A
and Fujita mitosis on skew pipe dreams in type
C
.
In Section
4
, we relate the constructions of the previous two sections using Gelfand–Zetlin polytopes in types
A
and
C
.
In Section
4
, we outline applications to Schubert calculus.

2.
Main construction

In this section, we give an elementary definition of simple geometric mitosis.
In Section
5
, we explain the meaning of this definition in the setting of intersection theory.

Let
P
,
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two convex polytopes of full dimension.
Denote by
\Delta:=\Delta(P,Q)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
their Cayley sum
P*Q
, that is, the convex hull of

(P\times 0)\cup(Q\times 1).

In what follows, we identify
P
and
Q
with facets
P\times 0
and
Q\times 1
of
\Delta
.
The
mitosis operation
acts only on those faces of
\Delta
that are contained in
P
(“horizontal faces”), and produces faces of
\Delta
that are not contained in
P
.

Definition 1
.

The face
F\subset P
is called
admissible
, if there exists a unique face
\exp(F)\neq F
with the property

F=\exp(F)\cap P.

It is easy to check that if
F
is admissible then
\dim\exp(F)=\dim F+1
.
The face
\exp(F)
can be thought of as an expansion of
F
from
P
to
\Delta
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
d=2
, and let
P,Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be two triangles obtained from a unit square by cutting along a diagonal.
More precisely, take
P=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ x_{1},x_{2}\leq 1,\ x_{1}+x_{2}\geq 1\}

and
Q=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ x_{1},x_{2}\geq 0,\ x_{1}+x_{2}\leq 1\}
.
Then
\Delta=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ |\ x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\leq 1,\ 1\leq x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}\leq 2\}
.
The face
P\subset\Delta
and edges of
P
are admissible faces, while vertices of
P
are not.

Let
v\in P
be a vertex.
In the definition below, we consider only those faces of
P
and
\Delta
that contain
v
.
We call them
v
-faces
.
In particular, the
mitosis operation

{\rm mitosis}^{v}(\cdot)
will depend on the choice of
v
.
Let
F\subset P
be an admissible
v
-face of dimension
\ell
.
We will define
{\rm mitosis}^{v}(F)
geometrically as a set of
v
-faces
\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}\}
of dimension
\ell+1
.

Definition 2
.

A
v
-face
E_{i}\subset\Delta
belongs to
{\rm mitosis}^{v}(F)
if
E_{i}
satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

E_{i}\not\subset P
,
Q
;

(2)

E_{i}\cap Q\subset\exp(F)\cap Q
.

Faces in
{\rm mitosis}^{v}(F)
will be called
offsprings
of
F
.

Informally, the first condition means that
E_{i}
is not a “horizontal” face, that is,
E_{i}
intersects both
P
and
Q
.
In particular, it is not possible to apply the same mitosis operation twice because none of the faces
E_{i}\in{\rm mitosis}^{v}(F)
lies in
P
.
The second condition tells us that the face
E_{i}
is not in general position with respect to
Q
(unless
E_{i}=\exp(F)
).

Example 2.2
.

Let
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Feigin–Fourier–Littelmann–Vinberg (FFLV) polytope given by inequalities
0\leq x_{1},x_{3}\leq 1
,
0\leq x_{2}
,
x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}\leq 2
.
Consider faces:

P_{1}=\{x_{3}=0\},\quad Q_{1}=\{x_{3}=1\},\quad P_{2}=\{x_{1}=1\},\quad Q_{2}=\{x_{1}=0\}.

Let
v
be the vertex
(1,1,0)
.
Clearly, there are two ways to decompose
\Delta
as the Cayley sum of two polygons:
\Delta=\Delta(P_{1},Q_{1})
and
\Delta=\Delta(P_{2},Q_{2})
.
Hence, there are two different mitosis operations
{\rm mitosis}^{v}_{1}
and
{\rm mitosis}^{v}_{2}
associated with these decompositions.

For instance,
{\rm mitosis}^{v}_{1}(F)
for the edge
F=\{x_{3}=0,\ x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=2\}
consists of two offsprings, namely,
\exp(F)=\{x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=2\}
and

P_{2}
.
However,
{\rm mitosis}^{v}_{2}(F)
consists of a single offspring for all admissible
v
-faces
F\subset P_{2}
.

Remark 2.3
.

In what follows, we sometimes apply the mitosis operation to a set
S
of faces of the same dimension.
By
{\rm mitosis}^{v}(S)
we mean

\bigcup_{F\in S}{\rm mitosis}^{v}(F).

3.
Combinatorial mitosis

In this section, we recall two combinatorial rules: Knutson–Miller mitosis on pipe dreams (type
A
) and Fujita mitosis on skew pipe dreams (type
C
).
Both rules can be defined uniformly using the same combinatorial operation called
two-row mitosis
.
A similar operation is implicitly used in the original definition of Knutson–Miller mitosis
[
KnM
,
M
]
.
We first define
two-row mitosis
explicitly, and then define mitosis operations in types
A
and
C
by reducing them to suitable two-row mitosis.
Note that the term
row
here does not necessarily mean a horizontal collection of items as in the original definition of Knutson–Miller mitosis.

3.1.
Two-row mitosis

Let
A
and
B
be two finite collections of squares such that the number of squares in
A
is greater by one than the number of squares in
B
.
Denote the number of squares in
B
by
\ell
.
We will label the squares in
A
by
a1
,
a2
,…,
a(\ell+1)
, and the squares in
B
by
b1
,
b2
,…,
b\ell
(like in chess notation).
Symbolically, we may represent
A
and
B
by
(\ell+1)
-row and
\ell
-row of squares, respectively.
In real life examples, we might need to arrange squares of
A
and
B
in more intricate ways.
Namely, we will identify squares in
A
and
B
with specific cells in various tables.

To get a
basic pipe dream
we fill some squares in
A
and
B
with
+
.
The other squares remain empty.
By
size
of a basic pipe dream
D
we mean the total number of
+
in the squares of
D
.

Two-row mitosis

M
is an operation on basic pipe dreams that sends a basic pipe dream
D
of size
s
to a (possibly empty) set
M(D)
of basic pipe dreams of size
(s-1)
.

To construct the set
M(D)
we use the following rules:

(1)

If square
a1
is empty, then
M(D)=\varnothing
.

(2)

If square
a1
contains
+
, then find the maximal index
r_{D}
such that the squares
a1
,…,
ar_{D}
are all filled with
+
.

(3)

If square
a1
contains
+
, define the set
\mathcal{J}(D)
of indices:

\mathcal{J}(D):=\{j\leq r_{D}\ |\ \mbox{ square }aj\mbox{ contains }+,\mbox{ square }bj\mbox{ is empty or }j=\ell+1\}.

(4)

For every
p\in\mathcal{J}(D)
, construct the offspring
D_{p}
as follows.
First, erase
+
in square
ap
.
Then move
+
from square
aj
down to square
bj
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}(D)
such that
j<p
.

Remark 3.3
.

It is easy to check that

3.3.
Mitosis in type
C

Similarly to type

(also called

Skew pipe dreams were recently used in

In

We do not know of any direct relation between skew pipe dreams and c-signed pipe dreams.

{\rm mitosis}_{i}^{v}
on certain Kogan faces is combinatorially equivalent to mitosis operation
M^{A}_{i}
on the corresponding pipe dreams.
The following theorem describes the precise relation between the geometric mitosis on Kogan faces of a GZ polytope and the combinatorial mitosis on GZ tables.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
F\subset GZ_{\lambda}^{A}
be a Kogan face such that
its diagram
D(F)
has
+
in cell
(1,i)
and no
+
in cells
(j,i+1)
for all
j
.
Then
{\rm mitosis}_{i}^{v}(F)
consists of Kogan faces whose diagrams are obtained from
D(F)
by the mitosis operation
M^{A}_{i}
on GZ tables.

Proof.

In order to compute
{\rm mitosis}_{i}^{v}(F)
we have to consider not only Kogan faces.
In general, a face of
GZ^{A}_{\lambda}
is given by equations of two types: either
x^{i-1}_{j}=x^{i}_{j}
(type
A
) or
x^{i}_{j}=x^{i-1}_{j+1}
(type
B
).

Definition 3
.

Following
[
Ko
]
define equations
A_{i,j}
and
B_{i,j}
, respectively, as

x^{i-1}_{j}=x^{i}_{j}
and
x^{i}_{j}=x^{i-1}_{j+1}
.

We now apply Definition
2
to
P:=P_{i}
and
Q:=Q_{i}
.
Note that any Kogan face is admissible because it contains a simple vertex, namely, the Kogan vertex
v
.
The face
E_{1}:=\exp(F)
is obtained from
F
by removing a single equation
A_{1,i}
.
Hence, the face
\exp(F)\cap Q
is given by the same equations as
F
with one exception: equation
A_{1,i}
is replaced by equation
B_{1,i}
.

Let
E
be a Kogan face of dimension
\ell+1
(where
\ell:=\dim F
) such that
E\cap Q\subset\exp(F)\cap Q
.
Let (
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
) be the collection of equations of type
A
that define
E
.
If
E\neq\exp(F)
then there exists an equation

(of type A) such that
\exp(F)
satisfies
Y
but
Y
does not follow from (
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
).
However,
Y
should follow from equations
B_{1,i}
,
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
.
Hence, the collection of equations (
B_{1,i}
,
Y
,
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
) is redundant, while the collection (
Y
,
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
) is not.
This is only possible if
A_{1,i+1}
is contained among the equations
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
as the following lemma shows:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{s})
be a collection of equations of type
A
that does not contain equation
A_{p,q}
.
If
A_{p,q}
follows from
(B_{j,i},X_{1},\ldots,X_{s})
then three conditions hold:

(1)

q=i
;

(2)

p>j
;

(3)

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{s})
contains
A_{k,i+1}
for all
k
such that
j\leq k<p
.

Proof.

The statement follows directly from Definition
3
.
∎

If (
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
) contains
A_{1,i+1}
,
A_{2,i+1}
,…,
A_{k,i+1}
but does not contain
A_{k+1,i+1}
for some
k\geq 1
,
then
A_{2,i}
,…,
A_{k+1,i}
are the only equations that follow from (
B_{1,i}
,
X_{1}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1})
.
Hence, if
F
satisfies
A_{p,q}
, and

is not contained among (
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}

A_{p,q}
must coincide with one of the equations
A_{2,i}
,…,
A_{k+1,i}
.
For dimension reasons, all
k

A_{2,i}
,…,
A_{k+1,i}
must be absent in (
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,…,
X_{d-\ell-1}
) (to compensate for the presence of
k
equations
A_{1,i+1}
,
A_{2,i+1}
,…,
A_{k,i+1}
).
In particular, if

does not satisfy

then
{\rm mitosis}_{i}^{v}(F)
consists of a single offspring

.

We now proceed by induction on

where

is the maximal number such that
F
satisfies the equations
A_{1,i}
,…,
A_{r,i}
.
Let

be the facet given by equation

, and

the face given by the same equations as

, that is,

.
It is easy to check that there is a bijection between
{\rm mitosis}^{v}_{i}(F^{\prime})
and
the subset

that consists of all faces

such that

.
Namely, a face

corresponds to the face

.
Hence, the faces in

can be described by the induction hypothesis applied to

.

It remains to describe the faces

.
By condition (2) of Definition

and part (3) of Lemma

applied to

, we have that if

, then
E_{i}
must satisfy equations
A_{1,i+1}
,
A_{2,i+1}
…,

.
Hence, there is a single offspring

E_{r}\in{\rm mitosis}^{v}_{i}(F)\setminus S
obtained from

by removing equation

and by replacing equations
A_{j,i}
by equations
A_{j,i+1}
for all

.
∎

(1)

We now explain how the notion of simple geometric mitosis fits into in the context of intersection theory.
In particular, we relate Corollary

Below we use the notion of
polytope ring
(aka

The definition can be found, for instance, in
[

We refer the reader to

We use notation of Section

Let

p^{*}p_{*}:CH^{*}(Y)\to CH^{*-1}(Y),

Remark 5.4
.

In type
A
, there is no combinatorial difference between presentation of Schubert cycles by Kogan faces and presentation by dual Kogan faces (see
[
F
, Theorem 5.25]
).
Both presentations can be related using the automorphism of the Dynkin diagram of type
A_{n}
(on the level of GZ diagrams this automorphism corresponds to the reflection
(i,j)\mapsto(i,n+2-i-j)
).
In type
C
, there is a combinatorial difference already for
n=2
(see
[
F
, Section 1]
for more details).
In type
A
, one can immediately recover mitosis on dual Kogan faces from mitosis on Kogan faces.
Both operations will be combinatorially equivalent to the Knutson–Miller mitosis.
In type
C
, the Fujita mitosis on Kogan faces does not yield a combinatorially equivalent mitosis on dual Kogan faces.

Example
5.3
shows that Definition
2
is too simple to capture completely the action of
p^{*}p_{*}
on faces of
\Delta
in the general setting.
However, it may be regarded as the first approximation of this action.
It can also be used to make an educated guess about a possible combinatorial mitosis on dual Kogan faces in type
C
and mitosis in type
D
.
