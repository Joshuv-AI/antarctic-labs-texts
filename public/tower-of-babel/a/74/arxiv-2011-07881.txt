Value Function Approximations via Kernel Embeddings for No-Regret Reinforcement Learning
Sayak Ray Chowdhury, Rafael Oliveira

Abstract
We consider the regret minimization problem in reinforcement learning (RL) in the episodic setting. In many real-world RL environments, the state and action spaces are continuous or very large. Existing approaches establish regret guarantees by either a low-dimensional representation of the stochastic transition model or an approximation of the Q Q -functions. However, the understanding of function approximation schemes for state-value functions largely remains missing. In this paper, we propose an online model-based RL algorithm, namely the CME-RL, that learns representations of transition distributions as embeddings in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space while carefully balancing the exploitation-exploration tradeoff. We demonstrate the efficiency of our algorithm by proving a frequentist (worst-case) regret bound that is of order O ~ ​ ( H ​ γ N ​ N ) \tilde{O}\big(H\gamma_{N}\sqrt{N}\big) 1 1 1 O ~ ​ ( ⋅ ) \tilde{O}(\cdot) hides only absolute constant and poly-logarithmic factors. , where H H is the episode length, N N is the total number of time steps and γ N \gamma_{N} is an information theoretic quantity relating the effective dimension of the state-action feature space. Our method bypasses the need for estimating transition probabilities and applies to any domain on which kernels can be defined. It also brings new insights into the general theory of kernel methods for approximate inference and RL regret minimization.

1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) is concerned with learning
to take actions to maximize rewards, by trial and error, in
environments that can evolve in response to actions. A Markov decision process (MDP)
(
Puterman 2014
)
is a popular framework to model decision making in RL environments. In the MDP, starting from
an initial observed state, an agent repeatedly (a)
takes an action, (b) receives a reward, and (c) observes the next
state of the MDP. The traditional RL objective is a
search
goal – find a
policy
(a rule to select an action for each state)
with high total reward using as few interactions with the
environment as possible, also known as the
sample complexity of RL
(
Strehl et al. 2009
)
. This is, however, quite different
from the corresponding
optimization
goal, where the learner
seeks to maximize the total reward earned from all its decisions,
or equivalently, minimize the
regret
or shortfall in total
reward compared to that of an optimal policy
(
Jaksch et al. 2010
)
. This objective
is relevant in many practical sequential decision-making
settings in which every decision that is taken carries utility
or value – recommendation systems, sequential investment
and portfolio allocation, dynamic resource allocation in communication
systems etc. In such
online
optimization settings, there is
no separate budget or time devoted to purely exploring the
unknown environment; rather, exploration and exploitation
must be carefully balanced.

1.1
Related work

Several studies have considered the task of regret minimization in
tabular
MDPs, in which the state and action spaces are finite, and the value function is represented by a table
(
Jaksch et al. 2010
;
Osband et al. 2013
;
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2017
;
Dann et al. 2017
;
Jin et al. 2018
;
Efroni et al. 2019
;
Zanette and Brunskill 2019
)
. The regret bound achieved by these works essentially is proportional to
\sqrt{SAN}
, where
S
and
A
denote the
numbers of states and actions, respectively, and
N
the total number of steps.
In many practical applications, however, the number of states and actions is enormous. For example, the game of Go has a state space with size
3^{361}
, and the state and action spaces of certain robotics applications can even be continuous. These continuous state and action spaces make RL a challenging
task, especially in terms of generalizing learnt knowledge across unseen states and actions. In such cases, the tabular model suffers from the “curse of dimensionality” problem. To tackle this issue, the popular “optimism in the face of uncertainty” principle from
Jaksch et al. 2010
has been extended to handle continuous MDPs,
when assuming some Lipschitz-like smoothness or regularity on the rewards and dynamics
(
Ortner and Ryabko 2012
;
Domingues et al. 2020
)
.

Another line of work considers
function approximation
, i.e., they use features to parameterize reward and transition models, with the hope that the features
can capture leading structures of the MDP
(
Osband and Van Roy 2014
;
Chowdhury and Gopalan 2019
)
. The
model-based
algorithms developed in these works assume oracle access to an optimistic planner to facilitate the learning. The optimistic planning step is quite prohibitive and often becomes computationally intractable for continuous state and action spaces.
Yang and Wang 2019
consider a low-rank bilinear transition model bypassing the complicated planning step; however, their algorithm potentially needs to compute the
value function
across all states. This suffers an
\Omega(S)
computational complexity and as a consequence cannot directly
handle continuous state spaces.

Ayoub et al. 2020
consider linear-mixture transition structure
that includes the bilinear model as a special case. However, their algorithm too suffers the

\Omega(S)
computational complexity. To alleviate the computational burden intrinsic to these model-based approaches, a recent body of work parameterizes the
value functions
directly, using
d
-dimensional state-action
feature maps, and develop
model-free
algorithms bypassing the need for fully
learning the reward and transition models
(
Jin et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Zanette et al. 2020a
)
. Under the assumption that the (action-)value function can be approximated by a linear or a generalized linear function of the feature vectors, these papers develop algorithms with regret bound proportional to
\text{poly}(d)\sqrt{T}
, which is independent of the size of the state and action spaces.
Wang et al. 2020
generalizes this approach by designing an algorithm that works with general (non-linear) value function approximators and prove a similar regret guarantee that depends on the eluder dimension
(
Russo and Van Roy 2013
)
and log-covering number of the
underlying function class.

A few recent works have proposed kernel-based value function approximation algorithms.
Yang et al. 2020
consider kernel and neural function approximations and designed algorithms with regret characterized by intrinsic complexity of the function classes. More closely related to our work,
Domingues et al. 2021
recently proposed a kernel-based RL algorithm via value function approximation. Their main assumption relies on Lipschitz continuity of the reward functions and the state transition kernels. In contrast to their work, we are able to obtain tighter regret bounds by applying typical assumptions in the kernel embeddings literature, which we show are satisfied for a variety of practical systems. Nevertheless, there is a lack of theoretical understanding in designing provably efficient model-based RL algorithms with (non-linear) value function approximation, which we aim to address.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we revisit function approximation in RL by modeling
the value functions as elements of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
(
Schölkopf and Smola 2002
)
compatible with a (possibly infinite dimensional) state feature map.
The main motivation behind this formulation is that the conditional expectations of any function in the RKHS become a linear operation, via the RKHS inner product with an appropriate distribution embedding, known as the
conditional mean embedding

(
Muandet et al. 2016
)
.
In recent years, conditional mean embeddings (CMEs) have found extensive applications in many machine learning tasks
(
Song et al. 2009
;
Song et al. 2010a
;
Song et al. 2010b
;
Song et al. 2013
;
Fukumizu et al. 2008
;
Fukumizu et al. 2009
;
Hsu and Ramos 2019
;
Chowdhury et al. 2020
)
. The foremost advantage of CMEs in our setup is that one can directly compute conditional expectations of the value functions based only on the observed data, since the alternative approach of estimating the transition probabilities as an intermediate step scales poorly with the dimension of the state space
(
Grünewälder et al. 2012
)
. The convergence of conditional mean estimates to the true embeddings in the RKHS norm has been established by
Grünewälder et al. 2012
assuming access to
independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d.
\xspace
) transition samples (the “simulator” setting). However, in the online RL environment like the one considered in this work, one collects data based on past observations, and hence the existing result fails to remain useful. Against this backdrop, we make the following contributions:

•

In the online RL environment, we derive a concentration inequality for mean embedding estimates of the transition distribution around the true embeddings as a function of the uncertainties around these estimates (
Theorem 1
). This bound not only serves as a key tool in designing our model-based RL algorithm but also is of independent interest.

•

Focusing on the value function approximation in the RKHS setting, we present the first model based RL algorithm, namely the
Conditional Mean Embedding RL
(
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
), that is provably efficient in regret performance and does not require any additional oracle access or stronger computational assumptions (
Section 4.1
). Concretely, in the general setting of an episodic MDP, we prove that
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
enjoys a regret bound of
\tilde{O}(H\gamma_{N}\sqrt{N})
, where
H
is the length of each episode,
\gamma_{N}
is a complexity measure relating the effective dimension of the RKHS compatible with the state-action features (
Theorem 2
).

•

Our approach is also robust to the RKHS modelling assumption: when the value functions are not elements of the RKHS, but
\zeta
-close to some RKHS element in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm, then (a modified version of)
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
achieves a
\tilde{O}(H\gamma_{N}\sqrt{N}+\zeta N)
regret, where the linear regret term arises due to the function class misspecification (
Theorem 3
).

2
Preliminaries

Notations

We begin by introducing some notations.
We let
\mathcal{H}
be a arbitrary Hilbert space with inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
and corresponding norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
. When
\mathcal{G}
is another Hilbert space, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{H}
, with the operator norm
\left\lVert\operatorname{A}\right\rVert:=\sup_{\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}=1}\left\lVert\operatorname{A}g\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
. We let
\HS(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
denote the subspace of operators in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
with bounded Hilbert-Schmidt norm
\left\lVert\operatorname{A}\right\rVert_{\HS}:=\big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\left\langle f_{i},\operatorname{A}g_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
, where the
f_{i}
’s form a complete orthonormal system (CONS) for
\mathcal{H}
and the
g_{j}
’s form a CONS for
\mathcal{G}
. In the case
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{H}
, we set
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
the set of all bounded, positive-definite linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{A}\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
if, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\left\langle h,\operatorname{A}h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}>0
.

Regret minimization in finite-horizon episodic MDPs

We consider episodic reinforcement learning in a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP) of episode length
H
with (possibly infinite) state and action spaces
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
, respectively, reward function
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
, and transition probability measure
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, where
\Delta(\mathcal{S})
denotes the probability simplex on
\mathcal{S}
.
The learning agent interacts with the MDP in episodes and, at each episode
t
, a trajectory
(s_{1}^{t},a_{1}^{t},r_{1}^{t},\ldots,s_{H}^{t},a_{H}^{t},r_{H}^{t},s_{H+1}^{t})
is generated. Here
a_{h}^{t}
denotes the action taken at state
s_{h}^{t}
,
r_{h}^{t}:=R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
denotes the immediate reward, and
s_{h+1}^{t}\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
denotes the random next state. The initial state
s_{1}^{t}
is assumed to be fixed and history independent, and can even be possibly chosen by an adversary. The episode terminates when
s_{H+1}^{t}
is reached, where the
agent cannot take any action and hence receives no reward. The actions are chosen following some policy
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H})
, where each
\pi_{h}
is a mapping from the state space
\mathcal{S}
into the action space
\mathcal{A}
. The agent would like to find a policy
\pi
that maximizes the long-term expected cumulative reward starting from every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and every step
h\in[H]
, defined as:

\displaystyle V_{h}^{\pi}(s):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\nolimits_{j=h}^{H}R\left(s_{j},\pi_{j}(s_{j})\right)\;\big|\;s_{h}=s\right]~.

We call
V_{h}^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the value function of policy
\pi
at step
h
.
Accordingly, we also define the action-value function, or
Q
-function,
Q_{h}^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as:

Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,a):=R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\nolimits_{j=h+1}^{H}R\left(s_{j},\pi_{j}(s_{j})\right)\;\big|\;s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a\right]~,

which gives the expected value
of cumulative rewards starting from a state-action pair at the
h
-th step and following the
policy
\pi
afterwards. Note that
V_{h}^{\pi}(s)=Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,\pi_{h}(s))
and it satisfies the Bellman equation:

V_{h}^{\pi}(s)=R(s,\pi_{h}(s))+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,\pi_{h}(s))}\left[V_{h+1}^{\pi}(X)\right],\quad\forall h\in[H]~,

(1)

with
V_{H+1}^{\pi}(s)=0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
We denote by
\pi^{\star}
an optimal policy satisfying:

\displaystyle V^{\pi^{\star}}_{h}(s)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}V^{\pi}_{h}(s),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\;\forall h\in[H],

where
\Pi
is the set of all non-stationary policies. Since the episode length is finite, such a policy exists when the action space
\mathcal{A}
is large but finite
(
Puterman 2014
)
. We denote the optimal value function by
V^{\star}_{h}(s):=V_{h}^{\pi^{\star}}(s)
. We also denote the optimal action-value function (or
Q
-function) as
Q_{h}^{\star}(s,a)=\max_{\pi}Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)
. It is easily shown that the optimal action-value function satisfies the Bellman optimality equation:

Q_{h}^{\star}(s,a):=R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V^{\star}_{h+1}(X)\right],\quad\forall h\in[H]~,

(2)

with
V^{\star}_{h}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)
. This implies that the optimal policy is the greedy policy with respect to the optimal action-value functions. Thus, to find the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
, it suffices to estimate the optimal action-value functions
(Q_{h}^{\star})_{h\in[H]}
.

The agent aims to learn the optimal policy by interacting
with the environment during a set of episodes. We measure performance of the agent by the cumulative (pseudo) regret accumulated over
T
episodes, defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(N):=\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\left[V^{\star}_{1}(s_{1}^{t})-V^{\pi^{t}}_{1}(s_{1}^{t})\right],

where
\pi^{t}
is the policy chosen by the agent at episode
t
and
N=TH
is the total number of steps. The regret measures the quantum of
reward that the learner gives up by not knowing the MDP
in advance and applying the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
from the start.
We seek algorithms that attain sublinear regret
\mathcal{R}(N)=o(N)
in the number of steps
they face, since, for instance, an algorithm that does not adapt
its policy selection behavior depending on past experience
can easily be seen to achieve linear (
\Omega(N)
) regret
(
Lai and Robbins 1985
)
.

Value function approximation in episodic MDPs

A very large or possibly infinite state and action space makes reinforcement learning a challenging task. To obtain sub-linear regret guarantees, it is necessary to posit some regularity assumptions on the underlying function class. In this paper, we use reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces to model the value functions. Let
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
be two RKHSs with continuous positive semi-definite kernel functions
k_{\psi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
k_{\varphi}:(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})\times(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with corresponding inner products
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}
, respectively. There exist feature maps
\psi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
and
\varphi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
such that
k_{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot)=\left\langle\psi(\cdot),\psi(\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
and
k_{\varphi}(\cdot,\cdot)=\left\langle\varphi(\cdot),\varphi(\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}
, respectively
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
.

The weakest assumption one can pose on the value functions is realizability, which posits that the optimal value functions
(V_{h}^{\star})_{h\in[H]}
lie in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
, or at least are well-approximated by
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. For stateless MDPs or multi-armed
bandits where
H=1
, realizability alone suffices for provably efficient algorithms
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
;
Chowdhury and Gopalan 2017
)
. But it does not seem to be sufficient when
H>1
, and in these settings it is common to make stronger assumptions
(
Jin et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
. Following these works, our main
assumption is a closure property
for all value functions in the following class:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\left\{s\mapsto\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{R(s,a)+\left\langle\varphi(s,a),\mu\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}+\eta\sqrt{\left\langle\varphi(s,a),\Sigma^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}}\right\}\right\}\right\},

(3)

where
0<\eta<\infty
,
\mu\in\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
and
\Sigma\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi})
are the parameters of the function class.

Assumption 1

(Optimistic closure)
.

For any
V\in\mathcal{V}
(cf.
Equation 3
), we have
V\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. Furthermore, for a positive constant
B_{V}
, we have
\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\leqslant B_{V}
.

While this property seems quite strong, we note
that related closure-type assumptions are common in the literature. We will relax this assumption later in
Section 4.3
.
In addition, our results do not require explicit knowledge of
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
nor its kernel
k_{\psi}
, as we will only interact with elements of
\mathcal{V}
via point evaluations and RKHS norm bounds.

3
RKHS embeddings of transition distribution

In order to find an estimate of the optimal value function, it is imperative to estimate the conditional expectations of the form
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(X)]
. In the model-based approach considered in this work, we do so by estimating the mean embedding of the conditional distribution
P(\cdot|s,a)
, which is the focus of this section.
For a bounded kernel
2
2

2

Boundedness of a kernel holds for any stationary kernel, e.g., the
squared exponential
kernel and the
Mat
\acute{e}
rn
kernel
(
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
)
.

k_{\psi}
on the state space
\mathcal{S}
, the mean embedding of the conditional distribution
P(\cdot|s,a)
in
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
is an element
\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
such that:

\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi},\quad\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(X)]=\left\langle f,\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}~.

(4)

The mean embedding can be explicitly expressed as a function:

\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[k_{\psi}(X,y)\right],

for all
y\in\mathcal{S}
.
If the kernel
k_{\psi}
is characteristic, such as a stationary kernel, then the mapping
P(\cdot|s,a)\mapsto\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}
is injective, defining a one-to-one relationship between transition distributions and elements of
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}

(
Sriperumbudur et al. 2011
)
.
Following existing works
(
Song et al. 2009
;
Grünewälder et al. 2012
)
, we now make a smoothness assumption
on the transition distribution.

Assumption 2
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
,
the function
(s,a)\mapsto\!\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[f(X)\right]
lies in
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
.

Under
Assumption 2
, the mean embeddings admit a linear representation in state-action features via the
conditional embedding operator

\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})
such that:

\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},\quad\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}=\Theta_{P}\varphi(s,a)~.

(5)

Assumption 2
always holds for finite domains with characteristic kernels. Though it is not necessarily true for continuous domains, we note that the CMEs for classical linear
(
Abbasi-Yadkori and Szepesvári 2011
)
and non-linear
(
Kakade et al. 2020
)
dynamical systems satisfy this assumption.

3.1
Sample estimate of conditional mean embedding

At the beginning of each episode
t
, given the observations
\mathcal{D}_{t}:=(s_{h}^{\tau},a_{h}^{\tau},s_{h+1}^{\tau})_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}
until episode
t-1
, we consider a sample based estimate of the conditional embedding operator.
This is achieved by solving the following ridge-regression problem:

\min_{\Theta\in\HS(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})}\sum\nolimits_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}\left\lVert\psi(s_{h+1}^{\tau})-\Theta\varphi(s^{\tau}_{h},a^{\tau}_{h})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\Theta\right\rVert_{\HS}^{2}~,

(6)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularising constant. The solution of
Equation 6
is given by:

\widehat{\Theta}_{t}=\sum\nolimits_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}\psi(s^{\tau}_{h+1})\otimes\varphi(s^{\tau}_{h},a^{\tau}_{h})\left(\widehat{\operatorname{C}}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I}\right)^{-1}~,

(7)

where
\widehat{\operatorname{C}}_{\varphi,t}:=\sum_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}\varphi(s^{\tau}_{h},a^{\tau}_{h})\otimes\varphi(s^{\tau}_{h},a^{\tau}_{h})
.
To simplify notations, we now let
n=(t-1)H
denote the total number of steps completed at the beginning of episode
t
.
We denote a vector
k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a matrix
\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
by:

k_{\varphi,t}(s,a):=\left[k_{\varphi}\left((s_{h}^{\tau},a_{h}^{\tau}),(s,a)\right)\right]_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H},\quad\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}:=[k_{\varphi}((s_{h}^{\tau},a_{h}^{\tau}),(s_{h^{\prime}}^{\tau^{\prime}},a_{h^{\prime}}^{\tau^{\prime}}))]_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}<t,h,h^{\prime}\leqslant H}~.

Then, via
Equation 7
, the conditional mean embeddings can be estimated as

\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}=\widehat{\Theta}_{t}\varphi(s,a)=\sum\nolimits_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}\left[\alpha_{t}(s,a)\right]_{(\tau,h)}\psi(s^{\tau}_{h+1})~,

(8)

where we define the weight vector
\alpha_{t}(s,a):=(\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1}k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)
.

3.2
Concentration of mean embedding estimates

In this section, we show that for any state-action pair
(s,a)
, the CME estimates
\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}
lies within a high-probability confidence region around the true embedding
\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}
. This eventually translates, via
Equation 4
, to a concentration property of
\left\langle f,\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
around
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(X)]
for any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. The uncertainty of CME estimates can be characterized by the variance estimate

\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s,a):=\lambda\left\langle\varphi(s,a),\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}
, where
\operatorname{M}_{t}:=\widehat{\operatorname{C}}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I}
. To see this, note that an application of Sherman-Morrison formula yields:

\sigma_{\varphi,t}^{2}(s,a):=k_{\varphi}((s,a),(s,a))-k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)^{\top}(\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1}k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~,

(9)

which is equivalent to the predictive variance of a Gaussian process (GP)
(
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
)
. Although a sample from a GP is usually not an element of the RKHS defined by its kernel
(
Lukic and Beder 2001
)
, the following result allows us to use
\sigma_{\varphi,t}^{2}(s,a)
as an error measure.

Theorem 1

(Concentration of the conditional embedding operator)
.

Suppose that
\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{k_{\psi}(s,s)}\leqslant B_{\psi}
. Then, under
Assumption 2
, for any
\lambda>0
and
\delta\in(0,1]
,

\mathbb{P}\left[{\forall t\in\mathbb{N},\;\;\left\lVert\big(\Theta_{P}-\widehat{\Theta}_{t}\big)\operatorname{M}_{t}^{1/2}\right\rVert\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta)}\right]\geqslant 1-\delta~,

where
\beta_{t}(\delta):=\sqrt{2\lambda B_{P}^{2}+256(1+\lambda^{-1})\log(\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t})^{1/2})\log(2t^{2}H/\delta)}
,
B_{P}\geqslant\left\lVert\Theta_{P}\right\rVert_{\HS}
.

Theorem 1
implies a concentration inequality for the CME estimates, since, for all
t\geqslant 1
:

\left\lVert\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}-\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\leqslant\left\lVert(\Theta_{P}-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\operatorname{M}_{t}^{1/2}\right\rVert\left\lVert\varphi(s,a)\right\rVert_{\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}}\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a),\,\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

with probability at least
1-\delta
. This forms the core of our value function approximations.

Remark 1
.

Considering the simulation setting,
Grünewälder et al. 2012
assume access to a sample
(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, drawn i.i.d. from a joint distribution

Proof sketch of
Theorem 1

To derive this result, we note that the sequence evaluation noise
\varepsilon_{h}^{t}:=\psi(s_{h+1}^{t})-\Theta_{P}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
at each step
h
of episode
t
forms a martingale difference sequence, with each element having a bounded RKHS norm. We overload notation to define, for each pair
(t,h)
, the operator
\operatorname{M}_{t,h}=\operatorname{M}_{t}+\sum_{j\leqslant h}\varphi(s^{t}_{j},a^{t}_{j})\otimes\varphi(s^{\tau}_{j},a^{\tau}_{j})
, and the estimate

\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h}=\left(\sum\nolimits_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}\psi(s^{\tau}_{h+1})\otimes\varphi(s^{\tau}_{h},a^{\tau}_{h})+\sum\nolimits_{j\leqslant h}\psi(s^{t}_{j+1})\otimes\varphi(s^{t}_{j},a^{t}_{j})\right)\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}~.

Now, we consider the random variable

z_{t,h}=\left\lVert(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{1/2}\right\rVert^{2}_{\HS}
, and prove a high-probability upper bound on it using Azuma-Hoeffding’s inequality for martingales. In fact, we show that
z_{t,h}\leqslant\beta^{2}_{t,h}(\delta)
uniformly over all pair
(t,h)
with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
\beta_{t,h}(\delta)
is defined similarly to
\beta_{t}(\delta)
with only
\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}
being replaced by
\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t,h}:=[k_{\varphi}((s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau}),(s_{j^{\prime}}^{\tau^{\prime}},a_{j^{\prime}}^{\tau^{\prime}}))]_{(\tau,j),(\tau^{\prime},j^{\prime})\leqslant(t,h)}
– the gram-matrix at step
h
of episode
t
. The proof then follows by noting that

\left\lVert(\widehat{\Theta}_{t}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{\HS}=z_{t-1,H}^{1/2}\leqslant\beta_{t-1,H}(\delta)\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta)
. The complete proof is given in the supplementary material.

4
RL exploration using RKHS embeddings

In this section, we aim to develop an online RL algorithm using the conditional mean embedding estimates that balances exploration and exploitation (near) optimally. We realize this, at a high level, by following the Upper-Confidence Bound (UCB) principle and thus our algorithm falls in a similar framework as in
Jaksch et al. 2010
;
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2017
;
Yang and Wang 2019
.

4.1
The Conditional Mean Embedding RL (
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
) algorithm

At a high level, each episode
t

consists of two passes over all steps. In the first pass, we maintain the
Q
-function estimates via dynamic programming. To balance the exploration-exploitation trade-off, we first define a confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
that contains the set of conditional embedding operators that are deemed to be consistent with all the data that has been collected in the past. Specifically, for any
\delta\in(0,1]
,
\lambda>0
and constants
B_{P}
and
B_{\psi}
,
Theorem 1
governs us to define the confidence set

\mathcal{C}_{t}:=\left\{\Theta\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi}):\left\lVert(\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\operatorname{M}_{t}^{1/2}\right\rVert\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\right\}~,

(10)

where
\beta_{t}(\cdot)
governs the exploration-exploitation trade-off. This confidence set is then used to compute the optimistic
Q
-estimates, starting with
V_{H+1}^{t}(s)=0
, and setting:

\displaystyle\text{for}\;h=H,H\!-\!1,\ldots,1,\quad V_{h}^{t}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)\right\},

(11)

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\max_{\Theta_{P^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P^{\prime}(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]~.

(12)

We note here that we only
require an optimistic estimate of the optimal
Q
-function. Hence, it is not necessary to solve the maximization problem in
Equation 12
explicitly. In fact, we can use a closed-form expression instead of searching for the optimal embedding operator
\Theta_{P^{\prime}}
in the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
. If the value estimate
V_{h+1}^{t}
lies in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
, we then have from
Equation 4
that
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P^{\prime}(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]=\left\langle V_{h+1}^{t},\vartheta_{P^{\prime}}^{s,a}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
, and from
Equation 8
that:

\left\langle V_{h+1}^{t},\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}=\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}v_{h+1}^{t}=k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)^{\top}(\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1}v_{h+1}^{t}~,

where we define the vector
v^{t}_{h+1}:=[V_{h+1}^{t}(s_{h^{\prime}+1}^{\tau})]_{\tau<t,h^{\prime}\leqslant H}
. Now, since the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is convex, the
Q
-updates given by
Equation 12
admit the closed-form expression:

Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)=R(s,a)+k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)^{\top}(\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1}v^{t}_{h+1}+\left\lVert V_{h+1}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

(13)

We now note that, by the optimistic closure property (
Assumption 1
), the value estimate
V_{h}^{t}
given by
Equation 11
lies in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
, rendering the closed-form expression in
Equation 13
valid.

In the second pass, we execute the greedy policy with respect to the
Q
-function estimates obtained in the first pass. Specifically,
at each step
h
, we chose the action:

a_{h}^{t}=\pi^{t}_{h}(s^{t}_{h})\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\nolimits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a)~.

(14)

The pseudo-code of
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
is given in
Section 4.1
. Note that, in order to implement
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
, we do not need to know the kernel
k_{\psi}
; only the knowledge of the upper bound
B_{V}
over the RKHS norm of
V_{h+1}^{t}
suffices our purpose. For
simplicity of representation, we assume that the agent, while not knowing the conditional mean embedding operator
\Theta_{P}

reward function
R

an element of the RKHS

Computational complexity of

The dominant cost is evaluating the
Q
-function estimates

4.2
Regret bound for
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}

In this section, we present the regret guarantee of our algorithm. We first define

\gamma_{N}\equiv\gamma_{\varphi,\lambda,N}:=\sup_{\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}:|\mathcal{X}|=N}\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,\mathcal{X}})~,

where
\mathcal{X}=\{(s_{i},a_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
and
\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,\mathcal{X}}=[k_{\varphi}((s_{i},a_{i}),(s_{j},a_{j}))]_{i,j\in[N]}
is the gram matrix over the data set
\mathcal{X}
.
\gamma_{N}
denotes the
maximum information gain
about a (random) function
f
sampled from a zero-mean GP with covariance function
k_{\varphi}
after
N
noisy observations,
obtained by passing
f
through an i.i.d. Gaussian
channel
\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda)
. Consider the case when
k_{\varphi}
is a squared exponential kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then it can be verified that
\gamma_{N}=O\left((\log N)^{d+1}\right)

(
Srinivas et al. 2009
)
.

Theorem 2

(Cumulative regret of
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
)
.

Under assumptions
1
and
2
, after interacting with the environment for
N=TH
steps, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
(
Section 4.1
) achieves the regret bound

\mathcal{R}(N)\leqslant 2B_{V}\alpha_{N,\delta}\sqrt{2(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)N\gamma_{N}}+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}~,

where
B_{\varphi}\geqslant\sup_{s,a}\sqrt{k_{\varphi}((s,a),(s,a))}
, and

\alpha_{N,\delta}:=\sqrt{2\lambda B_{P}^{2}+256(1+\lambda^{-1})\gamma_{N}\log(4N^{2}/\delta)}
.

Theorem 2
yields a
\tilde{O}(H\gamma_{N}\sqrt{N})
regret bound for
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
. Comparing to the minimax regret in tabular setting,
\Theta(H\sqrt{SAN})

(
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2017
)
, our bound replaces the sublinear dependency on the number of state-action pairs by a linear dependency on the intrinsic complexity measure,
\gamma_{N}
, of the feature
space
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
, which is crucial in the large state-action
space setting that entails function approximation. Additionally, in the kernelized bandit setting (
H=1
), our bound matches the best known upper bound
O(\gamma_{N}\sqrt{N})

(
Chowdhury and Gopalan 2017
)
. We note, however, that
while an MDP has state transitions, the bandits do not,
and a naive adaptation of existing kernelized bandit algorithms
to this setting would give a regret exponential in episode length
H
.
Furthermore, due to the Markov transition structure, the lower bound for kernelized bandits
(
Scarlett et al. 2017
)
does not directly apply here. Hence, it remains an interesting
future direction to determine the optimal dependency on
\gamma_{N}
.

Conversion to PAC guarantee

Similarly to the discussion in
Jin et al. 2019
, our regret bound directly translates
to a sample complexity or probably approximately correct (PAC) guarantee in the following sense. Assuming a fixed initial state
s_{1}^{t}=s
for each episode
t
, with at least a constant probability, we can learn an
\varepsilon
-optimal policy
\pi
that
satisfies
V^{\star}_{1}(s)-V^{\pi}_{1}(s)\leqslant\varepsilon
by running
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
for

episodes, where
d_{\text{eff}}
is a known upper bound over
\gamma_{N}
, and then output the greedy policy according to the
Q
-function at
t
-th episode, where
t
is sampled uniformly from
[T]
. Here

effectively captures the number of significant dimensions of
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
.

Remark 2
.

Yang and Wang 2019

assumes the model
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)=\left\langle\psi(s^{\prime}),\Theta_{P}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
, and propose an
algorithm with regret
\tilde{O}(H^{2}\gamma_{N}\sqrt{N})
. In comparison, we get an

factor improvement thanks to a tighter control over the sum of predictive variances.
Furthermore, their algorithm can’t be implemented exactly as
they need to apply random sampling to approximate the estimate

). Moreover, in contrast to

before
the algorithm runs.

Remark 3
.

Considering linear function approximation (
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
),
Jin et al. 2019
assumes that for any
V\in\mathcal{V}
(
Equation 3
), the map

).

Proof sketch of
Theorem 2

We start with a control on the
Q
-function estimates
Q_{h}^{t}
, which in turn leads to the regret bound, as our policy is based on
Q_{h}^{t}
. We prove that as long as
\Theta_{P}
lies in the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
, the
Q
-updates are optimistic estimates of the optimal
Q
-values, i.e.,
Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)\leqslant Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)
for all
(s,a)
, and thus, allow us to pick an optimistic action while sufficiently exploring the state space.
This implies
V^{\star}_{1}(s_{1}^{t})\leqslant V_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})
and thus, in turn, the regret

\mathcal{R}(N)\leqslant\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(V_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})-V_{1}^{\pi^{t}}(s_{1}^{t})\big)
. At this point, we let
g_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t}):=V_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})-V_{1}^{\pi^{t}}(s_{1}^{t})
denote the gap between the most optimistic value and the actual value obtained at episode
t
. We then have

\displaystyle g_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})\leqslant\sum\nolimits_{h=1}^{H}\big(Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\big(R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\big)+m_{h}^{t}\big),

where
(m_{h}^{t})_{t,h}
denotes a martingale difference sequence. We control this via the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality as
\sum_{t,h}m_{t,h}=O(H\sqrt{N})
. The rest of the terms inside the summation can be controlled, by
Theorem 1
and by design of the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
, using the predictive variances
\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
. In fact, for
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
, it holds that

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)-\left(R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)\leqslant 2B_{V}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

Now, the proof can be completed
by showing that
\sum_{t,h}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})=O\big(\sqrt{HN\gamma_{N}}\big)
. Complete proof of this result is given in the supplementary material.

4.3
Robustness to model misspecification

Theorem 2
hinges on the fact that any optimistic estimate of the value function can be specified as an element in
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. In this section, we study the case when there is a misspecification error. Formally, we consider the
following assumption.

Assumption 3

(Approximate optimistic closure)
.

There exists constants
\zeta>0
and
B_{V}>0
, such that for any
V\in\mathcal{V}
(
Equation 3
), there exists a function
\tilde{V}\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
which satisfies
\|V-\tilde{V}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\zeta
and
\|\tilde{V}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\leqslant B_{V}
. We call
\zeta
the misspecification error.

The quality of this approximation will further depend upon how well any
V\in\mathcal{V}
can be approximated by a low-norm function in
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. One specialization is to the
case when
\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathcal{S})
, the vector space of continuous and bounded functions on
\mathcal{S}
, and
k_{\psi}
is a
\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathcal{S})
-universal kernel
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
. In
this case, we can choose
\tilde{V}
such that
\|V-\tilde{V}\|_{\infty}
is
arbitrarily small. For technical reasons, we also make the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

The RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
contains the constant functions.
3
3

3

This is a mild assumption, because for any RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
, the direct sum
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}+\mathbb{R}
,
where
\mathbb{R}
denotes the RKHS associated with the positive definite kernel
k(s,s^{\prime})=1
, is again a RKHS with reproducing kernel
k_{\text{new}}(s,s^{\prime}):=k_{\psi}(s,s^{\prime})+1
.

The following theorem states that our algorithm is in fact robust to a small
model misspecification. To achieve this, we only need to adopt a different exploration term in
Equation 13
to account for the misspecification error
\zeta
. To this end, define the
Q
-function updates as

Q_{h}^{t}(s,a):=R(s,a)+k_{\varphi,t}(s,a)^{\top}(\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1}v^{t}_{h+1}+\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~,

(15)

where
\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
denotes the norm of the all-one function
s\mapsto 1
in
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
.

Theorem 3

(Cumulative regret under misspecification)
.

Under assumptions
2
,
3
and
4
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
achieves the regret bound

\mathcal{R}(N)\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\alpha_{N,\delta}\sqrt{2(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)N\gamma_{N}}+4\zeta N+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}~,

where
B_{\varphi}
and
\alpha_{N,\delta}
are as given in
Theorem 2
.

In comparison with
Theorem 2
,
Theorem 3
asserts that
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
will incur at most an
additional
O(\zeta\gamma_{N}\sqrt{HN}+\zeta N)
regret when the model is misspecified. This additional term is linear in
N
due to the intrinsic bias introduced by the approximation. This linear dependency is standard in the literature, e.g., it is present even in the easier setting of linear function approximation
(
Jin et al. 2019
)
. When
\zeta
is sufficiently small (as is typical for universal kernels

Conversion to PAC guarantee

Similar to
Theorem 2
, we can also convert

to a PAC guarantee.
Assuming a fixed initial state

\varepsilon

Remark 4

(Regret under unknown misspecification error)
.

When the misspecification error
\zeta
is unknown to the agent apriori, one can invoke the dynamic regret balancing scheme of

to get essentially a similar bound as

(albeit with a polylog factor blow-up). In fact,

Proof sketch of
Theorem 3

Similar to the proof of
Theorem 2
, we control the
Q

(cf.
Equation 15
), but with necessary modifications taking the effect of the misspecification error

, that

\displaystyle Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)-\!\left(R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)\!\leqslant 2\!\left(\!B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\!\!\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+2\zeta~.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a novel model-based RL algorithm with sub-linear regret guarantees under an optimistic RKHS-closure assumption on the value functions, without requiring a “simulator” access. The algorithm essentially performs an optimistic value iteration step, which is derived from a novel concentration inequality for the mean embeddings of the transition distribution. We have also shown robustness of our algorithm to small model misspecifications.

As future work, it remains an open research direction to relax the strong optimistic closure assumption to a milder one, as in
Zanette et al. 2020b
and
Domingues et al. 2021
, without sacrificing on the computational and regret performances. In terms of computational complexity,
Vial et al. 2022
proposed an algorithm for misspecified linear MDPs with bounded per-iteration computational complexity. Although our method has computational complexity growing with the number of data points, we highlight that constant cost per iteration is possible to be achieved with kernel-based approximations by means of low-rank decompositions
(
Gijsberts and Metta 2013
)
, which is another possible research direction for future work.

Appendix A
Closed-form solution for the operator optimization problem

In this section, we show that

\max_{\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(X)]

Although the term corresponding to

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})
:

\max_{\Theta\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})}\quad\left\langle f,\Theta\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\left\lVert(\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\operatorname{M}_{t}^{1/2}\right\rVert\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta/2)~,

(16)

where
\operatorname{M}_{t}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\varphi})
. The problem above admits a closed-form solution by applying the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions. Directly oing so would require us to take derivatives of the operator norm. However, observe that for any operator
\operatorname{M}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})
we have
\left\lVert\operatorname{M}\right\rVert\leqslant\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}}(\operatorname{M}^{\top}\operatorname{M})}
,
where the latter corresponds to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Compared to the operator norm, we can easily take derivatives of the trace to compute the KKT conditions. In addition, due to the upper bound, any solution satisfying the Hilbert-Schmidt norm constraint is also a solution under the operator norm constraint.
We replace
Equation 16
with the following problem:

\max_{\Theta\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})}\quad\left\langle f,\Theta\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad{\operatorname{tr}}((\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\operatorname{M}_{t}(\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})^{\top})\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta/2)^{2}~.

Applying the KKT conditions, we solve
\nabla_{\Theta}\ell(\Theta,\eta)=0

with respect to
\Theta\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\mathcal{H}_{\psi})
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\eta\geqslant 0
, where
\ell(\Theta,\eta):=\left\langle f,\Theta\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}-\eta({\operatorname{tr}}((\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\operatorname{M}_{t}(\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})^{\top})-\beta_{t}(\delta/2)^{2})
.
First, by
\nabla_{\Theta}\ell(\Theta,\eta)=0
, we have

\displaystyle f\otimes\varphi(s,a)-2\eta(\Theta-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\operatorname{M}_{t}=0\implies\Theta=\widehat{\Theta}_{t}+\frac{1}{2\eta}(f\otimes\varphi(s,a))\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}~.

(17)

Now, note that, for quadratically constrained linear program, the maximum should lie at the border of the constrained set. Replacing the result above into the constraint, we obtain

\beta_{t}(\delta/2)^{2}=\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}{\operatorname{tr}}((f\otimes\varphi(s,a))\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}(\varphi(s,a)\otimes f))=\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\left\langle\varphi(s,a),\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}\left\langle f,f\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}~,

so that
\eta=\frac{1}{2\beta_{t}(\delta/2)}\left\lVert\varphi(s,a)\right\rVert_{\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}
.
Combining the latter with
Equation 17
, the solution to
Equation 16
is then given by

\Theta_{*}:=\widehat{\Theta}_{t}+\frac{\beta_{t}(\delta/2)}{\left\lVert\varphi(s,a)\right\rVert_{\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}}(f\otimes\varphi(s,a))\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}~,

which finally yields

\begin{split}\max_{\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(X)]=\left\langle f,\Theta_{*}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}&=\left\langle f,\widehat{\Theta}_{t}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\!\!+\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\left\lVert\varphi(s,a)\right\rVert_{\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}}\\
&=\left\langle f,\widehat{\Theta}_{t}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\!\!+\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.\end{split}

Replacing
f
by
V_{h+1}^{t}
in the solution above and adding the reward function (cf.
Equation 12
), we recover
Equation 13
.

Note that
\widehat{\Theta}_{t}=\widehat{\Theta}_{t-1,H}

\widehat{\Theta}_{t}

z_{t,h}=\left\lVert(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{1/2}\right\rVert^{2}_{\HS}={\operatorname{tr}}\left((\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t,h}(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h}-\Theta_{P})^{\top}\right)~.

Define the operator
\operatorname{X}_{t,h}=(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t,h}

\operatorname{X}_{t,h}=\sum_{\tau<t,h\leqslant H}\varepsilon_{h}^{\tau}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{\tau},a_{h}^{\tau})+\sum_{j\leqslant h}\varepsilon_{j}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{j}^{t},a_{j}^{t})-\lambda\Theta_{P}=\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}+\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})~,

\operatorname{X}_{t,0}=\operatorname{X}_{t-1,H}

We then have

\displaystyle z_{t,h}

\displaystyle={\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{X}_{t,h}\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}\right)}_{\text{A}}+2\underbrace{{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}\right)}_{\text{B}}+\underbrace{{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right)}_{\text{C}}~.

\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h}(s,a)=\lambda\left\langle\varphi(s,a),\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}

\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s,a)=\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t-1,H}(s,a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{M}_{t,h}^{-1}=(\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}+\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}))^{-1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}-\frac{\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}}{1+\left\langle\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}),\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}-\frac{\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}~.

We thus have

A

\displaystyle={\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}\right)-{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}\frac{\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}\right)=z_{t,h-1}~,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{B}&={\operatorname{tr}}\Bigg(\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}\\
&\quad-\frac{\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\operatorname{X}_{t,h-1}^{\top}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\Bigg)\\
&={\operatorname{tr}}\left(\vphantom{\frac{\left\langle A,\widehat{B}^{\top}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}}{\sigma^{2}_{\varphi}}}\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h-1}-\Theta_{P})^{\top}\right.\\
&\quad\left.-\frac{\left\langle\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}),\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h-1}-\Theta_{P})^{\top}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\right)\\

&=\frac{\left\langle(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h-1}-\Theta_{P})\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}),\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}~,\quad\text{and}\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{C}&=\left\lVert\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}^{2}\Bigg(\left\langle\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}),\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}\\
&\qquad-\left\langle\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}),\frac{\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}\Bigg)\\
&=\left\lVert\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}^{2}\left(\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\frac{\lambda^{-2}\sigma^{4}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\right)\\

Putting these together, we have

\displaystyle z_{t,h}

\displaystyle\leqslant z_{t,h-1}+2\frac{\left\langle(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h-1}-\Theta_{P})\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t}),\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}+\left\lVert\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\left\lVert\Theta_{P}\right\rVert_{\HS}^{2}+2\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}\frac{\left\langle(\widehat{\Theta}_{\tau,j-1}-\Theta_{P})\varphi(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau}),\varepsilon_{j}^{\tau}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}\left\lVert\varepsilon_{j}^{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})}~.

For any

Note that

\displaystyle|y_{t,h}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{E}_{t,h-1}\left\lVert\varepsilon_{h}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\frac{\left\lVert(\widehat{\Theta}_{t,h-1}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{\HS}\left\lVert\operatorname{M}_{t,h-1}^{-1/2}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t,h-1}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}

\displaystyle\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}|y_{\tau,j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}4\beta^{2}_{\tau,j-1}(\delta)\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})

\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}y_{\tau,j}\leqslant\sqrt{16(1+\lambda^{-1})\beta^{2}_{t,h}(\delta)\log(\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t,h})^{1/2})\log(2t^{2}H/\delta)}\leqslant\beta^{2}_{t,h}(\delta)/4~,

(18)

Now, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle z_{t,h}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\left\lVert\Theta_{P}\right\rVert_{\HS}^{2}+2\!\!\!\!\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}\!\!\!\!\mathcal{E}_{\tau,j-1}\frac{\left\langle(\widehat{\Theta}_{\tau,j-1}-\Theta_{P})\varphi(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau}),\varepsilon_{j}^{\tau}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})}

\displaystyle\quad+\!\!\!\!\sum_{(\tau,j)\leqslant(t,h)}\!\!\!\!\left\lVert\varepsilon_{j}^{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})}{1+\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,\tau,j-1}(s_{j}^{\tau},a_{j}^{\tau})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\left\lVert\Theta_{P}\right\rVert_{\HS}^{2}+\beta^{2}_{t,h}(\delta)/2+8(1+\lambda^{-1})\log(\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t,h})^{1/2})\leqslant\beta^{2}_{t,h}(\delta)~,

as
\beta^{2}_{t,h}(\delta)\geqslant 2\lambda\left\lVert\Theta_{P}\right\rVert_{\HS}^{2}+16(1+\lambda^{-1})\log(\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,t,h})^{1/2})
. Now the proof follows from
Equation 18
and noting that

\left\lVert(\widehat{\Theta}_{t}-\Theta_{P})\operatorname{M}_{t}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{\HS}=z_{t-1,H}^{1/2}\leqslant\beta_{t-1,H}(\delta)\leqslant\beta_{t}(\delta)
.

B.2
Regret analysis of
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}

To prove the regret bound in
Theorem 2
, we establish a sequence of intermediate results to bound the performance gap between the optimal policy and the policy followed by
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
. In
Lemma 1
, we start with a control on the
Q
-function estimates
Q_{h}^{t}
, which in turn leads to the regret bound, as our policy is based on
Q_{h}^{t}
. The result implies that as long as the true transition distribution lies in the confidence set
C_{t}
, the
Q
-updates are optimistic estimates of the optimal
Q
-values and thus, allow us to pick an optimistic action while sufficiently exploring the state space.

Lemma 1

(Optimism)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
. Then,
Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)\leqslant Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)
for all
h
, and
(s,a)
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
h
. When
h=H
, the inequality holds by definition. Now we assume that the lemma holds for some
h^{\prime}=h+1
, where
1\leqslant h<H
. This implies that for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

V_{h+1}^{t}(s)=\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h+1}^{t}(s,a)]\right\}\geqslant\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h+1}^{\star}(s,a)]\right\}=V^{\star}_{h+1}(s)~.

We then have, for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, that

\displaystyle Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{\star}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\max_{\Theta_{P^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P^{\prime}(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{\star}(X)\right]\leqslant Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)~,

where the third step follows from
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Gap between optimistic and actual values)
.

Let
g_{h}^{t}(s)=V_{h}^{t}(s)-V_{h}^{\pi^{t}}(s)
, and
m_{h}^{t}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[g_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]-g_{h+1}^{t}(s_{h+1}^{t})
. Then

\displaystyle g_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})\leqslant\sum\nolimits_{h=1}^{H}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\left(R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)+\sum\nolimits_{h=1}^{H}m_{h}^{t}~.

Proof.

Note that
a_{h}^{t}=\pi_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a)
. Therefore

\displaystyle V_{h}^{\pi^{t}}(s_{h}^{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{\pi^{t}}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[g_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]-g_{h+1}^{t}(s_{h+1}^{t})-m_{h}^{t}~.

We also have
V_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t})=\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a)\right\}=\min\left\{H,Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\right\}\leqslant Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
.
Therefore,
g_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t})\leqslant Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\left(R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)+g_{h+1}^{t}(s_{h+1}^{t})+m_{h}^{t}
.
Since
g_{H+1}^{t}(s)=0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, a simple recursion over all
h\in[H]
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3

(Cumulative regret expressed through
Q
-estimates)
.

Let
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
for all
t\geqslant 1
. Then for any
\delta\in(0,1]
, the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta/2:

\mathcal{R}(N)\leqslant\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\left(R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}~.

Proof.

If
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
for all
t
, we have from Lemma
1
that

\forall t\geqslant 1,\quad V_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})=\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t},a)\right\}\geqslant\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{1}^{\star}(s_{1}^{t},a)\right\}=V^{\star}_{1}(s_{1}^{t})~.

Therefore the cumulative regret after
N=TH
steps is given by

\mathcal{R}(N)=\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\left[V^{\star}_{1}(s_{1}^{t})-V^{\pi^{t}}_{1}(s_{1}^{t})\right]\leqslant\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})-V_{1}^{\pi^{t}}(s_{1}^{t})\right)=\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}g_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})~.

We then have from Lemma
2
that

\mathcal{R}(N)\leqslant\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\left(R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)+\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}m_{h}^{t}~.

Note that
(m_{h}^{t})_{t,h}
is a martingale difference sequence adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t,h}
with
|m_{h}^{t}|\leqslant 2H
. Hence, by Azuma-Hoeffding inequality, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,
\sum_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}m_{h}^{t}\leqslant 2H\sqrt{2TH\log(2/\delta)}=2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}
,
which proves the result.
∎

Lemma 4

(Error in
Q
-estimates)
.

Let
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
.
Then, for all
h\leqslant H
and
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)-\left(R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)\leqslant 2B_{V}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

Proof.

For
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
, uniformly over all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the mean embeddings satisfy

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}-\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\Theta_{P}-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})\varphi(s,a)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\Theta_{P}-\widehat{\Theta}_{t})(\widehat{\operatorname{C}}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{1/2}\right\rVert\left\lVert(\widehat{\operatorname{C}}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1/2}\varphi(s,a)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\left\langle\varphi(s,a),(\widehat{\operatorname{C}}_{\varphi,t}+\lambda\operatorname{I})^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}^{1/2}=\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

Now, by
Assumption 1
,
V_{h+1}^{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. Hence, the
Q
-estimates (
Equation 13
) can written as
Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)=R(s,a)+\left\langle V_{h+1}^{t},\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}+B_{V}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{t}(s,a)-\left(R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle V_{h+1}^{t},\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}-\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}+B_{V}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)\leqslant 2B_{V}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~,

where the last step holds since
\left\lVert V_{h+1}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\leqslant B_{V}
and
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
.
∎

Lemma 5

(Sum of predictive variances)
.

Let
\sup_{s,a}\sqrt{k\left((s,a),(s,a)\right)}\leqslant B_{\varphi}
. Then

\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\leqslant(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)\log\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,T+1})~.

Proof.

We have from
Equation 9
that
\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s,a)={\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\otimes\varphi(s,a)\right)
.
We also note that
\operatorname{M}_{t}\geqslant\lambda\operatorname{I}
and
\varphi(s,a)\otimes\varphi(s,a)\leqslant B_{\varphi}^{2}\operatorname{I}
. Therefore
\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}\varphi(s,a)\otimes\varphi(s,a)\leqslant\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}\operatorname{I}
. Now, since
\operatorname{M}_{t+1}=\operatorname{M}_{t}+\sum_{h\leqslant H}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})
, we have

\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}=\big(\operatorname{I}+\operatorname{M}_{t}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{h\leqslant H}\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\otimes\varphi(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})\big)\operatorname{M}_{t+1}^{-1}\preceq(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)\operatorname{M}_{t+1}^{-1}~.

We then have

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T}{\operatorname{tr}}\left(\operatorname{M}_{t+1}^{-1}(\operatorname{M}_{t+1}-\operatorname{M}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T}\log\frac{\det(\operatorname{M}_{t+1})}{\det(\operatorname{M}_{t})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)\log\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{M}_{T+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)\log\det(\operatorname{I}+\lambda^{-1}\operatorname{K}_{\varphi,T+1})~.

Here, in the second step we have used that for two positive definite operators
\operatorname{A}
and
\operatorname{B}
such that
\operatorname{A}-\operatorname{B}
is positive
semi-definite,
{\operatorname{tr}}(\operatorname{A}^{-1}(\operatorname{A}-\operatorname{B}))\leqslant\log\frac{\det(\operatorname{A})}{\det(\operatorname{B})}
. The last step follows from Sylvester’s determinant identity.
∎

Based on the results in this section, we can finally derive a proof for
Theorem 2
.

B.2.1
Proof of
Theorem 2

We have from Lemma
3
and
4
that if
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
for all
t
, then with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, the cumulative regret

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(N)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2B_{V}\beta_{T}(\delta/2)\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2B_{V}\alpha_{N,\delta}\sqrt{TH\sum\nolimits_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2B_{V}\alpha_{N,\delta}\sqrt{2(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)N\gamma_{N}}+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}~.

The first step follows since
\beta_{t}(\delta)
increases with
t
, the second step is due to Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality and the fact that
\beta_{T}(\delta/2)\leqslant\alpha_{N,\delta}
, and the final step follows from
Lemma 5
. The proof now can be completed using
Theorem 1
and taking a union bound.

B.3
Regret analysis of
\mathrm{CME}\text{-}\mathrm{RL}
under model misspecification

To prove the regret bound in
Theorem 3
, we follow the similar arguments used in proving
Theorem 2
, but with necessary modifications taking the effect of the misspecification error
\zeta
into account. We first derive the following result.

Lemma 6

(Error in approximate Q-values)
.

Let
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
. Then, for all
h\in[H]
and
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, we have that:

\displaystyle Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)-\left(R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+2\zeta~.

Proof.

Note that the
Q
-estimates (
Equation 15
) can rewritten as

Q_{h}^{t}(s,a):=R(s,a)+\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}v_{h+1}^{t}+\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

By
Assumption 3
, there exists a function
\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
such that
\|V_{h+1}^{t}-\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\zeta
. We now define the vector
{\tilde{v}}_{h+1}^{t}:=[\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}(s_{h^{\prime}+1}^{\tau})]_{\tau<t,h^{\prime}\leqslant H}
and introduce the shorthand notation
\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[f]:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(X)]
.
We then have

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{v}_{h+1}^{t}-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{\tilde{v}}_{h+1}^{t}+\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}({v}_{h+1}^{t}-{\tilde{v}}_{h+1}^{t})-\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}]+\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}-V_{h+1}^{t}]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{\tilde{v}}_{h+1}^{t}-\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}]\right|+\left\lVert\alpha_{t}(s,a)\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert V_{h+1}^{t}-\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}-V_{h+1}^{t}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{\tilde{v}}_{h+1}^{t}-\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}]\right|+\zeta\left(1+\left\lVert\alpha_{t}(s,a)\right\rVert_{1}\right)~,

(19)

which follows by an application of Hölder’s inequality. Now, as
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
and
\left\lVert\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\leqslant B_{V}
, the following also holds:

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{\tilde{v}}_{h+1}^{t}-\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t}]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\tilde{V}_{h+1}^{t},\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}(s,a)-\vartheta_{P}(s,a)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant B_{V}\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

(20)

By
Assumption 4
, the constant function
1:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is an element of
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
. For stationary (radial) kernels
k_{\varphi}
, we have
[\alpha_{t}(s,a)]_{\tau,h}\geqslant 0
,
\forall\tau\leqslant t,h\leqslant H
. Now, as
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\alpha}_{t}(s,a)\right\rVert_{1}=\left\langle\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)},1\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)},1\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}+\left\langle\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}-\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)},1\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[1(X)]+\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\left\lVert\widehat{\vartheta}_{t}^{(s,a)}-\vartheta_{P}^{(s,a)}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}

\displaystyle=1+\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)~.

(21)

Combining
Equation 20
and
Equation 21
with
Equation 19
yields:

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{v}_{h+1}^{t}-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)(X)}[V_{h+1}^{t}]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant B_{V}\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+\zeta\left(2+\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+2\zeta~.

(22)

Finally, the result follows by noting that

\begin{split}&Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)-\left(R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)\\
&\leqslant\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{v}_{h+1}^{t}-\mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|s,a)}[V_{h+1}^{t}]+\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)\\
&\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+2\zeta~,\end{split}

which concludes the proof.
∎

With the result above, we can derive an upper bound on the optimal value
Q^{\star}_{h}
as follows.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
. Then
\forall h\in[H],\;\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},\quad Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)\leqslant Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)+2(H-h)\zeta
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
h
. When
h=H
, the result holds by definition. Now assume that it holds for some
h^{\prime}=h+1
, where
1\leqslant h<H
. This implies that

\displaystyle\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\quad V_{h+1}^{t}(s)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h+1}^{t}(s,a)]\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\left\{H,\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h+1}^{\star}(s,a)]\right\}-2(H-h-1)\zeta=V^{\star}_{h+1}(s)-2(H-h-1)\zeta~.

We then have, for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, that

\displaystyle Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{\star}(X)\right]

(23)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]+2(H-h-1)\zeta~.

Using
Equation 22
in the proof of
Lemma 6
, we now see that

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)]\leqslant\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{v}_{h+1}^{t}+\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+2\zeta

which holds as
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
. We then have from
Equation 23
that

\begin{split}Q^{\star}_{h}(s,a)&\leqslant R(s,a)+\alpha_{t}(s,a)^{\top}{v}_{h+1}^{t}+\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s,a)+2(H-h)\zeta\\
&=Q^{t}_{h}(s,a)+2(H-h)\zeta~,\end{split}

which follows from the definition of
Q
-estimates.
∎

Given
Lemma 6
and
Lemma 7
, we can finally prove
Theorem 3
.

B.3.1
Proof of
Theorem 3

If
\Theta_{P}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
for all
t\geqslant 1
, we have from
Lemma 7
that
V_{1}^{\star}(s_{1}^{t})\leqslant V_{1}^{t}(s_{1}^{t})+2(H-1)\zeta
.
Then following similar steps as in the proof of
Theorem 2
and
Lemma 3
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(N)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}Q_{h}^{t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})-\left(R(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P(\cdot|s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}\left[V_{h+1}^{t}(X)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}+2\zeta T(H-1)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\sum_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1/2}\beta_{t}(\delta/2)\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+2\zeta TH+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}

\displaystyle\quad+2\zeta T(H-1)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\beta_{T}(\delta/2)\sum_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1/2}\sigma_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})+4\zeta TH+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\alpha_{N,\delta}\sqrt{TH\sum_{t\leqslant T,h\leqslant H}\lambda^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\varphi,t}(s_{h}^{t},a_{h}^{t})}+4\zeta TH+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(B_{V}+\zeta\left\lVert 1\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\psi}}\right)\alpha_{N,\delta}\sqrt{2(1+\lambda^{-1}B_{\varphi}^{2}H)N\gamma_{N}}+4\zeta N+2H\sqrt{2N\log(2/\delta)}~.

The second step follows from
Lemma 6
, the third step from monotonicity of
\beta_{t}(\delta)
with
t
, the fourth step is due to Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality and the fact that
\beta_{T}(\delta/2)\leqslant\alpha_{N,\delta}
, and the final step follows from
Lemma 5
. The proof now can be completed using
Theorem 1
and taking a union bound.
