Comparing Apples to Oranges: LLM-powered Multimodal Intention Prediction in an Object Categorization Task
Hassan Ali, Philipp Allgeuer, Stefan Wermter

Abstract
Human intention-based systems enable robots to perceive and interpret user actions to interact with humans and adapt to their behavior proactively. Therefore, intention prediction is pivotal in creating a natural interaction with social robots in human-designed environments. In this paper, we examine using Large Language Models (LLMs) to infer human intention in a collaborative object categorization task with a physical robot. We propose a novel multimodal approach that integrates user non-verbal cues, like hand gestures, body poses, and facial expressions, with environment states and user verbal cues to predict user intentions in a hierarchical architecture. Our evaluation of five LLMs shows the potential for reasoning about verbal and non-verbal user cues, leveraging their context-understanding and real-world knowledge to support intention prediction while collaborating on a task with a social robot. Introduction

With the rapid AI advancements, social robots are playing increasingly prominent roles in human societies. Unlike traditional robots, social robots are designed to facilitate a natural and intuitive interaction with humans, resembling human-human interaction rather than solely relying on explicit commands
[
8
]
. Human intention prediction is a core aspect of such seamless interaction and for joint human-robot task completion. It involves perceiving subtle cues from human actions, gestures, and speech to infer underlying goals
[
28
]
. However, it is challenging due to the complexity and context-driven nature of human behavior, where intentions are conveyed multimodally with verbal and non-verbal cues.

Typical intention-based systems require collecting a vast amount of data, which is impractical and restrictive, limiting the system to specific use cases, modalities, or setups
[
14
]
. Recent work with LLMs in robotics has demonstrated their zero-shot abstract reasoning skills. Besides language proficiency, LLMs have exhibited notable inferential skills, broad real-world knowledge, and robust reasoning
[
23
]
. Also, they are performant at capturing context, making them a candidate for advancing intention prediction in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI). However, LLMs depend on textual input (user queries and prompts) to generate contextually relevant responses. Thus, it is challenging in robots for LLMs to replace human-aware models during the interaction
[
26
]
, even with the subsequent generation of multimodal LLMs, which still face difficulties processing and understanding basic visual information patterns
[
20
]
. Thus, a reliable method for equipping LLMs with multimodal awareness of human cues is still missing.

In this work, we use the Neuro-Inspired COLlaborator
[
10
]
(NICOL) robot (see Fig.
1
) to integrate multimodal user cues in an LLM-powered intent reasoning framework. We process user non-verbal cues by leveraging various machine learning vision pipelines and verbal commands via an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) system. In a two-layered processing method (
perceptive reasoning
and
task reasoning
), we use a text-based LLM to reason about the combined cues for identifying current user actions and anticipating user goals. We evaluate our system with a collaborative task for object categorization. Our experiments confirm that LLMs are viable assets for competent intention prediction. Overall, our work contributes to the integration of LLMs in robotics as well as human intention prediction in human-robot collaboration, effectively bridging the gap between machine understanding and the subtleties of human communication.

The system extends our previous work for LLM grounding to behave and interact as the physical NICOL robot instead of an AI language model
[
3
]
. This is achieved by giving the LLM facts about the surrounding environment through the robot sensors and linking the LLM’s commonsense reasoning with the robot’s physical actions in a modular open architecture. The framework processes user queries (user speech) and generates responses in the first person, encoding robot actions without explicit programming. The responses are parsed into tokens recognized by the robot’s ROS API platform and then mapped to concrete robot actions. For example, the token
SAY
leads to generated speech, while
ACTION
is translated into a robotic manipulation using the robot’s arms or head (see Prompt
3
).

An overview of our system is shown in Fig.
2
. We use the core system components and robot actions as in our previous work
[
3
]
, where the LLM is grounded by initial prompts, the environment state is collected through the ViLD
[
6
]
open-vocabulary object detector, and the user speech is recognized using Whisper
[
17
]
. Also, the robot can perform a set of known actions like giving, grasping, and looking at objects as well as displaying various facial expressions. While our previous work focused on open verbal communication with an embodied agent, we contribute new modules for interpreting user non-verbal cues as well as a task reasoning framework for combining user multimodal cues to predict intentions. Our proposed method for intention prediction is a dual process consisting of perceptive reasoning and task reasoning. In perceptive reasoning, the robot collects and analyzes information about the user’s non-verbal state and connects it to the proximate environment, thus formulating a set of “beliefs” guiding its behavior. Task reasoning incorporates explicit commands given by the user (user queries), given task prompts, and the history of the interaction in order to combine all inputs in a final LLM reasoning step for generating appropriate robot actions. The system consists of various ROS nodes, which communicate textually through designated topics with the LLM as a central component for information processing, allowing data streamlining in a standardized and uniform manner.

3.1
NICOL Robot and Environment Overview

We use NICOL
[
10
]
, a semi-humanoid robot developed at the Knowledge Technology Group of the University of Hamburg. NICOL is an adult-sized robot fixed on a tabletop and has two arms and a humanoid head. Each arm has a five-fingered hand and supports bio-inspired motion planning
[
7
]
to interact with adult-sized objects. Although NICOL’s design allows precise physical interaction with the environment, it also features social cues like robot facial expressions, gestures, and gaze, making it suitable for both social interaction and efficient collaboration with humans. The head features several human-like facial expressions: happiness, sadness, surprise, and anger. The NICOL API is built on top of ROS, which facilitates the integration of Python-based tools and models.

3.2
Non-verbal Cues Detection and Recognition

Human-aware robotic systems require perceiving not only user speech but also non-verbal cues
[
1
]
. Thus, detecting and recognizing human gestures and body language are essential components of our approach. Due to the real-time demands of robotic systems, we utilize the open-source Tasks API of MediaPipe (MP)
[
15
]
, which offers GPU-compatible computer vision models. MP was originally developed for mobile devices; however, our implementation using the NICOL robot shows its suitability for setups where the user collaborates with the robot in its proximate environment. MP constructs a pipeline of directed graphs that manage various data streams and perform specific calculations like image transformation, limb detection, limb tracking, or a machine learning inferential task using TensorFlow and Caffe, transforming the sensory input into body landmarks. We construct three MP pipelines to extract hand gestures, body poses, and facial expressions. The camera input is fed into different ROS nodes of the detection modules, extracting joint keypoints of the hand, pose, and face. Then, the user joints of each detection module are passed into a corresponding recognition module, each generating output as basic textual tokens about the non-verbal cues as expected by the LLM, e.g., the detected limb and recognized movement.

Hand Gestures

after detecting the user’s hands with a single-shot Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), MP predicts 21 3D keypoints based on the GHUM model for human shapes and poses, including four for each finger and one for the wrist. We utilize these landmarks to train a gesture recognition model based on a two-layered feedforward network for the following gestures: pointing, holding an object, and resting. Pointing is with an extended arm and index finger indicating an object on the table or the table itself, holding is with the fingers curled around an object, and we define resting as the hand positioned on the side of the table. Our gesture module can robustly recognize the user’s gesture using the left hand, right hand, or both. Also, the gesture detector can locate hands at various distances but we focus on the hand movements while the user collaborates with the robot using a set of objects on a table. Therefore, we consider proximate gestures that the user does with the hands over the table or within a defined physical area near the table’s edge and we filter out gestures far from the table.

Human-Pose

MP estimates user poses in a top-down way by detecting the human in the frame with an optimized MobileNetV2. User poses are estimated with 33 landmarks representing the joints with a BlazePose model. The extracted skeleton is mapped into 3D coordinates of the torso, arms, legs, and head. We utilize the 3D keypoints to provide the system with two features. First, we track the user’s location in real-time relative to the robot using an average of the head keypoints. Therefore, the system can perform human-aware actions like looking at the user and lively interacting with the user based on their location in the workspace. Second, we use the pose’s location to detect user proximity relative to the robot’s table, i.e., whether the user is present in front of the robot.

Facial Expressions

we utilize MP’s face detector which uses a BlazeFace model based on a single shot detector to identify the face region (user’s eyes, mouth, and nose) and convert it into a face mesh model. The model outputs 478 keypoints representing 3D face landmarks and a Blendshape model with 52 scores representing various facial features. We use these facial features to train a simple two-layered feedforward network for recognizing the user’s neutral and smiling facial expressions, which allows the robot to react to basic user emotional states.

3.3
Perceptive Reasoning

The textual tokens generated by the non-verbal recognition modules are alone insufficient for predicting intentions since they are not grounded in the context of the interaction and setup. Since the LLM initial prompts contain information about the robot’s sensory perception, surrounding environment, and possible multimodal user cues, the LLM can transform these textual tokens into coherent and contextually relevant phrases. For example, when the LLM is prompted as follows:
Convert the following pose cues into a single and short meaning sentence describing the user
for the input of
[bodypose, stand, in front]
, it generates output like “the user is standing in front of me”. The LLM also has knowledge of the environment state provided as a real-time information stream of objects on the robot’s table from the ViLD open-vocabulary object detector. Therefore, it comprehends the relationship between the user’s gesture and objects on the table, when applicable. For example, the following cues about the user gesture:
[right hand, point, apple]
are converted to “the user is indicating the apple with their right hand”. Fig.
3
summarizes the steps of perceptive reasoning with concrete examples. For this process, we consider proximate gestures only and the prompts are internal, invisible to the user, and meant to convert user states into meaningful textual representations for serving the next task reasoning step.

3.4
Task Reasoning

During a user-robot collaboration, the system considers the user’s non-verbal states from the perceptive reasoning step and user verbal cues, collected as utterances from an ASR system based on Whisper, while acting to achieve a mutual goal. Goals can be either open-ended or defined as tasks described in the form of
task prompts
, which are exchangeable and expandable in a modular way. Prompt
3.3
shows a task prompt example for categorizing objects, where the goal is to collaborate with the user to sort the objects on the table into two distinct groups by moving them from the center of the table to the right and left side from the robot’s perspective, representing category one and category two. When reasoning about the task, the system combines the user’s non-verbal states, user explicit commands, i.e., user queries, and any given task definitions to prompt the LLM. The output of this step is a combination of robot actions like arm manipulation, head manipulation, facial expressions, and generated speech. We show an example in Prompt
3.3
where the system must infer user-intended object categories without being explicitly told. After the user places a fruit and container on each side of the table, they instruct the robot to sort a specific object. The system then combines the environment state (location of objects on the table), user non-verbal cues (pointing gesture to the banana), user verbal cue (sorting command), and the defined categorizing task, to predict the user intention (sorting distinct fruits and containers) and generate an appropriate object manipulation action (moving the banana to the left side of the table).

4
Experiments and Evaluation

Following the proposed hierarchical architecture, the system can infer user goals and intentions in various open-ended scenarios while socially interacting with the user. For example, the robot can assist the user by giving suitable intended objects (see Prompt
3.3
). Also, our system exhibits various additional useful features, such as establishing joint object attention between the user and robot, utilizing the LLM’s language proficiency to perform conversation repairs, and identifying object ambiguities (see Prompt
3.3
), all while interacting naturally and intuitively with hand gestures. The robot can also identify user proximity, i.e., their location relative to the robot, thus initiating the interaction and playing both active and passive roles, while reacting socially to user facial expressions (cf. Prompt
3.3
). Since evaluating open-ended interactions is challenging, we test our system through a well-defined object categorization task, as explained next.

4.1
Object Categorization Task: Results and Discussion

In our task, the user and robot collaboratively categorize objects on the table into two distinct groups. The robot assists the user in finding the fit for each “uncategorized” object based on common properties and characteristics, like shape, color, and purpose. Unlike typical autonomous object categorization tasks in which categories are known
[
11
,
16
,
18
]
, the robot is not given an explicit definition of the categories but should infer it based on user actions. The task starts as the user places one object on each side of the table, i.e., representing
Category 1
and
Category 2
. Then, the robot responds to user queries about where to place a specific uncategorized object. An example of the categorization task is in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:
A concrete workflow example of the object categorization task. After the user moves an object to each side of the table, the robot assists in categorizing the remaining objects, e.g., the banana is sorted with the lemon since both are yellow fruits.

We evaluate the task with six objects: red apple, yellow banana, red can, yellow lemon, red bowl, and red cup. We select the objects due to their various purposes (fruits and containers), colors (red and yellow), and shapes (round and cylindrical). For performance comparison, we start the task with every possible object pair as category initiators. After category initialization, the robot is asked to sort a randomly selected object. We conduct 150 trials, containing every possible object pair, each with a reset chat history. Moreover, we evaluate the system using OpenAI and open-weight LLMs: GPT-3.5-turbo-0301, GPT-3.5-turbo-16k-0613, GPT-4-0613, Vicuna-1.3-33b, and Mistral-7b-instruct-v0.2. We follow a three-phase evaluation scheme (see Prompt
3.3
): 1) Task Understanding Check: five prompts testing the LLM’s task understanding and categories at hand, 2) Object Categorization Check: for each “uncategorized” object, a prompt is given as a categorization request (four prompts in total), and 3) Intention Understanding Check, in which the LLM’s understanding of user intentions and goals is tested with two prompts. The LLM is also expected to logically explain its decisions and actions. A summary of the results is in Table
1
with values representing model accuracy, averaged by the number of trials and object pairs.

Our system exhibited proficiency in predicting user goals and deriving intended categories of the various fruits and containers, regardless of the comparison order, especially GPT-4, with highly consistent accuracy. The performance of the GPT-3.5 models was comparable and dropped slightly when comparing objects with similar functions (fruit vs. fruit or container vs. container), indicating these models gave more importance to “usage” over other attributes. Nonetheless, GPT-4 showed competence and flexibility with category criteria that relied on subtle features like color or shape. All OpenAI models showed good task realization in Phase 1 and the occasional accuracy drop is explained by accidentally mixing the category location (swapping right and left), hinting that the LLM spatial reasoning and orientation have not yet progressed to a performant level.

The open-weight models came short in Phase 1 and had near-average accuracy in Phases 2 and 3. Mistral showed higher competence in Phase 2 than Vicuna despite the smaller size (7B vs. 33B parameters), which we hypothesize to be a result of its own training, i.e., Mistral is fine-tuned on instructions data making it more flexible with user commands, while Vicuna is fine-tuned on conversation data. However, our results showed a positive correlation between the model’s task understanding in Phase 1 and its performance in subsequent phases (cf. Table
2
). This becomes clearer by observing the error rates (see Fig.

), which are lower for
Category Reasoning
than grounding factors (
Category Definition
and
Uncategorized Objects
). Category Reasoning is the LLM’s ability to correctly identify what a collection of objects in a certain category have in common, while Category Definition and Uncategorized Objects represent errors in the LLM’s understanding of the categorization task (evaluation prompts of phase 1).

From the error rates, we observe that the smaller models like Vicuna and Mistral are more challenging to ground to a task definition, which negatively influences their performance. However, all LLMs showed high success rates at interpreting non-verbal cues through our perceptive reasoning with marginal error rates for Mistral and Vicuna (see
Perceptive Reasoning
in Fig.

Although ChatGPT models showed better resilience in task grounding, the results showcased that the highest error rates were related to not fully grasping some task-related concepts rather than the categorization itself, suggesting that the model’s reasoning aligns with the task objectives. Overall, our experiment showed the system’s ability to perform the assigned task, while the influence of grounding errors in smaller LLMs is expected to be mitigated as more advanced models become widely available.

5
Conclusion

We explored LLMs for predicting human intention, showing their ability to infer user goals and generate discrete actions to fulfill them in a human-robot collaborative task. We introduced a novel hierarchical dual process for predicting user intentions and anticipating goals by leveraging user verbal and non-verbal cues. Our perceptive reasoning module showed the high capacity of LLMs to multimodal user social cues. Through our task reasoning, the system was also able to adapt to an object categorization task, in a few-shot manner. Task prompts can be easily replaced or extended, highlighting the system’s potential for rapid task acquisition and deployment in diverse settings, thus laying the groundwork for effective intention prediction in robotic collaborative and social scenarios. Future work will assess the LLM scalability across a broader range of interactive scenarios to ascertain the transferability of its intention prediction skills across multiple tasks. We will also examine the applicability of multimodal LLMs for inferring user gestures and poses in real-time social interaction with the robot.
