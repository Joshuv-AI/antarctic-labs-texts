The random Weierstrass zeta function II. Fluctuations of the electric flux through rectifiable curves
Mikhail Sodin, Aron Wennman and Oren Yakir

Abstract
Consider a random planar point process whose law is invariant under planar isometries. We think of the process as a random distribution of point charges and consider the electric field generated by the charge distribution. In Part I of this work, we found a condition on the spectral side which characterizes when the field itself is invariant with a well-defined second-order structure. Here, we fix a process with an invariant field, and study the fluctuations of the flux through large arcs and curves in the plane. Under suitable conditions on the process and on the curve, denoted Γ \Gamma , we show that the asymptotic variance of the flux through R ​ Γ R\,\Gamma grows like R R times the signed length of Γ \Gamma . As a corollary, we find that the charge fluctuations in a dilated Jordan domain is asymptotic with the perimeter, provided only that the boundary is rectifiable. The proof is based on the asymptotic analysis of a closely related quantity (the complex electric action of the field along a curve). A decisive role in the analysis is played by a signed version of the classical Ahlfors regularity condition.



1
Introduction

1.1
Electric fields and charge fluctuations

Denote by
\Lambda
a stationary random point process in the
complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
with intensity
c_{\Lambda}

(i.e. the mean number of points of
\Lambda
per unit area).
If we think of
\Lambda
as a random distribution of identical point charges,
each sample generates an electric field which we identify
with a solution
V_{\Lambda}
to the equation

\bar{\partial}\,V_{\Lambda}=\pi\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\delta_{\lambda}-\pi c_{\Lambda}\hskip 1.0ptm,

(1.1)

where
\bar{\partial}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+{\rm i}\,\partial_{y})

and where
m
is the Lebesgue measure.
In
[
21
]
(henceforth referred to as Part I),
we found that, for processes with a spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
,
the spectral condition

\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}<\infty

(1.2)

characterizes those processes
\Lambda
for which
there exists a
stationary
electric field
V_{\Lambda}
with a
well-defined second-order (covariance) structure.
When it exists, the field is given by

V_{\Lambda}(z)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{|\lambda|<R}\frac{1}{z-\lambda}-\pi c_{\Lambda}\hskip 1.0pt\overline{z},

(1.3)

and furthermore, it is essentially unique. In addition,
the covariance structure of
V_{\Lambda}
is conveniently
expressed in terms of the covariance structure of
\Lambda
.
The field
V_{\Lambda}
can be seen as a random analogue of
the Weierstrass zeta function from the theory of elliptic functions.

Denote by
n_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{G})=\#(\Lambda\cap\mathcal{G})
the
counting measure (or “charge”)
of a Borel set
\mathcal{G}
.
The asymptotic variance
{\sf Var}[n_{\Lambda}(R\mathcal{G})]

of the charge fluctuations as
R\to\infty

is of central interest in statistical physics.
For the Poisson process, the size of the charge
fluctuations in a disk is proportional to the
area, while for more negatively correlated point processes
the fluctuations tend to be suppressed
and grow like
o(R^{2})
. Following Torquato-Stillinger, such
processes are called
hyperuniform
or

super-homogeneous
; see
[
23
]
.
Classical examples of hyperuniform point processes
include the Ginibre ensemble
[
8
]
and the zero set
of the Gaussian Entire Function (GEF)
[
3
]
.
For both these examples,
the variance of
n_{\Lambda}(R{\mathbb{D}})
grows like the perimeter
R
.
It is curious to ask
to what extent the geometry of the disk
is important, and in particular, what role is played by boundary
regularity
.

Since the divergence theorem expresses the charge in a domain as
the electric flux through its boundary, it seems natural
to ask for the behavior of the fluctuations of the flux
of the field
V_{\Lambda}
through more general rectifiable curves, which need not be
closed nor simple. Specifically, given
a rectifiable curve
\Gamma
with unit normal
N
, we
would like to describe the asymptotic variance of

\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{R\Gamma}V_{\Lambda}(z)\cdot N\,|{\rm d}z|

for large
R
, provided that
\Lambda
satisfies (
1.2
).
To isolate the effect of the geometry of
\Gamma
,
we will make the assumption that, in addition to being stationary, the
law of the point process
\Lambda
is invariant
under rotations. We refer to such a process
simply as “invariant”.

1.2
The electric action

For an invariant point process
\Lambda
subject to
the spectral condition (
1.2
),
we introduce the
electric action

\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Gamma}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z

(1.4)

where
{{\rm d}z}
denotes the usual holomorphic
1
-differential
and where
\Gamma
is an arbitrary rectifiable curve in the plane.
When
\Gamma
bounds a Jordan domain
\mathcal{G}
,
the action coincides with the flux, which in
turn equals

2{\rm i}
times
n_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{G})-c_{\Lambda}m(\mathcal{G})
.
In general, if
T
and
N
denote the unit tangent and normal vectors
to
\Gamma
, respectively, we obtain a decomposition

\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Gamma}\Big(V_{\Lambda}\cdot T+{\rm i}\,V_{\Lambda}\cdot N\Big)\,|{\rm d}z|\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)+{\rm i}\,\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)

of
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)

in terms of the flux
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
and the
work
\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
of the field along

\Gamma
. While we are mainly interested in flux, the
action appears to be more natural from an analytical
point of view.
Note that the work
\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
vanishes when the curve is closed.
It appears that in the general situation, the fluctuations of
\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)

are negligible compared with those of the flux
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)

(see Theorem
1.6
below).

1.3
Main results

We assume throughout that
\Lambda
has a finite second moment,
that is, that
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\Lambda}(B)^{2}\right]<\infty
for any bounded Borel set
B
.
Under this assumption, there exists a measure
\kappa_{\Lambda}

(
the reduced covariance measure
) with the property that

{\sf Cov}\left[n_{\Lambda}(\varphi),n_{\Lambda}(\psi)\right]=\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}\varphi(z)\,\overline{\psi(z^{\prime})}\,{\rm d}\kappa_{\Lambda}(z-z^{\prime})\,{\rm d}m(z)

for any test functions
\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{C}})
,
where
n_{\Lambda}(\varphi)
is the standard
linear statistic given by

n_{\Lambda}(\varphi)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\varphi(\lambda)
.
The spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
is the Fourier transform of
\kappa_{\Lambda}

(understood in the sense of distributions).

We will also assume that
the reduced truncated two-point measure

\tau_{\Lambda}\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\kappa_{\Lambda}-c_{\Lambda}\delta_{0}
has
a density
k_{\Lambda}
with respect to planar Lebesgue
measure (the
truncated two-point function
).
Here,
c_{\Lambda}
is the intensity of
\Lambda
, and
\delta_{0}
is the unit
point mass at the origin.

We recall also the standing assumption that the stationary point process

\Lambda
is
invariant
, i.e., that the law of
\Lambda
is invariant under all
planar isometries. Under this assumption, the two-point function is
automatically radially symmetric, so that

\kappa_{\Lambda}(z)=k_{\Lambda}(|z|)\,{\rm d}m(z)+c_{\Lambda}\delta_{0}(z).

(1.5)

Our results will require that
(1+t^{2})k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)
.
Then, by [Part I, Remark 5.3], the spectral
measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
has a radial density
h_{\Lambda}
which is
C^{1}
-smooth.
Furthermore, if
\kappa_{\Lambda}({\mathbb{C}})=0
(i.e.,
\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}k_{\Lambda}(|z|)\,{\rm d}m(z)=-c_{\Lambda}
),
we have the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{2}\,{\rm d}t=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{h_{\Lambda}(\tau)}{\tau^{2}}\,{\rm d}\tau,

(1.6)

see Remark
5.1
below.
Hence, the above moment assumption implies that
\rho_{\Lambda}
satisfies
the spectral condition (
1.2
).

By a
curve
we mean a continuous map
\Gamma:I\to{\mathbb{C}}
of an interval
I
into the plane.
We identify two maps if they differ by pre-composition with an order-preserving
homeomorphism of two intervals.
We stress that
\Gamma
need not be injective;
if this is required we call the curve simple.
The curve
\Gamma
is said to be
rectifiable

if it has finite
length

|\Gamma|=\sup_{t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\Gamma(t_{j})-\Gamma(t_{j-1})\big|=\int_{I}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\,{\rm d}t<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all partitions
a\leq t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}\leq b
of
I=[a,b]
,
and where, without loss of generality, it is tacitly assumed
that
\gamma
is a Lipschitz parametrization of
\Gamma
, so that
\gamma^{\prime}
exists a.e..
When no confusion should arise, we will abuse terminology somewhat and
identify a curve with its image.
When referring to a particular parametrization,
we will use lowercase Greek letters, and write e.g.
\gamma:I\to\Gamma
.

The following regularity notion plays a key role in our analysis.

Definition 1.1

(Weak Ahlfors regularity)
.

We say that a rectifiable curve
\Gamma
is

weakly Ahlfors regular
if there exists
a constant
C_{\Gamma}<\infty
such that

\left|\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{\rm d}z\right|\leq C_{\Gamma}|\partial D|,

(1.7)

for any Euclidean disk
D\subset{\mathbb{C}}
.

The terminology is borrowed from the classical Ahlfors regularity
condition,
which asks that

\int_{\Gamma\cap D}|{\rm d}z|\leq C_{\Gamma}|\partial D|.

While rectifiable Jordan curves
may fail to be Ahlfors regular,
it turns out that they are always weakly Ahlfors;
see Lemma
3.1
. Informally speaking, the weak Ahlfors condition
is meant to prevent excessive spiralling near the end points of
\Gamma
, which appears
to be the main obstruction to linear growth of charge fluctuations.

:

The purple arcs
J_{1}\subset\Gamma
are traversed
twice and the green arc
J_{2}

is traversed three times; these contribute
4\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{1})

and
9\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{2})
, respectively, to the signed length
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.

Right:
For the signed length
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
,
the arc
K_{1}
(orange) is counted with a positive sign,
while
K_{2}
(purple) is counted with negative sign.

Definition 1.2

(Signed length)
.

The
signed length
of the intersection
of two rectifiable curves
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
is given by

\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\bigg(\sum_{s\in\gamma_{1}^{-1}(z),\;t\in\gamma_{2}^{-1}(z)}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)\cdot\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(t)\bigg)\,{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(z),

(1.8)

where
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
denote the
arc-length parametrizations of the two curves, i.e. parametrizations with

|\gamma^{\prime}_{j}|=1
a.e., and where
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is one-dimensional
Hausdorff measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
.

The quantity (
1.8
), which was introduced in

[
3
]
, measures the length of
the intersection
\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}
taking orientation and multiplicity into account;
see Figure
1
.
The signed length

\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
is finite for any two weakly
Ahlfors regular curves;
see Remark
3.3
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Denote by
\Lambda
an invariant
point process and let
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Gamma}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z
.
Assume that the two-point function
k_{\Lambda}

of
\Lambda

satisfies
(1+t^{2})k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)
together with the zeroth
moment condition

\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t\,{\rm d}t=-c_{\Lambda}.

(1.9)

Then, for any weakly Ahlfors regular
rectifiable curves
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}

we have that

{\sf Cov}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\,\Gamma_{1})\,,\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\,\Gamma_{2})\big]=R\left(C_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})

(1.10)

as
R\to\infty
, where
\displaystyle{C_{\Lambda}=-8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{2}\,{\rm d}t}
.

Note that the identity (
1.6
) makes it clear that
C_{\Lambda}
is positive.
Since Jordan curves are always weakly Ahlfors regular (see Lemma
3.1
),
we get the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
\Lambda
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem
1.3
. Then for any Jordan domain
\mathcal{G}

with rectifiable boundary, we have the asymptotics

{\sf Var}\left[n_{\Lambda}(R\mathcal{G})\right]=\tfrac{1}{4}R\left(C_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)|\partial\mathcal{G}|

(1.11)

as
R\to\infty
.

It should be mentioned that the fact that the zeroth-moment
condition (
1.9
) yields the suppressed charge fluctuations (
1.11
)
for domains
\mathcal{G}
with smooth boundaries was known a long time ago, see
[
14
, Section 1.6]

Remark 1.5
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.3
yields that the weak Ahlfors condition
can be relaxed to the maximal function criterion
for the pair of rectifiable curves
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
:

\sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq 1}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{t}\left|\int_{\Gamma_{1}\cap{\mathbb{D}}(\zeta,t)}{{\rm d}z}\right|{\rm d}t\in L^{1}(\Gamma_{2},|{\rm d}\zeta|)

without essential modifications. We have chosen to work
with the stronger condition in Definition
1.1

to make the proofs as transparent as possible, but by doing
so we miss out on a few examples where the
maximal function condition would be needed.
For instance, this seems to be the case for the “nested squares” curve

\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}2^{-k}\partial Q
where
Q=[0,1]\times[0,1]
.

There ought to exist counterexamples
to the asymptotics of Theorem
1.3

if we do not impose anything like weak regularity.
At least this is the case for the Ginibre ensemble,
for which we show that for any

\varepsilon>0
, there exists a rectifiable Jordan arc

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
for which

{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma_{\varepsilon})\big]\gtrsim R^{2-\varepsilon}|\Gamma|

(1.12)

holds as
R\to\infty
; see §
6.1
–§
6.2
.

Our last result concerns the asymptotics of the work
\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
, i.e.
the real part of the complex action
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
(defined in §
1.2
).

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that the two-point function
k_{\Lambda}
of the invariant point
process
\Lambda
satisfies
(1+t^{3})k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)

along with the zeroth moment condition (
1.9
), and that

\Gamma
is a weakly Ahlfors regular rectifiable curve with distinct start and end points.
Then, as
R\to\infty
,

{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)\big]=\left(D_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\log R

where
\displaystyle{D_{\Lambda}=2\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{3}k_{\Lambda}(t){\rm d}t}
.
As a consequence, we have that, as
R\to\infty
,

{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)\big]={\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)\big]+O(\log R)\,.

In other words, under these assumptions the quantity
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)
really
measures the asymptotic electric flux through
R\Gamma
.

Remark 1.7
.

Denoting by
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
the probability space on which the stationary
point process
\Lambda
is defined, let
\mathcal{F}_{\sf inv}\subset\mathcal{F}

be the sigma-algebra of translation invariant events.
Throughout this paper, we make the simplifying assumption
that the spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
does not have an atom at the origin. As outlined in
[Part I, §2.3], this is the same as saying that the
conditional intensity
of
\Lambda
,
defined by

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}=\pi^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[n_{\Lambda}({\mathbb{D}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{{\sf inv}}\big]\,,

is non-random and equals
c_{\Lambda}
almost surely. While this assumption
simplifies the formulation of our results,
our methods can be adapted to deal also with the case when
\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\})>0
.
We will not dwell on the details, but mention only that in this setting,
the stationary vector field should be defined as in Part I by

V_{\Lambda}(z)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{|\lambda|<R}\frac{1}{z-\lambda}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\overline{z}\,,

and, in Theorem
1.4
, one should replace the variance
{\sf Var}[n_{\Lambda}(R\,\mathcal{G})]

with the re-centered variance

{\sf Var}\big[n_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{G})-\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}m(\mathcal{G})\big]
.
Since the spectral measure
\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}
and
two-point function
K_{V_{\Lambda}}
do not depend on the size of the atom
\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\})

([Part I, Theorem 5.8]),
only very minor modifications will be needed.

It is natural to ask about asymptotic
normality of the renormalized electric flux
R^{-1/2}\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)

(or, equivalently, of the renormalized electric action).
For the zeroes of GEFs and
C^{1}
-smooth curves, this was proven in
[
3
]

using the method of moments. An alternative is to use
the clustering property of
k
-point functions
(cf. Malyshev
[
13
]
, Martin-Yalcin
[
15
]
,
Nazarov-Sodin
[
18
, Theorem 1.5]
), which is easy to verify for the Ginibre
ensemble using its determinantal structure,
and which was proven in
[
18
]
for the zero
set of GEFs. We will not pursue the details here.

1.4
Related work

The study of charge fluctuations of stationary
point processes is a classical topic in mathematical
physics; see
[
6
]
for a recent survey.
Important early contributions were the works of
Martin-Yalcin
[
15
]
, Lebowitz
[
11
]
,
and Jancovici-Lebowitz-Manificat
[
9
]
(see also
Martin’s survey
[
14
]
).
Recently there has been a resurgence of
interest, due partly to the role of hyperuniform systems
in material science (see e.g.
[
22
]
).
This has highlighted the relationship between
charge fluctuations and properties of the spectral
measure; see the work
[
1
]
of
Adhikari-Ghosh-Lebowitz and
references therein for recent mathematical
developments.

If we do not impose any smoothness condition on the spectral measure,
there exist examples where the asymptotics (
1.11
) cease to hold.
To see this, simply consider the stationary point process obtained by
randomly shifting the lattice points
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}={\mathbb{C}}

by a random variable uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{2}
.
In this example, the spectral measure consists of unit point masses
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Denoting by
\mathbb{B}^{p}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

the unit ball in the
\ell^{p}
-metric, Kim and Torquato
[
10
]

showed that
{\sf Var}\big[n_{\Lambda}(R\,\mathbb{B}^{p})\big]
grows
like
R^{\sigma(p)}
, where
\sigma(p)
varies continuously between

1
and
2
as
p
ranges from
2
to infinity. It is also worth
mentioning that examples of this sort persists when considering
independent Gaussian perturbations of the “randomly shifted” lattice,
where the spectral measure is a mixture of an absolutely continuous
part and a singular part, see Yakir
[
24
]
. Another related
work is the recent study by Björklund and Hartnick
[
2
]
,
which investigated the hyperuniformity of various point processes
which are models for quasicrystals. In this case the asymptotic of
the number variance may depend on fine arithmetic properties of the quasicrystal.

For the zeros of the Gaussian Entire Function
F(z)
,
the flux
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)
corresponds to
the change in argument of
F(z)
along
R\Gamma
. This was introduced by Buckley-Sodin in

[
3
]
, though the idea was present already in Lebowitz work
[
11
]

on charge fluctuations in Coulomb systems.
Buckley and Sodin obtained the large
R
-asymptotics of

{\sf Cov}\left[\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma_{1}),\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma_{2})\right]

for piecewise
C^{1}
-regular curves
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
with a proof
which used the properties of that ensemble. It is not clear whether the proof given there
persists for weakly Ahlfors regular curves. We mention that the signed length recently appeared in the
work of Notarnicola-Peccati-Vidotto on the length of nodal
lines in Berry’s random planar wave model
[
19
]
.

For determinental point processes (including the infinite Ginibre ensemble) Lin
[
12
]

very recently obtained a stronger version of Theorem
1.4
, which is
valid for a more general class of domains
\mathcal{G}
having a bounded perimeter.
He also showed that for a very large class of domains
\mathcal{G}
,
{\sf Var}\big[n_{\Lambda}(R\mathcal{G})\big]

is comparable to
R^{\alpha(\mathcal{G})}
, where
\alpha(\mathcal{G})
is the
Minkowski dimension of
\partial\cal G
. Lin’s approach is based
on the study of asymptotics of the functional

\displaystyle\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\big({1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)-{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathcal{G}}(y)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\rho\big(R|x-y|\big)\,{\rm d}m(x)\,{\rm d}m(y)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}^{c}}\rho\big(R|x-y|\big)\,{\rm d}m(x)\,{\rm d}m(y)

as
R\to\infty
, where
\rho
is a non-negative integrable function
which is sufficiently fast decaying at infinity (cf. Dávila
[
4
]
).
In our case
\rho=-k_{\Lambda}
, the (truncated) two-point function of
\Lambda

taken with negative sign. Non-negativity of
\rho
seems to be an obstacle
for using this technique for non-determinental point processes.
Nevertheless, Lin mentions
[
12
, Remark 1.2.3]
that his techniques
can be also applied to zeros of Gaussian Entire Functions.

There is also a resemblance between the topic of this paper
and the study of “irregularities of distribution” e.g. in the work of
Montgomery
[
17
, Ch. 6]
.

1.5
Outline of the paper

The starting point for the asymptotic analysis of

{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)\big]
is the identity

{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)\big]={\sf p.v.}\iint_{R\Gamma\times R\Gamma}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}\bar{y},

(1.13)

where
K
is the (singular) covariance
kernel of
V_{\Lambda}
(see §
4
)
and where the principal value integral is understood in the sense
of the limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{R\Gamma\times R\Gamma}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}\bar{y}.

The article is organized as follows. In §
2

we recall various preliminaries, mainly on the second-order
structure of stationary processes. Section
3
is devoted
to geometric observations concerning the weak-Ahlfors condition.
In §
4
we establish the formula (
1.13
) for the variance
and recall a convenient representation of the kernel
K
from
Part I. The proof of the main results
are given in §
5
, and the existence
of rectifiable but not weakly Ahlfors regular Jordan
arcs with large charge fluctuations is discussed in
§
6
(this is made rigorous for the infinite Ginibre ensemble).

2.2
The covariance structure of

\Lambda

For a discussion of the spectral measure and various
covariance measures of the point process
\Lambda
, we refer to
§2 of Part I. For the reader’s
convenience, we recall the most central notions here.

We assume throughout that the point process has a finite
second moment, i.e. that
\mathbb{E}\big[n_{\Lambda}(B)^{2}\big]<\infty
holds
for any bounded Borel set
B
. Under this assumption there
exists a measure
\kappa_{\Lambda}
such that

{\sf Cov}\big[n_{\Lambda}(\varphi),n_{\Lambda}(\psi)\big]=\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}\varphi(z)\overline{\psi(z^{\prime})}\,{\rm d}\kappa_{\Lambda}(z-z^{\prime}){\rm d}m(z).

This is the so-called reduced covariance measure.
It is often more convenient to write
\kappa_{\Lambda}=\tau_{\Lambda}+c_{\Lambda}\delta_{0}
.
Indeed, for the standard point processes
that we have in mind (the Ginibre ensemble,
and the zero set of the GEF)
the measure
\tau_{\Lambda}
has a density
k_{\Lambda}
with respect
to planar Lebesgue measure, called the
(truncated) two-point function
.
For the Ginibre ensemble, the two-point function takes
the particularly simple form
k_{\Lambda}(t)=-\pi^{-2}e^{-\pi\,t^{2}}
, while
for the zeros of the GEF it is given by

k_{\Lambda}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}t^{2}}\,t^{2}(\coth t-1)\,.

The spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
is the Fourier transform of
\kappa_{\Lambda}
, and
it satisfies the Plancherel-type identity

{\sf Cov}\left[n_{\Lambda}(\varphi),n_{\Lambda}(\psi)\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\psi}(\xi)}\,{{\rm d}}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)=\langle\widehat{\varphi},\widehat{\psi}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho_{\Lambda})}\,,

(2.1)

for test functions
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{D}
.
This relation extends to
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}
;
see Remark 2.1 in Part I.

Under the spectral condition (
1.2
),
we define the stationary random field
V_{\Lambda}
by (
1.3
)
(see also §5.1, Part I).
The spectral measure

\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}
of
V_{\Lambda}
is given by

{\rm d}\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\xi)={1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}}(\xi)\,\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}},

see Theorem 5.8 in Part I.

2.3
Admissible measures

For most of the article, it will be convenient to replace
the current
\nu_{\Gamma}(f{\rm d}z)=\int_{\Gamma}f(z){{\rm d}z}

with the more general observables

\mu(f)\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\int f\,{{\rm d}\mu}

where
\mu
is a complex-valued measure of finite total variation.
The weak Ahlfors regularity condition then corresponds
to the following notion.

Definition 2.1

(Admissible complex-valued measures)
.

We say that a compactly
supported complex-valued Borel measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{C}}

of finite total variation is
admissible

if there exists a constant
C=C_{\mu}

such that

|\mu({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))|\leq Cr

for all
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
r>0
.

Denote by
\phi_{\varepsilon}
the Gaussian

\phi_{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}e^{-|z|^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}}.

(2.2)

Claim 2.2
.

Assume that
\mu
is admissible with admissibility
constant
C_{\mu}
, and define a regularized measure by

\mu_{\varepsilon}\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\phi_{\varepsilon}*\mu
.
Then
\mu_{\varepsilon}
is admissible with the
same constant
C_{\mu}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
, Fubini’s theorem,
and a linear change of variables
we get

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{D}}(x,r)}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\phi_{\varepsilon}(z-w)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(w){\rm d}m(z)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{{\mathbb{D}}(x,r)}(\zeta+w)\phi_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(w){\rm d}m(\zeta)

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mu\left({\mathbb{D}}(x+\zeta,r)\right)\phi_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}m(\zeta).

(2.5)

Since
\phi_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
with

\lVert\phi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{1}({\mathbb{C}},{\rm d}m)}=1
,
this gives the upper bound

\frac{\left|\mu_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))\right|}{r}\leq\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{|\mu\left({\mathbb{D}}(x+\zeta,r)\right)|}{r}\phi_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta\leq C_{\mu}

as claimed.
∎

3
Geometric lemmas

3.1
Rectifiable Jordan curves are weakly Ahlfors regular

We supply a simple proof that any Jordan curve is weakly Ahlfors regular.

Lemma 3.1
.

Every rectifiable Jordan curve
\Gamma
is weakly Ahlfors regular. In fact,
for any disk
D
we have the upper bound

\left|\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{{\rm d}z}\right|\leq|\partial D|.

(3.1)

Figure 2
:
Figure pertaining to the proof that Jordan curves
are weakly Ahlfors regular (Lemma
3.1
).
The union
\mathcal{C}
of circular arcs intersecting the
Jordan domain
\mathcal{G}

is shown in purple, and the domains enclosed by

(\Gamma\cap D)\cup\mathcal{C}
are lightly shaded.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{G}
the Jordan domain enclosed by
\Gamma

and fix an open disk
D
. We first assume that
\Gamma
is analytic, which implies that
either
\Gamma=\partial D
, or it has finitely many intersections
with
\partial D
. Indeed, after a translation and a rescaling,
we may assume that
D
is the unit disk. If
\phi:{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathcal{G}
is a conformal map,
it extends conformally past the boundary, and
f
is real-analytic
on the circle.
If
\#(\Gamma\cap\partial D)
were infinite, then the zeros
of the real-analytic
function
f(s)\overset{{\sf def}}{=}|\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})|^{2}-1
would have an accumulation point, which
implies that
f(s)=0
, so
\Gamma
coincides with a circle.
Hence,
\mathcal{G}\cap D
is a finite union of Jordan domains with piecewise
analytic boundaries.
Moreover,
\mathcal{C}\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\overline{\mathcal{G}\cap\partial D}
is a disjoint finite union
of circular arcs. If these are given the positive orientation, the domain
\mathcal{G}
always
remains on the left-hand side as we traverse
\mathcal{C}
, and we find that the curve

\left(\Gamma\cap D\right)\cup\mathcal{C}

is a finite union of piecewise smooth Jordan curves, each of which bounds
a connected component of
\mathcal{G}\cap D
; see Figure
2
.
Cauchy’s theorem then gives that

\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{{\rm d}z}=-\int_{\mathcal{C}}{{\rm d}z},

(3.2)

and we clearly have that

\left|\int_{\mathcal{C}}{{\rm d}z}\right|\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}}|{\rm d}z|=|\partial D|.

(3.3)

Hence, the proof is complete for analytic curves.

For a general rectifiable Jordan curve
\Gamma
, we argue by approximation.
By Carathéodory’s theorem, there exists a conformal mapping
\phi
of
{\mathbb{D}}
onto
\mathcal{G}
,
such that
\phi
extends to a homeomorphism

\phi:\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\to\overline{\mathcal{G}}
.
We obtain the desired approximation by taking

\Gamma_{t}
to be the curve parametrized by
\phi(te^{{\rm i}s})
,
s\in[0,2\pi)
,
for
0<t<1
and letting
t\to 1
.
Since
\Gamma
is rectifiable, a theorem of Riesz and Privalov (see
[
20
, 6.8]
)
asserts that the derivative
\phi^{\prime}(z)
belongs to
the Hardy space
H^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. In particular, the radial limit

\phi^{\prime}=\lim_{t\to 1}\phi^{\prime}_{t}
exists a.e. on
{\mathbb{T}}
, and
\phi_{t}^{\prime}\to\phi^{\prime}
in
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.
Since

\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})
supplies an absolutely continuous
parametrization of
\Gamma
, the quantity of interest may be expressed as

\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{\rm d}z=\int_{0}^{2\pi}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{D}\big(\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})\big)\partial_{s}\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})\,{\rm d}s=\int_{0}^{2\pi}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{D}\big(\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})\big){\rm i}e^{{\rm i}s}\phi^{\prime}(e^{{\rm i}s})\,{\rm d}s.

Next, note that
{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{D}\circ\phi_{t}\to{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{D}\circ\phi
holds
everywhere on the circle.
Indeed, since
D
is open, any point
\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})\in D
has a neighborhood

V\subset D
. But since
\phi_{t}(e^{{\rm i}s})\to\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})

for any
s
, we have
\phi_{t}(e^{{\rm i}s})\in V
for
t
sufficiently close to
1
.

As a consequence of these properties of the approximating parametrization
we find that for any fixed disk
D
,
we have

\lim_{t\to 1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{D}\big(\phi_{t}(e^{{\rm i}s})\big){\rm i}e^{{\rm i}s}\phi_{t}^{\prime}(e^{{\rm i}s})\,{\rm d}s=\int_{0}^{2\pi}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{D}\big(\phi(e^{{\rm i}s})\big){\rm i}e^{{\rm i}s}\phi^{\prime}(e^{{\rm i}s})\,{\rm d}s,

or, in other words,

\lim_{t\to 1}\int_{\Gamma_{t}\cap D}{\rm d}z=\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{\rm d}z.

Hence, for any given disk
D
and any
fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
we let
t
be sufficiently close to
1
so that

\left|\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{\rm d}z-\int_{\Gamma_{t}\cap D}{\rm d}z\right|\leq\varepsilon|\partial D|.

But since
\Gamma_{t}
is analytic, it follows from (
3.2
) and
(
3.3
) and the reverse triangle inequality that

\left|\int_{\Gamma\cap D}{\rm d}z\right|\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\partial D|.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

If a rectifiable Jordan arc
\Gamma
can
be completed to a rectifiable Jordan curve by appending
a weakly Ahlfors regular Jordan arc, then
\Gamma
is also
weakly Ahlfors regular; cf. Figure
3
.
The existence of such an arc would be guaranteed if, e.g.,

\Gamma
does not wind too wildly near any of its endpoints. Hence, at least for
rectifiable Jordan arcs, only the behavior near
the end-points matter for weak regularity.

3.2
The density of
\nu_{\Gamma}

We denote by
\gamma:I\to\Gamma
the arc-length
parametrization of
\Gamma
, and introduce the (“net tangent”)
function

\tau(x)=\tau_{\Gamma}(x)\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\sum_{t\in\gamma^{-1}(x)}\gamma^{\prime}(t).

(3.4)

Let
\nu_{\Gamma}
be the complex-valued measure given by the integration
current over
\Gamma
. That is,

\int h\,{\rm d}\nu_{\Gamma}=\int_{\Gamma}h(z)\,{\rm d}z=\int_{I}h(\gamma(t))\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm}{\rm d}t.

(3.5)

Then, for any Borel set
B
,
\displaystyle\nu_{\Gamma}(B)=\int_{\Gamma\cap B}{\rm d}z
.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Figure 3
:
A Jordan arc
\Gamma
(black) which can be completed to a Jordan
curve by appending
a weakly Ahlfors regular arc (red), implying that
\Gamma
is weakly Ahlfors; cf. Lemma

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
\Gamma
is a weakly Ahlfors regular rectifiable curve.
Then for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x
, it holds that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\nu_{\Gamma}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)}{2r}{\rm d}r=\tau(x).

Proof.

From (
3.5
), it is evident that
\nu_{\Gamma}
is
absolutely continuous with respect to arc-length measure
on
\Gamma
, which in turn is absolutely continuous with
respect to
\mathcal{H}^{1}\big|_{\Gamma}
.
In fact, by applying the change of
variables formula in
[
5
, Theorem 3.9]
to the
functions
g=(h\circ\gamma)\,\gamma^{\prime}
and

f=\gamma
(so that the Jacobian satisfies
Jf=1
a.e.),
we find that

\int_{I}h(\gamma(t))\gamma^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{\Gamma}h(x)\left(\sum_{t\in\gamma^{-1}(x)}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right){\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)=\int_{\Gamma}h(x)\tau(x)\,{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x),

so that
{\rm d}\nu_{\Gamma}(x)=\tau(x)\,{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\big|_{\Gamma}(x)
.
In view of the upper bound

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}|\tau(x)|\,{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma}\#\big\{t\in\gamma^{-1}(x)\big\}{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{I}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\,{\rm d}t=|\Gamma|,

and of the rectifiability of
\Gamma
,
we have
\tau\in L^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\Gamma})
.

We next claim that

\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))}{2r}\xrightarrow{r\to 0}1.

(3.6)

Indeed, the upper bound follows from the density bound
[
16
, Theorem 6.2]

for general rectifiable sets, and the lower bound is a consequence of
the fact that
\Gamma
has a tangent at
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in\Gamma
.

Since
\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\Gamma}
is a Borel regular measure
(see
[
16
, p. 57]
),
we may apply the Lebesgue-Besicovitch differentiation
theorem (
[
5
, Theorem 1.32]
) along with
(
3.6
)
to obtain

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\nu_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))}{2r}=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))}\int_{\Gamma\cap{\mathbb{D}}(x,r)}\tau(x){\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\cdot\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))}{2r}=\tau(x)

(3.7)

for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Observe next that

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\nu_{\Gamma}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)}{2r}{\rm d}r=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nu_{\Gamma}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,\varepsilon t)\right)}{2\varepsilon t}{\rm d}t,

and in view of the weak Ahlfors regularity
of
\Gamma
, the integrand on the right-hand side is bounded above by
1
.
Hence, the pointwise convergence (
3.7
) and the
bounded convergence theorem together give that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\nu_{\Gamma}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)}{2r}{\rm d}r=\int_{0}^{1}\tau(x){\rm d}t=\tau(x)

for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

In view of Lemma

, the

of the weakly Ahlfors regular rectifiable curve

(i.e., the density of
{{\rm d}z}

4
The covariance structure of
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)

The purpose of this section is to establish the basic formula
(
1.13
) for the covariance of the action of

V_{\Lambda}
along two weakly Ahlfors regular rectifiable curves.
The starting point is the following result taken from
Proposition 5.9 and Remark 5.11 in Part I.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
(1+t^{2})k_{\Lambda}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)
.
Then for
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

{\sf Cov}\left[V_{\Lambda}(\varphi),V_{\Lambda}(\psi)\right]=\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}\varphi(x)\overline{\psi(y)}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}m(x)\,{\rm d}m(y),

(4.1)

where the kernel
K=K_{V_{\Lambda}}
(the two-point function for
V_{\Lambda}
) is given by

K(z)=-4\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{+}\left(\frac{r}{|z|}\right)k_{\Lambda}(r)\,r\,{\rm d}r,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}.

Juxtaposing (
4.1
) with the formula for the covariance on the
Fourier side:

{\sf Cov}\left[V_{\Lambda}(\varphi),V_{\Lambda}(\psi)\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{\psi}(\xi)}\,\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}

(4.2)

(see Theorem 5.8, Part I), we conclude that

\widehat{K}(\xi)=|\xi|^{-2}h_{\Lambda}\,,

(4.3)

where
h_{\Lambda}
is the (radial) density of
\rho_{\Lambda}
. The Fourier
transform in (
4.3
) should be understood
in the sense of distributions, or alternatively in the sense of
Fourier transform acting on
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}},m)
functions.

For the remainder of this section, we will work in somewhat greater
generally with observables

\mu(V_{\Lambda})
, where
\mu
is an admissible measure (recall
Definition
2.1
above).
In order to show that the variance of
\mu(V_{\Lambda})
is well defined,
we will approximate
\mu
by
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mu*\phi_{\varepsilon}
,
where
\phi_{\varepsilon}(z)
are the Gaussians from
(
2.2
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
\mu
and
\nu
are admissible complex-valued measures.
Then we have
\mu(V_{\Lambda}),\nu(V_{\Lambda})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, and

{\sf Cov}\left[\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(z),\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}\nu(z)\right]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(y).

Proof.

It will suffice to show that

\mu(V_{\Lambda})=\int V_{\Lambda}{{\rm d}\mu}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

and that

{\sf Var}\left[\mu(V_{\Lambda})\right]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\mu_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(y).

Since
\mu_{\varepsilon}
is absolutely continuous with a density in

\mathcal{S}
, by (
4.3
) we have

{\sf Var}\left[\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|\widehat{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(\xi)|^{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}e^{-\varepsilon^{2}|\xi|^{2}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}.

Notice that the integrand is monotonically
increasing as
\varepsilon
decreases to
0
,
so it will be sufficient to establish that

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}e^{-\varepsilon^{2}|\xi|^{2}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}<\infty.

(4.4)

Indeed, it will then follow from monotone convergence
that
{\sf Var}\left[\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})\right]

converges to
\lVert\widehat{\mu}\rVert_{L^{2}(|\xi|^{-2}\rho_{\Lambda})}^{2}
as

\varepsilon\to 0
, which implies that

\lVert\widehat{\mu}_{\varepsilon}-\widehat{\mu}_{\delta}\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{C}},|\xi|^{-2}\rho_{\Lambda})}^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\Big(e^{-\varepsilon^{2}|\xi|^{2}}+e^{-\delta^{2}|\xi|^{2}}-2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}+\delta^{2}}{2}|\xi|^{2}}\Big)|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}\to 0

as
\varepsilon,\delta\to 0
.
Hence, we see that
\displaystyle\mu(V_{\Lambda})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})
in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
,
and the variance
may be expressed as

{\sf Var}[\mu(V_{\Lambda})]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(y).

In order to see why (
4.4
) holds,
notice that by Lemma
4.1
we have

\displaystyle{\sf Var}\left[\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})\right]

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(y)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=-4\pi^{2}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{+}\frac{t}{|x-y|}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t\,{\rm d}t\right){{\rm d}\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(y).

(4.6)

Note that the measure
|\mu_{\varepsilon}|
is absolutely continuous
with density in
\mathcal{S}
, so we may integrate against
|\mu_{\varepsilon}|\times|\mu_{\varepsilon}|

to get

\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{+}\frac{t}{|x-y|}|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,t\,{\rm d}|\mu_{\varepsilon}|(x)\,{\rm d}|\mu_{\varepsilon}|(y)\,{\rm dt}\\
\leq\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\big|\log|x-y|\big|+\big|\log t\big|\right)|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,t\,{\rm d}|\mu_{\varepsilon}|(x)\,{\rm d}|\mu_{\varepsilon}|(y)\,{\rm dt}\\
\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(1+|\log t|\big)|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,t\,{\rm d}t<\infty.

(4.7)

We may thus apply Fubini’s theorem to the right-hand side of (
4.5
)
to get

{\sf Var}\left[\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})\right]=-4\pi^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}k_{\Lambda}(t)I_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\mu_{\varepsilon}(x),

where

I_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log_{+}\frac{t}{|x-y|}\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(y)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,te^{-s})\right)\,{\rm d}s,

the last equality being a consequence of the
“layer cake formula”; that is, integration with
respect to the distribution function.
Hence, by the triangle inequality, we get that

{\sf Var}\left[\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})\right]\leq 4\pi^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\,|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,|I_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}|\mu_{\varepsilon}|(x).

By Claim
2.2
, the measure
\mu_{\varepsilon}

is admissible, with admissibility constant independent of
\varepsilon
.
Therefore,

|I_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{|\mu_{\varepsilon}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)|}{r}{\rm d}r\leq C_{\mu},

and, as a consequence,

{\sf Var}\left[\mu_{\varepsilon}(V_{\Lambda})\right]\leq C_{\mu}|\mu_{\varepsilon}|({\mathbb{C}})\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\,|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,{\rm d}t,

which is readily seen to be bounded above independently
of
\varepsilon
by use of the trivial bound
|\mu*\phi_{\varepsilon}|({\mathbb{C}})\leq|\mu|({\mathbb{C}})\int_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{\varepsilon}(z){\rm d}m(z)=|\mu|({\mathbb{C}})
.
∎

Since in the end we prefer to think in
terms of the principal value integral,
we should check that both regularizations of the integral

\displaystyle{\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\mu(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}(y)}
give the same result.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any two admissible measures
\mu
and
\nu
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\mu(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf def}}{=}{\sf p.v.}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y).

Proof.

Again, by polarization, it suffices to check the condition for
\mu=\nu
.
Recall that the convolution of
two centered Gaussians is again a centered Gaussian;

\phi_{\varepsilon}*\phi_{\varepsilon}=\phi_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}.

(4.8)

Thinking of
K
as a function in
{\mathbb{C}}
by the identification
K(z)=K(|z|)
,
we rewrite the regularized variance as

\displaystyle\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}_{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}[K*\phi_{\varepsilon}*\bar{\mu}]\,[\phi_{\varepsilon}*\mu]\,{\rm d}m

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}[K*\phi_{\varepsilon}*\bar{\mu}]*\phi_{\varepsilon}\,{{\rm d}\mu}

(4.10)

where we have used the general distributional identity

\left(f,g*h\right)=\left(f*g,h\right)

applied to
f=K*\phi_{\varepsilon}
,
g=\phi_{\varepsilon}
and
h=\mu

to arrive at the last equality. Using the associativity
of convolution along with the identity
(
4.8
), we recognize this as

\int_{\mathbb{C}}[K*\phi_{\varepsilon}*\bar{\mu}]*\phi_{\varepsilon}\,{{\rm d}\mu}=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}(y)\right){{\rm d}\mu}(x).

The quantity of interest is thus

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}(y)-\int_{|x-y|>\varepsilon}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\bar{\mu}(y)\bigg){{\rm d}\mu}(x)\\
=-4\pi^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{2}I^{\Delta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}\mu_{\varepsilon}(x)

(4.11)

where

\displaystyle I^{\Delta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mu_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,te^{-s})\right)-\mu\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,te^{-s})\setminus{\mathbb{D}}(x,\varepsilon)\right)\right]{\rm d}s

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)}{r}-\frac{\mu\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\setminus{\mathbb{D}}(x,\varepsilon)\right)}{r}\right){\rm d}r.

(4.13)

By the admissibility assumption and by the fact that convolution
preserves weak Ahlfors regularity (Claim
2.2
), the integrand on
the right-hand side is uniformly bounded, as

\bigg|\frac{\mu_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)}{r}-\frac{\mu\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\setminus{\mathbb{D}}(x,\varepsilon)\right)}{r}\bigg|\leq\frac{|\mu_{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))|}{r}+\frac{|\mu({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))|}{r}\leq 2\,C_{\mu}\,.

Therefore, we can apply the bounded convergence theorem for each fixed
t>0
and get

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I^{\Delta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=0

for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x
. Hence, in view of the condition that

t^{2}k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)
, the claim follows
from the dominated convergence theorem.
∎

5
Proof of the main results

5.1
The asymptotic covariance structure of the electric action

Recall that Theorem
1.3
asserts that if the
two-point function
k_{\Lambda}
is radial and satisfies

(1+t^{2})k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)
, then
for any weakly Ahlfors regular rectifiable curves
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
we have

{\sf Cov}\left[\int_{R\Gamma_{1}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z,\int_{R\Gamma_{2}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z\right]=R\left(C_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})

as
R\to\infty
, where
\displaystyle{C_{\Lambda}=-8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{2}\,{\rm d}t}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We will show that for any two admissible measures

\mu
and
\nu
such that the limit

\tau_{\nu}(x)\overset{{\sf def}}{=}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\nu({\mathbb{D}}(x,r))}{2r}{\rm d}r

(5.1)

exists
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e., we have

{\sf Cov}\left[\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{\Lambda}(Rz)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(z),\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{\Lambda}(Rz)\,{\rm d}\nu(z)\right]=\left(C_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\,R^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\overline{\tau_{\nu}(z)}\,{{\rm d}\mu}(z).

(5.2)

Theorem
1.3
will follow by combining
(
5.2
) with Lemma
3.2
, which states that
(
5.1
) holds for the complex-valued measure
\nu_{\Gamma}
,
which was defined by

\nu_{\Gamma}(f)=\int_{\Gamma}f\,{\rm d}z
.
This measure
is clearly admissible, since
\Gamma

is assumed to be weakly Ahlfors regular.
We note that

\int_{\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}}\overline{\tau_{\Gamma_{2}}(x)}\,{\rm d}\nu_{\Gamma_{1}}(x)=\int_{\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}}\tau_{\Gamma_{1}}(x)\,\overline{\tau_{\Gamma_{2}}(x)}\,{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}),

see Remark
3.3
.

Putting together the two formulas from Lemma
4.2
and
Lemma
4.3
for the regularized covariance, we have

\displaystyle{\sf Cov}\left[\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{\Lambda}(Rz)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(z),\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}V_{\Lambda}(Rz)\,{\rm d}\nu(z)\right]

\displaystyle={\sf p.v.}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(R|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)

(5.3)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}K(R|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y).

(5.4)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we may write the
truncated covariance integral as

\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}K(R|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)\\
=-4\pi^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\int_{|x-y|>\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{+}\frac{t/R}{|x-y|}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t\,{\rm d}t\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y).

(5.5)

This integral is absolutely convergent,
and an application of Fubini’s theorem
gives that

\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|x-y|>\varepsilon\}}K(R|x-y|)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)\\
=\frac{1}{R}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}I_{R,\varepsilon}(x)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)

where

\displaystyle I_{R,\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-4\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{2}\left(\frac{R}{t}\int_{|x-y|>\varepsilon}\log_{+}\frac{t/R}{|x-y|}\,{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)\right)\,{\rm d}t

(5.6)

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf def}}{=}-4\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{2}J_{R,t,\varepsilon}(x){\rm d}t.

(5.7)

Integrating with respect to the distribution function,
we arrive at

\displaystyle J_{R,t,\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{t}\int_{|x-y|>\varepsilon}\log_{+}\frac{t/R}{|x-y|}{\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t/R}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\nu}\left(\left\{y:\varepsilon<|x-y|<\frac{t}{R}e^{-s}\right\}\right){\rm d}s

(5.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t/R}\int_{\varepsilon}^{t/R}\frac{\bar{\nu}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\setminus{\mathbb{D}}(x,\varepsilon)\right)}{r}{\rm d}r.

(5.10)

By the definition of admissible measures, we have

|J_{R,t,\varepsilon}(x)|\leq C

uniformly in
x,R

t
and
\varepsilon
. What is more,
the existence of

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}J_{R,t,\varepsilon}(x)=J_{R,t,0}(x)

for all
x
follows from admissibility,
while the assumption (
5.1
) together with
admissibility of
\nu
ensure that for any fixed
t>0
,
we have

J_{R,t,0}(x)=\frac{1}{t/R}\int_{0}^{t/R}\frac{\bar{\nu}\left({\mathbb{D}}(x,r)\right)}{r}{\rm d}r\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}2\overline{\tau_{\nu}(x)}

for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Hence, we may apply the dominated convergence theorem to the integral
(
5.6
) to find

\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{R,\varepsilon}(x)=-8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{2}{\rm d}t\;\overline{\tau_{\nu}(x)}\overset{{\sf def}}{=}C_{\Lambda}\overline{\tau_{\nu}(x)}\,.

We also have for free the bound

|I_{R,\varepsilon}(x)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,|J_{R,t,\varepsilon}(x)|{\rm d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}k_{\Lambda}(t){\rm d}t

for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
If we decompose
\mu
as
\mu=\mu^{+}_{1}-\mu^{-}_{1}+{\rm i}\,\mu_{2}^{+}-{\rm i}\,\mu_{2}^{-}
where each
\mu_{i}^{\pm}
is a finite positive
measure, the bounded convergence theorem applied
to each of the four integrals then gives

\displaystyle{\sf p.v.}\iint_{{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}}K(R|x-y|){{\rm d}\mu}(x){\rm d}\bar{\nu}(y)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{R}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}I_{R,\varepsilon}(x)\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\left(C_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)R^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\overline{\tau_{\nu}(x)}\,{{\rm d}\mu}(x)

(5.12)

as
R\to\infty
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

The constant
C_{\Lambda}
in Theorem
1.3
is given by

C_{\Lambda}=-8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{2}\,{\rm d}t.

From this formula, it is not immediately clear that it is positive,
but another representation clarifies matters. Under the conditions of the theorem,
the spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
has a radial density
h_{\Lambda}
, and we have

h_{\Lambda}=c_{\Lambda}+\widehat{k}_{\Lambda},

where

\widehat{k}_{\Lambda}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}k_{\Lambda}(|z|)\,{\rm d}m(z).

Being the density of a positive measure,
h_{\Lambda}
is certainly positive. Moreover, the zeroth moment
condition (
1.9
) gives that
h_{\Lambda}(0)=0
. Since
(1+|z|)\,k_{\Lambda}(|z|)

belongs to
L^{1}({\mathbb{C}},{\rm d}m)
, we also have
h_{\Lambda}(|z|)\in C^{1}({\mathbb{C}})
. Then,
by positivity, we find that
h^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(0)=0
as well.
Since moreover
\lVert h_{\Lambda}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq|c_{\Lambda}|+\lVert k_{\Lambda}(|\cdot|)\rVert_{L^{1}({\mathbb{C}})}
, we have

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}h_{\Lambda}(\tau)=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}h_{\Lambda}(\tau)=0,

so integrating by parts we find that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{h_{\Lambda}(\tau)}{\tau^{2}}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\partial_{\tau}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{2\pi{\rm i}r\tau\cos\vartheta}k_{\Lambda}(r)r\,{\rm d}r{\rm d}\vartheta\right){\rm d}\tau

(5.13)

\displaystyle=2\pi{\rm i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\vartheta e^{2\pi{\rm i}r\tau\cos\vartheta}r^{2}k_{\Lambda}(r)\,{\rm d}r{\rm d}\vartheta{\rm d}\tau

(5.14)

\displaystyle=-4\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}J_{1}(2\pi r\tau)r^{2}k_{\Lambda}(r)\,{\rm d}r{\rm d}\tau,

(5.15)

where the last equality follows from the identity

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(\vartheta)e^{2\pi{\rm i}r\tau\vartheta}{\rm d}\vartheta=2\pi{\rm i}\,J_{1}(2\pi r\tau).

But
\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{1}(2\pi r\tau)}{\tau}{\rm d}\tau=1}
,
so an application of Fubini’s theorem gives that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{h_{\Lambda}(\tau)}{\tau^{2}}\,{\rm d}\tau=-4\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}k_{\Lambda}(r)\,{\rm d}r.

Since the left-hand side is clearly positive, it follows that
C_{\Lambda}
is positive as well.

The asymptotic charge fluctuations in rectifiable Jordan domains

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We fix a Jordan domain
\mathcal{G}
with rectifiable boundary
\Gamma=\partial\mathcal{G}
.
Recall that Lemma
3.1
asserts that any rectifiable

{\sf Var}\big[n_{\Lambda}(R\mathcal{G})\big]={\sf Var}\big[\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{\partial(R\mathcal{G})}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z\big]=\frac{1}{4}{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\partial(R\mathcal{G}))\big].

with

.

5.3
Logarithmic asymptotics for the work

The purpose of this section is prove Theorem
1.6
. That is,
we need to obtain the asymptotics of the variance
of the work

\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)=\operatorname{Re}\int_{R\Gamma}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{{\rm d}z},

of
V_{\Lambda}
along
R\Gamma
. We recall that for this result,
we assume that
\Lambda
is an invariant point process
subject to the stronger moment condition

(1+t^{3})k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{\rm d}t)
for the two-point
function
k_{\Lambda}
of
\Lambda
.

Below, we will express the work
\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)

in terms of the increments

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\Pi_{\Lambda}(z+a)-\Pi_{\Lambda}(z),

where
\Pi_{\Lambda}
is the random potential for
\Lambda
, i.e. the solution to

\Delta\Pi_{\Lambda}=2\pi(n_{\Lambda}-c_{\Lambda}\hskip 1.0ptm)
, defined in
§6 in Part I. The potential is given by
\Pi_{\Lambda}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\log|F_{\Lambda}(z)|-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\pi c_{\Lambda}|z|^{2}
,
where
F_{\Lambda}
is the random entire function

F_{\Lambda}(z)=\exp\bigl[-\Psi_{1}(\infty)z-\frac{1}{2}\,\Psi_{2}(\infty)z^{2}\bigr]\,\prod_{|\lambda|<1}(\lambda-z)\prod_{|\lambda|\geq 1}\left(\frac{\lambda-z}{\lambda}\,\exp\Bigl[\,\frac{z}{\lambda}\,+\frac{z^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}\,\Bigr]\right)\,,

where for
j=1,2
,

\Psi_{j}(\infty)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{1\leq|\lambda|\leq R}\frac{1}{\lambda^{j}}

with
convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
. For
j=1
, this limit exists under the
spectral assumption (
1.2
); see Lemma 3.3 in Part I.
It is worth mentioning that a straightforward computation
(see Theorem 6.2 in Part I) shows that

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi(0)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{|\lambda|\leq R}\big(\log|a-\lambda|-\log|\lambda|\big)-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi c_{\Lambda}|a|^{2},

where the convergence is locally uniform in
a
.

Before we proceed, we derive a useful formula for the variance of

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi(z)
. By Theorem 6.2 in Part I,

\Pi_{\Lambda}
has stationary increments, so it suffices to analyze

{\sf Var}\big[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\big]
.

Claim 5.2
.

For any
a\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

{\sf Var}\big[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\big]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}K(|s|)\Phi(a/s)\,{{\rm d}}m(s)

(5.16)

where

\Phi(a/s)\overset{{\sf def}}{=}2\log|s|-\log|s-a|-\log|s+a|=-\log\left|1-\frac{a^{2}}{s^{2}}\right|

(5.17)

and where
K(s)=K(|s|)
is the same as above (defined in Lemma
4.1
).

Proof.

Let

\varphi_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{\bar{z}-\bar{a}}-\frac{1}{\bar{z}}\Big)\,

and note that
\partial\varphi_{a}=\delta_{a}-\delta_{0}
. As
\partial\,\Pi_{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{2}V_{\Lambda}
, we get

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)=\frac{1}{2}V_{\Lambda}(\varphi_{a})\,.

(5.18)

By Remark
5.1
, the spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}

is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure and has a

C^{1}
-regular bounded (radial) density
h_{\Lambda}
. Furthermore, a simple computation shows that

\widehat{\varphi}_{a}(\xi)=\big(1-e^{-2\pi{\rm i}a\cdot\xi}\big)\,\frac{1}{{\rm i}\overline{\xi}}\,,

in the sense of distributions, and Theorem 5.8 from Part I implies that

{\sf Var}\left[V_{\Lambda}(\varphi_{a})\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\big|\widehat{\varphi}_{a}(\xi)\big|^{2}\,\frac{{{\rm d}}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\big|\widehat{\varphi}_{a}(\xi)\big|^{2}\,\frac{h_{\Lambda}(|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}\,{\rm d}m(\xi)<\infty\,.

(5.19)

In particular, by (
5.18
) we see that

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
and it remains to
prove the formula for
{\sf Var}\big[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\big]
.
By rotational invariance of
\Lambda
, we may assume that
a>0
.
Recall that the function
K
is the (distributional)
Fourier transform of
|\xi|^{-2}h_{\Lambda}
, the spectral measure of
V_{\Lambda}
,
see (
4.3
). As both
|\widehat{\varphi}_{a}(\xi)|^{2}

and
|\xi|^{-2}h_{\Lambda}
are in
L^{2}({\mathbb{C}},m)
, we may apply the
Plancherel identity to (
5.19
) and get that

{\sf Var}\left[V_{\Lambda}(\varphi_{a})\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\big(\varphi_{a}\ast\overline{\varphi}_{a}\big)(s)\,K(|s|)\,{{\rm d}}m(s)\,.

(5.20)

To compute the convolution
\varphi_{a}\ast\overline{\varphi}_{a}
, observe that

\big(\varphi_{a}\ast\overline{\varphi}_{a}\big)(s)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}\Big(\frac{1}{\bar{z}-a}-\frac{1}{\bar{z}}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{z-s-a}-\frac{1}{z-s}\Big)\,{\rm d}m(z)\,,

and by expanding the product we see that

\big(\varphi_{a}\ast\overline{\varphi}_{a}\big)(s)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{|z|\leq R}\Big(\frac{1}{(\bar{z}-a)(z-a-s)}-\frac{1}{(\bar{z}-a)(z-s)}\Big){{\rm d}}m(z)\\
+\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{|z|\leq R}\Big(\frac{1}{\bar{z}(z-s)}-\frac{1}{\bar{z}(z-a-s)}\Big){{\rm d}}m(z)\,.

(5.21)

Now, for any
\alpha>0
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have that

\displaystyle\int_{|z|\leq R}\frac{{{\rm d}}m(z)}{(\bar{z}-\alpha)(z-\beta)}

\displaystyle=2\int_{|z|\leq R}\bar{\partial}\log|z-\alpha|\,\frac{{{\rm d}}m(z)}{z-\beta}

\displaystyle=2\pi\log|\beta-\alpha|-\frac{1}{{\rm i}}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{\log|z-\alpha|}{z-\beta}\,{{\rm d}}z

\displaystyle=2\pi\log|\beta-\alpha|-2\pi\log R+o(1)

as
R\to\infty
. Combining this with (
5.21
), we find that

\big(\varphi_{a}\ast\overline{\varphi}_{a}\big)(s)=\frac{2}{\pi}\big(2\log|s|-\log|s-a|-\log|s+a|\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def_of_Phi}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{\pi}\Phi(a/s)\,.

Plugging back into (
5.20
) gives

{\sf Var}\left[V_{\Lambda}(\varphi_{a})\right]=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}K(|s|)\,\Phi(a/s)\,{{\rm d}}m(s)

which, together with (
5.18
), gives the claim.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume that the truncated two-point function
k_{\Lambda}
satisfies

(1+t^{3})k_{\Lambda}(t)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},{{\rm d}}t)
. Then

{\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\right]=\left(D_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\log|a|,

as
a\to\infty
, where

D_{\Lambda}=2\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{3}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,{{\rm d}}t.

Proof.

By Claim
5.2
, we have

{\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(z)\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}K(|s|)\Phi(a/s)\,{{\rm d}}m(s)

(5.22)

where
\Phi
is defined by (
5.17
) and

K(s)=-4\pi^{2}\int_{r}^{\infty}\log\Big(\frac{|s|}{t}\Big)k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t\,{{\rm d}}t.

We first note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\Big(\frac{a}{re^{{\rm i}\theta}}\Big){{\rm d}}\theta

\displaystyle=2\log r-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big(\log|a-re^{{\rm i}\theta}|-\log|a+re^{{\rm i}\theta}|\Big){{\rm d}}\theta

\displaystyle=-2\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{r}\Big).

Incorporating this formula into (
5.22
), we get

\displaystyle{\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(r)\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\Big(\frac{a}{re^{{\rm i}\theta}}\Big){{\rm d}}\theta\right]r\,{{\rm d}}r

\displaystyle=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}K(r)\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{r}\Big)r\,{{\rm d}}r

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{r}^{\infty}\log\Big(\frac{t}{r}\Big)k_{\Lambda}(t)t\,{{\rm d}}t\right]\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{r}\Big)r\,{{\rm d}}r

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t\left[\int_{0}^{t}\log\Big(\frac{t}{r}\Big)\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{r}\Big)r\,{{\rm d}}r\right]{{\rm d}}t.

The inner integral on the right-hand side simplifies to

\int_{0}^{t}\log\Big(\frac{t}{r}\Big)\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{r}\Big)r\,{{\rm d}}r=t^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{tu}\Big)u\,{{\rm d}}u,

whence

\displaystyle{\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\right]

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{tu}\Big)u\,{{\rm d}}u\right]{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{a}+\int_{a}^{\infty}\right)k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)\log_{+}\Big(\frac{a}{tu}\Big)u\,{{\rm d}}u\right]{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf def}}{=}8\pi^{2}\big(J_{1}(a)+J_{2}(a)\big)\,.

By our assumption
\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\infty}|k_{\Lambda}(t)|\,t^{3}{{\rm d}}t<\infty}
,

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)\left(\log\Big(\frac{a}{t}\Big)+\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)\right)u\,{{\rm d}}u\right]{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle=C_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\log\Big(\frac{a}{t}\Big){{\rm d}}t+C_{2}+o(1)

as
a\to\infty
, where
\displaystyle{C_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}\log(1/u)u\,{{\rm d}}u=\tfrac{1}{4}}
and

C_{2}=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\,{{\rm d}}t\right)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{1}\log^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)u\,{{\rm d}}u\right).

Moreover, we note that

\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\log\Big(\frac{a}{t}\Big){{\rm d}}t=\log a\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{3}\,\frac{\log a-\log t}{\log a}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{[0,a]}(t)\,{{\rm d}}t\right],

and the expression in brackets converges to
\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\,{{\rm d}}t}

as
a\to\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem. Hence,
the integral
J_{1}(a)
satisfies

J_{1}(a)=\Big(\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\,{{\rm d}}t\Big)\log a+O(1)

(5.23)

as
a\to\infty
. For the second integral
J_{2}(a)
, we have

\displaystyle J_{2}(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{3}\left[\int_{0}^{a/t}\log\Big(\frac{1}{u}\Big)\cdot\log\Big(\frac{a}{tu}\Big)u\,{{\rm d}}u\right]{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t\left[a^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\log\Big(\frac{t}{av}\Big)\cdot\log\Big(\frac{1}{v}\Big)v\,{{\rm d}}v\right]{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle=a^{2}\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t\left[C_{3}\log\Big(\frac{t}{a}\Big)+C_{4}\right]{{\rm d}}t\,,

for some explicit constants
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
. Furthermore, we have the bound

\displaystyle a^{2}\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t\log\Big(\frac{a}{t}\Big)\,{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle\leq a^{2}\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t\,\log t\,{{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle=a^{2}\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,\frac{t^{3}}{t^{2}\log t}\,{{\rm d}}t\lesssim\log a\int_{a}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)\,t^{3}\,{{\rm d}}t=o\big(\log a\big)

as
a\to\infty
. Combining the asymptotics for

J_{1}(a)
and
J_{2}(a)
with the identity

{\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\right]=8\pi^{2}\big(J_{1}(a)+J_{2}(a)\big)
,
we find that

{\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\right]=\left(2\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{\Lambda}(t)t^{3}\,{{\rm d}}t+o(1)\right)\log a

as
a\to\infty
, which is what we wanted.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Assume without loss of generality that

\Gamma
starts at the origin and ends at
a>0
,
and let
\gamma:I\to\Gamma
denote a parametrization of

\Gamma
. Notice first that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., no point
of
\Lambda
lies exactly on
R\Gamma
. Hence,
for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we may choose a branch
of the logarithm such that
\log(\gamma(t)-\lambda)^{-1}

is continuous on
I
, and we find that

\operatorname{Re}\int_{R\Gamma}\frac{{{\rm d}z}}{z-\lambda}=\log|Ra-\lambda|-\log|\lambda|.

As a consequence, we find that (see (
5.3
) for the definition
of
{\sf\Delta}\Pi_{a}(0)
)

\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)=\lim_{S\to\infty}\sum_{|\lambda|\leq S}\left(\log|Ra-\lambda|-\log|\lambda|\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}c_{\Lambda}R^{2}={\sf\Delta}_{Ra}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0),

where the convergence is in the sense of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

(this was justified in §6, Part I).
Hence, by applying Theorem
5.3
, we find that

\displaystyle{\sf Var}\left[\mathcal{W}_{\Lambda}(R\Gamma)\right]={\sf Var}\left[{\sf\Delta}_{Ra}\Pi_{\Lambda}(0)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(D_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\log(Ra)

\displaystyle=\left(D_{\Lambda}+o(1)\right)\log R.

This completes the proof.
∎

6
Rectifiable Jordan arcs with large variance

6.1
Nested disks with large charge fluctuations

In this section, we specialize to the case when

\Lambda
is the infinite Ginibre point process.
Denote by
n_{\Lambda}
the corresponding point
count measure. We will show that for any fixed
\varepsilon>0

there exists a rectifiable curve
\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}

such that

{\sf Var}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{R\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{{\rm d}z}\right]\gtrsim R^{2-\varepsilon}.

(6.1)

Let
\ell_{k}=k^{-1-\varepsilon}
. To describe the
desired curve
\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
, we begin with a
concatenation of circles (all with positive orientation)

\{|z|=\ell_{k}\}
, and add arcs
\{{\rm i}zt\mid\ell_{k}+1\leq t\leq\ell_{k}\}

which connect subsequent circles along the imaginary axis.
The curve
\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
is not simple nor closed
(in the upcoming
section, we will deform
\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
into a simple Jordan arc),
but since
(\ell_{k})
is a summable sequence it is rectifiable.
By the argument principle,

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{R\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{{\rm d}z}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}n_{\Lambda}(R\ell_{k}\,{\mathbb{D}})-\int_{0}^{R}V_{\Lambda}({\rm i}w)\,{\rm d}w\,.

Since the variance of the last term on the right-hand side is
of order
O(R)
(for instance, by Theorem
1.3
),
the lower bound (
6.1
)
will follow once we show that

{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}n_{\Lambda}(R\ell_{k}\,{\mathbb{D}})\right]\gtrsim R^{2-\varepsilon}\,,

(6.2)

which we will do by applying Kostlan’s theorem, a result
which is specific for radially symmetric determinantal point processes
such as the infinite Ginibre ensemble.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 4.7.1]
)
.

Let
\{\,|\lambda_{j}|:\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda\}
be the set of
absolute values of the Ginibre process ordered by non-decreasing modulus.
Then
|\lambda_{j}|
are independent with

|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\sim\text{\sf{Gamma}}(j,1)\,.

Here,
\text{\sf{Gamma}}(j,1)
denotes the standard
Gamma distribution with density given by
f_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(j)}x^{j-1}e^{-x}
,
x>0
.
We use Theorem
6.1
to prove
(
6.2
). Indeed,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}n_{\Lambda}(R\ell_{k}\,{\mathbb{D}})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\leq(R\ell_{k})^{2}\}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\leq(R\ell_{k})^{2}\}}\right)

and since
\{|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\}_{j\geq 1}
are independent
we get the lower bound

{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}n_{\Lambda}(R\ell_{k}\,{\mathbb{D}})\right]\geq{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{Z\leq(R\ell_{k})^{2}\}}\right]

where
Z=|\lambda_{1}|^{2}\sim\text{\sf exp}(1)\overset{{\sf def}}{=}{\sf Gamma}(1,1)
. In view of
the above, the lower bound (
6.2
)
follows from the following simple claim.

Claim 6.2
.

If
Z\sim\text{\sf exp}(1)
then

{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{Z\leq(R\ell_{k})^{2}\}}\right]\gtrsim R^{2-\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Denote by

Y_{R}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{Z\leq(R\ell_{k})^{2}\}}\,.

Since
\mathbb{P}(Z\geq t)=\exp(-t)
, we can bound
the expectation of
Y_{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{R}]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z\leq(R\ell_{k})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{-(R\ell_{k})^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq M+R^{2}\sum_{k=M}^{\infty}\ell_{k}^{2}\leq M+3R^{2}M^{-1-2\varepsilon}

for all
M\geq 1
. By choosing

M=\lfloor R^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}\rfloor
we obtain

\mathbb{E}[Y_{R}]\leq 5R^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}\,.

Furthermore, we have the inclusion of events

\displaystyle\left\{Y_{R}\geq 6R^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}\right\}

\displaystyle\supset\left\{Z\leq\left(R\ell_{\lfloor 6R^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}\rfloor}\right)^{2}\right\}\supset\left\{Z\leq 6^{-2(1+\varepsilon)}\right\}\supset\left\{Z\leq 0.01\right\}\,.

Since
Z\sim\text{\sf exp}(1)
, the latter event has
strictly positive probability, and combining all together we get

\displaystyle{\sf Var}[Y_{R}]=\mathbb{E}[(Y_{R}-

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{R}])^{2}]

\displaystyle\gtrsim R^{2/(1+\varepsilon)}\,\mathbb{P}(Y_{R}\geq 6R^{1/(1+\varepsilon)})\geq R^{2/(1+\varepsilon)}\,\mathbb{P}(Z\leq 0.01)

as desired.
∎

6.2
Nested disks to spirals

Figure 4
:
Left:
The sequence of seashell domains (shaded),
each component is enclosed between a circle,
one revolution of the spiral, and a vertical line segment.

Right:

A single seashell domain
D_{k}
, with the arcs
\gamma_{k}^{\prime}
,
\gamma_{k}^{\perp}

and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
indicated.

We continue our construction of a rectifiable Jordan arc
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
with
superlinear growth of
{\sf Var}[\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}(\Gamma)]
when
\Lambda
is the infinite
Ginibre ensemble. For a given
\varepsilon>0
, we have
found a curve
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
of the form

\mathcal{C}={\rm i}[0,\ell_{1}]\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 1}{\mathbb{D}}(0,\ell_{k})

such that

{\sf Var}\left[\int_{R\mathcal{C}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{{\rm d}z}\right]\gtrsim R^{2-\varepsilon}.

(6.3)

We want to turn
\mathcal{C}
into a Jordan arc with
the bound (
6.3
) preserved.
We do so by forming a spiral
\Gamma=\cup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}
,
where each revolution
\gamma_{k}
interpolates
between the intersection of successive circles

\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
with the imaginary axis; i.e.

\gamma_{k}=\left\{\left(\ell_{k}(1-t)+\ell_{k+1}t\right)e^{2\pi{\rm i}t}:0\leq t\leq 1\right\}.

With the help of (
6.3
), we prove that

{\sf Var}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{R\Gamma}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{{\rm d}z}\right]\gtrsim R^{2-\varepsilon}

as well. To see why this is so, we first recall that for the
Ginibre point process, the (radial) two-point function
k_{\Lambda}(t)
is given by

k_{\Lambda}(t)=-\pi^{-2}e^{-\pi t^{2}}\,,

see §2.4, Part I. Integration by parts yields

K(z)=4\int_{|z|}^{\infty}\log\Big(\frac{r}{|z|}\Big)re^{-\pi r^{2}}\,{{\rm d}}r=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{|z|}^{\infty}e^{-\pi r^{2}}\,\frac{{\rm d}r}{r}\,,

which implies that

\bar{\partial}K(z)=\frac{e^{-\pi|z|^{2}}}{\pi\bar{z}}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{C}},{\rm d}m)\,.

We let
\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
denote the
short vertical segment

\gamma^{\prime}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\{{\rm i}t:\ell_{k+1}\leq t\leq\ell_{k}\}
.
Then we obtain a closed curve by taking the union

\mathcal{C}_{k}\cup\gamma_{k}^{\perp}\cup\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
(traversing
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with its original orientation, and
where
\gamma_{k}^{\perp}
is
\gamma_{k}
traversed backwards),
which encloses a domain
D_{k}
;
cf. Figure
4
. All the
domains
D_{k}
are disjoint, and
we denote their union by
D
(the “seashell”).

By Green’s formula, we have for each
k\geq 0
that

\int_{R(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cup\gamma_{k}^{\perp}\cup\gamma^{\prime}_{k})}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y=2{\rm i}\int_{RD_{k}}\bar{\partial}_{y}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}m(y),

so that

\displaystyle\int_{R\gamma_{k}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{R\gamma_{k}^{\perp}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{R(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cup\gamma_{k}^{\prime})}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y-2{\rm i}\int_{RD_{k}}\bar{\partial}_{y}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}m(y).

Adding up the contributions for
k\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{R\Gamma}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{R(\mathcal{C}\cup i[0,\ell_{1}])}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y-2{\rm i}\int_{RD}\bar{\partial}_{y}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}m(y)

(6.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{R\mathcal{C}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y+O(1),

(6.5)

where we have used that fact that
\bar{\partial}K\in L^{1}({\mathbb{C}},{\rm d}m)
.
Integrating over
R\Gamma
, we find that

\displaystyle\iint_{R\Gamma\times R\Gamma}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}\bar{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{R\mathcal{C}}\left[\int_{R\Gamma}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}\bar{x}\right]{\rm d}y+O(R)

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\iint_{R\Gamma\times R\mathcal{C}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}\bar{x}+O(R).

(6.7)

Repeating the above
argument, we finally arrive at

\iint_{R\Gamma\times R\Gamma}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}\bar{x}=\iint_{R\mathcal{C}\times R\mathcal{C}}K(|x-y|)\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}\bar{x}+O(R),

from which the claim follows.
