A stochastic moving ball approximation method for smooth convex constrained minimization
Nitesh Kumar Singh, Ion Necoara

Abstract
In this paper, we consider constrained optimization problems with convex objective and smooth convex constraints. We propose a new stochastic gradient algorithm, called the Stochastic Moving Ball Approximation (SMBA) method, to solve this class of problems, where at each iteration we first take a (sub)gradient step for the objective function and then perform a projection step onto one ball approximation of a randomly chosen constraint. The computational simplicity of SMBA, which uses first-order information and considers only one constraint at a time, makes it suitable for large-scale problems with many functional constraints. We provide a convergence analysis for the SMBA algorithm using basic assumptions on the problem, that yields new convergence rates in both optimality and feasibility criteria evaluated at some average point. Our convergence proofs are novel since we need to deal properly with infeasible iterates and with quadratic upper approximations of constraints that may yield empty balls. We derive convergence rates of order 𝒪 ( k − 1 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(k^{-1/2}) when the objective function is convex, and 𝒪 ⁡ ( k − 1 ) \mathcal{O}(k^{-1}) when the objective function is strongly convex. Preliminary numerical experiments on quadratically constrained quadratic problems demonstrate the viability and performance of our method when compared to some existing state-of-the-art optimization methods and software.



keywords
Smooth convex constrained problems, quadratic approximation, stochastic projection algorithm, convergence rates.

Introduction

In this paper, we address the intricate challenges associated with the optimization of smooth functions subject to complex constraints. The central focus is on the following constrained optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{rl}f^{*}=&\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f(x)\\
&\text{subject to }\;h(x,\xi)\leq 0\;\;\forall\xi\in\Omega,\end{array}

(1)

where the constraints
h(\cdot,\xi):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\;\forall\xi\in\Omega
, are assumed to be proper, convex, continuously differentiable smooth functions, while the objective
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a proper convex function. Additionally,
\mathcal{Y}
is a nonempty closed convex set which admits easy projections and
\Omega
is an arbitrary collection of indices (can be even infinite). Hence, we separate the feasible set in two parts: one set,
\mathcal{Y}
, admits easy projections and the other part is not easy for projection as it is described by the level sets of some convex functions
h(\cdot,\xi)
’s. For such problems, we face many computational and theoretical challenges, particularly, when they involve a
large
number of constraints.

Motivation
. General constrained problems in the form of (
1
), with a large number of constraints, appear in many applications, such as systems and control
BerBac:22
;
NedDin:14
;
NecNed:21
, machine learning and data science
Vap:98
;
CheLi:21
;
BhaGra:04
, signal processing
Nec:20
;
Tib:11
, and operations research and finance
RocUry:00
. For example, in chance constrained minimization one needs to solve a problem of the form
JinWan:22
:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\;\;\;\texttt{Prob${\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(h(x,\xi)}\leq 0)$}\geq 1-\tau,

(2)

where
\xi
is a random variable defined over a probability space. It has been proven in
CamGar:11
that the feasible set
\{x\in\mathcal{Y}:{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h(x,\xi)}\leq 0\quad\forall\xi\in\Omega\}

approximates the true feasible set of (
2
) as the number of indices in
\Omega
is sufficiently large. Therefore, chance constrained minimization (
2
) can be well approximated by an optimization problem of the form (
1
). Moreover, quadratic functions are a powerful modeling construct in optimization and appear in various fields such as statistics, machine learning, portfolio optimization, optimal power flow, and control theory
CheLi:21
;
WanTro:21
. In all these applications one needs to solve quadratically constrained quadratic programs (QCQP) of the form:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Q_{f}x+q_{f}^{T}x\quad\text{s.t.}\;\;\;\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Q_{i}x+q_{i}^{T}x-b_{i}\leq 0\;\;\forall i=1:m,

(3)

which is a particular case of our optimization problem (
1
), when the matrices
Q_{f}
and
Q_{i}
, for all
i=1:m
, are positive semidefinite.

Related work
.
In the realm of optimization, various first-order methods were designed to address the smooth optimization problem (
1
). A notable algorithm, which also represents the motivation for our current work, is the moving balls approximation (MBA) method
AusTeb:10
. Specifically tailored for smooth (possibly nonconvex) constrained problems, MBA generates a sequence of feasible points that approximates the constraints set by a sequence of balls, thus converting the original problem into a sequence of convex subproblems. Despite the fact that MBA exhibits linear convergence for smooth and strongly convex objective and constraints and asymptotic convergence for the nonconvex case, it requires
initialization
at a feasible point and uses at each iteration the
full
set of constraints, thus making the subproblem difficult to solve when the number of constraints is large.
To obtain a simpler subproblem, in the same paper a variant of MBA is introduced that incorporates an active set technique, called MBA-AS, which has similar convergence properties as original MBA.
An enhanced version of MBA, named the sequential simple quadratic method (SSQM), is presented in
Aus:13
. SSQM offers similar convergence results to MBA but eliminates the necessity to initiate from a feasible point. Other extensions of the MBA method (with possibly unbiased estimates of the gradients) are provided in
BooLan:22
,
BooLan:23
, where composite objective and functional constraints are considered in the problem formulation and improved convergence results are derived in both convex and nonconvex settings. Finally,
Nes:18
presents a first-order method to solve the smooth strongly convex problem (
1
) by converting it into a sequence of parametric max-type convex subproblems and derives a linear convergence rate for such scheme. However, these algorithms suffer from two technical issues: first, they need to consider the
full
set of constraints in the subproblem making them intractable when the number of constraints is large; second, they require an
initialization
phase which can be computationally expensive.

It is well-known that stochastic methods can overcome such technical problems. Stochastic (sub)gradient framework is a prevalent methodology for minimizing finite sum objective functions
RobMon:51
;
NemYud:83
. When combined with simple constraints, and the computation of the prox or projection operators is straightforward, a plethora of methods emerge, such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and stochastic proximal point (SPP) algorithms
MouBac:11
;
NemJud:09
;
Nec:20
;
WanBer:16
. Numerous extensions of SGD/SPP have been devised for solving convex problem (
1
) with
nonsmooth
functional constraints. At each iteration, such schemes perform a stochastic subgradient step for the objective and then project this step onto a halfspace lower approximation of a randomly chosen constraint, see e.g.,
Ned:11
;
NecSin:22
, and their mini-batch variants
SinNec:23
;
NecNed:21
. Additionally, Lagrangian primal-dual stochastic subgradient methods
(i.e., algorithms where both the primal and dual variables are updated at each iteration)
for convex nonsmooth functional constraint problems have been also designed, see e.g.,
Xu:20
.
The results from
Xu:20
can be also extended to the smooth constrained case using only tools from nonsmooth optimization
. All these algorithms achieve sublinear rates of convergence in terms of optimality and feasibility for an average sequence when they are combined with decreasing stepsize rules. However, extensions of stochastic first-order methods to problems with
smooth
functional constraints are limited, mainly because for such constraints the corresponding quadratic
upper
approximations may lead to empty sets (balls) in the subproblem. For example, a single-loop stochastic augmented Lagrangian primal-dual method is proposed in
JinWan:22
for solving problem (
1
) with smooth
nonconvex
functional constraints. This method achieves a convergence rate of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/4})
, where
k
is the iteration counter, for finding an approximate KKT point. In cases where the initial point is feasible, this order reduces to
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/3})
. However, to ensure the boundedness of dual multipliers, the penalty parameter in augmented Lagrangian and the stepsizes must be chosen dependent on the final iteration counter, hence it needs to be fixed a priori.

Contributions
.
In this paper we remove some of the previous issues, proposing a novel method for solving smooth constrained problems, called the
Stochastic Moving Ball Approximation
(SMBA) algorithm, that performs first a (sub)gradient step for the objective and then projects this step onto a quadratic upper approximation of a randomly chosen constraint using an adaptive stepsize. Thus, SMBA updates only the primal decision variables, omitting dual updates, marking a distinctive contribution.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper presents the first convergence analysis of such a primal stochastic algorithm in the smooth settings
. Hence, this paper makes the following key contributions.

(i)
We consider a general optimization model in the form of an optimization problem (
1
), with the objective being either convex or strongly convex and with a large number of smooth convex functional constraints.

(ii)
We design a new primal algorithm, called Stochastic Moving Ball Approximation (SMBA), to solve such optimization problems with smooth, convex functional constraints. At each iteration, SMBA performs a (sub)gradient step for the objective function, and then takes a projection step onto a quadratic upper approximation of a randomly chosen constraint using an adaptive stepsize rule. The computational simplicity of the SMBA subproblem, which uses gradient information and considers only one constraint at a time, makes it suitable for problems with many functional constraints. Notably, SMBA updates only the primal decision variables and does not need to be initialized at a feasible point, which makes it advantageous over existing algorithms like MBA
AusTeb:10
or the method in
JinWan:22
. Moreover, our algorithm is novel, since it needs to deal properly with quadratic upper approximations of constraints that may yield empty balls.

(iii)
We provide a convergence analysis for the SMBA algorithm, under basic assumptions, that yields new convergence rates in both optimality and feasibility criteria evaluated at some average point when the functional constraints are convex. In particular, we derive convergence rates of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/2})
when the objective function is convex, and
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
when the objective function is strongly convex. To the best of our knowledge, these convergence results for SMBA are new and match the known convergence rates for stochastic first-order methods from the literature. Moreover, our proofs are novel, since we need to deal properly with infeasible iterates and with quadratic upper approximations of constraints that may yield empty sets.

(iv)
Finally, numerical simulations on large-scale (strongly) convex quadratic problems with convex quadratic constraints using synthetic and real data demonstrate the viability and performance of our method when compared to some state-of-the-art optimization algorithms, such as its deterministic counterparts MBA
and MBA-AS

AusTeb:10
, and dedicated software, e.g., FICO
FICO
and Gurobi
Gurobi
.

Notations and assumptions

Throughout this paper, we use the following notations. For optimization problem (
1
) we consider the individual sets
\mathcal{X}_{\xi}
as:

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}_{\xi}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(x,\xi)\leq 0\}\;\;\;\forall\xi\in\Omega.}

Thus, the feasible set of (
1
) is:

\mathcal{X}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{Y}\cap(\cap_{\xi\in\Omega}\mathcal{X}_{\xi})}.

We assume the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
to be non-empty and to be difficult to project onto it. We also assume that the optimization problem (
1
) has a finite optimum and we let
f^{*}
and
\mathcal{X}^{*}
to denote the optimal value and the optimal set, respectively:

f^{*}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x),\quad\mathcal{X}^{*}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\;f(x)=f^{*}\}\neq\emptyset.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote its projection onto the optimal set
\mathcal{X}^{*}
by
\bar{x}
, i.e.,
\bar{x}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}(x)
(we use the notation
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
for projection of a point
x
onto a closed convex set
\mathcal{B}
). For a given scalar
a
we denote
(a)_{+}=\max(a,0)
. We make no assumption on the differentiability of
f
and use, with some abuse of notation, the same expression for the gradient or the subgradient of
f
at
x
, that is
\nabla f(x)\in\partial f(x)
, where the subdifferential
\partial f(x)
is either a singleton or a nonempty set.
Let
g
be a smooth function (i.e., it has Lipschitz continuous gradient), then the quadratic upper approximation of
g
at a point
x
is denoted as:

\displaystyle q_{g}(y;x)=g(x)+\langle\nabla g(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L_{g}}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},

where
L_{g}>0
denotes the Lipschitz constant of the gradient of
g
. Equivalently, we have the following rewriting for the quadratic approximation:

\displaystyle\frac{2}{L_{g}}q_{g}(y;x)

\displaystyle=\left\|y-\left(x-\frac{1}{L_{g}}\nabla g(x)\right)\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{L_{g}^{2}}\|\nabla g(x)\|^{2}+\frac{2g(x)}{L_{g}}

\displaystyle=\|y-c_{x,g}\|^{2}-R_{x,g},

where we denote the center and the radius of the corresponding ball with

c_{x,g}=x-\frac{1}{L_{g}}\nabla g(x)\;\;\text{and}\;\;R_{x,g}=\frac{1}{L_{g}^{2}}\|\nabla g(x)\|^{2}-\frac{2g(x)}{L_{g}}.

Finally, for optimization problem (
1
), we impose the following assumptions. First, let us present the assumptions related to the objective function
f
:

Assumption 1
.

The function
f
satisfies a (strong) convexity condition, i.e., there exists
\mu\geq 0
such that the following relation holds:

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{Y}.

(4)

From Assumption
1
, the objective function
f
is convex when
\mu=0
and when
\mu>0
there exists a unique
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle f(x)-f(x^{*})\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}.

(5)

Next, we state the assumptions for the functional constraints
h(\cdot,\xi)
:

Assumption 2
.

The convex functional constraints
h(\cdot,\xi)
are smooth (have Lipschitz continuous gradient), i.e.,
there exists

L_{\xi}>0
such that the following relation is true:

\|\nabla h(x,\xi)-\nabla h(y,\xi)\|\leq L_{\xi}\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathcal{Y}}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\forall\xi\in\Omega.

As a consequence of this assumption we also have (see Lemma 1.2.3 in
Nes:18
):

\displaystyle h(y,\xi)\leq h(x,\xi)+\langle\nabla h(x,\xi),y-x\rangle+\frac{L_{\xi}}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\;\;\;\forall x,y\in{\mathcal{Y}},\;\forall\xi\in\Omega.

(6)

Note that the right side of (
6
) is the quadratic upper approximation for the smooth functional constraints. Finally, our last assumption is a linear regularity type condition on the functional constraints. Let us endow
\Omega
with a probability distribution
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the expectation of a scalar depending on the random variable
\xi\in\Omega
.

Assumption 3
.

The functional constraints satisfy the following regularity condition, i.e., there exists a non-negative constant
c>0
such that:

{\rm dist}^{2}(y,\mathcal{X})\leq c\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[(h(y,\xi))_{+}^{2}\right],\;\;\forall y\in{\mathcal{Y}}.

(7)

Note that Assumption
3
is used frequently in the literature, see e.g.,
Ned:11
;
NecSin:22
. It holds e.g., when the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
has an interior point, when the feasible set is polyhedral, or, more general, when a strengthened Slater condition holds for the collection of functional constraints, such as the generalized Robinson condition
LewPan:98
(Corollary 3).

3
Stochastic moving ball approximation algorithm

In this section, we propose a novel stochastic gradient algorithm, called the Stochastic Moving Ball Approximation (SMBA) method, to solve problem (
1
). At each iteration, SMBA takes a (sub)gradient step for the objective function, to minimize it, and then performs a projection step onto one ball approximation of a randomly chosen constraint to reduce the infeasibility. Hence, for a given iteration
k
, we consider the random variable
\xi_{k}
sampled from
\Omega
according to the probability distribution
\mathbf{P}
and then we perform the following steps (by convention we assume
0/0=0
):

Algorithm 1 (SMBA)
:

\text{Choose}\;x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\text{positive stepsize sequence}\;{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 0}}
and
\beta>0
.

\text{For}\;k\geq 0\;\text{repeat:}

\displaystyle\text{Sample}\;\xi_{k}\sim\textbf{P}\;\text{and update:}

\displaystyle v_{k}={\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}}(x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}))

(8)

Choose
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\geq 0
and compute:

\displaystyle z_{k}=v_{k}-\beta\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})

(9)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(z_{k}).

(10)

The computational simplicity of SMBA, which uses only the (sub)gradient of the objective and the gradient of a single constraint at a time, makes it suitable for large scale problems with many functional constraints. Note that many primal first-order algorithms
(i.e., algorithms updating at each iteration only the primal variables,
x
, based on gradient information)
have been proposed in the literature for solving optimization problems with smooth functional constraints
AusTeb:10
;
Aus:13
;
BooLan:22
;
Nes:18
. However, these methods are usually deterministic, i.e., at each iteration
they need to evaluate the whole set of constraints and thus they do not scale well w.r.t. the number of constraints
.
From our best knowledge, SMBA is the first primal gradient algorithm that is of stochastic nature for solving optimization problems of the form (
1
), i.e., it uses the gradient of only one randomly chosen constraint at each iteration and thus it is scalable w.r.t. the number of functional constraints.

For simplicity of the exposition, for the functional constraint
h(\cdot,\xi_{k})
we denote the center
c_{v_{k},h(\cdot,\xi_{k})}=c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
and the radius
R_{v_{k},h(\cdot,\xi_{k})}=R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
. In order to deal properly with quadratic upper approximations of constraints that may yield empty balls, we consider the following
novel choice
for
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
, which depends on the current iterate, hence it is adaptive:

\displaystyle p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}=\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}{\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{(R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}})_{+}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)}.

(11)

Our algorithm SMBA has three main steps. First, (
8
) is a projected (sub)gradient step, i.e., we minimizes a quadratic approximation of the objective function
f
:

{u_{k}}=\argmin\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q_{f}(y;x_{k})}:=f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),y-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{k}}\|y-x_{k}\|^{2}\right),

which yields:

{u_{k}}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}),

and then we project
u_{k}
onto the simple set
\mathcal{Y}
to obtain an intermittent point
v_{k}
, i.e.,
v_{k}=\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(u_{k})
. It is important to note that we perform the projections in steps (
8
) and (
10
) in order to have our iterates
v_{k}
and
x_{k}
in the set
\mathcal{Y}
where our Assumptions
1
-
3
are valid. Next, we have a stochastic gradient projection step minimizing the feasibility violation of the chosen random constraint
\xi_{k}
. Although our feasibility step has a compact expression, it describes three types of updates. Indeed, combining the expressions of
z_{k}
from (
9
) with that of
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
from (
11
), we get:

\displaystyle z_{k}=\!\begin{cases}v_{k},&\!\mbox{if }h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\leq 0\\
v_{k}-\frac{\beta}{L_{\xi_{k}}}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k}),&\!\mbox{if }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0,h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0\\
v_{k}-\frac{\beta}{L_{\xi_{k}}}\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k}),&\!\mbox{if }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0,h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0.\end{cases}

Alternatively, if define the ball corresponding to the quadratic upper approximation of the single functional constraint
h(\cdot,\xi_{k})
:

\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}=\left\{y:\;q_{h(\cdot,\xi_{k})}(y;{v}_{k})\leq 0\right\}=\left\{y:\;\|y-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|^{2}\leq R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\right\},

then the update in (
9
) can be written equivalently as (see Appendix A):

\displaystyle z_{k}=\begin{cases}v_{k},&\mbox{if }h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\leq 0\\
(1-\beta)v_{k}+\beta\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}(v_{k}),&\mbox{if }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0,\;h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0\\
v_{k}-\frac{\beta}{L_{\xi_{k}}}\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k}),&\mbox{if }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0,\;h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0.\end{cases}

Thus, in this second step for a constraint
\xi_{k}\in\Omega
if
v_{k}
is feasible for that constraint we do not move. Otherwise, we project the point
v_{k}
onto the ball
\mathcal{B}_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
, which is nonempty if
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
; otherwise, if
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0
, then the ball is empty and we choose to minimize the quadratic approximation of
h(\cdot,\xi_{k})
at
v_{k}
, denoted
q_{h(\cdot,\xi_{k})}(y;v_{k})
. Finally, in the third step, we project
z_{k}
onto the simple set
\mathcal{Y}
(by simple we mean that the projection onto this set is easy).

Next, we provide a lemma which shows that our choice for
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
is well-defined.

Lemma 4
.

Let Assumption
2
hold. Then, the parameter
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
from (
11
) is well-defined and satisfies:

\displaystyle p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\begin{cases}=0,&\text{ if }\;h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\leq 0,\\
>0,&\text{ if }\;h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0.\end{cases}

(12)

Proof.

We will prove this statement by dividing it in two cases:

Case (i):
When
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\leq 0\implies(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}=0
, in this case we get:

p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step2SMBA}}{=}0.

Case (ii):
When
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
, in this case
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
can be positive, negative or zero. First, let us consider that
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
, then we have:

\displaystyle 0<h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\implies 0<\frac{2h(v_{k},\xi_{k})}{L_{\xi_{k}}}\implies-\frac{2h(v_{k},\xi_{k})}{L_{\xi_{k}}}<0

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}{L^{2}_{\xi_{k}}}-\frac{2h(v_{k},\xi_{k})}{L_{\xi_{k}}}<\frac{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}{L^{2}_{\xi_{k}}}

\displaystyle\implies R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}<\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|^{2}\implies\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}<\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|.

(13)

which shows that
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
in this case. Finally, considering the case
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0\implies(R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}})_{+}=0
, thus from the expression in (
11
) we get,
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
. Thus combining both cases completes the proof.
∎

Next, we provide some useful bounds on
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
, which will play a key role in our convergence analysis below.

Lemma 5
.

Let Assumption
2
hold. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

We always have the following lower bound for

\displaystyle(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}L_{\xi_{k}}\leq p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}.

(14)

(ii)

When
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
, we have the following tighter bounds for
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}{2}\leq p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\begin{cases}\leq\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2},&\text{ if }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0,\\
=h(v_{k},\xi_{k})L_{\xi_{k}},&\text{ if }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0.\end{cases}

Proof.

From (

)
and from the expressions of

and

p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}=\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}L_{\xi_{k}}}{\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{(R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}})_{+}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)}\geq(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}L_{\xi_{k}}.

Next, to prove (

Case (i):
When

, we have:

\displaystyle p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step2SMBA}}{=}\frac{h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}{\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)}=\frac{h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}{\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)}\frac{\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}{\left(1-\frac{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|^{2}}\right)}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}{\left(1-1+\frac{2h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}\right)}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right).

From (
3
), we know that
\sqrt{R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}<\|v_{k}-c_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\|
, when
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
and
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
. Hence, we get:

\frac{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}{2}\leq p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\overset{\eqref{eq:mid1SMBA}}{\leq}\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}.

Case (ii):
When
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0
and
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
, we have:

R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0\implies\frac{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}{2}\leq h(v_{k},\xi_{k})L_{\xi_{k}}\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step2SMBA}}{=}p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}.

Hence, combining both cases, we obtain the desired bounds (
15
).
∎

In our convergence analysis below we also assume the following bounds to hold:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\nabla f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))\|\leq\bar{B}_{f},\;\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq B_{f},\\
&\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi)\|\leq B_{h},\;|h(v_{k},\xi)|\leq M_{h}\;\;\;\forall\xi\in\Omega,\;\;\forall k\geq 0.\end{split}

(16)

In (
16
), the upper bounds related to the objective function
f
always hold provided that e.g.,
f
is Lipschitz continuous or is continuously differentiable function and the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by algorithm SMBA is bounded. Moreover, the upper bounds related to the functional constraints
h(\cdot,\xi)
, with
\xi\in\Omega
, always hold when the sequence
(v_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by SMBA is bounded (recall that
h(\cdot,\xi)
, with
\xi\in\Omega
, are assumed smooth functions). Note that the boundedness of a sequence generated by some algorithm is frequently employed in optimization, see e.g.,
Lemma
4.4
in
BolTeb:14
and Lemma
3.4
in
CohTeb:22
. In our settings however, since we know that the sequences
(x_{k},v_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by SMBA are always in the set
\mathcal{Y}
, their boundedness can be automatically ensured if the set
\mathcal{Y}
in our problem (
1
) is considered compact.

4
Convergence analysis of SMBA

In this section, we provide a convergence analysis for the SMBA algorithm under the basic assumptions from Section
2
, yielding new global convergence rates in optimality and feasibility criteria.
Our proofs are novel, since we need to deal properly with infeasible iterates and with quadratic upper approximations of constraints that yield empty sets.
We start our convergence analysis with some preliminary lemmas that are crucial in the derivation of the convergence rates. First, we prove some feasibility relation between
x_{k}
and
v_{k}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let the bounds from (
16
) hold. Then, we have the following relation true for the sequences generated by SMBA algorithm:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})-\alpha_{k}^{2}B_{f}^{2}\leq{\rm dist}^{2}(v_{k},\mathcal{X})\quad\forall k\geq 0.

(17)

Proof.

From the definition of projection (see
NecSin:22
), we have:

{\|v-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(v)\|^{2}\leq\|v-y\|^{2}\;\;\;\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;y\in\mathcal{X}.}

Hence, taking
v=x_{k}
and
y=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(v_{k})\in\mathcal{X}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(v_{k})\|^{2}=\|x_{k}-{v_{k}+v_{k}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(v_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|v_{k}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(v_{k})\|^{2}+2{\|x_{k}-v_{k}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step1SMBA}}{=}2\|v_{k}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(v_{k})\|^{2}+2{\|\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x_{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(u_{k})\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\;{\rm dist}^{2}(v_{k},\mathcal{X})+2\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2},

where the second inequality uses the relation
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}+2\|b\|^{2}
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the last inequality uses that
x_{k}\in\mathcal{Y},u_{k}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})
and the nonexpansiveness property of the projection operator, i.e.,
\|\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x_{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(u_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|x_{k}-u_{k}\|^{2}
. After using the bound on the (sub)gradients of
f
from (
16
) and rearranging the terms we obtain the claimed result.
∎

Our next lemma establishes a relation between
\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}
and
\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let Assumptions
2
and
3
hold and
h(\cdot,\xi)
, for all
\xi\in\Omega
, be convex functions. Additionally, assume that the bounds (
16
) are valid and

\beta\in(0,1)
. Denote
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}=\max(B_{h}^{2},M_{h}\cdot\max_{\xi\in\Omega}L_{\xi})
. Then, for any
k\geq 0
, we have the following recurrence true for sequences generated by SMBA:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{k}}[\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})+\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\alpha^{2}_{k}B_{f}^{2}.

(18)

Proof.

Recall that
\bar{x}
denotes the projection of a vector
x
onto the optimal set
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. Then, using the basic properties of the projection operator, we have:

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\|\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(z_{k})-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}=\|\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(z_{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bar{v}_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|z_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|z_{k}-v_{k}+v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}=\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}+2\langle z_{k}-v_{k},v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\rangle+\|z_{k}-v_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step3SMBA}}{=}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}+2\beta\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}\langle\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k}),\bar{v}_{k}-v_{k}\rangle+\|z_{k}-v_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}+2\beta\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}\left(h(\bar{v}_{k},\xi_{k})-h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\right)+\|z_{k}-v_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step3SMBA}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-2\beta\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))^{2}_{+}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}+\beta^{2}\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))^{2}_{+}}{p^{2}_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2},

(19)

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the constraints are feasible at
\bar{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
h(\bar{v}_{k},\xi_{k})\leq 0
and that
(a)_{+}a=(a)_{+}^{2}
, for any scalar
a
. Furthermore, we proceed by considering the following
cases.

Case (i):
When
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\leq 0\implies(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}=0
. Then, from (
12
) we have
p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}=0
. Thus, the last two terms on the right-hand side of (
4
) will disappear (recall that by convention
0/0=0
). Hence, (
4
) can be written as follows for any constant
\mathcal{B}_{h}>0
:

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}.

(20)

Case (ii):
When
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
and
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
, relation (
4
) yields:

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_p2SMBA}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-2\beta\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}+2\beta^{2}\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-2\beta(1-\beta)\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}

\displaystyle\!\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_p2SMBA}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-2\beta(1-\beta)\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_xksmooth}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{B_{h}^{2}}(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}.

(21)

Case (iii):
When
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
and
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0
,
from the definition of
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}
we have:

p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}=h(v_{k},\xi_{k})L_{\xi_{k}}\;\;\text{and }R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}\leq 0\implies\|\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\|^{2}\leq 2h(v_{k},\xi_{k})L_{\xi_{k}}.

Using this in (
4
), we get:

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-2\beta\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}+2\beta^{2}\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{p_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-2\beta(1-\beta)\frac{(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}}{h(v_{k},\xi_{k})\cdot L_{\xi_{k}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_xksmooth}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{M_{h}\cdot\max_{\xi\in\Omega}L_{\xi}}(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}.

(22)

Now, combining (
20
), (
4
) and (
4
), we get the following common recurrence:

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2},

where
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}=\max(B_{h}^{2},M_{h}\cdot\max_{\xi\in\Omega}L_{\xi})
. After taking expectation conditioned on
\xi_{k}
and using the linear regularity condition from Assumption
3
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{k}}[\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{k}}[(h(v_{k},\xi_{k}))_{+}^{2}]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:lin_regsmooth}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(v_{k},\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:lem_feasSMBA}}{\leq}\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})+\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\alpha^{2}_{k}B_{f}^{2}.

This proves our statement. ∎

4.1
Convergence rates for SMBA under convex objective

In this section, we consider that the objective function
f
is convex, i.e., the Assumption
1
(ii)
holds with
\mu=0
and derive the convergence rates for SMBA. First, in the following lemma, we provide a main recurrence.

Lemma 8
.

Let Assumptions
1
(with
\mu=0
),
2
and
3
hold and the bounds from (
16
) be valid. Moreover,
h(\cdot,\xi)
for all
\xi\in\Omega
are convex functions. Further, let the stepsizes
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{k}>0
for all
k\geq 0
. Then, we have the following relation true for the sequences generated by SMBA algorithm:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}]-2\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\left(f(x_{k})-f(\bar{x}_{k})\right)]

(23)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})]+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\alpha^{2}_{k}\quad k\geq 0,

where we denote
C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}=\left({1}+\frac{{2}\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

From the basic properties of the projection and recalling that
\bar{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
and
u_{k}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})
, we get:

\displaystyle\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}\;{\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step1SMBA}}{=}\;\|\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(u_{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bar{x}_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|u_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\rangle+\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_xksmooth}}{\leq}\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(x_{k})-f(\bar{x}_{k})\right)+B_{f}^{2}\alpha_{k}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from the convexity of
f
. Now, using it in (
18
), yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{k}}[\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq\|v_{k}-\bar{v}_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})+\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\alpha^{2}_{k}B_{f}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(x_{k})-f(\bar{x}_{k})\right)-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})+\left({1}+\frac{{2}\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\right)B_{f}^{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(x_{k})-f(\bar{x}_{k})\right)-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\alpha^{2}_{k},

where we denote
C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}=\left({1}+\frac{{2}\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\right)
. After taking full expectation we get the desired result.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main (sublinear) convergence rates of SMBA in the convex case. Let us define the following average sequences:

\hat{x}_{k}=\frac{1}{S_{k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}x_{t},\;\;\text{ and },\;\;\hat{w}_{k}=\frac{1}{S_{k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}),

where
S_{k}=\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}
and note that
\hat{w}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e., it is feasible.

Theorem 9
.

Let Assumptions
1
(with
\mu=0
),
2
and
3
hold and the bounds from (
16
) be valid. Moreover,
h(\cdot,\xi)
for all
\xi\in\Omega
are convex functions. Then, choosing the stepsizes
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{k}>0
non-increasing, we get the following bounds for the average sequence
\hat{x}_{k}
in terms of optimality and feasibility violation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(f(\hat{x}_{k})-f^{*})]\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}}{2S_{k}}+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{2S_{k}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k},\mathcal{X})]\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\beta(1-\beta)}\left(\frac{\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}+C_{\beta,C,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{S_{k}}\right).

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{k}
is a decreasing sequence, we have
\alpha_{k}/\alpha_{0}\leq 1
for all
k>0
. Summing the relation (
23
) from
0
to
k-1
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}-2\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[(f(x_{t})-f(\bar{x}_{t}))]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{\alpha_{0}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}]+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}.

Now, divide the whole inequality by
S_{k}
, we have:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{S_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{S_{k}}\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{2}{S_{k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[(f(x_{t})-f(\bar{x}_{t}))]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{\alpha_{0}S_{k}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}]+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{S_{k}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\frac{1}{S_{k}}\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}-2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}}{S_{k}}(f(x_{t})-f^{*})\right]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{\alpha_{0}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}}{S_{k}}\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}\right]+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{S_{k}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq\frac{1}{S_{k}}\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}-2\mathbb{E}\left[(f(\hat{x}_{k})-f^{*})\right]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{\alpha_{0}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}\right]+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{S_{k}}},

where the first inequality uses the linearity of the expectation operator and the second inequality is derived from the definitions of
\hat{x}_{k}
and
\hat{w}_{k}
and also from the convexity of
f
and
\|\cdot\|^{2}
, i.e.,
\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}}{S_{k}}\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}\geq\left\|\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}}{S_{k}}(x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}))\right\|^{2}=\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}
. Now, using the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}]\geq 0
, we further obtain:

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[(f(\hat{x}_{k})-f^{*})]+\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{\alpha_{0}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{S_{k}}.

We get the following bound in expectation for the average sequence
\hat{x}_{k}
in terms of optimality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(f(\hat{x}_{k})-f^{*})]\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}}{2S_{k}}+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{2S_{k}}.

Finally,
by using the fact that
\hat{w}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, we obtain
the following bound in expectation for the average sequence
\hat{x}_{k}
in terms of feasibility violation of the constraints:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k},\mathcal{X})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\beta(1-\beta)}\left(\frac{\|x_{0}-\bar{x}_{0}\|^{2}}{S_{k}}+C_{\beta,c,\mathcal{B}_{h}}B_{f}^{2}\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}}{S_{k}}\right).

Hence, the statements of the theorem are proved.
∎

Now, Theorem
9
yields (sublinear) convergence rates for SMBA iterates under convex objective if the non-increasing stepsize
\alpha_{k}
satisfies e.g., the conditions:
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}<\infty\text{ or }\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}^{2}\sim\mathcal{O}(\ln{(k+1)})
for all
k\geq 1
.
Let us discuss a few possible choices for such stepsize
\alpha_{k}
:

1.

Consider
\alpha_{k}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\sqrt{k+2}\ln(k+2)}}\;\;\forall k\geq 1
, with
\alpha_{0}>0
. Note that this choice yields:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k+1}\alpha_{t}\geq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\alpha_{0}(k+1)}{\sqrt{k+3}\ln(k+3)}}\;\;\text{and }\;\;\sum_{t=1}^{k+1}\alpha_{t}^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ln(3)}}.

Thus, from Theorem
9
, we obtain the following sublinear convergence rates:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(f(\hat{x}_{k})-f^{*})]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln(k+3)}{\sqrt{k+1}}\right),\;\;\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k},\mathcal{X})]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln(k+3)}{\sqrt{k+1}}\right).

2.

Other choice is
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}\;\;\forall k\geq 1
with
\alpha_{0}>0
. This choice gives us:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k+1}\alpha_{t}\geq\alpha_{0}\sqrt{k+1}\;\;\text{and }\;\;\sum_{t=1}^{k+1}\alpha_{t}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{0}^{2}\ln(k+1)).

Thus, we have the following sublinear convergence rates from Theorem
9
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(f(\hat{x}_{k})-f^{*}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}+\frac{\ln(k+1)}{\sqrt{k+1}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k},\mathcal{X})]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}+\frac{\ln(k+1)}{\sqrt{k+1}}\right).

It is worth mentioning that although both previous choices for the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
give the same theoretical rates, in our practical implementations we observed that the first choice, i.e.,
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{L_{f}\sqrt{k+2}\ln(k+2)}
, performs better than the second choice
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}
.

4.2
Convergence rates for SMBA under strong convex objective

In this section, we consider that the objective function
f
satisfies the strong convexity condition given in (
4
) (with
\mu>0
) and obtain the convergence rates for SMBA. Note that due to the strong convexity assumption on
f
, we have a unique global minima for (
1
), denoted as
x^{*}
, i.e., in relation (
18
) we have
\bar{x}_{k+1}=\bar{v}_{k}=x^{*}
for all
k
. First, we provide a main recurrence and use it later to prove convergence rates for the SMBA algorithm in these settings.

Lemma 10
.

Let Assumptions
1
(with
\mu>0
),
2
and
3
hold and the bounds from (
16
) be valid. Also
h(\cdot,\xi)
for all
\xi\in\Omega
be convex functions. Choose
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{k}=\frac{2}{\mu(k+1)}>0
for all
k\geq 0
. Then, we have the following recurrence true for the iterates generated by SMBA algorithm:

\displaystyle(k+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq k^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{4}{\mu^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}

(24)

\displaystyle\;-2(k+1)\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})-\bar{x}_{k}\|^{2}]-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)(k+1)^{2}}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})],

where
\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}=\left({\frac{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\beta(1-\beta)}\bar{B}_{f}^{2}+\left(1+\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\right)B_{f}^{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

From the basic properties of the projection and recalling that
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
and
u_{k}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})
, we have:

\displaystyle\|v_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}\;{\overset{\eqref{eq:alg2step1SMBA}}{=}\;\|\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(u_{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}}(x^{*})\|^{2}\leq\|u_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k}-x^{*}\rangle+\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{as:strg_conv_fsmooth}}{\leq}(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f({x_{k}})-f(x^{*})\right)+\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))+\langle\nabla f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})),{x_{k}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})\rangle-f(x^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))-f(x^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{{1}}{\eta}\|\nabla f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))\|^{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}+\eta\|x_{k}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_xksmooth}}{\leq}(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))-f(x^{*})\right)+\eta\|x_{k}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\left(\frac{{1}}{\eta}\bar{B}_{f}^{2}+B_{f}^{2}\right)\alpha_{k}^{2},

where third inequality uses the convexity of
f
and the fourth inequality follows from the identity
2\langle a,b\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\|a\|^{2}+\eta\|b\|^{2}
, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\eta>0
. Now, choosing
\eta=\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}
and using it in (
18
), we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{k}}[\|x_{k+1}-\bar{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}]\leq(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\alpha_{k}\left(f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))-f(x^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})+\left({\frac{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\beta(1-\beta)}\bar{B}_{f}^{2}+\left(1+\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\right)B_{f}^{2}}\right)\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Taking full expectation and denoting
\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}=\left({\frac{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}{\beta(1-\beta)}\bar{B}_{f}^{2}+\left(1+\frac{2\beta(1-\beta)}{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\right)B_{f}^{2}}\right)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]-2\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[(f(\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k}))-f(x^{*}))]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})]+\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}\alpha^{2}_{k}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:QGsmooth}}{\leq}(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]-\mu\alpha_{k}\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})-x^{*}\|^{2}]

(25)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})]+\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}\alpha^{2}_{k}.

From the choice of
\alpha_{k}
, i.e.,
\alpha_{k}=\frac{2}{\mu(k+1)}
, (
4.2
) yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{2}{k+1}\right)\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]-\frac{2}{(k+1)}\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})]+\frac{4}{\mu^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}.

After multiplying the whole inequality by
(k+1)^{2}
, and using
k^{2}-1\leq k^{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle(k+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq k^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{4}{\mu^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad-2(k+1)\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{k})-x^{*}\|^{2}]-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)(k+1)^{2}}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X})].

Hence, we get the desired result (
24
).
∎

Before deriving the convergence rates, let us define the sum:

\displaystyle{\bar{S}_{k}=\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\sim\mathcal{O}(k^{3})\quad\forall k\geq 0,}

and the corresponding average sequences:

\displaystyle{\hat{x}_{k}=\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}x_{t}}{\bar{S}_{k}},\;\;\hat{w}_{k}=\frac{1}{\bar{S}_{k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\in\mathcal{X}.}

The following theorem provides the convergence rates for the SMBA algorithm when the objective is strongly convex.

Theorem 11
.

Let Assumptions
1
(with
\mu>0
),
2
and
3
hold and the bounds from (
16
) be valid. Further, choose stepsizes
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{k}=\frac{2}{\mu(k+1)}>0
, for all
k\geq 0
. Then, we have the following (sublinear) convergence rates for the average sequence
\hat{x}_{k}
in terms of optimality and feasibility violation for any
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\mu^{2}(k+1)}+\frac{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\beta(1-\beta)\mu^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k},\mathcal{X})]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\beta(1-\beta)\mu^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\right).

Proof.

For any
k\geq 0
, we have the relation (
24
) from Lemma
10
. Summing it from
0
to
k-1
, we obtain:

\displaystyle k^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq-2\sum_{t={0}}^{k-1}(t+1)\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\sum_{t={0}}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}]\!+\!\frac{4}{\mu^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}{k}.

Now, using the fact that
\frac{(t+1)^{2}}{(k+1)}\leq(t+1)
for all
t=0:k
, we have:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq-2\frac{1}{(k+1)}\sum_{t={0}}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})-x^{*}\|^{2}]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\quad-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\sum_{t={0}}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}]+\frac{4}{\mu^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=-2\frac{\bar{S}_{k}}{\bar{S}_{k}(k+1)}\sum_{t={0}}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})-x^{*}\|^{2}]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\quad-\frac{\bar{S}_{k}\beta(1-\beta)}{2\bar{S}_{k}c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\sum_{t={0}}^{k-1}(t+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t})\|^{2}]+\frac{4}{\mu^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}{k}.}

Now, using non-negativity of
(k+1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}
, the linearity of the expectation operator, convexity of
\|\cdot\|^{2}
and the expressions of
\hat{w}_{k}
and
\hat{x}_{k}
,
we further get:

\displaystyle\frac{2\bar{S}_{k}}{(k+1)}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]+\frac{\beta(1-\beta)\bar{S}_{k}}{2c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{4}{\mu^{2}}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}{k}.

After some simple calculations and keeping only the dominant terms, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\mu^{2}(k+1)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\beta(1-\beta)\mu^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\right).

Since
\hat{w}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, we get the following convergence rate for the average sequence
\hat{x}_{k}
in terms of feasibility violation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm dist}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k},\mathcal{X})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\beta(1-\beta)\mu^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\right).

Moreover, we get the convergence rate for average sequence
\hat{x}_{k}
in terms of optimality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{x}_{k}-\hat{w}_{k}\|^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\mu^{2}(k+1)}+\frac{c\mathcal{B}_{h}^{2}\hat{C}_{\beta,B_{f},\mathcal{B}_{h}}}{\beta(1-\beta)\mu^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\right).

Hence, we obtain the claimed results.
∎

The convergence rates from Theorem
9
(Theorem
11
) are new
and valid for the convex settings, i.e., when the objective function and the functional constraints are both convex in problem (
1
). In these settings, we obtain convergence rates
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/2})
or
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
in terms of optimality and feasibility for an average sequence, for convex or strongly convex objective, respectively. Also, it is important to note that, according to our convergence analysis, we need to choose the stepsize
\beta\in(0,1)
, although in numerical simulations (see Section 5 below) we observed that algorithm SMBA works also for
\beta\in[1,2)
. It is an open question how to modify our current convergence analysis to cover a larger interval choice for
\beta
.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first providing a simple stochastic primal gradient algorithm for solving convex smooth problems of the form (
1
) that has mathematical guarantees of convergence of order
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{k})
/
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
,
thus matching the convergence rates obtained in some other papers from the literature on stochastic (primal-dual) (sub)gradient methods, see e.g.,
NecSin:22
;
NecNed:21
;
Ned:11
;
WanBer:16
.

5.2
Solving multiple kernel learning in support vector machine

In this section, we test SMBA on Support Vector Machine (SVM) with multiple kernel learning using real data, which can be formulated as a convex QCQP. Let us briefly describe the problem (our exposition follows
CheLi:21
). Given a set of
N
data points
\mathcal{S}=\{(a_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
, where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}
is the
n_{d}
-dimension input vector and
y_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
is its class label, SVM searches for a hyperplane that can best separate the points from the two classes. When the data points cannot be separated in the original space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}
, we can search in a feature space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{f}}
, by mapping the input data space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}
to the feature space through a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{f}}
. Using the function
\varphi
, we can define a kernel function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\kappa(a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}):=\langle\varphi(a_{j}),\varphi(a_{j^{\prime}})\rangle
for any
a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product. Popular choices of kernel functions in the SVM literature include the linear kernel function
\kappa_{\text{LIN}}
, the polynomial kernel function
\kappa_{\text{POL}}
, and the Gaussian kernel function
\kappa_{\text{GAU}}
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{LIN}}(a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}})=a_{j}^{T}a_{j^{\prime}},\quad\forall a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{POL}}(a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}})=(1+a_{j}^{T}a_{j^{\prime}})^{r},\quad r>0,\;\forall a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}

\displaystyle\kappa_{\text{GAU}}(a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}})=\exp^{-\frac{\|a_{j}-a_{j^{\prime}}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},\quad\sigma>0,\;\forall a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}.

We separate the given set
\mathcal{S}
into a training set,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}=\{(a_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{tr}}}
and a testing set,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{te}}=\{(a_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{te}}}
, such that
N_{\text{tr}}+N_{\text{te}}=N
. Choosing a set of kernel functions
(\kappa_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, the SVM classifier is learned by solving the following convex QCQP problem on the training set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\text{tr}}},\;\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{tr}}}y_{j}\alpha_{j}=0,\;d\in\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2C}\|\alpha\|^{2}-e^{T}\alpha+Rd

(26)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2R_{i}}\alpha^{T}(G_{i}(K_{i,\text{tr}}))\alpha-d\leq 0\quad\forall i=1:m,

where the parameter
C>0
is taken from the soft margin criteria, the vector
e
denotes the
N_{\text{tr}}
-dimensional vector of all ones. Given a labeled training data set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}=\{(\mathbf{a}_{j},l_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{tr}}}
and an unlabeled test data set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{te}}=\{\mathbf{a}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{\text{te}}}
, a matrix
K_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{\text{tr}}+N_{\text{te}})\times(N_{\text{tr}}+N_{\text{te}})}
can be defined on the entire data set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\text{te}}
as follows:

K_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}K_{i,\text{tr}}&K_{i,(\text{tr,te})}\\
K_{i,(\text{tr,te})}^{\top}&K_{i,\text{te}}\end{pmatrix}.

The submatrix
K_{i,\text{tr}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{tr}}\times N_{\text{tr}}}
is a positive semidefinite matrix, whose
jj^{\prime}
th element is defined by the kernel function:
[K_{i,\text{tr}}]_{jj^{\prime}}:=\kappa_{i}(a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}})
for any
a_{j},a_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
. The submatrices
K_{i,(\text{tr,te})}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{tr}}\times N_{\text{te}}}
and
K_{i,\text{te}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{te}}\times N_{\text{te}}}
are defined in the same way but with different input vectors.
The matrix
G_{i}(K_{i,\text{tr}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{tr}}\times N_{\text{tr}}}
in the quadratic constraints of (
26
) is a positive semidefinite matrix with its
jj^{\prime}
th element being
[G_{i}(K_{i,\text{tr}})]_{jj^{\prime}}=y_{j}y_{j^{\prime}}[K_{i,\text{tr}}]_{jj^{\prime}}

R
and

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have considered an optimization problem with a smooth objective being either convex or strongly convex and with a large number of smooth convex functional constraints. We have designed a new primal stochastic gradient type algorithm, called Stochastic Moving Ball Approximation (SMBA), to solve such optimization problems where at each iteration SMBA performs a gradient step for the objective function to generate an intermittent point, and then takes a projection step onto one ball approximation of a randomly chosen constraint. The computational simplicity of the SMBA subproblem makes it suitable for problems with many functional constraints. We have provided a convergence analysis for our algorithm, deriving convergence rates of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1/2})
, when the objective function is convex, and
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
, when the objective function is strongly convex. Finally, we apply SMBA algorithm to solve QCQPs based on synthetic and real data, demonstrating its viability and performance when compared to MBA and two commercial softwares Gurobi and FICO.

Equivalent form of the feasibility step in SMBA algorithm.
In this appendix we prove that if
R_{v_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
and
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
, then the step (
9
) in SMBA algorithm can be written equivalently as:

\displaystyle v_{k}-\frac{\beta}{L_{\xi_{k}}}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k})=(1-\beta)v_{k}+\beta\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}(v_{k}).

Indeed, if
R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
, we can compute the projection of
v_{k}
onto the nonempty ball:

\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}=\left\{y:\;q_{h(\cdot,\xi_{k})}(y;{v}_{k})\leq 0\right\}=\left\{y:\;\|y-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|^{2}\leq R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\right\},

which has the explicit expression:

\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}(v_{k})=c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}+\frac{\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\max\left(\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|,\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}\right)}(v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}),

or, after adding and subtracting
v_{k}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}(v_{k})=v_{k}-\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\max\left(\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|,\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}\right)}\right)(v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}).

Note that when
h(v_{k},\xi_{k})>0
and
R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}>0
we have the following (see (
3
)):

\max\left(\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|,\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}\right)=\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|.

Thus, in this case, we have:

\displaystyle(1-\beta)v_{k}+\beta\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}(v_{k})

\displaystyle=v_{k}-\beta\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)(v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}})

\displaystyle=v_{k}-\frac{\beta}{L_{\xi_{k}}}\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{R_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}}}{\|v_{k}-c_{{v}_{k},\xi_{k}}\|}\right)\nabla h(v_{k},\xi_{k}).
