Efficient Risk Estimation for the Credit Valuation Adjustment
Michael B. Giles, Abdul-Lateef Haji-Ali, Jonathan Spence

Abstract
AMS Subject Classication: 65C05, 62P05 Keywords: Multilevel Monte Carlo, Nested simulation, Risk estimation The valuation of over-the-counter derivatives is subject to a series of valuation adjustments known as xVA, which pose additional risks for financial institutions. Associated risk measures, such as the value-at-risk of an underlying valuation adjustment, play an important role in managing these risks. Monte Carlo methods are often regarded as inefficient for computing such measures. As an example, we consider the value-at-risk of the Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA-VaR), which can be expressed using a triple nested expectation. Traditional Monte Carlo methods are often inefficient at handling several nested expectations. Utilising recent developments in multilevel nested simulation for probabilities, we construct a hierarchical estimator of the CVA-VaR which reduces the computational complexity by several orders of magnitude compared to standard Monte Carlo.

2.2
Nested Monte Carlo Estimate

To motivate our work and as a baseline for comparison, we begin by considering an estimator for the quantity

For a fixed sample of

Robust Error Estimation

Robust evaluation of the MLMC estimators (

3.1
Model for the Default Times

Following the approach in
[
8
, Section 3.3]
and
[
33
, Chapter 12]
, we assume an intensity model for the default times under the risk-neutral measure. Specifically, for a positive-valued process
h=(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, adapted to
{\mathcal{G}}
, representing a hazard rate, assume that under the risk-neutral measure

\mathsf{Q}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\tau\leq t\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=1-\exp\left(-\int_{s=0}^{t}h_{s}\text{d}s\right)

for all
t\geq 0
. Then, the instantaneous probability of default at time
t
is
h_{t}\text{d}t
. A common model for the hazard rates is

h_{t}=\lgd^{-1}c_{t},

which depends on the loss given default and the counterparties credit spread,
c_{t}
, which is a positive-valued,
{\mathcal{G}_{{t}}}
-measurable, random variable representing the difference in return between a counterparty bond and the risk-neutral bond
B_{t}

[
33
, Section 12.2.3]
. A large credit spreads implies the counterparty bond has high associated risk, which is a sign of poor credit worthiness and large default probabilities. Following
[
43
]
, we assume that the credit spread is constant under the risk-neutral measure
\mathsf{Q}
. Then, for any fixed
v\geq 0
, we have:

\mathsf{Q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,v\leq\tau\leq t+v\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\tau\geq v,c_{v}\nonscript\,}}\right]=1-\exp\left(-\frac{c_{v}t}{\lgd}\right).

In particular, conditioned on
\tau\geq v
, the random variable
(\tau-v)
follows an
\textrm{Exponential}(c_{v}/\lgd)
distribution.

As discussed above, we assume that
\mathsf{P}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\tau<\mathnormal{H}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=0
. Following
[
43
]
, to model random fluctuations in the creditworthiness of the counterparty over the short risk horizon, we assume that the credit spread follows a geometric Brownian motion under
\mathsf{P}
. In particular, assume

\text{d}c_{t}=\sigma^{(\textnormal{cs})}c_{t}\text{d}W_{t}^{(\textnormal{cs},\mathsf{P})},

(24)

where
c_{0}>0
can be inferred from market data,
\sigma^{(\textnormal{cs})}
is a constant representing the volatility of the credit spread and
\{W_{t}^{(\textnormal{cs},\mathsf{P})}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a
\mathsf{P}
-Brownian motion. Large values of the product
\sigma^{(\textnormal{cs})}c_{0}
imply that there is high initial volatility in the credit spread. This can lead to significant worsening of the credit worthiness of the counterparty by the risk horizon
\mathnormal{H}
, causing large losses due to fluctuations in the CVA. In
[
43
, Table A]
, the product
\sigma^{(\textnormal{cs})}c_{0}
is directly related to the credit rating of the counterparty to compute the standardised CVA formula.

To summarise, the above model for default times relies on two assumptions: Under the physical measure, the physical probability of counterparty default before
\mathnormal{H}
is negligible, but the credit spread fluctuates according to a geometric Brownian motion. As a result, risks posed by changing credit worthiness of the counterparty are captured by the dynamics (
24
) of
c_{t}
under
\mathsf{P}
. On the other hand, under the risk-neutral measure, conditioned on the credit spread
c_{v}
at time
v\geq 0
and the event
\tau\geq v
, the credit spread is fixed and the random variable
(\tau-v)
follows an
\textrm{Exponential}(c_{v}/\lgd)
distribution. Important to what follows, this model allows us to sample random default times
\tau
under the risk-neutral measure, conditioned on a given risk scenario, exactly. More general risk-neutral models for the default time are discussed in
[
33
,
8
]
, which do not rely on constant credit spreads under
\mathsf{Q}
. However, such models can lead to difficulties in sampling the default times which are beyond the scope of algorithms presented in this paper, and are left to future work. Nevertheless, as remarked in
[
43
]
, the assumption of constant credit spreads is practical since the information used to model credit spreads is often sparsely distributed in time.

3.3
Collateralization

A more realistic model for losses arising due to CVA should incorporate credit risk mitigation factors, with the aim of lowering the risk faced by institutions due to counterparty credit risk. In recent years, the initial and variational margins have become essential in reducing the exposure of the bank to its counterparties
[
33
,
32
]
. Assuming credit risk arises only through the counterparty, the variational margin requires the counterparty to post funds to a segregated account (free from default-risk) at a sequence of discrete time-points to cover the cost of their exposure to the bank. The initial margin is then based on a (conditional) Value-at-Risk formula on the exposure evaluated at each posting time of the variational margin to account for fluctuations which arise between posting times. Computing variational adjustments therefore requires evaluating additional nested expectations defining the exposure faced to the counterparty at the posting time immediately prior to default. On the other hand, initial margin computation requires a nested value-at-risk calculation, given the market state at the posting time prior to counterparty default. Defined in full generality, inclusion of nested risk measures will require, for example, nested stochastic approximation schemes to approximate the initial margin. Such methods fall beyond the scope of this paper and are left as an interesting avenue for future work. In the particular case where the assets
S
evolve according to a geometric Brownian motion, the initial margin can be related to the solution of a McKean backward stochastic differential equation
[
1
,
35
]
. In
[
7
]
, the authors use this approach to co
d
mpute the initial margin by approximating a pair of nested expectations using an antithetic MLMC estimator as discussed in
Section

2.2
. Defined in this manner, the initial margin computation will add an extra layer of nested expectations when computing the CVA. The additional layer of nested expectations can be handled by extending the estimator in
Section

2.2
to handle an additional nested expectation, which is possible using MLMC techniques for repeatedly nested expectations as discussed in
[
46
,
49
,
47
]
.

\mathsf{E}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\mathsf{E}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}
, but with
\mathsf{Var}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\mathcal{O}(\mathnormal{H})
, constituting a significant reduction in variance. In
Section

4.2
, importance sampling techniques are used to sample default times from the conditional distribution of the counterparty defaulting before
T
, resolving instability issues from rare event sampling.

4.1
Variance Reduction

We begin by using a change of measure to remove the need to sample
{\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}}
under a different rate from
{\tau_{c_{0}}}
. For the exponentially distributed default times in
Section

3.1
and any
F:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,F({\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}})|c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\infty}F(t)\frac{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}{\lgd}e^{-c_{\mathnormal{H}}(t-\mathnormal{H})/\lgd}\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}{c_{0}}e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{t\geq\mathnormal{H}}F(t)\frac{c_{0}}{\lgd}e^{-(c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0})t/\lgd}e^{-c_{0}t/\lgd}\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}\geq\mathnormal{H}}g_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}F({\tau_{c_{0}}})\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,}}\right],

where we define
g_{t}(x)\coloneqq c_{0}^{-1}xe^{-(x-c_{0})t/\lgd}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{{\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}}}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}},{\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}}\right)\right)\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}\geq\mathnormal{H}}g_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}},{\tau_{c_{0}}}\right)\right)\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,}}\right],

where the change of measure moves the dependency on
c_{\mathnormal{H}}
to the factor
g_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}
, and simulates default time using
{\tau_{c_{0}}}
opposed to
{\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}}
, eliminating the need to sample
{\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}}
.
Consequently, we henceforth express
{\tau_{c_{0}}}\equiv\tau
.
The above discussion then motivates replacing
\Phi
as in (
27
) with

\displaystyle\Phi^{\textrm{I}}\coloneqq{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau\geq\mathnormal{H}}\bigg(

\displaystyle g_{\tau}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}\underbrace{\left(f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}},\tau\right)\right)-f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\right)}_{\text{Market risk}}

(28)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left(g_{\tau}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}-1\right)}_{\text{Credit risk}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\bigg),

whenever
\tau\geq\mathnormal{H}
. By construction
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{I}}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{{\tau_{c_{0}}}\geq\mathnormal{H}}\Phi\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}
. The component of
\Phi
reflecting default before
\mathnormal{H}
, under
\mathsf{Q}
, is

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau<\mathnormal{H}}\Phi=-{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau<\mathnormal{H}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right).

Since this is independent of the risk factors at the horizon
\mathnormal{H}
, we can cancel the bias induced by considering only
\tau\geq\mathnormal{H}
in
\Phi^{\textrm{I}}
by setting

\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{I}}\coloneqq\lambda_{\varphi}+\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau<\mathnormal{H}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\nonscript\,}}\right],

which be pre-computed offline at insignificant cost compared with the cost of computing
\varphi
.
By the change of measure from
{\tau_{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}}
to
\tau
above, it follows that
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi-\lambda_{\varphi}|S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{I}}-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{I}}|S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}
. We consider variance reduction for the credit and market risk factors in turn.

4.1.1
Credit Risk

In
[
43
]
, it is shown that the standardised CVA capital charge can be derived by linearising the term
g_{\tau}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}}
as a function of
c_{\mathnormal{H}}
. We use this approach here to construct a control variate that reduces the variance of
\Phi
. In particular, since
g_{\tau}(c_{0})=1
, we have

\displaystyle g_{\tau}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}-1

\displaystyle=g_{\tau}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\mathnormal{H})\right)-g_{\tau}(c_{0})

(29)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0})\left(\frac{1}{c_{0}}-\frac{\tau}{\lgd}\right)}_{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\mathnormal{H}})}+\mathcal{O}(\mathnormal{H}),

where the difference
c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0}
is of order
\sqrt{\mathnormal{H}}
since the process
(c_{t})_{t\geq 0}
evolves under
\mathsf{P}
according to the dynamics (
24
) with an underlying
\mathsf{P}
-Brownian motion.
To remove this leading order term, we set

\Phi^{\textrm{II}}\coloneqq\Phi^{\textrm{I}}-{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau\geq\mathnormal{H}}(c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0})\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}}),

where we introduce the control variate

\Psi_{\tau}(s)\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{c_{0}}-\frac{\tau}{\lgd}\right)f\left(U_{1}(s,\tau)\right).

To ensure the control variate introduces no bias, we set

\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{II}}\coloneqq\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{I}}-(c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0})\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}})\nonscript\,\mathclose{]},

so that again
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{II}}-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{II}}|S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi-\lambda_{\varphi}|S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}
. Since
\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}})
does not depend on the risk factors,

\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}})\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}

can be computed offline at negligible cost. Consequently,
\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{II}}
may be computed with bounded cost for each risk scenario
c_{\mathnormal{H}}
.

4.1.2
Market Risk

The market risk component of
\Phi
is primarily composed of the term

f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}},\tau\right)\right)-f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)=\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)-\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}}\right),

where we introduce the functions
\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, dropping the dependency of
\bar{f}
on
\tau
, to simplify notation in what follows. As with the credit risk, we focus on the case where
\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T
.
Following the techniques in
[
26
, Section 3.2]
, this term can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)-\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)-\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)\right)+\left(\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)-\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}-S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)^{\intercal}\nabla\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)+\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\nabla_{S_{0}}\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\mathnormal{H}),

using a Taylor expansion in the final step. Both of the terms
S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}-S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}
and
S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}-S_{0}
are of order
\sqrt{\mathnormal{H}}
since they involve simulating the assets under different Brownian motions along the interval
[0,\mathnormal{H}]
. The former term can eliminated by utilising an antithetic pair of risk-neutral paths
S^{{0,S_{0},\pm}}
, each of which follows the
\mathsf{Q}
-Brownian path
\{\pm W_{t}^{\mathsf{Q}}\}_{0\leq t\leq\mathnormal{H}}
up to the risk horizon. After the risk horizon, each of the processes
S^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}
and
S^{{0,S_{0},\pm}}
should follow the same Brownian path
\{W_{t}^{\mathsf{Q}}\}_{\mathnormal{H}\leq t\leq\tau}
up to the default time. It then follows for the antithetic variate
[
26
,
29
]

\displaystyle\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}}\right)+\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{S_{0}}\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}}\right)+\nabla_{S_{0}}\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\mathnormal{H}).

By subtracting the term

\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{S_{0}}\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}}\right)+\nabla_{S_{0}}\bar{f}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}}\right)\right),

one obtains samples which are of
\mathcal{O}(\mathnormal{H})
, constituting a significant improvement to the variance. The components
\nabla_{S_{0}}\cdot\bar{f}
are referred to in financial settings as the delta control variate
[
29
]
.

Following the above approach and using the decomposition in (
28
) motivates the term

\displaystyle\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\coloneqq{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau\geq\mathnormal{H}}

\displaystyle\bigg(g_{\tau}(c_{\mathnormal{H}})e^{c_{\mathnormal{H}}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}},\tau\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}},\tau\right)\right)+f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}},\tau\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0})\left(\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}})+\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}})\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}},\tau\right)\right)+\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}},\tau\right)\right)\right)\bigg),

As before, to compensate for the bias induced by the delta control variate, we replace
\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{II}}
with

\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}\coloneqq\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{II}}-\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\>\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\nonscript\,}}\right]

so that
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Phi\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}-\lambda_{\varphi}
. Since all of the expectations appearing in the definition of
\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}
are independent of
S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}
and
c_{\mathnormal{H}}
, they may be pre-computed offline at insignificant cost.

4.2
Importance Sampling

Observe that the CVA loss,
\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
, is non-zero only when
\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T
. For credible counterparties this is a rare event, making it costly to simulate a single instance of default. To address this issue note that, defining
\tilde{p}\coloneqq\mathsf{P}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}
, we have

\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,}}\right]-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\tilde{p}\bigg|S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}},\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,}}\right]-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}.

(30)

Under the model for default times in
Section

3.1
,
\tau
is an
\textrm{Exponential}(c_{0}/\lgd)
random variable under the risk-neutral measure
\mathsf{Q}
. Hence,
for
\mathnormal{H}\leq t\leq T
, we have

\mathsf{Q}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\tau\leq t|\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\frac{\mathsf{Q}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq t\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}}{\mathsf{Q}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}}=\frac{e^{-c_{0}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}-e^{-c_{0}t/\lgd}}{e^{-c_{0}\mathnormal{H}/\lgd}-e^{-c_{0}T/\lgd}}.

By inverting this equation for
t
, we can sample directly from the conditional distribution using inverse transform sampling. This reduces the kurtosis of samples from the CVA loss by ensuring default always occurs.

The above approach relies explicitly on the model for default times described in
Section

3.1
. More complex models of the default time will require more advanced importance sampling techniques to achieve a similar result.

4.3
Hierarchical MLMC Estimates of the Loss

To summarize, combining the techniques above, we have expressed the problem (
25
) in the form

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}}\nonscript\,}}\right]>\lambda_{\varphi}\nonscript\,}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\tilde{p}-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}},\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,}}\right]>0\nonscript\,}}\right],

where
\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
depends only on the default times
\tau={\tau_{c_{0}}}
and has significantly reduced variance compared with
\Phi
as in (
27
). Furthermore, by sampling from the conditional distribution
\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T
we are able to sample directly from the conditional distribution of
\tau\in[\mathnormal{H},T]
. To counteract the bias induced replacing
\Phi
with
\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
, we set

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}=\lambda_{\varphi}

\displaystyle+\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau<\mathnormal{H}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\nonscript\,}}\right]

\displaystyle-(c_{\mathnormal{H}}-c_{0})\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Psi_{\tau}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}})\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}

\displaystyle-\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\>\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\nonscript\,}}\right],

where each expectation can be pre-computed offline at insignificant cost using, for example, (multilevel) Monte Carlo sampling, since they do not depend on the generated risk variables. Since the variable
\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
is a functions of the nested expectations
U_{1}{(s,\tau)}
for
s\in\{S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},\pm}},S_{\tau}^{{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}}\}
, the techniques of
Section

2.3
can be used to construct antithetic correction terms
\Delta_{\ell}^{(\textnormal{ant})}\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
satisfying

\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\tilde{p}-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}},\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,}}\right]=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\Delta_{\ell}^{(\textnormal{ant})}\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}},\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,}}\right].

The antithetic differences
\Delta_{\ell}^{(\textnormal{ant})}\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
combine nested (unbiased) MLMC estimation of the expectations
U_{1}{(s,\tau)}
with (Euler-Maruyama or Milstein) approximations of the underlying asset processes. Note that for the delta control variates used in
\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
, we have

\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},\pm}},\tau\right)\right)={\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\tau\leq T}\lgd\left(\nabla_{S_{0}}\max\left\{U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},\pm}},\tau\right),0\right\}\right),

which is a discontinuous function of the nested expectation
U_{1}(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},\pm}},\tau)
. Consequently, the delta control variates do not satisfy the conditions of
2.2
, and the analysis in
Section

2.3
cannot be applied directly. One method of balancing the variance reduction properties of the delta control variate with the faster convergence of antithetic MLMC for Lipschitz continuous observables is to consider the delta control variates at a single level
[
26
]
. Defining

\tilde{\Phi}^{\textrm{III}}=\Phi^{\textrm{III}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathnormal{H},S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}}-S_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\left(\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},+}},\tau\right)\right)+\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0},-}},\tau\right)\right)\right),

to be equivalent to
\Phi^{\textrm{III}}
but without the delta control variates, this motivates considering

{\tilde{\Delta}}_{\ell}^{(\textnormal{ant})}\Phi^{\textrm{III}}=\begin{cases}\Delta_{0}\Phi^{\textrm{III}}&\ell=0\\
\Delta_{\ell}\tilde{\Phi}^{\textrm{III}}&\ell>0.\end{cases}

To remove the bias induced from including the delta control variates only at level zero, one should ensure the approximation of
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\nabla_{S_{0}}f\left(U_{1}\left(S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}},\tau\right)\right)\nonscript\,}}\right]
within
\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}
only contains a level zero of the approximation of
S_{\tau}^{{0,S_{0}}}
and
U_{1}(\cdot)
. Then

\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\tilde{p}-\lambda_{\varphi}^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}},\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,}}\right]=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\tilde{\Delta}}_{\ell}^{(\textnormal{ant})}\Phi^{\textrm{III}}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{\mathnormal{H}},\mathnormal{H}\leq\tau\leq T\nonscript\,}}\right],

can be used as in
Section

2
to construct an (adaptive) MLMC estimate of
\varphi
. Since
Section

2
assumed that the variable
Z
in (
3
) can be sampled directly, we assume that
S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}
can be sampled exactly, which is a reasonable assumption provided
\mathnormal{H}\ll T
. If
\mathnormal{H}
represents a significant time interval for which approximation of
S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}
is costly, one can incorporate multilevel approximations of
Z\coloneqq S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}}
within the methods of
Section

2
. In this case,
2.1
and
2.5
should be extended naturally to include approximations
Z_{\ell}\approx Z
.

4.4
Extension to Multiple Counterparties

To simplify the preceding discussion, we have considered only the case where the contract occurs between the bank and a single counterparty.
Typically, the CVA capital charge considers large portfolios containing many options with
{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}\geq 1
counterparties. In this case, the adjustment made to the valuation of the banks portfolio is

\mathrm{CVA}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}\mathrm{CVA}_{t}^{(n)},

where
\mathrm{CVA}_{t}^{(n)}
denotes the valuation adjustment accounting for the banks exposure to counterparty
n
. In this approach, the credit spread at time
t\geq 0
becomes
c_{t}=(c_{t}^{(n)})_{n=1}^{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}
and loss given default will differ for each counterparty. The CVA capital charge becomes the value
\lambda_{\varphi}
solving

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}}\left(B_{\mathnormal{H}}^{-1}\mathrm{CVA}_{\mathnormal{H}}^{(n)}-\mathrm{CVA}_{0}^{(n)}\right)>\lambda_{\varphi}\nonscript\,}}\right]

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{(n)}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{0}^{(n)},c_{\mathnormal{H}}^{(n)}\nonscript\,}}\right]>\lambda_{\varphi}\nonscript\,}}\right]

where
\Phi^{(n)}
is an extension of (
27
) for the counterparty
n
. While we focused on the case
{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}=1
until now, the discussion applies in the extended case where
{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}>1
. We conclude this section by considering some additional points worth considering when
{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}\gg 1
.

•

(
Randomized subsampling of counterparties.
) For a large number of counterparties (
{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}\gg 1
in (
31
)) it can be more efficient to write the sum

\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{(n)}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{0}^{(n)},c_{\mathnormal{H}}^{(n)}\nonscript\,}}\right]=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\nonscript\,\Phi^{(\kappa)}q_{\kappa}^{-1}\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}S_{\mathnormal{H}}^{\mathsf{P}},c_{0}^{(\kappa)},c_{\mathnormal{H}}^{(\kappa)}\nonscript\,}}\right],

where
\kappa
is drawn from a discrete probability distribution on
\{1,\dots,{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}\}
with mass function
q_{k}
. As a result, we need only consider a single counterparty with each sample of the inner expectation rather than evaluate each of
\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}
for each sample. This approach, known as randomised sub-sampling
[
26
]
, can reduce the cost of the MLMC estimator for large values of
{N_{\textnormal{cp}}}
.

•

(
Simulation of market at large number of intermediate points.
) When there are many counterparties with different default times and/or many options per counterparty with differing maturities, it may be necessary to sample the assets at many intermediate time-points. In this case, it may be beneficial to use a linear interpolant to sample the market over discrete trajectories of the asset prices, opposed to generating discrete samples of the market at each default time. By combining the interpolant with an antithetic Milstein scheme, as in
[
28
, Theorem 4.13]
, one may do this without affecting the convergence rates required within
2.1
.

6
Conclusion

Due to large costs induced by the approximation of market factors combined with several nested Monte Carlo averages, accurate estimation of credit risk measures is often considered infeasible using traditional Monte Carlo methods. By combining recent developments in MLMC for nested simulation with variance reduction techniques, this paper illustrates an efficient framework for accurate simulation of these risk measures at greatly reduced costs. In the context of the CVA-VaR problem, we are able to reduce the cost of achieving accuracy
\varepsilon
from
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-5})
for traditional nested Monte Carlo to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2-\delta})
, for any
\delta>0
, using MLMC with an adaptive number of inner samples of the loss. This results in computational savings of several orders of magnitude for realistic problems.

Future directions of this work include numerical studies for larger portfolios, containing a greater number of counterparties and the posting of collateral as discussed in
Sections

3.3
and
4.4
, and a combination of the methods in this work with multilevel stochastic approximation techniques
[
16
,
13
,
19
]
to compute the value-at-risk
\lambda_{\varphi}
associated with a fixed confidence level
\varphi
. Further analysis of the numerical estimator is required to handle non-linear nested market factors such as the initial margin and also to include more general models for default which cannot be simulated directly, and will require more advanced importance sampling methods. In more general models for default, an important concept to consider is that of wrong way risk
[
33
,
30
,
38
]
, where the probability of counterparty default is positively correlated to the risk-neutral valuation of the portfolio, leading to greater credit risk.

Appendix B
Bayesian Estimates of the Error

In this appendix, we describe an approach for obtaining more robust estimates of the error and variance estimate in MLMC (
14
). We describe a Bayesian approach, based on a similar method in
[
18
]
. Throughout this section, let
V_{\ell}=\mathsf{Var}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}
and
E_{\ell}=\lvert\mathsf{E}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}\rvert
, where
\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}
represent either the non-adaptive multilevel differences defined through (
13
) or the adaptive differences (
21
). For
\ell\geq 0
there exists
p_{\ell},q_{\ell}\in[0,1]
such that the multilevel correction terms
\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}
take the form

\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}=\begin{cases}1&\text{probability }p_{\ell}\\
-1&\text{probability }q_{\ell}\\
0&\text{probability }1-p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}.\end{cases}

In particular,

\displaystyle V_{\ell}\leq p_{\ell}+q_{\ell},\quad E_{\ell}=\lvert p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}\rvert.

When
\ell
is large,
Theorem

2.6
implies that both
p_{\ell},q_{\ell}\ll 1
. Heuristically, when the number of samples
M_{\ell}
of
\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}
is small, it is extremely unlikely we will observe a non-zero sample, creating unstable approximations to
V_{\ell}
and
E_{\ell}
. The idea is to impose a Bayesian prior on
p_{\ell}
and
q_{\ell}
which incorporates knowledge of the theoretical convergence rates of
V_{\ell}
and
E_{\ell}
such that when the number of samples
M_{\ell}
is low, we may rely more heavily on the theoretical rates than on the observations, and vice-versa as
M_{\ell}
becomes large.

Specifically, assume that we know a relationship of the form

V_{\ell}\approx a_{0}2^{-\beta\ell},

where the constant
\beta
can be obtained a-priori given
Theorem

2.6
or estimated from data and
a_{0}
may be reasonably estimated using information from coarse levels where samples of
\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}
are inexpensive. We impose a Dirichlet prior density on the quantities
p_{\ell},q_{\ell}

\pi_{0,\ell}(p,q)=(1-p-q)^{c_{\ell}-1}p^{A}q^{A},

for constants
c_{\ell},A>0
. For the robustness of the MLMC estimator, it is preferable to over-estimate opposed to under-estimate
V_{\ell}
. Therefore, we require the condition

\mathsf{E}_{\pi_{0,\ell}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,p_{\ell}+q_{\ell}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}\geq V_{\ell},

which is true provided

c_{\ell}\leq 2(A+1)\left(V_{\ell}^{-1}-1\right).

Hence, using
V_{\ell}\approx a_{0}2^{-\beta\ell}
, a reasonable conservative estimate is

c_{\ell}=2A(a_{0}^{-1}2^{\beta\ell}-1).

Given observations
\{\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},m}\}_{m=1}^{M_{\ell}}
, define

\nu_{\ell}^{\pm}\coloneqq\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\ell}}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}^{(m)}\>=\>\pm 1}.

Then, the maximum-a-posteriori estimate of
p_{\ell}+q_{\ell}
is

\hat{V}_{\ell}\coloneqq\frac{\nu_{\ell}^{+}+\nu_{\ell}^{-}+2A}{M_{\ell}+2Aa_{0}^{-1}2^{\beta\ell}-1}.

The parameter
A
can be chosen to control how confident we are that
V_{\ell}\leq\hat{V}_{\ell}
for a small number of samples
M_{\ell}
. Larger values of
A
produce more reliable, conservative bounds.

To estimate
E_{\ell}=\lvert p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}\rvert
we construct biased estimates for
p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}
and
q_{\ell}-p_{\ell}
separately. First, in the case where
p_{\ell}>q_{\ell}
, we wish to estimate of
p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}
. In doing so, we impose a
\text{Dirichlet}(d_{\ell},B+1,1)
prior on
p_{\ell},q_{\ell}
with density

\overline{\pi_{0,\ell}}(p,q)=(1-p-q)^{d_{\ell}-1}p^{B},

for constants
d_{\ell}
and
B
.
Given the observations
\{\Delta_{\textnormal{ind},\ell}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M_{\ell}}
the posterior distribution becomes a
\text{Dirichlet}(d_{\ell},\nu_{\ell}^{+}+B+1,\nu_{\ell}^{-}+1)
distribution with maximum a posteriori estimator given by

\frac{\nu_{\ell}^{+}-\nu_{\ell}^{-}+B}{M_{\ell}+B+d_{\ell}-1}.

Under the prior distribution we have

\mathsf{E}_{\overline{\pi_{0,\ell}}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}=\frac{B}{B+d_{\ell}+2},

(37)

thus enforcing the condition

\mathsf{E}_{\overline{\pi_{0,\ell}}}\mathopen{[}\nonscript\,p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}\nonscript\,\mathclose{]}\geq e_{0}2^{-\alpha\ell}\approx\lvert p_{\ell}-q_{\ell}\rvert

requires

d_{\ell}\leq B(e_{0}^{-1}2^{\alpha\ell}-1)-2.

(38)

In the case where
q_{\ell}>p_{\ell}
one can repeat this process by interchanging the roles of
p_{\ell}
and
q_{\ell}
in (
37
). Taking the maximum of the two resulting estimators, with equality in (
38
), yields

\hat{E}_{\ell}\coloneqq\frac{\lvert\nu_{\ell}^{+}-\nu_{\ell}^{-}\rvert+B}{M_{\ell}+Be_{0}^{-1}2^{\alpha\ell}-3}.

This method is used for the numerical experiments in
Section

5
, where we set the constants
A=B=1
therein.
