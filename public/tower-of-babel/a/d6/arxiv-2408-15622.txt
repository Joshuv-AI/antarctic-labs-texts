Counting in nilpotent injectors and Carter subgroups Thanks: The first author is partially supported by the Hellenic Foundation for Research and Innovation, Project HFRI-FM17-1733.
S. Aivazidis, M. Loukaki, J. Shareshian

Abstract
. We investigate number-theoretic properties of the collection of nilpotent injectors or nilpotent projectors containing certain subgroups of finite soluble (or 𝒩 {\mathcal{N}} -constrained) groups.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group and let
\pi
be a set of primes. Divisibility results about the number of nilpotent groups
from a characteristic conjugacy class that contain a given subgroup of a group
G
appear throughout the literature.
For example, Iranzo, Medina, and Pérez Monasor proved in
[
IPMM05
]
the following theorem,
which generalises work of Navarro in
[
Nav03
]
and Turull in
[
Tur04
]
:

Given any
\pi
-separable group
G
and any
\pi
-subgroup
X\leqslant G
,
write
\nu_{\pi}(G,X)
for the number of Hall
\pi
-subgroups of
G
containing
X
.
If
X\leqslant K\leqslant G
, then
\nu_{\pi}(K,X)
divides
\nu_{\pi}(G,1)
.

A primary aim of this paper is to provide results of a similar nature in which
\pi
-subgroups are replaced with nilpotent subgroups
and Hall
\pi
-subgroups are replaced with one of two conjugacy classes of subgroups:

•

Recall that a group
G
is said to be
\mathcal{N}
-constrained
provided that
\cent_{G}(\fitt(G))\leqslant\fitt(G)
.
It is a standard fact that all finite soluble groups are
\mathcal{N}
-constrained.
Moreover, a subgroup
I
is called a
nilpotent injector
of
G

provided that for each subnormal subgroup
S
of
G
,

S\cap I
is a maximal nilpotent subgroup of
S
. In any finite soluble group,
nilpotent injectors exist and comprise a single conjugacy class,
as was proved by Fischer, Gaschütz and Hartley in
[
FGH67
]
.
In fact, by work of Mann
[
Man71
]

all finite
\mathcal{N}
-constrained groups contain nilpotent injectors;
again, such injectors comprise a single conjugacy class.

•

The dual notion of a nilpotent injector is that of a
Carter subgroup
.
These are, by definition, nilpotent self-normalising subgroups of
G
and were
introduced by Carter in
[
Car61
]
.
They were later seen to be specific instances of something more general;
they are the so-called projectors for the class of nilpotent groups.

The two classes just mentioned arise from two separate theories:
Fitting classes and the associated injectors and saturated formations
and the associated projectors.
In the case of
\pi
-subgroups the two notions coincide since the
class of
\pi
-groups is a saturated Fitting formation,
but in general injectors and projectors need not be the same (when they exist) and, indeed, this is the case
with the class of nilpotent groups.

Let
G
be an
\mathcal{N}
-constrained group and
H\leqslant G
a fixed nilpotent subgroup of
G
. We write
\inj(G,H)
for the set of nilpotent injectors of
G
containing
H
and
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)=\left\lvert{\inj(G,H)}\right\rvert
for its cardinality.
Every nilpotent injector of
G
contains the Fitting subgroup
\fitt(G)
of
G
and thus, using the notation above, the total number of nilpotent injectors of
G
is
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,\fitt(G))
, which for simplicity we will write as
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G)
. So
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G)=\left\lvert{\inj(G)}\right\rvert
.

Our first main result is the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be an
{\mathcal{N}}
-constrained group. If
G
is nilpotent, set
m_{G}=1
. Otherwise, define

(1.1)

m_{G}\coloneqq\gcd\left\{p-1:p\in\mathbb{P},\,p\mid(G:I)\,\,\text{ with }\,\,I\in\inj(G)\right\}.

If
H\leqslant G
, then either
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)=0
or
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)\equiv 1\,\left(\textnormal{mod}\ m_{G}\right)
.

Recall that the Möbius function
\mu_{P}
on the set of ordered pairs from a finite poset
P
is defined recursively by

\mu_{P}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\mbox{if }x\not\leqslant y,\\
1&\mbox{if }x=y,\\
-\sum_{x\leqslant z<y}\mu_{P}(x,z)&\mbox{if }x<y.\end{array}\right.

Another goal of this paper is to show that divisibility properties also occur in the case of sums of Möbius numbers defined on specific posets of subgroups of
G
. In particular, we show the following.

Theorem B
.

Given a nontrivial
{\mathcal{N}}
-constrained group
G
with nilpotent injector
I
,
let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of subgroups of
G
that are contained in at least one conjugate of
I
.
Order
\mathcal{X}
by inclusion and set

\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(\fitt(G))\coloneqq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(\fitt(G),K).

Then
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(\fitt(G))
is divisible by
(I:\fitt(G))
.

There is a topological interpretation of
B
,
as there is whenever a Möbius function is involved.
Given any poset
P
,
the order complex
\Delta P
is the abstract simplicial complex
whose
k
-dimensional faces are the totally ordered subsets of size
k+1
from
P
.
Let
\hat{P}
be the poset obtained from
P

by adding a minimum element
\hat{0}
and a maximum element
\hat{1}
.
The reduced Euler characteristic
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta P)
satisfies

\tilde{\chi}(\Delta P)=\mu_{\hat{P}}(\hat{0},\hat{1}),

as was proved by Philip Hall and appears as
[
Sta12
, Thm. 3.8.5]
.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{>\fitt(G)}
be the poset of all proper subgroups of
G

strictly containing
\fitt(G)
and contained in at least one nilpotent injector for
G
.

B
says that
\tilde{\chi}(\Delta\mathcal{X}_{>\fitt(G)})
is divisible by
(I:\fitt(G))
. There are various results about divisors of reduced Euler characteristics of posets of subgroups in the literature, some of which appear in
[
Smi11
, Sections 5.3 and 5.4]
.

We write
{\mathfrak{N}}={\mathfrak{N}}(G)
for the poset of nilpotent subgroups of
G
. Then in view of
B
, one might ask whether
|I|
divides

\theta_{\mathfrak{N}}^{G}(1):=\sum_{N\in{\mathfrak{N}}(G)}\mu(1,N).

Certainly such divisibility occurs when
G
is nilpotent. We will see, however, that
|I|/\theta_{\mathfrak{N}}^{G}(1)
can be an integer of arbitrarily large absolute value. Indeed, we have the following result.

Theorem C
.

If
p
is a prime and
a
is a positive integer,
then there exist a soluble group
G

and a nilpotent injector
I\leqslant G

such that

|I|/\theta_{\mathfrak{N}}^{G}(1)=-p^{a}.

The theorem of Iranzo, Medina, and Pérez Monasor stated at the beginning (
[
IPMM05
, Theorem 0.1]
) holds unconditionally for
\pi
-separable groups, but an analogous result, saying that the number
n_{C}(G,X)
of Carter subgroups of a soluble group
G
that contain a given nilpotent subgroup
X\leqslant G
divides the number
\left\lvert{\cart(G)}\right\rvert
of Carter subgroups, does not hold in general (see
D
).
However, if
G
has a normal Hall
q^{\prime}
-subgroup (for some prime divisor
q
of
|G|
) then one can obtain a simple formula for
n_{C}(G,X)
, see
E
. It follows that the desired analogous result holds when
G
has a Sylow tower, see
14
.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we first present the necessary background regarding nilpotent injectors and then we give the proofs of
A
,
B
and
C
. In Section
3
we consider Carter subgroups and we prove
D
and
E
.

All groups in this paper are assumed to be finite and we close this introduction by outlining some convenient notation. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we will write
\varpi(n)

for the set of prime numbers that divide
n

and set
\varpi(G)\coloneqq\varpi(|G|)
when
G
is a group.
Unless we explicitly say otherwise,

X_{p}
will stand for a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
X
.
We will mainly use this notation when
X
is nilpotent
in which case
X_{p}
is unique. Finally, we denote by
\mathbb{P}
the set of all prime numbers.

2.
Nilpotent Injectors and Möbius Function

For a soluble group
G
,
the authors in
[
IPMM05
, Cor. 1.3]
prove
that for
X
,
K
subgroups of
G

containing the Fitting subgroup
\fitt(G)
, with
X\leqslant K
and
X
nilpotent,

n_{\mathfrak{I}}(K,X)
divides
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G)

and in particular, if
\fitt(G)\leqslant K\leqslant G
then

n_{\mathfrak{I}}(K)
divides
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G)
.
They remark that both the assumption of solubility of
G

and of the inclusion
\fitt(G)\leqslant K
are necessary for
the conclusion to remain valid.

At the cost of being less precise,

A
removes those two restrictions.
Before presenting the proof of
A
, some preliminary remarks are in order.

Firstly, the modulus we are working with enjoys two key properties:

(i)

For each divisor
d
of
(G:\fitt(G))

we have
d\equiv 1\,\left(\textnormal{mod}\ m_{G}\right)
.
In fact,
m_{G}
is the greatest positive integer with that property.

(ii)

If
X\geqslant\fitt(G)
, then
m_{G}\mid m_{X}
.

Secondly, nilpotent subgroups that contain the Fitting subgroup
behave quite similarly to Sylow subgroups.
In particular, Theorem 2 in
[
Man71
]
asserts the following:

Let
G
be
\mathcal{N}
-constrained.
Let
F=\fitt(G)
,
S
be a nilpotent injector of
G

and
H
be a subgroup containing
F
.

(a)

H
is
\mathcal{N}
-constrained.
Denote by
T
a nilpotent injector of
H
.

(b)

T=S_{1}\cap H
for some nilpotent injector
S_{1}
of
G
.

(c)

S\cap H
is contained in some nilpotent injector of
H
.

(d)

If
H\leqslant K
, then
T
is contained in a nilpotent injector of
K
.

(e)

If
S\leqslant H
, then
S
is a nilpotent injector of
H
.

The following remark is proved by Mann when he shows that injectors exist while proving
[
Man71
, Theorem 1]
.

Remark 1

(Mann)
.

Let
G
be
\mathcal{N}
-constrained,
F=\fitt(G)
and
F_{p^{\prime}}=\oh^{p}(\fitt(G))
. If
{\mathbf{C}(p)}\coloneqq\cent_{G}(F_{p^{\prime}})
and
S_{q}\in\syl_{q}(\mathbf{C}(q))
for
q,p
distinct primes in
\varpi(G)
, then
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
is a normal subgroup of
G
that also normalises
S_{q}
.
Furthermore,
S_{p}
is the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup
of a nilpotent injector of
G
and
S_{p}
centralises
S_{q}
for all
p,q
as above. Finally, for any nilpotent subgroup
K
of
G
containing
F
, its
Sylow
p
-subgroup
K_{p}

centralises
F_{p^{\prime}}
and thus
K_{p}\leqslant{\mathbf{C}(p)}
.

Before giving the proof of
A
we will need one extra piece of information regarding nilpotent injectors that was already noted in
[
IPMM05
]
.

Lemma 2

(Iranzo et al.)
.

Let
G
be an
\mathcal{N}
-constrained group,
I
a nilpotent injector of
G
and
H\leqslant I
a fixed nilpotent subgroup of
G
.
For each
p\in\varpi(\fitt(G))
,
let
H_{p}
be the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup of
H

and note that
H_{p}\leqslant I_{p}\leqslant{\mathbf{C}(p)}=\cent_{G}(F_{p^{\prime}})
.
Then

n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)=\prod_{p\in\varpi}n_{p}({\mathbf{C}(p)},H_{p}),

where
for a fixed
p
-subgroup
X
of
Y
,

n_{p}(Y,X)
is the number of Sylow
p
-subgroups of
Y
that contain
X
.

Proof.

This follows from the first formula in the proof of Corollary 1.3 in
[
IPMM05
]
.

\blacksquare

We can now prove our first Theorem.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Suppose that the claim is false and let
G
be a counterexample.
Among subgroups for which
G
fails to satisfy the conclusion of the theorem,
let
H
have maximum possible order,
so that
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)\neq 0

and
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)\not\equiv 1\,\left(\textnormal{mod}\ m_{G}\right)
.
In particular,
H
is necessarily nilpotent and we may assume that
H\leqslant I

for a fixed nilpotent injector
I
of
G
.
We argue that
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)=n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H\fitt(G))

since every nilpotent injector of
G
contains
\fitt(G)
.
Thus, by the extremal choice of
H
, we see that
H\geqslant\fitt(G)
.

Let
\varpi=\varpi(G)
be the set of primes that divide
\left\lvert{G}\right\rvert
and write

\varpi=\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2}\cup\alpha
with

\displaystyle\pi_{1}

\displaystyle=\{p\in\varpi:F_{p}\in\syl_{p}(G)\}

\displaystyle\pi_{2}

\displaystyle=\{p\in\varpi:I_{p}\in\syl_{p}(G),I_{p}>F_{p}\}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\{p\in\varpi:p\mid(G:I)\}.

Observe that the above sets may be empty,
but if
p\in\pi_{1}
then
p\nmid(G:F)

and if
q\in\pi_{2}
then
q\nmid(G:I)
and
q\mid(G:F)
.
Finally,
m_{G}=\gcd\left\{p-1:p\in\alpha\right\}
.

Now let
\varpi(\fitt(G))
be the set of primes that divide
\left\lvert{\fitt(G)}\right\rvert

and note that

\varpi(\fitt(G))=\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2}\cup\pi_{3}

with
\pi_{3}\subseteq\alpha

(by the preliminary remarks we have
\varpi(\fitt(G))=\varpi(I)
).
So

\fitt(G)=F_{\pi_{1}}\times F_{\pi_{2}}\times F_{\pi_{3}}.

For each
p\in\varpi(\fitt(G))
,
let
H_{p}
be the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup of
H
. Then according to
2
we have
H_{p}\leqslant I_{p}\leqslant{\mathbf{C}(p)}=\cent_{G}(F_{p^{\prime}})
and

n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)=\prod_{p\in\varpi}n_{p}({\mathbf{C}(p)},H_{p}),

where
n_{p}({\mathbf{C}(p)},H_{p})
is the number of Sylow
p
-subgroups of
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
that contain
H_{p}
.

Assume first that
p\in\pi_{1}
.
Then
n_{p}({\mathbf{C}(p)},H_{p})=1

since
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
contains the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G

(whose uniqueness persists in
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
).

Now for each
p\in\pi_{2}\cup\pi_{3}
,
let
\beta=(\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2})\setminus\{p\}

and
I_{\beta}=K
be the Hall
\beta
-subgroup of
I
.
Then

K=\prod_{q\in\pi_{1}}F_{q}\times\prod_{q\in\pi_{2}\setminus\{p\}}I_{q}

and
K
is moreover a Hall
\beta
-subgroup of
G
.

Let
Q={\mathbf{C}(p)}\cap K
and observe that

Q
is a Hall
\beta
-subgroup of
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
as
{\mathbf{C}(p)}\unlhd G
.
By the construction of
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
(see
1
)
we get that
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
normalises
I_{q}
for all
q\neq p
and thus
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
normalises
Q
. So the Schur–Zassenhaus theorem ensures
that there exists a subgroup
T

such that
{\mathbf{C}(p)}=Q\rtimes T
with
\varpi(T)\subseteq\alpha\cup\{p\}
.
Without loss
(as all such
T
form a
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
-conjugacy class)
we may assume that
H_{p}\leqslant I_{p}\leqslant T
.
Now,
I_{p}
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
that centralises
Q
.
Hence

n_{p}({\mathbf{C}(p)},H_{p})=\left\lvert{\{I_{p}^{g}:H_{p}\leqslant I_{p}^{g},\,g\in{\mathbf{C}(p)}\}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert{\{I_{p}^{t}:H_{p}\leqslant I_{p}^{t},\,t\in T\}}\right\rvert=n_{p}(T,H_{p}).

But

n_{p}(T,H_{p})\equiv 1\,\left(\textnormal{mod}\ m_{p}\right)

by Corollary 8 in
[
AM23
]
,
where

m_{p}=\gcd\{q-1:q\mid\left\lvert{T}\right\rvert,\,q\neq p\}.

As
\varpi(T)\setminus\{p\}\subseteq\alpha
,
we get
m_{G}\mid m_{p}
.
Hence
n_{\mathfrak{I}}({\mathbf{C}(p)},H_{p})\equiv 1\,\left(\textnormal{mod}\ m_{G}\right)

for all
p\in\pi_{2}\cup\pi_{3}
and so
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)\equiv 1\,\left(\textnormal{mod}\ m_{G}\right)
,
against our initial assumption.
This contradiction shows that the original claim is valid and thus our proof is complete.
\blacksquare

We wish to present applications of
A

that are not covered by Corollary 1.3 in
[
IPMM05
]

or Corollary 8 in
[
AM23
]
.

Example 3
.

Let
R
be an
\mathcal{N}
-constrained insoluble group of even order such that
\fitt(R)=\mathbf{O}_{2}(R)
,
let
S
be a nontrivial group of odd order, and set
G=R\times S
.
Since
S
is itself
\mathcal{N}
-constrained, being soluble,
we see that
G
is
\mathcal{N}
-constrained.
Now observe that
\fitt(G)=\fitt(R)\times\fitt(S)

and that the nilpotent injectors of
R
are precisely its Sylow
2
-subgroups.
It follows that a nilpotent injector
I
of
G

(which exists owing to the fact that
G
is
\mathcal{N}
-constrained)
contains a full Sylow
2
-subgroup of
R
and thus has odd index in
G
.
Since
p-1
is even for all primes
p
such that
p\mid(G:I)
,
we see that
2\mid m_{G}
and so we may conclude that
for all subgroups
H
of
I
,
n_{\mathfrak{I}}(G,H)
is odd.
An example of a group
R
with the stated properties
is
\mathrm{AGL}_{n}(2)
with
n>2
.

Given a group
G
and a poset
\mathcal{X}
of subgroups of
G
,
we define the function
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}^{G}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
by

\theta_{\mathcal{X}}^{G}(H)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(H,K)=\sum_{H\leqslant K\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(H,K)\,.

In the case
G
is clearly understood, we write
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}^{G}(H)=\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(H)
.
A consequence of a result of Brown
[
Bro75
]
is that
if
\mathcal{X}
is the poset of all
p
-subgroups of
G
,
where
p
is some fixed prime divisor of
\left\lvert{G}\right\rvert
,
then
\left\lvert{G}\right\rvert_{p}
divides
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(1)
,
where
\left\lvert{G}\right\rvert_{p}
is the order of a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
.
In fact, Brown’s theorem was subsequently further generalised by Hawkes, Isaacs, and Özaydin in
[
HIÖ89
, Thm. 5.1]
.

We wish to provide an analogue of Brown’s theorem
for subgroups contained in at least one nilpotent injector of
G
.
So, let

\mathcal{X}=\{H:H\leqslant I\,\,\mbox{ for at least one }\,I\in\inj(G)\},

where as before
I
is some fixed nilpotent injector of
G

(the implicit assumption here is that
G
is non-trivial and
\mathcal{N}
-constrained).
Then
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(1)=0
by Lemma 2.4 in
[
HIÖ89
]

since
\mathrm{Core}_{G}(I)=\fitt(G)>1
.
However, the situation becomes more interesting
by restricting to the
“top part”
of
\mathcal{X}
,
i.e. by focussing on

\mathcal{Y}=\{H\in\mathcal{X}:\fitt(G)\leqslant H\}\,.

Observe that
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(\fitt(G))=\theta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\fitt(G))
because

\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(\fitt(G),K)=0
for all
K\notin\mathcal{Y}
.
We are now ready to give the proof of
B
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

As before, let
\varpi=\varpi(\fitt(G))

and write
F
in place of
\fitt(G)
.
Let
{\mathbf{C}(p)}=\cent_{G}(F_{p^{\prime}})
and
K
any nilpotent subgroup of
G
containing
F
. In view of
1
we get
K_{p}\leqslant{\mathbf{C}(p)}
and thus the poset of all
p
-subgroups of
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
containing
F_{p}

coincides with the poset of all
p
-subgroups of
\mathcal{X}
containing
F_{p}
.

We denote the above poset by
\mathcal{Y}_{p}
, that is

\mathcal{Y}_{p}=\{U\in\mathcal{X}:F_{p}\leqslant U\text{ is a $p$-group}\}=\{F_{p}\leqslant U\leqslant{\mathbf{C}(p)}:U\text{ is a $p$-group}\}.

Now, if
F=\fitt(G)\leqslant K\in\mathcal{X}

then
K=\prod_{p\in\varpi}K_{p}

with
K_{p}\in\mathcal{Y}_{p}
and thus

\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F,K)=\prod_{p\in\varpi}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{p},K_{p})\,,

by Theorem 8.4 in
[
HIÖ89
]
.
Next, notice that any selection
\{K_{p}\in\mathcal{Y}_{p}:p\in\varpi\}

defines a nilpotent subgroup
K=\prod_{p\in\varpi}K_{p}
of
G
above
F
.
Hence
K
is contained in a nilpotent injector of
G
and so
K\in\mathcal{X}
.
We conclude that

(2.1)

\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(F)=\sum_{F\leqslant K\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F,K)=\sum_{F\leqslant K\in\mathcal{X}}\prod_{p\in\varpi}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{p},K_{p})=\prod_{p\in\varpi}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{Y}_{p}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{p},U)\,.

Applying Corollary 3.9 in
[
HIÖ89
]

with
{\mathbf{C}(p)}
in place of
G

and
F_{p}
in place of
H
yields that

({\mathbf{C}(p)}:F_{p})_{p}=(I:F)_{p}\quad\quad\mbox{divides}\quad\sum_{U\in\mathcal{Y}_{p}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{p},U).

It follows that

\,(I:F)=\prod_{p\in\varpi}(I:F)_{p}\,
divides

\,\prod_{p\in\varpi}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{Y}_{p}}\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(F_{p},U)=\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(F)\,,

completing the proof.

\blacksquare

We mention that
B
is not true if
I

is an arbitrary nilpotent subgroup of
G

(with
\fitt(G)
being naturally replaced by
\mathrm{Core}_{G}(I)
)
nor is it true if
I
is specifically the dual of a nilpotent injector,
i.e. a Carter subgroup of
G
.
Concretely, the group
SmallGroup(54,5)
has a core-free
Carter subgroup
C
that is cyclic of order
6
.
But for
\mathcal{X}=\{1\leqslant H\leqslant C^{g}:g\in G\}
we get

(C:\mathrm{Core}_{G}(C))=6
and
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(1)=-2
.
Moreover, in the context of
B
it is
not
true
that
\left\lvert{I}\right\rvert
divides
\theta_{\mathcal{X}}(1)
.
This fails to be the case in

\blacksquare

We now begin work to show that in the presence of a normal Hall subgroup
there is such a version of
“divisibility”

(cf.
E
and the corollaries that follow)
and start with the next lemma which is a generalisation of Lemma 1.1 in
[
IPMM05
]
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
G
be a finite group with
X\leqslant C\leqslant G
and assume
N\unlhd G
with
G=N\rtimes C
.
If
\mathcal{A}=\{C^{g}:g\in G\}
is the conjugacy class of
C
in
G
, then

\left\lvert{\{C^{g}\in\mathcal{A}:X\leqslant C^{g}\}}\right\rvert=(\cent_{N}(X):\cent_{N}(C))\,.

Proof.

If
C^{g}\in\mathcal{A}
with
X\leqslant C^{g}

then
C^{g}=C^{n}
for some
n\in N
and
X\leqslant C^{n}\cap C
.
But
C\cap C^{n}\leqslant\cent_{C}(n)
.
Hence
X\leqslant\cent_{C}(n)
and thus

\cent_{N}(X)=\{n\in N:X\leqslant C^{n}\}.

Therefore,
\cent_{N}(X)
acts transitively on the set
\{C^{n}\in\mathcal{A}:X\leqslant C^{n}\}
,
which is the orbit of
C
under this action.
The stabiliser of
C
is
\cent_{\cent_{N}(X)}(C)=\cent_{N}(C)
,
so

\left\lvert{\{C^{n}:X\leqslant C^{n}\}}\right\rvert=(\cent_{N}(X):\cent_{N}(C))

and the proof is complete.

\blacksquare

The above implies:

Proposition 10
.

Assume that
G
is a finite soluble group,
X\leqslant C\leqslant G
with
C\in\cart(G)
,
and
N\unlhd G
such that
G=N\rtimes C
.
Then
n_{C}(G,X)=\left\lvert{\cent_{N}(X)}\right\rvert
divides
\left\lvert{\cart(G)}\right\rvert
.

Proof.

As
C\in\cart(G)
we have
\cent_{N}(C)\leqslant\N_{G}(C)\cap N=C\cap N=1
.
Hence
9
implies that
n_{C}(G,X)=\left\lvert{\cart(G,X)}\right\rvert=\left\lvert{\cent_{N}(X)}\right\rvert
,
since the Carter subgroups of
G
form a single
G
-conjugacy class.
But
\left\lvert{\cart(G)}\right\rvert=(G:\N_{G}(C))=(G:C)=|N|
,
and the proposition follows.

\blacksquare

The following lemma was communicated to the first author by I.M. Isaacs in the Winter of 2016 and with his kind permission we include it here.

Lemma 11

(Isaacs)
.

Let
N
be a normal Hall
\pi
-subgroup of the finite soluble group
G
,
where
\pi\subseteq\varpi(G)
,
and let
Q\in\Hall_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)
.
If
C
is a Carter subgroup of
G
then
C_{\pi^{\prime}}
is a Carter subgroup of some conjugate of
Q
.
Furthermore, for a given Carter subgroup
D
of the fixed subgroup
Q

we have

\cart(G,D)=\{DU:U\in\cart(\cent_{N}(D))\}.

Proof.

Let
C\in\cart(G)
and
D\in\cart(Q)
. Both
CN
and
DN
are full preimages of Carter subgroups of
G/N
and so, by conjugating appropriately, we may assume that
CN=DN
. Then
D
is a Hall
\pi^{\prime}
-subgroup of
DN
, and
C_{\pi^{\prime}}
is a Hall
\pi^{\prime}
-subgroup of
CN
, given that
N
is a
\pi
-group.
We conclude that
C_{\pi^{\prime}}=D^{x}
for some
x\in G
. As
D^{x}
is a Carter subgroup of
Q^{x}
,
the first claim of the lemma follows.

To prove that
\cart(G,D)=\{DU:U\in\cart(\cent_{N}(D))\}
,
we first fix
C\in\cart(G,D)
and we claim that
C\in\{DU:U\in\cart(\cent_{N}(D))\}
. Indeed,
C=U\times D
with
U\coloneqq C\cap N
being the unique Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
C
. Note that
U
centralises
D
, thus
U\leqslant Y\coloneqq\cent_{N}(D)
, and we want that
U
be Carter in
Y
. But
U
is nilpotent, so it suffices that
\N_{Y}(U)=U
. If some
g\in Y
normalises
U
, then it normalises
C
, since it centralises
D
, thus
g\in C\cap Y\leqslant U
.

Conversely, let
C\coloneqq DU
where
D\in\cart(Q)
and
U\in\cart(\cent_{N}(D))
as before. The aim is to show that
C\in\cart(G,D)
. Clearly, both
U
and
D
are nilpotent mutually centralising subgroups of
C
, thus both normal in
C
, and it follows that
C
is nilpotent. To complete the proof, it thus suffices to establish that
\N_{G}(C)=C
.
First, let us prove that
C
is Carter in
X\coloneqq ND
.

By Dedekind,
\N_{X}(C)=\N_{N}(C)D
, thus it suffices that
\N_{N}(C)=U
.
The uniqueness of Hall subgroups in nilpotent groups
forces
\N_{N}(C)
to normalise both
U
and
D
and thus
\N_{N}(C)=\N_{N}(U)\cap\N_{N}(D)
.
But
\N_{N}(D)=\cent_{N}(D)

and so

\N_{N}(C)=\N_{N}(U)\cap\cent_{N}(D)=\N_{\cent_{N}(D)}(U).

It follows that
\N_{N}(C)=U
, since
U\in\cart(\cent_{N}(D))
.

Now,
X/N
is Carter in
G/N
, and
C
is Carter in
X
.
Therefore,
C
is Carter in
G
by our preliminary remarks at the beginning of the section,
and the proof is complete.

\blacksquare

Let

be a normal Hall

,
where

is a prime dividing the order of

and let

Then every Carter subgroup

Furthermore, for a fixed Sylow

The only Carter subgroup of a

We can now prove

Theorem E
.

Let
G
be soluble, and suppose that
N

is a normal Hall
q^{\prime}
-subgroup of
G
, where
q\in\varpi(G)
.
Let
X\leqslant G
and assume that

X
is contained in some Carter subgroup
C
of
G
.
Write
X=X_{q^{\prime}}\times X_{q}
and
C=C_{q^{\prime}}\times C_{q}

for their respective decompositions according to Sylow
q
- and Hall
q^{\prime}
-subgroups.
Let
A=\cent_{G}(X_{q^{\prime}})
and note that
X_{q}\leqslant C_{q}\leqslant A
. Then
C_{q}
is a Sylow
q
-subgroup of both
G
and
A
and

(3.4)

n_{C}(G,X)\,=|\syl_{q}(A,X_{q})|\cdot n_{C}(\cent_{N}(C_{q}),X_{q^{\prime}})

Proof.

In view of
12
the group
C_{q}
is a Sylow
q
-subgroup of
G
and thus of
A
as it is contained in
A
. The same corollary implies that for any
S\in\cart(G,X)
we have
S_{q}\in\syl_{q}(G,X_{q})
. But
S_{q}
centralizes
S_{q^{\prime}}\geqslant X_{q^{\prime}}
and thus
S_{q}\leqslant A=C_{G}(X_{q^{\prime}})
which in turn implies that
S_{q}\in\syl_{q}(A,X_{q})
.

On the other hand, if
R\in\syl_{q}(A,X_{q})
then we claim that there exists a Carter subgroup
S\in\cart(G,X)
having
R
as its Sylow
q
-subgroup, i.e.
S_{q}=R
. Indeed,
R=(C_{q})^{a}
for some
a\in A
because both
R
and
C_{q}
are Sylow
q
-subgroups of
A
. Note that
(C_{q^{\prime}})^{a}
also lies above
X_{q^{\prime}}
because
C_{q^{\prime}}
contains
X_{q^{\prime}}
and
a
centralizes
X_{q^{\prime}}
. Therefore,
C^{a}
contains
X
and clearly
C^{a}
is a Carter subgroup of
G
.
Hence the desired Carter
S
is
S=C^{a}
for some
a\in A
.

We conclude therefore that the map sending any
S\in\cart(G,X)
to
S_{q}\in\syl_{q}(A,X_{q})
is well defined and onto. We want now to count the number of Carter subgroups of
G
over
X
that are mapped to the same Sylow
q
-subgroup of
A
(over
X_{q}
). Assume
R\in\syl_{q}(A,X_{q})
and let
S\in\cart(G,X)
with
S_{q}=R
.
Then
R
is also a Sylow
q
-subgroup of
G
and
by
12
, the Carter subgroups of
G
that contain
R
are exactly
the subgroups
RU
, where
U
is a Carter subgroup of
\cent_{N}(R)
. By

contains

, so we see that

contains
X
if
and only if
U
contains
X_{q^{\prime}}
. It follows that the number

that have
R
as a Sylow
q

is

m(R)=\left\lvert{\cart(G,R)\cap\cart(G,X)}\right\rvert=n_{C}(\cent_{N}(R),X_{q^{\prime}}).

We argue that
n_{C}(\cent_{N}(R),X_{q^{\prime}})=n_{C}(\cent_{N}(C_{q}),X_{q^{\prime}})

Indeed,

We would like to prove for some large class of soluble groups

, where

is a Carter subgroup of

and

.

(*)

Corollary 13
.

Suppose that
G
has a normal Hall
q^{\prime}
-subgroup
N
for some prime

,
and assume that
N
and all its subgroups satisfy
(*)
.
Then

satisfies
(*)
.

Proof.

Let
X\leqslant G
be a nilpotent subgroup of
G
and
C\in\cart(G,X)
, we will show that
n_{C}(G,X)
divides
(G:C)
.
By
E
, we have

n_{C}(G,X)=|\syl_{q}(A,X_{q})|\cdot n_{C}(\cent_{N}(C_{q}),X_{q^{\prime}})

where the notation is as in the theorem. Write
G=NC_{q}
since (as we have seen)
C_{q}\in\syl_{q}(G)
.
We claim that
C_{q^{\prime}}
is a Carter subgroup of
\cent_{N}(C_{q})
.
To see this, note that if
x\in\N_{\cent_{N}(C_{q})}(C_{q^{\prime}})
then

and thus the nilpotent subgroup

\blacksquare

Recall that a group
G
is said to possess a Sylow tower,
or equivalently to be a Sylow tower group,
if there exists a normal series where each successive quotient is isomorphic to a Sylow subgroup of
G
.
Groups with a Sylow tower are, of course, soluble.
Moreover, they comprise a subgroup-closed formation
and so quotients and subgroups of Sylow tower groups are again Sylow tower.

Corollary 14
.

Groups with a Sylow tower satisfy
(*)
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on the order of the group,
the base case of the trivial group being trivially true.
Let
G
be a group with a Sylow tower and suppose the
claim is valid for all groups with a Sylow tower and order
strictly less than
|G|
. In case
G=Q
is a
q
-group the claim is clearly true, and thus we may assume that the order of
G
is divisible by at least two primes.
Then there exists a prime
q
and a Hall
q^{\prime}
-subgroup
N

of
G
such that
N
is normal in
G
and
N<G
.
Clearly
N
is a group with a Sylow tower and thus, by induction,

N
and all its subgroups satisfy
(*)
.
Therefore, applying
13
yields that
G

satisfies
(*)
, completing the induction.

\blacksquare

A direct consequence of
14

is that supersoluble groups satisfy
(*)
.
