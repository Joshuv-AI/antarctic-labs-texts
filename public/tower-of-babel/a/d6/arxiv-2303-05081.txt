Sums of squares representations on singular loci
Ngoc Hoang Anh Mai, Victor Magron

Abstract
The problem of characterizing a real polynomial f f as a sum of squares of polynomials on a real algebraic variety V V dates back to the pioneering work of Hilbert in [ 20 ] . In this paper, we investigate this problem with a focus on cases where the real zeros of f f on V V are singular points of V V . By using optimality conditions and irreducible decomposition, we provide a positive answer to the following essential question of polynomial optimization: Are there always exact semidefinite programs to compute the minimum value attained by a given polynomial over a given real algebraic variety? Our answer implies that Lasserre’s hierarchy, which is known as a bridge between convex and non-convex programs with algebraic structures, has finite convergence not only in the generic case but also in the general case. As a result, we constructively prove that each hyperbolic program is equivalent to a semidefinite program .



Keywords:
sum of squares; Nichtnegativstellensatz; gradient ideal; singular locus; polynomial optimization; Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions; semidefinite programming

1
Introduction

Semidefinite programs, Positivstellensätze, and Polynomial optimization.

Semidefinite programming, a subfield of convex optimization, was developed in the early 1960s by Bellman and Fan
[
6
]
. Its goal is to minimize a linear objective function over the intersection of the cone of positive semidefinite matrices with an affine space. One of the significant applications of semidefinite programming is to relax a class of non-convex programs with algebraic structures, which are known as polynomial optimization problems. These problems have objective and constraint functions that are all polynomials.

In 2001, Lasserre introduced an appropriate hierarchy of semidefinite programs in
[
28
]
that returns a sequence of values approximately converging to the optimal value of a given polynomial optimization problem. To do this, he utilized Positivstellensätze, which are representations of polynomials positive on a basic semi-algebraic set, a set defined by a system of finitely many polynomial inequalities. For instance, Putinar’s Positivstellensatz
[
48
]
, which guarantees the convergence of Lasserre’s hierarchy, says that each polynomial positive on a compact basic semi-algebraic set
S
, satisfying the so-called Archimedean condition, can be decomposed as a linear combination of polynomials defining
S
, with weights that are sums of squares of polynomials.

Exact semidefinite programs and Nichtnegativstellensätze.

We aim to develop exact semidefinite programs for determining the optimal value of a given polynomial optimization problem.
In this context, the term “exact semidefinite program” means that, given the objective and constraint polynomials of a polynomial optimization problem, we can algorithmically construct a semidefinite program whose optimal value is precisely equal to that of the original problem.
Obtaining such programs is highly significant since it enables us to use convex optimization to solve non-convex problems.
Moreover, it is typically challenging to bound the gap between the convex relaxation and the original problem, as is the case with Goemans-Williamson’s algorithm for Max-Cut
[
15
]
.

To provide such exact semidefinite programs, we need to build Nichtnegativstellensätze that are the representations of polynomials non-negative on a basic semi-algebraic set.
Marshall showed in
[
37
,
38
]

a Nichtnegativstellensatz having the same form as Putinar’s under the so-called
boundary Hessian conditions
.
Based on this, Nie proves in
[
45
]
that Lasserre’s hierarchy has finite convergence under generic assumptions related to second-order optimality conditions.
The works of Marshall and Nie rely on the local-global principle stated by Scheiderer in
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
.
There Scheiderer proved the non-strict extension of Schmüdgen’s Positivstellensatz
[
52
]
.
The former says that every polynomial
f
positive on a compact basic semi-algebraic set
S
is a linear combination of products of polynomials defining
S
with weights that are sums of squares of polynomials.
In his proof, Scheiderer needs a finiteness assumption on the real zeros of
f
on
S
.
We refer the readers to
[
8
]
for some extensions by Burgdorf, Scheiderer, and Schweighofer that allow us to remove the finiteness assumption on the real zeros of
f
on
S
but still maintain the compactness of
S
.
Under strong regularity assumption, Nie obtains in his work
[
43
]
exact semidefinite programs when using the Jacobian of the input polynomials.

Nichtnegativstellensätze based on optimality conditions.

We consider a semi-algebraic set
S
as a real manifold.
A point
a
in
S
is called a singular (resp. regular) point of
S
if the tangent space of
S
at
a
has smaller (resp. the same) dimension than
S
.
The singular (resp. regular) locus of
S
is the set of all singular (resp. regular) points of
S
.

Given a polynomial
f
non-negative on a basic semi-algebraic set
S
, Demmel, Nie, and Powers provide in
[
11
]
a way to obtain a Nichtnegativstellensatz on the intersection
S\cap V^{\KKT}
.
Here
V^{\KKT}
represents for the variety defined by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions for the minimization of
f
on
S
.
Using the Fritz-John conditions, the first author extends this approach in
[
33
]
to the case where the image of the singular locus
S^{\sing}
of
S
under
f
, denoted by
f(S^{\sing})
, is finite.
He also indicates in
[
33
]
several examples where the previous Nichtnegativstellensätze are inapplicable since the real zeros of
f
on
S
are in
S^{\sing}
.
In
[
32
]
the first author provides the degree bounds for these Nichtnegativstellensätze and analyzes the convergence rate for their application to polynomial optimization.
We emphasize that the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions and the finiteness assumption on
f(S^{\sing})
belong to the
generic
case.
By generic, we mean that the properties hold in a Zariski open set in the space of the coefficients of the input polynomials with given degrees.
The remaining case, corresponding to a set
f(S^{\sing})
of infinite cardinality, has not been tackled so far.

Regarding the above two types of first-order optimality conditions, the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions and the Fritz-John conditions play roles in characterizing the real zeros of polynomial
f
on a semi-algebraic set
S
.
Moreover, it is not hard to constructively prove the sums of squares-based representations of
f
on the intersection of
S
with the varieties defined by these optimality conditions.
To do so, we use the fact that
f
is constant on each connected component of
V^{\KKT}
, the variety defined by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions associated to the minimization of
f
on
S
.
This property also holds for
V^{\FJ}
, the variety defined by the Fritz-John conditions, under the finiteness assumption on
f(S^{\sing})
.
The difference here is that
V^{\KKT}
contains the real zeros of
f
that are regular points of
S
while
V^{\FJ}
includes all real zeros of
f
on
S
even if they are singular points of
S
.

Contribution.

In the paper, we aim to provide some representations (with degree bounds) of a polynomial
f
non-negative on a basic semi-algebraic set
S
in the general case.

On one hand, we convert
S
to a real algebraic variety
V
(the common real zeros of a system of polynomials) in higher-dimensional real space and consider the representations of
f
on
V
.
Set
U:=V
.
We then decompose
U
into finitely many irreducible components
U_{j}
.
If
U_{j}
has zero dimension, then
f
is identical to a sum of squares of polynomials on
U_{j}
.
Otherwise,
f
is identical to a sum of squares of polynomials on
U_{j}^{\KKT}
, the subvariety of
U_{j}
defined by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions associated to the minimization of
f
on
U_{j}
.
In this case, we update
U:=U_{j}^{\sing}
with
U_{j}^{\sing}
being the singular locus of
U_{j}
and repeat the above process.
It will terminate after a finite number of steps because the singular locus
U_{j}^{\sing}
has lower dimension than
U_{j}
.

On the other hand, we provide the degree bounds of our sums of squares-based representations for
f
on
U_{j}^{\KKT}
(or zero-dimensional
U_{j}
).
Consequently, we analyze the convergence rate of Lasserre’s hierarchy applied to minimizing a polynomial
p
on each
U_{j}^{\KKT}
(or zero-dimensional
U_{j}
).
We prove that Lasserre’s hierarchy on some
U_{j}^{\KKT}
(or zero-dimensional
U_{j}
) has finite convergence to the minimum value
p^{\star}
attained by
p
on
V
.
Based on this and the algebraic structure of hyperbolic cones, we demonstrate that each hyperbolic program can be written as a semidefinite program.

Hyperbolic polynomials and hyperbolic programming.

For interested readers, hyperbolic programming, introduced by Güler in
[
16
]
, is concerned with optimizing a linear objective function over the intersection of an affine space with the so-called hyperbolic cone constrained by a hyperbolic polynomial.
Here a real polynomial
p
is hyperbolic with respect to a given vector
e
if the univariate polynomial
t\mapsto p(te-a)
has only real roots for all vectors
a
.
We refer the readers to
[
18
,
41
,
40
,
25
,
24
,
26
,
53
,
39
]

for some recent works on hyperbolic polynomials and hyperbolic programming.
Their studies have focused on attacking the generalized Lax conjecture, whose direct consequence is that every hyperbolic program is equivalent to a semidefinite program (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Remark 1.7]
).
In our paper, we prove the latter. Readers might wonder if the equivalence of the two programs indicates the coincidence of their feasible sets, leading to a solution for the generalized Lax conjecture.
From our point of view, the generalized Lax conjecture is about fixed cones used in different optimization problems. It is much different from saying that a hyperbolic optimization problem with a given objective function
f
can be solved by a specific semidefinite program depending on the hyperbolic program associated with
f
.

Previous works.

In
[
34
]
, the first author provides a sum of squares-based representation of a polynomial
f
nonnegative on a real algebraic variety
V
in the case where the regular locus
V^{\reg}
of
V
is dense in
V
.
The key idea is to consider
V
as the image of a regular variety
W
under a morphism
\varphi
, defined by a vector of polynomials.
This is done thanks to Hironaka’s resolution of singularities
[
21
,
22
]
.
Then we use Demmel–Nie–Powers’ Nichtnegativstellensatz to get the representation of
f\circ\varphi
on
W_{\KKT}
, the variety defined by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions for minimizing
f\circ\varphi
on
W
.
However, this method is not always applicable to cases where
V^{\reg}
is not dense in
V
(e.g.,
V
is the Whitney or Cartan umbrella).
Compared to this, there is no matter with our method in such challenging cases.

Bucero and Mourrain present in
[
7
]
a technique to obtain exact semidefinite programs for minimizing a polynomial
p
over a semi-algebraic set
S
in the generic case.
Firstly, they consider the equivalent minimization of
p
on a new semi-algebraic set
S_{\FJ}
, the intersection of
S
with the projection of the variety defined by the Fritz-John conditions for minimizing
p
on
S
.
Secondly, they decompose
S_{\FJ}
into two semi-algebraic sets
S_{\KKT}
and
S_{\sing}
, then consider the minimization of
p
on each of these two sets.
Here
S_{\KKT}
is the intersection of
S
with the projection of the variety defined by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions for minimizing
p
on
S
.
The set
S_{\sing}
contains the singular locus of
S
.
They obtain exact semidefinite relaxations for minimizing
p
on
S_{\KKT}
thanks to the work of Demmel, Nie, and Powers
[
11
]
.
The minimization of
p
on
S_{\sing}
is solved recursively.
If
S_{\sing}
is zero-dimensional, they obtain exact semidefinite programs for minimizing
p
on the finite set
S_{\sing}
.
The case of positive-dimensional
S_{\sing}
has not been handled entirely in their paper.

Regarding the exactness property, we emphasize that our representations in this paper are sum-of-squares exactness.
In his work
[
3
]
with Baldi, the third author considers moment exactness.
It is stronger than sum-of-squares exactness in practice since it yields the minimizers and tests exactness for polynomial optimization.
Moreover, it is shown in
[
3
, Theorem 4.14]
that if the polar variety defined by the product of minors (and related to the projection of the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker variety) is finite, then moment exactness also holds.

Motivation.

Our method described in this paper has potential applications to mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
31
]
).
These optimization problems are challenging because the complementarity constraints typically violate all standard constraint qualifications, making it difficult to find a solution. Constraint qualifications are known to be sufficient conditions for the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions in nonlinear programming.
Therefore, classical methods that rely on the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions to solve mathematical programs with complementarity constraints are limited.
In contrast, our method does not require any constraint qualifications

Section

Section

5
is to build higher-order optimality conditions that characterize global minimizers for a polynomial optimization problem.
Section
6

.

Preliminaries

2.1
Real algebraic varieties

Let
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
denote the ring of polynomials with real coefficients in the vector of variables
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.
Let
{\mathbb{R}}_{r}[x]
denote the linear space of polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
of degree at most
r
.

Given
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, we denote by
V(h)
the (real) algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined by the vector
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
, i.e.,

V(h):=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,h_{j}(x)=0\,,\,j=1,\dots,l\}\,.

(1)

In this case,
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
are called the polynomials defining
V(h)
.

Given
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, let
I(h)[x]
be the ideal generated by
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
, i.e.,

I(h)[x]:=\sum_{j=1}^{l}h_{j}{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,.

(2)

The real radical of an ideal
I(h)[x]
, denoted by
\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)[x]}
, is defined as

{\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)[x]}}:=\{f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,:\,\exists m\in{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,-f^{2m}\in\Sigma^{2}[x]+I(h)[x]\}\,.

(3)

Krivine–Stengle’s Nichtnegativstellensatz
[
23
]
imply that

\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)[x]}:=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,:\,p=0\text{ on }V(h)\}\,.

(4)

We say that
I(h)[x]
is real radical if
I(h)[x]=\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)[x]}
.

Given an algebraic variety
V
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we denote by
I(V)
the vanishing ideal of
V
, i.e.,

I(V):=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,:\,p(x)=0\,,\,\forall x\in V\}\,.

(5)

Note that
I(V)
is a real radical ideal.
If
V
is defined by
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
with
h_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, then
I(V)=\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)[x]}
.
To compute the generators of
I(V)
with given polynomials
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
defining
V
, we can use, e.g., Becker–Neuhaus’ method in
[
5
,
42
]
.
Thereby the degrees of the generators of
I(V)
are bounded from above by
d^{2^{\mathcal{O}(n^{2})}}
if the degrees of
h_{j}
s are at most
d
.
In addition, computing the generators of
I(V)
can be done via the kernel of the moment matrices (or annihilator of moment sequences) as in, e.g., Baldi–Mourrain’s method
[
4
]
.
Then it could be interesting to get bounds on the degree of the generators returned by this method.

Given an algebraic variety
V
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we say that
V
is irreducible if there do not exist two proper subvarieties
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
V=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
.

We recall the following result stated in
[
10
, Section 4.6, Theorem 4]
and
[
54
, Lemma 4.9]
about the structure of algebraic varieties:

Lemma 1
.

Let
V
be an algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then
V
can be written as a finite union

V=V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{r}\,,

(6)

where each
V_{j}
is an irreducible algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
V_{j}\not\subset V_{t}
if
j\neq t
.
Moreover, if
V
is defined by polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
, then
r
is bounded by a constant depending solely on
n
and
d
.

We call
V_{1},\dots,V_{r}
in Lemma
1
irreducible components of
V
.
There are several algorithms for finding the polynomials defining
V_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r
, with given polynomials defining
V
.
For instance, Gianni, Trager, and Zacharias suggest an irreducible decomposition (also called primary decomposition) in
[
14
]
.

2.2
Regular and singular loci

Given
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
with
h_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, we denote by
J(h)
the Jacobian matrix associated with
h
, i.e.,

J(h)(x):=\left(\frac{\partial h_{j}}{\partial x_{t}}(x)\right)_{1\leq t\leq n,1\leq j\leq l}\,.

(7)

We define the dimension of an algebraic variety
V
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, denoted by
\dim(V)
, to be the highest dimension at points at which
V
is a real submanifold.
For convenience we assume
\dim(\emptyset)=0
in this paper.

Let
V
be a algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of dimension
d
.
Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be the generators of
I(V)
.
Set
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
We say that
a\in V
is a regular point of
V
if the Jacobian matrix
J(h)(a)
has rank
n-d
.
Here the rank of a matrix
M
with real coefficients is the largest integer
r
such that all
(r+1)\times(r+1)
minors of
M
vanish.
The set
V^{\reg}
of all regular point of
V
is called the regular locus of
V
.
Let
V^{\sing}=V\backslash V^{\reg}
.
We say that
a\in V
is a singular point of
V
if
a\in V^{\sing}
, i.e., the Jacobian matrix
J(h)(a)
has rank smaller than
n-d
.
The set
V^{\sing}
is called the singular locus of
V
.
The tangent space of
V
at
a\in V
, denoted by

T_{a}(V)
, is the linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
given by

T_{a}(V):=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,J(h)(a)^{\top}u=0\}\,.

(8)

It is not hard to prove that
a\in V
is a regular point of
V
iff
\dim(T_{a}(V))=d=\dim(V)
.

Given
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
with
h_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, denote by
m_{t}(h)(x)
the vector of
t\times t
minors of the Jacobian matrix
J(h)(x)
.
Then
m_{t}(h)
has length
\binom{n}{t}\times\binom{l}{t}
.
Each entry of
m_{t}(h)
is in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and has degree at most
t\times\max_{j}\deg(h_{j})
.

The following lemma states some basic properties of singular loci:

Lemma 2
.

Let
V
be an algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of dimension
d
.
Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be the generators of
I(V)
.
Set
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
Then the following conditions hold:

1.

The singular locus of
V
is an algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined by
(h,m_{n-d}(h))
.

2.

The singular locus of
V
has lower dimension than
V
.

Proof.

The first statement is proved similarly to
[
56
, Section 6.2]
.
The proof of the second one can be found in
[
54
, Theorem 4.8]
(see also
[
55
, Section 12.4.2]
).

2.3
First-order optimality conditions

Given
h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, consider the following polynomial optimization problem:

h^{\star}:=\inf\limits_{x\in V(h)}h_{0}(x)\,,

(9)

where
V(h)
is the algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined by
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.

Remark 1
.

The more general form

\begin{array}[]{rl}\inf\limits_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}}&f(y)\\
\text{s.t.}&g_{j}(y)\geq 0\,,\,j=1,\dots,l\,,\end{array}

(10)

can be written as an instance of (
9
) by setting
x=(y,z)
,
h_{0}(x)=f(y)
,
h_{j}(x)=g_{j}(y)-z_{j}^{2}
.

Given
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, we denote by
\nabla p
the gradient of
p
, i.e.,
\nabla p=(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{n}})
.
We recall the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions in the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

Let
h_{0}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
Let
V
be an algebraic variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of dimension
d>0
.
Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be the generators of
I(V)
.
Let
x^{\star}
be a local minimizer for problem (
9
) with
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
Assume that
J(h)(x^{\star})
has rank
n-d
.
Then the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions hold for problem (
9
) at
x^{\star}
, i.e.,

\begin{cases}\exists(\lambda_{1}^{\star},\dots,\lambda_{l}^{\star})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\,:\\
h_{j}(x^{\star})=0\,,\,j=1,\dots,l\,,\\
\nabla h_{0}(x^{\star})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\lambda_{j}^{\star}\nabla h_{j}(x^{\star})\,.\end{cases}

(11)

Proof.

By assumption,
x^{\star}
is a regular point of the manifold
V\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then there exists a diffeomorphism
\Phi:U\to V
for some open set
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
x^{\star}=\Phi(t^{\star})
for some
t^{\star}\in U
.
The differential of
\Phi
at
t\in U
is defined by the linear mapping
D\Phi_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to T_{\Phi(t)}V
,
u\mapsto J(\Phi)(t)^{\top}u
, where
T_{a}V
is the tangent space of
V
at
a\in V
(defined as in (
8
)).
Since
x^{\star}
is a regular points of
V
,
D\Phi_{t^{\star}}
is bijective.
From this, we get
\rank(J(\Phi)(t^{\star}))=d
, which gives the null space of
J(\Phi)(t^{\star})
, denoted by
J(\Phi)(t^{\star})^{\perp}
, has dimension
n-d
thanks to the rank–nullity theorem.
By assumption,
t^{\star}
is a local minimizer of
h_{0}\circ\Phi
on
U
.
It implies that
0=\nabla(h_{0}\circ\Phi)(t^{\star})=J(\Phi)(t^{\star})\times\nabla h_{0}(x^{\star})
, which gives
\nabla h_{0}(x^{\star})
is in
J(\Phi)(t^{\star})^{\perp}
.
In addition, for
j=1,\dots,l
,
(h_{j}\circ\Phi)(t)=0
for all
t\in U
.
Take the gradient in
t
, we obtain
J(\Phi)(t)\times\nabla h_{j}(\Phi(t))=0
, for all
t\in U
, for
j=1,\dots,l
.
It implies that
\nabla h_{j}(\Phi(t))
,
j=1,\dots,l
, are in the null space of
J(\Phi)(t)
, for all
t\in U
.
By assumption, the linear span of
\nabla h_{j}(x^{\star})
,
j=1,\dots,l
, has dimension
n-d
.
Since
J(\Phi)(t^{\star})^{\perp}
has dimension
n-d
,
J(\Phi)(t^{\star})^{\perp}
is the linear span of
\nabla h_{j}(x^{\star})
,
j=1,\dots,l
.
Hence the result follows since
\nabla h_{0}(x^{\star})
is in

2.4
Semi-algebraic set

Given
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
with

the basic semi-algebraic set associated with

S(g):=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,g_{j}(x)\geq 0\,,\,j=1,\dots,m\}\,.

(14)

A semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a subset of the following form

\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\bigcap_{j=1}^{r_{i}}\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,f_{ij}(x)*_{ij}0\}\,,

(15)

where
f_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and
*_{ij}\in\{>,=\}
.
Note that (
15
) is the union of finitely many basic semi-algebraic sets.

Given two semi-algebraic sets
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we say that a mapping
f:A\to B
is semi-algebraic if its graph
\{(x,f(x))\,:\,x\in A\}
is a semi-algebraic set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}
.
A semi-algebraic subset
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is said to be semi-algebraically path connected if for every
x,y
in
A
, there exists a continuous semi-algebraic mapping
\phi:[0,1]\to A
such that
\phi(0)=x
and
\phi(1)=y
.

The following lemma can be found in
[
47
, Proposition 1.6.2 (ii)]
:

Lemma 4
.

Compositions of semi-algebraic maps are semi-algebraic.

The following lemma is given in
[
47
, Theorem 1.8.1]
:

Lemma 5
.

Let
f:(a,b)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a semi-algebraic
function. Then there are
a=a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{s}<a_{s+1}=b
such that, for each
i=0,\dots,s
, the restriction
f|_{(a_{i},a_{i+1})}
is analytic.

The following lemma follows from the mean value theorem:

Lemma 6
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous piecewise-differentiable function, i.e., there exist
0=a_{1}<\dots<a_{r}=1
such that
f
is continuous and
f
is differentiable on each open interval
(a_{i},a_{i+1})
.
Assume that
f
has zero subgradient.
Then
f(0)=f(1)
.

Proof.

By using the mean value theorem on each open interval
(a_{i},a_{i+1})
, we get
f(a_{i})=f(a_{i+1})
.
Hence
f(0)=f(a_{1})=\dots=f(a_{r})=f(1)
yields the result.
∎

Given

(16)

2.5
Sums of squares

Denote by
\Sigma^{2}[x]
(resp.
\Sigma^{2}_{r}[x]
) the cone of sums of squares of polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
(resp.
{\mathbb{R}}_{r}[x]
).
Given
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, let
P_{r}(g)[x]
be the truncated preordering of order
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
associated with
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
, i.e.,

P_{r}(g)[x]:=\{\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\sigma_{\alpha}g^{\alpha}\,:\,\sigma_{\alpha}\in\Sigma^{2}[x]\,,\,\deg(\sigma_{\alpha}g^{\alpha})\leq 2r\}\,,

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
and

If

, it holds that

.

Given

, let

be the truncated ideal of order
r
defined by
h
, i.e.,

I_{r}(h)[x]:=\{\sum_{j=1}^{l}h_{j}\psi_{j}\,:\,\psi_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,,\,\deg(h_{j}\psi_{j})\leq 2r\}\,.

(18)

We denote by
\bit(d)
the number of bits of
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.,

\bit(d):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }d=0\,,\\
k&\text{if }d\neq 0\text{ and }2^{k-1}\leq d<2^{k}.\end{cases}

(19)

Given
n,d,s\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define

b(n,d,s):=2^{2^{\left(2^{\max\{2,d\}^{4^{n}}}+s^{2^{n}}\max\{2,d\}^{16^{n}\bit(d)}\right)}}\,.

(20)

We recall the degree bounds for Krivine–Stengle’s Nichtnegativstellensätze by Lombardi, Perrucci, and Roy
[
30
]
in the following two lemmas:

Lemma 8
.

Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{m},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
.
Assume that
S(g)\cap V(h)=\emptyset
with

and

.
Set

.
Then it holds that

.

.

Let
p,g_{1},\dots,g_{m},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
in

.

vanishes on

2.6
Nichtnegativstellensätze on regular loci

Denote by
|\cdot|
the cardinality of a set and by
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta function at
(i,j)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
.

We state in the following lemma a sums of squares-based representation with degree bound for a polynomial which has finitely many non-negative values on a real algebraic variety:

Lemma 10
.

Let
h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
.
Assume that
h_{0}
is non-negative on
V(h)
and
h_{0}(V(h))
is finite with
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
Set
r:=|h_{0}(V(h))|
and
u:=\frac{1}{2}\times b(n,d,l+1)
.
Then there exists
\sigma\in\Sigma^{2}_{w}[x]
with
w=\max\{d(r-1),u+d\}
such that
h_{0}-\sigma
vanishes on
V(h)
.

Proof.

Consider the following two cases:

•

Case 1:
r=0
. It is obvious that
V(h)=\emptyset
.
Lemma
8
says that
-1\in\Sigma^{2}_{u}[x]+I_{u}(h)[x]
.
It implies that there exists
q\in P_{u}(g)[x]
such that

-1=q
on
V(h)
.
We write
h_{0}=s_{1}-s_{2}
, where
s_{1}=(h_{0}+\frac{1}{2})^{2}
and
s_{2}=h_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}
are in
\Sigma^{2}[x]
.
From this we get
h_{0}=s_{1}+qs_{2}
on
V(h)
.
Letting
\sigma=s_{1}+qs_{2}
gives
\sigma\in\Sigma^{2}_{u+d}[x]\subset\Sigma^{2}_{w}[x]
since
w\geq u+d
.
Thus
h_{0}-\sigma
vanishes on
V(h)
.

•

Case 2:
r>0
. By assumption, we can assume that

h_{0}(V(h))=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\}\subset[0,\infty)
,
where
t_{i}\neq t_{j}
if
i\neq j
.
For
j=1,\dots,r
, let

W_{j}:=V(h,h_{0}-t_{j})
.
Then
W_{j}
is a real variety defined by
l+1
polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
.
It is clear that
h_{0}(W_{j})=\{t_{j}\}
.
Define the following polynomials:

p_{j}(x):=\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{h_{0}(x)-t_{i}}{t_{j}-t_{i}}\,,\,j=1,\dots,r\,.

(21)

It is easy to check that
p_{j}(W_{i})=\{\delta_{ji}\}
and
\deg(p_{j})\leq d(r-1)
.
Note that
h_{0}=t_{i}\geq 0
on
W_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,r
.
Now letting
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{r}t_{i}p_{i}^{2}
,
we obtain
\sigma\in\Sigma^{2}_{w}[x]
since
\deg(t_{i}p_{i}^{2})\leq 2\deg(p_{i})\leq 2d(r-1)\leq 2w
.
Hence
h_{0}-\sigma
vanishes on
V(h)=W_{1}\cup\dots\cup W_{r}
, yielding the result.

∎

The following lemma is similar to
[
11
, Lemma 3.3]
but is proved by using the tools from real algebraic geometry (instead of the ones from complex algebraic geometry):

Lemma 11
.

Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
Let

be a polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
Let

be a semi-algebraically path connected component of

, where

. Then

is constant on

.

Proof.

Recall
\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})
.
Choose two arbitrary points
(x^{(0)},\lambda^{(0)})
,
(x^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)})
in
W
.
We claim that
h_{0}(x^{(0)})=h_{0}(x^{(1)})
.
By assumption, there exists a continuous semi-algebraic mapping
\phi:[0,1]\to W
defined by
\phi(\tau)=(x(\tau),\lambda(\tau))
such that
\phi(0)=(x^{(0)},\lambda^{(0)})
and
\phi(1)=(x^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)})
.
We claim that
\tau\mapsto h_{0}(x(\tau))
is constant on
[0,1]
.
The Lagrangian function

L(x,\lambda):=h_{0}(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}h_{j}(x)\,

(22)

is equal to
h_{0}(x)
on
V(\bar{h}_{\KKT})
, which contains
\phi([0,1])
.
By Lemma
4
, the function

is semi-algebraic.
Moreover, the function

is continuous since

and

are continuous.
It implies that

.
Note that the function

From Lemma

, it follows that
h_{0}(x(0))=(L\circ\phi)(0)=(L\circ\phi)(1)=h_{0}(x(1))
.
We now obtain

and hence

is constant on

.

2.7
Exact polynomial optimization in the generic case

We recall some preliminaries of the Moment-SOS relaxations originally developed by Lasserre in
[
28
]
.
Given
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}:=\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\,:\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\leq d\}
.
Given
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by
v_{d}
the vector of monomials in
x
of degree at most
d
, i.e.,
v_{d}=(x^{\alpha})_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}
with
x^{\alpha}:=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.
For each
p\in{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
, we write
p=c(p)^{\top}v_{d}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}p_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
, where
c(p)
is denoted by the vector of coefficient of
p
, i.e.,
c(p)=(p_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}
with
p_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Given
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
being symmetric, we say that
A
is positive semidefinite, denoted by
A\succeq 0
, if every eigenvalue of
A
is non-negative.

Given
y=(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, let
L_{y}:{\mathbb{R}}[x]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the Riesz linear functional defined by
L_{y}(p)=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}}p_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}
for every
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
Given
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and
y=(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, let
M_{d}(y)
be the moment matrix of order
d
defined by
(y_{\alpha+\beta})_{\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}_{d}}
.

The following lemma shows the connection between sums of squares and semidefinite programming (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Proposition 2.1]
):

Lemma 12
.

Let
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
2d\geq\deg(\sigma)
.
Then
\sigma\in\Sigma^{2}[x]
iff there exists
G\succeq 0
such that
\sigma=v_{d}^{\top}Gv_{d}
.

Given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, consider the following primal-dual semidefinite programs associated with
\bar{h}=(h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\tau_{k}(\bar{h}):=\inf\limits_{y}&L_{y}(h_{0})\\
\text{s.t}&M_{k}(y)\succeq 0\,,\\
&M_{k-r_{t}}(h_{t}y)=0\,,\,t=1,\dots,l\,,\,y_{0}=1\,,\end{array}

(28)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\rho_{k}(\bar{h}):=\sup\limits_{\xi,G,u_{t}}&\xi\\
\text{s.t}&G\succeq 0\,,\\
&h_{0}-\xi=v_{k}^{\top}Gv_{k}+\sum_{t=1}^{l}h_{t}u_{t}^{\top}v_{2(k-r_{t})}\,,\\
\end{array}

(29)

where
r_{t}=\lceil\deg(h_{t})/2\rceil
.
Using Lemma
12
, we obtain

\rho_{k}(\bar{h})=\sup_{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}}\{\xi\,:\,h_{0}-\xi\in\Sigma_{k}[x]+I_{k}(h)[x]\}\,.

(30)

Primal-dual semidefinite programs (
28
)-(
29
) is known as the Moment-SOS relaxations of order
k
for problem (
9
).

We state in the following lemma some recent results involving the Moment-SOS relaxations:

Lemma 13
.

Let
h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
.
Let
h^{\star}
be as in (
9
) with
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
Set
\bar{h}=(h_{0},h)
.
Assume that
h^{\star}>-\infty
.
Then the following statements hold:

1.

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\tau_{k}(\bar{h})\leq\tau_{k+1}(\bar{h})
and
\rho_{k}(\bar{h})\leq\rho_{k+1}(\bar{h})
.

2.

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\rho_{k}(\bar{h})\leq\tau_{k}(\bar{h})\leq h^{\star}
.

3.

If there exists
q\in\Sigma_{w}[x]
with
2w\geq d
such that
h_{0}-h^{\star}-q
vanishes on
V(h)
, then
\rho_{r}(\bar{h})=h^{\star}
with

is defined as in (

).

Proof.

The proofs of the first two statements are trivial.
Let us use Nie’s technique in
[

3
Decomposition of singular loci

The following algorithm allows us to obtain the zero-dimensional and positive-dimensional subvarieties containing singular points of a real algebraic variety:

Algorithm 1
.

Decomposition of singular loci.

•

Input: Algebraic variety
V
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined by polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.

•

Output: Sets
A
and
B
of subvarieties of
V
.

1.

Set
U:=V
,
A:=\emptyset
and
B:=\emptyset
.

2.

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{r}
be the irreducible components of
U
.

3.

Set
A:=A\cup\{U_{1},\dots,U_{r}\}
.

4.

For
j=1,\dots,r
, do:

(a)

If
U_{j}
is zero-dimensional, then set
A:=A\backslash\{U_{j}\}
and
B:=B\cup\{U_{j}\}
.

(b)

Otherwise, let
U_{j}^{\sing}
be the singular locus of
U_{j}
, set
U:=U_{j}^{\sing}
and run again Steps 2, 3, 4.

Remark 5
.

In order to verify if
U_{j}
is zero-dimensional as in Step 4 (a) of Algorithm
1
, we refer the readers to the work of Lairez and Safey El Din in
[
27
]
.
Moreover, their method also allows us to compute the dimension of a general real algebraic variety.

Remark 6
.

In Step 4 (b) of Algorithm
1
, we implicitly compute generators
h
of the real radical
I(U_{j})
to get the singular locus
U_{j}^{\sing}
as defined by
(h,m_{n-d}(h))
(see the first statement of Lemma
2
).
In particular, if

is defined by
(h,m_{n-d}(h))
for some vector of polynomials

defining
U_{j}
and having multiplicities (e.g.,

for some

), then we have

, and Algorithm
1
loops forever.
Other approaches based on deflation techniques and the so-called Thom–Boardman stratification could avoid this issue.
They allow us to apply directly to the equations and derivatives (see, e.g., the work of Hauenstein and Wampler
[
17
]
).
These methods are probably not very efficient but might give better bounds for polynomials defining singular loci than relying on the computation of real radical based on Becker–Neuhaus’ method in
[
5
,
42
]
.

l_{2}
-norm of a real vector.
Then
\|x\|_{2}^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}
is a polynomial in
x
.

Remark 9
.

To find a global minimizer for problem (
9
), we apply Algorithm
2
and the adding-spherical-constraints method in
[
36
, Section 4.3]
as follows:
Compute the minimum value
h^{\star}
for problem (
9
) by using Algorithm
2
.
Setting
\bar{h}=(h,h_{0}-h^{\star})
implies that
V(\bar{h})
is the set of global minimizers for problem (
9
).
We assume that
V(\bar{h})
is non-empty and find a point
x^{\star}
in
V(\bar{h})
.
Let
(a^{(t)})_{t=0}^{n}
be a finite sequence of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
a^{(t)}-a^{(0)},\,t=1,\dots,n
are linearly independent in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
We use Algorithm
2
to compute the following values:

\begin{array}[]{l}\xi_{0}:=\min\{\|x-a^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\,:\,x\in V(\bar{h})\}\,,\\
\xi_{t}:=\min\{\|x-a^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\,:\,x\in V(\bar{h},b_{0},\dots,b_{t-1})\}\,,\,t=1,\dots,n\,.\end{array}

(43)

where
b_{r}=\xi_{r}-\|x-a^{(r)}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Then there exists a unique real point
x^{\star}
in
V(\bar{h},b_{0},\dots,b_{n})
which satisfies the non-singular linear system of equations

(a^{(t)}-a^{(0)})^{\top}x^{\star}=-\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{t}-\xi_{0}+\|a^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}-\|a^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}),\ t=1,\dots,n\,.

(44)

We illustrate Algorithm
2
in the following example, which is based on
[
33
, Example 18]
:

Example 4
.

Let
n=5
,
h_{0}=x_{1}+x_{2}
,
h_{1}=x_{1}^{5}-x_{3}^{2}
,
h_{2}=x_{2}^{5}-x_{4}^{2}
, and
h_{3}=-x_{1}x_{2}-x_{5}^{2}
be as the input of Algorithm
2
.
Let
h^{\star}
be as in problem (
9
) with
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
Then it is not hard to prove that
h^{\star}=0
.
In Step 1 of Algorithm
2
, we obtain

A=\{V(q^{(1)}),V(q^{(2)})\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\{V(q^{(3)})\}\,,

(45)

where
q^{(1)}:=(x_{5},x_{4},x_{2},x_{1}^{5}-x_{3}^{2})
,
q^{(2)}:=(x_{5},x_{3},x_{2}^{5}-x_{4}^{2},x_{1})
and
q^{(3)}:=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})
.
For
j=1,2
, letting
\bar{q}^{(j)}:=(h_{0},q^{(j)})
gives
V(\bar{q}^{(j)}_{\KKT})=\emptyset
.
Indeed, for any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\nabla h_{0}(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{4}\lambda_{j}\nabla q^{(1)}_{i}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}1-5\lambda_{4}x_{1}^{4}\\
1-\lambda_{3}\\
2\lambda_{4}x_{3}\\
-\lambda_{2}\\
-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,,\\
\\
&\nabla h_{0}(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{4}\lambda_{j}\nabla q^{(2)}_{i}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}1-\lambda_{4}\\
1-5\lambda_{3}x_{2}^{4}\\
-\lambda_{2}\\
2\lambda_{3}x_{4}\\
-\lambda_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,.\end{array}

(46)

Then
\bar{q}^{(1)}_{\KKT}(x)=0
implies that
x_{1}^{5}=x_{3}^{2}
,
1-5\lambda_{4}x_{1}^{4}=2\lambda_{4}x_{3}=0
, which is impossible.
In addition,
\bar{q}^{(2)}_{\KKT}(x)=0

, which is impossible.
For

, since

, it holds that
\rho_{\xi}(h_{0},\bar{q}^{(j)}_{\KKT})=\infty
for sufficient large
\xi\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Since

, we get
\rho_{\xi}(h_{0},q^{(3)})=0
for sufficient large
\xi\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Thus we eventually obtain
T=\{\infty,0\}
, yielding
\bar{h}^{\star}=\min T=0=h^{\star}
.

Remark 10
.

Instead of using semidefinite programming, we can rely entirely on symbolic computations

being the generators of

.

Remark 12
.

The result of Theorem
7
requires the attainability of the infimum value
h^{\star}
.
In
[
35
]
, the first author proves that every polynomial optimization problem of the form (
9
) with finite infimum value
h^{\star}
can be symbolically transformed to an equivalent problem in one-dimensional space with attained optimal value
h^{\star}
.
To do this, he uses quantifier elimination, and algebraic algorithms that rely on the fundamental theorem of algebra and the greatest common divisor.
Let
d
be the upper bound on the degrees of
h_{j}
.
His symbolic algorithm has complexity
O(d^{O(n)})
to produce the objective and constraint polynomials of degree at most
d^{O(n)}
for the equivalent problem.

The following corollary states the equivalence of polynomial optimization problems and semidefinite programs:

Corollary 1
.

Let
h_{0},h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{d}[x]
.
Let
h^{\star}
be as in problem (
9
) with
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
.
Then there is a symbolic algorithm with input
h_{0},\dots,h_{l}
which produces a finite sequence of semidefinite programs with minimum values
(\zeta_{r})_{r=1}^{s}
such that (
9
) is equivalent to the semidefinite program with smallest minimum value
\zeta_{\bar{r}}=\min\limits_{r=1,\dots,s}\zeta_{r}
.

Proof.

If
h^{\star}\in\{-\infty,\infty\}
, it is not hard to construct a semidefinite program with infimum value
h^{\star}
.
If
h^{\star}
is finite but not attained, we can transform problem (
9
) to an equivalent polynomial optimization problem with attained minimum value
h^{\star}
thanks to Remark
12
.
Let us consider the remaining case.
Assume that (
9
) has a global minimizer.
Theorem
7
implies that Algorithm
2
produces finitely many semidefinite programs whose smallest minimum value is exactly
h^{\star}
.
Hence the result follows.
∎

Remark 13
.

The paper (particularly Corollary
1
) provides a theoretical overview of why every general polynomial optimization problem can be solved with a sequence of semidefinite programs obtained recursively.
However, our method should be far from efficient in practice because of the vast bounds (
41
) and (
42
) in Algorithm
2
.
This is unavoidable since we use symbolic computation to obtain the result.
Thus our method cannot be expected to be as efficient as approximation methods.

7
Equivalence of hyperpolic programs and semidefinite programs

A polynomial
f
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
is called
hyperbolic
w.r.t.
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if
f(e)\neq 0
and for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the univariate polynomial
t\mapsto f(te-a)
has only real roots.

Given a polynomial
f
hyperbolic w.r.t.
e\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we define the set

\Lambda_{+}(f,e):=\{a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,f(te-a)=0\Rightarrow t\geq 0\}\,,

(47)

which is called the hyperbolic cone of
f
in direction
e
.

As shown by Gårding in
[
13
]
,
\Lambda_{+}(f,e)
is a basic semi-algebraic set.
We state in the following lemma a weaker result than this statement:

Lemma 16
.

Let
f
be a hyperbolic polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
w.r.t.
e\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then
\Lambda_{+}(f,e)
is the projection of a basic semi-algebraic set defined by polynomials built from
e
and the coefficients of
f
.

Proof.

Let
t
be a single variable. Set
d:=\deg(f)
and
\varphi_{a}(t)=f(te-a)
.
Then
\varphi_{a}
is a polynomial in
t
of degree at most
d
.
Denote by
\varphi_{a}^{(j)}
,
j=0,\dots,d
, the coefficient of
\varphi_{a}
associated with monomial
t^{j}
,
j=0,\dots,d
, respectively.
Then each mapping
a\mapsto\varphi_{a}^{(j)}
is a polynomial in
a
.
Let
a\in\Lambda_{+}(f,e)
.
Then identity polynomial
t
is non-negative on the zero-dimensional algebraic variety
V(\varphi_{a})
.
Assume
V(\varphi_{a})=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\}
for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then it holds that
t_{j}\geq 0
,
j=1,\dots,r
.
Since
\varphi_{a}
has degree at most
d
, we get
r\leq d
.
Letting

p_{j}(t):=\prod\limits_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\scriptstyle i=1\\
\scriptstyle i\neq j\end{array}}^{r}\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{j}-t_{i}}\,,\,j=1,\dots,r\,,

(48)

we obtain
p_{j}(t_{i})=\delta_{ij}
.
Setting
\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{r}t_{j}p_{j}^{2}
gives
\sigma\in\Sigma^{2}_{r-1}[t]
.
Moreover,
t-\sigma
vanishes on
V(\varphi_{a})
.
Since
r\leq d
,
\sigma
has degree at most
2(d-1)
.
It implies that both
\varphi_{a}
and
\sigma
have degrees at most
2d
.
Set
\xi:=\frac{1}{2}\times b(1,2d,2)
and
s:=2\lfloor\xi/(2d)\rfloor
, where
b(\cdot)
is defined as in (
20
).
By Lemma
9
, it holds that

\exists\sigma\in\Sigma^{2}_{d-1}[t]\,:\,-(t-\sigma)^{s}\in\Sigma^{2}_{\xi}[t]+I_{\xi}(\varphi_{a})[t]

(49)

Note that
s
and
\xi
only depend on
d
.
From this, (
49
) is equivalent to “
t\geq 0
on
V(\varphi_{a})
”.
Moreover, (
49
) can be written as

\begin{cases}\exists G_{j}\succeq 0\,,\,\exists u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(\xi-d)+1}\,:\\
-(t-v_{d-1}^{\top}G_{0}v_{d-1})^{s}=v_{\xi}^{\top}G_{1}v_{\xi}+v_{2(\xi-d)}^{\top}u\varphi_{a}\,,\end{cases}

(50)

where
v_{\eta}
is the vector of monomials in
t
up to degree
\eta
.
It not hard to see that (
50
) is equivalent to

\begin{cases}\exists G_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}\,,\,\exists G_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(\xi+1)\times(\xi+1)}\,,\,\exists u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(\xi-d)+1}\,:\\
G_{j}^{\top}=G_{j}\,,\,F(G_{j})\geq 0\,,\,H(G_{0},G_{1},u,\varphi_{a}^{(0)},\dots,\varphi_{a}^{(d)})=0\,,\end{cases}

(51)

where
F
denotes the vector of principal minors of a matrix, and
H
is a vector of polynomials.
Since
\varphi_{a}^{(j)}
is a polynomial in
a
, (
51
) is equivalent to that
a
is in the projection of a basic semi-algebraic set on the coordinates w.r.t.
a
, which yields the result.
∎

We close the paper by stating what would be a consequence of the generalized Lax conjecture (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Remark 1.7]
):

Corollary 2
.

Let
f
be a hyperbolic polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
w.r.t.
e\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Consider the following hyperbolic program:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\inf\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}&c^{\top}x\\
\text{s.t.}&Ax=b\,,\,x\in\Lambda_{+}(f,e)\,,\end{array}

(52)

where
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
c\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are given.
Then there is a symbolic algorithm with input
A,b,c,f,e
which produces a sequence of semidefinite programs with minimum values
(\zeta_{r})_{r=1}^{s}
such that (
52
) is equivalent to the semidefinite program with smallest minimum value
\zeta_{\bar{r}}=\min\limits_{r=1,\dots,s}\zeta_{r}
.

Proof.

Let
h^{\star}
be the infimum value of problem (
52
).
Thanks to Lemma
16
, (
52
) can be written as polynomial optimization problem (
9
) with minimum value
h^{\star}
(see Remark
1
).
Note that the objective and constraint polynomials
h_{0},\dots,h_{l}
are built from
A,b,c,f,e
.
By Corollary
1
, there is a symbolic algorithm with input
h_{0},\dots,h_{l}
which produces a finite sequence of semidefinite programs such that (
9
) is equivalent to a semidefinite program of the smallest minimum value.
Hence the result follows.
∎

Remark 14
.

The framework of Corollary
2
provides a constructive way to obtain an equivalent semidefinite program for the hyperbolic program (
52
) with given
A,b,c,f,e
.
However, it has yet to easily be seen that the result of Corollary
2
provides a solution to the generalized Lax conjecture.
It is because the equivalence of hyperbolic and semidefinite programs might not directly imply the equality of their feasible sets.
