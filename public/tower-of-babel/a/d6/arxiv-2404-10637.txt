On Homomorphism Indistinguishability and Hypertree Depth
Benjamin Scheidt

Abstract
GC k \textsf{GC}^{k} is a logic introduced by Scheidt and Schweikardt (2023) to express properties of hypergraphs It is similar to first-order logic with counting quantifiers ( C ) adapted to the hypergraph setting. It has distinct sets of variables for vertices and for hyperedges and requires vertex variables to be guarded by hyperedge variables on every quantification. We prove that two hypergraphs G G , H H satisfy the same sentences in the logic GC k \textsf{GC}^{k} with guard depth at most k k if, and only if, they are homomorphism indistinguishable over the class of hypergraphs of strict hypertree depth at most k k . This lifts the analogous result for tree depth ≤ k \leq k and sentences of first-order logic with counting quantifiers of quantifier rank at most k k due to Grohe (2020) from graphs to hypergraphs. The guard depth of a formula is the quantifier rank with respect to hyperedge variables, and strict hypertree depth is a restriction of hypertree depth as defined by Adler, Gavenčiak and Klimošová (2012). To justify this restriction, we show that for every H H , the strict hypertree depth of H H is at most 1 larger than its hypertree depth, and we give additional evidence that strict hypertree depth can be viewed as a reasonable generalisation of tree depth for hypergraphs.

Introduction

The (
k
-dimensional) Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm describes a technique to classify the vertices (or
k
-tuples) of a graph, by iteratively computing a colouring (i.e., a classification) of the vertices (or
k
-tuples), which gets refined each iteration until it stabilises.
While it can be used as a way to (imperfectly) test graphs for isomorphism, it has found many other – seemingly very different – uses, e.g. reducing the cost of solving linear programs
[
14
]
, as graph kernels
[
28
]
or even as an architecture for graph neural networks
[
29
,
22
,
13
,
12
]
.
For a more in-depth overview on the expressive power of the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm itself, consult
[
17
]
as a starting point.
The success of the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm can in part be explained by its simplicity, but also by the fact that it appears to capture the structure of a graph really well.
This can be explained by its connection to first-order logic with counting quantifiers and to homomorphism counts over graphs of bounded tree width.
A classical result due to Cai, Fürer and Immerman
[
5
]
and Immerman and Lander
[
16
]
says that two graphs are indistinguishable by the
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm if, and only if, they satisfy the same sentences of first-order logic with counting quantifiers (
C
) and
k{+}1
variables (
\textsf{C}^{k{+}1}
).

Dvořák
[
9
]
and Dell, Grohe, Rattan
[
8
]
related the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm to homomorphism counts over graphs of bounded tree width (this was subsequently generalised to relational structures of bounded tree width by Butti and Dalmau
[
3
]
).
They showed that two graphs are homomorphism indistinguishable over the class
\mathsf{TW}_{k}
of graphs of tree width at most
k
if, and only if, they are indistinguishable by the
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm.
Here, two graphs
G
and
H
are
homomorphism indistinguishable
over a class
\mathfrak{C}
of graphs, if the number of homomorphisms from
F
into
G
equals the number of homomorphisms from
F
into
H
for all
F\in\mathfrak{C}
.
Dvořák used the previously mentioned connection to
\textsf{C}^{k{+}1}
and an inductive characterisation of the graphs of bounded tree width to prove this result, while Dell et al. used elaborate algebraic techniques on vectors containing homomorphism counts.

In recent years, a whole theory has emerged around homomorphism indistinguishability.
There are characterisations of homomorphism indistinguishability for classes of graphs other than
\mathsf{TW}_{k}
(cf.
[
7
,
10
,
21
,
25
]
), among which we would like to emphasise the following: A classical result by Lovász
[
18
]
, stating that two graphs are isomorphic if, and only if, they are homomorphism indistinguishable over all graphs; a well-received result by Mančinska and Roberson
[
20
]
, stating that two graphs are homomorphism indistinguishable over the class of planar graphs if, and only if, they are quantum isomorphic; and, of importance for this paper, Grohe
[
11
]
showed that two graphs are homomorphism indistinguishable over the graphs of tree depth at most
m
if, and only if, they satisfy the same sentences of
C
with quantifier rank at most
m
(
\textsf{C}_{m}
).
There is also work concerned with a more fundamental analysis of homomorphism counting from restricted classes (cf.
[
2
,
15
,
23
,
24
,
27
]
).

Some real-world problems can be represented by hypergraphs in a much more natural way than by graphs.
The great track record of the Weisfeiler-Leman method poses the question, whether a similar algorithm exists that works on
hypergraphs
.
A direct application of the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm on the incidence structure of a hypergraph is sometimes used.
But Böker noted in
[
4
]
, that this approach does not capture the hypergraph structure well, since the algorithm will mix up hyperedges and vertices.
Thus, a proper variant of the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm that works on hypergraphs is, to the best of our knowledge, still missing.
We believe that establishing results analogous to the ones mentioned so far can give valuable insight on how the algorithm should operate on hypergraphs.
A first step from this angle is a result by Scheidt and Schweikardt
[
26
]
, who lift Dvořák’s result to hypergraphs by proving the following: two hypergraphs
G
,
H
are homomorphism indistinguishable over the class
\mathsf{GHW}_{k}
of hypergraphs of generalised hypertree width at most
k
if, and only if, they satisfy the same sentences of the logic
\textsf{GC}^{k}
.

\textsf{GC}^{k}
is a novel logic introduced in
[
26
]
.
It has distinct variables for vertices and for hyperedges and counting quantifiers for both variable types.
The main feature of
\textsf{GC}^{k}
is that it bounds the number of variables for hyperedges by
k
, and it requires that vertex variables are
“guarded by”
(i.e., contained in) hyperedge variables on every quantification.

Contributions

As the main contribution of this work, we show that two hypergraphs satisfy the same sentences of the logic
\textsf{GC}^{k}
with guard depth at most
k
if, and only if, they are homomorphism indistinguishable over the class of hypergraphs of strict hypertree depth at most
k
(
Theorem

20
).
The guard depth is the quantifier depth of the hyperedge variables.
This theorem follows from an inductive characterisation of the class of hypergraphs of strict hypertree depth
\leq k
, combined with the main technical lemmas of Scheidt and Schweikardt
[
26
]
.
We believe that this inductive characterisation is interesting on its own, since the same technique combined with the core lemmata in Dvořák’s work
[
9
]
can be used to give a concise proof of the analogous result on graphs due to Grohe
[
11
]
, which was independently recognised and shown by Fluck et al.
[
10
]
recently.
Strict hypertree depth is a mild restriction of hypertree depth as defined by Adler, Gavenčiak and Klimošová
[
1
]
.
This (as it turns out only slight) deviation from hypertree depth is surprising at first.
Because of the properties and relations between strict hypertree depth and hypertree depth we acquire in this paper, we claim that strict hypertree depth can be viewed as a reasonable generalisation of tree depth for hypergraphs too.
In particular, we show that the strict hypertree depth of a hypergraph is at most 1 larger than its hypertree depth (
Theorem

5
).
Moreover, we show that the distinguishing power of homomorphism counts from hypergraphs of hypertree depth at most
k
is different from the distinguishing power of homomorphism counts from their respective incidence graphs (
Theorem

9
).
Compared to other hypergraph parameters, this is very unexpected.

Since we heavily rely on the work by Scheidt and Schweikardt

We denote the set of natural numbers
including
0 by
{\rm Nature}
, the set of
positive
natural numbers by
{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}
, and we write
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
To denote isomorphism of two objects, we use
\cong
.
A tuple is denoted using a bar, e.g.
\overline{a}
.
For a given
\ell
-tuple
\overline{a}
, we use
a_{i}
to denote the
i
-th element of
\overline{a}
, i.e.,
\overline{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\ell})
.
For any set
S
, let
\mathcal{P}(S)
be the set of subsets of
S
and let
\mathcal{P}_{k}(S)
be the subsets of cardinality exactly
k
.
If
S
is a set of sets, let
\bigcup S=\bigcup_{s\in S}s
.

For a finite set
S
of cardinality
\ell\in{\rm Nature}
, a total order
<
on
S
and any number
d\in{\rm Nature}
, we say that
\langle i_{d{+}1},i_{d{+}2}\dots,i_{d{+}\ell}\rangle
is the
<
-enumeration of
S
, if
i_{d{+}1}<i_{d{+}2}<\cdots<i_{d{+}\ell}
and
\{i_{d{+1}},\dots,i_{d{+}\ell}\}=S
.
If the order
<
is clear from the context, we simply say that
\langle i_{d{+}1},i_{d{+}2}\dots,i_{d{+}\ell}\rangle
is the enumeration of
S
.
Note that we usually let

, i.e., we usually write
\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\rangle
.
Furthermore, the enumeration
\langle i_{d{+}1},i_{d{+}2}\dots,i_{d{+}\ell}\rangle
of
S
is empty if, and only if,
S
is empty.

We denote a partial function
f
from
A
to
B
by
f\colon A\rightharpoonup B
, and we let
\operatorname{dom}(f)\coloneqq\{a\in A\,:\,f(a)\text{ is defined}\}
and
\operatorname{img}(f)\coloneqq\{b\in B\,:\,\text{ex. }a\in A\text{ s.t.\ }f(a)=b\}
.
We say that two functions
f
and
g
are
compatible
, if
f(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{dom}(g)
.
We identify a (partial) function
f
with the set
\{(x,f(x))\,:\,x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\}
whenever we are using set notation on functions.
For example, we write
f\subseteq g
to indicate
\operatorname{dom}(f)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(g)
and
f(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
.
In particular, by
f\cup g
we denote the function
h
with
\operatorname{dom}(h)=\operatorname{dom}(f)\cup\operatorname{dom}(g)
and
h(x)=f(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
and
h(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(g)\setminus\operatorname{dom}(f)
.
Note that
f
has
precedence
over
g
, but this only matters if
f
and
g
are not compatible.
For a (partial) function
f
and a set
S\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f)
we write
f(S)
to denote
\{f(x)\,:\,x\in S\}
, and we call the function
g\subseteq f
with
\operatorname{dom}(g)\coloneqq S
the
restriction of
f
to
S
.
Finally, we define partial functions inline like this:
\{a\to 3,b\to 2,c\to 5\}
.
In particular, the empty set
\varnothing
denotes a partial function with empty domain.

Graphs, Trees and Forests

An (undirected) graph is a tuple
G=(V(G),E(G))
, where
V(G)
is a finite set and
E(G)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{2}(V(G))
.
For a set
S\subseteq V(G)
,
{G}[{S}]
denotes the subgraph induced by
S
, i.e.,
V({G}[{S}])\coloneqq S
and
E({G}[{S}])\coloneqq E(G)\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(S)
.
A connected component of a graph is a maximal induced subgraph that is connected.
A tree is a connected acyclic graph and a forest is a graph were each connected component is a tree.
A rooted tree
T
is a tree with some distinguished node that we call its
root
, which we denote by
\omega_{T}
.
A rooted forest
F
is the disjoint union of a collection of rooted trees.
It therefore has a set of roots denoted by
\operatorname{\Omega}_{F}
.
We may omit the index if it is clear from the context.
Note that a rooted tree is also a rooted forest and that every node
n
in a rooted forest is contained in a unique connected component which is a tree that we call the
tree for
n
and whose root is the
root for
n
.

For a rooted forest
F
we let
\leq_{F}
be the induced partial order on the nodes, i.e., the roots are the minimal elements and
s\leq_{F}t
if
s
is on the unique path from
t
to its root in
\Omega
.
By
\operatorname*{P}(s,t)
we denote the set of nodes on the path from
s
to
t
(including
s
and
t
).
In particular, if no path from
s
to
t
exists,
\operatorname*{P}(s,t)=\varnothing
.
By
\operatorname*{P}(s)
we denote the set of nodes on the path from
s
to the root for
s
and by
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)
we denote the unique element, if it exists, where the paths
\operatorname*{P}(s)
,
\operatorname*{P}(t)
join, i.e.,
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)\coloneqq\max_{\leq_{F}}(\operatorname*{P}(s)\cap\operatorname*{P}(t))
.
Notice that
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)
is undefined iff
s
and
t
are not in the same tree, and that
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)=s
, iff
s\leq_{F}t
(and conversely,
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)=t
iff
t\leq_{F}s
).

The subtree
T_{t}
induced by
t\in V(F)
is the tree
{F}[{V}]
with root
t
and
V\coloneqq\{s\in V(T)\,:\,t\leq_{F}s\}
.
The
level
of a node
s\in V(F)
is defined as the number of elements on the path from
s
to its root, i.e.,
\operatorname{level}(s)\coloneqq|\operatorname*{P}(s)|
.
The
height
of a rooted tree
T
is the maximal level, i.e.,
0pt(T)\coloneqq\max\{\operatorname{level}(t)\,:\,t\in V(T)\}
and the height of a node
t\in V(T)
is the height of its induced subtree
T_{t}
, i.e.,
0pt(t)\coloneqq 0pt(T_{t})
.

Hyper- and Incidence Graphs

A
hypergraph
is a tuple
\mathcal{H}=(V,E,\beta)
, where
V
and
E
are disjoint finite sets and
\beta
is a total function from
E
to
\mathcal{P}(V)
with
V=\bigcup_{e\in E}\beta(e)
.
We call the elements of
V

vertices
and the elements of
E

hyperedges
and for every
e\in E
, we call
\beta(e)
its
contents
.
We denote
V
by
V(\mathcal{H})
,
E
by
E(\mathcal{H})
and
\beta
by
\beta_{\mathcal{H}}
, though we may omit the index if there is no ambiguity.
Notice that, in general, multiple hyperedges with the same content and hyperedges without content are allowed.
We call
\mathcal{H}

simple
if
\beta
is injective.

An
incidence graph
is a tuple
I=(R,B,E)
consisting of two disjoint finite sets
R
and
B
of
red
and
blue
vertices and a relation
E\subseteq B\times R
.
We denote
R
by
\mathcal{R}(I)
,
B
by
\mathcal{B}(I)
and
E
by
E(I)
.
For every
e\in\mathcal{B}(I)
, we let
\operatorname{\upbeta}(e)\coloneqq\{v\in\mathcal{R}(I)\,:\,(e,v)\in E(I)\}
.
Notice that
\operatorname{\upbeta}
is equivalent in its function to the map
\beta
for a hypergraph, hence we denote them similarly.
We only consider incidence graphs where for every
v\in\mathcal{R}(I)
there is an
e\in\mathcal{B}(I)
such that
(e,v)\in E(I)
.

It is easy to see that we can assign an incidence graph to every hypergraph and the other way around: For every hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
we let
I_{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq(V(\mathcal{H}),E(\mathcal{H}),E)
where
E\coloneqq\{(e,v)\,:\,e\in E(\mathcal{H}),v\in\beta(e)\}
.
Conversely, for every incidence graph
I
we let
\mathcal{H}_{I}\coloneqq(\mathcal{R}(I),\mathcal{B}(I),\beta)
where
\beta(e)\coloneqq\{v\in\mathcal{R}(I)\,:\,(e,v)\in E(I)\}
for all
e\in\mathcal{B}(I)
.

For every set
S\subseteq E(\mathcal{H})
we define the
induced subhypergraph

{\mathcal{H}}[{S}]
as
(V^{\prime},S,\beta_{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime})
where
V^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{e\in S}\beta(e)
and
\beta_{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is the restriction of
\beta_{\mathcal{H}}
to
S
.
We say that a hypergraph is connected if its incidence graph is connected.
An induced subhypergraph is a connected component, if its corresponding incidence graph is a connected component.

By
\mathcal{P}_{n}
we denote the path of
n
hyperedges, where each hyperedge contains 2 vertices.
I.e., we let
V(\mathcal{P}_{n})=[n{+}1]
,
E(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\{e_{i}\,:\,i\in[n]\}
and
\beta(e_{i})=\{i,i{+}1\}
for all
i\in[n]
.
We may use different names for the vertices if it is convenient.

2.1
Hypertree Depth

The following definition of
elimination forest
and
hypertree depth
is due to Adler, Gavenčiak and Klimošová
[
1
]
, though they refer to elimination forests as
“decomposition forests”
.
We call them elimination forests, since this reflects their conceptual similarity to elimination forests for graphs and avoids confusion with hypertree decompositions.
Further, we define this notion in terms of incidence graphs, because we mainly work on those.
Do notice however, that this definition easily translates to hypergraphs and that it is equivalent to the one given by Adler, Gavenčiak and Klimošová.

Definition 2

(Hypertree Depth and Elimination Forests,
[
1
]
)
.

Let
I
be an incidence graph.
An
elimination forest

(F,\Gamma)
for
I
consists of a forest
F
and a mapping
\Gamma\colon V(F)\to\mathcal{B}(I)
such that
Items

1
,
2
and
3
hold.
We write
\widehat{\Gamma}(t)
as shorthand for
\operatorname{\upbeta}(\Gamma(t))
.

1.

Completeness for vertices
:
For every red vertex
v\in\mathcal{R}(I)
, there is a
t\in V(F)
such that
v\in\widehat{\Gamma}(t)
.

2.

Hyperedge-Containment
:
For every blue vertex
e\in\mathcal{B}(I)
there are nodes
s,t\in V(F)
such that
s\leq_{F}t
and
\operatorname{\upbeta}(e)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}(\operatorname*{P}(s,t))
.

3.

Shared heritage
:
For all
s,t\in V(F)
, if
\widehat{\Gamma}(s)\cap\widehat{\Gamma}(t)\neq\varnothing
, then
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)
is defined and
\widehat{\Gamma}(s)\cap\widehat{\Gamma}(t)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}(\operatorname*{P}(\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)))
.

The intuition behind
Item

3
is that hyperedges can only share the vertices contained in their common ancestors in the elimination forest.
The height of an elimination forest
(F,\Gamma)
is simply the height of
F
.
The
hypertree depth of
I
is defined as the minimal height over all elimination forests for
I
, and we denote it by
\operatorname{hd}(I)
.
Analogously, we let
\operatorname{hd}(\mathcal{H})\coloneqq\operatorname{hd}(I_{\mathcal{H}})
for all hypergraphs.
We write
\mathsf{IHD}_{k}
to denote the class of incidence graphs of hypertree depth at most
k
and
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
to denote the corresponding class of hypergraphs.

We call an elimination forest
(F,\Gamma)

strict
if
\Gamma
is bijective.
It is easy to see that the first and second condition are trivially satisfied when
\Gamma
is bijective, thus we can redefine the notion of strict elimination forest in a more succinct manner.

Definition 3

(Strict Elimination Forest)
.

Let
I
be an incidence graph.
A
strict elimination forest

(F,\Gamma)
for
I
consists of a forest
F
and a
bijective
function
\Gamma\colon V(F)\to\mathcal{B}(I)
satisfying
Item

3
of
Definition

2
.
The
strict hypertree depth of
I
, denoted by
\operatorname{shd}(I)
, is defined as the minimal height over all strict elimination forests for
I
.
Again, we let
\operatorname{shd}(\mathcal{H})\coloneqq\operatorname{shd}(I_{\mathcal{H}})
for every hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
.
We write
\mathsf{ISHD}_{k}
to denote the class of incidence graphs of strict hypertree depth at most
k
and
\mathsf{SHD}_{k}
to denote the corresponding class of hypergraphs.

One can show that the strict hypertree depth of a hypergraph is at most its hypertree depth increased by one.
The main idea is that we can turn every elimination forest into a strict one, if we add a leaf for every hyperedge that is currently not being mapped to below the path that contains it according to

2
in

Theorem 5
.

For all hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
,

\operatorname{hd}(\mathcal{H})\;\leq\;\operatorname{shd}(\mathcal{H})\;\leq\;\operatorname{hd}(\mathcal{H}){+}1
.

Proof.

Let
I
be an incidence graph.
Since every strict elimination forest is also an elimination forest,
\operatorname{hd}(I)\leq\operatorname{shd}(I)
trivially holds.

To show
\operatorname{shd}(I)\leq\operatorname{hd}(I)+1
it suffices to show that any elimination forest
(F,\Gamma)
of

can be turned into a strict elimination forest
(F_{\text{s}},\Gamma_{\text{s}})
of
I
where
0pt(F_{\text{s}})\leq 0pt(F)+1
.
In the following we make
\Gamma
bijective by inserting new nodes into
F
as children of leaves.
Thus, at the end, the height of
F_{\text{s}}
has increased by at most 1.

But first we show that we may assume that
\Gamma
is already injective.
If it is not, then there are distinct
s,t\in V(F)
such that
\Gamma(s)=\Gamma(t)
and w.l.o.g.
\operatorname{level}(s)\geq\operatorname{level}(t)
and
s
is not the root.
Let
S\coloneqq\widehat{\Gamma}(s)\cap\widehat{\Gamma}(t)
(
=\widehat{\Gamma}(s)=\widehat{\Gamma}(t)
).
Since
(F,\Gamma)
is an elimination forest,
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)
must be defined,
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)\neq s
and

must hold according to
Item

3
.
If we remove the node

from

by contracting the edge between
s
and its parent
p
(which exists since

can not be the root) and assign
\Gamma(p)
to the resulting node, the result

is still an elimination forest.

Item

1

\widehat{\Gamma}(s)=\widehat{\Gamma}(t)
.
It can easily be verified that

2

e\in\mathcal{B}(I)
, let

such that
u\leq_{F}v
and
\operatorname{\upbeta}(e)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}(\operatorname*{P}(u,v))
.
Obviously,
\operatorname{\upbeta}(e)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}(\operatorname*{P}(u,\omega))
where
\omega
is the root for

.
Since
\widehat{\Gamma}(s)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}(\operatorname*{\land}(s,t))
and
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)\in\operatorname*{P}(s,\omega)\setminus\{s\}
,
\operatorname*{P}(u,\omega)\setminus\{s\}
is still a path in

and
\operatorname{\upbeta}(e)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\operatorname*{P}(u,\omega)\setminus\{s\})
.
Showing that

3
is still satisfied is not hard either:
Let
u,v\in V(F)
such that
\widehat{\Gamma}(u)\cap\widehat{\Gamma}(v)
is not empty.
If
\operatorname*{\land}(u,v)\leq_{F}s
or
\operatorname*{\land}(u,v)
is not
\leq_{F}
-comparable to

, then

obviously still holds.
If

, then
s\in\operatorname*{P}(\operatorname*{\land}(u,v))
and
\operatorname*{\land}(s,t)\in\operatorname*{P}(\operatorname*{\land}(u,v))
.
Using the same argument as before we can show that
\widehat{\Gamma}^{\prime}(u)\cap\widehat{\Gamma}^{\prime}(v)\subseteq\bigcup\widehat{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\operatorname*{P}(\operatorname*{\land}(u,v)))
still holds.

In total, we get that
(F^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})
is still an elimination forest and
0pt(F^{\prime})\leq 0pt(F)
.
We can repeat this procedure until the resulting
\Gamma^{\prime}
is injective.

Assume that
\Gamma
is already injective.
If
\Gamma
is surjective,
\Gamma
is also bijective.
Otherwise, let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{\ell}\}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}(I)\setminus\operatorname{img}(\Gamma)
be the set of blue vertices that are not being mapped to.
Since
(F,\Gamma)
is an elimination forest, there exist

for every

such that

and

and where

is minimal.
We insert a fresh node

as a child of

for every

and map

to

.
In precise terms, we let

for all

Obviously, the height of the tree

Since

is trivially satisfied.
Since

is bijective,

is trivially satisfied as well.

Let
u,v\in V(F_{\text{s}})

If

then this still holds since

Thus assume w.l.o.g. that

for some

2.2
Homomorphisms

While hypergraphs and incidence graphs are conceptually close, their
“natural”
notions of homomorphisms are not the same.
Since our interest lies in hypergraphs, but we are mainly working on incidence graphs in this paper, we have to relate these notions.
Following Scheidt and Schweikardt, we use the same definitions as Böker
[
4
]
.

A homomorphism from a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
into another hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
is a pair of functions
(h_{V}\colon V(\mathcal{H})\to V(\mathcal{G}),h_{E}\colon E(\mathcal{H})\to E(\mathcal{G}))
such that for every
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
the equality
h_{V}(\beta(e))=\beta(h_{E}(e))
holds.

A homomorphism from an incidence graph
I
into another incidence graph
J
is a pair of mappings
(h_{V}\colon\mathcal{R}(I)\to\mathcal{R}(J),h_{E}\colon\mathcal{B}(I)\to\mathcal{B}(J))
such that
(h_{E}(e),h_{V}(v))\in E(J)
holds for every edge
(e,v)\in E(I)
.
This is equivalent to the requirement
h_{V}(\operatorname{\upbeta}(e))\subseteq\operatorname{\upbeta}(h_{E}(e))
.
Thus, the equality that we require for a hypergraph homomorphism is relaxed to an inclusion for incidence graphs.

Let
A
,
B
and
\mathfrak{C}
be two hypergraphs and a class of hypergraphs or two incidence graphs and a class of incidence graphs.
We denote the number of homomorphisms from
A
to
B
by
\hom(A,B)
, and we let
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C},A)
be the
“vector”
that has a row for every
F\in\mathfrak{C}
containing
\hom(F,A)
.
We say that
A
and
B
are
homomorphism indistinguishable
over
\mathfrak{C}
(
A\equiv_{\mathfrak{C}}B
), if
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C},A)=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C},B)
, i.e., if
\hom(F,A)=\hom(F,B)
for all
F\in\mathfrak{C}
.

The following crucial theorem relates homomorphism indistinguishability over a class of hypergraphs to homomorphism indistinguishability over the corresponding class
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{I}}
of incidence graphs.
As noted in
[
26
]
, this theorem is implicit in
[
4
]
, consult Appendix A of the full version of
[
26
]
for details.

Theorem 7

(
[
4
,
26
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a class of hypergraphs and let
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{I}}
be its corresponding class of incidence graphs.
If
\mathfrak{C}
is closed under
pumping
and
local merging
, then
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C},\mathcal{G})=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C},\mathcal{H})
if, and only if,

\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{I}},I_{\mathcal{G}})=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{I}},I_{\mathcal{H}})
for all hypergraphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
.

\mathfrak{C}
is closed under pumping, if
H^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}
for every
H\in\mathfrak{C}
, where
H^{\prime}
is created from
H
by inserting a new vertex into
one
arbitrary hyperedge of
H
; and closed under local merging, if
H^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}
for every
H\in\mathfrak{C}
, where
H^{\prime}
is created from
H
by choosing an arbitrary hyperedge
e
and then merging two vertices
u,v
that are both contained in
e
.

It is easy to see that
\mathsf{SHD}_{k}
is closed under both pumping and local merging, whereas
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
is only closed under local merging.
See
Appendix

A
for details.

Proposition 8
.

Let
k\in{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}
.
The class
\mathsf{SHD}_{k}
is closed under pumping and local merging, the class
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
is closed under local merging but
not
under pumping.

The following theorem shows that homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
is not equal to homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathsf{SHD}_{k}
.
Because
\mathsf{SHD}_{k}\subseteq\mathsf{HD}_{k}
, counting homomorphisms from
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
is more powerful in the sense that it distinguishes more hypergraphs.
But we also show that it is unequal to homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathsf{IHD}_{k}
, which is unexpected.
Since this prohibits us from relating
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
to any fragment of
GC

Thus, we argue that the notion of strict hypertree depth can be viewed as a reasonable generalisation of tree depth, especially when we recall that
\mathsf{HD}_{k-1}\subseteq\mathsf{SHD}_{k}\subseteq\mathsf{HD}_{k}

3
k-Labeled Incidence Graphs

Our goal is to give an inductive characterisation of the incidence graphs of strict hypertree depth at most
k
(and thus also of hypergraphs of strict hypertree depth at most
k
).
The concepts presented in this section were first defined in
[
26
]
, and we adopt their notation and phrasing for the most part.
Note that the
k
-labeled incidence graphs defined here are inspired by the concept of
k
-labeled graphs as they are used in
[
6
,
9
,
10
,
19
]
and elsewhere.
In particular,
k
-labeled graphs are the main tool used by Dvořák
[
9
]
to prove his result.
In principle, a
k
-labeled incidence graph
is an incidence graphs that has
labels
attached to some of its red and blue vertices.
We have an unbounded number of red labels that can be attached to red vertices (though the number of labels actually used must always be finite), but we only have
k
labels that we can attach to blue vertices.
We are allowed to attach multiple labels to the same vertex, but we are not required to use all of them.
Every red label has an assigned
“guard”
, which is a blue vertex with a label on it.
In practice, we will require every red labeled vertex to be a neighbour of its guard (i.e., we want it to have a
real
guard, as defined in the next paragraphs), though it makes the proofs easier if we do not enforce this in the definition itself.
This idea is formalised as follows.

A
k
-labeled incidence graph is a tuple
L=(I,r,b,g)
, where
I
is an incidence graph,
r\colon{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{R}(I)
,
b\colon[k]\rightharpoonup\mathcal{B}(I)
and
g\colon{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}\rightharpoonup[k]
are partial mappings such that
\operatorname{dom}(r)
is finite and
\operatorname{dom}(g)=\operatorname{dom}(r)
.
We use
I_{L}
,
r_{L}
, …to denote the components of
L
.
But to keep the indices from getting overly complicated, we may write
I^{\prime},r^{\prime},\dots
and
I_{i},r_{i},\dots
instead of
I_{L^{\prime}},r_{L^{\prime}}
and
I_{L_{i}},r_{L_{i}},\dots
, respectively.
If it is clear from the context, we may omit the index altogether and simply write
I,r,b,g
.

We say that
L

has real guards (w.r.t.
g
)
, if for every
i\in\operatorname{dom}(r)
we have
g(i)\in\operatorname{dom}(b)
and
(b(g(i)),r(i))\in E(I)
.
A
k
-labeled incidence graph
L
is
label-free
if
r_{L}=b_{L}=g_{L}=\varnothing
.
We call
I
the
skeleton
of
L
.
Next, we define some operations on
k
-labeled incidence graphs.
For completeness, mathematically precise definitions can be found in
Appendix

C
.

For any set
X_{r}\subseteq{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}
of finite size
\ell
and any tuple
\overline{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\ell})\in{\mathcal{R}(I_{L})}^{n}
we write
L[X_{r}{\to}\overline{v}]
to denote a copy of
L
where we modified
r
such that
r(i_{j})=v_{j}
for all
j
along the enumeration
\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\rangle
of
X_{r}
, i.e., we introduce, and change the placement of, some red labels.
Similarly, for any
X_{b}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}\subseteq[k]
and any
\overline{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{\ell})\in{\mathcal{B}(I_{L})}^{\ell}
we write
L\langle X_{b}{\to}\overline{e}\rangle
to denote a copy of
L
where we modified
b
accordingly.
We write
L[X_{r}{\to}\bullet]
(
L\langle X_{b}{\to}\bullet\rangle
) to denote a copy of
L
where we
removed
the red (blue) labels in
X_{r}
(
X_{b}
).
Note, that we remove
just
the labels and
not
the vertices that carry them.

Definition

30
in
Appendix

C
gives mathematically precise definitions.

Intuitively, the
“product”

(L_{1}\cdot L_{2})
or
glueing
of two
k
-labeled incidence graphs
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
is the
k
-labeled incidence graph
L
that is created by first taking the disjoint union of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, followed by repeatedly merging pairs of red (blue) vertices, that carry a shared red (blue) label.
By merging we mean that we replace these vertices by a single fresh vertex, which inherits their neighbourhoods and labels.
We apply this procedure until there are no more such pairs.
The guard function of
(L_{1}\cdot L_{2})
is simply
g_{L_{1}}\cup g_{L_{2}}
, i.e., in theory,
g_{L_{1}}
has precedence over
g_{L_{2}}
.
In practice, we will require that
g_{L_{1}}
and
g_{L_{2}}
are compatible, which means the precedence of
g_{L_{1}}
will be irrelevant.
Note that the order in which we merge vertices does not matter, and that if a vertex carries two or more labels, all vertices carrying any one of these labels will be replaced by a single fresh vertex that carries
all
those labels and inherits
all
neighbourhoods.
Finally, for
i\in[2]
we define mappings
\operatorname{succ}^{R}_{L_{i}}\colon\mathcal{R}(I_{L_{i}})\to\mathcal{R}(I_{L})
and
\operatorname{succ}^{B}_{L_{i}}\colon\mathcal{B}(I_{L_{i}})\to\mathcal{B}(I_{L})
such that
\operatorname{succ}^{R}_{L_{i}}(v)
is the red vertex of

5
The Logic GC
k

This section introduces the logic
\textsf{GC}^{k}
as defined in
[
26
]
and its restricted fragment
\textsf{GC}_{k}
, consisting of all formulas of guard depth at most
k
.
Let
k
be a positive natural number, that is fixed for this section.

Variables

\textsf{GC}^{k}
uses two different kinds of variables:
\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{v}}\coloneqq\{\mathtt{v}_{1},\mathtt{v}_{2},\mathtt{v}_{3}\dots\}
to address vertices and
\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{e}}\coloneqq\{\mathtt{e}_{1},\mathtt{e}_{2},\dots,\mathtt{e}_{k}\}
to address hyperedges.
Notice that the number of variables for hyperedges is bounded by
k
, but unbounded for vertices.
We say that a tuple of the form
\overline{\mathtt{v}}=(\mathtt{v}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathtt{v}_{i_{\ell}})\in\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{v}}^{\ell}
or
\overline{\mathtt{e}}=(\mathtt{e}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathtt{e}_{i_{\ell}})\in\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{e}}^{\ell}
is a
\mathtt{v}
- or
\mathtt{e}
-tuple
, if
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{\ell}
.
We let
\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{v}})\coloneqq\{\mathtt{v}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathtt{v}_{i_{\ell}}\}
and
\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{e}})\coloneqq\{\mathtt{e}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathtt{e}_{i_{\ell}}\}
respectively.
We call
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}
the
index set
of
\overline{v}
and
\overline{e}
, respectively.

Logical Guards

The key idea behind
\textsf{GC}^{k}
is that on quantification, vertex variables must be
guarded
by hyperedge variables.
This is formalised by a partial function
g\colon{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}\rightharpoonup[k]
with finite domain (similar to the guard function of a
k
-labeled incidence graph, cf.
Section

3
) and its corresponding
logical guard

\Delta_{g}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{i\in\operatorname{dom}(g)}E(\mathtt{e}_{g(i)},\mathtt{v}_{i})
.
For the special partial function
g
with empty domain, we let
\Delta_{g}\coloneqq\top
, which is a special formula that always evaluates to true.

Definition 16
.

The logic
\textsf{GC}^{k}
is inductively defined along with the
free vertex variables
, the
free hyperedge variables
and the
guard depth
, as formalised by the functions

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}\colon\textsf{GC}^{k}\to\mathcal{P}(\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{v}})\,,\quad\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}\colon\textsf{GC}^{k}\to\mathcal{P}(\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{e}})\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname*{gd}\colon\textsf{GC}^{k}\to{\rm Nature}.

Atomic Formulas

For all
i,i^{\prime}\in{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}
and all
j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
the following formulas are in
\textsf{GC}^{k}
:

•

\varphi\,=\,\mathtt{v}_{i}\kern 1.0pt{=}\kern 1.0pt\mathtt{v}_{i^{\prime}}

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{\mathtt{v}_{i},\mathtt{v}_{i^{\prime}}\}

and

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\varnothing
;

•

\varphi\,=\,\mathtt{e}_{j}\kern 1.0pt{=}\kern 1.0pt\mathtt{e}_{j^{\prime}}

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\varnothing

and

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{\mathtt{e}_{j},\mathtt{e}_{j^{\prime}}\}
;

•

\varphi\,=\,E(\mathtt{e}_{j},\mathtt{v}_{i})

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{\mathtt{v}_{i}\}

and

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{\mathtt{e}_{j}\}
.

In all the above cases,
\operatorname*{gd}(\varphi)\coloneqq 0
.

Inductive Rules

Let
\chi,\psi
be formulas of
\textsf{GC}^{k}
.
The following formulas are in
\textsf{GC}^{k}
.

•

\varphi\,=\,\lnot\chi

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\chi)

and

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\chi)
,

and
\operatorname*{gd}(\varphi)\coloneqq\operatorname*{gd}(\chi)
;

•

\varphi\,=\,(\chi\land\psi)

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\chi)\cup\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\psi)

and
\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\chi)\cup\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\psi)
,

and
\operatorname*{gd}(\varphi)\coloneqq\max\{\,\operatorname*{gd}(\chi),\,\operatorname*{gd}(\psi)\,\}
.

Note that by the rules defined so far,
\operatorname*{gd}(\Delta_{g})=0
for all logical guards
\Delta_{g}
.

We say that
g\colon{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}\rightharpoonup[k]
is a guard function for
\varphi
if
\operatorname{dom}(g)=\{i\,:\,\mathtt{v}_{i}\in\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\}
.

Let
n\in{\rm Nature}_{\geq{}1}
, let
g
be a guard function for
\psi
and
\chi=(\Delta_{g}\land\psi)
.
The following formulas are in
\textsf{GC}^{k}
for every
\mathtt{v}
-tuple
\overline{\mathtt{v}}
with
\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{v}})\subseteq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\chi)
and every
\mathtt{e}
-tuple
\overline{\mathtt{e}}
with
\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{e}})\subseteq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\chi)
:

•

\varphi\,=\,\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}n}\overline{\mathtt{v}}\mathbin{.}\chi

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\chi)\setminus\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{v}})

and

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\chi)
,

and
\operatorname*{gd}(\varphi)\coloneqq\operatorname*{gd}(\chi)
;

•

\varphi\,=\,\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}n}\overline{\mathtt{e}}\mathbin{.}\chi

with

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\chi)

and

\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\chi)\setminus\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{e}})
,

and
\operatorname*{gd}(\varphi)\coloneqq\operatorname*{gd}(\chi)+|\operatorname{vars}(\overline{\mathtt{e}})|
.

For convenience, we let
\textstyle\operatorname*{free}(\varphi)\coloneqq\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{v}}(\varphi)\cup\textstyle\operatorname*{free}_{\mathtt{e}}(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in\textsf{GC}^{k}
.
Formulas of
\textsf{GC}^{k}
are evaluated over a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
via interpretations
\mathcal{I}=(I_{\mathcal{H}},\nu_{\mathtt{v}},\nu_{\mathtt{e}})
that consist of
\mathcal{H}
’s incidence graph
I_{\mathcal{H}}
and assignments
\nu_{\mathtt{v}}\colon\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{v}}\to\mathcal{R}(I_{\mathcal{H}})
and
\nu_{\mathtt{e}}\colon\textsf{VAR}_{\mathtt{e}}\to\mathcal{B}(I_{\mathcal{H}})
.
The semantics of
\textsf{GC}^{k}
are as expected and a definition can be found in Section 6 of the full version of
[
26
]
, thus we do not give one here.
A
sentence
is a formula
\varphi\in\textsf{GC}^{k}
that has neither free vertex, nor free hyperedge variables, i.e.,
\textstyle\operatorname*{free}(\varphi)=\varnothing
.
By
\textsf{GC}^{k}_{d}
we denote the fragment
\{\varphi\in\textsf{GC}^{k}\,:\,\operatorname*{gd}(\varphi)\leq d\}
, and we let
\textsf{GC}_{k}\coloneqq\textsf{GC}^{k}_{k}
.
We write
\mathcal{G}\equiv_{\textsf{L}}\mathcal{H}
to denote that
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
satisfy the same sentences in the fragment
\textsf{L}\subseteq\textsf{GC}^{k}
.

For simplicity, we omit logical guards if they are empty or equal to the formula they are guarding.
I.e., we may abbreviate subformulas of the form
(\top\land\varphi)
or
(\varphi\land\varphi)
as
\varphi
.
We may also omit parentheses in the usual way.
We write
\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$=$}n}(\overline{\mathtt{x}})\mathbin{.}(\Delta\land\varphi)
as shorthand for
\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}n}(\overline{\mathtt{x}})\mathbin{.}(\Delta\land\varphi)\land\lnot\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}n{+}1}(\overline{\mathtt{x}})\mathbin{.}(\Delta\land\varphi)
.
Clearly, these shorthands change neither the semantics, nor the free variables, nor the guard depth of a formula.

Example 17
.

The sentence
\varphi_{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}\land\psi_{3}
describes
\mathcal{G}
from
Example

1
up to isomorphism, where

\displaystyle\chi_{n}\;

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\bigwedge_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\lnot\mathtt{v}_{i}\kern 1.0pt{=}\kern 1.0pt\mathtt{v}_{j}\;\land\;\lnot\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}1}(\mathtt{v}_{n{+}1})\mathbin{.}\Bigl(E(\mathtt{e},\mathtt{v}_{n{+}1})\;\land\;\bigwedge_{i\in[n]}\lnot\mathtt{v}_{n{+}1}=\mathtt{v}_{i}\Bigr)\,,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$=$}4}(\mathtt{e})\mathbin{.}\mathtt{e}\kern 1.0pt{=}\kern 1.0pt\mathtt{e}\,,

\displaystyle\psi_{2}\,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$=$}1}(\mathtt{e})\mathbin{.}\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}1}(\mathtt{v}_{1},\mathtt{v}_{2},\mathtt{v}_{3})\mathbin{.}\Bigl(\textstyle\bigwedge_{i\in[3]}E(\mathtt{e},\mathtt{v}_{i})\;\land\;\chi_{3}\;\land\;\textstyle\bigwedge_{i\in[3]}\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$=$}3}(\mathtt{e})\mathbin{.}E(\mathtt{e},v_{i})\Bigr)\,\vphantom{\Bigl(\Bigr)},

\displaystyle\psi_{3}\,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$=$}3}(\mathtt{e})\mathbin{.}\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\geq$}1}(\mathtt{v}_{1},\mathtt{v}_{2})\mathbin{.}\Bigl(\textstyle\bigwedge_{i\in[2]}E(\mathtt{e},\mathtt{v}_{i})\;\land\;\chi_{2}\;\land\;\textstyle\bigwedge_{i\in[3]}\exists^{\scalebox{0.6}{$=$}3}(\mathtt{e})\mathbin{.}E(\mathtt{e},v_{i})\Bigr)\,.

It is easily verified that
\varphi_{\mathcal{G}}\in\textsf{GC}^{1}_{2}
.

\chi_{n}
is a helper formula, describing that there are precisely

\mathtt{v}_{1},\dots,\mathtt{v}_{n}
in the hyperedge
\mathtt{e}

\psi_{1}
describes that there are precisely four hyperedges,
\psi_{2}
describes that precisely one hyperedge contains precisely three vertices, each being contained in precisely 3 hyperedges.
Finally,
\psi_{3}
describes that there are exactly 3 hyperedges containing precisely 2 vertices, each being contained in precisely 3 hyperedges.
It is not hard to see that, in total, this describes
\mathcal{G}
up to isomorphism.

Atomic Formulas

•

For every formula

•

For

Final Remarks

This paper solves one of the open questions of Scheidt and Schweikardt
[
26
]
, who lift a result by Dvořák
[
9
]
from graphs to hypergraphs.
Dvořák shows that homomorphism indistinguishability over the graphs of tree width at most

is equivalent to indistinguishability over first-order logic with counting quantifiers (
C
) and
k{+}1
variables (
\textsf{C}^{k{+}1}

Scheidt and Schweikardt show that homomorphism indistinguishability over the class
\mathsf{GHW}_{k}
of hypergraphs of generalised hypertree width at most
k
is equivalent to indistinguishability over the logic
GC
with
k
guards (
\textsf{GC}^{k}
).
Grohe
[
11
]
gave a result complementing Dvořák’s:
C
with quantifier depth at most
m
(
\textsf{C}_{m}
) matches homomorphism indistinguishability over graphs of tree depth at most
m
.
An obvious expectation was that the distinguishing power of
\textsf{GC}_{m}
would match homomorphism indistinguishability over the class
\mathsf{HD}_{m}
of hypergraphs of hypertree depth at most
m
as it is defined by Adler et al.
[
1
]
.
However, this expectation did not manifest in this exact way.
Instead, we proved that the distinguishing power of
\textsf{GC}_{m}
matches homomorphism indistinguishability over hypergraphs of
strict
hypertree depth at most
m
, which is a (mild) restriction of hypertree depth.
Combining
Theorem

20
with the main result of
[
26
]
yields the following combined result.

Theorem 23
.

For all hypergraphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, the following equivalences hold:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\equiv_{\textsf{GC}_{k}}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\;\iff\;\mathcal{G}\equiv_{\mathsf{SHD}_{k}}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\;\iff\;I_{\mathcal{G}}\equiv_{\mathsf{ISHD}_{k}}I_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\equiv_{\textsf{GC}^{k}}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\;\iff\;\mathcal{G}\equiv_{\mathsf{GHW}_{k}}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\;\iff\;I_{\mathcal{G}}\equiv_{\mathsf{IGHW}_{k}}I_{\mathcal{H}}.

We took this unexpected mismatch between
\textsf{GC}_{k}
and
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
as an opportunity to investigate the relationship between
\mathsf{HD}_{k}
and
\mathsf{SHD}_{k}
.
In

5
we showed that the strict hypertree depth of a hypergraph is at most 1 larger than its hypertree depth.

To show that homomorphism counts from the class

are just as expressive as homomorphism counts from the class

Since

In fact, we showed in
