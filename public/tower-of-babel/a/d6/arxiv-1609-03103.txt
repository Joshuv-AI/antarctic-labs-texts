Uncertainty in Monotone Co-Design Problems
Andrea Censi

Abstract
This work contributes to a compositional theory of “co-design” that allows to optimally design a robotic platform. In this framework, the user describes each subsystem as a monotone relation between “functionality” provided and “resources” required . These models can be easily composed to express the co-design constraints among different subsystems. The user then queries the model, to obtain the design with minimal resources usage, subject to a lower bound on the provided functionality. This paper concerns the introduction of uncertainty in the framework. Uncertainty has two roles: first, it allows to deal with limited knowledge of the models; second, it also can be used to generate consistent relaxations of a problem, as the computation requirements can be lowered, should the user accept some uncertainty in the answer.

The design of a robotic platform involves the choice
and configuration of many hardware and software subsystems (actuation,
energetics, perception, control, …) in an harmonious entity.
Because robotics is a young discipline, there is still little work
towards obtaining systematic procedures to derive optimal designs.
Therefore, robot design is a lengthly process based on empirical evaluation
and trial and error.

The work presented here contributes to a theory of co-design
[
1
,
2
]

that allows to optimally design a robotic platform based on formal
models of its subsystems. The goal is to allow a designer to create
better designs, faster. This work on “co-design” is related to
and complementary to works that deal with “co-generation” (the
ability of synthesizing hardware and software blueprints for entire
robot platforms)
[
3
,
4
]
.

This paper describes the introduction of uncertainty in the theory
developed so far. In this framework, the user defines “design problems”
for each physical or logical subsystem. Each design problem (DP) is
a relation between “
functionality
” provided and “
resources
”
required by the component. The DPs can then composed in a graph, where
each edge represents a “co-design constraint” between two DPs
.

The resulting class of problems is called Monotone Co-Design Problems
(MCDPs).

An example of MCDP is sketched in Fig.
1a
. The
design problem consists in finding an optimal configuration of a UAV,
optimizing over actuators, sensors, processors, and batteries. In
this simplified example, the functionality of the UAV is parameterized
by three numbers: the
distance to travel
for each mission;
the
payload to transport
; the
number of missions
to fly.
The optimal design is defined as the one that satisfies the functionality
constraints while using the minimal amount of
resources
(
cost

and
mass
). In the figure, the model is exploded to show how
actuation and energetics are modeled. Perception is modeled as a relation
between
the velocity of the platform
and the
power
required
(the faster the platform, the more data needs to be processed).
Actuation is modeled as a relation between
lift
and
power
/
cost
.
Batteries are described by a relation between
capacity
and
mass
/
cost
.
In this example, there are different battery technologies (LiPo, etc.),
each specified by specific energy, specific cost, and lifetime, thus
characterized by a different relation between
capacity
,
number
of missions
and
mass
and
cost
. The interconnection between
design problems describe the “co-design constraints”, which could
be recursive: e.g., actuators must lift the batteries, the batteries
must power the actuators. Cycles represent design problems that are
coupled.

Once the model is defined, it can be queried to obtain the
minimal

solution in terms of resources — here,
total cost

and
total mass
. The output to the user is the Pareto front containing
all non-dominated solutions. The corresponding optimization problem
is, in general, nonconvex. Yet, with few assumptions, it is possible
to obtain a systematic solution procedure, and show that there exists
a dynamical system whose fixed point corresponds to the set
of minimal solutions.

This paper describes how to add a notion of
uncertainty
in
the MCDP framework. The model of uncertainty considered is interval
uncertainty on arbitrary partial orders. For a partially ordered set
(poset)
\left\langle\mathcal{P},\preceq\right\rangle
, these are sets
of the type
\{x\in\mathcal{P}\colon a\preceq x\preceq b\}
. I will show
how one can introduce this type of uncertainty in the MCDP framework
by considering ordered pairs of design problems. Each pair describes
lower and upper bounds for resources usage. These
uncertain
design problems
(UDPs) can be composed using series, parallel, and
feedback interconnection, just like their non-uncertain counterparts.

The output to the user is
two
Pareto fronts, describing
the minimal resource consumptions in the best case and in the worst
case according to the models specified. One or both the Pareto fronts
can be empty, meaning that the problem does not have a feasible solution.

This is different from the usual formalization of “robust optimization”
[
5
,
6
]
,
usually formulated as a “worst case” analysis, in which the uncertainty
in the problem is described by a set of possible parameters, and the
optimization problem is posed as finding the one design that is valid
for all cases.

Uncertainty plays two roles: it can be used as a
modeling

tool
,
where the relations are uncertain because of our limited knowledge,
and it can be used as a
computational

tool
, in which
we deliberately choose to consider uncertain relations as a relaxation
of the problem, to reduce the computational load, while maintaining
precise consistency guarantees. With these additions, the MCDP framework
can describe even richer design problems and to efficiently
solve them.

Section

Design Problems

A design problem

and

Functionality

\langle{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}},\preceq_{{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}}\rangle

\langle{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}},\preceq_{{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}\rangle

uses nodes for DPs and green and red edges for

.

Example 1
.

The first-order characterization of a battery is as a store of energy,
in which the

is the

(what

[kg] and

For a fixed functionality
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}
, the set of
minimal resources in
{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}
sufficient to perform the functionality
might contain two or more elements that are incomparable with respect
to
\preceq_{{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}
. For example, in the case of a battery, one
might consider different battery technologies that are incomparable
in the
mass
/
cost
resource space.

A subset with “minimal”, “incomparable” elements is called
“antichain”. This is the mathematical formalization of what is
informally called a “Pareto front”.

Definition 2
.

An
antichain

in a poset
\left\langle\mathcal{P},\preceq\right\rangle

is a subset of
\mathcal{P}
such that no element of
S
dominates another
element: for all
x,y\in S
and
x\preceq y
, then
x=y
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathsf{A}\mathcal{P}
be the set of antichains of
\mathcal{P}
.
\mathsf{A}\mathcal{P}

is a poset itself, with the partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{A}\mathcal{P}}

defined as

S_{1}\preceq_{\mathsf{A}\mathcal{P}}S_{2}\ \equiv\ \uparrow S_{1}\supseteq\,\uparrow S_{2},

(1)

where “
\uparrow
” denotes the upper closure of a set.

Definition 4
.

A
design problem
(DP) is a tuple
\left\langle{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}},{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}\right\rangle

such that
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}
are CPOs, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}}:{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}}\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}

is a monotone and Scott-continuous function (
[
9
]

or
[
2
, Definition 11]
). Each functionality
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}

(or vector of functionalities, if
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}
is a product of posets)
corresponds to an antichain of resources
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}({\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}})\in{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}
(Fig.
3
).

Fig. 3:
A DP is a represented by a map
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}}:{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}}\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}

from functionality to antichains of resources.

Monotonicity implies that, if the functionality is increased,
then the required resources increase as well.

III
Monotone Co-Design Problems

A Monotone Co-Design Problem (MCDP) is a multigraph of DPs. An
edge between a resource
{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{r}}_{1}
of a DP and a functionality
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}_{2}

of another denotes the partial order inequality constraint
{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{r}}_{1}\preceq{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}_{2}
.
Cycles and self-loops are allowed.

. We will also
use an “algebraic” representation, shown in Fig.

,
and defined in Def.
6
.

Series-parallel graphs (see, e.g.,

Equivalently, we are defining a symmetric traced monoidal category

or
[

]

Let us use a standard definition of “operators”, “terms”,
and “atoms” (see, e.g.,
[
16
, p.41]
). Given a set
of operators
\mathsf{ops}
and a set of atoms

Definition 6

(Algebraic definition of Monotone Co-Design Problems)
.

An MCDP is a tuple
\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle
,
where:

1.

\mathcal{A}
is any set of atoms, to be used as labels.

2.

The term
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}
in the
\{\mathsf{series},\mathsf{par},\mathsf{loop}\}
algebra
describes the structure of the graph:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathsf{Terms}(\{\mathsf{series},\mathsf{par},\mathsf{loop}\},\mathcal{A}).

3.

The
valuation

\boldsymbol{v}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathsf{DP}
assigns a DP to
each atom.

Semantics of MCDPs

We can now define the

(Semantics of MCDP)
.

Given an MCDP in algebraic form
\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle
,
the semantics

\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket\in\mathsf{DP}

\displaystyle\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},a,\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle\doteq\boldsymbol{v}(a),\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all}\ a\in\mathcal{A},

(2)

\displaystyle\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{series}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle\doteq\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket\,\mathbin{\varocircle}\,\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{par}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle\doteq\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket\,\mathbin{\varotimes}\,\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{loop}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle\doteq\varphi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket^{\dagger}.

(5)

The

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{1}\mathbin{\varotimes}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{2}:({\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}_{1}\times{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}_{2})

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}R}

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigcup_{{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{r}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}R}}\Big[\Min_{\preceq}\uparrow\bigcup_{s\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}({\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{r}})}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{b}(s)\mathbin{\boldsymbol{\times}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{c}(s)\Big]\ \cap\uparrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{r}}.

The least fixed point equation can be solved using Kleene’s algorithm
[
8
, CPO Fixpoint theorem I, 8.15]
.
A dynamical system that computes the set of solutions is given by

\begin{cases}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}R}_{0}&\leftarrow\{\bot_{{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}\},\\
{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}R}_{k+1}&\leftarrow\Psi_{{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}}({\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}R}_{k}).\end{cases}

The limit
\sup{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}R}_{k}
is the set of minimal solutions, which
might be an empty set if the problem is unfeasible for a particular
value
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}
.

This dynamical system is a proper algorithm only if each step can
be performed with bounded computation. An example in which this is
not the case are relations that give an infinite number of solutions
for each functionality. For example, the very first DP appearing in Fig.
1a

corresponds to the relation
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\text{travel distance}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{velocity}}\times{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{endurance}},

for which there are infinite numbers of pairs
\langle{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{velocity}},{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{endurance}}\rangle

for each value of
travel distance
. The machinery
developed in this paper will make it possible to deal with these infinite-cardinality
relations by relaxation.

IV
Uncertain Design Problems

This section describes the notion of Uncertain DPs (UDPs). UDPs are
an ordered pair of DPs that can be interpreted as upper and lower
bounds for resource consumptions (Fig.
1b
). We
will be able to propagate this interval uncertainty through an arbitrary
interconnection of DPs. The result presented to the user will be a

pair
of antichains — a lower and an upper bound
for the resource consumption.

IV-A

Partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}

Being able to provide both upper and lower bounds comes from the fact
that in this framework everything is ordered – there is
a poset of resources, lifted to posets of antichains, which is lifted
to posets of DPs, and finally, to the poset of uncertain DPs.

The first step is defining a partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}
on
\mathsf{DP}
.

Definition 13

(Partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}
)
.

Consider two DPs
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}
.
The DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{1}
precedes
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{2}
if it requires fewer resources
for all functionality
{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}
:

{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{1}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{1}({\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}})\preceq_{{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}),\ \text{for all }{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}.

Fig. 6:
The partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}
in the space of design problems.

In this partial order, there is both a top
\top_{\mathsf{DP}}
and a
bottom
\bot_{\mathsf{DP}}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\bot_{\mathsf{DP}}:{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\{\bot_{{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}}\}.

\displaystyle\top_{\mathsf{DP}}:{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathscr{F}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathsf{A}{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\mathscr{R}}},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\mathsf{f}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\emptyset.

(7)

\bot_{\mathsf{DP}}
means that any functionality can be done with zero
resources, and
\top_{\mathsf{DP}}
means that the problem is always infeasible
(“the set of feasible resources is empty”).

IV-B

Uncertain DPs (UDPs)

Definition 14

(Uncertain DPs)
.

An Uncertain DP (UDP)
\boldsymbol{u}
is a pair of DPs
\langle{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle

such that
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}
.

Definition 15

(Partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}
)
.

A UDP
\boldsymbol{u}_{a}
precedes another UDP
\boldsymbol{u}_{b}
if the interval
[{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{a},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{a}]

is contained in the interval
[{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{b},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{b}]
(Fig.
7
):

\boldsymbol{u}_{a}\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}\boldsymbol{u}_{b}\hskip 10.00002pt\equiv\hskip 10.00002pt{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{b}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{a}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{a}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{b}.

Fig. 7:
The partial order
\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}
has a top
\top_{\mathsf{UDP}}=\left\langle\bot_{\mathsf{DP}},\top_{\mathsf{DP}}\right\rangle.

This pair describes the state of maximum uncertainty about the DP:
we do not know if the DP is feasible with 0 resources (
\bot_{\mathsf{DP}}
),
or if it is completely infeasible (
\top_{\mathsf{DP}}
).

A DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}
is equivalent to a degenerate UDP
\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}\rangle
.

A UDP
\boldsymbol{u}
is a bound for a DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}
if
\boldsymbol{u}\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}\rangle
,
or, equivalently, if
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\boldsymbol{u}\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\boldsymbol{u}
.

V
Interconnection of Uncertain Design Problems

We now define the interconnection of UDPs, in an equivalent way to
the definition of MCDPs. The only difference between Def.
6

and Def.
16
below is that the valuation assigns to
each atom an UDP, rather than a DP.

Definition 16

(Algebraic definition of UMCDPs)
.

An Uncertain MCDP (UMCDP) is a tuple
\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle
,
where
\mathcal{A}
is a set of atoms,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathsf{Terms}(\{\mathsf{series},\mathsf{par},\mathsf{loop}\},\mathcal{A})

is the algebraic representation of the graph, and
\boldsymbol{v}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathsf{UDP}

is a valuation that assigns to each atom a UDP.

Next, the semantics of a UMCDP is defined as a map
\Phi
that
computes the UDP. Def.
17
below is analogous
to Def.
8
.

Definition 17

(Semantics of UMCDPs)
.

Given an UMCDP
\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle
,
the semantics function
\Phi
computes a UDP

\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket\in\mathsf{UDP},

and it is recursively defined as follows:

\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},a,\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket=\boldsymbol{v}(a),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all}\ a\in\mathcal{A}.

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{series}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle=({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket)\,\mathbin{\varocircle}\,({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket),

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{series}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle=({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket)\,\mathbin{\varocircle}\,({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket),

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{par}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle]

\displaystyle=({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket)\ \mathbin{\varotimes}\ ({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket),

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{par}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle]

\displaystyle=({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket)\ \mathbin{\varotimes}\ ({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket),

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{loop}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle=({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket)^{\dagger},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\mathsf{loop}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}),\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle=({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle\rrbracket)^{\dagger}.

The operators
\dagger,\mathbin{\varocircle},\mathbin{\varotimes}
are defined in Def.
10
–Def.

VI
Approximation results

The main result of this section is a relaxation result stated as Theorem
19

below. The following is an informal statement.

(a)
Consider an MCDP containing the DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}
.

(b)
Suppose that a bound
\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}\right\rangle

is known.

(c)
Replace the DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}
by the uncertain
bounds.

(d)
Create a pair of MCDPs by choosing either lower or
upper bound.

(e)
The resulting MCDPs are bounds for the original
MCDP.

Fig. 8:
Illustrations for the informal statement of the main result.

Informal statement

Consider an MCDP composed of many DPs, and call one
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}
(Fig.
8a
).
Suppose there exist two DPs
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}

that bound the DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}
in the sense that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}
(Fig.
8b
).
This can model either uncertainty in our knowledge of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}
,
or it can be a relaxation that we willingly introduce. The pair
\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}\right\rangle

forms a UDP that can be plugged in the MCDP in place of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}

(Fig.
8c
). We will see that if we plug in only
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}

or
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}
separately in place of the original
DP
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,1}{h}}_{a}
(Fig.
8d
), we obtain a pair of MCDPs
that form a UDP. Moreover, the solution of these two MCDPs are upper
and lower bounds for the solution of the original MCDP (Fig.
8e
).
Therefore, we can propagate the uncertainty in the model to the uncertain
in the solution. This result generalizes for any number of substitutions.

Formal statement

First, we define a partial order on the valuations. A valuation precedes
another if it gives more information on each DP.

Definition 18

(Partial order
\preceq_{V}
on valuations)
.

For two valuations
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathsf{UDP}
,
say that
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\preceq_{V}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
if
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(a)\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(a)

for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

At this point, we have enough machinery in place that we can simply
state the result as “the semantics is monotone in the valuation”.

Theorem 19

(
\Phi
is monotone in the valuation)
.

If
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\preceq_{V}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
, then

\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right\rangle\rrbracket\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}\Phi\llbracket\left\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right\rangle\rrbracket.

Proof:

This follows easily from the definitions in Def.
18
.
As intermediate results, first prove that the lower bound
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi

is monotone in the valuation with respect to the order
\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}
:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\rangle\rrbracket\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L}}}\Phi\llbracket\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle\rrbracket.

Then repeat the same reasoning for
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}
, to obtain:

{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle\rrbracket\preceq_{\mathsf{DP}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}}\Phi\llbracket\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\rangle\rrbracket.

These two together allow to conclude that
\Phi
is monotone with
respect to the valuation with respect to the order
\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}
:

\Phi\llbracket\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\rangle\rrbracket\preceq_{\mathsf{UDP}}\Phi\llbracket\langle\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle\rrbracket.

∎

This result says that we can swap any DP in a MCDP with a UDP relaxation
to obtain a UMCDP, which then we can solve to obtain inner and outer
approximations to the solution of the original MCDP. This shows that
considering interval uncertainty in the MCDP framework is easy because
it reduces to solving a pair of problems instead of one. The rest
of the paper consists of applications of this result.

VII-C

Relaxation for relations with antichains of infinite cardinality

Another way in which uncertain DPs can be used is to construct approximations
of DPs that would be too expensive to solve exactly. For example,
consider a relation like

{\color[rgb]{0.0949,0.5,0}\text{travel\_distance}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{velocity}}\times{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{endurance}},

(8)

which appears in the model in Fig.
1a
. If we take
these three quantities in (
8
) as belonging to
\mathbb{R}
,
then, for each value of the
travel distance
, there are infinite
pairs of
\left\langle{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{velocity}},{\color[rgb]{0.5558,0,0}\text{endurance}}\right\rangle

that are feasible. On a computer, if the quantities are represented
as floating point numbers, the combinations are properly not “infinite”,
but, still, extremely large. We can avoid considering all combinations
by creating a sequence of uncertain DPs that use finite and prescribed
computation.

VIII
Conclusions and future work

Monotone Co-Design Problems (MCDPs) provide a compositional theory
of “co-design” that describes co-design constraints among different
subsystems in a complex system, such as a robotic system.

This paper dealt with the introduction of uncertainty in the framework,
specifically, interval uncertainty. Uncertainty can be used in two
roles. First, it can be used to describe limited knowledge in the
models. For example, in Section
VII-A
,
we have seen how this can be applied to model mistrust about numbers
from Wikipedia. Second, uncertainty allows to generate relaxations
of the problem. We have seen two applications: introducing an allowed
tolerance in one particular variable (Section
VII-B
),
and dealing with relations with infinite cardinality using bounded
computation resources (Section
VII-C
).

Future work includes strengthening these results. For example, we
are not able to predict the resulting uncertainty in the solution
before actually computing it; ideally, one would like to know how
much computation is needed (measured by the number of points in the
antichain approximation) for a given value of the uncertainty that
the user can accept.

The least fixed point operator

is monotone, so we are left

∎
