Model Reduction for Quantum Systems: Discrete-time Quantum Walks and Open Markov Dynamics
Tommaso Grigoletto, Francesco Ticozzi

Abstract
A general approach to obtain reduced models for a wide class of discrete-time quantum systems is proposed. The obtained models not only reproduce exactly the output of a given quantum model, but are also guaranteed to satisfy physical constraints, namely complete positivity and preservation of total probability. A fundamental framework for exact model reduction of quantum systems is constructed leveraging on algebraic methods, as well as novel results on quantum conditional expectations in finite-dimensions. The proposed reduction algorithm is illustrated and tested on prototypical examples, including the quantum walk realizing Grover’s algorithm.



I
Introduction

Finding simpler descriptions for a dynamical model that is too large or complex to study or simulate is arguably a fundamental task in many scientific fields.
From a dynamical-system viewpoint, the problem has been studied extensively, aiming for both exact reductions, i.e. smaller models that are able to reproduce exactly the target evolution
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, and approximate ones
[
4
]
, in the linear as well as in non-linear settings
[
5
]
. In many cases, however, one may want to reduce a given model while maintaining certain constraints that characterize the dynamics, e.g. make the model physically admissible or satisfy some locality constraints. This problem, in general, proves difficult to solve: for example, model reduction for linear dynamical systems with positivity constraints is still a partially open issue
[
6
,
7
]
.

In this work, we tackle the problem of finding simpler and physically-admissible models that reproduce the output of a wide class of discrete-time quantum dynamical systems. For these systems to be physically admissible, in addition to linearity, minimal requirements are positivity and the preservation of the total probability, i.e. we seek Completely-Positive, Trace-Preserving (CPTP) dynamics. Potential direct applications include alternative, “compressed” version of quantum walks
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
and reduced models and filters for digital quantum control
[
12
,
13
]
.
Finding the minimal reduced model that preserves the physical constraints also allows for efficient simulations of dynamics on quantum computers
[
14
]
.
The problem of finding computationally-efficient representations is particularly relevant nowadays since the quantum computers available have very limited resources
[
15
]
. In addition, such a reduction allows us to understand what are the essential degrees of freedom of a model and which instead can be removed. Moreover, one can use the reduced model to investigate the “quantumness” of the key variables: by observing their structure one can understand if the system is intrinsically quantum, or it might be represented classically, or even with a hybrid quantum-classical model
[
16
]
.

The problem of finding smaller physical representations of quantum systems is of course not new: available model reduction methods such as derivation of master equations, rotating-wave approximations, adiabatic elimination
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, provide approximate models with a trade-off between the accuracy and the simplicity of the reduced model.
We here focus instead on developing a framework for finding models that are capable of reproducing the output of a given model
exactly
. While this request may seem too stringent, it leads to results and techniques that can be adapted to approximate, partial or asymptotically-exact model reductions. These extensions, as well as a scenario for continuous-time dynamics, will be treated in upcoming work
[
20
,
21
]
.
To the authors’ knowledge, no systematic methods for exact model reduction for quantum systems are available.

The models of interest in this work are discrete-time CPTP dynamics, paired with an output equation, which in analogy with the classical case we call Quantum Hidden-Markov (QHM) models, as defined in Section
II
. As outputs of interest we consider linear functions of the state: these allow us to cover single-time (unconditional) probability of given events, expectations of observables of interest, as well as reduced/marginal states. In particular, quantum walks can be seen as quantum CPTP dynamics, for which the output of interest is the probability distribution of a given observable
[
22
,
9
]
.
In this paper, where substantial work is devoted to building the foundation for a systematic model-reduction framework, we do not also include the evolution of states conditioned on previous measurements, i.e. quantum trajectories and quantum filters
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. Nonetheless, the results can be extended to this scenario adapting the approach of
[
26
,
27
]
from classical Markov processes to non-commutative ones. The quantum framework introduces some interesting peculiarities, and the details will be presented elsewhere
[
28
]
.
The seemingly simpler problem of reproducing the single-time probabilities is already challenging on its own, and has some key applications of interest, including the analysis, reduction and simulation of quantum walks and quantum algorithms. In fact, this work has been partially motivated by the results of
[
29
,
30
]
, where the simulatability of quantum walks with classical resources has been used to successfully replicate their mixing speedup and understanding its origin.

The method we are proposing to tackle exact model reduction for quantum systems hinges on key tools from linear system theory, namely reachability and observability analysis and realization theory
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
. Our quantum reachability and observability analysis is inspired by the one introduced for continuous-time, controlled quantum systems in
[
31
]
. Recently, it has been shown in
[
32
]
how these tools can be used to find a minimal linear reduction for discrete-time quantum systems, albeit the latter need not satisfy CPTP constraints. The system-theoretic ideas and these preliminary results are recalled in Section
III
, after the notation, models, and problem are presented in Section
II
.

The core idea of this work is to enlarge the minimal linear reduction subspaces to a
*
-algebra, and thus to a quantum probability space
[
33
,
34
,
23
,
35
]
. The reduction is then obtained using CPTP projections. Our construction exploits the theory of (finite-dimensional) quantum conditional expectations and their duals (CPTP projections), that we develop with some new results (see
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
, and our essential review in Section
IV
). In particular, in Section
V
, we show how to find the minimal set that includes a given subspace and admits a CPTP projection.

The main reduction algorithms are presented in Section
VI
.
Since classical Markov chains can be viewed as a discrete-time quantum systems restricted to an invariant abelian algebra, the results presented here also cover and improve the existing one for the classical case. Compared to those proposed in
[
26
]
, for
classical
Hidden Markov models, the results presented here differ in two significant ways. First, for non-commutative algebras even the existence of CPTP projections is not guaranteed, and hence the construction we propose requires the foundational results presented in Section
V
. Second, we here consider an iterative model reduction algorithm. This allows us to overcome the technical difficulties of the non-iterative algorithm proposed in
[
26
]
, which are recalled in Section
VI
, and avoid the need for additional conditions in order to have effective reductions.
Examples of application are discussed in Section
VII
, including the well-known quantum-walk implementation of Grover’s algorithm
[
39
]
.

II
Models and problem definition

II-A

Notation

In this work, we denote with
\mathcal{H}
the (finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
and with
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the set of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We then use capital letters to denote operators,
X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, andthe
{\dagger}
-superscript to denote their adjoints (as well as the transpose and conjugate of their matrix representation).
\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{H})
represents the set of self-adjoint operators, i.e.
\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{H})=\{X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})|X=X^{\dagger}\}
.
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of density operators, i.e.
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})=\{X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})|X=X^{\dagger}\geq 0,\,{\rm tr}[X]=1\}
. With few exceptions, the “script” notation is used to denote operator spaces, e.g.
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and the “calligraphic” notation to denote super-operators, e.g.
\mathcal{A}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

We denote with
\left<\cdot\middle|\cdot\right>
the standard inner product over
\mathcal{H}
, and with
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{HS}
the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product for
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, that is
\left<X,Y\right>_{HC}={\rm tr}(X^{\dagger}Y)
. In this work we employ also different inner products for
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
: from Friedrichs representation theorem, see e.g.
[
40
, Theorem 4]
, every inner product is associated to a self-adjoint positive-definite super operator
\mathcal{Q}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}>0
as
\left<X,Y\right>_{\mathcal{Q}}=\left<X,\mathcal{Q}(Y)\right>_{HS}
. In the rest of this work, we denote inner products by their positive super operator, i.e.
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{Q}}
denotes the inner product defined as
\left<\cdot,\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)\right>_{HS}.
Orthogonality with respect to a modified inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{Q}}
, is referred
\mathcal{Q}
-orthogonality.

If
\mathscr{V}
and
\mathscr{W}
are linear operator subspaces,
\mathscr{V}+\mathscr{W}
is used for the minimal subspace containing both spaces, while
\mathscr{V}\oplus\mathscr{W}
is used to indicate the same while also specifying that the two subspaces are orthogonal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. The symbol
\oplus_{\mathcal{Q}}
is used in case the orthogonality of the considered spaces is with respect to the inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{Q}}
.

As usual,
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the commutator of two operators. Given an operator
X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we define the support of
X
as the subspace orthogonal to its kernel, i.e.
{\rm supp}(X):=(\ker(X))^{\perp}
Similarly, given a set of operators
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
we define its support as the sum of the supports of the operators it contains, i.e
{\rm supp}(\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{S}}{\rm supp}(X).

In this work, by
*
-algebra we mean a linear operator space closed under composition and the adjoint involution
{\dagger}
. A
*
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
is said to be unital if it contains the identity, i.e.
I\in\mathscr{A}
. With some abuse of notation we will denote with
\mathfrak{H}(\mathscr{A})
and
\mathfrak{D}(\mathscr{A})
the set of Hermitian operators and density operators contained in the algebra
\mathscr{A}
respectively, i.e.
\mathfrak{H}(\mathscr{A}):=\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathscr{A}
and
\mathfrak{D}(\mathscr{A}):=\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathscr{A}
.
Further relevant facts about
*
-algebras, their representations and their properties will be introduced when needed, in particular in Section
IV
and
V
.

II-B

Quantum Hidden Markov Models and Reduction Task

In this work we describe quantum systems in an algebraic framework, following e.g.
[
33
,
34
,
23
,
35
]
. The quantum system of interest is associated with a
*
-(sub)algebra
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The self-adjoint operators in
\mathfrak{H}(\mathscr{A})
represent the observable quantities of the quantum system. The available information on the system is condensed in a linear positive and normalized functional

\mathbb{P}[A]=\left<\rho,A\right>_{HS}
, where
\rho
is a density operator in
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})=\{\rho|\rho=\rho^{\dagger}\geq 0,{\rm tr}(\rho)=1\}
. We will refer to
\rho
as the
state
of the system.

In the this setting, dynamics of the density operators correspond to completely-positive (CP), trace-preserving (TP) linear maps
\mathcal{E}:\mathscr{A}_{1}\mapsto\mathscr{A}_{2}.

These maps guarantee that
\mathfrak{D}(\mathscr{A}_{1})
is mapped into
\mathfrak{D}(\mathscr{A}_{2})
.
Any CP map admits an operator-sum representation (also known as Kraus representation
[
41
]
):
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sum_{k}C_{k}\rho C_{k}^{\dagger},
which is TP if and only if
\sum_{k}C_{k}^{\dagger}C_{k}=I.
The last property is also equivalent to the adjoint map
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
being unital:
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(I)=I.

CPTP maps are the non-commutative extension of stochastic maps between finite probability spaces.
We next present the dynamical models of interest for this paper.

Definition 1

(QHM model)
.

Let consider a
*
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
as state space and an output operator space
\mathscr{Y}
. Let then consider a CPTP evolution super-operator
\mathcal{A}[\cdot]:\mathscr{A}\mapsto\mathscr{A}
and a linear output map
\mathcal{C}[\cdot]:\mathscr{A}\mapsto\mathscr{Y}
. Finally let consider a set of initial conditions
\mathfrak{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}(\mathscr{A})
.
The quadruple
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
specifies an (unconditional) discrete-time quantum hidden Markov (QHM) model of the form:

\begin{cases}\rho(t+1)=\mathcal{A}[\rho(t)]\\
Y(t)=\mathcal{C}[\rho(t)]\end{cases}\quad\rho(0)\in\mathfrak{S}.

(1)

We call these models “hidden Markov” because the dynamics is Markovian only for the full, potentially non-accessible state
\rho(t),
while it can exhibit memory effects for the output of interest
Y(t).

In fact, these models include and extend their classical counterparts, hidden Markov models.
Quantum walks are also included as a special case, when the output map is chosen to be the probability of outcomes of a given observable. An example of this will be given in Section
VII-A
.

For the sake of simplicity, when clear from the context, we denote the QHM model using only the triplet
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
leaving the state space and the output space intended.

The main problem we address in this work is the following.

Problem 1

(QHM model reduction)
.

Given a QHM
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
find a QHM
(\widecheck{\mathscr{A}},\mathscr{Y},\widecheck{\mathcal{A}},\widecheck{\mathcal{C}},\widecheck{\mathfrak{S}})
, with
\dim(\widecheck{\mathscr{A}})\leq\dim(\mathscr{A})
, and a CPTP map
\Phi:\mathscr{A}\to\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
such that, for every initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
and every time
t\geq 0
, the two models provide the same output i.e.

\mathcal{C}[\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]]=\widecheck{\mathcal{C}}[\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}^{t}[\Phi[\rho_{0}]]].

III
Reachability, observability and

optimal linear reductions

In this section, we collect a series of definitions and results adapted from linear system theory
[
42
,
3
,
1
]
that provides the basis for solving Problem
1
and, at the same time provide the solution to a related and simpler problem.

III-A

Reachable subspace

Given an initial condition
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
a trajectory of the model
(\mathcal{A,C},\mathfrak{S})
from
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
is defined as the set of states
\rho(t)
that the system assumes at non-negative times
\mathfrak{T}_{\rho_{0}}:=\{\rho(t)=\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}],t\geq 0\}
. Consequently, the set of trajectories starting from points contained in
\mathfrak{S}
is defined as the union of the single trajectories
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathfrak{S}}=\bigcup_{\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{T}_{\rho_{0}}
.

Definition 2

(Reachable subspace from
\mathfrak{S}
)
.

We call reachable subspace (from
\mathfrak{S}
) of the model
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
the linear space generated by the set of trajectories
\mathfrak{T}_{\mathfrak{S}}
:

\mathscr{R}:={\rm span}\{\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}],\,t\geq 0,\,\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}\}.

(2)

It can be proven, in analogy of what is done in for classical linear systems with inputs
[
32
,
3
]
, that
\mathscr{R}
is the smallest
\mathcal{A}
-invariant subspace of
\mathscr{A}
that contains
{\rm span}\{\mathfrak{S}\}
. Using Caley-Hamilton, one can also prove that
\mathscr{R}={\rm span}\{\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}],\,t=0,\dots,n^{2},\,\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}\}
where
n=\dim(\mathcal{H})
.

III-B

Non-obervable subspace

We next characterize the set of operators that produce no output at all times.

Definition 3

(Non-observable subspace)
.

We call non-observable subspace of the model
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
the subspace

\mathscr{N}:=\{X\in\mathscr{A}|\mathcal{C}[\mathcal{A}^{t}[X]]=0,\,\forall t\geq 0\}.

(3)

It is possible to prove that
\mathscr{N}
is the largest
\mathcal{A}
-invariant subspace contained in
\ker{\mathcal{C}}

[
3
]
.

The non-observable subspace can be used to characterize the set of states that are not distinguishable from the output at any time. If we consider two initial states
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
and
\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\in\mathscr{N}
, then their output is equivalent at all times, namely
\mathcal{C}[\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{1}]]=\mathcal{C}[\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{2}]]
for all
t\geq 0
.

Since we assumed
\mathcal{C}
to be a linear map, we have a convenient way of computing the orthogonal of the non-observable subspace. Let
\{E_{i}\}
be an orthogonal basis for
\mathscr{Y}
, then any linear map
\mathcal{C}
can be written as
\mathcal{C}[\cdot]=\sum_{i}E_{i}{\rm tr}[C_{i}^{\dagger}\cdot]
for a set of operators
\{C_{i}\}\subset\mathscr{A}
. We can then compute, see e.g.
[
32
]
, the space orthogonal to
\mathscr{N}
, defined as
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}=\{X\in\mathscr{A}|\left<X,Y\right>_{HS}=0,\,\forall Y\in\mathscr{N}\},
in the following way:

\mathscr{N}^{\perp}={\rm span}\{\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}t}[C_{i}],\,t=0,\dots,n^{2}-1\}

where
n
is the dimension of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

III-C

Effective subspace and minimal linear realizations

Definition 4

(Effective subspace)
.

Given the reachable and observable subspace associated to model
(\mathcal{A,C},\mathfrak{S})
, i.e.
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{N}
we define an effective subspace
\mathscr{E}
as a (non-necessarily orthogonal) complement of the intersection
\mathscr{R}\cap\mathscr{N}
to
\mathscr{R}
, i.e.
\mathscr{E}
is any subspace such that
\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{E}+(\mathscr{R}\cap\mathscr{N})
.

Remark
.

It should be noted that the choice of
\mathscr{E}
is not unique, in fact, any representative of the quotient space
\mathscr{R}/(\mathscr{R}\cap\mathscr{N})
is a valid choice for
\mathscr{E}
. Usually, when solving a model reduction problem in control system theory, every choice of a representative of
\mathscr{R}/(\mathscr{R}\cap\mathscr{N})
is equivalent. When solving Problem
1
however, we aim to find a CPTP reduced dynamics. The additional positivity constraints in this scenario make the choice of a particular effective subspace relevant in the effort of finding the minimal model.

Intuitively, the effective subspace contains all the states that can be reached by the model minus the ones that can not be distinguished from the output. This is confirmed by the fact that the projection onto the effective subspace solves a simpler version of Problem
1
.

Problem 2

(Linear model reduction)
.

Given a QHM
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
find a linear model
(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{L},\mathcal{C}_{L},\mathfrak{S}_{L})
, of equations

\begin{cases}X(t+1)=\mathcal{A}_{L}[X(t)]&X\in\mathscr{V}\\
Y(t)=\mathcal{C}_{L}[X(t)]&Y\in\mathscr{Y}\end{cases}\quad x(0)\in{\mathfrak{S}_{L}}

possibly smaller, i.e.
\dim(\mathscr{V})\leq\dim(\mathscr{A})
, and a linear map
\Phi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{V}
,
\Phi[\rho_{0}]=X(0)
such that, for every initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
and every time
t\geq 0
, the two models provide the same output i.e.

\mathcal{C}[\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]]=\mathcal{C}_{L}[{\mathcal{A}_{L}}^{t}[\Phi[\rho_{0}]]].

The difference between Problems
1
and
2
is subtle but crucial. Problem
1
aims to find a reduced QHM model, namely the map
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}
has to be CPTP and the state space
\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
needs to be a
*
-algebra. In Problem
2
, this assumption is relaxed,
\mathcal{A}_{L}
only needs to be linear, and
\mathscr{V}
is simply an operator space, thus simplifying the problem.

An equivalent version of this problem (with zero initial condition but with linear inputs) has been solved originally by Rosenbrock
[
1
]
: it has been proven recently that the same approach can be used to solve Problem
2

[
32
,
26
]
. We report here the result for completeness.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
an effective subspace of the QHM model
(\mathscr{A},\mathcal{Y,A,C,S})
and let
\Pi_{\mathcal{E}}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathcal{E}
the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{E}
. Then
\mathcal{E}
is a subspace of minimal dimension such that, defining
\mathcal{A}_{L}=\Pi_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathcal{E}}
,
\mathcal{C}_{L}=\mathcal{C}\Pi_{\mathcal{E}}
,

\mathcal{C}[\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]]={\mathcal{C}_{L}}[{\mathcal{A}_{L}}^{t}[\Pi_{\mathcal{E}}[\rho_{0}]]].

The proof of this Proposition can be found in
[
32
]
. While this result provides the best linear exact reductions for a system, these do not preserve two key aspects of a QHM model: (i) the state space should be associated with an operator algebra; (ii) the dynamics should be CPTP. The rest of the paper is devoted to constructing reductions that are indeed QHM. Necessarily, these models have a dimension greater or equal to the ones obtained via reduction to an effective subspace.

IV
Conditional expectations, state extensions and their CPTP factorizations

IV-A

Conditional Expectations and State Extensions: A Brief Review

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a
*
-subalgebra of
\mathscr{B}=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\sim\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}.
It is known that, via some unitary
U,
any
*
-subalgebra can be put in an orthogonal block decomposition (also called Wedderburn decomposition)
[
43
]
, associated to a decomposition of the underlying vector space as:

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{SF}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{R}=\bigoplus_{\ell}\mathcal{H}_{S,\ell}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{R},

(4)

so that :

\mathscr{A}=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,\ell})\otimes I_{F,\ell}\oplus\mathbb{0}_{R}\right)U^{\dagger}.

(5)

In the following we focus on unital
*
-algebras, corresponding to
\mathcal{H}_{R}=0.

A
conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathscr{A}
, is a CP, unital map that satisfies
[
38
]
:

\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}(AB)=A\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}(B)

(6)

for every
A\in\mathscr{A}
,
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
By choosing
B=I
we have that
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
acts identically on
\mathscr{A},
so
{\rm Im}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}})=\mathscr{A},
and
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
, thus
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
is a projector onto
\mathscr{A}
. However, it is not in general self-adjoint, and thus not an orthogonal projection, with respect to the standard Hilbert-Schmidt product.

A
state extension
is the dual of a conditional expectation, that is the CPTP map
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}^{\dagger}
so that
\forall\rho,X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
we have

\left<\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}(\rho),X\right>_{HS}=\left<\rho,\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}(X)\right>_{HS}.

Since
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
is idempotent, then also
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}^{2}=\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}.
Moreover, if for some
\overline{\rho},
we have
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}(\overline{\rho})=\overline{\rho}
then
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
is said to preserve
\overline{\rho}.

As proved in
[
44
, Prop. 1.5]
, any conditional expectation on a unital
\mathscr{A}
with decomposition (
5
) can be written in the form:

\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}(X)=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[(I_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell})(V_{\ell}XV^{\dagger}_{\ell})]\otimes I_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger},

(7)

with
V_{\ell}
a linear operator from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{S,\ell}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}
such that
V_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\dagger}=I_{S,\ell}\otimes I_{F,\ell},
and
\tau_{\ell}
are full-rank density operators on
\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}.
This shows that the conditional expectations on a given
*
-algebra are completely parametrized by the
factor states

\tau_{F,\ell}.
The state extension takes a similar form.

Proposition 2
.

The dual of a
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
with decomposition (
7
) takes the form

\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}(X)=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}(V_{\ell}XV^{\dagger}_{\ell})\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger}.

(8)

Proof.

Let us start by recalling, from
[
45
, Lemma 1]
, that for
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{\ell}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
and
W=U(\bigoplus_{\ell}W_{\ell})U^{\dagger}
, we have
{\rm tr}(WY)=\sum_{\ell}{\rm tr}(W_{\ell}V_{\ell}YV_{\ell}^{\dagger})
, where
V_{\ell}
are the non-square isometries defined above. Through direct computation and using the fact that the dual of a partial trace is the tensor product with the identity on the traced-out subsystem, we then obtain

\displaystyle\left<X,\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}[Y]\right>_{HS}=

\displaystyle=\left<X,U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[(I_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell})(V_{\ell}YV_{\ell}^{\dagger})]\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left<V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger},{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[(I_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell})(V_{\ell}YV_{\ell}^{\dagger})]\otimes I_{F,\ell}\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left<{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger}],{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[(I_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell})(V_{\ell}YV_{\ell})]\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left<{\rm tr}_{F,\ell}[V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger}]\otimes I_{F,k},(I_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell})(V_{\ell}YV_{\ell}^{\dagger})\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\left<V_{\ell}^{\dagger}\left[{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger}]\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}\right]V_{\ell},Y\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\underbrace{U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}[V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger}]\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger}}_{=\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}[X]},Y\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}[X],Y\right>_{HS}

Which concludes the proof.
∎

We thus have that
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})
contains a full-rank state
\sigma=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}\frac{I_{S,\ell}}{d_{S,\ell}}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger}
and, using the representation above, it is easy to see that indeed
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
is self-adjoint with respect to any modified inner product of the form

\left<X,Y\right>_{\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}}={\rm tr}(X^{\dagger}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}Y\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}})
with
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\cdot):=\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}
, see e.g.
[
45
, Proposition 2]
. It then follows that
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
is an orthogonal projection, with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, if and only if all
\tau_{F,\ell}
are completely mixed states on their supports. This is equivalent to
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
being unital.

Similarly, we have that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
is self-adjoint with respect to the modified inner products

\left<X,Y\right>_{\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}}={\rm tr}(X^{\dagger}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}Y\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}})
with
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\cdot):=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

IV-B

Unital factorizations of conditonal expectations and their duals

Leveraging the Wedderburn decomposition of
\mathscr{A}
we can reduce the size of the representation of a
*
-subalgebra by avoiding the repeated blocks. In particular, given an algebra
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with Wedderburn decomposition as in (
5
) we can observe that it is isomorphic to
\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}=\bigoplus_{\ell}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,\ell})
,
\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
with
m=\sum_{\ell}\dim(\mathcal{H}_{S,\ell})
.
We next show that this reduction in representation is possible using CP unital or CPTP maps that factorize the conditional expectation or its dual, respectively. This result, albeit simple, is key to our aim, as it allows us to construct reductions of CP dynamics that remain CP.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathscr{B}
be an unital
*
-subalgebra with decomposition as in (
5
). Define
\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}=\bigoplus_{\ell}\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,\ell}).
Then for any conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
and state extension
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
exist (non-square) factorizations:

\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\mathcal{J}_{0}\circ\mathcal{R}_{0},\quad\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{R}

(9)

where
\mathcal{J}_{0}:\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}\rightarrow\mathscr{A},

\mathcal{R}_{0}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
are CP and unital, and
\mathcal{J}:\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{A}),

\mathcal{R}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
are CPTP, so that for any
X\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{H})
,
\rho\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
it holds:

{\rm tr}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}(X)\rho)={\rm tr}(\mathcal{R}_{0}(X)\mathcal{R}(\rho))={\rm tr}(X\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}(\rho)).

(10)

Proof.

Define the CP unital linear map
\Phi:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
as:

\Phi(A)=\Phi\left(U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}A_{S,\ell}\otimes I_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger}\right)=\bigoplus_{\ell}A_{S,\ell}.

Defining
V_{\ell}
as in the previous subsection, we can extend the action of
\Phi
to the full space as:

\Phi(X)=\bigoplus_{\ell}\frac{{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}(V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger})}{\dim(\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell})}.

Similarly, for any
\widecheck{A}\in\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
,
\widecheck{A}=\bigoplus_{\ell}A_{S,\ell},
we have

\Phi^{-1}\left(\widecheck{A}\right)=\Phi^{-1}\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}A_{S,\ell}\right)=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}\widecheck{A}_{S,\ell}\otimes I_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger}.

so that
\Phi^{-1}\Phi=\mathcal{I}_{\mathscr{A}}
, the identity super operator over the algebra. Notice that also
\Phi^{-1}
is unital.
We can then exploit this reduction in our problem by noting that

\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\Phi^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}},

and define the following (non-square) unital factorization for
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}
:

\mathcal{R}_{0}=\Phi\circ\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}},\quad\mathcal{J}_{0}=\Phi^{-1},

so that
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\mathcal{J}_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}.
Unitality can be verified directly from the definition of
\Phi
and its inverse.
In block decomposition, with the notation introduced above, we have:

\mathcal{R}_{0}(X)=\bigoplus_{\ell}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}\left(I_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}(V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger})\right).

We can use a similar representation for
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
as well, which follows directly from duality. In this case we use the dual, CPTP maps

\mathcal{R}(X)=\mathcal{J}_{0}^{\dagger}(X)=\Phi^{-{\dagger}}(X)=\bigoplus_{\ell}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,\ell}}(V_{\ell}XV_{\ell}^{\dagger})

(11)

and for some
\widecheck{A}=\bigoplus_{\ell}A_{S,\ell}
we have

\mathcal{J}(\widecheck{A})=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\dagger}(\widecheck{A})=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}A_{S,\ell}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger}.

(12)

The last equation follows from
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\mathbb{J}|^{\dagger}_{\mathscr{A}}
and the factorization we just defined.
∎

Notice that the explicit form of the
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{J}
is derived in the proof, see (
11
),(
12
).
Leveraging the the latter, we can obtain the reduced QHM model as described in the following instrumental result.

Proposition 3
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
be a QHM model and
\mathscr{B}\subseteq\mathscr{A}
be an algebra that allows for a state extension
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}
, factorized as
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R},
such that

\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}[\rho_{0}]

(13)

for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
. Then, the reduced QHM
(\widecheck{\mathscr{B}},\mathscr{Y},\widecheck{\mathcal{A}},\widecheck{\mathcal{C}},\widecheck{\mathfrak{S}})
with
\widecheck{\mathscr{B}}={\rm Im}(\mathcal{R})
,
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{R}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{J}
,
\widecheck{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{J}
and
\widecheck{\mathfrak{S}}=\mathcal{R}\mathfrak{S}
, along with
\Phi=\mathcal{R}
solves Problem
1
.

Proof.

Let us start by proving that the reduced model is a QHM model. From Theorem
1
we have that
\widecheck{\mathscr{B}}
is a
*
-algebra and the maps
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{R}
are CPTP. Then, since
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{J}
are CPTP,
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}
is also CPTP and
\widecheck{\mathfrak{S}}
is a set of density operators. Recalling then that
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}_{\widecheck{\mathscr{B}}}
the identity super operator over
\widecheck{\mathscr{B}}
, we have that
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{J})^{t}\mathcal{R}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}[\rho_{0}]
for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
and

proving that this is a solution for Problem
1
with
\Phi=\mathcal{R}
.
∎

This result allows us to focus on finding an algebra
\mathscr{B}
, that allows for a state extension
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{B}}
such that equation (
13
) holds.

The core idea follows naturally from the last results and two observations: (i) the reachable space
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
represent subspaces that support reductions for the dynamics; (ii) composing a CPTP injection map with a CPTP dynamics and a CPTP reduction map amounts to a reduced CPTP dynamics. What we need then is to close the reachable subspace, or the orthogonal to the non-observable subspace, to suitable algebras that admit a state extensions, which we can factorize in a CPTP injection and reduction pair.
We are then left with one open problem: how to construct an algebra so that it allows for an exact reduction, admits a state extension, and it is the minimal one having such properties. This will be the focus of the next section.

V-A

Distorted algebras and why we need them

We noticed above that
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})
contains a full-rank state

\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{\ell}\frac{I_{S,\ell}}{d_{S,\ell}}\otimes\tau_{F,\ell}\right)U^{\dagger},

with a block structure that is compatible with that of
\mathscr{A}.

For any positive-definite
Y
define the symmetry-preserving multiplicative operator
\mathcal{D}_{Y}(X)=Y^{\frac{1}{2}}XY^{\frac{1}{2}}.
By comparing the block representations (
7
) and (
8
) with the one of
\rho
, we then have that

\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}\circ\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}\circ\mathcal{D}_{\rho^{-1}},
and in particular if
A\in\mathscr{A}
then
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}(A)=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(A).

It follows that

{\rm Im}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})={\rm fix}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm Im}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{A}}))=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}).

Sets of this form correspond to fixed point sets of general CPTP maps - not just state extensions: in fact for any CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
there is a state extension
\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T+1}\sum_{i=0}^{T}\mathcal{E}^{i}
that projects onto its fixed points.

A set of the form
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A})
with
\rho\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{H})
is also called

\rho
-distorted algebra

[
45
,
46
,
47
]
. It is immediate (through the block-decomposition representation and using the properties of tensor product) to verify that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A})
is closed with respect to linear combination, adjoint, and the weighed product
X\cdot_{\rho}Y=X\rho^{-1}Y.

Let us fix some notation: for a subset
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we denote by
{{\rm alg}}\,\mathcal{S}
the minimal
*
-subalgebra that contains the set
\mathcal{S},
and similarly by
{{\rm alg}}_{\rho}\,\mathcal{S}
the minimal
\rho
-distorted algebra that contains
\mathcal{S}
, closed with respect to the modified product
\cdot_{\rho}
induced by
\rho
.

In a noncommutative setting, not all distorted algebras are images of state extensions: Takesaki’s work
[
36
]
employs modular theory to characterize “distortion” states for which this is indeed the case. Our reduction strategy hinges on the existence of a CPTP projection onto a distorted algebra, so we need to determine a valid density operator with respect to which the relevant operator subspace can be closed to a distorted algebra, but not only: we want the state to produce the
minimal
such distorted algebra. To the best of the authors knowledge this is an open problem.

But do we really need to consider distorted algebras with respect to general states? Given a set a generators
\cal S
,
{\rm alg}({\cal S})
is indeed a distorted algebra with respect to the completely mixed state, and as such admits a state extension. The following example shows how the choice of this algebra may lead to non-minimal reductions.

Example 1
.

Consider a CPTP map
\mathcal{A}
with a fixed point
\rho
, i.e.
\mathcal{A}(\rho)=\rho
. Consider as an initial condition
\mathfrak{S}=\{\rho\}
. The reachable space generated by the model
\rho(t+1)=\mathcal{A}(\rho(t))
is then
\mathscr{R}={\rm span}\{\rho\}
. When computing
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{R})
we obtain
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{R})={\rm span}\{\Pi_{i}\}
where
\Pi_{i}
are orthogonal projectors onto the eigenspaces of
\rho
and thus
\dim({\rm alg}(\mathscr{R}))
is the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\rho
.

However, we can observe that choosing
\widecheck{\rho}(0)=1
,
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}=1
and
\mathcal{J}(\widecheck{\rho})=\rho\widecheck{\rho}
we have that the model
\widecheck{\rho}(t+1)=\widecheck{A}[\widecheck{\rho}(t)]
and
\rho(t)=\mathcal{J}(\widecheck{\rho}(t))
with initial condition
\widecheck{\rho}(0)=1
, provides the correct trajectory at all times, i.e.
\rho(t)=\rho
for all
t\geq 0
, and, since the dimension of this model is 1, it must also be minimal. This shows that, although closing
\mathscr{R}
to an algebra provides a reduced quantum model, it is possible that that model is not minimal.

The rest of the section is going to be devoted to finding a distorted algebra that contains a given operator subspace so that it leads to an optimal reduction. This is attained by leveraging a number of existing and new results on conditional expectations.

V-B

Compatibility of states and algebras

The following proposition provides both a way to construct distorted algebras and an intuitive connection between algebras and distorted algebras.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a set and let
\rho\in\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{H})
be an Hermitian operator with full support, i.e.
{\rm supp}(\rho)=\mathcal{H}
.
Then

{{\rm alg}}_{\rho}(\mathcal{S})=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{S}))).

Moreover, if
{\rm supp}(\mathcal{S})=\mathcal{H}
, then
\rho\in{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

For convenience, let us define,
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}:={\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathcal{S})
,
\mathscr{A}=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{A}_{\rho})
and, on the right hand side
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}:={\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{S}))
,
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}:=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\widetilde{\mathscr{A}})
. By definition, we have that
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
is a
*
-algebra, and
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
is a
\rho
-distorted
*
-algebra.

We start by showing that
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
is also a
\rho
-distorted
*
-algebra and that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
.
Consider then
X,Y\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. We have:

•

\alpha X+\beta Y\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
, trivially from linearity of operator spaces;

•

X^{\dagger}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
, since
X=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
for some
\overline{X}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
and thus
X^{\dagger}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}^{\dagger}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
, since
\overline{X}^{\dagger}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
;

•

X\cdot_{\rho}Y\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
since also
Y=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
for some
\overline{Y}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
and
X\cdot_{\rho}Y=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}\cancel{\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{-1}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}}\overline{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}\overline{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
since
\overline{X}\overline{Y}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
;

•

any sequence of linear combinations, adjoints, and multiplications of elements in
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
must also be in
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
.

This proves that
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
is a
\rho
-distorted
*
-algebra. Moreover, by observing that
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{S})\supseteq\mathcal{S}
by definition, we have that
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\supseteq\mathcal{D}_{\rho^{-1}}(\mathcal{S})
and thus
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}\supseteq\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho^{-1}}(\mathcal{S}))=\mathcal{S}
. We thus have that
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
is a
\rho
-distorted
*
-agebra that contains
\mathcal{S}
. Since, by definition,
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathcal{S})
is the smallest
\rho
-distorted
*
-algebra that contains
\mathcal{S}
we have proven that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\rho}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
.

We shall notice that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\cdot)
is invertible and hence proving that
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}=\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
is equivalent to prove
\mathscr{A}=\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
. Then, to prove
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}\supseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
we can show that
\mathscr{A}
is also a
*
-algebra that contains
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{S})
and, since by definition we have that
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
is the smallest
*
-algebra that contains
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{S})
, this implies that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\subseteq\mathscr{A}
and hence
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
.

The proof now follows closely the steps of the first part of the proof. Consider
X,Y\in\mathscr{A}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. Then we have:

•

\alpha X+\beta Y\in\mathscr{A}
by the linearity of the operator spaces;

•

X^{\dagger}\in\mathscr{A}
since
X=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for some
\overline{X}\in\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
and thus
X^{\dagger}=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}^{\dagger}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathscr{A}
since
\overline{X}^{\dagger}\in\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
;

•

XY\in\mathscr{A}
since

for some
\overline{Y}\in\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
and
XY=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{Y}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{X}\cdot_{\rho}\overline{Y}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathscr{A}
since
\overline{X}\cdot_{\rho}\overline{Y}\in\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
;

•

any sequence of sums, multiplications and adjoints of elements in
\mathscr{A}
is in
\mathscr{A}
as well.

This proves that
\mathscr{A}
is a
*
-algebra. Now, observing that
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathcal{S})\supseteq\mathcal{S}
by definition, we have that
\mathscr{A}\supseteq\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathcal{S})
and hence, for the argument above, it holds
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
and thus combining the two inclusions, we have
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\rho}=\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{\rho}
and
\mathscr{A}=\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
.

To conclude the proof we can observe that

, (see e.g.

) and

and thus

.
∎

We next need to introduce a new concept, that captures density operators and algebras sharing compatible block-diagonal structures.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an algebra with decomposition:

\mathscr{A}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})_{S,l}\otimes I_{F,l}\oplus\mathbb{0}_{R}\right)U^{\dagger}.

(14)

We say that
\rho\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
is compatible with
\mathscr{A},
and write
\rho{\sim}\mathscr{A},
if
\rho
has block-structure:

\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\oplus\mathbb{0}_{R}\right)U^{\dagger},

(15)

where
\rho_{S,l}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
, and
\tau_{F,l}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{F,l})
.

By extension, we say that
\rho
is compatible with a
\sigma
-distorted algebra
\mathscr{A}_{\sigma}
and write
\rho{\sim}\mathscr{A}_{\sigma}
if it is compatible with the corresponding un-distorted algebra, i.e.
\rho{\sim}\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{A}_{\sigma})
.

Before we continue, let us recall some fundamental facts about algebras and their commutants. Given an algebra
\mathscr{A}
, its commutant is defined as
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})|[X,A]=0,\,\forall A\in\mathscr{A}\}
, while its center is defined as
\mathcal{Z}(\mathscr{A})=\mathscr{A}\cap\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
. Moreover, given the Wedderburn decomposition of
\mathscr{A}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
, we have that its commutant can be decomposed as
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}I_{S,l}\otimes\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{F,l})\right)U^{\dagger}
and its center
\mathcal{Z}(\mathscr{A})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathbb{R}_{l}I_{S,l}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
.

With these observations we have that, given an algebra
\mathscr{A}
, any
\rho\in\mathscr{A}
,
\sigma\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
and thus, in particular,
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathscr{A})=\mathscr{A}\cap\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
are such that
\rho{\sim}\mathscr{A}
,
\sigma{\sim}\mathscr{A}
and
\xi{\sim}\mathscr{A}
. The proof follows by comparing the block structures of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
.

The notion of compatible operators helps us harness the block structure provided by the Wedderburn decomposition and is of fundamental importance because it provides an intuitive interpretation of the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a CPTP projection on a distorted algebra provided by Takesaki theorem in the finite-dimensional case. This connection is explicitly proved next.

Theorem 2

(Takesaki theorem and compatibility)
.

Let
\rho
be a full-rank positive-definite operator,
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
a unital
*
-algebra and
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A})
a
\rho
-distorted algebra. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

There exists a state extension
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
such that its image is
{\rm Im}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})={\rm fix}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})=\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
;

2.

\rho{\sim}\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Takesaki theorem
[
48
]
states that 1) above is equivalent to
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
being invariant for the modular action defined as
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\cdot):=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}_{\rho})\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
. Its proof in the finite-dimensional case can be found in
[
38
, Theorem 9.2]
and

[
47
, Theorem 3 and 4]
.

We first show that compatibility, i.e. condition 2), implies
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}_{\rho})\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{\rho}.

Assume
\mathscr{A}
has Wedderburn decomposition
\mathscr{A}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
: thus
\rho{\sim}\mathscr{A}
implies
\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A})

\displaystyle=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\rho_{F,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\tau_{F,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}I_{F,l}\tau_{F,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)U^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\subseteq U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}=\mathscr{A},

and thus
\mathscr{A}
is
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
-invariant.

To conclude, we prove that 1) implies 2). By hypothesis we have that there exists a CPTP map
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}
, such that
{\rm Im}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})={\rm fix}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})=\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
. From
[
44
, Proposition 1.5]
and equation (
8
), we than have that such map has the form

\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}}(X)=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{F,l}}(\Pi_{l}X\Pi_{l})\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}.

Hence, its image has the structure
{\rm Im}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{A}})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}=\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
. From Proposition
4
we have that
\rho\in\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
, and thus
\rho
must have the block structure
\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
and is thus compatible with
\mathscr{A}=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{A}_{\rho})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
.
∎

Now that we have shown how the notion of compatibility with an algebra of the “distortion” state
\rho
is equivalent to the existence of a CPTP projection onto the corresponding distorted algebra, we want to construct states
\rho\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
that, given an operator space
\mathscr{V}
, guarantee that the distorted algebra
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
is compatible with
\rho
itself, i.e.
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
.
To this aim, we first prove the following instrumental result.

Lemma 1
.

Let consider
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
if and only if
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

The “if” implication is trivial since any operator
\rho
is compatible with
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
. We now prove the opposite implication by contradiction. Let us assume that
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))\subsetneq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then it must admit a nontrivial decomposition,
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))=U(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{m_{l}})U^{\dagger}
, meaning that either it admits at least two orthogonal components (
l
) or one of the identity factors is of dimension greater than one. Thus all
X\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
can be written as
X=U(\bigoplus_{l}X_{S,l}\otimes I_{m_{l}})U^{\dagger}
. Moreover, from assumptions we have that
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
and thus
\rho
has the form
\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. Then, since by definition we have
\mathscr{V}\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
, we can say that for the basis elements
\mathscr{V}={\rm span}\{V_{i}\}
we can write
V_{i}=U(\bigoplus_{l}V_{S,l}^{i}\otimes I_{m_{l}})U^{\dagger}
with
V_{S,l}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
for all
l
. However, because of the block-diagonal structure of
\{V_{i}\}
and the fact that addition, multiplication, and adjoint action leave the block-diagonal structure invariant, we have that
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}{\rm alg}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\}_{i}\otimes{\rm alg}(\tau_{F,l})\right)U^{\dagger}
where
{\rm alg}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\}_{i}:={\rm alg}\{\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}X_{S,l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
. This, however, implies that
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})\subsetneq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, which is a contradiction.
∎

We are now ready to state a sufficient condition for an operator
\rho\in{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
to be compatible with the distorted algebra it generates
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider an operator space
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with full support and let
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
be the Wedderburn decomposition of
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
. Let us consider a positive-definite operator
\rho\in{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
. Then:

1.

\mathscr{V}\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
;

2.

We can write
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))=\bigoplus_{l}\mathscr{A}_{S,l}\otimes I_{F,l}

where
\mathscr{A}_{S,l}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
are (sub)algebras;

3.

\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
if and only if
\mathscr{A}_{S,l}=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})

\forall l
.

Proof.

If
\{V_{i}\}_{i=0,\dots}
is a basis for
\mathscr{V}
we have that
V_{i}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}V_{S,l}^{i}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
and
{\rm alg}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\}_{i=0,\dots}=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
for all
l
. Moreover, because we assumed that
\rho
is an operator in
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
, we also have that
\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
with
\rho_{S,l}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
,
\rho_{S,l}>0.
Then we have that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})={\rm span}\{W_{i}\}
with
W_{i}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}V_{S,l}^{i}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. Observing that linear combinations, products, and adjoints of elements that have the structure of
W_{i}
maintain the same block-diagonal structure, we can say that
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}{\rm alg}(\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}V_{S,l}^{i}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. Defining
\mathscr{A}_{S,l}:={\rm alg}(\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}V_{S,l}^{i}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
we conclude the proof of statement 2). By observing the block-diagonal structure of
\mathscr{A}
we have that
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
. Then, using Proposition
4
, i.e.
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})))
and the fact that
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathscr{A}_{S,l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}

with
\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{S,l}}(\mathscr{A}_{S,l})\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
for all
l
we can conclude that
\mathscr{V}\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
, proving statement 1).
To prove the third statement and conclude the proof we can observe that for every block
l
, we have
\mathscr{V}_{S,l}:={\rm span}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\}_{i}
such that
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}_{S,l})=\mathfrak{B}({\mathcal{H}_{S,l}})
and we can thus apply Lemma
1
on every block
\mathscr{A}_{S,l}
for all
l
.
∎

Remarkably, any (full-rank) operator
\rho\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
is guaranteed to be compatible with the algebra it generates. This is summarized in the following Corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Consider an operator space
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with full support. Let us consider a positive-definite operator in the center of
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
,
\rho\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
. Then it holds that
\mathscr{V}\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
and
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3
, we have
\mathscr{V}\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
, since
\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
. To prove the fact that
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
we can simply observe that for
\rho\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
we have that in the block decomposition
\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
it must be
\rho_{S,l}=\lambda_{l}I_{S,l}
with
\lambda_{l}\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies that
\mathscr{A}_{S,l}:={\rm alg}(I_{S,l}V_{S,l}^{i}I_{S,l})=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
for all
l
and thus satisfies the third condition of Theorem
3
.
∎

This corollary shows that picking an operator
\rho
in the center
\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
not only guarantees that the distorted algebra it generates allows for a CPTP projection but also provides a smaller or equal algebra with respect to
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
thus allowing for a larger reduction. This is also shown in the following example.

Example 2
.

Let
\sigma_{i}
with
i\in\{x,y,z\}
indicate the Pauli matrices and
\sigma_{0}
indicate the identity.
Let us define a positive-definite operator
\xi=2\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{z}
and consider the following operator space
\mathscr{V}={\rm span}\{\xi\otimes\sigma_{x},\xi\otimes\sigma_{y},\xi\otimes\sigma_{z}\}
. Then
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})={\rm span}\{\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{i},\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{i},\,i=0,x,y,z\}\simeq\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
, with
\dim({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))=8
and we can observe that
\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))={\rm span}\{\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{0},\,j=0,z\}
.

Let us then consider
\rho=\xi\otimes\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
, then we have that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})={\rm span}\{\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{x},\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{y},\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{z}\}
. This implies that
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))={\rm span}\{\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{i},\,i=0,x,y,z\}=I_{2}\otimes\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
and thus
\dim(\mathscr{A}_{\rho})=4
and
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
is
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
-invariant or, in other words,
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
.

V-C

Optimal distorted algebras

Up to this point, we only showed that choosing
\rho\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
generates a distorted algebra that allows for a CPTP state extension, but one could ask if other states
\xi\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
are compatible with the algebra they generate, i.e.
\xi{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\xi}(\mathscr{V}),
and they provide smaller distorted algebras with respect to the ones in
\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
. We now show that this is not possible and that the optimal reduction is obtained through an operator
\rho
in the center
\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
. To this aim, we first need the following preliminary result.

Lemma 2
.

Consider an operator space
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with full support, an positive-definite operator
\rho\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
and consider also another positive-definite operator
\sigma\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
. If
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\sigma)\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
then
{\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})\right)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left({\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\right).

Proof.

Let us define a basis for
\mathscr{V}:={\rm span}\{V_{i}\}
. Since
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\cdot)
is a linear and invertible map, we have that
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}):={\rm span}\{\widehat{V}_{i}\}
, where
\widehat{V}_{i}=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}{V}_{i}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Similarly, we have that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})={\rm span}\{\widetilde{V}_{i}\}
where
\widetilde{V}_{i}=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}V_{i}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
or, equivalently,
V_{i}=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{V}_{i}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Combining the two we obtain

\widehat{V}_{i}=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{V}_{i}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. By linearity of the operator space we have that the same holds for every element in
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
, i.e. for every
\widehat{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
, there exist
\widetilde{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
such that
\widehat{X}=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{X}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Let us then denote
\widetilde{\sigma}:=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\sigma)
, or equivalently
\sigma=\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\sigma}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}
, from hypothesis we have that
\widetilde{\sigma}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
and also
\widetilde{\sigma}^{-1}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
.

We proceed to prove that elements in
{\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})\right)
are contained in
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left({\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\right)=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})))\right)
:
first notice that
\widehat{X}^{{\dagger}}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
, where
\widehat{X}^{\dagger}=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{X}^{\dagger}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and thus, since
\widetilde{X}^{\dagger}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
, we have that
\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})))\right)
.
Let then consider a second operator
\widehat{Y}\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
, for which
\widehat{Y}=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
where
\widetilde{Y}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
. We then have that
\widehat{X}+\widehat{Y}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
where, by linearity,
\widehat{X}+\widehat{Y}=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}(\widetilde{X}+\widetilde{Y})\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and thus, since
\widetilde{X}+\widetilde{Y}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
it holds that
\widehat{X}+\widehat{Y}\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})))\right)
.

Finally, we can consider the product

\displaystyle\widehat{X}\widehat{Y}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{X}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{X}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-1}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{X}\cancel{\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\widetilde{\sigma}^{-1}\cancel{\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}}\widetilde{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{X}\widetilde{\sigma}^{-1}\widetilde{Y}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

We can thus notice that
\widehat{X}\widehat{Y}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
and, since, by assumptions
\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}\in\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
and
\widetilde{\sigma}^{-1}\in{\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})\right)
, we have
\widetilde{X}\widetilde{\sigma}^{-1}\widetilde{Y}\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
and thus
\widehat{X}\widehat{Y}\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}({\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})))\right)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

An example of operators for which this last lemma holds is
\sigma\in\mathscr{V}
. We see next that this also holds for
\sigma\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
and that this lemma is fundamental to prove the next key result.

Theorem 4

(Minimal compatible distorted algebra)
.

Consider an operator subspace
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and a positive-definite operator
V\in\mathscr{V}
,
V=V^{\dagger}>0
.Define

\sigma:=\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))}\left[V\right]\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))}
is the orthogonal projection onto the center
{\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))}
. Then,
{\rm alg}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{V})
is the minimal distorted algebra that contains
\mathscr{V}
and admits a state extension.

Proof.

Consider a full rank
\rho.
Being
\mathscr{A}={\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
a
*
-algebra, it admits a Wedderburn decomposition:
\mathscr{A}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. Let
\{V_{i}\}
be a set of generators for
\mathscr{V}
, and for all
i
define
W_{i}=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(V_{i}).
From the structure of
\mathscr{A}
we also have that
W_{i}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}W_{S,l}^{i}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger},
and that
{\rm alg}\{W_{S,l}^{i}\}=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})
for all
l
. Moreover, assuming
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))
we have that
\rho
has the structure
\rho=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. Combining these two observations we have
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}.

By definition, we have that
\mathscr{V}\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
. This, with the structure of the basis of
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})
we just described, implies that for the basis elements
\mathscr{V}={\rm span}\{V_{i}\}
we can write

V_{i}=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\underbrace{\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}W_{S,l}^{i}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}}_{=:V_{S,l}^{i}}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}.

We can then compute the algebra
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})
. Since the
V_{i}
share a common block-diagonal structure, and the latter is invariant for sum, multiplication and adjoint, we have that
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{V})=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}{\rm alg}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\}_{i}\otimes{\rm alg}\{\tau_{F,l}\}\right)U^{\dagger}
. It follows that its center has the following structure:
\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\})\otimes\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}\{\tau_{F,l}\})\right)U^{\dagger}
.

Thus any
V\in\mathscr{V}
is of the form
V=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}V_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
and, since
\tau_{F,l}\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\tau_{F,l}))
, we have

\sigma=\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))}[V]=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\sigma_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}

where
\sigma_{S,l}\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}\{V_{S,l}^{i}\})
. Moreover, we have that

\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\sigma)\in{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})),
since

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{S,l}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\tau_{F,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tau_{F,l}\tau_{F,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)U^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{S,l}\rho_{S,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}

and
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}
. This shows that
\sigma
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
2
and we are guaranteed that

{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})).

(16)

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left({\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\right)=

\displaystyle=U\left(\bigoplus_{l}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{S,l}\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\rho_{S,l}^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{S,l}\otimes\tau_{F,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tau_{F,l}\tau_{F,l}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)U^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=U\left(\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S,l})\otimes I_{F,l}\right)U^{\dagger}={\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})).

(17)

Combining (
16
) and (
17
) we have that
for any
\rho{\sim}{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V}),
we have
{\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})\right)\subseteq{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{V}))=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left({\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})\right)
. Applying
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
on both sides of the previous equation we get:

\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}\left({\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{V})\right)\right)\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V}),
which by Proposition
4
is equivalent to
{\rm alg}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{V})\subseteq{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V}),
for any
\rho
. This implies that
{\rm alg}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{V})
is the minimal distorted algebra containing
\mathscr{V}.

∎

It follows from the proof of the above Theorem that the choice of the operator
V
in the statement does not affect the algebra we obtain. Moreover, while the choice above yields the optimal (minimal) distorted algebra containing
\mathscr{V}
that admits a CPTP projection, it is possible that there exists an operator
\rho\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
is smaller in dimension with respect to all
{\rm alg}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{V})
with
\sigma\in\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{V}))
. However, such
{\rm alg}_{\rho}(\mathscr{V})
would not admit a CPTP projection (state extension) and could not be used to obtain a CPTP reduced model via conditional expectations.

VI
Reductions to QHM models

The algorithm we propose is divided into two steps, each providing a solution to Problem
1
, but the solution provided by a single step is optimal only under certain conditions that we will discuss later. In the following subsections, we will start by discussing one step at a time and then show how to combine the two steps to obtain the full algorithm.

Remark
.

In the remainder of this work, we will only focus on algebras with full support. This is always possible since, if computing an algebra one finds that it does not have full support, one can always first restrict the QHM models and their analysis onto the support of the algebra itself. In fact, the set of operators over the support of the algebra is a
*
-algebra that allows for a state extension.

VI-A

Projection onto the reachable algebra

First, we need to define two objects that play a key role in the algorithm and in the subsequent Theorem. The state
\overline{\rho}
is defined as the weighted sum of the trajectories that generate the reachable space, i.e.

\overline{\rho}:=\frac{1}{|\mathfrak{S}|n^{2}}\sum_{\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}}\sum_{t=0}^{n^{2}}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]

(18)

where
n=\dim(\mathcal{H})
. Let us then denote
\mathcal{Z}_{R}:=\mathcal{Z}({\rm alg}(\mathscr{R}))
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}
the projector onto the center
\mathcal{Z}_{R}
. The projection of
\overline{\rho}
onto
\mathcal{Z}_{R}
is then denoted
\sigma:=\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}[\overline{\rho}]
.

We next introduce the
reachable algebra
,
\mathscr{D}={\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{R})\right)
. Recall that due to Theorem
5
the reachable algebra
\mathscr{D}
, does not depend on the state
\sigma
as long as
\sigma
is chosen as the projection onto the center
\mathcal{Z}
of a full support state.

We now present Algorithm
1
, which provides a partial solution to Problem
1
, and prove that the reachable algebra is the minimal algebra that allows the reduced model to reproduce the state dynamics for all possible observables.

Algorithm 1

Projection onto the reachable algebra.

Theorem 5

(
Reduction on the reachable algebra).

Consider the QHM model
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
and let
\mathscr{R}
be its reachable space. Let
\sigma
be as above.

Then the reachable algebra
\mathscr{D}={\rm alg}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathscr{R})\right)
is the smallest algebra that allows for a state extension
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}
onto
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{D})
, and such that for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
and for all
t\geq 0
we have,

\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\rho_{0}].

(19)

Proof.

We divide the proof into three parts:
i)
In the first part we prove that the assumption
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
is sufficient for equation (
19
) to hold for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
;
ii)
In the second part we prove that the same condition is also necessary;
iii)
Lastly, we prove that the dynamical algebra is minimal.

i)
We prove the first step by showing that, for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
, both sides of equation (
19
) are equivalent to
(\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}})^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]
, where
\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathscr{R}
. Starting from the left side of equation (
19
) we can observe, from the definition of
\mathscr{R}
, that
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
and hence
\rho_{0}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]
for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
. Moreover, from the definition of
\mathscr{R}
we have that
\mathscr{R}
is
\mathcal{A}
-invariant, hence
\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=(\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}})^{t}
. Combining these two observations proves the first equality:
\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{A}^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]=(\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}})^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]

Assume now that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
. Observing that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
we obtain
(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=(\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}{\Pi}_{\mathscr{R}})^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
. Through similar calculations, we then obtain the second equality
(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\rho_{0}]=(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]=(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]=(\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{A}{\Pi}_{\mathscr{R}})^{t}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]
.

ii)
Up to this point, we proved that the condition
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
is sufficient for equation (
19
) to hold at any time and any initial condition in
\mathfrak{S}
. However, it is also possible to prove that such a condition is also necessary. Assume that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}
is a projector onto a subspace such that equation (
19
) holds for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
. First of all, we can observe that requiring that equation (
19
) holds for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
is equivalent to requiring the same for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
. From the definition of
\mathscr{R}
we have that
\mathscr{R}\supseteq\mathfrak{S}
and hence it comes trivially that if equation (
19
) holds for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
it holds in particular for
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
. To prove the opposite implication we can proceed by contradiction. Assume that equation (
19
) holds for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
but there exist
\tau\in\mathscr{R}
and
t\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{A}^{t}[\tau]\neq(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\tau]
. Since
\tau\in\mathscr{R}
, there exists a set
\{\lambda_{k,\rho_{0}}\}
such that
\tau=\sum_{k}\sum_{\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}}\lambda_{k,\rho_{0}}\mathcal{A}^{k}[\rho_{0}]
. But then we have
\mathcal{A}^{t}[\tau]=\sum_{k}\sum_{\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}}\lambda_{k,\rho_{0}}\mathcal{A}^{t+k}[\rho_{0}]
and, on the other hand

\displaystyle(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\tau]=

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k}\sum_{\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}}\lambda_{k,\rho_{0}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\underbrace{\mathcal{A}^{k}[\rho_{0}]}_{(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{k}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\rho_{0}]}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k}\sum_{\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}}\lambda_{k,\rho_{0}}\underbrace{(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t+k}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\rho_{0}]}_{\mathcal{A}^{t+k}[\rho_{0}]}

which leads to an absurd.

We can then study equation (
19
) at
t=0
for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
, obtaining
(\mathcal{I}-\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})[\rho_{0}]=0
for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
. Equivalently we can write
(\mathcal{I}-\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}[\rho_{0}]=(\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}-\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}})[\rho_{0}]=0
for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
, which is true if and only if
\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}-\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=0
. This proves that a necessary condition for
\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\rho_{0}]
to hold is that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
, or, in other words,
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}
needs to act as the identity over the reachable space, i.e.
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})\supseteq\mathscr{R}
.

iii)
Finally, let us notice that, given
\overline{\rho}
as defined in equation (
18
) we have that
{\rm supp}(\overline{\rho})={\rm supp}(\mathscr{R})
. Then, from Theorem
3
with
\sigma=\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}[\overline{\rho}]
, we know that
\mathscr{D}_{\sigma}:=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{D})
, is the smallest distorted algebra that contains
\mathscr{R}
and that allows for a CPTP state extension onto it.
Moreover, we have that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}
acts as the identity on
\mathscr{D}_{\sigma}
and, since
\mathscr{D}_{\sigma}\supseteq\mathscr{R}
,
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}
acts as the identity on
\mathscr{R}
as well, hence
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}=\Pi_{\mathscr{R}}
. From the previous two points then we have that equation (
19
) holds for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
hence the outputs are also equivalent, i.e.
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{D}}[\rho_{0}]
for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
and thus the reduced model solves Problem
1
.
∎

To show that Algorithm
1
provides a suitable QHM model that solves Problem
1
it is then sufficient to combine the results of Theorem
5
and Proposition
3
.

We proved that the reduced model is a valid QHM model and that it satisfies stronger conditions than the one required by Problem (
1
), i.e. the reduced model provided by Algorithm
1
reproduces the full state dynamics (not only the output one), starting from any condition in
\mathscr{R}
(not only
\mathfrak{S}
).

On the other hand, notice that the reduced model is not in general guaranteed to reproduce the correct state dynamics or output starting from
any
initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{D})
. This is because the extension of the reachable state to an algebra might include some non-reachable densities.

Algorithm 2

Projection onto the observable algebra.

We next prove that Algorithm
2
not only provides a suitable QHM model that solves Problem
1
, but also that the observable algebra is the smallest algebra that allows the reduced model to reproduce the output dynamics for any initial condition.

The proof of this theorem is more involved than the proof of Theorem
5
, because in this case we have the freedom to choose over the notion of orthogonality with respect to which we construct the complement of
\mathscr{N}
. Nonetheless, we are able to show that the natural orthogonality notion provides the optimal solution.

Theorem 6

(
Reduction on the observable algebra).

Let
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
be a QHM model and consider its non-observable subspace
\mathscr{N}.
The observable algebra
\mathscr{O}={\rm alg}(\mathscr{N}^{\perp})
is the smallest algebra that allows for a state extension
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}
such that for all
t\geq 0
, and for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
we have

\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}].

(20)

Proof.

Recall that any choice of modified inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
induces an orthogonal complement of
\mathscr{N}
, denoted with
\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
, i.e.
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})=\mathscr{N}\bigoplus_{\mathcal{S}}\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and a projection
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
onto
\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
, which is
\mathcal{S}
-orthogonal. The latter is such that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}+\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}=\mathcal{I}
, the identity super operator, where
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}
is the
\mathcal{S}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathscr{N}
.

The proof is divided into three parts:
i)
In the first part we prove that a sufficient condition for equation (
20
) to hold is that there exists
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
such that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
;
ii)
In the second part we prove that the same condition is also necessary;

iii)
Lastly, we prove that the condition
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
is actually equivalent to
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})\supseteq\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
, for all choices of
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
and that
\mathscr{O}
is minimal.

i)
Let us start by proving that, for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, for any choice of
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
, we have
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}}(\widehat{\Pi}_{{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}})^{t}\widehat{\Pi}_{{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}}[\rho_{0}]
. From the definition of
\mathscr{N}
we have that
\mathscr{N}\subseteq\ker\mathcal{C}
, hence

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}+\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}})=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}+\cancel{\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}}=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}.

Moreover,
\mathscr{N}
is
\mathcal{A}
-invariant, i.e.
\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}
, hence,

\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}+\cancel{\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}

and similarly, by iterating the calculation above

\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}^{t}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}^{t-1}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}})^{t}.

From these, we obtain:
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]
.

Assume than that there exists
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
such that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
. Then, through similar calculations, we can notice that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}+\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}})\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}+\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}+\cancel{\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{N}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
and iterating for multiple steps we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}})^{t}.

It thus follows that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}[\rho_{0}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}[\rho_{0}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}[\rho_{0}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{A}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]

for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, concluding the first part of the proof.

ii)
We now want to prove that if equation (
20
) holds for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
then
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
. We start by proving that requiring equation (
20
) to hold for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
t\geq 0
is equivalent to require that
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{K}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]
holds for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
t\geq 0
and for all output maps
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathscr{Y}
such that
\ker\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
. The fact that this implies equation (
20
) comes from the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is one particular output map that satisfies
\ker\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
. To prove the opposite, we proceed by contradiction. Assume that
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{C}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]
holds for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
t\geq 0
but there exist
t\geq 0
,
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{K}
such that
\ker\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
for which
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]\neq\mathcal{K}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]
. Let us recall that the output map
\mathcal{C}
can be written as
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}E_{i}\left<C_{i},\cdot\right>_{HS}
for a set
\{C_{i}\}\subseteq\mathscr{A}
and for an orthonormal basis
\{E_{i}\}\subseteq\mathscr{Y}
. Similarly, we can write
\mathcal{K}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}E_{i}\left<K_{i},\cdot\right>_{HS}
. We can then notice:

•

\mathscr{N}^{\perp}={\rm span}\{\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}t}[C_{i}],\,t\geq 0\}
;

•

Necessary and sufficient condition for
\ker\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
to hold is that
{\rm span}\{K_{i}\}\subseteq\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
;

•

For simplicity, we can assume
\mathcal{K}
to be of the form
\mathcal{K}(\cdot)=E\left<K,\cdot\right>_{HS}
where
E\in\mathscr{Y}
and
K\in\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
.

•

K\in\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
implies that there exist a set of coefficients
\{\lambda_{i,k}\}
such that
K=\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}t}[C_{i}]
;

•

The fact that equation (
20
) holds implies that
\left<C_{i},\mathcal{A}^{t}[X]\right>_{HS}=\left<C_{i},\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[X]\right>_{HS}
or, in other words
\left<\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}t}[C_{i}],X\right>_{HS}=\left<(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}[C_{i}],X\right>_{HS}
, for all
C_{i}
, for all
t\geq 0
and
X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

With these observations, we then have that
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=E\left<K,\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]\right>=\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}E\left<\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}k}[C_{i}],\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]\right>=\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}E\left<C_{i},\mathcal{A}^{t+k}[\rho_{0}]\right>
and, on the other hand

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]=

\displaystyle\qquad=E\left<K,\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]\right>_{HS}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}E\left<\underbrace{\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}k}[C_{i}]}_{(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{k}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}[C_{i}]},\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]\right>

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}E\left<\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t+k}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}[C_{i}]}_{\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}t+k}[C_{i}]},\rho_{0}\right>_{HS}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i,k}\lambda_{i,k}E\left<C_{i},\mathcal{A}^{t+k}[\rho_{0}]\right>_{HS}.

Comparing the two equations we clearly have that
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{K}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]

which is a contradiction.

We can thus proceed to prove that if
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{K}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]
holds for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
t\geq 0
and for all output maps
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathscr{Y}
such that
\ker\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
then it implies that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
. Since if
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{A}^{t}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{K}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}(\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}})^{t}[\rho_{0}]
holds for any
\mathcal{K}
such that
\ker{\mathcal{K}}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
then, in particular it holds for
\mathcal{K}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
, for any choice of
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
. But then for
t=0
we have
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}[\rho_{0}]=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}[\rho_{0}]
for all
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, which holds true if and only if
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
, concluding the second step of the proof

iii)
We now prove that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
is equivalent to
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})\supseteq\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
for any choice of
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
. Applying the adjoint operation on both sides of
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
, we can obtain
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\dagger}
, where we recall that
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}
. From the orthogonality of
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
with respect to
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
, we have that
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{S}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{S}^{-1}
. Substituting this equality into
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{\dagger}
we obtain
\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{S}^{-1}=\mathcal{S}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{S}^{-1}
. Right-applying
\mathcal{S}
and left-applying
\mathcal{S}^{-1}
to both sides of the equation we obtain
\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S}\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
, which is equivalent to say that the super-operator
\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S}
acts as the identity over
{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
. Observe now that: a) the super-operator
\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S}
is a projector; b) as a projector
\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S}
acts as the identity over its image, hence its image is equal to the set of its fixed points; c) recalling that
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})=\mathscr{O}
we have
{\rm fix}(\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S})=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathscr{O}
. With this observations we can conclude that
\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S}
acts as the identity over
{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
if and only if
{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
is contained in
{\rm fix}(\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}}\mathcal{S})
, that is
\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathscr{O}\supseteq{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
or, equivalently
\mathscr{O}\supseteq\mathcal{S}{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
. We shall now notice that, for all
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathcal{S}}
, we have
{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
, since
X\in{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
if
\left<Y,X\right>_{\mathcal{S}}=0
for all
Y\in\mathscr{N}
and since
\left<Y,X\right>_{\mathcal{S}}=\left<Y,\mathcal{S}(X)\right>_{HS}
, by defining
Z=\mathcal{S}(X)
we have that
Z\in\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
and hence
X\in\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
. Including this relation into
\mathscr{O}\supseteq\mathcal{S}{\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{S}}}
, leads to
\mathscr{O}\supseteq\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
or, in other words
{\rm fix}(\mathbb{E}|_{\mathscr{O}})\supseteq\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
.

In order for
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}
to be a CPTP projection
\mathscr{O}
needs to be an algebra. In addition, for equation (
20
) to hold, as we showed in
iii)
,
\mathscr{O}
must contain
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}.
Hence, the smallest algebra that contains
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
and for which (
20
) holds is
\mathscr{O}={\rm alg}(\mathscr{N}^{\perp}).

∎

To prove that the reduced model generated by Algorithm
2
is a solution of our problem is then sufficient to combine the results of Theorem
6
and Proposition
3
.

Notice that any
\mathbb{J}|_{\mathscr{O}}
as in the Theorem statement projects onto a
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}(\mathscr{O}),
parametrized by a choice of
\sigma{\sim}\mathscr{O}
.
Notice also that in the proof of
6
we show that (
20
) holds for every initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
not only for the given output map, but also for any
\mathcal{K}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{Y}
such that
\ker\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathscr{N}
.

Composed reductions

To solve Problem
1
, we apply Algorithms
1
and
2
iteratively, as described in the following Algorithm
3
.

Input :
A QHM model
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
.

Assign
(\mathscr{A}_{0},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathfrak{S}_{0})=(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{C},\mathfrak{S})
;

1

Using Algorithm
1
on model
(\mathscr{A}_{0},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathfrak{S}_{0})
compute the model
(\mathscr{A}_{1},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{1})
;

2

Using Algorithm
2
on model
(\mathscr{A}_{1},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{1})
compute the model
(\mathscr{A}_{2},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{2})
;

3

if

\dim(\mathscr{A}_{0})\neq\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2})

:

4

Assign
(\mathscr{A}_{0},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathfrak{S}_{0})=(\mathscr{A}_{2},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{2})
;

5

Go back to step 2;

6

Output :

(\mathscr{A}_{2},\mathscr{Y},\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{2})

Algorithm 3

Iterative reduction.

Notice that one could obtain an alternative model-reduction algorithm by applying Algorithm
2
before Algorithm
1
at each iteration. The results of the two reductions are potentially different.

At each step, Algorithms
1
and
2
provide either a smaller or equivalent model then the previous one. If at some iteration they both do not decrease the dimension, that iteration leaves the model untouched and there is no need to proceed further and we obtain the optimal reduction for our approach. Since we work in a finite-dimensional setting, Algorithm
3
must converge to the optimal reduction in a finite number of steps.

It is worth remarking that, while inspired by the classical algorithm to construct minimal realizations by Rosenbrock
[
1
]
, the proposed approach needs to expand the rechable and observable subspaces to
algebras
: in doing so we need potentially to include non-reachable and non-observable parts of the state space. For this reason, in our setting a single iteration of the algorithm is not sufficient in general to obtain the best reduction. This is demonstrated in the next example.

Example 3
.

Consider a QHM model on two qubits i.e.
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{4}
, with
\mathfrak{S}=\{(I/2+\sigma_{x}/4)\otimes\tau,(I/2+\sigma_{y}/4)\otimes\tau\}
for some full-rank density operator
\tau\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
. Assume that
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}}
, the identity over
\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
and that
\mathcal{C}(\rho)={\rm tr}(\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}\rho)
.

Computing the reachable space we have
\mathscr{R}={\rm span}\{\mathfrak{S}\}
and, picking
\rho=I\otimes\tau
we have
{\rm alg}(\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{-1}(\mathscr{R}))=\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}\otimes I
. This algebra yields the reduced model
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\mathfrak{S}^{\prime})
, defined on
\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
with
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}=\{(I/2+\sigma_{x}/4),(I/2+\sigma_{y}/4)\}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(\cdot)={\rm tr}(\sigma_{z}\cdot){\rm tr}(\sigma_{z}\tau)
. Computing the subspace orthogonal to the non-observable subspace of the reduced model we obtain
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}={\rm span}\{\sigma_{z}\}
hence
\mathscr{O}={\rm alg}(\mathscr{N}^{\perp})={\rm span}\{I,\sigma_{z}\}
. This algebra then leads to the reduced QHM model
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime},\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime})
defined on
\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
with
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime}=\{I/2\}
,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)={\rm tr}(\sigma_{z}\cdot){\rm tr}(\sigma_{z}\tau)
. If we then compute the reachable subspace of the model
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime\prime},\mathfrak{S}^{\prime\prime})
we obtain
\mathscr{R}_{2}={\rm span}\{I\}
and hence
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{R}_{2})={\rm span}\{I\}
which leads to the reduced model
(\widecheck{\mathcal{A}},\widecheck{\mathcal{C}},\widecheck{\mathfrak{S}})
defined on
\mathbb{R}
and with
\widecheck{\mathfrak{S}}=\{1\}
,
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}=1
,
\widecheck{\mathcal{C}}={\rm tr}(\sigma_{z}\tau)
.

This simple example shows that two steps are not always sufficient to retrieve the minimal model that our method can produce.

Remark
.

We shall notice that in Algorithm
3
, every application of the subroutines, Algorithms
1
and
2
, provides a pair of injection and reduction maps. Let us name them for convenience
\{(\mathcal{R}_{i},\mathcal{J}_{i})\}_{i=0,1,\dots}
and notice that each of those maps is CPTP. Necessarily, both the composition of all the injections, say
\mathcal{J}_{*}=\mathcal{J}_{0}\mathcal{J}_{1}\dots
and of all the reductions
\mathcal{R}_{*}=\dots\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{0}
are CPTP maps, since they are compositions of CPTP maps. Also,
\mathcal{R}_{*}:\mathscr{A}\to\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}
and
\mathcal{J}_{*}:\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}\to\mathscr{A}
. Then,
\mathcal{J}_{*}\mathcal{R}_{*}
is a CPTP projection onto a distorted algebra. This shows that there exists a distorted algebra that allows for a CPTP projection (or state extension) onto it, such that the reduction of the model on this distorted algebra provides the same reduced model in a single step as Algorithm
3
returns in an iterative manner. The question of determining this distorted algebra in a more efficient manner than the one proposed here remains an open problem.

Examples

VII-A

Quantum walks: Reduction of Grover’s algorithm

Grover’s algorithm
[
39
,
41
]
is a quantum algorithm that solves the unstructured search problem: given a set of
N
elements and a query function
f(j):\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}\to\{0,1\}
we want to find an element
j\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
such that
f(j)=1
. This algorithm is well renowned in the quantum computing community as it provides a quadratic speed-up with respect to its classical counterpart and is also a prototypical example of quantum algorithms built with quantum walks.

Grover’s algorithm can be modeled
[
32
]
as a QHM model defined on
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
:

\begin{cases}\rho(t+1)=RO\rho(t)O^{\dagger}R^{\dagger}\\
\bm{p}(t)={\rm diag}(\rho(t))\end{cases}\quad\rho_{0}=\left|\psi\middle>\middle<\psi\right|.

The initial condition is
\rho_{0}=\left|\psi\middle>\middle<\psi\right|
where
\left|\psi\right>=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}[1,\dots,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
. The evolution of Grover’s algorithm is composed of two unitary evolutions in sequence: first, the oracle
O
is applied, i.e. a unitary operation such that
O\left|j\right>=(-1)^{f(j)}\left|j\right>
; second, a reflection
R=2\left|\psi\middle>\middle<\psi\right|-I
is applied. This leads to the discrete-time dynamics
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)=RO\cdot O^{\dagger}R^{\dagger}
. The output quantity of interest - the one we want the reduced model to reproduce - is the population of the state
\rho
, i.e. its diagonal in the standard basis,
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)={\rm diag}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left|i\middle>\middle<i\right|\cdot\left|i\middle>\middle<i\right|
.

To derive the reachable subspace we resort to the approach taken in
[
41
, Section 6.1.3]
. Let us define the set of indexes
S=\{j|f(x)=1\}
and let
M=|S|
. Define also the two states

\left|\alpha\right>:=(N-M)^{-1/2}\sum_{j\notin S}\left|j\right>,\quad\left|\beta\right>:=(M)^{-1/2}\sum_{j\in S}\left|j\right>

such that
\left|\psi\right>=\alpha_{0}\left|\alpha\right>+\beta_{0}\left|\beta\right>
, with
\alpha_{0}=\sqrt{(N-M)/N}
and
\beta_{0}=\sqrt{M/N}
. Performing the required calculations one can then observe that
U^{t}\left|\psi\right>=\alpha(t)\left|\alpha\right>+\beta(t)\left|\beta\right>
with
\alpha(t)=\cos(\frac{2t+1}{2}\theta)
,
\beta(t)=\sin(\frac{2t+1}{2}\theta)
and
\theta=2\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{N-M}{M}}\right)
. In
[
32
]
it has been shown that assuming
M\neq 0,N/2,N
, the first three time instants are sufficient to generate linearly independent operators, and hence
\mathscr{R}={\rm span}\{\left|\alpha\middle>\middle<\alpha\right|,\left|\beta\middle>\middle<\beta\right|,\left|\alpha\middle>\middle<\beta\right|+\left|\beta\middle>\middle<\alpha\right|\}
.

One can then observe that

{\rm alg}(\mathscr{R})={\rm span}\{\left|\alpha\middle>\middle<\alpha\right|,\left|\beta\middle>\middle<\beta\right|,\left|\alpha\middle>\middle<\beta\right|,\left|\beta\middle>\middle<\alpha\right|\}

which does not have full support. In fact, let
U
be any unitary matrix such that
U^{\dagger}\left|\alpha\right>=\left|0\right>
and
U^{\dagger}\left|\beta\right>=\left|1\right>
, then
{\rm alg}(\mathscr{R})=U(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}\bigoplus\mathbb{0}_{R})U^{\dagger}
with
\mathbb{0}_{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N-2)\times(N-2)}
. We can then define the non-square isometry
V=[I_{2}|\mathbb{0}_{2\times N-2}]\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times N}
to obtain the reduction and injection maps
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=VU^{\dagger}\cdot UV^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{J}(\cdot)=UV^{\dagger}\cdot VU^{\dagger}
, which are CPTP over their support.

These maps allow us to determine the reduced model. Let
\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}=\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
and let us denote
\left|0_{2}\right>,\left|1_{2}\right>\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
The reduced model then takes the form

\begin{cases}\widecheck{\rho}(t+1)=\widecheck{U}\rho(t)\widecheck{U}\\
\bm{p}(t)=\sum_{i\in S}\left|i\middle>\middle<1_{2}\right|\cdot\left|1_{2}\middle>\middle<i\right|\sum_{i\notin S}\left|i\middle>\middle<0_{2}\right|\cdot\left|0_{2}\middle>\middle<i\right|\end{cases}

with initial condition

\widecheck{\rho}_{0}=\mathcal{R}(\rho_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\begin{bmatrix}N-M&\sqrt{(N-M)M}\\
\sqrt{(N-M)M}&M\end{bmatrix}.

and unitary matrix

\widecheck{U}=\frac{N-2M}{N}I_{2}-i\frac{\sqrt{(N-M)M}}{N}\sigma_{y}.

Verifying that the reduced model is indeed observable is left to the reader.

This example shows that Grover’s algorithm can be efficiently simulated using a single qubit. Notice however that this does not mean that the unstructured search problem can be solved using a single qubit. This is because in order to compute the change of basis
U
that reduces the model, one needs to know the set
S
, which is the solution to the unstructured search problem.

VII-B

Open systems: system-environment with a qubit interface

Consider a system composed of three finte-dimensional interacting subsystems: the
system

\mathcal{H}_{S}
, the qubit
interface

\mathcal{H}_{I}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and the
environment
,
\mathcal{H}_{E}
,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
.

Consider then the following QHM model:

\begin{cases}\rho(t+1)=\mathcal{E}(U\rho(t)U^{\dagger})\\
\tau(t)={\rm tr}_{I,E}(\rho(t))\end{cases}\quad\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})

The CPTP dynamics for our initial QHM model is composed of two parts, a unitary evolution
U\cdot U^{\dagger}
and a dissipative evolution
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
. The unitary evolution is obtained by integrating over a time
\Delta t
the Hamiltonian
H=H_{S}\otimes\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{E}+I_{S}\otimes\sigma_{z}\otimes H_{E}
where
H_{S},H_{E}
can be any Hamiltonians for the system and the environment, respectively, thus obtaining
U=e^{-iH\Delta t}
. The dissipative part of the dynamics is a probabilistic bit flip over the qubit interface and a generic probabilistic unitary error over the system and environment, i.e.
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)=p_{I}\sigma_{x}^{(I)}\cdot\sigma_{x}^{(I)}+p_{S}U_{S}\cdot U_{S}^{\dagger}+p_{E}U_{E}\cdot U_{E}^{\dagger}+(1-p_{I}-p_{S}-p_{E})I\cdot I
where
\sigma_{x}^{(I)}=I_{S}\otimes\sigma_{x}\otimes I_{E}
, while
U_{S},U_{E}
can be any unitary acting only on the system and environment respectively, and
p_{I},p_{S},p_{E}\in[0,1]
, such that
p_{S}+p_{I}+p_{E}\leq 1
.

We are interested in reducing the QHM model we just described for any initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H})
. We also consider as output map the partial trace over the interface and the environment: let
\{S_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
, e.g. the generalized Gell-Mann matrices, then
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)={\rm tr}_{I,E}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}S_{i}{\rm tr}(S_{i}\otimes I_{I}\otimes I_{E}\cdot)
. As described in Subsection
III-B
, the non-observable subspace is orthogonal to

\mathscr{N}^{\perp}={\rm span}\{\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}t}[S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E}],\,t\geq 0\}.

In order to compute
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}
, let us start by noticing that, because of the fact that
[H_{S}\otimes\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{E},I_{S}\otimes\sigma_{z}\otimes H_{E}]=0
we can write
U=U_{2}U_{1}
with
U_{1}=e^{-iH_{S}\otimes\sigma_{z}\Delta t}\otimes I_{E}
and
U_{2}=I_{S}\otimes e^{-i\sigma_{z}\otimes H_{E}\Delta t}
. Moreover, since
\sigma_{z}=\left|0\middle>\middle<0\right|-\left|1\middle>\middle<1\right|
we have

U_{1}=[e^{-iH_{S}\Delta t}\otimes\left|0\middle>\middle<0\right|+e^{iH_{S}\Delta t}\otimes\left|1\middle>\middle<1\right|]\otimes I_{E},

U_{2}=I_{S}\otimes[\left|0\middle>\middle<0\right|\otimes e^{-iH_{E}\Delta t}+\left|1\middle>\middle<1\right|\otimes e^{iH_{E}\Delta t}].

One can then observe that
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
leaves
{\rm span}\{S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E}\}
invariant since
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E})=p_{S}U_{S}(S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E})U_{S}^{\dagger}+(1-p_{S})S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E}
and
U_{S}(S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E})U_{S}^{\dagger}\in{\rm span}\{S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E}\}
for any unitary
U_{S}
that acts only on the system. Similarly,
U_{2}^{\dagger}\cdot U_{2}
acts as the identity over
\{S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E}\}
.
U_{1}^{\dagger}\cdot U_{1}
, instead, makes the diagonal of the interface qubit, observable at one step. In fact,

\displaystyle U_{1}(S_{i}\otimes I_{I,E})U_{1}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=[e^{-iH_{S}\Delta t}S_{i}e^{iH_{S}\Delta t}\otimes\left|0\middle>\middle<0\right|+

\displaystyle\qquad e^{iH_{S}\Delta t}S_{i}e^{-iH_{S}\Delta t}\otimes\left|1\middle>\middle<1\right|]\otimes I_{E}

which is contained in
{\rm span}\{S_{i}\otimes\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\otimes I_{E},\,j=0,1\}.

In the second step then, one can verify that
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\cdot)
,
U_{2}^{\dagger}\cdot U_{2}
and
U_{1}^{\dagger}\cdot U_{1}
leave
{\rm span}\{S_{i}\otimes\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\otimes I_{E},\,j=0,1\}
invariant and thus one obtains
\mathscr{N}^{\perp}={\rm span}\{S_{i}\otimes\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\otimes I_{E},\,i=0,1\}
which is also an algebra. Let
W
be the unitary swap matrix defined by
W^{\dagger}(A\otimes B)W=B\otimes A
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{I})
, then

\mathscr{N}^{\perp}=(W\otimes I_{E})[\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\otimes I_{E}\bigoplus\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\otimes I_{E}](W^{\dagger}\otimes I_{E}).

This leads, after some manipulation, to injection and reduction maps defined as

\mathcal{J}[\widecheck{X}]=\widecheck{X}\otimes\frac{I_{E}}{\dim(\mathcal{H}_{E})}

for all
\widecheck{X}=X\otimes(\alpha\left|0\middle>\middle<0\right|+\beta\left|1\middle>\middle<1\right|)
and
X\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
and

\mathcal{R}(X)=\sum_{j=0,1}{\rm tr}_{E}\left(I_{S}\otimes\left<j\right|\otimes I_{E}XI_{S}\otimes\left|j\right>\otimes I_{E}\right)\otimes\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|.

These maps lead to the reduced model defined over the algebra
\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}={\rm span}\{S_{i}\otimes\left|j\middle>\middle<j\right|\}=W(\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\bigoplus\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S}))W^{\dagger}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{I})
and dynamics

\begin{cases}\widecheck{\rho}(t+1)=\widecheck{\mathscr{E}}(\widecheck{U}\widecheck{\rho}(t)\widecheck{U}^{\dagger})\\
\tau(t)={\rm tr}_{I}(\widecheck{\rho}(t))\end{cases}\quad\widecheck{\rho}_{0}\in\mathfrak{D}\left(\widecheck{\mathscr{A}}\right)

where

\widecheck{U}=e^{-iH_{S}\otimes\sigma_{z}\Delta t}
and
\widecheck{\mathcal{E}}(\cdot)=p_{I}\sigma_{x}^{(I)}\cdot\sigma_{x}^{(I)}+p_{S}U_{S}\cdot U_{S}^{\dagger}+(1-p_{I}-p_{S})I\cdot I
where
\sigma_{x}^{(I)}=I_{S}\otimes\sigma_{x}
.

This example shows that regardless of the Hamiltonians
H_{S}
and
H_{E}
, the unitaries
U_{S}
and
U_{E}
and the initial state
\rho_{0}
, the action of the environment can be completely removed because it is non-observable from the system state. This is due to the fact that the interface qubit completely decouples the action of the environment with that of the system. Moreover, the interface qubit behaves classically, since its coherences do not influence in any manner the evolution of the system state.

VIII
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we develop a general framework and foundational tools for a theory of CPTP model reduction for quantum dynamics.
While finding minimal linear models can be done by relying on linear system theory, as soon as positivity constraints are imposed the problem becomes challenging, and still open even for classical linear systems
[
7
]
. In quantum engineering, effective reduced models have been proposed for control and filtering purposes, but their properties are hard to characterize
[
49
,
50
]
.
We here propose a systematic way to construct reduced models for quantum dynamics that are guaranteed to be CPTP by construction: the map performing the reduction is essentially a projection onto a distorted algebra, constructed either from the reachable or the observable subspaces, obtained via (CPTP factors of) the dual of a conditional expectation. Alternating the two reductions, reachable and observable, one can exploit the knowledge of both the initial conditions and the output of interest to decrease the size of the description. An open problem remains: is the output of the procedure a CPTP model of minimal dimension for the dynamics of interest? While preliminary numerical tests suggest this is the case, further work is needed to prove optimality in general.
