Numerical bifurcation analysis of renewal equations via pseudospectral approximation Note: Declarations of interest: none. Journal: journal
Francesca Scarabel, Odo Diekmann, Rossana Vermiglio

Abstract
We propose an approximation of nonlinear renewal equations by means of ordinary differential equations. We consider the integrated state, which is absolutely continuous and satisfies a delay differential equation. By applying the pseudospectral approach to the abstract formulation of the differential equation, we obtain an approximating system of ordinary differential equations. We present convergence proofs for equilibria and the associated characteristic roots, and we use some models from ecology and epidemiology to illustrate the benefits of the approach to perform numerical bifurcation analyses of equilibria and periodic solutions. The numerical simulations show that the implementation of the new approximating system can be about ten times more efficient in terms of computational times than the one originally proposed in [Breda et al, SIAM Journal on Applied Dynamical Systems, 2016], as it avoids the numerical inversion of an algebraic equation.



1
Introduction

In our perspective, a renewal equation (RE) is a delay equation, i.e., a rule for extending a function of time towards the future on the basis of the (assumed to be) known history. The difference between delay differential equations (DDE) and RE is that, for the former, the rule specifies the derivative of the function in the current time point, while for the latter the rule specifies the function value itself.
For both, one defines a dynamical system by translation along the extended function, so by updating the history.
But while for DDE the natural state space consists of continuous functions (with the supremum norm), for RE it is more natural to consider integrable functions (with the
L^{1}
norm).
Stability and bifurcation results for RE are formulated in
[
1
]
as corollaries of the general theory presented in
[
2
]
.

RE arise routinely in the formulation of physiologically structured population models, see e.g.
[
3
]
. In that context, one would often like to go beyond a pen and paper analysis and perform a numerical bifurcation analysis. But the lack of tools that can handle this kind of delay equations clearly forms an obstruction.

In
[
4
]
the idea is launched to first reduce the infinite dimensional dynamical system corresponding to a delay equation to a finite dimensional one by pseudospectral approximation, and next use tools for ordinary differential equations (ODE) in order to perform a numerical bifurcation analysis. Several examples illustrate that this approach is promising (also see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
).
Note that we restrict to
bounded
maximal delay, as unbounded delays require to consider different (exponentially weighted) function spaces and corresponding results from weighted interpolation theory
[
11
,
12
]
.

A nice feature of pseudospectral approximation is that, in the resulting ODE, one can recognize that the dynamics involves both a rule for extension and translation. The latter is captured by a matrix, often called differentiation matrix, that depends on the choice of mesh points but
not
on the delay equation under consideration.

In the approximation of
a scalar DDE, the rule for extension is reflected in the expression for the derivative of exactly one component, so the nonlinear part of the ODE has one dimensional range;

in the approximation of
systems of DDE, the dimension of the range corresponds to the dimension of the system. The fact that, for RE, the rule for extension does specify the value, rather than the derivative, makes its incorporation in the ODE less straightforward. In
[
4
,
5
]
an ad hoc method was employed: the value in the current time point was computed from the (approximate) history and the right-hand side of the RE by way of a numerical solver. The aim of the present paper is to introduce a much more natural and elegant alternative, which also improves the efficiency of the numerical method.

The main new idea is to approximate the indefinite integral of the integrable function, rather than the integrable function itself. First of all, a reassuring consequence is that now we approximate a function that has well-defined point values (in contrast with an
L^{1}
equivalence class).
More importantly: in terms of the integrated function, the original RE becomes a DDE, as the rule for extension specifies its derivative in the current time point.
The difference with a “true” DDE is that we have to incorporate a (re)normalization condition in order to have a one-to-one relationship between the integrated function and its derivative. The resulting ODE therefore has a slightly different structure: for a scalar equation the nonlinear part again has one-dimensional range, but in natural coordinates the range is spanned by a different vector.

From a more abstract point of view, we
represent
L^{1}
by
AC_{0}
, the subspace of
NBV
(normalized bounded variation functions) consisting of absolutely continuous functions.
This embedding also features in the
sun-star
framework of
[
1
,
2
]
(where the “big” space
NBV
serves to represent the rule for extension as a perturbation with range spanned by a Dirac mass) and in the more recent theory of twin semigroups
[
13
]
.
The space
AC_{0}
also guarantees the convergence of the approximation: classical results from interpolation theory
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
ensure the convergence of polynomial interpolation, in supremum norm (and not necessarily in
NBV
norm), for functions that are at least absolutely continuous, for a suitable choice of the interpolation nodes in the bounded interval.

An important advantage of the current method compared to the approach proposed in
[
4
]
is the remarkable reduction of computational costs in all the simulations considered here (see for instance Figure
5
and Table
1
below).
The inversion of the nonlinear condition with a numerical solver is indeed the main bottleneck of the method in
[
4
]
.
The improved computational efficiency is fundamental especially when dealing with complex applications from population dynamics as the coupled RE/DDE models for
Daphnia

[
3
]
, that are particularly challenging to treat numerically and often need ad hoc techniques
[
10
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

In this paper we focus on the approximation of equilibria and their stability.
We start in the next section by providing some concrete examples that illustrate the main features and potential of the approximation approach.
The latter is introduced rigorously in Section
3
for general scalar nonlinear RE, together with some basic results regarding the approximation of equilibria.
Section
4
focuses on autonomous linear equations: we show that characteristic roots and exponential solutions are approximated with infinite order of convergence as the dimension of the approximation increases.
An outlook discussion is presented in Section
5
.

In population models the RE usually concerns the population level birth rate and for that reason we chose to use the character
b
to denote the variable. The integrated quantity corresponds to the cumulative birth rate and is denoted by
B
.

2
Some illustrative examples

In this section we consider some specific nonlinear RE.
All the equations are approximated with an ODE system using the method introduced rigorously in Section
3
. The dimension
M
of the approximating ODE system is specified each time.
The bifurcation diagram of the approximating system is then studied numerically using software for numerical bifurcation analysis of ODE.
Specifically we use the package MatCont (version 7p1)
[
20
]
running on MATLAB 2019a.
To improve efficiency, the integrals are computed using Clenshaw–Curtis quadrature formulas
[
21
]
.

MATLAB codes used to obtain the results in this paper are available at
http://cdlab.uniud.it/software
.

We have three main goals:
1) show the suitability of the approach (ODE approximation plus software for numerical bifurcation) to reveal bifurcations of equilibria and to study some more advanced dynamical behaviors; 2) carry out a preliminary study of the convergence of the approximations;
3) compare the performances and the output of the method presented here with the one proposed in
[
4
]
.

2.1
An SIRS model

Let
k\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a nonnegative and measurable function with support in
[0,1]
, and normalized such that
\int_{0}^{1}k(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=1
.
Consider the nonlinear equation

b(t)=\gamma\left(1-\int_{0}^{1}b(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\int_{0}^{1}k(s)b(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t>0,

(2.1)

In
[
22

, the authors derive this equation in the context of an SIRS epidemic model, and study the bifurcation with respect to

, and a positive stable equilibrium exists for

Under some conditions on the kernel

The authors also conjecture that (

We here consider a truncated Gamma-type kernel of the form

k(s)=\begin{cases}\alpha s^{m-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s}{\theta}}&s\leq 1,\\
0&s>1,\end{cases}

(2.2)

The kernel (

[
22
]
due to the cut-off at

The bifurcation diagram with respect to
\log\gamma
is shown in Figure
1
for different values of the dimension
M
of the approximating system.
A Hopf bifurcation is detected on the branch of positive equilibria, after which the equilibrium becomes unstable and a branch of stable periodic solutions appears.
Note that the minimum values of the periodic orbits come close to zero when
\log\gamma
approaches 3. When this happens,
M=10
is not sufficient to approximate the orbit, but for
M=20,40
the lower portions of the curves are indistinguishable.

2.2
Nicholson’s blowflies equation

Consider Nicholson’s blowflies DDE
[
23

A^{\prime}(t)=-\mu A(t)+\beta_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}A(t-1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-A(t-1)},\qquad t>0,

For

The equivalence is rigorously proved in

It is easy to see that the (unique) nontrivial equilibrium of (

The nontrivial equilibrium undergoes a Hopf bifurcation as

An explicit parametrization of the Hopf bifurcation curves in the plane

[
7
]
for the DDE (

Moreover, the numerical bifurcation analysis of (

[
24
,
25
]
. Therefore, the blowflies equation allows us to compare the output of MatCont computations on the pseudospectral approximation of (

) with analytic formulas for the Hopf bifurcation curves and with the output of DDE-BIFTOOL for bifurcations of periodic solutions of (

In order to apply the method presented here, we truncated the integral in (
2.4
) so that the probability of survival at a finite maximal time

\tau=10

Nonlinear renewal equations

In particular, given a (classical) solution
v
of (
3.9
)&(
3.10
), we reconstruct the solution of (
3.1
)&(
3.2
) by defining

b(t)=F(C_{0}v(t))
for
t>0
,
and (
3.7
) holds
.

It is then clear that the linear operator
C_{0}
captures the translation, while the rule for extension is represented explicitly by the nonlinear perturbation
qF\circ C_{0}
(where
\circ
denotes composition).

3.1
Reduction to ODE via pseudospectral discretization

To approximate the solution
v(t)
of (
3.9
)&(
3.10
), we fix a
discretization index

M\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a set of points
\Theta_{M}=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M}\}\subset[-\tau,0)
and
\theta_{0}=0
, with

-\tau\leq\theta_{M}<\cdots<\theta_{1}<\theta_{0}=0.

In the numerical simulations we consider the
Chebyshev zeros
, i.e., the roots of the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind of degree
M

[
27
]
, transformed to the interval
(-\tau,0)
, which are given explicitly by

\theta_{j}=\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\cos\left(\frac{2j-1}{2M}\pi\right)-1\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

(3.11)

Let
\ell_{j}
,
j=0,\dots,M
, be the Lagrange polynomials associated with
\{\theta_{0}=0\}\cup\Theta_{M}
, which are defined by

\ell_{j}(\theta):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{M}\frac{\theta-\theta_{k}}{\theta_{j}-\theta_{k}},\qquad\theta\in[-\tau,0].

We recall that
\ell_{j}(\theta_{k})=\delta_{jk}
, where
\delta_{jk}
denotes the Kronecker symbol, and that, for all
\theta\in[-\tau,0]
, the Lagrange polynomials have the properties

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M}\ell_{j}(\theta)=1,\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{M}\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\theta)=0,

and form a basis of the space of polynomials of degree less than or equal to
M
.
Using the Lagrange basis, the unique
M
-degree polynomial
p
interpolating a function
\varphi
with well-defined point values on the nodes
\{0\}\cup\Theta_{M}
can be expressed by

p(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{M}\varphi(\theta_{j})\ell_{j}(\theta),\qquad\theta\in[-\tau,0].

To obtain a finite dimensional approximation of (
3.9
) we need to approximate the operator
C_{0}
and the function
F
.
To do this, we introduce
P_{M}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to NBV
, the interpolation operator on
\{0\}\cup\Theta_{M}
with value zero in
\theta_{0}=0
, and
R_{M}\colon NBV\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the restriction on
\Theta_{M}
. More precisely,

\displaystyle P_{M}y

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{M}y_{j}\ell_{j},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(3.12)

\displaystyle(R_{M}\varphi)_{j}

\displaystyle:=\varphi(\theta_{j}),\qquad\varphi\in NBV,\ j=1,\dots,M.

The operator
C_{0}
is approximated by pseudospectral differentiation, i.e., by the finite dimensional operator
D_{M}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
defined by

D_{M}:=R_{M}C_{0}P_{M}.

At this point, the choice of a mesh like (
3.11
), which does not include the point
-\tau
, is even better justified, as the multi-valuedness of
C_{0}
disappears in the pseudospectral approximation. If the point
-\tau
belongs to
\Theta_{M}
, then we should define
C_{0}
as the (single-valued) inverse of the restriction of
V
on
NBV_{0}
, so that
D_{M}
is well defined.

To write explicitly the operator
D_{M}
, we consider a vector
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Then
P_{M}y
is given by (
3.12
) and, for all
k=1,\dots,M
,

\displaystyle(D_{M}y)_{k}

\displaystyle=(C_{0}P_{M}y)(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}y_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\theta_{k}).

The entries of the matrix
D_{M}
are therefore given explicitly by

(D_{M})_{kj}:=\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\theta_{k}),\qquad k,j=1,\dots,M.

Note that the elements
(D_{M})_{kj}
are entries of the differentiation matrix associated with the mesh
\{0\}\cup\Theta_{M}

[
21
]
.
More precisely,
D_{M}
is the submatrix of the differentiation matrix accounting for the fact that functions are zero in zero.

To approximate the perturbation
qF\circ C_{0}
, which captures the rule for extension, we let
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
denote the vector with all the entries equal to
1

(note that the vector
-\mathbf{1}
is the discretization of
q
)
, and introduce
F_{M}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
by

F_{M}:=F\circ C_{0}P_{M}.

Using (
3.12
), for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
we have

F_{M}(y)=F(\sum_{j=1}^{M}\ell_{j}^{\prime}y_{j}).

Then,
qF\circ C_{0}
is approximated by
-\mathbf{1}F_{M}
.

Putting everything together, we obtain the following finite dimensional ODE system

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=D_{M}x-F_{M}(x)\mathbf{1},

(3.13)

for
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
t\geq 0.
By construction, the
(M-1)
-degree polynomial
C_{0}P_{M}x(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\ell_{j}^{\prime}x_{j}(t)
furnishes an approximation of
b_{t}
, while
F_{M}(x(t))
approximates
b(t)
in (
3.1
). The computational advantage of (
3.13
) with respect to the ODE system derived in
[
4
]
is evident: the nonlinear contribution appears explicitly as a perturbation of the linear system, and there is no need to solve a nonlinear equation to impose the domain condition.

The dynamics of (
3.13
) can be investigated efficiently by using available software for numerical continuation and bifurcation study. It is therefore important to understand to which extent (
3.13
) mimics the dynamics of the original infinite dimensional dynamical system described by the RE (
3.1
). Here we examine the equilibria and their stability properties.

3.2
Correspondence of equilibria and linearized equations

In what follows we assume that the operator
F
in (
3.1
) is continuously Fréchet differentiable and we focus on constant solutions and the corresponding linearized equations.

We slightly abuse notation by using the symbol
\overline{b}
to represent both a real number and the constant function (defined on
[-\tau,0]
) taking that number as its one and only value.

Theorem 1
.

The equilibria of (
3.1
) and (
3.13
) are in one-to-one correspondence.
More precisely, if
\overline{b}=F(\overline{b})
, then
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
with

\overline{x}_{j}=\overline{b}\,\theta_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

(3.14)

satisfies
D_{M}\overline{x}-F_{M}(\overline{x})\mathbf{1}=0
and so defines an equilibrium of (
3.13
);
vice versa, if
D_{M}\overline{x}-F_{M}(\overline{x})\mathbf{1}=0
then
\overline{b}=\overline{x}_{j}/\theta_{j}
does not depend on
j
and satisfies
\overline{b}=F(\overline{b})
.

Proof.

We first derive a useful identity.
Since
M
-degree polynomial interpolation is exact on all polynomials with degree equal to or less than
M
, for the identity function we can write

\theta=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}\ell_{j}(\theta),\qquad\theta\in[-\tau,0].

By differentiation we then obtain

1=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}.

(3.15)

Assume first
\overline{b}=F(\overline{b})
, and define
\overline{x}
by (
3.14
). From (
3.15
),

\overline{b}=\overline{b}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\theta)

for all
\theta\in[-\tau,0]
and,
consequently,

[D_{M}\overline{x}]_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\theta_{k})\overline{x}_{j}=\overline{b}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}(\theta_{k})=\overline{b},

i.e.,

D_{M}\overline{x}=\overline{b}\,\mathbf{1}
.
Therefore we conclude

D_{M}\overline{x}-F_{M}(\overline{x})\mathbf{1}=D_{M}\overline{x}-F(\overline{b}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime})\,\mathbf{1}=\overline{b}\,\mathbf{1}-F(\overline{b})\,\mathbf{1}=0,

or, in words,

\overline{x}
is an equilibrium of (
3.13
).

Vice versa, assume that
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
satisfies

D_{M}\overline{x}-F_{M}(\overline{x})\,\mathbf{1}=0,

(3.16)

and define
\overline{b}:=F_{M}(\overline{x})=F(\overline{p}_{M-1})
, with
\overline{p}_{M-1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\overline{x}_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}
. Note that
\overline{p}_{M-1}
is a polynomial with degree
M-1
and, by definition,
(D_{M}\overline{x})_{k}=(\sum_{j=1}^{M}\overline{x}_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime})(\theta_{k})=\overline{p}_{M-1}(\theta_{k})
,
k=1,\dots,M
.
By (
3.16
), we conclude that
\overline{p}_{M-1}
takes the value
\overline{b}
on
M
distinct points and therefore
\overline{p}_{M-1}
is identically equal to
\overline{b}
. It now follows from the definition of
\overline{b}
that
\overline{b}=F(\overline{b})
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

The operations of linearization around an equilibrium and pseudospectral discretization commute.

Proof.

Let
\overline{b}
be an equilibrium of (
3.1
). The linearization around
\overline{b}
reads

b(t)=DF(\overline{b})\,b_{t},

and the corresponding approximating ODE system is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=D_{M}x-\big[DF(\overline{b})\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}\ell^{\prime}_{j}\big]\,\mathbf{1}.

(3.17)

On the other hand, let
\overline{x}
be an equilibrium of (
3.13
) with (
3.14
).
The linearization of (
3.13
) around
\overline{x}
reads

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=D_{M}x-[DF_{M}(\overline{x})x]\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=D_{M}x-\Big[DF(\overline{b}\,\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime})\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}\Big]\,\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=D_{M}x-\Big[DF(\overline{b})\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}\ell_{j}^{\prime}\Big]\,\mathbf{1},

where in the last step we used (
3.15
). Hence the linearized system coincides with (
3.17
).
∎

The principle of linearized stability
[
1
]
ensures that the local stability properties of an equilibrium
\overline{b}
of (
3.1
) are determined by the stability properties of the zero solution of the system linearized around
\overline{b}
, if the equilibrium is hyperbolic.
Therefore the next step in order to study whether the finite dimensional ODE (
3.13
) approximates the stability properties of the equilibria of (
3.1
) is to focus on linear equations.

4
The linear case: convergence analyses

In this section we focus on the linear case and study the convergence of exponential solutions, which are related to stability.
We consider the linear RE

b(t)=\int_{0}^{\tau}k(a)b(t-a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a,\qquad t>0,

(4.1)

where
k
is a bounded measurable function on
[0,\infty)
with support in
[0,\tau]
.

Following
[
13
]
, let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the pairing between bounded measurable functions and
NBV
, i.e.,

\langle k,\psi\rangle:=\int_{-\tau}^{0}k(-a)\psi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a),\qquad\psi\in NBV.

In particular, if
\psi\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}AC_{0}}
, the pairing reads

\langle k,\psi\rangle=\int_{-\tau}^{0}k(-a)\psi^{\prime}(a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a.

By defining
K_{M}
as the row vector with components

(K_{M})_{j}:=\langle k,\ell_{j}\rangle=\int_{-\tau}^{0}k(-a)\ell_{j}^{\prime}(a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a,\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

(4.2)

we can write the ODE system (
3.13
) approximating (
4.1
) as

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=D_{M}x-(K_{M}x)\,\mathbf{1}.

(4.3)

We want to study in which sense the finite dimensional approximation (
4.3
) captures the stability properties of the zero equilibrium of (
4.1
) as
M\to\infty
.
The asymptotic stability of the zero equilibrium is determined by the roots of the characteristic equation, a.k.a. “characteristic roots”.
Specifically, (
4.1
) has an exponential solution of the form
b(t)=\alpha\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}
with
\alpha\neq 0
if and only if
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is a root of the characteristic equation

1=\int_{0}^{\tau}k(a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a.

(4.4)

The real parts of the characteristic roots
\lambda
determine the stability of the zero solution of (
4.1
): if
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<0
for all
\lambda
, then the zero equilibrium is asymptotically stable; if there exists a root
\lambda
with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda>0
, then the zero equilibrium is unstable.

From now on we assume that the set
\Theta_{M}
is chosen such that the associated Lebesgue constant, defined as

\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}:=\max_{\theta\in[-\tau,0]}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|\tilde{\ell}_{j}(\theta)|,

for
\tilde{\ell}_{j}
Lagrange polynomials associated with
\Theta_{M}
, satisfies

\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}}{M}=0.

(4.5)

We remark that (
4.5
) is true for instance for the nodes (
3.11
), for which
\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}=O(\log M)

[
15
, Chapter 1.4.6]
.
We also remark that including additional nodes, for instance the extremum
-\tau
, does not affect the validity of (
4.5
),
[
15
, Chapter 4.2]
.

The other important ingredient for convergence is the regularity of the function
\varphi
: the smoother a function, the faster the convergence of its interpolants.
More specifically, let
\mathcal{L}_{M-1}
denote the polynomial interpolation operator on
\Theta_{M}
, such that
\mathcal{L}_{M-1}\varphi
is the unique
(M-1)
-degree polynomial with
[\mathcal{L}_{M-1}\varphi](\theta_{j})=\varphi(\theta_{j})
,
j=1,\dots,M
.
Although
\mathcal{L}_{M-1}
can be defined also for bounded variation functions, and some results for interpolation in
L^{p}
norm exist
[
28
]
, we here require that
\varphi
is at least continuous, and use error bounds of polynomial interpolation in terms of the uniform norm.
In the following, let
c
denote a constant (different from time to time) independent of
M
.
If
\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous, the bound

\|(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq c\,\frac{\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}}{M}\,\text{Lip}(\varphi)

(4.6)

holds, where Lip
(\varphi)
denotes the Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
,
and, under the assumption (
4.5
), the right-hand side of (
4.6
) tends to zero as
M\to\infty
.

The order of convergence depends on the regularity of the interpolated function. More precisely, the estimate

\|(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq c\,\frac{\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}}{M^{k}}\,\|\varphi^{(k)}\|_{\infty}

holds for
\varphi\in C^{k}
,
see for instance
[
15
, Chapters 1.2 and 1.4]
.
For analytic functions, the uniform error of polynomial interpolation behaves as
O(\rho^{-M})
for some
\rho>1
, a phenomenon called
spectral accuracy
, or
geometric convergence
, or
exponential convergence

[
15
,
16
,
21
]
.
Finally we remark that the uniform convergence of polynomial interpolation holds also for absolutely continuous functions interpolated at Chebyshev zeros
[
14
]
.

Before studying the approximation error, we note that the numerical computation of the vector
K_{M}
in (
4.2
) may involve the use of quadrature formulas to approximate the integrals. These approximations can introduce numerical errors in addition to those due to the pseudospectral approximation. In the following, we ignore these errors by assuming that the integrals are computed exactly.

4.1
Resolvent operators

Before focusing on characteristic roots, we study more in general the convergence of the pseudospectral approximations of the resolvent operators of
C_{0}
. Although the convergence of resolvent operators is not directly used to prove the convergence of the stability criteria of equilibria, it is fundamental to set the basis for a broader study of the approximation error, as solution operators are strictly connected with the resolvent of their generator via the Laplace transform.
For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\varphi\in NBV
, the resolvent operator of
C_{0}
is given by

((\lambda I-C_{0})^{-1}\varphi)(\theta)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\theta}\int_{\theta}^{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda s}\varphi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad\theta\in[-\tau,0].

(4.7)

In the following analysis of the approximation error, we require that
\varphi\in NBV
is continuous.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\varphi\in NBV\cap C
, let
B
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{C}
, and assume that (
4.5
) holds.
There exists
\overline{M}(B)
such that, for any index
M\geq\overline{M}(B)
and
\lambda\in B
, the polynomial

p_{M}:=P_{M}(\lambda I-D_{M})^{-1}R_{M}\varphi

(4.8)

is well defined, and, for
\psi:=(\lambda I-C_{0})^{-1}\varphi,

\|\psi-p_{M}\|_{NBV}\leq c(B)\;\big\|r_{M}(\lambda,\varphi)\big\|_{\infty},

(4.9)

where
r_{M}(\lambda,\varphi):=(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\psi^{\prime}
and

c(B)=2\tau\,\sup_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}\left(1+|\lambda|\frac{1-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}\right).

Proof.

The proof technique is similar to the one used in
[
29
, Proposition 5.1]
and
[
8
, Theorem 4.1]
. We therefore only sketch the main steps.

The function
\psi
satisfies

\begin{cases}\psi^{\prime}(\theta)=\lambda\psi(\theta)-\varphi(\theta),&\theta\in[-\tau,0],\\
\psi(0)=0.\end{cases}

(4.10)

For given
\varphi\in NBV
, the function
\psi
defined by (
4.7
) satisfies (
4.10
). Here, since
\varphi
is continuous, we have
\psi^{\prime}\in C
and the first identity also holds in
\theta=0
.

On the other hand, proving that (
4.8
) is well defined is equivalent to showing that the collocation problem

\begin{cases}p_{M}^{\prime}(\theta)=\lambda p_{M}(\theta)-\varphi(\theta),&\theta=\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M},\\
p_{M}(0)=0\end{cases}

admits a unique solution.
Since
p_{M}^{\prime}
has degree
M-1
, it can be expressed as interpolation polynomial on
\Theta_{M}
, i.e., as

p_{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}_{M-1}(\lambda p_{M}-\varphi).

(4.11)

Define
e_{M}:=\psi-p_{M}
. Subtracting (
4.11
) from the first equation in (
4.10
) and writing
e_{M}=Ve_{M}^{\prime}
, we obtain

e_{M}^{\prime}=\lambda\mathcal{L}_{M-1}Ve_{M}^{\prime}+\lambda(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\psi-(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\varphi.

(4.12)

Note that, since
\varphi,\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in C
, (
4.12
) can be interpreted as an equation in
C
. We show that, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the operator
(I-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{M-1}V)
is invertible in
C
by showing that it is a perturbation, in supremum norm, of (the restriction to
C
of) the operator
(I-\lambda V)
. In fact, since
V\varphi
is Lipschitz for all
\varphi\in C
, from (
4.6
) we can bound

\|\lambda(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})V\|_{\infty}\leq c\,\frac{\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}}{M}|\lambda|,

with
c
independent of
M
.
In
C
,
(I-\lambda V)
is invertible with

[(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\zeta](\theta)=\zeta(\theta)-\lambda\int_{\theta}^{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(\theta-s)}\zeta(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\zeta\in C,

and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\begin{cases}1+|\lambda|\frac{1-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda},&\text{if }\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\neq 0\\[6.0pt]
1+\tau\,|\lambda|,&\text{if }\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda=0.\end{cases}}

For
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\neq 0
, from Banach’s perturbation lemma (e.g.,
[
30
, Theorem 10.1]
), by taking
\tilde{M}(\lambda)
such that
\|\lambda(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})V\|_{\infty}\,\|(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\|_{\infty}<1/2
for all
M\geq\tilde{M}(\lambda)
, we conclude that
(I-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{M-1}V)
is invertible in
C
for all
M\geq\tilde{M}(\lambda)
, and

\|(I-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{M-1}V)^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\begin{cases}2\left(1+|\lambda|\frac{1-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}\right),&\text{if }\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\neq 0\\[6.0pt]
2(1+\tau|\lambda|),&\text{if }\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda=0.\end{cases}

(4.13)

Hence, from (
4.12
),

e_{M}^{\prime}=(I-\lambda\mathcal{L}_{M-1}V)^{-1}r_{M}(\lambda,\varphi),

with

\displaystyle r_{M}(\lambda,\varphi):=\lambda(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\psi-(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\varphi=(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\psi^{\prime}.

The bound (
4.9
) follows from the fact that
e_{M}=Ve_{M}^{\prime}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}AC_{0}}
and therefore

\|e_{M}\|_{NBV}\leq\tau\|e_{M}^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

taking
\overline{M}(B)=\sup_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}\tilde{M}(\lambda)
and using the fact that the right-hand side of (
4.13
) is continuous on
\mathbb{C}
.

∎

From (
4.9
), it is clear that
\|\psi_{M}-p_{M}\|_{NBV}\to 0
when
\|r_{M}\|_{\infty}\to 0
.

If
\varphi\in AC_{0}
,
\psi^{\prime}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}AC_{0}}
and therefore
\|r_{M}\|_{\infty}\to 0
when the grid consists of the Chebyshev zeros (
3.11
), cf.
[
14
]
.
If
\varphi\in D(C_{0})
, then
\psi^{\prime\prime}\in NBV
and the convergence
\|r_{M}\|_{\infty}\to 0
holds for any set of nodes satisfying (
4.5
), since

\|(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}\mathcal{E}_{M-1}(\varphi)\leq\frac{2\tilde{\Lambda}_{M}}{\pi(M-2)}\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{NBV},

where
\mathcal{E}_{M}
denotes the error of the best uniform approximation in
C
with polynomials of degree
M

[
16
, Chapter 7]
.
As already discussed, the order of convergence
\|r_{M}\|_{\infty}\to 0
is higher if
\psi^{\prime}
has higher regularity (at least Lipschitz continuous, see (
4.6
) and following discussion).

The proof of Lemma
3
can be extended to functions
\varphi\in NBV
that are continuous in
[-\tau,0)
, but have a jump discontinuity in
\theta=0
(note that this class includes the function
q
).
In fact, note that
(\lambda I-C_{0})^{-1}
maps
NBV
to
D(C_{0})
, hence
\psi=(\lambda I-C_{0})^{-1}\varphi\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}AC_{0}}
.
From (
4.10
), we note that if
\varphi
is discontinuous in
\theta=0
,
\psi^{\prime}
is discontinuous, too.
However, we can adapt the previous proof by considering
\varphi
and
\psi^{\prime}
extended by continuity in
\theta=0
, hence working in the space
C
.
In particular, the following special case for the function
q\in NBV
is relevant for the analysis of characteristic roots.

Lemma 4
.

Let
B
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}
and assume that (
4.5
) hold.
There exists
\overline{M}(B)
such that, for any index
M\geq\overline{M}(B)
and
\lambda\in B
, the polynomial

p_{M,\lambda}:=P_{M}(\lambda I-D_{M})^{-1}(-\mathbf{1})

is well defined, and, for
\psi_{\lambda}:=(\lambda I-C_{0})^{-1}q
,

\|\psi_{\lambda}-p_{M,\lambda}\|_{NBV}\leq C_{2}(B)\frac{C_{1}(B)^{M}}{M!}

(4.14)

where

\displaystyle C_{1}(B)

\displaystyle=\tau\,\max_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}|\lambda|,

\displaystyle C_{2}(B)

\displaystyle=2\,\sup_{\lambda\in\overline{B}}\left[\left(1+|\lambda|\frac{1-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}\right)\,\max\{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda},1\}\right].

Proof.

From (
4.7
) we obtain, for
\theta\in[-\tau,0]
,

\psi_{\lambda}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\theta}-1}{\lambda}},&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0,\\
\theta,&\text{if }\lambda=0.\end{cases}

(4.15)

Consider now the function
\eta_{\lambda}(\theta):=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\theta}
,
\theta\in[-\tau,0]
, which satisfies

\eta_{\lambda}(\theta)=\lambda\psi_{\lambda}(\theta)+q(\theta),\qquad\theta\in[-\tau,0),

(i.e.,
\eta_{\lambda}=\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
) and
\eta_{\lambda}
is continuous on
[-\tau,0]
.

As in the proof of Lemma

p_{M,\lambda}
be the solution of

\begin{cases}p_{M,\lambda}^{\prime}(\theta)=\lambda p_{M,\lambda}(\theta)+q(\theta),&\theta=\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M},\\
p_{M,\lambda}(0)=0,\end{cases}

and let

e_{M}:=\psi_{\lambda}-p_{M,\lambda}=Vz_{M}

(4.16)

for
\overline{r}_{M}(\lambda):=(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\eta_{\lambda}
.
Since

Using the Cauchy interpolation remainder

\|\overline{r}_{M}(\lambda)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\tau^{M}\|\psi^{(M)}\|_{\infty}}{M!}\leq\frac{\tau^{M}|\lambda|^{M}\max\{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda},1\}}{M!}

(4.17)

The error bound (
4.14
) follows from
e_{M}=Vz_{M}
, using (
4.16
), (
4.17
) and (
4.13
) for

.

∎

4.2
Characteristic roots

We first study the exponential solutions of (
4.3
) and specify the corresponding discrete characteristic equation.
Next, we prove the convergence of the roots of the characteristic equations and, in turn, of exponential solutions.

Lemma 5
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\lambda\notin\sigma(D_{M})
.
Then (
4.3
) has a solution of the form
x(t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}y
with nontrivial
y\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
if and only if
\lambda
is a root of the characteristic equation

1=K_{M}(\lambda I-D_{M})^{-1}(-\mathbf{1}).

(4.18)

Proof.

Substitution of
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}y
into (
4.3
) leads to

\lambda y=D_{M}y-(K_{M}y)\,\mathbf{1}.

First note that, for
M\geq\overline{M}(\lambda)
,
K_{M}y\neq 0
must hold. Indeed
\lambda\notin\sigma(D_{M})
and therefore
\lambda y=D_{M}y
can not hold.
Next, we can invert the equation to obtain

y=(K_{M}y)(D_{M}-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

Hence we should have

K_{M}y=(K_{M}y)\,K_{M}(D_{M}-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{1},

which amounts to (
4.18
) since
K_{M}y\neq 0
.

Vice versa, assume (
4.18
) holds and take
y:=(D_{M}-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{1}
. Note that
y\neq 0
because
(D_{M}-\lambda I)
has full rank. Moreover,
K_{M}y=\mathbf{1}
, hence

(D_{M}-\lambda I)y=\mathbf{1}=K_{M}y,

i.e., (
4.18
) holds and therefore
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}y
is a solution of (
4.3
).

∎

Let now

\displaystyle\chi(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=1-\int_{0}^{\tau}k(a)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda a}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a=1-\langle k,\psi_{\lambda}\rangle,

\displaystyle\chi_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=1-K_{M}(D_{M}-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{1}=1-\langle k,P_{M}(\lambda I-D_{M})^{-1}(-\mathbf{1})\rangle,

with
\psi_{\lambda}
defined in (
4.15
),
so that the roots of (
4.4
) and (
4.18
) coincide with the zeros of
\chi(\lambda)=0
and
\chi_{M}(\lambda)=0
, respectively.
We want to prove the convergence of the zeros of
\chi_{M}
to the zeros of
\chi
.
Lemma
4
allows us to state the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
B
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}
, and let (
4.5
) hold.
There exists
\overline{M}(B)
such that, for any index
M\geq\overline{M}(B)
and
\lambda\in B
,
\lambda\notin\sigma(D_{M})
,

|\chi(\lambda)-\chi_{M}(\lambda)|\leq C_{2}(B)\frac{C_{1}(B)^{M}}{M!}\,\|k\|_{\infty},

for
C_{1}(B)
and
C_{2}(B)
defined as in Lemma
4
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\big|\chi(\lambda)-\chi_{M}(\lambda)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\langle k,\psi_{\lambda}\rangle-\langle k,P_{M}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\lambda I-D_{M})^{-1}}\mathbf{1}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|k\|_{\infty}\|\psi_{\lambda}-P_{M}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\lambda I-D_{M})^{-1}}\mathbf{1}\|_{NBV},

and the assertion follows from (
4.14
).

∎

We are now ready to conclude that roots of (
4.4
) are approximated by roots of (
4.18
).
The proof of the following result follows the lines of
[
29
, Section 5.3.2]
. We repeat the proof here for completeness.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\lambda
be a root of (
4.4
) with multiplicity
\nu
, and let
B
be an open ball of center
\lambda
such that
there is no other characteristic root of (
4.4
) in
B
.
Under assumption (
4.5
), there exists a positive integer
\overline{M}=\overline{M}(B)
such that, for
M\geq\overline{M}
, there exist
\nu
roots
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\nu}
(each counted as many times as its multiplicity) of (
4.18
) with

\max_{j=1,\dots,\nu}\big|\lambda-\lambda_{j}\big|\leq\left(\frac{2\nu!}{|\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)|}\max_{z\in B\setminus\{\lambda\}}|\chi(z)-\chi_{M}(z)|\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu}}.

In particular,
\max_{j=1,\dots,\nu}|\lambda-\lambda_{j}|\to 0
as
M\to\infty
.

Proof.

We want to apply Rouché’s theorem from complex analysis, which states that, if
\chi
and
\chi_{M}
are continuous on a compact set
K
and holomorphic in the interior of
K
, and
|\chi(z)-\chi_{M}(z)|<|\chi(z)|
for all
z\in\partial K
, then
\chi
and
\chi_{M}
have the same number of zeros inside
K
, see
[
31
, Chapter 10]
or
[
32
, Section 7.7]
.
Clearly, the complex-valued functions
\chi
and
\chi_{M}
are holomorphic in
B
.
Let

\varepsilon_{M}(z):=\big|\chi(z)-\chi_{M}(z)\big|.

From Theorem
6
, we have that
\varepsilon_{M}(z)\to 0
for all
z\in B\setminus\{\lambda\}
.

Since
\lambda
has multiplicity
\nu
, we have
\chi^{(n)}(\lambda)=0
for
n=0,\dots,\nu-1
.
For
z
in a neighborhood
U\subset B
of
\lambda
, by Taylor expansion we have

\chi(z)=\frac{\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)}{\nu!}(z-\lambda)^{\nu}+R_{\nu}(z),

(4.19)

with

R_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{j=\nu+1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi^{(j)}(\lambda)}{j!}(z-\lambda)^{j}=\sum_{j=\nu+1}^{\infty}\frac{(z-\lambda)^{j}}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial B}\frac{\chi(w)}{(w-\lambda)^{j+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w.

If
r
denotes the radius of the ball
B
centered in
\lambda
, we have

|R_{\nu}(z)|\leq M_{B}\sum_{j=\nu+1}^{\infty}\frac{(z-\lambda)^{j}}{r^{j}}\leq M_{B}\frac{\alpha(z)^{\nu+1}}{1-\alpha(z)}

(4.20)

where
M_{B}:=\sup_{z\in\partial B}\frac{|\chi(z)|}{2\pi r}
and
\alpha(z)=\frac{|z-\lambda|}{r}<1
.
Take now
r_{1}=r_{1}(B)
such that
B(\lambda,r_{1})\subset B
and, for all
z\in B(\lambda,r_{1})
,

M_{B}\frac{\alpha(z)^{\nu+1}}{1-\alpha(z)}<\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)|}{\nu!}|z-\lambda|^{\nu}.

(4.21)

From (
4.19
), (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) we have that,

for all
z\in B(\lambda,r_{1})
,

|\chi(z)|>\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)|}{\nu!}|z-\lambda|^{\nu}.

(4.22)

Now take
\overline{M}=\overline{M}(B)
large enough so that, for all
M\geq\overline{M}
,

\max_{|z-\lambda|=r_{1}}\varepsilon_{M}(z)\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)|}{\nu!}r_{1}^{\nu},

(4.23)

and define
r^{*}=r^{*}(M)
by

r^{*}:=\left(\frac{\max_{|z-\lambda|=r_{1}}\varepsilon_{M}(z)}{\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)|}{\nu!}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu}}.

(4.24)

Note that, by (
4.23
), it is
r^{*}\leq r_{1}
.
Then, by combining (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), we have

\displaystyle\max_{|z-\lambda|=r^{*}}\big|\chi(z)-\chi_{M}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{|z-\lambda|=r_{1}}\big|\chi(z)-\chi_{M}(z)\big|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\chi^{(\nu)}(\lambda)|}{\nu!}(r^{*})^{\nu}

\displaystyle<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|\chi(z)|},

where the last inequality holds for all
z
such that
|z-\lambda|=r^{*}
.
Hence for all
M\geq\overline{M}
we can apply Rouché’s theorem on
B(\lambda,r^{*})
, and conclude that
\chi
and
\chi_{M}
have the same number of zeros in
B(\lambda,r^{*})
, where each zero is counted as many times as its multiplicity.

More precisely, there exist
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\nu}
such that, for all
j=1,\dots,\nu
,
\chi_{M}(\lambda_{j})=0
and

|\lambda-\lambda_{j}|<r^{*}.

Now note from (
4.24
) that
r^{*}=r^{*}(M)\to 0
as
M\to\infty
.
Hence we have proved the convergence of characteristic roots with their multiplicity.
∎

Thanks to the previous theorem we know that each “true” characteristic root is approximated as
M\to\infty
with its multiplicity.

Finally, given a sequence
\{\lambda_{M}\}_{M}
satisfying
\chi_{M}(\lambda_{M})=0
for
M\geq\overline{M}
for some
\overline{M}\in\mathbb{N}
, and such that
\lambda_{M}\to\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
as
M\to\infty
, from the continuity of
\chi
and
\chi_{M}
we can conclude that
\chi(\lambda)=0
.

Combining the result of Theorem
7
with the bound in Lemma
3
, it follows that the order of convergence of the characteristic roots depends on the interpolation error of
\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
.
In particular, Cauchy’s remainder theorem
[

, Theorem 3.1.1]
gives the more precise bound

\|(I-\mathcal{L}_{M-1})\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\tau^{M}\|\psi_{\lambda}^{(M)}\|_{\infty}}{M!}\leq\frac{\tau^{M}|\lambda|^{M-1}\max\{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\mathop{}\!\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda},1\}}{M!}.

(4.25)

Hence, the modulus of

affects the order of convergence of the roots of the discrete characteristic equation, as observed also in
[

,

]
. More precisely, characteristic roots that are smaller in modulus are approximated with better accuracy for the same value of

. This can indeed be observed experimentally in Figure

5
Discussion and outlook

We have proposed a new approach for the approximation of a nonlinear RE with a system of ODE.
Similarly as in
[
4
,
5
]
, the approach is based on the formulation of the problem as an abstract Cauchy problem on a space of functions and its approximation via pseudospectral techniques.
The novelty here is that the space of functions is chosen as the space
AC_{0}
, rather than
L^{1}
. This is done by first integrating the state and then considering the integrated variable as state variable. A similar idea has been proposed for PDE in
[
10
]
.
Compared with the method proposed in
[
4
]
, this approach returns an approximation that is naturally formulated as a system of ODE, avoiding the algebraic equation emerging from the rule for extension of the RE.
In terms of efficiency, the reduction in computational cost is approximately tenfold, as evidenced in Figure
5
and Table
1
.

The attention here is restricted to the subspace
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}AC_{0}}\subset NBV
consisting of absolutely continuous functions. In
[
13
]
, the variation-of-constants formula and the construction of the solution operators of linear equations are in fact extended to
NBV
. So one might wonder whether pseudospectral approximation can be extended from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}AC_{0}}
to
NBV
? In the
NBV
setting, solutions are allowed to make jumps when time proceeds.
To capture the jumps of
v(t)
, the discretized vector
x(t)
should be allowed to have jumps as well. As a consequence, one cannot in general describe the dynamics of the vector in terms of just a differential equation.
In this case one could consider other techniques like spectral or finite elements approximations.

Although the construction is carried out in the space
NBV
, we resorted to bounds of the interpolation error in supremum norm (hence in a subspace of
C
) when proving the error bounds in Lemma
3
, since the literature about convergence of interpolation in supremum norm is traditionally more extensive than the corresponding one in the
L^{1}
-norm. We wonder if similar (or even stronger) conclusions can be stated in terms of the
NBV
norm, possibly exploiting results about the interpolation of
NBV
functions and bounds in terms of bounded variation norm
[
15
,
16
,
28
]
.

In our theoretical results, the condition (
4.5
) concerning the asymptotic (for the number of points going to infinity) behavior of the Lebesgue constant plays a crucial role. It is known that for Chebyshev zeros, cf. (
3.11
), the condition is satisfied, see
[
15
, Chapter 1.4.6]
. For other meshes the condition is not guaranteed. For instance, for Chebyshev extremal nodes without one or both endpoints it is only known that the quantity at the left-hand side of (
4.5
) is bounded for
M
tending to infinity
[
15
, Chapter 4.2]
.
However, Chebyshev extrema are widely used for pseudospectral differentiation and integration in the bounded interval, and efficient numerical routines exist for the associated differentiation matrix, and interpolation and quadrature weights
[
21
,
35
]
.
Our experimental results (not included here), show comparable results when using Chebyshev zeros or extrema, both in terms of accuracy and computational times. It would be interesting if either (
4.5
) could be verified for a large class of meshes, or the proof of convergence could be restructured such that only a weaker variant of (
4.5
), known to hold for a large class of meshes, is needed.

We proved that every characteristic root of a linear(ized) RE is approximated by the characteristic roots of the corresponding pseudospectral approximation when the dimension
M
is large enough.
Vice versa, the limit of every convergent sequence of discrete characteristic roots is a “true” characteristic root of the delay equation. An open problem is proving that the dimension of the unstable manifold is preserved for
M
large enough, or alternatively that the infinite- and finite-dimensional problems have the same number of eigenvalues lying in any right-half of the complex plane, if
M
is large enough.

We also remark that proving convergence of Hopf bifurcations requires not only the convergence of the eigenvalues, but also to verify that the transversality conditions are satisfied at the bifurcation point, and the direction of bifurcation is preserved as
M\to\infty

[
1
,
2
]
.
For DDE, this is done in
[
7
]
. To adapt the proofs to RE, one should obtain explicit formulas for the direction of Hopf and verify the convergence (see
[
1
, Remark 2.22]
and
[
7
]
).

The natural next step beyond the study of equilibria is the approximation of periodic solutions and their convergence as
M\to\infty
. In this case, one should first ensure the convergence of the finite-time solution maps, and hence focus the attention on the initial value problems associated with the RE and its ODE approximation.
A study of the convergence of the solution operators is in the authors’ pipeline.
The approximation of stability of the periodic solutions then relies on the approximation of the multipliers of the (time periodic) linearized equations, as formally proved in
[
36
]
.

The analysis of Nicholson’s blowflies equation, and in particular Figure
3
, shows the impossibility of using a discretization of a truncated interval to approximate the behavior of an equation with infinite delay. In this case, techniques specific to the unbounded integration interval seem necessary, along the lines of
[
11
,
37
]
.

To keep the notation simple, in Section
3
we introduced the approximation approach for scalar equations. We stress however that the extension to systems of equations is quite straightforward and can be done along the lines of
[
4
]
. In fact, the combination of the current method for RE with the pseudospectral discretization of DDE
[
4
,
38
]
provides a strategy for approximating general systems where a RE is coupled with a DDE, which arise frequently in population dynamics, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Appendix B
Chebyshev zeros with one additional endpoint

We include here some useful formulas for the construction of the differentiation matrix and the barycentric weights associated with the mesh
\Theta_{M}\cup\{0\}
used for the numerical simulations in the paper.
We first summarise the formulas for the classical Chebyshev zeros in the interval
(-1,1)
with the addition of the endpoint
1
, and then show how to obtain the corresponding formulas for the nodes shifted in the delay interval
(-\tau,0]
.

The Chebyshev zeros are defined as the roots of the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind of degree
M
, see e.g.
[
27
]
for a recent review.
In the interval
(-1,1)
, the nodes are given by the explicit formulas

x_{j}=\cos\left(\frac{2j-1}{2M}\pi\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

(2.1)

or the equivalent expression

x_{j}=\sin\left(\frac{M-(2j-1)}{2M}\pi\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

which maintains the exact symmetry of
x_{j}
about the origin in floating-point arithmetic
[
27
]
.

Let

\ell(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{M}(x-x_{j})

be the node polynomial.
Using barycentric weights, defined by

w_{j}=\frac{1}{\prod_{k\neq j}(x_{j}-x_{k})}=\frac{1}{\ell^{\prime}(x_{j})},\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

the polynomial
p
interpolating the points
y_{j}
on the nodes
x_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,M
, is efficiently computed by the barycentric interpolation formula

p(x)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{w_{j}}{x-x_{j}}y_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{w_{j}}{x-x_{j}}}

(2.2)

see, e.g.,
[
39
]
. We recall that the main computational advantage of (
2.2
) is that
p(x)
can be evaluated in
O(M)
floating-point operations, once the barycentric weights
w_{j}
are given.

For the nodes (
2.1
), the weights
w_{j}
are given by the explicit formulas

w_{j}=\frac{2^{M-1}}{M}(-1)^{j}\sin\left(\frac{2j-1}{2M}\pi\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

(2.3)

It is important to note that the factor
\frac{2^{M-1}}{M}
, independent of
j
,
cancels out
in the numerator and denominator of (
2.2
), so it can be ignored for computational reasons. We decided to include it explicitly in (
2.3
) because it will become important with the later addition of the node
x=1
.

To compute the differentiation matrix
(d_{kj})_{j,k=1,\dots,M}
, we first define

c_{j}:=\prod_{k\neq j}(x_{j}-x_{k})=w_{j}^{-1}.

The off-diagonal entries are computed by

d_{kj}=\frac{1}{x_{k}-x_{j}}\frac{c_{k}}{c_{j}}\qquad k\neq j

and the diagonal entries
d_{kk}
,
k=1,\dots,M
can be computed for instance by imposing that
\sum_{j=1}^{M}d_{kj}=0
for all
k=1,\dots,M
, see, e.g.,
[
21
,
35
]
.

We now construct the corresponding elements
\ell^{+}_{j}
,
w^{+}_{j}
,
d^{+}_{kj}
for the set of
M+1
nodes formed by (
2.1
) with the addition of
x_{0}=1
.
Define

K:=\ell(1)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\prod_{k=1}^{M}(1-x_{k})},

then
the Lagrange polynomials become

\ell^{+}_{0}(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{M}\frac{x-x_{k}}{1-x_{k}}=K\ell(x),\qquad\ell^{+}_{j}(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{M}\frac{x-x_{k}}{x_{j}-x_{k}}=\frac{x-1}{x_{j}-1}\ell_{j}(x),\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

and, for the barycentric weights, we have

w^{+}_{0}=K,\qquad w^{+}_{j}=\frac{w_{j}}{x_{j}-1}=\frac{2^{M-1}}{M}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{x_{j}-1}\sin\left(\frac{2j-1}{2M}\pi\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

It is now clear that, when simplifying the factor
\frac{2^{M-1}}{M}
from
w_{j}
and
w^{+}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,M
, we should also normalize the weight
w^{+}_{0}
.
Next we have, for
c^{+}_{j}:=(w^{+}_{j})^{-1}
,

d^{+}_{kj}=\frac{1}{x_{k}-x_{j}}\frac{c^{+}_{k}}{c^{+}_{j}},\qquad k\neq j.

and the diagonal coefficients
d^{+}_{kk}
are then normalized as before by imposing
\sum_{j=0}^{M}d^{+}_{kj}=0
for all
k=0,\dots,M
.
To approximate integrals over the interval
(-1,1)
we can use quadrature formulas corresponding to the nodes (
2.1
), with corresponding weights

q_{k}=\frac{2}{M}\left(1-2\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor M/2\rfloor}\frac{\cos 2j\xi_{k}}{4j^{2}-1}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

where
\xi_{k}=\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{M}
are the arguments of the cosine in (
2.1
), see
[
27
]
.
We also recall that, to avoid cancellations when computing the differences
x_{k}-x_{j}
in the differentiation matrix, one can use the trigonometric identities (see
[
35
]
)

x_{k}-x_{j}=\cos\xi_{k}-\cos\xi_{j}=2\sin\left(\frac{\xi_{k}+\xi_{j}}{2}\right)\,\sin\left(\frac{\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}}{2}\right).

Finally, we can construct the desired mesh in the interval
(-\tau,0]
by simply taking
\theta_{0}=0
, and shifting and scaling the nodes
x_{j}
as

\theta_{j}=\frac{\tau}{2}(x_{j}-1),\qquad j=0,\dots,M.

The corresponding barycentric weights
w^{\tau}_{j}
are obtained by a suitable scaling factor via

w^{\tau}_{j}=\frac{1}{\prod_{k\neq j}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})}=\left(\frac{2}{\tau}\right)^{M}w^{+}_{j},\qquad j=0,\dots,M.

Note again that the multiplicative factor, independent of
j
, can be ignored as it cancels out in (
2.2
);
finally the entries of the differentiation matrix
(d^{\tau}_{kj})_{k,j=0,\dots,M}
and the quadrature weights
q^{\tau}_{k}
satisfy

d^{\tau}_{kj}=\frac{2}{\tau}d^{+}_{kj},\qquad q^{\tau}_{k}=\frac{\tau}{2}q_{k},\qquad k,j=0,\dots,M.
