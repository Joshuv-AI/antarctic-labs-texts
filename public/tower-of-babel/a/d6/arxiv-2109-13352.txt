Harmonic analysis of isotropic fields on the sphere with arbitrary masks
and Olivier Doré

Abstract
Obtaining constraints from the largest scales of a galaxy survey is challenging due to the survey mask allowing only partial measurement of large angular modes. This scatters information from the harmonic-space 2-point function away from the diagonal and introduces coupling between modes. In this paper, we derive a custom eigenbasis adapted to any particular survey geometry so that all information is retained on the diagonal. At the expense of a somewhat complex pixel- and selection-function-window, the result is a diagonal 2-point function with a simple shot noise, and a diagonal covariance matrix in the case of a Gaussian random field. We derive the basis on the surface of a sphere, and we use it to construct a 3D spherical Fourier-Bessel power spectrum estimator assuming a survey geometry that is separable in the angular and radial directions.

Introduction

For galaxy surveys over the full sky, spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})

provide a convenient basis for the analysis of an isotropic field that accounts
for all wide-angle effects. In practice, however, galaxy surveys are restricted
to the partial sky, e.g., due to the exclusion zone of the Milky Way or due to
time constraints of a deep survey. This non-isotropic mask scatters information
to off-diagonal terms in the harmonic/Fourier-space 2-point function, which
typically leads to a pseudo-
C_{\ell}
power spectrum with coupled modes and a
complicated covariance matrix.

For a homogeneous and isotropic random field in 3D or 2D, the 2-point function,
formally constructed as the outer product of the field, becomes
diagonal
in the eigenbasis of the Laplacian when restricted to the domain of
the survey (see
Section

2
for details). The basis functions
must satisfy

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f

\displaystyle=-k^{2}f

(1.1)

anywhere within the survey. For example, if the survey is a rectangular box in
3D, then the eigenfunctions are of the form
e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}
. On the 2D
sphere, the eigenfunctions are the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, and
adding the radial direction for the full volume of a sphere results in the
spherical Fourier-Bessel (SFB) basis
j_{\ell}(kr)\,Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, where

j_{\ell}(kr)
are spherical Bessel functions. The SFB basis is well-suited
for the radial/angular separation, and in

GrasshornGebhardt+:2021arXiv210210079G
we
allow the survey geometry to be a thick spherical shell, in which case the
spherical Bessels become superpositions of spherical Bessels of the first and
second kind
[
3
]
.

In this paper, we push this concept further by deriving an eigenbasis of the
Laplacian for arbitrary masks. For example, in a typical Fourier-analysis with a rectangular
analysis box, parts of the box are left empty, and this leads to the scattering
of information into off-diagonal terms and coupling between modes. By adapting
the basis functions to the survey geometry, we essentially fit the
Fourier-analysis box perfectly onto the survey. However, as a simplification we
assume that the radial selection function and angular masks are separable.

Several convenient properties follow from our procedure. First, no information
is scattered to off-diagonal terms in the 2-point function and modes are not
coupled by the window function. Second, Poissonian shot noise is simply

1/{\bar{n}}
and the local average effect (or integral constraint) can be modeled
in a very simple fashion, eliminating power in the
\ell=0
modes, only. Third,
to leading order the covariance matrix is diagonal and no harder to calculate
than modeling the 2-point function. Thus, compared to traditional methods, we
avoid the need for pseudo-
C_{\ell}
and complicated coupling matrices, and we
have a significantly reduced computational cost for producing an analytical
covariance matrix.

Our method has two main downsides. First, the survey geometry and pixel window
both enter the observed power spectrum in a nontrivial way, and this leads to a
somewhat complicated combination of pixel and geometric window that cannot be
easily inverted. Second, with an arbitrary mask,
Eq.

1.1
does not
have an analytical solution and must be solved numerically.
Therefore,
our method is currently limited to only large scales
due to the high up-front computational cost of deriving the eigenfunctions,
which typically scales as
N^{3}
, where
N
is the number of pixels on
the sky or radial bins (as we assume separable radial and angular masks).
In our current implementation, with a modern laptop, this allows the number of pixels
N
to be up to about

{10}^{4}
or
{10}^{5}
, corresponding to a
resolution
n_{\mathrm{side}}=32
or 64 for a full-sky survey, and correspondingly higher resolution for a smaller survey area.

In this paper, we follow
SAITO200868
,
DelSole+:2015JCli…28.7420D

to convert
Eq.

1.1
into an integral
equation that is readily adapted to arbitrary survey geometries, including
disconnected geometries, e.g., when there are northern and southern survey
areas. We will differ from
DelSole+:2015JCli…28.7420D
by using the HEALPix

[
6
]
scheme for pixelization on a sphere. We
show that the resulting eigenfunctions are linear combinations of spherical
harmonics with effective non-integer
\ell

The method relies on the calculation of eigenfunctions to the Laplacian,
adapted to a specific survey geometry. That is, we let the reader

2
Laplaceology

In this section we aim to explain the use of the Laplacian as a generator for
eigenfunctions that will be useful for cosmological analysis. In effect, the
harmonic transform generated by the Laplacian diagonalizes the 2-point
function of a homogeneous field, which then represents an efficient compression
of the data with a simple covariance matrix.

The symmetries to exploit are the translational and rotational invariance of
the statistical field. Rotational invariance manifests itself as a
2D-translational invariance on the curved sky, so here we will only consider
translational invariance explicitly. Translational invariance in the radial
direction is broken by line-of-sight effects such as the growth of structure.
However, as long as such effects are statistically slowly varying with
redshift, assumption of invariance will still give a convenient basis.

To demand compression is to demand that the power spectrum is the diagonal form
of the correlation function. To make this more precise, we define

\displaystyle\xi({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\delta({\bm{r}})\,\delta({\bm{r}}^{\prime})\right\rangle,

(2.1)

\displaystyle P({\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\delta({\bm{k}})\,\delta^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(2.2)

If our basis functions are
f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})
, then these second moments are related
by

\displaystyle P({\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,W({\bm{r}})\,f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\,W({\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,f^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,\xi({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,,

(2.3)

where the window function
W({\bm{r}})=1
inside the survey and vanishes outside
it.

To exploit the translational invariance, we assume that there is some
transformation parameterized by
\lambda^{\mu}
that leaves
the correlation function
\xi({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})
invariant. In our case
\lambda^{\mu}

parameterizes a translation. Then, the correlation function is

\xi({\bm{r}}+\lambda^{\mu},{\bm{r}}^{\prime}+\lambda^{\mu})
with
\mu=1,2,3
, and homogeneity
demands that the first derivative of
\xi
w.r.t. any parameter
\lambda^{\mu}

vanishes. Further, we can choose
\lambda^{\mu}=-{\bm{r}}
to show that the
correlation function only depends on
{\bm{s}}={\bm{r}}^{\prime}-{\bm{r}}
under this symmetry.

With this translation symmetry,
Eq.

2.3
becomes

\displaystyle P({\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}s\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,W({\bm{r}})\,f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})\,W({\bm{r}}+{\bm{s}})\,f^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{r}}+{\bm{s}})\,\xi({\bm{s}})\,.

(2.4)

Diagonalization is achieved when the basis functions
f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})
satisfy

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,\Big[W({\bm{r}})\,f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})\Big]\Big[W({\bm{r}}+{\bm{s}})\,f^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{r}}+{\bm{s}})\Big]

\displaystyle=\delta^{D}({\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})\,g({\bm{k}},{\bm{s}})\,,

(2.5)

for some function
g({\bm{k}},{\bm{s}})
. For example, if
W({\bm{r}})=1
everywhere in

\mathds{R}^{3}
, then the standard Fourier basis
f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})=e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{r}}}

satisfies this relation with
g({\bm{k}},{\bm{s}})=(2\pi)^{3}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{s}}}
.

For compression,
Eq.

2.5
must be satisfied for
all
{\bm{s}}
. This is especially true for an infinitesimally small
{\bm{s}}
. In the
limit
{\bm{s}}\to 0
,
Eq.

2.5
becomes the
orthogonality condition

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,\Big[W({\bm{r}})\,f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})\Big]\Big[W({\bm{r}})\,f^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{r}})\Big]

\displaystyle=\delta^{D}({\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})\,g({\bm{k}},0)\,.

(2.6)

Application of the gradient w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
in

Eq.

2.5
, and taking the limit
{\bm{s}}\to 0
gives
the further condition

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,\Big[W({\bm{r}})\,f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})\Big]\,\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Big[W({\bm{r}})\,f^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{r}})\Big]

\displaystyle=\delta^{D}({\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})\,\nabla_{{\bm{s}}}g({\bm{k}},{\bm{s}})|_{{\bm{s}}=0}\,.

(2.7)

Thus, the demand is that the basis functions
f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})
are orthogonal
functions over the domain of the survey, and they are also orthogonal to their
gradient provided that
{\bm{k}}\neq{\bm{k}}^{\prime}
.

As is evident from
Eq.

2.7
, choosing the basis
functions
f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})
to be eigenfunctions of the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{r}}

will satisfy
Eqs.

2.6
and
2.7
.

Applying the gradient twice to an eigenfunction, it is further evident that any
eigenfunction of the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{r}}
is also an eigenfunction of the
Laplacian
\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}
. Indeed, the set of eigenfunctions to the Laplacian
also satisfies

Eqs.

2.6
and
2.7
.

A caveat in our derivation is that, strictly speaking, we have limited our
results to only infinitesimal
{\bm{s}}
. However, repeatedly taking derivatives
w.r.t.
{\bm{s}}
allows us to build up an infinite series of conditions.
Indeed, we could have derived

Eqs.

2.6
and
2.7
by
expanding
W({\bm{r}}+{\bm{s}})f^{*}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{r}}+{\bm{s}})
in a Taylor series, and the
higher-order terms would lead to exactly these additional conditions.
Therefore, our result that
W({\bm{r}})f({\bm{k}},{\bm{r}})
as Laplacian eigenfunctions leads
to a diagonal 2-point function holds for finite
{\bm{s}}
as well in a
wide variety of cases.

Therefore, using eigenfunctions of the Laplacian gives us a basis that
exploits the translational symmetry for the 2-point function. This also
holds true for the isotropy in 2D on the spherical sky, which is essentially a
translation symmetry for the sky position
{\hat{\bm{r}}}
.
Furthermore, as we will show in
Section

4.6
, for a
Gaussian random field this also means that the covariance matrix will have a
very simple form, namely it will be diagonal.

Angular Eigenfunctions

In this section we extend
DelSole+:2015JCli…28.7420D
using the
HEALPix scheme. The key idea is to convert the differential equation

Eq.

1.1
to an integral equation using the Green’s function, because
the integral equation lends itself naturally to a generalization to
disconnected domains. Furthermore, the use of the HEALPix scheme allows some
simplification and better numerical accuracy.

3.1
Integral operator

Instead of using the differential operator
\nabla^{2}
,

DelSole+:2015JCli…28.7420D
use the equivalent integral equation,
derived as follows. The Green’s function
G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})
is the response of a
signal at
{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}
on the pixel at
{\hat{\bm{r}}}
. For the Laplacian, the Green’s
function is the solution to the equation

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=-\delta^{D}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}-{\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(3.1)

That is, the Green’s function is the inverse to the Laplacian,
\nabla^{-2}
.
Then, for some arbitrary source function
f({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, the equation

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}Y

\displaystyle=-f({\hat{\bm{r}}})

(3.2)

has the solution

\displaystyle Y({\hat{\bm{r}}})

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\,G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,f({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})

(3.3)

on the domain
D
. To solve the Helmholtz equation
Eq.

1.1
and
define the harmonic basis functions, one can substitute
f=\lambda Y
and obtain

\displaystyle Y({\hat{\bm{r}}})

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\,G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,Y({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,.

(3.4)

The Fredholm equation
Eq.

3.4
is formally equivalent to

Eq.

1.1
. However,
Eq.

3.4
is more readily adapted to
arbitrary boundaries, simply by changing the integration domain.

To discretize, we integrate
Eq.

3.4
over the pixel
i
, or define

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{i}^{s}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,,

(3.5)

where
\Omega_{i}
is the area of pixel
i
, the parameter
s
is chosen so that
the operator
G_{ij}
defined below becomes symmetric, and the window

w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})=1/\Omega_{i}
within the area of pixel
i
and vanishes outside. The
average value of
Y({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})
over the pixel
j
is then
\Omega_{j}^{-s}z_{j}
.
Next, split the integral in
Eq.

3.4
into a sum over pixels
j
, and
integrate over pixel
i
,

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\Omega_{i}^{s}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\sum_{j}\Omega_{j}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,Y({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})

(3.6)

\displaystyle\approx\lambda\sum_{j}\Omega_{i}^{s}\,\Omega_{j}^{1-s}\,z_{j}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,,

(3.7)

where we make the assumption that
Y({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})
does not vary much across the
pixel
j
and it can be pulled out of the integral. Written in matrix form, we
get the eigenequation

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda\,\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{z}\,,

(3.8)

where we defined the Green’s matrix
\mathbf{G}
with elements

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{i}^{s}\,\Omega_{j}^{1-s}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,.

(3.9)

G_{ij}
is the response of pixel
i
to a signal in pixel
j
. As

Eq.

3.1
shows,
G
is symmetric, i.e.,

G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=G({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime},{\hat{\bm{r}}})
, and so
G_{ij}=G_{ji}
is also symmetric,
provided we either choose

\displaystyle s=\frac{1}{2}\,,

(3.10)

or we choose a pixelization scheme such as HEALPix where all pixels have the
same area
\Omega_{i}
.

The basis functions satisfying
Eq.

3.8
are linear
combinations of the spherical harmonics, as we show in

Section

5
. Combining
Y_{\ell m}
with differeing

\ell
, therefore, may lead to an effective non-integer
\ell
for individual
basis functions.

The Green’s function for the Laplacian on a sphere is given by

\displaystyle G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\left(2\sin^{2}\frac{\rho}{2}\right),

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\Omega_{i}^{s}\,\Omega_{j}^{1-s}}{4\pi}\ln\bigg(2\sin^2\frac{\theta_j - \theta_i}{2}
+ 2\sin\theta_i \, \sin\theta_j \, \sin^2\frac{\phi_j - \phi_i}{2}\bigg),

(3.13)

where the integrals contributed a factor

When

\displaystyle G_{ii}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\Omega_{i}}\int_{i}\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,2\pi\int_{0}^{\rho_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho\,\rho\left[-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle G_{ii}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\Omega_{i}}{4\pi}\left(1-\ln\frac{\Omega_{i}}{2\pi}\right).

A crucial part of interpreting the cryo-measured power spectrum is its relation
to the full-sky standard-spherical harmonic power spectrum. This requires
modeling of both geometry (as part of the cryofunktions) and the pixel window.

Consider two cryobases
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
, where
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
is the basis for a
full sky, and
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
for a particular survey mask. We then define the effect
of the mask by linear transformation matrices
\mathbf{w}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
such
that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}_{0}\,,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{k}_{0}\,.

(3.28)

If the full sky has
N_{\mathrm{pix}}
pixels, and the survey has

N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\mathrm{survey}}\leq N_{\mathrm{pix}}
pixels, then the matrix

\mathbf{w}
is
N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\mathrm{survey}}\times N_{\mathrm{pix}}
and it selects
which pixels from the full sky are observed by the survey. The matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
has the same size as
\mathbf{w}
, and can be obtained from the
definition and the transform

Eqs.

3.23
and
3.24
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{Y}_{0}\mathbf{k}_{0}\,.

(3.29)

Hence,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{Y}_{0}\,.

(3.30)

By construction,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}\,,

(3.31)

since
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}^{T}
selects the rows and columns in the survey for any matrix

\mathbf{A}
, in particular
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}
.
Note that
Eq.

3.22
then implies

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}\,,\quad\text{if }\mathbf{B}=\alpha\mathbf{I}

(3.32)

for some scalar
\alpha
that is the same for both the full sky and partial
sky.

Then, the power spectra measured from two bases
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
are related
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{1}=\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{P}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{T}\,.

(3.33)

The matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
contains both the effect of the pixelization
operation as well as the effect of the survey geometry. However, by virtue of
using eigenfunctions to the Laplacian, both
\mathbf{P}_{1}
and
\mathbf{P}_{0}
are
statistically diagonal, as we have showed explicitly for a
Roman
-like
mask in
Fig.

7
.

3.7.1
Continuous bases

In practice, theoretical predictions will assume a continuous sky, or,

n_{\mathrm{side}}\to\infty
. Only in this limit is no information lost.
Therefore, in this subsection we take the
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
basis to be precisely this
continuous limit for a full-sky survey. The result is usually referred to as
the pixel window
correction
4
4

4

https://healpix.jpl.nasa.gov/html/intronode14.htm
.

Eq.

3.33
relates the measured power spectrum using
two different bases
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
. Nowhere have we assumed that these two
bases have a common origin (such as being eigenbases of the Laplacian). In the
limit
n_{\mathrm{side}}\to\infty
for the
Y_{0}
base, the key difference is that
we now have the
operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\to\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(3.34)

To be explicit, the transform from harmonic to configuration space for the
continuous
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
basis is

\displaystyle\delta_{0}({\hat{\bm{r}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}Y_{k}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\widetilde{\delta}_{0,k}\,,

(3.35)

where
k
indexes the
\ell m
modes, and for clarity we leave off the suffix 0
on
Y_{k}
. The pixelization scheme (for the
\mathbf{Y}_{1}
basis) gives pixel values

\displaystyle\delta_{1,j}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta_{0}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,,

(3.36)

and the transform

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}Y^{-1}_{1,ij}\,\delta_{1,j}\,.

(3.37)

Then, the harmonic decompositions in the two bases are related by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\widetilde{w}_{ik}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{0,k}\,,

(3.38)

where the transformation matrix is

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}Y^{-1}_{1,ij}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y_{k}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(3.39)

The indices
i
and
j
run from one to
N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\mathrm{survey}}
, and
the index
k
runs over infinitely many
\ell m
modes. In practice, of course,
the sum needs to be truncated and only a finite number of modes are kept.

Clearly, the challenge is in calculating the integral in

Eq.

3.39
, which is the pixel window for a
full-sky survey. Explicitly writing
k=\ell m
, that integral is

\displaystyle w_{\ell m}(j)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(3.40)

A well-established approximation for HEALPix is to write

\displaystyle w_{\ell m}(j)

\displaystyle\simeq w_{\ell}(j)\,Y_{\ell m}(j)\,,

(3.41)

where
Y_{\ell m}(j)
is typically evaluated at the center of pixel
j
.
For
Y_{\ell m}(j)
we take the real spherical harmonics
Eq.

A.3
.
Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{i,\ell m}

\displaystyle=w_{\ell}\sum_{j}Y^{-1}_{1,ij}\,Y_{\ell m}(j)\,,

(3.42)

where we assume that
w_{\ell}
is independent of the pixel
j
.

The power spectra are related by

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{1,m}\widetilde{\delta}^{*}_{1,n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{ks}\widetilde{w}_{mk}\,\widetilde{w}^{*}_{ns}\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{0,k}\widetilde{\delta}^{*}_{0,s}\right\rangle.

(3.43)

Next, we assume that only the diagonal of
\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{T}
contains information due
to the isotropy of the sky. Indeed, since we use the Laplacian we know that

\mathbf{P}_{1}
is statistically diagonal, and we write

\displaystyle C^{1,\mathrm{obs}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left|\widetilde{w}_{nk}\right|^{2}C_{k}\,.

(3.44)

In terms of
\ell
-modes,

\displaystyle C^{1,\mathrm{obs}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}C_{\ell}\sum_{m}\left|\widetilde{w}_{n,\ell m}\right|^{2}\,.

(3.45)

An example
\widetilde{H}^{\ell}_{\lambda}=\sum_{m}|\widetilde{w}_{\lambda\ell}|^{2}

transformation matrix is shown below in the left of

Fig.

.

We demonstrate our cryo-window calculation in the full-sky and for a

Euclid
-mask in
Fig.

. The model is sufficiently
complicated that an inversion is nontrivial except for the full sky.

Note that in the bottom panels of
Fig.

suppressed on all scales. We attribute this to the cryo-window being a

3.8
Shot noise

The sampling of the density field by a limited number of points leads to a shot
noise component in the power spectrum. To show that the shot noise is

1/{\bar{n}}
in the cryobasis, we use the equations from

Peebles:1973ApJ…185..413P
,
Feldman+:1994ApJ…426…23F
, but for
pixels on the sphere,

\displaystyle\left\langle n({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\left[1+\xi({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\right]+\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta^{D}({\hat{\bm{r}}}-{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,,

(3.46)

\displaystyle\left\langle n({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1}\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,,

(3.47)

\displaystyle\left\langle n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-2}\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})+\alpha^{-1}\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta^{D}({\hat{\bm{r}}}-{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,.

(3.48)

The number density of galaxies per solid angle is

\displaystyle n({\hat{\bm{r}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\delta^{D}({\hat{\bm{r}}}-{\hat{\bm{r}}}_{i})\,,

(3.49)

where
{\hat{\bm{r}}}_{i}
is the position of galaxy
i
.

Now, the density contrast on the sphere is dependent on the galaxy-assignment
scheme. We use the nearest-grid-point scheme (NGP). Thus, the number density of
galaxies in pixel
i
is

\displaystyle n_{i}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,,

(3.50)

where
w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
is non-zero inside the pixel and vanishes elsewhere. We
normalize
w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
by the pixel solid angle such that

\displaystyle 1=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(3.51)

The density contrast in pixel
i
is now

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{i}-\alpha\,n_{r,i}}{\bar{n}}=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\left[\frac{n({\hat{\bm{r}}})-\alpha\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}})}{\bar{n}}\right],

(3.52)

where
\bar{n}
is the average number density of galaxies in a pixel,

n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
is the number of galaxies in a pixel for a random catalogue, and

\alpha
adjusts the random catalogue size to the survey catalogue size. Hence,
the correlation function for pixels
i
and
j
is

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\left\langle n({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})-\alpha\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})-\alpha\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,n({\hat{\bm{r}}})+\alpha^{2}\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,n_{r}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(3.53)

With
Eqs.

3.46
,
3.47
and
3.48
we get

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\bigg[\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,\xi({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})+(1+\alpha)\,\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta^{D}({\hat{\bm{r}}}-{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\bigg].

(3.54)

As a simplification, we assume
\bar{n}({\hat{\bm{r}}})=\bar{n}
is the same in each
observed pixel. (That is, we consider binary masks only.) Further, we assume an
infinitely large random sample, or
\alpha\to 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,\xi({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(3.55)

Further, assuming pixels don’t overlap and have area
\Omega_{i}
,

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,w_{i}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}^{\prime}\,w_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,\xi({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})+\frac{\delta^{K}_{ij}}{\bar{n}\,\Omega_{i}}\,.

(3.56)

Transforming into cryo-space just the shot noise,

\displaystyle N_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{shot}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}\,\Omega_{i}}\sum_{ij}Y^{-1}_{\lambda,i}\,Y^{*,-1}_{\lambda^{\prime},j}\,\delta^{K}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\bar{n}\,\Omega_{i}}\left(\mathbf{Y}^{-1}\,\mathbf{Y}^{-1,T}\right)_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{K}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}}{\bar{n}}\,,

(3.57)

where we used the results from
Section

3.4
. In

8
the shot noise has been subtracted.

4
Spherical Fourier-Bessel decomposition

In this section we extend the
cryo
method to a 3D survey geometry. In
principle, any survey geometry could be accommodated. However, the
computational complexity rapidly increases with the number of
voxels
5
5

5

We use the term
voxel
to refer to cells in 3D space.
.

Furthermore, since, e.g., growth of structure will destroy the full 3D
translational symmetry, it is desirable to assume isotropy on the sky, only. To
achieve this, we write down the Laplacian in spherical coordinates,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi^{2}}\,.

(4.1)

The eigenfunctions of the Laplacian are separable. That is, the eigenfunctions
are composed of radial and angular eigenfunctions. For a full sky the angular
eigenfunctions are the
Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
with eigenvalue
\ell(\ell+1)
, and

Section

3
dealt with our angular cryofunctions for partial skies. The
radial basis for an infinite flat universe are the spherical Bessels

j_{\ell}(kr)
, and we turn to the cryo-version of these in the sections that
follow.

Using the eigenfunctions to
Eq.

4.1
allows
exploitation of the isotropy on the sky, while keeping the radial decomposition
as a separate problem. The transform is known as the
spherical
Fourier-Bessel
(SFB) transform. Compared to a more standard Yamamoto-estimator
[
11
,
12
,
13
]

where a single line of sight for each pair of galaxies is chosen, the SFB
transform allows each individual galaxy its own line of sight. Therefore,
wide-angle effects are fully modeled in this approach, and evolution with
redshift can be accounted for as well. In a previous paper we have developed an
estimator for the SFB power spectrum,
SuperFaB
, where we explicitly
modeled the window function and only retained modes in a pseudo-
C_{\ell}

fashion
[
2
]
. In this paper, we choose
such SFB-like approach because it makes the exploitation of the isotropic
symmetry simple for window functions that are separable into an angular mask
and radial selection function, and the cryofunks will allow the full
information of the 2-point function to be contained in the pseudo-
C_{\ell}

estimator.

The SFB power spectrum depends not just on the overall wavenumber

k
, but also on the angular quantum number
\ell
, and this is important to
distinguish radial and angular modes.
A full-volume eigendecomposition would,
in general, leave us with indeterminate
\ell
. Therefore, we here opt to
explicitly separate angular and radial modes.
However, this means that the survey geometry must look the same for
every sight line. More specifically, we require that the window can be written
as
W({\bm{r}})=\phi(r)\,M({\hat{\bm{r}}})
for some radial selection
\phi(r)
and angular
window
M({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, and in this paper we further limit ourselves to binary
selections.
We call the resulting estimator
CryoFaB
.

The angular eigenfunctions influence the radial eigenfunctions because the
angular mask will generally lead to non-integer

The full Laplacian is shown in
Eq.

4.1
. The angular part
was treated in
Section

3
. To treat the radial part, we first write an
eigenfunction as
f_{\ell}(k_{n\ell};{\bm{r}})=g_{n\ell}(r)\,Y_{\ell}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
(with

m
-dependency implicit in
\ell
, and
n
enumerating
k_{n\ell}
-modes), then
the angular part of the Laplacian acts on
Y_{\ell}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
only, and what is
left is the radial part of the Laplacian acting on the radial eigenfunction

g_{n\ell}(r)
,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\!\left(r^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)-\ell(\ell+1)\right],

(4.2)

and the differential equation for the radial eigenfunctions is

\displaystyle\nabla_{r}^{2}\,g_{n\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=-k_{n\ell}^{2}\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,,

(4.3)

where
-k_{n\ell}^{2}
are the eigenvalues.
The Green’s function satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{r}^{2}\,G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\delta^{D}(r^{\prime}-r)\,,

(4.4)

with solution

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\big(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\big)^{\ell}&\text{ for }r\leq r^{\prime}\,,\\
\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\big(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\big)^{-\ell-1}&\text{ for }r^{\prime}\leq r\,.\end{cases}

(4.5)

Eq.

4.5
is not symmetric under exchange of

r
and
r^{\prime}
. However, it can be written

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r^{p-2}\,r^{\prime p}\,\widetilde{G}(r,r^{\prime})\,.

(4.6)

Then, for any
p
, the kernel
\widetilde{G}(r,r^{\prime})
is symmetric. While symmetry
is not required, it allows the use of more efficient algorithms for determining
the eigenfunctions.

Using the Green’s function to get radial solutions to the eigenequation

Eq.

4.3
, we get the integral equation

\displaystyle g_{n\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=k_{n\ell}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\,G(r,r^{\prime})\,g_{n\ell}(r^{\prime})\,.

(4.7)

Same as for the angular part, we discretize by introducing a pixel-averaged
quantity

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta r_{i}^{s}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{m}\,w_{i}(r)\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,,

(4.8)

where
w_{i}(r)=1/\Delta r_{i}
inside the pixel and zero outside, and we introduce
a weight
r^{m}
that will help symmetrize the Green’s matrix. The approximation
for
g_{n\ell}(r^{\prime})
inside the pixel
j
is
\Delta r_{j}^{-s}\,r^{\prime-m}\,z_{j}
. Then,

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\simeq k^{2}\sum_{j}\Delta r_{i}^{s}\Delta r_{j}^{1-s}\,z_{j}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{p+m-2}\,w_{i}(r)\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\,r^{\prime p-m}\,w_{j}(r^{\prime})\,\widetilde{G}(r,r^{\prime})\,,

(4.9)

which is the matrix equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\,\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{z}\,,

(4.10)

where
\mathbf{G}
has elements

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Delta r_{i}^{s}\Delta r_{j}^{1-s}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{p+m-2}\,w_{i}(r)\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\,r^{\prime p-m}\,w_{j}(r^{\prime})\,\widetilde{G}(r,r^{\prime})\,.

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\Delta r_{i}^{s}\Delta r_{j}^{1-s}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{m}\,w_{i}(r)\int\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\,r^{\prime-m}\,w_{j}(r^{\prime})\,G(r,r^{\prime})\,.

(4.12)

G_{ij}
is symmetric provided that
s=\frac{1}{2}
and
m=1
. The Green’s function

Eq.

4.5
is well-defined everywhere except at
r=0
or

r^{\prime}=0
. Therefore, we evaluate
G_{ij}
by taking the center of each bin, or

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle\simeq\Delta r_{i}^{s}\Delta r_{j}^{1-s}\,r_{i}^{m}\,r_{j}^{-m}\,G(r_{i},r_{j})\,.

(4.13)

Similar to the matrix
\mathbf{B}
in
Eq.

3.18
that relates the eigenfunction
\mathbf{z}

of the symmetric
G_{ij}
to the basis function of the Laplacian,
there is a corresponding matrix
\mathbf{B}
for the radial eigenfunctions.
This matrix is diagonal with elements given by

\displaystyle B_{ij}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{ij}\,\Delta r_{i}^{s}\,r_{i}^{m}\,.

(4.14)

The procedure outlined here gives for each angular
\ell
-mode a set of radial
eigenfunctions with radial mode
k_{n\ell}
. The eigenfunctions are defined
only up to an overall phase factor which we fix so that all eigenvectors have
the same sign at
r_{\mathrm{max}}
. The first few eigenvectors are shown in

Fig.

. The exquisite agreement with

GrasshornGebhardt+:2021arXiv210210079G
shows that the boundary
conditions are in this case essentially the same as in that paper, namely they
are potential boundaries where the basis functions satisfy the Helmholtz
equation
Eq.

4.3
inside the survey and
\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}g_{n\ell}=0
outside the survey and matching at the boundary

[also see
14
, Appendix A]
.
For a more formal discussion of the boundary conditions, see
SAITO200868
.
The first few basis functions are illustrated in
Fig.

, where
we used
radial bins of size
\Delta r=$10\text{\,}{\mathrm{\text{$h$}}}^{-1}\text{\,}\mathrm{Mpc}$
. The small
differences to
GrasshornGebhardt+:2021arXiv210210079G
vanish when
decreasing the size of the bins.

k_{n\ell}
and

4.2
Into Cryospace

In this section we explicitly show the full 3D transform. Since the
eigenfunctions to the Laplacian can be separated into radial and angular parts,

f_{\ell}(k_{n\ell};{\bm{r}})=g_{n\ell}(r)\,Y_{\ell}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, the full 3D transform from
configuration space to cryo-space is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{n\ell}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,Y_{\ell}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta({\bm{r}})\,,

(4.15)

(again, with degenerate
\ell
-modes implicitly labeled). Discretizing and
performing the angular transform before the radial transform, we can write this
as a matrix equation,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\delta}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\bm{\delta}\,,

(4.16)

where
\mathbf{R}
is the matrix of radial eigenfunctions,
\mathbf{A}
are the angular
eigenfunctions, and
\mathbf{S}
is a matrix that reorders the elements in the data
vector, and we introduce it for convenience of the implementation, as follows.
If we first do the angular transform for each radial shell, then we can write
this operation as a block diagonal matrix
\mathbf{A}
acting on the data vector
sorted into radial shells. Similarly, the radial transform can be written as a
block diagonal matrix
\mathbf{R}
on a data vector sorted into angular modes. Thus,
the matrix
\mathbf{S}
is needed to reshuffle the data vector so that both
\mathbf{R}
and

\mathbf{A}
can be block diagonal, each block operating on a single angular mode or
single radial shell, respectively, and the full transform is given by

Eq.

4.16
.

Cryo-Window

In this section we calculate the combined effect of the pixel window and the
geometry of the survey. The separation of radial and angular scales passes all
the way through, starting with the assumption that the voxels are described by
a separable binning function. That is, the configuration-space voxel window for
radial bin
i
and angular pixel
j
is

\displaystyle w_{ij}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=b_{i}(r)\,h_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,,

(4.17)

where
h_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
specifies the
j
-th HEALPix pixel, and
b_{i}(r)
is a top-hat
specifying the
i
-th radial bin.

In the following we label the continuous-basis modes by
\delta_{\ell m}(k)

and the pixelized cryo-modes by
\delta_{N\lambda}
, where

k_{N\lambda}
are the radial cryomodes and
\lambda
are the angular
cryomodes. That is,
\lambda=(\ell,m)
is a combined index in case the

\ell
-modes are degenerate, and the doublet
(N,\lambda)
in cryospace takes
the role of the triplet
(k,\ell,m)
in the full-sky continuous basis.

Transforming from continuous-limit harmonic-space density contrast to
configuration space, then binning, and finally transforming into cryo-space
yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}^{1}_{N\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}R_{\lambda,Ni}^{1,-1}\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{1,-1}\int\mathrm{d}r\,b_{i}(r)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,h_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\int\mathrm{d}k\sum_{\ell m}R^{0}_{\ell}(r,k)\,A^{0}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\widetilde{\delta}^{0}_{\ell m}(k)\,,

(4.18)

where we attach the suffix
0
to indicate the full-sky continuous-limit basis,
and the index
1
to indicate the pixelized finite-volume basis. That is, the
harmonic-space pixelization operation is encoded in

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{\lambda}^{\ell m}(k_{N\lambda},k)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{b}^{\ell}_{\lambda}(k_{N\lambda},k)\,\widetilde{h}_{\lambda}^{\ell m}\,,

(4.19)

where we define the radial and angular harmonic-space pixelization matrices

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}^{\ell}_{\lambda}(k_{N\lambda},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}R_{\lambda,Ni}^{1,-1}\int\mathrm{d}r\,b_{i}(r)\,R^{0}_{\ell}(r,k)\,,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{\lambda}^{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{1,-1}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,h_{j}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\sum_{\ell m}A^{0}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(4.21)

Note that the second line is identical to
Eq.

3.39
.

The power spectrum in basis
i
is, due to isotropy,

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}^{i}_{N\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}^{i,*}_{N^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\,C^{i}_{\lambda NN^{\prime}}\,.

(4.22)

Therefore, the relation between two bases 0 and 1 corresponding to two distinct
surveys and/or resolutions is

\displaystyle C^{1}_{\lambda NN^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\int\mathrm{d}k^{\prime}\sum_{\ell m}\widetilde{b}^{\ell}_{\lambda}(k_{N\lambda},k)\,\widetilde{b}^{\ell,*}_{\lambda}(k_{N^{\prime}\lambda},k^{\prime})\left|\widetilde{h}_{\lambda}^{\ell m}\right|^{2}\,C^{0}_{\ell}(k,k^{\prime})\,.

(4.23)

In this proof-of-concept paper we only consider a radially homogeneous universe
as our test case. We thus neglect the evolution along the line of sight due to,
e.g., the growth of structure. Then,

C^{0}_{\ell}(k,k^{\prime})=\delta^{D}(k-k^{\prime})\,C^{0}_{\ell}(k)
and

C^{1}_{\lambda NN^{\prime}}=\delta^{K}_{NN^{\prime}}\,C^{1}_{\lambda N}
, and

Eq.

4.23
becomes

\displaystyle C^{1}_{\lambda N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{n}\widetilde{H}_{\lambda}^{\ell}\,\widetilde{B}^{\ell}_{\lambda}(k_{N\lambda},k_{n})\,C^{0}_{\ell}(k_{n})\,,

(4.24)

where the integral over
k
was discretized into a sum over
n
, and we defined
the angular and radial pixel functions

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{\lambda}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\left|\widetilde{h}_{\lambda}^{\ell m}\right|^{2},

(4.25)

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{\ell}_{\lambda}(k_{N\lambda},k_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{k_{n}-\Delta k/2}^{k_{n}+\Delta k/2}\mathrm{d}k\left|\widetilde{b}^{\ell}_{\lambda}(k_{N\lambda},k)\right|^{2},

(4.26)

assuming that
C^{0}_{\ell}(k)
is constant across a step of size
\Delta k
. Both
matrices
\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{B}
are sparse.
\widetilde{H}
is peaked
around
\ell\sim\lambda
, and
\widetilde{B}
is sharply peaked around
k_{N\lambda}\sim k
, with some deviation for larger
k_{N\lambda}
. We show examples in

Fig.

10
.

4.4
Local Average Effect

Before we can compare estimator results with our analytical model, the issue of
the local average effect, or integral constraint, must be addressed. In
practice, the average number density
{\bar{n}}(z)
must be measured from the
survey itself, and this leads to the local average effect, which decreases the
power in the
\ell=0
mode. This is often called the
integral constraint

[
15
,
16
]
or the

local average effect

[
17
,
18
]
. In this section, we detail the derivation.

Measuring the average number density as a function of redshift is accomplished
by dividing the total number of galaxies in a redshift slice by the volume of
that slice. That is, given the estimated number density
n({\hat{\bm{r}}},z)
, we get

[
19
, also see, e.g.,]

\displaystyle{\bar{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,n({\hat{\bm{r}}},z)\,.

(4.27)

Relating this to the true average number density
{\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}
(assumed
constant),
n({\hat{\bm{r}}},z)=\left(1+\delta({\bm{r}})\right){\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}
, we get

\displaystyle{\bar{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\bar{\delta}(z)\right){\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}\,,

(4.28)

where the average density contrast in the redshift slice at
z
is

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,\delta({\bm{r}})\,.

(4.29)

Therefore, the estimated density contrast is

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=\frac{n({\hat{\bm{r}}},z)-{\bar{n}}(z)}{{\bar{n}}(z)}=\frac{\delta({\bm{r}})-\bar{\delta}(z)}{1+\bar{\delta}(z)}\simeq\delta({\bm{r}})-\bar{\delta}(z)\,,

(4.30)

where in the last line we assume a large volume so that
\bar{\delta}(z)
is
small. Compared to, e.g.,
Taruya+:2021PhRvD.103b3501T
, we have no
explicit window function
W({\bm{r}})
as we are assuming a binary selection and
mask in this paper, and
{\bm{r}}
is understood to be within the survey.

Transforming into cryospace, the
\bar{\delta}(z)
term only adds to the
\ell=0

multipole. Hence, only
\ell=0
modes are affected by the local average effect,
or integral constraint.

Concretely, the transform is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}R_{ni}^{\lambda,-1}\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{-1}\left[\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j},z_{i})-\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\right]

(4.31)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\delta}_{n\lambda}-\sum_{i}R_{ni}^{\lambda,-1}\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{-1}\,,

(4.32)

where
i
enumerates redshift bins, and
j
enumerates angular pixels on the
sphere. Define

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{-1}\,.

(4.33)

Then, for a full-sky survey,

A_{\lambda}=\sum_{j}\Omega_{\mathrm{pix}}\,Y^{*}_{\lambda m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j})\simeq\sqrt{4\pi}\,\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}\delta^{K}_{m0}
.
More generally, for
\lambda=0
we find that

\displaystyle A_{j0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}}\,,

\displaystyle A_{0j}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{pix}}}{\sqrt{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}}\,,

(4.34)

which follows from

A_{0j}^{-1}=\Omega_{\mathrm{pix}}\,A_{j0}
and

\sum_{j}A_{0j}^{-1}\,A_{j0}=1
.
Thus,

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}}\,\frac{4\pi\,N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\mathrm{survey}}}{N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\text{full-sky}}}\,\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}=\sqrt{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}\,\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}\,.

(4.35)

The measured power spectrum is

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n^{\prime}\lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{n\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{n^{\prime}\lambda}\right\rangle-A_{\lambda}\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{-1}\sum_{ii^{\prime}}R_{ni}^{\lambda,-1}\,R_{n^{\prime}i^{\prime}}^{\lambda,-1}\left\langle\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j},z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-A_{\lambda}\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{-1}\sum_{ii^{\prime}}R_{n^{\prime}i}^{\lambda,-1}\,R_{ni^{\prime}}^{\lambda,-1}\left\langle\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j},z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+A^{2}_{\lambda}\sum_{ii^{\prime}}R_{ni}^{\lambda,-1}\,R_{n^{\prime}i^{\prime}}^{\lambda,-1}\left\langle\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle.

(4.36)

With
Eq.

4.34
the remaining angular transforms at
\lambda=0
are of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{j}A_{\lambda j}^{-1}\,\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j},z_{i})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}}{\sqrt{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}}\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}},z_{i})=A_{\lambda}\,\bar{\delta}(z)\,,

(4.37)

where we used the definition of
\bar{\delta}(z)
(
Eq.

4.29
) for the
last equality. Therefore, the last three terms in
Eq.

4.36
can be
combined, and we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n^{\prime}\lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{n\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{n^{\prime}\lambda}\right\rangle-A_{\lambda}^{2}\sum_{ii^{\prime}}R_{ni}^{\lambda,-1}\,R_{n^{\prime}i^{\prime}}^{\lambda,-1}\left\langle\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle.

(4.38)

Next, we express
\left\langle\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle
in terms of the
power spectrum
C_{lnn^{\prime}}
.
Discretizing
Eq.

4.29
and expressing the configuration-space density
contrast in terms of its cryo-transform, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{pix}}^{2}}{(4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}})^{2}}\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\left\langle\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j},z_{i})\,\delta({\hat{\bm{r}}}_{j^{\prime}},z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle

(4.39)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{pix}}^{2}}{(4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}})^{2}}\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\sum_{\Lambda\Lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{NN^{\prime}}A_{j\Lambda}\,A_{j^{\prime}\Lambda^{\prime}}\,R^{\Lambda}_{iN}\,R^{\Lambda^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}N^{\prime}}\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{\Lambda N}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{\Lambda^{\prime}N^{\prime}}\right\rangle.

(4.40)

Performing the sum over
j
and
j^{\prime}
first,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{pix}}\sum_{j}A_{j\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}\,\delta^{K}_{\Lambda 0}=A_{\Lambda}\,,

(4.41)

we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\bar{\delta}(z_{i})\,\bar{\delta}(z_{i^{\prime}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}\sum_{NN^{\prime}}\,R^{0}_{iN}\,R^{0}_{i^{\prime}N^{\prime}}\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{0N}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{0N^{\prime}}\right\rangle.

(4.42)

That is, a smaller sky coverage leads to a larger variance of
\bar{\delta}
.

Finally, inserting into
Eq.

4.38
, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n^{\prime}\lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(1-\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}\right)\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{n\lambda}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{n^{\prime}\lambda}\right\rangle,

(4.43)

or

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\lambda nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}\right)C_{\lambda nn^{\prime}}-\delta^{K}_{\lambda 0}\,N^{\mathrm{shot}}_{\lambda nn^{\prime}}\,,

(4.44)

where the shot noise is
N^{\mathrm{shot}}_{\lambda nn^{\prime}}=\delta^{K}_{nn^{\prime}}/{\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}
under our assumption of constant
{\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}
.

To summarize, to first order in cryospace the local average effect becomes very
simple: the power in the
\ell=0
modes vanishes, and we may get a negative
number due to the subtraction of the shot noise.

typically complicated nature of the covariance matrix, we refer to the specific

text.

Since the cryo-modes of a homogeneous field are uncoupled, that is, since

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{i}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{ij}\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{i}^{2}\right\rangle,

(4.45)

the covariance between cryo-modes
i
and
j
is

\displaystyle V_{ij}=\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{i}^{2}\,\widetilde{\delta}_{j}^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{i}^{2}\right\rangle\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{j}^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\,\widetilde{\delta}^{K}_{ij}\left\langle\widetilde{\delta}_{i}^{2}\right\rangle^{2},

(4.46)

where we used Wick’s theorem under the assumption of a Gaussian field, and
i

and
j
each stand for a tuple
(k_{n\ell},\ell)
. That is,
the covariance matrix is diagonal. Equivalently, the
1\sigma
variance is

\displaystyle\sigma\left(C_{\ell}(k)\right)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{k\ell}}}\,\left[C_{\ell}(k)+\frac{1}{{\bar{n}}}\right],

(4.47)

where
N_{k\ell}
is the number of modes collected into a bandpass in both
k

and
\ell
.

We show a comparison with lognormal simulations in
Fig.

13
. In
the figure, we show the full covariance matrices for each of our masks for the
largest modes
\ell<10
and
k<$2\text{\times}{10}^{-2}\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{$h$}}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$
, as well as the
ratio to the predicted value using
Eq.

4.47
with
N_{i}=1
.

5
Discussion

Several points deserve some discussion.
We discuss the nature of the boundary conditions, the interpretation
of the angular momentum
\ell
and the not-well-defined nature of the magnetic
quantum number
m
, and the relation to the methods by

Mortlock+:2002MNRAS.330..405M
.

5.1
Non-Local Boundary Conditions

The boundary conditions implied by our method are non-local
[
4
]

and not straightforward to interpret, as we show in the following.
First, we define the Laplacian operator such that

\mathcal{L}f({\bm{r}})=-\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}f({\bm{r}})
, and we define the Green’s operator

\mathcal{K}
such that in
n
dimensions

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}f({\bm{r}})=\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{n}r^{\prime}\,G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,.

(5.1)

Also, because
G
is a function of
{\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}
, we have

\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})=\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}^{2}G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})
.
Then, the commutator is

\displaystyle[\mathcal{K}\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{K}]f({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=-\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{n}r^{\prime}\left[G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}^{2}f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})-\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}^{2}G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})\right]

(5.2)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial D}\mathrm{d}^{n-1}r^{\prime}\,{\hat{\bm{n}}}\cdot\left[G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})-\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})\right],

(5.3)

where
{\hat{\bm{n}}}
is the outward orthogonal unit vector to the boundary and we used
Green’s second identity
Eq.

A.7
. The left hand side must
vanish for
{\bm{r}}\in D
if the Laplacian and its Green’s function are to commute,
as they must if
f({\bm{r}})
is an eigenfunction.
Then, for
{\bm{r}}\in\partial D
, we get the result that the boundary condition is

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}\mathrm{d}^{n-1}r^{\prime}\,G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,{\hat{\bm{n}}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}f({\bm{r}})+\int_{\partial D}\mathrm{d}^{n-1}r^{\prime}\,{\hat{\bm{n}}}\cdot\nabla_{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,f({\bm{r}}^{\prime})\,.

(5.4)

The extra term
-\frac{1}{2}f({\bm{r}})
appears when moving
{\bm{r}}
from the interior of

D
to the boundary. We refer the reader to
SAITO200868
and
references therein for a detailed derivation.

A basis function must both be an eigenfunction to the Laplacian and satisfy the
non-local boundary condition
Eq.

5.4
. Evidently, Green’s
function
G({\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime})
plays a crucial role in defining the boundary condition.
Indeed, any harmonic function, that is, one that satisfies
\nabla^{2}h=0
, can be
added to the Green’s function (thus also modifying the Laplacian on the
boundary), and that would lead to a different boundary condition.

SAITO200868
lists some examples, as is our procedure in

Section

3.3
to obtain the monopole.

5.2
Interpretation of effective
\ell
and
m

Here we argue in a qualitative manner that the magnetic quantum number
m
is
not well-defined, and
that the identification of our effective
\ell
in

Eq.

3.25
is justified as a measure of angular scale.

Unlike the standard spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, our eigenfunctions
to the Laplacian are not eigenfunctions to the
z
-component of the angular
momentum operator, and, therefore, the eigenfunctions do not have well-defined

m
. Instead, the
m
label potentially degenerate modes
\ell
. Of course, for
a power spectrum analysis of an isotropic field this does not pose a problem,
because the result does not depend on the magnetic quantum number
m
.
When moving to 3D, the
\ell
modes would in general be similarly
indeterminate. This poses a problem for the SFB power spectrum, as it does
depend on both
k
and
\ell
. Therefore, we have opted to restrict ourselves
to separable radial and angular selections.

The Laplacian eigenfunctions satisfy
\nabla^{2}f=-\ell(\ell+1)f

on the full and partial spheres. Indeed, this equation is satisfied everywhere
inside the survey. Hence, the
\ell
must probe similar scales, whether it is
the full sky or the partial sky.
We, therefore, argue that the identification
Eq.

3.25
leads to

\ell
that probe comparable angular scales.
Visual inspection of the eigenfunctions

Figs.

1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5

agrees with this interpretation.

A related question is the relation to the associated Legendre polynomials of
the first and second kinds,
P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)
and
Q_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)
,
for non-integer
\ell
.
Taking just the angular part of the Laplacian
Eq.

4.1
and
changing variables to
\mu=\cos\theta
, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\mu^{2}}-2\mu\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}+\lambda f+\frac{1}{1-\mu^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi^{2}}\,,

(5.5)

for an eigenfunction
f(\mu,\phi)
of the Laplacian,
\nabla^{2}f=-\lambda f
. In general,
our eigenfunctions are not eigenfunctions of the
{\hat{\bm{z}}}
-component of the
angular momentum operator. Therefore, if we write

\partial^{2}f/\partial\phi^{2}=-m^{2}f
, then, in general, we expect
m^{2}
to be a
function of
\phi
. The question is, whether
m^{2}
is a function of
\mu
. If
it is not, then the identification
\lambda=\ell(\ell+1)
is exact for
non-integer
\ell
, because

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\mu^{2}}-2\mu\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}+\ell(\ell+1)f-\frac{m^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\,f

(5.6)

is the associated Legendre equation as long as
m^{2}
is independent of

\mu=\cos\theta
.
We conjecture that
m^{2}
is indeed independent of
\mu
.

5.3
Relation to
Mortlock+:2002MNRAS.330..405M

A similar goal was pursued in
Mortlock+:2002MNRAS.330..405M
, with a
different approach to obtaining an orthonormal basis supported on the domain of
the survey. They start with the coupling matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,\mathbf{y}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\mathbf{y}^{T}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,,

(5.7)

where the integration is over the domain of the survey, and the observed
harmonic coefficients are
\mathbf{k}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{k}
for a full-sky analysis on the
partial sky. Then, they find linear combinations of the spherical harmonics

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}({\hat{\bm{r}}})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}({\hat{\bm{r}}})

6
6

6

This is a different
\mathbf{B}
than in

Eq.

3.18
.
such that the coupling matrix in this new basis becomes the
identity,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}=\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,\mathbf{y}^{\prime}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\mathbf{y}^{\prime T}({\hat{\bm{r}}})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^{T}=\mathbf{I}\,.

(5.8)

The goal then is to find an
N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\mathrm{survey}}\times N_{\mathrm{pix}}^{\text{full-sky}}
matrix
\mathbf{B}
that satisfies
Eq.

5.8
.
Their various approaches use the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{C}
or some
approximation thereof.

In this regard, their approach and ours agree, since our basis functions are
orthonormal over
D
by design and
Eq.

5.8
is satisfied. In this
sense, our method is a special case of the more general approach presented in

Mortlock+:2002MNRAS.330..405M
.

The specific form of our
\mathbf{B}
that contains the coefficients of the linear combination of spherical harmonics is obtained as follows.
We expand the Green’s function

G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})
of the operator
\nabla^{2}
in terms of spherical harmonics

Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
,

\displaystyle G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,a_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,,

(5.9)

for some coefficient functions
a_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
. Apply
\nabla^{2}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{2}G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell m}\nabla_{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{2}Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,a_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell m}\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,a_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,.

(5.10)

By definition of the Green’s function
Eq.

3.1
, and the
orthogonality of the eigenfunctions, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{2}G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=-\delta^{D}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}-{\hat{\bm{r}}})=-\sum_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,.

(5.11)

Setting
Eqs.

5.10
and
5.11
equal, multiplying by

Y^{*}_{LM}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
, and integrating over
{\hat{\bm{r}}}
, we get

\displaystyle L(L+1)\,a_{LM}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=-Y^{*}_{LM}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,.

(5.12)

Therefore,
Eq.

5.9
becomes

\displaystyle G({\hat{\bm{r}}},{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})=-\sum_{\ell m}\frac{Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})}{\ell(\ell+1)}\,.

(5.13)

Inserting
Eq.

5.13
into
Eq.

3.4
,

\displaystyle Z_{\lambda}({\hat{\bm{r}}})

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\sum_{\ell m}\frac{Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})}{\ell(\ell+1)}\,Z_{\lambda}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell m}\left[\frac{-\lambda}{\ell(\ell+1)}\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime}\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\,Z_{\lambda}({\hat{\bm{r}}}^{\prime})\right]Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,,

(5.14)

where we relabeled the cryofunction as
Z_{\lambda}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
to avoid confusion
with the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
. The term in square brackets
is some constant coefficient
c_{\lambda,\ell m}
that expresses the
cryofunctions in terms of spherical harmonics.

6
Conclusion

In this paper we have developed a proof-of-concept of using eigenfunctions of
the Laplacian adapted to the exact geometry of a survey. That is, we fit
the Fourier-transform box exactly onto the survey geometry. On the sphere, we
obtain linear combinations of the real spherical harmonics with effectively
non-integer
\ell
. We show some of the eigenfunctions in

Figs.

1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
.

In this limit both the 2-point function in harmonic space and its covariance
matrix become diagonal for a Gaussian random field (see

Figs.

7
and
13
). Poissonian shot noise
also takes on the simple form
1/{\bar{n}}
, and the first-order local
average effect can be treated exactly analytically. This comes at the
expense of a somewhat complex pixel and window function, which is
not straightforward to invert.
However, the simplicity of the covariance matrix gives hope for a relatively
efficient generalization to higher order statistics, though we have not looked
further into this.

Our approach builds on the work by
SAITO200868
,
DelSole+:2015JCli…28.7420D
in the applied mathematics and climate science
literature. Compared to them we formalize the symmetry of the discretized
Green’s function
G_{ij}
, generalize to HEALPix, and
we develop the pixel/geometry window and covariance matrix.

We also develop a 3D SFB power spectrum estimator by using the approach
separately in the radial and angular directions. We get essentially unbiased
results for
\ell\lesssim n_{\mathrm{side}}
, as shown in

Figs.

11
and
12
. However, we have assumed a
perfectly homogeneous universe, and this will need to be generalized if applied
to a real survey with, e.g., growth of structure along the light cone.

The cryo-approach is dependent on calculating eigenfunctions of the Green’s
matrix of size
N_{\mathrm{pix}}\times N_{\mathrm{pix}}
, where
N_{\mathrm{pix}}
is
the number of pixels in the survey. Therefore, only the largest scales are
computationally feasible: a full-sky survey at resolution
n_{\mathrm{side}}=32

will require 1.2 GB of storage; at the next higher resolution this becomes
19 GB; at
n_{\mathrm{side}}=128
it is 309 GB using 64-bit floats for the
angular transform.
This is a limitation of our current implementation on a modern laptop,
and it can likely be optimized.
In any case, these need to be computed only once for a given mask, and can
therefore be precomputed.

We have assumed a binary mask and selection function throughout.
However it is straightforward to generalize the method to non-binary masks,
e.g., when stars block out part of a pixel. We leave this to a future paper.

Our code will be available publically at

https://github.com/hsgg/CryoFaBs.jl
, once approved for release by our
institution.

Spherical Bessel functions and spherical harmonics satisfy orthogonality
relations

\displaystyle\delta^{D}(k-k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2kk^{\prime}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}{r}\,r^{2}\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,j_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)\,,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\delta^{K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{K}_{mm^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}{\Omega}_{{\hat{\bm{r}}}}\,Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(A.2)

Real spherical harmonics are defined as

\displaystyle Y^{\mathrm{real}}_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2}\,(-1)^{m}\,\imaginary[Y_{\ell|m|}(\theta,\phi)]&\text{ if }m<0\,,\\
Y_{\ell 0}(\theta,\phi)&\text{ if }m=0\,,\\
\sqrt{2}\,(-1)^{m}\,\real[Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)]&\text{ if }m>0\,.\end{cases}

(A.3)

An alternative to the Haversine formula
Eq.

3.12
is

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\arctan\frac{x}{y}\,,

(A.4)

where

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\Big([\sin\theta^{\prime}\,\sin\Delta\phi]^{2}+[\sin\theta\,\cos\theta^{\prime}-\cos\theta\,\sin\theta^{\prime}\,\cos\Delta\phi]^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(A.5)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\cos\theta\,\cos\theta^{\prime}+\sin\theta\,\sin\theta^{\prime}\,\cos\Delta\phi\,.

(A.6)

This requires proper inversion of the tangent, which in
Julia

is implemented as
atan(x,y)
.

Green’s second identity in three dimensions is

\displaystyle\int_{D}\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,\left(\psi\nabla^{2}\varphi-\varphi\nabla^{2}\psi\right)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial D}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,\,{\hat{\bm{n}}}\cdot\left(\psi\nabla\varphi-\varphi\nabla\psi\right),

(A.7)

where
{\hat{\bm{n}}}
is the outward-directed unit vector on the boundary at
{\bm{r}}
.

Appendix B
Radial Green’s function

We derive the radial Green’s function using two different techniques.

B.1
First derivation

Express the Green’s function in terms of its spherical Bessel transform,

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,\widetilde{G}(k,r^{\prime})\,.

(B.1)

Inserting into the defining equation for the Green’s function,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}k\,\widetilde{G}(k,r^{\prime})\,\big[-k^{2}j_{\ell}(kr)\big]

\displaystyle=-\delta^{D}(r^{\prime}-r)\,.

(B.2)

Integrate over
\frac{2k^{\prime 2}}{\pi}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,j_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)
to get

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(k^{\prime},r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{\prime 2}}{\pi}\,j_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r^{\prime})\,,

(B.3)

and

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{\prime 2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k^{\prime}\,j_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)\,j_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r^{\prime})=\frac{r^{\prime 2}}{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k^{\prime}\,k^{\prime-1}\,J_{\nu}(k^{\prime}r)\,J_{\nu}(k^{\prime}r^{\prime})\,,

(B.4)

where
\nu=\ell+\frac{1}{2}
, and we used that the spherical Bessel function is
related to the cylindrical Bessel function by
j_{\ell}(kr)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2kr}}\,J_{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}(kr)
. The integral has an analytic
solution for
r\leq r^{\prime}

[
23
, Eqs. 10.22.56 and 10.22.57]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\,\nu}\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)^{\nu}\,.

(B.5)

Furthermore, the integral is symmetric under exchange of
r
and
r^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
Eq.

B.4
becomes

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\big(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\big)^{\ell}&\text{ for }r\leq r^{\prime}\,,\\
\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\big(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\big)^{-\ell-1}&\text{ for }r^{\prime}\leq r\,.\end{cases}

(B.6)

B.2
Another derivation

The solution is the solution to the homogeneous differential equation in the
two regimes
r<r^{\prime}
and
r>r^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A\,r^{\ell}+B\,r^{-\ell-1}\,,&\text{ if }0\leq r<r^{\prime}\,,\\
C\,r^{\ell}+D\,r^{-\ell-1}\,,&\text{ if }r>r^{\prime}\,,\end{cases}

(B.7)

for some
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
.
Requiring finite Green’s function at
r=0
and
r\to\infty
,

\displaystyle B=C=0\,,

(B.8)

when
\ell\neq 0
.
Continuity at
r=r^{\prime}
demands

\displaystyle A\,r^{\prime\ell}

\displaystyle=D\,r^{\prime-\ell-1}\,.

(B.9)

Next, integrate
Eq.

4.4
over a small interval around

r^{\prime}
, more precisely the interval

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}[r^{\prime}-\epsilon,r^{\prime}+\epsilon]
for
\epsilon>0
. We find

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[r^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}G(r,r^{\prime})\right]_{r=r^{\prime}-\epsilon}^{r=r^{\prime}+\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-r^{\prime 2}\,,

(B.10)

or more explicitly,

\displaystyle A\,\ell\,r^{\prime\ell-1}+D\left(\ell+1\right)r^{\prime-\ell-2}

\displaystyle=1\,.

(B.11)

for
\ell\geq 1
.

Eqs.

B.9
and
B.11
are solved by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\,r^{\prime-\ell}\,,

(B.12)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\,r^{\prime\ell+1}\,.

(B.13)

Therefore,

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\big(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\big)^{\ell}&\text{ for }r\leq r^{\prime}\,,\\
\frac{r^{\prime}}{2\ell+1}\big(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\big)^{-\ell-1}&\text{ for }r^{\prime}\leq r\,.\end{cases}

(B.14)
