Bounds on an effective thermalization beyond the Zeno limit
Guilherme Zambon, Diogo O. Soares-Pinto

Abstract
Developing protocols for preserving information in quantum systems is a central quest for implementing realistic quantum computation. In this regard, the quantum Zeno effect has emerged as a widely utilized technique to safeguard classical information stored in quantum systems. However, existing results pertaining to this method often assume operations performed infinitely fast on the system of interest, which only serves as an approximation to real-world scenarios where the temporal resolution of any experimental apparatus is inherently finite. In this study, we go beyond this conventional assumption and derive the effective Zeno dynamics for any time interval between operations. Our analysis considers a qubit undergoing thermalization, as described by a generalized amplitude damping channel, while the operations performed consist of projections onto an orthonormal basis that may or may not coincide with the pointer basis to which the system is thermalizing. By obtaining the probability of successfully storing a bit of information after a given time, we investigate the performance of the protocol in two important scenarios: the limit of many interventions, with a first-order correction to the Zeno limit, and the limit of very few interventions. In doing so, we provide valuable insights into the protocol’s performance by establishing bounds on its efficacy. These findings enhance our understanding of the practical applicability of the quantum Zeno effect in preserving classical information stored in quantum systems, allowing for better design and optimization of quantum information processing protocols.



I
Introduction

Building a universal quantum computer has been so far one of the most challenging tasks of the twenty-first century Physics. Since Feynman’s famous insight on employing controlled quantum systems to simulate other quantum systems found in nature
Feynman 2018
, physicists have been developing theoretical and experimental tools to make these simulations possible. Ideally, everything that is needed to perform useful quantum computation is a set of physical qubits and the ability to experimentally implement any sequence of logical gates on this set. In reality, however, this might not be enough.

As no quantum system is ever truly isolated from its surroundings, during the computation the evolution of the experimentally controlled physical systems might be affected by the interactions between them and their environment
Rivas and Huelga 2012
. Such effects, generally known as noise, may continuously modify the quantum state stored in the qubits, which is problematic, as it is essential to preserve this state during and in between computational steps to reliably implement any algorithm on a real quantum computer.

To address these challenges, techniques such as quantum error correction and dynamical decoupling can be employed. While these methods offer valuable approaches for protecting quantum states, they have inherent drawbacks. Quantum error correction requires storing an informational qubit in multiple physical qubits, necessitating substantial overhead and resources
Terhal 2015
;
Shor 1995
;
Laflamme et al. 1996
;
Calderbank and Shor 1996
;
Steane 1996
;
Freedman et al. 2003
;
Brun et al. 2006
;
Pastawski et al. 2015
;
Fiusa et al. 2023
;
Boyers et al. 2019
. On the other hand, dynamical decoupling relies on precise knowledge of the qubit-environment dynamics, which may be challenging to ascertain in realistic scenarios
Viola and Lloyd 1998
;
Viola et al. 1999
;
Viola and Knill 2003
;
Khodjasteh and Lidar 2005
;
Uhrig 2007
;
West et al. 2010
;
Yang et al. 2011
;
Khodjasteh et al. 2013
;
Du et al. 2009
;
Szwer et al. 2010
;
Biercuk et al. 2009
;
Souza et al. 2012
;
Zhang and Suter 2015
;
Zurek 1981
.

Alternatively, if our goal is solely to preserve classical information stored in a quantum system, the quantum Zeno effect presents a compelling approach, as it circumvents the drawbacks of the aforementioned techniques
Itano et al. 1990
;
Presilla et al. 1996
;
Kondo et al. 2016
;
Möbus and Wolf 2019
;
Burgarth et al. 2019
;
Burgarth et al. 2020
;
Becker et al. 2021
. It consists in performing repeated projective measurements on the system of interest, which in the limit of infinitely frequent operations (Zeno limit) is known to freeze the dynamics at some subspaces and to generate an effective Zeno dynamics at others
Álvarez et al. 2006
;
Álvarez et al. 2007
;
Fernández-Alcázar and Pastawski 2015
.

Given a free dynamics and a set of operations to be periodically performed, one could obtain the effective dynamics in the Zeno limit using a suitable result, like those found in Refs.
Möbus and Wolf 2019
;
Burgarth et al. 2019
;
Burgarth et al. 2020
;
Becker et al. 2021
. However, in practical experimental scenarios there is always a limitation on the frequency at which external operations can be realized. This limitation implies that while the Zeno limit serves as a useful approximation in certain cases, it is ultimately unattainable.

Here, we address this limitation by explicitly determining the effective dynamics for any time interval between operations for the relevant case of a qubit undergoing thermalization, as described by a generalized amplitude damping channel. The operations performed are non-selective projective measurements in as basis that may or may not coincide with the pointer basis to which the qubit is thermalizing. We particularly explore the Zeno limit, a first order correction to it and also the limit of very few operations performed. Finally, these results are used to establish bounds on the protocol’s efficiency, as quantified by the probability of successfully storing one bit of classical information on a thermalizing qubit for some given period of time.

II
Setup

Consider a qubit used to store a classical bit of information over some period of time. Given its interaction with the environment, it must be treated as an open quantum system, whose state evolves non-unitarily
Rivas and Huelga 2012
. A common approach to this situation is to model the effects of the environment with a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}
, such that the state of the qubit at some time
t
will be given by

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{3}K_{i}(t)\rho_{\mathrm{in}}K_{i}^{\dagger}(t),

where
K_{i}(t)
are the Kraus operators associated to the channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
is the initial state of the qubit
Wilde 2013
.

For many relevant situations, the environment can be simply modeled as a thermal bath at some fixed temperature
T
, so the qubit is expected to thermalize at this temperature and asymptotically approach a thermal state. This type of evolution is achieved by using the amplitude damping channel
\mathcal{E}_{AD}

Nielsen and Chuang 2000
, whose Kraus operators are given by

K_{0}=\sqrt{p}\quantity(\ket{e_0}\bra{e_0}+\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket{e_1}\bra{e_1}),

(2a)

K_{1}=\sqrt{p\gamma}\ket{e_0}\bra{e_1},

(2b)

K_{2}=\sqrt{1-p}\quantity(\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket{e_0}\bra{e_0}+\ket{e_1}\bra{e_1}),

(2c)

K_{3}=\sqrt{(1-p)\gamma}\ket{e_1}\bra{e_0}.

(2d)

In the above equations,
\gamma:=\gamma_{t}
is a temporal parameter monotonically increasing in
t
, satisfying
\gamma_{0}=0
and
\gamma_{t}\to 1
as
t\to\infty
. It can be verified that
\gamma_{t}=0
implies
\mathcal{E}_{AD}(\rho)=\rho
, as expected, while
\gamma_{t}=1
implies
\mathcal{E}_{AD}(\rho)=p\ket{e_0}\bra{e_0}+(1-p)\ket{e_1}\bra{e_1}
, which is a thermal state in the basis
\{\ket{e_0},\ket{e_1}\}
. It follows that
p=p(T)
is the population of the ground state in thermal equilibrium. Setting its energy to be zero and the energy of the excited state to be
E
, yields

p(T)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-E/k_{B}T}},

(3)

implying
p\to 1
as
T\to 0
and
p\to 1/2
as
T\to\infty
.

Notice that in thermal equilibrium the system will be diagonal in the
pointer basis

\{\ket{e_0},\ket{e_1}\}
, determined by the system-environment dynamics
Zurek 1981
. Nevertheless, we assume the experimenter could choose any other basis to be the
computational basis

\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
in which the classical bit is initially stored, either as
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
or as
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\ket{1}\bra{1}
. Each
\ket{j}
can still be written in the pointer basis using a convenient parametrization,

\ket{0}=\cos\theta\ket{e_0}+e^{i\phi}\sin\theta\ket{e_1},

(4a)

\ket{1}=\sin\theta\ket{e_0}-e^{i\phi}\cos\theta\ket{e_1},

(4b)

for
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
. In Appendix
A
we show that this is indeed the most general form of the computational basis, given the orthogonality constraint
\bra{0}\ket{1}=0
.

To analyze the dynamics of the system we must propose an explicit expression for
\gamma_{t}
, which depends on the specific physical situation that is being represented by the amplitude damping channel. Here, we assume that the bath is much larger than the qubit and also that their coupling is sufficiently weak, implying that there are no memory effects due to information backflow, i.e., the dynamics of the qubit is Markovian
Rivas et al. 2014
;
Breuer et al. 2016
;
De Vega and Alonso 2017
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Chruściński 2022
. This is done by imposing that the family of quantum channels is CP-divisible and homogeneous in time

\mathcal{E}_{t+t^{\prime}}=\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{t}.

(5)

In Appendix
B
it is shown that this condition implies an exponential decay for
\gamma_{t}
, that is

\gamma_{t}=1-e^{-t/\tau},

(6)

where
\tau
is some characteristic relaxation time determined by the system-environment interaction dynamics
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
.

Consider the qubit starts in a pure state in the computational basis
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\ket{j}\bra{j},~j=0,1
. It is then subject to non-selective projective measurements in this basis after every interval
\Delta
of time, which can be seen as the temporal resolution of the experimental apparatus. In the Zeno limit we have
\Delta\to 0
, while in the limit of few measurements after some time
t
we have
\Delta\sim t
. We define the ground state population after some time
t
as

a(t):=\bra{0}\rho_{t}\ket{0},

(7)

with
a_{0}:=a(0)=1-j
. Now we determine how this population evolves in time.

III
Results and Discussion

III.1
Effective dynamics

Between measurements, the state of the qubit will simply evolve according to the amplitude damping channel, exponentially approaching the thermal state on the pointer basis. This implies the population
a(t)
will change and the coherences
\bra{0}\rho_{t}\ket{1}
and
\bra{1}\rho_{t}\ket{0}
will become non-zero. However, when the next measurement is performed these coherences will vanish, while the populations will remain unchanged. Therefore, the time dependence of
a(t)
will be determined by the probabilities of the system transitioning from one state to another within an interval
\Delta
of time. We use the conditional probability rule to define the transitioning probabilities

\displaystyle a(t+\Delta)=

\displaystyle P(0,t+\Delta)

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(0,t)P(0,t+\Delta|0,t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle P(1,t)P(0,t+\Delta|1,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(t)P_{0|0}+(1-a(t))P_{0|1},

where we have defined
P_{0|1}
as the probability of the qubit transitioning from
1
to
0
within an interval
\Delta
of time, and the normalization implies
P_{0|0}=1-P_{1|0}
.

Notice that due to the Markov hypothesis, the transition probabilities
P_{1|0}
and
P_{0|1}
are only functions of
\Delta
and not of
t
. Eq. (
8
) then yields

\displaystyle a(t+\Delta)=

\displaystyle P_{0|1}+a(t)\quantity[1-\qty(P_{0|1}+P_{1|0})].

(9)

In Appendix
C
we recurrently expand the above equation from
a_{0}
, resulting in the following ground state population for any
t=n\Delta

a(t)=a_{\infty}-\quantity(a_{\infty}-a_0)\quantity[1-\qty(P_{0|1}+P_{1|0})]^{t/\Delta},

(10)

where

\displaystyle a_{\infty}:=

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}a(t)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{0|1}}{P_{0|1}+P_{1|0}}

is the (computational basis) ground state population at thermal equilibrium.

In Appendix
D
, we explicitly determine the transition probabilities as functions of
\Delta
and
\theta
, which allows us to write

\displaystyle a(t,\Delta,\theta)=

\displaystyle a_{\infty}-\quantity(a_{\infty}-a_0)\Big[\sin^{2}(2\theta)e^{-\Delta/2\tau}

(12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\cos^{2}(2\theta)e^{-\Delta/\tau}\Big]^{t/\Delta}

and

a_{\infty}(\Delta,\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\frac{(2p-1)\cos(2\theta)}{\cos^2(2\theta)+\sin^2(2\theta)\frac{1-e^{-\Delta/2\tau}}{1-e^{-\Delta/\tau}}}].

(13)

It should be pointed out that this expression for the ground state population is only exact for instants right after the measurement is performed, that is, for
t=n\Delta
. With this result in hand, we are able to explore some relevant limits regarding
\Delta
.

III.1.1
Zeno limit

We first define dimensionless quantity
\delta:=\Delta/\tau
. The Zeno limit is then given by
\delta\to 0
, which means the interval between measurements is much smaller than the characteristic decay time of the thermalization,

\displaystyle a^{\mathrm{Zeno}}(t,\theta):=

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}a(t,\delta,\theta)

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{\infty}^{0}-e^{-t/\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}}(a_{\infty}^{0}-a_{0}),

where

\displaystyle a_{\infty}^{0}(\theta):=

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}a_{\infty}(\delta,\theta)

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(2p-1)\cos(2\theta)}{1+\cos^{2}(2\theta)}

is the ground state population at thermal equilibrium in the Zeno limit (or the zeroth order expansion of
a_{\infty}
in
\delta
), and

\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}(\theta):=\frac{4\tau}{3+\cos(4\theta)},

(16)

is the effective characteristic time of the thermalization.

As in this limit the system is never able to create coherences in the computational basis, the dynamics is fully described
a^{\mathrm{Zeno}}(t,\theta)
. Also, this expression is valid for any
t
. As we can see in Fig.
1
, the dynamics is a single exponential starting in
a_{0}
and asymptotically approaching
a_{\infty}
, with characteristic time
\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}
. Such behavior can be described as an effective thermalization, as the state is constrained by the measurements to remain diagonal in the computational basis and exponentially approaches an effective thermal state. As in general
a_{\infty}\neq p
, we might have an effective temperature which differs from the original one. Importantly, given that
\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}\geq\tau
, the effective thermalization is always slower than the original one, implying that the information about the initial state is kept for longer times in the Zeno effective dynamics.

between
\ket{e_0}
and
\ket{0}
is

\ket{e_0}

p=0.9
, implying the temperature of the environment is relatively low. The
\ket{0}
state population population exponentially approaches its equilibrium value, which is different from

. This happens for both
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
(
a_{0}=1
) and
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\ket{1}\bra{1}
(
a_{0}=0
), that is, independently of the classical bit being initially stored.

III.1.2
First order correction

In situations where the approximation
\delta\sim 0
is not a good one, a first order correction to the Zeno limit might be useful. We define
c^{1}
as the first order correction in
\delta
to
a(t,\delta,\theta)
,

a(t,\delta,\theta)=a^{\mathrm{Zeno}}(t,\theta)+\delta~c^{1}(t,\theta)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(17)

Performing the expansion of (
12
) up to first order in
\delta
yields the correction

c^{1}(t,\theta)=c_{\infty}^{1}-e^{-t/\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}}(c_{\infty}^{1}-c_{0}^{1}),

(18)

with

c_{\infty}^{1}(\theta)=-\frac{(2p-1)\cos(2\theta)\sin^{2}(2\theta)}{\quantity[3+\cos(4\theta)]^{2}}

(19)

and

c_{0}^{1}(t,\theta)=a_{0}\frac{\sin^{2}(4\theta)}{32}t.

(20)

Alternatively, we could explicitly write
a(t,\delta,\theta)
up to first order in
\delta
as

\displaystyle a^{1}(t,\delta,\theta)=a_{\infty}^{1}-e^{-t/\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}}(a_{\infty}^{1}-a_{0}^{1}),

(21)

where

\displaystyle a_{\infty}^{1}(\theta,\delta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2(2p-1)\cos(2\theta)}{3+\cos(4\theta)}

(22)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\delta\frac{(2p-1)\cos(2\theta)\sin^{2}(2\theta)}{\quantity[3+\cos(4\theta)]^{2}}

is the first order expansion of
a_{\infty}
in
\delta
, and

a_{0}^{1}(t,\delta,\theta):=a_{0}\quantity(1+\delta\frac{\sin^2(4\theta)}{32}t).

(23)

Interestingly, the first order correction to
a(t,\delta,\theta)
does not change the characteristic thermalization time
\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}
and provides a first order correction to the equilibrium value
a_{\infty}
. However, the dynamics will not be described by a simple exponential anymore, as there will be also a correction to the initial state which is not constant, but linear in time, as shown in Eq. (
23
). The comparison between the Zeno limit and the first order dynamics is shown in Fig.

III.1.3
Free limit

On the other side of the
\delta
limits we have the nearly-free dynamics, where very few measurements are performed within a period of time. Since our general expression for the ground state population is only exact for
t=n\Delta
, and we are treating the case
\Delta\sim t
, it is convenient to consider
\Delta=t
, which is the case in which we leave the qubit evolve freely during the whole time
t
, and only measure it in the end of the storage time. Eq. (
12
) then yields

\displaystyle a^{\mathrm{Free}}(t,\theta)=

\displaystyle a_{\infty}^{F}-\quantity(a_{\infty}^F-a_0)\Big[\sin^{2}(2\theta)e^{-t/2\tau}

(24)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\cos^{2}(2\theta)e^{-t/\tau}\Big],

where

a_{\infty}^{F}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\qty(2p-1)\cos(2\theta)].

(25)

Notice that in this case the dynamics is described by a combination of two exponentials, one decaying with
\tau
and the other with
2\tau
. The comparison between the free limit and the Zeno limit is shown in Fig.

III.2
Success probability

Now that we have determined how the ground state population of the pointer basis evolve in several situations, we proceed to the calculation of the probability of successfully storing the bit of classical information within a period of time. First, notice that the probability of a successful storage given that we started with
0
is simply the ground state population with
a_{0}=1
. On the other hand, the probability of a successful storage given that we started with
1
is the excited state population, which is given by
(1-a)
with
a_{0}=0
. Then, assuming the bits
0
and
1
are equally likely to be stored at this point of the computation, we obtain

P_{\mathrm{suc}}=~\frac{1}{2}\quantity[a_{a_0=1}+(1-a_{a_0=0})].

(26)

Combining this equation with the expressions for the ground state population we obtain for a general
\Delta

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{suc}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\{1+\Big[\sin^{2}(2\theta)e^{-\Delta/2\tau}+\cos^{2}(2\theta)e^{-\Delta/\tau}\Big]^{t/\Delta}\Bigg\},

(27)

while for the Zeno limit

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{suc}}^{\mathrm{Zeno}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+e^{-t/\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}}],

(28)

the first order expansion

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{suc}}^{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+e^{-t/\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}}\qty(1+\delta\frac{\sin^2(4\theta)}{32}t)],

(29)

and the free limit

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{suc}}^{\mathrm{Free}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1+\sin^2(2\theta)e^{-t/2\tau}+\cos^2(2\theta)e^{-t/\tau}].

(30)

Interestingly, as we assume both initial states to be equally likely, the expressions for the success probability do not depend on the pointer basis ground state population
p
, and therefore do not depend on the temperature of the environment. In Fig.
4
we see that
P_{\mathrm{suc}}^{\mathrm{Zeno}}
and
P_{\mathrm{suc}}^{\mathrm{Free}}
provide, respectively, lower and upper bounds to the general
P_{\mathrm{suc}}
.

IV
Conclusions

We have shown that the quantum Zeno effect might be useful for preserving classical information stored in quantum systems even beyond the Zeno limit. After explicitly determining the effective dynamics for any time interval between operations, we have established bounds on the protocol’s efficiency, as quantified by the probability of successfully storing classical information over a given period of time. This way, we have demonstrated the practical applicability and limitations of the quantum Zeno effect in our model. We expect these insights to contribute to the design and optimization of quantum information processing protocols, enabling better strategies for preserving information in the presence of noise. Further research in this direction may help advance the field of quantum information processing and pave the way for the realization of useful quantum computation in a near future.

Appendix A
Computational basis orthogonality constraint

Using a parametrization reminiscent of the Bloch sphere for each vector of the computational basis we obtain

\ket{0}=\cos\theta_{0}\ket{e_0}+e^{i\phi_{0}}\sin\theta_{0}\ket{e_1},

(31a)

\ket{1}=\cos\theta_{1}\ket{e_0}+e^{i\phi_{1}}\sin\theta_{1}\ket{e_1},

(31b)

for
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in[0,\pi/2]
and
\phi_{0},\phi_{1}\in[0,2\pi)
. Despite being parameterized by four angles, they cannot vary freely as the orthogonality between
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
must be satisfied. Writing explicitly

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\bra{0}\ket{1}

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{0}\cos\theta_{1}+e^{i(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})}\sin\theta_{0}\sin\theta_{1}.

As the left hand side is real, so must be the right hand side, then

e^{i(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})}=\pm 1,

(33)

but since both
\sin
and
\cos
of
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
are positive in the considered intervals, we must have

e^{i(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})}=-1,

(34)

as it yields

\sin\theta_{0}\sin\theta_{1}=\cos\theta_{0}\cos\theta_{1}.

(35)

Therefore, we have

e^{i\phi_{0}}=-e^{i\phi_{1}}:=e^{i\phi}.

(36)

Also,

\tan\theta_{0}\tan\theta_{1}=1,

(37)

which in the considered intervals imply

\sin\theta_{1}=\cos\theta_{0}:=\cos\theta,

(38)

yielding the expressions as presented in the main text.

Appendix B
Exponential decay

Given a general initial state for the qubit

\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b^{*}&1-a\end{bmatrix}

(39)

the effects of the Kraus operators are calculated from Eq. (
2
):

K_{0}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}K_{0}^{\dagger}=p\begin{bmatrix}a&b\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{1-\gamma}&1-a(1-\gamma)\end{bmatrix},

(40a)

K_{1}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}K_{1}^{\dagger}=p\gamma\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1-a\end{bmatrix},

(40b)

K_{2}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}K_{2}^{\dagger}=(1-p)\begin{bmatrix}a(1-\gamma)&b\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{1-\gamma}&1-a\end{bmatrix},

(40c)

K_{3}\rho_{\mathrm{in}}K_{3}^{\dagger}=(1-p)\gamma\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

(40d)

and from Eq. (
1
)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}p-(1-\gamma_{t})(p-a)&b\sqrt{1-\gamma_{t}}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{1-\gamma_{t}}&(1-p)+(1-\gamma_{t})(p-a)\end{bmatrix}.

Consider that we take the state
\rho_{t}
and apply
\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}
. Using the last result, we can set
a\to p-(1-\gamma_{t})(p-a)
and obtain for the
\ket{e_0}\bra{e_0}
term

\displaystyle\bra{e_0}\quantity[\mathcal{E}_{t^\prime}(\rho_t)]\ket{e_0}=

\displaystyle p-(1-\gamma_{t^{\prime}})(p-[p-(1-\gamma_{t})(p-a)])

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p-(1-\gamma_{t})(1-\gamma_{t^{\prime}})(p-a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p-f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}(p-a),

where we defined

f_{t}:=1-\gamma_{t},

(43)

which leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}(\rho_{t})=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}p-f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}(p-a)&b\sqrt{f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}}&(1-p)+f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}(p-a)\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

On the other hand, if we apply
\mathcal{E}_{t+t^{\prime}}
on
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t+t^{\prime}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}p-f_{t+t^{\prime}}(p-a)&b\sqrt{f_{t+t^{\prime}}}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{f_{t+t^{\prime}}}&(1-p)+f_{t+t^{\prime}}(p-a)\end{bmatrix}.

(45)

Notice that requiring Eq. (
5
) to hold implies

\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}\quantity[\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})]=\mathcal{E}_{t+t^{\prime}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})

(46)

for any
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
, meaning that for any
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
we should have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}p-f_{t+t^{\prime}}(p-a)&b\sqrt{f_{t+t^{\prime}}}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{f_{t+t^{\prime}}}&(1-p)+f_{t+t^{\prime}}(p-a)\end{bmatrix}=

(47)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}p-f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}(p-a)&b\sqrt{f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}}\\
b^{*}\sqrt{f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}}&(1-p)+f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}(p-a)\end{bmatrix},

which is equivalent to the condition

f_{t}f_{t^{\prime}}=f_{t+t^{\prime}}.

(48)

Now, we show that the only function that satisfies this condition is the exponential. Consider the derivative

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{t}=

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f_{t+h}-f_{t}}{h}

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f_{t}f_{h}-f_{t}}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{t}\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f_{h}-1}{h}.

The limit only exists if we further assume
\lim_{t\to 0}f_{t}=1
, or simply
f_{0}=1
for a continuous function, in which case the L’Hôpital rule applies

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{t}=

\displaystyle f_{t}\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\derivative{h}[f_{h}-1]}{\derivative{h}h}

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{t}\lim_{h\to 0}f^{\prime}_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{t}f^{\prime}_{0}.

This simple differential equation has solutions of the form

f_{t}=e^{\alpha t},~\alpha\in\mathbb{R},

(51)

while returning to
\gamma_{t}
yields

\gamma_{t}=1-e^{\alpha t}.

(52)

Given that by hypothesis we must have
\dot{\gamma}>0
, then
\alpha
is necessarily negative, which allows us to define a positive characteristic time for the dynamics

\tau:=-\frac{1}{\alpha},

(53)

finally obtaining

\gamma_{t}=1-e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}.

(54)
