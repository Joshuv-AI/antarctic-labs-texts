Vacuum magnetic fields with exact quasisymmetry near a flux surface. Part 1: Solutions near an axisymmetric surface
Wrick Sengupta \aff 1 \corresp, Elizabeth J. Paul \aff 2, Harold Weitzner \aff 1, Amitava Bhattacharjee \aff 2

Abstract
While several results have pointed to the existence of exactly quasisymmetric fields on a surface ( Garren & Boozer 1991a ; Garren & Boozer 1991b ; Plunk & Helander 2018 ) , we have obtained the first such solutions using a vacuum surface expansion formalism. We obtain a single nonlinear parabolic PDE for a function η \eta such the field strength satisfies B = B ⁡ ( η ) B=B(\eta) . Closed-form solutions are obtained in cylindrical, slab, and isodynamic geometries. Numerical solutions of the full nonlinear equations in general axisymmetric toroidal geometry are obtained, resulting in a class of quasi-helical local vacuum equilibria near an axisymmetric surface. The analytic models provide additional insight into general features of the nonlinear solutions, such as localization of the surface perturbations on the inboard side.



1
Introduction

Quasisymmetry is a continuous symmetry of the guiding center Lagrangian that, like axisymmetry, implies confinement of collisionless guiding center trajectories near a given flux surface on the drift time scale. Unlike axisymmetry, quasisymmetry does not require a continuous spatial symmetry. Thus three-dimensional quasisymmetric magnetic fields have the potential to possess vacuum rotational transform and improved confinement properties simultaneously. The discovery of quasisymmetry by Boozer
(
Boozer 1983
)
and the demonstration that quasi-symmetric equilibria could be obtained from asymptotic expansions near the magnetic axis
(
Garren & Boozer 1991a
;
Garren & Boozer 1991b
)
and numerical optimization of MHD equilibria
(
Nührenberg & Zille 1988
)
paved the way for the improved confinement of optimized stellarator configurations. More recently, the use of asymptotic expansions near the axis has proved to be fruitful in directly constructing quasisymmetric fields
(
Landreman & Sengupta 2018
;
Landreman et al. 2018
;
Landreman & Sengupta 2019
;
Landreman 2019
;
Jorge et al. 2019
)
and providing insight into their physical properties
(
Jorge & Landreman 2020
;
Landreman & Jorge 2020
)
.

There remain many open questions related to the existence and nature of quasisymmetric magnetic fields. There is recent evidence that nearly-quasisymmetric fields can be obtained in a volume under the assumption that a small deviation from magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) force balance is introduced
(
Constantin et al. 2020
)
. However, this result only applies sufficiently close to axisymmetry. By carrying out an asymptotic expansion in the distance near the magnetic axis, it was previously posited that quasisymmetric magnetic fields do not exist in a volume, as an overdetermined system arises at third order for a general axis
(
Garren & Boozer 1991a
;
Garren & Boozer 1991b
)
. If a deviation from MHD force balance is introduced in the form of an anisotropic pressure tensor, then the problem of overdetermination can be avoided, and the series can be continued to high order
(
Rodriguez & Bhattacharjee 2020b
;
Rodriguez & Bhattacharjee 2020a
)
. However, a solution with volumetric quasisymmetry remains to be obtained.

While the system of equations for near-axis quasisymmetric magnetic fields with scalar pressure becomes overdetermined, it has been argued that there remains enough freedom in the free functions to achieve exact quasisymmetry on one surface
(
Garren & Boozer 1991a
;
Garren & Boozer 1991b
;
Jorge et al. 2020
)
. A similar plausibility argument was made by demonstrating that an expansion in the distance from axisymmetry can be carried out to all orders to achieve exact vacuum quasi-axisymmetry on a single surface
(
Plunk & Helander 2018
)
. Nevertheless, exact quasisymmetry on a surface which is not asymptotically close to axisymmetry has not been demonstrated previously.

By expanding in the distance from a flux surface, we obtain vacuum magnetic fields with exact quasisymmetry on one surface. The existence of a surface sufficiently close to quasisymmetry has been shown to improve energetic particle confinement and increase the volume of surfaces with quasisymmetry
(
Henneberg et al. 2019
)
and may provide an effective transport barrier
(
Boozer 2019b
)
. Obtaining such configurations has previously been quite computationally demanding, requiring numerical optimization of an MHD equilibrium
(
Spong et al. 2001
;
Drevlak et al. 2018
)
to reduce the harmonics of the field strength in Boozer coordinates that break the desired symmetry
(
Sanchez et al. 2000
)
. Our approach uses a “direct construction,” like the near-axis or near-axisymmetric expansion techniques, in that numerical optimization of an equilibrium is not required. By directly obtaining solutions with exact quasisymmetry, we additionally gain insight into the nature of the class of solutions.

As we expand in the distance from a single flux surface, our approach uses a formalism similar to local equilibrium models
(
Hegna 2000
;
Boozer 2002
;
Skovoroda 2009
;
Candy & Belli 2015
)
. As noted previously, the local equilibrium equations have a free function of the two angles, which can be used to specify the straight field line angles on a surface
(
Hegna 2000
)
, the distance to a neighboring magnetic surface
(
Boozer 2002
)
, or the field strength on a surface
(
Skovoroda 2009
)
. In this work, we use this freedom to enforce exact quasisymmetry by defining a variable
\eta
(to be defined below), such that the field strength satisfies
B=B(\eta)
. In this way, Boozer coordinates are not required for the calculation, in contrast with recent work on stellarator vacuum fields near a surface
(
Boozer 2019a
)
.

Without quasisymmetry, there are three equations for three unknown functions that describe a vacuum field with surfaces, namely the field-line label, scalar potential, and the flux coordinate Jacobian. As quasisymmetry requires an additional constraint, the full global vacuum system with quasisymmetry is overdetermined. However, the near-surface expansion yields enough freedom to enforce quasisymmetry on one surface. As the out-of-surface derivative of the potential does not appear in the lowest order system, the corresponding constraint equation can be replaced by the quasisymmetry constraint in the surface expansion formalism. A limitation of this approach is that a local equilibrium solution is not guaranteed to exist within a global equilibrium. However, as we will discuss in Section
8
, the formalism can be extended to look for global quasisymmetric solutions. The near-surface theory presented in this work builds on the formalism developed by Sengupta and Weitzner
(
Weitzner 2016
;
Sengupta & Weitzner 2019
;
Jaquiery & Sengupta 2019
)
, which was used to study the existence of low-shear magnetic fields with surfaces and construct a global vacuum solution with closed field lines in slab geometry
(
Weitzner & Sengupta 2020
)
.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the equations for vacuum magnetic fields with surfaces, and in Section
3
we introduce the quasisymmetry constraint. In Section
4
, we expand the relevant equations in the distance from a flux surface, which is used to obtain a single parabolic PDE in Section
5
. Solutions of this equation in slab, cylindrical, and toroidal geometry are presented in Section
6
. In Section
7
, we demonstrate that numerical solutions of the nonlinear equations can be obtained near a general toroidal axisymmetric surface. These solutions are compared with the analytic models from Section
6
, and general features are discussed. Finally, in Section
8
we make some concluding remarks.

2
Vacuum fields with nested surfaces

We consider a generic toroidal coordinate system
(\psi,\theta,\phi)
where
\psi
is the toroidal flux and
\theta
and
\phi
are the poloidal and toroidal angles that are 2
\pi
periodic. Denoting the magnetic scalar potential and the field line label by
\Phi
and
\alpha
respectively, we represent the vacuum magnetic field with surfaces as

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha.

(2.1)

The three components of the above equation yield,

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{,\theta}\\
\Phi_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}g_{\theta\theta}\quad\quad g_{\theta\phi}\\
g_{\theta\phi}\quad\quad g_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha_{,\phi}\\
\alpha_{,\theta}\end{pmatrix}

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}\Phi_{,\psi}

\displaystyle=g_{\psi\phi}\alpha_{,\theta}-g_{\psi\theta}\alpha_{,\phi},

(2.2b)

where
\sqrt{g}=\left(\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\theta\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi\right)^{-1}
is the Jacobian. The first two equations are the “in-surface” derivatives of
\Phi
, whereas the third equation gives the “out-of-surface” derivative of
\Phi
. The magnetic differential operator is given by

\displaystyle\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\left(\alpha_{,\theta}\partial_{\phi}-\alpha_{,\phi}\partial_{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\{\alpha,\>\>\}_{(\theta,\phi)},

(2.3)

where,
\{f,g\}_{(\theta,\phi)}
denotes a Poisson bracket of
f
and
g
with respect to
(\theta,\phi)
. The field strength is computed from,

\displaystyle B^{2}=\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\left(\Phi_{,\phi}\alpha_{,\theta}-\Phi_{,\theta}\alpha_{,\phi}\right).

(2.4)

3
The quasisymmetry constraint on vacuum fields

In the literature various definitions of QS can be found. Here we discuss the following form of QS when the rotational transform is irrational, namely

\displaystyle\text{QS}:\quad\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}B-F(\psi)\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B=0

We note that
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
vanishes whenever
\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
does
(
Landreman & Catto 2012
)
, and their ratio remains finite.
This version of QS has been recently termed “weak quasisymmetry”
(
Rodriguez et al. 2020
;
Burby et al. 2020
;
Constantin et al. 2020
)
.

In
(\Phi,\psi,\alpha)
coordinates with
B^{2}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha
,

\displaystyle\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}=B^{2}\partial_{\alpha},\quad\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha\cdot\bm{\nabla}=B^{2}\partial_{\Phi}.

(3.1)

Since
B^{2}\neq 0
, QS implies

\displaystyle B=B(\Phi+F(\psi)\alpha,\psi).

(3.2)

We define,

\displaystyle\eta=\Phi+F(\psi)\alpha,

(3.3)

such that
B=B(\eta,\psi)
. Note that a similar function can be constructed even if the rotational transform is rational by replacing
\psi
by
\oint dl/B
where the integral is carried out along a closed field line
(
Elbarmi et al. 2020
)
.
The streamlines of
\eta
must close on a constant
\psi
surface. This implies that

\displaystyle\eta=M\theta-N\phi+\widetilde{\eta}(\theta,\phi)=\Phi+F(\psi)\alpha,

(3.4)

where
M
and
N
are integers which specify the helicity of the quasisymmetry. We note that
\Phi
and
\alpha
also generally have secular terms,

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\overline{I}\theta+\overline{G}\phi+\widetilde{\Phi}(\theta,\phi)

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\theta-\iota\phi+\widetilde{\alpha}(\theta,\phi),

(3.5b)

where tilde denotes periodic functions. This implies that

\overline{I}+F(\psi)=M
and
-\overline{G}+F(\psi)\iota=N
,
leading to
(
Helander 2014
)
,

\displaystyle F(\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{G}+N/M\overline{I}}{\iota(\psi)-N/M}.

(3.6)

While
\overline{I}
vanishes in a global vacuum field, the surface about which we expand may enclose currents which are not in the neighborhood of the surface. As we are performing a local expansion, this does not preclude the existence of currents asymptotically away from the surface. Thus we retain both secular terms in the scalar potential. In terms of the variable
\eta
one can write
B^{2}
as

\displaystyle B^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\{\alpha,\Phi\}_{\theta,\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\{\alpha,\eta\}_{\theta,\phi}.

(3.7)

The “in-surface” derivatives of
\Phi
,
\alpha
, and
\eta
can then be related to each other through

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{,\theta}\\
\Phi_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{g}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{,\theta}-F\alpha_{,\theta}\\
\eta_{,\phi}-F\alpha_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{\theta\theta}\quad\quad g_{\theta\phi}\\
g_{\theta\phi}\quad\quad g_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha_{,\phi}\\
\alpha_{,\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(3.8)

which yields,

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}F\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{,\theta}\\
\eta_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\quad g_{\theta\theta}\quad\quad\quad g_{\theta\phi}+F\sqrt{g}\\
\>g_{\theta\phi}-F\sqrt{g}\quad\quad\quad g_{\phi\phi}\quad\quad\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-F\alpha_{,\phi}\\
+F\alpha_{,\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.9)

This matrix equation can be inverted in order to obtain an equation for
\eta
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{g}F}{\mathcal{J}^{2}+F^{2}\sqrt{g}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\quad g_{\phi\phi}\quad\quad\quad-g_{\theta\phi}-F\sqrt{g}\\
\>-g_{\theta\phi}+F\sqrt{g}\quad\quad\quad g_{\theta\theta}\quad\quad\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{,\theta}\\
\eta_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-F\alpha_{,\phi}\\
+F\alpha_{,\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(3.10)

where we have defined the surface Jacobian to be
\mathcal{J}=\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}g_{\phi\phi}-g_{\theta\phi}^{2}}
.

4
Near-surface expansions

Let us now expand about a surface
\psi=\psi_{0}
such that,

\displaystyle\textbf{r}(\psi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\textbf{r}^{(0)}(\theta,\phi)+\delta\psi\>\textbf{r}^{(1)}(\theta,\phi)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\psi^{2})

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\alpha(\psi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{(0)}(\theta,\phi)+\delta\psi\>\alpha^{(1)}(\theta,\phi)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\psi^{2})

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\eta(\psi,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\eta^{(0)}(\theta,\phi)+\delta\psi\>\eta^{(1)}(\theta,\phi)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\psi^{2}),

(4.1c)

where
\delta\psi=\psi-\psi_{0}
. Note that we are using a standard Taylor expansion in linear power of
\psi
, as opposed to the expansion in
\sqrt{\psi}
employed in the near-axis expansions. As we are expanding about a non-degenerate surface away from the axis, the coordinate singularity is avoided and a regular Taylor series suffices.

At
\mathcal{O}((\delta\psi)^{0})
the vacuum equations (
2.2
) yield,

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}^{(1)}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi^{(0)}_{,\theta}\\
\Phi^{(0)}_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}g^{(0)}_{\theta\theta}\quad\quad g^{(0)}_{\theta\phi}\\
g^{(0)}_{\theta\phi}\quad\quad g^{(0)}_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha^{(0)}_{,\phi}\\
\alpha^{(0)}_{,\theta}\end{pmatrix}

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}^{(1)}\Phi^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{r}^{(1)}\cdot\left(\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,\phi}\alpha^{(0)}_{,\theta}-\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,\theta}\alpha^{(0)}_{,\phi}\right)

(4.2b)

where,

\displaystyle\sqrt{g}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{r}^{(1)}\cdot\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,\theta}\times\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,\phi}

(4.3a)

\displaystyle g^{(0)}_{x_{i}x_{j}}

\displaystyle=\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,x_{i}}\cdot\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,x_{j}},

(4.3b)

for
x_{i},x_{j}\in\{\theta,\phi\}
.

We can define the angles in the neighborhood of the surface such that the metric tensor simplifies
(
Boozer 2002
;
Imbert-Gerard et al. 2019
)
,

\displaystyle\textbf{r}^{(1)}\cdot\textbf{r}_{,\theta}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{r}^{(1)}\cdot\textbf{r}_{,\phi}^{(0)}=0.

(4.4a)

Defining
\rho^{(1)}=|\textbf{r}^{(1)}|
and
\mathcal{J}^{(0)}=\sqrt{g}^{(1)}/\rho^{(1)}
, we find that with such a choice of coordinates,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(0)}=|\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,\theta}\times\textbf{r}^{(0)}_{,\phi}|.

(4.5)

This eliminates the “out-of-surface” equation for
\Phi_{,\psi}
(
2.2b
) locally but not globally. So our vacuum equations simplify to,

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}\mathcal{J}^{(0)}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi^{(0)}_{,\theta}\\
\Phi^{(0)}_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}g^{(0)}_{\theta\theta}\quad\quad g^{(0)}_{\theta\phi}\\
g^{(0)}_{\theta\phi}\quad\quad g^{(0)}_{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha^{(0)}_{,\phi}\\
\alpha^{(0)}_{,\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.6)

As shown in Appendix
A
, a global toroidal coordinate system
(\psi,\theta,\phi)
can also be utilized to bring the “in-surface” vacuum equations to the same form. In order to carry out a fully global calculation, one must retain the equation for
\Phi_{,\psi}
(
2.2b
). In a local expansion, this equation only gives higher-order corrections to
\Phi
and will not be considered in this work. To enforce QS, we need to rewrite the vacuum equations in terms of the variable
\eta
. Using

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\eta^{(0)}-F^{(0)}\alpha^{(0)}

(4.7a)

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}\mathcal{J}^{(0)}B^{2}(\eta^{(0)})

\displaystyle=\{\alpha^{(0)},\Phi^{(0)}\}_{\theta,\phi}=\{\alpha^{(0)},\eta^{(0)}\}_{\theta,\phi},

(4.7b)

to eliminate
\Phi
, we obtain the following set of equations,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-F^{(0)}\alpha_{,\phi}^{(0)}\\
+F^{(0)}\alpha_{,\theta}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{(1)}F^{(0)}}{\mathcal{J}^{(0)}\left(1+\left(F^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}\right)^{2}\right)}\begin{pmatrix}\quad g_{\phi\phi}^{(0)}\quad\quad\quad-g_{\theta\phi}^{(0)}-F^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}\mathcal{J}^{(0)}\\
\>-g_{\theta\phi}^{(0)}+F^{(0)}\rho^{(1)}\mathcal{J}^{(0)}\quad\quad\quad g_{\theta\theta}^{(0)}\quad\quad\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{,\theta}^{(0)}\\
\eta_{,\phi}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}\mathcal{J}^{(0)}B^{2}(\eta^{(0)})

\displaystyle=\eta^{(0)}_{,\phi}\alpha^{(0)}_{,\theta}-\eta^{(0)}_{,\theta}\alpha^{(0)}_{,\phi}.

(4.8b)

For the remainder of the text, the superscripts will be dropped for brevity. If the surface is axisymmetric we have,

\displaystyle g_{\phi\theta}=0,\quad\mathcal{J}=\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}g_{\phi\phi}}.

(4.9)

Making a coordinate transformation to

\displaystyle\Theta=\int d\theta\,\frac{\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}}{\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}}},

(4.10)

we rewrite the vacuum equations and the QS condition as

\displaystyle\rho\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{,\Theta}\\
\Phi_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha_{,\phi}\\
\alpha_{,\Theta}\end{pmatrix}

(4.11a)

\displaystyle\rho g_{\phi\phi}B^{2}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha,\Phi\}_{\Theta,\phi}.

(4.11b)

The first two equations have the structure of the generalized Cauchy-Riemann condition and reduces to standard Cauchy-Riemann if
\rho=1
. For the QS problem,
\rho
must be self-consistently determined along with
\Phi
and
\alpha
. We note that
\Phi
and
\alpha
are therefore pseudo-analytic functions
(
Bers 1953
)
.

We similarly write the equations in terms of
\eta
and
\alpha
(
4.8
) with this change of variables as,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-F\alpha_{,\phi}\\
+F\alpha_{,\Theta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho F}{1+\left(F\rho\right)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\quad 1\quad-\rho F\\
\rho F\quad\quad 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{,\Theta}\\
\eta_{,\phi}\end{pmatrix}

(4.12a)

\displaystyle\rho B^{2}(\eta)G(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\eta_{,\phi}\alpha_{,\Theta}-\eta_{,\Theta}\alpha_{,\phi},

(4.12b)

where we have defined
G(\Theta)=g_{\phi\phi}
.

5
A parabolic PDE to enforce exact QS on a surface

We now simplify (
4.12
) to get a single PDE for
\eta
. Solving for
\rho F
in terms of
\eta
and
B(\eta)
we obtain,

\displaystyle 1+(\rho F)^{2}=\frac{\eta_{,\Theta}^{2}+\eta_{,\phi}^{2}}{G(\Theta)B(\eta)^{2}}.

(5.1)

Eliminating
\alpha
by equating mixed partial derivatives, we now get a single nonlinear partial differential equation for
\eta
,

\displaystyle(\partial^{2}_{\Theta}+\partial^{2}_{\phi})\eta+\frac{2}{\tan{2\Omega}}(\eta_{,\Theta}\partial_{\Theta}+\eta_{,\phi}\partial_{\phi})\Omega+2(\eta_{,\Theta}\partial_{\phi}-\eta_{,\phi}\partial_{\Theta})\Omega=0,

(5.2)

where,

\displaystyle\tan\Omega=\rho F=\frac{\sqrt{\eta_{,\Theta}^{2}+\eta_{,\phi}^{2}-G(\Theta)B(\eta)^{2}}}{G(\Theta)B(\eta)^{2}}.

(5.3)

The above equation when further simplified takes the form

(\rho F\eta_{,\Theta}+\eta_{,\phi})^{2}\partial^{2}_{\phi}\eta+2(\rho F\eta_{,\Theta}+\eta_{,\phi})(\eta_{,\Theta}-\rho F\eta_{,\phi})\partial_{\Theta}\partial_{\phi}\eta+(\eta_{,\Theta}-\rho F\eta_{,\phi})^{2}\partial^{2}_{\Theta}\eta=\\
(1+(\rho F)^{2})B^{3}\left(B^{\prime}(\eta)(1-(\rho F)^{2})+\frac{G^{\prime}(\Theta)}{2G(\Theta)}\left(\eta_{,\Theta}(1-(\rho F)^{2})-2\rho F\eta_{,\phi}\right)\right).

(5.4)

The right hand side of the
\eta
equation shows that the “source” terms are the gradients of the magnetic field strength
B(\eta)
, and the geometry factor
G(\Theta)
. Given these two source terms,
B(\eta)
and
G(\Theta)
, we can in principle solve the nonlinear
\eta
equation with the requirement that

\displaystyle\eta=a_{1}\Theta-a_{2}\phi+\widetilde{\eta}(\Theta,\phi),

(5.5)

where
\widetilde{\eta}
is doubly periodic.
We note that the partial differential equation that
\eta
satisfies is of parabolic type in general geometry (see Appendix
B
) in contrast to the generalized Grad-Shafranov equation
(
Burby et al. 2020
)
which is elliptic. The repeated characteristic of the parabolic equation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\Theta}{d\phi}=\frac{\eta_{,\Theta}-\rho F\eta_{,\phi}}{\rho F\eta_{,\Theta}+\eta_{,\phi}}=-\frac{F\alpha_{,\phi}}{F\alpha_{,\Theta}}.

(5.6)

The last expression follows from using the vacuum equations.
Therefore, constant
\alpha
lines are the repeated characteristics for the
\eta
equation.

We shall now make a few changes of variables that considerably simplify the nonlinear parabolic equation. Firstly, to bring the parabolic equation to the standard form we shall transform to
(\Theta,F\alpha)
coordinates. To further simplify the equations, in particular the source terms, we change variables from (
\eta,\Theta
) to (
\mathcal{U},\vartheta
), where,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\int\frac{d\eta}{B(\eta)},\quad\vartheta=\int\>d\Theta\>G(\Theta).

(5.7)

The QS equation in these variable reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta\vartheta}+\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta}\left(\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta}^{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{G^{\prime}(\vartheta)}{2G(\vartheta)}+\frac{B^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})}{B(\mathcal{U})}\frac{\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta}}{1-B\>\mathcal{U}_{,F\alpha}}\right)=0.

(5.8)

The quantity
\rho
is given by

\displaystyle\rho F=\pm\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta}^{2}-1}}{1-B\>\mathcal{U}_{,F\alpha}}.

(5.9)

The positive and negative signs of
\rho
(
|\textbf{r}_{,\psi}|
) represents outward and inward surface expansions corresponding to the assumption that the flux increases or decreases away from the axis. Eqns. (
5.8
)-(
5.9
) constitute the principal result of this work. The solution of (
5.8
) yields
\eta(\theta,\phi)
and thus the quasisymmetric field strength through
B(\eta)
. The necessary deformation of the axisymmetric geometry to support quasisymmetric fields is then provided by (
5.9
).

We now make a few observations from the structure of (
5.8
) and (
5.9
). Firstly, for
\rho
to be real, we must have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta}|>1.

(5.10)

The case
\mathcal{U}_{,\vartheta}=0
is not allowed since
\rho=0
implies that the flux surfaces overlap. Physically,
\rho=0
would correspond to the formation of islands. Secondly, if the gradients of
G
and
B
are zero,
\eta=B\mathcal{U}
and the general solution of
\mathcal{U}
is linear in the angle
\vartheta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=u_{1}(F\alpha)\>\vartheta+u_{2}(F\alpha).

(5.11)

Since
\eta
must satisfy the boundary condition (
5.5
),
u_{1}(F\alpha)
must be a constant and
u_{2}(F\alpha)
must be a periodic function of
F\alpha
. Furthermore,
u_{1}\neq 1
to avoid
\rho=1
. We note that the case
B^{\prime}=0
corresponds to local isodynamic fields
(
Palumbo 1968
)
.

In the next Section, we shall look for exact and approximate analytical solutions of the QS equation in slab, cylindrical, and toroidal geometries. We will show that there are critical differences in the nature of the QS solutions for slab and cylinder geometry for which
G
is a constant and a real axisymmetric torus for which
G=G(\vartheta)
.

6
Solutions of the QS equation in different geometries

6.1
Slab and cylindrical geometries

In slab and cylindrical geometry the geometry factor
G
is unity (Appendix
A
). Hence, the only source term in the QS equation is the gradient of
B
. Exact travelling wave solutions to the
\eta
equation (
5.4
) can be constructed such that
\eta=\eta(\Theta-c\phi)
for constant
c
. The resulting ordinary differential equation (ODE) in the variable
(\Theta-c\phi)
is,

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}-\frac{B^{\prime}(\eta)}{B(\eta)}\eta^{\prime 2}\left(2-(1+c^{2})\left(\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{B}\right)^{2}\right)=0,

(6.1)

which has an exact solution given by

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\eta}{kB(\eta)^{2}}\sqrt{1+(1+c^{2})(kB(\eta))^{2}}=\pm(\Theta-c\phi),

(6.2)

where
k
is the integration constant. Since
B(\eta)
is a periodic function of
\eta
, the integral on the left defines a function which has periodic terms in
\eta
and at most a linear secular term in
\eta
arising from the average of the integrand. This can be readily seen by Fourier expanding the integrand in
\eta
. Therefore, when the function of
\eta
is inverted, we get a solution which satisfies the boundary condition (
5.5
).

Similarly, there exists exact solutions of the form
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(\vartheta-cF\alpha)
to the QS equation (
5.8
) which reduces to the following ODE in the variable
(\vartheta-cF\alpha)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\left({\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}^{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{B^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})}{B(\mathcal{U})}\frac{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}{1+cB\>\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}\right)=0.

(6.3)

The ODE has the exact solution

\displaystyle-\int d\mathcal{U}\frac{\pm\sqrt{k(1-(cB(\mathcal{U}))^{2})+1}+(cB(\mathcal{U}))^{2}}{cB(\mathcal{U})\left(\pm\sqrt{k\left(1-(cB(\mathcal{U}))^{2}\right)+1}+1\right)}=\vartheta-cF\alpha,

(6.4)

with
k
as an integration constant. We observe that there are two possible travelling wave solutions in each of the above cases due to quadratic nature of the equations. This can be interpreted as solutions for which the toroidal flux increases in the direction toward or away from the axis. The existence of two linearly independent solutions is also obtained in the system of equations that arises in an asymptotic expansion near axisymmetry
(
Plunk & Helander 2018
;
Plunk 2020
)
.

6.2
Toroidal geometry with arbitrary aspect ratio and arbitrary shaping

If
G^{\prime}(\vartheta)\neq 0
, exact travelling wave solutions do not exist since
G(\vartheta)
breaks the travelling wave symmetry. We shall treat the QS equations in this case numerically in Section
7
. However, analytical progress can still be made in several interesting cases. In the following we shall give the analytical details of these cases to provide physical insight into the nature of the QS solutions and also to serve as benchmarks for the numerical solution.

6.2.1
Complete axisymmetry

The complete axisymmetric case for arbitrary aspect ratio and shaping can be tackled analytically. In this limit we have
G=G(\vartheta)
,
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(\vartheta)
,
B=B(\mathcal{U})
, and
\rho=\rho(\vartheta)
. The QS equation reduces to,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime 2}-1)\left(\frac{G^{\prime}}{2G}+\frac{B^{\prime}}{B}\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\right)=0.

(6.5)

The above equation can be integrated once and we obtain

\displaystyle GB^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{U}^{\prime 2}}\right)=c_{0},

(6.6)

where
c_{0}
is the integration constant. We can rewrite the above equation in the form of an energy equation with a potential
V(\mathcal{U},\vartheta)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{U}^{\prime 2}+V(\mathcal{U},\vartheta)=0,\quad V(\mathcal{U},\vartheta)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{GB^{2}}{c_{0}-GB^{2}}.

(6.7)

From (
5.9
) it follows that

\displaystyle\rho F=\pm\frac{\sqrt{c_{0}}}{\sqrt{GB^{2}-c_{0}}}.

(6.8)

6.2.2
Isodynamic fields (
B=B_{0},B^{\prime}=0
)

The QS equation reduces to the following ODE in the variable
\vartheta
when
B=B_{0}
is constant on the flux surface,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{U}^{\prime}({\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}^{2}-1)\frac{G^{\prime}}{2G}=0,

(6.9)

which has an exact solution

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\pm\int d\vartheta\>\sqrt{\frac{G(\vartheta)B^{2}_{0}}{G(\vartheta)B^{2}_{0}-c_{0}}}+\Gamma F\alpha+\overline{\mathcal{U}}(F\alpha).

(6.10)

The function
\overline{\mathcal{U}}(F\alpha)
is an arbitrary periodic function of
F\alpha
and
\Gamma
is a constant. The corresponding value of
\rho F
is

\displaystyle\rho F=\pm\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}}{GB^{2}_{0}-c_{0}}}\left(1-B_{0}(\Gamma+\overline{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}(F\alpha))\right)^{-1}

(6.11)

Palumbo’s original isodynamic solution
(
Palumbo 1968
)
was axisymmetric, but locally we can always find non-axisymmetric isodynamic fields provided
\Gamma\neq 0
and
\overline{\mathcal{U}}(F\alpha)
is not a constant.

6.2.3
Localization near
B^{\prime}=0

We shall now look at the behavior of a nontrivial non-axisymmetric solution in the neighborhood of
B^{\prime}=0
which we assume happens along
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{m}
. We shall restrict ourselves to the region
|\delta\mathcal{U}|=|\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}_{m}|\ll 1
. Even though
\delta U\ll 1
, we do not expect
\delta U^{\prime}
to be small. Therefore, we can have sharp variations in the solution near
B^{\prime}=0
.

In the region near
B^{\prime}=0
, we expect the solution to be close to the isodynamic case given by (
6.10
). We therefore make the ansatz that
\mathcal{U}
is of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{m}+\Gamma F\alpha+h(\vartheta),

(6.12)

where
\Gamma
is a constant.
Taylor expanding
B(\mathcal{U})
in powers of
\delta\mathcal{U}
and keeping only up to the quadratic power in
\delta\mathcal{U}
such that
B=B_{0}+B_{2}(\delta\mathcal{U})^{2}+\dots
, and keeping all nonlinearities in
\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, we get

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime\prime}+\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime}((\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime})^{2}-1)\left(\frac{G^{\prime}}{2G}+2\frac{B_{2}\delta\mathcal{U}}{B_{0}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}{1-B_{0}\Gamma}\right)=0,

(6.13)

where prime indicates differentiation with respect to
\vartheta
. The above equation can be integrated once such that

\displaystyle GB_{0}^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime 2}}\right)\exp{\left(\frac{2B_{2}}{B_{0}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{U}^{2}}{1-B_{0}\Gamma}\right)}=c_{0},

(6.14)

We can cast the above expression in a energy equation as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathcal{U}^{\prime 2}+V(\delta\mathcal{U},\vartheta)=0,\quad V(\delta\mathcal{U},\vartheta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{B_{0}^{2}G}e^{-\left(\frac{2B_{2}}{B_{0}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{U}^{2}}{1-B_{0}\Gamma}\right)}-1\right)^{-1}.

(6.15)

We shall show later that the solution has sharp peaks near the minimum of the magnetic field strength, and we compare with the numerically obtained solution in Section
7
.

6.2.4
Near-isodynamic limit

We shall now briefly discuss a bifurcation that can happen when the background geometry
G(\vartheta)
and magnetic field strength
B_{0}(\vartheta)
are axisymmetric. If we now look for a small deformation such that magnetic field is QS i.e.
B=B(\eta)
, we can have the following situations:

1.

Both the field strength and
\rho
are axisymmetric

2.

Both the field strength and
\rho
are non-symmetric.

Case (
1
) implies that
\eta=\eta(\vartheta)
and
\rho=\rho(\vartheta)
. We have analyzed this case completely in
6.2.1
. Case (
2
) implies that
\eta
must have dependence on both
\vartheta
and
F\alpha
, so that
\rho F
, given by (
5.9
), is non-symmetric. We now look for solutions of the QS equation (
5.8
) in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{0}(\vartheta,F\alpha)+\epsilon\mathcal{U}_{1}(\vartheta,F\alpha)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

(6.16a)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=B(\mathcal{U})=B_{0}+\epsilon B_{1}(\mathcal{U})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

(6.16b)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}+\epsilon\rho_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(6.16c)

where
B_{0}
is a constant background field. The expansion parameter
\epsilon
is similar to the large aspect ratio parameter, however we do not expand the geometry factor. We further require that to lowest order,
\mathcal{U}
depends on
F\alpha
only through a secular term
\Gamma F\alpha
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{0}(\vartheta,F\alpha)=\Gamma F\alpha+h_{0}(\vartheta).

(6.17)

For QA we must have
\Gamma=0
.

The lowest order calculations are analogous to the case described in Section
6.2.3
, since
B=B_{0}
is constant to this order. Denoting
\vartheta
derivatives by primes, (
5.8
) to lowest order implies

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}_{0}+h^{\prime}_{0}({h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}-1)\frac{G^{\prime}}{2G}=0,

which leads to the energy equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{h^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}+V(h_{0},\vartheta)=0,\quad V(h_{0},\vartheta)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{GB_{0}^{2}}{c_{0}-GB_{0}^{2}},

where, the integration constant
c_{0}
is a periodic function of
F\alpha
. It is convenient to separate
c_{0}
into a constant piece
C_{0}
and a periodic part
\widetilde{c}_{0}
.
The quantity
\rho_{0}F
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{0}F=\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}}{GB_{0}^{2}-c_{0}}}\frac{1}{1-B_{0}\Gamma}.

(6.18)

To
O(\epsilon)
, we obtain the following linear equation for
\mathcal{U}_{1}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{\frac{(GB^{2}_{0}-c_{0})^{3}}{GB^{2}_{0}}}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{c_{0}}{1-B_{0}\Gamma}\frac{B^{\prime}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{0})}{B_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{GB_{0}^{2}}{GB^{2}_{0}-c_{0}}}.

(6.19)

We note that since
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{1}
is periodic and
(B_{0},\Gamma,c_{0})
are all independent of
\vartheta
, the right hand side of (
6.19
) must satisfy the condition,

\displaystyle\oint d\vartheta\frac{B^{\prime}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{0})}{B_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{GB_{0}^{2}}{GB^{2}_{0}-c_{0}}}=0.

(6.20)

The above constraint is satisfied if both
B_{1}
and
G
are stellarator symmetric, for example.
Solving for
\mathcal{U}_{1}
we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{1}=c_{1}(F\alpha)\sqrt{\frac{GB^{2}_{0}}{(GB^{2}_{0}-c_{0})^{3}}}+\frac{c_{0}}{1-B_{0}\Gamma}\int d\vartheta\frac{B^{\prime}_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{0})}{B_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{GB_{0}^{2}}{GB^{2}_{0}-c_{0}}},

(6.21)

where,
c_{1}
is a function of
F\alpha
to be determined by going to the next order.

The second order equation is once again of the form (
6.19
) and the periodicity requirement on
\mathcal{U}_{2}
leads to a Riccati equation for
c_{1}
. The calculation is straightforward and we shall skip the details. The Riccati equation for
c_{1}
is of the form

\displaystyle\frac{dc_{1}}{d(F\alpha)}+2a_{1}c_{1}+a_{2}c_{1}^{2}+a_{3}=0,

(6.22)

where
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
a_{3}
are complicated functions of
c_{0}(F\alpha)
. The function
c_{1}
has to be a periodic function of
F\alpha
. Therefore, we must have

\displaystyle\oint dF\alpha\left(2a_{1}c_{1}+a_{2}c_{1}^{2}+a_{3}\right)=0.

(6.23)

Assuming that
c_{0}=C_{0}+\widetilde{c}_{0}
, we can determine
C_{0}
using (
6.23
) for a given periodic function
\widetilde{c}_{0}
. We note that if
\widetilde{c}_{0}=0
, i.e.
c_{0}
is a constant, the Riccati equation (
6.22
) has constant coefficients and can be solved exactly as

\displaystyle c_{1}=\frac{a_{1}-\Delta_{0}\tan\left(\Delta_{0}(F\alpha-F\alpha_{0})\right)}{a_{2}},\quad\Delta_{0}=\sqrt{a_{2}a_{3}-a_{1}^{2}}.

(6.24)

Thus, we see that unless
\Delta_{0}=0
,
c_{1}
is not periodic when
c_{0}
is a constant. When
\Delta_{0}=0
, we obtain an axisymmetric solution.

6.2.5
A model for QS fields near an axisymmetric surface

The previous analysis suggest a strong correlation between
\rho F
and the product of the geometry factor,
G
, and the magnetic field strength,
B^{2}
. We construct a simple model of QS that captures most of the interesting properties for a large class of travelling-wave like solutions for arbitrary geometry and field strength factors,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\approx\pm\int d\vartheta\>\sqrt{\frac{GB^{2}}{GB^{2}-c_{0}}}+\Gamma F\alpha

(6.25a)

\displaystyle\rho F

\displaystyle\approx\pm\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}}{GB^{2}-c_{0}}}\left(1-B\Gamma\right)^{-1}.

(6.25b)

This model is motivated by the solutions obtained in the near-isodynamic limit (
6.17
)-(
6.18
).
In Section
7
, we verify this model numerically.

7
Numerical solutions of the QS equations

Although full toroidal geometry can be treated analytically in a few limiting cases, we obtain numerical solutions for the general case. To do so, we construct a spectral solution for
\alpha
,
\eta
, and
\rho
,

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\theta-\iota\phi+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\alpha_{m_{i},n_{i}}\sin(m_{i}\theta-n_{i}\phi)

(7.1a)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\theta-N/M\phi+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\eta_{m_{i},n_{i}}\sin(m_{i}\theta-n_{i}\phi)

(7.1b)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}+1}\rho_{m_{i},n_{i}}\cos(m_{i}\theta-n_{i}\phi),

(7.1c)

where we have assumed stellarator symmetry and chosen a normalization such that the secular term in
\eta
is unity. We note that
N/M
must be a rational number, which determines the helicity of the quasisymmetry. A nonlinear set of equations is obtained by inserting (
7.1
) into (
4.8
) and integrating against a set of
N_{\text{modes}}+1
basis functions,

\displaystyle\{x_{m_{j},n_{j}}\}=\{\cos(m_{j}\theta-n_{j}\phi)\}.

(7.2)

The set of
3N_{\text{modes}}+3
unknowns is taken to be
\{\iota,\alpha_{m,n},F,\eta_{m,n},\rho_{m,n}\}
while the helicity
N/M
is prescribed in addition to the functional form of
B(\eta)
. The resulting nonlinear set of equations is solved with a trust region method with a prescribed analytic Jacobian matrix. In the following Sections, we verify the numerical solutions in several limits.

Once the solution is obtained, the Boozer angles can be computed as,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{B}

\displaystyle=\theta+\frac{-N/M\widetilde{\alpha}+\iota\widetilde{\eta}}{\iota-N/M}

(7.3a)

\displaystyle\varphi_{B}

\displaystyle=\phi+\frac{\widetilde{\eta}-\widetilde{\alpha}}{\iota-N/M},

(7.3b)

such that only secular terms remain in the potentials,

\displaystyle\Phi(\vartheta_{B},\varphi_{B})

\displaystyle=\overline{G}\varphi_{B}+\overline{I}\vartheta_{B}

(7.4a)

\displaystyle\alpha(\vartheta_{B},\varphi_{B})

\displaystyle=\vartheta_{B}-\iota\varphi_{B}.

(7.4b)

Complete axisymmetry

In the case of pure axisymmetry, we can confirm that solutions of (
4.8
) agree with numerical solutions of the Grad-Shafranov equation on a surface. To do so, we compute an axisymmetric equilibrium with the VMEC code
(
Hirshman & Whitson 1983
)
. We consider a boundary given by,

\displaystyle R(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=3+\cos(\theta)

(7.5a)

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta),

(7.5b)

and impose toroidal current and pressure profiles which are peaked near the axis,

\displaystyle I_{T}^{\prime}(\psi/\psi_{0})

\displaystyle=I_{0}e^{-(\psi/\psi_{0})^{2}/0.05^{2}}

(7.6a)

\displaystyle p(\psi/\psi_{0})

\displaystyle=p_{0}e^{-(\psi/\psi_{0})^{2}/0.05^{2}},

(7.6b)

to provide a non-zero rotational transform. The magnetic field on the surface at
\psi/\psi_{0}=0.995
is used to construct

from (

8
Discussion

In this work, we have introduced the formalism for obtaining vacuum magnetic fields with exact quasisymmetry on a flux surface. Quasisymmetry is enforced by introducing an unknown,
\eta
, such that
B(\eta)
. In solving the relevant equations at the lowest order, the free functions are the shape of the flux surface, the functional form of
B(\eta)
, and the desired helicity of the quasisymmetry. This is somewhat analogous to the free functions that arise in the axis expansion equations, for which the axis shape is specified in addition to certain harmonics of the field strength. While the shape of the axis determines the helicity of the quasisymmetry, the helicity can be specified in the surface-expansion problem.

As an initial step, we have made several simplifying assumptions in this work. We have focused on the vacuum equations, but the formalism can be easily extended to model force-free or MHD equilibrium fields following
Weitzner 2016
. We have also focused on solutions near an axisymmetric surface. While the surface itself is axisymmetric, non-axisymmetry is introduced in the nearby surfaces and is determined self-consistently as part of the solution. While the introduction of non-axisymmetry in the background geometry complicates the metric tensor (Appendix
A.2
), the formalism is not limited to axisymmetry.

Interestingly, we have shown that the resulting equation for
\eta
is generally parabolic (Appendix
B
). This in contrast to the generalized Grad-Shafranov equation for the flux function in quasisymmetry
(
Burby et al. 2020
)
, which is elliptic in nature. We leave further analysis of the properties of this nonlinear parabolic PDE to future work. Whether the PDE for
\eta

The solutions presented in this work have been obtained under the traveling wave ansatz such that the field strength is helical. For this reason, we were able to converge to quasi-helical solutions in general toroidal geometry by initializing with a traveling wave solution. In future work, we aim to explore quasi-axisymmetric and other possible classes of solutions within this formalism.

Although here we have taken a local equilibrium approach, which does not guarantee existence within a global equilibrium, we can extend the formalism to look for global traveling wave solutions in cylindrical and slab geometries. This is a special class of solutions which, if it exists, should satisfy the overdetermined system of equations and will be reported in subsequent work.
