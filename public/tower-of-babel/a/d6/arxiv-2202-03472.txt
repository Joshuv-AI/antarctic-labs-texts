New Bounds on the Size of Binary Codes with Large Minimum Distance
James (Chin-Jen) Pang, Hessam Mahdavifar, S. Sandeep Pradhan

Abstract
Let A ⁡ ( n , d ) A(n,d) denote the maximum size of a binary code of length n n and minimum Hamming distance d d . Studying A ⁡ ( n , d ) A(n,d) , including efforts to determine it as well to derive bounds on A ⁡ ( n , d ) A(n,d) for large n n ’s, is one of the most fundamental subjects in coding theory. In this paper, we explore new lower and upper bounds on A ⁡ ( n , d ) A(n,d) in the large-minimum distance regime, in particular, when d = n / 2 − Ω ⁡ ( n ) d=n/2-\Omega(\sqrt{n}) . We first provide a new construction of cyclic codes, by carefully selecting specific roots in the binary extension field for the check polynomial, with length n = 2 m − 1 n=2^{m}-1 , distance d ⩾ n / 2 − 2 c − 1 ​ n d\geqslant n/2-2^{c-1}\sqrt{n} , and size n c + 1 / 2 n^{c+1/2} , for any m ⩾ 4 m\geqslant 4 and any integer c c with 0 ⩽ c ⩽ m / 2 − 1 0\leqslant c\leqslant m/2-1 . These code parameters are slightly worse than those of the Delsarte–Goethals (DG) codes that provide the previously known best lower bound in the large-minimum distance regime. However, using a similar and extended code construction technique we show a sequence of cyclic codes that improve upon DG codes and provide the best lower bound in a narrower range of the minimum distance d d , in particular, when d = n / 2 − Ω ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ) d=n/2-\Omega(n^{2/3}) . Furthermore, by leveraging a Fourier-analytic view of Delsarte’s linear program, upper bounds on A ⁡ ( n , ⌈ n / 2 − ρ ​ n ⌉ ) A(n,\left\lceil n/2-\rho\sqrt{n}\,\right\rceil) with ρ ∈ ( 0.5 , 9.5 ) \rho\in(0.5,9.5) are obtained that scale polynomially in n n . To the best of authors’ knowledge, the upper bound due to Barg and Nogin [ 1 ] is the only previously known upper bound that scale polynomially in n n in this regime. We numerically demonstrate that our upper bound improves upon the Barg-Nogin upper bound in the specified high-minimum distance regime.

Introduction

An error-correcting code
C
of length
n
and minimum distance
d
over a finite field
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!q}
is a subset of the vector space
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!q}^{n}
with

, over all distinct

. Here,

is the Hamming distance between

and

. The code
C
is said to be linear if
C
is a subspace of the vector space
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!q}^{n}
.
The capabilities and limitations of error-correcting codes are, in general, closely related to their minimum distance. For instance, the maximum number of errors a code can correct in the Hamming space is upper bounded by half of its minimum distance. This has led to a vast range of studies spanning several decades to answer one of the most fundamental and classical problems in coding theory, which is to determine (or to derive bounds on) the maximum size
A_{q}(n,d)
of an error-correcting code
C
of length
n
over
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!q}
and with minimum distance
d

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Several of the most well-known results in the literature focus on the regime where
n{\ \rightarrow\ }\infty
and

is proportional to
n
, namely

, for some
0<\delta<1
.
The question then is to find the asymptotic maximal rate

of an error-correcting code with relative distance
\delta
, where we define
R(\delta)\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log_{q}A_{q}(n,\left\lfloor n\delta\right\rfloor)
.

Lower bounds on

is size of a Hamming ball of radius

in
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!q}^{n}
.
Several improvements have been proposed to strengthen the GV bound, see, e.g.,
[

,

{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
.
For prime powers

13

For

The best asymptotic upper bounds currently known are due to McEliece, Rodemich, Ramsey and Welch (MRRW)

In addition to the original proof in

I-A

Motivation

Low-rate codes are becoming increasingly important with the emergence of low-capacity scenarios, including the Internet of Things (IoT) and satellite communications. For instance, in IoT network, the devices need to operate under extreme power constraints and often need to communicate at very low signal-to-noise ratio
[
28
]
.
In the standard, legacy Turbo codes or convolutional codes at moderate rates together with many repetitions are adopted to support communication at low rates. It is expected, however, that repeating a moderate-rate code to enable low-rate communication will result in rate loss and suboptimal performance.
As a result, studying low-rate error-correcting codes for reliable communications in such low-capacity regimes has become a subject of extensive recent works
[

Motivated by the need to revisit various aspects of channel coding in the low-rate regime, from efficient code design to reliable decoding algorithms, in this paper we focus on the minimum distance properties of binary codes in the low-rate regime, which can be also described as the
large
minimum distance regime, to be specified later. Let
C
be a binary code of length
n
, size
M
, and minimum distance

, referred to as an
(n,M,d)
code (for the sake of simplifying the equations, we reserve the parameter

For ease of notation, we use

For

I-C

Our Contribution

We study the cardinality
A(n,d)
of binary codes in the large minimum distance regime where
j=n-2d
scales as
j=\Omega(\sqrt{n})\cap o(n)
. In particular, we show the following results:

•

Two code constructions with sizes scaling polynomially and quasi-polynomially in
n
are presented for cases with
d=n/2-\Omega(\sqrt{n})
and
d=n/2-\Omega(n^{2/3})
, respectively, by demonstrating explicit and carefully designed BCH-like cyclic linear codes.
Specifically, for
c\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the first construction has size
n^{c+\frac{1}{2}}
and
d\geqslant n/2-2^{c-1}\sqrt{n}
, and the second construction has size
n^{\frac{\log n}{6}+\frac{3c^{2}}{2}+\frac{5c}{2}+\frac{5}{6}}
and
d\geqslant n/2-2^{c-1}n^{2/3}-2^{2c-1}n^{1/3}
.

•

Compared with the state-of-the-art lower bounds on
A(n,d)
based on the Delsarte–Goethals codes, the first cyclic construction is inferior by a multiplicative factor of
\Theta(n^{3/2})
in the regime
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.
In the regime
j=\Theta({n}^{2/3})
, the second construction is superior to the DG codes by a multiplicative factor of
\Theta(n^{\frac{3}{2}c^{2}+\frac{3}{2}c-1})
and provides the best lower bound in this regime.

•

Asymptotic upper bounds for
A(n,n/2-\rho\sqrt{n})
, based on an improved bounding technique inspired by
[
21
]
and a new method to bound the maximal eigenvalues of adjacency matrix induced by a Hamming ball
B_{r}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with finite
r
, are shown.

•

The asymptotic scaling behaviour of the proposed Fourier-analytical based upper bounds for
A(n,n/2-\rho\sqrt{n})
and the spectral-based bounds derived from
[
1
]
, both of which are polynomial in
n
, are plotted for
\rho\in(0.5,9.5)
, where the former are slightly stronger.

II-A

Lower Bounds

We review some asymptotic lower bounds on
A(n,d)
in this section. The first one is the well-known GV lower bound. Note that there is an improvement to the GV bound by Jiang and Vardy
[
10
]
that is not considered here because the constraint on the relative distance

in
[

Theorem 1

(GV lower bound,
[
5
,
6
]
)
.

Let positive integers
n
and
d\leqslant n/2
be given. Then

A(n,d)\geqslant\frac{2^{n}}{\Vol(d-1,n)}.

(2)

Asymptotically, suppose
0\leqslant\delta<1/2
, then there exists an infinite sequence of
(n,M,d)
binary linear codes with
d/n>\delta
and rate
R=k/n
satisfying

R\geqslant 1-H_{2}(d/n).

To evaluate Theorem
1
when
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, consider
j=2a\sqrt{n}
.
The central limit theorem, coupled with the Berry–Esseen theorem, provides an upper bound

\Vol(d-1,n)=2^{n}\left[Q(2a)+O(1/\sqrt{n})\right],

where
Q(\cdot)
denotes the tail distribution function of the standard normal distribution.
Hence we have

A(n,n/2-a\sqrt{n})\geqslant\left[Q(2a)+O(1/\sqrt{n})\right]^{-1},

(3)

which is loose compared with the Plokin-Levenshtein bound
A(2d,d)=4d
.

The Delsarte–Goethals (DG) codes are a class of nonlinear codes that are associated with the Reed-Muller codes and are the best known codes for their parameters.

Theorem 2

(Delsarte–Goethals code

[
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
)
.

Let
m\geqslant 4
be an even integer and
0\leqslant r\leqslant{m}/{2}-1
be an integer. The Delsarte–Goethals code DG
(m,r)
is a binary code of block length
n=2^{m}
, size

, where
k=r(m-1)+2m
, and minimum distance
2^{m-1}-2^{m/2+r-1}
.
For

, DG
(m,r)=

(m,2)
, the second order Reed-Muller code. For
0\leqslant r\leqslant{m}/{2}-2
, DG
(m,r)
is a nonlinear subcode of RM
(m,2)
.

For the case when
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, one may consider DG
(m,r)
codes with a finite

, and show that
A(n,d)\geqslant 2^{-r}n^{r+2}
for

.
For the case when
j=\Theta(n^{2/3})
, considering DG
(m,r)
codes with

Asymptotic Upper Bounds

The following upper bounds on the size of binary codes can be found in standard coding theory textbooks, e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
. Bounds for the regime
j=n-2d=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
are derived and given following the general bounds, e.g. inequalities (
5
), (
6
), (
7
), and (
8
).
When the scaling behaviour of
j
matters, we choose
j=2a\sqrt{n}
, i.e.,

, for ease of comparison between bounds.

Theorem 3

(Hamming Bound)
.

For every
(n,M,d)
code
C\subset\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
,

M\leqslant 2^{n}/\Vol(e,n),

(4)

where
e=\left\lfloor(d-1)/2\right\rfloor
.

In the asymptotics, Theorem
3
bounds the rate from above, in terms of the relative distance
\delta
, by

R\lesssim 1-H_{2}(\delta/2).

For
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, the term
e=n/4-\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, and

\Vol(e,n)\geqslant 2^{H_{2}(1/4)n-o(n)}
.
Hence Theorem
3
becomes

M\leqslant 2^{(1-H_{2}(1/4))n+o(n)}\lesssim 2^{0.189n},

(5)

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Theorem 4

(Singleton Bound)
.

Let
C\subset\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
be a binary code with distance
d
and dimension
k
, then
k\leqslant n-d+1.

For
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, Theorem
4
yields
M\leqslant 2^{n/2+O(\sqrt{n})},

which is weak compared to (
5
).

Theorem 5

(Plotkin Bound,
[
48
]
)
.

The following holds for any code
C\subset\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with distance
d
.

1.

If
d=n/2
,
\left|C\right|\leqslant 2n
.

2.

If
d>n/2
,
\left|C\right|<2\left\lceil\frac{d}{2d-n}\right\rceil
.

One may use a combinatorial argument and Theorem
5
to derive the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

If a
(n,M,d)
binary code
C
has distance
d<n/2
,
then the size
M\leqslant d\cdot 2^{n-2d+2}
.

Using Corollary
6
, one may bound the size of any code with
d=(n-j)/2<n/2
by

M\leqslant d\cdot 2^{j+2}<2n\cdot 2^{j}.

(6)

When
j
scales as
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, the size
M
is bounded sub-exponentially in
n
. In particular, set
j=2a\sqrt{n}
, i.e.
d=\left\lceil n/2-a\sqrt{n}\,\right\rceil
, (
6
) becomes

M\leqslant 2n\cdot 2^{2a\sqrt{n}}.

(7)

Theorem 7

(Elias-Bassalygo Bound)
.

For sufficiently large
n
, every code
C\subset\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with relative distance
\delta\leqslant 1/2
and rate
R
satisfies the following:

R\lesssim 1-H_{2}(J_{2}(\delta)),

where
J_{2}(\delta)\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-2\delta})
.

Assuming
d=\left\lceil n/2-a\sqrt{n}\,\right\rceil
, one may adopt steps similar to the proof of Theorem
7
as in
[
3
, p.147]
to show an upper bound:

M\leqslant n^{3}\cdot 2^{\frac{a}{\ln{2}}\sqrt{n}+O(1)}.

(8)

The last upper bound we introduce is known as the
first linear programming bound
or the
MRRW bound
on binary error correcting codes, or, alternatively, on optimal packing of Hamming balls in a Hamming cube.
The bound was originally proved by McEliece, Rodemich, Rumsey, and Welch
[
18
]
, following Delsarte’s linear programming approach
[
19
]
, and is the best known asymptotic upper bound on the cardinality of a code with a given minimal distance scaling linearly in

, for a significant range of the relative distance.

Theorem 8

(MRRW Bound,
[
18
]
)
.

For sufficiently large
n
, every code
C\subset\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with relative distance
\delta
and rate

R\lesssim H_{2}\left(1/2-\sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}\right).

(9)

Remark 1
.

Another bound, known as the second linear programming bound, is also given in
[
18
]
in the form

R\lesssim\min_{0\leqslant u\leqslant 1-2\delta}1+g(u^{2})-g(u^{2}+2\delta u+2\delta),

(10)

where the function
g(x)\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}H_{2}((1-\sqrt{1-x})/2)
.
For
0.273\leqslant\delta\leqslant 0.5
, the bound (
10
) simplifies to that of (
9
). For
\delta<0.273
, the inequality (
10
) is strictly tighter than (
9
).

Plugging in
\delta=d/n
into (
9
), we have the following bound:

M\leqslant 2^{nH_{2}\left(1/2-\sqrt{d/n(1-d/n)}\right)+o(n)}.

(11)

Note that, due to the

term, the bound (
11
) is not tighter than (
6
) when
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. This appears to the contrary of the fact the MRRW bound is tighter than all the other bounds for relative distance
0.273<\delta<0.5
. However, a tailored treatment of the proof technique may lead to a nontrivial bound as in the derivation of (
8
) from Theorem
7
. In Section

, one such bound is given through an alternative proof of the Theorem

Remark 2
.

Another line of work pioneered by Levenshtein
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
has also attempted to derive bounds on both
A_{2}(n,d)
and
A_{q}(n,d)
for general
q
from Delsarte’s LP.
In the binary case, for many values of finite
n,d
, Levenshtein’s bound is better than the MRRW bound. In the regime
d=\delta n
and
n{\ \rightarrow\ }\infty
, the two bounds converge.
For general
q
, a recent work
[
52
]
has proposed refinements of the Levenshtein bound in
q
-ary Hamming spaces and also derived
q
-ary analogs of the MacEliece bound (Equation (
1
)).

II-C

Spectral-Based Upper Bound

One approach to proving the first linear programming bound is the spectral-based technique in
[
1
]
, which relies on the analysis of eigenvectors of some finite-dimensional operators related to the Krawtchouk polynomials.
While the main goal of the work
[
1
]
is to establish the MRRW bounds from a spectral perspective, some of the analytical results in it can be used to derive upper bounds on
A(n,d)
in the large minimum distance regime.
In particular, while all the other upper bounds on
A(n,d)
scale superpolynomially in
n
when
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, a sequence of bounds scaling polynomially in
n
can be derived from
[
1
]
.
A key result in
[
1
]
is the following bound on the size of a binary code with minimum distance
d
.

Theorem 9

(
[
1
]
Theorem 2, binary case)
.

Let
C
be an
(n,M,d)
binary code. Then

M\leqslant\frac{4(n-k)}{n-\lambda_{k}}\binom{n}{k}

(12)

for all
k
such that
\lambda_{k-1}\geqslant n-2d
, where
\lambda_{k}
is the maximal eigenvalue of the
(k+1)\times(k+1)
self-adjoint matrix
S=(s_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{k+1}
defined by
s_{i,i+1}=s_{i+1,i}=\sqrt{i(n+1-i)}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
and
s_{i,j}=0
otherwise.

Upper and lower bounds on
\lambda_{k}
are also provided.

Lemma 10

(
[
1
]
Lemma 2, binary case)
.

Let
k<n/2
. For all
s=2,\ldots,k+1
,

2\sqrt{k(n-k+1)}\geqslant\lambda_{k}\geqslant\frac{2(s-1)}{s}\sqrt{(k-s+2)(n-k+s-1)}.

To establish bounds on
A(n,d)
for the regime
d=n/2-\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, consider a finite
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
s\in\left\{2,\ldots,k+1\right\}
. Letting
n{\ \rightarrow\ }\infty
, we have

\lambda_{k}\geqslant\frac{2(s-1)}{s}\sqrt{(k-s+2)(n-k+s-1)}=\frac{2(s-1)}{s}\sqrt{k-s+2}(1+o(1))\sqrt{n}.

Thus
\lambda_{k}\gtrsim\underline{\lambda}_{k}\sqrt{n},
where
\underline{\lambda}_{k}
is given by

\underline{\lambda}_{k}=\max_{2\leqslant s\leqslant k+1}\left\{\frac{2(s-1)}{s}\sqrt{k-s+2}\right\}.

(13)

Since
\lambda_{k}
scales as
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
for all finite
k
, the bound on
A(n,d)
is asymptotically equivalent to

A(n,d)=O(n^{k})\mbox{ as long as }d\geqslant\frac{n}{2}-\frac{\underline{\lambda}_{k-1}}{2}\sqrt{n}.

(14)

By solving (
13
) for
k=1,2,3
, we obtain
\underline{\lambda}_{1}=1,\,\underline{\lambda}_{2}=\sqrt{2},\,\underline{\lambda}_{3}=\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{2}
, which, via (
14
), lead to

A(n,d_{1})=O(n^{2}),\,A(n,d_{2})=O(n^{3}),\,A(n,d_{3})=O(n^{4})
as long as
d_{1}\geqslant\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n},\,d_{2}\geqslant\frac{n}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\sqrt{n},\,d_{3}\geqslant\frac{n}{2}-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\sqrt{n},
respectively.
Many more bounds for the large minimum distance regime can be obtained by choosing other
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
These bounds and the new upper bounds shown in Section
IV-C
are both polynomial in
n
and are tighter than all other known bounds, as discussed in Section
II-B
.
In Section
IV-D
, the asymptotic behavior of the two types of bounds when
d=n/2-\rho\sqrt{n}
are plotted for
\rho\in(0.5,9.5)
.

III
Main Results - Lower Bounds

Two polynomial-based cyclic code constructions are given in this section. The first construction, described in Section
III-A
, leads to a family of codes where the term
j
ranges from
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
to
\Theta(n)
. The second construction, described in Section
III-B
, applies to a smaller range of
j
, between
\Theta({n}^{2/3})
and
\Theta(n)
, but are tighter than the first construction over this range.
Note that the results in this section are not asymptotic and hold for finite values of
n
, i.e., the first construction only requires
n\geqslant 15
and the second one requires
n\geqslant 63
.

III-A

Cyclic Code with High Minimum Distance

We construct a binary cyclic code
C
with high minimum distance as follows.

Theorem 11
.

Let
n=2^{m}-1
, and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
be an even integer with
m\geqslant 4
. Let
c
be an integer with
0\leqslant c\leqslant m/2-1
.
There exists a binary cyclic code
C
of length
n
, dimension
(c+1/2)m
, and minimum distance

d\geqslant 2^{m-1}-2^{m/2+c-1}\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{c-1}\sqrt{n}.

(15)

Proof:
Consider the finite field
F={\mathbb{F}}_{2^{m}}
and the subfield
K={{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}<F
. Let
\alpha
be a primitive root of unity in
F
, and set
\alpha_{i}=\alpha^{1+2^{m/2+i}}
for
i=0,1,2,\ldots,c
. Consider the binary cyclic code with the generator polynomial

g(x)=\frac{x^{n}-1}{\prod_{i=0}^{c}M_{\alpha_{i}}(x)},

where
M_{\beta}(\cdot)
is the minimal polynomial of
\beta
over
K
.
Note that the
\alpha_{i}
’s belong to different conjugacy classes, i.e,

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}\left\{\alpha_{i}^{2^{j}}\mid j=0,1,2,\ldots,m-1\right\}=\left\{\alpha^{2^{j}+2^{m/2+i+j}}\mid j=0,1,2,\ldots,m-1\right\}

are disjoint subsets of
F\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, and
\left|A_{0}\right|=m/2
,
\left|A_{i}\right|=m
for
i\neq 0
.
This is ensured by the particular choice of
\alpha_{i}
’s.
More specifically, let
P_{i}=\left\{2^{j}+2^{m/2+i+j}\mbox{ mod }2^{m}-1\mid j=0,1,\ldots,m-1\right\}
be the set of the exponents of
\alpha
for elements in
A_{i}
. Each
P_{i}
is a cyclotomic coset mod
2
in
F
and the length-
m
binary representation for each
p,p^{\prime}
in
P_{i}
are cyclic shifts of each other. Let
p_{i}=1+2^{m/2+i}
be the coset representative of
P_{i}
.
The claim on the size of
\left|A_{i}\right|
holds by noting that
\left|A_{i}\right|=\left|P_{i}\right|=m
for
i\neq 0
, and
\left|A_{0}\right|=\left|P_{0}\right|=m/2
.
To claim that
A_{i}
’s are disjoint, it suffices to show that the cyclotomic cosets
P_{i}
’s are disjoint. First note that for two cyclotomic cosets
P_{i}
and
P_{k}
, they are either disjoint or identical. Assume for some
i\neq k
, cosets
P_{i}
and
P_{k}
are identical. Then
p_{i}=1+2^{m/2+i}
is an element in
P_{k}
, that is, there is a
p^{\prime}=2^{\ell}+2^{m/2+k+\ell}\in P_{k}
for which
p_{i}=p^{\prime}
modulo
2^{m}-1
. As both
p_{i}
and
p^{\prime}
are sums of two powers of
2
, we note that neither
m\mid\ell
and
m\mid(\ell+k-i)
, nor
m\mid(\ell-m/2-i)
and
m\mid(m/2+k+\ell)
, can happen. Hence
p_{i}\notin P_{k}
, and thus
P_{i}
and
P_{k}
are disjoint.
Thus the degree of the polynomial
g(x)
is
n-(c+1/2)m
. Hence, the dimension of the code is at least
(c+1/2)m
.

For the minimum distance, let
t=2^{m-1}+2^{m/2+c-1}+1
. We show next that for
j=t,t+1,\ldots,2^{m}-1
,
\alpha^{j}
is a root for the generator polynomial
g(x)
. In other words,
P_{i}\cap\{t,t+1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}=\emptyset
, for
i=0,1,2,\dots,c
. This is by noting that the elements in
P_{i}
, after taking modulo
2^{m}-1
, can be written as a sum of two powers of two, i.e.,
2^{\ell}+2^{j}
, where the difference between
\ell
and
j
is at least
m/2-c
, and that such a number does not belong to
\{t,t+1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}
. Hence, the minimum distance of the code
d
is at least
2^{m}-t+1=2^{m-1}-2^{m/2+c-1}
by BCH bound
[
53
,
54
]
(see also
[
2
,
55
]
).

Note that the parameters of the codes constructed in Theorem
11
and the Delsarte–Goethals codes are both sitting between those of the first order and the second order Reed–Muller (RM) codes of length
n=2^{m}
.
More specifically, RM
(m,1)
has minimum distance equal to
n/2
and dimension equal to
m+1
, while RM
(m,2)
has minimum distance
n/4
, and dimension
1+m+\binom{m}{2}
.
A comparison between the DG codes and our new construction in the regime
j=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
can be made as follows.
Let
c\geqslant 0
be a finite integer. The DG
(m,c)
code has length
n=2^{m}
, minimum distance
d={n}/{2}-2^{c-1}\sqrt{n}
and size
2^{-c}n^{c+2}
. On the other hand, the code parameters given in Theorem
11
are length
n=2^{m}-1
, minimum distance
d\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{c-1}\sqrt{n}
, and size
(n+1)^{c+1/2}
.
Therefore the former leads to a stronger lower bound on the size, by a multiplicative factor of
2^{-c}n^{3/2}
, in the asymptotics.

III-B

Cyclic Constructions for
j=\Omega(n^{2/3})

We adopt an approach similar to that in the proof of Theorem
11
to construct a sequence of cyclic binary codes with
j=n-2d
scaling as
j=\Omega(n^{2/3})
in this section. This construction is preferred over that in Section
III-A
for all
j=\Omega(n^{2/3})
, and yields a tighter bound on
A(n,d)
than the DG codes does.

Theorem 12
.

Let
n=2^{m}-1
, and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
be a multiple of
6
. Let
c
be an integer with
1\leqslant c\leqslant m/3-1
.
There exists a binary cyclic code
C
of length
n
, dimension

k=m\left(\frac{m}{6}+\frac{3c^{2}}{2}+\frac{5c}{2}+\frac{5}{6}\right),

and minimum distance

d\geqslant 2^{m-1}-2^{\frac{2m}{3}+c-1}-2^{\frac{m}{3}+2c-1}\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{c-1}n^{\frac{2}{3}}-2^{2c-1}n^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(16)

Proof:
Consider the finite field
F={\mathbb{F}}_{2^{m}}
and the subfield
K={{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}<F
. Let
\alpha
be a primitive root of unity in
F
.
Let
\ell=m/3-c
, and define three sets consisting of triples of integers,

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{(m/3,m/3,m/3)\right\},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(d_{1},d_{1},d_{2})\mid d_{1}\geqslant\ell,\,d_{2}\geqslant\ell,\;d_{1}\neq d_{2},\;2d_{1}+d_{2}=m\right\},

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\{(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})\mid d_{1}>d_{2}>d_{3}\geqslant\ell\mbox{ or }d_{1}>d_{3}>d_{2}\geqslant\ell,\;d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}=m\}.

A combinatorial argument shows that the sizes of the three sets are

\displaystyle\left|S_{1}\right|=1,\hskip 8.53581pt\left|S_{2}\right|=\left\lfloor\frac{m-3l}{2}\right\rfloor,\hskip 8.53581pt\left|S_{3}\right|=\frac{1}{3}\left[\binom{m-3l+2}{2}-3\left\lfloor\frac{m-3l}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right].

For
(d,e,f)\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}S
, define
\alpha_{d,e,f}=\alpha^{p_{d,e,f}}\in F
where
p_{d,e,f}=2^{m-1}+2^{e+f-1}+2^{f-1}
.
We define a binary cyclic code with the generator polynomial

g(x)=\frac{x^{n}-1}{\prod_{(d,e,f)\in S}M_{\alpha_{d,e,f}}(x)\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{m/6+c}M_{\alpha_{i}}(x)}.

For
i=0,1,2,\dots,c
, define the sets
P_{i}=\left\{2^{j}+2^{m/2+i+j}\mbox{ mod }2^{m}-1\mid j=0,1,2,\ldots,m-1\right\}
and
A_{i}=\left\{\alpha^{p}\mid p\in P_{i}\right\}
(as in the proof of Theorem
11
).
Consider sets
A_{d,e,f},P_{d,e,f}
as follows:

\displaystyle A_{d,e,f}

\displaystyle\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}\left\{\alpha_{d,e,f}^{2^{j}}\mid j=0,1,2,\ldots\right\}

\displaystyle P_{d,e,f}

\displaystyle=\{2^{m-1+j}+2^{e+f-1+j}+2^{f-1+j}\mbox{ mod }2^{m}-1\mid j=0,1,2,\ldots\}.

The set
P_{d,e,f}
consists of the exponents of
\alpha
for elements in
A_{d,e,f}
.
Note that
\left|P_{d,e,f}\right|=m/3
for
(d,e,f)\in S_{1}
,
\left|P_{d,e,f}\right|=m
for
(d,e,f)\in S_{2}
, and
\left|P_{d,e,f}\right|=m
for
(d,e,f)\in S_{3}
.
Two cyclotomic cosets
P_{d,e,f}
and
P_{d^{\prime},e^{\prime},f^{\prime}}
are disjoint as long as
(d,e,f)\neq(d^{\prime},e^{\prime},f^{\prime})
. Since each element of
P_{i}
is a sum of two powers of
2
and each element of
P_{d,e,f}
a sum of three powers of
2
, we also have
P_{i}\cap P_{d,e,f}=\emptyset
, for all
i\in\left\{0,1,2,\dots,c\right\}
and
(d,e,f)\in S
.
Thus the degree of the polynomial
g(x)
is

\displaystyle\deg g(x)

\displaystyle=n-\left(m\left|S_{3}\right|+m\left|S_{2}\right|+m/3\left|S_{1}\right|\right)-m\left(m/6+c+1/2\right)

\displaystyle=n-m\left({m}/{6}+{3c^{2}}/{2}+{5c}/{2}+{5}/{6}\right).

Hence, the dimension of the code is at least
m\left({m}/{6}+{3c^{2}}/{2}+{5c}/{2}+{5}/{6}\right)
.

For the minimum distance, we proceed similarly to the steps taken in the proof of Theorem
11
. Let
t=2^{m-1}+2^{m-1-\ell}+2^{m-1-2\ell}+1
. We show next that for
j=t,t+1,\ldots,2^{m}-1
,
\alpha^{j}
is a root for the generator polynomial
g(x)
. In other words,
P_{i}\cap\{t,t+1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}=\emptyset
, for
i=0,1,2,\dots,m/6+c
, and
P_{d,e,f}\cap\{t,t+1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}=\emptyset
for
(d,e,f)\in S
.
This is by noting that elements in
P_{i}
and
P_{d,e,f}
are sums of two or three powers of
2
, and the powers differ by at least
\ell
. Such a number can not be found in
\{t,t+1,\dots,2^{m}-1\}
. Hence, by the BCH bound
[
53
,
54
]
(see also
[
2
,
55
]
), the minimum distance
d
of the code is at least
2^{m}-t+1=2^{m-1}-2^{m-1-\ell}-2^{m-1-2\ell}=2^{m-1}-2^{2m/3+c-1}-2^{m/3+2c-1}
.

For the regime
j=\Theta(n^{2/3})
we compare the performance of the DG codes and the construction in Theorem
12
.
Let
c
be a positive integer. The DG
(m,r)
codes with
r=m/6+c
has length
n=2^{m}
, minimum distance
d={n}/{2}-2^{c-1}n^{2/3}
, and size
M=n^{2}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\frac{\log n}{6}+c}
.
The cyclic code described in the proof of Theorem
12
has length
n=2^{m}-1
, minimum distance
d^{\prime}\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{c-1}n^{\frac{2}{3}}-2^{2c-1}n^{\frac{1}{3}}
, and size
M^{\prime}\geqslant n^{\frac{\log n}{6}+\frac{3c^{2}}{2}+\frac{5c}{2}+\frac{5}{6}}
.
While the terms
j=n-2d=2^{c}n^{\frac{2}{3}}
and
j^{\prime}=n-2d^{\prime}\leqslant 2^{c}n^{\frac{2}{3}}-2^{2c}n^{\frac{1}{3}}
are asymptotically equivalent, i.e.,
j\gtrsim j^{\prime}
, the bound of the size based on the new cyclic construction is stronger. More explicitly, we have
M^{\prime}\geqslant 2^{c}n^{\frac{3c^{2}}{2}+\frac{3c}{2}-1}\cdot M
, where the degree
\frac{3c^{2}}{2}+\frac{3c}{2}-1
is positive for all
c\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Remark 3
.

Codes with minimum distance scaling as
{n}/{2}-\Theta(n^{\frac{2}{3}})
can be constructed in the manner described in Theorem
11
too, by choosing
c\approx m/6
. Specifically, if one chooses
c=m/6+r
in Theorem
11
, the code would have dimension
k=(m/6+r+1/2)m
and minimum distance
d\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{r-1}n^{\frac{2}{3}}.
For the same
r
, if one chooses
c=r-1
in Theorem
12
, the code has dimension
k^{\prime}=\left(\frac{m}{6}+\frac{3(r-1)^{2}}{2}+\frac{5(r-1)}{2}+\frac{5}{6}\right)m
and minimum distance
d^{\prime}\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{r-2}n^{\frac{2}{3}}-2^{2r-3}n^{\frac{1}{3}}.

For all suffciently large
n
and
r\geqslant 2
, the latter construction provides a better trade-off since
k^{\prime}>k
and
{n}/{2}-2^{r-2}n^{\frac{2}{3}}-2^{2r-3}n^{\frac{1}{3}}>{n}/{2}-2^{r-1}n^{\frac{2}{3}}
.

The advantage of the second construction is even more evident when one considers the following cases. Taking
c=m/6+s\sqrt{m}
for
s>0
in Theorem
11
, we have a code
C
with dimension
k=(m/6+s\sqrt{m}+1/2)m
and minimum distance
d\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{s\sqrt{m}-1}n^{\frac{2}{3}}.
Taking
c=s\sqrt{m}
in Theorem
12
, we have a code
C^{\prime}
with dimension
k^{\prime}=\left(\frac{m}{6}+\frac{3s^{2}m}{2}+\frac{5s\sqrt{m}}{2}+\frac{5}{6}\right)m\geqslant\left(\frac{1+9s^{2}}{6}m+\frac{5}{6}\right)m
, and minimum distance
d^{\prime}\geqslant{n}/{2}-2^{s\sqrt{m}-1}n^{\frac{2}{3}}-2^{2s\sqrt{m}-1}n^{\frac{1}{3}}.

For large
n
, the bounds for the minimum distances
d
and
d^{\prime}
are almost the same, and the dimension
k^{\prime}
of the code
C^{\prime}
is multiple times larger than
k
, as
k^{\prime}\approx(1+9s^{2})k
.

Remark 4
.

The constructions used in the proofs of Theorem
11
and Theorem
12
draw on the fact that nonzero elements in
F
can be generated by a primitive root of unity
\alpha
.
Any nonzero element
\beta\in F
can thus be expressed as
\alpha^{p}
for some
p\in\left\{0,1,\dots,2^{m}-1\right\}
.
Using the binary expansion,
p=b_{m-1}2^{m-1}+b_{m-2}2^{m-2}+\dots+b_{1}2^{1}+b_{0}
, roots for
M_{\beta}(x)
are of the form
\alpha^{p\cdot 2^{j}}=\alpha^{p^{\prime}}
where
p^{\prime}=b_{m-1}2^{m-1+j}+b_{m-2+j}2^{m-2+j}+\dots+b_{1}2^{1+j}+b_{0}2^{j}\equiv b_{m-1-j}2^{m-1}+b_{m-2-j}2^{m-2}+\dots+b_{1}2^{1+j}+b_{0}2^{j}+b_{m-1}2^{j-1}+\dots+b_{m-j}
after taking modulo
2^{m}-1
.
The length-
m
binary expressions
p=(b_{m-1}b_{m-2}\dots b_{1}b_{0})_{2},p^{\prime}=(b_{m-1-j}b_{m-2-j}\dots b_{m-j})_{2}
are cyclic shifts of each other.
The key idea behind the proof techniques of Theorem
11
and Theorem
12
is to leverage the BCH bound with a focus on finding length-
m
binary sequences
(b_{m-1},b_{m-2},\dots,b_{1},b_{0})
and two integers
t_{1},t_{2}
with
t_{1}<t_{2}\leqslant 2^{m}-1
, such that cyclic shifts of the binary sequences do not correspond to values in the range
\left\{t_{1},t_{1}+1\ldots,t_{2}\right\}
.

Remark 5
.

According to the discussion in Remark
4
, the BCH-like construction technique for large minimum distance codes described in Sections
III-A
and
III-B
can be tailored towards deriving lower bounds in narrower ranges of
d
.
In particular, the construction in Section
III-A
admits all
m
-bit binary sequences with exactly two
1
’s spacing at least
\ell=m/2-c
bits apart (distance is evaluated in a wrap-around manner), and that in Section
III-B
all
m
-bit binary sequences with two or three
1
’s spacing at least
\ell=m/3-c
bits apart.
By extending such arguments to consider all
m
-bit binary sequences with Hamming weight between
2
and
w\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that the
1
’s are at least
\ell=m/w-c
bits apart, one can construct codes with minimum distance scaling as
d=n/2-O(n^{\frac{w-1}{w}})
.

IV
Main Results - Upper Bound

IV-A

Harmonic Analysis Approach

We adopt a covering argument similar to Navon and Samorodnitsky
[
21
]
and show upper bounds on the size of any code
C
with length
n
and minimum distance
d
scaling as
d\geqslant n/2-\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.
The viewpoint presented in
[
21
]
, providing an alternative proof to the MRRW bound, is different from the original proof found in
[
18
]
which relies on analytical properties of the Krawchouk polynomials, and instead employs Fourier analysis on the group
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
as their main tool.

In particular, the authors of
[
21
]
exploit the expediency of working with the maximal eigenfunctions of Hamming balls. One key finding was that, given any real-valued function
f
on
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with a small support
B\subset\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, such that the adjacency matrix of the Hamming cube acts on
f
by multiplying it pointwise by a large factor, the cardinality of error-correcting codes with minimum distance
d
can be upper bounded by
n\left|B\right|
.
The applicability will depend on the value of the multiplying factor. By proposing functions
f
supported on Hamming balls
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
of different radii
r
, one may derive a lower bound of the multiplying factor, formally called the
maximal eigenvalue of adjacency matrix of the subgraph incduced by
B
. This makes possible a simple proof of the first linear programming bound.

Let us now state the definition of the
maximal eigenvalue
of a graph. Let
G=(V,E)
be a (finite, undirected, simple) graph. Let
A_{G}=(A_{ij})
denote the
\left|V\right|\times\left|V\right|
adjacency matrix of
G
, defined by
A_{ij}=1
if
(i,j)\in E
and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise for vertices
i,j\in V
. Note that
A_{G}
is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real, and can be ordered as
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{n}
.
For any function
f
on
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
, the function
Af
sums at each point of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
the values of
f
at its neighbours. That is, the value taken by the function
Af
at a vertex
x\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
, denoted by
(Af)(x)
or
Af(x)
, is given by
Af(x)=\sum_{y\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}:w_{H}(x,y)=1}f(y)
.
When
B
is a subset of the cube
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
, set

\lambda_{B}\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}\max\left\{\frac{{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}}{{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}}\,\hbox{$\left|\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\,f:{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\mbox{ supp}(f)\subseteq B\right\},

(17)

where
{\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle}\mbox{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}$}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}}f(x)g(x)
for real-valued functions
f,g
on
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
.
That is,
\lambda_{B}
is the maximal eigenvalue of adjacency matrix of the subgraph of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
induced by
B
.

Two lemmas were shown in
[
21
]
to show (
9
).

Lemma 13

(
[
21
]
Prop 1.1)
.

Let
C
be a code with block length
n
and minimal distance
d
. Let
B
be a
subset of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with
\lambda_{B}\geqslant n-2d+1
. Then
\left|C\right|=M\leqslant n\left|B\right|
.

Lemma 14

(
[
21
]
Lemma 1.4)
.

Let
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
. The maximal eigenvalue associated with
B

is
\lambda_{B}\geqslant 2\sqrt{r(n-r)}-o(n)
.

To prove (
9
), we note that Lemma
14
implies that a radius
r^{*}=n/2-\sqrt{d(n-d)}+o(n)
exists such that
\lambda_{B_{r^{*}}}\geqslant n-2d+1
. Lemma
13
in turn shows that any code of length
n
and minimal distance
d
has at most
n\left|B_{r^{*}}\right|=n\cdot\Vol(r^{*},n)
codewords. The cardinality of a Hamming ball of radius
r
is
\Vol(r,n)=2^{H_{2}(r/n)n+o(n)}
. Equation (
11
) follows the above argument, hence yielding equation (
9
).

We note that the above argument can not be used directly to show an upper bound when
d=n/2-\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. In particular, the
o(n)
term in Lemma
14
renders the search for a meaningful
r^{*}
impossible, as we would ideally require a subset
B
with
\lambda_{B}
close to
n-2d+1=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.

IV-B

Improved Bounds for
d\geqslant n/2-\sqrt{n}

We show in this section an approach to lower bound
\lambda_{B}
for the Hamming ball
B=B_{3}({\mathbf{0}},n)
, which, when coupled with a new proposition stronger than Lemma
13
, leads to an upper bound scaling as
A(n,d)=O(n^{3.5})
for
d\geqslant n/2-\sqrt{n}
.

First we provide a proposition in place of Lemma
14
that does not require an
o(n)
term.

Proposition 15
.

Let
B=B_{3}({\mathbf{0}},n)\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
be the Hamming ball of radius
3
. The maximal eigenvalue associated with
B
is
\lambda_{B}\geqslant\left(\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{n}\gtrsim 2.334\sqrt{n}
.

Proof:
Recall the definition of the maximal eigenvalue in (
17
). We prove the proposition by constructing a function
f
with support in
B
, and for which
{{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}}/{{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}}\approx\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{n}
.
The function
f
will be symmetric, namely its value at a point will depend only on the Hamming weight of the point.
With a slight abuse of notation, such a function is fully defined by its values
f(0),f(1),\ldots,f(n)
at Hamming weights
0,1,\ldots,n
.

Set
f(0)=1
,
f(j)=0
for
j\geqslant 4
, and let

\lambda f(i)=Af(i)=if(i-1)+(n-i)f(i+1)

(18)

for
i=0,1,2
(assuming
f(-1)=0
), where
\lambda=t\sqrt{n}
. We have

\displaystyle f(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda f(0)}{n}=\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}},\hskip 14.22636ptf(2)=\frac{\lambda f(1)-1f(0)}{n-1}=\frac{t^{2}-1}{n-1},

\displaystyle f(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda f(2)-2f(1)}{n-2}=\frac{1}{n-2}\left(\frac{t^{2}-1}{n-1}t\sqrt{n}-2\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

We may use the values
f(i)
and calculate

\displaystyle 2^{n}{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=2t\sqrt{n}+t(t^{2}-1)^{2}\frac{n\sqrt{n}}{n-1}=\left(2t+t(t^{2}-1)^{2}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{n},

\displaystyle 2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=1+t^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{n-1}(t^{2}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\frac{n-1}{n-2}t^{2}\left[\frac{n}{n-1}(t^{2}-1)-2\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=1+t^{2}+(t^{2}-1)^{2}/2+t^{2}(t^{2}-3)^{2}/6+o(1).

We are now ready to optimize the value

\frac{{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}}{{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}}=\left[\frac{2t+t(t^{2}-1)^{2}}{(t^{6}-3t^{4}+9t^{2}+9)/6}+o(1)\right]\sqrt{n}

(19)

over
t>0
.
Taking
t=\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}
, the square bracket term in (
19
) achieves its maximum
\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}+o(1)
.

In order to provide a bound as tight as possible, we improve upon Lemma
13
and show the following proposition.

Proposition 16
.

Let
C
be a code with block length
n
and minimal distance
d
. Let
B
be a
subset of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with
\lambda_{B}>n-2d
. Then
\left|C\right|=M\leqslant\frac{n}{\lambda_{B}-(n-2d)}\left|B\right|
.

The proof can be shown using a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
13
in
[
21
]
, and is provided in Appendix-
-B
for reference.

With Propositions
15
and
16
, we are ready to state the upper bound on
A(n,\left\lceil n/2-\sqrt{n}\,\right\rceil)
.

Theorem 17
.

If a
(n,M,d)
binary code
C
has minimum distance
d\geqslant n/2-\sqrt{n}
, then

M\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}-2+o(1)}\Vol(3,n)=O(n^{3.5}).

Proof:
Let
B=B_{3}({\mathbf{0}},n)
. The maximal eigenvalue induced by
B
is
\lambda_{B}\geqslant\left({\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}+o(1)}\right)\sqrt{n}
according to Proposition
15
. Since
n-2d\leqslant 2\sqrt{n}\lesssim\lambda_{B}
, the cardinality of
C
can be upper bounded using Proposition
16
as

M\leqslant\frac{n}{\lambda_{B}-(n-2d)}\left|B\right|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}-2+o(1)}\Vol(3,n).

Remark 6
.

We note that the argument above can upper bound the size as
M=O(n^{3.5})
as long as
(n-2d)/\sqrt{n}
is strictly smaller than
\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}
. That is, for any
d\gtrsim n/2-\rho\sqrt{n}
, for some constant
\rho<\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6}}/2\approx 1.167
,
we have
A(n,d)=O(n^{3.5})
.

IV-C

Improved Bounds for
d\geqslant n/2-\Theta(\sqrt{n})

In general, it is possible to generalize the approach in Section
IV-B
that lower bounds
\lambda_{B}
for
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
from
r=3
to any given
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Specifically, setting
\lambda=t\sqrt{n}
, we would need to apply the recurrence equation (
18
) iteratively to find
f(i)
as a function of both
t
and
n
, for
i=1,\ldots,r
, compute inner products
{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}
and
{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}
, and solve the optimization problem that maximizes the quotient as in (
19
).
The procedure could be almost intractable for large (but finite)
r
.

A more feasible approach is given in this section to lower bound the maximal eigenvalue associated with
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
.
The approach is comprised of four parts.
The first part constructs a symmetric function
g
on
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
based on a recursive relation involving
\lambda=t\sqrt{n}
, and a scaled version of
g
denoted by
\tilde{g}
, both of which are defined independent of
r
and have support on the entire domain
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
.
In the second part, we define a function
f
which is identical to
g
on
B
and
0
elsewhere, evaluate the quotient seen in equation (
17
), i.e.,
{{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}}/{{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}}
, and show that it can be expressed concisely as the difference of
\lambda
and another term involving
\tilde{g}(r)
and
\tilde{g}(r+1)
, where
\tilde{g}(i)
depends on
i,n
and
t
. The quotient can thus be lower bounded by
\lambda
which guarantees that either
\tilde{g}(r)
or
\tilde{g}(r+1)
is
0
. (That is, for a given
r
, one may choose
t
and
n
appropriately so that
\tilde{g}(r)=0
, or
\tilde{g}(r+1)=0
, and
t\sqrt{n}
would be a lower bound of
\lambda_{B}
.)
In the third part, we introduce two other functions
h
and
\tilde{h}
which, broadly speaking, act as the respective proxies of
g
and
\tilde{g}
. In particular, as
n
grows large, the maximal root of
\tilde{g}(k)
(when viewed as a function of
t
) converges to that of
\tilde{h}(k)
, denoted by
t_{h}(k)
, a value independent of
n
.
The fourth part concludes the argument by showing that the quantity
\lambda_{B}/\sqrt{n}
is lower bounded by the maximal root of
\tilde{h}(r+1)
, when viewed as a function of
t
, for sufficiently large
n
. Finally we leverage Proposition
16
to show
A(n,d)=O(n^{r+1})
as long as
(n-2d)/{\sqrt{n}}<t_{h}(r+1)-s
for some
s>0
.
Throughout this section, we assume
\lambda=t\sqrt{n}
for some constant
t>0
.

Part 1

We first consider a symmetric function
g:{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, and with a slight abuse of notation, write
g(\mathbf{x})=g(w_{H}(\mathbf{x}))
for
\mathbf{x}\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
.
Define
g
by the initial condition
g(0)=1
, and the recurrence relations

\lambda g(i)=Ag(i)=ig(i-1)+(n-i)g(i+1)\mbox{ for }i=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1,

(20)

assuming
g(-1)=0
.
For example, we have

g(1)=\frac{\lambda}{n},\;g(2)=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}-1\right),\;g(3)=\frac{1}{n-2}\left(\frac{\lambda^{3}}{n(n-1)}-\frac{2\lambda}{n}-\frac{\lambda}{n-1}\right).

Define a real-valued function
\tilde{g}
by
\tilde{g}(i)=n^{i/2}g(i)
for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
.
The values
\tilde{g}(i)
for
i=0,1,2,3
are

\tilde{g}(0)=1,\;\tilde{g}(1)=t,\;\tilde{g}(2)=\frac{n}{n-1}(t^{2}-1),\;\tilde{g}(3)=\frac{n}{n-2}\left(\frac{t^{3}n}{n-1}-2t-\frac{tn}{n-1}\right).

Remark 7
.

For each
i=0,1,2,\ldots,
both
g(i)
and
\tilde{g}(i)
are functions of
t
and
n
.
For a fixed
t
,
\tilde{g}(i)
scales with
n
as
O(1)
, and that
g(i)=O(n^{-i/2})
.

Remark 8
.

For any finite
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
it can be shown that
\tilde{g}(k+1)=0
only when
\tilde{g}(k)\neq 0
and
\tilde{g}(k-1)\neq 0
, and that
\tilde{g}(k)
is a degree-
k
polynomial in
t
with leading coefficient
1+O(n^{-1})
. Specifically, for a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\tilde{g}(k)=\begin{cases}t^{k}(1+O(n^{-1}))-g_{k,k-2}t^{k-2}(1+O(n^{-1}))+\ldots+(-1)^{k/2}g_{k,0}(1+O(n^{-1}))&\mbox{ for even }k,\\
t^{k}(1+O(n^{-1}))-g_{k,k-2}t^{k-2}(1+O(n^{-1}))+\ldots+(-1)^{(k-1)/2}g_{k,1}(1+O(n^{-1}))&\mbox{ for odd }k,\end{cases}

where the coefficients
g_{k,k-2\ell},1\leqslant\ell\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
are positive integers independent of
n
and
t
.

Part 2

Let
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
for some finite
r
.
Consider a symmetric function
f
supported on
B
, defined by
f(i)=g(i)
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,r
, and
f(i)=0
for all
i>r
.
First, we have

2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}=f(0)^{2}+\binom{n}{1}f(1)^{2}+\ldots+\binom{n}{r}f(r)^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n}{i}f(i)^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{n}{i}g(i)^{2}.

Using the observation
g(k)=O(n^{-k/2})
and that
f(0)^{2}=1
, the sum scales as
2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}=\Theta(1)
. Note that
Af(i)=Ag(i)=\lambda g(i)
for
i=0,1,\ldots,r-1
and
Af(r)=rf(r-1)+(n-r)f(r+1)=rg(r-1)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle 2^{n}{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=Af(0)f(0)+\binom{n}{1}Af(1)f(1)+\ldots+\binom{n}{r}Af(r)f(r)

\displaystyle=\lambda g(0)^{2}+\binom{n}{1}\lambda g(1)^{2}+\ldots+\binom{n}{r-1}\lambda g(r-1)^{2}+\binom{n}{r}rg(r-1)g(r)

\displaystyle=\lambda 2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}+\binom{n}{r}\left(rg(r-1)g(r)-\lambda g(r)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda 2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}-\binom{n}{r}g(r)(n-r)g(r+1)

\displaystyle=\lambda 2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}-\left(n^{-r}\binom{n}{r}\right)\left(1-\frac{r}{n}\right)\left(n^{r/2}g(r)\right)\left(n^{\frac{r+1}{2}}g(r+1)\right)\sqrt{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda 2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}-\left(n^{-r}\binom{n}{r}\right)\left(1-\frac{r}{n}\right)\tilde{g}(r)\tilde{g}(r+1)\sqrt{n},

where the fourth equality holds by evaluating the recursion relation (
20
) with
i=r
.

The ratio between
{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}
and
{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}
is thus

\frac{{\left\langle{Af,f}\right\rangle}}{{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}}=\lambda-\frac{1}{2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}}\left(n^{-r}\binom{n}{r}\right)\left(1-\frac{r}{n}\right)\tilde{g}(r)\tilde{g}(r+1)\sqrt{n},

(21)

which scales as
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
since
2^{n}{\left\langle{f,f}\right\rangle}=\Theta(1)
.
Denote by
t_{g}(i)=t_{g}(i,n)
the maximal root of
\tilde{g}(i)=\tilde{g}(i,n,\lambda=t\sqrt{n})
when viewed as a function of
t
, for
i=1,2,\ldots
. For example,
t_{g}(1)=0
since
\tilde{g}(1)=t
,
t_{g}(2)=1
since
\tilde{g}(2)=\frac{n}{n-1}(t^{2}-1)
, and
t_{g}(3)
is the maximal root of the polynomial
t^{3}n-3tn+2t=0
.
Then
\lambda_{B}\geqslant\max{(t_{g}(r)\sqrt{n},t_{g}(r+1)\sqrt{n})}
because the second term in (
21
) is
0
when
\lambda
is either
t_{g}(r)\sqrt{n}
or
t_{g}(r+1)\sqrt{n}
.

The first two steps successfully simplify the problem for finding a lower bound on
\lambda_{B}
to the following. First solve
g(r)
and
g(r+1)
by recursively applying (
20
), and then find the maximal roots of
\tilde{g}(r)=2^{r/2}g(r)
and
\tilde{g}(r+1)=2^{(r+1)/2}g(r+1)
, which are viewed as functions of
t
. The recursive steps, however, still pose a great challenge when the radius
r
for
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
is large. For example,
g(3)=\frac{1}{n-2}\left[\frac{t\sqrt{n}}{n-1}\left(t^{2}-1\right)-\frac{2t}{\sqrt{n}}\right]
and

g(4)=\frac{1}{n-3}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\frac{t\sqrt{n}}{n-2}\left[\frac{t\sqrt{n}}{n-1}\left(t^{2}-1\right)-\frac{2t}{\sqrt{n}}\right]-\frac{3}{n-1}\left(t^{2}-1\right)\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}
.
Solving roots of
\tilde{g}(r)
and
\tilde{g}(r+1)
, which in general are complicated functions in both
n
and
t
, is an even greater challenge.
The following third step shows that
t_{g}(i)
converges to the maximal root of a simpler polynomial in
t
for all finite
i
when
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Part 3

Define by
h
a real-valued function
h:\left\{0,1,\ldots,n\right\}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by setting
h(0)=1
and the recurrence relation (assuming
h(-1)=0
)

\lambda h(i)=ih(i-1)+nh(i+1)\mbox{ for }i=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1,

(22)

which differs from (
20
) only in the coefficient of
h(i+1)
. It can be shown that
h(k+1)=0
only when
h(k)\neq 0
and
h(k-1)\neq 0
, and that
h(k)
is either
0
or scales as
\Theta(n^{-k/2})
for each finite
k
.
The functions
h
and
g
coincide asymptotically for all finite
k
. We state precisely a bound on the ratio between the two in the following lemma.

Lemma 18
.

For any given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\left\{0\right\}
, if
h(i)\neq 0
for all
i\leqslant k-1
, then

g(k)=\begin{cases}h(k)(1+O(n^{-1}))&\mbox{ if }h(k)\neq 0,\\
O(n^{-(k+2)/2})&\mbox{ if }h(k)=0.\end{cases}

Proof:
First note that
g(0)=h(0)=1
,
g(1)=h(1)=\frac{\lambda}{n}
and
g(2)=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}-1\right)=\frac{1}{n}(1+\frac{1}{n-1})\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}-1\right)=(1+O(n^{-1}))\frac{1}{n}\left(\lambda h(1)-1h(0)\right)=h(2)\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right)
. Also,
h(2)=0\mbox{ if and only if }g(2)=0
. Hence the lemma holds for
k=0,1,2
.
Assume, for some
k\geqslant 2
,
h(i)\neq 0
and
g(i)=h(i)(1+O(n^{-1}))
for
i=0,1,\ldots,k
. Then
g(i)
and
h(i)
both scale as
\Theta(n^{-i/2})
for
i=0,1,\ldots,k
. Equation (
22
) yields

h(k+1)={n^{-1}}\left(\lambda h(k)-kh(k-1)\right)

and (
20
) yields

\displaystyle g(k+1)

\displaystyle={(n-k)}^{-1}\left(\lambda g(k)-kg(k-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right)n^{-1}\left[\lambda h(k)(1+O(n^{-1}))-kh(k-1)\left(1+O(n^{-1})\right))\right],

which implies
g(k+1)=h(k+1)(1+O(n^{-1}))
when
h(k+1)\neq 0
since both
\lambda h(k)
and
kh(k-1)
scale as
\Theta(n^{-(k-1)/2})
. When
h(k+1)=0
,
g(k+1)=O(n^{-1}n^{-(k-1)/2}n^{-1})=O(n^{-(k+3)/2})
.
Hence the lemma holds by the principle of mathematical induction.

Consider a real-valued function
\tilde{h}:\left\{0,1,\ldots,n\right\}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
\tilde{h}(i)=n^{i/2}h(i)
. The values
\tilde{h}(i)
for
i=0,1,2,3,4
are

\tilde{h}(0)=1,\tilde{h}(1)=t,\tilde{h}(2)=t^{2}-1,\tilde{h}(3)=t^{3}-3t,\tilde{h}(4)=t^{4}-6t^{2}+3.

Remark 9
.

The function
\tilde{h}
satisfies the recurrence relation

t\tilde{h}(i)=i\tilde{h}(i-1)+\tilde{h}(i+1),

(23)

which follows from the recurrence relation (
22
) and that
\tilde{h}(i)=n^{i/2}h(i)
.

Remark 10
.

For
k
a finite positive integer,
h(k)
depends on both
n
and
t
, whereas
\tilde{h}(k)
is independent of
n
and is a degree-
k
monic polynomial of
t
.
The polynomial
\tilde{h}(k)
can be expressed as follows,

\tilde{h}(k)=\begin{cases}t^{k}-g_{k,k-2}t^{k-2}+\ldots+(-1)^{k/2}g_{k,0}&\mbox{ for even }k,\\
t^{k}-g_{k,k-2}t^{k-2}+\ldots+(-1)^{(k-1)/2}g_{k,1}&\mbox{ for odd }k,\end{cases}

where the coefficients
g_{k,k-2\ell},1\leqslant\ell\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
are the same as those in Remark
8
.

Denote by
t_{h}(i)
the maximal root of
\tilde{h}(i)
when viewed as a function of
t
, for
i\geqslant 1
. For example,
t_{h}(1)=0
,
t_{h}(2)=1
, and
t_{h}(3)=\sqrt{3}
.
We now show that, for all finite
k
, the maximal roots
t_{h}(i)
and
t_{g}(i)
are equal when
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
be finite. Then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}t_{g}(k,n)=t_{h}(k)
.

Proof:
First we prove that for any finite
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
(t_{h}(1),t_{h}(2),\ldots,t_{h}(k))
is a strictly increasing sequence, using the principle of mathematical induction. For
k=3
, the sequence is
(0,1,\sqrt{3})
, which verifies the claim.
Assume for a finite
k
, the sequence
(t_{h}(1),t_{h}(2),\ldots,t_{h}(k))
is strictly increasing. Note that
\tilde{h}(k+1)=t\tilde{h}(k)-k\tilde{h}(k-1)
is negative when
t=t_{h}(k)
since
\tilde{h}(k)=0
and
\tilde{h}(k-1)>0
. Since the leading term in
\tilde{h}(k+1)
is
t^{k+1}
and thus grows to positive infinity for
t
large enough, we must have
t_{h}(k+1)>t_{h}(k)
.

We now show that, the sequence of roots
t_{g}(k,n)
of
\tilde{g}(k)
, for
n=1,2,\ldots
, converges to
t_{h}(k)
.
Let
\delta\in(0,t_{h}(k)-t_{h}(k-1))
be a small constant such that

\tilde{h}(k,t)>0
for all
t\in(t_{h}(k),t_{h}(k)+\delta]
,
and

\tilde{h}(k,t)<0
for all
t\in[t_{h}(k)-\delta,t_{h}(k))
.
Let
\epsilon>0
be the minimum
\epsilon=\min\left\{-\tilde{h}(k,t_{h}(k)-\delta),\tilde{h}(k,t_{h}(k)+\delta)\right\}
.
Leveraging Lemma
18
,
\tilde{g}(k,t_{h}(k)+\delta)\geqslant\epsilon(1+O(n^{-1}))
and
\tilde{g}(k,t_{h}(k)-\delta)\leqslant-\epsilon(1+O(n^{-1}))
. This implies the existence of a root of

\tilde{g}(k)
when viewed as a function of
t
in the interval
(t_{h}(k)-\delta,t_{h}(k)+\delta)
, for all sufficiently large
n
. Note also that, for
t>t_{h}(k)+\delta
,
\tilde{g}(k,t)
is strictly positive for all sufficiently large
n
, because
\tilde{h}(k,t)\geqslant\epsilon
.
Since
\delta>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, the root

t_{g}(k,n)
converges to
t_{h}(k)
as
n
grows to infinity.

Remark 11
.

It is known that the roots of a polynomial (counting multiplicities and only up to permutation) depend continuously on the coefficients of the polynomial
[

,
57
]
. Hence, Theorem

also follows as a direct consequence of Remarks

and

We are now ready to state the main result in this section, which admits a practical approach to lower bound
\lambda_{B}
for fixed

Theorem 20
.

Let
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
and
\lambda_{B}
be the maximal eigenvalue of adjacency matrix of the subgraph of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
induced by
B
. Then
\lambda_{B}
is lower bounded by
(t_{h}(r+1)+o(1))\sqrt{n}

Proof:
Equation (
21
) guarantees that
\lambda_{B}\geqslant\max{\left\{t_{g}(r,n)\sqrt{n},t_{g}(r+1,n)\sqrt{n}\right\}}
. Since
t_{h}(k)
is strictly increasing in
k
, Lemma
19
implies that for large
n
, the bound reduces to
\lambda_{B}\geqslant(t_{h}(r+1)+o(1))\sqrt{n}
.

Theorem
20
generalizes Proposition
15
by establishing lower bounds on
\lambda_{B_{r}}
for fixed
r
other than the special case
r=3
. This in turn yields a sequence of bounds on
\left|C\right|
whose applicability depends on the scaling behaviour of the minimum distance in terms of the blocklength

Corollary 21
.

If a
(n,M,d)
binary code
C
has distance
d>\frac{1}{2}\left[n-(t_{h}(r+1)-s)\sqrt{n}\,\right]

Proof:
Let

\displaystyle M\leqslant\frac{n}{\lambda_{B}-(n-2d)}\left|B\right|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{n}}{(t_{h}(r+1)+o(1))-(t_{h}(r+1)-s)}\left|B\right|=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{s+o(1)}\Vol(r,n).

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we study bounds on the cardinality of codes with specified minimum distance
d
targeting the regime with
d=n/2-\Omega(\sqrt{n})
. The codes in this regime have vanishing rate, which renders known bounds that dictate the tradeoff between the code rate and relative distance ineffective. We obtain two families of codes based on specifically crafted BCH-like constructions, and a sequence of upper bounds for
d\geqslant n/2-\rho\sqrt{n}
for
\rho\in(0.5,9.5)
.

The proposed cyclic code constructions are targeted at the regimes
d\geqslant n/2-\Omega(\sqrt{n})
and
d\geqslant n/2-\Omega({n}^{2/3})
, and have sizes that are polynomial and quasi-polynomial in
n
, respectively. The proof of the upper bound makes extensive use of Fourier analysis on the Hamming cube as a group, and a new bounding technique for the maximal eigenvalue associated with Hamming balls of finite radii.

An interesting problem for future work is to study the potential of the Fourier-analytical approach to upper bound the sizes of constant weight codes with large minimum distance. This problem has been studied in the regime
d=\delta n
with
\delta\in(0,0.5)
and the best known result is the second linear programming bound.
Another interesting problem, as pointed out by authors of
[
1
]
, is the underlying similarities between the spectral-based and the Fourier-analytical approaches. For example, if one considers only the Hamming balls
B=B_{r}({\mathbf{0}},n)
among all subsets of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, and requires functions
f
to be symmetric, the maximal eigenvalue
\lambda_{B_{r}}
appears to be equivalent to the value
\lambda_{r}
in
[
1
]
. This implies that our bounds on
\lambda_{B_{r}}
improves on the bounds on
\lambda_{r}
for finite
r
, and that for
r
scaling sub-linearly in
n
, the latter may yield new bounds on
A(n,d)
in the regime
d=n/2-\Theta(n^{s})
for
s\in(0.5,1)
.

Harmonic Analysis

We compile in this section harmonic analysis preliminaries as in
[
21
,
58
]
. See
[
58
]
for a more detailed treatment.
Here we list several necessary definitions
and simple facts.

Consider the abelian group structure
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}=({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}
on the hypercube
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
.
The characters of the abelian group
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
are
\left\{\chi_{z}\right\}_{z\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}}
, where
\chi_{z}:\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}{\ \rightarrow\ }\left\{-1,1\right\}
is given by
\chi_{z}(x)=(-1)^{\langle x,z\rangle}
and
\langle x,z\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}z_{i}
.

Consider the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})=\left\{f:{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}{\ \rightarrow\ }{\mathbb{R}}\right\}
endowed with the inner
product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, associated with the uniform distribution on
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
:

\langle f,g\rangle=\E_{U_{n}}fg=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}}f(x){g(x)}.

(24)

The set of
2^{n}
characters
\left\{\chi_{z}\right\}_{z\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}}
form an orthonormal basis in the space
\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
, equipped with uniform probability distribution. That is, for each
z,z^{\prime}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
,

{\left\langle{\chi_{z},\chi_{z^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}=\delta_{z,z^{\prime}},
where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta function.
The
Fourier transform
of a function
f\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
is the function
\mathscr{F}(f)=\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
given by the
coefficients of the unique expansion of
f
in terms of the characters:

f(x)=\sum_{z}\widehat{f}(z)\chi_{z}(x)\mbox{ or equivalently, }\widehat{f}(z)={\left\langle{f,\chi_{z}}\right\rangle}.

(25)

One may show that
\mathscr{F}(\mathscr{F}({f}))=2^{n}f
, and
\E f=\widehat{f}(0)
.
For
f,g\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
, the Parseval’s identity holds:
{\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle}=\sum_{z}\widehat{f}(z)\widehat{g}(z)=2^{n}{\left\langle{\widehat{f},\widehat{g}}\right\rangle}
.
A special case of the above equality is the following equality:

\E{f}^{2}=\sum_{z}{\widehat{f}(z)}^{2}.

The convolution of
f
and
g
is defined by
(f*g)(x)=\E_{y}{f(y)g(x+y)}

=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{y\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}}f(y){g(x+y)}
.
The convolution transforms to dot product:
\widehat{f*g}=\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g}
. The convolution operator is commutative and associative.
For arbitrary functions
f,g,h\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
, the following equality holds:

{\left\langle{f*g,h}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{f,g*h}\right\rangle}.

(26)

Also, it can be shown that
\E(f*g)=\E f\cdot\E g
for all functions
f,g\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
.

In this section and in Appendix-
-B
,
L\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
is a function defined by
L(x)=2^{n}
for
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
with
w_{H}(x)=1
, and
L(x)=0
otherwise.
Let
A
denote the
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
adjacency matrix of
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
, such that
Af(x)=(Af)(x)=\sum_{y\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}:d_{H}(x,y)=1}f(y)
.
For any
f\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
holds
Af=f*L
because for
x\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
,
Af(x)=\sum_{y:d_{H}(x,y)=1}f(y)

=\sum_{y:w_{H}(y)=1}f(x+y)

=\E_{y}L(y)f(x+y)=(L*f)(x)=(f*L)(x)
.
The Fourier transform of
L
is the function
\mathscr{F}(L)=\widehat{L}
given by

\widehat{L}(z)={\left\langle{L,\chi_{z}}\right\rangle}

=\sum_{x:w_{H}(x)=1}

(-1)^{{\left\langle{x,z}\right\rangle}}

=n-2\cdot w_{H}(z)
.

For
C\subset{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
, let
1_{C}\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
be the indicator function of
C
. It can be shown that a code
C
has minimum distance
d
if and only if
(1_{C}*1_{C})(x)=0
for all
0<w_{H}{(x)}<d
.

Proof of Proposition
16

Let
f_{B}
be an eigenfunction supported on
B
corresponding to its maximal eigenvalue
\lambda_{B}
. That is
\lambda_{B}={\left\langle{Af_{B},f_{B}}\right\rangle}/{\left\langle{f_{B},f_{B}}\right\rangle}
. It is known that the maximum can be attained with an non-negative function
f_{B}
, and further we have
Af_{B}\geqslant\lambda_{B}f_{B}
(see
[
20
, p.13-15 and appendix C]
) for details). We write
f=f_{B}
and
\lambda=\lambda_{B}
interchangeably, and denote the Hamming weight of
x\in{{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n}
by
\left|x\right|=w_{H}(x)
, in this proof.
As
f
is supported on
B
, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields the following:

\E^{2}f={\left\langle{f,1_{B}}\right\rangle}^{2}\leqslant\E f^{2}\cdot\E(1_{B})^{2}=\E f^{2}\cdot\left|B\right|/2^{n}.

(27)

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{L}({{\mathbb{F}}}_{\!2}^{n})
be a function such that
(\widehat{\phi})^{2}=\widehat{\phi*\phi}=1_{C}*1_{C}
. Equivalently,
\phi*\phi=2^{n}\widehat{1_{C}*1_{C}}=2^{n}\widehat{1_{C}}^{2}
.
Therefore we have

\phi*\phi\geqslant 0\mbox{ and }\frac{\E(\phi^{2})}{\E^{2}(\phi)}=\frac{(\phi*\phi)(0)}{\widehat{\phi}^{2}(0)}=\left|C\right|.

(28)

Now let
F=\phi*f
. We estimate the product
{\left\langle{AF,F}\right\rangle}
in two ways.
First,

\displaystyle{\left\langle{AF,F}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{(\phi*f)*L,\phi*f}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\phi*\phi*f,f*L}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\phi*\phi*f,Af}\right\rangle}\geqslant{\left\langle{\phi*\phi*f,\lambda f}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\left\langle{\phi*f,\phi*f}\right\rangle}=\lambda{\left\langle{F,F}\right\rangle}=\lambda\E F^{2}.

Second, by Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle{\left\langle{AF,F}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=2^{n}{\left\langle{\widehat{AF},\widehat{F}}\right\rangle}=2^{n}{\left\langle{\widehat{L}\cdot\widehat{F},\widehat{F}}\right\rangle}=\sum_{z}\left(n-2\left|z\right|\right)\widehat{F}^{2}(z).

Since
\widehat{F}=\widehat{\phi}\cdot\widehat{f}
and
(\widehat{\phi})^{2}(z)=(1_{C}*1_{C})(z)
,

\widehat{F}(z)=0
for all
0<\left|z\right|<d
. We can estimate
{\left\langle{AF,F}\right\rangle}
by

\displaystyle\sum_{z}\left(n-2\left|z\right|\right)\widehat{F}^{2}(z)=n\widehat{F}^{2}(0)+\sum_{z:\left|z\right|\geqslant d}\left(n-2\left|z\right|\right)\widehat{F}^{2}(z)

\displaystyle\leqslant n\widehat{F}^{2}(0)+(n-2d)\sum_{z}\widehat{F}^{2}(z)=n\E^{2}F+(n-2d)\E F^{2}.

Combining the two estimates, we have the following inequality:
n\E^{2}F

\geqslant\left(\lambda-(n-2d)\right)

\E F^{2}
. Since

\E^{2}F=\E^{2}(\phi*f)=[\widehat{\phi*f}(0)]^{2}=[\widehat{\phi}(0)\widehat{f}(0)]^{2}=\E^{2}\phi\E^{2}f,

\displaystyle\E F^{2}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{F,F}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\phi*f,\phi*f}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{\phi*\phi,f*f}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\geqslant 1/2^{n}(\phi*\phi)(0)(f*f)(0)=1/2^{n}\E\phi^{2}\E f^{2},

as
\phi*\phi=2^{n}\cdot\widehat{1_{C}}^{2}\geqslant 0
,
we now have

n\E^{2}\phi\E^{2}f\geqslant\left(\lambda-(n-2d)\right)\frac{1}{2^{n}}\E\phi^{2}\E f^{2}.

(29)

Leveraging equations (
27
), (
28
), and (
29
),
the size of any code
C
with minimum distance
d
is

\left|C\right|=\frac{\E\phi^{2}}{\E^{2}\phi}\leqslant\frac{n}{\lambda-(n-2d)}\cdot 2^{n}\frac{\E^{2}f}{\E f^{2}}\leqslant\frac{n}{\lambda-(n-2d)}\left|B\right|.
