On Harmonic Analysis Operators in Laguerre–Dunkl and Laguerre-Symmetrized Settings
Adam NOWAK †, Krzysztof STEMPAK ‡ and Tomasz Z. SZAREK †

Abstract
We study several fundamental harmonic analysis operators in the multi-dimensional context of the Dunkl harmonic oscillator and the underlying group of reflections isomorphic to ℤ 2 d \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d} . Noteworthy, we admit negative values of the multiplicity functions. Our investigations include maximal operators, g g -functions, Lusin area integrals, Riesz transforms and multipliers of Laplace and Laplace–Stieltjes type. By means of the general Calderón–Zygmund theory we prove that these operators are bounded on weighted L p L^{p} spaces, 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty , and from weighted L 1 L^{1} to weighted weak L 1 L^{1} . We also obtain similar results for analogous set of operators in the closely related multi-dimensional Laguerre-symmetrized framework. The latter emerges from a symmetrization procedure proposed recently by the first two authors. As a by-product of the main developments we get some new results in the multi-dimensional Laguerre function setting of convolution type.

Introduction

Analysis related to Dunkl operators, that is differential-difference operators associated with reflection groups, is an important development in modern mathematics. For basic concepts of this theory see Dunkl’s pioneering work
[
16
]
and, for instance, the survey article by Rösler
[
35
]
. Harmonic analysis related to the Dunkl harmonic oscillator (DHO in short) has been intensively studied in recent years by the authors
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
43
,
44
]
and many other mathematicians, see, e.g.,
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
13
,
26
,
46
]
and references therein. A commonly appearing assumption in this literature is that the underlying multiplicity function is non-negative. The latter postulate is critical in several important aspects of the Dunkl theory, like existence of a convolution structure or existence and uniqueness of the intertwining operator. Nevertheless, there are few papers like
[
1
,
2
]
and the very recent paper
[
33
]
by the first two authors, where certain harmonic analysis aspects for the one-dimensional DHO are successfully studied with negative values of the multiplicity function admitted. This in a large extent motivated our present research since then the following question naturally arises. Does it make sense to study basic harmonic analysis operators in the context of the multi-dimensional DHO when the underlying multiplicity function takes also negative values? The answer we find is definitely affirmative, at least in the special case when the associated group of reflections is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}
. It occurs that negative multiplicity functions have the same rights in the sense of results obtained, but not in the sense of the associated analysis which is in this case more sophisticated and requires more subtle techniques. Similar conclusions were drawn recently in the more elementary Dunkl Laplacian context and the same group of reflections by Castro and the third-named author
[
14
]
. The latter paper was in fact an important inspiration for the present one.

The context of the DHO with the underlying group of reflections isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}
(written shortly
[{\rm DHO},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}]
) is sometimes called the Laguerre–Dunkl setting. The reason is that this Dunkl framework is deeply connected with the situation of Laguerre function expansions of convolution type, in particular the standard eigenfunctions of the
[{\rm DHO},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}]
are directly related to Laguerre functions of convolution type.
The results we obtain in the Laguerre–Dunkl context, see Theorem
3.1
, are weighted
L^{p}
mapping properties of several fundamental harmonic analysis operators like the heat semigroup maximal operator, mixed
g
-functions of arbitrary orders, mixed Lusin area integrals of arbitrary orders, Riesz transforms of arbitrary order, and spectral multipliers of Laplace and Laplace–Stieltjes transform type (these multiplier operators cover, as special cases, imaginary powers of the DHO and fractional integrals related to the DHO, respectively). This brings a number of new results that extend those existing in the literature by considering non-positive multiplicity functions and more general operators. This concerns, in particular, the authors’ papers
[
29
,
30
]
where heat semigroup maximal operator, first order Riesz transforms and imaginary powers of the
[{\rm DHO},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}]
were studied and
[
43
,
44
]
, where the Laplace type multipliers of both types and first order
g
-functions and Lusin area integrals for the
[{\rm DHO},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}]
were investigated. Further, we generalize in the above mentioned directions and, in addition, by allowing weights, the results of Ben Salem and Samaali
[
7
]
and Nefzi
[
26
]
, where the Hilbert transform and certain higher-order Riesz transforms for the [DHO,
\mathbb{Z}^{1}_{2}
] and
[{\rm DHO},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}]
, respectively, were studied. We also get a weighted extension of the results by Forzani, Sasso and Scotto
[
18
]
for the heat semigroup maximal operator in the context of
[{\rm DHO},\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}]
. It is worth mentioning that recently unweighted
L^{p}
-boundedness of first order Riesz transforms and imaginary powers associated with the DHO and an arbitrary finite group of reflections was obtained by Amri
[
3
]
and Amri and Tayari
[
6
]
, respectively. See
[
12
,
13
]
for more results in the general DHO setting. Our present analysis is a natural, but by no means trivial, first step towards conjecturing and proving further results for the DHO and a general reflection group, possibly with non-positive multiplicity functions admitted.

Our general strategy in proving
L^{p}
mapping properties of the above mentioned operators is essentially the same as in our previous papers
[
29
,
30
,
43
,
44
]
. Thus, using symmetries involved, we first reduce the analysis to a number of suitably defined auxiliary operators related to a smaller measure metric space, which is actually a space of homogeneous type. Then to treat these auxiliary operators we apply the general vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund theory for spaces of homogeneous type. Here the most difficult step is to show the so-called standard estimates for the integral kernels involved. To achieve that, we employ the technique of kernel estimates that was inspired by Sasso’s article
[
38
]
and then developed in the first two authors’ paper
[
28
]
, and later gradually refined by the first and third authors in
[
34
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
. Among all these references especially
[
34
]
is relevant for our purposes since the tools established there allow to cover non-positive multiplicity functions. It is worth pointing out that there exists a variant of the Calderón–Zygmund theory suited to the general Dunkl setting with arbitrary finite group of reflections, see Amri and Sifi
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, but as stated it does not cover the case of a non-positive multiplicity function and does not provide weighted results.

Another substantial aim of this paper is connected with the first two authors’ papers
[
27
,
32
]
. The latter article proposes a symmetrization procedure in a context of general discrete orthogonal expansions related to a second order differential operator
L
, a ‘Laplacian’. This procedure, combined with a unified conjugacy scheme established in
[
27
]
allows one to associate, via a suitable embedding, a differential-difference ‘Laplacian’
\mathbb{L}
with the initially given orthogonal system of eigenfunctions of
L
so that the resulting extended conjugacy scheme has the natural classical shape. In particular, the related ‘partial derivatives’ decomposing
\mathbb{L}
are formally skew-adjoint in an appropriate
L^{2}
space and they commute with Riesz transforms and conjugate Poisson integrals. Thus the symmetrization procedure overcomes the main inconvenience of the theory postulated in
[
27
]
, that is the lack of symmetry in the principal objects and relations resulting in essential deviations of the theory from the classical shape. The price is, however, that the ‘Laplacian’
\mathbb{L}
and the associated ‘partial derivatives’ are not differential, but differential-difference operators. It was shown in
[
32
]
that the symmetrization is supported by a good
L^{2}
theory. However, it seems to be practically impossible to develop the
L^{p}
theory on the level of generality assumed in
[
32
]
. Thus it is of interest and importance to look at the problem in concrete classical settings where proper tools and techniques are either known or can be effectively elaborated. Recently Langowski
[
22
,
24
,
25
]
verified that, in case of one-dimensional Jacobi trigonometric polynomial and function contexts, the symmetrization leads to an extended setting admitting a good
L^{p}
theory. In the present paper we take the opportunity to investigate another, this time multi-dimensional, concrete realization of the symmetrization procedure and find out that it admits a good
L^{p}
theory as well, giving further support for the theory in
[
32
]
. More precisely, we apply the real variant of the symmetrization procedure to the multi-dimensional Laguerre function setting of convolution type. This results in the Laguerre-symmetrized setting, which turns out to be closely related to the Laguerre–Dunkl context. Consequently, fundamental harmonic analysis operators can be analyzed by means of essentially the same strategy and technical tools. The outcome of our investigation is contained in Theorem
3.7
.

The aforementioned framework of Laguerre function expansions of convolution type was widely studied from harmonic analysis perspective in the last decade or two; see, for instance, the authors’ papers
[
28
,
31
,
33
,
34
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
and references given there. The results obtained in this paper for the Laguerre–Dunkl and the Laguerre-symmetrized situations contribute also to this line of research. This is because, roughly speaking, the Laguerre setting can be recovered either from the Laguerre–Dunkl or the Laguerre-symmetrized context via a restriction to symmetric functions. Then our present results can be projected suitably to deliver new information about
L^{p}
mapping properties of interesting variants of mixed
g
-functions, mixed Lusin area integrals and Riesz transforms (all of them of arbitrary orders) that were not investigated earlier in the Laguerre setting; see Theorem
3.13
. One also gets enhancements of existing results like, for instance,
L^{p}
-boundedness of first order Lusin area integrals valid for a complete range of the associated parameter of type; see Theorem
3.14
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the notation and the three settings considered, that is the Laguerre, Laguerre–Dunkl and Laguerre-symmetrized situations. In Section
3
we state the main results of the paper, Theorems
3.1
and
3.7
. Here we also introduce auxiliary Laguerre-type operators in the Laguerre–Dunkl and the Laguerre-symmetrized settings, which are related to a smaller space. Then we state Theorems
3.2
and
3.8
which allow us to reduce the proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.7
to a simpler situation
involving only the auxiliary operators. The ultimate reduction is due to the general Calderón–Zygmund theory, see Theorems
3.3
and
3.9
. We finish this section by stating new results pertaining to the Laguerre setting, see Theorems
3.13
and
3.14
, and indicating further results in all the contexts considered. The most technical part of the paper, Section
4
, is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
3.6
and
3.12
that contain standard estimates for kernels associated with the above mentioned auxiliary Laguerre-type operators and thus deliver the missing link in the proofs of Theorems
3.3
and
3.9
. The paper finishes with two appendices containing, respectively, a minor auxiliary result and a table summarizing notation of various objects in the three contexts considered in this paper.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

In the whole paper

\displaystyle d\mu_{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{2\alpha_{i}+1}\,dx_{i}.

The restriction of

to

.

Throughout the paper we use a fairly standard notation with essentially all symbols referring to the measure metric spaces

, where

stands for the Euclidean norm. In particular, by

. In an analogous way we interpret

2.2
Laguerre setting of convolution type

The Laguerre functions of convolution type are given by

\displaystyle\ell_{k}^{\alpha}(x)=c_{k}^{\alpha}\exp\big({-}\|x\|^{2}/2\big)\prod_{i=1}^{d}L_{k_{i}}^{\alpha_{i}}\big(x_{i}^{2}\big),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

where
c_{k}^{\alpha}>0
are the normalizing constants, and
L_{k_{i}}^{\alpha_{i}}
are the classical one-dimensional Laguerre polynomials. The system
\{\ell_{k}^{\alpha}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
.

The
\ell_{k}^{\alpha}
are eigenfunctions of the Bessel harmonic oscillator

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}=-\Delta-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2\alpha_{i}+1}{x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\|x\|^{2}

acting on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
. We have
L_{\alpha}\ell_{k}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{|k|}^{\alpha}\ell_{k}^{\alpha}
, where

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}=4n+2|\alpha|+2d,\qquad n\geq 0.

We denote by the same symbol
L_{\alpha}
the natural self-adjoint extension in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
whose spectral resolution is given by the
\ell_{k}^{\alpha}
.

Partial derivatives associated with
L_{\alpha}
emerge from the decomposition

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}=\lambda_{0}^{\alpha}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}^{*}\delta_{i},

where

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+x_{i},\qquad\delta_{i}^{*}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+x_{i}-\frac{2\alpha_{i}+1}{x_{i}},

\delta_{i}^{*}
being the formal adjoint of
\delta_{i}
in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
. Note that the action of
\delta_{i}
on
\ell_{k}^{\alpha}
is much simpler comparing to
\delta_{i}^{*}
, in fact we have (see
[
28
, p. 652]
or
[
27
, p. 694]
)

\displaystyle\delta_{i}\ell_{k}^{\alpha}(x)=-2\sqrt{k_{i}}x_{i}\ell_{k-e_{i}}^{\alpha+e_{i}}(x),\qquad\delta_{i}^{*}\ell_{k}^{\alpha}(x)=2\sqrt{k_{i}}x_{i}\ell_{k-e_{i}}^{\alpha+e_{i}}(x)+\left(2x_{i}-\frac{2\alpha_{i}+1}{x_{i}}\right)\ell_{k}^{\alpha}(x);

here and elsewhere we use the convention that
\ell_{k}^{\alpha}\equiv 0
if
k\notin\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Hence it is natural to consider
\delta_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, as the first order partial derivatives related to
L_{\alpha}
. This choice is further motivated by mapping properties of fundamental harmonic analysis operators involving derivatives, like Riesz–Laguerre transforms. On the other hand, the proper choice of higher-order derivatives is a more complicated matter. Taking into account a fixed
i
th axis, one can iterate
\delta_{i}
or interlace it with
\delta_{i}^{*}
. In fact, both possibilities are well motivated and of interest.

The heat semigroup
T_{t}^{\alpha}=\exp(-tL_{\alpha})
,
t>0
, has an integral representation in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
and the heat kernel is explicitly given by

\displaystyle G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{(\sinh 2t)^{d}}\exp\left(\!{-}\frac{1}{2}\coth(2t)\big(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\big)\!\right)\!\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}y_{i})^{-\alpha_{i}}I_{\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{x_{i}y_{i}}{\sinh 2t}\right),\!\!\!\qquad t>0.

Here
I_{\alpha_{i}}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
\alpha_{i}
. As a function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
I_{\alpha_{i}}
is smooth and strictly positive. The following representation of
G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
, which is crucial in case
\alpha\notin[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
, was derived in
[
34
]
:

\displaystyle G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^{d}}C_{\alpha,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1-\zeta^{2}}{2\zeta}\right)^{d+|\alpha|+2|\varepsilon|}(xy)^{2\varepsilon}\int\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\Pi_{\alpha+\boldsymbol{1}+\varepsilon}(ds),

(2.1)

where
C_{\alpha,\varepsilon}=[2(\alpha+\boldsymbol{1})]^{\boldsymbol{1}-\varepsilon}
,
t
and
\zeta
are related by
\zeta=\tanh t
or, equivalently,

\displaystyle t=t(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+\zeta}{1-\zeta},\qquad\zeta\in(0,1),

and
\Pi_{\nu}
is the measure on
(-1,1)^{d}
given by

\displaystyle\Pi_{\nu}(ds)=\frac{1}{\pi^{d/2}2^{|\nu|}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\big(1-s_{i}^{2}\big)^{\nu_{i}-1/2}\,ds_{i}}{\Gamma(\nu_{i}+1/2)},\qquad\nu\in(0,\infty)^{d}.

Here and elsewhere we omit writing the set of integration with respect to
\Pi_{\nu}
, which is always the cube
(-1,1)^{d}
. Recall that the integrated expression
\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})
depends implicitly also on
x
,
y
and
s
. We remark that there is a more elementary representation of
G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
in the spirit of (
2.1
), but it is restricted to
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
, see
[
28
,
34
]
.

In the Laguerre setting objects like
\ell_{k}^{\alpha}
,
L_{\alpha}
,
\delta_{i}
,
\delta_{i}^{*}
, are considered on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. Nevertheless, the same defining formulas extend them naturally to the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In what follows we will use these extensions with the same notation and without further mention. An analogous remark pertains to the heat kernel
G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
and the above formulas, possibly with a limiting interpretation when some coordinates of
x
or
y
vanish.

2.3
Laguerre–Dunkl setting

This situation corresponds to the Dunkl harmonic oscillator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the associated group of reflections isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}
. The multi-parameter
\alpha
represents the so-called multiplicity function, which is non-negative if and only if
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
. For
\alpha=-\boldsymbol{1}/2
the setting reduces to the context of the classical harmonic oscillator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The Laguerre–Dunkl functions are defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\alpha}(x)=(-1)^{|\lfloor{k/2}\rfloor|}2^{-d/2}x^{\overline{k}}\ell_{\lfloor{k/2}\rfloor}^{\alpha+\overline{k}}(x),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

In the terminology of Dunkl theory the
h_{k}^{\alpha}
are called generalized Hermite functions. The system
\{h_{k}^{\alpha}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
. We mention that
h_{k}^{\alpha}
is
\eta
-symmetric if and only if
\eta=\overline{k}
. The associated Laguerre–Dunkl Laplacian is the differential-difference operator given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}f(x)=L_{\alpha}f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\alpha_{i}+1/2)\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{i}x)}{x_{i}^{2}}.

We have
\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}h_{k}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}h_{k}^{\alpha}
. The natural in this context self-adjoint extension of
\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}
in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
will be denoted by the same symbol.

We have the symmetric decomposition

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big(\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}\mathfrak{D}_{i}+\mathfrak{D}_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}\big),

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{i}=T_{i}^{\alpha}+x_{i},\qquad\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}=-T_{i}^{\alpha}+x_{i},

are the mutual formal adjoints in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
, being

\displaystyle T_{i}^{\alpha}f(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(x)+(\alpha_{i}+1/2)\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{i}x)}{x_{i}}

(in the Dunkl theory
T_{i}^{\alpha}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, are called Dunkl operators). For symmetry reasons, both
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}
are the natural first order partial derivatives associated with
\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}
. Their action on
h_{k}^{\alpha}
is (see
[
30
, p. 546]
)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{i}h_{k}^{\alpha}=m(k_{i},\alpha_{i})h_{k-e_{i}}^{\alpha},\qquad\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}h_{k}^{\alpha}=m(k_{i}+1,\alpha_{i})h_{k+e_{i}}^{\alpha},

(2.2)

where
m(k_{i},\alpha_{i})=\sqrt{2k_{i}+2\overline{k_{i}}(2\alpha_{i}+1)}
; here and in other places we use the convention that
h_{k}^{\alpha}\equiv 0
when
k\notin\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

Higher-order derivatives associated with
\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}
are formed by arbitrary finite compositions of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
. For a given
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and a block multi-index
\omega=(\omega^{1},\ldots,\omega^{d})\in\{-1,1\}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\{-1,1\}^{n_{d}}=\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
we denote

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}=\mathfrak{D}_{d,n_{d},\omega^{d}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{D}_{1,n_{1},\omega^{1}},

where, for
i=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{i,n_{i},\omega^{i}}=\big(\omega_{n_{i}}^{i}T_{i}^{\alpha}+x_{i}\big)\circ\cdots\circ\big(\omega_{1}^{i}T_{i}^{\alpha}+x_{i}\big).

By convention,
\mathfrak{D}_{i,0,\omega^{i}}=\Id
. The action of
\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}
on
h_{k}^{\alpha}
can be exactly described by means of (
2.2
),
but we will not need this. For our purposes we only need to notice that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha}=\tau_{\omega}^{\alpha}(k)h^{\alpha}_{k-\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\omega^{i}|e_{i}},

(2.3)

where
|\omega^{i}|=\omega_{1}^{i}+\dots+\omega_{n_{i}}^{i}
and for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
the coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle 0\leq\tau_{\omega}^{\alpha}(k)\lesssim(|k|+1)^{|n|/2}\simeq\big(\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}\big)^{|n|/2},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\qquad\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}.

(2.4)

It is worth pointing out that
\tau_{\omega}^{\alpha}(k)
vanishes if and only if there exist
1\leq i\leq d
and
1\leq j\leq n_{i}
such that
k_{i}-(\omega_{1}^{i}+\dots+\omega_{j}^{i})<0
. Finally, observe that if
f
is
\eta
-symmetric then
\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}f
is
(\overline{\eta+n})
-symmetric. In particular,
\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha}
is
(\overline{k+n})
-symmetric.

The Laguerre–Dunkl heat semigroup
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}=\exp(-t\mathfrak{L_{\alpha}})
,
t>0
, has an integral representation in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
, and the integral kernel can be represented as, see, e.g.,
[
33
, equation (3)]
for the one-dimensional case,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}}(xy)^{\eta}G_{t}^{\alpha+\eta}(x,y),\qquad t>0.

(2.5)

This and (
2.1
) leads to the important representation

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\varepsilon,\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}}C_{\alpha+\eta,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1-\zeta^{2}}{2\zeta}\right)^{d+|\alpha|+|\eta|+2|\varepsilon|}(xy)^{\eta+2\varepsilon}\int\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\Pi_{\alpha+\eta+\boldsymbol{1}+\varepsilon}(ds).

Note that the sum in (
2.5
) contains some cancellations since all the terms except one may take negative values when certain coordinates of
x
and
y
have opposite signs. Nevertheless, the kernel
\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
is strictly positive when
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
. On the other hand, it may
be shown, see
[
33
]
, that the kernel takes both positive and negative values if
\alpha
does not satisfy the latter condition. Observe the correlation between positivities of the heat kernel and the multiplicity function.

2.4
Laguerre-symmetrized setting

This framework arises by applying the real variant of the symmetrization procedure proposed in
[
32
]
to the situation of Laguerre function expansions of convolution type, see
[
32
, Example 5.2]
.

The symmetrized system
\{\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
. The
\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}
coincide with
h_{k}^{\alpha}
up to signs (in particular,
\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}
is
\eta
-symmetric if and only if
\eta=\overline{k}
). More precisely,

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}(x)=(-1)^{|\lfloor k/2\rfloor|}h_{k}^{\alpha}(x)=2^{-d/2}x^{\overline{k}}\ell_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}^{\alpha+\overline{k}}(x),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

However, the Laguerre-symmetrized Laplacian
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}
differs slightly from the Laguerre–Dunkl Laplacian. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}f(x)=\mathfrak{L}_{\alpha}f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big[f(x)-f(\sigma_{i}x)\big].

Consequently,
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{|\lfloor(k+\boldsymbol{1})/2\rfloor|}^{\alpha}\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}
. Accordingly, we consider the natural in this context self-adjoint extension of
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}
in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
.

Partial derivatives corresponding to
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{i}f(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(x)+x_{i}f(\sigma_{i}x)+\frac{\alpha_{i}+1/2}{x_{i}}\big[f(x)-f(\sigma_{i}x)\big].

This is motivated by the following two facts. Each
\mathbb{D}_{i}
is formally skew-adjoint in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
and
\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}
decomposes simply as

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\alpha}=\lambda_{0}^{\alpha}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{D}_{i}^{2}.

Observe that there is no ambiguity in defining higher-order derivatives in this context. Note that (see
[
32
, Lemma 4.2]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{i}\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}=(-1)^{k_{i}+1}2\sqrt{\lfloor(k_{i}+1)/2\rfloor}\,\Phi^{\alpha}_{k-(-1)^{k_{i}}e_{i}}

(2.6)

with the convention that
\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}\equiv 0
if
k\notin\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Iteration of (
2.6
) easily leads to the description of the action of
\mathbb{D}^{n}=\mathbb{D}^{n_{d}}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbb{D}^{n_{1}}_{1}
on
\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}
, see
[
32
, Corollary 4.3]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{n}\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}=\rho_{n}(k)\Phi^{\alpha}_{k-(-\boldsymbol{1})^{k}\overline{n}},

(2.7)

where the coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle|\rho_{n}(k)|\lesssim(|k|+1)^{|n|/2}\simeq\big(\lambda_{|\lfloor(k+\boldsymbol{1})/2\rfloor|}^{\alpha}\big)^{|n|/2},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(2.8)

Notice that
\rho_{n}(k)
vanishes if and only if there exist
1\leq i\leq d
such that
k_{i}=0
and
n_{i}>0
. Further, it is easy to see that if
f
is
\eta
-symmetric, then
\mathbb{D}^{n}f
is
(\overline{\eta+n})
-symmetric. In particular,
\mathbb{D}^{n}\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}
is
(\overline{k+n})
-symmetric.

The Laguerre-symmetrized heat semigroup
\mathbb{T}_{t}^{\alpha}=\exp(-t\mathbb{L_{\alpha}})
,
t>0
, is expressed in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
by integrating against the kernel

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\exp\big({-}2|\eta|t\big)(xy)^{\eta}G_{t}^{\alpha+\eta}(x,y),\qquad t>0.

It should be pointed out that the Laguerre-symmetrized and the Laguerre–Dunkl settings reduce essentially to the Laguerre convolution setting after restriction to symmetric (reflection invariant) functions.

3.1
Laguerre–Dunkl setting

The main objects of our study in this subsection are the following operators, which are defined initially in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
.

(L-D.I)

The Laguerre–Dunkl heat semigroup maximal operator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{*}^{\alpha}f=\big\|\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},dt)}.

(L-D.II)

Riesz–Laguerre–Dunkl transforms of order
|n|>0
and type
\omega

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha}f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\big(\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}\big)^{-|n|/\penalty 2}\langle f,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}\,\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
.

(L-D.III)

Multipliers of Laplace and Laplace–Stieltjes transform types

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha}_{\mathfrak{m}}f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\mathfrak{m}(\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha})\langle f,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}h_{k}^{\alpha},

where either
\mathfrak{m}(z)=z\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tz}\psi(t)\,dt
with
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},dt)
or
\mathfrak{m}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}e^{-tz}\,d\nu(t)
with
\nu
being a signed or complex Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with its total variation
|\nu|
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}e^{-t\lambda_{0}^{\alpha}}\,d|\nu|(t)<\infty.

(3.1)

(L-D.IV)

Littlewood–Paley–Stein type mixed
g
-functions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}(f)=\big\|\partial_{t}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},t^{|n|+2m-1}dt)},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
|n|+m>0
, and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
.

(L-D.V)

Mixed Lusin area integrals

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}(f)(x)=\left(\int_{A(x)}t^{|n|+2m-1}\big|\partial_{t}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(z)\big|^{2}\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}(z)\,dt}{V_{\sqrt{t}}^{\alpha}(x)}\right)^{1/\penalty 2},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
|n|+m>0
, and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
. Further, here
A(x)
is the parabolic cone with vertex at
x
,

\displaystyle A(x)=(x,0)+A,\qquad A=\big\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon|z|<\sqrt{t}\big\}

(3.2)

(note that the exact aperture of this cone is meaningless for our developments) and
V_{t}^{\alpha}(x)
is the
\mu_{\alpha}
measure of the cube centered at
x
and of side lengths
2t
. More precisely,

\displaystyle V_{t}^{\alpha}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}V_{t}^{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}),\qquad V_{t}^{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})=\mu_{\alpha_{i}}\big((x_{i}-t,x_{i}+t)\big),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t>0.

(3.3)

The series defining
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha}_{\mathfrak{m}}
converge in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
and produce
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
-bounded operators. This is obvious in case of
\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha}_{\mathfrak{m}}
since the values of
\mathfrak{m}
stay bounded. For
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha}
it follows from (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), via Parseval’s identity. Moreover, the formulas defining
\mathfrak{T}^{\alpha}_{*}f
,
\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}(f)
and
\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}(f)
, understood in a pointwise sense, are valid (the series/integral defining
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
converges and produces a smooth function of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
) for
f\in L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{p}^{\alpha}
,
1\leq p<\infty
; see Proposition
A.1
in Appendix I.

Our main result in the Laguerre–Dunkl situation reads as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
and
W
is a weight in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
invariant under the reflections
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}
. Then the Riesz–Laguerre–Dunkl transforms
(L-D.II)
and the multipliers of Laplace and Laplace–Stieltjes transform types
(L-D.III)
extend to bounded linear operators on
L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{p}^{\alpha}
,
1<p<\infty
, and from
L^{1}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
to weak
L^{1}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{1}^{\alpha}
. Furthermore, the Laguerre–Dunkl heat semigroup maximal operator
(L-D.I)
, the mixed
g
-functions
(L-D.IV)
and the mixed Lusin area integrals
(L-D.V)
are bounded sublinear operators on
L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{p}^{\alpha}
,
1<p<\infty
, and from
L^{1}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
to weak
L^{1}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{1}^{\alpha}
.

Notice that for symmetric weights the condition
W\in A_{p}^{\alpha}
is equivalent to saying that
W^{+}\in A_{p}^{\alpha,+}
(recall that
W^{+}
is the restriction of
W
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
). We now relate in detail Theorem
3.1
to earlier results pertaining to the operators (L-D.I)–(L-D.V).

The Laguerre–Dunkl maximal operator (L-D.I) was considered in
[
30
]
by the first two
authors
and claimed to be bounded on
L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{p}^{\alpha}
,
1<p<\infty
, and from
L^{1}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
to weak
L^{1}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
,
W\in A_{1}^{\alpha}
, provided that
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
; see
[
30
, Theorem 3.1]
. Unfortunately, the argument justifying this in
[
30
, p. 545]
works only for symmetric weights. Thus in
[
30
, Theorem 3.1]
it should be assumed that
W
is reflection invariant. An unweighted version of this result, but with all
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
admitted, is due to Forzani, Sasso and Scotto
[
18
, Theorem 1.9(a)]
. Theorem
3.1
delivers new weighted results in case
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}\setminus[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
. We remark that the part of Theorem
3.1
related to (L-D.I) could be obtained in a more elementary way, essentially by the above mentioned arguments from
[
30
]
, where instead of Soni’s inequality one proceeds with the aid of standard Bessel function asymptotics getting the bound (cf.
[
30
, p. 545, line 9]
)

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)|\lesssim G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad t>0,

for all
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
and then appealing to
[
34
, Corollary 4.2]
rather than
[
28
, Theorem 2.1]
, since the former covers all
\alpha
as above. Nevertheless, the proof of Theorem
3.1
shows, roughly speaking, that
\mathfrak{T}^{\alpha}_{*}
can be “decomposed” into Calderón–Zygmund operators, which potentially provides an approach to investigating more subtle mapping properties of
\mathfrak{T}^{\alpha}_{*}
involving, for instance, Hardy and BMO spaces.

The Riesz–Laguerre–Dunkl transforms of order
1
defined by means of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
(whereas their counterparts based on

were not taken into account) were studied by the first two authors in

]
under the restriction

,

,

, and from

to weak

,

[

[

]
prove this only for reflection invariant weights

. We take this opportunity to point out the corrections needed in

, Section 4]
. The weak association from
[
30
, Proposition 4.1]
should be stated and proved for the operators

defined by the component kernels

Coming back to our bibliographical account, we next mention a paper by Ben Salem and Samaali
[

where, in dimension

and by allowing weights not admitted in
[
31
]
. On the other hand, the aim of
[
33
]
was to obtain sharp unweighted
L^{p}-L^{q}
estimates for the potential operators in dimension
1
, but with all
\alpha>-1
admitted. Again, Theorem
3.1
brings here some new results in the case
p=q>1
by allowing weights. Recently Wróbel
[
46
]
proved a Marcinkiewicz type multivariate spectral multiplier theorem in the Laguerre–Dunkl context. Assuming
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
he infers that multipliers much more general than (L-D.III) are
L^{p}
-bounded,
1<p<\infty
, with no weights admitted; see
[
46
, Theorem 4.1]
. Finally, also recently, imaginary powers of the DHO related to an arbitrary group of reflections were investigated by Amri and Tayari
[
6
]
. They proved that for non-negative multiplicity functions the imaginary powers are bounded on
L^{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, and from
L^{1}
to weak
L^{1}
(with no weights allowed). Clearly, the part of Theorem
3.1
related to (L-D.III) indicates a natural generalization of this result.

Passing to the Littlewood–Paley–Stein type
g
-functions (L-D.IV), essentially we can only invoke the third author’s paper
[
44
]
, where the first order
g
-functions were considered and the special case of Theorem

related to
\mathfrak{g}_{n,m,\omega}^{\alpha}
, determined by the restrictions
|n|+m=1
and
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
, was obtained; see
[
44
, Theorem 2.1]
. The special case
\alpha=-\boldsymbol{1}/2
(trivial multiplicity function) deserves additional comments in this context. It seems that so far only the vertical
g
-functions
\mathfrak{g}_{\boldsymbol{0},m,\omega}^{-\boldsymbol{1}/2}
were investigated and mapping properties as in Theorem
3.1
were obtained, though without requiring the symmetry of weights; see Thangavelu
[
45
, Section 4.1]
and
[
40
, Section 2]
by the second-named author and Torrea. So the part of Theorem
3.1
related to
g
-functions provides a meaningful generalization of existing results even in the framework of the classical harmonic oscillator.

Considering the Lusin area type integrals (L-D.V), again essentially we can only appeal to the third author’s paper
[
44
]
which delivers the special case of the part of Theorem
3.1
pertaining to
\mathfrak{S}_{n,m,\omega}^{\alpha}
given by the restrictions
|n|+m=1
and
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
; see
[
44
, Theorem 2.1]
. No more seems to have been done in the classical harmonic oscillator context (the case
\alpha=-\boldsymbol{1}/2
). We only mention that in this special case
[
44
]
extended the results of Betancor, Molina and Rodríguez-Mesa
[
11
]
, where one-dimensional vertical Lusin area integrals were studied (
n=0
,
m=1
), but in a slightly more general form emerging from involving
L^{r}
norms,
r\geq 2
, rather than
L^{2}
norms in the definition.

This somewhat lengthy account reveals importance and strength of Theorem
3.1
.
We will now proceed with proving this result.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
can be reduced to showing analogous mapping properties for certain,
suitably defined, auxiliary Laguerre-type operators emerging
from those introduced above and related to the smaller space
(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
.
To begin with, for each
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
we consider an auxiliary semigroup acting initially on

L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
and given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f=\big(\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f^{\eta}\big)^{+}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,\overline{k}=\eta}e^{-t\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}}\langle f^{\eta},h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}\,\big(h_{k}^{\alpha}\big)^{+},\qquad t>0;

observe that
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f^{\eta}
is
\eta
-symmetric, which means that the quantity
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f
is well defined. It is not hard to check that these auxiliary semigroups have the integral representations

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(y),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad t>0,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=(xy)^{\eta}G_{t}^{\alpha+\eta}(x,y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad t>0.

(3.4)

Further, these series/integral formulas coincide and provide a good definition of
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
on weighted
L^{p}
spaces for a large class of weights and produce always smooth functions of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, see Proposition
A.1
. Note that choosing
\eta_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
we have
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta_{0},+}=T^{\alpha}_{t}
.

For
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
we denote

\displaystyle\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}=\delta_{d,\eta_{d},n_{d},\omega^{d}}\circ\cdots\circ\delta_{1,\eta_{1},n_{1},\omega^{1}},

where for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
we put

\displaystyle\delta_{i,\eta_{i},n_{i},\omega^{i}}=\big(\omega^{i}_{n_{i}}\partial_{i,\overline{\eta_{i}+n_{i}-1}}+x_{i}\big)\circ\cdots\circ\big(\omega^{i}_{2}\partial_{i,\overline{\eta_{i}+1}}+x_{i}\big)\circ\big(\omega^{i}_{1}\partial_{i,\overline{\eta_{i}}}+x_{i}\big)

(by convention,
\delta_{i,\eta_{i},0,\omega^{i}}=\Id
) and

\displaystyle\partial_{i,\eta_{i}}=\partial_{x_{i}}+\eta_{i}\frac{2\alpha_{i}+1}{x_{i}}.

(3.5)

Notice that the derivatives
\partial_{i,\eta_{i}}
and
\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}
correspond to the action of
T_{i}^{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}
on
\eta
-symmetric functions, respectively. To be more precise, if
f
is
\eta
-symmetric, then
T_{i}^{\alpha}f=\partial_{i,\eta_{i}}f
and
\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}f=\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}f
. Moreover, we may also think that
\partial_{i,\eta_{i}}
and
\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}
act on functions defined on the restricted space
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
.

Now we are ready to introduce the auxiliary Laguerre-type operators, which are defined initially in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
. For each
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
we define the following objects.

(L-t.I)

The Laguerre-type heat semigroup maximal operator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{*}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f=\big\|\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},dt)}.

(L-t.II)

Laguerre-type Riesz transforms of order
|n|>0
and type
\omega

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,\overline{k}=\eta}\big(\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}\big)^{-|n|/\penalty 2}\langle f^{\eta},h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}\,\big(\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha}\big)^{+},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}

and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
.
Observe that if
\overline{k}=\eta
, then

\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha}=\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha}
is
(\overline{k+n})
-symmetric and hence
the quantity
\big(\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}h_{k}^{\alpha}\big)^{+}
is well defined.

(L-t.III)

Multipliers of Laplace and Laplace–Stieltjes transform types

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{\mathfrak{m}}f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,\overline{k}=\eta}\mathfrak{m}(\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha})\langle f^{\eta},h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}\,\big(h_{k}^{\alpha}\big)^{+},

where
\mathfrak{m}
is as in (L-D.III).

(L-t.IV)

Littlewood–Paley–Stein type mixed
g
-functions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)=\big\|\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},t^{|n|+2m-1}dt)},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
|n|+m>0
, and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
.

(L-t.V)

Mixed Lusin area integrals

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)(x)=\left(\int_{A(x)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d+1}_{+}}t^{|n|+2m-1}\big|\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(z)\big|^{2}\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(z)\,dt}{V_{\sqrt{t}}^{\alpha,+}(x)}\right)^{1/\penalty 2},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
|n|+m>0
, and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
. Further,
A(x)
is the parabolic cone with vertex at
x
, see (
3.2
). Here
V_{t}^{\alpha,+}(x)
is the
\mu^{+}_{\alpha}
measure of the cube centered at
x
and of side lengths
2t
, restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
. More precisely,

\displaystyle V_{t}^{\alpha,+}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}V_{t}^{\alpha_{i},+}(x_{i}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad t>0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle V_{t}^{\alpha_{i},+}(x_{i})=\mu_{\alpha_{i}}^{+}\big((x_{i}-t,x_{i}+t)\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}\big),\qquad x_{i}>0,\qquad t>0.

Notice that the Laguerre-type Lusin area integrals can be written as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)(x)=\big\|\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x+z)\sqrt{\Xi_{\alpha}(x,z,t)}\chi_{\{x+z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\}}\big\|_{L^{2}(A,t^{|n|+2m-1}dzdt)},

where the function
\Xi_{\alpha}
is given by

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha}(x,z,t)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(x_{i}+z_{i})^{2\alpha_{i}+1}}{V_{\sqrt{t}}^{\alpha_{i},+}(x_{i})},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad x+z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad t>0.

(3.7)

The series defining
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{\mathfrak{m}}
converge in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
and produce
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
-bounded operators. This follows from the analogous properties of
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha}_{\mathfrak{m}}
, for symmetry reasons. Further, the formulas defining
\mathfrak{T}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{*}f
,
\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)
and
\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)
make sense in a pointwise way for general functions
f
, see Proposition
A.1
.

Arguments similar to those given in
[
29
, p. 6]
and
[
44
, pp. 1522–1524]
allow us to reduce the proof of Theorem
3.1
to showing the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
and
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
. Then the Laguerre-type operators
(L-t.II)
and
(L-t.III)
extend to bounded linear operators on
L^{p}(Ud\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
,
U\in A_{p}^{\alpha,+}
,
1<p<\infty
, and from
L^{1}(Ud\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
to weak
L^{1}(Ud\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
,
U\in A_{1}^{\alpha,+}
.
Furthermore, the sublinear operators
(L-t.I)
,
(L-t.IV)
and
(L-t.V)
are bounded on
L^{p}(Ud\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
,
U\in A_{p}^{\alpha,+}
,
1<p<\infty
, and from
L^{1}(Ud\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
to weak
L^{1}(Ud\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
,
U\in A_{1}^{\alpha,+}
.

To prove Theorem
3.2
we will use the general Calderón–Zygmund theory. In fact, we will show that the Laguerre-type operators (L-t.I)–(L-t.V) are (vector-valued) Calderón–Zygmund operators in the sense of the space of homogeneous type
(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
. Then, in particular, the mapping properties claimed in Theorem
3.2
will follow from the general theory and arguments similar to those mentioned for instance in the proof of
[
42
, Corollary 2.5]
. To treat the Lusin area integrals we shall need a slightly more general definition of the standard kernel, or rather standard estimates, than the one used in the papers
[
29
,
30
,
43
]
. More precisely, we will allow slightly weaker smoothness estimates as indicated below, see for instance
[
14
,
44
]
.

Let
\mathbb{B}
be a Banach space and let
K(x,y)
be a kernel defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\backslash\{(x,y)\colon x=y\}
and taking values in
\mathbb{B}
. We say that
K(x,y)
is a standard kernel in the sense of the space of homogeneous type
(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu^{+}_{\alpha},\|\cdot\|)
if it satisfies the growth estimate

\displaystyle\|K(x,y)\|_{\mathbb{B}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu^{+}_{\alpha}(B(x,\|x-y\|))},\qquad x\neq y,

(3.8)

and the smoothness estimates

\displaystyle\|K(x,y)-K(x^{\prime},y)\|_{\mathbb{B}}\lesssim\left(\frac{\|x-x^{\prime}\|}{\|x-y\|}\right)^{\gamma}\frac{1}{\mu^{+}_{\alpha}(B(x,\|x-y\|))},\qquad\|x-y\|>2\|x-x^{\prime}\|,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|K(x,y)-K(x,y^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{B}}\lesssim\left(\frac{\|y-y^{\prime}\|}{\|x-y\|}\right)^{\gamma}\frac{1}{\mu^{+}_{\alpha}(B(x,\|x-y\|))},\qquad\|x-y\|>2\|y-y^{\prime}\|,\!\!\!

(3.10)

for some fixed
\gamma>0
. Notice that the bounds (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) imply analogous estimates with any
0<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
instead of
\gamma
.
Further, observe that in these formulas, the ball
B(x,\|y-x\|)
can be replaced by
B(y,\|x-y\|)
, in view of the doubling property of
\mu^{+}_{\alpha}
. Furthermore, when
K(x,y)
is scalar-valued (i.e.,
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}
) and
\gamma=1
, the difference bounds (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) are implied by the more convenient gradient estimate

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\!x,y}K(x,y)\|\lesssim\frac{1}{\|x-y\|\mu^{+}_{\alpha}(B(x,\|x-y\|))},\qquad x\neq y.

(3.11)

Similar reduction holds also in the vector-valued situations we consider. Here, however, we will also use (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) with
\gamma<1
and thus it is more convenient to verify the smoothness estimates rather than (
3.11
).

A linear operator
T
assigning to each
f\in L^{2}(d\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
a measurable
\mathbb{B}
-valued function
Tf
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
is a (vector-valued) Calderón–Zygmund operator in the sense of the space
(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu^{+}_{\alpha},\|\cdot\|)
if

(i)

T
is bounded from
L^{2}(d\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
to
L^{2}_{\mathbb{B}}(d\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
,

(ii)

there exists a standard
\mathbb{B}
-valued kernel
K(x,y)
such that

\displaystyle Tf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}K(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu^{+}_{\alpha}(y),\qquad\textrm{a.a.}\,\,\,x\notin\supp f,

for every
f\in L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
,
where
L_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
is the subspace of
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
of bounded measurable functions
with compact supports.

Here integration of
\mathbb{B}
-valued functions is understood in Bochner’s sense, and
L^{2}_{\mathbb{B}}(d\mu^{+}_{\alpha})
is the Bochner–Lebesgue space of all
\mathbb{B}
-valued
\mu^{+}_{\alpha}
-square integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
.

Classical theory of Calderón–Zygmund operators, see, e.g.,
[
15
, Chapter 6]
,

[
17
, Chapter 5]
or
[
19
, Chapter 4]
, is nowadays a standard tool in analysis.
Moreover, it is well known that a large part of this theory remains valid,
with appropriate adjustments, when the underlying space is of homogeneous type and the associated kernels
are vector-valued, see for instance
[
36
]
and
[
37
]
.

The following result, together with the arguments discussed above, implies Theorem
3.2
and thus also Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
and
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
. The Laguerre-type Riesz transforms
(L-t.II)
and the multipliers of Laplace and Laplace–Stieltjes transform types
(L-t.III)
are scalar-valued Calderón–Zygmund operators in the sense of the space
(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
.
Furthermore, the Laguerre-type heat semigroup maximal operator
(L-t.I)
, the mixed
g
-functions
(L-t.IV)
and the mixed Lusin area integrals
(L-t.V)
can be viewed as vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund operators in the sense of
(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
associated with the Banach spaces
\mathbb{B}=C_{0}
,
\mathbb{B}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},t^{|n|+2m-1}dt)
and
\mathbb{B}=L^{2}(A,t^{|n|+2m-1}dzdt)
, respectively.

Proving Theorem
3.3
splits naturally into showing the following three results (Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
, and Theorem
3.6
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
and
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
. Then the Laguerre-type operators from Theorem
3.3
are bounded on
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
.

Proof.

The
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
-boundedness of
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
and
\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{\mathfrak{m}}
is already justified, see the comment preceding the statement of Theorem
3.2
.

Considering
\mathfrak{T}_{*}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
, its
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
-boundedness follows from the
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
-boundedness of
\mathfrak{T}_{*}^{\alpha}
(see the comments following Theorem
3.1
) via restricting its action to
\eta
-symmetric functions. Alternatively, one can argue more directly, similarly as it was done in the Bessel–Dunkl setting
[
14
, p. 953]
. Observe that, see (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{*}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)=x^{\eta}T_{*}^{\alpha+\eta}\big({y^{-\eta}}f\big)(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},

where
T_{*}^{\alpha}f=\|T_{t}^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},dt)}
is the Laguerre heat semigroup maximal operator. Since
T_{*}^{\alpha}
is bounded on
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
(cf.
[
34
, Theorem 4.1]
and also references given there), we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{T}_{*}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f\big\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})}=\big\|T_{*}^{\alpha+\eta}\big({y^{-\eta}}f\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha+\eta}^{+})}\lesssim\big\|{y^{-\eta}}f\big\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha+\eta}^{+})}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})}.

Passing to
\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}
, we first note that the special cases
|n|+m=1
are contained in
[
44
, Proposition 2.4]
. Actually, that result is stated under the assumption
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
, nevertheless the argument given there is valid for all
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
. To show the
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
-boundedness of
\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}
in the general case it is enough to verify the
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
-boundedness of
\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}
, since then the desired property will follow via restricting to
\eta
-symmetric functions. Differentiating the series defining
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f
(this is legitimate, see the proof of Proposition
A.1
) and using (
2.3
) we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\big({-}\lambda^{\alpha}_{|k|/2}\big)^{m}\tau_{\omega}^{\alpha}(k)e^{-t\lambda^{\alpha}_{|k|/2}}\langle f,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}h^{\alpha}_{k-\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\omega^{i}|e_{i}}.

Now, changing the order of integration and then using Parseval’s identity and (
2.4
), we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}(f)\big\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})}^{2}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\big(\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}\big)^{|n|+2m}e^{-2t\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}}\big|\langle f,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}\big|^{2}\right)t^{|n|+2m-1}\,dt.

Changing the order of integration and summation, evaluating the integral and then using once again Parseval’s identity leads directly to the
L^{2}
bound for
\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha}_{n,m,\omega}
.

Finally, the
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
-boundedness of
\mathfrak{S}_{n,m,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
is a consequence of the same property for
\mathfrak{g}_{n,m,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
which is already justified. Indeed, with the aid of Lemma
4.7
(a) below one easily verifies that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)\big\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})}\simeq\big\|\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(f)\big\|_{L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})},\qquad f\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Formal computations and the results from papers
[

,

,

,

]
suggest that the Laguerre-type operators are associated with the following kernels related to appropriate Banach spaces
\mathbb{B}
.

(L-t.I)

The kernel associated with the Laguerre-type heat semigroup maximal operator,

The kernels associated with the Laguerre-type Riesz transforms,

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}
.

(L-t.IIIa)

The kernels associated with the Laplace transform type multipliers,

where
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},dt)
.

(L-t.IIIb)

The kernels associated with the Laplace–Stieltjes transform type multipliers,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{\nu}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\,d\nu(t),\qquad\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C},

where
\nu
is a signed or complex Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with the total variation
|\nu|
satisfying (
3.1
).

(L-t.IV)

The kernels associated with the mixed
g
-functions,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(x,y)=\big\{\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\big\}_{t>0},\qquad\mathbb{B}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},t^{|n|+2m-1}dt),

where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
|n|+m>0
, and
\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{|n|}

(L-t.V)

Proposition 3.5

Assume that
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
and

. Then the Laguerre-type operators

–

Proof.

The reasoning is fairly standard. In the cases of

and
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
we can proceed as in
[
28
,
30
]
since the arguments given there are actually valid for all

provided that the same is true about the standard estimates. Similarly, in the cases of
\mathfrak{K}_{\psi}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
and
\mathfrak{K}_{\nu}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
we can proceed as in
[
43
]
since an analogous remark applies.
Finally, in the cases of
\mathfrak{H}_{n,m,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{n,m,\omega}^{\alpha,\eta,+}
we can proceed as in
[
42
,
44
]
since, again, the same remark is in force. To be precise, in
[
44
]
only some special cases of the present operators are covered but the arguments used there apply also in the more general situation of mixed
g
-functions and mixed Lusin area integrals considered in this paper. For readers’ convenience we now sketch the proof of the association in case of the
g
-functions. Further details and the other cases are left to the reader.

Let
\mathbb{B}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},t^{|n|+2m-1}dt)
. By density arguments it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\Big\langle\big\{\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f\big\}_{t>0},h\Big\rangle_{L_{\mathbb{B}}^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\bigg\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\big\{\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\big\}_{t>0}f(y)\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(y),h\bigg\rangle_{L_{\mathbb{B}}^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})}

for every
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
and
h(x,t)=h_{1}(x)h_{2}(t)
such that
h_{1}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
,
h_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\supp f\cap\supp h_{1}=\varnothing
(notice that the linear span of functions
h
of this form is dense in
L_{\mathbb{B}}^{2}\big((\supp f)^{C},d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}\big)
). We first deal with the left-hand side of the desired identity. Using the
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
-boundedness of

we may change the order of integration and obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{|n|+2m-1}\overline{h_{2}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)\overline{h_{1}(x)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(x)\,dt.

On the other hand, using Fubini’s theorem (its application is legitimate in view of the growth condition for the kernel
\mathfrak{H}^{\alpha,\eta,+}_{n,m,\omega}(x,y)
) and changing the order of integration, we see that the right-hand side in question equals

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{|n|+2m-1}\overline{h_{2}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(y)\overline{h_{1}(x)}\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(x)\,dt.

Therefore, to finish the reasoning, it suffices to verify that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\partial_{t}^{m}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(y),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad t>0,

for each
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
. This, however, can be done by using the dominated convergence theorem and the estimates obtained in Lemma
4.1
below.
∎

The proof of Theorem

(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}^{+},\|\cdot\|)

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f=\big(\mathbb{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f^{\eta}\big)^{+}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,\overline{k}=\eta}e^{-t\lambda_{|\lfloor(k+\boldsymbol{1})/2\rfloor|}^{\alpha}}\langle f^{\eta},\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}\,\big(\Phi_{k}^{\alpha}\big)^{+},\qquad t>0;

observe that

is
\eta

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu_{\alpha}^{+}(y),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\qquad t>0,

\displaystyle\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{\eta,n}=\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{d,\eta_{d},n_{d}}\circ\cdots\circ\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{1,\eta_{1},n_{1}},\qquad\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{i,\eta_{i},n_{i}}=\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{i,\overline{\eta_{i}+n_{i}-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{i,\overline{\eta_{i}+1}}\circ\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{i,\overline{\eta_{i}}},

To begin with we prove the following result that allows us to control various derivatives of the auxiliary heat kernels under consideration.

.

\displaystyle\qquad{}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon,\rho,\xi\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{N}(g\circ f)(x)=\sum\frac{N!}{p_{1}!\cdots p_{N}!}\partial^{p_{1}+\dots+p_{N}}g\circ f(x)\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{1}f(x)}{1!}\right)^{p_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{N}f(x)}{N!}\right)^{p_{N}},

\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)
have a product structure, an application of Leibniz’ rule produces

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{r}\partial_{x}^{l}\partial^{m}_{t}\delta_{\eta,n,\omega,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=\sum_{M\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,|M|=m}c_{M}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{y_{i}}^{r_{i}}\partial_{x_{i}}^{l_{i}}\partial^{M_{i}}_{t}\delta_{i,\eta_{i},n_{i},\omega^{i},x_{i}}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha_{i},\eta_{i},+}(x_{i},y_{i})

and a similar identity related to
\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)
. Hence we see that to prove Lemma
4.1
it suffices to consider the one-dimensional situation. Therefore from now on we assume that
d=1
. We treat each of the two terms in the left-hand side in question separately.

Notice that the Laguerre–Dunkl heat semigroup
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}=\exp(-t\mathfrak{L_{\alpha}})
,
t>0
, satisfies the heat equation

\partial_{t}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)=-\mathfrak{L_{\alpha}}\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
. Thus the Laguerre–Dunkl heat kernel
\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
also satisfies this equation with respect to
x
, i.e., we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=(T_{x}^{\alpha})^{2}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)-x^{2}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y),

as can be easily checked by using the identity
\mathfrak{L_{\alpha}}=-(T^{\alpha})^{2}+x^{2}
. Denote for brevity
\partial_{\eta,x}=\partial_{1,\eta,x}
(see (
3.5
)) and observe that for each
\eta\in\{0,1\}
the functions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\partial_{\overline{\eta+1},x}\partial_{\eta,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)-x^{2}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)

are
\eta
-symmetric with respect to
x
, and with the aid of decomposition (
2.5
) they are both
\eta
-symmetric components of
2\partial_{t}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
. By the uniqueness of
\eta
-symmetric component we get

\displaystyle\partial_{\overline{\eta+1},x}\partial_{\eta,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=\partial_{t}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)+x^{2}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y).

Using this identity and proceeding inductively we infer that

\displaystyle\delta_{\eta,n,\omega,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=0,1\\
2p+q\leq n\end{subarray}}P_{n,p,q,\eta,\omega}(x)\partial_{t}^{p}\partial^{q}_{\eta,x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y),

where
P_{n,p,q,\eta,\omega}
is a
(\overline{n-q})
-symmetric polynomial of degree at most
n-2p-q
; here and later on we use the natural convention that
\partial^{q}_{\eta,x}=\id
for
q=0
. Combining this with Leibniz’ rule and
[
34
, Lemma A.3(d)]
we see that our task reduces to showing the estimate

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{y}^{R}\partial_{x}^{L}\partial_{t}^{M}\partial_{\eta,x}^{q}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\big|\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon,\rho,\xi=0,1\\
a,b=0,1,2\end{subarray}}x^{\eta-\rho\eta+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon}y^{\eta-\xi\eta+2\varepsilon-b\varepsilon}\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{1+\alpha+\eta+2\varepsilon}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\big|\partial_{y}^{R}\partial_{x}^{L}\partial_{t}^{M}\partial_{\eta,x}^{q}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\big|\lesssim}{}\times\zeta^{-1-\alpha-\eta-2\varepsilon-M-(R+L+q)/2+(\rho\eta+a\varepsilon+\xi\eta+b\varepsilon)/2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\big| \partial_y^R \partial_x^L \partial_t^M \partial_{\eta,x}^q \mathfrak{G}_t^{\alpha,\eta,+} (x,y) \big|\lesssim}{}\times\int\big(\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big)^{3/4}\Pi_{\alpha+\eta+1+\varepsilon}(ds),

uniformly in
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
s\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
; here
R,L,M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\eta,q\in\{0,1\}
are fixed and
\zeta=\zeta(t)=\tanh t
.
Combining (
3.4
) with the representation formula (
2.1
) of
G_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
our task can be further reduced to proving that

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{y}^{R}\partial_{x}^{L}\partial_{t}^{M}\big[\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}\zeta^{W_{2}}x^{\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon}y^{\eta_{2}+2\varepsilon}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big]\big|

\displaystyle\qquad{}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\xi=0,1\\
a,b=0,1,2\end{subarray}}x^{\eta_{1}-\rho\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon}y^{\eta_{2}-\xi\eta_{2}+2\varepsilon-b\varepsilon}\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}\times\zeta^{W_{2}-M-(R+L)/2+(\rho\eta_{1}+a\varepsilon+\xi\eta_{2}+b\varepsilon)/2}\big(\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big)^{3/4},

uniformly in
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
s\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
; here
R,L,M\in\mathbb{N}
,
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
are fixed, and
\zeta=\zeta(t)=\tanh t
. We now focus on proving (
4.3
).

By Leibniz’ rule for every
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta_{1},\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{L}\big[x^{\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon}f\big]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho=0,1\\
a=0,1,2\end{subarray}}c_{L,\eta_{1},\varepsilon,\rho,a}\,\chi_{\{L\geq\rho\eta_{1}+a\varepsilon\}}x^{\eta_{1}-\rho\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon}\partial_{x}^{L-\rho\eta_{1}-a\varepsilon}f,

where
c_{L,\eta_{1},\varepsilon,\rho,a}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants. This leads to the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{R}\partial_{x}^{L}\big[x^{\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon}y^{\eta_{2}+2\varepsilon}f\big]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\xi=0,1\\
a,b=0,1,2\end{subarray}}c_{L,R,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon,\rho,\xi,a,b}\chi_{\{L\geq\rho\eta_{1}+a\varepsilon,\,R\geq\xi\eta_{2}+b\varepsilon\}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_y^R \partial_x^L \big[ x^{\eta_1 + 2 \varepsilon} y^{\eta_2 + 2\varepsilon} f \big]=}{}\times x^{\eta_{1}-\rho\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon}y^{\eta_{2}-\xi\eta_{2}+2\varepsilon-b\varepsilon}\partial_{y}^{R-\xi\eta_{2}-b\varepsilon}\partial_{x}^{L-\rho\eta_{1}-a\varepsilon}f,

where
c_{L,R,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon,\rho,\xi,a,b}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants; here
R,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
are fixed. Further, denoting

\displaystyle F=F(\zeta,q_{\pm})=\log\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})=-\frac{1}{4\zeta}q_{+}-\frac{\zeta}{4}q_{-},

and using Faà di Bruno’s formula (
4.1
) (notice that
\partial_{x}^{3}F=\partial_{x}^{4}F=\dots=0
) we arrive at

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{L}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})=\sum_{L_{1}+2L_{2}=L}c_{L_{1},L_{2}}(\partial_{x}F)^{L_{1}}(\partial_{x}^{2}F)^{L_{2}}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm}),

where
c_{L_{1},L_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants; observe that this formula works also for
L=0
. By Leibniz’ rule and another application of (
4.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{R}\partial_{x}^{L}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+2p_{3}+2p_{4}=L+R}c_{L,R,p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}}(\partial_{x}F)^{p_{1}}(\partial_{y}F)^{p_{2}}(\partial_{x}^{2}F)^{p_{3}}(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F)^{p_{4}}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm}),

where
c_{L,R,p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants, possibly zero; here we used the identities
\partial_{x}^{2}F=\partial_{y}^{2}F
and
\partial_{y}^{R}[(\partial_{x}F)^{L_{1}}]=\chi_{\{L_{1}\geq R\}}c_{L_{1},R}(\partial_{x}F)^{L_{1}-R}(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F)^{R}
for some constants
c_{L_{1},R}\in\mathbb{R}
.
These facts altogether give the identity

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{R}\partial_{x}^{L}\partial_{t}^{M}\big[\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}\zeta^{W_{2}}x^{\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon}y^{\eta_{2}+2\varepsilon}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big]

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\xi=0,1\\
a,b=0,1,2\end{subarray}}\chi_{\{L\geq\rho\eta_{1}+a\varepsilon,\,R\geq\xi\eta_{2}+b\varepsilon\}}\bigg(x^{\eta_{1}-\rho\eta_{1}+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon}y^{\eta_{2}-\xi\eta_{2}+2\varepsilon-b\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}\times\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+2p_{3}+2p_{4}=L+R-\rho\eta_{1}-a\varepsilon-\xi\eta_{2}-b\varepsilon}c_{L,R,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon,\rho,\xi,a,b,p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}\times\partial_{t}^{M}\big[\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}\zeta^{W_{2}}(\partial_{x}F)^{p_{1}}(\partial_{y}F)^{p_{2}}(\partial_{x}^{2}F)^{p_{3}}(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F)^{p_{4}}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big]\bigg),

where
c_{L,R,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon,\rho,\xi,a,b,p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants. Hence to prove (
4.3
) it is enough to check that

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{t}^{M}\big[\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}\zeta^{W_{2}}(\partial_{x}F)^{p_{1}}(\partial_{y}F)^{p_{2}}(\partial_{x}^{2}F)^{p_{3}}(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F)^{p_{4}}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big]\big|

\displaystyle\qquad{}\lesssim\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}\zeta^{W_{2}-M-p_{1}/2-p_{2}/2-p_{3}-p_{4}}\big(\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big)^{3/4},

(4.4)

uniformly in
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
s\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
; here
M,p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}\in\mathbb{N}
and
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are fixed, and
\zeta=\zeta(t)=\tanh t
. We now justify this estimate.

Using
[
34
, equation (A.3)]
and
[
34
, Lemma A.3(a)]
we see that for
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
fixed we have

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{\zeta}^{M}\big[\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{S_{1}}\zeta^{S_{2}}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big]\big|\lesssim\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{S_{1}-M}\zeta^{S_{2}-M}\big(\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big)^{7/8},

(4.5)

uniformly in
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
s\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Further, since

\displaystyle\partial_{x}F=-\frac{1}{2\zeta}\Psi_{+}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\Psi_{-},\qquad

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}F=-\frac{1}{2\zeta}-\frac{\zeta}{2},\qquad

\displaystyle\Psi_{\pm}=\Psi_{\pm}(x,y,s)=x\pm ys,

\displaystyle\partial_{y}F=-\frac{1}{2\zeta}\Phi_{+}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\Phi_{-},\qquad

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F=s\left({-}\frac{1}{2\zeta}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\right),\qquad

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm}=\Phi_{\pm}(x,y,s)=y\pm xs,

by Newton’s formula we get

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}F)^{p_{1}}(\partial_{y}F)^{p_{2}}(\partial_{x}^{2}F)^{p_{3}}(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F)^{p_{4}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=s^{p_{4}}\sum_{j=-p_{3}-p_{4}}^{p_{3}+p_{4}}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{p_{1}}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{p_{2}}c_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{4},j,k_{1},k_{2}}\zeta^{j}\left(\frac{\Psi_{+}}{\zeta}\right)^{k_{1}}(\zeta\Psi_{-})^{p_{1}-k_{1}}\left(\frac{\Phi_{+}}{\zeta}\right)^{k_{2}}(\zeta\Phi_{-})^{p_{2}-k_{2}}.

Consequently, using (
4.5
) (with
S_{1}=W_{1}
,
S_{2}=W_{2}+j+p_{1}-2k_{1}+p_{2}-2k_{2}
) and
[
34
, Lemma A.3(b) and (c)]
we arrive at

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{\zeta}^{M}\big[\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}}\zeta^{W_{2}}(\partial_{x}F)^{p_{1}}(\partial_{y}F)^{p_{2}}(\partial_{x}^{2}F)^{p_{3}}(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}F)^{p_{4}}\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big]\big|

\displaystyle\qquad{}\lesssim\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{W_{1}-M}\zeta^{W_{2}-M-p_{1}/2-p_{2}/2-p_{3}-p_{4}}\big(\e(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big)^{3/4},

uniformly in
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
s\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
; here
M,p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}\in\mathbb{N}
and
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are fixed. Finally, an application of Faà di Bruno’s formula (
4.1
) and the identity
[
34
, equation (A.2)]
leads us to (
4.4
), and the desired estimate connected with
\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)
follows.

It remains to deal with the estimate in question for the kernel
\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)
. Proceeding in an analogous way as at the beginning of the proof we obtain the formula
\partial_{t}\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)=(\mathbb{D}_{x}^{2}-\lambda_{0}^{\alpha})\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)
and, consequently, for symmetry reasons,

\displaystyle\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{\eta,2,x}\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{0}^{\alpha})\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y).

Iterating the latter identity we infer that

\displaystyle\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{\eta,n,x}\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{0}^{\alpha})^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{\eta,\overline{n},x}\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y).

Since
\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{\eta,\overline{n},x}=\partial_{\eta,x}^{\overline{n}}+\overline{n}(-1)^{\eta}x
and
\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=e^{-2\eta t}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)
,
it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\delta^{\textrm{sym}}_{\eta,n,x}\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)=e^{-2\eta t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=0,1\\
2p+q\leq n\end{subarray}}P_{n,p,q,\eta}(x)\partial_{t}^{p}\partial_{\eta,x}^{q}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y),

where
P_{n,p,q,\eta}
is a
(\overline{n-q})
-symmetric polynomial of degree at most
n-2p-q
. Now applying Leibniz’ rule (to the variables
x
and
t
) and then using sequently (
4.2
) and
[
34
, Lemma A.3(d)]
we obtain the required bound for the quantity related to
\mathbb{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)
.

This finishes the whole reasoning justifying Lemma
4.1
.
∎

The next lemma is an essence of the method of proving standard estimates presented in this paper. It provides a link from the estimates obtained in Lemma

(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}^{+},\|\cdot\|)

[

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)^{d}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
W\in\mathbb{R}
,
C>0
,
\varepsilon,\eta,\rho,\xi\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and
a,b\in\{0,1,2\}^{d}
are fixed. Further, let
\tau\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
be such that
\tau\leq\eta-\rho\eta+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon
and let
D
be a fixed constant satisfying
D<d+|\alpha|
. Given
u\geq 0
, we consider the function
\Upsilon_{u}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\times(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}

defined by

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{u}(x,y,\zeta(t))=x^{\eta-\rho\eta+2\varepsilon-a\varepsilon-\tau}y^{\eta-\xi\eta+2\varepsilon-b\varepsilon}\big(\loo(\zeta)\big)^{|\tau|/2}\big(1-\zeta^{2}\big)^{d+|\alpha|+|\eta|+2|\varepsilon|-D}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Upsilon_{u}(x,y,\zeta(t)) =}{}\times\zeta^{-d-|\alpha|-|\eta|-2|\varepsilon|+(|\rho\eta|+|a\varepsilon|+|\xi\eta|+|b\varepsilon|)/\penalty 2-W/\penalty p-u/\penalty 2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Upsilon_{u}(x,y,\zeta(t)) =}{}\times\int\big(\ee(\zeta,q_{\pm})\big)^{C}\Pi_{\alpha+\eta+\boldsymbol{1}+\varepsilon}(ds),

where
W/\penalty p=0

satisfies the integral estimate

\displaystyle\big\|\Upsilon_{u}\big(x,y,\zeta(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+},t^{W-1}dt)}\lesssim\frac{1}{||x-y||^{u}}\frac{1}{\mu^{+}_{\alpha}(B(x,||y-x||))},

uniformly in
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
,

.

Remark 4.3

Proof of Theorem

We will treat each of the kernels separately.

The case of

(applied with

To verify the smoothness estimates, for symmetry reasons, it suffices to show only (

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)-\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x^{\prime},y)|\leq\|x-x^{\prime}\|\left\|\nabla_{\!x}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)\big|_{x=\theta}\right\|,

\displaystyle\theta\leq x\vee x^{\prime},\qquad\|x-\theta\|\leq\|x-x^{\prime}\|,\qquad\|x-x\vee x^{\prime}\|\leq\|x-x^{\prime}\|,

(4.6)

and Lemma
4.4
twice (first with

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x,y)-\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}(x^{\prime},y)|

\displaystyle\qquad{}\lesssim\|x-x^{\prime}\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon,\rho,\xi\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\

Appendix I

In this short section we shall prove the following useful result.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. If
W\in A^{\alpha}_{p}
and
f\in L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
, then the series/integrals defining
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
and
\mathbb{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
converge for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t>0
and produce smooth functions of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Similarly, if
U\in A^{\alpha,+}_{p}
and
f\in L^{p}(Ud\mu_{\alpha}^{+})
, then the series/integrals defining
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)
and
\mathbb{T}_{t}^{\alpha,\eta,+}f(x)
,

\eta\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, converge for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and
t>0
and produce smooth functions of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Recall that the definition of
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}g
for
g\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}g(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}e^{-t\lambda_{|k|/2}^{\alpha}}\langle g,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}h_{k}^{\alpha}(x),

(A.1)

(convergence in
L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
) and this easily leads to the integral representation

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\mathfrak{G}_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)g(y)\,d\mu_{\alpha}(y),\qquad g\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad t>0.

(A.2)

To prove the claim for
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
we use the following two auxiliary results. Firstly, given
1\leq p<\infty
,
W\in A^{\alpha}_{p}
and
f\in L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
, the coefficients
\langle f,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, exist and satisfy

\displaystyle|\langle f,h_{k}^{\alpha}\rangle_{d\mu_{\alpha}}|\lesssim(|k|+1)^{c_{d,\alpha,p,W}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})},

(A.3)

uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
f\in L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
. Secondly,

\displaystyle|h_{k}^{\alpha}(x)|\lesssim(|k|+1)^{c_{d,\alpha,p}},

(A.4)

uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, following the argument from
[
28
, pp. 647–648]
one checks that after replacing
g\in L^{2}(d\mu_{\alpha})
by
f\in L^{p}(Wd\mu_{\alpha})
in the right-hand side of (
A.1
) the series converges absolutely for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t>0
, and thus defines
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
. Moreover, with this definition of
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
the integral representation (
A.2
) remains valid for
f
replacing
g
(in particular, the relevant integral converges for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t>0
) and
\mathfrak{T}_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)
is a
C^{\infty}
function of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Coming back to (
A.3
) and (
A.4
), these are simple consequences of
[
44
, equations (2.3) and (2.4)]
. The assumption
\alpha\in[-1/2,\infty)^{d}
which was imposed in
[
44
]
is not essential for
[
