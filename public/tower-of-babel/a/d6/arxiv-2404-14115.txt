Pricing of European Calls with the Quantum Fourier Transform
Tom Ewen

Abstract
The accurate valuation of financial derivatives plays a pivotal role in the finance industry. Although closed formulas for pricing are available for certain models and option types, exemplified by the European Call and Put options in the Black-Scholes Model, the use of either more complex models or more sophisticated options precludes the existence of such formulas, thereby requiring alternative approaches. The Monte Carlo simulation, an alternative approach effective in nearly all scenarios, has already been challenged by quantum computing techniques that leverage Amplitude Estimation. Despite its theoretical promise, this approach currently faces limitations due to the constraints of hardware in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era. In this study, we introduce and analyze a quantum algorithm for pricing European call options across a broad spectrum of asset models. This method transforms a classical approach, which utilizes the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), into a quantum algorithm, leveraging the efficiency of the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT). Furthermore, we compare this novel algorithm with existing quantum algorithms for option pricing.



I
Introduction

In the finance sector the pricing of derivatives is an important task.
Derivatives are contracts that promise the buyer a payoff depending on an underlying asset, a simple example is the European Call which pays the buyer the difference between a predefined strike,
K
, and the value of the underlying at its maturity
S_{T}
, if that difference is positive, i.e.,
\max\{S_{T}-K,0\}
.
Since the underlying is modeled as a stochastic process, the payoff of this derivative is also stochastic.
Pricing this derivative involves finding the expectation of the payoff under the risk-free measure.
Speeding up this task would be a huge impact quantum computers could bring.
In the literature there are presented different approaches on how to achieve this using a Quantum Computer
[
1
,
2
]
.
Both approaches have in common that they mimic classical Monte-Carlo methods, where the potential for improvement comes from the Amplitude Estimation algorithm
[
3
]
.
In this work we will present a different approach that is based on numerically approximating the option price and calculating this approximation via the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT).
Here the potential speedup stems from the efficiency of the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT).
This classical part of this method was introduced to price European calls for models where the characteristic function of the log-price is known
[
4
]
.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows, we will introduce the numerical approximation of the option price in
Section

II
, and we will see how to perform this approximation on a quantum computer in
Section

III
.
In
Section

IV
we will compare the result with other approaches from the literature and give an intuition on error rates in
Section

V
.
Finally, we conclude with a short summary and a brief outlook in
Section

VI
.

II
Numerical Approximation

This section follows the ideas of
[
4
]
to find a numerical approximation of the price of a European Call that can be calculated with the DFT.
The goal is to calculate the fair price of a European call with strike
K\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and maturity
T\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
which is based on an underlying
\left(S_{t}\right)_{0\leq t\leq T}
.
The fair price is then given as the discounted expected payout under the risk-free measure

C=\mathrm{e}^{-rT}\mathbb{E}[\max\left\{S_{T}-K,0\right\}].

(1)

See for example
[
5
]
for a more detailed argumentation.

To be able to calculate this expectation we need to assume a model for the underlying.
For this approach we will assume that the characteristic function of the logarithm of the underlying’s price is known.
This is the case in many models, the Black-Scholes Model or the Heston Model being two prominent examples or the Variance Gamma model
[
6
]
being a more complex example.

We will calculate the expectation in
Equation

1
by expressing it as an integral and approximating this integral numerically.
Note that we replace the underlying’s price and the strike with their logarithms and write a lowercase letter instead of a capital one to denote the logarithm.
The Option Price is then given as

C=\mathrm{e}^{-rT}\int_{k}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{s}-\mathrm{e}^{k})f_{s_{T}}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(2)

The maximum from
Equation

1
is taken care of by starting the integral only from
k
instead of
-\infty
.
If the density
f_{s_{T}}
was known analytically, we could stop here, discretize the integral and approximate, but this is not the case for all models we are interested in.

II-A

Analytic transformation

First we will transform
Equation

2
into an expression that no longer depends on the density, but the characteristic function instead.
As a first step we will express the price in terms of the log-strike and introduce a dampening factor
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
to make the expression square integrable with respect to
k
.

\widetilde{C}(k)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\alpha k}C(k)\overset{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:option_price_integral}}{=}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha k}\mathrm{e}^{-rT}\int_{k}^{\infty}(\mathrm{e}^{s}-\mathrm{e}^{k})f_{s_{T}}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(3)

Now we take the Fourier Transform (FT) of
\widetilde{C}(k)

\psi(v)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}vk}\widetilde{C}(k)\mathrm{d}k.

(4)

Next, we plug in
Equation

3
and applying Fubini’s Theorem, which we are allowed to do, since the dampening factor guarantees the square integrability.
This leads us to

\psi(v)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-rT}f_{s_{T}}(s)\int_{-\infty}^{s}\left(\mathrm{e}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{k(\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}-\mathrm{e}^{k(1+\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}\right)\mathrm{d}k\mathrm{d}s.

The change of order of the integration allows us to explicitly calculate the inner integral

\int_{-\infty}^{s}\left(\mathrm{e}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{k(\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}-\mathrm{e}^{k(1+\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{s(\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}}{\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s(1+\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}}{1+\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v}.

Together with the definition of the characteristic function

\Phi(v)\coloneq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}vs}f_{s_{T}}(s)\mathrm{d}s

we can simplify
eq.

4
to

\psi(v)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-rT}\Phi\left(\frac{1+\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}}\right)}{(\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)(1+\alpha+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}v)}.

As we require for this approach the characteristic function to be known analytically, we know
\psi(v)
analytically as well.

In a last step we apply the inverse FT and reverse the dampening to get an expression for the Option Price

C(k)=\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha k}\widetilde{C}(k)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha k}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}vk}\psi(v)\mathrm{d}v.

(5)

II-B

Numerical Approximation

Now that we have an expression for the Price the next step is actually calculating it.
As
eq.

5
contains an improper integral, that we cannot calculate directly, we need to find an approximation, i.e., compute the price numerically.
At this point this work deviates from
[
4
]
to make the approximation more suitable for a quantum computer.
We will have to make two approximations in the process of numerically calculating
C(k)
.
Firstly we have to cut off the integral at some finite point, and secondly we will introduce a discretization error.
The second source of approximation error can be mitigated by employing more involved discretization schemes, like the Simpson Rule, but this is not the focus of this work and when looking at the results later on, not really necessary.

We fix a step size
\Delta_{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and the number of discretization steps
N\in\mathbb{N}
for the positive and negative part of the integration range each and discretize
Equation

5
as

C(k)\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha k}}{2\pi}\sum_{j=-N}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\Delta_{v}jk}\psi_{T}(\Delta_{v}j)\Delta_{v}.

Now we specify a discretization for the log-strike
k_{l}\coloneqq k_{0}+\Delta_{k}l
for
0\leq l<2N
while we fix the relation between the discretizations of
v
and
k
to
\Delta_{k}\Delta_{v}=\frac{\pi}{N}
.
With an additional shift of the index this will give us a discretization that fits into the structure needed for the DFT

\begin{split}C(k_{l})&\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(k_{0}+\Delta_{k}l)}}{2\pi}\Delta_{v}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\pi l}\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\frac{2\pi}{2N}jl}x_{j}\\
x_{j}&=\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\Delta_{v}jk_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\Delta_{v}Nk_{0}}\psi(\Delta_{v}(j-N)).\end{split}

(6)

As seen in
Figures

1
and
2
when choosing a good value for the dampening factor
\alpha
, even for moderate number of discretization steps the approximation is very accurate.

,

.

\Delta_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}

.
We see that the speed of convergence heavily depends on the choice of
alpha
.

III
Pricing with the Quantum Fourier Transform

In this section we will see the necessary steps to evaluate the approximation from
Section

II
on a quantum computer.
The idea is to calculate the sum from
Equation

6
with the QFT and thus achieving a benefit compared to the classical approach.

The QFT is a unitary operation
QFT_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
, operating on an
n
-qubit system, that transforms a given state
\Ket{x}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}x_{j}\Ket{j}
into
\Ket{y}=\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}y_{l}\Ket{l}
where

y_{l}=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{n}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}x_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\frac{2\pi}{2^{n}}jl}.

The inverse QFT is a unitary operation
QFT_{n}^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
, which maps a state
\Ket{x}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}x_{j}\Ket{j}
to
\Ket{y}=\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}y_{l}\Ket{l}
where

y_{l}=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{n}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}x_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\frac{2\pi}{2^{n}}lj}.

Note that the conventions for the QFT differ slightly from the classical version, for once the QFT corresponds to the inverse DFT.
Further the factor before the sum is in the QFT setting distributed between the two directions, whereas for the DFT only one direction of the transform has that factor.

Before we can apply the QFT, or to be precise the inverse QFT, we need to prepare the correct state.
As an
n
-qubit system allows for
2^{n}
amplitudes we will limit ourselves to discretizations
N=2^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The positive and negative part of the integral will be discretized individually, so in total we need
n+1
qubits.
We further have to make sure that the state we want to prepare is actually a valid state.
Thus, we normalize first

\widetilde{x}_{j}=\frac{x_{j}}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\left\lvert x_{l}\right\rvert^{2}}}.

We will call the unitary that prepares this state E

E\Ket{0}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\widetilde{x}_{j}\Ket{j}.

On that state we apply the inverse QFT

\Ket{y}=QFT^{-1}E\Ket{0}

where

\displaystyle y_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\frac{2\pi}{2^{n+1}}lj}\widetilde{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\left\lvert x_{j}\right\rvert^{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\frac{2\pi}{2^{n+1}}lj}x_{j}.

Now we can plug this into
Equation

6
to get

C(k_{l})\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(k_{0}+\Delta_{k}l)}}{2\pi}\Delta_{v}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\pi l}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\left\lvert x_{j}\right\rvert^{2}}2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}y_{l}.

(7)

Unfortunately the values
y_{l}
we need are encoded in the amplitude of a quantum state, and we do not have direct access to it.
This could be achieved by state tomography, but that is a very inefficient procedure, taking exponentially many measurements in the number of qubits
[
7
]
.
Alternatively we can retrieve the absolute values
\lvert y_{l}\rvert
by either Amplitude Estimation or by simply executing many shots.
While the latter is less efficient it estimates all amplitudes in one go and not only the one of a single state.

Knowing that the true price is real and positive and looking at
Equation

7
we see that
\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\pi l}y_{l}
needs to be positive and real if the approximation would be without error.
But the approximation is not perfect, thus we introduce another approximation error replacing
\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{i}}\pi l}y_{l}
by the square root of the observed probability of state
\Ket{l}
.
We will call the observed probabilities
p_{l}
.
The final approximation is given as

C(k_{l})\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(k_{0}+\Delta_{k}l)}}{2\pi}\Delta_{v}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\left\lvert x_{j}\right\rvert^{2}}2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\sqrt{p_{l}}.

(8)

The quality of the approximation strongly depends on how well the amplitudes are estimated, in
Figure

3
we can see that this again depends strongly the number of shots used for the approximation, even if the quantum computer is noise free, depicted here by the use of a simulator.

where
\alpha=2.5
,

and
k_{0}=\ln 100-\frac{N\Delta_{k}}{2}
. We see that even for an error free device the error introduced by estimating the probabilities from finite many realizations introduces an error.

IV
Comparison

In this section we will see how this novel approach compares to existing quantum algorithms for pricing options.
The approaches introduced in
[
1
,
2
]
all have in common, that they start with loading a distribution into a quantum register.
On this distribution they apply a quantum algorithm to encode the options payoff in the amplitude of one target qubit.
This amplitude can then be retrieved either by repeated measurement or amplitude estimation.

For the approach introduced in
[
1
]
we will rely on the implementation done by the qiskit-finance
[
8
]
python library, while for our novel QFT approach and the Conditional Parameterized Quantum Circuits (CPQC)
[
2
]
we will rely on our own implementation.

We will not look at results on actual hardware, as we are interested in comparing the algorithms in general and not how well they cope with current hardware restrictions.

A common way to analyze quantum algorithms is to look at the scaling of the circuit depth or the number of two qubit gates for an increasing number of qubits.
We see in
Figure

4
that this novel approach does not compare well in this metric.

That only tells us, that this new approach scales worse for an increasing number of qubits.
But in our setting increasing the number of qubits is solely done to improve the approximation, we don’t need a specific number of qubits to be able to calculate real world examples.
This means we should look at the relation between the achieved approximation quality and the quantum resources needed.
As seen in

This is possible, as the QFT based approach needs fewer qubits for getting good approximations.

V
Error Propagation

Until now we only looked at idealized error free quantum devices.
In this section we will get an Idea how good a quantum computer would need to perform to get practical results.
To do so we compare
Equation

7
and
Equation

8
the first one being the theoretical approximation making use of the amplitude and the latter being the observable approximation making use of the observed probability.
Let us call the error the quantum computer does
\epsilon_{l}=\lvert y_{l}-\sqrt{p_{l}}\rvert
then the absolute difference between the theoretical approximation and the observed approximation is

\mathcal{E}_{l}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(k_{0}+\Delta_{k}l)}}{2\pi}\Delta_{v}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-1}\left\lvert x_{j}\right\rvert^{2}}2^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\epsilon_{l}.

(9)

This expression depends on the strike for which we want to calculate the option price, but also on the parameter
\alpha
, which we can choose freely.
Note that the
x_{j}
also depend on
\alpha
, and thus it is not straight forward, how exactly the
\mathcal{E}_{l}
depend on
\alpha
.

VI
Conclusion

We have seen a novel approach in using quantum computers for pricing Options that makes use of the QFT.
This new approach performs better than previously introduced algorithms for a wide class of models, but only for European Calls and Puts.
The QFT promises a significant speedup compared to the classical FFT, which is unfortunately in parts counteracted by the expensive state preparation.

As no efficient procedure is currently known for preparing general states, further research could investigate whether there is an inherent structure in the states, needed for this novel approach, that permits more efficient preparation.
