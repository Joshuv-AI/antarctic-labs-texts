The conjecture can be stated in terms of Christoffel words
[
Reu19
]
.
Christoffel words are words over the alphabet
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
also satisfying
a binary tree structure:
\mathtt{0}
,
\mathtt{1}
and
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}
are Christoffel words and if

u,v,uv\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
are Christoffel words then
uuv
and
uvv
are Christoffel
words
[
BLRS09
]
,
see Figure
1
.
Note that these are usually named
lower
Christoffel words.
It is known that each Markoff number can be expressed in terms of a Christoffel
word.
More precisely,
let
\mu
be the monoid homomorphism
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by

\mu(\mathtt{0})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&1\\
1&1\end{array}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu(\mathtt{1})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}5&2\\
2&1\end{array}\right).

Each Markoff number is equal to
\mu(w)_{12}
for some Christoffel word
w

(see
[
Reu09
]
), that is, the element above the diagonal in the matrix
\mu(w)
.
Moreover, positive primitive elements of the free group

F_{2}
on two generators are in bijection with Markoff triples
[
Bom07
]
.
Both results are equivalent since primitive elements of the free group
F_{2}

are in bijection with Christoffel words
[
KR07
]
.

In other words, the map
w\mapsto\mu(w)_{12}
from Christoffel words to Markoff
numbers is surjective.
For example, the Markoff number 194 is associated with the Christoffel word
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}

as it is the entry at position
(1,2)
in the matrix

\mu(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1})=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}463&194\\
284&119\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Whether this map provides a bijection between Christoffel
words and Markoff numbers is equivalent to the uniqueness conjecture.
Indeed, the uniqueness conjecture can be expressed in terms of the
injectivity of the map
w\mapsto\mu(w)_{12}

[
Reu19
, §3.3]
.

Markoff Injectivity Conjecture
.

The map
w\mapsto\mu(w)_{12}
is injective on the set of Christoffel words.

The map
w\mapsto\mu(w)_{12}
is defined over the monoid
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
not
only on Christoffel words.
On this extended domain, Lapointe and Reutenauer showed that

w\mapsto\mu(w)_{12}
is strictly increasing (thus injective) over the language of
factors appearing in a Christoffel word

[
Lap20
,
LR21
]
, thus also for all Christoffel words on an infinite path in the binary tree of Christoffel words.
The map is not injective on
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
as for example,

\mu(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}=75=\mu(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1})_{12}
.
But Lapointe and Reutenauer conjectured that it is injective on the language of
all factors of Christoffel words
[
LR21
, Conjecture 2]
.

Markoff numbers and the
Markoff injectivity conjecture can be parametrized by introducing a parameter
q

Recall that the
q
-analog of a nonnegative integer

[n]_{q}=1+q+\dots+q^{n-1}=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}.

Recently,
the
q
-analog

[

]

It also defines naturally

[
MGO19
]

The approach is based on the following

R=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and

1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)

of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/\pm\mathrm{Id}

R_{q}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q&1\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad S_{q}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-q^{-1}\\
1&0\end{array}\right).

Since
\mu(\mathtt{0})=R^{2}SR

and
\mu(\mathtt{1})=R^{3}SR^{2}SR

the

and

[

]

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0})

\displaystyle=R_{q}^{2}S_{q}R_{q}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q+q^{2}&1\\

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{1})

\displaystyle=R_{q}^{3}S_{q}R_{q}^{2}S_{q}R_{q}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q+2q^{2}+q^{3}+q^{4}&1+q\\
q+q^{2}&1\end{array}\right).

Therefore, this defines a morphism of monoids
\mu_{q}:\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}\to\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1}])

The

Likewise, we may observe that

1.2.
Main results

In this article,
we propose a result which progresses in the direction of

1.3
.
More precisely, we prove that the map
w\mapsto\mu_{q}(w)_{12}
is strictly increasing
with respect to the radix order on the language of a fixed balanced sequence.

Recall that the radix order is defined
for every
u,v\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
as

u<_{radix}v\qquad\text{ if }\qquad\begin{cases}|u|<|v|\quad\text{ or }\\
|u|=|v|\quad\text{ and }\quad u<_{lex}v.\end{cases}

Also we define a partial order
\prec
on
\mathbb{Z}[q]
as

f\prec g\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad f\neq g\text{ and }g-f\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q].

Theorem A
.

Let
s\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a balanced sequence
and
u,v\in\mathcal{L}(s)
be two factors in the language of
s
.
If
u<_{radix}v
, then

\mu_{q}(u)_{12}\prec\mu_{q}(v)_{12}
,
i.e.,
\mu_{q}(v)_{12}-\mu_{q}(u)_{12}

is a nonzero polynomial of indeterminate
q
with nonnegative integer
coefficients.

As a consequence, we prove

1.3

when restricted to the language of a given balanced sequence.

Corollary 1
.

Let
s\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a balanced sequence.
The map
u\mapsto\mu_{q}(u)_{12}
is injective over the language
\mathcal{L}(s)
.

1
can also be deduced from
Corollary 1 of
[

]

are

2.

2.1.
Definition and example

Let
s\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence over a finite set

The language of

\mathcal{L}(s)=\{s_{k}s_{k+1}\cdots s_{k+n-1}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z},n\geq 0\}\subset\Sigma^{*}

is the set of subwords (or factors) occurring in

The
reversal
of a finite word

is
\widetilde{w}=(w_{n+1-i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Similarly, the
reversal
of a right infinite sequence

is the left infinite sequence
\widetilde{s}=(s_{-i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\leq 0}

.

A sequence

for every

and every letter

the number of

and

differ by at most 1.

2.2.
The Markoff property

It is worth recalling that balanced sequences appeared in the work of Markoff
himself
[
Mar79
,
Mar82
]
under an equivalent condition, called Markoff property (M) in

[
Reu06
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

[
Reu06
]

We say that
a biinfinite word

s\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

satisfies the
Markoff property
if for any factorization

s=uxyv
, where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
, one has

•

either
\widetilde{u}=v
,

•

or there is a factorization
u=u^{\prime}ym
,
v=\widetilde{m}xv^{\prime}
.

The Markoff property is related to the Markoff spectrum.
Let
U=(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a biinfinite sequence such that
a_{i}
are positive integers.
For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

\lambda_{i}(U)=a_{i}+[0;a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\dots]+[0;a_{i-1},a_{i-2},\dots].

The
Markoff supremum of
U
is

M(U)=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{i}(U).

Two results of Markoff can be stated in terms of Christoffel
words and balanced sequences as follows where

\sigma
is the substitution from
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
to
\{1,2\}^{*}
defined by
\mathtt{0}\mapsto 11
and
\mathtt{1}\mapsto 22
.
It provides an equivalence between sequences satisfying the
Markoff property and sequences of positive integers such that the Markoff
supremum is at most 3.
The equivalence between sequences satisfying the Markoff property and
balanced sequences was not proved by Markoff himself:
it was stated without proof in
[
CF89
]
and a proof was provided in

[
Reu06
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Markoff)
.

[
Reu06
, Theorem 3.1 and 7.1]

Let
s\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a biinfinite word. The following conditions are equivalent:

•

s
satisfies the Markoff property,

•

s
is balanced,

•

M(\sigma(s))\leq 3
.

The Markoff supremum of a purely periodic balanced sequence can be computed
from the Markoff number associated to the Christoffel word which is a period of
the sequence.

Theorem 2.4

(Markoff)
.

[
Reu19
, Theorem 6.2.1]

Let
w
be some lower Christoffel word associated with Markoff number

m=\mu(w)_{12}
.
Let
s
be the biinfinite sequence
{\vphantom{\sigma}}{}^{\infty}\sigma(w)^{\infty}
.
Then
M(s)=\sqrt{9-\frac{4}{m^{2}}}
.

2.3.
Mechanical sequences

It is known that right-infinite aperiodic balanced sequences correspond to
mechanical sequences
[
MH40
]
which are binary encodings of irrational
rotations, see the chapters
[
Fog02
, Chapter 6]
,

[
Lot02
, Chapter 2]
and
[
AS03
, Chapter 9]
.
A biinfinite balanced sequence can also be periodic and in this case expressed in terms of Christoffel words.
To be more precise, let
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
and consider
the
lower
and
upper mechanical sequences

s_{\alpha,\rho}
and

s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}

with
slope

\alpha
and
intercept

\rho

given respectively by

\begin{array}[]{rccl}s_{\alpha,\rho}:&\mathbb{Z}&\to&\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}\\
&n&\mapsto&\lfloor\alpha(n+1)+\rho\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha n+\rho\rfloor\end{array}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{array}[]{rccl}s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}:&\mathbb{Z}&\to&\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}\\
&n&\mapsto&\lceil\alpha(n+1)+\rho\rceil-\lceil\alpha n+\rho\rceil.\end{array}

When
\alpha
is rational, the sequences

s_{\alpha,\rho}
and
s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}
are periodic
and their period corresponds to a Christoffel word
[
BLRS09
]
.
When
\alpha
is irrational, then
s_{\alpha,\rho}
and
s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}

are not periodic.
It is clear that if
\rho-\rho^{\prime}
is an integer, then

s_{\alpha,\rho}=s_{\alpha,\rho^{\prime}}
and

s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho^{\prime}}
. Thus we may always assume

0\leq\rho<1
.
Moreover,
if
\mathbb{Z}\cap\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\rho=\varnothing
then

s_{\alpha,\rho}=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}
.

2.4.
Four classes of balanced sequences

Biinfinite balanced sequences can be split into four different types of
sequences.
Reutenauer proposed the following refinement of the Markoff property

[
Reu06
]
which was restated in
[
GLS08
]
as follows.
If a biinfinite sequence
u\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the Markoff property, then it falls into exactly one of the following classes:

(M_{1})

u
cannot be written as
u=\widetilde{p}xyp

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}

and
the lengths of the Christoffel words occurring
in
u
are bounded;

(M_{2})

u
cannot be written as
u=\widetilde{p}xyp

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}

and
the lengths of the Christoffel words occurring
in
u
are unbounded;

(M_{3})

u
has a unique factorization
u=\widetilde{p}xyp

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
;

(M_{4})

u
has at least two factorizations
u=\widetilde{p}xyp

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.

Morse and Hedlund gave a classification of balanced biinfinite sequences
into three classes (periodic, Sturmian, skew)
[
MH40
]
.
Since the Sturmian case naturally splits into two, Reutenauer proposed the
following four classes
(MH_{i})_{i\in\{1,2,3,4\}}

and proved their equivalence with the
(M_{i})
.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
Reu06
, Theorem 6.1]

Let
u\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a balanced sequence.
For every
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
,

u
satisfies
(M_{i})
if and only if

u
satisfies
(MH_{i})

where

(MH_{1})

u
is a purely periodic word

{\vphantom{w}}{}^{\infty}w^{\infty}
for some Christoffel word
w
,

(MH_{2})

u
is a generic aperiodic Sturmian word,
i.e.,
u=s_{\alpha,\rho}=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}

for some
\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}
and

\rho\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{Z}\cap\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\rho=\varnothing
.

(MH_{3})

u
is a characteristic aperiodic Sturmian word,
i.e.,
u=s_{\alpha,\rho}
or
u=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}

for some
\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}
and

\rho\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{Z}\cap\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\rho\neq\varnothing
.

(MH_{4})

u
is an ultimately periodic word but not
purely periodic, i.e.,

u=\cdots xxyxx\cdots
or
u=\cdots(ymx)(ymx)(ymy)(xmy)(xmy)\cdots

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
and
\mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}
is a Christoffel word.

2.5.
Indistinguishable asymptotic pairs

In this section, we give equivalent conditions for
balanced sequences satisfying cases
(M_{3})
or
(M_{4})
.
Cases
(M_{3})
and
(M_{4})
can be expressed in terms of
limits of mechanical words toward an irrational or rational slope from above or
from below which were shown to be equivalent to sequences that belong to an
indistinguishable asymptotic pair
[
BLS21
]
.

Concretely, given a finite set
\Sigma
, we consider the space of
sequences
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}=\{s\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\Sigma\}
endowed with
the prodiscrete topology and
the
shift

\sigma\colon\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
where

\left(\sigma(s)\right)_{m}=s_{m+1}\quad\mbox{ for every }m\in\mathbb{Z}\mbox{ and }s\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}.

The shift on
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is invertible and extends to
a shift action
\mathbb{Z}\overset{\sigma}{\curvearrowright}\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In this setting, two
sequences
s,t\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
are
asymptotic
if
s
and

t
differ in finitely many positions of
\mathbb{Z}
. The finite set
F=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:s_{n}\neq t_{n}\}
is called the
difference set
of

(s,t)
.

Given two asymptotic sequences
s,t\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we may compare the
number of occurrences of a fixed pattern. A
pattern
is a function

p\colon S\to\Sigma
where
S
, called

support
, is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}
.
The
occurrences
of a pattern
p\in\Sigma^{S}
in a sequence

s\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the set
\mathrm{occ}_{p}(s):=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\colon\sigma^{n}(s)|_{S}=p\}
.
Observe that when
s,t\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
are asymptotic sequences,
the difference
\mathrm{occ}_{p}(s)\setminus\mathrm{occ}_{p}(t)
is finite
because the occurrences of
p
are the same outside the difference set.
We say that
(s,t)
is
an
indistinguishable asymptotic pair

if
(s,t)
is asymptotic and

\#\left(\mathrm{occ}_{p}(s)\setminus\mathrm{occ}_{p}(t)\right)=\#\left(\mathrm{occ}_{p}(t)\setminus\mathrm{occ}_{p}(s)\right)

for every finite support
S\subset\mathbb{Z}
and
every pattern
p\in\Sigma^{S}
.
Extending the results proved in
[
BLS21
]
about indistinguishable
asymptotic pairs, we may prove equivalent conditions for balanced sequences
satisfying Markoff property
(M_{3})
or
(M_{4})
.
In the statement, we denote the position of the origin of a biinfinite
sequence
s=\cdots s_{-2}s_{-1}.s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
with a dot
(
.
) between positions
-1
and
0
.

Theorem B
.

Let
s\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The following are equivalent conditions describing balanced sequences
satisfying Markoff property
(M_{3})
or
(M_{4})
:

(1)

the sequence
s
has a factorization
\sigma^{n_{0}}s=\widetilde{p}x.yp
where

\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
;

(2)

there exists a sequence
(\alpha_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
with
\alpha_{k}\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}

such that
\sigma^{n_{0}}s=\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{\alpha_{k},0}

or
\sigma^{n_{0}}s=\lim_{k\to\infty}s^{\prime}_{\alpha_{k},0}
;

(3)

there exists a sequence
t\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

such that
(s,t)
is
an indistinguishable asymptotic pair
with difference set
\{n_{0}-1,n_{0}\}
.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2).
It is sufficient to prove it for
n_{0}=0
.
We suppose that
s
has a factorization

s=\widetilde{p}x.yp

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.
The symmetry
n\mapsto-n-1
keeps the sequence
s
invariant
except at
\{-1,0\}
. In other words,

s(n)=s(-n-1)
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\}

and
\{s(-1),s(0)\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.

Suppose that
s
satisfies case
(M_{3})
.
From
Theorem

2.5
,
s
also satisfies case
(MH_{3})
,
that is, there exists an irrational number
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}
and

\rho\in\mathbb{R}
such that

s=s_{\alpha,\rho}
or
s=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,\rho}

with
\mathbb{Z}\cap\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\rho\neq\varnothing
.
Since
s(n)=s(-n-1)
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\}
,
we must have
\rho=0
.
Because
\alpha
is irrational,
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\}
and
every sequence
(\alpha_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

of irrational numbers
\alpha_{k}

such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}=\alpha
,
we have

s_{\alpha,0}(n)=\lfloor\alpha(n+1)\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lfloor\alpha_{k}(n+1)\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha_{k}n\rfloor=\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{\alpha_{k},0}(n),

s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(n)=\lceil\alpha(n+1)\rceil-\lceil\alpha n\rceil=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lceil\alpha_{k}(n+1)\rceil-\lceil\alpha_{k}n\rceil=\lim_{k\to\infty}s^{\prime}_{\alpha_{k},0}(n).

Also, since each
\alpha_{k}
is irrational, we have

s_{\alpha,0}(0)s_{\alpha,0}(1)=\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}=\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{\alpha_{k},0}(0)s_{\alpha_{k},0}(1)

and

s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(0)s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(1)=\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}=\lim_{k\to\infty}s^{\prime}_{\alpha_{k},0}(0)s^{\prime}_{\alpha_{k},0}(1)
.
We conclude that

s=\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{\alpha_{k},0}

or
s=\lim_{k\to\infty}s^{\prime}_{\alpha_{k},0}
.

Suppose that
s
satisfies case
(M_{4})
.
From
Theorem

2.5
,
s
also satisfies case
(MH_{4})
,
that is,

s
is an ultimately periodic word but not
purely periodic, i.e.,

s=\cdots xxyxx\cdots
or
s=\cdots(ymx)(ymx)(ymy)(xmy)(xmy)\cdots

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
and
\mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}
is a Christoffel word.
From

[
BLS21
, Lemma 4.2]
, there exists
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
coprime
integers such that

\widetilde{p}\mathtt{1}.\mathtt{0}p=\lim_{\alpha\to\frac{a}{a+b}^{+}}s_{\alpha,0}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{p}\mathtt{0}.\mathtt{1}p=\lim_{\alpha\to\frac{a}{a+b}^{+}}s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}\quad\text{(limit from above)}

or

\widetilde{p}\mathtt{1}.\mathtt{0}p=\lim_{\alpha\to\frac{a}{a+b}^{-}}s_{\alpha,0}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{p}\mathtt{0}.\mathtt{1}p=\lim_{\alpha\to\frac{a}{a+b}^{-}}s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}\quad\text{(limit from below).}

(2)
\implies
(1).
If
\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
from
[
BLS21
, Lemma 4.2]
, we directly have that

\sigma^{n_{0}}s
has a factorization
\sigma^{n_{0}}s=\widetilde{p}x.yp
where

\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.
If
\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha_{k}=\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, then

\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{\alpha_{k},0}=s_{\alpha,0}

and

\lim_{k\to\infty}s^{\prime}_{\alpha_{k},0}=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}
.
Both are symmetric satisfying

s_{\alpha,0}(n)=s_{\alpha,0}(-n-1)

and

s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(n)=s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(-n-1)

for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\}
and

\{s_{\alpha,0}(-1),s_{\alpha,0}(0)\}=\{s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(-1),s^{\prime}_{\alpha,0}(0)\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.

(2)
\iff
(3).
It was proved in
[
BLS21
, Theorem B]
that (2) holds
if and only if there exists a sequence
t^{\prime}\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

(\sigma^{n_{0}}s,t^{\prime})
is an indistinguishable asymptotic pair with
difference set
\{-1,0\}
.
This holds
if and only if

(s,\sigma^{-n_{0}}t^{\prime})
is an indistinguishable asymptotic pair with
difference set
\{n_{0}-1,n_{0}\}
since
the shift preserves indistinguishable asymptotic pairs

[
BLS21

.
It concludes the proof if we let
t=\sigma^{-n_{0}}t^{\prime}
.
∎

2.6.
Language of a balanced sequence

Balanced sequences have other equivalent definitions, for example, in terms of
factor complexity
[
CH73
]
. A balanced sequence satisfies Markoff properties

(M_{2})
,
(M_{3})
or
(M_{4})
if and only if it has complexity
n+1
, see

[
Lot02
, Theorem 2.1.13]
stated for right-infinite sequences.

The language of a balanced sequence of complexity
n+1
can be compactly
represented in two ways as described in the following result.
It shows that there exist two words of length
2n
that contains the language
of factors of length
n\geq 1
occurring in a balanced sequence.
The proof follows easily from the notion of indistinguishable asymptotic pairs

[
BLS21
]
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
s\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a balanced sequence
having at least one factorization
s=\widetilde{p}xyp

where
\{x,y\}=\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
.
Let
n\geq 1
and
w
be the prefix of
p
of length
n-1
.
The two words

\widetilde{w}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}w

and

\widetilde{w}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}w

of length
2n

contain the
n+1
factors of
s
. More precisely,

\mathcal{L}_{n}(s)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widetilde{w}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}w)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widetilde{w}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}w)
.

Proof.

Let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that

\sigma^{n_{0}}s=\widetilde{p}x.yp
.
We may assume that
n_{0}=0
and
\sigma^{n_{0}}s=s
since shifting the
sequence
s
preserves its language. From

B
,
there exists a sequence
t\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

such that
(s,t)
is an indistinguishable asymptotic pair
with difference set
\{n_{0}-1,n_{0}\}=\{-1,0\}
.
In particular,
s
and
t
are equal outside of the difference set, i.e.,

s|_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\}}=t|_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\}}
,
and different on the difference set.
Since the alphabet is binary, we must have
t_{-1}t_{0}=yx
.
Therefore the sequence
t
satisfies
t=\widetilde{p}y.xp
.
Replacing
\{x,y\}
by
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
, we have that the indistinguishable asymptotic pair
is of the form
\{s,t\}=\{\widetilde{p}\mathtt{0}.\mathtt{1}p,\widetilde{p}\mathtt{1}.\mathtt{0}p\}
.
Thus, for every prefix
w
of length
n-1
of
p
, we have

\{s_{-n}s_{-n+1}\cdots s_{n-1},t_{-n}t_{-n+1}\cdots t_{n-1}\}=\{\widetilde{w}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}w,\widetilde{w}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}w\}
.
From
[
BLS21
, Corollary 3.6]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{n}(s_{-n}s_{-n+1}\cdots s_{n-1})=\mathcal{L}_{n}(t_{-n}t_{-n+1}\cdots t_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widetilde{w}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}w)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widetilde{w}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}w).\qed

For example, the following two words of length 16 contain one occurrence of each factor of length 8 occurring in the Fibonacci word:

1010010.01.0100101

1010010.10.0100101

We use
Corollary

2.6

in
Lemma

4.1

to show the existence of a bijection

f:\mathcal{L}_{n}(s)\to\mathcal{L}_{n}(s)

which is a cyclic permutation and
having the property, except for two words in
\mathcal{L}_{n}(s)
,
of flipping a
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}
into a
\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}
from
u
to
f(u)
.
This property is well-known in the context of the Burrows-Wheeler transform of
a Christoffel word, see
[
MRS03
,
BR06
]
or
more recently
[
Reu19
, Theorem 15.2.4]
.

Small local changes from a factor to the next (in radix order) can also be seen
in the language of a balanced sequence.
For example, we list below the factors of length 8 in the Fibonacci word
as well as the greatest (for the radix order) factor of length 7
and the smallest factor of length 9:

\begin{array}[]{lllll}&&\texttt{\lx@text@underline{1}010010}&=&\underline{\mathtt{1}}m\\
\texttt{0010\lx@text@underline{01}01}&=&\texttt{0010\lx@text@underline{01}01}&=&\mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}\\
\texttt{0\lx@text@underline{01}01001}&=&\texttt{0\lx@text@underline{01}01001}&&\\

\texttt{\lx@text@underline{01}010010}&=&\texttt{\lx@text@underline{01}010010}&=&\widetilde{u}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}v\\

&&\texttt{\lx@text@underline{1}0100101}&=&\underline{\mathtt{1}}m\mathtt{1}\\

In the left column, the 8 cyclic conjugates of the Christoffel word

are listed in lexicographic order.
It illustrates what happens in the context of the Burrows-Wheeler transform: in
each word (except the last), the factor
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}
that is changed into a
\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}
is
underlined.
In the middle column, the 9 factors of length
8
in the language of the Fibonacci word.
There is also the lexicographically largest factor of length 7 and the

balanced sequences. Thus the Markoff injectivity conjecture can not be
extended to the injectivity of the map
w\mapsto\mu_{q}(w)_{12}|_{q=\gamma}
for all
\gamma>0
.
We provide few counterexamples below.

Observe that

\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}<_{radix}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}
but

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0})_{12}

\displaystyle=1+3q+4q^{2}+4q^{3}+4q^{4}+2q^{5}+q^{6},

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}

\displaystyle=1+2q+5q^{2}+6q^{3}+6q^{4}+5q^{5}+3q^{6}+q^{7},

and

\mu_{q}(\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0})_{12}-\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}=q-q^{2}-2q^{3}-2q^{4}-3q^{5}-2q^{6}-q^{7}

has negative coefficients.

Another example is

\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}<_{radix}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}

but

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}-\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1})_{12}=q+3q^{2}+7q^{3}+12q^{4}+17q^{5}+20q^{6}+21q^{7}+19q^{8}

\displaystyle+14q^{9}+9q^{10}+4q^{11}+q^{12}-q^{13}-q^{14}

which has negative coefficients.

The words
\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}
,

\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}
and
\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}

are not Christoffel words, but even in the case of Christoffel words, there are counterexamples.
For example,

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1})_{12}

\displaystyle=1+4q+8q^{2}+13q^{3}+16q^{4}+17q^{5}+14q^{6}+10q^{7}+5q^{8}+q^{9},

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}

\displaystyle=1+3q+9q^{2}+16q^{3}+24q^{4}+29q^{5}+29q^{6}+25q^{7}+18q^{8}+10q^{9}+4q^{10}+q^{11},

and their difference

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1})_{12}-\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}=q-q^{2}-3q^{3}-8q^{4}-12q^{5}-15q^{6}-15q^{7}-13q^{8}-9q^{9}-4q^{10}-q^{11}

has negative coefficients.
Another counterexample is given by the Christoffel words
\mathtt{0}^{12}\mathtt{1}
and

\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}^{7}
.

The above counterexamples show that

2
does not hold for
\gamma>0
over the
language of all balanced sequences.
Thus a proof of Markoff Injectivity Conjecture needs to use the hypothesis that
polynomials are evaluated only at
q=1
,
perhaps extending the approach used in
[
BRS09
]
.

Finally, we observe that the map
w\mapsto\mu_{q}(w)_{12}
from
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^{*}
to
polynomials in the indeterminate
q
is not injective, as for example:

\displaystyle\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1})_{12}

\displaystyle=1+5q+16q^{2}+38q^{3}+70q^{4}+109q^{5}+145q^{6}+168q^{7}+171q^{8}

\displaystyle\qquad+152q^{9}+118q^{10}+79q^{11}+44q^{12}+19q^{13}+6q^{14}+q^{15}

\displaystyle=\mu_{q}(\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1})_{12}.
