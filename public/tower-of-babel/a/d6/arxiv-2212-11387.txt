Use of Schwinger-Dyson equation in constructing an approximate trivializing map
Akio Tomiya

Abstract
We construct an approximate trivializing map by using a Schwinger-Dyson equation. The advantage of this method is that: (1) The basis for the flow kernel can be chosen arbitrarily by hand. (2) It can be applied to the general action of interest. (3) The coefficients in the kernel are determined by lattice estimates of the observables, which does not require analytic calculations beforehand. We perform the HMC with the effective action obtained by the Schwinger-Dyson method, and show that we can have better control of the effective action than the known t t -expansion construction. However, the algorithmic overhead is still large and overwhelming the gain though faster decorrelation is observed for long-range observables in some cases. This contribution reports the preliminary results of this attempt.

2
Trivializing map

2.1
Review of Lüscher’s construction

Following Lüscher
[
7
]
,
we consider a field transformation:
\mathcal{F}:V\mapsto U
,
by which the original action
S(U)
of interest will be mapped to
the effective action
S_{\rm eff}(V)
according to the formula:

\displaystyle\int(dU)\,e^{-S(U)}

\displaystyle=\int(dV)\,{\rm det}\mathcal{F}_{*}(V)\,e^{-S(\mathcal{F}(V))}

\displaystyle\equiv\int(dV)\,e^{-S_{\rm eff}(V)},

(1)

i.e.,

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}(V)\equiv S(\mathcal{F}(V))-\ln\det\mathcal{F}_{*}(V).

(2)

The Jacobian matrix
\mathcal{F}_{*}(V)

can be defined by the
local parameterization
\theta_{x,\mu}^{a}
of the field space:

\displaystyle e^{\theta_{x,\mu}^{a}T^{a}}U_{x,\mu},

(3)

where
T^{a}
are the
{\mathfrak{s}u}(3)
generators
normalized as
{\rm tr}\,T^{a}T^{b}=-(1/2)\delta^{ab}
.
Note that the Haar measure can then be written as:

\displaystyle(dU)={\rm const.}\,\prod_{x,\mu,a}d\theta_{x,\mu}^{a}.

(4)

The derivative
\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\equiv\partial_{\theta_{x,\mu}^{a}}|_{\theta=0}

acts as

\displaystyle\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}U_{y,\nu}=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{\mu,\nu}T^{a}U_{x,\mu}.

(5)

Writing the indices in a short way as
A\equiv(x,\mu,a)
,
the Jacobian matrix
\mathcal{F}_{*}(V)\equiv(\mathcal{F}^{AB}_{*}(V))

relates the one-forms of the
U
-space and the
V
-space:

\displaystyle d\theta^{A}_{U}\equiv\mathcal{F}^{AB}_{*}(V)d\theta^{B}_{V},

(6)

where the subscript is supplied to distinguish the coordinates of
the two field spaces.

Lüscher obtained the
trivializing map

by considering a flow
{\mathcal{F}_{t}}:V\mapsto U_{t}\equiv{\mathcal{F}}_{t}(V)

and by demanding the effective action at flow time
t
to be:

\displaystyle S_{{\rm eff},t}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}(1-t)S({\mathcal{F}_{t}(V)}),

(7)

up to an irrelevant
t
-dependent constant
which we ignore hereafter.
The symbol
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}

means it is a relation we require
so that the
V
-space action becomes trivial at
t=1
.
We divide the trivializing map
{\mathcal{F}}_{t=1}

into infinitesimal steps:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t=1}=\mathcal{F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}\circ\mathcal{F}_{(n-2)\epsilon,\epsilon}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{F}_{0,\epsilon},

(8)

where
1=n\epsilon
, and use the gradient flow ansatz:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t,\epsilon}(U)_{x,\mu}=e^{-\epsilon T^{a}\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t}(U)}U_{x,\mu}.

(9)

The derivative acts on the direct argument of the function.
2
2

2

Therefore, the expression
\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t}(U)

can be thought of as a vector function of
U
:

\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t}(U)=(\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t})(U)
.
We use this convention throughout this contribution.

Using that, for
U^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{t,\epsilon}(U)
,

d=d\theta_{U^{\prime}}^{A}\partial_{U^{\prime}}^{A}=d\theta_{U}^{A}\partial_{U}^{A}
and

\displaystyle{d\theta_{U^{\prime}}}_{x,\mu}^{a}=-2{\rm tr}[T^{a}dU^{\prime}_{x,\mu}U_{x,\mu}^{\prime-1}],

(10)

one finds for an infinitesimal
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}_{t,\epsilon,*}}\,^{a}_{x,\mu}|^{b}_{y,\nu}=\delta_{x,y}\delta_{\mu,\nu}\delta^{ab}-\epsilon\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\partial_{y,\nu}^{b}\tilde{S}_{t}-\epsilon\delta_{x,y}\delta_{\mu,\nu}f^{abc}\partial_{x,\mu}^{c}\tilde{S}_{t},

(11)

where
f^{abc}\equiv-2{\rm tr}\big(T^{a}[T^{b},T^{c}]\big)

is the totally antisymmetric tensor.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\ln{\rm det}\,{\mathcal{F}_{t,\epsilon,*}}=-\epsilon(\partial^{A})^{2}{\tilde{S}}_{t}.

(12)

We note that the ansatz (
9
)
is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\dot{U}_{t,x,\mu}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{t}(V)_{x,\mu}=-T^{a}\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t}(U_{t})\,U_{t,x,\mu}.

(13)

Rewriting eq. (
7
) as

\displaystyle\ln\det\mathcal{F}_{t,*}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}-tS({\mathcal{F}_{t}(V)}),

(14)

and by taking the
t
-derivative,
we obtain the functional equation:

\displaystyle[-(\partial^{A})^{2}+t(\partial^{A}S)\partial^{A}]\tilde{S}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}S.

(15)

Since the differential operator

{\mathcal{L}}_{t}\equiv-(\partial^{A})^{2}+t(\partial^{A}S)\partial^{A}

is elliptic and
there is an inner product that makes
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}
a symmetric operator,
it is invertible for finite lattice up to the constant function,
which is the zero-mode
[
7
]
.
This fact suggests that the trivializing map formally exists.

Lüscher further gave a
way to construct
{\cal F}_{t=1}

as a
t
-expansion,
which was explicitly demonstrated for the Wilson plaquette action:

\displaystyle S_{W}=-\frac{\beta}{6}W_{0},

where

The construction begins with expanding

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}=\sum_{k\geq 0}t^{k}\tilde{S}^{(k)},

whose convergence radius is proven to be finite

Plugging the expansion into eq. (

we obtain the recurrence relation:

\displaystyle-(\partial^{A})^{2}\tilde{S}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}S_{W},

(18)

\displaystyle-(\partial^{A})^{2}\tilde{S}^{(k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}-(\partial^{A}S_{W})(\partial^{A}\tilde{S}^{(k-1)})\quad(k\geq 1).

(19)

These relations will be solved in the space of Wilson loops.
Note that the derivative operator
\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}

inserts

The contraction will then be calculated by the completeness relation;

\displaystyle{\rm tr}[T^{a}AT^{a}B]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Big({\rm tr}A{\rm tr}B-\frac{1}{3}{\rm tr}AB\Big),

\displaystyle{\rm tr}[T^{a}A]{\rm tr}[T^{a}B]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Big({\rm tr}AB-\frac{1}{3}{\rm tr}A{\rm tr}B\Big).

(21)

See figure

for examples shown graphically.

Since
W_{0}
is an eigenfunction of
-(\partial^{A})^{2}
,
we have for the leading order:

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{(0)}=-\frac{\beta}{32}W_{0}.

(22)

To obtain the next-to-leading order,
we need the Wilson loops
W_{0},\cdots,W_{7}
shown in figure

.
The operator
-(\partial^{A})^{2}
can be represented in this subspace,
and after inverting the matrix we get

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{(1)}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{192}\Big(-\frac{4}{33}W_{1}+\frac{12}{119}W_{2}+\frac{1}{33}W_{3}-\frac{5}{119}W_{4}+\frac{3}{10}W_{5}-\frac{1}{5}W_{6}+\frac{1}{9}W_{7}\Big).

(23)

Since the operation
\partial^{A}\cdot\partial^{A}\cdot

glues the Wilson loops in all possible ways,
the number of relevant Wilson loops
increases for the higher orders in a combinatorial manner.

2.2
Another look at the map from the space of effective actions

The reason for the rapid increase of the involved Wilson loop
can be understood as follows.
First note that, as can be seen in
eq. (
13
),
the flow runs from the trivial theory to a nontrivial theory.
Correspondingly, the
t
-expansion around
t
= 0
is an expansion around the trivial theory.
At small flow times,
we only need to add the plaquettes in the effective action,
and thus the expansion begins with
W_{0}
.
However, as we evolve the flow,
the effective action goes through the non-trivial theories,
at which the wave-like particle picture should become relevant.
To decrease these modes directly,
we expect that the extended Wilson loops
(presumably summed in certain linear combinations) become relevant.
Therefore, obtaining the exact kernel for the trivializing map is extremely difficult,
and thus we are forced for practical reasons
to choose a finite basis and construct
the efficient flow kernel that decreases the autocorrelations
within the chosen subspace.

For this purpose,
we reexamine the trivializing map
from a different point of view,
namely, from the effective action.
As we demonstrate momentarily,
while the flow in configuration space, eq. (
13
),
evolves from the trivial theory to the finite
\beta
theory,
in the action perspective
the flow time runs in the opposite direction,
from the finite
\beta
theory to the trivial theory.
We note that the difference is just the way to see the map,
and, if solved exactly, the two maps coincide.
The difference, however, can occur when we
approximate the map,
and such a functional space point of view
allows us to construct the map
without using the
t
-expansion,
whose example is the Schwinger-Dyson method
we give in section
3
.
To distinguish the maps in the two different viewpoints,
we add the superscript
(c)
to the expressions derived in
the previous configuration space viewpoint.

We define the effective action at time
t
,

S_{t}
(we drop the subscript
{\rm eff}

for notational simplicity),
by the following recurrence relation:

\displaystyle S_{t=0}(V)\equiv S_{W}(V),

(24)

\displaystyle S_{t+\epsilon}(V)\equiv S_{t}({\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}(V))-\ln\det{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon,*}(V),

(25)

where
{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}
is again the map that increases
t

by an amount
\epsilon
,
and
{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon,*}(V)
is its Jacobian matrix.
We here require that
{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}
satisfies the relation:

\displaystyle S_{t+\epsilon}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}S_{t}(V)-\epsilon S_{W}(V),

(26)

up to an irrelevant constant which we again ignore.
Using eqs. (
24
) and (
26
), we obtain:

\displaystyle S_{t}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}(1-t)S_{W}(V),

(27)

and thus we have the trivializing map at
t=1
.

Note that the composition ordering of
{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}
will be
the opposite from eq. (
8
).
In fact, from the recurrence formula, we have

\displaystyle S_{t=n\epsilon}(V)

\displaystyle=S_{(n-1)\epsilon}({\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}(V))-\ln\det\mathcal{F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon,*}(V)

\displaystyle=S_{(n-2)\epsilon}({\cal F}_{(n-2)\epsilon,\epsilon}\circ{\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}(V))

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\ln\det\mathcal{F}_{(n-2)\epsilon,\epsilon,*}({\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}(V))-\ln\det\mathcal{F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon,*}(V)

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=S_{0}({\cal F}_{0,\epsilon}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}(V))

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\ln\det\mathcal{F}_{\ell\epsilon,\epsilon,*}({\cal F}_{(\ell+1)\epsilon,\epsilon}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}(V)).

(28)

We identify
{\cal F}_{t}
with the composite function:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t}\equiv{\cal F}_{0,\epsilon}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon},

(29)

where the Jacobian matrix is given by

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t,*}(V)\equiv\prod_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{F}_{\ell\epsilon,\epsilon,*}({\cal F}_{(\ell+1)\epsilon,\epsilon}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}(V)).

(30)

The matrix product is taken in descending order from right to left.
It is easy to see that

S_{t}
is indeed the effective action for the map
{\cal F}_{t}
:

\displaystyle S_{t}(V)=S_{W}({\cal F}_{t}(V))-\ln\det{\cal F}_{t,*}(V).

(31)

To further rewrite the expression,
we again use the gradient flow ansatz:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}(V)_{x,\mu}=e^{-\epsilon T^{a}\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t}(V)}V_{x,\mu}.

(32)

Then the requirement (
26
)
gives the functional equation for
{\tilde{S}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle[-(\partial^{A})^{2}+(1-t)(\partial^{A}S_{W})\partial^{A}]\tilde{S}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}S_{W}.

(33)

Comparing eq. (
33
) with (
15
),
we notice that

\displaystyle 1-t=t^{(c)},

(34)

and thus

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}=\tilde{S}^{(c)}_{1-t}

(35)

and

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}={\cal F}^{(c)}_{1-t,\epsilon}.

(36)

From eqs. (
29
) and (
36
),
the map
{\cal F}_{t}
in the functional space viewpoint
can be expressed with
{\cal F}^{(c)}_{t,\epsilon}
and
1=n\epsilon
as:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t=m\epsilon}

\displaystyle={\cal F}_{n\epsilon,\epsilon}^{(c)}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{(n-m+1)\epsilon,\epsilon}^{(c)}.

(37)

In particular,

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{1=n\epsilon}={\cal F}_{n\epsilon,\epsilon}^{(c)}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{\epsilon,\epsilon}^{(c)},

(38)

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{1=n\epsilon}^{(c)}={\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon}^{(c)}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{0,\epsilon}^{(c)},

(39)

which should agree in
\epsilon\to 0
.

We therefore have the same trivializing map,
but the direction of the construction is
from finite
\beta
theory to the trivial theory
(see figure
3
).

Direction of constructing trivializing maps.

The complication is that
it is not straightforward to expand in terms of
t
,
as expected from the argument at the beginning of this section.
We will solve eq. (
33
) by picking up a finite basis,
for which we need a way to project the
entire function space to the truncated space.
For this purpose, we use a Schwinger-Dyson equation.

3
Schwinger-Dyson equation

In the Schwinger-Dyson method described below,
we sequentially determine the flow from the finite
\beta
theory
as explained in section
2.2
.
At each intermediate step,
we determine the effective couplings by a Schwinger-Dyson equation.
The flow will be designed to decrease the couplings from the lattice
expectation values of Wilson loops.

3.1
Schwinger-Dyson equation for determining coupling constants

In this subsection,
we review the use of a Schwinger-Dyson equation
to determine the couplings in effective actions,
based on refs
[
33
,
34
]

(see also
[
35
,
36
]
).
Suppose that the effective action
S_{\rm eff}

is expressed as a sum of Wilson loops (and their products):

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}W_{j}.

(40)

Then, the coefficients
\beta_{j}
satisfy
the linear equation:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\langle\partial^{A}W_{j}\partial^{A}W_{i}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}=\langle(\partial^{A})^{2}W_{i}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}},

(41)

where the expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}

is taken with respect to the action
{S_{\rm eff}}
.
In fact, under the variation
\delta V
using
W_{i}
in the gradient flow kernel:

\displaystyle\delta V_{x,\mu}\equiv-\epsilon\sum_{x,\mu}T^{a}(\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}W_{i})\,V_{x,\mu},

(42)

the path integral is invariant (we vary all the links
(x,\mu)
at the same time):

\displaystyle 0=\delta\int(dV)e^{-S_{\rm eff}(V)}=\epsilon\int(dV)e^{-S_{\rm eff}(V)}[-(\partial^{A})^{2}W_{i}+(\partial^{A}S_{\rm eff})\partial^{A}W_{i}].

(43)

Combining eqs. (
40
) and (
43
),
we obtain eq. (
41
).

The linear equation (
41
)
allows us to obtain the couplings
\beta_{j}

from the lattice expectation values.
However, we generically need an infinite number of basis functions
to fully parameterize the action,
and thus
\langle\partial^{A}W_{j}\partial^{A}W_{i}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}
in eq. (
41
)
becomes an infinite-dimensional matrix.
Practically, we cannot calculate an infinite number of
expectation values, and we need to introduce a truncation.
By choosing a finite basis
\{W_{j^{\prime}}\}
,
where now
j^{\prime}
only runs a finite range,
we approximate the action as

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}\approx{\sum_{j^{\prime}}}^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}W_{j^{\prime}}\equiv S_{\rm eff}^{\prime}.

(44)

The prime symbols indicate the truncation.
It then turns out that
\beta_{j}^{\prime}
determined by
the finite-dimensional counterpart of eq. (
41
):

\displaystyle{\sum_{j^{\prime}}}^{\prime}\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{\prime}\langle\partial^{A}W_{j^{\prime}}\partial^{A}W_{i^{\prime}}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}=\langle(\partial^{A})^{2}W_{i^{\prime}}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}},

(45)

gives the best approximation of
S_{\rm eff}

in the sense that they minimize the norm

\|S_{\rm eff}-S_{\rm eff}^{\prime}\|_{S_{\rm eff}}
,
where
\|\cdot\|_{S_{\rm eff}}
is the force norm:

\displaystyle\|S\|_{S_{\rm eff}}^{2}\equiv\langle(\partial^{A}S)^{2}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}.

(46)

In fact, by subtracting eqs. (
41
) and (
45
)
for the range of the index
i^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle{\sum_{j^{\prime}}}^{\prime}(\beta_{j^{\prime}}-\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{\prime})\langle\partial^{A}W_{j^{\prime}}\partial^{A}W_{i^{\prime}}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}=0.

(47)

This is equivalent to the stationary condition:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}\|S_{\rm eff}-S_{\rm eff}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}\langle[\partial^{A}(S_{\rm eff}-S_{\rm eff}^{\prime})]^{2}\rangle_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=-2{\sum_{j^{\prime}}}^{\prime}(\beta_{j^{\prime}}-\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{\prime})\langle\partial^{A}W_{j^{\prime}}\partial^{A}W_{i^{\prime}}\rangle_{S_{\rm eff}}.

(48)

Since the norm (
46
) is bounded from below but not from above,
we can generically expect the stationary point to be the minimum.
Therefore, this Schwinger-Dyson method
gives us a systematic way to project effective actions
onto a subspace of actions,
where we can measure its goodness from the force fields in numerical calculations.

3.2
Designing the kernel with the Schwinger-Dyson equation

We now apply the Schwinger-Dyson equation
in the determination of the approximate trivializing map.
The idea is to reduce the action with
t
as in eq. (
26
),
replacing the effective action with the approximate one, eq. (
44
),
so that we can work completely in the functional space spanned by the finite basis.
Below, we write down equations in this finite basis,
and we drop the prime symbols on
j
for notational simplicity.
We write as
{\cal S}
the subspace spanned by
the chosen finite set
\{W_{j}\}
of Wilson loops and their products.

For the effective action
S_{{\rm eff},t}
at a given time
t
,
we apply the Schwinger-Dyson method
to approximate it in
{\cal S}
:

\displaystyle S_{{\rm eff},t}\approx S_{{\rm eff},t}^{\prime}={\sum_{j}}^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}_{j,t}W_{j}.

(49)

Here the effective couplings
\beta_{j,t}
are determined by the linear equation:

\displaystyle\big\langle-(\partial^{A})^{2}W_{i}+{\sum_{j}}^{\prime}\beta_{j,t}^{\prime}\partial^{A}W_{j}\partial^{A}W_{i}\big\rangle_{S_{{\rm eff},t}}=0,

(50)

corresponding to eq. (
45
).
We again consider the infinitesimal map of the form:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t,\epsilon}(V)_{x,\mu}=e^{-\epsilon T^{a}\partial_{x,\mu}^{a}\tilde{S}_{t}(V)}V_{x,\mu},

(51)

where this time we construct
\tilde{S}_{t}
in the truncated space
{\cal S}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}={\sum_{k}}^{\prime}\gamma_{k,t}W_{k}.

(52)

By taking
d/dt
in eq. (
50
),
we obtain the linear equation:

\displaystyle{\sum_{k}}^{\prime}\gamma_{k,t}\big\langle\partial^{B}W_{k}\partial^{B}\big[-(\partial^{A})^{2}W_{i}+{\sum_{j}}^{\prime}\beta_{j,t}^{\prime}\partial^{A}W_{j}\partial^{A}W_{i}\big]\big\rangle_{S_{{\rm eff},t}}=-{\sum_{j}}^{\prime}\dot{\beta}_{j,t}^{\prime}\big\langle\partial^{A}W_{j}\partial^{A}W_{i}\big\rangle_{S_{{\rm eff},t}}.

(53)

The left hand side comes from the
t
-dependence of the Boltzmann weight,
and the right hand side from the explicit
t
-dependence of
\beta_{j,t}^{\prime}
.
Equation (
53
)
gives us the coefficients
\gamma_{k,t}

for a given
\dot{\beta}_{j,t}^{\prime}
,
i.e., for a given trajectory of
S_{{\rm eff},t}^{\prime}
in
{\cal S}
.
We here choose
\dot{\beta}_{j,t}^{\prime}
to be:

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{j,t}^{\prime}=-\frac{\beta_{j,t}^{\prime}}{1-t},

(54)

so that
\beta_{j,t}^{\prime}=(1-t)\beta_{j,t=0}^{\prime}=\beta_{j,t=0}
.
3
3

3

Another choice of
\dot{\beta}_{j,t}^{\prime}
leading to the same functional form
of
\beta_{j,t}

is
\dot{\beta}_{j,t}^{\prime}=-\beta_{j,t=0}
.
The difference between the two appears in practical calculation where
we have inexactness due to
O(\epsilon^{2})
terms and the statistical error.
We choose the form (
54
) as it
can take into account of these effects
from the observed
\beta^{\prime}_{j,t}
.
It is, however, uncertain if this is the optimal choice
though the difference is small compared to
the truncation effect in choosing
{\cal S}
.

After obtaining the coefficients
\gamma_{k,t}
,
and thus the map (
51
) at each time step

t=k\epsilon
,
(k=0,\cdots,n-1)
,
we compose them in the ordering:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{t}\equiv{\cal F}_{0,\epsilon}\circ\cdots\circ{\cal F}_{(n-1)\epsilon,\epsilon},

(55)

One of the advantages of designing the trivializing map with the Schwinger-Dyson equation
is that the basis for the flow kernel can be chosen arbitrarily by hand.
Furthermore, it can be applied to the general action of interest,
and the coefficients in the kernel,
\gamma_{j,t}
, are determined by the lattice estimates
of the observables. In this sense,
it contains the non-perturbative information of the theory
and there is no need to make analytic calculations beforehand.
Note also that the
truncation effects and goodness of the map can be measured by the force norm (
46
).

There is a practical note in the choice of
{\cal S}
.
Since there are linear relations among Wilson loops and their products
called Mandelstam constraints
[
37
]

(see also
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
),
we need to choose the basis functions carefully
so that the inversions (
45
) and (
53
)
are possible.
The simplest and relevant example is
(see Fig
4
for a graphical representation):

\displaystyle W_{6}=W_{5}+2W_{0}.

(56)

Simplest Mandelstam constraint.

This relation follows from a simple
SU(3)
identity: For
U\in{\rm SU}(3)
,

\displaystyle({\rm tr}\,U)^{2}={\rm tr}(U^{2})+2{\rm tr}\,U^{\dagger}.

(57)

Further relation can be obtained by the Cayley–Hamilton equation:

\displaystyle U^{3}=({\rm tr}U)U^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\big[({\rm tr}U)^{2}-{\rm tr}(U^{2})\big]U+1.

(58)

5
Discussion

In this work,
we proposed a way to design an approximate trivializing map
using a Schwinger-Dyson equation.
The algorithmic advantages of this method are that:
(1) The basis for the flow kernel can be chosen arbitrarily by hand.
(2) It can be applied to the general action of interest.
(3) The coefficients in the kernel are determined by lattice estimates of the observables,
which does not require analytic calculations such as those in the
t
-expansion.
It is also notable that
the truncation effects and goodness of the flow can be measured by the force norm.
We showed that
with the Schwinger-Dyson method, we can have better control of the effective action,
and observed a tendency to decrease the autocorrelation of long-range observables
by adding extended Wilson loops.
However, the decorrelation is not sufficient
compared to the induced overhead.
This indicates that,
though the effective action is getting closer
to the target action,
the large-size Wilson loops that are neglected in the construction
still leave significant contributions.

Indeed, to obtain the exact trivializing map,
in which case we can decrease the autocorrelation
of all the modes of the system,
we need an infinite number of Wilson loops
as reviewed in section
2.1
.
However, adding more and more Wilson loop basis
is not realistic because of the increasing algorithmic
overhead shown in subsection
4.3
.
Since it is likely that we need to restrict ourselves to
a few small Wilson loops,
one possible strategy may be to
be more specific to a particular slow observable,
e.g., the topological charge.
For example, as is well known,
the instanton potential with respect to its radius
can be different for the same lattice spacing
but with different gauge action.
We thus may be able to change the instanton potential
to stimulate tunneling
with the small number of basis functions in
{\cal S}

(cf.

[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
).
Studies along these lines are in progress and will be reported elsewhere.
