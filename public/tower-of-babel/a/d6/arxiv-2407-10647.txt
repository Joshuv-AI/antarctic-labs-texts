Non-quasiconvex dispersion of composite fermions and the fermionic Haffnian state in the first-excited Landau level
Hao Jin, Junren Shi

Abstract
It has long been puzzling that fractional quantum Hall states in the first excited Landau level (1LL) often differ significantly from their counterparts in the lowest Landau level. We show that the dispersion of composite fermions (CFs) is a deterministic factor driving the distinction. We find that CFs with two quantized vortices in the 1LL have a non-quasiconvex dispersion. Consequently, in the filling fraction 7 / 3 7/3 , CFs occupy the second Λ \Lambda -level instead of the first. The corresponding ground state wave function, based on the CF wave function ansatz, is identified to be the fermionic Haffnian wave function rather than the Laughlin wave function. The conclusion is supported by numerical evidence from exact diagonalizations in both disk and spherical geometries. Furthermore, we show that the dispersion becomes quasiconvex in wide quantum wells or for CFs with four quantized vortices in the filling fraction 11 / 5 11/5 , coinciding with observations that the distinction between the Landau levels disappears under these circumstances.

A two-dimensional electron gas

Theoretical understanding of the FQHE is challenging because the

diagonalizations (ED) in the lowest Landau level (LLL)
[

,

Furthermore, it has been shown that a deductive approach for
determining CF wave functions and corresponding physical wave

While the FQHE in the LLL can be well described by the CF theory,

\nu=5/2

A representative case is the filling fraction

This state is expected to be an analog of the

In this Letter, we demonstrate that the dispersion of CFs is a key

CF dispersion.—

A CF consists of an electron and a
vortex carrying an even number of quantized vortices. The
Coulomb attraction between the electron and the charge void
induced by the vortex gives rise to the binding energy of the
CF
[
2
]
. Read shows that the momentum of a CF can be defined as
being proportional to the spatial separation between the electron
and vortex
[
35
]
. Consequently, the binding energy, as a function
of the spatial separation
r
, can be interpreted as the
dispersion of the CF
[
36
]
. To determine the binding energy, we
first calculate the electron-vortex correlation function

h\quantity(r)
, which describes the electron density profile in the
vicinity of a vortex. The binding energy can then be determined
by
\epsilon_{b}\quantity(r)=\quantity(\rho_0/2)\int\differential[2]{\boldsymbol{r}'}v\quantity(\abs{\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}'})h\quantity(r')
, where

v\quantity(r)
is the interaction between electrons and
\rho_{0}
is
the average electron density
[
2
]
.

We can determine
h\quantity(r)
for CF
2
by assuming the ground
state to be the
1/3
Laughlin state. The many-body wave
function in the presence of a vortex at the origin is given by

\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}\quantity(\qty{z_i})=\prod_{i}z_{i}^{2}\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)^{3}
,
where
\quantity{z_i=x_i+y_i}
is the set of complex electron
coordinates
[
2
]
.
h\quantity(r)
is obtained by computing the
electron density distribution of
\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}
normalized
by
\rho_{0}
. The result is shown in the inset of Fig.
1

Different interactions

give rise to different binding
energies of CF
2
for the LLL and the 1LL. For the LLL,

is the Coulomb interaction

\tilde{v}\quantity(r)=\quantity(1+\frac{l_B^2\nabla^2}{2})^{2}v_{\mathrm{c}}\quantity(r),

(1)

where

We observe that the binding energy of CF

\Lambda
levels and wave functions.—

With the
binding energy of a CF, we can establish its effective
Hamiltonian. In the dipole picture, the electron and vortex in a
CF are confined in two separate LLLs induced by the physical
magnetic field and the emergent Chern-Simons magnetic field,
respectively
[
2
]
. As a result, the CF is described by a
bi-variate wave function
\psi\quantity(z,\bar\eta)
, which is
(anti-)holomorphic in the complex electron (vortex) coordinate

z
(
\eta\equiv\eta_{x}+\mathrm{i}\eta_{y}

LLLs,
\bar{z}
and
\eta
become the operators

\hat{\bar{z}}=2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z}
and

\hat{\eta}=2l_{b}^{2}\partial_{\bar{\eta}}
, respectively, where

l_{b}=l_{B}/\sqrt{2\tilde{\nu}}
is the magnetic length of
Chern-Simons magnetic field for
CF
2

[
4
,
2
]
. We can then define the ladder operators of

\Lambda
-levels as
\hat{a}=\quantity(z-\hat\eta)/\sqrt{\gamma}l_{B}
and

\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\quantity(\hat{\bar z}-\bar\eta)/\sqrt{\gamma}l_{B}
,
with
\gamma\equiv\absolutevalue{1/\tilde\nu-2}=1/q
for the filling
fraction
\tilde{\nu}=q/(2q+1)
. The effective Hamiltonian
operator of the CF is given by:

\hat{H}=:\epsilon_{b}\quantity(\hat r):,

(2)

where we express the binding energy as a function of

r^{2}\equiv\absolutevalue{z-\eta}^{2}
, which is then mapped to the operator

\hat{r}^{2}\equiv\gamma l_{B}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
. The colons
indicate the normal ordering of the ladder operators, which
places
\hat{a}^{\dagger}

\hat{a}

ordering of the ladder operators is a result of the particular
way that the binding energy is defined
[
2
]
.

The eigen-energies and eigen-states of
\hat{H}
can be
determined. It is easy to see that the number operator

\hat{n}\equiv\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}

[\hat{H},\hat{n}]=0

\Lambda
-levels as the eigenvalue of

-level is annihilated by the lowering operator

.
We thus have

\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)=f(z)\exp(z\bar{\eta}/2l_{b}^{2})
,
where

is a holomorphic function in
z

\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)

\psi_{n}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)=(\hat{a}^{\dagger n}/\sqrt{n!})\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)

\Lambda
-levels as a function of

[
3

\nu=7/3

The physical wave function of the
7/3

by applying the wave function ansatz of the CF theory, which maps
a many-body wave function of CFs to a physical wave function of

\Lambda

The electron wave function can then be obtained by overlapping

\Psi_{\text{CF}}

[

We obtain

(3)

We can obtain an explicit form of the wave function

For an even number of electrons, it is the fermionic Haffnian

Numerical verification.—

Using ED, we test our
conclusion for
\nu=7/3

a different topological shift

on a sphere. Moreover, the
pairing nature of the state suggests stability only for even
numbers of electrons. We therefore solve the ground state wave
functions of interacting electrons in both the disk and spherical
geometries. The results corroborate our conclusion well.

Non-quasiconvex to quasiconvex transition.—

To
further corroborate that the dispersion of CFs is a deterministic
underlying factor, we investigate two scenarios under which the
CF dispersion in the 1LL becomes quasiconvex. We anticipate that
the distinction between the 1LL and LLL should disappear when the
dispersion becomes quasi-convex. This is indeed observed.

The first scenario involves a different
h\quantity(r)
. We consider
CF
4
, which carries four quantized vortices and underlies the
filling fraction
11/5
. In this case, the binding energy can be
determined similarly to CF
2
, albeit using the wave function

\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}\quantity(\qty{z_i})=\prod_{i}z_{i}^{4}\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)^{5}
.
We find that the resulting binding energy is a quasiconvex
function of
r
, similar to that of CF
2
in the LLL, as shown
in Fig.
1
. This is consistent with the fact that the
11/5

state can be well described by the Laughlin wave function
[
21
,
24
]
.

The second scenario involves the change of the electron
interaction
v(r)
. We adopt the modified Coulomb interaction
investigated in Ref.
43
:

v_{\mathrm{c}}\quantity(r)=e^{2}/4\pi\epsilon\sqrt{r^{2}+w^{2}}
, where
w

is a parameter for characterizing the width of the quantum well
confining the two-dimensional system. We find that for CF
2

in the 1LL, the dispersion becomes quasiconvex for

w\gtrsim 0.7l_{B}
, as shown in Fig.
1
. This is consistent with
the ED results of Ref.
43
,
which show that the overlap between the exact ground state wave
function of the
7/3
state and the
1/3
Laughlin wave function
approaches unity for
w\gtrsim l_{B}
.

Summary and discussion.—

In summary, we show that
CF dispersion is a deterministic factor behind the peculiarities
of the FQHE in the 1LL. The deductive approach enables us to not
only explain the distinction between the
7/3
or
12/5
states
and their counterparts in the LLL, but also correctly predict the
evolution of electron states under varying conditions.

Remarkably, the Haffnian state, considered analogous to the
Pfaffian pairing state, can be linked to a non-interacting CF
state. The non-interacting nature also suggests that
quasi-electron or hole excitations of such a Haffnian ground
state should resemble those of the Laughlin state
[
44
]
, rather
than those expected from conformal field theory and model
Hamiltonian considerations
[
45
,
34
]
. This interpretation seems to be
consistent with the results of tunneling measurements
[
46
,
47
]
.

We need to point out that the single-particle mean-field approach
employed in this study may not be adequate in fully capturing
quantitative details of a state with strong pairing correlations,
as in the case of the Haffnian state. Numerically, the overlap
between the Haffnian wave function and the exact ground state
wave function for
\nu=7/3
is found to be moderate
[
32
]
. The
pairing correlations also complicate the self-consistent
determinations of the electron-vortex correlation function and
the CF dispersion
[
38
]
. Nevertheless, as pointed out in
Ref.
4
, it is not uncommon for a
qualitatively correct wave function to yield a low overlap with
the exact wave function. It is reasonable to expect that
considering the residual interaction between CFs could improve
the quantitative agreement
[
24
,
48
]
.

Notations

I.1
Bergman space

A wave function in the LLL can generally be written as
\psi(z)\exp(-|z|^{2}/4l_{B}^{2})
, where
\psi(z)
is a holomorphic function of the
particle coordinate
z\equiv x+\mathrm{i}y
, and
l_{B}\equiv\sqrt{\hbar/eB}

denotes the magnetic length.

Since the Gaussian factor is common for all states in the LLL, it is convenient
to represent the states using only the holomorphic part of the wave function.
In this work, the term “wave function” always refers to the holomorphic part,
i.e.,
\psi(z)
.

All normalizable holomorphic wave functions form a Bergman space. The inner
product in the space is defined as

\left\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\right\rangle=\int\differential\mu_{B}(\bm{z})\psi_{1}^{\ast}(z)\psi_{2}(z),

(S1)

where the integral measure is given by

\differential{\mu_B\qty(\boldsymbol{z})}\equiv\frac{\differential[2]{\boldsymbol{z}}}{2\pi l_{B}^{2}}\exp(-\abs{\boldsymbol{z}}^2/2l_B^2).

(S2)

The measure has a Gaussian weight, which compensates for the Gaussian factors
omitted in the holomorphic wave functions.

I.2
Reproducing kernel

One can define the reproducing kernel

K_{B}(z,\bar{\xi})=e^{z\bar{\xi}/2l_{B}^{2}},

(S3)

which is essentially the coordinate representation of the identity operator as
well as the projection operator of the Bergman space. The following identities
hold

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{B}(\bm{\xi})K_{B}(z,\bar{\xi})f(\xi)

\displaystyle=f(z),

(S4)

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{B}(\bm{\xi})K_{B}(z,\bar{\xi})\bar{\xi}^{k}f(\xi)

\displaystyle=(2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z})^{k}f(z).

(S5)

The second identity defines the projection of
\bar{z}
onto the LLL, underlying
the well known rule
\bar{z}\rightarrow 2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z}

[
1
]
.

I.3
Dipole picture

In the dipole picture, a CF is interpreted as a composite particle consisting of
an electron and a vortex. While the electron is confined in the physical Landau
level, the vortex is assumed to reside in a separate, fictitious Landau level
generated by a Chern-Simons magnetic field oriented in the opposite direction of
the physical magnetic field. The magnetic length of the fictitious Landau
level,
l_{b}
, is related to
l_{B}
by

\frac{1}{l_{b}^{2}}=\frac{2\tilde{\nu}}{l_{B}^{2}}.

(S6)

Further details of the dipole picture can be found in
Ref.
2
.

We can also define a Bergman space as well as its reproducing kernel for the
fictitious Landau level:

\displaystyle\differential{\mu_b\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta})}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\differential[2]{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{2\pi l_{b}^{2}}\exp(-\abs{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^2/2l_b^2),

(S7)

\displaystyle K_{b}(\bar{\eta},\zeta)

\displaystyle=e^{\bar{\eta}\zeta/2l_{b}^{2}}.

(S8)

The state of a composite fermion is thus described by a bivariate wave function

\psi(z,\bar{\eta})
, which depends on the complex electron coordinate
z
and the
complex-conjugate vortex coordinate
\bar{\eta}\equiv\eta_{x}-\mathrm{i}\eta_{y}
.

II
From the CF binding energy to the Haffnian wave
function

In this section, we supplement the details of the derivation from the CF binding
energy to the physical wave function of electrons.

II.1
Binding energy

The CF binding energy
\epsilon_{b}(r)
, determined in the main text, is
introduced in Sec. VB of Ref.
2
. It is defined such
that the energy functional of a CF can be written as

E=\int\differential{\mu_B\qty(\boldsymbol{z})}\differential{\mu_b\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\psi^{*}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)\int\differential{\mu_b\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}')}e^{\bar{\eta}\eta^{\prime}/2l_{b}^{2}}\epsilon_{b}\quantity(\boldsymbol{z};\bar\eta,\eta')\psi\quantity(z,\bar\eta'),

(S9)

where
\epsilon_{b}\quantity(\boldsymbol{z};\bar\eta,\eta')
is obtained from

\epsilon_{b}(r)
through analytic continuation by interpreting
r^{2}\equiv|\bm{z}-\bm{\eta}|^{2}
as
(\bar{z}-\bar{\eta})(z-\eta^{\prime})
.

II.2
Wave equation

The CF wave equation can be derived by applying the variational principle of
quantum mechanics. By minimizing the energy functional Eq. (
S9
) with respect to

\psi^{\ast}(z,\bar{\eta})
, subject to the normalization constraint
\int\differential\mu_{B}(\bm{z})\differential\mu_{b}(\bm{\eta})|\psi(z,\bar{\eta})|^{2}=1
, we obtain

\epsilon\psi(z,\bar{\eta})=\hat{H}\psi(z,\bar{\eta}),

(S10)

where the Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\left[\hat{H}\psi\right]\quantity(z,\bar\eta)

\displaystyle=\hat{P}^{\prime}\epsilon_{b}(\bm{z};\bar{\eta},\eta^{\prime})\psi(z,\bar{\eta})

(S11)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\differential{\mu_B\qty(\boldsymbol{z}')}\differential{\mu_b\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}')}\exp(\frac{z\bar z'}{2l_B^2})\exp(\frac{\bar\eta\eta'}{2l_b^2})\epsilon_{b}\quantity(\boldsymbol{z'};\bar\eta,\eta')\psi\quantity(z',\bar\eta'),

(S12)

where
\hat{P}^{\prime}
denotes the projection onto the physical and fictitious
Landau levels. It maps
\bar{z}
and
\eta^{\prime}
in
\epsilon_{b}(\bm{z},\bar{\eta},\eta^{\prime})
to the operators
\hat{\bar{z}}\equiv 2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z}

and
\hat{\eta}\equiv 2l_{b}^{2}\partial_{\bar{\eta}}
, respectively.

The ordering of operators after the mapping is dictated by the definition of the
projection explicitly given in Eq. (
S12
). We see that the projections for
\bm{z}

and
\bm{\eta}
take different forms, corresponding to the normal ordering and the
anti-normal ordering defined in Appendix B of Ref.
2
,
respectively. Consequently, after the projection,
\hat{\bar{z}}-\bar{\eta}

should always be placed to the left of
z-\hat{\eta}
.

The rules for promoting the binding energy
\epsilon_{b}(r)
to the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}
are summarized as follows:

•

Express
\epsilon_{b}(r)
as a function of
r^{2}
;

•

Replace
r^{2}
with the operator
(2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z}-\bar{\eta})(z-2l_{b}^{2}\partial_{\bar{\eta}})
;

•

Rearrange operators so that all
(2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z}-\bar{\eta})
’s are to
the left of
(z-2l_{b}^{2}\partial_{\bar{\eta}})
’s.

II.3
Ladder operators and
\Lambda
levels

Ladder operators for CF
\Lambda
levels, analogues to the ladder operators in
ordinary Landau levels, can be defined as

\displaystyle\hat{a}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}l_{B}}\quantity(z-2l_{b}^{2}\partial_{\bar\eta}),

(S13)

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}l_{B}}\quantity(2l_{B}^{2}\partial_{z} -\bar\eta),

(S14)

for a filling factor
\tilde{\nu}<1/2
. It is straightforward to verify the
commutation relation
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1
. Additionally, one can define
operators
\hat{b}
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}
to commute with
\hat{a}
and

\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and relate different states within a
\Lambda
-level. For
further details, see Appendix A of Ref.
2
.

Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
can then be expressed in terms of
\hat{a}
and

\hat{a}^{\dagger}
by mapping
r^{2}
to
\gamma l_{B}^{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
in the binding energy function

\epsilon_{b}(r)
, and arranging
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
operators
according to normal ordering. It yields Eq. (2) of the main text.

It is straightforward to verify that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
commutes with the
number operator
\hat{n}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
. Consequently,
\Lambda
-levels
can be defined as the eigen-states of the number operator and labeled by its
eigen-values.

II.4
Energies of
\Lambda
levels

It is straightforward to determine the energies of
\Lambda
levels by
expressing
\epsilon_{b}\quantity(r)
as a polynomial in
r^{2}
. Following the rules
outlined above, we make the substitution

r^{2k}\to\quantity(\gamma l_B^2)^{k}\hat{a}^{\dagger k}\hat{a}^{k}=\quantity(\gamma l_B^2)^{k}\hat{n}\quantity(\hat n-1)\cdots\quantity(\hat n-k+1)

(S15)

to promote the polynomial to a CF Hamiltonian. It results in a Hamiltonian
that is a function of
\hat{n}

[
3
]
. The eigen-energy of a
\Lambda
-level can
then be obtained by substituting
\hat{n}
in the Hamiltonian with the

\Lambda
-level index
n
.

II.5
Single-particle CF wave functions

The wave functions of the lowest
\Lambda
level, which has the lowest
eigen-value
n=0
for the number operator (rather than the energy), can be
determined by solving the equation
\hat{a}\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)=0
. The
solution is given by

\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)=f\quantity(z)\exp(\frac{z\bar\eta}{2l_b^2}),

(S16)

where
f\quantity(z)
is an arbitrary holomorphic function of
z
. It is convenient
to choose
f_{m}(z)\propto z^{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, to form a complete
basis set for the lowest
\Lambda
-level.

Wave functions for higher
\Lambda
levels with index
n>0
can be obtained by
applying
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
to
\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)
, as shown in the main
text. In particular, the wave function of the second
\Lambda
-level (
n=1
)
can be written as

\psi_{n}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\psi_{0}\quantity(z,\bar\eta)\propto\exp(\frac{z\bar\eta}{2l_b^2})\quantity(\partial_z-\frac{\bar\eta}{l^{2}})f\quantity(z),

(S17)

where
l=l_{B}/\sqrt{\tilde{\nu}\gamma}
denotes the effective magnetic length of
CFs.

II.6
Many body CF wave function

Many body CF wave functions
\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})
are
constructed by forming Slater determinants from single-particle CF wave
functions. For a fully-occupied lowest (
n=0
)
\Lambda
-level, using the
complete basis set defined above and Eq. (
S16
), we have:

\Psi^{0}_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})=\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)\prod_{i}\exp(\frac{z_i\bar\eta_i}{2l_b^2}).

(S18)

For the many-body CF wave function at
\nu=7/3
, where the second (
n=1
)

\Lambda
level is fully occupied, we apply Eq. (
S17
), and obtain:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\prod_{i}\quantity(2l_B^2\partial_{z_i}-\bar\eta_i)\Psi^{0}_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})

(S19)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\prod_{i}\exp(\frac{z_i\bar\eta_i}{2l_b^2})\quantity(2\partial_{z_i}-\frac{\bar\eta_i}{l^2})\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j).

(S20)

II.7
Electron wave function

The electron wave function is obtained by overlapping
\Psi_{\text{CF}}
with
the
1/2
Laughlin state of vortices
[
2
]
. We have:

\displaystyle\Psi\quantity(\qty{z_i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}\differential{\mu_{b}\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i)}\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})\prod_{i<j}\quantity(\eta_i-\eta_j)^{2}

(S21)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}\differential{\mu_{b}\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i)}\exp(\frac{z_i\bar\eta_i}{2l_b^2})\quantity(2\partial_{z_i}-\frac{\bar\eta_i}{l^2})\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)\quantity(\eta_i-\eta_j)^{2}.

To complete the integral, we note that the exponential factor is the complex
conjugate of the reproducing kernel of the
\bm{\eta}
-Bergman space Eq. (
S8
). The
integral maps
\eta_{i}
to
z_{i}
, and
\bar{\eta}_{i}
to

2l_{b}^{2}\partial_{z_{i}}
, as indicated by Eqs. (
S4
,
S5
). For
\nu=7/3
,
we have
l_{b}^{2}/l^{2}=1/2
. The electron wave function can then be written
as:

\Psi\quantity(\qty{z_i})\propto\lim\limits_{\{\eta_{i}\to z_{i}\}}\prod_{i}\quantity(2\partial_{z_i}-\partial_{\eta_i})\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)\quantity(\eta_i-\eta_j)^{2}.

(S22)

II.8
Haffnian wave function

To obtain an explicit form of the electron wave function, we first cast Eq. (
S22
)
into the form

\Psi\quantity(\qty{z_i})\propto\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)^{3}\lim\limits_{\eta\to z}\prod_{i}\quantity(\hat{\mathcal D}_i+\mathcal A_i),

(S23)

where we define
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\equiv 2\partial_{z_{i}}-\partial_{\eta_{i}}
and

\mathcal{A}_{i}\equiv 2\sum_{j\neq i}\quantity[1/\qty(z_i-z_j)-1/\qty(\eta_i-\eta_j)]
.

We then expand the product. Key observations are:

•

\lim\limits_{\eta\to z}\mathcal{A}_{i}=0
;

•

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\mathcal{A}_{j}=4/\quantity(z_i-z_j)^{2}+2/\quantity(\eta_i-\eta_j)^{2}
for

i\neq j
;

•

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\mathcal{A}_{k}=0
for
i\neq j\neq k
.

Consequently, non-zero combinations from the expansion must pair

\{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\}
and
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}
one by one, giving rise
to terms like
(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\mathcal{A}_{2})(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{3}\mathcal{A}_{4})\dots
and its permutations over the indices. Therefore, we have:

\Psi(\{z_{i}\})\propto\lim\limits_{\eta\to z}\sum_{\sigma\in P(N)}\prod_{i}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\sigma\quantity(2i-1)}\hat{A}_{\sigma\quantity(2i)})\propto\sum_{\sigma\in P(N)}\prod_{i}\frac{1}{\quantity(z_{\sigma\qty(2i-1)}-z_{\sigma\qty(2i)})^{2}},

(S24)

where the summation of
\sigma
is over
P(N)
, the set of all permutations of
\quantity{1,2,\cdots N}
. The final form is recognized as the Haffnian of a matrix
\mathcal{M}
with the
non-diagonal matrix elements
{\mathcal{M}}_{ij}=1/(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}
, which is denoted as

\mathrm{Hf}[1/(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}]
in the main text.

,

IV
Spherical geometry

IV.1
Landau levels

In the spherical geometry, a magnetic monopole with strength (the total number
of magnetic quantum fluxes)
2Q\in\mathbb{Z}
is placed at the center of a
sphere, generating a uniform magnetic field over its surface. It creates Landau
levels on the sphere, which are the eigenstates of the total angular momentum
operators
|\hat{\bm{L}}|^{2}
. A Landau level, indexed by a non-negative
integer
n\geq 0
, has the angular momentum quantum number
l=Q+n
and
degeneracy
2l+1

[
4
,
5
]
.

The system can be formulated in a form analogue to that of the disk geometry
using the stereographic projection
[
6
]

z=e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\tan\frac{\theta}{2},

(S25)

which maps a point with the polar angles
\quantity(\theta,\phi)
on a sphere of unit
diameter onto a complex plane. We introduce the operator
\hat{\Pi}_{Q}\equiv|\hat{\bm{L}}|^{2}-Q(Q+1)
. In terms of the coordinate on the plane, the
operator can be written as

\hat{\Pi}_{Q}=-\quantity(1+\abs{z}^{2})^{2}\quantity(\partial_{z}-Q\frac{\bar{z}}{1+\abs{z}^{2}})\quantity(\partial_{\bar{z}}+Q\frac{z}{1+\abs{z}^{2}}).

(S26)

Landau levels are eigenstates of
\hat{\Pi}_{Q}
, with eigenvalues

l(l+1)-Q(Q+1)
.

The wave functions of the LLL take the general form

\psi^{(Q)}_{0}(z)\left(1+|z|^{2}\right)^{-Q},

(S27)

where
\psi^{(Q)}_{0}(z)
is a holomorphic polynomial in
z
with a degree no
greater than
2Q
. The factor
(1+|z|^{2})^{-Q}
is analogous to the Gaussian
factor in the wave functions of the disk geometry. A complete basis set for the
LLL is given by the polynomials:

\psi^{(Q)}_{0,m}(z)\propto z^{m},\ m=0,1,2,\cdots 2Q.

(S28)

The wave functions of higher Landau levels can be constructed from those of the
LLL. For a system with monopole strength
2Q
, the Landau level with the index

n
has the degeneracy
2(Q+n)+1
, identical to the degeneracy of the LLL for a
monopole strength
2(Q+n)
. This allows for an one-to-one mapping between their
states. The wave function in the
n
-th Landau level for monopole strength
2Q

takes the form

\psi_{n}^{(Q)}(\bm{z})\left(1+|z|^{2}\right)^{-(Q+n)}.

(S29)

It can be related to the LLL wave function
\psi_{0}^{(Q+n)}(z)
for monopole
strength
2(Q+n)
by

\psi_{n}^{(Q)}(\bm{z})\propto\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat{A}_{2Q+n+i}\right)\psi_{0}^{(Q+n)}(z),

(S30)

where

\hat{A}_{q}\equiv(1+|z|^{2})\partial_{z}-q\bar{z},\,\,q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},

(S31)

acts as a raising operator for the spherical geometry. The following identity
holds:

\hat{\Pi}_{q}\hat{A}_{q+1}=\hat{A}_{q+1}\hat{\Pi}_{q+1}+2(q+1).

(S32)

Using the identity, it is straightforward to show that the wave function Eq. (
S27
)
constructed using Eq. (
S30
) is indeed an eigenstate of
\hat{\Pi}_{Q}
with the
expected eigenvalue for the
n
-th
\Lambda
level.

IV.2
Bergman space

We can define the Bergman space for the LLL on a sphere. It has the integral
measure:

\differential{\mu_{Q}\qty(\boldsymbol{z})}=\frac{\quantity(2Q+1)\differential[2]{\boldsymbol{z}}}{\pi}\quantity(\frac{1}{1+\abs{z}^{2}})^{2Q+2},

(S33)

and the reproducing kernel

K_{Q}\quantity(z,\bar\xi)=\quantity(1+z\bar\xi)^{2Q}.

(S34)

Projection identities on a sphere analogue to Eq. (
S5
) read

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}(\bm{\xi})K_{Q}(z,\bar{\xi})\frac{\bar{\xi}^{m}}{\left(1+\left|\xi\right|^{2}\right)^{m}}\psi(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2Q+1)!}{(2Q+1+m)!}\partial_{z}^{m}\psi(z),

(S35)

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{Q}(\bm{\xi})K_{Q}(z,\bar{\xi})\frac{\bar{\xi}^{m}}{\left(1+z\bar{\xi}\right){}^{m}}\psi(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2Q-m)!}{(2Q)!}\partial_{z}^{m}\psi(z).

(S36)

IV.3
\Lambda
levels on a sphere

For the dipole model of spherical geometry, we assume a monopole field strength

2Q
for electrons and a negative monopole strength
-2q=-2(N-1)
for vortices.

In the spherical geometry, just like the disk geometry,
\Lambda
-levels can be
defined as the eigen-states of the
\hat{r}^{2}
operator, where
r^{2}
is
interpreted as the squared chord distance between an electron and a vortex on
the surface of the sphere. The squared chord distance can be expressed in terms
of the stereographic coordinates of the electron and vortex:

r^{2}=\frac{\absolutevalue{z-\eta}^{2}}{\quantity(1+\abs{z}^2)\quantity(1+\abs{\eta}^2)},

(S37)

The
\hat{r}^{2}
operator is defined through a projection to the CF Bergman
space:

\hat{r}^{2}\psi\quantity(z,\bar{\eta})\equiv\int\differential{\mu_{Q}\qty(\bm{\xi})}\differential{\mu_{q}\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}')}K_{Q}(z,\bar{\xi})K_{q}(\bar{\eta},\eta^{\prime})\frac{(\bar{\xi}-\bar{\eta})(\xi-\eta^{\prime})}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+\bar{\eta}\eta^{\prime})}\psi\quantity(\xi,\bar{\eta}^{\prime}).

(S38)

By applying the projection identities Eqs. (
S35
,
S36
), we obtain the explicit
form of the operator:

\hat{r}^{2}=-\frac{\quantity(1+z\bar{\eta})^{2}}{4(Q+1)q}\quantity(\partial_{z}-2Q\frac{\bar{\eta}}{1+z\bar{\eta}})\quantity(\partial_{\bar{\eta}}-2q\frac{z}{1+z\bar{\eta}}).

(S39)

The
\hat{r}^{2}
operator can be related to the
\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}
operator for
ordinary Landau levels with monopole strength
2\mathcal{Q}\equiv 2(Q-q)
via an
orthogonal transformation combined with analytic continuation:

\hat{\Pi}_{\mathcal{Q}}\propto\evaluated{(1+z\bar{\eta})^{-Q-q}\hat{r}^{2}\qty(1+z\bar{\eta})^{Q+q}}_{\bar{\eta}\to\bar{z}}

(S40)

Thus, in the spherical geometry, similar to the disk geometry (see Appendix A of
Ref.
[
2
]
),
\Lambda
-level wave functions can be inferred from their
Landau-level counterparts using the relation:

\psi_{nm}(z,\bar{\eta})\propto\left.\psi^{(\mathcal{Q})}_{nm}(z,\bar{z})\right|_{\bar{z}\rightarrow\bar{\eta}}\left(1+z\bar{\eta}\right)^{2q-n},

(S41)

where
\psi_{nm}(z,\bar{\eta})
denotes a wave function of the
n
-th

\Lambda
-level, and
\psi^{(\mathcal{Q})}_{nm}(z,\bar{z})
a wave function of
the
n
-th Landau level.

IV.4
Many body CF wave function

Using Eqs. (
S28
,
S30
,
S41
), we can construct a complete basis set of the second

\Lambda
-level as follows:

\displaystyle\psi_{1,m}(z,\bar{\eta})

\displaystyle\propto\left(1+z\bar{\eta}\right)^{2q-1}\left[(1+z\bar{\eta})\partial_{z}-2(\mathcal{Q}+1)\bar{\eta}\right]z^{m}

(S42)

\displaystyle=K_{q}(z,\bar{\eta})\left(\partial_{z}-\frac{2(\mathcal{Q}+1)\bar{\eta}}{1+z\bar{\eta}}\right)z^{m},

(S43)

where we
K_{q}(z,\bar{\eta})
is the reproducing kernel defined in Eq. (
S34
).

The many body CF wave function
\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})
for
a fully occupied second (
n=1
)
\Lambda
-level is then written as

\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_{i},\bar{\eta}_{i}})\propto\prod_{i}K_{q}(z_{i},\bar{\eta}_{i})\left[\partial_{z_{i}}-\frac{2(\mathcal{Q}+1)\bar{\eta}_{i}}{1+z_{i}\bar{\eta}_{i}}\right]\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_{i}-z_{j}).

(S44)

IV.5
Electron wave function

The corresponding electron wave function is given by

\Psi\quantity(\qty{z_{i}})\propto\int\prod_{i}\differential{\mu_{q}\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i})}K_{q}(z_{i},\bar{\eta}_{i})\left[\partial_{z_{i}}-\frac{2(\mathcal{Q}+1)\bar{\eta}_{i}}{1+z_{i}\bar{\eta}_{i}}\right]\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_{i}-z_{j})\quantity(\eta_{i}-\eta_{j})^{2}.

(S45)

By applying Eq. (
S36
), it simplifies to

\Psi(\{z_{i}\})\propto\lim_{\{\eta_{i}\rightarrow z_{i}\}}\prod_{i}\left[\partial_{z_{i}}-\frac{\mathcal{Q}+1}{q}\partial_{\eta_{i}}\right]\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})(\eta_{i}-\eta_{j})^{2}.

(S46)

For a fully occupied second
\Lambda
-level, we have
2\mathcal{Q}+3=N
, and
using the identity
q=\quantity(N-1)
, it follows
(\mathcal{Q}+1)/q=1/2
. The
wave function then reduces to a form that is identical to Eq. (
S22
).

V
Exact diagonalizations

V.1
Disk geometry

We simulate a system of
N
interacting electrons on a disk of radius

R_{c}\equiv\sqrt{2N/\tilde{\nu}}
(with
l_{B}=1
). The disk contains a
neutralizing positive charge background with a uniform charge density within

R_{c}
. The presence of the neutralizing charge background induces a
single-body potential,
\Phi(r)
, experienced by electrons.

Exact diagonalizations are performed in a finite Hilbert space spanned by the
basis set
\phi_{m}(z)=z^{m}/\sqrt{m!}
, with
m<{\tilde{\nu}}^{-1}N+D
and
D\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. This allows electrons to extend beyond the disk radius by
D

additional orbits. For the results presented in the main text, we set
D=5
.

The interaction matrix elements can be determined analytically. For the LLL,
the explicit form for the Coulomb interacting matrix elements,

V_{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}m_{4}}
, is provided in Ref.
7
.
For the 1LL, the matrix elements
\tilde{V}_{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}m_{4}}
of the
effective interaction can be expressed in terms of
V_{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}m_{4}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}m_{4}}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\quantity(m_1+1)\quantity(m_3+1)}\left(\sqrt{\quantity(m_2+1)\quantity(m_4+1)}V_{m_{1}+1,m_{2}+1,m_{3}+1,m_{4}+1}-\quantity(m_2+m_4)V_{m_{1}+1,m_{2},m_{3}+1,m_{4}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sqrt{m_{2}m_{4}}V_{m_{1}+1,m_{2}-1,m_{3}+1,m_{4}-1}\right)

VII
Self-consistent determination of the CF dispersion

In the main text, we determine the dispersions of CF
2
for both the LLL and
1LL by assuming that electrons are in the
1/3
Laughlin state. While this is
self-consistent for the LLL, it is not for the 1LL, as its CF dispersion
suggests that
\nu=7/3
corresponds to a fermionic Haffnian state. Ideally, to
achieve full self-consistency, the dispersion should also be determined using
the Haffnian state.

Following the mean-field approach developed in Sec. VB of
Ref.
2
, we determine the electron wave function in
the presence of a vortex at the origin:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}\quantity(\qty{z_i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}\differential{\mu\qty(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i)}\eta_{i}^{2}\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})\prod_{i<j}\quantity(\eta_i-\eta_j)^{2}

(S49)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\{\eta_{i}\to z_{i}\}}\prod_{i}\quantity(2\partial_{z_i}-\partial_{\eta_i})\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)\quantity(\eta_i-\eta_j)^{2}\prod_{i}\eta_{i}^{2},

where
\Psi_{\text{CF}}\quantity(\qty{z_i,\bar\eta_i})
is given by Eq. (
S20
). The
electron density profile in the vicinity of the vortex can then be determined.

Following the steps outlined in Sec.
II
, we obtain the explicit form of
the wave function:

\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}\quantity(\qty{z_i})\propto\mathrm{lHf}(\mathcal{M})\prod_{i}z_{i}^{2}\prod_{i<j}\quantity(z_i-z_j)^{3},

(S50)

where
\mathcal{M}
is a matrix with elements
\mathcal{M}_{ij}=1/(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}
for

i\neq j
, and
\mathcal{M}_{ii}=-\sqrt{2/3}/z_{i}
.
\mathrm{lHf}(\mathcal{M})

denotes the loop Haffnian of the matrix
[
8
]
. The particular loop Haffnian can
also be written as a determinant:
\mathrm{lHf}({\mathcal{M}})=\mathrm{det}(\tilde{\mathcal{M}})
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a matrix
with elements
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{ij}=1/(z_{i}-z_{j})
for
i\neq j
, and

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{ii}=-\sqrt{2/3}/z_{i}
.

In Fig.
S4
, we present the CF dispersion and
\Lambda
level energies computed
using the loop Haffnian wave function. The dispersion is quasiconvex and
exhibits a steep increase with
r
. This is markedly different from the dispersion
obtained when assuming the Laughlin wave function.

We attribute the failure to the inadequacy of the mean-field approach, which
neglects the exchange symmetry between CFs. The resulting absence of Pauli
exclusion induces the issue, which is further amplified by the pairing nature of
the Haffnian state. To illustrate, consider the expansion of the loop Haffnian by its
diagonal elements:

\mathrm{lHf}\quantity(\mathcal M)=\sum_{s=0}^{N}\sum_{\{i_{1},i_{2}\cdots i_{s}\}}\frac{1}{z_{i_{1}}}\cdots\frac{1}{z_{i_{s}}}\times\mathrm{Hf}\quantity(m_{\{i_{1},i_{2}\cdots i_{s}\}}),

(S51)

where
\{i_{1},i_{2}\cdots,i_{s}\}
denotes a list of distinct indices,

m_{\{i_{1},i_{2}\cdots i_{s}\}}
is the matrix obtained by removing the columns
and rows with indices
i_{1},i_{2}\cdots i_{s}
in
\mathcal{M}
. The factor

1/z_{i_{1}}\cdots 1/z_{i_{s}}
indicates that there are
s
additional electrons
which have pairing correlations with the vortex at the origin, despite the
vortex already being bound to an electron which is not included in

\Psi^{\mathrm{v}}_{0}
, according to the assumption of the mean-field approach.
This is obviously an artifact that arises from the lack of Pauli-exclusion
between the vortex-bound electron and other electrons in the system. Its impact
is exacerbated by the
1/z
correlation, which is absent in the Laughlin wave
function.

To estimate the CF dispersion for the Haffnian state, we discard the
terms with
s\neq 0
in the expansion Eq. (
S51
), as these are considered unphysical.
The resulting wave function with a vortex is:

\Psi_{0}^{\mathrm{v}}\quantity(\qty{z_i})=\prod_{i}z_{i}^{2}\Psi^{\text{Hf}}\quantity(\qty{z_i}),

(S52)

where
\Psi^{\text{Hf}}\quantity(\qty{z_i})
is the fermionic Haffnian wave function
(Eq. [4] of the main text). The corresponding CF dispersion and
\Lambda
level
energies are shown in Fig.
S4
(a). The dispersion determined using the
Haffnian wave-function is indeed non-quasiconvex, although the minimum is
significantly shallower compared to that determined using the Laughlin wave
function.
