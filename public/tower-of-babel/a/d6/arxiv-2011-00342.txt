The Negative Reflection Principle and the Joint Distribution of the Telegraph Process and its Maximum
Fabrizio Cinque Department of Statistical Sciences, Sapienza University of Rome, Italy

Abstract
In this paper we study the joint distributions of the telegraph process and its maximum conditioned on the number of changes of direction and the initial velocity. We prove that in the case of positive starting velocity, a form of the reflection principle holds. We call it the negative reflection principle and we generalize it to the paths of the random motions moving with constant finite velocity. In the last section we obtain the conditional distribution of the first passage time of the telegraph process by using the known results on the maximum of the motion. Finally, we derive the distribution of the first returning time to the origin.



Keywords:
Telegraph Process; First Passage Time; Reflection Principle; Induction Principle

Introduction

The one-dimensional symmetric telegraph process describes the position of a particle starting from the origin
x=0
at time
t=0
and moving forwards and backwards on the real line. It moves alternatively with two finite constant velocity
+c
and
-c
, with
c>0
, and the initial speed is uniformly chosen between the two possible alternatives. The changes of direction are paced by a homogeneous Poisson process
\{N(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
of rate
\lambda>0
, meaning that the displacements of the particle are exponentially distributed with average length
c/\lambda
. By denoting the initial velocity of the motion with
V(0)\sim Unif\{-c,+c\}
, we can define the symmetric telegraph process
\{\mathcal{T}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
as follows

\mathcal{T}(t)\coloneqq V(0)\int_{0}^{t}(-1)^{N(s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=V(0)\sum_{i=0}^{N(t)-1}\Bigl(T_{i+1}-T_{i}\Bigr)(-1)^{i}+V(0)(-1)^{N(t)}\Bigl(t-T_{N(t)}\Bigr)

(1.1)

where
T_{i}
is the
i
-th arrival time of the Poisson process for
i=1,\dots,N(t)
and
T_{0}=0\ a.s.

The telegraph process is the prototype of the finite speed random motions and it has been formally presented in
[
12
]
. Several authors have studied the symmetric telegraph process (
1.1
), see
[
14
]
,
[
19
]
, as well as its generalizations. The most common one is the asymmetric motion with velocities
c_{1}
and
-c_{2},\ 0<c_{1}\not=c_{2}>0
, and two possible rates of reversals
\lambda_{1}\not=\lambda_{2}>0
, whose probability law was first obtained in
[
2
]
and has been further investigate in many papers, for instance
[
7
]
. We also recall the motion with Erlang distributed displacements, see
[
6
]
, and the motion describing a particle that uniformly chooses its velocity in the continuous set
[-c,c]
, see
[
4
]
.

Some researchers also undertook the study of the multidimensional extensions of the telegraph process. Among the most remarkable papers we recall
[
22
]
, where the author present a planar motion with orthogonal directions,
[
16
]
, regarding the planar random motion with infinite possible directions, and
[
21

regarding random motions with finite speed in spaces of dimension

.

The most relevant feature of the random motions with finite velocity is that they preserve the natural property of moving with finite speed along the same direction, therefore they are suitable to describe real motions and they emerge in several fields. For instance, in finance they can model the stock prices or the volatility of financial markets, see
[
15
]
,
[
23
]
, while in ecology, they model the displacements of wild animals on the soil, see
[

1.1

=P\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\ |\ N(t)=2k+2\}=\frac{(2k+1)!}{k!^{2}}\frac{(c^{2}t^{2}-x^{2})^{k}}{(2ct)^{2k+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(1.3)

for

, see

We also need to recall the fascinating result of

P\Big\{\max_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{T}(s)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta\ \Big|\ V(0)=c,\ N(t)=n\Big\}=2P\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta\ |\ N(t)=n\}.

(1.4)

The main result of this paper has been inspired by (
1.4

\beta\in[0,ct)
,
x\in(2\beta-ct,\beta]

\big\{\omega\in\Omega\ :s\mapsto X(\omega,s)\ s.t.\ V(\omega,0)=+c,\ N(\omega,t)=n,\ \max_{0\leq s\leq t}X(\omega,s)>\beta,\ X(\omega,t)=x\big\},

(1.5)

\big\{\omega\in\Omega\ :s\mapsto X(\omega,s)\ s.t.\ V(\omega,0)=-c,\ N(\omega,t)=n,\ X(\omega,t)=2\beta-x\big\},

(1.6)

where

is a finite constant velocity random motion (meaning the it moves with velocities

. We call this property

. This resembles the reflection principle, but the trajectories have opposite starting velocities. This means that the trajectories in the first set concerns a motion starting with the positive speed

In the third section we study the connection between the telegraph process at time

P_{n}^{\pm}\{\ \cdot\ \}\coloneqq P\{\ \cdot\ |\ V(0)=\pm c,\ N(t)=n\}.

We recall the incredible fact that the previous probability mass is independent of both the current time
t

We point out that the interested reader can easily obtain some other important conditional and unconditional distributions by means of the presented results. 2
The negative reflection principle

We begin by considering the positively initially oriented telegraph process and we present the intuition for the negative reflection principle of the telegraph process, which is the starting point of the whole study.

By considering the fundamental relationship (
1.4
), we can write

\displaystyle P_{n}^{+}\{\mathcal{T}(t)>\beta\}+P_{n}^{-}\{\mathcal{T}(t)>\beta\}

\displaystyle=2P\{\mathcal{T}(t)>\beta\ |\ N(t)=n\}=P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)>\beta\}

\displaystyle=P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)>\beta,\ \mathcal{T}(t)>\beta\}+P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)>\beta,\mathcal{T}(t)\leq\beta\},

for
n\in\mathbb{N},\ \beta\in[0,ct)
, thus

P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)>\beta,\ \mathcal{T}(t)\leq\beta\}=P_{n}^{-}\{\mathcal{T}(t)>\beta\}.

(2.1)

By conditioning on
N(t)=n
, each path of the telegraph process is uniquely determined by the Poisson times
T_{1},...,T_{n}
. Their joint random variable is uniformly distributed on the simplex since
N(t)
is a homogeneous Poisson process. This means that each trajectory of the process has the same probability (density). Thus relation (
2.1
) suggests that for each trajectory of the set
on the right-hand side there exits a trajectory in the set on the left-hand side. With this at hand, it is reasonable to study if there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the following sets of trajectories of the telegraph process,, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta\in[0,ct)

\{\omega\in\Omega\ :\ V(\omega,0)=+c,\ N(\omega,t)=n,\max_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{T}(\omega,s)>\beta,\ \mathcal{T}(\omega,t)\leq\beta\},

(2.2)

\{\omega\in\Omega\ :\ V(\omega,0)=-c,\ N(\omega,t)=n,\ \mathcal{T}(\omega,t)>\beta\},

(2.3)

with
\bigl(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P\bigr)
probability space.

In Theorem
3.1
we generalize result (
2.1
), by proving that for
\beta\in[0,ct)
and
x\in(2\beta-ct,\beta]

P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)>\beta,\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}=P_{n}^{-}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in 2\beta-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}

(2.4)

with natural
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We refer to this formula as the
negative reflection principle for the telegraph process
. The adjective
negative
is due to the inversion of the starting speed. We point out that the classical reflection principle, regarding Wiener process, does not take into account the initial speed of the motion, in fact Brownian motion has both infinite velocity and infinite rate of changes of direction.

Here, provide a proof of the negative reflection principle for a wide class of finite speed random motions, where the symmetric telegraph process is just a particular case.

We point out that in building a transformation between trajectories of a finite velocity stochastic motion, it is important not to create new constraints on the paths. For instance, to force a change of direction at a specific level is a serious mistake.

It is important to keep in mind that the negative reflection principle presented in Theorem
2.1
does not consider probabilities, it regards trajectories only. Result (
2.4
), that we prove in Theorem
3.1
, regarding the probabilities, is due to the uniform distribution of the arrival times in the case of the homogeneous Poisson process which implies that the trajectories have all the same probability (density).

The negative reflection principle holds for both continuous and discrete processes (it is sufficient to suitably adapt the point process
N(t)\,
and consider
X(t)
at the desired times only). For instance, the reader can prove that it holds in the case of simple random walks. In particular, for a simple symmetric random walk we can obtain an equality in terms of the probabilities since the sample paths of a symmetric random walk have all the same probability.

Example 2.1

(Simple symmetric random walk)
.

Let
S_{0}=0\ a.s.
and
S_{n}=S_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, with
X_{i}
independent uniformly distributed random variables in
\{-1,1\}
. Let
n\geq 2
, for
\beta\in\{0,1,...,n-2\}
and
x\in\{2(\beta+1)-n,...,\beta\}
we have that

\displaystyle P\{\max_{0\leq k\leq n}S_{k}>\beta,S_{n}=x\ |\ S_{1}=1\}

\displaystyle=P\{\max_{0\leq k\leq n-1}S_{k}>\beta-1,S_{n-1}=x-1\}

\displaystyle=P\{S_{n-1}=2\beta-x+1\}=P\{S_{n}=2\beta-x\ |\ S_{1}=-1\},

where in the second equality we used the known result on the maximum of a simple symmetric random walk, due to the classical reflection principle. The sets in the first and the fourth member respectively correspond to the sets (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), with no condition on the point process
N(t)
, in the case of random walks.

3
Joint distribution of the symmetric telegraph process and its maximum

In this section we present the conditional and unconditional joint distributions of the position of the telegraph particle at time
t>0
and its maximum in the time interval
[0,t]
. The results we present here, easily yield other conditional distributions of the symmetric telegraph process.

3.1
Motion starting with positive velocity

We now give a direct proof of the negative reflection principle for the telegraph process.

Theorem 3.1

(Negative reflection principle for the telegraph process)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{T}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a symmetric telegraph process. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)\leq\beta,\ \mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l l}0&if\ \beta<\max\{0,x\},\\
P_{n}^{+}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}-P_{n}^{-}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in 2\beta-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}&if\ \max\{0,x\}\leq\beta<\frac{ct+x}{2},\\
P_{n}^{+}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}&if\ \beta\geq\frac{ct+x}{2}.\end{array}\end{cases}

(3.1)

Proof.

When
N(t)=n=1
distribution (
3.1
) reduces to

P_{1}^{+}\{M(t)\leq\beta,\ \mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l l}0&\ if\ \beta<\frac{ct+x}{2},\\
P_{1}^{+}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}=\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{2ct}&\ if\ \beta\geq\frac{ct+x}{2},\end{array}\end{cases}

(3.2)

since the random variable
\mathcal{T}(t)
is uniformly distributed in
(-ct,ct)
if one Poisson event occurs in the time interval
[0,t]
, see (
1.3
) with
k=0
. To prove (
3.2
) we observe that the process reaches level
\beta
at time

, then it changes direction and it keeps moving with speed
-c
until time

where it will be located in
\beta-c(t-\frac{\beta}{c})=2\beta-ct=x
. Thus,
P_{1}^{+}\Big\{M(t)=\frac{ct+\mathcal{T}(t)}{2},\ \mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\Big\}=P_{1}^{+}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}
.

The first and third cases of (

and

P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)\leq\beta,\ \mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}=P_{n}^{+}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}-P_{n}^{-}\{\mathcal{T}(t)\in 2\beta-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}.

which coincides with result (

) when

) for all natural

n
even.

- Case 1. The motion has enough time to cross level

at time

\displaystyle P_{n}^{+}\{M(t)\leq\beta,\mathcal{T}(t)\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}=

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{\beta}{c}}\int_{t_{1}}^{\frac{ct-2\beta+x}{2c}+t_{1}}P_{n-2}^{+}\{M(t-t_{2})\leq\beta-2ct_{1}+ct_{2},\mathcal{T}(t-t_{2})\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-2ct_{1}+ct_{2}\}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times P\{T_{1}\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t_{1},T_{2}\in\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t_{2}|N(t)=n\}

Remark 3.6
.

From the distributions we displayed in this section, the interested reader can obtain some interesting conditional and unconditional probabilities.
