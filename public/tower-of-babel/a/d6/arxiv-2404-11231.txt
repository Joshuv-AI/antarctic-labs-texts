Binary forms with the same value set I
Étienne Fouvry, Peter Koymans

Abstract
Given a binary form F ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ X , Y ] F\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y] , we define its value set to be { F ⁡ ( x , y ) : ( x , y ) ∈ ℤ 2 } \{F(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\} . Let F , G ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ X , Y ] F,G\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y] be two binary forms of degree d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and with non-zero discriminant. In a series of three papers, we will give necessary and sufficient conditions on F F and G G to have the same value set. These conditions will be entirely in terms of the automorphism groups of the forms. In this paper, we will build the general theory that reduces the problem to a question about lattice coverings of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} , and we solve this problem when F F and G G have a small automorphism group. The larger automorphism groups D 4 D_{4} and D 3 , D 6 D_{3},D_{6} will respectively be treated in part II and part III.

Introduction

1.1
Origin of the problem

The idea of this work emerged when studying the work of Watson
[
24
]
where it is shown that if two positive definite binary quadratic forms
F
and
G
with real (not necessarily integral) coefficients have the same value set, which means
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, and are not
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent, then they respectively are
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to

c(X^{2}+XY+Y^{2})\text{ and }c(X^{2}+3Y^{2}),

(1.1)

for some real number
c>0
. Actually, Watson
[
25
]
realized that this result was already known to Delone (see for instance
[
6
, Theorem p.163]
). This is the reason why we would like to give the name
Delone–Watson forms
to the forms appearing in (
1.1
).

The case of indefinite binary quadratic forms, with integral coefficients and with equal images apparently seems to have not been discussed in the literature set apart from the work of Schering giving general criteria for two forms, definite or indefinite, to have the same value set (
[
20
]
, also mentioned in
[
7
, p. 26]
). The case of indefinite quadratic forms exhibits completely different situations as it will be shown in
[
9
]
by appealing to recent statistics on the class groups of real quadratic fields.

In this paper we are concerned with binary forms with degree at least
3
. For a precise statement of our results, we introduce the following notations and definitions.

If
d\geq 1
is an integer, we denote by
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
the set of binary forms
F=F(X,Y)
with degree
d
, with rational coefficients and with discriminant different from zero. The group
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
operates on
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
by the natural change of variables: if
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
belongs to
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
then, by definition

(F\circ\gamma)(X,Y):=F(aX+bY,cX+dY).

The
value set
(or
image
) of the form
F
is by definition
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2}):=\{F(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
and it satisfies the equality

(F\circ\gamma)(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})

(1.2)

for every
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. In this paper, we are concerned with the following question, which in some sense can be interpreted as the converse of (
1.2
) or an extension of the Delone–Watson result to higher degrees:

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F
and
G
be two forms of
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
such that
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Does there exist
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

such that

G=F\circ\gamma\,?

(1.3)

We can always suppose that
F
and
G
both belong to
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
, by multiplication by some integer different from zero, to eliminate the denominators.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
. A form
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
is said to be extraordinary if there exists
G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
satisfying
G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and no
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
satisfying
G=F\circ\gamma
. A form of
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
, which is not extraordinary, is called ordinary.

In Definition
1.1
, the word
extraordinary
has been chosen to indicate the scarcity of these forms, as it will appear below. The purpose of this paper is to classify extraordinary forms entirely in terms of elementary invariants attached to the form. It is more convenient to express the results in terms of the following two equivalence classes. For
F,G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
, we say that

•

F
and
G
have the
same value set
or
the same image
if and only if one has
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. This equivalence relation is denoted by
F\sim_{{\rm val}}G
and the equivalence class of
F
in
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
is denoted by
[F]_{\rm val}
,

•

F
and
G
are
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–
equivalent
(resp.
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–
equivalent
) if and only if there exists
\gamma
in
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
(resp.
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
) such that
G=F\circ\gamma
. This equivalence relation is denoted by
\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})}
(resp.
\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
) and the corresponding equivalence class is denoted by
[F]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})}
(resp.
[F]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
).

With these notations (
1.2
) is equivalent to the inclusion

[F]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\subseteq[F]_{\rm val},

and
F
is extraordinary if and only if one has the strict inclusion

[F]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\subsetneq[F]_{\rm val}.

To state our results when
d\geq 3
, we introduce some important algebraic notions attached to a binary form
F
: an element
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
is a
\mathbb{Q}
–
automorphism of the form
F

if it satisfies the equality
F\circ\gamma=F
. The set of all
\mathbb{Q}
–automorphisms of
F
forms the
group of automorphisms
, denoted
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
. We also have the conjugation formula

{\rm Aut}(F\circ\lambda,\mathbb{Q})=\lambda^{-1}{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})\,\lambda,\text{ for all }\lambda\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q}).

(1.4)

Similarly, we speak of
\mathbb{Z}
–automorphism of the form
F
and of the group
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Z})
, which is a subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
. The formula (
1.4
) remains true when replacing
\mathbb{Q}
by
\mathbb{Z}
.

If
G
and
H
are two subgroups of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
, the notation

G\simeq_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})}H\ (\text{resp. }G\simeq_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}H)

means that there exists an element

of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
(resp.
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
) such that
G=kHk^{-1}
. If it is the case, we say that
G
and

are
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–conjugate
(resp.
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–conjugate
). Similarly, we speak of elements of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
which are
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–conjugate
(resp.
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–conjugate
) and we use the same notation as for subgroups.

In Lemma
3.3
below, we explicitly give the complete list

of ten subgroups of

with the property that for every

, the group

is
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–conjugate to a unique element of

. The presentation of our results and of our proofs mandate the following partition of

into three subsets

\mathfrak{H}=\mathfrak{H}_{1}\sqcup\mathfrak{H}_{2}\sqcup\mathfrak{H}_{3},

(1.5)

with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{1}:=\{{\bf C}_{1},{\bf C}_{2},{\bf C}_{3},{\bf C}_{4},{\bf C}_{6},{\bf D}_{1},{\bf D}_{2}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{2}:=\{{\bf D}_{4}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{3}:=\{{\bf D}_{3},{\bf D}_{6}\}.

In the present paper, we are mainly interested with the possible existence of extraordinary forms
F
when
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
is
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–conjugate to one of the seven groups of the list
\mathfrak{H}_{1}
. The cases
\mathfrak{H}_{2}
and

require more delicate techniques concerning sets of lattices covering

. They will be treated in two forthcoming works

]
and
[

]
, for comments, see §

For
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, we introduce the following condition concerning a form
F
of
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})

(Ci):\text{ there exists }H\in\mathfrak{H}_{i}\text{ such that }{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})\simeq_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})}H.

1.2
Statement of the results

Here is the first characterization of extraordinary forms. Actually this corollary remains true if one replaces the condition
(C1)
concerning
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
by
(C2)
or
(C3)
. This will be respectively shown in the two forthcoming papers
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. We have

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
. Suppose that
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
satisfies the condition
(C1)
. Then
F
is extraordinary if and only if
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
contains an element

\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

(1.6)

with the following properties

1.

the order of
\sigma
is equal to
3
,

2.

the quadruple
(a,b,c,d)
of rational numbers satisfies one of the four properties

(a)

(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
,

(b)

(a,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
(b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\left(\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)
,

(c)

(a,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
(b,c)\in\left(\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\times\mathbb{Z}
,

(d)

(a,b,c,d)\in\left(\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4}
.

Since
\sigma
is of order
3
if and only if
a+d=-1
and
ad-bc=1
, each of the four possibilities (
2a
), (
2b
), (
2c
) and (
2d
) hold infinitely often. The only groups of the list
\mathfrak{H}_{1}
with cardinality divisible by
3
are
{\bf C}_{3}
and
{\bf C}_{6}
, thus we directly obtain the next consequence.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
d\geq 3
. There is no extraordinary form
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
such that
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
is
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–conjugate to
{\bf C}_{1}
,
{\bf C}_{2},

{\bf C}_{4},

{\bf D}_{1}
or
{\bf D}_{2}
.

Let
F
be an extraordinary form. In §
3.2.1
, we give a construction of a form
G
satisfying
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, but
G\not\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
. The formula depends on the condition
\eqref{1},\dots,\eqref{4}
satisfied by the form
F
.

The next criterion is the companion of Corollary
1.2
. It gives an explicit decomposition of
[F]_{\rm val}
in terms of distinct equivalence classes for
\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
. When
F
is extraordinary, this number of
{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
–equivalence classes is always two (a situation to be compared with (

)).

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
d\geq 3
. A form
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
satisfying
(C1)
is extraordinary if and only if there exists
G\in[F]_{\rm val}
such that

\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(G,\mathbb{Q}):\sigma^{3}={\rm id}\}\subseteq{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\textup{ and }3\mid|{\rm Aut}(G,\mathbb{Q})|.

(1.7)

Furthermore, we have a decomposition into two disjoint classes
\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
:

[F]_{\rm val}=[G]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\cup[H]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},

(1.8)

where
H
is the binary form
H(X,Y)=G(2X,Y)
.

Here and also in Theorem
1.5
below, we will replace the condition
(C1)
by
(C2)
or
(C3)
in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. Actually, these two corollaries are consequences of the following theorem, the proof of which occupies the most important part of this paper.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
d\geq 3
. Let
F_{1}\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
be an extraordinary form satisfying
(C1)
. Let
F_{2}\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
be such that

F_{1}\sim_{\rm val}F_{2}\textup{ and }F_{1}\not\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F_{2}.

(1.9)

Then we have
3\mid|{\rm Aut}(F_{1},\mathbb{Q})|
. Furthermore, there exist two forms
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that
G_{i}\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F_{i}
and such that

G_{1}(X,2Y)=G_{2}(X,Y)\textup{ or }G_{1}(X,2Y)=G_{2}(2X,2Y).

(1.10)

In the first case we have

\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(G_{1},\mathbb{Q}):\sigma^{3}={\rm id}\}\subseteq{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z}),

and in the second case, we have

\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(G_{2},\mathbb{Q}):\sigma^{3}={\rm id}\}\subseteq{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z}).

Remark 1.6
.

The second case case of (
1.10
) is symmetrical with the first one: the relation
G_{1}(X,2Y)=G_{2}(2X,2Y)
is equivalent to
G_{1}(X,Y)=G_{2}(2X,Y)
. Let, for
i\in\{1,2\}
,
H_{i}
be the binary form

H_{i}=G_{i}\circ\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The
H_{i}
are
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to the
G_{i}
and they satisfy the equality

H_{2}(X,2Y)=H_{1}(X,Y).

We recognize the first case of (
1.10
) after inverting the indices.

It is also natural to wonder what happens for
coprime value sets
. This question was also considered by Watson
[
24
, p. 73]
, who observed that the Delone–Watson forms have different coprime value sets.

More formally, define

W(F)=\{F(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb{Z},\gcd(x,y)=1\}.

This induces an equivalence relation
\sim_{\text{co}}
on the set of binary forms by setting
F\sim_{\text{co}}G
if
W(F)=W(G)
. We have inclusions

\displaystyle[F]_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\subseteq[F]_{\text{co}}\subseteq[F]_{\text{val}}.

(1.11)

The first inclusion uses that
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
permutes the set of primitive elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, while the second inclusion uses homogeneity. The following result shows that the situation becomes completely rigid for coprime value sets.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
. Then we have
[F]_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}=[F]_{\textup{co}}
.

In words, a binary form is completely determined (up to
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
) by its coprime value set. Our proof of Theorem
1.7
will rest in an essential way on our results for
\sim_{\text{val}}
. We will prove Theorem
1.7
in §
12
.

A second variation of the notion of equality of value sets concerns
value sets with multiplicities
that was suggested to us by Michel Waldschmidt. For
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
and
m
an integer different from zero, we introduce the finite number

R(F;m):=|\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:F(x,y)=m\}|.

We say the forms
F
and
G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
have the
same value sets with multiplicities
if for every
m\neq 0
, one has the equality

R(F;m)=R(G;m).

If this condition is satisfied, we write
F\sim_{{\rm mu}}G
and the analogue of (
1.11
) is the following straightforward inclusions between equivalence classes

[F]_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\subseteq[F]_{\text{mu}}\subseteq[F]_{\text{val}}.

Here also the situation becomes completely rigid for value sets with multiplicities.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Then we have
[F]_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}=[F]_{\textup{mu}}
.

This means that a binary form is completely determined (up to
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
) by its value set with multiplicities. The proof of Theorem
1.8
will rest in an essential way on our results for
\sim_{\text{val}}
. We will prove Theorem
1.8
in §
13
.

1.3
Some comments and illustrations

We will frequently meet the matrix

R:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(1.12)

This matrix is of order
3
and it generates the cyclic subgroup of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
called
{\bf C}_{3}:=\{{\rm id},R,R^{2}\}
, see Lemma

.

Example 1.9
.

We return to the Delone–Watson forms appearing in (
1.1
), with
c=1
. We respectively call them
F
and
G
. By computing their discriminants, these forms are not
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent. We have the equalities

(G\circ R^{2})(X,Y)=(-X-Y)^{2}+3X^{2}=4X^{2}+2XY+Y^{2}=:H(X,Y)

with
H(X,Y)=F(2X,Y)
. So we have
G\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}H
and
F\not\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}H
. To prove that
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, it is sufficient to prove that
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=H(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. We obviously have
H(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\subseteq F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. For the opposite inclusion, we use the fact that
F=F\circ R=F\circ R^{2}
, which implies that, for any integers
m
and
n
, we have the equalities

F(m,n)=F(n,-m-n)=F(-m-n,m).

(1.13)

For any
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, at least one of the integers
m
,
n
and
-m-n
is even, thus we can always write
F(m,n)=F(2m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=F(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
for some integers

and

. So we have the equality
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=H(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. By (

) we have the decomposition

[F]_{\rm val}=[G]_{\rm val}=[H]_{\rm val}=[F]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\cup[H]_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}.

Such a decomposition, in degree

, shows the analogy between the content of Corollary

in degree

and the Delone–Watson result.

Example 1.11

Consider now the cubic form

0&1\end{pmatrix}

It is natural to wonder if there are “infinitely many different” extraordinary forms. We make this precise in the following extension of Definition
1.1
.

Definition 1.14
.

Let
d\geq 3
. A form
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
is said to be extraordinary if there exists
G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
satisfying
G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and no
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
satisfying
G=F\circ\gamma
. The pair
(F,G)
is called a pair of linked extraordinary forms.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
,
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
. Then the form

a(F\circ\gamma)

is extraordinary if and only if
F
is extraordinary. Let
(F,G)
and
(F^{\prime},G^{\prime})
be two pairs of linked extraordinary forms. These two pairs are called equivalent if there exists
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
and
\gamma,\delta\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

F^{\prime}=aF\circ\gamma\textup{ and }G^{\prime}=aG\circ\delta.

By (
1.1
), we know that there exist only two non-equivalent pairs of linked extraordinary forms, if one restricts to the set of definite (positive or negative) quadratic forms. As we prove below, this is not the case in the set of binary cubic forms.

Corollary 1.15
.

There are infinitely many non-equivalent pairs of linked extraordinary forms in
{\rm Bin}(3,\mathbb{Q})
.

Proof.

We adopt the notations of Example
1.10
to consider the form

\Phi_{b}(X,Y):=F_{1,b}(X,Y)=X^{3}+bX^{2}Y+(b-3)XY^{2}-Y^{3},

where
b
is an integer. The discriminant of
\Phi_{b}
is the polynomial

{\rm disc}(\Phi_{b})=b^{2}(b-3)^{2}-18b(b-3)-27+4b^{3}-4(b-3)^{3}=(b^{2}-3b+9)^{2}.

Since
{\rm disc}(\Phi_{b})\neq 0
, we have
\Phi_{b}\in{\rm Bin}(3,\mathbb{Z})
. As it was proved in Example
1.10
, we have
{\rm Aut}(\Phi_{b},\mathbb{Q})={\bf C}_{3}.
Thanks to Corollary
1.4
we know that
(\Phi_{b}(X,Y),\Phi_{b}(2X,Y))
is a pair of linked extraordinary forms.

Let
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
be such that
\Phi_{b^{\prime}}=a\Phi_{b}\circ\gamma
. By using the transformation formulas of the discriminant, we have

{\rm disc}(\Phi_{b^{\prime}})=a^{4}\det(\gamma)^{3(3-1)}{\rm disc}(\Phi_{b})=a^{4}{\rm disc}(\Phi_{b}).

By classical analytic number theory we may find infinitely many
b
such that
b^{2}-3b+9>1
is squarefree. By the transformation formula, the resulting
\Phi_{b}
for such
b
can never be equivalent, and the corollary follows.
∎

A first list of definitions and lemmas regarding binary forms

Let
F
be a binary form with degree
d\geq 3
. The area of its fundamental domain is

A_{F}:=\iint_{|F(x,y)|\leq 1}dxdy.

We have
0<A_{F}<\infty
. Furthermore, for every
\gamma
in
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, we have the equality

A_{F\circ\gamma}=|\,\det\gamma\,|^{-1}\cdot A_{F}.

(2.1)

Our first lemma bounds the number of solutions to
F(x,y)=m
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
c=c(F,\varepsilon)
such that for every integer
m\neq 0
, the number of solutions in unknown integers
(x,y)
to the equation
F(x,y)=m
is less than
c|m|^{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in several steps.

\bullet
If
F
is irreducible and if we restrict to counting primitive solutions, which means with the restriction
{\rm gcd}(x,y)=1
,
this is a direct consequence of
[
8
, Theorem 6.4]
and
[
3
, Theorem]
containing improvements in the constant
c
.

\bullet
To drop the condition
{\rm gcd}(x,y)=1
, we use the homogeneity of
F
to consider at most
\tau(|m|)
equations of the following form

F(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=m/\kappa^{d},

where
\tau(n)
is the number of positive divisors of the integer
n\geq 1
, where
\kappa\geq 1
is an integer satisfying
\kappa^{d}\mid m
and where the unknowns
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
now are coprime integers. Summing over all the possible
\kappa
we obtain the result.

\bullet
Suppose now that
F
is reducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. We factorize it as
F_{1}=F_{1}\cdots F_{t}
, where
t\geq 2
, where the
F_{i}
are distinct and irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
and where
1\leq\deg F_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\deg F_{t}.

– If
\deg F_{t}\geq 3
, the equality
F(x,y)=m
implies that
F_{t}(x,y)=m^{\prime}
, where
m^{\prime}
is some positive or negative divisor of
m
. Then apply the above part of the discussion to the equation
F_{t}(x,y)=m^{\prime}
and sum over all the possible
m^{\prime}\mid m
to get the required bound.

– If
\deg F_{t}\leq 2
, we necessarily have
t\geq 2
. The number of solutions to
F(x,y)=m
is less than the total number of solutions to the two simultaneous equations
F_{t}(x,y)=m_{1}
and
F_{t-1}(x,y)=m_{2}
, where the product
m_{1}m_{2}
divides
m
. When
m_{1}m_{2}
is fixed, we find at most four solutions to this pair of equations, since it corresponds to the intersection of two distinct conics, of a conic and a line, or of two distinct lines. Summing over all the possible pairs
(m_{1},m_{2})
such that
m_{1}m_{2}\mid m
, we complete the proof.
∎

Let
X\geq 1
be a real number. We introduce the counting function

N(F,X):=|\{m:|m|\leq X,m=F(x_{1},x_{2})\text{ for some }(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}|.

(2.2)

We will use the following weak information on this function (for a precise study see
[
23
]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
c(\varepsilon)>0
and
B_{0}(\varepsilon)\geq 1
, such that, for
B\geq B_{0}(\varepsilon)
one has the inequality

N(F,B)\geq c(\varepsilon)B^{2/d-\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Let
\eta:=(\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}|a_{i}|)^{-1}
be the inverse of the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of the form
F(X,Y)=\sum_{i}a_{i}X^{i}Y^{d-i}
. Then for
|x|,|y|\leq(\eta B)^{1/d}
, one has the inequality
|F(x,y)|\leq B
. So we exhibited a set
\mathcal{E}
of
\gg B^{2/d}
pairs of integers
(x,y)
such that
|F(x,y)|\leq B
. Furthermore, we exclude from
\mathcal{E}
all the pairs
(x,y)
such that
F(x,y)=0
. This number is readily verified to be
O(B^{1/d})
, as they correspond to the rational linear factors of the form
F
.

Finally, to bound the number of distinct values of
F(x,y)
we use Lemma
2.1
. It asserts that the number of solutions to the equation
F(x,y)=m
, where the unknowns are the integers
(x,y)
and where
m\neq 0
is a parameter, is bounded by
\ll_{\varepsilon}|m|^{\varepsilon}
. It remains to combine these informations to complete the proof.
∎

A result of Fouvry and Waldschmidt, see
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
, shows that the intersection of the images of two forms, not
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–equivalent, is much smaller than each of these images.

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
d\geq 3
, there exists a constant
\kappa_{d}<2/d
such that for every
F,G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
, which are not
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–equivalent, and for every

, we have the upper bound

\left|\{m:|m|\leq N,m=F(x_{1},x_{2})=G(x_{3},x_{4})\textup{ for some }(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\}\right|=O_{F,G}\left(N^{\kappa_{d}}\right).

Combining Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
, we deduce

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
be such that
F_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=F_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Then there exists
\rho\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
such that

F_{1}\circ\rho=F_{2}.

(2.3)

This lemma answers positively the question (
1.3
), if we impose the much weaker condition
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
instead of
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

The goal of this section is to deduce Corollary

3.1
A second list of definitions and lemmas

We generalize the notion of automorphism as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
R\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{C}\}
. Let
d\geq 3
and let

. An element
\rho\in{\rm GL}(2,R)
is said to be an isomorphism from
F
to
G
if it satisfies the equality

F\circ\rho=G.

The set of all isomorphisms from
F
to
G
is denoted by
{\rm Isom}(F\rightarrow G,R)
. We have

{\rm Isom}(F\rightarrow F,\mathbb{C})={\rm Aut}(F,R).

The next lemma is obvious.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F,G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{C})
. We have the equality

{\rm Isom}(G\rightarrow F,\mathbb{C})={\rm Isom}^{-1}(F\rightarrow G,\mathbb{C}),

and the conjugation formula

{\rm Isom}(F\circ\gamma\rightarrow G\circ\delta,\mathbb{C})=\gamma^{-1}{\rm Isom}(F\rightarrow G,\mathbb{C})\delta

for any
\gamma,\delta\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

Suppose that
{\rm Isom}(F\rightarrow G,\mathbb{C})
is not empty. Let
\rho
be one of its elements. We then have the equality

{\rm Isom}(F\rightarrow G,\mathbb{C})=\rho\circ{\rm Aut}(G,\mathbb{C})={\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{C})\circ\rho.

Theorem
1.5
shows the crucial importance of the group
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
. As announced by (
1.5
), the following lemma recalls all the possibilities for such a group. We adopt the notations of
[

, Table 1, p.137]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

and let

. Then

is
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
–conjugate to one of the ten following subgroups

Let

Let

Lemma

\text{ either }\ \ F=G\circ\gamma\ \text{ or }\ F=G\circ\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\circ\gamma

(3.7)

for some
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. We will use the conjugation property (
1.4
).

•

If

, it is clear that

contains
\sigma:=\gamma^{-1}R\gamma
. This element

has order 3, has integer coefficient and satisfies (
2a
).

•

Now suppose that we are in the second case of (

). Since

belongs to
{\rm Aut}(G,\mathbb{Q})
, a non trivial element
of
{\rm Aut}(F,\mathbb{Q})
is

\gamma^{-1}\circ\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\circ R\circ\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\circ\gamma=\gamma^{-1}\circ\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\
-2&-1\end{pmatrix}\circ\gamma.

Writing

\gamma:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix},

where the

are integers such that
a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}=\pm 1
, we get

\displaystyle\gamma^{-1}\circ\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\
-2&-1\end{pmatrix}\circ\gamma

\displaystyle=\pm\begin{pmatrix}a_{4}&-a_{2}\\
-a_{3}&a_{1}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\
-2&-1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\pm\begin{pmatrix}2a_{1}a_{2}+a_{3}(a_{2}+a_{4}/2)&2a_{2}^{2}+a_{4}(a_{2}+a_{4}/2)\\
-2a_{1}^{2}-a_{3}(a_{1}+a_{3}/2)&-2a_{1}a_{2}-a_{4}(a_{1}+a_{3}/2)\end{pmatrix}.

Since
a_{3}
and
a_{4}
are not simultaneously even, the above matrix corresponds to the cases (
2b
), (
2c
) or (

) of Corollary
1.2
. This proves the
only if
part.

The proof of Corollary
1.2
is complete assuming the truth of Corollary
1.4
.

4
From Theorem
1.5
to Corollary
1.4

We adopt the hypotheses of Corollary
1.4
. So let

with

(C1)
. Let us first show that
[F]_{\rm val}
breaks into at most two equivalence classes under
\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
[F]_{\rm val}

,
F_{2}
and

–equivalent by Lemma

. So write

F_{2}=F_{1}\circ\gamma_{1},\quad F_{3}=F_{2}\circ\gamma_{2}

with

1.5
to the forms

We now claim that

. The

, applied to the form

. The

.

The final part of Corollary

.

5
Strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.5

We now pass to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. This proof is quite complicated, so we take some time to present its main steps.

We start from two forms
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
satisfying (
1.9
), i.e.
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have the same image but are not
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent. By Lemma
2.4
, there exists some
\rho\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})\setminus{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

This
\rho
is not unique, however we choose a
\rho
or
\rho^{-1}
, which, in some sense, is economical in terms of denominators (see (
7.1
)). Bringing
\rho
into Smith normal form leads to the existence of two forms
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
and integers
D
and
\nu
such that
G_{2}(DX,DY)=G_{1}(X,\nu Y)
(see (
7.4
)). We are now dealing with integers only. By construction
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have the same image, but they are not
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent. This implies the conditions (
7.10
) on
D
and
\nu
.

It is now natural to consider not only the projective surface
\mathbb{W}(G_{1}\cap G_{2})
defined by the equation
G_{1}(X,Y)-G_{2}(Z,T)=0
, but also the set of integers

\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:m=G_{1}(x,y)=G_{2}(z,t)\text{ for }(x,y,z,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\}.

Actually, we will prove that most of the

come from points situated on lines of the surface
\mathbb{W}(G_{1}\cap G_{2})
. Again by a counting process based on the geometry of varieties over
\mathbb{Q}
, we exhibit a non–trivial covering
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by lattices corresponding to the lines mentioned above.

These lines are defined by the isomorphisms between

and
G_{2}
(see Proposition
8.5
). The equality
G_{2}(DX,DY)=G_{1}(X,\nu Y)
produces one such obvious isomorphism. We obtain all the other by composing with automorphisms of
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
. Proposition
9.1
is crucial by giving an explicit covering
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by lattices. For the moment we did not use the information on the structure of the automorphism groups of the forms
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Recall that we have

{\rm Aut}(G_{i},\mathbb{Q})\simeq_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}{\rm Aut}(F_{i},\mathbb{Q})\quad(i\in\{1,2\})\quad\text{ and }\quad{\rm Aut}(F_{1},\mathbb{Q})\simeq_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})}{\rm Aut}(F_{2},\mathbb{Q}).

The end of the proof depends on the complexity of
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, its cardinality (number of lattices belonging to
\mathcal{C}
) is at most
|{\rm Aut}(F_{1},\mathbb{Q})|/2
if
-{\rm id}
belongs to

, and
|{\rm Aut}(F_{1},\mathbb{Q})|
otherwise (see Lemma

). By Lemma

this cardinality can take the values

,
2
,
3
,
4
and

. In this paper, we are concerned with the cardinalities

,
2
and

, corresponding to the condition

. The condition

avoids substantial difficulties coming from the complexity of the covering

, since there is no non–trivial covering
\mathcal{C}
with cardinality

or

. For cardinality

\mathcal{C}
(see Lemmas

&

).

When the cardinality of

is

\mathcal{C}
with cardinality

(proving this in itself is already a non-trivial task). It corresponds to the condition

and it will be treated in

]
by proving that there is no extraordinary form satisfying

[

]
.

Finally, when the cardinality of

to have a cardinality equal to

.

6
A third list of lemmas

6.1
About lattices

In our work, a
lattice
means a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with rank two. Let
\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{k}
, be
k
lattices. We say that the lattices
(\Lambda_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
are a
covering
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
when their union equals
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. A finite intersection of lattices is also a lattice. A lattice
\Lambda
is a
proper lattice
if and only if
\Lambda\neq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The
index
of
\Lambda
is the positive integer

[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\Lambda]=|\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\Lambda|,

and a lattice is proper if and only if its index is different from one. If
(\vec{u},\vec{v})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of
\Lambda
, then we have

[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\Lambda]=|\det(\vec{u},\vec{v})|.

(6.1)

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
. The lattice
L(\gamma)
associated with
\gamma
is by definition

L(\gamma)=\left\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\gamma(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}.

Since the elements of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
are bijections on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and preserve the index of lattices, we have

Lemma 6.2
.

For every
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
and for every
P,Q\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, one has the equalities

L(P\gamma)=L(\gamma),\quad L(\gamma Q)=Q^{-1}(L(\gamma))\ \ \textup{ and }\ \ \left[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(P\gamma Q)\right]=\left[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(\gamma)\right].

We now give several lemmas. We will omit the proofs.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
. Then

•

the equality
L(\gamma)=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
holds if and only if all entries of
\gamma
are integers,

•

the equalities
L(\gamma)=L(\gamma^{-1})=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
hold if and only if
\gamma
belongs to
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Lemma 6.4
.

We have
L(-\gamma)=L(\gamma)
for every
\gamma\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
.

Lemma 6.5
.

For every lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
there exists an integer
C\geq 1
such that
C\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subseteq\Lambda
.

Proof.

There exists a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis
(\vec{u},\vec{v})
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and positive integers
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
such that
(d_{1}\vec{u},d_{2}\vec{v})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
–basis of
\Lambda
. Choose
C=d_{1}d_{2}
for instance.
∎

6.2
Covering
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with lattices

We are now concerned with coverings made with two or three lattices.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
(\Lambda_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 2}
be a covering. Then at least one of these lattices is not proper.

Proof.

Consider the three points
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
and
(1,1)
. Then at least one of the
\Lambda_{i}
, say
\Lambda_{1}
, contains at least two of these three points. Then for any choice, these two points generate
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Thus
\Lambda_{1}
is not proper.
∎

We now study the covering of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by three lattices.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\Lambda_{0}
,
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be three proper lattices such that
(\Lambda_{i})_{0\leq i\leq 2}
is a covering. Then, up to permutation, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:u\equiv 0\bmod 2\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:v\equiv 0\bmod 2\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:u+v\equiv 0\bmod 2\}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the above proof with the difference that we consider four points. Let
\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
be three proper sublattices satisfying

\Lambda_{0}\cup\Lambda_{1}\cup\Lambda_{2}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

Define the following four points in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

P_{1}=(1,-1),\quad P_{2}=(1,0),\quad P_{3}=(1,1),\quad P_{4}=(0,1).

By appealing to (
6.1
), we check that for all distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,4\}

P_{i}\mathbb{Z}\oplus P_{j}\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\{i,j\}\neq\{1,3\}.

(6.2)

By the pigeonhole principle one of the
\Lambda_{i}
must contain at least two
P_{k}
. Up to renumbering we may assume that
\Lambda_{2}
contains at least two
P_{k}
. But since
\Lambda_{2}
is a proper sublattice, this forces

\Lambda_{2}=P_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus P_{3}\mathbb{Z}=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:u+v\equiv 0\bmod 2\}.

By (
6.2
) we also get that
(0,1)\in\Lambda_{0}
and
(1,0)\in\Lambda_{1}
, after a potential renumbering.

Now look at the points
(2,1)
and
(1,2)
. The point
(2,1)
is not in
\Lambda_{2}
, so it must be that
\Lambda_{0}
or
\Lambda_{1}
contains
(2,1)
. But since
\Lambda_{1}
is a proper sublattice containing
(1,0)
, we see that
(2,1)\not\in\Lambda_{1}
. Therefore we conclude that
(2,1)\in\Lambda_{0}
and similarly
(1,2)\in\Lambda_{1}
. This gives the lemma.
∎

The description of the coverings of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by four, five or six lattices is much more intricate. It will be given in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. See also the end of §
5
for more details.

6.3
About matrices

The first lemma gives a
canonical
way to express an element of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
in terms of integers.

Lemma 6.8
.

Every matrix
M\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
can be uniquely written as

M=\frac{N}{D}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
m_{3}&m_{4}\end{pmatrix},

where the integers
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
,
m_{3}
,
m_{4}
,
N
and
D
satisfy the conditions

N,D\geq 1,\quad\gcd(N,D)=1,\quad\gcd(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},m_{4})=1.

In particular when
\det M=\pm 1
, one has
N=1
.

Proof.

This is straightforward.
∎

We use the canonical form to compute the index of the lattice from Definition
6.1
:

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
f\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})
, whose associated matrix
M_{f}
has canonical form given by

M_{f}:=\frac{N}{D}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
m_{3}&m_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Then the lattice
L(f)
satisfies the equality

[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(f)]=\frac{D^{2}}{\gcd(D,m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3})}.

Finally,
[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(f)]
is an integral multiple of
|\det f|^{-1}
.

Proof.

Use the Smith normal form (see
[
5
, Theorem 2.4.12]
for instance) to write the matrix
M_{f}
as

M_{f}=\frac{N}{D}\cdot U\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\pm(m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3})\end{pmatrix}\cdot V,

where
U
and
V
belong to
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Then by Lemma
6.2
we have

[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(f)]=[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L],

where
L
is the lattice

L:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\bigl((N/D)x,(N/D)(m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3})y\bigr)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}.

Since
\gcd(N,D)=1
, the index of
L
equals
D\cdot D/\gcd(D,m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3})
. For the last part, notice that our formula implies that
[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(f)]
is an integral multiple of
D^{2}/(m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3})
, which is a multiple of
D^{2}/(N^{2}(m_{1}m_{4}-m_{2}m_{3}))=1/\det f
.
∎

6.4
About values of polynomials and application to binary forms

The following lemma has appeared in various places in the literature. Bhargava attributes this result to Pólya
[
17
]
.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
f(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
be a primitive polynomial of degree
k\geq 0
. Then

\gcd(\{f(a):a\in\mathbb{Z}\})\mid k!.

Proof.

This statement is trivial when
k=0
. For
k\geq 1
, see
[
1
, Theorem 2]
for a proof.
∎

We apply this result to situations concerning binary forms.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer and let
H(X,Y)
be a binary form with integer coefficients and with degree
d
. Suppose that there exists positive integers
D
,
N
and
\nu
such that
\gcd(D,N)=1
and such that

D^{d}H(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=\left[N^{d}H\circ\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}\right](\mathbb{Z}^{2}).

(6.3)

Then we have
N=1
and
D\mid\nu
.

Proof.

By dividing
H
by the gcd of its coefficients, we may assume that
H
is primitive. Since
D
and
N
are coprime, we deduce from the hypothesis that

H(m,n)\equiv 0\bmod N^{d}

for all integers
m
and
n
. Assume for the sake of contradiction that
N>1
and let
p
be a prime dividing
N
. Consider the polynomial
P(T):=H(T,1)
, which is a primitive polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[T]
with degree
d^{\prime}
satisfying
0\leq d^{\prime}\leq d
. By definition of
p
we have

P(t)=H(t,1)\equiv 0\bmod p^{d}

for every integer
t
. Applying Lemma
6.10
, we deduce that
p^{d}\mid d^{\prime}!\mid d!
. Such a divisibility property never happens since, by denoting the
p
–adic valuation by
v_{p}
, we have the inequality
v_{p}(d!)<d
, for every prime
p
and every integer
d\geq 1
. The hypothesis
N>1
leads to a contradiction. Therefore we have
N=1
.

To prove that
D\mid\nu
, we start from (
6.3
) with
N=1
. For an integer
t
, consider the values

\left[H\circ\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}\right](\nu,t)=H(\nu,\nu t)=\nu^{d}H(1,t).

We deduce from (
6.3
), that for every integer
t
, one has

D^{d}\mid\nu^{d}H(1,t),

that we write as

\left(\frac{D}{\gcd(\nu,D)}\right)^{d}\mid H(1,t).

Again appealing to Lemma
6.10
, we deduce that
D/\gcd(\nu,D)=1
. Hence the result.
∎

(F_{1},F_{2})
to

7.1
The endomorphism

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem

Swapping the roles of
F_{1}
and

\rho\in{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Q})

).
Using Lemma

, we write

\rho=\frac{N}{D}\cdot A,

(7.2)

The coefficients of the matrix

F_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=F_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2}).

Combining with (

\displaystyle D^{d}F_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=D^{d}F_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})

\displaystyle=\left[N^{d}F_{1}\circ A\right](\mathbb{Z}^{2})=\left[N^{d}F_{1}\circ P\circ\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

\displaystyle=\left[N^{d}F_{1}\circ P\circ\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}\right](\mathbb{Z}^{2}),

since

permutes
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Since

also permutes
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we deduce the equality of images

D^{d}G(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=\left[N^{d}G\circ\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

with

. We apply Lemma

Let

G_{1}:=F_{1}\circ P\text{ and }G_{2}:=F_{2}\circ Q^{-1},

so we have

F_{i}\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}G_{i}.

(7.3)

Furthermore, these binary forms satisfy the following

\begin{cases}G_{2}(DX,DY)=G_{1}(X,\nu Y)\\
G_{1}\sim_{\rm val}G_{2}\\
G_{1}\not\sim_{{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}G_{2},\end{cases}

(7.4)

where
D
and
\nu
are such that

D,\nu\geq 1,D\nu>1\text{ and }D\mid\nu.

(7.5)

The forms
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are those which appear in Theorem
1.5
. We now have to prove that
3\mid|{\rm Aut}(F_{1},\mathbb{Q})|
, which equals
|{\rm Aut}(F_{2},\mathbb{Q})|=|{\rm Aut}(G_{1},\mathbb{Q})|=|{\rm Aut}(G_{2},\mathbb{Q})|
, and that
(D,\nu)\in\{(1,2),(2,2)\}
.

7.2
Further remarks on
D
and
\nu

Of particular importance is the matrix

\gamma:=\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&D/\nu\end{pmatrix}.

(7.6)

With the notations of §
7.1
, we have the relations

\gamma=Q\,\rho^{-1}P,

and

G_{1}=G_{2}\circ\gamma.

(7.7)

Thus the element
\gamma
belongs to
{\rm Isom}(G_{2}\rightarrow G_{1},\mathbb{Q})
. By Lemmas
3.2
&
6.2
and by the definition (
7.1
) of

, we infer that

L(\gamma)\text{ has minimal index among }L(\tau)\text{ for }\tau\in{\rm Isom}(G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2},\mathbb{Q})\cup{\rm Isom}(G_{2}\rightarrow G_{1},\mathbb{Q}).

(7.8)

In particular, we have the inequality

\left[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(\gamma)\right]\leq\left[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(\gamma^{-1})\right].

(7.9)

The lattices
L(\gamma)
and
L(\gamma^{-1})
are respectively the sets of
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that

\begin{cases}Dx_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
D\nu^{-1}x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{cases}\text{ and }\begin{cases}D^{-1}x_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\nu D^{-1}x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{cases}.

The corresponding indices are

\left[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(\gamma)\right]=\frac{\nu}{{\rm gcd}(D,\nu)}\text{ and }\left[\mathbb{Z}^{2}:L(\gamma^{-1})\right]=D\cdot\frac{D}{{\rm gcd}(D,\nu)}.

By (
7.9
) we obtain the inequality
1\leq\nu\leq D^{2}.
By the condition (
7.5
), we are reduced to search for
(D,\nu)
satisfying (
7.6
), (
7.7
) and such that

D,\,\nu\geq 1,\,D\nu>1,\,D\mid\nu\text{ and }1\leq\nu\leq D^{2}.

(7.10)

However, in the sequel of the proof, we will sometimes rather appeal to the property (
7.8
), which is stronger than
the inequality
1\leq\nu\leq D^{2}
(see §
11.1
& §
11.2
).

8.1
Equations with binary forms

We recall Lemma
2.1
. From this lemma, we deduce

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
F\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Let
n\geq 1
,
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
be fixed integers. Then, for every

and for

tending to infinity, one has the asymptotic lower bound

|\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:1\leq|m|\leq Z,m=F(x,y)\textup{ for some }x\equiv n_{1}\bmod n,y\equiv n_{2}\bmod n\}|\gg Z^{2/d-\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We observe that there exists a constant
B_{F}
, depending on

only, such that one has the implication

|x|,\,|y|\leq B_{F}Z^{1/d}\Rightarrow|F(x,y)|\leq Z.

The number of such pairs of integers
(x,y)
satisfying, in addition, the imposed congruence conditions modulo

is
\gg Z^{2/d}
. We then appeal to Lemma
2.1
to bound the number of integer solutions
(x,y)
to the equation
F(x,y)=m
, where

is fixed and satisfies
1\leq|m|\leq Z.
Furthermore, the number of integral solutions to
F(x,y)=0
in the box
|x|,\,|y|\leq B_{F}Z^{1/d}
is negligible, since it is
\ll Z^{1/d}
. Combining these facts we complete the proof of Lemma

.
∎

to state

We have

Now use

8.3
Lines on the surface associated to two binary forms

Definition 8.4
.

Let

and let
F
and
G
two forms of

. The surface associated to the pair

is the surface

\mathbb{W}(F\cap G):F(X,Y)-G(Z,T)=0,

where
(X:Y:Z:T)
are the usual coordinates of a point of

It is easy to check that

is a non-singular surface. In order to describe the lines on

we consider the sets of zeroes of
F
and
G
. By definition, the

of a binary form

is

\mathcal{Z}(F):=\{(x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C}):F(x_{1},x_{2})=0\}.

From the proof of
[
2
, Prop. 4.1]
, we extract the following description of the lines on the surface
\mathbb{W}(F\cap G)
(for the particular case where
F=G
, see
[
4
, Lemma 5.1, p.27]
). To shorten notations, we write

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{C}}:={\rm Isom}(G\rightarrow F,\mathbb{C}),

and

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}:={\rm Isom}(G\rightarrow F,\mathbb{Q}).

We state the following.

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
F
and
G
be two binary forms of
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{C})
. A line
\mathbb{D}
of
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\mathbb{C})
belongs to the surface

if and only if it satisfies one of the following properties

1.

There exists
(x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}(F)
and
(x_{3}:x_{4})\in\mathcal{Z}(G)
such that the line
\mathbb{D}
goes through the point
(x_{1}:x_{2}:0:0)
and
(0:0:x_{3}:x_{4})
.

2.

There exists
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\mathbb{D}
has the following parametric equation

\mathbb{D}_{\rho}:(u,v)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\mapsto(u,v,\rho(u,v)).

Observe that if the point
(z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}:z_{4})
belongs to a line of the type
1
, we have
F(z_{1},z_{2})=G(z_{3},z_{4})=0
.

The following proposition studies common values of two forms coming from points which are not on these
\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
.

Proposition 8.6
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F
and
G
be two binary forms belonging to
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
. Let
\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
(
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{C}}
) be a line on the surface

|\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:|m|\leq B,\textup{ there exists }(x,y,z,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\textup{ such that }\\

(8.1)

Of course, this proposition is trivial when

8.3.1
Proof of Proposition

An important ingredient of the proof is the following result of Salberger that we state without specifying the value of

.

Lemma 8.7

(
[
19
, Theorem 0.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a geometrically integral projective surface over
\mathbb{Q}
with degree
d\geq 3
. For
B\geq 1
, let

{\rm M}(\mathbb{X},B)
be the cardinality of the set of points of
\mathbb{X}
, with height at most
B
, which do not belong to the union of lines of
\mathbb{X}
. Then for some
\xi_{d}<2

The first result of that type, with an exponent

, is due to Heath-Brown
[
15
]
. The value of

was improved and the hypotheses were enlarged in
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
. The beginning of the proof of Proposition

can be compared with the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 1.1, §2]
. Let
\Delta>1
be a parameter to be fixed later and let

B_{1}=B^{1/d}\Delta.

(8.2)

Let
\mathcal{P}(B)
be the set of integers

that appear at the left–hand side of (

where

•

,

contains all the

such that the equations

After checking that the surface

To deal with the cardinality of

|\mathcal{P}^{\geq}(B)|\leq|\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:0<|F(x,y)|\leq B,\max(|x|,|y|)\geq B_{1}\}|\\

(
[

, Prop. 2.6]
)

Let

and let

and

(8.6)

It remains to gather (

8.3.2
Extension of Proposition
8.6

We are now interested with the images of points which do not lie on some line with rational coefficients.

Proposition 8.9
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F
and
G
be two binary forms belonging to
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
. Let
\mathbb{D}_{\rho}
(
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}
) be a line on the surface
\mathbb{W}(F\cap G)
, as defined in part
2
of Proposition
8.5
. Then there exists a constant
\alpha<2/d
such that

|\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:|m|\leq B,\textup{ there exists }(x,y,z,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\textup{ such that }\\
(x,y,z,t)\notin\cup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathbb{D}_{\rho},m=F(x,y)=G(z,t)\}|=O(B^{\alpha}).

Proof.

We identify the isomorphim
\rho
with a matrix of
{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. We will deduce Proposition
8.9
from Proposition
8.6
as soon as we produce for each
\rho=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix}
belonging to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{C}}
, but not to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, an admissible bound. By hypothesis, there exists at least one entry of the matrix
\rho
which is not rational, say
a_{1}
or
a_{2}
.

1.

Suppose
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\{\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Q}a_{1}+\mathbb{Q}a_{2}\}=2
. This means that one of the

(say
a_{2}
) can be written as

where
\alpha
and

are rational numbers. The line given by
\rho
is
(x,y,a_{1}x+a_{2}y,a_{3}x+a_{4}y)
. Then the condition

implies

. The corresponding value

is determined by

and the number of these

is in

.

2.

Suppose

directly implies

.

Having covered all cases, the proof is complete.
∎

From
(G_{1},G_{2})
to covering by lattices

We recall Definition
6.1
and Lemma
3.2
. We consider a more general situation than (
7.4
) and prove the following crucial proposition, where we exhibit explicit coverings by lattices.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
belong to
{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
such that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Let
\gamma\in{\rm Isom}(G_{2}\rightarrow G_{1},\mathbb{Q})
. We then have the equalities

\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\bigcup_{\sigma_{1}\in{\rm Aut}(G_{1},\mathbb{Q})}L(\gamma\sigma_{1})=\bigcup_{\sigma_{2}\in{\rm Aut}(G_{2},\mathbb{Q})}L(\sigma_{2}\gamma),

(9.1)

and

\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\bigcup_{\sigma_{1}\in{\rm Aut}(G_{1},\mathbb{Q})}L(\sigma_{1}\gamma^{-1})=\bigcup_{\sigma_{2}\in{\rm Aut}(G_{2},\mathbb{Q})}L(\gamma^{-1}\sigma_{2}).

(9.2)

Proof.

We will concentrate on the proof of the first equality of (
9.1
) that we write as

\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\mathcal{L}^{\cup},

(9.3)

with

\mathcal{L}^{\cup}:=\bigcup_{\sigma_{1}\in{\rm Aut}(G_{1},\mathbb{Q})}L(\gamma\sigma_{1}).

The other equalities presented in (
9.1
) and (
9.2
) are consequences of Lemma
3.2
and of the equality
G_{2}=G_{1}\circ\gamma^{-1}
.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that the claim (
9.3
) is false. Let
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, which does not belong to
\mathcal{L}^{\cup}
. The intersection of lattices
\mathcal{L}^{\cap}:=\bigcap_{\sigma_{1}\in{\rm Aut}(G_{1},\mathbb{Q})}L(\gamma\sigma_{1})
is also a lattice. Then, by Lemma
6.5
, there exists
C>1
such that
C\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\cap}
. Consider the following subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\mathcal{E}:=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:u\equiv c_{1}\bmod C,v\equiv c_{2}\bmod C\}.

By construction
\mathcal{E}
satisfies

\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\cup}=\emptyset.

(9.4)

This construction of
\mathcal{E}
, which is a translate of a lattice, shows that many points (a positive proportion, in a natural sense) do not belong to
\mathcal{L}^{\cup}
. The image of
\mathcal{E}
by
G_{1}
is also a rather dense subset, since by Lemma
8.1
, we have the asymptotic lower bound

|\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:|m|\leq Z,\,m=G_{1}(u,v)\text{ for some }(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}\}|\gg_{\varepsilon}Z^{2/d-\varepsilon}.

Combining with the hypothesis
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:|m|\leq Z,m=G_{1}(u,v)=G_{2}(s,t)\text{ for some }(u,v,s,t)\in\mathcal{E}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}|\gg_{\varepsilon}Z^{2/d-\varepsilon}.

(9.5)

To shorten notations, we set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}:={\rm Isom}(G_{2}\rightarrow G_{1},\mathbb{Q})
. We decompose into two (not necessarily disjoint) subsets

\displaystyle\{m:|m|\leq Z,m=G_{1}(a,b)\text{ for some }(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\{m:|m|\leq Z,m=G_{1}(a,b)=G_{2}(c,d)\text{ for some }(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\}

\displaystyle=\{m:|m|\leq Z,m=G_{1}(a,b)=G_{2}(c,d)\text{ for some }(a,b,c,d)\in\left(\cup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{4}\}

\displaystyle\bigcup\{m:|m|\leq Z,m=G_{1}(a,b)=G_{2}(c,d)\text{ for some }(a,b,c,d)\not\in\left(\cup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\right),(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{C}_{1}\bigcup\mathcal{C}_{2},

by definition. Proposition
8.9
directly gives for some
\alpha<2/d

|\mathcal{C}_{2}|\ll Z^{\alpha}.

But this upper bound is less than the lower bound produced by (
9.5
), thus we deduce the following asymptotic lower bound

|\{m:|m|\leq Z,\text{ there exists }(a,b,c,d)\in(\cup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathbb{D}_{\rho})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{4}\text{ and }(u,v,s,t)\in\mathcal{E}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
\text{ such that }m=G_{1}(a,b)=G_{2}(c,d)=G_{1}(u,v)=G_{2}(s,t)\}|\gg_{\varepsilon}Z^{2/d-\varepsilon}.

We appeal to Lemma
8.3
(applied to the form
F=G_{1}
) to incorporate the constraint for

to be essentially represented and we obtain the asymptotic lower bound

|\{m:|m|\leq Z,m\text{ is essentially represented by }G_{1}\\
\text{ and there exists }(a,b,c,d)\in(\cup_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathbb{D}_{\rho})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{4}\text{ and }(u,v,s,t)\in\mathcal{E}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
\text{ such that }m=G_{1}(a,b)=G_{2}(c,d)=G_{1}(u,v)=G_{2}(s,t)\}|\gg_{\varepsilon}Z^{2/d-\varepsilon}.

For
m\neq 0
, let us consider the equation
m=G_{1}(a,b)=G_{1}(u,v)
. The above lower bound implies the existence of some
(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}
, some
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, some
(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{D}_{\rho}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{4}
and some

(9.6)

Since

, we have

Returning to (

), we deduce the equality

12
Coprime value sets

Throughout this section we will assume the truth of Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
. We recall some notation. We defined
W(F):=\{F(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb{Z},\gcd(x,y)=1\}
and we write
\sim_{\text{co}}
for the resulting equivalence relation, i.e.
F\sim_{\text{co}}G
if
W(F)=W(G)
. We also recall the inclusions (
1.11
)

[F]_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\subseteq[F]_{\text{co}}\subseteq[F]_{\text{val}}.

In order to prove Theorem
1.7
, it suffices to show that

Theorem 12.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F,G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Q})
. Suppose that
F\sim_{\textup{co}}G
. Then we have
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}G
.

Proof.

Suppose that
F\not\sim_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}G
. Since we certainly have that
F\sim_{\text{val}}G
, it follows from Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
that, upon interchanging
F
and
G
and replacing them by
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent forms if necessary, we have

G(X,Y)=F(X,2Y).

Furthermore,
{\rm Aut}(F)
has an element of order
3
contained in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. We now fix any tuple
(x_{0},y_{0})
with
x_{0}
even,
y_{0}
odd,
\gcd(x_{0},y_{0})=1
and
G(x_{0},y_{0})\neq 0
. By our assumption
W(F)=W(G)
, we may find some
(x_{1},y_{1})
with
\gcd(x_{1},y_{1})=1
such that

F(x_{1},y_{1})=G(x_{0},y_{0}).

Since
{\rm Aut}(F)
has an element of order
3
contained in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we may use the argument from Lemma
3.5
to ensure that
y_{1}
is odd (by changing, if necessary, the pair
(x_{1},y_{1})
to
\sigma(x_{1},y_{1})
for a suitable
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(F)\cap\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of order
3
). We then consider

G(2x_{1},y_{1})=F(2x_{1},2y_{1})=2^{d}F(x_{1},y_{1})=2^{d}G(x_{0},y_{0}),

which is primitively represented by
G
. But then it must also be primitively represented by
F
, so we find some
(x_{2},y_{2})
with
\gcd(x_{2},y_{2})=1
and
y_{2}
odd such that

F(x_{2},y_{2})=G(2x_{1},y_{1}).

We look at

G(2x_{2},y_{2})=F(2x_{2},2y_{2})=2^{d}F(x_{2},y_{2})=2^{d}G(2x_{1},y_{1})=2^{2d}G(x_{0},y_{0}).

Continuing in this way, we obtain a sequence of pairs
(x_{N},y_{N})_{N\geq 1}
such that

F(x_{N},y_{N})=2^{(N-1)d}G(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad\gcd(x_{N},y_{N})=1.

This gives infinitely many solutions to the Thue–Mahler equation
[
16
]
, contradiction.
∎

13
Value sets with multiplicities

In this section we prove Theorem
1.8
, which is equivalent to the following

Theorem 13.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
F,G\in{\rm Bin}(d,\mathbb{Z})
. Suppose that
F\sim_{\textup{mu}}G
. Then we have
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}G
.

Proof.

The beginning of the proof mimics what is contained in §
12
.
Suppose that
F\not\sim_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}G
. Since we certainly have that
F\sim_{\text{val}}G
, it follows from Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
that, upon interchanging
F
and
G
and replacing them by
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent forms if necessary, we have

G(X,Y)=F(X,2Y).

Therefore we have
R(F;m)=R(G;m)
by hypothesis.

Take integers
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
with
y_{0}
odd and with
m:=F(x_{0},y_{0})\neq 0
. We claim that
m
is represented more frequently by
F
than by
G
. Indeed, we have an injective map
\varphi

\varphi:\{(x,y):G(x,y)=m\}\rightarrow\{(x,y):F(x,y)=m\}

given by sending
(x,y)
to
(x,2y)
. Since
(x_{0},y_{0})
is not in the image of
\varphi
, we have proven the claim, and thus the theorem.
∎

The equivalence relation

is very restrictive when compared with

and one may wonder if there exists a more direct proof of Theorem
