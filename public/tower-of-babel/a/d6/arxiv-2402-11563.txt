A Gibbs Sampling Scheme for a Generalised Poisson-Kingman Class
Robert C. Griffiths, Ross A. Maller, Soudabeh Shemehsavar

Abstract
A Bayesian nonparametric method of James, Lijoi & Prunster (2009) used to predict future values of observations from normalized random measures with independent increments is modified to a class of models based on negative binomial processes for which the increments are not independent, but are independent conditional on an underlying gamma variable. Like in James et al., the new algorithm is formulated in terms of two variables, one a function of the past observations, and the other an updating by means of a new observation. We outline an application of the procedure to population genetics, for the construction of realisations of genealogical trees and coalescents from samples of alleles. Keywords: 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 60G51, 60G52, 60G55.

Introduction

James, Lijoi & Pr

¨
u

nster2009

provided a comprehensive Bayesian
nonparametric analysis of random probabilities,
considered as generalizations of the Dirichlet process,
obtained by normalizing random
measures with independent increments (NRMIs).
They formulated the posterior distribution of an NRMI as
a mixture with respect to the distribution of a specific latent variable, showed that the posterior distribution
is again an NRMI, and derived from the ensuing representation
a rule for predicting future values of observations, given those already observed.
This takes the form of a mixture of two variables, one a function of the past observations, and the other an updating by means of the new observation.
From this they developed a Gibbs sampling algorithm generalising
the Blackwell-MacQueen urn scheme
which provides a mechanism for simulating sample observations
from the NRMI,
producing explicit and tractable expressions suitable for
practical implementation.

This procedure is closely related to
algorithms due to

Griffiths & Tavaré 1994

for computing probability distributions of sample configurations of alleles in population genetics.

Griffiths & Tavaré 1994
derive a recursion formula satisfied by the sampling probabilities and use it to construct a Markov chain
framework for simulating the likelihood of the data, given a set of parameter values. This approach corresponds to an importance sampling procedure (
Felsensteinetal 1999
,
Stephens & Donnelly 2000
).
The recursive nature of the method results from looking
back in time in the coalescent tree to the most recent
event in the past (either a coalescence or a mutation) to see what type of tree
configuration
there must have been
at that time to produce the current tree configuration.

In the present paper we modify the

James, Lijoi & Pr

¨
u

nster2009
method
(also referring to
James2002
;
James2005
)
to handle a wide class of models
based on negative binomial processes for which the increments are not independent, but are independent conditional on an underlying gamma variable.
The formulae we derive allow adaptation of the

Griffiths & Tavaré 1994
methods
for the construction of realisations of trees and coalescents from observations, either simulated or actual, on the more general processes.

1.1
The setup

Let
(\xi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
,
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables on

\mathbb{R}
with a specified initial “base” distribution
G_{0}
.
Let
(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
P_{1}>P_{2}>\cdots>0
be a random weight sequence on the infinite simplex
\nabla_{\infty}=\{z_{i}\in[0,1]:z_{1}\geq z_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i}=1\}

and

a random discrete distribution generated from
(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.
With
\delta_{x}
denoting a point mass at
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

can be written as

G=\sum_{i\geq 1}P_{i}\delta_{\xi_{i}}.

(1.1)

The data sample
{\bf X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}

is obtained by drawing
n
values from

.
Thus, conditionally given
G

are i.i.d. with distribution

In the context of population genetics, the

may represent the allele types of a particular locus on the chromosome.

Due to the assumed discreteness of

{\bf Y}=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{K_{n}}\}
, and their corresponding multiplicities,

{\bf N}_{[n]}=(N_{[n]}^{1},N_{[n]}^{2},\ldots,N_{[n]}^{Kn})
.
Here
K_{n}
is a random variable increasing in

representing the number of unique values – clusters, or blocks – in
{\bf X}
.
Given
{\bf X}
, hence
K_{n}
,
K_{n}=k
, say,

{\bf N}_{[n]}
takes values among
k

{\bf n}=\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}

with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}=n
.
We consider the homogenous case where the
X_{i}
are independent of the
P_{i}
.

1.2
Distributions on the infinite simplex:

\textbf{\rm PK}(\rho)
and
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho)

In the Poisson case, the
P_{i}
in (
1.1
) are generated from
a Poisson point process
\mathbb{B}
whose intensity measure has density
\rho(s)
satisfying

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}\rho(s)=\infty,\quad\rho(s)<\infty\text{ for all }s>0,\quad\text{ and }\int_{0}^{1}s\rho(s){\rm d}s<\infty.

(1.2)

We can write
\mathbb{B}
in the form
\mathbb{B}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{S_{i}}
with points

S_{1}>S_{2}>\cdots
.
The
P_{i}
for the process
\mathbb{B}
are defined as the ratios
of the ranked points of
\mathbb{B}
normalised by their sum:

(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=\Big(\frac{S_{i}}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}S_{\ell}}\Big)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}.

(1.3)

This kind of construction was first given by
Kingman 1975
.
In his case the
S_{i}
were the ranked jumps
\Delta S_{1}^{(1)}>\Delta S_{1}^{(2)}>\cdots

of a subordinator
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with Lévy density
\rho(s)
taken at time
1
.

The distributions of the
(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
thus constructed are said to be in
\textbf{\rm PK}(\rho)
, the
Poisson-Kingman class
.
These processes have been intensively studied for certain specifications of
\rho
. We are interested in a generalised version which
we denote as
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho)
.
To construct it, we modify (
1.3
) as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

[Definition of
\mathcal{BN}(r,\rho)
]
Take a
\rho
satisfying (
1.2
) and a parameter
r>0
.
Following
Gregoire 1984
, introduce a negative binomial process,
\mathbb{B}^{(r)}
, defined in terms of its Laplace functional:

\mathbb{E}\big(e^{\mathbb{B}^{(r)}(f)}\big)=\Big(1+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(1-e^{-f(x)})\rho(x){\rm d}x\Big)^{-r},

(1.4)

for any bounded nonnegative measurable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Such processes are said to be in
\mathcal{BN}(r,\rho)
.

Basic properties of a negative binomial process are that numbers of points in non-overlapping Borel sets are negative binomially (but not independently) distributed.

(
1.4

\mathcal{BN}(r,\rho)
to be a Poisson point process with random intensity measure
\Gamma_{r}\rho
, where
\Gamma_{r}:=\Gamma_{r,1}
, a Gamma random variable
1

We use the notation

\Gamma_{\beta,\gamma}
for a Gamma random variable with parameter
(\beta,\gamma)
having density

(\gamma x)e^{-\gamma x}(\gamma x)^{\beta-1}/\Gamma(\beta)
,
\beta,\gamma>0
, where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function.

with parameter
(r,1)
.
Using such a

\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho)
as follows.

Definition 1.2
.

[Definition of
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho)
]
Write
\mathbb{B}^{(r)}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}J_{i}
in terms of
its ranked points

Analogous to (

random sequence on the infinite simplex is said to have distribution

\Big(\frac{J_{i}}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}{J_{\ell}}}\Big)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sim\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho),\quad r>0.

(1.5)

Lévy densities of particular interest are

\rho_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}x^{-\alpha-1},\quad\rho_{\theta}(x)=\theta x^{-1}e^{-x},\quad\rho_{G}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}x^{-\alpha-1}e^{-x},x>0,

where
\theta>0
and

We can identify important members of the
PK
class in the
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}

For example, the two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet process

\textbf{\rm PD}(\alpha,\theta)
(
Pitman & Yor 1997

\textbf{\rm PD}(\alpha,\theta)=\textbf{\rm PK}^{(\theta/\alpha)}(\rho_{G})
.
Further,

\textbf{\rm PD}(\alpha,0)=\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho_{\alpha})
for any
r>0
,
and

\textbf{\rm PD}(0,\theta)
can be derived as a limiting case:

\textbf{\rm PD}(0,\theta)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\textbf{\rm PK}^{(\theta/\alpha)}(\rho_{G})=\lim_{r\to\infty}\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho_{\theta}/r)

Pitman & Yor 1997
,
Ipsen & Maller 2017
).

The choice

\rho_{R}(x)=\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}{\bf 1}_{0<x\leq 1}

in (
1.2
) gives the class
\textbf{\rm PD}_{\alpha}^{(r)}

studied in
Ipsen, Maller & Shemehsavar 2020a
;
Ipsen, Maller & Shemehsavar 2021
and
Maller & Shemehsavar 2023b

and related to the negative binomial process in (
1.4
).

Chegini & Zarepour 2023
and
Labadi & Zarepour 2014
give further properties and applications of
\textbf{\rm PD}_{\alpha}^{(r)}
.
The distribution of
\textbf{\rm PD}_{\alpha}^{(r)}
converges to that of
\textbf{\rm PD}(\alpha,0)
as
r\downarrow 0

(
Maller & Shemehsavar 2023b
).

Posterior Distributions for
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho)

James2002
;
James2005
developed a framework to compute the likelihood and posterior distributions of partitioned data derived from an underlying Poisson point process. In particular,

James, Lijoi & Pr

¨
u

nster2009
computed the posterior analysis for
G
when

are in the
Poisson-Kingman class. We use the same framework to derive the posterior distribution of

when the weight sequence is distributed as
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}(\rho)

Like James et al. we make use of certain auxiliary random variables, denoted
(V_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

By analogy with their approach, we define the following negative binomial point process augmented by the inclusion of an independent sequence

(J_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Note that
\mathbb{M}^{(r)}
is no longer a Poisson random measure,

\Gamma_{r}

\mathbb{M}^{(r)}
is a Poisson point process with intensity measure
\Gamma_{r}\rho(\cdot)G_{0}(\cdot)

We can relate
\mathbb{M}^{(r)}

G(\cdot)=\frac{\mu(\cdot)}{T^{(r)}},\ \text{where}\ \mu(\cdot)=\int_{s>0}s\,\mathbb{M}^{(r)}({\rm d}s,\cdot)\ \text{and}\ T^{(r)}=\mu(\Omega)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}J_{i}.

(2.2)

We aim to use the methods of

James, Lijoi & Pr

¨

nster2009

Our first task is to compute the posterior distribution of the underlying augmented measure

In the following analysis, we fix

Conditional on

,

Theorem 2.1

Conditional on

and

\mathbb{M}_{\bf X}^{(r,V_{n})}=\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}^{(r+k,V_{n})}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{J_{i}^{V_{n}},Y_{i}},

5
Constructing Trees and Coalescents from a
\textbf{\rm PD}_{\alpha}^{(r)}
Sample

A genealogical tree is a diagram that depicts the ancestral history and relationships between genes in a sample from within a species.
The tree’s branches represent lines of descent and the
branches’ tips are labelled with the
allele types. The pattern of branching reflects relationships between alleles;
they are more closely related when there are fewer nodes on the path from one to another through the tree.
A fundamental objective of population genetics analysis
is to construct genealogical trees from DNA sequence data
with the aim of developing probability models to
describe the ancestral processes involved.

Good estimates of tree configurations
are crucial for quantifying statistical uncertainty and estimating parameters such as effective population size, mutation rate, allele age, etc. See
Hudson 1991
) for a general overview of the applications.

Griffiths & Tavaré 1994
describe a sequential importance sampling algorithm for calculating the probability of a sample of size
n
under mutation and genetic drift in an unstructured population.

De Iorio & Griffiths 2004
developed an improved method of constructing sequential
importance sampling proposal distributions on coalescent histories.
In this section we outline an extension of the
Griffiths & Tavaré 1994
algorithm to the
\textbf{\rm PK}^{(r)}
class,
deriving a backwards recursion, depending on ancestral histories, for the sampling distribution.
A time scale is introduced for the backwards processes.
Such kinds of recursion are important in sequential importance sampling and duality with diffusion processes as illustrated for
the one and two parameter Poisson-Dirichlet processes
in
Griffiths, Ruggiero, Spano, and Zhou 2023
and
Petrov 2009
.

Consider a population consisting of a number of genes, each of which has an
infinite number of nucleotide sites, such that at each site there are two or more
possible nucleotides, the original one and mutant ones.
In the infinitely-many-sites model of DNA sequences the “type” of a gene is given by a sequence of ls and 0s indicating at which sites a mutation has
occurred in the gene or one of its ancestors. The reproductive mechanism assumes in effect that each of the genes in a generation under consideration selects a parent
gene at random (with replacement) from the previous generation.
With a designated probability the offspring gene is
of the same type as the parent gene; with the complementary probability a mutation occurs, in which case a 0 changes to a 1 at a site where no mutation
has occurred in any gene. Which site is chosen for the mutation does not matter, as sites are unordered.
At an allele level sequences are recorded as being distinct if their DNA types are different, but the fine details of site mutations are not taken into account.
See
Ethier & Griffiths 1987
for more details.

In a reverse procedure, for a given set
D
(descendents) of input sequences in the generation under consideration, a genealogy for
D
can be constructed backward in time using the evolutionary events of mutation and coalescence.
Types are thought of as mutations and removing a singleton is like removing a mutation.
An ancestral configuration can be regarded as the multiset of all sequences present at a particular point in time in a possible genealogy for
D
.
It may be thought of as a tree (reading forward in time) or a coalescent (reading backward in time).

The random vector
{\bf N}_{[n]}
of Section
2
,
which takes values among partitions of
[n]
is now interpreted as counting the numbers of distinct alleles in a sample of size
n

arranged in their order of appearance in the sample.
Suppose we currently have an observation
{\bf n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
on
{\bf N}_{[n]}
, where
n_{i}
is the number of allele type
i
among the
n
alleles, of which there are
k
distinct types.
Let
{\bf e}_{i}=(0,0,\ldots,0,1_{i},0,\ldots,0,0)

be a unit
n
-vector with a 1 in the
i
-th position.
Recalling the notation in Theorem
3.1
, we argue that to obtain the configuration
{\bf n}
there was a birth from
{\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i}
with probability

\frac{n_{i}}{n}\frac{1}{n-1}\omega_{i}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i}),

(5.1)

when
n_{i}>1
,
or, for a singleton
(
n_{i}=1
), with probability

\frac{1}{n}\omega_{0}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i}).

(5.2)

All the terms when
n_{i}=1
are identical. The factor of
n_{i}/n
appears because each individual in the current sample (of size
n
) has an equal probability of being the new individual from the immediate ancestral sample (of size
n-1
).

A full investigation of the coalescent structure and biological relevance of (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) for particular models is beyond the scope of this paper and yet to be determined.
But we can give some preliminary observations as follows.
From (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) we get a fundamental equation
for the EPPF,
\text{p}(\bm{n})
, in (
2.10
):

\displaystyle\text{p}({\bf n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{i}>1}\frac{n_{i}}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n-1}\omega_{i}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})+\sum_{\{i:n_{i}=1\}}\frac{1}{n}\omega_{0}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i}).

(5.3)

The second term counts the number of singletons in the sample, multiplied by
the probability of a singleton,
\omega_{0}^{(n)}
.
All the terms in the second sum are identical.

Based on (
5.3
) we define a continuous time Markov process

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\text{p}({\bf n},t)

\displaystyle=-\varphi({\bf n})\text{p}({\bf n},t)

\displaystyle+\varphi({\bf n})\sum_{n_{i}>1}\frac{n_{i}}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\omega_{i}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}{\text{p}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}\cdot\text{p}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i},t)

\displaystyle+\varphi({\bf n})\sum_{\{i:n_{i}=1\}}\frac{1}{n}\frac{\omega_{0}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}{\text{p}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}\cdot\text{p}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i},t),

(5.4)

where
\varphi({\bf n})
is a total rate for the configuration
{\bf n}
.
Rescale
\text{p}({\bf n},t)
as

\displaystyle H({\bf n},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{p}({\bf n},t)}{\text{p}({\bf n})},\ t\geq 0,

so that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}H({\bf n},t)=-\varphi({\bf n})H({\bf n},t)+\varphi({\bf n})\sum_{n_{i}>1}\frac{n_{i}}{n}H({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i},t)+\varphi({\bf n})\sum_{\{i:n_{i}=1\}}\frac{1}{n}H({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i},t).

(5.5)

The rates in (
5.5
) are
\varphi({\bf n})n_{i}/n
when both
n_{i}>1
or
n_{i}=1
, and of course we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi({\bf n})\frac{n_{i}}{n}=\varphi({\bf n}).

H({\bf n},t)
is a function of a process
({\bf n}(t),t\geq 0)
, which begins at
{\bf n}(0)={\bf n}
and decreases back in time by coalescing within a group where
n_{i}>1
or removing a singleton (“mutation”) when
n_{i}=1
. The rates
{\bf n}\to{\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i}
are
\varphi(\bm{n})n_{i}/n
. The choice of
H({\bf n},0)
is important.
In the one and two parameter Poisson-Dirichlet processes the construction of
H({\bf n},t)
leads to a coalescent process dual to the diffusions.

It is easy to convert p
({\bf n})
into a partition distribution by taking

{\cal P}({\bf n})={n\choose{\bf n}}\frac{1}{m_{1}!m_{2}!\cdots m_{n}!}\text{p}({\bf n})

where
m_{\ell}
is the number of
n_{j}
’s equal to
\ell
, but we restrict ourselves mainly to the function
p
for simplicity.

The ratios after calculation for
n_{i}>1
and
n_{i}=1
are

\displaystyle\frac{n_{i}}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n-1}\frac{\omega_{i}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}{\text{p}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{i}}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{n-1}}{(\psi(v))^{r+k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\pi_{n_{j}}(v)dv}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{n-2}}{(\psi(v))^{r+k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\pi_{n_{j}-\delta_{ij}}(v)dv}

(5.6)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\frac{\omega_{0}^{(n-1)}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}{\text{p}({\bf n}-{\bf e}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{r+k-1}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{n-1}}{(\psi(v))^{r+k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\pi_{n_{j}}(v)dv}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{n-2}}{(\psi(v))^{r+k-1}}\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{k}\pi_{n_{j}}(v)dv}.

(5.7)

Note that the numerator in (
5.6
)
is the same in both the ratios, so can be factored out of the
sums in
(
5
)
and connected with
\varphi({\bf n})
.
The two ratios in (
5.6
) and
(
5.7
)
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{n_{i}}{n}\cdot\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(v;{\bf n})dv}{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(v;{\bf n}-{\bf e}_{j})dv}\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(v;{\bf n})dv}{\int_{0}^{\infty}g(v;{\bf n}-{\bf e}_{j})dv}.

The two ratios are the same, however the meaning is different because a singleton is removed in the second ratio.
For comparison, in the two-parameter Pitman-Yor model the two terms in (
5.6
) and
(
5.7
) are

\frac{n_{i}(n_{i}-\alpha-1)}{n(\theta+n-1)}\text{~and~}\frac{\theta+(k-1)\alpha}{n(\theta+n-1)}

respectively. These have an important meaning in duality with the two-parameter diffusion.
