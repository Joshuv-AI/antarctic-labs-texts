Backward martingale transport and Fitzpatrick functions in pseudo-Euclidean spaces
Dmitry Kramkov, Mihai Sîrbu

Abstract
We study an optimal transport problem with a backward martingale constraint in a pseudo-Euclidean space S S . We show that the dual problem consists in the minimization of the expected values of the Fitzpatrick functions associated with maximal S S -monotone sets. An optimal plan γ \gamma and an optimal maximal S S -monotone set G G are characterized by the condition that the support of γ \gamma is contained in the graph of the S S -projection on G G . For a Gaussian random variable Y Y , we get a unique decomposition: Y = X + Z Y=X+Z , where X X and Z Z are independent Gaussian random variables taking values, respectively, in complementary positive and negative linear subspaces of the S S -space.



Keywords:

martingale optimal transport, pseudo-Euclidean space,
Fitzpatrick function.

60G42, 91B24, 91B52.

2
Backward martingale transport

We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
the family of symmetric

d\times d
-matrices of full rank with
m\in\left\{{0,1,\dots,d}\right\}

positive eigenvalues. For
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
, the bilinear form

S(x,y):=\left\langle x,Sy\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}x^{i}S_{ij}y^{j},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

defines the scalar product on a pseudo-Euclidean space
\real{d}_{m}

with dimension
d
and index
m
, which we call the
S
-space. The
quadratic form
S(x,x)
is called the
scalar square
; its value
may be negative.

We view a point
(x,y)
in the product space

\real{2d}=\real{d}\times\real{d}
as the initial and terminal
values of the canonical
d
-dimensional process. Given the terminal
law
\nu=\nu(dy)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)
, our goal is to

\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{2}\int S(x,y)d\gamma\quad\text{over}\quad\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu),

(4)

where
\Gamma(\nu)
is the family of Borel probability measures

\gamma=\gamma(dx,dy)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{2d}}\right)
having
\nu
as their

y
-marginal and making a martingale out of the canonical process:

\Gamma(\nu):=\left\{{{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{2d}}\right)}\;|\;\gamma(\real{d},dy)=\nu(dy)\text{ and }\gamma(y|x)=x}\right\}.

The martingale property of
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
yields that

\displaystyle\int S(x,y)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int S(x,x)d\gamma,

\displaystyle\int S(x-y,x-y)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int S(y,y)d\nu-\int S(x,y)d\gamma.

Thus, the problem (
4
) is equivalent to the minimization of
the expected value of the scalar square between
x
and
y
over

\Gamma(\nu)
:

\text{minimize}\quad\int S(x-y,x-y)d\gamma\quad\text{over}\quad\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu).

(5)

Theorem
3.2
contains another reformulation of (
4
).

A set
G\subset\real{d}
is called
S
-
monotone
or

S
-

if

S(x-y,x-y)\geq 0,\quad x,y\in G,

that is, if the restriction to
G
of the scalar square takes values

[0,\infty)
. An
S
-monotone set

is called

S
-monotone set. Theorem
2.5

shows that a dual problem to (
4

\text{minimize}\int\psi_{G}(y)d\nu\quad\text{over}\quad G\in\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right),

(6)

where
\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
is the family of all maximal
S
-monotone sets and

\psi_{G}(y):=\sup_{x\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right),\quad y\in\real{d},

is a closed convex function taking values in
\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
. For general
quadratic forms
S
such function

was introduced

]

is convex
and
\psi_{G}(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)
. The second inequality
in (

) follows now from the conditional Jensen inequality
and the martingale property of
\gamma
. The first part
of (
10
) is a consequence of the martingale property of

\gamma
. The second part of (
10
) is obtained by
integrating (

) over
\gamma
.

We equip
\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)
with the Wasserstein
2-metric:

W_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\sqrt{\int\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2}d\pi},\quad\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the family of Borel probability measures on

\real{2d}
with
x
-marginal
\mu
and
y
-marginal
\nu
. We recall
that
(\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right),W_{2})
is a Polish space and
that
W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
if and only if
\mu_{n}(f)\to\mu(f)
for
every continuous function
f=f(x)
on
\real{d}
with quadratic
growth:

\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert\leq K(1+\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}),\quad x\in\real{d},

where
K=K(f)>0
is a constant. A set

A\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\real{d})
is
pre-compact
under
W_{2}

if and only if

\sup_{\mu\in A}\int_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}d\mu\to 0,\quad K\to\infty.

(11)

These results can be found in
[
1
, Chapter 2]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
C
is a compact set in
\left({\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right),W_{2}}\right)
, then
the set of martingale measures
D=\cup_{\nu\in C}\Gamma(\nu)
is
compact in
(\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{2d}}\right),W_{2})
.

Proof.

We first show that
D
is pre-compact. For

\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{d}}\right)
,
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
, and a constant

K>0
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int\left\lvert\gamma(y|x)\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma\leq\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K>\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}}\right\}}d\gamma+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}\geq K}\right\}}d\nu

\displaystyle\leq K\gamma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K)+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq\sqrt{K}}\right\}}d\nu,

\displaystyle K\gamma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\int\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}d\gamma=\frac{1}{K}\int\left\lvert\gamma(y|x)\right\rvert^{2}d\gamma\leq\frac{1}{K}\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}d\nu,

and then that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\left({\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}}\right)1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq 2K}\right\}}d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\nu

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}\left({\frac{1}{K}+1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq\sqrt{K}}\right\}}+1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq{K}}\right\}}}\right)d\nu.

The pre-compactness of
D
follows now from that of
C
in view
of (
11
).

To show that
D
is closed, we take a sequence

(\gamma_{n})\subset D
converging to
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\left({\real{2d}}\right)

under
W_{2}
. Let
\nu_{n}
and
\nu
be the
y
-marginals of

\gamma_{n}
and
\gamma
. For every continuous function
f=f(y)
on

\real{d}
with quadratic growth,

\int f(y)d\nu_{n}=\int f(y)d\gamma_{n}\to\int f(y)d\gamma=\int f(y)d\nu.

Thus,
\nu_{n}\to\nu
in
W_{2}
. As
C
is closed,
\nu\in C
. Let

g=g(x)
be a bounded continuous function on
\real{d}
. From the
martingale properties of
(\gamma_{n})
we deduce that

\int g(x)(y-x)d\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int g(x)(y-x)d\gamma_{n}=0.

It follows that
\gamma(y|x)=x
or, equivalently, that

\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
. As
\nu\in C
, we obtain that

\gamma\in D
, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.9

(First-order condition to (

We first establish (

In the general case, where

We start with some results from linear algebra. By

we denote the
identity matrix and by

the transpose of a matrix

. Let

for every

and say that

is

for every

. By

. A similar

if and only if

if and only if

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
and
\Sigma
be a symmetric positive definite

d\times d
matrix. There exist unique symmetric positive
semi-definite
d\times d
matrices
Q
and
R
such that

\Sigma=Q+R,\quad QSQ\geq 0,\quad RSR\leq 0,\quad QSR=0.

(16)

In this case,

\displaystyle\rank{Q}=m,\quad\rank{R}=d-m,\quad QSQ-RSR>0,

\displaystyle Q\Sigma^{-1}Q=Q,\quad R\Sigma^{-1}R=R,\quad Q\Sigma^{-1}R=0.

Proof.

The classical diagonalization theorem of linear algebra,

[
8
, Theorem 9, p. 314]
, yields a
d\times d
matrix
V

of full rank such that

V^{T}SV=\Lambda,\quad V^{T}\Sigma^{-1}V=I,

where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix:

\Lambda_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\lambda_{i}
, whose first
m
diagonal
elements are strictly positive and the remaining
d-m
diagonal
elements are strictly negative.

Observe now that the symmetric positive semi-definite matrices
Q

and
R
satisfy (
16
) if and only if

Q=VAV^{T},\quad R=VBV^{T},

where
A
and
B
are positive semi-definite matrices such that

I=A+B,\quad A\Lambda A\geq 0,\quad B\Lambda B\leq 0,\quad A\Lambda B=0.

We claim that the only such
A
and
B
are diagonal and given by

A_{ij}=\delta_{ij}1_{\left\{{i\leq m}\right\}},\quad B_{ij}=\delta_{ij}1_{\left\{{i>m}\right\}},\quad i,j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}.

Checking that the above matrices
A
and
B
are solutions is
immediate. The uniqueness is verified as follows.

From
A\Lambda B=0
and
A+B=I
we obtain that

\Lambda A=A\Lambda A
and
\Lambda B=B\Lambda B
. In particular,
the matrix
\Lambda A
is symmetric:

\lambda_{i}A_{ij}=\lambda_{j}A_{ji}\quad i,j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}.

Because
A
is symmetric and
\lambda_{i}>0
for
i\leq m
and

\lambda_{i}<0
for
i>m
, we deduce that

A_{ij}=A_{ji}=0,\quad i\leq m<j.

Since
\Lambda A=A\Lambda A\geq 0
, we have that

0\leq\sum_{ij}\lambda_{i}A_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}A_{ii}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})A_{ij}x_{i}x_{j},\quad x\in\real{d}.

Recalling that
A\geq 0
, taking distinct

k,l\in\left\{{m+1,\dots,d}\right\}
, and choosing
x_{i}=\delta_{ik}
and

x_{i}=\delta_{ik}+\delta_{il}
we obtain that

A_{kl}=0,\quad k,l>m.

Thus the matrix
A
has possible nonzero entries

only for

i,j\leq m
. Using
\Lambda B=B\Lambda B\leq 0
and
B\geq 0
we go
over identical arguments to obtain that the matrix
B
has possible
nonzero entries

only for
i,j>m
. The uniqueness follows
now from
A+B=I
.

We have shown that symmetric positive semi-definite matrices

and

R
satisfying (
16
) exist and are unique. The remaining
properties of
Q
and
R
follow from their counterparts for
A
and

B
:

\displaystyle\rank{A}=m,\quad\rank{B}=d-m,\quad A\Lambda A-B\Lambda B>0,

\displaystyle AIA=A,\quad BIB=B,\quad AIB=0.

∎

Clearly,
X
and
Z
are
d
-dimensional Gaussian random variables
with mean zero. Their covariance matrices are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(XX^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=P\mathbb{E}\left(YY^{T}\right)P^{T}=P\Sigma P^{T}=P\Sigma,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(ZZ^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=(I-P)\Sigma(I-P)^{T}=(I-P)\Sigma,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(XZ^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=P\Sigma(I-P)^{T}=0.

The last identity implies that
X
and
Z
are independent random
variables. The relations (
23
) readily follow from the
properties (
17
) of the idempotent matrix
P
.

Let
X^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
be independent
d
-dimensional Gaussian random
variables with mean zero, the covariance matrices
Q^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}
,
respectively, and such that

\displaystyle Y=X^{\prime}+Z^{\prime},

\displaystyle S(X^{\prime},X^{\prime})\geq 0,\quad S(Z^{\prime},Z^{\prime})\leq 0,\quad S(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime})=0.

As
X^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
are Gaussian random variables, their laws are
supported by the ranges of the covariance matrices. Accounting for
the independence of
X^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
we deduce that

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}+R^{\prime}=\Sigma,

\displaystyle S(x,x)\geq 0,\;S(z,z)\leq 0,\;S(x,z)=0,\quad x\in\range{Q^{\prime}},\;z\in\range{R^{\prime}},

and then that

Q^{\prime}SQ^{\prime}\geq 0,\quad R^{\prime}SR^{\prime}\leq 0,\quad Q^{\prime}SR^{\prime}=0.

Theorem
4.2
shows that
P=Q^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}
. Then

Q^{\prime}=P\Sigma
and
R^{\prime}=(I-P)\Sigma
are also the covariance
matrices of
X
and
Z
, respectively. It follows that

\Law(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime})=\Law(X,Z)
, that

\Law(X^{\prime},Y)=\Law(X,Y)=\Law(PY,Y),

and then that
X^{\prime}=PY=X
and
Z^{\prime}=Y-X^{\prime}=Y-X=Z
.

Finally, Theorem
4.4
yields the optimality and uniqueness
properties of

and
G:=\range{P}
.
∎

for the bilinear form and

for the family of maximal

S

, the inverse matrix to

,
belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
.

For

from Example
2.3
, many results of this

[

]
and

[

]
. The case of general symmetric

Theorem A.1
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
and
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed
convex function such that

\min(f(x),f^{*}(Sx))\geq\frac{1}{2}S(x,x),\quad x\in\real{d}.

(24)

Then the set
G:=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;Sx\in\partial f(x)}\right\}

belongs to
\mathfrak{M}\left({S}\right)
and has equivalent descriptions:

G=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;f(x)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right\}=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;f^{*}(Sx)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right\}.

(25)

Proof.

By the properties (
3
) of subdifferentials of convex
functions,

\displaystyle Sx\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^{*}(Sx)\iff f(x)+f^{*}(Sx)=S(x,x).

The latter representation and (
24
) imply that

G=\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;f(x)=f^{*}(Sx)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right\}.

Let
u\in\real{d}
. If
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)
, then
inequality (
24
) yields that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left({f(x+tu)-f(x)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2t}\left({S(x+tu,x+tu)-S(x,x)}\right)

\displaystyle=S(x,u)=\left\langle Sx,u\right\rangle.

It follows that
Sx\in\partial f(x)
, proving the first equality
in (
25
).

Similarly, if
f^{*}(Sx)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left({f^{*}(Sx+tu)-f^{*}(Sx)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2t}\left({S(x+tS^{-1}u,x+tS^{-1}u)-S(x,x)}\right)

\displaystyle=S(x,S^{-1}u)=\left\langle x,u\right\rangle.

It follows that
x\in\partial f^{*}(Sx)
or equivalently, that

Sx\in\partial f(x)
, proving the second equality
in (
25
).

If
x,y\in G
, then
(x,Sx)
and
(y,Sy)
belong to the graph of

\partial f
, which is a classical monotone set in
\real{2d}
:

S(x-y,x-y)=\left\langle x-y,Sx-Sy\right\rangle\geq 0.

Hence,
G
is
S
-monotone.

To show that
G
is maximal, we assume first that
S
is an
orthogonal matrix:

S=S^{-1}.

In this case, condition (
24
) becomes symmetric with respect
to
f
and
f^{*}
:

\max\left({f(x),f^{*}(x)}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}S(x,x),\quad x\in\real{d}.

(26)

For every
v\in\real{d}
, the Moreau decomposition from

[
19
, Theorem 31.5, p. 338]
yields unique

x\in\dom{\partial f}
and
y\in\partial f(x)
such that

v=x+y
. Taking
u\in\real{d}\setminus G
and setting

we
obtain that

u+Su=x+y.

The orthogonality of
S
yields that

Sx+Sy=Su+S^{2}u=Su+u=x+y.

It follows that
x-Sy=Sx-y
and

\left\lvert x-Sy\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert Sx-y\right\rvert^{2}=\left\langle x-Sy,Sx-y\right\rangle.

On the other hand, from (
26
), the orthogonality of
S
, and
the fact that

we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle x-Sy,Sx-y\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({S(x,x)+S(y,y)-\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-\left\langle Sx,Sy\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({S(x,x)+S(y,y)}\right)-\left\langle x,y\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq f(x)+f^{*}(y)-\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=0.

It follows that
Sx=y\in\partial f(x)
and then that
x\in G
. Since
u+Su=x+y=x+Sx
, we have that
x-u=Su-Sx
. It
follows that

proving the maximality of the
S
-monotone set

.

In the general case, we decompose the symmetric matrix

as

where

is a
d\times d
-matrix of full rank and

is symmetric
and orthogonal. The law of inertia for quadratic forms,

We define the closed convex function

and deduce from (

In view of (

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}S(V^{-1}x,V^{-1}x)=\frac{1}{2}U(x,x),

\displaystyle g^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\real{d}}\left({\left\langle Vx,y\right\rangle-g(Vx)}\right)=\sup_{x\in\real{d}}\left({\left\langle x,V^{T}y\right\rangle-f(x)}\right)=f^{*}(V^{T}y),

\displaystyle g^{*}(Ux)

Thus,

\dom{P_{G}}\subset D
and

SP_{G}(y)\subset\partial\psi_{G}(y)
,
y\in D
. Since

\partial\psi_{G}(y)=\partial\psi_{G}(y)+N_{\cl{D}}(y),\quad y\in D,

we obtain the inclusion:

\clconv\left({SP_{G}(y)}\right)+N_{\cl{D}}(y)\subset\partial\psi_{G}(y),\quad y\in\dom{P_{G}}.

(31)

To prove the opposite inclusion, we assume for a moment that

\interior{D}\not=\emptyset.

We recall that
\interior{D}=\interior{\dom{\psi_{G}}}
. We also
recall that for
y\in\interior{D}
, the classical gradient

\nabla\psi_{G}(y)
exists if and only if
\partial\psi_{G}(y)
is a
singleton. In this case,

\partial\psi_{G}(y)=\left\{{\nabla\psi_{G}(y)}\right\}
. We denote

\dom{\nabla\psi_{G}}:=\left\{{{y\in\interior{D}}\;|\;\nabla\psi_{G}(y)\text{ exists}}\right\}.

We claim that

S^{-1}\nabla\psi_{G}(y)\in G,\quad y\in\dom{\nabla\psi_{G}}.

(32)

If the claim fails for some
y\in\dom{\nabla\psi_{G}}
, then there
is
\epsilon>0
such that

G\subset\left\{{{x\in\real{d}}\;|\;\left\lvert x-x_{0}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}\right\},

where
x_{0}:=S^{-1}\nabla\psi_{G}(y)
. As

\displaystyle\psi_{G}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\real{d}}\left({\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-\psi_{G}^{*}(x)}\right)=\sup_{x\in\real{d}}\left({S(x,y)-\psi_{G}^{*}(Sx)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\psi_{G}(y),y\right\rangle-\psi_{G}^{*}(\nabla\psi_{G}(y))=S(x_{0},y)-\psi_{G}^{*}(Sx_{0}),

the function
f(x):=\psi^{*}_{G}(Sx)-S(x,y)
attains strict
minimum at
x_{0}
. The convexity of
f
yields that

f(x_{0})<\inf_{\left\lvert x-x_{0}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}f(x),

and we get the contradiction:

\psi_{G}(y)>\sup_{\left\lvert x-x_{0}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}\left({S(x,y)-\psi^{*}_{G}(Sx)}\right)\geq\sup_{x\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)}\right)=\psi_{G}(y).

Now, for
y\in{D}
we denote by
L(y)
the set of cluster points
of
\nabla\psi_{G}
:

L(y):=\left\{{{z=\lim\nabla\psi_{G}(y_{n})}\;|\;\dom{\nabla\psi_{G}}\ni y_{n}\rightarrow y}\right\}.

From (
29
), (
32
), and the continuity of
subdifferentials we obtain that

L(y)\subset SP_{G}(y),\quad y\in{D}.

According to
[
19
, Theorem 25.6, p. 246]
,

L(y)\not=\emptyset\text{ and }\partial\psi_{G}(y)=\clconv{L}(y)+N_{\cl{D}}(y),\quad y\in D.

It follows that

\partial\psi_{G}(y)\subset\clconv\left({SP_{G}(y)}\right)+N_{\cl{D}}(y),\quad y\in D.

Accounting for (
31
), we obtain (
30
). In
particular,
D=\dom{P_{G}}
.

To verify the final relation of the theorem, we fix

y\in\interior{D}
. Then the subdifferential
\partial\psi_{G}(y)

is bounded together with its subset
SP_{G}(y)
. As
SP_{G}(y)
is
closed, we have that

\cl\conv\left({SP_{G}(y)}\right)=\conv\left({SP_{G}(y)}\right).

Recalling that
N_{\cl{D}}(y)=0
for
y\in\interior{D}
, we
obtain the result.

We have proved (
30
) under the assumption that
D
has a
nonempty interior. The general case is reduced to this setting by
the following arguments. For
x_{0}\in\real{d}
, we observe that

\dom\psi_{G}=x_{0}+\dom\psi_{G-x_{0}}
and

P_{G}(y)=x_{0}+P_{G-x_{0}}(y-x_{0}),\;\partial\psi_{G}(y)=Sx_{0}+\partial\psi_{G-x_{0}}(y-x_{0}),\quad y\in\real{d}.

Therefore, we can assume that

0\in G\subset\dom\psi_{G}\subset\aff\dom\psi_{G}
. Under this
assumption,
F:=\aff\dom\psi_{G}
is a linear space. We write

F=E+E^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x^{\prime}\in F}\;|\;S(x^{\prime},y)=0,\;y\in F}\right\},

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{x\in F}\;|\;\left\langle x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle=0,\;x^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}}\right\},

and denote by
S_{E}
the restriction of the bilinear form
S
on

E
:

S_{E}(x,y):=S(x,y),\quad x,y\in E.

The Euclidean projection
G_{E}
of
G
on
E
is a maximal

S_{E}
-monotone set and the restriction of the Fitzpatrick function

\psi_{G}
to
E
is the Fitzpatrick function
\psi_{G_{E}}
in the

S_{E}
-space. By construction,

\interior{\dom{\psi_{G_{E}}}}\not=\emptyset
. Therefore, the
relation (
30
) holds for
\psi_{G_{E}}
in the

S_{E}
-space. Being expressed in the original coordinates, this
relation takes exactly the form (
30
).
∎
