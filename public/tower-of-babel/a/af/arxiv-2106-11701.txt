Abstract
. We study the problem of finding a function f f with “small support” that simultaneously tiles with finitely many lattices Λ 1 , … , Λ N \Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N} in d d -dimensional Euclidean spaces. We prove several results, both upper bounds (constructions) and lower bounds on how large this support can and must be. We also study the problem in the setting of finite abelian groups, which turns out to be the most concrete setting. Several open questions are posed.

1.
Introduction to the Steinhaus tiling problem and its variants

The classical Steinhaus tiling problem concerns tiling by translations only. This is the version of tiling where we have a set (the tile), say in the plane, and we are
translating
it around in such a way that every point of the plane is covered exactly once by these translates. In the Steinhaus tiling problem we are seeking a tile that can tile
simultaneously
with many different sets of translations. The most important case is: can we find a subset of the plane which can tile (by translations) with all rotates of the integer lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
?

There are two major variations of the Steinhaus problem: the
measurable
and the
set-theoretic
case. In the measurable case we demand our tile to be a Lebesgue measurable subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and we are, at the same time, relaxing our requirements and are allowing a subset of measure 0 of space not to be covered exactly once by the translates of the tile. In the set-theoretic case we allow the tile to by any subset and we typically ask that every point is covered exactly once, allowing no exceptions.

In this paper we are dealing with the measurable Steinhaus tiling problem. Here the tile is not necessarily a set but can be a measurable function. Tiling now means that this function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is translated around by a countable set of translates
T\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and these copies add up to a constant, almost everywhere

\sum_{t\in T}f(x-t)=\mathrm{const.},\ \ \ \text{for almost every $x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$.}

Clearly tiling by a function is a generalization of tiling by a set.

Satisfying the requirements of the Steinhaus tiling problem with a function instead of with a set is generally much easier. The problem becomes interesting only if one asks for further properties that this function should have. Therefore we strive to find a function with
small support
, or to prove that the support of such a function must necessarily be large. Asking for
f
to have a small support goes against
f
having the ability to tile space, especially with nany different sets of translations
T
. The reason is that for
f
to tile by translations with
T
its Fourier transform must contain a rich set of zeros
[
21
]
. This set of zeros must be able to support the Fourier transform of the measure
\delta_{T}=\sum_{t\in T}\delta_{t}
(which encodes the set of translations). By the well known uncertainty principle in harmonic analysis a rich set of zeros for
\widehat{f}
usually requires (in various different senses) a large support for
f

[
14
]
.

The diameter and volume of soft multi-lattice tiles

2.1.
Convolution tiles and their diameter

It has long been known
[
1
,
19
]
that for a function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to tile with all rotates of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
it is necessary and sufficient that
\widehat{f}
vanishes on all spheres centered at the origin that contain any integer lattice point. This easily implies that for
d\geq 2
any such function must have unbounded support and even more quantitative lower bounds for the the rate of decay of
f
near infinity
[
19
,
18
,
24
]
.

This is no longer true if one restricts the number of rotates of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
that we demand
f
tiles with. It makes sense to generalize the question and ask for a function
f
which tiles with a given set of lattices
\Lambda_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, and such that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f

is small. We remind
[
24
]
that for
f
to tile with the lattices
\Lambda_{i}
it is necessary and sufficient for
\widehat{f}
to vanish on the dual lattices
\Lambda_{i}^{*}
, except at 0. (The dual lattice of a lattice
\Lambda=A{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, width
A
being a non-singular
d\times d
matrix, is the lattice
\Lambda^{*}=A^{-\top}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.)

Therefore we have to find a function
f
, with support of small diameter, such that
\widehat{f}
is 0 on each
\Lambda_{i}^{*}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
.
The first thing that comes to mind is to take
f
to be a convolution. It takes a moment to verify that if
f
tiles with a set of translates
T
then so does
g*f
for any
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. One can either verify this by checking the definition of tiling for
g*f
or observe that tiling is a condition that can be checked on the Fourier side
[
21
]
and
\widehat{g*f}=\widehat{g}\cdot\widehat{f}
has an even richer set of zeros that
\widehat{f}
.

So, since
\widehat{f}
has to vanish on the dual lattices
\Lambda_{i}^{*}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
we can take

(2.1)

f={\mathds{1}}_{D_{1}}*{\mathds{1}}_{D_{2}}*\cdots*{\mathds{1}}_{D_{N}},

where
D_{i}
is a fundamental parallelepiped of
\Lambda_{i}
. Since
D_{i}+\Lambda_{i}
is a tiling it follows that
\widehat{{\mathds{1}}_{D_{i}}}
vanishes on
\Lambda_{i}^{*}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}
and that
f
vanishes on their union and hence tiles with all
\Lambda_{i}
. This can be slightly generalized by taking, instead if the indicator functions
{\mathds{1}}_{D_{i}}
any function
f_{i}
that tiles with
\Lambda_{i}

(2.2)

f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots f_{N}.

The following observation was already made in
[
24
]
in the case
f_{i}={\mathds{1}}_{D_{i}}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}
are lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of volume
c_{1}\leq{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{i}
,
f_{i}
tiles with
\Lambda_{i}
at some non-zero level and
f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots*f_{N}
then

(2.3)

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C_{d}N.

Proof.

By the pigeonhole principle on can pick a coordinate axis, say the first one, such that at least
N/d
of the functions
f_{i}
have
{\rm supp\,}f_{i}
project onto the first coordinate axis on a set of diameter
\geq a_{d}
, where
a_{d}
is a constant that depends on
d
only. For if not then we would be able to find a
f_{i}
whose support is contained in a cube of arbirarily small side. Since
f_{i}
tiles with
\Lambda_{i}
its support has to contain at least one element from almost all of the cosets of
\Lambda_{i}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. This contradicts our assumed lower bound on
{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{i}
.

The Tichmarsh convolution theorem
[
8
]
says that

{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}(A*B)={\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}A+{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}B,

where
{\rm co\,}
denotes the convex hull of a set and
A,B
are two arbitrary integrable functions of compact support.

If we write
\phi
for the convolution of those
f_{i}
that we collected in the first paragraph of this proof and
\psi
for the remaining
f_{i}
then
f=\phi*\psi
and

{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}f={\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}\phi+{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}\psi,

which implies that

(2.4)

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f={\rm diam\,}{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq{\rm diam\,}{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}\phi\geq{\rm diam\,}\pi_{1}{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}\phi,

where
\pi_{1}
denotes projection onto the first coordinate axis.

Again by the Titchmarsh convolution theorem,
\pi_{1}{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}\phi
is the sum of the
\pi_{1}{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}f_{i}
for those
f_{i}
that participate in the definition of
\phi
and for these we know that

{\rm diam\,}\pi_{1}{\rm co\,}{\rm supp\,}f_{i}\geq{\rm diam\,}\pi_{1}{\rm supp\,}f_{i}\geq a_{d}.

But for any two one-dimensional sets
E,F\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
we have
{\rm diam\,}(E+F)={\rm diam\,}E+{\rm diam\,}F
, which implies, using (
2.4
), that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq\frac{a_{d}}{d}N,

as we had to prove.

∎

If the lattices
\Lambda_{i}
satisfy some “roundness” assumption, e.g. if each
\Lambda_{i}
is assumed to have a fundamental domain of diameter bounded independent of

(as in the important case when all the lattices are rotates of

.

On the other hand we have the following rather general lower bound for the diameter of the support

Theorem 2.2
.

If
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(2.5)

\Lambda_{i}\cap\Lambda_{j}={\left\{{0}\right\}}\ \ \text{ for all $i\neq j$,}

then if

(2.6)

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C_{d}N^{1/d}.

The main question is therefore:

Question 1
.

Can the gap between the lower bound (
2.6
) and the linear upper bound

achievable by the convolution tile (
2.1

Are there examples of lattices
\Lambda_{i}
,

2.5
) and a non-zero function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
that tiles with all

In other words, do there exist collections of lattices for which a common tile

Our next result is that for some collections of lattices the linear upper bound cannot be improved.
The lattices given are both “round” (have a fundamental domain bounded independent of
N
) and satisfy the genericity assumption (
2.5
). There are however collinearities so, in some sense, this is not a generic situtation.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
d\geq 1
and for each
N
there are lattices
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, of volume 1, such that if
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
\int f\neq 0
, tiles with all of them then

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C_{d}N.

Proof.

We give the proof in the case
d=2
. It works with obvious changes in all dimensions
d>2
and it is even easier in dimension
d=1
.

Take
\Lambda_{i}^{*}
to be generated by the two vectors

u_{i}=(0,a_{i}),v_{i}=(1/a_{i},0),

where the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are linearly independent over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and

0.9<a_{i}<1.

If
f
tiles with all
\Lambda_{i}
then
\widehat{f}
vanishes on all points of the form

(0,k\cdot a_{i}),\ \ i=1,2,\ldots,N,\ \ k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}.

Since all these points are different it follows that the density of zeros on the
y
-axis is
\geq C\cdot N
. This implies that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}\pi_{2}(f)\geq C\cdot N

(say, by Jensen’s formula) where
\pi_{2}(f)
is the one-variable function

\pi_{2}(f)(y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x,y)\,dx.

(This is not an identically zero function by our assumption on the integral of
f
.)
This is turn implies

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\geq C\cdot N.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

Let us point out here that, on the real line at least, the following trick can shorten the diameter of a convolution tile by 1, when tiling simultaneously by two integer lattices. For instance, suppose we want to find a common tile in
{\mathbb{R}}
for the lattices
\Lambda_{1}=m{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\Lambda_{2}=n{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If we take the convolution of the individual tiles
{\mathds{1}}_{[0,m]}
and
{\mathds{1}}_{[0,n]}
then we obtain the commong tile

f_{1}={\mathds{1}}_{[0,m]}*{\mathds{1}}_{[0,n]},

which has diameter
m+n
.

But we can also work first in
{\mathbb{Z}}
and then extend to
{\mathbb{R}}
: the functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}

{\mathds{1}}_{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,m-1}\right\}}\text{ and }{\mathds{1}}_{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,n-1}\right\}}

tile
{\mathbb{Z}}
with the lattices
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
respectively. Taking their convolution in
{\mathbb{Z}}

g={\mathds{1}}_{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,m-1}\right\}}*{\mathds{1}}_{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,n-1}\right\}}

we obtain a function on
{\mathbb{Z}}
that tiles
{\mathbb{Z}}
with both
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
and
has

{\rm supp\,}g={\left\{{0,1,\ldots,m+n-2}\right\}}.

Defining now (with a slight abuse of notation)

f_{2}=g*{\mathds{1}}_{[0,1]}

we obtain a function
f_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
that tiles
{\mathbb{R}}
with both
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
and has support

{\rm supp\,}f_{2}=[0,m+n-1],

thus improving by 1 on the diameter of
f_{1}
.

2.2.
Common tiles whose support has small volume

Another measure of smallness of the support is its volume. Can we construct a common tile
f
for the lattices
\Lambda_{i}
such that
{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}
is small?

In the case of
f
given by (
2.1
) it is clear that

{\rm supp\,}{f}=D_{1}+D_{2}+\ldots+D_{N}.

To keep things concrete let us assume that all
{\left|{D_{i}}\right|}=1
in (
2.1
) (unimodular lattices). Then the Brunn-Minkowski inequality
[

]
says that

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}={\left|{D_{1}+\cdots+D_{N}}\right|}\geq\left({\left|{D_{1}}\right|}^{1/d}+\cdots+{\left|{D_{N}}\right|}^{1/d}\right)^{d}\geq N^{d}.

This lower bound

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}\geq CN^{d}

clearly holds also for functions of the form

(2.7)

f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots*f_{N},\ \ \ f_{i}\geq 0,

where for all

we assume that the
nonnegative
function
f
tiles with
\Lambda_{i}

We have proved:

Theorem 2.5
.

For any collection of lattices
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of volume at least 1 and any common tile

for them of the form

f=f_{1}*f_{2}*\cdots*f_{N},\ \ \ f_{i}\geq 0,

with
f_{i}
tiling with
\Lambda_{i}
, we have

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}\geq N^{d}.

But when the functions

are signed (or complex) we only have

{\rm supp\,}{f}\subseteq{\rm supp\,}{f_{1}}+\cdots{\rm supp\,}{f_{N}},

not necessarily equality, which brings us to the next question.

Question 2

If
f
is given by (
2.7

Suppose the nonnegative

If we assume the first equation in (

Proof.

2.3.
Allowing for lattices with many relations

If we have
N

\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

f

\sim 1

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=O(N)
, and this cannot be improved for functions

Fix a large prime

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{d}

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{d}
has

\frac{p^{d}-1}{p-1}\sim p^{d-1}=:N

different cyclic subgroups. For each such subgroup
G
, which we now view as a subset of
{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,p-1}\right\}}^{d}
, consider the lattice

\Lambda_{G}=(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}+G,

which contains the lattice
\Lambda=(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}
and has volume

{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{G}=\frac{{\rm vol\,}(p{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}}{{\left|{G}\right|}}=p^{d-1}=N.

The function
f={\mathds{1}}_{[0,p)^{d}}
,
[0,p)^{d}
being a fundamental domain of
\Lambda
, tiles with
\Lambda
and, therefore, with any larger group, so
f
is a common tile of all
\Lambda_{G}
.

In order to make the volume of the
\Lambda_{G}
equal to 1 we shrink everything by
N^{1/d}
:

\Lambda_{G}^{\prime}=N^{-1/d}\Lambda_{G},\ \ \ f^{\prime}(x)=f(N^{1/d}x).

So we have
\sim N
lattices
\Lambda^{\prime}_{G}
of volume 1 and a common tile
f^{\prime}
for them with

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f^{\prime}={\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\cdot N^{-1/d}=\sqrt{d}\,pN^{-1/d}=\sqrt{d}\,N^{\frac{1}{d-1}-\frac{1}{d}}=\sqrt{d}\,N^{\frac{1}{d(d-1)}}.

We have proved:

Theorem 2.10
.

In dimension
d\geq 2
and for arbitrarily large
N
we can find
N
lattices of volume
1
and a common tile
f
for them with

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=O_{d}\left(N^{\frac{1}{d(d-1)}}\right),

and, consequently, with

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}=O_{d}\left(N^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\right).

Question 5
.

Derive a lower bound for
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f
, for
f
tiling with
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{N}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and with
f\geq 0
(or just
\int f>0
) under no algebraic conditions for the lattices
\Lambda_{j}
, assuming only that
{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{j}\sim 1
.

The construction that we used to prove Theorem
2.10
gives nothing in dimension
d=1
. Yet, we can prove that, if we allow relations among the lattices, we can achieve
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=o(N)
in dimension 1 as well.

Let us start by defining

\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{N+j},\ \ \ \Lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}{\mathbb{Z}},\ \ \ (j=1,2,\ldots,N).

We will first construct a function
f
which tiles with all the
\Lambda_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,N
, such that

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f=o(1).

The Fourier transform of such an
f
must vanish on the dual lattices

\Lambda_{j}^{*}=\lambda_{j}^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}=(N+j){\mathbb{Z}},\ \ \ (j=1,2,\ldots,N)

except at 0.
Write

U=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(N+j){\mathbb{Z}}\ \setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}.

By a result of Erdős
[
9
]

U
, the set of integers which are divisible by one of the integers in
{\left\{{N+1,N+2,\ldots,2N}\right\}}
, has density tending to 0 with
N
. Tenenbaum
[
33
]
has given the estimate that this density is at most

(2.11)

\frac{1}{\log^{\delta-o(1)}N},

where
\delta=0.086071\cdots
is an explicit constant.

It is an important result of Beurling
[
2
]
that if
\Lambda
is a uniformly discrete set of real numbers of upper density
\rho
then for any
\epsilon>0
we can find a continuous function
f
, not identically zero, supported by the interval
[0,\rho+\epsilon]
such that
\widehat{f}(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
. We can even ask that
\widehat{f}(0)=1
if
0\notin\Lambda
. By Tenenbaum’s estimate (
2.11
) we can take
\rho=\log^{-\delta+o(1)}N
and the set
U
, being a set of integers and thus uniformly discrete, satisfies the assumptions of Beurling’s theorem, so there is a function
f
supported in the interval
[0,\log^{-\delta+o(1)}N]
, with integral 1, such that
\widehat{f}=0
on
U
. It follows that
f
tiles with all
\Lambda_{j}
.

We now scale by a factor of
N

f^{\prime}(x)=f(x/N),\ \ \ \Lambda_{j}^{\prime}=N\Lambda_{j},\ \ \ {\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f^{\prime}=O(N\log^{-\delta+o(1)}N)

and obtain the first half of the following theorem.

Theorem 2.11
.

We can find
N
lattices
\Lambda_{j}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
of with
{\rm vol\,}\Lambda_{j}\sim 1
and a function
f
with
\int f>0
and supported in an interval of length

\frac{N}{\log^{\delta-o(1)}N)}

which tiles with all
\Lambda_{j}
.

Furthermore, for any
\epsilon>0
any such function
f
must have

{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\gtrsim_{\epsilon}N^{1-\epsilon}.

Arguing similarly we can also prove the lower bound for
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f
in Theorem
2.11
. If we assume that
f
tiles with all
\Lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}
, with, say,
1\leq\lambda_{j}\leq 2
,
j=1,2,\ldots,N
, then
\widehat{f}
vanishes on

\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}\ \ \setminus{\left\{{0}\right\}}.

If this set is large then Jensen’s formula implies that
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f
is also large.
It was proved in
[
12
, Theorem 1.1, special case
\ell=n
]
that, for any
\epsilon>0
, the above union of arithmetic progressions contains at least
c_{\epsilon}N^{2-\epsilon}
points in
[0,2N]
. By Jensen’s formula then we have
{\rm diam\,}{\rm supp\,}f\gtrsim_{\epsilon}N^{1-\epsilon}
and this completes the proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Question 6
.

Can we ensure
f\geq 0
in the first half of Theorem
2.11
?

3.
The common fundamental domain problem without measurability

In
[
20
]
the following theorem was proved in §3.2.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\Lambda_{0},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
are lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of the same volume and with the sum
\Lambda_{0}+\Lambda_{1}+\cdots+\Lambda_{n}
being direct then there is a bounded common fundamental domain
F
for all these lattices.

Remark 3.2
.

No measurability is claimed for
F
and the set
F
constructed in
[
20
]
is not measurable.

The question was left open in
[
20
]
whether the equal volume assumption was necessary. This assumption is obviously necessary if we ask for a measurable tile as the volume of the tile equals the volume of each lattice it tiles with. But there is no a priori reason for this requirement to hold if we cannot measure volumes, as with tiles that are not necessarily measurable. But, we show here that, indeed it is necessary, by giving a pair of lattices, with no common elements, which have different volumes and have no bounded common fundamental domain.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda_{1}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
\Lambda_{2}=\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, with
\alpha
irrational and
d\geq 1
. Then there is no bounded set
F\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which consists of exactly one representative from each coset of each
\Lambda_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

Proof.

We give the proof in dimension
d=1
for clarity, as it is essentially the same for all
d
. Without loss of generality we take
\alpha>1
.

As explained in
[
20
, Proof of Theorem 1]
it is enough to show that
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
do not have a bounded common fundamental domain in the group

G=\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}={\left\{{m+n\alpha:m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\right\}}.

Suppose
F
is just such a bounded common fundamental domain

F={\left\{{m_{i}-n_{i}\alpha:i=1,2,\ldots}\right\}}\subseteq[-M,M].

For
F
to be a fundamental domain it must contain precisely one point in the set
k+\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}
and one point in the set
{\mathbb{Z}}+k\alpha
, for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. By renumbering then we can write

F={\left\{{m-n_{m}\alpha:m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\right\}}.

Let now
R>0
be large and consider the following set of values for
m
:

(3.1)

-R\leq m\leq R.

For such values of
m
and from the presumed bound

{\left|{m-n_{m}\alpha}\right|}\leq M

we have the bounds

(3.2)

-\frac{R+M}{\alpha}\leq n_{m}\leq\frac{R+M}{\alpha}.

As
R\to\infty
the number of values of
m
allowed by (
3.1
) are
\sim 2R
in number. The number of the corresponding values of
n
allowed by (
3.2
) is
\sim 2R/\alpha
, which is strictly smaller if
R
is large since we have taken
\alpha>1
. This is a contradiction as there must be exactly one
n_{m}
for each
m
and all the
n_{m}
’s are different.
∎

4.
The problem in finite abelian groups

Suppose we have a finite abelian group
G
and two subgroups
G_{1},G_{2}
of the same index

n=[G:G_{1}]=[G:G_{2}].

We can ask whether we can find a common fundamental domain for
G_{1},G_{2}
in
G
, i.e. a set of
n
elements

F={\left\{{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}}\right\}}

which tiles with both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. This always exists, even in the non-abelian case, if properly defined, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

If we drop the equal index assumption we can still ask for a
function

f
defined on
G
which tiles with both subgroups:

(4.1)

\forall x\in G:\ \ \sum_{g_{1}\in G_{1}}f(x-g_{1})={\left|{G_{1}}\right|},\ \ \sum_{g_{2}\in G_{2}}f(x-g_{2})={\left|{G_{2}}\right|}.

The question we are interested in here is:

Given
G,G_{1},G_{2}
how small can the size of the support of
f
be?

Under the assumption of equal index the answer to the above question is that, since we can find a common fundamental domain of
G_{1},G_{2}
in
G

[
20
]
, the size of the support of
f
can be as small as
[G:G_{1}]=[G:G_{2}]
. Of course it cannot be smaller than that. But once we drop the equal index assumption then the only general construction we know is the convolution of the indicator functions of the fundamental domains
D_{i}
of
G_{i}
:

(4.2)

f=c{\mathds{1}}_{D_{1}}*{\mathds{1}}_{D_{2}},

which gives, with
c=\frac{{\left|{G}\right|}}{{\left|{D_{1}}\right|}\cdot{\left|{D_{2}}\right|}}
,

f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}}=c{\mathds{1}}_{D_{2}}*{\mathds{1}}_{D_{1}}*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}}=c{\mathds{1}}_{D_{2}}*{\mathds{1}}_{G}=c{\left|{D_{2}}\right|}={\left|{G_{1}}\right|},

and similarly for
f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{2}}
.
But the support of
f
in (
4.2
) can be quite large, a priori as large as
{\left|{D_{1}}\right|}\cdot{\left|{D_{2}}\right|}
.

From now on we restrict our functions
f
to be nonnegative and normalized as shown in (
4.1
). We also usually restrict our tiles to be nonnegative functions.

Definition 4.1
.

If
G_{1},G_{2}
are subgroups of the finite abelian group
G
we write

S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}=\min{\left\{{{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}:\text{where }f:G\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0},f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}}={\left|{G_{1}}\right|},\ f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{2}}={\left|{G_{2}}\right|}}\right\}}.

It is always the case that

S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}\geq[G:G_{i}],\ \ \ (i=1,2).

Observe that if
G_{1},G_{2}
have a common fundamental domain
F
in
G
then

S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}={\left|{F}\right|}.

The following result says that we can always restrict our study to the case of
G_{1}\cap G_{2}
being trivial. In other words we may assume from now on that
G
is the direct sum of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
G_{1},G_{2}\subseteq G
are finite abelian groups and

\Gamma=G/(G_{1}\cap G_{2}),\ \ \Gamma_{i}=G_{i}/(G_{1}\cap G_{2}),\ \ \ (i=1,2)

then

(4.3)

S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}=S_{\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}}^{\Gamma}.

Proof.

In what follows
\Gamma
or
\Gamma_{i}
may denote either the quotient group or an arbitrary but fixed set of coset representatives of the subgroup
G_{1}\cap G_{2}
.

Let
f:G\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}
satisfy

{\left|{G_{i}}\right|}=f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{i}}=(f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}\cap G_{2}})*{\mathds{1}}_{\Gamma_{i}},\ \ \ (i=1,2).

If
{\rm supp\,}f
is minimal it follows from the above representation that
{\rm supp\,}f
has at most one point in each
(G_{1}\cap G_{2})
-coset, as we can always collect all the “mass” of
f
contained in one coset to one point of the coset without affecting
f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}
.

Define
F:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}
by

F(\gamma)=\frac{1}{{\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}}\sum_{g\in G_{1}\cap G_{2}}f(\gamma+g)=\frac{1}{{\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}}f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}(\gamma).

It follows, under the assumption that
{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}
is minimal, that

{\left|{{\rm supp\,}F}\right|}={\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}.

We also have, for
i=1,2
,

F*{\mathds{1}}_{\Gamma_{i}}=\frac{1}{{\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}}f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}*{\mathds{1}}_{\Gamma_{i}}=\frac{1}{{\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}}f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{i}}=\frac{{\left|{G_{i}}\right|}}{{\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}}={\left|{\Gamma_{i}}\right|}.

It follows that

S_{\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}}^{\Gamma}\leq S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}.

To prove the reverse inequality we start with a function
F:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}
satisfying

F*{\mathds{1}}_{\Gamma_{i}}={\left|{\Gamma_{i}}\right|},\ \ \ (i=1,2)

and define
f:G\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}
by taking
f(x)
to be

{\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}\cdot F(x+G_{1}\cap G_{2})

at precisely one point
x
in each
(G_{1}\cap G_{2})
-coset, and in all other points of the coset we take it to be 0.

It follows that
{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}={\left|{{\rm supp\,}F}\right|}
,
and we also have, for
i=1,2
, and viewing
F
as a function on
G
(constant on
(G_{1}\cap G_{2})
-cosets),

f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{i}}=f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}*{\mathds{1}}_{\Gamma_{i}}={\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}\cdot F*{\mathds{1}}_{\Gamma_{i}}={\left|{G_{1}\cap G_{2}}\right|}\cdot{\left|{\Gamma_{i}}\right|}={\left|{G_{i}}\right|}.

This concludes the proof of the reverse inequality and the Theorem.

∎

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
from now on. The following theorem is the best understood case.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
and
{\left|{G_{1}}\right|}
divides
{\left|{G_{2}}\right|}
then

(4.4)

S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}=[G:G_{1}]={\left|{G_{2}}\right|}.

Proof.

Enumerate the two subgroups arbitrarily as

G_{1}={\left\{{g^{1}_{1},\ldots,g^{1}_{m}}\right\}},\ \ G_{2}={\left\{{g^{2}_{1},\ldots,g^{2}_{n}}\right\}},

with
n=km
,
k\geq 1
.
Take
f={\left|{G_{1}}\right|}{\mathds{1}}_{F}
where the set
F
is constructed by taking sums of the elements of
G_{2}
with the “corresponding” elements of
G_{1}
(but the elements of
G_{1}
have to be repeated
k
times each). So
F
consists of the sums

\displaystyle\ g^{1}_{1}+g^{2}_{1},\ g^{1}_{2}+g^{2}_{2},\ldots,g^{1}_{m}+g^{2}_{m},

\displaystyle\ g^{1}_{1}+g^{2}_{m+1},\ g^{1}_{2}+g^{2}_{m+2},\ldots,g^{1}_{m}+g^{2}_{m+m}

\displaystyle\ g^{1}_{1}+g^{2}_{2m+1},\ldots,g^{1}_{m}+g^{2}_{2m+m}

\displaystyle\ \ldots

It is easy to see that
F
is a fundamental domain for
G_{1}
and that

f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{1}}={\left|{G_{1}}\right|},\ \ \ f*{\mathds{1}}_{G_{2}}=k{\left|{G_{1}}\right|}={\left|{G_{2}}\right|}.

Finally
{\left|{{\rm supp\,}f}\right|}={\left|{G_{2}}\right|}
, which proves the Theorem as we always have
S_{G_{1},G_{2}}^{G}\geq{\left|{G_{2}}\right|}
.
∎

It is clear now that in studying the problem in the group
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
the group structure is irrelevant and, writing
m={\left|{G_{1}}\right|}
and
n={\left|{G_{2}}\right|}
, the problem is to find a nonnegative real matrix

A\in({\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0})^{m\times n}

with row sums all equal to
n
and column sums all equal to
m
, and with as small a support (non-zero entries) as possible.

Matrices of this type or, rather, the matrices
\frac{1}{mn}A
, and, more generally, multivariate distributions with uniform marginals, are called
copulas
in statistics and have been studied extensively
[
30
]
. They can be used to “isolate” the marginal distributions of a general multivariate distribution from the dependence part of distribution.

Definition 4.4
.

Write
A(m,n)

for the set of all such
m\times n
matrices (with nonnegative entries and all row sums equal to
n
and all column sums equal to
m
) and

S(m,n)=\min{\left\{{{\left|{{\rm supp\,}A}\right|}:\ A\in A(m,n)}\right\}}.

In this notation Theorem
4.3
says that

S(m,km)=km,\ \ \text{ if }m,k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

In what follows we use
[
4
]
, where the structure of the matrices in
A(m,n)
of minimal support is described up to permutation of rows and columns (these operations obviously leave
A(m,n)
unchanged and also do not alter the size of the support of each matrix).

That the situation changes radically if
m
does not divide
n
can be seen, for example, by the following.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
k\geq 1
,
1\leq r<m
, then

S(m,km+r)\geq(k+1)m.

If
r=1
we have

S(m,km+1)=(k+1)m.

Proof.

Column sums are equal to
m
, so none of the entries is
>m
. Since row sums are equal to
km+r
it follows that we have at least
k+1
non-zero terms in each of the
m
rows. This shows the lower bound
S(m,km+r)\geq m(k+1)
. To show the next claim we check that the
m\times(km+1)
matrix

m\underbrace{\left\{\ \begin{bmatrix}1&\overbrace{m\cdots m}^{k}&\cdots&\cdots\\
1&\cdots&\overbrace{m\cdots m}^{k}&\cdots\\
\\
\ &\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
1&\cdots&\cdots&\overbrace{m\cdots m}^{k}\end{bmatrix}\right.}_{km+1}

has constant row and column sums. Since its support has size
(k+1)m
, which is the minimum possible by the first part of the Lemma, we are done.
∎

S(m,km+r)
