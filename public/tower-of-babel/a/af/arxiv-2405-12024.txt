Polynomials and algebraic curves related to certain binary and b b -ary overpartitions Thanks: Research supported in part by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, Grant # 145628481
Karl Dilcher, Larry Ericksen

Abstract
. We begin by considering a sequence of polynomials in three variables whose coefficients count restricted binary overpartitions with certain properties. We then concentrate on two specific subsequences that are closely related to the Chebyshev polynomials of both kinds, deriving combinatorial and algebraic properties of some special cases. We show that the zeros of these polynomial sequences lie on certain algebraic curves, some of which we study in greater detail. Finally, we extend part of this work to restricted b b -ary overpartitions for arbitrary integers b ≥ 2 b\geq 2 .

1.
Introduction

While the basic theory of binary partitions goes back to Euler

[
4
, p. 162ff.]
, quite recently Rødseth and Sellers
[
10
]

introduced and studied
b
-ary overpartitions for a fixed integer base

b\geq 2
, in analogy to ordinary overpartitions that had been introduced a
little earlier by Corteel and Lovejoy
[
2
]
.

A
b
-ary overpartition
of an integer
n\geq 1
is a non-increasing
sequence of nonnegative integer powers of
b
whose sum is
n
, and where the
first occurrence of a power
b
may be overlined. We denote the number of

b
-ary overpartitions by
\overline{S}_{b}(n)
, which differs from the notation
in
[
10
]
.

Example 1.1
.

(See
[
10
, p. 346]
). The binary overpartitions of
n=4
are

4,\;\overline{4},\;2+2,\;\overline{2}+2,\;2+1+1,\;2+\overline{1}+1,\;\overline{2}+1+1,\;\overline{2}+\overline{1}+1,\;1+1+1+1,\;\overline{1}+1+1+1.

Thus
\overline{S}_{2}(4)=10
.

As is illustrated in this example, the overlined parts form a
b
-ary
partition into distinct parts, while the non-overlined parts form an
ordinary
b
-ary partition. We can now see that the generating function is

(1.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{S}_{b}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1+q^{b^{j}}}{1-q^{b^{j}}}.

The concept of a
b
-ary overpartition can be restricted in different ways,
only one of which we will consider here. In analogy to the restricted
b
-ary
partitions, such as hyperbinary representations, we restrict the number of
times a non-overlined power of
b
may occur in a
b
-ary overpartition; we
denote this number by
\lambda
and call such overpartitions

\lambda
-
restricted
. In this case the generating function is

(1.2)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{S}_{b}^{\lambda}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(1+q^{b^{j}}\right)\left(1+q^{b^{j}}+q^{2\cdot b^{j}}+\dots+q^{\lambda\cdot b^{j}}\right),

where
\overline{S}_{b}^{\lambda}(n)
is the number of
b
-ary overpartitions of

n
in which each non-overlined power of
b
may occur at most
\lambda
times.

Example 1.2
.

Let
b=\lambda=2
. Then (
1.2
) becomes

(1.3)

1+2q+4q^{2}+5q^{3}+8q^{4}+10q^{5}+13q^{6}+14q^{7}+18q^{8}+21q^{9}+26q^{10}+\cdots

Thus, in particular,
\overline{S}_{2}^{2}(4)=8
, which is consistent with
Example
1.1
, where all but the last two binary overpartitions are counted by

\overline{S}_{2}^{2}(4)
. The series (
1.3
) can also be found in
[
7
]
.
This last paper deals with the case
\lambda=b
as well, but
in contrast to our work it focuses on congruences of the relevant numerical
sequences.

In the recent paper
[
3
]
we defined the concept of restricted multicolor

b
-ary partitions as a generalization of restricted
b
-ary overpartitions,
and further defined polynomial analogues of the relevant partition functions.
These polynomials then allowed us to not just count the partitions in question,
but to characterize them. We will not be concerned with this aspect of the
theory in the present paper.

In the special case of restricted
b
-ary overpartitions with
\lambda=2
, the
polynomials introduced in
[
3
]
specialize as follows. Let
Z=(x,y,z)
be
a triple of variables, and
T=(r,s,t)
a triple of positive integers. Then, in
the notation of
[
3
, Def. 2.4]
, we define

(1.4)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Omega_{b,T}^{(1,2)}(n;Z)q^{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(1+x^{r^{j}}q^{b^{j}}\right)\left(1+y^{s^{j}}q^{b^{j}}+z^{t^{j}}q^{2\cdot b^{j}}\right).

Comparing this with (
1.2
), we immediately get, for any base
b\geq 2
,

(1.5)

\overline{S}_{b}^{2}(n)=\Omega_{b,T}^{(1,2)}(n;1,1,1),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

where the triple
T
is arbitrary.

The main purpose of this paper is to consider various aspects of the
polynomial sequence defined by (
1.4
) in the special case
r=s=t=1
.
After deriving a few basic properties in Section
2
, we consider two
particular subsequences in Section
3
, which turn out to be closely
related to the Chebyshev polynomials of both kinds. In the following two
sections we then specialize the variables
x,y,z
in two different ways,
obtaining polynomial sequences in one, resp. two, variables with interesting
properties. In particular, we derive divisibility properties and combinatorial
interpretations of these polynomials. Section
7
is then devoted to
the zero distribution of these and a few other related polynomial sequences.
One such curve, a particularly interesting quartic of genus 0, is studied in
greater detail in Section
8
. Finally in Section
9
, we
show that much of the content of Sections
2
–
6
can be
generalized to an arbitrary integer base
b\geq 2
with
\lambda=b
.

3.
Connections with Chebyshev polynomials

The main objects of this section are two subsequences of the polynomial sequence

p_{n}(x,y,z)
, namely

(3.1)

Q_{n}(x,y,z):=p_{2^{n+1}-2}(x,y,z),\qquad R_{n}(x,y,z):=p_{2^{n}-1}(x,y,z),

for all
n\geq 0
.
Combining these definitions with the recurrence relation (
2.4
), we get
as a first consequence,

(3.2)

R_{n+1}(x,y,z)=(x+y)\cdot R_{n}(x,y,z)+xz\cdot Q_{n-1}(x,y,z),\qquad n\geq 1.

However, the following three-term recurrence relations will be more important.
For greater ease of notation we suppress the arguments
x,y,z
.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have
Q_{0}=1
,
Q_{1}=xy+x+y+z
,
R_{0}=1
,
R_{1}=x+y
, and for
n\geq 1
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(xy+x+y+z)\cdot Q_{n}-(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)\cdot Q_{n-1},

(3.4)

\displaystyle R_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(xy+x+y+z)\cdot R_{n}-(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)\cdot R_{n-1}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
, and for further ease of notation we set

P(n):=p_{n}(x,y,z)
. First, the expressions for
Q_{0},Q_{1},R_{0},R_{1}

follow from (
3.1
) and Table 1. Also, by (
3.1
) we have
Q_{2}=P(6)

and
R_{2}=P(3)
.
With the relevant entries in Table 1 we can now verify (
3.3
) and
(
3.4
) for
n=1
, which is the induction beginning.

Suppose now that (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are true up to some
n-1
in place
of
n
; our aim is to show that they hold also for
n
, that is, as written in
(
3.3
) and (
3.4
). By the induction hypothesis and (
3.1
) we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle P(2^{n}-2)

\displaystyle=(xy+x+y+z)P(2^{n-1}-2)-(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)P(2^{n-2}-2),

(3.6)

\displaystyle P(2^{n}-1)

\displaystyle=(xy+x+y+z)P(2^{n-1}-1)-(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)P(2^{n-2}-1).

We multiply both sides of (

) by

, and both sides of (

) by

. Applying (

) three times, namely for

∎

With the recurrence relations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) we can now obtain
generating functions for the two polynomial sequences.

Proposition 3.2
.

The polynomials
Q_{n}
and
R_{n}
satisfy the generating functions

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{n}(x,y,z)q^{n}=\frac{1}{1-(xy+x+y+z)q+(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)q^{2}},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}R_{n}(x,y,z)q^{n}=\frac{1-(xy+z)q}{1-(xy+x+y+z)q+(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)q^{2}}.

Proof.

We multiply both sides of (
3.9
) by the denominator on the right, and
take the Cauchy product with the power series on the left. Then the constant
coefficient is
Q_{0}(x,y,z)=1
, while the coefficient of
x
is zero since

Q_{1}(x,y,z)=xy+x+y+z
; all other coefficients also vanish, as a consequence of
(
3.3
). This proves (
3.9
), and (
3.10
) is obtained analogously,
using (
3.4

∎

Both Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
indicate that there might
be a connection with Chebyshev polynomials. This is indeed the case, as the

(3.11)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T_{n}(w)v^{n}=\frac{1-wv}{1-2wv+v^{2}},\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}U_{n}(w)v^{n}=\frac{1}{1-2wv+v^{2}}.

Using these polynomials, we can now state and prove the following identities.

Proposition 3.3
.

For all

(3.12)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\big(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz\big)^{n/2}U_{n}\left(\frac{xy+x+y+z}{2(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)^{1/2}}\right),

(3.13)

\displaystyle R_{n}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\big(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz\big)^{n/2}T_{n}\left(\frac{xy+x+y+z}{2(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)^{1/2}}\right)+\widetilde{U}_{n-1},

where

\widetilde{U}_{n-1}:=\frac{x+y-xy-z}{2}\cdot Q_{n-1}(x,y,z).

Comparing (
3.9
) with the second identity in (
3.11
), we see that

q=\frac{v}{(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)^{1/2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad w=\frac{xy+x+y+z}{2(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)^{1/2}}.

Equating coefficients of

then gives (
3.12
).

Next, with
w
and
v
as above, we rewrite the numerator on the right of

\displaystyle 1-(xy+z)q

\displaystyle=(1-wv)+\frac{x+y-xy-z}{2}\cdot q

\displaystyle=(1-wv)+\frac{x+y-xy-z}{2(x^{2}y+xy^{2}+yz)^{1/2}}\cdot v.

The term

, together with the first identity in (
3.11
), leads to the
first summand in (

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
.

(a)

Q_{n}(x)
and
R_{n}(x)
are palindromic polynomials of degree
n
.

(b)

Their zeros are real and negative, and with the exception of

they appear in pairs whose product is
1
and whose sum can be arbitrarily large

(c)

Q_{n-1}(x)
is a divisibility sequence: if

Q_{m-1}(x)|Q_{n-1}(x)
.

(d)

The sums of the coefficients are

Q_{n}(1)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(3^{n+1}-1\bigr),\qquad R_{n}(1)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(3^{n}+1\bigr),

and in particular,
R_{n+1}(1)=Q_{n}(1)+1
.

(a) With (

Proposition 4.3
.

For any integer

The even- and odd-index entries of the rows of the trinomial triangle are

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

We use the defining identities

\sin{\theta}\cdot U_{n-1}(\cos{\theta})=\sin(n\theta),\qquad T_{n}(\cos{\theta})=\cos(n\theta).

Multiplying both sides of the left identity by
i
, then adding both and using

2i\sin{\theta}=e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta},\,2\cos{\theta}=e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}
, and
w:=e^{i\theta}
, we get

(4.5)

\frac{w-w^{-1}}{2}U_{n-1}\left(\frac{w+w^{-1}}{2}\right)+T_{n}\left(\frac{w+w^{-1}}{2}\right)=w^{n},\qquad n\geq 1.

We now set

w=\sqrt{\frac{1+x+x^{2}}{1-x+x^{2}}}=\frac{1+x+x^{2}}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}+x^{4}}}.

Then after some straightforward manipulations, which involves the
factorization

, we get

(4.6)

\frac{w+w^{-1}}{2}=\frac{1+x^{2}}{(1+x^{2}+x^{4})^{1/2}}.

Similarly, we obtain

(4.7)

\frac{w-w^{-1}}{2}=\frac{x}{(1+x^{2}+x^{4})^{1/2}}.

Substituting (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) into (
4.5
), we get

(4.8)

\frac{x}{(1+x^{2}+x^{4})^{1/2}}U_{n-1}\left(\frac{w+w^{-1}}{2}\right)+T_{n}\left(\frac{w+w^{-1}}{2}\right)=\frac{(1+x+x^{2})^{n}}{(1+x^{2}+x^{4})^{n/2}}.

Finally, multiplying both sides of (
4.8
) by
(1+x^{2}+x^{4})^{n/2}
and
using (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we get the desired identity (
4.4
).
∎

Proposition 5.10

The fact that this expression is a polynomial with integer coefficients
follows, for instance, from Corollary
6.2
. If we denote it by

,
then we easily see that

, which shows that
Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})
is
self-reciprocal of degree
2n
. Since
f(0)=1
, this polynomial has constant
coefficient 1 and is thus also monic. The final statement follows from

f(1)=(3^{n+1}-1)/2
.
∎

Given the form of the numerator in (

), it is not
surprising that there should be a connection between the polynomials

Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})
and trinomial coefficients. In fact, the central coefficients
1, 2, 5, 12, 31, 82,
\ldots
, are listed in
[
8
, A097893]
as partial
sums of the central trinomial coefficients, with numerous properties shown
there.

The fact that the polynomials
Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})
are self-reciprocal, combined with
Corollary
6.4
, gives the following.

Corollary 6.7
.

Given
n\geq 1
, consider the set of all
2
-restricted binary overpartitions
of
2^{n+1}-2
, and let
j
be such that
0\leq j\leq 2n
.
Then the number of partitions with
j
non-overlined parts is equal to
those with
2n-j
non-overlined parts.

We conclude this subsection with some results on factors and irreducibility.
As usual,
\Phi_{d}(x)
will denote the

.

(a)

Proof of Proposition

.

We use the well-known identity

(6.13)

w^{n+1}-1=\prod_{d\mid n+1}\Phi_{d}(w),

substitute

(2z+1)^{n+1}-z^{n+1}=\prod_{d\mid n+1}\left(z^{\varphi(d)}\Phi_{d}(2+z^{-1})\right),

\sum_{d\mid n+1}\varphi(d)=n+1

Q_{n}(1,z,z)=\frac{z^{n+1}}{z+1}\left(\left(\frac{2z+1}{z}\right)^{n+1}-1\right),

which follows easily from (

note that
\Phi_{d}(w)
has degree

, so all factors

are indeed polynomials.

Next, since
\Phi_{d}(w)
is irreducible, the linear shift
\Phi_{d}(2+z^{-1})

gives an irreducible polynomial in

, and

z^{\varphi(d)}\Phi_{d}(2+z^{-1})
is then irreducible as a polynomial in
z

is irreducible when
n+1
is prime.

7.
Zero distributions of
Q_{n}(1,z,z)
and
Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})

Since
Q_{n}(1,z,z)
and
Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})
are single-variable polynomials,
it makes sense to consider their zero distribution. We begin with the
easier case.

Proposition 7.1
.

For any
n\geq 1
, the zeros of
Q_{n}(1,z,z)
are given by

(7.1)

z_{j}=\frac{1}{\zeta_{j}-2},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,n,

where
\zeta_{j}:=e^{2\pi ij/(n+1)}
is an
(n+1)
th root of unity.
Furthermore, the zeros
z_{j}
all lie on the circle given by

(7.2)

\left(x+\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{2}+y^{2}=\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We consider the identity (
6.15
) and note that
the term in large parentheses is zero exactly when
(2z+1)/z=\zeta_{j}
,

j=0,1,\ldots,n
, which is equivalent to
z=1/(\zeta_{j}-2)
. When
j=0
, we get

z=-1
; however, a limit argument shows that
z=-1
is not a zero of the
right-hand side of (
6.15
). This proves (
7.1
).

For the second statement, we note that by the theory of fractional linear
transformations all the
z_{j}
lie on a circle (or a straight line) since all

\zeta_{j}
lie on a circle. It therefore suffices to show that any three
distinct points
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|\zeta|=1
are mapped to points

z=x+iy
satisfying (
7.2
). It is easy to verify this with the choice of

\zeta=-1,\pm i
, for instance; this completes the proof.
∎

The zero distribution of
Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})
turns out to be more interesting than
that of
Q_{n}(1,z,z)
. We begin with a lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

For any
n\geq 1
, the
2n
zeros of
Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})
are given by

(7.3)

z_{j}^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\zeta_{j}-1+\left(\zeta_{j}^{2}-2\zeta_{j}-3\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right),\quad j=1,2,\ldots,n,

where
\zeta_{j}:=e^{2\pi ij/(n+1)}
are the
(n+1)
th roots of unity and the
superscript
\pm
indicates that for each
j
we have two values of (
7.3
).

Proof.

Upon slightly rewriting (
6.8
), we have

(7.4)

Q_{n}(1,z,z^{2})=\frac{z^{n+1}}{z^{2}+1}\left(\left(z+1+\frac{1}{z}\right)^{n+1}-1\right).

For this expression to vanish, we need

(7.5)

z+1+\frac{1}{z}=\zeta_{j},\quad\hbox{or}\quad z^{2}+(1-\zeta_{j})z+1=0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,n.

Solving this last equation, we get (
7.3
) with
j=0,1,\ldots,n
.
However, since
\zeta_{0}=1
, we have
z_{0}^{\pm}=\pm i
, a pair of solutions
which is canceled by the denominator in (
7.4
). Hence
j=0
needs to be
excluded, which completes the proof.
∎

In the paragraph after (
7.5

\pm i
cannot be zeros of

for any

. On the other hand, by substituting

Proposition 7.3
.

The zeros of all polynomials

(7.6)

x^{4}+2x^{2}y^{2}+y^{4}+2x^{3}+2xy^{2}+2x^{2}-2y^{2}+2x+1=0

(7.7)

\big(x^{2}+y^{2}+x\big)^{2}+(x+1)^{2}=2y^{2}.

Proof.

If

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\left|x+iy+1+\frac{x-iy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(x+1+\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(y-\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)^{2}

which is equivalent to (

), but this time we use the polar representations

z=r(\cos\theta+i\,\sin\theta),\qquad z^{-1}=r^{-1}(\cos\theta-i\,\sin\theta).

After some straightforward manipulations we see that (
7.8
) is equivalent
to

\left(r+\tfrac{1}{r}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+2\left(r+\tfrac{1}{r}\right)\cos\theta+\left(r-\tfrac{1}{r}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta=0,

which can be further transformed to

(8.5)

\left(r+\tfrac{1}{r}\right)^{2}+2\left(r+\tfrac{1}{r}\right)\cos\theta+4\cos^{2}\theta-4=0.

We solve (
8.5
) for
r+r^{-1}
, obtaining

(8.6)

r+\frac{1}{r}=-\cos\theta+\sqrt{4-3\cos^{2}\theta},\qquad\frac{\pi}{2}\leq|\theta|\leq\pi,

where the restriction on
\theta
comes from the fact that the curve in
question lies entirely in the left half-plane. Furthermore, we have just
“
+
” in front of the square root in (
8.6
) since

\sqrt{4-3\cos^{2}\theta}\geq 1
for all
\theta
, while the left-hand side of
(
8.6
) is
\geq 2
.

Differentiating both sides of (
8.6
), we get after some simplification,

(8.7)

2r\left(1-r^{-2}\right)\frac{dr}{d\theta}=\left(1+\frac{3\cos\theta}{\sqrt{4-3\cos^{2}\theta}}\right)\sin\theta.

The right-hand side of (
8.7
) vanishes when
\sin\theta=0
or the
expression in large parentheses is zero. In the first case we get
\theta=\pi
,
which means
r=1
and thus the left-hand side of (
8.7
) also vanishes.
But this has already been dealt with following Proposition
8.1
.

In the second case we have

(8.8)

-3\cos\theta=\sqrt{4-3\cos^{2}\theta},

and upon squaring and simplifying we get
\cos^{2}\theta=1/3
. But then, by
(
8.8
), only the solution
\cos\theta=-1/\sqrt{3}
is possible.
Substituting this into (
8.6
), we get

r+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{3},

which has the two solutions
r=\sqrt{3}
and
r=1/\sqrt{3}
. Using (
8.7
),
for instance, we can see that these two values are a maximum and a minimum,
respectively.

The final statement comes from the fact that
\cos\theta=-1/\sqrt{3}
implies

\sin\theta=\pm\sqrt{2/3}
, and thus
(x,y)=(-r/\sqrt{3},\pm r\sqrt{2/3})
.
∎

In concluding this section, we note that the curve we investigated here has
several properties in common with the
Besace curve
given by the equation

\left(x^{2}-by\right)^{2}=a^{2}\left(x^{2}-y^{2}\right),

with positive parameters
a
and
b
; see, e.g.,
[
5
]
. These similarities
include the facts that both are quartics, have genus 0, and are similar in
shape.

9.1.
Basic properties

In analogy to the beginning of Section

to

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}(Z)q^{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(1+xq^{b^{j}}\right)\left(1+y_{1}q^{b^{j}}+y_{2}q^{2\cdot b^{j}}+\cdots+y_{b}q^{b\cdot b^{j}}\right),

(9.3)

(9.4)

\displaystyle p_{bn}(Z)

\displaystyle=p_{n}(Z)+(y_{b}+xy_{b-1})\cdot p_{n-1}(Z),

(9.5)

\displaystyle p_{bn+1}(Z)

\displaystyle=(x+y_{1})\cdot p_{n}(Z)+xy_{b}\cdot p_{n-1}(Z),

(9.6)

\displaystyle p_{bn+j}(Z)

\displaystyle=(xy_{j-1}+y_{1})\cdot p_{n}(Z),\quad(2\leq j\leq b-1).

Since for

we have

)

) become (
2.3
) and (

), respectively, while
(

Next we state the base-
b
analogue of Proposition
2.1
. If we write
the polynomials

in the form

(9.7)

p_{n}(Z)=\sum_{i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{b}\geq 0}c_{n}(i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{b})\cdot x^{i}y_{1}^{j_{1}}\ldots y_{b}^{j_{b}},\quad n\geq 0,

then the generating function (
9.2
) gives
the following combinatorial interpretation.

Proposition 9.1
.

For any non-negative integers
n,i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{b}
, the coefficient

c_{n}(i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{b})
in (

) counts the number of

-restricted

-ary overpartitions of

different and single overlined parts,

j_{1}
different and single non-overlined parts, and

different k-tuples of non-overlined parts,

9.2.
Connections with Chebyshev polynomials

We now extend the results in Section
3
to arbitrary bases
b\geq 2
.
We consider two subsequences with subscripts

(9.8)

which we use to define

(9.9)

Q_{n}^{b}(Z):=p_{q(n)}(Z),\qquad R_{n}^{b}(Z):=p_{r(n)}(Z).

For

9.8
), (
9.9
) and

Q_{n}^{2}(Z)=Q_{n}(x,y,z),\qquad R_{n}^{2}(Z)=R_{n}(x,y,z).

The following is analogous to Proposition
3.1
. To simplify notation,
we set for
b\geq 2
and
x,y_{1},y_{b-1},y_{b}
as in (
9.1
),

(9.10)

W_{1}^{b}(Z):=xy_{b-1}+x+y_{1}+y_{b},\qquad W_{2}^{b}(Z):=x^{2}y_{b-1}+xy_{1}y_{b-1}+y_{1}y_{b}.

Proposition 9.2
.

We have
Q_{0}^{b}(Z)=1
,
Q_{1}^{b}(Z)=W_{1}^{b}(Z)
,
R_{0}^{b}(Z)=1
,

,
and for

(9.11)

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}^{b}(Z)

\displaystyle=W_{1}^{b}(Z)\cdot Q_{n}^{b}(Z)-W_{2}^{b}(Z)\cdot Q_{n-1}^{b}(Z),

(9.12)

\displaystyle R_{n+1}^{b}(Z)

\displaystyle=W_{1}^{b}(Z)\cdot R_{n}^{b}(Z)-W_{2}^{b}(Z)\cdot R_{n-1}^{b}(Z).

Further in analogy to Section
3
, the recurrence relations
(

), (

) lead to the following.

Proposition 9.3
.

The polynomials
Q_{n}^{b}
and

(9.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{n}^{b}(Z)q^{n}=\frac{1}{1-W_{1}^{b}(Z)q+W_{2}^{b}(Z)q^{2}},

(9.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}R_{n}^{b}(Z)q^{n}=\frac{1-(xy_{b-1}+y_{b})q}{1-W_{1}^{b}(Z)q+W_{2}^{b}(Z)q^{2}}.

At this point it will not be surprising that the polynomials

and

Proposition 9.4
.

For all

(9.15)

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{b}(Z)

\displaystyle=\left(W_{2}^{b}(Z)\right)^{n/2}U_{n}\left(\frac{W_{1}^{b}(Z)}{2\sqrt{W_{2}^{b}(Z)}}\right),

(9.16)

\displaystyle R_{n}^{b}(Z)

\displaystyle=\left(W_{2}^{b}(Z)\right)^{n/2}T_{n}\left(\frac{W_{1}^{b}(Z)}{2\sqrt{W_{2}^{b}(Z)}}\right)+\widetilde{U}_{n-1}^{b}(Z),

where

Likewise, the identity (
