Understanding resistance oscillation in CsV 3 Sb 5 superconductor
Jung Hoon Han, Patrick A. Lee

Abstract
A recent demonstration of the periodic oscillation of resistance in the thin film of CsV 3 Sb 5 superconductor with a hole in the film suggests that charge- 4 ​ e 4e and charge- 6 ​ e 6e Cooper pairs may have condensed in this compound. While exciting, such interpretation calls for a precise determination of the effective area for the passage of Cooper pairs from one end of the lead to the other. Unlike the traditional Little-Parks effect where the rim around the hole is thin, the effective hole area is not obviously defined for the “thick-rim geometry” adopted in Ref. Ge et al. 2022 . Here, we note that the experiment was conducted in a regime where the superconctivity is strongly fluctuating, which motivates an analysis based on the spacetime formulation of the time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau theory. We argue that under appropriate conditions, the optimal semi-classical path is not the geometrically shortest one, but the one that moves along the edge of the hole and takes advantage of the reduced fluctuations at the boundary of the hole. The condition for the cross-over from the geometrically shortest path to the path that sticks to the wall is clarified. In such a scenario, the geometric area of the hole indeed emerges as the effective area for the flux, providing a theoretical justification to the interpretation given in Ref. Ge et al. 2022 . The conclusion of our analysis may have implication for similar experiments in other superconductors where the geometry of the device is not obviously that of the Little-Parks experiment employing the thin wall, but of the thick-rim type such as used in Ref. Ge et al. 2022 .

Formulation

The linearized time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau (TDGL) equation near

is

\displaystyle\left[\overline{\alpha}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}({\bm{\nabla}}-ie^{*}{\bf A})^{2}\right]\Psi=-\overline{\alpha}\tau\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}.

(3)

The effective charge

8

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m\xi^{2}}

(4)

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{\hbar}{8k_{B}(T-T_{c})}.

(5)

In this paper we shall take

;

;

Ge et al. 2022

The TDGL equation of Eq. (

through the substitution

\displaystyle\Psi=e^{-t/\tau}\psi,

(6)

as the new

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi^{2}}{\tau}(-i{\bm{\nabla}}-e^{*}{\bf A})^{2}\psi

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar}{2m^{*}}(-i{\bm{\nabla}}-e^{*}{\bf A})^{2}\psi.

(7)

This resembles the Schrödinger equation in imaginary time, and the effective mass

\displaystyle m^{*}\equiv\frac{\hbar\tau}{2\xi^{2}},

(8)

differing from the mass of the charge-
6e

Equation (

,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\frac{\hbar}{2m^{*}}{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\psi\equiv D{\bm{\nabla}}^{2}\psi

\displaystyle\psi({\bf r}t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi Dt}\exp\left(-\frac{{\bf r}^{2}}{4Dt}\right),

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y^{2}\rangle=2Dt=\frac{\hbar t}{m^{*}}.

(10)

For

7

\displaystyle\psi({\bf r}t)=\int U({\bf r}t;{\bf r}_{0}t_{0})\psi({\bf r}_{0}t_{0})d{\bf r}_{0}

(11)

\displaystyle U({\bf r}t;{\bf r}_{0}t_{0})

\displaystyle=\int[{\cal D}{\bf r}]\exp\left[i\frac{2\pi}{\Phi^{*}_{0}}\int_{{\bf r}_{0}}^{\bf r}{\bf A}\cdot d{\bf r}-\frac{m^{*}}{2\hbar}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\dot{\bf r}^{2}dt\right],

(12)

where

The product of two wave functions at unequal times and positions is
\Psi^{*}({\bf r}_{1}t_{1})\Psi({\bf r}_{2}t_{2})

\displaystyle=e^{-(t_{2}\!-\!t_{0})/\tau}\int U({\bf r}_{2}t_{2};{\bf r}_{0}t_{0})\psi({\bf r}_{0}t_{0})d{\bf r}_{0}

\displaystyle~~~~~\int U({\bf r}_{2}t_{2};{\bf r}_{0}t_{1})\psi({\bf r}_{0}t_{1})\psi^{*}({\bf r}_{1}t_{1})d{\bf r}_{0}.

The non-local conductivity

\displaystyle\approx\frac{m^{*}ab}{t}-\left(\alpha ab+(\alpha-\beta)\frac{b^{3}}{a+b}\right)t

Now we give physical justification to the
\propto t
term in the effective action, estimate the coefficients
\alpha,\beta
, and carve out the conditions under which the crossover of paths from short to long can take place. As schematically shown in Fig.
3
, we consider fluctuations away from the classical paths and their effects on the action at the Gaussian level. A well-known correction to the classical action
S\sim(m^{*}/2)(l^{2}/t)
is the prefactor
t^{-d/2}
where
d
is the spatial dimension. Such correction, though, will modify the analysis (which concerns quantities in the exponent) only by logarithmic amount and not play a vital role. A more pertinent correction comes from changes in the AB phase
\oint{\bf A}\cdot d{\bf r}
. As shown in Fig.
3
, fluctuation of the classical path implies the change in the area, giving rise to the change in the phase factor

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{2\pi iB}{\Phi^{*}_{0}}\Delta A\right)

(30)

where
\Delta A
is the area enclosed between the classical and the fluctuating (non-classical) path - red and blue region in Fig.
3
. For the ‘free’ region the area
\Delta A
takes both positive and negative values. This is not the case, however, if the classical path is against the wall, i.e. it travels along the edges of the hole. In this case the area fluctuation is always of one sign:
\Delta A>0
. For the two paths shown in Fig.
2
(c), the difference arises in going from
{\bf r}_{2}
to
{\bf r}_{3\pm}
and from
{\bf r}_{4\pm}
to
{\bf r}_{2}
. In both cases, one only needs to compare the effective actions for the hypotenuse and the base+perpendicular paths of Fig.
2
(c).

If the time
t
is not too long, the typical fluctuating path will be ‘smooth’ and unlikely to cross the classical path. In that case the area
\Delta A
can be estimated reasonably well by the length
l
of the classical path multiplied by the width in the transverse direction. Given that the governing equation (
9
) is a diffusion equation, the condition for this is
t<t_{D}
where the diffusion time is given by

\displaystyle t_{D}\sim l^{2}/D.

(31)

The size of the transverse fluctuation can be estimated by using the one-dimensional diffusion equation (as we are interested in the fluctuation in the transverse direction to the classical path),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\frac{\hbar}{2m^{*}}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial y_{\perp}^{2}}\equiv D\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial y_{\perp}^{2}},

(32)

for the transverse coordinate
y_{\perp}
. Using its well-known solution,
\psi(y_{\perp},t)=(4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}\exp(-y_{\perp}^{2}/4Dt)
,
\langle y_{\perp}^{2}\rangle=2Dt
, we estimate

\displaystyle\Delta y_{\perp}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{2Dt}=\sqrt{\hbar t/m^{*}}=\xi\sqrt{2t/\tau},

\displaystyle\Delta A

\displaystyle\sim l\xi\sqrt{2t/\tau}

(33)

where
l
is the length of a particular classical trajectory and
t
is the time taken to traverse it.

Evaluation of the two-particle correlator, Eq. (
23
), ultimately depends on summing over various paths, and this implies summation over various areas
\Delta A
. We therefore introduce the Gaussian
distribution function for the area

\displaystyle P(\Delta A)=\frac{e^{-(\Delta A)^{2}/2\sigma_{A}^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{A}^{2}}},\left(\sigma_{A}=l\xi\sqrt{\frac{2t}{\tau}}\right),

(34)

and sum over
\Delta A
with this probability. For the area fluctuation against the edge where
\Delta A>0
, the normalization factor is only half as large:
\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{A}^{2}}\rightarrow\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{A}^{2}}/2
. The following
exact
integral identities are useful in estimating the effect of area fluctuation both without and with the wall:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(2\pi i\frac{B\Delta A}{\Phi^{*}_{0}}\right)P(\Delta A)d(\Delta A)

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha l^{2}t}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(2\pi i\frac{B\Delta A}{\Phi^{*}_{0}}\right)P(\Delta A)d(\Delta A)

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha l^{2}t}\left[1+i{\rm erfi}(\sqrt{\alpha t}l)\right].

(35)

In the case of the first line, one can think of the
\alpha l^{2}t
in the exponent as an addition to the classical action. Comparing this to the effective action in Eq. (
25
), we realize that this
\alpha
is precisely the phenomenological
\alpha
there, but now it is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\alpha=(2\pi B\xi/\Phi^{*}_{0})^{2}/\tau.

(36)

The imaginary error function erfi(
x
) in the second line of the identity has the property

\displaystyle{\rm erfi}(x)\equiv\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{y^{2}}dy=e^{x^{2}}D(x),

(37)

and the
Dawson function

D(x)
has the asymptotic properties

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle\sim x~~~(x\ll 1)

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle\sim 1/2x~~~(x\gg 1),

(38)

with the maximum value less than
1
at
x\approx O(1)
. Using the Dawson function one can re-write the half-infinite integral in the second line of Eq. (
35
) as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(2\pi i\frac{B\Delta A}{\Phi^{*}_{0}}\right)P(\Delta A)d(\Delta A)

\displaystyle~~=e^{-\alpha l^{2}t}+iD(\sqrt{\alpha t}l).

(39)

If we could ignore the Dawson function and keep the first term only, it means the parameter
\beta
equals
\alpha
. If instead the Dawson function dominates at long times, the decay with time is only algebraic, and
\beta
is effectively zero. The crossover from
\beta\approx\alpha
to
\beta\approx 0
takes place around the time
t_{\beta}
which is estimated by
\alpha l^{2}t_{\beta}\approx 1
, or in physical terms,

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\beta}}{\tau}=\left(\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{2\pi B\xi l}\right)^{2}.

(40)

We return to Eq. (
28
) for
S_{a}+S_{b}-S_{c}
and insert
\alpha
from Eq. (
36
). We consider time
t>t_{\beta}
(to be justified later) so that we can set
\beta=0
, to find

\displaystyle S_{a}+S_{b}-S_{c}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{m^{*}ab}{t}\Bigl(1-\left[1+\frac{b^{2}}{a(a+b)}\right]\left(\frac{t}{t_{c}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~~-\frac{a^{4}}{(a+b)^{4}}\left(\frac{t}{t_{c}}\right)^{4}\Bigr).

(41)

Accordingly, the crossover from the
c
-path to the
ab
-path takes place when

\displaystyle\frac{t}{t_{c}}\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{a^{2}+ab}{a^{2}+ab+b^{2}}}.

(42)

The factor on the r.h.s. is of order one for typical aspect ratios
a:b
, and we obtain that for
t\gtrsim t_{c}
the path crossover indeed takes place. We also need the estimate of the crossover time
t_{c}
in terms of the relaxation time
\tau
and other physical parameters

\displaystyle\frac{t_{c}}{\tau}=\frac{\sqrt{m^{*}/\alpha}}{\tau}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{B\xi^{2}}.

(43)

Comparing with Eq. (
40
), we find the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{t_{c}}{t_{\beta}}=\sqrt{2}\pi\frac{Bl^{2}}{\Phi^{*}_{0}}.

(44)

Since
l
is roughly half the linear dimension of the hole, we get
Bl^{2}\approx BA/4
,
A
being the area of the hole. Thus for a field
B_{1}
defined by
B_{1}A=\Phi^{*}_{0}
which corresponds to the first
AB
oscillation,
t_{c}
is already comparable or larger than
t_{\beta}
and this ratio increases for larger
B
. This justifies our use of
\beta=0
.

The analysis so far only concerned a part of the overall trajectory going from
{\bf r}_{1}
to
{\bf r}_{2}
. For a complete analysis let us go back the full edge-AB trajectory
{\bf r}_{1}
to
{\bf r}_{2}
and estimate its effective action:

\displaystyle S_{13}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{*}}{2}\frac{(a+b)^{2}}{t_{3}\!-\!t_{1}}+\alpha\frac{a^{3}}{a+b}(t_{3}-t_{1}),

\displaystyle S_{34}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{*}}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{t_{4}\!-\!t_{3}},

\displaystyle S_{42}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{*}}{2}\frac{(a+b)^{2}}{t_{2}\!-\!t_{4}}+\alpha\frac{a^{3}}{a+b}(t_{2}-t_{4}).

(45)

The subscript in
S_{ij}
means the trajectory from
{\bf r}_{i}
to
{\bf r}_{j}
. Note that the
ab
-path is already assumed in writing down
S_{13}
and
S_{42}
above. The linear-
t
term is absent in
S_{34}
since the fluctuation there is against the wall and we already showed
\beta=0
in that case. An estimate of
t_{3},t_{4}
minimizing the total action
S=S_{13}+S_{34}+S_{42}
is given by

\displaystyle t_{3}-t_{1}

\displaystyle=t_{2}-t_{4}=\frac{a+b}{l_{\rm eff}}(t_{2}-t_{1}),

\displaystyle t_{4}-t_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{l_{\rm eff}}(t_{2}-t_{1}),

(46)

where
l_{\rm eff}=2(a+b)+d
. The total action is

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=S_{13}+S_{34}+S_{42}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{*}}{2}\frac{l_{\rm eff}^{2}}{t_{2}\!-\!t_{1}}+\left(\alpha\frac{2a^{3}}{l_{\rm eff}}+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)(t_{2}-t_{1}),

(47)

where we now include the exponential time factor
e^{-(t_{2}-t_{1})/\tau}
as part of the effective action.

The conductivity
\sigma({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2})
follows from taking the Fourier transform of the current-current correlation
\langle{\bf J}({\bf r}_{1}t_{1})\cdot{\bf J}({\bf r}_{2}t_{2})\rangle
with respect to
e^{-i\omega(t_{2}-t_{1})}
and taking
\omega\rightarrow 0
limit. In turn,
\langle{\bf J}({\bf r}_{1}t_{1})\cdot{\bf J}({\bf r}_{2}t_{2})\rangle
is dominated by the path that maximizes the propagator, Eq. (
23
), or minimizes the total action
S
in Eq. (
47
). The minimization of
S
with respect to
t_{2}-t_{1}
gives

\displaystyle t_{2}-t_{1}=t^{*}=\frac{t_{c}}{\sqrt{2\left[2a^{3}/l_{\rm eff}^{3}+1/\alpha\tau l_{\rm eff}^{2}\right]}}

(48)

where
t_{c}
is the crossover time in Eq. (
43
). Among the two terms in the denominator,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\tau l_{\rm eff}^{2}}=\left(\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{2\pi B\xi l_{\rm eff}}\right)^{2}\approx\left(\frac{B_{1}}{B}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{A}{2\pi\xi l_{\rm eff}}\right)^{2}.

(49)

We estimate
\xi=2\times 10^{-8}
m and
l_{\rm eff}=5\times 10^{-7}
m. By way of comparison, the area of the hole
A
in the experiment is estimated to be
2.76\times 10^{-14}
m
2
, which corresponds to a linear dimension of about
1.7\times 10^{-7}
m. Thus
l_{\rm eff}
is about 3 times the dimension of the hole. With the
2\pi
factor in our favor, we find that this expression is comparable or less than one already at
B=B_{1}
, the field of the first
AB
oscillation and will decrease rapidly at larger magnetic field. On the other hand, the first term in the denominator of Eq. (
48
) is independent of
B
. For this reason we keep only the first term in the denominator and write

\displaystyle t^{*}\approx t_{c}\sqrt{\frac{l_{\rm eff}^{3}}{4a^{3}}}.

(50)

The value of
t^{*}
is then used to estimate the time to go from
{\bf r}_{1}
to
{\bf r}_{3\pm}
as

\displaystyle t_{3}-t_{1}=\frac{a+b}{l_{\rm eff}}t^{*}=t_{c}\sqrt{\frac{(2a+2b+d)(a+b)^{2}}{4a^{3}}}.

(51)

Recall, as we argued earlier, that the time
t_{3}-t_{1}
to complete the
ab
-path needs to be larger than
t_{c}
in order to justify the crossover from
c
-path to the
ab
-path. Now let’s estimate
t_{3}-t_{1}
explicitly. For
a:b:d=1:1:1
, for instance, we get
t_{3}-t_{1}=\sqrt{5}t_{c}>t_{c}
. For
a:b:d=1:1:2
we get an even larger
t_{3}-t_{1}=\sqrt{6}t_{c}
. The current device setup
Ge et al. 2022
is therefore consistent with the statement that
the time to travel between
{\bf r}_{1}
and
{\bf r}_{3\pm}
is sufficiently long to justify the choice of
ab
-path over the
c
-path
. Note further than this condition is geometric, i.e. does not depend on the strength of the magnetic field
B
.

For the
b
- and
d
-trajectories, we already concluded that there are no corrections to the classical action because
\beta
is effective zero. However, the area covered by a given trajectory always satisfies
\Delta A>0
and this might give rise to the increase in the effective area from the geometric area of the hole,
2b\times d
. Contrary to the naive expectation, this is not the case. This can be seen easily from the expression in Eq. (
39
), which contains no oscillatory terms. To be more explicit, for the segments of the path hugging the edge, we may use the half-integral identities in Eqs. (
39
) and (
38
) to obtain the compound action

\displaystyle\left(\frac{i}{2\sqrt{\alpha(t_{3}\!-\!t_{1})}b}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{i}{2\sqrt{\alpha(t_{4}\!-\!t_{3})}d}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{i}{2\sqrt{\alpha(t_{2}\!-\!t_{4})}b}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{1}{(t_{2}-t_{1})^{3}}.

(52)

This factor, being purely real, will have no contribution to the AB phase
\exp[(2\pi i/\Phi^{*}_{0})\oint{\bf A}\cdot d{\bf r}]
. Thus, contrary to naive expectation, the effective area stays the same as the geometric area of the inner square:
A_{\rm eff}=2b\times d
. The situation is analogous to what happens in three dimensional quantum oscillations, where the oscillation period is given by the extremal area, despite a broad distributions in the cross-section area that deviates from the extremal area with the same sign. Mathematically this is because the integral in the second line of Eq. (
35
has a sharp cutoff and the effective area is determined by that cut-off.

V
Discussion

Several time scales were introduced and discussed throughout the paper. Among them, the diffusion time
t_{D}
was crucial in allowing us to use the Gaussian probability function
P(\Delta A)
for the area fluctuation that increases diffusively with time
\Delta A\propto\sqrt{t}
, Eq. (
33
). Since
D=\hbar/2m^{*}
and
m^{*}=\hbar\tau/2\xi^{2}
,

\displaystyle t_{D}

\displaystyle=l^{2}/D=(l/\xi)^{2}\tau

(53)

The other two time scales,
t_{\beta}
and
t_{c}
, were defined in Eq. (
40
) and (
43
), respectively. Putting all three time scales together gives

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\beta}}{\tau}:\frac{t_{c}}{\tau}:\frac{t_{D}}{\tau}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{2\pi B\xi l}\right)^{2}:\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{2\sqrt{2}\pi B\xi^{2}}:\left(\frac{l}{\xi}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{2\pi Bl^{2}}\right)^{2}:\frac{\Phi^{*}_{0}}{2\sqrt{2}\pi Bl^{2}}:1.

(54)

For instance, the condition
t_{D}\gtrsim t_{c}
amounts to having
1\gtrsim\Phi^{*}_{0}/(2\sqrt{2}\pi Bl^{2})
, which is nicely fulfilled if we take
Bl^{2}
to be the AB flux through the hole, so that
B\gtrsim B_{1}
, i.e. at least one
AB
oscillations have been seen. The inequality
t_{c}\gtrsim t_{\beta}
holds under the similar condition.

As mentioned in the introduction, this paper does not address the microscopic justification of the charge-
6e
formation in the CVS superconductor. We take it for granted and write down the Ginzburg-Landau theory accordingly. The starting point of our investigation is the observation that the experimental setup of Ref.
Ge et al. 2022
calls for a theoretical justification of the effective area one should use in estimating the flux quantum. The effective area used by the authors of the experiment suggests that the area of hole (the inner square) is playing a dominant role, but on the basis of some earlier literature,
Kogan et al. 2001
;
Kogan et al. 2004
it is not clear whether this is justified. Our investigation shows that the use of the extremal area - the area of the inner square - does get justified under the experimentally viable circumstances
\Phi/\Phi^{*}_{0}\gtrsim 1
(
\Phi
=flux through the hole) if the pairing order is fluctuating and has only short range order.

Heuristically, the preferred path that dominate the contribution to the correlation function is not always the geometrically preferrred path, but the one that likes to ‘stick to the edge’ because there the fluctuation (and hence the effective action) is less. This simple picture arising from our analysis is obviously applicable to the more mundane, charge-
2e
superconductors under the similar device setup, as long as we are in the fluctuation regime. We surmise that this result may be more general, as long as the system is described by a sum over diffusing Feynman paths. The basic principle rests on the analog of Eq. (
35
) which states that the average over a distribution of
AB
areas is dominated by the extremal area in the presence of a sharp cut-off, and not by the average area. Thus the result that the
AB
oscillation is given by the area of the hole may also apply to the charge-
e
transport in a normal metal in the thick-rim geometry.
