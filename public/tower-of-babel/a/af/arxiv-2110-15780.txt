On a Bernstein-Sato polynomial of a meromorphic function To the memory of Professor Hikosaburo Komatsu 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 14F10, 14F18, 32C38, 32S40, Keywords: Bernstein-Sato polynomials, meromorphic functions, monodromy, multiplier ideal sheaves
Kiyoshi TAKEUCHI

Abstract
We define Bernstein-Sato polynomials for meromorphic functions and study their basic properties. In particular, we prove a Kashiwara-Malgrange type theorem on their geometric monodromies, which would be useful also in relation with the monodromy conjecture. A new feature in the meromorphic setting is that we have several b-functions whose roots yield the same set of the eigenvalues of the Milnor monodromies. We introduce also multiplier ideal sheaves for meromorphic functions and show that their jumping numbers are related to our b-functions.



1
Introduction

The theory of b-functions initiated by
Bernstein and Sato independently is
certainly on a crossroad of various branches
of mathematics, such as generalized functions,
singularity theory, prehomogenous vector
spaces, D-modules, number theory,
algebraic geometry, computer algebra and so on.
We often call them
Bernstein-Sato polynomials.
To see the breadth of their influence to mathematics,
we can now consult for example the excellent
survey articles by
[
2
]
and
[
6
]
.

Let us briefly recall the definitions of
classical Bernstein-Sato polynomials
and some related results. For this purpose,
let
X
be a complex manifold and

\mathcal{O}_{X}
the sheaf of holomorphic functions
on it. Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{X}
the sheaf of
differential operators with holomorphic
coefficients on
X
. Let
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}
be a holomorphic
function defined on a neighborhood of
a point
x_{0}\in X
such that
f(x_{0})=0
. Then the (local)
Bernstein-Sato polynomial
b_{f}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

of
f
(at
x_{0}\in X
) is the non-zero
polynomial
b(s)\not=0
of the lowest
degree satisfying the equation

b(s)f^{s}=P(s)f^{s+1}

(1.1)

for some
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]
.
In the algebraic and analytic cases,
the existence of such
b(s)\not=0

was proved by Bernstein and Björk
respectively. Then
Kashiwara
[
18
]
proved that the roots of
the Bernstein-Sato polynomial

b_{f}(s)
are negative rational numbers.
One of the most strinking results on
b_{f}(x)

is the Kashiwara-Malgrange theorem
in
[
19
]
and
[
23
]
, which
asserts that the set of the eigenvalues
of the local (Milnor) monodromies of

f
at various points
x\in f^{-1}(0)

close to
x_{0}\in f^{-1}(0)
is equal to
the one
\{\exp(2\pi i\alpha)\ |\ \alpha\in(b_{f})^{-1}(0)\}
. Motivated by it,
Denef and Loeser fomulated their celebrated monodromy
conjecture in
[
11
]
.
Later in
[
33
]
and
[
34
]
, Sabbah
developed a theory of b-functions of
several variables. More precisely, he considered
several holomorphic functions

f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}

(
k\geq 1
) and proved the existence
of a non-zero polynomial

b(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]=\mathbb{C}[s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}]
of

k
variables
s=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k})

satisfying the equation

b(s)\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}^{s_{i}}\Bigr)=P(s)\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}^{s_{i}+1}\Bigr)

(1.2)

for some
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]=\mathcal{D}_{X}[s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}]
. See also
Gyoja
[
16
]
for a different proof
and some additional results.
The non-zero ideal
I\subset\mathbb{C}[s]
thus
obtained is now called the Bernstein-Sato
ideal of
f=(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k})
. The
geometric meaning of this
I
was clarified
only recently in
Budur-van der Veer-Wu-Zhou
[
10
]
.
Moreover by Budur-Mustata-Saito
[
8
]
,
the theory of b-functios has been also generalized
to higher-codimensional subvarieties, i.e to
arbitrary ideals
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{O}_{X}
of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
.
Their b-functions are related to the monodromies of
the Verdier specializations along
\mathcal{J}
.
See
[
6
]
and
[
8
]
for the details.

The aim of this short note is to define Bernstein-Sato
polynomials for meromorphic functions
and study their basic properties.
For two holomorphic functions

F,G\in\mathcal{O}_{X}
such that
F\not=0
,

G\not=0
defined on a neighborhood of
a point
x_{0}\in X
and coprime to each other
such that
F(x_{0})=0
,
let us consider the meromorphic function

f(x)=\frac{F(x)}{G(x)}

(1.3)

associated to them. Let

D=F^{-1}(0)\cup G^{-1}(0)\subset X

be the divisor defined by
F\cdot G\in\mathcal{O}_{X}
and

\mathcal{O}_{X}\Bigl[\frac{1}{FG}\Bigr]=\Bigl\{\frac{h}{(FG)^{l}}\ |\ h\in\mathcal{O}_{X},l\geq 0\Bigr\}

(1.4)

the localization of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
along
D\subset X
.
Recall that this sheaf is endowed with the
structure of a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module. Then the
polynomial ring
(\mathcal{O}_{X}[\frac{1}{FG}])[s]

over it is naturally a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]
-module.
As in the classical case where
G=1
and
f
is
holomorphic, on the rank-one free module

\mathcal{L}:=\Bigl(\mathcal{O}_{X}\Bigl[\frac{1}{FG}\Bigr]\Bigr)[s]f^{s}\simeq\Bigl(\mathcal{O}_{X}\Bigl[\frac{1}{FG}\Bigr]\Bigr)[s]

(1.5)

over it, we define naturally a structure of
a left
\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]
-module and can consider
its
\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]
-submodule
\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]f^{s}\subset\mathcal{L}
generated by
f^{s}\in\mathcal{L}
.
However, in order to prove a Kashiwara-Malgrange type
theorem (see Theorem
1.4
below)
for b-functions on the geometric
monodromies of
f
in our meromorphic setting,
we have to consider also other types of

\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]
-submodules of
\mathcal{L}
. Considering

\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k}=\frac{f^{k}}{G^{m}}\cdot f^{s}\in\mathcal{L}

(1.6)

for various integers
m\geq 0
and
k\geq 0
,
we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m\geq 0
be a non-negative integer. Then
there exists a non-zero polynomial
b(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

such that

b(s)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)\in\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k}\Bigr)

(1.7)

i.e. there exist

P_{1}(s),P_{2}(s),\ldots,P_{N}(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]

for which we have

b(s)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(s)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k}\Bigr).

(1.8)

Although the proof of Theorem
1.1

relies on the classical theory of Kashiwara
and Malgrange, we need some new ideas to formulate
and prove it. See Section
2

for the details. This could be the reason
why Bernstein-Sato polynomials for meromorphic functions
were not defined nor studied before.

Definition 1.2
.

For
m\geq 0
we denote by
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
the minimal polynomial (i.e. the
non-zero polynomial of the lowest degree)
satisfying the equation in Theorem
1.1

and call it the Bernstein-Sato polynomial
or the b-function of
f
of order
m
.

By a theorem of Sabbah
[
33
]
there
exists a non-zero polynomial
b(s_{1},s_{2})\not=0
of
two variables
s_{1},s_{2}
such that

b(s_{1},s_{2})F^{s_{1}}G^{s_{2}}=P(s_{1},s_{2})F^{s_{1}+1}G^{s_{2}+1}

(1.9)

for some
P(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s_{1},s_{2}]
.
Then by setting
s_{1}=s
and
s_{2}=-s-m-2
we obtain
the desired condition

b(s,-s-m-2)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)=G^{2}P(s,-s-m-2)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+1}\Bigr).

(1.10)

This important remark is due to Oaku.
However, for the given
F(x),G(x)\in\mathcal{O}_{X}

it would not be so easy to verify that
the polynomial
b(s,-s-m-2)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

of
s
thus obtained is non-zero. Recall
that by Bahloul
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
, Bahloul-Oaku

[
5
]
, Oaku-Takayama
[
31
]
and
Ucha-Castro
[
37
]

we have algorithms to compute
the Bernstein-Sato ideal
I\subset\mathbb{C}[s_{1},s_{2}]

at least when
F
and
G
are polynomials. Motivated
by this observation, instead of
the equation (
1.7
)
one may also consider the simpler one

b(s)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+1}\Bigr).

(1.11)

Then of course, the minimal polynomial

b(s)\not=0
satisfying it is divided by
our b-function
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
,
but from the proof of Theorem
1.4
below
it looks that we do not have a
Kashiwara-Malgrange type result as in it
by this simpler
definition of b-functions. This explains
the reason why the right hand side of
the equation (
1.7
) is not so simple.
Note also that if
G=1
and
f=\frac{F}{G}=F
is holomorphic
we have
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}\subset\mathcal{D}_{X}
and for any
m\geq 0

our b-function
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
coincides
with the classical one
b_{f}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

introduced by Bernstein and Sato.
But in the meromorphic case, the relation among

b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
for various

m\geq 0
is not very clear so far.
See Lemma
3.3
for a weak relation
among their roots. Nevertheless, we can prove a
Kashiwara-Malgrange type result as follows.
First, recall the following theorem due to
[
15
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

(Gusein-Zade, Luengo and Melle-Hernández

[
15
]
)
For any point
x\in F^{-1}(0)

close to the point
x_{0}
there exists

\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}

and the open ball
B(x;\varepsilon)\subset X
of
radius
\varepsilon>0
with center at
x

(in a local chart of
X
) the restriction

B(x;\varepsilon)\setminus G^{-1}(0)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

(1.12)

of
f:X\setminus G^{-1}(0)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a locally trivial
fibration over a sufficiently small
punctured disk in
\mathbb{C}
with center at
the origin
0\in\mathbb{C}

We call the fiber in this theorem the Milnor fiber of
the meromorphic function
f(x)=\frac{F(x)}{G(x)}

at
x\in F^{-1}(0)
and denote it by
M_{x}
.
As in the holomorphic case (see Milnor
[
26
]
),
we obtain also its Milnor monodromy operators

\Phi_{j,x}:H^{j}(M_{x};\mathbb{C})\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}H^{j}(M_{x};\mathbb{C})\qquad(j\geq 0).

(1.13)

Then we have the following result.
Let
{\rm E}_{f,x_{0}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
be the set of
the eigenvalues
of the monodromies
\Phi_{j,x}
of
f
at the points

x\in F^{-1}(0)
close to
x_{0}
and
j\geq 0
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
m\geq 0
be a non-negative integer. Then
we have

\{\exp(2\pi i\alpha)\ |\ \alpha\in(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\}\subset{\rm E}_{f,x_{0}}.

(1.14)

If we assume moreover that
m\geq 2{\rm dim}X
,
then we have an equality

\{\exp(2\pi i\alpha)\ |\ \alpha\in(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\}={\rm E}_{f,x_{0}}.

(1.15)

Combining Theorem
1.4
with the results in

[
29
]
and
[
32
]
, one may
formulate a monodromy conjecture for
rational functions, like the original
one in
[
11
]
. For previous works in
this direction, see for example
[
14
]
and
[
38
]
.
Note that if in a coordinate system

F
and
G
depend on
separated variables we can easily see that
our
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
coincides with
the b-function
b_{F}(s)
of the holomorphic
function
F
. At the moment, except for such trivial
cases, we can not
calculate
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
explicity.
Instead, by
[
29
, Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.4]

for many
f=\frac{F}{G}
we can calculate

{\rm E}_{f,x_{0}}
completely. Namely, for

m\geq 2{\rm dim}X
the roots of

b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
of such
f

can be determined up to some shifts of
integers and multiplicities.
Moreover in Section
4
,
we also give an upper bound

(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset B_{f,m}^{\pi}\subset\mathbb{Q}\qquad(m\geq 0)

(1.16)

for the roots of
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

described in terms of
resolutions of singularities

\pi:Y\longrightarrow X

of the divisor
D\subset X
such that

\pi^{-1}(D)\subset Y
is normal crossing.
If
G=1
and
f
is holomorphic,
this corresponds to the negativity
of the roots of b-functions
proved by Kashiwara
[
18
]
.
Indeed, in particular for
m=0
our upper bound
means that the roots of
b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

are negative rational numbers.
Moreover by defining a reduced b-function

\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
of
f

we obtain also a lower bound

(\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset\mathbb{Q}\qquad(m\geq 0).

(1.17)

This
\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

could be a candidate for the b-function
of the meromorphic function
f
.
However to our regret, as we shall see in Proposition

4.5
, it has much less information
on the singularities
of
f
than
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
.
See Section
4
for the details.
Finally in Section
5
, we introduce
multiplier ideal sheaves for the
meromorphic function
f=\frac{F}{G}

and show that their jumping numbers are
contained in the set

\bigcup_{i=0,1,2,\ldots}\Bigl\{-(b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)+i\Bigr\}\subset\mathbb{Q}_{>0}.

(1.18)

This is an analogue for meromorphic functions
of the main theorem of Ein-Lazarsfeld-Smith-Varolin

[
13
]
. See Corollary
5.4

for the details.

After we posted this paper to the arXiv, we were
informed from the authors Àlvarez Montaner,
González Villa, León-Cardenal and Núñez-Betancourt
of
[
1
]
that they were also developing a
theory of b-functions for meromorphic functions
similar to but different from ours.
Among other things, for the meromorphic function

f=\frac{F}{G}
they define their b-function

b_{F/G}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
to be the minimal
polynomial
b(s)\not=0
satisfying the equation

b(s)f^{s}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]f^{s+1}

(1.19)

and apply it to the studies of the analytic
continuations of Archimedian local zeta functions
and multiplier ideals associated to
f=\frac{F}{G}
.
Moreover, in
[
1
, Theorem 6.7]

they obtain a result on the jumping numbers of
multiplier ideals similar to Corollary
5.4
.
Since our
b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
divides
their
b_{F/G}(s)
, it is not clear if
Corollary
5.4
follows
from
[
1
, Theorem 6.7]
.
In addition, our b-function
b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

satisfies a nice relationship with the V-filtration of
a holonomic D-module (see Theorem

5.3
). From this, we see also that
the minimal jumping number
\alpha>0
is
equal to the negative of the largest root
of
b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

(see Corollary
5.4
). Altogether,
the results in
[
1
]
look very useful
and complementary to ours.
Especially for some basic properties of
the multiplier ideals, we refer to

[
1
, Sections 6 and 7]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

We follow the classical arguments of
Kashiwara
[
18
]
, Malgrange
[
23
]
,
Gyoja
[
16
]
and Sabbah
[
25
]
.
For the theory of D-modules, we refer to

[
12
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
20
]
,

[
21
]
,
[
25
]
and use freely
the notions and the terminologies in them.
Let
\mathbb{C}[s,t]
be the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by
the two elements
s,t
satisfying the relation

ts=(s+1)t
i.e.
[t,s]=t
. Similarly,
we define
\mathbb{C}[s,t^{\pm}]
,

\mathcal{D}_{X}[s,t]
and
\mathcal{D}_{X}[s,t^{\pm}]
. Then there
exists a natural isomorphism

\mathbb{C}[s,t^{\pm}]\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{C}[t,\partial_{t}]\Bigl[\frac{1}{t}\Bigr]\qquad(s\longmapsto-\partial_{t}t)

(2.1)

of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras (see Gyoja
[
16
]
) and the one

\mathcal{D}_{X}[s,t^{\pm}]\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\Bigl[\frac{1}{t}\Bigr]

(2.2)

of
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-algebras associated to it. In the product
space
X\times\mathbb{C}
we define a hypersurface

Z\subset X\times\mathbb{C}
by

Z=\{(x,t)\in X\times\mathbb{C}\ |\ tG(x)-F(x)=0\}.

(2.3)

Note that
Z
is the closure of the graph of
the meromorphic function
f=\frac{F}{G}:X\setminus G^{-1}(0)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

in
X\times\mathbb{C}
. Let

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{[Z]}(\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}})\simeq\frac{\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}[\frac{1}{tG-F}]}{\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}}

(2.4)

be the first local cohomology sheaf of

\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}
along
Z\subset X\times\mathbb{C}

and define a regular holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}
-module

\mathcal{M}
by

\mathcal{M}:=\{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{[Z]}(\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}})\}\Bigl[\frac{1}{G}\Bigr]\simeq\frac{\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}[\frac{1}{(tG-F)G}]}{\mathcal{O}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}[\frac{1}{G}]}

(2.5)

which is endowed with the canonical section

\delta(t-f(x)):=\Bigl[\frac{1}{t-f(x)}\Bigr]=\Bigl[\frac{G(x)}{tG(x)-F(x)}\Bigr]\in\mathcal{M}.

(2.6)

Unlike the classical case where
f
is holomorphic,
this section does not necessarily generate
\mathcal{M}

over
\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}
(see Lemma
3.2
below).
Nevertheless,
as in Malgrange
[
23
]
and Gyoja
[
16
]

for any non-negative integer
m\geq 0

there exists an isomorphism

\mathcal{D}_{X}[s,t^{\pm}]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\Bigl[\frac{1}{t}\Bigr]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}\delta(t-f(x))\Bigr)

(2.7)

(
\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\longmapsto\frac{1}{G^{m}}\delta(t-f(x))
)
on a neighborhood of
F^{-1}(0)\subset X

which is linear over

\mathcal{D}_{X}[s,t^{\pm}]\simeq(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])[\frac{1}{t}]
.
Since there is no non-zero section of
\mathcal{M}

supported in
G^{-1}(0)\times\mathbb{C}\subset X\times\mathbb{C}
by Hilbert’s nullstellensatz,
to show (
2.7
) it suffices to
compare the annihilators of the
generators of its both sides on

X\setminus G^{-1}(0)
.
Here the right hand side of (
2.7
) is
understood to be a subsheaf of

(\mathcal{M}|_{\{t=0\}})[\frac{1}{t}]
and
the multiplication by
t
on it corresponds to
the action
s\longmapsto s+1
on the left hand
side (see e.g. Gyoja
[
16
]
for the details).
Restricting the isomorphism (
2.7
)
to a subsheaf, we obtain an isomophism

\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{D}_{X}[-\partial_{t}t]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}\delta(t-f(x))\Bigr).

(2.8)

Now let us consider the
V
-filtration

\{V_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}})\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

of
\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}
along the hypersurface

\{t=0\}=X\times\{0\}\subset X\times\mathbb{C}
. Similarly, we define
a filtration

\{V_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

of
\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}]\subset\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}|_{\{t=0\}}
.
Denote the section

\frac{1}{G^{m}}\delta(t-f(x))\in\mathcal{M}|_{\{t=0\}}

(2.9)

of
\mathcal{M}|_{\{t=0\}}
simply by
\sigma_{m}
.
Then by
t\cdot\delta(t-f)=f\cdot\delta(t-f)

we obtain isomorphisms

\displaystyle V_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\sigma_{m}

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k}\Bigr),

(2.10)

\displaystyle V_{-1}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\sigma_{m}

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k}\Bigr).

(2.11)

This implies that the
V_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])
-module

\mathcal{K}:=\frac{V_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\sigma_{m}}{V_{-1}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\sigma_{m}}

(2.12)

is isomorphic to

\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s](\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s](\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k})}.

(2.13)

Here we
used the identification

V_{0}(\mathcal{D}_{X}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}_{X}}\mathbb{C}_{X}[t,\partial_{t}])\simeq\mathcal{D}_{X}[s,t]

(2.14)

given by
-\partial_{t}t\longmapsto s
.
Moreover by Lemma
2.1
below there exists
also an isomorphism

4
Upper and lower bounds for the roots of
b-functions

Recall that in
[
18
]
Kashiwara proved that
if
f
is holomorphic the roots of
the Bernstein-Sato polynomial

b_{f}(s)
are negative rational numbers.
In this section, we prove an analogous result
for the meromorphic function
f=\frac{F}{G}
.
We can easily prove
that the roots of our b-function

b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
are rational numbers,
but their negativity does not follow from
our proof. For this reason, here we only give an upper bound

(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset B_{f,m}^{\pi}\subset\mathbb{Q}\qquad(m\geq 0)

(4.1)

for the set
(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)

in terms of resolutions of singularities
\pi

of
D\subset X
.
The precise statement is as follows.
Let
\pi:Y\longrightarrow X
be a
resolution of singularities
of the divisor
D=F^{-1}(0)\cup G^{-1}(0)\subset X
, which means that

\pi:Y\longrightarrow X
is a proper morphism
of
n
-dimensional
complex manifolds such that

\pi^{-1}(D)\subset Y
is normal crossing
and
\pi|_{Y\setminus\pi^{-1}(D)}:Y\setminus\pi^{-1}(D)\longrightarrow X\setminus D
is an isomorphism.
Then we define a meromorphic function
g
on
Y
by

g:=f\circ\pi=\frac{F\circ\pi}{G\circ\pi}.

(4.2)

From now on, we fix a non-negative integer
m\geq 0

and consider the (local) Bernstein-Sato polynomials
of
g
of order
m
. At each point
q\in\pi^{-1}(D)
of
the normal crossing divisor
\pi^{-1}(D)
,
there exists a local coordinate system

y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})
such that

q=(0,0,\ldots,0)
and

(F\circ\pi)(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{a_{i}}\quad(a_{i}\geq 0),\qquad(G\circ\pi)(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{b_{i}}\quad(b_{i}\geq 0).

(4.3)

Then we have

\bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{(a_{i}-b_{i})s-mb_{i}}.

(4.4)

It follows that the set
K_{q}\subset\mathbb{Q}

of the roots of the (local) Bernstein-Sato polynomial
of
g
at
q

K_{q}=\bigcup_{i:\ a_{i}>b_{i}}\Bigl\{\frac{mb_{i}}{a_{i}-b_{i}}-\frac{k}{a_{i}-b_{i}}\ |\ 1\leq k\leq a_{i}-b_{i}\Bigr\}\subset\mathbb{Q}

(4.5)

(see e.g.
[
20

It is clear this set
K_{q}
does not depend
on the choice of the local coordinates.
For the point
x_{0}\in D
its inverse
image

m\geq 0

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:=H^{0}\mathbf{D}\widetilde{\pi}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0}\simeq H^{0}\mathbf{D}\tilde{p}_{*}(\mathbf{D}\tilde{i}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0})

(4.16)

be the 0-th direct image of
\mathbf{D}\tilde{i}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0}
by

\tilde{p}

\tilde{p}_{*}\bigl[\Omega^{n}_{Y\times X/X}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{Y\times X}}\mathbf{D}\tilde{i}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0}\bigr]\longrightarrow H^{0}\mathbf{D}\tilde{p}_{*}(\mathbf{D}\tilde{i}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0})=\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

For the construction of

, see

[

Note that on the open subset

of

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}\sigma=\mathcal{M}
and

\sigma^{\prime}
coincides with
\sigma
.
For

U_{j}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=H^{0}\mathbf{D}\tilde{p}_{*}(\mathbf{D}\tilde{i}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0})

H^{0}R\tilde{p}_{*}\Bigl\{{\rm DR}_{Y\times X/X}\bigl(U_{j}(\mathbf{D}\tilde{i}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0})\bigr)\Bigr\}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}.

(4.17)

Then by the proof of
[
25

\{U_{j}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a
good
V

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and for any

p_{\tau}(\theta+j)U_{j}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\subset U_{j-1}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}).

(4.18)

Moreover by our construction, the section

\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is contained
in
U_{0}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
. Let

\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}\sigma^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

(4.19)

be the
\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}
-submodule of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

\sigma^{\prime}

Combining these results together, we get

p_{\tau}(\theta-(l-1))\cdots p_{\tau}(\theta-1)p_{\tau}(\theta)\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}\cap U_{-l}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\subset V_{-1}(\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}})\sigma^{\prime}.

(4.22)

This implies that the minimal polynomial

p_{\sigma^{\prime}}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

for the section
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

divides the product

p_{\tau}(s-(l-1))\cdots p_{\tau}(s-1)p_{\tau}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s].

(4.23)

Now, according to Kashiwara
[
20
]
, there exists

\mathbf{D}\widetilde{\pi}_{*}(\mathbf{D}\widetilde{\pi}^{*}\mathcal{M})\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}

(4.24)

\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}

\mathbf{D}\widetilde{\pi}^{*}\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{N}
(use e.g. the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence)
and
\mathcal{N}_{0}\subset\mathcal{N}
,
we obtain a morphism

\Psi:\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=H^{0}\mathbf{D}\widetilde{\pi}_{*}\mathcal{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}

(4.25)

\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}

Then the section
\Psi(\sigma^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}

of
\mathcal{M}

\sigma\in\mathcal{M}
on the

on the

p_{\sigma}(s)=b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(-s-1)

divides the one
p_{\sigma^{\prime}}(s)
. Now the

∎

We have seen that the roots of our b-functions

b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
are rational numbers.
Let
\rho:\mathbb{Q}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
be the

.

Next we shall give a lower bound for the
subsets
(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset\mathbb{Q}
.
In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we have seen
that the minimal polynomial of
s
acting on the

\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module

\mathcal{K}\simeq\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s](\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\mathcal{D}_{X}[s](\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+k})}

(4.26)

is equal to our b-function
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
.
Localizing it along the hypersurface

we obtain a new
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module

\mathcal{K}\Bigl[\frac{1}{G}\Bigr]\simeq\frac{\{\mathcal{D}_{X}[s](\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s})\}[\frac{1}{G}]}{\{\mathcal{D}_{X}[s](\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+1})\}[\frac{1}{G}]}

(4.27)

on which
s
still acts. Obviously, we have

b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)=0
on
\mathcal{K}[\frac{1}{G}]
.
By this observation, we obtain the following result.
We denote the localized ring
\mathcal{D}_{X}[\frac{1}{G}]

simply by
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
m\geq 0
be a non-negative integer. Then
there exists a non-zero polynomial
b(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

satisfying the equation

b(s)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s}\Bigr)=\tilde{P}(s)\Bigl(\frac{1}{G^{m}}f^{s+1}\Bigr)

(4.28)

for some
\tilde{P}(s)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]
.

Definition 4.3
.

For
m\geq 0
we denote by
\tilde{b}_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
the minimal polynomial
satisfying the equation in Theorem
4.2

and call it the reduced Bernstein-Sato polynomial
or the reduced b-function of
f
of order
m
.

Since
\tilde{P}(s)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]
in the
equation (
4.28
) can be rewritten as

\tilde{P}(s)=\frac{1}{G^{m}}\circ\tilde{Q}(s)\circ G^{m}\qquad(\tilde{Q}(s)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]),

(4.29)

in fact the condition on
b(s)
in Theorem
4.2

is equivalent to the existence of some

\tilde{Q}(s)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]
satisfying
the simpler equation

b(s)f^{s}=\tilde{Q}(s)f^{s+1}

(4.30)

independent of
m\geq 0
. This shows that we have

\tilde{b}_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}}(s)=\tilde{b}_{f,1}^{{\rm mero}}(s)=\tilde{b}_{f,2}^{{\rm mero}}(s)=\cdots\cdots\cdots.

(4.31)

Therefore we denote
\tilde{b}_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

simply by
\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
.
Then by our construction, for any
m\geq 0

our b-function
b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
is
divided by the reduced one
\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
.
We thus obtain a lower bound

(\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset\mathbb{Q}

(4.32)

for the subset
(b_{f,m}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)\subset\mathbb{Q}
.
Several authors studied (global) b-functions on algebraic

[
24
]

[
2
]

could be considered

Remark 4.4
.

It looks that the
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-modules
\mathcal{K}

and
\mathcal{K}[\frac{1}{G}]
above are regular holonomic,
but we could not prove it. We conjecture that
they are regular holonomic.

From now on, we consider the special case where
the meromorphic function
f=\frac{F}{G}
is
quasi-homogeneous. More precisely, for
a local coordinate system
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

of
X
such that
x_{0}=\{x=0\}
, we assume
that there exist a vector field

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}

(
w=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is a weight vector) and

d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
d:=d_{1}-d_{2}\not=0

such that

vF=d_{1}\cdot F,\quad vG=d_{2}\cdot G,\quad vf=d\cdot f\not=0.

(4.33)

Let us calculate
\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

of such
f
following the arguments in

[
20
, Section 6.4]
.
First, by the condition
vf=d\cdot f

(
d\not=0
) we have isomorphisms

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]f^{s}\simeq\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f^{s},\quad\mathcal{K}\Bigl[\frac{1}{G}\Bigr]\simeq\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f^{s}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f^{s+1}}

(4.34)

and for our reduced b-function
\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)

there exists
\tilde{P}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}

such that

\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)f^{s}=\tilde{P}f^{s+1}.

(4.35)

In this situation, by the proof of

[
20
, Lemma 6.6]
we see that

\mathcal{K}[\frac{1}{G}]
is a holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module and
Theorem
4.2
can be proved also by
using the trick in the proof of

[
20
, Theorem 6.7]
.
If we set
s=-1
in (
4.35
) we obtain

\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(-1)=f\tilde{P}(1).

(4.36)

Restricting this equality to the subset

F^{-1}(0)\setminus G^{-1}(0)\subset X\setminus G^{-1}(0)
we see that

\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(-1)=0
.
Namely, for a non-zero polynomial

\tilde{\beta}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]

we have

\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)=(s+1)\cdot\tilde{\beta}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s).

(4.37)

On the other hand, by (
4.36
) we
have
\tilde{P}(1)=0
and hence

\tilde{P}\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}\partial_{x_{i}}
. Namely there exist

\tilde{Q}_{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
(
1\leq i\leq n
)
such that
\tilde{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Q}_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
. Moreover, if we set

f_{i}:=f_{x_{i}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{F_{x_{i}}G-FG_{x_{i}}}{G^{2}}\quad(1\leq i\leq n),

(4.38)

then we have

\partial_{x_{i}}f^{s+1}=(s+1)f_{i}f^{s}\quad(1\leq i\leq n).

(4.39)

Therefore we obtain

\tilde{\beta}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)f^{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Q}_{i}f_{i}f^{s}.

(4.40)

Conversely, for
\tilde{Q}_{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}

(
1\leq i\leq n
) satisfying this equality
the differential operator
\tilde{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Q}_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}

satisfies the one (
4.35
).
Consequently, our
\tilde{\beta}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)\not=0
is the minimal polynomial

b(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
satisfying the condition

b(s)f^{s}\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f_{i}f^{s}
.
Since we have

v(f_{i}f^{s})=(d-w_{i}+d\cdot s)(f_{i}f^{s})\quad(1\leq i\leq n),

(4.41)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f_{i}f^{s}
is a

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]
-submodule of

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f^{s}\simeq\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}[s]f^{s}
. Set
h_{i}:=F_{x_{i}}G-FG_{x_{i}}=G^{2}f_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}

(
1\leq i\leq n
). Then the
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}
-module

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}:=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f^{s}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f_{i}f^{s}}\simeq\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}f^{s}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}h_{i}f^{s}}

(4.42)

has an action of
s
and the minimal polynomial
of
s
on it is equal to
\tilde{\beta}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
f=\frac{F}{G}
be as above and assume moreover
that
f^{-1}(0)=F^{-1}(0)\setminus G^{-1}(0)\subset X\setminus G^{-1}(0)
is smooth.
Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}=0
and

\tilde{b}_{f}^{{\rm mero}}(s)=s+1
.

Proof.

Let us consider the coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module

\mathcal{S}:=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{X}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{X}h_{i}}

(4.43)

and its localization

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}:=\mathcal{S}\Bigl[\frac{1}{G}\Bigr]\simeq\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{X}h_{i}}.

(4.44)

Note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
is a quotient
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
. Since
f=\frac{F}{G}

is quasi-homogeneous of degree

, there is no singular point of

f
in
X\setminus(F^{-1}(0)\cup G^{-1}(0))
.
Then by the smoothness of

(4.45)

This implies that the support of the coherent
\mathcal{D}_{X}
-module

\mathcal{S}
is contained in
G^{-1}(0)\subset X
. Then by
Hilbert’s nullstellensatz we get

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}=0
and hence
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}=0
.
∎

.

5
Multiplier ideals for meromorphic functions

In this section, we define multiplier ideal sheaves
for the meromorphic function
f=\frac{F}{G}

and study their basic properties.
Recall that multiplier ideals for
holomorphic functions were introduced by Nadel
[
28
]
.
For their precise properties, we refer to
the excellent book
[
22
]
by Lazarsfeld.
For the meromorphic function
f=\frac{F}{G}

we define them as follows. Denote by

{\rm L}^{1}_{{\rm loc}}
the set of locally
integrable functions on
X
.

Definition 5.1
.

For a positive real number
\alpha>0
we define
an ideal
\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{O}_{X}
of

\mathcal{O}_{X}
by

\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha}:=\Bigl\{h\in\mathcal{O}_{X}\ |\ \frac{|h|^{2}}{|f|^{2\alpha}}=\frac{|h|^{2}\cdot|G|^{2\alpha}}{|F|^{2\alpha}}\in{\rm L}^{1}_{{\rm loc}}\Bigr\}

(5.1)

and call it the multiplier ideal of
f

of order
\alpha>0
.

Let
\pi:Y\longrightarrow X
be a
resolution of singularities
of the divisor
D=F^{-1}(0)\cup G^{-1}(0)\subset X
as in
Section
4
. Here we assume moreover
that the meromorphic function

g=\frac{F\circ\pi}{G\circ\pi}
has no
point of indeterminacy on the whole
Y
.
Such a resolution
\pi:Y\longrightarrow X

always exists. Let
{\rm div}\ g
be the divisor on
Y

defined by
g
. Then there exist two
effective divisors
({\rm div}\ g)_{+}
and

({\rm div}\ g)_{-}
such that

{\rm div}\ g=({\rm div}\ g)_{+}-({\rm div}\ g)_{-}.

(5.2)

By our assumption, their supports, which we
denote by
g^{-1}(0)
and
g^{-1}(\infty)

respectively, are disjoint from each other.
By using such a resolution
\pi:Y\longrightarrow X
,
we can easily see that for
\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha>0

we have
\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha}
. Then, as in the case
where
f
is holomorphic, we can define
the jumping numbers of the multiplier ideals

\{\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
.
In the situation as above, we have
g^{-1}(\infty)\subset(G\circ\pi)^{-1}(0)
but

g:Y\setminus(G\circ\pi)^{-1}(0)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
can be extended to a
holomorphic function

\tilde{g}:Y\setminus g^{-1}(\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Let

\iota_{\tilde{g}}:Y\setminus g^{-1}(\infty)\longrightarrow Y\times\mathbb{C}\qquad(y\longmapsto(y,\tilde{g}(y)))

(5.3)

be the graph embedding defined by
\tilde{g}
.
From now, we shall use the terminologies of
mixed Hodge modules. For example, regarding
the holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{X\times\mathbb{C}}
-module
\mathcal{M}

as a mixed Hodge module on
X\times\mathbb{C}
,
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
we set

F_{p}V_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}=F_{p}\mathcal{M}\cap V_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}.

(5.4)

We denote the normal crossing divisor

(G\circ\pi)^{-1}(0)
in
Y
by
E
and
consider the regular holonomic
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
-module

\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)
as a mixed Hodge module.
Then its Hodge filtration
\{F_{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)\}_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}

satisfies the condition

F_{p}\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)\simeq 0\quad(p<0),\qquad F_{0}\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)\not=0.

(5.5)

Moreover
F_{0}\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)\subset\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)
is
the subsheaf of
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)
consisting of
meromorphic functions on
Y
having
poles of order
\leq 1
only along
E\subset Y
.
See Mustata-Popa
[
27
, Chapter D]
for the details
about the Hodge filtration of
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)
.
We denote the restriction of

F_{0}\mathcal{O}_{Y}(*E)\simeq\mathcal{O}_{Y}(E)
to

Y\setminus g^{-1}(\infty)

simply by
\mathcal{E}
. Then the following
propositon can be proved just by following
the arguments in Budur-Saito
[
9
]

(see also
[
7
, Sections 3 and 4]
for more precise
explanations). We set
Y^{\circ}:=Y\setminus g^{-1}(\infty)
and

\varpi:=(\pi\times{\rm id}_{\mathbb{C}})\circ\iota_{\tilde{g}}:Y^{\circ}\longrightarrow X\times\mathbb{C}\qquad(y\longmapsto(\pi(y),\tilde{g}(y))).

(5.6)

Let
K_{Y/X}
be the relative canonical
divisor of
\pi:Y\longrightarrow X
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\alpha>0
be a positive real number.
Then for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1

there exists an isomorphism

F_{1}V_{-\alpha}\mathcal{M}\simeq\varpi_{*}\Bigl\{\mathcal{O}_{Y^{\circ}}(K_{Y/X})\otimes\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{O}_{Y^{\circ}}\Bigl(-\lfloor(\alpha-\varepsilon)({\rm div}\ g)_{+}\rfloor\Bigr)\Bigr\}.

(5.7)

Now let
{\rm pr}_{X}:X\times\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow X
be the projection. Then there exists an
injective homomrphism of sheaves

\gamma:\mathcal{O}_{X}\longrightarrow({\rm pr}_{X})_{*}\mathcal{M}\qquad(h\longmapsto h\cdot\sigma_{0}),

(5.8)

by which we regard
\mathcal{O}_{X}
as a subsheaf of

({\rm pr}_{X})_{*}\mathcal{M}
. By Proposition

5.2
and the local
integrability condition in Definition
5.1
,
we obtain the following analogue for
meromorphic functions of
Budur-Saito
[
9
, Theorem (0.1)]

(see also
[
7
]
for the details).
Note that by Proposition
5.2

for any
\alpha>0
we have

\mathcal{O}_{X}\cap({\rm pr}_{X})_{*}F_{1}V_{-\alpha}\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{O}_{X}\cap({\rm pr}_{X})_{*}V_{-\alpha}\mathcal{M}.

(5.9)

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
be a positive real number.
Then we have

\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha}=\mathcal{O}_{X}\cap({\rm pr}_{X})_{*}V_{<-\alpha}\mathcal{M}=\{h\in\mathcal{O}_{X}\ |\ {\rm ord}_{\{t=0\}}(h\cdot\sigma_{0})>\alpha-1\}.

(5.10)

By this theorem and

{\rm ord}_{\{t=0\}}(h\cdot\sigma_{0})\subset\bigcup_{i=0,1,2,\ldots}\Bigl\{{\rm ord}_{\{t=0\}}(\sigma_{0})+i\Bigr\}\qquad(h\in\mathcal{O}_{X})

(5.11)

(see the proof of Lemma
3.3
),
we immediately obtain
the following generalization of
the celebrated theorem of Ein-Lazarsfeld-Smith-Varolin

[
13
]
to meromorphic functions.

Corollary 5.4
.

The jumping numbers of the multiplier ideals

\{\mathcal{J}(X,f)_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha>0}
are
contained in the set

\bigcup_{i=0,1,2,\ldots}\Bigl\{-(b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}})^{-1}(0)+i\Bigr\}\subset\mathbb{Q}_{>0}.

(5.12)

Moreover the minimal jumping number
\alpha>0
is
equal to the negative of the largest root
of
b_{f,0}^{{\rm mero}}(s)
.
