Piecewise Temperleyan dimers and a multiple SLE 8
Nathanaël Berestycki and Mingchang Liu

Abstract
We consider the dimer model on piecewise Temperleyan, simply connected domains, on families of graphs which include the square lattice as well as superposition graphs. We focus on the spanning tree 𝒯 δ \mathcal{T}_{\delta} associated to this model via Temperley’s bijection, which turns out to be a Uniform Spanning Tree with singular alternating boundary conditions. Generalising the work of the second author with Peltola and Wu [ LPW21 ] we obtain a scaling limit result for 𝒯 δ \mathcal{T}_{\delta} . For instance, in the simplest nontrivial case, the limit of 𝒯 δ \mathcal{T}_{\delta} is described by a pair of trees whose Peano curves are shown to converge jointly to a multiple SLE 8 pair. The interface between the trees is shown to be given by an SLE ( − 1 , … , − 1 ) 2 {}_{2}(-1,\ldots,-1) curve. More generally we provide an equivalent description of the scaling limit in terms of imaginary geometry. This allows us to make use of the results developed by the first author and Laslier and Ray [ BLR20 ] . We deduce that, universally across these classes of graphs, the corresponding height function converges to a multiple of the Gaussian free field with boundary conditions that jump at each non-Temperleyan corner. After centering, this generalises a result of Russkikh [ Rus18 ] who proved it in the case of the square lattice. Along the way, we obtain results of independent interest on chordal hypergeometric SLE 8 ; for instance we show its law is equal to that of an SLE ( ρ ¯ ) 8 {}_{8}(\bar{\rho}) for a certain vector of force points, conditional on its hitting distribution on a specified boundary arc.



1
Introduction

The dimer model is one of the simplest but also most intriguing models of statistical mechanics. Introduced in the pioneering work of Temperley and Fisher
[
TF61
]

and, independently and nearly simultaneously, Kasteleyn
[
Kas61
]
, in the 1960s, the model can be defined on any finite weighted graph
G=(V,E)
admitting a perfect matching, and is the probability measure on perfect matchings
\mathbf{m}
of
G
given by

\mu(\textbf{m})=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{e\in\mathbf{m}}w_{e}

(1.1)

where
(w_{e})_{e\in E}
denote the edge weights of
G
. The dimer model is particularly well studied in the case where
G
is planar and bipartite, in which case it is in some sense exactly solvable. Nevertheless, despite remarkable progress over the last sixty years, the dimer model on large but finite graphs (say, on subdomains of the plane) remains notoriously difficult to study due to its extreme sensitivity to the microscopic details of the boundary conditions.

The dimer model is typically studied through its
height function
,
h:F\to\mathbb{R}
introduced by Thurston and defined on the set of faces
F
of the graph. The height function’s definition depends on the choice of a reference dimer configuration
\mathbf{m}_{0}
or more generally reference flow, and even then is defined only up to constants. However, the centered height function
h-\mathbb{E}(h)
is canonically associated with the dimer model. See
[
Ken09
]
for references and relevant definitions. This height function turns the dimer model into a model of random surfaces and the main question concern its large scale behaviour. A remarkable conjecture of Kenyon and Okunkov predicts that the large scale behaviour is in great generality described by the Gaussian free field, albeit after a possibly nontrivial (and not conformal) change of coordinates. This conjecture was proved in the case of Temperleyan boundary conditions in the landmark paper of Kenyon
[
Ken00
]
,
[
Ken01
]
and in a handful of other cases
[
Rus18
,
BLR20
,
BLR19
,
Pet15
]
; see
[
Gor21
]
and references therein for an introduction in the case of lozenge tilings.

The Temperleyan boundary conditions introduced and studied by Kenyon in
[
Ken00
]
are perhaps the most canonical from the combinatorial point of view; they appear naturally both in the methods involving the discrete holomorphicity of the inverse Kasteleyn matrix, and for methods involving the Temperley bijection as studied say in
[
BLR20
,
BLR19
,
BLR22
]
and which will be relevant to this article. It is worth noting that they are in some sense
trivial
from the point of view of the phenomenology of the dimer model, since they exclude the so-called frozen and gaseous phases, and lead to GFF fluctuations without change of coordinates.

In this paper, we will consider the dimer model in
2n
-black-piecewise Temperleyan domains on the square lattice (or simply
2n
-piecewise Temperleyan in the following), which we will introduce precisely in Section
2.1
. These boundary conditions, first considered in the work of Russkikh
[
Rus18
]
, are perhaps the simplest way to relax the Temperleyan condition on the boundary.
By definition, in such a domain there is a total of
2n
white corners for some
n\geq 2
, of which necessarily
n-1
are concave, and
n+1
convex (see Figure
2.1
for an illustration).
We will make an additional assumption on these domains: between two consecutive concave white corners of
\Omega
(i.e., not separated by intermediate white corners), the boundary black squares belong to a fixed type of black vertices, which we take to be

\blacksquare_{0}
.

This can always be assumed without loss of generality if
n=2,3
but not if
n\geq 4
(in that case there exist examples of
2n
-piecewise Temperleyan domains which do not satisfy this additional condition).
In fact our results will be valid much more generally for graphs obtained by planar superposition satisfying very mild assumptions, see Section
2.2
for a precise description. For such graphs we will formulate a natural analogue of the boundary conditions described above (piecewise Temperleyan boundary together with the additional assumption).
Our main result, valid both for the square lattice and more generally for superposition graphs defined in Section
2.2
, is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a simply connected domain with
C^{1}
simple boundary and suppose that
\{\Omega_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
is a sequence of
2n
-black-piecewise Temperleyan domains satisfying the above assumption, which converges to
\Omega
. We denote by
h_{\delta}
the height function of the dimer model on
\Omega_{\delta}
and denote by
h
the Gaussian free field on
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Define
\chi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Then, we have for every
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
k\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|(h_{\delta}-\mathbb{E}[h_{\delta}],f)-\frac{1}{\chi}(h,f)\right|^{k}\right]\to 0.

See (
4.1
) for the precise notion of convergence of
\Omega_{\delta}
to
\Omega
. In the case of the square lattice, this result had already been proved by Russkikh
[
Rus18
]
. Russkikh’s proof follows the method developed in the groundbreaking work of Kenyon
[
Ken00
,
Ken01
]
which relies on the analysis of the inverse Kaseteleyn matrix. In the Temperleyan setting, Kenyon had shown that the inverse Kasteleyn matrix converges in a suitable to the holomorphic function associated with the derivative of the Green function with Dirichlet boundary conditions. These eventually lead Dirichlet boundary conditions for the field. By contrast, in the piecewise Temperleyan case analysed by Russkikh, the inverse Kasteleyn matrix corresponds to a Green function with
mixed Neumann–Dirichlet
boundary conditions. Yet surprisingly, as a result of fairly striking cancellations, she demonstrated that the limiting centered height field has purely Dirichlet boundary conditions, as is the case in Theorem
1.1
. We shed some light on this phenomenon, showing in a precise geometric way how the presence of non-Temperleyan corners influences the limiting geometry of the model.

This geometric phenomenon can in fact also be formulated purely in terms of height functions. This relies on the connection to
imaginary geometry
initiated in
[
BLR20
]
, and which we now begin to discuss.
As mentioned above,
h_{\delta}-\mathbb{E}[h_{\delta}]
does not depend on the choice of a reference flow. There is however
one
particular choice of reference flow which turns out to be quite canonical, and which corresponds to the normalised winding of the edges in the graph (see e.g.
[
KS04
,
BLR16
]
or
[
BLR19
]
for definitions).
For this choice, it will turn out that
\mathbb{E}[h_{\delta}]
converges to a harmonic function
u
(which will be defined in Section
2.3
) locally uniformly. This harmonic function will give the boundary values of the Gaussian free field. In contrast to the purely Temperleyan case, these boundary values are now nonconstant, and have jumps at each non-Temperleyan corner. Thus the effect of the non-Temperleyan boundary conditions is to create jumps at the corner (these jumps can already be seen to occur at the discrete level). Of course, when we subtract the expectation, the corresponding field becomes purely Dirichlet, and this cancels the effect of the non-Temperleyan corners. This explains why the limiting field in Russkikh’s work
[
Rus18
]
is the Dirichlet Gaussian free field, just as in the purely Temperleyan case.

Temperley’s bijection.

To formulate this geometrically, we make use of Temperley’s bijection which associates to any dimer configuration a spanning tree on a related graph. Let
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
denote the random tree associated to the dimer configuration
\mathbf{m}
of (
1.1
) in a piecewise-Temperleyan domain (to be precise,
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
is the
\blacksquare_{0}
-tree; Temperley’s bijection also associates to
\mathbf{m}
a tree living on the
\blacksquare_{1}
-lattice, but this is simply the planar dual of
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
). Let
u
denote the harmonic function from
\Omega
which jumps alternatively by
\pm\lambda
at each non-Temperleyan corner of
\partial\Omega
(see Figure
2.3
for an illustration): that is,
let us write the
2n
white corners as
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n}
as we go along the boundary in counterclockwise order, with both
x_{1}
and
x_{2n}
assumed to be convex (recall there are two more convex corners than concave). Assume first that
\Omega=\mathbb{H}
.
We define
u=u_{\mathbb{H}}
on the real line to be
+\lambda
on
(-\infty,x_{1})
and then jump by
-\lambda
on each convex corner, and
+\lambda
at each concave corner. Note that
u
will be equal to
-\lambda
on
(x_{2n},\infty)
. Then
u_{\mathbb{H}}
is simply the harmonic extension of this boundary data. When
\Omega
is not the upper-half plane,
u
is obtained by applying the conformal change of coordinates of imaginary geometry associated with
\kappa=2
, that is,

u_{\Omega}=u_{\mathbb{H}}\circ\varphi^{-1}-\chi\arg(\varphi^{-1})^{\prime};

where
\varphi:\mathbb{H}\to\Omega
is a conformal mapping and
\chi=2/\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2}/2=1/\sqrt{2}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

As the mesh size
\delta\to 0
, the Temperleyan tree
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
converges to the tree consisting of the flow lines for
\kappa=2
of the field
h_{\Omega}+u_{\Omega}
, where
h_{\Omega}
is a
\GFF
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\Omega
.

As announced above, note that the effect of the white corners is thus to change the boundary data of the limiting field (but, as already mentioned, this disappears when we take away the expectation). We also obtain a more direct, geometric description of the scaling limit of
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
in the particular case when
n=2
, which is the simplest non trivial case. In that case there are four white corners which we denote respectively by
a,b,c,d
. Consistent with our earlier notation, the single white concave corner is denoted by
b
. It can be shown that, deterministically, the arcs
(ab),\ldots,(da)
of the topological quadrilateral
(\Omega,a,b,c,d)
alternate between Dirichlet and Neumann portions for
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
in a precise sense. Furthermore,
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
contains a unique curve, which we call
\gamma_{\delta}
, starting from
b
and ending in a random location
z
on the arc
(cd)
. This curves splits
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}
into two trees, which we call
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{L},\mathcal{T}_{\delta}^{R}
respectively (left and right correspond to the planar orientation inherited from
\gamma_{\delta}
viewed as a curve oriented from
b
to
(cd)
). To this pair of trees we can associate a pair of Peano curves, which we call
\eta_{\delta}^{L}
and
\eta_{\delta}^{R}
respectively. These curves emanate from
d
and
c
respectively, and terminate in
a
and
b
respectively. See Figure
1.1
for an illustration. We now state a scaling limit result describing the limiting law of these random curves.

. In the limit, we get a multiple SLE

Theorem 1.3
.

As
\delta\to 0
, the random curves
\gamma_{\delta},\eta^{L}_{\delta},\eta^{R}_{\delta}
converge jointly to a triple
(\gamma,\eta^{L},\eta^{R})
. The interface
\gamma
between the two trees has the law of a chordal SLE
{}_{2}(-1,-1,-1,-1)
from
b
, stopped when hitting the arc
(cd)
(call
z
this hitting point).
Given
\gamma
,
\eta^{R}
and
\eta^{L}
are independent standard chordal SLE
8
in their respective domains.
Hence the joint law of
(\eta^{L},\eta^{R})
is a
multiple SLE
8
in the sense that given
\eta^{L}
, the curve
\eta^{R}
is simply a standard SLE
8
in
\Omega\setminus\eta^{L}
, from
c
to
b
(and the same holds for
\eta^{L}
given
\eta^{R}
).

Marginal laws.

The marginal laws of
\eta^{L}
and
\eta^{R}
can also be described:
\eta^{L}
has the law of a chordal SLE
{}_{8}(2,-2)
in
\Omega
from
d
to
a
with force points at
c
(corresponding to weight
\rho=2
) and
b
(corresponding to weight
\rho=-2
). Similarly,
\eta^{R}
has the law of a chordal SLE
{}_{8}(2,2)
in
\Omega
from
c
to
b
, with force points at
d
and
a
. Note that in this theorem, the target point of
\gamma
needs not be specified, as given the value of the force points (four force points of weight
\rho_{i}=-1
, summing up to a total weight of
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=-4=\kappa-6
), the curve has the locality property and is hence target independent.

Multiple SLE.

A joint law on curves
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
is called multiple (say chordal) SLE
κ
in a domain
D
, if the conditional law of
\eta_{i}
given
\eta_{2-i}
is a chordal SLE
κ
in the complementary domain. When
\kappa\leq 4
such laws are known to be unique by a result of Beffara, Peltola and Wu
[
BPW21
]
. This is however not known when
\kappa>4
. In fact, for
\kappa\geq 8
there can be no uniqueness as this is the space-filling regime: any choice of a law for the interface
\gamma
separating the two curves gives rise to a pair
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
of multiple SLEs in the sense of this definition. We do not know how to canonically characterise the law of the multiple SLE
8

(\eta^{L},\eta^{R})
appearing in the theorem, except by specifying the marginals as we have done.

Connection to hypergeometric SLE
8
.

[
HLW20
]

Preliminaries

2.1
Piecewise Temperleyan domain and Temperley’s bijection

In this subsection, we will recall the definition of black-piecewise Temperleyan domain and establish the corresponding Temperleyan’s bijection between dimer configurations and spanning trees.

First of all, let us recall the definition of black-piecewise Temperleyan domain, which was introduced in
[
Rus18
]
. Suppose
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is a simply connected domain, whose boundary
\partial\Omega
is a simple curve on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
\Omega
can be divided into squares of size one which inherit from
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
the checkboard black-white colouring. It is further useful to subdivide the black squares into two distinct sublattices: more precisely, denote by
\blacksquare_{0}
the black squares on even rows and denote by
\blacksquare_{1}
the black color on odd rows. We call
\Omega
is a
2n
-black-piecewise Temperleyan domain, if it has
2n
white corners. It can then be shown that of those, necessarily
n+1
are convex and
n-1
are concave corners. If
n\geq 3
, we assume that between two consecutive concave white corners of
\Omega
(i.e., not separated by intermediate white corners), the boundary black squares belong to

\blacksquare_{0}
. See the left hand side of Figure
2.1
for an example of black-piecewise Temperleyan domain in the case
n=2
. When
n\geq 3
, it is possible for two consecutive white corners to be concave, but the above assumption imposes restrictions on the combinatorics, as we will see below.

\Omega

-black-piecewise Temperleyan domain.
Define

to be the dual graph of
\Omega
. The vertices of
\Omega^{*}
are the centers of the squares of
\Omega
and the edges are straight lines connecting nearest pairs of vertices. We then say that a vertex of
\Omega^{*}
is
\blacksquare_{0}
,

\blacksquare_{1}
or white, depending on the colour of the corresponding square in
\Omega
. Define
\Omega_{0}^{*}
to be the graph whose vertices are the
\blacksquare_{0}

\Omega^{*}
and whose edges are straight lines connecting nearest pairs of vertices. Define
\overline{\Omega}^{*}_{0}
to be the graph whose vertices are the union of
\Omega_{0}^{*}
and
\blacksquare_{0}
vertices adjacent to
\Omega_{0}^{*}
and whose edges are straight lines connecting nearest pairs of vertices. The boundary
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}\setminus\Omega_{0}^{*}
consists of
n

x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n}
, labelled in counterclockwise order in such a way that the connected components are respectively formed by
(x_{2}x_{3}),\ldots,(x_{2n}x_{1})
. We can assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}
and
x_{2n}
are adjacent to two convex corners. (In general,

will be adjacent to a white corner when that corner is convex, and will be within distance 3 of that corner if that corner is concave). See Figure

is distinguished by the fact that it is the only connected portion of the boundary

This comes from our assumption that between two consecutive concave corners, the black vertices are all of type
\blacksquare_{0}
. As we see, this does not prevent concave white corners to come consecutively, but in particular no three consecutive white corners can exist.

We denote by

the vertices of
\Omega^{*}_{0}

). As already mentioned, by our assumption, for

A dimer cover of

Given a dimer configuration

Denote by

Lemma 2.1

(Temperley’s bijection)
.

The map
\omega\mapsto\omega^{*}
is a bijection between
\mathcal{D}(\Omega^{*})
and
\mathcal{U}(\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*})
.

Proof.

First, we will show that
\omega^{*}
is indeed an element in
\mathcal{U}(\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*})
. Since
\omega
is a dimer cover, it is clear that
\omega^{*}
contains all the vertices of
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}
. We need to show that
\omega^{*}
does not contain loops. Suppose that
L
is a loop on
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}
which is contained in
\omega^{*}
. We denote by
\tilde{L}
the union of
L
and the squares surrounded by
L
. By induction on the number
\blacksquare_{1}
squares in
\tilde{L}
, we have that the number of squares in
\tilde{L}
is odd. Note that if
L
belongs to
\omega^{*}
, the subgraph
\tilde{L}
is covered by edges in
\omega
. This implies that the number of squares in
\tilde{L}
should be even, which is a contradiction. Thus, the loop
L
can not belong to
\omega^{*}
. This implies that
\omega^{*}
is a spanning forest on
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}
.

To show that
\omega^{*}
is a tree, recall that starting from every vertex in
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}
, except on
c_{n}=(x_{2n}x_{1})
, there is a single oriented edge in
\omega^{*}
going out of that vertex. Following the edges in the forward direction, we obtain a path which necessarily ends in
c_{n}
. Thus every vertex is connected to
c_{n}
and
\omega^{*}
is in
\mathcal{U}(\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*})
(i.e., is a spanning tree), as by definition of
\omega^{*}
, this path connecting
c_{i}
to
c_{n}
starts with the oriented edge
z_{i}z_{i}^{+}
.

Second, we will show that this is a bijection. Define the dual graph of
\overline{\Omega}_{1}^{*}
similarly as defining
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}
by considering the
\blacksquare_{1}
squares. Note that every spanning tree on
\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*}
determines a dual forest on the dual graph of
\overline{\Omega}_{1}^{*}
. Since every dimer cover
\omega
is determined by
\omega^{*}
and the corresponding dual forest, we deduce that the map
\omega\mapsto\omega^{*}
is injective. For every
\omega^{*}\in\mathcal{U}(\overline{\Omega}_{0}^{*})
, it is clear that dividing the edges of
\omega^{*}
and the edges of the corresponding dual forest, we will get a dimer cover on
\Omega
. Thus, the map
\omega\mapsto\omega^{*}
is surjective. This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Generalisation

We now explain briefly how the setup considered in this paper can be generalised to a much bigger class of planar graphs. Roughly these graphs are obtained by superposing a planar graph and their dual (which is a setup in which Temperley’s bijection is known to apply, see
[
KPW00
]
) but where we pay attention to the choice of boundary conditions. Let
\{G_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
denote a sequence of graphs embedded in a domain
\Omega
, with an edge outer-boundary forming a simple curve
\lambda
.
Let us also fix

.

For

1.

(Bounded density)

.

(Good embedding)
The edges of the graph are embedded

(Irreducible)

(Invariance principle)

{(X_{t/\delta^{2}})_{t\geq 0}}\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0]{(d)}(B_{\phi(t)})_{t\geq 0}

(Uniform crossing estimate).
Let

We will consider the set

is directed, by definition, a spanning tree

is such that there is exactly one forward edge out of every non-wired vertex.
There is a natural measure on
\mathcal{U}(G_{\delta})
which is simply

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{t})\propto\prod_{e\in\mathbf{t}}w_{e};\quad\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(G_{\delta}).

We also consider the set
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}^{D}_{\delta})
of dimer configurations on
\mathcal{G}^{D}_{\delta}
, which we equip with a probability measure

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{m})\propto\prod_{e\in\mathbf{m}}w_{e};\quad\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}^{D}_{\delta}),

where an edge

of

inherits the weight

if it was part of a dual edge of
G_{\delta}
, and otherwise the weight of the corresponding primal edge oriented from black to white.

Temperley’s bijection has an obvious extension to this setting: namely, given

we associate to it the spanning tree
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{U}(G_{\delta})
by considering all the edges of
\mathbf{m}
emanating out of a primal black vertex and doubling them up: i.e., for each
(bw)\in\mathbf{m}
with
b\in V(G_{\delta})
, we add to
\mathbf{t}
the unique (primal) edge
bb^{\prime}
such that
w\in(bb^{\prime})
.

It is not hard to see that Temperley’s bijection can be generalised to this setup:

Lemma 2.2

(Generalised Temperley’s bijection)
.

The map
\mathbf{m}\mapsto\mathbf{t}
is a measure-preserving bijection between
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G}^{D}_{\delta})
and
\mathcal{U}(G_{\delta})
.

As a result of this bijection, all the results we prove for the dimer model on black-piecewise Temperleyan domains generalise in a straightforward manner to the dimer model on
\mathcal{G}^{D}_{\delta}
with the above law. We will not comment further on this distinction except where necessary, and we will write our proofs for concreteness in the setup of Section
2.1
.

).

Suppose first there exists

•

In the second case, we may assume that the free boundary intersect

For

\displaystyle g(z):=\begin{cases}\frac{h_{A}\circ\varphi^{-1}(z)}{h_{(cd)}\circ\varphi^{-1}(z)}&\text{ if }z\in\varphi(\Omega_{A})\\
\frac{\partial_{n}h_{A}\circ\varphi^{-1}(z)}{\partial_{n}h_{(cd)}\circ\varphi^{-1}(z)},&\text{ if }z\in(0,1)\cap\partial\varphi(\Omega_{A}).\end{cases}

(3.6)

Note that
g\circ\varphi
is continuous near
b
. Now, we consider a circle
\partial B(b,r)
and we denote by
l_{b,r}
the arc of that circle separating
b
and
(cd)
, and denote by
x:=l_{b,r}\cap(ab)
. We denote by
l_{b,r,\delta}
and
x_{\delta}
some discrete approximation on
\Omega_{\delta}
of
l_{b,r}
and
x
respectively. For every
\epsilon>0
, we may choose
r
small enough, such that for every
w\in B(b,r)\cap\Omega
, we have

|g\circ\varphi(w)-g\circ\varphi(b)|\leq\epsilon.

By uniform convergence in Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
, combining with
[
CW21
, Corollary 3.8]
, there exists
\eta>0
, such that if
v_{\delta}\in\Omega_{\delta}
with
|v_{\delta}-x_{\delta}|<\eta
, then we have

\left|\frac{h_{A_{\delta}}(v_{\delta})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(v_{\delta})}-g\circ\varphi(x)\right|\leq\epsilon,\quad\text{ for }\delta\text{ small enough}.

By the uniform convergence in Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
we have
\frac{h_{A_{\delta}}}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}}\big|_{l_{b,r,\delta}\cap B^{c}(x_{\delta},\eta)}
converges to
g\circ\varphi|_{l_{b,r}\cap B^{c}(x,\eta)}
uniformly (by positivity of
h_{(\tilde{c}\tilde{d})}
on
l_{b,r,}\cap B^{c}(x,\eta)
). Thus, we have

\left|\frac{h_{A_{\delta}}(v_{\delta})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(v_{\delta})}-g\circ\varphi(b)\right|\leq\epsilon,\quad\text{ for all }v_{\delta}\in l_{b,r,\delta}.

(3.7)

Moreover, we can assume without loss of generality that
r
is small enough that
l_{b,r,\delta}
seperates
A_{\delta}
and
b_{\delta}
in
\Omega_{\delta}
. We denote by
\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta})
the hitting time of random walk
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
at
l_{b,r,\delta}
and denote by
\tau_{\delta}((a_{\delta}b_{\delta}))
and
\tau_{\delta}(A_{\delta})
similarly. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{h_{A_{\delta}}(b^{+}_{\delta})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b^{+}_{\delta})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{b^{+}_{\delta}}[\mathbb{1}_{\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta})<\tau_{\delta}(A_{\delta})}h_{A_{\delta}}(\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta}))]}{\mathbb{P}_{b^{+}_{\delta}}[\mathbb{1}_{\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta})<\tau_{\delta}((c_{\delta}d_{\delta}))}h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta}))]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{b^{+}_{\delta}}\left[\mathbb{1}_{\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta})<\tau_{\delta}(A_{\delta})}\frac{h_{A_{\delta}}(\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta}))}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta}))}h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta}))\right]}{\mathbb{P}_{b^{+}_{\delta}}[\mathbb{1}_{\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta})<\tau_{\delta}((c_{\delta}d_{\delta}))}h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(\tau_{\delta}(l_{b,r,\delta}))]}.

Combining with (
3.7
), we have

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{h_{A_{\delta}}(b^{+}_{\delta})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b^{+}_{\delta})}=\frac{\partial_{n}h_{A}\circ\varphi^{-1}(b)}{\partial_{n}h_{(cd)}\circ\varphi^{-1}(b)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
z_{0}\in\Omega
. For any
\alpha>0
and
0<\beta\leq(1/4)\dist((ab),(cd))
, let
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{\delta})
denote the event that there are two points
o_{1},o_{2}\in\gamma_{\delta}
with

o_{1},o_{2}\in B(z_{0},\beta/4)
with
|o_{1}-o_{2}|\leq\alpha
, and such that the subarc of
\gamma_{\delta}
between
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
is not contained in
B(z_{0},\beta)
. For every
\epsilon>0
and every
\beta>0
, we can choose
\alpha=\alpha(\beta,\epsilon)
, small enough that for every
\delta>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{\delta})]\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Denote by
B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta)
the discrete approximation of
B(z_{0},\beta)
. By Wilson’s algorithm, we know that the law of
\gamma_{\delta}
is same as the law of loop-erasure of the random walk
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
, starting from
b_{\delta}^{+}
, conditional on hitting
(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})
before
(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})
. We will use the same notations in
[
HLW20
, Lemma C.5]
. Let
t_{0}=0
. For
j\geq 1
, define inductively

s_{j}:=\inf\left\{r\geq t_{j-1}:\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\in B\left(z_{0},\beta/4\right)\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad t_{j}:=\inf\{r\geq s_{j}:\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\notin B(z_{0},\beta)\}.

Let
\tau_{\delta}(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})
be the hitting time of
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
at
(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})
. For every
r>0
, define
\text{LE}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta}[0,r])
to be the loop erasure of
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}[0,r]
. Define the event
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\text{LE}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta}[0,t_{j}]))
similarly as
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{\delta})
and define the event
T_{j}:=\{t_{j}\leq\tau_{\delta}(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\}
that there are
j
crossings. Denote
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\text{LE}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta}[0,t_{j}]))
by
Y_{j}
for simplicity. Note that
Y_{1}=\emptyset
and

\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{\delta})\subset\{\cup_{j=2}^{\infty}(Y_{j}\cap T_{j})\}\subset\{T_{m+1}\cup(\cup_{j=2}^{m}Y_{j})\},\quad\text{ for every }m>1.

Thus, we only need to control

\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\cup_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]\text{ and }\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[T_{m+1}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})].

For the first estimate, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\cup_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\mathbb{1}_{\{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)\}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(s_{1})}[\{\cup_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}\}\cap\{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})\}]]}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{h_{\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)}(b_{\delta}^{+})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}\left(\max_{v_{\delta}\in\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)}\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\cup_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}]\right).

By the Beurling estimate of Lemma
3.2
, there exist
C>0
and
k>0
, such that

\max_{v_{\delta}\in\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)}\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\cup_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}]\leq Cm^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{k}.

If
b_{\delta}^{+}\in B(z_{0},\beta/4)
, then combining with Lemma
3.5
, there exists
C>0
, such that

\frac{h_{\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)}(b_{\delta}^{+})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}\leq C.

If
b_{\delta}^{+}\notin B(z_{0},\beta/4)
, then if we denote by
d=d(b_{\delta}^{+},\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4))
, we have

\frac{h_{\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)}(b_{\delta}^{+})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}\leq\frac{h_{\partial B_{\delta}(b_{\delta}^{+},d)}(b_{\delta}^{+})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}\leq C,

where the last inequality is from Lemma
3.5
.
Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\cup_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]\leq Cm^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{k}.

For the second estimate, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[T_{m+1}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\mathbb{1}_{\{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta)\}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}(t_{1})}[\{t_{j}\leq\tau\}\cap\{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})\}]]}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{h_{\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta)}(b_{\delta}^{+})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}\left(\max_{v_{\delta}\in\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta)}\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})\cup(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})]\right)^{j}.

By the same argument in
[
HLW20
, Equation C.2]
(or by using a uniform crossing estimate), we have that there exists
0<q=q(\beta)<1
such that for every
\delta>0
,

\min_{v_{\delta}\in\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta)}\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})\cup(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }\partial B_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta/4)]\geq q.

By the same argument in the first estimate, we have that there exists
C>0
, such that

\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[T_{m+1}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]\leq C(1-q)^{j}.

(See also Lemma 4.13 in
[
BLR20
]
for a similar estimates). Thus, for every
\epsilon>0
, by first choosing
m
large enough and then choosing appropriate
\alpha=\alpha(\beta,\epsilon)
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{\delta})]\leq\epsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

By Lemma

, we can deduce the tightness of
\{\gamma_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The sequence
\{\gamma_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
is tight. Moreover, any subsequential limit is a simple curve in
\overline{\Omega}
which intersects
[ab]\cup[cd]
only at two ends.

Proof.

The tightness of
\gamma_{\delta}
follows from Lemma
3.7

using the ingenious argument given in
[
LSW04
, Lemma 3.12]
: essentially, a family of curves without bubbles forms a compact set. See also the proof of
[
HLW20
, Lemma C.2]
where this argument is also discussed.
Since the proof of tightness is identical, we do not include here.

Thus let us suppose that
\gamma
is any subsequential limit. Without loss of generality and with a slight abuse of notation, we will use
\{\gamma_{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
for the subsequence converging to
\gamma
.
We begin with the proof that
\gamma
intersects
(ab)\cup(cd)
at exactly two ends.
For this we modify the argument in
[
HLW20
, Lemma C.2]
. It suffices to show that almost surely
\gamma
intersects
[ab]
at one end, since the proof for
[cd]
is identical.

For every
s>0
, we define
\tau_{s,\delta}
to be the first time that
\gamma_{\delta}
hits
\partial B_{\delta}(b^{+}_{\delta},s)
and define

\tau_{s,\delta}(\epsilon):=\inf\{t\geq\tau_{s,\delta}:d(\gamma_{\delta}(t),(a_{\delta}b_{\delta}))<\epsilon\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\tau_{s,\delta}(\epsilon)<\infty]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }\partial B_{\delta}(b_{\delta}^{+},s)\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]}{\mathbb{P}_{b_{\delta}^{+}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]}

\displaystyle\times\max_{v_{\delta}\in\partial B_{\delta}(b_{\delta}^{+},s)}\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits the }\epsilon\text{-neighbour of }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})\text{ and hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h_{\partial B_{\delta}(b_{\delta}^{+},s)}(b_{\delta}^{+})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}

\displaystyle\times\max_{v_{\delta}\in\partial B_{\delta}(b_{\delta}^{+},s)}\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits the }\epsilon\text{-neighbour of }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})\text{ and hits }(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})\text{ before }(a_{\delta}b_{\delta})].

Combining with the Beurling estimate of Lemma

and Lemma
3.5
on the limiting behaviour of the ratio of harmonic functions on the right hand side, we have that there exist

and

, such that

\mathbb{P}[\tau_{s,\delta}\leq\tau_{s,\delta}(\epsilon)<\infty]\leq C\epsilon^{c}.

Define
\tau_{s}
and
\tau_{s}(\epsilon)
for
\gamma
similarly as defining
\tau_{s,\delta}
and
\tau_{s,\delta}(\epsilon)
for
\gamma_{\delta}
. By letting

, we have

\mathbb{P}[\tau_{s}\leq\tau_{s}(\epsilon)<\infty]\leq C\epsilon^{c}.

By letting
\epsilon\to 0
, we know that almost surely,
\gamma
will never hit
(ab)
after the time

. By letting

, we know that almost surely,
\gamma
intersects
[ab]
only at one end. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof.

By

By the same argument as in Lemma

, we can prove that

converges a.s. locally uniformly (under this coupling distribution). We denote the limit by

h_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}\circ\phi^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}^{-1}\begin{cases}=1&\text{ on }(\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}},\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d)))\\
=0&\text{ on }(\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a)),\infty)\\

By
[
HLW20
, Lemma 4.6]
, we have that
\frac{|SF_{2}^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}{|ST^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}
converges to the conformal modulus of the domain
(\Omega\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}])
with marked points
(a,b,\tilde{\eta}(t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}),d)
, as
\delta\to 0
(as can be seen from that proof, the only requirement for this convergence is the Carathéodory convergence of the domains). Thus, if we denote by
f_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}
the conformal map from

is a rectangle with unit width, then we have

h_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}}(\cdot)\times\frac{|SF_{2}^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}{|ST^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}

locally uniformly on

(as this is the only bounded harmonic function satisfying the required boundary conditions on

From the Schwarz–Christorffel formula, we have

f_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(z)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\frac{z-\tilde{W}_{t}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}}\left(s(s-1)\left(s-\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\right)\right)^{-1/2}ds}{\int_{0}^{1}\left(s(s-1)\left(s-\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\right)\right)^{-1/2}ds}.

For the denominator, if we change the variable by setting

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(s(s-1)\left(s-\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\right)\right)^{-1/2}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}}\times\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\sin^{2}\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}}\times{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1/2,1/2,1;\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\right),

where the last equality is obtained from the standard relation between elliptic function and hypergeometric function. In particular, as

f^{\prime}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(z)\sim\sqrt{{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}}\times z^{-3/2}\times\frac{1}{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1/2,1/2,1;\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\right)}.

Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{h_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}}(b^{+}_{\delta})\times\frac{|SF_{2}^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}{|ST^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})\times\frac{|SF_{2}^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta})|}{|ST^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta})|}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\phi(a)-\tilde{W}_{0}}}\times\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1/2,1/2,1;\frac{\phi(d)-\tilde{W}_{0}}{\phi(a)-\tilde{W}_{0}}\right)}{{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1/2,1/2,1;\frac{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(d))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}{\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}(\phi(a))-\tilde{W}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}}\right)}.

(3.11)

M_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{\epsilon}}
. Note that

\frac{h_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{b_{\delta},\epsilon},(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b^{+}_{\delta})}{h_{(c_{\delta}d_{\delta})}(b_{\delta}^{+})}\leq C

with uniformly positive probability (depending only on
\epsilon

Moreover, we claim that the ratio

\frac{|SF_{2}^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}{|ST^{\delta}(\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\tilde{\eta}_{R}^{\delta}[0,t\wedge\tilde{\tau}^{\delta}_{\epsilon}])|}

\epsilon
but not on
\tilde{\eta}

\delta_{n}\to 0
and a a sequences of paths
\eta^{n}
such that the ratio above tends to

3.2
Convergence of the discrete trees

In this section, we will prove Theorem
3.1
. Recall that at this stage we have proved convergence of the interface
\gamma_{\delta}
towards an SLE
2
type chordal curve, and we have proved convergence of the Peano curves on either side of it, but only up until they hit
(ab)
. This is not completely sufficient to get convergence of the discrete trees in the Schramm sense, because in order to describe all the branches of the tree we would need to know the convergence of the full Peano curves not just stopped when they hit
(ab)
. On the other hand, it is clear that, at the discrete level, given
\gamma_{\delta}
, the two components describing the rest of the tree can be viewed as a uniform spanning trees in their respective domain with Dobrushin boundary conditions, with the arcs
(da)
and
(bc)
reflecting, and every other part of the boundary being wired. This is very close to the setup of the original paper of Lawler, Schramm and Werner
[
LSW04
]
, but there is a technical subtlety: namely, in order to get a strong enough form of convergence (say uniform) the authors of
[
LSW04
]
require the boundary to be smooth, which obviously is not the case here (convergence in the sense of driving function and hence Carathéodory convergence does not require smoothness in their paper, unfortunately this is not sufficient here).

However, in order to upgrade the form of convergence from driving function convergence to strong (or uniform up to reparametrisation) convergence, it suffices to prove tightness with respect to this strong topology, as then the limit law is uniquely identified by the convergence of the driving function (which, we recall, holds without assumption on the domain). But tightness in this sense is in fact not hard to show: it follows along the same line as already argued in Lemma
3.8
, and in fact is considerably simpler since there is no need to condition on an event of asymptotically degenerate probability.
We leave the details to the reader; and obtain the convergence of the branches of the tree
\mathcal{T}^{\delta}
to the brances of
\mathcal{T}
in the sense of finite-dimensional distribution of the branches. To conclude the proof of Theorem
3.1
, it remains to apply the following well known compactness argument, due to Schramm
[
Sch00
, Theorem 10.2]
(this is sometimes known as Schramm’s lemma). Its proof is unchanged, and so we can simply quote the result here:

Lemma 3.13
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exist
\delta_{0}>0
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
, such that the following holds. For any set
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
with the property that every
z\in\Omega
, there exists
z_{i}
such that there exists a curve connecting
z
and
z_{i}
whose length is less than
\epsilon_{0}
. We view
(ab)
and
(cd)
as two points and define
V:=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},(ab),(cd)\}
. Denote by
V_{\delta}
the discrete approximation on
\Omega_{\delta}
. Denote by
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(V)
the minimal subtree containing
V_{\delta}
. Then,we have

\mathbb{P}[d_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta},\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(V))>\delta_{0}]\leq\epsilon.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

and simple such that

d((x^{\delta}_{2i-1}x^{\delta}_{2i}),(x_{2i-1}x_{2i}))\leq C\delta,\quad d((x^{\delta}_{2i}x^{\delta}_{2i+1}),(x_{2i}x_{2i+1}))\to 0;

and for

(4.2)

Recall the spanning trees appearing in Temperley’s bijection are described by a set

It turns out dealing with

[

\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\delta}

with alternating boundary conditions (where

Now, we consider a graph
G^{*}

Thus an equivalent description of the event

Finally, define the event

Note that there is a bijection from

In this section, we will therefore focus on consider the convergence of the uniform spanning tree

on

above.

and ending at the first time it hits

Theorem 4.1
.

The discrete tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}
converges as
\delta\to 0
under the metric

. Denote by
\mathcal{T}^{*}
the limit.
The continuous tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}
can be constructed as follows. Fix any dense set
\{z_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
in
\Omega
. We first sample the coupling of
\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\}
and the GFF

as in Theorem

. Recall that we denote by
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{n}
the connected components of

. For each

the unique index

such that

. Then, we sample the flow line starting from interior point
\gamma_{z_{i}}
in

is the closure of

By the same arguments as in Section

, it suffices to show the convergence of the boundary branches:

Theorem 4.2
.

The discrete curves
\{\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}\}
converge as

to the flow lines
\{\gamma_{1}^{I},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{I}\}

4.1
Tightness of the boundary branches

We start with the tightness of the boundary branches. First of all, we will give an estimate about the probability of
E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})
. Fix a small
r>0
such that
\partial B(x_{i},2r)\cap\partial B(x_{j},2r)=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
.
We denote by
\partial B(x_{i}^{\delta},r)
the discrete approximation of
\partial B(x_{i},r)
on
\Omega_{\delta}
. For every
v_{\delta}\in\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}B(z_{i}^{\delta},r)
, we define the harmonic function

h^{\delta}_{r}(v_{\delta}):=\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ hits }\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\partial B(z_{i}^{\delta},r)\text{ before }\cup_{i=1}^{n}(x_{2i}^{\delta}x_{2i+1}^{\delta})],

where
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
has the same law as the random walk killed at
\cup_{i=1}^{n}(x_{2i}^{\delta}x_{2i+1}^{\delta})
, and with reflecting boundaries
\cup_{i=1}^{n}(x_{2i-1}^{\delta}x_{2i}^{\delta})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
C>0
and
c>0
such that

c\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}\in E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]}{\Pi_{i=1}^{n-1}h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})}\leq C.

Proof.

For the lower bound, we consider
n-1
disjoint open sets
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}
such that
B(z_{i},r)\cap\Omega\subset U_{i}
and
\partial U_{i}\cap\partial D\subset(x_{2n}x_{1})\cup(B(z_{i},r)\cap\partial\Omega)
. Denote
U_{i}^{\delta}
a discrete approximation of
U_{i}
on
\Omega_{\delta}
(see Figure
4.1
).

Figure 4.1
:
This is an illustration of the discrete neighbour
U_{1}^{\delta}
and
U_{2}^{\delta}
when
n=3
.

Note that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}\in E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]\geq\mathbb{P}[\cap_{i=1}^{n-1}\{\gamma_{i}^{\delta}\subset U_{i}^{\delta}\}].

For
v_{\delta}\in\Omega_{\delta}
, we define

h_{i}^{\delta}(v_{\delta}):=\mathbb{P}_{v_{\delta}}[\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\text{ leaves }U_{i}^{\delta}\text{ in }(x_{2n}^{\delta}x_{1}^{\delta})],

where
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
, as usual, is a random walk killed at
\cup_{i=1}^{n}(x_{2i}^{\delta}x_{2i+1}^{\delta})
, and with reflecting boundaries on
\cup_{i=1}^{n}(x_{2i-1}^{\delta}x_{2i}^{\delta})
. Thus, by Wilson’s algorithm, we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}\in E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]\geq\Pi_{i=1}^{n-1}h_{i}^{\delta}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})

By Lemma
3.5
, we have there exists a constant
c>0
, such that for every
1\leq i\leq n
,

\frac{h_{i}^{\delta}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})}{h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})}\geq c.

The lower bound follows.

The upper bound is much more delicate and uses a careful combinatorial analysis together with choice of ordering in Wilson’s algorithm. Roughly, we want to say that if we were to generate the spanning tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}
with Wilson’s algorithm, we would roughly have
n-1
independent walks which must at least verify the event defining
h_{r}^{\delta}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})
. The trouble is that it is
a priori
possible for a walk starting from
z_{i}^{\delta,+}
to come very close to
z_{j}^{\delta,+}
(closer than distance
r
) for some
j\neq i
. This would prevent us from comparing the probability of
E^{*}
with that of
n-1
independent events.

To deal with this, we will show up to a finite number of combinatorial possibilities, it is always possible to reveal the curves one at a time, in in a certain order such that, each time, the walk we choose to reveal must escape the corresponding ball before touching the wired boundary (which includes the paths already revealed so far, as dictated by Wilson’s algorithm).

More precisely, we must consider events of the following type. Call an index
1\leq i\leq m-1

good
if
\gamma^{\delta}_{i}\cap(\cup_{j}B_{j})=\emptyset
, with
B_{j}=B(z_{j}^{\delta,+},r)
, and call it bad otherwise. Let
I
denote the set of good indices.
We then consider the events

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}\in E^{*},I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\})

(4.5)

where
1\leq m\leq n-1
,
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq n-1
. For each such event that we need to consider, we want to bound its probability by
C\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}h_{r}^{\delta}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})
. To do so we use Wilson’s algorithm with an order that depends specifically on the event we are considering. Namely, in a first stage, we reveal the
good
branches
\gamma^{\delta}_{j}
for
j\in I
, using independent random walks
\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\delta}
from the point
z_{j}^{\delta,+}
for
j\in I
. Clearly, for
E^{*}
to be satisfied, it must be the case that during this first stage, for each
j\in I
,
\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\delta}
escapes
B_{j}
. By independence, this has a probability
\prod_{j\in I}h_{r}^{\delta}(z^{\delta,+}_{j})
. Now in a second stage, given the good branches, let us wire together the target arc
(x_{2n},x_{1})
and the good branches; this forms a new wired boundary in Wilson’s algorithm; see Figure
4.2
.

Figure 4.2
:

An illustration of
\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\gamma_{2}^{\delta},\gamma_{3}^{\delta}
for
n=4
. In this case, only the green curve
\gamma_{1}^{\delta}
is good initially, and so we start by revealing this in Wilson’s algorithm. Once it is revealed and added to the wired boundary, we observe that the blue curve
\gamma_{2}^{\delta}
becomes good, and so we reveal it at this point. Only once this is done, does the curve
\gamma_{3}^{\delta}
become good and can be revealed.

We can now consider, among the remaining bad branches, those which are
good
with respect to this new target. By iterating this procedure (i.e., induction) can reduce the number
n-1
of branches that we need to consider; at each stage of this induction we bound the conditional probability of
E^{*}
by the product over all the good indices of
h_{r}^{\delta}(z^{\delta,+}_{j})
. The only exception is when all the branches are bad.

Thus let us assume that all the branches are bad, i.e., let us explain how to bound the probability in (
4.5
) when
I=\emptyset
. Consider
\gamma_{1}^{\delta}
, which is an oriented path from
z_{1}^{\delta,+}
to the (current) wired boundary. We let
J
be the index
j
of the last (with respect to the chronological order of the path
\gamma_{1}^{\delta}
) ball
B_{j}
such that
\gamma_{1}^{\delta}\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset
, and
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
merges with
\gamma_{1}^{\delta}
in
B_{j}
. If there is no such an index, we define
J:=1
.

Note that by definition of
J
, on the event
\{J=j\}
, for all
i\neq j
,
\gamma_{i}^{\delta}
does not merge with
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
in
B_{i}
: this is because
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
coincides with the path
\gamma_{1}^{\delta}
after the latter hits
B_{j}
, and
J
was defined to be the last (chronologically) index where this property holds. However we mention that it is possible that
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
enters
B_{i}
, however in that case the path
\gamma^{\delta}_{i}
does not merge with
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
there.
Either way, we add the event
\{J=j\}
to (
4.5
), and try to bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{\delta}\in E^{*},I=\emptyset,J=j)
by
first choosing to reveal first
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
in Wilson’s algorithm. Note that on this event, the walk
\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\delta}
must leave
B_{j}
, and so we pick up another term
h_{r}^{\delta}(z^{\delta,+}_{j})
in the conditional probability. We then iterate the argument one more step. (Having revealed
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
, note the following delicate subtlety: although as explained above
\gamma_{j}^{\delta}

may
have entered
B_{i}
for some
i\neq j
, on the event
\{J=j\}
it is nevertheless the case that
\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\delta}
will need to escape
B_{i}
before the touching the wired boundary, and that will bring a factor
h_{r}^{\delta}(z^{\delta,+}_{j})
to the subsequent conditional probability in the later steps of this induction).

Altogether, there is only a finite (combinatorial) number of cases that one needs to consider. In each case, at each step of the inductive argument the conditional probability is bounded by a product of term of the form
h_{r}^{\delta}(z^{\delta,+}_{j})
and the corresponding indices are then removed from the set of unexplored indices. The induction only stops when this set is empty, so in total, in each combinatorial case the overall probability is bounded by
\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}h_{r}^{\delta}(z^{\delta,+}_{j})
. Summing over all the (finite number of) possible combinatorial cases, we obtain the desired upper-bound.
∎

We have the following corollary, which is needed to prove the tightness of
\{\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
.

Corollary 4.4
.

For
1\leq i\leq n-1
, let
(x_{2e_{i}}^{\delta}x_{2e_{i}+1}^{\delta})
be the boundary arc in which
\gamma_{i}^{\delta}
terminates. Then, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}[\text{there exists }i\text{ such that }d(\gamma_{i}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq i,e_{i}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{0}|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]<\epsilon.

Proof.

We decompose over possible combinatorial cases considered in
in Lemma
4.3
and suppose without loss of generality that the corresponding order in which the curves are revealed is
\{\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
(let
A
be this event).
Note that this implies that, for
j<k
,
\gamma^{\delta}_{k}
does not merge with
\gamma^{\delta}_{j}
before leaving
B_{k}
. We will show the following statement by induction: for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
, such that

\mathbb{P}[A;d(\gamma_{i}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq i,e_{i}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{0}|E^{*}]\leq\epsilon_{i}.

for some choice of
\epsilon_{i}
which can be made arbitrarily small. For
i=1
, note that by Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
3.2
, there exists
C>0
and
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[A;d(\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq 1,e_{1}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{0}|E^{*}]\leq C\frac{\mathbb{P}[d(\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq 1,e_{1}}(x_{2j-1}^{\delta}x_{2j}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{0}]}{h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{1}^{\delta,+})}\leq C\epsilon_{0}^{c}.

By choosing
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{0}^{c}
small enough, the statement is proved for
i=1
.
Suppose the statement holds for
i\leq l-1
.
We choose
\epsilon_{l}
which is much smaller than
\epsilon_{l-1}
. Then, for
i=l
, by Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
3.2
, there exist
C>0
and

, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[A;d(\gamma_{l}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq l,e_{l}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{l}|E^{*}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\epsilon_{i}+C\frac{\mathbb{P}[A;E^{*}\cap\{d(\gamma_{l}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq l,e_{l}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{l},d(\gamma_{k}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq k,e_{k}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))>\epsilon_{l-1}\text{ for }k<l\}]}{\Pi_{k=1}^{n-1}h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{l}^{\delta,+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\epsilon_{i}+C\frac{\mathbb{P}[d(\gamma_{l}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq l,e_{l}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))<\epsilon_{l}\,|\,d(\gamma_{k}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq k,e_{k}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))>\epsilon_{l-1}\text{ for }k<l]}{h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{l}^{\delta,+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\epsilon_{i}+C\left(\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{\epsilon_{l-1}}\right)^{c}.

By considering
\epsilon_{l}\ll\epsilon_{l-1}
, we complete the statement for
i=l
. Thus, by induction, we get the result (see Figure

).

Next, we consider the tightness of the branches
\{\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

The sequence of discrete curves
\{\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}\}_{\delta>0}

\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}\}
, almost surely, the limiting curve

The proof is very similar to the proofs of Lemma

\epsilon

\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\gamma_{i}^{\delta})

with

\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\gamma_{i}^{\delta}

and

(which potentially goes via the wired boundary) is not contained in

. We choose
\alpha
such that
\alpha<\frac{1}{C^{n}_{0}}
, where

is a large constant we will determine later. Once again we will generate
\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\gamma_{\delta}^{i}
according to the order given in Lemma

\gamma_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,\gamma_{n-1}^{\delta}
(let

be this event). Recall that we denote by
(x^{\delta}_{2e_{i}}x^{\delta}_{2e_{i}+1})
the arc which is hit by
\gamma^{\delta}_{i}
. Define

E_{i}:=\{d(\gamma_{l}^{\delta},\cup_{j\neq l,e_{l}}(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta}))>\epsilon_{0},\text{ for }1\leq l\leq i-1\}

and set
E=\cap_{i=1}^{n-1}E_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{N}_{i}(r)
denote the

neighbourhood of
\cup_{j<i}\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
. Define also the event

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{i}:=

\displaystyle\{\gamma^{\delta}_{i}\text{ hits }\mathcal{N}_{i}(1/C_{0}^{i}),\text{then exits }\mathcal{N}_{i}(1/C_{0}^{i-1})\text{ and then comes back to $\mathcal{N}_{i}(1/C_{0}^{i})$}\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[A\cap E\cap\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\gamma_{i}^{\delta})|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}[A\cap E\cap\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{i}^{\delta})|E^{*}]+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}[A\cap E_{i}\cap\tilde{E}_{i}|E^{*}].

By applying Lemma
4.3
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[A\cap E\cap\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{i}^{\delta})|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{\mathbb{P}[E_{i}\bigcap(\cap_{j=1}^{i}\{\gamma_{j}^{\delta}\text{ does not connect to }(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta})\})\bigcap\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{i}^{\delta})]}{\Pi_{j=1}^{i}h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{j}^{\delta,+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{\mathbb{P}[\{\gamma_{i}^{\delta}\text{ does not connect to }(x_{2i}^{\delta}x_{2i+1}^{\delta})\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(z_{0},\beta,\alpha;\gamma_{i}^{\delta})\,|\,E_{i}\bigcap(\cap_{j=1}^{i-1}\{\gamma_{j}^{\delta}\text{ does not connect to }(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta})\})]}{h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\epsilon,

(4.6)

where the last inequality is obtained by applying Lemma
3.7
to
\Omega_{\delta}\setminus\cup_{j<i}\gamma_{j}^{\delta}
, and choosing
C_{0}
large enough.

Second, note that if
E_{i}\cap\tilde{E}_{i}\cap E^{*}\left(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+}\right)\neq\emptyset
, we must have that the distance of

and
(x_{2i}^{\delta}x_{2i+1}^{\delta})
is larger than
\epsilon_{0}-2\beta>\epsilon_{0}/2
. Then using once again Lemma
4.3
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[A;E_{i}\cap\tilde{E}_{i}|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{\mathbb{P}[\cap_{j=1}^{i}\{\gamma_{j}^{\delta}\text{ does not connect to }(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta})\}\cap E_{i}\cap\tilde{E}_{i}]}{\Pi_{j=1}^{i}h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{j}^{\delta,+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\times C_{0}^{-c}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\cap_{j=1}^{i-1}\{\gamma_{j}^{\delta}\text{ does not connect to }(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta})\}]}{\Pi_{j=1}^{i-1}h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{j}^{\delta,+})}\times\frac{h^{\delta}_{\epsilon_{0}/2}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})}{h^{\delta}_{r}(z_{i}^{\delta,+})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\epsilon,

(4.7)

where the second inequality is obtained from the discrete Beurling estimate given in Lemma
3.2
and the last inequlity is obtained by choosing large constant

, Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
4.3
.
By Corollary
4.4
, we have

\mathbb{P}[E^{c}|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1}^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]\leq\epsilon.

(4.8)

Combining (

Lemma 4.7
.

We have the following explicit formula:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}[\gamma_{1}^{\delta}[0,\tau_{U}^{\delta}]=\eta_{\delta}]}{\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{\delta}[0,\tilde{\tau}_{U}^{\delta}]=\eta_{\delta}]}=

\displaystyle\frac{1-h_{\Omega_{\delta}}(z_{1}^{\delta,+};(x^{\delta}_{2}x^{\delta}_{3}))}{\det(F(\Omega_{\delta}))}\frac{\det(F(\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta})))}{1-h_{\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta})}(z_{1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta});(x^{\delta}_{2}x^{\delta}_{3}))}.

where the matrix
F(\Omega_{\delta})
is defined by

F(\Omega_{\delta})=I-(h_{\Omega_{\delta}}(z_{i}^{\delta,+};(x_{2j}^{\delta}x_{2j+1}^{\delta})))_{1\leq i,j\leq n-1}.

Proof.

This is a version of Fomin’s determinantal formula (
[
Fom01
]
and see also Proposition 9.6.2 in
[
LL10
]
).
Note that if we write
E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})
for
E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};x_{1},^{\delta},\ldots,x_{2n}^{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})
and likewise with
\tilde{E}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}[\gamma_{1}^{\delta}[0,\tau_{U}^{\delta}]=\eta_{\delta}\,|\,E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]}{\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{\delta}[0,\tilde{\tau}_{U}^{\delta}]=\eta_{\delta}\,|\,\tilde{E}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+})]}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\tilde{E}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+})|}{|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})|}\frac{|E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta});z_{1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta}),\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta}))|}{|\tilde{E}(\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta});z_{1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta}))|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\tilde{E}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+})]}{\mathbb{P}[E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]}\frac{\mathbb{P}[E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta});z_{1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta}),\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta}))]}{\mathbb{P}[\tilde{E}(\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta});z_{1}^{\delta,+}(\eta_{\delta}))]}.

(4.9)

Clearly the numerator of the first fraction and the denominator of the second are given by
1-h(z_{1}^{\delta,+};(x_{2}^{\delta},x_{3}^{\delta}))
in their respective domains
\Omega_{\delta}
and
\Omega_{\delta}(\eta_{\delta})
. The remaining two terms can be written as determinants, as follows.
Recall the definition of
E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})
in (
4.4
). By the inclusion-exclusion formula, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j-1}\sum_{(i_{1,1},\ldots,i_{1,s_{1}}),\ldots,(i_{j,1},\ldots,i_{j,s_{j}})}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{i_{1,1},\ldots,i_{1,s_{1}}}\cap\cdots\cap\mathcal{A}_{i_{j,1},\ldots,i_{j,s_{j}}}],

where the sum is taken over all the non-intersecting set of numbers and we recall that the events
\mathcal{A}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{s}}
are defined in (
4.3
). Note that if we fix a subset of
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, which we denote by
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\}
, then every partition of
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\}
appears in the sum exactly one time. We note that each term in this sum corresponds uniquely to a permutation
\sigma
of the indices
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
: for instance the event
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1,1},\ldots,i_{1,s_{1}}}
specifies that the permutation contains a cycle mapping
i_{1,1}
to
i_{1,2},\ldots,i_{1,s_{1}}
to
i_{1,1}
. Furthermore, the sign
(-1)^{j}
then can be written as
(-1)^{k}\text{sgn}(\sigma)
, where
k=|\text{Supp}(\sigma)|
is the size of the support of
\sigma
.
Thus, if we simply denote by
\sigma
the corresponding partition and denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
the intersection of the corresponding events, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[E^{*}(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n-1}:|\text{Supp}(\sigma)|=k}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}].

Note that on the event
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
, the branches from
z_{r_{j}}^{\delta,+}
for
1\leq j\leq k
are disjoint. This fact comes from our assumptions on
(\Omega;x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n};z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})
that
z_{j}
equals
x_{2j-1}
or
x_{2j}
for
1\leq j\leq n-1
and that the branches are oriented. By
[
Fom01
, Theorem 6.2]
(see also see also Proposition 9.6.2 in
[
LL10
]
), we know that for any edges
\{e^{\delta}_{1},\ldots,e^{\delta}_{k}\}
such that
e^{\delta}_{j}
connects to
(x_{2r_{j}}^{\delta}x_{2r_{j}+1}^{\delta})
for
1\leq j\leq k
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma:\sigma\text{ is a permutation on }\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}[\cap_{j=1}^{k}\{z_{r_{j}}^{\delta,+}\text{ connects to }(x^{\delta}_{2\sigma(r_{j})}x^{\delta}_{2\sigma(r_{j})+1})\text{ in }\Omega_{\delta}\text{ through }e_{\sigma(r_{j})}^{\delta}\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det((h_{\Omega_{\delta}}(z_{r_{j}}^{\delta,+},e^{\delta}_{l}))_{1\leq j,l\leq k}).

By summing over all the possible edges
\{e^{\delta}_{1},\ldots,e^{\delta}_{k}\}
, we deduce that

\sum_{\sigma:\sigma\text{ is a permutation on }\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\}}\text{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}]=\det((h_{\Omega_{\delta}}(z_{r_{j}}^{\delta,+},(x_{2r_{l}}^{\delta}x_{2r_{l}+1}^{\delta})))_{1\leq j,l\leq k}).

Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}[E(\Omega_{\delta};z_{1}^{\delta,+},\ldots,z_{n-1}^{\delta,+})]=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\sum_{1\leq r_{1}<\ldots<r_{k}\leq n-1}\det((h_{\Omega_{\delta}}(z_{r_{j}}^{\delta,+},(x_{2r_{l}}^{\delta}x_{2r_{l}+1}^{\delta})))_{1\leq j,l\leq k}).

The conclusion comes from a simple observation: suppose
A=(A_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n-1}
is a
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix, then we have

\det(I-rA)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-r)^{k}\sum_{1\leq r_{1}<\ldots<r_{k}\leq n-1}\det((A_{r_{j},r_{l}})_{1\leq j,l\leq k}).

(This is well known and easily seen by expanding the left side and considering the coefficient of
r^{j}
for
0\leq j\leq n-1
.) We conclude the proof by setting
r=1
in the above.
∎

Now, we come to the third step. We will first show the convergence of

. To do this, let us recall some basic facts about Schwarz–Christoffel maps.
Recall that
\varphi
is a fixed conformal map from

(4.10)

where the parameter

(the pre-image of the tip of the

-th slit) is uniquely determined by the following equations:

\phi_{j}(y_{2l+2})-\phi_{j}(y_{2l+1})=C_{j}\int_{y_{2l+1}}^{y_{2l+2}}\frac{\Pi_{l=1}^{n-2}(u-\mu_{l}^{(j)})}{\Pi_{j=1}^{2n}(u-y_{j})^{1/2}}du=0,\quad\text{ for }l\neq j-1,j,

(with

the factor on the right hand side of (

)).
We will only need the fact that

is a smooth function of

, by the inverse function theorem.
Note that from the boundary condition, by the same argument as in Lemma
3.3
(i.e., convergence of random walk to Brownian motion with orthogonal reflection along the corresponding arcs), we know that

converges to

uniformly for

. For the same reason, if we let
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\tilde{g}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{U}}(\mathbf{y})
, where
\tilde{g}_{t}
is the Loewner flow associated with
\varphi(\tilde{\gamma}_{1})
in
\mathbb{H}
, then

converges to

uniformly for
