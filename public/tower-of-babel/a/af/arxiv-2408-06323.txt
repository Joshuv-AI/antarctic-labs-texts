Infer-and-widen, or not?
Ronan Perry, Zichun Xu, Olivia McGough, Daniela Witten

Abstract
In recent years, there has been substantial interest in the task of selective inference: inference on a parameter that is selected from the data. Many of the existing proposals fall into what we refer to as the infer-and-widen framework: they produce symmetric confidence intervals whose midpoints do not account for selection and therefore are biased; thus, the intervals must be wide enough to account for this bias. In this paper, we investigate infer-and-widen approaches in three vignettes: the winner’s curse, maximal contrasts, and inference after the lasso. In each of these examples, we show that a state-of-the-art infer-and-widen proposal leads to confidence intervals that are wider than a non-infer-and-widen alternative. Furthermore, even an “oracle” infer-and-widen confidence interval — the narrowest possible interval that could be theoretically attained via infer-and-widen — can be wider than the alternative.

Introduction

In classical statistics, the target of inference — for instance, the null hypothesis that we wish to test, or the parameter for which we wish to construct a confidence interval — is fixed. However, motivated by the explosion of data across a variety of fields, recent interest has focused on the setting where the target of inference is itself a function of the data, and therefore is random.
The terms
selective inference
and
post-selection inference
describe the set of strategies available for conducting inference on a random target.

To formalize this, consider a random variable
Y
with domain
\mathcal{Y}
, and a collection of parameters
\theta
whose elements can be indexed by a (possibly infinite) index set
\mathcal{I}
. For a fixed index
i\in\mathcal{I}
, and for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, a
classical

1-\alpha
confidence interval for
\theta_{i}
, which we will denote
\mathrm{CI}_{i}^{\alpha}
, satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{i}\not\in\mathrm{CI}_{i}^{\alpha}(Y)\right)\leq\alpha.

(1.1)

Now, suppose that
i\in\mathcal{I}
is no longer a fixed index; instead, some selection rule
{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{I}
uses the data to select a parameter on which to conduct inference. A confidence interval provides valid
unconditional coverage

[
berk_valid_2013
]
at level
\alpha
if

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}\not\in\mathrm{CI}_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}^{\alpha}(Y)\right)\leq\alpha.

(1.2)

Unconditional coverage is
not
guaranteed by classical confidence intervals. In this paper, we consider methods that yield unconditional guarantees, in the sense of
1.2
.
Some of these confidence intervals also yield (stronger)
conditional
guarantees
[
fithian_optimal_2017
]
, in the sense that

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}\not\in\mathrm{CI}_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}^{\alpha}(Y)~|~\mathcal{C}(Y)\right)\leq\alpha

(1.3)

for an event
\mathcal{C}(Y)
satisfying
\sigma\left(\mathcal{C}(Y)\right)\supseteq\sigma\left({\mathcal{S}}(Y)\right)
, where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the sigma algebra generated by a random variable, i.e., the coverage guarantee in
1.3
conditions on at least the selection event. By the law of total expectation, conditional coverage,
1.3
, implies unconditional coverage,
1.2
.

1.1
The infer-and-widen framework

As presented in Box
1.1
, the infer-and-widen framework involves (Step 1) defining a target of inference using some or all of the data, and then (Step 2) symmetrically adjusting the critical values arising from “classical” inference to account for the fact that the target of inference is not fixed in order to achieve unconditional coverage, (
1.2
). The simplest examples of this strategy are the Bonferroni
[
bonferroni_teoria_1936
,
dunn_multiple_1961
]
, Holm-Bonferroni
[
holm_simple_1979
]
, and Šidák
[
sidak_rectangular_1967
]
procedures, which are suitable when a finite number of targets of inference are considered (and in the case of Šidák, under additional assumptions). In the special case of inference on contrasts of a linear model, the Scheffé correction is available
[
scheffe_analysis_1959
]
; this idea is further refined by the PoSI method
[
berk_valid_2013
]
. While conceptually attractive, these approaches tend to be quite conservative (Bonferroni, Holm-Bonferroni, Šidák), are limited to a linear model setting (Scheffé, PoSI), or are often computationally intractable (PoSI).

Other recent infer-and-widen approaches have attempted to address these shortcomings. The
local simultaneous inference
approach of
zrnic_locally_2024
focuses a simultaneous correction on the subset of hypotheses that are most likely given the observed data. The
algorithmic stability
proposal of
[
zrnic_post-selection_2023
]
also adapts to the details of the selection algorithm. By drawing connections with the field of differential privacy
[
dwork_book_2014
]
, the “stability” of the approach for selecting the target of inference in Step 1 of Box
1.1
is quantified; this, in turn, leads to a stability-specific adjustment of the critical values in Step 2 of Box
1.1
(see also Section A of the online supplement). In a sense, these two approaches achieve the lofty goal laid out in the PoSI proposal of
[
berk_valid_2013
]
by creating frameworks to compute selection-specific adjustments to the critical values.

Box 1:
Infer-and-widen.

1.

A target of inference is selected from the data.

2.

“Classical” inference is conducted, with a symmetric adjustment of the critical values to account for the data-driven selection of the target of inference.

1.2
Other approaches for valid selective inference

Not all approaches that yield unconditional guarantees of the form (
1.2
) fall into the infer-and-widen framework. Some methods produce
asymmetric
confidence intervals around the biased point estimate: for instance,
benjamini_confidence_2019
and
fuentes_confidence_2018
construct an asymmetric Bonferroni correction that accounts for the direction of the bias. More recently,
zrnic_flexible_2025
extend the local simultaneous approach of
zrnic_locally_2024
by additionally adapting the correction to the specific selection algorithm. The hybrid approaches of
[
andrews_inference_2024
]
and
[
mccloskey_hybrid_2024
]
apportion a fraction of the Type 1 error budget to rule out unlikely selection events, and then construct a conditionally-valid confidence interval on the likely hypotheses; note that this only guarantees unconditional coverage,
1.2
. Lastly, there is a line of empirical Bayes work whose intervals are centered at shrinkage estimators which, while likely biased, improve upon the non-selective estimator
[
hwang_empirical_2013
]
.

Furthermore, a number of methods yield coverage guarantees conditional on the selection event, i.e., (
1.3
). Some methods do this by obtaining independent training and test sets; then, the training set can be used to define the target of inference, and the test set to conduct inference on this target. Because the two sets are independent, we can conduct inference using the test set
as though the target of inference were fixed
, despite the fact that it is actually a function of the training data. Sample splitting, applicable in the setting of independent and identically distributed observations, provides one approach to obtain independent training and test sets
[
cox_note_1975
]
.
More recent
data thinning
proposals generalize the idea of sample splitting, and can be applied when only a single observation is available
[
rasines_splitting_2023
,
neufeld_data_2024
,
dharamshi_generalized_2024
]
.

In cases where it is either impossible or undesirable to create independent training and test sets, strategies that achieve conditional guarantees of the form
1.3
may still be available. For instance, a target of inference can be defined using
all
of the data, and then inference can be conducted
conditional
on the event that the data led to this particular target of inference. Examples include the proposals of
[
fithian_optimal_2017
,
lee_exact_2016
,
chen_selective_2023
,
gao_selective_2022
,
andrews_inference_2024
]
.
Alternatively, if a researcher is willing to define the target of inference using only a subset of the data or using a noisy version of the data, then
data fission

[
leiner_data_2023
]
or
randomized conditional selective inference

[
tian_selective_2018
,
panigrahi_exact_2024
]
may be applicable. Randomization in this manner preserves information for inference, overcoming a limitation of conditioning on selection using all of the data.

1.3
Contributions and organization

In this paper, we conduct an empirical and theoretical evaluation of infer-and-widen strategies in the context of three vignettes. The first two vignettes involve inference after a randomized selection rule, and we derive alternative confidence intervals to compare to an existing infer-and-widen approach. In the third vignette, we compare a suite of existing methods.
The bottom line of our paper is as follows: when comparing infer-and-widen strategies to alternative selective inference methods that make use of
identical
selection events, the latter often outperform the former in terms of confidence interval width. Furthermore, in some settings, the latter even outperform the “oracle” infer-and-widen procedure: the narrowest possible interval that could be theoretically attained within the infer-and-widen framework.

The organization is as follows. In Section
2
, we formalize the infer-and-widen framework. In Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
, we compare its performance to new alternative intervals that we derive in two vignettes considered in
[
zrnic_post-selection_2023
]
, and to existing alternative intervals in an example involving model selection via the lasso. In Sections
3
and
4
, the selection event is randomized, whereas in
Section

5
it is not. In
Section

6
, we show analytically that the infer-and-widen interval can suffer from a highly biased midpoint. We close with a discussion in Section
7
. Theoretical results and additional empirical comparisons are proven in the online supplement. Scripts to fully reproduce all numerical results can be found at
https://github.com/rflperry/infer_and_widen
.

2
Formalizing the infer-and-widen framework

We now formalize the
infer-and-widen
(I&W) framework, outlined in Box
1.1
. This involves a modification of the classical confidence interval
\mathrm{CI}_{i}^{\alpha}
in (
1.1
) to account for the fact that
\theta_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}
is a data-driven parameter. Based on the particulars of the selection function
\mathcal{S}(\cdot):\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
and data
Y
, a modified
\alpha
-level,
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
, is derived so that
\mathrm{CI}^{H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)}_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}(Y)
is unconditionally valid at level
\alpha
in the sense of
1.2
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}\not\in\mathrm{CI}_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}^{H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)}(Y)\right)\leq\alpha.

(2.1)

In effect, this procedure symmetrically widens the classical confidence interval to account for bias in its midpoint.

In a Bonferroni correction we have
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)=\alpha/|\mathcal{I}|
, and in the Holm-Bonferroni correction it is slightly larger. Since these approaches adapt neither to the selection rule
\mathcal{S}(\cdot):\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
nor to the data
Y
, the constant
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
is easy to compute but overly conservative.
Despite the notation
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
used in (
2.1
), the aforementioned infer-and-widen proposals result in confidence intervals whose widths do not depend on the particulars of the selection rule
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
nor data
Y
.

The foundational PoSI proposal
[
berk_valid_2013
]
considers the setting of linear regression with fixed regressors. They derive a constant
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
that enables inference on the coefficient(s) in
any
submodel, regardless of the form of
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
or observed data
Y
. However, for a particular selection rule
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
, the resulting confidence interval may be quite conservative, and computing the constant
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
is challenging or infeasible.

In contrast are two newer approaches, which provide narrower constants
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
that depend on the selection rule
{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot)
. The locally simultaneous inference method of
zrnic_locally_2024
apportions the Type-1 error budget
\alpha
, first to use the data
Y
to rule out the most unlikely hypotheses, and then to perform a simultaneous correction over a
subset
of all
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|
hypotheses.
The algorithmic stability proposal of
[
zrnic_post-selection_2023
]
adjusts the critical value
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}},Y)
based on how “stable” the selection rule
{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot)
is; intuitively, a more “stable” selection rule requires a less substantial adjustment.
Section A in the online supplement
contains a more detailed overview.

We close with a remark about the infer-and-widen framework:
at first glance, it appears that an “advantage” of infer-and-widen, over alternative selective inference approaches, is that the interval in (
2.1
) is of the same form as the classical interval in the absence of selection (
1.1
), i.e., no bespoke methods for computing a confidence interval are required. However, this is a double-edged sword, in the sense that
bias induced by the selection event is perpetuated into the confidence interval
.
Thus, an infer-and-widen confidence interval that attains the nominal coverage may be
extremely
wide, to account for bias in the interval’s midpoint.
We will see that this is a major drawback of infer-and-widen.

Vignette #1: The winner’s curse

Suppose that we observe
n
independent Gaussian random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
with common known variance, and are interested in conducting inference on the unknown mean of the largest observed variable. That is,

Y\sim{\bf{\rm N}}\,_{n}(\mu,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),

(3.1)

and the selection rule is

\mathcal{S}(Y):=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{i\in[n]}Y_{i}.

(3.2)

It is not hard to see that a
1-\alpha
confidence interval for
\mu_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}
that

\mathcal{S}(Y)

[

]
.

To achieve a “stable” algorithm,
[

]

3.2

\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Laplace}}(Y):=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{i\in[n]}(Y_{i}+\zeta_{i}),

(3.3)

where
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ind.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Laplace}\left(c\right)
and
c
is a positive constant that governs the amount of additive noise. When
c=0
,
\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Laplace}}(Y)
simplifies to
\mathcal{S}(Y)
in (
3.2
).

(An infer-and-widen interval based on (

[

]

Under

The bias of infer-and-widen intervals

We now investigate why infer-and-widen intervals are too wide.
To facilitate the analysis, we consider Vignette #1 with a simple selection rule that uses Gaussian noise,

\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y):=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{i\in[n]}(Y_{i}+c\zeta_{i}),\;\;\;\;\zeta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\bf{\rm N}}\,(0,\sigma^{2}).

(6.1)

We know from
Section

2
that any infer-and-widen interval is centered around
Y_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}
.

Proposition 5
(Bounding the bias of the infer-and-widen midpoint).

Under (
3.1
), with
\mu_{1}=\dots=\mu_{n}
, consider the selection rule
\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)
in (
6.1
). Then

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}-\mu_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}\right]\geq\frac{\sigma}{2}(\sqrt{1+c^{2}}-c)\sqrt{\log n}.

That is, the bias increases on the order of
\sqrt{\log n}
. Note that as
c\to\infty
, i.e., as the selection rule becomes fully random, this lower bound goes to zero as expected.

Noting that
\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)
in (
6.1
) is independent of
Y-\frac{1}{c}\zeta

[
neufeld_data_2024
]
, it is straightforward to show that
[(Y-\frac{1}{c}\zeta)_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}\pm\sigma(1+c^{-2})^{1/2}z_{1-\alpha/2}]
is a valid
1-\alpha
conditional confidence interval for
\mu_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}
in the sense of
1.3
. Furthermore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Y-\frac{1}{c}\zeta\right)_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}-\mu_{\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{Gaussian}}(Y)}\right]=0,

i.e., the midpoint is unbiased for the mean of the selected observation.

Thus, infer-and-widen here leads to a confidence interval that is centered in the wrong place, but a simple alternative that makes use of the same selection event yields an interval that is correctly centered.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we compared the performance of the “infer-and-widen” approach to simple alternative methods in three vignettes: two taken from
[
zrnic_post-selection_2023
]
, and the third involving feature selection via the lasso. Our findings are as follows:

1.

In Vignette #1, the “oracle” interval was wider than the alternative approaches we consider; thus, there may be limited value in developing narrower infer-and-widen methods that can be computed in practice.

2.

In Vignettes #2 and #3, the “oracle” interval is the narrowest interval, but infer-and-widen intervals that can be computed in practice are often much wider.

One may wonder whether our findings conflict with those of
goeman_selection_2024
, who proved that any method that guarantees conditional coverage, (
1.3
), is dominated by an unconditional multiple testing procedure defined on the full universe of hypotheses. However, their setting is quite different from ours: they consider inference on non-selected parameters and unconditional procedures outside of the infer-and-widen framework.

Appendix A
A summary of algorithmic stability

The notion of
stability
underlying the algorithmic stability proposal of
zrnic_post-selection_2023
is as follows.

Definition A.1

(Definitions 4.1 and 4.2,
zrnic_post-selection_2023
)
.

Two random variables
Q
and
T
are

(\eta,\tau)
-indistinguishable
from each other, denoted by
Q\approx_{(\eta,\tau)}T
, if for any measurable set
\mathcal{O}
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}(Q\in\mathcal{O})\leq e^{\eta}\mathbb{P}(T\in\mathcal{O})+\tau.

(A.1)

A (possibly randomized) selection rule
{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\mathcal{I}
is
(\eta,\tau,\nu)
-stable
with respect to a distribution
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{Y}
if there exists a (possibly random, possibly dependent on
\mathcal{P}
) variable
{S}_{0}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(Y)\approx_{(\eta,\tau)}{S}_{0}\right)\geq 1-\nu.

(A.2)

Note that the probability is taken solely with respect to
Y\sim\mathcal{P}
.

Since
S_{0}
is independent of
Y
, we can use the classical interval (1.1) to conduct inference on a target given by
S_{0}
, i.e., it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{S_{0}}\not\in\mathrm{CI}_{S_{0}}^{\alpha}(Y)\right)\leq\alpha
.
The key insight underlying algorithmic stability is that if
\mathcal{S}(Y)
is stable, then for most
Y\sim\mathcal{P}
the value of
\mathcal{S}(Y)
is close (in the sense of
Definition

A.1
) to
S_{0}
. Thus, it is “almost as if” the selection rule
\mathcal{S}(Y)
did not involve
Y
. This notion is formalized in
Theorem

A.2
.

Theorem A.2

(Theorem 5.2,
zrnic_post-selection_2023
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
be a
(\eta,\tau,\nu)
-stable selection rule with parameters possibly dependent on the data distribution
\mathcal{P}
of
Y
.
Assume that for any fixed
\theta_{i}\in\Theta
, we have access to a confidence interval
\mathrm{CI}_{i}^{\alpha}(Y)
for
\theta_{i}
with coverage at least
1-\alpha
, i.e., it satisfies (1.1). Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}\not\in\mathrm{CI}_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}^{(\alpha-\tau-\nu)e^{-\eta}}(Y)\right)\leq\alpha.

(A.3)

Thus, to conduct inference on a data-driven target
\theta_{\mathcal{S}(Y)}
, we can apply the infer-and-widen interval in (2.1) with
H(\alpha,{\mathcal{S}})=(\alpha-\tau-\nu)e^{-\eta}
, a function of the stability of the selection rule
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
.
The values of
\eta,\tau,
and
\nu
that govern the stability of
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
directly determine the width of the confidence interval: the smaller these parameters, the narrower the confidence interval.
zrnic_post-selection_2023
construct stable selection rules in a number of settings via the introduction of auxiliary randomness.

and

Lemma B.1

(Data fission with Laplace noise)
.

Let
Y\sim{\bf{\rm N}}\,_{n}(\mu,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
\zeta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\bf{\rm Laplace}}\,(c)

Y^{\mathrm{train}}:=Y+\zeta
,
\delta:={\rm sign}(Y^{\mathrm{train}}-Y)
, and the set
A:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\delta_{i}y_{i}\leq\delta_{i}Y^{\mathrm{train}}_{i}\;\forall i\in[n]\right\}
.
Then the density of the conditional distribution
Y_{i}\mid(Y^{\mathrm{train}},\delta)
has the following form:

Y\mid(Y^{\mathrm{train}},\delta)\sim{\bf{\rm N}}\,_{n,A}(\mu+c^{-1}\sigma^{2}\delta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),

(B.1)

where
{\bf{\rm N}}\,_{n,A}
denotes an
n
-dimensional multivariate normal distribution truncated to the set
A
.

Proof.

The conditional density is as follows:

\displaystyle f_{Y|(Y^{\mathrm{train}},\delta)}(Y)

\displaystyle\propto f_{\delta|Y,Y^{\mathrm{train}}}(\delta)f_{Y^{\mathrm{train}}|Y}(Y^{\mathrm{train}})f_{Y}(Y)

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{I}[Y\in A]f_{Y^{\mathrm{train}}-Y|Y}(Y^{\mathrm{train}}-Y)f_{Y}(Y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[Y\in A]f_{\zeta}(Y^{\mathrm{train}}-Y)f_{Y}(Y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[Y\in A]\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\zeta_{i}}(Y^{\mathrm{train}}_{i}-Y_{i})f_{Y}(Y)

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{I}[Y\in A]\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(Y-\mu)^{\top}(Y-\mu)-c^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y^{\mathrm{train}}_{i}-Y_{i}|\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[Y\in A]\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(Y-\mu)^{\top}(Y-\mu)+c^{-1}\delta^{\top}(Y-Y^{\mathrm{train}})\right\}

\displaystyle\propto\mathbb{I}[Y\in A]\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(Y-\mu-c^{-1}\sigma^{2}\delta)^{\top}(Y-\mu-c^{-1}\sigma^{2}\delta)\right\}.

(B.2)

Thus the density of
Y|(Y^{\mathrm{train}},\delta)
is that of multivariate normal distribution with independent marginals, truncated to
A

Y^{\mathrm{train}}
.
∎

\mu_{i}
