1.
Introduction

1.1.
The equation

Let
H
be a (separable) real Hilbert space, endowed with the scalar product and norm

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,
and
\|\cdot\|
, respectively, and let

A:{\rm{dom}}(A)\subset H\to H

be a strictly positive selfadjoint linear operator.
We define the product Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}={\rm{dom}}(A^{\frac{1}{2}})\times H,

endowed with the standard Euclidean product norm

\|(u,v)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}.

Denoting by
\sigma(A)\subset(0,\infty)
the spectrum of
A
, let

f:\sigma(A)\to(0,\infty)

be a continuous function subject to the structural assumptions

(1.1)

\inf_{s\in\sigma(A)}f(s)>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{s\in\sigma(A)}\frac{f(s)}{s}<\infty.

For
t>0
, we consider the abstract damped wave equation in the unknown
u=u(t)

(1.2)

\ddot{u}+2f(A)\dot{u}+Au=0,

the
dot
standing for derivative with respect to time,
subject to the initial conditions

u(0)=u_{0}\quad\text{and}\qquad\dot{u}(0)=v_{0},

where the vector
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{H}
is arbitrarily assigned.
Here,
f(A)
is the selfadjoint linear operator on
H

constructed via the functional calculus of
A
,
namely,

f(A)=\int_{\sigma(A)}f(s)\,dE_{A}(s),

being
E_{A}
the spectral measure of
A
(see, e.g.,
[
20
]
).
Calling
\boldsymbol{u}=(u,v)
, and introducing the linear operator

{\mathbb{A}}(u,v)=(v,-Au-f(A)v),

with domain

{\rm{dom}}({\mathbb{A}})=\big\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{H}:\,{\mathbb{A}}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{H}\big\},

we can rewrite (
1.2
)
as the first order ODE in
\mathcal{H}

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}={\mathbb{A}}\boldsymbol{u}.

The linear operator
{\mathbb{A}}
is known to be the infinitesimal generator
of a
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
-semigroup

S(t)=e^{t{\mathbb{A}}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}

of linear contractions (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).
Hence, for any given
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{H}
, the unique mild solution

\boldsymbol{u}(t)
to the equation above, subject to the initial
condition
\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
is given by

\boldsymbol{u}(t)=(u(t),\dot{u}(t))=S(t)\boldsymbol{u}_{0}.

We denote (twice) the energy of such a solution by

{\mathsf{E}}(t)=\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}u(t)\|^{2}+\|\dot{u}(t)\|^{2}.

1.2.
Exponential decay of the semigroup

As far as the sole existence of
S(t)
is concerned,
we could have taken

any
continuous function
f:\sigma(A)\to[0,\infty)
. However,
as shown for instance in
[
8
]
,
the exponential stability of the semigroup occurs if and only if (
1.1
) is in place.
Recall that
S(t)
is said to be
exponentially stable
if
there exist
\omega>0
and
C\geq 1
such that

(1.3)

\|S(t)\|_{L(\mathcal{H})}\leq Ce^{-\omega t},

where the norm is taken in the Banach space
L(\mathcal{H})
of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Once the exponential stability is known,
one would like to find the best possible
\omega>0

(i.e., the largest) for which (
1.3
) holds. This number is the (exponential)
decay rate
, defined
as
1
1

1

The number
-\omega_{*}
is usually called the
growth bound
of the semigroup.

\omega_{*}=\sup\big\{\omega>0:\,\|S(t)\|_{L(\mathcal{H})}\leq Ce^{-\omega t}\big\},

for some
C=C(\omega)\geq 1
.
However, for general (exponentially stable) semigroups, computing
\omega_{*}
might not be an easy task.
Another relevant quantity, usually much easier to detect, is
the
spectral bound
of
{\mathbb{A}}

\sigma_{*}=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma({\mathbb{A}})}{\mathfrak{Re}}\,\lambda,

\sigma({\mathbb{A}})
being the spectrum of (the complexification of)
{\mathbb{A}}
,
which is related to
\omega_{*}
through
the (possibly strict) inequality
\omega_{*}\leq-\sigma_{*}
(see
[
9
,
19
]
).

Definition 1.1
.

The semigroup
S(t)
satisfies the
spectrum determined growth
(SDG) condition
if
\omega_{*}=-\sigma_{*}
.

In view of finding the decay rate, it is then
of paramount importance to establish whether or not the SDG condition is satisfied.
This is true, for instance, for eventually norm continuous (such as analytic or differentiable)
semigroups
[
9
]
. Still, even if
S(t)
fulfills the SDG condition, this does not mean at all that (
1.3
) holds for

\omega=\omega_{*}
, as the following simple example shows.

Example 1.2
.

Consider the damped pendulum equation

\ddot{u}+2a\dot{u}+u=0,

a particular instance of (
1.2
) for
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
A=1

and
f\equiv a>0
.
The related semigroup, being norm continuous, always fulfills the SDG condition. In particular,
the decay rate is attained whenever
a\neq 1
. But, when
a=1
,
the norm of the solution corresponding to the initial datum
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0})
reads

\|S(t)\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}+v_{0}^{2}+2(u_{0}^{2}-v_{0}^{2})t+2(u_{0}+v_{0})^{2}t^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}}.

The decay rate, equal to
\frac{1}{2}
in this case, is certainly not attained, and the reason is that
a resonance phenomenon is encountered.

This motivates the next definition.

Definition 1.3
.

The semigroup
S(t)
satisfies the
strong spectrum determined growth
(SSDG) condition
if the decay rate
\omega_{*}=-\sigma_{*}
is attained, that is, if (
1.3
) holds with

\omega=\omega_{*}=-\sigma_{*}
.

1.3.
Earlier contributions

The equation (
1.2
) of this paper belongs to a general class of abstract differential
models introduced in
[
2
]
, and “exhibiting the empirically observed damping rates in elastic systems”.
For this reason,
A
is usually called the
elastic operator
,
while
f(A)
is the
damping operator
.
The prototypical case occurs when
f(s)
controls
and is controlled by
s^{\theta}
for some
\theta\in[0,1]
,
namely, when
f(A)
is comparable with the powers
A^{\theta}
. Here,
the semigroup is know to be analytic for

\theta\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
and differentiable (actually, of Gevrey type) for
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
See, among others, the classical papers
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Further works,
dealing with a damping operator not necessarily comparable
with
A^{\theta}
, include
[
1
,
12
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Nevertheless, the literature concerning
the analysis of the decay rate
\omega_{*}
of
S(t)
, with particular reference
to whether or not it is actually attained,
is exceptionally bare.
The full solution to the problem has been found
only for the weakly damped wave equation,
sometimes referred to as the
telegrapher’s equation
,
corresponding to the choice
f(s)=a>0
(see
[
6
,
10
]
).
The much more general (and difficult) situation of a nonconstant
f
has been
tackled in
[
11
]
. There, within a certain number
of assumptions, which prevent
f(s)
to grow at infinity
faster than
s^{\theta}
with
\theta<\frac{1}{2}
, the authors obtain sharp exponential decay estimates
for trajectories originating from sufficiently regular initial data, but they cannot generally
exhibit the decay rate of the semigroup.
On the other hand, the necessary
and sufficient condition (
1.1
) for the exponential stability of
S(t)
clearly
allows
f(s)
to have up to a linear growth
at infinity. And indeed, the special case
f(s)=s
corresponds to the widely
studied strongly damped wave equation,
also known by the name of
Kelvin-Voigt equation
, which appears in several areas of Mathematical Physics.

1.4.
Our result

The aim of this paper is to provide a complete answer.
Within the sole
condition (
1.1
), we show that the semigroup
S(t)

fulfills (
1.3
) with

\omega=\omega_{*}=-\sigma_{*}
, except in some particular cases, called resonant,
where the term
e^{-\omega_{*}t}
is penalized by a factor
(1+t)
.
This result is optimal. The key idea of the proof
is to decompose
\sigma(A)

into the disjoint union of suitably chosen subsets
\sigma_{\imath}
, and then perform energy estimates
with
ad hoc
multipliers
on each of the (orthogonal) subspaces
E_{A}(\sigma_{\imath})H
,
making use of the functional calculus of
A
.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next Section
2
, we introduce
two objects that will play a crucial role in our analysis.
In Section
3
, we recall the properties of the spectrum of the operator
{\mathbb{A}}
.
The main result is stated and proved in Section
4
, and an application is then discussed
in Section
5
.
The final Sections
6
-
9
are devoted to the proofs of four lemmas
encountered in the proof of the main theorem.

A word of warning

Although we work with a real Hilbert space
H
, having in mind the concrete examples of
Mathematical Physics,
all the results of this paper hold
verbatim
if
H
is a complex Hilbert space.
The proofs are exactly the same, up to replacing in the calculations any occurrence of a scalar product
with its real part.

2.
The Function
{\phi}
and the Number
{m_{*}}

A crucial object for our analysis is the continuous function
\phi:\sigma(A)\to(0,\infty)

defined as

\phi(s)=\begin{cases}f(s)&\quad\text{if }f(s)\leq\sqrt{s}\,,\\
\vskip 4.2679pt\cr f(s)-\sqrt{f^{2}(s)-s}&\quad\text{if }f(s)>\sqrt{s}\,,\end{cases}

along with the number

m_{*}=\inf_{s\in\sigma(A)}\phi(s)>0.

The fact that
m_{*}>0
merely follows by observing that

f(s)=\begin{cases}\phi(s)&\quad\text{if }f(s)\leq\sqrt{s}\,,\\
\vskip 1.42262pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{s}{2\phi(s)}+\frac{\phi(s)}{2}&\quad\text{if }f(s)>\sqrt{s}\,.\end{cases}

Hence,
m_{*}=0
would violate (
1.1
).
Then, in the region
f(s)>\sqrt{s}\,
, we have that

(2.1)

\frac{f(s)}{s}\leq\frac{1}{2m_{*}}+\frac{m_{*}}{2s},

this fact being equivalent to
\phi(s)\geq m_{*}
.
Finally, for every
m>0
and every sequence
s_{n}\to\infty
, we have the implication

(2.2)

\frac{f(s_{n})}{s_{n}}\to\frac{1}{2m}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\phi(s_{n})\to m.

Indeed, the first convergence implies in particular that

\frac{s_{n}}{f^{2}(s_{n})}\to 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(s_{n})>\sqrt{s_{n}}\,,

the latter for all
n
large. Accordingly,

\phi(s_{n})=f(s_{n})\Big(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{s_{n}}{f^{2}(s_{n})}}\,\Big)\sim\frac{s_{n}}{2f(s_{n})}\to m.

3.
The Spectrum of the Infinitesimal Generator

For every fixed
s\in\sigma(A)
, we introduce the pair of complex numbers

\lambda_{s}^{\pm}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-f(s)\pm i\sqrt{s-f^{2}(s)}&\quad\text{if }f(s)\leq\sqrt{s}\,,\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr\displaystyle-f(s)\pm\sqrt{f^{2}(s)-s}&\quad\text{if }f(s)>\sqrt{s}\,,\end{cases}

which are nothing but the solutions to the second order equation

\lambda^{2}+2f(s)\lambda+s=0.

We define

\Sigma=\bigcup_{s\in\sigma(A)}\big\{\lambda_{s}^{\pm}\big\}.

We also consider the (possibly empty) set

\Lambda=\Big\{\lambda<0:\,\exists\,s_{n}\in\sigma(A)\,\,\,\text{such that}\,\,\,s_{n}\to\infty\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(s_{n})}{s_{n}}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Big\}.

Then, as shown in
[
8
]
, the spectrum of (the complexification of) the operator
{\mathbb{A}}

reads

\sigma({\mathbb{A}})=\Sigma\cup\Lambda,

where the union is not necessarily disjoint.
In light of (
2.2
), it is apparent that
\Lambda
belongs to the closure of
\Sigma
.
This yields the equality

(3.1)

\sigma_{*}=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma({\mathbb{A}})}{\mathfrak{Re}}\,\lambda=\sup_{\lambda\in\Sigma}{\mathfrak{Re}}\,\lambda=-\inf_{s\in\sigma(A)}\phi(s)=-m_{*}.

Remark 3.1
.

Concerning the structure of the spectrum of
{\mathbb{A}}
, we have that

\sigma({\mathbb{A}})=\sigma_{\rm p}({\mathbb{A}})\cup\sigma_{\rm c}({\mathbb{A}}),

where

\sigma_{\rm p}({\mathbb{A}})=\bigcup_{s\in\sigma_{\rm p}(A)}\big\{\lambda_{s}^{\pm}\big\}.

With standard notation,
\sigma_{\rm p}
and
\sigma_{\rm c}

are the point spectrum and the continuous spectrum, respectively.
This characterization, although not explicitly stated in
[
8
]
, can be
inferred from the calculations of that paper, by arguing as in
[
7
, Sec. 5]
.

4.
The Theorem

4.1.
Statement of the result

We begin with the rigorous definition of resonance.

Definition 4.1
.

The semigroup
S(t)
is said to be
resonant
if
there exists
s_{*}\in\sigma(A)
such that

m_{*}=\phi(s_{*})\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(s_{*})=\sqrt{s_{*}}\,.

In other words, the resonance phenomenon appears whenever
the real part of the spectrum of
{\mathbb{A}}
attains its supremum
-m_{*}
and the equality

-m_{*}=\lambda^{+}_{s_{*}}=\lambda^{-}_{s_{*}}

holds for some
s_{*}\in\sigma(A)
. In fact, if such a point
s_{*}
exists, it is easily seen to be unique.

Theorem 4.2
.

There exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that

I.

\|S(t)\|_{L(\mathcal{H})}\leq Ce^{-m_{*}t},\,\,\,
if
S(t)
not resonant; and

II.

\|S(t)\|_{L(\mathcal{H})}\leq C(1+t)e^{-m_{*}t},\,\,\,
if
S(t)
resonant.

On account of (
3.1
), the theorem produces a straightforward consequence.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
S(t)
is not resonant, then it fulfills the
SSDG
condition. Instead,
if
S(t)
is resonant, it fulfills the
SDG
condition, but not the
SSDG
one.

Remark 4.4
.

In view of Example
1.2
, the result is optimal.

It is sometimes possible to provide a precise estimate of the constant
C
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume that the semigroup is subdamped, that is,

\sup_{s\in\sigma(A)}\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}=\ell<1.

Then point
I
of Theorem
4.2
holds with

C=\sqrt{\frac{1+\ell}{1-\ell}}.

Also in this case, the result is optimal, as the following example shows.

Example 4.6
.

Consider the subdamped pendulum equation

\ddot{u}+2a\dot{u}+u=0,\quad a\in(0,1),

which meets the hypotheses of the proposition above with
\ell=a
.
Taking the unit vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
,
the norm of the solution at time
t
reads

\|S(t)\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-a\cos\big(2\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\,t\big)}{1-a}}\,e^{-\frac{a}{2}t}.

Choosing

t=t_{k}=\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\,},

for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we get exactly

\|S(t_{k})\|_{L(\mathcal{H})}\geq\|S(t_{k})\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+a}{1-a}}\,e^{-\frac{a}{2}{t_{k}}}.

4.2.
Some preparatory lemmas

For any given
K\geq 2
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we decompose the spectrum of
A
into the disjoint union

\sigma(A)=\sigma_{0}\cup\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}\cup\sigma_{3},

where the four regions
\sigma_{\imath}
(some of which possibly empty) are defined as

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=\Big\{s\in\sigma(A):\,\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}>K\Big\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\Big\{s\in\sigma(A):\,\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\leq 1-\varepsilon\Big\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\{s\in\sigma(A):\,1+\varepsilon\leq\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\leq K\Big\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=\Big\{s\in\sigma(A):\,1-\varepsilon<\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}<1+\varepsilon\Big\}.

The proofs of Theorem
4.2
and the subsequent Proposition
4.5

are based on the following four lemmas,
which hold for all regular initial data, that is,
for all
(u_{0},v_{0})
in the domain of
{\mathbb{A}}
.
Given the trajectory

(u(t),\dot{u}(t))=S(t)(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\rm{dom}}({\mathbb{A}}),

we split the corresponding energy at time
t\geq 0
into the sum

{\mathsf{E}}(t)=\sum_{\imath=0}^{3}{\mathsf{E}}_{\imath}(t),

where

{\mathsf{E}}_{\imath}(t)=\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}E_{A}(\sigma_{\imath})u\|^{2}+\|E_{A}(\sigma_{\imath})\dot{u}\|^{2}.

Note that
{\mathsf{E}}_{\imath}\equiv 0
if
\sigma_{\imath}=\emptyset
.
The constants
K
and
\varepsilon
in the
statements of the lemmas are understood to be the ones
appearing in the definitions of
\sigma_{\imath}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For every
K\geq 2
large enough we have the inequality

{\mathsf{E}}_{0}(t)\leq 3{\mathsf{E}}_{0}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have the inequality

{\mathsf{E}}_{1}(t)\leq\frac{2-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{E}}_{1}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

Lemma 4.9
.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and every
K\geq 2
,
we have the inequality

{\mathsf{E}}_{2}(t)\leq\frac{9K^{2}}{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

Lemma 4.10
.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
such that
\sigma_{3}\neq\emptyset
,
we have the inequality

{\mathsf{E}}_{3}(t)\leq\frac{8}{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{E}}_{3}(0)e^{-2m_{3}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)t},

where
m_{3}=\inf_{s\in\sigma_{3}}\phi(s)
.

The rather technical proofs of the four lemmas, heavily based on the functional calculus of
A
, will be
carried out in the final Sections
6
,
7
,
8
and
9
of the paper.

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
4.2

We consider the two cases separately.

I.

Let us choose
K\geq 2
sufficiently large in order for Lemma
4.7
to hold.
We claim that, up to fixing
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
small enough,

{\mathsf{E}}_{3}(t)\leq\frac{8}{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{E}}_{3}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

This is trivially true if

\sigma_{3}=\emptyset
for some
\varepsilon
.
Let us then assume that
\sigma_{3}\neq\emptyset
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
.
We preliminarily observe that, for all
\varepsilon
small,

m_{3}>m_{*}.

If not, there would exist a sequence
s_{n}\in\sigma(A)
such that

\frac{f(s_{n})}{\sqrt{s_{n}}}\to 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(s_{n})\to m_{*}=m_{3}.

If
s_{n}
is unbounded, then (up to a subsequence)
s_{n}\to\infty
. Therefore,

m_{*}\sim\phi(s_{n})\sim f(s_{n})\sim\sqrt{s_{n}}\,\to\infty,

a contradiction. This tells that
s_{n}
must be bounded. But
then (up to a subsequence)
s_{n}\to s_{*}\in\sigma(A)
, as
\sigma(A)
is closed.
By continuity, we deduce that

f(s_{*})=\sqrt{s_{*}}
and
\phi(s_{*})=m_{*}
, against the assumption that
S(t)
is not resonant. At this point,
the claim follows by fixing
\varepsilon
in Lemma
4.10

small enough that
m_{3}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\geq m_{*}

(just note that
m_{3}
is a decreasing function of
\varepsilon
).
Once the claim is proven, setting

M=\frac{9K^{2}}{\varepsilon},

and taking advantage of Lemmas
4.7
-
4.9
, we conclude
that

{\mathsf{E}}(t)=\sum_{\imath=0}^{3}{\mathsf{E}}_{\imath}(t)\leq M{\mathsf{E}}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

Due to the continuity of the semigroup,
the latter inequality remains valid by density for all initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})
belonging
to the phase space
\mathcal{H}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

II.
In this case, we have the equality
m_{3}=m_{*}
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
.
Select then any
K\geq 2
sufficiently large in order for Lemma
4.7
to hold. From
Lemmas
4.7
-
4.10
we learn that the inequality

{\mathsf{E}}(t)\leq\frac{9K^{2}}{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{E}}(0)e^{-2m_{*}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)t}

holds for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
.
Fixing an arbitrary
\varepsilon_{*}\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
,
we choose for every
t\geq 0

\varepsilon=\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\varepsilon_{*}}{(1+t)^{2}}.

Then, calling

M=\frac{9K^{2}}{\varepsilon_{*}}e^{8m_{*}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{*}}\,},

we finally obtain

{\mathsf{E}}(t)\leq M(1+t)^{2}{\mathsf{E}}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

As before, the sought inequality is valid by density for all
initial data.
∎

4.4.
Proof of Proposition
4.5

If the semigroup is subdamped, then
\sigma(A)=\sigma_{1}
with
\varepsilon=1-\ell
.
Accordingly, the energy
{\mathsf{E}}
reduces to
{\mathsf{E}}_{1}
, and point
I
of Theorem
4.2

is nothing but Lemma
4.8
.
∎

5.
An Application: Wave Equations with Fractional Damping

For
a>0
and
\theta\in[0,1]
, we consider equation (
1.2
) with

f(s)=as^{\theta}
,
namely,

\ddot{u}+2aA^{\theta}\dot{u}+Au=0.

The function
f
clearly complies with (
1.1
), so that Theorem
4.2

and Proposition
4.5
apply.
We denote by
s_{0}>0
the minimum
of the spectrum of
A
.

Remark 5.1
.

Given a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, a concrete instance of this model is obtained by taking

H=L^{2}(\Omega)
and the Laplace-Dirichlet operator
A=-\Delta
with domain

{\rm{dom}}(-\Delta)=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

In particular, for
\theta=0
we have the weakly damped wave equation,
whereas for
\theta=1
we have the strongly damped wave equation.
Another physically relevant model is the beam (for
N=1

In this case, the operator

For this choice of

\phi(s)=\begin{cases}as^{\theta}&\quad\text{if }s\geq a^{\frac{2}{1-2\theta}},\\

\vskip 4.2679pt\cr as^{\theta}-\sqrt{a^{2}s^{2\theta}-s}&\quad\text{if }s>a^{\frac{2}{1-2\theta}},\end{cases}\qquad\text{if }\theta>\frac{1}{2},

whereas

\phi(s)=\begin{cases}a\sqrt{s}\,&\quad\text{if }a\leq 1,\\
\vskip 4.2679pt\cr\big(a-\sqrt{a^{2}-1}\,\big)\sqrt{s}\,&\quad\text{if }a>1,\end{cases}\qquad\text{if }\theta=\frac{1}{2}.

Note that

In general, the picture strongly depends on the exponent

\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\,=as^{\theta-\frac{1}{2}}

.

is never subdamped.

.

\boldsymbol{\theta\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)}

-

where

6.
Proof of Lemma
4.7

We choose
K
sufficiently large in order to have the set inclusion

(6.1)

\sigma_{0}\subset(K,\infty).

Indeed, recalling the second constraint in (
1.1
), if
s\leq K
with
K
large then

\frac{f(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\leq\sup_{s\in\sigma(A)}\frac{f(s)}{s}\,\sqrt{K}\,\leq K.

Defining the function

g(s)=f(s)-\frac{m_{*}}{2},

we rewrite equation (
1.2
) in the equivalent form

\ddot{u}+2g(A)\dot{u}+m_{*}\dot{u}+Au=0.

Note that, due to (
2.1
),

(6.2)

s\geq 2m_{*}g(s),\quad\forall s\in\sigma_{0}.

Next, setting
w=E_{A}(\sigma_{0})u
, we introduce the functional

{\mathsf{F}}_{0}={\mathsf{E}}_{0}+2m_{*}^{2}\|w\|^{2}+4m_{*}\langle w,\dot{w}\rangle.

In what follows, as well as in the proofs of the remaining three lemmas, all the calculations are rigorous,
since we are working with regular solutions. In particular, we use the fact that the selfadjoint projection

E_{A}(\sigma_{0})
commutes with all the functions of
A
.
Multiplying the equation by the test function

2\dot{w}+4m_{*}w
, we are led to the differential identity

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{0}+2m_{*}{\mathsf{F}}_{0}+2{\mathsf{G}}_{0}=0,

where

{\mathsf{G}}_{0}=m_{*}\big[\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}-\|(2m_{*}g)^{\frac{1}{2}}(A)w\|^{2}\big]+2\big[\|g^{\frac{1}{2}}(A)(m_{*}w+\dot{w})\|^{2}-m_{*}\|m_{*}w+\dot{w}\|^{2}\big].

In light of (
6.2
), we see at once that

\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}-\|(2m_{*}g)^{\frac{1}{2}}(A)w\|^{2}\geq 0.

Besides, from the very definition of
g
and
\sigma_{0}
,

g(s)\geq K\sqrt{s}-\frac{m_{*}}{2},\quad\forall s\in\sigma_{0}.

Thus, we infer from (
6.1
) that
g(s)\geq m_{*}
provided that
K
is large enough.
Accordingly,

\|g^{\frac{1}{2}}(A)(m_{*}w+\dot{w})\|^{2}-m_{*}\|m_{*}w+\dot{w}\|^{2}\geq 0.

In summary, we have proved that

{\mathsf{G}}_{0}\geq 0
. Therefore,

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{0}+2m_{*}{\mathsf{F}}_{0}\leq 0,

and by the Gronwall Lemma we conclude that

{\mathsf{F}}_{0}(t)\leq{\mathsf{F}}_{0}(0)e^{-2m_{*}t}.

The proof is finished once we show the double inequality

\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{E}}_{0}\leq{\mathsf{F}}_{0}\leq\frac{3}{2}{\mathsf{E}}_{0}.

Indeed, from the Cauchy-Schwarz and the Young inequalities,
and exploiting (
6.1
),

2m_{*}^{2}\|w\|^{2}+4m_{*}|\langle w,\dot{w}\rangle|\leq 10m_{*}^{2}\|w\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{w}\|^{2}\leq\frac{10m_{*}^{2}}{K}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{w}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{E}}_{0},

as soon as
K
is sufficiently large.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.8

Setting
w=E_{A}(\sigma_{1})u
, we introduce the functional

{\mathsf{F}}_{1}={\mathsf{E}}_{1}+2\langle f(A)w,\dot{w}\rangle.

Multiplying (
1.2
) by the test function

2\dot{w}+2f(A)w
,
we get the differential identity

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{1}+2m_{*}{\mathsf{F}}_{1}+2{\mathsf{G}}_{1}=0,

having set (recall that in this region
s-f^{2}(s)>0
)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{G}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\|f^{\frac{1}{2}}(A)(A-f^{2}(A))^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}-m_{*}\|(A-f^{2}(A))^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|f^{\frac{1}{2}}(A)(f(A)w+\dot{w})\|^{2}-m_{*}\|f(A)w+\dot{w}\|^{2}.

Since

m_{*}\leq\phi(s)=f(s),\quad\forall s\in\sigma_{1},

it is apparent that
{\mathsf{G}}_{1}\geq 0
.
Therefore, we arrive at

(7.1)

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{1}+2m_{*}{\mathsf{F}}_{1}\leq 0.

Finally, we claim that

(7.2)

\varepsilon{\mathsf{E}}_{1}\leq{\mathsf{F}}_{1}\leq(2-\varepsilon){\mathsf{E}}_{1}.

Indeed, recalling that
f(s)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{s}
for every
s\in\sigma_{1}
, from the Cauchy-Schwarz and the Young inequalities,
we get

2|\langle f(A)w,\dot{w}\rangle|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}+(1-\varepsilon)\|\dot{w}\|^{2}.

Collecting (
7.1
)-(
7.2
), and exploiting the Gronwall
Lemma, the conclusion follows.
∎

8.
Proof of Lemma
4.9

In this proof, as well as in the following one, the symbol
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
will also be
used to denote the duality product.
Setting
w=E_{A}(\sigma_{2})u
, we introduce the functional

{\mathsf{F}}_{2}={\mathsf{E}}_{2}+2\langle(f^{2}(A)-A)w,w\rangle+2\langle f(A)w,\dot{w}\rangle.

Multiplying (
1.2
) by the test function
2\dot{w}+2f(A)w

we get the differential identity

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{2}+2m_{*}{\mathsf{F}}_{2}+2{\mathsf{G}}_{2}=0,

having set

\displaystyle{\mathsf{G}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(Af(A)+m_{*}A-2m_{*}f^{2}(A))w,w\rangle+\langle(f(A)-m_{*})\dot{w},\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+2\langle(A-m_{*}f(A))w,\dot{w}\rangle.

We claim that
{\mathsf{G}}_{2}\geq 0
. Indeed, since

m_{*}\leq\phi(s)=f(s)-\sqrt{f^{2}(s)-s}\,<f(s),\quad\forall s\in\sigma_{2},

exploiting the Cauchy-Schwarz and the Young inequalities,

\displaystyle 2\langle(A-m_{*}f(A))w,\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle(f(A)-m_{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(A-m_{*}f(A))w,(f(A)-m_{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-\langle(f(A)-m_{*})^{-1}(A-m_{*}f(A))^{2}w,w\rangle-\langle(f(A)-m_{*})\dot{w},\dot{w}\rangle.

Therefore,
since

sf(s)+m_{*}s-2m_{*}f^{2}(s)-\frac{(s-m_{*}f(s))^{2}}{f(s)-m_{*}}=\frac{f^{2}(s)-s}{f(s)-m_{*}}(s+m_{*}^{2}-2m_{*}f(s)),

we get

{\mathsf{G}}_{2}\geq\langle(f(A)-m_{*})^{-1}(f^{2}(A)-A)(A+m_{*}^{2}-2m_{*}f(A))w,w\rangle.

The claim follows by noting that, for every
s\in\sigma_{2}
,

s+m_{*}^{2}-2m_{*}f(s)=\big(f(s)-\sqrt{f^{2}(s)-s}\,-m_{*}\big)\big(f(s)+\sqrt{f^{2}(s)-s}\,-m_{*}\big)\geq 0.

Accordingly, we end up with the differential inequality

(8.1)

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{2}+2m_{*}{\mathsf{F}}_{2}\leq 0.

Next, we show that

(8.2)

\frac{\varepsilon}{3}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}\leq{\mathsf{F}}_{2}\leq 3K^{2}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}.

To this end, we further apply
the Cauchy-Schwarz and the Young inequalities, to get

2|\langle f(A)w,\dot{w}\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\|f(A)w\|^{2}+\tau\|\dot{w}\|^{2},

for every
\tau>0
.
Since
f^{2}(s)\leq K^{2}s
for every
s\in\sigma_{2}
, by setting
\tau=1
we obtain

{\mathsf{F}}_{2}\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{2}+2(K^{2}-1)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}+K^{2}\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}+\|\dot{w}\|^{2}\leq 3K^{2}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}.

As far as the other inequality is concerned, we have

{\mathsf{F}}_{2}\geq(1-\tau){\mathsf{E}}_{2}+\langle((2-\tau^{-1})f^{2}(A)+(\tau-2)A)w,w\rangle.

Recalling that
f^{2}(s)\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{2}s
for every
s\in\sigma_{2}
,
and selecting
\tau=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
, we end up with

{\mathsf{F}}_{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}+\varrho(\varepsilon)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2},

where, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\varrho(\varepsilon)=\frac{(3-2\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{3-\varepsilon}-1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}=\frac{2\varepsilon(6-\varepsilon-3\varepsilon^{2})}{3(3-\varepsilon)}>0.

At this point, the conclusion is drawn from
(
8.1
)-(
8.2
) and the Gronwall
Lemma.
∎

9.
Proof of Lemma
4.10

Along the proof, besides the constraint
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
,
we will use the fact that

m_{3}\leq\phi(s)\leq f(s)<(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{s}\,,\quad\forall s\in\sigma_{3}.

Setting
w=E_{A}(\sigma_{3})u
, we introduce the functional

{\mathsf{F}}_{3}={\mathsf{E}}_{3}+2(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle f(A)w,\dot{w}\rangle+8\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\|f(A)w\|^{2}.

Multiplying (
1.2
) by the test function
2\dot{w}+2(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)f(A)w
, we get the
differential identity

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{3}+2m_{3}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,){\mathsf{F}}_{3}+2{\mathsf{G}}_{3}=0.

Here,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{G}}_{3}=\langle p(A)w,w\rangle+(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle(f(A)-m_{3})\dot{w},\dot{w}\rangle+2(1-16\varepsilon)\langle f(A)(f(A)-m_{3})w,\dot{w}\rangle,

having set

\displaystyle p(s)

\displaystyle=(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)sf(s)-m_{3}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)s-8m_{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,(1-16\varepsilon)f^{2}(s)

\displaystyle\geq(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)sf(s)-m_{3}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)s-8m_{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,(1-16\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)^{2}s,

\displaystyle=(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)s(f(s)-m_{3})+8m_{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\big[1-(1-16\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\big]s

\displaystyle\geq(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)s(f(s)-m_{3}).

Accordingly,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{G}}_{3}

\displaystyle\geq(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle A(f(A)-m_{3})w,w\rangle+(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle(f(A)-m_{3})\dot{w},\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-16\varepsilon)\langle f(A)(f(A)-m_{3})w,\dot{w}\rangle.

By an application of the
Cauchy-Schwarz and the Young inequalities, we find

\displaystyle 2(1-16\varepsilon)\langle f(A)(f(A)-m_{3})w,\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)^{\frac{1}{2}}f(A)(f(A)-m_{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}w,(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)^{\frac{1}{2}}(f(A)-m_{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-(1-16\varepsilon)(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle f^{2}(A)(f(A)-m_{3})w,w\rangle-(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle(f(A)-m_{3})\dot{w},\dot{w}\rangle,

implying in turn

\displaystyle{\mathsf{G}}_{3}

\displaystyle\geq(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle(f(A)-m_{3})(A-(1-16\varepsilon)f^{2}(A))w,w\rangle.

Observing that

s-(1-16\varepsilon)f^{2}(s)\geq\big[1-(1-16\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\big]s\geq 0,

we conclude that

{\mathsf{G}}_{3}\geq 0
, and we arrive at

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{3}+2m_{3}(1-4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,){\mathsf{F}}_{3}\leq 0.

After an application of the Gronwall Lemma, the proof is finished
once we show that

\varepsilon{\mathsf{E}}_{3}\leq{\mathsf{F}}_{3}\leq 8{\mathsf{E}}_{3}.

Indeed, the second inequality is a straightforward consequence
of the Cauchy-Schwarz and the Young inequalities, and is left to the reader.
Concerning the first one,
we have

2(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)\langle f(A)w,\dot{w}\rangle\geq-(1-\varepsilon)\|\dot{w}\|^{2}-\frac{(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\|f(A)w\|^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{F}}_{3}

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon{\mathsf{E}}_{3}+(1-\varepsilon)\|A^{\frac{1}{2}}w\|^{2}+\Big[8\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)-\frac{(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\Big]\|f(A)w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon{\mathsf{E}}_{3}+\Big[\frac{1-\varepsilon}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}+8\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)-\frac{(1+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\Big]\|f(A)w\|^{2},

and the quantity in the square brackets is positive for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{16})
.
∎
