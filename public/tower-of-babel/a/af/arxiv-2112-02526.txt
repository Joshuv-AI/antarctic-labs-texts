On the Numerical Approximation of the Karhunen-Loève Expansion for Random Fields with Random Discrete Data
Michael Griebel, Guanglian Li, Christian Rieger

Abstract
Many physical and mathematical models involve random fields in their input data. Examples are ordinary differential equations, partial differential equations and integro–differential equations with uncertainties in the coefficient functions described by random fields. They also play a dominant role in problems in machine learning. In this article, we do not assume to have knowledge of the moments or expansion terms of the random fields but we instead have only given discretized samples for them. We thus model some measurement process for this discrete information and then approximate the covariance operator of the original random field. Of course, the true covariance operator is of infinite rank and hence we can not assume to get an accurate approximation from a finite number of spatially discretized observations. On the other hand, smoothness of the true (unknown) covariance function results in effective low rank approximations to the true covariance operator. We derive explicit error estimates that involve the finite rank approximation error of the covariance operator, the Monte-Carlo-type errors for sampling in the stochastic domain and the numerical discretization error in the physical domain. This permits to give sufficient conditions on the three discretization parameters to guarantee that an error below a prescribed accuracy ε \varepsilon is achieved. Keywords : covariance operators, eigenvalue decay, approximation of Gaussian-type random fields, error estimates, Galerkin methods for eigenvalues, finite elements, tapering estimators for sample covariance

Introduction

Mathematical models with random coefficients or random input data have been widely employed to describe applications
that are affected by a certain amount of uncertainty arising from imperfect or insufficient information about the problem. The range of applications is broad and diverse and includes uncertainty quantification with ordinary differential equations, partial differential equations and integro-differential equations or problems in machine learning and data analysis in, e.g. oil field modeling, quantum mechanics or finance.

For instance in oil field modeling, a common source of uncertainty stems from the unknown soil parameters.
Often, one assumes a statistical model for the soil, for instance a Gaussian process with specified mean and covariance, and fits the possibly
remaining hyper-parameters of the process to the measured data.
Moreover in machine learning, a common source of randomness stems from noisy observed data. This renders all subsequent quantities to be random fields. Again one often assumes a statistical model for the noise, for instance a Gaussian model, which perfectly fits to the linear Bayesian framework as then the posterior is also Gaussian.
Anyway, let now a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be given, where
\mathcal{F}
denotes a
\sigma
-algebra of measurable sets and
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure on this
\sigma
-algebra. On this probability space, we consider a Gaussian random field

\displaystyle\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}.

Having such a Gaussian random field at hand, one usually parametrizes
it by means of the Karhunen-Loève (KL) expansion or a polynomial chaos (PC) expansion

[
9
]
, which fits to the
L^{2}
setting, i.e.,

\displaystyle\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\psi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}).

This greatly facilitates the subsequent treatment of problems with
\kappa
modeling their random input or their random coefficient functions,
e.g., by the stochastic Galerkin method or the stochastic collocation method. Alternatively, in the Banach space setting, one may expand the random field with respect
to the hierarchical Faber basis or some wavelet type basis; see
[
3
]

for details.

To further fix notation, a realization of the associated stochastic process is the function

\displaystyle\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot):\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega\text{ fixed}.

We now can interpret the stochastic process
\kappa
as a
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
-valued random variable, i.e.,

\displaystyle K:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}},\quad\omega\mapsto\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot):\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R},

where
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
denotes the set of all maps
\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, using the notion of a push forward measure, we define a distribution of this random variable
K
, and thus the random
process
\kappa
, as

\displaystyle\mu=\mathbb{P}\circ K^{-1}.

Any Gaussian random field
\kappa
is determined completely by its first two moments, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa\right]

\displaystyle:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\kappa]=\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\text{Cov}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R},\quad(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\kappa-\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\right)\left(\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]\right)\right].

For the sake of simplicity, we will assume that
\kappa
is a centered Gaussian field, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[K\right]\equiv 0
.
This yields

\displaystyle\text{Cov}_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=:R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(1.1)

where the series is a Mercer-type expansion.
We will assume that the eigenvalues are sorted in strictly decreasing order, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\dots\geq 0.

(1.2)

In the case of multiple eigenvalues the projection onto the eigenspaces, i.e.,

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{\ell}\Pi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{N(\ell,k)}\phi_{\ell,k}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell,k}(\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}),

(1.3)

is to be employed of course, but for reasons of simplicity we will stick here to the notationally simpler Mercer series (
1.1
) instead.
By repeating multiple eigenvalues and modifying our approach accordingly, the more general case of multiple eigenvalues can be covered in an analogous way.

Usually, such an expansion will be truncated yielding a so-called finite noise approximation. To be precise, the approximation

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx R^{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(1.4)

is considered,
where the quality of the approximation relies on sharp eigenvalue decay estimates as given in Theorem
3.1
later on. This approach however needs the set
\{\phi_{\ell}\}
of continuous eigenfunctions of the covariance operator
R
in the first place, which is in general unknown. While its knowledge is often a priorily assumed in the literature for further numerical analysis, such complete information is mostly not available in practice and such an approach is thus often not viable.

Therefore, in the following, we consider a different situation: We do not assume to have access to the moments of the random field
\kappa
in our analysis. Thus we can not compute its Karhunen Loève expansion directly as an eigenvalue problem for the integral operator with the covariance functions as kernel.
Instead, we will only assume

\displaystyle C_{\infty}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty.

(1.5)

Note that (
1.5
) implies uniform bounds on mean and covariance functions of the random field
\kappa
.
Indeed, the definition of the mean function and an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality leads to

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right|\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}1\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{\infty}.

(1.6)

Moreover, we can obtain by the definition of the covariance function and an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality the bound

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\right)\left(\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 4C_{\infty}^{2}.

(1.7)

Furthermore, we define

C_{H^{s}}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty
for later use as well.
We refer to
[
16
, Theorem 5.2]
for conditions on
\kappa
which allow for such types of bounds.

Next we assume some spatial regularity of the random field
\kappa
.

Assumption 1.1
.

Let
\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and thus
K:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
be contained in the Bochner space

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega,H^{s}(\mathcal{D}))\quad\text{for some }s\geq 0,

(1.8)

where, for
s=0
, we have
H^{0}(\mathcal{D})=L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
Alternatively, let
\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and thus
K:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
be contained in the Bochner space

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega,H^{s}(\mathcal{D}))\quad\text{for some }s>\frac{d}{2}.

(1.9)

Now we introduce a discretization of the function space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}:=\{f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}\}
.
To be precise, we consider an underlying finite element space of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{h}:=\{v\in H^{1}(\mathcal{D}):v|_{K}\in P^{\lceil s\rceil}(K)\text{ for all }K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}},

(1.10)

where
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a regular quasi-uniform triangulation over the
physical domain
\mathcal{D}
with a maximal mesh size
h
and
\lceil s\rceil\in\mathbb{N}
will depend on the spatial regularity of
\kappa
.
We denote the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{h}
by
Q_{h}<\infty
. Moreover, we expect
Q_{h}=\mathcal{O}(h^{-d}s^{d})
.
For the rest of the paper, we will fix some arbitrary basis of
\mathcal{V}_{h}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{h}=\text{span}\left\{\theta_{k}^{(h)}\ :\ 1\leq k\leq Q_{h}\right\}.

Here we may employ a nodal basis as well as a discrete orthonormal basis later on.
Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
be

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\left(\theta^{(h)}_{1},\dots,\theta^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}\right)^{T}.

Any function
v^{(h)}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
then admits the expression

\displaystyle v^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}=\left(V^{(h)}_{1},\dots,V^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},

and each function
v^{(h)}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
can be identified with its coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
.

Next we consider the projection map
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathcal{V}_{h}
. Thus
s=0
in (
1.8
) from Assumption
1.1
, which is here the weakest assumption possible.
Given such regularity, we recall the approximation power of the
L^{2}
-projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathcal{V}_{h}
, which is given in
[
5
, Theorem 4.4.20]
as

\displaystyle\left\|v-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}h^{s}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\text{ for all }v\in H^{s}(\mathcal{D}),

(1.11)

\displaystyle\left\|v-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}v\right\|_{C\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\text{ for all }v\in H^{s}(\mathcal{D})\text{ for }s>d/2.

(1.12)

Here, the positive constants
C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}
and
C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}
depend only on the shape regularity parameter of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and are independent of the mesh size
h
.

In contrast to other approaches where the continuous random field
\kappa:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and thus
K:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}
are assumed to be completely known, we in the following only assume to have access to

\displaystyle\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(1.13)

where
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
is a multivariate random variable defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\mapsto\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\left(K_{1}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),\dots,K_{Q_{h}}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{T}.

(1.14)

Furthermore we assume that the coefficients, i.e. the random variables
\{K^{(h)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\}_{j=1,\cdots,Q_{h}}
, are independent identically distributed with a common distribution
\mu^{(h)}
.
As we work with centered Gaussian random fields, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]=\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
.

This resembles more closely the practical situation in real applications where in general the full random field
K
is never completely accessible but only given at a finite number of sample points which stem from independent measurements in the first place.
The random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
is indeed the information we practically have on the random input.

Note that an alternative approach would be to consider directly the
Carleman operator

R
(see (
1.1
)) for centered fields and to approximate it by
\bar{R}^{(h)}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\approx R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle\approx M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{m},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=:\bar{R}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

This kernel
\bar{R}^{(h)}
gives rise to an integral operator
f\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{D}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\bar{R}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)d\boldsymbol{x}
for which the stochastics is integrated out. Our aim is however to derive a kernel via sampling estimation instead. But this sampling is not involved in the above reference kernels
{R}
and
\bar{R}^{(h)}
at all. Therefore, we consider the kernel
R^{(h)}
as integral kernel on the discretized space
\mathcal{V}_{h}
. In particular, we will have a Mercer-type expansion of the form

\displaystyle R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\quad\text{with}\quad\phi^{(h)}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

This gives rise to a generalized matrix eigenvalue problem and we merely estimate the associated stiffness matrix from samples.

Now, due to (
1.9
) from Assumption
1.1
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}+\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}+C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\quad\text{for }\mathbb{P}\text{ almost all }\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega.

(1.15)

Hence, after squaring and integrating over
\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\|\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(\left\|\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}+C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}h^{s-\frac{d}{2}}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\left\|\ \kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}^{2}+C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}^{2}h^{2s-d}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\leq 2C_{\infty}^{2}+2C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};C}}^{2}h^{2s-d}C_{H^{s}}^{2}.

Thus, with
\kappa^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right):=\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)
, we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\infty}^{(h)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty.

(1.16)

Analogously by employing the
H^{s}
norm instead of the
C(\mathcal{D})
norm, we have

\displaystyle C_{H^{s}}^{(h)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa\left(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot\right)\right\|_{H^{s}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty.

As in the continuous case (
1.6
) and (
1
) , this implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\leq C_{\infty}^{(h)}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{Cov}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq 4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}\quad\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}.

(1.17)

Furthermore, with the representation (
1.13
) and since we deal with (centered) random vectors, the covariance is given as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\otimes\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

(1.18)

In contrast to the vast majority of the literature, where the covariance is assumed to be known, we only assume to have given
M
independent identically distributed discrete samples
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}_{M}
of the random variable
\kappa
. From these finitely many values we merely aim to derive an estimate of the true covariance function. To this end, we have to deal with the high-dimensional covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
.
Since
Q_{h}\sim h^{-d}
, this involves the well-known curse of dimension. Consequently the estimation of the covariance matrix gets more challenging the smaller the values of
h
get. Therefore, we will assume that the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
has a certain off-diagonal decay, that is

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(C_{\mathcal{F};1},C_{\mathcal{F};2}),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}\ :\ \max_{k=1,\dots,Q_{h}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\dimVhrun^{\prime}=1}{\left|\dimVhrun^{\prime}-\dimVhrun\right|>c }}^{Q_{h}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right|\leq C_{\mathcal{F};1}c^{-\alpha}\text{ for all }1\leq c\leq Q_{h}\text{ and }\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq C_{\mathcal{F};2}\right\}.

(1.19)

Here,
\alpha
modulates the speed of decay and
C_{\mathcal{F};1},C_{\mathcal{F};2}
are positive constants.
This specific class of matrices contains several relevant examples of discrete covariance functions, see
[
4
]
and the overview
[
6
]
.
Moreover, the choice of this class of matrices will influence the approximation of continuous covariance functions via so-called tapering weights, see (
4.39
). We will make use of the optimal rates derived in
[
4
]
.
We furthermore will work with generalized eigenvalue problems. i.e., we do not compute the eigensystem of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{K^{(h)}}
but instead that of a certain transformed matrix, see (
4.14
).
The eigensystem of this system gives rise to a computable Mercer-like expansion

\displaystyle R^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx R^{(L;h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(1.20)

where the
(h;M)
–notation indicates that we use an estimator which is based on the
M
finite samples
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}_{M}
and makes use of certain appropriate tapering weights. Moreover

indicates the finite-noise approximation.

The main aim of this article to derive a bound on the approximation error
\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|
and of its expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|\right]
.
To this end, we couple the discretization parameters

and consider their limits
h\to 0,L\to Q_{h}\to\infty,M\to\infty
to recover the true continuous covariance operator.
For the approximation step with respect to
L
, we will make use of the optimal decay estimates for the eigenvalues of the true covariance operator as given
[
11
]
. For the two approximations steps involving

and

, we will derive corresponding estimates for the spatial discretization error and the sampling error.
Finally we properly combine the three approximation steps. This coupling is our central result, see Theorem

.
There we show that the bound

\displaystyle\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-\frac{2s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}+\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L)\right)\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\ ,

holds with high probability, where

is a function of the truncation parameter

that depends on spectral properties of the true operator associated to

as given in (
5.2
). Moreover
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
denotes the finite element discretization of the true operator and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}
denotes its sampling approximation, c.f. (

) and (

).

Since sampling involves randomness we are also interested in estimates in expectation.
Our second main result is Theorem
5.2
. It states that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-\frac{2s}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}+\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L)\right)\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)

\displaystyle+L^{\frac{1}{2}}h^{-d}\exp\left(-M\rho_{1}H(L)\lambda_{\max}^{-2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\right),

where
H(L)
is again a function of the truncation parameter
L
that depends on spectral properties (
5.29
) of the true operator,
\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M)
measures the approximation quality of our employed (tapering) estimator,
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
is a classical finite element mass matrix and
\rho_{1}
denotes a constant given in (
4.54
) which is determined by the sub-Gaussian property of the involved random variables.
This permits to give sufficient conditions on the three discretization parameters to guarantee that an error below a prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
is achieved.

A novel contribution is the combination of our recent sharp bound (see
[
11
]
) on spectra of covariance operators with optimal statistical covariance estimation methods, i.e., we derive an error analysis for the reconstruction of the continuous covariance operator from finite measurements. We believe that the presented framework is useful if only finite measured information on uncertain problem parameters is available and no a priori modeling assumption for the random field can be given. Such a situation is indeed often encountered in many practical problems in uncertainty quantification and machine learning.

Notation and basic facts

We start with some notation. Let two Banach spaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be given. Then,
\mathcal{B}(V_{1},V_{2})
stands for the Banach space composed of all continuous linear operators from
V_{1}
to
V_{2}
and
\mathcal{B}(V_{1})
stands for
\mathcal{B}(V_{1},V_{1})
. The set of non-negative integers is denoted by
\mathbb{N}
. For any index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
|\alpha|
is the
sum of its components. The letters
L
,
M
and
h
are reserved for the truncation number of the KL modes, the number of sampling points and the mesh size. We write
A\lesssim B
if
A\leq cB
for some absolute constant
c
which is independent of
L
,
M
and
h
, and we likewise write
A\gtrsim B
. Moreover, for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we follow
[
1
]
and define the Sobolev space
W^{s,p}(\mathcal{D})
by

W^{s,p}(\mathcal{D})=\{u\in L^{p}(\mathcal{D}):D^{\alpha}u\in L^{p}(\mathcal{D})\text{ for }0\leq|\alpha|\leq s\}.

It is equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathcal{D})}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \Big(\sum\limits_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq s}\left\|D^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{D})}^{p}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}},&\text{ if }1\leq p<\infty,\\
\max\limits_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq s}\left\|D^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})},&\text{ if }p=\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

The space
W_{0}^{s,p}(D)
is the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
in
W^{s,p}(D)
. Its dual space is
W^{-s,q}(\mathcal{D})
, with
{1}/{p}+{1}/{q}=1
. Also we use
H^{s}(\mathcal{D})=W^{s,p}(\mathcal{D})
for
p=2
. Finally
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{D}}
denotes the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

We now recall the classical Monte–Carlo algorithm.
To this end, we consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and a given probability distribution
\mu
. Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{M}
for
M\in\mathbb{N}
be independent identically distributed real-valued random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with the same associated probability distribution
\mu
, i.e,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}_{m}(B))=\mu(B)\quad\text{for all }B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and all }1\leq m\leq M.

Here,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
Each random variable
X_{m}
induces a sub
\sigma
-algebra, defined by

\displaystyle\sigma(X_{m})=\left\{X^{-1}_{m}(B)\ :\ B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

Note that the
\{\sigma(X_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
are
\mathbb{P}
-independent because the random variables
\{X_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are pairwise independent, i.e., for every finite set
\{m_{1},\dots m_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
and every
B_{m_{j}}\in\sigma(X_{m_{j}})
for
1\leq j\leq k
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}B_{m_{j}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{m_{j}}\right).

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Borel function. Then
Y_{m}=f(X_{m})
is also a random variable, with its associated probability distribution being

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((f\circ X_{m})^{-1}(B))=\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}_{m}(f^{-1}(B)))=\mu(f^{-1}(B))\quad\text{for all }B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, the composite random variables
\{Y_{m}\}
are also identically distributed, with their generated
\sigma
-algebras defined by

\displaystyle\sigma(Y_{m})=\left\{(f\circ X_{m})^{-1}(B)\ :\ B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\right\}=\left\{X^{-1}_{m}(f^{-1}(B))\ :\ B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\right\}\subset\sigma(X_{m}).

Consequently, the random variables
\{Y_{m}\}
are pairwise independent as a result of the independence of the
\{X_{m}\}
.

Next we introduce a real-valued random variable
X
equipped with the probability
\mu
. Let
Y
be a random variable defined by
Y=f(X)
. Assume that the first two moments of
Y
exist. Then its mean and variance are given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Y(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{\Omega}(f\circ X)(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[Y]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])^{2}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(f(x)-\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty.

By definition,
Y
and
\{Y_{m}\}
have the same mean

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{m}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y]

and hence, by linearity, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}\right]=\mathbb{E}[Y].

Furthermore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}-\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)^{2}\right]=\text{Cov}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m},\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{m,\tilde{m}=1}^{M}\text{Cov}\left(Y_{m},Y_{\tilde{m}}\right)=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\text{Cov}\left(Y_{m},Y_{m}\right)=\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{V}[Y],

(2.1)

where we used the definition of covariance and the fact that mixed terms vanish due to independence.
The idea of Monte–Carlo is to approximate the mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
by the estimator
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[Y]
defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[Y]:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}f(X_{m}).

Then an application of (
2.1
) yields the corresponding RMSE error

RMSE

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[Y]-\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)^{2}\right]}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbb{V}\left[Y\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For any
\epsilon>0
, a combination of Chebyshev’s inequality and the equality (
2.1
) shows

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\left|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[Y]-\mathbb{E}[Y]\right|>\epsilon\right\}\right)\leq\epsilon^{-2}\mathbb{V}\left[\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[Y]\right]=\frac{1}{M\epsilon^{2}}\mathbb{V}(Y).

Taking
\epsilon:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M\delta}}\left(\mathbb{V}(Y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
for some
\delta>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\left|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[Y]-\mathbb{E}[Y]\right|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{M\delta}}\left(\mathbb{V}(Y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\right)\leq\delta.

3
Karhunen-Loève expansion: Continuous level

This section is concerned with the Karhunen-Loève expansion of the centered Gaussian random field
\kappa
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})
be the Lebesgue measure on the physical domain
\mathcal{D}
. For the sake of simplicity,
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and
L^{2}(\Omega)
are short for
L^{2}(\mathcal{D};\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}))
and
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P})
. We denote the associated integral operator
\mathcal{S}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}v=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)v\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)},

(3.1)

whereas its adjoint operator
\mathcal{S}^{*}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{*}v=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\cdot)v\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

(3.2)

Let
\mathcal{R}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
be defined by
\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{S}^{*}\mathcal{S}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}
is a non-negative self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt operator with kernel

R\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})
given by

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]

(3.3)

Moreover, for any
v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}v(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\mathcal{D}}R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})v(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\,v(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(3.4)

The standard spectral theory for compact operators
[
19
]
implies that the operator
\mathcal{R}
has
at most countably many discrete eigenvalues with zero being the only accumulation point and each non-zero
eigenvalue has only finite multiplicity. Let
\{\lambda_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
be the sequence of eigenvalues (with multiplicity counted) associated to

\mathcal{R}
, which are ordered non-increasingly, and let
\{\phi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
be the corresponding eigenfunctions that are orthonormal in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
Furthermore, for any
\lambda_{\ell}\neq 0
, define

\psi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(3.5)

One can verify that the sequence
\{\psi_{\ell}\}_{{\ell}=1}^{\infty}
is uncorrelated and
orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and therefore,
\{\psi_{\ell}\}_{{\ell}=1}^{\infty}
are i.i.d normal random functions.
Note that the sequence
\{\lambda_{\ell}\}_{{\ell}=1}^{\infty}
can be characterized by the so-called approximation numbers (cf.
[
14
, Section 2.3.1]
). They are defined by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}=\inf\{\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))}:\mathcal{T}\in\mathfrak{F}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D})),{\text{rank}}(\mathcal{T})<\ell\},

(3.6)

where
\mathfrak{F}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))
denotes the set of the finite rank operators on
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
This equivalency is frequently employed to estimate eigenvalues by constructing finite rank approximation operators.

The KL expansion of the bivariate function
\kappa
then refers to the expression

\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\psi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),

(3.7)

where the series converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\cong L^{2}(\Omega\times D)
.

3.1

Now we will truncate the KL expansion and discuss the resulting error. The studies on the

[

∎

4.1
Spatial approximation

In this section, we investigate the influence of the spatial discretization.
Recall that the random field
\kappa^{(h)}

\kappa

\displaystyle R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right]

Lemma 4.1

(Semi-discrete spatial approximation error estimate)

Let

Furthermore, for the associated integral operators
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{h}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{(h)}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))}\leq C_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}(C_{\infty}C_{H^{s}}+C_{\infty}^{(h)}C_{H^{s}}^{(h)})h^{s}.

Using
ab-a_{h}b_{h}=(a-a_{h})b+a_{h}(b-b_{h})

\displaystyle\left\|R^{(h)}-R\right\|_{C(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}

Using

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{R}^{(h)}\phi_{\ell}^{(h)},v^{(h)}\right)_{\mathcal{D}}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})v^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(h)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\mathcal{D}}\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(h)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\left(\int_{\mathcal{D}}\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(h)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(h)}.

Here
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
and
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
are the mass matrix and the stiffness matrix defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}}^{T})_{\mathcal{D}}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}.

(4.5)

Note that the symmetry of the mass matrix
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
implies the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
.

Thus, the corresponding matrix form of the generalized eigenvalue problem (
4.4
) is to seek the eigenpair
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
for
\ell=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}.

(4.6)

This is a generalized eigenvalue problem. We now transform it to a conventional eigenvalue problem. To this end, observe that the mass matrix
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}
is symmetric positive-definite, and thus possesses a Cholesky factorization

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}=:\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

Now let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
be a symmetric positive-definite matrix defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}.

Then we can solve for
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
for
\ell=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}.

(4.7)

Suppose now that
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
is an eigenpair of (
4.7
).
Using

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}

we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h)}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h)}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}.

Thus, we have shown the equivalence

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue3}}\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue2}.}

Note that, with
\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h)}_{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
, we obtain a Mercer-like representation

\displaystyle R^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h)}_{k}\phi_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx R^{(L;h)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h)}_{k}\phi_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(4.8)

for
1\leq L\leq Q_{h}
.

We now give a classical error analysis by means of the
approximation theory of conforming finite element methods.
To this end, consider the error operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(h)}=\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{(h)}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),

(4.9)

which is a self-adjoint
operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. We have the following error representation.

Lemma 4.2
.

The error operator
\mathcal{E}^{(h)}
has the property

\left(\mathcal{E}^{(h)}v,v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}=\left(v,(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}})\mathcal{R}(I+\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}})v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad v\in L^{2}(D),

where we use the operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathcal{V}_{h}
from (
1.12
).

Proof.

For given
v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and since
\mathcal{R}^{(h)}=\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\mathcal{R}\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}
and
(\mathcal{R}\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}v-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\mathcal{R}v,v)=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{E}^{(h)}v,v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\mathcal{R}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\mathcal{R}\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\right)v,v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}+\left(\mathcal{R}\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}v-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\mathcal{R}v,v\right)_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\right)\mathcal{R}\left(I+\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}\right)v,v\right)_{\mathcal{D}},

which gives the assertion.
∎

A direct consequence of Lemma
4.2
is the upper bound for the operator norm of
\mathcal{E}^{(h)}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{E}^{(h)}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))}\leq 2C_{\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{h};L^{2}}}h^{s}\left\|\mathcal{R}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),H^{s}(\mathcal{D}))},

(4.10)

where we employed (
1.12
).

Finally, we are ready to present the main result of this section.

) from Assumption

of

can be selected such that

Here, the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are independent of

and
h_{0}>0
is to be sufficiently small.

Proof.

The proof follows from
[
2

.
∎

A consequence of Proposition

is that, for any
\epsilon>0
and for the Lebesgue measure

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(d)}\left(\left\{\ \left|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}\right|\geq\epsilon\right\}\right)\leq\epsilon^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}=\epsilon^{-2}\left\|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\leq C_{2}\epsilon^{-2}\lambda_{\ell}^{-2}h^{2s}.

(4.11)

4.2
Approximation of the covariance operator from samples

We will approximate the spectral decomposition (
4.8
), which is based on the unknown stiffness matrix
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
by approximating the true covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
.
Let us assume for the moment that we have such an estimate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
is also symmetric. Then by repeating the procedure for the derivation (
4.8
) in an analogous way, we obtain the approximations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\approx\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}=\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\quad\text{and}\quad

(4.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\approx\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)},

(4.13)

and we encounter the following eigenvalue problem: Seek
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}
for all
\ell=1,\cdots,Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}.

(4.14)

As
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}
is a symmetric matrix, we have that
\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)_{\ell_{2}}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
after normalization.
Moreover, we encounter the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{(h;M)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}=\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}.

(4.15)

Analogously, we have the equivalence

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue3-estimator}}\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{-T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}\text{ is an eigenpair of \eqref{genalizedeigenvalue4-estimator}}.

Thus we can derive approximations to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
and
\boldsymbol{S}^{(h)}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k}\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{S}^{(h;M)}=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

(4.16)

Then the eigensystem (
4.16
) gives rise to a computable Mercer-like expansion

\displaystyle R^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{k}\phi^{(h;M)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\phi^{(h;M)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(4.17)

Here, we use analogously

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{(h;M)}:=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}\text{ for all }k=1,\cdots,Q_{h}.

(4.18)

We observe, using
\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}=\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}},

that

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{k}^{(h;M)},\phi_{k^{\prime}}^{(h;M)}\right)_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k^{\prime}}=\left(\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k},\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(h;M)}_{k^{\prime}}\right)_{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k^{\prime}}\right)_{\ell_{2}}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}.

(4.19)

To bound the sampling error for the eigenvalues, we can
apply Weyl’s inequality (see for instance
[
12
, (Eq. 3.1)]
) to any of the two eigenvalue problems (
4.14
) or (
4.15
), respectively. We will focus on (
4.14
) as (
4.15

We obtain

\displaystyle\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\leq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq\min\{Q_{h},L\}.

(4.20)

Now we can take Proposition
4.1
into account to get that, under Assumption
1.1
, there are constants
C_{1}
and
h_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\lambda_{\ell}^{-1}h^{2s}+\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\quad\text{ for all }\quad 0<h\leq h_{0}\text{ and }1\leq\ell\leq\min\{Q_{h},L\}.

(4.21)

For perturbation bounds on the eigenvectors, see
[
18
]
, and for a recent versions of the Davis-Kahan theorem including randomness, see
[
13
]
.
We use the variant presented in
[
20
, Corollary 1]
. To this end, recall the assumption that the eigenvalues are ordered non-increasingly, i.e.,
\lambda^{(h)}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}
and
\lambda^{(h;M)}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda^{(h;M)}_{Q_{h}}
.
We define the discrete spectral gap

\displaystyle\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}:=\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\},\quad\text{with }\lambda^{(h;M)}_{0}=\infty\text{ and }\lambda^{(h;M)}_{Q_{h}+1}=-\infty.

(4.22)

The quantity
\delta_{\ell}^{(h;M)}
is problematic as it contains
\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell},\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell\pm 1}
. We therefore aim to replace it by a quantity which only depends on the continuous problem. To this end, we adopt the strategy presented in
[
20
]
:
Let
\delta_{\ell}
be the continuous spectral gap defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}:=\min\left\{\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda_{\ell},\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell+1}\right\}\text{ with }\lambda_{0}=\infty.

(4.23)

Assumption 4.1

(Spectral gap)
.

Assume that there is a sufficiently small positive parameter
h_{1}\leq h_{0}
and assume that

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}
is a good approximation to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}
, i.e., for
h\leq h_{1}
, there holds

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}\geq 4C_{1}h^{2s}\lambda_{\ell+1}^{-1}+4\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

(4.24)

Theorem 4.1
.

Let (
4.24
) be valid. It then holds

\displaystyle\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell}.

(4.25)

Furthermore it holds

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\leq C\frac{\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}}{\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}}\leq 4C\frac{\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}}{\delta_{\ell}},

(4.26)

where we fixed a sign for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}
such that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h)}_{\ell}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\geq 0
.
Moreover for
\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{(h;M)}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}
the bound

\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{\ell}^{(h)}-\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C\frac{\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}}{\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}}\leq 4C\frac{\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}}{\delta_{\ell}}

(4.27)

holds.

Proof.

The first assertion is a generalization of an argument presented in
[
20
]
to the case of several discretization parameters.
Recall the discrete spectral gap
\delta^{(h;M)}_{\ell}
in (
4.22
).
We show (
4.25
) by the following strategy:
First, we observe

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|=\left|\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}+\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}+\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|.

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell+1}\right|=\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}+\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}+\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}-\lambda_{\ell+1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}-\lambda_{\ell+1}\right|.

Using Proposition
4.1
and the fact that the eigenvalues are sorted, i.e.,
\lambda_{\ell-1}\geq\lambda_{\ell}\geq\lambda_{\ell+1}
, we obtain for all

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda_{\ell+1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\}\leq C_{1}\lambda_{\ell+1}^{-1}h^{2s}\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell},

where we have used the spectral gap assumption (
4.24
) in the last step.
Hence, we infer

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}\leq\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\ell}=\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\ell}.

This inequality implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\ell}\leq\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}=\min\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\right\}.

(4.28)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|=\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}+\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|

and also

\displaystyle\delta^{(h)}_{\ell}\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|=\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}+\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell+1}\right|.

4.20

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell-1}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell-1}\right|,\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\right\}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\ell}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{(h)}_{\ell},

where we have used (

The two other assertions follow directly with the Davis-Kahan theorem as in

Finally, we are left with bounding

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\right)^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{\left(\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right)\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{\ell_{2}}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}}

(4.29)

where the choice
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{x}
is admissible since
\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}
is regular.

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}\leq\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}\right)\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}

and we observe
\lambda_{\min}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\right)=\lambda^{-1}_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)
and
\lambda_{\max}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)^{-1}\right)=\lambda^{-1}_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)

Consequently, we obtain the bound

\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

(4.30)

The matrix norm
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}

will be estimated in probabilistic terms in Section

.

4.3
Sub-Gaussian tails for observed random vectors

In order to invoke recent results for bounding the sampling error, we first need to consider the sub-Gaussian property of the random variables (
1.14
), i.e., of the random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
.
This random vector consists of the coefficients of the discrete random field
\kappa^{(h)}:\Omega\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}K^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\theta_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}).

We mainly focus on two different basis functions. First, we choose
\{\theta^{(h)}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq Q_{h}}
to be a nodal basis and, next, we choose
\{\theta^{(h)}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq Q_{h}}
to be an
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis.

4.3.1
Nodal basis

To start, let us consider the setting of so-called standard information. To this end,
recall the point set
X_{Q_{h}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{Q_{h}}\right\}\subset\mathcal{D}
, which determines the nodal basis functions and thus the degrees of freedom for the finite element space
\mathcal{V}_{h}
.
In this case, it holds

\displaystyle\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\kappa^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\theta_{k}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\mapsto\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\left(\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),\dots,\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x}_{Q_{h}})\right)^{T}.

(4.31)

Since
\kappa^{(h)}
is assumed to be a centered Gaussian random field, it follows by definition that the random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
is also distributed according to a multi-variate Gaussian law, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right].

We can invoke (
1.17
) and (
1.18
), i.e.,
\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}\leq 4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}
.
Finally, we obtain by Chernoff’s inequality
[
8
]
the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)>t\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}}\right)

for any
\boldsymbol{v}
with
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1
.
Note that this implies
4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}=\rho^{-1}
in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 1]
.

4.3.2
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis

Now let us consider the setting of so-called linear information. Here, we can not directly rely on the definition of a centered Gaussian random field to deduce that the coefficients are distributed with a multivariate normal law.
In this case, the random vector (
1.14
) gets encoded as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\theta^{(h)}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\theta^{(h)}_{Q_{h}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
\{\theta_{j}^{(h)},j=1,\ldots,Q_{h}\}
is an
L_{2}
-orthonormal basis.
The individual random variables are given as

\displaystyle K^{(h)}_{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\theta^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(4.32)

Hence, for the expected value, we observe

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[K^{(h)}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\theta^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\theta^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\theta^{(h)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})=0,

which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{0}.

For the variance, we observe

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\otimes\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]=:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}},

where

\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\theta_{k}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\theta_{k^{\prime}}^{h}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}

(4.33)

and

\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}}\leq\|\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\leq 4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}\cdot|\mathcal{D}|.

(4.34)

For all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{Q_{h}}\left(\kappa^{(h)}(\omega_{j},\cdot),\theta_{j}^{(h)}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}v_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\boldsymbol{v}).

Furthermore, given
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, it holds

\displaystyle\kappa^{(h)}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})\right).

(4.35)

Therefore we can invoke Chernoff’s inequality to obtain for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}
and
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\ \left|\kappa^{(h)}(\omega,\boldsymbol{x})\right|>t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\text{Cov}_{\kappa^{(h)}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}|\mathcal{D}|}\right),

which is uniform for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, see (
4.34
). Consequently, we have, for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1
, the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}\right]\right)\right|>t\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{{8(C_{\infty}^{(h)}})^{2}|\mathcal{D}|}\right).

Note that this implies
4(C_{\infty}^{(h)})^{2}|\mathcal{D}|=\rho^{-1}
in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 1]
.
Hence, the random vector
\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}
obeys a sub-Gaussian bound in the sense of
[
7
, Eq. (7)]
.
We will make use of this estimate together with the results of
[
7
]
to bound the variance of the sampled covariance matrix later on.

4.4
Covariance estimation from samples using tapering and decay assumptions

In this subsection, we focus on the sampling error and specifically on the induced variance of our estimator for the covariance matrix. We assume in this subsection the parameter
h
to be fixed. Thus the dimension of the covariance matrix is given as
Q_{h}
. We will provide a constructive approach to obtain an estimation to the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
. To this end, with given samples
\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}
for
m=1,\cdots,M
, let the sample mean and the maximum likelihood estimator for the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
be
[
7
, Eq. (2)]

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h;M)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}}\quad\text{and}

(4.36)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h)}\right)\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}.

(4.37)

We know from
[
6
, Lemma 1]
that the direct covariance estimator (
4.37
) suffers from the curse of dimension with respect to high spatial resolution
Q_{h}
. Consequently, we need a better estimator when
Q_{h}
is large. To this end, we follow
[
6
]
and assume an off diagonal decay of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}
, i.e., we assume
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(C_{\mathcal{F};1},C_{\mathcal{F};2})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}\ :\ \max_{k=1,\dots,Q_{h}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\dimVhrun^{\prime}=1}{\left|\dimVhrun^{\prime}-\dimVhrun\right|>c }}^{Q_{h}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k,k^{\prime}}\right|\leq C_{\mathcal{F};1}c^{-\alpha}\text{ for all }1\leq c\leq Q_{h}\text{ and }\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\leq C_{\mathcal{F};2}\right\}.

(4.38)

Here,
\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is the maximum eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and
\alpha
modulates the speed of decay while
C_{\mathcal{F};1}
and
C_{\mathcal{F};2}
are positive parameters.
Now, let weights for any even integer
1\leq\tau\leq Q_{h}
(see
[
7
, (Eq. 5)]
) be given as

\displaystyle w_{k,k^{\prime}}:=w_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tau):=\begin{cases}1,&\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|\leq\frac{\tau}{2},\\
2\left(1-\frac{\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|}{\tau}\right),&\frac{\tau}{2}<\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|<\tau,\\
0,&\left|k-k^{\prime}\right|\geq\tau.\end{cases}

(4.39)

Then the associated tapering estimator (c.f.
[
7
, (Eq. 4)]
) is defined element-wise by

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{{}_{k,k^{\prime}}}:=w_{k,k^{\prime}}(\tau)\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{h;M}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}},\quad 1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq Q_{h}.

(4.40)

Note that the estimator
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
is self-adjoint. Moreover the bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\tau+\log(Q_{h})}{M},

(4.41)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{-2\alpha}

(4.42)

on the variance and the bias hold, see
[
7
, (Eqs. 13 & 14)]
.
By the triangle inequality we deduce the convergence of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}
in expectation as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M;\tau)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\tau+\log(Q_{h})}{M}+\tau^{-2\alpha},

(4.43)

which motivates the choice of the tapering parameter
\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
, c.f.
[
7
, (Eq. 15)]
.
This gives the following result, c.f.
[
7
, Theorem 2 & (Eq. 31)]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For the tapering estimator (
4.40
) with
\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{{}_{k,k^{\prime}}}:=w_{k,k^{\prime}}(M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}})\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)_{k,k^{\prime}},\quad 1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq Q_{h},

(4.44)

there holds the error estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\lesssim M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{\log(Q_{h})}{M}

(4.45)

if the condition
Q_{h}\geq M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
is satisfied.
In the case
Q_{h}<M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
, we can directly use the estimator from (
4.37
)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h)}\right)\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{(m;h)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{K}}^{(h)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{h}\times Q_{h}}

and obtain the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{Q_{h}}{M}.

(4.46)

Note that the result is in fact
rate optimal
.

We introduce the notation

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(M)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{\log(Q_{h})}{M},&Q_{h}\geq M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\\
\frac{Q_{h}}{M},&Q_{h}<M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\end{cases}

(4.47)

\displaystyle\lesssim\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M):=\begin{cases}M^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{d\log(h^{-1})}{M},&Q_{h}\geq M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\\
\frac{h^{-d}}{M},&Q_{h}<M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}\end{cases},

(4.48)

using
Q_{h}\sim s^{d}h^{-d}
for the definition of
\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M)
.
Then, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.1
.

For the tapering estimator (
4.40
) with
\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
the bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right),

(4.49)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}\right]\lambda_{\max}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\lesssim\rho_{h}(M)\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)

(4.50)

hold.

Proof.

First, we obtain by (
4.30
)

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

(4.51)

We can apply Jensen’s inequality for the convex function
\psi(x)=x^{2}
,

\displaystyle\psi\left(\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\psi(X)\right].

Plugging (
4.51
) into Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right]\right)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}\right]\lambda_{\max}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right).

Thus, we get from Proposition
4.2
and with the definition (
4.47
) the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right]\lesssim\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right).

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}\right]\leq\lambda_{\max}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}\right]\lesssim\rho_{h}(M)\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right).

∎

Next, we need to verify that Assumption (
4.24
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}\geq 4C_{1}h^{2s}\lambda_{\ell+1}^{-1}+4\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\quad\text{for all }1\leq\ell\leq L,

holds at least with high probability. To this end, we have the following result:

Proposition 4.3
.

Let the mesh size
h
be sufficiently small and
M
be sufficiently large such that, for all
1\leq\ell\leq L
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{\ell}}{2}\geq 4C_{1}h^{2s}\lambda_{L+1}^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad CM^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\leq\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{16\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)}

(4.52)

holds.
Then we have with probability

\displaystyle p_{0}:=1-2Q_{h}5^{\tau}\exp\left(-M\rho_{1}\left(\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{48\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)}\right)^{2}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}

(4.53)

that the condition (
4.24
) in our spectral gap assumption
4.1
is satisfied.

Proof.

Recall (
4.30
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

Thus, it is sufficient to ensure

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}\geq 4C_{1}h^{2s}\lambda_{\ell+1}^{-1}+4\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\quad\text{for all }1\leq\ell\leq L.

Condition (
4.52
) reduces this to

\displaystyle\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{8\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)}\geq\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}.

We aim to show that this inequality is only violated with small probability.
To this end, we derive by
repeating the arguments of
[
7
, Lemmata 2 & 3]
and
[
7
, Remark 1]
the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}>t\right\}\leq 2Q_{h}5^{\tau}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{9}Mt^{2}\rho_{1}\right)\quad\text{for all }0<t<\rho_{1},

(4.54)

where
\rho_{1}
is a constant, see
[
7
, page 2142]
, and
\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
.
Thus, we obtain with (
4.42
) and by setting
\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}>t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}>t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}+CM^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}>t\right\}.

(4.55)

Now, let
\bar{t}\in(0,\rho_{1})
be fixed and let
M
be large enough such that
CM^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\leq\frac{\bar{t}}{2}
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}>\bar{t}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}>\frac{\bar{t}}{2}\right\}\leq 2Q_{h}5^{\tau}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{36}M\bar{t}^{2}\rho_{1}\right).

(4.56)

Now, we set
\bar{t}:=\frac{1}{8}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}
and observe that the second inequality in condition (
4.52
) ensures

CM^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\bar{t}=\frac{1}{16\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}
.
Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right\|_{2\to 2}>\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{8}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(h;M)}_{\boldsymbol{K}^{(h)}}\right]\right\|_{2\to 2}>\frac{\bar{t}}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2Q_{h}5^{\tau}\exp\left(-M\rho_{1}{\left(\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{48\penalty\ \lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)}\right)^{2}}\right),

(4.57)

using
36*8^{2}=9*2^{8}
and thus
\sqrt{36*8^{2}}=3*2^{4}=48
.
This proves the claim.
∎

This establishes a further central ingredient for our later analysis, namely the optimal bound for the sampling error.

5
Error analysis for covariance operator reconstruction

We are now in the position to derive an overall error bound for the reconstruction of the continuous covariance operator from finite samples. To this end recall the
covariance kernel
R
in (
1.1
), i.e.,

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{x})\kappa(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

Furthermore let
\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}
be given as in (
4.18
) and let

L\in\mathbb{N}
be a truncation parameter with
1\leq L\leq Q_{h}
. We define

\displaystyle R^{(L;h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{\ell}^{(h;M)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

(5.1)

We are now interested in the approximation error
R-R^{(L;h;M)}
.
To this end, let us define

\displaystyle G(L):=\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\ell}}{\delta_{\ell}}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(5.2)

which is a function of the truncation parameter
L
that is determined by spectral properties of the unknown covariance kernel
R
only.

However, in special cases, it is possible to compute
G(L)
explicitly.
As an example, let us consider Brownian motion. In the univariate case we have the kernel

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{B}:(0,1)^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\quad R^{(1)}_{B}(x,x^{\prime}):=\min\{x,x^{\prime}\}.

(5.3)

Then it is known that

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)=\pi^{-2}\left(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-2}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}\left(2\ell-1\right)^{-2}\in\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}(2\mathbb{N}-1)^{-2}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell):=\frac{2\ell}{\pi^{2}(\ell^{2}-\frac{1}{4})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{\ell}{\left(\ell^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}}

and consequently we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}=\frac{2}{\ell}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{2\ell-1}\right)^{2}=2\frac{\left(2\ell+1\right)^{2}}{16\ell}=\frac{\left(2\ell+1\right)^{2}}{8\ell}.

Finally, we get

\displaystyle G^{2}_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(L)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{(2\ell+1)^{4}}{\ell^{2}}\approx L^{3}\quad\text{for }L\text{ large.}

Moreover, for a two-dimensional Brownian motion, we have the eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}):=\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell_{1})\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell_{2})=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{4}(2\ell_{1}-1)^{-2}(2\ell_{2}-1)^{-2},\quad\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, we observe

\displaystyle\text{image}(\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}})=\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{4}(2\mathbb{N}-1)^{-2},

i.e., the image of
\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}
consists up to the pre-factor
\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{4}
again of the squared inverses of the odd numbers.
Hence, we can sort the eigenvalues (ignoring multiplicities) by the ordering in the odd natural numbers. Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(2)}_{B}}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(1)\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}\left(k(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\right),\quad\text{where }k(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}):=\frac{(2\ell_{1}-1)(2\ell_{2}-1)+1}{2}.

(5.4)

In the general
d
-dimensional situation, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell_{j})=\left(\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(1)\right)^{d-1}\lambda^{(1)}(k(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})),

where

\displaystyle k(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}(2\ell_{j}-1)+1\right).

Furthermore, we have with
\ell:=k(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})

\displaystyle\delta_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell)=\left(\lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(1)\right)^{d-1}\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell).

Thus, for the
d

\displaystyle G^{2}_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(L)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\lambda_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(d)}_{B}}(\ell)}\right)^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\ \lambda_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}{\delta_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(\ell)}\right)^{2}=G^{2}_{R^{(1)}_{B}}(L)=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{(2\ell+1)^{4}}{\ell^{2}}\approx L^{3}\quad\text{for }L\text{ large.}

(5.5)

Note here that
G_{R^{d}_{B}}(L)
is now completely independent of the dimension.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{E}_{3;1}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq L^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right]\lesssim L^{1/2}\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)

Next, we define the set, where the assumption
4.1
is violated, as bad and the good set simply as the complement of the bad set, i.e.,

\displaystyle M_{\text{bad}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega:\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{4}<\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right\},

\displaystyle M_{\text{good}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega:\frac{\min_{1\leq\ell\leq L}\delta_{\ell}}{4}\geq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right\}.

\displaystyle\left|M_{\text{bad}}\right|\leq 2Q_{h}5^{\tau}\exp\left(-M\rho_{1}H(L)\lambda_{\max}^{-2}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\right)\quad\text{where}\quad\tau=M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}.

Now, we split the term
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\right]
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\right]=\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in M_{\text{bad}}}\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})+\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in M_{\text{good}}}\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega}),

\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\leq C
holds.
First, we use (

) to get at least

\displaystyle\left\|\phi^{(h)}_{\ell}-\phi^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\phi^{(h)}_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}+2\left\|\phi^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\leq 4.

Moreover, we use again (

\displaystyle\left|\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|\leq\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}-\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right|+\left|\lambda^{(h)}_{\ell}\right|\leq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}+\left|\lambda_{\ell}\right|.

Thus, we obtain for

\displaystyle\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell})^{2}\left\|\phi^{(h)}_{\ell}-\phi^{(h;M)}_{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)}^{2}\leq 4\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(\lambda^{(h;M)}_{\ell})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}+\left|\lambda_{\ell}\right|\right)^{2}\leq 8\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}+8\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left|\lambda_{\ell}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 8L\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|^{2}_{2\to 2}+8L\left|\lambda_{1}\right|^{2}

using

for
a,b\geq 0
.

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in M_{\text{bad}}}\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\leq 2\sqrt{2}\ L^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in M_{\text{bad}}}\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})+2\sqrt{2}\ L^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|M_{\text{bad}}\right|\lambda_{1}.

For the first term, we use (

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in M_{\text{bad}}}\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in M_{\text{bad}}}\left\|{E}_{3;j}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\lesssim 2\sqrt{2}L^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)+2\sqrt{2}\left|M_{\text{bad}}\right|L^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda_{1}.

We are left with estimating the integral of
\left\|E_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}
over the set
M_{\text{good}}
. To this end, we employ the bound (
5.28
), which is only valid on the good set as we have the condition (
5.26
). Recall now

\displaystyle\left\|{E}_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\leq\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}CL^{\frac{1}{2}}+4\sqrt{2}CG(L)\right)\quad\text{on }M_{\text{good}}.

Thus we get

\displaystyle\int_{M_{\text{good}}}\left\|E_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle\leq\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}CL^{\frac{1}{2}}+4\sqrt{2}CG(L)\right)\int_{M_{\text{good}}}\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle\leq\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}CL^{\frac{1}{2}}+4\sqrt{2}CG(L)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h)}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{S}}^{(h;M)}\right\|_{2\to 2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}CL^{\frac{1}{2}}+4\sqrt{2}CG(L)\right)\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right).

and the notation from (

6
Discussion

It now remains to put the result of Theorem 5.2 into context. To this end, let us aim at an error bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\|R-R^{(L,h,M)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\right]\leq c\varepsilon

with a prescribed, fixed accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and a small constant
c
. The question is then: What are the conditions on the discretization parameters
L,h,M
to achieve this aim? For the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves here to the situation
\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx 1
, which is satisfied for instance for any orthonormal basis.

1

For the nodal basis, we would obtain, after proper scaling,
\lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx\lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{(h)}\right)\approx h^{d}
. The resulting discussion for this case is left to the reader.

Moreover, we assume the smoothness assumption (
1.9
), i.e.,
s>d/2
.
We proceed in a term by term fashion as follows:

For the first term in our error bound (
5.2
) we obtain from

directly the condition
L\geq\varepsilon^{-2d/(4s+d)}
.
We also point out that choosing

much larger will destroy the balance of the error contributions. Hence, we will assume

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}:=\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{4s+d}}\rceil.

(6.1)

The coupling (
6.1

in terms of

4.3

4.52

5.15
). We get as sufficient condition on

\displaystyle h^{2s}\lesssim\min\left\{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(L)\lambda_{L+1},\lambda^{2}_{L}\right\}\lesssim\min\left\{\delta_{\ell}\lambda_{L+1},\lambda^{2}_{L}\ :\ 1\leq\ell\leq L\right\}

(6.2)

with the notation (
5.29
).

For the second error term we now assume
G(L)=L^{\tilde{\gamma}}

for some
\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus

\displaystyle L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L)\lesssim L^{\gamma},\quad\text{with}\quad\gamma:=\max\{\frac{1}{2},\tilde{\gamma}\}.

Consequently, we have
(L^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L))\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)\lesssim L^{\gamma}\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h}(M)

\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M)
in (
4.48

In the case

Moreover we get from (
4.48
) the bound
\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M)\lesssim h^{-d}M^{-1}
. We therefore infer the inequality

\displaystyle(L_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}+G(L_{\varepsilon}))\tilde{\rho}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{h_{\varepsilon}}(M_{\varepsilon})\lesssim L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}M_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2(2\alpha+1)}}M_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=M_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}.

Thus, we obtain the sufficient condition

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{\leq}}\varepsilon

(6.5)

which implies an error bound of size
\varepsilon
.
From this condition, we would like to infer a condition on

.
The sufficient condition (

) implies

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}\leq\varepsilon\Leftrightarrow M_{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha}}L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha}},

sufficiently large.
Precisely, we have

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}\gtrsim\max\left\{\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha}}L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha}},\left(\min\left\{H^{\frac{1}{4s}}(L_{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\frac{1}{2s}}_{L_{\varepsilon}+1},\lambda^{\frac{1}{s}}_{L_{\varepsilon}}\right\}\right)^{-d(2\alpha+1)}\right\}.

(6.6)

Furthermore we have for the third error contribution the condition

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}h_{\varepsilon}^{-d}\exp\left(-M_{\varepsilon}\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{\leq}}\varepsilon\Leftrightarrow\exp\left(-M_{\varepsilon}\rho_{1}H(L_{\varepsilon})\right)\leq\varepsilon L_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2}}h_{\varepsilon}^{d}.

) and (

\bar{M}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}

(6.7)

Altogether, to ensure an overall error bound of size
4\varepsilon
in the case
Q_{h}<M^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+1}}
, this yields the sufficient conditions

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{4s+d}}\rceil,

(6.8)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\max\left\{\bar{M}_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha}}L_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma\frac{2\alpha+1}{\alpha}},\left(\min\left\{H^{\frac{1}{4s}}(L_{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\frac{1}{2s}}_{L_{\varepsilon}+1},\lambda^{\frac{1}{s}}_{L_{\varepsilon}}\right\}\right)^{-d(2\alpha+1)}\right\},

(6.9)

\displaystyle h_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\sim\min\left\{M_{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{d(2\alpha+1)}},h_{0}\right\},

stems from Proposition

\tilde{\rho}_{h}(M)

i.e., we have no lower bound for

\varepsilon

Corollary 6.1
.

Fix an accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and choose
L_{\varepsilon}=\lceil\varepsilon^{-\frac{2d}{4s+d}}\rceil
.
Then, we the choose
M_{\varepsilon}
according to (

), (

) and (

). Then, we choose

) or as maximal value of the potential intervals in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{R}-R^{(L;h;M)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D})}\right]\leq 3\varepsilon

Note at this point that a practical goal is to select

as large as possible and
M_{\varepsilon}
as small as possible and to still ensure an error level of size

.
Hence, we advocate to stick to the second case, where a reasonable choice as in (

) and furthermore for

as in (

) is possible. This leads naturally to the tapering estimator.

) and (
5

7
Concluding remarks

We discussed the problem to recover approximately the covariance of a Gaussian random field from a finite number of discretized observations. To this end, we coupled recent sharp estimates on the eigenvalue decay of continuous covariance operators with optimal statistical (tapering) estimators for covariance matrices. The combination of these techniques additionally involved a finite element discretization which made all operators finite rank and made our approach feasible.

We provided new and sharp error estimates in
expectation for the reconstruction of the full covariance operator taking the number of samples, the finite element discretization and the truncation of the Karhunen Loève expansion of the covariance operator and thus its regularity into account.

Note that in contrast to most analytical approaches, we do not need to have access to the continuous covariance function. We instead reconstruct the covariance operator from a finite number of discrete samples. This makes our approach feasible when only measurements of the random coefficients are available. Such a situation is indeed often encountered in many practical problems in uncertainty quantification and machine learning.
