Sunyaev-Zeldovich Signals from L ∗ L^{*} Galaxies: Observations, Analytics, and Simulations
Yossi Oren, Amiel Sternberg, Christopher F. McKee, Yakov Faerman, Shy Genel

Abstract
We analyze measurements of the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) effect arising in the circumgalactic medium (CGM) of L ∗ L^{*} galaxies, reported by Bregman et al. 2022 and Das et al. 2023 . In our analysis we use the Faerman et al. 2017 ; Faerman et al. 2020 CGM models, a new power-law model (PLM), and the TNG100 simulation. For a given M vir M_{\rm vir} , our PLM has four parameters; the fraction, f hCGM f_{\rm hCGM} , of the halo baryon mass in hot CGM gas, the ratio, ϕ T \phi_{T} , of the actual gas temperature at the virial radius to the virial temperature, and the power-law indices, a P , th a_{P,{\rm th}} and a n a_{n} for the thermal electron pressure and the hydrogen nucleon density. The 13 Compton- y y profile implies steep electron pressure slopes ( a P , th ≃ 2 a_{P,{\rm th}}\simeq 2 ). For isothermal conditions the temperature is at least 1.1 × 10 6 1.1\times 10^{6} K, with a hot CGM gas mass of up to 3.5 × 10 11 3.5\times 10^{11} M ⊙ for a virial mass of 2.75 × 10 12 2.75\times 10^{12} M ⊙ . However, if isothermal the gas must be expanding out of the halos. An isentropic equation of state is favored for which hydrostatic equilibrium is possible. The 13 and 19 results are consistent with each other and with recent (0.5-2 keV) CGM X-ray observations ( Zhang et al. 2024 ) of Milky Way mass systems. For M vir ≃ 3 × 10 12 M_{\rm vir}\simeq 3\times 10^{12} M ⊙ , the scaled Compton pressure integrals, E ( z ) − 2 / 3 Y 500 / M vir , 12 5 / 3 E(z)^{-2/3}Y_{500}/M_{\rm vir,12}^{5/3} , lie in the narrow range, 2.5 × 10 − 4 2.5\times 10^{-4} to 5.0 × 10 − 4 5.0\times 10^{-4} kpc 2 , for all three sets of observations. TNG100 underpredicts the tSZ parameters by factors ∼ 0.5 \sim 0.5 dex for the L ∗ L^{*} galaxies, suggesting that the feedback strengths and CGM gas losses are overestimated in the simulated halos at these mass scales.

Introduction

(

et al. 2013

et al. 2015

II
The FSM Models

25
and
26

presented two analytic phenomenological (forward) models for the
hot gas in the
CGM around star-forming galaxies at the mass scale of the Milky Way. In these models we approximated the halo virial mass,
M_{\rm vir}
, as
M_{100}
, i.e. the mass within which the mean over-density is 100, since
Bryan &
Norman 1998
found this is very close to the density of a virialized halo at redshift
z=0
. For the Milky Way we adopted
M_{\rm vir}=M_{100}=1\times 10^{12}
M
⊙
. We note that
Posti &
Helmi 2019
found
M_{\rm vir}=(1.3\pm 0.3)\times 10^{12}
M
⊙
for the Milky Way.

The basic simplifying assumption of the FSM models is that the initially hot CGM gas is in hydrostatic equilibrium (HSE) in the dominating gravitational potential of the dark-matter halo and stellar disk. (The CGM gas is not self-gravitating.)
In the FSM models the CGM extends from an inner radius,
r_{0}=8.5
kpc, comparable to twice the half-mass radius,
r_{e}
, of the Milky Way stellar disk
(
Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016
)
, out to near the virial radius.
The modeled properties include the hot CGM gas mass, its spatial distribution, and the temperatures, metallicities,
dispersion measures
, and ionization states of the gas. In addition to thermal pressure, there is (kinetic) pressure associated with turbulent motions, and non-thermal pressure due possibly to cosmic-rays and magnetic fields, all of which support the gas against gravity. The focus is on

T\gtrsim 10^{5}
K gas, near to the characteristic virial temperature, and as constrained by UV/Xray spectral line absorptions and emissions of highly ionized species, mainly OVI, OVII, and OVIII, in the Galactic halo and around other galaxies.

In any model (or simulation), a major challenge is to account for the simultaneous presence of the multiple ionization states observed within the CGM volume
(
Faerman &
Werk 2023
)
. In
25
we developed a model in which hot gas with
a constant volume averaged mean temperature of
1.5\times 10^{6}
K
is hydrostatically supported, and gives rise to collisionally ionized OVII and OVIII. A separate

\sim 3.0\times 10^{5}
K OVI component then condenses out of the hot gas via cooling instabilities in turbulent, isobaric density fluctuations. The initial hot gas component is
in HSE
at a constant mean temperature throughout, and we refer to
25
as our “isothermal model”. Following the cooling out of the OVI component the thermal structure of the remaining hot gas is altered slightly, and it is no longer precisely isothermal.
Furthermore, the OVI component may not be isothermal nor is it precisely in HSE.

In the fiducial
25
model the hot CGM gas mass,
M_{\rm hCGM}=1.2\times 10^{11}
M
⊙
within the virial radius, corresponding to a hot gas fraction
f_{\rm hCGM}=0.77
relative to the halo cosmic baryon budget of
1.56\times 10^{11}
M
⊙
for a virial mass of
10^{12}
M
⊙
. For the Milky Way mass of
\sim 6.3\times 10^{10}
M
⊙

(
Licquia &
Newman 2016
)
, including stellar disk, bulge, and ISM, the fiducial
25
model exceeds the cosmic baryon budget by
\sim 20
%.

In
26
we presented an alternate model in which we assumed a barotropic equation of state for the CGM gas. We refer to
26
as our “isentropic model”. We assumed that accreting intergalactic gas is heated by the virial shock to temperatures
\sim 3\times 10^{5}
K as appropriate for a
10^{12}
M
⊙
halo, and close to the OVI production peak for collisional ionization. The
Ansatz
is that feedback heating (by AGN and/or stellar feedback) drives the CGM gas to a convective equilibrium at constant entropy. We again assumed hydrostatic balance, with the (necessary) inclusion of non-thermal cosmic-ray and magnetic support. Due to the adiabatic compression at small radii, the gas is hotter closer to the inner galaxy. In this picture, the OVI extends out to the virial radius, as observed (and as in our isothermal model) but the OVII and OVIII, for which the Milky Way spatial distributions are as yet unknown, are instead produced in the inner parts of the CGM. In
26
(isentropic) the CGM is a single adiabatic structure with a temperature gradient. In
25
(isothermal) the
OVI gas reflects a thermal instability and is fully mixed within the hotter OVII/OVIII medium.
In
26
we also included a radially declining metallicity distribution to account for possible enrichment by stellar winds and supernovae.
In
26
the outer boundary of the CGM is set at 1.1 times the virial radius. The inferred hot gas mass within the virial radius is
M_{\rm hCGM}=4.6\times 10^{10}
M
⊙
, or
f_{\rm hGCM}=0.29
,

a factor
\approx 2
lower than in
25
.
In
26
metagalactic photoionization of the oxygen ions plays a role, in addition to collisional ionization, because the gas densities are somewhat lower, as low as
\sim 10^{-5}
cm
-3
near the virial radius.

For the Milky Way, and for similar star-forming
L^{*}
galaxies, the
25
and
26
hot CGM gas masses are sufficient to account for the “missing baryons” in such galaxies, and to power star-formation for longer than a Hubble time. The overall CGM gas metallicity must be high,
\gtrsim 0.5
solar, indicative of significant enrichment.

An important finding of the FSM models is that the resulting hydrostatic CGM pressure profiles within the dark-matter halos are well approximated by simple radial power-laws,
from the assumed inner boundary at
8.5
kpc
near to the central galaxy, through the entire CGM, out to the virial radius.
Both the total hydrostatic pressures and just the thermal pressures alone are well represented by power-laws in the FSM models.
This behavior motivates the power-law model that we now describe.

III
Power-Law Model

III.1
Pressure and density profiles

The thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) distortions produced by the CGM of a galaxy depend primarily on the electron pressure profiles of the hot gas responsible for the Compton scattering
(
Sunyaev &
Zeldovich 1972
;
Reid &
Spergel 2006
;
Nagai
et al. 2007
;
Arnaud
et al. 2010
;
Battaglia et al. 2012
)
.
In our power-law model (PLM)
we represent the hot component of the CGM, and the associated tSZ Compton parameters, assuming power-law distributions for the thermal electron pressures and

gas
densities. Our PLM is motivated by our more detailed and observationally based FSM analytics, as well as the TNG100 cosmological simulations, as we discuss in §
VII
.

For a galaxy halo with virial mass
M_{\rm vir}
, the virial radius at any redshift
z
is

\begin{split}R_{\rm vir}\ &\equiv\ \bigl(\frac{4\pi}{3}\Delta\rho_{c}\bigr)^{-1/3}M_{\rm vir}^{1/3}\\
&=\ 267\ \Bigl(\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{10^{12}\ {\rm M}_{\odot}}\Bigr)^{1/3}E^{-2/3}(z)\ {\rm kpc}\ \ \ ,\end{split}

(1)

where
\rho_{c}
is the cosmological critical density, and
\Delta
is the halo over-density factor. For the
standard
\Lambda
CDM cosmology
(
Planck
Collaboration et al. 2020a
)

\rho_{c,0}=8.53\times 10^{-30}\ {\rm g~cm^{-3}}
at redshift
z=0
. The factor
E^{2}(z)\equiv\rho_{c}/\rho_{c,0}=\Omega_{\rm m}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}
accounts for the increase in the critical density with redshift. We assume an overdensity factor
\Delta=100
, which is appropriate for virialized halos at
z=0

(
Bryan &
Norman 1998
)
.

For the hot gas electron pressure we write

\frac{P_{e}(r)}{k_{\rm B}}=x_{e}n_{{\rm H,vir}}T(R_{\rm vir})\left(\frac{r}{R_{\rm vir}}\right)^{-a_{P,{\rm th}}}

(2)

where
r
is the radial distance from the central galaxy,

x_{e}\equiv n_{e}/n_{\rm H}
is the electron abundance,
n_{\rm H}
is the hydrogen gas density,
n_{\rm H,vir}
and
T(R_{\rm vir})
are the hot gas hydrogen density and temperature at
R_{\rm vir}
, and
k_{\rm B}
is the Boltzmann constant.
In this expression,
a_{P,{\rm th}}
is the power-law index for the thermal pressure, and it is a basic free parameter.
We assume a constant metallicity throughout, with a helium abundance,
n_{\rm He}/n_{\rm H}=0.085

Asplund et al. 2009
. For a fully ionized gas,
x_{e}=1.17
.

Following
26
, we define the characteristic virial temperature of the halo as

\begin{split}T_{\rm vir}&\ \equiv\ \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{G{\bar{m}}}{k_{\rm B}}\right)\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{R_{\rm vir}},\\
&=\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\Delta\rho_{c}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{G{\bar{m}}}{3k_{\rm B}}\right)M_{\rm vir}^{2/3},\\
&=3.90\times 10^{5}\Bigl(\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{10^{12}\ {\rm M}_{\odot}}\Bigr)^{2/3}E^{2/3}(z)\ \ {\rm K}\\
&=3.90\times 10^{5}\Bigl(\frac{R_{\rm vir}}{267\ {\rm kpc}}\Bigr)^{2}E^{2}(z)\ \ \ {\rm K}\ ,\end{split}

(3)

where
{\bar{m}}=0.60m_{p}=1.00\times 10^{-24}
g is the mean mass per gas particle (ions plus electrons),

m_{p}
is the proton mass, and
G
is the gravitational constant. The virial temperature is proportional to the surface area of the dark matter halo.

In the FSM models and TNG100 simulations, as well as for our PLM representations, the actual gas temperatures at the virial radii,
T({R_{\rm vir}})
, are not necessarily equal to the characteristic virial temperatures
T_{\rm vir}
of the halos.
We define the ratio of these temperatures as the parameter
\phi_{T}
,

T(R_{\rm vir})\equiv\phi_{T}T_{\rm vir}\ \ \ .

(4)

We express the total hot CGM gas mass within the virial radius as a fraction
f_{\rm hCGM}
(nominally 50%) of the total baryonic mass within the halo–i.e.,

\begin{split}M_{\rm hCGM,vir}&=f_{\rm hCGM}\left(\frac{\Omega_{b}}{\Omega_{m}}\right)M_{\rm vir}\\
&=7.80\times 10^{10}\ \left(\frac{f_{\rm hCGM}}{0.5}\right)\Bigl(\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{10^{12}\ {\rm M}_{\odot}}\Bigr)\ \ {\rm M_{\odot}}\ \ \ ,\end{split}

(5)

where
{\Omega_{b}}/{\Omega_{m}}=0.156

(
Planck
Collaboration et al. 2020a
)

is the cosmic baryon fraction relative to the total matter content (dark plus baryonic).

We also assume that the gas density alone obeys a power-law distribution,

n_{\rm H}(r)\ =\ n_{\rm H,vir}\Bigl(\frac{r}{R_{\rm vir}}\Bigr)^{-a_{n}}\ \ \ ,

(6)

so that for
a_{n}<3
the enclosed hot CGM gas mass is

M_{\rm h}(r)\ =\ \Bigl(\frac{r}{R_{\rm vir}}\Bigr)^{3-a_{n}}M_{\rm hCGM,vir}\ \ \ .

(7)

Together with equations (
2
) and (
4
), the gas temperature is then

T(r)\ =\ \phi_{T}T_{\rm vir}\Bigl(\frac{r}{R_{\rm vir}}\Bigr)^{-a_{T}}\ \ \ ,

(8)

with
a_{T}=a_{P,{\rm th}}-a_{n}
.
If
{\bar{n}_{\rm H}}
is the mean
hot
CGM gas density within the halo, then

\begin{split}n_{\rm H,vir}\ &=\ \left(\frac{3-a_{n}}{3}\right){\bar{n}_{\rm H}}\\
&=\left(\frac{3-a_{n}}{3}\right)f_{\rm hCGM}{\frac{\Omega_{b}}{\Omega_{m}}}\frac{\Delta\rho_{c}}{\mu_{\rm H}}\\
&=2.9\times 10^{-5}E^{2}(z)\left(\frac{3-a_{n}}{3}\right)\frac{f_{\rm hCGM}}{0.5}\ \ {\rm cm}^{-3}\ \ \ .\end{split}

(9)

Here
\mu_{\rm H}=1.37m_{p}=2.29\times 10^{-24}
g is the mean particle mass per hydrogen nucleus.
It is also convenient to express
n_{{\rm H,{\rm vir}}}
in terms of
M_{\rm hGGM,vir}
:

n_{\rm{H,{\rm vir}}}=\frac{(3-a_{n})M_{\rm hGGM,vir}}{4\pi\mu_{\rm H}R_{\rm vir}^{3}}\ \ \ .

(10)

For a finite
M_{\rm hCGM}
we must have
a_{n}<3
.
For a given hot CGM mass fraction the mean hot gas density,

{\bar{n}_{\rm H}}
, is independent of the virial mass, and is a constant proportional to the universal virial overdensity
\Delta\rho_{c}
.
For example, for
f_{\rm hCGM}=0.5
,
{\bar{n}_{\rm H}}=2.9\times 10^{-5}E^{2}(z)
cm
-3
from equation (

III.2
Hydrostatic Equilibrium

The FSM models assume
hydrostatic equilibrium (HSE), but with the inclusion of non-thermal components. In these models the resulting thermal pressure profiles are well approximated by power-laws
(as we discuss in §
V
)
. However, an arbitrary power-law thermal pressure profile alone is not necessarily in HSE within a given gravitational potential. The degree to which just thermal pressure can maintain HSE is measured by the ratio of the gravitational force to the pressure force,

\eta\equiv\frac{\rho g}{dP_{\rm th}/dr},

(12)

where
\rho=\mu_{\rm H}n_{\rm H}(r)
is the gas density,

P_{\rm th}
is the thermal pressure
, and the gravitational acceleration
g=-GM(r)/r^{2}
where
M(r)
is the total gravitating mass within
r
. In other words,
\eta
is the ratio of the pressure gradient required for HSE to the actual thermal pressure gradient. If
\eta>1
additional pressure components must be present to maintain HSE. If
\eta<1
the large thermal pressure gradients alone will cause the system to expand.

For a power-law pressure profile,
P_{\rm th}\propto r^{-a_{P,{\rm th}}}
, the HSE parameter at the virial radius is

\eta_{\rm vir}=\frac{3}{a_{P,{\rm th}}\phi_{T}}

(13)

with the aid of Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
).
As just noted, if
\eta_{\rm vir}>1
, additional pressure components must be present to maintain HSE. If
\eta_{\rm vir}<1
, thermal pressure gradients will cause the system to expand.

VI.2
HSE?

Is HSE possible for our best fitting PLM? First, for HSE, and with the inclusion of non-thermal pressure,
\eta_{\rm vir}=3/(\phi_{T}a_{P,{\rm th}})\geq 1
(Eq. [
13
]). For our best-fitting
a_{P,{\rm th}}=2.03
we obtain the
upper
limit
\phi_{T}\leq 1.48
. For a larger
\phi_{T}
the thermal pressure alone is too large for HSE and the gas must expand. Thus, for the
13
interpretation (isothermal,
a_{P,{\rm th}}=1.8
, and
\phi_{T}=3.9
) the hot tSZ gas cannot be in HSE.

Second, as given by Equation (
33
),
\phi_{T}^{*}\leq 2\phi_{T}f_{\rm hCGM,max}(3-a_{n})
, where
f_{\rm hCGM,max}
is the maximal hot gas fraction. Our best-fitting
\phi_{T}^{*}=2.39
then gives the
lower
limit
\phi_{T}\geq 1.20/[f_{\rm hCGM,max}(3-a_{n})]
. For the
13
stack, we estimate
f_{\rm hCGM,max}=0.83
, if cool and intermediate gas is negligible with
f_{\rm cCGM}=0
. This follows from equation (
14
) assuming a stack stellar mass
\log M_{*}=10.83
, plus an additional
\sim 10\%
for gas within the ISM of the stellar disks, and with
M_{\rm vir,12}=2.75
. We then obtain the lower limits
\phi_{T}\geq 1.48
for isothermal gas
(a_{n}=2.03)
or
\phi_{T}\geq 0.81
for isentropic gas
(a_{n}=1.22)
.
For
\phi_{T}
below these limits the gas is too cool to produce the observed Compton-
y
profile even for the maximal CGM gas mass.

Together with our upper limit
\phi_{T}\leq 1.48
we conclude that HSE is possible for isentropic gas, but only marginally if the hot gas is isothermal. With just a small inclusion of cool and intermediate temperature gas,
f_{\rm hCGM,max}<0.83
and isothermal HSE is not possible for the hot gas.
Thus, stable isothermal conditions appear unlikely for the
13
stack for any temperature. For isentropic gas, HSE is possible for hot gas fractions
f_{\rm hCGM}
between 0.45 and 0.83, corresponding to
\phi_{T}
between 1.48 and 0.81,
\eta_{\rm vir}
between 1 and 1.8, and
T(R_{\rm vir})
between
1.1\times 10^{6}
and
6.2\times 10^{5}
K. Nonthermal pressure components would be needed to maintain HSE for
\eta_{\rm vir}>1
. It is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss such pressure components here. However, this analysis suggests that the tSZ observations favor isentropic conditions, as we now argue in comparing our FSM models directly to the
13
data. Our
13
PLM parameters for the isothermal and isentropic options for which HSE is possible are summarized in Table
2
.

VII
tSZ in TNG100

We now turn to cosmological galaxy evolution simulations, and for this purpose we make use of IllustrisTNG. We postprocess the simulation outputs to compute line-of-sight Compton-
y
maps and
Y
pressure integrals for a large set of galaxy halos spanning a wide range of virial masses.
In §
VIII
we compare the simulation results to the tSZ observations, and to our FSM models.

We focus on the intermediate resolution TNG100 simulation. Briefly, for TNG100 the hydrodynamics box size is 75
h^{-1}
Mpc (co-moving) on a side, and includes
2\times 1820^{3}
resolution elements, with a spatial resolution of
\gtrsim 1
kpc, a baryonic mass resolution of
1.4\times 10^{6}
M
\odot
, and a dark matter mass resolution of
7.5\times 10^{6}
M
\odot
. By redshift
z=0
over 4 million dark matter halos (FOF groups) have formed within the large scale structure, spanning
2\times 10^{8}<M_{\rm vir}/{\rm M}_{\odot}<4\times 10^{14}
. The simulation follows five types of particles: gas, dark matter, star/wind particles, black holes, and passive tracers. The gas particles in particular are relevant for the tSZ parameters that we compute.

The TNG100 simulation is a powerful tool for analyzing the intergalactic medium and the CGM around individual galaxies. Its implementation of various feedback mechanisms, via AGN and stellar winds, models for chemical enrichment, and tracking of gas heating and cooling enable it to emulate the complex environment that comprises the CGM. The large box size and large number of halos enable exploration of the dynamic and complicated evolutionary processes involving inflows from the IGM, outflows from the ISM, heating and cooling, and evolution of the multiphase gas itself over time.

VII.2
CGM gas and thermal fractions

To compute the Compton-
y
and

For each halo
we construct line-of-sight

10

II

As we demonstrate below the hot component (as we have defined it) dominates the production of the

There is a noticeable dip in the gas fraction, primarily in the dominant hot gas component, at

epsecially for the quenched galaxies. This feature is associated with the onset of kinetic AGN feedback. In the TNG100 subgrid model, younger, lower mass AGN produce feedback as pure thermal energy that heats the surrounding gas and lowers star formation. Weinberger
et al. 2017

et al. 2019b

This accounts for the difference between the CGM gas fractions in halos surrounding star forming galaxies compared to the fractions in halos surrounding quenched galaxies.

IX
Discussion and Summary

Our objective is to develop physically motivated models for the gaseous circumgalactic medium (CGM) of star-forming
L^{*}
galaxies that are consistent with observations of the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ) effect.

In this paper we analyze recent observations, reported by
Bregman et al. 2022
and
Das
et al. 2023
(
13
and
19
), of tSZ cosmic-microwave-background (CMB) distortion signals arising in the CGM surrounding
L^{*}
galaxies, or at virial dark-matter halo mass scales down to
10^{12}
M
⊙
. Both observational studies make use of galaxy stacks to detect the tSZ signatures, and to extract the line-of-sight Compton-
y
parameters and
Y
pressure integrals that are the basic measures of the tSZ effect. For the CGM of an individual galaxy with virial mass
M_{\rm vir}\sim 3\times 10^{12}
M
⊙
,
13
and
19
report characteristic (1-halo) Compton-
y
parameters (or CMB distortions
\Delta T/T)
of order a few times 10
-8
. It is remarkable that such small tSZ signals can now be detected. These measurements provide new constraints on the properties of the hot Compton-scattering gas and the CGM baryon content and distributions for individual galaxies at Milky Way mass scales. We compare the tSZ measurements to recent (0.5-2 keV) X-ray observations of galaxies at these mass scales
Zhang
et al. 2024
(
88
) and show that they are consistent with the tSZ measurements.

We analyze the tSZ observations using three interrelated theoretical tools. First are the phenomenological
Faerman
et al. 2017
and
Faerman
et al. 2020
hydrostatic equilibrium models for the hot CGM around Milky Way type galaxies. These models reproduce UV observations of OVI absorption lines in the COS-Halos sample of galaxies and X-ray observations of OVII and OVIII absorption in the Milky Way. Second is an analytic power-law model (PLM) for the thermal pressures and density distributions for the hot CGM gas, with analytic expressions for the radially dependent Compton-
y
and
Y
profiles. Third is the TNG100 cosmological galaxy evolution hydrodynamics simulation, which we use to construct Compton-
y
maps and to compute
Y
pressure integrals for a subset of the simulated halos with a
wide range of virial masses.

In §
II
we review the FSM models, dubbed “
25
isothermal” and “
26
isentropic,”
where we also introduce a gas-rich version of the isentropic model that we name FSM20-maximal. These three models provide specific predictions for the thermal electron pressures, and the Compton-
y
and
Y
integrals, for halos with virial mass
M_{\rm vir}=M_{100}=1.0\times 10^{12}
M
⊙
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. FSM20-maximal is an “upper-limit” isentropic model: It contains a cosmologically maximal amount of hot halo gas to enhance the tSZ signals, but with little room for any cool CGM gas.

An important property of the FSM models is that the resulting hydrostatic thermal electron pressure profiles vary approximately as simple radial power-laws within the CGM spheres. This motivates our power-law model described in §
III.1
. The PLM is for the hot CGM component, and can be used to describe the FSM models, the hot gas distributions in the TNG100 halos, and the observations. For a given
M_{\rm vir}
, the PLM structures depend on the hot gas mass fraction,
f_{\rm hCGM}
, relative to the expected cosmic baryon mass within the halo; the ratio
\phi_{T}\equiv T(R_{\rm vir})/T_{\rm vir}
of the actual gas temperature at the virial radius,
T(R_{\rm vir})
, to the halo virial temperature
T_{\rm vir}
; and the power-law indices,
a_{P,{\rm th}}
and
a_{n}
, for the radially dependent electron pressures and hot hydrogen gas densities through the halo out to the virial radii. We focus on isothermal (
a_{n}=a_{P,{\rm th}}
) or isentropic (
a_{n}=3a_{P,{\rm th}}/5)
options. A given thermal gas pressure profile may or may not be in hydrostatic equilibrium (HSE) within the gravitational potential of the parent halo, and we state a condition for HSE in terms of
a_{P,{\rm th}}
and
\phi_{T}
.

Alternate analytic models for the CGM may also be well represented by PLMs
(
Singh et al. 2024
, see Fig. 2 of)
. This includes cooling flows
(
Stern et al. 2019
)
, precipitation models
(
Sharma et al. 2012
;
Voit 2019
)
and baryon pasting models
(
Ostriker
et al. 2005
;
Osato &
Nagai 2023
)
.

In §
III.3
we define and discuss observational constraints on the cool (

The

Our focus in this paper has been analysis of tSZ observations from the

in the CGM of
L^{*}

Simultaneous tSZ and UV/Xray emission/absorption line observations of the CGM around individual galaxies would be very valuable, and together enable improved constraints on the gas metallicities, temperatures, and mass distributions.
It is satisfying that three independent data sets,
13
and
19
for the tSZ effect, and
88

The data are well described by our FSM models that also account for the high-ionization oxygen absorbers and emitters. Our PLM formalism provides a simple way to parameterize observations and simulation outputs, and will be useful in analyzing future tSZ observations to ever lower galaxy masses.

Appendix B
What if the CGM extends beyond the virial radius?

In our PLM, we consider the virial radius,
R_{\rm vir}
to be the limit of the CGM, and assume that there is no contribution to the Compton-
y
parameter from outside of it. However, the definition of
R_{\rm vir}
can vary between works, e.g.
Das
et al. 2023
, who used an overdensity
\Delta=200
when defining their virial masses. Even the very assumption that the CGM does not extend beyond the virial radius has been tackled. For example,
26
take the CGM radius to be
1.1R_{{\rm vir}}
, motivated by the O VI measurements from the eCGM survey
(
Johnson
et al. 2015
)
.

To account for the possibility of the CGM not being limited to the virial radius, we expand on our derivation in §
III
. Our CGM virial mass (Eq. [
5
]), temperature (Eq. [
3
]), and hydrogen density (Eq.[
9
]) are all defined based on their properties at the virial radius, and remain unaltered. The total CGM mass in the PLM that now extends to
R_{\rm CGM}
becomes:

M_{\rm CGM}=\left(\frac{R_{\rm CGM}}{R_{\rm vir}}\right)^{3-a_{n}}M_{\rm CGM,vir}.

(B1)

We define our new impact parameter relative to the CGM radius instead of the virial radius:

b_{\rm CGM}\equiv\frac{r_{\perp}}{R_{\rm CGM}}.

(B2)

The Compton-
y
parameter is generalized to:

y_{\rm CGM}(b_{\rm CGM})=\left(\frac{R_{\rm CGM}}{R_{\rm vir}}\right)^{1-a_{P,{\rm th}}}y(b_{\rm CGM}),

(B3)

where
y(b_{\rm CGM})
is given by equation (
15
) with

b
replaced by
b_{\rm CGM}
.
Note that the right-hand side of this equation varies as

\left(\frac{R_{\rm CGM}}{R_{\rm vir}}\right)^{1-a_{P,{\rm th}}}\left(\frac{r_{\perp}}{R_{\rm CGM}}\right)^{1-a_{P,{\rm th}}}I_{y,a_{P,\rm th}}\left(\frac{r_{\perp}}{R_{\rm CGM}}\right),

(B4)

which increases monotonically with
R_{\rm CGM}
since it cancels in the first two factors and
I_{y}(b)
increases monotonically as
b
decreases.

The spherical
{Y}(r)

is unaffected by the change from
R_{\rm vir}
to
R_{\rm CGM}
:

Y_{\rm CGM}(r)=Y(r)=Y_{\rm vir}\Bigl(\frac{r}{R_{\rm vir}}\Bigr)^{3-a_{P,{\rm th}}}~~~~~(r\leq R_{\rm CGM}),

(B5)

except that now the radius can extend to
R_{\rm CGM}\neq R_{\rm vir}
.
For the projected
{Y}_{\perp}(r_{\perp})
, we obtain:

Y_{\perp,{\rm CGM}}(b_{\rm CGM})=\left(\frac{R_{\rm CGM}}{R_{\rm vir}}\right)^{3-a_{P,{\rm th}}}Y_{\perp}(b_{\rm CGM}),

(B6)

where
Y_{\perp}
is given by equation (
22
).
For example,
26
adopted
R_{\rm CGM}=1.1R_{\rm vir}
so that
b_{\rm CGM}=0.91b
in the above expressions.
