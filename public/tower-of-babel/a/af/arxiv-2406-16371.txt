Introduction

Let
X
be a compact metric space and for
1\leq i\leq k
,
f_{i}:X\rightarrow X
a continuous map.
Let
\Sigma_{k}
be the full shift on
k
symbols and let
\mathcal{L}(\Sigma_{k})=\{u:u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\text{ is a word in }\Sigma_{k},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
its language. Then under the concatenation of words,

\mathcal{L}(\Sigma_{k})
is a semi-group and the action of

\mathcal{L}(\Sigma_{k})
on
X
defined by

(u_{1}\cdots u_{n},\,x)\mapsto f_{u}(x):=f_{u_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{u_{1}}(x)

(1.1)

is a topological dynamical system called a
“conventional” iterated function system
(IFS).
The IFS was first introduced by M. Barnsley and S. Demko for the scenario where
f_{i}
’s were contractions and
\Sigma
a full shift on
k
symbols
[
2
]
. Building upon Hutchinson’s earlier findings
[
10
]
, their goal was to devise a technique for generating fractals, or sets with non-integer Hausdorff dimensions. This concept gained prominence through the work of Peitgen and Richter
[
14
]
and Barnsley et al.
[
3
]
; notably, they produced digital images that evoke natural phenomena.
Now, many researchers believe that IFS has the potential to give mathematical models for some elements in nature. See for instance
[
8
]
and
[
12
]
.
These beliefs stem from the fact that chance plays a major role in nature and an IFS.
So it is reasonable to study IFS from different viewpoints;
namely, probabilistic, geometric-measure theory, and topological.
Here, we will focus primarily on topological aspects and defer the study of ergodic behavior for future research.

For us, an IFS is a triple
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
where
\mathcal{F}
is the
set of above
k
self-maps
that, unless otherwise specified, are contractions, and

\Sigma
is an arbitrary sub-shift on
k
symbols satisfying (
1.1
). This seems natural; as one would anticipate that in general, certain sequences of map iterations are forbidden, so leading to a generic sub-shift.
Note that under this framework,
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma_{k})
represents the conventional IFS.

For a conventional IFS, Hutchinson utilized the Hausdorff metrics on

\mathcal{C}=\{A\subseteq X:\;A\text{ is compact}\}

and introduced
Hutchinson operator

H:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
defined as

H(A):=\bigcup_{a=1}^{k}f_{a}(A)
.
Consequently,

H^{n}(A)=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma_{k})}f_{u}(A)

(1.2)

holds true, where

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma_{k})\subset\mathcal{L}(\Sigma_{k})
denotes the collection of words with length
n
and
H^{n}(\cdot)
is the
n
th iteration of
H
.
Subsequently, it was established that there exists
S\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\lim_{n}H^{n}(A)=S
,
identifying this distinctive compact set as the
attractor
of the IFS.
Additionally, the relation

H^{n}(S)=S

(1.3)

was validated for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
f_{i}
is a contractive homeomorphism,
then (
1.3
) means that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
S
has a self-similarity; that is,
S
consists of
|\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma_{k})|=k^{n}
parts all topologically similar to
S
. We call (

.

In this note, we consider
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
stands for an arbitrary sub-shift on a character set
\mathcal{A}
such that if not specified
|\mathcal{A}|=k\geq 2
.

By substituting
\Sigma
with
\Sigma_{k}
in (
1.2
), we define

H^{n}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, which is no longer the iteration of
H
as mentioned earlier.
In general, (
1.3
) loses its validity.
However, we demonstrate that if
\Sigma
is a coded sub-shift, that is, a sub-shift generated by a set of words, then
(
1.3
) holds
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore, if
\Sigma
is mixing, then for sufficiently large

,
(
1.3
) is valid.
This validity will apply to all

n
, if our
coded
\Sigma

possesses a specified fixed point.
Given the infinite number of subsystems of
\Sigma_{k}
, that are coded, in cases where

f_{u}
’s are contractive homeomorphisms,
there exists an infinite array of self-similarities within the attractor of a an IFS. This offers a basic understanding of why numerous distinct similarities emerge from a set of finite forces
\mathcal{F}
acting on a space like our universe.

Assuming (
1.3
) holds for all

in our
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
with the attractor

, then
\mathfrak{I}_{S}=(S,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
becomes an IFS as well, inheriting many dynamical properties from
\Sigma
. Moreover, for any

, define
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
as the set of characters in
\sigma
and
\Sigma_{\sigma}
as the subsystem of
\Sigma
obtained from the closure of points in the orbit of
\sigma
.
Then, we consider
\mathfrak{I}_{\sigma}=(X,\,\mathcal{F}_{\sigma},\,\Sigma_{\sigma})
with an attracting set
S_{\sigma}
and the non-autonomous system
f_{1,\,\infty}
with
f_{i}=f_{\sigma_{i}}
and will demonstrate that these two systems share dynamics with

2
Preliminaries

A dynamical system represents an action of a semi-group on a set. However, our focus here is on the ”classical” topological dynamical system, where
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
acts on a compact metric space.
Our ”non-classical” dynamical systems include iterated function systems and non-autonomous dynamical systems, which will be discussed later. To be precise, consider a compact metric space
X
with a continuous map
f:X\rightarrow X
. Let
G\in\{\mathbb{N},\,\mathbb{Z}\}
and define
G\times X\rightarrow X
as

(n,\,x)\mapsto f^{n}(x):=\overbrace{f\circ f\circ\cdots\circ f}^{n\text{ times}}(x)
.
The
forward orbit
of
x\in X
is

\{x,\,f(x),\ldots,\,f^{n}(x),\ldots\}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{O}^{+}(x)
.
If
f
is a homeomorphism, then
\mathcal{O}_{-}(x)
, the
backward orbit
of
x
, is defined similarly, and
\mathcal{O}^{+}_{-}(x)=\mathcal{O}^{+}(x)\cup\mathcal{O}_{-}(x)
represents the
full orbit
of
x
.
Let
\mathcal{O}(x)
denote either
\mathcal{O}^{+}(x)
or
\mathcal{O}^{+}_{-}(x)
depending on whether
G=\mathbb{N}
or
G=\mathbb{Z}
respectively.

2.1
Symbolic dynamics

We require symbolic dynamics. Therefore, we revisit certain necessary concepts, primarily from
[
13
]
.

Consider the set
\mathcal{A}
to be a non-empty finite set and let
\Sigma_{|\mathcal{A}|}=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
(resp.
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
) represent the collection of all bi-infinite (resp. right-infinite) sequences of symbols from
\mathcal{A}
. The map
\tau
on
\Sigma_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, known as the
shift map
, is defined by
\tau(\sigma)=\sigma^{\prime}
where
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}
. The pair
(\Sigma_{|\mathcal{A}|},\tau)
is called the
full shift
, and any closed invariant subset
\Sigma
of
\Sigma_{|\mathcal{A}|}
is referred to as a
sub-shift
or a
shift space
. A
word
or
block
over
\mathcal{A}
is a finite sequence of symbols from
\mathcal{A}
.
Denote by

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)

the set of all admissible words of length
n
and define

\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)

as the
language
of

\Sigma
.
According to this definition, every word

possesses a right prolongation. This implies that if
u\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
, then there exists some

such that
ui\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
.
The left prolongation occurs in sub-shifts of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and not necessarily in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
For example, if
\Sigma=\{21^{\infty},\,1^{\infty}\}\subset\{1,\,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, it forms a sub-shift that lacks left prolongation.
To simplify matters, it is often assumed that
\tau
is totally invariant, meaning
\tau^{-1}(\Sigma)=\Sigma
. This implies that under this assumption, any

will invariably possess a left prolongation.

For
u\in\mathcal{L}_{k}(\Sigma)
, let the
cylinder

{}_{l}[u]_{l+k-1}=[u_{l}\ldots u_{l+k-1}]
be the set
\{\sigma=\cdots\sigma_{-1}\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\cdots\in\Sigma:\sigma_{l}\cdots\sigma_{l+k+1}=u\}
. Let
\bf{F}
be a collection of blocks over
\mathcal{A}
, which we will think of as being the

. For any such

, define

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
which do not contain any block in
\bf{F}
. Now we can say that a sub-shift is a subset
\Sigma
of a full shift
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\bf{F}}
for some collection
\bf{F}
of forbidden blocks over
\mathcal{A}
. A shift space
\Sigma
is
irreducible
if for every ordered pair of words
u,\,v\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
there is a word
w\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
so that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
. A point
\sigma\in\Sigma
is
doubly transitive
if every word in
\Sigma
appears in
\sigma
infinitely often to the left and the right.

Definition 2.1
.

[
13
, §1.5]

\Sigma
(resp.
\Sigma^{\prime}

\mathcal{A}

The map

Shift spaces defined by a finite set of forbidden blocks are termed

(SFT), and their factors are known as

A word

is regarded as

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma
represent a shift space over
\mathcal{A}
, and let
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
. We label the pair
(\Sigma,\,\tau)
as a

. The set
\mathcal{W}
is denoted as the

of the coded sub-shift
\Sigma

Coded systems, which are an extension of sofic systems, were first introduced by Blanchard and Hansel in
[

[

The detailed understanding of half-synchronized systems is not necessary; however, it is important to note that a half-synchronized system is mixing if and only if there exists a generator

such that

[

3
Iterated functions systems (IFS) over an arbitrary sub-shift

The
conventional iterated function system
, or simply the conventional IFS,

(X,\,\mathcal{F})

comprises a compact metric space

X

and a finite set of continuous self-maps

\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,\,f_{k}\}

on

X.

We represent this system as

\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma_{|\mathcal{A}|}),

(3.1)

where

\Sigma_{|\mathcal{A}|}

is the full shift on

k=|\mathcal{A}|

symbols.
A “generic” IFS, or simply an IFS
refers to the scenario where our sub-shift is no longer a full shift.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a sub-shift on

\mathcal{A}=\{1,\,2,\ldots,k\}

and
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
a set of
k
continuous self-maps on the compact metric space
X

Call

\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)

an IFS where for

f_{u}:X\longrightarrow X
is given by

f_{u}(x)=f_{u_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{u_{1}}(x).

(3.3)

The
orbit of

is set as

\mathcal{O}^{+}(x)=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)}f_{u}(x).

(3.4)

Consider
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma
, and let the sequence

be the trajectory of
x
along
\sigma
. The set of points in this trajectory, denoted as
\mathcal{O}^{+}_{\sigma}(x)
, is called the forward orbit of
x
along
\sigma
. This orbit defines a non-autonomous system denoted by

f_{\sigma_{1},\,\infty}.

(3.5)

We say
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,\,f_{k}\}
has property
P
, if all maps in
\mathcal{F}
have that property.
If
\mathcal{F}
is homeomorphism, then

{\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}}_{-}(x)
,

{\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}}^{+}_{-}(x)

, or

{\mathcal{O}}_{\sigma}(x)

can be defined similarly as in the classical dynamical systems at the beginning of Section
2
. We adopt the notion

f_{\sigma_{1},\infty}

for all cases.

Analogous to classical dynamical systems, if
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
has property
P
, and if
\mathcal{F}
is a homeomorphism, then
{\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}}_{-}(x)
,
{\mathcal{O}_{\sigma}}^{+}_{-}(x)
, or
{\mathcal{O}}_{\sigma}(x)

Recall that a non-autonomous system is typically denoted by

]

.

Let

Definition 3.7

Let
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
be an IFS.
A point

3.2
The attractor set of an IFS

Hereinafter, we assume that the maps in
\mathcal{F}
are contractions. This means that for
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
,
f_{i}:(X,\,d)\rightarrow(X,\,d)
, there exists a constant
0\leq r_{i}<1
such that
d(f_{i}(x),\,f_{i}(y))\leq r_{i}d(x,\,y)
for all
x,\,y\in X
. Let
\{r_{1},\ldots,\,r_{k}\}
be the
ratio list
associated with
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{C}
be the set of compact subsets in
X
. Subsequently,
(\mathcal{C},\,d_{H})
will be a compact space where
d_{H}
denotes the Hausdorff metric. We use
B_{H}(\cdot,\,\epsilon)
to represent a ball with radius
\epsilon
in the Hausdorff metric.

In
[
10
]
,
Hutchinson observed that when

\Sigma

represents a full shift, (referred here as the conventional IFS),

H:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}

is defined by

H(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{A}|}f_{i}(A)

(3.9)

is a contraction. Thus, according to the Contraction Mapping Theorem, there exists a unique

S\in\mathcal{C}
, known as the attractor,
such that
for any
A\in\mathcal{C}
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}H^{n}(A)=S.

(3.10)

and

H(S)=S.

(3.11)

The map

H

is known as the
Hutchinson operator
, and
H^{n}(A)
represents the
n
th iteration of
H
. Hence, if
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then from (
3.11
), we obtain
H^{n}(S)=S
(
H=H^{1}
).
We extend the definition of
H^{n}
to cases where

\Sigma

is no longer a full shift; in that scenario,
H(=H_{\Sigma})
, as defined in (
3.9
), is not a map, but rather

H^{n}(A)=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)}f_{u}(A)\in\mathcal{C}

(3.12)

with

H^{n+1}(A)=\bigcup_{ui\in\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(\Sigma),\,i\in\mathcal{A}}f_{ui}(A)

(3.13)

is well understood and is non-empty. This is due to the fact that any word in
\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
has a right prolongation.
We refer to
H=H^{1}
as the ”generalized”
Hutchinson operator
(for simplicity, we refer to it as the Hutchinson operator, dropping the subscript
\Sigma
for
H^{n}
and
H^{n+1}
as mentioned above).
Note that if we define a forbidden word as
f_{u}(A)=\emptyset
, then we can express

H^{n+1}(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\mathcal{|}\mathcal{A}|}\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)}f_{ui}(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\mathcal{|}\mathcal{A}|}f_{i}(H^{n}(A)),

which is similar to the conventional case definition. We will see that this definition of

ensures the limit in (
3.10
) but not the equality in (
3.11
).

Proposition 3.9
.

Consider
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)

\mathcal{F}
is a contraction.
Suppose

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}H^{n}(A)

is unique, and we will have

First, we establish the existence of

3.2.1
Abundance of self-similarities in an attractor

A compact topological space
Y
is considered

if there exists a finite set of non-surjective homeomorphisms
\{h_{j}:Y\overset{h_{j}}{\rightarrow}Y;1\leq j\leq l\}
such that
Y=\bigcup_{j=1}^{l}h_{j}(Y)
. Therefore, when
Y
is self-similar, parts resemble the whole up to homeomorphism. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
be an IFS and let
S
be its attractor. If for
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)
,
f_{u}
is a non-surjective homeomorphism on
S
and
S=H^{n}(S)=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)}f_{u}(S)
, then we say that
S
has a self-similarity of order
n
.

In part 3 of the following example, an IFS is presented such that its associated maps are contractive homeomorphisms on
X
, but there exist some
n\in\mathbb{N}
where none of
f_{u}
’s are contractive homeomorphisms on
S
for
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)
.

Proposition 3.14

Let

\varphi_{1}
is a factor code with memory

and anticipation
n
. Then,
\varphi_{2}(S)=S^{\prime}
where
S
and
S^{\prime}
are the attractors of
\mathfrak{I}
and
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}
respectively. Moreover, if

{H^{\prime}}^{\ell}(S^{\prime})=S^{\prime}
. Here,
H^{\prime}
is the Hutchinson operator for
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First assume that
\varphi_{1}
is a 1-block factor map with block map

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}({H}^{\ell}(X))

\displaystyle=\varphi_{2}(\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\Sigma)}f_{u}(X))=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\Sigma)}\varphi_{2}f_{u}(X)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\Sigma)}f^{\prime}_{\varPhi_{1}(u)}(\varphi_{2}(X))=\bigcup_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\Sigma^{\prime})}f^{\prime}_{u^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})

(3.15)

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\ell}(X^{\prime}).

But

is continuous and so continuous as

Remark 3.19
.

A coded shift may possess a fixed point without being rooted in a fixed point. For example, let

be generated by
\mathcal{W}=\{21^{2n-1}:\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}

.
On the other hand, a synchronized coded shift is mixing if and only if it contains at least one generator

When

is the full shift, then for

So when

is a homeomorphism,

For a general coded

,
we have the following instead.

Proposition 3.20
.

Let

\Sigma

be a coded shift in

​.
Then for infinitely many

is a homeomorphism, then for infinitely many

Let

be a member of an arbitrary generator

of

Since

(a_{1},\,b_{1})=(0,\,0)
,
(a_{2},\,b_{2})=(1,\,0)
, and
(a_{3},\,b_{3})=(0,\,1)
. Then, this IFS satisfies (
3.22
) but not (
3.21
).

3.3
Attractor as the phase space of an IFS

We observed in Example
3.8
that in an IFS, the attracting set may not be preserved by maps in
\mathcal{F}
. However, if
\mathcal{F}
preserves
S
such as in Lemma
3.18
where
\Sigma
is a coded shift rooted in a fixed point, then
{\mathfrak{I}_{S}=}(S,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
is an IFS again. Denote IDS
S
and
T_{S}
, the associated IDS and IDS map for
\mathfrak{I}_{S}
respectively.

Proposition 3.28
.

Let
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{F}
contraction. Assume that
\mathcal{F}
preserves the attractor set
S
. Then,

1.

any point in
S
is transitive in
\mathfrak{I}_{S}=(S,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
.

2.

if
\Sigma
is irreducible and
t\in\Sigma
is transitive, then
(t,\,s)\in\Sigma\times S
is a transitive point for
T_{S}
. In particular, if
\Sigma
is a minimal sub-shift, then IDS
S
is a minimal system.

Proof.

1.

Let
s\in S
be arbitrary. We must show that

S=\overline{\{f_{u}(s):\;u\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)\}}.

(3.25)

By Remark
3.21
,
S
is the limit set of
\{f_{u^{(n)}}(s)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and by the hypothesis,
f_{u^{(n)}}(s)\in S
, so (
3.25
) holds.

2.

This is a trivial consequence of part
1
.

∎

The following example shows that irreducibility of
\Sigma
is necessary but not sufficient for part
2
of Proposition
3.28
.

Example 3.29
.

Let
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma=\{1^{\infty},\,2^{\infty}\}
and for
i=1,\,2
,
f_{i}
a contraction with
f_{i}(x_{i})=x_{i}
,
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
preserves
S=\{x_{1},\,x_{2}\}
and conclusions of parts 2 and 3 in Proposition
3.28
do not hold. However, if
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then all conclusions in that proposition are true. Here,
\Sigma
is reducible.

Topological transitivity and point transitivity are equivalent in a classical dynamical system, which is not true for an IFS. In fact, in an IFS, topological transitivity always implies point transitivity
[
1
, Proposition 3.2]
, but the converse is not necessarily the case
[
1
, Proposition 3.3]
. The subsequent result directly follows from part
1
of the aforementioned proposition.

Corollary 3.30
.

Let
\mathfrak{I}
and
\Sigma
be as described in Proposition
3.28
. Then, for
\mathfrak{I}_{S}=(S,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
, topological transitivity and point transitivity are equivalent.

3.3.1
Dynamical properties of the sub-shift versus the IFS on the attractor

The subsequent proposition reflects the dynamical properties of a sub-shift on the attractor when
\mathcal{F}
preserves the attractor
S
.

Proposition 3.31
.

For an IFS where
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction and preserves
S
,
\Sigma
has property
P\in\mathfrak{P}=\{
Devaney chaos, mixing, weak mixing, totally transitive
\}
if and only if
T_{S}:\Sigma\times S\rightarrow\Sigma\times S
has property
P
.

Proof.

The sufficiency is evident, so a proof for necessity will be given. Assume
\Sigma
has Devaney chaos. By part
2
of Proposition
3.28
, we only need to prove that periodic points are dense in

\Sigma\times S
. Taking an arbitrary open set
V\times W
in
\Sigma\times S
and
[v]\subseteq V
for some word
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(\Sigma)
, choose a transitive point
t=vt_{n+1}\cdots
in
[v]
and find
b=vt_{n+1}\cdots t_{\ell}
such that
f_{b}(S)\subseteq W
. Given the density of periodic points in
\Sigma
, there exists
u^{\prime\infty}\in[b]
. Set
u^{\prime}:=bq\in\mathcal{L}_{m}(\Sigma)
and
\sigma:=u^{\infty}=\tau^{|b|}(u^{\prime\infty})=[qb]^{\infty}
. Consequently,
f_{u}(S)=f_{b}(f_{q}(S))\subseteq W
. By another part of Proposition
3.23
, the cycle
S_{\sigma}=\{x_{0},f_{u_{1}}(x_{0}),\ldots,f_{u_{1}\ldots u_{m-1}}(x_{0})\}\subseteq S
of length
m
contains
x_{0}
and thus
x_{0}\in W
with
f_{u}(x_{0})=x_{0}
.

For the other properties in
\mathfrak{P}
, consider
[u_{i}]\times V_{i}
as an open set in
\Sigma\times S
for
i=1,\,2
and let
t^{(i)}\in[u_{i}]
be transitive.
Then, there is
u_{i}^{\prime}
such that
u_{i}u_{i}^{\prime}
is the initial of
t^{(i)}
and
f_{u_{i}u_{i}^{\prime}}(S)\subset V_{i}
.
Given
w
such that
u_{1}u^{\prime}_{1}\,w\,u_{2}u^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
, there is
s\in f_{u_{1}u_{1}^{\prime}}(S)\subset V_{1}
such that
f_{w\,u_{2}u^{\prime}_{2}}(s)\in f_{u_{2}u^{\prime}_{2}}(S)\subset V_{2}
. This implies
T^{l}_{S}(V_{1})\cap V_{2}\neq\emptyset
where
l=|w\,u_{2}u^{\prime}_{2}|
. Now, if
\Sigma
is mixing, there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
l\geq M
and some
w\in\mathcal{L}_{l}(\Sigma)
,
u_{1}u^{\prime}_{1}\,w\,u_{2}u^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)
, leading to the conclusion that
T_{S}
is mixing. Similar reasoning applies for the other properties.
∎

Recall that an IFS has Devaney chaos if it is transitive and possesses a dense collection of periodic points. Thus, the above proposition directly implies the following.

Corollary 3.32
.

Assume
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
and
\mathfrak{P}
are as in Proposition
3.31
.
If
\Sigma
has property
P\in\mathfrak{P}
, then
\mathfrak{I}_{S}=(S,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
has property
P
as well.

The IFS with a trivial attractive set in Example
3.27
consists of
S=\{s_{0}\}
. Regardless of the choice of
\Sigma
,
\mathfrak{I}_{S}
is mixing. Therefore, the converse of the above corollary is not generally valid.

Conclusion.

Let
\mathfrak{I}=(X,\,\mathcal{F},\,\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is an arbitrary sub-shift, and
\mathcal{F}
is a homeomorphism. We demonstrated that self-similarities can emerge in the attractor of
\mathfrak{I}
when
\Sigma
is coded, regardless of the choice of
\mathcal{F}
. However, the identification of self-similarities in other types of sub-shifts, particularly minimal sub-shifts, is not known. A key issue is to have
\mathcal{F}
preserving the attractor. Example
3.8
highlights the potential challenge of this preservation when
\Sigma
represents a minimal non-fixed cycle. In contrast, Proposition
3.31
, as a classical dynamical system, must have minimal sub-systems that result in IFS with minimal sub-shifts. The structure of these minimal sub-shifts evades our current understanding.
