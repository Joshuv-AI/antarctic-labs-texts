Abstract
. We study a family of convex polytopes, called SIM-bodies, which were introduced by Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias (2018) to analyze so-called Straight-Jacket Auctions. First, we show that the SIM-bodies belong to the class of generalized permutahedra. Second, we prove an optimality result for the Straight-Jacket Auctions among certain deterministic auctions. Third, we employ computer algebra methods and mathematical software to explicitly determine optimal prices and revenues.

1.
Introduction

The design of auctions that maximize the revenue of a seller is a central question of economic theory.
In the basic model, there is a single item and a set of buyers interested in obtaining the item from the seller.
Every buyer individually holds a private value, which is the maximal price that they are willing to spend in order to receive the item.
The exact valuation of a buyer is unknown to both the seller and the other buyers, but it is drawn from a probability distribution that is common knowledge.
In this setting, a
mechanism
(or auction) is a procedure that governs how the seller elicits information from the buyers regarding their private valuations and decides to whom to sell the item at which price.
The seller seeks to maximize their expected revenue, i.e., the price received from the successful buyer.
The mathematical properties of auctions in these settings are at the heart of two Nobel Memorial Prizes in Economics. Vickrey
[
32
]
received the price in 1996 for showing that many popular auction formats give the same revenue to the seller, and Myerson
[
25
]
received the prize in 2007 for a precise characterization of the revenue-maximizing auction.

While auctions for selling a single item are reasonably well understood, the case where multiple items are for sale is much more challenging due to the underlying combinatorics of the problem.
The main difficulty is to decide which subset of items (or randomizations thereof) to sell to the buyer.
It has been observed that even in the simplest case of a single buyer whose valuation for the items is additive, the optimal auction may be randomized
[
12
,
30
]
and may even require randomization among an uncountable set of allocations
[
13
]
.
Optimal auctions are also challenging from a computational point of view since computing the optimal auction is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for distributions of support three
[
8
]
and
\mathsf{\#P}
-hard in general
[
12
]
. There are two main ways to deal with these complexities.
The first one is to study suboptimal auctions; this approach avoids the combinatorial complexity by selling only all items or only a single item; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
18
,
22
]
.
The second one considers specific distributions that allow to study the combinatorial structure of the problem.
Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias
[
17
]
examined the case where the valuation for each item is drawn from the uniform distribution and devise a deterministic auction, called
Straight-Jacket Auction (SJA)
.
As their main result, Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias showed that SJA is optimal for up to to six items
[
17
, Theorem 4.8]
.

Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias recognized that crucial properties of SJA are expressed in terms of certain convex polytopes and their volumes; in
[
17
]
these polytopes were called
SIM-bodies
; yet they previously occurred as
“
Q
-polytopes”
in work of Doker
[
14
, §2.4]
.
The SIM-bodies form our point of departure, and we derive crucial structural information.
Most notably, these turn out to be generalized permutahedra; this is our Theorem
9
.
Generalized permutahedra were introduced by Postnikov
[
26
]
, and they received considerable attention in the algebraic combinatorics community and beyond.
Moreover, generalized permutahedra are most tightly related to submodular functions, polymatroids and
M
-convexity studied in optimization; see Frank and Murota
[
15
]
for a survey.
For instance, generalized permutahedra already occur as
“cores of cooperative games”
in work of Danilov and Koshevoy
[
11
, Proposition 5]
.
As our main theoretical contribution, in Theorem
17
, we prove a general uniqueness result for submodular and deterministic auctions.
This leads us to proving the optimality of SJA for any number of items, but for a restricted choice of auctions, and subject to a technical condition (Corollary
25
).
The existence of such auctions is far from obvious; in fact, the article
[
17
]
left open if SJA exists for
n\geq 7
items.
A SIM-body arises as that portion of the domain of the utility of the buyer where no bundle is sold.
Equivalently, SIM-bodies can be described as regions of tropical hypersurfaces induced by the utilities.
In this sense our present work is in line with recent efforts to employ methods from tropical geometry to topics in mechanism design
[
3
,
31
,
10
,
19
]
.

The rest of our paper is devoted to actually computing the SJA-prices and their revenues.
Our method builds on a new parametric volume formula for SIM-bodies, Proposition
34
, which we derive from a general algorithm of Lawrence
[
21
]
.
We exploit that this method for computing the volume of a convex polytope is particularly nice for (rational) polytopes whose normal fans are smooth in the sense of toric geometry.
An
n
-dimensional SIM-body depends on
n
parameters, which are real numbers corresponding to the prices of the
n
items in the auction;.
The normalized volume is an integral homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
in these
n
parameters.
Computing the SJA-prices then amounts to finding real roots of a system of
n
polynomials equations in
n
indeterminates and testing submodularity conditions (which yield linear inequalities).
In this way, we establish the existence of SJA for
n\leq 12
items by computations using
polymake

[
16
]
and
HomotopyContinuation.jl

[
6
]
.
Furthermore, it turns out that a certain linear substitution of the volume polynomials of the SIM-bodies gives Lorentzian polynomials
[
4
]
.

2.
SIM-bodies

Our approach to studying auctions is primarily geometric and algorithmic.
To this end it is useful to begin with certain classes of (convex) polytopes.
We refer to
[
33
]
and
[
20
]
for the basics of polyhedral geometry and related algorithms.

The
permutahedron

{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1})
is the convex hull of all
(n+1)!
points which arise from coordinate permutations of
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1})
, and the parameters
\alpha_{i}
are arbitrary real numbers.
By construction the symmetric group
\Sym(n+1)
of degree
n+1
operates on
{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1})
by linear automorphisms.
Examples include the
regular permutahedron

{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(n,n-1,\dots,0)
and the
hypersimplex

\Delta(k,n)={\mathcal{P}}_{n}(1,\dots,1,0,\dots,0)
, with
k
ones and
n-k
zeros.
The following is an
H
-description of permutahedra, which is known; see
[
26
, Proposition 2.5]
, where that result is attributed to Rado
[
27
]
.

Lemma 1
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n+1}
we have

\begin{split}{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(&\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1})\\
\ &=\ \biggl\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\biggm|\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}=\bar{\alpha}\,,\ \sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}\text{ for }I\in\tbinom{[n+1]}{k}\,,\ k\geq 1\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt,\\
\end{split}

where
\bar{\alpha}:=\sum\alpha_{i}
.

Throughout we abbreviate
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and we write
\tbinom{[n]}{k}
for the set of
k
-element subsets of
[n]
.
A key construction comes from considering a map
z
that assigns each nonempty subset
I\subseteq[n+1]
a real number
z_{I}
.
Following Postnikov
[
26
, §6]
this gives rise to the polyhedron

(1)

{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}\{z\}\ =\ \biggl\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\biggm|\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}=z_{[n+1]}\,,\ \sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq z_{[n+1]}-z_{I^{c}}\text{ for }\emptyset\neq I\subseteq[n+1]\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt,

where
I^{c}=[n+1]\setminus I
is the complement of
I
in
[n+1]
.

Example 2
.

For
z_{I}=\alpha_{n+2-k}+\dots+\alpha_{n+1}
with
k=\#I
we recover the permutahedron
{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n+1})={\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}\{z\}
.

Now we pick a fixed weakly descending sequence of
n
nonnegative real numbers
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
.
Specializing (
1
) by letting

(2)

z_{I}\ =\ \begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{\#I-1}\alpha_{n+1-i}&\text{ if }n+1\in I\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\kern 5.0pt,\end{cases}

we obtain the polyhedron
\Gamma(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}):={\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}\{z\}
.
Observe that
z_{[n+1]}=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}=\bar{\alpha}
.
Moreover, we define
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
as the orthogonal projection of
\Gamma(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
gotten by omitting the last coordinate.
As the polyhedron
\Gamma(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is contained in the affine subspace
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}=\bar{\alpha}
, the orthogonal projection
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is a polyhedron in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which is affinely isomorphic.

Proposition 3
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
we have

\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\ =\ \biggl\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{n}\biggm|\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}\text{ for }I\in\tbinom{[n]}{k}\text{ and }k\geq 1\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt,

Proof.

Let
\pi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the projection which omits the last coordinate, and let
z_{I}
be defined for nonempty
I\subseteq[n+1]
as in (
2
).
Applying one Fourier–Motzkin step yields

\pi\bigl(P_{n+1}\{z_{I}\}\bigr)\ =\ \biggl\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\biggm|z_{I}\leq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\bar{\alpha}-z_{I^{c}}\text{ for }\emptyset\neq I\subseteq[n]\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt,

where
I^{c}=[n+1]\setminus I
.
This gives the claim.
∎

Proposition 4
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
we have

\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\ =\ \conv\left\{\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},0,\dots,0)\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

Moreover, if
\alpha_{1}>\cdots>\alpha_{n}>0
, those points form the pairwise distinct vertices.

Proof.

A direct inspection shows that the points
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},0,\dots,0)
and their permutations are feasible.
Assume
\alpha_{1}>\cdots>\alpha_{n}>0
.
Then
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},0,\dots,0)
is a vertex of
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
because it is the only feasible point that maximizes the linear objective function
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},-1,\dots,-1)
.
If the parameters
\alpha_{i}
are not pairwise distinct, the claim follows from the generic case by continuity.
∎

Example 5
.

For instance, for
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>0
the
2
-dimensional SIM-body

\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\ =\ \conv\bigl\{(0,0),\,(\alpha_{1},0),\,(0,\alpha_{1}),\,(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),\,(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1})\bigr\}

is a convex pentagon, which resembles a SIM-card used to authenticate subscribers on mobile phones; hence the name.
In the telecommunication context the term
“SIM”
is short for
“subscriber identity module”
.

There are several immediate consequences of Proposition
4
.
Recall that
normally equivalent
polytopes, by definition, share the same normal fan.
In particular, such polytopes are affinely (and thus combinatorially) isomorphic.

Corollary 6
.

Let
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
.

(a)

The SIM-body
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is bounded; i.e., it is a polytope;

(b)

that polytope is integral if and only if all parameters
\alpha_{i}
are integers;

(c)

we have
\dim\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})=n
if and only if
\alpha_{1}>0
;

(d)

for pairwise distinct parameters
\alpha_{i}
the SIM-body
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is normally equivalent to
\Lambda(n,n-1,\dots,1)
;

(e)

the linear automorphism group of
\Lambda(n,n-1,\dots,1)
is the symmetric group
\Sym(n)
, and it has precisely
n+1
vertex orbits;

(f)

the number of vertices of
\Lambda(n,n-1,\dots,1)
equals
\sum_{k=0}^{n}n!/k!
.

For
\alpha_{1}>\cdots>\alpha_{n}>0
we call
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
a
proper
SIM-body of
(permutation) degree

n
.
By Corollary
6

d
), there is only one combinatorial type of proper SIM-bodies for each degree.
That is, for combinatorial purposes it suffices to study the
regular
SIM-body
\Lambda_{n}:=\Lambda(n,n-1,\dots,1)
of rank
n
.
The sequence
a(n):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}n!/k!
from Corollary

\Lambda_{n}
, occurs as A000522 in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
[

.
The regular SIM-body
\Lambda_{n}
occurs as
“
Q_{n}
”
in the work of Doker
[

, §2.4]
; general SIM-bodies are special cases of

in
[

]
.

Example 7
.

For the most part we will focus on proper SIM-bodies, but there are some interesting special cases which are not proper.
For instance,
\Lambda(1,1,\dots,1)=[0,1]^{n}
is the unit cube, and
\Lambda(1,0,\dots,0)=\conv\{0,e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
is the standard simplex.

Next we will show that the
H
-description of
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
from Proposition
3
is nonredundant if the SIM-body is proper.
Moreover, we will determine the vertex-facet incidences.
Any nonempty set
I\subseteq[n]
yields the inequality

(3)

\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\ \leq\ \alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}\kern 5.0pt.

Consider the vertex
v:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell},0,\dots,0)
of
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.
Then we say that the set
I
with
\#I=k
is
active
at
v
if the inequality (
3
) is tight at
v
, i.e.,

\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}\ =\ \sum_{i\in I\cap[\ell]}\alpha_{i}\ =\ \alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}\kern 5.0pt,

or equivalently
I\cap[\ell]=[k]
.
The latter holds if and only if

(4)

k\leq\ell\quad\text{and}\quad I=[k]\kern 5.0pt.

Lemma 8
.

A proper SIM-body is a simple polytope, and it has exactly
2^{n}+n-1
facets.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1}>\cdots>\alpha_{n}>0
.
Then the SIM-body
\Lambda:=\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is
n
-dimensional.
We need to show that each vertex is incident with exactly
n
facets.
As in our previous deliberations we consider the vertex
v:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell},0,\dots,0)
.
From (
4
) we obtain exactly
\ell
active sets at
v
.
These contribute the same number of inequalities like (
3
) which are tight at
v
.
Additionally, exactly the last
n-\ell
nonnegativity constraints are tight at
v
.
The facet count results from
2^{n}-1
nonempty active sets and
n
nonnegativity constraints.
∎

We are now able to prove our main result about the geometry of SIM-bodies.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
.
Then each edge of the homogeneous SIM-body
\Gamma(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is parallel to the difference of two standard basis vectors.

A polytope with that property is called a
generalized permutahedron

[
26
]
.

Proof.

First, consider the special case where
\alpha_{k}=n-k+1
for
k\in[n]
; i.e.,
\Gamma_{n}:=\Gamma(n,n-1,\dots,1)
is the regular homogeneous SIM-body of rank
n
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
.
We abbreviate
\bar{\ell}:=\ell{+}1+\ell{+}2+\dots+n
.
Then
v:=(1,\dots,\ell,0,\dots,0,\bar{\ell})
is a vertex of
\Gamma_{n}
.
There are
\ell
active sets and
n-\ell
(lifts of) nonnegativity constraints which define the
n
facets incident with
v
.
Let
w
be a vertex adjacent to
v
.
Then there are exactly
n-1
facets incident to both vertices.
Several cases arise.
For instance, it may happen that
\ell-1
of the facets from sets active at
v
and all
n-\ell
(lifts of) nonnegativity constraints are tight at
w
.
Then
w=(1,\dots,\ell{-}1,0,\dots,0,\overline{\ell{-}1})
, and
v-w=\ell(e_{\ell}-e_{n+1})
.
The same reasoning, with opposite signs, applies if the number of active sets increases when we walk from
v
to
w
.
It remains to discuss the situation when the number of facets from active sets incident with
v
and
w
is the same.
This occurs if and only if
v
and
w
lie in the same orbit of the action of
\Sym(n)
.
From Proposition
4
we see that these vertices form the vertices of the permutahedron
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(n,\dots,\ell,0,\dots,0)
, after projection to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
So in this case the claim follows from Lemma
1
.

Now Corollary
6
(
d
) proves the claim for an arbitrary SIM-body which is proper, i.e., it satisfies
\alpha_{1}>\cdots>\alpha_{n}>0
.
This leaves the degenerate cases where some, or all, of the parameters coincide.
Then some edge lengths shrink to zero, but this does not affect the edge directions.
∎

3.
Optimal Deterministic Auctions

We now turn to our main topic.
A single buyer is interested in buying
n
items.
The value of the buyer for item
i\in[n]
is denoted by
x_{i}
and drawn from the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. The value
x_{i}
corresponds to the amount of money the buyer is willing to spend in order to receive item
i
and is the private information of the buyer.
A
direct revelation mechanism
is a tuple
M=(a,r)
of functions
a:[0,1]^{n}\to[0,1]^{n}
and
r:[0,1]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
The mechanism elicits a bid vector
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{n}
from the buyer.
It then sells to the buyer at a price of
r(x^{\prime})
the following lottery: For each item
i\in[n]
the buyer receives item
i
with probability
a_{i}(x^{\prime})
where the probabilities for different items are independent of each other.
We call a direct revelation mechanism an
auction
.
An auction is
deterministic
if
a(x^{\prime})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
for all
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{n}
. The price
r(x^{\prime})
payed by the buyer is also called the
revenue
of the auction. The buyer is risk-neutral and has additive valuations, meaning that their utility is equal to the expected value of the items received by the mechanism minus the payment. Fixing a direct revelation mechanism
M=(a,r)
, the utility of the buyer with valuation
x
who bids
x^{\prime}
is given by

u(x^{\prime}\mid x)\ =\ -r(x^{\prime})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x^{\prime})x_{i}\kern 5.0pt.

We impose the following two assumptions on the design space of direct revelation mechanisms.
First, we require that the buyer prefers to bid according to their actual valuation vector
x
over claiming any other bid vector
x^{\prime}\neq x
. Bidding the true valuation is also called
truthful
bidding. A mechanism is called
incentive compatible
if bidding truthfully is an optimal strategy of the buyer, i.e.,

(IC)

u(x\mid x)\geq u(x^{\prime}\mid x)\quad\text{for all $x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{n}$}\kern 5.0pt.

Second, we require that the mechanism is
individually rational
. This property (which is sometimes also called
voluntary participation
) requires that for every realization
x
of the buyers’ valuations, they are guaranteed to leave the auction with a nonnegative utility when bidding truthfully. Formally, the individual rationality constraint reads

(IR)

\displaystyle u(x\mid x)\geq 0\quad\text{for all $x\in[0,1]^{n}$}\kern 5.0pt.

Let
u(x)=u(x\mid x)
for all
x\in[0,1]^{n}
.
Our goal is to design an auction that maximizes the expected revenue under the constraints that the auction is incentive compatible and individual rational, i.e., we are interested in solving

\displaystyle\sup\quad

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[r(x)]

s.t.

\displaystyle a:[0,1]^{n}\to[0,1]^{n}\text{ and }r:[0,1]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle M=(a,r)\text{ satisfies \eqref{eq:IC} and \eqref{eq:IR}}\kern 5.0pt,

where the expectation in the objective is taken over all draws of
x_{i}
from the uniform distribution in
[0,1]
.
The following result of Rochet
[
28
]
characterizes the set of mechanisms satisfying (
IC
) in terms of convexity.

Proposition 10
.

The auction
M=(a,r)
satisfies (
IC
) if and only if the utility
u(x)
is convex and differentiable almost everywhere with

\displaystyle{\partial u(x)}/{\partial x_{i}}=a_{i}(x)\quad\text{ for all $i\in[n]$ and almost all $x\in[0,1]^{n}$}\kern 5.0pt.

Proposition
10
allows to parameterize the space of all auctions satisfying (
IC
) by the utility functions
u:[0,1]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that they impose on the buyer.
The constraint that the auction is (
IR
) can then be incorporated by requiring that
u:[0,1]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
.
Together with the observation that
r(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)x_{i}-u(x)
, we obtain the following optimization problem to determine a revenue maximizing auction satisfying (
IC
) and (
IR
)

(5)

\displaystyle\sup

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\Biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\partial u(x)}/{\partial x_{i}}\ x_{i}\,-\,u(x)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle u:[0,1]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\text{ is convex}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\leq 1\quad\text{ for all $i\in[n]$ and almost all $x\in[0,1]^{n}$}\kern 5.0pt.

For the following, let us assume that the primal solution is a deterministic auction.
Formally, let
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
be a
price schedule
where we require
p_{\emptyset}=0
.
A price schedule defines a unique deterministic auction where the buyer reports their valuation and is assigned a subset
I\subseteq[n]
of items that maximizes their utility which is the valuation attached to the items contained in the set minus the price
p_{I}
for set
I
.
Consequently, the utility of the buyer for this auction is given by the function

(6)

u(x)\ =\ \max\biggl\{\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}-p_{I}\biggm|I\subseteq[n]\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt,

which is the evaluation of a
\max
-tropical polynomial of degree
n
.
The set of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
where that maximum is attained at least twice is the
tropical hypersurface

{\mathcal{T}}(u)
; see
[
23
, §3.1]
or
[

Lemma 11
.

Let
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
be an arbitrary price schedule.
Further, let
x\in[0,1]^{n}
and
I\subseteq[n]
such that
x\in D_{I}
.
Then
x_{-i}\in D_{I}^{i}
for all
i\in I
.

Proof.

Fixing
i\in I

.
As
x\in D_{I}
, we have

x_{i}+\sum_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}x_{j}-p_{I}\ \geq\ \sum_{j\in J}x_{j}-p_{J}

for all
J\subseteq[n]
.
If
i\notin J
the left hand side of that inequality increases if we exchange
x
for
x^{\prime}
, whereas the right hand side stays the same.
Otherwise, if
i\in J
, we have:

\sum_{j\in I}x^{\prime}_{j}-p_{I}\ =\ 1+(x_{i}-x_{i})+\sum_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}x_{j}-p_{I}\ \geq\ 1-x_{i}+\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}-p_{J}\ =\ \sum_{j\in J}x^{\prime}_{j}-p_{J}\kern 5.0pt.

In both cases we obtain

.

The deficiency condition (

Lemma 12
.

Let
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
be an arbitrary price schedule.
Further, let
I\subseteq[n]
with
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
.
Then
p_{I}<p_{J}
for all
J\supsetneq I
.

Proof.

By definition
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
if and only if there exists

such that

\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}-p_{I}\ >\ \sum_{j\in J}x_{j}-p_{J}

for all

.
For

we have
\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\geq\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}
, whence we conclude

.

Let

Lemma 13
.

Let
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
be an arbitrary price schedule.
Further, let
I\subseteq[n]
with
\vol_{n}D_{J}>0
for all
J\subseteq I
.
Then

\pi_{I}(D_{J})\ =\ \biggl\{x\in[0,1]^{I}\biggm|\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}-p_{J}\geq\sum_{j\in K}x_{j}-p_{K}\text{ for all }K\subseteq I\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof.

Let

be a subset of

As in Lemma

also

is contained in

This implies that

arises from

So let

We are particularly interested in the following special class of price schedules.
A price schedule
p
is
submodular
, if

(8)

p_{I\cup J}+p_{I\cap J}\ \leq\ p_{I}+p_{J}\kern 5.0pt.

for all
I,J\subseteq[n]
; see Figure

for an example with

The submodularity of a price schedule is a desirable property for the seller.
Namely, it prevents a buyer interested in the items in

from first buying

and
J
separately at prices
p_{I}
and
p_{J}
and then returning the (duplicate) items in the intersection

at price
p_{I\cap J}
, instead of buying the set
I\cup J
at price
p_{I\cup J}
directly.
For a more comprehensive discussion see
[
24
, Definition 4]
.
The regions of submodular price schedules can be written as products of some of their projections.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
be a submodular price schedule.
Then
D_{I}=\pi_{I}(D_{I})\times\pi_{I^{c}}(D_{I})
for all
I\subseteq[n]
and
I^{c}=[n]\setminus I
.
Moreover,
D_{I}^{i}=\pi_{I\setminus\{i\}}(\pi_{I}(D_{I}))\times\pi_{I^{c}}(D_{I})
for all
i\in I
.

Proof.

The first claim has been proved by Vincent and Manelli
[
24
, Lemma 2]
.
The second claim then follows from Lemma
11
.
∎

Since we are interested in maximizing the revenue it is natural to analyze the critical points of the revenue function.
However, we also need to take care of boundary effects.
To this end we investigate bundles where the submodularity condition (
8
) is attained with equality.
If
p_{I\cup J}+p_{I\cap J}=p_{I}+p_{J}
we call
I\cup J
and
I\cap J

left tight
, while we call
I
and
J

right tight
.
A bundle is

if it is left or right tight.
The following describes the necessary critical point conditions and may be seen as a refinement of
[
24
, Theorem 1]
for uniform valuations and submodular price schedules.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
be a submodular price schedule which maximizes the expected revenue among all submodular price schedules.
Then we have, for all
I\subseteq[n]
:

(1)

If
I
is not tight and
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
, then
\delta_{n}(I)=0
;

(2)

if
I
is not tight and
\vol_{n}D_{I}=0
, then
\delta_{n}(I)\geq 0
;

(3)

if
I
is just left tight, then
\delta_{n}(I)\geq 0
;

(4)

if
I
is just right tight and
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
, then
\delta_{n}(I)\leq 0
.

If
I
is right tight and
\vol_{n}D_{I}=0
, there is no condition on
\delta_{n}(I)
. The same applies if
I
is left tight as well as right tight.

Proof.

First assume that
I\subseteq[n]
is not tight.
Then the prices involving
I
are strictly submodular, i.e., (
8
) is strictly satisfied for all
J\neq I
.
Hence we can slightly increase or decrease
p_{I}
, without loosing the submodularity.
Suppose that
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
.
As
p
is optimal and using Lemma
15
, this forces
\delta_{n}(I)=0
, which says that
p
is a critical point of the revenue function.

Now, if
I
is not tight but
\vol_{n}D_{I}=0
the volume of
D_{I}
(and thus the revenue) can only change if we decrease
p_{I}
.
The optimality of the prices entails
\delta_{n}(I)\geq 0
, again using Lemma
15
.

We are left with the analysis of those bundles which are tight.
So we consider
I\neq J
with
p_{I\cup J}+p_{I\cap J}=p_{I}+p_{J}
.
Decreasing
p_{I\cup J}
or
p_{I\cap J}
keeps the submodularity.
Therefore, as in the case
\vol_{n}D_{I}=0
above, we conclude
\delta_{n}({I\cup J})\geq 0
and
\delta_{n}({I\cap J})\geq 0
.
This takes care of the left tight sets.
For
I
right tight with
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
the situation is symmetric, but with opposite signs.
∎

Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias
[

]
used a generalization of the deficiency
\delta_{n}(I)
, which is a property of the region

, to arbitrary subsets of
[0,1]^{n}
.
In Proposition

\vol_{n}D_{I}=0
.
Note however, that if
\vol_{n}D_{I}=0
we can set

arbitrarily high without affecting the auction.
Following
[

a price schedule is

if

, whenever

.
This entails unique prices, and so price schedules bijectively correspond to auctions.
Throughout we assume that our price schedules are normalized, unless stated otherwise.

A set of bundles
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is a
selling candidate
for
n
items if, firstly,
[n]\in{\mathcal{I}}
, secondly,
{\mathcal{I}}
contains all singletons, and, thirdly, for all
I\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{[n]\}
and

we have
J\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
That is to say,
{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{[n]\}
is an abstract simplicial complex with vertex set
[n]
.
In fact, that simplicial complex can be seen geometrically embedded into the vertex figure of the vertex
{\mathbf{1}}
of the unit cube
[0,1]^{n}
, which is also a vertex of the region
D_{[n]}
.
We say that a price schedule
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}

admits
a selling candidate
{\mathcal{I}}
if and only if
\vol_{n}D_{I}>0
for all
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
Moreover, the price schedule is
critical
with respect to
{\mathcal{I}}
if additionally
\delta_{n}(I)=0
for all
I\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
.
The following is our main technical result; it complements
[
17
, Lemma 6.5]
and generalizes
[
17
, Appendix A]
to nonsymmetric prices.
Our proof is constructive; the special case of most interest to us is spelled out as Algorithm
1
in Section
5
.

Theorem 17
.

For each selling candidate
{\mathcal{I}}
there is at most one critical price schedule which is submodular.
Moreover, it satisfies
p_{I}=p_{J}
whenever
\#I=\#J
and
I,J\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

Proof.

Let
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
, where
{\mathcal{I}}
is a selling candidate for
n
items.
We seek to determine a price for the bundle
I
, and we will do so by induction on the cardinality of
I
.
Let
i\in I
, whence the singleton
\{i\}
is contained in
{\mathcal{I}}
.
We set
p_{\{i\}}=\tfrac{n}{n+1}
.
Then, by Lemma
13
,

(10)

\delta_{n}({\{i\}})\ =\ \vol_{1}(\bar{D}_{\{i\}})-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\vol_{0}(\bar{D}_{\{i\}}^{i})\ =\ (1-p_{\{i\}})-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\ =\ 0\quad\text{for all }i\in[n]\kern 5.0pt.

This computation also shows that
p_{\{i\}}
is uniquely determined.
This case serves as the basis of the induction.

Now let
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
with
\#I\geq 2
, and assume that
p_{J}
is fixed for all
J\subsetneq I
such that the following conditions are satisfied:
\delta_{n}(J)=0
,
p_{J}=p_{J^{\prime}}
whenever
\#J=\#J^{\prime}
, and
\{p_{J}\}_{J\subsetneq I}
is submodular.
We will now construct
p_{I}
such that
\delta_{n}(I)=0
and
\{p_{J}\}_{J\subseteq I}
is submodular, or decide that such a price
p_{I}
doesn’t exist.

Since
p_{\{i\}}=\frac{n}{n+1}
for all
i\in[n]
, we have
(1,x_{-i})\notin D_{\emptyset}
for all
x_{-i}\in[0,1]^{\#I-1}
.
Further, by Lemma
13
we know that
\{\pi_{I}(D_{J})\}_{J\subseteq I}
is a polyhedral complex supported on
[0,1]^{I}
.
Therefore we get

(11)

\sum_{\{i\}\subseteq J\subseteq I}\vol_{\#I-1}\pi_{I}(D_{J}^{i})\ =\ 1\kern 5.0pt,

for all
i\in I
.
The last equation uses the fact that for
i\in I
and
J\subset I
with
i\notin J
we have
D_{J}^{i}=\emptyset
.
This can be seen as follows.
Let
x\in[0,1]^{n}
satisfying
x_{i}=1
.
Then
x\in D_{J}^{i}
if and only if
J=\argmax\left\{\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}-p_{I}\mid I\subseteq[n]\right\}
.
But from the submodular inequality
p_{\emptyset}+p_{J\cup\{i\}}\leq p_{\{i\}}+p_{J}
and
p_{\{i\}}<1
, we have
\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}-p_{J}<\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}-(p_{J\cup\{i\}}-1)=\sum_{j\in J\cup\{i\}}x_{j}-p_{J\cup\{i\}}
.
Hence,
D_{J}^{i}=\emptyset
.
Now, using (
11
) and the fact that
\delta_{n}(J)=0
for all
J\subsetneq I
, the condition
\delta_{n}(I)=0
is equivalent to

(12)

\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq I}\vol_{\#I}\pi_{I}(D_{J})\ =\ \sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq I}\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\vol_{\#I-1}\pi_{I}(D_{J}^{j})\ =\ \frac{\#I}{n+1}\kern 5.0pt.

From Lemma
13
we get
\vol_{\#I}\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})=1-\sum_{\emptyset\neq J\subseteq I}\vol_{\#I}\pi_{I}(D_{J})
.
Hence (
12
) becomes

(13)

\vol_{\#I}\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})\ =\ 1-\frac{\#I}{n+1}\kern 5.0pt.

Note that
\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})=\{x\in[0,1]^{I}\mid\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\leq p_{J}\text{ for }J\subseteq I\}
due to Lemma
13
.
Since the prices
p_{J}
are fixed for
J\subsetneq I
, the volume
\vol_{\#I}\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})
depends differentiably on the variable
p_{I}
.
We define
\hat{p}_{I}
to be the maximal value for
p_{I}
, such that all submodularity conditions of the form

p_{I}+p_{J\cap J^{\prime}}\ \leq\ p_{J}+p_{J^{\prime}}

are satisfied for all
J,J^{\prime}\subseteq I
with
J\cup J^{\prime}=I
.
Choosing
J,J^{\prime}
such that
J\cap J^{\prime}=\emptyset
, we get

\hat{p}_{I}\ \leq\ \max\biggl\{\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\biggm|x\in[0,1]^{I}\text{ and }\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\leq p_{J}\text{ for all }J\subsetneq I\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt.

Therefore, the volume
\vol_{\#I}\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})
is strictly increasing on the interval
[0,\hat{p}_{I}]
.
Hence, there is at most one solution to (
13
) in this interval.
If there is no solution in this interval, there can not be a price
p_{I}
satisfying both
\delta_{n}(I)=0
and the submodularity of
\{p_{J}\}_{J\subseteq I}
.
On the other hand, if the solution exists, it satisfies both properties by the way we defined it.
Further, since the computation of the solution does not depend on prices
p_{J}
for bundles
J
of higher or equal cardinality than
I
, the solution is the same for all
I^{\prime}
with
\#I^{\prime}=\#I
.
Note however, that the solution for
p_{I}
, may be strictly lower than a price
p_{J}
for some
J\subseteq I
.
In that case
D_{J}
is empty, whence
\vol_{\#J}(D_{J})=0
, and there is no submodular critical price schedule for the selling candidate
{\mathcal{I}}
.

For the case
I=[n]
we assume that
p_{J}
is fixed for all
J\in{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\{[n]\}
and we also fix
p_{J}=\#J
for all
J\notin{\mathcal{I}}
.
This way,
D_{J}=\emptyset
for
J\notin{\mathcal{I}}
and hence
\delta_{n}(J)=0
is trivially satisfied.
We have that
\{D_{J}\}_{J\subseteq[n]}
is a polyhedral complex supported on
[0,1]^{n}
.
Moreover, for
i\in[n]
and
J\subset[n]
with
i\notin J
, we get
D_{J}^{i}=\emptyset
, since for
J\in{\mathcal{I}}
the prices
p_{J}
are submodular, and for
J\notin{\mathcal{I}}
the statement is trivially satisfied.
Therefore (
11
) still holds and we can apply the rest of the proof to
D_{[n]}
.
In order to normalize the price schedule, we will redefine
p_{J}=p_{n}
for all
J\notin{\mathcal{I}}
.
This does not change the region
D_{\emptyset}
whence the computation of
p_{n}
stays consistent.
∎

Remark 18
.

We call the candidate set
{\mathcal{I}}

solvable
if there is a solution of (
13
) in the interval
[0,\hat{p}_{I}]
for each bundle
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
However, even if
{\mathcal{I}}
is solvable, a submodular critical price schedule may fail to exist.
This happens if there exists
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
whose solution in the interval
[0,\hat{p}_{I}]
is smaller than the price
p_{J}
for some
J\subsetneq I
with
J\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
This means that
\vol_{n}(D_{J})
vanishes despite that the bundle
J
is intended to be sold.
This can always be fixed by removing bundles from the selling candidate.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a selling candidate for
n
items for which a submodular critical price schedule
\{p_{I}\}_{I\subseteq[n]}
exists; the latter is unique by Theorem
17
.
Recall that a price schedule induces a utility function
u(x)
as in (
6
), which itself defines an auction.
To be precise, the functions
a:[0,1]^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
and
r:[0,1]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of the auction are given by

(14)

a(x)=I\quad\text{and}\quad r(x)=p_{I}\qquad\text{for }x\in D_{I}\kern 5.0pt;

where we identify
I\subseteq[n]
with its characteristic vector in
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
The functions
a
and
r
are well-defined as the regions
D_{I}
, which depend on the prices
p_{I}
, form a polyhedral complex which covers the unit cube.
We call the auction
M({\mathcal{I}}):=(a,r)
the
generalized straight jacket auction
(GSJA) for
n
items with selling candidate
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Next, we are focusing on symmetric price schedules.
That is, the prices only depend on the cardinality of the sets
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
.
We define the
straight jacket auction
(SJA) as follows.
Let
k\in[n]
be the maximal number for which submodular, symmetric critical prices exist for the selling candidate
{\mathcal{I}}_{k,n}:=\tbinom{[n]}{1}\cup\dots\cup\tbinom{[n]}{k}\cup\{\emptyset,[n]\}
.
Then we call
M({\mathcal{I}}_{k,n})
the
straight jacket auction
(SJA) for
n
items.
This deviates slightly from the definition of Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias
[
17
]
.
They proved that SJA is optimal among all auctions, deterministic or nondeterministic, for the uniform distribution and
n\leq 6
items.
Yet from their definition
[
17
, Definition 4.1]
it is not clear if such an auction exists for all
n
.
Our definition avoids this technical difficulty since
{\mathcal{I}}_{1,n}
always admits a (symmetric) submodular critical price schedule.
We say that the difference
n-k-1
is the
gap
of the SJA with
n
items.
If the maximal selling candidate
{\mathcal{I}}_{n-1,n}
is solvable, then the gap counts the layers
\tbinom{[n]}{n-1},\dots,\tbinom{[n]}{k+1}
which must be removed to obtain a submodular critical price schedule.

For the remainder of this section, we slightly abuse notation writing
p_{k}
for the price for all sets of cardinality
k
.
Moreover, we will refer to the price vector
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
associated to SJA as the
SJA-prices
.
The next proposition shows that SJA is a candidate for the optimal auction.

Proposition 19
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\mathcal{I}}_{n-1,n}
is solvable.
Then SJA for

Proof.

Condition (

.
We want to check condition (

Let

be the vector of SJA-prices, and
{\mathcal{I}}_{k,n}
be the corresponding candidate set.
We need to show that

for all

Remark 20

If

The next two lemmas are useful for the computation of the SJA-prices and their expected revenue.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in[n]}
be a submodular and symmetric price schedule and

.
Then the projected region
\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})
equals the SIM-body
\Lambda(p_{1},p_{2}-p_{1},\dots,p_{\#I}-p_{\#I-1})
, provided

.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\pi_{I}(D_{\emptyset})\

\displaystyle=\ \left\{x\in[0,1]^{I}\;\Bigg|\;\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\leq p_{k}\text{ for all $J\in\tbinom{I}{k}$, $k\geq 1$}\right\}

\displaystyle=\ \Lambda(p_{1},p_{2}-p_{1},\dots,p_{\#I}-p_{\#I-1})\kern 5.0pt,

since

.
Note that for the first equality we used Lemma
13
, and for the latter equality we used Proposition

.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in[n]}
be a submodular and symmetric price schedule.
Then the region
D_{[k]}
is congruent to the product of SIM-bodies

\Lambda\Big(1-(p_{k}-p_{k-1}),\dots,1-(p_{2}-p_{1}),1-p_{1}\Big)\times\Lambda\Big(p_{k+1}-p_{k},\dots,p_{n}-p_{n-1}\Big)\kern 5.0pt,

for all
k\in[n]
.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
14
that for a submodular price schedule the region
D_{I}
can be written as

D_{I}=\pi_{I}(D_{I})\times\pi_{I^{c}}(D_{I})\kern 5.0pt,

where
\pi_{I}
is the projection onto the coordinates in

and

.
Lemma 6.4 in
[
17
]
shows, that for a region

of SJA we get

\pi_{I}(D_{[k]})\ =\ \biggl\{x\in[0,1]^{k}\biggm|\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\geq p_{k}-p_{k-\#J}\text{ for all }J\subseteq[k]\biggr\}

and

\pi_{I^{c}}(D_{[k]})\ =\ \biggl\{x\in[0,1]^{n-k}\biggm|\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\leq p_{k+\#J}-p_{k}\text{ for all }J\subseteq[n-k]\biggr\}\kern 5.0pt.

The proof of those equations only uses the fact, that the price schedule of SJA is submodular and symmetric, thus it is applicable in our more general sense.
Next, it is immediate, that
\pi_{I^{c}}(D_{[k]})=\Lambda(p_{k+1}-p_{k},\dots,p_{n}-p_{n-1})
.
The central symmetry around the point
\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}
, where

is the all ones vector, shows that

is congruent to

.
∎

Lemma 23
.

Let
p=\{p_{k}\}_{k\in[n]}
be a submodular and symmetric price schedule with
p_{1}<1
.
Then there exists
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
such that
p
admits
{\mathcal{I}}_{k,n}
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, let us assume there is a number
\ell<n
such that
\vol D_{[\ell]}>0
but
\vol D_{[\ell-1]}=0
.
Since
\vol D_{[\ell]}>0
, we can apply Lemma
12
and conclude that
p_{\ell}<p_{\ell+1}
.
Next, we want to show that
p_{\ell}\leq p_{\ell-1}
.
Let
x\in[0,1]^{n}
with
x_{i}=1
for
i\in[\ell-1]
and
x_{i}=0
otherwise.
Since
\vol D_{[\ell-1]}=0
, there is some
\ell^{\prime}
such that

\sum_{i\in[\ell-1]}x_{i}-p_{\ell-1}\ \leq\ \sum_{i\in[\ell^{\prime}]}x_{i}-p_{\ell^{\prime}}\kern 5.0pt.

If
\ell^{\prime}<\ell-1
we get
p_{\ell-1}\leq p_{\ell^{\prime}}+(\ell-1-\ell^{\prime})p_{1}
by submodularity, which results in

\sum_{i\in[\ell-1]}x_{i}-p_{\ell-1}\ =\ \ell-1-p_{\ell-1}\ \geq\ \ell^{\prime}-p_{\ell^{\prime}}+(\ell-1-\ell^{\prime})(1-p_{1})\ >\ \ell^{\prime}-p_{\ell^{\prime}}\ =\ \sum_{i\in[\ell^{\prime}]}x_{i}-p_{\ell^{\prime}}\kern 5.0pt.

Therefore
\ell^{\prime}>\ell-1
.
But then
\sum_{i\in[\ell^{\prime}]}x_{i}=\ell-1=\sum_{i\in[\ell-1]}x_{i}
, whence
p_{\ell^{\prime}}\leq p_{\ell-1}
.
Applying Lemma
12
again, from
\ell^{\prime}>\ell-1
we obtain
p_{\ell}\leq p_{\ell-1}
.
Finally, the submodularity of

implies
p_{\ell+1}\leq p_{\ell}+p_{\ell}-p_{\ell-1}\leq p_{\ell}
, and this contradicts
p_{\ell}<p_{\ell+1}
.

Further, we need to show that
\vol D_{[n]}>0
.
We do this by showing that the point

is in
D_{[n]}
.
This is enough because also

if
x_{i}^{\prime}\geq x_{i}
for all

.
By submodularity we get
p_{n}\leq p_{\ell}+(n-\ell)\cdot p_{1}
for all

.
Therefore

\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}-p_{n}\ =\ n\cdot p_{1}-p_{n}\ \geq\ \ell\cdot p_{1}-p_{\ell}+(n-\ell)(p_{1}-p_{1})\ \geq\ \sum_{i\in[\ell]}x_{i}-p_{\ell}\kern 5.0pt,

which proves that

.
∎

We call a submodular auction

if at least one of the submodularity conditions is satisfied with equality, i.e., there are bundles which are tight.
By Proposition

SJA is a candidate for the optimal auction.
The next results shows, that it is the only candidate among all submodular and symmetric auctions which are nondegenerate.

Theorem 24
.

Let
M
be a deterministic, submodular and symmetric auction for
n
items which is not degenerate.
If

satisfies the necessary critical point conditions (1)–(4) of Proposition

Proof.

Let
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
be the vector of SJA-prices for
n
items and let

{\mathcal{I}}_{k,n}
is the selling candidate of SJA.
Let

be the price schedule of

.
Assume that
q\neq p
.
We will show, that
q
then infringes one of the critical conditions.
If
q_{1}=1

for

Therefore

2

Hence we assume

Now we can apply Lemma

{\mathcal{I}}_{k^{\prime},n}

Since

For

Therefore, the prices

Since SJA is critical with respect to

Next, we claim

With this notation and using the proof of Theorem

Note that SJA can be degenerate.
This is the case if the gap is at least

Still SJA is a strong candidate for the optimal auction.

Corollary 25
.

SJA is optimal among all deterministic, submodular and symmetric auctions, unless other critical degenerate auctions exist.

Proof.

The set of all deterministic, submodular and symmetric auctions is given by the set of submodular and symmetric price schedules.
This set is bounded and closed, hence it is compact.
Since the expected revenue is a continuous function depending on the price schedule, there is an optimal price schedule which maximizes the expected revenue.
If there is no critical price schedule other than SJA, that must be optimal.
∎

4.
Volume Formulae

In Section
3
we have proven that critical price schedules are characterized by the volumes of polytopes that appear as the regions of a tropical hypersurface, and that the projections of these polytopes are SIM-bodies.
The latter have been recognized as generalized permutahedra in Section
2
.
This opens up several paths for computing and analyzing volumes of SIM-bodies, all of which have their merits.
Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias proposed to recursively evaluate integrals
[
17
, (12)]
.
Throughout we fix parameters
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
and abbreviate
\Lambda=\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.

Dragon marriage

A substantial part of Postnikov’s work
[
26
]
deals with rewriting generalized permutahedra in order to obtain various formulae for volumes and counting lattice points.
Here we follow the approach in
[
26
, §9]
.
With this, for
I\in\tbinom{[n+1]}{k}
and
k\geq 1
, we set

(16)

y_{I}\ =\ \begin{cases}\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}(-1)^{i+k}\binom{k-2}{i}\alpha_{n-i},&\text{ if }n+1\in I\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}\kern 5.0pt.\end{cases}

Note that the value
y_{I}
only depends on the cardinality of
I
and the parameters
\alpha_{i}
.
We write standard simplices as
\Delta_{I}=\conv\{e_{i}\mid i\in I\}
.
The following is a description of the SIM-bodies in terms of
“nested sets”
in the sense of
[
26
, §7]
.

Proposition 27
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
we have

(17)

\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\ =\ P_{n+1}\{z_{I}\}\ =\ \sum_{I\subseteq[n+1]}y_{I}\Delta_{I}\kern 5.0pt,

where
y_{I}
is defined as in (
16
), and
z_{I}
as in (
2
).

Proof.

Verify that
\sum_{J\subseteq I}y_{J}=z_{I}
for
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq[n+1]
.
With Proposition
3
the claim follows from
[
26
, Remark 6.4]
.
∎

Remark 28
.

Note that the decomposition (
17
) is not necessarily a Minkowski sum of standard simplices, as the parameters
y_{I}
may be negative.
For instance, with
n=3
and
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(4,3,1)
, we obtain
y_{[4]}=\alpha_{3}-2\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}=-1
.

Let
\Phi_{n}=\{d\in[n]^{n}\mid d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}\}
be the set of weakly ascending integer vectors of length
n
, whose entries lie in the set
[n]
.
For
d\in\Phi_{n}
we let
M_{d}
denote the number of labeled bipartite graphs with left degree vector
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, sorted in weakly ascending order, which satisfy Hall’s condition; i.e., they include a perfect matching.

Corollary 29
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
the volume of
\Lambda=\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
equals

\vol_{n}(\Lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{d\in\Phi_{n}}M_{d}\ \omega_{d_{1}}\cdots\omega_{d_{n}}\kern 5.0pt,

where

\omega_{k}\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i+k-1}\binom{k-1}{i}\alpha_{n-i}\kern 5.0pt.

In particular, the normalized volume
n!\cdot\vol_{n}(\Lambda)
is an integral polynomial which is homogeneous of degree
n
in the parameters.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the generic case
\alpha_{1}>\dots>\alpha_{n}>0
, from which the degenerate cases follow by continuity.
By Proposition
27
and
[
26
, Corollary 9.4]
we have

(18)

\vol_{n}(\Lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{(J_{1},...,J_{n})}y_{J_{1}}\cdots y_{J_{n}}\kern 5.0pt,

where the sum is taken over ordered collections of nonempty subsets
J_{1},\dots,J_{n}\subseteq[n+1]
such that, for pairwise distinct
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
, we have

(19)

\#\bigl(J_{i_{1}}\cup\dots\cup J_{i_{k}}\bigr)\ \geq\ k+1\kern 5.0pt.

The volume formula (
18
) can be simplified by removing zero terms arising from the case distinction in (
16
); moreover,
y_{I}=y_{I^{\prime}}
whenever
I
and
I^{\prime}
share the same cardinality.
We have
\omega_{k}=y_{I}
if
I
contains
n+1
and exactly
k
additional elements in
[n]
.

For
d_{i}=\#J_{i}-1
the product
\omega_{d_{1}}\cdots\omega_{d_{n}}
equals
y_{J_{1}}\cdots y_{J_{n}}
, if each
J_{i}\subseteq[n+1]
satisfies
n+1\in J_{i}
.
From
J_{1},\dots,J_{n}
with weakly ascending cardinalities we obtain a bipartite graph by taking
J_{i}\setminus\{n+1\}
as the set of right neighbors of the left node
i
.
Now the requirement (
19
) on
J_{1},\dots,J_{n}
translates into
\#((J_{i_{1}}\setminus\{n+1\})\cup\dots\cup(J_{i_{k}}\setminus\{n+1\}))\geq k
, which is Hall’s condition.
∎

The inequality (
19
) is the
dragon marriage condition

[
26
, Proposition 5.4]
.
There are many other ways to compute volumes of SIM-bodies: for instance, via integration
[
17
, Equation (12)]
.

Lawrence’s method

We can exploit that the regular SIM-bodies are simple polytopes.
Let
P=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid Ax\leq b\}
be a rational simple
n
-polytope with vertex set
V
such that
(A\ b)
is facet defining.
We assume that the rows of
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m\times n}
are the primitive (outward) facet normals.
Then each vertex
v
is incident with exactly
n
facets, i.e., there is an invertible
n{\times}n
-submatrix
A_{v}
of
A
such that
A_{v}v=b_{v}
, where
b_{v}
is the subvector of
b
corresponding to the rows of
A_{v}
.
Let
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
be a linear objective function which is not constant on any edge of
P
.
That is,
c
induces an acyclic orientation on the vertex-edge graph of
P
.
We consider the vector
\gamma^{v}:={A_{v}}^{-\top}c
, where
{A_{v}}^{-\top}={({A_{v}}^{\top})}^{-1}
is the inverse transpose.
The vector
c
is
generic
for
P
if it is not constant on edges and additionally
\gamma_{v}\neq 0
for all vertices
v
of
P
.
For such data Lawrence
[
21
]
showed

(20)

\vol_{n}(P)\ =\ \frac{1}{n!}\cdot\sum_{v\in V}\frac{\langle c,v\rangle^{n}}{|\det A_{v}|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{v}}\kern 5.0pt.

See also
[
7
, §3]
.

A rational polytope
P
is called
Delzant
if the primitive edge directions at each vertex form a lattice basis.
This property is equivalent to the condition that the projective toric variety associated with the normal fan of
P
does not have any singularities, i.e., it is smooth in the sense of differential geometry; see
[
9
, Theorem 2.4.3]
.
Since a lattice basis obviously needs to form a basis, a smooth polytope is necessarily simple, but the converse does not hold, in general.
We will show that the SIM-bodies have that property.
Note that Lawrence formula is particularly useful for Delzant polytopes since then
|\det A_{v}|=1
in (
20
), which simplifies the computation; this will be used in Section
5
.
We define

A_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}C_{k}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&-I_{n-k}\end{array}\right],\quad C_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&&&0\\
1&1&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
1&1&\dots&1\end{array}\right],\quad b_{\alpha^{(k)}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right),

where
A_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
C_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
, and
I_{n-k}
is the identity matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}
.
By Proposition
4
each vertex
v
of
\Lambda
can be written as
\sigma(\alpha^{(k)})
, where
\alpha^{(k)}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},0,\dots,0)
, and
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
is a permutation.
It follows from Proposition
3
that
A_{k}\alpha^{(k)}=b_{\alpha^{(k)}}
.
Note that the matrix
A_{k}
only depends on
k
, not on the parameters
\alpha_{i}
.

Proposition 31
.

Any proper SIM-body is a Delzant polytope.

Proof.

This follows from
\det(A_{k})=\pm 1
.
∎

Now (
20
) specializes as follows.

Lemma 32
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
the volume of
\Lambda=\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
equals

(21)

\vol_{n}(\Lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{n!}\cdot\sum_{k\in[n]}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sym(n)}\frac{{\langle\sigma(c),\alpha^{(k)}\rangle}^{n}}{(n-k)!\prod_{i=1}^{n}({A_{k}}^{-\top}\sigma(c))_{i}}\kern 5.0pt,

for any generic objective function
c
.

Proof.

Again it suffices to consider the generic case
\alpha_{1}>\dots>\alpha_{n}>0
, from which the degenerate cases follow by continuity.
Let
P_{\sigma}
be the permutation matrix corresponding to
\sigma
, i.e.,
v=P_{\sigma}\alpha^{(k)}
.
Then we obtain
A_{v}=A_{k}P_{\sigma}^{-1}
and
b_{v}=b_{\alpha^{(k)}}
.
In (
21
) we can leave out the origin, which is a vertex, since it does not contribute to the final volume.
The factor
(n-k)!
in the denominator comes from the fact, that all permutations which permute the
0
-entries of
\alpha^{(k)}
count the same vertex.
Moreover, the scalar product in the numerator satisfies
\langle c,P_{\sigma}\alpha^{(k)}\rangle=\langle P_{\sigma}^{-1}c,\alpha^{(k)}\rangle
, and
P_{\sigma}^{-1}c=\sigma^{-1}(c)
.
Finally, notice that iterating over all permutations
\sigma
or their inverses is the same.
∎

This leads us to identifying those linear objective functions which are generic.

Lemma 33
.

An objective function
c
is generic for
\Lambda
if and only if the coefficients of
c
are nonzero and pairwise distinct.
In particular,
c=(1,2,\dots,n)
is generic.

Proof.

The edge directions of
\Lambda
are
e_{i}-e_{j}
for
i\neq j
or
e_{i}
.
Consequently,
c
is not constant on any edge of
\Lambda
if and only if
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
for
i\neq j
and
c_{i}\neq 0
.
In that case, a routine computation yields
({A_{k}}^{-\top}\sigma(c))_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
,
k
and
\sigma
.
∎

While the volume formula (
21
) is fairly compact, it can be rewritten to improve the efficiency of evaluation.
Our goal is to get rid of the factor
(n-k)!
in the denominator by enumerating each vertex only once.
To this end we gather some useful observations.
First, the objective function
c=(1,2,\dots,n)
does not depend on the parameters
\alpha_{i}
.
Second, we compute

{A_{k}}^{-\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}{C_{k}}^{-\top}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&-I_{n-k}\end{array}\right]\,,\qquad{C_{k}}^{-\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-1&&&0\\
&1&-1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&1&-1\\
0&&&&1\end{array}\right]\kern 5.0pt.

We fix some
k\in[n]
.
Recall from the proof of Lemma
32
permuting the vector
\alpha^{(k)}
gives the same as permuting the objective function
c
by the inverse permutation.
Then two permutations
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
correspond to the same vertex if and only if the first
k
coordinates of
\sigma_{1}^{-1}(c)
and
\sigma_{2}^{-1}(c)
are the same.
This allows us to first choose
k
entries of
c=(1,2,\dots,n)
and then go through all
k!
permutations of those vectors.
We arrive at the following.

Proposition 34
.

For
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
the volume of
\Lambda=\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
equals

\vol_{n}(\Lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k\in[n]}\sum_{L\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sym(k)}\frac{{\langle\sigma(L),\alpha^{[k]}\rangle}^{n}}{\sigma(L)_{k}\cdot\prod_{i\in[k-1]}\left(\sigma(L)_{i}-\sigma(L)_{i+1}\right)\cdot\prod_{i\notin L}(-i)}\kern 5.0pt,

where
\alpha^{[k]}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
, and we sort
L\in\binom{[n]}{k}
ascendingly to obtain a vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

Lorentzian polynomials

In this section we will exhibit yet another representation of the SIM-bodies which is similar to (
17
), but here we obtain a proper Minkowski sum decomposition.
This will reveal interesting structural properties of the volume polynomials.
To this and we set
\beta_{k}:=\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k+1}
and
\alpha_{n+1}:=0
.
As the parameters
\alpha_{i}
are strictly decreasing, the new parameters
\beta_{i}
are strictly positive.

Proposition 35
.

We have

(22)

\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}\,Q(i,n)\kern 5.0pt,

where

Q(k,n)\ :=\ \Biggl\{x\in[0,1]^{n}\Biggm|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq k\Biggr\}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof.

We will first check the inclusion from right to left.
Let
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}q^{(k)}
with
q^{(k)}\in\beta_{k}\,Q(k,n)
.
For
j,k\in[n]
we have
q^{(k)}_{j}\leq\beta_{k}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}q^{(k)}_{i}\leq k\beta_{k}
.
Thus each set
J\subseteq[n]
satisfies
\sum_{j\in J}q^{(k)}_{j}\leq\beta_{k}\cdot\min\{k,\#J\}
.
We get

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\ =\ \sum_{j\in J}\sum_{k=1}^{n}q^{(k)}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\cdot\min\{k,\#J\}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}(\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k+1})\cdot\min\{k,\#J\}\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{\#J}\alpha_{k}\kern 5.0pt,

and thus
x\in\Lambda
.

For the reverse inclusion consider an arbitrary point
x\in\Lambda
.
This can be written as a convex combination of the vertices.
As the origin is a vertex of
\Lambda
, we obtain a function
\gamma:[n]\times S_{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\gamma(k,\sigma)\leq 1
such that

x\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\gamma(k,\sigma)\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}e_{i}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Note that
\gamma
is not unique, since
x
can be written as a convex combination in more than one way.
Permutations are linear, and so we get:

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma(k,\sigma)\alpha_{i}\sigma(e_{i})\ =\ \sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma(k,\sigma)\left(\sum_{j=i}^{n}\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j+1}\right)\sigma(e_{i})

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j+1})\sum_{i=1}^{j}\sum_{k=i}^{n}\gamma(k,\sigma)\sigma(e_{i})

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}\left[\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\gamma(k,\sigma)\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}\right)+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}\gamma(k,\sigma)\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}e_{i}\right)\right]\kern 5.0pt.

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\gamma(k,\sigma)\leq 1
, the vector enclosed in brackets
[...]
is contained in the polytope
\conv\{\bigcup_{k=1}^{j}{\mathcal{P}}(\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i})\cup\{{\mathbf{0}}\}\}=Q(j,n)
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Following Brändén and Huh
[
4
]
a homogeneous real polynomial with positive coefficients is
Lorentzian
if all its iterated partial derivatives all the way down to degree two yield quadratic forms of Lorentzian signature
({-}{+}{+}\cdots{+})
.
It turns out that the volume polynomials of the SIM-bodies belong to this class, up to a linear substitution.

Theorem 36
.

The polynomial

\lambda(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})\ =\ \vol_{n}\Big(\Lambda\left(\,\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{n},\,\beta_{2}+\dots+\beta_{n},\,\dots,\,\beta_{n}\,\right)\Big)

is Lorentzian.

Proof.

Recall that
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
if and only if
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\geq 0
.
Proposition
35
shows that
\Lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i},\sum_{i=2}^{n}\beta_{i},\dots,\beta_{n}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}Q(i,n)
, and the claim follows from
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

.

The normalized volume of a 3-dimensional SIM-body reads

\displaystyle\vol_{3}(

\displaystyle\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}))\ =

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{3}+3\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}+3\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{3}+3\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{2}+6\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}-6\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}^{2}-2\alpha_{2}^{3}-6\alpha_{2}^{2}\alpha_{3}+3\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}^{2}+\alpha_{3}^{3}\kern 5.0pt.

Substituting
\beta_{3}=\alpha_{3}
,
\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2}-\beta_{3}
,
\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}-\beta_{2}-\beta_{3}
results in

\displaystyle\lambda(

\displaystyle\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})\ =

\displaystyle\beta_{1}^{3}+6\beta_{1}^{2}\beta_{2}+9\beta_{1}^{2}\beta_{3}+12\beta_{1}\beta_{2}^{2}+36\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}+18\beta_{1}\beta_{3}^{2}+5\beta_{2}^{3}+18\beta_{2}^{2}\beta_{3}+18\beta_{2}\beta_{3}^{2}+6\beta_{3}^{3}\kern 5.0pt.

Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias listed the SJA-prices for up to six items

Here we extend that computation to

items using
polymake

[

HomotopyContinuation.jl

[

While some of our computations employ numerical methods, our results for

We employ the volume computations for SIM-bodies as laid out in Section

Consider the SJA of
n

p_{k}^{(n)}

When

Notice that the price polynomial

does not depend on

does.

Finding the SJA price schedule

The Algorithm
1
cannot be applied naively.
It rather requires an extra layer of considerations which we discuss now.
The nontrivial steps of the procedure is finding the real zeros of a polynomial with real coefficients, which is, of course, classical.
Yet Algorithm
1
pretends that those computations are exact.
The multivariate volume polynomial of the SIM-bodies and its linear substitution, the volume polynomial
v_{k}
, have rational coefficients; so there is no issue with an exact representation.
Yet the univariate polynomial
w(x)=v_{k}(p_{1},p_{2}-p_{1},\dots,p_{k-i}-p_{k-i-1},x-p_{k-i},0,\dots,0)
depends on the prices
p_{1},\dots,p_{k-1}
, which are algebraic numbers.
A naive approach would be to apply numerical methods to find the first price, substitute and iterate the process.
However, the increasing degrees of the price polynomials amplify even a small initial error enormously.
Therefore, instead of this iterative approach we applied
HomotopyContinuation.jl
to the entire polynomial system (
23
), as this can return exact certificates
[
5
]
.
In this way we were able to compute numerical approximations with at least 15 exact decimal digits (precision at 53 bits) for all
n\leq 10
.
For

We could apply the (nonexact) iterative method for
n=11
and
n=12

6.
Conclusion

The revenue optimization of auctions with multiple items is notoriously difficult.
In the present paper this is reflected in metric intricacies concerning special classes of generalized permutahedra and ensuing algebraic questions.
We believe that answers to the following open questions will lead to a better understanding.

As already suggested by Giannakopoulos and Koutsoupias, the SJA seems to be the only immediate candidate for an optimal auction in the submodular and symmetric setting.
A major technical obstacle in any attempt to confirm or refute the corresponding
[
17
, Conjecture 4.3]
seems to be the following.

Question 39
.

How large is the gap of SJA for
n
items?

From Table
1
we see that the gap equals two for
n=11
, and it is one for
n=12
.
So as a function of
n
the gap is not monotone.

There may be more to say from the point of view of tropical geometry.
The support of the tropical polynomial
u
in (
6
) is formed by the vertices of the unit cube.
The prices, which enter as the coefficients of
u
, give rise to a regular subdivision,
{\mathcal{S}}(u)
, on
[0,1]^{n}
which is dual to the tropical hypersurface
{\mathcal{T}}(u)
; see
[
19
, §1.2]
.
In this way, finding optimal prices becomes a nonlinear optimization problem over the secondary cone of
{\mathcal{S}}(u)
.

Question 40
.

What can be learned from this dual point of view?
Does it help to understand submodular auctions which are degenerate?
