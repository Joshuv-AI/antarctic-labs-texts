Coincidence point results involving a generalized class of simulation functions
D.K. Patel, P.R. Patle, L. Budhia, D. Gopal

Abstract
. The purpose of this work is to introduce a general class of C G C_{G} -simulation functions and obtained some new coincidence and common fixed points results in metric spaces. Some useful examples are presented to illustrate our theorems. Results obtained in this paper extend, generalize and unify some well known fixed and common fixed point results.

Introduction

Because of its large number of applications in various branches of mathematical sciences, fixed point theory enticed many mathematicians. The most relevant result of the theory is Banach contraction principle (BCP). Several authors have studied on various generalizations of Banach contraction mapping principle. 3.
Main results

In this section, we introduce the new concept of Suzuki type contractions involving
C_{G}
-simulation functions which is used to prove several coincidence point theorems in complete metric spaces.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
f,g:X\to X
be self-mappings.
A mapping
f
is called a Suzuki type
(Z_{G},g)
-contraction if there exists
\zeta\in Z_{G}
such that

\frac{1}{2}d(gx,fx)<d(gx,gy)\implies\zeta(d(fx,fy),d(gx,gy))\geq C_{G}

(3.1)

for all
x,y\in X
with
gx\neq gy.

If
g=i_{X}
(identity mapping on
X
) and
C_{G}=0
, we get Suzuki type
\mathcal{Z}
-contraction of
[
17
]
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
f,g:X\to X
be self-mappings.
The mapping
f
is called a Suzuki type generalized
(Z_{G},g)
-contraction if there exists
\zeta\in Z_{G}
such that

\frac{1}{2}d(gx,fx)<d(gx,gy)~~~\Longrightarrow~~~\zeta\left(d(fx,fy),M(x,y)\right)\geq C_{G}

(3.2)

for all
x,y\in X
with
gx\neq gy
, where

M(x,y)=\max\left\{d(gx,gy),d(gx,fx),d(gy,fy),\frac{d(gx,fy)+d(gy,fx)}{2}\right\}.

If
g=i_{X}
(identity mapping on
X
) and
C_{G}=0
, we get Suzuki type generalized
\mathcal{Z}
-contraction.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
f,g:X\to X
be self-mappings and
f
be a Suzuki type generalized
(Z_{G},g)
-contraction. Assume that the following conditions hold:

(i)

f(X)\subseteq g(X)
,

(ii)

g(X)
or
f(X)
is complete.

Then
f
and
g
have unique point of coincidence.

Proof.

Suppose that the point of coincidence exists, then the uniqueness follows as: let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be two distinct point of coincidence of
f
and
g
i.e.
u_{1}=fv_{1}=gv_{1}
and
u_{2}=fv_{2}=gv_{2}
for some
v_{1},v_{2}\in X
. Now, since

\frac{1}{2}d(gv_{1},fv_{1})=0<d(gv_{1},gv_{2}),

so by using (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle C_{G}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta(d(fv_{1},fv_{2}),M(v_{1},v_{2}))

\displaystyle=\zeta(d(u_{1},u_{2}),d(u_{1},u_{2}))<G(d(u_{1},u_{2}),d(u_{1},u_{2}))\leq C_{G},

which is a contradiction. Thus, the point of coincidence is unique.

Consider the sequences
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
defined as

y_{n}=fx_{n}=gx_{n+1}~\text{for all}~n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

and assume that
y_{n}\neq y_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Let
x=x_{n+1},y=x_{n+2}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},fx_{n+1})=\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2})<d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2}).

So from (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle C_{G}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\Big(d(fx_{n+1},fx_{n+2}),M(x_{n+1},x_{n+2})\Big)

\displaystyle=\zeta\Big(d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2}),\max\left\{d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})\right\}\Big)

\displaystyle<G\Big(\max\left\{d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})\right\},d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})\Big).

Further, using (i) of Definition
2.2
, we have

\max\left\{d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})\right\}>d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2}).

Hence for all for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we have
d(y_{n},y_{n+1})>d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})
. So
\{d(y_{n},y_{n+1})\}
is a monotonically decreasing sequence of nonnegative real numbers, and hence there exists
l\geq 0
such that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}d(y_{n},y_{n+1})=l
. Assume that
l>0
. Since
d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})<M(x_{n+1},x_{n+2})
, both
d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2})
and
M(x_{n+1},x_{n+2})
tends to
l
as
n\to\infty
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d(y_{n},y_{n+1})=\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},fx_{n+1})<d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2})=d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),

then using (
3.2
) and (b) of Definition
2.4
, we get

C_{G}\leq\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sup\zeta\Big(d(y_{n+1},y_{n+2}),M(x_{n+1},x_{n+2})\Big)<C_{G},

which is a contradiction and hence
l=0
.
Now if we assume that
y_{n}=y_{m}
for some
n>m
. Then we can choose
x_{n+1}=x_{m+1}
and hence also
y_{n+1}=y_{m+1}
. Then using the similar arguments as above we get

d(y_{n},y_{n+1})<d(y_{n-1},y_{n})<\cdots<d(y_{m},y_{m+1})=d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),

which is a contradiction. Similarly if
y_{n}=y_{m}
for some
n<m
, we get a contradiction. Hence
y_{n}\neq y_{m}
for all
n\neq m
.

Now we prove that
\{y_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence. If not, then by Lemma
2.2
we have

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}d(x_{m(k)},x_{n(k)})=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}d(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})=\varepsilon,

and consequently,

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}M(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})=\varepsilon.

We claim that there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{1}{2}d(y_{m(k)},y_{m(k)+1})<d(y_{m(k)},y_{n(k)}),

\text{i.e.}~~~\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m(k)+1},fx_{m(k)+1})<d(gx_{m(k)+1},gx_{n(k)+1})~~~~~~

for all
k\geq k_{0}
. If not, then letting
k\to\infty
, we get
\varepsilon\leq 0
, which is not true. Also, using (
3.2
) and (i) of Definition
2.2
, we have
d(y_{m(k)+1},y_{n(k)+1})<M(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})
, then by (b) of Definition
2.4
, we get

C_{G}\leq\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\sup\zeta\Big(d(y_{m(k)+1},y_{n(k)+1}),M(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})\Big)<C_{G}

a contradiction. Hence
\{y_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence.

Suppose
g(X)
is complete subspace of
X
. Then
\{y_{n}\}
being contained in
g(X)
has a limit in
g(X)
, say
z\in X
such that
y_{n}\to gz
, i.e.
gx_{n}\to gz
as
n\to\infty
. We will show that
z
is the coincidence point of
f
and
g
. We can suppose
y_{n}\neq fz,gz
for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, otherwise we are done.
We claim that

\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n},gx_{n+1})<d(gx_{n},gz)~~\text{or}~~\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2})<d(gx_{n+1},gz)

(3.3)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. If not, then there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
for which

\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})\geq d(gx_{m},gz)~~\text{and}~~\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})\geq d(gx_{m+1},gz)

holds. Then

2d(gx_{m},gz)\leq d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})\leq d(gx_{m},gz)+d(gz,gx_{m+1})

which implies
d(gx_{m},gz)\leq d(gz,gx_{m+1})\leq\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2}).
Further since

\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m},fx_{m})=\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})<d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1}),

so from (
3.1
), we have

C_{G}\leq\zeta(d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1}),M(x_{m},x_{m+1}))<G(M(x_{m},x_{m+1}),d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1})),

and in view of (i) of Definition
2.2
, we get
M(x_{m},x_{m+1})=d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})>d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1})
. Now

\displaystyle d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1})

\displaystyle<d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq d(gx_{m},gz)+d(gz,gx_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})+\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})

\displaystyle=d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})=d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1}),

which is a contradiction. Hence (
3.3
) holds. Now from (
3.3
) and (
3.1
) we have

C_{G}\leq\zeta\Big(d(fx_{n},fz),M(x_{n},z)\Big)<G\Big(M(x_{n},z),d(fx_{n},fz)\Big)

and then by (i) of Definition
2.2
, we get
d(fx_{n},fz)<M(x_{n},z).
Now, if we assume
fz\neq gz
, then taking limit as
n\to\infty
, we get
d(gz,fz)<d(gz,fz)
, a contradiction. Hence
gz=fz.
In same manner if
\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2})<d(gx_{n+1},gv)
we can show that
gz=fz
. In case
f(X)
is a complete subspace of
X
, the sequence
\{y_{n}\}
converges in
g(X)
since
f(X)\subseteq g(X)
. So the previous argument works.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space,
f,g:X\to X
be self-mappings and
f
be a Suzuki type generalized
(Z_{G},g)
-contraction.
Assume that the following conditions hold:

(i)

f(X)\subseteq g(X)
,

(ii)

g
is a continuous,

(iii)

f
and
g
are commuting.

Then
f
and
g
have unique point of coincidence.

Proof.

If the coincidence point exist then it is unique (as proved in Theorem
3.1
).
Construct the sequences
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
as

y_{n}=fx_{n}=gx_{n+1}~\text{for all}~n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Then the sequence
\{y_{n}\}
is Cauchy (as shown in proof of Theorem
3.1
).

Since the space
(X,d)
is complete, there exists
z\in X
such that
y_{n}\to z
or
gx_{n}\to z
as
n\to\infty
. As
g
is continuous,
g^{2}x_{n}\to gz
as
n\to\infty
. We claim that

\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{n},ggx_{n+1})<d(ggx_{n},ggz)~~\text{or}~~\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{n+1},ggx_{n+2})<d(ggx_{n+1},ggz)

(3.4)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. If not, then there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
for which

\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m},ggx_{m+1})\geq d(ggx_{m},ggz)~~\text{and}~~\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m+1},ggx_{m+2})\geq d(ggx_{m+1},ggz)

holds.
Then

2d(ggx_{m},ggz)\leq d(ggx_{m},ggx_{m+1})\leq d(ggx_{m},ggz)+d(ggz,ggx_{m+1})

which implies

d(ggx_{m},ggz)\leq d(ggz,ggx_{m+1})\leq\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m+1},ggx_{m+2}).

Further since
f
and
g
are commutative, we have

\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m},fgx_{m})=\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m},gfx_{m})<d(ggx_{m},ggx_{m+1}).

So from (
3.2
), we have

C_{G}\leq\zeta\Big(d(fgx_{m},fgx_{m+1}),M(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})\Big)<G\Big(M(gx_{m},gx_{m+1}),d(fgx_{m},fgx_{m+1})\Big)

and in view of (i) of Definition
2.2
, we get
M(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})=d(ggx_{m},ggx_{m+1})>d(fgx_{m},fgx_{m+1})
. Now

\displaystyle d(fgx_{m},fgx_{m+1})

\displaystyle<d(ggx_{m},ggx_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq d(ggx_{m},ggz)+d(ggz,ggx_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m+1},ggx_{m+2})+\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{m+1},ggx_{m+2})

\displaystyle=d(ggx_{m+1},ggx_{m+2})=d(fgx_{m},fgx_{m+1}),

which is a contradiction. Hence (
3.4
) holds. Now from (
3.4
) and (
3.2
), we have

C_{G}\leq\zeta\Big(d(fgx_{n},fz),M(gx_{n},z)\Big)<G\Big(M(gx_{n},z),d(fgx_{n},fz)\Big)

and then by (i) of Definition
2.2
and
f,g
are commuting, we get
d(fgx_{n},fz)=d(gfx_{n},fz)<M(gx_{n},z)
. Now, if we assume
fz\neq gz
, then taking limit as
n\to\infty
and by continuity of
g
, we get
d(gz,fz)<d(gz,fz)
, a contradiction. Hence
gz=fz.
In same manner if
\frac{1}{2}d(ggx_{n+1},ggx_{n+2})<d(ggx_{n+1},ggv)
we can show that
gz=fz.

∎

Then the sequence

is Cauchy (as shown in proof of Theorem

is complete subspace of

being contained in

has a limit in

, say

such that

, i.e.

as

n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, otherwise we are done. We also claim that

\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n},gx_{n+1})<d(gx_{n},gz)~~\text{or}~~\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2})<d(gx_{n+1},gz)

(3.5)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. If not, then there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
for which

\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})\geq d(gx_{m},gz)~~\text{and}~~\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})\geq d(gx_{m+1},gz)

holds. Then

2d(gx_{m},gz)\leq d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})\leq d(gx_{m},gz)+d(gz,gx_{m+1})

which implies
d(gx_{m},gz)\leq d(gz,gx_{m+1})\leq\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2}).
Further since

\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m},fx_{m})=\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})<d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1}),

so from (
3.1
), we have

C_{G}\leq\zeta(d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1}),d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1}))<G(d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1}),d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1})),

and in view of (i) of Definition
2.2
, we get
d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})>d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1}).

\displaystyle\text{Now}~~~~~d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1})

\displaystyle<d(gx_{m},gx_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq d(gx_{m},gz)+d(gz,gx_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})+\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})

\displaystyle=d(gx_{m+1},gx_{m+2})=d(fx_{m},fx_{m+1})

which is a contracdiction. Hence (
3.5
) holds. Now from (
3.5
) and (
3.1
), we have

C_{G}\leq\zeta\Big(d(fx_{n},fz),d(gx_{n},gz)\Big)<G\Big(d(gx_{n},gz),d(fx_{n},fz)\Big)

and then by (i) of Definition
2.2
, we get
d(fx_{n},fz)<d(gx_{n},gz).
Taking limit as
n\to\infty
we get
fx_{n}\to fz.
Hence
gz=fz.
In same manner if
\frac{1}{2}d(gx_{n+1},gx_{n+2})<d(gx_{n+1},gv)
, we can show that
gz=fz
. In case
f(X)
is a complete subspace of
X
, the sequence
\{y_{n}\}
converges in
g(X)
since
f(X)\subseteq g(X)
. So the previous argument works.
∎

4.
Coincidence points of rational type quasi-contractions

If
f
and
g
be two self-mappings on a metric space
(X,d)
satisfying
f(X)\subseteq g(X)
and
x_{0}\in X
, let us define
x_{1}\in X
such that
fx_{0}=gx_{1}
. Having defined
x_{n}\in X
, let
x_{n+1}\in X
be such that
fx_{n}=gx_{n+1}
. We say that
\{fx_{n}\}
is a
f
-
g
-sequence of initial point
x_{0}
. Letting
fx_{n}=gx_{n+1}=y_{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, denote

\mathcal{O}(y_{k};n)=\{y_{k},y_{k+1},y_{k+2},\cdots,y_{k+n}\}
. Let
\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{k};n)]
denotes the diameter of
\mathcal{O}(y_{k};n)
. If
\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{k};n)]>0
for
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
then we have
\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{k};n)]=d(y_{i},y_{j})
, where
k\leq i<j\leq k+n
.

Definition 4.1
.

A mapping
G:[0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
has a property
C_{G}^{m}
, if there exists an

C_{G}\geq 0
such that

(i)

G(s,t)>C_{G}
implies
\dfrac{s}{s+1}>t
,

(ii)

G(t,t)\leq C_{G}
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Denote
m(x,y)=\max\left\{d(gx,gy),d(gx,fx),d(gy,fy),d(gx,fy),d(gy,fx)\right\}.

Using the above property we prove following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
f,g:X\to X
be self-mappings satisfying the condition

C_{G}\leq\frac{m(x,y)}{m(x,y)+1}-d(fx,fy)<G(m(x,y),d(fx,fy))

(4.1)

where
G:[0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C
-class function.
Assume that the following conditions hold:

(i)

f(X)\subseteq g(X)
,

(ii)

g(X)
or
f(X)
is complete.

(iii)

if
\{t_{n}\}
and
\{s_{n}\}
are sequences in
(0,\infty)
such that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}s_{n}>0
and

t_{n}<\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n}+1}
, then
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sup\left(\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n}+1}-t_{n}\right)<C_{G}
.

Then
f
and
g
have unique point of coincidence.

Proof.

Suppose that the point of coincidence exists, then the uniqueness follows as: let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be two distinct points of coincidence of
f
and
g
, i.e.
u_{1}=fv_{1}=gv_{1}
and
u_{2}=fv_{2}=gv_{2}
for some
v_{1},v_{2}\in X
.
Then using (
4.1
) and (ii) of Definition
4.1
, we get

\displaystyle C_{G}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{m(v_{1},v_{2})}{m(v_{1},v_{2})+1}-d(fv_{1},fv_{2})<G\Big(m(v_{1},v_{2}),d(fv_{1},fv_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow C_{G}<G(d(u_{1},u_{2}),d(u_{1},u_{2}))\leq C_{G},

which is a contradiction. Thus, the point of coincidence is unique.

Now, consider the sequences
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
defined as

y_{n}=fx_{n}=gx_{n+1}~\text{for all}~n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

and assume that
y_{n}\neq y_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Taking
x=x_{n},y=x_{n+1}
in (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle C_{G}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{m(x_{n},x_{n+1})}{m(x_{n},x_{n+1})+1}-d(fx_{n},fx_{n+1})<G\Big(m(x_{n},x_{n+1}),d(fx_{n},fx_{n+1})\Big).

Using (i) of Definition
4.1
, we get

d(fx_{n},fx_{n+1})<\dfrac{m(x_{n},x_{n+1})}{m(x_{n},x_{n+1})+1}

which gives

d(y_{n},y_{n+1})<\dfrac{\max\left\{d(y_{n-1},y_{n}),d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),d(y_{n-1},y_{n+1})\right\}}{\max\left\{d(y_{n-1},y_{n}),d(y_{n},y_{n+1}),d(y_{n-1},y_{n+1})\right\}+1}

\text{i.e.}~~\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n};1)]<\frac{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-1};2)]}{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-1};2)]+1}~~~

\text{or}~~\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n};1)]<\lambda_{1}\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-1};2)],~~~

(4.2)

where

\lambda_{1}=\dfrac{1}{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-1};2)]+1}<1
.
Again using (
4.1
) and (i) of Definition
4.1
, we get

\displaystyle\delta[

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(y_{n-1};2)]=d(y_{r},y_{p}),~~\text{where}~n-1\leq r<p\leq n+1

\displaystyle<\dfrac{\max\left\{d(y_{r-1},y_{p-1}),d(y_{r-1},y_{r}),d(y_{p-1},y_{p}),d(y_{r-1},y_{p}),d(y_{p-1},y_{r})\right\}}{\max\left\{d(y_{r-1},y_{p-1}),d(y_{r-1},y_{r}),d(y_{p-1},y_{p}),d(y_{r-1},y_{p}),d(y_{p-1},y_{r})\right\}+1}

\text{i.e.}~~~\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-1};2)]<\dfrac{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-2};3)]}{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-2};3)]+1}=\lambda_{2}\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-2};3)].

(4.3)

From (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we have

\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n};1)]<\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-2};3)].

(4.4)

Continuing in same manner, we get

\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n};1)]<\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\ldots\lambda_{n}\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)],

(4.5)

where
\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)]=d(y_{r},y_{q})
for some positive
0\leq r<q\leq n+1
and
\lambda_{i}=\dfrac{1}{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n-i};i+1)]+1}<1
. Let
\lambda=\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
, then we have

\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n};1)]<\lambda^{n}\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)].

(4.6)

Now

\displaystyle\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)]=d(y_{r},y_{q})

\displaystyle\leq d(y_{r},y_{r+1})+d(y_{r+1},y_{q})

\displaystyle=d(y_{r},y_{r+1})+d(fx_{r+1},fx_{q})

\displaystyle\leq d(y_{r},y_{r+1})+\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{1},n)]

\displaystyle\leq d(y_{r},y_{r+1})+\frac{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)]}{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)]+1}

\displaystyle=d(y_{r},y_{r+1})+\beta\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)],

where
\beta=\dfrac{1}{\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)]+1}<1
.
Hence

\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{0};n+1)]\leq\frac{1}{1-\beta}d(y_{r},y_{r+1})

(4.7)

Thus, from (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), we have

\delta[\mathcal{O}(y_{n};1)]<\frac{\lambda^{n}}{1-\beta}d(y_{0},y_{1})

(4.8)

with
r=0
. Therefore, because of
\lambda<1
, we get

d(y_{n},y_{n+1})\rightarrow 0~~as~~n\to\infty.

(4.9)

Now, we claim that the sequence
\{y_{n}\}
is Cauchy. If not, then by Lemma
2.2
we have

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}d(x_{m(k)},x_{n(k)})=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}d(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})=\varepsilon,

and consequently,

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}m(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})=\varepsilon.

Also, from (
4.1
) and (i) of Definition
4.1
, we have

d(y_{m(k)+1},y_{n(k)+1})<\dfrac{m(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})}{m(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})+1}.

So by assumption (iii), we get

C_{G}\leq\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\sup\Big(\dfrac{m(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})}{m(x_{m(k)+1},x_{n(k)+1})+1}-d(y_{m(k)+1},y_{n(k)+1})\Big)<C_{G},

(4.10)

a contradiction. Hence
\{y_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence.

Now, suppose
g(X)
is complete subspace of
X
. Then there exists a point, say
z\in X
such that
y_{n}\to gz
, i.e.
gx_{n}\to gz
as
n\to\infty
. We will show that
z
is the coincidence point of
f
and
g
. We can suppose
y_{n}\neq fz,gz
for

, otherwise we are done.

Now, using (
4.1
) for
x=x_{n}
and
y=z
, we have

C_{G}\leq\dfrac{m(x_{n},z)}{m(x_{n},z)+1}-d(fx_{n},fz)<G\Big(m(x_{n},z),d(fx_{n},fz)\Big),

and then by (i) of Definition
4.1
, we get
d(fx_{n},fz)<\dfrac{m(x_{n},z)}{m(x_{n},z)+1}
. If we assume

, we get

, a contradiction. Hence

. In case

is a complete subspace of

\{y_{n}\}
converges in
g(X)
since
f(X)\subseteq g(X)
. In same manner we can show that

.
∎
