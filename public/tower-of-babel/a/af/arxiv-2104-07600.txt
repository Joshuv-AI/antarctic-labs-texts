The Effect of Population Flow on Epidemic Spread: Analysis and Control
Brooks Butler, Ciyuan Zhang, Ian Walter, Nishant Nair, Raphael Stern, and Philip E. Paré*

Abstract
In this paper, we present a discrete-time networked SEIR model using population flow, its derivation, and assumptions under which this model is well defined. We identify properties of the system’s equilibria, namely the healthy states. We show that the set of healthy states is asymptotically stable, and that the value of the equilibria becomes equal across all sub-populations as a result of the network flow model. Furthermore, we explore closed-loop feedback control of the system by limiting flow between sub-populations as a function of the current infected states. These results are illustrated via simulation based on flight traffic between major airports in the United States. We find that a flow restriction strategy combined with a vaccine roll-out significantly reduces the total number of infections over the course of an epidemic, given that the initial flow restriction response is not delayed.



I
Introduction

Global interconnectivity has proven to be a key factor in the propagation of infectious diseases
[
1
,
2
]
. Most recently, we have seen evidence of such connectivity through the rapid spread of the COVID-19 pandemic, which propagated
from its origin in Wuhan, China to every major population center globally in a matter of weeks
[
3
]
. Given the implications of global population flow on disease spread, it becomes important to accurately model
this
flow, as reliable modeling is an essential step to developing effective and efficient mitigation strategies.
Various infection models have been proposed based on characteristics of individual pathogens and studied in the literature, including susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS), susceptible-infected-removed (SIR), and susceptible-infected-removed-susceptible (SIRS)
[
4
,
5
]
. For this paper, we consider the recent COVID-19 pandemic as a motivating case for the model selection and construction. Due to the delay in onset of COVID-19 symptoms
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
and large asymptomatic populations estimated between
17-81\%

[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, we choose the susceptible-exposed-infected-removed (SEIR) model as the foundation of our model development.

Previous work involving the incorporation of population flows in epidemic process models include analysis of a networked SIS model with flows
[
14
]
as well as using a networked SIR model with flows to predict arrival times for various epidemics using global flight data
[
15
]
, where both models are developed in continuous time. This paper uses similar derivation techniques to define our discrete-time epidemic model.
However, we contribute to the development of such models by including the exposed state in our model formulation, as well as provide analysis of the discrete time dynamics. While other work has considered capturing the effect of transportation on the spread of COVID-19 using the SEIR model
[
16
]
, the key distinction in this work is that infection propagation over the network is modeled by the relocation of infected individuals to other sub-populations rather than assuming direct contact and intermingling between sub-populations. Furthermore, while previous work assumes the likelihood of individuals traveling is independent of their infection state, this work considers the effect of being infected on the probability of an individual traveling.

I-A

Contributions

In this paper, we explore a discrete-time networked SEIR model to analyze the effect of population flows on the propagation of an infectious disease. We summarize the contributions of this paper as follows:

1.

We derive a discrete-time networked SEIR model incorporating population flows, including the conditional probabilities of a given state affecting the likelihood of an individual to be traveling.

2.

We present
assumptions
on the parameters for the model to be
well defined given proper initial conditions.

3.

We prove model convergence and show that the equilibria (i.e., the healthy states) are homogeneous, that is, the same value for all sub-populations.

4.

We present a feedback control law that restricts system flow globally using the infection states, show that under the control effort the model is still well defined, and illustrate its
behavior
via simulation.

In the remainder of this paper, we present our model derivation and necessary assumptions in Section
II
, provide analysis of the healthy states in Section

, propose a feedback control in Section

II
SEIR Model with Network Flows

In this section, we present a networked SEIR model incorporating the population flow
of
individuals between sub-populations. First, consider a group of
n
sub-populations in a graph, where each sub-population
i\in[n]
is represented by a node
in the graph. We use the SEIR model to describe how suceptible individuals in sub-population
i
become exposed, infected, and eventually recover
as the result
of an infectious disease
[
17
]
.
We begin with defining the SEIR model behavior without graph connections
for each sub-population
i\in[n]
where
S_{i},E_{i},I_{i}
, and
R_{i}
represent the number of susceptible, exposed, infected, and recovered individuals in sub-population
i
, respectively,

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}(t)\frac{I_{i}(t)}{N_{i}}S_{i}(t)

(1a)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}(t)\frac{I_{i}(t)}{N_{i}}S_{i}(t)-\sigma_{i}(t)E_{i}(t)

(1b)

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}(t)E_{i}(t)-\delta_{i}(t)I_{i}(t)

(1c)

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}(t)I_{i}(t),

(1d)

where
\beta_{i}
is the infection rate,
\sigma_{i}
is transition rate from exposed to infected, and
\delta_{i}
is the healing rate.
We assume
S_{i}(t)+E_{i}(t)+I_{i}(t)+R_{i}(t)=N_{i}
for all
t
, i.e.,
a fixed population size
for each sub-population. We assume fixed sub-populations as the intended time scale of the model is such that population change due to birth/death rates and migration is negligible (e.g. rapid disease propagation over weeks or months). While all the variables (and model parameters, except population), will (may) continue to vary with time, we remove the time-dependence notation for convenience and ease of reading from this point forward.

To account for the flow of individuals between sub-populations we
we expand the model
in (
1
):

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}\frac{I_{i}}{N_{i}}S_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}\frac{S_{j}}{N_{j}}-F_{ji}\frac{S_{i}}{N_{i}}\right)

(2a)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}\frac{I_{i}}{N_{i}}S_{i}-\sigma_{i}E_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}\frac{E_{j}}{N_{j}}-F_{ji}\frac{E_{i}}{N_{i}}\right)

(2b)

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}E_{i}-\delta_{i}I_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}\frac{I_{j}}{N_{j}}-F_{ji}\frac{I_{i}}{N_{i}}\right)

(2c)

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}I_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}\frac{R_{j}}{N_{j}}-F_{ji}\frac{R_{i}}{N_{i}}\right),

(2d)

where
F_{ij}
represents the number of individuals flowing from sub-population
j
to
i
, where
F_{ii}=0
. By making a substitution of variables where
s_{i}=S_{i}/N_{i},e_{i}=E_{i}/N_{i},x_{i}=I_{i}/N_{i},r_{i}=R_{i}/N_{i}
we can model the proportion of individuals
as follows

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}s_{j}-F_{ji}s_{i}\right)

(3a)

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}-\sigma_{i}e_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}e_{j}-F_{ji}e_{i}\right)

(3b)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}e_{i}-\delta_{i}x_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}x_{j}-F_{ji}x_{i}\right)

(3c)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}x_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}r_{j}-F_{ji}r_{i}\right),

(3d)

where
s_{i}+e_{i}+x_{i}+r_{i}=1
. Note that both (
2
) and (
3
)
assume
the sub-populations are well mixed and that the likelihood of an individual traveling
is independent of their infectious state, that is, whether they are
susceptible, exposed, infected, or recovered.
We now extend our model to include the probability that an individual is traveling, given their infectious state.

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}P(s_{j}|T_{j})-F_{ji}P(s_{i}|T_{i})\right)

(4a)

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}-\sigma_{i}e_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}P(e_{j}|T_{j})-F_{ji}P(e_{i}|T_{i})\right)

(4b)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}e_{i}-\delta_{i}x_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}P(x_{j}|T_{j})-F_{ji}P(x_{i}|T_{i})\right)

(4c)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}x_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}P(r_{j}|T_{j})-F_{ji}P(r_{i}|T_{i})\right),

(4d)

where
P(q_{i}|T_{i}),\,q_{i}\in\{s_{i},e_{i},x_{i},r_{i}\}
is the probability of an individual at sub-population
i
being in a certain infectious state given that they are also traveling. Note that

P(s_{i}|T_{i})+P(e_{i}|T_{i})+P(x_{i}|T_{i})+P(r_{i}|T_{i})=1

(5)

for all
i\in[n]
. We can compute the probability that an individual from sub-population
i
is traveling given that they are in state
q_{i}

using Bayes’ rule,

P(q_{i}|T_{i})=\frac{P(T_{i}|q_{i})P(q_{i})}{P(T_{i})},

(6)

where

P(T_{i})
is the probability of an individual traveling from sub-population
i

and
P(q_{i})=q_{i}
is the probability they are in state
q_{i}
.
We compute the probability of an individual traveling from a given sub-population
i\in[n]
as

P(T_{i})=\gamma_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}F_{ji}}{N_{i}},

(7)

where
\gamma_{i}
is the proportion of the population flowing out of sub-population
i
and
\gamma_{i}\in[0,1]
as it is reasonable to assume that
\sum_{j\neq i}F_{ji}\ll N_{i}
(as
\sum_{j\neq i}F_{ji}=N_{i}
would imply that the entire population is traveling at a given time). Since measuring
P(q_{i}|T_{i})
is practically challenging, we instead parameterize
p_{i}^{q}:=P(T_{i}|q_{i})
, for
q_{i}\in\{s_{i},e_{i},x_{i},r_{i}\}
, as follows. Using (
5
)-(
7
) we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}(p_{i}^{s}s_{i}+p_{i}^{e}e_{i}+p_{i}^{x}x_{i}+p_{i}^{r}r_{i})

\displaystyle=1.

(8)

Therefore, assuming that
p_{i}^{s}=p_{i}^{e}=p_{i}^{r}=p_{i}^{T}
, we have

\displaystyle p_{i}^{T}(s_{i}+e_{i}+r_{i})+p_{i}^{x}x_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i}.

Thus, solving for
p_{i}^{T}
yields

p_{i}^{T}=\frac{\gamma_{i}-p_{i}^{x}x_{i}}{s_{i}+e_{i}+r_{i}},

(9)

which allows us to use
p_{i}^{x}\in[0,1]
as a parameter to describe how likely an individual will be traveling given that they are infected. Furthermore, we can compute the number of individuals flowing from sub-population
j
to
i
as

F_{ij}=\gamma_{j}w_{ij}N_{j},

(10)

where
w_{ij}
is the proportion of traveling individuals flowing from sub-population
j
to
i
computed as

w_{ij}=\frac{F_{ij}}{\sum_{l\neq j}F_{lj}}

(11)

with
w_{ii}=0
. Thus, we can derive the dynamics for the susceptible proportion at sub-population
i
as

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(F_{ij}P(s_{j}|T_{j})-F_{ji}P(s_{i}|T_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}+\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(\gamma_{j}w_{ij}N_{j}\frac{p_{j}^{T}s_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}-\gamma_{i}w_{ji}N_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{T}s_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\left(\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}p_{j}^{T}s_{j}-w_{ji}p_{i}^{T}s_{i}\right).

Using the fact that
\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ji}=1
, by (
11
), we have that

\dot{s}_{i}=-(\beta_{i}x_{i}+p_{i}^{T})s_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}p_{j}^{T}s_{j}.

By similar derivations, we can rewrite (
4
) as

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=-(\beta_{i}x_{i}+p_{i}^{T})s_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}p_{j}^{T}s_{j}

(12a)

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}x_{i}s_{i}-(\sigma_{i}+p_{i}^{T})e_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}p_{j}^{T}e_{j}

(12b)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}e_{i}-(\delta_{i}+p_{i}^{x})x_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}p_{j}^{x}x_{j}

(12c)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}x_{i}-p_{i}^{T}r_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}p_{j}^{T}r_{j}.

(12d)

We choose to discretize our model due to the nature of the collected data on the spread of pandemics, where the highest resolution data is typically recorded once per day. Using Euler’s method, we can write (
12
) in discrete time as

\displaystyle s_{i}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=s_{i}^{k}+h\left(-(\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}+p_{i}^{T,k})s_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}^{k}p_{j}^{T,k}s_{j}^{k}\right)

(13a)

\displaystyle e_{i}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=e_{i}^{k}+h\left(\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}-(\sigma_{i}^{k}+p_{i}^{T,k})e_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}^{k}p_{j}^{T,k}e_{j}^{k}\right)

(13b)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{k}+h\left(\sigma_{i}^{k}e_{i}^{k}-(\delta_{i}^{k}+p_{i}^{x,k})x_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}^{k}p_{j}^{x,k}x_{j}^{k}\right)

(13c)

\displaystyle r_{i}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=r_{i}^{k}+h\left(\delta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}-p_{i}^{T,k}r_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}^{k}p_{j}^{T,k}r_{j}^{k}\right),

(13d)

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is a given time step and
h\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a sampling parameter, yielding our discrete time model.

For the model in (
13
) to be well-defined we require the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

Let
\sum_{i\neq j}F_{ij}^{k}=\sum_{i\neq j}F_{ji}^{k}
for all
i\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

This assumption requires that the total flow of individuals into a given sub-population must be equal to the total flow out.
Furthermore, we impose the following assumption on the model parameters.

Assumption 2
.

For all
i\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
\beta_{i}^{k},\delta_{i}^{k},\sigma_{i}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
h\beta_{i}^{k},h\delta_{i}^{k},h\sigma_{i}^{k}\in(0,1]
,
h(\beta_{i}^{k}+p^{T,k}_{i})\leq 1,h(\sigma_{i}^{k}+p^{T,k}_{i})\leq 1,
and
h(\delta_{i}^{k}+p^{T,k}_{i})\leq 1
.

Under these assumptions, we can show that given proper initial conditions the model will always remain well defined.

Lemma 1
.

Consider the model in (
13
) under Assumptions
1
-
2
. Suppose
s_{i}^{0},e_{i}^{0},x_{i}^{0},r_{i}^{0}\in[0,1]
and
s_{i}^{0}+e_{i}^{0}+x_{i}^{0}+r_{i}^{0}=1
for all
i\in[n]
. Then, for all
k\geq 0
and
i\in[n]
,
s_{i}^{k},e_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k},r_{i}^{k}\in[0,1]
and
s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k}=1
.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction. By assumption, it holds for the base case
k=0
. We now show the inductive step, where given
s_{i}^{k},e_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k},r_{i}^{k}\in[0,1]
and
s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k}=1
for all
i\in[n]
, we show that the same holds for
k+1
. By (
13
) and Assumption
2
we have

\displaystyle q_{i}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\geq h\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}^{k}p_{j}^{T,k}q_{j}^{k}\geq 0,

(14)

where
q_{i}^{k}\in\{s^{k}_{i},e^{k}_{i},x^{k}_{i},r^{k}_{i}\}
for all
i\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Furthermore, computing
s_{i}^{k+1}+e_{i}^{k+1}+x_{i}^{k+1}+r_{i}^{k+1}
yields

\displaystyle s_{i}^{k+1}+e_{i}^{k+1}+x_{i}^{k+1}+r_{i}^{k+1}=s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k}+h\left(-\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}+\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{k}e_{i}^{k}+\sigma_{i}^{k}e_{i}^{k}-\delta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}+\delta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}\Big(F_{ij}^{k}(P(s_{i}|T_{i})+P(e_{i}|T_{i})+P(x_{i}|T_{i})+P(r_{i}|T_{i}))-F_{ji}^{k}(P(s_{j}|T_{j})+P(e_{j}|T_{j})+P(x_{j}|T_{j})+P(r_{j}|T_{j}))\Big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k}+\frac{h}{N_{i}}\left(F^{+}-F^{-}\right)=1,

(15)

where
F^{+}=\sum_{j\neq i}F_{ij}^{k}
and
F^{-}=\sum_{j\neq i}F_{ji}^{k}
denote the total flow in and out of each sub-population, respectively, which are equal by Assumption
1
. Since
each variable is non-negative by (
14
) and their sum must add to
one by (
15
), we have that
s_{i}^{k+1},e_{i}^{k+1},x_{i}^{k+1},r_{i}^{k+1}\leq 1
.
Thus, we have shown
that
s_{i}^{k+1},e_{i}^{k+1},x_{i}^{k+1},r_{i}^{k+1}\in[0,1]
, completing the inductive step.
∎

Remark 1
.

Assumption

requires that the sampling parameter be small enough in relation to the model spread parameters such that the model remains well defined. Furthermore, requiring

III
Model Analysis

In this section we analyze the equilibria of the model in (

), i.e., the healthy states of the system, which we define as
q_{i}^{*}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}q_{i}^{k}
for all
i\in[n]
where

. We use the following result given in
[
18
]
on the conditions for discrete-time consensus models.

Lemma 2
.

Let a discrete-time system defined by the transition matrix
L^{k}
satisfy the following properties, where
l_{ij}^{k}
is the corresponding entry in the
i
th row and
j
th column at time step
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
:

(i)

The graph
G=(\mathbb{V},\cup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}^{k})
, where the edge weights at time step
k
are given by
L^{k}
,
is
K
-strongly connected.

(ii)

There exists a positive constant
y\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that for all
i,j\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we have

(a)

l_{ii}^{k}\geq y

(b)

l_{ij}^{k}\in\{0\}\cup[y,1]

(c)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}l_{ij}^{k}=1
.

Then, the system dynamics defined by
L^{k}
guarantee asymptotic consensus.

We now present results on the asymptotic convergence of the healthy states for the system in (

).

Theorem 1
.

Consider the model in (
13
) under Assumptions
1
-
3
. Given that there exists some
i\in[n]
such that
x_{i}^{0}\in(0,1]
or
e_{i}^{0}\in(0,1]
, then there exists a set of asymptotically stable equilibria of the form
(\mathbf{s}^{*},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},1-\mathbf{s}^{*})
,
where
\mathbf{s}^{*}=\alpha\mathbf{1}
,
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

We prove the result by splitting it into
two parts, namely,

1.

e_{i}^{*}=0
and
x_{i}^{*}=0
, for all
i\in[n]

2.

s_{i}^{*}=\alpha
for all
i\in[n]
.

The parts are presented sequentially:

1)
We denote
\mathbf{S}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{k}\in[0,n]
,
\mathbf{E}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{k}\in[0,n]
and
\mathbf{X}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{k}\in[0,n]
as the sum of the susceptible, exposed, and infected states of all the sub-populations, respectively. Hence, based on the dynamics of the system states in (
13
) and by Assumption
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}^{k}-h\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}

(16a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}^{k}+h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{k}e_{i}^{k}\right)

(16b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}^{k}+h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{k}e_{i}^{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}\right).

(16c)

By Assumption
2
and Lemma
1
, we have that the rate of change of
\mathbf{S}^{k}
,
-h\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}
, is non-positive for all
k\geq 0
and
\mathbf{S}^{k}
is lower bounded by zero. Hence, by Lemma
1
, we obtain that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{S}^{k}
exists, and

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}-h\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}=0.

(17)

Accordingly, we can write that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbf{E}^{k+1}-\mathbf{E}^{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}-h\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{k}e_{i}^{k}
.
From Assumption
2
, we know that
h\sigma_{i}^{k}>0
for all
i\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Therefore, by Lemma
1
, we conclude that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{E}^{k}=0
and thus
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}e_{i}^{k}=0
for all
i\in[n]
.

Similarly, we acquire that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mathbf{X}^{k+1}-\mathbf{X}^{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}-h\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{k}x_{i}^{k}
, since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}e_{i}^{k}=0
for all
i\in[n]
. By Assumption
2
and Lemma
1
,
h\delta_{i}^{k}>0
and
x_{i}^{k}
is well-defined, we acquire that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{X}^{k}=0
and thus
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}x_{i}^{k}=0
for all
i\in[n]
.

2) In order to show that the susceptible states become equally mixed
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we leverage Lemma
2
.
First, we construct dynamics for the susceptible states in (
13a
) when
x_{i}^{*}=e_{i}^{*}=0
for all
i\in[n]
, as proven in 1), which yields

s_{i}^{k+1}=s_{i}^{k}+h\left(-\gamma_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}w_{ij}^{k}\gamma_{i}^{k}s_{j}^{k}\right),

(18)

where
p^{T,k}_{i}=\gamma_{i}
by (
9
) and Lemma
1
. We can express these dynamics in matrix form as

s^{k+1}=\underbrace{\left(I+h\left(-\Gamma^{k}+N^{-1}W^{k}\Gamma^{k}N\right)\right)}_{L^{k}}s^{k},

(19)

where
s^{k}=[s_{1}^{k},\dots,s_{n}^{k}]^{\top}
,
\Gamma^{k}=\text{diag}(\gamma_{1}^{k},\dots,\gamma_{n}^{k})
,
N=\text{diag}(N_{1},\dots,N_{n})
, and
W^{k}
is the matrix defined by entries
w_{ij}^{k}
on the
i
th row and
j
th column. In order to guarantee that
s_{i}^{*}
is homogeneous for all
i\in[n]
,
we will apply Lemma
2
to (
19
).

Property
(i)
of
Lemma
2
holds
by Assumption
3
. Furthermore,
(ii.a)
and
(ii.b)
are true by construction. We now show
(ii.c)
is true by computing
L^{k}\mathbf{1}
, which yields

\displaystyle L^{k}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}+h\left(-\bm{\gamma}^{k}+N^{-1}W^{k}\Gamma^{k}N\mathbf{1}\right),

(20)

where
\bm{\gamma}^{k}=[\gamma_{i}^{k},\dots,\gamma_{n}^{k}]^{\top}
. Computing
N^{-1}W^{k}\Gamma^{k}N\mathbf{1}
yields

\displaystyle N^{-1}W^{k}\Gamma^{k}N\mathbf{1}=N^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j\neq 1}\gamma_{j}^{k}w_{1j}^{k}N_{j}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j\neq n}\gamma_{j}^{k}w_{nj}^{k}N_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

Using (
10
), we can write (
21
) as

\displaystyle N^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j\neq 1}F_{1j}^{k}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j\neq n}F_{nj}^{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j\neq 1}\frac{F_{1j}^{k}}{N_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j\neq n}\frac{F_{nj}^{k}}{N_{n}}\end{bmatrix},

(22)

which, by Assumption
1
and (
7
), becomes

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i\neq 1}\frac{F_{i1}^{k}}{N_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{i\neq n}\frac{F_{in}^{k}}{N_{n}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}^{k}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{n}^{k}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{\gamma}^{k}.

Thus,
L^{k}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
, showing that
L^{k}
meets the requirements to guarantee consensus of the susceptible states as stated in Lemma
2
. Therefore,

s_{i}^{*}=\alpha
for all
i\in[n]
, where by Lemma
1
we have that
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
∎

IV
Feedback Control

We now propose a feedback control strategy for the model in (
13
) derived from the current infection states. Given that
\gamma_{i}^{k}
represents the unimpeded flow out of sub-population
i
at time step
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we can implement a scheme that restricts travel between all sub-populations proportionally:

\tilde{\gamma}_{i}^{k}=\theta^{k}\gamma_{i}^{k},

(23)

where

\theta^{k}\in[0,1]
is the flow restriction penalty.
We propose a flow restriction penalty that is a function of the average infection level:

\theta^{k}=1-(\bar{x}^{k})^{\frac{1}{\eta}},

(24)

where

\bar{x}^{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{k}
is the average proportion of infected individuals across all sub-populations and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
can be viewed as a sensitivity parameter, where
\eta>1
denotes a higher sensitivity and
\eta<1
denotes a lower sensitivity to the average infection level in the network.
The magnitude of
\eta
can also be viewed as the strength of the controller in reaction to the overall infection.
We now show that applying the strategy in (
23
) still maintains the assumptions imposed in Section
II
,
enforcing that the model remains well defined.
We define
\widetilde{\eqref{eq:flows_ind_node_disc}}
as the system with dynamics in (
13
) including the control strategy in (
23
).

Proposition 1
.

Consider
\widetilde{\eqref{eq:flows_ind_node_disc}}

under Assumption
2
and with
\theta^{k}\in[0,1]
for all
k\geq 0
. If with
s_{i}^{0},e_{i}^{0},x_{i}^{0},r_{i}^{0}\in[0,1]
and
s_{i}^{0}+e_{i}^{0}+x_{i}^{0}+r_{i}^{0}=1
for all
i\in[n]
, then
s_{i}^{k},e_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k},r_{i}^{k}\in[0,1]
and
s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k}=1
, for all
k\geq 0
and
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

As control is only applied to
\gamma_{i}^{k}
for all
i\in[n]
, we must only verify that Assumption
1
is met under the proposed control strategy and the rest will follow from Lemma
1
. Since
\theta^{k}\in[0,1]
we have by (
7
) that
\tilde{\gamma}_{i}^{k}\in[0,1]
.
Furthermore, by (
10
) we have

\tilde{F}_{ij}^{k}=\tilde{\gamma_{j}}^{k}w_{ij}^{k}N_{j}.

(25)

Computing the flows in versus the flows out,
\tilde{F}^{+}-\tilde{F}^{-}
, yields

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{+}-\tilde{F}^{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{F}_{ij}^{k}-\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{F}_{ji}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{\gamma_{j}}^{k}w_{ij}^{k}N_{j}-\sum_{j\neq i}\tilde{\gamma_{i}}^{k}w_{ji}^{k}N_{i}

\displaystyle=\theta^{k}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\gamma_{j}^{k}w_{ij}^{k}N_{j}-\sum_{j\neq i}\gamma_{i}^{k}w_{ji}^{k}N_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta^{k}(F^{+}-F^{-})=0.

Thus, Assumption
1
is maintained. Therefore, by Lemma
1

s_{i}^{k},e_{i}^{k},x_{i}^{k},r_{i}^{k}\in[0,1]
and
s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k}=1
, for all
k\geq 0
and
i\in[n]
.
∎

By restricting
\gamma_{i}^{k}
, we directly reduce the flow of both infected and non-infected individuals according to (
9
) as

\small\theta^{k}\left(p_{i}^{T,k}(s_{i}^{k}+e_{i}^{k}+r_{i}^{k})+p_{i}^{x,k}x_{i}^{k}\right)=\theta^{k}\gamma_{i}^{k}.

(26)

Thus, our controlled flow rates, with respect to the conditional probability parameters, are given by

\tilde{p}_{i}^{q,k}=\theta^{k}{p}_{i}^{q,k}

(27)

where
p_{i}^{q,k}\in\{p_{i}^{s,k},p_{i}^{e,k},p_{i}^{x,k},p_{i}^{r,k}\}
.

The control strategy in (
23
)-(
24
) effectively restricts flow between all sub-populations proportionally in the presence of any amount of system infection. Note that by construction
\theta^{k}=1-(\bar{x}^{k})^{\frac{1}{\eta}}\in[0,1]
for all
k\geq 0
. Thus, by Proposition
1
, the system is well defined.

It should be noted that while proportional restrictions to all flow are not the most precise form of control that can be applied to this model, this approach is not dissimilar to the travel policies on global and regional flights during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic
[
19
]
. In the following section we apply this control strategy to a simplified model of a travel network between populous cities based on median flight data, and evaluate its effectiveness on mitigating disease spread.

Second, we simulate the control strategy proposed in Section

with and without an additional heuristic for vaccine distribution. With no vaccine strategy applied, these simulations show that exclusively implementing a control law that uniformly reduces travel
based on
\bar{x}^{k}
, namely using (

)-(

), does
not have a significant impact on the total number of people infected.
Furthermore, in many cases increasing the strength of the controller will cause the total number of recovered individuals to increase.
This phenomenon is illustrated in the plot at the bottom of Figure

(except for very small

This behavior is the consequence of at least two reasons: 1) the controller does not prevent infections from occurring inside the sub-populations (i.e. no lock downs), and 2) the controller in some cases ‘flattens the curve’ which prolongs the outbreak and increases the integral under the curve (i.e. a higher number of total infections).

On the other hand, a reduction in the peak infected population
reduces
strain on healthcare systems, improving medical outcomes and decreasing fatality rates
[
23
]
,
and creates an opportunity for a vaccine to be more impactful.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have constructed a networked discrete-time SEIR epidemic model that incorporates population flows,
presented conditions under which the model is well-defined, and shown asymptotic convergence to the healthy states, the set of
equilibria. Additionally, we have proposed a control policy for restricting population flow which can be interpreted as implementing travel restrictions/bans,
showed it is well defined, and illustrated its
behavior
via simulation. We have found
that only restricting the flow of the population is typically insufficient to reduce
the total number of infections over the course of an epidemic.
More severe restrictions on the population flow can decrease the peak infection level, which can alleviate stress on healthcare facilities. Further, applying population flow restrictions
together with
a vaccination strategy can significantly reduce the total number of infections.

For future work we plan to incorporate the possibility of
infections occurring while individuals are traveling (i.e., infections occurring on the edges of the graph) as well as using real travel and infection data from the COVID-19 pandemic to learn the model parameters.
Finally, note that
our
model
does not capture asymptomatic transmission of the virus,
a key component of
the infectious behavior of COVID-19, therefore, developing a similar SAIR formulation
remains open to future work.
