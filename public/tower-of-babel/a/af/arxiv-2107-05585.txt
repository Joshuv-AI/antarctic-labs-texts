Differentially Private Stochastic Optimization: New Results in Convex and Non-Convex Settings
Raef Bassily, Cristóbal Guzmán, Michael Menart

Abstract
We study differentially private stochastic optimization in convex and non-convex settings. For the convex case, we focus on the family of non-smooth generalized linear losses (GLLs). Our algorithm for the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} setting achieves optimal excess population risk in near-linear time, while the best known differentially private algorithms for general convex losses run in super-linear time. Our algorithm for the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} setting has nearly-optimal excess population risk O ~ ​ ( log ⁡ d n ​ ε ) \tilde{O}\big(\sqrt{\frac{\log{d}}{n\varepsilon}}\big) , and circumvents the dimension dependent lower bound of [ 3 ] for general non-smooth convex losses. In the differentially private non-convex setting, we provide several new algorithms for approximating stationary points of the population risk. For the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -case with smooth losses and polyhedral constraint, we provide the first nearly dimension independent rate, O ~ ​ ( log 2 / 3 ⁡ d ( n ​ ε ) 1 / 3 ) \tilde{O}\big(\frac{\log^{2/3}{d}}{{(n\varepsilon)^{1/3}}}\big) in linear time. For the constrained ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -case with smooth losses, we obtain a linear-time algorithm with rate O ~ ​ ( 1 n 1 / 3 + d 1 / 5 ( n ​ ε ) 2 / 5 ) \tilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}+\frac{d^{1/5}}{(n\varepsilon)^{2/5}}\big) . Finally, for the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -case we provide the first method for non-smooth weakly convex stochastic optimization with rate O ~ ​ ( 1 n 1 / 4 + d 1 / 6 ( n ​ ε ) 1 / 3 ) \tilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}+\frac{d^{1/6}}{(n\varepsilon)^{1/3}}\big) which matches the best existing non-private algorithm when d = O ⁡ ( n ) d=O(\sqrt{n}) . We also extend all our results above for the non-convex ℓ 2 \ell_{2} setting to the ℓ p \ell_{p} setting, where 1 < p ≤ 2 1<p\leq 2 , with only polylogarithmic (in the dimension) overhead in the rates.

Introduction

Stochastic optimization (SO) is a fundamental and pervasive problem in machine learning, statistics and operations research. Here, the goal is to minimize the expectation of a loss function (often referred to as the
population risk
), given only access to a sample of i.i.d. draws from a distribution. When such a sample entails privacy concerns, differential privacy (DP) becomes an important algorithmic desideratum.

Consequently, differentially private stochastic optimization (DP-SO) has been actively investigated for over a decade. Despite major progress in this area, some crucial problems remain with existing methods. One major problem is the lack of linear-time

\ell_{p}

Finally, our understanding of DP-SO in the non-convex case is still quite limited.
In the non-convex domain,
there are only a few prior results, all of which have several limitations.
First, all existing works either assume that the optimization problem is unconstrained or only consider the empirical version of the problem known as differentially private empirical risk minimization (DP-ERM). Obtaining population guarantees based on the empirical risk potentially limits the applicability of the existing methods either in terms of accuracy or in terms of computational efficiency. In particular, all existing methods require super-linear running time w.r.t. the dataset size. Second, most of the existing works consider only the Euclidean setting.

The goal of this work is to provide faster and more accurate methods for DP-SO. Some of the settings we investigate are also novel in the DP literature.

We conclude with the case of weakly convex non-smooth stochastic optimization, where we devise algorithms to compute

Preliminaries

Normed Spaces.

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space of dimension
d,
and let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
an arbitrary inner product over
\mathbf{E}
(not necessarily inducing the norm
\|\cdot\|
). Given
x\in\mathbf{E}
and
r>0
, let
{\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|}(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbf{E}:\|y-x\|\leq r\}
.
The dual norm over
\mathbf{E}
is defined as usual,
\|y\|_{\ast}\triangleq\max_{\|x\|\leq 1}\langle y,x\rangle
. With this definition,
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{\ast})
is also a
d
-dimensional normed space. As a main example, consider the case of
\ell_{p}^{d}\triangleq(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
, where
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
\|x\|_{p}\triangleq\big(\sum_{j\in[d]}|x_{j}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}
. As a consequence of the Hölder inequality, one can prove that the dual of
\ell_{p}^{d}
corresponds to
\ell_{q}^{d}
, where
1\leq q\leq\infty
is the conjugate exponent of
p
, determined by
1/p+1/q=1
.

Differential Privacy
[
14
]
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
(\varepsilon,\delta)
differentially private (abbreviated
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP) if for any pair of datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}
differing in one point and any event
\mathcal{E}
in the range of
\mathcal{A}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(S)\in\mathcal{E}]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}]+\delta.

Lemma 1

(Advanced composition
[
16
,
15
]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0,\delta\in[0,1),
and
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, the class of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private algorithms satisfies
(\varepsilon^{\prime},k\delta+\delta^{\prime})
-differential privacy under
k
-fold adaptive composition,
for
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon\sqrt{2k\log(1/\delta^{\prime})}+k\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon}-1)
.

Stochastic Optimization.

In the Stochastic Optimization problem with
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
-setting, we have a normed space
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
; a feasible set
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbf{E}
which is closed, convex and with diameter at most
D
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
; and loss functions
f:\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
are assumed to be
L_{0}
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
. Sometimes, we also consider losses which are
L_{1}
-smooth: i.e., for all
w,v\in{\cal W}
,
\|\nabla f(w)-\nabla f(v)\|_{\ast}\leq L_{1}\|w-v\|
. In this problem, there is an unknown distribution
{\cal D}
over a set
\mathcal{Z}
, and our goal is to minimize a certain accuracy measure that depends on the population risk, defined as
F_{\cal D}(w)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim{\cal D}}[f(w,z)]
, when only given access to a sample
S=(z_{1},...,z_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{D}
. In Differentially Private Stochastic Optimization (DP-SO) one is concerned with solving this problem under the constraint that the algorithm used is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP w.r.t.
S
.

Depending on additional assumptions of the losses, the accuracy measure in DP-SO may vary.
In the
convex case
, the accuracy of a stochastic optimization algorithm is naturally measured by the excess population risk, defined as

F_{\mathcal{D}}(w)-\min_{v\in\mathcal{W}}F_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
. For the non-convex case, providing guarantees on the excess population risk is often intractable.

Non-Convex Stochastic Optimization.

In the
non-convex smooth
case, a common performance measure to use is the
stationarity gap
of the population risk, which for
w\in{\cal W}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{Gap}_{F_{\cal D}}(w)=\max_{v\in{\cal W}}\langle\nabla F_{\cal D}(w),w-v\rangle.

(1)

Note that if the stationarity gap is zero, then
w
is indeed a stationary point of the risk. For the
non-convex non-smooth
case, near stationarity (i.e., small stationarity gap) is often a stringent concept, as the set of points with small stationarity gap may coincide with the stationary points themselves. Hence, we will consider instead the goal of finding
close to nearly-stationary points

[
13
,
12
]
, which we formally introduce in Section
5
.

Definition 2

Let

We will occasionally refer to the

is
(L_{0}R)

By smoothing the function
f

A critical component of the following results is a technique known as Moreau envelope smoothing

\ell_{\beta}^{(y)}(m)\triangleq\min_{u\in\mathcal{M}}\big[\ell^{(y)}(u)+\frac{\beta}{2}|u-m|^{2}\big].

Denote the proximal operator with respect to

\mbox{prox}_{\ell^{(y)}}^{\beta}(m)=\arg\min\limits_{u\in\mathcal{M}}\big[\ell^{(y)}(u)+\frac{\beta}{2}|u-m|^{2}\big].

The accuracy guarantee
\left\|\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}(w,(x,y))-\nabla f_{\beta}(w,(x,y))\right\|_{*}\leq\alpha
then follows straightforwardly using part (b) of Lemma

and the facts that

and

.
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
have
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-diameter at most

.
Let
f:\mathcal{W}\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

L_{0}

(\varepsilon,\delta)

Proof.

The proof follows similarly to
[
18
]
, but additionally we account for the change in gradient sensitivity and extra error introduced by using the approximate gradient oracle of the smoothed loss,
\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}
.
Let PSGD(
\mathcal{O},\eta,w_{0},T
) (used in Algorithm
2
) denote the process which computes
w_{t}=\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}[w_{t-1}+\eta\mathcal{O}(w_{t-1})]:\forall t\in[T]
, where
\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}
is the projection onto constraint set
\mathcal{W}
.
By Lemma
4
,
f_{\beta}
is a
(2L_{0}R)
-Lipschitz and
(\beta R^{2})
-smooth loss function.
Further, the increase in error due to using
\alpha
-approximate gradients in SGD is at most
2\alpha D
(see, e.g.,
[
17
,
6
]
).
Let
F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w)=\underset{z\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{\beta}(w)\right]

. For notational convenience, let
w_{0}=w_{\beta}^{*}
and
\sigma_{0}=D
. We have (following from
[
18
, Lemma 4.5 & Proof of Theorem 4.4]

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w_{K})-F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w_{\beta}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w_{k})-F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w_{k-1})\right]+\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{w}_{K})-F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w_{K})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{d\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+2\eta_{k}L_{0}^{2}R^{2}+2D\alpha\right)+2L_{0}R\,\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{K}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\left(\frac{d\sigma_{k-1}^{2}}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+2\eta_{k}L_{0}^{2}R^{2}\right)+2L_{0}R\sqrt{d}\sigma_{K}+2DK\alpha.

By the setting of

.
It can be verified that the rest of the expression is

O\left(L_{0}RD\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\rho n}\right)\right)

(see
[

).
To convert to population loss with respect to the original function, we provide the following analysis. Let
w^{*}=\min\limits_{w\in\mathcal{W}}F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{*})

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{D}}(w)-F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{*})

\displaystyle\leq F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w)-F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w^{*})+\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w)-F_{\beta,\mathcal{D}}(w_{\beta}^{*})+\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{\beta}.

Thus by the setting
\beta=\sqrt{n}L_{0}/(RD)

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{w}_{K})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{*})\right]=O\left(L_{0}RD\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\rho}\right)\right).

Plugging in our value of

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{w}_{K})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{*})\right]=O\left(L_{0}RD\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{n\varepsilon}\right)\right).

For privacy, note that

\|\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}(w,z)\|\leq(2L_{0}R+\frac{L_{0}R}{n})
,
and thus the sensitivity of the approximate gradient is bounded by
3L_{0}R
.
Thus, by setting the parameters of Phased SGD as they would be for a
(3L_{0}R)

[

]
implies that Algorithm

satisfies (
\varepsilon,\delta)

. It’s easy to see that the condition on
\eta
holds.
∎

Furthermore, it is possible to adapt this technique to the unconstrained case as well (
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
). It was shown in
[

]
that in the unconstrained case, dimension independent rates are attainable. The following theorem establishes that such rates are achievable in near linear time (as opposed to the super linear rates of
[

Before stating the theorem, a few preliminaries are necessary. Let
V
be a matrix whose columns are an eigenbasis for
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
.
For any

denote the semi-norm of

induced by

, and let

. Here, we assume knowledge of some upper bound
\theta
on
\underset{S\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{Rank}(V)\right]
. Note that this is no loss of generality since we always have
\underset{S\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{Rank}(V)\right]\leq n

.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
f:\mathcal{W}\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

L_{0}
-Lipschitz and

O\left(L_{0}R\left(\|\tilde{w}_{0}-w_{\beta}^{*}\|^{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{\theta\log(1/\delta)}}{n\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)

To prove the theorem, we start by providing the following lemma.
As before, denote
w_{0}=w_{\beta}^{*}
and define
\xi_{0}=\tilde{w}_{0}-w_{\beta}^{*}

Lemma 8
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,R
be as in Theorem
7
. Then the output,

, of phase

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,D}(w_{k})-F_{\beta,D}(w_{k-1})\right]\leq\frac{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\tilde{w}_{k-1}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}^{2}\right]}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+\frac{5\eta_{k}L_{0}^{2}R^{2}}{2}+\frac{L_{0}R\left(\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\tilde{w}_{k-1}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}\right]+1\right)}{\sqrt{n}\log(n)}.

Proof.

Let
\{u_{0},\dots,u_{T_{k}}\}
denote the iterates generated by round
k
of PSGD (where
u_{0}=\tilde{w}_{k-1}
), and let
z_{t}
be the datapoint sampled during iteration
t
. For all
t\in\{0,...T_{k}\}
, define the potential function
\Phi^{(t)}\triangleq\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}^{2}
. Using standard algebraic manipulation, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{(t)}-2\eta_{k}\langle\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}(u_{t},z_{t}),u_{t}-w_{k-1}\rangle_{V}+\eta^{2}_{k}\|\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}(u_{t},z_{t})\|^{2}_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi^{(t)}-2\eta_{k}\langle\nabla f_{\beta}(u_{t},z_{t}),u_{t}-w_{k-1}\rangle_{V}+2\eta_{k}\alpha\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}+\eta^{2}_{k}(\alpha^{2}+4L_{0}^{2}R^{2}),

where the inequality follows from the fact that

\|\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}(u_{t},z_{t})-\nabla f_{\beta}(u_{t},z_{t})\|\leq\alpha

and the nonexpansiveness of the projection onto the span of
V
.
Since the gradient is in the span of
V
, we have

\Phi^{(t+1)}\leq\Phi^{(t)}-2\eta_{k}\langle\nabla f_{\beta}(u_{t},z_{t}),u_{t}-w_{k-1}\rangle+2\eta_{k}\alpha\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}+\eta^{2}_{k}(\alpha^{2}+4L_{0}^{2}R^{2}).

Hence

\langle\nabla f_{\beta}(u_{t},z_{t}),u_{t}-w_{k-1}\rangle\leq\frac{\Phi^{(t)}-\Phi^{(t+1)}}{2\eta_{k}}+\alpha\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}(\alpha^{2}+4L_{0}^{2}R^{2}).

Taking the expectation w.r.t. all randomness (i.e., w.r.t.
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and the Gaussian noise random variables), we have

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\nabla F_{\beta,D}(u_{t}),u_{t}-w_{k-1}\rangle\right]\leq\frac{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi^{(t)}-\Phi^{(t+1)}\right]}{2\eta_{k}}+\alpha\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}\right]+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}(\alpha^{2}+4L_{0}^{2}R^{2}).

Moreover, by the convexity of
F_{\beta,D}
we have

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\nabla F_{\beta,D}(u_{t}),u_{t}-w_{k-1}\rangle\right]\geq\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,D}(u_{t})-F_{\beta,D}(w_{k-1})\right]
.
Combining this inequality with the above, and using the fact that

w_{k}=\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}u_{t}
together with the convexity of
F_{\beta,D}
,
we have

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,D}(w_{k})-F_{\beta,D}(w_{k-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\left(\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,D}(u_{t})-F_{\beta,D}(w_{k-1})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi^{(0)}\right]}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+\frac{\alpha}{T_{k}}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}\right]+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}(\alpha^{2}+4L_{0}^{2}R^{2}).

To bound
\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}\right]
in the above, observe that,

\displaystyle\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{t-1}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}+\|u_{t}-u_{t-1}\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{w}_{k-1}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\|u_{j}-u_{j-1}\|_{V}.

Hence

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|u_{t}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\tilde{w}_{k-1}-w_{k-1}\|_{V}\right]+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|u_{j}-u_{j-1}\|_{V}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sqrt{\Phi^{(0)}}\right]+\eta_{k}t(2L_{0}R+\alpha),

where the last inequality follows from the definition of
\Phi^{(0)}
and the fact that

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|u_{j}-u_{j-1}\|_{V}\right]=\eta_{k}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}(u_{j-1},z_{j-1})\|\right]\leq\eta_{k}(2L_{0}R+\alpha).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,D}(w_{k})-F_{\beta,D}(w_{k-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi^{(0)}\right]}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+\alpha\left(\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sqrt{\Phi^{(0)}}\right]+T_{k}\eta_{k}(2L_{0}R+\alpha)\right)+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}(\alpha^{2}+4L_{0}^{2}R^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi^{(0)}\right]}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+\frac{5\eta_{k}L_{0}^{2}R^{2}}{2}+\alpha\left(\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sqrt{\Phi^{(0)}}\right]+3T_{k}\eta_{k}L_{0}R\right).

The last step follows from the fact that
\alpha=\frac{L_{0}R}{n\log(n)}\leq L_{0}R
.
Further, since

\eta_{k}=\frac{1}{3L_{0}R_{0}}\min\{\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\theta}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}\leq\frac{1}{3L_{0}R\sqrt{n}}
and
T_{k}\leq n

it holds that
3T_{k}\eta_{k}L_{0}R\leq\sqrt{n}
.
Thus by the setting of
\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,D}(w_{k})-F_{\beta,D}(w_{k-1})\right]\leq\frac{\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi^{(0)}\right]}{2\eta_{k}T_{k}}+\frac{5\eta_{k}L_{0}^{2}R^{2}}{2}+\frac{L_{0}R\left(\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sqrt{\Phi^{(0)}}\right]+1\right)}{\sqrt{n}\log(n)}.

∎

With this result established, we can now prove Theorem
7

(2)

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\xi_{k-1}\|_{V}^{2}\right]

Theorem 9

.
Let
f:\mathcal{W}\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
R
-bounded GLL with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.
Let
\beta=\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{n\varepsilon}}{RD\log^{1/4}(1/\delta)\sqrt{\log(J)\log(n)}}
and

\alpha=\frac{1}{n\log(n)}
.
Then Noisy Frank Wolfe (Algorithm
3
)
with oracle

\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}

and dataset
S\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy. Further, if
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
the output of Noisy Frank Wolfe has expected excess population risk

O\left(L_{0}RD\left(\frac{\log^{1/4}(1/\delta)\sqrt{\log(J)\log(n)}}{\sqrt{n\varepsilon}}+\frac{\sqrt{\log{d}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the analysis of noisy Frank Wolfe from
[
35
]
. Let

F_{\beta,S}(w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in S}f_{\beta}(w,z)
.
Define
w_{\beta,S}^{*}
as the minimizer
F_{\beta,S}
in
\mathcal{W}
.

Define

\gamma_{t}={\langle\tilde{v_{t}},\tilde{\nabla}_{t}\rangle-\min\limits_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\langle v,\tilde{\nabla}_{t}\rangle}.
Since
F_{\beta,S}
is
(\beta R^{2})
-smooth (by Lemma
4
), standard analysis of the Noisy Frank-Wolfe algorithm yields (see, e.g.,
[
34
]
)

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,S}(w_{T})-F_{\beta,S}(w_{\beta,S}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\beta R^{2}D^{2}}{T}\right)+D\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{t}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\tilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\beta,S}(w_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{t}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\gamma_{t}\right].

By a standard argument concerning the maximum of a collection of Laplace random variables, we have for all
t\in[T]

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\gamma_{t}\right]\leq 2s\log(|\mathcal{V}|)
.
Note also that for all
t
, by the approximation guarantee of
\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}
, we have (with probability 1)

\left\|\tilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\beta,S}(w_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\ \leq\alpha
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\beta,S}(w_{T})-F_{\beta,S}(w_{\beta,S}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\beta R^{2}D^{2}}{T}\right)+\log(T)\big(D\alpha+s\log(|\mathcal{V}|)\big)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\beta R^{2}D^{2}}{T}\right)+\log(T)\left(\frac{L_{0}RD}{n\log(n)}+\frac{L_{0}RD\sqrt{8T\log(1/\delta)}\log(|\mathcal{V}|)}{{n\varepsilon}}\right),

where the second equality follows from the setting of
\alpha=\frac{L_{0}R}{n\log(n)}
and the noise parameter
s
.

Using the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
6
, we arrive at the following bound on the excess empirical risk for the unsmoothed empirical loss
F_{S}
:

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{S}(w_{T})-F_{S}(w_{S}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\beta R^{2}D^{2}}{T}+\frac{L_{0}RD\sqrt{72T\log(1/\delta)}\log(|\mathcal{V}|)\log(T)}{{n\varepsilon}}+\frac{L_{0}RD\log(T)}{n\log(n)}+\frac{L_{0}^{2}}{\beta}\right).

By the setting of
\beta=\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{n\varepsilon}}{RD\log^{1/4}(1/\delta)\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{V}|)\log(n)}}
and
T=\frac{n\varepsilon}{\log(|\mathcal{V}|)\log(n)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}
,

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{S}(w_{T})-F_{S}(w_{S}^{*})\right]=O\left(\frac{L_{0}RD\log^{1/4}(1/\delta)\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{V}|)\log(n)}}{\sqrt{n\varepsilon}}\right).

Via a standard Rademacher-complexity argument, we know that the generalization error of GLLs is bounded as
O\left(\frac{L_{0}RD\sqrt{\log{d}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
(see
[
32
]
Theorem 26.15). This gives the claimed bound.

The privacy guarantee follows almost the same argument as in
[
34
]
.
Note that the sensitivity of the approximate gradients generated by
\mathcal{O}_{\beta,\alpha,R}
is at most
\frac{3L_{0}R}{n}
since
f_{\beta}
is
(2L_{0}R)
-Lipschitz and the error due to the approximate oracle is less than
L_{0}R
.
We then guarantee privacy via a straightforward application of the Report-Noisy-Max algorithm
[
15
,
8
]
and advanced composition for differential privacy.

∎

4
Algorithms for Non-convex Smooth Losses

In this section, we describe differentially private algorithms for non-convex smooth stochastic optimization in the
\ell_{p}
-setting for
1\leq p\leq 2
. We provide formal convergence guarantees in terms of the stationarity gap (see (
1
) in Section
2
). Our algorithms are inspired by the variance-reduced stochastic Frank-Wolfe algorithm
[
41
]
. However, our algorithms involve several crucial differences from their non-private counterpart. In particular, they are divided into a number of rounds
R=O(\log(n))
, where each round
r\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\}
involves
2^{r}
updates for the iterate. Each round
r
starts by computing a fresh estimate for the gradient of the population risk at the current iterate based on a large batch of data points, then such gradient estimate is updated recursively using disjoint batches of decreasing size sampled across the
2^{r}
iterations of that round. Using this round-based structure and batch schedule, together with carefully tuned step sizes, allows us to effectively control the privacy budget while attaining small stationarity gap w.r.t. the population risk. Moreover, our algorithms make a
single pass
on the input sample, i.e., they run in linear time.

In this section, we assume that
\forall z\in\mathcal{Z},\penalty\ f(\cdot,z)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth loss in the respective
\ell_{p}
norm. Our algorithms can be applied to general spaces whose dual has a sufficiently smooth norm. To quantify this property, we use the notion of
regular spaces

[
23
]
. Given
\kappa\geq 1
, we say a normed space
(\mathbf{E},\left\|\cdot\right\|)
is
\kappa
-regular, if there exists
1\leq\kappa_{+}\leq\kappa
and a norm
\|\cdot\|_{+}
such that
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{+})
is
\kappa_{+}
-smooth, i.e.,

\|x+y\|_{+}^{2}\leq\|x\|_{+}^{2}+\langle\nabla(\|\cdot\|_{+}^{2})(x),y\rangle+\kappa_{+}\|y\|_{+}^{2}\qquad(\forall x,y\in\mathbf{E}),

(3)

and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{+}
are equivalent with constant
\sqrt{\kappa/\kappa_{+}}
:

\left\|x\right\|^{2}\leq\|x\|_{+}^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{+}}\left\|x\right\|^{2}\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbf{E}).

(4)

One relevant fact is that
d
-dimensional
\ell_{q}
spaces,
2\leq q\leq\infty
, are
\kappa
-regular with
\kappa=\min\left(q-1,2\log d\right)
.
Also, if
\|\cdot\|
is a polyhedral norm defined over a space
\mathbf{E}
with unit ball
{\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|}=\mbox{conv}({\cal V})
, then
its dual
(\mathbf{E},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*})
is
(2\log|{\cal V}|)
-regular.

4.1
Algorithm for Polyhedral and
\ell_{1}
Settings

We consider the
polyhedral
setup, namely, we consider a normed space
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
, where the unit ball w.r.t. the norm,
{\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|}
is a convex polytope with at most
J
vertices. The feasible set
\mathcal{W}
, is a polytope with at most
J
vertices and
\|\cdot\|
-diameter
D>0
.

Algorithm 4

\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{polySFW}}
: Private Polyhedral Stochastic Frank-Wolfe Algorithm

0:

Dataset
S=(z_{1},\ldots z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
,
privacy parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, polyhedral set
\mathcal{W}
with
J
vertices
\mathcal{V}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{J})
, number of rounds
R
, batch size
b
, step sizes
\left(\eta_{r,t}:r=0,\ldots,R-1,\penalty\ t=0,\ldots,2^{r}-1\right)
.

1:

Choose an arbitrary initial point
w_{0}^{0}\in\mathcal{W}

2:

for

r=0
to
R-1

do

3:

Let
s_{r}=2D(L_{0}+L_{1}D)\frac{2^{r}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{b\varepsilon}

4:

Draw a batch
B_{r}^{0}
of
b
samples without replacement from
S

5:

Compute
{\nabla_{r}^{0}}=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{0}}\nabla f(w_{r}^{0},z)

6:

v_{r}^{0}=\argmin\limits_{v\in\mathcal{V}}{\{\langle v,\nabla_{r}^{0}\rangle+u_{r}^{0}(v)\}}
, where
u_{r}^{0}(v)\sim\mathsf{Lap}\left(s_{r}\right)

7:

w^{1}_{r}\leftarrow(1-\eta_{r,0})w^{0}_{r}+\eta_{r,0}v_{r}^{0}

8:

for

t=1
to
2^{r}-1

do

9:

Draw a batch
B_{r}^{t}
of
b/(t+1)
samples without replacement from
S
.

10:

Compute
\Delta_{r}^{t}=\frac{t+1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\left(\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)-\nabla f(w_{r}^{t-1},z)\right)

11:

\nabla_{r}^{t}=(1-\eta_{r,t})\left(\nabla_{r}^{t-1}+\Delta_{r}^{t}\right)+\eta_{r,t}\frac{t+1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)

12:

Compute
v_{r}^{t}=\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{V}}{\langle v,\nabla_{r}^{t}\rangle+u_{r}^{t}(v)}
, where
u_{r}^{t}(v)\sim\mathsf{Lap}\left(s_{r}\right)

13:

w_{r}^{t+1}\leftarrow(1-\eta_{r,t})w_{r}^{t}+\eta_{r,t}v_{r}^{t}

14:

w_{r+1}^{0}=w_{r}^{2^{r}}

15:

Output
\widehat{w}
uniformly chosen from
\big(w_{r}^{t}:r\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\},t\in\{0,\ldots,2^{r}-1\}\big)

Remark concerning the choice of parameters
R
and
b
:

Note that the total number of samples used the algorithm is
\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\sum_{t=0}^{2^{r}-1}b/(t+1)\leq b\sum_{r=0}^{R}\left(\ln(2^{r})+1\right)=b\sum_{r=0}^{R}\left(r\ln(2)+1\right)<bR^{2}
. Moreover, the batch drawn in each iteration
(r,t)
is
b/(t+1)
. Hence, for the algorithm to be properly defined, it suffices to have
bR^{2}\leq n
and
b\geq 2^{R}
. Note that our choices of
R
and
b
(in Lemma
12
and Theorem
11
below) satisfy these conditions.
Note also that we assume w.l.o.g. that
n
is large enough so that the claimed bound on the stationarity gap is non-trivial. Hence, the choice of
R
is meaningful.

The formal guarantees of Algorithm
4
are stated below.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\eta_{r,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\penalty\ \forall r,t
. Then, Algorithm
4
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private.

Proof.

Since the batches used in different rounds
r=0,\ldots,R-1
are disjoint, it suffices to prove the privacy guarantee for a given round
r.
The rest of the proof follows by parallel composition of differential privacy and the fact that differential privacy is closed under post processing. For notational brevity, let
g_{r}^{t}=\frac{t+1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)
. By unravelling the recursion in the gradient estimator (Step
11
of Algorithm
4
) and using the setting of
\eta_{r,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}
, we have for any
t\in[2^{r}-1]
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{r}^{t}=a^{(1)}_{t}\cdot\nabla_{r}^{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}\left(a^{(k)}_{t}\cdot\Delta_{r}^{k}+c^{(k)}_{t}\cdot g_{r}^{k}\right)

(5)

where, for all
k\in[t],

a^{(k)}_{t}=\prod_{j=k}^{t}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{j+1}})
and
c^{(k)}_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+1}}\prod_{j=k+1}^{t}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{j+1}}).
Note also that
a^{(k)}_{t}<1
and
c^{(k)}_{t}<1
for all
t,k
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
be any neighboring datasets (i.e., differing in exactly one data point). Let
\nabla_{r}^{t},\left\{\Delta_{r}^{k}:k\in[t]\right\},\left\{g_{r}^{k}:k\in[t]\right\}
be the quantities above when the input dataset is
S
; and let
\nabla_{r}^{{}^{\prime}t},\left\{\Delta_{r}^{{}^{\prime}k},:k\in[t]\right\},\left\{g_{r}^{{}^{\prime}k}:k\in[t]\right\}
be the corresponding quantities when the input dataset is
S^{\prime}
. Now, since the batches
B_{r}^{0},\ldots,B_{r}^{t}
are disjoint, changing one data point in the input dataset can affect at most one term in the sum (
5
) above, i.e., it affects either the
\nabla_{r}^{0}
term, or exactly one term corresponding to some
k\in[t]
in the sum on the right-hand side. Moreover, since
f
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz, we have
\left\|\nabla_{r}^{0}-\nabla_{r}^{{}^{\prime}0}\right\|_{*}\leq L_{0}/b
, and
\left\|g_{r}^{t}-g_{r}^{{}^{\prime}t}\right\|_{*}\leq L_{0}(t+1)/b
. Also, by the
L_{1}
-smoothness of
f
and the form of the update rule (Step
13
of Algorithm
4
), for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,2^{r}-1\}
, we have
\left\|\nabla f(w_{r}^{k},z)-\nabla f(w_{r}^{k-1},z)\right\|_{*}\leq L_{1}\left\|w_{r}^{k}-w_{r}^{k-1}\right\|\leq L_{1}D\eta_{r,k}\leq L_{1}D/\sqrt{k+1}
. Hence,
\left\|\Delta_{r}^{k}-\Delta_{r}^{{}^{\prime}k}\right\|_{*}\leq\frac{k+1}{b}\frac{L_{1}D}{\sqrt{k+1}}=L_{1}D\sqrt{k+1}/b
. Using these facts, it is then easy to see that for any
t\in[2^{r}-1]
,

\left\|\nabla_{r}^{t}-\nabla^{{}^{\prime}t}_{r}\right\|_{*}\leq\max\left(\frac{L_{0}}{b},\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}D)\sqrt{t+1}}{b}\right)\leq\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}D)2^{r/2}}{b}.

Hence, for each
v\in\mathcal{V}
, the global sensitivity of
\langle v,\nabla_{r}^{t}\rangle
is upper bounded by
\frac{D(L_{0}+L_{1}D)2^{r/2}}{b}
.
By the privacy guarantee of the Report Noisy Max mechanism
[
15
,
8
]
, the setting of the Laplace noise parameter
s_{r}
ensures that each iteration
t\in\{0,\ldots,2^{r}-1\}
is
\frac{\varepsilon 2^{-r/2}}{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}
-DP. Thus, by advanced composition (Lemma
1
) applied to the
2^{r}
iterations in round
r
, we conclude that the algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
R=\frac{2}{3}\log\left(\frac{n\varepsilon}{\log^{2}(J)\log^{2}(n)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\right)
,
b=\frac{n}{\log^{2}(n)}
, and
\eta_{r,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall r,t
. Let
\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{Z}

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Gap}_{F_{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{w})\right]=O\left(D(L_{0}+L_{1}D)\cdot\frac{\log^{2/3}(J)\log^{2/3}(n)\log^{1/6}(1/\delta)}{n^{1/3}\varepsilon^{1/3}}\right).

The proof of convergence will rely on the following lemma.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be any distribution over
\mathcal{Z}

\textstyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla^{t}_{r}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t})\right\|_{*}\right]\leq 4L_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\log(J)}{b}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\right)^{t+1}+4\left(L_{1}D+L_{0}\right)\frac{\log(J)}{\sqrt{b}}(t+1)^{1/4}.

Proof.

Recall that we consider the
polyhedral
setup, where the feasible set
\mathcal{W}

J

(\mathbf{E},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*})

(2\log(J))

Fix any
r\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\}

t\in\{1,\ldots,2^{r}-1\},
we can write

\displaystyle\nabla_{r}^{t}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t})

\displaystyle=(1-\eta_{r,t})\penalty\ \left[\nabla_{r}^{t-1}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t-1})\right]+(1-\eta_{r,t})\penalty\ \left[\Delta_{r}^{t}-\left(\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t})-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t-1})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\eta_{r,t}\penalty\ \left[\frac{t+1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t})\right].

Let

Recall that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}

(2\log(J))
-regular,
and denote
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{+}

\kappa_{+}

4

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t}

\gamma_{r}^{t}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla_{r}^{t}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t})\right\|_{+}^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\gamma_{r}^{t}

\underset{z\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla f(w,z)\right]=\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w)

(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}

and

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\Delta_{r}^{t}-\overline{\Delta}_{r}^{t}\right\|_{+}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\frac{t+1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\left(\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)-\nabla f(w_{r}^{t-1},z)-\overline{\Delta}_{r}^{t}\right)\right\|_{+}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{+}\frac{(t+1)^{2}}{b^{2}}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)-\nabla f(w_{r}^{t-1},z)-\overline{\Delta}_{r}^{t}\right\|_{+}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\frac{(t+1)^{2}}{b^{2}}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)-\nabla f(w_{r}^{t-1},z)-\overline{\Delta}_{r}^{t}\right\|_{*}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\left(L_{1}D\right)^{2}\log(J)\eta_{r,t}^{2}\,(t+1)}{b},

where the inequality before the last one follows from the fact that
\kappa_{+}\leq\kappa
, and the last inequality follows from the fact that
\kappa=2\log(J)
.
Similarly, since the loss is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz, using the same inductive approach, we can bound

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\frac{t+1}{b}\sum_{z\in B_{r}^{t}}\nabla f(w_{r}^{t},z)-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w_{r}^{t})\right\|_{+}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{Q}_{r}^{t-1}\right]\leq\frac{4L_{0}^{2}\log(J)\,(t+1)}{b}.

Using the above bounds and the setting of
\eta_{r,t}

\displaystyle\gamma_{r}^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\right)^{2}\gamma_{r}^{t-1}+\frac{8\kappa_{+}(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}D^{2})\log(J)}{b}.

Unravelling the recursion, we can further bound
\gamma_{r}^{t}

\displaystyle\gamma_{r}^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{r}^{0}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\right)^{2t}+\frac{8\kappa_{+}(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}D^{2})\log(J)}{b}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\right)^{2j}

(6)

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}})^{2j}\leq\frac{1}{1-(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}})^{2}}\leq\sqrt{t+1}.

Moreover, observe that we can bound
\gamma_{r}^{0}
using the same inductive approach we used earlier:

Proposition 16

(\exists\hat{w}\in{\cal W})(\exists g\in\partial f(\hat{w})):\quad\|w-\hat{w}\|\leq\vartheta\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sup_{v\in{\cal W}}\langle g,\hat{w}-v\rangle\leq\vartheta.

Above,

is the accuracy parameter. This accuracy measure states that

is at distance at most
\vartheta
from a
\vartheta
-nearly stationary point.
It is then apparent how the proximal-type operator can certify (
13
). For convenience, we define a notion of efficiency in weakly-convex DP-SO, particularly geared towards algorithms that certify close to near stationarity via the proximal-type mapping.

Definition 18

(Proximal Near Stationarity)
.

A randomized algorithm
{\cal A}:{\cal Z}^{n}\mapsto\mathbf{E}
, for the stochastic optimization problem
\min_{w\in{\cal W}}F_{\cal D}(w)
, achieves
(\vartheta,\beta)
-proximal near stationarity if

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal A}}\big[\|\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))-{\cal A}(S)\|\big]\leq\vartheta/\max\{1,\beta D\}.

(14)

Notice the maximum in the denominator is a normalizing factor, inspired by Lemma
17
.
Note further that, by Lemma
17
, an algorithm with proximal near stationarity ensures closeness to nearly stationary points through its proximal-type mapping: namely, if
{\cal A}
satisfies Definition
18
, then

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal A}}\big[\|\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))-{\cal A}(S)\|\big]\leq\vartheta\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal A}}\big[\mathsf{Gap}_{F_{\cal D}}\big(\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))\big)\big]\leq\vartheta.

In the above, some technical caution must be taken to define the gap function in the stochastic non-smooth case, which we defer to Appendix
B.2.1
.
Although not defined under this name, this is precisely the certificate achieved in weakly-convex SO in recent literature
[
13

12
]
.

B.1
Background Information on Weakly Convex Functions and their Subdifferentials

Definition 22
.

We say that a function
f:{\cal W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is
\rho
-weakly convex w.r.t. norm
\|\cdot\|
if for all
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and
w,v\in{\cal W}
, we have

f(\lambda w+(1-\lambda)v)\leq\lambda f(w)+(1-\lambda)f(v)+\frac{\rho\lambda(1-\lambda)}{2}\|w-v\|^{2}.

For nonconvex functions, defining the subdifferential can be done in a local fashion.

Definition 23
.

Let
f:\mathbf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. We define the
(regular) subdifferential
of
f
at point
w\in\mathbf{E}
, denoted
\partial f(w)
, as the set of vectors
g\in\mathbf{E}
such that

\liminf_{v\to w,v\neq w}\frac{f(v)-f(w)-\langle g,v-w\rangle}{\|v-w\|}\geq 0.

We say that
f
is subdifferentiable at
w
if
\partial f(w)\neq\emptyset
. We will say
f
is subdifferentiable if it is subdifferentiable at every point.

We will need a characterization of the regular subdifferential in terms of directional derivatives. We recall the definition of the directional derivative of a function
f
at point
w
in direction
e
:

f^{\prime}(x;e):=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0,c\to e}\frac{f(w+\varepsilon e)-f(w)}{\varepsilon}.

Proposition 24

(Regular subdifferential and directional derivatives)
.

Let
f:\mathbf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function which is subdifferentiable at
w
, then

\partial f(w)=\{g\in\mathbf{E}:\,\langle g,e\rangle\leq f^{\prime}(w;e)\,\,\forall e\in\mathbf{E}\}.

Proof.

Let
L_{0}
be the Lipschitz constant of
f
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
.
We prove both inclusions. First (
\subseteq
), if
g\in\partial f(w)
, then let
e\in\mathbf{E}\setminus\{0\}
. Using the definition of subdifferential for
w
and
v=w+\varepsilon c
(where
\varepsilon\to 0
and
c\to e
), we get

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0,c\to e}\frac{f(w+\varepsilon c)-f(w)}{\varepsilon\|c\|}-\frac{\langle g,c\rangle}{\|c\|}\geq 0

Taking first the limit
c\to e
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get
f^{\prime}(w;e)\geq\langle g,e\rangle
, concluding the desired inclusion.

For the reverse inclusion (
\supseteq
), let
g\in\mathbf{E}
be s.t.
\langle g,e\rangle\leq f^{\prime}(w;e)
, for all
e\in\mathbf{E}
. Now let
v\to w
, and consider any
e\in\mathbf{E}
accumulation point of
(v-w)/\|v-w\|
(they exist by compactness of the unit sphere). Next, let
\varepsilon=\|v-w\|
, and notice that
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then

\displaystyle f(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(w)+[f(v)-f(w+\varepsilon e)]+[f(w+\varepsilon e)-f(w)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(w)-L_{0}\|(v-w)-\varepsilon e\|+\frac{f(w+\varepsilon e)-f(w)}{\varepsilon}\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(w)+\frac{f(w+\varepsilon e)-f(w)}{\varepsilon}\varepsilon-L_{0}\|v-w\|\Big(\frac{v-w}{\|v-w\|}-e\Big).

Taking
v\to w
(which is equivalent to
\varepsilon\to 0
), we get

\displaystyle f(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(w)+f^{\prime}(w;e)\varepsilon+o(\|v-w\|)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(w)+\langle g,\varepsilon e\rangle+o(\|v-w\|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(w)+\langle g,v-w\rangle+\varepsilon\Big\langle g,e-\frac{(v-w)}{\varepsilon}\Big\rangle+o(\|v-w\|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(w)+\langle g,v-w\rangle+o(\|v-w\|),

where in the second step we used the starting assumption.
∎

Finally, we present the well-known fact that weak convexity implies that the variation of the function compared to its subgradient approximation is lower bounded by a negative quadratic.

Proposition 25

(Characterization of weak convexity from the regular subdifferential)
.

Let
f:{\cal W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be subdifferentiable and Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
. Then
f
is
\rho
-weakly convex if and only if for all
w,v\in\mathbf{E}
, and

We prove both implications. For
\Rightarrow

v,w\in\mathbf{E}
, and
0<\lambda<1
. By
\rho
-weak convexity:

\displaystyle f((1-\lambda)v+\lambda w)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)f(v)+\lambda f(w)+\frac{\rho\lambda(1-\lambda)}{2}\|v-w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad(1-\lambda)[f(v)-f(w)]

\displaystyle\geq f((1-\lambda)v+\lambda w)-f(w)-\frac{\rho\lambda(1-\lambda)}{2}\|v-w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad f(v)-f(w)

\displaystyle\geq\lim\inf_{\lambda\to 1}\Big[\frac{f(w+(1-\lambda)(v-w))-f(w)}{(1-\lambda)}-\frac{\rho\lambda}{2}\|v-w\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(w;v-w)-\frac{\rho}{2}\|v-w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\langle g,v-w\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|v-w\|^{2},

Next, for

\displaystyle f(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f((1-\lambda)w+\lambda v)+\langle g,(1-\lambda)(v-w)\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|(1-\lambda)(v-w)\|^{2}

\displaystyle f(w)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f((1-\lambda)w+\lambda v)+\langle g,\lambda(w-v)\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\lambda(v-w)\|^{2}.

B.1.1
Basic Rules of the Subdifferential, Optimality Conditions and Stationarity Gap

We know provide some basic tools regarding subdifferentials and optimality conditions in weakly convex programming, which will also allow us to introduce the notion of stationarity gap in this setting.

To start, we provide a basic calculus rule for the subdifferential of a sum of weakly convex functions.

Theorem 27

(Corollary 10.9 from
[
31
]
)
.

If
f:\mathbf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be weakly convex, and
g:\mathbf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be convex, lower semicontinuous, and such that
w\in\mbox{dom}(g)
. Then
\partial(f+g)(w)=\partial f(w)+\partial g(w).

Next, we provide a relation between directional derivatives and the regular subdifferential.

Proposition 28

(From Proposition 8.32 in
[
31
]
)
.

If
\varphi:\mathbb{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is weakly convex, then

\mbox{dist}(0,\partial\varphi(w))=-\inf_{\|e\|\leq 1}\varphi^{\prime}(w;e).

With these results, we can now provide optimality conditions for weakly convex optimization

Proposition 29

(Stationarity conditions for weakly convex optimization)
.

Let
f:{\cal W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be
\rho
-weakly convex and
L_{0}
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
, and
{\cal W}
a closed and convex set. Then, if
w^{\ast}\in\arg\min\{f(w):\,w\in{\cal W}\},
then there exists
g\in\partial f(w^{\ast})
such that

\langle g,v-w^{\ast}\rangle\geq 0\qquad(\forall v\in{\cal W}).

Proof.

First, we observe that without loss of generality,
f:\mathbf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
(this is a consequence of the Lipschitz extension Theorem). Let now
g(w)=\chi_{\cal W}(w)
(i.e., the convex indicator function, as defined in the beginning of this section). Since
w^{\ast}\in{\cal W}
, by Proposition
27
, we have

\partial(f+g)(w^{\ast})=\partial f(w^{\ast})+\partial g(w^{\ast}).
Now we apply Proposition
29
to
\varphi(w)=f(w)+g(w)
; since
w^{\ast}
is a minimizer of
\varphi
, we have that
\varphi^{\prime}(w^{\ast};e)\geq 0
for all
e
, and hence
\mbox{dist}(0,\partial\varphi(w^{\ast}))=0
. Since
\partial g(w^{\ast})={\cal N}(w^{\ast})
, we get that

0=\mbox{dist}(0,\partial f(w^{\ast})+{\cal N}_{\cal W}(w^{\ast})),

and this implies that there exists
g\in\partial f(w^{\ast})
, such that
g\in-{\cal N}_{\cal W}(w^{\ast})
, i.e.,

\langle g,v-w^{\ast}\rangle\geq 0\qquad(\forall v\in{\cal W}).

∎

The previous result leads to a natural definition of the stationarity gap in weakly convex optimization:

\mathsf{Gap}_{f}(w)=\inf_{g\in\partial f(w)}\sup_{v\in{\cal W}}\langle g,v-w\rangle.

(20)

Notice that, by Proposition
29
, any minimizer of a weakly convex and Lipschitz function is such that its stationarity gap is equal to zero.

Missing Details in Consequences of Proximal Near Stationarity

Now we explain some technical details behind the derivation of the following consequence for proximal nearly-stationary algorithms

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal A}}\big[\|\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))-{\cal A}(S)\|\big]\leq\vartheta\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal A}}\big[\mathsf{Gap}_{F_{\cal D}}\big(\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))\big)\big]\leq\vartheta.

(21)

First, we suppose
{\cal A}
is
(\vartheta,\beta)
-proximal nearly stationary. From this, we directly conclude the first property,

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal A}}\big[\|\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))-{\cal A}(S)\|\big]\leq\vartheta.

For the second property, we first recall the stationarity gap in weakly convex
optimization (see eqn. (
20
)): here, for
w\in{\cal W}
and objective
f:{\cal W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, define

\mathsf{Gap}_{f}(w)=\inf_{g\in\partial f(w)}\sup_{v\in{\cal W}}\langle g,w-v\rangle.

Now, if
{\cal B}:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a randomized algorithm, its expected gap corresponds to

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal B}}[\mathsf{Gap}_{F_{\cal D}}({\cal B}(S))]=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal B}}\Big[\inf_{g\in\partial F_{\cal D}({\cal B}(S))}\sup_{v\in{\cal W}}\langle g,{\cal B}(S)-v\rangle\Big].

Finally, under this definition of the expected gap, we have that if
{\cal B}(S)=\mathsf{prox}_{F_{\cal D}}^{\beta}({\cal A}(S))
, then by Lemma
17
and
(\vartheta,\beta)
-proximal near stationarity,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Gap}_{F_{\cal D}}({\cal B})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal B}}\Big[\inf_{g\in\partial F_{\cal D}({\cal B}(S))}\sup_{v\in{\cal W}}\langle g,{\cal B}(S)-v\rangle\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim{\cal D}^{n},{\cal B}}\Big[\beta D\|{\cal B}(S)-{\cal A}(S)\|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\vartheta,

concluding the claim.
