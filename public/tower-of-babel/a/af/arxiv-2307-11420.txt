OU-HET 1176 Quantum phase transition and absence of quadratic divergence in generalized quantum field theories
Hikaru Kawai, Kiyoharu Kawana, Kin-ya Oda, and Kei Yagyu

Abstract
In ordinary thermodynamics, around first-order phase transitions, the intensive parameters such as temperature and pressure are automatically fixed to the phase transition point when one controls the extensive parameters such as total volume and total energy. From the microscopic point of view, the extensive parameters are more fundamental than the intensive parameters. Analogously, in conventional quantum field theory (QFT), coupling constants (including masses) in the path integral correspond to intensive parameters in the partition function of the canonical formulation. Therefore, it is natural to expect that in a more fundamental formulation of QFT, coupling constants are dynamically fixed a posteriori , just as the intensive parameter in the micro-canonical formulation. Here, we demonstrate that the automatic tuning of the coupling constants is realized at a quantum-phase-transition point at zero temperature, even when the transition is of higher order, due to the Lorentzian nature of the path integral. This naturally provides a basic foundation for the multi-critical point principle. As a concrete toy model for solving the Higgs hierarchy problem, we study how the mass parameter is fixed in the ϕ 4 \phi^{4} theory at the one-loop level in the micro-canonical or further generalized formulation of QFT. We find that there are two critical points for the renormalized mass: zero and of the order of ultraviolet-cutoff. In the former, the Higgs mass is automatically tuned to be zero and thus its fine-tuning problem is solved. We also show that the quadratic divergence is absent in a more realistic two-scalar model that realizes the dimensional transmutation. Additionally, we explore the possibility of fixing quartic coupling in ϕ 4 \phi^{4} theory and find that it can be fixed to a finite value.

2.1
Statistical mechanics

To clarify the idea, let us briefly recall statistical mechanics.
When control parameters are temperature
T
and volume
V
, the system is described by the canonical partition function

\displaystyle Z_{V}(T)=e^{-F_{V}(T)/T}=\sum_{n}e^{-E_{n}/T},

(1)

where
E_{n}
denotes the energy eigenvalue and
F_{V}(T)=-T\log Z_{V}(T)
is the Helmhortz free energy.

On the other hand, we can also consider the micro-canonical formulation where
E
is a control parameter instead of
T
.
The corresponding partition function, the number of states, is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{V}(E)=\Delta E\sum_{n}\delta(E_{n}-E)=e^{S_{V}(E)},

(2)

where
S_{V}(E)
denotes the entropy and
\Delta E
denotes a sufficiently small energy interval whose effects on observables vanish in the thermodynamic limit
V\rightarrow\infty
.
In the following, we omit the subscript
V
for simplicity.

The equivalence between the canonical and micro-canonical formulations can be shown as

\displaystyle e^{-F(T)/T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}e^{-E_{n}/T}=\int dE\sum_{n}\delta(E-E_{n})e^{-E/T}=\frac{1}{\Delta E}\int dE\,e^{S(E)-E/T}

\displaystyle=\frac{V}{\Delta E}\int d\varepsilon\,e^{V\left(s(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon/T\right)},

(3)

where
s=S/V
(
\varepsilon=E/V
) represents the entropy (energy) density.
The integration is dominated by the saddle point in the thermodynamic limit
V\rightarrow\infty
as

\displaystyle Z_{V}(T)=e^{-F(T)/T}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{T(2\pi C)^{1/2}}{\Delta E}e^{V(s(\varepsilon_{*})-\varepsilon_{*}/T)},

(4)

\displaystyle\therefore\ \frac{F(T)}{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{*}}{T}-S(E_{*})+{\cal O}(\log V),

(5)

where
C=\partial E/\partial T
is the specific heat and
E_{*}=\varepsilon_{*}V
is the solution of

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}=\frac{1}{T}.

(6)

Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) are nothing but the Legendre transformation between the free energy and the entropy in thermodynamics.
It is also straightforward to check the equivalence of the ensemble averages of a general observable
\hat{x}
.
The canonical ensemble average is given by

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{\rm can}=\frac{1}{Z_{V}(T)}\sum_{n}x_{n}e^{-E_{n}/T},

(7)

where
x_{n}=\langle E_{n}|\hat{x}|E_{n}\rangle
.
This can be written as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}\rangle^{\rm can}_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{V}(T)}\sum_{n}x_{n}\int dEe^{-E/T}\delta(E-E_{n})

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{V}(T)\Delta E}\int dEe^{S(E)-E/T}\langle\hat{x}\rangle^{\rm mic}_{E}~,

(9)

where

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}\rangle^{\rm mic}_{E}=\frac{\sum_{n}x_{n}\delta(E-E_{n})}{\Omega_{V}(E)}

(10)

is the micro-canonical ensemble average.
Because the integrand in Eq. (
9
) has a strong peak at
E=E_{*}
as in Eq. (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{T}^{\rm can}=\langle\hat{x}\rangle^{\rm mic}_{E}

(11)

in the thermodynamic limit
V\rightarrow\infty
.
In particular, during the first-order phase transition,
T
is equal to the critical temperature
T_{c}(E)
, but
E
varies between the two phases.
This means that the coexisting phases are described in the finite parameter region of the micro-canonical scheme.
In this sense, a fine-tuning to a first-order phase transition point
T=T_{c}(E)
is automatically realized as long as
E
is the finite region.

2.2
Micro-canonical formulation of QFT

Now let us return to QFT.
Conventionally we start from “canonical” partition function with the bare coupling constants
\set{\lambda}:=\set{\lambda_{j}}_{j=0,1,2,\dots}
:

\displaystyle Z\left(\set{\lambda}\right)=\int{\cal D}\phi\exp\left(i\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}O_{j}[\phi]\right),

(12)

where
O_{j}[\phi]
(
j=0,1,2,\dots
) denotes a spacetime integral of a local operator such as
\int d^{d}x{1\over 2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\phi(x)\right)^{2}
,
\int d^{d}x{1\over 2}\left(\phi(x)\right)^{2}
,
\int d^{d}x{1\over 4!}\left(\phi(x)\right)^{4}
, etc.
After the renormalization procedure, we can calculate physical observables finitely as functions of (renormalized) couplings.
However, the problem is that there is no principle to pick up specific values of
\set{\lambda}
theoretically, and this is the very origin of the fine-tuning problems.
Here comes the analogy between QFT and statistical mechanics into play:
What if we start from the “micro-canonical” picture in QFT?

The number of states
\Omega(E)
in statistical mechanics can naturally be promoted to the partition function in QFT as

\displaystyle\Omega\!\left(\set{A}\right)=\int{\cal D}\phi\prod_{j}\delta\!\left(O_{j}[\phi]-A_{j}\right),

(13)

where we write
\set{A}:=\set{A_{j}}_{j=0,1,2,\dots}
and each
A_{j}
is an “extensive parameter” corresponding to
O_{j}[\phi]
, which is proportional to the spacetime volume
V_{d}
.
By using the Fourier transform of the delta function, Eq. (
13
) can be written as

\displaystyle\Omega\!\left(\set{A}\right)=\int\left(\prod_{j}d\lambda_{j}\right)\int{\cal D}\phi\exp\left(i\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}\left(O_{l}[\phi]-A_{l}\right)\right)=\int\left(\prod_{j}d\lambda_{j}\right)Z\!\left(\set{\lambda}\right)\,e^{-i\sum_{l}\lambda_{l}A_{l}}.

(14)

Now, one can see that we have an ensemble average of various coupling constants and their weight is proportional to the canonical partition function (
12
).

Further, we can generalize

Fixing mass in free scalar theory

We study the free scalar theory in the generalized QFT and show how the bare mass parameter is fixed.

3.1
Partition function of free scalar theory

We consider the free scalar theory in the
d
-dimensional spacetime:

\displaystyle S_{0}=-\int d^{d}x\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\phi\right)^{2}.

(17)

Here, we introduce the bare mass term according to the generalized QFT, while leaving the kinetic term (
17
) as is, for simplicity.
Then the generalized partition function is defined by

\displaystyle\Omega(A)

\displaystyle:=\int{\cal D}\phi e^{iS_{0}}W\!\left(A-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\phi^{2}(x)\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\int{\cal D}\phi\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)\exp\left\{i\left[S_{0}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}\left(A-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\phi^{2}(x)\right)\right]\right\}

(19)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)e^{im_{\text{B}}^{2}A}Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right),

(20)

where
\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
is the Fourier transform of
W(x)
and

Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
denotes the ordinary canonical partition function

\displaystyle Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)=\int{\cal D}\phi\exp\left[i\left(S_{0}-\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{d}x\phi^{2}(x)\right)\right].

(21)

We assume that
\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
is an ordinary smooth function.
We can now perform the path-integral as

\displaystyle\Omega(A)

\displaystyle\propto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)\exp\left[im_{\text{B}}^{2}A-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\log\left[i\left(-\Box+m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i\varepsilon\right)\right]\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)\exp\left[iV_{d}\left(am_{\text{B}}^{2}-F\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)\right)\right],

(23)

where
\Box=\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}
,
a=A/V_{d}
,
i\varepsilon
is the Feynman’s prescription, and

\displaystyle F\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\log\!\left(p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i\varepsilon\right)+\rm const.,

(24)

with
p_{\text{E}}^{\mu}
being the Euclidean momentum.
This is apparently UV divergent and we need a regularization.

First, let us study the cut-off regularization.
As a function of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
, the integrand in Eq. (

) has a branch cut at

\displaystyle-\Lambda^{2}+i\varepsilon\leq m_{\text{B}}^{2}\leq i\varepsilon,

(25)

and the integration line
-\infty<m_{\text{B}}^{2}<+\infty
is located below this branch cut as shown in the left panel in Fig.

Then, Eq. (
24
) can be evaluated as

\displaystyle F\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{d-1}}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}dp_{\text{E}}p_{\text{E}}^{d-1}\log\!\left(p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i\varepsilon\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{d-1}\Lambda^{d}}{4(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}dxx^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\log\!\left(x+\xi-i\varepsilon\right),\quad\xi=m_{\text{B}}^{2}/\Lambda^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{d-1}\Lambda^{d}}{4(2\pi)^{d}}\times\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{1}dxx^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\log(x+\xi)&\text{for }\xi>0\\
\int_{0}^{1}dxx^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\log|x+\xi|-i\frac{2\pi}{d}(-\xi)^{\frac{d}{2}}&\text{for }0>\xi>-1\\
\int_{0}^{1}dxx^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\log|x+\xi|-i\frac{2\pi}{d}&\text{for }-1>\xi\end{cases},

(28)

where
S_{d-1}
denotes the area of a

We see that the function
F(m_{\text{B}}^{2})
contains the imaginary part for
m_{\text{B}}^{2}<0
, which gives a large suppression in the partition function when
V_{d}
is large.
Qualitatively, the contribution from
m_{\text{B}}^{2}<0
is

\displaystyle\int^{0}_{-\infty}d\xi e^{-V_{d}\Lambda^{d}(-\xi)^{d/2}}={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{(V_{d}\Lambda^{d})^{\frac{2}{d}}}\right).

(29)

On the other hand, there is no such suppression for
m_{\text{B}}^{2}>0
, and the integrand is a rapidly oscillating function of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
.
In this case, the saddle point can exist at the point determined by

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{dF}{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}=\frac{S_{d-1}\Lambda^{d-2}}{4(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\frac{x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}{x+\xi},

(30)

When

\displaystyle a=\frac{S_{3}\Lambda^{2}}{4(2\pi)^{4}}\left(1-\xi\log\frac{1+\xi}{\xi}\right)=:\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}f(\xi),

(31)

where

Thus, there can be a saddle point when

, while the exponent,
am_{\text{B}}^{2}-F\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
, becomes a monotonic function when
a/\Lambda^{2}>\left(32\pi^{2}\right)^{-1}
.

Dimensional regularization

Let us also calculate the partition function in the dimensional regularization.
We define
\epsilon=4-d
.
In this case, the free energy is calculated as

\displaystyle F\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)^{2}}{2(4\pi)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\log 4\pi-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i{\varepsilon}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=:-\frac{\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)^{2}}{2(4\pi)^{2}}\left(c_{\overline{\rm MS}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i{\varepsilon}}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)\right),

(32)

where
\mu
is the renormalization scale and

contains both the finite and divergent terms.

For
m_{\text{B}}^{2}\leq 0
, we again have the imaginary part from the logarithmic term, and the partition function is highly suppressed.
On the other hand, for
m_{\text{B}}^{2}>0
, the saddle point is determined by

\displaystyle a=\frac{dF}{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}=\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{\mu^{2}e^{1+2c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}}\right),

(33)

which always has a solution unlike the cut-off case. If we identify
\mu e^{c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}
as the cut-off scale,
the location of the saddle points is

\displaystyle m_{\text{B}}^{2}\sim\Lambda^{2}\sim\mu^{2}e^{2c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}~

(34)

in both of the regularizations.

Mass tuning in free scalar theory

As we have seen, the existence of a saddle point depends on the regularization scheme.
But in any case,
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
holds over a wide range of the parameter space.
First, if there is no saddle point, the
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
integration is dominated by the boundary
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
by the mathematical formula (
88
) for any smooth weight function
\omega(m_{\text{B}}^{2})
.
On the other hand, if there is a saddle point,
am_{\text{B}}^{2}-F(m_{\text{B}}^{2})
is monotonic below the saddle point
m_{\text{B}}^{2}\lesssim\Lambda^{2}
.
Thus, when the weight function
\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
has a finite support for
m_{\text{B}}^{2}\lesssim\Lambda^{2}
, the free energy
am_{\text{B}}^{2}-F\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
is monotonic, and the boundary
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
is always dominant due to the mathematical formula (
88
). In conclusion,
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
appears to be a unique critical point that is physically reasonable in the generalized partition function (
23
).

3.3
Equivalence in large volume limit

Finally, let us confirm the equivalence between generalized QFT and canonical QFT in the large volume limit.
When
A\neq 0
, the first derivative of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}A/V_{d}-F(m_{\text{B}}^{2})
is continuous and nonzero at
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
while the second derivative is discontinuous.
Then, we can use the mathematical formula (
92
) and
\Omega(A)
is evaluated as

\displaystyle\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\Omega(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega(m_{\text{B}}^{2})e^{im_{\text{B}}^{2}A}Z(m_{\text{B}}^{2})={\cal N}\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{Z(m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0)}{V_{d}^{2}}~,

(35)

where
{\cal N}
is an unimportant numerical factor.
That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\log\Omega(A)=\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\log Z(m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0)+{\cal O}(\log V_{d}).

(36)

We apparently see the equivalence in the large volume limit.

We can also check the equivalence of correlation functions.
We introduce a source term as

\displaystyle\Omega[A;J]

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\phi e^{iS_{0}-i\int d^{d}xJ(x)\phi(x)}W\!\left(A-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\phi^{2}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)e^{iV_{d}\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}a-F(m^{2})\right)}\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\int d^{d}xJ(x)(-\Box+m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i\epsilon)^{-1}J(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)e^{iAm_{\text{B}}^{2}}Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)G(J;m_{\text{B}}^{2})~.

(37)

As long as
J(x)
is a finite supported function, i.e., when it has no volume dependence, the factor
G(J;m_{\text{B}}^{2})
does not have exponentially large volume dependence, and the
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
integral in Eq. (
37
) is dominated by the critical point of
Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
.
Namely, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\Omega[A;J]={\rm const.}\times G(J;m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0).

(38)

Then, by taking the functional derivatives with respect to
J(x)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle T\left\{\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\right\}\right\rangle_{\rm mic}=\left\langle T\left\{\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\right\}\right\rangle_{\rm can}\bigg|_{m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0},

(39)

which corresponds to Eq. (
11
) in statistical mechanics.

4
Fixing mass in
\phi^{4}
theory

In this section, we analyze the
\phi^{4}
theory in the scope of generalized QFT.
In particular, we study how the mass term is fixed by studying its critical point at the one-loop level, and also in the large-
N
extended model.
Our investigation reveals that the renormalized mass settles at zero in the one-loop analysis as well as in the large-
N
model, due to the discontinuity of the derivative of the vacuum energy.

In this section, we assume that the quartic coupling is prefixed at a certain value, as in the ordinary QFT. The possibility of its tuning will be discussed in the next section.

4.1
Mass-squared at one-loop level

We first introduce a bare action without the bare mass term:

\displaystyle S_{\text{B}}[\phi]=\int d^{d}x\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{4!}\phi^{4}-\Lambda_{\rm B}\right),

(40)

where we have also included the bare cosmological constant term for later convenience.
For simplicity, we have imposed the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
\phi\rightarrow-\phi
, which leaves only the quadratic and quartic terms in the renormalizable potential.

Our starting point is the generalized partition function
\Omega(A)
which can be expressed in terms of the canonical partition function
Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)
by the same procedure as in Sec.
3.1
:

\displaystyle\Omega(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)e^{im_{\text{B}}^{2}A}\,Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right),

(41)

where

\displaystyle Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\phi e^{iS[\phi]},

\displaystyle S[\phi]

\displaystyle=S_{\text{B}}[\phi]-\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}\int d^{d}x\phi^{2}.

(42)

The canonical partition function
Z
can be obtained from the ordinary effective action
\Gamma[\phi]
via
Z=e^{i\min_{\phi}\Gamma[\phi]}
. At the one-loop level, we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma[\phi]

\displaystyle=S[\phi]-\frac{1}{2i}{\rm tr}\log\left(-\Box+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{2}\phi^{2}-i\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=S[\phi]+\frac{V_{d}}{2}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\log\left(p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{2}\phi^{2}-i\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=S[\phi]+\frac{V_{d}}{2}\frac{(M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi))^{2}}{(4\pi)^{2}}\left[c_{\overline{\rm MS}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi)-i\varepsilon}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)\right],

(43)

where
M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi)=m_{\text{B}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{2}\phi^{2}
.

Now the one-loop effective potential is given by

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(\phi):=-\frac{\Gamma[\phi]}{V_{d}}\bigg|_{\phi={\rm const}}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\text{B}}+\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{4!}\phi^{4}-\frac{(M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi))^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\left[c_{\overline{\rm MS}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi)-i\varepsilon}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\text{R}}+\frac{m_{\text{R}}^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{R}}}{4!}\phi^{4}+\frac{(M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi))^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{M_{\phi}^{2}(\phi)-i\varepsilon}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right),

(44)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\text{B}}-c_{\overline{\rm MS}}\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{4}}{32\pi^{2}},

\displaystyle m_{\text{R}}^{2}

\displaystyle=m_{\text{B}}^{2}-\frac{c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}{16\pi^{2}}\lambda_{\text{B}}m_{\text{B}}^{2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{R}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\text{B}}-\frac{3c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}{16\pi^{2}}\lambda_{\text{B}}^{2},

(45)

denote the renormalized parameters.
We can regard these renormalized couplings as our parameters instead of the bare couplings
(m_{\text{B}}^{2},\lambda_{\text{B}})
.

As a consistency check, let us consider the free theory limit:
\lambda_{\text{B}}=\lambda_{\text{R}}=0
.
In this case, there is no difference between the bare mass and the renormalized mass, and the effective potential is exactly given by

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(\phi)\bigg|_{\lambda_{\text{B}}=0}=\Lambda_{\text{R}}+\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{4}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i{\varepsilon}}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right),

(46)

which has a trivial minimum at
\phi=0
and the corresponding vacuum energy is

\displaystyle V_{\rm min}=\Lambda_{\text{B}}-\frac{\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\left(c_{\overline{\rm MS}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}-i{\varepsilon}}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)\right),

(47)

which is nothing but Eq. (
32
).

Let us come back to the interacting scalar theory.
We assume
\lambda_{\text{R}}>0
to ensure the stability of the effective potential.
As usual, we can obtain the RG improved effective potential by choosing the renormalization scale
\mu
appropriately.
For
m_{\text{R}}^{2}\geq 0
, the VEV is trivial
v=0
, and we take
\mu=m_{R}e^{-3/4}
, which will give a simple expression of the vacuum energy (as the first line in Eq. (
50
) below).
On the other hand, for
m_{\text{R}}^{2}<0
, the field
\phi
develops a nonzero VEV,
which is determined by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V_{\rm eff}(\phi)}{\partial\phi}=0

\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m_{\text{R}}^{2}v+\frac{\lambda_{\text{R}}}{6}v^{3}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{R}}v}{32\pi^{2}}\left(M_{\phi}^{2}(v)\log\left(\frac{M_{\phi}^{2}(v)}{\mu^{2}e}\right)\right)=0~.

(48)

By choosing the renormalization scale at
\mu^{2}=M_{\phi}^{2}(v)e^{-1}
,
the VEV is given by

\displaystyle v^{2}=-\frac{6m_{\text{R}}^{2}}{\lambda_{\text{R}}}~.

(49)

Now the vacuum energy as a function of
m_{\text{R}}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle V_{\rm min}(m_{\text{R}}^{2})=\Lambda_{\text{B}}-\frac{c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}{32\pi^{2}}(m_{\text{R}}^{2})^{2}+\begin{cases}0&\text{for }m_{\text{R}}^{2}\geq 0\\
-\frac{3(m_{\text{R}}^{2})^{2}}{2\lambda_{\text{R}}}-\frac{\left(2m_{\text{R}}^{2}\right)^{2}}{128\pi^{2}}&\text{for }m_{\text{R}}^{2}<0\end{cases},

(50)

which shows that the second derivative of
V_{\rm min}(m_{\text{R}}^{2})
is discontinuous at
m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\rm min}}{\partial(m_{\text{R}}^{2})^{2}}\bigg|_{m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0+}

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}{16\pi^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\rm min}}{\partial(m_{\text{R}}^{2})^{2}}\bigg|_{m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0-}

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}{16\pi^{2}}-\frac{3}{\lambda_{\text{R}}}+\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}~.

(51)

Note that the first derivative of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}A/V_{d}-V_{\rm min}(m_{\text{R}}^{2})
is continuous and nonzero at
m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0
as long as
A\neq 0
, which means that we can use the mathematical formula Eq. (
92
).
Then,
the partition function is evaluated as

\displaystyle\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}e^{im_{B}^{2}A+\log Z(m_{\text{R}}^{2})}=\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}e^{im_{B}^{2}A-iV_{d}V_{\rm min}(m_{\text{R}}^{2})}\propto\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{-iV_{d}V_{\rm min}(m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0)}}{V_{d}^{2}}\delta(m_{\text{R}}^{2}),

(52)

which leads to

\displaystyle\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\Omega(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{R}}^{2}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\partial m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{\partial m_{\text{R}}^{2}}\right)\omega(m_{\text{B}}^{2})e^{im_{\text{B}}^{2}A+\log Z(m_{\text{R}}^{2})}=\frac{{\cal N}}{V_{d}^{2}}e^{\log Z(m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0)},

(53)

where
{\cal N}
is an unimportant factor.
That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\log\Omega(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{V_{d}\rightarrow\infty}\log Z(m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0)+{\cal O}\!\left(\log V_{d}\right),

(54)

We see that the equivalence between two formulations still holds in the
\phi^{4}
theory at the one-loop level in the large volume limit.
In particular, the renormalized mass parameter
m_{\text{R}}^{2}
is fixed at zero because of the discontinuity of the second derivatives of the vacuum energy.
3
3

3

Precisely speaking, there is also a saddle point solution
m_{\text{R}}^{2}\sim-\frac{A}{V_{d}c_{\overline{\rm MS}}}
.
The value of which depends on the regularization schemes and the way the large volume limit
A/(c_{\overline{\rm MS}}V_{d})=
fixed is taken.
On the other hand, the critical point
m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0
has no such uncertainty and is uniquely determined as in the free case.

This result implies that the quadratic divergence problem is absent in the micro-canonical formulation.
Moreover, in the simple
\phi^{4}
theory, the conclusion is not affected by higher-loop corrections as long as
\lambda_{\text{R}}\lesssim 1
.
However, the situation can be different in more general theories with more than one field
because another mass scale can be radiatively generated by other fields.
See Sec.
6
for a concrete two-scalar model.

4.2
Mass-squared in Large-
N
model

In this section, we turn to the
O(N+1)
symmetric scalar theory with
\phi_{i}
(
i=0,1,\dots,N
), and discuss how the mass parameter is fixed in the generalized partition function in the large-
N
limit.
The bare action is given by

\displaystyle S_{\text{B}}=\int d^{d}x\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi_{i})^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{4!}(\phi_{i}^{2})^{2}\right),

(55)

where we have omitted the bare mass term as before and apply the Einstein summation convention for the field index
i
.

The generalized partition function now becomes

\displaystyle\Omega(A)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)e^{im_{\text{B}}^{2}NA}\int{\cal D}\phi e^{iS}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2\pi}\omega\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)e^{im_{\text{B}}^{2}NA}Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right),

(56)

where

\displaystyle S=\int d^{d}x\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m_{\text{B}}^{2}\phi_{i}^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{4!}(\phi_{i}^{2})^{2}\right)~.

(57)

We can generally separate the original field
\phi_{i}
as

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{N}s&i=0\\
\pi_{i}&i=1,2,\cdots,N\end{cases}\quad,

(58)

where
s
is the field that may acquire a VEV.
The Lagrangian now becomes

\displaystyle{\cal L}=N\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}s)^{2}-\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}s^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\pi_{i})^{2}-\frac{m_{\text{B}}^{2}}{2}\pi_{i}^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\text{B}}}{4!}(Ns^{2}+\pi_{i}^{2})^{2}.

(59)

We can introduce an auxiliary scalar field
c
in the Lagrangian such that the partition function does not change after performing the path-integral over
c
:

\displaystyle{\cal L}

\displaystyle=N\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}s)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c)s^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\pi_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c)\pi_{i}^{2}+\frac{3}{2\lambda_{\text{B}}}c^{2}.

(60)

By performing the path-integral of
\pi_{i}
, the partition function is given as

\displaystyle Z(m_{\text{B}}^{2})=\int{\cal D}\phi e^{iS}=\int{\cal D}c\int{\cal D}s\exp\bigg[iN\int d^{d}x\bigg(

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}s)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c)s^{2}+\frac{1}{4\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}c^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\log(p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c-i\epsilon)\bigg)\bigg].

(61)

where
\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}:=\lambda_{\text{B}}N/6
. The variation of
c
gives

\displaystyle-s^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}c-\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c-i\epsilon}=0,

(62)

while the variation of
s
gives

\displaystyle(-\Box+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c)s=0.

(63)

As long as we focus on the ground state, we can omit
\Box s
.
For a given value of
\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}
, the parameter space of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
is divided into two regions as shown in Fig.
2
, which correspond to the broken phase (blue) and the unbroken phase (uncolored) respectively.
Now let us discuss the behavior of the partition function in each region.

Broken phase

When
s\neq 0
, we have
m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c=0
.
Eq. (
62
) then becomes

\displaystyle s^{2}=-\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}m_{\text{B}}^{2}-\int^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{p_{\text{E}}^{2}}>0,

(64)

which indicates the parameter space of broken phase.
This is shown by the blue region in Fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
Left: Parameter space in the large
N
limit.

We represent the critical value of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
as
-m_{\Lambda}^{2}
.
For
d=4
, it is

\displaystyle m_{\Lambda}^{2}=\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}\Lambda^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}~.

(65)

For the parameters obeying Eq. (
64
), the canonical partition function Eq. (
61
) becomes very simple in the large
N
analysis.
In fact,

\displaystyle e^{im_{B}^{2}NA}Z\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\exp\left(N\left\{{\rm const}.+im_{\text{B}}^{2}A+\frac{i}{4\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}\int d^{d}xc^{2}\right\}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[N\left\{{\rm const}.+i\frac{V_{d}}{4\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}+\frac{2\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}A}{V_{d}}\right)^{2}\right\}\right]~.

(67)

One can see that there is a saddle point at
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=-2\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}A/V_{d}

if it is smaller than the critical value
-m_{\Lambda}^{2}
.
Otherwise, the exponent in Eq. (
67
) is a monotonic function for
m_{\text{B}}^{2}\leq-m_{\Lambda}^{2}
.
In particular, the second derivative is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\log Z(m_{\text{B}}^{2})}{\partial(m_{\text{B}}^{2})^{2}}=i\frac{NV_{d}}{2\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}~.

(68)

Unbroken phase

When
s=0
, the system is in the unbroken phase.
In this case,
c
is determined by the gap equation

\displaystyle c=\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c-i\varepsilon}.

(69)

This equation has a solution only for

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}m_{\text{B}}^{2}-\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{p_{\text{E}}^{2}}<0,

(70)

which is consistent with Eq. (
64
).
In this case, the canonical partition function becomes

\displaystyle Z\!\left(m_{\text{B}}^{2}\right)\sim\exp\left[iNV_{d}\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\log(p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c-i\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{4\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}c^{2}\right\}\right],

(71)

where
c
depends on
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
via the gap equation (
69
).
The exponent in Eq. (
71
) is a monotonically decreasing function of
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
and
the second derivative with respect to
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
at the critical point
-m_{\Lambda}^{2}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\log Z(m_{\text{B}}^{2})}{\partial(m_{\text{B}}^{2})^{2}}\bigg|_{m_{\text{B}}^{2}=-m_{\Lambda}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{iNV_{d}}{2}\left[\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(p_{\text{E}}^{2}+m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{dc}{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}\left(\frac{dc}{dm_{\text{B}}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]\bigg|_{m_{\text{B}}^{2}=-m_{\Lambda}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{iNV_{d}}{2\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(p_{\text{E}}^{2})^{2}}}{1+\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}\int\frac{d^{d}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{1}{(p_{\text{E}}^{2})^{2}}}~.

(72)

By comparing Eq. (
68
) and Eq. (
72
), one can see that the second derivative is discontinuous at the critical point
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=-m_{\Lambda}^{2}
.
Thus, we conclude that
m_{\text{B}}^{2}
is fixed at
-m_{\Lambda}^{2}
by the mathematical formula (
92
) (as long as
A\neq 0
) at which the renormalized mass
m_{\text{R}}^{2}=m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c
is zero.

5
Fixing quartic coupling in
\phi^{4}
theory

We briefly comment on the possibility of fixing the quartic coupling.
As well as the mass term, we can also consider the generalized partition function for the quartic coupling

\displaystyle\int dm_{\text{R}}^{2}\int d\lambda_{\text{R}}\omega(m_{\text{R}}^{2},\lambda_{\text{R}})e^{im_{\text{R}}^{2}A_{m^{2}}+i\lambda_{\text{R}}A_{\lambda}}Z(m_{\text{R}}^{2},\lambda_{\text{R}}),

(73)

where we take the renormalized couplings as the integration parameters instead of the bare couplings for simplicity.
4
4

4

The Jacobian from the change of variables has been absorbed by the redefinition of
\omega
.

Note that
A_{\lambda}
is another extensive parameter proportional to the spacetime volume
V
.
For simplicity, let us restrict the parameter space to
\lambda_{\text{R}}\geq 0
to guarantee the stability of the system.

At the one-loop level of the
\phi^{4}
theory, the vacuum energy (
50
) vanishes at the critical point of the mass-squared
m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0
for any
\lambda_{\text{R}}\geq 0
, which means that the above partition function becomes

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda_{\text{R}}\omega(\lambda_{\text{R}})e^{i\lambda_{\text{R}}A_{\lambda}}.

(74)

As long as
A_{\lambda}\neq 0
, the exponent is a linear function of
\lambda_{\text{R}}
, and this integration is strongly dominated by
\lambda_{\text{R}}=0
by the formula Eq. (
88
) in the large volume limit.
Thus, the free scalar theory seems to be realized in the generalized QFT in the
\phi^{4}
theory at least at one-loop level.
As long as the renormalized mass
m_{\text{R}}^{2}
is fixed at zero and we focus on the cut-off independent part of the vacuum energy, this conclusion will not be significantly altered by the higher-loop effects because the cut-off independent part should be proportional to
(m_{\text{R}}^{2})^{2}
by dimensional analysis and they vanish at the critical point
m_{\text{R}}^{2}=0
.

On the other hand, non-trivial saddle point can appear if we also include the cut-off dependent parts.
For example, at the three-loop level, we have the following contributions to the vacuum energy:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm R}\frac{A_{\lambda}}{V_{4}}+(c_{1}\lambda_{\rm R}-c_{2}\lambda_{\rm R}^{2})\Lambda^{4}~,

(75)

where
c_{i}>0
are just constants containing the loop suppression factors.
Thus, one can see that there exists a saddle point at

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{R}}\sim\left(\frac{A_{\lambda}}{V_{4}}+c_{1}\right)\bigg/c_{2}~.

(76)

This is a very interesting possibility but its physical meaning is subtle as in the mass parameter case. More detailed studies are left for future investigations.
See also Appendix
B
for the possibility of fixing
\lambda_{\text{R}}
in the large
N
limit.

Dimensional transmutation in two-real-scalar model

As a next non-trivial example, we study a two-real-scalar model
[

,

,

,
35
,
36
,

We show that an automatic tuning of the mass-squared parameter is realized so that the dimensional transmutation is successfully achieved.

For simplicity, we again impose the

\displaystyle{\cal L}=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial S)^{2}-\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}-\frac{m_{S}^{2}}{2}S^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{4}\phi^{2}S^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{\phi}}{4!}\phi^{4}-\frac{\lambda_{S}}{4!}S^{4}.

On the other hand, the
S
direction is always dominated by the tree-level potential, that is, its VEV is well determined by
m_{S}^{2}S^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{S}}{4!}S^{4}
.

Under these conditions, we will show that there is a critical point at
m_{S}^{2}=0
and
m_{\phi}^{2}\neq 0
which corresponds to a quantum first-order phase transition point.
Although the conventional classical conformal point
m_{S}^{2}=m_{\phi}^{2}=0
is not realized in the current formulation, our result serves as a concrete way of dimensional transmutation without assuming an artificial symmetry such as classical conformality.

Now let us discuss the details.
The one-loop effective potential in the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme is given by

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(\phi,S)=\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{m_{S}^{2}}{2}S^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{4}\phi^{2}S^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi}}{4!}\phi^{4}+\frac{\lambda_{S}}{4!}S^{4}

\displaystyle+\frac{(M_{+}^{2}(\phi,S))^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{M_{+}^{2}(\phi,S)}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)+\frac{(M_{-}^{2}(\phi,S))^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{M_{-}^{2}(\phi,S)}{\mu^{2}e^{3/2}}\right),

(78)

where

\displaystyle M_{\pm}^{2}(\phi,S)=\frac{1}{2}\left(M_{\phi}^{2}+M_{S}^{2}\pm\sqrt{(M_{\phi}^{2}-M_{S}^{2})^{2}+4\lambda_{\phi S}^{2}\phi^{2}S^{2}}\right)

(79)

with

\displaystyle M_{\phi}^{2}=m_{\phi}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}S^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi}}{2}\phi^{2},\quad M_{S}^{2}=m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{S}}{2}S^{2}.

(80)

Here, all the coupling constants are the renormalized ones.
As usual, we can take the renormalization scale at the point
\mu=M
where
\lambda_{\phi}
vanishes.
In the following, we first examine how
m_{\phi}^{2}
is fixed for a given value of
m_{S}^{2}
and then discuss the fixing of
m_{S}^{2}
.

First, let us consider the parameter space
m_{S}^{2}\geq 0
.
In this case,
\langle S\rangle=0
as long as the tree-level potential dominates in the
S
direction.
The effective potential for
\phi
is then

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(0,\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)+\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{(m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}\phi^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}\phi^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)~.

(81)

In Fig.
3
, we show the plots of
V_{\rm eff}(0,\phi)
where the different colors correspond to the different values of
m_{\phi}^{2}
.

Figure 3:
One loop effective potential of
\tilde{\phi}
.
Different colors correspond to the different values of
m_{\phi}^{2}
.

In particular, one can see that there exists a quantum first-order phase transition at

\displaystyle m_{\phi}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}e^{1/2}}{64\pi^{2}}M^{2}=:m_{c}^{2}~,

(82)

meaning that
\phi
achieves a nonzero VEV,
\langle\phi\rangle=v_{\phi}
, for
m_{\phi}^{2}<m_{c}^{2}
.
See Appendix
C
for the derivation of Eq. (
82
).
Correspondingly, the VEV and the vacuum energy are given by

\displaystyle\langle\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&{\rm for}~m_{\phi}^{2}\geq m_{c}^{2}\\
v_{\phi}&{\rm for}~m_{\phi}^{2}<m_{c}^{2}\end{cases}~,

(83)

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(0,\langle\phi\rangle)-\frac{(m_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(m_{S}^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{S}^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)&{\rm for}~m_{\phi}^{2}\geq m_{c}^{2}\\
\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{2}v_{\phi}^{2}+\frac{(m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}v_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}v_{\phi}^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)&{\rm for}~m_{\phi}^{2}<m_{c}^{2}\end{cases}~.

(84)

As in the simple
\phi^{4}
theory, the additional contribution in the second line gives the discontinuity of the second derivative of the vacuum energy at
m_{\phi}^{2}=m_{c}^{2}
, and this point is dominant in the generalized partition function.

Second, we turn to the case
m_{S}^{2}<0
.
In this case,
S
already has a VEV at
\langle S\rangle^{2}=-6m_{S}^{2}/\lambda_{S}
at tree level, and there exists the tree-level vacuum energy
-3(m_{S}^{2})^{2}/(2\lambda_{S})
as in the simple
\phi^{4}
theory discussed in the previous sections.
As for the
\phi
potential, nonzero VEV of
S
just changes the effective mass in Eq. (
80
) as

\displaystyle M_{S}^{2}=m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{S}}{2}\langle S\rangle^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}\phi^{2}=2|m_{S}^{2}|+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}\phi^{2}.

(85)

Note that
M_{\phi}^{2}
is just a constant because of
\lambda_{\phi}=0
.
Thus, we can repeat the same discussion as above and conclude that
m_{\phi}^{2}
is still fixed at the critical point
m_{\phi}^{2}=m_{c}^{2}
.

Finally, at such a critical point of
m_{\phi}^{2}
, the vacuum energy as a function of
m_{S}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle V_{\rm min}\bigg|_{m_{\phi}^{2}=m_{c}^{2}}\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{(m_{S}^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{S}^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)&\text{for $m_{S}\geq 0$}\\
-\frac{3(m_{S}^{2})^{2}}{2\lambda_{S}}+\frac{(m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}v_{\phi}^{2})^{2}}{64\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{m_{S}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\phi S}}{2}v_{\phi}^{2}}{M^{2}e^{3/2}}\right)&\text{for $m_{S}<0$}\end{cases}~.

(86)

As in the simple
\phi^{4}
theory,
m_{S}^{2}
is fixed at zero because the second derivative of this equation is discontinuous at
m_{S}^{2}=0
.
The conventional Coleman-Weinberg point
m_{S}^{2}=m_{\phi}^{2}=0
is not realized in the current formation because such a point
m_{S}^{2}=m_{\phi}^{2}=0
corresponds to the degeneracy of false vacua as discussed in Ref.
[
35
]
.
We leave the discussion on the criticality of general extrema for future investigation.

7
Summary

In this paper, we have investigated the Higgs hierarchy problem in the generalized QFT.
We first studied the free scalar theory and found that there are two critical points in the large volume limit: One is
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
and the other is
m_{\text{B}}^{2}={\cal O}(\Lambda^{2})
.
While the former does not depend on the regularization methods, the latter does, implying that
m_{\text{B}}^{2}=0
is a physically reasonable critical point.
This can be a theoretical origin/explanation of classical conformality which is implicitly assumed in many literatures.

We then studied the
\phi^{4}
theory at the one-loop level, as well as the large
N
model.
In this case, we found that a critical point exists at the point where the renormalized mass
m^{2}=m_{\text{B}}^{2}+\delta m_{\rm UV}^{2}
vanishes due to the discontinuity of the vacuum energy’s derivative.
This further backs up the fine-tuning of the (Higgs) mass-squared being automatically accomplished in the generalized QFT.

We have also discussed the possibility that the quartic coupling is automatically fixed, with a positive result.

As a next non-trivial example, we examined the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant two-real-scalar model at the one-loop level by focussing on a simple parameter space where only one real scalar
\phi
can develop a nonzero VEV
\langle\phi\rangle\neq 0
.
Under these conditions, we found that there exists a critical point where
m_{\phi}^{2}=m_{c}^{2}>0
and
m_{S}^{2}=0
, which corresponds to a quantum first-order phase transition point of the theory.
This result gives a complete realization of dimensional transmutation without assuming classical conformality which has been implicitly assumed in many literatures.

Appendix A
Mathematical formulas

In the following, we often use mathematical formulas of generalized functions.
We summarize them here.
The first one is well known as the saddle point approximation:

\displaystyle\lim_{V\rightarrow\infty}e^{-Vg(x)}\simeq e^{-Vg(x_{0})}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{Vg^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})}}\times\delta(x).

(87)

where
g(x)
is a smooth function and
x_{0}
is a saddle point,
g^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
.
The proof of this equation is textbook level and we will not repeat it here.
The second formula is

\displaystyle\lim_{V\rightarrow\infty}e^{iVg(x)}\theta(x)\simeq\frac{i}{V}\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}e^{iVg(0)}\times\delta(x),

(88)

where
g(x)
is now a real, smooth and monotonic function for
x\geq 0
and satisfies
g^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.
The proof is as follows.
By multiplying a test function
f(x)
with a finite support and integrating from
0
to
\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dxe^{iVg(x)}f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{g(0)}^{g(\infty)}dg\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}e^{iVg}f(x=x(g))

(89)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{e^{iVg}}{iV}\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}f(x(g))\right]_{g(0)}^{g(\infty)}-\frac{1}{iV}\int_{g(0)}^{g(\infty)}dg\frac{d}{dg}\left(\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}f(x(g))\right)e^{iVg}

\displaystyle=i\frac{e^{iVg(0)}}{V}\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}f(x)\bigg|_{x=0}-\frac{1}{(iV)^{2}}\left[\frac{d}{dg}\left(\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}f(x(g))\right)e^{iVg}\right]_{g(0)}^{g(\infty)}+\cdots

\displaystyle=i\frac{e^{iVg(0)}}{V}\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}f(x)\bigg|_{x=0}+{\cal O}(V^{-2}),

(90)

which confirms Eq. (
88
).
Note that the contribution from
x=\infty
is zero because we have assumed that
f(x)
has a finite support.
More generally, when
g(x)
is a monotonic and smooth function for both sides
x>0
and
x<0
, but its derivative
g^{\prime}(x)
is discontinuous at
x=0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{V\rightarrow\infty}e^{iVg(x)}=\lim_{V\rightarrow\infty}\frac{i}{V}\left[\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}_{0+}-\left|\frac{dg}{dx}\right|^{-1}_{0-}\right]e^{iVg(0)}\times\delta(x).

(91)

Note that when
g^{\prime}(x)
is also continuous at
x=0
, the right-hand side vanishes and higher order terms dominate.
In general, when the derivatives of
g(x)
are continuous and nonzero up to
(n-1)
-th order but the
n
-th derivative of
g(x)
is discontinuous, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{V\rightarrow\infty}e^{iVg(x)}=c\lim_{V\rightarrow\infty}\frac{i^{n}}{V^{n}}e^{iVg(0)}\times\delta(x),

(92)

where
c
is a coefficient determined by
g^{\prime}(0),g^{\prime\prime}(0),\cdots,d^{(n-1)}g(0)/dx^{n-1},dg^{(n)}(0+)/dx^{n},dg^{(n)}(0-)/dx^{n}
.

Appendix B
Fixing quartic coupling in large
N
model

In this Appendix, we study a possibility of fixing the quartic coupling by taking the
N^{-1}
corrections.
In the following, the mass term
m^{2}
is fixed at the critical point
m^{2}=m_{\text{B}}^{2}+c_{\rm cl}=0
and we focus on
d=4
.

Around the large
N
solution
(c,s)=(c_{\rm cl},s_{\rm cl})
studied in Section
4.2
, the effective action in Eq. (
61
) becomes

\displaystyle S=\frac{N\Lambda^{4}V_{4}}{4\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}\left(\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}{16\pi^{2}}\Lambda^{2}\right)^{2}+N\int d^{4}x\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\delta s)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\delta c\delta s^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}+\int\frac{d^{4}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{(p_{\text{E}}^{2})^{2}}\right)\delta c^{2}+\cdots\right),

(93)

where
\delta s=s-s_{\rm cl},~\delta c=c-c_{\rm cl}
and
\cdots
represents the higher order terms.
Note that the first term is the leading vacuum energy contribution and it is proportional to
\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}
.
Thus, this can be absorbed into the definition of
A_{\lambda}
.
In the following, we represent

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}+\int\frac{d^{4}p_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{(p_{\text{E}}^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{\text{B}}}+\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\right):=\frac{1}{N\lambda(\mu)},

(94)

where
\mu
is some (IR) scale.
We can check that
\lambda(\mu)
corresponds to the usual renormalized quartic coupling because Eq. (
94
) is nothing but the solution of RGE in the large
N
limit:

\displaystyle\frac{d\lambda}{d\log\mu}\approx\frac{N}{8\pi^{2}}\lambda^{2}.

(95)

In Eq. (
93
),
\delta c
part can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4N\lambda(\mu)}\left(\delta c-N\lambda(\mu)\delta s^{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{N\lambda(\mu)}{4}\delta s^{4}.

(96)

By the field redefinitions
N^{1/2}\delta s\rightarrow\delta s
and
N^{1/2}(\delta c-N\lambda(\mu)\delta s^{2})\rightarrow\delta c
, the action becomes

\displaystyle S=\int d^{4}x\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\delta s)^{2}-\frac{\lambda(\mu)}{4}\delta s^{4}+\frac{1}{4N\lambda(\mu)}\delta c^{2}+{\cal O}(N^{-1})\right),

(97)

Now we can perform the one-loop integration of
\delta c
as

\displaystyle-\frac{V_{4}}{2}\int\frac{d^{4}q_{\text{E}}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\log(N\lambda(\mu)/2)^{-1}=-\frac{F}{2}\log\left(\frac{2}{N\lambda(\mu)}\right)=-\frac{F}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{B}}}+\frac{N}{8\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\right)\right)+{\rm const},

(98)

where
F=V_{4}\Lambda^{4}/(32\pi^{2})
.
Note that we have singular points at

\displaystyle\lambda(\mu)=0,\quad\pm\infty.

(99)

Now the
\lambda_{\text{B}}
integration in the micro-canonical partition function is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\lambda_{\text{B}}}{2\pi}\exp\left(i\lambda_{\text{B}}NA_{\lambda}-i\frac{F}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{B}}}+\frac{N}{8\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\right)\right)\right)

(100)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\lambda_{\text{B}}}{2\pi}e^{iNA_{\lambda}G(\lambda(\mu))},

(101)

where

\displaystyle G(\lambda(\mu)):=\frac{\lambda(\mu)}{1-\frac{N\lambda(\mu)}{8\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\right)}+\frac{F}{2NA_{\lambda}}\log\lambda(\mu)~.

(102)

One can check that there exist two saddle points:

\displaystyle N\lambda(\mu)=N\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{B}\left(1-\frac{A_{\lambda}}{FB}\pm\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{A_{\lambda}}{FB}\right)^{2}-1}\right),

(103)

where

\displaystyle B=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\right).

(104)

We can see that
N\lambda_{\pm}
is real and positive if we take the following large
N
limit.

\displaystyle N\rightarrow\infty~\quad N\lambda_{\text{R}}(\mu)={\rm fixed},\quad\frac{A_{\lambda}}{F}={\rm fixed},\quad\bigg|1-\frac{A_{\lambda}}{FB}\bigg|\geq 1~.

(105)

More detailed analysis is necessary to verify the validity of this limit because we now have additional parameter
A_{\lambda}
that is absent in the usual large
N
analysis.
