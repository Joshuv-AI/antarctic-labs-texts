Unifying Approaches in Active Learning and Active Sampling via Fisher Information and Information-Theoretic Quantities
Andreas Kirsch andreas.kirsch@cs.ox.ac.uk

Abstract
Recently proposed methods in data subset selection, that is active learning and active sampling, use Fisher information, Hessians, similarity matrices based on gradients, and gradient lengths to estimate how informative data is for a model’s training. Are these different approaches connected, and if so, how? We revisit the fundamentals of Bayesian optimal experiment design and show that these recently proposed methods can be understood as approximations to information-theoretic quantities: among them, the mutual information between predictions and model parameters, known as expected information gain or BALD in machine learning, and the mutual information between predictions of acquisition candidates and test samples, known as expected predictive information gain . We develop a comprehensive set of approximations using Fisher information and observed information and derive a unified framework that connects seemingly disparate literature. Although Bayesian methods are often seen as separate from non-Bayesian ones, the sometimes fuzzy notion of “informativeness” expressed in various non-Bayesian objectives leads to the same couple of information quantities, which were, in principle, already known by Lindley 1956 and MacKay 1992 .

Introduction

Label and training efficiency are key to a wider deployment of deep learning. Deep learning generally requires a lot of data, much of which must be annotated. This is expensive and time-consuming. Together with semisupervised and unsupervised approaches,
active learning

Cohn et al. 1994

helps increase label efficiency:
given access to unlabeled data, active learning selects the most informative samples to label for a given model, thus decreasing the number of required annotations to reach a given level of performance.
In addition to label efficiency, training deep learning models is also expensive and time-consuming, and
active sampling
improves training efficiency by filtering the training set to focus on the samples that will be the most informative for the model.

Several new approaches in data subset selection for deep learning have been proposed recently: amongst them, BADGE
(
Ash et al. 2019
)
, BAIT
(

)
, PRISM

By examining how Fisher information and second-order posterior approximations (Gaussian approximations) can be used for estimating information quantities,

Probabilistic Model.
We assume a supervised setting: for inputs

\Omega

\opp(\omega)

\displaystyle\opp({\{y_{i}\}},\omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}})=\opp({\{y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega)\,\opp(\omega)=\opp({y}_{1},\ldots,{y}_{n}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x}_{1},\ldots,{x}_{n},\omega)\,\opp(\omega).

\opp({y}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x})=\opExpectation_{#1}[\opp({y}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x},\omega)]

Transductive Objectives.
When an objective uses (additional) data
{\mathcal{D}^{\text{eval}}}
, unlabeled or labeled, to guide acquisitions, we refer to the objective as a
transductive
objective
(
Yu et al. 2006
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
.

Active Learning.
To increase label efficiency, instead of labeling data indiscriminately, active learning iteratively selects and acquires labels for the
most informative
unlabeled data from a
pool set

{\mathcal{D}^{\text{pool}}}
according to some
acquisition function
. An acquisition function scores the informativeness of an unlabeled candidate sample
{x^{\text{acq}}}
, and the sample that maximizes this score is selected for labeling. After each acquisition step, the model is retrained to take the newly labeled data into account. Labels can be acquired individually or in batches (
batch acquisition
, see below). The
expected information gain (EIG)

\displaystyle\opMI[\Omega;{Y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}]

(EIG/BALD)

and
(joint) expected predictive information gain (JEPIG and EPIG, respectively)

\displaystyle\opMI[{\{Y^{\text{eval}}_{i}\}};{Y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{eval}}_{i}\}},{x^{\text{acq}}}],

(JEPIG)

\displaystyle\opMI[{Y^{\text{eval}}};{Y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{X^{\text{eval}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}]

(EPIG)

are examples of such acquisition functions.
EPIG and JEPIG are transductive acquisition functions as they depend on
{X^{\text{eval}}},{\{x^{\text{eval}}_{i}\}},
respectively.

Active Sampling.
To increase training efficiency, instead of training with all samples, active sampling (sometimes also called
data pruning
)
(
Paul et al. 2021
)
selects the most informative sample
({x^{\text{acq}}},{y^{\text{acq}}})
from the training set to train on next. This can be done statically before training the model, in which case this is also referred to as core-set selection, or dynamically, in which case it is also referred to as curriculum learning. The
information gain (IG)

\displaystyle\opMI[\Omega;{y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}],

(IG)

and
(joint) predictive information gain (JPIG or PIG, respectively)

\displaystyle\opMI[{\{y^{\text{eval}}_{i}\}};{y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{eval}}_{i}\}},{x^{\text{acq}}}],

(JPIG)

\displaystyle\opMI[{Y^{\text{eval}}};{y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{X^{\text{eval}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}]\quad(=\opExpectation_{\hat{\opp}_{#1}({x^{\text{eval}}},{y^{\text{eval}}})}{\opMI[{y^{\text{eval}}};{y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{eval}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}]})

(PIG)

are examples of such acquisition functions.
PIG and JPIG are transductive acquisition functions.

Batch Acquisition.
In batch active learning, the acquisition function is applied to a batch of candidates
\{{x^{\text{acq}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
and the batch that maximizes the acquisition function is selected for labeling.
Similarly, in batch active sampling, the acquisition function is applied to a batch of training samples
\{({x^{\text{acq}}}_{i},{y^{\text{acq}}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{B}
and the batch that maximizes the acquisition function is selected for training.
For deep learning, batch acquisition is often the only feasible option in both active learning and active sampling. We extend the above acquisition functions to the batch case by substituting a set of samples in the definitions:
{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
for
{x^{\text{acq}}}
,
{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
for
{Y^{\text{acq}}}
, and
{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
for
{y^{\text{acq}}}
, respectively, and treating the sets as joint random variables

Kirsch et al. 2019
)
.

Submodular Acquisition Functions.
Choosing the subset
{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
naively is intractable due to the exponential number of possible acquisition batches. Instead of maximizing the acquisition function on all possible batches, we can often use submodularity
(

A set function

\displaystyle f(A\cup B)\leq f(A)+f(B)-f(A\cap B).

(submodular)

An acquisition batch

Although the EIG is (monotone) submodular, leading to efficient batch acquisition

Second-Order Posterior Approximation

Laplace approximations are a standard tool in Bayesian statistics and machine learning
(
Daxberger et al. 2021
;
Immer et al. 2020
)
. In this section, we review the Laplace approximation and introduce it as a special case of a more flexible second-order posterior approximation, a
Gaussian approximation
. It is central to approximating information quantities using observed information, defined in this section, and Fisher information, defined in §
4
.

Our goal is to approximate the posterior
\opp(\omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}})
using a (multivariate) Gaussian distribution, where
{\mathcal{D}}=\{({x}_{i},{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are additional (new) samples, and we start with
\opp(\omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}})
as the “prior” distribution—we will drop
{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}
and use
\opp(\omega)
when possible, to shorten the notation.

To begin, we complete the square of a second-order Taylor approximation around the log-parameter likelihood for a fixed
\omega^{*}
:

\displaystyle\log\opp(\omega)\approx\log\opp(\omega^{*})+\nabla_{\omega}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})](\omega-\omega^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\omega-\omega^{*})^{T}\nabla_{\omega}^{2}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})](\omega-\omega^{*})

(10)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}(\omega-(\omega^{*}-\nabla_{\omega}^{2}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})]^{-1}\nabla_{\omega}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})])^{T}\nabla_{\omega}^{2}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})](\omega-(\omega^{*}-\nabla_{\omega}^{2}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})]^{-1}\nabla_{\omega}[\log\opp(\omega^{*})]))

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\ldots.

(11)

Importantly, we can express this more concisely by extending the notation of
\opEntropy[\cdot]
to its derivatives:

Notation 3.1
.

We write
\opEntropy^{\prime}[\cdot]
for the Jacobian and
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\cdot]
for the Hessian of
\opEntropy[\cdot]
:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime}[\cdot]

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\nabla_{\omega}\log\opp(\cdot),

(12)

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\cdot]

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\nabla_{\omega}^{2}\log\opp(\cdot).

(13)

This notation will be helpful throughout this paper, as both observed information and Fisher information can be expressed in terms of the Hessian of the negative log-parameter likelihood.

Then, we can write:

\displaystyle\opEntropy[\omega]

\displaystyle\approx\opEntropy[\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}](\omega-\omega^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\omega-\omega^{*})^{T}\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]\,(\omega-\omega^{*})

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\omega-(\omega^{*}-\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}])^{T}\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]\,(\omega-(\omega^{*}-\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}]))+\ldots.

(15)

Comparing this to the information content of a multivariate Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle\opEntropy[\mathcal{N}(w;\,{\mu},\,{\Sigma})]=\frac{1}{2}(\omega-\mu)^{T}\,\Sigma^{-1}\,(\omega-\mu)+\ldots,

(16)

we obtain the Gaussian approximation, which we will apply throughout this paper:

Proposition 3.2
.

The
Gaussian approximation
of the distribution
\opp(\omega)
of
\Omega
around some
\omega^{*}
is given by:

\displaystyle\Omega\overset{\approx}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\omega^{*}-\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}]},\,{\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}}),

(17)

where
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]
must be positive-definite.
If
\omega^{*}
is also a (global) minimizer of
\opEntropy[\omega]
(that is,
\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}]=0
), we obtain the
Laplace approximation
:

\displaystyle\Omega\overset{\approx}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\omega^{*}},\,{\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}}).

(18)

Approximation Quality.
However, this approximation can be arbitrarily bad depending on
\opp(\omega)
and
\omega^{*}
. Given enough data, it is often argued that
\opp(\omega)
will concentrate around the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, giving rise to the Laplace approximation. In statistics, the Bernstein-von Mises theorem is often used to motivate this, but insufficient data to reach concentration of parameters and multimodality in over-parameterized models
(
Long 2021
)
can be an issue for deep active learning and active sampling.

Flat Minimum Intuition.
A positive definite Hessian implies that the information content (point-wise entropy) is convex around
\omega^{*}
and, equivalently, that the (log) posterior is concave around
\omega^{*}
. The latter provides an intuition for the Gaussian approximation:
the Hessian measures curvature, and the “flatter” the Hessian, e.g., the smaller the largest eigenvalue or the smaller the determinant, the less the loss changes when
\omega^{*}
is perturbed. This leads to the search for flat minima as a way to improve generalization
(
Hinton and Van Camp 1993
;
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1994
;
Smith and Le 2017
)
.

Notation 3.3
.

To further shorten the notation, we write
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]
instead of
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]
.

Posterior Approximation of
\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}
.
While the Laplace approximation is centered on a (global) minimizer, the Gaussian approximation can be used for a (potentially low-quality) posterior approximation in general.
We can expand
\opEntropy[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}]
using Bayes’ theorem and the additivity of the logarithm. That is, we have:

\displaystyle\opEntropy[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}]=\opEntropy[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy[\omega^{*}]-\opEntropy[{\mathcal{D}}],

(19)

and then, as
\opEntropy[{\mathcal{D}}]
is independent of
\omega
:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}]

\displaystyle=\opEntropy^{\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}]+0=\opEntropy^{\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime}[\omega^{*}],

(20)

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}]

\displaystyle=\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}].

(21)

Proposition 3.4
.

The
observed information

\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]
is additive:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{y}_{i}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x}_{i},\omega^{*}]=\sum_{i}-\nabla^{2}_{\omega}\log\opp({y}_{i}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x}_{i},\omega^{*}).

(22)

Note that the observed information has the opposite sign compared to other works because it simplifies the exposition.

Uninformative Prior.
For a Gaussian prior
\opp(\omega)\sim\mathcal{N}({\mu},\,{\Sigma})
, we have
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]=\Sigma^{-1}
and

\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}]=\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]+\Sigma^{-1}.

For an uninformative prior with “infinite prior variance”
\Sigma^{-1}\to 0
, we have
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]=0
, and
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}}]=\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The entropy of the second-order approximation of
\opp(\omega)
around
\omega^{*}
is

\displaystyle\opEntropy[\Omega]\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]+C_{k},

(23)

where
C_{k}=\tfrac{k}{2}\log 2\pi e
is a constant (independent of
{\mathcal{D}}
and
\omega^{*}
) and
k
is the number of dimensions of
\omega
.

While
Proposition

3.5
is straightforward, it is the main result for this section as it will allow us to approximate all the mentioned information quantities in §
5
and §
6
.

4
Fisher Information

Fisher information plays a central role in the approximations of information quantities because, unlike the observed information, it is always positive semidefinite.
We use Fisher information to unify various acquisition functions in §
7
.
The following section revisits Fisher information, its properties, special cases, and common approximations.
All proofs are given in §
A
.

In particular, we look at two special cases with more favorable properties: following
Kunstner et al. 2019
, when we can write our model as
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega)})
, where
{\hat{f}(x;\omega)}
are the logits, and
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})
is a distribution from the exponential family, Fisher information is independent of
y
, which has useful consequences as we shall see; and following
Chaudhuri et al. 2015
, when we have a
Generalized Linear Model (GLM)
, observed information also is independent of
y
.
The results for the GLM are often applied as an approximation known as
Generalized Gauss-Newton approximation (GGN)
.
Together with numerical approximations, such as a diagonal approximation or low-rank factorizations, observed information and Fisher information can then be efficiently approximated for large deep neural networks
(
Daxberger et al. 2021
)
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
Fisher information

\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{Y}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x},\omega^{*}]
is the expectation over observed information using the model’s own predictions
\opp({y}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x},\omega^{*})
for a given
{x}
at
\omega^{*}
:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{Y}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x},\omega^{*}]=\opExpectation_{#1}[\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{y}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x},\omega^{*}]].

(24)

This notation of the Fisher information is consistent with the notation for information quantities from §
2
, introduced in
Kirsch and Gal 2021
, but extended to the observed information: the Fisher is but an expectation over the observed information, and the observed information is the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood.

Proposition 4.2
.

Like observed information, Fisher information is additive:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{Y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]=\sum_{i}\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\{Y_{i}\}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x_{i},\omega^{*}].

(25)

There are two other equivalent definitions of Fisher information:

Proposition 4.3
.

Fisher information is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]

\displaystyle=\opExpectation_{#1}[\opEntropy^{\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]^{T}\,\opEntropy^{\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]]=\mathrm{Cov}[\opEntropy^{\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]].

(26)

Special Case: Exponential Family.

Kunstner et al. 2019
show in their appendix that if we split a discriminative model into prelogits
{\hat{f}(x;\omega)}
and a predictor
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega)})
, Fisher information does not depend on
y
when
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})
is a distribution from an exponential family (independent of
\omega
). This covers a normal distribution for regression parameterized by mean and variance predictions or a categorical distribution via the softmax function.
The following statements and proofs follow
Kunstner et al. 2019
:

Proposition 4.4
.

The Fisher information
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]
for a model
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})})
is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\opExpectation_{#1}[\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}\opEntropy[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}]]\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})},

(27)

where
\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}\opEntropy[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}]
is short for
\left.\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}\opEntropy[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}]\right|_{{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

The Fisher information
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]
of a model of the form
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})})
is independent of
y
, where
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})
is a distribution from an exponential family, i.e.,
\log\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})={\hat{z}}^{T}T(y)-A({\hat{z}})+\log h(y)
:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,{\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}A({\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})})}\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}.

(28)

It is crucial that the exponential distribution not depend on
\omega
.

This simplifies computing Fisher information: no expectation over
y
s is needed anymore. The full outer product may not be needed explicitly either.

As examples, we will consider two common parameteric distributions from the exponential family:

Gaussian Distribution.
When
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})=\mathcal{N}(y;\,{{\hat{z}}},\,{1})
, we have
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}]=1
for all
y,{\hat{z}}
, and thus

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}.

(29)

Categorical Distribution.
When
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})=\softmax({\hat{z}})_{y}
, we have
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}]=\diag(\pi)-\pi\,\pi^{T},
with
\pi_{y}=\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})
, and thus:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,(\diag(\pi)-\pi\,\pi^{T})\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}.

(30)

Special Case: Generalized Linear Models.

Chaudhuri et al. 2015
require that observed information is independent of
y
, which we will also use later. This holds for Generalized Linear Models:

Definition 4.6
.

A
generalized linear model (GLM)
is a model
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega)})
such that
\log\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}})={\hat{z}}^{T}T(y)-A({\hat{z}})+\log h(y)
is a distribution of the exponential family, independent of
\omega
, and
{\hat{f}(x;\omega)}=\omega^{T}\,x
is linear in the parameters
\omega
.

Proposition 4.7
.

The observed information
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]
of a GLM is independent of
y
.

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}\opEntropy[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\hat{z}}={\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}]\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}

(31)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}A(w^{T}x)\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}.

(32)

Proposition 4.8
.

For a model such that the observed information
\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]
is independent of
y
, we have:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]

(33)

for any
y^{*}
, and also trivially:

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\opExpectation_{#1}[\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]].

(34)

Note that the expectation is over
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x)
and not
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*})
, and
\opExpectation_{#1}[\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]]=\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{Y_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]
is additive then.

Proposition 4.9
.

For a GLM, when
{\hat{f}(x;\omega)}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
, where
K
is the number of classes (outputs),
D
is the number of input dimensions,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times K}
, and
assuming the parameters are flattened into a single vector for the Jacobian, we have
\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega)}=\mathrm{Id}_{K}\otimes x^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times(K\cdot D)}
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product, and:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}A(\omega^{T}x)\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}=\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}A(w^{T}x)\otimes x\,x^{T}.

(35)

This property is useful for computing the Fisher information of a GLM in practice
(
Ash et al. 2021
)
.

\boldsymbol{\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*})}
vs
\boldsymbol{\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x)}
.
Having a GLM solves an important issue we will encounter in §
5
: approximating the EIG requires taking an expectation over
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x)
and not
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*})
. One can approximate
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x)\approx\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*})
, which can be justified in the limit, but this is probably not a good approximation in the cases interesting for active learning and active sampling. With a GLM, this is not a problem.

Generalized Gauss-Newton Approximation.
In the case of an exponential family but not a GLM, the equality in
Proposition

4.7
is often used as an approximation for the observed information—we simply use the respective Fisher information as an approximation of observed information (via
Proposition

4.5
):

\displaystyle\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]\approx\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[Y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega^{*}]=\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}^{T}\,\nabla_{\hat{z}}^{2}A(w^{T}x)\,\nabla_{\omega}{\hat{f}(x;\omega^{*})}.

(36)

This is known as
Generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) approximation

(
Kunstner et al. 2019
;
Immer et al. 2020
)
. This approximation has the advantage that it is always positive semidefinite unlike the true Hessian.

Last-Layer Approaches.
GLMs are often used in deep active learning
(
Ash et al. 2019
;
Ash et al. 2021
;
Kothawade et al. 2022
;
Kothawade et al. 2021
)
. If we split the model into
\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x,\omega)=\opp(y\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{z}=\omega^{T}\,{f(x)})
, where
{z}={f(x)}
are the embeddings and treat the encoder
{f(x)}
as fixed, we obtain a GLM based on the weights of the last layer, which uses the embeddings as input.

Armed with this knowledge, we can now derive approximations for the information quantities of interest using observed information and Fisher information and consider their properties. The GGN approximation and last-layer approaches feature heavily in the literature to make computing these approximations more tractable as they reduce computational requirements and memory usage.

5.1
Approximate Expected Information Gain

The expected information gain is a popular acquisition function in Bayesian optimal experimental design
(
Lindley 1956
)
and in active learning, where it is also known as BALD
(
Houlsby et al. 2011
;
Gal et al. 2017
)
.

We can approximate the EIG
\opMI[\Omega;{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}]
of acquisition candidates
{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
using Gaussian approximations:

\displaystyle\opMI[\Omega;{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}]

\displaystyle=\opEntropy[\Omega]-\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}]

(37)

\displaystyle=\opEntropy[\Omega]-\opExpectation_{#1}[\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}]]

(38)

\displaystyle\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]-\opExpectation_{#1}[-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}]]

(39)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\opExpectation_{#1}[\log\det\left((\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}])\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}\right)]

(40)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\opExpectation_{#1}[\log\det\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}+Id\right)].

(41)

using
Proposition

3.5
twice, where the constant
C_{k}
cancels out in
eq.

39
as we subtract two entropy terms.

Generalized Linear Model.
When we have a GLM, we can use
Proposition

4.8
to obtain:

\displaystyle\opMI[\Omega;{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}]

\displaystyle\approx\ldots=\tfrac{1}{2}\opExpectation_{#1}[\log\det\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}+Id\right)]

(42)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}{\log\det\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}+Id\right)}.

(43)

We can upper-bound the log determinant and obtain:

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\tr\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\tr\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}_{i},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}]^{-1}\right).

(45)

where we have used the following inequality (proof in §
B.1
):

Lemma 5.1
.

For symmetric, positive semidefinite matrices
A
, we have (with equality iff
A=0
):

\displaystyle\log\det(A+Id)\leq\tr(A).

(46)

General Case & Exponential Family.
For the general case, we need to make a strong approximation:

\displaystyle\opp({\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}})\approx\opp({\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}),

(47)

which might hold for a mostly converged posterior but probably not in cases with little data.
This turns the approximation into an upper bound.
Alternatively, we could use the GGN approximation when we have an exponential family for the same result (but not an upper bound).
See §
B.1
for the derivation.

Proposition 5.2

(EIG)
.

The expected information gain can be approximately upper bounded via:

\displaystyle\opMI[\Omega;{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]

\displaystyle\overset{\approx}{\leq}\tfrac{1}{2}{\log\det\left(\sum_{i}\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{Y^{\text{acq}}}_{i}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}_{i},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]^{-1}+Id\right)}

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\tr\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{Y^{\text{acq}}}_{i}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}_{i},\omega^{*}]\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]^{-1}\right).

(49)

Furthermore, we have the following proxy objective:

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}}\opMI[\Omega;{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}}-\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]\text{ , and}

(50)

\displaystyle-\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]

\displaystyle\overset{\approx}{\leq}\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\left(\sum_{i}\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{Y^{\text{acq}}}_{i}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}_{i},\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]\right)-C_{k}.

(51)

The second statement follows from
Equation

37
, since
\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]
is constant and provides a proxy objective when we are only interested in optimizing the EIG. In §
7
, we connect it to the expected gradient length approach in active learning and show that an ablation in
Ash et al. 2021
examines the wrong objective.

Batch Acquisition Pathologies.
Importantly, this approximation of the EIG using the trace is additive, whereas the one using the log determinant is not. This means that the trace approximation ignores the dependencies between the samples and can only lead to naive top-k batch acquisition; see
Kirsch et al. 2019
;
Kirsch et al. 2021a
for details of the pathologies of top-k batch acquisition.

5.2
Approximate Information Gain

Following the same steps, we can also approximate the information gain, which is useful for active sampling:

Proposition 5.3

(IG)
.

The
information gain

\opMI[\Omega;{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]=\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]-\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]
can be approximately upper bounded via:

\displaystyle\opMI[\Omega;{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]

\displaystyle\approx\tfrac{1}{2}{\log\det\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]^{-1}+Id\right)}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\tr\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{y^{\text{acq}}}_{i}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{acq}}}_{i},\omega^{*}]\,\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]^{-1}\right).

(53)

Furthermore, we have the following proxy objective:

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}}\opMI[\Omega;{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}}-\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]\text{, and}

(54)

\displaystyle-\opEntropy[\Omega\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]

\displaystyle\approx\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\left(\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[{\{y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},\omega^{*}]+\opEntropy^{\prime\prime}[\omega^{*}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]\right)-C_{k}.

(55)

Comparison to EIG.
Importantly, when we have a GLM or use the GGN approximation, this approximation of the IG is equal to the one of the EIG. This tells us that active learning on a GLM with the EIG approximation will work as well as if we had access to the labels. Equivalently, active sampling via IG with the GGN approximation will not work better than the respective active learning approach.

5.3
Approximate (Joint) Expected Predictive Information Gain

In transductive active learning, we have access to an (empirical) distribution
\hat{\opp}_{#1}({x^{\text{eval}}})
, e.g., the pool set, and want to find the
{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
that maximize the
expected predictive information gain (EPIG)

(
Kirsch et al. 2021b
)
.
The approximations here will help us connect BAIT
(
Ash et al. 2021
)
to EPIG.
For simplicity, we consider the non-batch case here. The batch case can be handled analogously. The EPIG objective is defined as:

\displaystyle\argmax_{x^{\text{acq}}}\opMI[{Y^{\text{eval}}};{Y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{X^{\text{eval}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}]=\argmax_{x^{\text{acq}}}\opExpectation_{\hat{\opp}_{#1}({x^{\text{eval}}})}\opMI[{Y^{\text{eval}}};{Y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{x^{\text{eval}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}],

(56)

We expand the objective as follows:

\displaystyle\opMI[{Y^{\text{eval}}};{Y^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{X^{\text{eval}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}]

\displaystyle=\opMI[\Omega;{Y^{\text{eval}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{X^{\text{eval}}}]-\opMI[\Omega;{Y^{\text{eval}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{X^{\text{eval}}},{Y^{\text{acq}}},{x^{\text{acq}}}],

of the data matrix directly and samples a diverse batch based on the Euclidean distance between these Jacobians.
However, sampling from k-DPPs does not pick the most informative batch overall, and the ablations in
Ash et al. 2019
show that k-MEANS++ outperforms k-DPP.
Finally, the paper only motivates using gradient embeddings with hard pseudo-labels through intuitions: the gradient length captures information about the model’s uncertainty, and diverse update directions capture information about the model’s diversity
(
Ash et al. 2019
)
. The paper makes no explicit connection to information theory.

Following
Proposition

6.1
, since BADGE can be seen as using a last-layer approach for the similarity matrix
{S[{\mathcal{D}^{\text{acq}}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\omega^{*}]}
with hard pseudo-labels, BADGE approximates
\opMI[\Omega;{\{Y^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}},{\mathcal{D}^{\text{train}}}]
with an uninformative posterior distribution:

Proposition 7.2
.

BADGE maximizes an approximation of the EIG with an uninformative prior.

Comparison to BatchBALD.
Similarly, BatchBALD
(
Kirsch et al. 2019
)
approximates the EIG in the batch acquisition case but by using prediction-space samples. Moreover, BatchBALD uses a greedy approach to select batch candidates instead of sampling via a k-DPP or k-MEANS++.

As the EIG is submodular, determining the acquisition batch is a submodular optimization problem and, therefore, can be solved by greedy selection with
1-\tfrac{1}{e}
optimality
(
Nemhauser et al. 1978
)
.

Research Questions.
Hard pseudo-labels lead to biased estimates.
Would one-sample estimates perform better?

And could greedy batch selection work better than sampling via a k-DPP?
This would be closer to the batch acquisition strategy followed by BatchBALD.

8
Conclusion & Outlook

We have examined Fisher information and Gaussian approximations and have derived weight-space approximations for various information quantities. This has allowed us to connect these information quantities to objectives already used in the literature.
Moreover, we can make the following concluding points:

Last-Layer Approaches.
Methods that only use last-layer Fisher information or similar perform data subset selection on embeddings only, despite feature learning being arguably the most important strength of deep neural networks. However, these approaches can find great use with large pre-trained models, which are only fine-tuned on new data domains
(
Tran et al. 2022
)
.

Bias in Hard Pseudo-Labels.
Several methods (BADGE, PRISM, SIMILAR) use hard pseudo-labels for computing the similarity matrices, leading to biased estimates. Can one-sample estimates perform better? Can the equivalent approximations that do not make use of similarity matrices perform better?

Trace vs
\log\det
Approximations.
We have presented a hierarchy of approximations and bounds. Ablating these approximations along multiple dimensions, including whether to use the trace or
\log\det
approximation and whether to use a GLM, the GGN approximation or the full Fisher information, could provide interesting insights into what is attainable.

Batch Acquisition Pathologies.
Approaches that use the matrix trace instead of the log determinant can end up being additive for batch candidates
{\{x^{\text{acq}}_{i}\}}
and, therefore, by definition, cannot take redundancies between batch candidates into account, leading to failures detailed in
Kirsch et al. 2019
;
Kirsch et al. 2021a
. This is an issue for trace approximations of the (E)IG. Does the trace approximation of (JE)PIG handle batch acquisition pathologies better?
Similarly, most information quantities are not submodular, yet we use a greedy algorithm to select the acquisition batches.
Is the heuristic proposed by
Ash et al. 2021
generally beneficial?

Weight vs. Prediction Space.
BADGE, BAIT, and the LogDet objectives of PRISM and SIMILAR
(
Ash et al. 2019
;
Ash et al. 2021
;
Kothawade et al. 2021
;
Kothawade et al. 2022
)
approximate information quantities in weight space, while (Batch)BALD, (J)EPIG, and (J)PIG
(
Houlsby et al. 2011
;
Kirsch et al. 2019
;
Kirsch et al. 2021b
;
Mindermann et al. 2022
)
approximate the information quantities in prediction space.
Both approaches have their limitations:

Weight-space approaches can suffer from the Gaussian approximation being of low quality: the Laplace approximation only captures the posterior distribution well once it concentrates sufficiently.
However, this is unlikely to happen in a low-data regime.

Prediction-space approaches can suffer from a combinatorial explosion as the batch acquisition size increases because prediction configurations have to be enumerated or sampled to approximate the information quantities. In addition, many parameter samples might be needed to obtain low-variance estimates.

Importantly, prediction space approaches also require drawing samples from the posterior distribution but do not estimate the information quantities using the posterior distribution, unlike weight space approaches.

Informativeness Scores.
Taking a step back, we have seen that a Bayesian perspective using information quantities connects seemingly disparate literature. Although Bayesian methods are often seen as separate from (non-Bayesian) active learning and active sampling, the sometimes fuzzy notion of “informativeness” expressed through various different objectives in non-Bayesian settings collapses to the same couple of information quantities, which were, in principle, already known by
Lindley 1956
and
MacKay 1992
.
