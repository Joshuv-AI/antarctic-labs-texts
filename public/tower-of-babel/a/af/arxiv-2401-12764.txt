Fast Nonlinear Two-Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation: Achieving 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k ) {\cal O}(1/k) Finite-Sample Complexity
Thinh T. Doan

Abstract
This paper proposes to develop a new variant of the two-time-scale stochastic approximation to find the roots of two coupled nonlinear operators, assuming only noisy samples of these operators can be observed. Our key idea is to leverage the classic Ruppert–Polyak averaging technique to dynamically estimate the operators through their samples. The estimated values of these averaging steps will then be used in the two-time-scale stochastic approximation updates to find the desired solution. Our main theoretical result is to show that under the strongly monotone condition of the underlying nonlinear operators the mean-squared errors of the iterates generated by the proposed method converge to zero at an optimal rate 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k ) {\cal O}(1/k) , where k k is the number of iterations. Our result significantly improves the existing result of two-time-scale stochastic approximation, where the best known finite-time convergence rate is 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 / 3 ) {\cal O}(1/k^{2/3}) . We illustrate this result by applying the proposed method to develop new reinforcement learning algorithms with improved performance.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of finding the root of two coupled nonlinear operators. In particular, given two operators
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we seek to find
x^{\star}
and
y^{\star}
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &F(x^{\star},y^{\star})=0\\
&G(x^{\star},y^{\star})=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

We will be interested in the setting where
F,G
are unknown. Instead, we assume that there is a stochastic oracle that outputs noisy values of
F(x,y)
and
G(x,y)
for a given pair
(x,y)
, i.e., for any
x
and
y
we have access to
F(x,y)\,+\,\xi
and
G(x,y)\,+\,\psi
, where
\xi
and
\psi
are two random variables. For solving problem (
1.1
) in this stochastic setting, two-time-scale stochastic approximation (SA)
[
1
]
, a generalized variant of the classic SA
[
2
]
, is a natural choice. This method iteratively updates the variables
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
, the estimates of
x^{\star}
and
y^{\star}
, respectively, for all
k\geq 0
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x_{k+1}&=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\left(F(x_{k},y_{k})+\xi_{k}\right)\\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k}-\beta_{k}\left(G(x_{k},y_{k})+\psi_{k}\right),\end{aligned}

(1.2)

where
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
are arbitrarily initialized in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In (
1.2
),
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are two nonnegative step sizes chosen such that
\beta_{k}\ll\alpha_{k}
, i.e., the second iterate is updated using step sizes that are very small as compared to the ones used to update the first iterate. Thus, the update of
x_{k}
is referred to as the “fast-time scale” while the update of
y_{k}
is called the “slow-time scale”, explaining the name of two-time-scale SA. The time-scale difference here is loosely defined as the ratio between the two step sizes, i.e.,
\beta_{k}/\alpha_{k}
. In addition, the update of the
fast iterate
depends on the
slow iterate
and vice versa, that is, they are coupled to each other. To handle this coupling, the two step sizes have to be properly chosen to guarantee the convergence of the method. Indeed, an important problem in this area is to properly select the two step sizes so that the two iterates converge as fast as possible. This task, however, is challenging due to the coupling between the iterates and the impact of sampling noise on their updates.

At present, the asymptotic convergence of two-time-scale SA, i.e., whether
(x_{k},y_{k})
converges to
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
, is well-studied by using the method of ordinary differential equation (ODE). This result can be found in several textbooks, see for example
[
1
,
3
]
. The asymptotic convergence rates of this method have been also studied via the central limit theorem in both linear and nonlinear settings
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. In particular, these works show that two-time-scale SA asymptotically converges in distribution at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k)
. On the other hand, the finite-sample complexity (or finite-time convergence rate) of this method in the mean-squared regime is rather incomplete. In the linear setting, i.e., when
F,G
are linear on
x,y
, the mean-square error of the iterates generated by (
1.2
) converges to zero at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k)

[
7
,
8
]
. When
F,G
are nonlinear, only a suboptimal convergence rate,
{\cal O}(1/k^{2/3})
, is known
[
9
]
. Motivated by the work in
[
9
]
, the authors in
[
10
]
show that the two-time-scale SA in (
1.2
) converges at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k)
, however, this result requires a restrictive condition on the high order of the smoothness of the operators (see the discussion in Remark
4
for more detail). This leaves the question of whether the two-time-scale SA in (
1.2
) can find the solution
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
of (
1.1
) with an
{\cal O}(1/k)
finite-time sample complexity. The overarching goal of this paper is to (partially) answer this question, where our main contribution is:

Main Contributions.

This paper proposes to develop a new variant of the two-time-scale SA in (
1.2
), leveraging the classic Ruppert-Polyak averaging technique. The mean square errors of the iterates generated by the proposed methods are shown to converge to zero at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k)
, which significantly improves the best known result
{\cal O}(1/k^{2/3})
in the setting of strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous nonlinear operators. We will also leverage the proposed method to develop new reinforcement learning algorithms with improved performance.

Prior Works

Stochastic Approximation (SA).
The two-time-scale SA in (
1.2
) is a generalized variant of the classic SA introduced by

such that
F(x^{\star})=0
based on the noisy observations

. The estimate

of

is iteratively updated by moving along the direction of

scaled by some step size. Through a careful choice of this step size, the “noise” induced by the random samples

can be averaged out across iterations, and the algorithm converges to
x^{*}
. Both asymptotic convergence and finite-sample complexity of SA are very well-studied under general conditions of the operators and noise models; see for example
[

,
3
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,

,

. One can apply SA to solve problem (
1.1
), i.e., by considering

as a single operator. This approach requires that

that is not applicable to the existing results of SA (see the discussion in Remark 2 for more details).

is proposed to find the solution

1.2
Motivating Applications

In this section, we present a few existing applications of two-time-scale SA in (

), which serve as the main motivation of this paper. Our improved convergence rate in Theorem

below suggests that one can apply the development in this paper to develop new algorithms for solving problems in these applications.

1.2.1
Reinforcement Learning.

Two-time-scale SA has been used extensively to model reinforcement learning methods, for example, gradient temporal difference (TD) learning and actor-critic methods
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
. For example, the gradient TD learning for solving the policy evaluation problem under nonlinear function approximations studied in
[
31
]
can be viewed as a variant of (
1.2
). In this problem, we want to estimate the cumulative rewards
V
of a stationary policy using function approximations
V_{y}
, that is, our goal is to find
y
so that
V_{y}
is as close as possible to the true value
V
. Here,
V_{y}
can be represented by a neural network where
y
is the weight vector of the network. Let
\zeta
be the environmental sate,
\gamma
be the discount factor,
\phi(\zeta)=\nabla V_{y}(\zeta)
be the feature vector of state
\zeta
, and
r
be the reward return by the environment. Given a sequence of samples
\{\zeta_{k},r_{k}\}
, one version of GTD are given as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(\delta_{k}-\phi(\zeta_{k})^{T}x_{k})\phi(\zeta_{k})

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=y_{k}+\beta_{k}\Big[\left(\phi(\zeta_{k})-\gamma\phi(\zeta_{k+1})\right)\phi(\zeta_{k})^{T}x_{k}-h_{k}\Big],

where
\delta_{k}
and
h_{k}
are defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{k}+\gamma V_{y_{k}}(\zeta_{k+1})-V_{y_{k}}(\zeta_{k})

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=(\delta_{k}-\phi(\zeta_{k})^{T}x_{k})\nabla^{2}V_{y_{k}}(\zeta_{k})x_{k},

which is clearly a variant of (
1.2
) under some proper choice of
F
and
G
. It has been observed that the GTD method is more stable and performs better when compared to the single-time-scale counterpart (TD learning) under off-policy learning and nonlinear function approximations
[
13
]
.

Another reinforcement learning method, namely, the online actor-critic algorithm
[
32
]
, to find the underlying optimal policy can also be viewed as a variant of (
1.2
). In this algorithm, there is a critic used to estimate the state-action value function of the current policy. The estimate of the critic is then used by an actor to update the current policy toward the optimal policy. It is known that the update of the critic is implemented at a faster time scale (e.g., using a bigger step size) than the update of the actor (e.g., using a smaller step size), resulting a two-time-scale learning algorithm.

While the existing gradient TD learning and online actor-critic methods can be formulated as different variants of (
1.2
), the proposed method in this paper suggests that it will be better to use the Ruppert-Polyak averaging steps on the samples to reduce the impacts of noise before updating the main iterates in these two methods. In Section
4
we provide numerical experiments to illustrate this observation.

1.2.2
Game Theory.

Two-time-scale SA can also be used to represent different learning methods in game theory, in particular, in zero-sum settings. For example, consider a two-player Markov game characterized by
{\cal M}=({\cal S},{\cal A},{\cal B},{\cal P},\gamma,r)
. Here,
{\cal S}
is the finite state space,
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
are the finite action spaces of the two players,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor, and
r:{\cal S}\times{\cal A}\times{\cal B}\rightarrow[0,1]
is the reward function. Let
\Delta_{{\cal F}}
denote the probability simplex over a set
{\cal F}
, and
{\cal P}:{\cal S}\times{\cal A}\times{\cal B}\rightarrow\Delta_{{\cal S}}
be the transition probability kernel, with
{\cal P}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a,b)
specifying the probability of the game transitioning from state
s
to
s^{\prime}
when the first player selects action
a\in{\cal A}
and the second player selects
b\in{\cal B}
.
The policies of the two players are denoted by
\pi\in\Delta_{{\cal A}}^{{\cal S}}
and
\phi\in\Delta_{{\cal B}}^{{\cal S}}
, with
\pi(a\mid s)
,
\phi(b\mid s)
denoting the probability of selecting action
a
,
b
in state
s
according to
\pi
,
\phi
.
Given a policy pair
(\pi,\phi)
, we measure its performance in state
s\in{\cal S}
by the value function

\displaystyle V^{\pi,\phi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\{a_{k},b_{k},s_{k+1}\}}\Big[\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r\left(s_{k},a_{k},b_{k}\right)\mid s_{0}=s\Big].

Under a fixed initial distribution
\rho\in\Delta_{{\cal S}}
, we define by
J(\pi,\phi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}\sim\rho}[V^{\pi,\phi}(s_{0})]
the discounted cumulative reward under
(\pi,\phi)
. The goal is to find a Nash equilibrium
(\pi^{\star},\phi^{\star})
, which can be shown to be the solution of the following minimax optimization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{{\cal A}}^{{\cal S}}}\min_{\phi\in\Delta_{{\cal B}}^{{\cal S}}}J(\pi,\phi)=\min_{\phi\in\Delta_{{\cal B}}^{{\cal S}}}\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{{\cal A}}^{{\cal S}}}J(\pi,\phi)=J(\pi^{\star},\phi^{\star}).

When the transition probability kernel
{\cal P}
is known, one can apply dynamic programming approach to solve this problem. On the other hand, when
{\cal P}
is unknown one can use reinforcement learning methods to find the Nash equilibrium. For example, we can apply the decentralized Q-learning method recently studied in
[
38
]
, which is a variant of two-time-scale SA. This method maintains two variables, namely, local value functions and local q-function for each player, that are updated at different time scales by using different step sizes. In particular, let
v_{1}^{\pi}(s),q_{1}^{\pi}(s,a)
be the local value function and state-action value function at Player
1
, respectively. Similar notation is defined for Player
2
. Player
1
will update its local functions as (similar to Player
2
)

\displaystyle q_{i}^{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})

\displaystyle=q_{i}^{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left(r(s_{k},a_{k},b_{k})+\gamma v_{i}^{k}(s_{k}^{\prime})-q_{i}^{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})\right),

\displaystyle v_{i}^{k+1}(s_{k})

\displaystyle=v_{i}^{k}(s_{k})+\beta_{k}\big(\big[\bar{\pi}_{i}^{k}\big]^{T}q_{i}^{k+1}(s_{k},\cdot)-v_{i}^{k},(s_{k})\big),

where
\beta_{k}\leq\alpha_{k}
and
\bar{\pi}_{i}^{k}
is the best response policy w.r.t
q_{i}^{k+1}
. While asymptotic convergence of this method has been studied in
[
38
]
based on the ODE method, its finite-time complexity is completely unknown. Thus, an interesting question is to study whether one can apply the technique studied in this paper to derive the convergence rate of the decentralized Q-learning method. Another question is to see whether one can improve the existing convergence rates of policy gradient descent-ascent algorithm for zero-sum Markov games studied in
[
39
]
, where the authors show that this algorithm converges at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k^{1/3})
. Although this result is not comparable to the one studied in Theorem
1
, the objective function
J(\pi,\phi)
is nonconvex and nonconcave, therefore, one cannot immediately apply our analysis to the zero-sum Markov game setting.

2
Fast Two-Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation

The main difficulty in analyzing the convergence of the classic two-time-scale SA in (
1.2
) is to handle the coupling between the two iterates, i.e., the update of
x
depends on
y
and vice versa. A common approach to address this problem is to choose proper step sizes
\beta_{k}\ll\alpha_{k}
, e.g.,
\beta_{k}\sim 1/k\leq\alpha_{k}\sim 1/k^{2/3}
. Then one can show that the update of
x
is asymptotically independent on
y
and vice versa. However, under the impact of stochastic noise due to the sampling of operators, such a choice of step sizes yields a suboptimal finite-time convergence rate of two-time-scale SA, e.g.,
{\cal O}(1/k^{2/3})
. In this paper, we propose an alternative approach that will help to decouple the impact of noise on the update of the iterates. In particular, we consider the following variant of two-time-scale SA in
(
1.2
)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}f_{k+1}=(1-\lambda_{k})f_{k}+\lambda_{k}(F(x_{k},y_{k})+\xi_{k})\\
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}f_{k},\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}g_{k+1}=(1-\gamma_{k})g_{k}+\gamma_{k}(G(x_{k},y_{k})+\psi_{k})\\
y_{k+1}=y_{k}-\beta_{k}g_{k}.\end{array}\right.

Here,
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
are used to estimate the time-weighted average of the operators by using the two nonnegative step sizes
\gamma_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
. These estimates are then used to update the iterates
x,y
. When
\lambda_{k}=\gamma_{k}=1
, the updates in (
2
) and (
2
) reduce to the ones in (
1.2
). On the other hand, when
\lambda_{k},\gamma_{k}<1
one can view the updates of
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
as a variant of the classic Ruppert-Polyak averaging technique
[
66
,
67
]
. Ruppert-Polyak averaging technique is often applied to estimate the time-weighted average values of the iterates in optimization and SA literature. The average variable often achieves a better performance than the one without using averaging; see for example
[
14
,
8
,
68
]
and the references therein. In the proposed method in (
2
) and (
2
), we utilize the Ruppert-Polyak averaging technique on the operators, which will help to decompose the coupling between sampling noise and the iterate updates. Indeed, we will show that by choosing
\lambda_{k},\gamma_{k}<1
, one can separate the impact of the noise on the updates of the iterates, therefore, achieving an optimal rate
{\cal O}(1/k)
under a proper choice of step sizes
\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}
.

Remark 1
.

In our approach, we use the Ruppert-Polyak averaging steps on the samples of the operators, which is different to the existing works where these steps are often applied to the main iterates. The benefit of using this averaging technique to the iterates has been observed in the literature. On the other hand, we are not aware of any prior work that uses this technique to improve the estimate of the operators in parallel with the iterate updates. An interesting question is to study the performance of these two types of averaging steps when they are implemented in parallel.

2.1
Notation and Main Assumptions

In this section, we present the main assumptions which we will be used to study the finite-time convergence rate of (
2
) and (
2
). These assumptions are fairly standard and have been used in prior works under different variants
[
4
,
22
,
DoanR2019
,
23
,
24
,
7
,
5
]
. Our first assumption is on the smoothness of the operators.

Assumption 1
.

Given
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists an operator
H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x=H(y)
satisfies

\displaystyle F(H(y),y)=0.

In addition, we assume that the operators
F,G,H
are Lipschitz continuous with a positive constant
L
, i.e., the following conditions satisfy for all
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hskip-5.69046pt\|H(y_{1})-H(y_{2})\|&\leq L\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,\\
\hskip-5.69046pt\|F(x_{1},y_{1})-F(x_{2},y_{2})\|&\leq L(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|),\\
\hskip-5.69046pt\|G(x_{1},y_{1})-G(x_{2},y_{2})\|&\leq L\left(\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|+\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|\right).\end{aligned}

(2.8)

Second, we will study the setting when
F,G
are strongly monotone, which implies that
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
is unique.

Assumption 2
.

Given
y
,
F
is strongly monotone w.r.t
x
, i.e., there exists a constant
\mu_{F}>0
such that for all
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\left\langle x-z,F(x,y)-F(z,y)\right\rangle\geq\mu_{F}\|x-z\|^{2}.

(2.9)

Moreover,
G
is
1
-point strongly monotone w.r.t
y^{\star}
, i.e., there exists a constant
\mu_{G}\in(0,\mu_{F}]
such that for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\left\langle y-y^{\star},G(H(y),y)\right\rangle\geq\mu_{G}\|y-y^{\star}\|^{2}.

(2.10)

Remark 2
.

First, Assumption
1
is often referred to as the global Lipschitz continuity. Our analysis in this paper can be easily extended to the local Lipschitz condition as studied in
[
6
]
(with extra constant terms in our theoretical bounds), i.e.,
H
(similarly,
F,G
) satisfies

\displaystyle\|H(y)\|\leq L(1+\|y\|)\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Second, it is also worth to point out that in general Assumption
2
does not imply that the operator
W=[F;G]
is strongly monotone. Indeed, if
W
is strongly monotone, one can apply the single-time-scale SA to solve (
1.1
), e.g., by considering SA to update

. This statement can be verified in the linear setting, where

and

are linear operators

\displaystyle F(x,y)

\displaystyle={\bf A}_{11}x+{\bf A}_{12}y-b_{1}

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle={\bf A}_{12}x+{\bf A}_{22}y-b_{2}.

In this case, Assumption
2
implies that
{\bf A}_{11}
and
\Delta={\bf A}_{22}-{\bf A}_{21}{\bf A}_{11}^{-1}{\bf A}_{12}
are negative definite (but not necessarily symmetric), and

. It is straightforward to see that

is unique and satisfies

\displaystyle x^{*}

\displaystyle={\bf A}_{11}^{-1}(b_{1}-{\bf A}_{12}y^{*}),

\displaystyle y^{*}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-1}\left(b_{2}-{\bf A}_{21}{\bf A}_{11}^{-1}b_{1}\right).

However, the matrix

defined below is not negative definite

\displaystyle{\bf A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf A}_{11}&{\bf A}_{12}\\
{\bf A}_{12}&{\bf A}_{22}\end{array}\right].

To see this, we consider the following example

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{11}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-3.5&-8.5\\
1&0\end{array}\right],\quad{\bf A}_{12}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}2&0\\
0&2\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{21}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}5&0\\
0&5\end{array}\right],\quad{\bf A}_{22}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-10&-1.6\\

Then the eigenvalues of

,

are

\displaystyle\text{eig}({\bf A}_{11})

\displaystyle=-1.75\pm 2.33i,\quad\text{eig}(\Delta)=-3.44\pm 14.24i

\displaystyle\text{eig}({\bf A})

\displaystyle=\{-8.53\pm 4.51i,\;1.28\pm 4.24i\},

which shows that
{\bf A}_{11}
and

are negative but

is not.

Finally, we consider i.i.d noise models, that is,
\xi_{k}
and
\psi_{k}
are Martingale difference. We denote by

the filtration containing all the history generated by (

) up to time

{\cal Q}_{k}=\{x_{0},y_{0},\xi_{0},\psi_{0},\xi_{1},\psi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k},\psi_{k}\}.

Assumption 3
.

The random variables
\xi_{k}
and
\psi_{k}
, for all

, are independent of each other and across time, with zero mean and common variances given as follows

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{T}\xi_{k}\,|\,{\cal Q}_{k-1}]=\Gamma_{11},\quad\mathbb{E}[\psi_{k}^{T}\psi_{k}\,|\,{\cal Q}_{k-1}]=\Gamma_{22}.

Remark 3
.

We note that our analysis in this paper can be extended to cover the Markov setting where
\xi_{k}
and
\psi_{k}
are generated by Markov processes. In this case,
\xi_{k}
and
\psi_{k}
are dependent and the samples are biased. In this setting, if the Markov processes are ergodic, i.e., they have geometric mixing time, one can apply the technique proposed in
[

to handle the dependent and biased samples. This will result in an extra factor

in our theoretic bounds, where

is the number of iterations.

3
Main Results

In this section, we present in details the main results of this paper, that is, we provide a finite-time analysis for the convergence rates of the two-time-scale stochastic approximation in (
2
) and (
2
). Under assumptions presented in Section
2.1
, we show that the mean square errors of these iterates,
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\|y_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}]
, converge to zero at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k)
. To show this result, we introduce the following four residual variables,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta f_{k}=f_{k}-F(x_{k},y_{k})\quad\text{and}\quad{\hat{x}}_{k}&=x_{k}-H(y_{k}),\\
\Delta g_{k}=g_{k}-G(x_{k},y_{k})\quad\text{and}\quad{\hat{y}}_{k}&=y_{k}-y^{\star}.\end{aligned}

(3.11)

One can rewrite the updates of
x,y
in (
2
) and (
2
) as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}F(x_{k},y_{k})-\alpha_{k}\Delta f_{k}

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\beta_{k}G(x_{k},y_{k})-\beta_{k}\Delta g_{k},

If
(\Delta f_{k},\Delta g_{k})
goes to zero then the impact of sampling noise on
x,y
will be negligible. In addition, if
({\hat{x}}_{k},{\hat{y}}_{k})
go to zero then
(x_{k},y_{k})\rightarrow(x^{\star},y^{\star})

(x_{k},y_{k})
to
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
one can instead study the convergence of
(\Delta f_{k},\Delta g_{k})
and
({\hat{x}}_{k},{\hat{y}}_{k})
to zero. Indeed, our main result presented in Theorem
1
below is to study an upper bound for the convergence rate of these quantities. We will do this by considering the following choice of step sizes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lambda_{k}&=\frac{C_{\lambda}}{(k+h+1)},\quad\gamma_{k}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{(k+h+1)}\\
\alpha_{k}&=\frac{C_{\alpha}}{k+h+1},\quad\beta_{k}=\frac{C_{\beta}}{k+h+1},\end{aligned}

where

. To the end of this paper, we will assume that Eq. (

4.2
Online Actor-Critic Methods for LQR.

We next apply the so-called online actor-critic method, an online variant of policy gradient methods to solve the following infinite-horizon average-cost LQR problem

\displaystyle\underset{\{u_{k}\}}{\text{minimize}}\quad\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{T}\left(x_{k}^{\top}Qx_{k}+u_{k}^{\top}Ru_{k}\right)\mid x_{0}\Big]

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}+w_{k},

where
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are the state and the control variables,
w_{k}\sim N(0,\Psi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
is time-invariant system noise,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
are the system transition matrices, and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}},R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{2}}
are positive-definite cost matrices.
It is well-known (see, for example,
[
70
, Chap. 3.1]
) that the optimal control sequence
\{u_{k}\}
that solves the LQR problem is a time-invariant linear function of the state

\displaystyle u_{k}^{\star}=-K^{\star}x_{k},

where
K^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
is a matrix that depends on the problem parameters
A,B,Q,R
. This fact will allow us to reformulate the LQR as an optimization program over the feedback gain matrix
K
, i.e., we aim to search over a set of stochastic linear state feedback controller

\displaystyle u_{k}=-Kx_{k}+\sigma\epsilon_{k},\quad\epsilon_{k}\sim\text{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}),

with
\sigma\geq 0
. In particular, by defining
\Psi_{\sigma}=\Psi+\sigma^{2}BB^{\top}
, we can re-express the LQR problem as

\displaystyle\underset{K}{\text{minimize }}

\displaystyle J(K)\triangleq\operatorname{trace}(P_{K}\Psi_{\sigma})+\sigma^{2}\operatorname{trace}(R)

s.t.

\displaystyle P_{K}=Q+K^{\top}RK+(A-BK)^{\top}P_{K}(A-BK).

We will apply the online actor-critic method studied in
[
27
]
to solve the optimization problem above, a variant of (
1.2
). Moreover, in the context of LQR, the underlying operators of actor-critic methods satisfy the assumptions in
3.1
. In
[
27
]
, the authors show that the online actor-critic method converges at a rate
{\cal O}(1/k^{2/3})
. On the other hand, by using the proposed development in this paper, online actor-critic method converges at a better rate
{\cal O}(1/k)

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, by leveraging the Ruppert-Polyak averaging techniques we propose to develop a new variant of the classic two-time-scale SA that achieves a finite-time convergence rate
{\cal O}(1/k)
in mean-squared regime. Our result significantly improves the existing convergence rate of two-time-scale SA, which is
{\cal O}(1/k^{2/3})
. The key idea of our approach is to apply the averaging technique to estimate the operators from their samples before updating the main iterates. This can help to reduce the impact of the sampling noise on the updates of the iterates, therefore, improving its convergence towards the desired solutions. An interesting future direction of this paper is to apply the proposed method to improve the performance of existing control, optimization, and learning algorithms that can be represented as variants of two-time-scale SA.
