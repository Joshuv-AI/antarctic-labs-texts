Moduli spaces on the Kuznetsov component of Fano threefolds of index 2
Matteo Altavilla, Marin Petković, Franco Rota

Abstract




Abstract.
General hyperplane sections of a Fano threefold
Y
of index 2 and Picard rank 1 are del Pezzo surfaces, and their Picard group is related to a root system. To the corresponding roots, we associate objects in the Kuznetsov component of
Y
and investigate their moduli spaces, using the stability condition constructed by Bayer, Lahoz, Macrì, and Stellari, and the Abel–Jacobi map.
We identify a subvariety of the moduli space isomorphic to
Y
itself, and as an application we prove a (refined) categorical Torelli theorem for general quartic double solids.

Keywords.
Derived Categories; Bridgeland stability conditions; Fano threefolds; moduli spaces; Abel–Jacobi map

1. Introduction

Stability conditions on triangulated categories were introduced by Bridgeland in
[
Bri07
]
.
A remarkable feature of Bridgeland’s construction is that the set
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
of all stability conditions admits a natural topology, and is endowed with the structure of a complex manifold
[
Bri07
, Theorem 1.2]
. Even when
\mathcal{D}=D^{b}(\Coh(X))
is the bounded derived category of a smooth projective variety
X
, it is challenging to study
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
, and questions about its non-emptiness, connectedness, or simple-connectedness are difficult problems.

On the other hand, stability conditions allow for the construction of moduli spaces of stable objects of
\mathcal{D}
. It is interesting to investigate properties of these moduli spaces, like non-emptiness, irreducibility, smoothness, or projectivity, or to relate them to other classical moduli spaces: for example, Bridgeland stability regulates the birational geometry of the Hilbert scheme of points of surfaces (see
[
ABCH13
]
for the case of
\mathbb P^{2}
, and
[
BC13
]
for other surfaces), or of moduli spaces of sheaves on a K3 surface
[
BM14a
]
.

If
\mathcal{D}
admits an exceptional collection, Bayer, Lahoz, Macrì, and Stellari give a sufficient condition to induce a stability condition on the right orthogonal of the collection, starting from a (weak) stability condition on
\mathcal{D}

[
BLMS17
]
. The criterion applies for example to cubic fourfolds and Fano threefolds of Picard rank one
[
BLMS17
]
and to Gushel-Mukai varieties
[
PPZ19
]
.

In this paper, we consider smooth Fano threefolds
Y
of Picard rank 1 and index 2: they belong to one of 5 families indexed by their degree
d\in\{1,\ldots,5\}
.
The derived category of
Y
admits a semi-orthogonal decomposition
D(Y)=\langle\mathsf{Ku}(Y),\mathcal{O}_{Y},\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1)\rangle
whose non-trivial part
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
is called the
Kuznetsov component of
Y
. Its
numerical Grothendieck group
is a rank 2 lattice spanned by two classes
v
and
w
(see Section
3
for the details).

The focus of this work is on moduli spaces of complexes of class
w
, with particular attention to the degree 2 and 1 cases.
A more detailed study of moduli of class
w
for cubic threefolds appears in
[
BBF+20
]
; moduli of objects of class
v
are studied in
[
PY20
]
(for degrees
d\geq 2
) and in
[
PR20
]
for the case of degree 1.

Summary of the results

The main result of our work is a description of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
, which parametrizes complexes in
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
of class
w
, semistable with respect to one of the stability conditions
\sigma
constructed in
[
BLMS17
]
. Throughout the paper, we work under some mild generality assumptions for
Y
, to control the singularities that may appear in the hyperplane sections of
Y
(see Section
3.1
) and to rely on previous results of
[
Wel81
,
Tik82
]
.

First, we study the moduli spaces of Gieseker-stable sheaves on
Y
of class
w
. By a
root
of
Y
we mean a sheaf of the form
\iota_{*}(\mathcal{O}_{S}(D))
, with
S\in\lvert\mathcal{O}_{Y}(1)\rvert
and
D\in\Pic(S)
satisfying
D^{2}=-2
and
D\cdot H_{|S}=0
(see Definition
3.1
). We have:

Proposition 1.1

(
cf.
Proposition
4.1
)
.

Let
Y
be a general Fano threefold of Picard rank 1 and index 2. The moduli
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)
of Gieseker-semistable objects of class
w
on
Y
has two irreducible components.

One component, denoted
\mathcal{P}
, has dimension
d+3
and parametrizes ideal sheaves
I_{p|S}
of a point in a hyperplane section
S
of
Y
. A point
[I_{p|S}]\in\mathcal{P}
is smooth in
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)
iff
p
is a smooth point of
S
.
The second component has dimension
d+1
and its smooth points parametrize roots of
Y
.

Both components are smooth outside their intersection, which is the locus parametrizing
I_{p|S}
, with
S
singular at
p
.

We relate
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)
and
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
via wall-crossing: there is a two-parameter family
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
of weak stability conditions on
D^{b}(Y)
. We show that the moduli space of
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable complexes of class
w
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)
(resp. to
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
) for
\beta=-\frac{1}{2}
and
\alpha\gg 0
(resp.
0<\alpha\ll 1
) in Proposition
4.7
and Proposition
4.12
. Then, we deform the parameter
\alpha
: there is a unique wall separating
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)
and
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
and the universal family of
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)
induces a wall-crossing morphism, defined at the level of sets by replacing objects
I_{p|S}
with complexes defined by triangles

(1)

\mathcal{O}(-1)[1]\to E_{p}\to I_{p}

(Proposition
4.11
). In this way we obtain a general description for all
d
:

Theorem 1.2

(
cf.
Theorems
4.18
,
5.2
, and
5.5
)
.

Let
Y
be a general Fano threefold of Picard rank 1 and index 2. Then, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
is proper. It contains two smooth subvarieties
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{C}
.
The locus
\mathcal{Y}
is isomorphic to
Y
and it parametrizes objects
E_{p}
of the form (
1
), while the locus
\mathcal{C}
has dimension
d+1
and parametrizes roots of
Y
.

For
d\geq 3
,
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
is projective and irreducible and
\mathcal{Y}
is contained in the closure of
\mathcal{C}
;
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{C}
are distinct irreducible components for degree
d\leq 2
.

For
d\leq 2
, we consider the intermediate Jacobian
J(Y)
of
Y
and study the Abel–Jacobi map

\Psi\colon\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)\to J(Y)

sending a complex to its second Chern class (see Section
4.4
for the details).

In degree 1, we show that
\mathcal{C}\simeq F(Y)
, the Fano surface of lines of
Y
, and the intersection
\mathcal{Y}\cap F(Y)
in
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
is a curve
C
. The map
\Psi
is an embedding outside
C
, and its image suffices to determine
Y

[
Tik82
]
.

In degree 2,
Y
is a double cover of
\mathbb P^{3}
ramified over a quartic K3 surface
R
. The intersection
\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{C}
is isomorphic to
R
(see Theorem
5.2
),
\Psi
contracts the component
\mathcal{Y}
to a point, and it is a generic embedding on the component
\mathcal{C}
(Corollary
5.4
).
Still in the case of degree 2, we apply our result to show a (refined) categorical Torelli theorem:

Theorem 1.3

(
cf.
Theorem
6.1
)
.

Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
be two general quartic double solids. There exists an equivalence
u\colon\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})\simeq\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
if and only if
Y^{\prime}\simeq Y
.

Our proof technique is inspired by that of
[
BMMS12
]
; we use the equivalence
u
to construct an isomorphism between moduli spaces and argue that this is sufficient to conclude.

Related work and further questions

In the case of degree 5, Theorem
1.2
recovers the description of
Y_{5}
within the Hilbert scheme of three points on
\mathbb P^{2}

[
Muk92
]
. If the degree is 4,
Y
is the intersection of 2 quadrics: its Kuznetsov component is equivalent to the derived category of a genus 2 curve
C

[
BO95
]
and Theorem
1.2
recovers results of Reid’s
[
Rei72
]
, who shows that the Fano surface of lines on
Y
, the intermediate Jacobian of
Y
(introduced in
[
CG72
]
), and the Jacobian variety of
C
are isomorphic.

The main categorical techniques we use are developed in
[
BLMS17
,
Kuz11
]
.

The general expectation is that the Kuznetsov component contains sufficient information to recover
Y
.
Reconstruction theorems are known to hold in a few cases: for example,
\mathsf{Ku}(X)
determines
X
if
X
is a cubic fourfold (see
[
BLMS17
]
for general
X
, and
[
LPZ18
]
for all
X
) or if
X
is an Enriques surface
[
LNSZ19
,
LSZ21
]
.

In the case of Fano threefolds, four of the five families considered satisfy a refined Torelli theorem:
Y_{5}
is rigid in moduli, so the statement is vacuously true. Degrees 4 and 3 are showed in
[
BO95
]
and
[
BMMS12
]
(
[
PY20
]
gives an alternative proof of the cubic case). For degree 2, our result (Theorem
1.3
) strengthens the statement of Bernardara–Tabuada
[
BT16
, Corollary 3.1(iii)]
, who show that the same holds under the additional assumption that the equivalence is of Fourier–Mukai type.

There are some natural questions which remain unanswered by this work.

(i)

(
Torelli theorem for
d=1
) It is expected that an analogue of Theorem
1.3
holds in degree 1 as well. Unfortunately, the heart of the stability condition
\sigma
has homological dimension 3 (instead of 2) in degree 1
[
PR20
, Remark 2.6]
, so a different technique will be needed.

(ii)

(
Equivalences of Fourier–Mukai type
) A conjecture of Kuznetsov
[
Kuz07
, Conjecture 3.7]
implies, in the case of quartic double solids, that every equivalence
u\colon\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
is of Fourier–Mukai type (
i.e.
that the composition
D(Y^{\prime})\to\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})\xrightarrow{u}\mathsf{Ku}(Y)\to D(Y)
is a Fourier–Mukai functor). Combining Theorem
1.3
with
[
BT16
, Proposition 3.5]
shows that
\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})
and
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
are equivalent if and only if they are Fourier–Mukai equivalent. However, this is not sufficient to prove the statement of
[
Kuz07
, Conjecture 3.7]
for degree 2.
Interestingly, this conjecture is incompatible with Kuznetsov’s conjecture on Fano threefolds (see
[
Kuz09
, Conjecture 3.7]
for the conjecture, and
[
BT16
, Theorem 4.2]
for a proof of incompatibility). The conjecture on Fano threefolds has been recently disproven in
[
Zha20a
]
.

Structure of the paper

Section
2
recollects preliminary categorical notions, such as (weak) stability conditions (
2.1
,
2.2
), and base change of semi-orthogonal decompositions (
2.3
).
Section
3
is a brief description of smooth Fano threefolds of Picard rank 1 and index 2 and of the geometry of their hyperplane sections (
3.1
).

In Section
4
we describe the moduli spaces of Gieseker-stable sheaves of class
w
(Proposition
4.1
), of
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
-stable complexes in
D^{b}(Y)
(Proposition
4.11
) and of
\sigma
-stable complexes in
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
(Theorem
4.18
). We conclude with the definition, and some properties, of the Abel–Jacobi map
\Psi
(Section
4.4
).

Section
5
illustrates Theorem
4.18
on a case by case basis, comparing it with results known in the literature, and providing more details to the description of
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
in the remaining cases of degree
d=2,1
.

Finally, Section
6
contains the proof of the Torelli theorem for quartic double solids (Theorem
6.1
).

2. Preliminaries

2.1. Weak stability conditions

In this section we refer to
[
BLMS17
]
and briefly summarize definitions and results on (weak) stability conditions.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a triangulated category and
K(\mathcal{D})
its Grothendieck group; fix a finite rank lattice
\Lambda
and a surjective homomorphism
v\colon K(\mathcal{D})\twoheadrightarrow\Lambda
.

Definition 2.1
.

A full abelian subcategory
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{D}
is called a
heart of a bounded t-structure
if the following holds:

(a)

For all
E,\,F\in\mathcal{A}
and
n<0
we have
\Hom(E,F[n])=0
;

(b)

For every
E\in\mathcal{D}
there exist a filtration,
i.e.
objects
E_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
, integers
k_{1}>\dots>k_{m}
and triangles

\textstyle{0=E_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\dots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E_{m-1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{E_{m}=E\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A_{1}[k_{1}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A_{2}[k_{2}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A_{m-1}[k_{m-1}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A_{m}[k_{m}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

such that
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
; the
A_{i}
objects are called the
cohomologies
of
E
and are denoted by
\mathcal{H}^{-k_{i}}_{\mathcal{A}}(E)
.

A
weak stability function
on
\mathcal{A}
is a group homomorphism
Z\colon K(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{C}
such that for all
E\in\mathcal{A}
we have
\Im Z(E)\geq 0
and
\Im Z(E)=0
implies
\Re Z(E)\leq 0
. A weak stability function
Z
is a
stability function
if moreover
\Im Z(E)=0
implies
\Re Z(E)<0
for all
E\neq 0
.

A weak stability function has an associated slope:

\mu_{Z}(E)=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\Re Z(E)}{\Im Z(E)}\quad\text{if }\ \Im Z(E)>0\\
+\infty\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a triangulated category, and let
\Lambda
and
v
be as above; a
weak stability condition
on
\mathcal{D}
is a pair
\sigma=(\mathcal{A},Z)
where
\mathcal{A}
is the heart of a bounded t-structure and
Z
is a group homomorphism
Z\colon\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
, satisfying the following properties:

(a)

The composition
Z\circ v\colon K(\mathcal{A})=K(\mathcal{D})\to\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
is a weak stability function on
\mathcal{A}
;

(b)

All
E\in\mathcal{A}
have a Harder–Narasimhan filtration with factors
F_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
semistable with respect to
Z
, with strictly decreasing slopes;

(c)

There exists a quadratic form
Q
on
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{R}
that is negative definite on
\ker Z
, such that
Q(v(E))\geq 0
for
E
semistable.

If
Z
is a stability function, then the pair
(\mathcal{A},Z)
is a
Bridgeland stability condition
.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that an object
E\in\mathcal{A}
is
(semi)stable
with respect to
\sigma
if for every quotient
E\twoheadrightarrow Q
in
\mathcal{A}
with
0\neq Q\neq E
we have
\mu_{Z}(E)\leq\mu_{Z}(Q)
(resp.
\mu_{Z}(E)<\mu_{Z}(Q)
).

An object
E\in\mathcal{D}
is called (semi)stable if there exists
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
E[m]\in\mathcal{A}
and
E[m]
is (semi)stable, in which case
-m+\frac{1}{\pi}\arg Z(E)
is the
phase
of
E
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a weak stability condition
\sigma
and a real number
\mu
, the extension closure defined by
\mathcal{A}^{\mu,\sigma}\coloneqq\langle\mathcal{T}^{\mu,\sigma},\mathcal{F}^{\mu,\sigma}[1]\rangle
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\mu,\sigma}=\{E\in\mathcal{A}\ |\text{ All HN factors $F$ of $E$ have slope $\mu_{Z}(F)>\mu$}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mu,\sigma}=\{E\in\mathcal{A}\ |\text{ All HN factors $F$ of $E$ have slope $\mu_{Z}(F)\leq\mu$}\}

is the heart of a bounded t-structure called a
tilt
of
\mathcal{A}

[
HRS96
,
BLMS17
]
.

2.2. Weak stability conditions on
D^{b}(X)

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
, and let
H
be an ample divisor on
X
. We follow the setup of
[
BLMS17
, Section 2]
and recall the construction of weak stability conditions on
D^{b}(X)
.

For
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
define lattices
\Lambda_{H}^{j}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{j+1}
generated by

\left(H^{n}\Ch_{0},H^{n-1}\Ch_{1},\ldots,H^{n-j}\Ch_{j}\right)\subset\mathbb{Q}^{j+1}

with the sujective map
v_{H}^{j}\colon K(X)\to\Lambda_{H}^{j}
given by the Chern character.
Then, Mumford slope gives rise to a weak stability condition
\sigma_{H}\coloneqq(\Coh(X),Z_{\mu})
where
Z_{H}\colon\Lambda_{H}^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
is defined by

Z_{H}(E)\coloneqq-H^{n-1}\Ch_{1}(E)+iH^{n}\Ch_{0}(E).

We can define a new weak stability condition from the one above via tilting: for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\Coh^{\beta}(X)
to be the heart obtained from
\sigma_{H}
via tilting at slope
\mu_{H}=\beta
. Before introducing the central charge, recall the notation
\Ch^{\beta}(E)\coloneqq\Ch(E)\cdot e^{-\beta H}
. For convenience, we make the first three terms explicit:

\begin{split}\Ch_{0}^{\beta}=\Ch_{0},\qquad\Ch_{1}^{\beta}=\Ch_{1}-\beta H\Ch_{0},\qquad\Ch_{2}^{\beta}=\Ch_{2}-\beta H\Ch_{1}+\frac{\beta^{2}H^{2}}{2}\Ch_{0}.\end{split}

We have the following:

Proposition 2.5

(
[
BLMS17
, Proposition 2.12]
)
.

For all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}
, the pair given by
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}=(\Coh^{\beta}(X),Z_{\alpha,\beta})
with

Z_{\alpha,\beta}(E)=-H^{n-2}\Ch_{2}^{\beta}(E)+\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}H^{n}\Ch_{0}^{\beta}(E)+iH^{n-1}\Ch_{1}^{\beta}(E)

defines a weak stability condition on
D^{b}(X)
. Moreover, these weak stability conditions vary continuously with
(\alpha,\beta)
.

In virtue of the proposition above, we will consider the upper half plane

\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,\alpha>0\}
as a space parametrizing the weak stability conditions
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
. We write
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}\coloneqq\mu_{Z_{\alpha,\beta}}
for the slope function associated to
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
. We can now define
walls
and
chambers
:

Definition 2.6
.

Fix a vector
v\in\Lambda^{2}_{H}
.

A
numerical wall
for
v
is the set of pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}
such that there exists a vector
w\in\Lambda^{2}_{H}
for which
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(v)=\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(w)
.

A
wall
for
F\in\Coh^{\beta}(X)
is a numerical wall for
v=\Ch_{\leq 2}(F)
such that for every
(\alpha,\beta)
in the numerical wall there exists a short exact sequence of semistable objects
0\to E\to F\to G\to 0
satisfying
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(E)=\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(F)=\mu_{\alpha,\beta}(G)
.

A
chamber
is a connected component of the complement of the union of walls in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}
.

The key feature of this wall and chamber decomposition is that walls with respect to a fixed
v\in\Lambda^{2}_{H}
are locally finite in the upper half-plane. In particular, if
v=\Ch_{\leq 2}(E)
for some
E\in\Coh^{\beta}(X)
, stability of
E
is constant as
(\alpha,\beta)
varies within a chamber (see
[
BMS16
, Proposition B.5]
).

We conclude recalling another construction of a weak stability condition. In the notation of Definition
2.4
, fix
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\Coh^{\mu}_{\alpha,\beta}(X):=\Coh^{\beta}(X)^{\mu,\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}}
endowed with a stability function

Z^{\mu}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\dfrac{1}{u}Z_{\alpha,\beta},

where
u\in\mathbb{C}
is the unit vector in the upper half plane such that
\mu=-\dfrac{\Re u}{\Im u}
.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
BLMS17
, Proposition 2.15]
)
.

The pair
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha,\beta}=(\Coh^{\mu}_{\alpha,\beta}(X),Z^{\mu}_{\alpha,\beta})
is a weak stability condition on
D(X)
.

Finally, again in
[
BLMS17
]
, the authors induce a stability condition on the Kuznetsov component of
Y
using weak stability conditions:

Proposition 2.8

(
[
BLMS17
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
Y
be a Fano threefold of index 2 with
\Pic(Y)=\mathbb{Z}
, and let
\sigma^{0}_{\alpha,-\frac{1}{2}}
be the weak stability condition constructed in Proposition
2.7
for
\mu=0
,
\beta=-\dfrac{1}{2}
and
0<\alpha\ll 1
; let moreover
\mathcal{A}=\mathsf{Ku}(Y)\cap\Coh^{0}_{\alpha,\beta}(Y)
and
Z=Z^{0}_{\alpha,-\frac{1}{2}}
. Then the pair
\sigma=(\mathcal{A},Z)
is a Bridgeland stability condition on
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
.

2.3. Projection functors and base change

The following construction of
[
Kuz11
]
is crucial to some parts of this paper. For any quasi-projective variety
T
, we will use
D(T)
to denote the category of perfect complexes on
T
. Let
Y
be a smooth projective variety.

Suppose
E
is an exceptional object on
Y
. Then, the category

\langle E\rangle_{T}\coloneqq\langle E\boxtimes G\,|\,G\in D(T)\rangle\subset D(Y\times T)

gives rise to projection functors
\mathbb R_{E}^{T}
and
\mathbb L_{E}^{T}
to
\!{}^{\perp}\langle E\rangle_{T}
and
\langle E\rangle_{T}^{\perp}
respectively (
[
Kuz11
, Section 2.3]
). They are defined as the cones of the (co)evaluation morphisms.

Both
\langle E\rangle_{T}
and the projection functors are
T
-linear
(this means that they are preserved under tensoring with pull-backs from
D(T)
)
[

3. Fano threefolds of Picard rank 1 and index 2

Let

be a smooth Fano threefold of Picard rank 1 and index 2 (

Let

Definition 3.1

Let

Every smooth del Pezzo surface of degree

along a set

, Proposition 8.1.25]

We now describe hyperplane sections of

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be a rank 1 index 2 Fano threefold, and let

be a hyperplane section of

(1)

(2)

The general

. In any of the cases above,

Since

[

4.1. The Gieseker moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{H}(w)

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

be a Fano threefold of Picard rank 1, index 2, and degree

.
The moduli

of Gieseker-semistable objects of class

(see Definition

The components intersect in the locus parametrizing

with

Proof.

Since the class

must be pure. This implies that

for some sheaf

would give a destabilizing subsheaf of smaller dimension. Stability of

is a torsion-free rank-one stable sheaf on

Now we claim that

must have the form

on

and

must be an ideal sheaf of a subscheme supported in codimension 2. In particular, we have

Next, we apply the Grothendieck-Riemann-Roch theorem to compute

, where

. Then, there are two possibilities:

To proceed, we need to be more precise about the second Chern class for reflexive rank-one sheaves. The Riemann-Roch formula on normal surfaces presents a correction term

For

[

, Theorem 9.1]

Lan00

Consistently with the Riemann-Roch formulae, we define the second Chern character

Let

If

Fix

of class

.

\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)

We start by relating stability with respect to

\sigma^{0}_{\alpha,-\frac{1}{2}}

Remark 4.13

Notice that with this choice of stability condition,

Lemma 4.14

Let

Proof.

If

Conversely, if

Lemma 4.15
.

If

be

-semistable object of class

, then

-semistable.

Proof.

Since

-semistable, because otherwise the destabilizing subobject of

5.4.1. Conics on

The following construction expresses a relation between

A

Let

Conversely, given a root

Therefore, the assignment

is a root of a smooth

Consider the subscheme of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{0}=\{Z\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\,|\,Z\subset S\text{ with $S$ smooth}\}

The restriction of the Abel–Jacobi map to

Suppose

6. A categorical Torelli theorem for quartic double solids

As an application of the previous construction, this section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we state it here for the reader’s convenience. In this section,
d=2
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
be two general quartic double solids such that there exists an equivalence
u\colon\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
. Then
Y^{\prime}\simeq Y
.

This theorem improves a result of Bernardara and Tabuada
[
BT16
, Corollary 3.1 (iii)]
who show that the same holds under the additional assumption that

is of Fourier–Mukai type. The authors use noncommutative motives to construct isogenies between intermediate jacobians. In
[
Per20
]
, the author obtains similar results on intermediate jacobians, using only cohomological methods. Our technique is inspired by that of
[
BMMS12
]
; we construct an isomorphism between moduli spaces and use the Abel–Jacobi map to argue that this is sufficient to conclude.

6.1. Images of stable objects

First, we prove that given an equivalence as in Theorem

, we can produce another equivalence with nice properties on the objects parametrized by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

We start with a series of Lemmas, and denote by
\mathcal{A}=\mathsf{Ku}(Y)\cap\Coh^{0}_{\alpha,-\frac{1}{2}}(Y)
the heart of
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
associated to the stability condition

(Proposition

). We say that a heart of a bounded t-structure

. Then we have:

Lemma 6.2
.

The heart

Proof.

By definition,

. Clearly

since up to shift

is the Serre functor of

. Then, it suffices to show that

Observe that

Since

∎

.

l
, which has class
v
, and checks that
[L_{\mathcal{O}_{Y}}(I_{p}(1))]=[I_{l}(1)]-2[\mathcal{O}_{Y}]=w-v
.
∎

Now if we take an object
E\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, then
u(E)
has class
\pm w
or
\pm(2v-w)
. In the latter case we can replace
u
with
u\circ\mathsf{R}
so that
u(E)
has class
\pm w
. Lemmas
6.6
and
6.7
show that there exists an integer
n
such that
u(E)[n]\in\mathcal{A}
and is stable of class
w
(in particular, it has phase 1).

We want to prove that the shift can be taken uniformly,
i.e.
that, for the same
n
and any other object
F
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, we have
u(F)[n]\in\mathcal{A}
. For all such
F
, we must have
\Hom(E,F[1])=\Ext^{1}(E,F)\neq 0
, since
\Hom(E,F)=0
by stability,
\Ext^{3}(E,F)=\Ext^{-1}(F,E)=0
by use of the Serre functor on
\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})
, and
\chi(E,F)=-2
because of their numerical class. Likewise,
\Hom(F,E[1])\neq 0
. Applying
u(-)[n]
we get
\Hom(u(E)[n],u(F)[n+1])\neq 0
and
\Hom(u(F)[n],u(E)[n+1])\neq 0
. Now,
u(E)[n]
has phase 1, and let
\phi\in\mathbb{Z}
be the phase of
u(F)[n]
: from the above we get
1<\phi+1
and
\phi<2
, hence
\phi=1
and
u(F)[n]\in\mathcal{A}
.

We can thus summarize the results of this section with the following roposition:

Proposition 6.9
.

The equivalence
u\colon\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
induces a bijection on closed points
u\colon\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(k)\to\mathcal{M}(k)
.

Proof.

We have shown that the assignment
E\mapsto u(E)
sends points of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
to points of
\mathcal{M}
(and similarly,
u^{-1}
takes
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
). Since
u
is an equivalence, the induced map is a bijection on closed points.
∎

6.2. Universal families and convolutions

Let
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
denote the moduli spaces of objects of class
w
in
\mathsf{Ku}(Y)
, resp.
\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})
. As usual, we will denote by
\mathcal{Y}
the component isomorphic to
Y
in
\mathcal{M}
(we use the same convention in the case of
Y^{\prime}
).
The component
\mathcal{Y}
admits a universal family, whose construction we outline here (see also the proof of Proposition
4.11
). Let
\mathcal{I}\in D^{b}(Y\times\mathcal{Y})
be the pull back of the ideal sheaf of the diagonal via the isomorphism
Y\times\mathcal{Y}\xrightarrow{\sim}Y\times Y
. Then, define
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\mathbb R_{\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-1)}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{I})[-1]
to be the projection to
\!{}^{\perp}\langle\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-1)\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
(notation as in Section
2.3
). It is the desired universal family: for example the restriction of
\mathcal{E}
to a fiber
Y\simeq Y\times\{s\}
above a closed point
s\in S
fits in a triangle

I_{s}\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}(-1)[2]\to\mathcal{E}_{s}[1],

whence
\mathcal{E}_{s}=E_{s}
(this triangle is (
1
)). Likewise, define
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
to be the universal family above
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
.

Define the composite functor

F\colon D(Y^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\rho^{\prime}}\mathsf{Ku}(Y^{\prime})\xrightarrow{u}\mathsf{Ku}(Y)\xrightarrow{\epsilon}D(Y)

where
\rho^{\prime}
is the natural projection,
\epsilon
the full embedding, and
u
an exact equivalence.

If
F
is a Fourier–Mukai functor,
i.e.
,
F\simeq\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}
for some
\mathcal{G}\in D^{b}(Y\times Y^{\prime})
, then one defines

\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}\times\id_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}\coloneqq\Phi_{\mathcal{G}\boxtimes\mathcal{O}_{\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}}}\colon D^{b}(Y^{\prime}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})\to D^{b}(Y\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})

and the object

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\coloneqq\Phi_{\mathcal{G}}\times\id_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\in D^{b}(Y\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})

is a family of objects of
D(Y)
parametrized by
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
, which defines a morphism
Y^{\prime}\simeq\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}
.

If
F
is not a Fourier–Mukai functor, then one can use
convolutions
as in
[
BMMS12
, Section 5.2]
, whose method applies without changes to our case, and produces a family
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\in D^{b}(Y\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
.

In either case,
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
can be used to show that the map defined in Proposition
6.9
is a morphism which restricts to an isomorphism
Y^{\prime}=\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\simeq\mathcal{Y}=Y
. For a closed point
s\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
, denote by
i_{s}
(resp.
i^{\prime}_{s}
) the inclusion
Y\times\{s\}\to Y\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
(resp.
Y^{\prime}\times\{s\}\to Y^{\prime}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
). Then we have:

Lemma 6.10
.

For any closed point
s\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
we have
i_{s}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{E}})\simeq F((i^{\prime}_{s})^{*}\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

This is
[
BMMS12
, Lemma 5.3]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Whether
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is constructed with a Fourier–Mukai functor or by means of convolutions, one sees that
i_{s}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{E}})\simeq F((i^{\prime}_{s})^{*}\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=u(E^{\prime}_{s})
for all
s\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. In other words,
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is a family of objects of
\mathcal{M}
parametrized by
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
, and it yields a proper morphism
\alpha\colon\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}
with the property that
\alpha(s)\in\mathcal{M}
corresponds to the object
u(E^{\prime}_{s})
for all
s\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
is irreducible,
\alpha
factors through one of the components of
\mathcal{M}
. We claim that
\alpha
must factor through
\mathcal{Y}
. Granting the claim for a moment, smoothness of
\mathcal{Y}
implies that
\alpha
is the desired isomorphism
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{Y}
.

To establish the claim, observe that
\alpha
is, in particular, a morphism dominating one of the components of
\mathcal{M}
and
\alpha
is birational onto its image. Since
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
is rationally connected and
\alpha
preserves this property (see for example
[
Kol96
]
), its image cannot lie in
\mathcal{C}
, which is not rationally connected as a consequence of Corollary
5.4
.
∎

Remark 6.11

(Generality assumption)
.

The theorem should still hold assuming that
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are smooth, but possibly not general. In our proof, we use Corollary
5.4
, which holds for any smooth
Y
, to distinguish the two components
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. However, if
Y
is not general we cannot rule out that the wall-crossing of Proposition
4.11
introduces additional components in
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(w)
.
