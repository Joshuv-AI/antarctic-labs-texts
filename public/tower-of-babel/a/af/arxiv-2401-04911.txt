On the Rees algebras of t t -path ideals of cycles
Oleksandra Gasanova, Jürgen Herzog, Jiawen Shan

Abstract
. In this paper we explore certain properties of the Rees algebra of I t ​ ( C n ) I_{t}(C_{n}) , the t t -path ideal of an n n -cycle. Our main focus is on the cases when such ideals are of fiber type.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite simple graph with the vertex set
V=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and let
1\leq t\leq n
be an integer. A sequence
\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{t}}\}
is called a
t
-path
of
G
if
\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}}\},\{x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}}\},\ldots,\{x_{i_{t-1}},x_{i_{t}}\}
are distinct edges of
G
. The
t
-path ideal

I_{t}(G)
associated to
G
is the squarefree monomial ideal

I_{t}(G)=(x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{t}}\mid\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{t}}\}\textrm{~is~a}~t\textrm{-path~of}~G)

in the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Note that if we set
t=2
, we recover the definition of the edge ideal, defined and studied by Villareal in
[
11
]
. Here it was also proven that for a connected graph
G
the ideal
I_{2}(G)
is of linear type if and only if
G
is a tree or has a unique cycle of odd length. The notion of a path ideal as defined above was first introduced by Conca and De Negri in
[
3
]
in 1999 as a generalisation of the notion of an edge ideal. The authors also showed that if
G
is a tree, then
I_{t}(G)
is of linear type and
R(I_{t}(G))
is normal and Cohen-Macaulay.
In
[
1
]
Brumatti and da Silva focused on the case when
G=C_{n}
is a cycle, and discussed in which cases the ideal
I_{t}(C_{n})
is of linear type. They showed that

(1)

If
t=n-1
, then
I_{t}(C_{n})
is of linear type;

(2)

If
n
is odd and
t=n-2
or
t=\frac{n-1}{2}
, then
I_{t}(C_{n})
is of linear type;

(3)

If
gcd(n,t)>1
, then
I_{t}(C_{n})
is not of linear type;

(4)

If
gcd(n,t)=1

and
[\frac{n-1}{2}]<t\leq n-3
, then
I_{t}(C_{n})
is not of linear type;

(5)

If
gcd(n,t)=1
,
tl\equiv 1\pmod{n}
and
1<t,l\leq[\frac{n-1}{2}]
, then
I_{t}(C_{n})
is not of linear type;

(6)

If
gcd(n,t)=1
,
tl\equiv 1\pmod{n}
,
1<t\leq[\frac{n-1}{2}]
,
[\frac{n-1}{2}]<l<n
and
n=p(n-l)+a
,
2\leq a<n-l
, then
I_{t}(C_{n})
is not of linear type.

The authors also provided references for presence of linear type in a few other cases for instance,
t=1
,
t=n
, and
t=2
when
n
is odd.

The next best possible situation after linear type is so-called
fiber type
. Villarreal proved in
[
12
, Theorem 8.2.1]
that
I_{2}(G)
is of fiber type.
In
[
6
]
it was shown that all polymatroidal ideals are of fiber type.
This allows to prove that for instance
t
-path ideals of complete graphs are of fiber type, since they are squarefree Veronese ideals and hence polymatroidal.

In this paper we are mainly focusing on path ideals of cycles
C_{n}
, and study which
I_{t}(C_{n})
are of fiber type, but not of linear type.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we give the necessary background: we recall the definitions of the Rees algebra, the symmetric algebra and the fiber cone of an ideal, and explain the notions of linear type and fiber type. In Section 3 we discuss the dimension and defining relations of the fiber cone
F(I_{t}(C_{n}))
. In Section 4 we compute a Gröbner basis of the defining ideal of
R(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))
. It allows us to conclude that
I_{n-2}(C_{n})
is of fiber type, and to compute the Hilbert series and Cohen-Macaulay type of
R(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))
. In Section 5 we compute a Gröbner basis of the defining ideal of
R(I_{\frac{n}{2}}(C_{n}))
when
n
is even, and conclude that
I_{\frac{n}{2}}(C_{n})
is also of fiber type. Section 6 contains a summary or results regarding linear and fiber type of
I_{t}(C_{n})
, and a few open questions.

2.
Preliminaries and background

In this section we provide some algebraic background and notation which will be used throughout this paper.

Let
I=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
be an equigenerated graded ideal of the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
over the field
K
. Let
s
be a variable over
S
. We denote by
R(I)=S[u_{1}s,\ldots,u_{m}s]
the Rees algebra of
I
. Then
R(I)
is bigraded with
\deg x_{i}=(1,0)
for
i=1,\ldots,n

\deg u_{j}s=(0,1)
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

Let
T=S[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
be the polynomial ring over
S
in the variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
. We define a bigrading on
T
by setting
\deg x_{i}=(1,0)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\deg y_{j}=(0,1)
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Then the natural surjective bigraded
K
-algebra homomorphism

\varphi:T\longrightarrow R(I)

with
\varphi(x_{i})=x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\varphi(y_{j})=u_{j}s
for
j=1,\ldots,m
is bigraded.

Let
J=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)
be the defining ideal of
R(I)
. One says that
I
satisfies the
x
-condition
if there exists a monomial order
<
on
T
such that for any minimal monomial generator
u
of
\operatorname{in}_{<}(J)
we have
\deg_{x_{i}}u\leq 1
for all
i
. The
x
-condition guarantees that all powers of
I
have linear resolution (see
[
5
, Corollary 10.1.7]
). Moreover, if
<
is a product order of an arbitrary
<^{\#}
on
B_{1}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
, and
<_{lex}
induced by
x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n}
on
B_{2}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, then all powers of
I
have linear quotients (see
[
5
, Theorem 10.1.9]
).

Let
\alpha=(u_{ij})_{m\times r}
be a relation matrix of
I
. Then for
j=1,\ldots,r
, the polynomials
\ell_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{ij}y_{i}\in\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)
.
Let
L=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r})
. Then
T/L
is isomorphic to the symmetric algebra
S(I)
. Since
L\subseteq J
, there exists a surjective bigraded
K
-algebra homomorphism
\psi:S(I)\longrightarrow R(I)
.

The ideal
I
is called of
linear type
, if
\psi
is an isomorphism, that is, if
\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)=L
.

In a sense the next best case after linear type is fiber type, which is defined as follows. Let
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be the graded maximal ideal of
S
. The
K
-algebra
F(I)=R(I)/{\mathfrak{m}}R(I)
is called the
fiber cone
of
I
. If
I
is equigenerated, then
F(I)\cong K[u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}]
.

The map
\varphi:T\longrightarrow R(I)
induces a surjective
K
-algebra homomorphism
\varphi^{\prime}:T/{\mathfrak{m}}T\longrightarrow R(I)/{\mathfrak{m}}R(I).
Since
T/{\mathfrak{m}}T\cong K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
, the map
\varphi^{\prime}
may be identified with

K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]\longrightarrow K[u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}],\quad y_{i}\mapsto u_{i}.

The elements in
H=\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi^{\prime})
are called
the fiber relations
of
I
. They are polynomials in
R=K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
.

Obviously,
HT\subseteq J
and therefore,
L+HT\subseteq J
. The ideal is called of
fiber type
, if
J=L+HT
. Note that

\text{linear type}\Rightarrow\text{fiber type}\Rightarrow\text{general case},

and none of the implications can be reversed.

In this paper we are mainly focusing on path ideals of cycles
C_{n}
, and study which
I_{t}(C_{n})
are of fiber type, but not of linear type. Throughout the paper, we consider
1<t<n-1
, since the case
t=n
is trivial, and
I_{t}(C_{n})
are of linear type when
t=1
and
t=n-1
. We have
m=n
since the number of
t
-paths equals the number of vertices, and the homomorphism
\varphi
defined above will map
y_{j}
to
x_{j}\cdots x_{j+t-1}s
. Since we are working with an
n
-cycle, the indices of all the variables (both
x_{i}
and
y_{j}
) will be taken modulo
n
. In particular, we will interchangeably use
x_{0}
and
x_{n}
(
y_{0}
and
y_{n}
) to mean the same thing.

3.
Dimension and relations of
F(I_{t}(C_{n}))

In this section we focus on some properties of the fiber cone of the path ideals of cycles.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d=\gcd(n,t)
. Then
\dim F(I_{t}(C_{n}))=n-d+1
. In particular,
F(I_{t}(C_{n}))
is a polynomial ring if and only if
\gcd(n,t)=1
.

Proof.

It is known that the dimension of a toric ring equals the rank of the matrix whose column vectors are the exponent vectors of the monomial generators of the algebra. In our case,

A=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{n-1}&\ldots&c_{2}&c_{1}\\
c_{1}&c_{0}&\ldots&c_{3}&c_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&~~&\vdots&\vdots&\\
c_{n-2}&c_{n-3}&\ldots&c_{0}&c_{n-1}\\
c_{n-1}&c_{n-2}&\ldots&c_{1}&c_{0}\end{pmatrix},

where
c_{0}=c_{1}=\cdots=c_{t-1}=1
and
c_{t}=c_{t+1}=\cdots=c_{n-1}=0
.
This is a so-called
circulant
matrix, and by
[
8
]
its rank equals
n-\deg\gcd(f(x),x^{n}-1)
, where
f(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}x+\cdots+c_{n-1}x^{n-1}
. In our case,

f(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^{t-1}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi^{t}=1\\
\xi\not=1\end{subarray}}(x-\xi)

and

x^{n}-1=\prod_{{\xi^{n}=1}}(x-\xi).

Therefore, our goal is to show that these polynomials have exactly
d-1
roots in common. Equivalently, our goal is to show that
x^{t}-1
and
x^{n}-1
have exactly
d
roots in common. On the one hand, every
d
th root of
1
is also a
n
th and a
t
th root of
1
, so we have at least
d
common roots. On the other hand, if
\xi
is both a
n
th and a
t
th root of
1
, it has to be a
d
th root of
1
. Indeed,
d=k_{1}n+k_{2}t
for some integers
k_{1},k_{2}
, which then implies
\xi^{d}=\xi^{k_{1}n+k_{2}t}=\xi^{k_{1}n}\xi^{k_{2}t}=1
. Therefore, we can not have more than
d
common roots. We conclude that the two polynomials have exactly
d
common roots, as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider the
K
-algebra homomorphism
\varphi^{\prime}:R=K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]\longrightarrow F(I_{t}(C_{n}))
given by
\varphi^{\prime}(y_{i})=x_{i}x_{i+1}\cdots x_{i+t-1}
. Let
d=\gcd(n,t)
and let

m_{i}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
j\equiv i\pmod{d}\end{subarray}}y_{j}

for
i=1,\ldots,d
. Then the defining ideal
H
of
F(I_{t}(C_{n}))
coincides with the ideal
H^{\prime}=(m_{1}-m_{d},m_{2}-m_{d},\ldots,m_{d-1}-m_{d})
.

Proof.

We may assume that
d>1
, otherwise the theorem follows directly from
3.1
. Let
n=n^{\prime}d
and
t=t^{\prime}d
. Note that
\varphi^{\prime}(m_{i})=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})^{t^{\prime}}
for all
i
.
We will prove this for
i=1
, the proof for the other
i
is analogous.
Let

v_{1}=x_{1}\cdots x_{d},v_{2}=x_{d+1}\cdots x_{2d},\ldots,v_{n^{\prime}}=x_{n-d+1}\cdots x_{n}.

In other words,
v_{j}
encodes a
d
-path ending at
dj
for all
j=1,\ldots,n^{\prime}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(y_{1})

\displaystyle=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{t^{\prime}-1}v_{t^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(y_{d+1})

\displaystyle=v_{2}v_{3}\cdots v_{t^{\prime}}v_{t^{\prime}+1},

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(y_{n-2d+1})

\displaystyle=v_{n^{\prime}-1}v_{n^{\prime}}\cdots v_{t^{\prime}-3}v_{t^{\prime}-2},

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(y_{n-d+1})

\displaystyle=v_{n^{\prime}}v_{1}\cdots v_{t^{\prime}-2}v_{t^{\prime}-1}.

Therefore,
\varphi^{\prime}(m_{1})
is the product of all of these expressions. We can see that the product of each column equals
v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{n^{\prime}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
, and there are
t^{\prime}
columns in total, which implies
\varphi^{\prime}(m_{1})=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})^{t^{\prime}}
. This shows that
m_{i}-m_{d}\in H
for
i=1,\ldots,d-1
. Therefore,
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
.
3.1
implies that
\operatorname{height}H=d-1
, therefore, it is enough to show that
H^{\prime}
is a prime ideal of height
d-1
. The initial terms of the set
\mathcal{G}=\{m_{1}-m_{d},m_{2}-m_{d},\ldots,m_{d-1}-m_{d}\}
with respect to the lexicographical order
<
are the monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{d-1}
. Since these monomials are pairwise coprime, it follows that
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
H^{\prime}
, and hence
\operatorname{in}_{<}(H^{\prime})=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d-1})
. Therefore,
\operatorname{height}H^{\prime}=\operatorname{height}\operatorname{in}_{<}(H^{\prime})=d-1
. From this Gröbner basis we also see that all variables dividing
m_{d}
are non-zerodivisors modulo
\operatorname{in}_{<}(H^{\prime})
, and therefore modulo
H^{\prime}
. Since
H^{\prime}=(m_{i}-m_{j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n)
, we obtain by symmetry that all
y_{i}
are non-zerodivisors modulo
H^{\prime}
.

Let
y=y_{d}\cdots y_{n}
. Then
y
is a non-zerodivisor modulo
H^{\prime}
.
Therefore, the natural map
R/H^{\prime}\longrightarrow R_{y}/H^{\prime}R_{y}
is injective. We will show that
R_{y}/H^{\prime}R_{y}
is a domain. This will then imply that
R/H^{\prime}
is a domain, as desired. We know that
R_{y}=K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-1},y_{d}^{\pm 1},\ldots,y_{n}^{\pm 1}]
and that
H^{\prime}R_{y}
is generated by all the
m_{i}-m_{d}=y_{i}y_{d+i}\cdots y_{n-d+i}-y_{d}y_{2d}\cdots y_{n}
,
i=1,\ldots,d-1
. Equivalently, we can say that
H^{\prime}R_{y}
is generated by all the
y_{i}-(y_{d}y_{2d}\cdots y_{n})\cdot(y_{d+i}\cdots y_{n-d+i})^{-1}
,
i=1,\ldots,d-1
. This implies that
R_{y}/H^{\prime}R_{y}\cong K[y_{d}^{\pm 1},y_{d+1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,y_{n}^{\pm 1}]
, which finishes the proof.
∎

4.
The structure of the Rees ring of
I_{n-2}(C_{n})

Let
n\geq 3
be a integer and let
L
be the defining ideal of the symmetric algebra
S(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))
.
By
[
1
, Proposition 2.5]
we know that
L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1})
, where
f_{j}={x_{j-2}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
and
g_{1}=x_{0}y_{1}-x_{n-2}y_{0}
. Note that if we extend the definition of
f_{j}
to
j=n
, we will obtain
g_{1}=-f_{n}
. However, for reasons that will become clear later, it is not convenient to make such an extension for Gröbner bases computations. For all other purposes we may say
L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
.
As a preparation for a computation of a Gröbner basis for
L
(and then later for
J
, the defining ideal of
R(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))
), we extend the set of its generators.

Proposition 4.1
.

L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1},\ldots,g_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}),

where

g_{k}={x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}}-x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}~~for~~k=1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor.

Proof.

We will show by induction that
g_{k}\in L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1})
for all
k=1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. The base case holds by definition. Now, modulo
L
we have:

\displaystyle x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}=y_{2k-2}\left(x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k-2)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\right)=y_{2k-2}\left(x_{2k-4}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k-2)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{2k-2}y_{2k-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k-2)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}=x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

Here the second equality follows from the induction hypothesis and the third equality comes from
x_{2k-4}y_{2k-2}-x_{2k-2}y_{2k-1}\in L
. ∎

Theorem 4.2
.

The generators of
L
given in
4.1
form a Gröbner basis of
L
with respect to any monomial order
<
on
T
which is the product order of
<_{1}
and
<_{2}
, where
<_{1}
is the reverse lexicographic order on
R
induced by
y_{1}>y_{2}>\cdots>y_{n-1}>y_{0}
, and
<_{2}
is an arbitrary monomial order on
S
.

The proof of this fact can be found in the appendix. There, and further in this paper, the leading terms of binomials are underlined for convenience whenever a Gröbner basis or an initial ideal is being computed.

Recall that by
3.2
we have
H=0
if
n
is odd, and if
n
is even we have
H=(h)
, where

h=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}-\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

We will now describe a Gröbner basis of
L+HT
, which, if
n
is even,
will later turn out to be equal to
J
, the defining ideal of
R(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
n
be even.
Then the polynomials
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1},\ldots,g_{\frac{n}{2}},h\}
form a Gröbner basis for
L+HT
with respect to any monomial order given in
4.2
.

The proof of this fact can be found in the appendix.

We are now ready to describe the defining ideal
J
of
R(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let as before
L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
, where
f_{j}=x_{n-2+j}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. As before, let
H=(h)
, where
h=\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}y_{2i}-\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}y_{2i+1}
. Then
J=L
if
n
is odd, and
J=L+HT
if
n
is even.

Proof.

The proof is known when
n
is odd, see
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
. So we only have to deal with the case when
n
is even. We set
J^{\prime}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},h)
. Then obviously
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
.

We have
\dim R(I_{n-2}(C_{n}))=\dim S+1=n+1
. Thus
J^{\prime}=J
, once we have shown that
J^{\prime}
is a prime ideal and that
\dim T/J^{\prime}=n+1
.

It can be seen from the Gröbner basis of
J^{\prime}
given in
4.3
that
y_{0}
is a non-zerodivisor modulo
J^{\prime}
. By rotational symmetry it follows that all
y_{i}
are non-zerodivisors modulo
J^{\prime}
. This implies that
T/J^{\prime}\longrightarrow T_{y}/J^{\prime}T_{y}
is injective, where
y=y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1}
, and
J^{\prime}T_{y}=(\{x_{i}-x_{i-2}w_{i}\mid i=1,\ldots,n-1\},h)
with
w_{i}=y_{i}y_{i+1}^{-1}
. In particular,
x_{1}-x_{n-1}w_{1}
and
x_{3}-x_{1}w_{3}
belong to
J^{\prime}T_{y}
. It follows that
x_{3}-x_{1}w_{3}+w_{3}(x_{1}-x_{n-1}w_{1})=x_{3}-x_{n-1}w_{1}w_{3}\in J^{\prime}T_{y}
, and we may replace
x_{3}-x_{1}w_{3}
by
x_{3}-x_{n-1}w_{1}w_{3}
in the set of generators of
J^{\prime}T_{y}
, since
x_{3}-x_{1}w_{3}
can also be written as a linear combination of
x_{1}-x_{n-1}w_{1}
and
x_{3}-x_{n-1}w_{1}w_{3}
.
By applying similar substitutions, we may replace

x_{i}-x_{i-2}w_{i}
by
x_{i}-x_{n-1}\prod_{j=0}^{\frac{i-1}{2}}w_{2j+1}
, if
i
is odd, and we may replace it by
x_{i}-x_{0}\prod_{j=0}^{\frac{i-2}{2}}w_{2j+2}
, if
i
is even. In particular,
x_{n-1}-x_{n-3}w_{n-1}
can be replaced by
x_{n-1}-x_{n-1}\prod_{j=0}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}w_{2j+1}
, which after multiplication with the unit
\prod_{j=0}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}y_{2j}
, can be replaced by
x_{n-1}h
. Similarly,
x_{n}-x_{n-2}w_{n}
can be replaced by
x_{n}h
.

We conclude that

T_{y}/J^{\prime}T_{y}\cong(R[x_{0},x_{n-1}]/(h)R[x_{0},x_{n-1}])_{y}
. It follows that
T_{y}/J^{\prime}T_{y}
is a domain, since
h
is an irreducible polynomial in the polynomial ring
R[x_{0},x_{n-1}]
. This proves that
J^{\prime}
is a prime ideal.

Finally, notice that

\displaystyle\dim T/J^{\prime}=\dim T_{y}/J^{\prime}T_{y}=\dim(R[x_{0},x_{n-1}]/(h)R)_{y}

\displaystyle=\dim R[x_{0},x_{n-1}]/(h)R[x_{0},x_{n-1}]=n+1,

We are ready to compute the Hilbert series and Cohen-Macaulay type of

Theorem 4.6
.

Let

. Then:

(a)

if

, we have

\operatorname{HS}(R(I))=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k+1}z^{s-1-k}}+z^{s}}{(1-z)^{2s+2}},

(b)

if

\operatorname{HS}(R(I))=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k}z^{s-1-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+1}}

Proof.

As before, let
f_{1}=\underline{x_{n-1}y_{1}}-x_{1}y_{2}
,
f_{2}=\underline{x_{0}y_{2}}-x_{2}y_{3}
,
\ldots,

f_{n-1}=\underline{x_{n-3}y_{n-1}}-x_{n-1}y_{0}

i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}

. Also recall from

, where

x_{2s-4}y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-3},y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-1}).

Now, if

, the corresponding initial ideal is

K_{2s}=(x_{2s-1}y_{1},x_{0}y_{2},\ldots,x_{2s-3}y_{2s-1},x_{0}y_{1},x_{2}y_{1}y_{3},\ldots,\\
x_{2s-4}y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-3},y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-1}).

Note that our Gröbner basis is not reduced and the leading term of

divides the leading term of

.
From this point on, the proof will be divided into two steps:

Step 1: We will show that
(a)
is equivalent to

\operatorname{HS}(T/K^{\prime}_{2s+1})=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k+1}z^{s-1-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+2}}.

Indeed, we have

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{HS}(T/K_{2s+1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{HS}(T/(K_{2s+1}+(y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-1})))+z^{s}\operatorname{HS}(T/(K_{2s+1}:(y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-1})))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{HS}(T/K^{\prime}_{2s+1})+\frac{z^{s}}{{(1-z)}^{2s+2}}.

Step 2: We will prove the theorem by induction on

odd to

even.

(1) Let us assume we know the theorem is true for all

. We want to prove it for

.

We are working inside the ring

. The factor ring
T/K_{2s}T
has the Hilbert series given by (b), divided by
(1-z)^{2}
(since we have
2

x_{2s}
and

).
Let
\tilde{K}_{2s}\subset T

. Clearly,

has the same Hilbert series as

(which we know by the induction hypothesis). Now,

\tilde{K}_{2s}+(x_{2s-2}y_{2s})=(x_{2s}y_{1},x_{0}y_{2},\ldots,x_{2s-3}y_{2s-1},x_{2s-2}y_{2s}x_{0}y_{1},x_{2}y_{1}y_{3},\\
\ldots,x_{2s-4}y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-3},y_{1}y_{3}\cdots y_{2s-1})=K^{\prime}_{2s+1}

and
\tilde{K}_{2s}:(x_{2s-2}y_{2s})=\tilde{K}_{2s}
. Therefore

\operatorname{HS}(T/\tilde{K}_{2s})=\operatorname{HS}(T/K^{\prime}_{2s+1})+z^{2}\operatorname{HS}(T/\tilde{K}_{2s})

and hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{HS}(T/K^{\prime}_{2s+1})

\displaystyle=(1-z^{2})\operatorname{HS}(T/\tilde{K}_{2s}t)=(1-z^{2})\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k}z^{s-1-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k+1}z^{s-1-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+2}},

which by Step 1 is equivalent to having the desired Hilbert series for

.

(2) Let us assume we know the statement is true for all
n\leq 2s+1
, we want to prove it for

.

Now we are working inside the ring
T=K[x_{0},\ldots,x_{2s+1},y_{0},\ldots,y_{2s+1}]
.
The factor ring
T/K_{2s+1}T
has the Hilbert series given by (a), divided by

(since we have extra variables

and

).

and

\tilde{K}_{2s+2}:(y_{2s+1})=(x_{2s}y_{1},x_{0}y_{2},\ldots,x_{2s-3}y_{2s-1},x_{2s-2}y_{2s},x_{2s-1},\\

Then we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{HS}(T/K_{2s+2})=\operatorname{HS}(T/\tilde{K}_{2s+2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{HS}(T/(K_{2s+1}+(y_{2s+1}))T)+z\operatorname{HS}(T/(K^{\prime}_{2s+1}+(x_{2s-1}))T)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k+1}z^{s-1-k}}+z^{s}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}+\frac{z\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k+1}z^{s-1-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{s}+(1+z)\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2k+1}z^{s-1-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}=\frac{z^{s}+\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}{(1+z)^{2(k+1)}z^{s-(k+1)}}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{s}+\sum_{k=1}^{s}{(1+z)^{2k}z^{s-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{s}{(1+z)^{2k}z^{s-k}}}{(1-z)^{2s+3}}.

∎

Let
I=I_{n-2}(C_{n})
.
Since
R(I)
is a domain and its

-vector is symmetric, it follows from
4.6
and
[

, Theorem 4.4]
that

is Gorenstein when
n
is even.
4.6
also implies that
R(I)
is not Gorenstein, if
n
is odd. We actually have the following

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
n>1
be an odd integer and let
I=I_{n-2}(C_{n})
. Then the Cohen-Macaulay type of
R(I)
is
2
.

Proof.

The Rees algebra
R(I)
modulo the image of the sequence

{\mathbf{s}}=x_{1}-y_{3},x_{2}-y_{4},\ldots,x_{i-2}-y_{i},\ldots,x_{n-2}-y_{n},x_{n-1}-y_{1},x_{n},y_{2}

in

is isomorphic to
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/\bar{J}
, where

\bar{J}=(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}x_{3},x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}x_{4},\ldots,x_{n-3}^{2}-x_{n-2}x_{n-1},x_{n-2}^{2},x_{n-1}^{2},x_{1}x_{2}).

We denote by
<
the lexicographical order on
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
induced by
x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n-1}
. Then
\operatorname{in}_{<}(\bar{J})
contains
x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{2}
, This implies that
\dim R(I)/({\mathbf{s}})=0
. By
4.5
(a) the ring
R(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay. Since
\dim R(I)
coincides with the length of the sequence
{\mathbf{s}}
, it follows therefore from
[
2
, Theorem 2.1.2(c)]
that
{\mathbf{s}}
is an
R(I)
-sequence. From this we deduce that
R(I)
and
\bar{R}=R(I)/({\mathbf{s}})
have the same Cohen-Macaulay type, see
[
5
, Proposition A.6.2]
. Thus is suffices to show that the Cohen-Macaulay type of
\bar{R}
is
2
.

Let
A=(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}x_{3},\ldots,x_{n-3}^{2}-x_{n-2}x_{n-1},x_{n-2}^{2},x_{n-1}^{2})\subset K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
. Then
\bar{J}=A+(x_{1}x_{2})
, and
A
is generated by a regular sequence since
\operatorname{in}_{<}(A)=(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{2})
is generated by a regular sequence. Given this, it is well-known that the canonical module
\omega_{\bar{R}}
is isomorphic to
(A:(x_{1}x_{2}))/A
. For the convenience of the reader we show why this is the case.

The ring
\bar{S}=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/A
is a complete intersection and hence Gorenstein. In particular, up to a shift in the grading, we have that
\omega_{\bar{S}}\cong\bar{S}
. Then it follows from
[
2
, Theorem 3.3.7]
that, up to a shift,

\omega_{\bar{R}}\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\bar{S}}(\bar{R},\bar{S})\cong 0:_{\bar{S}}(x_{1}x_{2})\bar{S}.

The last equation results from the fact that
\bar{R}=\bar{S}/(x_{1}x_{2})\bar{S}
.

By
[
2
, Proposition 3.3.11]
, the minimal number of generators of
\omega_{\bar{R}}
is the Cohen-Macaulay type of
\bar{R}
. Thus it remains to be shown that
(A:(x_{1}x_{2}))/A
is generated by
2
elements.

In fact, we will show that
A:(x_{1}x_{2})=A+(x_{1},x_{2}x_{4}\ldots x_{n-3}x_{n-1})
. In other words, we want to determine for which polynomials
p
we have
px_{1}x_{2}\in A
. The inclusion
A+(x_{1},x_{2}x_{4}\ldots x_{n-3}x_{n-1})\subseteq A:(x_{1}x_{2})
is obvious, so we will focus on the other one. Since
\operatorname{in}_{<}(A)=(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{2})
, the squarefree monomials of
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
form a
K
-basis of
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/A
.
One way of proving that an element belongs to
A
is to prove that it reduces to
0
by the division algorithm with respect to a Gröbner basis of
A
. In this case the reduction algorithm can be described as substituting
x_{1}^{2}\rightarrow x_{2}x_{3},\ldots,x_{n-3}^{2}\rightarrow x_{n-2}x_{n-1},x_{n-2}^{2}\rightarrow 0,x_{n-1}^{2}\rightarrow 0
. Let
\bar{p}
denote the remainder of
p
with respect to this Gröbner basis. Since
p\equiv\bar{p}\operatorname{mod}A
, we may assume without loss of generality that
p=\bar{p}
. In other words, we may assume that all terms of
p
are squarefree. Now we need to apply the reduction algorithm to
x_{1}x_{2}p
. In the first step of the proof we will show by induction the following claim: for every pair of squarefree monomials
m_{1}\not=m_{2}
such that
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}=\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}}
we have
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}=\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}}=0
. This will imply that each monomial in the support of
x_{1}x_{2}p
has to reduce to
0
individually, which will reduce our problem to the case where
p
is a monomial. In the second step of the proof we will show by induction the following claim: if
m
is a squarefree monomial such that
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m}=0
, then
m\in A+(x_{1},x_{2}x_{4}\ldots x_{n-3}x_{n-1})
.
Note that if
n=3
, we have
A=(x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2})
and
A:(x_{1}x_{2})=A+(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1},x_{2})
. Both claims hold trivially in this case, and this settles the base case for inductive proofs of both claims.

To prove the first claim by induction, assume that the exists a pair of monomials
m_{1}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}
and
m_{2}=x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\beta_{n-1}}
with each
\alpha_{i}
and

either

or

m_{1}\not=m_{2}
and
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}=\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}}\not=0
. First of all note that none of the
m_{i}
is divisible by
x_{1}
, in other words,
\boxed{\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}=0}
. Indeed, if
\alpha_{1}=1
, then
x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}
is divisible by
x_{1}^{2}x_{2}\in A
because
x_{1}\in A:(x_{1}x_{2})
, and therefore
x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}
reduces to
0
. The same holds if
\beta_{1}=1
.

Now notice that
\alpha_{2}=\beta_{2}
. Indeed, if, say,
\alpha_{2}=0
and
\beta_{2}=1
, then
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}=x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}
(since
x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}
is squarefree), while
x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}=x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{\beta_{3}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\beta_{n-1}}\rightarrow x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}x_{3}^{\beta_{3}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\beta_{n-1}}
.
From this first reduction step it is already clear that
x_{2}
does not divide
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}}

x_{2}
does divide
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}
, which is a contradiction. If
\alpha_{2}=\beta_{2}=0
, we have
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}=x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}
and
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}}=x_{1}x_{2}m_{2}
, which then implies

, which is a contradiction. So we will assume

m_{1}=x_{2}m_{1}^{\prime}
and

, where

m_{2}^{\prime}=x_{3}^{\beta_{3}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\beta_{n-1}}

and similarly

. Clearly,
x_{1}
will not be involved in any further reductions. Therefore, it is enough to consider
x_{3}x_{4}m_{1}^{\prime}
and
x_{3}x_{4}m_{2}^{\prime}
. Note that
m_{1}^{\prime}
and
m_{2}^{\prime}
are squarefree monomials in

, and the only reduction rules we can use to reduce them are

. This means we are doing a reduction of
x_{3}x_{4}m_{1}^{\prime}
and
x_{3}x_{4}m_{2}^{\prime}
modulo
A^{\prime}=(x_{3}^{2}-x_{4}x_{5},\ldots,x_{n-3}^{2}-x_{n-2}x_{n-1},x_{n-1}^{2},x_{n})
inside
K[x_{3},x_{4},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
. By the induction hypothesis we know that
\overline{x_{3}x_{4}m_{1}^{\prime}}=\overline{x_{3}x_{4}m_{2}^{\prime}}\not=0
implies
m_{1}^{\prime}=m_{2}^{\prime}
therefore,
m_{1}=m_{2}
, which is a contradiction.

Now it remains to prove the second claim. Let
m=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}
be a squarefree monomial such that
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m}=0
. Assume
m\not\in A+(x_{1},x_{2}x_{4}\ldots x_{n-3}x_{n-1})
. We first note that
\boxed{\alpha_{1}=0}
, otherwise we immediately get a contradiction. If
\alpha_{2}=0
, then
\overline{x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}}=x_{1}x_{2}m_{1}\not=0
. Therefore,
\boxed{\alpha_{2}=1}
. Let
m=x_{2}m^{\prime}
, where
m^{\prime}=x_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}
. Then
x_{1}x_{2}m=x_{1}x_{2}^{2}m^{\prime}\rightarrow x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}m^{\prime}
. Clearly,
x_{1}
will not be involved in any further reductions, so it suffices to consider
x_{3}x_{4}m^{\prime}
. Note that
m^{\prime}
is a squarefree monomial in
K[x_{3},x_{4},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
, and the only reduction rules we can use to reduce

are
x_{3}^{2}\rightarrow x_{4}x_{5},\ldots,x_{n-3}^{2}\rightarrow x_{n-2}x_{n-1},x_{n-1}^{2}\rightarrow 0,x_{n}\rightarrow 0
. In other words, we are doing a reduction of
x_{3}x_{4}m^{\prime}
modulo
A^{\prime}=(x_{3}^{2}-x_{4}x_{5},\ldots,x_{n-3}^{2}-x_{n-2}x_{n-1},x_{n-1}^{2},x_{n})
inside
K[x_{3},x_{4},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
. By the induction hypothesis we know that the squarefree monomials modulo

which reduce to

x_{3}
or multiples of
x_{4}x_{6}\cdots x_{n-1}
. Assume

is a multiple of
x_{3}
modulo

(therefore, also modulo

a multiple of

The structure of the Rees ring of

The methods and the results in this section will be similar to those in

4
. Note, however, that the roles of

: recall that the extra elements

does not have a linear resolution except the case

are also somewhat harder to compute in this case.

For the rest of this section, let

be an even integer. Let

be the defining ideal of the symmetric algebra

.
By
[
1
, Proposition 2.5]
we know that

, where
f_{j}={x_{\frac{n}{2}+j}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1}
for

and

. Note that if we extend the definition of

to

.
As a preparation for a computation of a Gröbner basis for

(which is the defining ideal of

), we first extend the set of generators of

.

Proposition 5.1
.

L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1},\ldots,g_{\frac{n}{2}-1}),

where

g_{k}={y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\quad\textrm{for}\quad k=1,\ldots,\frac{n}{2}-1.

Proof.

We will show by induction that
g_{k}\in(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1})
for all

. The base case holds by definition. Now, modulo

we have:

\displaystyle y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle=(x_{k-1}y_{k})\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}x_{i}\overset{f_{k-1}}{=}x_{\frac{n}{2}+k-1}y_{k-1}\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}x_{i}=x_{\frac{n}{2}+k-1}(x_{k-2}y_{k-1})\prod_{i=0}^{k-3}x_{i}

\displaystyle\overset{f_{k-2}}{=}(x_{\frac{n}{2}+k-1}x_{\frac{n}{2}+k-2})y_{k-2}\prod_{i=0}^{k-3}x_{i}=\cdots\overset{f_{1}}{=}\prod_{i=1+\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}(x_{0}y_{1})\overset{g_{1}}{=}y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}.

∎

Recall that by

H=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\frac{n}{2}-1})

.

.

Let

Theorem 5.3
.

Let as before

, where

for

. As before, let

, where

.

Proof.

Let
J^{\prime}=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n-1},h_{1},\ldots,h_{\frac{n}{2}-1})
. We apply the same strategy as in the proof of
4.4
in order to show that

. It follows from the description of the Gröbner basis of

, that

for

.

.

It follows that

\{v_{l}v_{\frac{n}{2}+l}y_{n}^{2}-v_{n}v_{\frac{n}{2}}y_{n}^{2}:l=1,\ldots,\frac{n}{2}-1\}).

Since
J^{\prime}\subseteq J=\ker(\phi)

, we conclude that these binomials vanish under this substitution.
Since they are homogeneous in

, it follows that the coefficients of

in the first binomial must be equal, and the coefficients of

in the remaining binomials must also be equal.

This implies that

and since

∎

.

(b) This can be seen from the generators of

.

∎

2k+2\leq n
and so we can use the interval
[0,2k+2)
to rewrite our binomial as

x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k+2)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}-x_{2k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k+2)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

Note that this is simply
-g_{k+1}
. Also note that the indices of
g
’s are only allowed to up until
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, and so index
k+1
is allowed since
2(k+1)=2k+2\leq n
. This is not the case in the Subcase 1.a, where
g_{k+1}
merely does not exist.

Case 2: The two leading terms are not coprime because of a common
y-
factor, in other words, if
j
is an odd number belonging to
[0,2k)
. Then we get

\displaystyle S(f_{j},g_{k})

\displaystyle=S\left(\underline{x_{j-2}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1},\quad\underline{x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}}-x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{j}x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}-g_{k}x_{j-2}

\displaystyle=x_{j-2}x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}-\underline{x_{2k-2}x_{j}y_{j+1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}}

Note that the underlined term is indeed the leading term: unless
j+1=n
, we see that
y_{0}
is present only in the support of the first term (making it the smaller one). If
j+1=n
, and noting that
j=n-1
is an odd number belonging to
[0,2k)
, we conclude that
2k=n
. Then the above binomial becomes
x_{n-3}x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}-{x_{n-2}x_{n-1}y_{0}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n-2)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}}
. We see that
y_{0}
is present on both sides, then
y_{n-1}
is not present anywhere, and
y_{n-2}
is only present in the first term (making it the smaller one). We will show that the leading term reduces to the other one:

\displaystyle x_{2k-2}x_{j}y_{j+1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}=x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,j+3)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j}y_{j+2}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j,j+2\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{j+2}}x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,j+3)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j+2}y_{j+3}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j,j+2\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,j+5)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j+2}y_{j+4}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j,j+2,j+4\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{j+4}}x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,j+5)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j+4}y_{j+5}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j,j+2,j+4\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,j+7)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j+4}y_{j+6}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j,j+2,j+4,j+6\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\xrightarrow{\cdots}

We see that right after the reduction by
j+2s
the monomial is

x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,j+2s+1)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j+2s}y_{j+2s+1}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\setminus\{j,j+2,\ldots,j+2s\}\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

We do it as many times as it is needed to remove
2k-1
from the set of odd numbers. In other words, if we set
s=\frac{2k-1-j}{2}
, then
j+2s=2k-1
(which is in the range of the allowed indices for
f
’s) and we get

x_{2k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{2k-1}y_{2k}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,j-1)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

It is not difficult to notice that any monomial of type
x_{i}x_{i+1}y_{i+2}
(for any
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, and indices interpreted modulo
n
) reduces to
x_{n-2}x_{n-1}y_{0}
. Indeed,

x_{n-1}x_{0}y_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}x_{0}x_{1}y_{2}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}x_{1}x_{2}y_{3}\xrightarrow{\ldots}\ldots\xrightarrow{f_{n-2}}x_{n-3}x_{n-2}y_{n-1}\xrightarrow{f_{n-1}}x_{n-2}x_{n-1}y_{0}.

Therefore, in the monomial above we may replace
x_{2k-2}x_{2k-1}y_{2k}
with
x_{n-2}x_{n-1}y_{0}
and get

\displaystyle x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot\left[x_{n-1}y_{0}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,j-1)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\right]

Note that the monomial in square brackets is almost exactly the same as the leading term in Subcase 1.a, with the only difference that here the product is taken over a shorter interval. The reduction process of this monomial will be exactly the same as before, but with fewer steps. Analogously to Subcase 1.a, we do reductions with
f_{1},f_{3},\ldots
, and we
see that right after the reduction by
f_{2s+1}
the monomial in square brackets is

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2s+2)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot(x_{2s+1}y_{2s+2})\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[2s+2,j-1)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

if
j=1
, this monomial already equals the one we want to reduce to and we are done. Otherwise we set
s=\frac{j-3}{2}
and obtain that after the reduction by
f_{2s+1}=f_{j-2}
we get

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,j-1)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot(x_{j-2}y_{j-1})\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j-1,j-1)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,j+1)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j-2}.

Now that we have reduced the part in square brackets, the entire monomial then reduces to

x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[j+1,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,j+1)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}\cdot x_{j-2}=x_{j-2}x_{n-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,2k)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i},

which is precisely what we need.

Step 3:
The
S-
pairs
S(f_{i},f_{j})
all reduce to
0
.

This is obvious since the leading terms of
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
are coprime for
i\not=j
.

∎

4.3
.

Let
n
be even.
Then the polynomials
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1},\ldots,g_{\frac{n}{2}},h\}
form a Gröbner basis for
L+HT
with respect to any monomial order given in
4.2
. Here, as before,
H=(h)
, where

h=\underline{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}}-\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

Proof of
4.3
.

Let
n=2s
. We only need to check that the
S
-pairs involving
h
reduce to
0
. Note that
g_{s}=x_{n-2}h
, and so the
S
-pairs involving
h
will reduce very similarly to those involving
g_{s}
. We will briefly go through all the cases, essentially mimicking the proof of
4.2
.

Step 1:
The
S-
pairs
S(h,g_{k})
all reduce to
0
.

If
k=s
, there is nothing to check, so we will assume
k=s-a
,
a\geq 1
. It is not hard to verify that
S(g_{s},g_{s-a})=x_{n-2}S(h,g_{s-a})
.

In Step 1 in the proof of
4.2
we computed all
S(g_{k},g_{k-a})
, the result was divisible by
x_{n-2}
, which we factored out, and the remaining binomial reduced to
0
. Therefore, with the exact same reduction process
S(h,g_{s-a})
will reduce to
0
.

Step 2:
The
S-
pairs
S(f_{j},h)
all reduce to
0
.

We will mimic the proof of Step 2 of
4.2
.
Recall that

f_{j}=\underline{x_{j-2}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1},\quad h=\underline{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}y_{i}}-\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,n)\\
i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}y_{i}.

Again, we need to consider only the cases when the leading terms of these binomials are not coprime. They cannot have a common
x
-factor, so Case 1 of Step 2 of
4.2
cannot happen here. If the two leading terms are not coprime, it has to be because of a common
y
-factor, and this brings us to Case 2 of Step 2 of
4.2
. Then, as in the proof of
4.2
,
j
needs to be an odd number, in our case lying in
[0,n)
. We once again see that
S(f_{j},g_{s})=x_{n-2}S(f_{j},h)
. Following the proof of Case 2 of Step 2 of
4.2
(with
n=2s
and
k=s
), we see that
x_{2k-2}
(which now becomes
x_{n-2}
) is not involved in any reduction except in the step where
x_{2k-2}x_{2k-1}y_{2k}
reduces
x_{n-2}x_{n-1}y_{0}
. In our case, however, this is actually no reduction since
n=2k
. Therefore, performing the same chain of reductions we will reduce
S(f_{j},h)
to
0
.
∎

5.2
.

Let
<
be the product order given in
4.2
.
Then the polynomials

f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1},\ldots,g_{\frac{n}{2}-1},h_{1},\ldots,h_{\frac{n}{2}-1}

form a Gröbner basis for
L+HT
. Here
f_{j}=\underline{x_{\frac{n}{2}+j}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1}
,

g_{k}=\underline{y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i},

and
h_{l}=\underline{y_{l}y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}}-y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}
. As before,
L=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},g_{1},\ldots,g_{\frac{n}{2}-1})
and
H=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\frac{n}{2}-1})
.

Proof of
5.2
.

The proof will be divided into the following steps.

Step 1:
The
S-
pairs
S(f_{j_{1}},f_{j_{2}})
and
S(h_{l_{1}},h_{l_{2}})
all reduce to
0
.

This is obvious since the leading terms of
f_{j_{1}}
and
f_{j_{2}}
(resp.
h_{l_{1}}
and
h_{l_{2}}
) are coprime for
j_{1}\not=j_{2}
(resp.
l_{1}\not=l_{2}
).

Step 2:
The
S-
pairs
S(g_{k_{1}},g_{k_{2}})
all reduce to
0
.

Let
1\leq k-s<k\leq\frac{n}{2}-1
. We would like to show that
S(g_{k},g_{k-s})
reduces to
0
.

\displaystyle S(g_{k},g_{k-s})

\displaystyle=S\left(\underline{y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i},\quad\underline{y_{k-s}\prod_{i=0}^{k-s-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-s-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=g_{k}y_{k-s}-g_{k-s}y_{k}\prod_{i=k-s}^{k-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle=y_{0}y_{k}\prod_{i=k-s}^{k-1}x_{i}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-s-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}-y_{0}y_{k-s}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}

\displaystyle=y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-s-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\left(y_{k}\prod_{i=k-s}^{k-1}x_{i}-\underline{y_{k-s}\prod_{i=k-s+\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}}\right).

Therefore, reducing the leading term of the binomial in parentheses using
f_{k-s},f_{k-s+1},\ldots f_{k-1}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle y_{k-s}\prod_{i=k-s+\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}=\left(y_{k-s}x_{k-s+\frac{n}{2}}\right)\prod_{i=k-s+1+\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{k-s}}x_{k-s}y_{k-s+1}\prod_{i=k-s+1+\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{k-s}\left(y_{k-s+1}x_{k-s+\frac{n}{2}+1}\right)\prod_{i=k-s+2+\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{k-s+1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{f_{k-1}}x_{k-s}\cdots x_{k-1}y_{k}=y_{k}\prod_{i=k-s}^{k-1}x_{i}.

Alternatively, one can do one reduction step (by
f_{k-s}
) and notice that the obtained monomial is
x_{k-s}
times the monomial we are reducing, with
s\rightarrow s-1
. The rest follows easily by induction.
Therefore,
S(g_{k},g_{k-s})
reduces to
0
.

Step 3:
The
S-
pairs
S(f_{j},g_{k})
all reduce to
0
.

Recall that
f_{j}=\underline{x_{\frac{n}{2}+j}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1}
and

g_{k}=\underline{y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}
.
We need to consider only the cases when the leading terms of these binomials are not coprime. This can happen in the following two cases:

Case 1: The two leading terms are not coprime because of a common
x-
factor, in other words,

0\leq j+\frac{n}{2}\leq k-1
. Then we have:

\displaystyle S(f_{j},g_{k})

\displaystyle=S\left(\underline{x_{\frac{n}{2}+j}y_{j}}-x_{j}y_{j+1},\quad\underline{y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{k}\cdot y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}+j+1}^{k-1}x_{i}-g_{k}\cdot y_{j}

\displaystyle=x_{j}\left(y_{0}y_{j}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{j-1}x_{i}\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}-\underline{y_{k}y_{j+1}\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}+j+1}^{k-1}x_{i}}\right)

Note that the underlined monomial is indeed the leading term of this binomial. In order to see this, it is enough to show that the underlined term is not divisible by
y_{0}
. Clearly,
k\not=0
given the range where
g_{k}
’s are defined. If we assume
j+1=0
, then
0\leq j+\frac{n}{2}\leq k-1
implies
0\leq\frac{n}{2}-1\leq k-1
, which is again a contradiction with the range where
g_{k}
’s are defined. We will first reduce a divisor of the leading term as follows:

\displaystyle y_{k}y_{j+1}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}+j+1}^{k-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{j+1}}x_{j+1}y_{k}y_{j+2}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}+j+2}^{k-1}x_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{j+2}}x_{j+1}x_{j+2}y_{k}y_{j+3}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}+j+3}^{k-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{j+3}}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}}y_{k}y_{k+\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\xrightarrow{h_{k}}y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}.

Therefore, we have reduced the leading term in parentheses to
y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}
. It is now sufficient to show that
y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}
can be reduced to
y_{j}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{j-1}x_{i}
. But this is true since

\displaystyle y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{\frac{n}{2}}}x_{\frac{n}{2}}y_{\frac{n}{2}+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{\frac{n}{2}+1}}x_{\frac{n}{2}}x_{\frac{n}{2}+1}y_{\frac{n}{2}+2}\prod_{i=2}^{\frac{n}{2}+j-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{\frac{n}{2}+2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{j-1}}y_{j}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{j-1}x_{i}.

Therefore,
S(f_{j},g_{k})
reduce to
0
in this case.

Case 2: The two leading terms are not coprime because of a common
y-
factor, in other words,
j=k\in\{1,\ldots,\frac{n}{2}-1\}
. In this case we have:

\displaystyle S(f_{k},g_{k})

\displaystyle=S\left(\underline{x_{\frac{n}{2}+k}y_{k}}-x_{k}y_{k+1},\quad\underline{y_{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{k}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}-g_{k}\cdot x_{\frac{n}{2}+k}=y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{k+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}-\underline{y_{k+1}\prod_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}}.

Note that this binomial equals
-g_{k+1}
, unless
k=\frac{n}{2}-1
. If
k=\frac{n}{2}-1
, we have:

\displaystyle y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=0}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}x_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{\frac{n}{2}}}x_{\frac{n}{2}}y_{\frac{n}{2}+1}\prod_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}x_{i}=x_{\frac{n}{2}}(x_{1}y_{\frac{n}{2}+1})\prod_{i=2}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{\frac{n}{2}+1}}x_{\frac{n}{2}}x_{\frac{n}{2}+1}y_{\frac{n}{2}+2}\prod_{i=2}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}x_{i}=x_{\frac{n}{2}}x_{\frac{n}{2}+1}(x_{2}y_{\frac{n}{2}+2})\prod_{i=3}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{f_{\frac{n}{2}+2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{f_{n-1}}x_{\frac{n}{2}}x_{\frac{n}{2}+1}\cdots x_{n-1}y_{0}=y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{n-1}x_{i},

which is exactly what we need.
Therefore,
S(f_{j},g_{k})
all reduce to
0
.

Step 4:
The
S-
pairs
S(g_{k},h_{l})
all reduce to
0
.

Notice that the leading terms of
g_{k}
and
h_{l}
are coprime except
k=l
. In this case we have:

\displaystyle S(g_{l},h_{l})

\displaystyle=S\left(\underline{y_{l}\prod_{i=0}^{l-1}x_{i}}-y_{0}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{l-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i},\quad\underline{y_{l}y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}}-y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=g_{l}y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}-h_{l}\prod_{i=0}^{l-1}x_{i}=y_{0}\left(\underline{y_{\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=0}^{l-1}x_{i}}-y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{l-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}\right).

Note that the leading term of the binomial in parentheses is very similar to the leading term of the
S
-pair in the previous Step 3 (the case
k=\frac{n}{2}-1
). We do the exact same reduction of this monomial, but with fewer steps. We use
f_{\frac{n}{2}},f_{\frac{n}{2}+1},\ldots,f_{\frac{n}{2}+l-1}
and from Step 3 we see that after these reductions we will obtain
x_{\frac{n}{2}}\cdots x_{\frac{n}{2}+l-1}y_{\frac{n}{2}+l}=y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{i=\frac{n}{2}}^{l-1+\frac{n}{2}}x_{i}
, which is exactly what we need. We conclude that all
S(g_{k},h_{l})
reduce to
0
.

Step 5:
The
S-
pairs
S(f_{j},h_{l})
all reduce to
0
.

Notice that the leading terms of
f_{j}
and
h_{l}
are coprime except the case
j=l\in\{1,\ldots,\frac{n}{2}-1\}
and the case
l\in\{1,\ldots,\frac{n}{2}-1\}
,
j=l+\frac{n}{2}
. In the former case we have:

\displaystyle S(f_{l},h_{l})

\displaystyle=S\left(\underline{x_{\frac{n}{2}+l}y_{l}}-x_{l}y_{l+1},\quad\underline{y_{l}y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}}-y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}\right)=f_{l}y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}-h_{l}x_{\frac{n}{2}+l}

\displaystyle=x_{\frac{n}{2}+l}y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}-\underline{x_{l}y_{l+1}y_{l+\frac{n}{2}}}\xrightarrow{f_{l+\frac{n}{2}}}x_{\frac{n}{2}+l}y_{0}y_{\frac{n}{2}}-x_{l+\frac{n}{2}}y_{l+1+\frac{n}{2}}y_{l+1}=-x_{\frac{n}{2}+l}h_{l+1}.

Note that the last equality makes sense unless
l=\frac{n}{2}-1
, in which case
h_{l+1}
is not defined. However, in this case the left hand side of this equality equals
0
.
Similarly, in the latter case
S(f_{l+\frac{n}{2}},h_{l})
reduces to

x_{l}h_{l+1}
using
f_{l}
(and the same remark about
l=\frac{n}{2}-1
applies). Therefore,
S(f_{j},h_{l})
all reduce to
0
.
