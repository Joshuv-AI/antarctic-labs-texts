A symbolic algorithm for exact polynomial optimization strengthened with Fritz John conditions
Ngoc Hoang Anh Mai

Abstract
Consider a polynomial optimization problem. Adding polynomial equations generated by the Fritz John conditions to the constraint set does not change the optimal value. As proved in [arXiv:2205.04254 (2022)], the objective polynomial has finitely many values on the new constraint set under some genericity assumption. Based on this, we provide an algorithm that allows us to compute exactly this optimal value. Our method depends on the computations of real radical generators and Gröbner basis. Finally, we apply our method to solve some instances of mathematical program with complementarity constraints.



Keywords:
Gröbner basis; real radical; gradient ideal; Fritz John conditions; Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions; polynomial optimization

Introduction

Besides numerical methods, symbolic tools that allow us to obtain the exact optimal value for a polynomial optimization problem have various applications in engineering science.
Therefore developing such methods to address the exactness challenge becomes essential.

Polynomial optimization with singularities.

Problem statement.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
(resp.
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
) stand for the ring of polynomials with real (resp. rational) coefficients in the vector of variables
x
.
We denote by
{\mathbb{R}}_{r}[x]
the linear space of polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
over
{\mathbb{R}}

r

Given
f,g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, consider polynomial optimization problem:

f^{\star}:=\inf\limits_{x\in S(g)}f(x)\,,

(1)

where
S(g)
is the basic semi-algebraic set associated with
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
, i.e.,

S(g):=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,g_{j}(x)\geq 0\,,\,j=1,\dots,m\}\,.

(2)

First-order optimality conditions.

The gradient of
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, denoted by
\nabla p
, is defined by
\nabla p=(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{n}})
.
We say that the Fritz John conditions hold for problem (
1
) at
u\in S(g)
if

\begin{cases}\exists(\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{m})\in[0,\infty)^{m+1}\,:\\
\lambda_{0}\nabla f(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\nabla g_{j}(u)\,,\\
\lambda_{j}g_{j}(u)=0\,,\,j=1,\dots,m\,,\\
\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{2}=1\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}\exists(\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}\,:\\
\lambda_{0}^{2}\nabla f(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{2}\nabla g_{j}(u)\,,\\
\lambda_{j}^{2}g_{j}(u)=0\,,\,j=1,\dots,m\,,\\
\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{2}=1\,.\end{cases}

(3)

In addition, the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions hold for problem (
1
) at
u\in S(g)
if

\begin{cases}\exists(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})\in[0,\infty)^{m}\,:\\
\nabla f(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\nabla g_{j}(u)\,,\\
\lambda_{j}g_{j}(u)=0\,,\,j=1,\dots,m\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}\exists(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}\,:\\
\nabla f(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{2}\nabla g_{j}(u)\,,\\
\lambda_{j}^{2}g_{j}(u)=0\,,\,j=1,\dots,m\,.\end{cases}

(4)

If
u
is a local minimizer for problem (
1
), then the Fritz John conditions hold for problem (
1
) at
u
.
The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions can be considered a particular case of the Fritz John conditions.
When some constraint qualification holds at
u
, the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions hold for problem (
1
) at
u
.
However, there exist cases where the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions do not hold for problem (
1
) at any local minimizer of this problem.
Interested readers are referred to
[
52
,
51
,
26
,
29
,
51
]
theoretical and practical results involving first-order optimality conditions in polynomial optimization.

In the unconstrained case of problem (
1
) (i.e.,
S(g)={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
), the Fritz John and Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions ((
3
) and (
4
)) reduce to
\nabla f(u)=0
.
In this case, Magron, Safey El Din, and Vu
[
39
]
rely on the sum-of-squares strengthenings with gradient ideals by Nie, Demmel, and Sturmfels
[
48
]
to provide an exact method for computing

when the coefficients of

Motivation for the Fritz John conditions.

In nonlinear programming, constraint qualifications are known to be sufficient conditions for the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions.
However, in many recent applications, constraint qualifications have not been satisfied, which leads to limitations of the classical methods using the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions.
Mathematical program with complementarity constraints
[
1
]
is a class of challenging optimization problems that belong to such a situation.
It is because complementarity constraints typically result in the violation of all standard constraint qualifications.
Our method based on the Fritz John conditions has the potential to overcome this limitation as it does not requires any constraint qualification.
We illustrate this on some instances of mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (see Example
3
).

and

(resp.

Contribution.

Our goal in this paper is to design a symbolic algorithm to compute the exact optimal value
f^{\star}
attained by
f
over
S(g)
(problem (
1
)) under mild conditions.
Let
s\in\{1,2,3,4\}
be fixed.
The idea of our method is as follows:

Relying on the computations of Gröbner basis and real radical generators, we obtain univariate polynomials
\hat{h}_{1},\dots,\hat{h}_{r}
whose set of common real zeros is identically the Zariski closure of the image
f(W_{s})
.
Let
d
be the upper bound on the degrees of
f,g_{i},h_{j}
.
Then each
\hat{h}_{t}
has degree not larger than

2\times d^{2^{{O((n+2m)^{2})}^{2^{n+2m}}}}\,.

(12)

Moreover, we prove that
f(W_{s})
coincides with its Zariski closure when the image
f(W_{s})
has finitely many elements.
In this case, we get all elements of
f(W_{s})
by solving the system of the univariate polynomials
\hat{h}_{1}=\dots=\hat{h}_{r}=0
.
By (
11
),
f^{\star}
is the smallest one among these elements.

It remains to provide sufficient conditions for
f(W_{s})
to be finite.
Thanks to our previous works in
[
40
,
41
]
, the image
f(W_{1})
(resp.
f(W_{2})
) is finite when the set of critical points
C(g)
(resp.
C^{+}(g)
) defined later in (
24
) (resp. (
25
)) is finite.
Moreover, if the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions hold for problem (
1
) at some global minimizer, the images
f(W_{3})
and
f(W_{4})
are finite.

Due to its high computational cost (
12
), we illustrate our method with some modest-sized examples.

2.1
Ideals

Denote by
\Sigma^{2}[x]
(resp.
\Sigma^{2}_{r}[x]
) the cone of sum of squares of polynomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
(resp.
{\mathbb{R}}_{r}[x]
).
Given
h_{1},\dots,h_{l}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, we call
I(h)
the ideal generated by
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
, if

I(h):=\sum_{j=1}^{l}h_{j}{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,.

(13)

Note that the intersection of two ideals is also an ideal.
The real radical of an ideal
I(h)
, denoted by
\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)}
, is defined as

{\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)}}=\{f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,:\,\exists m\in{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,-f^{2m}\in\Sigma^{2}[x]+I(h)\}\,.

(14)

Krivine–Stengle’s Nichtnegativstellensätze
[
34
]
imply that

\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)}=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,:\,p=0\text{ on }V(h)\}\,.

(15)

We say that
I(h)
is real radical if
I(h)=\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I(h)}
.

Remark 1
.

Becker and Neuhaus provide a method in
[
3
,
45
]
to compute a generator of the real radical
\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I}
with a given generator of an ideal
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
The ideal is as follows: They first reduce the general situation to the univariate case by using factorization and quantifier elimination.
They then handle zero-dimensional ideals after localizing and contracting the one-dimensional ideals.
Thereby the degrees of elements in the generator of
\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I}
are bounded from above by
d^{2^{O(n^{2})}}
if polynomials in generator of
I
have degrees at most
d
.

Safey El Din, Yang, and Zhi provide in
[
18
]
a probabilistic algorithm with complexity
(rnd^{n})^{O(1)}
to compute a generator of
\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I}
from a generator
(h_{1},\dots,h_{r})\subset{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
of
I
.
Another method of Baldi and Mourrain
[
2
]
to compute a generator of
\sqrt[{\mathbb{R}}]{I}
depends on the numerical solutions of moment relaxations.

Given
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
I(A)
denote the vanishing ideal of
A
, i.e.,

I(A)=\{f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,:\,f=0\text{ on }A\}\,.

(16)

Given

being a finite subset of
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, let

stand for the real variety defined by

V(J)=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,p(x)=0\,,\,\forall p\in J\}\,.

(17)

The Zariski closure of a subset

, denoted by

, is the smallest real variety containing

.
By
[

, Proposition 1, Section 4, Chapter 4]
, it holds that

Z(A)=V(I(A))\,.

(18)

2.2
Gröbner basis

Given
p=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{\alpha_{j}}x^{\alpha_{j}}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
such that
p_{\alpha_{j}}\neq 0
and
x^{\alpha_{r}}>\dots>x^{\alpha_{r}}
, according to lex order, we call
p_{\alpha_{r}}x^{\alpha_{r}}
the leading term of
p
, denote by
\LT(p)
.
Given
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x]
being an ideal other than
\{0\}
, we denote by
\LT(I)
the set of leading terms of elements of
I
.

A finite subset
G=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x]
generating an ideal
I
is called a Gröbner basis if

V(\LT(h_{1}),\dots,\LT(h_{r}))=V(\LT(I))
.

Remark 2
.

Buchberger suggests in
[
13
]
an algorithm to compute a Gröbner basis
h^{G}
of an ideal
I
with a given generator
h
(see also F4 and F5 algorithms by Faugère in
[
20
,
21
]
).
For every couple

in
h
, denote by
a_{ij}
the least common multiple of
\LT(h_{i})
and
\LT(h_{j})
.
Buchberger’s algorithm consists of the following steps:

1.

Set
h^{G}:=h
.

2.

Choose two polynomials
h_{i},h_{j}
in
h^{G}
and let

s_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{LT(h_{i})}h_{i}-\frac{a_{ij}}{LT(h_{j})}h_{j}
.

3.

Reduce
s_{ij}
, with the multivariate division algorithm relative to the set
h^{G}
until the result is not further reducible. If the result is non-zero, add it to
h^{G}
.

4.

Repeat Steps 2 and 3.

Assume that each element of the generator
h
has degree at most
d
.
Dubé proves in
[
17
]
that Buchberger’s algorithm has complexity

d^{2^{n+o(1)}}

(19)

to produce the Gröbner basis
h^{G}
, whose elements have degrees at most
2\left({\frac{d^{2}}{2}}+d\right)^{2^{n-2}}
.

To explore recent developments of Groebner basis, we refer the readers to
[
6
,
7
,
5
,
11
,
22
]
.

We restate the elimination theorem (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem 2, Section 1, Chapter 3]
) in the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be an ideal and let

be a Gröbner basis of

w.r.t. lex order where

. Then

, the set

2.3
Semi-algebraic sets

A semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a subset of the form

\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\bigcap_{j=1}^{r_{i}}\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,f_{ij}(x)*_{ij}0\}\,,

(20)

where
f_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and
*_{ij}
is either
>
or
=
.
Equivalently, a semi-algebraic set is a finite union of basic semi-algebraic sets.
We define the dimension of a semi-algebraic subset
S
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, denoted by
\dim(S)
, to be the highest dimension at points at which
S
is a real submanifold.

We recall some properties of semi-algebraic sets in the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

The following statements hold:

1.

The image of a semi-algebraic set under a polynomial is semi-algebraic.

2.

A semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
is a finite union of intervals and points in
{\mathbb{R}}
.

The first statement of Lemma
2
is proved by Korkina and Kushnirenko in
[
33
]
.
The second statement is given in
[
53
, Example 1.1.1]
.

Let
x_{n+1}
be a single-variable independent from
x
.
We provide the following two lemmas which characterize the image of a semi-algebraic set under a polynomial:

Lemma 3
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]
,
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{l})
with
h_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
.
We define the projection

\hat{\pi}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\,,\hskip 9.24994pt(x,x_{n+1})\mapsto\hat{\pi}(x,x_{n+1})=x_{n+1}\,.

(21)

Then it holds that
\hat{\pi}(V(h,x_{n+1}-f))=f(V(h))
.

Proof.

The result follows from the following equivalences:

\begin{array}[]{rl}&a_{n+1}\in f(V(h))\\
\Leftrightarrow&\exists a\in V(h)\,:\,a_{n+1}=f(a)\\
\Leftrightarrow&\exists a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,(a,a_{n+1})\in V(h,x_{n+1}-f)\\
\Leftrightarrow&a_{n+1}\in\hat{\pi}(V(h,x_{n+1}-f))\,.\\
\end{array}

(22)

∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
A
be a closed semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
f
be a polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
. Then
\dim(f(A))=\dim(Z(f(A)))
.
Moreover, if
f(A)
is finite, then
f(A)=Z(f(A))
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2
,
f(A)
is a finite union of intervals and points in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then one of the following two cases occurs:

•

Case 1:
f(V(h))
has non-empty interior. Then
f(V(h))
has dimension one. We also get
Z(f(A))={\mathbb{R}}
, which has dimension one. It implies that
\dim(f(A))=\dim(Z(f(A)))=1
.

•

Case 2:
f(V(h))
is finite. Then it is easily seen that
f(A)=Z(f(A))
, which gives
\dim(f(A))=\dim(Z(f(A)))=0
.

Hence the result follows.
∎

Remark 3
.

The conclusion of Lemma
4
still holds if we assume that

is a semi-algebraic function (see
[

, Section 2.2.1]

2.4
Sets of critical points

Given
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, let
\varphi^{g}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{(n+m)\times m}
be a function associated with
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
defined by

\varphi^{g}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla g(x)\\
\diag(g(x))\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla g_{1}(x)&\dots&\nabla g_{m}(x)\\
g_{1}(x)&\dots&0\\
.&\dots&.\\
0&\dots&g_{m}(x)\end{bmatrix}\,.

(23)

Given a real matrix
A
, we denote by
\rank(A)
the dimension of the vector space generated by the columns of
A
over
{\mathbb{R}}
.
We say that a set
\Omega
is finite if its cardinality is a non-negative integer.
Let
C(g)
be the set of critical points associated with
g
defined by

C(g):=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,\rank(\varphi^{g}(x))<m\}.

(24)

Given a real matrix
A
, we denote by
\rank^{+}(A)
the largest number of columns of
A
whose convex hull over
{\mathbb{R}}
has no zero.
Let
C^{+}(g)
be the set of critical points associated with
g
defined by

C^{+}(g):=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,:\,\rank^{+}(\varphi^{g}(x))<m\}.

(25)

Remark 4
.

Let
\Phi^{g}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a mapping defined by

\Phi^{g}(x,y)=(g_{1}(x)e^{y_{1}},\dots,g_{m}(x)e^{y_{m}})\,.

(26)

Then
\Phi^{g}
is differentiable and the Jacobian of
\Phi^{g}
is of the form

\nabla\Phi^{g}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}e^{y_{1}}\nabla g_{1}(x)&\dots&e^{y_{m}}\nabla g_{m}(x)\\
e^{y_{1}}g_{1}(x)&\dots&0\\
.&\dots&.\\
0&\dots&e^{y_{m}}g_{m}(x)\end{bmatrix}=\varphi^{g}(x)\times\begin{bmatrix}e^{y_{1}}&\dots&0\\
.&\dots&.\\
0&\dots&e^{y_{m}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(27)

It implies that
\rank(\nabla\Phi^{g}(x,y))=\rank(\varphi^{g}(x))
since
e^{y_{j}}\neq 0
.
Note that the standard set of critical points of
\Phi^{g}
is defined by

C_{\Phi^{g}}=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}\,:\,\rank(\nabla\Phi^{g}(x,y))<m\}\,.

(28)

From this, we get
C_{\Phi^{g}}=C(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Thus our set of critical points

is the projection of
C_{\Phi^{g}}
on the linear space spanned by the first
n

The following algorithm allows us to obtain univariate polynomials whose set of common real zeros is identically the Zariski closure of the image of a given real variety under a given polynomial:

•

Input:

and

with

.

•

Output:

with

1.

of the real radical

Compute a Gröbner basis

of

Remark 7

in the input of Algorithm

We apply the elimination theorem to prove the following lemma:

Lemma 6
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
and

with

Proof.

By the first step of Algorithm

.
From this, we get

(31)

since

is a Gröbner basis of

.
Moreover, the elimination theorem (see, e.g.,
[

\hat{h}
is a Gröbner basis of the ideal

.
Let
\hat{\pi}
be as in (

).
The result follows from the following equivalences:

\begin{array}[]{rl}&t\in V(\hat{h})\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in I(\check{h})\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]\,:\,p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]\,:\,p\in I(\check{h})\Rightarrow p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]\,:\,(p=0\text{ on }V(\check{h}))\Rightarrow p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]\,:\,(p=0\text{ on }V(h,x_{n+1}-f))\Rightarrow p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]\,:\,(p=0\text{ on }\hat{\pi}(V(h,x_{n+1}-f)))\Rightarrow p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{n+1}]\,:\,(p=0\text{ on }f(V(h)))\Rightarrow p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\forall p\in I(f(V(h)))\Rightarrow p(t)=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&t\in V(I(f(V(h))))\\
\Leftrightarrow&t\in Z(f(V(h)))\,.\end{array}

(32)

The sixth equivalence relies on Lemma
3

The final equivalence is based on (

∎

\pi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the projection defined by

\pi(x,\lambda)=x\,,\,\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,,\,\forall\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\,.

(38)

Then the following statements hold:

1.

C(g)=\pi(V(h_{\text{sing}}))
and
C^{+}(g)=\pi(V(h_{\text{sing}}^{+}))
.

2.

f(C(g))=f(V(h_{\text{sing}}))
and
f(C^{+}(g))=f(V(h_{\text{sing}}^{+}))
.

3.

S(g)\cap C(g)=\pi((S(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap V(h_{\text{sing}}))
and

S(g)\cap C^{+}(g)=\pi((S(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap V(h_{\text{sing}}^{+}))
.

4.

f(S(g)\cap C(g))=f((S(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap V(h_{\text{sing}}))
and

f(S(g)\cap C^{+}(g))=f((S(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap V(h_{\text{sing}}^{+}))
.

Proof.

The equality
C(g)=\pi(V(h_{\text{sing}}))
holds thanks to the following equivalences:

\begin{array}[]{rl}&x\in C(g)\\
\Leftrightarrow&\rank(\varphi^{g}(x))<m\\
\Leftrightarrow&\exists\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\,:\,\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{2}=1\,,\,\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}\nabla g_{j}(x)=0\,,\,\lambda_{j}g_{j}=0\\
\Leftrightarrow&\exists\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\,:\,(x,\lambda)\in V(h_{\text{sing}})\\
\Leftrightarrow&x\in\pi(V(h_{\text{sing}}))\,.\end{array}

(39)

Note that
C(g)=\pi(V(h_{\text{sing}}))
implies
f(C(g))=f(V(h_{\text{sing}}))
and
S(g)\cap C(g)=\pi((S(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap V(h_{\text{sing}}))
, which yields
f(S(g)\cap C(g))=f((S(g)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap V(h_{\text{sing}}))
.
The remaining cases are handled similarly.
∎
