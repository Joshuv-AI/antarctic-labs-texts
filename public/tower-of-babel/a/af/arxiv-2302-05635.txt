First-Principles Calculation of the Optical Rotatory Power of Periodic Systems: Modern Theory with Modern Functionals
Jacques K. Desmarais, Bernard Kirtman, Michel Rérat

Abstract
An analysis of orbital magnetization in band insulators is provided. It is shown that a previously proposed electronic orbital angular-momentum operator generalizes the “modern theory of orbital magnetization” to include non-local Hamiltonians. Expressions for magnetic transition dipole moments needed for the calculation of optical rotation (OR) and other properties are developed. A variety of issues that arise in this context are critically analyzed. These issues include periodicity of the operators, previously proposed band dispersion terms as well as, if and where needed, evaluation of reciprocal space derivatives of orbital coefficients. Our treatment is used to determine the optical rotatory power of band insulators employing a formulation that accounts for electric dipole - electric quadrupole (DQ), as well as electric dipole-magnetic dipole, contributions. An implementation in the public Crystal program is validated against a model finite system and applied to the α \alpha -quartz mineral through linear-response time-dependent density functional theory with a hybrid functional. The latter calculations confirmed the importance of DQ terms. Agreement against experiment was only possible with i) use of a high quality basis set, ii) inclusion of a fraction of non-local Fock exchange, and iii) account of orbital-relaxation terms in the calculation of response functions.

II
Formalism: Wannier Function Perspective

II.1
Review of Modern Theory for Band Insulators

With infinite periodic systems, the eigenfunctions
|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle
of the single particle Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\epsilon_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(3)

are crystalline orbitals (COs), which may be written in Bloch form as in Eq. (
2
).

A key quantity in the discussion of magnetic properties of periodic systems is the Chern (vector) invariant
\mathbf{C}
:
Thouless et al. 1982

\mathbf{C}=\frac{\imath}{2\pi}\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\ \sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}u_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\wedge|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}u_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(4)

In Eq. (
4
) and elsewhere the superscript occ refers to the occupied manifold while the subscript BZ indicates integration over the first Brillouin zone. As will be discussed below, we are concerned here with systems where
\mathbf{C}
vanishes in the absence of a magnetic field:

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{0}

(5)

In the case of insulating systems with a vanishing
\mathbf{C}
, a unitary transformation of the BFs provides localized Wannier functions (WFs)
|w_{i,\mathbf{g}}\rangle
:

|w_{i,\mathbf{g}}\rangle=\Omega\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\ e^{\imath\mathbf{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{g}\right)}|u_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(6a)

with the inverse transform given by:

|u_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{g}}e^{-\imath\mathbf{k}\cdot\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{g}\right)}|w_{i,\mathbf{g}}\rangle

(6b)

where
\Omega
is the volume of the FBZ and
\mathbf{g}
is a direct lattice vector (with the corresponding sum running over the infinite set). The transformation of Eq. (
6
) is of course, like the orbitals themselves, only determined up to an arbitrary phase
e^{\imath\phi_{i}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)}
. For the moment we set
\phi_{i}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)=0
, its effect being discussed further on.

In the case of band-insulators, the density matrix has exponential decay in direct space,
Pisani et al. 1988
and it is possible to represent the orbitals in well-localized form, as per Eq. (
6a
). This procedure is also applicable to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological insulators.
Soluyanov and Vanderbilt 2011
On the other hand, for metals, the power-law decay of the density matrix means that the localization procedure diverges.
Resta 2006
A similar behavior occurs for Chern insulators, due to the existence of conducting chiral edge states.
Thonhauser and
Vanderbilt 2006

For “Wannier representable” band (or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological) insulators, CTVR write the orbital magnetization of a large, finite sample of
N_{c}
cells cut from the bulk with
N_{b}
orbitals as:
Ceresoli et al. 2006

\mathbf{M}=-\frac{1}{2c\Omega N_{c}}\sum_{i}^{N_{b}}\langle w_{i}|\mathbf{r}\wedge\imath\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]|w_{i}\rangle

(7)

where
c
is the speed of light and the index
\mathbf{g}
has been dropped for general large, finite samples. Eq. (
7
) is justified and computationally convenient for local Hamiltonians, with matrix elements of the velocity operator
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
that read:

\langle w_{i}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}/\imath|w_{i}\rangle=\langle w_{i}|\imath\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]|w_{i}\rangle

(8)

Indeed, substituting Eq. (
8
) into Eq. (
7
),
\mathbf{M}
reduces to the standard first-order perturbation theory expression, in terms of a matrix elements of the electronic angular-momentum operator
\hat{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{r}\wedge\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}/\imath
. At this point, we note that the situation is different, for calculations with a non-local GKS Hamiltonian since the replacement:

-\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]\to\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}

(9)

fails to account for the fact that
\mathbf{r}
does not commute with the non-local terms. Of course, the non-commutativity may be corrected for hybrid functionals, for instance, by an explicit calculation of the commutator
\left[H_{\text{Fock}},\mathbf{r}\right]
with the Fock operator
H_{\text{Fock}}
. However, this is impractical for actual calculations as it involves the evaluation of electron-electron integrals of increased complexity.

In the thermodynamic limit (i.e.
N_{s}/N_{b}\to 0
, with
N_{b}
the number of WFs
w_{i}
centered in the interior of the sample and
N_{s}
the number centered at the surface), using translational symmetry, the contribution to Eq. (
7
) from the bulk WFs becomes:
Ceresoli et al. 2006

\mathbf{M}_{\text{LC}}=-\frac{1}{2c\Omega}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\langle w_{i,\mathbf{0}}|\mathbf{r}\wedge\imath\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]|w_{i,\mathbf{0}}\rangle

(10)

On the other hand, the contribution to Eq. (
7
) from the
N_{s}
surface orbitals reads:
Ceresoli et al. 2006

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\text{IC}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2c\Omega N_{c}}\Big[\sum_{s}^{N_{s}}\langle w_{s}|\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{s}\right)\wedge\imath\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]|w_{s}\rangle

(11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{s}\wedge\langle w_{s}|\imath\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]|w_{s}\rangle\Big]

where, we have used the shorthand notation
\mathbf{r}_{s}=\langle w_{s}|\mathbf{r}|w_{s}\rangle
to denote the centers of the surface WFs. Indeed, in the thermodynamic limit, even though the first term vanishes,
\mathbf{r}_{s}
is large and a non-vanishing contribution from the second term in Eq. (
11
) remains.
Ceresoli et al. 2006
Then, Eq. (
10
) is related to currents circulating in the bulk of the solid (the so-called local-circulation, LC, term), while Eq. (
11
) originates from current circulating at the surface of the solid (the itinerant-circulation, IC, term). Remarkably, however, both contributions may be rewritten solely in terms of WFs centered in the bulk.

In reciprocal space, the final expressions are, for the LC term:
Ceresoli et al. 2006

\mathbf{M}_{\text{LC}}=\frac{1}{2c}\Re\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}u_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\wedge H_{\mathbf{k}}/\imath|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}u_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(12a)

and for the IC term:
Ceresoli et al. 2006

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\text{IC}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c}\Re\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}u_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\wedge|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}u_{l,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(12b)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\langle u_{l,\mathbf{k}}|H_{\mathbf{k}}/\imath|u_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

where:

H_{\mathbf{k}}=e^{-\imath\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}He^{\imath\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

(13)

The total orbital-magnetization is the sum of the LC and IC contributions:
Ceresoli et al. 2006

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}_{\text{LC}}+\mathbf{M}_{\text{IC}}

(14)

In deriving Eq. (
12b
), CTVR insert
\mathbbm{1}=\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}|w_{l}\rangle\langle w_{l}|
into Eq. (
11
), yielding the relation:

\langle w_{s}|\imath\left[H,\mathbf{r}\right]|w_{s}\rangle=2\Re\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}\langle w_{s}|\mathbf{r}|w_{l}\rangle\langle w_{l}|H/\imath|w_{s}\rangle

(15)

Here we note that for calculations in a finite basis set, it is then necessary to remove the identity, from resulting expressions. In other words, for finite basis set calculations it is necessary to make the replacement:

\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}|w_{l}\rangle\langle w_{l}|\to\mathbbm{1}

(16)

in Eq. (
12b

In the following, we develop expressions that are generally valid for non-local Hamiltonians and a finite basis set, in a manner that achieves the replacements warranted by Eqs. (
9

16

Springborg et al. 2017

III
Formalism: Perturbation Theory Perspective

The result obtained in section
II
(for the magnetization in terms of a “Hermitized angular momentum” operator) that was derived using Wannier functions may alternatively be developed through perturbation theory (PT). The latter approach is similar to the derivation presented by SVXN in generalizing the CTVR treatment to metals and Chern insulators.
Shi et al. 2007
This approach is also more convenient for extension to higher-order properties.

We begin by considering a transition matrix element of the first-order interaction Hamiltonian for the orbital response to a magnetic field which, in the CO basis, is:

-\frac{\imath}{c}\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rangle

27

where

is the (constant) amplitude of the magnetic field with wavevector

and frequency

(

\mathbf{B}=\left[B_{x},0,0\right]^{T}

\displaystyle\frac{B_{0x}}{cq^{2}}\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|e^{\imath\left(q_{y}r_{y}+q_{z}r_{z}\right)}\left(q_{y}\nabla_{z}-q_{z}\nabla_{y}\right)|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rangle

(34)

IV
Effect of the Orbital Phases and Gauge-Origin Invariance

It was mentionned in section
II.1
, that the complex Bloch orbitals and WFs are only determined up to an arbitrary phase
e^{\imath\phi_{i}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)}
. From Eq. (
26b
), the effect on the orbital magnetization of changing the phase of the Bloch orbitals is to make the replacement:

\mathbf{r}\to\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}\phi_{i}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)

(36)

in matrix elements of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
. Therefore, changing the phase of
|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle
has exactly the same effect as changing the gauge-origin by an amount
-\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}\phi_{i}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
for the matrix element involving orbital
|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle
. Eq. (
36
) has already been pointed out by SMK. The particular choice of the phase, then, becomes irrelevant for gauge-origin invariant calculations (for instance, at the complete basis set, CBS, limit, see Eq. (
39
) below). In our calculations, we set the term
-\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}\phi_{i}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
to zero. Generally speaking, an approximate calculation may depend on the gauge-origin, and, by extension on the choice of the phase. In the particular case of OR, Rérat and Kirtman have shown that the trace and optic axis component of the OR tensor are gauge-origin invariant if the velocity operator is used for the electric field Hamiltonian.
Rérat and Kirtman 2021

Another criterion of relevance for Eq. (
26
) is the behaviour of the “angular-momentum” operator under a lattice translation from
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)
to
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\left(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{g}\right)
. A periodic operator
\hat{O}
satisfies
\hat{O}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)=\hat{O}\left(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{g}\right)
. We can work out the effect of translation by a lattice vector
\mathbf{g}
by returning to Eq. (
26
), from which we find:

-\frac{1}{4c}\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\left(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{g}+\imath\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}\right)\wedge\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}/\imath|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle+\text{H.c.}

(37)

The translation therefore leads to the (generally) non-vanishing contribution:

-\frac{1}{4c}\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\mathbf{g}\wedge\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}/\imath|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle+\text{H.c.}

(38)

and, in the general case, we obtain
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)\neq\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\left(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{g}\right)
Of course, the translation is equivalent to changing the gauge-origin by
\mathbf{g}
. Then, just like in the case of gauge-origin,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
becomes periodic if the orbitals are exact (i.e. at the CBS limit). Indeed, at the CBS limit, we may insert Eq. (
16
), as well as Eq. (
9
) into Eq. (
26
) yielding:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4c}\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\left(\mathbf{r}+\imath\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}\right)|\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(39)

\displaystyle\wedge

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}/\imath|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle+\text{H.c.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4c}\int_{\text{BZ}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\frac{\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}|\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}\rangle}{\epsilon_{i,\mathbf{k}}-\epsilon_{l,\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle\wedge

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}/\imath|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle+\text{H.c.}

which is obviously both periodic and gauge-origin invariant, and is also independent on the choice of
-\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}\phi_{i}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
.

Because
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
is only periodic at the CBS limit, in practice, exact comparison against large finite systems can only be expected with a large basis set. Moreover, the usual reciprocity between sampling of reciprocal space (i.e. number of
\mathbf{k}
points to sample the FBZ) and direct-space (i.e. size of the supercell expansion) is only verified at the CBS limit. In section
VII
we present numerical results that explicitly demonstrate this behaviour.

In the case of finite systems, it has very recently been shown that a finite basis set of London atomic orbitals yields the same translational behaviour as the exact orbitals.
Peters et al. 2022
Whether a similar relationship can be developed for infinite, periodic systems remains to be demonstrated.

For OR, following Stephen,

\displaystyle\left[\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\mathbf{p}|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\wedge\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\dagger}}{2}\vee\mathbf{p}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\right]_{u}

for the rotatory strengths, where

\displaystyle\text{DQ}+\text{DD}=\left[\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\mathbf{p}|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\wedge\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\dagger}}{2}\vee\mathbf{p}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\right]_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v,w}\epsilon_{u,v,w}\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|p_{v}|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle\Big[\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\frac{\hat{\Omega}_{u}+\hat{\Omega}^{\dagger}_{u}}{2}p_{w}+p_{u}\frac{\hat{\Omega}_{w}+\hat{\Omega}^{\dagger}_{w}}{2}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\frac{\hat{\Omega}_{u}+\hat{\Omega}^{\dagger}_{u}}{2}p_{w}-p_{u}\frac{\hat{\Omega}_{w}+\hat{\Omega}^{\dagger}_{w}}{2}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\Big]

With the DQ term (i.e. the first term in square brackets) being traceless, orientational averaging (summing over

\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\mathbf{p}|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\cdot\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\dagger}}{2}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

), as well as Eq. (

V.1
), yielding:

\displaystyle\text{DQ}+\text{DD}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}\Big[-\langle\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}|\mathbf{p}|\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\wedge\frac{\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla_{r}}|\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}\rangle}{\epsilon_{i,\mathbf{k}}-\epsilon_{l,\mathbf{k}}}

(47)

\displaystyle\vee

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}|\mathbf{p}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\Big]_{u}+\text{H.c.}

In the electronic supporting information (ESI) we develop a formula for the diagonal elements of the OR tensor
\beta_{u}
(including both magnetic-dipole as well as electric-quadrupole contributions) based on the operators of Eq. (
V.1

\boldsymbol{\beta}
relates the components of the magnetic-dipole plus electric-quadrupole moments induced by the electric field
\boldsymbol{\eta}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

Condon 1937

\boldsymbol{\eta}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\left(t,\omega\right)

For practical calculations, we expand the COs in a finite set of functions
\varphi_{\mu,\mathbf{k}}
labelled by the index
\mu

|\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\sum_{\mu}C_{\mu,l}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)|\varphi_{\mu,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(49)

with the CO coefficients
C_{\mu,l}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
being determined from the solution of the field-free GKS-DFT equations. To write a compact expression for
\beta_{u}
, it is convenient to use an analytical expression for the derivatives of the coefficients
C_{\mu,l}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
in terms of a matrix
\mathbf{Q}
:
Otto et al. 1999

\boldsymbol{\nabla_{k}}C_{\mu,l}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)=\sum_{l^{\prime}}^{\text{all}}\mathbf{Q}_{l^{\prime},l}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)C_{\mu,l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)

Similarly, the
u
-component electric-field perturbed coefficients
C_{\mu,i}^{(u),\pm}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)

\mathbf{U}
(in the non-canonical treatment only anti-Hermitian virt-occ interbank elements

Karna and Dupuis 1991

In Eq. (
50
)
\mathbf{Q}
is determined from the derivative of the GKS equation and orthonormality condition:
Otto et al. 1999

\mathbf{Q}_{l,l^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\mathbf{K}_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)-\epsilon_{l^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{R}_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)}{\epsilon_{l^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}-\epsilon_{l,\mathbf{k}}}

(52a)

for off-diagonal
l\neq l^{\prime}
elements and:

\Re\left[\mathbf{Q}_{l,l}(\mathbf{k})\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{l,l}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)

(52b)

for diagonal elements. Here
\mathbf{K}_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
and
\mathbf{R}_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
are the
\mathbf{k}
-derivatives of the GKS-DFT Hamiltonian and overlap matrices, respectively, for fixed CO coefficients. The imaginary part of
\mathbf{Q}_{l,l}(\mathbf{k})

\phi\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
of Eq. (
2
),
Bishop et al. 2001
which we set to zero, as discussed in section
IV
.

In our implementation, we work with the Hermitian quantity:

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{l,l^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{Q}_{l,l^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)

(53)

In terms of these coefficients, the final formula for

\displaystyle\beta_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega}\text{P.V.}\int^{\prime}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{i}^{\text{occ}}\sum_{a}^{\text{virt}}\Im\Bigg[\left(\mathbf{U}_{ai}^{+}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)-\mathbf{U}_{ai}^{-}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right)

(54)

\displaystyle\wedge

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ia}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)+\mathbf{q}_{ia}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right)\Bigg]_{u}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{ia}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l}^{\text{all}}\Big[\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{l,i}(\mathbf{k})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{l,i}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right)\vee\langle\psi_{a,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{r}|\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{l,a}^{\ast}(\mathbf{k})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{l,a}^{\ast}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)\right)\vee\langle\psi_{l,\mathbf{k}}|\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{r}|\psi_{i,\mathbf{k}}\rangle\Big]

and

In Eq. (

VIII
Conclusions

A previously proposed electronic “angular-momentum” operator is shown to generalize the “modern theory of orbital magnetization” to non-local Hamiltonians (e.g. hybrid exchange-correlation functionals of generalized Kohn-Sham theory). A rigorous development of the theory demonstrates that previously suggested “band dispersion” terms, as well as terms involving reciprocal space derivatives of the perturbed wave function, can be avoided. Finally, it is shown that while the “angular momentum” operator is (in principle) periodic at the complete basis set limit, it is not so in the general case.

The formalism is applied to calculating the optical rotatory power (OR) of band insulators in the public
Crystal
program, where expressions are developed in terms of the electric dipole - electric quadrupole (DQ) as well as the electric dipole - magnetic dipole (DD) contributions. For effective calculations, a strategy is developed to deal with quasi-degeneracies in obtaining derivatives of the orbital coefficients with respect to the wave-vector. Our implementation is validated by comparison with a model finite system and we report on an application to the
\alpha
-quartz mineral, with linear-response time-dependent density functional theory calculations, that employ a hybrid as well as a non-hybrid functional. This application confirms the importance of DQ terms for OR calculations in the solid state. In the case of
\alpha
-quartz, agreement versus experiment was only possible with an explicit account of i) use of a high quality basis set ii) inclusion of a fraction of non-local exact exchange in the exchange-correlation functional and iii) taking account of orbital-relaxation.

For an implementation that is less dependent on the number of
\mathbf{k}
points used to sample reciprocal space, as well as other numerical parameters, we will present in the future an improved scheme for the treatment of quasi-degeneracies in determining derivatives of the orbital coefficients with respect to the wave-vector. This will be done in conjunction with new applications for periodic systems of interest.

\iiint_{V}dV\ \boldsymbol{\nabla}\wedge\mathbf{F}=\oiint_{S}d\mathcal{S}\ \mathbf{n}_{k}\wedge\mathbf{F}

C.3

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

\mathcal{S}
