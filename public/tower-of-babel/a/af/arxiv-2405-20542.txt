On the Connection Between Non-negative Matrix Factorization and Latent Dirichlet Allocation
Benedikt Geiger, Peter J. Park

Abstract
Non-negative matrix factorization with the generalized Kullback–Leibler divergence (NMF) and latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) are two popular approaches for dimensionality reduction of non-negative data. Here, we show that NMF with ℓ 1 \ell_{1} normalization constraints on the columns of both matrices of the decomposition and a Dirichlet prior on the columns of one matrix is equivalent to LDA. To show this, we demonstrate that explicitly accounting for the scaling ambiguity of NMF by adding ℓ 1 \ell_{1} normalization constraints to the optimization problem allows a joint update of both matrices in the widely used multiplicative updates (MU) algorithm. When both of the matrices are normalized, the joint MU algorithm leads to probabilistic latent semantic analysis (PLSA), which is LDA without a Dirichlet prior. Our approach of deriving joint updates for NMF also reveals that a Lasso penalty on one matrix together with an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} normalization constraint on the other matrix is insufficient to induce any sparsity.



1
Introduction

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) and latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) are two prominent techniques for unsupervised machine learning and data analysis. Both approaches decompose non-negative data into an interpretable parts-based representation and have been used successfully across a wide range of areas including topic modeling, image and audio signal processing, recommendation systems, and bioinformatics
[
24
,
46
,
17
]
. However, despite their conceptual similarities and widespread use, the relationship between NMF and LDA remains underexplored. Although partial results exist, prior work is written from a probabilistic perspective, and empirical comparisons often fall short to appreciate the depth of their connection.

Non-negative matrix factorization
X\approx WH
with an appropriately chosen loss function is commonly viewed as a constrained optimization problem, with linear algebra algorithms employed to solve it. In the case of the widely used Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence loss, the most popular approach for solving the optimization problem is the so-called multiplicative updates approach
[
17
]
. Originally derived by Richardson
[
39
]
and Lucy
[
31
]
in the context of deconvolution, it was rediscovered and applied to NMF in the seminal work of Lee and Seung
[
28
,
27
]
. The algorithm alternates between updating each of the matrices
W
and
H
of the factorization in a multiplicative manner. Latent Dirichlet allocation was introduced as a generative statistical model of text data to learn the underlying topics, topic proportions of documents, and probabilistic topic assignments of individual words
[
4
]
. As a bag-of-words model, LDA assumes that the order of the words in each document does not play a role, and only the word-document count matrix
X
of a corpus is used during training and inference. The generative process of LDA extends that of its predecessor probabilistic latent semantic analysis (PLSA)
[
23
,
22
]
by adding a Dirichlet prior to the topic proportions. Thus, a solution of PLSA can also be interpreted as a maximum
a posteriori
estimate of LDA under a uniform Dirichlet prior
[
18
]
.

In addition to these connections to LDA, PLSA is well-known to be closely related to NMF. For normalized inputs
X
, fixed points of the PLSA algorithm can be mapped to fixed points of the MU algorithm of NMF with the KL divergence, and
vice versa

[
16
]
. The identical result has also been shown for global maxima of the PLSA likelihood and global minima of the KL loss of NMF
[
12
]
. Noticing similarities between the update rules and underlying generative processes of NMF, PLSA, and LDA dates back to earlier work on unified frameworks for probabilistic matrix decomposition
[
5
,
6
]
. Various probabilistic latent variable models with joint parameter updates closely resembling the update rules of NMF have also been studied in
[
41
,
42
,
47
]
. However, an important detail frequently overlooked in the literature is that PLSA
jointly
updates the topic and topic proportion matrices, whereas the MU algorithm of NMF
sequentially
updates one matrix given the other.

In this paper, we reveal a deeper connection between NMF and topic models like PLSA and LDA. We solve NMF with additional normalization constraints, and show that this leads to algorithms resembling or identical to the PLSA algorithm. Our main contributions are the following:

1.

For NMF with the KL divergence, adding normalization constraints to the columns of one or both matrices of the decomposition makes it possible to derive
joint
update rules for both matrices. The obtained algorithms save one matrix multiplication per iteration.

2.

NMF with the KL divergence and additional normalization constraints on the columns of both matrices is equivalent to PLSA. Both the objective function and the canonical joint update rules are identical. In contrast to the existing literature, we establish an algorithmic equivalence, and our result neither requires normalizing the input matrix
X
nor transforming the trained model parameters.

3.

With a Dirichlet prior on the columns of the matrix corresponding to the topic proportions, NMF with the KL divergence and additional normalization constraints on the columns of both matrices is equivalent to LDA. Both the objective function and the canonical variational inference algorithm are identical.

4.

Joint update equations can be derived to speed up and better understand variations of NMF. We demonstrate this by revisiting sparse NMF with the KL divergence
[
30
]
.

Joint update rules for NMF have recently been introduced in the more general case of
\beta
-divergence loss functions
[
33
]
. Both in
[
33
]
and in our work, the results are based on a majorization-minimization scheme in which an upper bound of the objective function is constructed and minimized in every iteration
[
43
]
. The approach differs from previous work by jointly considering both matrices of the decomposition instead of constructing an upper bound for one of the two matrices at a time
[
15
,
36
]
. In contrast to
[
33
]
, however, the additional normalizations allow us to derive a closed-form expression for the optimum of the upper bound.

That NMF with Dirichlet priors on the columns of
both
matrices of the decomposition leads to LDA with Dirichlet smoothing on the topics was briefly mentioned in
[
49
,
48
]
and suggested in
[
9
, Section 3.5]
. By revisiting the connection between PLSA and NMF, our perspective emphasizes the role of the normalization constraints rather than the Dirichlet distribution giving rise to the connections between generative models and algorithms.

Bayesian NMF.

, obtain uncertainty estimates

, or model bounded support data

,

,

,

,

. The main reason for this choice is the analytical convenience resulting from the Gamma distribution being the conjugate prior of the Poisson distribution. The obtained Gamma–Poisson model with fixed prior parameters has been shown to be related to LDA: When

3
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, our terminology draws inspiration from the topic modeling literature. That is, when considering the non-negative matrix decomposition
X\approx WH
, we use the terms (unnormalized) topic matrix to refer to
W
and topic weights to refer to
H
. When additional normalization constraints are in place, we use (normalized) topic matrix and topic proportions instead. We use the notation
\mathbb{R}_{+}
to refer to the non-negative real numbers, and for any positive integer
M
, we denote the
M-1
dimensional standard simplex by
\Delta_{M-1}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M}\,|\,\sum_{m}y_{m}=1\}
. When using the abbreviation NMF, we refer to non-negative matrix factorization with the KL divergence unless noted otherwise.

In the context of topic modelling, the goal of NMF, PLSA, and LDA is to identify the underlying topics in a corpus of documents and to represent each document as an additive combination of the learned topics. All three methods are bag-of-words models—they assume that learning the decomposition does not require taking the order of words in the documents into account. Therefore, the input of the algorithms is the term-document matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times D}_{+}
, where each entry
x_{vd}
is the number of times the term
v
occurs in document
d
. The number of topics
K
is a hyperparameter of all three approaches.

3.1
Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)

NMF decomposes the term-document matrix
X
into two non-negative matrices
W\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{V\times K}
and
H\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times D}
. The columns of
W
and
H
can then be interpreted as the unnormalized topics and topic weights of the documents, respectively, and these parameters are learned by minimizing an appropriate “distance“
D(X\,||\,WH)
between the data matrix and its reconstruction. A common choice is the (generalized) Kullback–Leibler divergence

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)=\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log\frac{x_{vd}}{(WH)_{vd}}-x_{vd}+(WH)_{vd},

(1)

which leads to the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{W\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{V\times K},\,H\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times D}}D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH).

(2)

Algorithms to solve (
2
) typically alternate between updating each of the matrices
W
and
H
while fixing the other one
[
19
]
. The motivation for this approach is two-fold
[
17
]
. First, the factorization
X\approx WH
is symmetric to
X^{T}\approx H^{T}W^{T}
. An update rule for one of the matrices thus immediately yields an update rule for the other. Second, the optimization problems induced by fixing each of the two matrices in (
2
) are convex. The most common algorithm consists of multiplicatively updating each matrix iteratively until convergence (see
Algorithm

1
). It was popularized by Lee and Seung, who demonstrated the potential of learning parts-based representations and proved the monotonic convergence of the objective function
[
27
,
28
,
46
]
. The key idea of their derivation is to construct a well-behaved function
G(H,H^{\prime})
for
F(H)=D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)
such that

\begin{split}G(H,H^{\prime})&\geq F(H)\quad\text{for all }H,\,H^{\prime},\\
G(H^{\prime},H^{\prime})&=F(H^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }H^{\prime}.\end{split}

(3)

Once such an
auxiliary function

G
is found, the objective function
F
can be shown to be non-increasing by updating an iterate
H^{(n)}
to the argument of the minimum of
H\mapsto G(H,H^{(n)})
(see
Appendix

A
). While the perspective on (
1
) and the construction of the auxiliary function in
[
28
]
via Jensen’s inequality is entirely non-probabilistic, there is also a probabilistic framework for
Algorithm

1

[
8
,
14
]
. Assuming conditional independence of the counts
X_{vd}
, the model
X_{vd}\sim\Poisson(WH)_{vd}
leads to a log-likelihood identical to the negative KL divergence (
1
) (up to a constant). By augmenting the generative process to a latent variable model via

z_{vkd}\sim\Poisson(w_{vk}h_{kd}),\quad X_{vd}=\sum_{k}z_{vkd},

(4)

a standard expectation-maximization (EM)
[
10
]
procedure yields a
joint auxiliary function

G
for the model parameters
(W,H)
, i.e.,

\begin{split}G((W,H),(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}))&\geq D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)\quad\text{for all }(W,H),\,(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}),\\
G((W^{\prime},H^{\prime}),(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}))&=D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,W^{\prime}H^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}).\end{split}

(5)

With

\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}\coloneqq\frac{w_{vk}^{(n)}h_{kd}^{(n)}}{(W^{(n)}H^{(n)})_{vd}}

(6)

and modulo terms independent of the model parameters, the auxiliary function is given by

G((W,H),(W^{(n)},H^{(n)}))=-\sum_{v,k,d}x_{vd}\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}\log\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}}+\sum_{v,d}(WH)_{vd},

(7)

cf.
[
8
, eq. (18)]
. Although a closed-form joint optimum does not exist,
G
induces auxiliary functions for both individual matrices via

\begin{split}\widetilde{G}(W,W^{(n)})&=G((W,H^{(n)}),(W^{(n)},H^{(n)})),\\
\widetilde{G}(H,H^{(n)})&=G((W^{(n)},H),(W^{(n)},H^{(n)})).\end{split}

(8)

Alternating between optimizing each of the induced auxiliary functions recovers the multiplicative updates from Lee and Seung, revealing that
Algorithm

1
is in fact a generalized EM algorithm.

In practice, it is often desirable to increase the interpretability of the matrices and to eliminate the scaling ambiguity of the factorization caused by
WH=WDD^{-1}H
for any invertible diagonal matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}_{+}
. A common strategy to achieve both is to normalize the columns of
W
to probability distributions and to scale the rows of

accordingly after each iteration or after convergence.

3.2
Probabilistic latent semantic analysis (PLSA)

In PLSA
[
23
]
, the generative process of the term-document matrix is unfolded in the sense that the words are generated individually. The scheme for each of the
n=1,\ldots,N_{d}
words in document
d
is the following:

1.

Sample a latent topic
z_{nd}
with probability
p(z_{nd}=k)=h_{kd}
.

2.

Sample a word
\nu_{nd}
with probability
p(\nu_{nd}=v|z_{nd}=k)=w_{vk}
.

The resulting log-likelihood is given by

\log p(X|W,H)=\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log(WH)_{vd}+c(X),

(9)

where the constant
c(X)
arises from bagging the sequence of words, cf.
[
6

. In the following, we will always ignore this constant because it only depends on the observed counts and does not affect model training. The relevant part of the log-likelihood (
9

\mathcal{L}^{\text{PLSA}}(X|W,H)\coloneqq\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log(WH)_{vd}.

(10)

The algorithm to fit the model parameters is an EM algorithm, described in
[
23
]

(W^{(n)},H^{(n)})
equal to

\displaystyle p(z_{nd}=k|\nu_{nd}=v)^{(n)}=\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}\propto w_{vk}^{(n)}h_{kd}^{(n)},

(11)

the E-step of the derivation naturally leads to an auxiliary function of the negative objective function (
10

G((W,H),(W^{(n)},H^{(n)}))=-\sum_{v,k,d}x_{vd}\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}\log\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}}.

(12)

The minimization of

3.3
Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)

LDA was developed to overcome the issue that the number of model parameters of PLSA grows linearly with the number of documents on which the model is trained
[
4
,
3
]
. To this end, LDA introduces a model parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{>0}
and a Dirichlet prior
h\sim\Dirichlet(\alpha)
that turn the topic proportions into latent variables. The generative process for each document
d
then becomes

1.

Sample the topic proportions
h_{d}\sim\Dirichlet(\alpha).

2.

For each of the
n=1,\ldots,N_{d}
words in document
d
:

(a)

Sample a latent topic
z_{nd}
with probability
p(z_{nd}=k)=h_{kd}
,

(b)

Sample a word
\nu_{nd}
with probability
p(\nu_{nd}=v|z_{nd}=k)=w_{vk}
.

This results in the log-likelihood

\displaystyle\log p(x_{d}|W,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\log\int p(h|\alpha)\prod_{v}(Wh)_{v}^{x_{vd}}\,dh,

(13)

where
p(h|\alpha)
denotes the density of the Dirichlet distribution. The introduction of the Dirichlet prior causes the posterior distribution of the latent variables
(h_{d},z_{d})
to be intractable. Blei et al. therefore resort to variational inference (VI), which is one of the approximate inference algorithms applicable to the LDA model
[
25
,
4
,
1
]
. Following their mean-field approach, the true posterior is approximated with a variational distribution
q
given by

\displaystyle q(h,z|\beta,\phi)=\prod_{d}q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d}),\qquad q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d})=q(h_{d}|\beta_{d})\prod_{n}q(z_{nd}|\phi_{nd}),

(14)

where
\beta_{d}
and
\phi_{nd}
are parameters of a Dirichlet and categorical distribution, respectively. In addition to the model parameters
W
and
\alpha
, these so-called variational parameters are iteratively optimized by the algorithm. Instead of maximizing the exact log-likelihood, the idea of variational inference is to optimize a lower bound given by

\mathcal{L}^{\text{LDA}}(X|W,\alpha,\beta,\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(X,h,z|W,\alpha)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h,z|\beta,\phi)\right].

(15)

Here and in the following,
\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the expectation with respect to the variational distribution. Similar to PLSA, the key insight that reveals algebraic connections to NMF is that the optimal variational approximation of the posterior of the topic assignments
z_{nd}
does not depend on the position
n
, but only on the word
\nu_{nd}
at position
n
, i.e.,
\phi_{nd}
can be reindexed. Together with

\widetilde{h}_{kd}(\beta_{d})\coloneqq\exp\big(\mathbb{E}_{q(h_{d}|\beta_{d})}[\log h_{kd}]\big),

(16)

which can be computed explicitly using the digamma function, the variational lower bound (
15

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\text{LDA}}(X|W,\alpha,\beta,\phi)&=\sum_{v,k,d}x_{vd}\phi_{vkd}\log\frac{w_{vk}\widetilde{h}_{kd}}{\phi_{vkd}}+\sum_{d}\Big(\log\Gamma(\textstyle{\sum}_{k}\alpha_{k})-\log\Gamma(\textstyle{\sum}_{k}\beta_{kd})\Big)\\
&\phantom{=}+\displaystyle\sum_{k,d}\Big(\log\Gamma(\beta_{kd})-\log\Gamma(\alpha_{k})+(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{kd})\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]\Big),\end{split}

(17)

cf.
[
4
, A.1, eq. (15), (16)]
. The VI algorithm is a variational EM procedure. It alternates between optimizing the approximation of the posterior distribution of the latent variables for fixed model parameters (variational E-step) and optimizing the model parameters for a fixed variational distribution (variational M-step). That is, (

(\beta,\phi)
during the variational E-step and with respect to
(W,\alpha)
during the variational M-step. Since there is no closed-form optimum for the parameter of the Dirichlet prior

4
Algorithms for NMF with normalization constraints

We now consider the NMF optimization problem (
2
) with additional normalization constraints on the topics or both the topics and the topic weights. We describe the connections between the original formulation and the sum-constrained optimization problems, and derive NMF algorithms with joint update rules. These new NMF algorithms are based on the observation that a constrained optimization of the joint auxiliary function (
7
) is possible.

More precisely, the two optimization problems we consider are NMF with normalized topics

\displaystyle\min_{W\in\Delta_{V-1}^{K},\,H\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times D}}D_{\text{KL}}(X||WH),

(18)

as well as NMF with both normalized topics and topic proportions

\displaystyle\min_{W\in\Delta_{V-1}^{K},\,H\in\Delta_{K-1}^{D}}D_{\text{KL}}(X||WH).

(19)

At first glance, it might seem unnatural to normalize both matrices
W
and
H
without adjusting the input matrix
X
. However, with both constraints, we have
\sum_{v}(WH)_{vd}=1
, and the objective function of (
19
) becomes

-\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log(WH)_{vd}=-\sum_{d}\Big(\sum_{v^{\prime}}x_{v^{\prime}d}\Big)\sum_{v}\frac{x_{vd}}{\sum_{v^{\prime}}x_{v^{\prime}d}}\log(Wh_{d})_{v}.

(20)

The inner sum corresponds to the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the empirical distribution induced by the observed counts
(x_{vd})_{v}
and the model distribution
(Wh_{d})_{v}

[
22
, eq. (10)]
. In particular, optimization problem (
19
) and PLSA have the same objective function. Both models project the samples onto the simplex spanned by the topics based on the KL divergence. The following lemmas clarify the connections between all three NMF problems with different normalization constraints.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(W,H)
be a solution of the standard NMF optimization problem (
2
) and let
\lambda_{k}=\sum_{v}w_{vk}
. Then
(\widetilde{W},\widetilde{H})
with
\widetilde{w}_{vk}=w_{vk}/\lambda_{k}
and
\widetilde{h}_{kd}=\lambda_{k}h_{kd}
is a solution of NMF with a normalization constraint on
W
(
18
). Conversely, every solution of (
18
) is a solution of (
2
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(W,H)
be a solution of NMF with a normalization constraint on
W
(
18
) and let
\lambda_{d}=\sum_{v}x_{vd}
. Then
(W,\widetilde{H})
with
\widetilde{h}_{kd}=h_{kd}/\lambda_{d}
is a solution of NMF with a normalization constraint on both
W
and
H
(
19
).
Conversely, let
(W,H)
be a solution of (
19
). Then
(W,\widetilde{H})
with
\widetilde{h}_{kd}=\lambda_{d}h_{kd}
is a solution of (
18
).

Lemma

4.1
follows directly from
WH=WDD^{-1}H
for any invertible non-negative diagonal matrix
D
and the proof of
Lemma

4.2
is deferred to
Appendix

B
. Taken together, these two results show that a solution of one of the three NMF problems (
2
), (
18
) or (
19
) can be readily converted into a solution of one of the other optimization problems. In fact, one can also see that an analogous result holds for the fixed points of their MU algorithms (see below).

Contrary to intuition, the additional normalization constraints simplify the NMF optimization problem. Recall that the joint auxiliary function (
7
) of
D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)
is given by

\displaystyle G((W,H),(W^{(n)},H^{(n)}))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v,k,d}x_{vd}\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}\log\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi_{vkd}^{(n)}}+\sum_{v,d}(WH)_{vd}.

Under the constraints
w_{k}\in\Delta_{V-1}
, the second summand reduces to
\sum_{k,d}h_{kd}
and
G
can be jointly optimized in all model parameters
(W,H)
. Using Lagrange multipliers, we obtain the joint multiplicative update equations given in
Algorithm

4
(see
Appendix

B
for details). When additionally imposing
h_{d}\in\Delta_{K-1}
, the relevant summands of the auxiliary function are identical to the auxiliary function (
12
) of PLSA. Accordingly, the canonical multiplicative algorithm for (
19
) is the PLSA algorithm, cf.
Algorithms

6
and
2
. The two models are equivalent both in the sense that their optimization problems are the same and in the sense that their MU algorithms coincide.

Although it is also possible to derive update rules that alternate between updating
W
and
H
using the induced auxiliary functions (
8
), the joint update equations have one major advantage. They do not require
WH
to be recomputed after updating one of the two matrices, which reduces the number of matrix multiplication per iteration from four to three. From a theoretical perspective, the joint updates turn NMF from a generalized EM algorithm into an EM algorithm. Another interesting observation is that the generative process (
4

Connection between NMF and LDA

The previous section revealed the equivalence of NMF with additional normalization constraints and PLSA. Since the only difference between PLSA and LDA is the Dirichlet prior on the topic proportions, it is natural to wonder whether NMF with normalization constraints and a Dirichlet prior on the columns of

With the normalization constraints

on the topic matrix

1.

.

2.

Sample the topic contributions
z_{vkd}\sim\Poisson(w_{vk}h_{kd})
.

3.

X_{vd}=\sum_{k}z_{vkd}

Denoting the slice

of the latent tensor

and the fiber

\displaystyle q(h,z|\beta,\phi)=\prod_{d}q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d}),\qquad q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d})=q(h_{d}|\beta_{d})\prod_{v}q(z_{vd}|x_{vd},\phi_{vd}),

(21)

where
\beta_{d}
and

are Dirichlet and multinomial parameters, respectively. With the joint likelihood of the observed and latent variables of a single document being equal to

p(x_{d},h_{d},z_{d}|W,\alpha)=\Big(\prod_{v}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{k}z_{vkd}=x_{vd}}\Big)p(h_{d}|\alpha)\prod_{v,k}p(z_{vkd}|W,h_{d}),

the relevant terms of the variational lower bound of the Dirichlet–Poisson model with normalized topic matrix

Sparse NMF

In sparse NMF, a penalty on
H
is added to decrease the number of topics used in the reconstruction of each document. This is especially useful when the number of topics is high, as it can prevent overfitting and improve the interpretability of
W

[
26
]
. For a hyperparameter
\lambda>0
, the objective function of
\ell_{1}
-penalized NMF is given by

D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)+\lambda\|H\|_{1}.

(23)

An important technical difficulty of sparse NMF is that the scaling ambiguity of the matrix product
WH
in combination with a penalty on
H
would cause
H
to converge to zero and
W
to blow up unless further constraints are imposed. For this reason, the columns of
W
are usually required to be normalized, and the optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{W\in\Delta_{V-1}^{K},\,H\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times D}}D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)+\lambda\|H\|_{1}.

(24)

With the
\ell_{1}
normalization constraints on the columns of
W
in place, the results from
Section

4
can be readily applied to obtain joint update rules to solve (
24
). Similar to before, the objective function value can be shown to be non-increasing under the following joint updates:

\displaystyle w^{(n+1)}_{vk}\propto w^{(n)}_{vk}\sum_{d}x_{vd}\frac{h^{(n)}_{kd}}{(W^{(n)}H^{(n)})_{vd}},\qquad h^{(n+1)}_{kd}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}h^{(n)}_{kd}\sum_{v}x_{vd}\frac{w^{(n)}_{vk}}{(W^{(n)}H^{(n)})_{vd}}.

(25)

The only difference between
\ell_{1}
-penalized NMF and NMF without a penalty (
18
) is that the topic weights
H
are scaled by a factor of
\frac{1}{1+\lambda}
, cf.
Algorithm

4
. Surprisingly, however, this holds true not only for the first, but for
every
iteration of the algorithm. Our observation is not a consequence of the algorithmic approach, but a property of the optimization problem itself:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(W,H)
be a solution of NMF with an
\ell_{1}
normalization constraint on
W
(
18
) and let
\lambda>0
. Then
(W,\tfrac{1}{1+\lambda}H)
is a solution of Lasso-penalized NMF with an
\ell_{1}
normalization constraint (
24
). Conversely, let
(W,H)
be a solution of (
24
). Then
(W,(1+\lambda)H)
is a solution of (
18
).

A proof by contradiction is provided in
Appendix

F
. The Lasso penalty being insufficient to induce sparse topic weights
H
has, to the best of our knowledge, not been reported in the literature
[
34
]
. Interestingly, however, replacing the
\ell_{1}
normalization constraints on the columns of
W
with
\ell_{2}
normalization constraints and using the heuristic gradient-based algorithm from
[
26
]
leads to sparsity
[
29
, Section IV]
. In fact, any normalization constraints on the columns of
W
and penalty terms on
H
can be proven to act as competing regularizers on the two matrices (see
Appendix

F
). Together with
Lemma

6.1
, this demonstrates that the effect on the sparsity of the model parameters not only depends on the penalty on
H
, but also on the choice of the normalization constraint on
W
and the reconstruction error.

7
Conclusion

This paper has examined NMF with normalization constraints on the columns of one or both matrices of the decomposition. By taking these constraints into account explicitly during the majorization-minimization scheme, it became possible to derive joint update rules that speed up model training significantly. The joint update rules further bridge the gap between NMF optimization problems and algorithms from the topic modeling literature such as PLSA and LDA. Leveraging these connections, we have demonstrated that different latent structures of equivalent generative models can highlight complementary aspects of the same model.

The existing theoretical and applied research on other aspects and extensions of NMF or LDA is beyond the scope of our work. However, we anticipate that the outlined connections are not limited to the classical full-batch variational inference algorithms of these base models. Regarding NMF, directly resolving the scaling ambiguity in the case of the Kullback–Leibler divergence allowed us to re-examine the Lasso penalty for sparse NMF and might also be helpful in answering questions on the convergence of the model parameters
[
2
]
. An interesting but ambitious direction would be to extend these ideas to more general loss functions.

Appendix A
Auxiliary functions for NMF

We give a brief overview of the fundamental idea of majorization-minimization algorithms and the construction of auxiliary functions for NMF with the KL divergence. This section is based on
[
43
,
28
,
17
]
.

Consider the optimization problem

\min_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}F(Y)

for
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M}
and a function
F\colon\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
Within the context of the majorization-minimization framework, the strategy to minimize
F
consists of two-steps. First, an auxiliary function
G\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle G(Y,Y^{\prime})\geq F(Y),

\displaystyle G(Y^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=F(Y^{\prime})

for all
Y,Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
is constructed. The majorizer
G
of
F
is then minimized in every iteration, that is,

Y^{(n+1)}=\argmin_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}G(Y,Y^{(n)}).

Indeed, this procedure yields a non-increasing objective function value due to

F(Y^{(n+1)})\leq G(Y^{(n+1)},Y^{(n)})\leq G(Y^{(n)},Y^{(n)})=F(Y^{(n)}).

In the case of NMF with the KL divergence, the goal is to decompose
X\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{V\times D}
into the product of two non-negative matrices
W\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{V\times K}
and
H\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times D}
by minimizing the reconstruction error

D_{\text{KL}}(X\,||\,WH)=\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log\frac{x_{vd}}{(WH)_{vd}}-x_{vd}+(WH)_{vd}.

Note that it is equivalent to minimize

F(W,H)=-\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log(WH)_{vd}+(WH)_{vd}.

For simplicity, we will therefore often refer to
D_{\text{KL}}
and
F
interchangeably. An auxiliary function of
D_{\text{KL}}
can now be constructed using the following two well-known results.

Lemma A.1

(
[
28
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Consider the function
F(W,H)=-\log(WH)_{vd}
for two indices
v,d
. Then the function
G
defined by

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{w^{\prime}_{vk}h^{\prime}_{kd}}{(W^{\prime}H^{\prime})_{vd}},

\displaystyle G((W,H),(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}\log\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}}

is an auxiliary function of
F.

Proof.

The equality
G((W^{\prime},H^{\prime}),(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}))=F(W^{\prime},H^{\prime})
is easy to verify and Jensen’s inequality implies

\log(WH)_{vd}=\log\sum_{k}\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}}\geq\sum_{k}\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}\log\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}}.

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Consider finitely many function
F_{i}\colon\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with auxiliary functions
G_{i},
and let
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then
G=\sum_{i}c_{i}G_{i}
is an auxiliary function of
F=\sum_{i}c_{i}F_{i}.

Proof.

Follows from the definition of auxiliary functions.
∎

Corollary A.3

(
[
28
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Consider the function

F(W,H)=-\sum_{v,d}x_{vd}\log(WH)_{vd}+(WH)_{vd}.

Then the function
G
defined by

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}

\displaystyle=\frac{w^{\prime}_{vk}h^{\prime}_{kd}}{(W^{\prime}H^{\prime})_{vd}},

\displaystyle G((W,H),(W^{\prime},H^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{v,k,d}x_{vd}\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}\log\frac{w_{vk}h_{kd}}{\phi^{\prime}_{vkd}}+\sum_{v,d}(WH)_{vd}

is an auxiliary function of
F
.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

A.1
and
Lemma

A.2
.
∎

Appendix C
Dirichlet–Poisson model

In this section, we derive the variational lower bound of the Dirichlet–Poisson model with a normalized topic matrix
W\in\Delta_{V-1}^{K}
described in
Section

5
. Recall the generative process of the word counts of a single document
d
:

1.

Sample the topic proportions
h_{d}\sim\Dirichlet(\alpha)
.

2.

Sample the topic contributions
z_{vkd}\sim\Poisson(w_{vk}h_{kd})
.

3.

X_{vd}=\sum_{k}z_{vkd}
.

Under this model, the joint likelihood of the observed counts
x_{d}
and the latent variables
(h_{d},z_{d})
is given by

p(x_{d},h_{d},z_{d}|W,\alpha)=\Big(\prod_{v}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{k}z_{vkd}=x_{vd}}\Big)p(h_{d}|\alpha)\prod_{v,k}p(z_{vkd}|W,h_{d}),

where

p(z_{vkd}|W,h_{d})=(w_{vk}h_{kd})^{z_{vkd}}\frac{e^{-w_{vk}h_{kd}}}{z_{vkd}!}

is the probability mass function of the Poisson distribution. Identically to LDA, the introduction of the Dirichlet prior on the topic proportions results in an intractable posterior distribution of the latent variables. We therefore resort to variational inference and approximate the true posterior with a variational distribution
q
given by

\displaystyle q(h,z|\beta,\phi)=\prod_{d}q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d}),\qquad q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d})=q(h_{d}|\beta_{d})\prod_{v}q(z_{vd}|x_{vd},\phi_{vd}),

where
\beta_{d}
and
\phi_{vd}
are Dirichlet and multinomial parameters, respectively. We will now compute the individual terms of the per-document variational lower bound

\displaystyle\log p(x_{d}|W,\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(x_{d},h_{d},z_{d}|W,\alpha)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},\phi_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(h_{d}|\alpha)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(x_{d},z_{d}|W,h_{d})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h_{d})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(z_{d})\right]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathcal{L}^{\text{DP}}_{d}(x_{d}|W,\alpha,\beta_{d},\phi_{d})

of the Dirichlet–Poisson model. Plugging in the density of the Dirichlet distribution, the first and third term are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(h_{d}|\alpha)\right]

\displaystyle=\log\Gamma(\textstyle\sum_{k}\alpha_{k})-\displaystyle\sum_{k}\log\Gamma(\alpha_{k})+\sum_{k}(\alpha_{k}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\log\Gamma(\textstyle\sum_{k}\beta_{kd})-\displaystyle\sum_{k}\log\Gamma(\beta_{kd})+\sum_{k}(\beta_{kd}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]

for

\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]=\psi(\beta_{kd})-\psi(\textstyle\sum_{k^{\prime}}\beta_{k^{\prime}d})\eqqcolon\log(\widetilde{h}_{kd})

(30)

and the digamma function
\psi
, cf.
[
4
, A.1, eq. (14), (15)]
. Using the normalization constraints on the topic matrix and topic weights, the second term reduces to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(x_{d},z_{d}|W,h_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(z_{vkd}|W,h_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[z_{vkd}\right]\log(w_{vk}\widetilde{h}_{kd})-\sum_{v,k}w_{vk}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]-\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,k}\phi_{vkd}x_{vd}\log(w_{vk}\widetilde{h}_{kd})-1-\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right],

and the last term is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(z_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vd}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\log x_{vd}!-\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right]+\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[z_{vkd}\right]\log\phi_{vkd}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\log x_{vd}!-\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right]+\sum_{v,k}\phi_{vkd}x_{vd}\log\phi_{vkd}.

When combining all terms except data-dependent constants, the summands involving
\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right]
cancel each other and the per-document variational lower bound simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DP}}_{d}(x_{d}|W,\alpha,\beta_{d},\phi_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,k}x_{vd}\phi_{vkd}\log\frac{w_{vk}\widetilde{h}_{kd}}{\phi_{vkd}}\,+\,\log\Gamma(\textstyle{\sum}_{k}\alpha_{k})-\log\Gamma(\textstyle{\sum}_{k}\beta_{kd})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Big(\log\Gamma(\beta_{kd})-\log\Gamma(\alpha_{k})+(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{kd})\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]\Big).

Finally, the variational lower bound of the Dirichlet–Poisson model is

\mathcal{L}^{\text{DP}}(X|W,\alpha,\beta,\phi)=\sum_{d}\mathcal{L}^{\text{DP}}_{d}(x_{d}|W,\alpha,\beta_{d},\phi_{d}),

which is identical to the variational lower bound (
17
) of LDA.

Appendix D
Equivalent generative models

The connections between NMF and PLSA or LDA exist because of the close relationships of their underlying generative models, which we summarize in this section.

We say that a parameter of a model is trivial if the marginal distribution of the observed variables
X
has a unique global optimum with respect to this parameter that can be immediately estimated from the observations

In the Gamma–Poisson model we consider, the topics
W\in\Delta_{V-1}^{K}
are normalized model parameters. The topic weights
h_{d}
are not normalized and Gamma priors
h_{kd}\sim\Gamma(\alpha_{k},a_{k})
for additional model parameters
\alpha,a\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{>0}
are introduced. The generative process of each document

then becomes

1.

Sample the topic weights
h_{kd}\sim\Gamma(\alpha_{k},a_{k})
.

2.

Sample the topic contributions
z_{vkd}\sim\Poisson(w_{vk}h_{kd})
.

3.

X_{vd}=\sum_{k}z_{vkd}
.

Here and in the following,
\Gamma(\alpha_{k},a_{k})
denotes the Gamma distribution with shape parameter
\alpha_{k}
and rate parameter
a_{k}
. The joint likelihood of the observed counts
x_{vd}
and the latent variables
(h_{d},z_{d})
is then given by

p(x_{d},h_{d},z_{d}|W,\alpha)=\Big(\prod_{v}\mathbbm{1}_{\sum_{k}z_{vkd}=x_{vd}}\Big)\prod_{k}p(h_{kd}|\alpha_{k},a_{k})\prod_{v,k}p(z_{vkd}|W,h_{d}),

where

\displaystyle p(h_{kd}|\alpha_{k},a_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{k})}h_{kd}^{\alpha_{k}-1}e^{-a_{k}h_{kd}},

\displaystyle p(z_{vkd}|W,h_{d})

\displaystyle=(w_{vk}h_{kd})^{z_{vkd}}\frac{e^{-w_{vk}h_{kd}}}{z_{vkd}!}

denote the density of the Gamma distribution and probability mass function of the Poisson distribution, respectively. Similar to the Dirichlet–Poisson / LDA model, the introduction of the Gamma prior on the topic weights results in an intractable posterior distribution of the latent variables. We apply variational inference and approximate the true posterior with a variational distribution
q
given by

\displaystyle q(h,z|\beta,b,\phi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d}q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},b_{d},\phi_{d}),

\displaystyle q_{d}(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},b_{d},\phi_{d})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k}q(h_{kd}|\beta_{kd},b_{kd})\prod_{v}q(z_{vd}|x_{vd},\phi_{vd}),

where
(\beta_{kd},b_{kd})
and
\phi_{vd}
are Gamma and multinomial parameters, respectively. For completeness, we will now compute the individual terms of the per-document variational lower bound

\displaystyle\log p(x_{d}|W,\alpha,a)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(x_{d},h_{d},z_{d}|W,\alpha,a)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h_{d},z_{d}|\beta_{d},b_{d},\phi_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(h_{d}|\alpha,a)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(x_{d},z_{d}|W,h_{d})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h_{d})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(z_{d})\right]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathcal{L}^{\text{GaP}}_{d}(x_{d}|W,\alpha,a,\beta_{d},b_{d},\phi_{d})

of the Gamma–Poisson model. Plugging in the density of the Gamma distribution, the first and third term are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(h_{d}|\alpha,a)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Big(\alpha_{k}\log a_{k}-\log\Gamma(\alpha_{k})+(\alpha_{k}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]-a_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]\Big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(h_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Big(\beta_{kd}\log b_{kd}-\log\Gamma(\beta_{kd})+(\beta_{kd}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]-b_{kd}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]\Big)

for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]

\displaystyle=\psi(\beta_{kd})-\log b_{kd}\eqqcolon\log(\widetilde{h}_{kd}),

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{kd}}{b_{kd}}

and the digamma function
\psi
, cf.
[
6
, eq. (14)]
. Identically to the Dirichlet–Poisson model, the second term reduces to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log p(x_{d},z_{d}|W,h_{d})\right]=\sum_{v,k}\phi_{vkd}x_{vd}\log(w_{vk}\widetilde{h}_{kd})-\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]-\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right],

and the last term is given by

\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log q(z_{d})\right]=\sum_{v}\log x_{vd}!-\sum_{v,k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log z_{vkd}!\right]+\sum_{v,k}\phi_{vkd}x_{vd}\log\phi_{vkd}.

Notice that in contrast to the Dirichlet–Poisson model, the sum
\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]
is not a constant because the topic weights are not normalized. When combining all terms except data-dependent constants, the per-document variational lower bound simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{GaP}}_{d}

\displaystyle(x_{d}|W,\alpha,a,\beta_{d},b_{d},\phi_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,k}x_{vd}\phi_{vkd}\log\frac{w_{vk}\widetilde{h}_{kd}}{\phi_{vkd}}-\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]+\sum_{k}\Big(\alpha_{k}\log a_{k}-\beta_{kd}\log b_{kd}\Big)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{k}\Big(\log\Gamma(\beta_{kd})-\log\Gamma(\alpha_{k})+(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{kd})\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log h_{kd}\right]+(b_{kd}-a_{k})\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[h_{kd}\right]\Big).

Finally, the variational lower bound of the Gamma–Poisson model is

\mathcal{L}^{\text{GaP}}(X|W,\alpha,a,\beta,b,\phi)=\sum_{d}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GaP}}_{d}(x_{d}|W,\alpha,a,\beta_{d},b_{d},\phi_{d}).

\widetilde{H}=D_{p}(W^{*})H^{*}
fulfill

D(X\,||\,\widetilde{W}\widetilde{H})+R(\widetilde{H})<D(X\,||WH)+R(H),

contradicting that
(W,H)
is a solution of (
36
). The proof of the second statement is analogous.
∎

.
