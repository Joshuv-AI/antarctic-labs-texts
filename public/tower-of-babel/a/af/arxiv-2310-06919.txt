Simplicial pseudohyperplane arrangements give weak Garside groups
Katherine M. Goldman

Abstract
. In this note we connect the language of Bessis’s Garisde categories with Salvetti’s metrical-hemisphere complexes in order to find new examples of weak Garside groups. As our main example, we show that the fundamental group of the (appropriately defined) complexified complement of a pseudohyperplane arrangement is a weak Garside group. As a consequence of the Folkman-Lawrence topological realization theorem, we also show that the fundamental group of the Salvetti complex of a (“simplicial”) oriented matroid is a weak Garside group. This provides novel examples of weak Garside groups.



Weak Garside groups were formalized by Bessis in
[
Bes06
]
. A traditional Garside group is a group endowed with a certain combinatorial structure which provides fruitful information about the group.
The structure on a Garside group generalizes readily to groupoids, giving Bessis’s Garside groupoids. A weak Garside group is a group which is equivalent (as a category) to a Garside groupoid

We clarify that a “weak Garside group” is not the same as a “quasi Garside group”. A quasi Garside group may be thought of as an “infinite-type” Garside group.
. In general, weak Garside groups may not be Garside groups, but the weak Garside property has shown to be rather fruitful on its own. Many results for Garside groups can be easily extended to weak Garside groups, and some recent work has been done to study weak Garside groups in their own right.

Primary examples of (weak) Garside groups come from complements of
complex hyperplane arrangements
.
A complex hyperplane arrangement is a finite collection
\mathcal{H}
of linear hyperplanes (i.e., codimension-1 linear subspaces) in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We denote the complement of this collection by

M(\mathcal{H})=\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{H}}H.

There are two beautiful results concerning when
\pi_{1}(M(\mathcal{H}))
is a weak Garside group.
The first, by Deligne
[
Del72
]
, considers the following class: suppose
\mathcal{H}
is a finite collection of
real
linear hyperplanes (i.e., codimension-1 linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).
We call a connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{H}}H
a
chamber
, and we call
\mathcal{H}

simplicial
if each chamber is a simplicial cone (i.e., the
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-span of a simplex).
Let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}=\{\,H_{\mathbb{C}}:H\in\mathcal{H}\,\}
, where
H_{\mathbb{C}}
denotes the complexification of the linear space
H
.
Then if
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial,
\pi_{1}(M(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}))
is a weak Garside group
[
Del72
]
.
The prototypical example of a Garside group arising in this way is a braid group on
n
-strands
[
Gar69
]
, or, more generally, the spherical-type Artin groups
[
BS72
]
(of which the braid groups are examples).

The second major example comes from Bessis, who in
[
Bes15
]
proved the following. Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is the set of reflection hyperplanes for a well-generated complex reflection group. (A well-generated complex reflection group is a finite subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
generated by exactly
n
complex reflections; complex reflections are finite-order linear transformations of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
fixing a hyperplane.) Then
\pi_{1}(M(\mathcal{H}))
is a weak Garside group
[
Bes15
]
.

One may ask if the assumption that the hyperplanes are linear in the previous examples may be dropped. That is, are there similar results for finite collections of submanifolds which are simply complex codimension-1 and intersect at the origin? While it is certainly unfeasable to expect this to be true of any arbitrary submanifolds, if we restrict ourselves to a certain class of submanifolds, called pseudohyperplanes, we show that the answer is yes; i.e., the fundamental group of the complement of a simplicial pseudohyperplane arrangement is a weak Garside group. We give the precise statements now.

These definitions are based on those found in
[
Man82
]
.
Consider an embedding
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{d}
with image
S
.
The subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d}
is called a
pseudosphere
if the components of
\mathbb{S}^{d}\setminus S
are homeomorphic to the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In this case, the components of
\mathbb{S}^{d}\setminus S
are called the (open) halfspaces of
S
.
(One can verify that there are always two such components.)
Let
\mathcal{A}=\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a finite collection of pseudospheres of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
For
J\subseteq I
, let
S_{J}=\bigcap_{j\in J}S_{j}
.
We call
\mathcal{A}
an
arrangement of pseudospheres (in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
)
if for each
J\subseteq I
and
k\in I
satisfying
S_{J}\not\subset S_{k}
, we have that
S_{J}\cap S_{k}
is a pseudosphere in
S_{J}
with halfspaces coming from the intersection of
S_{J}
with the halfspaces of
S_{k}
. (Compare to the intersection of a hyperplane arrangement with the unit sphere.) If
\mathcal{A}
is a pseudosphere arrangement, then the cone

\displaystyle c\mathcal{A}=\{\,cS:S\in\mathcal{A}\,\}

is called a
pseudohyperplane arrangement
. (The cone
cS
on a pseudosphere
S
is the set
cS=\{\,\alpha x:\alpha\in[0,\infty),x\in S\,\}
.) To a pseudohyperplane arrangement, one can associate a natural “complexified complement” (even though these are not linear subspaces) by

M(c\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\bigcup_{H\in c\mathcal{A}}(H+iH),

where
H+iH=\{\,h_{1}+ih_{2}:h_{i}\in H\,\}
. One of our main results (part of Theorem
33
) is as follows (comparable to
[
Del72
]
).

Theorem
.

If
c\mathcal{A}
is a centrally symmetric simplicial pseudohyperplane arrangement, then
\pi_{1}(M(c\mathcal{A}))
is a weak Garside group.

In particular, this positively answers a question of Deshpande regarding the bi-automaticity of such groups
[
Des16
, p. 419]
, as well as showing that they possesses the other properties of weak Garside groups given above.

Our proof generalizes a result of Cordovil
[

, Thm. 4.1]
, who shows that under the same assumptions on an oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}
, its Salvetti complex

. Although the result in said article does not show further group theoretic or geometric properties of

, and in particular does not discuss Garside structures or normal forms as in

\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{M}))

Our main technical tool, defined in Section
1.2
, are the
metrical-hemisphere complexes introduced by Salvetti in
[
Sal93
]
. These complexes act as a basis for generalizing the construction of the “Salvetti complex” of a hyperplane arrangement to the pseudosphere/pseudohyperplane arrangements.
It is shown in
[
Des16
]
that, analagous to the hyperplane case, the Salvetti complex of a pseudohyperplane arrangement is homotopic to its complexified complement.
We may thus adapt many of the arguments involving the Salvetti complex of a hyperplane arrangement to those of pseudohyperplane arrangements—and in fact, our arguments may be applied to the Salvetti complex of any (suitable) metrical-hemisphere complex. This is the content of Section
2
. See Theorem
14
and Corollary
24
for the full statement of our most general results.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section 1, we provide the relevant background information regarding Garside categories and metrical hemisphere complexes. In Section 2, we show that the Salvetti complex of appropriate metrical hemisphere complexes gives rise to a natural categorical Garside structure, and the isotropy group of this Garside structure is isomorphic to the fundamental group of the Salvetti complex. In Section 3, we apply this result to simplicial pseudohyperplane arrangements and (simplicial) oriented matroids.

1.1.
Garside categories

The material in this section originally comes from
[
Bes06
]
, but our statements are primarily adapted from
[
HH22
]
.
We recall some notions from basic category theory first, mainly to establish notation. Throughout this paper, we assume all categories are small.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a (small) category. The set of objects of
\mathcal{C}
is denoted
\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
and the set of morphisms is denoted
\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
. We denote the identity functor of
\mathcal{C}
by
1_{\mathcal{C}}
.
For
x,y\in\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
, let
\mathcal{C}_{x\rightarrow}
denote the set of morphisms starting at
x
,
\mathcal{C}_{\rightarrow y}
the set of morphisms ending at
y
, and
\mathcal{C}_{x\rightarrow y}
the set of morphisms from
x
to
y
.
If
f\in\mathcal{C}_{x\rightarrow y}
, we sometimes write
x\xrightarrow{f}y
. If
x\xrightarrow{f}y\xrightarrow{g}z
then the composition of
f
and
g
is
x\xrightarrow{fg}z
(so we adopt the “path” style of the order of composition rather than the “function” style).

We recall that a natural transformation
\tau
between functors
F,G:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
, which we denote by
\tau:F\to G
, is a mapping from
\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
to
\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{D})
which for
x,y\in\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
and
x\xrightarrow{f}y
satisfies

(1)

F(x)\xrightarrow{\tau(x)}G(x)
, and

(2)

F(f)\tau(y)=\tau(x)G(f)
.

For
f,g\in\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
, we write
f\preccurlyeq g
if there is an
h\in\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
such that
g=fh
.
We write
g\succcurlyeq f
if there is an
h\in\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
such that
g=hf
. A nontrivial
f\in\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
which cannot be factored into two nontrivial factors is called an atom.

The category
\mathcal{C}
is cancellative if for
a,b,f,g\in\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
,
afb=agb
implies
f=g
. It is homogeneous if there is a function
\ell:\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
\ell(fg)=\ell(f)+\ell(g)
, and
\ell(f)=0
if and only if
f
is trivial.
Note that if
\mathcal{C}
is homogeneous, then
(\mathcal{C}_{x\rightarrow},\preccurlyeq)
and
(\mathcal{C}_{\rightarrow y},\succcurlyeq)
are posets.

Definition 1
.

A
Garside category
is a homogeneous cancellative category
\mathcal{C}
equipped with an automorphism
\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
and a natural transformation
\Delta:1_{\mathcal{C}}\to\phi
satisfying the following properties.

(1)

For all
x,y\in\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
, the posets
(\mathcal{C}_{x\rightarrow},\preccurlyeq)
and
(\mathcal{C}_{\rightarrow y},\succcurlyeq)
are lattices.

(2)

All atoms in
\mathsf{Hom}(\mathcal{C})
are
simple
(
x\xrightarrow{f}y
is simple if there exists an
y\xrightarrow{f^{*}}\phi(x)
so that
ff^{*}=\Delta(x)
).

Sometimes for
x\in\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{C})
, we write
\Delta_{x}=\Delta(x)
and
\Delta^{x}=\Delta(\phi^{-1}(x))
. If
x
is clear from context, we may write
\Delta=\Delta_{x}
. If the set of simple morphisms is finite, we call
\mathcal{C}

finite type
.

Associated to a Garside category
\mathcal{C}
is a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
into which
\mathcal{C}
embeds, obtained by adding formal inverses to all morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
. We call such a
\mathcal{G}
a
Garside groupoid
. For
x\in\mathsf{Obj}(\mathcal{G})
, we sometimes write
\mathcal{G}_{x}
for
\mathcal{G}_{x\rightarrow x}
, and call
\mathcal{G}_{x}
the
isotropy group at
x
.
A
weak Garside group
is a group which is isomorphic to the isotropy group of some object of a Garside groupoid. A
Garside group
is a Garside groupoid with one object. A
Garside monoid
is a Garside category with one object. Each of these objects will be called
finite type
if the corresponding Garside category is finite type.

The following definition will be of use later.

Let

\displaystyle=\alpha\hat{\gamma}_{2}\beta,

We can now define a category for an MH complex

2.
Simplicial MH complexes

We now turn our attention to the main objects of study, which generalize simplicial hyperplane arrangements.

Definition 12

Let

Definition 13

We now have the terminology to state our first main theorem.

Theorem 14
.

Suppose

is a flat, involutive, simplicial MH complex (or FISMH). Then

is a Garside category under
\Delta
(i.e., the map

) and

We will prove this theorem in a series of lemmas. The first follows immediately from the definitions.

Lemma 15

Suppose

to

The next lemma follows immediately from our previous discussions, namely that if

Lemma 16

If

The following paraphrases

Lemma 18

If

Proof.

First, it is clear from the definition that

After translating notation, this is the statement of

The last, and least trivial, portion of the argument is to show that

[

Proposition 20

[

[

Suppose (1), (2), and (3) are satisfied. Let

*
)

.

. Proposition

Let

be the collection of all sets

. Lemma

3.
Application to arrangements and matroids

We now discuss applications to simplicial pseudohyperplane arrangements and their associated matroids. First, we will give further terminology regarding these arrangements.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a pseudosphere arrangement. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
proper
(also called
essential
in some texts) if
\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{A}}S=\varnothing
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is centrally symmetric if each element
S\in\mathcal{A}
is preserved under the central symmetry
-1
of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, that is, if
-S=S
for all
S\in\mathcal{A}
. We call two arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}

homeomorphic
, and write
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cong\mathcal{A}_{2}
, if there is a homeomorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
which is a bijection between
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

An
(open) chamber
of
\mathcal{A}
is a connected component of
\mathbb{S}^{d}\setminus\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{A}}S
.
The union of the closures of the chambers gives a cellulation of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
(where the pseudospheres of
\mathcal{A}
are codimension-1 subcomplexes).
We call
\mathcal{A}

simplicial
if this induced cell structure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is.
The
dual complex

Q(\mathcal{A})
to
\mathcal{A}
is the dual of this cell structure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

We say that the pseudohyperplane arrangement
c\mathcal{A}
satisfies the above properties (e.g., proper, simplicial, etc.) if
\mathcal{A}
does. A chamber of
c\mathcal{A}
is the cone on a chamber of
\mathcal{A}
. We define
Q(c\mathcal{A})=Q(\mathcal{A})
.

We note that centrally symmetric simplicial arrangements are always proper, since the intersection of centrally symmetric pseudospheres which bound a simplex must have empty intersection.
We also note that two arrangements are homeomorphic if and only if their dual complexes are, hence homeomorphism is determined combinatorially.

The following connects arrangements and MH complexes.

Proposition 26
.

[
Sal93
, Prop. 6]

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is an arrangement of pseudospheres in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Then the complex
Q(\mathcal{A})
dual to
\mathcal{A}
is MH.

Thus, as with the other MH complexes we have considered, there is a Salvetti complex
\mathrm{Sal}(Q(\mathcal{A}))
, which for brevity we denote
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A})
. (We define
\mathrm{Sal}(c\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A})
.) We now determine those arrangements
\mathcal{A}
which give FISMH complexes
Q(\mathcal{A})
, and hence for which Corollary
14
holds.

Lemma 27
.

[
Sal93
, Thm. 20]
Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a proper pseudosphere arrangement. Then
Q(\mathcal{A})
is flat.

The following is clear from the definitions.

Lemma 28
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a proper centrally symmetric pseudosphere arrangement, then
Q(\mathcal{A})
is involutive with involution
\phi(x)=-x
.

Since centrally symmetric simplicial arrangements are always proper,
we have the following

Proposition 29
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a centrally symmetric simplicial pseudosphere arrangement, then
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A}))=\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(c\mathcal{A}))
is a weak Garside group.

We can connect the topology of
\mathrm{Sal}(c\mathcal{A})
more directly with the arrangement
c\mathcal{A}
itself. This connection is completely analagous to the connection between a hyperplane arrangement and its Salvetti complex via the complement of its complexification.

Definition 30
.

Let
c\mathcal{A}
be a pseudohyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
H\in c\mathcal{A}
, define

\displaystyle H_{\mathbb{C}}=H+iH\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}

(where
H+iH=\{\,h_{1}+ih_{2}:h_{i}\in H\,\}
).
Then, we define

\displaystyle M(c\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\bigcup_{H\in c\mathcal{A}}H_{\mathbb{C}}.

We would like to say that
M(c\mathcal{A})
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathrm{Sal}(c\mathcal{A})
, but this is not quite true. Instead, we need the following variation.

Let
\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}(\mathcal{A})=\hat{Q}(c\mathcal{A})
be the cellulation of the unit ball
D^{d+1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
obtained by identifying
\partial D^{d+1}
with the cellulation
Q(\mathcal{A})
of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. We have the following

Proposition 31
.

[
Sal93
, Prop. 9]

The complex
\hat{Q}(\mathcal{A})
is an MH complex with exactly one cell (namely,
D^{d+1}
) of top dimension.

Thus we can define
\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(c\mathcal{A})=\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\mathrm{Sal}(\hat{Q}(\mathcal{A}))
, which we call the
completed Salvetti complex
. Note that the
d
-skeletons of
Q
and
\hat{Q}
are the same, so the
d
-skeletons of
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A})
and
\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(\mathcal{A})
are the same. In particular, since we’re assuming

, the 2-skeletons are the same, so
\pi_{1}(\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(\mathcal{A}))\cong\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A}))
.
The relevant result for us is the following (translated into our notation)

Proposition 32
.

[
Des16
, Thm. 2.30]
If
c\mathcal{A}
is a pseudohyperplane arrangement, then
M(c\mathcal{A})
is homotopy equivalent to
\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(c\mathcal{A})
.

In particular,
\pi_{1}(M(c\mathcal{A}))\cong\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(c\mathcal{A}))
. Moreover,
[
Sal93
, §6]
implies
\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(c\mathcal{A})
is a
K(\pi,1)
for simplicial arrangements, i.e., has contractible universal cover. (This was also shown in
[
Des16
]
.) So, we can summarize all of the above results (in addition to the known result for
d=1
) into the following

Theorem 33
.

If
c\mathcal{A}
is a proper simplicial pseudohyperplane arrangement, then
\pi=\pi_{1}(M(c\mathcal{A}))
is a weak Garside group and
M(c\mathcal{A})
is a
K(\pi,1)
.

The combinatorial information contained in a pseudosphere arrangement can be extracted to objects known as
oriented matroids
, which have broad uses in mathematics. We recall this connection here, and explain how our above results apply to this setting.

Definition 34
.

An oriented matroid is a triple
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{E},{}^{*})
where
E
is a finite set,
\mathcal{E}
a collection of non-empty subsets of
E
, and
{}^{*}:E\to E
a “fixed point-free” involution of
E
(so
(x^{*})^{*}=x
and
x^{*}\not=x
) such that

(1)

If
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
and
A\subseteq B
, then
A=B
,

(2)

If
S\in\mathcal{E}
then
S^{*}\in\mathcal{E}
and
S\cap S^{*}=\varnothing
, and

(3)

For
S,T\in\mathcal{E}
with
x\in S\cap T^{*}
and
S\not=T^{*}
, there is a
C\in\mathcal{E}
with
C\subseteq(S\cup T)\setminus\{x,x^{*}\}
.

We call
E
the
ground set
and the elements of
\mathcal{E}
the
circuits
of the oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}
. The
rank

\mathrm{rk}(\mathcal{M})
of
\mathcal{M}
is the minimal cardinality of a set in
\mathcal{E}
. An
isomorphism
between oriented matroids is a bijection between ground sets which induces a bijection on the circuits and commutes with the involution.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a pseudosphere arrangement in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
with each element endowed with a choice of orientation. Let

\displaystyle E=E(\mathcal{A})=\{\,H:H\text{ a halfspace of }S\in\mathcal{A}\,\}.

Define
∗
on
E
by exchanging the halfspaces of any given pseudosphere, e.g., if
H_{1},H_{2}
are the halfspaces of
S
then
H_{1}^{*}=H_{2}
and
H_{2}^{*}=H_{1}
.
We then let
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{A})
be the collection of minimal subsets
C
of
E
with

(1)

C\cap C^{*}=\varnothing
, and

(2)

\bigcup_{H\in C}\overline{H}=\mathbb{S}^{d}
,

where
\overline{H}
is the topological closure of the halfspace
H
in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
By
[
FL78
, Thm. 16]
,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq(E(\mathcal{A}),\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{A}),{}^{*})
is an oriented matroid of rank
d+1
. (
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is not to be confused with
M(c\mathcal{A})
.) The content of the Folkman-Lawrence realization theorem is the following.

Proposition 35
.

[
FL78
, Thm. 20]

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{E},{}^{*})
be an oriented matroid with

\mathrm{rk}(\mathcal{M})=r
. Then there is a proper central pseudosphere arrangement
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M})
in
\mathbb{S}^{r-1}
such that the set of cells of the corresponding dual complex
Q(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}))
are order-isomorphic to the subsets
C
of
\mathcal{E}
with
C\cap C^{*}=\varnothing
, with vertices of
Q(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}))
mapping to elements of
\mathcal{E}
.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{M}
is an oriented matroid, then

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}))\cong\mathcal{M},

and if
\mathcal{A}
is a proper pseudosphere arrangement, then

\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}))\cong\mathcal{A}

(where
\cong
denotes isomorphism and homeomorphism, respectively).

In particular, two pseudosphere arrangements are homeomorphic if and only if their corresponding oriented matroids are isomorphic.

This allows us to sensibly define
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{M})=\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}))
and
\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(\mathcal{M})=\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M}))
for any oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}
. By unraveling definitions, one sees that these are equivalent to typical definitions of a Salvetti complex of an oriented matroid
[
Zie87
, §5.5]
. Thus Theorem
33
can be rephrased in terms of matroids as follows.

Theorem 36
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is an oriented matroid and its pseudosphere realization
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{M})
is simplicial, then
\pi=\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{M}))
is a weak Garside group and
\widehat{\mathrm{Sal}}(\mathcal{M})
is a
K(\pi,1)
.
