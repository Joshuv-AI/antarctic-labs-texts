Artificial Precision Timing Array: bridging the decihertz gravitational-wave sensitivity gap with clock satellites
Lucas M. B. Alves, Andrew G. Sullivan, Imre Bartos, Doğa Veske, Sebastian Will, Zsuzsa Márka, Szabolcs Márka

Abstract
Gravitational-wave astronomy has developed enormously over the last decade with the first detections across different frequency bands, but has yet to access 0.1 ​ – ​ 10 0.1–10 Hz gravitational waves. Gravitational waves in this band are emitted by some of the most enigmatic sources, including intermediate-mass binary black hole mergers, early inspiralling compact binaries, and possibly cosmic inflation. To tap this exciting band, we propose the construction of a detector based on pulsar timing principles, the Artificial Precision Timing Array (APTA). We envision APTA as a solar system array of artificial “pulsars”—precision-clock-carrying satellites that emit pulsing electromagnetic signals towards Earth or other centrum. In this fundamental study, we estimate the clock precision needed for APTA to successfully detect gravitational waves. Our results suggest that a clock relative uncertainty of 10 − 17 10^{-17} , which is currently attainable, would be sufficient for APTA to surpass LISA’s sensitivity in the decihertz band and observe 10 3 ​ – ​ 10 4 10^{3}–10^{4} M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} black hole mergers. Future atomic clock technology realistically expected in the next decade would enable the detection of an increasingly diverse set of astrophysical sources, including stellar-mass compact binaries that merge in the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA band, extreme-mass-ratio inspirals, and Type Ia supernovae. This work opens up a new area of research into designing and constructing artificial gravitational-wave detectors relying on the successful principles of pulsar timing.

To reach this unexplored GW band, we propose a new detector concept: the Artificial Precision Timing Array (APTA), a network of satellites distributed around the Earth carrying high-precision clocks to act as artificial “pulsars”. Having satellite signals aimed at a small station (or stations) dedicated to their reception, APTA can avoid the cadence constraint of regular PTAs and achieve high sensitivity at much higher frequencies. Then, if the satellite clocks are timed at least every
\sim
0.1 s, APTA can reach high sensitivity to 0.1–10 Hz GWs for observations lasting 10 s or more.

The idea of using high-precision clocks in space to detect GWs has been proposed and applied before. The global positioning system (GPS) was used to constrain the strain amplitude of the GW background in the 0.01–1 Hz band to
h_{c}<4.8\times 10^{-12}(1~{\rm Hz}/f)

Aoyama et al. 2014

;
Armstrong 2006
;
Vutha 2015
. Another study proposed the use of two satellites in heliocentric orbits equipped with optical lattice atomic clocks to detect GWs, using a technique similar to Doppler tracking
Kolkowitz et al. 2016
. Another proposed detector, the Space Atomic Gravity Explorer (SAGE), consists of two satellites carrying ultracold atomic strontium systems to either perform atom interferometry with the strontium or Doppler tracking utilizing Sr optical atomic clocks
Tino et al. 2019
. Nevertheless, the idea of equipping artificial “pulsars” in the solar system with high-precision clocks to detect GWs with pulsar-timing-type methods is unprecedented.

In this paper, we describe the groundwork for a solar system APTA targeting the 0.1–10 Hz GW frequency band. In Section
II
, we list and discuss several interesting astrophysical targets observable in this band. In Section
III
, we summarize the basics of PTA sensitivity
Moore et al. 2015a

IV

V

VI

III
Pulsar Timing Array Sensitivity

PTAs, including NANOGrav
(
Agazie et al. 2023a
)
, the European Pulsar Timing Array
Desvignes et al. 2016
;
Antoniadis et al. 2023
, the Parkes Pulsar Timing Array
Manchester et al. 2013
;
Reardon et al. 2023
;
Zic et al. 2023
, the Chinese Pulsar Timing Array
Xu et al. 2023
, and the MeerKAT interferometer in South Africa
Bailes et al. 2020
, monitor the time of arrival (ToA) of pulsar radio pulses. Pulsars, rapidly rotating magnetized neutron stars, emit regular radio pulses with periods in the range [10
-3
s, 10 s]
Condon and Ransom 2016
. With very precisely measured and stable periods over long timescales, millisecond pulsars make for powerful natural clocks; the change in their spin periods
\dot{P}
is
\dot{P}\lesssim 10^{-19}
ss
-1

Lorimer 2008
. A GW passing between a pulsar and the Earth alters the radio pulse travel time from the star to the telescope. Subtracting a deterministic model for the ToAs from the observed data yields a set of timing residuals, deviations from the expected ToAs, which may come from GWs or noise processes. In this section, we summarize the fundamentals of PTA sensitivity, following Ref.
Moore et al. 2015a
.

When collecting data, GW detectors are subject to several sources of noise that complicate the identification of signals from astrophysical sources. The data stream received by the detector
\mathbb{s}(t)
is the sum of a noise term
\mathbb{n}(t)
and a (possible) signal term
\mathbb{h}(t)
:

\mathbb{s}(t)=\mathbb{n}(t)+\mathbb{h}(t).

(4)

The significance of the signal is determined by the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
Taking a frequentist approach to GW signal processing, one defines a statistic
\mathfrak{s}
by cross-correlating the timing residuals with a symmetric filter matrix
\mathbb{K}(t)
:

\mathfrak{s}=\int\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathbb{s}^{T}(t)\mathbb{K}^{\dagger}(t-t^{\prime})\mathbb{s}(t^{\prime})=\int\mathrm{d}f\;\mathbb{\tilde{s}}(f)^{T}\mathbb{\tilde{K}}^{\dagger}(f)\mathbb{\tilde{s}}(f),

(5)

where the tilde denotes a Fourier transform.
The SNR
\rho
is the ratio of the average of this statistic in the presence of a signal to its root mean square (rms) value with only noise:

\rho^{2}=\frac{\langle\mathfrak{s}\rangle^{2}_{s=n+h}}{\langle\mathfrak{s}^{2}\rangle_{s=n}-\langle\mathfrak{s}\rangle_{s=n}^{2}}.

(6)

In GW signal processing, it is common practice to look for the “matched” filter
\mathbb{\tilde{K}}(f)
that maximizes
\rho
in order to optimally extract the signal from the noise.
\mathbb{\tilde{K}}(f)
and its corresponding
\rho
may be evaluated by assuming Gaussian white noise and that the pulsars are far enough from each other to ensure the noise of individual pulsars is uncorrelated
Moore et al. 2015a
. In this case, for a monochromatic GW emitted by a slowly evolving compact object binary, the SNR is

\rho^{2}=\frac{1}{2}N_{p}(N_{p}-1)\frac{8\chi^{4}h_{c}^{4}}{T}\int_{1/T}^{1/\delta t}\mathrm{d}f\;\frac{\sin^{4}{\left[\pi(f-f_{0})T\right]}}{\pi^{4}(f-f_{0})^{4}f^{4}S_{n}^{2}},

(7)

where
N_{p}
is the number of pulsars,
1/\delta t
is the cadence of observations,
T
is the total time of observation of the source, and
S_{n}=2\sigma^{2}\delta t
is the noise power spectral density for white noise, where
\sigma
is the rms error in the timing residuals.
\chi
is a sky-averaged version of the redshift
z
of pulsar signals. In general,
z
is

z=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\hat{p}^{i}\hat{p}^{j}}{1+\hat{\Omega}\cdot\hat{p}}\left(h_{ij}(t-|\mathbf{p}|(1+\hat{\Omega}\cdot\hat{p}),\hat{\Omega})-h_{ij}(t,\hat{\Omega})\right),

(8)

where
t
is the detection time,
\mathbf{p}
is the position of the pulsar in an Earth-centered coordinate system,
\hat{\Omega}
is the direction of propagation of the GW, and
h_{ij}
is the GW perturbation to the background metric
Anholm et al. 2009
. In PTAs one often assumes that the pulsar term, i.e. the first term in Eq.
8
, is zero
Moore et al. 2015a
. We discuss the implications of this in Section
IV.2
. Besides the sky-averaging, we average
z
over GW polarization and source inclination angle so as not to overestimate the sensitivity of APTA, resulting in
\chi\approx 0.317
.

The integral in Eq.
7
can be analytically approximated in the high and low-frequency regimes
Moore et al. 2015a
. Choosing a detection-threshold value
\rho_{th}
and inverting Eq.
7
, the dimensionless characteristic GW strain
h_{c}
in the high and low-frequency regimes is

h_{c}^{\mathrm{HIGH}}(f)\approx\left(\frac{16\rho_{th}^{2}}{3\chi^{4}N_{p}(N_{p}-1)}\right)^{1/4}\sigma f\sqrt{\frac{\delta t}{T}}

(9a)

h_{c}^{\mathrm{LOW}}(f)\approx\frac{3\sqrt{\rho_{th}}}{2^{7/4}\chi\pi^{3}}\left(\frac{13}{N_{p}(N_{p}-1)}\right)^{1/4}\frac{\sigma}{T^{3}f^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\delta t}{T}}\sec{\phi}

(9b)

where
\phi
is chosen to make
h_{c}^{\mathrm{HIGH}}(2/T)=h_{c}^{\mathrm{LOW}}(2/T)
. The total sensitivity is hence
Moore et al. 2015a

h_{c}(f)\approx h_{c}^{\mathrm{HIGH}}(f)+h_{c}^{\mathrm{LOW}}(f).

(10)

As this model shows, sensitivity curves fundamentally depend on the timing precision, the threshold SNR, the number of pulsars, and the cadence and total duration of observation.

IV
Artificial Precision Timing Array

To construct a GW detector based on PTA principles, we envision distributing pulsing electromagnetic sources in the sky. These light sources need to have their periodic pulses timed to high precision so that GWs may be observed with them. This can be achieved with an array of atomic-clock-carrying satellites, which would play the same role as the astrophysical pulsars in regular PTAs. They could orbit around the Earth or the Sun. Alternatively, rather than Earth-based radio receivers, one could imagine a near-Earth receiving base to avoid atmospheric interference, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The details of this communication mechanism, including the electromagnetic pulse features and its use at detection, are beyond the scope of this paper, which focuses on clock requirements. However, this communication pathway must not degrade the required clock accuracy.

The satellites, equipped with atomic clocks to allow for precision timing, play the role of the pulsars in this pulsar-timing-like GW detector. Objects are not drawn to scale, and the satellite array’s orbit may encircle some solar system planets, contrary to what the figure suggests.

The results of the model from Section
III
do not depend on the pulsar/satellite communication frequency because
f_{photon}\gg f_{GW}
for reasonable signal frequency choices
Isaacson 1968
;
Stappers et al. 2011
. To observe
0.1–10
Hz GWs, radio waves would do, but visible light would give a pulse-to-GW frequency ratio comparable to that of regular PTAs. X-rays may also become a good choice given recent advances in the generation of high-frequency phase-stable pulses
(
Zhang et al. 2022
, e.g.)
. We leave the optimal photon wavelength for APTA pulses as a subject for future investigation.

IV.1
Timing error and cadence

PTAs observing for a total time
T
with a cadence of
1/\delta t
are generally sensitive to GW frequencies in the range
(1/T)\lesssim f_{GW}\lesssim(1/\delta t)

Moore et al. 2015b
. Thus accessing the
0.1–10
Hz band can be achieved straightforwardly simply by searching for GW transients and continuous signals that last at least
\sim 10
s and taking data at least every 0.1 s.

The lowest detectable characteristic strain depends linearly on the error in the PTA timing residuals
\sigma
as indicated in Eqs.
9
. We estimate the attainable sensitivities for APTA given various timing errors consistent with present and future atomic clock precision. We defer the discussion of additional sources of error to Section
V
.
The best atomic clocks at present can reach fractional frequency uncertainties
\xi=\Delta f_{clock}/f_{clock}\sim 10^{-18}
in 1 s of averaging and
\sim 10^{-19}–10^{-20}
in 1 h
Bothwell et al. 2022
;
Oelker et al. 2019
;
Campbell et al. 2017
. In the next few years, we may reach
\xi\sim 10^{-20}
in 1 s averaging with current 3D optical lattice clock technology. In a decade, use of squeezing and increased atom number may allow
\xi\sim 10^{-21}

Ludlow et al. 2015
. Note that
\xi\propto t_{avg}^{-1/2}
, for
t_{avg}
the time of averaging. Our averaging time is limited by cadence so
\xi
over time
\delta t
of averaging is

\xi_{\delta t}=\xi_{1\text{ s}}\left(\frac{\delta t}{1\text{ s}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(11)

where
\xi_{1\text{ s}}
is the fractional uncertainty in 1 s of averaging.
We assume APTA’s clocks reach a stable optimal fractional uncertainty
\xi_{\delta t}
after averging and use this value to compute the timing error as
\sigma=\xi_{\delta t}\delta t
.

APTA can be sensitive to much higher frequencies than regular PTAs because its precision timing can be carried out continuously, whereas PTAs are limited by radio telescope access. Continued observation can be achieved if the beacon satellites are locked to a central observing station. In this case, the cadence
1/\delta t
is bounded by the frequency at which communication pulses can be stably emitted and received, thereby fundamentally limited by the atomic clocks’ frequency.

For communication purposes, an extremely efficient way to transfer the timing precision of optical atomic clocks to the radiofrequency domain is provided by optical frequency comb technology
Udem et al. 2002
;
Diddams et al. 2020
. It has the potential to generate precision APTA pulses at a carrier frequency of 10 MHz to 100 GHz. We also note that intense efforts are underway to miniaturize and ruggedize the technology of strontium optical lattice clocks
Poli et al. 2014
;
Koller et al. 2017
;
Origlia et al. 2018
;
Takamoto et al. 2020
. Progress has been fast, which makes the prospect of deploying precision optical lattice clocks within the next decade highly realistic.

In the simplified timing model described in Section
III
, lowering
\delta t
always lowers the sensitivity.
This is a feature of assuming white noise: when
\delta t\rightarrow 0
, the measurement becomes continuous, so noise does not remain at any frequency. The effects of more realistic noise will be considered in future work. The current state of the art for phase-stable X-ray pulses is
10^{-15}
s
Zhang et al. 2022
For this study, we use
\delta t=0.01
s, an order of magnitude below the minimum requirement for accessing 10 Hz GWs. Therefore, to focus on clock uncertainty requirements, we utilize state-of-the-art low values of
\xi
alongside conservative values of
\delta t
. The same sensitivities could be reached with higher
\xi
by lowering
\delta t
. The determination of the optimal set of parameters for constructing APTA is left for subsequent studies.

IV.2
Distance to the satellite array

The resulting sensitivity curve in Eqs.
9
and
10
does not depend on the distance between the pulsars and the observer because the correlated timing residual at each pulsar is assumed to be zero across all pulsars. Recent studies have considered the sensitivity enhancement from modeling non-zero pulsar terms in the timing residual (see discussion in Ref.
Zhu et al. 2016
and references therein), but we leave a more detailed treatment of the pulsar term for future work. This approximation assumes that pulsars are far enough from each other that their separation is much larger than the GW wavelength (for stochastic GW searches) and consequently the GW phase is not coherent across the beacons. While this may not be the case for APTA in practice, we use this model for the purposes of this proof of concept study.

In this initial study, we assume operators have perfect control of the satellites. Hence, increasing the number of satellites
N_{s}
will always increase the detector’s sensitivity. For a realistic scenario, we may consider a handful of satellites,
N_{s}\approx 6
in orbit at roughly 1-2 AU from the sun. Although the assumption of uncorrelated satellite timing residuals may start to break down at these distances, the detector can still work with non-zero correlations between the satellite timing residuals included in the real sensitivity model.

V
Timing model Considerations

Several additional effects must be taken into account in a realistic timing model. Although detailed calculation of these effects is beyond the scope of this study, we discuss some of them below.

Shapiro time delay, the change in the travel time of light between two points due to the presence of massive bodies
Shapiro 1964
, from various solar system objects will introduce delays of
2GM/c^{3}
, e.g.
\sim 10^{-5}
for the Sun,
\sim 10^{-8}
s for Jupiter, and
\sim 10^{-13}
s for the Moon. Since the main asteroid belt has an estimated total mass of
18\times 10^{-10}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

Krasinsky et al. 2002
, asteroids may also contribute with delays
\sim 10^{-14}
s. These known delays may need to be included in the timing model, depending on where the satellites are placed. The gravity gradient noise of asteroids, on the other hand, only contributes marginally at much lower frequencies (
10^{-9}–10^{-5}
Hz)
Fedderke et al. 2022
;
Sullivan et al. 2022
, and so should not affect APTA.

After setting the satellites in orbit, their positions should be tracked with sufficient precision to account for their motion and added time delays. Additionally, it will be important to track disruptions to the network (e.g. a satellite being captured by a planet or colliding with an asteroid). Disruptions to one or a few elements will not necessarily prevent GW detection since the signals detected by the other satellites remain correlated. If further analyses reveal that this is an important concern, the detector can be designed with a redundantly large number of satellites.

Because of APTA’s small solar-system scale, some components of traditional PTA models, including parallax
Deng and Finn 2011
as well as Einstein and Roemer delays (related to the use of a coordinate system centered in the solar system barycenter
Hobbs et al. 2006
), are not as important.
The solar wind
Marsch 2006
contributes to the dispersion measure (DM), i.e. the integrated column density of free electrons between the observer and a light source. While accounting for the effects of the time-dependent DM on the ToAs is not straightforward
Agazie et al. 2023b
, this problem is easier for APTA than for PTAs, since the only contributions are from the solar system rather than the interstellar medium.

To first approximation, clock synchronization should not play an important role in the functioning of APTA. Regular PTAs are of course not synchronized clocks, and have already been proven successful by relying on the long-term timing precision of the individual pulsars. Still, in general, significant time precision losses may happen in the process of time transfer
(
Caldwell et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Preserving, transferring, and utilizing the satellite clocks’ precision is also mission-critical for success. Therefore, the generation of the electromagnetic pulses and their locking to the clock should preserve the accuracy of the time reference. This can be done with state-of-the-art frequency comb technology
Udem et al. 2002
;
Diddams et al. 2020
. Additionally, the pulses’ phase information and intensity must not degrade sufficiently during their travel to compromise the recovery of the timing information. The multi-beam detection scheme at the central location must also preserve the mission-critical relative timing information. Each of these interwoven problems will require its own individual technical analysis as the required clock accuracy only sets the performance specifications of the downstream systems engineering.

VI
Conclusion

We have presented a proof-of-principle study into the use of an artificial solar-system-scale timing array to detect 0.1–10 Hz GWs, focusing on the required time reference precision. APTA would consist of
\sim 10
satellites equipped with precision clocks orbiting and preferably communicating with a near-Earth base so the atmosphere is not in the path. The desired frequency band can be reached by targeting astrophysical signals at least 10 s long and ensuring that the beacons’ pulses are received at least every 0.1 s.

APTA’s sensitivity is fundamentally limited by
\sigma
, the error in the timing residuals. With the current state-of-the-art atomic clock technology
Bothwell et al. 2022
;
Oelker et al. 2019
;
Campbell et al. 2017
, APTA can achieve better dHz sensitivity than LISA and TianQin, allowing for detection of
10^{3}–10^{4}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
black hole mergers and the early inspirals of some ground-based GW sources. With future clock technology available
Ludlow et al. 2015
, APTA could compete with and eventually surpass the sensitivity of other proposed detectors for the 0.1–10 Hz frequency range without the same challenges in high-precision interferometry they face. Then, it could detect most astrophysical sources in this band. These exciting prospects motivate the continued development of atomic clock technology and its application to GW detection.

While focusing on the accuracy of the APTA’s time reference, we have only discussed briefly the effects of additional noise sources, technical challenges, and technical developments required. This will require more detailed future work. Nevertheless, with conservative choices for measurement cadence
\delta t
, we have shown that high sensitivity can be still be achieved. Future studies may indicate that
\delta t
can be lowered without straining APTA’s engineering requirements. With this and future papers laying the foundation, we propose APTA to observe uncharted territory of the gravitational-wave sky and gain new insight into high-energy astrophysics and cosmology.
