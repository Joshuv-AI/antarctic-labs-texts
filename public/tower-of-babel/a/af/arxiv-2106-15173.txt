1.
Introduction

Random embeddings have been studied extensively over the last few decades as a way of exposing a set’s structure. Intuitively, a set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
without distorting most of its metric structure (e.g., almost preserving mutual distances between most of its points) as long as
m
is at least as large as
T
’s effective dimension. The meaning of ‘effective dimension’ is rather vague at this point, and various features of the set could serve as its effective dimension; our choice will be clarified in what follows.

The Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma
[
9
]
was one of the first examples to exhibit this general phenomenon. It states that a subset
T
of a Hilbert space, that is of cardinality
n
, can be embedded in
\ell_{2}^{m}
for
m=c(\varepsilon)\log n
, with the embedding distorting mutual distances between all pairs of points in
T
by at most a multiplicative factor of
1\pm\varepsilon
. Moreover, the embedding is random—in the original proof of the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma the embedding was a normalized orthogonal projection onto a subspace selected according to the Haar measure on a Grassmann manifold of the right dimension. That choice of a random embedding is by no means unique, and over the years many different random ensembles have been shown to be, with high probability, embeddings that satisfy the Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma.

However, the dimension
m=c(\varepsilon)\log n
is, at times, loose. It is a rather coarse upper estimate on any reasonable notion of effective dimension: it does not distinguish between two sets that have the same cardinality but might have totally different metric structures.

The body of work that studies various notions of random embeddings is substantial and we make no attempt to survey it here.
Rather, we focus on one particular way of embedding a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
: a random ensemble
A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\widetilde{A}
, where
\widetilde{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a random matrix whose columns are independent copies of a symmetric random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We assume throughout that
X
is symmetric and isotropic, i.e., that
X
and
-X
have the same distribution and that its covariance is the identity (implying, in particular, that for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbb{E}\left\langle X,u\right\rangle^{2}=\|u\|_{2}^{2}
).
Thus, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{E}\|At\|_{2}^{2}=\|t\|_{2}^{2}
, which explains the normalization of
1/\sqrt{m}
used in the definition of
A
.

Our goal is to study when a satisfactory estimate on

(1.1)

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|

is possible for an arbitrary
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. While an upper estimate on (
1.1
) seems a rather weak notion of structure preservation, when applied to
T=V-V=\{x-y:x,y\in V\}
for
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, (
1.1
) captures the worst distortion of distances in
V
caused by the embedding
A
.

It is important to stress the significant difference between the case we focus on here—when
A
has
n
independent copies of a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as columns, and the more standard scenario of a random embedding, in which the random ensemble has
m
independent copies of a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as rows. The fact that the columns of
A
are independent, combined with our goal of a nontrivial estimate for any
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, automatically leads to an obstruction that has to be overcome by an additional assumption on
X
. Indeed, one of the possible sets
T
is very simple—the standard basis
\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
. For such a set
T
we have

(1.2)

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\|Ae_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-1\right|=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right|,

implying that at the very least,

(1.3)

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right|

has to be well-behaved with reasonable probability. That can only happen if
\|X\|_{2}^{2}
exhibits a sufficiently strong concentration around its mean—a so-called
thin-shell bound
. Thin-shell bounds are known for a variety of random vectors (for estimates in the log-concave case, see, e.g.
[
10
,
8
,
6
]
and Corollary
1.6
, below).

Because it is unreasonable to consider embeddings that fail when the given set contains the standard basis, it makes sense to focus only on ensembles for which (
1.3
) is small. Thus, the key question is what happens when this obstruction is resolved:

Question 1.1
.

Assume that
X
is well-behaved, in the sense that with probability at least
1-\gamma
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right|\leq\delta.

When is it possible to control (
1.1
) in a satisfactory way for every
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
?

To find what ‘satisfactory’ should mean here, let us turn to the obvious example, when
A
is generated by independent copies of the standard Gaussian random vector
G
; that is, the random vector whose coordinates are independent, standard Gaussian random variables.

Remark 1.2
.

In what follows we will not specify the dimension of each Gaussian random vector we encounter; that will be clear from the context in each instance.

The way a Gaussian random matrix acts on an arbitrary set
T
is well understood. To describe the features that are relevant in the context of Question
1.1
we require two important notions. The first one is the
Gaussian mean-width
associated with
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
; that is,

\ell_{*}(T)=\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}|\left\langle G,t\right\rangle|=\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}t_{i}\right|.

To explain the reasoning for its name, note that

\ell_{\ast}(T)\sim\sqrt{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}\sup_{t\in T}|\left\langle\theta,t\right\rangle|\,d\sigma(\theta)

where
\sigma
denotes the Haar measure on the Euclidean unit sphere
S^{n-1}
; in other words,
\ell_{\ast}(T)
is equivalent to the normalized average over all directional widths
\sup_{t\in T}|\left\langle\theta,t\right\rangle|
when assigning equal weight to all directions
\theta\in S^{n-1}
.

The second notion is the so-called
Dvoretzky-Milman
dimension (or
critical dimension
). Here and in what follows denote

d_{T}=\sup_{t\in T}\|t\|_{2},

and set

k_{*}(T)=\left(\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{d_{T}}\right)^{2}

to be the Dvoretzky-Milman dimension. For reasons that will be clarified immediately, it will serve as our choice of the set’s effective dimension.
More information on these notions and their role in the study of
Asymptotic Geometric Analysis
can be found in
[
3
]
.

One of the main achievements of Asymptotic Geometric Analysis is Milman’s version of Dvoretzky’s Theorem
[
14
]
. For the Gaussian formulation we use here see, e.g.,
[
15
]
or
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
T
be a convex body—that is, a convex, bounded, centrally symmetric subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a nonempty interior. Set
k_{*}=k_{*}(T)
and
\varepsilon>0
, and let
m=c(\varepsilon)k_{*}
for a well chosen constant
c(\varepsilon)
that depends only on
\varepsilon
.
Let
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the normalized Gaussian matrix.
Then, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}k_{*})
,

(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}B_{2}^{m}\subset AT\subset(1+\varepsilon)\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}B_{2}^{m},

where
B_{2}^{m}
is the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

At the same time, the Gaussian random matrix
A
satisfies a uniform concentration estimate that holds for every
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Indeed, one can show that for
u>0
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{0}u^{2}k_{*})
,

(1.4)

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq c_{1}\left(ud_{T}\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+u^{2}\frac{\ell_{*}^{2}(T)}{m}\right).

A more general version of (
1.4
) can be found in
[
11
]
.

The performance of the Gaussian random operator will be our benchmark. Thus, at least at the intuitive level, one should expect that when
m\lesssim k_{*}(T)
the random embedding
A
erases all of
T
’s metric structure, as is the case for the Gaussian ensemble. Indeed, if the complexity of two convex bodies,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, is roughly the same—in the sense that the two have the same Euclidean diameter and mean-width—, the typical images
AT_{1}
and
AT_{2}
are almost the same: by Theorem
1.3
both are close to a Euclidean ball of essentially the same radius.

However, once the dimension of the image space increases beyond
k_{*}(T)
, (
1.4
) implies that the Gaussian ensemble preserves some of
T
’s structure. Therefore, it is natural to ask whether a version of (
1.4
) is true for a random mapping
A
whose independent columns are distributed according to a general symmetric, isotropic random vector rather than as the Gaussian one, and the interesting case is when
m\geq k_{\ast}(T)
.

Our main result is that, if the random ensemble generated by
X
can overcome the trivial obstruction and deal with
T=\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
, only a mild additional assumption on
X
suffices to ensure that an almost Gaussian estimate holds in (
1.1
) for an arbitrary
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A hint on the features that
X
needs to satisfy can be found in standard properties of the Gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
: in addition to a thin-shell bound—which the Gaussian random vector satisfies with parameters
\delta=c(\beta)\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}
and
\gamma\sim n^{-\beta}
(e.g., by Bernstein’s inequality)—, the Gaussian vector satisfies
L_{p}
-
L_{2}
norm equivalences in the following sense: there is an absolute constant
C
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and every
p\geq 1
,

\|\left\langle G,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq C\sqrt{p}\|\left\langle G,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}.

It turns out that a combination of a thin-shell bound and a mild tail decay on linear functionals suffices to (almost) recover the Gaussian estimate on (
1.1
). To be more accurate, we require the following:

Definition 1.4
.

We say that the symmetric, isotropic random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
suitable
with constants
\delta\in[0,1]
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in(0,2]
,
R
and
L
if the following holds:

(1)

X
satisfies that with probability at least
1-\gamma
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right|\leq\delta.

(2)

for every
2\leq p\leq R\log n
,

\|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq Lp^{1/\alpha}\|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}\ \ \text{for every }\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Part
(1)
of Definition
1.4
is the bare-minimum: the thin-shell bound that is needed to remove the trivial obstruction caused by
\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
. Part
(2)
is an
L_{p}
-
L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
L
, corresponding to a
\psi_{\alpha}
behaviour for some
0<\alpha\leq 2
(rather than the
\psi_{2}
behaviour exhibited by the Gaussian random vector)—but only up to
p=R\log n
.
The larger
R
is, the wider the range of
L_{p}
norms for which the norm equivalence holds, and that implies that linear functionals
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
have faster tail decays.

With this notion set in place, let us formulate our main result. Recall that
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a random matrix whose columns are
n
normalized independent copies of the symmetric, isotropic random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\beta\geq 1
and assume that
X
is suitable in the sense of Definition
1.4
, with constants
\delta
,
\gamma
,
\alpha
,
R=R(\beta)
and
L
.
Consider
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies
k_{*}(T)\geq\log n
. Then with probability at least
1-\gamma-2\exp(-c_{0}k_{*}(T))-n^{-\beta}
,

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq 2\delta d_{T}^{2}+c(L,\alpha,\beta)\left(d_{T}\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\ell_{*}^{2}(T)}{m}\right)\log^{2/\alpha}\left(\frac{en}{k_{*}(T)}\right).

A version of Theorem
1.5
holds when
k_{*}(T)<\log n
(see Section
2.7
below), but taking into account that even for the Gaussian random vector, the thin-shell bound implies that
\delta
is of the order of
\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}
, Theorem
1.5
is more interesting when
k_{*}(T)\geq\log n
. For such sets, and up to the term needed for dealing with the trivial obstruction, the error is (almost) the same as in the Gaussian case, only incurring an additional logarithmic factor:

\sim\left(d_{T}\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\ell_{*}^{2}(T)}{m}\right)\log^{2/\alpha}\left(\frac{en}{k_{*}(T)}\right).

Theorem
1.5
is actually rather surprising, for two different reasons. First of all, the error is almost the same as for the Gaussian ensemble, which is unexpected; after all, linear functionals
\left\langle X,x\right\rangle
can exhibit a significantly heavier tail behaviour than the Gaussian one. There is no apparent source of mixing that would lead to a Gaussian-like behaviour, and so the origin of the almost Gaussian behaviour is rather mysterious. Secondly, when
T
is very large—with
k_{*}(T)\sim n
, it seems that there is not enough independence in
A
to justify a Gaussian error. We give a concrete example of this insufficient independence in Section
1.1
and explain why the thin-shell bound is the source of the extra mixing that compensates for the insufficient independence.

1.1.
Example: log-concave random vectors

Let
X
be a symmetric, isotropic, log-concave vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
; that is, it has a density that is a log-concave function (for an extensive survey on log-concavity and its role in Asymptotic Geometric Analysis, see
[
5
,
3
]
). Observe that
X
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
1.5
:

(1)

The thin-shell estimate needed for Theorem
1.5
follows from a recent result due to Chen
[
6
]
: setting
\theta_{m}=\sqrt{\log{m}\log\log{m}}
, Chen’s estimate on the
thin-shell parameter
implies that there is an absolute constant
c
such that for
u\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-u)
,

\left|\frac{\|X\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right|\leq cu\frac{\theta_{m}}{\sqrt{m}}.

Therefore,
X
satisfies the wanted thin-shell bound with probability
1-\gamma
for

\delta=c\frac{\theta_{m}}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{en}{\gamma}\right).

(2)

By Borell’s Lemma (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
), a log-concave random vector satisfies a
\psi_{1}
-
L_{1}
norm equivalence; that is, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and every
p\geq 1
,
\|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq Cp\|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{1}}
.

Theorem
1.5
leads to the following:

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy
k_{*}(T)\geq\log n
. Then with probability
1-\gamma-2\exp(-c_{0}k_{*}(T))-n^{-\beta}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq

\displaystyle cd_{T}^{2}\frac{\theta_{m}}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{en}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle c(\beta)\left(d_{T}\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\ell_{*}^{2}(T)}{m}\right)\log^{2}\left(\frac{en}{k_{*}(T)}\right).

In particular, setting
T=S^{n-1}
, Corollary
1.6
is an estimate on the extremal singular values of
A
: if

\sup_{t\in S^{n-1}}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon,

then on that event the extremal singular values of
A
satisfy that

(1.5)

1-\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{\min}(A)\leq\lambda_{\max}(A)\leq 1+\varepsilon.

It is possible to show that the smallest
\varepsilon
one can hope for when
m\geq n
—say, with probability
1/2
—, is
\varepsilon\sim\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}
even if
A
is a Gaussian matrix. As it happens, such an optimal dependence holds for a wide variety of random matrices that have “enough randomness”. Specifically, when
m\geq cn
for
c\gg 1
, the random matrix consists of rows that are
m
independent copies of a random vector
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
Z
is well-behaved in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|Z_{i}\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{n}

and linear functionals exhibit an
L_{p}
-
L_{2}
norm equivalence for some
p>4

[
18
]
.

This extra independence—the fact that there are more independent random vectors (the
m
rows of the random matrix) than the dimension
n
of the underlying space, is crucial when trying to control the way the matrix acts of
S^{n-1}
(see, for example, the proofs in
[
1
,
13
,
18
]
).

In the case that interests us the situation is different.
Seemingly, there is insufficient independence at one’s disposal:
A
consists of
n
independent columns and to ensure a satisfactory estimate on the extremal singular values,
AS^{n-1}
must be contained in a thin shell around
S^{m-1}
.
Despite the lack of independence, Theorem
1.5
is enough to yield the optimal estimate: we have that
k_{*}(S^{n-1})\sim n
, and when
X
is symmetric, isotropic and log-concave, then
\theta_{m}\leq c_{0}(\zeta)m^{\zeta}
for any
\zeta>0
. Therefore,
X
is suitable for

\delta=c_{0}(\zeta)m^{\zeta-1/2}\cdot\log\left(\frac{en}{\gamma}\right)

with parameters
\alpha=1
,
L
that is an absolute constant, and
R
that can be arbitrarily large.
Corollary
1.6
implies that, with probability at least
0.9
,

\sup_{t\in S^{n-1}}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-1\right|\leq 2\delta+c\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}\leq c_{1}(\zeta)\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}}

provided that

n\leq m\leq c_{2}(\zeta)\left(\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}\right)^{1/2\zeta}.

Thus, as long as
m
is polynomial in
n
and
n
is sufficiently large, a typical realization of the random matrix
A
satisfies the optimal estimate on the extremal singular values.

The key to the optimal estimate is the thin-shell bound: it was shown in
[
16
,
7
]
(see also
[
2
,
4
]
) that if
\|X\|_{2}^{2}/m
exhibits non-trivial concentration around its mean, then for a typical
\theta\in S^{m-1}
,
\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle
is not far from a Gaussian random variable. Unfortunately, the notion of distance in this type of estimate is far too weak to be of use for us, but still this indicates where the extra mixing comes from: a Gaussian-like behaviour of enough marginals of
X
that implies “internal cancellation”.

1.2.
Highlights of the argument

Observe that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\|At\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}X_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\not=j}t_{i}t_{j}\left\langle X_{i},X_{j}\right\rangle.

The second term is mean-zero, and so it is reasonable to expect that

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|At\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|

is equivalent to the maximum of

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right)t_{i}^{2}\right|,

and

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\not=j}t_{i}t_{j}\left\langle X_{i},X_{j}\right\rangle\right|.

Clearly,

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right)t_{i}^{2}\right|\leq d_{T}^{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right|,

and following the premise of Question
1.1
,

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\right)t_{i}^{2}\right|\leq d_{T}^{2}\delta\ \ \text{with probability at least }1-\gamma.

Hence, to control (
1.1
) one has to derive an appropriate bound on the second term.

As it happens, some features of the proof from
[
13
]
—on the extremal singular values of random matrices with iid rows—, play a central role in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. We use some ideas from the argument in
[
13
]
to show that if
X
is suitable then
A
acts on sparse vectors almost as if it were a Gaussian matrix (see Theorem
3.1
for an accurate formulation). At the same time, the argument from
[
13
]
cannot be used to prove a version of Theorem
1.5
even in the case
T=S^{n-1}
, let alone for a general set
T
; there is not enough independence for that proof to work.

The other component of the proof is based on the method developed in
[
12
]
. We consider a deterministic matrix
A
that is well-behaved on sparse vectors (in a somewhat different sense than in
[
12
]
), and show that by randomizing the columns of
A
using independent signs
\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{n}
, one obtains a matrix
AD_{\varepsilon}=A{\rm diag}(\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{n})
that exhibits an almost Gaussian behaviour. More accurately, for every
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
k_{*}(T)\geq\log n
, and with high probability with respect to
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,

\sup_{t\in T}\left|\|AD_{\varepsilon}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq 2\delta d_{T}^{2}+c\left(d_{T}\frac{\ell_{*}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\ell_{*}^{2}(T)}{m}\right)\log^{2/\alpha}\left(\frac{en}{k_{*}(T)}\right).

The combination of those two facts (presented in Section
2
and in Section
3
), immediately leads to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Indeed, if
A
has independent columns that are symmetric random vectors then
A
and
AD_{\varepsilon}
have the same distribution.

1.3.
Notation

Throughout
absolute constants
are denoted by
c,c_{1},c_{2},\dots
. Their value may change from line to line. If a constant
c
depends on a parameter
\beta
we write
c=c(\beta)
;
a\lesssim b
denotes that
a\leq cb
for an absolute constant
c
; and
a\sim b
implies that both
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
.

If
Y
and
Z
are independent random variables or vectors and
f
is a measurable function of both,
\mathbb{E}_{Y}f
denotes the expectation only w.r.t.
Y
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{Y}f=\int f(y,Z)\,d\mathbb{P}_{Y}(y)
.

The term
random sign
refers to a Rademacher random variable, that is, a symmetric random variable taking the values
+1
and
-1
.
Similarly, we use the term
selector
for a
\{0,1\}
-valued random variable with mean
1/2
and for iid selectors
\eta=(\eta_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, we set

I_{\eta}:=\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:\eta_{i}=1\}.

For
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
let
\mathrm{proj}_{I}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the orthogonal projection on
{\rm span}(e_{i})_{i\in I}
. Thus,
\mathrm{proj}_{I}x=\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}e_{i}
. If
(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, set
y_{I}:=(y_{i})_{i\in I}
; we identify
(y_{i})_{i\in I}
with a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The
cardinality
of a (finite) set
A
is denoted by
|A|
.

Definition 1.7

(Spheres and sparse vectors)
.

For
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

\mathcal{S}_{I}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|_{2}=1\text{ and }x_{i}=0\text{ for }i\in I^{c}\}\subset S^{n-1}.

Thus,
\mathcal{S}_{I}
is the Euclidean
sphere
supported on coordinates in
I
.
For
\ell\geq 1
, set

\mathcal{S}_{I,\ell}:=\{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I}:|\{i\in I:x_{i}\neq 0\}|\leq\ell\}

to be the subset of
\mathcal{S}_{I}
consisting of
\ell
-sparse
vectors.

2.
Randomizing columns

Here we show that if a deterministic operator from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is well-behaved on sparse vectors, then an external randomization by
n
independent random signs results in an operator that, with high probability, acts on an arbitrary sets almost as if it were Gaussian.

More precisely, in this section we fix a deterministic linear operator

A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Let
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a vector consisting of iid random signs, and consider the random operator
A_{\varepsilon}
obtained by multiplying all columns of
A
with the random signs:

A_{\varepsilon}:=A\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\varepsilon_{n}\end{pmatrix}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Let
\eta=(\eta_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a vector whose coordinates are iid selectors (that are also independent of
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
), recall that
I_{\eta}=\{i:\eta_{i}=1\}
, and define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{s}}&:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{s}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{s}}}\langle Ax,Ay\rangle,\\
\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}&:=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{O}_{I_{\eta},2^{s}}\end{split}

for
s\geq 0
.

Before we go any further, let us explain what we mean by ‘
A
is well-behaved’ on sparse vectors.

Assumption 2.1

(Assumptions on
A
)
.

There are
\delta\in[0,1]
,
\alpha\in(0,2]
and
C_{A}
such that the following holds.

(1)

We have that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\big|\|Ae_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-1\big|\leq\delta.

(2)

For every
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
,

\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}\leq C_{A}\max\Big\{\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}},\frac{2^{s}}{m}\Big\}\log^{2/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big).

Remark 2.2
.

The random matrix
A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
satisfies that
Ae_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}X_{i}
. Thus, Part (1) of Assumption
2.1
is just a thin-shell bound. And, we will show in Theorem
3.1
that if
X
is suitable then the matrix
A
satisfies Part (2) of Assumption
2.1
as well (with a non-trivial probability).

The following is the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.3

(Column randomization)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
is satisfied.
Then there is an absolute constant
c_{1}
and a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(C_{A})
such that the following holds.

For every set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
k_{\ast}(T)\geq\log n
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}k_{\ast}(T))
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in T}\big|\|A_{\varepsilon}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta d_{T}^{2}+c_{2}\Big(\frac{d_{T}\ell_{\ast}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\ell^{2}_{\ast}(T)}{m}\Big)\log^{2/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{k_{\ast}(T)}\Big).

Remark 2.4
.

The situation when
k_{\ast}(T)<\log n
is explained in Section
2.7
.

Let us point out that if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\delta\leq c\sqrt{\frac{k_{\ast}(T)}{m}}

for an absolute constant
c
, then the distortion caused by
A_{\varepsilon}
coincides (up to the logarithmic term) with the distortion caused by a Gaussian operator:

Corollary 2.5
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.3
, if
\delta
satisfies (
2.2
), then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}k_{\ast}(T))
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in T}\big|\|A_{\varepsilon}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\Big(\frac{d_{T}\ell_{\ast}(T)}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\ell^{2}_{\ast}(T)}{m}\Big)\log^{2/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{k_{\ast}(T)}\Big).

2.1.
Preliminaries on chaining

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is based on a chaining argument. A comprehensive study on chaining method and their applications can be found in
[
17
]
. In what follows we will only state the few facts that are needed in the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Definition 2.6
.

A family
(T_{s})_{s\geq 0}
of subsets of
T
is an
admissible sequence
if
|T_{0}|=1
and
|T_{s}|\leq 2^{2^{s}}
for
s\geq 1
.
For
t\in T
, let
\pi_{s}t
be a point in
T_{s}
that minimizes the Euclidean distance to
t
. Set
\Delta_{0}t:=\pi_{0}t
and
\Delta_{s}:=\pi_{s+1}t-\pi_{s}t
for
s\geq 1
.

The
\gamma_{2}
functional of
T\subset(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\ \|_{2})
is defined by

\gamma_{2}(T,\|\cdot\|_{2}):=\inf_{(T_{s})_{s\geq 0}}\,\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{s\geq 0}\sqrt{2^{s}}\|\Delta_{s}t\|_{2},

where the infimum is taken over all admissible sequences of
T
.
By Talagrand’s
majorizing measure theorem
(see the presentation in
[
17
]
), there are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that, for every set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

c_{1}\ell_{\ast}(T)\leq\gamma_{2}(T,\|\cdot\|_{2})\leq c_{2}\ell_{\ast}(T).

From now on, fix an (almost) optimal admissible sequence
(T_{s})_{s\geq 0}
for
\gamma_{2}(T,\|\cdot\|_{2})
.

Remark 2.7
.

Observe that by the definition of the
\gamma_{2}
functional and the majorizing measure theorem,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{s\geq s^{\prime}}\|\Delta_{s}t\|_{2}\lesssim\frac{\ell_{\ast}(T)}{\sqrt{2^{s^{\prime}}}}

for every
s^{\prime}\geq 0
.

Fix
0\leq s_{0}\leq s_{1}
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}2^{s_{0}}&=k_{\ast}(T),\\
2^{s_{1}}&=\max\{k_{\ast}(T),m\},\end{split}

and assume without loss of generality that
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
are integers. To explain the choice of
s_{1}
, note that
\max\Big\{\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}},\frac{2^{s}}{m}\Big\}
, a term that appears in the upper bound on
\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}
in Assumption
2.1
, is attained by
\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}}
for
2^{s}<m
and by
\frac{2^{s}}{m}
otherwise. In the nontrivial case—when
k_{\ast}(T)\leq m
,
s_{1}
is when the transition between the two occurs.

Now define

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\|A_{\varepsilon}\Delta_{s}t\|_{2},

\displaystyle\Psi^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in T}\big|\|A_{\varepsilon}\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}\big|.

The first step in the proof of Theorem
2.3
is to decompose the distortion caused by
A_{\varepsilon}
into several pieces:

Lemma 2.8
.

We have that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in T}\big|\|A_{\varepsilon}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\Psi^{2}+2\Phi\sqrt{\Psi^{2}+d_{T}^{2}}+\Phi^{2}+\sup_{t\in T}\big|\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|

and

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in T}\big|\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|\leq\sup_{t\in T}\Big(\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\|\Delta_{s}t\|_{2}\Big)^{2}+2d_{T}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\|\Delta_{s}t\|_{2}.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward: for every
t\in T
,

\displaystyle\|A_{\varepsilon}t\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|A_{\varepsilon}(t-\pi_{s_{1}}t)+A_{\varepsilon}\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\|A_{\varepsilon}(t-\pi_{s_{1}}t)\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle A_{\varepsilon}(t-\pi_{s_{1}}t),A_{\varepsilon}\pi_{s_{1}}t\rangle+\|A_{\varepsilon}\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}.

Writing
t-\pi_{s_{1}}t
as the telescopic sum
\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\Delta_{s}t
, the first term in (
2.1
) is dominated by
\Phi^{2}
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\|A_{\varepsilon}\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big|\|A_{\varepsilon}\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}\big|+d_{T}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Psi^{2}+d_{T}^{2}

and by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

\displaystyle\big|\|A_{\varepsilon}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|\leq\Psi^{2}+2\Phi\sqrt{\Psi^{2}+d_{T}^{2}}+\big|\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}\big|.

As
t\in T
was arbitrary, this completes the proof of the first claim.

Turning to the second claim, note that

\displaystyle\big|\|t\|_{2}^{2}-\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|t-\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}+2|\langle t-\pi_{s_{1}}t,\pi_{s_{1}}t\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|t-\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}+2\|t-\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\|\Delta_{s}t\|_{2}\Big)^{2}+2\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\|\Delta_{s}t\|_{2}d_{T},

as required.
∎

Applying the estimate from (
2.3
) to (
2.5
), it is evident that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{t\in T}|\|\pi_{s_{1}}t\|_{2}^{2}-\|t\|_{2}^{2}|&\lesssim\frac{\ell_{\ast}^{2}(T)}{2^{s_{1}}}+\frac{d_{T}\ell_{\ast}(T)}{\sqrt{2^{s_{1}}}}\\
&\lesssim\frac{\ell_{\ast}^{2}(T)}{m}+\frac{d_{T}\ell_{\ast}(T)}{\sqrt{m}},\end{split}

where the last inequality is actually an equality if
m\geq k_{\ast}(T)
.
Thus, we may now turn our attention to estimating
\Psi
and
\Phi
, a task that requires some preparation.

2.2.
A decoupling argument

Observe that for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\|A_{\varepsilon}u\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle Ae_{i},Ae_{j}\rangle\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Ae_{i}\|_{2}^{2}u_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\langle Ae_{i},Ae_{j}\rangle\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}.

Throughout we refer to the first term (and other terms of a similar nature) as the
diagonal term
and to the second one as the
off-diagonal term
.
Thanks to Part (1) of Assumption
2.1
, the estimate on the diagonal term is immediate:

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Ae_{i}\|_{2}^{2}u_{i}^{2}-\|u\|_{2}^{2}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|Ae_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-1)u_{i}^{2}\Big|\leq\delta\|u\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore, sufficient control on the off-diagonal term would lead to an estimate on the error between
\|A_{\varepsilon}u\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|u\|_{2}^{2}
.

To that end, let us introduce the following notation.

Definition 2.9
.

For
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
and
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

\displaystyle Z_{u}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\langle Ae_{i},Ae_{j}\rangle\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j},

\displaystyle W_{I,u}

\displaystyle:=A^{\ast}A\Big(\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}u_{i}e_{i}\Big),\ \ \ {\rm and}

\displaystyle V_{I,u,v}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}u_{i}\big(W_{I^{c},v}\big)_{i}.

In addition, let
\eta
be a vector consisting of iid selectors (that are also independent of
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
) and recall that
I_{\eta}=\{i:\eta_{i}=1\}
. A standard decoupling argument shows that

(2.8)

\displaystyle 4\mathbb{E}_{\eta}V_{I_{\eta},u,u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}4\eta_{i}(1-\eta_{j})\langle Ae_{i},Ae_{j}\rangle\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}u_{i}u_{j}=Z_{u}

and in particular,

\|A_{\varepsilon}u\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Ae_{i}\|_{2}^{2}u_{i}^{2}+4\mathbb{E}_{\eta}V_{I_{\eta},u,u}

for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.3.
Preliminary estimates on Bernoulli processes

As will become clear in what follows, it is important to find estimates on
V_{I,u,v}
that hold with high probability. And, by Markov’s inequality, for a random variable
Y
and
p\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(|Y|\geq e\big(\mathbb{E}|Y|^{p}\big)^{1/p}\Big)\leq\exp(-p).

Thus, it suffices to estimate
L_{p}
-norms of
Y
for suitable choices of
p
. In the context of a chaining procedure, the natural choices are
p=2^{s}
for
s\geq 0
.

The following estimate on the growth of the (iterated) Bernoulli process
V
is a crucial component in the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
To formulate it, recall that

\mathcal{O}_{I,p}=\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,p}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},p}}\langle Ax,Ay\rangle

and

\mathcal{O}_{p}=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{O}_{I_{\eta},p}.

Proposition 2.10
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that the following holds.
Let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and set
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then for
p\geq\log n
,

\big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}|V_{I^{c},u,v}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}\leq c\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\mathcal{O}_{I,p}.

Corollary 2.11
.

For every
a>0
there is a constant
c=c(a)
, such that the following holds.
For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
2^{s}\geq\log n
, with
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}
-probability at least

1-\exp(-a2^{s}),

we have that

\displaystyle|Z_{u}|

\displaystyle\leq c\|u\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}},\ \ \ {\rm and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\eta}|V_{I_{\eta},u,v}|+\mathbb{E}_{\eta}|V_{I^{c}_{\eta},u,v}|

\displaystyle\leq c\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\,\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}.

Proof.

We shall only prove the estimate on
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}|V_{I_{\eta},u,v}|
; the required bounds on
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}|V_{I^{c}_{\eta},u,v}|
and
|Z_{u}|
follow an identical path and are omitted.

Set
f(\varepsilon,\eta)=|V_{I_{\eta},u,v}|
(as the latter depends on both random vectors
\varepsilon
and
\eta
). The aim is to show that with
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}
-probability at least
1-\exp(-a2^{s})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\eta}f(\varepsilon,\eta)\leq c(a)\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\,\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}.

Set
p=2^{s}
and denote by
\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}(\varepsilon)}
the
L_{p}
-norm taken w.r.t.
\varepsilon
. By Jensen’s inequality and the independence of
\eta
and
\varepsilon
it is evident that

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\eta}f(\varepsilon,\eta)\|_{L_{p}(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\|f(\varepsilon,\eta)\|_{L_{p}(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\mathcal{O}_{p},

where the last inequality follows from Proposition
2.10
and since
\mathcal{O}_{p}=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{O}_{I_{\eta},p}
.

By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\eta}f(\varepsilon,\eta)\geq c_{2}\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\mathcal{O}_{p}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big|\mathbb{E}_{\eta}f(\varepsilon,\eta)\big|^{p}}{(c_{2}\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\mathcal{O}_{p})^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{1}^{p}}{c_{2}^{p}}\leq\exp(-ap)

for
c_{2}:=c_{1}\exp(a)
, which completes the proof. ∎

The proof of Proposition
2.10
is based on the growth rate of moments of Bernoulli processes, established in
[
12
]
(see Equation (2.15) there). We present the proof of that estimate for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.12
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that the following holds.
Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
p\geq 1
, and
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Consider a partition of
I
to the disjoint union
\cup_{\ell}I_{\ell}
, where
I_{1}\subset I
is the set of the
p
largest coordinates of
(|a_{i}|)_{i\in I}
,
I_{2}\subset I\setminus I_{1}
is the set of the
p
largest remaining coordinates and so on. Then

\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}a_{i}b_{i}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\leq c\|a\|_{2}\max_{\ell}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell}}b\|_{2}.

Proof.

It is standard to verify that there is an absolute constant
c_{1}
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}a_{i}b_{i}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\leq\sum_{i\in I_{1}}|a_{i}b_{i}|+c_{1}\sqrt{p}\Big(\sum_{i\in I\setminus I_{1}}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Indeed, first use the triangle inequality for the
L_{p}
-norm to split the sum over
I
into a sum over
I_{1}
and
I\setminus I_{1}
.
To get the first term, use the
L_{\infty}
bound
|\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\varepsilon_{i}a_{i}b_{i}|\leq\sum_{i\in I_{1}}|a_{i}b_{i}|
.
To get the second term, use that
\sum_{i\in I\setminus I_{1}}\varepsilon_{i}a_{i}b_{i}
is subgaussian with parameter
(\sum_{i\in I\setminus I_{1}}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{2})^{1/2}
(by Hoeffding’s inequality) and the growth of the
L_{p}
-norms of subgaussian random variables (see, e.g.,
[
17
, Exercise 3.2.4]
).

The first term in (
2.9
) can be bounded by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{1}}|a_{i}b_{i}|\leq\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{1}}a\|_{2}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{1}}b\|_{2}\leq\|a\|_{2}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{1}}b\|_{2}.

Turning to the second term, let
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
be the norm in
\ell_{\infty}^{n}
.
By the choice of the sets
I_{\ell}
we have that

\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell+1}}a\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell}}a\|_{2}}{\sqrt{p}}

for every
\ell\geq 1
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I\setminus I_{1}}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{2}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{i\in I_{\ell+1}}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell+1}}a\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell+1}}b\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell}}a\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\Big)\max_{\ell\geq 1}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell+1}}b\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|a\|_{2}^{2}\max_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell+1}}b\|_{2}^{2}}{p}.

That, combined with (
2.9
) completes the proof. ∎

Proof of Proposition
2.10
.

We use a twofold application of Lemma
2.12
and Fubini’s Theorem.
Throughout fix
u,v,I,p
as in the proposition.

Step 1.

To deal with the randomness originating from
\varepsilon_{I^{c}}
, we work conditionally on
\varepsilon_{I}
.
Observe that there is a set
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{S}_{I^{c}}
of cardinality at most
\exp(c_{1}p)
such that

\big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I^{c}}}|V_{I^{c},u,v}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}\leq c_{2}\|u\|_{2}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i}.

Indeed, denote by
I^{c}=\cup_{\ell}J_{\ell}
the partition of
I^{c}
, where
J_{1}
consists of the
p
largest coordinates of
(|u_{i}|)_{i\in I^{c}}
,
J_{2}
consists of the
p
largest remaining coordinates and so on.
There are at most
\frac{n}{p}+1
such sets
J_{\ell}
and by
Lemma
2.12

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I^{c}}}|V_{I^{c},u,v}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I^{c}}}\Big|\sum_{j\in I^{c}}\varepsilon_{j}u_{j}(W_{I,v})_{j}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}\|u\|_{2}\max_{\ell}\|\mathrm{proj}_{J_{\ell}}W_{I,v}\|_{2}.

Using a standard volumetric estimate and successive approximation (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Corollary 4.1.15]
) for every
\ell
, it follows that there are sets
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{J_{\ell}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{J_{\ell}}
, each of cardinality

|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{J_{\ell}}|\leq\exp(c_{4}p)

such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{J_{\ell}}}\langle x,y\rangle\leq 2\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{J_{\ell}}}\langle x,y\rangle.

In particular,

\|\mathrm{proj}_{J_{\ell}}x\|_{2}=\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{J_{\ell}}}\langle x,y\rangle\leq 2\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{J_{\ell}}}\langle x,y\rangle

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
When applied to
x=W_{I,v}
we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{proj}_{J_{\ell}}W_{I,v}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{J_{\ell}}}\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i}

for every
\ell
.
Setting
\mathcal{Y}:=\cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{J_{\ell}}
, then
|\mathcal{Y}|\leq\exp(c_{1}p)
, because there are at most
\frac{n}{p}+1\leq 2n

\ell
’s, and
p\geq\log n
by assumption.
Note that the sets
(J_{\ell})_{\ell}
depend only on
u
.
Hence the set
\mathcal{Y}
depends only on
u
as well, and, in particular, it does not depend on
\varepsilon_{I}
.
Thus

\big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I^{c}}}|V_{I^{c},u,v}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}\leq c_{2}\|u\|_{2}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i},

which completes the proof of the first step.

Step 2.

Let us turn to the randomness originating from
\varepsilon_{I}
.
Denote by
I=\cup_{\ell}I_{\ell}
the partition of
I
, where
I_{1}
consists of the
p
largest coordinates of
(|v_{i}|)_{i\in I}
,
I_{2}
are the remaining
p
-largest coordinates and so on.
Then, for fixed but arbitrary
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, it follows from Lemma
2.12
that

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I}}\Big|\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\leq c_{3}\|v\|_{2}\max_{\ell}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell}}A^{\ast}Ay\|_{2}.

Next, still for an arbitrary
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle\max_{\ell}\|\mathrm{proj}_{I_{\ell}}A^{\ast}Ay\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{I_{\ell}}}\langle x,A^{\ast}Ay\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x\in\cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{I_{\ell}}}\max_{z\in\cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{J_{\ell}}}\langle Ax,Az\rangle\leq\mathcal{O}_{I,p}

where the first inequality follows as
\mathcal{Y}\subset\cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{J_{\ell}}
and the last inequality follows from the definition of
\mathcal{O}_{I,p}
and by noting that

\cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{I_{\ell}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{I,p}\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \cup_{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{J_{\ell}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},p}.

Combining the two observations,

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Big|\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I}}\Big|\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}|\mathcal{Y}|^{1/p}\|v\|_{2}\mathcal{O}_{I,p}

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}\|v\|_{2}\mathcal{O}_{I,p},

where the last inequality holds because
|\mathcal{Y}|\leq\exp(c_{1}p)
.

Step 3.

The proof is completed via an application of Fubini’s Theorem:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}|V_{I^{c},u,v}|^{p}=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I}}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I^{c}}}|V_{I^{c},u,v}|^{p}\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{I}}c_{2}^{p}\|u\|_{2}^{p}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Big|\sum_{i\in I}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(A^{\ast}Ay)_{i}\Big|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}^{p}\|u\|_{2}^{p}c_{5}^{p}\|v\|_{2}^{p}\mathcal{O}_{I,p}^{p}.

∎

The goal here is to estimate

Proposition 2.13

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}\frac{\ell_{\ast}(T)}{\sqrt{2^{s_{1}}}}+\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{s\geq s_{1}}\sqrt{|Z_{\Delta_{s}t}|}

∎

3.
Structural estimates on the matrix generated by

Here we show that, with a nontrivial probability, the random matrix

.

To simplify notation, set
\mathbb{X}:=(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{s}}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{s}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{s}}}\langle Ax,Ay\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{s}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{s}}}\Big\langle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\frac{X_{i}}{\sqrt{m}},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle,

As always,

Also, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I,2^{s}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{M}_{\{1,\dots,n\},2^{s}}=\max_{\{x\in S^{n-1}:|{\rm supp}(x)|\leq 2^{s}\}}\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\frac{X_{i}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\|_{2}

and recall that
X
suitable with constants
\delta
,
\gamma
,
\alpha
,
R
and

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\Big|\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}-1\Big|\leq\delta\Big)\geq 1-\gamma

\|\langle X,x\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq Lp^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\|\langle X,x\rangle\|_{L_{2}}\quad\text{for }2\leq p\leq R\cdot\log en.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\beta\geq 1
and assume that
X
is suitable with constants
\delta
,
\gamma
,
\alpha
,
R=R(\beta)
and
L
. Then there is a constant
c_{1}(\alpha,\beta,L)
such that, with probability at least
1-\gamma-n^{-\beta}
, for every
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}\leq c_{1}\max\Big\{\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}},\frac{2^{s}}{m}\Big\}\log^{2/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big).

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Throughout this section we fix the exponent
\beta
and assume that
X
is suitable with an appropriate constant
R=R(\beta)
for a value that is specified in what follows.
Also, denote by
(y^{\ast}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
the monotone nonincreasing rearrangement of
(|y_{i}|)_{i\in I}
; in particular,
y^{\ast}_{1}=\max_{i\in I}|y_{i}|
and so on. Hence, for a vector
(y_{i})_{i\in I}
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{s}}}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}y_{i}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{s}}y_{i}^{\ast 2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Lemma 3.2

(Dimension reduction)
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and, for every
1\leq 2^{r}\leq n
and
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, there are sets

\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}\text{ such that }|\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}^{\prime}|\leq\exp\Big(c_{1}2^{r}\log\frac{en}{2^{r}}\Big)

for which the following holds:
setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big(\sum_{i=2^{r-1}+1}^{2^{r}}\Big\langle X_{i},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle^{\ast 2}\Big)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{I^{c},2^{r}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I,2^{r-1}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big(\sum_{j=2^{r-1}+1}^{2^{r}}\Big\langle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\frac{X_{i}}{\sqrt{m}},X_{j}\Big\rangle^{\ast 2}\Big)^{1/2},

we have that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\Big(\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{0}}+\sum_{r=1}^{s}\big(\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}+\mathcal{F}_{I^{c},2^{r}}\big)\Big).

Let us stress that, in the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}
, the monotone nonincreasing rearrangement is only with respect to coordinates in
I
.
That is, the sum appearing in
\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}
equals
\sum_{i=2^{r-1}+1}^{2^{r}}y_{i}^{\ast 2}
where
(y^{\ast}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is the monotone nonincreasing rearrangement of the vector
(|y_{i}|)_{i\in I}
defined by
y_{i}:=\langle X_{i},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\rangle
for
i\in I
.
Similarly, the monotone nonincreasing rearrangement in
\mathcal{F}_{I^{c},2^{r}}
is only with respect to coordinates in
I^{c}
.

Proof.

Let
c_{3}\geq 2
be an absolute constant to be chosen later and set

\delta_{2^{r}}:=\Big(\frac{2^{r}}{en}\Big)^{c_{3}}

for
1\leq 2^{r}\leq n
.
Observe that there are
\binom{n}{2^{r}}
subsets of
\{1,...,n\}
of cardinality
2^{r}
. Therefore, by a standard volumetric estimate and a successive approximation argument, there are sets
\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}
of cardinality as at most

\exp\Big(2^{r}\log\frac{en}{2^{r}\delta_{2^{r}}}\Big)=\exp\Big((1+c_{3})2^{r}\log\frac{en}{2^{r}}\Big)

such that

\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}}\langle x,y\rangle\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}^{\prime}}\langle x,y\rangle

for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The analogous statement holds for
\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{r}}
.

By the definition of
\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r}}
and interchanging the order of two maxima,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r}}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\langle A^{\ast}Ax,y\rangle\leq\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}}\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{r}}^{\prime}}\langle A^{\ast}Ax,y\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{r}}^{\prime}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}}\langle Ax,Ay\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r}}}\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\Big\langle X_{i},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle.

Applying (
3.1
) to the last term, it is evident that

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{r}}\Big\langle X_{i},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle^{\ast 2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Splitting the sum over
i=1,\dots,2^{r}
into a sum over
\{1,...,2^{r-1}\}
and over
\{2^{r-1}+1,...,2^{r}\}
,

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{r}}\Big\langle X_{i},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle^{\ast 2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\cdot\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{r-1}}\Big\langle X_{i},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle^{\ast 2}\Big)^{1/2}+\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}.

Using (
3.1
) once again,

\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\Big(\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r-1}}}\Big\langle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\frac{X_{i}}{\sqrt{m}},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle+\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}\Big).

Applying the same arguments (with the roles of
x
and
y
reversed), we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{I,2^{r-1}}}\Big\langle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\frac{X_{i}}{\sqrt{m}},\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}\frac{X_{j}}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r-1}}}\Big(\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r-1}}+\mathcal{F}_{I^{c},2^{r}}\Big)

and combining the two estimates,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r}}}\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2^{r-1}}}\Big(\mathcal{O}_{I,2^{r-1}}+\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}+\mathcal{F}_{I^{c},2^{r}}\Big).

Finally note that

\prod_{1\leq 2^{r}\leq n}(1-\delta_{2^{r}})(1-\delta_{2^{r-1}})\geq c_{4},

for a constant
c_{4}=c_{4}(c_{3})>0
.
Hence (
3.3
) can be applied iteratively starting with

and until

—thus completing the proof.
∎

In order to derive probabilistic estimates on the terms that appear in Lemma

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
be constants.
Then there are constants
R=R(c_{1},c_{2},\beta)
and
c_{3}=c_{3}(c_{1},c_{2},\alpha,\beta,L)
such that the following holds.
Let
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
and let
\mathcal{Z}
be a collection of random variables satisfying

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Z}|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(c_{1}2^{s}\log\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big),

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq Lp^{1/\alpha}\|Z\|_{L_{2}}\quad\text{for every }2\leq p\leq R\cdot\log en\text{ and every }Z\in\mathcal{Z}.

Then, for every
u\geq e
, with probability at least

1-\exp\Big(-c_{2}\beta(\log u)\cdot 2^{s}\log\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big),

\max_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}Z^{\ast}_{2^{s}}\leq c_{3}u\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big)\max_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|Z\|_{L^{2}};

here
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
denote independent copies of each random variable
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

Set
U:=\max_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|Z\|_{L_{2}}
and let
v\geq 0
.
Using the union bound, a binomial estimate, and the independence
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
for each
Z
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}Z^{\ast}_{2^{s}}\geq vU\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{P}\Big(\text{there is }I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}\text{ s.t.\ }|I|={2^{s}}\text{ and }|Z_{i}|\geq vU\text{ for all }i\in I\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\binom{n}{{2^{s}}}\mathbb{P}^{2^{s}}\big(|Z|\geq vU\big)

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\exp\Big({2^{s}}\log\frac{en}{{2^{s}}}+2^{s}\log\mathbb{P}(|Z|\geq vU)\Big).

Set

R:=c_{1}+c_{2}\beta+1\quad\text{and}\quad p:=R\log\frac{en}{{2^{s}}},

and assume without loss of generality that
p\geq 2
.
Then, for
v:=Lp^{1/\alpha}u
, Markov’s inequality and the assumption on the growth of moments imply that

\max_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{P}\big(|Z|\geq vU\big)\leq\Big(\frac{p^{1/\alpha}U}{vU}\Big)^{p}=u^{-p}.

The claim follows from (
3.4
) because
v\leq c_{3}(c_{1},c_{2},\alpha,\beta,L)u\log^{1/\alpha}(\frac{en}{{2^{s}}})
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

There is a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(\alpha,\beta,L)
such that, for every
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
, with probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
at least
1-n^{-2\beta}
,

\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big)\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}.

Proof.

Following the notation of Lemma
3.2
, one may integrate with respect to
\eta
Inequality (
3.2
), obtained in Lemma
3.2
. Hence,

\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}\leq c_{2}\Big(\mathcal{O}_{2^{0}}+\sum_{r=1}^{s}\big(\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{E}_{I_{\eta},2^{r}}+\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{F}_{I^{c}_{\eta},2^{r}}\big)\Big).

We shall only estimate the terms
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{E}_{I_{\eta},2^{r}}
; the required bounds on
\mathcal{O}_{2^{0}}
and on
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{F}_{I^{c}_{\eta},2^{r}}
are based on the same argument and are omitted.

Let us show that, with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
-probability at least
1-n^{-2\beta}
,

\sum_{r=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{E}_{I_{\eta},2^{r}}\leq c_{1}(\alpha,\beta,L)\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big)\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}.

Step 1.
Fix
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Then, for
1\leq 2^{r}\leq n
,

\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{r}}{m}}\Big\langle X_{i},\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}X_{j}\Big\rangle^{\ast}_{2^{r-1}}.

Indeed, this holds by replacing all the terms in
\{2^{r-1}+1,\dots,2^{r}\}
by the largest one.

Consider the set

\mathcal{Z}:=\Big\{\Big\langle X,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{j\in I^{c}}y_{j}X_{j}\Big\rangle:y\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}\Big\}.

Note that
\mathcal{Z}
consist of at most
|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I^{c},2^{r}}|
elements, i.e., it is of cardinality at most
\exp(c_{3}2^{r}\log\frac{en}{2^{r}})
(see Lemma
3.2
). Moreover, for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
and every
2\leq p\leq R\log en
,

\|Z\|_{L_{p}}\leq Lp^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\|Z\|_{L_{2}}\leq Lp^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathcal{M}_{I^{c},2^{r}}.

Applying Lemma
3.3
(conditionally on
(X_{i})_{i\in I^{c}}
), and for an absolute constant
c_{4}
to be chosen later, there are constants
R=R(\beta,c_{3},c_{4})
and
c_{5}=c_{5}(c_{3},c_{4},\alpha,\beta,L)
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}_{X_{I}}\Big(\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}\geq c_{5}u\sqrt{\frac{2^{r}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{r}}\Big)\mathcal{M}_{I^{c},2^{r}}\Big)\\
&\leq\exp\Big(-c_{4}\beta(\log u)2^{r}\log\frac{en}{2^{r}}\Big)\end{split}

for every
u\geq e
.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{I^{c},2^{r}}\leq\mathcal{M}_{2^{r}}
for every realization of
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, it follows from Fubini’s Theorem that one may replace
\mathbb{P}_{X_{I}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{I^{c},2^{r}}
in (
3.5
) by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2^{r}}
, respectively.
In particular, by the union bound, with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
-probability at least

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{r=1}^{s}\exp\Big(-c_{4}\beta(\log u)2^{r}\log\frac{en}{2^{r}}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\big(-c_{4}c_{6}\beta(\log u)\log n\big)

\displaystyle=1-u^{-c_{4}c_{6}\beta\log n},

we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{s}\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}\sum_{r=1}^{s}u\sqrt{\frac{2^{r}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{r}}\Big)\mathcal{M}_{2^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{7}c_{5}u\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big)\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}=:u\cdot(\ast).

Let the constant
c_{4}
be large enough to ensure that
c_{4}c_{6}\geq 4
.
Thus, using tail integration,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\Big|\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{s}\mathcal{E}_{I,2^{r}}}{(\ast)}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\leq c_{8}

for
p:=2\beta\log n
.

Step 2.

As (
3.6
) holds for any
I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, one can integrate over
\eta
and apply Jensen’s inequality (just as in the proof of Corollary
2.11
). Hence,

\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{X}}\Big|\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{E}_{I_{\eta},2^{r}}}{(\ast)}\Big|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\leq c_{8},

and by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\mathcal{E}_{I_{\eta},2^{r}}\geq c_{8}e\cdot(\ast)\Big)\leq\exp(-p)=n^{-2\beta},

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The proof relies on a self-bounding argument.
In a first step, following the decoupling argument detailed in Section
2.2
, for every
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\{x\in S^{n-1}:|{\rm supp}(x)|\leq 2^{s}\}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{m}x_{i}x_{j}\frac{\langle X_{i},X_{j}\rangle}{m}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}+4\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}.

The assumption that
X
is suitable implies that, with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
-probability at least
1-\gamma
, for every
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2+4\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}.

Next, observe that

|\{s:1\leq 2^{s}\leq n\}|\leq\log n\quad\text{and}\quad(\log n)n^{-2\beta}\leq n^{-\beta}.

Combining Lemma
3.4
and the union bound, there is a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(\alpha,\beta,L)
, such that, with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
-probability at least
1-n^{-\beta}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{2^{s}}\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big)\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}

for all
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
.

Thus, on the intersection of the events on which (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) hold (which has
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
-probability at least
1-\gamma-n^{-\beta}
) we conclude that

\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}\leq\max\Big\{\sqrt{2},c_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{m}}\log^{1/\alpha}\Big(\frac{en}{2^{s}}\Big)\Big\}

for all
1\leq 2^{s}\leq n
.
Using this estimate for
\mathcal{M}_{2^{s}}
in (
3.8
) completes the proof. ∎
