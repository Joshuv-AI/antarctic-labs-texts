Remarks on Loss Function of Threshold Method for Ordinal Regression Problem
Ryoya Yamasaki, Toshiyuki Tanaka

Abstract
Threshold methods are popular for ordinal regression problems, which are classification problems for data with a natural ordinal relation. They learn a one-dimensional transformation (1DT) of observations of the explanatory variable, and then assign label predictions to the observations by thresholding their 1DT values. In this paper, we study the influence of the underlying data distribution and of the learning procedure of the 1DT on the classification performance of the threshold method via theoretical considerations and numerical experiments. Consequently, for example, we found that threshold methods based on typical learning procedures may perform poorly when the probability distribution of the target variable conditioned on an observation of the explanatory variable tends to be non-unimodal. Another instance of our findings is that learned 1DT values are concentrated at a few points under the learning procedure based on a piecewise-linear loss function, which can make difficult to classify data well.

Introduction

Ordinal regression
(
OR
, or called ordinal classification) is
the classification of
ordinal data
in which the underlying target variable is
labeled from a categorical
ordinal scale
that is considered to be equipped
with a
natural ordinal relation
for the underlying explanatory variable,
as formalized in Section
2.1
.
The ordinal scale is typically formed as
a graded summary of objective indicators like age groups
{‘0–9’, ‘10–19’, …, ‘90–99’, ‘100–’}
or graded evaluation of subjectivity like human rating
{‘excellent’, ‘good’, ‘average’, ‘bad’, ‘terrible’}.
OR techniques are employed in a variety of practical applications,
for example, age estimation
(
Niu et al. 2016
;
Cao et al. 2020
)
,
information retrieval
(
Liu 2011
)
,
movie rating
(
Yu et al. 2006
)
, and
questionnaire survey
(
Bürkner and Vuorre 2019
)
.

Threshold methods
are popular for OR problems
as a simple way to capture the ordinal relation of ordinal data,
and have been studied vigorously in machine learning research

(
Shashua and Levin 2003
;
Lin and Li 2006
;
Chu and Keerthi 2007
;
Lin and Li 2012
;
Li and Lin 2007
;
Pedregosa et al. 2017
;
Yamasaki 2023
)
.
Those methods learn a
one-dimensional transformation (1DT)

of the observation of the explanatory variable so that an observation
with a larger class label tends to have a larger 1DT value;
they then assign a label prediction to the learned 1DT according to
the rank of an interval to which the 1DT belongs among intervals
on the real line separated by
threshold parameters
.
See also Section
2.2
for their formulation.

Despite its popularity, the question of what data distribution
threshold methods will work well or poorly for is not sufficiently elucidated.
For example,
Rennie and Srebro 2005
;
Gutierrez et al. 2015

performed numerical experiments to compare the classification
performance of OR methods including threshold methods,
but did not attempt to elucidate factors behind the difference in those performances.
Therefore, with the aim of providing an answer to this question,
we study the influence of the underlying data
distribution and of the learning procedure of the 1DT on
the classification performance of the threshold method
(see Section
2.3
for a mathematical
formulation of the research objectives of this work).
We present theoretical analysis and conjectures in Section
3
,
and verify these results via numerical experiments as described
in Sections
4

and Appendix
A
.

2.1
Formulation of Ordinal Regression Tasks and Ordinal Data

2.1.1
Ordinal Regression Tasks

Suppose that we are interested in behaviors of the categorical target variable

Y\in[K]:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
given a value of the explanatory variable
{\bm{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
and that we can access data
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[K]

that are considered to be independent and identically distributed observations
of the pair
({\bm{X}},Y)
and have a natural ordinal relation like examples described
in Section
1
, where
K,d,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
K\geq 3
;
we call such data ordinal data.
An OR task is a classification task for ordinal data
and often formulated as searching for a classifier
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[K]

that is good in minimization of the
task risk

\bbE[\ell(f({\bm{X}}),Y)]

for a user-specified
task loss function

\ell:[K]^{2}\to[0,+\infty)
,
where the expectation
\bbE[\cdot]
is taken for
all included random variables (here
{\bm{X}}
and
Y
).
2
2

2

For evaluation in OR tasks, one may use criteria that cannot be
decomposed into a sum of losses for each sample point such as
quadratic weighted kappa
(
Cohen 1960
;
Cohen 1968
)
.
One of the main topics in this paper concerns troubles caused in relation
to the learning procedure (defined later in Section
2.2.2
),
and those troubles are there similarly even for such other criteria.
Our formulation is not comprehensive for the OR task, but
restricting the formulation does not seriously affect our discussion.

Popular task loss functions for OR tasks include not only
the zero-one task loss
\ell_{\rm zo}(k,l):=\bbI(k\neq l)

for minimization of misclassification rate, but also V-shaped losses
for cost-sensitive classification tasks that reflect user’s preference
of smaller prediction errors over larger ones such as
the absolute task loss
\ell_{\rm ab}(k,l):=|k-l|
,
squared task loss
\ell_{\rm sq}(k,l):=(k-l)^{2}
, and

\ell_{{\rm zo},\epsilon}(k,l):=\bbI(|k-l|>\epsilon)
with
\epsilon>0
,
where
\bbI(c)
is 1 if the condition
c
is true and 0 otherwise.

2.1.2
Ordinal Data

OR tasks assume that ordinal data have a natural ordinal relation.
As the natural ordinal relation, in this work,
we basically suppose the
unimodality hypothesis
adopted in

da Costa et al. 2008
;
Beckham and Pal 2017
;
Yamasaki 2022
.
Namely, we assume that many ordinal data are almost unimodal,
on the basis of the following terminologies:
For a
probability mass function
(
PMF
)
{\bm{p}}=(p_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\Delta_{K-1}:=\{(q_{k})_{k\in[K]}\mid\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}=1;q_{k}\in[0,1],\forall k=[K]\}
,
we call
M({\bm{p}})\in\argmax_{k}(p_{k})_{k\in[K]}
a
mode
of
{\bm{p}}
,
and say that
{\bm{p}}
is
unimodal
if it satisfies
p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{M({\bm{p}})}

and
p_{M({\bm{p}})}\geq\cdots\geq p_{K}
for every mode
M({\bm{p}})
.
Moreover, if the
conditional probability distribution
(
CPD
)

(\Pr(Y=y|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}}))_{y\in[K]}
of the target variable
Y
conditioned
on an observation
{\bm{x}}
of the explanatory variable
{\bm{X}}

is unimodal for any
{\bm{x}}
in the support
{\mathcal{X}}

of the probability distribution of
{\bm{X}}
,
we say that the data is
unimodal
;
if the CPD
(\Pr(Y=y|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}}))_{y\in[K]}
is unimodal at any
{\bm{x}}
in
a sub-domain
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
with a large or small probability
\Pr({\bm{X}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0})
,
we say that the data is
almost unimodal
or
almost non-unimodal
.

Yamasaki 2022
verified that many data,
which have been treated as ordinal data by other previous OR
studies
(
Chu and Ghahramani 2005
;
Gutierrez et al. 2015
)
,
tend almost unimodal.

As further preparation, we introduce qualitative notions regarding scale of the data as well:
For a PMF
{\bm{p}}=(p_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\Delta_{K-1}
having a mode
m\in[K]
,
we call the degree of spread of
{\bm{p}}
around
m
the
scale
of
{\bm{p}}
.
Refer to Figure
1
;
we say that a more right PMF in Figure
1

has a larger scale than a more left one.
If the scale of the CPD
{\bm{p}}({\bm{x}})=(\Pr(Y=y|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}}))_{y\in[K]}
tends to be large or small
over whole the support of the probability distribution of the explanatory variable
{\bm{X}}
,
we say that the data has large scale or small scale.
Also, if the scale of the CPD
{\bm{p}}({\bm{x}})
is similar or dissimilar over whole the support,
we say that the data is
homoscedastic
or
heteroscedastic
.
In particular, we refer to heteroscedastic data as

mode-wise heteroscedastic
or
overall heteroscedastic
data,
if the scale of their CPD
{\bm{p}}({\bm{x}})
is similar or can be dissimilar in
each domain where the conditional mode
M({\bm{p}}({\bm{x}}))
is the same.

{\bm{p}}=(p_{k})_{k\in[10]}
with the mode 5.

2.2
Formulation of Threshold Methods

2.2.1
Overview

A
1DT-based method

(
Yamasaki 2023
)
is formulated with two procedures,

learning procedure
and
labeling procedure
of the 1DT.
In the learning procedure, 1DT-based methods learn
a 1DT (a real-valued function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
) of
the explanatory variable
{\bm{X}}
based on the data
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n})
.
In the labeling procedure,
1DT-based methods construct a classifier
f
as
f=h\circ\hat{a}

with a learned 1DT
\hat{a}
and a
labeling function

h:{\mathbb{R}}\to[K]
.
Many of those methods predict a label for
{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}}
as
f({\bm{x}})=h_{\rm thr}(\hat{a}({\bm{x}});{\bm{t}})

using a
threshold labeling function
,

\displaystyle h_{\rm thr}(u;{\bm{t}}):=1+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\bbI(u\geq t_{k})

(1)

with a
threshold parameter vector

{\bm{t}}=(t_{k})_{k\in[K-1]}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}
.
In particular, such 1DT-based methods that employ a threshold labeling
function are called threshold methods or threshold models,
and have been studied actively in machine learning research

(
Shashua and Levin 2002
;
Chu and Keerthi 2005
;
Rennie and Srebro 2005
;
Lin and Li 2006
;
Lin and Li 2012
;
Pedregosa et al. 2017
;
Cao et al. 2020
;
Yamasaki 2023
)
.
The succeeding two sections detail the learning and labeling procedures further.

2.2.2
Learning Procedure

The learning procedure of many existing threshold methods is designed
according to the idea of the
surrogate risk minimization

(
Pedregosa et al. 2017
)
that allows for easier
continuous optimization instead of directly minimizing the task risk.
In this work, we study threshold methods that can be seen
to learn a 1DT
a:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
from a function class
{\mathcal{A}}
and a

bias parameter vector

{\bm{b}}=(b_{k})_{k\in[K-1]}
from a class
{\mathcal{B}}

by minimizing the
empirical surrogate risk

for a specified
surrogate loss function

\phi
,

\displaystyle\min_{a\in{\mathcal{A}},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathcal{B}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(a({\bm{x}}_{i}),{\bm{b}},y_{i})

(2)

(we denote the minimizer
(\hat{a},\hat{{\bm{b}}})

with
\hat{{\bm{b}}}:=(\hat{b}_{k})_{k\in[K-1]}
)
or its regularized version.
Discussion in the main part of this paper focuses on
the non-regularized (empirical) surrogate risk minimization;
see Appendix
B
for supplemental discussion on the regularized version.

In this work, we consider popular surrogate loss functions
in the class of
all-threshold (AT) losses
or

immediate-threshold (IT) losses

(
Pedregosa et al. 2017
)
.
An AT loss is representable as

\displaystyle\phi(a({\bm{x}}),{\bm{b}},y)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\varphi(b_{k}-a({\bm{x}}))&\text{if~}y=1,\\
\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\varphi(a({\bm{x}})-b_{k})&\text{if~}y=K,\\
\sum_{k=1}^{y-1}\varphi(a({\bm{x}})-b_{k})+\sum_{k=y}^{K-1}\varphi(b_{k}-a({\bm{x}}))&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3)

and an IT loss is representable as

\displaystyle\phi(a({\bm{x}}),{\bm{b}},y)=\begin{cases}\varphi(b_{1}-a({\bm{x}}))&\text{if~}y=1,\\
\varphi(a({\bm{x}})-b_{K-1})&\text{if~}y=K,\\
\varphi(a({\bm{x}})-b_{y-1})+\varphi(b_{y}-a({\bm{x}}))&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

with a certain function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty)
.
As a function
\varphi
, one often applies a convex loss function
that is an upper bound of the function
u\mapsto\bbI(u\leq 0)

and commonly used in binary classification.

As a class of the 1DT
{\mathcal{A}}
, one applies, for example, a linear model class

\{{\bm{x}}\mapsto{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+w_{0}\mid{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},w_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
and
a neural network model with a fixed network architecture
and learnable bias and weight parameters.
In this paper, we do not discuss the design of the class of the 1DT,
and simply assume
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\{a:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}

in the following discussion.

The AT and IT surrogate losses

\phi(a({\bm{x}}),{\bm{b}},y)
have the translation invariance:

\phi(a({\bm{x}}),{\bm{b}},y)=\phi(a({\bm{x}})+c,{\bm{b}}+c\bm{1}_{K-1},y)
with
all-1 vector
\bm{1}_{K-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}
for any
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Also, many methods incorporate
the order constraint
b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{K-1}
on the bias parameter vector
{\bm{b}}
.
Thus, we study the popular instances of the class
{\mathcal{B}}
,

{\mathcal{B}}_{0}:=\{{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}\mid b_{1}=0\}
(
non-ordered class
) and

{\mathcal{B}}^{\rm ord}_{0}:=\{{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}\mid b_{1}=0\leq b_{2}\leq\cdots\leq b_{K-1}\}
(
ordered class
).
The order constraint on the bias parameter vector
{\bm{b}}
can be implemented,
for example, by
b_{k+1}=b_{k}+c_{k}^{2}
for
k\in[K-2]
with other parameters

c_{1},\ldots,c_{K-2}\in{\mathbb{R}}

(
Franses and Paap 2001
)
.

2.2.3
Labeling Procedure

The threshold labeling
h_{\rm thr}(u;{\bm{t}})
is non-decreasing in
u\in{\mathbb{R}}

and the simplest labeling function in the sense that the resulting
1DT-based classifier has only
(K-1)
decision boundaries
for the learned 1DT at most
(
Yamasaki 2023
)
.
Partly because of this simplicity, the threshold labeling
is preferred for many 1DT-based methods.

Pedregosa et al. 2017
;
Cao et al. 2020
adopt
the learned bias parameter vector
\hat{{\bm{b}}}
to the threshold parameter vector
{\bm{t}}
,
and
Shashua and Levin 2003
;
Chu and Keerthi 2007
use
the labeling function
h(u)=\min(\{k\in[K-1]\mid u<\hat{b}_{k}\}\cup\{K\})
.
The latter labeling function is also a certain threshold labeling,
which is same as (resp. different from) the former
h_{\rm thr}(\cdot;\hat{{\bm{b}}})

when
\hat{{\bm{b}}}
are ordered (resp. non-ordered)

(
Yamasaki 2023
, Proposition 2)
.

Yamasaki 2023
proposed to use the

empirical optimal threshold parameter vector

that minimizes the empirical task risk,

\hat{{\bm{t}}}\in\argmin_{{\bm{t}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(h_{\rm thr}(\hat{a}({\bm{x}}_{i});{\bm{t}}),y_{i})

for the learned 1DT
\hat{a}
and specified task loss
\ell
.
This minimization problem can be solved by
a dynamic-programming-based algorithm
(
Lin and Li 2006
)

or computationally efficient parallel algorithm
(
Yamasaki and Tanaka 2024
)
for tasks with a task loss function
that is convex with respect to the first argument.
Also,
Yamasaki 2023
experimentally confirmed that
this labeling function can lead to better classification performance
than other labeling functions including the above-mentioned two previous ones.
Therefore, we suppose to employ the empirical optimal
threshold parameter vector
\hat{{\bm{t}}}
in this work.

2.3
Research Objectives of This Work and Related Works

We are ultimately interested in the
generalization error

(or
prediction error
)
\bbE[\ell(f({\bm{X}}),Y)]
for the classifier

f(\cdot)=\hat{f}(\cdot):=h_{\rm thr}(\hat{a}(\cdot);\hat{{\bm{t}}})

of the threshold method, where the learned 1DT
\hat{a}

and learned threshold parameter vector
\hat{{\bm{t}}}

are obtained by the two-step optimization

\displaystyle(\hat{a},\hat{{\bm{b}}})\in\underset{a\in{\mathcal{A}},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathcal{B}}}{\argmin}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(a({\bm{x}}_{i}),{\bm{b}},y_{i}),\quad\hat{{\bm{t}}}\in\underset{{\bm{t}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}}{\argmin}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(h_{\rm thr}(\hat{a}({\bm{x}}_{i});{\bm{t}}),y_{i}).

(5)

The generalization error
\bbE[\ell(\hat{f}({\bm{X}}),Y)]
is
often understood through the decomposition into
the
approximation error
and
estimation error
,

\displaystyle\bbE[\ell(\hat{f}({\bm{X}}),Y)]=\underbrace{\bbE[\ell(\bar{f}({\bm{X}}),Y)]}_{\text{approximation error}}+\underbrace{\bbE[\ell(\hat{f}({\bm{X}}),Y)]-\bbE[\ell(\bar{f}({\bm{X}}),Y)]}_{\text{estimation error}},

(6)

like
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
, where
the classifier
\bar{f}(\cdot):=h_{\rm thr}(\bar{a}(\cdot);\bar{{\bm{t}}})

is defined with the surrogate risk minimizer
(\bar{a},\bar{{\bm{b}}})

and the optimal threshold parameter vector
\bar{{\bm{t}}}
:

\displaystyle(\bar{a},\bar{{\bm{b}}})\in\underset{a\in{\mathcal{A}},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathcal{B}}}{\argmin}\,\bbE[\phi(a({\bm{X}}),{\bm{b}},Y)],\quad\bar{{\bm{t}}}\in\underset{{\bm{t}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}}{\argmin}\,\bbE[\ell(h_{\rm thr}(\bar{a}({\bm{X}});{\bm{t}}),Y)].

(7)

Behaviors of the generalization error
\bbE[\ell(f({\bm{X}}),Y)]
for
a classifier of the threshold method have not been sufficiently understood.
One important theoretical analysis of the threshold method
is
Lin and Li 2006
;
Lin and Li 2012
;
Li and Lin 2007
:
they gave a sample-based probably-approximately-correct
bound of the generalization error
\bbE[\ell(f({\bm{X}}),Y)]

for a classifier
f(\cdot)=h_{\rm thr}(a(\cdot);{\bm{t}})

with a boosting-based 1DT
a
and ordered threshold parameter vector
{\bm{t}}
.
Their bound has a term of the order
{\mathcal{O}}(\log(n)/\sqrt{n})
regarding
the sample size
n
and another sample-based constant-order term,
which are interpretable as corresponding to the estimation and approximation errors respectively.
However, the influence of the learning procedure of the threshold method on
that constant-order approximation error term has not been sufficiently studied.

Therefore, in this work, we focus only on the approximation error as a first step.
In general, as the training sample size
n
increases,
the estimation error is expected to approach zero,
and in such cases the approximation error becomes
dominant in the generalization error.
Thus, we expect that our consideration will be
suggestive for behaviors of the generalization error of
the threshold method with the sufficiently large sample size
n
.

Typical binary or multi-class classification methods such as
logistic regression have a guarantee that their surrogate risk
minimization with the most representable model class possible
(counterpart of
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})
) yields a
Bayes optimal classifier

(i.e., approximation error becomes the so-called
Bayes error

\min_{f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[K]}\bbE[\ell(f({\bm{X}}),Y)]
)
for any probability distribution of
({\bm{X}},Y)
;
such a guarantee is known as
Fisher consistency
or classification calibration

(
Bartlett et al. 2006
;
Tewari and Bartlett 2007
;
Liu 2007
;
Pires et al. 2013
)
.
In contrast, most threshold methods do not,
as described in
Pedregosa et al. 2017
,
namely,
\bbE[\ell(\bar{f}({\bm{X}}),Y)]
defined via (
7
) is
not necessarily equal to
\min_{f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[K]}\bbE[\ell(f({\bm{X}}),Y)]

even if
{\mathcal{A}}=\{a:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
.
In other words, deviation of the approximation error of a threshold method from
the Bayes error generally varies depending on the underlying data distribution
and its learning procedure.

Therefore, we study the dependence of the underlying data distribution and of
the learning procedure of the 1DT on the approximation error of the threshold method,
in order to qualitatively understand what threshold methods work well or poorly for what data.
It aims to advance the previous theoretical result
(
Pedregosa et al. 2017
, Section 4)

that just suggests that many threshold methods do not have Fisher consistency,
and refine the experimental comparison of loss functions
regarding the classification performance of the classifier
h_{\rm thr}(\hat{a}(\cdot);\hat{{\bm{b}}})

by
Rennie and Srebro 2005
;
Gutierrez et al. 2015

to a theoretical comparison regarding the classification performance of the classifier
\hat{f}
.
Also, this study is the first attempt to discuss properties of threshold methods
under a more in-depth characterization of the natural ordinal relation
(here, the unimodal hypothesis) as far as we know.

3
Analyses and Conjectures

3.1
All-Threshold (AT) Loss

3.1.1
Order of Optimized Bias Parameters

In Section
3.1
,
we give analysis of the surrogate risk minimization of
the threshold method that uses an AT surrogate loss function,
and conjecture about what data distributions
the resulting classifier will perform well or poorly for.

We first discuss behaviors of the optimized bias parameter vector in Section
3.1.1
.
The following theorem states that, for many popular AT surrogate loss functions,
minimization of the (empirical) surrogate risk yields the bias parameter vector
that satisfies the order condition even without imposing the order condition.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\{a:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{\rm ord}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{0}

and introduce the conditions

(
a1
)

\varphi(u_{1})-\varphi(-u_{1})-\varphi(u_{2})+\varphi(-u_{2})\geq 0
for any
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
s.t.
u_{1}<u_{2}
;

(
a2
)

\varphi(u)
is non-increasing in
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
(e.g.,
\varphi=\varphi_{\rm ramp}
);

(
a3
)

\varphi(u)-\varphi(-u)
is non-increasing in
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
(e.g.,
\varphi=\varphi_{\rm abso}
);

(
a4
)

\varphi(u_{1})-\varphi(-u_{1})-\varphi(u_{2})+\varphi(-u_{2})>0
for any
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
s.t.
u_{1}<u_{2}
;

(
a5
)

\varphi(u)
is decreasing in
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
(e.g.,
\varphi=\varphi_{\rm logi},\varphi_{\rm expo}
);

(
a6

3.2.3
Overlap of Optimized Bias Parameters

For an IT loss
\phi
, a minimizer
(a,{\bm{b}})=(\bar{a}^{\prime},\bar{{\bm{b}}}^{\prime})

of the surrogate risk
\bbE[\phi(a({\bm{X}}),{\bm{b}},Y)]
over
{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{B}}_{0}

has no guarantee to satisfy the order condition unlike that for an AT loss,
namely, it may satisfy
\bar{{\bm{b}}}^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{\rm ord}
.
When
\bar{{\bm{b}}}^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{\rm ord}
,
the bias parameter vector of the surrogate risk minimizer

(a,{\bm{b}})=(\bar{a},\bar{{\bm{b}}})
over
{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{\rm ord}

has overlapped components.
Such a situation is not only exemplified in
Theorem
5
(
f3
), but occurs frequently in practice.
In this section, we describe an inherent trouble of the threshold method
that adopts an IT loss function and the ordered class of the bias parameter vector,
owing to the overlap of optimized bias parameters.

Assume that the surrogate risk minimizer
(\bar{a},\bar{{\bm{b}}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{k}=c\text{ for all }k\in\{l,\ldots,m\}\text{ with some }c\in{\mathbb{R}},l,m\in[K-1]\text{ s.t. }l<m.

(14)

Under this setting, the conditional surrogate risk at
{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}}
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bbE[\phi(\bar{a}({\bm{x}}),\bar{{\bm{b}}},Y)]&=\Pr(Y\in\{l+1,\ldots,m\}|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}})\{\varphi(\bar{a}({\bm{x}})-c)+\varphi(c-\bar{a}({\bm{x}}))\}\\
&+\sum_{y\in\{\ldots,l,m+1,\ldots\}}\Pr(Y=y|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}})\{\varphi(\bar{a}({\bm{x}})-\bar{b}_{y-1})+\varphi(\bar{b}_{y}-\bar{a}({\bm{x}}))\}.\end{split}

(15)

If
\Pr(Y\in\{\ldots,l,m+1,\ldots\}|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}})=0
for
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}

with a some domain
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
the optimized 1DT value
\bar{a}({\bm{x}})\in\argmin_{a({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}}\bbE[\phi(a({\bm{x}}),\bar{{\bm{b}}},Y)]

for
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
is determined independently of the conditional probabilities

\Pr(Y=l+1|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}}),\ldots,\Pr(Y=m|{\bm{X}}={\bm{x}})
.
Especially when
\varphi
is strictly convex,
optimized 1DT values are concentrated like

\bar{a}({\bm{x}})=c
for any
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
.
Then, the resulting classifier cannot classify data in
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
well.

The above demonstration is on an extreme situation.
However, even in more mild situation,
overlap of the optimized bias parameter vector makes it difficult
to reflect variation of the data distribution in the learning of the 1DT.
This trouble is inherent to the combination of
the IT loss and ordered class
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{\rm ord}
of bias parameter vector
and may lead to the sub-optimal approximation error.

3.2.4
Conjecture

According to the discussion (Theorems
5

and
6
) in Section
3.2.1
,
for the case using IT losses we present a conjecture similar to that for AT losses:
for IT losses based on a non-increasing function
\varphi
that has no special property,
the classifier of threshold methods would tend to perform well for
homoscedastic unimodal data and mode-wise heteroscedastic unimodal data,
and may perform poorly for overall heteroscedastic unimodal data and almost non-unimodal data.
Also, Theorem
7
gives a closed-form expression
of the surrogate risk minimizer based on
\phi=\phi_{\rm squa\text{-}it}
.
According to that expression, we conjecture that the learning procedure
with an IT loss based on a V-shaped function
\varphi
like
\varphi_{\rm squa}

(with no special property; see Section
3.3
)
performs well when the data follow a unimodal CPD with a small scale.
Furthermore, the order constraint on the bias parameter vector
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{\rm ord}

for IT losses may cause the overlap of learned bias parameter vector,
which has a risk to negatively influence the classification performance,
as described in Section
3.2.3
.

3.3
Piecewise-Linear (PL) Loss

We here study the learning procedure based on
a
piecewise-linear
(
PL
) function
\varphi
.

We define the piecewise-linearity as follows:

Definition 1
.

If a function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies that
\varphi(u)=a_{i}+b_{i}u
for
u
in
i

of the intervals
(-\infty,c_{1}],(c_{1},c_{2}],\ldots,(c_{I-1},c_{I}],(c_{I},+\infty)

i\in[I+1]

with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{I+1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{I+1},c_{1},\ldots,c_{I}\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

a_{i}\neq a_{i+1}
or
b_{i}\neq b_{i+1}
for all
i\in[I]
and
c_{1}<\cdots<c_{I}
,
we say that
\varphi
is PL with
I

knots

c_{1},\ldots,c_{I}
.

For example, the hinge loss function
\varphi_{\rm hing}

and the absolute loss function
\varphi_{\rm abso}
are PL with 1 knot,
and the ramp loss function
\varphi_{\rm ramp}
is PL with 2 knots.

When
\varphi
is PL, the corresponding AT and IT loss functions and
the conditional surrogate risk
\bbE[\phi(a({\bm{x}}),{\bm{b}},Y)]
are also PL:

Theorem 8
.

Assume that
\varphi
is PL with
I
knots,
and
{\bm{b}}=(b_{k})_{k\in[K-1]}
consists of different
J
values,
with
I\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
J\in[K-1]
.
Then, the AT loss
\phi(\cdot,{\bm{b}},y)
defined by (
3

\varphi

is PL with at most
IJ
knots,
and the IT loss
\phi(\cdot,{\bm{b}},y)
defined by (
4
) with
\varphi

is PL with
I
knots if

or at most
2I
knots otherwise.
Moreover, for both of the AT and IT losses,
the conditional surrogate risk
\bbE[\phi(\cdot,{\bm{b}},Y)]

is PL with at most
2IJ
knots.

In general, the PL conditional surrogate risk
\bbE[\phi(a({\bm{x}}),{\bm{b}},Y)]

is minimized with respect to

at one of its knots
(except for, when the CPD

When the optimized 1DT

the resulting classifier cannot classify data on such

4.1
Simulation Study

With the aim of understanding how the design of the learning procedure
for a threshold method affects the approximation error of that method,
we presented several theorems and conjectures in Section
3

In order to verify these results experimentally, we performed the following simulation study:
As a simulation of the surrogate risk minimization

\min_{a\in{\mathcal{A}},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathcal{B}}}\bbE[\phi(a({\bm{X}}),{\bm{b}},Y)]

we solved the numerical optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathcal{B}}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{K}p_{i,y}\phi(a_{i},{\bm{b}},y)

with

and
K=10
.
This problem simulates the learning procedure of a threshold
method with the surrogate loss function

As the surrogate loss function

We considered three popular OR tasks (Tasks-Z, -A, and -S)

\ell=\ell_{\rm zo},\ell_{\rm ab},\ell_{\rm sq}

We denote the solution of (

{\mathcal{B}}

For the task loss
\ell
and learned 1DT values
\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{N}

we calculated the optimal threshold parameter vector

\bar{{\bm{t}}}_{\ell}\in\argmin_{{\bm{t}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{K}p_{i,y}\ell(h_{\rm thr}(\bar{a}_{i};{\bm{t}}),y)

following a way that modifies a dynamic-programming-based algorithm

(

)
that is for a sample-based situation.
To evaluate the method’s label prediction

\bar{f}_{\ell,i}=h_{\rm thr}(\bar{a}_{i};\bar{{\bm{t}}}_{\ell})

we calculated the approximation errors,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{K}p_{i,y}\ell(\bar{f}_{\ell,i},y)

(MAE), and mean squared error (MSE)) respectively for Tasks-Z, -A, and -S.

{\bm{x}}^{[i]}
is

\tilde{f}_{\ell,i}\in\argmin_{k\in[K]}\sum_{y=1}^{K}p_{i,y}\ell(k,y)
,
and the optimal error (Bayes error) is

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{K}p_{i,y}\ell(\tilde{f}_{\ell,i},y)
.
Note that, in the following, the evaluation for Task-S
is reported as ‘root of MSE’ (RMSE), not as MSE itself.

4.2
Synthesis Data Experiment

Our ultimate interest is in the prediction performance of threshold methods.
We have proceeded with the study under the working hypothesis
that the approximation error dominates the prediction error

To test this working hypothesis and
to show that our discussion is suggestive in studying the ultimate interest,
we performed a performance comparison experiment with synthesis data,

4.3
Real-World Data Experiment

Finally, we performed an OR experiment with real-world data,
mainly to confirm troubles owing to
the use of an IT loss and ordered class of the bias parameter vector
and the use of a PL loss for practical OR tasks.
We here addressed an OR problem of estimating age from facial image
by a discrete-value prediction, with reference to the experiments in
the previous OR studies
(
Cao et al. 2020
;
Yamasaki 2023
)
.
It would be supposed that the distribution of age of people
conditioned on their facial image tends to be unimodal.

Datasets

We used MORPH-2, CACD, and AFAD datasets

(
Ricanek and Tesafaye 2006
;
Chen et al. 2014
;
Niu et al. 2016
)
.
We purchased MORPH-2 (MORPH Album2) dataset at

https://ebill.uncw.edu/C20231_ustores/web/
and preprocessed
it so that the face spanned the whole image with the nose tip, which was
located by facial landmark detection
(
Sagonas et al. 2016
)
, at the center.
We used 55,013 instances with ages from 16 to 70 in this dataset.
We downloaded CACD dataset from
https://bcsiriuschen.github.io/CARC/
,
and preprocessed this dataset similarly to MORPH-2.
Excluding images, in which no face or more than two faces were
detected in the preprocessing, we used 159,402 facial images in the age range of 14–62 years.
For AFAD dataset (
https://github.com/afad-dataset/tarball
),
because faces in its images were already centered, we took no preprocessing.
We used its 164,418 instances with ages 15–40.
Because data of MORPH-2 were collected in a laboratory setting
and data of CACD and AFAD were collected by web scraping, CACD and AFAD are inferred to have a larger scale
(this can also be inferred from that errors for CACD and AFAD were larger
as shown in Table
12
in Appendix
A.3
).
For each dataset, we resized all images to
128\times 128
pixels of 3 RGB channels and
randomly split the dataset into 72 % training, 8 % validation, and 20 % test sets.
The training phase used images randomly cropped to
120\times 120
pixels of 3 channels
as input to improve the stability of the model against the difference of facial positions,
and validation and test phases used images center-cropped to the same size.

Methods

We tried 21 threshold methods and MLR as in
the experiment in Section
4.2
.
We implemented all the methods with ResNet34
(
He et al. 2016
)
.
We trained the network using Adam optimization
with the mini-batch size 256 and the descending learning rate

0.1^{2.5+t/T}
at
t
-th epoch during
T=200
epochs.

Evaluation

We took 5 trials, and considered the three OR tasks with the task loss

\ell=\ell_{\rm zo},\ell_{\rm ab},\ell_{\rm sq}
and used the test MZE, MAE, and RMSE
for the evaluation similarly to the experiment in Section
4.2
.

Results

Table
12
in Appendix
A.3

shows the mean and SD of MZE, MAE, and RMSE over the 5 trials,
and Figure
7
in Appendix
A.3

shows plots of
(y_{i},\hat{a}({\bm{x}}_{i}))

These results lead to the following findings:

(
p1

Non-PL losses, especially, non-PL AT losses,
gave the prediction performance comparable to MLR.
Plots of
(y_{i},\hat{a}({\bm{x}}_{i}))

(Figure

(

Plots of
(y_{i},\hat{a}({\bm{x}}_{i}))

5
Conclusion and Future Works

We studied the influence of the underlying data distribution
and of the learning procedure of the 1DT
on the approximation error of threshold methods,
under the expectation that it would lead to understand
the prediction performance of those methods
learned with a sufficiently large-size sample.
First, Theorems
2
and
5

clarify data distributions for which the threshold methods based on
the learning procedure with the Logistic and Exponential-AT and -IT losses
can yield the minimum approximation error.
On the ground of these results and Theorems
3
and
6
,
we conjecture that threshold methods using
the Logistic and Exponential-AT and -IT losses or similarly-shaped losses
will perform well for homoscedastic unimodal data and mode-wise heteroscedastic unimodal data
and may perform poorly for overall heteroscedastic unimodal data and almost non-unimodal data.
Second, according to Theorems
4
and
7
,
which give a closed-form expression of the surrogate risk minimizer
with the Squared-AT and -IT losses respectively, we also conjecture that
threshold methods using the Squared-AT and -IT losses or similarly-shaped losses
will perform well for unimodal data with a small scale.
Third, the learning procedure based on an IT loss and ordered class of the bias parameter vector
can output overlapped learned bias parameter vector:
In Section
3.2.3
, we demonstrated that such overlap
may deteriorate the ability of the threshold method to reflect
variation in the underlying data distribution.
Fourth, we showed in Section
3.3
that,
under the learning procedure based on a PL loss,
learned 1DT values are concentrated at a few points.
It is considered that these troubles can act as negative factors
that degrade the prediction performance.
We confirmed these results and conjectures as
(
l1
)–(
l3
), (
n1
)–(
n3
), and (
p1
)–(
p3
) experimentally.
Consequently, these results advance
the previous theoretical work
(
Pedregosa et al. 2017
, Section 4)
,
which suggests the absence of Fisher consistency for many threshold methods,
and the experimental comparisons
(
Rennie and Srebro 2005
;
Gutierrez et al. 2015
)

of threshold methods using various surrogate loss functions.

The results of this study may be useful in developing
novel learning procedures for threshold methods.
On the other hand, these results also indicate that
a classifier of several threshold methods based on
the strict surrogate risk minimization may not be good.
Devised design of the optimization procedure,
including the setting of initial parameters and appropriate stopping,
may improve the practical classification performance of the classifier.
Also, it would be still important to analyze the estimation error of
the classifier based on the two-step optimization (
5
).
These topics are future works.
