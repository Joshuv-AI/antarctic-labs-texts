Switching Checkerboards
David Ellison, Bertrand Jouve, Lewi Stone

Abstract
In order to study 𝐌 ⁡ ( R , C ) \mathbf{M}(R,C) , the set of binary matrices with fixed row and column sums R R and C C , we consider sub-matrices of the form ( 1 0 0 1 ) \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix} and ( 0 1 1 0 ) \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix} , called positive and negative checkerboard respectively. We define an oriented graph of matrices G ⁡ ( R , C ) G(R,C) with vertex set 𝐌 ⁡ ( R , C ) \mathbf{M}(R,C) and an arc from 𝐀 \mathbf{A} to 𝐀 ′ \mathbf{A}^{\prime} indicates you can reach 𝐀 ′ \mathbf{A}^{\prime} by switching a negative checkerboard in 𝐀 \mathbf{A} to positive. We show that G ⁡ ( R , C ) G(R,C) is a directed acyclic graph and identify classes of matrices which constitute unique sinks and sources of G ⁡ ( R , C ) G(R,C) . Given 𝐀 , 𝐀 ′ ∈ 𝐌 ⁡ ( R , C ) \mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{M}(R,C) , we give necessary conditions and sufficient conditions on 𝐌 = 𝐀 ′ − 𝐀 \mathbf{M}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{A} for the existence of a directed path from 𝐀 \mathbf{A} to 𝐀 ′ \mathbf{A}^{\prime} . We then consider the special case of 𝐌 ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) \mathbf{M}(\mathcal{D}) , the set of adjacency matrices of graphs with fixed degree distribution 𝒟 \mathcal{D} . We define G ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) G(\mathcal{D}) accordingly by switching negative checkerboards in symmetric pairs. We show that Z 2 Z_{2} , an approximation of the spectral radius λ 1 \lambda_{1} based on the second Zagreb index, is non-decreasing along arcs of G ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) G(\mathcal{D}) . Also, λ 1 \lambda_{1} reaches its maximum in 𝐌 ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) \mathbf{M}(\mathcal{D}) at a sink of G ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) G(\mathcal{D}) . We provide simulation results showing that applying successive positive switches to an Erdős-Rényi graph can significantly increase λ 1 \lambda_{1} .



Keywords
.

Graph theory, binary matrices, spectral radius, graph modifications

Introduction

In the study of diffusion processes along a network, the largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix, called the spectral radius of the network and denoted by
\lambda_{1}
, plays a critical role. For instance, in epidemic models, having a reproduction number
R_{0}
greater or smaller than
1/\lambda_{1}
determines whether the epidemic will die out or keep spreading. Also, in the problem of synchronisation of coupled oscillators on a network, a key threshold criterion for the stability of the synchronised solution is based on
\lambda_{1}
. The largest eigenvalue similarly affects other percolation processes such as wildfires or rumors spreading in social networks and is also of interest in the study of random matrices.

The parameter which has the greatest impact on the value of
\lambda_{1}
is the degree distribution of the network. Indeed, some lower bounds and approximations of
\lambda_{1}
are entirely determined by the degree distribution
[
14
]
. Such approximations can be very precise for the vast majority of cases, but tend to become wildly inaccurate in extreme cases of networks with very particular topological properties. In this article, we consider the degree distribution fixed in order to analyse how the topology of the network affects
\lambda_{1}
. The study of matrices with fixed degree distribution has applications to epidemic models
[
6
]
, synchronisation problems
[
11
]
, ecology
[
5
]
, LT codes
[
8
]
and many other areas. Once the degree distribution is fixed, the degree assortativity is the most important parameter of real networks
[
16
,
12
]
.

When working with a fixed degree distribution, we rely on a basic operation which consists in switching the
1
s and
0
s in a
2\times 2
sub-matrix of the adjacency matrix of the form
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

or
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, called a checkerboard. Any such switching amounts to rewiring two edges in a way that leaves the degree distribution invariant. This allows us to visualise the set of binary matrices with a given degree distribution as the vertex set of a graph where two matrices are adjacent if they differ by one switch. It was shown in
[
15
]
that this graph of matrices is connected.

In this paper, we call the two above sub-matrices positive and negative checkerboards. This polarisation of checkerboards defines an orientation of the edges of the graph of matrices. In
Section

1
, we introduce the basic definitions surrounding checkerboards. Then, in
Section

2
, we show that the oriented graph of matrices is a directed acyclic graph and discuss its sources and sinks as well as when
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
can be reached from
\mathbf{A}
by following an oriented path. Finally, in
Section

3
, we describe how the second Zagreb index, an approximation of
\lambda_{1}
, evolves throughout the graph of matrices. We then show that
\lambda_{1}
reaches its global maximum at a sink of the graph of matrices and describe the evolution of
\lambda_{1}
through simulations. The proofs of two theorems from
Section

2
have been placed in the appendix for the sake of improving readability. In order to maximise the generality of our results, we have considered non-symmetric binary matrices with fixed row and column sums in all sections except
Section

3
, where we consider only adjacency matrices. Note that a non-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
can be interpreted as a characteristic sub-matrix of
\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{A}\\
{}^{t}\mathbf{A}&0\end{pmatrix}
, where
\mathbf{M}
is the adjacency matrix of a bipartite graph.

1
Positive and Negative Switches

Given a
(0,1)
-matrix
\mathbf{A}
, a
checkerboard
of
\mathbf{A}
is a
2\times 2
sub-matrix of
\mathbf{A}
of the form either
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

or
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. A checkerboard found on rows
i
and
j
and columns
k
and
l
, with
i<j
and
k<l
, is said to have
coordinates

(i,j,k,l)
. A
unitary checkerboard
is a checkerboard of area
1
and has coordinates
(i,i+1,k,k+1)
.
Switching
a checkerboard refers to replacing one form with the other. Note that row and column sums are invariant under this operation. The following result was shown by Ryser in 1957:

Theorem 1

(
[
15
]
)
.

Given two matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
in the class of
(0,1)
-matrices having specified row and column sums, one can pass from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
by a finite sequence
of switches.

This property makes switching checkerboards an essential tool for studying classes of matrices with fixed row and column sums as well as classes of graphs with fixed degree distributions. Applying successive random switches to a binary matrix creates a Markov chain which visits all possible matrices with the same row and column sums. This Markov chain can then be used to sample randomly matrices with a fixed degree distribution
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we attribute a sign to checkerboards and switches: a checkerboard of the form
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
is said to be
positive
while a checkerboard of the form
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
is said to be
negative
. A
positive switch
corresponds to replacing a negative checkerboard with a positive one while a
negative switch
is the reverse. We define a
switching matrix

\mathbf{C}_{i,j,k,l}
, with
i<j
and
k<l
, as having coefficients
c_{ik}=c_{jl}=1
,
c_{il}=c_{kj}=-1
and 0 elsewhere. Thus, a positive (resp. negative) switch of coordinates
(i,j,k,l)
corresponds to adding (resp. subtracting)
\mathbf{C}_{i,j,k,l}
. Note that
\mathbf{C}_{i,j,k,l}=\sum_{i\leq p<j,k\leq q<l}\mathbf{C}_{p,p+1,q,q+1}
; i.e. any switching matrix is a sum of
unitary switching matrices
. Note also that the unitary switching matrices are linearly independent and form a basis of the space of zero-sum (for rows and columns) matrices.

2
Oriented Graph of Matrices

2.1
Sources and Sinks

Given vectors
R\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
and
C\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
, let
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
denote the set of binary matrices with row and column sums
R
and
C
. We assume
\mathcal{M}(R,C)\neq\emptyset
. Let
G(R,C)
denote the oriented graph with vertex set
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
and there is an arc from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
if
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
with a positive switch.

As mentioned in the introduction, the undirected graph underlying
G(R,C)
, called
interchange graph
in
[
4
]
, is well-studied in the literature. In
[
2
]
, multiple algorithms which generate random networks with a fixed degree distribution are based on this graph. Our purpose here, however, is to focus on properties which derive from the orientation.

Proposition 1
.

The graph
G(R,C)
is connected and acyclic.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(R,C)
, let
I(\mathbf{A})=\sum_{i,j}ija_{ij}
. Let
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
with a positive switch of coordinates
(i,j,k,l)
where
i<j
and
k<l
; i.e there is an arc from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
in
G(R,C)
. Then,

I(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})-I(\mathbf{A})=I(\mathbf{C}_{i,j,k,l})=(i-j)(k-l)>0.

Since
I
increases along arcs, and therefore along directed paths,
G(R,C)
is acyclic.

The connectedness property is Ryser’s theorem: a sequence of (positive or negative) switches leading from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
corresponds to an undirected path in
G(R,C)
.
∎

Recall that directed acyclic graphs have
sources
and
sinks
which are vertices with zero in-degree and out-degree, respectively. In
G(R,C)
, sources and sinks are, respectively, matrices with no positive checkerboards and no negative checkerboards.

A matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(R,C)
is said to be
nested
(resp. anti-nested) if the sequence
01
(resp.
10
) does not appear in any row or column; i.e if the 1s occur before the
0
s (resp.
0
s before
1
s) in every row and column. Nested graphs have important applications, in particular in ecology
[
5
]
. For our purpose, the nested case is both trivial and theoretically important, as indicated by the following proposition and its corollary.

Proposition 2
.

If
R
and
C
are non-increasing, then
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(R,C)
is nested if and only if it has no checkerboards.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{A}
is nested, the sequence
01
does not appear in any row or column of
\mathbf{A}
. Hence, there is no checkerboard in
\mathbf{A}
.

Conversely, if
\mathbf{A}
is not nested, there is a row or column containing the sequence
01
. Say it is a row; since the column with the
0
has degree at least equal to that with the
1
, there is another row containing
10
in the same two columns. Hence, there is a checkerboard.
∎

Corollary 1
.

The set
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
is a singleton if and only if it includes a matrix which becomes nested after reordering its rows and columns by non-increasing degree.

Proof.

This result derives from
2
by using
Theorem

1
and the fact that the number of checkerboards in a matrix is invariant under row and column permutation.
∎

We define a
zebra
as a matrix in
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
which is the sum of two matrices, one nested and one anti-nested. The name zebra refers to the three stripes formed by the
1
s and
0
s in the matrix, as shown in
Example

1
. We say that a zebra is
split vertically
(resp.
horizontally
) if no column (resp. row) has
1
s from both the nested and anti-nested parts (see
Example

1
). In other words, a split zebra can be split along a vertical (or horizontal) axis so that the left (resp. top) half is nested while the right (resp. bottom) half is anti-nested. Note that the two "halves" need not be of equal size. Also, we define an
anti-zebra
as the complement of the vertical reflection of a zebra; i.e.
b_{ij}=1-a_{n-i,j}
(see
Example

1
).

Like nested graphs, zebras and anti-zebras can be characterised by the absence of certain sub-matrices:

Claim 1
.

If we exclude full (resp. empty) rows and columns, zebras (resp. anti-zebras) are exactly the matrices without
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
*&0\end{pmatrix}
or

\begin{pmatrix}0&*\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
(resp.
\begin{pmatrix}*&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
or

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

) sub-matrices, where

denotes either

or

.

Proof.

The absence of those sub-matrices derives from the geometry of the zebra. The converse, which is not needed to prove the theorem, we leave as an exercise.
∎

Note that the forbidden sub-matrices include negative checkerboards; thus zebras and anti-zebras form sinks of
G(R,C)
.

Claim 2
.

Split zebras, split anti-zebras and their complements include at most one of the following vectors as sub-matrix:
\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\end{pmatrix}
,
\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\end{pmatrix}
,

\begin{pmatrix}1\\

and
\begin{pmatrix}0\\

0\end{pmatrix}
. In particular, horizontally split zebras have no
\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\end{pmatrix}
,
\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
sub-matrix.

Proof.

This derives from the geometry of the split zebra and anti-zebra.
∎

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
contains a split zebra or a split anti-zebra, then that split zebra or anti-zebra is the only element of
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
without negative checkerboards; i.e it is the unique sink in
G(R,C)
.

(Proof in the appendix)

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{M}(R,C)
be a split zebra or a split anti-zebra. For all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{M}(R,C)
,
\mathbf{B}
can be reached from
\mathbf{A}
via a sequence of positive switches.

Corollary 3
.

If
\mathcal{M}(R,C)
contains the complement of a split zebra or a split anti-zebra, then it is the unique source in
G(R,C)
.

Proof.

The complement of a unique sink is a unique source.
∎

2.2
Adapting Ryser’s Theorem to the Oriented Graph of Matrices

It follows from
Theorem

1
that the undirected version of
G(R,C)
is connected. Having introduced an orientation naturally raises the question of when can a matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
be reached from matrix
\mathbf{A}
via a sequence of positive switches. The remainder of this section is devoted to answering this question. We will denote by
\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
that there is a directed path connecting
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
in
G(R,C)
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
R\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
,
C\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
, let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(R,C)
and let
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{A}
. If
\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
, then
\mathbf{M}
is a sum of unitary switching matrices; i.e.

(i)

\exists!\mathbf{T}\in\mathcal{M}_{p-1,q-1}(\mathbb{N})
:
\mathbf{M}=\sum_{i,k}t_{ik}\mathbf{C}_{i,i+1,k,k+1}
.

Proof.

Each positive switch corresponds to adding a switching matrix and each switching matrix is a sum of unitary switching matrices. The unicity of
\mathbf{T}
follows from the linear independence of the unitary switching matrices.
∎

Remark 1
.

•

Note that
\mathbf{T}
is uniquely determined by

\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
(see

2

•

Given

(i)

\mathbf{T}

We denote by

Spectral Radius and Second Zagreb index

3.1
Extrema

In this section, we consider the class of adjacency matrices of simple graphs with fixed degree distribution
\mathcal{D}
. We investigate how the topology of simple graphs affects their spectral radius. To this purpose, we analyse the effect of successive positive checkerboard switches on the spectral radius
\lambda_{1}
as well as on the second Zagreb index
M_{2}
, which was used in
[
1
]
to create an approximation of
\lambda_{1}
:
Z_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m}}
.
Amongst all the existing approximations for
\lambda_{1}
, we chose to focus on
Z_{2}
because we are able to map very precisely how it varies throughout the set of adjacency matrices of fixed degree distribution. In contrast, most other commonly used approximations are determined by the degree distribution, meaning that they are invariant under checkerboard switching.
[
13
,
14
]
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m
and adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
. Let
d_{i}
denote the degree of vertex
i\in V
. The
spectral radius
of
G
, denoted by
\lambda_{1}(G)
or
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
, is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
. The
first
and
second Zagreb indices
are defined as
M_{1}=\sum_{i\in V}d_{i}^{2}
and
M_{2}=\sum_{ij\in E}d_{i}d_{j}
and we have
Z_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{M_{1}}{n}}
and
Z_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{M_{2}}{m}}
. Note that
Z_{1}
is the quadratic average over
V
of the degrees, while
Z_{2}
is the quadratic average over
E
of
\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}
. It is well-known that
\lambda_{1}\geq Z_{1}
, with equality if
G
is a regular graph
[
7
]
. Thus, it is the heterogeneity of
\mathcal{D}
that allows
\lambda_{1}
and
Z_{2}
to vary among the class of graphs with degree distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Also, for a fixed degree distribution,
M_{2}
is proportional to the
degree assortativity coefficient

r
, which is the standard Pearson coefficient for correlation between the degrees
[
10
]
:

r=\frac{M_{2}-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}d_{i}^{2})^{2}/m}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}d_{i}^{3}-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}d_{i}^{2})^{2}/m}.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
be the degree distribution of a simple graph. We now consider the class
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
of adjacency matrices of simple graphs of order
n
with fixed degree distribution
\mathcal{D}
; i.e. symmetric binary matrices with zeroes on the diagonal and row and column sums
\mathcal{D}
. Due to the absence of loops, we only consider, in this section, checkerboards with no coefficient on the diagonal. Also, given the symmetry of the matrices, checkerboards always come in symmetric pairs which are always switched together. In terms of graphs, a checkerboard corresponds to a 4-cycle of alternating edges and non-edges. Switching a checkerboard means switching the edges and non-edges. We define the
symmetric switching matrix

\mathcal{C}_{ijkl}=\mathbf{C}_{ijkl}\;+\;^{t}\mathbf{C}_{ijkl}
. Note that we now require the coordinates
(i,j,k,l)
to be all different. The distinction between positive and negative checkerboards for graphs is dependent on an ordering of the vertices. We will always sort the vertices by non-increasing degree; i.e
\mathcal{D}
is non-increasing. A positive (resp. negative) switch of coordinates
(i,j,k,l)
now corresponds to adding (resp. subtracting)
\mathcal{C}_{ijkl}
to the adjacency matrix. We define
G(\mathcal{D})
as the directed graph with vertex set
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
and an arc joins
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
when
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by a positive switch.

Proposition 4
.

The graph
G(\mathcal{D})
is connected and acyclic.

Proof.

The connectedness is shown in
[
3
]
. The acyclicity follows from the acyclicity in the asymmetric case.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
be such that
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by a positive switch of coordinates
(i,j,k,l)
. We have
M_{2}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})-M_{2}(\mathbf{A})=(d_{i}-d_{j})(d_{k}-d_{l})
.

Proof.

\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by adding the edges
ik
and
jl
and removing
il
and
kj
. So
M_{2}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})=M_{2}(\mathbf{A})+d_{i}d_{k}+d_{j}d_{l}-d_{i}d_{l}-d_{j}d_{k}
.
∎

Proposition 5
.

M_{2}
and
Z_{2}
are non-decreasing along arcs of
G(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

This follows from
Lemma

3
and
\mathcal{D}
being non-decreasing.
∎

Note that
M_{2}
and
Z_{2}
are constant only along arcs of
G(\mathcal{D})
which correspond to a checkerboard of coordinates
(i,j,k,l)
where
d_{i}=d_{j}
or
d_{k}=d_{l}
.

Corollary 4
.

The sources and sinks of
G(\mathcal{D})
are, respectively, the local minima and maxima of
M_{2}
and
Z_{2}
.

The above results give a clear picture of the variations of
M_{2}
and
Z_{2}
along
G(\mathcal{D})
. Unfortunately, the variations of
\lambda_{1}
are not quite so neatly organised. The following lemma gives a lower bound to the effect of a single positive switch on the spectral radius:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
such that
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by a sequence of positive switches of coordinates
(i_{p},j_{p},k_{p},l_{p})
. Let

denote the normalised principal eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}
. We have

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq 2\sum_{p}(x_{i_{p}}-x_{j_{p}})(x_{k_{p}}-x_{l_{p}}).

Proof.

Let
X^{\prime}
denote the normalised principal eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
. Recall that

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})=\penalty\ ^{t}X\mathbf{A}X=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},||x||=1}\penalty\ ^{t}x\mathbf{A}x,

where
||\penalty\ ||
is the
L^{2}
-norm. We have:

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})=\penalty\ ^{t}X^{\prime}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}X^{\prime}-\penalty\ ^{t}X\mathbf{A}X\geq\penalty\ ^{t}X\mathbf{A}^{\prime}X-\penalty\ ^{t}X\mathbf{A}X

=\penalty\ ^{t}X\sum_{p}\mathcal{C}_{i_{p},j_{p},k_{p},l_{p}}X=2\sum_{p}(x_{i_{p}}-x_{j_{p}})(x_{k_{p}}-x_{l_{p}}).

∎

Unfortunately, the coefficients of the principal eigenvector are not always in the same order as the degrees. They are, however, strongly correlated
[
9
]
; so
\lambda_{1}
increases along most arcs of
G(\mathcal{D})
. In fact, they are perfectly correlated in the case where
\lambda_{1}
is maximum:

Proposition 6
.

If
\mathbf{A}
realises the maximum of
\lambda_{1}
over
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
, then the coefficients of the principal eigenvector
X
of
\mathbf{A}
are in the same order as the degrees; i.e.
d_{i}>d_{j}\Rightarrow x_{i}\geq x_{j}
.

Proof.

Assume we have
d_{i}>d_{j}
and
x_{i}<x_{j}
. Let
E
be a set of
d_{i}-d_{j}
vertices adjacent to
i
and not
j
, with
j\notin E
. Let
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by replacing the edges between
i
and
E
with edges between
j
and
E
. Note that all the non-zero coefficients of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{A}
are in rows and columns
i
and
j
. Let
X^{\prime}
be the normalised principal eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
. We have:

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\penalty\ ^{t}X\mathbf{A}^{\prime}X-\penalty\ ^{t}X\mathbf{A}X=2(x_{j}-x_{i})\sum_{k\in E}x_{k}\geq 0.

Consider the case where
2(x_{j}-x_{i})\sum_{k\in E}x_{k}=0
. According to the Perron-Froebenius theorem for non-negative matrices,
X
has non-negative coefficients. Thus, since
x_{j}>x_{i}
,
\forall k\in E,x_{k}=0
. Let
k\in E
, then
\sum_{l}a_{kl}x_{l}=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})x_{k}=0
. Since all terms in
\sum_{l}a_{kl}x_{l}
are non-negative and
k
is adjacent to
i
, we deduce that
x_{i}=0
. Similarly, any vertex
l
in the connected component of
i
has
x_{l}=0
. Since
x_{j}>0
,
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is the spectral radius of the connected component of
j
, to which
i
does not belong. Thus, the connected component of
j
in
\mathbf{A}
is a proper sub-graph of that in
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
. So
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})>\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
in this case and in all cases.

Let
\mathbf{A}^{\prime\prime}
be obtained from
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
by switching the
i
-th and
j
-th rows and the
i
-th and
j
-th columns.
\mathbf{A}^{\prime\prime}
has the same row and column sums as
\mathbf{A}
and we have
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime\prime})=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})>\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
. Thus,
\mathbf{A}
does not maximise
\lambda_{1}
.
∎

From this, we deduce the following:

Theorem 4
.

The maximum of
\lambda_{1}
is reached at a sink of
G(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}
maximise
\lambda_{1}
over
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
. For each set of same-degree vertices, we can reorder the rows and columns of
\mathbf{A}
so that within these sets, the coefficients of the principal eigenvector are non-increasing. This operation does not affect
\lambda_{1}
. It follows from
6
that all the coefficients of
X
are now non-increasing. Let
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
be obtained from
\mathbf{A}
by a sequence of positive switches of coordinates
(i_{p},j_{p},k_{p},l_{p})
. It follows from
Lemma

4
that

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq 2\sum_{p}(x_{i_{p}}-x_{j_{p}})(x_{k_{p}}-x_{l_{p}})\geq 0.

Since
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
is maximum, any matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
such that
\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
also maximises
\lambda_{1}
, including the sinks that can be reached from
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

4
Conclusion

Our analysis of the oriented graph of matrices has given us the tools needed to study how the topology of graphs impacts their spectral radius
\lambda_{1}
. We have shown that the global maximum of
\lambda_{1}
is reached for a matrix without negative checkerboards. Our simulations show that successively switching negative checkerboards to positive can yield a high increase in
\lambda_{1}
, especially for sparse graphs.
