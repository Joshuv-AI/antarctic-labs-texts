Learning the structure of wind: A data-driven nonlocal turbulence model for the atmospheric boundary layer
B. Keith, U. Khristenko, B. Wohlmuth

Abstract
We develop a novel data-driven approach to modeling the atmospheric boundary layer. This approach leads to a nonlocal, anisotropic synthetic turbulence model which we refer to as the deep rapid distortion (DRD) model. Our approach relies on an operator regression problem which characterizes the best fitting candidate in a general family of nonlocal covariance kernels parameterized in part by a neural network. This family of covariance kernels is expressed in Fourier space and is obtained from approximate solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations at very high Reynolds numbers. Each member of the family incorporates important physical properties such as mass conservation and a realistic energy cascade. The DRD model can be calibrated with noisy data from field experiments. After calibration, the model can be used to generate synthetic turbulent velocity fields. To this end, we provide a new numerical method based on domain decomposition which delivers scalable, memory-efficient turbulence generation with the DRD model as well as others. We demonstrate the robustness of our approach with both filtered and noisy data coming from the 1968 Air Force Cambridge Research Laboratory Kansas experiments. Using this data, we witness exceptional accuracy with the DRD model, especially when compared to the International Electrotechnical Commission standard.

II
Wind in the atmospheric boundary layer

The ABL is characterized by constant shear stress in the vertical direction
\mathbf{e}_{3}
and is generally recognized to be
neutrally stable
at high wind speeds.
Kaimal and Finnigan 1994

That is, the buoyancy forces due to temperature gradients may be assumed to be negligible in comparison to surface-driven friction forces.
These physical assumputions are important to this work.
Relying on them, we will derive a statistical model which characterizes the ABL as a mass-conserving random field
\mathbf{U}
with homogeneous turbulent fluctuations
\mathbf{u}
.

In this work, we denote the atmospheric turbulent velocity field as
\mathbf{U}(\bm{x})=(U_{1}(\bm{x}),U_{2}(\bm{x}),U_{3}(\bm{x}))
, where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(x,y,z)
denotes standard Cartesian coordinates with the
x
-axis indicating the direction of the mean wind field and
z
indicating the height above ground.
The turbulent fluctuations around the mean wind field are denoted
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{U}-\langle\mathbf{U}\rangle=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
, with
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
denoting the ensemble average.
In this section, we review several common modeling assumptions for both the mean velocity profile
U(z)
and the turbulent fluctuations
\mathbf{u}(\bm{x})
in the ABL.

II.1
Mean profile

Ground friction is dominated by pressure drag, which is a force generated by pressure differences near the surface and caused by wind flowing across surface obstacles.
Depending on the local terrain, the variety of obstacles affecting ground friction can change vastly.
For instance, consider that different friction forces will arise from flow across grass, forests, open water, or urban canopies.
In this study, we strongly simplify the many effects of ground friction and assume that the mean wind field may be represented by a
uniform shear
flow in the
x
-direction,
\langle\mathbf{U}(\bm{x})\rangle=\langle U_{1}(z)\rangle\,\mathbf{e}_{1}
, where

\langle U_{1}(z)\rangle=A+Bz,\qquad A,B\geq 0.

(1)

In our approach, the ABL turbulent fluctuations
\mathbf{u}(\bm{x})
are modeled as though they have been distorted by the shearing mean profile
Eq.

1
.
This approximation will only be appropriate to first order, locally in
z
, and will degrade as the length scale of the eddies grows.
Nevertheless, such simplified models are well-established in the literature; see, e.g., Ref.
Mann 1994
, p. 145
.

In most terrain categories,
JCSS 2001
a logarithmic profile such as

\langle U_{1}(z)\rangle=\frac{u_{\ast}}{\kappa}\ln\bigg(\frac{z}{z_{0}}+1\bigg),

(2)

u_{\ast},z_{0}>0,\penalty\ \kappa\approx 0.41
, is quite appropriate for larger length scales, yet is unnecessary for our present purposes.
In this expression,
z_{0}
is generally referred to as the roughness height,
u_{\ast}
is the friction velocity, and
\kappa
is the von Kármán constant.
In
Section

III
, we will show that
Eq.

1
leads to a family of models with free parameters which can be calibrated with experimental data to partially overcome its discrepancy from
Eq.

2
and other forthcoming simplifying assumptions.

II.2
Spectra

We have set out to base our model only on the second-moment statistical structure of the ABL.
Holding true to this aim, we will neglect higher moment modeling errors and assume that the fully developed turbulent velocity field
\mathbf{u}
is Gaussian.
In turn, we focus on the two-point correlation tensor

R_{ij}(\bm{r},\bm{x})=\langle u_{i}(\bm{x})u_{j}(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\rangle.

In spatially homogeneous flows, we have that
R_{ij}(\bm{r},\bm{x})=R_{ij}(\bm{r})
, and so it is convenient to transform the correlation tensor to Fourier space.
This transformation defines the velocity-spectrum tensor,

\Phi_{ij}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{i}\bm{k}\bcdot\bm{r}}R_{ij}(\bm{r})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{r},

where
\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
is the wavevector.
Due to the cross-correlation theorem, the velocity-spectrum tensor can also be written

\Phi_{ij}(\bm{k})=\langle\overline{\widehat{u}}_{i}(\bm{k})\widehat{u}_{j}(\bm{k})\rangle,

(3)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}=(\widehat{u}_{1},\widehat{u}_{2},\widehat{u}_{3})
denotes the Fourier transform of
\mathbf{u}
.

A standard form of the spectral tensor used in isotropic, stationary, homogeneous turbulence models is

\Phi_{ij}^{\mathrm{VK}}(\bm{k})=\frac{E(k)}{4\pi k^{2}}\,\bigg(\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}\bigg)\,,

(4)

where
k=|\bm{k}|
is the wavenumber (the magnitude of the wavevector) and
E(k)
is called the energy spectrum function.
A common empirical model for
E(k)
, dating back to von Kármán,
Von Karman 1948
is given by the expression

E(k)=c_{0}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}\bigg(\frac{kL}{(1+(kL)^{2})^{1/2}}\bigg)^{17/3}.

(5)

Here,
\varepsilon
is the viscous dissipation of the turbulent kinetic energy,
L
is a length scale parameter, and
c_{0}^{2}\approx 1.7
is an empirical constant.
The von Kármán model
\Phi^{\mathrm{VK}}(\bm{k})
encodes important nonlocal information about isotropic turbulence.
In fact, the energy spectrum
Eq.

5
permits the convenient description of such turbulence as the solution of a (nonlocal) fractional diffusion equation.
Keith, Khristenko, and Wohlmuth 2021a

It is typically not possible to directly measure a spectral tensor
\Phi_{ij}
in a shearing flow.
Instead, one often collects the one-point spectra
Simiu and Yeo 2019

F_{ij}(k_{1})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R_{ij}((r_{1},0,0)\operatorname{e}^{-\operatorname{i}k_{1}\cdot r_{1}}\!\,\mathrm{d}r_{1},

with
i,j=1,2,3
.

Obviously, the one-point spectra cannot give a complete description of the turbulent wind field.
For this reason, it is necessary to construct physical models which can fit these experimental observations.
One of the issues in constructing these models is that turbulence in the atmospheric boundary layer is not spatially homogeneous.
Nevertheless, the common surface layer scaling assumption
Kaimal et al. 1972
is that the length scales are proportional to
z
and the variances are proportional to
u_{\ast}^{2}
.
For instance, after analyzing measurements taken above flat homogeneous terrain in Kansas, Kaimal et al.

proposed taking

\frac{k_{1}F_{11}(k_{1}z)}{u_{\ast}^{2}}=J_{1}(f):=\frac{52.5f}{(1+33f)^{5/3}},

(6a)

\frac{k_{1}F_{22}(k_{1}z)}{u_{\ast}^{2}}=J_{2}(f):=\frac{8.5f}{(1+9.5f)^{5/3}},

(6b)

\frac{k_{1}F_{33}(k_{1}z)}{u_{\ast}^{2}}=J_{3}(f):=\frac{1.05f}{1+5.3f^{5/3}},

(6c)

where
f=(2\pi)^{-1}k_{1}z
, alongside
F_{12}=F_{23}=0
and

-\frac{k_{1}F_{13}(k_{1}z)}{u_{\ast}^{2}}=J_{4}(f):=\frac{7f}{(1+9.6f)^{12/5}}.

(6d)

IV

III
Model

In this section, we derive a family of velocity spectrum models for the ABL.
After introducing more preliminary notation, we derive a time-dependent parameterization
\Phi(\bm{k},\tau)
from a linearized form of the Navier–Stokes equations.
We then enrich this parameterization with a general eddy lifetime model
\tau=\tau(\bm{k})
which includes physical symmetries and energy constraints.

III.1
Gaussian hypothesis

As with the two-point correlation tensor, it is convenient to consider the Fourier transform of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}

In such cases, we express the field in terms of a generalized Fourier–Stieltjes integral,

(7)

where

Let us consider three-dimensional additive white Gaussian noise

and

, respectively, such that

\bm{\xi}(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{k})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{W}(\bm{k}),

(8)

where
\bm{W}(\bm{k})

Our Gaussian assumption is that

III.2
Rapid distortion

The rapid distortion equations
(
Townsend 1980
;
Hunt and Carruthers 1990
;
Pope 2001
, see, e.g.,)
are a linearization of the Navier–Stokes equations in free space which holds when the turbulence-to-mean-shear time scale ratio is arbitrarily large.
To write them, we must first define the average total derivative of the turbulent fluctuations, namely

\frac{\bar{D}u_{i}}{\bar{D}t}=\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial t}+\langle U_{j}\rangle\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\,.

With this definition in hand, the rapid distortion equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{D}u_{i}}{\bar{D}t}

\displaystyle=-u_{i}\frac{\partial\langle U_{j}\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{i}},

(9a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\Delta p

\displaystyle=-2\frac{\partial\langle U_{i}\rangle}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\,,

(9b)

where
\rho
and
p
stand for the mass density and the hydrostatic pressure, respectively.

Some exact solutions of
Eq.

9

Townsend 1980

We are unaware of any closed-form solution with the mean profile in
Eq.

2

However, with the uniform-shear mean profile

1

and a non-dimensional time parameter
\tau=Bt

For greater perspective, we begin with the general case where
\partial\langle U_{i}\rangle/\partial x_{j}

First, we define the rate of change of each frequency

{\!\,\mathrm{d}k_{i}}/{\!\,\mathrm{d}t}=-k_{j}{\partial\langle U_{j}\rangle}/{\partial x_{i}}.

(10)

We then have the following Fourier representation of the average total derivative of

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{D}u_{i}}{\bar{D}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\Bigg(\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\,\mathrm{d}k_{j}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{j}}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}Z_{i}(\bm{k},t)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\Bigg(\frac{\bar{D}\,\mathrm{d}Z_{i}(\bm{k},t)}{\bar{D}t}\Bigg).

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{2}k_{0}^{2}}{(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2})^{3/2}}\arctan x\biggr|_{k_{3}/\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}}^{k_{30}/\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}}.

III.3
Eddy lifetime

The spectral tensor
\Phi(\bm{k},\tau)
characterizes a time-dependent family of anisotropic covariance kernels which are stretched in the direction of the constant mean shear.
One flaw in this model is that physical eddies will break apart after a certain amount of distortion.

An important extension of the rapid distortion model involves replacing the distortion parameter
\tau
by a wavenumber-dependent
eddy lifetime
function
\tau(\bm{k})
.
The benefit of this substitution is two-fold: not only does it produce a stationary (time-
independent
) spectral tensor
\Phi(\bm{k},\tau(\bm{k}))
, but it provides a mechanism to recover missing physics which are neglected in the original modeling assumptions.

Various eddy lifetime models have been proposed in the literature;
Comte-Bellot and Corrsin 1971
;
Lesieur 1987
;
Mann 1994
each of which involves restricting the eddy lifetime
\tau
to a radial function of the wavevector,
\tau=\tau(k)
.
For instance, a widely used radial function is presented in Ref.
Mann 1994
and results in a spectral tensor model which was subsequently standardized by the IEC.
IEC 2014

In this approach, the destruction of an eddy with size
k^{-1}
is assumed to be mainly due to eddies of comparable or smaller size.
Thus, the square of the characteristic velocity of all influential eddies is given by
\int_{k}^{\infty}E(p)\,\mathrm{d}p
.
By matching units, the eddy lifetime
\tau
may be proportional to a length scale divided by a velocity , e.g.,

\tau\propto k^{-1}\left[\int_{k}^{\infty}E(p)\,\mathrm{d}p\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(15)

After substituting
Eq.

5
, this expression results in

\tau^{\rm IEC}(k)=\frac{TB^{-1}\,(kL)^{-\frac{2}{3}}}{\sqrt{{}_{2}F_{1}(1/3,17/6;4/3;-(kL)^{-2})}},

(16)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)
denotes the hypergeometric function, and the time scale
T
is a free parameter.
To facilitate an accurate comparison to this model in
Section

IV
, we point out that

\tau^{\rm IEC}(k)\propto\begin{cases}k^{-1}\quad&\text{for }k\to 0,\\
k^{-3/2}\quad&\text{for }k\to\infty.\\
\end{cases}

Substituting
Eq.

16
into the rapid distortion spectral tensor, results in the Mann uniform shear model,

\Phi^{\rm IEC}(\bm{k})=\Phi(\bm{k},\tau^{\rm IEC}(|\bm{k}|)).

(17)

Expanded formulas for
\Phi^{\rm IEC}(\bm{k})
can be found in Refs.
Mann 1994
;
IEC 2014
.

Given the anisotropic nature of shear flow, it is natural to expect that the lifetime of an eddy could depend on its initial spatial orientation.
However, it is also clear that this dependence should satisfy some basic physical conditions.
For instance, owing to translational symmetry of the flow in the transversal direction (
y
-axis), we expect
\tau(\bm{k})
to be an even function of
k_{2}
.
We note that this restriction guarantees that the transversal components of the one-point cospectra,
F_{12}
and
F_{23}
, vanish; cf.
Eq.

6
.

We now propose a method to discover complete wavevector eddy lifetime functions
\tau(\bm{k})
from measured data.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first effort to accomplish this task.
Our approach involves writing
\tau(\bm{k})
as a feedforward neural network.

III.4
Neural network model for the eddy lifetime

Our goal is to look for
\tau
in the form of a neural network with the wavevector
\bm{k}
as input.
Then, learning its parameters by fitting the one-point spectra data, we identify the function
\tau(\bm{k})
as well as the complete spectral tensor model.

Aside from physical symmetries, the eddy lifetime must satisfy certain asymptotic behavior in the limits
k\to 0
and
k\to\infty
.
To this end, we first rewrite the eddy lifetime function as follows:

\tau(\bm{k})=\frac{T\,|\bm{a}|^{\nu-\frac{2}{3}}}{(1+|\bm{a}|^{2})^{\nu/2}},\qquad\bm{a}=\bm{a}(\bm{k}),

(18)

where
T
is characteristic timescale,
\nu
is a tunable exponent,
and
\bm{a}
is a intermediary variable, which we refer to as the “augmented” wavevector.
We will define
\bm{a}(\bm{k})
in terms of a neural network
\bm{a}=\mathcal{O}(k)
in both limits
k\to 0
and
k\to\infty
.
Therefore, the form of the fractional exponents in (
18
) will allow us to control the asymptotic behavior of
\tau(\bm{k})
.
This is important since the asymptotic slopes are objects of discussion in the literature.
Thus, while the slope at zero is controlled by
\nu
, the well-known slope at infinity,
Landau and Lifshitz 1987
;
Mann 1994
;
Pope 2001

k^{-2/3}
, is recovered for any value of
\nu
.

We define

\bm{a}(\bm{k}):=\mathrm{abs}(\bm{k})+\mathrm{NN}(\mathrm{abs}(\bm{k})).

(19)

Here,
\mathrm{abs}(\cdot)
denotes the element-wise absolute value and
\mathrm{NN}(\cdot)
is a fully-connected multilayer perceptron, namely

\mathrm{NN}(\bm{y}):=W_{n}\,\mathrm{ReLU}\circ\cdots\circ\mathrm{ReLU}\circ W_{1}\bm{y},

(20)

where
\mathrm{ReLU}(\cdot)
stands for the rectified linear activation function and
W_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, are dense matrices (no bias terms).
The choice of a ReLU function is motivated by the requirement that
\mathrm{NN}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
, thus,
\bm{a}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
.
Other activation functions, such as the softplus activation function will not guarantee this property.

Note that taking the absolute value of the first argument in
Eq.

19
provides a reflection symmetry in
\tau(\bm{k})
with respect to each Cartesian axis.
This is obviously more than necessary to satisfy symmetry in the
k_{2}
-component.
Our experience has shown that that there is no benefit to the accuracy of the model, when fitting one-point spectra data, if we relax the symmetry the
k_{1}
- and
k_{3}
-components.

We use one input and one output layer, each of size
3
, accompanied by
n-2
hidden layers of size
m
.
In other words,
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times m}
,
W_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
j=2,\ldots,n-1
, and
W_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 3}
, whose sum total of entries constitute a new vector of learnable parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}
.
Note that if any
W_{j}=0
,
j=1,\ldots n
, then
\mathrm{NN}=0
.
This forces the magnitude of the augmented wavevector to agree with the true wavenumber,
|\bm{a}|=k
.
From this point-of-view the neural network acts in a way like a piecewise-linear perturbation of the original wavevector.

Once the eddy lifetime function
Eq.

18
has been substituted into the rapid distortion model
\Phi(\bm{k},\tau)
, we arrive at the DRD spectral tensor model:

\Phi^{\rm DRD}(\bm{k},\bm{\theta}):=\Phi(\bm{k},\tau(\bm{k})),

(21)

where the vector of all learnable parameters, i.e.,

\bm{\theta}=\{C,L,T,\nu,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}\},

(22)

consists of the spectrum amplitude
C:=c_{0}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2/3}/u_{\ast}^{2}
, the characteristic length and time sales,
L
and
T
, respectively, the exponent
\nu
and the weights
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}
of the neural network (
20
).

IV
Calibration

In this section, we discuss various aspects of the model regression problem which calibrates the eddy lifetime
\tau(\bm{k})
together with the other free parameters in the DRD model
Eq.

22
.
More specifically, we document the problem formulation, state important details from our implementation,
Keith, Khristenko, and Wohlmuth 2021b
and summarize our results.

IV.1
Optimization problem formulation

Let us consider the one-point spectra
Eq.

6
on the interval
\mathcal{D}=[0.1,100]
.
We wish to find the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
which best fit this data.
To set the stage, define

\widetilde{F}_{ij}(k_{1};\bm{\theta})=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi_{ij}^{\rm DRD}(\bm{k},\bm{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}k_{2}\,\mathrm{d}k_{3},

(23)

and

\widetilde{J}_{i}(f;\bm{\theta})=C\,k_{1}\widetilde{F}_{ii}(k_{1}z;\bm{\theta}),\quad i=1,2,3,

(24)

and
\widetilde{J}_{4}(f;\bm{\theta})=C\,k_{1}\widetilde{F}_{13}(k_{1}z;\bm{\theta})
, where
f=(2\pi)^{-1}k_{1}z
.
Note that the learnable magnitude
C
involves the constants
c_{0}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2/3}
and
u_{\ast}^{2}
.

To find the optimal parameter vector
\bm{\theta}_{\rm opt}
, we solve the optimization problem

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\big\{\operatorname{MSE}[\bm{\theta}]+\alpha\operatorname{Pen}[\bm{\theta}]+\beta\operatorname{Reg}[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}]\big\},

(25)

where
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and each function
\operatorname{MSE}[\bm{\theta}]
,
\operatorname{Pen}[\bm{\theta}]
, and
\operatorname{Reg}[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}]
is defined as follows.
The mean squared error is defined as

\operatorname{MSE}[\bm{\theta}]:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\big(\log|\textstyle{J_{i}}(f_{j})|-\log|\textstyle{\widetilde{J}_{i}(f_{j},\bm{\theta})}|\big)^{2},

(26)

where
L
is the number of the data points
f_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
.
The data
J_{i}(f_{j})
is evaluated using the Kaimal spectra (
6a
)–(
6d
).
The penalization term is defined as

\operatorname{Pen}[\bm{\theta}]:=\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{D}\right|}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left\|\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\log\left|\widetilde{J}_{i}(\cdot,\bm{\theta})\right|}{(\partial\,\log k_{1})^{2}}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2},

(27)

where

\left|\mathcal{D}\right|=\left\|1\right\|_{\mathcal{D}}

and the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{D}}
is defined

\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|g(f)\right|^{2}\operatorname{d}(\log f).

(28)

This term penalizes the curvature of the log-spectra in order to obtain convex curves and to minimize oscillations, thus protecting against overfitting.
Associating
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}
with a vector
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we write

\operatorname{Reg}[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{NN}}]:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{\mathrm{NN},i}^{2},

(29)

where
N
denotes the total number of weights of the neural network
Eq.

20
.
The term accelerates convergence and also helps to avoid overfitting.

IV.2
Implementation details

The model regression problem
Eq.

25
is implemented and solved using the PyTorch package
Paszke et al. 2019
.
In particular, we use a full-batch L-BFGS optimization algorithm with the strong Wolfe line search method and learning rate equal to
1
.
We use
n=2
hidden layers of size
m=10
(see
Figure

1
) and begin by using the penalty and regularization parameters
\alpha=1
and
\beta=10^{-5}
, respectively.

The integrals in (
23
) are truncated and approximated using the trapezoidal quadrature rule on a logarithmic grid from
10^{-3}
to
10^{3}
with
100\times 100
nodes spanning the frequencies
k_{2}
and
k_{3}
.
The second derivative in (
27
) is approximated with central finite differences.
And the norm
Eq.

28
is approximated using the trapezoidal rule.

V
Random field generation

In this section, we briefly discuss computational aspects of turbulence generation with the DRD and other spectral tensor models.
We take particular care to focus on aspects related to the use of such models for generating turbulent inlet conditions for LESs.

V.1
Turbulent inlet conditions

Synthetic turbulent inlet conditions are used to drive many important turbulence simulations.
Tabor and Baba-Ahmadi 2010
;
Wu 2017

For numerical wind tunnel simulations, it is important that the statistics of the input wind match the simulated environment.
This can be accomplished by relying on a synthetic turbulence model,
Sanderse, Van der Pijl, and Koren 2011
such as the DRD introduced above.

In this section, we choose to follow an approach established in the wind engineering industry; see, e.g., Refs.
Michalski et al. 2011
;
Michalski et al. 2015
;
Andre, Mier-Torrecilla, and Wüchner 2015
and references therein.
In this approach, a long channel of synthetic turbulence is treated as an inflowing velocity field with updated cross-sections projected onto the LES domain boundary at each time step.
For a 3D depiction of the process, including the accompanied LES, we refer to Ref.
Keith, Khristenko, and Wohlmuth 2021a
, Figure 7
.

An established technique to accomplish this process is to create a long unbroken section of synthetic wind offline and read in a moving cross-section of it at each time step of the LES simulation.
In this next subsection, an alternative on-the-fly domain decomposition technique is described.
This technique was used to generate the snapshots depicted in
Fig.

6
as well as the LES simulation in Ref.
Keith, Khristenko, and Wohlmuth 2021a
, Figure 7
.
Before we begin, we note that there is nothing specific to the DRD model about this technique.
It may be applied to any spectral turbulence model, including the model in IEC 61400-1.
Mann 1998
;
IEC 2014

V.2
On-the-fly generation

The representation of the turbulent fluctuations (
7
) can be formally written as a convolution of a covariance kernel with Gaussian noise
\bm{\xi}
in the physical domain:

\mathbf{u}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\mathcal{G}\widehat{\bm{\xi}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}\bm{\xi},

(30)

where
\mathcal{F}
stands for the Fourier transform and the operator
\mathcal{G}
corresponds to point-wise multiplication by
\bm{G}(\bm{k})
, which is any positive-definite “square root” of the spectral tensor, satisfying
\bm{G}(\bm{k})\,\bm{G}^{\ast}(\bm{k})=\Phi(\bm{k})
.

Experience shows that solving all-at-once for a unbroken section of synthetic wind, long enough to be fed into a complete CFD simulation, can be very costly.
First of all, the cost of solving
Eq.

30
scales at best log-linearly with the domain length.
Second of all, storing the solution data may take up a very large amount of computer resources.
In fact, for a standard 600s LES simulation, the entire synthetic wind field may require more than 10GB to store.

It turns out the much of this computational cost can be avoided simply by piecing together the random field box-by-box.
This technique can be combined with inlet condition generation; see, e.g., Ref.
Keith, Khristenko, and Wohlmuth 2021a
, Figure 7
.
In this application, the technique allows for on-the-fly synthetic wind generation, since only a small box of wind pertaining to the given time step needs to be generated in order to progress the simulation.

Figure 6
:

Turbulent fluctuations generated with the DRD model with learned parameters from fitting the Kaimal spectrum (cf.
Figure

2(b)
) and superimposed on the mean profile
Eq.

2
:
(A) wind generated box-by-box; (B) combined boxes: (C) snapshots.

Begin with any box domain and extend it on all sides by a “buffer region.”
Seeding a Cartesian grid inside the extended box with additive white Gaussian noise and then computing the convolution
Eq.

30
will results in a periodic random vector field.
Restricting the solution to the original domain will break this periodicity.
Indeed, it will result in a random field with a (non-zero) correlation between opposing ends of the original domain.
This correlation is controlled by the size of the buffer region and as well as the shape of the covariance kernel.
Khristenko et al. 2019

It is common practice to extend the buffer region until this correlation is negligible.
A simplified diagram which shows a long 2D box (this can be thought of as a transversal cross-section of a long 3D turbulence box) with a buffer only at two opposing end is depicted in
Fig.

7
.

Figure 7
:
Simplified diagram of a 2D computational domain with buffer regions (colored) at only two opposing ends.
The fluctuations in the buffer regions are highly correlated so they are typically discarded during post-processing.

In order to generate matching wind in a neighboring box, it is required for the interface of the new neighboring buffer region (cf. the “copy-paste” lines in
Fig.

8
) to align with the interface of the old neighboring buffer region and that common Gaussian noise be used in the overlapping domain.
After seeding the remainder of the new box with new additive white Gaussian noise and applying
Eq.

30
, one arrives at a new box of wind matching its neighbor, at their common interfaces, up to an accuracy again controlled by the size of the buffer region.
Once the solutions in the buffer regions are discarded, the original neighboring boxes of wind field can be grouped together to form a large unbroken wind field or processed into snapshots and fed into a CFD simulation; cf.
Fig.

6
(C).

Figure 8
:
Technique to generate a continuous wind field box-by-box.
Note that
only
the noise is being copied from the end of one block to the beginning of the next block.
The turbulence fluctuations
\mathbf{u}
are still recomputed block-by-block, using the partially shared noise.

To understand why this technique works, note that the common noise in the overlap forces the two wind fields to match at their buffer region interfaces, at least up to the accuracy allowed by the size of the buffers.
Moving away from the interface, the wind field changes at a fixed regularity, with the influence of the common overlap steadily diminishing.
Khristenko et al. 2019

This strategy can be applied to any homogeneous spectral tensor turbulence model, not only the DRD model or the Mann uniform shear model.

VI
Conclusion

We have presented the deep rapid distortion (DRD) model: a new nonlocal, data-driven spectral model for the atmospheric boundary layer.
The DRD model is derived from rapid distortion theory and leverages an eddy lifetime function parameterized in terms of a carefully-chosen neural network.
The model is calibrated using a regression problem involving the fitting of (possibly noisy) one-point spectrum data.
Using this data, we witness exceptional accuracy with the DRD model, especially when compared to the current IEC standard.
Finally, we present a domain decomposition method for using the DRD model and other spectral models to generate synthetic turbulence.
For the purpose of reproduction and wider adoption, this work is accompanied by an open-source Python implementation.
