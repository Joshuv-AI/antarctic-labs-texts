Gaussian curvature on random planar maps and Liouville quantum gravity
Andres A. Contreras Hip and Ewain Gwynne University of Chicago

Abstract
We investigate the notion of curvature in the context of Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surfaces. We define the Gaussian curvature for LQG, which we conjecture is the scaling limit of discrete curvature on random planar maps. Motivated by this, we study asymptotics for the discrete curvature of ϵ \epsilon -mated CRT maps. More precisely, we prove that the discrete curvature integrated against a C c 2 C_{c}^{2} test function is of order ϵ o ⁡ ( 1 ) , \epsilon^{o(1)}, which is consistent with our scaling limit conjecture. On the other hand, we prove the total discrete curvature on a fixed space-filling SLE segment scaled by ϵ 1 4 \epsilon^{\frac{1}{4}} converges in distribution to an explicit random variable.

Introduction

Liouville quantum gravity is a canonical 1-parameter family of models of a random surface first introduced by
[
Pol81
]
. LQG is conjectured to be the scaling limit of various random planar map models, which are planar graphs embedded in
\mathbb{C}
in such a way that no two edges cross, viewed modulo orientation-preserving homomorphisms. There are cases where the convergence to LQG has already been proven: for example, uniform planar maps (uniform triangulations, uniform quadrangulations etc.) have been proven to converge in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to
\sqrt{8/3}
-LQG (
[
Le 13
,
Mie13
,
MS20
,
MS21
,
HS23
]
). Other random planar map models are expected to converge to LQG, see e.g.
[
GHS23
]
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,2),
and let
Q
be defined by
Q=\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma}.
Suppose that
\Phi
is a GFF or a variant such as the quantum cone. Formally, the LQG metric tensor associated with
\Phi
is defined as
e^{\gamma\Phi}(dx^{2}+dy^{2}).
A distance metric
D_{h}
and measure
\mu_{h}
can be defined via approximation of
\Phi
by appropriate mollifications (see
[
Kah85
,
RV14
,
Ber17
,
DS11
,
DDDF20
,
DG23
]
.

There is a vast literature on LQG: see
[
Gwy20
,
She23
,
BP
,
GKR24
]
for surveys on the main techniques and results in the area. Many geometric properties about LQG surfaces have been studied, such as the confluence property of LQG geodesics established in
[
GM20
]
, the KPZ formula for Hausdorff dimensions of LQG subsets
[
GP22
,
DS11
,
Aru15
,
GHS19
,
GHM20
,
BGRV16
,
GM21a
,
BJRV13
,
BS09
,
DMS21
,
DRSV14
]
, and more general properties about the LQG metric’s behavior
[
DDG23
]
. However, given the non smooth nature of LQG surfaces, most of the study of their geometry is restricted to first order information which does not require derivatives of the underlying field. Moreover, LQG does not always behave as its smooth counterpart (e.g. LQG surfaces have Hausdorff dimension greater than
2,
and the LQG measure and metric scale differently). Given this, it is natural to ask which geometric properties are shared with smooth 2d Riemannian manifolds, and to what extent notions from Riemannian geometry can be extended for LQG.

A central concept in Riemannian geometry is curvature. In this regard, a first question is whether there is a way of defining Gaussian curvature for LQG surfaces so that it extends the usual definition, and at the same time is compatible with the known LQG theory, in the sense that it arises naturally as a limit of the corresponding discrete curvature for all reasonable random planar map approximations. In this paper, we define the Gaussian curvature for LQG surfaces (
1.1
), and we conjecture that it arises as the scaling limit of the discrete curvature defined in (
1.2
). To this end, the first question to study is whether the discrete curvature converges in a reasonable random planar maps model, and if so, under what scaling. The particular random planar map model we look at in this paper is the Poisson mated CRT map defined in Section
2
. This implies that we need to study the asymptotics of this discrete curvature as the mesh size of the planar map tends to
0,
which is the main goal of this paper and is the purpose of Theorem
1.5
. After this, we study the total curvature on a “canonical” set, namely space filling SLE segments. In Theorem
1.7
we show that the scaling limit of this total curvature converges in law to an explicit random variable. We note that surprisingly the scaling for this quantity is different from that of the discrete Gaussian curvature when summed against a
C_{c}^{2}
test function.

1.1
Curvature for LQG surfaces

Definition 1.1
.

The whole plane Gaussian free field (GFF)
\Phi
is the centered Gaussian random generalized function with covariance structure given by

\mathrm{Cov}(\Phi(z),\Phi(w))=G(v,w)=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\max\{|z|,1\},\max\{|w|,1\}}{|z-w|}}}\right).

Note that this correlation function corresponds to a GFF normalized so that the average over the unit circle is
0

[
Var17
, Section 2.1.1]
.

Definition 1.2
.

An LQG surface is an equivalence class defined as follows. We say pairs
(D_{1},\Phi_{1}),(D_{2},\Phi_{2})
consisting of a domain in
\mathbb{C}
and a generalized function on the domain are equivalent if there exists a conformal map
f:D_{1}\to D_{2}
with

\Phi_{2}=\Phi_{1}\circ f^{-1}+Q\log|(f^{-1})^{\prime}|,

where here we recall that
Q=2/\gamma+\gamma/2
.
Then any equivalence class is called
\gamma
-LQG surface.

If
\Phi
is a GFF, or more generally if
\Phi=\Phi^{0}+f
, where
\Phi^{0}
is a GFF and
f
is a possibly random continuous function, then for
\gamma\in(0,2)
we can define the
LQG area measure

\mu_{\Phi}
as a limit of regularized versions of
e^{\gamma\Phi}\,dz
, where
dz
denotes two-dimensional Lebesgue measure
[
Kah85
,
RV14
,
Ber17
,
DS11
]
. This measure is compatible with changes of coordinate, in the sense that if
(D_{1},\Phi_{1})
and
(D_{2},\Phi_{2})
are related as in Definition
1.2
, then the conformal map
f
pushes forward
\mu_{\Phi_{1}}
to
\mu_{\Phi_{2}}

[
DS11
, Proposition 2.1]
.

One can intuitively define the Gaussian curvature associated with LQG as follows. If we consider a general metric in isothermal coordinates
e^{\phi}(dx^{2}+dy^{2}),
then the Gaussian curvature is given by

\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}e^{-\phi}.

Given this, if
\Phi
is the underlying field, after a formal computation one obtains that the Gaussian curvature on an LQG surface
K_{\Phi}
is given by

\int_{\mathbb{C}}K_{\Phi}(z)f(z)d\mu_{\Phi}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Delta\Phi e^{-\gamma\Phi}f(z)e^{\gamma\Phi}dz=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Delta\Phi(z)f(z)dz=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Phi(z)\Delta f(z)dz

where
f
is any
C_{c}^{2}
test function, and so it is natural to define the Gaussian curvature weakly by

\int_{\mathbb{C}}K_{\Phi}(z)f(z)d\mu_{\Phi}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Phi(z)\Delta f(z)dz.

(1.1)

Note that this is invariant under LQG coordinate change: indeed, suppose that
U
is a bounded open set and that
f
is a
C_{c}^{2}
test function supported on
U.
If
\psi:U\to V
is a conformal map, then for
{\bar{\Phi}}=\Phi\circ\psi+Q\log|\psi^{\prime}|
we have

\int_{U}K_{\bar{\Phi}}(z)f(z)d\mu_{\mu_{\bar{\Phi}}}(z)=\int_{U}\Delta f\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Phi\circ\psi)dz+\int_{U}\Delta fQ\log|\psi^{\prime}|dz.

Since
\log|\psi^{\prime}|
is harmonic and the Dirichlet energy is invariant under conformal transformations, after integrating by parts we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{U}K_{\Phi}(z)f(z)d\mu_{\Phi}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Phi\circ\psi(z)\Delta f(z)dz=\int_{V}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Phi(z)(\Delta f)\circ\psi^{-1}(z)|\psi^{\prime}(z)|^{-2}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{V}\frac{\gamma}{2}\Phi(z)\Delta(f\circ\psi^{-1})(z)dz=\int_{V}K_{\Phi}(z)f\circ\psi^{-1}(z)d\mu_{\Phi}(z).

1.2
Discrete curvature

Suppose that
M
is a planar map embedded in
\mathbb{C}.
To define the curvature, suppose we set all angles at each corner of a face
F
to be
\frac{|F|-2}{|F|}\pi,
where
|F|
is the number of vertices incident to
F.
Inspired by the Gauss-Bonnet formula, it is natural to define the discrete curvature at a vertex
v
as

K_{M}(v):=2\pi-\sum_{F}\frac{|F|-2}{|F|}\pi

where the sum is taken over all possible faces
F
incident to
v.
Note that in the case the graph
\mathcal{G}
is a triangulation, this is equivalent to

K_{M}(v)=\frac{\pi}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(6-\deg(v)}}\right).

(1.2)

We conjecture that the discrete curvature
K_{M}
converges to the curvature
K_{\Phi}
for any planar map model in the
\gamma
-LQG universality class.

Conjecture 1.3
.

Let
\{M_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of infinite random planar maps believed to be in the universality class of
\gamma
-LQG (e.g. uniform infinite triangulations for
\gamma=\sqrt{8/3}
). Suppose we embed the map in the plane via any “reasonable embedding”, such as the circle packing embedding or the Tutte embedding. Then for an appropriate scaling factor
c_{n},
we have that for any
C_{c}^{2}
test function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R},

\frac{1}{c_{n}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}M_{n}}f(v)K_{M_{n}}(v)\xrightarrow{d}\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(z)K_{\Phi}(z)d\mu_{\Phi}(z)

where
\Phi
is an appropriate variant of the GFF. Moreover, the convergence in distribution holds jointly for any finite collection of
C_{c}^{2}
test functions.

1.3
Mated CRT maps

Mated CRT maps are a family of random planar map models which are in the universality class of
\gamma
-LQG for
\gamma\in(0,2)
. These maps are particularly natural since they are closely connected both to LQG and to other random planar map models. Indeed, as we discuss below, mated CRT maps have a direct connection to SLE and LQG thanks to the results of
[
DMS21
]
. On the other hand, mated CRT maps can be seen as “course grained approximations” to other types of random planar maps (e.g., uniform triangulations, spanning tree decorated maps, bipolar oriented maps) due to discrete mating of trees bijections. We refer to
[
GHS23
]
for a survey of various results related to mated-CRT maps.

In other literature, the CRT map is defined by discretizing time using
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}.
However, in this paper we consider a variant which uses a Poisson point process of intensity
\varepsilon^{-1}
instead. The two resulting graphs are not equivalent; however, they should both be in the same universality class. One can call the variant considered in this paper “Poisson mated CRT map”, but for the sake of brevity we will usually just refer to it as the mated CRT map (there is no risk of ambiguity since we will not consider any other variant). The advantage of the Poisson mated CRT map is that the space filling SLE segments depend locally on the underlying field (see Lemma
3.7
).

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0.
We let
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}=\{y_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
denote a Poisson point process chosen on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity
\varepsilon^{-1}
defined so that
\{y_{j}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
is an increasing sequence. Suppose that
(L,R):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a pair of correlated two sided standard linear Brownian motions, normalized such that
L_{0}=R_{0}=0
and such that
\mathrm{corr}(L_{t},R_{t})=-\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi\gamma^{2}}{4}}}\right)
for
t\neq 0.
The mated CRT map is defined to be the random planar map
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
obtained by mating (gluing together) discretized versions of the continuum random trees constructed from
L
and
R,
that is, two vertices
y_{j},y_{k}\in\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
such that
j<k
are connected if either

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{t\in[y_{j-1},y_{j}]}L_{t}}}\right)\vee\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{t\in[y_{k-1},y_{k}]}L_{t}}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{t\in[y_{j},y_{k-1}]}L_{t}}}\right)

(1.3)

or the same holds with
L
replaced by
R.
If
|j-k|\geq 2
and (
1.3
) holds for both
L
and
R,
then
y_{j},y_{k}
are connected by two edges (see Figure
1
, left).

One can draw the graph
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
in the plane by connecting two vertices
y_{j},y_{k}\in\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
by an arc above or below the real line according to whether (
1.3
) holds for
L
or
R
respectively (see Figure
1
, right). This makes the mated-CRT map into a planar map.

Figure 1:
Left:
Poisson mated CRT map. The bottom graph corresponds to the graph of
L,
while the top graph corresponds to the graph of
C-R
for some large enough constant
C.
The red lines are the vertical identifications (hence the “mating”), while the black horizontal lines correspond to the identifications when (
1.3
) holds.

Right:
A portion of the Poisson mated CRT map. The top blue arcs correspond to when (
1.3
) holds for
R,
while the bottom red ones correspond to when (
1.3
) holds for
L.
The vertex set is given by the Poison point process
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}.

In fact, the mated-CRT map is a triangulation. Indeed, with the above embedding of the planar graph, each face of
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
corresponds to a horizontal strip below the graph of
L
or above the graph of
R,
which is bounded by two horizontal segments on the real line between points in
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
and two segments of either the graph of
L
or of
R.
Almost surely, neither
L
nor
R
attains a local minimum at any point in
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
and neither
L
nor
R
has two local minima where it attains the same value. Hence a.s. one of the horizontal segments is a single point. This means that a.s. each face of
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
has exactly three vertices on its boundary, and thus
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
is a triangulation.

Note that the law of a mated CRT map does not depend on the parameter
\varepsilon,
however the parameter
\varepsilon
is relevant when taking scaling limits.

The mated CRT maps constructed above have a well established relation to SLE decorated LQG. Let
\tilde{\Phi}
be the
\gamma
-quantum cone field (a particular variant of the GFF, see subsection
2.1
), and let
\tilde{\eta}
be a whole-plane space-filling
\mathrm{SLE}_{\kappa}
from
\infty
to
\infty
sampled independently from
\tilde{\Phi},
then parameterized by
\gamma
-quantum mass with respect to
\tilde{\Phi},
where
\kappa=16/\gamma^{2}>4.
That is,
\mu_{\tilde{\Phi}}(\tilde{\eta}[a,b])=b-a
for each
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\tilde{\eta}(0)=0
.

Then we can define the graph
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
with vertex set
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
where two vertices
y_{i},y_{j}
are connected if the space filling SLE segments
\tilde{\eta}([y_{i-1},y_{i}])
and
\tilde{\eta}([y_{j-1},y_{j}])
(called
cells
) share a nontrivial boundary arc, with two edges occurring between the two segments if they share nontrivial boundary arcs in both the left and right boundaries. By
[
DMS21
]
, the resulting graph agrees in law with the mated CRT map defined above. This construction yields a natural embedding of the mated CRT map into
\mathbb{C}
, called the
SLE/LQG
embedding, by assigning each vertex
v\in\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
to a point in the corresponding mated CRT map cell. It is convenient to take this point to be
\tilde{\eta}(v)
. See Section
2.2
for more details. We will use this fact throughout the proof of the main Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
below.

1.4
Main results

Henceforth when we talk about the mated CRT map
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon},
we will always assume that it is coupled with
(L,R,\Lambda^{\varepsilon})
as described at the end of Section
1.3
. In particular,
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}\ni v\mapsto\tilde{\eta}(v)
is the SLE/LQG embedding of the mated CRT map.
To show a result such as Conjecture
1.3
for
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
, one would not only need a definition of the Gaussian curvature that is compatible with the discrete setting, but also precise control over the discrete curvature in order to find the correct scaling factor, and later prove its scaling limit matches our notion of LQG curvature. This is also related to the problem of finding an observable on a random planar map converging to the underlying field: indeed, if
f
is a
C_{c}^{2}
test function and we assume that

\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}f(\tilde{\eta}(v))K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v)\to\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(z)K_{\tilde{\Phi}}(z)d\mu_{\tilde{\Phi}}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta f(z)\tilde{\Phi}(z)dz,

for some appropriate scaling factor
a_{\varepsilon},
then we have

\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}f(\tilde{\eta}(v))\Delta^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v)\to\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(z)\tilde{\Phi}(z)dz,

and so, at least formally, we should have that
\Delta^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}\to\tilde{\Phi}
in some weak sense.

Our main theorem is a first step towards proving the convergence of the discrete curvature. If we let
f
be a
C_{c}^{2}
test function, and we look at the expression
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}}_{\varepsilon}}f(\tilde{\eta}(v))K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v),
at first glance one could expect it to be of order
\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}
since it is a sum over
\approx\varepsilon^{-1}
terms of random signs times values of
f
at the graph’s vertices, and so if we expect square root cancellation one would obtain the order
\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}.
However, our first main theorem tells us something much stronger: the sum
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}}_{\varepsilon}}f(\tilde{\eta}(v))K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v)
is in fact of a much smaller order.

Now we will state our first main theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
be the
\varepsilon
mated CRT map defined as in Definition
1.4
, with
\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}=\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
defined to be its vertex set. Let
K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}
denote the discrete curvature as defined in (
1.2
). Suppose that
f\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is any compactly supported twice differentiable function on
\mathbb{C}.
Then with probability going to
1
as
\varepsilon\to 0,
we have that

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}f(\tilde{\eta}(v))K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v)=\varepsilon^{o(1)}.

(1.4)

The
\varepsilon^{o(1)}
asymptotics in the statement of Theorem
1.5
is necessary: the next theorem tells us that if an appropriate normalization factor for the curvature summed against a
C_{c}^{2}
test function exists, then it must be slow varying.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\tilde{\Phi}
be the embedding of the
\gamma
-quantum cone used to define
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
.
Assume that there exists a twice continuously differentiable, compactly supported function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is not identically zero, and constants
\{a_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
such that we have the convergence in probability

\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(x)f(\tilde{\eta}(x))\to\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(z)K_{\tilde{\Phi}}(z)\,d\mu_{\tilde{\Phi}}(z)

(1.5)

with respect to the weak topology on generalized functions. Then
\{a_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is slowly varying as
\varepsilon\to 0
, i.e.,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{a_{C\varepsilon}}{a_{\varepsilon}}=1,\quad\forall C>0.

(1.6)

The proof is given in Section
2.4
.

One could ask whether the correct scaling factor should in fact be of constant order. We believe the answer is no. A heuristic argument for this is that if one makes a local change in
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
such as artificially increasing the degree of a vertex by
1,
then while the sum
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v)f(\tilde{\eta}(v))
is affected by a fixed amount for any
\varepsilon>0,
the scaling limit should not be, hence the scaling factor must make such local changes negligible.

Another interesting quantity is the total discrete curvature in a small region, such as a space filling SLE segment. More precisely, let
C=\tilde{\eta}([0,1]).
We define the discrete total curvature on
C
to be the sum of the discrete curvature on every vertex in
C,

K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(C)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}\cap C}K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(v).

(1.7)

This represents the total curvature in a space filling SLE segment.

Our second main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.7
.

There exists a deterministic constant
\alpha>0
such that

\frac{K_{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}}(C)}{\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{4}}}\to\alpha\mathcal{B},

in law, where the law of
\mathcal{B}
can be described as follows. Sample
\mathcal{L}
according to the law of the boundary length of the mated CRT map cell
C.
Now let
\Theta
be sampled according to a Gaussian distribution with mean
0
and variance
\mathcal{L}.
Then
\mathcal{B}
and
\Theta
have the same law.

Note that Theorem
1.5
tells us that if there is a scaling limit of the discrete curvature summed against a
C_{c}^{2}
test function, then the scaling used must be of order
\varepsilon^{o(1)}.
However, Theorem
1.7
tells us the correct scaling for the total curvature on a mated CRT map cell is
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}.
This discrepancy suggests that it is impossible to define the Gaussian curvature together with the curvature on rough or lower dimensional sets, which is consistent with the fact that it is not so straightforward to define the total Gaussian curvature on a space-filling SLE segment since
\int_{C}\Delta\tilde{\Phi}(z)dz
is not defined due to the poor regularity of
\Delta\tilde{\Phi}.
Moreover, it seems that defining the Gaussian curvature integrated against
C_{c}^{2}
test functions and the geodesic curvature on a mated CRT map cell in a consistent manner is impossible. Indeed, if one is to define the geodesic curvature on the boundary of space filling SLE segments, it must be defined in a manner that is compatible with the Gaussian curvature in the sense of the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, such that when “integrated” over the boundary gives us the total curvature over a space-filling SLE segment. Since the total Gaussian curvature uses a different scaling factor than the Gaussian curvature, this suggests that not all notions of curvature (at least on boundaries of space-filling SLE segment) are simultaneously compatible.

One of the main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.5
is a cancellation when computing
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}}f(\eta(v))K_{\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}}(v):
one can rewrite this sum as a sum of discrete gradients of
f
along appropriately oriented edges in
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}.
This is proved in Section
3
via purely combinatorial arguments. After this, we rewrite the sum as an integral of a function which is constant in each mated CRT map cell. To estimate this integral, we estimate its variance and split the double integral into two parts: the diagonal and off diagonal parts (see Propositions
4.12
and
4.14
respectively). For the diagonal, we use the fact that the measure of the diagonal region is small. For the off diagonal region, we use the long range independence properties of the GFF (see (
4.33
)). These estimates are proved in subsections
4.4
and
4.5
respectively. After this, we transfer all our results back to the case of the quantum cone in Section
5
. Part of our proof is inspired by techniques in
[
GMS19
]
, Section 3, however substantial new ideas are needed to get a very exact cancellation. In some sense, our argument pushes the technique in
[
GMS19
]
as far as possible.

For the proof of Theorem
1.7
, the main ingredients are a discrete analogue of Gauss-Bonnet about sums degrees on triangulations (see Lemma
6.2
), together with Donsker’s theorem to ensure the convergence of an average on the boundary of a mated CRT map cell.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper we will need the definitions of discrete gradient and Laplacian. We define them now. Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a triangulation of the plane. Suppose that this graph is embedded in
\mathbb{C}
and suppose that
f:\mathcal{V}\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C},
where
\mathcal{V}\mathcal{G}
is the set of vertices in
\mathcal{G}.
We define the discrete Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}f
by

\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}f(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}(f(y)-f(x)).

Similarly, for any oriented edge
\overrightarrow{e}
with starting vertex
v_{1}
and ending vertex
v_{2},
we define

\mathcal{D}f(\overrightarrow{e})=f(v_{2})-f(v_{1}).

(2.1)

We will slightly abuse notation, and define
\mathcal{D}f
the same way for functions
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
by identifying vertices
v\in\mathcal{G}
with their counterparts
\tilde{\eta}(v)\in\mathbb{C}.

2.1
Quantum cones

First we start with the definition of the quantum cone, an important field (random generalized function) which we will be using throughout the proof of Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\alpha<Q.
An
\alpha
-quantum cone is a doubly marked quantum surface. Its law can be described in the following way. Let
A_{s}
be the process defined by

A_{s}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{matrix}B_{s}+\alpha s,&s>0\\
\hat{B}_{-s}+\alpha s,&s<0,\end{matrix}}}\right.

where
B_{s}
is a Brownian motion with
B_{0}=0,
and
\hat{B}
is a Brownian motion independent of
B
conditioned so that
\hat{B}_{t}+(Q-\alpha)t>0
for all

2.2

The Schramm-Loewner evolution

]

\kappa.

\kappa\geq 8

[
RS05
]

\kappa>4

introduced in
[

]

SLE_{\kappa}
from
\infty
to
\infty
in
\mathbb{C}
here. To define it, let

2\pi\chi_{\mathrm{IG}}
to be an equivalence class of random distributions as follows. First we sample
\Psi
from the law of a whole-plane GFF normalized so that
\Psi_{1}(0)=0.
Suppose that we fix
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi].
Then by
[
MS17
, Theorem 1.1]
we can define the flow line of

at
z
with angle

To define the space filling

we will need the flow lines started at points in

with angles

or
\frac{\pi}{2},
which will be denoted
\tilde{\eta}_{z}^{L},\tilde{\eta}_{z}^{R}

For distinct

the flow lines
\tilde{\eta}_{z}^{L},\tilde{\eta}_{w}^{L}
merge a.s. upon intersecting, and similarly for
\tilde{\eta}_{z}^{R},\tilde{\eta}_{w}^{R}.
The two flow lines
\tilde{\eta}_{z}^{L},\tilde{\eta}_{w}^{R}

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\varepsilon}
be defined as in Definition
1.4
(with the
\gamma
-quantum cone field
\tilde{\Phi}
), and let
K
be a deterministic compact set. Let

denote the vertex associated with the mated CRT map cell containing the origin. There are constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that for any

we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{deg}(v_{0})>n)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}n^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma 2.5 in
[
GMS19
]
. Recall that the Poisson point process is denoted
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}=\{y_{j}\}.
First, let
j_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
denote the index such that
0\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j_{0}+1}),
and let

S:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\geq j_{0}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j_{0}+1}]}L_{t}}}\right)\vee\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}L_{t}}}\right)<\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j}]}L_{t}}}\right\}

To prove the lemma, it suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}(\#S>n)\leq c_{0}e^{-c_{1}n^{\frac{1}{2}}}

for appropriate constants
c_{0},c_{1}.
Note that if
j\in S,
then in particular we have that
L_{t}
has not yet reached
\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j_{0}+1}]}L_{t}
at time
y_{j},
but

\inf_{t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}L_{t}<\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j}]}L_{t}\leq 0.

Therefore if we define
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
as

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}=\#\{j\geq j_{0}:\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j}]}L_{t}>\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j_{0}+1}]}L_{t},\inf_{t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}L_{t}<0\},

then
\#S\lesssim\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}.
It suffices to bound
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}>n).

Now we will define a sequence of stopping times. Let
A
denote the infimum

A=\inf_{t\in[y_{j_{0}},y_{j_{0}+1}]}L_{t}.

We define the times
\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}
as follows: we let

\sigma_{n}=\tau_{n}+A^{2},

and

\tau_{n}=\min\{t:t\geq\sigma_{n-1}:L_{t}=0\}.

Define

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}:=\min\{n:\exists t\in[\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}]\mbox{ s.t. }L_{t}\leq A\}.

Then by definition of
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
we have that

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}}\#(\Lambda^{\varepsilon}\cap[\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}]).

(4.5)

First, we will bound
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}>n}}\right).
After this, we will use a union bound to bound the probability that simultaneously, every interval
[\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}]
has a bounded number of points in
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}.

By the Markov property of Brownian motion, we have that the expression

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists t\in[\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}]\mbox{ s.t. }L_{t}\leq M|\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}}}\right)

only depends on the constant
M,
where
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
L|_{[y_{j_{0}},\tau_{n}]}.

We will need the following estimate (which is a consequence of Bennett’s inequality): if

has a Poisson distribution of intensity

\mathbb{P}(X>x+\lambda)\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2(\lambda+x)}}.

(4.6)

In particular, using the fact that the number of points in
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}
contained in a fixed interval follows a Poisson distribution, we have that if
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\leq n
then we have that for some constant

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\Lambda^{\varepsilon}\cap[\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}]|\leq A^{2}n|A}}\right)\geq 1-ne^{-c_{1}A^{2}n^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Note that

\mathbb{P}(A>M)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{t\in[0,\ell]}L_{t}>M}}\right)\varepsilon e^{-\varepsilon\ell}d\ell\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-C\ell M}\varepsilon e^{-\varepsilon\ell}d\ell\lesssim e^{-CM}.

Hence for some

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A^{2}>n}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A>n^{\frac{1}{2}}}}\right)\leq e^{-c_{2}n^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Thus we obtain that with probability at most

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}>n.

Now, note that after conditioning on the intervals

the quantities
\#(\Lambda^{\varepsilon}\cap[\tau_{n},\sigma_{n}]),\#(\Lambda^{\varepsilon}\cap[\tau_{m},\sigma_{m}])
are independent. Since the interval

Therefore using a union bound and (

we have that
\mathrm{diam}(C)\geq\varepsilon^{\beta}
for any space filling SLE segment

Combining this with Lemma

∎

We also have that space-filling SLE segments cannot have very large

measure.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact set. There exists a
\tilde{\beta}>0
such that the following holds. With probability going to
1
as
\varepsilon\to 0,
for any mated CRT map cell
C
such that
C\cap K\neq\varnothing,
we have

\mathrm{area}(C)\leq\varepsilon^{\tilde{\beta}}.

Proof.

We proceed as in Lemma 3.8 in
[
DG20
]
. We have the standard moment bound

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\Phi}(B_{\delta}(z))^{p}}}\right)\leq\delta^{w(p)+o_{\delta}(1)}

where
p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}}}}\right),

\delta\in(0,1),
and
w(p)
is defined by

w(p):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}}\right)p-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}p^{2}

(See Theorem 2.14 in
[
RV14
]
and Lemma 5.2 in
[
GHM20
]
). Using Markov’s inequality we obtain

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\Phi}(B_{\delta}(z))>\delta^{\tilde{\beta}^{-1}}}}\right)\leq\delta^{w(p)-\tilde{\beta}^{-1}p+o_{\delta}(1)}.

Now taking
p=\frac{4+\gamma^{2}-2\tilde{\beta}^{-1}}{2\gamma^{2}}
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\Phi}(B_{\delta}(z))>\delta^{\tilde{\beta}^{-1}}}}\right)\leq\delta^{\frac{(4\gamma^{2}-2\tilde{\beta}^{-1})^{2}}{8\gamma^{2}}+o_{\delta}(1)}

after applying a union bound we conclude.
∎

Now we are ready to define the sets
Z_{j}.
The idea is to split into sets of
z

\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}
(as defined in (

)) has its diameter at a fixed scale. More precisely, we let

Note that by choosing

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sup_{z:\mathrm{Supp}f\cap H_{z}^{\varepsilon}\neq\varnothing}\mathrm{diam}\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{N}}}}\right),\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{z:\mathrm{Supp}f\cap H_{z}^{\varepsilon}\neq\varnothing}\mathrm{diam}\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{1}}}}\right)

4.6
and
4.7

2.4
we see that the probability that

4.4
Diagonal integral

Lemma 4.13
.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(z)X_{z}^{\varepsilon}|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z)}}\right)

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(z)\frac{\Gamma_{\tilde{f}_{z}}(z)}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}=a,\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}}-\zeta}(z)=b}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{a,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\tilde{f}_{z}}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}M_{a,b}}}\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(z)\frac{\Gamma_{\tilde{f}_{z}}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{a,b}-1}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}_{a,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(z)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\tilde{f}_{z}}}{\mathrm{area}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H_{z}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}_{a,b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((M_{a,b}-1)^{2}}}\right)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Proof of Proposition

.

We have

\displaystyle\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}\bar{X}_{z}^{\varepsilon}\bar{X}_{w}^{\varepsilon}dzdw

\displaystyle=\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{Z_{j}}(z)1_{Z_{j}}(w)X_{z}^{\varepsilon}X_{w}^{\varepsilon}dzdw

\displaystyle-2\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{Z_{j}}(z)X_{z}^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(w))|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w)}}\right)dzdw

\displaystyle+\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(z)X_{z}^{\varepsilon}|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z)}}\right)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(w)X_{w}^{\varepsilon}|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}(w)}}\right)dzdw

\displaystyle=:A_{1}-2A_{2}+A_{3}

(4.17)

Now we will bound each term individually. For any
z,w\in\mathbb{C},
let
E_{z,w}
denote the event that
|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta},
and
z,w\in Z_{j}.
We have

\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{Z_{j}}(z)1_{Z_{j}}(w)X_{z}^{\varepsilon}X_{w}^{\varepsilon}dzdw=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}X_{z}^{\varepsilon}X_{w}^{\varepsilon}1_{E_{z,w}}dzdw.

(4.18)

Let
V_{\varepsilon}
be defined by

V_{\varepsilon}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}:H_{v}^{\varepsilon}\cap\mathrm{Supp}\;f\neq\varnothing\}.

Going back to the discrete sum form, we have that

|A_{1}|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{\mathbb{C}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}X_{z}^{\varepsilon}X_{w}^{\varepsilon}1_{E_{z,w}}dzdw}}\right|\leq\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}|\Gamma_{f}(z)||\Gamma_{f}(w)|1_{E_{z,w}}.

(4.19)

Now using Lemma
3.9
we obtain that

\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}|\Gamma_{f}(z)||\Gamma_{f}(w)|1_{E_{z,w}}\leq\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}(\mathrm{deg}(z)+1)(\mathrm{deg}(w)+1)\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})1_{E_{z,w}}.

(4.20)

Note that if
E_{z,w}
holds, then

\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon^{2\alpha_{j}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}(\mathrm{deg}(z)+1)(\mathrm{deg}(w)+1)\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})1_{E_{z,w}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2\alpha_{j}}\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}(\mathrm{deg}(z)+1)(\mathrm{deg}(w)+1)1_{E_{z,w}}.

(4.21)

Then by the definition of
Z_{j}
we have

\sup_{z\in V_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{deg}(z)1_{Z_{j}}(z)\leq C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}.

This implies by (
4.10
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathrm{Supp}f}(\mathrm{deg}(z)+1)(\mathrm{deg}(w)+1)\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})1_{E_{z,w}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)^{6}\varepsilon^{2\alpha_{j}}\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}1_{E_{z,w}}.

Combining this with (
4.20
) we obtain

\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}|\Gamma_{f}(z)||\Gamma_{f}(w)|1_{E_{z,w}}\leq\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)^{6}\varepsilon^{2\alpha_{j}}\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}1_{E_{z,w}}.

(4.22)

Now we will estimate the sum

\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}1_{E_{z,w}}.

We have

\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}}1_{E_{z,w}}=\sum_{z\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}}\#\{w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:E_{z,w}\mbox{ holds}\}.

We also have that

\#\{w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:E_{z,w}\mbox{ holds}\}\leq\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:H_{w}^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z)\neq\varnothing,\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{w}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}},\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}}}}\right]}}\right\}.

(4.23)

By (
4.10
), if
w\in V_{\varepsilon},
there are at most
C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}
cells
y
such that
y\sim w.
Therefore if
\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{H}_{w}^{\varepsilon})\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}},
then there exists a
w
such that either
y=w
or
y\sim w
and

\mathrm{diam}(H_{y}^{\varepsilon})\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}{C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}}.

Hence by (
4.10
)

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:H_{w}^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z)\neq\varnothing,\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{w}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\in B_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}+\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}}}(z):\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H_{y}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}+\zeta}}{C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}}}}\right\}.

(4.24)

Now, using (
4.10
) we have that

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\in B_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}+\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}}}(z):\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H_{y}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}+\zeta}}{C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\in B_{2\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z):\mathrm{area}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H_{y}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{(2\alpha_{j}+3\zeta)}}{C^{2}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{6}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 4\varepsilon^{2\alpha_{j}-2\zeta}\frac{\varepsilon^{-(2\alpha_{j}+3\zeta)}}{C^{-2}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{-6}}=4C^{2}\varepsilon^{-5\zeta}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{6}

where in the last line we used the fact that the cells intersect only along their boundaries. Putting this together with (
4.23
),(
4.24
) we obtain

\#\{w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:E_{z,w}\mbox{ holds}\}\leq 4C^{3}\varepsilon^{-5\zeta}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{9}

(4.25)

and so

\#\{w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:E_{z,w}\mbox{ holds}\}\leq\varepsilon^{-5\zeta+o(1)}.

(4.26)

Hence

\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}}1_{E_{z,w}}\leq\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}},\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}}}}\right]}}\right\}\varepsilon^{-5\zeta+o(1)}.

(4.27)

Using (
4.10
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}},\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}}}}\right]}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq C\log\varepsilon^{-1}\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}{C\log\varepsilon^{-1}}}}\right\}.

where in the last line we used the fact that there are at most
(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}+1
cells in
\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}
, and so if
\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}
then there is a cell in
\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}
whose diameter is at least
\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}{C\log\varepsilon^{-1}}.
Now using the same argument as for (
4.25
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}:\mathrm{diam}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{H}_{z}^{\varepsilon}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq C(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j+1}}}{C\log\varepsilon^{-1}}}}\right)^{-2}\varepsilon^{-2\zeta}\mathrm{area}(\mathrm{Supp}f)

\displaystyle\lesssim C^{3}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{5}\varepsilon^{-2\alpha_{j+1}-2\zeta}.

Combining this with (
4.27
) we obtain

\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}}1_{E_{z,w}}\leq\varepsilon^{-2\alpha_{j+1}-7\zeta+o(1)}.

(4.28)

Plugging this into (
4.22
) and then back into (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) we obtain that

|A_{1}|\leq\varepsilon^{o(1)-9\zeta}.

(4.29)

Now we will bound
A_{2}.
By Lemma
4.13
we have

\displaystyle|A_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{Z_{j}}(z)|X_{z}^{\varepsilon}||\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Z_{j}}(z)X_{w}^{\varepsilon}|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w)}}\right)|dzdw+\varepsilon^{o(1)}

\displaystyle\leq\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{Z_{j}}(z)|X_{z}^{\varepsilon}|\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\mathrm{deg}(x_{w}^{\varepsilon})+1)\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})}|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w)}}\right)dzdw+\varepsilon^{o(1)}

\displaystyle=\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{Z_{j}}(z)|X_{z}^{\varepsilon}|\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.(\mathrm{deg}(x_{w}^{\varepsilon})+1)\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})}}}\right|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w)}}\right)dzdw+\varepsilon^{o(1)}.

Note that by Lemma
3.9
we have

|X_{z}^{\varepsilon}|\leq(\mathrm{deg}(x_{z}^{\varepsilon})+1)\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{2}|}{\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.1_{E_{z,w}}(\mathrm{deg}(x_{z}^{\varepsilon})+1)(\mathrm{deg}(x_{w}^{\varepsilon})+1)\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})}}}\right|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(w)}}\right)dzdw

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{o(1)}.

Now by definition of
Z_{j}
we have that

\displaystyle(\mathrm{deg}(x_{z}^{\varepsilon})+1)(\mathrm{deg}(x_{w}^{\varepsilon})+1)\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})}1_{E_{z,w}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2\zeta+o(1)}\frac{1}{\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{2}|}{\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2\zeta+o(1)}\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}\frac{1_{E_{z,w}}}{\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}dzdw+\varepsilon^{o(1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-3\zeta}\varepsilon^{o(1)}\int\int_{|z-w|<\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-3\zeta}}\frac{1_{E_{z,w}}\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{area}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})}dzdw+\varepsilon^{o(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{o(1)}\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-4\zeta}\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}}\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{diam}(H_{w}^{\varepsilon})1_{E_{z,w}}+\varepsilon^{o(1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{o(1)}\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-4\zeta}\varepsilon^{2\alpha_{j}-2\zeta}\sum_{z,w\in V_{\varepsilon}\cap Z_{j}}1_{E_{z,w}}+\varepsilon^{o(1)}.

By (
4.28
), we have that

\frac{|A_{2}|}{\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-15\zeta+o(1)}

hence

|A_{2}|\lesssim\varepsilon^{-15\zeta+o(1)}.

(4.30)

To bound
A_{3}
we note that by Lemma
4.13
together with (
4.10
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|1_{Z_{j}}(z)X_{z}^{\varepsilon}||\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\nabla^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.1_{Z_{j}}(z)(\mathrm{deg}(x_{z}^{\varepsilon})+1)\frac{\mathrm{diam}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\mathrm{area}(H_{z}^{\varepsilon})}}}\right|\Phi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z),\Psi_{\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}(z)}}\right)+\varepsilon^{o(1)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{o(1)-\zeta},

thus

|A_{3}|\leq\varepsilon^{o(1)-2\zeta}\int\int_{|z-w|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha_{j}-\zeta}}1_{\mathrm{Supp}(f)}(z)1_{\mathrm{Supp}(f)}(w)dzdw+\varepsilon^{o(1)}=\varepsilon^{o(1)-2\zeta}|\mathrm{supp}\;f|^{2}+\varepsilon^{o(1)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{o(1)-2\zeta}.

Combining this with (
4.29
), (
4.30
) and (
4.17
) yields the result of Proposition
4.12
.

The main result of this section is the following.

.

We have

Lemma 6.1

Let

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M}|\Lambda\cap I_{j}|}}\right)^{2}}}\right|M}}\right)}}\right)+2\mathbb{E}(M^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(CM^{2}}}\right)<\infty,
