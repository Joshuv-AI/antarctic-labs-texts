Abstract
. The circuit satisfaction problem 𝖢𝖲𝖠𝖳 ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) \CSAT(\mathbf{A}) of an algebra 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is the problem of deciding whether an equation over 𝐀 \mathbf{A} (encoded by two circuits) has a solution or not. While solving systems of equations over finite algebras is either in P or NP-complete, no such dichotomy result is known for 𝖢𝖲𝖠𝖳 ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) \CSAT(\mathbf{A}) . In fact, Idziak, Kawałek and Krzaczkowski [ IKK20 ] constructed examples of nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras 𝐀 \mathbf{A} , for which, under the assumption of the exponential time hypothesis and an open conjecture in circuit complexity, 𝖢𝖲𝖠𝖳 ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) \CSAT(\mathbf{A}) can be solved in quasipolynomial, but not polynomial time. The same is true for the circuit equivalence problem 𝖢𝖤𝖰𝖵 ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) \CEQV(\mathbf{A}) . In this paper we generalize their result to all nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras of Fitting length bigger than 2. This not only advances the project of classifying the complexity of 𝖢𝖲𝖠𝖳 \CSAT (and 𝖢𝖤𝖰𝖵 \CEQV ) for algebras from congruence modular varieties, but we also believe that the tools developed are of independent interest in the study of nilpotent algebras.



1.
Introduction

Solving equations over a given algebraic structure is one of the most fundamental problems in mathematics. In its various forms, it was one of the driving forces in developing new methods and entire new fields in algebra (e.g. Gaussian elimination, Galois theory, and algebraic geometry, to only mention a few). A big interest nowadays often lies in studying the problem from a computational perspective. While solving equations over infinite algebras can be undecidable (as it was for instance famously shown for Diophantine equations
[
Mat70
]
), it can always be done in non-deterministic polynomial time in finite algebras. So for finite algebras, one of the main tasks is to distinguish algebras, for which solving equations is tractable (in P), hard (NP-complete), or possibly of some intermediate complexity.

Solving
systems
of polynomial equations over finite algebras is closely connected to the constraint satisfaction problems (CSP) of finite relational structures: in fact, it is straightforward to see that solving such systems over an algebra
\mathbf{A}=(A,f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
can be modeled as the CSP, whose constraint relations are the function graphs of the operations
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
and all constants (e.g. observed in
[
LZ07
]
). Therefore the CSP dichotomy theorem, which was independently shown by Bulatov
[
Bul17
]
and Zhuk
[
Zhu17
,
Zhu20
]
, implies that also solving systems of equations over
\mathbf{A}
is either in P or NP-complete.

But, interestingly, not much is known for the version of the problem, in which we restrict the input to a bounded number of equations over
\mathbf{A}
. In fact, even the base case, in which the input consists of
one
polynomial equation is far from being understood. In the literature this problem is usually referred to as the
polynomial satisfiability problem
(or
equation solvability problem
)
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
.

Complete classifications of
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
exists for some classes algebras, such as rings
[
Hor11
]
, or lattices
[
Sch04
]
. The problem is also quite well understood for finite groups (see
[
GR02
,
Föl17
,
FH20
,
Wei20
]
), despite the situation not being completely resolved for solvable groups of Fitting length
2
(Problem 1 in
[
IKKW22
]
).

Classifying the complexity of
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
for arbitrary algebras
\mathbf{A}
seems to be out of reach at the moment. One of the main obstacles is, that the complexity of
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
is sensitive to changes of the language: In
[
HS12
]
it was shown that
\PolSAT
of the alternating group
(A_{4},\cdot,1,^{-1})
is in P, but, if we also allow expressions using commutator brackets
[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy
in the input, the problem becomes NP-complete.

To resolve this problem, Idziak and Krzaczkowski suggested in
[
IK18
]
to look at the circuit satisfaction problem
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
instead, in which the input equation is not encoded by polynomials, but by circuits over
\mathbf{A}
. Then, unlike for
\PolSAT
, the complexity is stable under changes of the signature of
\mathbf{A}
up to polynomial equivalence, which allows for an efficient use of methods from universal algebra.

The same paper also introduced the circuit equivalence problem
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
, which asks if two circuits over
\mathbf{A}
are equivalent, and initiated a systematic study of
\CSAT
and
\CEQV
for algebras from
congruence modular varieties
. This class of algebras, encompasses many algebras of interest in algebra and computer science (such as lattices, Boolean algebras, groups, quasigroups, rings, fields, vector spaces, modules), while still exhibiting some nice regularity properties.

In many cases, the computational hardness of
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
(and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
) is then intimately connected to existence of circuits that allow to express conjunctions. For example, in a finite field
(\mathbb{F}_{p},+,0,-,\cdot)
the polynomial
t_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{1}^{p-1}\cdot x_{2}^{p-1}\cdots x_{n}^{p-1}
can model the conjunctions of
n
Boolean variables, as it is equal to
0
, if there is an
x_{i}=0
, and equal to
1
else. Equations of the form
t_{n}(x_{1}-y_{1},\ldots,x_{n}-y_{n})=1
can then straightforwardly be used to encode the
p
-colouring problem, thus
\CSAT((\mathbb{F}_{p},+,0,-,\cdot))
is NP-complete. However, if we remove the multiplication
\cdot
from the signature (and therefore the ability to form conjunctions), we are left with the problem of solving a linear equation over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, which is clearly in P. The polynomials
t_{n}
are examples of so-called
absorbing
operations.

The results in
[
IK18
]
link the existence of non-trivial absorbing polynomials, commutator theoretical properties, and the complexity of
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
for algebras from congruence modular varieties: For so called
supernilpotent
algebras
\mathbf{A}
, which do not have absorbing polynomials of arbitrary big arities, the problems are in P (for
\CSAT
this was independently shown in
[
Kom18
]
, and for
\CEQV
already in
[
AM10
]
). On the other hand,
non-nilpotent
algebras allow to efficiently construct circuits, which express non-trivial absorbing polynomial operations. These circuits were used to prove that the circuit equivalence problem of non-nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras is coNP-complete, and the circuit satisfiability problem NP-complete
[
IKK22b
]
(and, modulo taking quotients, versions of these hardness results also hold in the general congruence modular case
[
IK18
]
).

This left an intriguing gap for nilpotent, but not supernilpotent Mal’tsev algebras (see Problem 2 in
[
IK18
]
). Although such algebras admit non-trivial absorbing polynomial operations of all arities, there are some known examples
\mathbf{A}
, for which
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
are solvable in polynomial time (see
[
IKK18
,
KKK19
]
). An explanation for this counter-intuitive phenomenon is, that these non-trivial absorbing polynomials cannot
efficiently
be represented by circuits.

For nilpotent algebras in general, this is ties to an open conjecture by Barrington, Straubing and Thérien in circuit complexity, which states that CC-circuits of bounded width (or equivalently, programs over solvable groups) require exponential size to express conjunctions
[
BST90
]
. This conjecture was shown in
[
Kom22
]
to be equivalent to absorbing polynomials in nilpotent algebras having the following lower bound on their size:

Conjecture 1

(
[
BST90
,
Kom22
]
)
.

For every finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra
\mathbf{A}
, there is a constant
0<q\leq 1
such that any circuit
C_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
over
\mathbf{A}
that is not constant and absorbing, has size at least
2^{\Omega(n^{q})}
.

Under Conjecture
1
we get following conditional result, which indicated that the problems might be easy for nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras:

Theorem 2

(
[
Kom22
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra. Under the assumption of Conjecture
1
,
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
can be solved in quasipolynomial time
2^{O((\log{n})^{t})}
(for
t=q^{-1}+1
).

In
[
IKK20
]
Idziak, Kawałek and Krzaczkowski contrasted this theorem with examples of nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras
\mathbf{D}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}]
, obtained as an extensions of Abelian groups, in which there are non constant
n
-ary absorbing circuits of size
2^{O(n^{\frac{1}{k-1}})}
with some additional nice properties. Assuming the
exponential time hypothesis (ETH)
, this allowed them to prove also proper, quasipolynomial lower bounds for the complexity of
\CSAT(\mathbf{D}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}])
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{D}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}])
, which indicates, together with Theorem
2
, that both
\CSAT(\mathbf{D}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}])
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{D}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}])
have NP-intermediate complexities.

We are going to generalize their result to all nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra of
Fitting length
k\geq 2
(where the Fitting length, is a straightforward generalization of the same concept for groups, and measures how far the algebra is from being supernilpotent). So, the main result of this paper states as follows:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra of Fitting length
k\geq 2
. Then, under the exponential time hypothesis (ETH), the deterministic complexity of
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
has a lower bound of
2^{o((\log{n})^{k-1})}
.

Theorem
3
together with Theorem
2
then implies the following:

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra of Fitting length
k>2
. Then, under the ETH and Conjecture
1
,
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
can be solved in quasipolynomial, but not polynomial time. So then, in particular

•

\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
is NP-intermediate,

•

\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
is coNP-intermediate.

By Corollary
4
, nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras of Fitting length
k=2
remain essentially the only algebras from congruence modular varieties, for which we do not have a (conditional) classification of the complexity of
\CSAT
and
\CEQV
(see the discussion in Section
7
).

Our proof of Theorem
3
is based on commutator theory, and in particular the concept of
wreath product representation
of nilpotent algebras, which might have some other future applications in understanding algorithmic and structural aspects of nilpotent algebras. Using such wreath product representations, we construct circuits over
\mathbf{A}
, which allow us to reduce 3-SAT (respectively p-COLOR) to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
in subexponential time, proving Theorem
3
.

An alternative construction of such circuits using methods from tame congruence theory was found by Idziak, Kawałek and Krzaczkowsi in
[
IKK22b
]
. In fact, they even constructed such
polynomials
, proving that also
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\PolEQV(\mathbf{A})
have intermediate complexity in the setting of Corollary
4
. We would however like to point out that a preprint of the underlying paper
[
Kom21
]
pre-dates
[
IKK22b
]
. We further believe that it can be beneficial to have different proof of the same result. In the case of this paper, we believe that wreath product representations might have future applications in studying nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras outside the scope of
\CEQV
and
\CSAT
(such as the subpower membership problem, or the finite basis problem).

This paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
we give an overview over necessary notions and results from universal algebra and commutator theory. In particular we define Fitting series and the Fitting length for general algebras
\mathbf{A}
. In Section
3
and
4
we extend some known results about linearly closed clonoids, respectively the higher commutator. In Section
5
we use the representation of nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras as wreath products of affine algebras, to characterize properties of certain congruences. We further construct the absorbing polynomials, which are necessary to finish the proof of Theorem
3
in Section
6
. In Section
7
we discuss some open problems.

2.
Background

In this section, we discuss some necessary definitions and results from universal algebra, especially commutator theory. For general background in universal algebra we refer to
[
Ber11
]
. For background in commutator theory (in congruence modular varieties) we refer to the book
[
FM87
]
, and the papers
[
AM10
,
Moo18
]
(for the higher commutator).

An algebra
\mathbf{A}
is a pair
(A,(f_{i}^{\mathbf{A}})_{i\in I})
, where
A
is a set and
(f_{i}^{\mathbf{A}})_{i\in I}
is a family of finitary operation
f_{i}^{\mathbf{A}}\colon A^{k_{i}}\to A
. The set
A
is called the
universe
of
\mathbf{A}
, and the functions
(f_{i}^{\mathbf{A}})_{i\in I}
are called the
basic operations
of
\mathbf{A}
. The
signature
of
\mathbf{A}
is the family
((f_{i},k_{i}))_{i\in I}
, i.e. it consists of the
operation symbols

f_{i}
, together with their arity
\arity(f_{i})=k_{i}
. In this paper,
finite algebras
are algebras that have both finite universe and finite signature.

The
polynomial operations
of
\mathbf{A}
are the finitary operations on
A
that can be defined by compositions of basic operations and elements of
A
. We denote the set of all polynomial operations of
\mathbf{A}
by
\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, and the set of all polynomials of arity
k
by
\Pol^{k}(\mathbf{A})
. If
\Pol(\mathbf{A})=\Pol(\mathbf{B})
we say that
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are
polynomially equivalent
.

The most common way to encode the polynomial operations of
\mathbf{A}
is by
polynomials
, i.e. terms that use operation symbols from the signature of
\mathbf{A}
and constant symbols for elements of
A
. For example
p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(x_{1}\cdot x_{4}+(-3))\cdot(3\cdot x_{2})+1
, is a polynomial over the ring of integers
\mathbf{A}=({\mathbb{Z}},+,0,-,\cdot)
that defines a 4-ary polynomial operation
p^{\mathbf{A}}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.

However, in an effort to compress the input, one can also encode polynomial operations by
circuits
over
\mathbf{A}
, i.e.
A
-valued circuits, with a unique output gate, whose gates are labeled by the basic operation of
\mathbf{A}
(see e.g.
[
IK18
]
). Polynomials can be regarded as those circuits, whose underlying digraph is a tree.

Occasionally, when it is clear from the context, we are not going to formally distinguish between an operation symbol
f
and the corresponding basic operation
f^{\mathbf{A}}
. Similarly we are sometimes also not going to distinguish between a polynomial
p
(circuit
C
) and the induced polynomial operation
p^{\mathbf{A}}
(or
C^{\mathbf{A}}
), but this should never cause any confusion.

For a fixed finite algebra
\mathbf{A}
the circuit satisfaction problem
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and the circuit equivalence problem
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
are defined as

Circuit satisfaction

\CSAT(\mathbf{A})

Input:
Two circuits
C_{1},C_{2}
over
\mathbf{A}
with input gates
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

Question:
Is there a tuple
\bar{a}\in A^{n}
such that
C_{1}^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{a})=C_{2}^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{a})
?

Circuit equivalence

\CEQV(\mathbf{A})

Input:
Two circuits
C_{1},C_{2}
over
\mathbf{A}
with input gates
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

Question:
Does
\mathbf{A}\models C_{1}(\bar{x})\approx C_{2}(\bar{x})
, i.e., does
C_{1}^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{a})=C_{2}^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{a})
hold for all
\bar{a}\in A^{n}
?

If we only allow polynomials in the input, then we obtain the polynomial satisfaction problem
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
and polynomial equivalence problem
\PolEQV(\mathbf{A})
. Thus
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
trivially reduces to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
, and
\PolEqv(\mathbf{A})
to
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
.

A congruence
\alpha
of
\mathbf{A}
is an equivalence relation on
A
that is preserved by the operations of
\mathbf{A}
. The set of all congruences of
\mathbf{A}
forms the congruence lattice
\Con(\mathbf{A})
, whose maximal element is
\mathbf{1}_{A}=A\times A
, and whose minimum is
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\{(x,x)\colon x\in A\}
. For two tuples
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in A^{n}
of the same length, we are going to write
(\bar{a},\bar{b})\in\alpha
, if the
(a_{i},b_{i})\in\alpha
, for all indices
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Algebras in this paper will always be from
congruence modular varieties
, i.e. from varieties, in which every algebra has a modular congruence lattice. In fact, we are only going to study algebras which have a
Mal’tsev term

m
, i.e. a ternary term satisfying the identities
m(y,x,x)\approx m(x,x,y)\approx y
. For short, we call such algebras
Mal’tsev algebras
. It is well-known that a variety has a Mal’tsev term if and only if it is
congruence permutable
. In particular this implies that every Mal’tsev algebra generates a congruence modular variety. The opposite implication is not true in general, but holds in the setting of nilpotent algebras
[
FM87
]
. Thus, for simplicity we are going to refer to such algebras simply as
nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras
, instead of the lengthy (albeit more general) “nilpotent algebras from congruence modular varieties”.

2.1.
Commutator theory

The commutator is an additional operation on the congruence lattice
\Con(\mathbf{A})
, that allows us to generalize concepts like Abelianess, nilpotence or solvability from group theory. In the following, higher dimensional form, the commutator was first introduced by Bulatov in
[
Bul01
]
; but it was studied for the binary case
n=2
long before that
[
Smi76
,
FM87
]
.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\delta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
congruences. We then say that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}
centralize
\alpha_{n}
modulo
\delta
, and write
C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n};\delta)
, if for all arities
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n}\geq 1
, for all polynomial operations
p(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n})\in\Pol^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}(\mathbf{A})
and all tuples
\bar{a}_{i},\bar{b}_{i}\in A^{k_{i}}
such that

•

(\bar{a}_{i},\bar{b}_{i})\in\alpha_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, and

•

(p(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n-1},\bar{a}_{n}),p(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n-1},\bar{b}_{n}))\in\delta
,

for all
(\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{n-1})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\{\bar{a}_{i},\bar{b}_{i}\}\setminus\{(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{n-1})\}
,

it also holds that

(p(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{n-1},\bar{a}_{n}),p(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{n-1},\bar{b}_{n}))\in\delta.

The
(higher) commutator

[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
is the smallest congruence
\delta
such that
C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\delta)
holds.

In groups,
[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
is the congruence given by the commutator subgroup
[N_{1},N_{2},\ldots,N_{n}]=[N_{1},[N_{2},\ldots,[N_{n-1},N_{n}]\ldots]]
, where
N_{i}=[e]_{\alpha_{i}}
is the normal subgroup corresponding to
\alpha_{i}
. Similarly, in rings,
[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
is given by the product of the ideals corresponding to the congruences
\alpha_{i}
.

Using the commutator, we define the following generalizations of solvability and nilpotence from group theory:

Definition 6
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we call a congruence
\alpha\in\Con(\mathbf{A})

(1)

n
-solvable
if
\alpha^{[n]}=\mathbf{0}_{A}
in the series defined by
\alpha^{[0]}=\alpha
and
\alpha^{[i+1]}=[\alpha^{[i]},\alpha^{[i]}]
,

(2)

n
-nilpotent
if
\alpha^{(n)}=\mathbf{0}_{A}
in the series defined by
\alpha^{(0)}=\alpha
and
\alpha^{(i+1)}=[\alpha,\alpha^{(i)}]
,

(3)

n
-supernilpotent
, if
[\alpha,\ldots,\alpha]_{n}=[\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{n+1\text{ times}}]=\mathbf{0}_{A}
.

Further
\alpha\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
is called
solvable (nilpotent, supernilpotent)
, if there is an
n
such that
\alpha
is
n
-solvable (
n
-nilpotent,
n
-supernilpotent)
.
The algebra
\mathbf{A}
is called (
n
-)
solvable (nilpotent, supernilpotent)
, if the full congruence
\mathbf{1}_{A}
is (
n
-)
solvable (nilpotent, supernilpotent)
.

We want to stress that, although nilpotence and supernilpotence agree in groups and rings, the same is not true in general Mal’tsev algebras (as e.g. demonstrated by the loop in
[
VL83
]
). The higher commutator has the following monotonicity properties (see e.g.
[
AM10
]
):

(HC1)

[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]\leq\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}

(HC2)

If
\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for all
i
, then
[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]\leq[\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}]

(HC3)

[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]\leq[\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]

In congruence modular variety, furthermore the following properties hold
[
Moo18
]
:

(HC4)

[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]=[\alpha_{\pi(1)},\ldots,\alpha_{\pi(n)}]
for all permutations
\pi\in\Sym(n)

(HC5)

[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]\leq\beta
if and only if
C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\beta)

(HC6)

[\alpha_{1}/\beta,\ldots,\alpha_{n}/\beta]=([\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]\lor\beta)/\beta
in
\mathbf{A}/\beta

(HC7)

[\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{i-1},\bigvee\Gamma,\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]=\bigvee_{\gamma\in\Gamma}[\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{i-1},\gamma,\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
, for all
i=1,\ldots,n

(HC8)

[\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{i-1},[\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]]\leq[\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
for all
i=2,\ldots,n-2

Instead of working with Definition
5
itself, we are often going to use a characterization of the commutator in Mal’tsev algebras by Aichinger and Mudrinski. For this we need to define absorbing operations:

Definition 7
.

An operation
c\colon A^{n}\to A
is called
absorbing at

\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in A^{n}
, if it satisfies the identities
c(a_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\approx c(x_{1},a_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n})\approx\cdots\approx c(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\approx c(\bar{a})
. In the special case that
\bar{a}=(0,0,\ldots,0)
, and
c(\bar{a})=0
for some fixed
0\in A
, the operation
c
is called
0
-absorbing
.

Then the higher commutator can be described as follows:

Theorem 8

(Lemma 6.9 in
[
AM10
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’tsev algebra, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
. Then
[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}]
is generated as a congruence by the set

S=\{(c(\bar{a}),c(\bar{b}))\colon\bar{a},\bar{b}\in A^{n},(a_{i},b_{i})\in\alpha_{i}\text{ for all }i,\text{ and }c\in\Pol^{n}(\mathbf{A})\text{ is absorbing at }\bar{a}\}.

In particular, Theorem
8
was used in
[
AM10
]
to prove the following characterization of supernilpotent algebras:

Theorem 9

(Proposition 7.7. in
[
AM10
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’tsev algebra. Then
[\mathbf{1}_{A},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{A}]_{k}=\mathbf{0}_{A}
if and only if every
0
-absorbing polynomial of
\mathbf{A}
of arity
l>k
is constant.

Furthermore, a finite Mal’tsev algebras is supernilpotent if and only if it is a direct product of nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras of prime power sizes
[
Kea99
]
. This result was generalized to the following characterization of supernilpotent congruences by Mayr and Szendrei:

Theorem 10

(Theorem 4.2. in
[
MS21
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite algebra such that
\HSP(\mathbf{A})
omits type
\mathbf{1}
. Then a congruence
\alpha\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
is supernilpotent if and only there are
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\leq\alpha
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\alpha
is nilpotent

(2)

\beta_{1}\land\cdots\land\beta_{k}=0

(3)

(\beta_{1}\land\cdots\land\beta_{i-1})\circ\beta_{i}=\beta_{i}\circ(\beta_{1}\land\cdots\land\beta_{i-1})=\alpha
for every
i>1

(4)

For every
i
there is a prime
p_{i}
, such that the size of every block of
\alpha/\beta_{i}
in
\mathbf{A}/\beta_{i}
is a power of
p_{i}
.

Theorem
10
in particular applies to finite Mal’tsev algebras
\mathbf{A}
.

2.2.
Fitting series

In this section we generalize the group theoretic notion of Fitting series to arbitrary algebras, and make some basic observations.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra, and
\alpha\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
. Then:

•

A sequence of congruences
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{m}=\alpha
is called a
Fitting series
of
\alpha
, if for every
i=1,\ldots,m
there is an integer
j_{i}
, such that
[\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{i}]_{j_{i}}\leq\alpha_{i-1}
(so
\alpha_{i}
is supernilpotent modulo
\alpha_{i-1}
).

•

The
Fitting length of
\alpha
is the smallest
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
\alpha
has a Fitting series of length
m
. If
\alpha
has no Fitting series, we say it has Fitting length
\infty
.

•

Fitting series (Fitting length) of
\mathbf{1}_{A}
are also called
Fitting series (Fitting length)
of
\mathbf{A}
.

Algebras
\mathbf{A}
of Fitting length
\leq m
were also called
m
-step supernilpotent
in
[
IKK20
]
.

Note that, since in groups nilpotence and supernilpotence coincide, a Fitting series of a group is also a Fitting series in the sense of Definition
11
. (We remark that, for this reason, one could equally make the point of defining Fitting series to be series of congruences in which every element is
nilpotent
modulo its predecessor. We are however not aware of any previous such use of the notion in the literature. Considering structural results such as Theorem
10
, we think that Definition
11
is the more useful and hence more fitting generalization of “Fitting series” from group theory.)

As in group theory, we can describe algebras of finite Fitting length by their
upper
and
lower
Fitting series.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra such that
\Con(\mathbf{A})
is finite. Then
\mathbf{A}
has Fitting length
m<\infty
if and only if the series defined by
\gamma_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
, and
\gamma_{i+1}=\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}[\gamma_{i},\ldots,\gamma_{i}]_{n}
reaches
\mathbf{0}_{A}
after exactly
m
steps. We call
\gamma_{0}\geq\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\cdots
the
lower Fitting series
of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{A}
has only finitely many congruences (and by (HC3)),
\gamma_{i+1}=[\gamma_{i},\ldots,\gamma_{i}]_{l}
for a big enough
l
. Thus, if the lower Fitting series reaches
\mathbf{0}_{A}
after
m
many steps, then
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\gamma_{m}\leq\gamma_{m-1}\leq\cdots\leq\gamma_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
is a Fitting series of length
m
.

To show that
m
is minimal, let
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
be an arbitrary Fitting series. We then prove by induction on
i=0,\ldots,k
that
\gamma_{i}\leq\alpha_{k-i}
. For
i=0
this is clearly true. For an induction step
i\to i+1
note that, by (HC2):
[\gamma_{i},\ldots,\gamma_{i}]_{l}\leq[\alpha_{k-i},\ldots,\alpha_{k-i}]_{l}
for every
l
. For big enough
l
we get
\gamma_{i+1}=[\gamma_{i},\ldots,\gamma_{i}]_{l}\leq[\alpha_{k-i},\ldots,\alpha_{k-i}]_{l}\leq\alpha_{k-i-1}
. We conclude that
\gamma_{k}\leq\alpha_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{A}
and therefore
m\leq k
.
∎

Observation 13
.

Assume that
\mathbf{A}
generates a congruence modular variety. Then it follows directly from (HC7) and (HC3) that
[\alpha\lor\beta,\ldots,\alpha\lor\beta]_{k+l}\leq[\alpha,\ldots,\alpha]_{k}\lor[\beta,\ldots,\beta]_{l}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
. As a consequence, the join of two supernilpotent congruences is again supernilpotent. So if
\Con(\mathbf{A})
is finite, this implies that there is a maximal supernilpotent congruence.

Definition 14
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra from a congruence modular variety, such that
\Con(\mathbf{A})
is finite. Then, the
Fitting congruence

\lambda
of
\mathbf{A}
is the maximal supernilpotent congruence.

Note that the Fitting congruence generalizes the concept of the
Fitting subgroup
, which is the maximal (super-)nilpotent normal subgroup of a group. The existence of such a Fitting congruence allows us to generalize upper Fitting series from group theory:

Lemma 15
.

Assume
\mathbf{A}
is from a congruence modular variety and
\Con(\mathbf{A})
is finite. We then set
\lambda_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{A}
and define
\lambda_{i+1}\geq\lambda_{i}
to be the maximal congruence that is supernilpotent modulo
\lambda_{i}
for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We call
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots
the
upper Fitting series
. Then
\mathbf{A}
has Fitting length
m<\infty
if and only if the lower Fitting series reaches
\mathbf{1}_{A}
after exactly
m
steps.

Proof.

By Observation
13
, the upper Fitting series is well-defined. By its definition, for every
i
there is a
j_{i}
such that
[\lambda_{i},\ldots,\lambda_{i}]_{j_{i}}\leq\lambda_{i-1}
. Thus, if
\lambda_{m}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
, then the Fitting length of
\mathbf{A}
is bounded by
m
.

For the other direction, assume that there is some other Fitting series
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
. We claim that
\alpha_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
for every
i=0,\ldots,k
. For
i=0
this is clearly true. For an induction step
i\to i+1
, note that
[\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{i+1}]_{l}\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
for some
l
, so
\alpha_{i+1}
is supernilpotent modulo
\lambda_{i}
. But
\lambda_{i+1}
is the join of all congruences that are supernilpotent modulo
\lambda_{i}
, thus
\alpha_{i+1}\leq\lambda_{i+1}
.
∎

We can further generalize the fact that a group is solvable if and only if it has finite Fitting length:

Lemma 16
.

Every solvable algebra
\mathbf{A}
has finite Fitting length (which is bounded by the degree of solvability).
If
\mathbf{A}
generates a congruence modular variety, also the converse holds.

Proof.

For solvable
\mathbf{A}
the derived series
\mathbf{1}_{A}^{[0]},\mathbf{1}_{A}^{[1]},\mathbf{1}_{A}^{[2]},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{A}^{[m]}=\mathbf{0}_{A}
is clearly a Fitting series; so
\mathbf{A}
has finite Fitting length.

On the other hand, assume that
\mathbf{A}
generates a modular variety and has a finite Fitting series
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{m}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
. By (HC3) there is an integer
l
such that
[\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{i}]_{2^{l}}\leq\alpha_{i-1}
for every
i
. From property (HC8) of the higher commutator it follows that
\alpha_{i}^{[l]}\leq[\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{i}]_{2^{l}}\leq\alpha_{i-1}
. Therefore
\mathbf{A}
is solvable.
∎

Note that Lemma
12
,
15
and
16
can be generalized to characterize
congruences
of finite Fitting length.

2.3.
Nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras

By Definition
6
an algebra
\mathbf{A}
is (
n
-)nilpotent, if the series
\mathbf{1}_{A}\geq[\mathbf{1}_{A},\mathbf{1}_{A}]\geq[\mathbf{1}_{A},[\mathbf{1}_{A},\mathbf{1}_{A}]]\geq\cdots
reaches
\mathbf{0}_{A}
after
n
-many steps. It is not hard to see that this is equivalent to the existence of any
central series
of length
n
, i.e. any series of congruences
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
, such that
[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{i+1}]\leq\alpha_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Finite nilpotent algebras show some very regular behaviour in congruence modular varieties. In particular, they always have a Mal’tsev term
[
FM87
, Chapters 6 & 7]
, therefore we are only going to refer them as nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras from now on.

A Mal’tsev algebra
\mathbf{A}
is 1-nilpotent (or
Abelian
) if and only if it is
affine
, i.e. polynomially equivalent to a module
[
Smi76
,
Her79
]
. Here, a module is an algebra
(A,+,0,-,(r)_{r\in R})
, where
(A,+,0,-)
is the underlying Abelian group and every ring element
r\in R
is considered as a unary operation
r(x)=r\cdot x
. Therefore all polynomial operations of an affine algebra
\mathbf{A}
are affine operations
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}x_{i}+c
for some scalars
r_{i}\in R
and a constant
c\in A
. In particular, any Mal’tsev polynomial of
\mathbf{A}
must be of the form
m(x,y,z)=x-y+z
.

General nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras can be written as the
wreath products
of affine algebras, which we will define below:

Definition 17
.

Let
\mathbf{L}=(L,(f^{\mathbf{L}})_{f\in F})
and
\mathbf{U}=(U,(f^{\mathbf{U}})_{f\in F})
be two algebras of the same signature
F
, such that
\mathbf{L}
is affine. Further let
T=(\hat{f})_{f\in F}
be a family of operations
\hat{f}\colon U^{\arity(f)}\to L
. Then we define
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{L}\otimes^{T}\mathbf{U}
as the algebra such that:

•

its universe is
A=L\times U

•

every basic operation on
\mathbf{A}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{pr}_{U}(f^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{x}))

\displaystyle=f^{\mathbf{U}}(\mathrm{pr}_{U}(\bar{x}))

\displaystyle\mathrm{pr}_{L}(f^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{x}))

\displaystyle=f^{\mathbf{L}}(\mathrm{pr}_{L}(\bar{x}))+\hat{f}(\mathrm{pr}_{U}(\bar{x})),

where
\mathrm{pr}_{U}(\bar{x})
and
\mathrm{pr}_{L}(\bar{x})
denote the (coordinate-wise) projection of a tuple
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
to
U^{n}
, or
L^{n}
respectively, and
+
is the addition on
\mathbf{L}
.

We are going to call
\mathbf{L}\otimes^{T}\mathbf{U}
a
wreath product
, since it is a special case of a wreath product according to the definition of VanderWerf in
[
Van94
,
Van96
]
. In Proposition 7.1. of
[
FM87
]
, Freese and McKenzie showed that Mal’tsev algebras
\mathbf{A}
with center
\zeta
can be written as the wreath product of
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}/\zeta
, and an affine algebra
\mathbf{L}
. Following their proof, it is not hard to see that the result holds for arbitrary central congruences
\zeta
:

Theorem 18

(Proposition 7.1. in
[
FM87
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’tsev algebra,
\zeta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
such that
[\mathbf{1}_{A},\zeta]=\mathbf{0}_{A}
, and
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}/\zeta
. Then there is an affine algebra
\mathbf{L}
in the same signature and a family of operations
T
, such that
\mathbf{A}\cong\mathbf{L}\otimes^{T}\mathbf{U}
.

Note that
\zeta
is the kernel of the projection
\mathrm{pr}_{U}
. Theorem
18
directly implies the following:

Corollary 19
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’tsev algebra, let
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
be a series of congruences such that
[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{i}]\leq\alpha_{i-1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, and let
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}/\alpha_{n}
. Then there are affine algebras
\mathbf{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{L}_{n-1}
in the same signature as
\mathbf{A}
, and families of operations
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n-1}
, such that

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle\cong\mathbf{L}_{1}\otimes^{T_{1}}(\cdots(\mathbf{L}_{n-1}\otimes^{T_{n-1}}\mathbf{U})).

Definition 20
.

For simplicity we abbreviate the representation of the Mal’tsev algebra
\mathbf{A}
in (
1
) by
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{U}
, and call it a
wreath product representation of
\mathbf{A}
with respect to
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
. Note that (if we identify the domains of
\mathbf{A}
and
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{U}
) then, for every basic operation
f
, its projection to the component
L_{j}
is of the form

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(f^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{x}))

\displaystyle=f^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(\bar{x}))+\hat{f}_{i}(\mathrm{pr}_{(L_{i+1}\times\cdots\times L_{n}\times U)}(\bar{x})),

for a function
\hat{f}_{i}\colon(L_{i+1}\times\cdots\times L_{n}\times U)^{k}\to L_{i}
.

We remark that there is some freedom in how to pick a wreath product representation of
\mathbf{A}
for a series
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
, that stems from the choice of a constant
0^{L_{i}}
in every affine component
\mathbf{L}_{i}
and, with it, the addition
x+y=m^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}(x,0^{L_{i}},y)
. Thus, implicitly, every wreath product representation comes with a constant
0=(0^{L_{1}},0^{L_{2}},\ldots,0^{L_{n}})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}
. For a fixed such constant we can ensure that
\hat{f}_{i}
and
f_{i}^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}
are uniquely determined by requiring that
f_{i}^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}(0^{L_{i}},0^{L_{i}},\ldots,0^{L_{i}})=0^{L_{i}}
for all
i
. For notational simplicity, we are not going to distinguish the zeros of the different affine compontents, i.e.
0=(0,0,\ldots,0)\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}
; this should however never cause any confusion.

If
\alpha_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
we simply write
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}
. It is not hard to see that every Mal’tsev algebra of the form
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}
is
n
-nilpotent. So by Corollary
19
, a Mal’tsev algebra is
n
-nilpotent if and only if, it is isomorphic to some wreath product
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}
.

Note that also for every polynomial operation
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, and every component
L_{i}
, there is a decomposition of
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(p^{\mathbf{A}})
into
p^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}
and
\hat{p}_{i}\colon(L_{i+1}\times\cdots\times L_{n}\times U)^{k}\to L_{i}
as in (
2
). A well-known consequence of this, is that nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras define loop operations in the following sense:

Theorem 21

(Corollary 7.4. in
[
FM87
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a nilpotent algebra with Mal’tsev polynomial
m\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, and let
0\in A
. Then
x+y=m(x,0,y)
is a loop multiplication with neutral element
0
. Furthermore also the left and right inverse
x/y
and
x\backslash y
are polynomials of
\mathbf{A}
.

We would like to emphasize that the above loop operation ’
+
’ is in general neither commutative, nor associative, but we choose the additive notation, since this works well with wreath product representations. If e.g.
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}
is a representation of
\mathbf{A}
(with respect to the constant
0
), then
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(x+^{\mathbf{A}}y)
is equal to the sum
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(x)+^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(y)
plus some “distortion” function
\hat{\phi}(\mathrm{pr}_{(L_{i+1}\times\cdots\times L_{n})}(x,y))
.

Also absorbing polynomials behave nicely with respect to wreath product decomposition, by the following observation:

Observation 22
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{U}
be a wreath product representation of
\mathbf{A}
, and
p\in\Pol^{k}(\mathbf{A})
be a
0
-absorbing polynomial of arity
k\geq 2
. Then, for every
i=1,\ldots,n
:

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(p^{\mathbf{A}}(\bar{x}))=\hat{p}_{i}(\mathrm{pr}_{(L_{i+1}\times\cdots\times L_{n}\times U)}(\bar{x})),

for an absorbing function
\hat{p}_{i}\colon(L_{i+1}\times\cdots\times L_{n}\times U)^{k}\to L_{i}
. This follows directly from the fact that
p^{\mathbf{L}_{i}}
must be a
k
-ary affine absorbing operation, and therefore constant.

We define the
exponent
\exp(\mathbf{L}_{i})
of an affine algebra
\mathbf{L}_{i}
to be the exponent of the group reduct
(L_{i},+,0,-)
of
\mathbf{L}_{i}
(i.e. the smallest integer
n
such that
n\cdot x=x+\cdots+x=0
for all
x\in L_{i}
). Most of the time, we are only going to consider wreath products, such that every affine
\mathbf{L}_{i}
has a prime exponent, so
(L_{i},+,0,-)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{m}
, where
p
is a prime. Let us call such wreath products
elementary
(as the groups
(L_{i},+,0,-)
are elementary Abelian). It is enough to focus on elementary wreath products by the following observation:

Observation 23
.

If
\mathbf{L}
is a module, and
\mathbf{M}
a submodule of
\mathbf{L}
, then
\mathbf{L}\cong\mathbf{M}\otimes(\mathbf{L}/\mathbf{M})
. In particular, note that if
\mathbf{M}=\{p\cdot x\colon x\in L\}
for a prime divisor
p
of
|L|
, then
\exp(\mathbf{L}/\mathbf{M})=p
, and the unary polynomial
p\cdot x
maps
L
to
M\times\{0\}
. It is not hard to see that this is also true for affine algebra (wreath product decompositions are preserved under polynomial equivalence).

As a consequence, every wreath product representation
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{U}
of a Mal’tsev algebra can be refined into an elementary wreath product, by decomposing every factor
\mathbf{L}_{i}
into elementary components as described above. In particular, every central series
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}\prec\alpha_{1}\prec\ldots\prec\alpha_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
, in which
\alpha_{i}
covers
\alpha_{i-1}
induces an elementary wreath product.

At last, note that a wreath product also encodes other congruences (and corresponding quotient algebras) than just the series
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
in the following sense:

Observation 24
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{U}
be a wreath product representation of
\mathbf{A}
, and let
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that for every polynomial
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
and every
j\notin I
,
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(p(\bar{x}))
does not depend on
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(\bar{x})
for all
i\in I
. Then, projecting
\mathbf{A}
to
\bigotimes_{j\notin I}L_{j}\otimes U
is a well-defined homomorphism.

3.
A result about linearly closed clonoids

In this section we discuss a result about polynomial clonoids, which we define as follows:

Definition 25
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
,
\mathbf{U}
be two algebras with universes
L
and
U
respectively. Let us call a set of operations
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}L^{U^{n}}
a
(polynomial)
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{U})
-clonoid
, if

•

f\circ(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in\mathcal{C}
for all
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
and
f\in\Pol(\mathbf{L})

•

g\circ(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})\in\mathcal{C}
for all
g\in\mathcal{C}
and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in\Pol(\mathbf{U})

So, in words,
\mathcal{C}
is a
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{U})
-clonoid, if it is closed under composition with polynomials of
\mathbf{L}
from the outside, and polynomials of
\mathbf{U}
from the inside. We remark that our notion of a
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{U})
-clonoid is a strengthening of the original definition of an
\mathbf{L}
-clonoid
in
[
AM16
]
, which is a set of operations
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}L^{U^{n}}
, that is only closed under the
term
operations of
\mathbf{L}
.

Studying a wreath product goes hand in hand with studying clonoids. In Observation
22
we saw, for instance, that
0
-absorbing polynomials
p
over a 2-nilpotent algebra
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{U}
satisfy
\mathrm{pr}_{U}p(\bar{x})=0
and
\mathrm{pr}_{L}(p(\bar{x}))=\hat{p}(\mathrm{pr}_{U}(\bar{x}))
for some
\hat{p}\colon U^{k}\to L
. Thus
p
can be identified with the map
\hat{p}\colon U^{k}\to L
; composing
p
with polynomials
\Pol(\mathbf{A})
from the inside, and those who preserve
L\times\{0\}
from the outside corresponds to composing
\hat{p}
with polynomials of
\mathbf{U}
from the inside, and polynomials of
\mathbf{L}
from the outside. Thus the closure under such compositions gives rise to a
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{U})
-clonoid.

We are going to show that, for coprime elementary Abelian groups
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{U}
, a
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{U})
-clonoid consists either of only constant functions, or is able to express conjunctions in the following sense:

Proposition 26
.

Let
\mathbf{L}=({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{l},+,0,-)
and
\mathbf{U}=({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{k},+,0,-)
for distinct primes
p\neq q
, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{U})
-clonoid which contains a non-constant operation. Then there exist a hyperplane
H<\mathbf{U}
(i.e. a maximal subgroup), an element
l\in L\setminus\{0\}
, and
n
-ary functions
t_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

•

t_{n}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=l
if
u_{i}\in H
for all
i=1,\ldots,n

•

t_{n}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=0
else.

Furthermore
t_{n}
can be constructed as a linear combination of expressions
t_{1}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\cdot u_{i}+c)
in time
O(p^{n+1})
.

We remark that, for the special case
l=k=1
, this was already shown in
[
AM06
, Lemma 5.2]
. Further
[
IKK18

4.
Absorbing Polynomials

In this section we discuss, based on Theorem
8
, how absorbing polynomials can be used to characterize the higher commutator in nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras
\mathbf{A}
. In particular, we show that in the finite case any block of the congruence
[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}]
can be written as the image of the corresponding blocks of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
under some polynomial
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
.

Lemma 29
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra,
0\in A
,
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
,
\gamma=[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}]
and
L=[0]_{\alpha_{1}}\times[0]_{\alpha_{2}}\times\cdots\times[0]_{\alpha_{k}}
. Then

(1)

\gamma
is generated, as a congruence, by the pairs

R=\{(0,p(\bar{b}))\colon\bar{b}\in L\text{ and }p\in\Pol^{k}(\mathbf{A})\text{ is $0$-absorbing}\}

(1’)

\gamma
is generated, as a congruence, by the pairs

R^{\prime}=\{(0,p(\bar{b}))\colon\bar{b}\in L\text{ and }p\in\Pol^{k}(\mathbf{A})\text{ such that }p|_{L}\text{ is $0$-absorbing}\}

(2)

If
|A|
is finite, then
[0]_{\gamma}=p_{1}(L)+p_{2}(L)+\cdots+p_{m}(L)
, where all
p_{i}
are
k
-ary
0
-absorbing polynomials (and the loop product with respect to
+
is left associated).

Proof.

By Theorem
8

\gamma=[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}]
is generated by the pairs

S=\{(c(\bar{a}),c(\bar{b}))\colon(a_{i},b_{i})\in\alpha_{i}\text{ for all }i,\text{ and }c\in\Pol^{k}(\mathbf{A})\text{ is absorbing at }\bar{a}\}.

It is clear that
R\subseteq S\subseteq\gamma
. For the other inclusion, it is enough to prove that a congruence generated by
R
contains
S
. To see this, let
(c(\bar{a}),c(\bar{b}))\in S
for a polynomial
c
that is absorbing at
\bar{a}
. We then define the polynomial
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=c(x+a_{1},\ldots,x+a_{k})/c(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
, and the tuple
\bar{u}=(b_{1}/a_{1},\ldots,b_{k}/a_{k})\in L
. Note that

is
0
-absorbing, and thus
(0,p(\bar{u}))\in R
. Since
(0,p(\bar{u}))+(c(\bar{a}),c(\bar{a}))=(c(\bar{a}),c(\bar{b}))

, also must contain
(c(\bar{a}),c(\bar{b}))

For (1’) note that
R\subseteq R^{\prime}
, hence the congruence generated by
R^{\prime}
must contain
\gamma
. On the other hand, it is not hard to see that

, by applying the Definition
5
for
\gamma
and any
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, such that
p|_{L}
is
0
-absorbing,

For (2) note that, since
\gamma
is a congruence,
[0]_{\gamma}
is closed under the loop multiplication
x+y
. Therefore, for every list of
k
-ary
0
-absorbing polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
we have
p_{1}(L)+p_{2}(L)+\cdots+p_{m}(L)\subseteq[0]_{\gamma}
. Let

be a maximal set of the form
p_{1}(L)+p_{2}(L)+\cdots+p_{m}(L)
(such a maximal set exists, since

is finite).

We claim that

. For contradiction assume that there is an element
d\in[0]_{\gamma}\setminus N
. By Mal’tsev’s chain lemma (see e.g.
[
IK18
, Lemma 3.1.(1)]
), we know that there is a chain of elements
0=a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},a_{n+1}=d
, such that for every
i=0,\ldots,n
, either
(a_{i},a_{i+1})
or
(a_{i+1},a_{i})
is the image of a pair
(0,p(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}))\in R
under a unary polynomial

. Without loss of generality we can further assume that

(otherwise we replace

If
(a_{n},a_{n+1})=(u(0),u(p(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})))

a_{n}\backslash a_{n+1}=u(0)\backslash u(p(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}))

L

0

q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=u(0)\backslash u(p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))

∎

In the proof of Lemma

5.
Wreath product representations

In this section we discuss some properties of the wreath product representation of nilpotent algebras from Definition

5.1.
The lower central series

By Lemma
29

\alpha\geq[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha]\geq[\mathbf{1}_{A},[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha]]\geq\cdots

in a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra, its congruence blocks can be defined as the image of

and

under a suitable polynomial

. Using the wreath product representation, we can prove the existence of such

with even nicer properties:

Lemma 30
.

For a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra
\mathbf{A}
and
\alpha\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
, let
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\ldots<\alpha_{n}=\alpha
be defined by
\alpha_{i}=[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{i+1}]
and let
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha
the corresponding wreath product representation. Then there is a
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a polynomial

, such that
h(0,\ldots,0)=0
, and for
\bar{x}\in([0]_{\alpha})^{k}
:

•

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}(h(\bar{x}))=0
for

,

•

there is a surjective
\hat{h}\colon L_{n}^{k}\to L_{1}
, such that
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(h(\bar{x}))=\hat{h}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{n}}(\bar{x}))
.

We prove the statement by induction on

. If

,
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
is central, and we simply set

. For an induction step

, by the induction assumption, there is already a polynomial

, such that for
\bar{x}
from
[0]_{\alpha}
:

•

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{2}}(h^{\prime}(\bar{x}))=\hat{h}^{\prime}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{n}}(\bar{x}))
, such that
\hat{h}^{\prime}\colon L_{n}^{k}\to L_{2}
is surjective, and
\hat{h}^{\prime}(0,\ldots,0)=0

•

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}(h^{\prime}(\bar{x}))=0
for every
j>2
.

By Lemma
29
(1),
\alpha_{1}=[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{2}]
is generated by pairs
(0,c(a,x))
, such that

is absorbing,
a\in A
and
x\in[0]_{\alpha_{2}}
. For every such

and

let us define

. With respect to the wreath product representation

this implies that

c_{a}((l_{1},l_{2},0,\ldots,0))=(\hat{c}_{a}(l_{2}),0,\ldots,0),

for some non-constant

(cf. Observation

). As in Lemma

(2), one can prove that every element of

is in the closure of elements

under the loop product

p(\bar{x})=c_{1,a_{1}}(x_{1})+c_{2,a_{2}}(x_{2})+\cdots+c_{r,a_{r}}(x_{r})
that surjectively maps

to
[0]_{\alpha_{1}}

. The composition
h=p(h^{\prime}(\bar{y}_{1}),h^{\prime}(\bar{y}_{2}),\ldots,h^{\prime}(\bar{y}_{s}))

∎

5.2.
Supernilpotent congruences

In this section, we give a characterization of supernilpotent congruences in (elementary) wreath products. We first prove the following lemma:

Lemma 31
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{L}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha_{2}
be a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra, such that
\exp(\mathbf{L}_{1})\neq\exp(\mathbf{L}_{2})
are two distinct primes. Then, either

•

[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{2}]=\mathbf{0}_{A}
, and for every
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
,
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}p
does not depends on
L_{2}
, or

•

\alpha_{2}
is not supernilpotent, and there is a
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, such that
p((l_{1},l_{2},0))=(\hat{p}(l_{2}),0,0)
, for a non-constant
\hat{p}\colon L_{2}\to L_{1}

Proof.

We distinguish two cases. If
[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{2}]=\mathbf{0}_{A}
, then we know by Theorem
18
that there is an affine algebra
\mathbf{L}
, such that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{L}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha_{2}\cong\mathbf{L}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha_{2}
. Without loss of generality, we can identify the universe of
\mathbf{L}
with
L_{1}\times L_{2}
. Note that
\mathbf{L}
is polynomially equivalent to a module, and has
\mathbf{L}_{2}
as a quotient. So (by the fundamental theorem of finitely generated modules)
\mathbf{L}\cong\mathbf{L}_{1}\times\mathbf{L}_{2}
. In particular this implies that there is no polynomial
p\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, such that that
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}p
depends on
L_{2}
.

In the remaining case
\mathbf{0}_{A}<[\mathbf{1}_{A},\alpha_{2}]\leq\alpha_{1}
holds. By Lemma
30
, there is a polynomial
h
such that, for
\bar{x}
from
[0]_{\alpha_{2}}

h(\bar{x})=(\hat{h}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{2}}(\bar{x})),0,0),

and
\hat{h}
is not constant.

If we close
h
under compositions with the loop multiplication
+
(and constants
(0,l_{2},0)
from the inside, respectively constants
(l_{1},0,0)
from the outside), we obtain polynomials
f\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
with

f(\bar{x})=(\hat{f}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{2}}(\bar{x})),0,0),\text{ for }\bar{x}\in L_{1}\times L_{2}\times\{0\},

for every
\hat{f}\colon L_{2}^{n}\to L_{1}
that is in
((L_{1},+,0,-),(L_{2},+,0,-))
-clonoid generated by
\hat{h}
. In particular, by Corollary
28

[0]_{\alpha_{2}}=L_{1}\times L_{2}\times\{0\}
, but not constant. By Lemma
29
(1’),
\alpha_{2}
is not supernilpotent.
∎

We next use Lemma
31
to characterize supernilpotent congruences by their (elementary) wreath product representations. It can be seen as a special case of Theorem
10

Proposition 32
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra, and let
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha
be an elementary wreath product representation. Then
\alpha
is supernilpotent if and only if for every polynomial
f\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
,

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}f
only depends on
L_{j}
if
i\leq j
and
\exp(\mathbf{L}_{i})=\exp(\mathbf{L}_{j})
.

Proof.

We start by proving the ’only if’ direction. For contradiction, assume that there is a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha
, such that
\alpha
is supernilpotent, but there are components
L_{i}
and
L_{j}
of different exponent, and a polynomial
f
such that
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}f
depends on
L_{j}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
i=1
is the only value of
i
, for which this is true; otherwise we take the quotient
\mathbf{A}/\alpha_{i-1}
, for the maximal such
i
.

We can further assume that
j=2
: if this is not the case, let
j
be the minimal index, such that
L_{1}
and
L_{j}
have different exponent, and there is a polynomial
f
such that
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}f
depends on
L_{j}
. Note that, by the minimality of

, for any polynomial

, its projection
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}f
only depends on
L_{m}
with
m<j
, if

has the same exponent as
L_{1}
. Let
I=\{1<m<j\colon\exp(\mathbf{L}_{1})\neq\exp(\mathbf{L}_{m})\}
; then,
\prod_{m\notin I}\mathbf{L}_{i}\times\mathbf{A}/\alpha
is a well-defined nilpotent algebra (cf. Observation
24
), and also a counterexample to the theorem. So, without loss of generality, we can assume that all factors
\mathbf{L}_{m}
for

have the same exponent as
\mathbf{L}_{1}
. Without loss of generality we can further assume

(if this is not the case, we can move
\mathbf{L}_{j}
to the second position; this is possible since, by the uniqueness of

\mathbf{L}_{1}

\mathbf{L}_{j}
). By Lemma

\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{2}
cannot be supernilpotent. Since
\alpha_{2}\leq\alpha
,
\alpha
is not supernilpotent - contradiction.

For the ’if’ direction, note then that by Observation

, for every supernilpotent congruence
\alpha
, and every prime

∎

\mathbf{A}

This follows straightforwardly from refining

We next prove that, for supernilpotent congruences

Lemma 34

(1)

u_{q^{l}}(x)
is bijective on
A

q\neq p

(2)

u_{p^{l}}(x)\approx 0
for all

Proof.

We prove both statements by induction on

(A,+,0,-)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{m}
for some

To show (1) in general, note that by induction hypothesis we know that the projection of

to

only depends on
\prod_{i=2}^{k}L_{i}
(in a bijective manner). Furthermore
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}u_{q^{l}}(x)=(u_{q^{l}}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(x))+\hat{u}_{q^{l}}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{2}\times\cdots\times L_{k}}(x))
, for some map
\hat{u}_{q^{l}}
. Since

, the affine part
u_{q^{l}}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(x))=q^{l}\cdot\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(x)
is a bijective polynomial of
\mathbf{L}_{1}
. Thus

is bijective on all of

.

In order to see (2), note that by the induction hypothesis
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}u_{p^{l-1}}(x)=0

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(u_{p^{l-1}}(x))=\hat{u}_{p^{l-1}}(\mathrm{pr}_{L_{2}\times\cdots\times L_{k}}(x)))

has exponent

, and thus
u_{p^{l}}(x)\approx 0
.
∎

As a direct consequence of Lemma

we obtain:

Lemma 35
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a finite nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra,
\alpha\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
be a supernilpotent congruence. Further let
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\alpha
be an elementary wreath product representation. Then, for every prime
p
, there is a polynomial

, such that for every

:

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}(r_{p}(x))=\begin{cases}\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}(x)&\text{ if }\exp(\mathbf{L}_{j})=p,\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Note that
([0]_{\alpha},+,/,\backslash)=(\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\times\{0/_{\alpha}\},+,/,\backslash)
is a supernilpotent loop. By Proposition
32
it is the direct product of loops of the form
K_{p}=\{x\colon\mathrm{pr}_{L_{i}}(x)=0
if
\exp(\mathbf{L}_{i})\neq p\}
, where
p
runs through all prime exponents
p
of the affine algebras
\mathbf{L}_{i}
. By Lemma
34
the polynomial
u_{q^{n}}
is bijective on
K_{p}
if
q\neq p
, and
0
otherwise.

Let
t_{p}
to be a composition (in any order) of all function

, such that
q
divides
[0]_{\alpha}
and
q\neq p
. Then
t_{p}
is a bijection on
K_{p}
, and

on all other
K_{q}
for

to be a suitable power of

that is the identity on

.
∎

Lemma 36

\mathbf{A}

Let

Let

6.
Proof of Theorem
3

In this section we complete the proof of Theorem
3
. Using Proposition
37
we construct suitable subexponential reductions from 3-SAT, respectively
C
-COLOR, i.e. the problem of coloring graphs with
C
-colors, to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
. This, together with the following two consequences of the exponential time hypothesis (ETH) then implies Theorem
3
.

Theorem 38
.

Assume that the exponential time hypothesis is true. Then

(1)

3
-SAT cannot be solved in time
2^{o(n+m)}
, where
n
is the number of variables, and
m
is the number of clauses in the input formula.

(2)

For any
C>2
,
C
-COLOR cannot be solved in time
2^{o(|V|+|E|)}
, where
V
is the vertex set, and
E
the edge set of an input graph.

We recall that the original definition of the exponential time hypothesis
[
IP01
]
is the statement that there is a
c>0
such that
3
-SAT cannot be solved by any algorithm of complexity
2^{cn+o(n)}
. Statement (1) follows from ETH by the so called Sparsification Lemma in
[
IP01
]
(see e.g. Theorem 14.4 in
[
CFK+15
]
). Statement (2) follows directly from (1) and the existence of linear reductions from
3
-SAT to
C
-COLOR (cf. Theorem 14.6 in
[
CFK+15
]
).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a nilpotent Mal’tsev algebra of Fitting length
k\geq 2
. By Lemma
15
it has an upper Fitting series
\mathbf{0}_{A}=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{k-1}<\lambda_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{A}
of length
k
. Further let
0\in A
be an arbitrary constant.

First, note that there is a congruence
\rho>\lambda_{k-1}
, such that
\mathbf{A}/\lambda_{k-1}=\mathbf{M}\otimes(\mathbf{A}/\rho)
and there is a polynomial
h(\bar{x})\in\Pol(\mathbf{A})
, which maps
A/\lambda_{k-1}
to
[0]_{\rho/\lambda_{k-1}}=M\times\{0\}
. This follows directly from Lemma
30
. Furthermore, we can assume that
\exp(\mathbf{M})=p
for a prime
p
(if this is not the case, we apply Observation
23
to
\mathbf{M}
).

We are first going to prove the theorem for the case in which
p
is an odd prime. Then, the idea is to reduce p-COLOR, i.e. the p-coloring problem for graphs in subexponential time to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
(or
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
respectively). To construct such a reduction, we first need to prove the following claim:

Claim 1:
There is an elementary wreath product representation
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{M}\otimes(\mathbf{A}/\rho)
, a hyperplane
H<(M,+,0,-)
and an element
l\in L_{1}
, such that for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there are circuits
t_{n}(\bar{x})
of arity
n^{k-1}
, and size
2^{O(n)}
such that for
\bar{x}\in([0]_{\rho})^{n^{k-1}}
:

•

\mathrm{pr}_{M}(t_{n}(\bar{x}))=0
,
\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}(t_{n}(\bar{x}))=0
for all
1<j\leq m
and

•

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(t_{n}(\bar{x}))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\exists i:\mathrm{pr}_{M}(x_{i})\in H\\
l\neq 0,&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Proof of Claim 1:

We prove the statement by induction on
k
. If
k=2
, this straightforwardly follows from Proposition
37
. For an induction step
k-1\to k
, note that by the induction hypothesis, there is already a wreath product representation of
\mathbf{A}/\lambda_{1}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{K}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\rho
, an element
a\in K_{1}
and circuits
s_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n^{k-2}})
of size
2^{O(n)}
, such that, when restricted to
\bar{x}\in[0]_{\lambda_{1}}^{n^{k-2}}
:

•

\mathrm{pr}_{K_{j}}(s_{n}(\bar{x}))=0
for all
1<j\leq r
and

•

\mathrm{pr}_{K_{1}}(s_{n}(\bar{x}))=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\exists i:\mathrm{pr}_{M}(x_{i})\in H\\
a&\text{ else}.\end{cases}

Without loss of generality we can assume that the module associated with
\mathbf{K}_{1}
is cyclic, and generated by
a
(otherwise we refine the wreath product accordingly, cf. Observation
23
). Let
\rho_{1}>\lambda_{1}
be the congruence such that
\mathbf{A}/\rho_{1}=\bigotimes_{i=2}^{r}\mathbf{K}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\rho
. By Proposition
37
, there is an elementary wreath product representation
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{j}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{K}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}/\rho
, and there are circuits
f_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
of length
2^{O(n)}
, and a hyperplane
H_{1}<(K_{1},+,0,-)
such that for
\bar{x}\in[0]_{\rho_{1}}^{n}

•

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{j}}(f_{n}(\bar{x}))=0
for all
1<j\leq r
and

•

\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(f_{n}(\bar{x}))=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\exists i:\mathrm{pr}_{L_{1}}(x_{i})\in H_{1}\\
l&\text{ else},\end{cases}

In the case that
a\notin H_{1}
, we simply set

t_{n}=f_{n}(s_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n^{k-2}}),\ldots,s_{n}(x_{n^{k-2}(n-1)+1}\ldots,x_{n^{k-1}})).

By construction

has the desired property with respect to the wreath product representation

and is of size

. In the case that

, note that, since
\mathbf{K}_{1}
is generated by

as a module, there must be an

with

and

. We then use

instead of

. This finishes the proof of Claim 1.∎

We are next going to use the circuits

to reduce
p
-COLOR to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
in subexponential time. For this let

be an undirected graph. We are going to model the vertices

by terms

, where

is the polynomial defined above, and
\bar{x}_{v}
is a tuple of distinct variables (different for every

). By construction of

, the image of

is in

, and the projection of

to

is surjective.

Without loss of generality let us assume that the graph

many edges, for some

(otherwise we count some of the edges multiple times), and that
G
is connected (and hence
|V|\leq|E|+1
). Then, let us consider the polynomial

(where ’

, this polynomial evaluates to

if there is a pair

, such that

and
\mathrm{pr}_{M}(h_{w})
are in the same coset of

and

, otherwise. So

The circuits constructed in this reduction have size

, where

. So

can be reduced to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
(and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
) in sub-exponential time

for some

. If the exponential time hypothesis holds, then by Theorem
38
(2) there is a lower bound of

on the time complexity of

-coloring a connected graph. Thus, our instance of size

of
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
cannot be solved faster than in time

. The same result holds for
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
; this finishes the proof for the case
\exp(\mathbf{M})=p>2
.

In the case
\exp(\mathbf{M})=p=2
, we similarly construct a sub-exponential reduction of 3-SAT to
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
(respectively
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
). Such a reduction can be obtained, if, instead of the circuits

in Claim 1, we use circuits

as in the following claim:

Claim 2:
There is an elementary wreath product representation
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{M}\otimes(\mathbf{A}/\rho)
, a hyperplane

and an element

, such that for every

, there are circuits

of arity

, and size

such that for values of

(which follows from Proposition

by identifying every variable

and every negated variable

, where

. Then, a 3-CNF

. As in the previous case, this gives rise to a subexponential reduction of 3-SAT to

, respectively

. Together with Theorem

for

and

∎

7.
Discussion

We finish by discussing the two main directions for generalizations of Theorem
3
.

As shown in
[
IKKW22
]
,
\PolSAT(\mathbf{G})
for finite groups
\mathbf{G}=(G,\cdot,1,^{-1})
of Fitting length
k
, exhibits the same quasipolynomial lower bounds as
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
for nilpotent algebras of Fitting length
k
(assuming ETH). This begs the question, if the lower bounds in Theorem
3
even hold for
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
. Or, more generally, one may ask if
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
has quasipolynomial lower bounds for all finite
solvable
Mal’tsev algebras, which would subsume both the results of this paper and
[
IKKW22
]
. And, in fact, in the conference paper
[
IKK22b
]
, Idziak, Kawałek and Krzaczkowski proved exactly this. Their proof uses tame congruence theory to construct
polynomials
with properties similar to the
circuits

t_{n}
in our proof of Theorem
3
.

This leads to the question, whether also in our proof of Theorem
3
, it is enough to consider polynomials. An analysis shows that the only point, in which we need to be careful in substitute circuits by polynomials is Proposition
37
; in it we used circuits to efficiently express the iterated products with respect to the loop operation
+
. Thus, if
+
(or the Mal’tsev operation
m(x,y,z)
) is also part of the signature of the nilpotent algebra
\mathbf{A}
, then the lower bounds of Theorem
3
even hold for
\PolSAT(\mathbf{A})
. Also, for general
\mathbf{A}
one can find a workaround by using
+
-terms of depth
\log(n)
to define the “sum” of
n
variables (using balanced trees), and then substitute every
+

IKK22b
]

Secondly, recall that Theorem
3
almost completly finishes the complexity classification of
\CSAT
and
\CEQV
for finite Mal’tsev algebras, assuming ETH and Conjecture
1
. By Corollary
4
, nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras of Fitting length
k\leq 2
are the only algebras from congruence modular varieties, for which
\CSAT(\mathbf{A})
and
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
could be tractable under the exponential time hypothesis. While they are both known to be tractable in the supernilpotent case (
k=1
)
[
Kom18
,
AM10
]
, the classification for
k=2
is still open. There are some tractability results for 2-nilpotent algebras
[
IKK18
,
KKK19
]
, i.e. algebras that are the wreath product of two affine algebras. We further know, that not all algebras of Fitting length 2 (and not even all 2-nilpotent algebras) have tractable
\CSAT
problems: Examples with proper quasipolynomial lower bounds (assuming ETH) were constructed in
[
IKK22a
, Example 7.2.]
. In fact, those examples indicate that polynomial time algorithms for
\CSAT
might only be feasible for algebras
\mathbf{A}
, in which
\gamma_{1}=\bigwedge_{i\geq 1}[\mathbf{1}_{A},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{A}]_{i}
has blocks of prime power cardinality (and direct products of such algebras). On the other hand, it is still consistent with our current knowledge, that all circuit equivalence problems
\CEQV(\mathbf{A})
, for nilpotent Mal’tsev algebras of Fitting length
k=2
are tractable.
