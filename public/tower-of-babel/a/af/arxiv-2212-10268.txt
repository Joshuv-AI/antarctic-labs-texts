fastMI : a fast and consistent copula-based nonparametric estimator of mutual information Journal: Journal of Multivariate Analysis
Soumik Purkayastha, Peter X.-K. Song

Abstract
As a fundamental concept in information theory, mutual information ( M ​ I MI ) has been commonly applied to quantify association between random vectors. Most existing nonparametric estimators of M ​ I MI have unstable statistical performance since they involve parameter tuning. We develop a consistent and powerful estimator, called fastMI , that does not incur any parameter tuning. Based on a copula formulation, fastMI estimates M ​ I MI by leveraging Fast Fourier transform-based estimation of the underlying density. Extensive simulation studies reveal that fastMI outperforms state-of-the-art estimators with improved estimation accuracy and reduced run time for large data sets. fastMI provides a powerful test for independence that exhibits satisfactory type I error control. Anticipating that it will be a powerful tool in estimating mutual information in a broad range of data, we develop an R package fastMI for broader dissemination.

Introduction

Investigating dependence between two random variables is a key issue in statistical science. It is known that classical measures like Pearson’s
r
, Kendall’s
\tau
, and Spearman’s
\rho

[
9
]
, although widely used in practice,
are incapable of capturing a non-linear, non-monotonic association, which cannot cannot be properly estimated using ranks and their monotonic transformations. To address this practical need, more sophisticated measures, like the distance correlation (dCor)
[
32
]
, the Heller-Heller-Gorfine (HHG)
[
13
]
statistic, and the maximal information coefficient (MIC)
[
25
]
have been introduced to study more complex association patterns such as the dependence of two random vectors.

Among many non-linear association metrics, mutual information (
MI
)
[
5
]
has recently re-emerged in the statistics and machine learning literature with many exciting applications
[
35
]
. Originally introduced in a communication theory context
[
28
]
,
MI
presents a remarkably general and intuitive measure of dependence. Its widespread use in practice is due largely to the self-equitability
[
16
]
of
MI
- the ability to characterize dependency strength for both linear and non-linear relationships.

For continuous data, which shall be the focus of this paper, there are three distinct
MI
estimation approaches. The first approach implements a binning method to group continuous data into different bins and estimates
MI
from the binned data
[
30
,
23
]
. The success of this simple method depends heavily on proper specification of both number and position of said bins. Another approach is based on a k-nearest neighbors (kNN) estimation method, utilized by the Kraskov-Stögbauer-Grassberger (KSG) estimator
[
17
]
. As is the case with all kNN-based methods, the KSG estimator greatly depends on properly specifying the number of neighbors. The third approach is based on estimates of probability density functions (PDFs), using histograms, kernel density estimation (KDE)
[
21
]
, B-splines
[
8
]
, or wavelets
[
24
]
. This nonparametric approach typically relies on a tuning parameter (e.g. bandwidth) that needs to be specified in the estimation routine.

Although the approaches mentioned above demonstrate good properties
[
23
,
17
,
21
,
8
,
24
]
, they are all sensitive to proper specification of tuning parameter(s) in a chosen smoothing technique. As a consequence, the resulting estimators may be numerically unstable and/or suffer from serious bias. A recent study
[
39
]
presents a tuning-free KDE approach to estimate
MI
in which the bandwidth parameter is automatically set to maximize the jackknifed version of
MI
(henceforth referred to as
JMI
). Not only does this method exhibit better estimation efficiency than other existing
MI
estimation approaches but it also provides a stable hypothesis test for independence that is shown to be more powerful than its competitors such as the dCor, HHG, or MIC. According to existing literature,
JMI
serves as the current gold standard for estimating
MI
as well as is the choice of test for independence
[
39
]
.

Along the line of tuning-free estimation, an improvement on
JMI
is proposed in this paper. This new estimator is motivated by the self-consistent density estimator proposed by
[
2
,
22
]
to minimize the mean integrated squared error (MISE) between the estimated density and the true density without incurring any manual parameter tuning. The estimation process relies on fast Fourier transforms (FFT).

Furthermore, utilizing this ‘optimal’ density estimator, we propose a plug-in estimator of
MI
, termed as the
fastMI
, which is shown to be consistent and manyfold faster than the original
JMI
for large data sets. This proposed
fastMI
automatically determines the bandwidth by minimizing the MISE objective function in a data-adaptive way.

Simulation studies comparing estimation accuracy reveal
fastMI
outperforms the current gold standard
JMI
estimator. Through extensive numerical experiments,
fastMI
demonstrates improved estimation efficiency, higher empirical power when testing for independence, and reduced computation time. All these lead to a recommendation of our new methodology to practitioners.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we define
MI
and underline a key connection of
MI
with copula. In Section
3
we describe the self-consistent density estimation method in detail. In Section
4
we provide theoretical guarantees on the asymptotic behaviour of the
fastMI
estimator. We present extensive simulation studies in Section
5
to highlight the strengths and advantages of using
fastMI
over the existing state-of-the-art estimator, the
JMI
estimator. Further, we benchmark our findings by means of an empirical copula estimator-based
MI
as well. Finally, we present an application of
fastMI
to real data in Section
6
before summarizing our findings in the concluding Section
7
. All heavy technical details are included in Section
8
.

Methods

To formalize the problem, suppose that
d\in\mathbb{N}
can be written as
d=p+q
for some
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, that
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are random vectors taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, respectively, and that
\boldsymbol{Z}=(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top})^{\top}
has density
f_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We write
f_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
for the marginal Lebesgue densities of the random vectors
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
, respectively. Given independent and identically distributed copies
\left\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}_{n}\right\}
of
\boldsymbol{Z}
, one of our primary tasks is to test the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that the
vectors

\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are independent against the alternative that
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are not independent.

2.1
Mutual information and its copula-based formulation

In order to test the independence of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
we consider mutual information
MI(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})
, defined by

MI(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{XY}}\left[\ln\left\{\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{XY}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})}{f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{X})f_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{Y})}\right\}\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
denotes expectation under the joint density
f_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
. It is known that
MI
is a valid measure of association; that is, it is equal to zero if and only if
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are independent and positive otherwise. Larger values of
MI
indicate a stronger association.
MI
is invariant under monotonic transformations, which is an important property allowing various rank-based transformations. Further,
MI
satisfies the self-equitability condition
[
39
]
, implying that it detects associations without a bias for specific association patterns, unlike the
MIC

[
16
]
.

Interestingly,
MI
may be rewritten as a function of copulas, a class of dependence models
[
15
]
. Note that by using Sklar’s theorem
[
7
]
, we can apply monotonic marginal transformations to reduce the technical complexity by using uniform transformations
\boldsymbol{U_{X}}=\left(F_{X_{1}}(X_{1}),\ldots,F_{X_{p}}(X_{p})\right)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}=\left(F_{Y_{1}}(Y_{1}),\ldots,F_{Y_{q}}(Y_{q})\right)^{\top}
, where
F_{X_{i}}
and
F_{Y_{j}}
are the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
, respectively, for
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq q
. We use
c_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
c_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
c_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
to denote the copula density functions of
(\boldsymbol{U_{X}}^{\top},\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}^{\top})^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{U_{X}}
, and
\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}
respectively. Using this marginal uniform transformation trick, we arrive at an alternative copula-based formulation of
MI
:

\displaystyle MI(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle=MI(\boldsymbol{U_{X}},\boldsymbol{U_{Y}})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{p}}\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\ln\left\{\frac{c_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{u_{X}},\boldsymbol{u_{Y}})}{c_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}(\boldsymbol{u_{X}})c_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{u_{Y}})}\right\}c_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{u_{X}},\boldsymbol{u_{Y}})d\boldsymbol{u_{X}}d\boldsymbol{u_{Y}}.

(1)

MI
in Equation
2.1
may be estimated by plug-in copula density estimators.
The marginal uniform transformation trick allows us to consider ranks instead of raw data, making copula-based estimation methods robust to any marginal irregularity, in contrast with methods which use raw data to estimate
MI
, such as the kNN-based KSG estimator. Recognizing
MI
as an integral in Equation
2.1
, we may invoke data generative methods, including classical Monte Carlo methods
[
26
]
for estimation purposes.
The estimated copula density functions
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
are used to obtain an estimate of the underlying
MI
. Using a fast Fourier transform-based density estimation technique
[
2
,
22
]
, which is described in greater detail in Section
2.2
and Section
3
, we obtain said copula density estimates. This estimation routine will be utilized in a powerful
MI
-based hypothesis test for independence of
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
.

2.2
Improved nonparametric estimation of copula density using probit transformation

From Equation
2.1
we note the need to estimate three multivariate copula density functions, namely
{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
. Here we describe the nonparametric KDE of a generic
d
-variate copula density function for vector
\boldsymbol{U}=\left(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\right)^{\top}
on unit hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
. The naive KDE
[
29
]
, is not suitable for unit hypercube-supported copula densities, mainly because it is known to be heavily affected by boundary bias issues. Most kernel estimators, for instance, have problems with such bounded support because, for points close to the boundaries, they typically place some positive mass outside of the support.
In addition, kernel estimators are not consistent for unbounded densities
[
10
]
. This is undesirable since many copula families of interest admit unbounded densities. For example, in the bivariate Gaussian copula case with moderate correlation, the copula density is unbounded in two corners of
[0,1]^{2}
. Therefore, any good estimator
\hat{c}
should be able to cope with such unboundedness. To facilitate this, a variable transformation approach in the kernel estimation of copula densities was proposed
[
20
,
10
]
, which we describe below.

We define a vector of normal scores
\boldsymbol{V}=\left(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d}\right)^{\top}
where
V_{i}=\Phi^{-1}(U_{i}),1\leq i\leq d,
with
\Phi
denoting the standard normal CDF and
\Phi^{-1}
denoting its quantile (probit) function. Given that each
U_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, we have each
V_{i}
distributed as a standard normal variable, although this does not force the joint distribution of
\boldsymbol{V}
to be multivariate normal. Then, the joint density function of
\boldsymbol{V}
, given by
g
, may be expressed as follows:

c(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=\frac{g\left\{\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(u_{d})\right\}}{\phi\left\{\Phi^{-1}(u_{1})\right\}\ldots\phi\left\{\Phi^{-1}(u_{d})\right\}},\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in[0,1]^{d}.

The motivation for this probit transformation is as follows: if
c(\boldsymbol{u})>0
Lebesgue-a.e. over
[0,1]^{d}
,
\boldsymbol{V}
has unconstrained support over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and estimating its density
g
no longer suffers from boundary issues. In addition, due to its normal margins, one may expect
g
to be smooth and well-behaved, and its estimation becomes relatively easy and accurate. It is clear that any estimator
\hat{g}
of
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
will produce a corresponding estimator of the copula density on
(0,1)^{d}
:

\hat{c}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=\frac{\hat{g}\left\{\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(u_{d})\right\}}{\phi\left\{\Phi^{-1}(u_{1})\right\}\ldots\phi\left\{\Phi^{-1}(u_{d})\right\}},\quad\boldsymbol{u}\in(0,1)^{d}.

(2)

Further, when necessary,
\hat{c}
can also be defined at the boundaries of
[0,1]^{d}
by continuity
[
10
]
. This probit transformation trick confers many advantages to the estimator
\hat{c}
, including (i)
\hat{g}
, being an unconstrained density estimator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
does not suffer from boundary bias issues and does not allocate any probability to
\hat{c}
outside
[0,1]^{d}
; (ii) if
\hat{g}
is a
bona fide
density estimator, i.e.,
\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{v})\geq 0

\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{v})d\boldsymbol{v}=1
, then by change of variable
U_{i}=\Phi(V_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq d
we have
\hat{c}(\boldsymbol{u})\geq 0

\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in[0,1]^{d}
and
\int_{{[0,1]}^{d}}\hat{c}(\boldsymbol{u})d\boldsymbol{u}=1
; (iii) if
\hat{g}
is a uniformly (weak or strong) consistent estimator for
g
, we note that the corresponding
\hat{c}
inherits the same behavior on any compact subset of
[0,1]^{d}
.

The key challenge now lies in obtaining a ‘good’ PDF estimator
\hat{g}
. Kernel density estimators (KDEs) are commonly used for estimating PDFs. A well-known technical challenge in the KDE method is to determine a kind of optimal bandwidth
H
in addition to a specifically chosen kernel density
[
39
]
. Albeit a vast literature exists on this issue of bandwidth tuning, selecting optimal
H
remains case-dependent and computationally burdensome, and oftentimes this task involves a manual user intervention
[
29
]
. A review of automatic selection methods
[
12
]
recommends a variety of different approaches that are dependent on data set characteristics (including sample size, smoothness, and skewness) and thus are hard to implement properly in practice. In Section
3
, we consider an alternative approach to density estimation that relies on fast Fourier transforms.

In summary, using the probit-transformation trick and an FFT-based density estimation method, we are able to obtain the estimated copula density functions
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
via Equation
2
. These estimated densities are used to compute our estimator
fastMI
for
MI
as described in Section
2.3
.

2.3
fastMI
: fast nonparametric estimation of MI

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}=\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{\top}
, with
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\left(X_{1i},\ldots,X_{pi}\right)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}=\left(Y_{1i},\ldots,Y_{qi}\right)^{\top}
for
i\leq i\leq n
be a random sample drawn from a
d
-variate distribution
f_{\boldsymbol{XY}}
. As a preliminary processing step, we define the vectors of empirical probability integral transforms
\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{X}i}=\left(\hat{F}_{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}}(X_{1i}),\ldots,\hat{F}_{\boldsymbol{X}_{p}}(X_{pi})\right)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{Y}i}=\left(\hat{F}_{\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}}(Y_{1i}),\ldots,\hat{F}_{\boldsymbol{Y}_{q}}(Y_{qi})\right)^{\top}
. Here,
\hat{F}_{X_{i}}
and
\hat{F}_{Y_{j}}
are the empirical CDFs of
X_{i}
and
Y_{j}
respectively for
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq q
. Next, we invoke the probit transformation as described in Section
2.2
to obtain the estimated copula density functions:
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
, as described by Equation
2
. These estimated densities are used to compute our plug-in estimator
fastMI
estimator using Equation
2.1
. Consequently,
fastMI
is given by

\widehat{MI}_{fast}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\left\{\frac{\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{X}i},\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{Y}i})}{\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{X}i})\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{Y}i})}\right\}.

(3)

Note that we use
fastMI
and
\widehat{MI}_{fast}
interchangeably in this article. In Section
3
we discuss a data-driven fast Fourier transform-based density estimation technique that yields consistent and fast estimates of the underlying density function without being encumbered by bandwidth selection issues.

3
Self-consistent density estimation via fast Fourier transforms

A density estimation technique that has no need for user-selected parameter tuning was introduced by
[
2
]
for univariate continuous random variables and later extended to higher dimensions by
[
22
]
. The density estimator called the self-consistent (SC) estimator, was shown to converge almost surely to the true underlying density for the univariate case. In this section, we present the derivation of the SC estimator for the multivariate case and present proof of almost sure consistency of the SC estimator to the true
d-
dimensional density under mild assumptions. To our knowledge, while proof of consistency for the SC estimator exists for the univariate case, this is the first attempt at proving the consistency of the estimator for the multivariate case.

The SC estimator has desirable large-sample properties in addition to enjoying greatly improved computations speeds. Using the SC estimator and Equation
2
, we obtain the estimated copula density functions
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
, which are then used to compute
fastMI
using Equation
3
.

3.1
Formulation

Let
\mathcal{S}=\left\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},j=1,\ldots,n\right\}
denote a random sample drawn from from PDF
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
. Assume that the true PDF
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
belongs to the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions, namely
\mathcal{L}^{2}=\left\{g:\int g^{2}(\boldsymbol{z})d\boldsymbol{z}<\infty\right\}
. We propose to estimate
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
by the following convolution of an arbitrary kernel
K
and the set of delta functions centered on the dataset:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle:={n}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{j})\quad\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(4)

\displaystyle={n}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(\boldsymbol{t})\delta(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{j}-\boldsymbol{t})d\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle={n}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(K\ast\delta\right)(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{j}),

where
\delta(\boldsymbol{z})
is the Dirac delta function
[
18
]
and
f\ast g
denotes the convolution of two functions
f
and
g
:
(f\ast g)(\boldsymbol{t}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{\tau})g(\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{\tau})d\boldsymbol{\tau}.
Here
K
is chosen such that the resulting estimator
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

Denote the space of candidate kernel functions by

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{K:K(\boldsymbol{z})\geq 0;K(\boldsymbol{z})=K(-\boldsymbol{z});\int K(\boldsymbol{z})d\boldsymbol{z}=1\right\}.

Given a random sample
\mathcal{S}
, our aim is to identify an optimal kernel
\hat{K}\in\mathcal{K}
, that achieves the minimal mean integrated square error (MISE) between the true density
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
and an estimator
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{K}

\displaystyle:=\underset{{K}\in\mathcal{K}}{\text{argmin}}\ \text{MISE}(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}},{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}})

(5)

\displaystyle=\underset{{K}\in\mathcal{K}}{\text{argmin}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\{\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z})-{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z})\}^{2}d\boldsymbol{z}\right],

where the
\mathbb{E}
operator denotes expectation taken over the entire support of
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
.

To perform minimization in Equation
5
, we follow a procedure for signal deconvolution
[
38
]
via Fourier transforms. First, consider the Fourier transform of the true density
f_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
, namely the characteristic function (CF) given by

\phi(\boldsymbol{t}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z})\exp(i\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z})d\boldsymbol{z},\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then, using the Fourier convolution theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{K(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{j})\}(\boldsymbol{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\{K(\boldsymbol{z})\ast\delta(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{Z}_{j})\}(\boldsymbol{t})

(6)

\displaystyle=\kappa(\boldsymbol{t})\exp(i\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{j}),\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform operator and
\kappa(\boldsymbol{t}):=\mathcal{F}\left\{K(\boldsymbol{z})\right\}(\boldsymbol{t})
denotes the Fourier transform of the kernel
K
. From Equation
6
and the linearity of Fourier transforms in Equation
4
, we obtain
\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})
, the Fourier transform of the estimate
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
, as follows:

\begin{aligned} \hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})&=\mathcal{F}\left\{\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z})\right\}(\boldsymbol{t})\\
&=\kappa(\boldsymbol{t})\ n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(i\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{j})\\
&=\kappa(\boldsymbol{t})\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\end{aligned},\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(7)

where
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(i\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{j})
is the empirical characteristic function (ECF). Since the data
\left\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}_{n}\right\}
are i.i.d., it is easy to see that the ECF is an unbiased estimator of the corresponding CF, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\right\}={\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})
. It can also be shown that
\mathbb{E}\ \lvert\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})-{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}=\left(1-\lvert{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}\right)/n
. We refer the reader to
[
34
, Chapter 3]
for more details on the ECF and its properties. It follows that
\mathbb{E}\left\{\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\right\}=\kappa(\boldsymbol{t}){\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})
for
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The MISE in Equation
5
corresponds to the mean-square distance between the true density
f_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
and the estimate
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
, in terms of the Euclidean metric in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}^{2}
. We rewrite Equation
5
in Fourier space using Parseval’s theorem as follows:

\displaystyle\text{MISE}(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}},{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}})=(2\pi)^{-d}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})-{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}d\boldsymbol{t}\right].

(8)

Since
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z}),{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t}),\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
, we may interchange the expectation and integral operations
[
3
]
and rewrite the MISE in Equation
8
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{MISE}(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}},{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\mathbb{E}\lvert\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})-{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}d\boldsymbol{t}\right]

(9)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\mathbb{E}\lvert\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})-\mathbb{E}\left\{\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\right\}+\mathbb{E}\left\{\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\right\}-{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}d\boldsymbol{t}\right]

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[n^{-1}\lvert\kappa(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}\left\{1-\lvert{\phi(\boldsymbol{t})}\rvert^{2}\right\}+\lvert{\phi(\boldsymbol{t})}\rvert^{2}\lvert 1-\kappa(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}\right]d\boldsymbol{t}.

Since the integrand in the last line of Equation
9
is quadratic in
\kappa
it is straightforward to find the optimal Fourier-transformed kernel
{\kappa}_{OPT}
that minimizes MISE, by equating the functional derivative of MISE with respect to
\kappa
to zero and solving for
{\kappa}_{OPT}
, given by

\displaystyle{\kappa}_{OPT}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{n}{n-1+\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{-2}},\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(10)

Equation
10
reveals that a unique ‘optimal’ Fourier transformed kernel can be derived as a function of the power spectrum of the (unknown) density that is to be estimated. Although this finding was first reported in
[
37
]
, the result alone is unfortunately of little use since the power spectrum
\phi
of the true density
f_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
is not known. We follow the suggestions in
[
2
]
and take a step further to plug in to Equation
7
, the transformed kernel
\kappa_{OPT}
obtained in Equation
10
to write the Fourier transform
\hat{\phi}
of the density estimate
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{t})=\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t}){\kappa}_{OPT}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{n\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})}{n-1+\lvert\phi(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{-2}},\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(11)

Then, an iterative procedure proposed by
[
2
,
22
]
may be applied to determine the exact fixed point of Equation
11
, with the details described in Section
3.2
.

3.2
Multivariate self-consistent estimation

An iterative procedure is defined by the following sequence of estimates, begun with an initial guess
\tilde{\phi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{t})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{n\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})}{n-1+\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{-2}},\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(12)

The convergent point from Equation
12
yields an estimate
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})
which satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{n\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})}{n-1+\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{-2}},\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(13)

The existence of
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})
is warranted as Equation
13
has two fixed and unique non-null solutions. We show that Equation
13
has only one stable non-null solution. Noting that
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}
is complex-valued, we take the absolute value of Equation
13
. When the null solution
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}=0
is removed, we obtain a simple quadratic equation:

\displaystyle\left(n-1\right)\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert^{2}=n\lvert\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert.

Provided
\lvert\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert\geq 4(n-1)/n^{2}
, the above equation has two solutions denoted by the superscripted
\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})^{\pm}\rvert
,

\displaystyle\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})^{\pm}\rvert=\frac{n\lvert\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert}{2(n-1)}\left[1\pm\sqrt{\left\{1-\frac{4(n-1)}{n^{2}|\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}}\right\}}\right].

When substituted in Equation
13
, they return the solution for
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})^{\pm}
of the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})^{\pm}=\frac{n\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})}{2(n-1)}\left[1\pm\sqrt{\left\{1-\frac{4(n-1)}{n^{2}|\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}}\right\}}\right],\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\cap\left\{\boldsymbol{t}:\lvert\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert\geq 4(n-1)/n^{2}\right\}.

Since these two solutions only depend on the ECF
\mathcal{C}
, they may be used to estimate
\phi
. Below, we identify a stable solution to be used in Equation
11
.

Whereas
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{+}
is normalized,
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{-}
is not; that is,
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{0})=1
implies
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{+}(\boldsymbol{0})=1
, which is desirable; in contrast
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{-}(\boldsymbol{0})=1/(n-1)
, which is undesirable. Furthermore, we compute the derivative

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{n+1}\rvert}{\mathrm{d}\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{n}\rvert}\right|_{\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{n}\rvert=\lvert\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{\pm}\rvert}=1\mp\sqrt{\left\{1-\frac{4(n-1)}{n^{2}|\mathcal{C}(t)|^{2}}\right\}}.

Thus, under
\lvert\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\rvert\geq 4(n-1)/n^{2}
,
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{+}
has a derivative smaller than one, indicating a stable equilibrium point, whereas
\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{-}
has a derivative larger than one, indicating instability. In summary, we choose
\hat{\phi}_{SC}=\tilde{\phi}_{SC}^{+}
as the solution to Equation
11
in the construction of the estimate. That is,

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})=\frac{n\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})}{2(n-1)}\left[1-\sqrt{\left\{1-\frac{4(n-1)}{n^{2}|\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}}\right\}}\right]\mathbb{I}_{A_{n}}(\boldsymbol{t}),\quad\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(14)

where
A_{n}
serves as a low-pass filter that ensures the stability of the estimation process. Since
\hat{\phi}_{SC}
and
A_{n}
are bounded (see remarks below), Equation
14
can be antitransformed back to the estimate in real space, given as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{x})=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{\phi}_{SC}(\boldsymbol{t})\exp(-i\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{t}.

(15)

Remark 1:
The purpose of the filter
\mathbb{I}_{A_{n}}(\boldsymbol{t})
is to define a Fourier-based low-pass filter on the ECF
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})
that yields a stable optimal estimate in the minimum MISE sense. Primarily, the set
A_{n}
is specified such that:

A_{n}=\left\{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left|\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})\right|^{2}\geq\mathcal{C}_{\text{min}}^{2}=\frac{4({n}-1)}{{n}^{2}}\right\}.

(16)

Here, the primary filter is necessary for the stability of the iteration method, since the lower bound
C_{\text{min}}
can ensure a well-defined square root term in Equation
14
. Moreover, according to
[
2
]
, the set
A_{n}
may exclude an additional small subset of frequencies to produce a smoother density estimate
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
. In order for
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
to converge to the true density
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
as
{n}
increases, we require that this set of additionally excluded frequencies must shrink, so that the set
A_{n}
of included frequencies grows with increasing
{n}
.

Remark 2:
According to
[
22
]
, the multivariate ECF
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{t})
consists of a finite set of contiguous hypervolumes denoted by
\left\{HV_{1}^{n},\ldots,HV_{k_{n}}^{n}\right\}
where
k_{n}
is a finite integer. Each hypervolume permits ‘above-threshold’ frequency values
\boldsymbol{t}
for which the constraint in Equation
16
holds. Note that at least one such contiguous hypervolume containing
\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{0}
is guaranteed to exist since
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{0})=1
due to normalisation and the primary filter
A_{n}
has a lower bound
\mathcal{C}_{\text{min}}\leq 1
. Following the suggestion by
[
22
]
we employ the
lowest contiguous hypervolume
filter, choosing the only hypervolume centered at
\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{0}
, which we denote as
HV_{1}^{n}
for notational convenience. We make the following observations about
HV_{1}^{n}
:

1.

The set of frequencies included in the lowest contiguous hypervolume filter is bounded above since the frequencies will always be contained within a finite-sized hypervolume around the origin.

2.

The volume of the lowest contiguous hypervolume filter grows as the number of data points
{n}
increases, implying more frequencies are included for larger sample sizes.

The resulting filter satisfies the convergence conditions described by
[
2
]
. Hence, we set
A_{n}=HV_{1}^{n}
, and study convergence of
\hat{f}_{SC}
to the true
{f}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
as
{n}
increases. For notational convenience, let
\bar{A}_{n}
denote the complement set of
A_{n}
and
\mathcal{V}(A_{n})
denote the volume of
A_{n}
.

When compared to the classical kernel estimation approach
[
29
]
that assumes a specific form of the kernel with a need of tuning bandwidth, the advantage of the above SCE lies in making minimal assumptions on the functional form of
K
, while determining the estimate
\hat{\phi}_{SC}
as a function of the ECF
\mathcal{C}
, along with a well-specified low-pass frequency filter
A_{n}
entirely by a data-driven approach. The proposed optimization via Equation
11
enjoys computational efficiency when ‘finding’ the functional form of the optimal kernel.

4
Theoretical guarantees

4.1
Large sample properties

We now present key large-sample properties for the SCE estimator and the subsequent plug-in estimator of
MI
, beginning with Theorem
1
that establishes strong consistency of the SCE estimator
\hat{f}_{SC}
at all points on the support of
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let the true density
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
be square integrable and its corresponding Fourier transform
\phi
be integrable, then the self consistent estimator
\hat{f}_{SC}
, which is defined by Equation
15
, converges almost surely to
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, under the additional assumptions
\mathcal{V}(A_{n})\rightarrow\infty
,
\mathcal{V}(A_{n})/\sqrt{{n}}\rightarrow 0
and
\mathcal{V}(\bar{A}_{n})\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Further, assuming
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
to be continuous on support
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have uniform almost sure convergence of
\hat{f}_{SC}
to
{f}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section
8.1
. Note that
\hat{f}_{SC}
can be used in conjunction with the probit transformation trick described in Section
2.2
in order to obtain uniformly strong consistent estimators for the copula density functions
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
and subsequently obtain the
fastMI
estimator described by Equation
3
.
fastMI
is shown to be consistent, as established by Theorem
2
below.

Theorem 2
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. Further, we assume the true underlying copula functions
{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
,
{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}}
, and
{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}
are bounded away from zero and infinity on their support. Then, the estimator
fastMI
given by Equation
3
converges in probability to the true
MI
given in Equation
2.1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
8.2
.

4.2
Test for independence

On the basis of Theorem
2
, using the estimator
\widehat{MI}_{fast}
, we propose a permutation-based test for independence, i.e., a test for
H_{0}:MI=0
against the alternative
H_{a}:MI>0
.

Typically, rejection rules of a test based on large-sample theory require data with large sample sizes, which may not be always available in practice. Leveraging the fast computational speed of our SC estimation method, in this paper we consider a hypothesis testing method that is deemed stable and reliable. Thus, we implement a permutation-based test as it is known to give a precise finite-sample distribution of the test statistic for even small samples
[
19
]
. For a random sample of
n
observations,
S=\left\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{Y}_{n})\right\}
, let
\{\delta(1),,\ldots,\delta(n)\}
be a random permutation of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Based on
\delta
-permuted data set
S_{\delta}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\delta(1)}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\delta(n)})\right\}
, we calculate the corresponding estimate
\widehat{MI}_{fast}^{\delta}
. On repeating the above procedure
r
times,
\mathcal{T}_{perm}=\left\{\widehat{MI}^{\delta_{1}}_{fast},\ldots,\widehat{MI}^{\delta_{r}}_{fast}\right\}
, a collection of estimates is obtained, which may be used to approximate the null distribution of
\widehat{MI}_{fast}
, under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are independent. At significance level
\alpha
, we reject the null hypothesis when
\widehat{MI}_{fast}
based on the original data is greater than the
(1-\alpha)
th empirical quantile of
\mathcal{T}_{perm}
.

5
Simulation Experiments

Previous replicable studies
[
39
]
have compared the performance of the
JMI
-based test with other popular methods, including dCor, HHG and MIC-based tests, and concluded that
JMI
appears to be the most stable test for independence.
Further, as a benchmark we compare the performance of our
fastMI
estimator with the empirical copula-based estimator of
MI
(named
ECMI
), in which a naive bandwidth-driven kernel density estimation route is taken when estimating the copula functions described in Equation
2.1
.

In this section, our proposed nonparametric estimator is compared with the
ECMI
and
JMI
from three distinct but inter-related perspectives: (i) estimation accuracy, (ii) ability to test for independence, and (iii) computation time. We design and implement exhaustive simulation-based experiments for our study. In brief: relative to the
ECMI
as well as the
JMI
, the
fastMI
is the more accurate estimator with reduced estimation error and is able to conduct a hypothesis test for independence with a higher power while exhibiting satisfactory type I error control. Reduced computation times further indicate it is more amenable to applications in large data sets when compared to
ECMI
and
JMI
.

Real Data Analysis

To illustrate an application of our method in practice, we re-analyse the dependence between
X
= ‘death rate’ and
Y
= ‘birth rate’ in 229 countries and territories around the world in the first trimester of 2020
[
11
]
. This dependence was previously found in
[
11
]
to be complex with a departure from linearity and monotonicity.
(X,Y)
denote the number of deaths and births per year per 1000 individuals in the country. Figure
5
presents a scatterplot of the data
(X_{i},Y_{i})
denoting the number of deaths and births per year per 1000 individuals in the
i
th country, indicated by one point on the plot. It demonstrates an interesting ‘C’ shape. For ease of exposition, we stratified countries by the continent they belong to, marked by different symbols in the figure. Note that we clubbed North American and South American countries together in Figure
5

A closer investigation by
[
11
]
reveals the presence of two possible and opposite relationship patterns. First, it shows an ‘decreasing’ trend from ‘moderate’ birth rate to ‘low’ birth rate as death rate increases, for the industrialized countries (mostly Europe, Oceania and the Americas). Second, an ‘increasing’ trend is present from ‘moderate’ birth rate to ‘high’ birth rate as death rate increases, mostly for African nations. The downward trend is more pronounced, while the increasing trend is more diffused.

The strength of such nonlinear dependence and non-monotonic patterns is hardly captured by Pearson’s correlation whose estimate is
\hat{r}=-0.125
, implying an insignificant association with

-value equal to
0.97

We further note the disparity between estimated values of

estimators, respectively. Based on our knowledge learned from the simulation studies in Section

, namely
fastMI
-based estimate having reduced estimation error, it is rational to deduce that the
JMI
method overestimated the strength of association between death rate and birth rate from the data, when compared with our
fastMI
method.
This may lead to erroneous inference in other applications.
This example illustrates the advantage of
fastMI
as being a useful and meaningful measure of dependence to capture complex nonlinear relationships.

7
Concluding Remarks

This paper develops a fast and consistent
MI
estimator through a powerful nonparametric copula estimation approach. Through extensive simulation studies, we have demonstrated that the proposed nonparametric method has several desirable properties, outperforming the current gold standard, the
JMI
as well as the benchmark
ECMI
. Since the proposed method is nonparametric, it does not assume any parametric copula models and works under minimal model assumptions about relationships. An advantage of the
MI
-based approach is that the quantification of dependence is little dependent on marginal distributions, in which by virtue of its focus on the copula, the proposed method only takes into account the intrinsic association between variables without suffering from potential irregularities in the marginal distributions. An appealing technical advance pertains to that we overcome issues that plague bandwidth-based
MI
estimators, such as bandwidth selection and slow computation times for large data sets.
fastMI
relies on a data-adaptive Fast Fourier transformation-based approach to nonparametrically estimating the underlying copula structures. Its run time is many-fold faster than both
ECMI
as well as
JMI
for large data sets.
fastMI
exhibits reduced estimation error and provides a more powerful test of independence than both
ECMI
and
JMI
.

While typically used to study pairwise association,
MI
may be extended in a multivariate setting. However, as evidenced by the discussion below,
MI
may lose some of its original interpretability, which affects its legitimate use in testing for independence. Let consider a
d
-dimensional random vector
\boldsymbol{X}=\left(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\right)^{\top}
with joint density function
f_{\boldsymbol{X}}
with marginal density functions
\left\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\right\}
. It follows from Sklar’s theorem that there exists a unique associated copula density given by

c\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\right)=\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{X}}\left(F_{1}^{-1}\left(u_{1}\right),\ldots,F_{d}^{-1}\left(u_{d}\right)\right)}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}\left(F_{i}^{-1}\left(u_{i}\right)\right)}.

Here copula density
c(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})
can in turn, be used to define
MI(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
, known as the multivariate mutual information (
MMI
). Other names of
MMI
include total correlation
[
36
]
or multi-information
[
31
]
. Basically,
MMI
quantifies the amount of information shared among the different random variables, which characterizes the relatedness of the random variables in a group.
[
33
]
proved the possible negativity of
MMI
when
d\geq 3
. Thus,
MMI
loses one key property of non-negativity, which makes
MMI
illegitimate to quantify the size of information (which is believed to be non-negative).
MMI
is deemed undesirable for its role as a metric of information volume.

Nevertheless, mathematically,
MMI
may still be used as a tool to test for independence. From Theorem 2 of
[
1
]
, we know that
d
variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
are mutually independent if and only if all
MMI
s of
2^{d}-d-1
sub-vectors vanish, i.e.,
MI(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}})=0
with
d\geq k\geq 2
, where the indices
\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\right\}
are a subset of size
k
of
\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}
. This would lead to an unduly tedious testing procedure if
MMI
s were used to investigate independence. Some alternative forms of MI are worth future exploration with no sacrifice of non-negativity and avoidance of exhaustive tests.

In conclusion, due to its adaptability to complex associations and robustness to sample size,
fastMI
may be potentially applicable in a wide range of practical problems where complex non-linearities
[
27
]
are of particular interest. To encourage use of our proposed toolkit for estimation and testing purposes, we developed an
R
package
fastMI
that is publicly available on
Github
.

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left\{\hat{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{X}i},\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{Y}i})\right\}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left\{{c}_{\boldsymbol{U_{X}}\boldsymbol{U_{Y}}}(\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{X}i},\boldsymbol{U}_{\boldsymbol{Y}i})\right\}\right|+

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\ast},
