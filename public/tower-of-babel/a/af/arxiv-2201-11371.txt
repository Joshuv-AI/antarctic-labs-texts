Introduction to Part I

This is a first step guide to
the theory of cluster algebras.

Cluster algebra theory was founded by the series of
papers entitled as “Cluster Algebras I–IV” (commonly
referred to as CA1–4)
by Fomin and Zelevinsky, together with Berenstein

[
FZ02a
,
FZ03
,
BFZ05
,
FZ07
]
.
These fundamental papers are still the best sources to learn
the basic notions, formulations, techniques, important examples, and the perspective
on cluster algebras.
However, even for someone who seriously wishes to learn cluster algebra theory,
reading through all of them, even selectively, at the beginning is not an easy task.
This is partly because each of CA1–4 has multiple goals,
and some of them are rather advanced and/or specific
for the beginners.

Fortunately, several excellent texts/monographs/reviews (including preliminary drafts) are already available
to fill the gap.
(e.g.,
[
Car06
]
,
[
Kel10
]
,
[
GSV10
]
,
[
Kel13
]
,
[
Mar13
]
,
[
Wil14
]
,
[
GR17
]
,
[
FZW16
]
,

[
FZW17
]
,
[
FZW20
]
,
[
FZW21
]
).
As another text of the same kind,
we especially focus on
basic aspects
in cluster algebra theory.
To be more explicit,
we paid attention
in the following points while preparing the manuscript.

•

Most importantly, the text is aimed for anyone
who starts to learn cluster algebras seriously without any
preliminary knowledge.

•

The text should be read in a self-contained way.
Also, it should be reasonably concise.

•

Consequently,
we focus on (most but not all of) the basic and fundamental aspects of cluster algebra theory,
namely, basic notions, techniques, and results concerning seeds,
cluster patterns, and cluster algebras in CA1–4.

•

We especially employ the formulation of seeds and mutations in CA4 throughout the text,
because the structure of mutations
is most transparent in our point of view.
By the same reason,
in some part of proofs,
we use the notion of
free coefficients

(coefficients in a universal semifield)
that replaces the role of coefficients of geometric type
in CA1–4.

•

We try to present
elementary manipulations of mutations
in the proofs of basic results explicitly,
even if many of them are easy or straightforward.
This is based on our belief that
the essence of cluster algebras is found
in these details of mutation mechanism.

•

Naturally, we do not try to go deep into a particular topic.
In particular, we have to omit the proof
of the finite type classification of cluster algebras in CA2,
because it requires deep analysis in conjunction with root systems of finite type.

•

We add a short
section on generalized cluster algebras
as extra material beyond CA1–4.

All results presented in this text are well-known,
and, except for the last section,
they are taken from CA1–4 or easily derived from the results therein
unless otherwise mentioned.

Bon voyage!

0.1
Getting Started

0.1.1
The very first step: pentagon periodicity

Let us play with a prototypical example of the cluster algebraic structure
without explaining the background.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}
be formal variables.
We consider the following recursion, or discrete dynamical system with discrete time
t=0,1,2,\dots
:

•

initial condition:
x_{1}(0)=x_{1}
,
x_{2}(0)=x_{2}
.

•

time development: For even
t
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{1}(t+1)=\frac{1}{x_{1}(t)}(x_{2}(t)+1),\\
x_{2}(t+1)=x_{2}(t),\end{cases}

(0.1.1)

and, for odd
t
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{1}(t+1)=x_{1}(t),\\
\displaystyle x_{2}(t+1)=\frac{1}{x_{2}(t)}(x_{1}(t)+1).\rule{0.0pt}{18.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.2)

Let us calculate
x_{1}(t)
and
x_{2}(t)
up to
t=5
.
Anyone who wishes to learn cluster algebras seriously has to work on the
following calculations by oneself.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{1}(0)=x_{1},\\
x_{2}(0)=x_{2}.\end{cases}

(0.1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{1}(1)=\frac{x_{2}+1}{x_{1}},\\
x_{2}(1)=x_{2}.\end{cases}

(0.1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{1}(2)=\frac{x_{2}+1}{x_{1}},\\
\displaystyle x_{2}(2)=\frac{1}{x_{2}}\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}+1}{x_{1}}=\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}+1}{x_{1}x_{2}}.\rule{0.0pt}{17.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.5)

So far, nothing special happened.
Let us continue.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{1}(3)=\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}+1}\frac{x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}+1}{x_{1}x_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{!}}\frac{x_{1}+1}{x_{2}},\\
\displaystyle x_{2}(3)=\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}+1}{x_{1}x_{2}}.\rule{0.0pt}{18.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.6)

Now, at the equality with symbol “
!
” in (
0.1.6
), the first nontrivial reduction happens.
Let us continue, and we mark the equality
with symbol “
!
”
whenever a similar reduction
occurs.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{1}(4)=\frac{x_{1}+1}{x_{2}},\\
\displaystyle x_{2}(4)=\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}+1}\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}+1}{x_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{!}}x_{1},\rule{0.0pt}{18.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{1}(5)=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+1}(x_{1}+1)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{!}}x_{2},\\
\displaystyle x_{2}(5)=x_{1}.\end{cases}

(0.1.8)

After
t=3
, reductions occur
systematically
,
so that at
t=5
the result is extremely reduced.

Let us observe the above result more closely.

Observation 0.1.1
.

(a).
Periodicity.

We have a
half
periodicity at
t=5
.
If we continue calculation, we have a
full
periodicity at
t=10
.

(b).
Laurent phenomenon.

Thanks to the reduction,
each
x_{i}(t)
is expressed, not as a general rational function,
but as a
Laurent polynomial
in the initial variables

x_{1}
,
x_{2}
with integer coefficients.

(c).
Laurent positivity.

Moreover, every nonzero coefficient of
the above Laurent polynomial is
positive
.

Remark 0.1.2
.

The property (c) is nontrivial even though the rules
(
0.1.1
) and (
0.1.2
) do not contain any negative coefficients.
For example, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x^{3}+1}{x+1}=x^{2}-x+1.

(0.1.9)

Next, let us consider a similar but different system.

Let
y_{1},y_{2}
be another formal variables.
We consider the following recursion, or discrete dynamical system with discrete time
t=0,1,2,\dots
:

•

initial condition:
y_{1}(0)=y_{1}
,
y_{2}(0)=y_{2}
.

•

time development: For even
t
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{1}(t+1)=y_{1}(t)^{-1},\\
y_{2}(t+1)=y_{2}(t)(1+y_{1}(t)),\end{cases}

(0.1.10)

and, for odd
t
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{1}(t+1)=y_{1}(t)(1+y_{2}(t)),\\
y_{2}(t+1)=y_{2}(t)^{-1}.\end{cases}

(0.1.11)

We calculate up to
t=5
.
As before, we mark the symbol “
!
” whenever some
reduction occurs.
Again, it is important to do it oneself.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{1}(0)=y_{1},\\
y_{2}(0)=y_{2},\end{cases}

(0.1.12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{1}(1)=y_{1}^{-1},\\
y_{2}(1)=y_{2}(1+y_{1}),\end{cases}

(0.1.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle y_{1}(2)=\frac{1+y_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}}{y_{1}},\\
\displaystyle y_{2}(2)=\frac{1}{y_{2}(1+y_{1})},\rule{0.0pt}{20.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.14)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle y_{1}(3)=\frac{y_{1}}{1+y_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}},\\
\displaystyle y_{2}(3)=\frac{1}{y_{2}(1+y_{1})}\frac{1+y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}}{y_{1}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{!}}\frac{1+y_{2}}{y_{1}y_{2}},\rule{0.0pt}{18.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle y_{1}(4)=\frac{y_{1}}{1+y_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}}\frac{1+y_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}}{y_{1}y_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{!}}\frac{1}{y_{2}},\\
\displaystyle y_{2}(4)=\frac{y_{1}y_{2}}{1+y_{2}},\rule{0.0pt}{18.0pt}\hskip-1.0pt\end{cases}

(0.1.16)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}y_{1}(5)=y_{2},\\
\displaystyle y_{2}(5)=\frac{y_{1}y_{2}}{1+y_{2}}\frac{1+y_{2}}{y_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{!}}y_{1}.\end{cases}

(0.1.17)

Unlike the previous case, the variables
y_{i}(t)
are not necessarily
Laurent polynomials in the initial variables
y_{1}
,
y_{2}
.
Nevertheless, similar reductions systematically occur after
t=3
,
and the same periodicity is obtained.
It is natural to speculate that there is a close relation between two systems.

Indeed this is the simplest example of mutations of
cluster variables

for
x_{i}(t)

and
coefficients

for
y_{i}(t)
, which we are going to study.
The periodicity we observed is the celebrated
pentagon periodicity

for a cluster algebra of type
A_{2}
.

0.1.2
Semifields

We introduce the notion of a semifield.
There are some variations of the definition depending on the purpose, and we especially use the one
in
[
FZ03
]
.

Definition 0.1.3

(Semifield)
.

A multiplicative abelian group
\mathbb{P}

equipped with
a binary operation

\oplus

is called a
semifield
if
the following properties hold:
For any
a,b,c,\in\mathbb{P}
,

\displaystyle a\oplus b

\displaystyle=b\oplus a,

(0.1.18)

\displaystyle(a\oplus b)\oplus c

\displaystyle=a\oplus(b\oplus c),

(0.1.19)

\displaystyle(a\oplus b)c

\displaystyle=ac\oplus bc.

(0.1.20)

The operation
\oplus
is called the
addition
in
\mathbb{P}
.
Note that there is no
subtraction
in
\mathbb{P}
.

Example 0.1.4
.

The set of all positive rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{+}

is a semifield by the usual multiplication and addition.
On the other hand,
the set of all nonnegative rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}

is
not
a semifield by the usual multiplication and addition,
because it is not a multiplicative group due to the presence of
0
.
Similarly,
the set of all positive real numbers
\mathbb{R}_{+}

is a semifield by the usual multiplication and addition.

The following three examples are especially important for cluster algebras.

Example 0.1.5
.

(a).
Universal semifield

\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
be an
n
-tuple of
formal variables.
Let
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{u})
be the rational function field of
\mathbf{u}
.
We say that a
rational function
f(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{u})

has a
subtraction-free expression

if it is expressed as

f(\mathbf{u})=p(\mathbf{u})/q(\mathbf{u})
, where both
p(\mathbf{u})
and
q(\mathbf{u})

are
nonzero
polynomials in
\mathbf{u}
whose coefficients
are
nonnegative
integers.

For example,
f(\mathbf{u})=u_{1}^{2}-u_{1}+1=(u_{1}^{3}+1)/(u_{1}+1)
has a subtraction-free expression.
Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})

be the set of all rational functions in
\mathbf{u}

having subtraction-free expressions.
Then,
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})

is a semifield by the usual
multiplication and addition in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{u})
.

(b).
Tropical semifield

\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
be an
n
-tuple of
formal variables.
Let
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
be the set of all
Laurent monomials in
\mathbf{u}
with coefficient 1,
which is a multiplicative abelian group by the usual
multiplication.
We define the addition
\oplus
by

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{a_{i}}\oplus\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{b_{i}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{\min(a_{i},b_{i})}.

(0.1.21)

Then,
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
becomes a semifield.
The addition
\oplus
is called the
tropical sum
.

(c).
Trivial semifield

\mathbbm{1}
.
Let
\mathbbm{1}=\{1\}
be the trivial multiplicative group.
We define the addition by

1\oplus 1=1
.
Then,
\mathbbm{1}
becomes a semifield.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be any semifield.
For any
a\in\mathbb{P}
and any positive integer
m
,
we write

\displaystyle ma:=\underbrace{a\oplus\cdots\oplus a}_{\text{$m$ times}}.

(0.1.22)

Also, any positive integer
m
is identified with an element of
\mathbb{P}

as
m=m1\in\mathbb{P}
.
For example, in the trivial semifield
\mathbbm{1}
,
we have
2=1\oplus 1=1
,
which is a little confusing.
(See Remark
0.1.12

for another problem of this notation.)

Definition 0.1.6

(Semifield homomorphism)
.

For any semifields
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
,
a map
\varphi:\mathbb{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
is called a

semifield homomorphism

if it preserves the multiplication and the addition.

Example 0.1.7

(Trivial homomorphisms)
.

For any semifield
\mathbb{P}
,
the map

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathrm{triv}}:\mathbb{P}\rightarrow\mathbbm{1},\ a\mapsto 1

(0.1.23)

is a semifield homomorphism.

The following fact
justifies the name “the universal semifield” for
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
.

Proposition 0.1.8
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
be the universal semifield
with
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be any semifield, and let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be any

n
-tuple of elements in

\mathbb{P}
.
Then, there is a unique semifield homomorphism
\pi:\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})\rightarrow\mathbb{P}

such that
\pi(u_{i})=a_{i}
for any
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

First,
for any nonzero polynomial
p(\mathbf{u})
with nonnegative integer coefficients,
the image
\pi(p(\mathbf{u}))
is uniquely determined
by replacing
u_{i}
with
a_{i}
and
+
with
\oplus
in
\mathbb{P}
in the polynomial
p(\mathbf{u})
,
where the notation (
0.1.22
) is taken into account.
The equality
\pi(p(\mathbf{u})q(\mathbf{u}))=\pi(p(\mathbf{u}))\pi(q(\mathbf{u}))
is guaranteed by the
axiom (
0.1.18
)–(
0.1.20
).
Next, for a given
f(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
,
take any subtraction-free expression
f(\mathbf{u})=p(\mathbf{u})/q(\mathbf{u})
.
Then, the image
\pi(f(\mathbf{u}))
is defined by
\pi(p(\mathbf{u}))/\pi(q(\mathbf{u}))
.
To see that it is well-defined,
let us take another subtraction-free expression
f(\mathbf{u})=p^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})/q^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})
.
Then, we have
p(\mathbf{u})q^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})=p^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})q(\mathbf{u})
. Thus,
\pi(p(\mathbf{u}))\pi(q^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}))=\pi(p^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}))\pi(q(\mathbf{u}))
.
Therefore, we have
\pi(p(\mathbf{u}))/\pi(q(\mathbf{u}))=\pi(p^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}))/\pi(q^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}))
.
It is easy to show that
\pi
is a semifield homomorphism.
∎

Definition 0.1.9

(Specialization)
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
,

\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{P}
, and
\pi
be
the ones in Proposition
0.1.8
.
For
f(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
,
the image
\pi(f(\mathbf{u}))

is called the
specialization

of
f(\mathbf{u})
at
\mathbf{a}
in
\mathbb{P}
,
and denoted by
f|_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{a})
.

Example 0.1.10

(Tropicalization homomorphism)
.

Let us
consider the semifields
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
and

\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
with common formal variables

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
.
By applying Proposition
0.1.8
with

\mathbb{P}=\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
and
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{u}
,
we have a unique semifield homomorphism

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{trop}}:\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})\rightarrow\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})

(0.1.24)

such that
\pi_{\mathrm{trop}}(u_{i})=u_{i}
for any
i=1,\dots,n
.
We call it the
tropicalization homomorphism
.
For example, for
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
,

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{trop}}\left(\frac{3u_{1}u_{2}^{2}u_{3}^{2}+2u_{1}^{2}u_{2}u_{3}}{3u_{2}^{2}+u_{1}^{2}u_{2}^{2}+u_{1}u_{2}^{3}u_{3}}\right)=\frac{u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}}{u_{2}^{2}}=u_{1}u_{2}^{-1}u_{3}.

(0.1.25)

Roughly speaking, it extracts the “leading Laurent monomial” of
a function
f(\mathbf{u})
in
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{u})
.

Since a semifield
\mathbb{P}
is a multiplicative abelian group,
we can construct the group ring
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbb{P}
.
The addition in
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
,
denoted by
+
as usual,
should be distinguished from
the addition
\oplus
in
\mathbb{P}
.

To construct the field of fractions of the ring
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
,
the ring
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
should be a domain.
This is automatically guaranteed.

Proposition 0.1.11
.

For any semifield
\mathbb{P}
, the following facts hold.

(a).
There is no torsion element in
\mathbb{P}
other than 1.
Namely, for any
p\in\mathbb{P}
,
if
p^{n}=1
for some positive integer
n
,
then
p=1
.

(b).
The group ring
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
is a domain.
Namely, there is no zero divisor other than 0.

Proof.

(a). Suppose that
p^{n}=1
for some positive integer
n
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle p=\frac{p\oplus p^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus p^{n}}{1\oplus p\oplus\cdots\oplus p^{n-1}}=\frac{p\oplus p^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus 1}{1\oplus p\oplus\cdots\oplus p^{n-1}}=1.

(0.1.26)

(b). This follows from the known fact that, for any multiplicative abelian group
G

with no torsion element other than
1
,
its group ring
\mathbb{Z}G
is a domain (e.g.,
[
May69
]
).
For completeness, let us give a proof.
Take any
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}G
.
They are written as finite sums
a=\sum_{i}m_{i}g_{i}
,
b=\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}g^{\prime}_{i}

(
g_{i},g^{\prime}_{i}\in G
) with nonzero coefficients

m_{i},m^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
H
be the subgroup of
G
generated by
all
g_{i},g^{\prime}_{i}
.
Then,
H
is finitely generated,
and we have
a,b,ab\in\mathbb{Z}H
.
By the assumption,
H
has no torsion element other than
1
.
Therefore, thanks to the fundamental theorem of
finitely generated abelian groups,
we have
H\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for some
n
.
Thus,
\mathbb{Z}H
is isomorphic to the Laurent polynomial ring

\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
, which is a domain. Therefore, if
ab=0
, then
a=0
or
b=0
.
∎

Thanks to Proposition
0.1.11
,
the field of fractions of
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
is well-defined,
and it is denoted by
\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}
.

Remark 0.1.12
.

There is some notational conflict between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
(and also
\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}
).
Namely, for
a\in\mathbb{P}
,

2a
, for example, may stand for
a\oplus a
in
\mathbb{P}
, or
a+a

in
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
depending on the context, and they are distinct.
Fortunately, we do not have serious difficulty from this conflict,
because usually this notation appears together with
\oplus
or
+
,
which clarifies the context.
Anyway, we have to be careful.

0.1.3
Matrix and quiver mutations

Below we fix a positive integer
n
,
which is called the
rank
of the forthcoming seeds, cluster patterns,
cluster algebras, etc.

Definition 0.1.13

(Skew-symmetrizable matrix)
.

An
n\times n
integer matrix
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}

is said to be
skew-symmetrizable

if there is a diagonal matrix
D=(d_{i}\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}

whose diagonal entries
d_{i}
are positive rational numbers
such that
DB
is skew-symmetric,
i.e,

\displaystyle d_{i}b_{ij}=-d_{j}b_{ji}.

(0.1.27)

The matrix
D
is called a
(left) skew-symmetrizer

of
B
,
which is not unique to
B
.

In particular, any skew-symmetric matrix is skew-symmetrizable
with a skew-symmetrizer
D=I
.

The condition (
0.1.27
) can be rephrased in the matrix notation as

\displaystyle DB=-B^{T}D,\ \text{or }DBD^{-1}=-B^{T},

(0.1.28)

where
B^{T}
is the transpose of
B
.
By (
0.1.27
), we have

\displaystyle b_{ii}=0,

(0.1.29)

\displaystyle b_{ij}=0\Longleftrightarrow b_{ji}=0,

(0.1.30)

\displaystyle b_{ij}>0\Longleftrightarrow b_{ji}<0.

(0.1.31)

Example 0.1.14
.

The following matrices exhaust all
2\times 2
skew-symmetrizable matrices:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&\mp b\\
\pm a&0\end{pmatrix}\quad(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}).

(0.1.32)

In the former case, any diagonal matrix
whose diagonals are positive integers
is
a skew-symmetrizer.
In the latter case, a skew-symmetrizer is given by

\displaystyle D=\left(\begin{matrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{matrix}\right).

(0.1.33)

For any integer
a
,
we define

\displaystyle[a]_{+}:=\max(a,0).

(0.1.34)

We have the following useful equalities:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=[a]_{+}-[-a]_{+},

(0.1.35)

\displaystyle a[b]_{+}+[-a]_{+}b

\displaystyle=a[-b]_{+}+[a]_{+}b,

0.2
Basic Notions

In this section we introduce basic notions for cluster algebras.

0.2.1
Seeds and mutations

Let us introduce the most fundamental notions in cluster algebra theory,
namely,
seeds
and
mutations
.

Recall that, for a given semifield
\mathbb{P}
,
\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}

is the field of fractions of the group algebra
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}

in Section
0.1.2
.

Definition 0.2.1

(Seed/Cluster variable/Coefficient)
.

Let
n
be any positive integer.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be any semifield,
and let

\mathcal{F}
be a field that is isomorphic to
the rational function field of
n
-variables
with coefficients in the field
\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{P}
.

•

A
(labeled) seed

with coefficients in
\mathbb{P}

(or a seed in
\mathcal{F}
) of rank
n

is a triplet

\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)

such that

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is an

n
-tuple of algebraically independent and generating elements
in
\mathcal{F}

(i.e., a transcendence basis of
\mathcal{F}
),

\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
is an

n
-tuple of any elements
in
\mathbb{P}
,
and
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}

is an
n\times n
skew-symmetrizable (integer) matrix.

•

We call
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
, and
B
,
respectively,
the
cluster
,
the
coefficient tuple
,
and the
exchange matrix
of
\Sigma
.
The elements
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
, respectively, are called
the
cluster variables
and the
coefficients
.

•

We call
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathcal{F}
, respectively, the
coefficient semifield

and the
ambient field
of a seed
\Sigma
,
and also, of the forthcoming cluster patterns, cluster algebras, etc.

Remark 0.2.2
.

Sometimes, cluster variables
x_{i}
and coefficients
y_{i}
are
casually called
x
-variables
and
y
-variables
, respectively.
Or, we may simply say
variable
x_{i}
,
variable
y_{i}
, etc.
Alternatively, they are also denoted by
A_{i}
and
X_{i}
, and called
A
-coordinates (
A
-variables)
and
X
-coordinates (
X
-variables)
, respectively,
following the convention of another pioneering works on cluster algebra theory by Fock and Goncharov
[
FG09a
,
FG09b
]
.

There are some related notions to Definition

0.2.1
.

Definition 0.2.3

(Seed without coefficients/
Y
-seed)
.

•

When the coefficient semifield
\mathbb{P}
is taken to be the trivial semifield
\mathbbm{1}
,
all coefficients

are 1.
Then, we can reduce a triplet
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)

in Definition

0.2.1

to a pair
(\mathbf{x},B)
, which is called
a
seed without coefficients
.

•

For any coefficient semifield
\mathbb{P}
,
we have an option to ignore cluster variables
and concentrate on a pair
Y=(\mathbf{y},B)

in Definition

0.2.1
,
which is called a
Y
-seed
in
\mathbb{P}
.

For any seed
\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
,
we attach an
n
-tuple

\hat{\mathbf{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\dots,\hat{y}_{n})

of elements in
\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{b_{ji}}.

(0.2.1)

They play an important role in the cluster algebra theory.
We call them
\hat{y}
-variables
.

Definition 0.2.4

(Seed mutation)
.

For any seed
\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
in
\mathcal{F}

and

,

\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{k}^{-1}\Biggl(\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{[-b_{jk}]_{+}}\Biggr)\frac{1+\hat{y}_{k}}{1\oplus y_{k}}&i=k,\\
x_{i}&i\neq k,\end{cases}

(0.2.2)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle y_{k}^{-1}&i=k,\\
y_{i}y_{k}^{[b_{ki}]_{+}}(1\oplus y_{k})^{-b_{ki}}&i\neq k,\end{cases}

(0.2.3)

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-b_{ij}&\text{$i=k$ or $j=k$,}\\
b_{ij}+b_{ik}[b_{kj}]_{+}+[-b_{ik}]_{+}b_{kj}&i,j\neq k,\end{cases}

(0.2.4)

where

in (
0.2.2

0.2.2
Cluster patterns and cluster algebras

We introduce cluster patterns and cluster algebras,
which are the main objects to be studied
in cluster algebra theory.

Definition 0.2.11

(
n
-Regular tree)
.

•

Let
\mathbb{T}_{n}
denote the
n
-regular tree
;
namely, it is a tree graph such that
each vertex has exactly
n
edges attached to it.
Moreover, the edges are labeled by
1
,…,
n

so that the edges attached to each vertex
are labeled without duplication.
By abusing the notation,
the set of vertices of
\mathbb{T}_{n}
is also denoted by

\mathbb{T}_{n}
.

•

We say that a pair of vertices
t
and
t^{\prime}

in
\mathbb{T}_{n}
are
k
-adjacent
, or
t^{\prime}
is
k
-adjacent to
t
,

if they are connected with an edge labeled by
k
.

The graph
\mathbb{T}_{n}
is finite for
n=1
and infinite otherwise.

.

n
-regular trees
\mathbb{T}_{n}

for

Definition 0.2.13

(Cluster pattern/
Y
-pattern/
B
-pattern)
.

•

A collection of seeds
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\{\Sigma_{t}=(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t},B_{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{P}

indexed by
\mathbb{T}_{n}

is called a
cluster pattern with coefficients in
\mathbb{P}

if,
for any pair
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
that are
k
-adjacent,
the equality
\Sigma_{t^{\prime}}=\mu_{k}(\Sigma_{t})
holds.
A cluster pattern is also called a
seed pattern
.

•

We replace the above
\mathbf{\Sigma}

with
a collection of seeds
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\{\Sigma_{t}=(\mathbf{x}_{t},B_{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
without coefficients.
Then,
it is called a
cluster pattern without coefficients.

•

We replace the above
\mathbf{\Sigma}

with
a collection of
Y
-seeds
\mathbf{Y}=\{Y_{t}=(\mathbf{y}_{t},B_{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
in
\mathbb{P}
.
Then, it is called a
Y
-pattern in
\mathbb{P}
.

•

From the above
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, we extract
a collection of the exchange matrices
\mathbf{B}=\{B_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
.
We call it the
B
-pattern of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

Often, it is convenient to
choose arbitrarily a distinguished
vertex
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
called the
initial vertex
.
Since any cluster pattern

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\{\Sigma_{t}=(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t},B_{t})\mid t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\}
is uniquely determined from
the
initial seed

\Sigma_{t_{0}}
at
t_{0}

by repeating mutations,
we may write
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{t_{0}})
.

For a seed
\Sigma_{t}=(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t},B_{t})

in a cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
we use the notation

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}=(x_{1;t},\dots,x_{n;t}),\quad\mathbf{y}_{t}=(y_{1;t},\dots,y_{n;t}),\quad B_{t}=(b_{ij;t})_{i,j=1}^{n}.

(0.2.36)

Often, we omit the index
t_{0}
for the initial seed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}=\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),\quad\mathbf{y}_{t_{0}}=\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}),\quad B_{t_{0}}=B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}.

(0.2.37)

We use similar notations for
\hat{y}
-variables
as well:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=(\hat{y}_{1;t},\dots,\hat{y}_{n;t}),\quad\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t_{0}}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\dots,\hat{y}_{n}).

(0.2.38)

By fixing the initial vertex,
one may regard each coefficient

as a rational function
with a subtraction-free expression
in the initial coefficients

\mathbf{y}
,
since the mutations of coefficients in (

)
are subtraction-free.
Similarly,
one may regard each cluster variable
x_{i;t}
as a rational function
with a subtraction-free expression
in the initial cluster variables

\mathbf{x}
;
moreover, its coefficients are
rational functions
with subtraction-free expressions
in
\mathbf{y}
.

Now we give the definition of a cluster algebra.

Definition 0.2.14

(Cluster algebra)
.

For any cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
the
cluster algebra

associated with

is the

has only finitely many distinct cluster variables,

is clearly finitely generated.
On the other hand, if
\mathbf{\Sigma}
has infinitely many distinct cluster variables,

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
may be finitely generated or not, depending on
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

(0.2.52)

and

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}_{t_{0}}=((x_{i})_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}},B^{\prime}),\quad\Sigma^{\prime\prime}_{t_{0}}=((x_{i})_{i=n^{\prime}+1}^{n},(y_{i})_{i=n^{\prime}+1}^{n},B^{\prime\prime}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\simeq\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime\prime}).

Let us introduce a notion,

Definition 0.2.22
.

We say that a cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}

is
with free coefficients at
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}

if the following conditions are satisfied:

•

The coefficient semifield of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is the universal semifield

\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{y})
with generators
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
,
where
n
is the rank of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

•

The coefficient tuple
\mathbf{y}_{t_{0}}
at
t_{0}
coincides with
\mathbf{y}
.

Note that, for each
t
,

y_{1;t}
, …,
y_{n;t}
are algebraically independent.

Remark 0.2.23
.

It might be natural to call such coefficients
universal coefficients
.

Remark 0.2.24
.

Though it is not necessary, we often identify the above base vertex
t_{0}

with the initial vertex for
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
In that case the notation

For any

Recall the universality of

We are going to extend this homomorphism

0.3
Fundamental Results

In this section we present some of the most fundamental
results in cluster algebra theory.

0.3.1
Laurent phenomenon

We prove the Laurent phenomenon observed in rank 2 examples.
This is the main result in CA1
and also the most fundamental fact on cluster algebras.

Theorem 0.3.1

(Laurent phenomenon
[
FZ02a
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}
be any cluster pattern with coefficients in any semifield
\mathbb{P}
.
Let
t_{0},t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
be any vertices.
Then, any cluster variable
x_{i;t}
is expressed as a Laurent polynomial in

\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
.

Here we present the “classic proof” in CA1.
In view of Proposition
0.2.25
,
it is enough to prove Theorem
0.3.1
for
any cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}
with
free coefficients at
t_{0}
.
This is because
the Laurent polynomial expression of
x_{i;t}
in
\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}

for
\mathbf{\Sigma}
reduces to
the one for any choice of coefficients
under the specialization
in Proposition
0.2.25
.

(Coprime)

From now on, we assume that

The proof in CA1 relies on the following lemma.
(The assumption of coefficients is necessary only for the claim (b).)

0.3.2
Finite type classification

We present the finite type classification
of cluster algebras and cluster patterns without proofs.
This is the main result in CA2.

To state the result,
we briefly explain the background in Lie theory.

The following definition is the counterpart of Definition
0.1.13
.

Definition 0.3.5

(Symmetrizable matrix)
.

An integer square matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}

is said to be
symmetrizable

if there is a diagonal matrix
D=(d_{i}\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}

whose diagonal entries
d_{i}
are positive integers
such that
DA
is symmetric,
i.e,

\displaystyle d_{i}a_{ij}=d_{j}a_{ji}.

(0.3.18)

The matrix
D
is called a
(left) symmetrizer
of
A
.
In particular, any symmetric matrix is symmetrizable.

Definition 0.3.6

(Cartan matrix)
.

An
n\times n
(integer) square matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}

is called a
(generalized) Cartan matrix
if
it satisfies the following conditions:

•

For any
i
, we have
a_{ii}=2
.

•

For any
i,j
(
i\neq j
),
we have
a_{ij}\leq 0
; moreover,
a_{ij}<0
if and only if
a_{ji}<0
.

For each symmetrizable Cartan matrix
X
, one can define the associated
Kac-Moody algebra, root system, and Weyl group
[
Kac90
]
.

There is a natural (many-to-one) correspondence from a skew-symmetrizable matrix to a symmetrizable Cartan matrix.

Definition 0.3.7

(Cartan counterpart)
.

With any skew-symmetrizable matrix
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
,
we associate a symmetrizable Cartan matrix
A=A(B)
as

\displaystyle a_{ij}=\begin{cases}2&i=j,\\
-|b_{ij}|&i\neq j.\end{cases}

(0.3.19)

The matrix
A(B)
is called the
Cartan counterpart
of
B
.

Definition 0.3.13

By the assumption, there are

0.3.3
Cluster algebras of geometric type

Below we focus on a class of cluster patterns whose
coefficient semifields are
tropical semifields
.
They are important in various applications of cluster algebras.

Let
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
be the tropical semifield in Example
0.1.10
.

Definition 0.3.19

(Geometric type)
.

A cluster pattern (resp. cluster algebra) with coefficients
in a tropical semifield
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
is called
a cluster pattern (resp. cluster algebra) of
geometric type
,
where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
and
m
is taken independently of
the rank
n
of a cluster pattern.

Remark 0.3.20
.

The terminology originates in the fact
that cluster algebras of this type typically arise as the coordinating rings
of certain algebraic varieties,
which are called the
geometric realization
of cluster algebras
in
[
FZ03
]
.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}
be a cluster pattern of geometric type.
Recall that
an element of
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
is a Laurent monomial in
\mathbf{u}

with coefficient 1.
Therefore, any coefficient
y_{i;t}
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
at
t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
is represented as

\displaystyle y_{i;t}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{c_{ji;t}},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(0.3.26)

This determines an
m\times n
integer matrix
C_{t}=(c_{ij;t})

for each
t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
,
and the coefficient tuple
\mathbf{y}_{t}
and the matrix
C_{t}

are identified.
Under this identification, the mutation in (
0.2.3
)
is translated as follows.

Proposition 0.3.21
.

Let
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
be
k
-adjacent vertices.
Then, we have the following mutation formula of matrices
C_{t}
:

\displaystyle c_{ij;t^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}-c_{ik;t}&j=k,\\
c_{ij;t}+c_{ik;t}[b_{kj;t}]_{+}+[-c_{ik;t}]_{+}b_{kj;t}&j\neq k.\end{cases}

(0.3.27)

Proof.

For any integer
a
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\min(0,a)=-[-a]_{+}.

(0.3.28)

Thus, by the definition of the tropical sum in (
0.1.21
), we have

\displaystyle{1\oplus y_{k;t}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{-[-c_{ik;t}]_{+}}.

(0.3.29)

By replacing the index
i
in (
0.2.3
) with
j
,
and comparing the power of
u_{i}
, we obtain
(
0.3.27
).
∎

Observe that the formula (
0.3.27
)
is parallel to the mutation (
0.2.4
)
of the exchange matrix
B_{t}
.
This motivates us to define the following
(n+m)\times n

matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{B_{t}}=\left(\begin{matrix}B_{t}\\
C_{t}\end{matrix}\right),

(0.3.30)

which is called an
extended exchange matrix
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

Abusing the notation,
let us write the
(i,j)
-entry of
\tilde{B_{t}}
as
b_{ij}
.
Then, we can unify the mutations (
0.2.4
)
and
(
0.3.27
)
as a single matrix mutation of
\tilde{B_{t}}
defined by

\displaystyle b_{ij;t^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}-b_{ij;t}&\mbox{$i=k$ or $j=k$},\\
b_{ij;t}+b_{ik;t}[b_{kj;t}]_{+}+[-b_{ik;t}]_{+}b_{kj;t}&i,j\neq k,\end{cases}

(0.3.31)

where
i=1,\dots,n+m
, and
j,k=1,\dots,n
.
Next,
let us observe that
the mutations of cluster variables
can be also expressed in terms of the matrix
\tilde{B}_{t}
.
By (
0.3.29
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k;t}}{1\oplus y_{k;t}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{[c_{jk;t}]_{+}},\quad\frac{1}{1\oplus y_{k;t}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{[-c_{jk;t}]_{+}}.

(0.3.32)

Thus,
the mutation in the form of (
0.2.5
)
is written as

\displaystyle x_{i;t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{k;t}}\Biggl(\,\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{[c_{jk;t}]_{+}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j;t}^{[b_{jk;t}]_{+}}+\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{[-c_{jk;t}]_{+}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j;t}^{[-b_{jk;t}]_{+}}\Biggr)&i=k,\\
x_{i;t}&i\neq k.\end{cases}

(0.3.33)

To summarize the result so far, the coefficients
\mathbf{y}_{t}

of geometric type
can be safely replaced with
the lower part
C_{t}
of the extended exchange matrices
\tilde{B_{t}}
.

To make the picture complete,
we introduce an
extended cluster
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=(x_{1;t},\dots,x_{n+m:t}):=(x_{1;t},\dots,x_{n;t},u_{1},\dots,u_{m}).

(0.3.34)

Then, together with the extended exchange matrix notation,
the mutation (
0.3.33
) is written as,
for
k=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle x_{i;t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{k;t}}\Biggl(\,\prod_{j=1}^{n+m}x_{j;t}^{[b_{jk;t}]_{+}}+\prod_{j=1}^{n+m}x_{j;t}^{[-b_{jk;t}]_{+}}\Biggr)&i=k,\\
x_{i;t}&i\neq k.\end{cases}

(0.3.35)

This formally coincides with the mutation of cluster variables

without coefficients
.

Let us formulate this observation more completely in terms of
a cluster pattern of rank
n+m

without coefficients
.
We consider the extension of
\tilde{B}_{t_{0}}

to a full
(n+m)\times(n+m)

skew-symmetrizable matrix.
Such an extension (together with its skew-symmetrizer) is not unique.
To given an example,
let
D
be a skew-symmetrizer of
B_{t_{0}}
,
and let
d
be the least common multiple
of the diagonal entries of
D
.
Then, the following matrix gives a skew-symmetrizable extension
of
\tilde{B}_{t_{0}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{t_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{t_{0}}&-D^{-1}dC_{t_{0}}^{T}\\
C_{t_{0}}&O\end{pmatrix},

(0.3.36)

where a skew-symmetrizer is given by
\hat{D}=D\oplus dI_{m}
.
(The factor
d
ensures that the matrix
\hat{B}_{t_{0}}
is an integer matrix.)
For a given initial vertex
t_{0}
in
\mathbb{T}_{n+m}
, we regard
\mathbb{T}_{n}

as a subtree of
\mathbb{T}_{n+m}

such that
\mathbb{T}_{n}
contains
t_{0}
and all vertices
that are sequentially adjacent to
t_{0}
with edges labeled by
\{1,\dots,n\}

in
\mathbb{T}_{n+m}
.
Then, the above cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}
of geometric type
is equivalent to consider a cluster pattern of rank
n+m

without coefficients
, restricted to the subtree
\mathbb{T}_{n}\subset\mathbb{T}_{n+m}
,
and with the initial seed

(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{0}},\hat{B}_{t_{0}})
.
The elements
x_{n+1;t}=x_{n+1}
, …,
x_{n+m;t}=x_{n+m}
are called the
frozen variables
,

because they are not mutated.
We note that the right half of
\hat{B}_{t}

does not influence neither (
0.3.31
)
nor (
0.3.35
).
Thus, we may safely replace seeds
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\hat{B}_{t})
with

(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\tilde{B}_{t})
without losing information.

Let us summarize the results obtained above as a proposition:

Proposition 0.3.22
.

We have the following three equivalent presentations
of a cluster pattern of geometric type:

•

a cluster pattern consisting seeds
(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t},B_{t})

with coefficients in
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
, by definition.

•

a cluster pattern consisting seeds
(\mathbf{x}_{t},\tilde{B}_{t})

whose coefficients in
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
are encoded in
the extend exchange matrices
\tilde{B}_{t}
.

•

a cluster pattern consisting extended seeds
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\hat{B}_{t})

or
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\tilde{B}_{t})

without coefficients and with frozen variables
x_{n+1}
, …,
x_{n+m}

that are identified with
u_{1}
, …,
u_{m}
.
(In this case we still say that it is of rank
n
.)

Even if the above three pictures are equivalent as
cluster patterns
,
there is some discrepancy for the corresponding
cluster algebras

due to the difference of the coefficient semifields.
Note that, for
\mathbb{P}=\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{u})
, the group ring

\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
is identified with the Laurent polynomial ring

\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{u}^{\pm 1}]
.
Then, for the first two pictures,
the corresponding cluster algebras are
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{u}^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}_{t}]_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}=\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}^{\pm 1}]_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}},

(0.3.37)

while, for the last one,
including the frozen variables as honorary members,
the corresponding cluster algebra is
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}]_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}=\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}]_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}.

(0.3.38)

Therefore, one has to be careful in which sense cluster algebra is considered.

For a cluster pattern of geometric type,
the Laurent phenomenon in Theorem
0.3.1

claims

\displaystyle x_{i;t}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{u}^{\pm 1}][\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1}]=\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1},\mathbf{u}^{\pm 1}].

(0.3.39)

Actually,
the following strong version of the
Laurent phenomenon holds.

Theorem 0.3.23

(
[
FZ03
]
)
.

For any cluster pattern of geometric type, we have

\displaystyle x_{i;t}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1},\mathbf{u}].

(0.3.40)

Proof.

We consider the following claim, which is slightly stronger than the desired result.

Claim.
For each
j
,
x_{i;t}
is a polynomial in
u_{j}

whose constant term (with respect to
u_{j}
) is a
nonzero
polynomial
with a subtraction-free rational expression in
\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1}

and
\mathbf{u}
other than
u_{j}
.
(See Definition
0.1.5
for a subtraction-free expression.)

We prove the claim by the induction on the distance
d=d(t_{0},t)
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}
.
For
d=0
, we have
x_{i;t}=x_{i}
, so that the claim holds.
Suppose that the claim holds for
d=d(t_{0},t)
.
Let
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
be
k
-adjacent to
t
.
It is enough to prove the claim for
x_{k;t^{\prime}}
.
Let us look at the first case of (
0.3.33
).
Note that, for each
j
, either
[c_{jk;t}]_{+}
or
[-c_{jk;t}]_{+}
is zero.
Thus, by the induction assumption, the numerator in
(
0.3.33
) is a polynomial in
u_{j}

whose constant term
is a nonzero polynomial
with a subtraction-free rational expression
in
\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1}

and
\mathbf{u}
other than
u_{j}
.
(Here, the condition of having a subtraction-free expression
guarantees
that, even in the special case
c_{jk;t}=0
, the constant terms from
the two products in (
0.3.33
) never cancel accidentally.)
Also, the denominator
x_{k;t}
has the same property.
Since we already know that
x_{k;t^{\prime}}
is in

\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1},\mathbf{u}^{\pm 1}]
,
the denominator divides the numerator such that
the result is a polynomial in
u_{j}

having the same property.
∎

Remark 0.3.24
.

The above claim in the proof does not imply that
x_{i;t}
has a nonzero constant term
in
\mathbf{u}
.

0.3.4
Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)

Let us present a prototypical example
of a cluster algebra (of geometric type) appearing in Lie theory.

First, we briefly recall basic definitions/facts on Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
.
See
[
Ful97
, Section 9]
for the background and more information.

Definition 0.3.25

(Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
)
.

•

In short, the
Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)

is the complex projective variety consisting of all 2-dimensional subspaces in a
5-dimensional vector space
V
over
\mathbb{C}
.

•

An element of
\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)
is identified with
its basis, which is represented by
a
2\times 5
complex matrix
M=(m_{ij})
of rank 2 modulo the left action of
GL(2,\mathbb{C})
.
This tells us that
\dim\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)=10-4=6
.

•

For each pair
1\leq i<j\leq 5
,
let

\displaystyle p_{ij}=p_{ij}(M):=\left|\begin{matrix}m_{1i}&m_{1j}\\
m_{2i}&m_{2j}\end{matrix}\right|.

(0.3.41)

Then, we define a map
i:\mathrm{Gr}(2,5)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{9}
by

\displaystyle i:M\mapsto[p_{12}:p_{13}:\dots:p_{45}],

(0.3.42)

where
[z_{1}:\dots:z_{10}]
is the homogeneous coordinates of
the complex projective space
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{9}
.
The map is injective, and it is called the
Plücker embedding
,
while
p_{ij}
are called the
Plücker coordinates
.

•

For
1\leq i<j<k<\ell\leq 5
, the Plücker coordinates satisfy the relations

\displaystyle R_{ijk\ell}:\ p_{ij}p_{k\ell}-p_{ik}p_{j\ell}+p_{i\ell}p_{jk}=0.

(0.3.43)

They are called the

0.4
Separation formulas

In this section we present the

given in CA4.
The are particularly important to study the

0.4.1
Principal coefficients

We introduce the notion of cluster patterns with principal coefficients.
They are a special class of cluster patterns of geometric type
and play an important role in studying cluster patterns.

Definition 0.4.1
.

We say that a cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}

is
with principal coefficients at
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}

if the following conditions are satisfied:

•

The coefficient semifield of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a tropical semifield

\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{y})
with generators
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
,
where
n
is the rank of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

•

The coefficient tuple
\mathbf{y}_{t_{0}}
at
t_{0}
coincides with
\mathbf{y}
.

The same remark in Remark
0.2.24
is applicable
for the notation.

In other words, a cluster pattern with principal coefficients at
t_{0}

is a cluster pattern of geometric type such that
the extended exchange matrix in (
0.3.30
) at
t_{0}

is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{t_{0}}=\left(\begin{matrix}B_{t_{0}}\\
I\end{matrix}\right).

(0.4.1)

All properties of cluster patterns of geometric type
hold to cluster patterns with principal coefficients.

Remark 0.4.2
.

In contrast to a cluster pattern of free coefficients in
Definition
0.2.22
,
the notion of principal coefficients crucially depends on the choice
of the base vertex
t_{0}
.
To explain it in more detail,
let
\mathbf{y}_{t}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{t}
(

) are principal coefficients at
base vertices
t_{0}
and

, respectively.
Then, the correspondence

\varphi:y_{i;t}\mapsto y^{\prime}_{i;t}

cannot
be
extended to a semifield homomorphism from

\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{y}_{t_{0}})
to
\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{y}_{t^{\prime}_{0}})
,
in general.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}
be a cluster pattern with principal coefficients
at
t_{0}
.
One may regard the coefficients of
\mathbf{\Sigma}

as the

tropicalization
of free coefficients
at
t_{0}
.

Namely,
let
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}
be a cluster pattern with free coefficients
at
t_{0}

such that it shares the common

-pattern
with
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
Then,
as a special case of Proposition

,

and

are related by the

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\pi_{\mathrm{trop}}:&\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{sf}}(\mathbf{y})&\rightarrow&\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{y})\\

0.5.1
Upper cluster algebras

Upper cluster algebras was introduced in CA3.
The motivation was two-fold as follows:

•

They are useful to study the structure of cluster algebras.

•

The coordinate rings of some algebraic varieties
(e.g., double Bruhat cells) are isomorphic to some upper cluster algebras,
not cluster algebras.
In other words, cluster algebras are too small in some situation.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}
be any cluster pattern with coefficients in any
semifield
\mathbb{P}
,
and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
be the associated cluster algebra
in the ambient filed
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 0.5.1

(Upper cluster algebra)
.

The
upper cluster algebra

\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})

is a
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{F}

defined by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\pm 1}].

(0.5.1)

Thanks to the Laurent phenomenon in Theorem
0.3.1
,
any cluster variable
x_{i;t}
belong to
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\subset\overline{\mathcal{A}}.

(0.5.2)

In some cases,
\mathcal{A}=\overline{\mathcal{A}}
occurs, but,
in general,
\mathcal{A}\neq\overline{\mathcal{A}}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1},x_{2},x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},x_{3}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]=\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1},x_{2},x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime\prime}_{2},x_{3}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}].

(0.5.47)

Let us prove that the upper bound
\mathcal{U}_{t}
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is independent of
t
.
Let
t_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,n
) be the vertex
that is
i
-adjacent to
t_{0}
.
Let
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i;t_{i}}
.
It is enough to prove
\mathcal{U}_{t_{0}}=\mathcal{U}_{t_{1}}
.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{U}_{t_{0}}&=\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}^{\pm 1}]\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}_{t_{i}}^{\pm 1}]\\
&=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}^{\pm 1}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}_{t_{i}}^{\pm 1}]\right)\\
&=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{i-1}^{\pm 1},x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i},x_{i+1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]\quad\text{(by \eqref{1eq:ZZZ1})}\\
&=\bigcap_{i=2}^{n}(\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{i-1}^{\pm 1},x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i},x_{i+1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}])\\
&=\bigcap_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{i-1}^{\pm 1},x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i},x_{i+1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]\\
&=\bigcap_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{i-1}^{\pm 1},x_{i},x^{\prime\prime}_{i},x_{i+1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]\quad\text{(by \eqref{1eq:LL2})}\\
&=\mathcal{U}_{t_{1}},\end{split}

(0.5.48)

where the last equality is obtained by reversing the above procedure.
Then, the upper cluster algebra
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\pm 1}]=\bigcap_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}\mathcal{U}_{t}=\mathcal{U}_{t_{0}}.

(0.5.49)

On the other hand, for the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}]\subset\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}\mathcal{U}_{t}=\mathcal{U}_{t_{0}}.

(0.5.50)

Therefore, we obtain
\mathcal{A}\subset\overline{\mathcal{A}}
.
∎

0.6
Generalized cluster algebras

To conclude this introductory guide, we present a generalization of cluster algebras
introduced by Chekhov and Shapiro
as an extra material beyond basics.
Essentially all nice properties of cluster algebras are inherited to this generalization.
Therefore, they extensively widen the perspective of cluster algebra theory.

0.6.1
Generalized cluster algebras

Chekhov and Shapiro
[
CS14
]
introduced a generalization of
cluster algebras called
generalized cluster algebras
(GCA, for short),
motivated by
some examples naturally appeared in the study of Teichmüller space for
the Riemann surface with orbifold points.
Moreover, it turned out that
they are indeed a natural generalization such that
all essential properties of cluster algebras presented in this text are shown or conjectured to hold

[
CS14
,
Nak15
]
.

Let us explain the idea shortly.
The mutations of ordinary cluster algebras
are defined by
binomials

1+\hat{y}_{k}

and

1\oplus y_{k}
in (
0.2.2
) and (
0.2.3
).
We may ask how/why this binomial condition is essential.
It turns out that one can replace it with fairly general

polynomials
,
and they are still as good as the ordinary one,
if
we adequately
adjust the other parts of mutations.

Definition 0.6.1

(Mutation data)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a given semifield.
We fix the following
mutation data

(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{z})
in
\mathbb{P}
:

•

an
n
-tuple of positive integers

\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
, which are called
mutation degrees
.

•

a collection

\mathbf{z}=(z_{i,s})_{i=1,\dots,n;s=0,\dots,r_{i}}

of elements in
\mathbb{P}
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\mbox{(unit constant term/monic property)}\quad z_{i,0}

\displaystyle=z_{i,r_{i}}=1,

(0.6.1)

\displaystyle\mbox{(reciprocity)}\quad z_{i,s}

\displaystyle=z_{i,r_{i}-s}.

(0.6.2)

They are equivalent to give an
n
-tuple of
monic
and
reciprocal

polynomials in a single formal
variable
y
with coefficients in
\mathbb{P}
,

\displaystyle P_{i,\mathbf{z}}(y)=\sum_{s=0}^{r_{i}}z_{i,s}y^{s},\quad(i=1,\dots,n).

(0.6.3)

In the simplest case
\mathbf{r}=(1,\dots,1)
, they reduce to
P_{i,\mathbf{z}}(y)=1+y
, which is the
binomial for the ordinary mutations of seeds
mentioned above.

Definition 0.6.2

(Seed mutation for GCA)
.

Let
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{z})
be a mutation data in
\mathbb{P}
.
Let
\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},B)
be an (ordinary) seed with coefficients in
\mathbb{P}
in Definition

0.2.1
.
For the polynomials in (
0.6.3
), let

\displaystyle P_{k,\mathbf{z}}(\hat{y}_{k})=\sum_{s=0}^{r_{k}}z_{k,s}\hat{y}_{k}^{s},\quad P_{k,\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbb{P}}(y_{k})=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{r_{k}}z_{k,s}y_{k}^{s},

(0.6.4)

where
\hat{y}_{k}
is the ordinary
\hat{y}
-variable for
\Sigma
.
Then, the
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{z})
-mutation of
\Sigma

in direction
k
,
\mu_{k}(\Sigma)=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
,
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle x_{k}^{-1}\Biggl(\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{[-b_{jk}r_{k}]_{+}}\Biggr)\frac{P_{k,\mathbf{z}}(\hat{y}_{k})}{P_{k,\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbb{P}}(y_{k})}&i=k,\\
x_{i}&i\neq k,\end{cases}

(0.6.5)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle y_{k}^{-1}&i=k,\\
y_{i}y_{k}^{[r_{k}b_{ki}]_{+}}P_{k,\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbb{P}}(y_{k})^{-b_{ki}}&i\neq k,\end{cases}

(0.6.6)

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-b_{ij}&\text{$i=k$ or $j=k$,}\\
b_{ij}+b_{ik}[r_{k}b_{kj}]_{+}+[-b_{ik}r_{k}]_{+}b_{kj}&i,j\neq k.\end{cases}

(0.6.7)

It is easy to check the following properties.

Proposition 0.6.3
.

(a). Any skew-symmetrizer
D
of
B
is also
a skew-symmetrizer of
B^{\prime}
.

(b). The mutation
\mu_{k}
is an involution.
In particular,
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is a seed.

(c). The
\hat{y}
-variables mutate as

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k}^{-1}&i=k,\\
\hat{y}_{i}\hat{y}_{k}^{[r_{k}b_{ki}]_{+}}P_{k,\mathbf{z}}(\hat{y}_{k})^{-b_{ki}}&i\neq k.\end{cases}

(0.6.8)

(d). The
\varepsilon
-expressions, which are parallel to
the ones in Proposition
0.2.7
, hold,
where we put
\varepsilon
in every
[\,\cdot\,]_{+}

and also replace

and

and
P_{k,\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbb{P}}(y_{k}^{\varepsilon})

in (

)–(

).

Proof.

(a). This is proved in the same way as
Proposition
0.1.18
(a).

(b).
By the reciprocity condition (

),
we have a parallel formula to (

\displaystyle\frac{P_{k,\mathbf{z}}(\hat{y}_{k}^{-1})}{P_{k,\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbb{P}}(y_{k}^{-1})}=\frac{P_{k,\mathbf{z}}(\hat{y}_{k})}{P_{k,\mathbf{z}}|_{\mathbb{P}}(y_{k})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{-b_{jk}r_{k}}.

(0.6.9)

Then, one can repeat the proofs of Propositions
0.1.18
(c)
and

The properties (c) and (d) can be proved in a similar way as

0.2.6
and

.
∎

Remark 0.6.4

One can lift the reciprocity condition (

Definition 0.6.5
.

For a given mutation data

in
\mathbb{P}

Theorem 0.6.6

CS14

0.6.2
Separation formulas for GCA

As one more confirmation of the rightness of GCA,
we present the separation formulas for generalized cluster patterns,
which are parallel to the ordinary ones.

Definition 0.6.8
.

We say that a generalized cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}

with mutation data
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{z})

is
with principal coefficients at

if the following conditions are satisfied:

•

The coefficient semifield of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a tropical semifield

\mathrm{Trop}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
with generators
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
,

\mathbf{z}=(z_{i,s})_{i=1,\dots,n;\,s=1,\dots,r_{i}-1}

with
z_{i,s}=z_{i,r_{i}-s}
as formal variables.

•

The coefficient tuple
\mathbf{y}_{t_{0}}
at
t_{0}
coincides with
\mathbf{y}
.

•

The mutation data
\mathbf{z}
coincides with
\mathbf{z}
.

We need a stronger version
of the Laurent phenomenon,
which is parallel to Theorem
0.3.23
.

Theorem 0.6.9

(
[
Nak15
]
)
.

For any generalized cluster pattern with principal coefficients at
t_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle x_{i;t}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}^{\pm 1},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}].

(0.6.20)

Proof.

This is proved by showing a similar claim
in the proof of Theorem
0.3.23

for both variables
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
,
where the property (
0.6.1
) of
\mathbf{z}
is essential.
∎

For a generalized cluster pattern
one can define its (generalized)
C
- and
G
-matrices and
F
-polynomials in two ways:

•

Define them through a generalized cluster pattern
with principal coefficients at
t_{0}

•

Define them by the underlying (generalized)

-pattern
\mathbf{B}
and
t_{0}
.

Below we skip the first definition, which is parallel to the ordinary case,
and only give the
second

One notable difference to the ordinary case is that the

-polynomials are
now polynomials in
both
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
,
where
\mathbf{z}
are the formal variables
\mathbf{z}

corresponding to the mutation data
\mathbf{z}

(under our usual abuse of notations).

.

For a given generalized cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}

\displaystyle c_{ij;t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle G_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle=I,

.
.

(Separation Formulas
