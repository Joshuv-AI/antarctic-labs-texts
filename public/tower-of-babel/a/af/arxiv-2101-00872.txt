New sequences of non-free rational points
Ilia Smilga

Abstract
We exhibit some new infinite families of rational values of τ \tau , some of them squares of rationals, for which the group or even the semigroup generated by the matrices ( 1 1 0 1 ) \left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\ 0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right) and ( 1 0 τ 1 ) \left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\ \tau&1\end{smallmatrix}\right) is not free.



For each
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, we define the group

\Gamma(\alpha,\beta):=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\beta&1\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle,

and we define
S(\alpha,\beta)
to be the semigroup generated by the same two matrices. The problem of determining values of the product
\tau:=\alpha\beta
for which the group
\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)
(conjugate by
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&\alpha\end{smallmatrix}\right)
to
\Gamma(1,\tau)
) is free has already attracted a considerable amount of attention
[
San47
,
Bre55
,
CJR58
,
Ree61
,
LU69
,
KS94
,
Gil08
]
; the corresponding problem for the semigroup
S(\alpha,\beta)
has also been studied
[
BC78
,
Sł15
]
. In particular, a lot of people have focused on the special case where
\tau
is the square of a rational
[
LU69
,
Ign86
,
Bea93
,
TT96
]
(which is a natural condition as it corresponds to studying
\Gamma(\mu,\mu)
with rational
\mu
), or more generally any rational number
[
Bam00
,
KK21
]
.

To avoid confusion, we should warn the reader that previous authors have variously used as the key parameter either the product
\alpha\beta
(e.g.
[
KS94
,
KK21
]
), its half, usually denoted by
\lambda
(focusing on
\Gamma(2,\lambda)
) (e.g.
[
CJR58
,
Ree61
,

,
Bam00
,

,
Sł15
]
), or its square root, usually denoted by
\mu
(e.g.
[

Bre55
,

,
Bea93
,
TT96
]
). We make the former choice, hence the following terminology:

Definition 1
.

We say that a number
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
is
free
(resp.
semigroup-free
) if the group
\Gamma(1,\tau)
(resp. the semigroup
S(1,\tau)
) is free.

An elementary observation (first made by Brenner
[
Bre55
]
) is that for
\tau\geq 4
, the group
\Gamma(1,\tau)
is always Schottky, hence free. So for groups, it suffices to study the interval
(-4,4)
(in fact even
(0,4)
, given the symmetry
\Gamma(1,\tau)=\Gamma(1,-\tau)
). Similarly, for
\tau\geq 1
, the semigroup
S(1,\tau)
is Schottky (see also
[
BC78
, Theorem 2.6]
), so it suffices to study the interval
(-4,1)
.

Beyond this, very little is known. It is conjectured that all rational
\tau\in(-4,4)
are non-free, and that all rational
\tau\in(-4,1)
are non-semigroup-free. There are no known counterexamples, but only a limited number of values for which these two conjectures have been proved. Indeed, group relations are easiest to find for
\tau
close to
0
. A spectacular development in this direction is
[
KK21
]
, proving that all rational numbers
\tau\in(-4,4)
whose numerator is at most
23
, or between
25
and
27
, are non-free. On the other hand, as
\tau
gets closer to
4
, finding relations becomes extremely hard.

It is even harder to find accumulation points of non-free rational values. It seems that the only ones known so far (besides
0
, which is trivial as
\frac{1}{n}
is never free) are those of the form
\frac{1}{n}

[
Bea93
]
and those of the form
\frac{n}{n\pm 1}

[
TT96
]
. As for accumulation points of non-semigroup-free rational values, only
0
is known.

Our goal in this paper has been to find accumulation points of non-free and non-semigroup-free rational values (or squares of rationals) that lie as close as possible to the endpoints of the corresponding interval. The result that we obtain can be summarised as follows.

Theorem 2
.

The set of
\tau
that are rational and non-semigroup-free (hence in particular non-free) has
-2\pm\sqrt{2}
,
-\phi^{\pm 2}
,
-2

1

\phi=\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}
is the golden ratio). The set of
\tau
that are squares of rationals and non-semigroup-free (hence in particular non-free) has
1
as an accumulation point.

Proof.

This follows from the more precise Theorem
3

•

the values given in
(iv)

(resp. of

The technique used to prove points

In fact, instead of relations, we will exhibit so-called “half-relations”, that we will define right now. We will then see (Proposition
5
) that each half-relation easily allows one to write down an actual relation.

Definition 4
.

Let
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
, and let us fix the notations
g:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
h_{\tau}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
\tau&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
We say that a sequence
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
is a
half-relation
for
\tau
if:

(i)

for odd
l
, the matrix
M=g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots h_{\tau}^{a_{l-1}}g^{a_{l}}
satisfies

\tau c_{12}(M)-c_{21}(M)=0;

(12)

(ii)

for even
l
, the matrix
M=g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots g^{a_{l-1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{l}}
satisfies

c_{11}(M)-c_{22}(M)=0,

(13)

where
c_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-th coefficient, so that

\forall M,\quad M=:\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}(M)&c_{12}(M)\\
c_{21}(M)&c_{22}(M)\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

The point of half-relations is that they allow us to construct a special kind of relations for the group or semigroup corresponding to
\pm\tau
:

Proposition 5
.

A sequence
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
is a half-relation for
\tau
if and only if the identity

g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots f^{a_{l}}=h_{\tau}^{a_{l}}g^{a_{l-1}}\cdots f^{a_{1}},\qquad\text{ where }f:=\begin{cases}g&\text{ if $l$ is odd}\\
h_{\tau}&\text{ if $l$ is even,}\end{cases}

(15)

or equivalently

g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots g^{-a_{2}}h_{\tau}^{-a_{1}}=\Id,

(16)

holds. Such an identity provides a nontrivial relation:

•

for the group
\Gamma(1,\tau)
if
\forall i,\;a_{i}\neq 0
;

•

for the semigroup
S(1,\tau)
if
\forall i,\;a_{i}>0
;

•

for the semigroup
S(1,-\tau)
if
\forall i,\;(-1)^{i}a_{i}>0
.

Proof.

Let
M=g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots
denote the left-hand side of (
15
). Then
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
is a half-relation for
\tau
if and only if
M
is a fixed point of:

•

the involution
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&\smash{c/\tau}\\
\tau b&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, or equivalently
M\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&\tau\end{smallmatrix}\right)M^{\mathrm{t}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&\tau\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{-1}
, for odd
l
;

•

the involution
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}d&b\\
c&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, or equivalently
M\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)M^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{-1}
, for even
l
.

Both of them are antimorphisms; moreover the former switches
g
and
h_{\tau}
, whereas the latter fixes both of them. In both cases, the involution maps both sides of (
15
) to each other.

Now assume that this is true. Then (
15
), usually rewritten as (
16
) for groups, is a relation in
\Gamma(1,\tau)
, which is nontrivial if the coefficients are all nonzero. If they are all positive, then (
15
) is in fact a relation in
S(1,\tau)
. If they have alternating signs (note that whenever
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
is a half-relation, so is
(-a_{1},\ldots,-a_{l})
), then (
15
) (for even
l
) or (
16
) (for odd

) is a relation in
S(1,-\tau)
.
∎

Let us also observe that being a half-relation is a polynomial condition: more precisely, the left-hand side of (

) (for odd

) or (

) (for even

and
\tau

, so we can factor

Definition 6
.

For each

P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{l}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l};\tau):=\begin{cases}(c_{12}-\frac{1}{\tau}c_{21})(g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots g^{a_{l}})&\text{ if $l$ is odd;}\\
\frac{1}{\tau}(c_{11}-c_{22})(g^{a_{1}}h_{\tau}^{a_{2}}\cdots h_{\tau}^{a_{l}})&\text{ if $l$ is even}\end{cases}

(17)

(where
c_{ij}
is the

14
)).

Thus, by construction, whenever
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
is a root of some polynomial
P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{l}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
for some tuple
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
of nonzero (resp. positive) integers, it is non-free (resp. non-semigroup-free).
Explicitly, for small

, these polynomials are as follows:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{1}(a_{1};\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{1}

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2};\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{3}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3};\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\tau+a_{1}-a_{2}+a_{3}

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{4}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{4};\tau)

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}\tau+a_{1}a_{2}-a_{2}a_{3}+a_{3}a_{4}+a_{1}a_{4}

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\mathrm{HR}}^{5}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{5};\tau)&=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}a_{5}\tau^{2}+(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}+a_{1}a_{2}a_{5}+a_{1}a_{4}a_{5}+a_{3}a_{4}a_{5})\tau\\
&\qquad+a_{1}-a_{2}+a_{3}-a_{4}+a_{5}.\end{split}

The observation that non-free values can be obtained as roots of certain polynomials has already been made previously. Even more specifically, when

is odd, the polynomial

coincides (up to the substitution

[

]
