Heavy States in 3d Gravity and 2d CFT
David Grabovsky

Abstract
We discuss correlators of light fields in heavy states in AdS 3 \mathrm{AdS}_{3} gravity and holographic 2d CFTs. In the bulk, the propagator of free fields in AdS backgrounds containing a conical defect or a BTZ black hole can be obtained by solving a wave equation, as well as by the method of images. On the boundary, these geometries are sourced by heavy operator insertions, and the propagator is dual to a heavy-light (HHLL) correlator. By matching its expansion in Virasoro blocks to our bulk results, we determine the OPE coefficients of all contributing states in both the s s and t t channels. In the s s channel, these states are excitations of the light field on top of the heavy state, and their OPE coefficients are the amplitudes to create them. The t t -channel OPE is dominated by the Virasoro vacuum block, but there is also an infinite family of light two-particle states that contribute to the correlator. The OPE coefficients that couple these states to heavy operators represent their expectation values in heavy backgrounds. We determine them exactly, derive their asymptotic form at large twist, and discuss their behavior near and above the BTZ threshold, where they become thermal one-point functions.

Introduction and Overview

How can we understand high-energy states in quantum gravity? Our intuition from general relativity suggests that the answer is universal: at high energies, essentially any localized object will form a black hole. At the semiclassical level, black holes are characterized by a universal Hawking temperature, and in AdS/CFT
Witten:1998qj
;
Gubser:1998bc
;
Banks:1998dd
they are dual to thermal states of the boundary theory. One way to understand their properties is to add light probe fields to the system and ask how they behave in the presence of a heavy object. We can often answer such questions precisely by studying the correlation functions of light fields in heavy states, especially in holographic settings.

There are two ways to understand how heavy objects affect light ones in the bulk. From one perspective, the light state and the heavy state come together, bind gravitationally, and form two-particle composite states that can be thought of as orbiting configurations of the light field around the heavy object. From another perspective, the light and heavy states each propagate independently but exchange gravitons and other virtual particles, like light-light or heavy-heavy composites, to mediate the interactions between them. In the dual conformal field theory (CFT), the “binding” and “exchange” philosophies correspond to the
s
- and
t
-channel expansions of a holographic correlator, respectively, and are related by crossing symmetry. The requirement of crossing symmetry is highly constraining because the data that enters a calculation in one channel can often be used to bootstrap what must happen in the other channel
Belavin:1984vu
. In this way, one can gain access to detailed information about CFT observables and their bulk duals. In particular, (
1
) can teach us about the amplitudes to create the composite states described above, as well as their expectation values in heavy states.

In this paper, we will make this precise within the framework of
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathrm{CFT}_{2}
, a model where many calculations that are essentially intractable in higher dimensions simplify and often yield exact results. Below, we review how the relative simplicity of 3d gravity
Deser:1983tn
;
Deser:1983nh
and the powerful tools available in 2d CFT
Belavin:1984vu
improve our analytical control.

1.1
3d Gravity with Defects

In
(2+1)
-dimensional gravity, there are no local propagating bulk degrees of freedom
Deser:1983tn
. As a consequence, every solution to pure 3d gravity with a negative cosmological constant is locally isometric to the empty anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime. The only solutions are
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
itself and certain quotients of
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
, the simplest of which is the BTZ black hole
Banados:1992wn
;
Banados:1992gq
. BTZ black holes of any size can exist, but their masses must lie above a critical threshold mass
M_{*}=\frac{1}{8G}
separating them from the pure
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
vacuum.
1
1

1

We work throughout in natural units
c=\hbar=1
, and we set the AdS radius
\ell_{\mathrm{AdS}}=1
. We also take the energy of the pure
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
vacuum to be zero, instead of
-1
as is more commonly done.
What happens if the mass goes below the BTZ threshold?

In pure gravity, there are no such geometries. But if the theory is coupled to pointlike sources, then it admits conical defect solutions describing a massive particle in
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
with mass below the BTZ threshold
Deser:1983nh
. An important class of defects is the
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
orbifolds, which have been important in string theory
Lunin:2001jy
;
Balasubramanian:2005qu
;
Giusto:2004ip
;
Giusto:2020mup
and in connection with gravitational path integrals
Lewkowycz:2013nqa
;
Benjamin:2020mfz
. But the parameter
N
characterizing the defect’s strength does not need to be an integer, and most conical defect solutions are not quotients of
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
in an obvious way.
2
2

2

There are also conical excess solutions, but we do not consider them here. Their masses lie below the AdS vacuum, and they are dual to CFT states with negative conformal dimensions. A theory containing such states cannot be unitary; nevertheless, it would be interesting to extend our results to this regime.
In fact, the metric of conical AdS is identical to that of BTZ, but with the horizon radius analytically continued so that the mass falls below the BTZ threshold. This suggests that we should be able to probe the physics of black holes from below the threshold by treating heavy and thermal states on the same footing.

We pursue this treatment by studying the propagation of free fields in heavy AdS backgrounds. We allow the fields to be massive, but we require them to be light enough that they do not backreact on the geometry. Both below and above the BTZ threshold, we construct the propagator for free fields by solving the Klein–Gordon equation and summing over the spectrum of normal or quasinormal modes, which play the role of the orbiting bound states on the LHS of (
1
). We also obtain the propagator in a different way: using the method of images for quotients of
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
. A sum over images is always available in BTZ, but only makes sense below the threshold in
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. In both cases, it may be viewed as a saddle-point sum over the lengths of all geodesics between two points in the quotient geometry. Although it is not immediately obvious, this representation of the propagator corresponds to the “exchange” calculation on the RHS of (
1
).

1.2
HHLL Correlators in 2d CFT

Semiclassical
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
gravity has a dual description in terms of a holographic 2d CFT living at the asymptotic AdS boundary.
3
3

3

The existence of such a CFT has not been shown, and the appropriate holographic dual may instead involve an ensemble of theories
Chandra:2022bqq
. However, since every member of this ensemble must share the same semiclassical properties, we may refer to “the” boundary theory when discussing its large-
c
behavior.
Holographic CFTs have a very large central charge
c=\frac{3}{2G}\gg 1

Brown:1986nw
corresponding to the large-
N
limit of gauge theories. We work at leading order in the
1/c
expansion, where many aspects of the CFT become universal
Hartman:2014oaa
;
Fitzpatrick:2014vua
;
Fitzpatrick:2015zha
;
Collier:2019weq
.

In the CFT, the bulk field is dual to a light primary operator
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
with conformal dimension
\Delta_{\mathrm{L}}=\Delta
, where “light” means that
\frac{\Delta}{c}\longrightarrow 0
as
c\longrightarrow\infty
. Since the bulk field is free, its CFT dual is a generalized free field: this encodes the notion of an effective field theory where fluctuations on top of the heavy state are essentially free. Meanwhile, a conical defect is created by a heavy CFT operator
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}
whose conformal dimension
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}=O(c)
lies below the BTZ threshold.
4
4

4

Some authors use “heavy” to refer to conformal dimensions that lie strictly above the BTZ threshold as
c\longrightarrow\infty
. We relax this condition slightly and refer to both conical defects and BTZ black holes as heavy.
The defects we consider are static and non-rotating, so in the CFT they are represented by scalar primaries. By the operator-state correspondence, they can also be thought of as highly excited pure states
\ket{\O_{\up{H}}}
. By contrast, BTZ black holes are dual to thermal states in the CFT. But the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH)
Srednicki:1994mfb
tells us that up to exponentially small corrections, it does not matter whether we consider a thermal state or a pure state of the same average energy. Therefore both above and below the threshold, we may describe heavy states by CFT primary insertions.

Figure 1

The configuration of fields defining the HHLL correlator is shown on the left in AdS (where we have a nontrivial background geometry) and on the right in the CFT (where, in radial quantization, the background is created by two heavy insertions).

With heavy AdS backgrounds viewed in this way, our bulk propagator becomes a heavy-heavy-light-light (HHLL) CFT correlator of the form
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}\rangle
. This situation is illustrated in Figure
1
, both in AdS (left) and in radial quantization (right). CFT correlators are computed by using the operator product expansion (OPE) to sum over intermediate states exchanged by the external operators, as in (
1
). The organization of CFT states into irreducible representations of the Virasoro algebra turns the OPE into an expansion in functions called Virasoro conformal blocks, which have figured prominently in the conformal bootstrap program
Heemskerk:2009pn
;
El-Showk:2011yvt
;
Fitzpatrick:2012yx
;
Komargodski:2012ek
;
Jackson:2014nla
and the study of holographic entanglement
Ryu:2006ef
;
Headrick:2010zt
;
Hartman:2013mia
;
Asplund:2014coa
. Fortunately, the semiclassical HHLL Virasoro blocks are known
Fitzpatrick:2015zha
, leaving only the questions of which states are exchanged and how much they each contribute.

The main contributions to the OPE come from double-trace, or double-twist, operators built from
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}
. In the
s
channel, these are heavy-light composite states that describe excitations of the light field in the bulk and correspond to the Fourier modes one obtains by solving the wave equation in AdS. The associated OPE coefficients, which we deduce by matching the OPE to our bulk result, are interpreted as the amplitudes of these excitations. In the
t
channel, the dominant contribution comes from the Virasoro vacuum block, which encodes gravitational interactions between
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}
. The vacuum block gives the same contribution to the correlator as the minimal geodesic in the sum over images
Hartman:2013mia
and fully captures the singular structure of the OPE limit where the two light operators are brought together
Berenstein:2022ico
. The non-minimal geodesics making up the rest of the correlator are then responsible for the regular terms in the OPE, and should have a CFT interpretation in terms of non-vacuum exchanges. Indeed, there is an infinite family of light-light double-twist states which provides exactly these contributions.

The OPE coefficients of these primaries are identified as their expectation values in heavy states, and much of the technical work in this paper is devoted to computing them explicitly by matching the
t
-channel OPE to the sum over images. Our main results, comprising Sections
5
–
8
, include explicit expressions for the expectation values of low-lying operators, asymptotic formulæ for the HHLL Virasoro blocks and OPE coefficients at large twist, and a discussion of their behavior near and above the BTZ threshold.

1.3
Main Results and Outline

By carrying out the analysis described above, we aim to achieve four goals:

1.

Understand the emergence of thermal behavior.
By studying conical defects of any mass (not just
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
), we fill the gaps between the integers and illustrate how physics below the BTZ threshold smoothly connects to the physics in the black hole regime. In particular, we describe how to analytically continue the sum over images for non-integer
N
. The expectation values we compute analytically continue, above the threshold, to thermal one-point functions in BTZ or thermal AdS.

2.

Give a unified treatment from a Lorentzian perspective.
By treating both defects and black holes as heavy operator insertions on the Lorentzian cylinder, we avoid the need to switch to Euclidean signature above the BTZ threshold. (Of course, the ETH tells us that it makes no difference whether we work on the torus.)

3.

Move beyond vacuum dominance.
By focusing on non-vacuum
t
-channel exchanges, we point out that there is more to learn about CFT correlators beyond their perturbative expansions in singular powers. These effects are inaccessible to large-spin asymptotics in the
s
channel, but are required by crossing symmetry.

4.

Compute double-trace expectation values.
In higher dimensions, it is of great interest—but even greater difficulty—to understand the correlators of double-trace operators in black hole backgrounds. Now, at least in a simplified toy model, we have the answers. The expectation values we compute do not factorize at large
N
, in the sense that (for example)
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\rangle\neq\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle
. Instead, the effective classical background created by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}
modifies the expectation values of
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\rangle
directly.

This paper is organized into two broad parts. In the first part, we compute the HHLL correlator in four different ways: twice in the bulk, first by solving the wave equation (Section
2
and Appendix
A
) and then using the method of images (Section
3
); and twice on the boundary, using the
s
-channel and
t
-channel OPEs (Section
4
). These computations are the bulk and boundary avatars of the LHS and RHS of (
1
), respectively. To some extent these preliminary sections review old material, although some details of the construction and structure of the correlator had not been discussed in the literature before.

In the second part, we calculate the expectation values of light double-trace operators (Section
5
) and analyze their properties. We derive an asymptotic formula for the expectation values at large twist (Section
6
), study their behavior as a function of the mass of the heavy state (Section
7
and Appendix
B
), and discuss their interpretation as thermal one-point functions (Section
8
). We conclude and discuss future directions in Section
9
.

2
Summing Over Field Modes

The background geometry created by a static, heavy object in
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
can be written in global coordinates
(t,\theta,r)
, where
\theta\sim\theta+2\pi
, as follows:

\displaystyle\differential s^{2}

\displaystyle=-f(r)\differential t^{2}+\frac{\differential r^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}\differential\theta^{2},\qquad f(r)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle r^{2}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}&\text{below the threshold},\\[11.0pt]
r^{2}-r_{0}^{2}&\text{above the threshold}.\end{cases}

(5)

Below the BTZ threshold, the parameter
N\geq 1
describes the strength of a conical defect. Integer values of
N
correspond to the cyclic orbifolds
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, and the limit
N\longrightarrow\infty
corresponds to the BTZ threshold. Above the threshold, a horizon of radius
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
forms, and the state becomes thermal with a temperature given by
r_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{\beta}=2\pi T_{\mathrm{H}}
. The geometries above and below the threshold are related by the analytic continuation
N\longleftrightarrow-\frac{i}{r_{0}}
, so conical defects can be viewed as black holes with imaginary temperature, and conversely BTZ can be thought of as a conical geometry with an imaginary angular deficit. To unify our descriptions of the two regimes, we write
f(r)=r^{2}+\alpha^{2}
, where

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{1}{N}\longleftrightarrow ir_{0}=\frac{2\pi i}{\beta},\qquad\mathrm{i.e.}\qquad\alpha\equiv\begin{cases}\frac{1}{N}\in[0,1]&\text{below the threshold}\\
ir_{0}\in i\mathbb{R}_{+}&\text{above the threshold}.\end{cases}

(6)

Consider a free massive scalar field
\phi
propagating in this background. We decompose the solutions of its equation of motion, the Klein–Gordon equation, into Fourier modes:

\displaystyle\left(\Box-m^{2}\right)\phi_{\omega,\ell}=0,\qquad\phi_{\omega,\ell}(t,\theta,r)=e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\ell\theta}R_{\omega,\ell}(r),\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

(7)

On this ansatz, (
7
) reduces to a differential equation for the radial modes:

\displaystyle\quantity[\f{1}{r} \dv{}{r} \p{r f(r) \dv{}{r}} + \f{\w^2}{f(r)} - \f{\ell^2}{r^2} - m^2]R_{\omega,\ell}(r)=0.

(8)

The solutions to the radial equation can be expressed in terms of hypergeometric functions. With appropriate boundary conditions, these mode solutions can be used to construct the propagator. We review how this is done in Appendix
A
, which includes a detailed treatment of the wave equation, its solutions in various backgrounds, the relevant boundary conditions, and the resulting propagators. Below, we summarize the essential results.

2.1
Correlators Below the Threshold

In conical AdS, we require the radial modes to be regular at the origin and normalizable, i.e. decaying at infinity. Those solutions
R_{\omega,\ell}(r)
which are regular at
r=0
behave near the AdS boundary as linear combinations of normalizable and non-normalizable modes:

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(r)\sim\mathcal{N}_{\omega,\ell}\left(A(\omega,\ell)r^{\Delta-2}+B(\omega,\ell)r^{-\Delta}\right)\qquad\mathrm{as}\quad r\longrightarrow\infty.

(9)

Here
\mathcal{N}_{\omega,\ell}
is a normalization constant,
\Delta
is the conformal dimension of the CFT operator dual to
\phi
, and
A(\omega,\ell)
and
B(\omega,\ell)
are certain ratios of Gamma functions. To ensure the normalizability of the modes, we must have
A(\omega,\ell)=0
. This condition is satisfied on a discrete set of normal frequencies
\omega_{n\ell}
derived in Appendix
A.3
and given by

\displaystyle\omega_{n\ell}=|\ell|+\frac{1}{N}\big(\Delta+2n\big)\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},\;\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(10)

The resulting normal modes can be made orthonormal with respect to the Klein–Gordon inner product, and any solution to (
7
) can be expanded in normal modes:

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\sum_{n=0}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\quantity[\phi_{n\ell}(x) a_{n\ell} + \phi_{n\ell}^*(x) a_{n\ell}^{\dagger}],\qquad\phi_{n\ell}(t,\theta,r)=e^{-i\omega_{n\ell}t}e^{i\ell\theta}R_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}(r).

(11)

We quantize the field by promoting
a_{n\ell}
and
a_{n\ell}^{\dagger}
to operators obeying the canonical commutation relations
[a_{n\ell},a_{n^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
. We choose the Hartle–Hawking vacuum
\ket{0}
as the one annihilated by all of the lowering operators
a_{n\ell}
, and finally we construct the bulk-to-bulk propagator

as a sum over the normal modes:

\displaystyle G(x,x^{\prime})=\matrixelement{0}{\phi(x) \phi(x')}{0}=\sum_{n\ell}e^{-i\omega_{n\ell}(t-t^{\prime})}e^{i\ell(\theta-\theta^{\prime})}R_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}(r)R_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}^{*}(r^{\prime}).

(12)

As we send
r\longrightarrow\infty
, the boundary values of the field modes are rescaled by

\displaystyle\phi_{n\ell}(t,\theta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\Delta}\phi_{n\ell}(t,\theta,r)=\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}e^{-i\omega_{n\ell}t}e^{i\ell\theta},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\equiv\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\Delta}R_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}(r).

(13)

The coefficients

We obtain the boundary-to-boundary propagator by sending

2.2
Correlators Above the Threshold

The story in BTZ is similar, but there are some important differences. For instance, normal modes are not well defined because waves in black hole backgrounds can fall through the horizon. To probe the black hole’s thermal response to perturbations, we need to impose boundary conditions suitable for the retarded Green’s function
G_{\mathrm{R}}(x,x^{\prime})
. The modes should be purely ingoing at the horizon and decaying at infinity:

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(r)\sim\begin{cases}(r-r_{0})^{-\frac{i\omega}{2r_{0}}},&r\longrightarrow r_{0},\\
r^{-\Delta},&r\longrightarrow\infty\end{cases}

(15)

The ingoing modes behave like (
9
) near the boundary, and the condition

(which ensures that
R_{\omega,\ell}(r)

\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}=-\ell-ir_{0}\big(\Delta+2n\big)\in\mathbb{H}_{-},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},\;\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

(16)

The real and imaginary parts of
\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}
represent the energy and decay rate of the QNMs, respectively.
\mathrm{Im}(\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell})<0
ensures that the black hole is stable to small perturbations. The QNMs have a characteristic lifetime
\tau_{n}=\frac{1}{r_{0}\left(\Delta+2n\right)}
, independent of their angular momentum. The longest-lived modes (
n=0
) dominate the retarded propagator at late times, leading to an exponential decay
G_{\mathrm{R}}(t,\theta)\sim e^{-r_{0}\Delta t}
in the semiclassical limit.

The QNMs are neither normalizable nor complete

and

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(\omega,\ell)\rangle_{J}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}(\omega,\ell)J(\omega,\ell)\implies\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}(\omega,\ell)=\frac{B(\omega,\ell)}{A(\omega,\ell)}.

2.3
Analytic Continuation to BTZ

The formal similarity between the conical and BTZ propagators (

\displaystyle\omega_{n\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\longrightarrow}}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\big)^{2}.

\displaystyle f_{n\ell}(\beta)=\frac{\sinh\left(\beta|\ell|/2+i\pi\Delta\right)}{\sinh\left(\beta|\ell|/2\right)},\qquad\big|f_{n\ell}(\beta)\big|^{2}=\cos^{2}\left(\pi\Delta\right)+\coth^{2}\left(\frac{\beta|\ell|}{2}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\pi\Delta\right)

The Method of Images

In spacetimes that can be realized as quotients of

2
, and relate the images to bulk geodesics.

3.1
The Sum Over Images

The bulk-to-bulk propagator in pure
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
is known and can be expressed as a function of the geodesic distance
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
between two bulk points. It takes the form

(28)

where
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})=\cosh^{-1}(1/\xi)

\displaystyle\xi\equiv\frac{1}{\cosh\sigma(x,x^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(r^{2}+1\right)\left(r^{\prime 2}+1\right)}\cos(t-t')-rr^{\prime}\cos(\th-\th')}.

(29)

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{AdS}}(t,\theta)=\frac{1}{\big(2\cos t-2\cos\theta\big)^{\Delta}}.

\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, i.e. for

. Above the threshold, it applies to black holes of any mass, since every BTZ spacetime is a quotient of
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}

3.2
Short-Distance Behavior

As the two field insertions are brought close together, the propagator diverges. The nature of this singularity is controlled by the direct (
k=0
) image in (
31
–
33
), which inherits its UV behavior from the pure AdS propagator. All of the other images are regular in the coincident limit, since they correspond to configurations where the field insertions remain well separated (see Figure
4
). This gives us a natural decomposition of the propagator into its regular and singular parts: we have
G(x,x^{\prime})=G_{0}(x,x^{\prime})+G_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,x^{\prime})
, with

\displaystyle G_{0}(x,x^{\prime})=\alpha^{2\Delta}\frac{e^{-\Delta\sigma_{0}(x,x^{\prime})}}{e^{-2\sigma_{0}(x,x^{\prime})}-1},\qquad G_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,x^{\prime})=\alpha^{2\Delta}\sum_{k\neq 0}\frac{e^{-\Delta\sigma_{k}(x,x^{\prime})}}{e^{-2\sigma_{k}(x,x^{\prime})}-1}.

(34)

At short distances, the bulk-to-bulk propagator exhibits a divergence that goes like the inverse of the shortest geodesic length
\sigma_{0}(x,x^{\prime})
between nearby points:

\displaystyle G(x,x^{\prime})\approx G_{0}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{\alpha^{2\Delta}e^{-\Delta\sigma_{0}}}{e^{-2\sigma_{0}}-1}=-\frac{\alpha^{2\Delta}}{2\sigma_{0}}+(\mathrm{regular})\qquad\mathrm{as}\quad x\longrightarrow x^{\prime}.

(35)

For short radial geodesics, the geodesic length is approximately
\sigma_{0}(x,x^{\prime})\sim\frac{r}{\alpha}
; therefore
G(x,x^{\prime})
diverges like
1/r
. We may understand this behavior physically by zooming in on two nearby points with
|r-r^{\prime}|\ll R_{\mathrm{AdS}}=1
. At these scales the curvature of AdS becomes negligible, so
G(x,x^{\prime})
approaches the flat-space propagator in 3 dimensions—but this is nothing more than the Green’s function
1/|r-r^{\prime}|
for the flat-space Laplacian.

The short-distance behavior of the boundary propagator is markedly different, since points on the conformal boundary with very small
t-t^{\prime}
and
\theta-\theta^{\prime}
are still formally infinitely far apart. The “coincident” limit is still controlled by the
k=0
image, but this time
G(z,\bar{z})
exhibits a much stronger power-law divergence. From (
33
), near
z,\bar{z}=1
we have

\displaystyle G_{0}(z,\bar{z})=|z|^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\absolutevalue{\f{1-z^{\a}}{\a}}^{-2\Delta}=\absolutevalue{1-z}^{-2\Delta}\Big(1+O\big(|1-z|\big){}_{\!}\Big).

(36)

Meanwhile, the regular part of
G(z,\bar{z})
remains finite at
z,\bar{z}=1
:

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})=\absolutevalue{z}^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\sum_{k\neq 0}\absolutevalue{\f{1 - \zeta^k z^{\a}}{\a}}^{-2\Delta}=\sum_{k\neq 0}\absolutevalue{\f{2}{\a} \sin(\pi k\a)}^{-2\Delta}+O\big(|1-z|\big).

(37)

It can be checked that
G_{0}(z,\bar{z})
has the same singular structure as the full mode sum (
25
). This is easiest to see explicitly by comparison with the equal-time propagator. Taking
z=e^{i\theta}
and expanding (
36
) in powers of
\theta\ll 1
, we get

\displaystyle G_{0}(\theta)=\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\sin\left(\frac{\alpha\theta}{2}\right)\right)^{-2\Delta}=\theta^{-2\Delta}\left(1+\frac{\Delta\alpha^{2}}{12}\theta^{2}+\frac{\Delta(5\Delta+1)\alpha^{4}}{1440}\theta^{4}+O\big(\theta^{6}\big){}_{\!}\right),

(38)

in agreement with (
27
). We see that
G_{0}(z,\bar{z})
characterizes the asymptotic behavior of the mode sum at large
|\ell|
, while
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
is influenced by the small-
|\ell|
terms in the series, and is sensitive to the thermal factor
f_{n\ell}(\beta)
above the threshold.

3.3
Analytic Continuation in
N

While the BTZ propagator is manifestly analytic in
r_{0}
, in conical AdS the sum over images is finite and only defined for integer
N
. However, most conical geometries have
N\notin\mathbb{Z}
and are not quotients of
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
, so it is desirable to analytically continue (
32a
) to arbitrary
N
. A partial solution to this problem is to approximate
G(z,\bar{z})
by its singular term
G_{0}(z,\bar{z})
, which is analytic in
N
and dominates near
z,\bar{z}=1
. But this is a poor approximation away from
z,\bar{z}=1
, and it does not explain how one might also analytically continue
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
to non-integer
N
. Below, we explain how to proceed more carefully.

The full correlator.

We begin by applying Newton’s binomial formula

\displaystyle\left(1-x\right)^{-\Delta}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\Delta)}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+m)}{\Gamma(1+m)}x^{m},\qquad|x|<1,

(39)

in (
33
), taking
x=\zeta^{k}z^{\alpha}
. Expanding in
z
and
\bar{z}
separately, we obtain

\displaystyle G(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2\Delta}}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}}\absolutevalue{z}^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+m)\Gamma(\Delta+m^{\prime})}{\Gamma(1+m)\Gamma(1+m^{\prime})}z^{\alpha m}\bar{z}^{\alpha m^{\prime}}\zeta^{k(m-m^{\prime})}.

(40)

Now we can sum over the roots of unity
\zeta^{k(m-m^{\prime})}
to get rid of the sum over
k
. When this is done carefully,
9
9

9

The sum of the roots of unity is a Kronecker-delta comb spaced by multiples of
N
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi ik(m-m^{\prime})/N}=N\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}+N\ell}.

(41)

Substituting this into (
40
) sets
m^{\prime}=m+N|\ell|
and replaces the sum over
m^{\prime}
with a sum over
\ell
. The absolute value is necessary because we may have either
\ell\geq 0
or
\ell<0
, but
m^{\prime}
must be nonnegative.
one obtains precisely the sum over normal modes (
25
). We conclude that the method of images knows about the wave equation; and vice versa, the sum over modes analytically continues the sum over images to cases where it does not apply. As we will discuss in Section
4.4
, this calculation implements the crossing symmetry of CFT four-point functions and is related to the conformal bootstrap.

Regular and singular parts.

This calculation shows that
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
may be analytically continued to non-integer
N
by writing it as the difference between the mode sum (
25
) and the singular image (
36
). This prescription can be somewhat unwieldy, however, because it involves the direct subtraction of two divergent quantities.

We can do better by taking advantage of a symmetry: in
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, the
k
th image in (
33
) is equal to the
(N-k)
th image; and in BTZ, the
k
th image is equal to the
-k
th image. Moreover, near
z,\bar{z}=1
, the dominant contribution to
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
comes from (twice) the
k=1
image. All of the others are suppressed because they correspond to geodesics with nontrivial winding around the singularity—see the discussion below. By keeping only one contribution, we get an approximate analytic continuation of
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
:

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})\approx 2|z|^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\absolutevalue{\f{1 - \zeta z^{\a}}{\a}}^{-2\Delta},\qquad\alpha\in i\mathbb{R}_{+}\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\alpha\leq\frac{1}{3}.

(42)

In conical AdS, the condition
N\geq 3
is necessary because the
k=1
and
k=N-1
images do not really exist for defect geometries with fewer than 3 distinct images. That said, (
42
) is exact for
N=3
, and is also exact for
N=2
without the leading factor of 2.

3.4
Images and Geodesics

The form of the propagator obtained from the method of images has a nice geometric interpretation in terms of the geodesic approximation for heavy correlators. For sufficiently massive fields,
G(z,\bar{z})
is well approximated by a saddle-point sum over all bulk geodesics between two boundary points
Balasubramanian:1999zv
. There are
N
such geodesics in
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, and infinitely many in BTZ. Each geodesic
\gamma_{k}
contributes
e^{-m\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ren}}[\gamma_{k}]}
to the propagator, where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ren}}[\gamma_{k}]
is the renormalized geodesic length of
\gamma_{k}
. Direct calculations of the geodesic length
Berenstein:2022ico
;
Martinez:2019nor
show that each of the images in (
32
) have precisely this form.

In general, the geodesic approximation is valid only for very heavy fields; in particular, one requires that
\Delta=1+\sqrt{1+m^{2}}\approx m\gg 1
. However, in the present case the method of images is exact for any
\Delta
and matches the geodesic method, so in fact the assumption
\Delta\gg 1
is unnecessary. What we do need to assume is that the field is light enough that it does not backreact on the geometry. This means that the CFT operator dual to
\phi
has a conformal dimension independent of the central charge, which is taken to be very large. Probe operators with dimensions
1\ll\Delta\ll c
are sometimes called “hefty,” to distinguish them from heavy operators whose dimensions scale (at least) linearly with
c
.

For nearby boundary points, the singular part dominates the correlator because it corresponds to
\gamma_{0}
, which is always the shortest geodesic between nearby two points. The other geodesics either pass around the other side of the conical singularity or horizon, or else have a nontrivial winding number around it. As a result, they are all longer than
\gamma_{0}
, and their contributions to the propagator are exponentially suppressed at large
\Delta
. But for larger opening angles,
\gamma_{0}
starts to compete for dominance with another geodesic on the other side of the boundary cylinder. In conical AdS, this is
\gamma_{N-1}
, while in BTZ, it is
\gamma_{-1}
: see Figure
4
. This Ryu–Takayanagi phase transition
Hartman:2013mia
occurs at
\theta=\pi
(i.e.
z,\bar{z}=-1
) and cannot be seen solely by looking at the singular part of the correlator.

4
HHLL Correlators in 2d CFT

We now turn to the boundary description of heavy states in
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
gravity. We consider a holographic 2d CFT with large central charge

Fitzpatrick:2015zha

3

4.1

At the semiclassical level, the spectrum of a holographic 2d CFT dual to pure

Another way to say this is that conical defects and BTZ black holes admit infinitely many species of quantum hair.

4.3
Heavy-Light Virasoro Blocks

Now we assemble the complete
s
- and
t
-channel OPEs for
G(z,\bar{z})
, including regular terms, and match them to our bulk results to find the OPE coefficients in both channels.

The
s
channel.

The
s
-channel Virasoro blocks
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(\mathrm{s})}(z)
are sums of three-point functions of the form
\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{h}\rangle
, where the internal operator
\mathcal{O}_{h}
is either a double-twist primary
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}
or one of its descendants. In the case of primary exchange, we get

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{h}(\infty)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(z)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}(0)\rangle=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{HL}}_{h}z^{h-h_{\mathrm{H}}-h_{\mathrm{L}}}\implies\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(\mathrm{s})}(z)\supset z^{h-h_{\mathrm{H}}-h_{\mathrm{L}}},

(52)

where
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{HL}}_{h}
is an OPE coefficient and
\supset
means “contains as a term.” In fact, this is all we need, since all descendant OPE contributions are suppressed at large
c
. To see this, note that descendant three-point functions can be obtained from (
52
) by applying a differential operator to
\mathcal{O}_{h}
and then dividing by the norm of the descendant state. The differential operator generates a function of
z
with a prefactor polynomial in
h-h_{\mathrm{H}}-h_{\mathrm{L}}
, while the norm of the descendant is a polynomial in
c
and
h
of the same degree as the level of the descendant. (For instance,
L_{-1}\mathcal{O}_{h}=\partial\mathcal{O}_{h}
has norm
\matrixelement{h}{L_1 L_{-1}}{h}=2h
.) The HL double-twists have
h=O(c)
but
h-h_{\mathrm{H}}-h_{\mathrm{L}}=O(1)
, so the contribution of a level-
k
descendant is
O(c^{-k})
. Thus the
s
-channel blocks of the
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}
reduce to scaling blocks,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(\mathrm{s})}(z)=z^{h-h_{\mathrm{H}}-h_{\mathrm{L}}}\big(1+O(1/c)\big),\qquad h=h_{[\mathrm{HL}]_{n\ell}}.

(53)

To put together the full correlator, we multiply in the anti-holomorphic part of the block and sum over the double-twist spectrum, as in (
4.2
):

\displaystyle G(z,\bar{z})=\sum_{n\ell}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\right)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{[\mathrm{HL}]_{n\ell}}^{(\mathrm{s})}(z)\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{[\mathrm{HL}]_{n\ell}}^{(\mathrm{s})}(\bar{z})=\absolutevalue{z}^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\sum_{n\ell}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\right)^{2}\absolutevalue{z}^{2\alpha n}z^{\ell}.

(54)

As promised, this OPE takes the same form as the mode sum (
25
). Matching the two expressions allows us to identify the OPE coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}
with the normal mode amplitudes given in (

).

Above the BTZ threshold, the frequencies
\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}
and coefficients

are associated to quasinormal modes in the bulk. Since they are complex-valued, they should not be interpreted as the conformal dimensions or OPE data of physical states in a unitary CFT. See

The
t
channel.

The semiclassical HHLL
t
-channel Virasoro blocks were worked out by Fitzpatrick, Kaplan, and Walters
Fitzpatrick:2015zha
. Their key insight was that the presence of heavy operators in the correlator modifies the background geometry in which the light fields propagate. The result is that the HHLL blocks reduce to the all-light (i.e. global) conformal blocks, with the light fields evaluated in a new set of coordinates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(\mathrm{t})}(z)

\displaystyle=z^{(\alpha-1)h_{\mathrm{L}}}\left(\frac{w}{\alpha}\right)^{h-2h_{\mathrm{L}}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(h,h;2h;w\right),\qquad w(z)=1-z^{\alpha}.

(55)

The replacement
z\mapsto z^{\alpha}
above is the same as the change of boundary coordinates from pure
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
to a heavy background that shows up in the method of images. From (
55
), we obtain the vacuum block by setting
(h,\bar{h})=(0,0)
, and we get the LL double-twist blocks by substituting the MFT spectrum (
44
). The vacuum contribution is

\displaystyle G_{0}(z,\bar{z})=\absolutevalue{\cal{F}_0^{(\up{t})}(z)}^{2}=\absolutevalue{z}^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\absolutevalue{\f{1 - z^{\a}}{\a}}^{-2\Delta},

(56)

in agreement with the singular image (
36
), while the contributions of the LL double-twist blocks must sum together to reproduce the regular piece of the correlator:

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{z}^{(\alpha-1)\Delta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\ell\\
\ell\;\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}\absolutevalue{\f{w}{\a}}^{2n}\left(\frac{w}{\alpha}\right)^{\ell}\Big|{}_{2}F_{1}(h_{n\ell},h_{n\ell};2h_{n\ell};w)\Big|^{2}.

(57)

Here we set
h_{n\ell}=h_{[\mathrm{LL}]_{n\ell}}
and recall that absolute values are shorthand for holomorphically factorized products. The products
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
must be even under
\ell\longrightarrow-\ell
, because the primaries
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n,\pm\ell}
should contribute equally to the OPE
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\times\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
of two scalars.

4.4
Crossing and Lorentzian Inversion

Let us make several bootstrap-adjacent comments complementary to our perspective.

The vacuum block.

The Virasoro vacuum block in 2d CFT is nontrivial: it contains the identity, the stress tensor, and an infinite tower of multi-stress tensor descendants. In the bulk, these states excite topological degrees of freedom called boundary gravitons, and their
t
-channel exchange between
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
creates the effective classical background defined by the
w
coordinate. In the
w
-plane, the vacuum block becomes the two-point function of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
, which is determined by a geodesic length. This is one way in which the vacuum block knows about bulk geometry. But the vacuum block does not know everything, even at the semiclassical level: it is not single-valued in
z
, and as we have discussed it fails to capture
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
. None of the blocks (
55
) are single-valued either, but the fact that their sum must be puts strong constraints on the coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
.

Crossing symmetry.

Crossing symmetry is the statement that the
s
-channel and
t
-channel OPEs compute the same four-point function
G(z,\bar{z})
. This is formally manifest in (
48
), and our calculation (
40
) proving the equivalence of the mode sum and the method of images provides an explicit demonstration of crossing. The fact that
G(z,\bar{z})
is a linear combination of Virasoro blocks in both channels implies that each block in one channel can be expressed as a linear combination of the blocks in the other. The matrix that inverts individual
t
-channel blocks into combinations of
s
-channel blocks is known exactly and is called the Virasoro fusion kernel
Collier:2018exn
. The use of this kernel to bootstrap OPE data gives rise to the Euclidean and Lorentzian inversion formulæ
Simmons-Duffin:2017nub
.

We are essentially working in the opposite direction: by independently computing
G(z,\bar{z})
using bulk methods, we start with the answer for
G(z,\bar{z})
as well as all of its contributing Virasoro blocks. By matching the answer to its OPE, we can deduce all of the OPE coefficients and thereby “derive” the fusion kernel.

Lorentzian inversion.

We have seen that the singular structure of (
56
) controls the asymptotic behavior of the OPE (
54
). This is the statement that applying an inversion formula to the vacuum block produces the dominant contribution to the
s
-channel OPE data. But as emphasized above, the vacuum cannot be the only contribution. Indeed, the LL double-twist blocks were inverted in
Collier:2018exn
(see also
Kusuki:2018wpa
) and shown to give nontrivial subleading contributions to the
s
-channel OPE data. Our “inversion” of the full sum over images in (
40
) is another way to see that the OPE coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}
receive important contributions from both vacuum and non-vacuum exchange.

5
Expectation Values in Heavy States

In this section, we initiate our study of the OPE coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
. Both sets of coefficients are normalization constants for three-point functions involving
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}=\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle,\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}=\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}\rangle.

(58)

The coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}
represent the amplitudes to fuse two copies of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
to create
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}
; they are known in MFT. The coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
are more interesting: they are the expectation values of the LL double-twist states in heavy backgrounds. Above the BTZ threshold, they become thermal one-point functions—see Section
8
for a discussion.

In order to find the OPE coefficients for a correlator whose form is already known, we can Taylor-expand both the correlator and each of the blocks in its OPE, and then match the Taylor coefficients on both sides order by order.
12
12

12

We could also use an orthogonality relation for Virasoro blocks or conformal partial waves
Simmons-Duffin:2017nub
, which would enable us to obtain the OPE data by integrating each block against the correlator. The results should be the same, and we leave a more detailed exploration of this approach to future work.
We begin by demonstrating how this strategy works in the case of the light double-twist amplitudes
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}
before adapting it to determine the products
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
, first in general and then at large twist.

5.1
Universal OPE Data in Mean-Field Theory

Consider the four-point function of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
in MFT. By Wick’s theorem, it is given by

G_{\mathrm{L}}(z,\bar{z})\equiv\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(\infty)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(1)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(z,\bar{z})\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{|z|^{2\Delta}}+\frac{1}{|1-z|^{2\Delta}}+1.

(59)

But we can also compute
G_{\mathrm{L}}(z,\bar{z})
using an OPE. The only primaries that appear in the OPE of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
with itself are the identity and the LL double-twists. The Virasoro blocks of these primaries reduce to global conformal blocks in the semiclassical limit, so

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{L}}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|z|^{2\Delta}}\left(1+\sum_{n\ell}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\right)^{2}\big|k_{h_{n\ell}}(z)\big|^{2}\right),\qquad k_{h}(z)=z^{h}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(h,h;2h;z\right),

(60)

where as before
h_{n\ell}=h_{[\mathrm{LL}]_{n\ell}}
with
\ell\in 2\mathbb{Z}
. Now we set (
59
) equal to (
60
) and strip off the vacuum contribution
1/|z|^{2\Delta}
from both sides: what remains is the part of
G_{\mathrm{L}}(z,\bar{z})
that is regular at
z,\bar{z}=0
. We change summation variables from
(n,\ell)
to
(m,m^{\prime})
as in (
45
) and define
p^{n}_{\ell}=\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\right)^{2}
. The vacuum-subtracted correlator becomes

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{|1-z|^{2\Delta}}=\frac{1}{|z|^{2\Delta}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1 + (-1)^{m-m'}]p^{\mathrm{min}(m,m^{\prime})}_{m-m^{\prime}}k_{\Delta+m}(z)k_{\Delta+m^{\prime}}(\bar{z}).

(61)

Next, we Taylor-expand both sides of (
61
) in powers of
z,\bar{z}
around the origin:

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}a^{(r,r^{\prime})}z^{r}\bar{z}^{r^{\prime}}=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1 + (-1)^{m-m'}]p^{\mathrm{min}(m,m^{\prime})}_{m-m^{\prime}}\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}b_{m,m^{\prime}}^{(r,r^{\prime})}z^{r}\bar{z}^{r^{\prime}},

(62)

where the Taylor coefficients
a^{(r,r^{\prime})}=\alpha^{(r)}\alpha^{(r^{\prime})}
and
b_{m,m^{\prime}}^{(r,r^{\prime})}=\beta_{m}^{(r)}\beta_{m^{\prime}}^{(r^{\prime})}
are given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{(r)}=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+r)}{\Gamma(\Delta)\Gamma(1+r)},\qquad\beta_{m}^{(r)}=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+r)^{2}\Gamma(2\Delta+2m)}{\Gamma(\Delta+m)^{2}\Gamma(1+r-m)\Gamma(2\Delta+r+m)}.

(63)

Finally, we match Taylor coefficients at each order
z^{r}\bar{z}^{r^{\prime}}
on both sides of (
62
):

\displaystyle\delta_{r,0}\delta_{r^{\prime},0}+a^{(r,r^{\prime})}=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\quantity[1 + (-1)^{m-m'}]b_{m,m^{\prime}}^{(r,r^{\prime})}\,p^{\mathrm{min}(m,m^{\prime})}_{m-m^{\prime}}.

(64)

This power-matching condition reflects the fact that multiple LL double-twist conformal blocks contribute to the regular part of the correlator at each order in
z,\bar{z}
. The nontrivial Taylor expansion of each
k_{h_{n\ell}}(z)
is what causes the products
p^{n}_{\ell}
to appear in complicated linear combinations at each order; to disentangle them, we view (
64
) as an infinite linear system, with one equation at each order
(r,r^{\prime})
, to be solved for the
p^{n}_{\ell}
.

In the simplest case
(r,r^{\prime})=(0,0)
, we are looking at the constant term in the Taylor series. From (
61
), the value of the vacuum-subtracted correlator at
z,\bar{z}=0
is 2, and the only block that contributes to the constant term is the one with
m=m^{\prime}=0
, corresponding to the composite operator
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{00}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
. Thus we find

\displaystyle 2=p^{0}_{0}=\left(\mathcal{C}_{00}^{\mathrm{LL}}\right)^{2}\implies\mathcal{C}_{00}^{\mathrm{LL}}=\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\rangle=\sqrt{2}.

(65)

This OPE coefficient reflects the correct Boltzmann counting one has to do to account for the bosonic exchange statistics that arise when combining two
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
states to form
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
. The higher coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}
should be understood as combinatorial generalizations of the Boltzmann factor which serve to properly normalize the states
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}
.

The linear system (
64
) is upper-triangular, which means it can be solved recursively by back-substitution. To see how this works, observe that the sums over
m,m^{\prime}
are all finite: they truncate when
m
reaches
r
or when
m^{\prime}
reaches
r^{\prime}
, due to the Gamma function poles in the denominator of
b^{(r,r^{\prime})}_{m,m^{\prime}}
. Therefore only finitely many of the OPE coefficients enter at each order. In fact, the set of equations one gets by truncating the expansion at order
(r,r^{\prime})
is sufficient to determine all of the OPE coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}
with
n\leq\min(r,r^{\prime})
and
|\ell|\leq|r-r^{\prime}|
. One can then increase
r
and
r^{\prime}
until the next “batch” of equations closes, solve for the remaining undetermined OPE coefficients, and repeat the process.

Remarkably, this system has a closed-form solution, first written down in
Heemskerk:2009pn
:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\right)^{2}=\quantity[1 + (-1)^{\ell}]c_{n}c_{n+\ell},\qquad c_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+n)^{2}\Gamma(2\Delta+n-1)}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}\Gamma(1+n)\Gamma(2\Delta+2n-1)}.

(66)

This can be deduced either by guessing a pattern from the first few OPE coefficients and checking that (
66
) solves (
64
), or by applying the Lorentzian inversion formula.

13

13

As an aside, another way to formulate the problem is to ask for the differential operator
\mathcal{D}_{n\ell}\sim\Box^{n}\overset{{}_{\leftrightarrow}}{\partial}{}^{\ell}
for which
[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{D}_{n\ell}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
is a properly normalized primary state. Then one may obtain the desired OPE coefficients by taking derivatives of (
59

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}=\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(\infty)[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}(1)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(0)\rangle=\mathcal{D}_{n\ell}\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(\infty)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(1)\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(z,\bar{z})\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}(0)\rangle\Big|_{z,\bar{z}=1}

(67)

This was the perspective of
Berenstein:2021pxv
, where a recursion relation for
\mathcal{D}_{n\ell}
was found and solved, in the case of SL(2), for the normalization coefficients of quasiprimaries.

5.2
Heavy-Heavy-Light OPE Coefficients

Let us return to the problem of matching the HHLL correlator
G(z,\bar{z})
to its
t
-channel OPE. This problem is similar to the one treated above: in both cases, the vacuum-subtracted correlator
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
is set equal to an infinite sum of LL double-twist Virasoro blocks and then Taylor-expanded to extract the OPE coefficients. In the present case, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(w,\overline{w})=\sum_{k\neq 0}\absolutevalue{\f{1 - \zeta^k (1-w)}{\a}}^{-2\Delta}=\frac{1}{|w|^{2\Delta}}\sum_{n\ell}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}|\alpha|^{-(2n+|\ell|)}\big|k_{h_{n\ell}}(w)\big|^{2}.

(68)

Here we have stripped off an unimportant prefactor from (
37
) and (
57
) by defining
\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(w,\overline{w})\equiv|z|^{-(\alpha-1)\Delta}G_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,\bar{z})
. Taking the same approach as in Section
5.1
, we change summation variables from
(n,\ell)
to
(m,m^{\prime})
, define
\widetilde{p}^{n}_{\ell}=\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}
, expand in powers of
w,\overline{w}
, and equate the Taylor coefficients on both sides order by order:

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(w,\overline{w})=\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{a}^{(r,r^{\prime})}w^{r}\overline{w}^{r^{\prime}}=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}|\alpha|^{-(m+m^{\prime})}\widetilde{p}^{\mathrm{min}(m,m^{\prime})}_{m-m^{\prime}}\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}b_{m,m^{\prime}}^{(r,r^{\prime})}w^{r}\overline{w}^{r^{\prime}}.

(69)

The coefficients
b_{m,m^{\prime}}^{(r,r^{\prime})}
are the same as the ones in (
63
), while the new coefficients
\widetilde{a}^{(r,r^{\prime})}
can be obtained by expanding the images
\left(1-\zeta^{k}(1-w)\right)^{-\Delta}

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}^{(r,r^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\,r^{\prime}!}\partial^{r}\overline{\partial}{}^{r^{\prime}}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(w,\overline{w})\evaluated{}_{w,\overline{w} = 0}=\sum_{k\neq 0}\widetilde{\alpha}_{k}^{(r)}\widetilde{\alpha}_{-k}^{(r^{\prime})},

(70)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}^{(r)}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\alpha|^{\Delta}\left(-\zeta^{k}\right)^{r}}{\left(1-\zeta^{k}\right)^{\Delta+r}}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+r)}{\Gamma(\Delta)\Gamma(1+r)}\right).

From (
69
), the linear system that determines the products
\widetilde{p}^{n}_{\ell}
is

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}^{(r,r^{\prime})}=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}|\alpha|^{-(m+m^{\prime})}b_{m,m^{\prime}}^{(r,r^{\prime})}\widetilde{p}^{\mathrm{min}(m,m^{\prime})}_{m-m^{\prime}}.

(71)

This matrix can, at least in principle, be inverted to yield OPE coefficients schematically of the form
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}\sim(b_{n\ell}^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{a})/\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{00}^{\mathrm{HH}}=\langle\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{H}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(0,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k\neq 0}\absolutevalue{\f{2}{\a} \sin(\pi k \a)}^{-2\Delta}.

The next few OPE coefficients are readily obtained explicitly in terms of

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{10}^{\mathrm{HH}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{02}^{\mathrm{HH}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{02}^{\mathrm{LL}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}^{(2,0)}(0,0)-\frac{\Delta(1+\Delta)^{2}}{4(1+2\Delta)}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(0,0)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{20}^{\mathrm{HH}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{4}}{\mathcal{C}_{20}^{\mathrm{LL}}}\bigg(\frac{1}{4}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}^{(2,2)}(0,0)-\frac{\Delta(1+\Delta)^{2}}{8(1+2\Delta)}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}^{(2,0)}(0,0)-\frac{(1+\Delta)^{2}}{4}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}^{(1,1)}(0,0)+\frac{\Delta^{2}(1+\Delta)^{2}}{16}\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}(0,0)\bigg),

6
Asymptotic Analysis at Large Twist

For larger values of
n
, it becomes cumbersome to evaluate the
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
analytically. However, the Virasoro blocks of LL double-twist operators with
very
large
n
simplify dramatically, and we regain analytical control over the OPE. This will lead to an asymptotic formula for the expectation values of highly excited double-twist operators.

6.1
Virasoro Blocks with Heavy Exchange

For preparation, consider the semiclassical
t
-channel blocks (
55
) at asymptotically large internal dimension,
h\gg 1
.
14
14

14

Here the large-
c
limit is taken first, at fixed
h
, before letting
h
become large. In other words, we take the limits
h\longrightarrow\infty
and
c\longrightarrow\infty
, but with
h/c\longrightarrow 0
, so that the internal operator is still “light.”
A classic result of Watson on the asymptotic behavior of hypergeometric functions at large parameters
Luke1969
states that

\displaystyle k_{h}(w)=w^{h}{}_{2}F_{1}(h,h;2h;w)\sim\frac{(4\rho)^{h}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\qquad\mathrm{as}\;\,h\longrightarrow\infty,

(75)

where
\rho
is the “radial coordinate” defined by

\displaystyle w=\frac{4\rho}{\left(1+\rho\right)^{2}}\iff\rho=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-w}}{1+\sqrt{1-w}},\qquad|\rho|<1.

(76)

The transformation
w\mapsto\rho(w)
maps the cut plane
\mathbb{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus{}_{{}_{\!\!\!\!}}\scriptstyle\setminus$}}}[1,\infty)
to the unit disk. It was first studied in
Pappadopulo:2012jk
;
Hogervorst:2013sma
, where its use improved the convergence rate of certain OPEs.

It follows from (
75
) that the blocks (
55
) have the following asymptotics:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{h}^{(\mathrm{t})}(z)\sim z^{(\alpha-1)h_{\mathrm{L}}}\frac{\left(1+\rho\right)^{4h_{\mathrm{L}}}}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\left(\frac{4\rho}{\alpha}\right)^{h-2h_{\mathrm{L}}}\qquad\mathrm{as}\;\,h\longrightarrow\infty.

(77)

Evidently, at large
h
the semiclassical HHLL blocks reorganize into scaling blocks in the radial variable
\rho
. In this new coordinate, no descendants are excited at all, and the OPE (
68
) turns into an ordinary power series in
\rho
. This fact will allow us to carry out our power-matching procedure much more directly than in the case of arbitrary
h
.

Since we want both the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic blocks to behave like (
77
), it is natural to take the twist
\tau=\min(h,\bar{h})
to be large, rather than either
h
or
\bar{h}
. In the case of LL double-twist exchange, we have
\tau=\Delta+n
, so (
77
) is an asymptotic formula either in the hefty regime
1\ll\Delta\ll c
or in the highly excited regime
1\ll n\ll c
.

6.2
Darboux’s Method: Preliminaries

Our next step is to reformulate the matching problem (
68
) in terms of
\rho,\bar{\rho}
at large twist. We substitute the asymptotic Virasoro blocks (
77
) into the LL double-twist OPE, change variables
(n,\ell)\longrightarrow(m,m^{\prime})

7.2

8
Thermal One-Point Functions in BTZ

In 2d CFT, conformal invariance forbids any nontrivial primary operator
\mathcal{O}
from acquiring a nonzero expectation value if the theory lives on a cylinder. On a torus, however, there is no such restriction. Thermal one-point functions like
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\beta}
can be nontrivial in a CFT with a spatial a circle, but they must vanish at zero temperature because the thermal circle decompactifies as
\beta\longrightarrow\infty
. It follows from modular invariance that
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\beta}
must also rapidly decay to zero at high temperatures. The bulk analog of this argument is that the horizon of a very large BTZ black hole effectively becomes planar. At infinite volume, the bulk geometry is just a solid cylinder, which again forces all one-point functions to vanish.

8.1
The Cylinder and the Torus

We have arrived at essentially the same conclusions from a Lorentzian perspective: instead of working on the torus, we have modeled thermal states by insertions of a heavy CFT primary on the cylinder. In our treatment, the coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}=\matrixelement{\O_{\up{H}}}{[\O_{\up{L}} \O_{\up{L}}]_{n\ell}}{\O_{\up{H}}}
compute expectation values in highly excited states. But above the BTZ threshold, heavy operators act as effective thermal backgrounds
Fitzpatrick:2015zha
, so we can also view the
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
as thermal one-point functions
\langle[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}\rangle_{\beta}
at a temperature set by the conformal dimension of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{H}}
. This interpretation is supported by Figure
9
, which confirms that—as expected for thermal one-point functions—the
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
quickly decay to zero at high temperature.

) represent subdominant one-point functions: they arise only if one holds the bulk topology fixed while varying the temperature, forcing the thermal circle to stay contractible. This is how one obtains massless BTZ, rather than pure AdS, as a zero-temperature limit.

Our choice to work on the cylinder rather than the torus comes with advantages and disadvantages. On one hand, it affords a unified treatment of expectation values in heavy states that analytically continues across the BTZ threshold, and it allows us to describe how thermal behavior arises in black holes without the need to add a thermal circle to the analysis by hand. On the other hand, we have seen that it has the potential to obscure some of the low-temperature physics. Nevertheless, we needed to understand the BTZ one-point functions at all temperatures to get a complete picture of how they arise by analytic continuation from expectation values in pure excited states.

To be clear, the present discussion is specific to the canonical ensemble. We have been working microcanonically, where there is no Hawking–Page transition and therefore no problem with our expectation values. Our aim here is only to point out that the low-temperature behavior of our expectation values should be interpreted with some care.

9
Discussion and Future Directions

Summary of main results.

In this paper, we discussed holographic correlators in heavy
\mathrm{AdS}_{3}
backgrounds and identified an infinite family of composite operators that have nontrivial expectation values in these backgrounds. We derived the propagator for free scalar fields in the bulk by solving the wave equation and summing over modes of the field, as well as by the method of images. On the CFT side, we studied an HHLL four-point function where the heavy insertions create a massive particle or a BTZ black hole in the bulk. By matching its expansion in Virasoro blocks to our bulk results, we obtained the spectrum and OPE data of all states that contribute to the
s
-channel and
t
-channel OPEs.

The OPE coefficients that couple light intermediate states to the heavy insertions represent their expectation values in heavy backgrounds. We determined these expectation values exactly, discussed them in several approximate and asymptotic forms, and described their behavior as the mass of the heavy state varies across the BTZ threshold. We found that they are finite and analytic at the threshold, but become exponentially suppressed for large black holes. In black hole backgrounds, the expectation values are identified with thermal one-point functions on the torus, although below the Hawking–Page temperature they are dominated by the corresponding one-point functions in thermal AdS.

Thermality without temperature.

One broad conclusion of our work is that the physics of conical defects contains hints of thermal behavior, even below the BTZ threshold. From Figure
3
, we see that long-range correlations in conical AdS become suppressed as the mass of the defect increases: this effect resembles the shortening of thermal correlation lengths as the temperature or energy increases. Some other indications of a transition to thermality include the analytic continuation of the metric between conical AdS and BTZ, the phase transition in the linear response function, and the expectation values that analytically continue into BTZ one-point functions.

Much of this discussion is related to our decision to work with individual operator insertions rather than averaged quantities. Our results can be viewed as a realization of the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH), which is often used to argue that averaged OPE coefficients in thermal states are nearly identical to individual OPE coefficients in pure states with the same energy
Srednicki:1994mfb
;
Collier:2019weq
. It would be interesting to sharpen what the ETH says about the extent to which we can treat black holes as CFT primaries.

Beyond the vacuum block.

Vacuum dominance is a powerful phenomenon that often allows one to focus exclusively on the Virasoro vacuum block. We have tried to emphasize in this paper that the analytic structure of holographic correlators encodes important physics—present even at leading order in the semiclassical limit—that the vacuum block cannot see. The vacuum contribution correctly predicts the singular structure of four-point functions in the OPE limit, but there are additional regular terms in the OPE that are necessary to preserve crossing symmetry and which have physical interpretations as quantum hair, or nontrivial expectation values of double-trace operators in heavy states. To our knowledge, this careful accounting of non-vacuum
t
-channel exchanges represents the first detailed calculation of double-trace expectation values in AdS/CFT.

Comparison to Cardyology.

The expectation values we have computed have been studied from a different perspective by Kraus and Maloney
Kraus:2016nwo
. They used modular invariance to derive a universal formula for the averaged three-point coefficients
\overline{\matrixelement{E}{\cal{O}}{E}}
, where
\mathcal{O}
is a CFT primary and
E
is the energy of an excited state. They argued that such three-point functions are generated in the bulk by a cubic coupling
\phi_{\chi}^{2}\phi_{\mathcal{O}}
in the action between the fields dual to
\mathcal{O}
and the lightest nontrivial primary
\chi
for which
\matrixelement{\chi}{\O}{\chi}\neq 0
. The corresponding Witten diagram computation, shown in Figure
10(a)
, suggests that these three-point coefficients arise from geodesics (or, more precisely, propagators) that wind nontrivially around the horizon of the black hole when
\mathcal{O}
splits into two
\chi
fields.

Our work provides an explicit realization of this story in the case where the composite operators
\mathcal{O}=[\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}]_{n\ell}
acquire expectation values by coupling to the light field
\chi=\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
through the OPE coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}=\matrixelement{\chi}{\O}{\chi}
. Here the cubic coupling arises naturally (because
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{LL}}
allows two copies of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{L}}
to fuse into a composite state), rather than being put in by hand. It should be noted that all of our operator insertions lie on the boundary, so we have no explicit cubic vertices in the bulk, leading to the diagram in Figure
10(b)
. Nevertheless, our results for the expectation values
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}=\matrixelement{\O_{\up{H}}}{[\O_{\up{L}} \O_{\up{L}}]_{n\ell}}{\O_{\up{H}}}
agree with the Kraus–Maloney formula for
\overline{\matrixelement{E}{\cal{O}}{E}}
, and our analysis extends the intuition of winding geodesics to geometries with no horizon. In our case, this bulk interpretation of three-point coefficients doubles as a CFT interpretation of non-minimal bulk geodesics: their role is played on the boundary by the exchange of light double-trace operators.

Multiparticle wave functions.

It is natural to ask whether the wave functions of the HL and LL double-twist primaries can be extended into the bulk. On the boundary, these wave functions are the scaling parts of the Virasoro blocks (

By analogy with our analysis on the boundary, we expect that all of the information necessary to compute wave functions and expectation values in the bulk is contained in the regular part of the bulk-to-bulk propagator. Connections to the bootstrap.

Several parallels between our work and the conformal bootstrap deserve investigation. It would be instructive to compute the
\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HH}}
using the orthogonality of conformal partial waves
Simmons-Duffin:2017nub
, i.e. using Lorentzian inversion. It would also be good to make closer contact with related work on spacetime bananas
Abajian:2023jye
, universal dynamics at finite central charge
Collier:2019weq
, wormholes and ensemble averaging
Chandra:2022bqq
, thermal one-point functions
Grinberg:2020fdj
;
David:2022nfn
, and the thermal bootstrap program
Iliesiu:2018fao
;
Alday:2020eua
.

In higher dimensions, there has been great progress on bootstrapping universal OPE data in the stress tensor sector of HHLL correlators
Fitzpatrick:2019zqz
;
Fitzpatrick:2019efk
;
Kulaxizi:2019tkd
. The prospect of extending our work to higher dimensions by using these results is exciting, but also challenging. A closed form for the stress tensor contributions to
G(z,\bar{z})
is not known; moreover, in the bulk, there are local curvature effects and one generically loses the method of images. So it is not immediately clear how to write down the regular and singular parts of
G(z,\bar{z})
.

Future work.

Our analysis can be extended by adding charge or spin to the light or heavy states: see
li2024spinning
for a study of spinning conical defects. It would also be interesting to compute off-diagonal matrix elements
\matrixelement{E}{\cal{O}}{E'}
for the LL double-twists and compare the results to
Brehm:2018ipf
. It should then be straightforward to compute generic transition amplitudes of the form
\matrixelement{E, J}{\O_1 \, \O_2}{E', J'}
, where
\ket{E,J}
represents a heavy state with energy
E
and angular momentum
J
, and where
\mathcal{O}_{1}
,
\mathcal{O}_{2}
are light operators.

In this work have restricted ourselves to the strict limit of large
c
, but subleading corrections to the Virasoro blocks are well understood
Perlmutter:2015iya
, and the backreaction of free fields in heavy states has also been studied
Belin:2021htw
;
Emparan:2022ijy
. (In the bulk, backreaction causes a horizon to form, creating a “quantum BTZ” geometry.) So in principle, it should be possible to piece together a more refined perturbative understanding of our results.

It may be possible to employ methods similar to ours to study a model of black hole formation. Following the ideas of
Anous:2016kss
;
Haehl:2023lfo
, one could imagine colliding two light particles or shock waves, sent in at early times, in a two-sided geometry. If they are highly boosted (i.e. highly excited descendants), their center-of-mass energy could exceed the BTZ threshold and a black hole could form
Gott:1990zr
. It would be illuminating to find a CFT interpretation of this process, and to connect it to out-of-time-ordered correlators and quantum chaos.

On the boundary, the operator dual to
\phi_{n\ell}
is obtained by rescaling the bulk wave function by
r^{\Delta}
as we take the limit
r\longrightarrow\infty
to keep it finite. From (
115
), we obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{n\ell}(t,\theta)=\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\Delta}\phi_{n\ell}(t,\theta,r)=\big(\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}B(\omega,\ell)\big)e^{-i\omega_{n\ell}t}e^{i\ell\theta}.

(123)

Combining (

) with (

), we obtain the boundary amplitudes

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\right)^{2}\equiv\big(\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}B(\omega,\ell)\big)^{2}=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+n)\Gamma(\Delta+n+|\ell|)}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}\Gamma(1+n)\Gamma(1+n+|\ell|)}=\mathcal{G}(\omega_{n\ell},\ell).

(124)

As indicated in the main text, this is the momentum-space Green’s function in pure AdS, evaluated at the normal frequencies. As a meromorphic function of
\omega
, it reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\omega,\ell)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(\Delta+\omega+|\ell|)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(\Delta+\omega-|\ell|)\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(2-\Delta+\omega+|\ell|)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(2-\Delta+\omega-|\ell|)\right)}.

(125)

A.3
Below the BTZ Threshold

The analysis in conical AdS is nearly identical to the analysis in pure AdS, differing only by certain factors of
N
. See

for a more detailed exposition.

The solution to the radial equation (

Its asymptotic behavior near infinity is given by (

\displaystyle A(\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{\Delta-2-N(|\ell|+\omega)}\Gamma(\Delta-1)\Gamma\big(1+N|\ell|\big)}{\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta+N(|\ell|-\omega)\big){}_{\!}\Big)\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta+N(|\ell|+\omega)\big){}_{\!}\Big)},

(127a)

\displaystyle B(\omega,\ell)

(127b)

The normal mode spectrum resulting from the normalizability condition

\displaystyle A(\omega,\ell)=0\implies\omega=\omega_{n\ell}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\Delta+2n\right)+|\ell|,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},\;\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

and

\displaystyle\big\|\phi_{n\ell}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{n\ell}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r\,\differential r}{\left(r^{2}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right)}\big|R_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}(r)\big|^{2}

(130)

\displaystyle=\big|\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}\big|^{2}N^{-2\Delta-4n-4N|\ell|-1}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+n)\Gamma(1+n)\Gamma(1+N|\ell|)^{2}}{\Gamma(\Delta+n+N|\ell|)\Gamma(1+n+N|\ell|)}\right)

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}\big|^{2}=N^{2\Delta+4n+4N|\ell|+1}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+n+N|\ell|)\Gamma(1+n+N|\ell|)}{\Gamma(\Delta+n)\Gamma(1+n)\Gamma(1+N|\ell|)^{2}}\right),

(131)

and the corresponding boundary amplitudes (compare (

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\right)^{2}=\big(\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{n\ell},\ell}B(\omega_{n\ell},\ell)\big)^{2}=\frac{N^{1-2\Delta}}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\Delta+n+N|\ell|)\Gamma(\Delta+n)}{\Gamma(1+n+N|\ell|)\,\Gamma(1+n)}=\mathcal{G}(\omega_{n\ell},\ell).

This gives the momentum-space Green’s function in conical AdS,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\omega,\ell)=\frac{N^{1-2\Delta}}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta+N(\omega+\ell)\big){}_{\!}\Big)\,\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta+N(\omega-\ell)\big){}_{\!}\Big)}{\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(2-\Delta+N(\omega+\ell)\big){}_{\!}\Big)\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big(2-\Delta+N(\omega-\ell)\big){}_{\!}\Big)}.

in agreement with (

A.4
Above the BTZ Threshold

In black hole backgrounds, we are interested in the retarded propagator. Accordingly, we impose ingoing boundary conditions at the horizon in addition to decay at infinity. With these conditions, the solutions to (
112
) become rather messy. It is therefore prudent to introduce the “planar” coordinate
z=1-\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)^{2}
, which puts the horizon at
z=0
and the AdS boundary at
z=1
. The radial equation transforms into

\displaystyle z(1-z)\derivative[2]{R_{\w,\ell}}{z}+(1-z)\derivative{R_{\w,\ell}}{z}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}/r_{0}^{2}}{z}-\frac{\Delta(\Delta-2)}{1-z}-\ell^{2}/r_{0}^{2}\right)R_{\omega,\ell}=0,

(134)

and the ingoing boundary conditions (
15

\displaystyle R_{\omega\ell}(z)\sim\begin{cases}\displaystyle z^{-\frac{i\omega}{2r_{0}}},&z\longrightarrow 0\quad\;(\text{BTZ horizon})\\
\displaystyle\left(1-z\right)^{\Delta/2},&z\longrightarrow 1\quad(\text{AdS boundary})\end{cases}

(135)

The solution to (

) that is purely ingoing at the horizon is
26
26

26

To lighten the notation, we will take
\ell\geq 0
throughout. Absolute values around
\ell

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\omega,\ell}\left(1-z\right)^{\Delta/2}z^{-\frac{i\omega}{2r_{0}}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}(\ell+\omega)}{2},\frac{\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}(\omega-\ell)}{2};1-\frac{i\omega}{r_{0}};z\right)

(136)

Near the AdS boundary, the ingoing solutions (

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(z)

\displaystyle r_{0}^{2-\Delta}A(\omega,\ell)

(138a)

\displaystyle r_{0}^{\Delta}B(\omega,\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}^{\Delta}\Gamma(1-\Delta)\Gamma\left(1-\frac{i\omega}{r_{0}}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(2-\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}\left(\omega+\ell\right)\right)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(2-\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}\left(\omega-\ell\right)\right)\right)}.

To respect the boundary condition at infinity, we must take

\displaystyle A(\omega,\ell)=0\implies\omega=\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}=-\ell-ir_{0}\left(\Delta+2n\right),\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},\;\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(139)

The modes themselves can once again be expressed in terms of Jacobi polynomials:

\displaystyle R_{\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell},\ell}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell},\ell}\left(1-z\right)^{\Delta/2}z^{-\frac{i\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}}{2r_{0}}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-n,-n+\frac{i\ell}{r_{0}};1-\Delta-2n+\frac{i\ell}{r_{0}};z\right)

(140)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell},\ell}\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+n)\Gamma\big(1-\frac{i\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}}{r_{0}}\big)}{\Gamma\big(1+n-\frac{i\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}}{r_{0}}\big)}\right)\left(1-z\right)^{\Delta/2}z^{-\frac{i\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}}{2r_{0}}}P^{\big(-\frac{i\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}}{r_{0}},\,\Delta-1\big)}_{n}(1-2z).

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}(\omega,\ell)\propto\frac{r_{0}^{2\Delta-2}\Gamma(1-\Delta)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}\left(\omega+\ell\right)\right)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}\left(\omega-\ell\right)\right)\right)}{\Gamma(\Delta-1)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(2-\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}\left(\omega+\ell\right)\right)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(2-\Delta-\frac{i}{r_{0}}\left(\omega-\ell\right)\right)\right)}.

As described in Section

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{R}}(t,\theta)=\sum_{n\ell}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\big)^{2}e^{-i\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}t}e^{i\ell\theta},\qquad\textbf{}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{n\ell}^{\mathrm{HL}}\big)^{2}\equiv i\,\mathrm{Res}\big[\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{R}}(\omega,\ell),\,\widetilde{\omega}_{n\ell}\big].

These residues can be computed directly from (

A.5
The Case of Massless BTZ

The massless BTZ (mBTZ) geometry is a somewhat singular limit of both conical AdS (
N\longrightarrow\infty
) and BTZ (
r_{0}\longrightarrow 0
). The hypergeometric solutions to the wave equation, both above and below the BTZ threshold, become ill-defined in mBTZ, and it is not immediately obvious which boundary conditions one should impose at the origin. The metric is

\displaystyle\differential s^{2}=-r^{2}\differential t^{2}+\frac{\differential r^{2}}{r^{2}}+r^{2}\differential\theta^{2},

(144)

and the radial equation (
112
) in mBTZ takes the form

\displaystyle\quantity[\f{1}{r} \dv{}{r} \p{r^3 \dv{}{r}} + \f{\W^2}{r^2} - \Delta(\Delta- 2)]R_{\omega,\ell}(r)=0,\qquad\Omega^{2}\equiv\omega^{2}-\ell^{2}.

(145)

By a change of variables, this differential equation can be brought to the form of the Bessel equation. The Bessel equation is a degenerate limit of the hypergeometric equation: it has one regular singular point (at the AdS boundary) and one irregular singular point (at the origin), as opposed to the three regular singularities of the hypergeometric equation. This structure suggests that we should still be able to demand algebraic decay of the solutions at infinity, but that near
r=0
they could behave rather erratically.

The general solution to (
145
) is given by

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(r)=\frac{\Omega}{2r}\quantity[A(\w,\ell) J_{1-\Delta}\p{\f{\W}{r}} + B(\w,\ell) J_{\Delta- 1}\p{\f{\W}{r}}],

(146)

where
J_{\nu}(x)
is a Bessel function of the first kind, and
A(\omega,\ell)
and
B(\omega,\ell)
are constants analogous to those in (
115
) and (
137
). Since
J_{\nu}(z)\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}
as
z\longrightarrow 0
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(r)\sim\frac{A(\omega,\ell)}{\Gamma(2-\Delta)}\left(\frac{2r}{\Omega}\right)^{\Delta-2}+\frac{B(\omega,\ell)}{\Gamma(\Delta)}\left(\frac{2r}{\Omega}\right)^{-\Delta}\quad\mathrm{as}\;\,r\longrightarrow\infty.

(147)

In order to ensure that
R_{\omega,\ell}(r)\sim r^{-\Delta}
at infinity, we must set
A(\omega,\ell)=0
. As for the behavior near the origin, the large-argument asymptotics of
J_{\nu}(z)
give

\displaystyle R_{\omega,\ell}(r)\sim\frac{B(\omega,\ell)}{2}e^{-\Delta\pi i/2}\sqrt{\frac{i\Omega}{2\pi r}}\quantity[e^{i\W/r} - ie^{\Delta\pi i} e^{-i\W/r}]\quad\mathrm{as}\;\,r\longrightarrow 0.

(148)

From this behavior, we see that there is no way to make the modes regular, purely ingoing, or purely outgoing at the origin. The best we can do is to admit the boundary condition (
148
), conceding that all normalizable solutions to (
145
) will behave pathologically near the singularity. Since there is no constraint on
\omega
, the spectrum will be continuous. We will, however, demand that
\omega^{2}>\ell^{2}
, so that
\Omega
is real and positive. This will at least force the solutions to oscillate near the origin instead of blowing up exponentially.
28
28

28

The case
\omega=\ell
leads to the normalizable modes
R_{\ell}(r)=B(\ell)r^{-\Delta}
. These behave worse at the origin than the
\omega^{2}>\ell^{2}
modes, which go like
r^{-1/2}
, but better than the exponentially divergent
\omega^{2}<\ell^{2}
modes.

Despite the problems at
r=0
, it is nevertheless possible to normalize the modes

\displaystyle\phi_{\omega,\ell}(t,\theta,r)=B(\omega,\ell)\frac{\Omega}{2r}J_{\Delta-1}\left(\frac{\Omega}{r}\right)e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\ell\theta}

(149)

in the Klein–Gordon inner product (
118
). We have
\sqrt{g_{\Sigma}}=1
and
\hat{n}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{r}\delta^{\mu,t}
; as before, the
\theta
integral gives
2\pi\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
, but we drop the
2\pi
. The inner product reduces to

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\omega,\ell},\phi_{\omega^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\left(\omega+\omega^{\prime}\right)\frac{\Omega\Omega^{\prime}}{4}B(\omega,\ell)B^{*}(\omega^{\prime},\ell)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential r}{r^{3}}J_{\Delta-1}\left(\frac{\Omega}{r}\right)J_{\Delta-1}^{*}\left(\frac{\Omega^{\prime}}{r}\right),

(150)

where
\Omega^{2}=\omega^{2}-\ell^{2}
and
\Omega^{\prime 2}=\omega^{\prime 2}-\ell^{2}
. To evaluate the integral, we change variables to
z=\frac{1}{r}
and use the orthogonality relation for Bessel functions, which reads

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential z\,zJ_{\Delta-1}(\Omega z)J_{\Delta-1}(\Omega^{\prime}z)=\frac{\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})}{\Omega}.

(151)

It follows that our modes are delta-function normalizable, with amplitudes given by

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\omega,\ell},\phi_{\omega^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}\rangle=\frac{\big|B(\omega,\ell)\big|^{2}}{2}\omega\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\ell^{2}}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\implies\big|B(\omega,\ell)\big|^{2}=\frac{2}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\ell^{2}}}.

(152)

The bulk-to-bulk propagator is then constructed by integrating over modes:

\displaystyle G(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\ell}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,R_{\omega,\ell}(r)R_{\omega,\ell}(r^{\prime})e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\ell\theta}.

(153)

Finally, we pass to the AdS boundary using (
147
) and obtain the propagator:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\Delta}R_{\omega,\ell}(r)=\frac{B(\omega,\ell)}{\Gamma(\Delta)}\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{\Delta}\implies G(t,\theta)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\Delta)^{2}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\ell}^{\infty}\frac{\differential\omega}{\omega}\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2\Delta-1}e^{-i\omega t}e^{i\ell\theta}.

(154)

Very Light Conical Defects
