Abstract
. We study a lattice point counting problem for spheres arising from the Heisenberg groups. In particular, we prove an upper bound on the number of points on and near large dilates of the unit spheres generated by the anisotropic norms ‖ ( z , t ) ‖ α = ( | z | α + | t | α / 2 ) 1 / α \|(z,t)\|_{\alpha}=(\left|z\right|^{\alpha}+\left|t\right|^{\alpha/2})^{1/\alpha} for α ≥ 2 \alpha\geq 2 . As a first step, we reduce our counting problem to one of bounding an energy integral. The primary new challenges that arise are the presence of vanishing curvature and uneven dilations. In the process, we establish bounds on the Fourier transform of the surface measures arising from these norms. Further, we utilize the techniques developed here to estimate the number of lattice points in the intersection of two such surfaces.



1.
Introduction

Estimating the number of integer lattice points that are in, on, and near convex surfaces is a classic subject in number theory, harmonic analysis, and related areas of mathematics.
The Gauss circle problem is a time honored example.
It concerns approximating the number of integer lattice points in a large dilate of the unit disc in terms relative to the area of the disk:

(1.1)

\#\left(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\left|x\right|\leq R\}\right)=\pi R^{2}+E(r),

where

\#(\cdot)
denotes the size of a finite set.
It is conjectured that the best bound for the error is
|E(R)|\lesssim R^{1/2+\epsilon}
.

x\lesssim y
means that there exists some constant
C
such that
x\leq Cy
, and

x\sim y
means that both
x\lesssim y
and

.

In 2003, Huxley
[
16
]
utilized advanced exponential sum estimates to show that
|E(R)|\lesssim R^{\frac{131}{208}}
. More recently, Bourgain and Watt improved this slightly to
|E(R)|\lesssim R^{\frac{517}{824}}
in
[
3
]
, building on their work in
[

]
.

Variants of the Gauss circle problem in which circles are replaced by more intricate surfaces have attracted much attention over the years (see, for instance,
[
1
,
6
,
19
,
24
,
33
]
).
Notably, Lettington studied the number of integer lattice points near smooth surfaces with non-vanishing curvature (
[
24
]

He established that for
d\geq 3
,
\delta>0
,

(1.2)

\#\left(\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\ :R\leq\|k\|_{B}\leq R+\delta\}\right)\leq C\max\{R^{d-2+\frac{2}{d+1}},R^{d-1}\delta\},

where
C>0
is a universal constant and

\|x\|_{B}=\inf\{t>0:x\in tB\}.

This result was previously known to hold when
d=2
(see the discussion in
[

, Introduction]
), and is known to be sharp (see the discussion in
[
17
]
).
A simple Fourier analytic proof of Lettington’s result, as well as an extension to a variable coefficient setting, was provided by Iosevich and the second listed author in
[
17
]
.

The work in
[
17
]
, as well as Lettington’s original result, are contributions towards a conjecture of W. Schmidt
[
33
]
.
This conjecture concerns the number of lattice points on a given surface with non-vanishing curvature, and states that if B
\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d\geq 3
, is a symmetric convex body with a smooth boundary that has non-vanishing Gaussian curvature, then for any
\epsilon>0
,

\#\left(\{R\partial B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}\right)\leq C_{\epsilon}R^{d-2+\epsilon}.

A more complex problem arises when we consider lattice point counting problems for surfaces with points of vanishing curvature.
This includes error estimates in the case of super spheres
[
21
,

]
and
\ell^{p}

[

See also

An example more specific to this paper arises from the

Heisenberg norm balls, defined by

(1.3)

B_{R}^{\alpha,A}:=\big\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\textsf{ x }\mathbb{R}:|z|^{\alpha}+A|t|^{\alpha/2}\leq R^{\alpha}\big\}.

Depending on which value of

The Heisenberg norm balls were considered by

1.1.
Main Result

In this paper, we provide an upper bound on the number of lattice points
near the surfaces of the Heisenberg norm balls.
These surfaces present an interesting evolution in lattice point counting problems due to their points of vanishing curvature.
Before stating our main result, we give a brief overview of the Heisenberg group: we refer to
[
10
, Chapter 1]
or
[
35
, Chapter 12]
for a deeper treatment.

The
Heisenberg group
is defined to be

\mathsf{H}_{d}=\ \mathbb{R}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{R}=\{(z,t):\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},t\in\mathbb{R}\},

where
multiplication is defined
by

(x,y,t)\cdot(u,v,s)=\big(x+u,y+v,t+s-\frac{1}{2}\big(\langle x,v\rangle-\langle u,y\rangle\big)\big),

for
x,y,u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\langle x,v\rangle
is the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 1
.

The
Heisenberg norm
is defined by the following family of gauge functions on the Heisenberg group

(1.4)

\|(z,t)\|_{\alpha,A}=\big(\left|z\right|^{\alpha}+A\left|t\right|^{\alpha/2}\big)^{1/\alpha},

for
\alpha,A>0
, and where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},t\in\mathbb{R}
and
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
When
\alpha=4
, this anisotropic norm is more commonly referred to as the
Cygan

[
7
,
8
]
and
Korányi

[
20
]
norm. For simplicity, we will assume
A=1
.

A useful manipulation of elements of the Heisenberg group is the
Heisenberg dilation
, which is a class of functions indexed by
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and defined as
\varphi_{a}:(z,t)\mapsto(az,a^{2}t)
. These dilations describe an automorphism group of
\mathsf{H}_{d}
through which a notion of homogeneity on the group is understood.

We note that the Heisenberg groups are examples of non-abelian Lie groups, and as such play an active role in modern harmonic analysis. A number of recent results have appeared investigating mapping properties of maximal averaging operators associated with the Heisenberg groups. See, for instance,
[
32
]
and the references therein.

With these concepts in tow, we define the
Heisenberg sphere
(or Korányi sphere) of radius
R
and index
\alpha

by

(1.5)

\partial B_{R}^{\alpha}=\left\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}:\|(z,t)\|_{\alpha}=R\right\}.

A particularly interesting feature of these surfaces of revolution is that they have points of maximal vanishing curvature when
\alpha>2
; this is discussed further in Section
3
.

Our main result on the number of lattice points on and near the Heisenberg spheres follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
\alpha\geq 2
be integers, and set
n=2d+1
. For
(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}
, let
\|(z,t)\|_{\alpha}
be the Heisenberg norm defined by

\|(z,t)\|_{\alpha}=\big(\left|z\right|^{\alpha}+\left|t\right|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Then

(1.6)

\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:R-\delta\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)\lesssim\begin{cases}\max\{R^{\,n-\frac{1}{(\alpha-1)}},R^{n}\delta\},\text{ if }\alpha\geq 2(n-1),\\
\max\{R^{n-\frac{1}{(2n-3)}},R^{n}\delta\},\!\text{ if }2<\alpha<2(n-1),\\
\max\{R^{n-\frac{(n-2)}{n}},R^{n}\delta\},\,\text{ if }\alpha=2,\\
\end{cases}

for
0\leq\delta<1
and
R>1
.

In Section
5
, we will see that Theorem
1.1
is sharp, meaning that (
1.6
) is an equality, for all
\alpha
provided
\delta
obeys a lower bound dependent on
\alpha
and
n
.

We now proceed by giving some further context to Theorem
1.1
with some commentary and comparisons to known literature. We begin by observing a simple trivial bound.

1.1.1.
Comparison of Theorem
1.1
to a trivial bound

For large values of
R
,
the left-hand-side of (
1.6
) is bounded trivially by

\left|B_{(R+2)}^{\alpha}\right|-\left|B_{(R-2)}^{\alpha}\right|,

where

|\cdot|
is used to denote the Euclidean volume and
B_{R}^{\alpha}
is
a dilate by
R
of the unit ball under the map
\varphi_{R}:(z,t)\rightarrow(Rz,R^{2}t)
.
As such,
|B_{R}^{\alpha}|=R^{2d+2}|B_{1}^{\alpha}|
, which is evident upon noting that

\displaystyle|B_{R}^{\alpha}|

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{R}\ :\ |z|^{\alpha}+|t|^{\alpha/2}\leq R^{\alpha}\right\}}dzdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{R}\ :\left|\frac{z}{R}\right|^{\alpha}+\left|\frac{t}{R^{2}}\right|^{\alpha/2}\leq 1\right\}}dzdt.

Making the change of variables,
w=\frac{z}{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
s=\frac{t}{R^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}
, yields

\displaystyle|B_{R}^{\alpha}|

\displaystyle=R^{2d+2}\int_{\left\{(w,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{R}\ :\ |w|^{\alpha}+|s|^{\alpha/2}=1\right\}}dzdt

\displaystyle=R^{2d+2}|B_{1}^{\alpha}|.

In conclusion, the expression on the left-hand-side of (
1.6
) is bounded trivially by a constant multiple of
R^{2d+1}=R^{n}
, and our bound is an improvement for all
\alpha
.

1.1.2.
Comparison of Theorem
1.1
to Iosevich-Taylor’s
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]

The first step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
consists of transforming our lattice point counting problem into one of bounding an energy integral of a fractal-like set. This idea was
introduced by Iosevich and Taylor in
[
17
]
.
However,
due to the irregular scaling inherent to the geometry of the Heisenberg norms, the arising energy integral in our context is more complex than those handled in
[
17
]
.
Consequently, a much more delicate analysis is required, as well as
decay estimates on the Fourier transforms of the surface measures associated with the Heisenberg norms.

Though it is possible to expand the method in
[
17
]
to surfaces with vanishing curvature–as the first listed author does in her thesis
[
5
, Proposition 1.3.5]
–the Heisenberg spheres fall outside its scope.
In more detail, Campolongo’s extension of
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
does not apply to the Heisenberg spheres to prove Theorem
1.1
.
Indeed, in the language of
[
5
, Proposition 1.3.5]
or
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
,
set
\alpha_{i}=a
for each
i=1,\dots,(n-1)
and
\alpha_{n}=2a
, where
a=\frac{n}{(n+1)}
.
Observing that in order to apply this proposition we must have

2a\leq\frac{n}{n-\gamma}
,
where
\gamma
is the decay exponent on the Fourier side for the surface,
we see it is necessary that
n-\gamma\leq\frac{n}{2a}
, which is equivalent to
\frac{n-1}{2}\leq\gamma
.
Since the Heisenberg spheres
have points of vanishing curvature, such a lower bound on
\gamma
is not possible. In particular, we show in Proposition
2.4
that for any given
\alpha\geq 3
, the decay is
\gamma=\min\!\left\{\frac{n-1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{2}\right\}<\frac{n-1}{2}
for
n\geq 3
, and for
\alpha=2
that
\gamma=\frac{n-2}{2}<\frac{n-1}{2}
, thus excluding such an application.

1.1.3.
Comparison of Theorem
1.1
to Garg-Nevo-Taylor’s
[
11
]

As a final comment on Theorem
1.1
,
in Section
5.2
we consider an alternate way to bound the lattice point count based on the work of
[
11
]
. This alternate method provides an improved bound for small values of
\delta>0
, and this result is stated in Proposition
5.2
. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
,
on the other hand, offers a more direct approach in that it does not rely on the work in
[
11
]
and all oscillatory integrals are computed directly without the use of Bessel functions.

1.2.
Intersections of Heisenberg norm balls

We finish this section by considering a lattice point count near the intersection of two Heisenberg spheres (
1.5
).
For
0<\|p\|_{\alpha}\leq CR
fixed, we are interested in bounding

(1.7)

\#\big(\big\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:|\|m\|_{\alpha}-R|\leq\delta\text{ and }|\|m-p\|_{\alpha}-R|\leq\delta\big\}\big).

For a typical
p
, this will be the number of lattice points near the intersection of two Koryani spheres, and this intersection is maximized when
p
is near the origin. The following average bound follows as an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.2

(Intersecting Heisenberg Spheres)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
\alpha\geq 2
be integers, set
n=2d+1
, and
let
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
be the Heisenberg norm defined by

\|(z,t)\|_{\alpha}=\big(\left|z\right|^{\alpha}+\left|t\right|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Then for
R>1

and
0\leq\delta<1
,

\displaystyle\#\big(\{(m,p)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,\mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}^{n}\!:|\|m

\displaystyle\|_{\alpha}\!-R|\!\leq\delta\text{ and }|\|m-p\|_{\alpha}\!-R|\!\leq\delta\}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\{R^{2\left(n-\frac{\gamma(\alpha)}{n-1-\gamma(\alpha)}\right)},\left(R^{n}\delta\right)^{2}\},

where
\gamma(\alpha)=\min\!\left\{\frac{n-1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{2}\right\}
when
\alpha\geq 3
,
and

\gamma(\alpha)=\frac{n-2}{2}
when
\alpha=2
.

The proof is very short so we include it here.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Observe that

(1.8)

\#\big(\big\{(m,p)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}^{n}\!:|\|m\|_{\alpha}-R|\!\leq\delta\text{ and }|\|m-p\|_{\alpha}-R|\!\leq\delta\big\}\big)

is simply

(1.9)

\big(\#\big\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\!:\!|\|m\|_{\alpha}-R|\!\leq\delta\big\}\big)\!\cdot\!\big(\#\big\{p\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\!:\!|\|p\|_{\alpha}-R|\!\leq\delta\big\}\big)\!.

From Theorem
1.1
, we know that the size of each of these sets is simply bounded by

(1.10)

\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:R-\delta\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)\lesssim\max\{R^{n-\frac{\gamma(\alpha)}{n-1-\gamma(\alpha)}},R^{n}\delta\}.

Thus, (
1.2
)
\lesssim\max\{R^{2\left(n-\frac{\gamma(\alpha)}{n-1-\gamma(\alpha)}\right)},\left(R^{n}\delta\right)^{2}\}
.
∎

Remark 1.3
.

We note that while the proof of Corollary
1.2
is very short, it sets the stage for a deeper investigation of lattice points near the intersection of two more general surfaces. In light of recent developments on
L^{2}
bounds on operators of the form
\sigma*\mu
(see
[
2
,
18
]
), it would be interesting to study intersections of two surfaces in the non-isotropic setting investigated in
[
17
]
.

2.
Method: From Lattice Points to Fractal Geometry

In this section, we use a series of lemmas to break down the proof of Theorem
1.1
and reduce matters to two key propositions. Throughout,
n=2d+1
, where
d\geq 1
is an integer.

As a technical point, in order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will first establish that

(2.1)

q^{-n}\#(\{(n,m)\!\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}^{n}:\|n\|_{\alpha},\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq Cq^{a},\,\left|\|n-m\|_{\alpha}-q^{a}\right|\leq q^{a-\tau}\})\lesssim q^{n-\tau},

for

\tau\in\left(a,\frac{(n-1)a}{n-1-\gamma(\alpha)}\right]
, where
\gamma(\alpha)
is defined as in Theorem
1.1
(and derived in Proposition
2.4
) below).
Setting
R=q^{a}
and
\delta=q^{a-\tau}
, then plugging in values of
\tau
yields the result; further details of this deduction are given in Section
2.3
.
Regarding the bounds on
\tau
, the lower bound
a<\tau
results in the natural restriction that
\delta<1
, and the upper bound on
\tau
is a requirement of our proof technique and will make an appearance in Section
4
.

2.1.
An overview of the proof of (
2.1
)

We first truncate and scale the lattice according to the geometry of the surfaces of the Heisenberg spheres defined in (
1.5
).
Next, we define a measure
\mu_{q}
on the resulting scaled and thickened lattice (see Definition

).
Through a series of lemmas, we reduce the proof of (
2.1

(a decay estimate) and Proposition
2.5
(an energy bound).

2.2.
Reduction of the proof

We rely on the following construction, which is similar to that used in
[
17
]

Recall

(2.2)

L_{n,q}:=\big\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\!:\!b_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,\lceil q^{a}\rceil\},i\neq n,\!\text{ and }b_{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,\lceil q^{2a}\rceil\}\!\big\},

Lemma 2.3

Let

Proof.

Let
\epsilon=q^{-\tau}

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mu_{q}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mu_{q}

\displaystyle\left(\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}^{n}:1-\epsilon\leq\|x-y\|_{\alpha}\leq 1+\epsilon\}\right)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\{x:\left|\|x-y\|_{\alpha}-1\right|<\epsilon\}}d\mu_{q}(x)d\mu_{q}(y)

A computation shows that

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\chi_{A_{1,\epsilon}}(\cdot)\sim\sigma_{\alpha}\!*\!\rho_{c\epsilon}(\cdot)
,
where

Now, through this relation, we may rewrite (

Using Fourier inversion and Fubini, we have

(2.15)

\displaystyle\epsilon\iint\sigma_{\alpha}\!*\!\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)d\mu_{q}(x)d\mu_{q}(y)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\iiint\widehat{\sigma_{\alpha}\!*\!\rho_{\epsilon}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\xi(x-y)}d\xi d\mu_{q}(x)d\mu_{q}(y)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}(\xi)\widehat{\rho}(\epsilon\xi)\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)}d\xi.

(2.17)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int\left|\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)\right|^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}(\xi)\widehat{\rho}(\epsilon\xi)d\xi.

Finally, plugging

and observing that

∎

Next, we require an estimate on

.
Due to the presence of vanishing curvature, known oscillatory integral estimates are not sufficient for our purposes,

Curvature and its role in oscillatory integral estimates is the topic of Section

Proposition 2.4

(Decay estimate for Heisenberg surface measure)
.

With

\left|\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}(\xi)\right|\lesssim(1+\left|\xi\right|)^{-\gamma(\alpha)},

where

The proof of Proposition

is found in Section

Next, plugging the estimate of Proposition

\displaystyle q^{n}\mu_{q}\,\mathsf{x}\,\mu_{q}\big(\big\{(x,y)\!\in\!E_{q}\ \mathsf{x}\ E_{q}\!:\!\left|\|x-y\|_{\alpha}\!-\!1\right|\!\leq q^{-\tau}\big\}\big)

\displaystyle\sim q^{n-\tau}\int\left|\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)\right|^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim q^{n-\tau}\int\left|\widehat{\mu}_{q}(\xi)\right|^{2}(1+\left|\xi\right|)^{-\gamma(\alpha)}d\xi,

and using elementary properties of the Fourier transform,

(2.18)

\int_{|\xi|>1}\big|\widehat{\mu_{q}^{\alpha}}(\xi)\big|^{2}\left|\xi\right|^{-\gamma(\alpha)}d\xi\sim\!\iint\left|x-y\right|^{\gamma(\alpha)-n}d\mu_{q}^{\alpha}(x)d\mu_{q}^{\alpha}(y),

\big(n-\gamma(\alpha)\big)
-energy of the function

(see

In conclusion, following the string of relations from Lemma

3.
Curvature and Decay

In this section we explore the curvature of the
Heisenberg spheres
and present the proof of Proposition
2.4
.

3.1.
Curvature

We begin with some useful definitions summarized from Shakarchi and Stein’s
[
34
, Chapter 8]
.
Given a local defining function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
the
principal curvatures
are the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix
(
\nabla^{2}\varphi=\{\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{k}}\}_{1\leq i,k\leq n-1}
)
, and the
Gaussian curvature
is the product of the principal curvatures.
The surface of the Heisenberg sphere is given by

\big((z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{2d}^{2})^{\alpha/2}+|t|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big)^{1/\alpha}=1.

At the
equator
, that is the intersection of
\partial{}B_{1}^{\alpha}
and the hyperplane
t=0
,
we use rotational symmetry to reduce matters to considering the curvature at the point
(1,0,\dots,0)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
.
In particular, we solve for
z_{1}
in terms of
z_{2},\dots,z_{2d}
and
t
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle z_{1}=\big[(1-|t|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})^{2/\alpha}-(z_{2}^{2}+\cdots+z_{2d}^{2})\big]^{1/2}.

We then take derivatives with respect to
t
and
z_{k}
,
k\neq 1
and evaluate at
(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
Note that while the derivative with respect to
t
does not exists for
\alpha=2
and the second derivative with respect to
t
does not exists when
\alpha=3
, we can differentiate twice with respect to all other variables.

For
\alpha=4,
the Hessian is a diagonal matrix with non-zero diagonal terms, and we conclude that the Gaussian curvature is non-zero.
For
\alpha>4
, the Hessian is also a diagonal matrix, but the second derivative in
t
vanishes, i.e., one principal curvature vanishes at each point of the equator.
Similarly, when
\alpha=2,3
, there are
2d-1=n-2
non-vanishing curvatures.
This can be seen by differentiating twice with respect to each variable in
\{z_{2},\dots,z_{2d}\}
and arriving at a quantity that is not zero.
More details these computations can be found in the thesis of the first listed author
[
5
, Chapter 4]
.

At the
poles
, the intersection of the set
\partial{}B_{1}^{\alpha}
with the
t
-axis, we solve for
t>0
in terms of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle t=\big(1-(z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{2d}^{2})^{\alpha/2}\big)^{2/\alpha}.

Note it suffices to consider the north pole,
(0,\dots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
, as the behavior at the south pole is equivalent by symmetry.
By calculating the first and second derivatives in each
z_{k}
, it is evident that all the principal curvatures vanish for all
\alpha\geq 3
.
The exception is
\alpha=2
, where none of the principal curvatures vanish and the Gaussian curvature is non-vanishing.

In summary,
the poorest behavior for
\alpha=2
occurs at each point of the equator where there are
n-2
non-vanishing curvatures,
and
the worst behavior for
\alpha\geq 3
occurs at the poles where all principle curvatures vanish.

At such points of maximal vanishing curvature,
there are no results of which we are aware that would apply to give an estimate for the decay
of the Fourier transform of the surface measures.
For this reason, a direct computation of the decay is necessary, the results of which are recorded in Proposition
2.4
.
On the other hand, in the instances when there is at least one non-vanishing curvature,
we may rely on known results to establish the decay of the Fourier transform of the surface measure (see Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
).

3.2.
Surface Measure and Decay

Before moving on to the proof of Proposition
2.4
, we introduce the definition of surface measure and its Fourier transform, and we discuss two well-known decay estimates. We denote by
d\sigma
the
induced Lebesgue measure
on a
C^{\infty}
-hypersurface
M
, defined as follows: Given any continuous function
f
on
M
with compact support,

(3.3)

\int_{M}fd\sigma=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{d(x,M)<\delta}Fdx,

where
F
is the extension of
f
to a continuous function on a
\delta
-neighborhood of
M
.

The surface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
may be described locally up to a transformation as

\{(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in B:x_{n}=\varphi(x^{\prime})\}
,
where
B
is a ball centered at the origin and
\varphi
is a smooth function (for more details, see Chapter 7, Section 4 of
[
34
]
).
Through this
local description of
M
, we may rewrite (
3.3
) as

(3.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}f(x^{\prime},\varphi(x^{\prime}))(1+\left|\nabla_{x^{\prime}}\varphi\right|^{2})^{1/2}dx^{\prime}.

From this representation, if a measure
d\nu
is of the form
d\nu=\psi d\sigma
, for some
C^{\infty}
function
\psi
with compact support, then
d\nu
is a
surface measure
on
M
with smooth density.

We define the Fourier transform of such a surface measure by

(3.5)

\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=\int_{M}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}d\nu(x),

which is bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
provided
d\nu
is a finite measure.

Hence, letting
\tilde{\psi}(x^{\prime})=\psi\big(x^{\prime},\varphi(x^{\prime})\big)\big(1+\left|\nabla_{x^{\prime}}\varphi\right|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\in C^{\infty}
with compact support, we may rewrite (
3.5
) as

(3.6)

\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-2\pi i(x^{\prime},\varphi(x^{\prime}))\cdot\xi}\tilde{\psi}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Note that (
spt(\tilde{\psi})\subseteq spt(\psi)
).

The following theorem and corollary will be useful for computing the decay for non-polar points.
Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
, originally due to Hlawka
[
15
]
and Littman
[
25
]
, respectively, are well-known and appear in
[
34
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Decay estimate with non-vanishing Gaussian curvature)
.

Let
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a hypersurface with non-vanishing Gaussian curvature at all points
z\in spt(\nu)
. Then

\big|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\big|\lesssim\left|\xi\right|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}.

Corollary 3.2

(Decay estimate when
m
principal curvatures do not vanish)
.

Let
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a hypersurface such that at any point
z\in spt(\nu)
at least
m
principal curvatures do not vanish.
(
This is equivalent to the statement that the Hessian of
\Phi
has rank
m
.
)
Then

\big|\widehat{\nu}(\xi)\big|\lesssim\left|\xi\right|^{-\frac{m}{2}}.

Referring to the curvature computations of Section
3.1
,
we apply Theorem
3.1
to estimate the decay at points with non-vanishing Gaussian curvature, and we apply Corollary
3.2
with
m=n-2
to estimate the decay at points with
n-2
non-vanishing principal curvatures.
Once we establish decay estimates in both regions, we take the minimal decay to obtain a universal decay estimate for the surface.

At the poles, we recall that all the principal curvatures vanish for
\alpha\geq 3
, so neither Theorem
3.1
nor Corollary
3.2
are applicable.
Instead, we must estimate the decay for these points using our Proposition
2.4
.
We start with the proof for
n=3
and
\alpha=4
.

3.3.
Proof of Proposition
2.4

Following the discussion above,
when

, Corollary
3.2
is used to establish decay at points of the equator and Theorem

is used to establish decay at the poles as well as at all remaining points.

It remains to prove Proposition

We focus on the north pole,

In short, we use a Taylor expansion to reformulate the arising phase function.
Next, a polar change of variables is used to exploit the radiality of the function

For simplicity of presentation, we begin with the proof when

and then give the more general proof when

4.
Energy Estimate

In this section, we give the proof of Proposition
2.5
.
In particular, we establish that
given
\alpha\geq 2
,
\tau\in\left(a,\frac{(n-1)a}{n-1-\gamma(\alpha)}\right]
and
t=n-\gamma(\alpha)
, then

(4.1)

\iint\left|x-y\right|^{-t}d\mu_{q}(x)d\mu_{q}(y)\lesssim 1,

where
\gamma(\alpha)
is defined as in Proposition
2.4
.

We present only the proof of Proposition
2.5
when
n=3
, and refer to the thesis of the first listed author,
[
5
]
, for the details of the proof when
n>3
. The reasoning for this is that the proof for
n>3
is rather lengthy and simply recycles the ideas presented here in the
n=3
case.

For computational purposes, it will be convenient to note that when
n=3
,

(4.2)

2.5\leq t<3

for all
\alpha\geq 2
.
Furthermore, with
\tau
and
t
as above,
when
n=3
,

(4.3)

\tau\leq\frac{(n-1)a}{n-1-\gamma(\alpha)}=\frac{2a}{t-1}.

4.1.
Preliminaries and strategy for the proof of the energy estimate

We use the definition of
\mu_{q}
from Definition
2.1
to
expand out the energy integral on the left-hand-side of (
4.1
).

First, we briefly recall the set-up from Section
2.2
in the case
n=3
.

R_{\tau}
denotes the
q^{-\tau}\ \mathsf{x}\ q^{-\tau}\ \mathsf{x}\ q^{-a-\tau}
rectangular box centered at the origin.
The set
E_{q}
, defined in (
2.3
), is a subset of the unit square made up of
q^{3}
shifted copies of the rectangle
R_{\tau}
.
For
b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
R_{b}=R_{\tau}+b
, so that
b
is the center of this shifted rectangle.

V_{R}
denotes the volume of such a rectangle so that
V_{R}=q^{-a-3\tau}
.

N_{R}=q^{3}
denotes the number rectangles making up
E_{q}
.
Furthermore,
\left|E_{q}\right|=N_{R}\cdot V_{R}
, where
|E_{q}|
denotes the volume of
E_{q}
.

Recall,
L_{3,q}
denotes the truncated lattice:

L_{3,q}=\{b=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:0<b_{1}\leq q^{a},\,\,0<b_{2}\leq q^{a},\,\,0<b_{3}\leq q^{2a}\},

and observe, since
a=3/4
, that
|L_{3,q}|=q^{3}=N_{R}
.
With this notation, we have

E_{q}=\bigcup_{b\in L_{3,q}}\left\{R_{\tau}+b\right\}.

The left-hand-side of (
4.1
) is now equivalent to

\displaystyle\iint\left|x-y\right|^{-t}d\mu_{q}(x)d\mu_{q}(y)\sim

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)^{\!2}\sum_{b,b^{\prime}\in L_{a,q}}\int_{R_{b}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dydx

(4.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b\in L_{a,q}}\int_{R_{b}}J(b,b^{\prime})dx

where
b=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
,
b^{\prime}=(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2},b^{\prime}_{3})
, and

(4.5)

J(b,b^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}\in L_{a,q}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

Matters are now reduced to showing that for each
b,b^{\prime}\in L_{a,q}
,

(4.6)

J(b,b^{\prime})\lesssim 1,

when
\tau\in\left(a,\frac{2a}{t-1}\right]
. Indeed, upon
establishing (
4.6
), the expression in (
4.4
) is bounded by

\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b\in L_{a,q}}\int_{R_{b}}J(b,b^{\prime})dx\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b\in L_{a,q}}V_{R}=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)N_{R}\cdot V_{R}=1,

and Proposition
2.5
will be proved.

In order to establish (
4.6
) for an arbitrary choice of
b=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}),b^{\prime}=(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime},b_{3}^{\prime})\in L_{a,q}
, we conduct a case analysis on
b,b^{\prime}
:

(1):

b=b^{\prime}
(
x
and
y
are in the same rectangle) or

(2):

b\neq b^{\prime}
(
x
and
y
are in different rectangles).

The second case, when
b\neq b^{\prime}
, is further divided into subcases depending on the proximity of the rectangles.
We present the various sub-cases in order of increasing level of complexity.

In Section
4.2
, we address the first case where
b=b^{\prime}
using a dyadic shell argument that considers the intersection of the shells with the rectangle.
In Sections
4.3
through
4.5
, we address the second case when
b\neq b^{\prime}
.
Specifically,
in Section
4.3
, we address the cases where
either
b_{1}\neq b_{1}^{\prime}
or
b_{2}\neq b_{2}^{\prime}
. In these cases, we say that the rectangles
R_{b}
and
R_{b^{\prime}}
are “sufficiently separated”.
Our arguments depend on (
4.14
) below and the fact that the arithmetic mean dominates the geometric mean.

In Sections
4.4
and
4.5
, we consider the cases when
b_{3}\neq b^{\prime}_{3}
but one or both of the other
b_{i}
are equal. Here, we say that the two distinct rectangles are within “close proximity,” and the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality does not suffice. In
Section
4.4
, a brute-force case analysis is conducted based on dominant terms when one
b_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}
.
In Section
4.5
, a more delicate analysis based on further decomposition of rectangles is required when both
b_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}
.

Note that when
b\neq b^{\prime}
, there are a number of cases that are resolved through symmetry. For example, all instances of
b_{1}=b^{\prime}_{1}
and
b_{2}\neq b^{\prime}_{2}
are handled identically to those where
b_{1}\neq b^{\prime}_{1}
and
b_{2}=b^{\prime}_{2}
by relabeling the coordinates.

4.2.
Case
b^{\prime}=b
in 3 Dimensions: Dyadic Shells

Inspecting the definition of
J(b,b^{\prime})
in (
4.5
), we see that when
b^{\prime}=b
, we have the single term

(4.7)

J(b,b^{\prime})=J(b,b)=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\int_{R_{b}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

We partition the rectangle
R_{b}
into its intersection with dyadic shells of the form
S_{j}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:2^{-(j+1)}\leq\left|x-y\right|\leq 2^{-j}\}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Note, we need only consider
j\geq\log_{2}q^{\tau}
, as otherwise the shell and the rectangle have empty intersection, and so

(4.8)

\int_{R_{b}}|x-y|^{-t}dy=\!\!\sum_{j\geq\log_{2}q^{\tau}}\int_{S_{j}\cap R_{b}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

As
j
ranges, depending on whether
S_{j}
is completely contained in the rectangle
R_{b}
or not, we have two regions over the sum in
j
to consider: (A)
0\leq r=2^{-j}\leq q^{-a-\tau}
or (B)
q^{-a-\tau}\leq r=2^{-j}\leq q^{-\tau}
. (See Figure
2
)

Figure 2.
Example of
S_{j}
contained in
R_{b}
and exceeding
R_{b}

In the first case,
j>\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}
and the shell
S_{j}\subseteq R_{b}
. In the latter case,
\log_{2}q^{\tau}\leq j\leq\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}
, and
S_{j}\cap R_{b}\neq\emptyset
, but the shell is no longer completely contained in
R_{b}
. Accordingly, we further divide the integral in (
4.8
) as follows:

\left(\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}^{\infty}\int_{S_{j}\cap R_{b}}\!\!\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy\right)+\left(\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{\tau}}^{\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}\int_{S_{j}\cap R_{b}}\!\!\left|x-y\right|^{-t}\!dy\right)=I_{A}(b)+I_{B}(b).

We will consider the sums in
I_{A}(b)
and
I_{B}(b)
separately.

Bounding
I_{A}
:

When
j>\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}
, the shell is completely contained in the rectangle so that
S_{j}\cap R_{b}=S_{j}
.
Since
|x-y|^{-t}\sim 2^{jt}
on
S_{j}
and
|S_{j}|\sim 2^{-3j}
, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle I_{A}(b)\,\,

\displaystyle\sim\!\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}^{\infty}2^{jt}|S_{j}|

\displaystyle\sim\!\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}^{\infty}2^{jt}2^{-3j}.

Since
t<3
, (
4.9
) is equivalent to

\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}^{\infty}2^{-j(3-t)}\sim(q^{a+\tau})^{-(3-t)}.

Bounding
I_{B}
:

When
\log_{2}q^{\tau}\leq j\leq\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}
, the shell containing
S_{j}
exceeds
R_{b}
in the vertical direction. For such
j
, the intersection
S_{j}\cap R_{b}
is contained in a cylinder of height
q^{-a-\tau}
and radius
2^{-j}
, so that
|S_{j}\cap R_{b}|\lesssim 2^{-2j}q^{-a-\tau}
.
Thus, since
|x-y|^{-t}\sim 2^{jt}
on
S_{j}
,

(4.10)

\displaystyle I_{B}(b)\,\,

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{\tau}}^{\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}2^{jt}|S_{j}\cap R_{x}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{\tau}}^{\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}2^{jt}2^{-2j}q^{-a-\tau}.

Recalling that
t>2
, (
4.10
) equals

(4.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=\log_{2}q^{\tau}}^{\log_{2}q^{a+\tau}}2^{j(t-2)}q^{-(a+\tau)}\sim(q^{a+\tau})^{t-2}q^{-(a+\tau)}.

Bounding
I_{A}+I_{B}
:

It now follows that

(4.12)

\displaystyle I_{A}(b)+I_{B}(b)\lesssim(q^{a+\tau})^{t-3}.

Plugging (
4.12
) into
J(b,b)
in (
4.7
) and recalling that
|E_{q}|=N_{R}\cdot V_{R}
,
V_{R}=q^{-a-3\tau}
,
N_{R}=q^{3}
, and
a=\frac{3}{4}
, we see that
J(b,b)
is bounded by a constant multiple of
1
provided that
3t+4\tau t\leq 18
, which is guaranteed since
2.5\leq t<3
from (
4.2
) and
\tau\leq\frac{2a}{t-1}
from (
4.3
).
Thus, we have verified (
4.6
) when
b=b^{\prime}
.

4.3.
Sufficiently Separated Rectangles in 3-dimensions

In this section we address the cases where (1)
b^{\prime}_{3}=b_{3}
and
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}
for exactly one
i\in\{1,2\}
(see Section
4.3.1
), (2)
b^{\prime}_{3}=b_{3}
and
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
(see Section
4.3.2
), and (3) the case where
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
(see Section
4.3.3
).

Throughout the remainder of this proof, for
x\in R_{b}
and
y\in R_{b^{\prime}}
, we use the observation that

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left|x-y\right|\,

\displaystyle\sim\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|+\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|+\left|x_{3}-y_{3}\right|

(4.14)

\displaystyle\sim\left|\frac{b_{1}}{q^{a}}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\right|+\left|\frac{b_{2}}{q^{a}}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{2}}{q^{a}}\right|+\left|\frac{b_{3}}{q^{2a}}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\right|.

4.3.1.
Assume
b^{\prime}_{3}=b_{3}
, but
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}
for
i=1
or 2

Without loss of generality, assume
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}
, but
b^{\prime}_{2}=b_{2}
. Here,

J(b,b^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

We estimate
\left|x-y\right|^{-t}
by
\left(\frac{\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!-t}
so that

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{q^{at}}{|E_{q}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|^{-t}dy.

Observe that the integral in
y
simply yields a factor of
V_{R}
. Applying the integral test and recalling that
2.5\leq t<3
, this expression is bounded by a constant multiple of

\frac{q^{at}V_{R}}{|E_{q}|}=\frac{q^{at}}{N_{R}}=q^{at-3},

which is bounded by 1 since
a=\frac{3}{4}<1
and
t<3
.

4.3.2.
Assume
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}
,
b^{\prime}_{2}\neq b_{2}
, and
b^{\prime}_{3}=b_{3}

Here,

J(b,b^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{2}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{2}\neq b_{2}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

Similar to the previous case, we estimate
\left|x-y\right|
using (
4.14
). However, unlike the previous case, we now must sum in
b^{\prime}_{2}
and a tighter estimate will be needed to assure the sums converge.
Since, by assumption,
b^{\prime}_{3}=b_{3}
, we estimate

\left|x-y\right|\geq\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|+\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|\sim\left|\frac{b_{1}}{q^{a}}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\right|+\left|\frac{b_{2}}{q^{a}}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{2}}{q^{a}}\right|.

Thus, using the fact that the arithmetic mean dominates the geometric mean, we bound
\left|x-y\right|^{-t}
by

\displaystyle 2^{-t}\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|^{-\frac{t}{2}}\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|^{-\frac{t}{2}}\sim\left(\frac{\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!-\frac{t}{2}}\left(\frac{\left|b_{2}-b^{\prime}_{2}\right|}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!-\frac{t}{2}}.

Hence, the expression
J(b,b^{\prime})
is bounded by

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{q^{at}\,V_{R}}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{2}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{2}\neq b_{2}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|^{-\frac{t}{2}}\left|b_{2}-b^{\prime}_{2}\right|^{-\frac{t}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{q^{at}}{N_{R}}\right)\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|^{-\frac{t}{2}}\!\Bigg]^{2}.

Since
t>2
, an application of the integral test yields the same bound as above,

\left(\frac{q^{at}}{N_{R}}\right)=q^{at-3},

which is bounded by 1 since
2<t<3
and
a=\frac{3}{4}
.

Note 4.1
.

Note that the seemingly similar case when
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}
and
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}
for precisely one
i\in\{1,2\}

cannot
be handled using an argument similar to the one above in Section
4.3.2
. An alternative argument using dominant terms will be given to handle this case in Section
4.4
.

4.3.3.
Assume
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}
and both of the other
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}

Here,

J(b,b^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{2}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{2}\neq b_{2}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{3}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}\end{subarray}}^{q^{2a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

This case is handled analogously to the previous, except that we have a lengthier sum.
We will again make use of the fact that the arithmetic mean dominates the geometric mean:

\displaystyle\left|x-y\right|\sim\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|\!+\!\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|\!+\!\left|x_{3}-y_{3}\right|\geq 3\!\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|^{\frac{1}{3}}\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|^{\frac{1}{3}}\left|x_{3}-y_{3}\right|^{\frac{1}{3}}

where
x_{i}\sim\frac{b_{i}}{q^{a}}
for
i=1,2
,
x_{3}\sim\frac{b_{3}}{q^{2a}}
and
y_{i}\sim\frac{b^{\prime}_{i}}{q^{a}}
for
i=1,2
,
y_{3}\sim\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\left|x-y\right|^{-t}\lesssim\left(\frac{\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!-\frac{t}{3}}\left(\frac{\left|b_{2}-b^{\prime}_{2}\right|}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!-\frac{t}{3}}\left(\frac{\left|b_{3}-b^{\prime}_{3}\right|}{q^{2a}}\right)^{\!\!-\frac{t}{3}},

and we can bound the integral
J(b,b^{\prime})
by

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigg(\frac{\big(q^{a}\big)^{\frac{4}{3}t}\,V_{R}}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\Bigg)\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{2}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{2}\neq b_{2}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{3}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}\end{subarray}}^{q^{2a}}\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|^{-\frac{t}{3}}\left|b_{2}-b^{\prime}_{2}\right|^{-\frac{t}{3}}\left|b_{3}-b^{\prime}_{3}\right|^{-\frac{t}{3}}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{\big(q^{a}\big)^{\frac{4}{3}t}}{N_{R}}\Bigg)\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\left|b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1}\right|^{\!-\frac{t}{3}}\Bigg]^{2}\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{3}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}\end{subarray}}^{q^{2a}}\left|b_{3}-b^{\prime}_{3}\right|^{\!-\frac{t}{3}}\Bigg].

Since
t<3
, using the integral test, this is bounded by

J(b,b^{\prime})\lesssim\left(\frac{\big(q^{a}\big)^{\frac{4}{3}t}}{N_{R}}\right)\left(q^{a(1-\frac{t}{3})}\right)^{2}\left(q^{2a(1-\frac{t}{3})}\right).

Plugging in
N_{R}=q^{3}
and
a=\frac{3}{4}
, this expression is identically equal to
1
.

4.4.
“Numerous Rectangles” in 3-dimensions

In this section, we handle the case when

b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}
and exactly one of the other
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}

Upon inspection, it becomes clear that this case cannot be handled using the fact that the geometric mean dominates the arithmetic mean. Instead, an argument using dominant terms is given.

We give the proof for
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}
and
b^{\prime}_{2}=b_{2}
, as both cases are symmetric.
Here,

J(b,b^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{1}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{1}\neq b_{1}\end{subarray}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{3}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}\end{subarray}}^{q^{2a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

For
x\in R_{b}
and
y\in R_{b^{\prime}}
, we estimate

x_{1}\sim\frac{b_{1}}{q^{a}}
,
x_{2}\sim\frac{b_{2}}{q^{a}}
, and
x_{3}\sim\frac{b_{3}}{q^{2a}}
, as well as

y_{1}\sim\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}
,
y_{2}\sim\frac{b_{2}}{q^{a}}
, and
y_{3}\sim\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
, and we appeal to
(
4.13
).

For simplicity, we will conduct the estimate for
b=(0,0,0)
, as the more general argument follows similarly. Thus,
for
y\in R_{b^{\prime}}
,
\left|x-y\right|\sim\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}+\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
, where
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\in\big\{\frac{1}{q^{a}},\frac{2}{q^{a}},\dots,\frac{q^{a}}{q^{a}}\big\}
and
\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\in\big\{\frac{1}{q^{2a}},\frac{2}{q^{2a}},\dots,\frac{q^{2a}}{q^{2a}}\big\}
.
This naturally leads to two cases:

Case I:

The horizontal distance in
z_{1}
dominates the vertical:
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
.

Case II:

The vertical distance between
x
and
y
dominates:
\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}>\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}
.

We now proceed to bound
J(b,b^{\prime})
in each case.

Case I:

Assume
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
, and estimate
\left|x-y\right|
by
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}
. Observe that since
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\Leftrightarrow b^{\prime}_{3}\leq b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}
, we need only sum the
b^{\prime}_{3}
to
b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}\leq q^{2a}
, resulting in the following estimation:

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{V_{R}}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}}\left(\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!-t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{R}}\,q^{at}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}(b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a})(b^{\prime}_{1})^{-t}.

Applying the integral test and recalling that
2.5\leq<3
, this expression is bounded by

\frac{1}{N_{R}}\,q^{at}q^{a}\lesssim 1,

since
N_{R}=q^{3}
and
a=\frac{3}{4}

Case II:

Assume
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
, so we estimate
\left|x-y\right|
by
\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
.
Observe that since
\frac{b^{\prime}_{1}}{q^{a}}~<~\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\Leftrightarrow b^{\prime}_{3}>b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}
, we need only sum
b^{\prime}_{3}
from
b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}
to the upper bound,
q^{2a}
. Now

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}}^{q^{2a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy

\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{V_{R}}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}}^{q^{2a}}\left(\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\right)^{\!\!-t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{N_{R}}\right)\,q^{2at}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}}^{q^{2a}}(b^{\prime}_{3})^{-t}.

Applying the integral test for
2<t<3
,

\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}}^{q^{2a}}(b^{\prime}_{3})^{-t}\sim\big(b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}\big)^{1-t}.

Plugging this back into the bound for
J(b,b^{\prime})
we have

J(b,b^{\prime})\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{N_{R}}\right)\,q^{2at}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{q^{a}}\big(b^{\prime}_{1}q^{a}\big)^{1-t}.

Again applying the integral test to the sum in
b^{\prime}_{1}
, we see that

J(b,b^{\prime})\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{N_{R}}\right)\,q^{2at}\big(q^{a}\big)^{1-t}=\frac{1}{N_{R}}\,q^{at}q^{a},

the same upper bound as in Case I.

4.5.
“High Proximity Rectangles” in 3-dimensions

Finally, we consider the most complex case when

b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}
and both of the other

for

Upon inspection, it becomes clear that this case cannot be handled using the techniques of the previous sections. Instead, a finer decomposition of the rectangle

Observe that the minimal vertical distance between distinct rectangles

Consequently, this case requires a more cautious consideration of the locations of

Hence,

J(b,b^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1}{\left|E_{q}\right|}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{\prime}_{3}=1\\
b^{\prime}_{3}\neq b_{3}\end{subarray}}^{q^{2a}}\int_{R_{b^{\prime}}}|x-y|^{-t}dy.

For

\displaystyle|x-y|\sim\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}}+\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}+\left|\frac{b_{3}}{q^{2a}}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\right|.

As in the previous case, we will conduct the estimate for

, as the more general argument follows from a similar argument. Now

|x-y|\sim\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}}+\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}+\left|\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}\right|.

This leads to four cases:

Case I:

The horizontal distances in both directions
dominate the vertical:
\frac{\ell_{i}}{q^{a+\tau}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
for both

, which occurs when

Note that, since

Case II:

The horizontal distance in

dominates the vertical:
\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

Case III:

The horizontal distance in

\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

Case IV:

The vertical distance between

\frac{\ell_{i}}{q^{a+\tau}}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
for both

, which occurs when

\ell_{i}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}
.

Case I:

Assume
\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}},\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

\left|x-y\right|\sim\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\left|\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\right|.

Recall that
\ell_{i}\leq q^{a}

b^{\prime}_{3}

since the vertical distance is dominant for larger

J(b,b^{\prime})\cdot\left|E_{q}\right|=\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\int_{C_{\ell,b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy.

We further divide this sum into two sums, one where
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}

\left|x-y\right|\sim\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\left|\ell_{1}\right|

\left|x-y\right|\sim\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\left|\ell_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{\ell_{1}}\int_{C_{\ell,b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy+\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}}^{q^{a}}\int_{C_{\ell,b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{\ell_{1}}\!\!\!V_{C}\left(\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\left|\ell_{1}\right|\right)^{\!\!\!-t}\!+\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}}^{q^{a}}\!V_{C}\left(\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\left|\ell_{2}\right|\right)^{\!\!\!-t}

\displaystyle\sim V_{C}\!\left(\!\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\!\right)^{\!\!\!-t}\!\,\,\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\!\!\left|\ell_{1}\right|^{-t}\!\left(\!\ell_{1}-\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\!\right)\!+V_{C}\bigg(\!\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\!\bigg)^{\!\!\!-t}\,\,\!\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\!\!\ell_{1}^{1-t}

\displaystyle=I+II

since

.
We next apply the integral test to sum in
\ell_{1}
, recognizing

\displaystyle\int_{b^{\prime}_{3}/q^{a-\tau}}^{q^{a}}x^{-t}\left(x-\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)dx\ \leq\int_{b^{\prime}_{3}/q^{a-\tau}}^{q^{a}}x^{1-t}dx\sim\left(\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!2-t}\!.

\displaystyle I+II\,

\displaystyle\lesssim V_{C}\left(\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\right)^{\!\!\!-t}\,\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\left(\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!2-t}

\displaystyle=V_{C}\,q^{2at}\left(\frac{q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!2}\,\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}(b^{\prime}_{3})^{2-t}.

Once again utilizing the integral test and recognizing that

by

(4.16)

V_{C}\,q^{2at}q^{2\tau-2a}(q^{2a-\tau})^{3-t}=V_{C}\,q^{2\tau-2a}q^{6a}(q^{-\tau})^{3-t}=V_{C}\,q^{4a}(q^{\tau})^{t-1}.

Seeking a bound for

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})\lesssim\frac{1}{|E_{q}|}V_{C}\,q^{4a}\big(q^{\tau}\big)^{t-1}=\frac{V_{C}\,q^{4a}\big(q^{\tau}\big)^{t-1}}{N_{C}\cdot V_{C}}=\frac{(q^{\tau})^{t-1}}{q^{2a}},

since

.
This is bounded since

Case II:

Assume
\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}}\geq\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}
, and
\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

.

(4.17)

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})\cdot\left|E_{q}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}\int_{C_{\ell,b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}V_{C}\left(\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\left|\ell_{1}\right|\right)^{\!\!-t}

\displaystyle=V_{C}\left(\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\right)^{\!\!-t}\!\left(\frac{1}{q^{a-\tau}}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}b^{\prime}_{3}\sum_{\ell_{1}=\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\left|\ell_{1}\right|^{-t}.

Applying the integral test to sum in
\ell_{1}
, and since

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}x^{-t}dx=x^{1-t}\biggr|_{\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}^{q^{a}}\sim\left(\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!1-t}.

Thus, (

\displaystyle V_{C}\left(\frac{1}{q^{a+\tau}}\right)^{\!\!-t}\left(\frac{q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}b^{\prime}_{3}\left(\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}\right)^{\!\!1-t}

\displaystyle=V_{C}\,q^{2at}q^{2\tau-2a}\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}(b^{\prime}_{3})^{2-t}.

Once again, we apply the integral test to the sum in

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}x^{2-t}dx=x^{3-t}\biggr|_{1}^{q^{2a-\tau}}\sim\big(q^{2a-\tau}\big)^{3-t}

since

.
Thus we have

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})\cdot\left|E_{q}\right|\,

\displaystyle\sim V_{C}\,q^{2at}q^{2\tau-2a}(q^{2a-\tau})^{3-t}

\displaystyle=V_{C}\,q^{4a}(q^{-\tau})^{1-t}.

Noticing that this is the same bound as that obtained in (

) in Case 1, we conclude that
J(b,b^{\prime})\lesssim 1

since
\tau\leq\frac{2a}{t-1}
by (
4.3
).

Case IV:

Assume
\frac{\ell_{1}}{q^{a+\tau}},\frac{\ell_{2}}{q^{a+\tau}}<\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}}{q^{2a}}

(4.20)

\displaystyle J(b,b^{\prime})\cdot\left|E_{q}\right|\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{b^{\prime}_{3}=1}^{q^{2a}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{\frac{b^{\prime}_{3}q^{\tau}}{q^{a}}}\int_{C_{\ell,b^{\prime}}}\left|x-y\right|^{-t}dy

5.
Sharpness of the Main result and An improvement for small Delta

In this section, we use the error estimate of Garg, Nevo, and the second listed author in
[
11
]

to show that our main result is sharp when
\delta
obeys a lower bound dependent on
\alpha
and
n
.
Further, for sufficiently small values of
\delta
where sharpness is not attained, we demonstrate a method to improve Theorem
1.1
.
As an example, when
\alpha=2
, our main theorem is sharp when
\delta>R^{-\frac{(n-2)}{n}}
, but an improvement is possible when
\delta<R^{-\frac{(n-2)}{n}}
.

5.1.
Sharpness

We begin by establishing sharpness of Theorem
1.1
in several regimes by showing that the inequality in (
1.6
) is in fact an equality for a range of
\delta
dependent on
\alpha
and
n
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
d
and
\alpha
be integers, and set
n=2d+1
.
Then

(5.1)

\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}:R-\delta\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)\sim R^{n}\delta,

for
R>1
and
n\geq 3
,
when
\alpha\geq 2(n-1)
and
\delta\geq R^{-\frac{1}{(\alpha-1)}}
;
when
2<\alpha\leq 2(n-1)
and
\delta\geq R^{-\frac{1}{(2n-3)}}
;
or
when
\alpha=2
and
\delta\geq R^{-\frac{(n-2)}{n}}
.

To prove (
5.1
),
recall the definition of the Heisenberg norm ball
B_{R}^{\alpha}=B_{R}^{\alpha,1}
in (
1.3
), and recall the volume computation,

|B_{R}^{\alpha}|=R^{2d+2}|B_{1}^{\alpha}|.

Defining

(5.2)

E(R):=\#\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2d+1}\cap B_{R}^{\alpha}\big)-|B_{R}^{\alpha}|,

we have

\displaystyle\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}:R\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)

\displaystyle=\#\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2d+1}\cap B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}\big)-\#\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2d+1}\cap B_{R}^{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=|B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}|+E(R+\delta)-\big(|B_{R}^{\alpha}|+E(R)\big)

\displaystyle=|B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}|-|B_{R}^{\alpha}|+E(R+\delta)-E(R)

\displaystyle\geq cR^{2d+1}\delta-\left(|E(R+\delta)|+|E(R)|\right)

for some constant
c>0
, where in the last line we observe that
|B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}|-|B_{R}^{\alpha}|\leq cR^{2d+1}\delta
and subtract the absolute values of the errors.
We now verify that the first term,
cR^{2d+1}\delta
, dominates the error terms and hence is the desired lower bound.

The following bounds on the error were achieved by Garg, Nevo, and Taylor in
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
for all
\alpha
:

|E(R)|\lesssim\left\{\begin{IEEEeqnarraybox}[]{[}][c]{l?s}\IEEEstrut R^{2d}&for$d\geq 1$($n\geq 3$)and$\alpha=2$\\
R^{2+\max{\{0,\delta(\alpha)\}}}\log(R)&for$d=1$($n=3$)and$\alpha>2$\\
R^{4}\big(\log(R)\big)^{2/3}&for$d=2$($n=5$)and$\alpha> 2$\\
R^{2d}&for$d\geq 3$($n \geq 7$)and$\alpha> 2$,\IEEEstrut\end{IEEEeqnarraybox}\right.

where
\delta(\alpha)=\frac{2\alpha-8}{3\alpha-4}
so that in particular
\max{\{0,\delta(\alpha)\}}=0
for
\alpha\leq 4
.

With these bounds in tow, it is not hard to verify that
|E(R)|\leq\frac{c}{100}R^{n}\delta
when
\delta\geq C\max\{\frac{\log{R}}{R},R^{-1+\max{\{0,\delta(\alpha)\}}}\}
, for a sufficiently small constant
C
.
A computation shows that the lower bounds on
\delta
assumed in Proposition
5.1
are more strict, and we conclude then that

(5.3)

\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}:R\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)\gtrsim cR^{2d+1}\delta.

In summary, the lower bound in (
5.3
) agrees with the upper bound in (
1.6
)
for the range of
\delta
specified in Proposition
5.1
,
and Theorem
1.1
is sharp in this regime.

5.2.
An improvement to Theorem
1.1
for small
\delta

Utilizing the results from
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
, we can achieve the following bounds on the number of lattice points within a
\delta
-thickening of a given Heisenberg sphere of radius
R
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For

d\geq 1
,
R>1
, and
\delta\in[0,1)
,

(5.4)

\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}:R\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)\lesssim\max\{R^{n}\delta,\left|E(R)\right|\}

where
\left|E(R)\right|
is the error term defined above from
[
11
]
.

Proof.

Following (
5.2
), similar to the lower bound computation above, we have

\displaystyle\#\big(\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}\ \mathsf{x}\ \mathbb{Z}:R\leq\|m\|_{\alpha}\leq R+\delta\}\big)

\displaystyle=\#\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2d+1}\cap B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}\big)-\#\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2d+1}\cap B_{R}^{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=|B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}|+E(R+\delta)-\big(|B_{R}^{\alpha}|+E(R)\big)

\displaystyle=|B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}|-|B_{R}^{\alpha}|+E(R+\delta)-E(R)

\displaystyle\leq cR^{2d+1}\delta+\left|E(R+\delta)\right|+\left|E(R)\right|

for some constant
c>0
, where in the last line we observe that
|B_{R+\delta}^{\alpha}|-|B_{R}^{\alpha}|\leq cR^{2d+1}\delta
and add the absolute values of the errors.
∎

Observe that, for small
\delta>0
, Proposition
5.2
improves on Theorem
1.1
.
For instance, when
\alpha=2
and
\delta\leq R^{-\frac{(n-2)}{n}}
,
Theorem
1.1
yields an upper bound of
R^{n-\frac{(n-2)}{n}}
,
while
Proposition
5.2
yields an improved upper bound of

\max\{R^{n-1},R^{n}\delta\}
for
\delta\leq R^{-\frac{(n-2)}{n}}
.
We note that such improvements come at the cost of a slightly more complicated proof as the error bounds on which Proposition
5.2
rely depend on the use of intricate Bessel function estimates, unlike that of Theorem
1.1
. Nevertheless, the two results working together yield the current best known upper bounds for counting lattice points near the Heisenberg spheres, and in many regimes, as detailed above, Theorem
1.1
is sharp.
