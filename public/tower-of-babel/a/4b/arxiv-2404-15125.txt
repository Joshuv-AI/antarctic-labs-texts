Abstract
. In this paper we consider representations of certain combinatorial categories, including the poset 𝒟 {\mathscr{D}} of positive integers and division, the Young lattice 𝒴 \mathscr{Y} of partitions of finite sets, the opposite category of the orbit category 𝒵 \mathscr{Z} of ( ℤ , + ) (\mathbb{Z},+) with respect to nontrivial subgroups, and the category 𝒞 ​ ℐ \mathscr{CI} of finite cyclic groups and injective homomorphisms. We describe explicit upper bounds for homological degrees of their representations, and deduce that finitely presented representations (resp., representations presented in finite degrees) over a field form abelian subcategories of the representation categories. We also give an explicit description for the category of sheaves over the ringed atomic site ( 𝒵 , J a ​ t , ℂ ¯ ) (\mathscr{Z},\,J_{at},\,\underline{\mathbb{C}}) , and show that irreducible sheaves are parameterized by primitive roots of the unit.



1.
Introduction

The poset
{\mathscr{D}}
of positive integers and division is a well known example of lattices, and is closely related to quite a few important posets in combinatorics. For example, by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, it is isomorphic to the poset
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
whose elements are sequence
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
of natural numbers with all but finitely many entries being 0, and whose order is given by
{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant{\mathbf{y}}
if
x_{i}\leqslant y_{i}
for each
i
. Furthermore, the infinite symmetric group
S_{\infty}=\varinjlim_{n}S_{n}
acts on
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
in a natural way and this action respects the partial order, so the quotient set consisting of orbits is again a poset, and is isomorphic to the Young latttice
\mathscr{Y}
whose elements are partitions of
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
with
n
ranging over all positive integers and whose partial order is given by containment.

These posets and related combinatorial categories have been extensively considered in various areas such as combinatorics, combinatorial topology, operad theory and representation theory of symmetric groups or Lie algebras
[
5
,
8
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. Recently, their representations also attract attentions of experts in many fields. For instances,
persistence modules
are widely applied in topological data analysis and related areas
[
2
,
3
,
16
]
, and
{\mathrm{FI}}
-modules play a central role in representation stability theory
[
4
]
. Thus representation theory of posets and combinatorial categories is a valuable topic, not only because of its own interest, but also because of the rich application in other areas such as algebraic topology, commutative algebra, applied combinatorics and applied topology. As a particular example, we note that representations of
{\mathscr{D}}
are precisely multi-graded modules of the polynomial ring with countably many variables, and correspondingly representations of
\mathscr{Y}
are closely related to
S_{\infty}
-symmetric modules of this polynomial ring, which are actively studied by quite a few authors; see
[
1
,
7
,
12
,
14
,
15
]
and in particular the nice short survey paper
[
11
]
.

In this paper we study representations of categories
{\mathscr{C}}
sharing the following combinatorial features of
{\mathscr{D}}
, called
combinatorial categories of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
-type
:

•

{\mathscr{C}}
is a small skeletal
EI category
, that is, every endomorphism in
{\mathscr{C}}
is an isomorphism;

•

there is a functor
\iota:{\mathscr{D}}\to{\mathscr{C}}
which is a bijection between the object sets;

•

for each pair of objects
x
and
y
, the group
G_{x}={\mathscr{C}}(x,x)
acts freely and transitively on
{\mathscr{C}}(x,y)
from the right side, while the group
G_{y}
acts transitively on
{\mathscr{C}}(x,y)
from the left side.

Besides the poset
{\mathscr{D}}
, typical examples include:

(1)

the opposite category of the orbit category
\mathscr{Z}
of
(\mathbb{Z},+)
with respect to the family of nontrivial subgroups;

(2)

the opposite category of the category
\mathscr{CS}
of finite cyclic groups and surjective group homomorphisms;

(3)

the category
\mathscr{CI}
of finite cyclic groups and injective group homomorphisms.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a combinatorial category of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
-type, and
k
a commutative ring. A
representation
of
{\mathscr{C}}
(or called a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module
) is a covariant functor from
{\mathscr{C}}
to
k\Mod
, the category of all
k
-modules. The category
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
is a rather big abelian category, in practice one may work in relatively smaller abelian subcategories consisting of
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules satisfying certain extra conditions. However, the usual notion of finitely generated modules is not a proper choice. Therefore, we want to find alternative conditions such that
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules satisfying them form an abelian subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
. The first result of this aspect is:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a combinatorial category of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
-type and
k
a field. If the endomorphism group
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is finite for each
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, then the category of finitely presented
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules is abelian.

There are many interesting finitely generated
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules (even the simple modules) are not finitely presented. Moreover, for practical purpose people frequently have to deal with infinitely generated
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules. In this case we introduce a few homological numerical invariants and impose some finiteness conditions on them. It turns out that
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules satisfying these conditions also form an abelian subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
.

Let us give some details about these finiteness conditions. Since the object set
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
can be identified with the set of finite sequences
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of natural numbers, we impose the
sup norm
on
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
by setting
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|
to be the maximal entry in the sequence. Given a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module
V
, we determine the smallest subset
S\subseteq\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
such that
V
is generated by its values on objects in
S
. The zeroth
homological degree

\hd_{0}(V)
is defined to be the supremum of those
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|
for
{\mathbf{x}}\in S
. By a standard homological process we can define
\hd_{i}(V)
for all
i\geqslant 0
. We say that
V
is
presented in finite degrees
if both
\hd_{0}(V)
and
\hd_{1}(V)
are finite.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
,
k
a commutative ring,
\|\bullet\|
the sup norm on
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, and
V
a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module. Then

\hd_{s}(V)\leqslant\max\{\hd_{0}(V),\,\hd_{1}(V)\}

for
s\geqslant 0
. In particular, the category of
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules presented in finite degrees is abelian.

At a first glance this result seems unexpectedly simple. This happens because we define
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|
to be the maximal entry in
{\mathbf{x}}
, which is very weak in the sense that too many distinct objects share the same norm. Instead, if we define homological degrees with respect to strong norms, for instance, the
sum norm
by setting
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|
to be the sum of all entries in
{\mathbf{x}}
, then we cannot bound higher homological degrees in terms of the first two; see Example
3.6
. But for the Young lattice (which as a quotient category of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
is not a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
), we do get an affirmative answer.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathscr{Y}
be the Young lattice,
k
a field, and
\|\bullet\|
the sum norm on
\Ob(\mathscr{Y})
. If
s\geqslant 2
and
\hd_{s-1}(V)>0
, then

\hd_{s}(V)\leqslant\hd_{s-1}(V)(\ln(\hd_{s-1}(V))+1).

In particular, the category of
\mathscr{Y}
-modules presented in finite degrees is abelian.

Another important subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
which has a natural abelian structure (but not inherited from that of
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
) is the category
{\mathrm{Sh}}({\mathscr{C}}^{\op},\,J,\,\underline{k})
of sheaves over
{\mathscr{C}}^{\op}
equipped with a certain Grothendieck topology
J
and the constant structure sheaf
\underline{k}
. The main motivation for us to consider this category lies in three aspects: the sheaf category over orbit categories equipped with the atomic Grothendieck topology
J_{at}
is closely related to continuous representations of topological groups (see for instances
[
6
,
10
,
13
]
); the sheaf theoretic approach can provide a novel viewpoint for representations, for instance, the complicated shift functor constructed in Section 3 turns out to be the sheafification functor; and finally, for some examples studied in this paper, the sheaf category is much simpler than the representation category. For instance, if
{\mathscr{C}}
is a poset, then
{\mathrm{Sh}}({\mathscr{C}}^{\op},\,J_{at},\,\underline{k})
is equivalent to
k\Mod
. Although the situation becomes more complicated for other examples considered in this paper, we still obtain the following result for a specific category.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field with characteristic 0,
\mathscr{Z}
the orbit category of
(\mathbb{Z},+)
with respect to the family of nontrivial subgroups, and
J_{at}
the atomic topology on
\mathscr{Z}
. Then
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\mathscr{Z},\,J_{at},\,\underline{k})
is a semisimple category, and irreducible sheaves are parameterized by primitive roots of the unit in
k
.

At this moment we have not obtained similar results for
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\mathscr{CS},\,J_{at},\,\underline{k})
or
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\mathscr{CI}^{\op},\,J_{at},\,\underline{k})
. The main obstacle is that an explicit description for automorphisms groups of finite cyclic groups is not available.

This paper is organized as follows: in the second section we introduce categories of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
and some homological invariants of their representations. Section 3 is devoted to representations of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. We give an upper bound for homological degrees, and investigate shift functors in details. In Section 4 we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
for combinatorial categories
{\mathscr{C}}
of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, relying on the assumption that
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
can be viewed as a subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}
and the observation that the restriction functor
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod\to\mathbb{N}^{\infty}\Mod
has a nice property. Representations of Young lattice is considered in Section 5, where we prove Theorem
1.3
. A sheaf theoretic approach as well as a proof of Theorem
1.4
is given in the last section.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce combinatorial categories of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, describe some examples, and propose a few important invariants for their representations.

2.1.
Combinatorial categories of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a small skeletal EI category; that is, every endomorphism in
{\mathscr{C}}
is an isomorphism. Then
G_{x}={\mathscr{C}}(x,x)
is a group for every object
x
in
{\mathscr{C}}
. Furthermore, the binary relation
\preccurlyeq
on
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
given by
x\preccurlyeq y
if
{\mathscr{C}}(x,y)\neq\emptyset
is a partial order.

Definition 2.1
.

A small skeletal EI category
{\mathscr{C}}
is said to be a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

there is a functor
\iota:\mathbb{N}^{\infty}\to{\mathscr{C}}
which is a bijection between the object sets;

(2)

the group
G_{x}
acts regularly (i.e., freely and transitively) on
{\mathscr{C}}(x,y)
from the right side for
x,y\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
;

(3)

the group
G_{y}
acts transitively on
{\mathscr{C}}(x,y)
from the left side for
x,y\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})

By the first condition, we can identify the underlying poset
(\Ob({\mathscr{C}}),\preccurlyeq)
of
{\mathscr{C}}
and
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, so in the rest of this paper by abuse of notation we frequently denote objects in
{\mathscr{C}}
by
{\mathbf{x}}
with
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
.

Recall the following definition of left Ore condition: given morphisms
f:{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{y}}
and
g:{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{z}}
in
{\mathscr{C}}
, there exists an object
{\mathbf{w}}
as well as morphisms
f^{\prime}:{\mathbf{y}}\to{\mathbf{w}}
and
g^{\prime}:{\mathbf{z}}\to{\mathbf{w}}
such that the diagram commutes

\textstyle{{\mathbf{x}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{g}
\textstyle{{\mathbf{y}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f^{\prime}}
\textstyle{{\mathbf{z}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g^{\prime}}
\textstyle{{\mathbf{w}}.}

Dually, one can define right Ore condition.

The following facts can be easily deduced from definitions.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. Then one has:

(1)

every morphism in
{\mathscr{C}}
is a monomorphism;

(2)

{\mathscr{C}}
satisfies the left Ore condition.

Proof.

(1) Given two morphisms
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
from
{\mathbf{x}}
to
{\mathbf{y}}
and a morphism
\beta
from
{\mathbf{y}}
to
{\mathbf{z}}
such that
\beta\alpha=\beta\alpha^{\prime}
, since
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
acts regularly on
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
, we can find an element
g\in G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha g
. Consequently,
\beta\alpha=\beta\alpha g
. But
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
also acts regularly on
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{z}})
, this happens if and only if
g
is the identity in
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
, so
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
.

(2) Since
{\mathscr{C}}
is of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, we can let
{\mathbf{w}}
be a common upper bound of
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{z}}
and take
f^{\prime}:{\mathbf{y}}\to{\mathbf{w}}
and
g_{1}:{\mathbf{z}}\to{\mathbf{w}}
to be arbitrary morphisms. Since
G_{{\mathbf{w}}}
acts transitively on
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},\,{\mathbf{w}})
, we can find a certain
\delta\in G_{{\mathbf{w}}}
such that
\delta\circ g_{1}\circ g=f^{\prime}\circ f
. Then
\delta\circ g_{1}
is the desired
g^{\prime}
.
∎

Here are some interesting examples of combinatorial categories of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be the poset of positive integers and division. This poset is isomorphic to
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. Indeed, we can order all primes as follows
p_{1}<p_{2}<p_{3}<\ldots
and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic asserts that every positive integer
n
uniquely corresponds a sequence
(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\ldots)
such that
n=p_{1}^{x_{1}}p_{2}^{x_{2}}\ldots
. It is clear that the assignment
n\mapsto(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\ldots)
gives the desired isomorphism of posets.

Example 2.4
.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z},+)
and
\mathcal{H}
the set of nontrivial subgroups of
G
. Define
\mathscr{Z}
to be the orbit category of
G
with respect to
\mathcal{H}
. Explicitly, objects are cosets
{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and morphisms
{\mathbf{m}}
to
{\mathbf{n}}
are
G
-equivariant maps. We check that
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}
is a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
.

(1)

\mathscr{Z}({\mathbf{n}},\,{\mathbf{n}})
is a cyclic group of order
n
, so
\mathscr{Z}
is a small skeletal EI category, and so is
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}
.

(2)

There is an embedding functor
\iota:{\mathscr{D}}\to\mathscr{Z}^{\op}
sending a morphism
m\to n
in
{\mathscr{D}}
to the following
G
-equivariant map

\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z},\quad 1+n\mathbb{Z}\mapsto 1+m\mathbb{Z}

in
\mathscr{Z}
, which is a morphism in
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}({\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{n}})
. Clearly, this functor gives a bijection on object sets.

(3)

A direct computation shows that
\mathscr{Z}({\mathbf{n}},{\mathbf{n}})
(resp.,
\mathscr{Z}({\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{m}})
) acts regularly (resp., transitively) on
\mathscr{Z}({\mathbf{m}},\,{\mathbf{n}})
from the left (resp., right) side when
n
divides
m
, so
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}({\mathbf{n}},{\mathbf{n}})
(resp.,
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}({\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{m}})
) acts regularly (resp., transitively) on
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}({\mathbf{n}},\,{\mathbf{m}})
from the right (resp., left) side.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\mathscr{CI}
be the category whose objects are finite cyclic groups
{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and morphisms are injective group homomorphisms. We check that
\mathscr{CI}
is a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
.

(1)

There is a natural embedding functor from
{\mathscr{D}}
to
\mathscr{CI}
sending a morphism
m\to n
in
{\mathscr{D}}
to the following group homomorphism

\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\quad 1+m\mathbb{Z}\mapsto\frac{n}{m}+n\mathbb{Z}.

(2)

When
n
divides
m
,
{\mathbf{m}}
contains a unique subgroup isomorphic to
{\mathbf{n}}
, so
\mathscr{CI}({\mathbf{n}},\,{\mathbf{n}})
acts regularly on
\mathscr{CI}({\mathbf{n}},{\mathbf{m}})
from the right side.

(3)

When
n
divides
m
, every automorphism of
{\mathbf{n}}
can extend to an automorphism of
{\mathbf{m}}
, so
\mathscr{CI}({\mathbf{m}},\,{\mathbf{m}})
acts transitively on
\mathscr{CI}({\mathbf{n}},{\mathbf{m}})
from the left side.

Example 2.6
.

Let
\mathscr{CS}
be the category whose objects are finite cyclic groups
{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and morphisms are surjective group homomorphisms. The reader can check that
\mathscr{CS}^{\op}
is a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. Note that
\mathscr{CS}
is different from the orbit category
\mathscr{Z}
in Example
2.4
since here we regard
{\mathbf{n}}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module, while in Example
2.4
we only regard
{\mathbf{n}}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-set of the additive group
\mathbb{Z}
.

From now on let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. A function
\|\bullet\|:\Ob({\mathscr{C}})\to\mathbb{N}
is called a
norm
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\|{\mathbf{x}}\|\geqslant 0
and the equality holds if and only if
{\mathbf{x}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the sequence whose each entry is 0;

(2)

\|a{\mathbf{x}}\|=a\|{\mathbf{x}}\|
for each
a\in\mathbb{N}
;

(3)

\|{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}\|\leqslant\|{\mathbf{x}}\|+\|{\mathbf{y}}\|
.

In this paper we mainly consider the following norms, called the
sup norm
and the
sum norm
respectively:

{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\sup\{x_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\};\quad{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}x_{i}.

2.2.
Representations and homological invariants

Throughout this subsection let
k
be a commutative ring. A representation of
{\mathscr{C}}
over
k
, or a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module, is a covariant functor from
{\mathscr{C}}
to
k\Mod
. Denote the value of
V
on an object
{\mathbf{x}}
by
V_{{\mathbf{x}}}
, and the
support
of
V
is set to be

\supp(V)=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})\mid V_{{\mathbf{x}}}\neq 0\}.

The category of all
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules is a Grothendieck category admitting enough projectives. For every object
{\mathbf{x}}
in
{\mathscr{C}}
, denote by
P({\mathbf{x}})
the
k
-linearization of the representable functor
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
.

Let
k{\mathscr{C}}
be the category algebra, which is a free
k
-module with a basis consisting of morphisms in
{\mathscr{C}}
, and whose multiplication is defined by setting
f\cdot g=f\circ g
if they can be composed, and otherwise setting
f\cdot g=0
. It is well known that there is an embedding functor

\iota:{\mathscr{C}}\Mod\to k{\mathscr{C}}\Mod,\quad V\mapsto\bigoplus_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})}V(x)

and another functor

\pi:k{\mathscr{C}}\Mod\to{\mathscr{C}}\Mod,\quad M\mapsto(V:{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto 1_{{\mathbf{x}}}M)

where
1_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is the identity morphism on the object
{\mathbf{x}}
. Clearly,
\pi\circ\iota
is isomorphic to the identity functor on
{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
, but
\iota\circ\pi
is isomorphic to the identity functor on
k{\mathscr{C}}\Mod
if and only if
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
is a finite set. In other words, a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module is a
graded

k{\mathscr{C}}
-module graded by
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
.

The category algebra
k{\mathscr{C}}
has a two-sided ideal
\mathfrak{I}
which as a free
k
-module is spanned by all morphisms
f:{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{y}}
such that
{\mathbf{x}}\neq{\mathbf{y}}
. Given a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module
V
, we define the zeroth homology group of
V
by

H_{0}(V)=k{\mathscr{C}}/\mathfrak{I}\otimes_{k{\mathscr{C}}}V

which is a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module, and its value on
{\mathbf{x}}
is isomorphic to

V_{{\mathbf{x}}}/\sum_{{\mathbf{y}}<{\mathbf{x}}\atop f\in{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})}V_{f}(V_{{\mathbf{y}}}).

Since
k{\mathscr{C}}/\mathfrak{I}\otimes_{k{\mathscr{C}}}-
is right exact, for each
i\geqslant 0
, we can define the
i
-th homology group of
V
by

H_{i}(V)=\Tor_{i}^{k{\mathscr{C}}}(k{\mathscr{C}}/\mathfrak{I},\,V).

Correspondingly, the
i
-th homological degree with respect to a fixed norm
\|\bullet\|
is defined to be

\hd_{i}(V)=\sup\{\|{\mathbf{x}}\|\mid{\mathbf{x}}\in\supp(H_{i}(V))\}

which might be infinity. We set
\hd_{i}(V)
to be
-1
when the above set is empty by convention. For brevity, we write
\gd(V)
for
\hd_{0}(V)
and
\pd(V)
for
\max\{\hd_{0}(V),\,\hd_{1}(V)\}
, and call them the
generation degree
and
presentation degree
. The
{\mathscr{C}}
-module
V
is said to be
generated in finite degrees
(resp.,
presented in finite degrees
) if
\gd(V)
is finite (resp.,
\pd(V)
is finite).

Remark 2.7
.

It is easy to see from the above definitions that if
V
as a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module is generated by its values on objects in a subset
S\subseteq\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, then
S
contains
\supp(H_{0}(V))
as a subset. It is also clear that every non-invertible morphism in
{\mathscr{C}}
acts on
H_{i}(V)
as the zero map for each
i\geqslant 0
.

Now we turn to torsion theory of
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules. Given a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module
V
and an object
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, an element
v\in V_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is called
torsion
if there is a morphism
f:{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{y}}
such that
V_{f}(v)=0\in V_{{\mathbf{y}}}
. Since
{\mathscr{C}}
satisfies the left Ore condition by Lemma
2.2
, it follows that all torsion elements in
V
form a
{\mathscr{C}}
-submodule of
V
, called the
torsion part
of
V
and denoted by
V_{T}
. The quotient module
V/V_{T}
is called the
torsion free part
of
V
. We say that
V
is
torsion
if
V=V_{T}
, and
torsion free
if
V_{T}=0
. The
torsion set
of
V
along the
i
-th direction is

\ts_{i}(V)=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})\mid\exists\,0\neq v\in V_{{\mathbf{x}}}\text{ such that there is a map }f\in{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},\,{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i})\text{ satisfying }V_{f}(v)=0\},

where
{\mathbf{1}}_{i}
is the sequence of natural numbers such that the
i

Again, if

Remark 2.8
.

By definition,
\tht_{i}(V)=n
if and only if for any morphism

3.
Representations of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}

In this section we let
k
be a commutative ring and let
\mathscr{N}
be the poset
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. As mentioned before, its representations are closely related to modules of the polynomial ring

with countably many variables. Note that modules over

coincide with representations of the quiver with one vertex and countably many loops such that every two loops commute. Since there is a natural quotient functor from
\mathscr{N}
to this quiver (viewed as a category), every module over

can be viewed as a representation of
\mathscr{N}
. Explicitly, given an

-module

, one can construct a representation

of
\mathscr{N}
by setting

for
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob(\mathscr{N})
, and the morphism

for each

can be defined by the action of
X_{i}
on
M
. Conversely, given a
\mathscr{N}
-module

, one can define a multi-graded
A
-module

and the action of

on

is given by maps

componentwise. These two procedures are functorial, but are not inverse to each other even up to equivalence.

3.1.
Bounds for invariants with respect to the sup norm

In this subsection we use the sup norm

We generalize the conclusion of the above lemma to the general situation. Recall that an

Proposition 3.5

Let
V

{\mathscr{N}}
-module. Then
\hd_{s}(V)\leqslant\pd(V)
for

{\mathscr{N}}
-modules presented in finite degrees is abelian.

Proof.

The inequality holds trivially for
s\leqslant 1
. Take a short exact sequence

such that
P
is a torsion free projective
{\mathscr{N}}
-module and
\gd(P)=\gd(V)
. For

\hd_{s}(V)=\hd_{s-1}(W)\leqslant\gd(W)\leqslant\max\{\gd(V),\,\hd_{1}(V)\}=\pd(V),

Let

be a short exact sequence of

∎

3.2.
Bounds for invariants with respect to the sum norm

It might seem to the reader that some results established in previous subsection with respect to the sup norm are too strong to be interesting, for instance Proposition
3.5
. The reason is that the sup norm is too weak, and hence too many distinct objects share the same norm. For example, objects in the following set

\{{\mathbf{1}}_{1}+{\mathbf{1}}_{2}+\ldots+{\mathbf{1}}_{s}\mid s\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}

all have norm 1, which seems quite unnatural. Indeed, this norm does not respect the poset structure of
{\mathscr{N}}
; that is, one does not have
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|<\|{\mathbf{y}}\|
when
{\mathbf{x}}<{\mathbf{y}}
.

In this subsection we consider the more reasonable sum norm. Throughout this subsection all homological degrees are defined with respect to the sum norm, but we use the same notation. Hopefully this would not cause too much confusion to the reader. Unfortunately, the situation becomes much more complicated. For instance, in contrast to Proposition
3.5
, the category of
{\mathscr{N}}
-modules presented in finite degrees with respect to the sum norm is no longer abelian, as illustrated by the following example.

.

\textstyle{P^{\prime\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{V^{\prime\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

Since
\dim_{k}(H_{0}(V^{\prime\prime}))\leqslant\dim_{k}(H_{0}(P^{\prime\prime}))=\omega-1
and
\hd_{1}(V^{\prime\prime})\leqslant\gd(W^{\prime\prime})\leqslant\gd(W)=\hd_{1}(V)
, the induction hypothesis tells us that

\hd_{2}(V^{\prime\prime})\leqslant 2^{\omega-1}\hd_{1}(V^{\prime\prime})\leqslant 2^{\omega-1}\gd(W^{\prime\prime})\leqslant 2^{\omega-1}\hd_{1}(V).

By looking at the first column, we deduce that

\hd_{1}(V^{\prime})\leqslant\gd(W^{\prime})\leqslant\max\{\gd(W),\,\hd_{1}(W^{\prime\prime})\}\leqslant\max\{\gd(W),\,2^{\omega-1}\hd_{1}(V)\}=2^{\omega-1}\hd_{1}(V).

Again, by the established conclusion for
\omega=1
, we have
\hd_{2}(V^{\prime})\leqslant 2\hd_{1}(V^{\prime})\leqslant 2^{\omega}\hd_{1}(V)
. By looking at the last column, we get the desired upper bound.
∎

3.3.
Shift functors

In this subsection we introduce shift functors and describe some elementary properties. Throughout this subsection let
k
be a commutative ring and all numerical invariants are defined with respect to the sup norm.

Note that for each positive integer
i
there is a functor
\iota_{i}:{\mathscr{N}}\to{\mathscr{N}}
sending
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{N}})
to
{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}
, and the unique morphism
{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{y}}
to the unique morphism
{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}\to{\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}
. The shift functor
\Sigma_{i}:{\mathscr{N}}\Mod\to{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
along the
i
-th direction is the pullback of
\iota_{i}
, mapping an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module
V
to
V\circ\iota_{i}
. The following lemma collects some elementary properties of
\Sigma_{i}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\Sigma_{i}
be the
i

(1)

\Sigma_{i}
is exact;

(2)

\Sigma_{i}\circ\Sigma_{j}=\Sigma_{j}\circ\Sigma_{i}
;

(3)

there is a natural transformation
\phi^{i}
between the identity functor on
{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
and
\Sigma_{i}
;

(4)

the kernel
K_{i}V
of the map
V\to\Sigma_{i}V
has the following description

K_{i}V=\bigoplus_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{N}})}\{v\in V_{{\mathbf{x}}}\mid v\text{ is sent to 0 by the map }{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}\}.

(5)

the natural maps
V\to\Sigma_{i}V
is injective for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if
V
is torsion free;

(6)

for
P({\mathbf{x}})=k{\mathscr{N}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
, one has

\Sigma_{i}P({\mathbf{x}})\cong\begin{cases}P({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{1}}_{i}),&\text{if }x_{i}\geqslant 1;\\
P({\mathbf{x}}),&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

The first two statements follow readily from the definition. The third statement follows from the existence of a natural transformation between the identity functor on
{\mathscr{N}}
and
\iota_{i}
, illustrated by the diagram

\textstyle{{\mathbf{x}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{{\mathbf{y}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\iota_{i}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota_{i}f}
\textstyle{\iota_{i}{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}.}

This diagram also implies the forth statement, which in turn applies the fifth statement. The last statement holds by a very simple observation.
∎

Now we construct the shift functor along all directions, which intuitively can be viewed as an infinite composite of
\Sigma_{i}
for all

. By statement (3) of Lemma
3.11
, one can construct a sequence

\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{V}^{1}}
\textstyle{\Sigma_{1}V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi^{2}_{\Sigma_{1}V}}
\textstyle{\Sigma_{[2]}V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi^{3}_{\Sigma_{[2]}V}}
\textstyle{\Sigma_{[3]}V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ldots,}

where
\Sigma_{[n]}=\Sigma_{n}\circ\Sigma_{n-1}\circ\ldots\circ\Sigma_{1}
for

. Taking the colimit of this diagram we obtain an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module. Clearly, the rule assigning
V
to this colimit is functorial, so induces an endofunctor in
{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
denoted by
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Furthermore, there is a natural map from
V
to
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V
such that the following diagram commutes

(3.1)

\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi^{1}_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{\Sigma_{1}V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi^{2}_{\Sigma_{1}V}}
\scriptstyle{\psi^{1}}
\textstyle{\Sigma_{[2]}V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi^{2}}
\textstyle{\ldots}
\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V.}

We describe some useful facts about
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose that
V
is a nonzero
{\mathscr{N}}
-module. One has:

(1)

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is an exact functor;

(2)

\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)\leqslant\gd(V)
, and
\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)\leqslant\gd(V)-1
when
\gd(V)\geqslant 1
;

(3)

for

, one has
\tht_{i}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)\leqslant\tht_{i}(V)-1
whenever
\tht_{i}(V)\geqslant 0
;

(4)

the map

is injective if and only if

is torsion free;

(5)

if
V
is torsion free, so is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V
.

(6)

if
V
is torsion, so is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V

Proof.

All facts follow from standard arguments about direct limits.

(1) This is clear since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is the direct limit of a sequence of exact functors, and hence is exact.

(2) First we prove the conclusion for the special case that
V=P({\mathbf{x}})
. Let
N
be the minimal integer such that
x_{i}=0
for
i>N
. By (6) of Lemma
3.11
,
\Sigma_{[i]}V\cong P({\mathbf{y}})
for
i\geqslant N
where

y_{j}=\begin{cases}x_{j},&\text{if }x_{j}=0;\\
x_{j}-1,&\text{if }x_{j}\geqslant 1.\end{cases}

Furthermore, all but finitely many maps appearing in diagram (3.1) are isomorphisms. Consequently,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}P({\mathbf{x}})\cong P({\mathbf{y}})
, and
\|{\mathbf{y}}\|=\|{\mathbf{x}}\|-1
whenever
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|\geqslant 1
, so the conclusion holds for this special case.

Now consider the general case. Take a surjection

P=\bigoplus_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\supp(H_{0}(V))}P({\mathbf{x}})^{c_{{\mathbf{x}}}}\to V

where the multiplicity
c_{{\mathbf{x}}}
might be infinity. It is clear that
\gd(P)=\gd(V)
. Applying
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
to it we obtain a surjection
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}P\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V
. If
\gd(V)\geqslant 1
, the conclusion for the special case asserts that
\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}P)=\gd(P)-1
, so

\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)\leqslant\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}P)=\gd(P)-1=\gd(V)-1

as desired.

(3) Without loss of generality we can assume that
\tht_{i}(V)=n\geqslant 0
is finite. By Remark

, for each object
{\mathbf{x}}
with

is injective. Then when

, for any object
{\mathbf{y}}

, the map
(\Sigma_{[s]}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}}\to(\Sigma_{[s]}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}}
is injective by Remark

since
(\Sigma_{[s]}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}}=V_{{\mathbf{z}}}

z_{j}=\begin{cases}y_{j}+1,&j\leqslant s;\\
y_{j},&j\geqslant s+1,\end{cases}

and in particular
z_{i}=y_{i}+1\geqslant n+1
. Taking direct limit for the commutative diagram

\textstyle{V_{{\mathbf{y}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\Sigma_{1}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\Sigma_{[2]}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ldots}
\textstyle{V_{{\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\Sigma_{1}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\Sigma_{[2]}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ldots}

and noting that all but finitely many vertical maps are injective, we conclude that the map

is also injective. Again, by Remark

, it follows that

.

(4) If
V
is not torsion free, then by statement (5) of Lemma

, there is a certain

such that the natural map

is not injective; that is,
\tht_{i}(V)\geqslant 0
. It is easy to check that
\tht_{i}(\Sigma_{[i]}V)<\tht_{i}(V)
. Consequently, in the top row of diagram (

), the composite map from

to

Conversely, if

) are injective. If

is not injective, then one may find an object

and a certain

such that

eventually becomes 0 by maps in the top row, which is impossible.

(5) This is clear. Indeed, if
V
is torsion free, then by the previous statement, the natural map

is injective. Since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is exact, the natural map
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)
is also injective. Again by the previous statement,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V
is torsion free.

(6) For each object
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{N}})
as well as an arbitrary element

, we want to show that

is torsion. By definition,
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is the direct limit of the following sequence

V_{{\mathbf{x}}}\to(\Sigma_{1}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}\to(\Sigma_{[2]}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}\to\ldots.

Then for a sufficiently large

\tilde{v}\in(\Sigma_{[n]}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}
of

. It is easy to see that
\Sigma_{[n]}V
is torsion, so we can find another object

such that the map

sends

to

. By looking at the commutative diagram

\textstyle{(\Sigma_{[n]}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\Sigma_{[n]}V)_{{\mathbf{y}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

we conclude that

∎

Remark 3.13
.

In general one can not expect that
\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)=\gd(V)-1
if
\gd(V)\geqslant 1
. For example, let
V
be a torsion free module such that
V_{{\mathbf{x}}}=0
if
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|\leqslant 1
, and

otherwise. Then
\gd(V)\geqslant 2
since clearly the object

is contained in

. However, a direct computation shows that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V\cong k{\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{0},-)
, so
\gd(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V)=0
.

Remark 3.14
.

Let
\boldsymbol{K}
be the kernel functor induced by the natural transformation
\psi
in diagram (
3.1
). It is easy to see that an element

lies in

if there is a non-invertible morphism

such that

for

and
V_{f}(v)=0
.

Now we introduce a type of special
{\mathscr{N}}
-modules, which is a generalization of projective

-modules.

Definition 3.15

Let
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}
be the subcategory of
{\mathscr{N}}
consisting of the single object

. A

-module

is said to be induced at

if

is isomorphic to the left Kan extension of

with respect to the inclusion functor

If

, then for

V_{{\mathbf{y}}}\cong V_{{\mathbf{x}}}
if

, and

otherwise. Correspondingly, each map

is either an isomorphism or the zero map.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
V
be an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module presented in finite degrees. Then for

,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V
is either the zero module or induced at
\boldsymbol{0}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}
is the composite of

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

When
V
is torsion,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V
is also torsion by statement (6) of Lemma

. However, by statement (3) of this lemma as well as Proposition
3.2
, we know that
\tht_{i}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V)=-1
for all

; that is,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V
is torsion free. This happens if and only if
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V
is the zero module.

If
V
is not torsion free, we consider the short exact sequence
0\to V_{T}\to V\to V_{F}\to 0
where
V_{T}
is the torsion part and
V_{F}
is the torsion free part. Applying
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}
to it and using the same argument as in the previous paragraph we deduce that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V_{T}=0
, so
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V\cong\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V_{F}
, which is torsion free by statement (5) of Lemma

, and whose generation degree is at most 0 by statement (2) of Lemma

. Since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V_{F}
cannot be the zero module, it follows that it is a torsion free

-module with generation degree 0, which is clearly induced at
\boldsymbol{0}
.
∎

Corollary 3.17
.

If
V
is a torsion free
{\mathscr{N}}
-module presented in finite degrees, then it can be embedded into an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module induced at
\boldsymbol{0}
.

Proof.

Let
s=\pd(V)
. The composite of the following sequence is an injective map

V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}V\to\ldots\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V

with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}V
the desired
{\mathscr{N}}
-module induced at
\boldsymbol{0}
.
∎

In the rest of this subsection we want to generalize the above results to arbitrary

-modules. For this purpose, we need to introduce the “infinite product” of

We denote the colimit of this sequence in

Remark 3.18
.

We construct
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}
as the direct limit of

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
V
be an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module. One has:

(1)

If
V
is torsion, then
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V=0
; otherwise,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V

.

(2)

If

is torsion free, then it can be embedded into
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V
.

(1) Suppose that
V
is torsion. Then for any
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{N}})
and any

, one can find a representative
\tilde{v}\in(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{r}V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}
for a large enough

. By statement (6) of Lemma

,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{r}V
is also torsion. In particular, we can find a morphism

such that

to 0. Let

By Remark

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V

(2) Since all maps in the sequence

4.
Homological degrees of representations of
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
-type combinatorial categories

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be an arbitrary combinatorial category of type
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}

-modules in terms of their first two homological degrees, generalizing Proposition

Denote by
{\mathscr{N}}
the poset
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}

\iota:{\mathscr{N}}\to{\mathscr{C}}

\iota
is a bijection restricted to object sets. The functor
\iota:{\mathscr{N}}\to{\mathscr{C}}
induces a restriction functor
\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}:{\mathscr{C}}\Mod\to{\mathscr{N}}\Mod

Lemma 4.1
.

Given
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})

,

P({\mathbf{x}})\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\cong Q({\mathbf{x}})^{|G_{{\mathbf{x}}}|},

where
|G_{{\mathbf{x}}}|
is the order of
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
.

Proof.

Note that
P({\mathbf{x}})
as a free
k
-module has a basis consisting of morphisms in
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
. Without loss of generality we can view
{\mathscr{N}}
as a subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}
, so
{\mathscr{N}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
is a subset of
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
. For
g\in{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})
, define

{\mathscr{N}}({\mathbf{x}},-)g=\{fg\mid f\in{\mathscr{N}}({\mathbf{x}},-)\}.

Since
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
acts regularly on
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
for any
{\mathbf{y}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, it follows that
{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
is a disjoint union of
{\mathscr{N}}({\mathbf{x}},-)g
with
g
ranging over all elements in
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
. The conclusion then follows from this observation.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
V
be a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module, and
\|\bullet\|
be either the sup norm or the sum norm on
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
. Then
\gd(V)=\gd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})
and
\pd(V)=\pd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})
.

Proof.

Take a short exact sequence
0\to W\to P\to V\to 0
such that
P
is a projective
{\mathscr{C}}
-module and
\gd(P)=\gd(V)
. Applying the restriction functor we get the following short exact sequence of
{\mathscr{N}}
-modules

0\to W\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\to P\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\to V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\to 0,

which implies that

\gd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\leqslant\gd(P\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})=\gd(P)=\gd(V)

where the first equality comes from Lemma
4.1
. On the other hand, for any object
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
such that
{\mathbf{x}}\in\supp(H_{0}(V))
, we know that

\sum_{{\mathbf{y}}<{\mathbf{x}}\atop f\in{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})}V_{f}(V_{{\mathbf{y}}})\subsetneqq V_{{\mathbf{x}}}.

Since
{\mathscr{N}}
can be viewed as a subcategory of
{\mathscr{C}}
, it follows that

\sum_{{\mathbf{y}}<{\mathbf{x}}\atop f\in{\mathscr{N}}({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})}V_{f}(V_{{\mathbf{y}}})\subsetneqq V_{{\mathbf{x}}},

that is,
{\mathbf{x}}\in\supp(H_{0}(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}))
. Consequently,
\gd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\geqslant\gd(V)
, so the first equality follows.

Now we turn to the second equality. On one hand, we have

\hd_{1}(V)\leqslant\gd(W)=\gd(W\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\leqslant\max\{\gd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}),\,\hd_{1}(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\}=\pd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})

by considering the above two short exact sequences. By the same argument, we also have

\hd_{1}(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\leqslant\gd(W\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})=\gd(W)\leqslant\max\{\gd(V),\,\hd_{1}(V)\}=\pd(V).

Consequently, one has
\pd(V)\leqslant\pd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})
and
\pd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\leqslant\pd(V)
, and the second equality holds.
∎

The main result of this section is a combination of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\|\bullet\|
be the sup norm on
\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, and
V
a
{\mathscr{C}}
-module. Then for
s\geqslant 0
, one has

\hd_{s}(V)\leqslant\pd(V),

so the category of
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules presented in finite degrees is abelian. Moreover, if
k
is a field and
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is a finite group for each
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{C}})
, then the category of finitely presented
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules is abelian.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
V
is presented in finite degrees. The conclusion holds trivially for
s\leqslant 1
. As before, take a suitable short exact sequence
0\to W\to P\to V\to 0
of
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules, which induces a short exact sequence of
{\mathscr{N}}
-modules:

0\to W\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\to P\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\to V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}\to 0,

For
s\geqslant 2
, we have

\displaystyle\hd_{s}(V)

\displaystyle=\hd_{s-1}(W)\leqslant\max\{\gd(W),\,\hd_{1}(W)\}

by the induction hypothesis

\displaystyle=\max\{\gd(W\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}),\,\hd_{1}(W\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\}

by Lemma
4.2

\displaystyle=\max\{\gd(W),\,\hd_{2}(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\}

by Lemma
4.2

\displaystyle\leqslant\max\{\gd(W),\,\gd(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}),\,\hd_{1}(V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}})\}

by Proposition
3.5

\displaystyle=\max\{\gd(W),\,\gd(V),\,\hd_{1}(V)\}

by Lemma
4.2

\displaystyle=\max\{\gd(V),\,\hd_{1}(V)\}

as desired.

Now suppose that
k
is a field and each
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
is a finite group. The argument in the proof of Lemma
4.2
tells us that the
{\mathscr{C}}
-module
V
is finitely generated if and only if so is the
{\mathscr{N}}
-module
V\downarrow_{{\mathscr{N}}}^{{\mathscr{C}}}
. The second statement then follows from this observation as well as Lemma
3.7
and Remark
3.9
.
∎

5.
Representations of the Young lattice

Throughout this section let
k
be a field,
{\mathscr{N}}
the poset
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, and let
{\mathscr{Y}}
be the Young lattice, which is precisely the full subcategory of
{\mathscr{N}}
consisting of objects
{\mathbf{x}}
such that
x_{i}\geqslant x_{j}
whenever
i\leqslant j
. We obtain a natural inclusion functor
\iota:{\mathscr{Y}}\to{\mathscr{N}}
and hence a restriction functor
\iota^{\ast}:{\mathscr{N}}\Mod\to{\mathscr{Y}}\Mod
.

The category
{\mathscr{Y}}
can also be viewed as a quotient category of
{\mathscr{N}}
by the action of the infinite symmetric group. Explicitly, let

S_{\infty}=\varinjlim S_{n}=\{\sigma:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\mid\sigma\text{ is a bijection and }\sigma(n)=n\text{ for }n\gg 0\}.

This group naturally acts on
{\mathscr{N}}
as follows:
\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{\sigma(1)},\,x_{\sigma(2)},\,\ldots)
. Moreover,
{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant{\mathbf{y}}
if and only if
\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{y}}
for any
\sigma\in S_{\infty}
. Therefore, one can obtain a quotient poset
{\mathscr{N}}/S_{\infty}
, whose objects are orbits
[{\mathbf{x}}]
under this action, and
[{\mathbf{x}}]\leqslant[{\mathbf{y}}]
if and only if there are
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in[{\mathbf{x}}]
and
{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\in[{\mathbf{y}}]
such that
{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant{\mathbf{y}}
. It is easy to see that
{\mathscr{Y}}
is isomorphic to this quotient poset. Via identifying
{\mathscr{Y}}
with this quotient poset, we obtain a quotient functor
q:{\mathscr{N}}\to{\mathscr{Y}}
which induces a fully faithful lift functor
q^{\ast}:{\mathscr{Y}}\Mod\to{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
. Explicitly, given a
{\mathscr{Y}}
-module
W
, the corresponded
{\mathscr{N}}
-module
q^{\ast}W
has the following structure: for a morphism
f:{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{y}}
in
{\mathscr{N}}
, one has
(q^{\ast}W)_{{\mathbf{x}}}=W_{[{\mathbf{x}}]}
,
(q^{\ast}W)_{{\mathbf{y}}}=W_{[{\mathbf{y}}]}
, and
(q^{\ast}W)_{f}
is defined to be
W_{[f]}:W_{[{\mathbf{x}}]}\to W_{[{\mathbf{y}}]}
.

Since the composite
q\iota
is the identity functor on
{\mathscr{Y}}
, it follows that
\iota^{\ast}q^{\ast}
is isomorphic to the identity functor on
{\mathscr{Y}}\Mod
. Furthermore, the image of
q^{\ast}
is the full subcategory of
S_{\infty}
-symmetric
{\mathscr{N}}
-modules
. To define this notion, let
V
be an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module and
\sigma
an element in
S_{\infty}
. One can define another
{\mathscr{N}}
-module
\sigma V
as follows: for a morphism
f:{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{y}}
in
{\mathscr{N}}
, one lets
(\sigma V)_{{\mathbf{x}}}=V_{\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}
,
(\sigma V)_{{\mathbf{y}}}=V_{\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{y}}}
, and
(\sigma V)_{f}
is defined to be
V_{\sigma\cdot f}:V_{\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}\to V_{\sigma\cdot{\mathbf{y}}}
. We say that
V
is
S_{\infty}
-symmetric if
\sigma V
is isomorphic to
V
for every
\sigma\in S_{\infty}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
k
be a field. Then the category of finitely generated
{\mathscr{Y}}
-modules over
k
is abelian.

Proof.

It suffices to show that every submodule
V
of
k{\mathscr{Y}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
is finitely generated for
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
. If
V
is not finitely generated, then since the dimension of
V_{{\mathbf{y}}}
is either 1 or 0 for
{\mathbf{y}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
, it follows that
\supp(H_{0}(V))
must be an infinite set. Furthermore, elements in this set are pairwise incomparable. Therefore, we get an infinite anti-chain in the poset
{\mathscr{Y}}
. This is impossible. Indeed, since
\mathbb{N}
is an well-ordered set, it follows from Higman’s lemma (see
[
18
]
) that the set of finite sequences over
\mathbb{N}
is quasi-well-ordered with respect to the following ordering:

{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\ldots,\,x_{m})\preccurlyeq{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},\,y_{2},\,\ldots,\,y_{n})

if and only if there is a strictly increasing map
\sigma:[m]\to[n]
such that
x_{i}\leqslant y_{\sigma(i)}
for each
i\in[m]
. But it is easy to see that
{\mathbf{x}}\preccurlyeq{\mathbf{y}}
if and only if
x_{i}\leqslant y_{i}
since entries in
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are listed in a decreasing way; that is,
\preccurlyeq
coincides with the usual ordering
\leqslant
used in this paper. Consequently,
\leqslant
is a quasi-well-order, and hence the poset
{\mathscr{Y}}
cannot have an infinite anti-chain.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

In other words, the incidence algebra
k{\mathscr{Y}}
is locally Noetherian, or the incidence algebra
k{\mathscr{N}}
is
S_{\infty}
-symmetric Noetherian: if an
S_{\infty}
-symmetric
{\mathscr{N}}
-module is generated by its values on objects lying in only finitely many
S_{\infty}
-orbits and each such value is finite dimensional, then its
S_{\infty}
-symmetric submodules also have this property. Parallel results for
A=k[X_{1},\,X_{2},\,\ldots]
were established in the literature, see for instances
[
1
,
12
]
.

Note that the sum norm on
\Ob({\mathscr{N}})
induces the sum norm on
{\mathscr{Y}}
, which is compatible with the poset structure on
\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
. One can also define homological degrees of
{\mathscr{Y}}
-modules with respect to this norm. For an
{\mathscr{N}}
-module, the finiteness of its zeroth and first homological degrees cannot guarantee the finiteness of the second homological degree, as shown in Example
3.6
. But for
{\mathscr{Y}}
-modules, we have a different situation.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
k
be a field,
\|\bullet\|
the sum norm on
\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
, and
V
a
{\mathscr{Y}}
-module. If
{\mathscr{Y}}
is presented in finite degrees, then
\hd_{s}(V)
is finite for all
s\geqslant 1
. In particular, the category of
{\mathscr{Y}}
-modules presented in finite degrees is abelian.

Proof.

We only prove the first statement since the second one follows immediately. Take a short exact sequence
0\to W\to P\to V\to 0
such that
P
is a projective
{\mathscr{Y}}
-module and
H_{0}(P)\cong H_{0}(V)
(the reader can easily check that this is possible). As in the proof of Proposition
3.5
, it suffices to show that
\hd_{2}(V)=\hd_{1}(W)
is finite. We give a proof similar to that of Lemma
3.7
.

Without loss of generality suppose that
W
is nonzero and
\gd(W)=n
. Let
S=\supp(H_{0}(W))
, which is a nonempty finite set, and let
W({\mathbf{x}})
be the submodule of
W
generated by
W_{{\mathbf{x}}}
. Then

W=\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in S}W({\mathbf{x}}).

Since
W({\mathbf{x}})
is torsion free, it is actually a direct sum of
k{\mathscr{Y}}({\mathbf{x}},-)
. Thus we obtain a surjection

Q=\bigoplus_{{\mathbf{x}}\in S}W({\mathbf{x}})\longrightarrow\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in S}W({\mathbf{x}})=W,

and hence a short exact sequence
0\to K\to Q\to W\to 0
, from which we deduce that
\hd_{1}(W)\leqslant\gd(K)
.

Now we investigate
K
. Fix
{\mathbf{z}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
such that
K_{{\mathbf{z}}}\neq 0
. Then
{\mathbf{z}}\geqslant{\mathbf{x}}
for a certain
{\mathbf{x}}\in S
. Define

T=S\cap\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{N}})\mid{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant{\mathbf{z}}\}

which is again a nonempty finite set. We have

K_{{\mathbf{z}}}=\{(W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}}))_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}\mid w_{{\mathbf{x}}}\in W_{{\mathbf{x}}},\,\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}})=0\},

where
{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{z}}
is the morphism from
{\mathbf{x}}
to
{\mathbf{z}}
.

Note that
{\mathbf{z}}
is an upper bound of elements in
T
. Let
\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}
be the least upper bound of elements in
T
, which always exists since
T
is a finite set. Then
{\mathbf{z}}\geqslant\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}
. However, if
{\mathbf{z}}>\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}
, then

W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}})=W_{\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}(W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}})).

Note that the map
W_{\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}
is injective since
W
as a submodule of
Q
is torsion free. Consequently, we have

\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}})=0,

so
(W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}}))_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}
is contained
K_{\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}}
. Furthermore, the map
K_{\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}
sends
(W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}}))_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}
to
(W_{{\mathbf{x}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}(w_{{\mathbf{x}}}))_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}
. Consequently, the map
K_{\tilde{{\mathbf{z}}}\to{\mathbf{z}}}
is surjective, and hence
{\mathbf{z}}
is not contained in
\supp(H_{0}(K))
.

We have shown that
{\mathbf{z}}\in\supp(H_{0}(K))
only if there is a nonempty finite set
T
of
S
such that
{\mathbf{z}}
is precisely the least upper bound of elements in
T
. Since
S
is a finite set, there are only finitely many such least upper bounds. Consequently,
\supp(H_{0}(K))
is a finite set, so
\gd(K)
is finite as well. This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

By comparing the proof of this theorem and Example
3.6
, the reason why the above conclusion is not valid for
{\mathscr{N}}
-modules
V
becomes transparent: in that case
\supp(H_{0}(V))
in general is an infinite set, so it has infinitely many finite subsets and correspondingly one can get infinitely many least upper bounds.

Remark 5.5
.

Note that for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are only finitely many objects
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
such that the sum norm
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|=n
. Therefore, a
{\mathscr{Y}}
-module
V
is presented in finite degrees if and only if it is presented in finitely many objects, an intrinsic property independent of norms on
\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
.

Finally we describe a recursive upper bound for homological degrees of
{\mathscr{Y}}
-modules.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
k
be a field,
\|\bullet\|
the sum norm on
\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
, and
V
a
{\mathscr{Y}}
-module. If
s\geqslant 2
and
\hd_{s-1}(V)>0
, then one has

\hd_{s}(V)\leqslant\hd_{s-1}(V)(\ln(\hd_{s-1}(V))+1).

Note that
\hd_{s-1}(V)=-1
if and only if
H_{s-1}(V)=0
, and in this case
\hd_{s}(V)=-1
. If
\hd_{s-1}(V)=0
, then there is a projective resolution
\ldots\to P^{1}\to V\to 0
such that the image
V^{s-1}
of
P^{s}\to P^{s-1}
is a torsion free
{\mathscr{Y}}
-module generated by the value of
V^{s-1}
on the object
\boldsymbol{0}
. But it follows that
V^{s-1}
is projective, so
\hd_{s}(V)=-1
as well.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the inequality for
s=2
. Let
W
and
K
be as in the proof of Theorem
5.3
. Since we have assumed that
H_{0}(P)\cong H_{0}(V)
, it follows that
\hd_{1}(V)=\gd(W)
. Without loss of generality we can assume this number to be
n
. Let
T
be the subset of
\Ob({\mathscr{Y}})
consisting of objects
{\mathbf{y}}
with
\|{\mathbf{y}}\|=n
, and let
S=\supp(H_{0}(W))
. For every
{\mathbf{x}}\in S
, since
\|{\mathbf{x}}\|\leqslant n
, we can find a certain
{\mathbf{y}}\in T
such that
{\mathbf{x}}\leqslant{\mathbf{y}}
. Consequently, the least upper bound
\bigvee_{{\mathbf{x}}\in S}{\mathbf{x}}
of
S
is less than or equal to
\bigvee_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}{\mathbf{x}}
.

It is not hard to see from the proof of Theorem
5.3
that
\gd(K)
is bounded by
\|\bigvee_{{\mathbf{x}}\in S}{\mathbf{x}}\|
, so

\hd_{2}(V)\leqslant\gd(K)\leqslant\|\bigvee_{{\mathbf{x}}\in S}{\mathbf{x}}\|\leqslant\|\bigvee_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}{\mathbf{x}}\|.

But
T
consists of all partitions of
[n]
which have a common upper bound
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
with
\lambda_{i}=\lfloor n/i\rfloor\leqslant n/i
. Consequently, we have

\hd_{2}(V)\leqslant\|\bigvee_{{\mathbf{x}}\in T}{\mathbf{x}}\|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{n}{i}=n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\leqslant n(\ln n+1).

∎

6.
A sheaf theoretic interpretation

In this section we use sheaf theory over ringed sites to give an interpretation of results established in previous sections from another viewpoint. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and
k
a commutative ring. Recall that a presheaf of
k
-modules over
\mathcal{C}^{\op}
is precisely a
\mathcal{C}
-module. Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the left Ore condition, so one can impose the atomic Grothendieck topology
J_{at}
on
\mathcal{C}^{\op}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op}
be the site
(\mathcal{C}^{\op},\,J_{at})
, and let
\underline{k}
be the constant structure sheaf. For details about atomic topology and sheaves over ringed sites, please refer to
[
6
]
.

The following result is taken from
[
6
]
.

Theorem 6.1
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-module
V
is a sheaf over the ringed site
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\underline{k})
if and only if

\Hom_{\mathcal{C}\Mod}(T,V)=0=\Ext_{\mathcal{C}\Mod}^{1}(T,V)

for any torsion
\mathcal{C}
-module
T
. The category
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
of sheaves of
k
-modules is equivalent to the Serre quotient
\mathcal{C}\Mod/\mathcal{C}\Mod^{\tor}
, where
\mathcal{C}\Mod^{\tor}
is the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}\Mod
consisting of torsion
{\mathscr{C}}
-modules.

When
\mathcal{C}
is a poset satisfying the left Ore condition, we have a very trivial description for
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Every sheaf over
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
is a constant sheaf, and hence
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})\simeq k\Mod
.

Proof.

An explicit equivalence is given as follows. Firstly, since
\mathcal{C}^{\op}
is equipped with the atomic topology,
\mathcal{C}
is a directed set. Therefore, the direct limit functor

\varinjlim:\mathcal{C}\Mod\to k\Mod

is exact. It has a right adjoint, the diagonal functor
\Delta:k\Mod\to\mathcal{C}\Mod
sending each
k
-module to the corresponded constant
\mathcal{C}
-module. Furthermore, a
\mathcal{C}
-module
V
is sent to 0 by
\varinjlim
if and only if
V
is torsion. Therefore, by Theorem
6.1
and
[
9
, Proposition III.2.5]
, we have

{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})\simeq\mathcal{C}\Mod/\mathcal{C}\Mod^{\tor}\simeq k\Mod,

and moreover each sheaf over
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
is an image of
\Delta
, so must be a constant sheaf.
∎

6.1.
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}
as the sheafification functor

Throughout this subsection let
{\mathscr{N}}
be the poset
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
. Recall that the inclusion functor
\textsl{inc}:{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})\to{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
has a left adjoint
\sharp
, the
sheafification functor
, where
\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op}=({\mathscr{N}}^{\op},J_{at})
. It follows from the proof of Corollary
6.2
that
\sharp
is isomorphic to the following composite of functors

\textstyle{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varinjlim}
\textstyle{k\Mod\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Delta}
\textstyle{{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op},\,\underline{k}).}

Furthremore, by Proposition
3.19
and Corollary
6.2
, the shift functor
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}
can be viewed as a functor from
{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
to
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
. Surprisingly, these two functors coincide up to isomorphism.

Proposition 6.3
.

The functor
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}:{\mathscr{N}}\Mod\to{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
is isomorphic to the sheafification functor.

Proof.

Take
V
in
{\mathscr{N}}\Mod
and
W
in
{\mathrm{Sh}}(\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
. We want to construct a natural isomorphism

\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V,\,W)\cong\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(V,\,W);

that is,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}
is the left adjoint of
inc
, where on the right side we write
W
for
\textsl{inc}\,W
for brevity.

Applying
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}
to the short exact sequence
0\to V_{T}\to V\to V_{F}\to 0
we get
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V\cong\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V_{F}
by Proposition
3.19
. Therefore, without loss of generality we can assume that
V
is torsion free, so by Proposition
3.19
we have another short exact sequence

0\to V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V\to U\to 0,

which induces a long exact sequence

0\to\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(U,\,W)\to\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V,\,W)\to\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(V,\,W)\to\Ext_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}^{1}(U,\,W)\to\ldots.

By Remark
3.20
,
U
is a torsion
{\mathscr{N}}
-module. Since
W
is a sheaf over
(\boldsymbol{{\mathscr{N}}}^{\op},\,\underline{k})
, Theorem
6.1
asserts that the first term and the last term vanish. Consequently, we have

\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V,\,W)\cong\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(V,\,W).

Furthermore, all constructions to establish this isomorphism are natural, so the conclusion holds.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

We give a more illustrative explanation that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}
indeed coincides with the sheafification functor. Since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V
is the colimit of the following sequence of
{\mathscr{N}}
-module homomorphisms

V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}V\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{3}V\to\ldots,

\Hom_{{\mathscr{N}}\Mod}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\infty}V,\,W)
is isomorphic to the set of sequences
(\phi^{0},\,\phi^{1},\,\phi^{2},\,\ldots)
of

6.2.

In this subsection let

satisfies the right Ore condition, we can impose the atomic Grothendieck topology on

Recall that every object in

-module, since the canonical morphism

as a

-module homomorphism. In particular, let

be a

-th primitive root of 1, then the corresponded irreducible

-module

can be regarded as an irreducible

-module while

. Denote by

the

-submodule (actually, an irreducible direct summand) of

generated by

is

The following result classifies all indecomposable injective objects in

-modules by

Theorem 6.6

Let

be an algebraically closed field with characteristic 0. Then

gives a bijective correspondence between the set of all primitive roots of 1 and the set of isomorphism classes of indecomposable injective torsion free
\mathscr{Z}^{\op}
-modules.

Proof.

Suppose that
\xi
is a primitive
n
-th root of 1. Clearly,
\underline{L}_{\xi}
is torsion free and indecomposable. We show that it is injective; that is, the functor

be the submodule satisfying the following condition: for every

Since for each object

such that

consisting of all objects

Note that

•
