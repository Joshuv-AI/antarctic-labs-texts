1.
Preamble: The MGT Equation

Given a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
the Moore-Gibson-Thompson (MGT) equation
is the third-order PDE

(1.1)

\partial_{ttt}u+\alpha\partial_{tt}u-\beta\Delta\partial_{t}u-\gamma\Delta u=0,

ruling the evolution of the unknown variable

u=u(\boldsymbol{x},t):\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Here,
-\Delta
is the Laplace-Dirichlet operator, while

\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
are fixed structural parameters.

From the physical viewpoint,
(
1.1
) is a wave-type equation arising
in the context of acoustic wave propagation
with the so-called second sound, being
u

the acoustic pressure,
where the paradox of the infinite speed of propagation
is eliminated by replacing the Fourier law by the Maxwell-Cattaneo one. Such a feature
explains the presence of the third-order derivative in time.
The model equation first appeared in a very old paper of Stokes
[
28
]
,
but it has received a considerable attention only in recent years, where a number of authors devised
many possible applications in nonlinear acoustic and in thermal relaxation in viscous
gases and fluids (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
13
,
25
,
29
]
).

The mathematical analysis of (
1.1
) is very intriguing, and it has been carried out
in several papers (see
[
2
,
3
,
9
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
24
]
).
For all values of the positive parameters

\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, the (linear) equation turns out to generate a strongly continuous semigroup of solutions
on the phase space

{\mathcal{H}}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega).

Here,
L^{2}(\Omega)
is the Lebesgue space of square summable functions on
\Omega
, whereas
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

is the Sobolev space of square summable functions, along with their derivatives, with null trace on the
boundary
\partial\Omega
. However, the asymptotic behavior of the solutions is strongly influenced by the

stability number

\varkappa=\beta-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}.

Indeed, the picture is the following:

\bullet

If
\varkappa>0
the solutions decay exponentially fast.

\bullet

If
\varkappa=0
the energy of the system is conserved.

\bullet

If
\varkappa<0
there are solutions with an exponential blow up.

For this reason, the regimes
\varkappa>0
,
\varkappa=0
and
\varkappa<0
are usually referred to
in the literature as

subcritical
,
critical
and
supercritical
, respectively.
Quite interestingly, in the subcritical case
\varkappa>0
, equation (
1.1
) serves also
as a model for the description of linearly viscoelastic solids (see
[
6
,
9
]
).

2.
Introduction

Lately, the following integral relaxation
of the MGT equation (
1.1
) has been proposed:

\partial_{ttt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{tt}u(t)-\beta\Delta\partial_{t}u(t)-\gamma\Delta u(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\Delta w(t-s)ds=0,

where
w
is a linear combination of
u
and
\partial_{t}u
,
and
g
is a positive decreasing kernel.
The convolution term
has been added in order to take into account molecular relaxation phenomena,
which introduce a sort of delay in the dynamics, producing nonlocal effects in time

[
21
,
26
]
.
Loosely speaking, the outcome is that at any time
t
the system keeps memory of
(and it is influenced by) its past evolution.
Depending on the physical properties of the medium and the environment,
the variable
w(t)
can fall into one of the following types:

w(t)=\begin{cases}u(t),\\
\partial_{t}u(t),\\
\lambda u(t)+\partial_{t}u(t),\quad\lambda>0.\end{cases}

The three situations above are usually named memory of type I, type II and type III, respectively.
For the cases of memory of type II and III, we address the reader to the works
[
1
,
8
,
20
]
.

In the present paper, we are interested to the case of memory of type I, which is in a sense the most natural,
and certainly the one that has received greater attention.
Accordingly, our equation reads

(2.1)

\partial_{ttt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{tt}u(t)-\beta\Delta\partial_{t}u(t)-\gamma\Delta u(t)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\Delta u(t-s)ds=0.

The two main issues concerning (
2.1
) are:

\bullet

Existence and uniqueness of weak solutions in the phase space
{\mathcal{H}}
.

\bullet

Uniform decay properties of the associated natural energy, given by
1
1

1

Here,

\|\cdot\|
denotes the usual norm in
L^{2}(\Omega)
or in
[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{3}
.

{\mathsf{E}}(t)=\|\nabla u(t)\|^{2}+\|\nabla\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}+\|\partial_{tt}u(t)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}-g^{\prime}(s)\|\nabla u(t)-\nabla u(t-s)\|^{2}ds.

Evidently, suitable hypotheses on the kernel
g
have to be made.
The basic assumptions on
g
, employed with no exception in the literature,
are:

g\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
with
g\geq 0
and
g^{\prime}\leq 0
, both summable. Besides,
the total mass of
g
is required to be strictly less than
\gamma
.

Within the further assumption that
g
is convex, existence and uniqueness of solutions
is proved in
[
20
]
for the subcritical case
\varkappa>0
, and in

[
1
]
for the critical case
\varkappa=0
(but asking also (
2.2
)
below).
Actually, by means of a “pumping” technique introduced in
[
9
]
,
it is not hard to extend the result
for all negative values of the stability number
\varkappa
, hence covering
the supercritical case as well.
In fact, the convexity of
g
plays a crucial role in the well-posedness
analysis.
One exception is the paper
[
19
]
(see also
[
23
]
), where the convexity of
g
is not required, but it
is replaced by the rather restrictive condition

g(0)<\alpha^{2}\varkappa,

which clearly can take place only in the subcritical regime
\varkappa>0
.

As far as the uniform decay of the energy is concerned, here we are interested to analyze the
exponential case, although other decay patterns are possible (such as polynomial), depending on the form of
g
.
A first observation is that the memory term in (
2.1
) introduces an extra dissipation
with respect to the one of the MGT equation (
1.1
).
Hence, in principle, one could expect (
2.1
) to exhibit stronger stability properties
than (
1.1
). For instance, one could think that the critical regime
\varkappa=0
is driven into
subcritical when memory is added. This is utterly false
(unless
-\Delta
is replaced by a
bounded
operator), even in the simplest possible case of the
exponential kernel. A counterexample in that direction is given in
[
7
]
.
Accordingly, in order to hope for exponential stability, we shall confine ourselves to the
subcritical case
\varkappa>0
.

The exponential decay at infinity of the energy
{\mathsf{E}}(t)
is always proved within the classical assumption

(2.2)

g^{\prime}(s)+\delta g(s)\leq 0,

for some
\delta>0
(see
[
1
,
19
,
20
,
22
,
23
]
), implying in
particular the exponential decay of the kernel

g(s)\leq g(0){\rm e}^{-\delta s}.

The differential inequality (
2.2
) is to some extent unsatisfactory, for it
imposes severe restrictions on the shape of the kernel, whereas the
exponential decay of
{\mathsf{E}}(t)
should be reasonably expected if

g
is exponentially decaying solely, with no further information.

In light of the discussion above, the aim of the present paper is twofold:

\bullet

Prove an existence and uniqueness result removing the convexity
assumption on
g
, and requiring in place a much weaker condition: roughly speaking,
the derivative
g^{\prime}
cannot be “too small” where
g
is concave down.

\bullet

Prove the exponential decay of the energy in the subcritical regime
\varkappa>0
, assuming only that

g
is controlled from above by a negative exponential.

Remark 2.1
.

In fact, by applying the trick devised in
[
9
]
, our proof of the existence and uniqueness
result can be immediately extended to cover all the values of
\varkappa
.

We finally mention that the idea of studying the exponential decay of the energy
relaxing condition (
2.2
) has been first devised
in the paper
[
4
]
, in the context of linear viscoelasticity. Nonetheless,
our techniques can be applied to obtain also decay types
other than exponential
(see e.g.
[
19
,
22
]
),
without assuming a differential inequality on
g
, but only requiring a proper control from above,
along the line of the recent work
[
5
]
.

3.
Functional Setting and Notation

Geometric spaces

Let
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\|\cdot\|)
be a real Hilbert space,
and let
A:H\to H
be a strictly positive selfadjoint operator
with domain
{\mathfrak{D}}(A)\subset H
.
For
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the hierarchy of (continuously) nested Hilbert spaces

H^{\sigma}={\mathfrak{D}}(A^{\sigma/2}),

endowed with the scalar products and norms

\langle u,v\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle A^{\sigma/2}u,A^{\sigma/2}v\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|u\|_{\sigma}=\|A^{\sigma/2}u\|.

The index
\sigma
appearing in the scalar products and norms will be always omitted whenever zero.
For
\sigma>0
, it is understood that
H^{-\sigma}

denotes the completion of the domain, so that
H^{-\sigma}
is the dual space of
H^{\sigma}
.
The symbol
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
will also be used to denote the duality pairing between

H^{-\sigma}
and
H^{\sigma}
.
Finally, we introduce the phase space of our problem, namely, the product Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}=H^{1}\times H^{1}\times H,

with the usual product norm

\|(u,v,w)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}=\|u\|_{1}^{2}+\|v\|_{1}^{2}+\|w\|^{2}.

Remark 3.1
.

For the concrete equation (
2.1
) of the previous section,
A
is the
Laplace-Dirichlet operator
-\Delta
on

H=L^{2}(\Omega)
, while
H^{1}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{2}=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Properties of the memory kernel

We assume that the memory kernel
g
is a nonnull function
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{+}=(0,\infty)
satisfying the following set of hypotheses:

(g1)

g
and its derivative
g^{\prime}
are summable and absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Besides,

\kappa=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)ds<\gamma.

(g2)

g(s)\geq 0
and
g^{\prime}(s)\leq 0
for every
s>0
.

(g3)

\exists\,\delta>0
such that for a.e.
s>0

(\alpha-\delta)g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq 0.

Without loss of generality we may (and do) assume
\delta<\alpha
.

Remark 3.2
.

Condition (g1) implies that

g(0)=\lim_{s\to 0}g(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}-g^{\prime}(s)ds<\infty.

Remark 3.3
.

The derivative
g^{\prime}
can be unbounded about zero.
In addition, condition (g3) along with the fact that
g^{\prime}
is summable yield

\lim_{s\to\infty}g^{\prime}(s)=0.

The proof of this limit is a nice real analysis exercise, and is left to the interested reader.
Besides, knowing that
g^{\prime}
is differentiable almost everywhere, (g3) is completely
equivalent to require

(3.1)

-g^{\prime}(s+t)\leq-g^{\prime}(s){\rm e}^{(\alpha-\delta)t},

for all
s>0
and
t\geq 0
. Indeed, one implication follows from the Gronwall lemma,
whereas the converse is proved by performing the limit of the quotient
\frac{g^{\prime}(s+t)-g^{\prime}(s)}{t}

as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Example 3.4
.

Taking without loss of generality
\alpha-\delta=1
and
\gamma
large enough, the memory kernel

g(s)=\frac{1}{37}{\rm e}^{-s}\big[148+6\cos 6s+\sin 6s\big]

is easily seen to satisfy (g1)-(g3). Indeed,

g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime\prime}(s)=-{\rm e}^{-s}\big[8+2\sin 6s-6\cos 6s\big]\leq 0.

However, its derivative

g^{\prime}(s)=-{\rm e}^{-s}\big[4+\sin 6s\big]

is oscillating all over
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Accordingly,
g(s)
is not convex, and not even ultimately convex
as
s\to\infty
.

4.
Statement of the Main Results

Let (g1)-(g3) hold. In greater generality,
within the subcritical condition

(4.1)

\varkappa=\beta-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}>0,

we will consider for
t>0
the equation

(4.2)

\partial_{ttt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{tt}u(t)+\beta A\partial_{t}u(t)+\gamma Au(t)-\int_{0}^{t}g(s)Au(t-s)ds=0,

subject to the initial conditions

(4.3)

\begin{cases}u(0)=u_{0},\\
\partial_{t}u(0)=v_{0},\\
\partial_{tt}u(0)=w_{0},\end{cases}

where
(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})
is an arbitrarily given vector of
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Definition 4.1
.

A function
u
is said to be a weak solution to problem (
4.2
)-(
4.3
)
on the time interval
[0,T]
if:

(i)

(u,\partial_{t}u,\partial_{tt}u)\in{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{H}})
;

(ii)

the initial conditions (
4.3
) are satisfied; and

(iii)

for every test function
\zeta\in H^{1}
and a.e.
t\in[0,T]
,

\langle\partial_{ttt}u(t),\zeta\rangle+\alpha\langle\partial_{tt}u(t),\zeta\rangle+\beta\langle\partial_{t}u(t),\zeta\rangle_{1}+\gamma\langle u(t),\zeta\rangle_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\langle u(t-s),\zeta\rangle_{1}ds=0.

We also define the corresponding energy by

(4.4)

{\mathsf{E}}(t)=\|u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{tt}u(t)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}-g^{\prime}(s)\|u(t)-u(t-s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

The first theorem concerns with the well-posedness of the problem.
As previously mentioned in the Introduction, such a result is not obvious at all, due to the lack of convexity
of the memory kernel
g
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For every set of initial data
(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})\in{\mathcal{H}}
, problem (
4.2
)-(
4.3
)
admits a unique global solution (i.e., defined for all
T>0
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
.
Moreover,
there exists
M\geq 1
, independent of the initial data, such that

(4.5)

{\mathsf{E}}(t)\leq M{\mathsf{E}}(0).

The next result is the exponential decay of the energy. We remark that such a decay is obtained under the sole assumption
that the memory kernel is exponentially decaying as well.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that there exist
M_{g}>0
and
\omega_{g}>0
such that

(4.6)

g(s)\leq M_{g}{\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}s}.

Then there exist
M\geq 1
and
\omega>0
, both independent of the initial data,
such that the energy fulfills the exponential decay estimate

(4.7)

{\mathsf{E}}(t)\leq M{\mathsf{E}}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega t}.

The kernel
g
of Example
3.4
, although not convex, fulfills the
condition (
2.2
) employed in the earlier literature. Hence, in particular,
it fulfills (
4.6
), so that Theorem
4.3
applies.
Nonetheless, it is possible to exhibit a nonconvex
g
satisfying (g1)-(g3) and (
4.6
),
but not (
2.2
), as the following construction shows.

Example 4.4
.

Without loss of generality, condition (g3) is here assumed with
\alpha-\delta=1
.
Besides, we take
\gamma\geq 1
.
Let
f
be an absolutely continuous positive function satisfying
the following properties:

(i)

0<f(s)\leq{\rm e}^{-s}
.

(ii)

f(s)=\varepsilon_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}(s-n^{2})
on any interval
I_{n}=[n^{2},n^{2}+n+1]
with
n\geq 2
integer,
where
\varepsilon_{n}=\frac{{\rm e}^{-(n^{2}+n+1)}}{n+2}
.

(iii)

f
is decreasing outside the intervals
I_{n}
.

Then, define

g(s)=\int_{s}^{\infty}f(y)dy.

Observe that
g
is not convex, since its derivative
g^{\prime}=-f
is decreasing on
I_{n}
.
It is also clear that (g1)-(g2) hold, and the same can be said
of (
4.6
), due to (i). Condition (g3) is trivially verified outside
I_{n}
,
where
g^{\prime}
is negative and increasing, while on
I_{n}
we have

g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq 0=-\big[\varepsilon_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}(s-n^{2})\big]+\varepsilon_{n}\leq 0.

We are left to show that (
2.2
) fails to hold.
By contradiction, suppose (
2.2
) true on every interval
[n^{2},n^{2}+1]\subset I_{n}
.
In which case, for any
\tau\in[0,1]
, we have

g^{\prime}(n^{2}+\tau)+\delta g(n^{2}+\tau)=-\varepsilon_{n}(1+\tau)+\delta\int_{n^{2}+\tau}^{\infty}f(y)dy\leq 0.

But

\int_{n^{2}+\tau}^{\infty}f(y)dy\geq\int_{n^{2}+1}^{n^{2}+n+1}f(y)dy\geq n\varepsilon_{n}.

We conclude that

(\delta n-2)\varepsilon_{n}\leq-\varepsilon_{n}(1+\tau)+\delta n\varepsilon_{n}\leq 0,

which is clearly false as
n\to\infty
.

The rest of the paper is devoted to the proofs of Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.3
.
In some sense, we may say that the strategy behind the proofs is classical: we use suitable multipliers
in order to obtain differential inequalities.
However, the presence of the convolution integral introduces essential difficulties.
Indeed, when performing the estimates, integration by parts has to be exploited
several times. This produces boundary terms that cannot be possibly handled in any way.
The only possibility is then
working within a regularization scheme, where these boundary terms simply disappear.
Accordingly, we will consider a family of regularized problems, depending on a small parameter
\varrho>0
,
which converge to (
4.2
)-(
4.3
) in the limit
\varrho\to 0
.
In particular, this will allow us to take advantage of some general technical tools developed in the
study of equations with memory in the past history framework.

5.
The Right-Translation Semigroup on the Memory Space

Within assumptions (g1)-(g3), we begin to analyze the strongly continuous semigroup of right translations
on a suitable Lebesgue space. This will play a crucial role in the forthcoming proofs.
To this end, let us introduce the space

{\mathcal{M}}=L^{2}_{-g^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{+};H^{1}),

namely, the space of square summable functions
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to H^{1}
with respect to the
measure
-g^{\prime}(s)ds
, endowed with the Hilbert scalar product and norm

\langle\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}-g^{\prime}(s)\langle\eta_{1}(s),\eta_{2}(s)\rangle_{1}ds,\qquad\|\eta\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}-g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

Next, we define the linear operator

{\mathbb{T}}:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{M}}
acting as

{\mathbb{T}}\eta=-\eta^{\prime},

with domain

{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}})=\big\{\eta\in{\mathcal{M}}:\,\eta^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}},\,\,\eta(0)=0\big\},

where
\eta^{\prime}
denotes the weak derivative of
\eta=\eta(s)
with respect to
s
,
and
\eta(0)
stands for the limit in
H^{1}
of
\eta(s)
as
s\to 0
.

Before going any further, let us discover an important feature of
{\mathbb{T}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
\eta\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}})
we have the equality

\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{{\mathcal{M}}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

In particular, this means that the integral in the right-hand side exists whenever
\eta\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Proof.

Since
\eta\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}})
, the product
\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{{\mathcal{M}}}
makes sense.
By definition,

\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{ds}\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds=\lim_{\nu\to 0,\,N\to\infty}\int_{\nu}^{N}g^{\prime}(s)\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{ds}\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

Therefore, integrating by parts,

\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\lim_{\nu\to 0,\,N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\bigg[g^{\prime}(N)\|\eta(N)\|_{1}^{2}-g^{\prime}(\nu)\|\eta(\nu)\|_{1}^{2}-\int_{\nu}^{N}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\bigg].

The conclusion is a consequence of the two limits

\lim_{\nu\to 0}g^{\prime}(\nu)\|\eta(\nu)\|^{2}_{1}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}g^{\prime}(N)\|\eta(N)\|^{2}_{1}=0.

To prove the first limit, we use the Hölder inequality and (
3.1
), to get

\displaystyle-g^{\prime}(\nu)\|\eta(\nu)\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-g^{\prime}(\nu)\bigg[\int_{0}^{\nu}\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|_{1}dy\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\nu\int_{0}^{\nu}-g^{\prime}(y+\nu-y)\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|_{1}^{2}dy

\displaystyle\leq\nu\int_{0}^{\nu}-g^{\prime}(y){\rm e}^{(\alpha-\delta)(\nu-y)}\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|_{1}^{2}dy

\displaystyle\leq\nu\|{\mathbb{T}}\eta\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}{\rm e}^{(\alpha-\delta)\nu},

which tends to zero as
\nu\to 0
.
Hence,

\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\bigg[g^{\prime}(N)\|\eta(N)\|_{1}^{2}-\int_{0}^{N}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\bigg].

Since the integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds

exists,
setting
h(s)=(\alpha-\delta)g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime\prime}(s)
,
we learn that the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigg[g^{\prime}(N)\|\eta(N)\|_{1}^{2}+\int_{0}^{N}h(s)\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\bigg]

exists as well.
On the other hand, we know from (g3) that
h(s)\leq 0
. Accordingly,
both terms in the limit above are negative, hence the integral converges, being
monotone decreasing with respect to
N\to\infty
. In turn

\lim_{N\to\infty}g^{\prime}(N)\|\eta(N)\|_{1}^{2}

must exists.
But the function
g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}
is summable on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
which forces the latter limit to be zero, as desired.
∎

Our aim is showing that
{\mathbb{T}}
generates
the
right-translation semigroup

R(t)
on
{\mathcal{M}}
, defined as

[R(t)\eta](s)=\begin{cases}0&s\leq t,\\
\eta(s-t)&s>t.\end{cases}

Checking that
R(t)
is a strongly continuous semigroup of linear operators is standard matter:
the only nontrivial thing to observe is that
R(t)
maps
{\mathcal{M}}
into
{\mathcal{M}}
. Indeed,
in light of (

\|R(t)\eta\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\int_{t}^{\infty}-g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s-t)\|_{1}^{2}ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}-g^{\prime}(s+t)\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\leq\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}{\rm e}^{(\alpha-\delta)t}.

The characterization of the infinitesimal generator of

This translates into the fact that

is not a contraction semigroup (hence its infinitesimal generator

Nonetheless, the following holds.

Theorem 5.2
.

The infinitesimal generator of the right-translation semigroup
R(t)

on
{\mathcal{M}}
is the linear operator
{\mathbb{T}}
defined above.

Proof.

Setting

\omega=\frac{\alpha-\delta}{2}>0,

we will equivalently prove
that the linear operator

{\mathbb{S}}={\mathbb{T}}-\omega{\mathbb{I}},\qquad{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{S}})={\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}}),

is the infinitesimal generator of the semigroup

R_{*}(t)={\rm e}^{-\omega t}R(t).

We begin to show that
{\mathbb{S}}
generates a contraction semigroup
S(t)
.
This will be done via the Lumer-Phillips theorem (see
[
27

),
by completing the following two steps.

(i)

{\mathbb{S}}
is dissipative, i.e.,
\langle{\mathbb{S}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 0

\eta\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{S}})

(ii)

{\rm Range}({\mathbb{I}}-{\mathbb{S}})={\mathcal{M}}
.

Point (i) is an immediate consequence of Lemma

and (g3).
Indeed, given any

\langle{\mathbb{S}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[(\alpha-\delta)g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime\prime}(s)\big]\|\eta(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\leq 0.

To prove (ii), let

be arbitrarily fixed. We verify that the equation

A straightforward integration, along with the requirement

We need to prove that such an

{\mathcal{M}}

\eta\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{S}})

To this aim, an exploitation of (

\displaystyle\sqrt{-g^{\prime}(s)}\|\eta(s)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}{\rm e}^{-(s-y)}\sqrt{-g^{\prime}(s){\rm e}^{-2\omega(s-y)}}\|\xi(y)\|_{1}dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}{\rm e}^{-(s-y)}\sqrt{-g^{\prime}(y)}\|\xi(y)\|_{1}dy.

Denoting

{\mathcal{E}}(s)={\rm e}^{-s}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(s)=\sqrt{-g^{\prime}(s)}\|\xi(s)\|_{1},

the latter integral is nothing but the convolution product

2

Since

by a classical result of convolution theory (see, e.g.

So far we only proved that

In order to prove the equality

Fix then

\exists\,\,{\mathcal{M}}\text{-}\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{R_{*}(t)\eta-\eta}{t}\quad(\text{hence equal to }{\mathbb{S}}_{*}\eta).

Explicitly,

\frac{[R_{*}(t)\eta](s)-\eta(s)}{t}=\begin{cases}-\frac{\eta(s)}{t}&s\leq t,\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr\frac{{\rm e}^{-\omega t}\eta(s-t)-\eta(s)}{t}&s>t.\end{cases}

By the very definition of

{\mathcal{M}}\text{-}\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\omega t}\eta(s-t)-\eta(s)}{t}\chi_{(t,\infty)}=-\eta^{\prime}(s)-\omega\eta(s).

Hence, we are done if we establish the convergence as

Indeed, making use of the Hölder inequality together with (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}-g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}-g^{\prime}(s)\bigg[\int_{0}^{s}\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|_{1}^{2}dy\bigg]ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|^{2}_{1}\bigg[\int_{y}^{t}-g^{\prime}(s)ds\bigg]dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|^{2}_{1}\bigg[\int_{y}^{t}-g^{\prime}(y){\rm e}^{2\omega(s-y)}ds\bigg]dy

\displaystyle\leq{\rm e}^{2\omega t}\int_{0}^{t}-g^{\prime}(y)\|\eta^{\prime}(y)\|^{2}_{1}dy.

Since

It is worth noting that,

The nonhomogeneous Cauchy problem

For a fixed
T>0
, let

. Consider the nonhomogeneous
Cauchy problem in
{\mathcal{M}}

for the unknown variable
\eta^{t}=\eta^{t}(s)
with initial datum
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(s)\in{\mathcal{M}}

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\eta^{t}={\mathbb{T}}\eta^{t}+f(t),&0<t\leq T,\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr\eta^{0}=\eta_{0}.\end{cases}

Note that

) of

It is then well known that the problem above admits a unique mild solution on

Explicitly, we have the representation formula

\eta^{t}(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{t-s}^{t}f(y)dy&s\leq t,\\

is actually
a strong solution (see

The Approximated Problem

Let (g1)-(g3) and (
4.1
) hold.
For an arbitrarily fixed
\varrho>0
, we set

{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s)=\begin{cases}0&\mathrm{if}\ s<\varrho/2,\\
\frac{2s}{\varrho}-1&\mathrm{if}\ \varrho/2\leq s\leq\varrho,\\
1&\mathrm{if}\ s>\varrho,\end{cases}

and we define the (positive) function

{\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}g(s)[1-{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s-t)]ds.

Observe that, as
g
is decreasing, we have the bound

(6.1)

{\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq\varrho g(t).

Then, for any initial data
(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we analyze the Cauchy problem

(6.2)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{ttt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{tt}u(t)+\beta A\partial_{t}u(t)+\gamma Au(t)-\int_{0}^{t}g(s)Au(t-s)ds={\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho}(t)Au_{0},\\
\vskip-2.84526pt\cr u(0)=u_{0},\\
\vskip 1.99168pt\cr\partial_{t}u(0)=v_{0},\\
\vskip 1.99168pt\cr\partial_{tt}u(0)=w_{0}.\end{cases}

This is a sort of a nonhomogeneous version of our original problem (
4.2
)-(
4.3
), with a
forcing term given by
{\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho}Au_{0}
, depending itself on
(the first component of) the initial data.
On account of (
6.1
), problem (
4.2
)-(
4.3
)
is formally recovered in the limit
\varrho\to 0
.

Defining, for
t\geq 0
and
s>0
, the function

(6.3)

\eta^{t}(s)=\begin{cases}u(t)-u(t-s)&s\leq t,\\
u(t)+[{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s-t)-1]u_{0}&s>t,\end{cases}

the first equation of (
6.2
) becomes

(6.4)

\partial_{ttt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{tt}u(t)+\beta A\partial_{t}u(t)+(\gamma-\kappa)Au(t)+\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)A\eta^{t}(s)ds=0.

Indeed, using (g1),

\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)A\eta^{t}(s)ds=\kappa Au(t)-\int_{0}^{t}g(s)Au(t-s)ds+Au_{0}\int_{t}^{\infty}g(s)[{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s-t)-1]ds.

From our discussion in Section
5
,
assuming
u
to be known and such that
\partial_{t}u\in{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],H^{1})
,
the function
\eta^{t}
defined via the representation formula (
6.3
)
is exactly the strong solution on
[0,T]
of the nonhomogeneous differential equation in
{\mathcal{M}}

(6.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\eta^{t}={\mathbb{T}}\eta^{t}+\partial_{t}u(t),

with initial datum

\eta^{0}(s)={\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s)u_{0},

which is readily seen to belong to
{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Remark 6.1
.

In particular,
\eta^{t}\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathbb{T}})
for all

and Lemma

applies, so we have

\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta^{t},\eta^{t}\rangle_{{\mathcal{M}}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|\eta^{t}(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

This is exactly the reason why we introduced the approximating
\varrho
-problem:
if

In spite of the complete equivalence between (

To this purpose,

In particular, since

7.
The Approximated Problem: Existence and Uniqueness

The following theorem holds.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\varrho>0
be fixed.
For every
T>0
and every initial data
(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})\in{\mathcal{H}}
, the Cauchy problem
(
6.2
) admits a unique weak solution

possessing the regularity

(u,\partial_{t}u,\partial_{tt}u)\in{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{H}}).

Proof.

The argument, based on a Galerkin approximation procedure, is carried out in four steps.
The main ingredient is the
basic energy inequality (
8.6
) of Remark

, proved in the next section.

I. Galerkin approximations.

Let
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset H^{2}

For every
n

H_{n}=\mathrm{span}\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
,

onto

We approximate the given initial data

(u_{0n},v_{0n},w_{0n})=(P_{n}u_{0},P_{n}v_{0},P_{n}w_{0})\to(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})\quad\text{in }{\mathcal{H}}.

(7.1)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{ttt}u_{n}(t),\zeta\rangle+\alpha\langle\partial_{tt}u_{n}(t),\zeta\rangle+\beta\langle\partial_{t}u_{n}(t),\zeta\rangle_{1}+\gamma\langle u_{n}(t),\zeta\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\langle u_{n}(t-s),\zeta\rangle_{1}ds={\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho}(t)\langle u_{0n},\zeta\rangle_{1},

along with the initial conditions

\begin{cases}u_{n}(0)=u_{0n},\\
\partial_{t}u_{n}(0)=v_{0n},\\

Indeed, choosing

We claim that

8.
The Approximated Problem: The Energy Inequality

Let
\varrho>0
be fixed. Along this section,

u(t)
will be any solution to (
6.2
),
while
C
will always denote a generic positive constant independent
of
\varrho
and of the particular
u(t)
.
Our goal is to establish the dissipative character of (
6.2
).
To this end, considering the equation in the form (
6.4
) with
\eta^{t}

given by (
6.3
),
we introduce the energy-like functional

\displaystyle{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u(t)+\alpha u(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{\varkappa\alpha+\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\|\partial_{tt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\eta^{t}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}^{2}+\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta^{t}(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds+2\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta^{t}(s),\partial_{t}u(t)\rangle_{1}ds,

keeping in mind that
\gamma>\kappa
and
\varkappa>0
.
Offhand, it is not even clear whether
{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)
is well defined on the trajectories
of (
6.2
). Indeed, this will be a consequence of the forthcoming
estimates. First, let us show that
{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)
is “almost” equivalent to the energy
{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)

given by (
6.7
).

Lemma 8.1
.

There exists
C\geq 1
such that

(8.1)

\frac{1}{C}{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq C\bigg({\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)+\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta^{t}(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\bigg).

Moreover, for a possibly bigger
C
,

(8.2)

{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(0)\leq C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0).

Proof.

By means of the Hölder and the Young inequalities,

\bigg|2\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta^{t}(s),\partial_{t}u(t)\rangle_{1}ds\bigg|\leq\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta^{t}(s)\|^{2}_{1}+\frac{\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}_{1}.

Accordingly

{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)\geq\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u(t)+\alpha u(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\varkappa\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\|\partial_{tt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}+\|\eta^{t}\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{M}}}.

Since

(8.3)

\|(u,v,w)\|_{*}^{2}=\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{\alpha}\|v+\alpha u\|_{1}^{2}+\varkappa\|v\|_{1}^{2}+\|w+\alpha v\|^{2}

is readily seen to be an equivalent norm on
{\mathcal{H}}
, we obtain the first bound in (
8.1
).
The second bound follows directly by the Hölder and the Young inequalities,
together with the continuous embedding
H^{1}\subset H
.
Finally, recalling that
\eta^{0}(s)={\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s)u_{0}
,
and using the fact that
0\leq{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}\leq 1
, we get

\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta^{0}(s)\|_{1}^{2}ds\leq\kappa\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{1}\leq\kappa{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0),

implying the desired control (
8.2
) at
t=0
.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

If the classical condition (
2.2
) on the kernel is also assumed, it is immediately seen that

{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)
and
{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)
are actually equivalent.

The next step is showing that
{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)
is a decreasing function.
This will be attained via a differential inequality. Such an inequality
will make sense for the Galerkin approximations of
u(t)
, i.e., the solutions
u_{n}(t)
to (
7.1
)-(
7.2
).
Indeed, the scope of the inequality is twofold: first,
to produce a uniform estimate on the Galerkin approximations, and only in a second moment to extend the very same estimate
to the actual solution
u(t)
. Accordingly, it is understood that all the multiplications hereafter are carried out
within the approximation scheme.

Theorem 8.3
.

The following inequality holds:

(8.4)

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)+2\varkappa\alpha\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\delta\|\eta^{t}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 0.

In particular,
{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)
is decreasing.

Proof.

In light of the equivalence between the differential equation in (
6.2
) and
(
6.4
), we
multiply (
6.4
) by
\partial_{tt}u+\alpha\partial_{t}u
in
H
, to get
(omitting everywhere the dependence on
t
)

(8.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u+\alpha u\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{\varkappa\alpha+\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}+\|\partial_{tt}u+\alpha\partial_{t}u\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+2(\varkappa\alpha+\kappa)\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}={\mathfrak{I}}_{1}+{\mathfrak{I}}_{2},

where

{\mathfrak{I}}_{1}=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),\alpha\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}ds\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathfrak{I}}_{2}=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),\partial_{tt}u\rangle_{1}ds.

Let us examine
{\mathfrak{I}}_{1}
. Exploiting (
6.5
), we have

{\mathfrak{I}}_{1}=-\frac{d}{dt}\bigg(\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds\bigg)+2\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta(s),\eta(s)\rangle_{1}ds.

Besides,

2\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle{\mathbb{T}}\eta(s),\eta(s)\rangle_{1}ds=-\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\frac{d}{ds}\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds=\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

The boundary terms of the latter integration by parts vanish.
Indeed, for any fixed
t>0
, we infer from the representation formula (
6.3
)
that

\|\eta^{t}(N)\|_{1}=\|u(t)\|_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\eta^{t}(\nu)\|_{1}=\|u(t)-u(t-\nu)\|_{1},

whenever
N
is large enough and
\nu
small enough. Knowing that

g(\infty)=0
and
g(0)<\infty
, and by the time-continuity of
u
,
we conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}g(N)\|\eta(N)\|_{1}^{2}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{\nu\to 0}g(\nu)\|\eta(\nu)\|_{1}^{2}=0.

In summary,

{\mathfrak{I}}_{1}=-\frac{d}{dt}\bigg(\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds\bigg)+\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

By the same argument, we find

{\mathfrak{I}}_{2}=-\frac{d}{dt}\bigg(2\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}ds\bigg)+2\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\langle\eta(s),\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}ds+2\kappa\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}.

Substituting now
{\mathfrak{I}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{I}}_{2}
into (
8.5
),
we arrive at

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}+2\varkappa\alpha\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}=\frac{d}{dt}\|\eta\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}^{2}+\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds+2\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\langle\eta(s),\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}ds.

At this point, we multiply equation (
6.5
) by
\eta
in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
On account of (
6.6
), this yields

\frac{d}{dt}\|\eta\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{M}}}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime\prime}(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds-2\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(s)\langle\eta(s),\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}ds.

Collecting the last two identities, we end up with

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}+2\varkappa\alpha\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[\alpha g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime\prime}(s)\big]\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

The desired inequality follows
by assumption (g3) on the memory kernel.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

In particular, an integration in time of (
8.4
) together with
Lemma
8.1
entail the (uniform) estimate

(8.6)

\|u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{tt}u(t)\|^{2}\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0),

for every
t\geq 0
and some
C\geq 1
.

Remark 8.5
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, if we are only interested in proving the existence
of a solution on any interval
[0,T]
to (
6.2
), and in turn to (
4.2
),
we do not really need assumption (
4.1
).
Indeed, if (
4.1
) fails to hold, by adding the term
m\partial_{tt}u
to both sides of (
6.2
),
up to taking
m>0
large enough that

\varkappa_{m}=\beta-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha+m}>0,

instead of (
8.4
) we find the estimate

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t),

for some
C>0
. This is enough to conclude that, for a possibly bigger
C
,

\|u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{tt}u(t)\|^{2}\leq{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{CT},

for every
t\in[0,T]
.

9.
The Approximated Problem: Exponential Decay of the Energy

We now dwell on the exponential decay of the energy
{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)
of the approximated problem. Again, in what follows,

u(t)
will be any solution to (
6.2
), for a fixed
\varrho>0
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let assumption (
4.6
) hold. Then there exist

M_{0}\geq 1
and
\omega>0
, both independent of
\varrho
and of the initial data,
such that the energy (
6.7
) fulfills the exponential decay estimate

(9.1)

{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq M_{0}{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega t}.

The proof of the theorem requires a number of passages: the starting point is the dissipative estimate (
8.4
),
along with the controls provided by Lemma
8.1
. However, such an estimate alone
is not enough to drive uniformly the energy to zero. Accordingly,
we need to introduce suitable auxiliary functionals, in order to attain an efficient differential inequality.
All the constants appearing in this section, such as the generic constant
C>0
, are understood to be independent
of
\varrho
and of the particular solution
u(t)
.

With reference to (
4.6
), let us denote

\Theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}(t-s)}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

A preliminary lemma is needed.

Lemma 9.2
.

There exists a functional
\Psi(t)
satisfying the differential inequality

(9.2)

\frac{d}{dt}\Psi(t)+\vartheta{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq C\big[\|\partial_{t}u(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\eta^{t}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\big],

along with the bounds

(9.3)

-C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq\Psi(t)\leq C\big[{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)+{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}t}+\Theta(t)\big],

for some
\vartheta>0
and
C>0
.

Proof.

Calling

G(s)=\int_{s}^{\infty}g(y)dy,

we define our functional
\Psi
to be the sum

\Psi(t)=\Psi_{1}(t)+\Psi_{2}(t),

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{t}u(t)-\alpha u(t),\partial_{tt}u(t)+\alpha\partial_{t}u(t)\rangle,

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}G(s)\|\eta^{t}(s)-u(t)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

For some
C>0
, we have the inequalities

(9.4)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{1}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t),

(9.5)

\displaystyle 0\leq\Psi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq C\big[{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}t}+\Theta(t)\big].

Indeed, (
9.4
) is straightforward. Concerning (
9.5
),
we first observe that the exponential decay (
4.6
) of
g
implies that

G(s)\leq M_{G}{\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}s}
, with
M_{G}=M_{g}/\omega_{g}
.
Hence, writing explicitly
\eta^{t}-u(t)
via (
6.3
),

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha}\Psi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\infty}G(s)[{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s-t)-1]^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{1}ds+\int_{0}^{t}G(t-s)\|u(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{G}}{\omega_{g}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}t}+M_{G}\Theta(t).

Collecting (
9.4
)-(
9.5
),
the bounds (
9.3
) are established.
We are left to prove (
9.2
).
Exploiting (
6.4
), we compute the time-derivative of
\Psi_{1}
as
(omitting the dependence on time)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Psi_{1}+\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u+\alpha u\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{tt}u+\alpha\partial_{t}u\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma-3\kappa-\varkappa\alpha}{\alpha}\langle\partial_{t}u+\alpha u,\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}+2\alpha\langle\partial_{tt}u+\alpha\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}u\rangle+\frac{2\kappa+2\varkappa\alpha}{\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u\|^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),\partial_{t}u\rangle_{1}ds-\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),u\rangle_{1}ds.

By the Young and the Hölder inequalities, for some
C>0
large enough we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Psi_{1}+\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{2\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u+\alpha u\|^{2}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{tt}u+\alpha\partial_{t}u\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds-\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),u\rangle_{1}ds.

The time-derivative of
\Psi_{2}
, in light of (
6.5
), reads

\frac{d}{dt}\Psi_{2}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds+\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\langle\eta(s),u\rangle_{1}ds.

Here, an integration by parts is performed. Similarly to the previous cases,
as we are working with the approximated problem, the boundary terms vanish.
Summing the differential inequalities for
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
, we finally get

\frac{d}{dt}\Psi+\frac{\gamma-\kappa}{2\alpha}\|\partial_{t}u+\alpha u\|^{2}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{tt}u+\alpha\partial_{t}u\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\|\eta(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds\leq C\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}.

On account of (
8.1
), and recalling that
\|\cdot\|_{*}
defined in (
8.3
)
is an equivalent norm on
{\mathcal{H}}
, the desired conclusion (
9.2
) follows by adding the term

\|\partial_{t}u\|_{1}^{2}+\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}
to both sides of the inequality.
∎

We can now proceed to the proof of the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
9.1
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we define the functional

\Lambda(t)={\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)+\varepsilon\Psi(t).

Up to choosing
\varepsilon
small enough, we deduce from (
9.3
), together with
the first inequality in (
8.1
), the bounds

(9.6)

\varepsilon{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq\Lambda(t)\leq 2{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)+{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}t}+\Theta(t).

Besides, by possibly further reducing
\varepsilon
,
collecting (
8.4
) and (
9.2
) we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda(t)+\varepsilon\vartheta{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq 0.

We remark that
\varepsilon
has been chosen independently of
\varrho
and
u(t)
.
Leaning again on (
8.1
),
we can find
\mu>0
such that, for every
\omega>0
small,

\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda(t)+2\omega{\mathsf{F}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq-\mu\|u(t)\|^{2}_{1}.

At this point, we take advantage of (
9.6
), arriving at the inequality

\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda(t)+\omega\Lambda(t)\leq\omega{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0){\rm e}^{-\omega_{g}t}+\omega\Theta(t)-\mu\|u(t)\|^{2}_{1}.

Taking
\omega<\omega_{g}
,
an application of the Gronwall lemma, along with the first inequality in (
9.6
), yield

\varepsilon{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq\Lambda(t)\leq\Big[\Lambda(0)+\frac{\omega}{\omega_{g}-\omega}{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0)+{\mathfrak{I}}(t)\Big]{\rm e}^{-\omega t},

where

{\mathfrak{I}}(t)=\omega\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{\omega s}\Theta(s)ds-\mu\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{\omega s}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

Observe that, exchanging the order of integration,

\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{\omega s}\Theta(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{\omega_{g}s}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{1}\bigg(\int_{s}^{t}{\rm e}^{-(\omega_{g}-\omega)y}dy\bigg)ds\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{g}-\omega}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm e}^{\omega s}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{1}ds.

Accordingly, choosing
\omega
small enough that
\omega\leq\mu(\omega_{g}-\omega)
,
we have that
{\mathfrak{I}}(t)\leq 0
. Hence, we end up with the energy inequality

{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Lambda(t)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big[\Lambda(0)+\mu{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0)\big]{\rm e}^{-\omega t}.

Noting that
\Lambda(0)
is bounded
by a multiple of
{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(0)
, due to (
9.6
) and (
8.2
),
the conclusion follows.
∎

10.
Proofs of the Main Results

We have now all the ingredients to prove Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
.
To this aim, for an arbitrarily given vector

(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})\in{\mathcal{H}}
and any
\varrho>0
, we denote by

u_{\varrho}
the solution to the approximating problem (
6.2
).

I.

We claim that, for every
T>0
, the family
z_{\varrho}=(u_{\varrho},\partial_{t}u_{\varrho},\partial_{tt}u_{\varrho})
is Cauchy in
{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{H}})

as
\varrho\to 0
. Indeed, take
\varrho_{1}\leq\varrho_{2}
, and consider the corresponding
solutions
u_{\varrho_{1}}
and
u_{\varrho_{2}}

to (
6.2
).
Observe that the two forcing terms in the equations are different, namely,

{\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho_{1}}(t)Au_{0}
and

{\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho_{2}}(t)Au_{0}
.
Setting

\bar{u}(t)=u_{\varrho_{2}}(t)-u_{\varrho_{1}}(t),

we define

{\bar{\eta}}^{t}(s)=\begin{cases}\bar{u}(t)-\bar{u}(t-s)&s\leq t,\\
\bar{u}(t)+[{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho_{2}}(s-t)-{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho_{1}}(s-t)]u_{0}&s>t.\end{cases}

Then
\bar{u}

solves the analogue of equation (
6.4
), i.e.,

\partial_{ttt}\bar{u}(t)+\alpha\partial_{tt}\bar{u}(t)+\beta A\partial_{t}\bar{u}(t)+(\gamma-\kappa)A\bar{u}(t)+\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)A{\bar{\eta}}^{t}(s)ds=0,

but with null initial conditions

\bar{u}(0)=\partial_{t}\bar{u}(0)=\partial_{tt}\bar{u}(0)=0.

At this point, defining the energy of the system above as

{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}}(t)=\|\bar{u}(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{t}\bar{u}(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{tt}\bar{u}(t)\|^{2}+\|\bar{\eta}^{t}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}},

the very same computations of Section
8
lead us to
an inequality completely analogous to (
8.6
),
that is,

{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}}(t)\leq C{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}}(0)=C\|\bar{\eta}^{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{M}},

for some
C\geq 1
independent of
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}
.
Since

\bar{\eta}^{0}

is supported on the interval
[\varrho_{1}/2,\varrho_{2}]
and
|{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho_{2}}-{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho_{1}}|\leq 1
,

\|\bar{\eta}^{0}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}^{2}=\int_{\varrho_{1}/2}^{\varrho_{2}}-g^{\prime}(s)[{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho_{2}}(s)-{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho_{1}}(s)]^{2}\|u_{0}\|_{1}^{2}ds\leq\|u_{0}\|_{1}^{2}\big[g(\varrho_{1}/2)-g(\varrho_{2})\big]\to 0

as
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\to 0
.
This establishes the claim.
∎

II.

As a consequence, for every
T>0
, we have the convergence
z_{\varrho}\to z
in
{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{H}})
as
\varrho\to 0
, for some
vector
z=(u,\partial_{t}u,\partial_{tt}u)
. The next step is showing that

u
is a solution to the original Cauchy problem (
4.2
)-(
4.3
).
The desired initial conditions are trivially satisfied, so we are left to verify
that (
4.2
) holds true in the weak sense.
Since
u_{\varrho}
solves the differential equation (
6.2
), the result follows by the convergence

\lim_{\varrho\to 0}{\mathsf{Q}}_{\varrho}(t)\langle u_{0},\zeta\rangle_{1}=0,

for any given
\zeta\in H^{1}
and every
t\in[0,T]
, clearly implied by the bound (
6.1
).

III.

Exploiting the representation formula (
6.3
), we now write the energy

{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)
of
u_{\varrho}
, defined in (
6.7
),
in the equivalent form

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)

\displaystyle=\|u_{\varrho}(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{t}u_{\varrho}(t)\|^{2}_{1}+\|\partial_{tt}u_{\varrho}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}-g^{\prime}(s)\|u_{\varrho}(t-s)-u_{\varrho}(t)\|_{1}^{2}ds+\int_{t}^{\infty}-g^{\prime}(s)\|u_{\varrho}(t)+[{\mathsf{q}}_{\varrho}(s-t)-1]u_{0}\|_{1}^{2}ds.

The convergence
z_{\varrho}\to z
in
{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{H}})
, for every
T>0
, entails that

\lim_{\varrho\to 0}{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)={\mathsf{E}}(t)+g(t)\|u(t)\|_{1}^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0,

where
{\mathsf{E}}(t)
is the energy of the original problem (
4.2
)-(
4.3
),
defined in (
4.4
).
Passing to the limit
in the energy inequality (
8.6
), we readily obtain (
4.5
)
(recall that
g(0)<\infty
). This finishes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
By the same token, when (
4.6
) holds,
the exponential decay estimate (
9.1
) for
{\mathsf{E}}_{\varrho}(t)
of Theorem
9.1

yields in the limit (
4.7
) with

M=M_{0}[1+g(0)].

The proof of Theorem
4.3
is done.
∎
