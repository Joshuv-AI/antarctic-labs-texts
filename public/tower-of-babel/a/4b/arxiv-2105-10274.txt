On the convergence of the regularized entropy-based moment method for kinetic equations
Graham W. Alldredge, Martin Frank, Jan Giesselmann

Abstract
The entropy-based moment method is a well-known discretization for the velocity variable in kinetic equations which has many desirable theoretical properties but is difficult to implement with high-order numerical methods. The regularized entropy-based moment method was recently introduced to remove one of the main challenges in the implementation of the entropy-based moment method, namely the requirement of the realizability of the numerical solution. In this work we use the method of relative entropy to prove the convergence of the regularized method to the original method as the regularization parameter goes to zero and give convergence rates. Our main assumptions are the boundedness of the velocity domain and that the original moment solution is Lipschitz continuous in space and bounded away from the boundary of realizability. We provide results from numerical simulations showing that the convergence rates we prove are optimal.



1
Introduction

Kinetic equations model systems consisting of large numbers of particles that
interact with each other or with a background medium and arise in a wide
variety of applications including
rarefied gas dynamics
[
6
]
,
neutron transport
[
18
]
,
radiative transport
[
20
]
,
and semiconductors
[
19
]
.
The numerical solution of kinetic equations remains an area of active research.
In this work, we consider the entropy-based moment method
[
17
]
, which
is a discretization of the velocity variable in the kinetic equation.
It has many desirable theoretical properties but is computationally expensive
and challenging to implement.

Recently in
[
2
]
a regularized version of the entropy-based
moment equations was proposed to simplify the implementation of numerical
methods for the entropy-based moment equations.
These regularized entropy-based moment equations require the selection of a
regularization parameter, and the authors in
[
2
]
proposed a
rule for selecting the regularization parameter so that the error introduced by
the regularization was of the order of the error in the spatiotemporal
discretization.
With this selection rule the authors produced numerical results which
showed that the regularized equations could be used to compute accurate results
of the original entropy-based moment equations.

In this work, we prove that exact solutions of the regularized entropy-based
moment equations converge to the solutions of the original equations.
We quantify the difference between these two solutions using the relative entropy,
and the convergence rate is quadratic in the regularization parameter.
Under some assumptions, this is equivalent to linear convergence in the
regularization parameter in the
L^{2}
norm.

To obtain our results we require the following main assumptions:

(i)

the set of velocities of the kinetic equation is bounded;

(ii)

periodic boundary conditions;

(iii)

Lipschitz continuity of the function defining the collision term in the
original entropy-based moment equations;

(iv)

the solution of the original entropy-based moment equations is both
Lipschitz continuous in space and bounded away from the boundary of the set
of realizable moment vectors.

The relative-entropy techniques we use are very similar to what was done in

[
3
,
4
,
11
,
22
]
.
In general, relative entropy estimates are a widely applicable tool for
comparing thermomechanical theories having the form of hyperbolic balance laws
that are endowed with a strictly convex entropy

[
10
]
.
A general limitation of this methodology is that it requires the solution to the
limiting system to be Lipschitz continuous - this property can (usually) only be
expected for short times since shocks may form.
There is recent progress in overcoming this limitation, at least in one space
dimension, by using the relative entropy with shifts methodology that was
developed by Vasseur and co-workers
[
16
,
21
]
.
However, this condition can most probably not be removed in two or more space
dimensions since it is connected to non-uniqueness of entropy solutions
to hyperbolic balance laws.

To present our results, we first introduce the entropy-based moment equations
and their regularized version in Section
2
and precisely state our
assumptions.
Then in Section
3
introduce the technique of relative entropy and
give a general version of our main result.
We prove the estimates upon which our main result relies and give the subsets
of the realizable set on which they hold in Section
4
.
Next we present the results of numerical experiments confirming the
theoretically predicted rates of convergence in Section
5
, and
finally we draw conclusions and discuss directions for future work in
Section
6
.

2
Entropy-based moment equations and regularization

2.1
The kinetic equation

Kinetic equations evolve the
kinetic density function

{f\colon[0,\infty)\times X\times V\to[0,\infty)}
according to

\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)=\mathcal{C}(f(t,x,\cdot))(v)

(1)

(when neglecting long-range interactions).
The function
f
depends on time
t\in[0,\infty)
, position

{x\in X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, and a velocity variable
v\in V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The operator
\mathcal{C}
introduces the effects of particle collisions; at each
x

and
t
, it
is an integral operator in
v
.
In order to be well-posed, (
1
) must be accompanied by appropriate
initial and boundary conditions.

The results in this work depend strongly on the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The set of velocities
V
is bounded.

We will see the crucial consequences of this assumption in the next subsection.
For any
g\in L^{1}(V)
we use the notation

\displaystyle\left\langle g\right\rangle:=\int_{V}g(v)\,dv.

(2)

We define

\displaystyle|V|:=\left\langle 1\right\rangle=\int_{V}\,dv\,.

(3)

Many of the constants below depend on
\max_{v\in V}\|v\|
, though for
clarity of exposition we do not give this dependence explicitly.

We consider kinetic equations where the collision operator satisfies an
entropy-dissipation property:
Let
\eta:D\to\mathbb{R}
, where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, be a strictly convex function
and
\mathbb{F}(V):=\{g\in L^{1}(V):\range(g)\subseteq D\}
.
We call
\eta
the
kinetic entropy function
.
Then the local entropy
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{F}(V)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f):=\left\langle\eta(f)\right\rangle.

(4)

This entropy is dissipated if the collision operator
\mathcal{C}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\langle\eta^{\prime}(g)\mathcal{C}(g)\right\rangle\leq 0

(5)

for all
g:V\to D
such that the integral is defined.
Furthermore, we assume that
\eta
is sufficiently smooth:

Assumption 2
.

The kinetic entropy satisfies
\eta\in C^{3}(D)
,
\eta^{\prime\prime}>0
on
D
, and
(
5
), i.e.,
\eta
is an entropy dissipated by the
kinetic equation (
1
).

2.2
The original entropy-based moment equations and realizability

The original entropy-based moment equations are a semidiscretization of the
kinetic equation (
1
) in the velocity variable
v
.
For an overview, see
[
17
]
.
The velocity dependence of
f
at each point in time and space is replaced by
the vector of moments

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t,x):=(u_{0}(t,x),u_{1}(t,x),\ldots,u_{N}(t,x))\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1},

(6)

which contains the approximations of velocity integrals of
f
multiplied by
the basis functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(v):=(m_{0}(v),m_{1}(v),\ldots,m_{N}(v)),

(7)

that is,
{u_{i}(t,x)\simeq\langle m_{i}f(t,x,\cdot)\rangle}
for all

{i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}}
.
Usually the basis functions are polynomials.
We make the following assumptions on the basis functions:

Assumption 3
.

(i)

For every
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
we have
m_{i}\in L^{\infty}(V)
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\|m_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(V)}
is bounded
by one:

\displaystyle\|m_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(V)}=\sup_{v\in V}|m_{i}(v)|\leq 1\qquad\text{for }i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}.

(8)

(ii)

The constant function is in the linear span of the basis functions.
Without loss of generality we assume
m_{0}(v)\equiv 1
.

For each moment vector, the entropy-based moment method reconstructs an ansatz
for the the kinetic density by solving the constrained optimization problem

\operatorname*{minimize}_{g\in\mathbb{F}(V)}\>\mathcal{H}(g)\qquad\operatorname*{subject~to}\>\left\langle\mathbf{m}g\right\rangle=\mathbf{u}

(9)

(recall the definition of
\mathcal{H}
in (
4
)).
Under Assumption
1
, this problem has a unique solution for every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}

[
15
]
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:=\left\{\mathbf{u}:\text{ there exists a }g\in\mathbb{F}(V)\text{ such that }\left\langle\mathbf{m}g\right\rangle=\mathbf{u}\right\}

(10)

is the set of all
realizable
moment vectors, and consequently, the
system of moment equations is well-defined and hyperbolic for all realizable
moment vectors.

1

V
is not bounded, such as in entropy-based moment equations for the

\displaystyle G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\eta^{\prime}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})

\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
maps a moment vector
\mathbf{u}
to the solution
of the dual problem

\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u})=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\left\langle\eta_{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle\right\};

(12)

here

The components of

Now we are ready to give the entropy-based moment equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u}),

(13a)

where the flux function
\mathbf{f}
and relaxation term
\mathbf{r}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle v\mathbf{m}G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u})}\right\rangle\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\mathcal{C}(G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u})})\right\rangle.

(13b)

Classical solutions of the moment equations (
13
) satisfy the entropy
dissipation law

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(\mathbf{u})+\nabla_{x}\cdot j(\mathbf{u})=h^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u})\leq 0

(14)

for the entropy and entropy flux

h(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle\eta(G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u})})\right\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad j(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle v\eta(G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u})})\right\rangle.

(15)

Note that
h^{\prime}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, and that
h
is strictly convex as a consequence of
Assumption
2

[
13
]
.
For readers unfamiliar with the derivations of the dual problem and the entropy
dissipation law and related properties of the entropy-based moment equations,
we review these in Appendix
B
.

We make the following assumption on the collision term:

Assumption 4
.

The function
\mathbf{r}
in the collision term of the original entropy-based moment
equations (
13
) is Lipschitz continuous with constant
C_{\mathbf{r}}
and
satisfies
\lim_{\mathbf{u}\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u})=0
.

This applies, for example, to linear collision operators like in the case of
isotropic scattering.
This assumption cannot be expected to apply for the Boltzmann collision
operator, at least not globally, since it is quadratic.
The assumption
\lim_{\mathbf{u}\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u})=0
is natural since
otherwise there would be collision effects in the moment equations when no
particles are present.

Notice that the flux
\mathbf{f}
and the source
\mathbf{r}
in (
13
) can only be
defined when the optimization problem (
9
) is feasible, i.e.,
when the
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}
.
When
D=[0,\infty)
, this set corresponds to the set of vectors which
contain the moments of a nonnegative density, which are indeed the only moment
vectors we want to consider since the kinetic density
f
should be nonnegative.
This theoretical advantage is however impractical for implementations because
it is in general difficult to keep a high-order numerical solution of
(
13
) within the realizable set
\mathcal{R}
.

Finally, we note that a consequence of Assumption
1
is that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a
smooth bijection from
\mathcal{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
.
Its inverse is the function which gives the moment vector of the entropy ansatz
corresponding to a given multiplier vector:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle.

(16)

This function plays a role in the analysis later.

2.3
The regularized entropy-based moment equations

To work around the problem of realizability, the regularized entropy-based
moment equations were proposed in
[
2
]
.
Let
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
be the regularization parameter.
Then the regularized entropy-based moment equations are given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}),

(17a)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle v\mathbf{m}G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})}\right\rangle\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathbf{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\mathcal{C}(G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})})\right\rangle.

(17b)

The ansatz
G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})}
has the same form as above (i.e.,

{G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})}=\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{m})}
) but is the solution of the
unconstrained optimization problem

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{g\in\mathbb{F}(V)}\>\left\langle\eta(g)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\left\langle\mathbf{m}g\right\rangle-\mathbf{u}\right\|^{2},

(18)

which is feasible for any moment vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
(again under
Assumption
1
).
The new multiplier vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})
is the solution of the corresponding
dual problem

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}):=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\left\langle\eta_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|^{2}\right\}

(19)

and satisfies the first-order necessary conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}))+\gamma\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}).

(20)

Classical solutions of the regularized equations (
17
) satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})+\nabla_{x}\cdot j_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})=h_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\leq 0.

where

h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle\eta(G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\left\langle\mathbf{m}G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})}\right\rangle-\mathbf{u}\right\|^{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad j_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}):=\left\langle v\eta(G_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})})\right\rangle.

(21)

Analogously to the original case, we have
h_{\gamma}^{\prime}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}
.
In this work, we are mostly concerned with entropy solutions of the regularized equations, i.e. weak solutions of (
17
) satisfying the admissibility criterion

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})+\nabla_{x}\cdot j_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\leq h_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}).

(22)

Note that any Lipschitz continuous solution of (
17
) is automatically an entropy solution.

Like
h
, the entropy
h_{\gamma}
of the regularized equations is convex, and its
Legendre dual
(h_{\gamma})_{*}
will prove to be useful in the analysis.
The Legendre dual
(h_{\gamma})_{*}
and its first and second derivatives are given by

[
2
]

\displaystyle(h_{\gamma})_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\eta_{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|^{2},

(23a)

\displaystyle(h_{\gamma})^{\prime}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\gamma\boldsymbol{\alpha},\text{ and}

(23b)

\displaystyle(h_{\gamma})^{\prime\prime}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^{T}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\gamma I,

(23c)

where
I
is the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
identity matrix.
Note that
(h_{\gamma})^{\prime}_{*}\circ h_{\gamma}^{\prime}=(h_{\gamma})^{\prime}_{*}\circ\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}=\id
, so we also
have
h_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}=((h_{\gamma})^{\prime\prime}_{*}\circ\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma})^{-1}
, where the inverse indicates
the matrix inverse.
One also immediately recognizes from the form of
(h_{\gamma})^{\prime\prime}_{*}
that
(h_{\gamma})_{*}
and
thus
h_{\gamma}
are strictly convex for any
\gamma>0
.

3
Relative entropy for convergence

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-cube and
T\in(0,\infty)
.
We consider the initial-value problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle(t,x)

\displaystyle\in(0,T]\times X,

(24a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{0}(x)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in X,

(24b)

where
\mathbf{u}^{0}
are the given initial conditions, and we use periodic boundary
conditions in space.
For any
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, the regularized moment equations are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle(t,x)

\displaystyle\in(0,T]\times X,

(25a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{0}(x)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in X,

(25b)

and we also take periodic boundary conditions.
Notice that the initial conditions are the same.

Following Dafermos
[
8
]
, we introduce the relative entropy and
relative entropy flux relative to

Definition 1
.

Given moments
\mathbf{u}_{\gamma},\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
the
relative entropy
and

relative entropy flux
are given by

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}|\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})-h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}-\mathbf{u})\text{ and}

(26)

\displaystyle j_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}|\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=j_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})-j_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot(\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})-\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})),

respectively.

Note that using the relative entropy with respect to
h_{\gamma}
allows us (in
principle) to use non-realizable moment vectors in both arguments of the
relative entropy.
For our subsequent convergence result, however, there will be a strong
difference between the first and the second slot of the relative entropy.
In particular, in our convergence results, Corollaries

Let

\displaystyle\leq h_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma})

4.2

\gamma\in(0,\infty)\end{subarray}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{w})\|.

So we define

\displaystyle C_{\mathbf{f}^{\prime\prime},L}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\conv(\mathcal{R}^{L})\\
\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0})\end{subarray}}\|\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{w})\|

for some
\gamma_{0}\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}|\mathbf{u})\|\leq C_{\mathbf{f}^{\prime\prime},L}\|\mathbf{u}_{\gamma}-\mathbf{u}\|^{2}

.

\mathbf{f}^{\prime}

\mathcal{R}^{M}

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}))-\mathbf{u}\|\leq M\gamma

from

as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})))(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\circ\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma})^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\|

(a)

Lemma 5

Proof.

4.4
The sublinear case

In contrast to the Maxwell–Boltzmann-like entropies, the entropies
\eta
we
consider in this class are not bounded from below but

{\lim_{z\to\infty}\eta^{\prime}(z)=0}
.
Consequently
{\dom(\eta_{\ast})\subseteq(-\infty,0)}
, and the multipliers must
satisfy
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m}<0
for all
v\in V
.
For such entropies we replace the assumption in (
55
) with

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}^{M,m}:=\left\{\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|\leq M\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m}(v)\leq-m\text{ for all }v\in V\right\},

(111)

for some
M\in(0,\infty)
and
m\in(0,\infty)
.
Note that as
M\to\infty
and
m\to 0
, the set
\mathcal{R}^{M,m}
approaches the
full realizable set
\mathcal{R}
.
Related to the parameter
m
is

\displaystyle p_{0}:=-\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{R}^{M,m}\\
\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0})\\
v\in V\end{subarray}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{m}(v)>0;

(112)

for some
\gamma_{0}\in(0,\infty)
.

The additional condition parameterized by
m
in
\mathcal{R}^{M,m}
ensures that the
ansatz
G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
in the sublinear case is bounded away from zero.
In the superlinear case, the ansätze
G_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{R}^{M}
are
already bounded away from zero, but this is not so for the sublinear case,
where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
must also fulfill
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m}(v)<0
for all

v\in V
.

Note that
u_{M,m}
and
h_{M,m}
can be defined as in (
56
):

\displaystyle u_{M,m}:=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{R}^{M,m}}\|\mathbf{w}\|\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad h_{M,m}:=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{R}^{M,m}}h(\mathbf{w});

(113)

both are finite.
We can also derive similar bounds on
h_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}
.
Let
\mathbf{c}
be the unit-length eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue
of

for some
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}^{M,m}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}((h_{\gamma})^{\prime\prime}_{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}\cdot\left(\left\langle\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^{T}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\gamma I\right)\mathbf{c}

(114a)

\displaystyle=\left\langle(\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\gamma

(114b)

\displaystyle\leq|V|\left(\sup_{y\in[-M,-p_{0}]}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime\prime}(y)\right)+\gamma_{0}

(114c)

for

.
Similarly, if we now let
\mathbf{c}
be the unit-length eigenvector associated with

for

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}((h_{\gamma})^{\prime\prime}_{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})))=\left\langle(\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{m})^{2}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\gamma\geq|V|\inf_{y\in[-M,-p_{0}]}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime\prime}(y).

(115)

Thus for the sublinear case there exist positive constants

\lambda_{\min,h^{\prime\prime},M,m}

and
C_{h^{\prime\prime},M,m}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}\cdot h_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}&\geq\lambda_{\min,h^{\prime\prime},M,m}\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\\
\|h_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{u})\|&\leq C_{h^{\prime\prime},M,m}\end{aligned}\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\text{, }\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}^{M}\text{, and }\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0});

(116)

(cf. the superlinear case (
60
)).

5
Numerical Results

We consider the toy problem from
[
12
,
2
]
.
There the authors considered the moment equations for the linear kinetic
equations in slab geometry (see e.g.,
[
18
]
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f=\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\langle f\right\rangle-f\right).

(122)

where
V=[-1,1]
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}\in[0,\infty)
.
For the spatial domain we take
X=[0,1]
.

Remark 4
.

More generally, the kinetic equation includes terms for absorption of particles
by a background medium as well as a source term, as in

[
12
,
2
]
.
While the resulting terms in the moment equations can be straightforwardly
incorporated into our analysis and do not affect our main results, we have left
them out in this work for clarity of exposition.

The corresponding entropy-based moment equations are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\partial_{x}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})=\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}R\mathbf{u},

(123)

where
R=\diag\{0,-1,\dots,-1\}
.
For the basis functions we used the Legendre polynomials.
We used the Maxwell–Boltzmann entropy (
52
).
The collision term
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{u})=\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}R\mathbf{u}
clearly satisfies
Assumption
4
.

For numerical computations we used the fourth-order Runge–Kutta discontinuous
Galerkin (RKDG) method as in
[
2
]
with 160 spatial cells and no
slope limiter.
With this spatial resolution the numerical solutions were accurate enough to
observe the convergence in
\gamma
.

The initial conditions we used are constructed as follows.
Let
{\omega(x):=\frac{1}{2}M_{0}(1+\cos(2\pi x))}
be a periodic function
which we
use to define the multiplier vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\log\left(\frac{\omega(x)}{2\sinh(\omega(x))}\right)\\
\omega(x)\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(124)

Then the initial conditions are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{0}(x)=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\exp(\boldsymbol{\beta}(x)\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle.

(125)

Note that
\beta_{0}
is chosen such that the zeroth order moment of the initial
condition is one, i.e.,
u^{0}_{0}(x)\equiv 1
.
The solution
\mathbf{u}(t,x)
of the original entropy-based moment equations with
these initial conditions satisfies the assumption
{\mathbf{u}(t,x)\in\mathcal{R}^{M}}
of
Corollary
1
for
M\approx M_{0}
.
Indeed the maximum value of
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u}(t,x))\|
in space tends to
decrease as time advances depending on the value of
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
: the larger

\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
is, the faster the norms of the multipliers decrease in time.
For
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}=0
, the value of
\max_{x}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u}(t,x))\|
is nearly
constant in time.

We ran the solutions until the final time
T=0.1
.
We used various values of
N
up to 15 and found that the results did not
depend qualitatively on the value of
N
.
We tried several values of
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
from zero to one and here did observe that
the results depended on the value of
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
:
for very small values of
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
the solutions appear not to enter the
regime of second-order convergence until
\gamma
is very small, on the order
of
10^{-11}
.
Such values of
\gamma
are so small, that for these solutions the error due to
the numerical optimizer started to dominate errors due to the regularization.

We remind the reader that in order to evaluate the flux function
\mathbf{f}_{\gamma}
we
compute the multiplier vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}
by numerically solving the dual
problem (
19
).
We used the numerical optimizer described in
[
2
]
but found that
for problems with
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}=0
the value of tolerance
\tau
on the norm of the
dual gradient used in the stopping criterion, namely
\tau=10^{-7}
, was not
small enough to observe convergence in
\gamma
for the very small values of

\gamma
where the equations enter the regime of second-order convergence.
The difficulty here is that, in our experience, one cannot reliably
bring the norm of the dual gradient below
10^{-7}
when the norm of the
multipliers at the solution is about ten or bigger.

But for smaller values of
M_{0}
, we found that using a combination of the
smaller tolerance
\tau=10^{-8}
as well as modifying the optimizer to make
efforts to further reduce the norm of the dual gradient when possible allowed
us to decrease the numerical errors from the optimizer enough so that we could
observe near second-order convergence.
This modification is described in pseudocode in Algorithm
1
and
works as follows:
The optimizer runs as usual until the norm of the dual gradient is smaller than

\tau

Then, the optimizer continues to take up to

, which we call the

If the optimizer is unable to bring the norm of the dual gradient under

in

additional iterations, the optimizer still exits
successfully (as long as the current multiplier vector still satisfies the
original stopping criterion).
In all of the results reported here, we used
{\tau_{d}=10^{-11}}
and

{\ell_{\max}=10}
.

6
Conclusions

The regularized entropy-based moment method for kinetic equations
keeps many of the desirable properties of the original entropy-based moment
method but removes the requirement that the moment vector of the solution
remains realizable.
This facilitates the design and implementation of high-order numerical methods
for the regularized moment equations.
However, the regularized equations require the selection of a regularization
parameter, and the error caused by regularization needs to be accounted for and
balanced with other error sources.
Our contribution is to rigorously prove the convergence as the regularization
parameter goes to zero expected by formal arguments and to provide convergence
rates.
Numerical experiments show that these rates are indeed optimal.

Our results hold for wide classes of entropy functions including the
Maxwell–Boltzmann entropy and the Bose–Einstein entropy.
Our analysis relies on some key assumptions: The solution to the original
moment equations needs to be Lipschitz and bounded away from the boundary of the
set of realizable states.

Relaxing our assumptions would of course strengthen our results.
One would like to be able to work with kinetic equations with unbounded
velocity domains, but here the original moment equations have fundamental
problems
[
14
,
15
,
13
]
which remain in the
regularized equations.
Nevertheless, the Euler equations, which are a case of the entropy-based moment
method, do not have these problems and would be an interesting starting point
for extending our analysis.
One would also like to allow the solution to have values arbitrarily close to or even on
the boundary of the realizable set, but not enough work has been done to
consider the behavior of the moment equations near or on the boundary of the
realizable set, such as in
[
7
]
.
It is not even known whether the realizable set is invariant under the time
evolution of the original entropy-based moment equations.
Finally, requiring a Lipschitz continuous solution to the limiting system is typical for relative entropy estimates, see
[
8
]
, and in multiple space dimensions this is connected with non-uniqueness of entropy solutions for certain moment systems such as the Euler equations.

Appendix B
Entropy relationships

In this appendix we quickly review the computations from
[
17
]
showing
the relationships between
h
,
h_{*}
,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, in particular
the key result that
h^{\prime}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
Start with the definition of the entropy
h
,

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{u}):=\min_{g\in\mathbb{F}(V)}\left\{\left\langle\eta(g)\right\rangle:\left\langle\mathbf{m}g\right\rangle=\mathbf{u}\right\},

(130)

i.e., the minimal value of the primal problem (
9
) as a function
of the moment vector.
The corresponding Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle L(g,\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\left\langle\eta(g)\right\rangle+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\left(\mathbf{u}-\left\langle\mathbf{m}g\right\rangle\right),

(131)

and thus the dual problem is

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\min_{g\in\mathbb{F}(V)}L(g,\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\left\langle\eta_{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle,

(132)

cf. (
12
), where to get this equality one takes the minimization
inside the integral and applies the definition of the Legendre dual of
\eta
.
Because the duality gap is zero
[
13
, Thm. 16]
we
have

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{u})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\left\langle\eta_{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle\right\},

(133)

so
h
is the Legendre transformation of

\displaystyle h_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\left\langle\eta_{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle.

(134)

Its derivative is readily computed:

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\left\langle\mathbf{m}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle=:\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}).

(135)

Now, recall that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u})
in (
12
) is defined to be the
multiplier vector that solves the dual problem (
132
).
Then the first-order necessary conditions for the dual problem imply

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\mathbf{u}))=\mathbf{u}.

(136)

The reverse, i.e.,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))=\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, is a consequence of
the uniqueness of the solution to the dual problem (thanks to convexity).
Thus
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is the inverse function of
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
.
Finally, since the derivative of Legendre duals are inverses of each other, we
have

\displaystyle h^{\prime}=(h^{\prime}_{*})^{-1}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{-1}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

(137)

Appendix C
The regularized solution for zero vector as
\gamma\to 0

In this section we quickly consider

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(0)),

(138)

where
0\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
.
This comes up in Section
4.1
when deriving global estimates on the function

\hat{\mathbf{u}}\circ\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}
using Lipschitz continuity.

For convenience we assume that the basis functions are orthogonal to each
other, which since
m_{0}\equiv 1
(recall Assumption
3
) in
particular implies that
\langle m_{i}\rangle=\langle m_{0}m_{i}\rangle=0
for all

i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
As a consequence, most of the components of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(0)
are easy to
determine.
Consider the first-order necessary conditions:

\displaystyle 0=\left\langle m_{i}\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(0)\cdot\mathbf{m})\right\rangle+\gamma\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,i}(0),\qquad i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}.

(139)

If we set
\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,i}(0)=0
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, then
the entropy ansatz is constant in
v
, and by orthogonality of
\{m_{i}\}
, we
see that the first-order necessary conditions are satisfied for

i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
It remains to determine the zeroth component
\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,0}(0)
, for
which we need to solve

\displaystyle 0=|V|\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,0}(0))+\gamma\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,0}(0).

(140)

From this equation, it is clear that

\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,0}(0)<0
and thus that

\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,0}(0)\to-\infty
monotonically (recall
(
48
)) as
\gamma\to 0
.
The limiting value must be unbounded because
0\not\in\mathcal{R}
.
Now we recall that for the
\eta
considered in this work we have

\range(\eta_{\ast}^{\prime})=\dom(\eta^{\prime})=(0,\infty)
, and furthermore that
\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}

is a monotonically increasing function because
\eta_{\ast}
is convex.
Therefore
\eta_{\ast}^{\prime}(\hat{\alpha}_{\gamma,0}(0))\to 0
as
\gamma\to 0
.
It follows that also
{\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\gamma}(0))\to 0}
.

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma
4

Let
L\in(M,\infty)
.
We want to show

\displaystyle C_{h,M,L}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L}\\
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}^{M}\\
\gamma\in(0,\gamma_{0})\end{subarray}}h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{u})>0.

(141)

The basic idea is that, by strict convexity of
h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{u})
in its first
argument, it only achieves its minimum value, zero, when
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}
.
But this is ruled out on

{(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})\in(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L})\times\mathcal{R}^{M}}
because

{\overline{\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L}}\cap\mathcal{R}^{M}=\emptyset}
.

We can get a more explicit bound as follows.
Let
Q\in(M,L)
.
We claim that for every
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L}
and

\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}^{M}
there exists a
\lambda_{Q}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{Q}:=(1-\lambda_{Q})\mathbf{u}+\lambda_{Q}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{R}^{L}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{Q}.

(142)

For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L}
this straightforward, because the function

\displaystyle f(\lambda)=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}((1-\lambda)\mathbf{u}+\lambda\mathbf{v})\|

(143)

is a continuous function with
f(0)\leq M
and
f(1)\geq L
.
For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}
, by convexity of
\mathcal{R}
there exists a
unique
\lambda_{\mathcal{R}}\in(0,1)
such that

(1-\lambda_{\mathcal{R}})\mathbf{u}+\lambda_{\mathcal{R}}\mathbf{v}\in\partial\mathcal{R}
.
But then, since
\mathcal{R}^{L}\subset\subset\mathcal{R}
, there must also be a

\lambda_{L}\in(0,\lambda_{\mathcal{R}})
such that

(1-\lambda_{L})\mathbf{u}+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L}
, and so the first
argument can be applied again.

Now, for any
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})\in(\mathcal{R}^{L}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{Q})\times\mathcal{R}^{M}
we have

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:rel-ent-bnd-below-L}}}{{\geq}}\lambda_{\min,h^{\prime\prime},\widetilde{L}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\geq\lambda_{\min,h^{\prime\prime},\widetilde{L}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}^{L}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{Q}}\\
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}^{M}\end{subarray}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}\|^{2}=:C_{L}>0

(144)

where the strict positivity follows from

\mathcal{R}^{M}\cap\overline{\mathcal{R}^{L}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{Q}}=\emptyset
.

Finally, let
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})\in(\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{L})\times\mathcal{R}^{M}
and

\mathbf{w}_{Q}
be as in (
142
).
By convexity of the relative entropy in its first argument, we have

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{Q})h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{u})+\lambda_{Q}h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{u})\geq h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{w}_{Q}|\mathbf{u})\geq C_{L}.

(145)

But with
h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{u})=0
and
\lambda_{Q}\in(0,1)
, we immediately have

h_{\gamma}(\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{u})\geq C_{L}
, and by taking the infimum as in
(
141
) we conclude
C_{h,M,L}\geq C_{L}
.
