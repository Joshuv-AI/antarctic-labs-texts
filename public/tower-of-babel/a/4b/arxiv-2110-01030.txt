A Recursive Algorithm for Solving Simple Stochastic Games
Xavier Badin de Montjoye

Abstract
We present two recursive strategy improvement algorithms for solving simple stochastic games. First we present an algorithm for solving SSGs of degree d \displaystyle d that uses at most O ⁡ ( ⌊ ( d + 1 ) 2 / 2 ⌋ n / 2 ) \displaystyle O\left(\left\lfloor(d+1)^{2}/2\right\rfloor^{n/2}\right) iterations, with n \displaystyle n the number of MAX vertices. Then, we focus on binary SSG and propose an algorithm that has complexity O ⁡ ( φ n ​ P ​ o ​ l ​ y ​ ( N ) ) \displaystyle O\left(\varphi^{n}Poly(N)\right) where φ = ( 1 + 5 ) / 2 \displaystyle\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2 is the golden ratio. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first deterministic strategy improvement algorithm that visits 2 c ​ n \displaystyle 2^{cn} strategies with c < 1 \displaystyle c<1 .

Introduction

Simple stochastic games (SSG) are a restriction introduced by Condon
[
6
,
7
]
of the notion of stochastic games defined by Shapley
[
12
]
. An SSG is a turn-based zero-sum game with perfect information played by two players named
max
and
min
. A token is placed on a directed graph and moves alongside the graph arcs. The set of vertices is partitioned in
max
,
min
, Random and Sink vertices. If the token is in a
max
or
min
vertices, the corresponding player chooses its next position in the outneighbourhood of the currently occupied vertex. In the case of random vertices, the token is moved randomly. Finally, when a sink vertex
\displaystyle s
is reached, the game ends and player
min
must pay some penalty value
\displaystyle\mbox{Val}(s)
to player
max
. The goal of
min
is to minimise the expected penalty, and the goal of
max
is to maximise it. One reason to study SSGs and their related complexity is that the stochastic versions of classical games such as parity games, mean and discounted payoff are all equivalent to SSG
[
1
]
.

Simple stochastic games admit a pair of optimal strategies
[
7
]
whose expected value satisfies a Nash equilibrium. Our goal is to compute such a pair of optimal strategy. This problem is known to be in
PPAD

[
10
]
a subset of the class
FNP
, but is not known to be in
FP
. SSG have several applications, such as modelling autonomous urban driving
[
5
]
or in the domain of model checking of modal
\displaystyle\mu
-calculus
[
13
]
.

We note
\displaystyle n
the number of
max
vertices,
\displaystyle N
the number of total vertices and
\displaystyle r
the number of random vertices. Every known algorithm in the literature for solving SSGs has exponential time complexity bounds. There exist several FPT algorithms that can be used for specific families of SSGs. For instance, for SSGs with random vertices of degree
\displaystyle 2
and uniform probability distribution, Ibsen-Jensen and Milterson gives a value iteration algorithm in
[
9
]
with complexity
\displaystyle O\left(r2^{r}(r\log(r)+N)\right)
.

One of the main families of algorithms for solving SSGs are strategy improvement algorithms. In the case of SSG with
max
vertices of outdegree exactly
\displaystyle 2
, Tripathi, Valkanova and Kumar present in
[
14
]
an algorithm with
\displaystyle O\left(2^{n}/n\right)
iterations, which is the only current bounds that deterministically improve the trivial
\displaystyle 2^{n}
iterations of checking every possible strategies of player
max
. Ludwig offers a randomised algorithm in
[
11
]
, which does
\displaystyle\displaystyle 2^{O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)}
iterations on average. In this paper, we provide a deterministic algorithm for the same family of SSGs that has complexity
\displaystyle O\left(2^{cn}Poly(N)\right)
for some
\displaystyle c<1
. Moreover, we will provide the first deterministic algorithm of this family with parametrised complexity in the degree
\displaystyle d
of
max
vertices and number of
max
vertices, improving the trivial bound of
\displaystyle O\left(d^{n}Poly(N)\right)
to
\displaystyle O\left(((d+1)/\sqrt{2})^{n}Poly(N)\right)
.

In the general case, Gimbert and Horn give in
[
8
]
a strategy improvement algorithm whose complexity is a function of the random vertices with an algorithm of complexity
\displaystyle\displaystyle O\left(r!Poly(N)\right)
. Moreover, in
[
3
]
, Auger Coucheney and Strozecki present a stochastic algorithm that runs in
\displaystyle 2^{O(r)}
.

Auger, Badin de Montjoye and Strozecki present in
[
2
]
a general formulation for strategy improvement algorithms that offers a general bound on the complexity of all such algorithms depending on the format of the probability value of the random nodes. It states that if there is some
\displaystyle q
such that all probabilities are of the format
\displaystyle p/q
and if each iteration of the algorithm is done in polynomial time, then any strategy improvement algorithm runs in
\displaystyle O\left(nq^{r}Poly(N)\right)
.

Contributions

In this paper, we focus on SSGs whose
max
vertices have outdegree
\displaystyle d
with no constraints on the probability distribution of random vertices. We introduce two new recursive algorithms to solve SSGs. The first one, in Section
4
, fixes the strategies on two vertices and recursively solves the rest of the SSG. This algorithm has bound
\displaystyle O\left(\left(\left\lfloor\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)^{n/2}Poly(|G|)\right)
. The second algorithm presented in Section
5
works only for SSG of degree
\displaystyle 2
. However, it achieves a better bound that the
\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{3}^{n}Poly(N)\right)
of the first algorithm by reaching
\displaystyle O\left(\varphi^{n}Poly(N)\right)
with
\displaystyle\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
the golden ratio, which is, to the best of our knowledge, the best complexity for deterministic algorithm on this family of SSGs. Moreover, our algorithm does not require SSGs to be stopping, a common technical hypothesis, which may require a squarring of the number of vertices to be met.

An Overview of Simple Stochastic Games

Simple Stochastic Games where introduce by Anne Condon in
[
6
]
. We give a definition close to the one given in

Definition 1
.

A Simple Stochastic Game (SSG) is a directed graph
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
with a partition of the vertex set
\displaystyle V
in
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle V_{R}

max
,

, random and sink vertices such that:

•

every vertex of
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
and
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
has outdegree at least two.

•

every vertex
\displaystyle x
of
\displaystyle V_{R}
has outdegree at least one, and an associated rational probability distribution
\displaystyle p_{x}(\cdot)
on the outneighbourhood of
\displaystyle x
.

•

every vertex
\displaystyle x\in V_{S}
, there is an associated rational value
\displaystyle\mbox{Val}(x)
in the closed interval
\displaystyle[0,1]
.

Definition 2
.

A binary SSG is an SSG where every vertex of
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
has outdegree
\displaystyle 2
. An SSG is of degree
\displaystyle d
if its
max
vertices are of degree at most
\displaystyle d
.

In this article we denote
\displaystyle|V_{\text{\sc max}}|
by
\displaystyle n
. We will write
\displaystyle|G|
the size of the representations of the game
\displaystyle G
in bits. We present an instance of an SSG in Figure

.

The game is played by two players named
max
and
min
. The game starts by placing a token on some initial vertex
\displaystyle x_{0}
. Then, the token is moved according to the following rule. If the token is in a
max

min

\displaystyle x
, then the corresponding player moves the token according to an outgoing arc from
\displaystyle x
. If the token is in a random vertex
\displaystyle x

\displaystyle p_{x}
. When the token reaches a sink
\displaystyle s
, then player

has to pay player
max

\displaystyle\mbox{Val}(x)
. Informally, the goal of the game for
max
is to maximise the value of the final sink and, conversely, the goal of the game for
min

This game is turn-based, with perfect information. The strategies consider by both players should thus be deterministic and only relies on the current position of the token. This is a well-known result on simple stochastic games and a proof of this can be found in
[
6
,
14
]
. We thus only consider positional strategies.

Definition 3
.

A positional
max
strategy is a function
\displaystyle\sigma
from
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
to
\displaystyle V
such that for all
\displaystyle x
in
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle(x,\sigma(x))
is an arc of
\displaystyle G
.

A positional
min
strategy is a function
\displaystyle\tau
from
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
to
\displaystyle V
such that for all
\displaystyle x
in
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle(x,\tau(x))
is an arc of
\displaystyle G
.

In this paper when we talk about a pair of strategies
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
,
\displaystyle\sigma
is a positional
max
strategy and
\displaystyle\tau
is a positional
min
strategy. We can now define the value vector of a pair of strategy.

Definition 4
.

For
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
a pair of strategies, and
\displaystyle x_{0}
a vertex of
\displaystyle V
, the value
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}(x_{0})
is the expected gain for
max
if both players play according to
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\tau
. In other words:

v_{\sigma,\tau}(x_{0})=\sum\limits_{s\in V_{S}}\mathds{P}_{\sigma,\tau}(x_{0}\longrightarrow s)\mbox{Val}(s)

where
\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\sigma,\tau}(x_{0}\longrightarrow s)
is the probability that the game ends in
\displaystyle s
while starting in
\displaystyle x_{0}
and such that when the token is in a
max
vertex (resp.
min
vertex)
\displaystyle x
it moves to
\displaystyle\sigma(x)
(resp.
\displaystyle\tau(x)
).

The value vector
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}
is the vector
\displaystyle\left(v_{\sigma,\tau}(x)\right)_{x\in V}
. We compare value vectors according to the pointwise order. For two value vector
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle v^{\prime}
,
\displaystyle v>v^{\prime}
if, for all
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle v(x)\geq v^{\prime}(x)
and there is some
\displaystyle y\in V
such that
\displaystyle v(y)>v^{\prime}(y)
. As usual,
\displaystyle v\geq v^{\prime}
if
\displaystyle v>v^{\prime}
or if
\displaystyle v=v^{\prime}
.

Computing the value vector of a pair of strategies is equivalent to solving a Markov chain which can be done in time polynomial in
\displaystyle|G|
.

For a
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
we say that a
min
strategy
\displaystyle\tau
is a best response for
\displaystyle\sigma
if and only if, for every
min
strategy
\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}
, we have
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}\leq v_{\sigma,\tau^{\prime}}
. It is also well known that best response exists.

Proposition 5

(
[
6
]
)
.

For every positional
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
, there exists a positional
min
strategy
\displaystyle\tau
that is a best response for
\displaystyle\sigma
. Moreover, a best response can be computed in polynomial time by linear programming.

In the same way, we can define the best response for a
min
strategy.

Definition 6
.

For every
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
, we write
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}=v_{\sigma,\tau(\sigma)}
where
\displaystyle\tau(\sigma)
is a best response to
\displaystyle\sigma
.

We say that
\displaystyle\sigma
is better than
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
or has greater value, or we note
\displaystyle\sigma>\sigma^{\prime}
if
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}>v_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.

It is well known in the literature (
[
6
,
14
]
) that there is a pair of positional strategies
\displaystyle(\sigma^{*},\tau^{*})
that are called optimal strategies such that they are best response of each other. The value vector equilibrium
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{*},\tau^{*}}
is unique. Our goal is to compute this value vector. A known characteristic of a pair of optimal strategy is that its associated value vector satisfies local optimality.

Proposition 7

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
be an SSG. For
\displaystyle\sigma
a
max
strategy,
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau(\sigma))
is a pair of optimal strategy if and only if
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}
satisfies the following local optimality condition:

•

for
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x)=\max\{v_{\sigma,\tau}(y)\;|\;(x,y)\in E\}

•

for
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x)=\min\{v_{\sigma,\tau}(y)\;|\;(x,y)\in E\}

It is important to notice that the converse of the proposition is true only because we consider the best response to a
max
strategy. If we consider a
min
strategy and its best response, then it does not hold anymore because of possible loops.

Switch set and super-switch

3.1
Switch set

In this section we present an important concept for strategy improvement algorithm: the switch set. We present several properties of the switch set for SSG that we use to prove the complexity of our algorithms.

Definition 8
.

Let
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
be an SSG and
\displaystyle\sigma
a
max
strategy. The switch set of
\displaystyle\sigma
, written
\displaystyle S_{\sigma}
, is the set of vertices
\displaystyle x
such that there is
\displaystyle y
with
\displaystyle(x,y)\in E
and
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x)<v_{\sigma}(y)

Definition 9
.

Let
\displaystyle\sigma
be a
max
strategy. For all
\displaystyle x\in S_{\sigma}
, the improvement set, written
\displaystyle IS_{\sigma}(x)
is the set of neighbours
\displaystyle y
of
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(y)>v_{\sigma}(x)
and the best improvement option is defined as
\displaystyle bio_{\sigma}(x)=\argmax\limits_{y\in IS_{\sigma(x)}}\{v_{\sigma}(y)\}
.

In other words, the switch set of a
max
strategy is the set of
max
vertices that do not satisfy the local optimality condition presented Proposition
7
. This notion directly gives the concept of
\displaystyle\sigma
-switch.

Definition 10
.

Let
\displaystyle\sigma
be a
max
strategy with a non-empty switch set
\displaystyle S_{\sigma}
. A
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
is said to be a
\displaystyle\sigma
-switch if
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\neq\sigma
, for all
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc max}}\smallsetminus S_{\sigma}
,
\displaystyle\sigma(x)=\sigma^{\prime}(x)
and for all
\displaystyle x\in S_{\sigma}
such that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)\neq\sigma(x)
,
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)\in IS_{\sigma}(x)
.

A
\displaystyle\sigma
-switch is a strategy, where the strategy on vertices that satisfy local optimality for
\displaystyle\sigma
has been kept and it had been changed on some vertices that did not satisfy local optimality. Informally, as the name implies, it is a strategy where we ”switch” the strategy on some vertices that can achieve immediate better value by selecting another child. A representation of a switch is given in Figure

.

Definition 11
.

For
\displaystyle\sigma
a
max
strategy, the total switch
\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}
is the
\displaystyle\sigma
-switch where for all
\displaystyle x\in S_{\sigma}
,
\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(x)=bio_{\sigma}(x)
.

The demonstration of this proposition uses the condition that the SSG is stopping, which means that the SSG ends in a sink with probability

Corollary 13

A positional

This property directly gives a family of algorithms called Hoffmann-Karp algorithms. Starting from some

\displaystyle\sigma
