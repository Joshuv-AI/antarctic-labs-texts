The Aldous–Lyons Conjecture I: Subgroup Tests
Lewis Bowen, Michael Chapman, Alexander Lubotzky, Thomas Vidick

Abstract
. This paper, and its companion [ 21 ] , are devoted to a negative resolution of the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture [ 6 , 7 ] . This conjecture, originated in probability theory, is well known (cf. [ 37 ] ) to be equivalent to the statement that every invariant random subgroup of the free group is co-sofic. We disprove this last statement. In this part we introduce subgroup tests . These tests are finite distributions over continuous functions from the space of subgroups of the free group to { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} . Subgroup tests provide a general framework in which one can study invariant random subgroups of the free group. Classical notions such as group soficity and group stability arise naturally in this framework. By the correspondence between subgroups of the free group and Schreier graphs, one can view subgroup tests as a property testing model for certain edge-labeled graphs. This correspondence also provides the connection to random networks. Subgroup tests have values , which are their asymptotic optimal expectations when integrated against co-sofic invariant random subgroups. Our first main result is that, if every invariant random subgroup of the free group is co-sofic, then one can approximate the value of a subgroup test up to any positive additive constant. Our second main result is an essentially value preserving correspondence between certain non-local games and subgroup tests. By composing this correspondence with a stronger variant of the reduction in 𝖬𝖨𝖯 ∗ = 𝖱𝖤 \mathsf{MIP}^{*}=\mathsf{RE} [ 47 ] , proved in the companion paper [ 21 ] , we deduce that approximating the sofic value of a subgroup test is as hard as the Halting Problem, and in particular, undecidable. The combination of our two main results proves the existence of non co-sofic invariant random subgroups of the free group.

The Aldous–Lyons Conjecture

What is soficity?

Roughly speaking, a countable group
\Gamma
is sofic if it admits a sequence of partial actions on finite sets which approximates the action of
\Gamma
on itself by left-translations. If
\Gamma=\langle S|R\rangle
is finitely presented then this is equivalent to the existence of a sequence of finite graphs which converge to the Cayley graph of
\Gamma
in the sense of Benjamini–Schramm, which is a kind of local-on-average convergence.

To be more precise, a
rooted graph
is a pair
(G,o)
where
G=(V,E)
is a graph and
o\in V
is a distinguished vertex called the
root
. A random rooted graph
(G,o)
is
sofic
if there exists a sequence
(G_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite graphs such that, if
o_{i}
is a uniformly random vertex of
G_{i}
, then
(G_{i},o_{i})
converges to
(G,o)
in distribution (this means: for every
r>0
, the radius
r
neighborhood of the root in
G_{i}
converges in distribution to the radius
r
neighborhood of the root in
G
). This notion of convergence is due to Benjamini and Schramm
[
19
]
: They proved that if each finite graph
G_{i}
is planar and there is a uniform degree bound, then the limit
(G,o)
is almost surely recurrent.

The notions of Benjamini–Schramm convergence and soficity naturally generalize from graphs to labeled graphs or networks
[
6
]
, simplicial complexes
[
35
]
, manifolds
[
2
]
, measured equivalence relations
[
33
]
, measured groupoids

[

and most generally, measured metric spaces
[

Again, a finitely presented group is sofic if one of its Cayley graphs is sofic.

If one of the Cayley graphs is sofic, then all those that use finitely many generators are.
For example, amenable groups are sofic. In fact, the Cayley graph of an amenable group admits a sequence of finite sub-graphs in which the isoperimetric ratio (number of boundary vertices to number of vertices) tends to zero, which implies this sequence Benjamini–Schramm converges to the Cayley graph. Also, residually finite groups are sofic because they admit sequences of finite quotient groups whose Cayley graphs approximate the Cayley graph of the given group.

Gromov implicitly introduced sofic groups in
[

The soficity property has been useful in obtaining positive results about

It is a major open problem to determine whether all countable groups are sofic. We do not directly address this problem because the class of objects we study, described next, is more general than the class of groups.

What is unimodularity?

The main tool for analyzing Benjamini–Schramm limits is called the
Mass Transport Principle
which makes precise the intuitive notion of statistical homogeneity. To explain, a
doubly-rooted graph
is an ordered triple
(G,o_{1},o_{2})
, where
o_{1},o_{2}
are vertices of
G
. A
transport function
is a function
f
, satisfying some measurability condition, which takes as input a doubly rooted graph and outputs a non-negative real number. The interpretation is that
f(G,o_{1},o_{2})
is the amount of mass the first root
o_{1}
sends to the second root
o_{2}
. So if
(G,o)
is a random rooted graph then
\Ex_{(G,o)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{x\in V(G)}f(G,o,x)}}\right]
is the average amount of mass sent out of the root. Symmetrically,
\Ex_{(G,o)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{x\in V(G)}f(G,x,o)}}\right]
is the average amount of mass sent into the root. If these two quantities are equal for every transport function
f
, then we say
(G,o)
satisfies the
Mass Transport Principle
. Equivalently, we say it is
unimodular
(this is the term used by Aldous and Lyons in
[
6
]
). Alternatively, unimodularity can be formulated in terms of graphings or as invariance with respect to the root-changing equivalence relation on the space of rooted graphs. The former arises from the ergodic theory of measured equivalence relations (e.g.
[
4
]
). The latter point-of-view was introduced in
[
50
]
and further developed in
[
49
]
.

The term unimodular comes from the following special case. Suppose
G
is a connected transitive graph; this means that its automorphism group
\textrm{Aut}(G)
acts transitively on its vertex set. Then
(G,o)
is unimodular if and only if the automorphism group
\textrm{Aut}(G)
is unimodular in the sense that its left and right Haar measures agree.

The Mass Transport Principle arose in Häggström’s study of percolation clusters on trees
[
42
]
. In fact, it is an exercise to show that if
(G,o)
is unimodular and
p\in[0,1]
is given, then the Bernoulli
p
-percolation cluster containing
o
is also unimodular. Unimodularity was further developed in
[
18
]
(see also
[
20
]
where it was used to construct a version of the Euler characteristic for unimodular planar maps).

It is straightforward to verify that (1) unimodularity is closed under weak limits and (2) if
G
is a finite graph and
o
is a uniformly random vertex of
G
then
(G,o)
is unimodular. It follows from these observations that soficity implies unimodularity. Question 10.1 of
[
6
]
asks whether unimodularity implies soficity.

As above, unimodularity has been generalized to random rooted labeled graphs, simplicial complexes, manifolds and so on. Benjamini and Schramm note that many results which are known to hold in the deterministic setting of unimodular transitive graphs can be generalized to unimodular random graphs
[
19
]
. Aldous and Lyons demonstrate this with results about random walks, amenability, ends of graphs, spanning forests, percolation, and so on
[
6
]
.

What is an invariant random subgroup?

As a first step towards our goal, let us describe the algebraic formulation of the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture in terms of invariant random subgroups, which were introduced in
[
70
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
23
]
(and implicitly in
[
66
]
). The concept of invariant random subgroups has been profitably studied in the context of locally compact groups (e.g.
[
66
,
1
,
37
]
).
However, we will restrict our focus to the case of most relevance to this paper: finitely generated groups.
Already in this discrete setup, invariant random subgroups play an important role in graph convergence
[
44
]
, stability properties of groups
[
13
]
and the analysis of dynamical systems
[
66
,
23
]
for example.

So, fix a finitely generated group
\Gamma
. An
invariant random subgroup
(or IRS) of
\Gamma
is a random variable taking values in the space of all subgroups of
\Gamma
, whose law is invariant under the conjugation action of
\Gamma
. For example, every normal subgroup of
\Gamma
is an IRS. More generally, if
H\leq\Gamma
has only finitely many conjugates, then a uniformly random sample of its conjugates is an IRS. As finite index subgroups have only finitely many conjugates, they induce IRSs which are called
elementary
. To generate more examples, one can take convex combinations and weak* limits of elementary IRSs, and the IRSs that arise this way are called
co-sofic
. It turns out that for some groups every IRS is co-sofic, and for others there are non co-sofic IRSs (cf.
[
13
]
). The Aldous–Lyons Conjecture can be formulated as follows:

Are all IRSs of a (non-commutative) free group co-sofic?

For the rest of the paper, this is the formulation that we tackle, and it is known — and we explain it in the following paragraphs — to be equivalent to the probability theoretic formulation on Benjamini–Schramm limits of finite graphs.

The law (or distribution) of an IRS is a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathfrak{sub}(\Gamma)
, the space of subgroups of
\Gamma
. We let
{\rm IRS}(\Gamma)
denote the space of all such measures. By abuse of language, we say that
\mu
is an IRS if
\mu\in{\rm IRS}(\Gamma)
.
To see the relation between the two formulations of the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture, note that if
H
is a subgroup of
\Gamma
, then the Schreier coset graph of
{}_{H}\backslash^{\Gamma}
with respect to some generating set
S
is a rooted (directed, edge-labeled) graph, with the root being the coset of the identity. This gives a map from the space of subgroups
\mathfrak{sub}(\Gamma)
to the space of isomorphism classes of rooted (edge-labeled, directed) graphs.

If
\mu\in{\rm IRS}(\Gamma)
then
\mu
pushes forward under this map to a unimodular measure
[
3
, Proposition 14]
.
Moreover, if
\mu
is elementary, then its pushforward is induced by uniformly choosing a root of some finite connected graph. Therefore, if
\mu
is co-sofic, then its pushforward is sofic.

Now, consider the special case in which
\Gamma=\mathcal{F}(S)
is the free group generated by a finite set
S
. Suppose
\mu
is an invariant random subgroup of
\Gamma
, and that the (random) Schreier coset graph of
\mu
is sofic (in the directed, edge-labeled rooted graph category). This means there exists a sequence of finite directed, edge-labeled graphs
G_{i}
which Benjamini–Schramm converge to the (random) Schreier coset graph of
\mu
. After perturbing the given sequence if necessary, we may assume that each
G_{i}
is itself a Schreier coset graph with respect to some finite-index subgroup
H_{i}
of
\mathcal{F}(S)
— the existence of such perturbations is exactly where the
freeness
of the group
\mathcal{F}(S)
plays a role. Moreover, if
\widetilde{H}_{i}
is a uniformly random conjugate of
H_{i}
, then the distribution of
\widetilde{H}_{i}
converges to
\mu
. Therefore, a positive solution to the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture implies that every IRS of
\mathcal{F}(S)
is co-sofic.

2

2

There is a standard “decoration of graphs” technique that allows one to show that the directed, edge labeled (with finitely many labels) version of the Aldous–Lyons conjecture is
equivalent
to the non-directed, non-edge labeled version. As Bálint Virág pointed to us, the same technique is used, e.g., to show that every group is the automorphism group of some graph. As this is standard, we do not elaborate on it anymore.

Our approach

What is a subgroup test?

The set
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
of all IRSs of the free group and the set
{\rm IRS}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{F})
of co-sofic IRSs of the free group are both convex. Thus, the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture is asking whether certain convex sets can be separated. A natural way to distinguish between two convex sets is to use (continuous) linear functionals. So, in order to separate the above, one should search for a
rich enough
collection of functionals.
Subgroup tests
and their
values
will play this exact role.

A
challenge
is a continuous map from
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
to
\{0,1\}
. A subgroup test
\mathcal{T}
is a probability distribution on a finite set of challenges. One can integrate any subgroup test against any probability measure over
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
. If
\pi
is in
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
, then this integral is called the
value
of the
strategy

\pi
against the test
\mathcal{T}
.
Many natural and well studied properties of groups, specifically soficity and pointwise permutation stability, can be formulated as properties of subgroup tests.

The terminology of “tests” and “values” originates in the field of
interactive proofs
in computer science.
An interactive proof designates an interaction between two entities, a “verifier” and a “prover”. The goal of the prover is to convince the verifier of the validity of a certain claim, and the goal of the verifier is to test the prover so that it accepts the interaction only if the claim is indeed correct.
Examples of “claims” studied in computer science are the
3
-colorability of a given input graph, or that a Turing machine whose description is passed as input halts.
In our approach, the methods of the verifier and the prover are restricted. The prover is required to sample an element of
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
.
The sampling method itself is restricted to some sub-class of IRSs (e.g., elementary IRSs); the choice of distribution according which to sample is the only degree of freedom the prover has, and is called its
strategy
. The verifier gets access to (the indicator function of) the prover’s sampled subgroup
H
, and it makes a decision whether to
accept
or
reject
this subgroup.
To decide, the verifier holds some finite set
K\subseteq\mathcal{F}
— known beforehand to the prover — and according to which combinations of elements of
K
are contained in
H
, it accepts or rejects; the
rules
that control which combinations of elements result in acceptance and which combinations result in rejection are also know to the prover beforehand. The
value
of a strategy in a game is the probability, taken over the prover’s sampling and the verifier’s probabilistic choices, that the verifier makes the decision to accept when interacting with a prover using this strategy.

In spite of these restrictions, it is possible to verify hard computational problems in this setup.

3

3

For the expert, we note that restrictions on the prover and verifier can affect both the completeness and soundness properties of a proof system. Hence, a priori, they may either lower or raise the complexity of the associated class of interactive proofs compared to classical single-prover interactive proofs.
For example, it is not difficult to design a subgroup test such that there is a value-
1
strategy for this test if and only if a given graph is
3
-colorable. Jumping ahead, the correct analogy is with the theory of
multiprover
interactive proofs, and even more specifically interactive proofs with quantum provers sharing entanglement, also known as non-local games. We explain below this connection, which plays an essential role in the second part of this paper.

What is a non-local game?

Morally,
non-local games
originated in Bell’s resolution
[
16
]
of the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox
[
32
]
. Einstein was famously concerned by the ability of space-time separated particles to
correlate
in ways that might violate relativity, a possibility that seemed to be suggested by the mathematical modeling of quantum mechanics. Einstein argued for a
local hidden variable
theory — namely, that the particles
did
share some information beforehand, which allowed them to correlate — and that physicists should search for this hidden information.
Bell provided a thought experiment, that in modern jargon is an instance of a non-local game, which proves that the kinds of correlations that arise from isolated quantum mechanical systems are intrinsically different from those that could be generated in any hidden variable model.
Bell’s work lay the foundation for the subsequent design, and execution, of experiments which verify the existence of quantum entanglement (and thus refute Einstein’s approach; see also
[
27
]
). The 2022 Nobel prize in physics was awarded to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger, partly for performing non-local games as experiments and verifying that the winning statistics in the games exceeds what local hidden variable models allow.

A
correlation
is a function
p\colon A\times A\times X\times X\to[0,1]
, where
X
and
A
are finite sets, such that for every pair
x,y\in X
the function
p(\cdot,\cdot|x,y)
is a probability distribution over
A\times A
— the quantity
p(a,b|x,y)
should be thought of as the answer to “what is the probability
a
and
b
are provided as answers given that
x
and
y
were asked as questions?”; this cryptic phrase will soon be clarified. A correlation
p
is said to be
deterministic
if there are two functions
f_{1},f_{2}\colon X\to A
such that
p(a,b|x,y)=1
only if
f_{1}(x)=a
and
f_{2}(y)=b
.
The convex hull
C_{c}
of deterministic correlations is the set of
local
or
classical
correlations.
4
4

4

An example of a
non-local
correlation is
p(a,b|x,y)=1
only when
a=y
and
b=x
. Intuitively, this is because this correlation implies
signaling
from one system to the other.

On the other hand, the
quantum correlations

C_{q}
are those that can be generated by performing quantum measurements on a (finite-dimensional) bipartite physical system, where the measurement on one part depends only on
x
and generates
a
, while the measurement on the other part depends only on
y
and generates
b
. For a formal description, see Remark
6.8
. The set
C_{q}
is convex, but interestingly it is not closed
[
64
]
.

A
non-local
game
\mathcal{G}
is specified by a probability distribution
\mu
on the set
{X}\times{X}
, and a
decision function

D:X\times X\times A\times A\to\{0,1\}
.

5

5

As can be seen in Section
1.5
, our definition of non-local games is slightly different. This difference is mainly cosmetic, and is driven by our motivation to relate non-local games to subgroup tests.

The interpretation of this combinatorial object as a “game” comes from thinking of it (similarly to subgroup tests) as an interactive proof, but this time with two provers, a la
[
17
]
.
The common dramatization goes as follows: Two provers are spatially separated. The verifier samples a pair of “questions”
x,y\sim\mu
, and “asks” one prover
x
and the other
y
. The provers then apply some local procedure to choose their “answers”
a
and
b
respectively. Finally, the verifier accepts if
D(x,y,a,b)=1
and rejects otherwise.

Since the process by which the provers generate their answers is hidden to us, we can only “observe” (samples from) the distribution of answers given questions, namely, the underlying correlation. So, the correlation can be thought of as the “strategy” used by the provers (similar to the way IRSs are seen as strategies for the prover in a subgroup test). Bell’s separation of
C_{c}
from
C_{q}
amounts to devising a game that is “easy” for players that use quantum correlations, yet “hard” for players that use only classical ones.

Now, there is more than one candidate mathematical model for entanglement in quantum mechanics. A slightly generalized model, suggested by Tsirelson
[
68
]
, gives birth to a set of correlations known as
quantum commuting
correlations
C_{qc}
. This set is closed and contains the quantum correlations. Tsirelson famously asked whether the closure of
C_{q}
is equal to
C_{qc}

[
69
]
. At this point, we hope the type of problem already resonates with the reader, as this is again a
separation of convex sets
type of problem.
6
6

6

Connes’ embedding problem (CEP) is also a separation of convex sets type of problem, specifically, separating all characters of the free group from those that are limits of finite dimensional characters. This problem was shown
[
36
,
48
,
59
]
to be equivalent to Tsirelson’s problem, and also has close connections with invariant random subgroups — as every IRS induces a character. We omit discussions on the relations between this work and CEP from the text, as they are not particularly helpful for understanding our approach.

Harnessing undecidability

It was known that if the quantum correlations are dense in the quantum commuting correlations, then the complexity class of multiprover interactive proofs with entangled provers (
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
) contains only decidable languages.
By proving that the Halting Problem, which is undecidable, is in
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}
, the authors of
[
47
]
were able to resolve Tsirelson’s problem in the negative.

In this paper a similar path is followed: Rather than directly find a functional that separates the co-sofic and general IRSs of the free group, we describe a computational problem — approximating the sofic value of a subgroup test — whose undecidability implies a negative resolution of the Aldous–Lyons conjecture. Our approach can be described in two steps: First, show that a positive solution to the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture implies that approximating the sofic value of a subgroup test is decidable. Second, prove that this computational task is undecidable.

Elaborating on the first, given a subgroup test
\mathcal{T}
, we prove that its optimal value against co-sofic IRSs — which we call the
sofic value
of
\mathcal{T}
— can be approximated from below, and its optimal value against
any
IRS — which we call the
ergodic value
of
\mathcal{T}
— can be approximated from above. Thus, if the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture has a positive answer, then the sofic and ergodic value of a test always agree, and using the aforementioned approximation procedures they can be calculated to any predetermined accuracy.

Elaborating on the second, we provide a mechanism for translating certain non-local games (see Section
1.5
), which we call
tailored non-local games
, to subgroup tests. This translation is essentially value preserving. Namely, if the non-local game had a (certain kind) of perfect quantum strategy — which we call a
Z
-aligned permutation strategy that commutes along edges — then the analogous subgroup test has a perfect (co-sofic) strategy. In the other direction, if the subgroup test has an almost perfect (co-sofic) strategy, then the non-local game has an almost perfect strategy. Hence, a certain strengthening of the reduction in
[
47
]
, which states that small additive constant approximations to the value of
tailored
non-local game are undecidable (Theorem
7.4
), implies that the sofic value of a game
cannot
be approximated to arbitrary precision. Thus, the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture has a negative solution.

1.1.
Subgroup Tests

Let
S
be a finite set,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(S)
be the free group with basis
S
,
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
the collection of subgroups of
\mathcal{F}
and
{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
the set of all Borel probability measures on
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
.
A
challenge
is a pair
(K;D)
such that
K
is a finite subset of
\mathcal{F}
, and
D
is a function from subsets of
K
to
\{0,1\}
. A subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}

passes
the challenge
(K;D)
if
D(A\cap K)=1
, and
fails
it otherwise.
A
test

\mathcal{T}
is a finite collection of challenges
\{(K_{i};D_{i})\}_{i\in Q}
, where
Q
is a finite index set, together with a probability distribution
\mu
over the set
Q
.
A
strategy
is a (Borel) probability distribution over subgroups of the free group, namely an element
\pi\in{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
.
One can
run
the test
\mathcal{T}
against
\pi
, as follows.
First the verifier samples
i\in Q
according to
\mu
. Next, the prover samples a subgroup
H
according to
\pi
. Finally, the verifier makes the decision to
accept
if
H
passes the
i^{\rm th}
challenge
(K_{i};D_{i})
, and
reject
if
H
fails the
i^{\rm th}
challenge.

The
value
of using the strategy
\pi
against the test
\mathcal{T}
is its acceptance probability, namely

{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi)=\Ex_{H\sim\pi}\Ex_{i\sim\mu}[D_{i}(H\cap K_{i})].

For a fixed
\mathcal{T}
, the value is a continuous linear functional from probability distributions over subgroups of
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, it enables us to study convex subsets of
{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
by their optimal value against a given test.

Remark 1.1
.

As mentioned earlier, a challenge is, essentially, a continuous map from the space of subgroups of
\mathcal{F}
to
\{0,1\}
. One can extract from any such continuous map a challenge
(K;D)
that describes it and vice versa. Therefore, a test is a convex combination of continuous maps from
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
to
\{0,1\}
, and the value is integration of this map against a (probability) measure.

1.2.
Invariant random subgroups

As
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
inherits the product topology
from the power set
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
, it is compact.
Every action of a group
\Gamma
on a set
X
extends naturally to an action of
\Gamma
on the power set
\{0,1\}^{X}
by

\forall\gamma\in\Gamma,A\subseteq X\ \colon\ \ \gamma.A=\{\gamma.a\mid a\in A\}\;.

Hence, the conjugation action of
\mathcal{F}
on itself is inherited by
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
. Also,
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
is preserved by this action. Hence, the conjugation action extends to its power set
\{0,1\}^{\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})}
by

\forall w\in\mathcal{F},A\subseteq\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})\ \colon\ \ w.A=\{wHw^{-1}\mid H\in A\}\;.

The conjugation action of
\mathcal{F}
further extends to
{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
:
For every Borel set
B\subseteq\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
and
\mu\in{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
, we have

(w.\mu)(B)=\mu(w^{-1}.B)\;.

Definition 1.2
.

The set of
invariant random subgroups

{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})

consists of all probability measures in
{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))

that are invariant under the action of
\mathcal{F}
.

The
ergodic value
of a test
\mathcal{T}
is the best performance of a strategy
\pi\in{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
against it, namely

(1.1)

{\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})=\sup\{{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi)\mid\pi\in{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})\}\;.

Remark 1.3
.

The extremal points in the convex set
{\rm IRS}(\Gamma)
are commonly referred to as
ergodic IRSs
.
As we take the supremum of a linear functional over a convex compact set, the optimum is obtained on an extremal point, which explains the phrase
ergodic value
.

1.3.
Finitely described invariant random subgroups

Let
X
be a finite set, and let
{\rm Sym}(X)
be the symmetric group acting on
X
. Every map
\sigma\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(X)
extends uniquely to an action of
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(S)
on
X
.
For a vertex
x\in X
, we can define its
stabilizer
to be

(1.2)

{\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in\mathcal{F}\mid\sigma(w).x=x}}\right\}\;,

which is a subgroup of
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, we can associate with
\sigma\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(X)
an IRS of
\mathcal{F}
via the following sampling procedure:

(1)
Choose
x\in X
uniformly at random.
(2)

Output
{\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)
.
We denote by
\Phi
the map from finite actions of
\mathcal{F}
to
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
defined by this procedure, namely

(1.3)

\Phi(\sigma)=\Ex_{x\in X}\big[{\bf 1}_{{\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)}\big]\;,

where
{\bf 1}_{H}
is the Dirac measure concentrated on the subgroup
H
and
\Ex_{x\in X}
is the expectation according to the uniform measure over
X
. The set of
finitely described invariant random subgroups

{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})
is the image of the map
\Phi
in
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
.

Definition 1.4
.

The
sofic value
of a test
\mathcal{T}
is the (asymptotic) best performance of a strategy
\pi\in{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})
against it, namely

(1.4)

{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T})=\sup\,\big\{{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\Phi(\sigma))\mid|X|<\infty,\ \sigma\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(X)\big\}\;.

Remark 1.5
.

The name sofic value is appropriate, as demonstrated in Section
4.2
. Furthermore, we often abuse notation and use
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\sigma)
instead of
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\Phi(\sigma))
, even though the action itself is not an IRS.

Since
{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
, we have
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T})\leq{\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})
for every test
\mathcal{T}
. The Aldous–Lyons conjecture
[
6
]

can
be formulated as follows:

Conjecture 1.6

(Aldous–Lyons, cf. Section 6 of
[
37
]
)
.

Is
{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})

weak* dense in
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
?

Remark 1.7
.

In their paper
[
6
]
, Aldous and Lyons ask this as a question. In the passing of time, the positive form of this problem, namely that
{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})

is
dense in
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
, received the name
The Aldous–Lyons Conjecture
(cf.
[
7
]
). Thus, we use the more common “Conjecture” phrasing instead of Problem.

The algebraic formulation we provided earlier, which can also be found in Section 6 of
[
37
]
, may seem different from the above. The question was whether every invariant random subgroup of
\mathcal{F}
is
co-sofic
, where co-sofic means an IRS which is the weak* limit of convex combinations of uniform distributions over finite index subgroups. Since the finitely described IRSs are dense in the co-sofic ones, the formulation in Conjecture
1.6
is equivalent to this standard (algebraic) formulation.

Lastly, the term co-sofic is closely related to the better known term of a
sofic group
(cf. Definition
4.4
).
A finitely presented group
\mathcal{F}/N
is sofic if and only if the Dirac measure concentrated on
N
is a co-sofic IRS (cf. Proposition 6.1 in
[
37
]
).
Thus, a positive solution to the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture

implies in particular that every group is sofic.
7
7

7

The way we formulate it, a positive solution to the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture only implies that all finitely presented groups are sofic. It is standard to deduce it for all groups from the finitely presented ones
[
34
]
.

Corollary 1.8
.

Since
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\cdot)
is a continuous functional, a positive solution to the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture
1.6
implies that for every test
\mathcal{T}
,
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T})={\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})
.

Remark 1.9
.

Due to the relation between subgroups of the free group and Schreier graphs, one can view subgroup tests as a new property testing model for (edge-labeled) graphs:
Let
X
be a finite set and
\sigma\colon S\to X
a transitive action (the non-transitive case is a convex combination of transitive ones). For
x\in X
, the stabilizer
{\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)
is exactly the set of labeled paths in the Schreier graph of
\mathcal{F}(S)
with respect to
{\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)
and
S
that begin and end at
x
, namely, closed paths originating from
x
. Thus, when the finitely described IRS
\Phi(\sigma)
is put against a test
\mathcal{T}
, we actually test the aforementioned Schreier graph in the following way:
First, choose a vertex
x\in X
uniformly at random and sample
i\sim\mu
.
List the paths from
K_{i}
and for each one check whether it is closed or open. According to this check, decide using
D_{i}
whether to accept or reject.

1.4.
Decidability of approximating the sofic value

If the challenge distribution
\mu
in a subgroup test
\mathcal{T}
is rational, then
\mathcal{T}
can be encoded as a finite bit string. There are many (non-equivalent) ways of doing so.
Assume we fixed
some
encoding for tests with rational challenge distributions such that, given the encoding, all underlying combinatorial data of the test can be calculated in finite time.
Namely, the set of generators
S
, the indexing set of challenges
Q
, the collection of challenges
\{(K_{i};D_{i})\}_{i\in Q}
and the distribution
\mu
can all be
read
from the encoding in finite time.

Theorem 1.10

(Main Theorem I)
.

For every fixed encoding of tests such that all combinatorial data of the test can be read from it in finite time:

(1)

There is a Turing machine
M_{1}
that takes as an input (the encoding of) a test
\mathcal{T}
, and outputs an infinite sequence of non-decreasing numbers
(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[0,1]
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha_{t}={\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T})
.

(2)

There is a Turing machine
M_{2}
that takes as an input (the encoding of) a test
\mathcal{T}
, and outputs an infinite sequence of non-increasing numbers
(\beta_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[0,1]
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta_{t}={\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})
.

Remark 1.11
.

Clause
(2)
of Theorem
1.10
should be compared to the Navascués–Pironio–Acín (NPA) hierarchy in the study of non-local games
[
58
]
. See also the introduction of
[
47
]
.

Corollary 1.12
.

If the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture
1.6
has a positive solution, then for every
\theta>0
, there is a Turing machine which accepts a subgroup test

as input and outputs its sofic value

.

1.5.
Tailored non-local games and their associated subgroup tests

As described before, the standard definition of a (non-local) game consists of two finite sets, a questions set
X
and an answers set
A
, together with a probability distribution
\mu
over
X\times X
and a decision function
D\colon X\times X\times A\times A\to\{0,1\}
. In this paper, we use a slightly modified definition, that makes the connection with subgroup tests clearer. Before diving into our alternative definition, note first that the distribution
\mu
defines a graph structure on
X
, by associating edges with its support. Furthermore, as the exact choice of
A
is irrelevant, we can assume it is the set of all bit strings up to some length
\Lambda
.

A (synchronous, non-local) game consists of a graph
G=(V,E)
, a distribution
\mu
over
E
,
a length function
\ell\colon V\to\mathbb{N}
, formal sets of generators
S_{x}=\{\mathsf{X}^{x,i}\mid 1\leq i\leq\ell(x)\}
— the union of which is denoted by
S=\bigcup_{x\in V}S_{x}
— and decision functions
D_{xy}\colon\{0,1\}^{S_{xy}}\to\{0,1\}
for every edge
xy\in E
, where
S_{xy}=S_{x}\cup S_{y}
.
8
8

8

So, the main difference in our definition, is that the decision function
D
rejects automatically answers that are not of the appropriate length according to
\ell
.

A (synchronous, quantum)
strategy
for
\mathcal{G}
is a map
\rho\colon\bigcup_{x\in V}S_{x}\to U(n)
, where the images are involutions, and
\rho({S_{x}})
commute for every fixed
x\in V
.
9
9

9

This is again not the standard definition of a quantum strategy. Unpacking our definition,
\rho
associates an observable (Hermitian operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space that squares to the identity) with each bit of the answer when asked for
x\in V
. Since the answer must be totally measured, all the observables associated with a specific vertex must commute. The fact there is a single map
\rho
, and not one for each prover, is the
synchronicity
of this strategy.

Such a strategy defines for every
xy\in E
a probability distribution over maps
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
in the following way:
For every
\mathsf{X}\in S
, let
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}
be the projection on the
(+1)
-eigenspace of
\rho(\mathsf{X})
, and
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{1}
the projection on its
(-1)
-eigenspace.
Furthermore, let
\tau
be the dimension normalized trace on
n\times n
matrices.
Then, the probability that
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
is sampled according to
\rho
is by definition

(1.5)

\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S_{x}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}\prod_{\mathsf{Y}\in S_{y}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{Y})}}}\right)\;.

A map
\gamma
sampled that way is said to be sampled
according to

\rho
, and we denote it by
\gamma\sim\rho
.
10
10

10

Note that this sampling depends on the chosen edge
xy\in E
. Namely, though implicit in the notation,
\gamma
is defined from
S_{xy}
for some edge
xy\in E
. It is important to note that
\gamma
is
not
globally defined on all of
S
.

Relating to standard notations, we remark that by letting
a=\gamma|_{S_{x}}
and
b=\gamma|_{S_{y}}
, the correlation
p(a,b|x,y)
induced by the quantum strategy
\rho
(as in Remark
6.8
) agrees with formula (
1.5
).
In a similar manner to subgroup tests, one can run the game
\mathcal{G}
against the strategy
\rho
:
First, the verifier samples an edge
xy\in E
according to
\mu
. Then, the prover samples
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
according to
\rho
. Finally, the verifier accepts if
D_{xy}(\gamma)=1
, and rejects otherwise.

As for subgroup tests, the
value
of the strategy
\rho
against the non-local game
\mathcal{G}
is its acceptance probability when ran against the game. Namely,

\begin{split}{\rm val}(\mathcal{G},\rho)=\Ex_{xy\sim\mu}\Ex_{\gamma\sim\rho}[D_{xy}(\gamma)]\;.\end{split}

The (synchronous)
quantum value
of
\mathcal{G}
,
{\rm val}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
, is the supremum of
{\rm val}(\mathcal{G},\rho)
over all possible quantum strategies against
\mathcal{G}
.

The main result in
[
47
]
(recalled in detail in Theorem
6.10
herein) is a reduction from the Halting Problem to approximating the quantum value of a game.
Our main contribution in the second half of this paper (Sections
6
,
7
and
8
) is a mapping from a specific sub-class of non-local games — which we term
tailored
non-local games
and describe further below — to subgroup tests, that (almost) preserves their values.

Theorem 1.13

(Main Theorem II, informal. For the formal version see Theorem
7.3
)
.

There is a transformation that takes as input a
tailored
non-local game (see Definition
6.22
) and outputs a subgroup test (which we call the associated synchronous subgroup test, see Definition
7.1
), such that:

(1)

If the game had a perfect (i.e., a value
1
)
Z
-aligned, commuting along edges, permutation strategy
(see Definitions
6.11
and
6.24
), then the associated subgroup test has a perfect strategy.

(2)

Every almost perfect finitely described strategy for the associated subgroup test can be transformed into an almost perfect quantum strategy to the original tailored non-local game.

Informally, a tailored game is one where the decision function
D_{xy}
takes the following form. First,
D_{xy}
examines the value taken by
\gamma
on a
subset
of the generators
S_{x}\cup S_{y}
(we refer to these as
readable
variables). Based on this,
D_{xy}
determines a collection of linear equations on the entire string
\gamma
. Finally,
D_{xy}
accepts if and only if all these equations are satisfied. This form for the game can be seen as a generalization of binary linear constraint system games (Example
6.26
), which corresponds to the case where the set of readable variables is empty.

A
permutation strategy
, as its name suggests, is a quantum strategy which is induced by permutations. Such a strategy is said
Z
-aligned
when the observables associated with the readable variables (i.e. the bits of the answer
\gamma
that control the parity checks in the tailored game) are all diagonal matrices (with respect to some natural basis). The restriction to
Z
-aligned strategies is crucial for item (1) in Theorem
7.3
above to hold.

In a companion paper
[
21
]
, we prove that the reduction of the Halting Problem to approximating the quantum value of a game presented in
[
47
]
can be modified such that the resulting non-local game is tailored and satisfies the required stronger assumptions (Theorem
7.4
): If the input Turing machine halts, then the game has a perfect
Z
-aligned permutation strategy that commutes along edges, and if the input Turing machine does not halt, then the value of the game is bounded from above by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Therefore, together with our mapping from non-local games to subgroup tests, we deduce that approximating the sofic value of a game is undecidable. In turn, by Corollary
1.12
,
the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture
1.6
has a negative solution.

Remark 1.14
.

In
[
47
, Section 12.3]
, the authors were able to specify a non-local game whose quantum value is bounded above by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
but has a perfect quantum commuting strategy. Using the results of
[
54
]
, which we elaborate on further in the next remark, this implies the existence of a finitely presented
*
-algebra which can be endowed with a tracial state and cannot be embedded into an ultrapower of the hyperfinite
II_{1}
factor. This is a somewhat constructive counter example, but the state itself is only proven to exist and does not have a constructive form (at this point). By the various formulations of Connes’ embedding problem, this proves that there is a non-hyperlinear character of the free group, which is the analogue of a non co-sofic IRS of the free group, but it is provided in a non-constructive form.

By applying the “same” construction as in
[
47
, Section 12.3]
with the algorithm constructed in Theorem
1.10
replacing the NPA hierarchy algorithm, one can pin down an explicit subgroup test with perfect ergodic strategy but all sofic strategies bounded above by some constant (which can be approximated by following the proofs). This is the most constructive we can get in this case, namely, the specific non co-sofic IRS which is the perfect strategy for this subgroup test is proven only to exist, and is not constructed. Thus, we decided not to specify this test.

It would be very interesting to find an explicit non co-sofic IRS, namely separate between
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
and
\overline{{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})}
in a constructive manner.

Remark 1.15
.

We comment on a natural attempt to extend the construction of
[
47
]
so as to directly deduce the existence of a non-sofic group, a result which would imply a negative answer to the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture.

The idea (which is folklore, and explicitly mentioned in
[
60
]
) is as follows. It is well-known that to every synchronous game
\mathcal{G}
one can associate an algebra
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{G})
such that there is a tight connection between the
C^{*}
-representation theory of this algebra and perfect values for the game in various correlation sets
[
61
,
54
,
46
]
. In the special case where
\mathcal{G}
is a linear constraint system (LCS) game, there is a group
\Gamma
such that
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{G})
is (isomorphic to the completion of) the complex group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]

[
54
,
39
]
.
11
11

11

Actually, it is isomorphic to the completion of
\nicefrac{{\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]}}{{\langle\mathsf{J}+1\rangle}}
, where
\mathsf{J}
is a special generator in
\Gamma
and
\langle\mathsf{J}+1\rangle
is the two-sided ideal generated by
\mathsf{J}+1
in
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
.

Now, if the reduction given in
[
47
]
returned only LCS games, then one would obtain the following result: there exists an LCS game with a perfect quantum commuting strategy, but no (asymptotically) perfect quantum strategy. By the connection mentioned above this implies that the group
\Gamma
associated with
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{G})
is non-hyperlinear. As every sofic group is hyperlinear, one would thus obtain an example of a non-sofic group.

Since the reduction in
[
47
]

does not
return LCS games, a workaround could be to find an embedding from the algebra
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{G})
, where
\mathcal{G}
is returned by
[
47
]
, into
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
where
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is an LCS game. Obstacles to this “black-box” approach are discussed in
[
60
]
.

One can view our approach as finding a “sweet spot” that is in some sense half-way between synchronous games, which were used in
[
47
]
, and LCS games. This is because tailored games are a natural generalization of LCS games. In the companion paper we show that
[
47
]
can be adapted to return tailored games, and in the present paper we show that this suffices to resolve the Aldous–Lyons Conjecture. The existence of non-sofic groups (and non-hyperlinear groups) remains an elusive open problem.

2.1.
Topology (and other properties) of invariant random subgroups

12
12

12

The content of this section is standard. For example, it appears in Section 3 of
[
3
]
.

We first go through some properties of the space
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
(induced with the product topology). For every
K,L\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, let

\mathcal{C}(K,L)=\{A\subseteq\mathcal{F}\mid K\subseteq A,\ L\cap A=\emptyset\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}.

Then,

(1)

When
K
and
L
are finite, the set
\mathcal{C}(K,L)
is open and closed. Moreover, the collection

\{\mathcal{C}(K,L)\mid K,L\subseteq\mathcal{F},\ |K|,|L|<\infty\}

is a basis for the topology of
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
. In addition,
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
is compact.

(2)

For every
w\in\mathcal{F}
and subsets
K,L\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have

w.\mathcal{C}(K,L)=\mathcal{C}(wKw^{-1},wLw^{-1}).

The following is a standard fact. A proof is added because elements of it are later used in Lemma
2.12
.

Claim 2.1
.

The set of subgroups
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
is closed in the collection of all subsets
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
.

Proof.

A subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is a subgroup if it satisfies the following three conditions:

\begin{split}(1)\ {\rm Id}_{\mathcal{F}}\in A\ ,\quad(2)\ w\in A\implies w^{-1}\in A\ ,\quad(3)\ w,w^{\prime}\in A\implies w\cdot w^{\prime}\in A.\end{split}

For every
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, define the following open sets: The subsets not containing
{\rm Id}_{\mathcal{F}}
,
C_{{\rm Id}}=\mathcal{C}(\emptyset,\{{\rm Id}_{\mathcal{F}}\})
. The subsets containing
w\in\mathcal{F}
but not
w^{-1}
,
C_{w}=\mathcal{C}(\{w\},\{w^{-1}\})
. The subsets containing
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
but not
w\cdot w^{\prime}
,

C_{w,w^{\prime}}=\mathcal{C}(\{w,w^{\prime}\},\{w\cdot w^{\prime}\})
.
Then,

\Big(\bigcup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}C_{w,w^{\prime}}\Big)\cup\Big(\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{F}}C_{w}\Big)\cup C_{\rm Id}=\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}\setminus\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}),

which proves that
\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})
is a closed set.
∎

Recall the definition of
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
, the invariant random subgroups of the free group
\mathcal{F}
(Definition
1.2
).

Fact 2.2
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
. Then,

(1)

\mu=\nu
if and only if
\mu(\mathcal{C}(K,L))=\nu(\mathcal{C}(K,L))
for every finite
K,L\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.

(2)

\mu
is conjugation invariant if and only if for every
s\in S
we have
s.\mu=\mu
. Hence,
\mu\in{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
if and only if for all
s\in S
and finite
K,L\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have
\mu(\mathcal{C}(K,L))=\mu(s.\mathcal{C}(K,L))
.

A sequence of measures
\mu_{n}\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
is said to weak* converge to
\mu
if for all continuous
f\colon\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\Ex_{H\sim\mu_{n}}[f(H)]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\Ex_{H\sim\mu}[f(H)].

We denote weak convergence by
\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}\mu
.

Remark 2.3
.

In our context, there is a more “down to earth” way of viewing weak* convergence. For every finite subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, and every probability measure
\mu\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
, we can define a probability measure
\mu\cap A\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{A})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2^{A}}
by

\forall B\subseteq A\ \colon\ \ \mu\cap A(B)=\mu(\{H\subseteq\mathcal{F}\mid H\cap A=B\}).

Then,

\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{w^{*}}\mu\iff\forall A\subseteq\mathcal{F},|A|<\infty\ \colon\ \ \mu_{n}\cap A\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\mu\cap A,

where the convergence on the right is the standard one in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2^{A}}
.

Claim 2.4
.

The space
{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
is compact in the weak* topology.

Proof.

Since
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}
is compact, the fact can be deduced from the Banach–Alaoglu Theorem (cf. Theorem 3.15 in
[
63
]
).
∎

2.2.
Pseudo subgroups

For every
B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, the restriction map
R_{B\subseteq C}\colon\{0,1\}^{C}\to\{0,1\}^{B}
is

\forall A\subseteq C\ \colon\ \ R_{B\subseteq C}(A)=A\cap B.

When
C=\mathcal{F}
we remove it from the notation and write
R_{B}
instead of
R_{B\subseteq\mathcal{F}}
.
For sets
K,L\subseteq B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we can define

\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L)=\{A\subseteq B\mid K\subseteq A,\ A\cap L=\emptyset\}.

Note that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{F}}(K,L)=\mathcal{C}(K,L)
.

Claim 2.5
.

Let
K,L\subseteq B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Then
R_{B\subseteq C}(\mathcal{C}_{C}(K,L))=\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L)
and
R_{B\subseteq C}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))=\mathcal{C}_{C}(K,L).

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq B
and
A^{\prime}\subseteq C
such that
A=A^{\prime}\cap B
. As
K,L\subseteq B
, the intersection of
A^{\prime}
with
K
and
L
depends only on
A^{\prime}\cap B=A
. Hence

(2.1)

A\in\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L)\iff A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{C}(K,L).

Adding this observation to the fact
R_{B\subseteq C}
is onto proves the desired claim.
∎

Now, we can pushforward measures along the inverse system of restrictions. Namely, given
B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and a probability measure
\pi
on
\{0,1\}^{C}
we can define the probability measure
R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi
on
\{0,1\}^{B}
by

\forall Y\subseteq_{\rm Borel}\{0,1\}^{B}\ \colon\ \ R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi(Y)=\pi(R_{B\subseteq C}^{-1}(Y)).

Corollary 2.6
.

Claim
2.5
implies that
R_{B\subseteq C}
is continuous. Therefore,
R_{B\subseteq C*}\colon{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{C})\to{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{B})
is continuous.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. A subset
A\subseteq B
is said to be a
pseudo subgroup
of
B
if there is a subgroup
H\leq\mathcal{F}
such that
R_{B}(H)=H\cap B=A
. Denote by
P\mathfrak{sub}(B)
the collection of pseudo subgroups of
B
.
13
13

13

Note that this collection is a closed subset of the subsets of
B
.

Claim 2.8
.

Let
A\subseteq B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Then,
A
is a pseudo subgroup of
B
if and only if
\langle A\rangle\cap B=A
, where
\langle A\rangle
is the subgroup of
\mathcal{F}
generated by
A
.

Proof.

If there is a subgroup
H
for which
A=R_{B}(H)=H\cap B\subseteq H
, then in particular
\langle A\rangle\subseteq H
. Because
A
is always contained in
\langle A\rangle\cap B
, we can conclude.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
A\subseteq B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. If
A
is
not
a pseudo subgroup of
B
, and
A^{\prime}\subseteq C
restricts to
A
(i.e.,
A^{\prime}\cap B=A
), then
A^{\prime}
is
not
a pseudo subgroup of
C
.

Proof.

By Claim
2.8
, if
A
is not a pseudo subgroup of
B
, then there is a
w\in B
such that
w\notin A
while
w\in\langle A\rangle
. Since
w\in B\subseteq C
, and
A^{\prime}\cap B=A
, the same
w
satisfies that
w\notin A^{\prime}
while
w\in\langle A\rangle\subseteq\langle A^{\prime}\rangle
, proving that
A^{\prime}
is not a pseudo subgroup of

.
∎

Definition 2.10
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a subset.

(1)

The set
\mathcal{P}_{B}
is the collection of all
\pi\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{B})
that are
concentrated on
pseudo subgroups of
B
, namely
\pi(P\mathfrak{sub}(B))=1
. Equivalently,
\pi(\{0,1\}^{B}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(B))=0
.

(2)

The set
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
is the subset of
\mathcal{P}_{B}
of distributions that are
S
-invariant when defined
. Namely, assuming
K,L\subseteq B
are finite subsets such that

\forall s\in S\cup S^{-1}\ \colon\ \ sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}\subseteq B,

we have
\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}))
. We call probability distributions in
Q_{B}

pseudo invariant random subgroups
over
B
, or pseudo IRSs in short.

(3)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B}=R_{B*}^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_{B})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}=R_{B*}^{-1}(\mathcal{Q}_{B})
.

Remark 2.11
.

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{F}}={\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{F}}=\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathcal{F}}={\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be subsets.

(1)

The restriction to
B
of a distribution over pseudo subgroups of
C
, is a distribution over pseudo subgroups of
B
. Namely,

\forall\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{C}\ \colon\ \ R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{B}.

Similarly, the restriction to
B
of a pseudo IRS of
C
is a pseudo IRS of
B
. Namely,

\forall\pi\in\mathcal{Q}_{C}\ \colon\ \ R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}.

(2)

The (Borel) probability distributions over subgroups of
\mathcal{F}
are the intersection of pull-backs of all distributions over pseudo subgroups in finite subsets. Similarly, the IRSs of
\mathcal{F}
are the intersection of pull-backs of all pseudo IRSs of finite subsets. Namely,

\bigcap_{B\subseteq\mathcal{F},|B|<\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B}={\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F})),\ \bigcap_{B\subseteq\mathcal{F},|B|<\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}={\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F}).

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{C}
. By definition,

\pi(\{0,1\}^{C}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(C))=0.

By Corollary
2.9
,

R_{B\subseteq C}^{-1}(\{0,1\}^{B}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(B))\subseteq\{0,1\}^{C}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(C).

Hence, by the definition of the pushforward,

\begin{split}R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi(\{0,1\}^{B}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(B))&=\pi(R_{B\subseteq C}^{-1}(\{0,1\}^{B}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(B)))\\
&\leq\pi(\{0,1\}^{C}\setminus P\mathfrak{sub}(C))\\
&=0,\end{split}

and so
R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{B}
.

Assume further that
\pi\in\mathcal{Q}_{C}
. By Claim
2.5
, for every finite
K,L\subseteq B
,

R^{-1}_{B\subseteq C}(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))=\mathcal{C}_{C}(K,L).

Thus

\begin{split}R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))&=\pi(R_{B\subseteq C}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L)))=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{C}(K,L)).\end{split}

If
K,L\subseteq B
such that
sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}\subseteq B
for every
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
, then the same applies to
C
since it contains
B
, and hence

\begin{split}R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))&=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{C}(K,L))\\
&=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{C}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}))\\
&=R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1})).\end{split}

Therefore
R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi
is in
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
and
(1)
is proved.

By clause
(1)
, for every
B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{C}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{C}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
. In particular, when
C=\mathcal{F}
we have
{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B}
and
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
. Hence

{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))\subseteq\bigcap_{B\subseteq\mathcal{F},|B|<\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B},\quad{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\bigcap_{B\subseteq\mathcal{F},|B|<\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}.

We are left to prove the reverse containments. Recall the open cover of the complement of the subgroups described in the proof of Claim
2.1
.
Given
\pi\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
such that
\pi\notin{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))
, we have

\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}C_{w,w^{\prime}}}}\right)\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{F}}C_{w}}}\right)\cup C_{\rm Id}}}\right)=\pi(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}}\setminus\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))>0.

Hence, by the union bound (Boole’s inequality), there is either a pair
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\pi(C_{w,w^{\prime}})>0
, or a single element
w\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\pi(C_{w})>0
, or
\pi(C_{\rm Id})>0
. Thus, if
B=\{{\rm Id}_{\mathcal{F}},w,w^{\prime},w\cdot w^{\prime},w^{-1}\}
, then
\pi\notin\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B}
. In particular
\pi\notin\bigcap\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{B}
, proving the first reverse containment.

Lastly, let
\pi\in\bigcap\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
. For every finite
K,L\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, there is a larger enough finite set
B
such that
K,L\subseteq B
and also
sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}\subseteq B
for every
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
. Since
\pi\in\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
, by Claim
2.5
applied to
C=\mathcal{F}
, we have for every
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
that

\begin{split}\pi(\mathcal{C}(K,L))&=\pi(R_{B}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L)))\\
&=R_{B*}\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))\\
&=R_{B*}\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}))\\
&=\pi(R_{B}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{B}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1})))\\
&=\pi(\mathcal{C}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1})).\end{split}

Hence, by Fact
2.2
,
(2)
is deduced.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

The system of finite subsets of
\mathcal{F}
forms a directed system induced by inclusion. The pushforwards along restriction maps
R_{B\subseteq C*}
thus form an inverse system of maps between the compact sets
{\rm Prob}(B)
for
B\subseteq\mathcal{F},|B|<\infty
. In this language, Lemma
2.12
says that

{\rm Prob}(\mathfrak{sub}(\mathcal{F}))=\varprojlim_{B}\mathcal{P}_{B}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})=\varprojlim_{B}\mathcal{Q}_{B}.

2.3.
Computational aspects of pseudo invariant random subgroups

Claim 2.14
.

Given
A\subseteq B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
where
|B|<\infty
, there is an algorithm deciding whether
A
is a pseudo subgroup of
B
.

Proof.

Given a finite set of elements
A=\{w_{1},...,w_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, and another element
w\in\mathcal{F}
, there is an algorithm to decide whether
w\in\langle A\rangle
. Indeed, one can use the following:

(1)

Construct
{\rm Core}(A)
the core graph of
\langle A\rangle
via the method of Stallings’ foldings
[
65
,
51
]
.

(2)

Check whether the path that begins at the basepoint of
{\rm Core}(A)
and traverses
w
is closed.

(3)

If the path is closed, then
w\in\langle A\rangle
. Otherwise
w\notin\langle A\rangle
.

By Claim
2.8
,
A
is not a pseudo subgroup of
B
if and only if there exists a
w\in B
such that
w\notin A
but
w\in\langle A\rangle
. Hence, a sufficient and necessary condition for
A
to be a pseudo subgroup of
B
is to be accepted by the following algorithm:

(1)

Construct
{\rm Core}(A)
.

(2)

For every
w\in B
satisfying
w\notin A
,
reject
if
w
is a closed path in
{\rm Core}(A)
.

(3)

Accept
if
A
passed
(2)
for every
w\in B
.

∎

Remark 2.15
.

Recall that a free group is LERF (locally extended residually finite), i.e., for every finite set
A=\{w_{1},...,w_{k}\}
and every
w\in\mathcal{F}
such that
w\notin\langle A\rangle
, there exists a finite index subgroup
H
for which

A\subseteq H\ \textrm{and}\ w\notin H.

This was originally proved by M. Hall
[
43
]
, and can be proven using the method of Stallings’ foldings
[
65
,
51
]
. It follows that for every
A
a pseudo subgroup of a finite
B
, there exists a
finite index
subgroup
H
for which
R_{B}(H)=A
.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a finite set. Then, the set
\mathcal{P}_{B}
of distributions over pseudo subgroups of
B
and the set
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
of pseudo IRSs over
B
are computable polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{B}}
. Namely, they are defined by (computable) integral linear equations and inequalities.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{Q}_{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{B}\subseteq{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{B})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{B}}
. Namely, all these distributions are vectors which are indexed by subsets of
B
.
We describe the defining system of equations and inequalities forcing a vector
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{B}}
to belong to
\mathcal{P}_{B}
. First, it needs to be a probability measure, hence

\begin{split}\forall A\subseteq B\ \colon\ \ \pi(A)&\geq 0,\\
\sum_{A\subseteq B}\pi(A)&=1.\end{split}

Further, for every
A\subseteq B
, we can run the algorithm in Claim
2.14
to decide whether
A
is a pseudo subgroup, and if not to add the constraint
\pi(A)=0
. This is the defining system of
\mathcal{P}_{B}
. To get the defining system of
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
, we need to add the following:
For every
K,L\subseteq B
, we can check whether for all
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
, the sets
sKs^{-1}
and
sLs^{-1}
are also contained in
B
, and if so add the constraints

\forall s\in S\cup S^{-1}\ \colon\ \ \underbrace{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L)}\pi(A)}_{\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(K,L))}=\underbrace{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{C}_{B}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1})}\pi(A)}_{\pi(\mathcal{C}_{B}(sKs^{-1},sLs^{-1}))}.

∎

3.
Main Theorem I: Approximating the values of subgroup tests

Recall the definitions of challenges, tests and values from Section
1.1
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a test over the generating set
S
, with challenges
(K_{i};D_{i})_{i\in Q}
and
rational
distribution
\mu
over
Q
. Let
K=\bigcup_{i\in Q}K_{i}
, which is a finite subset of
\mathcal{F}(S)=\mathcal{F}
. We can extend the definition of
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\cdot)
in the following way: For every
B
satisfying
K\subseteq B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, and for every distribution
\pi\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{B})
, let

{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi)=\Ex_{A\sim\pi}\Ex_{i\sim\mu}[D_{i}(A\cap K_{i})].

The next claim is formalizing the following intuitive idea: Since
D_{i}
depends only on the restriction of
H
to
K_{i}
, then replacing
\pi
by its push-forward along any restriction
R_{B}
will not change its value against
\mathcal{T}
.

Claim 3.1
.

Under the extended definition of the value, for every
B
and
C
such that
K\subseteq B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, and for every
\pi\in{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{C})
, we have

{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi)={\rm val}(\mathcal{T},R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi).

Proof.

By definition, sampling
A\sim R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi
is the same as sampling
A^{\prime}\sim\pi
and outputting
A=A^{\prime}\cap B
.
Also,
K_{i}=B\cap K_{i}
since
K_{i}\subseteq K\subseteq B
. Hence,

\begin{split}{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi)&=\Ex_{A\sim R_{B\subseteq C*}\pi}\Ex_{i\sim\mu}[D_{i}(A\cap K_{i})]\\
&=\Ex_{A^{\prime}\sim\pi}\Ex_{i\sim\mu}[D_{i}((A^{\prime}\cap B)\cap K_{i})]\\
&=\Ex_{A^{\prime}\sim\pi}\Ex_{i\sim\mu}[D_{i}(A^{\prime}\cap K_{i})]\\
&={\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi).\end{split}

∎

Recall the first clause of Theorem
1.10
: There is a Turing machine
M_{1}
that takes as an input the encoding of a test
\mathcal{T}
, and outputs an infinite sequence of non-decreasing numbers
(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[0,1]
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha_{t}={\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10

(1)
.

Let
\sigma\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(X)
be a finite action. The value of
\Phi(\sigma)
against
\mathcal{T}
is

\begin{split}{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\Phi(\sigma))&=\Ex_{H\sim\Phi(\sigma)}\Ex_{i\sim\mu}D_{i}(H\cap K_{i})\\
&=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{i\in Q}\frac{\mu(i)}{|X|}\cdot D_{i}({\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)\cap K_{i})\end{split}

which is a rational combination of evaluations of
D_{i}
. Since the sets
K_{i}
, functions
D_{i}
and coefficients
\mu(i)
can be calculated in finite time from the encoding of
\mathcal{T}
, and
{\rm Stab}(\sigma,x)\cap K_{i}
can be calculated from
\sigma
for every
x\in X
, the value of
\Phi(\sigma)
against
\mathcal{T}
can be computed in finite time.

There is a computable countable enumeration of all (equivalence classes of) finite
\mathcal{F}(S)
-actions. To see that, note that a map
\sigma\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
is defined by the tuple
(n\ ;\ \{\sigma(s)\}_{s\in S})
. Since the elements of
{\rm Sym}(n)
can be ordered (e.g., lexicographically), as well as the elements of
S
, the infinite set of tuples
(n\ ;\ \{\sigma(s)\}_{s\in S})
inherits a countable enumeration. Denote by
\sigma_{t}
the
t^{\rm th}
action according to this enumeration.
For
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\alpha_{t}=\max_{s\leq t}\{{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\Phi(\sigma_{s}))\}.

By our discussion so far, the number
\alpha_{t}
can be computed in finite time. Moreover,
(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
is a non-decreasing sequence by definition.
Since
{\rm Im}(\Phi)={\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})
, the sequence
(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
tends to the supremum of
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\cdot)
over
{\rm IRS}_{{\rm fd}}(\mathcal{F})
, which is, by (
1.4
),
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T})
.
∎

We now recall the second clause of Theorem
1.10
: There is a Turing machine
M_{2}
that takes as an input the encoding of a test
\mathcal{T}
, and outputs an infinite sequence of non-increasing numbers
(\beta_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[0,1]
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta_{t}={\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10

(2)
.

Let
K=\bigcup K_{i}
as before, and let
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a finite subset containing
K
. Recall the definition of
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
the pseudo invariant random subgroups over
B
(Definition
2.10
), and its pre-image
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
. Recall also Claim
3.1
and the discussion before it, where we extended the notion of the value to probability distributions over subsets of
\mathcal{F}
that contain
K
. The
B
-approximation to the ergodic value of
\mathcal{T}
is defined to be

{\rm val}_{B}(\mathcal{T})=\sup\{{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi)\mid\pi\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}\}=\sup\{{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi)\mid\pi\in\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}\}.

Let
\{B_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of finite subsets of
\mathcal{F}
, all containing
K
, such that
B_{t}\subseteq B_{t+1}
and
\bigcup_{t=1}^{\infty}B_{t}=\mathcal{F}
.
We now claim the following:

(1)

{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})
is computable.

(2)

The sequence
{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})
is non-increasing.

(3)

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})={\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})
.

By Lemma
2.16
,
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
is defined by a computable integral system of equations and inequalities. Thus, maximizing a linear functional
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\cdot)
over it can be done using linear programming in finite time, proving
(1)
.

By Lemma
2.12
, whenever
B\subseteq C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have
R_{B\subseteq C*}(\mathcal{Q}_{C})\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{B}
. Hence
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B_{t+1}}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B_{t}}
, which implies
{\rm val}_{B_{t+1}}(\mathcal{T})\leq{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})
, proving
(2)
.

Moreover,
{\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})=\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B_{t}}
which implies
{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})\geq{\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T})
.
On the other hand, by Claim
2.4
the space
{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
is compact, and since
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
are closed sets they are also compact. Hence there is a distribution
\pi_{t}\in\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B_{t}}
satisfying
{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})={\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi_{t})
. By the compactness of
{\rm Prob}(\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{F}})
, there is a weak limit to some subsequence of
(\pi_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
, which we denote by
\pi_{\infty}
. Without loss of generality the subsequence is the original sequence. Since
\pi_{s}\in\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B_{t}}
for every
s\geq t
, and since
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}
are closed, we deduce that
\pi_{\infty}\in\bigcap\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{B_{t}}={\rm IRS}(\mathcal{F})
. By the definition of a weak limit, since
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\cdot)
is a continuous functional, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi_{t})={\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\lim_{w^{*}}\pi_{t})={\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\pi_{\infty})\leq{\rm val}_{{\rm erg}}(\mathcal{T}),

proving
(3)
. Therefore, choosing
\beta_{t}={\rm val}_{B_{t}}(\mathcal{T})
finishes the proof.
∎

4.1.
Relations verification test

Given a finite subset

, the
R
-
verification test

\mathcal{V}_{R}
checks whether

is contained in the sampled subgroup

. It contains a single challenge

with

The sofic value of
\mathcal{V}_{R}
is always
1
, e.g., using the Dirac measure concentrated on the group
\mathcal{F}
itself (which associates via

4.3.
Formula satisfaction test

Let
\varphi(y_{1},...,y_{n})
be a boolean formula. The test
\mathcal{T}_{\varphi}
is defined over the formal set of generators
S=\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
. It has a single challenge
(K;D)
, where
K=S
. Note that the characteristic function of every subset

is already in the form
{\bf 1}_{A}\colon\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}\to\{0,1\}
, and thus induces an assignment for the formal variables. The decision predicate

accepts

if

induces a satisfying assignment for
\varphi

D(A)=\varphi({\bf 1}_{A}(x_{1}),...,{\bf 1}_{A}(x_{n})).

It is straightforward that
{\rm val}(\mathcal{T}_{\varphi})=1
if and only if
\varphi
is satisfiable.

There is a more interesting version of this test, when the formula
\varphi
is a 3CNF. Recall that a 3CNF is an and of clauses of the form
\varphi_{t}=y_{i}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\lor y_{j}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\lor y_{k}^{\varepsilon_{3}}
, where
y_{i},y_{j},y_{k}
are taken from a fixed set of formal generators (as before),
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}\in\{0,1\}
, and we interpret
y^{0}=y
and
y^{1}=\lnot y
. Now, checking that
\varphi
is satisfied by
{\bf 1}_{A}
is the same as going over all the clauses and checking that they are all satisfied. Thus, we can define a randomized version of this check by associating a challenge
(K_{t};D_{t})
with each clause
\varphi_{t}
, by letting
K_{t}=\{x_{i},x_{j},x_{k}\}
and
D_{t}(A)=\varphi_{t}({\bf 1}_{A}(x_{i}),{\bf 1}_{A}(x_{j}),{\bf 1}_{A}(x_{k}))
. Then, by choosing a uniform distribution over challenges, the associated test
\mathcal{T}_{\varphi}
checks whether a uniformly chosen clause is satisfied by

. The famous PCP theorem
[
9
]
says that any 3CNF

can be (efficiently) transformed into a not much larger 3CNF

, such that if
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T}_{\varphi})=1
, then
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T}_{\varphi^{\prime}})=1
, and if
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T}_{\varphi})=0
, then
{\rm val}_{{\rm sof}}(\mathcal{T}_{\varphi^{\prime}})\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}.

The scaled up version of this argument shows that any
\mathsf{NEXP}
problem (see
[
11
]
) can be reduced to approximating the sofic value of a test.

\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\bf 1}_{{\rm Stab}(\rho,y)}(w)\neq{\bf 1}_{{\rm Stab}(\varphi,y)}(w)}}\right]\leq\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho(w).y\neq\varphi(w).y}}\right].

Inequality (
5.4
) is a consequence of the following implication: If
\rho(w)
acts on
y
the same way as
\varphi(w)
, then in particular
w
is either in both
{\rm Stab}(\rho,y)
and
{\rm Stab}(\varphi,y)
or in none of them. For
1\leq k\leq\ell
, let
w_{k}
be the suffix of
w
from position
k
, namely
w_{k}=\prod_{j=k}^{\ell}s_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}}
. Moreover, let
w_{\ell+1}
be the empty word
{\rm Id}_{\mathcal{F}}
. Then

(5.5)

\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho(w).y\neq\varphi(w).y}}\right]\leq\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigvee_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\neq\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y}}\right].

Inequality (
5.5
) is a consequence of the following implication: If the endpoint of the path beginning at
y
and labeled by
w^{-1}
in
{\rm Sch}(\rho,S)
is different than the endpoint of the same path in
{\rm Sch}(\varphi,S)
, then the two paths diverged at
some
point. Now,

(5.6)

\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigvee_{1\leq k\leq\ell}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\neq\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y}}\right]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\neq\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\land\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k+1}}}\right).y=\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k+1}}}\right).y}}\right].

Inequality (
5.6
) is a consequence of the union bound and the logic tautology
\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}\varphi_{j}=\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}(\varphi_{j}\land\lnot\varphi_{j-1})
, where
\varphi_{0}={\rm False}
. But, since

\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\neq\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\land\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k+1}}}\right).y=\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k+1}}}\right).y\implies\rho(s_{k}^{\varepsilon_{k}})\circ\rho(w_{k+1}).y\neq\varphi(s_{k}^{\varepsilon_{k}})\circ\rho(w_{k+1}).y,

and since
\rho(w_{k+1}).y
is uniformly distributed given that
y
is uniformly distributed, we can deduce that

(5.7)

\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\neq\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k}}}\right).y\land\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k+1}}}\right).y=\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w_{k+1}}}\right).y}}\right]\leq\underbrace{\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho(s_{k}).y\neq\varphi(s_{k}).y}}\right]}_{=d_{H}(\rho(s_{k}),\varphi(s_{k}))}.

All in all,

(5.8)

\Pro_{y\in Y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\bf 1}_{{\rm Stab}(\rho,y)}(w)\neq{\bf 1}_{{\rm Stab}(\varphi,y)}(w)}}\right]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}d_{H}(\rho(s_{j}),\varphi(s_{j}))=\sum_{s\in S}v_{s}(w)d_{H}(\rho(s),\varphi(s))

Hence, by the union bound and inequality (
5.8
), we have

(5.9)

\begin{split}\Pro_{i\sim\mu}\Pro_{y\in Y}[{{\rm Stab}(\rho,y)}\cap{K_{i}}\neq{{\rm Stab}(\varphi,y)}\cap{K_{i}}]&\leq\Ex_{i\sim\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{w\in K_{i}}\sum_{s\in S}v_{s}(w)d_{H}(\rho(s),\varphi(s))}}\right]\\
&=\sum_{s\in S}\underbrace{\Ex_{i\sim\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{w\in K_{i}}v_{s}(w)}}\right]}_{\eqref{equation:weight_function_of_a_test}}d_{H}(\rho(s),\varphi(s))\\
&=\sum_{s\in S}\mathfrak{s}_{\mathcal{T}}(s)d_{H}(\rho(s),\varphi(s))\\
&=d^{\mathfrak{s}_{\mathcal{T}}}(\rho,\varphi)\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

Lastly, by combining

\begin{split}|{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\rho)-{\rm val}(\mathcal{T},\varphi)|&\leq\Pro_{i\sim\mu}\Pro_{y\in Y}[D_{i}({\rm Stab}(\rho,y)\cap{K_{i}})\neq D_{i}({\rm Stab}(\varphi,y)\cap{K_{i}})]\\
&\leq\Pro_{i\sim\mu}\Pro_{y\in Y}[{\rm Stab}(\rho,y)\cap{K_{i}}\neq{\rm Stab}(\varphi,y)\cap{K_{i}}]\end{split}

with
(
5.9
), we deduce the claim.
∎

Non-local games preliminaries

We repeat the definitions which appeared in Section

Definition 6.1
.

A (synchronous)
non-local game

\mathcal{G}
consists of a finite graph
G=(V,E)
, a length function
\ell\colon V\to\mathbb{N}
, formal sets of generators
S_{x}=\{\mathsf{X}^{x,i}\mid 1\leq i\leq\ell(x)\}
for every vertex
x\in V
, a distribution
\mu
over the edges
E
, and decision functions
D_{xy}\colon\{0,1\}^{S_{xy}}\to\{0,1\}
for every edge
xy\in E
, where
S_{xy}=S_{x}\cup S_{y}
. We denote by
S
the set
\bigcup_{x\in V}S_{x}
.

Remark 6.2
.

Anyone familiar with the definition of a non-local game will immediately notice that the standard formalism is different than the one we chose here. Usually, a (synchronous) non-local game is defined as a pair of finite sets
X,A
, commonly referred to as the question and answer sets respectively, together with a distribution
\mu
over pairs of questions
X\times X
and a decision predicate
D\colon X\times X\times A\times A\to\{0,1\}
satisfying
D(x,x,a,b)=0
for any
x\in X
and
a\neq b\in A
.

It is quite straightforward to move between the definitions. A way one can extract the data of Definition
6.1
from the above is as follows: Let
\ell
be the constant function
\Lambda=\lceil\log|A|\rceil
, and fix an embedding of
A
into
\{0,1\}^{\Lambda}
. The vertices
V
of the underlying graph
G
will be
X
, and the support of
\mu
will be the edge set
E\subseteq X\times X
. The formal generator
\mathsf{X}^{x,i}
corresponds to the
i^{\rm th}
bit of the answer
a
when
x\in X
is asked as a question.
Then, given that
x,y
were asked, a pair of answers
a,b
can be encoded as a map
\gamma\colon S_{x}\cup S_{y}\to\{0,1\}
, where

(6.1)

\gamma|_{S_{x}}=a\quad{\rm and}\quad\gamma|_{S_{y}}=b\;.

The notation (
6.1
) will be used repeatedly in the text.
Lastly,
D_{xy}(\gamma)=D(x,y,a,b)
, where
\gamma
is the aforementioned encoding.

In the other direction, we can define
A
to be all bit strings shorter than
\Lambda=\max_{x\in V}\ell(x)
,
X
to be the vertex set
V
, and
\mu
stays the same. Finally,
D(x,y,a,b)=D_{xy}(\gamma)
when
a,b
are of the correct lengths, i.e.
a=\ell(x),b=\ell(y)
, and is
0
when either of them is of the incorrect length. Since we consider only synchronous strategies, the condition
D(x,x,a,b)
is never checked in practice for
a\neq b
and we can assume it was satisfied beforehand.
Because of this correspondence, we may refer to
\ell
as the
answer length
function.

Non-local games are often dramatized as two-prover interactive proofs with one round
[
17
]
. This perspective may help some readers to absorb the upcoming technicalities better.
Two players, that can share some resources beforehand (random bits in the classical case and entangled qubits in the quantum case), are separated spatially — e.g., they are seated in far away rooms.
A referee samples a pair of questions and sends one to each player.
The players then use their shared resources to come up with answers, and send them back to the referee.
The referee then decides, using the decision predicate, whether the players won or lost.
The decision predicate as well as the distribution over possible questions are assumed to be known to the players beforehand.
We demonstrate this dramatization in Example
6.26
.

The following is the quantum mechanics’ analogue of a probability distribution. Similar to the way probability distributions (over finite sets) are collections of non-negative real numbers that add up to
1
, the quantum analogue would be “non-negative” matrices that add up to the identity.

Definition 6.3
.

A
positive operator valued measure
(POVM) of dimension
n
with outcomes in a set
A
is a mapping
\mathsf{P}\colon A\to M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\mathsf{P}_{a}
is a positive (semi-definite) matrix for every
a\in A
and
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{P}_{a}={\rm Id}_{n}
. It is called
projective
, or a
projection valued measure
(PVM), if in addition
\mathsf{P}_{a}
is an orthogonal projection for every
a\in A
, namely
(\mathsf{P}_{a})^{2}=\mathsf{P}_{a}=(\mathsf{P}_{a})^{*}
, where
*
is the conjugate transpose operation.
Given that
\mathsf{P}
is a PVM and
A=\{0,1\}^{\Lambda}
, we define for
1\leq i\leq\Lambda
the
i^{\rm th}
marginal of
\mathsf{P}
to be
\mathsf{P}^{i}\colon\{0,1\}\to M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
by

(6.2)

\mathsf{P}^{i}_{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\colon\Lambda\to\{0,1\}\\
a_{i}=0\end{subarray}}\mathsf{P}_{a}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathsf{P}^{i}_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\colon\Lambda\to\{0,1\}\\
a_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\mathsf{P}_{a}.

Thus,
\mathsf{P}_{a}=\prod_{i=1}^{\Lambda}\mathsf{P}^{i}_{a_{i}}
. Furthermore, the matrix
\mathsf{U}^{i}=\mathsf{P}^{i}_{0}-\mathsf{P}^{i}_{1}
is an order
2
unitary, which we call the
i^{\rm th}
binary observable. All in all, a PVM can be given either as a map
\mathsf{P}\colon\{0,1\}^{\Lambda}\to M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{N})
, or as a map
\mathsf{U}\colon[\Lambda]\to U(n)
such that the images of
\mathsf{U}
are commuting involutions — i.e., they square to the identity.

As its name suggests, every POVM
\mathsf{P}
defines a probability distribution over its answer set
A
as follows

(6.3)

\Pro[a\ {\rm is\ sampled}]=\tau(\mathsf{P}_{a}),

where
\tau
is the dimension normalized trace on
n\times n
matrices. Such an answer is said to be
sampled according
to
\mathsf{P}
and we denote it by
a\sim\mathsf{P}
.

Remark 6.4
.

In the case of a PVM with outcome set
A=\{0,1\}^{\Lambda}
, the observables
\{\mathsf{U}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{\Lambda}
generate a unitary representation of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\Lambda}
. The
\mathsf{P}_{a}
’s in this case can be read from the unitary representation using the Fourier transform of the representation. Hence, for the case of
A=\{0,1\}^{\Lambda}
, a PVM can be given as a collection of mutually perpendicular ortohgonal projections that sum up to the identity, or as a unitary representation of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\Lambda}
, and one can move from one perspective to the other without losing information.

Definition 6.5
.

A (synchronous quantum)
n
-dimensional
strategy
for
\mathcal{G}
is a map
\rho\colon S\to U(n)
, where the images are involutions, and
\rho({S_{x}})
commutes for every fixed
x\in V
— by saying that a set of matrices (or later permutations) commutes, we mean that every pair of elements in this set commute.
Another way of viewing strategies is by saying that they associate with every vertex
x\in V
an
n
-dimensional PVM (in observable form).
The strategy
\rho
is said to be
commuting along edges
if
\rho(S_{xy})
commutes for every edge
xy\in E
.

Such a strategy defines for every edge
xy\in E
a probability distribution over functions
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

(6.4)

\Pro[\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}\ {\rm is\ sampled}]=\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S_{x}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}\prod_{\mathsf{Y}\in S_{y}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{Y})}}}\right),

where
\tau
is again the dimension normalized trace on
n\times n
matrices,
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the projection on the
(+1)
-eigenspace of
\rho(\mathsf{X})
, and
\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{1}\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the projection on its
(-1)
-eigenspace.
18
18

18

Note that
\rho(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}-\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{1}
, which exactly shows how to move from this observable form of the PVMs induced by
\rho
back to its projection based form.
We say that
\gamma
was
sampled according to

\rho
if it has this distribution, and we denote it by
\gamma\sim\rho
.

For
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
, let
\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}=\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S_{x}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}.
Then, the map
\mathsf{P}^{xy}\colon\{0,1\}^{S_{xy}}\to M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
defined by
\mathsf{P}^{xy}_{\gamma}=\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}
is a POVM. Thus,

is a special case of sampling according to a POVM (

), which in this case is
\mathsf{P}^{xy}
. In the spirit of the notation in (

and
\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}=\mathsf{P}^{y}_{b}
.

Remark 6.6
.

The images of
\mathsf{X}
according to

, and the value

is usually called the

Claim 6.7
.

There is a procedural (i.e., algorithmic) way of sampling
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
according to
\rho
:
Since
\rho(S_{x})\subseteq U(n)
commutes, these matrices have a mutual orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
B_{x}
. Similarly,
\rho(S_{y})
have a mutual orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
B_{y}
.
First, sample
\vec{v}\in B_{x}
uniformly at random.
Then, sample
\vec{u}\in B_{y}
according to the squared length of its projection on
v
, namely

\Pro[\vec{u}\in B_{y}\ {\rm is\ sampled}\mid\vec{v}\in B_{x}\ {\rm was\ sampled}]=|\langle\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2},

where
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
is the standard inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e.,
\langle\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{v}_{i}\cdot u_{i}
.
Finally,
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
is determined as follows — for
\mathsf{X}\in S_{x}
,
\gamma(\mathsf{X})=\begin{cases}0&\rho(\mathsf{X})\vec{v}=\vec{v},\\
1&\rho(\mathsf{X})\vec{v}=-\vec{v},\end{cases}
, and for
\mathsf{Y}\in S_{y}
,
\gamma(\mathsf{Y})=\begin{cases}0&\rho(\mathsf{Y})\vec{u}=\vec{u},\\
1&\rho(\mathsf{Y})\vec{u}=-\vec{u}.\end{cases}
.
All in all,

\Pro[(\vec{v},\vec{u})\ {\rm is\ sampled}]=\nicefrac{{|\langle\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}}}{{n}},

which is independent of the ordering between
x
and
y
. Note also that the probability a specific
\gamma
is measured is independent of the chosen bases
B_{x}
and
B_{y}
.

Proof.

The function
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is sampled in the above procedure if for the resulting pair
(\vec{v},\vec{u})
we have
\vec{v}\in{\rm Im}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S_{x}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}}}\right)
and
\vec{u}\in{\rm Im}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{Y}\in S_{y}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{Y}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{Y})}}}\right)
. Denote by
\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}
the product
\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S_{x}}\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}
, and similarly
\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}
. So,

\Pro[\gamma\ {\rm is\ sampled}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v}\in{\rm Im}(\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma})\\
\vec{u}\in{\rm Im}(\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma})\end{subarray}}\frac{|\langle\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}}{n}.

By the fact that
B_{x}
is a mutual orthonormal basis of eigenvectors for
\rho(S_{X})
, every
\vec{v}\in B_{x}
is either in the image of
\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}
or in its kernel. Therefore, the above sum can be written as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v}\in{\rm Im}(\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma})\\
\vec{u}\in{\rm Im}(\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma})\end{subarray}}\frac{|\langle\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}}{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v}\in B_{x}\\
\vec{u}\in B_{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\langle\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}}{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v}\in B_{x}\\
\vec{u}\in B_{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\langle\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}}{n}.

Since
B_{y}
is an orthonormal basis and
\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma},\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}
are orthogonal projections, for every
\vec{v}\in B_{x}
we have

\sum_{\vec{u}\in B_{y}}|\langle\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}=\|\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}\|^{2}=\langle\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}\rangle=\langle\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\vec{v}\rangle.

Thus,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v}\in B_{x}\\
\vec{u}\in B_{y}\end{subarray}}\frac{|\langle\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\vec{u}\rangle|^{2}}{n}=\sum_{\vec{v}\in B_{x}}\frac{\langle\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\vec{v}|\vec{v}\rangle}{n}=\tau(\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma})=\tau(\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}),

where the last equality uses the cyclicity of the trace and the fact
\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}
is a projection. All in all,

\Pro[\gamma\ {\rm is\ sampled}]=\tau(\mathsf{P}^{x}_{\gamma}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{\gamma}),

as needed.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

The formula (
6.4
) for the probability a specific
\gamma
is sampled is motivated by the Born measurement rule from quantum mechanics. In quantum mechanics, a (projective) measurement is specified by choosing a PVM
\mathsf{P}\colon A\to B(\mathcal{H})
acting on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
(Definition
6.3
provided the finite dimensional case).
Given a quantum state (unit vector)
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, a measurement of
\psi
returns the outcome
a
with probability

\Pro[a\ {\rm is\ the\ measurement\ outcome}]=\psi^{*}\mathsf{P}_{a}\psi.

If there are two systems (e.g. two “players”), then a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
is associated to each of them
respectively, such that the
joint
Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
The joint distribution on outcomes is then given by probabilities

(6.5)

\Pro[(a,b)\ {\rm are\ the\ measurement\ outcomes}]=\psi^{*}(\mathsf{P}_{a}^{x}\otimes(\mathsf{P}^{y}_{b})^{\dagger})\psi,

where
\dagger
is transposition with
no
complex conjugation, as opposed to
*
.
19
19

19

It is common to drop the transposition
\dagger
on the
\mathcal{H}_{B}
POVM in (
6.5
). But, in this case, a transposition will appear in the calculation using traces (
6.6
). So, we decided on this equivalent formulation.

The case where
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
have the same (finite) dimension
n

\psi=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i}e_{x,i}\otimes e_{y,i}
for orthonormal bases
\{e_{x,i}\}
and
\{e_{y,i}\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
respectively, termed “maximally entangled,” yields

(6.6)

\Pro[(a,b)\ {\rm are\ the\ measurement\ outcomes}]=\tau(\mathsf{P}^{x}_{a}\mathsf{P}^{y}_{b}).

Definition 6.9
.

The (synchronous)

\mathcal{G}
,

, is the supremum over all possible strategies against

.

The main result in
[
47

is a reduction from the Halting Problem to approximating the quantum value of a game:

Theorem 6.10

(
\mathsf{MIP}^{*}=\mathsf{RE}
)
.

There exists a polynomial time algorithm that takes as input (the encoding of) a Turing machine

and outputs (the encoding of) a non-local game

such that:

(1)

according to

from the encoding of the game

can be done in time

, where

is the bit-length of the encoding of

(2)

If

(3)

If

The goal of the next section is to show that a somewhat stronger variation of Theorem

6.2.
Permutation strategies

Our goal in Section
7
is to relate values of subgroup tests to values of non-local games and vice versa. The strategies for subgroup tests are finitely described IRSs, which are (induced by) maps of the form
\sigma\colon S\to{\rm Sym}(n)
, while quantum strategies for non-local games are maps of the form
\rho\colon S\to U(n)
adhering to certain conditions. These already seem quite related, as embedding
{\rm Sym}(n)
into
U(n)
as permutation matrices is natural, and indeed transforms every (nice enough) finitely described IRS into a quantum strategy. But, there is a problem with doing this naively:
Say that we want to construct a quantum strategy, such that for a certain
\mathsf{X}\in S
, regardless of anything else,
\gamma(\mathsf{X})
will always be
1
. Then, we can achieve it by choosing
\rho(\mathsf{X})=-{\rm Id}
. But,
-{\rm Id}
is not a permutation matrix. Therefore, any
\sigma
that will be naively transformed into a quantum strategy by embedding
{\rm Sym}(n)
into
U(n)
as permutation matrices cannot ensure a specific
\mathsf{X}
is always evaluated to
1
.
To amend that, we use a tool developed in the study of solution groups of linear constraint system games (cf.
[
64
]
):
We add a special generator
\mathsf{J}
that plays the role of
-{\rm Id}
.
Since
-{\rm Id}
is a central involution without fixed points, the image of
\mathsf{J}
under
\sigma
needs to behave the same.
This forces
\sigma
to act on an even sized set.
Now, if we want
\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\sigma(\mathsf{J})
to translate into
\gamma(\mathsf{X})
being always
1
, we will need to restrict ourselves to the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\sigma(\mathsf{J})
. This leads us to the following two definitions.

Definition 6.11
.

A
permutation strategy
for a non-local game
\mathcal{G}
is a map

\sigma\colon S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\}\to{\rm Sym}(2n)

with the following properties:

(1)

The image of
\mathsf{J}
is a central involution with no fixed points
. Namely,

\begin{split}&\sigma(\mathsf{J})^{2}={\rm Id},\ d_{H}(\sigma(\mathsf{J}),{\rm Id})=1,\\
&\forall\mathsf{X}\in S\ \colon\ \ \sigma(\mathsf{J})\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\sigma(\mathsf{X})\sigma(\mathsf{J}).\end{split}

(2)

The rest of the images are involutions as well
. Namely,

\forall\mathsf{X}\in S\ \colon\ \ \sigma(\mathsf{X})^{2}={\rm Id}.

(3)

The image of
S_{x}
commutes
. Namely, for every fixed vertex
x\in V
,

\forall\mathsf{X},\mathsf{X}^{\prime}\in S_{x}\ \colon\ \ \sigma(\mathsf{X})\sigma(\mathsf{X}^{\prime})=\sigma(\mathsf{X}^{\prime})\sigma(\mathsf{X}).

Remark 6.12
.

Let
\sigma\colon S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\}\to{\rm Sym}(2n)
be a permutation strategy for
\mathcal{G}
. Embed
{\rm Sym}(2n)
in

as permutation matrices. Note that in this viewpoint,

is acting naturally on functions

, and the matrices are represented with respect to the standard basis, which consists of the indicators

\forall\star\in[2n]\ \colon\ \ {\bf 1}_{\star}(\diamond)=\begin{cases}1&\diamond=\star,\\
0&\diamond\neq\star.\end{cases}

Moreover, for a permutation
\zeta\in{\rm Sym}(2n)
and function

(6.7)

\forall\star\in[2n]\ \colon\ \ (\zeta.f)(\star)=f(\zeta^{-1}.\star).

By
(1)
of Definition
6.11
,
\sigma(\mathsf{J})
is a fixed point free involution, and thus its
(+1)
-eigenspace
W^{+}
and
(-1)
-eigenspace

are of the same dimension
n
. Note that
W^{+}
consists of all functions

that are constant along
\mathsf{J}
-labeled edges in the Schreier graph

{\rm Sch}(\sigma,S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\})

If we denote

\star^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathsf{J}).\star

Remark 6.13
.

Given that
\rho\colon S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\}\to{\rm Sym}(2n)
is a permutation strategy, there are
n
orbits of

acts on them in a well-defined manner. If we arbitrarily denote each orbit as

Definition 6.14
.

Let
\sigma\colon S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\}\to{\rm Sym}(2n)
be a permutation strategy for
\mathcal{G}
. Recall from Remark

that
W^{-}

\sigma(\mathsf{J})
, and that
\mathsf{Q}\colon\mathbb{C}^{2n}\to W^{-}

Claim 6.15
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation strategy. Then, the quantum strategy
\rho
induced by
\sigma
, as in Definition

, is
indeed
a quantum strategy for
\mathcal{G}
, as in Definition
6.5
.

Proof.

The criterion for being a quantum strategy as in Definition
6.5
is to be a map into
unitaries
such that the image of the generators

associated with any specific vertex
commute
, and all the images are

.
First,

(6.9)

\rho(\mathsf{X})^{*}=(\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*})^{*}=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})^{*}\mathsf{Q}^{*}=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{*}.

Now, the fact that
\sigma(\mathsf{J})
commutes with
\sigma(\mathsf{X})
for any

implies that
\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\rho(\mathsf{X})^{*}\rho(\mathsf{X})&=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\\
&=\mathsf{Q}\underbrace{\sigma(\mathsf{X})^{-1}\sigma(\mathsf{X})}_{{\rm Id}_{2n}}\mathsf{Q}^{*}\underbrace{\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{*}}_{{\rm Id}_{W^{-}}}\\
&=\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{*}={\rm Id}_{W^{-}},\end{split}

and the image of

(2)
of Definition

for all

) gives

\rho(\mathsf{X})^{*}=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{*}=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*}=\rho(\mathsf{X}),

i.e., the images of
\rho
are involutions. Finally, since
\sigma(S_{x})
commutes for every fixed

, we have

\begin{split}\forall\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}\in S_{x}\ \colon\ \ \rho(\mathsf{X})\rho(\mathsf{Y})&=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{Y})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\\
&=\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})\sigma(\mathsf{Y})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\\
&=\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{Y})\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\\
&=\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{Y})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\mathsf{Q}\sigma(\mathsf{X})\mathsf{Q}^{*}\\
&=\rho(\mathsf{Y})\rho(\mathsf{X}),\end{split}

which finishes the proof.
∎

Example 6.16

(Classical permutation strategies)
.

Let
{\rm Sym}(2)=\{{\rm Id},\zeta\}
be the group of permutations acting on two elements. For every fixed
f\colon S\to\{0,1\}
, we can define an action
\sigma\colon S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\}\to{\rm Sym}(2)
as follows

\sigma(\mathsf{J})=\zeta\quad;\quad\sigma(\mathsf{X})=\begin{cases}{\rm Id}&f(\mathsf{X})=0,\\
\zeta&f(\mathsf{X})=1.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to see that the quantum strategy associated with
\sigma
satisfies
\rho(\mathsf{X})=(-1)^{f(\mathsf{X})}
, and thus
\gamma\colon S_{xy}\to\{0,1\}
that is sampled according to
\rho
is deterministically
f|_{S_{xy}}
.
Strategies of this form are usually called
deterministic
. By taking direct sums of such actions (for potentially different
f
’s), we can get any (rational) distribution over deterministic strategies. These strategies are usually called
classical
. So, every (rational) classical strategy can be obtained as a permutation strategy.

Claim 6.17
.

Let
\sigma\colon S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\}\to{\rm Sym}(2n)
be a permutation strategy, and
\rho
the associated quantum strategy. Then, if for a word
w\in\mathcal{F}(S\cup\{\mathsf{J}\})
we have
\rho(w)={\rm Id}
, then
\sigma(w)={\rm Id}
.

Proof.

Recall from Remark
6.12
that the images of
\rho
act naturally, and in
the same way
as
\sigma
, on
W^{-}
, the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\sigma(\mathsf{J})
, which consist of functions
f\colon[2n]\to\mathbb{C}
that flip along
\mathsf{J}
-edges. Recall also the notation
\star^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathsf{J}).\star
from (
6.8
).
So, the fact that
\rho(w)={\rm Id}
means that for any function
f\in W^{-}
,
\sigma(w).f=f
as well.
This is true in particular for
B^{-}=\{{\bf 1}_{\star}-{\bf 1}_{\star^{\prime}}\}_{\star\in[2n]}
, the basis of
W^{-}
described in the same remark. Note that for every
\zeta\in{\rm Sym}(2n)
we have
\zeta.{\bf 1}_{\star}={\bf 1}_{\zeta.\star}
.
Hence, for every
\star\in[2n]
, we have

{\bf 1}_{\star}-{\bf 1}_{\star^{\prime}}=\sigma(w).({\bf 1}_{\star}-{\bf 1}_{\star^{\prime}})={\bf 1}_{\sigma(w).\star}-{\bf 1}_{\sigma(w).\star^{\prime}}.

By evaluating the functions on both sides of the above equation on the point
\star\in[2n]
, we get

1={\bf 1}_{\sigma(w).\star}(\star)-{\bf 1}_{\sigma(w).\star^{\prime}}(\star),

which means
\sigma(w).\star=\star
. Since
\star
was any point in
[2n]

\sigma(w)={\rm Id}
and we are done.
∎

Definition 6.19
.

Let
\sigma\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\to{\rm Sym}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k})
be the standard action of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
on itself, namely
\sigma(w).v=v+w

of

is the following collection of functions, denoted by
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{k}

, let

be

\forall w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\ \colon\ \ \widehat{u}(w)=\frac{(-1)^{\langle u,w\rangle}}{\sqrt{2^{k}}},

where
\langle u,w\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}w_{i}
is the standard bilinear form on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
.

21

21

We try to keep
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the bilinear form over a finite vector space, and
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
for the complex inner product.

Claim 6.20
.

The collection
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{k}
is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors for the action
\sigma
.

Proof.

The action
\sigma
acts as permutations on the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}}
, and thus extends to act on the whole vector space of functions from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
to
\mathbb{C}
. Using the standard inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}}
as before, we can check that

\forall u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\ \colon\ \ \langle\widehat{u}|\widehat{u}\rangle=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}}\overline{\widehat{u}(w)}\cdot\widehat{u}(w)=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}}\frac{1}{2^{k}}=1.

Furthermore,

\begin{split}\forall u\neq v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\ \colon\ \ \langle\widehat{v}|\widehat{u}\rangle&=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}}\overline{\widehat{v}(w)}\cdot\widehat{u}(w)\\
&=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}}(-1)^{\langle v+u,w\rangle}\\
&=0,\end{split}

where the last equality is derived from the fact

. Lastly,

\begin{split}\forall u,v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\ \colon\ \ (\sigma(w).\widehat{v})(u)&=\widehat{v}(u+w)\\
&=\frac{(-1)^{\langle u+w,v\rangle}}{\sqrt{2^{k}}}\\
&=(-1)^{\langle w,v\rangle}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{\langle u,v\rangle}}{\sqrt{2^{k}}}\\
&=(-1)^{\langle w,v\rangle}\widehat{v}(u),\end{split}

and
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{k}
is indeed a collection of mutual eigenvectors for
\sigma
.
∎

Recall basic
group actions
definitions.
Let
G
be a group, and
\sigma\colon G\to{\rm Sym}(X),\tau\colon G\to{\rm Sym}(Y)
be two homomorphisms, which induce actions of
G

and

Note that for every set

, such a map induces a morphism of oriented edge-labeled graphs between
{\rm Sch}(\sigma,S)
and
{\rm Sch}(\tau,S)
.
The actions
\sigma
and
\tau
are said to be

{\rm Sch}(\sigma,S)
and
{\rm Sch}(\tau,S)

Given two actions, we can define their

\sigma\oplus\tau\colon G\to{\rm Sym}(X\sqcup Y)

\forall w\in G,z\in X\sqcup Y\,\colon\quad\\
(\sigma\oplus\tau(w)).z=\begin{cases}\sigma(w).z&z\in X,\\

If
\sigma

\sigma(w).x

Assume now that

For every

\frac{|O_{x}\cap O_{y}|}{2n}

whose intersection of supports is

is

This shows that the above sampling procedure provides the same distribution as the one in Claim

Tailored games

As permutation strategies allow us to translate finitely described strategies of subgroup tests to quantum strategies of non-local games, the soon to be defined

Remark 6.23
.

Though being tailored seems to be quite a restrictive form for a non-local game, every game can be tailored in a trivial manner. First, all variables are declared readable, namely

and

Example 6.26

It then expects the row player to respond with an assignment to the variables appearing in this constraint, and from the column player to respond with an assignment to its single variable. The referee declares this to be a winning condition if the row player’s assignment satisfied the constraint, and both players agree on the value of the variable given to the column player.
The column player is thought to hold a global assignment and answer according to it, while the row player is assumed to have a satisfying assignment to each constraint — intuitively, by checking consistency between the players, the referee verifies that the global assignment of the column player indeed satisfies all the constraints.

\Pro_{\gamma\sim\rho}[\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle=0]=1\iff\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S}\rho(\mathsf{X})^{\alpha(\mathsf{X})}={\rm Id}.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that

As before, we can write

\rho(\mathsf{X})

-eigenspace. By Definition

\begin{split}\Pro_{\gamma\sim\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{\mathsf{X}\in S}\gamma(\mathsf{X})=0}}\right]&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\colon S\to\mathbb{F}_{2}\\
\sum\gamma(\mathsf{X})=0\end{subarray}}\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S}\mathsf{P}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}^{\mathsf{X}}}}\right).\end{split}

Note that

\displaystyle{\sum_{\gamma\colon S\to\mathbb{F}_{2}}}\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S}\mathsf{P}_{\gamma(\mathsf{X})}^{\mathsf{X}}}}\right)

Therefore,

\begin{split}\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S}\rho(\mathsf{X})}}\right)&=\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\mathsf{X}\in S}(\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{0}-\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{X}}_{1})}}\right)\\

Thus,

\begin{split}1-{\rm val}(\mathcal{G},\rho)\overset{\eqref{eq:tg-1}}{=}\Ex_{xy\sim\mu}\Ex_{\star\in[2n]}[\delta_{xy}(\star)]\leq 2\cdot 2^{2\Lambda}\Ex_{xy\sim\mu}\Ex_{\star\in[2n]}[\varepsilon_{xy}(\star)]\overset{\eqref{eq:tg-2}}{=}2\cdot 2^{2\Lambda}(1-{\rm val}(\tilde{\mathcal{T}},\sigma)),\end{split}

Claim 8.8

(Almost involutions are close to involutions)
.

Let
\zeta\in{\rm Sym}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
. Then,
there is
\tau\in{\rm Sym}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
such that
\tau^{2}={\rm Id}
and

d_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\zeta,\tau}}\right)=d_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\zeta^{2},{\rm Id}}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
W=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\in X\mid\zeta^{2}.x=x}}\right\}
.
Note that the restriction
\zeta|_{W}
is an involution
W\rightarrow W
.
Define

\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\begin{cases}\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)&x\in W,\\
x&x\notin W.\end{cases}

Then
\tau^{2}={\rm Id}
and
d_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\zeta,\tau}}\right)=1-\frac{|W|}{|X|}=d_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\zeta^{2},{\rm Id}}}\right)
.
∎

Claim 8.9

(Almost fixed point free are close to fixed point free)
.

Let
\zeta\in{\rm Sym}(X)
be an involution. Assume
d_{H}(\zeta,{\rm Id})\geq 1-\varepsilon
. Then, there is an involution with no fixed points
\tau\in{\rm Sym}(Y)
, where
|Y|=2\cdot\lceil\nicefrac{{|X|}}{{2}}\rceil
, such that
d_{H}(\zeta,\tau)\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
W=\{x\in X\mid\zeta.x=x\}
. Since
d_{H}(\zeta,{\rm Id})=1-\nicefrac{{|W|}}{{|X|}}
, we can deduce that
|W|\leq\varepsilon|X|
. If
W
is odd, add a vertex to it and make it even. Now that
W
is even sized, we can choose any perfect matching on it and define
\tau
to be the involution induced by this perfect matching. Extend
\tau
to the rest of
X
by letting it act as
\zeta
on the vertices out of
W
. Then the resulting
\tau
is an involution with no fixed points, and

d_{H}(\zeta,\tau)\leq\frac{|W|+1}{|X|}\leq 2\varepsilon.

∎

Claim 8.10

(Almost commuting involutions are close to commuting involutions)
.

Given two involutions
\zeta,\tau\in{\rm Sym}(X)
, there is an involution
\tau^{\prime}\in{\rm Sym}(X)
such that

[\tau^{\prime},\zeta]={\rm Id}\quad{\rm and}\quad d_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\leq d_{H}([\tau,\zeta],{\rm Id}).

Proof.

Let
\delta=d_{H}([\tau,\zeta],{\rm Id})
, and let
W=\{x\in X\mid\zeta\tau.x=\tau\zeta.x\}
. Then
|W|=(1-\delta)|X|
. Moreover, for every
x\in W
,

\begin{split}\zeta\tau.(\zeta.x)&=\zeta.(\tau\zeta.x)=\zeta^{2}\tau.x=\tau.x=\tau\zeta.(\zeta.x)\implies\zeta.x\in W;\\
\tau\zeta.(\tau.x)&=\tau.(\zeta\tau.x)=\tau^{2}\zeta.x=\zeta.x=\zeta\tau.(\tau.x)\implies\tau.x\in W.\end{split}

Hence,
W
is invariant under the actions of
\zeta
and
\tau
. Thus, the following is well defined

\tau^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}\tau(x)&x\in W,\\
x&x\notin W.\end{cases}

We therefore can conclude that
d_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\leq 1-\frac{|W|}{|X|}=\delta
, and

\begin{split}\forall x\in W\ \colon\ \ \tau^{\prime}\underbrace{\zeta.x}_{\in W}&=\tau\zeta.x=\zeta\tau.x=\zeta\tau^{\prime}.x;\\
\forall x\notin W\ \colon\ \ \tau^{\prime}\underbrace{\zeta.x}_{\notin W}&=\zeta.x=\zeta\tau^{\prime}.x,\end{split}

which implies
[\tau^{\prime},\zeta]={\rm Id}
as required.
∎

Claim 8.12

(Almost actions of finite groups are close to actual actions)
.

Let

be a finite group, and let

be a function. Assume that for every

Let

be a vertex such that

\forall g,h\in G\ \colon\ \ f(gh).x=f(g)f(h).x.

Then, if

We assumed for every
