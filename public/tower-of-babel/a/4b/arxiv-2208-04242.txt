Master Thesis
Abstract error analysis for Cahn–Hilliard type equations with dynamic boundary conditions

Abstract




Introduction

In this thesis we consider the Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions, in particular where a Cahn–Hilliard equation is prescribed on the boundary. That is for
u:\bar{\Omega}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta(-\Delta u+W_{\Omega}^{\prime}(u))\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\Gamma}(-\Delta_{\Gamma}u+W^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(u)+\partial_{\nu}u)-\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta_{\Gamma}u+W^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(u)+\partial_{\nu}u)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma.

(1b)

The Cahn–Hilliard equation, first described by
Cahn&Hilliard (1958)
,
[
3
]
, is an equation in mathematical physics and describes the phase separation of a binary fluid. The functions
W_{\Omega},W_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are chemical potentials, a typical example could be the double-well potential
W(u)=(u^{2}-1)^{2}
. The solution
u\in[-1,1]
is the concentration of the two components of the fluid,
u=\pm 1
then indicates a pure occurrence of one of the components.
The two components are mixed in the beginning and then start building segregated domains, which are changing in time. To model the interactions with the wall in confined systems, dynamic boundary conditions are introduced, which is described in
Goldstein et al. (2016)
,
[
8
]
.

This work addresses the problem of solving the Cahn–Hilliard equation numerically. For that we introduce an abstract formulation for Cahn–Hilliard type equations with dynamic boundary conditions, we conduct the spatial semidiscretization via finite elements and prove error bounds based on the technique of energy estimates. The variational formulation for Cahn–Hilliard/Cahn–Hilliard coupling, will apply to a larger abstract class of problems and is similar to the usual weak formulation of parabolic problems. In contrast to problems with non dynamic boundary conditions, the Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)
are exchanged with the spaces
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
and
\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\gamma v\in H^{1}(\Gamma)\}
, respectively.
Because we are considering a fourth-order differential equation, which will be described by a system of two second-order differential equations, the variational formulation also consists of a system of two equations.

The starting point of this thesis was a paper by Balázs Kovács and Christian Lubich from 2016 on numerical analysis of parabolic problems with dynamic boundary conditions,
[
10
]
. Our work has a similar structure and notations and uses some of the results shown in this paper. The Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions is mentioned in this paper, but no numerical analysis is conducted on it. This thesis will show results for the Cahn–Hilliard/Cahn–Hilliard coupling, analogous to results shown in
Kovács & Lubich (2016)
for other parabolic problems with dynamic boundary conditions.

The thesis is organized as follows:

In the Chapter
1
we consider a linear version of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions and derive a weak formulation for it. This weak formulation is then generalized, which yields an evolution equation in an abstract setting. Staying in this abstract setting, we use the technique of energy estimates to show a perturbation result, it bounds the error made by a perturbed solution of the weak formulation and will be later used to prove convergence.

Chapter 2 treats the semidiscretization in space by finite elements in an abstract setting. A consistency result is proved, which together with the stability result from Chapter 1 then results in a convergence estimate. For proving consistency and convergence we collect lemmata for bounding errors of the interpolation, the geometric approximation and the Ritz map.

The abstract setting as well as the convergence theorem are generalized in Chapter 3 to the nonlinear variant of the Cahn–Hilliard equation, by pointing out the main differences as compared to the linear theory. The proofs for stability and consistency will be partly similar to the linear case but more technical.

This work concludes with a description of the time discretization (Chapter 4), and presenting some results of numerical experiments, illustrating our theoretical results in Chapter 5.

Chapter 1
The Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions

We start this chapter by introducing a linear variant of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions. A weak formulation of it is derived, which fits into the abstract variational formulation we will present afterwards. In this abstract setting we will prove a perturbation result, which will later be applied to the semidiscrete case, and generalized in the nonlinear case later.

By introducing a new function
w:\bar{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we rewrite the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1
) as a system of two second-order partial differential equations: For
u,w:\bar{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta w(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T]

(1.1a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta u(x,t)+W^{\prime}_{\Omega}(u)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T],

(1.1b)

with dynamic Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\Gamma}w(x,t)-\partial_{\nu}w(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T]

(1.2a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}u(x,t)+W^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(u)+\partial_{\nu}u(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T].

(1.2b)

We assume that the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded and smooth, and define
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
as its boundary. Furthermore
\partial_{\nu}
denotes the normal derivative and
\nu
the unit normal on
\Gamma
. The operator
\Delta_{\Gamma}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator, given by
\Delta_{\Gamma}u=\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u
where
\nabla_{\Gamma}(\gamma u)=(I-\nu\nu^{T})\gamma(\nabla u)
is the tangential gradient,
\nabla_{\Gamma}u
instead of
\nabla_{\Gamma}(\gamma u)
is written for brevity.

To consider a linear variant of the Cahn–Hilliard/Cahn–Hilliard coupling the derivatives of the chemical potentials,
W^{\prime},W_{\Gamma}^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, are replaced by the inhomogeneities
f_{\Omega}:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
f_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
respectively.
We obtain, for
u,w:\bar{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta w(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T]

(1.3a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta u(x,t)+f_{\Omega}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T],

(1.3b)

with dynamic linear Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\Gamma}w(x,t)-\partial_{\nu}w(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T]

(1.4a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}u(x,t)+f_{\Gamma}(x,t)+\partial_{\nu}u(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T].

(1.4b)

1.1
Weak formulation

In this section we will derive a weak formulation of the linear Cahn–Hilliard equation with linear dynamic boundary conditions.

The first equation of the Cahn–Hilliard problem (
1.3a
) is multiplied with the test function
\varphi^{u}\in\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\gamma v\in H^{1}(\Gamma)\}
, the second equation with
\varphi^{w}\in\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\gamma v\in H^{1}(\Gamma)\}
. Then both equations are integrated over the domain
\Omega

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta w\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\Delta u\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}f_{\Omega}(x,t)\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x.

We denote by
\gamma v
the trace of
v
on
\Gamma
. On the terms on the right-hand side Green’s formula is applied, therefore the above system changes into

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu}w\gamma\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}f_{\Omega}\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu}u\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

The dynamic boundary conditions (
1.4a
) and (
1.4b
) are now plugged into the equations

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}w\gamma\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma\partial_{t}u\gamma\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}f_{\Omega}\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma w\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}u\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)+\int_{\Gamma}f_{\Gamma}\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

Green’s formula is applied again, now on the boundary, and we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma\partial_{t}u\gamma\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}f_{\Omega}\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma w\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)+\int_{\Gamma}f_{\Gamma}\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

Rearranging the equations yields

\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma\partial_{t}u\gamma\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\Big)+\Big(\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}w\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\varphi^{u}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\Big)

\displaystyle=0

(1.5a)

\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle w\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma w\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\Big)-\Big(\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f_{\Omega}\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}f_{\Gamma}\gamma\varphi^{w}\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

(1.5b)

This derivation yields the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Every classical solution
(u,w)
of the Cahn–Hilliard/Cahn–Hilliard coupling (
1.3
)–(
1.4
) is a solution of the weak formulation (
1.5
).

For a sufficiently smooth solution of the weak formulation the reverse statement holds as well.
With the proof above and fundamental lemma of variational calculus can be shown:

Remark 1
.

Every weak solution which is sufficiently regular is a strong solution.

Abstract setting

The abstract setting is the standard abstract framework for parabolic problems, the difference is that we will have an equation system consisting of two equations.

m(\varphi,v)=||v||\Big(\frac{m(\varphi,v)}{||v||}\Big)\leq||\varphi||_{\ast}||v||.

(1.6)

We consider another norm on

Lemma 2

There exists

c_{0}||v||\leq||v||_{a^{\ast}}\leq c_{1}||v||,\qquad v\in V.

Proof.

The first inequality is shown by the Gårding inequality and the second one by using the continuity of the embedding and the bilinear form.
Let

\max(1,\mu)||v||_{a^{\ast}}^{2}\geq||v||_{a}^{2}+\mu|v|^{2}\geq\alpha||v||^{2},

The Cahn–Hilliard equation in the abstract setting

Now we will show how the Cahn–Hilliard equation with Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions fits into the proposed abstract framework.

We define

and

The bilinear form and the inner product are set as

\displaystyle m(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}uv\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\gamma u\gamma v\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle a(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}v\mathrm{d}\sigma(x).

Plugging these definitions into the abstract formulation (

We define the following norms on

\displaystyle||v||^{2}

\displaystyle:=a(v,v)+m(v,v)\qquad

\displaystyle|v|^{2}

\displaystyle:=m(v,v)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }v\in H,

We then have

Furthermore we define a

1.3
A continuous perturbation result

We consider the perturbed solution

Chapter 2
Semidiscrete error analysis

In this section we study the spatial discretization with linear finite elements of the linear Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions. We present a semidiscrete weak formulation, which still fits into the abstract framework introduced in the former chapter. Therefore the stability of the semidiscretization follows from the perturbation result Proposition
1
. The bulk and the surface are both triangulated, the spaces
V
and
H
are replaced by finite dimensional spaces, the inner product and the bilinear form by semidiscrete analogues. To prove convergence in this setting we combine stability bounds with consistency estimates. We will present several error bounds for interpolation, the Ritz map and the geometric approximation before. The stability analysis conducted in this chapter can be applied to the linear variant of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions, but we will work within the abstract formulation, which makes it possible to use this results for other equations as well.

The domain
\Omega
is assumed to be smooth and is approximated by a polyhedral domain
\Omega_{h}
, which is triangulated with a mesh width
h
. The triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is supposed to be quasi-uniform. The surface
\Gamma
is approximated by the boundary of
\Omega_{h}
,
\Gamma_{h}:=\partial\Omega_{h}
. The domain
\Omega_{h}
is constructed such that the vertices of
\Gamma_{h}
are on
\Gamma
. For each node
x_{k}
there is a basis function
\varphi_{k}
such that
\varphi_{j}(x_{k})=\delta_{jk}
for
j,k=1,\ldots,N
. The basis functions are continuous and linear on each element. We define
V_{h}:=\langle\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{N}\rangle
as the span of the nodal basis functions. The restrictions of the basis functions to the boundary
span the boundary finite element space, i.e.
S_{h}=\langle\gamma_{h}\varphi_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{h}\varphi_{N}\rangle
, with the trace operator
\gamma_{h}
on
\Gamma_{h}
.
The discretization of the second Hilbert space
H
is the same as for

,
H_{h}:=V_{h}
.

This semidiscrete variational formulation is a special case of the abstract variational formulation.
The inner product and bilinear form on
V_{h}
are denoted by

and

and have the same properties as

and

in the abstract formulation. The continuous norms
|\cdot|
and
||\cdot||
are replaced by
||\cdot||_{h}
and

|v_{h}|^{2}_{h}:=m_{h}(v_{h},v_{h})

||v_{h}||^{2}_{h}:=a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h}).

We define the following notations of discrete semi-norms and norms on

analogous to the continuous case:

||v||_{a,h}^{2}:=a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h})\qquad\text{for }v_{h}\in V_{h}

and the semidiscrete dual norm

\displaystyle||\varphi||_{\ast,h}:=\sup_{v\in V\setminus\{0\}}\frac{m_{h}(\varphi,v)}{||v||_{h}}.

(2.1)

The weak formulation can then be written as follows: Find

such that the following holds

\displaystyle m_{h}(\partial_{t}u_{h},\varphi^{u}_{h})+a_{h}(w_{h},\varphi^{u}_{h})

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\varphi^{u}_{h}\in V_{h},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle m_{h}(w_{h},\varphi^{w}_{h})-a_{h}(u_{h},\varphi^{w}_{h})

\displaystyle=m_{h}(\widetilde{I}_{h}f,\varphi^{w}_{h})\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\varphi^{w}_{h}\in V_{h},

(2.2b)

with initial condition

\displaystyle m_{h}(u_{h}(0),\varphi_{h}^{u})

\displaystyle=m_{h}(\widetilde{I}_{h}u_{0},\varphi_{h}^{u})\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\varphi^{u}_{h}\in V_{h},

for initial values
u_{0}\in H

We present
u_{h}(t)

With the time dependent coefficient vectors

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}\dot{\text{{u}}}(t)+{\text{{A}}}{\text{{w}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

The proof is using the lemma on the geometric approximation error and the triangle inequality.

2.2.3
The Ritz map

The Ritz map is an almost a-orthogonal map onto the finite element space. We define it here as in
Kovács&Lubich (2016)
,
[
10

.

The Ritz map
R_{h}:V\rightarrow V_{h}^{l}
is defined in two steps:
We determine a solution of the following equation

a_{h}(\widetilde{R}_{h}u,\varphi_{h})=a(u,\varphi_{h}^{l}).

The

.

Let

\gamma u\in H^{2}(\Gamma)

||R_{h}u-u||_{a}^{2}\leq Ch^{2}||u||_{2}^{2}.

(2.19)

The error of the Ritz map in the

For the proof we refer to
Kovács& Lubich (2016)
, Lemma 3.11 and Lemma 3.13.

Adding the inequalities (

) and (

-norm

||R_{h}u-u||^{2}\leq Ch^{2}||u||_{2}^{2}.

(2.21)

3.10
) is fulfilled because of assumption (
3.5
).

The assumption of
e_{h}^{u}(0)=0
and
e_{h}^{w}(0)=0
implies
d_{h}^{u}(0)=0
and
d_{h}^{w}(0)=0
. Applying Proposition
4
yields the following stability estimate for the errors

\displaystyle||

\displaystyle e_{h}^{u}(t)||_{h}^{2}+||e_{h}^{w}(t)||_{h}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{t}e_{h}^{u}(s)||_{h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}||e_{h}^{w}(s)||_{h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq c\Big(||d_{h}^{u}(t)||_{\ast,h}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}||d_{h}^{u}(s)||_{\ast,h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{t}d_{h}^{u}(s)||_{\ast,h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}||d_{h}^{w}(s)||_{\ast,h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{t}d_{h}^{w}(s)||_{\ast,h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\Big),

with
c
independent from
h
.

Since we chose
u_{h}^{\ast}=\widetilde{R}_{h}u
and
w_{h}^{\ast}=\widetilde{R}_{h}w
, the consistency result, Proposition
3
, applies and we obtain

\displaystyle||

\displaystyle e^{u}_{h}(t)||_{h}^{2}+||e^{w}_{h}(t)||_{h}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{t}e^{u}_{h}(s)||_{h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}||e^{w}_{h}(s)||_{h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq ch^{4}.

Because it is
|\cdot|_{h}\leq||\cdot||_{h}
, we obtain as well

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle e^{u}_{h}(t)|_{h}^{2}+|e^{w}_{h}(t)|_{h}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{t}e^{u}_{h}(s)|_{h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}|e^{w}_{h}(s)|_{h}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq ch^{4}.

This inequalities, the equivalence of discrete and continuous norms and the bounds for the Ritz error yield the statements of Theorem
2
.
∎

Chapter 4
Time discretization

To finally solve the Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions numerically we will discretize in time using the backward difference formulae (BDF). For the linear case we use the usual BDF method, for the nonlinear case the linearly implicit method.
The
k
-step BDF method is determined by the coefficients of the generating polynomial
\delta(\xi)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\delta_{l}i^{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{1}{l}(1-\xi)^{l}
. The classical BFD method is zero-stable for
k\leq 6
and has order
k
, see
Hairer&Wanner (1996)
,
[
9
, Chapter V]
. The method is
A
-stable for
k\leq 2
and
A(\alpha)
-stable for
3\leq k\leq 6
.

4.1
Linear case

We recall the linear Cahn–Hilliard/Cahn–Hilliard coupling (
1.3
)–(
1.4
) and add another inhomogeneity to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta w(x,t)+f_{1}^{\Omega}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T]

(4.1a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta u(x,t)+f_{2}^{\Omega}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T],

(4.1b)

with dynamic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\Gamma}w(x,t)-\partial_{\nu}w(x,t)+f_{1}^{\Gamma}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T]

(4.2a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}u(x,t)+\partial_{\nu}u(x,t)+f_{2}^{\Gamma}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T].

(4.2b)

We recall the matrix-vector-formulation (
2.3
)

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}\dot{\text{{u}}}(t)+{\text{{A}}}{\text{{w}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{1}(t)

(4.3a)

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}{\text{{w}}}(t)-{\text{{A}}}{\text{{u}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{2}(t),

(4.3b)

with
{\text{{b}}}_{1}^{(i)}(t):=m_{h}(\widetilde{I}_{h}f_{1}(t),\varphi_{i})
and
{\text{{b}}}_{2}^{(i)}(t):=m_{h}(\widetilde{I}_{h}f_{2}(t),\varphi_{i})
.

For a time step size
\tau>0
we set
t^{n}=n\tau\leq T
.
Applying the k-step BDF method on (
4.3
) yields the following linear equation system

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}{\text{{M}}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\delta_{j}{\text{{u}}}(t^{n-j})+{\text{{A}}}{\text{{w}}}(t^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{1}(t^{n})

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}{\text{{w}}}(t^{n})-{\text{{A}}}{\text{{u}}}(t^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{2}(t^{n})

which, by setting
{\text{{u}}}^{n}:={\text{{u}}}(t^{n})
and
{\text{{w}}}^{n}:={\text{{w}}}(t^{n})
, can be rewritten as follows

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\delta_{0}}{\tau}{\text{{M}}}&{\text{{A}}}\\
-{\text{{A}}}&{\text{{M}}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\text{{u}}}^{n}\\
{\text{{w}}}^{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\text{{b}}}_{1}(t^{n})-\frac{1}{\tau}{\text{{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{j}{\text{{u}}}^{n-j}\\
{\text{{b}}}_{2}(t^{n})\end{pmatrix}.

This linear system has to be solved for every time step. The starting values
{\text{{u}}}^{0},\ldots,{\text{{u}}}^{k-1}
and
{\text{{w}}}^{0},\ldots,{\text{{w}}}^{k-1}
are assumed to be given, e.g. obtained by a Runge–Kutta method.

4.2
Nonlinear case

We recall the nonlinear Cahn–Hilliard/Cahn–Hilliard coupling (
3.1
)–(
3.2
). We add inhomogeneities
f_{1}^{\Omega},f_{2}^{\Omega},f_{1}^{\Gamma}
and
f_{2}^{\Gamma}
, rename the nonlinearities as
F_{\Omega}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
F_{\Gamma}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta w(x,t)+f_{1}^{\Omega}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T]

(4.4a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta u(x,t)+f_{2}^{\Omega}(x,t)+F_{\Omega}(u(x,t))\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T],

(4.4b)

with dynamic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\Gamma}w(x,t)-\partial_{\nu}w(x,t)+f_{1}^{\Gamma}(x,t)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T]

(4.5a)

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}u(x,t)+\partial_{\nu}u(x,t)+f_{2}^{\Gamma}(x,t)+F_{\Gamma}(u(x,t))\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \Gamma\times[0,T].

(4.5b)

The matrix-vector formulation then appears as

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}\dot{\text{{u}}}(t)+{\text{{A}}}{\text{{w}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{1}(t)

(4.6a)

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}{\text{{w}}}(t)-{\text{{A}}}{\text{{u}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{2}(t)+{\text{{F}}}({\text{{u}}}(t)),

(4.6b)

with
{\text{{b}}}_{1}^{(i)}(t):=m_{h}(\widetilde{I}_{h}f_{1}(t),\varphi_{i})
,
{\text{{b}}}_{2}^{(i)}(t):=m_{h}(\widetilde{I}_{h}f_{2}(t),\varphi_{i})
and
{\text{{F}}}({\text{{u}}}(t))_{i}=m_{h}(F(u),\varphi_{i})
, where
F=(F_{\Omega},F_{\Gamma})
.

To have the nonlinearity given explicitly in each time step we approximate
{\text{{u}}}(t^{n})
, where it appears as an argument of the nonlinearity, by the extrapolated value
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{j}{\text{{u}}}^{n-j-1}\approx{\text{{u}}}(t^{n})
. The coefficients
\gamma_{j}
are defined as the coefficients of the polynomial
\gamma(\xi)=\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{l}\xi^{l}=(1-(1-\xi)^{k})/\xi
.
The linearly implicit BDF method applied on (
4.6
) is described by following system

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}{\text{{M}}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\delta_{j}{\text{{u}}}(t^{n-j})+{\text{{A}}}{\text{{w}}}(t^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{1}(t^{n})

\displaystyle{\text{{M}}}{\text{{w}}}(t^{n})-{\text{{A}}}{\text{{u}}}(t^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\text{{b}}}_{2}(t^{n})+{\text{{F}}}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{j}{\text{{u}}}^{n-j-1}\Big)

which can be rewritten as follows

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\delta_{0}}{\tau}{\text{{M}}}&{\text{{A}}}\\
-{\text{{A}}}&{\text{{M}}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\text{{u}}}^{n}\\
{\text{{w}}}^{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\text{{b}}}_{1}(t^{n})-\frac{1}{\tau}{\text{{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{j}{\text{{u}}}^{n-j}\\
{\text{{b}}}_{2}(t^{n})+{\text{{F}}}(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{j}{\text{{u}}}^{n-j-1})\end{pmatrix}.

This linear system has to be solved for every time step. The starting values
{\text{{u}}}^{0},\ldots,{\text{{u}}}^{k-1}
and
{\text{{w}}}^{0},\ldots,{\text{{w}}}^{k-1}
are assumed to be given, e.g. obtained by a Runge–Kutta method.
The linearly implicit BDF method remains to be zero-stable and has still order
k
, cf.
Akrivis &Lubich (2015)
,
[
1
]
.

Chapter 5
Numerical experiments

In this chapter we present our numerical results for linear and nonlinear variants of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic Cahn–Hilliard boundary conditions, which illustrate the proven convergence bounds, Theorem
1
and
2
.

For spatial discretization the implementation uses linear finite elements and for time discretization we use the BDF3 method as presented in the former chapter. As the domain we use the two-dimensional unit disk, and meshes that are generated via DistMesh

12

. We will show results for different mesh sizes and different time step sizes. We choose the inhomogeneities,

4.1

4.2
) respectively (
4.4

4.5

For the nonlinear case we consider the Cahn–Hilliard equation with double-well potential, both in the bulk and on the surface, therefore we choose the nonlinearity

We consider the error between the exact solution at time

and the approximated solution we computed, via the methods described in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, respectively. In Figures
5.1
and
