Multidisperse Random Sequential Adsorption and Generalizations
Roger Fan, Nitya Mani

Abstract
. In this paper, we present a unified study of the limiting density in one-dimensional random sequential adsorption (RSA) processes where segment lengths are drawn from a given distribution. In addition to generic bounds, we are also able to characterize specific cases, including multidisperse RSA, in which we draw from a finite set of lengths, and power-law RSA, in which we draw lengths from a power-law distribution.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The field of
random sequential adsorption
(RSA) studies processes in which particles are sequentially adsorbed onto a substrate such that the particles do not overlap. Known also as
simple sequential inhibition
,
on-line packing
, and the
hard-core model
, RSA is a fundamental process that has been extensively studied in mathematics and statistical physics. RSA also has many applications to biological and chemical processes: for example, reactions on polymer chains have been modelled with RSA, along with various chemisorption (chemical adsorption) processes. See
[
1
,
2
]
for surveys of RSA and its applications.

Meanwhile, the theoretical study of RSA has proven difficult. There is not much quantitative theory for RSA in more than one dimension (some notable results include
[
3
,
4
]
); even in one dimension, only a small number of RSA processes are rigorously understood, despite the large amount of interest in them. In statistical physics, many papers study RSA heuristically, often employing large scale Monte-Carlo simulations
[
5
,
6
]
.

The mathematical study of RSA began with the
Rényi parking problem
, proposed by Alfred Rényi
[
7
]
in 1958. On the interval
[0,L]
, we randomly park a length-
1
segment by choosing its left endpoint uniformly at random from
[0,L-1]
. Then, we randomly park a second length-
1
segment. If the second segment intersects the first, we discard it and randomly choose a new length-
1
segment until it does not overlap with the first. In this way, we continue parking length-
1
segments by randomly choosing “parking spots” on
[0,L]
that we discard if they would cause the segment to overlap with any previously parked segments. We repeat this process until the gaps between segments are too small for any more segments to be parked, at which point we say the process has reached
saturation
.

Figure 1
.
Rényi’s parking problem.

Rényi studied the number of segments parked at
saturation
, given by the random variable
N_{L}
, deriving an integral recurrence equation for its expected value:

\EE[N_{L+1}]=1+2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[N_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t.

By studying the Laplace transform, he was able to characterize the limiting density
\EE[N_{L}]/L
as
L\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.1

(Rényi)
.

Let
N_{L}
be defined as above. Then,

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[N_{L}]}{L}=\alpha,

where
\alpha
is the
Rényi parking constant
, given by

\alpha=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-u}}{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\approx 0.747598.

The asymptotics of
\EE[N_{L}]
are known to much greater precision
[
8
]
, along with a similar asymptotic characterization of the variance of
N_{L}

[
8
,
9
]
. Together with fairly general central limit theorems
[
4
,
3
,
10
]
, this body of work characterizes the limiting distribution of
N_{L}
in one-dimensional random sequential adsorption.

In this paper, we study generalizations of Rényi’s parking problem in which we park segments of varying lengths. These processes are also known as
cooperative
and
competitive RSA

[
11
,
1
,
12
]
.
One of the most highly studied process we investigate in this work is
multidisperse RSA
, in which there are
n
different possible segment lengths, each with a unique probability of arriving at a given time. In other words, when we randomly choose a segment to park, we first randomly choose its length from the
n
different lengths according to some underlying distribution. Then, we choose a random location for the segment on the interval by choosing its left endpoint uniformly at random from
[0,L]
. If the segment is not fully contained within the interval
[0,L]
, or if it overlaps with another segment, we discard it and choose a new segment with a new random length and position. Multidisperse RSA has been widely studied in statistical physics
[
12
,
13
,
6
,
5
]
and is motivated by many chemisorption processes in which a mixture of chemicals are absorbed onto a substrate
[
14
,
15
]
.

1.2.
Main results

Our first main result concerns
multidisperse RSA
and generalizes work of
[
12
]
who studied the setting of two segment lengths. For any collection of
n
segment lengths and an associated distribution, we show that each segment length is expected to cover a linear fraction of the interval
[0,L]
at saturation. Further, in
Theorem

4.4
, we give an exact analytical expression for the limiting fraction of
[0,L]
covered by segments of each length at saturation.

We also study a much vaster generalization of the Rényi parking problem, given by choosing segment lengths from an arbitrary continuous distribution. We describe the possible lengths and their weights with a
length distribution function
(ldf)
\nu:[1,\infty)\rightarrow\RR
, which describes the relative probabilities of choosing each possible segment length. When
\int_{1}^{\infty}\nu(\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\ell<\infty
, we call
\nu

convergent
, and when the integral diverges, we call
\nu

divergent
. Intuitively, convergent ldfs tend to weight small segment lengths more, whereas divergent ldfs tend to weight large segment lengths more (c.f.
Fig.

2
).

S_{L}
, the amount of
empty space
that is
not
covered by segments at saturation. We derive the following integral recurrence equation for

\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
Z_{\nu}(L)=\int_{0}^{L}\nu(\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\ell
is the normalizing constant of
\nu
. By studying this recurrence, we are able to show that convergent and divergent ldfs yield fundamentally different behavior.

In

Different RSA processes in which lengths are drawn from a distribution have been previously considered by mathematicians

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

Throughout this paper, we always park
segments
on an
interval
. We always use
L
to denote the length of interval,
T
to denote time in RSA processes,
\nu
to denote length distribution functions (c.f.
Definition

2.1
), and
Z_{\nu}
to denote the normalizing constant of
\nu
(c.f.
Definition

2.2
). We use
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. To write that
x
is a real number drawn uniformly at random from an interval
[a,b]
, we write
x\xleftarrow{R}[a,b]
.

We use
\RR^{>0}
to denote the positive reals and
\RR^{\geq 0}
to denote the nonnegative reals. We define the function
\Gamma:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
as the Gamma function, given by

\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

For function
f:I\to\RR
that is
j
-times differentiable over connected interval
I
, we let
f^{(i)}(x)
denote the
i
th derivative of
f
with respect to
x
for
1\leq i\leq j.

Finally, we use the following symbols for asymptotic notation (always taken as
L\rightarrow\infty
):

•

f=o(g)
and
f\ll g
denote
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}f(L)/g(L)=0
.

•

f(L)\sim g(L)
denotes
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}f(L)/g(L)=1
.

2.2.
The
\nu
-RSA process

We study the
\nu
-RSA process, in which segment lengths are drawn from a distribution given by a
length distribution function
. Note that if we allowed segment lengths to be any positive real,
\nu
-RSA processes with positive support on all of
(0,\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
would never reach saturation; at every time step, the remaining space could always fit one additional segment drawn from
\nu
. Thus, without loss of generality, we require that the minimum segment length in the distribution is
1
. More formally we have the following:

Definition 2.1
.

A
length distribution function
(or
ldf
) is a nonnegative integrable function
\nu:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\RR^{\geq 0}
such that
\nu(\ell)=0
for
\ell\in[0,1)
, and
\int_{1}^{\ell}\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t>0
for all
\ell>1
(the condition that the minimum segment length is equal to
1
).

We will construct distributions on
[0,L]
induced by an ldf
\nu
on
[0,L]
. To normalize the distribution, we define a
normalizing constant
for every ldf:

Definition 2.2
.

Given ldf
\nu(\ell)
, its
normalizing constant
is the function
Z_{\nu}:\RR^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\RR^{\geq 0}
given by

Z_{\nu}(L)=\int_{0}^{L}\nu(\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\ell.

We also make the following distinction between
convergent
and
divergent
ldfs:

Definition 2.3
.

We say an ldf
\nu(\ell)
, with normalizing constant
Z_{\nu}
, is
convergent
if

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}Z_{\nu}(L)<\infty.

Otherwise,
\nu(\ell)
is
divergent
.

Given an ldf, we describe an RSA process in which segment lengths are drawn from the ldf truncated at
L
. We call this the
\nu
-RSA process
, formally defined as follows:

Definition 2.4

(The
\nu
-RSA process)
.

Let
\nu(\ell)
be an ldf. Then, let the
\nu
-RSA process
be the following stochastic process, in which we attempt to park segments on an interval of length
L
where the segment lengths drawn are from
\nu(\ell)
:

Initialize:

•

I_{0}=[0,L],
the empty region not occupied by parked segments,

•

P_{0}=\emptyset
, the set of parked segments.

Then, for
T=1,2,\ldots
:

•

Sample
b\xleftarrow{R}[0,L]
, and choose a length
\ell\in[1,L]
according to the probability density function
p_{L}(\ell)\propto\nu(\ell)
, i.e.
p_{L}(\ell)=\frac{\nu(\ell)}{Z_{\nu}(L)}.

•

If the segment
(b,b+\ell)\subseteq I_{T-1}
, let
I_{T}=I_{T-1}\setminus(b,b+\ell)
and
P_{T}=P_{T-1}\cup\{(b,b+\ell)\}
. We say the segment
(b,b+\ell)
has been
parked
. Otherwise, let
I_{T}=I_{T-1}
and
P_{T}=P_{T-1}
, and we say that the segment
(b,b+\ell)
has been
rejected
.

•

If all connected intervals in
I_{T}
are of length less than
1
, we say the process is
at saturation
.

We define the
empty space
at saturation to be the random variable
S_{L}
, defined as the total length not covered by parked segments at saturation, viz.

S_{L}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\lambda(I_{T}),

where
\lambda(I_{T})
denotes the Lesbegue measure of
I_{T}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Ney in
[
16
]
and Ananjevskii in
[
17
]
analyze similar processes to the one described above. However, in their processes, we first choose the segment length and
then
place the segment randomly on the interval, with no possibility of rejection. Because of this, their process tends to weight large segments more than ours. Moreover, they consider length distributions with segment length bounded above, whereas we allow arbitrarily large segments, which yields a different analysis and a broader class of questions.

A version of the
\nu
-RSA process is the
multidisperse process
(c.f.
Definition

4.1
), in which we draw segment lengths from a discrete set of lengths
\ell_{1}=1,\ell_{2},\cdots,\ell_{n}
, according to probabilities
q_{1},\cdots,q_{n}
. The multidisperse process can be thought of as the
\nu
-RSA process with

\nu(\ell)=q_{1}\delta(\ell-\ell_{1})+\cdots+q_{n}\delta(\ell-\ell_{n}),

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. We will study multidisperse processes in
Section

4
.

2.3.
Analytic Tools

We employ the Laplace transform heavily throughout this paper, so we briefly recall its definition and some of its basic properties below.

Definition 2.6
.

The
Laplace transform
of a function
f:\RR^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\RR
is the function
\Lap\{f\}:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
given by

\Lap\{f\}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proposition 2.7

(Laplace Transform Properties)
.

Let
F(s),G(s)
be the Laplace transforms of functions
f,g
, and let
\star
be the convolution operator, defined as

(f\star g)(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)g(x-t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-t)g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

The Laplace transform enjoys the following properties:

(1)

(Linearity) For
a,b\in\RR
,
\Lap\{af(x)+bg(x)\}=aF(s)+bG(s).

(2)

(Differentiation) Let
f
be
n
-times differentiable. Moreover, let
f(0^{+})
denote the limit
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(x)
, and let
f^{(n)}
denote the
n
-th derivative of
f
. Then,
\Lap\{f^{(n)}(x)\}=s^{n}F(s)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}s^{n-k}f^{k-1}(0^{+}).

(3)

(Integration)
\Lap\left\{\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right\}=\frac{F(s)}{s}.

(4)

(Translation) For
a\in\RR
,
\Lap\left\{f(x+a)\right\}=e^{as}\left(F(s)-\int_{0}^{a}f(x)e^{-sx}\,\mathrm{d}x\right).

(5)

(Time-Multiplication) For
n\in\ZZ^{>0}
,
\Lap\{x^{n}f(x)\}=F^{(n)}(s).

(6)

(Convolution)
\Lap\{(f\star g)(x)\}=F(s)G(s).

(7)

(Abelian Final Value Theorem) If
f
is bounded and there exists a constant
C
for which
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)=C
, then
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}sF(s)=C.

In our analysis, we apply the Hardy-Littlewood Tauberian Theorem (c.f.
[
19
]
p. 30), which relates the behavior of a function’s Laplace transform around
0
to the behavior of the function at infinity. This will allow us to relate various quantities related to
N_{L}/L
and
S_{L}/L
as
L\to\infty
to the Laplace transforms of related functions at
0
.

Theorem 2.8

(Hardy-Littlewood Tauberian Theorem)
.

If
f:\RR\rightarrow\RR
is positive and integrable,
e^{-st}f(t)
is integrable, and as
s\rightarrow 0
, there exist constants
H,\beta
such that
\Lap\{f\}(s)\sim\frac{H}{s^{\beta}},

then as
x\rightarrow\infty
,

\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\sim\frac{H}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}x^{\beta}.

We also will later require a couple technical propositions, which we will prove here. Analogous forms of
Proposition

2.9
and
Proposition

2.10
with the opposite inequality also hold.

First, we observe that relationships between the
k
-th derivatives of two functions sometimes yield inequalities between the functions.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
y,v:[a,b)\rightarrow\RR
be
n
-times differentiable functions, and for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, let
c_{k}
be a nonnegative function
c_{k}:[a,b)\to\RR^{\geq 0}
. Finally, let
C
be a function
C:[a,b)\rightarrow\RR
. If
y^{(k)}(a)=v^{(k)}(a)
for each
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and if for all
x\in[a,b)
,

y^{(n)}(x)<C(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}(x)y^{(k)}(x),\qquad v^{(n)}(x)=C(x)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}(x)v^{(k)}(x),

then for all
x\in[a,b)
,
y(x)\leq v(x)
.

Proof.

Consider
f(x)=v(x)-y(x)
. Note that
f^{(k)}(a)=0
for
k<n
, and

(2.1)

f^{(n)}(x)>\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}(x)f^{(k)}(x).

Notably,
f^{(n)}(a)>0
. We now claim there exists
\varepsilon
such that
f^{(k)}(x)
is positive on
(a,a+\varepsilon)
for all
k\leq n
. This is because
f^{(k)}(x)
satisfies

\left(f^{(k)}\right)^{(1)}(a)=\left(f^{(k)}\right)^{(2)}(a)=\cdots=\left(f^{(k)}\right)^{(n-k-1)}(a)=0,\qquad\left(f^{(k)}\right)^{(n-k)}(a)>0,

where
\left(f^{(k)}\right)^{(i)}
is the
i
-th derivative of
f^{(k)}
. The general derivative test on
f^{(k)}
then implies that
f^{(k)}
is strictly increasing in a small neighborhood
[a,a+\varepsilon_{k})
around
a
. As
f^{(k)}(a)=0
, this implies that
f^{(k)}(x)
is positive on
(a,a+\varepsilon_{k})
. Letting
\varepsilon
be the minimum of the
\varepsilon_{k}
gives us our desired interval
(a,a+\varepsilon)
.

Now, for contradiction, assume there exists
x\in[a,b)
such that
f(x)<0
. Then, the following infimum exists:

m:=\inf\{x\in[a,b):f^{(k)}(x)<0\text{ for some }k<n\}.

Note that
m\geq a+\varepsilon
, as the
f^{(k)}
’s are nonnegative on
[a,a+\varepsilon)
. We claim there exists some
K<n
for which
f^{(K)}(m)\leq 0
. Otherwise,
f^{(k)}(m)>0
for all
k<n
, and by continuity of the
f^{(k)}
’s, there exists a sufficiently small
\varepsilon^{\prime}
for which all
f^{(k)}(x)
are positive on
[m,m+\varepsilon^{\prime}]
, which contradicts the definition of
m
.

Thus, let
f^{(K)}(m)\leq 0
, and let
x_{K}=m
. By the mean value theorem, there exists some
x_{K+1}
in
(a,m)
such that

f^{(K+1)}(x_{K+1})=\frac{f^{(K)}(x_{K})-f^{(K)}(a)}{x_{K}-a}=\frac{f^{(K)}(x_{K})}{x_{K}-a}\leq 0.

We may repeatedly apply the mean value theorem in this fashion, showing that there exists
x_{K+2}\in(0,m)
for which
f^{(K+2)}(x_{K+2})\leq 0
, and so on, until we find
x_{n}\in(a,m)
for which
f^{(n)}(x_{n})\leq 0
. However, because
x_{n}<m
, we know
f^{(k)}(x_{n})\geq 0
for all
k<n
. By
Eq.

2.1
, we have

0\geq f^{(n)}(x_{n})>\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}(x_{n})f^{(k)}(x_{n})\geq 0.

Of course, it is impossible for
0>0
, so
f(x)=v(x)-y(x)\geq 0
on
[a,b)
. This implies the lemma.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
y,v:(a,b]\rightarrow\RR
be differentiable functions such that

and for all

,

-y^{\prime}(x)<C(x)+c_{0}(x)y,

-v^{\prime}(x)=C(x)+c_{0}(x)v,

and

on

.

3.
Warmup: The Multidisperse Ghost Process

Here, we compute the behavior of the expected saturation density of multidisperse ghost processes, formally defined below. This process admits a simpler analysis than the multidisperse process because each step is independent of the previous ones. While in the usual multidisperse process, we must consider if any previous segments have been parked, we do not have to consider this in the ghost process.

Definition 3.1

(Multidisperse Ghost Process)
.

Fix lengths
\ell_{1}=1,\ldots,\ell_{n}\in\RR^{>0}
and rates
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\in\RR^{>0}
such that
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{n}
and
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}=1.
Then, let the
((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
-
multidisperse ghost process
(abbreviated as
\mcMG((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
) be the stochastic process defined as follows.

Initialize:

•

I_{0}=[0,L],
the empty interval not occupied by parked or ghost segments,

•

P_{0}=\emptyset
, the set of parked segments.

At times
T=1,2,\ldots
,

(1)

Sample candidate segment center
c\xleftarrow{R}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{n},L+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{n}\right]
, and sample
k\in[n]
with probability
q_{k}
. We say that segment
\left(c-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k},c+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}\right)
is a
candidate segment
of
type
k
.

(2)

If
\left(c-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k},c+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}\right)\subseteq I_{T-1}
, let
P_{T}=P_{T-1}\cup\{\left(c-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k},c+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}\right)\}.
We say the candidate segment has been
parked
. Otherwise, let
P_{T}=P_{T-1}
, and we say that the candidate segment has become a
ghost
.

(3)

Regardless of whether the segment has been parked, let
I_{T}=I_{T-1}\setminus\left(c-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k},c+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}\right)
.

We define the
type
k
saturation number
as the total number of type
k
segments parked, at saturation:

(3.1)

N_{k,L}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}|\{A\in P_{t}:\lambda(A)=\ell_{k}\}|.

Moreover, let
J_{L}
be the random variable representing the total length of parked segments, viz.

(3.2)

J_{L}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{A\in P_{T}}\lambda(A).

This can also be expressed in terms of the variables
N_{k,L}
, as
J_{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ell_{k}N_{k,L}.

Remark 3.2
.

This definition allows segment centers to be parked on
[-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{n},0]
and
[L,L+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{n}]
. If this weren’t allowed, then the behavior of the process would change at the ends of the interval
[0,L]
. Moreover, we use segment centers in this definition to simplify the following proofs, but for most other RSA processes, (c.f.
Definition

2.4
,
Definition

4.1
), we use the left endpoints of parked segments to reference them.

For the remainder of the section, the
\ell_{i}
’s will always be positive reals representing the segment lengths in the multidisperse ghost process, and the
q_{i}
’s will always be positive reals summing to
1
that represent the probabilities of choosing each segment. We implicitly assume
\ell_{1}=1
and
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{n}
. Moreover, we will always use
\overline{\ell}
to denote the
average segment length
, defined as follows:

Definition 3.3
.

In
\mathcal{M}_{G}((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
, the
average segment length

\overline{\ell}
is given by
\overline{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\ell_{i}.

We are now able to derive a simple asymptotic for the multidisperse ghost process.

Proposition 3.4
.

In
\mcMG((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
, the type
k
saturation number
N_{k,L}
(c.f.
Definition

3.1
) satisfies

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[N_{k,L}]}{L}=\frac{q_{k}}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}.

Moreover,

(3.3)

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{q_{k}\ell_{k}}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}},

Proof.

Fix
k\in[n]
. At time
T
, we choose a type
k
candidate segment with probability
q_{k}
, and this segment is entirely contained within
[0,L]
exactly when the segment center
c\in\left[\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k},L-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}\right]
, which occurs with probability
\frac{L-\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}
.

Now, assume we have chosen candidate segment
\left(c-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k},c+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{k}\right)\subseteq[0,L],
so
c
is fixed. Fix time
T^{\prime}<T
. To compute the probability that the candidate segments at times
T
and
T^{\prime}
intersect, we condition over the type of the candidate segment at time
T^{\prime}
.

Assume that at time
T^{\prime}
, we have chosen a candidate segment of type
i
with center
c^{\prime}
. The candidate segments at times
T
and
T^{\prime}
do not intersect when
|c-c^{\prime}|>\frac{1}{2}(\ell_{i}+\ell_{k})
. Since
c^{\prime}
is uniformly distributed on
\left[-\frac{1}{2}\ell_{n},L+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{n}\right]
, the candidate segments do not intersect with probability
\frac{L+\ell_{n}-(\ell_{i}+\ell_{k})}{L+\ell_{n}}.

The probability of choosing type
i
at time
T^{\prime}
is simply
q_{i}
, so the probability that the candidate segments at times
T
and
T^{\prime}
do not intersect is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\cdot\frac{L+\ell_{n}-(\ell_{i}+\ell_{k})}{L+\ell_{n}}=1-\frac{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}.

The candidate segments before time
T
are independently chosen, so in fact, the probability that the candidate at time
T
intersects with no previous candidate is
\left(1-\frac{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}\right)^{T-1}.
Thus, the probability that at time
T
, we successfully park a type
k
segment is

q_{k}\cdot\frac{L-\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}\right)^{T-1}.

Summing over times
T
from
1
to
\infty
, the expected number of parked type
k
segments is

\EE[N_{k,L}]=\sum_{T=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\cdot\frac{L-\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}{L+\ell_{n}}\right)^{T-1}=\frac{q_{k}(L-\ell_{k})}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}.

Thus,
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[N_{k,L}]}{L}=\frac{q_{k}}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}
. The formula for
J_{L}
follows by
J_{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ell_{k}N_{k,L}
.
∎

We now prove bounds on
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

In
\mcMG((\ell_{1},q_{1}),(\ell_{2},q_{2}))
, the jamming length satisfies

\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{2}})^{2}}\leq\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

The maximum is achieved when either
q_{1}=0
or
q_{2}=0
. The minimum is achieved when
\overline{\ell}=\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}.

Proof.

Fix the lengths
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}
, and let
F:[0,1]\rightarrow\RR
be given as
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}
when
q_{1}=q
and
q_{2}=1-q
, viz.

F(q)=\frac{q\ell_{1}}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{1}}+\frac{(1-q)\ell_{2}}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{2}}.

We wish to bound
F
both above and below. Taking the derivative and simplifying, we have

F^{\prime}(q)=(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})\left(\frac{\ell_{1}}{(\ell_{1}+\overline{\ell})^{2}}-\frac{\ell_{2}}{(\ell_{2}+\overline{\ell})^{2}}\right).

When
q<\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{2}}}{\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{2}}}
, then
\overline{\ell}>\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}
, and it can be shown that
F^{\prime}(q)<0
. Meanwhile, when
q>\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{2}}}{\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{2}}}
, then
\overline{\ell}<\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}
and
F^{\prime}(q)>0
. Thus,
F
must have a global minimum at
q=\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{2}}}{\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{2}}}
, where
F(q)=\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{2}})^{2}}
and
\overline{\ell}=\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}
. Moreover, the global maximum must lie at either
q=0
or
q=1
. Upon inspection, both yield a maximum of
F(q)=\frac{1}{2}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

We now extend our work with the process
\mcMG((\ell_{1},q_{1}),(\ell_{2},q_{2}))
to the more general process
\mcMG((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\ldots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
.

Corollary 3.6
.

In
\mcMG((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
, the jamming length satisfies

\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{n}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{n}})^{2}}\leq\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

Fix the lengths
\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{n}
. We wish to bound the function
F:\RR^{n}\rightarrow\RR
, given by

F(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{q_{k}\ell_{k}}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k}}.

Let
\mcP
be the region
\{(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})\in\RR^{n}:q_{1}+\cdots+q_{n}=1,q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\geq 0\}.
We only consider
F
in this region
\mcP
, and we will induct on the value of
n
, where the base case with
n=2
is proved in
Corollary

3.5
(and the case where
n=1
is trivial).

Assume now that
Corollary

3.6
holds for
n=M-1
. We will show it holds for
n=M
, where
M\geq 3
. We first investigate relative extrema within the region
R
using Lagrange multipliers. If such an extrema existed, we must have
\nabla F=c\langle 1,\cdots,1\rangle
for some real
c
, or equivalently,
\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}q_{1}}=\cdots=\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}q_{M}}.

Fix arbitrary
i,j\in[M]
. Then,
\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}q_{i}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}q_{j}}
becomes

\ell_{i}\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{i}}-\sum_{k\in[M]}\frac{q_{k}\ell_{k}}{(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k})^{2}}\right)=\ell_{j}\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\ell}+\ell_{j}}-\sum_{k\in[M]}\frac{q_{k}\ell_{k}}{(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k})^{2}}\right),

which simplifies to

\frac{\overline{\ell}}{(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{i})(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{j})}=\sum_{k\in[M]}\frac{q_{k}\ell_{k}}{(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{k})^{2}}.

The right hand side is a constant expression not depending on
i
and
j
. Thus,
\frac{\overline{\ell}}{(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{i})(\overline{\ell}+\ell_{j})}
too must be constant for any choice of
i
and
j
, which only can occur when all the lengths are equal, viz.
\ell_{1}=\cdots=\ell_{M}
. This cannot happen, as our lengths are all distinct. Thus,
F
has no relative extrema in the interior of the region
\mcP
.

Both the minimum and maximum of
F
must then lie on the boundary of
R
, so fix an arbitrary
q_{i}=0
. This now reduces our problem to the case with
n=M-1
, and by the inductive hypothesis, the maximum is always
\frac{1}{2}
.

Meanwhile, if
i=1
, the minimum is
\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{2}\ell_{M}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{2}}+\sqrt{\ell_{M}})^{2}}
. If
i=M
, the minimum is
\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{M-1}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{M-1}})^{2}}
. Otherwise, the minimum is
\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{M}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{M}})^{2}}
. By writing
\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{M}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{M}})^{2}}
as
\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{1}}-\sqrt{\ell_{M}}}{\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{M}}}\right)^{2}
and the others analogously, it is straightforward to show that
\frac{2\sqrt{\ell_{1}\ell_{M}}}{(\sqrt{\ell_{1}}+\sqrt{\ell_{M}})^{2}}
is the least of the three minima, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

In the multidisperse ghost process, we are able to completely bound the jamming density
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[J_{L}]}{L}
. We are generally unable to do this in the multidisperse process, which admits a much more complex analysis.

4.
The Multidisperse RSA Process

In this section, we study multidisperse RSA processes that we formally define below.

Definition 4.1

(Multidisperse Process)
.

Fix segment length
1=\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{n}
and probabilities
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\in\RR^{>0}
(with
\sum_{i\in[n]}q_{i}=1
). The
((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
-multidisperse process
, abbreviated as
\mcM((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
is defined to be the following random process:

Initialize:

•

I_{0}=[0,L],
the empty region not occupied by parked segments,

•

P_{0}=\emptyset
, the set of parked segments.

Then, for
T=1,2,\ldots:

(1)

Sample left endpoint
b\xleftarrow{R}[0,L]
, and segment type
i\in[n]
with
\mathbf{P}(i=k)=q_{k}
. We say that segment
(b,b+\ell_{i})
is a
candidate segment
of
type

i
.

(2)

If
(b,b+\ell_{i})\subseteq I_{T-1}
, let
I_{T}=I_{T-1}\setminus(b,b+\ell_{i})
and
P_{T}=P_{T-1}\cup\{(b,b+\ell_{i})\}.
We say the segment
(b,b+\ell_{i})
has been
parked
. Otherwise, let
I_{T}=I_{T-1}
and
P_{T}=P_{T-1}
, and we say that the segment
(b,b+\ell_{i})
has been
rejected
.

We define the
type
k
saturation number
to be the total number of type
k
segments parked at saturation:

N_{k,L}:=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}|\{A\in P_{T}:\lambda(A)=\ell_{k}\}|,

where
\lambda(A)
denotes the Lesbegue measure of
A
.

Like in the multidisperse ghost process, for the remainder of the section, the
\ell_{i}
’s will always be positive reals representing the segment lengths, and the
q_{i}
’s will always be positive reals summing to
1
that represent the probabilities of choosing each segment. The shortest segment length,
\ell_{1}
, is always implicitly taken to be
1
. Moreover, we will let
\overline{\ell}
be the
average segment length
, so that

\overline{\ell}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}q_{i}\ell_{i}.

We will study the expected number of type
k
segments placed. We begin with the following integral recurrence formula:

Proposition 4.2
.

In
\mathcal{M}((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
, for
L\geq\ell_{n}
,

\EE[N_{k,L}]=\frac{1}{L-\overline{\ell}}\left(q_{k}(L-\ell_{k})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{i}}\EE[N_{k,s}]\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

Proof.

Consider parking segments on interval
[0,L]
with
L\geq\ell_{n}
. At time
T=1
, note that we succeed in parking a segment of type
i
with probability
q_{i}\cdot\frac{L-\ell_{i}}{L}
, as we must first choose a type
i
segment and then successfully park it by choosing its left endpoint to be in
[0,L-\ell_{i}]
, which occurs with probability
\frac{L-\ell_{i}}{L}
. These two events are independent.

Let
A_{i}
be the event that the first segment eventually parked is type
i
. If no segment is parked on the first attempt, we repeatedly make new, independent attempts, so
\mathbf{P}(A_{i})\propto q_{i}\cdot\frac{L-\ell_{i}}{L}.
Since
\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbf{P}(A_{i})=1
, by renormalizing the probabilities we see that
\PP(A_{i})=\frac{q_{i}(L-\ell_{i})}{L-\overline{\ell}}.

Conditioned on
A_{i}
, let the position of the first segment’s left endpoint be
s
. Note that
s
is a random variable uniformly distributed on
[0,L-\ell_{i}]
. After placing the length
\ell_{i}
segment down on
[0,L]
at left endpoint
s
,
[0,L]
is then broken into two subintervals of length
s
and
L-\ell_{i}-s
, on which the same multidisperse process continues. The expected numbers of type
k
intervals placed on the two subintervals are then
\EE[N_{k,s}]
and
\EE[N_{k,L-\ell_{i}-s}]
, respectively. Thus, integrating over the random variable
s
, we have that when
i\neq k
,

\EE[N_{k,L}\mid A_{i}]=\frac{1}{L-\ell_{i}}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{i}}(\EE[N_{k,s}]+\EE[N_{k,L-\ell_{i}-s}])\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{2}{L-\ell_{i}}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{i}}\EE[N_{k,s}]\,\mathrm{d}s.

When
i=k
, we have just placed a type
k
segment and must accordingly add
1
to
\EE[N_{k,L}]
:

\EE[N_{k,L}\mid A_{k}]=1+\frac{2}{L-\ell_{k}}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{k}}\EE[N_{k,s}]\,\mathrm{d}s.

Combining these gives

\EE[N_{k,L}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\PP[A_{i}]\EE[N_{k,L}\mid A_{i}]=\frac{1}{L-\overline{\ell}}\left(q_{k}(L-\ell_{k})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{i}}\EE[N_{k,s}]\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

∎

Using this recurrence, we will use Laplace transforms to derive precise asymptotics for
\EE[N_{k,L}]
as
L\rightarrow\infty
. We will make use of
\Ein:\RR\rightarrow\RR
to denote the modified exponential integral function, viz.

(4.1)

\Ein(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1-e^{-t}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Remark 4.3
.

The
bidisperse process
, i.e. the multidisperse process with
n=2
different segment lengths, has been investigated before by various authors. In particular, Subashiev and Luryi derive in
[
12
]
an exact expression for
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[N_{k,L}]}{L}
in the bidisperse process. Our work here extends their work to the multidisperse process, and by choosing
n=2
, one recovers their formula.

Theorem 4.4
.

Consider
\mcM((\ell_{1},q_{1}),\cdots,(\ell_{n},q_{n}))
, and fix
k\in[n]
. Define functions
P_{i;k}:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
as

(4.2)

P_{i;k}(s):=\int_{\ell_{n}-\ell_{i}}^{\ell_{n}}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L,

and define
G_{k}:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
as

(4.3)

G_{k}(s):=e^{-(\ell_{n}-\overline{\ell})s}\Big(q_{k}+s(\ell_{n}-\overline{\ell})\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}]\Big)+2se^{\overline{\ell}s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}e^{-\ell_{i}s}P_{i;k}(s).

Then,

(4.4)

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[N_{k,L}]}{L}=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{k}(t)\exp\left(-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\Ein(\ell_{i}t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Remark 4.5
.

The functions
P_{i;k}
arise from the behavior of
N_{k,L}
when
L\leq\ell_{n}
, which isn’t governed by the integral recurrence in
Proposition

4.2
.

Example 4.6
.

Consider
\EE[N_{1,L}]
in the multidisperse process
\mcM((1,\frac{1}{2}),(\frac{13}{10},\frac{3}{10}),(\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{5}))
, which has 3 possible segment lengths of
1
,
\frac{13}{10}
, and
\frac{3}{2}
. Note that because
\EE[N_{1,L}]=0
when
L<1
, each
P_{i;1}
is equal to
\int_{1}^{3/2}\EE[N_{1,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L
in this case. To compute
P_{i;1}
, we compute that
\EE[N_{1,L}]
is
1
when
1\leq L<\frac{13}{10}
and
\frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(L-1\right)}{\frac{1}{2}(L-1)+\frac{3}{10}\left(L-\frac{13}{10}\right)}
when
\frac{13}{10}\leq L<\frac{15}{10}
. Then, noting
\overline{\ell}=\frac{31}{100}
, we have

G_{1}(s):=e^{-31s/100}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}s\right)+2s\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{19s/100}+.3e^{-11s/100}+.2e^{-31s/100}\right)\int_{1}^{3/2}\EE[N_{1,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L,

and numerically integrating
Eq.

4.4
yields

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[N_{1,L}]}{L}=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{1}(t)\exp\left(-2\left(\frac{1}{2}\Ein(t)+\frac{3}{10}\Ein\left(\frac{13}{10}t\right)+\frac{1}{5}\Ein\left(\frac{3}{2}t\right)\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\approx.4204.

That is,
\EE[N_{1,L}]\sim.4204L

and

(c.f.

Proof of
Theorem

4.4
.

Fix
k\in[n]
. We first derive a formula for the Laplace transform of
\EE[N_{k,L}]
and then determine the behavior of this Laplace transform around
0
. Finally, we apply
Theorem

2.8
to determine the behavior of
\EE[N_{k,L}]
as
L\rightarrow\infty
.

Define

(4.5)

\varphi(s):=\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L.

Note
\varphi(s)
is not exactly the Laplace transform of
\EE[N_{k,L}]
. Notably, the lower limit of
L
is
\ell_{n}
rather than
0
, as the multidisperse process has a fundamentally different behavior for small
L
(segments of length
\ell_{n}
are never parked).

For brevity, define constants

(4.6)

\rho:=\ell_{n}-\overline{\ell}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{i}:=\ell_{n}-\ell_{i}

for
i\in[n]
. The
\rho_{i}
’s are the differences in length between the largest segment and the other segments, and
\rho
is the difference between the largest segment length and the mean. Because
\ell_{n}
is the largest segment length,
\rho
and
\rho_{i}
are all nonnegative.

We now proceed to formulate a differential equation for
\varphi(s)
:

Lemma 4.6.1
.

Let
w(s)=e^{\overline{\ell}s}\varphi(s)
. Then, with
G_{k}(s)
as defined in
Theorem

4.4
,

w^{\prime}(s)+\frac{2w(s)}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}e^{-\ell_{i}s}+\frac{G_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}=0.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.2
with
L+\ell_{n}
as the interval length, we have

\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]=\frac{1}{L+\rho}\left(q_{k}(L+\rho_{k})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{0}^{L+\rho_{i}}\EE[N_{k,t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

This equation holds for all
L\geq 0
. Rearrange and differentiate with respect to
L
to get

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}L}\Big[(L+\rho)\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]\Big]=q_{k}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\EE[N_{k,L+\rho_{i}}],

which has Laplace transform

(4.7)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}L}\Big[(L+\rho)\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]\Big]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L=q_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\rho_{i}}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L.

We integrate the right and left sides separately. We first observe that

(4.8)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L=e^{\ell_{n}s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]e^{-s(L+\ell_{n})}\,\mathrm{d}L=e^{\ell_{n}s}\varphi(s).

Then, by integration by parts, the left side of
Eq.

4.7
is equal to

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}L}

\displaystyle\Big[(L+\rho)\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]\Big]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L

\displaystyle=s\int_{0}^{\infty}(L+\rho)\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L-\rho\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}]

\displaystyle=-s\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L\right]+s\rho\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\ell_{n}}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L-\rho\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}]

\displaystyle=-s\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}[e^{\ell_{n}s}\varphi(s)]+s\rho e^{\ell_{n}s}\varphi(s)-\rho\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}]\qquad\qquad

(4.10)

\displaystyle=-se^{\ell_{n}s}(\varphi^{\prime}(s)+\overline{\ell}\varphi(s))-\rho\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}].

To evaluate the right hand side of
Eq.

4.7
, first observe that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\rho_{i}}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L

\displaystyle=e^{\rho_{i}s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L+\rho_{i}}]e^{-s(L+\rho_{i})}\,\mathrm{d}L

\displaystyle=e^{\rho_{i}s}\left(\int_{\rho_{i}}^{\ell_{n}}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L+\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\rho_{i}s}\left(P_{i;k}(s)+\varphi(s)\right),

where
P_{i;k}(s)
is as defined in
Eq.

4.2
. The right side of
Eq.

4.7
then simplifies to

(4.11)

\frac{q_{k}}{s}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\cdot e^{\rho_{i}s}(P_{i;k}(s)+\varphi(s)).

Equating
Eq.

4.10
and
Eq.

4.11
yields

-se^{\ell_{n}s}(\varphi^{\prime}(s)+\overline{\ell}\varphi(s))-\rho\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}]=\frac{q_{k}}{s}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\cdot e^{\rho_{i}s}(P_{i;k}(s)+\varphi(s)).

Rearranging and multiplying the equation by
\frac{e^{-\rho s}}{s}
yields

e^{\overline{\ell}s}(\varphi^{\prime}(s)+\overline{\ell}\varphi(s))+\frac{2e^{\overline{\ell}s}\varphi(s)}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}e^{-\ell_{i}s}+\frac{e^{-\rho s}}{s^{2}}\left(q_{k}+s\rho\EE[N_{k,\ell_{n}}]\right)+\frac{2e^{\overline{\ell}s}}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}e^{-\ell_{i}s}P_{i:k}(s)=0.

Consider
G_{k}(s)
as in
Eq.

4.3
, and note that
\frac{G_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}
is exactly the constant term in the above first order differential equation for
\varphi(s)
. That is,

e^{\overline{\ell}s}(\varphi^{\prime}(s)+\overline{\ell}\varphi(s))+\frac{2e^{\overline{\ell}s}\varphi(s)}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}e^{-\ell_{i}s}+\frac{G_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}=0.

Finally, substituting in
w(s)=e^{\overline{\ell}s}\varphi(s)
yields the desired result.
∎

We will solve the above differential equation for
w(s)
. To ensure our calculations are well defined, the integral in
Eq.

4.4
must actually exist. Verifying this is a routine calculus exercise, which we omit for brevity.

Observation 4.7
.

The following integral is finite:

\alpha_{k}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{k}(t)\exp\left(-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\Ein(\ell_{i}t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We proceed to analyze the behavior of the Laplace transform of
\EE[N_{k,L}]
around
s=0
:

Lemma 4.7.1
.

As
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L\sim\frac{\alpha_{k}}{s^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
w(s)=e^{\overline{\ell}s}\varphi(s)
, as in
Lemma

4.6.1
. The maximum number of type
k
segments we can place on an interval of length
L
is
\frac{L}{\ell_{k}}
, so
\EE[N_{k,L}]\leq\frac{L}{\ell_{k}}
, and

w(s)=e^{\overline{\ell}s}\varphi(s)\leq e^{\overline{\ell}s}\int_{\ell_{n}}^{\infty}\frac{L}{\ell_{k}}\cdot e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L=\frac{e^{-\rho s}}{\ell_{k}}\left(\frac{\ell_{n}}{s}+\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right).

This implies the initial condition
\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}w(s)=0.

By
Lemma

4.6.1
,
w(s)
satisfies the differential equation

w^{\prime}(s)+\frac{2w(s)}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}e^{-\ell_{i}s}+\frac{G_{k}(s)}{s^{2}}=0.

Using our initial condition and the method of integrating factors, we may solve this first order linear differential equation, which yields

w(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\int_{s}^{\infty}G_{k}(t)\exp\left(-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\ell_{i}u}}{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Thus, as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
, by dominated convergence we have

w(s)\sim\frac{1}{s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{k}(t)\exp\left(-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\ell_{i}u}}{u}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{s^{2}},

with
\alpha_{k}
defined as the expression in
4.7
. Because
\varphi(s)=e^{\overline{\ell}s}w(s)
, as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
we have
\varphi(s)\sim\frac{\alpha_{k}}{s^{2}}
as well.

Again applying our simple bound
0\leq\EE[N_{k,L}]\leq\frac{L}{\ell_{k}},
we see that as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
,
\varphi(s)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L\leq\frac{\ell_{n}^{2}}{2\ell_{k}}+\varphi(s),

and so
\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[N_{k,L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L\sim\frac{\alpha_{k}}{s^{2}}
.
∎

Applying
Theorem

2.8
with
H=\alpha_{k},\beta=2
, the above implies that

\int_{0}^{L}\EE[N_{k,t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\sim\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\cdot L^{2}.

Recall that by
Proposition

4.2
,

\EE[N_{k,L}]=\frac{1}{L-\overline{\ell}}\left(q_{k}(L-\ell_{k})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{i}}\EE[N_{k,t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

Substituting in our asymptotic for the integral of
\EE[N_{k,L}]
into the right side of this equation completes the proof that
\EE[N_{k,L}]\sim\alpha_{k}L.

∎

Remark 4.8
.

We can derive the variance of
N_{k,L}
by using a similar method to study the second moment,
\EE[N_{k,L}^{2}]
. Similar to
Proposition

4.2
, one can derive the following recurrence:

\displaystyle\EE[N_{k,L}^{2}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L-\overline{\ell}}\Big(q_{k}(L-\ell_{k})+2q_{k}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{k}}\EE[N_{k,t}]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}2q_{i}\int_{0}^{L-\ell_{i}}\left(\EE[N_{k,t}]\EE[N_{k,L-\ell_{i}-t}]+\EE[N_{k,t}^{2}]\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big).

This admits an analysis by Laplace transforms, but the corresponding calculation is even more involved.

5.
General Length Distributions

In this section, we consider more general
\nu
-RSA processes (c.f.
Definition

2.4
) that do not necessarily have discrete support. In particular, we study the behavior of
\EE[S_{L}]
as
L\rightarrow\infty
, where the random variable
S_{L}
measures the amount of empty space (not occupied by segments) at saturation. Similar to our study of multidisperse RSA, we begin by deriving a general integral recurrence formula for
\EE[S_{L}]
.

Remark 5.1
.

In
[
11
]
, Burridge and Mao derive a similar recurrence equation for length distributions with finite support (i.e. there exists
C
for which
\ell>C
implies
\nu(\ell)=0
). They then use this recurrence to study a specific case of bidisperse RSA (c.f.
Remark

4.3
). Here, we extend their recurrence to general length distributions, and we provide a proof for completeness.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\nu(\ell)
be an ldf (see
Definition

2.1
). Then, in the
\nu
-RSA process, for all
L\geq 0
,

\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

We must have
Z_{\nu}(t)=0
for
t<1
, so when
L\leq 1
, the proposition reduces to
0=0
. Thus consider interval length
L>1
.

Let
\mu_{L}(\ell)
be the probability density function for the length of the segment that will be parked first. In the
\nu
-RSA process, segment lengths are chosen according to a distribution proportional to
\nu(\ell)
. Moreover, the segment is then parked successfully on the interval with probability
\frac{L-\ell}{L}
. Thus,
\mu_{L}(\ell)\propto\nu(\ell)(L-\ell)
. Normalizing this distribution then yields

\mu_{L}(\ell)=\frac{(L-\ell)\nu(\ell)}{\int_{0}^{L}(L-t)\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}.

We now compute
\EE[S_{L}]
. Given the length
\ell
of the first segment parked, we use an identical argument to the proof of
Proposition

4.2
to show that
\EE[S_{L}]=\frac{2}{L-\ell}\int_{0}^{L-\ell}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using our probability function
\mu_{L}(\ell)
, we now integrate over the possible values of
\ell
:

\EE[S_{L}]=\int_{0}^{L}\left(\frac{(L-\ell)\nu(\ell)}{\int_{0}^{L}(L-t)\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\frac{2}{L-\ell}\int_{0}^{L-\ell}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\,\mathrm{d}\ell,

which simplifies to

(5.1)

\left(\int_{0}^{L}(L-t)\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]=2\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L-\ell}\nu(\ell)\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\ell.

Switching the order of integration on the right side yields

2\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L-\ell}\nu(\ell)\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\ell=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\int_{0}^{L-t}\nu(\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\ell\,\mathrm{d}t=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Meanwhile, by integration by parts,
\int_{0}^{L}(L-t)\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.
Substituting this into
Eq.

5.1
then proves the proposition.
∎

Using Laplace transforms with the recurrence equation in
Proposition

5.2
, we will later find that
\EE[S_{L}]
grows linearly for a general class of convergent ldfs, whereas it grows sublinearly for a general class of divergent ldfs (see
Definition

2.3
).

5.1.
Convergent Length Distributions

We first consider the
\nu
-RSA process for convergent ldfs
\nu(\ell)
. When considering such ldfs, we always assume
\int_{1}^{\infty}\nu(\ell)\,\mathrm{d}\ell=1
without any loss of generality.

Note as
L\rightarrow\infty
, we must have
Z_{\nu}(L)\rightarrow 1
. If
Z_{\nu}(L)=1-o(L^{-\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
, the improper integral
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-Z_{\nu}(L)}{L}\,\mathrm{d}L
is finite. Our analysis will rely on the weak condition that this integral is finite, which holds for many natural length distributions (c.f.

Theorem 5.3
.

If
\nu(\ell)
is a convergent ldf such that
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-Z_{\nu}(L)}{L}\,\mathrm{d}L<\infty,
then in the
\nu

\alpha_{\nu}
such that as
L\rightarrow\infty

\EE[S_{L}]\sim\alpha_{\nu}L.

.

Proof of
Theorem

5.3
:

Let
\varphi:=\Lap\{\EE[S_{L}]\}
be the Laplace transform of
\EE[S_{L}]
so that

\varphi(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\EE[S_{L}]e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L.

Define
V:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
as
V:=\Lap\{1-Z_{\nu}(L)\}
, and define the function
V_{\star}:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
as a modified version of
V
,

(5.2)

V(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-Z_{\nu}(L))e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L,\qquad V_{\star}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}(1-Z_{\nu}(L))e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L.

Since
Z_{\nu}(L)=0
for
L<1
, we have
V(s)=\frac{1-e^{-s}}{s}+V_{\star}(s).
We will first use these functions to derive differential inequalities satisfied by
\varphi(s)
.

Lemma 5.4.1
.

The following differential inequalities hold when
s>0
:

-\varphi^{\prime}(s)>\frac{2}{s}(e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s))\varphi(s),\qquad-\varphi^{\prime}(s)<-\left(\frac{V(s)}{s}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{2}{s}(e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s))\varphi(s).

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.2
,

(5.3)

\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Because
Z_{\nu}(t)\leq 1
, we have
\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t<L
, and

(5.4)

L\EE[S_{L}]>2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t-2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}](1-Z_{\nu}(L-t))\,\mathrm{d}t,

Taking Laplace transforms, we have that by the time-multiplication property (c.f.
Proposition

2.7
),
\Lap\{L\EE[S_{L}]\}=-\varphi^{\prime}(s).
Moreover, the integration property implies

\Lap\left\{2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\right\}=\frac{2\varphi(s)}{s}
, and the convolution property implies

\Lap\left\{-2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}](1-Z_{\nu}(L-t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right\}=-2\varphi(s)V(s).

Taking the Laplace transform of
Eq.

5.4
now yields
-\varphi^{\prime}(s)>\frac{2\varphi(s)}{s}(1-sV(s)),
and substituting
V_{\star}(s)
for
V(s)
yields the first differential inequality.

To derive the second inequality, we reuse
Eq.

5.3
. This time, we note
\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=L-\int_{0}^{L}(1-Z_{\nu}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t
, so

L\EE[S_{L}]=\left(\int_{0}^{L}(1-Z_{\nu}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]+2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

By definition,
S_{L}\leq L
, so
L\EE[S_{L}]\leq L\int_{0}^{L}(1-Z_{\nu}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t,
or equivalently,

(5.5)

L\EE[S_{L}]\leq L\int_{0}^{L}(1-Z_{\nu}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t-2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}](1-Z_{\nu}(L-t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

Taking Laplace transforms, we note that

\Lap\left\{L\int_{0}^{L}(1-Z_{\nu}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right\}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\Lap\left\{\int_{0}^{L}(1-Z_{\nu}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right\}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left(\frac{V(s)}{s}\right).

Taking the Laplace transform of
Eq.

5.5
then yields

-\varphi^{\prime}(s)\leq-\left(\frac{V(s)}{s}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{2\varphi(s)}{s}-2\varphi(s)V(s),

and substituting in
V_{\star}(s)
as before gives the second differential inequality.
∎

We now find functions that use the differential inequalities to directly bound
\varphi(s)
. If we treat the first inequality in
Lemma

5.4.1
as an equality, then the solution to the differential equation is the function
b:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR
, given by

(5.6)

b(s):=\frac{r(s)}{s^{2}},\qquad\text{where }r(s):=\exp\left(2\int_{1}^{s}\frac{1-e^{-t}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{1}^{s}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

We will show
b(s)
yields a lower bound on
\varphi(s)
.

Lemma 5.4.2
.

Fix
\eta>0
. Then, for
s\in(0,\eta)
,

\varphi(s)\geq\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot b(s).

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{\star}(s)=\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot b(s)
. We may check that

\varphi_{\star}(\eta)=\varphi(\eta)
and that
\varphi_{\star}
is a solution to the differential equation

-\varphi_{\star}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{2}{s}(e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s))\varphi_{\star}(s).

By
Lemma

5.4.1
,
\varphi(s)
satisfies
-\varphi^{\prime}(s)>\frac{2}{s}(e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s))\varphi(s),

and we quickly note
V_{\star}(s)\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}1\cdot e^{-sL}\,\mathrm{d}L=\frac{e^{-s}}{s},
which implies
e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s)>0
. Therefore,
Proposition

2.10
holds here and implies that when
s\in(0,\eta)
,
\varphi(s)\geq\varphi_{\star}(s)
.
∎

Define function
h_{\eta}(s)
as

(5.7)

h_{\eta}(s):=\int_{s}^{\eta}-\left(\frac{V(t)}{t}\right)^{\prime}\cdot\frac{b(s)}{b(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The function
h_{\eta}(s)
is the error when approximating
\varphi(s)
with
b(s)
. By adding
h_{\eta}(s)
to
b(s)
, we will derive an upper bound for
\varphi(s)
.

Lemma 5.4.3
.

Fix
\eta>0
. Then, for
s\in(0,\eta)
,

\varphi(s)\leq\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot b(s)+h_{\eta}(s).

Proof.

Let
\varphi^{\star}(s)=\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot b(s)+h_{\eta}(s)
. Because
h_{\eta}(\eta)=0
, we have

\varphi^{\star}(\eta)=\varphi(\eta)
. Moreover,
\varphi^{\star}(s)
satisfies the differential equation

-\varphi^{\star\prime}(s)=-\left(\frac{V(s)}{s}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{2}{s}(e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s))\varphi^{\star}(s).

Lemma

5.4.1
implies
-\varphi^{\prime}(s)<-\left(\frac{V(s)}{s}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{2}{s}(e^{-s}-sV_{\star}(s))\varphi(s).
Thus,
Proposition

2.10
again proves that
\varphi(s)<\varphi^{\star}(s)
when
s\in(0,\eta)
, completing the lemma.
∎

We now prove useful results about the functions
r(s)
and
h_{\eta}(s)
, which will allow us to bound
\varphi(s)
.

Lemma 5.4.4
.

The function
r(s)
is positive, continuous, and increasing when
s\in(0,\infty)
. It also satisfies

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}r(s)=r(0)>0.

Proof.

Recall

r(s):=\exp\left(2\int_{1}^{s}\frac{1-e^{-t}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{1}^{s}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

Note
r(s)
is continuous (increasing) because the integrals are continuous (increasing), and it is positive by inspection. Moreover,
\frac{1-e^{-t}}{t}
is bounded on
(0,1]
, so
\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-e^{-t}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
exists.

Meanwhile, we have assumed that
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-Z_{\nu}(L)}{L}\,\mathrm{d}L<\infty,
and

(5.8)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-Z_{\nu}(L)}{L}\,\mathrm{d}L=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-Z_{\nu}(L))e^{-tL}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}L=\int_{0}^{\infty}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the last integral interchange is allowable due to Fubini’s Theorem. Since the limit
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{s}^{1}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
is strictly less than the above value, it is finite as well, which proves that
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}r(s)
exists and is equal to
r(0)
, which by definition of
r
must be positive.
∎

This lemma will allow us to bound the approximation error
h_{\eta}(s)
by a function that converges to
0
as
\eta
becomes small.

Lemma 5.4.5
.

There exist continuous functions
H_{\eta}:[0,\eta]\rightarrow\RR
for each
\eta>0
that satisfy
\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}H_{\eta}(0)=0
and
s^{2}h_{\eta}(s)\leq H_{\eta}(s)
when
s\in(0,\eta)
.

Proof.

Fix
\eta>0
. First, note
-\left(\frac{V(s)}{s}\right)^{\prime}
is positive, as
V(s)
is a decreasing function (c.f.
Eq.

5.2
). Then, for all
0<s<\eta
,

\displaystyle h_{\eta}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{r(s)}{s^{2}}\int_{s}^{\eta}-\left(\frac{V(t)}{t}\right)^{\prime}\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{r(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r(s)}{s^{2}}\cdot\int_{s}^{\eta}-\left(\frac{V(t)}{t}\right)^{\prime}\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{r(0)}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(5.9)

\displaystyle h_{\eta}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}\cdot\frac{r(s)}{r(0)}\cdot\int_{s}^{\eta}-\left(\frac{V(t)}{t}\right)^{\prime}\cdot t^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

We may use integration by parts on the integral, which yields

\int_{s}^{\eta}-\left(\frac{V(t)}{t}\right)^{\prime}\cdot t^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=sV(s)-\eta V(\eta)+2\int_{s}^{\eta}V(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq sV(s)+2\int_{s}^{\eta}V(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Recall
V(t)=\frac{1-e^{-t}}{t}+V_{\star}(t)\leq 1+V_{\star}(t)
. Substitution yields

sV(s)+2\int_{s}^{\eta}V(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq sV(s)+2(\eta-s)+2\int_{s}^{\eta}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting this upper bound back into
Eq.

5.9
yields the estimation
h_{\eta}(s)\leq\frac{H_{\eta}(s)}{s^{2}},
with
H_{\eta}:(0,\eta]\rightarrow\RR
given by

H_{\eta}(s):=\frac{r(s)}{r(0)}\cdot\left(sV(s)+2(\eta-s)+2\int_{s}^{\eta}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

The continuity of
H_{\eta}(s)
follows by the continuity of
r(s)
and
V(s)
. Note
H_{\eta}(s)
is not yet defined at
0
since
V
is not defined at
0
. However, because as
L\rightarrow\infty
, we have
1-Z_{\nu}(L)\rightarrow 0
. The abelian final value theorem (c.f.
Proposition

2.7
) then implies
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}sV(s)=0.
Moreover, we have previously shown
\int_{0}^{\eta}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
is finite (c.f.
Eq.

5.8
). It follows that

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}H_{\eta}(s)=2\eta+2\int_{0}^{\eta}V_{\star}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Define
H_{\eta}(0)
to be this value. By inspection of this formula,
\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}H_{\eta}(0)=0
.
∎

Finally, we analyze the behavior of
\varphi(s)
as
s\rightarrow 0
.

Lemma 5.4.6
.

As
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
, there exists a positive constant
\alpha_{\nu}
such that

\varphi(s)\sim\frac{\alpha_{\nu}}{s^{2}}.

Proof.

Fix any
\varepsilon>0
. We will now find
l_{\varepsilon},\delta_{\varepsilon}
that satisfy the conditions of
Proposition

2.11
for our choice of
\varepsilon
, i.e. that for all
0<s<\delta_{\varepsilon}
, we have
l_{\varepsilon}<s^{2}\varphi(s)<l_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon
.

We use the functions
H_{\eta}
as defined in
Lemma

5.4.5
. Choose sufficiently small
\eta
such that
H_{\eta}(0)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.
Then, choose sufficiently small
\delta_{1}
such that for all
0<s<\delta_{1}
, we have

(5.10)

H_{\eta}(s)<H_{\eta}(0)+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}<\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}.

Finally, because
r(s)
is increasing, we may choose
\delta_{2}
such that for all
0<s<\delta_{2}
,

(5.11)

r(0)<r(s)<r(0)+\frac{b(\eta)}{\varphi(\eta)}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Let
\delta=\inf\{\eta,\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
. By
Lemma

5.4.2
, for
0<s<\delta
, we have
\varphi(s)\geq\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot b(s)
, so

s^{2}\varphi(s)\geq\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot r(s)>\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot r(0).

Meanwhile, by
Lemma

5.4.3
, for
0<s<\delta
, we have
\varphi(s)\leq\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot b(s)+h_{\eta}(s),
so

s^{2}\varphi(s)\leq\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot r(s)+s^{2}h_{\eta}(s)<\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot r(0)+\varepsilon,

where the second inequality follows from
Eq.

5.11
and from
s^{2}h_{\eta}(s)\leq H_{\eta}(s)<\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}
(c.f.
Lemma

5.4.5
and
Eq.

5.10
).

Define
\delta_{\varepsilon}=\delta
and
l_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varphi(\eta)}{b(\eta)}\cdot r(0).
We have shown that for
0<s<\delta_{\varepsilon}
, we have
l_{\varepsilon}<s^{2}\varphi(s)<l_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,
which satisfies the conditions of
Proposition

2.11
and proves that
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}s^{2}\varphi(s)
exists. Denote this limit
\alpha_{\nu}
. Notably, all the lower bounds
l_{\varepsilon}
are positive, so
\alpha_{\nu}
must also be positive, concluding the lemma.
∎

By the Hardy-Littlewood Tauberian Theorem (c.f.
Theorem

2.8
),
\varphi(s)\sim\frac{\alpha_{\nu}}{s^{2}}
implies that as
L\rightarrow\infty
,

\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\sim\frac{\alpha_{\nu}}{2}L^{2}.

From here, an analytical argument will complete the proof.

Pick arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. We will first show there exists
L_{\ell}
such that
L>L_{\ell}
implies
\frac{\EE[S_{L}]}{L}>\alpha_{\nu}(1-\varepsilon)
, and we will later show there exists
L_{u}
such that
L>L_{u}
implies
\frac{\EE[S_{L}]}{L}<\alpha_{\nu}(1+\varepsilon)
, proving
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[S_{L}]}{L}=\alpha_{\nu}
.

Begin by choosing
L_{1}
for which
L>L_{1}
implies
\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t>(1-\varepsilon)^{1/4}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{\nu}}{2}L^{2}
. Moreover, choose
L_{2}
for which
L>L_{2}
implies
Z_{\nu}(L)\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{1/4}
. By
Proposition

5.2
,

\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We always have
Z_{\nu}(L)<1
. Therefore,

L\EE[S_{L}]\geq\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]=2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 2(1-\varepsilon)^{1/4}\int_{0}^{L-L_{2}}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the last equality follows by the definition of
L_{2}
. Then, when
L-L_{2}>L_{1},
we have

2(1-\varepsilon)^{1/4}\int_{0}^{L-L_{2}}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t>(1-\varepsilon)^{1/2}\alpha_{\nu}(L-L_{2})^{2}.

Choose
L_{\ell}
sufficiently large so that
L>L_{\ell}
implies
(L-L_{2})>(1-\varepsilon)^{1/4}L
, and so that
L_{\ell}>L_{1}+L_{2}
. Then, for
L>L_{\ell}
,

(1-\varepsilon)^{1/2}\alpha_{\nu}(L-L_{2})^{2}>(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{\nu}L^{2}.

Thus, for
L>L_{\ell}
, we have
L\EE[S_{L}]>(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{\nu}L^{2}
, or
\EE[S_{L}]>(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{\nu}L.

We now show there exists
L_{u}
such that
L>L_{u}
implies
\frac{\EE[S_{L}]}{L}<\alpha_{\nu}(1+\varepsilon)
. Choose
L_{3}
for which
L>L_{3}
implies
\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t<(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{\nu}}{2}L^{2}
, and choose
L_{4}
for which
L>L_{4}
implies
Z_{\nu}(L)>(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/3}.
For
L>L_{3}+L_{4}
, we have similarly to before that

\displaystyle\alpha_{\nu}(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}L^{2}

\displaystyle>2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 2\int_{0}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{L_{4}}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]>\frac{L-L_{4}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}}\EE[S_{L}].

Choose
L_{u}
sufficiently large so that
L>L_{u}
implies
(L-L_{4})>\frac{L}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}}
, and so that
L_{u}>L_{3}+L_{4}
. Then,
\frac{L-L_{4}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}}\EE[S_{L}]>\frac{L\EE[S_{L}]}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2/3}}.
Thus, for
L>L_{u}
, we have
\alpha_{\nu}(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}L^{2}>\frac{L\EE[S_{L}]}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2/3}}
, or
\EE[S_{L}]<(1+\varepsilon)\alpha_{\nu}L.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.2.
Divergent Length Distributions

In this section we consider the
\nu
-RSA process for a divergent ldf
\nu(\ell)
(c.f.
Definition

2.1
). For any divergent ldf
\nu
, consider its normalizing constant
Z_{\nu}
. We must have

\frac{\int_{0}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}\geq\frac{L}{2},

because if we treat
Z_{\nu}
as an non-normalized probability distribution on
[0,L]
, and we sample random variable
X
from the distribution, the left hand side is simply
\EE[X]
. But
Z_{\nu}
is a strictly increasing function, and so
\EE[X]
is skewed to above
\frac{L}{2}
. The difference between the left hand side and the right hand side is intuitively a measure of how quickly
Z_{\nu}
grows, as when
Z_{\nu}
grows very quickly,
\EE[X]
is skewed higher. Our next theorem shows that
\EE[S_{L}]=o(L)
for any divergent ldf that satisfies a slightly stronger version of the above inequality.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\nu
be a divergent ldf. If there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for sufficiently large
L
, we have
\frac{\int_{0}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}\geq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{L}{2},

then in the
\nu
-RSA process,

\EE[S_{L}]=o(L).

Example 5.6
.

Theorem

5.5
applies to the following natural families of ldfs, where we show
\EE[S_{L}]
grows sublinearly with
L
:

(1)

The ldf
\nu(\ell)=\ell^{p}
for all
\ell>1
for any fixed
p>-1
(we give more precise asymptotics for this case in
Section

6
).

(2)

The the ldf
\nu(\ell)=e^{a\ell}
for all
\ell>1
for any fixed
a>0
.

We will prove
Theorem

5.5
using the following lemma:

Lemma 5.6.1
.

Given any integer
n\geq 0
, there exists
b
such that
L>b
implies

\EE[S_{L}]\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{n}L.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction. The lemma immediately holds for
n=0
: for any
L
, we must have
\EE[S_{L}]\leq L
, as the empty space
S_{L}
cannot exceed
L
.

Given that
Lemma

5.6.1
holds for
n=k-1
, we will now show it holds for
n=k
. That is, given that there exists
b
such that
L>b
implies
\EE[S_{L}]\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{k-1}L
, we will show
\EE[S_{L}]\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{k}L
also holds for sufficiently large
L

For brevity, define
r:=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{k-1}
. When
L>b
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L}]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{b}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{b}^{L}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{b}tZ_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t+2r\int_{b}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(5.12)

\displaystyle=2r\int_{0}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t+2\left(1-r\right)\int_{0}^{b}tZ_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Note that
\int_{0}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t=L\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let
L^{\star}
be sufficiently large so that
L>L^{\star}
implies
\frac{\int_{0}^{L}tZ_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}\geq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{L}{2},
as described by the theorem statement. Then, when
L>L^{\star}
, we may rearrange
Eq.

5.12
into

\displaystyle\EE[S_{L}]

\displaystyle\leq 2rL-(1+\varepsilon)rL+2\left(1-r\right)\cdot\frac{\int_{0}^{b}tZ_{\nu}(L-t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_{0}^{L}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon)rL+2\left(1-r\right)b.

But
2(1-r)b
is constant, so it follows that
\EE[S_{L}]<r\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)L=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{k}L
for sufficiently large
L
, completing the induction.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.5
:

For any
\eta>0
, we may find
n
such that
\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{n}<\eta
.
Lemma

5.6.1
then implies that
\EE[S_{L}]<\eta L
for sufficiently large
L
. Thus,
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\EE[S_{L}]}{L}=0
, completing the proof of
Theorem

5.5
.
∎

6.
Power Function Distribution

In this section, we consider the case when the ldf is given by a power-law function,
\nu(\ell)=(\ell-1)^{\beta-1}
with
\beta>0
. This is an example of a divergent ldf, where
\EE[S_{L}]=o(L)
per
Theorem

5.5
. However, by using the nice property that the convolution of two power functions is again a power function, we are in fact able to derive much sharper bounds on
\EE[S_{L}]
.
We will show that
\EE[S_{L}]
is intimately connected to a beta distribution associated the the power law. For the rest of this section, we will take
\Beta
to be the Beta function, given by

\Beta(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}t^{z_{1}-1}(1-t)^{z_{2}-1}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\Gamma(z_{1})\Gamma(z_{2})}{\Gamma(z_{1}+z_{2})}.

Observation 6.1
.

Let
\Xi:\RR^{>0}\rightarrow\RR^{>0}
be a function of
\beta>0
, defined as the positive solution in
\theta
to

\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)=\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)}.

Then, the function
\Xi
is well-defined and at most
1
.

Proof.

We first note
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}t.
The right hand side is strictly decreasing in
\theta
, so
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)
must also be decreasing in
\theta
. When
\theta=0
,
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)=\frac{1}{\beta+1}>\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)}
. Meanwhile, when
\theta=1
,
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)=\frac{1}{(\beta+1)(\beta+2)}<\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)}.
By the continuity of
\Beta
, there must exist
\theta
with
0<\theta<1
for which
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)=\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)}
. This equation is equivalent to the condition in the corollary statement. Moreover, because
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)
is strictly decreasing in
\theta
, our value of
\theta
is unique, proving that
\Xi
is well-defined.
∎

For
\nu(\ell)=(\ell-1)^{\beta-1}
, we will prove
\EE[S_{L}]
is bounded below by power functions of the form
L^{\Xi(\beta)-\varepsilon}
, and above by
L^{\Xi(\beta)}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Fix
\beta>0
and consider ldf
\nu(\ell)=(\ell-1)^{\beta-1}
. Then, in the
\nu
-RSA process, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

L^{\Xi(\beta)-\varepsilon}\ll\EE[S_{L}]\leq L^{\Xi(\beta)}.

Remark 6.3
.

Consider the case when
\beta=1
, with ldf
\nu(\ell)=1
. Then,
\Beta(\beta+1,\theta+1)=\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)}
reduces to the polynomial equation

(\theta+1)(\theta+2)=4,

which has unique positive solution
\theta=\Xi(1)=\frac{\sqrt{17}-3}{2}\approx 0.562.

Theorem

6.2
now implies that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

L^{(\sqrt{17}-3)/2-\varepsilon}\ll\EE[S_{L}]\leq L^{(\sqrt{17}-3)/2}.

Similar processes to the
\nu
-RSA process with the uniform length distribution
\nu(\ell)=1
have been studied before. In particular, Coffman et. al. in
[
20
]
analyze a process in which they park segments with lengths drawn uniformly at random from
[0,L]
. Instead of studying the empty space left at saturation (arbitrarily small segments may be parked in their model, so it never in fact reaches saturation), they study the expected number of parked segments after
n
attempts to park segments. Using methods different from ours, they derive that the number of parked segments grows as
n^{(\sqrt{17}-3)/2}
. Notably, this exponent is exactly the exponent that we derive for the empty space at saturation in
Remark

6.3
, providing an interesting connection between these two related processes.

Proof of
Theorem

6.2
.

For simplicity, we take
\nu(\ell)=\frac{1}{\beta}(\ell-1)^{\beta-1}
(for
\ell\geq 1
) so that
Z_{\nu}(L)=(L-1)^{\beta}
(for
L\geq 1
). Define
f_{\theta}(L):=\frac{\EE[S_{L}]}{L^{\theta}}.
We will prove
f_{\Xi(\beta)}(L)\leq 1
, and that for all
\theta<\Xi(\beta)
, we have
f_{\theta}(L)\geq c_{\theta}>0
. Let
\mu:[0,1]\rightarrow\RR
be the pdf of
\text{Beta}(\theta+1,\beta+1)
:

\mu(t):=\frac{t^{\theta}(1-t)^{\beta}}{\Beta(\theta+1,\beta+1)},

so that
\int_{0}^{1}\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=1
. We now use
\mu
to derive an integral recurrence relation on
f_{\theta}
.

Lemma 6.3.1
.

For
\theta,L>0
,

f_{\theta}(L+1)=2(\beta+1)\Beta(\theta+1,\beta+1)\cdot\left(\frac{L}{L+1}\right)^{\theta}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}f_{\theta}(Lt)\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

By
Proposition

5.2
with
L+1
as the length of the interval,

\left(\int_{0}^{L+1}Z_{\nu}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\EE[S_{L+1}]=2\int_{0}^{L+1}\EE[S_{t}]Z_{\nu}(L+1-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting in
Z_{\nu}(L)=(L-1)^{\beta}
(when
L>1
) and
\EE[S_{L}]=L^{\theta}f_{\theta}(L)
yields

f_{\theta}(L+1)=\frac{2(\beta+1)L^{\theta}}{(L+1)^{\theta}}\cdot\int_{0}^{L}\frac{t^{\theta}(L-t)^{\beta}}{L^{\theta+\beta+1}}f_{\theta}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting
Lt
for
t
and rearranging then proves the lemma.
∎

We may now establish the upper bound of the theorem. By
Lemma

6.3.1
with
\theta=\Xi(\beta)
(and noting
2(\beta+1)\Beta(\Xi(\beta)+1,\beta+1)=1
by the definition of
\Xi
), we have

(6.1)

f_{\Xi(\beta)}(L+1)=\left(\frac{L}{L+1}\right)^{\Xi(\beta)}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\Xi(\beta)}(Lt)\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t<\sup_{0\leq t\leq L}f_{\Xi(\beta)}(t).

But
\EE[S_{L}]=L
for
L<1
. Because
\Xi(\beta)<1
, we have
f_{\Xi(\beta)}(L)=L^{1-\Xi(\beta)}<1
when
L<1
.
Eq.

6.1
then implies that
f_{\Xi(\beta)}(L)<1
for all
L
, and thus that
\EE[S_{L}]\leq L^{\Xi(\beta)}
.

Now, fix any
\theta<\Xi(\beta)
. Recall
\Beta(\theta+1,\beta+1)
is decreasing in
\theta
(c.f.
6.1
), so
2(\beta+1)\Beta(\theta+1,\beta+1)>1.
Then, there exists some
\varepsilon>0
and some lower bound
L_{1}>0
such that
L>L_{1}
implies

2(\beta+1)\Beta(\theta+1,\beta+1)\cdot\left(\frac{L}{L+1}\right)^{\theta}>1+\varepsilon,

and thus that
f_{\theta}(L+1)>(1+\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{1}f_{\theta}(Lt)\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Choose sufficiently large
L_{2}
for which
\int_{2/L}^{1}\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t>\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}
for all
L>L_{2}
. Let
L^{\star}=\sup\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
, so that for
L>L^{\star},

(6.2)

f_{\theta}(L+1)>(1+\varepsilon)\int_{2/L}^{1}f_{\theta}(Lt)\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\inf_{2\leq t\leq L}f_{\theta}(t).

Note that
Lemma

6.3.1
implies
f_{\theta}(L)>0
for
L>1
. Then, by
Eq.

6.2
, for all
L>L^{\star},
we have
f_{\theta}(L)>\sup_{2\leq t\leq L^{\star}}f_{\theta}(t),
where
\sup_{2\leq t\leq L^{\star}}f_{\theta}(t)
is positive.

Thus, for every
\theta<\Xi(\beta)
, we have shown there is a constant
c_{\theta}>0
such that
f_{\theta}(L)>c_{\theta}
for sufficiently large
L
, or equivalently that
\EE[S_{L}]>c_{\theta}L^{\theta}
. This in fact implies
\EE[S_{L}]\gg L^{\theta}
for all
\theta<\Xi(\beta)
, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

We may also derive the following more tractable form of
\Xi(\beta)
when
\beta
is an integer.

Corollary 6.4
.

If
\beta>0
is an integer, then let
\theta
be the unique positive root to

\prod_{i=1}^{\beta+1}(\theta+i)=2(\beta+1)!.

Consider ldf
\nu(\ell)=(\ell-1)^{\beta-1}
. Then, in the
\nu
-RSA process, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

L^{\theta-\varepsilon}\ll\EE[S_{L}]\leq L^{\theta}.

Proof.

When
\beta
is an integer, the equation
\Beta(\theta+1,\beta+1)=\frac{1}{2(\beta+1)}
reduces to the polynomial
\prod_{i=1}^{\beta+1}(\theta+i)=2(\beta+1)!
. The uniqueness of
\theta
follows from
6.1
, and then
Corollary

6.4
immediately follows from
Theorem

6.2
.
∎
