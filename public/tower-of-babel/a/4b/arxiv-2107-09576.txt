Bass-Serre theory for groupoids
Giulia dal Verme 1 and Thomas Weigel 2 ∗

Abstract
. In this paper a Bass-Serre theory in the groupoid setting is developed and a structure theorem (cf. Theorem 7.3 ) is established. Any groupoid action without inversion of edges on a forest induces a graph of groupoids, while any graph of groupoids satisfying certain hypothesis admits a canonical associated groupoid, called the fundamental groupoid, and a forest, called the Bass-Serre forest, such that the fundamental groupoid acts on the Bass–Serre forest. The structure theorem states that these processes are mutually inverse.

18B40, 20L05

1.
Introduction

Actions of groups on trees play a fundamental role in many areas of mathematics.
A part of J-P. Serre’s extensive contribution to the theory was the introduction of graphs of groups in
[
12
]
. The theory of graphs of groups was further developed by H. Bass in
[
2
]
, and is now known as Bass-Serre theory.

The well-known Bass-Serre theory gives a complete and satisfactory description of groups acting on trees via the structure theorem.
A graph of groups consists of a connected graph
\Gamma
together with a group for each vertex and edge of
\Gamma
, and group monomorphisms from each edge group to the adjacent vertex groups. Any group action (without inversion of edges) on a tree induces a graph of groups,
while any graph of groups has a canonical associated group, called the
fundamental group
, and a tree, called the
Bass–Serre tree
, such that the fundamental group acts on the Bass–Serre tree.
The structure theorem says that these processes are mutually inverse, so that graphs of groups “encode”  group actions on trees.
Graphs of groups are now fundamental tools in geometric group theory and low-dimensional topology. The fundamental group of a graph of groups generalises two basic constructions in combinatorial group theory, namely free products with amalgamation and HNN extensions.
These constructions correspond in topology to taking a connected sum and adding a handle, respectively.

Groupoids are small categories in which every morphism is invertible (see
[
3
]
).
From an algebraic point of view,
groupoids can be thought as a generalization of a group,
i.e., a groupoid is a set with partial multiplication on it that could
contain many identities.
Every groupoid defines a disjoint union of groups, and thus a choice of base points is part of the language. This leads to the definition of the
source
and
range
maps for a groupoid. Despite the analogies between groups and groupoids, generalizing structural results from groups to groupoids is in general a highly non-trivial task.
The purpose of this paper fits in this paradigm: our intention is to establish a Bass-Serre theory for groupoids.

The concept of a group action on a space was generalized to a groupoid action and it has applications to dynamical systems, representation theory and operator algebras. If groups can roughly be described as the set of symmetries of certain objects, then groupoids can be thought as the set of symmetries of fibered objects.

In Sections
2
and
3
it is convenient to look at groupoids from a categorical point of view, while in Sections
4
,
5
,
6
and
7
we use an algebraic point of view.
The collection
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
of idempotent elements in a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is called its

unit space
, since these are precisely the elements
x
that satisfy
x\alpha=\alpha

and
\beta x=\beta
whenever these products are defined.
When considering an action of
\mathcal{G}
on a graph
\Gamma
,
this leads to a fibered structure of
\Gamma
over
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

A graph of groupoids
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
is given by a connected graph
\Gamma
together with a groupoid for each vertex and edge of
\Gamma
, and monomorphisms from each edge groupoid to the adjacent vertex groupoid.
We will only work with graph of groupoids having discrete vertex and edge groupoids.
We associate to any graph of groupoids
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
a groupoid
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
, called the
fundamental groupoid
, and a forest
X_{\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)}
,
called the
Bass-Serre forest
, such that
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
acts on
X_{\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)}
.
We will use an algebraic approach to define the
fundamental groupoid
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
by generators and relations (see
[
8
]
for presentations of groupoids).

One of the main differences between the group and groupoid settings is the following:
in the classical setting, given a group action without inversion on a graph, one of
the ingredients used to build a graph of groups is the quotient graph given by such an action;
in the groupoid context, there is no canonical graph associated to the action of a groupoid
on a graph.
In the groupoid framework the construction of a graph which works similar to the quotient graph in the group setting has been challenging and requires some technical arguments.
Given a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
acting without inversion on a forest
F
, we use the fibered structure of
F
on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and the equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
defined by the action
\mathcal{G}
on
F
to recover the vertex set
F^{0}
.
We then define a
tree of representatives
of the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
F
.
Subsequently, we are able to associate a graph of groupoids to the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
F
via the definition of a

desingularization
of a groupoid action on a graph.
As a consequence, we obtain the following structure theorem (cf. Theorem
7.3
) in this setting.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid acting without inversion of edges on a forest
F
such that each fiber (with respect to the momentum map) of the forest is a tree.
Then
\mathcal{G}
is isomorphic to the fundamental groupoid of the graph of groupoids defined by a desingularization of the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
F
.

In classical Bass-Serre theory, given a group action without inversion on a tree
G\curvearrowright T
, one defines the vertex group
G_{x}
,
x\in\Lambda^{0}
, where
\Lambda=G\backslash T
is the quotient graph, to be the stabilizers of a vertex representative
x^{\prime}\in p^{-1}(x)
, where
p\colon T\to G\backslash T
is the natural projection.
Given a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
acting on a forest
F
, we define the vertex groupoids to be the stabilizers of the partial sections given by a desingularization of the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
F
.
Moreover, as in the group context, the vertex groupoids are isomorphic by the conjugacy relation.

In case one considers graph of groupoids given by groupoids whose unit space is a singleton,
one recovers the classical Bass-Serre theory.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Graphs

The notion of graph we use in this paper is the one used by J-P. Serre in
[
12
]
, apart from the fact that we allow the graph with empty vertex set. This graph will be called the
empty graph
.
A
graph

\Gamma
(in the sense of J-P. Serre) consists of a set of vertices
\Gamma^{0}
,
a set of edges
\Gamma^{1}
, a terminus map
t\colon\Gamma^{1}\to\Gamma^{0}
,
an origin map
o\colon\Gamma^{1}\to\Gamma^{0}

and an edge-reversing map
\bar{}\,\colon\Gamma^{1}\to\Gamma^{1}
satisfying

\bar{e}\neq e,\quad\bar{\bar{e}}=e,\quad t(\bar{e})=o(e).

Such a graph
\Gamma
can be viewed as an
unoriented
(or
undirected
) graph
in which each geometric edge is replaced by a pair of edges
e
and
\bar{e}
.

A
subgraph

\Lambda
of
\Gamma
consists of a non-empty subset
\Lambda^{0}\subseteq\Gamma^{0}
and a subset
\Lambda^{1}\subseteq\Gamma^{1}

such that
\overline{\Lambda^{1}}\subseteq\Lambda^{1}
,

t(\Lambda^{1})\subseteq\Lambda^{0}
and
o(\Lambda^{1})\subseteq\Lambda^{0}
.

An
orientation

\Gamma_{+}^{1}\subseteq\Gamma^{1}
of
\Gamma
is a set of edges containing
exactly one edge from each pair
\{e,\bar{e}\}
,
e\in\Gamma^{1}
.

Let
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
be two graphs. A
graph homomorphism
is a pair of mappings

\psi=(\psi^{0},\psi^{1})
, where

\psi^{0}\colon\Gamma^{0}\to\Lambda^{0}
and
\psi^{1}\colon\Gamma^{1}\to\Lambda^{1}
, which commutes
with
t
,
o
and
\,\bar{}
, i.e.,

\psi^{0}(o(e))=o(\psi^{0}(e)),\quad\psi^{0}(t(e))=t(\psi^{0}(e)),\quad\psi^{1}(\bar{e})=\overline{\psi^{1}(e)}.

A homomorphism of graphs is called an
isomorphism
if
\psi^{0}
and
\psi^{1}
are bijective.
By definition, any subgraph
\Lambda
of a graph
\Gamma
defines a canonical injective homomorphism of graphs
\iota\colon\Lambda\to\Gamma
.

For
v\in\Gamma^{0}
we call

\mathrm{st}_{\Gamma}(v)=\{e\in\Gamma^{1}\mid t(e)=v\}

the
star
of
v
in
\Gamma
. The cardinality of
\mathrm{st}_{\Gamma}(v)
is called the
valence
of
v
in
\Gamma
.
We say that
\Gamma
is
locally-finite
if each vertex has finite valence.
A locally-finite graph such that each vertex has valence
k
will be called
k
-regular
.

A vertex of
\Gamma
is said to be
singular
if it has valence one, i.e.,
if it is the terminus (equivalently, origin) of a unique edge.
We say that
\Gamma
is
nonsingular
if it has no singular vertices.

2.2.
Paths

A
path
of length
n
in
\Gamma
is either a vertex
v\in\Gamma^{0}
(when
n=0
),
or a sequence of edges
e_{1}\dots e_{n}
with
o(e_{i})=t(e_{i+1})
for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
.
For a path
p=e_{1}\dots e_{n}
with
t(e_{1})=v
and
o(e_{n})=w
we say that
p
is a path from
v
to
w
.
We put
t(p)=t(e_{1})
and
o(p)=o(e_{n})
and denote by
\ell(p)=n
the length of the path
p
.
If
e_{i+1}\neq\bar{e}_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
, then we say that
p
is
without backtracking
.
A path of length
n
is said to be
reduced
if either
n=0
or if there is no backtracking.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{v,w}
the set of all paths from
v
to
w
without backtracking.
A
cycle
is a path with origin equal to its terminus.
We say that the non-empty graph
\Gamma
is
connected
if
\mathcal{P}_{v,w}\neq\emptyset
for all
v,w\in\Gamma^{0}
.
For a path
p=e_{1}\dots e_{n}
, we define the
reversal
path
\bar{p}=\bar{e}_{n}\cdots\bar{e}_{1}
.

In a connected graph
\Gamma
one defines a distance function

\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma^{0}\times\Gamma^{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
by

(2.1)

\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(v,w)=\min\{\,\ell(p)\mid p\in\mathcal{P}_{v,w}\,\},\quad v,w,\in\Gamma^{0},

which satisfies
\mathrm{dist}_{\Gamma}(v,w)=0
if and only if
v=w
.

Definition 2.1
.

A graph
\Gamma
is said to be a
tree
if it is connected and for every
v\in\Gamma^{0}
,
the only reduced path which starts and ends at
v
is the path of length
0
at
v
.
A
forest
is a graph whose connected components are trees.

Equivalently,
\Gamma
is a tree if
|\mathcal{P}_{v,w}|=1
for all
v,w\in\Gamma^{0}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph. The set
\operatorname{SubTr}(\Gamma)
of subgraphs of
\Gamma
which are trees, ordered by inclusion, is directed and non-empty.
If
\Omega\subseteq\operatorname{SubTr}(\Gamma)
is a totally ordered subset,
then
\bigcup_{T\in\Omega}T
is a subtree of
\Gamma
, and thus, an upper bound for
\Omega
.
Thus, by Zorn’s lemma,
\operatorname{SubTr}(\Gamma)
has maximal elements, which are called
maximal subtrees
.
Note that for a maximal subtree
T\subseteq\Gamma
one has that
T^{0}=\Gamma^{0}

(see
[
12
, Proposition I.11]
).

2.3.
Categories and Groupoids

The algebraic structures that will be studied in the subsequent sections are groupoids.
For this purpose we recall the basic notions in category theory. For further details, the interested the reader is referred to
[
4
]
.

A
category

\mathcal{C}
consists of

(i)

a class
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
, called the class of
objects
of
\mathcal{C}
;

(ii)

for each
x,y
in
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
a set
\mathcal{C}(x,y)
called the set of
morphisms
in
\mathcal{C}
from
x
to
y
;

(iii)

a function, called

, which assigns to each
g\in\mathcal{C}(y,z)
and to each

an element
gf\in\mathcal{C}(x,z)
; that is, composition is a function

\mathcal{C}(y,z)\times\mathcal{C}(x,y)\to\mathcal{C}(x,z)
.

These data must satisfy the axioms:

(1)

(Associativity) If
h\in\mathcal{C}(z,w)
,
g\in\mathcal{C}(y,z)
,
f\in\mathcal{C}(x,y)
, then
h(gf)=(hg)f
.

(2)

(Existence of identities) For each
x
in
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
there is an element
1_{x}
in
\mathcal{C}(x,x)
such that for all
g\in\mathcal{C}(w,x)
and
f\in\mathcal{C}(x,y)
one has
1_{x}g=g
and
f1_{x}=f
.

For each
x
in
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
the
identity in
\mathcal{C}(x,x)
is unique, since if
1_{x}
,
1_{x}^{\prime}
are both identities in
\mathcal{C}(x,x)
, then
1_{x}=1_{x}1_{x}^{\prime}=1_{x}^{\prime}
.

We shall always assume that the various sets
\mathcal{C}(x,y)
are disjoint.

.

(i)

(iv)

(v)

.

\mathcal{C}
is said to be

, we denote by

.
For a small category

(ii)

;

(iii)

;

(iv)

the identity in

.

The subcategory

\mathcal{C}

Definition 2.3
.

A category whose objects form a set and in which every morphism is an isomorphism is called a
groupoid
.

Equivalently, a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is a small category in which all the morphisms are invertible.
A groupoid can also be viewed as a generalization of a group
which has a partially-defined product.
We denote by
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
the set of objects and call it the
unit space
of
\mathcal{G}
, and by
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
the set of morphisms of
\mathcal{G}
.
We call
units
the elements of
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.
We denote by
r,s\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
the
range
and
source
maps, respectively.
We call
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}=\{\,(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\mid s(g_{1})=r(g_{2})\,\}
the set of
composable pairs
.
Note that
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=s(\mathcal{G})=r(\mathcal{G})
and the units of
\mathcal{G}
are the identity morphisms of the category
\mathcal{G}
,
in the sense that
gs(g)=g=r(g)g
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

A groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is said to be
connected

if the map
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\times\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
g\mapsto(r(g),s(g))
is surjective.

For
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, we say that
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}\mid s(g)=x=r(g)\}
is the
isotropy group
of
\mathcal{G}
at
x
.
We call
\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}\mid s(g)=r(g)\}
the
isotropy
of
\mathcal{G}
.
By defining

\displaystyle\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{G})^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{G})(x,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{G})(x,y)&\mbox{ if }x=y\\
\hfil\emptyset\hfil&\mbox{ if }x\neq y\\
\end{cases}

for
x,y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
one verifies easily
that the isotropy is a subgroupoid of
\mathcal{G}
.

A
homomorphism of groupoids

f\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
is essentially a functor, i.e., it consists of a pair of functions

f^{(0)}\colon\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\to\mathcal{H}^{(0)}
,

f^{(1)}\colon\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1)}
, preserving all the structure.
Thus groupoids and their homomorphisms form a category
Grpd
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid. A
subgroupoid
of
\mathcal{G}
is a subcategory
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{G}
such that if
h\in\mathcal{H}
, then
h^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}
; that is,
\mathcal{H}
is a subcategory which is also a groupoid.
We say
\mathcal{H}
is
full (wide)
if
\mathcal{H}
is a
full (wide)
subcategory of
\mathcal{G}
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
be a homomorphism of groupoids.
We define the
kernel
and the
image
of

to be the subsets

\ker f=\{\,g\in\mathcal{G}\mid f(g)\in\mathcal{H}^{(0)}\,\}
and

\mathrm{im}\,f=\{\,f(g)\mid g\in\mathcal{G}\,\}
, respectively.
Notice that if
f\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
is a homomorphism of
groupoids then
\mathrm{im}f
is not usually a subgroupoid of
\mathcal{H}
.

The following proposition will be useful for our purpose.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
13
]

.

A homomorphism of groupoids
\phi\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
is injective if and only if
\ker\phi=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

Example 2
.

(i)

A group, regarded as a category with one object, is also a groupoid.

(ii)

The category of reduced paths in a tree is a groupoid.

Remark 2.5
.

An important class of functors are
forgetful functors
,
a general term that is used for any functor that forgets structure.
For example,

sends a group to its underlying set and a group

We will use the forgetful functor

which sends a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
to its underlying graph

and a groupoid homomorphism

to its underlying graph homomorphism.

3.
Groupoid actions

The notion of a groupoid action on a space is a straightforward generalization of
group actions.
Much of this section is inspired by
[

]

Definition 3.1
.

A groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is said to
act (on the left)
on a set
X

if there are given a surjective map
\varphi\colon X\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
called the
momentum
,
and a map
\mathcal{G}\circledast X\to X
,
(g,x)\mapsto gx
,
where

\mathcal{G}\circledast X=\{(g,x)\in\mathcal{G}\times X\mid s(g)=\varphi(x)\}
is called the
fibered product
of
\mathcal{G}
on
X
,
that satisfy

(A1)

\varphi(gx)=r(g)
for all
(g,x)\in\mathcal{G}\circledast X
;

(A2)

(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)},(g_{2},x)\in\mathcal{G}\circledast X
implies

(g_{1}g_{2},x),(g_{1},g_{2}x)\in\mathcal{G}\circledast X
and

g_{1}(g_{2}x)=(g_{1}g_{2})x;

(A3)

\varphi(x)x=x
for all
x\in X
.

We say that
X
is a
left
\mathcal{G}
-set
.
A left
\mathcal{G}
-set
X
is said to be
transitive
if given
x,y\in X
there exists
g\in\mathcal{G}

such that

The concept of conjugation for subgroupoids of a given groupoid is rather recent and unexplored.
The following definition is taken from

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid and let
\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{H}
be subgroupoids of
\mathcal{G}
.
We say that
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{H}
are

(or

)
if there are a functor
F\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
which is an equivalence of categories and
a family
\{g_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(0)}}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that

(i)

g_{x}\in\mathcal{G}(F(x),x)
for all

(ii)

for all

one has that
F(k)=g_{x_{2}}\,k\,g_{x_{1}}^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}\big(F(x_{2}),F(x_{1})\big)

Remark 3.10
.

Equivalently, as shown in
[
7
]
,
two subgroupoids
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{G}
with monomorphisms
\tau_{\mathcal{K}}\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{G}
and

\tau_{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}

are conjugated if there is an equivalence

F\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
between their underlying categories and a natural transformation
\mathfrak{g}\colon\tau_{\mathcal{H}}\circ F\to\tau_{\mathcal{K}}
.
It follows that
\mathfrak{g}
is actually a natural isomorphism.
It is also shown in
[
7
]
that the conjugacy relation is
reflexive, symmetric and also transitive, i.e., it is an equivalence relation on the set of all subgroupoids of a given groupoid.

In contrast with the classical group setting, conjugated subgroupoids are not
necessarily isomorphic. In fact, we only know that conjugated subgroupoids
have equivalent underlying categories (see
[
7
]
, Example 4.9).

We will use conjugated subgroupoids such that the functor
F
is an injective equivalence of categories. In particular, one has the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid and let
\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{H}
be conjugated subgroupoids of
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
i_{g}\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
denote the conjugation map, i.e.,

i_{g}(k)=g_{r(\gamma)}\,k\,g_{s(\gamma)}^{-1}
.
If the equivalence of categories
F\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{H}
is injective, then
i_{g}
is an injective
homomorphism of groupoids.

Proof.

We first prove that
i_{g}
is a groupoid homomorphism.
For
x\in\mathcal{K}^{(0)}
, one has that

(3.2)

i_{g}(x)=g_{x}xg_{x}^{-1}=g_{x}g_{x}^{-1}=F(x)\in\mathcal{H}^{(0)}.

Moreover, for
k,l\in\mathcal{K}
such that
s(k)=r(l)
, one has

(3.3)

i_{g}(kl)=g_{r(k)}\,k\,l\,g_{s(l)}^{-1}=g_{r(k)}\,k\,g_{s(k)}^{-1}\,g_{r(l)}\,l\,g_{s(l)}^{-1}=i_{g}(k)\,i_{g}(l).

Hence,
i_{g}
is a groupoid homomorphism.
Suppose that there exist
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
i_{g}(h)=i_{g}(h^{\prime})
. Then one has

(3.4)

g_{r(h)}\,h\,g_{s(h)}^{-1}=g_{r(h^{\prime})}\,h^{\prime}\,g_{s(h^{\prime})}^{-1},

which implies that

(3.5)

r(g_{r(h)})=r(g_{r(h^{\prime})})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad s(g_{s(h)}^{-1})=s(g_{s(h^{\prime})}^{-1}).

Hence, by (i) of Definition
3.9
, one has that

(3.6)

F(r(h))=F(r(h^{\prime}))\quad\mbox{ and }\quad F(s(h))=F(s(h^{\prime})).

Since
F
is injective, this implies that
r(h)=r(h^{\prime})
and
s(h)=s(h^{\prime})
.
Then one has that

(3.7)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=g_{r(h)}^{-1}\,g_{r(h)}\,h\,g_{s(h)}^{-1}\,g_{s(h)}

\displaystyle=g_{r(h)}^{-1}\,g_{r(h^{\prime})}\,h^{\prime}\,g_{s(h^{\prime})}^{-1}\,g_{s(h)}

\displaystyle=g_{r(h^{\prime})}^{-1}\,g_{r(h^{\prime})}\,h^{\prime}\,g_{s(h^{\prime})}^{-1}\,g_{s(h^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}.

That is,
i_{g}
is injective.
∎

5.1.
Fiber spaces

A set

a surjective map
\pi\colon F\to X

, such that
\pi^{-1}(x)

We call

of

A

of

of

Remark 5.1
.

In the case we are interested in, the target set

of the projection

is countable too.
If

is a fiber space on

.
One may suppose that the enumeration in each fiber of

.
