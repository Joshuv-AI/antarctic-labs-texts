Monoidal Structures in Orthogonal Calculus
Leon Hendrian

Abstract
Orthogonal Calculus, first developed by Weiss in 1991 [ 41 ] , provides a “calculus of functors” for functors from real inner product spaces to spaces. Many of the functors to which Orthogonal Calculus has been applied since carry an additional lax symmetric monoidal structure which has so far been ignored. For instance, the functor V ↦ BO ​ ( V ) V\mapsto\text{BO}(V) admits maps BO ​ ( V ) × BO ​ ( W ) → BO ​ ( V ⊕ W ) \text{BO}(V)\times\text{BO}(W)\to\text{BO}(V\oplus W) which determine a lax symmetric monoidal structure. Our first main result, Corollary 4.2.0.2 , states that the Taylor approximations of a lax symmetric monoidal functor are themselves lax symmetric monoidal. We also study the derivative spectra of lax symmetric monoidal functors, and prove in Corollary 5.4.0.1 that they admit O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) -equivariant structure maps of the form Θ n ​ F ⊗ Θ n ​ F → D O ⁡ ( n ) ⊗ Θ n ​ F \Theta^{n}F\otimes\Theta^{n}F\to D_{O(n)}\otimes\Theta^{n}F where D O ⁡ ( n ) ≃ S Ad n D_{O(n)}\simeq S^{\text{Ad}_{n}} is the Klein-Spivak dualising spectrum of the topological group O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) . As our proof methods are largely abstract and ∞ \infty -categorical, we also formulate Orthogonal Calculus in that language before proving our results. This article is largely identical to the authors PhD thesis [ 22 ] .

Chapter 2
Other Work on Orthogonal Calculus

This chapter is a collection of several versions of, calculations with and statements about Orthogonal Calculus that are in the literature. We hope that it might serve as a useful starting point for the reader who wishes to delve deeper into the subject.

2.1
Formalisms for Orthogonal Calculus

Orthogonal Calculus has been developed and used in different formalisms. We briefly mention some of them and their differences.

2.1.1
The Original Version (Weiss)

In Weiss’s original paper
[
41
]
, the category
\mathcal{J}
of finite-dimensional real inner-product spaces and linear isometric embeddings is a
1
-category enriched in (topological) spaces, and the functors
F
which are studied are supposed to be continuous in the sense that the evaluation maps

\text{mor}(V,W)\times F(V)\to F(W)

are continuous. If the category of spaces occuring as the target of the functors is understood to be a convenient category of topological space in the sense that it is cartesian closed (e.g. CGWH spaces), then one can equivalently ask for the maps

\text{mor}(V,W)\to\text{mor}(F(V),F(W))

to be continuous, hence the setting is that of categories enriched in nice topological spaces and enriched functors between them.

In this framework, one has to be careful about the meaning of

\lim_{0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}F(U\oplus V)

It is a
holim
, and extra care has to be taken as the holim is not indexed over a set or a finite diagram, but over a space. See also
[
41
, Comments to Definition 5.1]
.

In defining the derivatives, an important role is played by categories called
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. These are categories enriched in pointed spaces with the same objects as
\mathcal{J}
and

\text{mor}_{\mathcal{J}_{n}}(V,W)=\text{Th}(\gamma_{n}(V,W))

where
\gamma_{n}\to\text{mor}_{\mathcal{J}}(V,W)
is the
n
-th tensor power of the orthogonal complement bundle on
\text{mor}_{\mathcal{J}}(V,W)
and

denotes the Thom space. Starting with a functor
F
from
\mathcal{J}
to spaces, one obtains a functor

on
\mathcal{J}_{n}
by left Kan extension.
F^{(n)}
might be called the unstable
n
-th derivative of
F
. The derivative spectrum
\Theta^{n}F
is related to

by

. An inductive construction using fibre sequences for

is also available (see e.g.
[
41
]
, p.2), but obscures the

-action.

A slight difference in the (statement of) properties of Orthogonal Calculus is that in this enriched,
1
-categorical setup, the subcategory of polynomial functors

is not quite a reflective subcategory. (See
[
41
, Chapter 6]
for details.)

2.1.2
Model Categories (Barnes-Oman)

Orthogonal Calculus has been re-developed in the language of model categories by Barnes and Oman (
[
7
]
). In their description, there are several model structures on the category of functors from

to spaces. More concretely, there is a model category

in
W
, i.e.

O(V)\to O(V\oplus W)

\varphi\to\begin{pmatrix}\varphi&0\\
0&id_{W\ominus f(V)}\end{pmatrix}

The continuity is obvious.
The checks required for the other examples work similarly.
∎

Remark 3.1.0.6
.

We observe that the functoriality checks only makes use of the definition of
\mathcal{J}
, in particular the isometricity condition in the mapping spaces and the inner product, insofar as the (natural) orthogonal complements are used to extend automorphisms on subspaces to the whole vector space. One can wonder whether this is a starting point for a generalisation for Orthogonal Calculus. See also
6.3.0.4
.

3.2
Polynomial Functors

The central notion to functor calculus is that of polynomial functors.

Recall from ordinary calculus that for a polynomial function
f
of degree at most
n
, the interpolation polynomial at
n+1
distinct points is
f
itself.

In Orthogonal Calculus, a similar interpolation property is turned into a definition.

Definition 3.2.0.1
.

A functor
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}
is called
polynomial of degree
\leq n
or simply

n
-polynomial
if the canonical natural transformation

F(-)\to\lim_{0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\text{ linear subspace}}F(-\oplus U)

is an equivalence.

A functor
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}_{*}
is
polynomial of degree
\leq n
if its composition with the forgetful map
\mathcal{S}_{*}\to\mathcal{S}
is.

We denote by
\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})\subseteq\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})
and
\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})
the full subcategories of functors which are polynomial of degree
\leq n
.

Remark 3.2.0.2
.

The limit

\lim_{0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\text{ linear subspace}}F(-\oplus U)

appearing in the previous definition is formed over the slice category in
\mathcal{J}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Note that, from a classical viewpoint, this is not simply the poset of sub-vector spaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, but takes into account the topology.

Example 3.2.0.3
.

Examining
Definition

3.2.0.1
for
n=0
, we see that a
0
-polynomial functor
F
is equivalent to the constant functor with value
F(0)
.

Example 3.2.0.4
.

Let
n=1
. Then the indexing category
0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
takes the shape of a cone over
\mathbb{R}P^{1}
, as there is an
\mathbb{R}P^{1}
worth of
1
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and a single
2
-dimensional subspace, into which all of them include.

Example 3.2.0.5
.

Let
\Theta\in Sp^{\text{BO}(n)}
. Then the functor

V\mapsto\Omega^{\infty}\left(\left(S^{nV}\wedge\Theta\right)_{hO(n)}\right)

is polynomial of degree
n
.

Definition/Proposition 3.2.0.6
.

Let
F\in\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})
. Define

\tau_{n}F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}

V\mapsto\lim_{0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\text{ linear subspace}}F(V\oplus U)

By definition,
F\in\text{Poly}^{\leq n}
iff the natural map
\rho\colon F\to\tau_{n}F
is an equivalence.

Define furthermore
T_{n}F
to be the colimit of
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{n}F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{n}^{2}F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{n}(\rho)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{n}^{2}(\rho)}

The inclusion into the first term induces a natural transformation

\eta_{n}\colon\text{id}_{\text{Fun}}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})\to T_{n}

Weiss showed in
[
41
]
, Theorem 6.3 that
T_{n}F
is always
n
-polynomial.

Polynomial Functors Form A Reflective Subcategory

As Michael Weiss already remarked in the original article,
\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})\subset\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})

“wants”
to be a reflective subcategory, but could not be in the
1
-categorical world. In
\infty
-land, we now show that this problem disappears and
T_{n}\colon\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})\to\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})
is a left adjoint to the inclusion, and
\eta_{n}
is the unit of this adjunction.

Definition 3.2.0.7

(
[
27
]
, 5.2.7.9)
.

A full subcategory
\mathcal{C}^{0}
of an
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
is called
reflective subcategory
if the inclusion
\mathcal{C}^{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}
admits a left adjoint.

Much of the work needed to establish an
\infty
-categorical version of Orthogonal Calculus has already been done in the original article
[
41
]
(and the erratum
[
40
]
), especially in Theorem 6.3. We repeat it here, noting that we have already adapted the notation.

Theorem 3.2.0.8

(
[
41
, Thm. 6.3]
,
[
40
]
)
.

For any
n\geq 0
, there exists a functor

T_{n}\colon\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})\to\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})

(constructed as in
3.2.0.6
)
taking equivalences to equivalences, and a natural transformation

\eta_{n}\colon id_{\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})}\to T_{n}

with the properties

1.

is polynomial of degree
\leq n
for all

2.

If

is already polynomial of degree
\leq n

\eta_{n,E}\colon E\to T_{n}E

3.

For every

This theorem covers much of what one needs to know to use the

3.3
Homogeneous Functors and Derivative Spectra

If one imagines
F\to T_{n}F
to be the
“
n
-th Taylor polynomial of
F
”
, the natural next question is

“What is the
n
-th derivative of
F
?”

Sticking to the analogy with real calculus for just a moment, for
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we observe

(T_{n}f-T_{n-1}f)(x)=x^{n}\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}

In functor calculus, the role corresponding to the term on the right-hand side of this equation is played by
n
-homogeneous functors.

Definition 3.3.0.1

(
[
41
]
, Definition 7.1.)
.

A functor
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}
or
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}_{*}
is
n
-homogeneous
if it is polynomial of degree
\leq n
and all values of
T_{n-1}F
are contractible.

Denote by

\displaystyle\text{Homog}^{n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\text{Homog}^{n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})

the full subcategories on
n
-homogeneous functors.

These categories can also be characterised by pullback diagrams of
\infty
-categories

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Homog}^{n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Poly}^{\leq n-1}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Homog}^{n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Poly}^{\leq n-1}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

where the maps

and

map to the constant functor with value the one point space, which is

-polynomial for any

.

In the pointed case, it is easy to associate

-homogeneous functors to any

in the following way.

.

Let

. For

. This construction defines a functor
L_{n}\colon\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})\to\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})
.

Proposition 3.3.0.3
.

L_{n}
is right adjoint to the inclusion
\text{Homog}^{n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})\hookrightarrow\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})
, i.e.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Homog}^{n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dashv}

Proof.

Let

and

\text{Nat}_{\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}(H,L_{n}P)\simeq\text{Nat}_{\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}(H,P)

Using the definition and the adjunction of

\displaystyle\text{Nat}_{\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S}_{*})}(H,T_{n-1}P)

\displaystyle\simeq*

hence by the fibre sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{L_{1}F}}

3.4
Some Topos-Theoretical Remarks

This short section contains some remarks about the categories
\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})
from a topos-theoretical viewpoint. It is not essential to the rest of the thesis and could be safely skipped.

A trivial observation is that since

\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})=\text{Fun}((\mathcal{J}^{\text{op}})^{\text{op}},\mathcal{S})=\text{PSh}(\mathcal{J}^{\text{op}})

is a presheaf category (valued in spaces), it is an example of an
\infty
-topos.

Recall the definition:

Definition 3.4.0.1

(Definition 6.1.0.4 of
[
27
]
)
.

An
\infty
-category
\mathcal{X}
is called an
\infty
-topos if there exists a small
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
and an accessible left exact localisation functor
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathcal{X}
.

In
1
-category theory, this definition of a topos is equivalent to the following other definition:

Definition 3.4.0.2

(
[
27
, Prop. 6.1.0.1]
)
.

In classical category theory, a category
\mathcal{C}
is called a topos if the following equivalent conditions are satisfied

•

\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to the category of sheaves of sets on a Grothendieck site.

•

\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to a left exact localisation of the category of presheaves of sets on some small category
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

•

Giraud’s axioms are satisfied.

Proposition 3.4.0.3
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\text{Poly}^{\leq n}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})
is an
\infty
-topos and

T_{n}\colon\text{Fun}(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{S})\to\text{Poly}^{\leq n}

is a left-exact localisation.

Proof.

This follows directly from
Corollary

3.2.0.10
and the definition.
∎

In contrast to the
1
-categorical case, not every
\infty
-topos arises from a Grothendieck topology. Recall that there are two classes of localisations of
\infty
-topoi:

Definition 3.4.0.4

(
[
27
]
, 6.5.2.17)
.

Let

be an
\infty
-topos and let

be an accessible left exact localisation of
X
.
Y
is a

of
X
if the localisation

does not invert any monomorphisms which were not already equivalences in
X
.

Definition 3.4.0.5

(
[
27
]
, 6.2.1.4)
.

Let
X
be an
\infty
-topos and let

be an accessible left exact localisation of
X
.
Y
is a
topological localisation
of
X
if any morphism that is inverted by the localisation

is a monomorphism in

.

Any (accessible) left exact localisation of

]
, 6.5.2.19). Topological localisations of

(

]
, 6.2.2.17).

Thus, it makes sense to ask the following question.

Answer 3.4.0.7

No.

It follows from work of Anel, Biedermann, Finster and Joyal
(
[

]
[Example 4.1.19] and
[

The Symmetric Monoidal Structure on

We want to equip

Warning 4.1.1.3

\mathcal{J}

Proof.

Let

and
V

.

The case of a coproduct of different inner product spaces works analogously after choosing an embedding of one into the other.

Proposition/Definition 4.1.1.4

Example 4.1.2.5

\text{mor}(V,-)\circledast\text{mor}(W,-)\simeq\text{mor}(V\oplus W,-)

Proof.

By the pointwise formula (

(\text{mor}(V,-)\circledast\text{mor}(W,-))(X)\simeq\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{A\oplus B\to X}\text{mor}(V,A)\times\text{mor}(W,B)

The terms on the right-hand side admit maps

\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{A\oplus B\to X}\text{mor}(V,A)\times\text{mor}(W,B)\to\text{mor}(V\oplus W,X)

This map is an equivalence. An inverse is given by

\displaystyle\text{mor}(V\oplus W,X)

\displaystyle\to\text{mor}(V,\text{im}_{f}(V))\times(W,\text{im}_{f}(W))

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{A\oplus B\to X}\text{mor}(V,A)\times\text{mor}(W,B)

\displaystyle(f\colon V\oplus W\to X)

\displaystyle\mapsto(f_{|V},f_{|W})

By

\displaystyle\simeq\lim_{0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\mathcal{F}(A,\text{sh}_{U}^{*}P)

\displaystyle\simeq_{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{internal_hom_commutes_with_products}}\mathcal{F}\left(A,\lim_{0\neq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\text{sh}_{U}^{*}P\right)

{\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\to V}A(U_{1})\vee B(U_{2})}\to{\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\to V}A(U_{1})\times B(U_{2})}\to{\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\to V}A(U_{1})\wedge B(U_{2})}

after using

{\operatorname{colim}\displaylimits_{U_{1}\oplus U_{2}\to V}A(U_{1})\vee B(U_{2})}\simeq A(V)\vee B(V)

Hence it is a cofibre sequence since the sequences

A(U_{1})\vee B(U_{2})\to A(U_{1})\times B(U_{2})\to A(U_{1})\wedge B(U_{2})

are cofibre sequences.

On the level of derivative spectra, the required splitting is induced by

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\circledast B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\times B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\circledast*\vee*\circledast B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\vee B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}

where the map
A\circledast B\to A\times B
is the product of
A\circledast B\to A\circledast*
and
A\circledast B\to*\circledast B
(and we are using the stability of
Sp^{\text{BO}(n)}
).

∎

.

Let

,

for

. Recall here that we denote by

the inverse equivalence to

Then, by Yoneda, the following calculation for arbitrary

shows that

\Theta^{n}(A\circledast_{\text{red}}B)\simeq D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\otimes M\otimes N

\displaystyle\simeq\text{Map}_{Sp^{\text{BO}(n)}}(D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\otimes M,\text{Map}_{Sp}(N,C))

Almost all of the steps are straightforward applications of the equivalence

5.4
Final Result on Derivative Spectra of Monoidal Functors

One of the questions which started this project was

For a monoidal functor
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}
, what can be said about its
n
-th derivative spectra
\Theta^{n}F
?

Using
Proposition

5.3.1.1
, we can finally give the following answer

Corollary 5.4.0.1
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}
be a monoidal functor. Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are
O(n)
-equivariant maps

\Theta^{n}F\otimes\Theta^{n}F\to\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
F
is monoidal, it lifts to pointed spaces by
Proposition

5.1.0.2
. By our main theorem (
Theorem

4.2.0.1
),
T_{n}F
and
T_{n-1}F
are monoidal. By
Corollary

5.2.0.1
, the
n
-homogeneous layer
L_{n}F
, which corresponds to
\Theta^{n}F
under the classification of homogeneous functors (
Theorem

3.3.0.6
) is monoidal. Hence there is a structure map

L_{n}F\circledast L_{n}F\to L_{n}F

Using the internal hom - adjunction and
Proposition

5.3.0.4
, we obtain

L_{n}F\to\mathcal{F}(L_{n}F,L_{n}F)\to\mathcal{F}_{*}(L_{n}F,L_{n}F)

Applying
\Theta^{n}
to this and using
Proposition

5.3.1.1
, we get a map

\Theta^{n}F\to\text{Map}_{Sp}(\Theta^{n}F,\Theta^{n}F)\otimes D_{O(n)}

As we pointed out in
Remark

5.3.1.7
(last point),
D_{O(n)}\simeq S^{\text{Ad}_{n}}
has an inverse
D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\simeq S^{-\text{Ad}_{n}}
. Thus this map is equivalent to a map

\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\to\text{Map}_{Sp}(\Theta^{n}F,\Theta^{n}F)

By the internal hom adjunction in
Sp^{\text{BO}(n)}
, this corresponds to a map

\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\otimes\Theta^{n}F\to\Theta^{n}F

which corresponds to the desired map

\Theta^{n}F\otimes\Theta^{n}F\to\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}

∎

Remark 5.4.0.2
.

These maps on derivative spectra will satisfy additional consistencies such as homotopies making

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Theta^{n}F\otimes\Theta^{n}F\otimes\Theta^{n}F}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Theta^{n}F\otimes\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}\otimes\Theta^{n}F}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}^{\otimes 2}}}

commute, since the map

m\colon F\circledast F\to F

comes with homotopies making the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F\circledast F\circledast F}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F\circledast F}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F\circledast F}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m\circledast id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id\circledast m}

commute.

Conjecture 5.4.0.3
.

For
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}_{*}
lax symmetric monoidal, these maps make
\widetilde{\Theta^{n}}F:=\Theta^{n}F\otimes D_{O(n)}^{\vee}
into a nonunital
E_{\infty}
-ring spectrum.

Proposition 5.4.0.4
.

There is a (non-equivariant) equivalence

\widetilde{\Theta^{n}}F\simeq\Omega^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\Theta^{n}F

Proof.

As

\dim O(n)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}

we have (non-equivariantly)

D_{O(n)}\simeq\Sigma^{\infty}S^{\text{Ad}_{n}}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}

thus

D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}

thus

\widetilde{\Theta^{n}}F\simeq D_{O(n)}^{\vee}\otimes\Theta^{n}F\simeq\Omega^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\Theta^{n}F

∎

5.4.0.3
would then imply

Conjecture 5.4.0.5
.

For
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}
lax symmetric monoidal,
\Theta^{1}F
is a nonunital
E_{\infty}
-ring spectrum.

Proof.

As
O(1)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2
is
0
-dimensional, the dualising spectrum
D_{O(1)}
is the sphere spectrum, hence
\widetilde{\Theta^{1}}F\simeq\Theta^{1}F
.
∎

6.2
A Potential Alternative Description of the Multiplication On
\Theta^{1}F

Remark 6.2.0.1

(Potential alternative description of a multiplication on the first derivative spectrum)
.

There is a potential alternative explicit description of a multiplication on the first derivative spectrum of a monoidal functor using the explicit construction of the homotopy fibre of a map
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
as

\mathrm{hofib}f=\{(a,p)\in X\times PY\mid p(0)=f(a)\text{ and }p(1)=y_{0}\}

Recall that (e.g. as a special case of
[
41
, Prop. 5.3]
) there is a fibre sequence

F^{(1)}(V)\to F(V)\to F(V\oplus\mathbb{R})

Applying this we get

F^{(1)}(V)=\{(a,p)\in F(V)\times PF(V\oplus\mathbb{R})\mid p(0)=i_{*}(a),p(1)=*\}

The monoidal structure on
F
provides maps

m_{V,W}\colon F(V)\times F(W)\to F(V\oplus W)

Using this one can also write down a multiplication map in the following way.

Define

\tilde{m}_{V,W}\colon F^{(1)}(V)\times F^{(1)}(W)\to\Omega F^{(1)}(V\oplus W\oplus\mathbb{R})

by

\displaystyle\tilde{m}((a,p),(b,q))=(\eta,\nu)

\displaystyle\in\Omega F^{(1)}(V\oplus W\oplus\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\subset\Omega F(V\oplus W\oplus\mathbb{R})\times\Omega PF(V\oplus W\oplus\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R})

where

\eta=i_{*}(q)\ast m_{V\oplus\mathbb{R},W}(p,b)\ast\overline{m_{V,W\oplus\mathbb{R}}(a,q)}\ast i_{*}(\overline{p})

Here,
\overline{\gamma}
for a path
\gamma
denotes the reverse path.

The construction of
\nu
, which needs to be a path in
\Omega F^{(1)}(V\oplus W\oplus\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R})
, proceeds similarly. Concretely, if
s
is the path-space variable,
\nu
can be defined by

v_{s}(t)=\begin{cases}m(p,q)(s\cdot 4t,1)&\text{if }0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{4}\\
m(p,q)(s,s\cdot 4(t-\frac{1}{4}))&\text{if }\frac{1}{4}<t\leq\frac{1}{2}\\
m(p,q)(s\cdot(1-4(t-\frac{1}{2})),s)&\text{if }\frac{1}{2}<t\leq\frac{3}{4}\\
m(p,q)(s(1-4(t-\frac{3}{4})),s)&\text{if }\frac{3}{4}<t\leq 1\end{cases}

We have now described maps

F^{(1)}(V)\times F^{(1)}(W)\to\Omega F^{(1)}(V\oplus W\oplus\mathbb{R})

Since

(\Theta^{1}F)_{n}\simeq F^{(1)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

these maps together describe a map

m\colon\Theta^{1}F\otimes\Theta^{1}F\to\Theta^{1}F\simeq\Sigma\Omega\Theta^{1}F

for a monoidal functor
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}_{*}
.

We conjecture that this map models the map

\Theta^{1}F\otimes\Theta^{1}F\to\Theta^{1}F

that is predicted by our theory (see
5.4.0.5
). If so, this much more concrete perspective might enable concrete calculations. It seems poorly suited for establishing more abstract coherence properties though.

Further Open Questions

Here are some questions that we can’t answer as of now but which might be interesting for further study.

Question 6.3.0.1

(Rational
K
-Theory of
\mathbb{Z}

.

We already mentioned in

\Theta^{1}(V\mapsto\text{BTOP}(V))\simeq A(*)\simeq K(\mathbb{S})\simeq\mathbb{S}\vee\text{Wh}^{\text{DIFF}}(*)

Question 6.3.0.2
.

Although unexplained in this work, one possible purpose of Orthogonal Calculus is to generalise the theory of characteristic classes. E.g. for
F\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathcal{S}
one can construct a cohomology class on

in case of a monoidal functor

Question 6.3.0.3

(The gluing datum in the orthogonal tower and chromatic localization)

Even if one understands all the homogeneous layers of the orthogonal tower, the question
how to reconstruct the
n
-polynomial approximations can remain difficult. In Goodwillie Calculus, the gluing data can be described as maps to certain Tate constructions, e.g. for functors to spectra, by a result of Kuhn (

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{d}F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\Delta_{d}F)^{h\Sigma_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{d-1}F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\Delta_{d}F)^{t\Sigma_{d}}}

In orthogonal calculus, a similar concrete description of the gluing data is still missing and should be investigated.

A fascinating consequence of the description via Tate constructions in Goodwillie Calculus is that, after certain chromatic localizations, i.e.
T(n)

Orthogonal calculus too can be done locally with respect to such localisations (see e.g. Taggart
