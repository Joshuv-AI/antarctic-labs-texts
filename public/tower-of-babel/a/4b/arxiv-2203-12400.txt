Tight Bounds for Repeated Balls-into-Bins
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald

Abstract
We study the repeated balls-into-bins process introduced by Becchetti, Clementi, Natale, Pasquale and Posta [ 4 ] . This process starts with m m balls arbitrarily distributed across n n bins. At each round t = 1 , 2 , … t=1,2,\ldots , one ball is selected from each non-empty bin, and then placed it into a bin chosen independently and uniformly at random. We prove the following results: • For any n ⩽ m ⩽ poly ⁡ ( n ) n\leqslant m\leqslant\mathrm{poly}(n) , we prove a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( m / n ⋅ log ⁡ n ) \Omega(m/n\cdot\log n) on the maximum load. For the special case m = n m=n , this matches the upper bound of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log n) , as shown in [ 4 ] . It also provides a positive answer to the conjecture in [ 4 ] that for m = n m=n the maximum load is ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) \omega(\log n/\log\log n) at least once in a polynomially large time interval. For m ∈ [ ω ⁡ ( n ) , n ​ log ⁡ n ] m\in[\omega(n),n\log n] , our new lower bound disproves the conjecture in [ 4 ] that the maximum load remains 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log n) . • For any n ⩽ m ⩽ poly ⁡ ( n ) n\leqslant m\leqslant\mathrm{poly}(n) , we prove an upper bound of 𝒪 ⁡ ( m / n ⋅ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log n) on the maximum load for all steps of a polynomially large time interval. This matches our lower bound up to multiplicative constants. • For any m ⩾ n m\geqslant n , our analysis also implies an 𝒪 ⁡ ( m 2 / n ) \mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n) waiting time to reach a configuration with a 𝒪 ⁡ ( m / n ⋅ log ⁡ m ) \mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m) maximum load, even for worst-case initial distributions. • For any m ⩾ n m\geqslant n , we show that every ball visits every bin in 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ​ log ⁡ m ) \mathcal{O}(m\log m) rounds. For m = n m=n , this improves the previous upper bound of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n) in [ 4 ] . We also prove that the upper bound is tight up to multiplicative constants for any n ⩽ m ⩽ poly ⁡ ( n ) n\leqslant m\leqslant\mathrm{poly}(n) .

Introduction

We consider the allocation processes involving
m
balls (jobs or data items) to
n
bins (servers or memory cells), by allowing each ball to choose from a set of randomly chosen bins. The goal is to allocate (or re-allocate) balls efficiently, while also keeping the load distribution balanced. The balls-into-bins framework has found numerous applications in
hashing, load balancing, routing (we refer to the surveys
[
26
]
and
[
31
]
for more details).

A classical sequential allocation algorithm is the
d
-Choice
process introduced by Azar, Broder, Karlin and Upfal
[
2
]
and Karp, Richard, Luby, and Meyer auf der Heide
[
21
]
, where for each ball to be allocated, we sample
d\geqslant 1
bins uniformly and then place the ball in the least loaded of the
d
sampled bins. It is well-known that for the
One-Choice
process (
d=1
), the maximum load is w.h.p.

\Theta(\log n/\log\log n)
for
m=n
and
m/n+\Theta\bigr(\sqrt{m/n\cdot\log n}\bigr)
for
m=\Omega(n\log n)
. In particular, this gap between maximum and average load grows significantly as
m/n\rightarrow\infty

heavily loaded case
. For
d=2
,
[

]
proved that the maximum load is only
m/n+\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)

m=n

[

who proved that the same guarantee also holds for

m/n\rightarrow\infty
, the difference between the maximum and average load remains a slowly growing function in
n
that is independent of
m

This improvement of
Two-Choice
over
One-Choice
has been widely known as the “power of two choices”.

In this work, we investigate the
repeated balls-into-bins (RBB)
process, introduced by Becchetti, Clementi, Natale, Pasquale and Posta
[
4
]
. In this process, there are
m
balls initially allocated arbitrarily across
n
bins. In each round, one ball is removed from each non-empty bin and then each of these balls is allocated to one bin sampled uniformly at random (see
Fig.

1
). This setting differs from the classical balls into bins setting in that the number of balls is fixed and the amount of balls we re-allocate in each round varies from
1
to
n
. Unlike
Two-Choice
(or
d
-Choice
), this re-allocation is performed without inspecting the load of any bin or taking additional samples.

Becchetti et al.
[
4
]
proved that for
m=n
, starting from an arbitrary configuration, w.h.p. after
\mathcal{O}(n)
rounds, the process reaches a maximum load of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
and remains in such a configuration for
\operatorname{poly}(n)
rounds. Thus, the RBB process is a natural instance of a self-stabilizing system, and falls into a long line of research on random-walk based algorithms for stabilization and consensus
[
5
,
15
,
18
,
19
,
28
]
. More recently, Cancrini and Posta
[
12
]
proved that the mixing time is
\mathcal{O}(L)
where
L
is the maximum load at the initial configuration.

Our Results.
In this work, we settle two conjectures stated in
[
4
]
and prove tight bounds for the more general case with
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

Becchetti et al.
[
4
]
conjectured that the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
upper bound holds for all
m=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
. They also conjectured that for
m=n
, the maximum load is
\omega(\log n/\log\log n)
. We resolve both conjectures, proving an
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
lower bound on the maximum load w.h.p. in any interval of length
\Omega(m^{2}/n^{2}\cdot\log^{4}n)
and for any
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
(
3.4
). This disproves the first conjecture, but confirms the second one, showing that for
m=n
, the maximum load is w.h.p.
\Theta(\log n)
.

For the case
m\geqslant n
, we also prove that starting from an arbitrary configuration after
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n)
rounds, w.h.p. we reach a configuration with a maximum load of
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m)
(
4.12
). For
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
, we show that the process stabilizes in such a configuration there for at least
m^{2}
rounds (
4.14
).

Becchetti et al.
[
4
]
also studied the
cover time
(or
traversal time
) of a ball, which is the time required to visit all
n
bins. For
m=n
, they proved an
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
bound on the traversal time. For any
m\geqslant n
, we improve this to
\mathcal{O}(n\log m)
, and also show that it is tight up to constant factors for any
m=\operatorname{poly}(n)
(
5.1
).

Intuition and Techniques.
For the upper bound we use an exponential potential
\Phi
with smoothing parameter
\Theta(n/m)
. Provided that
\Phi
is
\operatorname{poly}(m)
, we immediately obtain the
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m)
bound on the maximum load. Our analysis exploits that after only
\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
rounds, sufficiently many bins will become empty, which in turn will reduce the number of balls being re-allocated. This then helps to reduce the load of any non-empty bin, since these are guaranteed to lose one ball per round, but only receive in expectation less than one ball in total from the other non-empty bins. As we will prove, the actual equilibrium will have most bins being empty roughly every
\mathcal{O}(m/n)
rounds. To establish this, we employ some martingale and drift-arguments to first prove that any bin which starts at load
\mathcal{O}(m/n)
, becomes empty after
\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
rounds with constant probability
>0
. Secondly, we prove that if this happens to a fixed bin, the empty load state will be revisited
\Omega(m/n)
times during the next
\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
rounds. In some sense, this is a generalization of the approach in
[
4
]
, where they also bounded the fraction of empty bins for the case
m=n
.

A kind of reversed argument is used for the lower bound. Here, the goal is to prove that each bin is only empty every
\mathcal{O}(m/n)
rounds on average. This shows that the RBB process can be approximated by a
One-Choice
process where at least an
1-\mathcal{O}(n/m)
fraction of the balls are allocated. For
t=\Omega(m^{2}/n^{2}\cdot\log n)
, this yields a maximum load of
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
. To prove that bins are not empty “too often”, we establish a link between a quadratic potential and the number of empty bins, similar to that in
[
25
, Lemma 6.2]
. This connection essentially implies that whenever the fraction of empty bins is
\omega(n/m)
, then the quadratic potential decreases. By aggregating sufficiently over many rounds, we can conclude that, on average, the number of empty bins cannot be too large.

Further Related Work.
Cancrini and Posta investigated the behavior of the RBB process for a large number of rounds,
and established “propagation of chaos”
[
11
]
, meaning that under some conditions on the initial load distribution, the load of the bins become eventually independent. In
[
11
]
, the authors prove results for the RBB process considered here, while
[
13
]
considered more general re-allocation rules.
Another variant of the RBB setting was studied in
[
9
]
, where in each round one ball is deleted from each bin and an expected
\lambda n
new balls arrive and are distributed in parallel to the bins. In contrast to the RBB model, this means that the number of balls in the system is not fixed.

The RBB is an instance of a discrete time closed Jackson network
[
20
,
22
]
. However, in RBB, updates are happening synchronously and in parallel, while in most queuing models updates occur asynchronously based on independent point processes. As also pointed out in
[
11
,
13
]
, this leads to a non-reversible Markov Chain, which seems to make the computation of the stationary distribution intractable. Furthermore, formal methods have been used to prove guarantees for RBB with
m=n

[
3
]
. The RBB setting has also been applied to analyze protocols in short packet communications
[
32
]
.

Czumaj, Riley and Scheideler
[
16
]
studied a similar re-allocation process where in each round one random ball is allocated to a random of
d
bin choices. These are also related to randomized rerouting protocols studied in
[
8
,
10
]
. In another parallel allocation processes, Berenbrink, Czumaj, Englert, Friedetzky and Nagel
[
6
]
proved an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
gap for the
Two-Choice
process where balls are allocated in batches of
n
balls and was recently improved to
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\log\log n)
in
[
24
]
.

Notation and Definitions

We consider a set of
n
bins labeled
[n]:=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
.
By
x^{t}
we denote the
n
-dimensional
load vector
after
t
rounds, and
x^{0}
is the initial load vector. In our processes, no balls are added or removed, and the existing
m
balls are only re-allocated; hence,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{t}=m
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

By
F^{t}:=\left|\left\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{t}=0\right\}\right|
we denote the number of
empty (free) bins
and by
f^{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\cdot F^{t}
the fraction of empty bins. Similarly
\kappa^{t}:=n-F^{t}
is the number of
non-empty bins
. Since it will be important to track the number of empty bins over a time interval, we also define
F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}
as the total number of pairs of empty bins and rounds in the entire interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
, i.e.,

F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}:=\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}F^{t}.

t
) which has distribution
\mathsf{Bin}(\kappa^{t},1/n)
.

Similarly, assuming each bin acts as a FIFO queue on the incoming and departing balls, we can follow the trajectory of an arbitrary single ball. Only if the ball is at the front of its queue, it will be re-allocated to a bin chosen randomly from
[n]
in the next round. A natural question is the so-called
cover time
(or

. This is related to the well-studied
cover time
of parallel random walks on graphs, but with the constraint that only one walk can leave each vertex (=bin) at a time.

3
Lower Bound on the Maximum Load for
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)

In this section we prove a lower bound on the maximum load of
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
which holds w.h.p. every
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n^{2}\cdot\log^{4}n)
steps for any
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
. This matches the upper bound of
Section

4.5
up to multiplicative constants.

3.1
Quadratic Potential and Empty Bins

We now define the
quadratic potential function
, as

\Upsilon^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}^{t})^{2}.

We prove an important relation between the quadratic potential
\Upsilon^{t}
and the number of empty bins
F^{t}
. These relations are similar to the ones used in
[
25
]
to show that the absolute value potential is small in a constant fraction of the rounds. The key insight is that the quadratic potentials drops in expectation as soon as the fraction of empty bins is of order
\Omega(n/m)
. This will be crucial in the derivation of our lower bounds.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider the RBB setting with any
m\geqslant 1
. Then, for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}-2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F^{t}+2n.

Proof.

Let us define the binomial random variable
Z\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\kappa^{t},\frac{1}{n})
. For any bin
i\in[n]
with load
x_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}(x_{i}^{t}+z-1)^{2}\cdot\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{z}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\kappa^{t}-z}

\displaystyle=(x_{i}^{t})^{2}\cdot\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{z}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\kappa^{t}-z}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}(z-1)\cdot\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{z}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\kappa^{t}-z}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}(z-1)^{2}\cdot\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{z}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\kappa^{t}-z}

\displaystyle=(x_{i}^{t})^{2}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z\,\right]+2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z-1\,\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\,(Z-1)^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}(x_{i}^{t})^{2}+2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}-1\Big)+\kappa^{t}\cdot(\kappa^{t}-1)\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}+1

\displaystyle\leqslant(x_{i}^{t})^{2}+2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}-1\Big)+2,

having used in
(a)
that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z\,\right]=\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}
and
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{2}\,\right]=\kappa^{t}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot(1-\frac{1}{n})+(\kappa^{t})^{2}\cdot(\frac{1}{n})^{2}
, and thus

\mathbf{E}\left[\,(Z-1)^{2}\,\right]=\kappa^{t}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)+(\kappa^{t})^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}-2\cdot\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}+1=\kappa^{t}\cdot(\kappa^{t}-1)\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}+1.

Similarly for an empty bin
i\in[n]
with
x_{i}^{t}=0
, the contribution is

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}z^{2}\cdot\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{z}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\kappa^{t}-z}=\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}+\frac{\kappa^{t}\cdot(\kappa^{t}-1)}{n^{2}}.

Hence, by aggregating the contributions of the
\kappa^{t}
bins non-empty bins and the
n-\kappa^{t}
empty bins we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Upsilon^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+\sum_{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1}\left(2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}-1\right)+2\right)+\sum_{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}^{t}=0}\left(\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}+\frac{\kappa^{t}\cdot(\kappa^{t}-1)}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+\left(\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}-1\right)\cdot 2\cdot m+2\kappa^{t}+(n-\kappa^{t})\cdot 2

\displaystyle=\Upsilon^{t}-2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F^{t}+2n,

where in the last inequality we used that
\kappa^{t}\leqslant n
, which finishes the proof.
∎

3.2
Upper Bounding the Number of Empty Bins

The key insight is that the quadratic potential drops in expectation as soon as the fraction of empty bins is of order
\Omega(n/m)
. This is crucial to upper bound the number of empty bins in an interval. This relation is similar to the ones used in
[
24
,
25
]
, where an interplay between the quadratic potential and the absolute value potential was used to show that the absolute value potential is small in a constant fraction of the rounds.

The next lemma shows that for any sufficiently long interval, either there is a maximum load that is
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
or the fraction of empty bins in the interval is
\mathcal{O}(n/m)
. Note that we indeed need the interval to be long enough as starting with the perfectly balanced load vector may require several rounds to reach a gap of
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
even for the
One-Choice
process.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider the RBB process with any
n\leqslant m\leqslant n^{k}
for some constant
k\geqslant 1
and any
1\leqslant\hat{c}\leqslant n
. Then, for any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and
t_{1}:=t_{0}+\hat{c}\cdot\big(\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\big)^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\left\{F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}<\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\right\}\cup\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}>\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-e^{-\frac{\hat{c}}{18}}.

Proof.

We define for any
t\geqslant t_{0}
the sequence

Z^{t}:=\Upsilon^{t}-2\cdot(t-t_{0})\cdot n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t-1},

where
F_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}-1}=0
.
This sequence forms a super-martingale since by
3.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.Z^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Upsilon^{t+1}-2\cdot(t-t_{0}+1)\cdot n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t}~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Upsilon^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]-2\cdot(t-t_{0}+1)\cdot n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t}+2\cdot n-2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F^{t}-2\cdot(t-t_{0}+1)\cdot n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t}

\displaystyle=\Upsilon^{t}-2\cdot(t-t_{0})\cdot n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=Z^{t}.

Further, let
\tau:=\min\{t\geqslant t_{0}\colon\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}>\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\}
and consider the stopped random variable

\widetilde{Z}^{t}:=Z^{t\wedge\tau},

which is then also a super-martingale.

To prove concentration of
\widetilde{Z}^{t}
, we will now derive an upper bound on
\left|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}\right|
conditional on
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
.

Case 1
[
t\geqslant\tau
].
In this case,
\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}=Z^{(t+1)\wedge\tau}=Z^{\tau}
, and similarly,
\widetilde{Z}^{t}=Z^{t\wedge\tau}=Z^{\tau}
, so
|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}|=0
.

Case 2
[
t<\tau
]. Hence for
t
we have
\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n
and thus
A.5
implies that the biggest change in the quadratic potential is w.h.p. at most
2\cdot m\cdot\log n
and under this condition,

|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}|\leqslant 2\cdot m\cdot\log n+2n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot n\leqslant 3\cdot m\cdot\log n.

Combining the two cases above, we conclude,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}-1]}\left\{|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}|\leqslant 3\cdot m\cdot\log n\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0})\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)},

since
t_{1}-t_{0}\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

Using the concentration inequality
A.8
with bad event,

\mathcal{B}^{t}:=\neg\bigcap_{t\in[t_{0},t]}\{|\widetilde{Z}^{t+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t}|\leqslant 3\cdot m\cdot\log n\}
and
\lambda=\hat{c}\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n}\cdot\log^{2}n
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{Z}^{t_{1}+1}-\widetilde{Z}^{t_{0}}>\lambda\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(3\cdot m\cdot\log n)^{2}}\right)+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{B}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\hat{c}^{2}\cdot\Big(\frac{m^{2}}{n}\cdot\log^{2}n\Big)^{2}}{18\cdot\hat{c}\cdot\big(\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\big)^{2}\cdot(m\cdot\log n)^{2}}\right)+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{B}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-\frac{\hat{c}}{18}}+n^{-\omega(1)}\leqslant 2\cdot e^{-\frac{\hat{c}}{18}},

Thus,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{Z^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant Z^{t_{0}}+\lambda\right\}\cup\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}\,\right]<1-2\cdot e^{-\frac{\hat{c}}{18}}.

For the sake of a contradiction, assume now that

F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant\frac{4n^{2}}{m}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1).

If
Z^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant Z^{t_{0}}+\lambda
holds, then we have

\Upsilon^{t_{1}+1}-2\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\cdot n+2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t}\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+\lambda.

Rearranging the inequality above gives

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{t_{1}+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+\lambda+2\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\cdot n-2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot F_{t_{0}}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+\lambda+2\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\cdot n-8\cdot n\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Upsilon^{t_{0}}+\lambda-6\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\cdot n.

(3.1)

Recall that we start from a round
t_{0}
where
\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n
, and therefore also
\Upsilon^{t_{0}}\leqslant n\cdot\big(\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\big)^{2}
. Thus, by (
3.1
) we have

\Upsilon^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant n\cdot\Big(\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\Big)^{2}+\hat{c}\cdot n\cdot\Big(\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\Big)^{2}-6\cdot\hat{c}\cdot\Big(\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\Big)^{2}\cdot n<0

which is a contradiction for large
n
since
\hat{c}\geqslant 1
. We conclude that if
Z^{t_{1}+1}\leqslant Z^{t_{0}}+\lambda
, then
F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}<\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)
or the stopping time was reached, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}<\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\right\}\cup\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2\cdot e^{-\frac{\hat{c}}{18}}.\qed

3.3
Completing the Proof of the Lower Bound

To complete the derivation of the lower bound we need to show that in an interval of length
T=\Theta((m/n\cdot\log n)^{2})
with an
\mathcal{O}(n/m)
fraction of empty bins, the maximum load is
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
. This follows by coupling the allocations of the RBB process in the interval with a
One-Choice
process with
T\cdot(1-\mathcal{O}(n/m))
balls. By the following standard expression, for the maximum load, setting
c:=\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{200}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}
(for
\gamma=\Theta(n/m)
), we get the
\Omega(m/n\cdot\log n)
lower bound on the maximum load for the RBB setting.

Lemma 3.3

(Restated, page
A.2
)
.

Consider the
One-Choice
process for
m=cn\log n
balls where
c\geqslant 1/\log n
.
Then, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\geqslant\Big(c+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\Big)\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Putting the lemmas together, we get the desired lower bound.

Lemma 3.4
.

Consider the RBB process with any
n\leqslant m\leqslant n^{k}
for some constant
k\geqslant 1
and let
\gamma:=\frac{n}{4m}
. Then, for any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and for
t_{1}:=t_{0}+\frac{1-\gamma}{200}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\cdot\log^{4}n
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\geqslant 0.008\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

Proof.

Using
3.2
(for
k:=\frac{1-\gamma}{200}\cdot 16\cdot\log^{2}n\geqslant 3\cdot 18\cdot\log n
), we have for
t_{1}=t_{0}+\frac{1-\gamma}{200}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\cdot\log^{4}n
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}<\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\right\}\cup\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\geqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

(3.2)

Consider the
\log^{3}n
sub-intervals
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{\log^{3}n}
of length
\Delta=\frac{1-\gamma}{200}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\cdot\log n
with starting points
s_{j}:=t_{0}+\Delta\cdot(j-1)
. We also define the events for
j\in[\log^{3}n]
,

\mathcal{C}_{j}:=\left\{F_{s_{j}}^{s_{j}+\Delta}<\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot\Delta\right\}.

Running the RBB process over the interval
[s_{j},s_{j}+\Delta]
involves reallocating
\Delta\cdot n-F_{s_{j}}^{s_{j}+\Delta}
balls, meaning that we take at least
\Delta\cdot n-F_{s_{j}}^{s_{j}+\Delta}
samples uniformly at random. So if the event
\mathcal{C}_{j}
holds, then we sample in total

\Delta\cdot n-\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot\Delta=(1-\gamma)\cdot\Delta\cdot n=:\overline{m}

bins. Hence these re-allocations correspond to a
One-Choice
process with
\overline{m}
balls into
n
bins; let us denote its load vector by
y^{t}
for any round
t\geqslant 0
starting from the empty load configuration.
By
A.2
with
c:=\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{200}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}
, we obtain

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{\overline{m}}\geqslant\left(c+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\right)\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Further, note that

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{\overline{m}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{200\gamma^{2}}+\frac{1}{10}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{200\gamma^{2}}}\right)\cdot\log n

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1-\gamma}{200\gamma^{2}}\cdot\log n+0.002\cdot\frac{\log n}{\gamma}=\Delta+0.002\cdot\frac{\log n}{\gamma}.

In
\Delta
rounds, at most
\Delta
balls can be removed from any single bin, so for any bin
i\in[n]
,

x_{i}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\geqslant x_{i}^{s_{j}}+y_{i}^{\overline{m}}-\Delta\geqslant y_{i}^{\overline{m}}-\Delta.

and hence

\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\geqslant\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{\overline{m}}-\Delta\geqslant 0.002\cdot\frac{\log n}{\gamma}.

Next, we define for any round
t\geqslant 0

\mathcal{E}^{t}:=\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\geqslant 0.008\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}.

We have shown that for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant\log^{3}n
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\cup\neg\mathcal{C}_{j}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

By taking the union bound over the
\log^{3}n
sub-intervals, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\left(\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\geqslant 0.008\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n\right\}\cup\neg\mathcal{C}_{j}\right)\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-(\log^{3}n)\cdot n^{-2}.

(3.3)

Assuming that
\big\{F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}<\frac{n^{2}}{4m}\cdot(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)\big\}
holds, then using the pigeonhole principle, at least one of these intervals
j
satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{j}
, i.e.,
\big\{\cup_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{C}_{j}\big\}
holds. Hence, by the union bound of
Eq.

3.2
and
Eq.

3.3
we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\mathcal{E}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{E}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\bigcap_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\left(\mathcal{E}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\cup\neg\mathcal{C}_{j}\right)\cup\bigcap_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\neg\mathcal{C}_{j}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\left(\mathcal{E}^{s_{j}+\Delta}\cup\neg\mathcal{C}_{j}\right)\,\right]-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{j\in[\log^{3}n]}\neg\mathcal{C}_{j}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-n^{-2}-(\log^{3}n)\cdot n^{-2}\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.\qed

4
Upper Bounds on the Maximum Load and Convergence Time

In this section, we present the upper bounds on the maximum load and on the converge time for the RBB process. In
Section

4.1
, we introduce the exponential potential function for the RBB process and prove an upper bound on its expected change. In
Section

4.2
, we demonstrate its application to the simpler setting where
m<n
, where deterministically there are many empty bins in every round. In
Section

4.3
, we establish that w.h.p. for
m\geqslant n
, an
\Omega(n/m)
fraction of the bins are empty. In
Section

4.4
, we combine this with refined upper bounds on the exponential potential to show that the RBB converges w.h.p. in
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n)
rounds to a configuration with an
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m)
maximum load. In
Section

4.5
, for
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
, we show that w.h.p. it remains in a configuration with an
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log n)
maximum load for at least
m^{2}
rounds. This matches the lower of
Section

3
up to multiplicative constants.

4.1
Exponential Potential

In this subsection, we introduce the exponential potential function for the RBB process and prove an upper bound on its expected change, relating to the number of non-empty bins
\kappa^{t}
at round
t
. This bound is sufficient to obtain upper bounds for the maximum load in the case
m<n
(
Section

4.2
). In
Section

4.4
, we extend this to analyze the case
m\geqslant n
.

The
exponential potential function
with smoothing parameter
\alpha>0
, is defined as

\Phi^{t}:=\Phi^{t}(\alpha):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha x_{i}^{t}},

where
x_{i}^{t}
is the load of bin
i
at round
t
. Note that when
\Phi^{t}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
, then
\Gap(t)=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\log n}{\alpha}\big)
. We will now relate the expected change of
\Phi^{t}
over one round with the number of non-empty bins
\kappa^{t}
in that round.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider the RBB process with any
m\geqslant n
. For the potential
\Phi:=\Phi(\alpha)
with any
\alpha>0
, it holds for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}+(n-\kappa^{t})\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}.

Proof.

Consider the expected contribution of a bin
i\in[n]
with
x_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}e^{\alpha(x_{i}^{t}+z-1)}\cdot\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{z}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\kappa^{t}-z}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot\left(\frac{e^{\alpha}}{n}\right)^{z}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\kappa^{t}-z}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{e^{\alpha}}{n}\right)^{\kappa^{t}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}},

using in
(a)
the binomial identity
\sum_{z=0}^{k}\binom{k}{z}p^{z}q^{k-z}=(p+q)^{k}
and in
(b)
that
1+z\leqslant e^{z}
for any
z\geqslant 0
.

For an empty bin
i\in[n]
, its expected contribution is

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]=\sum_{z=0}^{\kappa^{t}}\binom{\kappa^{t}}{z}\cdot e^{\alpha z}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{z}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\kappa^{t}-z}=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{e^{\alpha}}{n}\right)^{\kappa^{t}}\leqslant e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}.

Aggregating over all bins, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:x_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]+\sum_{i:x_{i}^{t}=0}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}+(n-\kappa^{t})\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}.\qed

4.2
Maximum Load Upper Bound for
m<n

In this subsection, we will investigate the simpler setting where
m
is much smaller than
n
, to demonstrate the use of the exponential potential function. This implies that in each round, deterministically at least
n-m
bins are empty. As we prove below, this implies for example, that for
m=\frac{n}{\log n}
we get w.h.p. a maximum load of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
after
\mathcal{O}(\frac{n}{\log n})
rounds.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider the RBB process with
m\leqslant\frac{1}{e^{2}}n
. Then for any round
t\geqslant 2m
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\leqslant 4\cdot\frac{\log n}{\log\left(\frac{n}{em}\right)}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

We will use the potential
\Phi:=\Phi(\alpha)
with
\alpha:=\log\left(\frac{n}{em}\right)\geqslant 1
(since
m\leqslant\frac{1}{e^{2}}n
). Note that for
m=o(n)
the potential is super-exponential, as in
[
23
]
. Since
\kappa^{t}\leqslant m
, we have

\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}\leqslant e^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{m}{n}=\frac{1}{e}.

Hence, by using
4.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi^{t+1}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}+(n-\kappa^{t})\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{1/e}+e\cdot n

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}+e\cdot n,

using in the last inequality that
\alpha\geqslant 1
.

At round
t=0
we have
\Phi^{0}\leqslant e^{\alpha m}
. Hence applying
A.4
, we have for any
t\geqslant 2m
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi^{t}\,\right]\leqslant e^{\alpha m}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\alpha t}+\frac{e\cdot n}{1-e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\leqslant 1+\frac{e\cdot n}{1-e^{-1/2}}\leqslant 3e\cdot n.

By applying Markov’s inequality for any
t\geqslant 2m
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Phi^{t}\leqslant 3e\cdot n^{3}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

When
\{\Phi^{t}\leqslant 3e\cdot n^{3}\}
holds, we have for any bin
i\in[n]
,

x_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\left(\log(3e)+3\log n\right)\leqslant 4\cdot\frac{\log n}{\log\left(\frac{n}{em}\right)},

completing the proof.
∎

4.3
Lower Bounding the Number of Empty Bins for
m\geqslant n

We now proceed to the more natural and challenging case where
m\geqslant n
.

Central to our analysis is an invariant about the number of empty bins. The basic idea is inspired by
[
4
, Lemma 19]
, who proved that in case of
m=n
, for each round, a constant fraction of the bins are empty with very high probability. This is useful, as it implies a constant additive drift for the load of each non-empty bin, which will drop by a constant term
>0
in expectation.

However, for general
m\gg n
, there will be starting configurations, in which all bins remain non-empty for several rounds. Only if the process runs for a sufficiently long time, a small fraction of bins will become (and, to some extent, remain) empty. The following lemma quantifies this behavior and proves that, after a waiting time of
\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
(the square of the average load), a fraction of
\mathcal{O}(n/m)
of the bins will be empty per round on average. Hence for a time interval of length
(m/n)^{2}
, the aggregated “empty bin/round pairs” will be
\approx(m/n)^{2}\cdot n\cdot(n/m)=m
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider the RBB process with
m
balls, where
m\geqslant n
and any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. Then, for round
t_{3}:=t_{0}+744(m/n)^{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.F_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\geqslant\frac{1}{384}\cdot m~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\Omega(n)}.

First, let us remark that for the simpler case
m=n
, a stronger result was shown in
[
4
, Lemma 1]
, proving that for
any
round
t\geqslant 1
,
F^{t}=\Omega(n)
holds with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(n))
. In fact adjusting the proof in
[
4
]
slightly, the same result holds for any
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
.
Therefore, we may assume in the following proof for convenience, that
m\geqslant C\cdot n
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
(we will choose
C:=6
). Alternatively, we can also reduce the case with
m
balls for some
m\in[n,C\cdot n]
balls to the case with
C\cdot n
balls, by using the fact that
F_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
becomes stochastically smaller if we add more balls.

In order to establish
4.3
, we will relate the RBB process to a simpler process, which we call the
idealized process
. In the idealized process, we also remove one ball from each non-empty bin at each round, but we allocate exactly
n
balls, regardless of how many bins are empty.

Formally, fix any load configuration of
m
balls with load vector
x^{t_{0}}
. The load vector of the idealized process is denoted by
y^{t},t\geqslant t_{0}
and defined as follows. For any bin
i\in[n]
,
y_{i}^{t_{0}}:=x_{i}^{t_{0}}
. Further, for any
t\geqslant t_{0}
, let
Z_{1}^{t},Z_{2}^{t},\ldots,Z_{n}^{t}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
be
n
independent, uniform random samples. Then define,

\displaystyle y_{i}^{t+1}:=y_{i}^{t}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}>0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{j}^{t}=i}.

(4.1)

Note that the marginal distribution of
y_{i}^{t+1}
can be expressed as

y_{i}^{t+1}=y_{i}^{t}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}>0}+\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n).

Comparing this to the RBB process (see
Eq.

2.1
) we have the same distribution apart from that
\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)
is replaced by
\mathsf{Bin}(\kappa^{t},1/n)
. Thus we see that the
idealized process
is a bit simpler and also has the advantage that the number of balls that are added to the bins does not depend on the load configuration.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and load vector
x^{t_{0}}
, there is a coupling between the load vectors
(x^{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
and
(y^{t})_{t\geqslant t_{0}}
such that for all rounds
t\geqslant t_{0}
and for all
i\in[n]
,

x_{i}^{t}\leqslant y_{i}^{t}.

Proof.

This claim follows by induction. First, the claim holds for
t=t_{0}
by definition, as
y^{t_{0}}=x^{t_{0}}
. For the induction step, let
y^{t}
and
x^{t}
such that
x_{i}^{t}\leqslant y_{i}^{t}
for all
i\in[n]
. We define a coupling between the RBB and the idealized process such that they share the same sequence of samples
(Z_{j}^{t})_{j\in[n]}
. Then, recall that the idealized process has

y_{i}^{t+1}:=y_{i}^{t}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}>0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{j}^{t}=i},

and we define the RBB to have

x_{i}^{t+1}:=x_{i}^{t}-\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}^{t}>0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\kappa^{t}}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{j}^{t}=i}.

Hence, it follows that the number of balls that are added to
y_{i}^{t}
is at least as large as the number of balls that are added to
x_{i}^{t}
. If
x_{i}^{t}=y_{i}^{t}
, then this implies that
x_{i}^{t+1}\leqslant y_{i}^{t+1}
. Further, if
x_{i}^{t}+1\leqslant y_{i}^{t}
, then since
\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}^{t}>0}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}>0}\geqslant-1
, we also have
x_{i}^{t+1}\leqslant y_{i}^{t+1}
. This completes the induction and the lemma follows.
∎

Based on the coupling, we also define for two rounds
t_{0}\leqslant t_{3}
,

G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}:=\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}.

Note that
4.4
implies that
F_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
is stochastically larger than
G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
, therefore it suffices to analyze
G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
in the following.

Our first lemma proves that starting from any load configuration with
m
balls at time
t_{0}
, any bin
i\in[n]
whose load is about the average load, has a constant probability
>0
of reaching zero load after
\mathcal{O}(\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}})
rounds.

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider the idealized process with an arbitrary initial load configuration at time
t_{0}
with
m\geqslant 6n
balls. Let
i\in[n]
be any bin with
y_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2\cdot m/n
. Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{t_{1}\in[t_{0},t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}]}\left\{y_{i}^{t_{1}}=0\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},y_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2\cdot\frac{m}{n}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

Fix any bin
i\in[n]
with load
y_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2\cdot m/n
. Let us define the stopping time

\tau:=\min\left\{t\geqslant t_{0}\colon|y_{i}^{t}-y_{i}^{t_{0}}|\geqslant y_{i}^{t_{0}}\right\},

so
\tau
is the first time after round
t_{0}
when the load if
i
has either zeroed or doubled. Since

y_{i}^{t}=y_{i}^{t-1}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t-1}>0}+\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n),

it follows by taking expectations that for any
t\in[t_{0}+1,\tau]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.y_{i}^{t}-y_{i}^{t-1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]=0.

Hence a drift argument (
A.9
) implies that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y^{\tau}=0\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

In order to upper bound
\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau\,\right]
, we define
\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}:=\min\{y_{i}^{t},2\cdot y_{i}^{t_{0}}\}
for any
t\geqslant t_{0}
.
Note that for any
t\in[t_{0}+1,\tau]
with
\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t-1}\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}=\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t-1}-1\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)=0\,\right]=\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{n}\geqslant e^{-2n/n}=e^{-2},

using that
1-z\geqslant e^{-2z}
for any
z\in[0,0.75]
.
Similarly, if
\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t-1}=0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)\neq 0\,\right]=1-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{n}\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{e}.

This implies that
D^{t}:=\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}-\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t-1}
satisfies for any

t\in[t_{0}+1,\tau]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.(D^{t})^{2}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]\geqslant 1^{2}\cdot e^{-2}>0.

Also,
D^{t}\leqslant 0
, as
\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}-\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t-1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.y_{i}^{t}-y_{i}^{t-1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]\leqslant 0
for any
t\geqslant t_{0}
.
Recall
\tau=\min\{t\geqslant t_{0}:\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}=0\vee\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}=2\cdot\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t_{0}}\}
.
Hence by
A.10
with
s=x_{i}^{t_{0}}
and
\sigma^{2}=e^{-2}>1/9
, it follows that

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau-t_{0}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{5s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\leqslant 45s^{2}.

Using Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau-t_{0}\geqslant 180s^{2}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{4},

Hence, using the union bound and
s=x_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2(m/n)
, we conclude that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{t_{1}\in[t_{0},t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}]}\left\{y_{i}^{t_{1}}=0\right\}\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{y_{i}^{\tau}=0\right\}\cap\left\{\tau\leqslant t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}\right\}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{4}.\qed

In the next lemma, we will prove that once
y_{i}^{t}=0
occurs, then with constant probability bin
i
will have zero load in
\Omega(m/n)
further rounds until time
\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Consider the idealized process with an arbitrary load configuration at round
t_{1}
with
m\geqslant 6n
balls, such that there is a bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}^{t_{1}}=0
. Then, for round
t_{2}:=t_{1}+24\cdot(m/n)^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{1}},y_{i}^{t_{1}}=0\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

First, let us consider any round
s\geqslant t_{1}
such that
y_{i}^{s}=0
. We define

\tau(s):=\min\{t>s\colon y_{i}^{t}=0\wedge y_{i}^{t}\geqslant m/n\}.

We seek to prove that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{i}^{\tau(s)}=0\,\right]\geqslant 1-\mathcal{O}(n/m).

By
A.9
, for any
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{i}^{\tau(s)}\geqslant m/n~\Big|~y_{i}^{s+1}=\gamma\,\right]\leqslant\frac{n}{m}\cdot\gamma.

Further, as
y_{i}^{s}=0
,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{i}^{s+1}=\gamma\,\mid\,y_{i}^{s}=0\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)=\gamma\,\right]\leqslant 2^{-\gamma}
(
A.3
). Also by Markov’s inequality,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)\geqslant m/n\,\right]\leqslant n/m
. Combining the last three inequalities,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{i}^{\tau(s)}\geqslant m/n\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{m/n-1}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.y_{i}^{s+1}=\gamma\,\right|\,y_{i}^{s}=0\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.y_{i}^{\tau(s)}\geqslant m/n\,\right|\,y_{i}^{s+1}=\gamma\,\right]+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{i}^{s+1}\geqslant m/n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\gamma=1}^{n}2^{-\gamma}\cdot\gamma\cdot\frac{n}{m}+\frac{n}{m}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\cdot\frac{n}{m},

where the last inequality used the fact that
\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\infty}2^{-\gamma}\cdot\gamma\leqslant 2
.
Therefore,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{i}^{\tau(s)}=0\,\right]\geqslant 1-3\cdot\frac{n}{m}.

Next define
\rho:=\min\{t\geqslant t_{1}\colon y_{i}^{t}\geqslant m/n\}
. With that, the probability that the load
0
state is visited at least
\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}
times in the time-interval
[t_{1},\rho]
, is at least

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Bigl|\left\{t_{1}\leqslant s\leqslant\rho\colon y_{i}^{s}=0\right\}\Bigr|\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-3\cdot\frac{n}{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

(4.2)

where the last inequality used Bernoulli’s inequality, i.e., the fact that
(1+z)^{r}\geqslant 1+r\cdot z
for all
z\geqslant-1,r\geqslant 1
(which applies since
m\geqslant 6n
).

The remaining part of the proof is to show that with some constant probability
>0
,
\rho=\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
.
First recall that
y_{i}^{t}
has the following distribution,

y_{i}^{t}=y_{i}^{t-1}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t-1}>0}+\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n).

For any
t\in[t_{1},\rho]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.(y_{i}^{t}-y_{i}^{t-1})^{2}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Var}\left[\,\left.\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t-1}>0}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Var}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)\,\right]

\displaystyle=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

Further for any
t\in[t_{1},\rho]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,y_{i}^{t}-y_{i}^{t-1}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]\geqslant 0.

Hence it follows by
A.11
that,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\rho-t_{1}\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,(y_{i}^{\rho})^{2}\,\right].

(4.3)

We will now upper bound
\mathbf{E}\left[\,(y_{i}^{\rho})^{2}\,\right]
.
In each round, we will apply the principle of deferred decision to expose
Z_{j}^{t}
by looking at each of the
n
trials individually. Thus, as soon as we know that
\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{\ell}^{t}=i}\geqslant(m/n-y_{i}^{t-1})+1
for some
t
and
k
, we know that
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant m/n
and hence
\rho=t
. Conditional on
\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{\ell}^{t}=i}\geqslant(m/n-y_{i}^{t-1})+1
means that, in distribution,

y_{i}^{t}=y_{i}^{\rho}=m/n+\mathsf{Bin}(n-k,1/n),

for some
n\geqslant k\geqslant m/n-y_{i}^{\rho-1}
. Clearly, the random variable
\mathsf{Bin}(n-k,1/n)
is stochastically the largest if
k
is as small as possible. Thus we pessimistically take
k=0
, and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,(y_{i}^{\rho})^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,(m/n+\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n))^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot(m/n)^{2}+2\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,(\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n))^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\cdot(m/n)^{2},

using that
m\geqslant 6\cdot n
.

Using this in
Eq.

4.3
, and applying Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\rho-t_{1}\geqslant 24\cdot(m/n)^{2}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{4}.

(4.4)

We can now conclude the argument by a union bound.
First, we proved in (
4.2
) that with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, the load
0
state will be visited at least
\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}
times before reaching a load level larger than
\frac{m}{n}
. Secondly, the event in (
4.4
) ensures that a load level larger than
\frac{m}{n}
will be reached before round
t_{1}+24\cdot(m/n)^{2}
. Therefore, with
t_{2}=t_{1}+24\cdot(m/n)^{2}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}^{t}=0}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{4}.\qed

By combining
4.5
and
4.6
, we can easily derive the following lower bound on
\mathbf{E}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\,\right]
(and so also on
\mathbf{E}\left[\,F_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\,\right]
).

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider the idealized process with
m
balls, where
m\geqslant 6\cdot n
and any round
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. Then for round
t_{3}:=t_{0}+744(m/n)^{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{192}\cdot m.

Proof.

Decomposing, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{i\in[n]\colon y_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2\cdot m/n}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right].

In order to lower bound the last expectation for some bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant 2\cdot m/n
, let us define
t_{1}
as a stopping time, i.e.,
t_{1}:=\min\{t\colon r\geqslant t_{0}\colon y_{i}^{r}=0\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\bigcup_{r\in[t_{0},t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}]}\left(\left\{t_{1}=r\right\}\cap\left\{\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{3}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\right\}\right)~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=t_{0}}^{t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\left\{t_{1}=r\right\}\cap\left\{\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{3}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\right\}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geqslant}}\sum_{r=t_{0}}^{t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,t_{1}=r~\left|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\right.\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{1}},t_{1}=r,y_{i}^{r}=0\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{1}{4}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,t_{1}\in\left[t_{0},t_{0}+720\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}\right]~\Bigg|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{4}.

where in
(a)
we used the definition of
t_{2}:=t_{1}+24\cdot(m/n)^{2}
(see
4.6
),
(b)
we used
4.7
and in
(c)
we used
4.6
. This implies for the expectation for any bin
i
with
y_{i}^{t}\leqslant 2\cdot(m/n)
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}=0}~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{m}{n}=\frac{1}{96}\cdot\frac{m}{n}.

Since at any time, in particular at time
t_{0}
, it holds deterministically that at least half of all bins have load at most
2\cdot m/n
, it follows that

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{192}\cdot\frac{m}{n},

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Consider the idealized process with
m
balls, where
m\geqslant 6\cdot n
.
For any
\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}
, define
f:=f((Z_{i}^{t})_{t\in[t_{0},t_{3}),i\in[n]}):=G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
, where
t_{0}\leqslant t_{3}
are arbitrary rounds. This is a function of the set of independent random variables
Z_{i}^{t},i\in[n],t\in[t_{0},t_{3}]
(see
Eq.

4.1
). Then changing one
Z_{j}^{t}
changes
G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
by at most
1
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary load vector
y^{t_{0}}
. By definition of the idealized process (see, e.g.,
Eq.

4.1
), for any
i\in[n]
and
t\in[t_{0},t_{3})
,

y_{i}^{t+1}:=y_{i}^{t}-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}>0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{Z_{j}^{t}=i}.

Note that any assignment of bins
(z_{i}^{t})_{t\in[t_{0},t_{3}),i\in[n]}
to the random variables
(Z_{i}^{t})_{t\in[t_{0},t_{3}),i\in[n]}
such that
Z_{i}^{t}=z_{i}^{t}
completely determines the evolution of all
y^{t}
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{3}]
.

For any
j\in[n]
and any
r\in[t_{0},t_{3}]
, we now consider an alternative assignment of bins
\widetilde{z}_{i}^{t}
to coincide with
z_{i}^{t}
apart from
z_{j}^{r}
, i.e.,

\widetilde{z}_{i}^{t}~\begin{cases}=z_{i}^{t}&\mbox{ if $i\neq j$ or $t\neq r$, }\\
\neq z_{i}^{t}&\mbox{ if $i=j$ and $t=r$.}\\
\end{cases}

To simplify notation, let
k:=z_{j}^{r}
and
\widetilde{k}:=\widetilde{z}_{j}^{r}
; recall that
k\neq\widetilde{k}
. Also let us denote by
\widetilde{y}^{t}
the load vector determined by the assignment
\widetilde{z}
. Further, for any
\ell\in[n]
, let us denote by
e(\ell)
the
n
-dimensional unit-vector defined by
e(\ell)_{i}:=\mathbf{1}_{\ell=i}
,
i\in[n]
. Then it is clear that
\widetilde{y}^{t}=y^{t}
for any
t\in[t_{0},r)
, and

\widetilde{y}^{r}+e(k)=y^{r}+e(\widetilde{k}).

which means that the load vectors
\widetilde{y}
and
y
have a
\ell_{1}
-distance of
2
, since
k\neq\widetilde{k}
. Since
\widetilde{z}_{i}^{t}=z_{i}^{t}
for any
t\in(r,t_{3})
, it follows that both
\widetilde{y}
and
y
receive the same number of balls in each round, i.e., for any
t\in(r,t_{3})
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{z_{j}^{t}=i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{z}_{j}^{t}=i}.

The only difference may come from the terms
-\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{y}_{i}^{t}>0}
and
-\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{t}>0}
.
To this end, let us define two stopping times
\rho(k)
and
\rho(\widetilde{k})
, when the bins
k
and
\widetilde{k}
, respectively, become empty,

\displaystyle\rho(k):=\min\left\{t\geqslant r\colon\widetilde{y}_{k}^{t}=0\right\},

\displaystyle\rho(\widetilde{k}):=\min\left\{t\geqslant r\colon y_{\widetilde{k}}^{t}=0\right\}.

Note that by simple induction, for any
t\in(r,\rho(k))
,

\widetilde{y}_{k}^{t}=y_{k}^{t}+1.

At iteration
\rho(k)
,
\widetilde{y}_{k}^{t}=0
but
y_{k}^{t}=1
, so
\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{y}_{k}^{t}>0}=0
and
\mathbf{1}_{y_{k}^{t}>0}=1
and thus

\widetilde{y}_{k}^{\rho(k)+1}=y_{k}^{\rho(k)+1},

and more generally, for any
t\geqslant\rho(k)+1
,

\widetilde{y}_{k}^{t}=y_{k}^{t}.

The analogous argument holds for the stopping time
\rho(\widetilde{k})
and bin
\widetilde{k}
. Thus it follows that we compare
G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
(which is defined via the load vector
y
) and
\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
(which is defined via the load vector
\widetilde{y}
), the only difference occurs at rounds
\rho(k)
when bin
k
is empty in
\widetilde{y}
but not
y
, and at round
\rho(\widetilde{k})
when bin
\widetilde{k}
is empty in
y
but not in
\widetilde{y}
. Hence
G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
and
\widetilde{G}_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
differ by exactly one if
\rho(k)<t_{3}
and
\rho(\widetilde{k})>t_{3}
(or vice versa), while in all other cases, they are equal.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Consider the idealized process with
m
balls, where
m\geqslant 6\cdot n
. Then, for any
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and
t_{3}=t_{0}+744\cdot(m/n)^{2}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\geqslant\frac{1}{384}\cdot m~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\Omega(n)}.

Proof.

By
4.8
,
f:=f((Z_{i}^{t})_{t\in[t_{0},t_{3}],i\in[n]})=G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
,
f
is a function of
(t_{3}-t_{0}+1)\cdot n
independent random variables. By
4.7
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,f~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{192}\cdot m.

As shown in
4.8
, changing one
Z_{i}^{t}
changes
f
by at most
1
.
Hence by the Method of Bounded Differences (
A.6
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\,\right]-\lambda~\Big|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1^{2}}\right).

Choosing
\lambda=\frac{1}{384}\cdot m
yields

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}\leqslant\frac{1}{384}\cdot m~\right|~\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{2\left(\frac{1}{384}\cdot m\right)^{2}}{744(m/n)^{2}\cdot n}\right)\leqslant\exp(-\Omega(n)).\qed

As mentioned before,
4.4
implies that
F_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
is stochastically larger than
G_{t_{0}}^{t_{3}}
, hence
4.3
follows immediately from
4.9
.

4.4
Bounding the Convergence Time for
m\geqslant n

We will now bound the convergence time for
m\geqslant n
. In particular, we will analyze the exponential potential function for
\alpha=\Theta(n/m)
and show that in
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n)
rounds the process reaches a configuration with
\Phi^{t}<\frac{48}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot n
, which implies a maximum load of
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{-1}\cdot(\log(\alpha^{-1})+\log n))=\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m)
, which is
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log n)
for
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

We start by proving that potential drops in expectation when it is sufficiently large and there is a large fraction of empty bins.

Lemma 4.10
.

Consider the RBB process for any
m\geqslant n
, and the potential
\Phi:=\Phi(\alpha)
with
\alpha:=\frac{1}{2\cdot 384\cdot 744}\cdot\frac{n}{m}
. Then for any round
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}+6n.

In particular,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi^{t+1}\,\,\left|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Phi^{t}>\frac{48}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot n\right.\,\right]\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{1.5\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}.

Proof.

Using
4.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}+(n-\kappa^{t})\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}+6n,

since
e^{\frac{e^{\alpha}-1}{n}\cdot\kappa^{t}}\leqslant e^{e^{\alpha}-1}\leqslant 6
, as
\alpha\leqslant 1
and
\kappa^{t}\leqslant n
.

When
\alpha<1.75
, we have that
e^{\alpha}\leqslant 1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}
, so

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot e^{(\alpha+\alpha^{2})\cdot\frac{\kappa^{t}}{n}}+6n,

which proves the first statement.

For the second statement, in particular, since
\kappa^{t}=n\cdot(1-f^{t})
, we get

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}+6n.

Assuming that
\Phi^{t}>\frac{48}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot n
, the first term dominates the additive increase,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi^{t+1}\,\,\left|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Phi^{t}>\frac{48}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot n\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{1.5\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}\cdot e^{-0.5\alpha^{2}}+6n

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{1.5\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}\Big)+6n

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{1.5\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}-\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}\cdot e^{-\alpha}\cdot\frac{48}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot n+6n

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{1.5\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}}-6n+6n

\displaystyle=\Phi^{t}\cdot e^{1.5\alpha^{2}-\alpha f^{t}},

using in
(a)
that
e^{z}\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{2}z
for
z\in[-1,0]
and in
(b)
that
e^{-\alpha}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

We now define the event

\mathcal{E}^{t}:=\left\{\Phi^{t}\leqslant\frac{48}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot n\right\}.

When
\mathcal{E}^{t}
holds, the potential is small enough to imply a maximum load of
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m)
. When it is larger, it drops by a multiplicative factor. We now define for any
t_{0}\geqslant 0
, the
adjusted exponential potential function

\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}:=\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}(\alpha)
, is defined as
\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}}:=\Phi^{t_{0}}(\alpha)
and for any
s>t_{0}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{s}:=\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{t\in[t_{0},s)}\neg\mathcal{E}^{t}}\cdot\Phi^{s}(\alpha)\cdot e^{\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{s-1}(\alpha f^{t}-1.5\alpha^{2})}.

We will now show that it forms a super-martingale.

Lemma 4.11
.

For any
t_{0}\geqslant 0
and
\alpha>0
as defined in
4.10
, the sequence
(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{s}(\alpha))_{s\geqslant t_{0}}
forms a super-martingale.

Proof.

Consider any
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. Then we need to prove for any
s\geqslant t_{0}
that,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{s+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{s}\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{s}.

(4.5)

We continue with a case distinction.

Case A [
\mathcal{E}^{s}
holds]:
Then
\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{t\in[t_{0},s+1)}\mathcal{E}^{t}}=0
and
\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{s+1}=0
, so the inequality in
Eq.

4.5
holds.

Case B [
\mathcal{E}^{s}
does not hold]:
Then

Lemma 4.12

(Convergence)

Consider the RBB process for any
m\geqslant n
and the potential

\alpha>0

. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,F_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\geqslant 16\cdot 384\cdot 744\cdot m\cdot n\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\Omega(n)}.

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}}=\Phi^{t_{0}}.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{n}\cdot e^{\alpha m}\cdot e^{-\alpha\cdot 16\cdot 384\cdot 744\cdot m+1.5\alpha^{2}\cdot 16\cdot 384^{2}\cdot 744^{2}\cdot\frac{m^{2}}{n}}

\displaystyle=e^{n}\cdot e^{\alpha m}\cdot e^{-8\cdot n+6\cdot n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}e^{n}\cdot e^{n/(2\cdot 384\cdot 744)}\cdot e^{-2\cdot n}

\displaystyle<1,

where in
(a)
we used that for any
t\geqslant 0
,
\Phi^{t}\leqslant e^{\alpha m}
(the potential is maximized if all
m
balls are in the same bin) and in

the definition

. Since

∎

5
The Multi-Token Traversal Time

As mentioned in
[
4
]
, it is natural to regard the RBB process as a multi-token traversal problem, in which each ball should visit all bins as frequently as possible. This can be seen as a “cover time” of parallel and dependent random walks, which is the first time until each ball has been allocated at least once to every bin. In
[
4
, Corollary 1]
, a w.h.p. bound of
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
on this quantity was established (it was also shown that this bound holds even in an adversarial setting, where an adversary is able to re-allocate all tokens arbitrarily every
\mathcal{O}(n)
rounds). For the original setting without the adversary, we give the following improvement:

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider the RBB with any
m\geqslant n
. Then, with probability
1-m^{-2}
, each of the
m
balls traverses all
n
bins within
28m\cdot\log m
rounds. Furthermore, any fixed ball needs with probability at least
1-o(1)
at least
1/16\cdot m\cdot\log n
rounds until all
n
bins are traversed.

Note that for
m=\operatorname{poly}(n)
, the upper and lower bounds match each other up to constant factors.

Proof.

For the upper bound, we assume that balls are inserted into bins using the FIFO scheme, with ties among the balls being inserted into the same bin in the same round broken arbitrarily.

Fix a ball, w.l.o.g., ball
1
, and let us denote by
1\leqslant t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{\tau(1)}
the sequence of time-steps in which the ball is re-allocated to another bin, and let
\tau(1)\geqslant n-1
be the number of re-allocations (switches) until ball
1
has been in all bins. Recall that the random variable
\tau(1)
is also known as the coupon collector problem
[
27
]
, and we can use the following standard bound:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau(1)\geqslant 4n\cdot\log m\,\right]\leqslant n\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{4n\cdot\log m}\leqslant m^{-3},

(5.1)

where in the last inequality we used
m\geqslant n
.
Every time-step
t_{i}
, ball
1
switches to its
i
-th bin, it will be in the same bin as some other ball
j\neq 1
with probability
\frac{1}{n}
. This other ball
j
(if it is not reallocated), will be in front of ball
i
with probability equal
\frac{1}{n}
; if
j
is also reallocated in round
t_{i}
, then this probability is at most
\frac{1}{n}
. Thus the total delay that ball
j
causes to bin
1
before
4n\cdot\log m+1
switches can be bounded from above by

X(j):=\sum_{k=1}^{4n\cdot\log m}X_{k},

where the
X_{k}\in\{0,1\}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with parameter
1/n
. Hence
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X(j)\,\right]=4\log m
. Using a Chernoff Bound for binomial random variables,
[
27
, Theorem 4.4]
, for any
R\geqslant 6\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,X(j)\,\right]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X(j)\geqslant R\,\right]\leqslant 2^{-R},

and thus with
R=24\log m
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X(j)\geqslant 24\log m\,\right]\leqslant m^{-10}.

Taking the union bound over all other
m-1
balls
j\in[m]\setminus\{1\}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{j\in[m]\setminus\{1\}}\left\{X(j)\geqslant 24\log m\right\}\,\right]\leqslant m^{-9}.

Let
X:=\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus\{1\}}X(j)
, which is the total delay caused by other balls until ball
1
achieves
4n\cdot\log m
switches. By the above,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant 24m\cdot\log m\,\right]\leqslant m^{-9}.

(5.2)

Combining
Eq.

5.1
and
Eq.

5.2
, we conclude by the union bound for ball
1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,t_{\tau(1)}\leqslant 24m\cdot\log m+4n\cdot\log m\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{\tau(1)\geqslant 4n\cdot\log m\right\}\cap\left\{X\leqslant 24m\cdot\log m\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-m^{-3}-m^{-9}\geqslant 1-m^{-2}.

The upper bound now follows by another union bound over all balls
i\in[n]
.

We now turn to the lower bound. Again, we assume that balls are allocated using the FIFO scheme, but now we also assume that ties among the balls reaching the same bin in the same round are broken randomly.

Again, first consider ball
1
w.l.o.g. We may assume that
t_{1}=1
, meaning that ball
1
switches in the very first round. For the coupon collector problem, one can derive the following lower bound based on Chebyshev’s inequality
[
27
, Chapter 3]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\tau(1)\leqslant 1/2\cdot n\cdot\log n\,\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{\log n}.

(5.3)

Following the arguments in the upper bound, the total delay any fixed ball
j\neq 1
causes to bin
1
before
1/2\cdot n\cdot\log n
switches, can be bounded from below by

Y(j):=\sum_{k=1}^{1/2\cdot n\cdot\log n}Y_{k},

where the
Y_{k}\in\{0,1\}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with parameter
1/(2n)
(in the pessimistic case for the lower bound, ball
j
and
1
are always re-allocated in the same round). Hence
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y(j)\,\right]=1/4\cdot\log n
.
Applying the Chernoff bound
[
27
, Theorem 4.5]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y(j)\leqslant(1-\delta)\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y(j)\,\right]\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\delta^{2}/2\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y(j)\,\right]\right),

with
\delta=1/2
, we obtain

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y(j)\leqslant 1/8\cdot\log n\,\right]\leqslant n^{-1/32}.

Next define
\mathcal{I}:=\left\{j\in[m]\setminus\{1\}\colon Y(j)\leqslant 1/8\cdot\log n\right\}
. Then
\mathbf{E}\left[\,|\mathcal{I}|\,\right]\leqslant m\cdot n^{-1/32}
, and by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,|\mathcal{I}|\geqslant m/2\,\right]\leqslant n^{-1/33}.

Let
Y:=\sum_{j\in[m]\setminus\{1\}}Y(j)
be the delay caused by all other
m-1
balls before ball
1
makes
1/2\cdot n\cdot\log n+1
switches. Then by the above,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y\leqslant m/2\cdot 1/8\cdot\log n\,\right]\leqslant n^{-1/33}.

(5.4)

By the union bound of
Eq.

5.1
and
Eq.

5.4
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,t_{\tau(1)}\geqslant 1/16\cdot m\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{\log n}-n^{-1/33}\geqslant 1-o(1).\qed

7
Conclusions

We revisited the RBB process and proved that for any
m\geqslant n
w.h.p. after
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n)
rounds it achieves an
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log m)
maximum load. For
n\leqslant m\leqslant\operatorname{poly}(n)
we show that it stabilizes in a configuration with an
\mathcal{O}(m/n\cdot\log n)
maximum load, for at least
m^{2}
rounds and also prove a lower bound matching up to multiplicative constants. This resolved two conjectures in
[
4
]
. We also obtained an upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(m\cdot\log m)
on the traversal time for the balls, which was shown to be tight for any
m=\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

There are several possible extensions, such as generalizing the stabilization result for
m=n^{\omega(1)}
, determining whether the
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}/n)
convergence time is tight for
m=\omega(n)
and determining tight bounds for the maximum load when
m<n
.

Finally, as mentioned in
[
4
]
, an interesting but also challenging generalization is the RBB process on graphs. We hope that at least some of our arguments could be leveraged, for example, the insight in
4.3
that many bins become empty within
\mathcal{O}((m/n)^{2})
rounds might extend to graphs.

Appendix A

A.1
Facts about the
One-Choice
Process

In this section, we prove several basic facts about the
One-Choice
process. We start by upper bounding the quadratic potential.

Lemma A.1
.

For the
One-Choice
process for
n
balls into
n
bins, for any
t\geqslant 0

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Upsilon^{t}\leqslant 3\cdot n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

Proof.

Recall

\Upsilon^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Upsilon_{i}^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}^{t})^{2}.

Since
x_{i}^{t}
has distribution
\mathsf{Bin}(t,1/n)
for any
t\geqslant 0
,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,(x_{i}^{n})^{2}\,\right]=(1-1/n)+1=2-\frac{1}{n}
.
Hence by linearity of expectations,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Upsilon^{n}\,\right]\leqslant 2n.

Define

\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{\Upsilon_{i}^{n},\log^{2}n\},

and note that
\Upsilon^{n}=\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}
if and only if the maximum load is at most
\log n
.
Then,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Upsilon^{n}\,\right]\leqslant 2n.

Further,
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}
is a function of
n
independent random variables (the random bin choices of the
n
balls), and changing one of these choices can change
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}
by at most
(\log n)^{2}-(\log n-1)^{2}\leqslant 2\log n
. Hence by the Method of Bounded Differences (
A.6
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}-\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}\,\right]\geqslant\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}4(\log n)^{2}}\right),

and choosing
\lambda=n
yields,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}\geqslant 3n\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}\geqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}\,\right]+n\,\right]\leqslant n^{-\omega(1)}.

Further, since the maximum load is larger than
\log n
with probability
1-n^{-\omega(1)}
, we have by the union bound

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Upsilon^{n}\geqslant 3n\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{n}\geqslant 3n\right\}\cup\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{n}>\log n\right\}\,\right]\leqslant n^{-\omega(1)}+n^{-\omega(1)}=2n^{-\omega(1)}.\qed

The next standard result was also used in
[
29
, Section 4]
and is based on
[
30
]
. For convenience of the reader, we give a self-contained proof, obtaining high probability bounds.

Lemma A.2
.

Consider the
One-Choice
process for
m=cn\log n
balls where
c\geqslant 1/\log n
.
Then, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\geqslant\Big(c+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\Big)\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

In order to use the Poisson Approximation
[
27
, Chapter 5]
,
let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
be
n
independent Poisson random variables with parameter
\lambda=\frac{m}{n}=c\log n
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}\geqslant\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n}}{(\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n)!}.

Using that
z!\leqslant\sqrt{2\pi z}\left(\frac{z}{e}\right)^{z}e^{\frac{1}{12z}}
for any integer
z\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{-\lambda}\cdot\left(\frac{e\lambda}{\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n}\right)^{\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\log n}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{10\sqrt{c}}\right)^{-\lambda-\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\log n}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{10\sqrt{c}}\cdot(\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\log n-\frac{1}{10\sqrt{c}}\lambda-\frac{1}{100}\log n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{100}\log n}

Since for any
k\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=k+1\,\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=k\,\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{k+1},

we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}\geqslant\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\sqrt{\lambda}-1}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n+k\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n+\sqrt{\lambda}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{i}=\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\,\right]\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\sqrt{\lambda}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n+k}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{100}\log n}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{\sqrt{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{100}\log n}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{5\sqrt{c}}\right)^{-\sqrt{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{4\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{100}\log n}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{5}\sqrt{\log n}}

\displaystyle\geqslant e^{-\frac{1}{99}\log n}=n^{-1/99},

where the last inequality holds for sufficiently large
n
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\{Y_{i}\geqslant\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\left(1-n^{-1/99}\right)^{n}\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

Hence for
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}:=\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}Y_{i}\geqslant\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\right\}
, we have
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\,\right]\leqslant n^{-3}
. Note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is a monotone event under adding balls, and thus with
\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\geqslant\lambda+\frac{\sqrt{c}}{10}\cdot\log n\right\}
, we have by
[
27
, Corollary 5.11]
)

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\mathcal{E}\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot n^{-3}\leqslant n^{-2}.\qed

A.2
Auxiliary Probabilistic Inequalities

In this section, we prove two simple probabilistic inequalities.

Lemma A.3
.

For any
n\geqslant 8
and
\gamma\in[n]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)=\gamma\,\right]\leqslant 2^{-\gamma}.

Proof.

Note that for
\gamma\geqslant 4
, we have
\gamma!\geqslant 2^{\gamma}
. For
n\geqslant 8
, we also have that
e^{1-\frac{\gamma}{n}}\cdot\gamma!\geqslant 2^{\gamma}
for
\gamma<4
, and so for all
\gamma\in[n]
.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/n)=\gamma\,\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{n-\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{n-\gamma+1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma!}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{n-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma!}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{n-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma!}\cdot e^{-1+\gamma/n}\leqslant 2^{-\gamma},

using that
1+z\leqslant e^{z}
for any
z
and
\gamma\leqslant n
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Consider a sequence of random variables
(X^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there are
0<a<1
and
b>0
such that every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant X^{i-1}\cdot a+b.

Then for every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+\frac{b}{1-a}.

Proof.

We will prove by induction that for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}.

For
i=0
,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{0}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}
. Assuming the induction hypothesis holds for some
i\geqslant 0
, then since
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{0}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{i}\,\right]\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\cdot a+b

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}\Big)\cdot a+b

\displaystyle=X^{0}\cdot a^{i+1}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i}a^{j}.

The claims follows using that for
a\in(0,1)
,
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^{j}=\frac{1}{1-a}
.
∎

A.3
Simple Bounds for the RBB process

We will now use the upper bound for the quadratic potential for
One-Choice
(
A.1
) to upper bound the maximum change of the quadratic potential for the RBB process over one round.

Lemma A.5
.

Consider the RBB process with
m\geqslant n
balls and
n
bins. For any round
t\geqslant 0
, such that
\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n
, we have,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}|\leqslant 2\cdot m\cdot\log n+3n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

Proof.

Let
k_{i}\in[0,n]
be the number of balls that each bin receives at round
t
. For any bin
i\in[n]
with
x_{i}^{t}>0
,

\displaystyle|\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}-\Upsilon_{i}^{t}|

\displaystyle=|(x_{i}^{t}+k_{i}-1)^{2}-(x_{i}^{t})^{2}|=|2\cdot(k_{i}-1)\cdot x_{i}^{t}+(k_{i}-1)^{2}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot k_{i}+(k_{i})^{2}+1.

For any bin
i\in[n]
with
x_{i}^{t}=0
,

|\Upsilon_{i}^{t+1}-\Upsilon_{i}^{t}|=k_{i}^{2}.

Aggregating over all bins, we have

\displaystyle|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\cdot x_{i}^{t}\cdot k_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k_{i})^{2}+n.

(A.1)

Using
A.1
we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}^{2}\leqslant 3\cdot n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\omega(1)}.

Assuming that the event
\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}^{2}\leqslant 3\cdot n
holds, and since by assumption
\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n
and
m\geqslant n
, we finally conclude from
Eq.

A.1

|\Upsilon^{t+1}-\Upsilon^{t}|\leqslant 2\cdot n\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cdot\log n+3n=2\cdot m\cdot\log n+3n.\qed

Theorem A.6

(Corollary 5.2 in
[
17
]
)
.

Consider a function
f:\prod_{i\in[N]}\Omega_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
such that it satisfies the Lipschitz condition with bounds

. For independent random variables
X^{1},\ldots,X^{N}

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,f(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})\geqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,f(X^{1},\ldots,X^{N})\,\right]+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{2\cdot\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

Lemma A.7

(Azuma’s Inequality for Super-Martingales
[
17

)
.

Let
X^{0},\ldots,X^{N}
be a super-martingale satisfying

, then for any

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{N}\geqslant X^{0}+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

In order to state the concentration inequality for supermartingales conditional on a bad event not occurring, we introduce the following definitions from
[
14
]
. Consider any random variable
X
(in our case it will be the
Z^{t}
, the adjusted quadratic potential in
3.2
) that can be evaluated by a sequence of decisions
Y^{1},Y^{2},\ldots,Y^{N}
of finitely many outputs (the allocated balls). We can describe the process by a
decision tree

T
, a complete rooted tree with depth
n
with vertex set
V(T)
. Each edge
uv
of
T
is associated with a probability
p_{uv}
depending on the decision made from
u
to
v
.

We say
f:V(T)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies an
admissible condition

P
if
P=\{P_{v}\}
holds for every vertex
v
. For an admissible condition
P
, the associated bad set
\mathcal{B}^{i}
over the
X_{i}
is defined to be

\mathcal{B}^{i}=\{v\mid\text{the depth of $v$ is $i$, and $P_{u}$ does not hold for some ancestor $u$ of $v$}\}.

Theorem A.8

(Theorem 8.3 in
[
14
]
)
.

For a filtration
\mathbf{F}
,

\{\emptyset,\Omega\}=\mathfrak{F}^{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}^{1}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathfrak{F}^{N},

suppose that the random variable
X^{i}
is
\mathfrak{F}^{i}
-measurable for

. Let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{N}
denote the bad set with the following admissible condition:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{i-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant X^{i-1},

\displaystyle|X^{i}-X^{i-1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{i},

for

and for

. Then, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{N}\geqslant X^{0}+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{2}}\right)+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{B}\,\right].

We continue with three drift inequalities, whose proofs follow from some straightforward martingale techniques and the Optional Stopping Theorem.

Lemma A.9
.

Let
X^{t}
be a stochastic process on the integers
\{0,1,\ldots,M\}
, for some finite
M>0
. Let
D^{t}:=X^{t}-X^{t-1}
, and assume for all

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1},X^{t-1}>0\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 0.

Let
X^{0}>0
and define for some integer
k\geqslant 1
,
\tau:=\min\{t\geqslant 0\colon X^{t}=0\vee X^{t}\geqslant k\}
. Then,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{\tau}=0\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{X^{0}}{k}.

Proof.

First, define
Y^{t}:=X^{t\wedge\tau}
. Then,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]\leqslant Y^{t},

so
Y^{t}
is a super-martingale. Further, note that
|Y^{t}|\leqslant M
holds deterministically.
By the Optional Stopping Theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y^{\tau}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant Y^{0}=X^{0},

but also

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y^{\tau}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{\tau}=0\,\right]\cdot 0+\left(1-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{\tau}=0\,\right]\right)\cdot k.

Combining these, yields

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{\tau}=0\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{X^{0}}{k}.

∎

Lemma A.10
.

Let
X^{t}
be a stochastic process on the integers
\{0,1,\ldots,2s\}
, where
X^{0}=s
.
Define
D^{t}:=X^{t}-X^{t-1}
, and further let
\tau:=\min\{t\geqslant 0\colon X^{t}=0\vee X^{t}=2s\}
. Assume for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1},X^{t-1}>0\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sigma^{2}.

Then,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau\,\left|\,X^{0}=s\right.\,\right]\leqslant\frac{5s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Proof.

Define
Z^{t}:=(X^{t})^{2}+\lambda X^{t}+\mu\cdot t
, and recall that
D^{t}:=X^{t}-X^{t-1}
.

We now show that for
\mu:=-\sigma^{2}
and
\lambda:=-4s
, for any
t-1<\tau
,
Z^{t}
forms a submartingale,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.(X^{t-1}+D^{t})^{2}+\lambda(X^{t-1}+D^{t})+\mu t\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=(X^{t-1})^{2}+\lambda X^{t-1}+\mu\cdot t+\left(2X^{t-1}+\lambda\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle=Z^{t-1}+\mu+\left(2X^{t-1}+\lambda\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant Z^{t},

using in the last inequality that
2X^{t-1}\leqslant 4s
(for
t-1<\tau
),
\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\right.\,\right]
and
\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]\geqslant\sigma^{2}
.

The state space of
X
is finite and using
A.9
for
k=2x^{\rho}
,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{t}=0~\Big|~X^{\rho}=x^{\rho}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}
for any
t\geqslant\rho
and any
x^{\rho}\in[M+1]
, implying that
\mathbf{E}[\,\tau\,]<\infty
. Further note that
|Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}|\leqslant M^{2}+4sM+\mu<\infty
. Hence, applying the Optional Stopping Theorem,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{\tau}\,\right]\geqslant Z^{0},

and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,(X^{\tau})^{2}\,\right]-4s\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{\tau}\,\right]-\sigma^{2}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant s^{2}-4s^{2}=-3s^{2}.

(A.2)

Further, by
A.9
, with
k=2\cdot s
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,(X^{\tau})^{2}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{\tau}=0\,\right]\cdot 0^{2}+\left(1-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X^{\tau}=0\,\right]\right)\cdot(2s)^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot(2s)^{2}.

Dropping the negative term in the middle of
Eq.

A.2
and rearranging, yields,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau\,\right]\leqslant\frac{5s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.\qed

Lemma A.11
.

Let
X^{t}
be a stochastic process on the integers
\{0,1,\ldots,M\}
, for some finite
M>0
.
Define
D^{t}:=X^{t}-X^{t-1}
and assume for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 0

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sigma^{2}.

Let
X^{0}=s
, and define for some
k
with
s<k\leqslant M
, the stopping time
\tau:=\min\{t\geqslant 0\colon X^{t}\geqslant k\}
. Then,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau\,\left|\,X^{0}=s\right.\,\right]\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[\,(X^{\tau})^{2}\,\left|\,X^{0}=s\right.\,\right]-s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Proof.

Similarly to
A.10
, define

Z^{t}:=(X^{t})^{2}-\sigma^{2}\cdot t.

We now show that
Z^{t}
is a submartingale for any
t-1<\tau
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{t}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,(X^{t-1}+D^{t})^{2}-\sigma^{2}\cdot t\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]+2\cdot X^{t-1}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]+(X^{t-1})^{2}-\sigma^{2}\cdot t

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geqslant}}\sigma^{2}+0+(X^{t-1})^{2}-\sigma^{2}\cdot t

\displaystyle=Z^{t-1},

using in
(a)
that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,D^{t}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]\geqslant 0
and
\mathbf{E}\left[\,(D^{t})^{2}\,\mid\,\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]\geqslant\sigma^{2}
.

The preconditions imply that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,D^{t}>0\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t-1}\,\right]>\kappa
for some
\kappa:=\kappa(\sigma)>0
, so
\mathbf{E}[\,\tau\,]<\infty
. Further note that
|Z^{t+1}-Z^{t}|\leqslant M^{2}+\sigma^{2}
. Hence, applying the Optional Stopping Theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z^{\tau}\,\right]\geqslant Z^{0}=s^{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle s^{2}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.(X^{\tau})^{2}\,\right|\,X^{0}=s\,\right]-\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\tau\,\left|\,X^{0}=s\right.\,\right].

By re-arranging we get the claim.
∎
