A Harris Theorem for Enhanced Dissipation, and an Example of Pierrehumbert Thanks: This work has been partially supported by the National Science Foundation under grants 2108080, 2342349, 2303355, 2406853 and the Center for Nonlinear Analysis.
William Cooperman, Gautam Iyer, Seungjae Son

Abstract
. In many situations, the combined effect of advection and diffusion greatly increases the rate of convergence to equilibrium – a phenomenon known as enhanced dissipation . Here we study the situation where the advecting velocity field generates a random dynamical system satisfying certain Harris conditions . If κ \kappa denotes the strength of the diffusion, then we show that with probability at least 1 − o ⁡ ( κ N ) 1-o(\kappa^{N}) enhanced dissipation occurs on time scales of order | ln ⁡ κ | \lvert\ln\kappa\rvert , a bound which is known to be optimal. Moreover, on long time scales, we show that the rate of convergence to equilibrium is almost surely independent of diffusivity. As a consequence we obtain enhanced dissipation for the randomly shifted alternating shears introduced by Pierrehumbert ’94.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Results

We begin by stating our results.
Following this, we will survey the literature and place our work in the context of existing results.
Let
u
be a (possibly time dependent) divergence free vector on the torus,
\kappa>0
, and
\rho
solve the advection diffusion equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}\rho+u\cdot\nabla\rho-\kappa\Delta\rho=0\,,

on the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Multiplying (
1.1
) by
\rho
, using the fact that
\nabla\cdot u=0
, integrating and using the Poincaré inequality implies

(1.2)

\lVert\rho(\cdot,t)-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leqslant e^{-\lambda_{1}\kappa t}\lVert\rho_{0}-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{2}}\,,

where

(1.3)

\bar{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{0}(x)\,dx\,,

is the (constant) equilibrium solution to (
1.1
) and
\lambda_{1}>0
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the negative Laplacian.

Enhanced dissipation
is the phenomenon where solutions to (
1.1
) converge to equilibrium faster than the upper bound (
1.2
).
Our main result shows that if the flow of
u
generates a random dynamical system (RDS) that satisfies the Harris conditions (stated below), then enhanced dissipation occurs.
The enhanced dissipation rate is optimal at short times with large probability, and is almost surely
independent
of the diffusivity for long times.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose the flow of
u
generates a random dynamical system that satisfies the Harris conditions stated in Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
, below.
For any
\alpha>0
,
q<\infty
there exists
\gamma>0
and a
\kappa
-dependent random variable
D_{\kappa}
such that for every initial data
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the solution to (
1.1
) satisfies

(1.4)

\lVert\rho(\cdot,t)-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\frac{D_{\kappa}}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+\alpha}}e^{-\gamma t}\lVert\rho_{0}-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{1}}\,.

Moreover, there exist a
\kappa
-independent, deterministic, constant
\bar{D}_{q}
such that

(1.5)

\bm{E}D_{\kappa}^{q}\leqslant\bar{D}_{q}\,.

Remark 1.2
.

The bound (
1.5
) implies that for any
\beta>0
,
\bm{P}(D_{\kappa}\geqslant\kappa^{-\beta})\leqslant\kappa^{\beta q}\bar{D}_{q}
.
Using this in (
1.4
) will show that with probability at least
1-\bar{D}_{q}\kappa^{\beta q}
, we have

(1.6)

\lVert\rho(\cdot,t)-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+\alpha+\beta}}e^{-\gamma t}\lVert\rho_{0}-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{1}}\,,

for all
t\geqslant 0
, and all
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}
.

An equivalent probabilistic formulation of this result is as follows.
Consider the Markov process defined by the SDE

(1.7)

dX^{\kappa}_{t}(x)=-u(X^{\kappa}_{t}(x),t)\,dt+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,dW_{t}\,,\quad X^{\kappa}_{0}(x)=x\,,

on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Since
\nabla\cdot u=0
, the (unique) stationary distribution of
X^{\kappa}
is the Lebesgue measure.
Let
p^{\kappa}_{t}(x,y)
denote the transition density of
X^{\kappa}_{t}
, and recall the
uniform mixing time

[
LP17
,
MT06
]
is defined by

(1.8)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\infty}(X^{\kappa},\varepsilon)=\inf\Big\{t\geqslant 0\nonscript\>\Big|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\lVert p^{\kappa}_{t}(x,\cdot)-1\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<\varepsilon\Big\}\,.

It is easy to see that the
uniform mixing time
of
X^{\kappa}
satisfies

(1.9)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\infty}(X^{\kappa},\varepsilon)\leqslant\frac{C\lvert\ln\varepsilon\rvert}{\kappa}\,,

for some constant
C>0
.
Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the following uniform mixing time estimate.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose the flow of
u
generates a random dynamical system satisfying the Harris conditions (Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
, below).
For any
\alpha>0
,
q<\infty
there exists
\gamma>0
and a
\kappa
-dependent random variable
D_{\kappa}
such that

(1.10)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\infty}(X^{\kappa},\varepsilon)\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma}\ln\Big(\frac{D_{\kappa}}{\varepsilon\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+\alpha}}\Big)

almost surely.
Moreover, there exists a
\kappa
-independent (deterministic) constant
\bar{D}_{q}
such that (
1.5
) holds.

Remark 1.4
.

We clarify that
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\infty}(X^{\kappa},\varepsilon)
is random as it depends on
u
(it is, of course, independent of
W
).
For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
(independent of
\kappa
), Chebychev’s inequality (
1.5
) and (
1.10
) show that for any
\beta>0
we have

(1.11)

\bm{P}\Big(t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\infty}(X^{\kappa},\varepsilon)\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma}\ln\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+\alpha+\beta}}\Big)\Big)\geqslant 1-\bar{D}_{q}\kappa^{\beta q}\,.

Moreover, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain the
\kappa
-independent uniform mixing time bound

(1.12)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{t_{\mathrm{mix}}(X^{\kappa},\varepsilon)}{\lvert\ln\varepsilon\rvert}\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma}\,,\quad\text{almost surely}\,.

Remark 1.5
.

While Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
are easy to state (see Section
2.3
, below), they aren’t easy to verify in practice.
Recent papers
[
BBPS22
,
BCZG22
]
instead assume certain conditions which are stronger than Harris conditions, but are easier to verify.
For convenience of the reader, we state these conditions in Section
2.4
, below.

Remark 1.6

(Pulsed diffusions)
.

We can also obtain similar results for pulsed diffusions.
Namely, define the Markov process
Y^{\kappa}
by

(1.13)

Y^{\kappa}_{n+1}=\varphi_{n+1}(Y^{\kappa}_{n})+\zeta^{\kappa}_{n+1}\,,

where
\zeta^{\kappa}_{n}
are i.i.d. periodized Gaussians with variance
\kappa
, and
\varphi_{n}
is a RDS on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, independent of
\zeta^{\kappa}
.
If the RDS
\varphi_{n}
satisfies the Harris conditions (Assumption
2.1
–
2.3
), then we obtain the same mixing time bound (
1.10
) for the process
Y^{\kappa}
.
The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1.3
, and in this setting several technical steps become much simpler.

As an immediate consequence, we can show enhanced dissipation if
u
is obtained by randomly shifting and alternating sinusoidal shears.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
A>0
,
d=2
and
\zeta_{n}
a sequence of i.i.d. random variables that are uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[2n,2n+2)
define

(1.14)

u(x,t)=\begin{cases}A\sin(2\pi(x_{2}-\zeta_{2n}))\,e_{1}&t\in[2n,2n+1)\,,\\
A\sin(2\pi(x_{1}-\zeta_{2n+1}))\,e_{2}&t\in[2n+1,2n+2)\,,\end{cases}

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and
e_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector.
Then, almost surely, we have the enhanced dissipation bound (
1.4
) for a random variable
D_{\kappa}
that satisfies the
\kappa
-independent bounds (
1.5
).
Consequently, with probability at least
1-\bar{D}_{q}\kappa^{\beta q}
, the enhanced dissipation bound (
1.6
) holds.

This example was introduced by Pierrehumbert
[
Pie94
]
and
[
BCZG22
]
recently verified that it satisfies conditions that are stronger than our Harris assumptions.
As a result the Corollary
1.7
follows immediately from Theorem
1.1
and
[
BCZG22
]
(see Proposition
2.9
, below).
We mention, however, that even though
[
BCZG22
]
abstractly check Assumption
2.2
(the existence of a Lyapunov function) for (
1.14
), the system is simple enough that a Lyapunov function can be constructed explicitly.
We do this in Proposition
9.1
in Section
9
, below.

Remark 1.8
.

Instead of using a sine shear profile in (
1.14
), we can use shears with a piecewise linear profile.
In this case the results in
[
CFIN23
]
will show that Assumption
2.1
–
2.4
are satisfied, and so Theorems
1.1
,
1.3
apply and will give the enhanced dissipation bound (
1.4
), and the equivalent uniform mixing time bound (
1.10
).

1.2.
Motivation and Literature Review

We now survey the literature and place our results in the context of existing results.

Enhanced dissipation
is a phenomenon that can be observed in every day life:
Pour some cream in your coffee.
If left alone, it will take hours to mix.
Stir it a little and it mixes right away.
This effect arises due to the interaction between the advection (stirring) and diffusion, and plays an important role in many applications concerning hydrodynamic stability and turbulence and occurs on scales ranging from micro fluids to meteorological / cosmological
[
LTD11
,
Thi12
,
Are84
,
SSA04
]
.

To describe this mathematically, let
u
be the velocity field of the ambient incompressible fluid, and
\rho
denote the concentration of a passively advected solute with molecular diffusivity
\kappa>0
.
The evolution of
\rho
is governed by the advection diffusion equation (
1.1
).
For simplicity, in this paper we only consider (
1.1
) with periodic boundary conditions on the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

If the ambient fluid is incompressible, the velocity field
u
satisfies divergence free condition

(1.15)

\nabla\cdot u=0\,.

In this case, an elementary energy estimate shows (
1.2
) and hence the
L^{2}
distance of the concentration from the equilibrium distribution decreases at most exponentially with a rate proportional to
\kappa
.

Of course, (
1.2
) is only a crude upper bound.
In many practical situations one expects the convergence to happen much faster than (
1.2
).
Indeed, the advection term
u\cdot\nabla\rho
typically causes filamentation and moves energy towards small scales.
The diffusion term
\kappa\Delta\rho
damps small scales faster, and the combination of these two effects leads to enhanced dissipation – faster convergence of
\rho(\cdot,t)
to
\bar{\rho}
.

Several authors have proved enhanced dissipation by showing all solutions to (
1.1
) satisfy the decay estimate

(1.16a)

\lVert\rho(\cdot,t)-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leqslant\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{t}{T(\kappa)}-1\Big)^{+}\Big)\lVert\rho_{0}-\bar{\rho}\rVert_{L^{2}}\,,

for every
t\geqslant 0
, and some time scale
T(\kappa)
for which

(1.16b)

\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\kappa T(\kappa)=0\,.

Seminal work of Constantin et al.
[
CKRZ08
]
(see also
[
Zla10
,
KSZ08
]
) shows that if
u
is time independent, then such a
T(\kappa)
exists if and only if
u\cdot\nabla
has no eigenfunctions in
H^{1}
.
For shear flows classical work of Kelvin
[
Kel87
]
shows one can choose
T(\kappa)=\kappa^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha<1
.
There are now several results studying enhanced dissipation in more generality and for nonlinear equations (see
[
FNW04
,
Wei19
,
BCZ17
,
CZD21
,
FMN23
,
CZG23
,
ABN22
,
CH23
,
Sei23
]
).

The purpose of this paper is to further investigate the link between enhanced dissipation and mixing properties of
u
.
Recall, a velocity field
u
is said to be
exponentially mixing
if, in the absence of diffusion, a dye that is initially localized to ball of size
\varepsilon
will get spread throughout the torus in time
O(\lvert\ln\varepsilon\rvert)
(see for instance
[
SOW06
]
).
In the presence of diffusion, a dye localized to a point gets spread to a ball of size
O(\sqrt{\kappa})
in time
O(1)
.
If
u
is exponentially mixing, then this dye is spread throughout the torus by the flow in time
O(\lvert\ln\kappa\rvert)
.
As a result, in this case we expect (
1.16a
) should hold with
T(\kappa)=O(\lvert\ln\kappa\rvert)
.

Surprisingly, this is not easy to prove, and is an open question in this generality.
Currently available results
[
FI19
,
Fen19
,
CZDE20
]
show that if
u
is exponentially mixing, then one can choose
T(\kappa)=O(\lvert\ln\kappa\rvert^{2})
in (
1.16a
).
For a few specific exponentially mixing systems, available results
[
BBPS21
,
ELM23
,
ILN23
]
show that one can choose
T(\kappa)=O(\lvert\ln\kappa\rvert)
in (
1.16a
).
However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no general theorem (in discrete or continuous time) that shows that for any exponentially mixing flow one can choose
T(\kappa)=O(\lvert\ln\kappa\rvert)
in (
1.16a
).

One elementary observation is that if almost every realization of the stochastic flows
X^{\kappa}
is exponentially mixing, then one has enhanced dissipation as in (
1.16a
) with
T(\kappa)=C\lvert\ln\kappa\rvert
for some
C
that can be explicitly computed in terms of the mixing rate.
Thus, a natural question to ask is is whether or not the notion of exponentially mixing is stable with respect to
\kappa
.

Question 1.9
.

If
u
is exponentially mixing, then for sufficiently small
\kappa>0
must almost every realization of
X^{\kappa}
be exponentially mixing (with a controlled rate)?

Since the notion of exponentially mixing involves the long time behavior, it is not easy to determine the answer to Question
1.9
.
We instead look for stronger conditions on
u
which will will guarantee that for all sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, almost every realization
X^{\kappa}
is exponentially mixing (with a controlled rate).

A general principle that is well known to the Sinai school is that for
random dynamical systems (RDS)
, geometric ergodicity of the two point process implies almost sure exponential mixing (see
[
DKK04
,
BBPS22
]
, or the proof of Lemma
3.3
, below).
One could then ask whether or not this property is stable in
\kappa
.

Question 1.10
.

If the two point process associated to the flow of a random dynamical system
u
is geometrically ergodic, then is the two point processes associated to the SDE (
1.7
) also geometrically ergodic for all small
\kappa>0
?

If Question
1.10
is answered affermatively, then for all sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, must almost every realization
X^{\kappa}
be exponentially mixing.
Not surprisingly, this question is also hard to answer.
Geometric ergodicity involves questions about long time limits which are not stable as
\kappa
varies.
There is, however, a classical result of Harris
[
Har55
,
MT09
]
that proves geometric ergodicity of a Markov process provided there is a Lyapunov function, and a small set.
A version of this condition turns out to be stable in
\kappa
(see Lemma
3.1
, below) which in turn leads to almost sure exponential mixing of the flows
X^{\kappa}
(see Lemma
3.3
, below), which in turn yields Theorem
1.1
.

Finally, we mention that if
u
is regular, and uniformly bounded in time, then we must have
T(\kappa)\geqslant O(\kappa)
in (
1.16a
) (see for instance
[
MD18
,
Poo96
,
BBPS21
,
Sei22
]
).
When
u
is irregular, one can even choose
T(\kappa)=O(1)
.
This is known as
\emph{anomalousdissipation}
, and examples of this were recently proved in
[
DEIJ22
,
CCS22
,
AV23
]
.
In this paper we only consider regular velocity fields, and so are in a situation where anomalous dissipation can not ocur.

2.
Notation and Preliminaries.

In this section we set up our notational convention and state the assumptions required for Theorem
1.1
and
1.3
.

2.1.
Notation and setup.

We will now define a setup that chooses the velocity field
u
randomly from a finite dimensional family of
C^{2}
, incompressible vector fields, and repeats this choice on after time intervals of length
1
.
Let
\mathscr{M}
be a complete smooth Riemannian manifold, and
\mathscr{U}\colon\mathscr{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{2}
-function such that

(2.1)

\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathscr{U}(\xi,x,t)=0\,.

Now let
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{F}_{0},\bm{P}_{0})
be a probability space and let
\omega=(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\dots)
be a sequence of
\mathscr{M}
-valued random variables whose distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the volume measure on
\mathscr{M}
.
Define the (random) velocity field
u
by

(2.2)

u(x,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{U}(\omega_{n},x,t-n)\quad\text{when }t\in[n,n+1)\,,

where, following standard convention, we suppress the dependence of
u
on
\omega
.
The flow of
u
is defined by the ODE

(2.3)

\partial_{t}X^{0}_{t}=u(X^{0}_{t},t)\,,\qquad X^{0}_{0}=\mathrm{Id}\,.

Restricting
X^{0}
to integer times gives a Markov process, which we refer to as the RDS generated by
u
.

To define the processes
X^{\kappa}
, let
(\Omega_{W},\mathcal{F}_{W},\bm{P}_{W})
be a probability space, and
W
be a
\mathbb{T}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion on this space.
We will now consider the product space
\Omega=\Omega_{W}\times\Omega_{0}
with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{W}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{0}
and product measure
\bm{P}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{P}_{W}\otimes\bm{P}_{0}
.
By a slight abuse of notation, we will sometimes treat random variables on each of the coordinate spaces
\Omega_{0}
,
\Omega_{W}
as random variables on the product space
\Omega
by composing with the corresponding coordinate projection.
In this sense, we may treat
u,W
as independent processes on
\Omega
, and let
X^{\kappa}_{t}
be the solution of (
1.7
) on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

2.2.
Two point and projective chains.

In order to state the Harris conditions, we need to define the
two point
and
projective chains
, and formalize the dependence on the noise history.
Given
\xi=(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n-1})\in\mathscr{M}^{n}
, define
\mathscr{U}_{n}\colon\mathscr{M}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,n)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

(2.4)

\mathscr{U}_{n}(\xi,x,t)=\mathscr{U}(\xi_{m},x,t-m)\quad\text{if }t\in[m,m+1)\,.

Now define
\mathscr{X}_{n}\colon\mathscr{M}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
to be the flow of
\mathscr{U}_{n}
after time
n
.
That is, define

(2.5)

\mathscr{X}_{n}(\xi,x)=\varPhi_{n}(x)\,,

where
\varPhi
is defined by

(2.6)

\partial_{t}\varPhi_{t}=\mathscr{U}_{n}(\xi,\varPhi_{t},t)\,,\quad\varPhi_{0}=\mathrm{Id}\,.

Given
\xi\in\mathscr{M}^{n}
,
(x,v)\in T\mathbb{T}^{d}
(the tangent bundle of the torus), and
y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\lVert v\rVert=1
,
y\neq x
, we define the derivative, projective and two point maps by

(2.7)

\mathscr{A}_{n}(\xi,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}D_{x}\mathscr{X}_{n}(\xi,x)\,,\quad\hat{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(\xi,x,v)=\Big(\mathscr{X}_{n}(\xi,x),\frac{\mathscr{A}_{n}(\xi,x)v}{\lVert\mathscr{A}_{n}(\xi,x)v\rVert}\Big)

and

(2.8)

\mathscr{X}_{n}^{(2)}(\xi,x,y)=(\mathscr{X}_{n}(\xi,x),\mathscr{X}_{n}(\xi,y))\,,

respectively. Note that
\hat{\mathscr{X}_{n}}
takes values on
S\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the unit sphere bundle (see for instance
[
dC92
]
) defined by

(2.9)

S\mathbb{T}^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\{(x,v)\in T\mathbb{T}^{d}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak\lVert v\rVert=1\}\,.

The map
\mathscr{X}^{(2)}_{n}
takes values on
\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
where

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}-\Delta\,,

and

\Delta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak x=y\}
.

Notice that the RDS defined by (
2.3
) can be written in terms of
\mathscr{X}_{n}
using the identity

(2.11)

X^{0}_{n}(x)=\mathscr{X}_{n}(\underline{\omega_{n}},x)\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\underline{\omega_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(\omega_{0},\dots,\omega_{n-1})\in\mathscr{M}^{n}\,,

for
n\geqslant 1
.
We clarify that for
t\in[n-1,n)
the velocity field
u
in
(
2.3
)
is determined from
\omega_{n-1}
, the
n^{\text{th}}
term in
\omega
.
The flow map
X^{0}_{n}
, however, relies on the entire history
\underline{\omega_{n}}=(\omega_{0},\dots,\omega_{n-1})
.

We also define the derivative, projective and two point processes by

(2.12)

A^{0}_{n}(x)=\mathscr{A}_{n}(\underline{\omega_{n}},x)\,,\quad\hat{X}_{n}(x,v)=\hat{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(\underline{\omega_{n}},x,v)\,,\quad X^{0,(2)}_{n}(x,y)=\mathscr{X}^{(2)}_{n}(\underline{\omega_{n}},x,y)\,,

where as before
(x,v)\in S\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
.
We denote the
n
-step transition kernel of these processes by

(2.13)

\displaystyle P_{0}^{n}(x,A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{P}_{0}[X_{n}^{0}(x)\in A]\,,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\quad\hat{P}_{0}^{n}((x,v),A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{P}_{0}\Big[\hat{X}_{n}^{0}(x,v)\in A\Big]\,,

(2.15)

\displaystyle P_{0}^{(2),n}((x,y),A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{P}_{0}\big[X_{n}^{0,(2)}\in A\big]\,.

For brevity, when
n=1
we will drop the superscript.
The action of
P_{0}^{n}
on functions is defined defined by

(2.16)

P_{0}^{n}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}P_{0}^{n}(x,dy)f(y)=\bm{E}_{0}f(X^{0}_{n}(x))\,,

where

is the expectation with respect to the measure

The action of

and

on functions are defined similarly.

For

obtained by restricting the solution to (

Given

denote the transition probability of

That is, for

we define

As before, when

for

The two point process

is defined analogously by

2.3.
Harris Conditions.

Harris theorems typically assume the existence of a Lyapunov function and a small set, and show that the associated system is uniformly geometrically ergodic
[
MT09
,
Har55
,
HM11
]
.
We will now state these conditions for the two point chain
P^{(2)}_{0}
defined in (
2.15
).
We reiterate that our assumptions only concern the RDS generated by the flow of
u
with
\kappa=0
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The two point chain with kernel
P_{0}^{(2)}
is Feller, topologically irreducible, strongly aperiodic.

Our next assumption concerns the existence of a Lyapunov function for the two point chain near a small neighborhood of the diagonal.
To state this, let
s>0
and define a punctured neighborhood of the diagonal
\Delta(s)
by

(2.19)

\Delta(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\allowbreak 0<d(x,y)<s\}\,,

where

is the torus distance between
x
and
y
.
On the flat torus it is easy to see that for
x,y\in\Delta(1/2)
, we can find a unique

such that
\exp_{x}(v)=y
, where

denotes the exponential map (see for instance
[

]
).

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists a Lyapunov function

and constants

,
s_{*}\in(0,1/2)
such that

(2.20)

P^{(2)}_{0}V<\tilde{\gamma}V\quad\text{on }\Delta(s_{*})\,.

Moreover, there exists
p\in(0,1)
such that the function
V
is of the form

(2.21)

V(x,y)=d(x,y)^{-p}\psi\Big(x,\frac{\exp_{x}^{-1}(y)}{d(x,y)}\Big)\quad\text{on }\Delta(s_{*})\,.

where
\psi:S\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a continuous, strictly positive function.

The standard Harris Theorem
[
MT09
]
requires the assumptions

,
2.2
, and the existence of a small set for

, and shows ergodicity of the chain.
However, for Lemma

, we will need to show that for sufficiently small

there exists a small set with
\kappa
-independent bound on the minorizing measure.
We are presently unable to do this assuming only the existence of a small set for

.
We can, however, prove this under a slightly stronger condition which is not hard to verify in practice.

Assumption 2.3
.

There exist
n\geqslant 1
and
(\xi_{*},x_{*})\in\mathscr{M}^{n}\times\mathscr{\mathbb{T}}^{d,(2)}
such that the following hold.

(1)

be the density of the

-valued random variable

such that for every

with

(2)

The map

is a submersion at

Finally, we need a uniform bound on the velocity field so that the gradients of the diffeomorphisms

are controlled uniformly in the noise.

Assumption 2.4
.

The function

is
such that

(2.22)

\displaystyle\sup_{(\xi,t)\in\mathscr{M}\times[0,1]}\lVert\mathscr{U}(\xi,\cdot,t)\rVert_{C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle<\infty

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\text{and}\hskip 10.22217pt}\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,1]}\lVert\nabla_{\xi}\mathscr{U}(\cdot,x,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})}

-independent constants.
As a result, a quantitative Harris theorem

-independent rate (Lemma

Proof of Theorem

Then using (

(4.16)

\displaystyle\psi^{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\psi(X_{1}^{0}(x),\hat{\omega}(X_{1}^{0}(x),X_{1}^{0}(y)))\,,

(4.17)

\displaystyle Z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\check{X}_{1}^{0}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1}^{0}(\check{y})\,,

(4.18)

Gradient estimates on the stochastic flows

It remains to prove Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
. Lemma
4.2
is a direct result of applying Grönwall’s inequality twice to the difference
\lvert X_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-X_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert
near
t=0
and
t=1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

For the upper bound, we see that for any
\kappa\geqslant 0
and
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
,

(4.57)

\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})=\check{x}-\check{y}+\int_{0}^{t}u(X_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x}),s)-u(\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{y}),s)ds\,,

so

(4.58)

\lvert\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert\leqslant\lvert\check{x}-\check{y}\rvert+A_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert ds\,.

By Grönwall’s inequality, we have

(4.59)

\lvert\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(x)-\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(y)\rvert\leqslant e^{A_{1}}\lvert\check{x}-\check{y}\rvert\,,

where

(4.60)

A_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathscr{U}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{M}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,1])}

Similarly, for the lower bound, we see that for any
\kappa\geqslant 0
and
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
,

(4.61)

\displaystyle\check{X}_{1-t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1-t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})

\displaystyle=\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{y})-\int_{1-t}^{1}\Big(u(\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x}),s)-u(\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{y}),s)\Big)ds

(4.62)

\displaystyle=\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{y})-\int_{0}^{t}\Big(u(\check{X}_{1-s}^{\kappa}(\check{x}),s)-u(\check{X}_{1-s}^{\kappa}(\check{y}),s)\Big)ds

so

(4.63)

\lvert\check{X}_{1-t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1-t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert\leqslant\lvert\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert+A_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert\check{X}_{1-s}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1-s}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert ds\,.

By Grönwall’s inequality, we have

(4.64)

\lvert\check{x}-\check{y}\rvert\leqslant e^{A_{1}}\lvert\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{1}^{\kappa}(\check{y})\rvert\,.

Thus, setting
C_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}e^{A_{1}}
concludes the proof.
∎

To prove Lemma
4.3
, we first explicitly write down the differences
\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})
and
\check{X}_{t}^{0}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{0}(\check{y})
by using the differential equations they satisfy. Then, we use mean value theorem and Grönwall’s inequality multiple times to estimate their difference.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

For
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
, we have

(4.65)

\displaystyle\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})

\displaystyle=\check{x}-\check{y}+\int_{0}^{t}(u(\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x}),s)-u(\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{y})),s)\,ds

(4.66)

\displaystyle=\check{x}-\check{y}+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}u(\beta_{1}(s),s)\cdot(\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{y}))\,ds

for some
\beta_{1}(s)=(\beta_{1}^{1}(s),\beta_{1}^{2}(s),\ldots,\beta_{1}^{d}(s))\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, where

(4.67)

\nabla_{x}u(\beta_{1}(s),s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(\nabla u_{1}(\beta_{1}^{1}(s),s),\nabla u_{2}(\beta_{1}^{2}(s),s),\ldots,\nabla u_{d}(\beta_{1}^{d}(s),s))\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\,.

Similarly,

(4.68)

\check{X}_{t}^{0}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{0}(\check{y})=\check{x}-\check{y}+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}u(\beta_{2}(s),s)\cdot(\check{X}_{s}^{0}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{s}^{0}(\check{y}))\,ds

for some
\beta_{2}(s)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.
We define

(4.69)

S(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{\kappa}(\check{y})-(\check{X}_{t}^{0}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{t}^{0}(\check{y}))\,.

Then, by taking the difference between (
4.65

which implies

(4.71)

where

(4.72)

For each
(s,i)\in[0,t]\times\{1,\ldots,d\}

the points
\beta_{1}^{i}(s)
, and
\beta_{2}^{i}(s)
are on the line segments joining

and
\check{X}^{0}_{s}(\check{y})
, and
\check{X}^{\kappa}_{s}(\check{x})
and

\displaystyle\lvert\beta_{1}(s)-\beta_{2}(s)\rvert\leqslant\max\{

(4.74)

\displaystyle\lvert\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{y})-\check{X}_{s}^{0}(\check{x})\rvert,\lvert\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{s}^{0}(\check{y})\rvert\}

\displaystyle\bm{1}_{\{\check{W}_{1}^{*}\leqslant\alpha\}}\lvert\check{X}_{s}^{\kappa}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{s}^{0}(\check{x})\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha\sqrt{\kappa}e^{A_{1}s}

Using Grönwall’s inequality and Lemma

(4.78)

5.
A stable small set.

Next, in order to obtain the minorizing condition (
3.2
) stated in Lemma
3.1
, we will show that Assumptions
2.3
and
2.4
imply the existence of a
\kappa
-independent small set.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.3
and
2.4
hold.
Then there exist nonempty open sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
and a constant
\beta>0
such that

(5.1)

\inf_{x\in A}P_{\kappa}^{(2),n}(x,\cdot)\geqslant\beta\Leb|_{B}(\cdot)\,.

for all sufficiently small
\kappa\geqslant 0
.
Here
\Leb|_{B}
denotes the restriction of the Lebesgue measure to
B
.

To prove Lemma
5.1
, we need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f\colon B_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{1}
function such that
f(0)=0
and
\rank(Df(0))=d
.
Then there exist
\delta,s>0
such that, for any
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
Lipschitz with
\|f-g\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leqslant\delta
, we have

(5.2)

g(B_{r})\supseteq B_{s}

Here

B_{r}
denotes the open ball centered at
0
.

Proof.

By the constant rank theorem, there are diffeomorphisms
\alpha
and
\beta
which fix the origin such that
(\alpha\circ f\circ\beta)(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
. Choose
\delta>0
so that
\|(\alpha\circ f\circ\beta)-(\alpha\circ g\circ\beta)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
. Let
x_{d+1},\dots,x_{N}
be arbitrary with
x_{d+1}^{2}+\dots+x_{N}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
and define
h(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=(\alpha\circ g\circ\beta)(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},x_{d+1},\dots,x_{N})
. Then
h\colon B_{3/4}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
|h(x)-x|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}

7.
V-geometric ergodicity (Lemma
3.2
).

Given Lemma
3.1
,
V
-geometric ergodicity of the two point chains
P_{\kappa}^{(2)}
follows directly from a theorem of Harris
[
Har55
,
MT09
]
.
The usual Harris theorem, however, isn’t quantitative enough to yield (
3.3
) with
\kappa
-independent constants
C,\beta
.
We will instead use the version in
[
HM11
]
which is quantitative and can be used to prove Lemma
3.2
.

For the proof, we define the metric
\rho_{\beta}
by

(7.1)

\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}}(1+\beta V)\,d\lvert\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\rvert\,,

where
\beta\geqslant 0
,
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
are probability measures, and
\lvert\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\rvert
denotes the variation of the signed measure
\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}
.
The quantitative Harris theorem from
[
HM11
]
shows that
P^{(2),l}_{\kappa}
is a contraction under
\rho_{\beta}
, and for readers convenience we now restate this result in our context.

Theorem 7.1

(Theorem 1.3 in
[
HM11
]
)
.

Make the same assumptions as in Lemma
3.1
.
For any
\alpha_{0}\in(0,\alpha)
, and any
\gamma_{0}\in(\gamma_{3}+\frac{2K}{R},1)
, define

(7.2)

\beta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\alpha_{0}}{K}\,,\qquad\bar{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\max\Big\{1-(\alpha-\alpha_{0}),\frac{2+R\beta\gamma_{0}}{2+R\beta}\Big\}\,.

Then, for any two probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
we have

(7.3)

\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{1}P^{(2),l}_{\kappa},\mu_{2}P^{(2),l}_{\kappa})\leqslant\bar{\alpha}\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\,.

Referring to
[
HM11
]
for the proof of Theorem
7.1
, we will now prove Lemma
3.2
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

We will first show there exist constants
C>0
,
\gamma_{2}\in(0,1)
, and
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all sufficiently small
\kappa\geqslant 0
, any
\varphi:\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\lVert\varphi\rVert_{V}<\infty
, and any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(7.4)

\Big\lVert P_{\kappa}^{(2),lm}\varphi-\int\varphi\,d\pi^{(2)}\Big\rVert_{V}\leqslant C\gamma_{2}^{m}\Big\lVert\varphi-\int\varphi\,d\pi^{(2)}\Big\rVert_{V}\,.

For this, we first define a weighted norm

(7.5)

\|\varphi\|_{\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{x}\frac{\lvert\varphi(x)\rvert}{1+\beta V(x)}\,.

Then, since
V\geqslant 1
on
\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
, we see that the norms
\|\cdot\|_{V}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\beta}
are equivalent for any
\beta>0
with

(7.6)

\frac{1}{1+\beta}\|\varphi\|_{V}\leqslant\|\varphi\|_{\beta}\leqslant\frac{1}{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{V}\,.

We also note that given any two probability measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
on
\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
,

(7.7)

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{\beta}\leqslant 1}\langle\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\varphi\rangle

always holds, where
\langle\mu,\varphi\rangle
denotes the dual pairing

(7.8)

\langle\mu,\varphi\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}}\varphi\,d\mu\,.

Now, we’re ready to prove (
7.4
). From here on, we set
P=P_{\kappa}^{(2),l}
for notational simplicity.
By Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
7.1
we obtain the contraction estimate (
7.3
).
In particular, for any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
and
\mu_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\delta_{x}
,
\mu_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\pi^{(2)}
, we have

(7.9)

\rho_{\beta}(\delta_{x}P^{n},\pi^{(2)})\leqslant\bar{\alpha}^{n}\rho_{\beta}(\delta_{x},\pi^{(2)})\,.

By using (
7.7
) and then (
7.1
), we notice

(7.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert\langle\delta_{x}P^{n}-\pi^{(2)},\varphi-\langle\pi^{(2)},\varphi\rangle\rangle\rvert}{\|\varphi-\langle\pi^{(2)},\varphi\rangle\|_{\beta}}\leqslant\bar{\alpha}^{n}\rho_{\beta}(\delta_{x},\pi^{(2)})\leqslant\bar{\alpha}^{n}(1+\beta V(x)+\langle\pi^{(2)},1+\beta V\rangle)\,,

and hence

(7.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\Big\lvert(P^{n}\varphi)(x)-\langle\pi^{(2)},\varphi\rangle\Big\rvert}{1+\beta V(x)}\leqslant\bar{\alpha}^{n}(1+\langle\pi^{(2)},1+\beta V\rangle)\|\varphi-\langle\pi^{(2)},\varphi\rangle\|_{\beta}\,.

This holds for any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
so

(7.12)

\Big\lVert P^{n}\varphi-\langle\pi^{(2)},\varphi\rangle\Big\rVert_{\beta}\leqslant C_{\beta}\bar{\alpha}^{n}\|\varphi-\langle\pi^{(2)},\varphi\rangle\|_{\beta}\,.

Finally, using (
7.6
) yields (
7.4
) where
C
and
\gamma_{2}
depend on
\alpha,\gamma_{3},K,R
but not on
\kappa
. This completes the proof for (
7.4
).

The proof that (
7.4
) implies (
3.3
) is a standard argument.
For any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
and any mean
0
function
g
we note

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\frac{(P_{\kappa}^{(2)}g)(x)}{V(x)}\Big\rvert=\Big\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}}\frac{g(y)}{V(y)}\frac{V(y)}{V(x)}P_{\kappa}^{(2)}(x,dy)\Big\rvert\leqslant\|g\|_{V}\frac{(P_{\kappa}^{(2)}V)(x)}{V(x)}\leqslant(\gamma_{1}+b)\|g\|_{V}\,,

where
\gamma_{1}
and
b
are the constants defined in (

).
Thus,

(7.13)

\big\lVert P_{\kappa}^{(2)}g\big\rVert_{V}\leqslant(\gamma_{1}+b)\|g\|_{V}\,.

This and (

) imply that for any

,

(7.14)

\Big\lVert P_{\kappa}^{(2),lm+r}g\Big\rVert_{V}\leqslant C\max(\gamma_{1}+b,1)^{r}\gamma_{2}^{m}\lVert g\rVert_{V}\leqslant C\gamma_{2}^{m}\lVert g\rVert_{V}\,,

(7.15)

\Big\lVert P_{\kappa}^{(2),n}g\Big\rVert_{V}\leqslant C(\gamma_{2}^{\frac{1}{l}})^{n}\|g\|_{V}

8.
Exponential Mixing of the Stochastic Flows (Lemma
3.3
).

In general, the geometric ergodicity of the two-point chain implies almost sure exponential mixing of the random dynamical system.
To the best of our knowledge this principle was introduced in
[
DKK04
]
and was also used in
[
BBPS22
,
BCZG22
]
.
We reproduce it here keeping track of the constants introduced in the proof and their dependence on
\kappa
in order to prove that
\gamma
in (
3.4
) is
\kappa
-independent.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{Z}^{d}-\{0\}
and denote
\{e_{m}:m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
as the orthogonal basis
e_{m}(x)=e^{im\cdot x}
for
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
and denote

(8.1)

f=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d}}\hat{f}_{m}e_{m}\,,\quad g=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d}}\hat{g}_{m}e_{m}\,,

as the fourier expansions of
f
and
g
. We note that and
\hat{f}_{0}=\hat{g}_{0}=0
as
f
and
g
are mean-zero.

Now, fix
\zeta>0
and for
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d}
and
\kappa>0
, define random variables

\displaystyle N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\max\Big\{n\in\mathbb{N},~\Big\lvert\int e_{m}(x)e_{m^{\prime}}\circ X^{\kappa}_{n}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert>e^{-\zeta n}\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle K_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\max\Big\{\lvert m\rvert\vee\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert:e^{\zeta N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}>\lvert m\rvert\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{D}_{\kappa}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\max_{\lvert m\rvert,\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert\leqslant K_{\kappa}}e^{\zeta N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}\,.

Then by the definition of
N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}
and Chebyshev, we get

(8.2)

\displaystyle\bm{P}[N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}>l]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{n>l}\bm{P}\bigg[{~\Big\lvert\int e_{m}(x)e_{m^{\prime}}\circ X^{\kappa}_{n}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert>e^{-\zeta n}}\bigg]

(8.3)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{n>l}e^{2\zeta n}\bm{E}\Big\lvert\int e_{m}(x)e_{m^{\prime}}\circ X_{n}^{\kappa}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert^{2}\,.

Observe

(8.4)

\bm{E}\Big\lvert\int e_{m}(x)e_{m^{\prime}}\circ X_{n}^{\kappa}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert^{2}=\int e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}P^{(2),n}_{\kappa}e_{m}^{(2)}\,,

where

(8.5)

\displaystyle e_{m}^{(2)}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}e_{m}(x)\overline{e_{m}}(y)\,,

(8.6)

\displaystyle\pi^{(2)}(dx,dy)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\pi(dx)\pi(dy)\,.

From (
3.3
), we see

(8.7)

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\int e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}P^{(2),n}_{\kappa}e_{m}^{(2)}d\pi^{(2)}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\Big\lvert\int\Big(e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}P^{(2),n}_{\kappa}-\Big(\int e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}d\pi^{(2)}\Big)\Big)e_{m}^{(2)}d\pi^{(2)}\Big\rvert

(8.8)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\Big\lvert e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}P^{(2),n}_{\kappa}-\int e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}d\pi^{(2)}\Big\rvert\,d\pi^{(2)}

(8.9)

\displaystyle\leqslant Ce^{-\beta n}\|e_{m^{\prime}}^{(2)}\|_{V}\int Vd\pi^{(2)}=C_{V}e^{-\beta n}\,.

This implies

(8.10)

\displaystyle\bm{P}[N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}>l]\leqslant C_{V}e^{(2\zeta-\beta)l}\,,

provided

(8.11)

2\zeta-\beta<0\,.

From now on, we make additional assumptions that
\zeta
is small enough to satisfy

(8.12)

\displaystyle d+\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}<0\,,

(8.13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta q}(2\zeta-\beta)+1<-1\,,

(8.14)

\displaystyle\frac{5d}{2}+\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}<-1\,.

Equations (
8.10
) and (
8.11
) imply that
\bm{P}(N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}<\infty)=1
and we have the estimate

(8.15)

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\int e_{m}(x)e_{m^{\prime}}\circ X_{n}^{\kappa}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert\leqslant e^{\zeta N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}-\zeta n}\,,

(8.16)

\displaystyle\text{ hence }\Big\lvert\int f(x)g\circ X_{n}^{\kappa}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert\leqslant e^{-\zeta n}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\lvert\hat{f}_{m}\rvert\lvert\hat{g}_{m^{\prime}}\rvert e^{\zeta N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}\,.

Moreover, using (
8.10
) and (
8.12
), we observe that

(8.17)

\displaystyle\bm{P}[K_{\kappa}>l\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d},\lvert m\rvert>l}\bm{P}\bigg[e^{\zeta N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}>\lvert m\rvert\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert\bigg]

(8.18)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{2}}\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert^{\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{2},\lvert m\rvert>l}\lvert m\rvert^{\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}}

(8.19)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}+d-1}\sum_{n>l}^{\infty}n^{\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}+d-1}\lesssim l^{d+\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}}\,,

where the constants in the inequalities are independent of
\kappa
.
Hence
\bm{P}(K_{\kappa}<\infty)=1
.

Noting that

e^{\zeta N_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}\leqslant\widehat{D}_{\kappa}\lvert m\rvert\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert

always holds, we conclude from (
8.16
) that

(8.20)

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\int f(x)g\circ X_{n}^{\kappa}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert\leqslant\widehat{D}_{\kappa}(\underline{\omega})e^{-\zeta n}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+2}}\|g\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+2}}\,.

Finally, the same arguments in Lemma 7.1 and Section 7.3 of
[
BBPS22
]
show that for any
s,q>0
,

(8.21)

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\int f(x)g\circ X_{n}^{\kappa}(x)\pi(dx)\Big\rvert\leqslant\widehat{D}_{\kappa}(\underline{\omega})e^{-(\frac{2s\zeta}{d+4})n}\|f\|_{H^{s}}\|g\|_{H^{s}}\,.

Moreover, using (
8.10
) and (
8.19
), they show that

(8.22)

\displaystyle\bm{E}[\widehat{D}_{\kappa}^{q}]=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\bm{E}\big[1_{\{K_{\kappa}=l\}}\max_{\lvert m\rvert,\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert\leqslant l}e^{\zeta qN_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}\big]

(8.23)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\bm{P}[K_{\kappa}=l]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big\lVert\max_{\lvert m\rvert,\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert\leqslant l}e^{\zeta qN_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}\Big\rVert_{L^{2}}

(8.24)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l^{d+\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}}\bigg(\sum_{\lvert m\rvert,\lvert m^{\prime}\rvert\leqslant l}\|e^{\zeta qN_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\kappa}}\|_{L^{2}}\bigg)

(8.25)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(1+\frac{\zeta q}{\beta-2\zeta(1+q)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l^{\frac{5d}{2}+\frac{2\zeta-\beta}{\zeta}}<\infty\,,

provided (
8.13
) and (
8.14
).
This completes the proof of the theorem with the choice of
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{2s\zeta}{d+4}
which can be made independent of
\kappa
since the conditions for
\zeta
, (
8.11
)–(
8.14
), are independent of
\kappa
.
∎

9.
An explicit Lyapunov function for Pierrehumbert flows.

It was proved in Section 5 of
[
BCZG22
]
that the Pierrehumbert flows defined in Corollary
1.7
satisfy the assumptions
2.5
–
2.8
. Then by Proposition
2.9
, we see that Assumption
2.1
–
2.3
(and hence Corollary
1.7
) must hold.
However, in the case of Pierrehumbert flows, we can explicitly construct a simple Lyapunov function and verify Assumption
2.2
directly.

Proposition 9.1
.

Consider the RDS of alternating shears defined in Corollary
1.7
.
If the flow amplitude
A
(in (
1.14
)) is sufficiently large, then
there exists
s_{*},p>0
such that the function
V
defined by

(9.1)

V(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}|x-y|_{\infty}^{-p}\text{ on }\Delta(s_{*})\,,

and extended continuously to
\mathbb{T}^{d,(2)}
is a Lyapunov function that satisfies Assumption
2.2
.
(Here
\lvert z\rvert_{\infty}=\max_{i}\lvert z_{i}\rvert
.)

Proof.

In this proof, we use
C
as a generic constant that doesn’t depend on
A
or
\kappa
.
For a given
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we write
x_{1},x_{2}
as the first and second coordinates of
x
, respectively.
Let
u(x,t)
be defined as in Corollary
1.7
.

First, we note that for some small
s_{*}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and any
x,y\in\Delta(s_{*})
, we can find
\check{x},\check{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
[\check{x}]=x,[\check{y}]=y
, and

(9.2)

d(X_{2}^{0}(x),X_{2}^{0}(y))=\lvert\check{X}_{2}^{0}(\check{x})-\check{X}_{2}^{0}(\check{y})\rvert\leqslant CA^{2}\lvert\check{x}-\check{y}\rvert=CA^{2}d(x,y)<\frac{1}{2}\,,

so with slight abuse of notation, we still write
x,y,X_{2}^{0}(x),
and
X_{2}^{0}(y)
for
\check{x},\check{y},\check{X}_{2}^{0}(\check{x}),
and
\check{X}_{2}^{0}(\check{y})
, respectively.

We aim to show that there is a constant
0<\beta<1
such that, for any
(x,y)\in\Delta(s_{*})
, we have

(9.3)

\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))]\leqslant\beta V(x,y),

where
\Phi_{t}
denotes the flow map induced by
u
at time
t
.

Fix
(x,y)\in\Delta(s_{*})
. First, note that we have
|x-y|_{\infty}\leqslant CA^{2}|\Phi_{2}(x)-\Phi_{2}(y)|_{\infty}
for some large
A
and therefore
V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))\leqslant{(CA^{2})}^{p}V(x,y)
almost surely. We break into two cases.

Case I:
|x_{2}-y_{2}|\geqslant|x_{1}-y_{1}|
.

Let
E_{0}
be the event

(9.4)

E_{0}=\{\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert<2|x_{2}-y_{2}|\}\,.

Since
\lvert\Phi_{2}(x)-\Phi_{2}(y)\rvert_{\infty}\geqslant\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert_{\infty}
, we have

(9.5)

\displaystyle\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))]

\displaystyle\leqslant\bm{E}[\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert^{-p}]

(9.6)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bm{E}[\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert^{-p}\bm{1}_{E_{0}}]+\bm{E}[\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert^{-p}\bm{1}_{E_{0}^{c}}]

(9.7)

\displaystyle\leqslant{(CA^{2})}^{p}V(x,y)P(E_{0})+2^{-p}V(x,y)\,.

We will now estimate
P(E_{0})
.
For this, we use the explicit form of the vector field
u
to write

\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}=x_{1}-y_{1}+A\sin(2\pi(x_{2}-\zeta_{0}))-A\sin(2\pi(y_{2}-\zeta_{0})).

Thus
E_{0}
is contained in the event that

|x_{1}-y_{1}|+|A\sin(2\pi(x_{2}-\zeta_{0}))-A\sin(2\pi(y_{2}-\zeta_{0}))|<2|x_{2}-y_{2}|.

In view of the assumption
\lvert x_{2}-y_{2}\rvert\geqslant\lvert x_{1}-y_{1}\rvert
, the above inequality is implied by

(9.8)

|A\sin(2\pi(x_{2}-\zeta_{0}))-A\sin(2\pi(y_{2}-\zeta_{0}))|<|x_{2}-y_{2}|.

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus, the left-hand side above can be written as the convolution
\lvert\left(2\pi A\cos(2\pi\cdot)\ast\mathbf{1}_{[x_{2},y_{2}]}\right)(\zeta_{0})\rvert
.
Here
[x_{2},y_{2}]
denotes the smallest interval (mod
\mathbb{Z}
) with
x_{2}
and
y_{2}
as endpoints.
Since the derivative of
\cos(2\pi\cdot)
is bounded away from zero near the zeros of
\cos(2\pi\cdot)
, the same is true for the convolution (rescaling by
|x_{2}-y_{2}|
).
This implies that the set of
\zeta_{0}
which satisfy (
9.8
) has measure at most
CA^{-1}
.
This in turn implies
\bm{P}(E_{0})\leqslant C/A
.

Using this in (
9.7
) implies

(9.9)

\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))]\leqslant CA^{2p-1}V(x,y)+2^{-p}V(x,y)\,.

Choosing
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
A>0
sufficiently large, we can ensure

(9.10)

\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))]\leqslant 2^{-p/2}V(x,y)\,,

as desired.

Case II:
|x_{2}-y_{2}|<|x_{1}-y_{1}|
.

Let
E_{1}
be the event that
\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert\leqslant A^{-1/2}\lvert x_{1}-y_{1}\rvert
. Then
\bm{P}[E_{1}]\leqslant CA^{-1/2}
by the same argument as in the previous case. On the other hand, if
E_{2}
is the event that
\lvert\Phi_{2}(x)_{2}-\Phi_{2}(y)_{2}\rvert\leqslant 2A^{1/2}\lvert\Phi_{1}(x)_{1}-\Phi_{1}(y)_{1}\rvert
, then we similarly have
\bm{P}[E_{2}]\leqslant CA^{-1/2}
. Putting these together, we conclude

\displaystyle\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))]

\displaystyle=\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))\bm{1}_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}]+\bm{E}[V(\Phi_{2}(x),\Phi_{2}(y))\bm{1}_{(E_{1}\cup E_{2})^{c}}]

\displaystyle\leqslant{(CA^{2})}^{p}V(x,y)(CA^{-1/2})+2^{-p}V(x,y),

so for
p\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
we can choose
A>0
large enough to conclude as in the previous case.
∎
