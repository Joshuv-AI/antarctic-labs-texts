On the Pythagorean Structure of the Optimal Transport for Separable Cost Functions
G. Auricchio

Abstract
In this paper, we study the optimal transport problem induced by separable cost functions. In this framework, transportation can be expressed as the composition of two lower-dimensional movements. Through this reformulation, we prove that the random variable inducing the optimal transportation plan enjoys a conditional independence property. We conclude the paper by focusing on some significant settings. In particular, we study the problem in the Euclidean space endowed with the squared Euclidean distance. In this instance, we retrieve an explicit formula for the optimal transportation plan between any couple of measures as long as one of them is supported on a straight line.



Keywords
: Wasserstein distance, Optimal Transport, Structure of the Optimal Plan

Introduction

The Optimal Transport (OT) problem is a classical minimization problem dating back to the work of Monge
[
17
]
and Kantorovich
[
14
,
12
]
. In this problem, we are given two probability measures, namely
\mu
and
\nu
, and we search for the cheapest way to reshape
\mu
into
\nu
. The effort needed in order to perform this transformation depends on a cost function, which describes the underlying geometry of the product space of the support of the two measures. In the right setting, this effort induces a distance between probability measures.

During the last century, the OT problem has been fruitfully used in many applied fields such as the study of systems of particles by Dobrushin
[
9
]
, the Boltzmann equation by Tanaka
[
23
,
22
,
18
]
, and the field of fluidodynamics by Yann Brenier
[
7
]
. All these results pointed out that, by a qualitative description of optimal transport, it was possible to gain insightful information on many open problems. For this reason, the OT problem has become a topic of major interest for analysts, probabilists and statisticians
[
24
,
3
,
21
]
. In particular, a plethora of results concerning the uniqueness
[
11
,
8
,
10
]
, the structure
[
1
,
2
,
20
]
, and the regularity
[
16
,
6
]
of the optimal transportation plan in the continuous framework has been proved.

In this paper, we specialize the problem to the separable cost functions. A cost function is said to be separable if it is the sum of two independent pieces. On
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, this means

\displaystyle c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptc(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=c_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})+c_{2}(x_{2},y_{2}),

where
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
\textbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
. The most famous ground distances meeting this condition are the cost functions induced by
l^{p}-
norms, which are

c_{p}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=(|x_{1}-y_{1}|)^{p}+(|x_{2}-y_{2}|)^{p}.

(1)

In
[
4
]
, the authors exploited the geometry induced by this class of cost functions to reformulate the transportation problem between discrete measures as an efficient uncapacitated minimum cost flow problem. In this paper, we delve further into the properties of those flows, which we will be calling
cardinal flows
, to retrieve significant information on the Wasserstein cost and the structure of optimal transportation plans. Let
(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})
be an optimal cardinal flow between
\mu
and
\nu
for the generic separable cost function
c=c_{1}+c_{2}
and let
\zeta
be the common marginal between
f^{(1)}
and
f^{(2)}
, i.e.

\zeta=(\mathfrak{p}_{Y_{1}\times X_{2}})_{\#}f^{(1)}=(\mathfrak{p}_{Y_{1}\times X_{2}})_{\#}f^{(2)}.

In our first and main result, we show that the restriction of
f^{(1)}
to a horizontal line is an optimal transportation plan between the restriction of
\mu
and
\zeta
on the same line. Similarly, the restriction of
f^{(2)}
on any vertical line is an optimal transportation plan between the restriction of
\zeta
and
\nu
.
By expressing this property through the conditional laws of
\mu
,
\zeta
, and
\nu
, we find

W_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}W_{c_{1}}(\mu_{|x_{2}},\zeta_{|x_{2}})d\mu_{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}W_{c_{2}}(\zeta_{|y_{1}},\nu_{|y_{1}})d\nu_{1},

(2)

where
W_{c}(\mu,\nu)
is the Wasserstein cost between
\mu
and
\nu
. We call a measure
\zeta
satisfying the identity (
2
) a
pivot measure
. We show that, given an optimal cardinal flow and its pivot measure, it is possible to retrieve an optimal transportation plan. Moreover, among all the possible transportation plans, there exists one whose first and last marginals are independent given the other two.

We conclude the paper by analyzing formula (
2
) in two specifics frameworks.

In the first one the cost function is the sum of two independent distances, i.e.
c=d
is a separable distance. In this setting, the Wasserstein distance
W_{d}
inherits a weaker version of the separability, in particular, we have

W_{d}(\mu,\nu)=W_{d}(\mu,\zeta)+W_{d}(\zeta,\nu).

for any pivot measure
\zeta
.

The other case we consider is
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
c
is a cost function as in (
1
). In this case, knowing the pivot measure is enough to retrieve an optimal transportation plan. This is possible because the optimal transportation plan between one-dimensional measures has an explicit formula. In particular, we can rewrite formula (
2
) through the pseudo-inverse cumulative functions of the conditional laws of
\mu
,
\nu
, and
\zeta
and find

\displaystyle W_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\zeta}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{[0,1]}(|F^{[-1]}_{\mu_{|x_{2}}}(s)-F^{[-1]}_{\zeta_{|x_{2}}}(s)|)^{p}dsd\mu_{2}

\displaystyle\;+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{[0,1]}(|F^{[-1]}_{\zeta_{|y_{1}}}(t)-F^{[-1]}_{\nu_{|y_{1}}}(t)|)^{p}dtd\nu_{1}.

Finally, we show that, when the cost function is the squared Euclidean distance, this formula allows us to express the optimal cardinal flow (and therefore the optimal transportation plan) between a measure supported on a straight line and any other measure through a closed formula.

2.1
Basic Notions of Measure Theory

Given a Polish space
(X,d)
, we denote with
\mathcal{P}(X)
the set of all the Borel probability measures over
X
. Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, we denote with
L^{p}_{\mu}
the set of
L^{p}
integrable functions with respect to the measure
\mu
. Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, we denote with
\mu\otimes\nu
the direct product measure of
\mu
and
\nu
. We say that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
has finite
p-
th moment if, given any
x_{0}\in X
,

\int_{X}d(x,x_{0})^{p}d\mu<+\infty.

Finally, we denote with
P_{p}(X)
the set of measures with finite
p-
th moment.

Definition 1

(Push-forward of measures)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two Polish spaces,
T:X\to Y
a measurable function, and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. The push-forward measure of
\mu
through
T
is defined as

T_{\#}\mu(B):=\mu(T^{-1}(B))

for each Borel set
B\subset Y
.

Remark 1
.

Given a measurable function
T:X\to Y
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, there is an integral equation that characterize the push-forward measure
T_{\#}\mu
. Given any measurable function
\phi
on
Y
the push-forward measure of
\mu
through
T
is the only probability measure
\nu
satisfying the identity

\int_{X}\phi\circ Td\mu=\int_{Y}\phi d\nu.

(3)

Lemma 1

(Chain Rule for Push-forwards)
.

Let
X
,
Y
, and
Z
be three Polish spaces and let
T_{1}:X\to Y
,
T_{2}:Y\to Z
be two measurable functions. Given any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, it holds true the chain rule

(T_{2}\circ T_{1})_{\#}\mu=(T_{2})_{\#}(T_{1})_{\#}\mu.

Let us assume that the Polish space
X
is the direct product of two Polish spaces
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. The projections over
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are then defined as

(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{1}})(\textbf{x}):=x_{1}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}})(\textbf{x}):=x_{2}

respectively, where
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
is a generic point of
X
. Those functions are continuous (and hence measurable).

Definition 2

(Marginal Probabilities)
.

Let
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
be a Polish space and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. The first marginal of
\mu
is the probability measure
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(X_{1})
defined as

\mu_{1}:=(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{1}})_{\#}\mu.

Similarly, the second marginal of
\mu
is the probability measure
\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(X_{2})
defined as

\mu_{2}:=(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}})_{\#}\mu.

Lemma 2

(Gluing Lemma)
.

For
i=1,2,3
, let
X_{i}
be Polish spaces and
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(X_{i})
. Moreover, let us take
\pi_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(X_{1}\times X_{2})
such that

(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{1}})_{\#}\pi_{1}=\mu_{1}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}})_{\#}\pi_{1}=\mu_{2}

and
\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(X_{2}\times X_{3})
such that

(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}})_{\#}\pi_{2}=\mu_{2}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{3}})_{\#}\pi_{2}=\mu_{3}.

Then, there exists
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3})
such that

(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{1}\times X_{2}})_{\#}\pi=\pi_{1}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}\times X_{3}})_{\#}\pi=\pi_{2}.

Definition 3

(Disintegration of a Measure)
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a measurable function and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X).
We say that a family
\{\mu_{y}\}_{y\in Y}
is a disintegration of
\mu
according to
f
if every
\mu_{y}
is a probability measure concentrated on
f^{-1}(\{y\})
such that

y\to\int_{X}\phi d\mu_{y}

is a measurable function and

\int_{X}\phi d\mu=\int_{Y}\bigg(\int_{X}\phi d\mu_{y}\bigg)d(f_{\#}\mu)

(4)

for every
\phi\in C(X)
. With a slight abuse of notation, the disintegration of
\mu
is also written as

\mu=\mu_{y}\otimes\nu

(5)

where
\nu=f_{\#}\mu.

Whenever
f
is measurable and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, the disintegration of
\mu
with respect to
f
exists (see
[
5
]
, Chapter
10
). In particular, if
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
and
f=(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{1}})
, formula (
5
) reads as

\mu=\mu_{|x_{1}}\otimes\mu_{1}.

The measure
\mu_{|x_{1}}
is called conditional law of
\mu
given
x_{1}
.

2.2
The Optimal Transport Problem

The first formulation of the transportation problem is the one due to Monge and, in modern language, to Kantorovich. In
[
13
]
, the author modelized the transshipment of mass through a probability measure over the product space
X\times Y
. He called those measures transportation plans.

Definition 4

(Transportation Plan)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two measures over two Polish spaces
X
and
Y
. The probability measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
is a transportation plan between
\mu
and
\nu
if

(\mathfrak{p}_{X})_{\#}\pi=\mu\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt(\mathfrak{p}_{Y})_{\#}\pi=\nu.

We denote with
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the set of all the transportation plans between
\mu
and
\nu.

Definition 5

(Transportation Functional)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, and let
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lower semi-continuous function such that there exist two upper semi-continuous functions
a\in L^{1}_{\mu}
and
b\in L^{1}_{\nu}
such that

c(x,y)\geq a(x)+b(x)

(6)

for each
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. The transportation functional
\mathbb{T}_{c}:\Pi(\mu,\nu)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is defined as

\mathbb{T}_{c}(\pi):=\int_{X\times Y}cd\pi.

(7)

The conditions asked to the cost function in Definition
5
are the minimal ones for which it makes sense defining the integral in (
7
). Ensured those conditions, we define the following minimum problem.

Definition 6

(Minimal Transportation Cost)
.

Let us take a cost function
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
as in Definition
5
. The minimal transportation cost functional
W_{c}:\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is defined as

(\mu,\nu)\to W_{c}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{T}_{c}(\pi).

(8)

The value
W_{c}(\mu,\nu)
is also called Wasserstein cost between
\mu
and
\nu
.

By making further assumptions on
c
, it is possible to prove that the infimum in (
8
) is a minimum. In particular, when the cost function is non negative, a minimizing solution exists. We denote with
\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
the set of minimizers. For a complete discussion on the existence of the solution, we refer to
[
24
, Chapter 4]
.

Lemma 3

(Measurable Selection of Plans, Villani
[
24
]
, Chapter 5, Corollary 5.22)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two Polish spaces and
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
a continuous cost function such that
\inf c>-\infty
. Given
\Omega
a Polish space and
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, consider a measurable map

\omega\to(\mu_{\omega},\nu_{\omega})

that goes from
\Omega
to
\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)
. Then there is a measurable choice

\omega\to\pi_{\omega}

where for each
\omega
,
\pi_{\omega}
is the optimal transportation plan between
\mu_{\omega}
and
\nu_{\omega}.

2.2.1
One Dimensional Case

When both the measures
\mu
and
\nu
are supported on
\mathbb{R}
and the cost function
c
is convex, the solution exists, is unique, and is characterized by the pseudo-inverse function of
\mu
and
\nu
.

Definition 7
.

Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, the co-monotone transportation plan
\gamma_{mon}
between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

\gamma_{mon}:=(F^{[-1]}_{\mu},F^{[-1]}_{\nu})_{\#}\mathcal{L}_{|[0,1]},

where
F^{[-1]}_{\mu}
and
F^{[-1]}_{\nu}
are the pseudo-inverse of the cumulative functions of
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively, and
\mathcal{L}_{|[0,1]}
is the Lebesgue measure restricted on
[0,1]
.

Theorem 1

(Optimality of the co-monotone plan, Santambrogio
[
21
]
, Chapter 2, Theorem 2.9)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a strictly convex function such that
h(0)=0
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. Consider the cost

c(x,y)=h(|x-y|)

and suppose that this cost is feasible for the transportation problem. Then the Optimal Transportation problem has a unique solution which is
\gamma_{mon}
.

Knowing how to express the optimal transportation plan, allow us to express the Wasserstein cost through the pseudo-inverse of the cumulative functions of
\mu
and
\nu
.

Corollary 1

(Santambrogio
[
21
]
, Chapter 2, Proposition 2.17)
.

Let us take
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. If
c(x,y)=(|x-y|)^{p},
with
p\geq 1
, then

W_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{[0,1]}(|F^{[-1]}_{\mu}-F^{[-1]}_{\nu}|)^{p}d\mathcal{L}.

Moreover, for
p=1
,

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F_{\mu}(t)-F_{\nu}(t)|dt.

2.2.2
Wasserstein Distance

If we take
X=Y
and choose
d
as the cost function, the optimal transportation problem lifts the distance
d
over the space
P_{p}(X)
. The resulting distance is called the Wasserstein distance.

Definition 8

(Wasserstein Distance)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a Polish space and
p\in[1,\infty)
. The
p-
order Wasserstein distance between the probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
X
is defined as

W_{d^{p}}^{p}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{T}_{d^{p}}(\pi).

When
p=1
, the
1-
Wasserstein distance is also known as Kantorovich-Rubistein distance.

The Wasserstein distance
W_{d^{p}}^{p}
is well defined when used to compare measures in
P_{p}(X)
.

Theorem 2
.

The
W_{d^{p}}
distance is a finite distance over
P_{p}(X)
. When the set
X
is bounded, the
W_{d^{p}}
distance induces the weak topology on the space
P_{p}(X)
. Moreover,
(P_{p}(X),W_{d^{p}})
is a Polish space.

Our Contribution

In this section, we report our main results. In paragraph
3.1
, we study the properties of the cardinal flows and introduce the pivot measure. Our main result on the structure of the Wasserstein cost is stated in Theorem
4
.

In paragraph
3.2

Finally, in paragraph

3.1
The Cardinal Flow and the Pivot Measure Formulation

From now on, we assume
X
and
Y
to be the product of smaller Polish spaces, i.e.
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
and
Y=Y_{1}\times Y_{2}
. In this framework, we can introduce the separable cost function and reformulate the optimal transportation problem as an optimal cardinal flow problem.

Definition 9

(Separable Cost Function)
.

Let
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
and
Y=Y_{1}\times Y_{2}
be two Polish spaces. We say that
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
is
separable
if there exist a pair of functions
c_{1}:X_{1}\times Y_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
and
c_{2}:X_{2}\times Y_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

c(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}):=c_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})+c_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})

for each
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in X
and for each
\textbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})\in Y
.

Definition 10

(Cardinal Flow)
.

Let us take
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
. We say that the couple of measures
(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y_{1})\times\mathcal{P}(X_{2}\times Y)
is a
cardinal flow
between
\mu
and
\nu
if it satisfies the following conditions

•

The marginal on
X
of
f^{(1)}
is equal to
\mu
, i.e.

\mu=(\mathfrak{p}_{X})_{\#}f^{(1)}.

(9)

•

The marginal on
Y
of
f^{(2)}
is equal to
\nu
, i.e.

\nu=(\mathfrak{p}_{Y})_{\#}f^{(2)}.

(10)

•

The flows
f^{(1)}
and
f^{(2)}
have the same marginal on
Y_{1}\times X_{2}
, i.e.

(\mathfrak{p}_{Y_{1}\times X_{2}})_{\#}f^{(1)}=(\mathfrak{p}_{Y_{1}\times X_{2}})_{\#}f^{(2)}.

(11)

We call the measures
f^{(1)}
and
f^{(2)}
first and second cardinal flow, respectively.
We denote with
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
the set of all cardinal flows between
\mu
and
\nu
.

Remark 2
.

For any couple of probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
, the set
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
is nonempty. In fact, the couple
(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})
, defined as

f^{(1)}=\mu\otimes\nu_{1}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994ptf^{(2)}=\mu_{2}\otimes\nu,

is an element of
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
.

Remark 3
.

The sets
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
and
\mathcal{F}(\nu,\mu)
are, in general, not equal. For instance, let us take
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\mu=\delta_{(0,0)}
and
\nu=\delta_{(1,1)}
. In this case,
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)=\{\delta_{((0,0);1)}\}
while
\mathcal{F}(\nu,\mu)=\{\delta_{((1,1);0)}\}
.

Definition 11

(Cardinal Flow Functional)
.

Given two probability measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, and a separable cost function
c=c_{1}+c_{2}
over
X\times Y
. We define the first and second cardinal transportation functionals as

\mathbb{CT}_{c}^{(1)}(f^{(1)})=\int_{X\times Y_{1}}c_{1}df^{(1)}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbb{CT}_{c}^{(2)}(f^{(2)})=\int_{X_{2}\times Y}c_{2}df^{(2)}

where
F=(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})\in\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
. The total cardinal flow functional is then defined as

\mathbb{CT}_{c}(F)=\mathbb{CT}_{c}^{(1)}(f^{(1)})+\mathbb{CT}_{c}^{(2)}(f^{(2)}).

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, and let
c:X\times Y\to[0,\infty)
be a separable cost function, then

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{T}_{c}(\pi)=\inf_{(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})\in\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{CT}_{c}(f^{(1)},f^{(2)}).

Proof.

The proof of this Theorem, in the discrete case, has been proposed in
[
4
]
. The proof in our generic setting follows from similar arguments.
∎

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, let us consider the function
L:\Pi(\mu,\nu)\to\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
defined as

L(\pi)=((\mathfrak{p}_{X\times Y_{1}})_{\#}(\pi),(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}\times Y})_{\#}(\pi)).

(12)

By the chain rule
1
, it is easy to see that
L(\pi)\in\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
for each
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Vice versa, given
(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})\in\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
, by using the Gluing Lemma (Lemma
2
), we find
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that
L(\pi)=(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})
. This proves the identity
\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)=L(\Pi(\mu,\nu))
. The function
L
allows us to relate
\mathbb{T}_{c}
and
\mathbb{CT}_{c}
, as it follows

\mathbb{T}_{c}(\pi)=\mathbb{CT}_{c}(L(\pi)),\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),

which, in conjunction with the identity
L(\Pi(\mu,\nu))=\mathcal{F}(\mu,\nu)
, allows us to conclude that the infimum of
\mathbb{CT}_{c}
is actually a minimum and that the set of minimizers of
\mathbb{CT}_{c}
coincides with the image of
\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
through
L
. In particular, the cardinal flow problem inherits the uniqueness of the solution from the Optimal Transportation problem.

Corollary 2
.

If the optimal transportation plan is unique, so is the optimal cardinal flow.

Remark 4
.

Since the operator
L
is only surjective and not injective, the reverse implication is not true, i.e., given an optimal cardinal flow
(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})
, there might exist a plethora of optimal transportation plans such that
L(\pi)=(f^{(1)},f^{(2)})
.

Definition 12

(Intermedium Measures)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
. We define the set of intermedium measures between
\mu
and
\nu
as

\mathcal{I}(\mu,\nu):=\bigg\{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(Y_{1}\times X_{2})\text{ s.t. }(\mathfrak{p}_{X_{2}})_{\#}(\lambda)=\mu_{2}\text{ and }(\mathfrak{p}_{Y_{1}})_{\#}(\lambda)=\nu_{1}\bigg\}.

Definition 13

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X\times X_{1}}d\circ\big((\mathfrak{p})^{(1)},(p^{(2)}_{1},p^{(1)}_{2})\big)df^{(1)}

\displaystyle+\int_{X_{2}\times X}d\circ\big((p^{(2)}_{1},p^{(1)}_{2}),(\mathfrak{p})^{(2)}\big)df^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X\times X}d\;d\pi^{(1)}+\int_{X\times X}d\;d\pi^{(2)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle W_{d}(\mu,\zeta)+W_{d}(\zeta,\nu).

The latter inequality, in conjunction with (
29
), allows us to conclude

W_{d}(\mu,\nu)=W_{d}(\mu,\zeta)+W_{d}(\zeta,\nu)

for any pivot measure
\zeta
.
∎

Remark 6
.

The previous Theorem states that any pivot measure minimizes the functional

\Theta:\lambda\to W_{d}(\mu,\lambda)+W_{d}(\lambda,\nu).

However, the reverse implication is not true. Let us consider, for
instance,
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\mu=\dfrac{1}{2}\big[\delta_{(0,0)}+\delta_{(7,1)}\big]\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ and }\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\nu=\dfrac{1}{2}\big[\delta_{(1,1)}+\delta_{(8,0)}\big].

It is easy to see that the only pivot measure is
\zeta=\dfrac{1}{2}\big[\delta_{(1,0)}+\delta_{(8,1)}\big]
. Since
\mu_{2}=\nu_{2}
, we have
\nu\in\mathcal{I}(\mu,\nu)
and, therefore

W_{d}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\lambda\in\mathcal{I}(\mu,\nu)}W_{d}(\mu,\lambda)+W_{d}(\lambda,\nu)\leq W_{d}(\mu,\nu)+W_{d}(\nu,\nu)=W_{d}(\mu,\nu)

hence
\nu
minimizes
\Theta
, although it is not the pivot measure.
