Learning operators for identifying weak solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations
Dixi Wang, Cheng Yu

Abstract
This paper focuses on investigating the learning operators for identifying weak solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations. Our objective is to establish a connection between the initial data as input and the weak solution as output. To achieve this, we employ a combination of deep learning methods and compactness argument to derive learning operators for weak solutions for any large initial data in 2D, and for low-dimensional initial data in 3D. Additionally, we utilize the universal approximation theorem to derive a lower bound on the number of sensors required to achieve accurate identification of weak solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations. Our results demonstrate the potential of using deep learning techniques to address challenges in the study of fluid mechanics, particularly in identifying weak solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations.

Introduction

The incompressible Navier-Stokes equations on the space-time domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,T]
is written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}+\nabla p-\nu\Delta\bm{u}=0~~~~~~~~~~~~(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,T],

(1)

\displaystyle\text{div}~\bm{u}=0~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times[0,T],

(2)

with initial data

\displaystyle\bm{u}(\cdot,t)|_{t=0}=\bm{u}_{0}~~~~~\text{in}~\mathbb{T}^{d}.

(3)

Here
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a periodic domain with
d=2~\text{or}~3
,
T>0
.

\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
is the velocity field,
p(\bm{x},t)
is the pressure, and

\nu>0
is the viscosity coefficient.

The Navier-Stokes equations are fundamental in the fields of science and engineering. Despite extensive research, the mathematical understanding of the incompressible Navier-Stokes (ICNS) remains incomplete. One of the most significant challenges is the lack of global-in-time smooth solutions for ICNS in three dimensions, which has been a long-standing open problem. While global Leray-Hopf weak solutions have been proven to exist for dimensions
d\geq 2
, uniqueness has only been established in 2D, leaving the uniqueness of 3D Leray-Hopf solutions an open problem
[
Lio96
]
. These unresolved issues highlight the complexity of the Navier-Stokes equations and the need for continued research in this area. Therefore, this paper focuses on investigating the learning operators for weak solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations, aiming to contribute to the understanding and development of this critical field.

Deep neural networks, as
universal approximators
can approximate finite-dimensional continuous functions up to arbitrary accuracy
[
Bar93
]
.
In recent years, we have witnessed tremendous success of network-based PDE solvers working with smooth solutions in explicit form with high accuracy. For example,
physics

(PINNs) can solve both forward problems as well as inverse problems
[
RK18
]
,
[
RPK19
]
. When the solution attains singularities,
[

]
parameterized both the weak solution and the test function by neural networks and learn the parameters in an adversarial manner governed by weak formulation. In
[
Che+22
]
, the authors proposed the Friedrichs learning to transform the PDE problem into a minmax optimization problem, their algorithm is capable to capture the discontinuity of the solution without a priori knowledge.

Although deep learning has achieved huge success in many applications, its statistical theory, particularly on operator approximations between infinite-dimensional spaces, is still limited. The universal approximation theorem, which allows networks to approximate operators between two compact subsets of infinite-dimensional Banach spaces, was first proved by
[
CC95
]
. Later
[
LJK21
]
introduced DeepONets, a combination of branch and trunk nets, that can approximate a continuous operator mapping from a compact subset of an infinite-dimensional space to another Banach space within any desired precision. However, neither of these results provides explicit estimation on the complexity of the networks involved.
In 2017, Yarotsky
[
Yar17
]
described a ReLU network architecture with a certain depth and complexity that can approximate any functions within the unit ball of the Sobolev space
\mathcal{W}^{n,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
to any desired precision.
[
Liu+22
]
derived an upper bound on the generalization error for learning Lipschitz operators between infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces by neural networks, which reveals a direct relationship between generalization error and the number of data samples.
[
LMK22
]
weakened the assumptions on continuity and compactness of the input space and provided explicit bounds for DeepONets.
[
Jia+23
]
derived an error bound for Hölder continuous functions, with a prefactor in the error bound dependent on the dimension in polynomial order. Overall, these findings provide insights into the capabilities and limitations of neural networks in approximating operators between infinite-dimensional spaces and shed light on the complexity of the networks required for different levels of precision.

Both
[
Liu+22
]
and
[
LMK22
]
employ a model reduction method, which is a common technique in data-driven learning for time-dependent parametrized PDEs as documented in the literature
[
Roz14
,
Bha+21
]
. In order to approximate maps between infinite-dimensional spaces, a typical first step is to reduce the dimensions of both input and output spaces using encoders and decoders (also known as projectors and reconstructors). Encoders are used to project high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional space, while decoders are used to map the reduced data back to the original space. Popular choices of encoders include orthogonal basis of a separable Hilbert space and PCA (principal component analysis) encoder. Basis encoders, such as orthogonal polynomials and trigonometric basis, are independent of the training data and only depend on the underlying Hilbert space. In contrast, PCA encoder is data-driven and has become increasingly popular in recent years due to its simplicity of implementation and good numerical performance
[
Sha+05
,
BBZ07
]
.

The Navier-Stokes equations present challenges for accurately capturing weak solutions using traditional numerical methods in various settings. Deep neural networks, on the other hand, offer a highly flexible approach for approximating solutions to some PDEs under different settings. Hence, in this paper, we explore the use of deep learning operators to identify weak solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations, which can provide a more efficient and accurate approach to fluid dynamics problems.

3.1
Galerkin approximation.

From now on,

The global existence of Leray-Hopf solutions was established based on Galerkin approximation.
The eigenfunctions
\{\bm{w}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
of the Stokes operator

form an orthonormal basis of
H

for each

We re-label the sequence
\{\bm{w}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

For any
\bm{h}\in H

\bm{h}

\bm{h}=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}\langle\bm{h},\bm{w}_{i}\rangle\bm{w}_{i}.

\displaystyle P_{m}(\bm{h})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\bm{h},\bm{w}_{i})\bm{w}_{i}.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}^{m}+P_{m}[(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m}]-\nu\Delta\bm{u}^{m}=0,

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{m}_{0}=P_{m}(\bm{u}_{0}).

d^{m}_{k}(t),~k=1,2,\dots,m
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}d^{m}_{k}(t)+\nu\lambda_{k}d^{m}_{k}(t)+\sum^{m}_{j,l=1}d^{n}_{j}(t)d^{m}_{l}(t)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\bm{w}_{j}(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla)\bm{w}_{l}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{w}_{k}(\bm{x})\text{d}\bm{x}=0.

(19)

By classical ODE theory, there exists
T>0
such that the ODE system (
19
) admits unique solutions
\{d^{m}_{k}(t)\}^{m}_{k=1}\in C^{1}((0,T)).

Let
t\in(0,T]
, multiply
3.1
by
\bm{u}^{m}
and integrate over
\bm{x}
to get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{t}\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\bm{u}^{m}~\text{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\bm{u}^{m}~\text{d}\bm{x}-\nu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\bm{u}^{m}~\text{d}\bm{x}=0.

By the incompressibility of
\bm{u}^{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\bm{u}^{m}~\text{d}\bm{x}=0.

(20)

Thus we can obtain the following
m
-dimensional energy equality

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\|\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\nu\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=0.

Integrating on
t\in[0,T],~T>0
, this energy equality implies that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\dfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},\\
&\nu\int^{T}_{0}\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\text{d}t\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.\end{split}

(21)

Therefore

\{\bm{u}^{m}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;V)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;H)
for any positive
T
. In particular,
\{d^{m}_{k}(t)\}^{m}_{k=1}
is uniformly bounded in
t
, hence
T
can be
\infty
.

By classical theory of weak solutions,
\{\bm{u}^{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
has a subsequence, still denoted by
\{\bm{u}^{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
, that strongly converges to
\bm{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
, and
\bm{u}
is a Leray-Hopf weak solution to the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
1
-
3
. Readers may refer to
[
Lio96
]
for more details.

3.2
Properties of Approximating Operators

In this Section,
m\in\mathbb{N}
is a fixed order in the Galerkin approximation.
We have seen in Section
3.1

that the first
m
principal eigenfunctions
\{\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\}
together with the projection operator
P_{m}
provide a way to reduce the dimension of the target operator between infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces to a mapping between finite dimensional vector space. The goal of this subsection is to show that the solution operator is Lipschitz in term of initial data.

Let
\bm{u}_{0}\in H
, denote
\bm{u}^{m}_{0}
to be

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{m}_{0}:=P_{m}(\bm{u}_{0}):=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\langle\bm{u}_{0},\bm{w}_{i}\rangle\bm{w}_{i}.

(22)

By Assumption
2.4
,

\|\bm{u}^{m}_{0}\|_{H}\leq\|\bm{u}_{0}\|_{H}\leq R
.

Denote by
\Psi^{m}:\text{span}\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\}\to Y
the operator mapping
\bm{u}^{m}_{0}
to
\bm{u}^{m}
, where
\bm{u}^{m}
is the unique solution of
m
-th order Galerkin equation (
3.1
).
By energy inequality (
21
) and Assumption
2.4
,

\|\Psi^{m}(\bm{u}^{m}_{0})\|_{Y}=\|\bm{u}^{m}\|_{Y}\leq C(\nu)\|\bm{u}_{0}\|_{H}\leq C(\nu)R,

where
C(\nu)
is a positive constant depending on
\nu
, but independent of
m
. The next lemma shows that
\Psi^{m}
is a Lipschitz operator with respect to the initial function.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d=2,3
, and
T>0
.
For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Psi^{m}:\text{span}\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\}\to Y

is a Lipschitz operator, i.e. for any
\bm{u}_{0},\bm{v}_{0}\in H
satisfying (
13
) and
\bm{u}^{m}_{0},\bm{v}^{m}_{0}
given in (
22
),

\displaystyle\|\Psi^{m}(\bm{u}^{m}_{0})-\Psi^{m}(\bm{v}^{m}_{0})\|_{Y}\leq L_{\Psi^{m}}\|\bm{u}^{m}_{0}-\bm{v}^{m}_{0}\|_{H}

(23)

for some
L_{\Psi^{m}}>0
.

Proof.

Without any ambiguities, we omit the subscript of norms.
Let
\bm{u}_{0},\bm{v}_{0}\in H
with finite initial energy (
13
), we denote by

\bm{u}^{m}:=\Psi^{m}(\bm{u}^{m}_{0}),\;\;\bm{v}^{m}:=\Psi^{m}(\bm{v}^{m}_{0})

the corresponding solutions respectively to the
m
-th order Galerkin approximation (
3.1
) in the form
18
.

Accordingly, we denote by

\bm{h}^{m}_{0}=\bm{u}^{m}_{0}-\bm{v}^{m}_{0},\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\bm{h}^{m}=\bm{u}^{m}-\bm{v}^{m}.

For any
s\in(0,T]
, multiply (
3.1
) at time
s
with
\bm{h}^{m}(s)
and integrate in
x
to get

\displaystyle-\langle\bm{u}^{m},\partial_{t}\bm{h}^{m}\rangle+\nu\langle\nabla\bm{u}^{m},\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\rangle+\langle(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle=-\dfrac{d}{dt}\langle\bm{u}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle,

(24)

Similarly,

\displaystyle-\langle\bm{v}^{m},\partial_{t}\bm{h}^{m}\rangle+\nu\langle\nabla\bm{v}^{m},\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\rangle+\langle(\bm{v}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle=-\dfrac{d}{dt}\langle\bm{v}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle.

(25)

The difference of
24
and
25
gives

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}+\nu\|\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big|\langle(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m}-(\bm{v}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\langle(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle-\langle((\bm{u}^{m}-\bm{h}^{m})\cdot\nabla)(\bm{u}^{m}-\bm{h}^{m}),\bm{h}^{m}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\langle(\bm{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{h}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle+\langle(\bm{h}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle-\langle(\bm{h}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{h}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle\big|.

(26)

By (
20
) and the incompressibility of
\bm{h}^{m}
, the first and the last terms on the right side of
3.2
vanish. Thus we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}+\nu\|\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}\leq\big|\langle(\bm{h}^{m}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}^{m},\bm{h}^{m}\rangle\big|\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\bm{h}^{m}|^{2}|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}|d\bm{x}.

(27)

The Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality with
\theta=\frac{d}{4}
implies that

\displaystyle\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}_{L^{4}}\leq C\|\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\|^{d/2}\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2-d/2}

holds for
d=2,3
. Then
27
becomes

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}+\nu\|\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}\|\cdot\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2-d/2}\cdot\|\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\|^{d/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}\cdot\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}\|^{4/4-d}+\nu\|\nabla\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}.

Here we used Young’s inequality and the constant
C
depends on
d
and
\nu
.

By Gronwall’s inequality, we have

\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{h}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\exp\Big(C\int_{0}^{s}\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}\|^{4/4-d}dt\Big).

(28)

When
d=2
, one may use (
21
) and (
13
) such that (
28
) can be bounded by
\|\bm{h}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\exp(CR^{2})
with
C
depending on
\nu
and
d
but independent of
m
. In this case,
by the definition of
\bm{h}^{m}
and
\bm{h}^{m}_{0}
,
\Psi^{m}
is a Lipschitz operator with Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi^{m}}=\exp(CR^{2})
independent of
m
.

When
d=3
, (
28
) can be written as

\displaystyle\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{h}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\exp\Big(C\int_{0}^{s}\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}\|^{4}dt\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{h}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\exp\Big(C\underbrace{\sup_{t}\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}}_{\text{I}}\cdot\underbrace{\int^{s}_{0}\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}\text{d}t}_{\text{II}}\Big).

(29)

It is clear that II is uniformly bounded by
\|\bm{u}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\leq R^{2}
according to (
21
). As for I, we use energy inequality (
21
) and the fact that
\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\}
is an orthonormal basis and the corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
is non-decreasing to get

\displaystyle\sup_{t}\|\nabla\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\|\sum^{m}_{k=1}d^{m}_{k}(t)\nabla\bm{w}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\sum^{m}_{k=1}\lambda_{k}(d^{m}_{k}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{m}\sup_{t}\|\bm{u}^{m}(t)\|^{2}\leq\lambda_{m}\|\bm{u}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\leq\lambda_{m}R^{2}.

Now (
3.2
) becomes

\|\bm{h}^{m}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{h}^{m}_{0}\|^{2}\exp(CR^{4}\lambda_{m})

with
C
depending on
\nu
,
d
and
\lambda_{m}
depending on
m
. Therefore in 3D case,
\Psi^{m}
is a Lipschitz operator with Lipschitz constant

L_{\Psi^{m}}=\exp(CR^{4}\lambda_{m})

depending on
m
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In 2-dimensional case, the Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi^{m}}
is independent of
m
, this implies that by taking
m\to\infty
,
\Psi
is also Lipschitz with the same Lipschitz constant as each
\Psi^{m}
. The Lipschitz condition on
\Psi
provides an alternative approach to prove the uniqueness of 2D Leray-Hopf weak solution.

Remark 3.3
.

In 3-dimensional case, the Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi^{m}}=\exp(CR^{4}\lambda_{m})
depends on
m
as we arrange the basis functions
\{\bm{w}_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=1}
in an order that
\lambda_{k}
is non-decreasing. The Galerkin approximating sequence
\{\bm{u}^{m}\}
converges to
\bm{u}
in
L^{2}(0,T;V)
as
m\to\infty
, the corresponding
L_{\Psi^{m}}\to\infty
exponentially fast.

3.3
The Empirical Projection Error

The empirical projection errors on both
H
and
Y
spaces w.r.t. a finite collection of training data
S(N)=\{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
can be bounded using a
Monte Carlo type estimate. Given encoding dimension
d_{H},d_{Y}>0
, the projection errors are denoted by:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|\bm{x}-\Pi_{d_{H}}\bm{x}\|^{2}_{H},~~~~~~\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}\|\bm{y}-\Pi_{d_{Y}}\bm{y}\|^{2}_{Y}.

(30)

And the corresponding empirical projection errors are given by:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(N):=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\|\bm{x}_{j}-\Pi_{d_{H}}\bm{x}_{j}\|^{2}_{H},~~~~~~\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}(N):=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\|\bm{y}_{j}-\Pi_{d_{Y}}\bm{y}_{j}\|^{2}_{Y}.

(31)

We state the following lemma which is a partial result of Theorem 3.4
[
Bha+21
]
, and the proof can be found therein.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exist constants
Q_{H},Q_{Y}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathbb{P}}[\text{Pr}(N)]\leq\sqrt{\frac{Q_{H}d_{H}}{N}}+\text{Pr},~\text{and}~~\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}_{j}\sim\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}[\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}(N)]\leq\sqrt{\frac{Q_{Y}d_{Y}}{N}}+\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}.

(32)

3.4
The Networks Approximation Error

In this subsection, we prove an auxiliary lemma on the networks approximation error, together with projection errors in both
H,Y
spaces we can prove our main results Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
2.8
. This lemma is inspired by
[
Bha+21
]
Theorem 3.5, the difference comes from the choice of encoder and decoder. In
[
Bha+21
]
, the authors use PCA as an empirical dimension reduction method; while in our work, we use deterministic basis which is independent of training data.

Given
d_{H},d_{Y}>0
, we define
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{H}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}
as

\displaystyle\psi:=\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ\mathcal{D}^{d_{H}},

(33)

We aim to approximate
\psi
by some ReLU neural networks
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d_{H}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}
. We also define two operators

\Psi_{\Delta},\Psi_{NN}:H\to Y
as

\displaystyle\Psi_{\Delta}:=\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ\psi\circ\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}},

\displaystyle\Psi_{NN}:=\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Gamma\circ\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
H,Y
be two separable Hilbert spaces defined in Section
2
.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure on
B_{R}(H):=\{x\in H;~\|x\|_{H}\leq R\}
with compact support.
Let
\Psi:H\to Y
be a Lipschitz operator with Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi}
.
Fix
d_{H},d_{Y}>0
, the encoders and decoders on
H,Y
:
\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}},\mathcal{D}^{d_{H}},\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}},\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}
are defined in (
8
),(
9
) and (
2.3
). Let
\delta>0
, there exist a constant
c=c(d_{H},d_{Y})>0
and a ReLU neural network

\Gamma_{NN}\in\mathcal{F}_{NN}(d_{H},d_{Y},L,r,\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}})

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Gamma_{NN}\circ\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}<2\delta^{2}+4L_{\Psi}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{Q_{H}d_{H}}{N}}+\text{Pr}\bigg)+4\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{Q_{Y}d_{Y}}{N}}+\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}\bigg)

for some
Q_{H},Q_{Y}\geq 0
, where

\displaystyle L\leq c\bigg[\log{\bigg(\dfrac{R\sqrt{d_{Y}}}{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}\bigg)}+1\bigg]~,~~\text{and}~~~r\leq c\bigg(\dfrac{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}{2R}\bigg)^{-d_{H}}\bigg[\log{\bigg(\dfrac{R\sqrt{d_{Y}}}{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}\bigg)}+1\bigg]~.

Proof.

We simply write
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathbb{P}}
as
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}
, and decompose
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Psi_{NN}(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}
as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Psi_{NN}(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}\leq 2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\|\Psi_{NN}(\bm{x})-\Psi_{\Delta}(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}}_{\text{I}}+2\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Psi_{\Delta}(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}}_{\text{II}}.

(34)

We first look at II, recall the definition of
\Pi_{d_{H}},\Pi_{d_{Y}}
in (
10
) and (
12
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Psi_{\Delta}(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ\mathcal{D}^{d_{H}}\circ\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Pi_{d_{Y}}\Psi(\Pi_{d_{H}}(\bm{x}))-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Pi_{d_{Y}}\Psi(\Pi_{d_{H}}(\bm{x}))-\Pi_{d_{Y}}\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}+2\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Pi_{d_{Y}}\Psi(\bm{x})-\Psi(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{\Psi}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\|\Pi_{d_{H}}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}\|^{2}_{H}+2\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}\|\Pi_{d_{Y}}\bm{y}-\bm{y}\|^{2}_{Y}

\displaystyle=2L_{\Psi}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathbb{P}}[\text{Pr}(N)]+2\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}_{i}\sim\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}[\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}(N)]

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{\Psi}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{Q_{H}d_{H}}{N}}+\text{Pr}\bigg)+2\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{Q_{Y}d_{Y}}{N}}+\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}\bigg),

(35)

where we use Lemma
3.4
in the last inequality. We next control I by first approximating
\psi
by some ReLU networks. From (
33
),
\psi
is a Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi}
. For any
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{H}}
,

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{a})=(\psi^{(1)}(\bm{a}),\cdots,\psi^{(d_{Y})}(\bm{a})),

with
\psi^{(j)}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d_{H}};\mathbb{R}),j=1,\dots,d_{Y}.
In order to satisfy Assumption
2.4
, we require that

\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d_{H}})\in\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\big]^{d_{H}}.
Next we perform a change of variables, define
\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}:[0,1]^{d_{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}(\bm{s})=\dfrac{\sqrt{d_{H}}}{2R}\psi^{(j)}\bigg(\dfrac{2R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\bm{s}-\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\bigg),~~\bm{s}\in[0,1]^{d_{H}}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\psi^{(j)}(\bm{s})=\dfrac{2R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}\bigg(\dfrac{\sqrt{d_{H}}\bm{s}+R}{2R}\bigg),

and one can check that
\tilde{\psi}^{(j)},j=1,2,\dots,d_{Y}
is Lipschitz and share the same Lipschitz constant as
\psi^{(j)}
. Given
\delta>0
, we apply
[
Yar17
]
Theorem 1 to
\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}
for each
j
and obtain ReLU networks

\tilde{f}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{f}^{(d_{Y})}:[0,1]^{d_{H}}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}^{(j)}(\bm{s})-\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}(\bm{s})|<\dfrac{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}{2R\sqrt{d_{Y}}},~~\text{for all}~~\bm{s}\in[0,1]^{d_{H}}.

Denote by
L^{(j)}
the depth of
\tilde{f}^{(j)}
, and
r^{(j)}
the number of weights and bias of
\tilde{f}^{(j)}
.
L^{(j)},r^{(j)}
satisfy:

\displaystyle L^{(j)}\leq c^{(j)}\bigg[\log{\bigg(\dfrac{R\sqrt{d_{Y}}}{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}\bigg)}+1\bigg]~,~~~~~~~r^{(j)}\leq c^{(j)}\bigg(\dfrac{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}{2R}\bigg)^{-d_{H}}\bigg[\log{\bigg(\dfrac{R\sqrt{d_{Y}}}{\delta\sqrt{d_{H}}}\bigg)}+1\bigg]

for some
c^{(j)}>0
depending on
d_{H}
. Then we define
f^{(j)}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle f^{(j)}(\bm{a}):=\dfrac{2R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\tilde{f}^{(j)}\bigg(\dfrac{\sqrt{d_{H}}\bm{a}+R}{2R}\bigg)

(36)

for any
\bm{a}\in\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\big]^{d_{H}},

and
|(f^{(1)}(\bm{a}),\dots,f^{(d_{Y})}(\bm{a}))-\psi(\bm{a})|_{2}<\delta.

Now, define
\Gamma_{NN}(\bm{a}):=(f^{(1)}(\bm{a}),\dots,f^{(d_{Y})}(\bm{a})),
and set
\Gamma_{NN}\equiv 0
outside
\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\big]^{d_{H}}
. Such

\Gamma_{NN}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{a}\in\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{d_{H}}}\big]^{d_{H}}}{|\Gamma_{NN}(\bm{a})-\psi(\bm{a})|_{2}}<\delta.

Finally, we can bound I as

\displaystyle~~~~~\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|\Psi_{NN}(\bm{x})-\Psi_{\Delta}(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(H)}\|\Psi_{NN}(\bm{x})-\Psi_{\Delta}(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}~\text{d}\mathbb{P}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(H)}\|\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Gamma_{NN}\circ\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ\psi\circ\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{Y}~\text{d}\mathbb{P}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}(H)}|\Gamma_{NN}(\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}(\bm{x}))-\psi(\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}}(\bm{x}))|^{2}_{2}~\text{d}\mathbb{P}(\bm{x})<\delta^{2}.

(37)

Here we used
L_{\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}}=1
and
\mathbb{P}(B_{R}(H))\leq 1
.
Combining (
3.4
) with (
34
) and (
3.4
), we finish the proof.

∎

3.5
Approximation Theory of Leray-Hopf Solutions

In this subsection, we combine Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
to prove Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
2.8
on the existence of a finite ReLU neural network that can approximate the Leray-Hopf solutions of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.

3.5.1
2D Leray-Hopf Solutions

Let
\epsilon>0
. In the case that
d=2
, thanks to Lemma
3.1
and Remark
3.2
, the solution operator
\Psi:H\to Y
is a Lipschitz operator and
L_{\Psi}=e^{CR^{2}}
with
C=C(\nu)
and
\nu
is the viscosity coefficient. Suppose we have a training dataset
S(N)=\{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
,
we first bound the projection error on
H
.

\displaystyle\text{Pr}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathbb{P}}\|\bm{x}-\Pi_{d_{H}}(\bm{x})\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(H)}\|\sum^{\infty}_{i=d_{H}+1}(\bm{x},\bm{w}_{i})\bm{w}_{i}\|^{2}_{H}~\text{d}\mathbb{P}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=d_{H}+1}\int_{B_{R}(H)}|(\bm{x},\bm{w}_{i})|^{2}~\text{d}\mathbb{P}(\bm{x})

Thus
Pr
is non-negative and monotone decreasing as
d_{H}\to\infty
. We can choose
d_{H}
and
N
large enough such that
\text{Pr}+\sqrt{\frac{Q_{H}d_{H}}{N}}<\frac{1}{16L_{\Psi}}\epsilon
.
Similarly, we choose
d_{Y}
and further enlarge
N
such that
\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}+\sqrt{\frac{Q_{Y}d_{Y}}{N}}<\frac{1}{16}\epsilon
. Note that the choice of
d_{H},d_{Y}
is independent of
N
.
Let
\delta=\dfrac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}
in Lemma
3.5
, we complete the proof of Theorem
2.7
.

3.5.2
3D Leray-Hopf Solutions

The major difference between 2D and 3D cases is that, we do not have the uniform boundedness of the Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi^{k}},k=1,2,\dots
as discussed in Remark
3.2
and Remark
3.3
. When the Galerkin approximation order
k\to\infty
, the corresponding Lipschitz constant
L_{\Psi^{k}}\to\infty
exponentially fast for each
\Psi^{k}
. This property can cause an extremely slow convergence of the approximation error, even failure in convergence. To alleviate the curse of dimensionality, we propose Assumption
2.5
as a special case in 3D setting.

Let
\epsilon>0
and

\bm{u}_{0}\in\text{span}\{\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2},\dots\,\bm{w}_{D}\}
, the Galerkin approximations produce a sequence of solutions
\{\bm{u}^{1},\bm{u}^{2},\dots,\bm{u}^{D},\bm{u}^{D},\dots\}
with all the rest terms being
\bm{u}^{D}
. Extracting a convergent subsequence, the 3D Leray-Hopf solution
\bm{u}
under Assumption
2.5
is equal to
\bm{u}^{D}
.
By Lemma
3.1
,
\Psi=\Psi^{D}
is a Lipschitz operator in terms of the initial function.

Let
d_{H}=D
, then the projection error on
H
vanishes. By Lemma
3.4
, we can choose

\tilde{d_{Y}}
and
N
large enough such that

\displaystyle\text{Pr}^{\Psi_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}}+\sqrt{\frac{Q_{Y}\tilde{d_{Y}}}{N}}<\dfrac{1}{8}\epsilon.

Let
\delta=\dfrac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}
in Lemma
3.5
and choose
d_{Y}:=\max\{\tilde{d}_{Y},D\}
, we finish the proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Remark 3.6
.

When the initial function lies in a finite-dimensional subspace of
H
, the Lipschitzness of
\Psi
in terms of initial function implies the uniqueness of 3D weak solution to the ICNS.

4
Data Generation in 3D

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.9
, in which we investigate how many sensors are needed to achieve accuracy
\epsilon
for identifying weak solutions to the ICNS using neural networks with 1 hidden layer.

Let
d_{H},d_{Y}>0
. According to Section
3.5.2
, we assume that
d_{H}=D
. The initial function
\bm{u}_{0}
satisfies Assumption
2.4
and Assumption
2.5
, i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{0}=\sum^{D}_{i=1}a_{i}\bm{w}_{i},~~\text{with each}~~a_{i}:=\langle\bm{u}_{0},\bm{w}_{i}\rangle,~\text{and}~~\|\bm{u}_{0}\|_{H}=\big(\sum^{D}_{i=1}a^{2}_{i}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq R

(38)

where
\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{D}
are the first
D
components of the orthonormal basis
\{\bm{w}_{i}\}^{\infty}_{i=1}
of
H
. In practice, we generate
\bm{u}_{0}
by randomly sampling
\bm{a}
from the compact subset

\textbf{a}:=(a_{1},\dots,a_{D})\in\bigg[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}}\bigg]^{D}.

As we consider the 3D problem in periodic domain, one can choose uniformly
m+1
points
\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}
as location of sensors. For each
\bm{w}_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
,
i=1,2,\dots,D
, we define a function
\bm{w}^{m}_{i}(\bm{x})
to be the trilinear interpolation of
\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{x})
based on
m+1
sensors
\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}
.
Denote the operator mapping
\bm{w}_{i}
to
\bm{w}^{m}_{i}
by
T_{m}
, then
T_{m}
is a continuous operator.
Moreover, by Taylor expansion up to the second order, there exists a constant
\kappa(D,m)\sim\frac{D}{m}
such that

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{x}\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}}|\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{x})-\bm{w}^{m}_{i}(\bm{x})|\leq\kappa(D,m),

(39)

and
\kappa(D,m)\to 0
as
m\to\infty
.
For each
\bm{u}_{0}
in (
38
), we define
\bm{u}^{m}_{0}
based on
m
sensors as:

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{m}_{0}(\bm{x}):=\sum^{D}_{i=1}a_{i}\bm{w}^{m}_{i}(\bm{x}).

(40)

Let
\Psi:H\to Y
be the operator discussed in Section
3.5.2
. Then
\Psi
is a Lipschitz operator with

L_{\Psi}=L_{\Psi^{D}}=e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}

where
C=C(\nu)
and
\nu
is the viscosity coefficient. Let
\mathcal{D}^{D}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to H
be the decoder on
H
defined in (
9
) and
\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}
be the encoder on
Y
defined in (
2.3
). By Lemma
2.3
,
\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant 1. We can derive that

\displaystyle~~~~\|\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})-\mathcal{P}^{d_{y}}\circ\Psi\circ T_{m}\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}\|\bm{u}_{0}-\bm{u}^{m}_{0}\|_{H}\leq C_{1}e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}\kappa(D,m),

(41)

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(D,R)>0
.
Let
V\subset\text{span}\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{D}\}
such that
V
contains all the possible initial functions
\bm{u}_{0}
. Then
V
is a compact subset as it is the image of a continuous map on a compact domain:

V=\bigg\{\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a}):\bm{a}\in\bigg[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}}\bigg]^{D}\bigg\}.

We define

\displaystyle G_{m}

\displaystyle:=\{(\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x}_{0}),\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x}_{1}),\dots,\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x}_{m}))^{T}~|~\bm{u}_{0}\in V\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}(m+1,3),

\displaystyle V^{m}

\displaystyle:=\{\bm{u}^{m}_{0}~|~\bm{u}_{0}\in V\},

where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}(m+1,3)
denotes the set of all real
(m+1)\times 3
matrices.
Naturally there is a bijection between
G_{m}
and
V^{m}
, and
\mathcal{D}^{D}
is also a bijection between
\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}}\big]^{D}
and
V
.

Define
\phi_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}

by

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(\bm{a}):=\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ T_{m}\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a}).

Then
\phi_{m}
is a continuous function. Given an approximation error
\epsilon
, choose the number of sensors
m
such that the right hand side of (
4
) satisfies

\displaystyle C_{1}e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}\kappa(D,m)<\frac{1}{2}\epsilon.

(42)

We can apply the universal approximation theorem
[
CC95
]
which states that there exist a ReLU neural network with weights and bias

W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times D},\;\;b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},\;\;W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{Y}}\times k},\;\;b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}},

such that

\displaystyle\|\phi_{m}(\bm{a})-(W_{2}\cdot\sigma(W_{1}\cdot\textbf{a}^{T})+b_{2})\|_{2}<\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon-C_{1}e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}\kappa(D,m)

holds for all
\bm{a}\in\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}}\big]^{D}
, then we have

\displaystyle~~~~~\|\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})-(W_{2}\cdot\sigma(W_{1}\cdot\textbf{a}^{T})+b_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})-\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ T_{m}\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})\|_{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~+\|\mathcal{P}^{d_{Y}}\circ\Psi\circ T_{m}\circ\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})-(W_{2}\cdot\sigma(W_{1}\cdot\textbf{a}^{T})+b_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle<C_{1}e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}\kappa(D,m)+\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon-C_{1}e^{CR^{4}\lambda_{D}}\kappa(D,m)=\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon.

(43)

Suppose
d_{Y}
is chosen sufficiently large such that for any
\bm{u}_{0}\in V
,

\displaystyle\|\Psi(\bm{u}_{0})-\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\Pi_{D}(\bm{u}_{0})))\|_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi(\bm{u}_{0})-\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\bm{u}_{0}))\|_{Y}+\|\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\bm{u}_{0}))-\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\Pi_{D}(\bm{u}_{0})))\|_{Y}

\displaystyle=\|\Psi(\bm{u}_{0})-\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\bm{u}_{0}))\|_{Y}<\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon.

(44)

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{E}^{d_{H}},\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}
in (
8
)-(
2.3
) and Lemma
2.3
, we combine (
4
) and (
4
) to get

\displaystyle~~~~~\|\Psi(\bm{u}_{0})-\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ(W_{2}\cdot\sigma(W_{1}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{D}(\bm{u}_{0})^{T})+b_{2})\|_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi(\bm{u}_{0})-\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\Pi_{D}(\bm{u}_{0})))\|_{Y}+\|\Pi_{d_{Y}}(\Psi(\Pi_{D}(\bm{u}_{0})))-\mathcal{R}^{d_{Y}}\circ(W_{2}\cdot\sigma(W_{1}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{D}(\bm{u}_{0})^{T})+b_{2})\|_{Y}

\displaystyle<\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon+\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon=\epsilon

holds for any
\bm{u}_{0}=\mathcal{D}^{D}(\bm{a})\in V
, and
\bm{a}\in\big[-\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}},\frac{R}{\sqrt{D}}\big]^{D}
. Therefore, we conclude that the number of sensors
m
should at least satisfy (
42
) to achieve an approximation accuracy
\epsilon
.
