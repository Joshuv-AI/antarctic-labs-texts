Introduction

A partition
\lambda
of
n
is a nonincreasing sequence
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
of positive integers (called parts) such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}=n
. We refer to
\lambda_{i}
as the
i
th part of
\lambda
, and as usual denote by
p(n)
the number of integer partitions of
n
. We note that
p(x)=0
if
x\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, and since the empty partition
\emptyset
is the only partition of
0
, we have that
p(0)=1
. The generating function for
p(n)
satisfies the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)\,q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}},

(1)

where we use the customary
q
-series notation

\displaystyle(a;q)_{n}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{for $n=0$,}\\
(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1}),&\text{for $n>0$;}\end{cases}

\displaystyle(a;q)_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(a;q)_{n}.

Moreover, we use the short notation

(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n};q)_{\infty}=(a_{1};q)_{\infty}(a_{2};q)_{\infty}\cdots(a_{n};q)_{\infty}.

Because the infinite product
(a;q)_{\infty}
diverges when
a\neq 0
and
|q|\geqslant 1
, whenever

(a;q)_{\infty}
appears in a formula, we shall assume
|q|<1
.

We denote by
pod(n)
the function which enumerates the partitions of
n
with odd
parts distinct and even parts unrestricted. Elementary techniques in the theory of partitions give the following equivalent expressions for the generating function for
pod(n)
:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q^{2})}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}=\frac{(q^{2};q^{4})}{(q;q)_{\infty}}.

(2)

In this article, we consider the

Definition 3

Let

The following result introduces new combinatorial interpretations for the partition functions

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geqslant 0
the following hold.

(i)

(-1)^{n}\,Q_{0}(n)=pod_{e}(n)-pod_{o}(n)

(ii)

(-1)^{n}\,Q_{2}(n)=b_{4,e}(n)-b_{4,o}(n)

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 0
.

(i)

Q_{0}(n)
and
pod(n)
have the same parity.

(ii)

Q_{2}(n)
and
b_{4}(n)
have the same parity.

We also have the following result relating
Q_{0}(n)
and
Q_{2}(n)
to
pod(n)

Theorem 1.3
.

For
n\geqslant 0
the following hold.

(i)

\displaystyle{Q_{0}(n)=pod(n)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\,pod\big(n-4k^{2}\big)}

(ii)

\displaystyle{Q_{2}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,pod\big(n-k(k+1)\big)}

In addition,
Q_{0}(n)
and
Q_{2}(n)
satisfy similar linear recurrence relations involving the triangular numbers. To make these easier to state, we first define

\xi_{n}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{m}\cdot 2&\text{if $n=4m^{2}$ for some $m>0$,}\\
1&\text{if $n=0$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

\chi_{n}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n/2}&\text{if $n=m(m+1)$ for some $m\geqslant 0$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Theorem 1.4
.

For
n\geqslant 0
the following hold.

(i)

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,Q_{0}\big(n-k(k+1)/2\big)=\xi_{n}}

(ii)

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,Q_{2}\big(n-k(k+1)/2\big)=\chi_{n}}

Proof of Theorem
1.1

2.1
Analytic Proof

Define

\displaystyle F(z,q):=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-zq^{4k+1})(1-zq^{4k+3})(1-zq^{4k+4})}.

Then, using Euler’s identity

1/(q;q^{2})_{\infty}=(-q;q)_{\infty}
and (
3
), we have

\displaystyle F(-1,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-q,-q^{3},-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}=\frac{(-q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(-q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(q;q^{2})_{\infty}(-q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}.

On the other hand, the fact that

F(z,q)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n,m)\,z^{m}\,q^{n},

where
pod(n,m)
is equal to the number of partitions of
n
with
m
parts none of which are congruent to
2
modulo
4
, implies that

F(-1,q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(pod_{e}(n)-pod_{o}(n)\big)\,q^{n},

which establishes (i).
To prove (ii), we define

G(z,q):=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-zq^{4k+1})(1-zq^{4k+2})(1-zq^{4k+3})}.

Then, using Euler’s identity
and (
4
), we have

\displaystyle G(-1,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-q,-q^{2},-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}}=\frac{(-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(-q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(q;q^{2})_{\infty}(-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,Q_{2}(n)\,q^{n}.

Moreover, since

G(z,q)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(n,m)\,z^{m}\,q^{n},

where
b_{4}(n,m)
is the number of
4
-regular partitions of
n
with
m
parts, we also have

G(-1,q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(b_{4,e}(n)-b_{4,o}(n)\big)\,q^{n},

which establishes (ii).

2.2
Combinatorial Proof

For the rest of this article we will use calligraphy style capital letters to denote the set of partitions enumerated by the function denoted by the same letters. For example,
\mathcal{P}(n)
denotes the set of partitions of
n
and
\mathcal{POD}_{e}(n)
is the set of partitions of
n
into an even number of parts which are not congruent to
2
modulo
4
. Recalling that
pod(n)
has more than one partition theoretic interpretation, in the sequel we assume that

\mathcal{POD}(n)
represents the set of partitions of
n
having distinct odd parts.

If
\lambda
is a partition of
n
, we say that the size of
\lambda
is
n
and write
|\lambda|=n
. The length of
\lambda
, denoted by
\ell(\lambda)
, is the number of parts in
\lambda
. We make the convention that
\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)+1}=0
. Given two partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
, we denote by
\lambda\cup\mu
the partition whose parts are precisely the parts of
\lambda
and
\mu
(with multiplicity). If each part of
\mu
is also a part of
\lambda
with equal or larger multiplicity, we denote by
\lambda\setminus\mu
the partition obtained from
\lambda
by removing the parts of
\mu
(with multiplicity).
Finally, we will often write a partition
\lambda
as
(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})
, where
\lambda^{e}
(respectively
\lambda^{o}
) consists of the even (respectively odd) parts of
\lambda
.

We start by proving the first identity of Theorem
1.1
.
Denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(n)
the set of partitions of
n
into odd parts repeated no more than three times.
Glaisher’s transformation
[
24
]
, which iteratively splits each even part of a distinct partition into two equal parts until all parts are odd, yields a bijection between
\mathcal{Q}_{0}(n)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(n)
.

For any partition
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(n)
, we have
\ell(\lambda)\equiv n\pmod{2}
. Thus, if
n
is even (respectively odd),
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(n)
is a subset of
\mathcal{POD}_{e}(n)
(respectively
\mathcal{POD}_{o}(n)
).

Let
\mathcal{A}(n)=(\mathcal{POD}_{e}(n)\cup\mathcal{POD}_{o}(n))\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(n)
. Inspired by
[
27
]
, we define an involution
\varphi:\mathcal{A}(n)\to\mathcal{A}(n)
that reverses the parity of the length of partitions. First, we introduce more useful notation. For any partition
\lambda
, if
d
is a part of
\lambda
with multiplicity
m_{d}
, we denote by
s_{d}
the nonnegative integer satisfying
2^{s_{d}}\leq m_{d}<2^{s_{d}+1}
.
If
\lambda\in\mathcal{A}(n)
has an odd part
d
with
m_{d}\geq 4
, we let
r_{\lambda}
be the largest value of
2^{s_{d}}d
among all such parts (else we let
r_{\lambda}=0
). If
\lambda^{e}=\emptyset
, it follows from the definition of
\mathcal{A}(n)
that
r_{\lambda}\neq 0
. We define
\varphi(\lambda)
as follows.

(i)

If
r_{\lambda}\geq\lambda_{1}^{e}
, merge
2^{s_{d}}
copies of
d
into a new part to obtain
\varphi(\lambda)
.

(ii)

If
r_{\lambda}<\lambda_{1}^{e}
, split
\lambda_{1}^{e}=2^{k_{1}}c_{1}
, where
k_{1}\geq 1
and
c_{1}
is odd, into
2^{k_{1}}
parts equal to
c_{1}
obtain
\varphi(\lambda)
.

Partitions from case (i) are mapped by
\varphi
to partitions from case (ii) and vice-versa. Moreover,
\varphi
is its own inverse and reverses the parity of the length of partitions.
This finishes the proof of the first identity of Theorem
1.1
.

For the second identity,
denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
the set of partitions
\lambda=(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})
of
n
such that
\lambda^{o}
has distinct parts and each part of
\lambda^{e}
is congruent to
2
modulo
4
and has even multiplicity. We use the following variant of Glaisher’s transformation to create a bijection between
\mathcal{Q}_{2}(n)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
: given
\eta\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}(n)
,
split each part of
\eta
having the form
2^{k}c
, with
k\geqslant 2
and
c
odd, into
2^{k-1}
parts equal to
2c
. The inverse of this transformation iteratively merges equal parts of a partition in
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
until all parts are distinct. Since even parts of partitions in
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
have even multiplicity, all obtained even parts are divisible by
4
.

For any partition
\lambda\in\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
we have
\ell(\lambda)\equiv n\pmod{2}
. Thus, if
n
is even (respectively odd),
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
is a subset of
\mathcal{B}_{4,e}(n)
(respectively
\mathcal{B}_{4.o}(n)
).

Let
\mathcal{B}(n)=(\mathcal{B}_{4,e}(n)\cup\mathcal{B}_{4,o}(n))\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}(n)
. Notice that for every partition
(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})\in\mathcal{B}(n)
, if
\lambda^{o}
has distinct parts, then there is at least one part in
\lambda^{e}
with odd multiplicity. We define a transformation
\varepsilon:\mathcal{B}(n)\to\mathcal{B}(n)
as follows.

(i)

If
\lambda
has a part
4k+2
with odd multiplicity with the property that all parts less than
2k+1
in
\lambda^{o}
have multiplicity one, let
4a+2
be the smallest such part and define
\varepsilon(\lambda)=(\lambda^{e}\setminus(4a+2),\lambda^{o}\cup(2a+1,2a+1))
.

(ii)

Else, let
2c+1
be the smallest repeated part in
\lambda^{o}
and define
\varepsilon(\lambda)=(\lambda^{e}\cup(4c+2),\lambda^{o}\setminus(2c+1,2c+1))
.

Then
\varepsilon
is an involution on
\mathcal{B}(n)
that maps a partition from case (i) to a partition from case (ii) and vice-versa, and changes the length of a partition by one. This finishes the proof of the second identity of Theorem
1.1
.

3.1
Analytic proofs

The following theta identities are often attributed to Gauss
[
5
, p.23, Eqs. (2.2.12), (2.2.13)]
:

1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{n^{2}}=\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}}{(-q;q)_{\infty}}

(5)

and

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)/2}=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}.

(6)

The Jacobi triple product identity (cf.
[
23
, Eq. (1.6.1)]
) states that

(z;q)_{\infty}(q/z;q)_{\infty}(q;q)_{\infty}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-z)^{n}\,q^{n(n-1)/2}.

(7)

We can write

\displaystyle(-q,-q^{2},-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(-q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(q^{4};q^{8})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{4n^{2}},

(8)

and

\displaystyle(-q,-q^{3},-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{8})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(q^{2},q^{6},q^{8};q^{8})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{2n(2n-1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)},

(9)

where we have invoked (
7
) with
q
replaced by
q^{8}
and
z
replaced by
q^{2}
.
Thus, we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{4n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{4n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(pod(n)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\,pod\big(n-4k^{2}\big)\right)q^{n},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{2}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,pod\big(n-k(k+1)\big)\right)q^{n},

from which Theorem
1.3
follows.

Using (
6
), the relations (
8
) and (
9
) can be written as

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)/2}\right)=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{4n^{2}}

and

\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{2}(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)/2}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)}.

We rewrite these identities as

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,Q_{0}\big(n-k(k+1)/2\big)\right)q^{n}=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\,q^{4n^{2}}

and

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,Q_{2}\big(n-k(k+1)/2\big)\right)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}\,q^{n(n+1)},

from which Theorem
1.4
follows.

\alpha=(18,16,12,10,6,4)
and
\beta=(6,4)
.

The triple
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)
is completely determined by
(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})
and
k
. Moreover
|\lambda^{o}|+|\alpha|+|\beta|=n
and
k\leqslant\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\leqslant k+1
. Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{k}(n)
the set of triples of partitions
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)
, where
\lambda^{o}
is a partition into distinct odd parts,
\alpha
and
\beta
are partitions into distinct even parts,
|\lambda^{o}|+|\alpha|+|\beta|=n
, and
k\leqslant\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\leqslant k+1
.
Then the transformation described above is a bijection between
\mathcal{POD}(n-k(k+1))
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}(n)
(for the inverse transformation we refer the reader to
[
9
, Section 2.1]
, where a similar transformation for ordinary Ferrers diagrams is defined). Thus, it remains to show that

\displaystyle{Q_{2}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}\,|\mathcal{A}_{k}(n)|}.

(10)

Let
\mathcal{A}(n)
be the set of triples
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)
, where
\lambda^{o}
is a partition into distinct odd parts and
\alpha
and
\beta
are partitions into distinct even parts such that
|\lambda^{o}|+|\alpha|+|\beta|=n
and
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\geqslant 0
.

Each triple
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}(n)
with
\ell(\alpha)>\ell(\beta)
appears in exactly two of the sets
\mathcal{A}_{k}(n)
, namely when
k=\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)
and when
k=\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)-1
. Triples
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}(n)
with
\ell(\alpha)=\ell(\beta)
appear only in
\mathcal{A}_{0}(n)
. Thus, from the parity of triangular numbers, the contribution of
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)
with
|\lambda^{o}|+|\alpha|+|\beta|=n
to the righthand side of (
10
) is

\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if }\ell(\alpha)=\ell(\beta),\\
0&\mbox{ if }\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\equiv 1\pmod{2},\\
2&\mbox{ if }\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
-2&\mbox{ if }\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\equiv 2\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

We denote by
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{0}(n)
be the multiset of triples
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}(n)
satisfying
\alpha\neq\beta
,
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, and if
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)>0
, the triple has multiplicity
2
in
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{0}(n)
, and if
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)=0
, the triple has multiplicity
1
. Similarly, we denote by
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
be the multiset of triples
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}(n)
with
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and each triple has multiplicity
2
.

For any triple
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}(n)
let
i
be the smallest positive integer such that
\alpha_{i}\neq\beta_{i}
(we make the convention that
\beta_{\ell(\beta)+1}=0
).

We now define a map from
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{0}(n)
to
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
as follows.
Start with
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{o}(n)
and suppose
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)=k
.

Case 1:
\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
. Let
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha\cup(\beta_{i})
and
\tilde{\beta}=\beta\setminus(\beta_{i})
. Then,
\ell(\tilde{\alpha})-\ell(\tilde{\beta})=k+2
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}>\tilde{\beta}_{i}
, and the first
i-1
parts of
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\beta}
are equal. Moreover,
the triple
(\lambda^{o},\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})
lies in
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
.

Case 2:
\beta_{i}<\alpha_{i}
. Let
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha\setminus(\alpha_{i})
and
\tilde{\beta}=\beta\cup(\alpha_{i})
. Then,
\ell(\tilde{\alpha})-\ell(\tilde{\beta})=k-2
,
\tilde{\beta}_{i}>\tilde{\alpha}_{i}
, and the first
i-1
parts of
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\beta}
are equal.
If
k>0
, the triple
(\lambda^{o},\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})
lies in
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
. If
k=0
, since
\ell(\tilde{\beta})-\ell(\tilde{\alpha})=2
, the triple
(\lambda^{o},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\alpha})
lies in
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
. Notice that this partition is also obtained from
(\lambda^{o},\beta,\alpha)
which is in Case 1.

For the inverse of the transformation, start with
(\lambda^{o},\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
and suppose
\ell(\tilde{\alpha})-\ell(\tilde{\beta})=k
.

Case I:
\tilde{\beta}_{i}<\tilde{\alpha}_{i}
. Let
\alpha=\tilde{\alpha}\cup(\tilde{\beta}_{i})
and
\beta=\tilde{\beta}\setminus(\tilde{\beta}_{i})
. Then
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)=k+2
and
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{o}(n)
.

Case II:
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}<\tilde{\beta}_{i}
. Let
\alpha=\tilde{\alpha}\setminus(\tilde{\alpha}_{i})
and
\beta=\tilde{\beta}\cup(\tilde{\alpha}_{i})
Then
\ell(\alpha)-\ell(\beta)=k-2
and
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{o}(n)
. If
k=2
, one copy of
(\lambda^{o},\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})
is mapped to
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)
and the second copy is mapped to
(\lambda^{o},\beta,\alpha)
.

Therefore, the transformation defined above is a bijection between
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{0}(n)
and
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{A}_{2}(n)
. Then, the righthand side of (
10
) is equal to the number of triples
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}(n)
with
\alpha=\beta
. These triples are in bijection with the partitions in
\mathcal{Q}_{2}(n)
via the mapping that takes
(\lambda^{o},\alpha,\alpha)
to
\lambda^{o}\cup 2\alpha
, where
2\alpha
is the partition obtained from
\alpha
by doubling each of its parts.

4
Linear inequalities involving
pod(n)

Linear inequalities involving partition functions have been studied extensively
[
7
,
8
,
26
,
31
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
For example, V. J. W. Guo and J. Zeng
[
26
]
proved that

\displaystyle(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,pod\big(n-j(j+1)/2\big)\geqslant 0,

for
n,k>0
.
Recently, G. E. Andrews and M. Merca
[
8
, Corollary 10]
established a
partition theoretic interpretation of this inequality by showing that

\displaystyle(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,pod\big(n-j(j+1)/2\big)=MP_{k}(n),

where
MP_{k}(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
in which the first part larger
than
2k-1
is odd and appears exactly
k
times and all other odd parts appear at
most once. For example,
MP_{2}(19)=10
, and the partitions in question are

\displaystyle(9,9,1),\ (9,5,5),\ (8,5,5,1),\ (7,7,3,2),\ (7,7,2,2,1),\ (7,5,5,2),

\displaystyle(6,5,5,3),\ (6,5,5,2,1),\ (5,5,3,2,2,2),\ (5,5,2,2,2,2,1).

Shortly after that, C. Ballantine, M. Merca, D. Passary and A. J. Yee
[
11
]
gave combinatorial proofs of this interpretation.

In this section, inspired by Theorem
1.3
, we obtain new infinite families of linear inequalities for
pod(n)
. To this end, we recall that
an overpartition of
n
is a nonincreasing sequence of natural numbers whose sum is
n
in which the first occurrence of a number may be overlined
[
15
]
. For example, the eight overpartitions of
3
are

(3),\ (\overline{3}),\ (2,1),\ (2,\overline{1}),\ (\overline{2},1),\ (\overline{2},\overline{1}),\ (1,1,1),\ (\overline{1},1,1).

G. E. Andrews and M. Merca
[
8
]
introduced the function

\overline{M}_{k}(n)
which counts the number of overpartitions of
n
in which the first part larger than
k
appears at least
k+1
times. For example,
\overline{M}_{2}(12)=16
, with the relevant overpartions being

\displaystyle(4,4,4),\,(\overline{4},4,4),\,(3,3,3,3),\,(\overline{3},3,3,3),\,(3,3,3,2,1),\,(3,3,3,\overline{2},1),

\displaystyle(3,3,3,2,\overline{1}),\,(3,3,3,\overline{2},\overline{1}),\,(\overline{3},3,3,2,1),\,(\overline{3},3,3,\overline{2},1),\,(\overline{3},3,3,2,\overline{1}),

\displaystyle(\overline{3},3,3,\overline{2},\overline{1}),\,(3,3,3,1,1,1),\,(3,3,3,\overline{1},1,1),\,(\overline{3},3,3,1,1,1),\,(\overline{3},3,3,\overline{1},1,1).

We now prove an identity that has Theorem
1.3
.(i) as its limiting case when
k\to\infty
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
n,k>0
, we have

(-1)^{k}\left(pod(n)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,pod\big(n-4j^{2}\big)-Q_{0}(n)\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor n/4\right\rfloor}Q_{0}(n-4j)\,\overline{M}_{k}(j).

Proof.

According to G. E. Andrews and M. Merca
[
8
, Theorem 7]
, we have
the following truncated version of (
5
):

\displaystyle\frac{(-q;q)_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,q^{j^{2}}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\qquad=1+(-1)^{k}\frac{(-q;q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{2\,q^{j(k+1)}}{1-q^{j}}\cdot\frac{(-q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}{(q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}.

As explained in
[
8
, Proof of Corollary 8]
, the series on the right hand side of this identity is the generating function for
\overline{M}_{k}(n)
, i.e.,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{M}_{k}(n)\,q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{2\,q^{j(k+1)}}{1-q^{j}}\cdot\frac{(-q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}{(q^{j+1};q)_{\infty}}.

Therefore, replacing
q
by
q^{4}
in (
11
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{(-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,q^{4j^{2}}\right)=1+(-1)^{k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{M}_{k}(n)\,q^{4n}.

Multiplying both sides of this identity by

(-q,-q^{2},-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,q^{4j^{2}}\right)-(-q,-q^{2},-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(-1)^{k}\,(-q,-q^{2},-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{M}_{k}(n)\,q^{4n}.

Then, using (
2
) and (
3
), the identity becomes

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,q^{4j^{2}}\right)-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{M}_{k}(n)\,q^{4n}\right)

The theorem follows by comparing coefficients of
q^{n}
on the two sides
of this equation.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
4.1
we obtain the following infinite family of linear inequalities involving
pod(n)
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For
n,k>0
, we have

(-1)^{k}\left(pod(n)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}\,pod\big(n-4j^{2}\big)-Q_{0}(n)\right)\geqslant 0,

with strict inequality if and only if
n\geqslant 4(k+1)^{2}
.

Remark
.

For example,

\displaystyle pod(n)-2pod(n-4)\leqslant Q_{0}(n),

\displaystyle pod(n)-2pod(n-4)+2pod(n-16)\geqslant Q_{0}(n),

\displaystyle pod(n)-2pod(n-4)+2pod(n-16)-2pod(n-36)\leqslant Q_{0}(n),

\displaystyle pod(n)-2pod(n-4)+2pod(n-16)-2pod(n-36)+2pod(n-64)\geqslant Q_{0}(n).

Our next result has Theorem
1.3
.(ii) as its limiting case when
k\to\infty
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For
n,k>0
, we have

(-1)^{k}\left(Q_{2}(n)-\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,pod\big(n-j(j+1)\big)\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}Q_{2}(n-2j)\,MP_{k}(j).

Proof.

According to G. E. Andrews and M. Merca
[
8
, Theorem 9]
, we have
the following truncated version of (
6
):

\displaystyle\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-q)^{j(j+1)/2}

\displaystyle\qquad=1+(-1)^{k-1}\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{k}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k-1}}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(2j+1)}\,(-q^{2j+3};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2j+2};q^{2})_{\infty}}.

(12)

As explained in
[
8
, Proof of Corollary 10]
, the series on the right hand side of this identity is the generating function for
MP_{k}(n)
, i.e.,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}MP_{k}(n)\,q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{k}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k-1}}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(2j+1)}\,(-q^{2j+3};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2j+2};q^{2})_{\infty}}.

Therefore, replacing
q
by
q^{2}
in (

) yields

\displaystyle\frac{(-q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,q^{j(j+1)}=1+(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}MP_{k}(n)\,q^{2n}.

Multiplying both sides of this identity by

(-q,-q^{3},-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,q^{j(j+1)}-(-q,-q^{3},-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(-1)^{k-1}\,(-q,-q^{3},-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}MP_{k}(n)\,q^{2n}.

Then, using (
2
) and (
4
), the identity becomes

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,q^{j(j+1)}\right)-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{2}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(-1)^{k-1}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{2}(n)\,q^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}MP_{k}(n)\,q^{2n}\right).

The theorem follows by comparing coefficients of
q^{n}
on the two sides
of this equation.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
4.3
we obtain the following infinite family of linear inequalities involving

.

Corollary 4.4
.

For
n,k>0
, we have

(-1)^{k}\left(Q_{2}(n)-\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(-1)^{j(j+1)/2}\,pod\big(n-j(j+1)\big)\right)\geqslant 0,

with strict inequality if and only if

Remark

For example,

\displaystyle pod(n)-pod(n-2)\geqslant Q_{2}(n),

\displaystyle pod(n)-pod(n-2)-pod(n-6)+pod(n-12)\leqslant Q_{2}(n),

\displaystyle pod(n)-pod(n-2)-pod(n-6)+pod(n-12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+pod(n-20)-pod(n-30)\geqslant Q_{2}(n),

\displaystyle pod(n)-pod(n-2)-pod(n-6)+pod(n-12)

6
Open problems and concluding remarks

In this paper, inspired by the decompositions of
Q_{0}(n)
and
Q_{2}(n)

in terms of the partition function
pod(n)
given by Theorem
1.3
, we derived new infinite families of linear inequalities involving
pod(n)
.
Here we show that there is another way to decompose
Q_{0}(n)
using
pod(n)
, and also establish decompositions of
Q_{2}(n)
and
b_{4}(n)
in terms of the function
\overline{p}(n)
which enumerates the overpartitions of
n
(see
[
15
]
).

Theorem 6.1
.

For
n\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle Q_{0}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}pod\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\right).

(14)

Analytic proof.

By (
3
) we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}=(-q,-q^{2},-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}=(-q,-q^{3},q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(-q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}.

Next, using (
2
) and replacing
q
by
q^{4}
and
z
by
-q
in (
7
), we find

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{0}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{2n^{2}-n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n(n+1)/2}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}pod(n)\,q^{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}pod\Big(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\Big)\right)q^{n}.

∎

Combinatorial proof.

We begin by recalling a result due to G. N. Watson
[
59
]
. If we denote by
Q_{odd}(n)
the number of partitions of
n
into distinct odd parts, then

Q_{odd}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{4}\right).

(15)

A combinatorial proof of (
15
) is given in
[
61
]
using abacus displays, which were first introduced in
[
30
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we briefly describe the construction here, using the language of
[
51
, section 2]
.

Given a partition
\lambda
, fill the boxes of its Ferrers diagram with alternating
0
s and
1
s starting with
0
in the upper left corner. Draw horizontal lines through rows ending in
0
and vertical lines through columns ending in
1
. The boxes at the intersection of the lines (pushed toward the northwest) form the Ferrers diagram of a partition
\alpha
. Repeat the process with horizontal lines through rows ending in
1
and vertical lines through columns ending in
0
to obtain a partition
\beta
. The pair
(\alpha,\beta)
is referred to as the
2
-quotient of
\lambda
. The
2
-core of
\lambda
is the staircase partition
\delta
whose Ferrers diagram is obtained from the diagram of
\lambda
by removing
2
-hooks (either two boxes in a row or two boxes in a column) such that after every removal the obtained diagram is the Ferrers diagram of a partition. Then

|\lambda|=|\delta|+2|\alpha|+2|\beta|.

It is shown in
[
30
, Theorem 2.7.30]
that the triple
(\delta,\alpha,\beta)
completely determines
\lambda
. Clearly, if
\lambda
is self-conjugate, then
\alpha=\beta
and
(\delta,\alpha)
determines
\lambda
.

Given a partition
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}_{odd}(n)
, consider the partitions
\mu^{sc}
whose Ferrers diagram is obtained from that of
\mu
by straightening the hooks nested along the diagonal (this is the classical bijection proving that the number of partitions of
n
into distinct odd parts equals the number of self-conjugate partitions of
n
). Define
\xi(\mu)
to be the partition
\alpha
in the
2
-quotient
(\alpha,\alpha)
of
\mu^{sc}
. Then the transformation

\xi:\mathcal{Q}_{odd}(n)\to\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{4}\right)

is a bijection.

To prove (
14
), start with a partition
\lambda=(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}(n)
. The parts of
\lambda^{e}
are congruent to
2
modulo
4
. Let
\tilde{\lambda}^{e}
be the partitions whose parts are the parts of
\lambda^{e}
divided by
2
. Thus,
\tilde{\lambda}^{e}
is a partition with distinct odd parts. We denote by
\tilde{\lambda}^{o}
the partition whose parts are the parts of
\xi(\lambda^{o})
multiplied by
2
. Let
\psi(\lambda)=\tilde{\lambda}^{o}\cup\tilde{\lambda}^{e}
. Then

\psi:\mathcal{Q}_{0}(n)\to\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{POD}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\right)

is a bijection.

∎

In a similar way,
Q_{2}(n)
and
b_{4}(n)
can be expressed in terms of
\overline{p}(n)
.

Theorem 6.2
.

For
n\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle Q_{2}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{4}\right),

and

\displaystyle b_{4}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\right).

Analytic proof.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
6.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{2}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle=(-q,-q^{3},q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(-q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n(n+1)/2}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}(n)\,q^{4n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}\Big(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{4}\Big)\right)q^{n}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{4}(n)\,q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}=(-q;q)_{\infty}(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(-q,-q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=(-q,-q^{3},q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}\cdot\frac{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n(n+1)/2}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}(n)\,q^{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{p}\Big(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\Big)\right)q^{n}.

∎

Combinatorial proof.

We start with a partition
\lambda=(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}(n)
. The parts of
\lambda^{e}
are congruent to
0
modulo
4
. Let
\bar{\lambda}^{e}
be the overpartition whose parts are the parts of
\lambda^{e}
divided by
4
and each part is overlined. Let
\rho(\lambda)=\xi(\lambda^{o})\cup\bar{\lambda}^{e}
. Then

\rho:\mathcal{Q}_{2}(n)\to\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{4}\right)

is a bijection.

To prove the second identity we make use of the Fu-Tang combinatorial proof
[
21
]
of another identity due to G. N. Watson
[
59
]
. If we denote by
Q(n)
the number of partitions of
n
into distinct parts, then the Fu-Tang bijection

\zeta:\mathcal{Q}(n)\to\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\right)

(see Section 2 of
[
10
]
for succinct description of
\zeta
) yields

Q(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\right).

(16)

Start with
\lambda=(\lambda^{e},\lambda^{o})\in\mathcal{B}_{4}(n)
. The parts of
\lambda^{e}
are congruent to
2
modulo
4
. We divide each part of
\lambda^{e}
by
2
to obtain a partition
\tilde{\lambda}^{e}
into odd parts. We use Glaisher’s bijection to transform
\tilde{\lambda}^{e}
into a partition with distinct parts whose parts we overline to obtain an overpartition
\bar{\lambda}^{e}
.
Next, we apply Glaisher’s bijection to
\lambda^{o}
to obtain a partition
\tilde{\lambda}^{o}
into distinct parts and let
\chi(\lambda)=\zeta(\tilde{\lambda}^{o})\cup\bar{\lambda}^{e}
. Then the function

\chi:\mathcal{B}_{4}(n)\to\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{\mathcal{P}}\left(\frac{n-k(k+1)/2}{2}\right)

is a bijection.
∎

We note that Theorems

and
