Analysis of a class of Multi-Level Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms based on Independent Metropolis-Hastings Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Juan P. Madrigal-Cianci, Fabio Nobile, Raúl Tempone

Abstract
In this work we present, analyze, and implement a class of Multi-Level Markov chain Monte Carlo (ML-MCMC) algorithms based on independent Metropolis-Hastings proposals for Bayesian inverse problems. In this context, the likelihood function involves solving a complex differential model, which is then approximated on a sequence of increasingly accurate discretizations. The key point of this algorithm is to construct highly coupled Markov chains together with the standard Multi-level Monte Carlo argument to obtain a better cost-tolerance complexity than a single level MCMC algorithm. Our method extends the ideas of Dodwell, et al. “A hierarchical multilevel Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm with applications to uncertainty quantification in subsurface flow,” SIAM/ASA Journal on Uncertainty Quantification 3.1 (2015): 1075-1108, to a wider range of proposal distributions. We present a thorough convergence analysis of the ML-MCMC method proposed, and show, in particular, that (i) under some mild conditions on the (independent) proposals and the family of posteriors, there exists a unique invariant probability measure for the coupled chains generated by our method, and (ii) that such coupled chains are uniformly ergodic. We also generalize the cost-tolerance theorem of Dodwell et al., to our wider class of ML-MCMC algorithms. Finally, we propose a self-tuning continuation-type ML-MCMC algorithm (C-ML-MCMC). The presented method is tested on an array of academic examples, where some of our theoretical results are numerically verified. These numerical experiments evidence how our extended ML-MCMC method is robust when targeting some pathological posteriors, for which some of the previously proposed ML-MCMC algorithms fail.

Introduction

By now, Multi-Level Monte Carlo (MLMC) methods are well-established computational techniques
[
12
]
to compute expectations that arise in stochastic simulations in cases in which the stochastic model can not be simulated exactly, rather approximated, at different levels of accuracy and, as such, at different computational costs. Despite their wide-spread applicability, extending these MLMC ideas to Multi-Level Markov Chain Monte Carlo (ML-MCMC) methods to compute expectations with respect to (w.r.t) a complex target distribution from which independent (whether exact or approximate) sampling is not accessible, has only recently been attempted, with only a handful of works dedicated to this task. This situation arises, for instance, in Bayesian inverse problems where one wishes to compute the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]
of some output quantity of interest
\mathsf{QoI}
with respect to the posterior measure
\mu^{y}
of some parameters
\theta\in\mathsf{X}
given some indirect noise measurements
y={\mathcal{F}}(\theta)+\eta
, where
\eta
is the additive noise and
{\mathcal{F}}
is the forward operator, which may involve the solution of a differential equation. At their core, ML-MCMC methods for BIP introduce a hierarchy of discretization levels
\ell=0,1,\dots,\mathsf{L}
of the underlying forward operator, which in turn induce a family of posterior probability measures
\mu^{y}_{\ell}
, approximating
\mu^{y}
with increasing levels of accuracy as
\ell\to\infty
. Given some
\mu^{y}
-integrable quantity of interest
\mathsf{QoI},
one can approximate the expectation of
\mathsf{QoI}
over
\mu^{y}
by the usual telescoping sum argument of MLMC, namely

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\simeq\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{0}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{0}]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1}]\right)

(2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\Delta\mathsf{E}_{\ell},

(3)

with
\Delta\mathsf{E}_{\ell}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1}]
,
\Delta\mathsf{E}_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{0}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{0}]
and where, for
\ell=0,1,\dots,\mathsf{L},

\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}
is a
\mu^{y}_{\ell}
-integrable, level-
\ell
approximation of the quantity of interest
\mathsf{QoI}
. This telescoping sum presents the basis for various types of multi-level techniques for Bayesian inverse problems. The work
[
15
]
, for example, approximates the expectation (
3
) by splitting each
\Delta\mathsf{E}_{\ell}
into three different terms, which are then computed using a mixture of importance-sampling and MCMC techniques. A multi-index generalization of such method is presented in
[
16
]
. In addition, similar multi-level ideas have also been attempted in the context of Multi-Level Sequential Monte Carlo (MLSMC) in the works
[
2
,
17
,
20
]
.

In this work, we rather follow the approach proposed in
[
9
]
, which is probably the first proposition of multi-level ideas for BIP and which consists in approximating
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]
by the following ergodic estimator:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}\mathsf{QoI}_{0}(\theta_{0,0}^{(n)})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}\underbrace{\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell,\ell}^{n})-\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1}(\theta_{\ell,\ell-1}^{n})}_{\text{$:=Y^{n}_{\ell}$}},

(4)

where
\{\theta^{n}_{\cdot,\ell}\}_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}
is an ergodic Markov chain with invariant distribution
\mu^{y}_{\ell}
. The key idea here is to couple the chains
\{\theta_{\ell,\ell-1}^{n},\theta_{\ell,\ell}^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}
so that they are highly correlated and the variance of the ergodic estimator
\mathbb{V}[N_{\ell}^{-1}\sum_{n}Y_{\ell}^{n}]
is smaller and smaller as
\ell
increases. By carefully choosing
N_{\ell},
this method can achieve a much better sampling complexity (in terms of cost versus tolerance) than its single level counterparts (see
[
9
]
).

Most of the existing literature on ML-MCMC has focused on the construction of these types of couplings
[
7
,
9
]
. In
[
9
]
, the authors use (an approximation of) the posterior distribution at the previous discretization level
\ell-1
as a proposal for level
\ell
. This is practically implemented by sub-sampling from the chain
\{\theta^{n}_{\ell-1,\ell-1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{\ell-1}}
.

Such an idea has been recently expanded in
[
7
]
, where the sub-sampling idea is combined with the so-called Dimension Independent Likelihood Informed (DILI) MCMC method of
[
8
]
to generate proposed samples at level 0 in their ML-MCMC algorithm. Some further work combining multi-level Monte Carlo ideas with Bayesian inference has been presented in
[
18
]
, where the authors use rejection-free Markov transitions kernels, such as Gibbs sampler, in order to couple the multi-level MCMC chains at two consecutive levels.

However, investigating more theoretical aspects of ML-MCMC algorithms, such as the existence of an invariant measure for the coupled chains and the type of convergence to such measure (provided it exists), have been widely overlooked, and one of the aims of this paper is to fill this gap.

This work presents several novel contributions. First, we present a ML-MCMC algorithm where chains are coupled using independent Metropolis Hastings-type proposals as in
[
9
]
, however, allowing for a wider class of admissible proposals. In particular, we show that the sub-sampling approach in
[
9
]
can be replaced by a properly chosen independent Metropolis proposal (IMH) (that is, a proposal for which the proposed state is independent of the current state of the chain), which proposes the same state to the two chains
\{\theta_{\ell,\ell-1}^{n},\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell}\}_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}
targeting
\mu^{y}_{\ell-1},\mu^{y}_{\ell}
respectively, which is then accepted by the usual Metropolis-Hastings criterion. This ensures the coupling of the chains. Such proposal can be, for example, the prior, a Laplace approximation, or even a kernel density approximation of the posterior at the previous level. Obviously, the choice of proposal has a direct impact on the joint invariant distribution
\nu_{\ell}
of the coupled chain
\{\theta_{\ell,\ell-1}^{n},\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell}\}_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}
provided it exists, hence on the variance of the ergodic estimator
N_{\ell}^{-1}\sum_{n}Y_{\ell}^{n}
.

The main contribution of this work is an in-depth convergence analysis of our extended ML-MCMC method. More precisely, we provide sufficient conditions on the (marginal) level
\ell
posterior and on the proposal probability measure
Q_{\ell}
so that there exists a unique joint invariant probability measure for the coupled chain. Such a contribution is presented in Theorem
6
, where it is shown that, under some mild conditions on
Q_{\ell},\mu^{y}_{\ell},\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}
, the ML-MCMC algorithm presented here-in (i) has a unique, invariant probability measure for the joint chain at level
\ell
and (ii) is uniformly ergodic. Following
[
32
]
, we provide also computable, quantitative, non-asymptotic error estimators for the ergodic estimator (
3
). This allows us on the one hand, to generalize the cost-tolerance result of
[
9
]
to our extended MLMCMC method and, on the other hand, to propose an adaptive ML-MCMC algorithm in which the number of levels
\mathsf{L}
and chain lengths
N_{\ell}
are determined on the fly, in the spirit of the continuation MLMC method of
[
6
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce some notation and standard results in the theory of Markov chains and Bayesian inverse problems. We present our ML-MCMC method in Section
3
, and then proceed to analyze its convergence in Section
4
. Section
5
is dedicated to the generalization to our case of the cost-tolerance analysis result of
[
9
]
. In Section
6
we discuss the continuation-type algorithm and implementation details. In Section
7
we numerically verify some of our results in two academic examples, and, lastly, we present some conclusions and finalizing remarks in Section
8
.

2.1
Background on Markov chains

We recall some definitions and standard results from the theory of Markov chains. Let
(\mathsf{X},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{X}})
be a separable Banach space with Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}),
and denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X})
the set of probability measures on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))
. For some
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X})
and any
q\in[1,\infty],
we define the Banach spaces

\displaystyle L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f:\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ $\mu$-measurable s.t. }\int_{\mathsf{X}}|f|^{q}(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }q<\infty,

(5)

\displaystyle L_{\infty}(\mathsf{X},\mu)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f:\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ $\mu$-measurable s.t. }\underset{\theta\in\mathsf{X}}{\mathrm{ess}\sup}|f(\theta)|<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }q=\infty,

(6)

endowed with the norms

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}:=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathsf{X}}|f|^{q}(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)\right)^{1/q},\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }q<\infty,

(7)

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}:=

\displaystyle\underset{\theta\in\mathsf{X}}{\mathrm{ess}\sup}|f(\theta)|,\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }q=\infty.

(8)

In the particular case where
q=2
,
L_{2}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(\theta)g(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta),\ f,g\in L_{2}(\mathsf{X},\mu).

(9)

For any
q\in[1,\infty],
we define the subspace
L_{q}^{0}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
of
L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
as

\displaystyle L^{0}_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu):=\left\{f\in L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)\text{ s.t. }\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)=0\right\}.

(11)

Recall that a
Markov transition kernel
is a function
p:\mathsf{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})\rightarrow[0,1]
such that

1.

For each
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})
, the mapping
\mathsf{X}\ni\theta\to p(\theta,A)
, is a
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})
-measurable real-valued function.

2.

For each
\theta
in
\mathsf{X}
, the mapping
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})\ni A\to p(\theta,A)
, is a probability measure on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))
.

Given a Markov transition kernel
p
, we denote by
P
its associated
Markov transition operator
which acts to the left on measures,
\mu\to\mu P\in\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X}),
and to the right on functions,
f\to Pf,
measurable on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})),
such that

\displaystyle(\mu P)(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{X}}p(\theta,A)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}),

(12)

\displaystyle(Pf)(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(\vartheta)p(\theta,\mathrm{d}\vartheta),\quad\forall\theta\in\mathsf{X}.

(13)

We say that a Markov operator
P
is
\mu
-
invariant
if
\mu P=\mu
. We say that a Markov operator
P
is
\mu
-
reversible
if

\displaystyle\int_{B}p(\theta,A)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\int_{A}p(\theta,B)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}).

(14)

It is a well-known fact that if
P
is
\mu
-reversible, then
P
is
\mu
-invariant. The reverse is in general not true. The main idea behind using Markov
chain Monte Carlo methods to sample a measure of interest
\mu
on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})),
is to create a Markov chain with initial state
\theta^{0}\sim\lambda^{0}
, for some
\lambda^{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X}),
using a
\mu
-invariant Markov transition operator
P
. The Markov chain
\{\theta^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is then generated by sampling
\theta^{n}\sim p(\theta^{n-1},\cdot),\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
. Under some suitable conditions, it can be shown that
\lambda^{0}P^{n}\to\mu
as
n\to\infty
where the convergence is with respect to a suitable distance between probability measures.

2.2
Bayesian inverse problems and Markov chain Monte Carlo

Let
(\mathsf{X},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{X}})
and
(\mathsf{Y},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{Y}})
be separable Banach spaces with associated Borel
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}),\ \mathcal{B}(\mathsf{Y})
, and let us define the forward operator
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y}
. In inverse problems, we use some data
y\in\mathsf{Y}
, usually polluted by some random noise
\varepsilon\sim\mu_{\textrm{noise}},\ \varepsilon\in\mathsf{Y}
, to determine a possible state
\theta\in\mathsf{X}
that may have generated the data. Assuming an additive noise model, the relationship between
\theta
and
y
is given by:

\displaystyle{y}={{\mathcal{F}}}(\theta)+{\varepsilon},\quad\varepsilon\sim\mu_{\textrm{noise}}.

(15)

Here,
\theta
can be a set of parameters of a possibly non-linear Partial Differential Equation (PDE) and the data correspond to some observable (output) quantities related to the solution of the PDE. The forward operator
{\mathcal{F}}
describes then the parameter-to-outputs map and its evaluation at any
\theta\in\mathsf{X}
implies the solution of a PDE. As such, it is, in general, not possible to evaluate
{\mathcal{F}}
exactly and one has rather to use numerical methods. We denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathsf{L}}
the numerical approximation of the forward map at an accuracy level
\mathsf{L}
. In a Bayesian setting, we consider the parameter
\theta
to be uncertain and model it as a random variable with a given
prior
probability measure
{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}
on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))
. Such a prior models the knowledge we have on the uncertainty in
\theta
, before observing the data
y
. For simplicity, we will assume that
\mathsf{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\geq 1,
and that
\mu_{\textrm{noise}}
has a density
\pi_{\textrm{noise}},
with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and that such a measure is strictly positive and bounded, i.e.,
\exists\mathsf{A}>0
such that
0<\pi_{\textrm{noise}}(z)\leq\mathsf{A},

\forall z\in\mathsf{Y}
. Furthermore, if we assume that the noise
\varepsilon
and
\theta
are statistically independent (when seen as random variables on their respective spaces), then, we have that
\mathbb{P}(y-{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)\in\cdot|\theta)=\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon\in\cdot)
, i.e.,
y-{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)
conditioned on
\theta
has the same distribution as
\varepsilon
. This allows us to define the
likelihood
function

\pi_{\mathsf{L}}(y|\theta):=\pi_{\text{noise}}(y-{{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathsf{L}}}(\theta)),

(16)

and write it in terms of a
potential
function
\Phi_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta;y):\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}\to[0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta;y)=-\log\left[\pi_{\textrm{noise}}(y-\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta))/\mathsf{A}\right].

(17)

The scaling factor
\mathsf{A}^{-1}
in the definition of the potential is introduced to make it non-negative.

The function
\Phi_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta;y)
is a measure of the misfit between the recorded data
y
and the predicted value (at accuracy level
\mathsf{L}
)
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)
, and often depends only on
\left\|y-\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)\right\|_{\mathsf{Y}}
. From Bayes theorem (see e.g.,
[
10
, Section 10.2]
), it follows that the Bayesian Inverse Problem (BIP) consists in determining (or approximating) the conditional probability measure
\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}=\mathbb{P}(\theta\in\cdot|y)
of
\theta\in\mathsf{X}
given the observed data
y\in\mathsf{Y}
, which we write in terms of its Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}
:

\displaystyle\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}}=\frac{\pi_{\text{noise}}(y-{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta))}{\int\pi_{\textrm{noise}}(y-{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta))\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathsf{L}}}\exp\left(-\Phi_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta;y)\right),

(18)

\displaystyle\text{with }Z_{\mathsf{L}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathsf{X}}\exp(-\Phi_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta;y)){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}\theta).

(19)

Notice that
\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
(resp.
\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
) are a numerical approximation of a usually unattainable posterior measure (resp. density)
\mu^{y}
(resp.
\pi^{y}
). The goal of Bayesian analysis is often to explore the posterior measure or compute posterior expectations of some
\mu^{y}
-integrable output quantity of interest
\mathsf{QoI}:\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by drawing samples from the posterior measure. A common method for performing such a task is to use Markov chains Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, the most celebrated of which is, perhaps, the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
[
23
,
14
]
, which we describe briefly. Let
Q:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a continuous and strictly positive function such that, for any
\theta\in\mathsf{X}
,
\int_{\mathsf{X}}Q(\theta,z){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z)=1
. Thus, for any fixed
\theta\in\mathsf{X},

Q(\theta,\cdot)
induces a probability measure
\hat{Q}(\theta,\cdot)
having Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to the prior given by
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{Q}(\theta,\cdot)}{\mathrm{d}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}}(z)=Q(\theta,z).
Notice that, since
Q(\theta,z)
is assumed to be strictly positive for any
\theta,z\in\mathsf{X}
,
{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}
and
\hat{Q}
are equivalent probability measures. Furthermore, define the following three probability measures on
(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}))
:

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathsf{L}}(\mathrm{d}\theta,\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle:=\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\hat{Q}(\theta,\mathrm{d}z),

(20)

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}(\mathrm{d}\theta,\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle:=\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(\mathrm{d}z)\hat{Q}(z,\mathrm{d}\theta),

(21)

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}(\mathrm{d}\theta,\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle:={\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}\theta){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z).

(22)

It is known (see e.g.,
[
34
]
) that whenever
\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t
\eta_{\mathsf{L}}
(
\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}\ll\eta_{\mathsf{L}}
), one can construct a well-defined Metropolis-Hastings algorithm targeting
\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
. Clearly, since both
\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
and
Q
are strictly positive, both
\eta_{\mathsf{L}}
and
\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}
are equivalent with respect to
\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}
, with Radon-Nikodym derivatives give by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}}(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{Q}(\theta,\cdot)}{\mathrm{d}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}}(z)=\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)Q(\theta,z),

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}}(z)\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{Q}(z,\cdot)}{\mathrm{d}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}}(\theta)=\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(z)Q(z,\theta).

(24)

As such, one can define the so-called (level
\mathsf{L}
) Metropolis acceptance ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathsf{L}}}(\theta,z)=\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}}{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)(\theta,z)=\frac{\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(z)Q(z,\theta)}{\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta)Q(\theta,z)},

(25)

and, similarly, one can define the (level
\mathsf{L}
) Metropolis acceptance probability
\alpha_{\mathsf{L}}:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{X}\to[0,1]
by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta,z)=\min\left\{1,\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathsf{L}}}{\mathrm{d}\eta_{\mathsf{L}}}(\theta,z)\right\}.

(26)

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm constructs a Markov chain
\{\theta^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
by iteratively proposing at each step
n
a candidate state
z\sim Q(\theta^{n},\cdot)
, and setting
\theta^{n+1}=z
with probability
\alpha_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta^{n},z)
, and otherwise setting
\theta^{n+1}=\theta^{n}.
This procedure produces a Markov chain
\{\theta^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
whose samples are asymptotically distributed according to
\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
, provided the chain is ergodic. The procedure is depicted in Algorithm
1
.

Once samples
\{\theta^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, drawn approximately from
\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
have been obtained by some MCMC algorithm, the posterior expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]
can be approximated by the following ergodic estimator

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]\approx\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta^{n}),

(27)

where
\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}:\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}
-integrable numerical approximation of
\mathsf{QoI}
.

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm induces the following Markov transition kernel

\displaystyle p(\theta,A)=\int_{A}\alpha_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta,z)Q(\theta,z){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z)+\mbox{1\hskip-4.25pt{1}}_{\left\{\theta\in A\right\}}\int_{\mathsf{X}}(1-\alpha_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta,z))Q(\theta,z){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z),

for every

and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})
. Here,
\mbox{1\hskip-4.25pt{1}}_{\left\{\theta\in A\right\}}
is the characteristic function of the set
A
, i.e.,
\mbox{1\hskip-4.25pt{1}}_{\left\{\theta\in A\right\}}=1
if
\theta\in A
and
\mbox{1\hskip-4.25pt{1}}_{\left\{\theta\in A\right\}}=0
otherwise. Thus, such an algorithm can be understood as iteratively applying the Markov transition operator
P
associated to
p
to the initial probability measure
\lambda^{0}
.

3
Multi-level Markov Chain Monte Carlo

In general, the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm requires a large number of samples to converge —it is not uncommon for
N
to be of the order of tens of thousands. Furthermore, as it can be seen from Algorithm
1
, every time
\alpha_{\mathsf{L}}(\theta^{n},z)
is evaluated, one needs to evaluate the posterior density at each new proposed state. In PDE-based inverse problems, where evaluating
\pi^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}(z)
implies solving a possibly non-linear and time-dependent PDE on a sufficiently fine mesh (i.e., with high accuracy), the cost associated to the MH algorithm can rapidly become prohibitive. One way to alleviate this issue is by introducing multi-level techniques. To that end, let
\{M_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}

be a hierarchy of discretization parameters of the underlying mathematical model
{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)
in (
15
), which could, for instance, represent the number of degrees of freedom used in the discretization of the underlying PDE. In what follows, we consider only geometric sequences for
\{M_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}
with
M_{\ell}=sM_{\ell-1}
for some
M_{0}>0
and
s>1
. We denote the corresponding discretized forward models by
{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}(\cdot)
and the corresponding approximate quantity of interest by
\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}
. We assume that the accuracy of the discretization, as well as the cost of evaluating the discretized model, increases as
\ell
(and hence
M_{\ell}
) increases. This hierarchy of discretizations, in turn, induces a hierarchy of posterior probability measures
\{\mu^{y}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}
approximating
\mu^{y}
with increasing accuracy and cost. Notice that we can write the posterior expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}],
approximated on the finest available discretization level
\mathsf{L},
in terms of the following telescoping sum:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{0}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{0}]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1}]\right).

(29)

This motivates introducing the following MLMCMC version of the ergodic estimator (
27
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}:=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{0}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{0}(\theta_{0,0}^{n})]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{L}}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}\underbrace{\left(\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}(\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell})-\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1}(\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell-1})\right)}_{\text{$:=Y_{\ell}^{n}$}}.

(30)

Here we have introduced a notation where, for every
\theta_{\ell,j}\in\mathsf{X}
, the first sub-index
\ell
,
\ell=1,\dots,\mathsf{L}
, represents the current level in the telescoping sum (
30
), whilst the second sub-index
j=\ell-1,\ell,
represents the discretization level of the posterior measure associated to
\theta_{\ell,j}
, i.e.,
\theta_{\ell,j}\sim\mu^{y}_{j}
asymptotically. Notice that the terms
Y^{n}_{\ell}
are small, in general, if
(\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell-1},\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell})
are
close
. The key of the method is then to design a coupled Markov chain
\{\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell-1},\theta_{\ell,\ell}^{n}\}
for which
\theta_{\ell,\ell-1}^{n},\theta_{\ell,\ell}^{n}
stay highly correlated and close to each other for every
n
, while, at the same time, keeping the right marginal invariant distributions
\mu^{y}_{\ell-1},\mu^{y}_{\ell}
, respectively. This is necessary for the terms in (
30
) to telescope in the mean. Constructing a coupled Markov chain (with marginal target measures
\mu^{y}_{\ell-1},\mu^{y}_{\ell}
) for which
(\theta_{\ell,\ell-1}^{n},\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell})\to 0
in a suitable sense, as
\ell\to\infty
, will in turn result in
\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[Y_{\ell}]\to 0
as
\ell\to\infty
, where
\nu_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X}^{2})
is the invariant measure of the coupled Markov chain (provided it exists). Hence, by using an adequate proposal distribution and properly choosing
\mathsf{L}
and
\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}
one can obtain a significantly better complexity than that of a single-level MCMC estimator ( see
[
9
]
for a general complexity result of the ML-MCMC approach). To achieve this, following
[
9
]
, we will use what we call an
Independent Metropolis-Hastings coupling
(IMH-coupling) of
\theta_{\ell,\ell-1},\theta_{\ell,\ell}
. The main idea of such a coupling is to create two simultaneous Markov Chains
\{\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell-1},\theta^{n}_{\ell,\ell}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
at two adjacent discretization levels, using as a proposal a probability measure
\tilde{Q}_{\ell}
(where
\mu^{y}_{j}\ll\tilde{Q}_{\ell}

j=\ell-1,\ell

{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}
-density

generates proposed states

independently of the current state of either chain, and (ii) at every iteration, the same candidate state

u\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
. This will in turn guarantee that, marginally

(that is, the marginal chains follow the right distribution), and that the pair

4
Convergence analysis of the ML-MCMC algorithm

We now proceed to analyze the convergence of the level-wise coupled chains generated by Algorithm
2
. The main result of this section is stated in Theorem
6
. Loosely speaking, this theorem (i) gives conditions for the existence and uniqueness of a joint invariant measure of the multi-level Markov transition kernel (
35
), and (ii) shows that, under certain conditions, such a kernel generates a uniformly ergodic chain, i.e., a chain that converges exponentially fast to its invariant distribution with a constant that does not depend on the initial state of the chain.

Notice that, at each level
\ell
, Algorithm
2
creates two coupled chains using the same proposal
Q_{\ell}
. This in turn induces two Markov transition kernels, each generating a marginal chain. We formalize this in the following definition.

Definition 1
(Marginal kernel).

For a given level
\ell
,
\ell=1,2\dots,\mathsf{L}
and proposal
Q_{\ell}
, we define the
\mu^{y}_{j}
-invariant
Marginal Markov Transition Kernel

p_{\ell,j}:\mathsf{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})\rightarrow[0,1],
with
j=\ell-1,\ell
, as

\displaystyle p_{\ell,j}(\theta_{\ell,j},A)

\displaystyle:=\int_{A}\alpha_{j}(\theta_{\ell,j},z)Q_{\ell}(z){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z)

(39)

\displaystyle+\mbox{1\hskip-4.25pt{1}}_{\left\{\theta_{\ell,j}\in A\right\}}\int_{\mathsf{X}}\left(1-\alpha_{j}(\theta_{\ell,j},z)\right)Q_{\ell}(z){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z),

(40)

for any
\theta_{\ell,j}\in\mathsf{X}
, and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}).
Similarly, we denote by
P_{\ell,j}
its corresponding
marginal Markov Transition Operator
.

Clearly, the marginal chain
\{\theta^{n}_{\ell,j}\}_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}},\ j=\ell-1,\ell
generated by (
39
) is indeed a Markov chain. Furthermore, notice that, by construction,
{P_{\ell,j}}
is
\mu^{y}_{j}
-invariant, i.e.,
\mu^{y}_{j}{P_{\ell,j}}=\mu^{y}_{j}
.

We make the following assumptions on the proposal and the (marginal) posterior densities.

Assumption 1
(Assumptions on positivity and continuity).

The following conditions hold for all
\ell=1,\dots,\mathsf{L}
:

1.1
.

The proposal density is continuous and positive, i.e.,
Q_{\ell}(z)>0,\ \forall z\in\mathsf{X}
.

1.2
.

The posterior density is continuous and positive, i.e.,
\pi^{y}_{\ell}(z)>0,\ \forall z\in\mathsf{X}
.

1.3
.

For any
\ell=1,2,\dots,\mathsf{L}
,
j=\ell-1,\ell
, the quotient
Q_{\ell}(\theta)/\pi^{y}_{j}(\theta)
has compact level sets, i.e., for any
c_{r}>0,
the set
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})
defined as
A:=\left\{\theta\in\mathsf{X}:\frac{Q_{\ell}(\theta)}{\pi^{y}_{j}(\theta)}\leq c_{r}\right\}
is compact.

1.4
.

There exists a positive constant
c\in(0,1),
independent of
\ell
, such that

\mathrm{ess}\inf_{z\in\mathsf{X}}\left\{Q_{\ell}(z)/\pi^{y}_{j}(z)\right\}\geq c>0,\quad j=\ell-1,\ell.

1.5
.

There exist positive constants
r>1,
and
C_{r}
, independent of
\ell
, such that
\int_{\mathsf{X}}Q_{\ell}^{r}(\theta){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq C_{r},
for any
\ell
.

We remark that Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
are relatively mild and straightforward to satisfy. In particular, under the assumption that
\Phi_{\ell}(\theta;y)
is positive everywhere,
1.2
is “inherited” from the forward operator
{\mathcal{F}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{noise}}
, while
1.1
is user defined.
Assumption
1.3
is common in the study of converge of Markov chains when using coupling arguments (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
). It is satisfied, for instance, if
\mathsf{X}
is compact or if it is finite-dimensional and
\lim_{|\theta|\to\infty}Q_{\ell}(\theta)/\pi^{y}_{\ell}(\theta)=+\infty
. Assumption
1.4
implies, in particular, that the tails of the proposal
Q_{\ell}
must decay more slowly than those of
\mu^{y}_{\ell}
,
\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}
at infinity, i.e.,
Q_{\ell}
has heavier tails than
\mu^{y}_{j},\ j=\ell-1,\ell
. In practice, this is indeed a more restrictive assumption than
1.1
and
1.2
. It is, however, crucial for the convergence of both the marginal IMH and the multi-level algorithm. Lastly, Assumption

is an integrability condition on
Q_{\ell}
with respect to the prior. This assumption is quite mild and will become useful in the next section (c.f. Lemma
26

Remark 2
.

It is a consequence of Assumptions

1.3
that
\mathrm{ess}\inf_{z\in\mathsf{X}}\left\{Q_{\ell}(z)/\pi^{y}_{j}(z)\right\}\geq c_{\ell}>0

4.1
Convergence of the level-wise coupled chain

We say that a Markov kernel
p:\mathsf{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})\rightarrow[0,1]

\sigma
-finite measure
\psi

(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))

is

and a non-trivial, positive measure

\displaystyle p^{m}(\theta,A)\geq\nu(A),\quad\forall\theta\in S,\ A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathsf{X}}).

(41)

In most Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, one typically designs a Markov chain with a given invariant probability measure. This automatically ensures the existence of (at least) one invariant probability measure. However, this is unfortunately not the case for Multi-level MCMC algorithms (including the one presented here), and as such, a significant part of the paper will be devoted to show that such an invariant measure uniquely exists. We recall here a standard result in the theory of Markov chains (see e.g.,
[
24

relies upon.

Theorem 3
(Existence and uniqueness of an invariant probability measure).

Consider a (discrete-time) Markov chain on a general state space
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))
with a Markov transition kernel
p:\mathsf{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})\to[0,1].
Suppose the following conditions hold:

A1
.

The chain is
\psi
-irreducible and aperiodic.

A2
.

There exists a measurable function
V:\mathsf{X}\to[1,\infty)
, called
Lyapunov function
, a small set
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}),
and positive constants
\lambda<1
,
\kappa<\infty
satisfying

\displaystyle(PV)(\theta)\leq\lambda V(\theta)+\kappa\emph{\mbox{1\hskip-4.25pt{1}}}_{\{\theta\in S\}}.

(42)

Then,

has a unique invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))
. In addition, there exist positive constants
M<\infty
and

such that

\displaystyle\underset{|f|\leq V}{\sup}\left|(P^{n}f)(\theta)-\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta)\right|\leq MV(\theta)r^{n},\quad\forall\theta\in\mathsf{X},\ n\in\mathbb{N},

(43)

where the supremum is taken over all
\mu
-integrable functions

satisfying
|f|\leq V
.

Markov chains that satisfy (
43

geometrically ergodic

.

Proof.

See
[
24

On the other hand, when
\bm{\theta}_{\ell}\in\widehat{S}_{\ell}
, we have that

\displaystyle{\bm{P}_{\ell}}V_{\ell-1,\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}P_{\ell,\ell-1}V_{\ell-1}(\theta_{\ell,\ell-1})+\frac{1}{2}P_{\ell,\ell}V_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell,\ell})

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda V_{\ell-1}(\theta_{\ell,\ell-1})+\kappa\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda V_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell,\ell})+\kappa\right)

(95)

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda V_{\ell-1,\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})+\kappa-\frac{\kappa}{1+\Gamma}V_{\ell-1,\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})

(96)

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda V_{\ell-1,\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})+\tilde{\kappa},

(97)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}:=\frac{\Gamma}{1+\Gamma}\kappa.

(98)

Thus, the joint Markov operator
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
satisfies a drift condition of the form
A2
with a small set
\widehat{S}_{\ell}
, and constants given by (
93
) and (
98
).

We now have all the required results to prove Theorem
6
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The first claim of the theorem (the one pertaining to the existence and uniqueness of the invariant measure) follows immediately from Theorem
3
and Lemmata
8
,
9
, and

associated to

Remark 13
.

Notice that, contrary to many other MCMC methods where one typically constructs the Markov transition kernel in such a way that the algorithm generates samples from a target invariant probability measure (whose Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to some reference probability measure is accessible up to a normalization constant), the level
\ell
joint invariant measure
\nu_{\ell}
is not known and it is induced by the joint Markov transition kernel. In particular, it depends on the proposal
Q_{\ell}
. We aim at further investigating the influence of
Q_{\ell}
on the invariant measure in future works. It is worth saying that, by construction,
\nu_{\ell}
will in any case have the desired marginals
\mu^{y}_{\ell},\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}
.

We have shown that the joint chain generated by the multi-level algorithm with independent proposals (i) has an invariant measure and (ii) is uniformly ergodic.

The choice of
Q_{\ell}
is delicate for the ML-MCMC algorithm to work. For instance, consider the case
\mathsf{L}=1
,
\mu^{y}_{0}=\mathcal{N}(1,1)
and
\mu^{y}_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\frac{1}{2},1)
. What might look at first sight as a good proposal for the coupled chain at level
(\ell-1,\ell)=(0,1)
is to take
Q_{1}=\mu^{y}_{0}
, i.e., the (exact) posterior at the previous level. However, this choice of proposal (which is unfeasible in practice, as direct sampling from
\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}
is inaccessible) will not lead to a geometrically ergodic chain in view of Theorem
6
, since
Q_{1}(z)/\mu^{y}_{1}(z)
has essential infimum 0. The idea of proposing from the previous level is somehow what is advocated in
[
9
]
, which could work only if
\exists c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
c_{1}\leq\pi^{y}_{\ell-1}(z)/\pi^{y}_{\ell}(z)\leq c_{2}
,
\forall\theta\in\mathsf{X}
and
\forall\ell
.

Lastly, notice that, by construction, the ML-MCMC Algorithm
3
starts from a measure
\widehat{\lambda}^{0}(A):=\lambda^{0}(A_{\Delta})
,
\lambda^{0}\ll{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}
, where, for any set
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}^{2})
, we define

. We now show that, for any level
\ell=1,2,\dots,\mathsf{L}
,
\widehat{\lambda}^{0}\ll\nu_{\ell}

Theorem 14
(Absolute continuity of initial measure).

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem

,

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle 0=\nu_{\ell}(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{X}^{2}}{\bm{p}_{\ell}}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell},A)\nu_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathsf{X}^{2}}\int_{A_{\Delta}}\min_{j}\left\{\min\left\{Q_{\ell}(z),\frac{\pi^{y}_{j}(z)Q_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell,j})}{\pi^{y}_{j}(\theta_{\ell,j})}\right\}\right\}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z)\nu_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{\ell})

(100)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{K_{\epsilon}}\int_{A_{\Delta}}\min_{j}\left\{\min\left\{Q_{\ell}(z),\frac{\pi^{y}_{j}(z)Q_{\ell}(\theta_{\ell,j})}{\pi^{y}_{j}(\theta_{\ell,j})}\right\}\right\}{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(\mathrm{d}z)\nu_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{\ell}).

(101)

By Assumption

and compactness of

and

,
\forall\bm{\theta}_{\ell}\in K_{\epsilon}
,
\forall z\in A_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:bndnula}\geq c^{\prime}(1-\epsilon){\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(A_{\Delta}),

(102)

which implies that
{\mu_{\mathrm{pr}}}(A_{\Delta})=0
. Moreover, since

.
Now, suppose

4.2
Non-asymptotic bounds on the level-wise ergodic estimator

Recall that if the Markov operator
P
defines a map
P:L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)\to L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
, for some
q\in[1,\infty],
we can define the norm of
P
as

\displaystyle\left\|P\right\|_{L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)\to L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}:=\underset{\underset{\left\|f\right\|_{L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}=1}{f\in L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}}{\sup}\left\|Pf\right\|_{L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}.

(103)

It is well-known that
P
is always a weak contraction in
L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
, i.e.,
\left\|P\right\|_{L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)\to L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)}\leq 1
.
We define the
L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
-
spectral gap
of
P:L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)\to L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\mu)
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{q}[P]:=1-\left\|P\right\|_{L_{q}^{0}\to L_{q}^{0}}.

(104)

Whenever
\gamma_{q}[P]>0
, it can be shown that
\nu^{0}P^{n}
converges to
\mu
for any
\nu^{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathsf{X})
in some appropriate distance for probability measures (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
32
]
).

We now turn to analyze the level-wise contribution to the ML-MCMC ergodic estimator (
30
), which we write hereafter in more general terms, including a burn-in phase.

For
\ell=1,2,\dots,\mathsf{L},
let
\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}-\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1}=:Y_{\ell}:\mathsf{X}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\nu_{\ell}
square-integrable function, and
\nu^{0}
be a probability measure on
(\mathsf{X}^{2},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}^{2}))
such that
\nu^{0}\ll\nu_{\ell}
. In addition, denote by
\mathsf{E}_{\nu^{0},\bm{P}_{\ell}}[Y_{\ell}]
the expectation of
Y_{\ell}
over the Markov chain generated by
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
, starting from an initial probability measure
\nu^{0}
, and consider the following ergodic estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{\ell,N_{\ell},n_{b,\ell}}:=\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}Y_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{n+n_{b,\ell}}),\quad\bm{\theta}^{n}_{\ell}\sim{\bm{p}_{\ell}}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{n-1},\cdot),

(105)

where
n_{b,\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
is the usual
burn-in period
. The aim of this section is to provide error bounds on the
non-asymptotic statistical Mean Square Error
of (
105
), namely

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{Y}_{\ell,N_{\ell},n_{b,\ell}};\nu^{0}):=\mathsf{E}_{\nu^{0},\bm{P}_{\ell}}\left[\left(\hat{Y}_{\ell,N_{\ell},n_{b,\ell}}-\nu_{\ell}(Y_{\ell})\right)^{2}\right].

(106)

In particular, we aim at obtaining a bound of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{Y}_{\ell,N_{\ell},n_{b,\ell}};\nu^{0})\leq C_{\mathrm{mse},\ell}\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[Y_{\ell}]}{N_{\ell}},

(107)

for some level-dependent, positive constant
C_{\mathrm{mse},\ell}.
Such a bound is presented in Theorem
15
, the main result of this subsection. A bound of the form (
107
) will be required for the cost analysis of Section
5
. Bounds such as (
107
) are known to exist for geometrically ergodic and reversible Markov transition kernels
[
32
]
. However, it is easy to see that the chain generated by
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
is not
\nu_{\ell}
-reversible.
As a consequence, we can not directly apply the non-asymptotic bounds presented in
[
32
]
. Instead, inspired by the error analysis of
[
32
]
and the so-called
pseudo-spectral
approach of
[
26
]
, we construct a bound of the form (
107
) for general (i.e., not necessarily multi-level) non-reversible, discrete-time Markov chains. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this result is new.

For any
q,q^{\prime}\in[1,\infty]
with
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1,
we denote the
adjoint operator
of
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}:L_{q}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})\to L_{q}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})
by
{\bm{P}^{*}_{\ell}}:L_{q^{\prime}}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})\to L_{q^{\prime}}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell}).
It can be shown
[
32
]
that a Markov operator acting on
L_{2}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})
is self-adjoint if and only if it is
\nu_{\ell}
-reversible. Notice that, even though
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
is not reversible, the multiplicative reversibilization
\bm{R}_{\ell}=({\bm{P}^{*}_{\ell}}{\bm{P}_{\ell}})
is.

Moreover, we define the so-called
pseudo-spectral gap
of the Markov operator
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}:L_{2}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})\to L_{2}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[{\bm{P}_{\ell}}]:=\underset{k\geq 1}{\max}\left\{\gamma_{2}[({\bm{P}_{\ell}}^{*})^{k}{\bm{P}_{\ell}}^{k}]/k\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(108)

As mentioned in
[
26
]
, the pseudo-spectral gap can be understood as a generalization of the
L_{2}
-spectral gap of the so-called
multiplicative reversibilization

\bm{R}_{\ell}=({\bm{P}^{*}_{\ell}}{\bm{P}_{\ell}}).
It is shown in
[
26
, Proposition 3.4]
that for a uniformly ergodic chain with Markov kernel,
\bm{P}_{\ell}
,
\exists\tau_{\ell}<\infty
such that
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[\bm{P}_{\ell}]\geq\frac{1}{2\tau_{\ell}}>0
, where
\tau_{\ell}
is the so-called
mixing time
.

Theorem 15
(Non-asymptotic bound on the mean square error).

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. Furthermore, for any
\ell=1,2,\dots,\mathsf{L},
let
Y_{\ell}\in L_{2}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})
, and write
g_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})=Y_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})-\int_{\mathsf{X}^{2}}Y_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})\nu_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{\ell})
, and assume the Markov chain generated by
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
is started from a measure
\nu^{0}
with
\nu^{0}\ll\nu_{\ell}
, and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\ell}}\in L_{\infty}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell}).
Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{Y}_{\ell,N_{\ell},n_{b,\ell}};\nu^{0}):=\mathsf{E}_{\nu^{0},\bm{P}_{\ell}}\left|\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}g_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{n+n_{b,\ell}})\right|^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{mse},\ell}\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[Y_{\ell}]}{N_{\ell}},

(109)

where
C_{\mathrm{mse},\ell}=C_{\mathrm{inv},\ell}+C_{\mathrm{ns},\ell},
with

C_{\mathrm{inv},\ell}=\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[\bm{P}_{\ell}]}\right),\quad C_{\mathrm{ns},\ell}=\left(2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\ell}}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[{\bm{P}_{\ell}}]}\right)\right),

and where
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[{\bm{P}_{\ell}}]
is the pseudo-spectral gap of
{\bm{P}_{\ell}},
defined in (
108
).

The proof of this theorem is technical and presented in the appendix for a general uniformly ergodic (non-reversible) Markov chain.
Notice that the Assumption
\nu^{0}\ll\nu_{\ell}
holds in our setting by Theorem
14
for
\nu^{0}(A)=\lambda^{0}(A_{\Delta})
.

Notice also that, even though the constants
C_{\mathrm{inv},\ell}
,
C_{\mathrm{ns},\ell}
depend on the level
\ell
, we do not expect them to degenerate as
\ell\to\infty
. In particular, the dependency on the level is given by two terms, namely
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[\bm{P}_{\ell}]
and
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\ell}}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}.
For the first term, we expect
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[{\bm{P}_{\ell}}]
to become smaller and smaller as
\ell\to\infty
and for it to converge to a limit value
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[\bm{P}_{\infty}]>0
(see also the discussion of sychronization of the coupled chains in Section
5
). For the second term
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\ell}}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}},
notice that
\nu_{\ell}
converges to a measure that has all of its mass in the diagonal set of
\mathsf{X}^{2}
. Since
\nu^{0}
is a finite measure on such a diagonal, we also expect that this term remains bounded as
\ell\to\infty
. We are, however, not able to prove these claims at the moment, reason why we formulate the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

There exist a level independent constant
C_{\mathrm{mse}}
such that, for any
\ell=0,1,\dots,
it holds that
C_{\mathrm{mse},\ell}<C_{\mathrm{mse}}
.

The fact that
C_{\mathrm{mse},\ell}
does not blow-up as
\ell\to\infty
is an important requirement on the asymptotic analysis of ML-(MC)MC methods.

We remark that the bound (
109
) should be compared to the one presented in
[
32
, Theorem 3.34]
. In particular, that work presents a sharper bound than (
109
), however, such a bound necessitates more restrictive Assumptions which we list in the next theorem for completeness, whose proof is an easy adaptation of
[
32
, Theorem 3.34]
to our setting and its omitted.

Theorem 16
.

Suppose that the Assumptions of Theorem
15
hold. In addition, assume that for any
\ell=1,2,\dots,\mathsf{L}
:

C1
.

(
L_{2}
-spectral gap) there exists
b_{\ell}\in(0,1)
such that

\left\|{\bm{P}_{\ell}}\right\|_{L^{0}_{2}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})\to L^{0}_{2}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})}<b_{\ell},

C2
.

(
L_{1}
-exponential convergence) there exists
\tilde{c}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},a_{\ell}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nu^{0}{\bm{P}_{\ell}}^{n}-\nu_{\ell}\right\|_{L_{1}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})}:=\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\nu^{0}{\bm{P}_{\ell}}^{n})}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\ell}}-1\right\|_{L_{1}(\mathsf{X}^{2},\nu_{\ell})}\leq\tilde{c}_{\ell}a_{\ell}^{n},

(110)

Then, the non-asymptotic MSE is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\nu^{0},\bm{P}_{\ell}}\left|\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}g_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{n+n_{b,\ell}})\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[Y_{\ell}]}{N_{\ell}}\left(\frac{2}{(1-b_{\ell})}+\frac{2\tilde{c}_{\ell}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\ell}}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}a_{\ell}^{n_{b},\ell}}{N_{\ell}(1-a_{\ell})^{2}}\right),

(111)

where the first term in the parenthesis is associated to the variance contribution to the MSE, while the second term corresponds to the statistical squared bias and is of higher order in
N_{\ell}
.

In general, the stronger Assumptions
C1
and
C2
are known to hold for Markov chains which are both reversible and geometrically ergodic. However, due to its construction, the Markov transition kernel
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
of the ML-MCMC algorithm is not reversible. Nevertheless, we believe that the algorithm presented herein satisfies Assumptions
C1
and
C2
and as such, a bound on the MSE of the form (
111
), should hold. However, we are currently unable to verify this claim either, and we will restrict to the bound of theorem
15
and the less restrictive Assumption
2
. Further examination of the convergence properties of ML-MCMC algorithms will be the topic of future work.

5
Cost analysis of the ML-MCMC algorithm

For
\ell=0,1,\dots,\mathsf{L},
let
\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell}:\mathsf{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a
\mu^{y}_{\ell}
-integrable quantity of interest. The
Total Mean Square Error
of the multi-level estimator (

) is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\right)^{2}\right],

(112)

where we have denoted expectation over the whole multi-level Markov chain by

, i.e., without a subscript. We remark that the estimator

as well as the burn-in and initial measure for each level; however, for the sake of readability, we opted not to explicitly write these dependencies throughout this section. Notice that the previous term can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}})=\mathbb{V}[\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}]+\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\right]^{2}

(114)

where we have denoted by
\mathbb{V}
the variance over the whole multi-level Markov chain. Furthermore, from Cauchy-Schwarz, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2(\mathsf{L}+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[\hat{Y}_{\ell}],

(115)

and

\displaystyle 2\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\mathsf{L}+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{\ell}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\ell}}[{Y}_{\ell}]\right)^{2}.

(116)

Thus, from (
109

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}})\leq

(117)

\displaystyle\underbrace{2(\mathsf{L}+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{Y}_{\ell})}_{\text{Total statistical error}}+\underbrace{2\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\right]^{2}}_{\text{Discretization error}}=:\mathrm{e}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}}).

Implementation

We begin with a discussion on how to choose the optimal number of samples
N_{\ell}
.
Recall that, for
\ell=0,\dots,\mathsf{L}
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
the total cost of producing one coupled sample of
(\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell-1},\mathsf{QoI}_{\ell})
at level
\ell
using Algorithm
3
. The total cost of the multi-level MCMC estimator is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\left(\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L},\{N_{\ell}\}}\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\mathcal{C}_{\ell}N_{\ell}.

(217)

In order to bound the statistical contribution of the total error bound, we will require, from (
109
) and (
118
),that

\displaystyle 2(\mathsf{L}+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}C_{\mathrm{mse}}\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[Y_{\ell}]}{N_{\ell}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{tol}^{2}}{2},

(218)

where
\mathsf{tol}
is some user-prescribed tolerance. However, it is in general not a simple task to compute or estimate the constant
C_{\mathrm{mse}}
. We will ignore it hereafter, and aim at bounding the quantity

\displaystyle 2(\mathsf{L}+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[\hat{Y}_{\ell}]}{N_{\ell}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{tol}^{2}}{2}.

(219)

To that end we will use the so-called
batched means estimator
of
\mathbb{V}_{\nu_{\ell}}[\hat{Y}_{\ell}]
denoted by
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\ell}
(see
[
11
]
for further details). In this case, treating
N_{\ell}
as a real number and minimizing (
217
) subject to (
219
), gives the optimal samples sizes

\displaystyle N_{\ell}=\left\lceil 2\mathsf{tol}^{-2}\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\ell}/\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{j}\mathcal{C}_{j}}\right)\right\rceil,

(220)

where
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is the ceiling function. Lastly, we also need to ensure that the second contribution to the total error, i.e., the discretization bias at level
\mathsf{L}
, is such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\right|\leq\frac{\mathsf{tol}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(221)

Notice that from
T1
it follows

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{\mathsf{L}}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{\mathsf{L}}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}}[\mathsf{QoI}]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=\mathsf{L}+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{j}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{j}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{y}_{j-1}}[\mathsf{QoI}_{j-1}]\right|

(222)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\left|[\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L}}-\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L}-1}]\right|}{1-s^{-\alpha_{w}}}.

(223)

Thus, to achieve a total (estimated) MSE of the ML-MCMC estimator less than
\mathsf{tol}^{2}
, we need to check that

\displaystyle 2(\mathsf{L}+1)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}}\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}}{N_{\ell}}\right)+2\left(\frac{\left|[\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L}}-\widehat{\mathsf{QoI}}_{\mathsf{L}-1}\right|}{1-s^{-\alpha_{w}}}\right)^{2}\leq\mathsf{tol}^{2}.

(224)

In practice, the set of parameters
\mathcal{P}:=\{C_{w},\alpha_{w},\{\hat{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}\}_{\ell=0}^{\mathsf{L}},C_{\sigma},\beta,C_{\gamma},\gamma\}
need to be estimated with a preliminary run over
\mathsf{L}_{0}
levels, using
\tilde{N}_{\ell},\ \ell=0,1,\dots,\mathsf{L}_{0}
samples per level. However, the main disadvantage of this procedure is that for computationally expensive problems, this screening phase can be quite inefficient. In particular, if
\mathsf{L}_{0}
is chosen too large, then the screening phase might turn out to be more expensive than the overall ML-MCMC simulation on the optimal hierarchy
\{0,1,\dots,\mathsf{L}\}
. On the other hand, if
\mathsf{L}_{0}
(or
\tilde{N}_{\ell}
) is chosen too small, the extrapolation (or estimation) of the values of
\mathcal{P}
might be quite unreliable, particularly at higher levels. In the MLMC literature, one way of overcoming these issues is with the so-called continuation Multi-level Monte Carlo method
[
6
]
. We will present a continuation-type ML-MCMC (C-ML-MCMC) algorithm in the following subsection, based on the works
[
6
,
27
]
.

6.1

A continuation-type ML-MCMC

The key idea behind this method is to iteratively implement a ML-MCMC algorithm with a sequence of decreasing tolerances while, at the same time, progressively improving the estimation of the problem dependent parameters
\mathcal{P}
. As presented before, these parameters directly control the number of levels and sample sizes. Following
[
6
]
, we introduce the family of tolerances
\mathsf{tol}_{i},\ i=0,1,\dots,
given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{tol}_{i}=\begin{cases}r_{1}^{i_{E}-i}r_{2}^{-1}\mathsf{tol}&i<i_{E},\\
r_{2}^{i_{E}-i}r_{2}^{-1}\mathsf{tol}&i\geq i_{E},\end{cases}

(225)

where
r_{1}\geq r_{2}>1,
so that
\mathsf{tol}_{i_{E}-1}\geq\mathsf{tol}>\mathsf{tol}_{i_{E}}
, with

\displaystyle i_{E}:=\left\lfloor\frac{-\log(\mathsf{tol})+\log(r_{2})+\log(\mathsf{tol}_{0})}{\log(r_{1})}\right\rfloor.

(226)

The idea is then to iteratively run the ML-MCMC algorithm for each of the tolerances
\mathsf{tol}_{i},

i=0,1,\dots
until the algorithm achieves convergence, based on the criterion defined in the previous subsection. Iterations for which
i<i_{E}
, are used to obtain increasingly more accurate estimates of
\mathcal{P}
. Notice that when
i=i_{E}
, the problem is solved with a slightly smaller tolerance
r_{2}^{-1}\mathsf{tol}
for some carefully chosen
r_{2}
. Solving at this slightly smaller tolerance is done in order to prevent any extra unnecessary iterations due to the statistical nature of the estimated quantities. Furthermore, if the algorithm has not converged at the
i_{E}^{\mathrm{th}}
iteration, it keeps running for even smaller tolerances
\mathsf{tol}_{i}
,
i>i_{E}
, to account for cases where the estimates of
\mathcal{P}
are unstable. Thus, at the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
iteration of the C-ML-MCMC algorithm, we run the Algorithm
3
with an iteration-dependent number of levels
\mathsf{L}_{i}
, where
\mathsf{L}_{i}
is obtained by solving the following discrete constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\underset{\mathsf{L}_{i-1}\leq\mathsf{L}\leq\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{max}}}{\mathrm{arg}\ \mathrm{min}}\left\{2\mathsf{tol}_{i}^{-2}2(\mathsf{L}+1)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\mathsf{L}_{i}}\sqrt{C_{\beta}s^{-\beta_{j}}\mathcal{C}_{j}}\right)^{2}\right\},\\
&\textrm{s.t.}\quad C_{w}s^{-\alpha_{w}\mathsf{L}_{i}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{tol}_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}.\end{cases}

(227)

Here,
\mathsf{L}_{-1}=\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{0}}
is a given minimum number of levels,
\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{\max}}
, is chosen as the maximum number of levels given a computational budget (which could be dictated, for example, by the minimum mesh size imposed by memory or computational restrictions). Furthermore, notice that (
227
) is easily solved by exhaustive search.

Conclusions and future outlook

In the current work we have presented a general ML-MCMC framework based on independent Metropolis-Hastings proposals, which can be understood as an extension of currently existing methods (
[
9
]
). Furthermore, we have provided what is, to the best of the authors knowledge, the first thorough study on the theory of ML-MCMC algorithms based on independent proposals; we have investigated necessary conditions for the existence and convergence to a unique invariant probability measure for the methods discussed herein, as well as presented sufficient conditions under which the cost-tolerance result presented in
[
9
]
can be generalized to our method. Furthermore, we have verified our theoretical results on three academic examples. Lastly, we have presented a non-asymptotic bound on the statistical MSE in the spirit of
[
32
]
under minimal assumptions on the Markov transition operator. We remark that such a bound can be applied outside the context of ML-MCMC.

We intend to carry out a number of future extensions of the work presented herein, both from a theoretical and computational perspective. On the one hand, from a theoretical perspective, there are still some unanswered questions regarding the class of methods at hand. In particular, it is not clear for us if the multi-level Markov transition operator
{\bm{P}_{\ell}}
satisfies Assumptions
C1
and
C2
in Theorem
16
, which would in turn produce a sharper bound on the statistical component of the MSE. In addition, given the unattainable nature of the invariant joint probability measure of the ML-MCMC algorithm, it is also unclear at this point how such a measure depends on the choice of proposal.

On the other hand, from a computational perspective, one future research direction stems from the construction of the level
\ell
proposal
Q_{\ell}
. Intuitively, such a proposal should resemble the posterior
\mu^{y}_{\ell-1}
or
\mu^{y}_{\ell}
(or a convex combination of both) as much as possible, while at the same time satisfying Assumption
1.4
. Some preliminary experiments suggest that density approximation techniques, such as kernel density approximation (KDE)
[
33
]
or, more recently, flow-based generative models
[
25
]
, can be used to construct efficient ML-MCMC proposals. Finally, we would also be interested in extending these results to a multi-index setting as in
[
13
]
.

Bounding the statistical MSE

We now present a non-asymptotic bound of the mean square error of the ergodic estimator
\hat{f}_{N,n_{b}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\theta^{n+n_{b}})
obtained with a non-reversible Markov chain
\{\theta^{n}\}
. We remark that the bound presented in this work has utility beyond the multi-level construction. Throughout this section we will let
(\mathsf{X},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{X}})
be a separable Banach space with associated Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})
. We will also let
P:L_{2}(\mathsf{X},\nu)\mapsto L_{2}(\mathsf{X},\nu)
be a
\nu
-invariant Markov operator for some non-trivial probability measure
\nu
on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X})).
Lastly, we define the
averaging operator
as
\widehat{\nu}f:=\nu(f)=\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(\theta)\nu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

Theorem 28
(Non-asymptotic bound on the mean square error).

Let
f\in L_{2}(\mathsf{X},\nu)
, be a
\nu
-square integrable function and write
g(\theta)=f(\theta)-\int_{\mathsf{X}}f(\theta)\nu(\mathrm{d}\theta)
. Suppose the Markov operator
P
is uniformly ergodic and non-necessarily reversible. In addition, assume the chain generated by
P
starts from an initial probability measure
\nu^{0}\ll\nu
, with
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\in L_{\infty}(\mathsf{X},\nu)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{f}_{N,n_{b}};\nu^{0})=\mathsf{E}_{\nu^{0},P}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\theta^{n+n_{b}})\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu}[f]}{N}(C_{\mathrm{inv}}+C_{\mathrm{ns}}),

(234)

where
C_{\mathrm{inv}}=\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right),

C_{\mathrm{ns}}=\left(2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right)\right)
, where
\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]
is the pseudo-spectral gap of
P,
defined in (
108
).

The proof of Theorem
28
is decomposed into a series of auxiliary results.
We now present a first bound of the form (
234
) for chains which are started at stationarity, i.e., whenever
\nu^{0}=\nu
. Although this is usually not the case, the following Lemma will be useful in the proof of Theorem
28
.

Lemma 29
(MSE bound starting at stationarity).

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
28
and with
\nu^{0}=\nu,
it holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{f}_{N,n_{b}=0};\nu):=\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\theta^{n+n_{b}})\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu}[f]}{N}\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right).

(235)

Proof.

We follow a similar approach to those presented in
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
31
, Section 3]
. In order to ease notation, for the remainder of this proof we write
L_{q}=L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\nu),\ q\in[1,\infty]
. We can write the MSE of a Markov chain generated by
P
starting at
\nu
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\theta^{n})\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}[g(\theta^{n})^{2}]+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i=j+1}^{N}\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}[g(\theta^{i})g(\theta^{j})].

(236)

Working on the expectation of the second term on the right hand side we get from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}[g(\theta^{i})g(\theta^{j})]

\displaystyle=\langle g,P^{i-j}g\rangle_{\nu}=\langle g,(P-\widehat{\nu})^{i-j}g\rangle_{\nu}

(237)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\left\|(P-\widehat{\nu})^{i-j}\right\|_{L_{2}\mapsto L_{2}}.

(238)

Notice that for any
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|(P-\widehat{\nu})^{i-j}\right\|_{L_{2}\mapsto L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(P-\widehat{\nu})^{k}\right\|_{L_{2}\mapsto L_{2}}^{\lfloor\frac{i-j}{k}\rfloor}

(239)

\displaystyle=\left\|(P^{*}-\widehat{\nu})^{k}(P-\widehat{\nu})^{k}\right\|_{L_{2}\mapsto L_{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{i-j}{k}\rfloor}

(240)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function. Now, let
k_{\mathrm{ps}}
be the smallest integer such that

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{ps}}\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]=\gamma[(P^{*})^{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}P^{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}]=1-\left\|(P^{*}-\widehat{\nu})^{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}(P-\widehat{\nu})^{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}\right\|_{L_{2}\mapsto L_{2}},

(241)

which, is strictly positive for uniformly ergodic chains (see
[
29
, Section 3.3]
). Then, from (
236
), (
240
), and (
241
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i=j+1}^{N}\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}[g(\theta^{i})g(\theta^{j})]\leq\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i=j+1}^{N}\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\left(1-k_{\mathrm{ps}}\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{i-j}{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}\rfloor}.

(242)

For notational simplicity we write
\varrho=(1-k_{\mathrm{ps}}\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P])
. We then have that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i=j+1}^{N}\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{i-j}{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}\rfloor}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}}}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{m}{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}\rfloor}\leq\frac{2\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}}k_{\mathrm{ps}}}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}m}

(243)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}}k_{\mathrm{ps}}}{N}\frac{1}{1-\varrho^{1/2}}=\frac{2\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}}k_{\mathrm{ps}}}{N}\frac{1+\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1-\varrho}

(244)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}}}{N\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]},

(245)

where the second inequality comes from the definition of the floor function
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
.

We now shift our attention to the first term in (
236
). Using Hölder’s inequality with
q=\infty,\ q^{\prime}=1
, together with the fact that
P
is a weak contraction in
L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\nu)
, for any
q\in[1,\infty]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}[g(\theta^{n})^{2}]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle 1,P^{n}g^{2}\rangle_{\nu}\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\|P^{n}g^{2}\right\|_{L_{1}}\leq\frac{\left\|g^{2}\right\|_{L_{1}}}{N}.

(246)

Lastly, notice that

\left\|g^{2}\right\|_{L_{1}}=\int_{\mathsf{X}}|g^{2}(\theta)|\nu(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\int_{\mathsf{X}}g^{2}(\theta)\nu(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\left\|g\right\|^{2}_{L_{2}}.

Hence, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}[g(\theta^{n})^{2}]\leq\frac{\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}{N}.

(247)

Thus, from (
236
) and (
247
), together with the observation that
\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathsf{X}}(f(\theta)-\widehat{\nu}(f))^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\mathbb{V}_{\nu}[f]
, we finally obtain,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\theta^{n})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}{N}\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right)

(248)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu}[f]}{N}\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right).

(249)

The previous result should be compared to
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
31
, Theorem 5]
. We now consider the more general case where the chain is not started from stationarity, i.e., when
\theta^{0}\sim\nu^{0}
, where
\nu^{0}\ll\nu
is a probability measure on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{X}))
. We recall the following result from
[
32
]

Lemma 30
.

Denote by
n_{b}\in\mathbb{N}
the burn-in period and let
\{\theta^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Markov chain generated by
P
starting from an initial measure
\nu^{0}
and invariant probability measure
\nu
, with
\nu^{0}\ll\nu
. Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
28
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\nu^{0},P}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\theta^{n+n_{b}})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{\nu,P}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\theta^{n})\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(g^{2})

(250)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(gP^{k-j}g),

(251)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{i}(h)=\left\langle(P^{i}-\widehat{\nu})h,\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right)\right\rangle_{\nu},\quad i\in\mathbb{N},\ h\in L_{2}(\mathsf{X},\nu),

(252)

Proof.

See
[
32
, Proposition 3.29]
.

We can now prove Theorem
28
.

Proof of Theorem
28
.

Once again, for the remainder of this proof we write
L_{q}=L_{q}(\mathsf{X},\nu),\ q\in[1,\infty].
From Lemma
30
we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(g^{2})

\displaystyle=\left\langle(P^{j+{n_{b}}}-\widehat{\nu})g^{2},\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right)\right\rangle_{\nu},

(253)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(gP^{k-j}g)

\displaystyle=\left\langle(P^{j+{n_{b}}}-\widehat{\nu})(gP^{k-j}g),\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right)\right\rangle_{\nu}.

(254)

Using Hölder’s inequality with
q^{\prime}=\infty
,
q=1
on the right hand side of (
253
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(g^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left\|(P^{j+{n_{b}}}-\widehat{\nu})g^{2}\right\|_{L_{1}}

(255)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left\|(P^{j+{n_{b}}}-\widehat{\nu})\right\|_{L_{1}\mapsto L_{1}}\left\|g^{2}\right\|_{L_{1}},

(256)

where the last inequality comes from the definition of operator norm. Moreover, since the Markov operators are weak contractions, we have that
\left\|(P^{j+{n_{b}}}-\widehat{\nu})\right\|_{L_{1}\mapsto L_{1}}\leq 2,\forall j\in\mathbb{N}
,
which gives the bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(g^{2})\leq 2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

(257)

Summing over
j
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(g^{2})\leq\frac{2\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}{N}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}.

(258)

Following similar procedure for (
254
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(gP^{k-j}g)\leq 2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left\|g(P^{k-j})g\right\|_{L_{1}}.

(259)

Furthermore, from Hölder’s inequality (with
q^{\prime}=q=2)
and the fact that
\widehat{\nu}(g)=0
we get

\displaystyle\left\|g(P^{k-j})g\right\|_{L_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}\left\|P^{k-j}g\right\|_{L_{2}}=\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}\left\|(P-\widehat{\nu})^{k-j}g\right\|_{L_{2}}

(260)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\left\|(P-\widehat{\nu})^{k-j}\right\|_{L_{2}\mapsto L_{2}}\leq\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\left(1-k_{\mathrm{ps}}\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{k-j}{k_{\mathrm{ps}}}\rfloor},

(261)

where the last inequality follows from the same pseudo-spectral gap argument used in the proof of Lemma
29
. Adding over

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{j+{n_{b}}}(gP^{k-j}g)\leq\frac{8\left\|g\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}{N\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}.

(262)

Notice then that Equations (

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:mse_ns}\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu}[f]}{N}\left(1+\frac{2}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right)+\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\nu}[f]}{N}\left(2\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-1\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\left(1+\frac{4}{\gamma_{\mathrm{ps}}[P]}\right)\right).
