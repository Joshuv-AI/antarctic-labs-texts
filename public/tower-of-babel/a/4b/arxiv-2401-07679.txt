Some counterexamples to Alt-Caffarelli-Friedman monotonicity formulas in Carnot groups Thanks: F.F. is partially supported by 2024-INDAM-GNAMPA project: Free boundary problems in noncommutative structures and degenerate operators. Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35R03, 35R35.
Fausto Ferrari and Davide Giovagnoli

Abstract
. In this paper we continue the analysis of an Alt-Caffarelli-Friedman (ACF) monotonicity formula in Carnot groups of step s > 1 s>1 confirming the existence of counterexamples to the monotone increasing behavior. In particular, we provide a sufficient condition that implies the existence of some counterexamples to the monotone increasing behavior of the ACF formula in Carnot groups. The main tool is based on the lack of orthogonality of harmonic polynomials in Carnot groups. This paper generalizes the results proved in [ 17 ] .

1.
Introduction

The regularity of the solutions of two-phase free boundary problem finds crucial the use of monotonicity formulas, such as the one introduced in
[
2
]
. In this note, we continue to investigate this subject in the noncommutative setting of Carnot groups, improving the results already obtained in
[
16
,
15
,
17
,
14
]
. In fact, this paper follows the main stream of the research started in
[
16
]
and its aim is to extend some nonexistence results obtained first in
[
17
]
, in the framework of the first Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{1}
, to a larger class of Carnot groups.

More precisely, in Theorem
3.1
, we state a sufficient condition under which

(1.1)

\Phi(r)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(M)\right\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM

satisfies a monotone decreasing behavior in a right neighborhood of
0
,
for a fixed nice
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic function
u
such that
u(0)=0
, where
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}
denotes the fundamental solution of the subLaplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
in a Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
and
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u
the horizontal intrinsic gradient of
u
.

The results have been obtained exploiting the non-commutative features of the Carnot groups and they exhibit a direct method to construct counterexamples to the simplest Alt-Caffarelli-Friedman formula (
1.1
) in all the Carnot groups.

In order to get a better overview about the relevance of this result, we recall the role of such monotonicity formulas in two-phase free boundary problems.

To our knowledge,
the first formalization of the monotonicity formula applied to a two-phase free boundary problem appeared in
[
2
]
, considering functions that are minimizers of the energy functional

(1.2)

\mathcal{J}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}+\lambda_{+}\chi_{\{u>0\}}+\lambda_{-}\chi_{\{u\leq 0\}}\,dx,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set endowed with locally Lipschitz boundary and
\lambda_{\pm}>0
some given positive numbers.

Minima of
\mathcal{J}
belong to some suitable subsets of the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega)
, determined assuming particular conditions on
u
, see
[
2
]
, on which we don’t wish to enter here.

Nevertheless, in this framework, a special role is played by the set
F(u):=\partial\Omega^{+}(u)\cap\Omega
, traditionally named as
the free boundary
of the problem.

Assuming some further hypotheses, see
[
2
]
one more time, the condition that minima satisfy on
F(u)
may be understood as

\left\lvert\nabla u^{+}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\nabla u^{-}\right\rvert^{2}=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}.

In addition, minima of
\mathcal{J}
, in the sense of the
domain variation
,
[
33
]
, satisfy the following system studied in
[
6
]
,

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lclclc}\Delta u=0&\qquad\text{in $\Omega^{+}(u):=\{x\in\Omega:u>0\}$},\\
\Delta u=0&\qquad\text{in $\Omega^{-}(u):=\{x\in\Omega:u\leq 0\}^{o}$},\\
\left\lvert\nabla u^{+}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\nabla u^{-}\right\rvert^{2}=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}&\qquad\text{on $F(u)$,}\end{array}\right.

with viscosity tools, see also
[
12
]
for a gentle introduction about the domain variation approach.

Problem (
1.3
) may be
read as entailing the Euler-Lagrange equations associate with the minima of the functional (
1.2
).

In this setting, supposing that
0\in F(u)
, the monotonicity result, proved in
[
2
]
, states that for every
solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
of (
1.3
), denoting
u^{+}:=\sup\{u,0\}
and
u^{-}:=\sup\{-u,0\}
the positive and negative part of
u
respectively, the function

(1.4)

J_{u}(r)=\frac{1}{r^{4}}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{\left\lvert\nabla u^{+}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{n-2}}\,dx\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{\left\lvert\nabla u^{-}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{n-2}}\,dx

is monotone increasing for all
r\in(0,R),
for a suitable
R>0
.

Function (
1.4
), and its monotonicity properties, has been widely studied in the Euclidean setting and, usually, it is called the Alt-Caffarelli-Friedman monotonicity formula (ACF formula or, simply, monotonicity formula for future references) since, in this framework, it is monotone increasing in a right neighborhood of
0
.

In addition, in the same paper
[
2
]
, it has been shown how (
1.4
) basically provides bounds to the product of the gradients of
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
at the points of the free boundary. Thus, this monotonicity formula allows to deduce the Lipschitz continuity of the global solution
u
of the problem (
1.3
).

ACF formula has several applications in many different framework, see for instance
[
9
,
27
,
1
,
30
,
11
,
23
]
and it has had several generalizations like in
[
6
,
5
,

,
7
,
10
,
13
,
29
,
24
,
31
,
28
,
22
]
.

Hence, the same questions posed in the Euclidean setting, concerning the regularity of functions satisfying companion systems like (
1.3
), arise when the problem is stated in Carnot groups, where, as it is well known, it is possibile to study the parallel problem of (
1.3
) that appears to have the following form, see
[
12
]
,

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lclc}\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u=0&\qquad\text{in $\Omega^{+}(u)$},\\
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u=0&\qquad\text{in $\Omega^{-}(u)$},\\
\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u^{+}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u^{-}\rvert^{2}=1&\qquad\text{on $F(u).$}\end{array}\right.

In particular, here,
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
and
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
denote respectively the sub-Laplacian and the horizontal gradient on
\mathbb{G}
, we refer to Section
2
for precise definitions.

As a consequence, to investigate the existence of an
intrinsic
companion ACF formula of (
1.4
), in the noncommutative framework, appears, in a sense, natural and useful as well.

The Free Boundary Problem (
1.5
) suggests that the ACF formula candidate to this two-phase scenario has to be the following one:

(1.6)

J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)=\frac{1}{r^{4}}\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u^{+}(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM\cdot\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u^{-}(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM,

where
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}
denotes the fundamental solution of
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
, with pole at the origin, and
B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)
is the right superlevel set of
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}
.

It is worth to warn here the reader about some delicate points. Instead of considering, in the integrals of (
1.1
) and (
1.6
), the fundamental solution of the sub-Laplacian, some powers of different homogeneous norms might be considered. For instance, instead of the so called
\mathcal{L}-
gauge norm associated with the fundamental solution, it is possible to deal with

d_{CC}^{2-Q}
, where
d_{CC}
denotes the Carnot-Charathéodory distance in the Carnot group and
Q
is the homogeneous dimension. In addition, instead of considering the horizontal gradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
it is possible to study the right horizontal gradients, see for instance
[
22
]
.

Anyhow, in view of the problem (
1.5
) and recalling some of the applications associated with the regularity of its solutions as well, we are interested in the properties of the functions defined in (
1.1
) and (
1.6
).

In this direction some results have been already obtained: in
[
15
,
16
]

the authors discuss about the form of (

) and retrace a part of the fundamental tools in parallel with the Euclidean proofs for the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Following an idea recalled in

, about classical harmonic functions, through the use of harmonic homogeneous polynomials, in

More recently, in
[

, a sufficient condition involving a mean value formula of the norm of the gradient has been established and used to provide another family of counterexamples in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
.

We point out as well that, in
[
22
]
, the author studied a monotonicity formula with right invariant vector fields in a different perspective of research.

The main result we obtain in this paper is the following one.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
of step
s
, with
s>1
, there exists an intrinsic harmonic function
u
such that (
1.1
) fails to be monotone increasing in a right neighborhood of
0
.

Indeed, in Theorem

, we prove that in every Carnot group an intrinsic harmonic function exists such that

On the other hand, since (

) appears also to be
a factor of (

), this fact may be exploited to prove, as well as in

in the Heisenberg group only, that Theorem

, holds.

More precisely, we can generalize the result of the nonexistence of an ACF formula as (
1.6
) to all Carnot groups of step two. In fact the following result holds.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
of step
2
, there exists a continuous function
u
such that
u
is harmonic in
\{u>0\}
as well as
u
is harmonic in
\{u\leq 0\}^{o}
and

fails to be increasing in a right neighborhood of
0
.

We proved this result in such generality only in two step Carnot groups since we can exploit both a symmetry property with respect to the variables associated to the first stratum of the function in the counterexample built starting from Theorem
1.1
, and the symmetry property of the fundamental solution with respect the first stratum variables, as it can be recognized in the representation formula due to Beals, Gaveau and Greiner
[
3
]
.

Carnot group of step
s>2
, in general, to our knowledge don’t have a so explicit representation of the fundamental solution, even if, it is well known that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P)=\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P^{-1}),
see for instance
[
20
]
, where
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}
denotes the fundamental solution, with pole in

, of the sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
in the Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
.

Hence, Theorem
5.1
applies only whenever it is possible to build a counterexample with an

, see Section

We point out that Theorem

is a consequence of Theorem

2.
Carnot group settings

In this section we introduce the main definitions and notations used in the framework of Carnot groups.
For the notations we follow
[
18
]
and we refer to
[
4
]
, and
[
32
]
for a broader exposition of Carnot groups.

A Lie group
\mathbb{G}
is a manifold endowed with a structure of differential group, i.e. a group where the maps

(x,y)\mapsto xy\in\mathbb{G},\quad x\mapsto x^{-1}\in\mathbb{G}\text{ are $C^{\infty}$ for any $x,y\in\mathbb{G}$}.

A vector space
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be a Lie algebra if there exists a bilinear and anti-symmetric map
[\cdot,\cdot]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\mapsto\mathfrak{g}
which satisfies the Jacobi’s identity, i.e.,

[X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0\quad\text{for all $X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{g}.$}

Given two subalgebras
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
we will denote with
[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}]
the vector subspace generated by the elements of
\{[X,Y]\,:\,X\in\mathfrak{a},Y\in\mathfrak{b}\}.
We denote
\mathfrak{g}^{1}:=\mathfrak{g}
and by induction
\mathfrak{g}^{l+1}:=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}^{l}]
.

We will say that
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent of step
s
if
\mathfrak{g}^{s}\neq\{0\}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{s+1}=\{0\}.

A connected and simply connected stratified nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}
is said to be a Carnot group of step
s
if its associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
admits a stratification of step
s
, if
\mathfrak{g}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{g}_{s}
linear subspaces exist such that,
\mathfrak{g}
can be written as the direct sum of the
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
and the
(i+1)
-th subspace is generated by commutating the elements of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
, i.e.

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{p},\quad[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{i}]=\mathfrak{g}_{i+1},\quad\mathfrak{g}_{s}\neq\{0\},\quad\mathfrak{g}_{i}=\{0\}\text{ for $i>s$}.

The first layer
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, often called horizontal layer, has a key role since can generate the whole space
\mathfrak{g}
by commutation.

The homogeneous dimension
Q
of
\mathbb{G}
is,

Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{p}i\,dim(\mathfrak{g}_{i}).

Let us denote with
e
the unit element on
\mathbb{G}
. We recall that the map
X\mapsto X(e)
, that associates with a left-invariant vector field
X
its value at
e
, is an isomorphism from
\mathfrak{g}
to the tangent space
T\mathbb{G}_{e}
, identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
m_{i}=dim(\mathfrak{g}_{i})
and
h_{i}=m_{1}+\dots+m_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,p
. Hence it holds
h_{p}=n
.
We choose the basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
adapted to the stratification of
\mathfrak{g}
, in the sense of having

e_{h_{j-1}+1},\dots,e_{h_{j}}\text{ as the basis of }\mathfrak{g}_{j}\text{ for each }j=1,\dots,p.

Moreover, let
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
be the family of left invariant vector fields such that
X_{i}(e)=e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n.

The subbundle of the tangent bundle
T\mathbb{G}
spanned by the vector fields associated to the horizontal layer
X_{1},\dots,X_{m_{1}}
is called the horizontal bundle
H\mathbb{G}
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{G}
, each fiber
H\mathbb{G}_{x}=span\{X_{1}(x),\dots,X_{m_{1}}(x)\}
is endowed by a scalar product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
such that
\{X_{1}(x),\dots,X_{m_{1}}(x)\}
becomes an orthonormal basis.

We identify the Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
, through exponential coordinates, with the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\cdot)
, where
n
is the dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
, endowed with a suitable group operation.

For any
x\in\mathbb{G}
, the left translation
\tau_{x}:\mathbb{G}\mapsto\mathbb{G}
is defined as

z\mapsto\tau_{x}z:=x\cdot z.

For any
\lambda>0
, the dilations
\delta_{\lambda}:\mathbb{G}\mapsto\mathbb{G},
is defined as

\delta_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(\lambda^{d_{1}}x_{1},\dots,\lambda^{d_{n}}x_{n}),

where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
is called homogeneity of the variable
x_{i}\in\mathbb{G}
(see
[
21
]
Chapter 1) and is defined as

d_{j}=i\quad\text{whenever }h_{i-1}+1\leq j\leq h_{i},

hence,
1=d_{1}=\dots=d_{m_{1}}<2=d_{m_{1}+1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}=p.

We follow the notation of
[
4
]
, Chapter 20, so that
\beta
denotes a multi-index with
n
entries,
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Let

\displaystyle x^{\beta}

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot x_{n}^{\beta_{n}},

\displaystyle\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{n},

\displaystyle\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert_{\mathbb{G}}=d_{1}\beta_{1}+\dots+d_{n}\beta_{n}.

We introduce the
\mathbb{G}
-polynomial as the polynomial
\mathcal{P}
with respect to the coordinate system
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{G}
and we define the
\mathbb{G}
-degree of a
\mathbb{G}
-polynomial as follows,

(2.1)

\deg_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P}):=\max\left\{\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert_{\mathbb{G}}\;:\;\mathcal{P}(x)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}c_{\beta}x^{\beta}\text{ with $c_{\beta}\neq 0$ for every $\beta$}\right\}.

From now on, for simplicity, a
\mathbb{G}
-polynomial and the
\mathbb{G}
-degree of a
\mathbb{G}
-polynomial are denoted omitting the letter
\mathbb{G}
.

We exploit the Haar measure of
\mathbb{G}
which is the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to have a notion of an integral in
\mathbb{G}
. Once the basis
X_{1},\dots,X_{m_{1}}
of the horizontal layer is fixed, we define for any function
f:\mathbb{G}\mapsto\mathbb{R},
for which
X_{j}f
exists, the horizontal gradient of
f
, denoted by
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f
, as the horizontal section

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}(X_{i}f)X_{i},

whose coordinates are
(X_{1}f,\dots,X_{m_{1}}f)
. In the same way it is possible to extend to higher order this rappresentation.
Moreover, if
\phi=(\phi_{1}\dots,\phi_{m_{1}})
is a horizontal section such that
X_{j}\phi_{j}\in L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
for
j=1,\dots,m_{1},
we define
\div_{\mathbb{G}}\phi
as the real valued function

\div_{\mathbb{G}}(\phi):=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X_{j}\phi_{j}.

We denote by
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
the sub-Laplacian on
\mathbb{G}
the operator

\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X_{j}^{2}.

As well as to the Euclidean case, we are interested in fundamental solution of
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
.
For a deeper overview on the existence, the properties and the global estimates of fundamental solutions of sub-Laplacians we refer, for instance, to
[
21
,
4
]
.
In particular, a function
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\cdot,P):\mathbb{G}\setminus\{P\}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the fundamental solution of
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
with pole in
P\in\mathbb{G}
if:

(i)

\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\cdot,P)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G}\setminus\{P\})
;

(ii)

\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\cdot,P)\in L_{loc}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
and
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M,P)\rightarrow 0
when
M
tends to infinity;

(iii)

\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\cdot,P)=\delta_{P}
, being
\delta_{P}
the Dirac measure supported at
{P}
. More explicitly,

\int_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M,P)\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}\varphi(M)dM=-\varphi(P)\quad\text{for any $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{G}).$}

We further introduce, as well, the
\mathbb{G}
-ball centered at
P
with radius
r
,
B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(P)
, as the superlevel set of the fundamental solution
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\cdot,P)
,
[
4
]
,

B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(P)=\{M\in\mathbb{G}\,:\,\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M,P)\geq r^{-(Q-2)}\}.

3.
A characterization via harmonic homogeneous polynomials

In this framework, each of two factors of the ACF Formula in Carnot groups has the form,

(3.1)

\Phi(r)=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(P)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(M)\right\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M,P)\,dM.

A key role in establishing an ACF Monotonicity Formula relies on the properties of the fundamental solution.

However, an explicit formula of the fundamental solution can be given only for a small class of Carnot groups. For instance, apart the Euclidean case, it can be done explicitly dealing with
H-
type groups that include Heisenberg groups, see
[
19
]
.

In general the fundamental solution of a sub-Laplacian in a Carnot group is not known explicitly. Nevertheless, see
[
4
]
, a homogeneous norm exists
d(\cdot):=\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
such that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M,P)=C_{Q}d^{2-Q}(M^{-1}\circ P)
. Then it is possible to exploit the scale invariant properties of the fundamental solutions as well as those of the intrinsic harmonic functions to get a useful representation of (
1.1
).

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, let
u_{\lambda}(P):=u(\delta_{\lambda}P)
. Then, the following properties hold:

(G1)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u_{\lambda}(P)=\lambda(\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u)(\delta_{\lambda}P);

(G2)

If
u
satisfies
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u=0
then
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u_{\lambda}=0
;

(G3)

For any
M\in\mathbb{G},

\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\delta_{\lambda}M,P)=\lambda^{2-Q}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M,P)
.

The main result of this section is the following one.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group. If
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{3}
are homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
1
and
3
respectively, such that

(3.2)

a_{2}:=\int_{B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\displaystyle\left<\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{P}_{1}(P),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{P}_{3}(P)\right>\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P)\,dP\text{ is positive.}

Then, the function
u:=\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{3}
enjoys
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u=0
and (
1.1
) is monotone decreasing in a right neighborhood of
0
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\Phi(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(M)\right\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(\delta_{r}P)\right\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\delta_{r}P)r^{Q}\,dP

where
M\in B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)
is seen as
\delta_{r}P
, with
P\in B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0),

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{(G1)}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u(\delta_{r}P))r^{-1}\right\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\delta_{r}P)r^{Q}\,dP

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{(G3)}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u(\delta_{r}P))r^{-1}\right\rvert^{2}r^{2-Q}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P)r^{Q}\,dP

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)}\left\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u(\delta_{r}P))r^{-1}\right\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P)\,dP.

Suppose now that
u
is the sum of two homogeneous harmonic polynomials. Let
\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}
be two homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
m
and
h
respectively, i.e.

\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}(\alpha\mathcal{P}_{m}+\beta\mathcal{P}_{h})=0,\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R},

by homogeneity one also have that
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\delta_{r}P)=\lambda^{m}\mathcal{P}_{m}(P)
so,

\lambda[\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{P}_{m}](\delta_{\lambda}P)\underbrace{=}_{(G1)}\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P}_{m}(\delta_{\lambda}P))=\lambda^{m}[\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{P}_{m}](P),

therefore it holds

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem

We recall here the short proof, see e.g.
[

]
.
From coarea formula we obtain

(3.4)

\int_{B_{1}(0)}\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{h}(x),\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k}(x)\right>\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2-n}dx=\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{2-n}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{h}(x),\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k}(x)\right>\,d\sigma(x)\,d\rho.

Each component of the gradient of
\mathcal{P}_{h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{k}
are still homogeneous polynomials so we obtain the thesis if

(3.5)

\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}\mathcal{P}_{h}(x)\mathcal{P}_{k}(x)\,d\sigma(x)=0.

The equation (
3.5
) is a consequence of the divergence theorem. Indeed, being
\frac{x}{\rho}
the outward normal to
\partial B_{\rho}(0)
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\Delta\mathcal{P}_{k}-\mathcal{P}_{k}\Delta P_{h})\,dx=\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\div(\mathcal{P}_{h}\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k}-\mathcal{P}_{k}\nabla\mathcal{P}_{h})\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}\left<\mathcal{P}_{h}\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k}-\mathcal{P}_{k}\nabla\mathcal{P}_{h},\frac{x}{\rho}\right>\,d\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k},\nu\right>-\mathcal{P}_{k}\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{h},\nu\right>)\,d\sigma(x),

where
\nu:=\frac{x}{\rho}.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and

\mathcal{P}_{k}(x)=\sum_{\beta,\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=k}b_{\beta}x^{\beta},

then

(3.7)

\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k}(x)=\sum_{\beta,\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=k}b_{\beta}(\beta_{1}x_{1}^{\beta_{1}-1}x_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\dots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}},\dots,\beta_{n}x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}-1}).

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k},\nu\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{\beta,\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=k}b_{\beta}(\beta_{1}x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}x_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\dots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}}+\dots+\beta_{n}x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{\beta,\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=k}b_{\beta}(\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{n})x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}x_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\dots x_{n}^{\beta_{n}}=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{\beta,\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert=k}b_{\beta}\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert x^{\beta}=\frac{k\mathcal{P}_{k}}{\rho}.

Applying (
3.8
) to (
3.6
), we get

0=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}(\mathcal{P}_{h}\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{k},x\right>-\mathcal{P}_{k}\left<\nabla\mathcal{P}_{h},x\right>)\,d\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}(\mathcal{P}_{h}k\mathcal{P}_{k}-\mathcal{P}_{k}h\mathcal{P}_{h})\,d\sigma(x),

that gives for
h\neq k

\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(0)}\mathcal{P}_{h}\mathcal{P}_{k}\,d\sigma=0.

u
is harmonic in
\{u>0\}
as well as
u
is harmonic in
\{u\leq 0\}^{o}
such that
J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}
fails to be increasing in a neighborhood of
0
.

Proof.
Let us denote, for brevity,

\displaystyle I_{u^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r):=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u^{+}(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM=\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}\cap\{u>0\}}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM,

\displaystyle I_{u^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r):=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u^{-}(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM=\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}\cap\{u<0\}}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM,

for which we have that
J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)=I_{u^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)\cdot I_{u^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)
.

The goal is to show the monotone decreasing behavior of
J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}
:

(5.1)

\frac{d}{dr}J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)=\biggl(\frac{d}{dr}I_{u^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)\biggl)I_{u^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)+I_{u^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)\biggl(\frac{d}{dr}I_{u^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)\biggl).

Apparently, the proof we need has to depend on the special function we have determined only. In fact, the harmonic polynomials we selected in Carnot groups, have a special behavior, in comparison with the companion Euclidean harmonic polynomials.

Since
I_{u^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}
and
I_{u^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}
are nonnegative, we reach the desired result if we prove that they are both monotone decreasing or there exists a relationship with the polynomials whose existence we proved in the previous section.

Indeed, fixing

u:=u_{b,p,q}
provided by Theorem
1.1
, we have that
u
is a difference of two homogeneous polynomials of degree one and three respectively and both intrinsicly harmonic.

Moreover, recalling (
4.14
), we point out that

u_{b,p,q}(-x_{1},-x_{2},\dots,-x_{m_{1}},y)=-u_{b,p,q}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m_{1}},y).

Hence,

\mathcal{S}(\{u_{b,p,q}>0\})=\{u_{b,p,q}<0\}
, where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the change of variables that moves
x_{i}
in
-x_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m_{1}
and leaves unchanged the other variables.
Moreover, the hypothesis on the symmetry of
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}
assure also that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)=\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{S}(M))
for every
M\in\mathbb{G}
.

Thus from the construction of
u_{b,p,q}
, as a consequence of the previous remark, recalling the change of variables
\mathcal{S}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle I_{u_{b,p,q}^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}\cap\{u>0\}}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u_{b,p,q}(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM=\int_{\mathcal{S}\left(B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}\cap\{u>0\}\right)}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u_{b,p,q}(\mathcal{S}(M))\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{S}(M))\,dM

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}^{\mathbb{G}}\cap\{u<0\}}\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u_{b,p,q}(M)\rvert^{2}\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(M)\,dM=I_{u_{b,p,q}^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)

Therefore

J_{u_{b,p,q}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)=I_{u_{b,p,q}^{+}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)\cdot I_{u_{b,p,q}^{-}}^{\mathbb{G}}(r)=\frac{1}{4}(I_{u_{b,p,q}}^{\mathbb{G}})^{2}.

As a consequence, by the positivity of
I_{u_{b,p,q}}^{\mathbb{G}}
, the decreasing monotonic behavior of
J_{u_{b,p,q}}^{\mathbb{G}}
descends from the decreasing monotonic behavior of
I_{u_{b,p,q}}^{\mathbb{G}},
as we proved in Theorem
3.1
.

\Box

We are now in position to obtain the proof of Theorem
1.2
as a corollary of Theorem
5.1
.

Proof.
(of Theorem
1.2
)
The special case of a Carnot group of step two allows us to apply Theorem
5.1
. In fact, in the step two case we can prove directly the symmetry of the fundamental solution with respect to variables of the first layer.
This property derives from the integral representation formula of the fundamental solution found in
[
3
]
(Theorem
5.12.1
in
[
4
]
in Carnot group settings). Moreover, there exists a isomorphism between every Carnot group of step two with one endowed with inner law governed by skew symmetric matrices (see for instance Proposition
3.5.1
in
[
4
]
). This explicit isomorphism fixes the first
m_{1}
variables and thus the symmetry of the fundamental solution descends.
In this way we can conclude applying Theorem
5.1
.

\Box

Concerning Carnot groups of step
s>2
, to our knowledge, neither an explicit representation formula of the fundamental solution is known, in general, nor much is known about the symmetry with respect to the variables of the first stratum.
Nevertheless it is possible to provide another sufficient condition for the decreasing behavior of
J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}
exploiting the well known
\mathbb{G}
-symmetry of
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}
with respect to the origin, precisely
\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P)=\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(P^{-1}),
see for instance
[
20
]
.
Then, in order to state the result described in the following remark, we need to introduce the definition of an
intrinsic odd
function, precisely a function is said to be
intrinsic odd
if
u(P)=-u(P^{-1})
for every
P\in\mathbb{G}
.

Remark 5.2
.

For any Carnot group
\mathbb{G}
of step
s
, with
s>1
, if it were possible to build a harmonic function
u=\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{3}
such that

\left<\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{P}_{1}(P),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{P}_{3}(P)\right>>0\>\>\text{ a.e. in $B_{1}^{\mathbb{G}}(0)$}

which is
intrinsic odd
as well, then
u
would be harmonic in
\{u>0\}
as well as
u
is harmonic in
\{u\leq 0\}^{o}
and
J_{u}^{\mathbb{G}}
would fail to be increasing in a neighborhood of
0
.
This can be seen as another sufficient condition to obtain a counterexample and its proof descends straightforwardly from the proof of Theorem
5.1
considering, instead of
\mathcal{S}
, the change of variables that maps
P
into
P^{-1}
.

6.
Final remarks

In this last part, we provide an explicit application of the procedure showed in the case in the first Engel group
\mathbb{E}
, which is a three step Carnot group.

The first Engel group can be introduced as
\mathbb{R}^{4}
endowed with the inner law that for every
P,M\in\mathbb{E}
associate
P\circ M
in the following way

\displaystyle P\circ M

\displaystyle=(x_{1},x_{2},y,t)\circ(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(x_{1}+x^{\prime}_{1},\,x_{2}+x^{\prime}_{2},\,y+y^{\prime}+x_{1}x^{\prime}_{2},\,t+t^{\prime}+x_{1}y^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}x^{\prime}_{2}).

A basis of left invariant vector fields is given by

X_{1}=\partial_{x_{1}},\;X_{2}=\partial_{x_{2}}+x_{1}\partial_{y}+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}\partial_{t},\;Y=\partial_{y}+x_{1}\partial_{t},\;T=\partial_{t}.

The fields
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
generate the horizontal layer
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
of
\mathbb{E}
. The commutator between the vector fields is

[X_{1},X_{2}]=Y,\quad[X_{1},Y]=T,

otherwise is
0.

Thus we have

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{3},

where

\mathfrak{g}_{1}=\Span\{X_{1},X_{2}\},\quad\mathfrak{g}_{2}=\Span\{Y\},\quad\mathfrak{g}_{3}=\Span\{T\}.

The sub-Laplacian on
\mathbb{E}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle=(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})=\big(\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}^{2}+(\partial_{x_{2}}+x_{1}\partial_{y}+\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{2}\partial_{t})^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}^{2}+\partial_{x_{2}x_{2}}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\partial_{yy}^{2}+\frac{x_{1}^{4}}{4}\partial_{tt}^{2}+2x_{1}\partial_{x_{2}y}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\partial_{x_{2}t}^{2}+x_{1}^{3}\partial_{yt}^{2}\big).

Now, applying the procedure of Section
4
with
p=0,q=\frac{1}{2},b=1
by (
4.9
) we obtain

u=\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{3}=x_{2}-\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+\frac{1}{6}x_{2}^{3}+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}y\biggl),

for which

\displaystyle\left<\nabla_{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{P}_{1},\nabla_{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{P}_{3}\right>=\left<(0,1),(\frac{1}{2}y-x_{1}x_{2},\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2})\right>=\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2},\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{E}}u=0.

Hence, invoking Theorem
1.1

we can conclude that
u
provides a counterexample for the increasing monotone behavior of (
1.1
).

The structure of the fundamental solution of the sublaplacian to
\Delta_{\mathbb{E}}
in the Engel group, to our knowledge, is not explicit. Hence we cannot conclude applying the Theorem
5.1
. In fact, only if
\Gamma_{\mathbb{E}}
enjoyed the symmetry requested by Theorem
5.1
, we would be in position to produce a counterexample even to the increasing monotonicity of
J_{u}^{\mathbb{E}}
.

Remark 6.1
.

One could also try to look for harmonic functions being
intrinsic odd
hoping to apply Remark
5.2
.
For a general Carnot group is not so simple because the expression of
P^{-1}
depends on the inner law of the group that could be very complicated.

In the first Engel group
\mathbb{E}
, for instance, the expression of the inverse of
(x_{1},x_{2},y,t)
is given by
(-x_{1},-x_{2},-y-x_{1}x_{2},-t+x_{1}y+\frac{3}{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2})
and it is not possible to build a
intrinsic odd
polynomial of degree three.
However, if we consider polynomials of degree five it is possible to do so considering

\mathcal{P}_{5}=x_{1}y^{2}-2yx_{1}^{2}x_{2}+2tx_{1}x_{2}+\frac{x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}}{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{3}

then
u=\mathcal{P}_{1}-\mathcal{P}_{5}
with
\mathcal{P}_{1}=x_{2}
is
intrinsic odd
and
\left<\nabla_{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{P}_{1},\nabla_{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{P}_{5}\right>=3x_{2}^{2}x_{1}^{2}>0
almost everywhere in
B_{1}^{\mathbb{E}}(0)
but is not harmonic.
