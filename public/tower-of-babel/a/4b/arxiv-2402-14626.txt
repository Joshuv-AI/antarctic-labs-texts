Self-arresting earthquakes and critical sliding nucleation theory
Didier Sornette 1 , Xueting Wei 2 and Xiaofei Chen 1,2

Abstract
We develop a statistical thermodynamic approach for understanding earthquake nucleation on homogeneous faults, explaining the occurrence of both self-arresting and run-away unstable ruptures (subshear and supershear events) previously observed in numerical simulations. Our theory identifies the conditions under which self-arresting earthquakes occur, based on critical sliding distance and dynamical stress drop. We also derive the Gutenberg-Richter distribution for these earthquakes, linking the fractal nature of faulting with the nucleation physics through the critical size’s dependence on dynamical stress drop. Furthermore, we connect our findings to the dragon-king theory, which suggests that the largest earthquakes differ significantly in physical and statistical properties from smaller ones, offering new insights for earthquake prediction and risk assessment.



Earthquakes fascinate physicists with their power law size distribution, multi-scale faulting patterns, underlying self-organized critical
behavior and still largely unpredictable behavior, while they embody the interplay between Earth’s physical processes and their profound societal impacts.
The processes that lead to the onset and halting of an earthquake, as well as its preparatory stages, are still not fully understood.
For decades, nucleation has been key to understanding earthquake mechanisms, referring in literature to the preparatory stage before an earthquake occurs

Ohnaka and Kuwahara 1990
;
Ellsworth and Beroza 1995
.
Slip-weakening models describe earthquakes as starting at asperities –fault patches with higher pre-stresses– and ending at barriers with greater frictional strength. In contrast, rate-and-state models suggest frictional strength increases with slip, aiding in stopping and healing. Although physical models of earthquake nucleation at asperities vary, they agree that post-nucleation, sliding becomes irreversibly unstable, propagating at near-sonic speeds until barriers halt the rupture.

The necessity of a barrier to halt every earthquake implies strict conditions and suggests a higher frequency of large earthquakes, given that barriers may not be present at all quake locations. However, the prevalence of small earthquakes over large ones in reality hints at a tendency for earthquakes to self-arrest rather than being stopped by barriers.
This prompted detailed elastodynamical rupture simulations using the boundary integral equation method,
revealing that, under a broad range of parameters, earthquakes on homogeneous faults can self-arrest after initial propagation, i.e., stop spontaneously without barriers
Xu et al. 2015
;
Wei et al. 2021
.
In addition to numerical investigations, field observations further validate the existence of self-arresting ruptures
Wen et al. 2018
;
Galis et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2023
.

Despite evidence supporting the existence of these seismic events, the underlying physical mechanisms responsible for their occurrence remain elusive.
Here, we offer a quantitative physical explanation for the existence of self-arresting ruptures across diverse mechanical fault properties,
adapting the thermodynamic Griffith fracture theory to frictional sliding. This approach is inspired by classical nucleation theory,
traditionally used to describe the spontaneous emergence of a new thermodynamic phase from a metastable state.
Different from the term “nucleation” commonly used in the context of earthquakes, our derivation suggests
a need to update our understanding of earthquake nucleation through the lens of statistical thermodynamics.

The standard picture of an earthquake is that it arises from an irreversible process,
beginning with slow creep or sliding, followed by a gradual acceleration of sliding and/or damage.
Ultimately, this leads to the elastodynamic rupture of the earthquake, which is perceived as the outcome of an irreversible sliding instability.
In the literature, earthquake nucleation refers to the initial process that leads to an earthquake.
It may be associated with one or several of the following processes:
a gradual increase of stress and deformation across fault networks, progressive damage and creep processes,
evolution from stable frictional sliding on the future fault rupture surface,
various possible poro-hydro-thermal-chemical-mechanical processes
in fault zones and so on.
While there is a large variety of descriptions, earthquake nucleation is systematically thought of as an irreversible maturation
development towards a new regime where the stored elastic energy is released suddenly, causing the fault to slip along
sliding zones with boundaries propagating at velocities close to Rayleigh wave velocities, thus generating
seismic waves perceived as an earthquake.

How the concept of earthquake nucleation is used in seismological literature is exemplified by the work of
Ohnaka and Kuwahara who defined the nucleation zone as the (transition) zone where a crack grows stably with accelerating speeds
from the point where the slip failure nucleus is formed to the critical point, beyond which the crack grows unstably
at a speed close to sonic velocities
Ohnaka and Kuwahara 1990
. To account for their laboratory experimental data, they envision four stages,
(i) static formation of the slip failure nucleus, (ii) subsequent quasistatic steady crack growth defined as stable
rupture caused by further increase in the applied external stress, (iii) stable, but accelerating crack
growth up to a critical crack length, (iv) beyond which instability begins to occur. This nucleation picture is further
supported by recent experiments
Ben-David and Fineberg 2011
.
Beroza and Ellsworth called “seismic nucleation phase” the initial regime of low moment rate relative to the rest of the earthquake rupture

Ellsworth and Beroza 1995
.
Abercrombie and Mori analyzes the first three seconds of the Landers earthquake for information concerning its “nucleation” and early growth
Abercrombie and Mori 1994
.
Ide suggested that the initial onsets of earthquakes may be related to the nucleation process, believed to be
initiated before the dynamic rupture process in rocky materials
Ide 2019
.
One perspective is that there is a ‘pre-slip’ nucleation process before the development of the full rupture dynamics
radiating seismic waves, during which a confined portion of the fault, referred to as the
nucleation zone, undergoes a period of slow aseismic and transiently stable sliding.
This sliding progressively accelerates until the slipping patch reaches a “critical size”
Ohnaka and Kuwahara 1990
;
Dieterich 1992
;
Ohnaka 1993
;
Latour et al. 2013
;
McLaskey 2019
.
In Ohnaka’s theory, the “critical size” is the length beyond which the slip in the nucleation zone transitions into an accelerated unstable regime
leading to a fast sub-Rayleigh or supershear rupture.
Ohnaka argued that the nucleation process is itself an immediate earthquake precursor
Ohnaka 1992

and proposed to infer the “critical size” of the nucleation zone from the immediate foreshock activity
Ohnaka 1993
.
Similar concepts pervade the interpretation of laboratory stick-slip experiments
Ben-David and Fineberg 2011
;
Bolton et al. 2023
,
as well as in theoretical and numerical studies examining the spatial and temporal characteristics of foreshocks and nucleation processes

Ruina 1983
;
Rice and Gu 1983
;
Campillo and Ionescu 1997
;
Dascalu et al. 2000
;
Uenishi and Rice 1983
.
A general loose view is to define the nucleation stage as the time period during which the fault
slip velocity increases slowly from a background rate to a higher slip rate
Bolton et al. 2023
leading to
a transition into an accelerated and unstable regime, ultimately resulting in the rapid sub-Rayleigh or supershear rupture.

However, the above smooth nucleation paradigm has been suggested to be relevant only to unnaturally homogeneous faults
McLaskey 2019
.
Alternative models of earthquake initiation, such as cascades and accumulation of small stochastic events
may be more appropriate. In this alternative view, seismic nucleation involves the accumulation of small stochastic events,
which ultimately leads to the largest sub-event of the earthquake. This physical picture is commonly developed in ‘cascade’ models
of sequential or interconnected patterns of earthquake events, where the energy released by one
earthquake serves as a stress perturbation that can trigger another earthquake on a nearby fault segment

Wyss and Brune 1967
;
Allègre et al. 1982
;
Fukao and Furumoto 1985
;
Abercrombie and Mori 1994
;
Ide and Aochi 2005
.
In this spirit, Brune discussed a triggering rupture propagation model and focused on the rupture initiations
Brune 1979
.
Examples of cascades models include also self-organised critical models
Sornette and Sornette 1989
;
Bak and Tang 1989
;
Sornette et al. 1990
.

The standard concept is thus that aseismic creep or stochastic small instabilities are an intrinsic part of
the nucleation process of earthquakes. In other words, earthquakes are considered to be preceded by
a preparatory stage called earthquake nucleation that leads to irreversible dynamical ruptures.

While the above physical pictures have contributed to the understanding of earthquakes,
there are still important open questions.
In particular, the discovery of self-arresting earthquakes through numerical simulations and empirical analyses

Xu et al. 2015
;
Wei et al. 2021
;
Wen et al. 2018
;
Galis et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2023
reveals that elastodynamic ruptures may spontaneously come to a stop in the absence of barriers.
This finding challenges the longstanding belief that earthquake rupture is irreversible, revealing the process of earthquake nucleation to be more complex than previously understood.

We now propose a simple theory for the existence of self-arresting earthquakes
based on a reasoning analogous to that used in the classical theory of nucleation
in statistical thermodynamics. In this way, we propose an extension of the concept of nucleation
as used in seismology. Our reasoning also parallels Griffith’s fundamental principle, which
forms the foundation of modern linear elastic fracture mechanics.
Griffith’s theory is based on comparing the decrease in potential energy that accompanies fracturing
and the increase in surface energy resulting from the formation of new free surfaces as a crack expands
Griffith 1921
.
Rapid fracture initiation, as described by the Griffith criterion, happens when the bulk potential energy
released by the growth of an existing crack exceeds the energy required for fracture.

In order to understand the possibility for self-arresting earthquake ruptures, we take inspiration
from the analogy between rupture sliding nucleation and the classical Gibbs theory of droplet nucleation
Rundle and Klein 1989
;
Rundle et al. 1997
;
Saichev and Sornette 2005
.
Our formulation contains physical ingredients similar to those entering the calculation of the
minimum patch for a rupture to expand in the case of a 2-D in-plane fault
Andrews 1976
,
for a circular fault
Day 1982
and for the rupture propagation along a long strike-slip fault and along a rectangular asperity
Madariaga and Olsen 2000
.
In particular, Madariaga and Olsen
Madariaga and Olsen 2000
obtain a phase diagram similar to that shown in figure
1
below. However,
the boundary they report is between subshear propagating ruptures and a parameter domain coined “does not rupture”, instead of
the “self-arresting” rupture domain in our case. They did not consider the scenario of “stopping after rupture broke the asperity”
as an independent seismic event but rather considered it a failure in the simulation. This interpretation was based on the belief that
earthquakes must reach a critical nucleation size and undergo an unstable dynamic propagation phase.
In contrast, by taking seriously the possibility of genuine rupture propagation followed by self-arrest,
Xu et al.
Xu et al. 2015
and Wei et al.
Wei et al. 2021
discovered that, in a large domain of parameter space, an earthquake nucleation along an homogeneous fault can lead to an earthquake that self-arrests.

Based on an analogy with classical Gibbs theory of droplet nucleation, in which
droplets continuously form, grow and evaporate when not reaching the critical radius, our physical picture is
based on the existence of asperity patches of all possible sizes
on which sliding “nucleates” but then self-arrests when their initial size is smaller than a critical value derived below.
Hence, the critical bifurcation described by Madariaga and Olsen
Madariaga and Olsen 2000
should be interpreted
as one between transiently growing ruptures that self-arrest (similarly to a droplet that first grows and evaporates before nucleating the
macroscopic liquid state) and the unstoppable (in the absence of barriers) sub-Rayleigh earthquakes.

The free energy
F(R)
associated with the nucleation of a sliding event occurring over a disk of radius
R
can be decomposed into two terms:
(a) the decrease of free energy
F_{s}(R)
resulting from the stress drop induced by the sliding event and (b) the work
F_{f}
against friction (analogous to the creation
of interfaces in crack or droplet nucleation).
F_{s}(R)
can be evaluated as

F_{s}(R)=-{1\over 2}\epsilon_{e}T_{e}\cdot\eta{4\pi\over 3}R^{3}~~~~{\rm where}~\epsilon_{e}={T_{e}\over\mu}~.

(1)

\epsilon_{e}
is the deformation associated with the dynamical stress drop
T_{e}
in a medium of shear modulus
\mu

and the corresponding energy density gain is
{1\over 2}\epsilon_{e}T_{e}
(energy per unit volume). The term
\eta{4\pi\over 3}R^{3}

represents an estimation of the volume over which the elastic energy drop occurs in the nucleation sliding domain. This volume
is the volume of a sphere of radius equal to the nucleation patch times a geometrical coefficient
\eta
of order unit.
In the case of 2-D in-plane fault
Andrews 1976
,
\eta=\pi(1-\nu)/2\approx 1.18
for a typical value for the Poisson modulus
\nu=0.25
.
For a circular fault
Day 1982
,
\eta=12/(7\pi)\approx 0.546
. For an anti-plane rupture
Ida 1972
,
\eta=\pi/2\approx 1.57
.
Madariaga and Olsen
Madariaga and Olsen 2000
report values corresponding to
\eta
between
0.625
and
0.83
.

The work
F_{f}
against friction is the integral over the sliding displacement of the frictional force,
itself obtained as the product of the frictional stress times the surface of the nucleating domain.
In the calculations of
Xu et al. 2015
;
Wei et al. 2021
, the frictional stress is taken to decrease
linearly as a function of slip over the critical distance
D_{c}
and then crosses over to a constant level
\sigma_{r}
for
D>D_{c}
.
For
D>D_{c}
, we thus obtain

F_{f}=\pi R^{2}\left(\sigma_{r}D+{1\over 2}T_{u}D_{c}\right)~,

(2)

where
T_{u}
is the breakdown stress drop.
Since we are interested in the nucleation regime
D\approx D_{c}
, and given that
\sigma_{r}\ll T_{u}
, we can approximate
expression (
2
) as

F_{f}\simeq{1\over 2}\pi R^{2}~T_{u}D_{c}~,

(3)

The total free energy of a nucleation disk of size
R
is thus

F(R)=-{1\over 2}{T_{e}^{2}\over\mu}\cdot\eta{4\pi\over 3}R^{3}+{1\over 2}T_{u}D_{c}\cdot\pi R^{2}~.

(4)

F(R)
first grows as
R
increases as the work against friction dominates and then decreases without bounds beyond a
critical radius
R^{c}
given by

{dF(R)\over dR}_{|R=R^{c}}=0~\to R^{c}={1\over 2\eta}{T_{u}\over T_{e}}{\mu\over T_{e}}D_{c}~.

(5)

The total free energy (
4
) thus predicts that a sliding event occurring over a disk of radius
R

will self-arrest if its radius
R
is smaller than the critical value
R^{c}
given by expression (
5
).
On the other hand, for
R>R^{c}
, the sliding event grows without bounds until it encounters a sufficiently strong barrier.
This is similar to the classical theory for crack or droplet nucleation. Our derivation in terms of a free energy for the
nucleation of a sliding disk rationalises and interprets the existence of the “self-arresting ruptures” as being
the equivalent to the stable nucleating cracks or droplets in classical nucleation theory.

We now derive the Gutenberg-Richter distribution for the self-arresting earthquakes with radius smaller than

R^{c}
given by expression (
5
), by linking the fractal nature of faulting
with the nucleation physics through the critical size’s dependence on dynamical stress drop.
In the real Earth crust,
R^{c}
is not a constant but is expected to be inhomogeneous distributed in space
since the elastic modulus
\mu
, the breakdown stress drop
T_{u}
, the dynamic stress drop
T_{e}
and the critical sliding distance
D_{c}

are material dependent and are expected to vary from place to place.
Given that
R^{c}
is inversely proportional to the square of
T_{e}
,
the largest contribution to the variations of
R^{c}
at large values comes from
the variations of the dynamic stress drop
T_{e}
at small values.
Let us assume that
T_{e}
is distributed according to some spatial distribution
p(T_{e})
, which is regular for small
T_{e}
.
The simplest forms are either a constant or an exponential distribution for its probability density function (pdf) for small
T_{e}
’s
and give the same asymptotic result and the same Gutenberg-Richter distribution. Let us take

p(T_{e})={1\over T_{0}}e^{-{T_{e}\over T_{0}}}
,
which is normalised to
1
for
T_{e}
spanning from
0
to infinity (this upper boundary is of no consequence for the distribution of

R^{c}
at intermediate and large values).
Expression (
5
) together with the exponential form of
p(T_{e})
leads to the pdf of
R^{c}
given by

p(R^{c})=\sqrt{{1\over 2\eta}{T_{u}\over T_{0}}{\mu\over T_{0}}D_{c}}~e^{-{1\over T_{0}}\sqrt{{T_{u}\mu D_{c}\over 2\eta R^{c}}}}~{1\over(R^{c})^{3\over 2}}~.

(8)

This implies
p(R^{c})\propto{1\over(R^{c})^{3\over 2}}
for
R^{c}\geq{T_{u}\mu D_{c}\over 2\eta T_{0}^{2}}
.
Consider a fault network of span
L
. The number of independent patches of size
R
on this fault network is proportional to
\left({L\over R}\right)^{D_{f}}
,
where
D_{f}
is the fractal dimension of the fault network.
Thus the total number of nucleation domains that are in the self-arresting rupture regime is the sum over all possible
critical radii
R^{c}
weighted by its pdf:

p(R)=\left({L\over R}\right)^{D_{f}}\int_{R}^{+\infty}dR^{c}~p(R^{c})\simeq{1\over R^{D_{f}+{1\over 2}}}~.

(9)

The lower bound in the above integral expresses the fact that a given asperity of size
R
is in the self-arresting rupture regime
if it is smaller than its local critical radius. The pdf of the disk radius
R
translates into the pdf of seismic moment
M_{0}

using the standard scaling relation
M_{0}\propto R^{3}
. Using the conservation of probabilities under a change of variable

p(R)dR=p(M_{0})dM_{0}
yields

p(M_{0})\simeq{1\over M_{0}^{1+{D_{f}-{1\over 2}\over 3}}}~.

(10)

Kagan and Knopoff
Kagan and Knopoff 1980

D_{f}=2.2
, which should be taken as a lower bound since it is obtained from
the two-point correlation function of the distribution of earthquakes. Davy et al.
Davy et al. 1990

D_{f}=1+1.7=2.7
from
analog laboratory experiments of the India-Asia collision. Sornette

in the range
2.5-2.7
. Taking
D_{f}=2.5
recovers exactly the
2/3
exponent of the Gutenberg-Richter distribution
of seismic moments, equivalent to the
b
-value equal to
1

when expressed in terms of magnitudes.
This derivation informs that the Gutenberg-Richter distribution holds for self-arresting earthquakes.
In the present framework, the Gutenberg-Richter distribution derives from the combination of the fractal nature of faulting and the physics of earthquake nucleation
via the dependence of the critical nucleation size as a function of the dynamical stress drop.
Variations of the
b
-value could be associated with different fault network properties with distinct fractal dimensions.

Interpreting self-arresting ruptures as analogous to stable nucleating cracks or droplets in classical nucleation suggests
to consider them as similar to how droplets cease growing in classical thermodynamical nucleation theory.
Conversely, if a nucleation patch exceeds a critical size, the sliding rupture escalates uncontrollably until encountering a substantial barrier. This mirrors the unstable nucleation of a new phase in classical theory, indicating a coexistence of self-arresting stable ruptures and runaway unstable ruptures.
The run-away unstable ruptures (the subshear and supershear events) are subjected
to positive feedbacks, i.e., as the rupture develops, more elastic energy is released
that further propels the rupture and the frictional forces are unable to stop the propagating front.
A dynamical balance is reached via the additional dissipation mechanism involving seismic radiation.
In contrast, for self-arresting ruptures, friction together with seismic radiation combine as dissipation mechanisms to stop the rupture.
This shows that the two types of ruptures have different mechanisms and should be indeed
in different classes.
We propose that the run-away unstable ruptures are instantiations of the concept of dragon-kings
Sornette 2009
;
Sornette and Ouillon 2012
, special events
that are both extremely large (“king”), and born of unique origins (“dragon”) relative to its peers.
The run-away unstable ruptures (the subshear and supershear events) that are stopped only by strong geometrical barriers are the dragon-kings
while the sub-critical self-arresting ruptures are the “normal” events, distributed according to the
standard Gutenberg-Richter distribution (
10
).

The run-away unstable ruptures (the subshear and supershear events) are stopped only by strong geometrical barriers.
The distribution of their magnitudes can be expected to be different, more associated with geometric features of faults
and fault networks. This is in agreement with the theory of dragon-kings
Sornette 2009
;
Sornette and Ouillon 2012
that predicts that
the distribution of the dragon-kings are in general different from the distribution of the smaller normal events.
Indeed, within the nucleation picture developed above, the dragon-king subshear and supershear earthquakes should be rarer than
the self-arresting earthquakes. They should also be larger overall because only barriers can stop them
and their sizes are thus determined by the likelihood that a barrier is present once an unstable rupture has been nucleated.

The classification into several classes of earthquakes has implications for the interpretation of many physical and statistical properties of earthquakes.
It remains to be seen how this novel understanding could be related to the origin of the two-branched Gutenberg-Richter distribution
Nandan et al. 2019

and the fact that large earthquakes (typically above magnitude 6 in California) may be preferentially triggered by large earthquakes
Nandan et al. 2022
.

In reviewing earthquake nucleation, we noted that the smooth nucleation model might only apply to unnaturally uniform faults.
Drawing parallels with heterogeneous nucleation theory, our theory can
in principle be extended to heterogeneous faults, by considering that the friction work
F_{f}
, akin to creating interfaces in nucleation,
could vary greatly within irregular faults.
