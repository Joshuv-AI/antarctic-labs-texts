Analytic thin wall false vacuum decay rate arXiv: 2202.04498
Aleksandar Ivanov, Marco Matteini, Miha Nemevšek, Lorenzo Ubaldi

Abstract
We derive a closed-form false vacuum decay rate at one loop in the thin wall limit, where the true and false vacua are nearly degenerate. We obtain the bounce configuration in D D dimensions, together with the Euclidean action with a higher order correction, counter-terms and renormalization group running. We extract the functional determinant via the Gel’fand-Yaglom theorem for low and generic orbital multipoles. The negative and zero eigenvalues appear for low multipoles and the translational zeroes are removed. We compute the fluctuations for generic multipoles, multiply and regulate the orbital modes. We find an explicit finite renormalized decay rate in D = 3 , 4 D=3,4 and give a closed-form expression for the finite functional determinant in any dimension.

Introduction

Local ground states in physical systems, described by a field theory, may not be stable.
A deeper minimum may exist, or appear with varying temperature, and quantum/thermal tunneling can trigger
a first-order phase transition.
Such a transition takes the system from the metastable false vacuum (FV) to a deeper minimum,
called the true vacuum (TV).
It is initiated by a sudden appearance of a bubble of TV upon the homogenous configuration of FV
and the probability of such events is given by the FV decay rate.

The theory of bubble nucleation was pioneered by the works of Langer
Langer:1967ax
,
and applied to field theory by Kobzarev et al.
Kobzarev:1974cp

Derivation of the rate was revisited in

Developing a theoretical understanding of bubble nucleation and subsequent expansion is important
for several reasons.
The stability of the potential in the Standard Model (SM) depends largely on the masses of the SM
Higgs and the top.
The bound on the Higgs mass was historically considered in

There has been tremendous progress on quantifying the SM rate, for example in recent

In most theories beyond the SM, the presence of new particles may affect the stability of the Higgs
vacuum or even alter the landscape of the vacua altogether.
From these, only the physical vacua need to be selected, which requires them to be either globally
stable or at least sufficiently long-lived when metastable.
Thus, a precise knowledge of the decay rate puts specific constraints on the parameter space of
such models.

The usual approach to the calculation of the bubble nucleation rate is based on the semi-classical saddle
point approximation in Euclidean spacetime.
This requires finding the bounce solution, which is an unstable

that extremizes the action and interpolates between the two minima.
An important simplification is that the bounce is proven to be

This proof was extended to multi-fields in

Currently, various ways of finding the bounce configuration are available.
The first solution was found already in the original work

so-called thin wall (TW) limit, where the two minima are nearly degenerate.
In such a setting, the size of the instanton becomes large, and therefore the transition region (or the wall
of the bounce) from FV to TV becomes narrow, hence thin wall.
This limit may be useful as an order of magnitude estimate, e.g. in thermal field theory when the nucleation
temperature is not far away from the critical one.
However, its applicability is limited and deteriorates

;
Sarid:1998sn
when minima

Once the bounce is found, one can include one quantum corrections
Callan:1977pt
, which
is a rather more involved calculation.
It includes fluctuations from all the fields that couple to the bounce and thus enter into the second
variation of the action, which ought to be evaluated in the presence of the bounce.
This is similar to the path integral in quantum mechanics
Feynman:1965
;
Kleinert:2004ev
, where
one computes the (semi)classical particle trajectory and then performs the path integral around it.
To compute the path integral in field theory, we have to find the eigenvalues of the fluctuation
operator, which is the second functional derivative of the action, and then integrate over the resulting
Gaussian coefficients.
We are thus left with a functional determinant
Callan:1977pt
, a product of all the eigenvalues
of the operator.
Because the bounce is
O(D)
symmetric, the fluctuations also come from a radially symmetric
operator, which can therefore be decomposed into orbital multipoles.
The product over all the multipoles is divergent and has to be properly regulated, e.g. with
dimensional regularization, where poles of
\Gamma
functions and the renormalization scale enter.
When the rate is written in terms of renormalized couplings, these artifacts go away, and one
is left with a finite result.
This much was stated already in the original work, however, an explicit solution is missing.

claiming a numerical discrepancy with

4.2
Renormalized bounce action

Because we are calculating a one loop quantity, we need to renormalize the bounce action,
which requires adding the action counter-terms and running the relevant couplings.
We follow the usual procedure with dimensional regularization, where one encounters

Four dimensions.

Counterterms.

\displaystyle=\langle\phi\rangle V^{(4)}\,.

Moving on to the tadpole, we remove the divergence with the quadratic counter-term

Other dimensions.

In the
\overline{\text{MS}}
scheme the poles of
\Gamma

In higher even dimensions, diagrams with more than two propagators become divergent, which means

In case there is a clear physical motivation to consider a particular dimension

However, the main focus of this work is on the functional determinant, therefore a general treatment of

5
Fluctuations

With the Euclidean action and the associated bounce at hand, we are ready to move on to the main
objective, the calculation of the fluctuations.
We wish to evaluate the ratio of determinants in (
4
)

\left|\frac{\det^{\prime}\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\left|\prod_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\det^{\prime}{\cal O}_{l}}{\det{\cal O}_{l{\rm FV}}}\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(25)

Exploiting the
O(D)
symmetry of the problem, we perform the angular separation of the radial variable
\rho
,
and expand the fluctuations in hyper-spherical multipoles
l
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\text{d}^{2}}{\text{d}\rho^{2}}-\frac{D-1}{\rho}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\rho}+\frac{l\left(l+D-2\right)}{\rho^{2}}+V^{(2)}\,,

(26)

where
V^{(2)}=\text{d}^{2}V/\text{d}\phi^{2}
is evaluated on the bounce.
The operator
{\cal O}_{l{\rm FV}}
is the same as
{\cal O}_{l}
, with
V^{(2)}
replaced by
V^{(2)}_{\rm FV}
.
The prime in
\det^{\prime}
means that the zero eigenvalues are removed.

The functional determinant
\det\mathcal{O}_{l}
is computed around the bounce configuration, i.e.

\phi=\overline{\phi}+\psi
, where
\overline{\phi}
is the bounce solution computed in the previous section
and
\psi
are the fluctuations around it.
To compute the determinant, one needs to define the basis for
\psi
and find the eigenvalues of the fluctuation
operator with fixed Dirichlet boundary conditions at
\rho=0
and
\infty
, and then multiply them.
However, instead of solving the entire eigensystem, we take advantage of the Gel’fand-Yaglom
theorem
Gelfand:1959nq
, according to which

\frac{\det{\cal O}_{l}}{\det{\cal O}_{l\rm{FV}}}=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\left(\frac{\psi_{l}(\rho)}{\psi_{l\rm{FV}}(\rho)}\right)^{d_{l}}\,,

(27)

where the degeneracy is given by
Kleinert:2004ev
;
Dunne:2006ct

\displaystyle d_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2l+D-2)(l+D-3)!}{l!(D-2)!}\,.

(28)

Setting
l=0
we get
d_{0}=1
regardless of
D
and thereby a single
s
-wave eigenmode, whose eigenvalue
should be negative
Coleman:1987rm
.
On the other hand, plugging
l=1
into (
28

d_{1}=D
and corresponds to translations
of the nucleated bubble in all possible
D
directions.
Since the bounce is
O(D)
symmetric and can nucleate anywhere, we expect to get
D
eigenmodes with
zero eigenvalues due to translational symmetry.
We will take care of them in section
5.2
.

The
\psi_{l}
and
\psi_{l{\rm FV}}
that enter in (
27
) are now solutions of

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{l}\,\psi_{l}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{l\text{FV}}\,\psi_{l\text{FV}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(29)

with the boundary conditions
\psi_{l}(\rho\sim 0)\sim\rho^{l}
,
\psi_{l\rm{FV}}(\rho\sim 0)\sim\rho^{l}
fixed
only at the origin.
The theorem allows to compute the ratio of the two determinants

\displaystyle R_{l}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\psi_{l}}{\psi_{l{\rm FV}}}\,,

and

\displaystyle\frac{\det{\cal O}_{l}}{\det{\cal O}_{l\rm{FV}}}

\displaystyle=R_{l}(\infty)^{d_{l}}\,,

(30)

without explicitly computing all the eigenvalues.
We can trade the
\psi_{l}
in (

R_{l}
(see Appendix
B
), and solve

\displaystyle\ddot{\psi}_{l{\rm FV}}+\frac{D-1}{\rho}\dot{\psi}_{l{\rm FV}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{l\left(l+D-2\right)}{\rho^{2}}+V_{\rm FV}^{(2)}\right)\psi_{l\rm{FV}}\,,

(31)

\displaystyle\ddot{R}_{l}+2\left(\frac{\dot{\psi}_{l\text{FV}}}{\psi_{l\text{FV}}}\right)\dot{R}_{l}

\displaystyle=\left(V^{(2)}-V^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\right)R_{l}\,,

(32)

with boundary conditions

\displaystyle\psi_{l\rm{FV}}(\rho\sim 0)

\displaystyle\sim\rho^{l}\,,

\displaystyle R_{l}(\rho=0)

\displaystyle=1\,,

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{l}(\rho=0)

\displaystyle=0\,.

(33)

The main advantage of analyzing
R_{l}
directly

is that it is bounded on the entire
\rho
interval,
because
V^{(2)}-V^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\xrightarrow{\rho\to\infty}0
.
This was not the case for
\psi_{l}
and
\psi_{l\text{FV}}

, even

Once we solve (

\displaystyle R_{l}(\infty)=\begin{cases}<0,&l=0\,,\\
0,&l=1\,,\\
1,&l\gg 1\,.\end{cases}

(34)

The first two come from the instability of the bounce and translational invariance.
We will calculate the spectrum in this low multipole/IR limit in the upcoming section
5.1

and then show how to remove the zeroes from the determinant in

.
The last limit in (

In this UV limit, the orbital term dominates over the rest, and the

Therefore, there is no significant difference between

The behavior of

5.2
Zero removal

The

that enters (
25

In the Gel’fand-Yaglom approach, we cannot simply handpick and remove any individual eigenvalue,

Instead, we subtract the zeroes perturbatively

\displaystyle R^{\varepsilon}_{1}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\infty)}{\psi^{\text{FV}}_{1}(\infty)}\simeq\frac{\left(\mu^{2}_{\varepsilon}+\gamma_{1}\right)\prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\gamma_{\textbf{n}}}{\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{\text{FV}}_{\textbf{n}}}=\mu^{2}_{\varepsilon}R^{\prime}_{1}(\infty)\,.

corresponds to

D

.

In the TW power counting scheme, the

multipoles enter the iterative expansion already at the leading
order (instead of
\Delta^{2}
as in section
5.1
).
The leading order FV solution is thus given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\nu\text{FV}}

\displaystyle\simeq e^{k_{\nu}z}\,,

\displaystyle k_{\nu}^{2}=1+\frac{\Delta^{2}\nu^{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}\,,

(57)

where the multipoles are characterized by
k_{\nu}
, which goes from 1 for low multipoles (in
agreement with the low multipole solution) and up to
\infty
.
Next, we consider the UV-valid equation for
R_{\nu}
at the leading order, written in terms of the
FV fluctuations

\frac{\text{d}^{2}}{\text{d}x^{2}}R_{\nu 0}+\frac{2k_{\nu}+1}{x}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}R_{\nu 0}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\rm FV}\right)R_{\nu 0}=0\,,

(58)

which is solved by

\displaystyle R_{\nu 0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(1+x\right)^{2}}\left(1+4\frac{k_{\nu}-1}{2k_{\nu}+1}x+\frac{\left(k_{\nu}-1\right)\left(2k_{\nu}-1\right)}{\left(k_{\nu}+1\right)\left(2k_{\nu}+1\right)}x^{2}\right)\,.

(59)

For low multipoles we have
k_{\nu}\to 1
, which reduces the above to
R_{\nu 0}\simeq 1/(1+x)^{2}
and
matches with (
43
).
On the other hand, at large
x
we have

\displaystyle R_{\nu 0}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(k_{\nu}-1\right)\left(2k_{\nu}-1\right)}{\left(k_{\nu}+1\right)\left(2k_{\nu}+1\right)}\,.

(60)

However, the leading term alone is not enough, one needs to proceed to all orders in
n
, to
recuperate and sum all the
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{0})
terms.
A systematic procedure of isolating all such terms is explained in detail in Appendix
B
.
While getting the individual terms is rather complicated, the final result after all the orders are summed
up is remarkably simple

U=3r_{0}\left(k_{\nu}-\sqrt{k_{\nu}^{2}-1}\right)\,.

(61)

Together with
R_{\nu 0}
in (
60
), one gets the complete leading-order result

\displaystyle R_{\nu}(\infty)

\displaystyle=R_{\nu 0}(\infty)e^{U}\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\ln R_{\nu}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\ln\frac{(k_{\nu}-1)(2k_{\nu}-1)}{(k_{\nu}+1)(2k_{\nu}+1)}+3r_{0}\left(k_{\nu}-\sqrt{k_{\nu}^{2}-1}\right)\,.

(63)

Before proceeding, let us comment on the salient features of this elegant result.

•

The final result in (
62
) is a
complete
expression for the product of eigenvalues
at a given multipole, which is non-zero at the leading order in
\Delta
.
This is in contrast to the low-
l
result in (
46
), where the first non-vanishing term appeared
at
{\cal O}(\Delta^{2})
.
If we expand (
62
) for low multipoles (
\nu\ll 1/\Delta
)
up to
{\cal O}(\Delta^{2})
, using the definition of
k_{\nu}
in (
57
),
we do not recover (
46
). This is not a surprise: the result (
62
)
is only valid at the leading order,
{\cal O}(\Delta^{0})
, does not include all the
{\cal O}(\Delta^{2})

corrections, so it cannot reproduce (
46
). That is why we needed a separate
calculation for the low multipoles.
Note when
k_{\nu}\to 1
one gets

U(k_{\nu}\to 1)=3r_{0}
, confirming the
e^{D-1}
factor which appears in (
46
),
and traces back to (
44
).
As we shall see in the following section, the large multipoles are the ones that dominate in the determinant,
and at the leading order those are fully accounted for by (
63
).

•

At large multipoles when
k_{\nu}\to\infty
, the determinant goes to unity, in agreement with the expectation
from (
34
).
Higher order corrections in
U
behave as
\sim 1/k_{\nu}
and vanish for large
k_{\nu}
, such that they do
not affect the correct behavior of
R_{\nu 0}
.
However, they do provide sub-leading powers of
k_{\nu}
, which are important in the sum we have to perform
to obtain the determinant, as we will see in the next section.

6
Renormalized determinant

In the previous section we computed the negative
l=0
eigenmodes and removed the zero modes with
l=1
,
now we wish to evaluate the infinite product in (
52
).
Taking the log and using Gel’fand-Yaglom (
27
), one has

\ln\left(\frac{\det\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)=\sum_{\nu=D/2-1}^{\infty}d_{\nu}\ln R_{\nu}\,,

(64)

where in this section we denote the asymptotic value
R_{\nu}(\infty)
with
R_{\nu}
, to ease the notation.
The sum in (
64
) formally starts from
l=0
, corresponding to
\nu=D/2-1
,
and goes to
\infty
, but as we shall shortly see, it
is dominated by large multipoles in the TW limit
7
7

7

The sum starting at
l=0
is in contrast with the product left in (
52
) starting at
l=2
.
However it is convenient to define the starting point at
l=0
here, in order to deal properly with the regularization,
as we will see shortly.

.
The low multipoles are suppressed by powers of
\Delta
and we can use the generic multipole fluctuations
from (
63
) to obtain the dominant contribution to the sum.

Let us examine the structure of divergences in the UV limit when
\nu\to\infty
.
The degeneracy factor in terms of
\nu
is given by

d_{\nu}=\frac{2\nu\left(\nu+\frac{D}{2}-2\right)!}{(D-2)!\left(\nu-\frac{D}{2}+1\right)!}\simeq\frac{2}{(D-2)!}\nu^{D-2}\,,

(65)

where the last approximation is valid in general
D
for large
\nu
, but is exact for any
\nu
in
D=2,3,4
.
Then we expand (
63
) up to including
{\cal O}(\nu^{-3})

\sum_{\nu\gg 1}d_{\nu}\ln R_{\nu\gg 1}\sim-\frac{3r_{0}(2-r_{0})}{(D-2)!\Delta}\,\sum_{\nu\gg 1}\nu^{D-2}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-\frac{1}{\nu^{3}}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{2\Delta}\right)^{2}\right)\,.

(66)

This sum clearly diverges and the number of divergent terms depends on the dimension
D
.
In
D=2,3
, only the first term diverges: it produces a log divergence in
D=2
and a linear one in
D=3
.
In
D=4
, the first term gives a quadratic divergence and the second a logarithmic one, and so on to
higher dimensions.

This is hardly surprising - the functional determinant is a one-loop quantity, which may diverge and
must be regularized.
To obtain a consistent finite result, we have to use the same regularization scheme that was adopted to compute
the renormalized bounce action in Section
4.2
, i.e. the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme.
That can be done either by computing the one-loop effective action using Feynman diagrams
Baacke:2003uw
,
or with the
\zeta
-function regularization scheme developed in
Dunne:2006ct
, which
was shown to be equivalent to
\overline{\rm MS}
8
8

8

As a further check that the two schemes are indeed equivalent we provide an explicit example in
Appendix
D
, where we use the bounce solution for a purely quartic potential
Andreassen:2017rzq
,
which has a simpler form compared to the TW bounce.
.
In the rest of this section we compute the renormalized determinant explicitly in three and four dimensions.
We begin with some specifications useful in generic dimensions.

Finite sum.

To perform the sum over the multipoles in (
64
) we use the Euler-Maclaurin (EuMac) approximation.
For later convenience it is useful to define
\Sigma_{D}
as the log of the determinant for generic
D
,
with individual terms in the sum
\sigma_{D}
given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=\nu_{0}}^{\infty}\,\sigma_{D}=\sum_{\nu=\nu_{0}}^{\infty}d_{\nu}\left(\ln R_{\nu}-\ln R_{\nu}^{a}\right)\,.

(67)

The lower boundary is given by
\nu_{0}=D/2-1

and the
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
term stands for the generic asymptotic subtraction, needed to regulate
the UV part (
\nu\to\infty
) and make the sum finite.
The EuMac formula approximates the sum as an integral,
\Sigma_{D}^{\int}
, plus corrections from the boundaries,

\Sigma_{D}^{\text{bnd}}
, which in our case consist of the summand
\sigma_{D}

and its derivatives
\sigma_{D}^{(j)}
evaluated at
\nu_{0}
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma_{D}^{\int}+\Sigma_{D}^{\text{bnd}}+R_{p}\,,

(68)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}^{\int}

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu_{0}}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\,\sigma_{D}\,,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}^{\text{bnd}}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{D}(\nu_{0})-\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor{\frac{p}{2}}\right\rfloor}\frac{B_{2j}}{(2j)!}\sigma_{D}^{(2j-1)}(\nu_{0})\,.

(69)

Here,
B_{2j}
are the Bernoulli numbers and
R_{p}
is the remainder, dependent on
p
, which is the
desired order of approximation.
In general
\Sigma_{D}^{\text{bnd}}
also contains
\sigma_{D}
evaluated at the upper boundary. Here, such
terms
\sigma_{D}^{(p\geq 0)}(\infty)
, vanish because the subtraction of the asymptotic part guarantees
that the sum is finite.

Three dimensions.

The renormalized functional determinant
Dunne:2005rt
;
Dunne:2006ct
requires the subtraction of
a single term

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\det\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}d_{\nu}\left(\ln R_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2\nu}I_{1}\right)\,.

(70)

Here
d_{\nu}=2\nu
.
The first term in the parenthesis is the ratio of determinants that we computed in (
63
).
The second term is the asymptotic subtraction
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
, which makes the sum finite.
Note we only have to remove terms proportional to
1/\nu
.
The
I_{1}
coefficient is given by
Dunne:2005rt
;
Dunne:2006ct

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\rho\,\rho\left(V^{(2)}-V^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\right)\simeq-3\left(2-r_{0}\right)\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)\,.

(71)

It is convenient to introduce
y=\Delta\nu/r_{0}
, which counts as
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{0})

and thus helps to clarify the overall
\Delta
dependence. The variable
k_{\nu}
, which enters
in
\ln R_{\nu}
, see (
63
), is related to
y
as
k_{\nu}=\sqrt{1+y^{2}}
.
Using (
71
),
r_{0}=2/3
, and comparing (
70
) to (
67
),
we get the asymptotic form
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}=-2/y
.
The integral piece of the EuMac approximation, after performing the change of variable
\nu\to y
, is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}^{\int}

\displaystyle\simeq 2\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{2}\int_{y_{0}}^{\infty}\text{d}y\,y\left(\ln R_{\nu}+\frac{2}{y}\right)=\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}}\frac{20+9\ln 3}{27}\,,

(72)

and does not depend on the precise value of the lower boundary of

y
, which can be extended to zero.
Here it becomes clearer why the low-multipole result (
46
) is not important when we sum
(integrate) over multipoles. Were we to use (
46
), instead of
R_{\nu}

from (
62
), in (
72
)
to integrate over low multipoles, we would get a contribution suppressed by
\Delta^{2}

compared to (
72
). That is subdominant, and we are only interested in the leading order terms
in this section. We learn that the integral in (
72
) is manifestly dominated by the high multipoles.

For the remaining terms in the EuMac, we need to evaluate
\sigma_{3}(\nu_{0})
by treating
\nu=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
expanding in small
\Delta
.
The leading term comes from the asymptotic part and goes as
\sigma_{3}\simeq 8/(3\Delta)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{0})
,
and is therefore suppressed compared to the leading
\Delta^{-2}
dependence in (
72
).
When taking higher derivatives over
\nu
, we get corrections that
are further suppressed by powers of
\Delta
;
the Bernoulli terms are thus irrelevant.

The final result for the determinant
is
\ln\left(\frac{\det\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)=\Sigma_{3}^{\int}
,
with
\Sigma_{3}^{\int}
given in (
72
). This enters directly in the final expression
for the decay rate (
2
).
Recall that we are working in the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme, so there are no counter-terms
and no running in
D=3
.

Thermal field theory.

At high temperatures, when the periodic boundary conditions go below the size of the instanton, the time
coordinate in 4D quantum field theory gets compactified and we end up with a 3D theory.
In such case
Linde:1980tt
;
Affleck:1980ac
(see also
Laine:2016hma
), the rate is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma}{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\frac{\lambda_{-}}{2\pi}\right)\left(\frac{S_{3}}{2\pi T}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}{\det^{\prime}\mathcal{O}}}\,e^{-S_{3}/T}\,,

(73)

where
(-\lambda_{-}^{2})
is the single negative eigenvalue of the 3D fluctuation operator.
The Euclidean action is exactly the one given as
S_{3}
in (
14
), but with the usual 4D counting
of dimensions.
Likewise, the determinant in (
73
) has to be calculated in
D=3
, precisely what we
got in (
72
) above, and is given by

\displaystyle\ln\sqrt{\frac{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}{\det^{\prime}\mathcal{O}}}\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{3}\,.

(74)

Four dimensions.

In this case the renormalized determinant needs the following subtractions
Dunne:2005rt
;
Dunne:2006ct
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ln\left(\frac{\det\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)&=\sum_{\nu}d_{\nu}\left(\ln R_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2\nu}I_{1}+\frac{1}{8\nu^{3}}I_{2}\right)-\frac{1}{8}\tilde{I}_{2}\,.\end{split}

(75)

Here we have
d_{\nu}=\nu^{2}
.
The
I_{1}
coefficient in (
71
) is also valid for
D=4
, while
I_{2}
and
\tilde{I}_{2}
are
obtained by inserting two powers of
V^{(2)}
. In the TW limit one has

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\rho\,\rho^{3}\left(V^{(2)2}-V^{(2)2}_{\text{FV}}\right)\simeq-3\left(2-r_{0}\right)\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{3}\,,

(76)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{I}_{2}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\rho\,\rho^{3}\left(V^{(2)2}-V^{(2)2}_{\text{FV}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\gamma_{E}+1+\ln\left(\frac{\mu\rho}{2}\right)\right)\\
&\simeq I_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\gamma_{E}+\frac{5}{4}+\ln\left(\frac{\mu}{2\sqrt{\lambda}v\Delta}\right)\right)\,.\qquad\qquad(D=4)\end{split}

(77)

We anticipate that the
I_{2}
will be valid for the asymptotic subtractions in higher
D
.
Note that the
\tilde{I}_{2}
part of the renormalized determinant is outside the sum, and in the last line we have
written the result valid for
D=4
.
It contains the
1/\varepsilon
pole and depends on the renormalization scale
\mu
.
The role of
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}=I_{1}/(2\nu)-I_{2}/(8\nu^{3})
, inside the sum, is to remove the divergent
parts of
\ln R_{\nu}
:
the
I_{1}
term regulates the quadratic divergence,
while
I_{2}
removes the logarithmic one.

Using
r_{0}=1
and the variable
y=\Delta\nu/r_{0}
, we have
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}=-3/(2y)+3/(8y^{3})

that enters in (
67
). The integral part of the EuMac approximation is then

\displaystyle\Sigma_{4}^{\int}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta^{3}}\int_{y_{0}}^{\infty}\text{d}y\,y^{2}\left(\ln R_{\nu}+\frac{3}{2y}-\frac{3}{8y^{3}}\right)=\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}\left(\frac{9-4\sqrt{3}\pi}{36}+\ln 2y_{0}\right)\,.

(78)

Here, the finite piece
\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}\left(\frac{9-4\sqrt{3}\pi}{36}\right)
comes from intermediate multipoles
with
\Delta\nu\sim 1
and behaves as
1/\Delta^{3}
.
As in the
D=3
case discussed above, the low-multipole contribution from (
46
)
would be suppressed, so we do not need to account for it in the integral.
On the other hand, we get a
\ln y_{0}
term that seems to depend on the low multipoles.
It comes solely from the term in the asymptotic subtraction
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}

which goes as
1/y^{3}
and integrates into
\ln y_{0}
,
due to lower boundary
y_{0}=\Delta\nu_{0}/r_{0}
in (
78
).
However, this is an artifact of the
\zeta
-regularization scheme.
As we shall see,
y_{0}
is just a regulator that will disappear from the final result.

Let us move on to the EuMac corrections given by
\Sigma_{D}^{\text{bnd}}
in (
68
).
Here the lower boundary is at
\nu_{0}\sim 1
, so we can expand the summands
\sigma_{D}
from (
67
)
in small
\Delta
and keep only the leading part, which is of order
\Delta^{-3}
.
The only terms that contribute come from the
3/8y^{3}
piece of
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
, and are of the form

\sigma_{4}^{(j)}(\nu_{0})=3(-)^{j+1}j!/(8\Delta^{3}\nu_{0}^{j+1})
, with
j\geq 0
. Recall that
the superscript
(j)
here denotes the
j
-th derivative with respect to
\nu
.
Since
\nu_{0}\sim 1
, when we plug
\sigma_{4}^{(j)}(\nu_{0})
into the EuMac approximation in (
68
),
the Bernoulli terms eventually grow large and the sum starts to diverge.

We can delay the onset of this divergence by raising the
\nu_{0}
in the Bernoulli terms.
To accomplish this, we split the original sum in (
67
) as follows

\Sigma_{D}=\Sigma_{D}^{\rm low}+\Sigma_{D}^{\rm high}=\sum_{\nu=\nu_{0}}^{\nu_{1}}\sigma_{D}+\sum_{\nu=\nu_{1}+1}^{\infty}\sigma_{D}\,.

(79)

Here the splitting point is such that
{\cal O}(1)=\nu_{0}\ll\nu_{1}<1/\Delta
.
To evaluate
\Sigma_{D}^{\rm high}
we use the EuMac formula and replace
\nu_{0}
in (
69
) with

\nu_{1}+1\simeq\nu_{1}
. Following the reasoning above, we now find that the EuMac boundary
corrections
\Sigma^{\rm bnd}
containing
\sigma_{4}^{(j)}(\nu_{1})
are formally suppressed, because
\nu_{1}\gg 1
,
so we can neglect them.
Then the high sum is well approximated by the integral (
78
), with
y_{0}
replaced by

y_{1}=\Delta\nu_{1}/r_{0}
, such that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{4}^{\rm high}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}\left(\frac{9-4\sqrt{3}\pi}{36}+\ln 2y_{1}\right)\,.

(80)

The low sum
\Sigma_{4}^{\rm low}
can be performed explicitly. In the range up to
\nu_{1}<1/\Delta

the leading piece in the summand
\sigma_{4}
comes from the
3/(8y^{3})
term in
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
, that
is
3/(8\Delta^{3}\nu)
once multiplied by
d_{\nu}=\nu^{2}
.
Compared to it, all the other terms in
\sigma_{4}

are suppressed by powers of
\Delta
, so we neglect them. Then
\Sigma_{4}^{\rm low}

is given by the Harmonic number

H_{\nu_{1}}
, which can be expanded for
\nu_{1}\gg 1

\displaystyle\Sigma_{4}^{\text{low}}=-\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\nu_{1}}\frac{1}{\nu}=-\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}H_{\nu_{1}}\simeq-\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}\left(\ln\nu_{1}+\gamma_{E}\right)\,.

(81)

The low-multipole sum of the fluctuations from (
46
) still do not play any role.
They would give terms suppressed by
\Delta^{2}
compared to (
81
), which we neglect.
Adding up (
81
) and (
80
), we get

\displaystyle\Sigma_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{8\Delta^{3}}\left(\frac{9-4\sqrt{3}\pi}{36}-\gamma_{E}+\ln 2\Delta\right)\,.

(82)

It is reassuring that the dependence on the arbitrary point of separation
\nu_{1}
is gone.

Renormalized rate.

To complete the calculation of the decay rate, we need to sum up the renormalized functional
determinant, consisting of the finite sum
\Sigma_{2}
above and the
\tilde{I}_{2}
term in (
77
),

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\det\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)=\Sigma_{4}-\frac{\tilde{I}_{2}}{8}\,,

(83)

with the renormalized Euclidean action
S_{R}+S_{\rm ct}
in (
24
), that gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ln\frac{\Gamma}{\mathcal{V}}&\ni-S_{R}-S_{\rm ct}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma_{4}-\frac{\tilde{I}_{2}}{8}\right)=-S-\frac{1}{\Delta^{3}}\frac{27-2\pi\sqrt{3}}{96}\,.\end{split}

(84)

The action
S
is given in (
15
) in terms of renormalized couplings.
The finite expression (
84
) enters in the final result of (
2
).
Both, the
1/\varepsilon
pole and the
\ln\mu
from the running of
\lambda
have canceled out.
The remaining
\mu_{0}\simeq\sqrt{\lambda}v
in (
24
) cancels with
\sqrt{\lambda}v
in the
\ln

part of (
77
), and the combination
-\gamma_{E}+\ln 2\Delta
in (
82
) also
cancels against the analogous terms in (
77
).

The procedure of obtaining a finite functional determinant can be generalized to any even or odd dimension

7
Conclusions and outlook

In this work we presented a dimensionally unified treatment of the false vacuum decay for a
single real scalar in the benchmark TW limit.
We started by reviewing the bounce solution, which is valid for any
D
, including the second order
correction in the expansion parameter
\Delta
, as well as the counter-terms and RGE running.
Employing the Gel’fand-Yaglom theorem, we found the orbital multipoles of the functional
determinant around the bounce in two regions.
The first region was the low multipole one, where the negative eigenvalues and the translational
zeroes appear.
The zeroes are removed perturbatively and that is how we get the correct physical dimension
of the rate.
The second region is computed by modifying the
\Delta
counting such that
\Delta l=\mathcal{O}(1)

and is therefore valid for higher multipoles, going up to infinity.

The renormalization section shows how the infinities in the orbital summation get regulated
in the TW limit.
In particular, we review how the subtractions in inverse powers of multipoles,

1/\varepsilon
poles and the
\mu

dependence are derived and evaluated in the TW limit, which gives us the asymptotic
part of the determinant that subtracts the leading divergencies.
Finally, we show how the multipoles are added up using the Euler-Maclaurin approximation.

It turns out that the summations are somewhat different for even and odd dimensions.
In odd dimensions, the dominant contribution to the determinant comes from higher multipoles
and no renormalization scale dependence appears.
In even dimensions, the single term from the asymptotic subtraction brings in a dependence
on low multipoles, which has to be treated carefully.
We resolve this issue by splitting the sum into two parts and deriving a consistent result for lower
and larger multipoles, while showing that the exact point of separation does not enter in the final
result.

A natural extension of this work would be to include additional degrees of freedom,
such as a globally symmetric potential with additional scalars, would-be-Goldstones, gauge
bosons and fermions
wip:2022
, analytically in the TW limit.
This would require a somewhat general approach to renormalization, similar to the simplification
that was done for the running of couplings
Jones:1981we
.
It may also be desirable to develop a framework for evaluating the total rate at one loop for
general potentials.
Such a formalism may be developed from a semi-analytical polygonal bounce
Guada:2018jek

setup, where the shape of the bounce is given explicitly on each segment.
The upshot of this construction is that it applies to a broad class of models and matches
exactly to the known Euclidean action in the TW limit.
Most significantly, the bounce part was already suited for multi-fields, which would make it
a good starting point for developing the theory of one-loop fluctuations for generic BSM setups.

\displaystyle+12r_{0}x-36r_{0}x\ln(x+1)-12r_{0}\ln(x+1)

\displaystyle-4\nu^{2}x^{6}+x^{6}-24\nu^{2}x^{5}+6x^{5}-36\nu^{2}x^{4}+9x^{4}-16\nu^{2}x^{3}+4x^{3}\bigg)\,.

(199)

This function has the following asymptotic behavior:

\displaystyle f_{l2}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to 0]{}\frac{8\nu^{2}-18r_{0}\ln^{2}(x)+6r_{0}\left(6r_{0}+5\right)\ln(x)+18r_{0}^{2}-\left(55+6\pi^{2}\right)r_{0}-2}{12r_{0}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle f_{l2}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}-\frac{x\left(-4\nu^{2}+9r_{0}^{2}+6r_{0}+1\right)}{24r_{0}^{2}}\,.

(201)

Note that

diverges linearly at large

.

Next, we compute
\ln R_{ln}
, which from the definition (

) is simply given by

\ln R_{ln}(x)=\int_{e^{-r}}^{x}{\rm d}tf_{ln}(t)\,.

Here, the lower limit of integration is to enforce the boundary condition
R_{l}(x=e^{-r})=1
.
At

, using (

\displaystyle\ln R_{l1}(x)

\displaystyle=3\left(\ln(x)+r\right)\,,

(203)

\displaystyle R_{l1}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{3r_{0}}{\Delta}}x^{3}\,,

\displaystyle R_{l1}^{\Delta}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{3r_{0}}x^{3\Delta}\,,

(204)

where we have used

.
At
n=2
we have

\ln R_{l2}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}{\rm d}tf_{l2}(t)\,.

(205)

Here we can set the lower limit of integration to zero.
Doing so, given the asymptotic behavior (
200
), we are throwing away terms suppressed by
e^{-r}
.
The integral (
205
) can be computed analytically plugging in (
199
).
However, we only need its asymptotic behavior at large
x
, which is easily obtained by integrating (

\ln R_{l2}(x\to\infty)=\int^{x}{\rm d}tf_{l2}(t\to\infty)=-\frac{x^{2}\left(-4\nu^{2}+9r_{0}^{2}+6r_{0}+1\right)}{48r_{0}^{2}}\,.

(206)

We can now put together our low
l
result for
R_{l}
up to
\Delta^{2}
.
Starting from (
179
) and (
180
), and using (
204
), we can factor out
e^{3r_{0}}
and write

R_{l}(x)=e^{3r_{0}}R_{l0}(x)\left(1+3\Delta\ln(x)+\Delta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}9\ln^{2}(x)+\ln R_{l2}(x)\right)+{\cal O}(\Delta^{3})\right)\,.

We are interested in the
x\to\infty
limit.
From (
184
) we have
R_{l0}(x\to\infty)=x^{-2}
, and using (
206
) we obtain

R_{l}(x\to\infty)=-\Delta^{2}e^{3r_{0}}\frac{-4\nu^{2}+9r_{0}^{2}+6r_{0}+1}{48r_{0}^{2}}+{\cal O}(\Delta^{3})\,.

(208)

Substituting
\nu=l+D/2-1
and
r_{0}=(D-1)/3
we can rewrite it as

\lim_{\rho\to\infty}R_{l}(\rho)=R_{l}(x\to\infty)=\Delta^{2}\frac{3}{4}e^{D-1}\frac{(l-1)(l+D-1)}{(D-1)^{2}}+{\cal O}(\Delta^{3})\,,

(209)

which corresponds to (
46
).
Some comments are in order.

•

The leading term in this low
l
result is of order
\Delta^{2}
, with the
{\cal O}(\Delta^{0})
and
{\cal O}(\Delta)

terms vanishing.
It has the structure we expected: it is negative for
l=0
, it vanishes for
l=1
.
The latter signals the zero eigenvalues related to the translational invariance of the bounce.
We will soon go through a procedure to remove them.

•

The
e^{3r_{0}}
is an overall factor in (
207
) and as such it ends up contributing to the first term, that is zeroth
order in the
\Delta
expansion.
Note we got
e^{3r_{0}}
at
n=1
[see (
203
) and (
204
)], rather than
n=0
, as a consequence
of the boundary condition at
x=e^{-r}
(corresponding to
\rho=0
).
In other words, we set up our equations based on the
\Delta^{n}
power counting, but then found a solution at
n=1

that fed back into
n=0
.
Let us refer to it as an order-breaking term.
While this feature might be puzzling at first sight, there is no reason to panic.
The calculation is sensible and the procedure well defined:
at each order one computes
f_{ln}

from (
192
), and then checks from (
202
) if there are contributions to
\ln R_{ln}

from the boundary at
x=e^{-r}
, with
r=r_{0}/\Delta
, that feed back into lower orders.
In the low
l
calculation this happens only at
n=1
.
In the next section, we will study the problem systematically for generic values of
l
(or, better, of
\nu
) and show
that order-breaking terms will appear for every
n\geq 1
, but that indeed at low
l
only the one from
n=1
survives.

•

There is at least another way of obtaining the
e^{3r_{0}}
factor.
Instead of using (
179
), one can approach the problem with an additive expansion,

R_{l}=R_{l0}+\Delta R_{l1}+\Delta^{2}R_{l2}+\cdots
, and solve the second order differential equation for
R_{l}

order by order.
The calculation is more involved, compared to the one we have presented here, and one finds at each order
boundary terms of the form
\Delta^{n}r^{n}
, which therefore feed back into
n=0
.
Summing all such terms, up to
n=\infty
, one recovers exactly the exponential
e^{3r_{0}}
.

Zero removal at low multipoles.

With the explicit solution valid up to second-order corrections in
\Delta^{2}
, one can proceed to
perturbatively remove the zero eigenvalue, as discussed in section
5.2
.
The orbital mode dependence comes in at
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})
.
To remove the
l=1
modes it is then sufficient to off-set the potential at this order with an
infinitesimal dimensional parameter

\displaystyle V^{(2)}\to V^{(2)}+\Delta^{2}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,,\quad\text{ or }\quad\tilde{V}^{(2)}\to\tilde{V}^{(2)}+\frac{\Delta^{2}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\,.

(210)

This offset does not affect the zero and first-order solutions, once
\mu_{\varepsilon}\to 0
,
thus
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
remain the same.
On the other hand, the second-order solution with the above off-set is easily obtained starting from (
192
),

\displaystyle f_{l2}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+x)^{4}}{x^{3}}\int_{e^{-r}}^{x}{\rm d}t\frac{t^{3}}{(1+t)^{4}}\left(P_{f_{l2}}(t)+\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right)

(211)

\displaystyle=f_{l2}+\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\frac{(1+x)^{4}}{x^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}t\frac{t}{(1+t)^{4}}\,.

(212)

\displaystyle=f_{l2}+\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\frac{(1+x)^{4}}{6x^{3}}\,.

(213)

From here, we plug the
f_{l2}^{\varepsilon}(x)
into (
202
), such that

\displaystyle\ln R_{21}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int^{x}\text{d}t\,f_{l2}^{\varepsilon}(t)

(214)

\displaystyle=\int^{x}\text{d}t\left(f_{l2}+\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\frac{(1+t)^{4}}{6t^{3}}\right)

(215)

\displaystyle=\ln R_{21}+\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\frac{x^{2}}{12}=\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda v^{2}}\frac{x^{2}}{12}\,,

(216)

which is precisely what was given in (
50
).

Perturbative solutions for generic multipoles.

In the previous section we computed
\lim_{x\to\infty}R_{\nu}(x)
, but our result was only valid for small
values of
\nu
.
In this section we are going to revisit our calculation, generalizing it to generic values of
\nu
. The starting point
is to consider the combination
\Delta\nu
as on order one parameter instead of a
\Delta
suppressed one.
Then the
\psi_{\nu{\rm FV}}
equation, see (
38
), has the leading order solution

\psi_{\nu{\rm FV}}^{\rm lead}=c_{\rm FV}e^{k_{\nu}z}\,,

(217)

with

k_{\nu}=\sqrt{1+\frac{\Delta^{2}\nu^{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}}\,,

(218)

which counts as an
{\cal O}(\Delta^{0})
parameter. We define

\frac{1}{\psi_{\nu{\rm FV}}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\psi_{\nu{\rm FV}}\equiv g_{\nu}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Delta^{n}g_{\nu n}\,.

(219)

Given (
217
), we immediately have that
g_{\nu 0}=k_{\nu}
.
Note, as a rather trivial check, that using (
218
), expanding in
\Delta
and keeping only the zeroth order,
that is

, we match the low
l

g_{\nu 0}(k_{\nu}=1)=g_{l0}=1
.
With (
219
) the differential FV equation (
36
) turns into

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}g_{\nu}+g_{\nu}^{2}=\frac{\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{(z+r)^{2}}+\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\,.

(220)

With the counting stated above the right hand side has the expansion

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{(z+r)^{2}}+\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}

\displaystyle=k_{\nu}^{2}+\left(-3-2(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)\frac{z}{r_{0}}\right)\Delta+\left(-3-\frac{1}{4r_{0}^{2}}+3(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)\frac{z^{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}\right)\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{15}{2}+\frac{z}{2r_{0}^{3}}-4(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)\frac{z^{3}}{r_{0}^{3}}\right)\Delta^{3}+{\cal O}(\Delta^{4})\,.

(221)

Using this and pugging (
219
) into (
220
), we get the
n=1
equation

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}g_{\nu 1}+2k_{\nu}g_{\nu 1}=-3-2(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)\frac{z}{r_{0}}\,,

(222)

which has the solution

g_{\nu 1}=\frac{-2(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)}{2k_{\nu}}\frac{z}{r_{0}}+\frac{k_{\nu}^{2}-1-3k_{\nu}r_{0}}{2k_{\nu}^{2}r_{0}}\,.

(223)

Note the first term is linear in
z
, while the second is
z
independent.
With this we can solve (
220
) at
n=2
, where we find

g_{\nu 2}=\frac{1}{2k_{\nu}}\left(3(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)-4(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)^{2}\frac{1}{(2k_{\nu})^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{z}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}+{\rm terms\ with\ lower\ powers\ of}\ z\,.

(224)

Here we don’t bother writing explicitly the terms with lower powers of
z

because they are not needed for the rest of the calculation,
as we are going to explain.
Let us keep the higher orders
g_{\nu n}
on hold for a moment, and turn to
R_{\nu}
.

The
R_{\nu}
equation to solve for generic multipoles,

\frac{1}{R_{\nu}}\frac{\text{d}^{2}}{\text{d}x^{2}}R_{\nu}+\frac{2g_{\nu}+1}{x}\frac{1}{R_{\nu}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}R_{\nu}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\rm FV}\right)=0\,,

(225)

reads the same as (
177
), but
g_{l}
from (
174
) is replaced with
g_{\nu}
from (
219
).
At order zero we have

\frac{1}{R_{\nu 0}}\frac{\text{d}^{2}}{\text{d}x^{2}}R_{\nu 0}+\frac{2k_{\nu}+1}{x}\frac{1}{R_{\nu 0}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}R_{\nu 0}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\rm FV}\right)_{0}=0\,,

(226)

with the last term given by (
183
). The solution is

R_{\nu 0}(x)=\frac{1-4x+x^{2}+2k_{\nu}^{2}(1+x)^{2}+3k_{\nu}(1-x^{2})}{(1+k_{\nu})(1+2k_{\nu})(1+x)^{2}}\,.

(227)

This matches the boundary condition
R_{\nu 0}(x\to 0)=1
, reduces to (
184
) for
k_{\nu}=1
, and has
the asymptotic behavior

R_{\nu 0}(\infty)=\frac{(1-k_{\nu})(1-2k_{\nu})}{(1+k_{\nu})(1+2k_{\nu})}\,.

(228)

Then we introduce

f_{\nu}\equiv\frac{1}{R_{\nu}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}R_{\nu}\,,

(229)

in terms of which (
225
) becomes

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}f_{\nu}+f_{\nu}^{2}+\frac{2g_{\nu}+1}{x}f_{\nu}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\rm FV}\right)=0\,,

(230)

with the boundary condition

e^{-r}f_{\nu}(e^{-r})=0\,.

(231)

We expand

f_{\nu}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Delta^{n}f_{\nu n}\,,

(232)

and
g_{\nu}
with (
219
), then solve (
230
) order by order in
\Delta
.
The

can be immediately obtained from (

f_{\nu 0}(x)=\frac{-6(1+k_{\nu}-x+k_{\nu}x)}{(1+x)\left(1-4x+x^{2}+2k_{\nu}^{2}(1+x)^{2}+3k_{\nu}(1-x^{2})\right)}\,.

(233)

For order

, from (

) we get

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}x}f_{\nu n}+\left(2f_{\nu 0}+\frac{2k_{\nu}+1}{x}\right)f_{\nu n}=P_{f_{\nu n}}\,,

(234)

with

P_{f_{\nu n}}=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\rm FV}\right)_{n}-\frac{2}{x}\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{\nu j}\ f_{\nu(n-j)}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}f_{\nu j}\ f_{\nu(n-j)}\,.

Here the last sum contributes only for

The solution to (

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{\nu n}(x)=&\frac{x^{-(2k_{\nu}+1)}(1+x)^{4}}{\left(1-4x+x^{2}+2k_{\nu}^{2}(1+x)^{2}+3k_{\nu}(1-x^{2})\right)^{2}}\,\\
&\int_{e^{-r}}^{x}{\rm d}t\frac{t^{2k_{\nu}+1}}{(1+t)^{4}}\left(1-4t+t^{2}+2k_{\nu}^{2}(1+t)^{2}+3k_{\nu}(1-t^{2})\right)^{2}P_{f_{\nu n}}(t)\,,\end{split}

and from the definition (

\ln R_{\nu n}(x)=\int_{e^{-r}}^{x}{\rm d}t\ f_{\nu n}(t)\,.

(237)

Analogously to the low

dropping only terms exponentially suppressed in

in doing so.
On the contrary, in (
237

as the lower limit of integration, which implements
the boundary condition
R_{\nu}(\rho=0)=R_{\nu}(x=e^{-r})=1

R_{\nu 0}(x=e^{-r}\to 0)=1

\ln R_{\nu n}(x=e^{-r})=0

Formally we have the recipe to compute
\ln R_{\nu n}
at any

\displaystyle R_{\nu}

\displaystyle=R_{\nu 0}R_{\nu 1}^{\Delta}R_{\nu 2}^{\Delta^{2}}R_{\nu 3}^{\Delta^{3}}\cdots

\displaystyle=R_{\nu 0}\left(1+\Delta\ln R_{\nu 1}+\Delta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln^{2}R_{\nu 1}+\ln R_{\nu 2}\right)+{\cal O}(\Delta^{3})\right)\,,

(239)

up to the desired order in
\Delta

In practice, we can solve the integral in (

n=1
, the result containing hypergeometric
functions, but already fail when trying to integrate it to compute the full form of
\ln R_{\nu 1}
.
We have already found that

in (

in the low-

. Thus, it seems like we already have
the leading order term in the expansion (

In the low-
l

that fed back into
n=0
.
It came from the lower boundary of integration of
\ln R_{l1}
. We should then check if we also get

To do so, we must study the lower boundary of the integral (
237
), for which
it is sufficient to know the
x\to 0
limit of
f_{\nu n}
. That is obtained from (

f_{\nu n}(x\to 0)=x^{-(2k_{\nu}+1)}\int_{0}^{x}{\rm d}t\ t^{2k_{\nu}+1}P_{f_{\nu n}}(t\to 0)\,.

At

P_{f_{\nu 1}}=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\right)_{1}-\frac{2}{x}g_{\nu 1}\ f_{\nu 0}\,,

with [see (

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\right)_{1}=\frac{6}{1+x}

\displaystyle g_{\nu 1}(x)=-2\frac{k_{\nu}^{2}-1}{2k_{\nu}}\frac{\ln(x)}{r_{0}}\,,

(242)

and
f_{\nu 0}
given in (

). Here we have kept only the highest power of

in

.
In the limit

:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\tilde{V}^{(2)}-\tilde{V}^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}\right)_{1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to 0]{}\frac{6}{x^{2}}\,,

(243)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{x}g_{\nu 1}\ f_{\nu 0}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to 0]{}2\frac{\ln(x)}{x}\left(-2\frac{k_{\nu}^{2}-1}{2k_{\nu}}\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{-6}{1+2k_{\nu}}\right)\,.

From the first term, (

\displaystyle P_{f_{\nu 1}}(x\to 0)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{x^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle f_{\nu 1}(x\to 0)

\Delta\ln R_{\nu 1}\ni\Delta\int_{e^{-r}}{\rm d}x\ f_{\nu 1}(x\to 0)=3r_{0}\frac{1}{k_{\nu}}\equiv U_{1}\,.

This is an order-breaking term which feeds back into the

Note that

It is easy to check that the other term, (

)

At

\displaystyle\frac{2}{x}g_{\nu 1}\ f_{\nu 1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to 0]{}\ 2\cdot\frac{-2(k_{\nu}^{2}-1)}{2k_{\nu}}\frac{1}{r_{0}}\frac{3}{k_{\nu}}\frac{\ln(x)}{x^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{x}g_{\nu 2}\ f_{\nu 0}

\displaystyle f_{\nu 1}^{2}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to 0]{}\ \frac{9}{k_{\nu}^{2}x^{2}}\,.

At

.
Here we have used (

Appendix D
Quartic functional determinant

We discuss the calculation of the functional determinant of an (unstable) quartic potential, specified
by
V=\lambda/4\phi^{4}
, where
\lambda<0
.
It can be computed either by use of Feynman diagrams or via the
\zeta
/WKB formalism.
The bounce is given by
\phi=\sqrt{8/(-\lambda)}R/(R^{2}+\rho^{2})
and the ratio of multipoles
R_{l}
can be
calculated using Gel’fand-Yaglom
Isidori:2001bm
;
Andreassen:2017rzq
;
Guada:2020ihz
with
R_{l}=l(l-1)/(l+2)/(l+3)
.
To compute the determinant, we have to regulate the sum of
d_{l}\ln R_{l}
, such that we first subtract the divergent
growth at large
l
and then renormalize it.

The Feynman diagrammatic approach
Isidori:2001bm
;
Andreassen:2017rzq
specifies the subtraction of
\ln R_{l}

in powers of
V^{(2)}-V^{(2)}_{\text{FV}}
insertions.
The renormalization in
D=4
is performed by calculating the Feynman diagrams with one and two insertions
in momentum space, using the
\overline{\text{MS}}
scheme.
After performing the finite sum and adding back the renormalized determinant, one gets

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\det^{\prime}\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)=\frac{3}{\epsilon}-\frac{5}{4}\left(1-2\ln\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)\right)+3\ln\left(\frac{\mu R}{2}\right)+6\zeta^{\prime}(-1)\,,

(326)

as stated e.g. in Eq. (5.6) of
Andreassen:2017rzq
(here we translated to our convention
\varepsilon=4-D
).

According to the
\zeta
/WKB prescription, we use (
75
) with the UV integrals coming from integrating

V^{(2)}=3\lambda\phi^{2}
using the quartic bounce given above, such that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=-12\,,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-48\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=48\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+1+\gamma_{E}+\ln\left(\frac{\mu R}{2}\right)\right)\,.

(327)

The finite sum is performed by starting from
l=2
(or
\nu=3
)

\displaystyle S_{\text{fin}}=\sum_{\nu=3}^{\infty}\nu^{2}\left(\ln R_{l}+\frac{6}{\nu}+\frac{6}{\nu^{3}}\right)=-\frac{37}{2}+9\ln 2+5\ln 3-12\zeta^{\prime}(-1)\,.

(328)

To complete the calculation, we have to add the
l=0
and
l=1
terms.
This includes the
\ln R_{l}
with zeroes removed, given by
R^{\prime}_{0}=-1/5
and
R^{\prime}_{1}=1/10
(see e.g (4.37)
of
Andreassen:2017rzq
), as well as the asymptotic subtractions, such that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\det^{\prime}\mathcal{O}}{\det\mathcal{O}_{\text{FV}}}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln|R^{\prime}_{0}|+4\ln R^{\prime}_{1}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{2}\left(6\nu+\frac{6}{\nu}\right)+S_{\text{fin}}-\frac{1}{8}\tilde{I}_{2}\right)\,.

(329)

Although the intermediate steps in these two approaches differ significantly, the final result precisely agrees
with (
326
).
This further confirms that a single integration over
\rho
in (
75
) is equivalent to using
Feynman diagrams in the
\overline{\text{MS}}
scheme.

Appendix E
Finite determinants in generic dimensions

The procedure that we discussed for the specific
D=3,4
above, deriving the asymptotics, subtracting
the infinities and applying the EuMac approximation, can be generalized for generic
D
.
We first focus on the asymptotic behavior of the determinant that needs to be subtracted to regulate the
sum, then on the sums in generic
D
.
We treat odd and even dimensions in separate sub-sections, starting with the easier odd ones.
Some parts of the summation are valid for any
D
, so we will keep the discussion general for as long
as possible and reduce to a specific choice when this becomes unavoidable.

Asymptotics of determinants.

We will derive the asymptotic behavior of the determinant for high multipoles in inverse powers of
\nu
.
To compute the finite sum in (
67
), we need to obtain
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
that regulates the
infinites with a minimal number of
\nu^{-j}
terms.
The order depends on the dimension, because the degeneracy factor at high
\nu
scales as

d_{\nu}\propto\nu^{D-2}
.
We thus have to expand to high enough powers of
\nu^{-j}
, to get to the lowest
one, which is
d_{\nu}\ln R_{\nu}\sim\nu^{-1}
.
This defines the notion of minimal subtraction.

To keep track of the calculation, we will separate the asymptotic into two pieces: the logarithmic
one
\ln R_{\nu 0}^{a}
, and the
U
factor, such that
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}=\ln R_{\nu 0}^{a}+U^{a}
.
In what follows we will be evaluating the integrals by performing a large
k_{\nu}
expansion of

\ln R_{\nu}
, integrating the pieces of the sums and summing them back up.
For this purpose, it is sensible to first expand in large
k_{\nu}
, which is by definition larger than
one, thus the sums with
k_{\nu}^{-j}
converge, and then further expand in powers
of
\nu^{-j}
or better
y^{-j}
.
The last step brings us to the well defined
1/\nu
expansions, which we get during the process
of renormalization.

Let us start with the logarithmic piece and rewrite the log of (
60
) as:

\displaystyle\ln R_{\nu 0}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+2^{-2j+1}}{2j-1}k_{\nu}^{-2j+1}\,,

(330)

where the powers of
k_{\nu}
are going down by factors of 2.
Further expanding the powers of
k_{\nu}
in various roots of large
y\gg 1
, using

\displaystyle k_{\nu}^{-2j+1}=y^{-2j+1}\left(1+y^{-2}\right)^{-j+\frac{1}{2}}=y^{-2j+1}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{-j+\frac{1}{2}}{p}y^{-2p}\,,

(331)

we then express it as a double infinite sum

\displaystyle\ln R_{\nu 0}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+2^{-2j+1}}{2j-1}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{-j+\frac{1}{2}}{p}y^{-2(j+p)+1}\,.

(332)

As discussed above, the powers in the asymptotic functions have to stop when we reach
\nu^{-1}
.
To derive the upper bound in the sum, note that the degeneracy factor goes as
d_{\nu}\propto y^{D-2}

in the UV and we need to remove all the negative
y
powers, including the highest one with
y^{-1}
.
Comparing the powers leads to the maximal one at
j=m
, for both odd and even dimensions,
where
D=2m+1
for the odd ones and
D=2m
for the even, thus

\displaystyle\ln R_{\nu 0}^{a}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1+2^{-2j+1}}{2j-1}\sum_{p=0}^{m-j}\binom{-j+\frac{1}{2}}{p}y^{-2(j+p)+1}\,.

(333)

We proceed along similar lines to obtain the asymptotics of the
U
factor

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=-3r_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{j}(-)^{j}k_{\nu}^{-2j+1}=-3r_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{j}(-)^{j}y^{-2j+1}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{-j+\frac{1}{2}}{p}y^{-2p}\,,

(334)

and truncating the series at the appropriate maximal power

\displaystyle U^{a}

\displaystyle=-3r_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{j}(-)^{j}y^{-2j+1}\sum_{p=0}^{m-j}\binom{-j+\frac{1}{2}}{p}y^{-2p}\,.

(335)

With these minimal subtractions in powers of
y
, the sum in (
67
) becomes convergent
and can be evaluated using the EuMac approximation in (
68
).
Plugging the appropriate boundaries in (
333
) and (
335
), we get precisely
the asymptotic functions
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
, which were calculated in
D=3,4
via the integrals

I_{1,2}
in (
71
) and (
76
).

From the generic behavior of the asymptotics, we already see that the odd and even
D
will
behave differently.
Because the degeneracy factor grows as
y^{D-2}
and the UV expansion of
\ln R_{\nu 0}+U
behave as

y^{-2j+1}
, we get
y^{-1}
terms only for even
D
.
After the summation in
\nu
, these become
\ln y
divergent terms.
Conversely, there are no log divergencies in odd dimensions, which makes them
generally easier to handle.

Generic odd
D

Let us generalize the summation procedure to generic
D
, beginning with an integral part of the

-factor in the EuMac approximation

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu_{0}}^{\infty}\text{d}\nu\,d_{\nu}\left(U-U^{a}\right)\,.

The dominant contribution to the integral comes from

In the

Using this approximation of

349
).
Perhaps surprisingly, the final expression comes out to be remarkably simple

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2m+1}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{2m+1}^{\int U}+\Sigma_{2m+1}^{\int\ln}

(362)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\frac{(-)^{m+1}}{m\binom{m-\frac{1}{2}}{m}}\left(\frac{1}{m}+\!\,_{2}F_{1}\left(1,\frac{1}{2}-m;\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{4}\right)-\frac{3}{2}r_{0}\right)\,,

(363)

Evaluating the hypergeometric function gives us the following numbers for a couple of low odd
D
s

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}=\frac{2}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\begin{cases}\frac{5}{6}+\frac{3}{8}\ln 3\,,&D=3\,,\\
-\frac{37}{240}-\frac{9}{64}\ln 3\,,&D=5\,,\\
-\frac{3}{224}+\frac{9}{128}\ln 3\,,&D=7\,.\end{cases}

(364)

Generic even
D
.

The main difference for generic even dimensions is the appearance of a log term on the lower boundary
and thus the importance of low multipoles in
\ln R_{\nu}^{a}
.
This translates into the fact that the EuMac sum over the Bernoulli numbers diverges if we apply the EuMac
approximation naïvely on the entire interval from
\nu_{0}=\mathcal{O}(1)
to
\infty
, as we explained
in section
6
.

Let us discuss how these issues appear and how to resolve them in general
D=2m
.
In even dimensions, the asymptotic parts will always contain a term that goes as
1/\nu
.
When we take higher derivatives in the last term of (
68
) to sum over the Bernoulli numbers,
the
1/\nu
will turn into a term that goes as
\nu^{-(p>1)}
.
If we plug in the lower boundary with
\nu=D/2-1
, the sum over the Bernoulli numbers will eventually diverge.

A way out of this impasse is the same as in
D=4
: split up the sum over
\nu
into two parts, the first
coming from
\nu_{0}=\mathcal{O}(1)
up to
\nu_{1}\lesssim r_{0}/\Delta
and the other from
\nu_{1}
to
\infty
.
As we shall see, the precise position of this separation is irrelevant, as long as
\nu_{1}\gg 1
.
Let us now consider the low part of the sum in more concrete detail.
Here we need to extract the leading powers of
\Delta
from the asymptotic terms
\ln R_{\nu 0}^{a}
and
U^{a}
,
which means we have to set
p=m-j
and perform the sum over
j
, just as in (
361
).
This gives us the most relevant low multipole terms, which can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma_{2m}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{d_{\nu}}{y^{2m-1}}C_{m}\,,

(365)

where the
\nu
-independent coefficient
C_{m}
is given by

\displaystyle C_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{2m}{m-\frac{1}{2}}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{m}\left(1+\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,_{2}F_{1}\left(1,1-m;\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{4}\right)\right)+3r_{0}\,\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{m}\,.

(366)

The sum over low multipoles gives

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2m}^{\text{low}U}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=m-1}^{\nu_{1}}\sigma_{2m}=\frac{2C_{m}}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\prod_{j=1}^{2m-3}\frac{\nu-m+1+j}{\nu^{2m-2}}\,.

(367)

We can further rework it into a product of pairs and ultimately into a sum

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{2m-3}\frac{\nu-m+1+j}{\nu^{2m-2}}=\frac{1}{\nu^{2m-3}}\prod_{j=1}^{m-2}\left(\nu^{2}-j^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m - 2}{j}(-)^{j}\nu^{-2j}\,,

(368)

where the curly binomial stands for the multiply nested sum

\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m-2}{j}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{m-2}\sum_{i_{2}=i_{1}+1}^{m-2}\ldots\sum_{i_{j}=i_{j-1}+1}^{m-2}\left(i_{1}\ldots i_{j}\right)^{2}\,.

(369)

This is just a convenient way to write the numerical coefficients, which are independent of
\nu
.
The finite sums of powers of
1/\nu^{2p+1}
, on the other hand, are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=m-1}^{\nu_{1}}\frac{1}{\nu^{n}}=\begin{cases}\ln\nu_{1}-\psi(m-1)\,,&n=1\,,\\
-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\psi^{(n-1)}(m-1)\,,&n\geq 1\,,\end{cases}

(370)

where
\psi^{(n)}
is the
n
-th derivative of the digamma function.
We thus end up with

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{2m}^{\text{low}}=&\frac{2C_{m}}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\biggl(\ln\nu_{1}-\psi(m-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m - 2}{j}\frac{(-)^{j+1}}{(2j)!}\psi^{(2j)}(m-1)\biggr)\,.\end{split}

(371)

With the low-
\nu
summation at hand, we can proceed to the second part of the sum over high multipoles

\nu\in[\nu_{1},\infty]
via the usual EuMac approximation in (
68
).
Because we delayed the lower end of the sum to
\nu_{1}\gg 1
, we do not have to worry
about EuMac corrections in (
68
).
All of the terms from the lower boundary at
\nu_{1}
are
\Delta
suppressed and we can drop them safely.

Most of the work needed for the high-multipole sum has already been done in the previous section,
so we can easily handle the EuMac integrals by specifying the even
D=2m
in the generic
terms derived above.
Let us begin with the simpler
U
-factor and augment the unsubtracted part in (
340
)
to even dimensions, such that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2m\text{ uns}}^{\int U}

\displaystyle=\frac{6r_{0}}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\frac{\left(2m-2\right)!}{4^{m}m!^{2}}\,.

(372)

Moving on to the subtracted part, we take the expression from (
345
), specify

D=2m
, shift the lower bound from
y_{0}\to y_{1}
and expand for smallish
y_{1}

\displaystyle\eqref{eqIntSumpGenD}\xrightarrow[y_{1}\ll 1]{D=2m}\frac{j-m-1}{2m-1}\binom{\frac{1}{2}-j}{m+1-j}\left(H_{m-j}-H_{m-\frac{3}{2}}+2\ln y_{1}\right)\,,

(373)

which leaves us with the following finite sum

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{2m\text{ sub}}^{\int U}=\frac{6r_{0}}{(D-2)!}&\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{j}\frac{(-)^{j}(m+1-j)}{2m-1}\times\\
&\binom{\frac{1}{2}-j}{m+1-j}\left(H_{m-j}-H_{m-\frac{3}{2}}+2\ln y_{1}\right)\,.\end{split}

(374)

While we do not get a closed-form expression when summing the
H_{m-j}
term,
the two remaining terms, which are independent of
j
, nicely sum into

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2m\text{ sub}}^{\int U}\ni-\frac{6r_{0}}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}H_{m-\frac{3}{2}}-\ln y_{1}\right)\,.

(375)

This again shows that when we combine with the low part of the sum in (
371
),
the
\nu_{1}
parameter cancels out (i.e. the part that comes from the
U
-factor of
C_{m}
),
so that the final result does not depend on the precise position where we split the sum.

The two final remaining pieces come from the integration of the log terms.
The unsubtracted one is

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2m\text{ uns}}^{\int\ln}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\left(\frac{1}{\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1,1;m+\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{4}\right)}{4^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right)\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\right)\,.

(376)

The subtracted part is very similar to the
U
-factor above, where the sum over
p

was given in (
373
).
Here the coefficient enters into the sum defined by
\ln R_{\nu 0}^{a}
in (
333
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{2m\text{ sub}}^{\int\ln}=\frac{4}{(D-2)!}&\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1+2^{-2j+1}}{2j-1}\frac{m+1-j}{2m-1}\times\\
&\binom{\frac{1}{2}-j}{m+1-j}\left(H_{m-j}-H_{m-\frac{3}{2}}+2\ln y_{1}\right)\,.\end{split}

(377)

Just as in the case of the
U
-factor, we do not find a closed-form expression when summing over the
H_{m-j}

term, while the last two terms sum into

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{2m\text{ sub}}^{\int\ln}\ni-\frac{4}{(D-2)!}&\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\frac{m}{m-\frac{1}{2}}\binom{\frac{1}{2}}{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}H_{m-\frac{3}{2}}-\ln y_{1}\right)\times\\
&\left(1+\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,_{2}F_{1}\left(1,1-m;\frac{3}{2};\frac{1}{4}\right)\right)\,.\end{split}

(378)

Let us compare this to the first part of the
C_{m}
coefficient in (
366
) that multiplies
the
\ln\nu_{1}
term in (
371
).
Again, we see how the arbitrary splitting point defined by
\nu_{1}
cancels out and
we are left with an unambiguous result for any even
D
.

To summarize this arduous section, the final result for the finite sum is obtained by collecting all
of the five terms above into

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2m}=\Sigma_{2m}^{\text{low}}+\Sigma_{2m}^{\int}=\Sigma_{2m}^{\text{low}}+\Sigma_{2m\text{ uns}}^{\int U}+\Sigma_{2m\text{ sub}}^{\int U}+\Sigma_{2m\text{ uns}}^{\int\ln}+\Sigma_{2m\text{ sub}}^{\int\ln}\,.

(379)

In this final result, the
\ln\nu_{1}
terms partially cancel with
\ln y_{1}
ones, leaving us with
a residual dependence on
\ln\Delta/r_{0}
.
Combining the closed-form expressions obtained above does not lead to
a simple expression, as the one we found for odd
D
s.
Still, plugging in the integers leads to rather compact expressions for the first couple of even
D

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}^{\text{low}}=\frac{2}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\begin{cases}\frac{5}{2}\left(\gamma_{E}+\ln\nu_{1}\right)\,,&D=2\,,\\
-\frac{3}{8}\left(\gamma_{E}+\ln\nu_{1}\right)\,,&D=4\,,\\
\frac{1}{10}\left(\gamma_{E}-\zeta(3)+\ln\nu_{1}\right)\,,&D=6\,,\end{cases}

(380)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{D}^{\int}=\frac{2}{(D-2)!}\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\Delta}\right)^{D-1}\begin{cases}\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}}-\frac{11}{4}-\frac{5}{2}\ln 2y_{1}\,,&D=2\,,\\
-\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{3}}+\frac{3}{32}+\frac{3}{8}\ln 2y_{1}\,,&D=4\,,\\
\frac{3\sqrt{3}\pi}{160}-\frac{211}{4800}-\frac{1}{10}\ln 2y_{1}\,,&D=6\,.\end{cases}

(381)

Together with the result in generic odd
D
s, these finite sums provide us with
the finite log of the determinant in any dimension.
To complete the calculation of the decay rate one
has to perform the renormalization procedure, as described in section
6

This might be a useful exercise if there is enough physical

We point out that what enters in this calculation will be integrals with
a certain number of insertions of

and the accompanying powers of
\rho

These types of integrals, as shown in (
