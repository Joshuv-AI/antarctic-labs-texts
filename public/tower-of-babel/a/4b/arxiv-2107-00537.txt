About Evaluation Metrics for Contextual Uplift Modeling
C. RENAUDIN, M. MARTIN

Abstract
. In this tech report we discuss the evaluation problem of contextual uplift modeling from the causal inference point of view. More particularly, we instantiate the individual treatment effect (ITE) estimation, and its evaluation counterpart. First, we unify two well studied fields: the statistical ITE approach and its observational counterpart based on uplift study. Then we exhibit the problem of evaluation, based on previous work about ITE and uplift modeling. Moreover, we derive a new estimator for the uplift curve, built on logged bandit feedback dataset, that reduces its variance. We prove that AUUC fails on non randomized control trial (RCT) datasets and discuss some corrections and guidelines that should be kept in mind while using AUUC (re-balancing the population, local importance sampling using propensity score).



1.
Introduction

Uplift modeling has been widely used to estimate the effect of a treatment on an outcome, at the user/individual level.
It has effectively been used in fields such as marketing and customer retention, to target
those customers that are most likely to respond due to the campaign or treatment.
This allows the selection of the subset of entities
for which the effect of a treatment will be ”large enough” and, as such, allows the maximization of
the overall ”reward”. Specifically, it produces uplift scores which are used to essentially create a ranking between user inputs. From a budget constraint perspective, one aims to target first the customers with the highest ”change in behavior” between being treated, and not treated.

In section
3
, we first introduce the underlying statistical problem we aim at solving.
We formally define the AUUC maximization problem we want to solve, based on the fundamental problem of estimating treatment effect: for a given user we can only observe its outcome under the treatment he was facing.
ITE and Uplift modeling are very similar notions. They are formally introduced in section
3
, as well as the assumptions guaranteeing that they are the same quantity. Another view of such a problem is through the lens of a two-armed bandit. Indeed, the ITE problem can be viewed as an instantiation of the off-line logged-bandit feedback problem, with binary action set
\{0,1\}
. In order to identify ITE, one would need a full feedback for the outcome. The ITE can be reconstructed, based on the observed features, leveraging outcomes under both treatment and control.

In section
4
we define the usual metrics used to evaluate uplift models: PEHE and AUUC, and highlight both advantages and drawbacks. Specifically, we highlight AUUC problems: it should be used only for RCT studies and it needs special attention when the treatment group is not balanced (50%-50%). We generalize the theoretical AUUC quantity, based on the underlying distribution of the data.

We further present in Section
5
some counter-examples, when the dataset is not RCT, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(t=1\mid x)
is not constant along features
x
, or if the treatment/control ratio of the dataset is not 50%-50%.

In Section
6
, we introduce the generalized AUUC formula, and the ”two axis” re-balancing to extend the AUUC metrics to non-RCT experiments.

Finally, in section
7
we propose a new construction of the uplift curve, with new rules. We theoretically prove and empirically show that this new way of building the approximated uplift curve dramatically reduces the variance of derived AUUC.

3.
Fundamental problem of uplift modeling

3.1.
Potential outcome framework

We consider in this report the problem based on the Rubin-Neyman potential outcome (PO) framework
[
15
]
, where subject’s
i
response/outcome with and without treatment are denoted respectively by
y_{i}^{(1)}
and
y_{i}^{(0)}
. These variables are known as the potential outcomes. Moreover, in real life experiments, one can only inject user
i
either a treatment (i.e.
t_{i}=1
) or no-treatment (i.e.
t_{i}=0
), and observe the response of the latter injection dose, (i.e. observes only
y_{i}^{(t_{i})}
). The fundamental problem of counterfactual learning is based on the fact that one cannot observe the 2 potential outcomes
\overrightarrow{y}_{i}=(y_{i}^{(1)},y_{i}^{(0)})
for individual
i
. The hypothetical outcome
y_{i}^{(1-t_{i})}
is the unrealized
“counterfactual outcome”
[
3
]
and can only be estimated through a similar user
j\neq i
that did receive the alternative treatment
t_{j}=1-t_{i}
. The notion of similarity shall be inferred based on
observed
features (hereafter denoted by ”features”).

For instance, in medicine, the variable
y_{i}^{(t_{i})}
could model the fact that ”subject
i
has been cured after receiving the treatment
t_{i}
” where this boolean random variable (r.v.) is 1 if the patient took the drug, and 0 otherwise. In advertisement, one could model the fact subject
i
has bought or not a product (boolean outcome variable
y_{i}^{(t_{i})}
), after receiving a display (boolean condition
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}
).

3.2.
Full information / Observable features

We denote the characteristics space
\overline{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For each user, a characteristic vector
\overline{x}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
and a full feedback reward vector
\overrightarrow{y}=(y^{(1)},y^{(0)})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are drawn from a joint distribution
(\overline{x},\overrightarrow{y})\sim\mathcal{D}
. We stress that
\overrightarrow{y}
may be dependent on
\overline{x}
as they are jointly distributed. Specifically user
i
’s behavior is endowed in a
n
-dimensional characteristics vectors
\overline{x}_{i}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
. User
full information
characteristics
\overline{x}_{i}
fully describes the exact behavior (i.e. the outcome distribution) of user
i
.

In the logged-bandit feedback, one only ”sees” the value of
y^{(t)}
, meaning only half of the full feedback reward vector
\overrightarrow{y}
is revealed, whereas in the ”full feedback” problem, the whole reward
\overrightarrow{y}
is revealed. This ”full feedback” is unrealistic, but it can be seen as the ideal dataset one could face to learn such treatment causal effect. The PO distribution
\left(y^{(1)}_{i},y^{(0)}_{i}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\overrightarrow{y}|\overline{x}}(\cdot|\overline{x_{i}})
, with the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\overrightarrow{y}|\overline{x}}
being the marginal of
\mathcal{D}
.
In particular for each user
i
, we assume there exists a r.v.

\left(y_{i}^{(1)},y_{i}^{(0)}\right)=\overrightarrow{y_{i}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},

defined on the complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. This r.v. can be viewed as the underlying outcome distribution of user
i
, if he/she has been treated or not.

In practice, real life constraints (ability to measure and/or privacy-preserving regulation) limit the amount of accessible information for user
i
; i.e.
full information
characteristics
\overline{x}_{i}
is only known through a function
g_{\text{obs.}}
. Specifically one only observes a feature vector
x_{i}=g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the function
g_{\text{obs.}}
is the anonymization mapping into the
observable
feature space
\mathcal{X}
we have access to: for instance, part of the browsing history accessible by an real time bidding (RTB) actor. From the recent privacy-preserving regulation, with related Turtledove
1
1

1

https://github.com/WICG/turtledove
and Sparrow
2
2

2

https://github.com/WICG/sparrow
proposals, the
observable
features
x_{i}
might be the cohort
j\in[J]:=\{1,\dots,J\}
user
i
belongs to, i.e.
g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x}_{i})\in[J]
.

Typically the dimension of both feature space
\mathcal{X}
and characteristics space
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
satisfy
d\ll n
. For example, the
observed features
used by advertisers to process inference (online recommendation system, etc), captures a small set of the user’s behavioral attributes such as demographic information or part of its browsing history.

The key about privacy preserving design is how well the function
g_{\text{obs.}}
captures the original complete user information
\overline{x}_{i}
. The complete spectrum of such anonymization function
g_{\text{obs.}}
goes from the identity mapping, meaning a full leakage of user privacy, to a uniform random assignment, in the cohort setting for instance where user privacy is guaranteed, i.e.
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x})\sim\mathcal{U}([J])
. The whole story of privacy preserving design is to find the right trade-off between these two extremes, guaranteeing a minimum of causal effect between
g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x})
and the associated reward vector
\overrightarrow{y}
while ensuring
g_{\text{obs.}}
preserves the user’s privacy.

Remark 1
.

An interesting question is how to estimate the causal effect between the 2 variables
g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x})
and
\overrightarrow{y}
for a given function
g_{\text{obs.}}
(potentially designed from an adversarial manner), as a correlation would potentially not be sufficient and an intervention would not be possible, at least not directly on the variable
g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x})
. One could wonder, assuming there exists some ”causal link” between
\overline{x}
and
\overrightarrow{y}
, whether a correlation between
g_{\text{obs.}}(\overline{x})
and
\overrightarrow{y}
means a causal link, for every function
g_{\text{obs.}}
.

Remark 2
.

An interesting metric to compute while anonymizing is the information loss
[
1
,
16
]
that quantifies how much information we lose from the whole vector of characteristics
\overline{x}_{i}
, while guaranteeing some minimal performance for prediction. The story is usually set the other way around. The law imposes some minimal privacy guarantee for each user, and based on such a constraint, companies try to optimize the performance of their predictive algorithms (see Figure

3.3.
Treatment assignment

On action set
\mathcal{T}
, a treatment assignment policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{T})
maps each feature (aka ”context”)
x\in\mathcal{X}
to a distribution over the action set
\mathcal{T}
, i.e. the ”treatment assignment”. Specifically,
\pi(\cdot|x)
is a distribution over the action set
\mathcal{T}
. In the simple setting with binary treatment
\mathcal{T}=\{0,1\}
and binary reward
\overrightarrow{y}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{2}
,
\pi(\cdot|x)
is often a Bernoulli distribution that can be summarized by the contextual value
\mathbb{P}(t=1|x)
,
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
. A particular case is when the treatment assignment is stochastic, and
does not depend
on the feature
x
. This is called Randomized Control Trial (RCT).

Definition 1

(RCT)
.

A treatment policy
\pi(\cdot\mid x)
is said to be RCT, if and only if there exists a distribution
\pi_{RCT}
over the action set, independent of
x
and such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}

\pi(\cdot\mid x)=\pi_{RCT}(\cdot)
. In particular for a binary action set, the r.v.
t
sampled using
\pi
is such that
\mathbb{P}(t=1|x)=\alpha
,
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\alpha
being a constant.

Although being sometimes expensive or unethical, RCT is the gold-standard for studying the causal relationship between a treatment and an outcome. Randomization eliminates the bias inherent to observational studies, where surprising phenomena like Simpson’s paradox might occur
[
13
, Chapter 6.3]
.

3.4.
Logged-bandit versus full feedback

We define here the two types of dataset we consider hereafter, namely the logged-bandit feedback one, and the full feedback one.

The full feedback dataset
D_{N}^{\text{full}}
is composed by the feature vector and the reward vector.

D_{N}^{\text{full}}=\left\{x_{i},\overrightarrow{y_{i}}\right\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad\overrightarrow{y_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

No policy is required at this point, and any treatment policy could be plugged in afterward.

On the other hand, the logged-bandit
D_{N}
(aka observational dataset) of size
N
is a list of instances:

D_{N}=\left\{x_{i},t_{i},y_{i},q_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad\text{with}\quad q_{i}=\pi(t_{i}|x_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad y_{i}=\overrightarrow{y}^{(t_{i})}_{i}.

Remark 3
.

N.B.: In many observational datasets, particularly in causal inference, we do not have access to the point-wise probability of being treated
q_{i}
. This is then estimated through a nuisance model, in order to fit the propensity score. This is not part of the present work, where we assume to have access to such
q_{i}
.

Note that the terminology
observational dataset
should not be mismatched with
observational studies
.
While the first covers both RCT and observational studies, the second only covers the process to assign treatment from a non random process, i.e. non-RCT.

3.6.
ITE

The Individual Treatment Effect (ITE) is a r.v. defined by the difference between the two PO:

\tau=\left\langle\overrightarrow{y},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle=\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}-\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}.

Specifically for subject
i
, the ITE
\tau_{i}
is the incremental benefit of being treated, versus not:

\tau_{i}:=\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}_{i}-\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}_{i}.

As explained before, in the real world, one can never observe at the same time both quantities
\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}_{i}
and
\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}_{i}
, and then the ITE
\tau_{i}
is not
identifiable
. A generalization trick is based on estimating the so called Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE). The CATE is based on the observable features
x
only, and generalizes around similar
x
to reconstruct such ITE. Specifically, we define the CATE
\tau(x)
by:

(5)

\tau(x):=\mathbb{E}[\tau|x]=\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}-\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}|x].

Similarly, if we consider an RCT assignment, and if the r.v.
y=t\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}+(1-t)\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}
, then the uplift is defined as follows:

(6)

u(x):=\mathbb{E}[y|t=1,x]-\mathbb{E}[y|t=0,x]=p_{1}(x)-p_{0}(x).

3.7.
Linking the treatment effect heterogeneity and uplift objectives

Based on
[
17
]
we can decompose the CATE as:

(7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tau({x})=&\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}-\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}\mid{x}]\\
=&\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}\mid t=1,{x}]P(t=1)+\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}\mid t=0,{x}]P(t=0)\\
&-\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}\mid t=1,{x})P(t=1)-\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}\mid t=0,{x}]P(t=0)\\
=&\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}\mid t=1,{x}]-\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}\mid t=0,{x}]}_{\text{observed }}\\
&+P(t=1)\{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}\mid t=0,{x}]}_{\text{observed }}-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(0)}\mid t=1,{x}]\}}_{\text{unobserved }}\\
&+P(t=0)\{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}\mid t=0,{x}]}_{\text{unobserved }}-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\overrightarrow{y}^{(1)}\mid t=1,{x}]\}}_{\text{observed }}\end{split}

The last equality splits the CATE into 3 terms, hereafter deeply explained under three common assumptions.

The first term is equal to the uplift term
u(x)
defined in equation (
6
), because the PO equals the observing outcome when conditioning on
t
. The second and third terms are null under the following unconfoundedness.

Assumption 1

(Unconfoundedness, aka strong ignorability)
.

The potential outcome couple is independent of the treatment
t
, when conditioning on the observed co-variate variables
x
, i.e.

(8)

\left\{y^{(0)},y^{(1)}\right\}\perp t\mid x

Under Assumption
1
, one immediately gets:

\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}\mid t=0,x]=\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}\mid x],

leading to:

\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}\mid t=0,x]-\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}\mid t=1,x]=\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}\mid x]-\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}\mid x]=0.

The estimation of the uplift term (
6
) only involves observational data without any counterfactual. It can be estimated with no bias guaranteed, under the 2 following conditions: overlapping and SUTVA.

Assumption 2

(Overlapping treatment)
.

Any subject
i
has a non-zero probability of receiving treatment and control, i.e. for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(9)

0<\mathbb{P}(t=1\mid x)<1

Remark 4
.

In assumption
2
, we somehow extend the RCT setting, as the propensity score
\mathbb{P}(t=1\mid x)
may depend on
x
. Assumption
2
remains true in RCT experiments if
\epsilon\leq\pi_{\text{RCT}}(\cdot)\leq 1-\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
.

Assumption 3

(Stable Unit Treatment Values Assumption)
.

A subject’s potential outcome is not affected by other subjects treatment. In other words, treatment applied to one subject does not affect the outcome of other subjects.

As explained in
[
17
]
, Assumption
3
often applies in health related application. But, in online advertising, because of the wide existence of crowd-sourced coupon sharing websites, this assumption may fail. Indeed not treating a user does not guarantee that this user will effectively not be treated by a competitor.

From a same perspective, Assumption
2
requires to maintain a minimum exploration level for every feature. It might be the case for low traffic/short period experiments, but certainly not from a production machinery in advertising for instance.

Remark 5
.

In many papers about uplift modeling, it is implicitly assumed that the data comes from RCT experiments or A/B tests
[
9
]
. In particular, RCT implies the equivalence between conditioning and intervening
[
12
]
, i.e.
t=1\Leftrightarrow\text{do}(t=1)
, because
T
has no parents in the associated SCM, and
\emptyset
is a valid adjustment set for
(T,Y)

[
13
]
.

Remark 6
.

Assumption
1
is not testable from dataset
D_{N}
only. In short, we want to check if the projected observable feature information
x
is enough in order to fully explain the heterogeneity in treatment, and ultimately, to build uplift models. An easy answer has been proposed in
[
10
]
and requires that
x
contains all direct causes of the outcome
y
and contains no effect variables of
y
, which is usually not verified in advertising, considering a sequential timeline: as the available feature is very limited (
d\ll n
), the variable
y_{t}
may influence future feature
x_{t+1}
. Assumption
1
fails if this feedback loop phenomenon happens.

3.8.
Modeling uplift in practice

Literature has increased fast about building more and more precise models in order to fit at best
\tau
. This is beyond the scope of this tech report, but a recent survey
[
17
]
clearly explains all subtleties of each standard method.
A general model fitting the CATE
x\mapsto\tau(x)
of Uplift
x\mapsto u(x)
will be hereafter denoted by
\widehat{\tau}
and
\widehat{u}
.

4.
Metrics for evaluation

4.1.
Metrics with known ground truth

For synthetic or semi-synthetic datasets, as we know the generating model for
y\mid t,x
, we can use the ground truth to evaluate the learned model. The most popular metric to do so is the Precision in Estimation of
Heterogeneous Effects (PEHE) (Hill, 2011), which computes the MSE between the estimated CATE
\widehat{\tau}
and the exact one using the ground truth
\tau
. Formally, PEHE is defined as

\text{PEHE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}(x_{i})-\tau(x_{i})\right)^{2}

4.2.
Metrics without ground truth

4.2.1.
Qini and Uplift curves

As recall in
[
7
]
, when the true CATE
\tau
is not known, the usual principle of uplift modeling is to find the best way of ranking instances by their respective uplift values
\widehat{\tau}
. Specifically,
[
14
]
suggests to visualize the so called
incremental expected uplift value
for an incrementally larger subgroup of the ranked population. For example, for the top 10%, 20%, …, 100% of the instances.

Figure 3
.
Incremental gain curve (blue) versus the expected theoretical gain using random targeting (black)

Figure
3
shows such an
incremental expected uplift value
: the blue line represents the cumulative incremental gains as a function of the selected fraction of the ranked population, while the black line represents the expected value of a random sub-sample of that size, called the random baseline. We expect a good uplift model to rank first the individuals likely to respond when treated, leading to higher estimated uplift values in the early parts of the plot. Different approaches have been proposed in the literature to do so, the two best known being the Qini Curve and the Uplift Curve. We stress that there is a further difference
between the curves themselves: which values are plotted on the
y
-axis and
x
-axis, and which area under the curve is returned (with or without subtracting the random line).

As noticed by the authors of
[
7
]
, we cannot find unique definitions for Qini and Uplift curves in the broad literature. Nevertheless, we can split all these definitions into 2 categories:

•

separated
versus
joint
ranking: is the ranking computed on treatment and control groups
separately
or as one
joint
group?

•

absolute
versus
relative
incremental gains: are the incremental gains computed as absolute values or as values relative to the number of individuals?

The second difference (
absolute
versus
relative
) is actually related to the special care required to compute uplift metrics when the dataset is unbalanced (i.e. when the treatment/control split ratio is different than 50%-50%). They require a re-balancing that can be
partially
(this re-balancing along the
y
-axis is developed in Section
4.2.2
) implemented with relative incremental gains or with absolute incremental gains associated with re-scaling factors.

We want to highlight here a third difference among variants in literature, that is still related to the re-balancing topic, but is orthogonal to the second difference (this third difference is not introduced as such in
[
7
]
, but presented through examples): the re-balancing can be implemented with global factors (based on the sizes of the complete treatment and control groups) or with current estimated factors (based on the sizes of the incremental treatment and control subgroups defined by the first
k
elements).

4.2.2.
Definition of some variants

Let
\widehat{u}
be a general uplift model thought of as an approximation of the true uplift
u
.

To formalize the
joint
variants, we first denote by
\Upsilon(D_{N},k)
the decreasing order of the dataset
D_{N}
under
\widehat{u}(\cdot)
. Specifically
\Upsilon(D_{N},k)
represents the first
k
instances in
D_{N}
according to the ordering induced through the score
\widehat{u}(\cdot)
, i.e.

(10)

\displaystyle\Upsilon(D_{N},k)

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}\right)\in D_{N}\right\}_{i=1,\ldots,k}\text{ such that }\forall i\leq k,\forall k<l,\,\widehat{u}\left(x_{i}\right)\geq\widehat{u}\left(x_{l}\right)

We define the permutation of
[N]

\phi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
that sorts the dataset instances by
decreasing
uplift model value
\widehat{u}(x_{i})
(with ties arbitrarily split), i.e. let
\phi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
s.t.

(11)

i<j\Rightarrow\widehat{u}(x_{\phi(i)})\geq\widehat{u}(x_{\phi(j)}).

Among these top-
k
ranked individuals, we define

(12)

\displaystyle N_{\Upsilon}^{T}(D_{N},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}\right)\in\Upsilon(D_{N},k)}\mathbbm{1}_{t_{i}=1}

(13)

\displaystyle N_{\Upsilon}^{C}(D_{N},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}\right)\in\Upsilon(D_{N},k)}\mathbbm{1}_{t_{i}=0}

and the number of responders among the top-
k
individuals are defined as:

(14)

\displaystyle R_{\Upsilon}^{T}(D_{N},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}\right)\in\Upsilon(D_{N},k)}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{i}=1}\mathbbm{1}_{t_{i}=1}

(15)

\displaystyle R_{\Upsilon}^{C}(D_{N},k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}\right)\in\Upsilon(D_{N},k)}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{i}=1}\mathbbm{1}_{t_{i}=0}

To formalize the
separated
variants, we consider the treatment group
T=\{(x,t,y)\in D_{N}\mid t=1\}
and the control group
C=\{(x,t,y)\in D_{N}\mid t=0\}
, and we denote by
\Upsilon(T,k)
(resp.
\Upsilon(C,k)
) the decreasing order of the treatment group (resp. control group) under
\widehat{u}(\cdot)
. We define the number of responders among the top-k individuals on each group as:

4.3.
In practice, how to select the best Uplift model?

4.3.1.
Pragmatic criteria to select an uplift model

We state here the 6 criteria by Radcliffe in his pioneer work
[
14
]
when one wants to choose the ”best” uplift model among several models learned on the same dataset:

•

”Validated qini”: the highest qini/AUUC value.

•

”Monotonicity of incremental gains”: intuitively, after sorting the individuals by decreasing estimated uplift score, each segment
[p,p+\delta]
with
p\in[0,1]
should have an average slope ”smaller” than the previous one, producing a concave-like uplift curve.

•

”Maximum impact”: how much extra positive outcomes the model predicts at its peak.

•

”Impact at cutoff”: the cumulative uplift at a given percentile threshold.

•

”Tight validation”: is the estimated curve on validation set similar to the estimated curve on the training dataset?

•

”Range of predictions.”: models with a larger prediction range are supposed to be more useful.

4.3.2.
Practical questions

This raises the question of how we can choose among uplift models if their AUUC/Qini is equal but their uplift curves have different shapes. It depends on the quantity of interest (QoI) and the budget constraints we face. Usually the optimization problem writes:

,

•

\text{constr}\in\left\{\text{RoI},\text{\# targeted users}\approx\text{budget},\dots\right\}

with
\text{RoI}=\frac{\text{\#incremental user }}{\text{\#treated user}}\approx

Definition 3

.

For an uplift model

we define the Normalized Uplift Curve
\operatorname{V_{N}}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as:

\operatorname{V_{N}}(r\in[0,1])=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tau(x)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x)\geq\xi(r)]}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)\quad\text{where}\quad\xi(r)=\inf\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(x:\widehat{u}(x)\geq\xi)\leq r\}.

Equivalently, we can define

\xi(r)=\inf\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}_{2}([\xi,+\infty[)\leq r\}
.

Intuitively,
\xi(r)
corresponds to the threshold such that a proportion

We define the Area Under the Normalized Uplift Curve (
\operatorname{AUNUC}
) as:

(21)

\operatorname{AUNUC}(p\in[0,1])=\int_{[0,p]}\operatorname{V_{N}}(r)\mathrm{d}r

For a given dataset
D_{N}
,
\operatorname{AUUC}
differs from
\operatorname{AUNUC}
only by a constant factor that depends on the dataset size (and the re-balancing method chosen for
V(p)
to manage unbalanced datasets) but not on the evaluated uplift models themselves. Thus comparing uplift models with
\operatorname{AUNUC}
is equivalent to comparing them with
\operatorname{AUUC}
, as long as the dataset is the same.

4.5.

Here we recall the way of approximating the AUUC, in the case where we face a logged-bandit dataset, i.e. we do not access the full feedback dataset, but only

•

•

Then, we convert the sorted units

(1)

5.1.
What does non-RCT means?

First, let us recall the definition of RCT:

RCT:
\exists\,\alpha\in]0,1[
, s.t.
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid x)=\alpha

Then the negation of the latter definition becomes:

non-RCT:
\exists\,x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid x_{1})\neq\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid x_{2})

The generalization of non RCT setting, based on neighborhoods, assuming that the function
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid x)
is continuous writes:

non-RCT on neighborhood:
\exists\,\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}\subset\mathcal{X}\text{ with }\mathcal{X}_{1}\cap\mathcal{X}_{2}=\emptyset
, s.t.
\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid\mathcal{X}_{1})\neq\mathbb{P}(T=1\mid\mathcal{X}_{2})

\beta_{1}^{1}=0.4
,
\beta_{1}^{0}=0.2
,
\beta_{2}^{1}=0.2
and
\beta_{2}^{0}=0.1
.
We can then assign values to approximated uplift
\alpha_{1}=0.1
and
\alpha_{2}=0.2
to obtain a model
\widehat{u}
that outperforms the exact uplift
u
i.e. with AUUC scores satisfying (
\text{AUUC}(\widehat{u})>\text{AUUC}(u)
).

Remark 9
.

The latter counter-example is not possible anymore if
q_{0}=0.5
(i.e. RCT 50%-50%), as
\text{slope}(\mathcal{X}_{1})=0.5(\beta_{1}^{1}+\beta_{1}^{0})-\beta_{1}^{0}=u(\mathcal{X}_{1})/2
and then the uplift curve cannot be non-concave anymore.

Remark 10
.

Once again, this issue can be avoided by using the generalized formula of the uplift curve defined in Section
6
. We can note that with this non 50%-50% RCT setting, we can easily estimate the propensity score
q_{0}
(when not known) from data.

6.
Working with Unbalanced Datasets

The previous section presented examples showing the AUUC metric as defined in Section
4.4
should not be directly applied on an unbalanced dataset. In the current section, we show how we can manage that case and justify the
y
-axis and
x
-axis re-balancing of the uplift curve mentioned in Section
4.2.3
.

6.1.
Introducing Observed and Underlying Distributions

Reminder:
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the user feature space, with the associated probability measure
\mathbb{P}
. The full reward for every point
x\in\mathcal{X}
is provided by
\overrightarrow{y}(x)=(y^{(1)}(x),y^{(0)}(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The true uplift is therefore
\tau(x)=y^{(1)}(x)-y^{(0)}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.

To simulate a real dataset, with a clear separation between the treatment and the control groups, we introduce
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
with the common ITE estimator (
signed
outcome function)
\widehat{\tau}
:

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}

\widehat{\tau}\colon\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\widehat{\tau}(x,t)=y^{(1)}(x)t-y^{(0)}(x)(1-t)=\begin{cases}y^{(1)}(x)&t=1\\
-y^{(0)}(x)&t=0\end{cases}

\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
is associated with two probability measures:

•

an
observed
probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{o}
:
\mathbb{Q}_{o}(x,t)=\mathbb{P}(x)\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x)

•

an
underlying
probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{u}
:
\mathbb{Q}_{u}(x,t)=\mathbb{P}(x)\mathbb{S}_{u}(t)
. This is our target.

While the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
over
\mathcal{X}
is the same for both cases, the sampling measures
\mathbb{S}_{o}
and
\mathbb{S}_{u}
differ. The
observed
sampling measure
\mathbb{S}_{o}
is the cause of the possible dataset imbalance. It may or may not depend on
x
; it does not depend on
x
in the case of a (possibly unbalanced) RCT. One constraint we need is that
\forall(x,t)\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}},\mathbb{P}(x)\neq 0\implies\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x)\neq 0
, i.e. for any point of
\mathcal{X}
with a non zero probability, we always have access to both treatment and control parts (Assumption
2

Overlapping treatment
in Section
3.7
).

The
underlying
probability measure is chosen as the result of a balanced RCT process:
\mathbb{S}_{u}(1)=\mathbb{S}_{u}(0)=1/2
. By definition, it does not depend on
x
.

6.2.
y
-axis re-balancing with
\operatorname{V_{N}}

Using the
observed
probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{o}
for
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
, we want to compute
\operatorname{V_{N}}
and
\operatorname{AUUC}
as if we observe directly the balanced
underlying
probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{u}
for
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}
, or, even better,
\mathbb{P}
for
\mathcal{X}
. Starting from Definition
3
, we show in Appendix
B
that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{V_{N}}(r\in[0,1])

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tau(x)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x)\geq\xi(r)]}\mathbb{P}(x)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{where}\quad\xi(r)=\inf\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(x:\widehat{u}(x)\geq\xi)\leq r\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\tau}(x,t)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x)\geq\xi(r)]}2\mathbb{Q}_{u}(x,t)\mathrm{d}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\tau}(x,t)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x)\geq\xi(r)]}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{o}(x,t)}{\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x)}\mathrm{d}(x,t)

The
1/\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x)
corresponds to the
y
-axis re-balancing of the uplift curve mentioned in Section
4.2.2
, where we presented
V(\cdot)
functions that compute similar quantities but in different ways (absolute vs. relative incremental gains, global factors vs. local factors).

6.3.
x
-axis re-balancing with
\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}

To highlight the necessary
x
-axis re-balancing when computing the
\operatorname{AUNUC}
, we have to get closer to the
joint
setting. As explained in Section
4.2.3
, when considering a
separated
setting (that corresponds to formula (
21
) with an integration over
[0,1]
), the
x
-axis normalization is implicit.

Let
\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a variant function of
\operatorname{V_{N}}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a variant function of
\operatorname{AUNUC}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tau(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime})

In Appendix
A
, we show that
\operatorname{V_{N}}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\operatorname{AUNUC}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}
, i.e. that:

\operatorname{V_{N}}(R(x))=\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x)

\operatorname{AUNUC}(R(x))=\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}(x)

Where
R(x)\in[0,1]
associates
x
to the minimal ratio
r
such as
x
is contained in the top-r% when sorting the dataset by decreasing predicted uplift.

We show in Appendix
B
that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x\in\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tau(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\tau}(x^{\prime},t)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}2\mathbb{Q}_{u}(x^{\prime},t)\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

(23)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\tau}(x^{\prime},t)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{o}(x^{\prime},t)}{\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}(x\in\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{Q}_{u}(x^{\prime},t)\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

(24)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{o}(x^{\prime},t)}{2\,\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

Now we have a
1/\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x)
factor for both
\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}
(
y
-axis re-balancing of the uplift curve) and
\operatorname{AUNUC}
(
x
-axis re-balancing of the uplift curve).

6.4.
Logged-bandit feedback datasets

Reminder: the logged-bandit feedback dataset
D_{N}
is a list of
N
records:

D_{N}=\left\{x_{i},t_{i},y_{i}^{(t_{i})},q_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N},\quad\text{with}\quad q_{i}=\pi(t_{i}|x_{i}).

For the sake of simplicity, we consider this dataset as already sorted by decreasing predicted uplifts according to a given uplift model.

We consider here that all individuals have a distinct predicted uplift value. Managing group of individuals with a constant predicted uplift is the subject of the next section.

Formula (
23
) leads to definitions for
\operatorname{V_{N}}
and
V
for the discrete case with a
joint
setting:

\operatorname{V_{N}}(k\in\{1...N\})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{1}{q_{i}}\left(y^{(1)}_{i}t_{i}-y^{(0)}_{i}(1-t_{i})\right)}

(25)

\displaystyle V(k\in\{1...N\})=N\operatorname{V_{N}}(k\in\{1...N\})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{1}{q_{i}}\left(y^{(1)}_{i}t_{i}-y^{(0)}_{i}(1-t_{i})\right)}

Similarly with Formula (
24
) for
\operatorname{AUNUC}
and
\operatorname{AUUC}
:

\operatorname{AUNUC}(k\in\{1...N\})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{\operatorname{V_{N}}(i)}{2q_{i}}}

(26)

\displaystyle\operatorname{AUUC}(k\in\{1...N\})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{V(i)}{2q_{i}}}

Note that in the RCT case,
q_{i}
only depends on the sizes
|T|
and
|C|
of the treatment and control groups. Thus:

V(k\in\{1...N\})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\left(\frac{y^{(1)}_{i}t_{i}}{|T|}-\frac{y^{(0)}_{i}(1-t_{i})}{|C|}\right)(|T|+|C|)}

\displaystyle\operatorname{AUUC}(k\in\{1...N\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|T|+|C|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\left(\frac{t_{i}}{|T|}+\frac{1-t_{i}}{|C|}\right)V(i)\frac{|T|+|C|}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\left(\frac{t_{i}}{|T|}+\frac{1-t_{i}}{|C|}\right)V(i)}

When using the
\operatorname{AUUC}
metric to compare several uplift models with one given dataset, we can omit the constant factors
(|T|+|C|)
and
1/2
.

Finally, let’s verify that the sums of the individual scaling factors along the
y
-axis (with
V
) and along the
x
-axis (with
\operatorname{AUUC}
) actually give the excepted values.

Along the
y
-axis, with scaling factors from
V
:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\left(\frac{t_{i}}{|T|}+\frac{(1-t_{i})}{|C|}\right)(|T|+|C|)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{|T|}{\frac{1}{|T|}}+\sum_{i=1}^{|C|}{\frac{1}{|C|}}\right)(|T|+|C|)=2(|T|+|C|)

Along the
x
-axis, with scaling factors from
\operatorname{AUUC}
:

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\left(\frac{t_{i}}{|T|}+\frac{1-t_{i}}{|C|}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{|T|}{\frac{1}{|T|}}+\sum_{i=1}^{|C|}{\frac{1}{|C|}}\right)=1

Indeed, the balanced uplift curve spans from 0 to 1 on the
x
-axis, and corresponds to a virtual dataset with twice the size of the original dataset (re-balancing effect) on the
y
-axis.

6.5.
Logged-bandit feedback datasets with iso-predicted-uplift sub-sequences

Intuitively, when we sort the dataset by decreasing predicted uplift, the sub-sequences of individuals with the same predicted uplift are internally ordered in an indefinite way. This indeterminacy impacts the corresponding parts of the uplift curve drawn with Formula (
25
), then the computed
\operatorname{AUUC}
(Formula

Indeed, formulas (

\widehat{u}(x^{\prime}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\geq}\widehat{u}(x)

Let

be the set of the last individuals of all iso-predicted-uplift sub-sequences, i.e.
\forall i\in L:\;\widehat{u}(i)<\widehat{u}(i+1)\lor i=N
.

One way to compute

with iso-predicted-uplift sub-sequences is to:

(1)

Compute

(or

).

(2)

Only keep the
V(k\in L)
values. Indeed, the indefinite ordering of an iso-predicted uplift sub-sequence does not impact the

(3)

Re-compute

using a linear interpolation based on the
V(k\in L)
values. (For all individuals sorted before the

, use the origin

as the left point for the interpolation.)

(4)

Compute
AUUC(k\in\{1..N\})
(or
AUNUC(k)
) using Formula (
26
) with the
interpolated
version of

.

7.3.
Variance reduction for approximating uplift
u

Without loss of generality, we assume here that the permutation is the identity, with
\phi(i)=i
,
\forall i\in[N]
.

We aim at computing the AUUC by integrating one uplift curve estimator
V_{i}
. The variance of the chosen estimator is crucial, as the smaller it is, the smaller the AUUC metric variance is.

We introduce here an estimator based on a linear combination of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, i.e.
V_{\nu}=(1-\nu)V_{1}+\nu V_{2}
, where
\nu
is chosen to minimize the variance of such an estimator.

In appendix
C
, we show
that the variance of
V(\nu)
is minimized when
\nu=\mathbb{P}(y=1|t=0)\mathbb{P}(t=1)+\mathbb{P}(y=1|t=1)\mathbb{P}(t=0)
. Or, equivalently, when
\nu+\mathbb{P}(y=1)=\mathbb{P}(y=1|t=0)+\mathbb{P}(y=1|t=1)
.

In particular, when the dataset is balanced (i.e.
\alpha=0.5
), we have
\nu=\frac{p_{0}+p_{1}}{2}=\mathbb{P}(Y=1)
.

8.
Conclusion

In this tech report we highlight some weaknesses of the traditional AUUC metric, denoted by
V_{1}
in previous sections. More particularly, its potential very high variance, and its construction on an historically arbitrary rule. To overcome this potential problem, we proposed the ’dual’ version of AUUC (
V_{2}
) which is build on somehow the reverted label of both treatment
T
and outcome
Y
. Moreover, we propose a trade-off of both metrics, called
V_{\nu}
, being a linear combination of both metrics (
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
) and we show that this linear version strictly minimizes the variance of this trade-off metric. This result is assessed in various synthetic experiments, with available code.

Remark

(Proper Subsets)
.

From (1) and (2) we deduce that

Proof.

r1\neq r2\implies\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1}))\neq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2}))\implies\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1})\neq\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2})

∎

.

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1}))<\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2}))\iff r_{1}<r_{2}\iff\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1})\subset\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2})

Proof.

\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2})\implies\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1}))=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2}))\implies r_{1}=r_{2}

∎

.

.

\forall r\in[0,R(x)[\cap\mathbb{R}\quad x\notin\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r)

A.1.2.
Riemann-to-Lebesgue transformation

Theorem A.2

(The Transformation Theorem)
.

\int_{[0,1]}f(N(r))\,\mathrm{d}r=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(R(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)

Proof.

We use twice
[
8
, Th. 1.6.9]
(
Change of variables formula
).

First, by using the push-forward measure of
\mathbb{P}
from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{R}
, with a variable substitution using
R(x)
:

\int_{\mathcal{R}}f(r)\,\mathrm{d}(\mathbb{P}\circ R^{-1})(r)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(R(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x)

Second, by using the push-forward measure of
\mu
from
[0,1]
to
\mathcal{R}
, with a variable substitution using
N(r)
(where
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
as defined in
[
8
, Th. 1.1.4]
):

\int_{\mathcal{R}}f(r)\,\mathrm{d}(\mu\circ N^{-1})(r)=\int_{[0,1]}f(N(r))\,\mathrm{d}r

So we just need to demonstrate that
\forall A\subseteq\mathcal{R},(\mathbb{P}\circ R^{-1})(r)=(\lambda\circ N^{-1})(r)

More precisely, we show that
\forall(r_{1},r_{2})\in\mathcal{R}^{2}
, with
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
:

(\mathbb{P}\circ R^{-1})(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R})=(\lambda\circ N^{-1})(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R})=r_{2}-r_{1}

Part 1:
(\mathbb{P}\circ R^{-1})
.

We first prove Theorem
A.1
:
\{x\in\mathcal{X},R(x)\leq r\}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r)\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{R}

(
\implies
)
R(x)\leq r\implies\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(R(x))\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r)
, by definition of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}
. We know that
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(R(x))
, so
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r)

(
\impliedby
) Let us consider an
x\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r)
such as
R(x)>r
. So,
\forall r^{\prime}<R(x)\quad x\notin\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r^{\prime})
. In particular with
r^{\prime}=r
, we have a contradiction. Therefore
R(x)\leq r
.

\forall(r_{1},r_{2})\in\mathcal{R}^{2}
, with
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
:

\displaystyle R^{-1}(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:r_{1}<R(x)\leq r_{2}\}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:R(x)\leq r_{2}\}\setminus\{x\in\mathcal{X}:R(x)\leq r_{1}\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2})\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R^{-1}(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2}))-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1}))\quad\textit{$\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{1})$ being a subset of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}(r_{2})$}

\displaystyle=r_{2}-r_{1}

∎

Part 2:
(\lambda\circ N^{-1})
.

We first show:
N^{-1}(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R})=]r_{1},r_{2}]\quad\forall(r_{1},r_{2})\in\mathcal{R}^{2}

with

r_{1}\leq r_{2}

(
\implies
)
\forall r\in]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R}
:
N^{-1}(r)=r\in]r_{1},r_{2}]

(
\impliedby
)
\forall r\in]r_{1},r_{2}]
:
N(r)\in\mathcal{R}
and
r\leq N(r)\leq r_{2}
(by definition of
N
and because
r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
). So
N(r)\in]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R}
, therefore
r\in N^{-1}(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R})
.

\forall(r_{1},r_{2})\in\mathcal{R}^{2}
, with
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mu(N^{-1}(]r_{1},r_{2}]\cap\mathcal{R}))

\displaystyle=\mu(]r_{1},r_{2}])

\displaystyle=r_{2}-r_{1}\quad\textit{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{durrett2019probability}{}{}, Th. 1.1.4]}}

∎

∎

Remark
.

Measure of singletons of
\mathcal{R}
: a singleton

has a non-zero measure iff

and r is a lower bound of one of the closed intervals composing

.

Proof.

(
\implies
)
\forall r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
such as

and

that is a contradiction with the definition of

.

(

∎

Similarly, we show that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x\in\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\tau(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\tau}(x^{\prime},t)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}2\,\mathbb{Q}_{u}(x^{\prime},t)\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\tau}(x^{\prime},t)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{o}(x^{\prime},t)}{\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

B.2.
\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{AUNUC^{\mathcal{X}}}(x\in\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\sum_{t\in\{0,1\}}{\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})}\mathbb{S}_{u}(t)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime})\quad\textit{where}\quad\mathbb{S}_{u}(t)=1/2

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathbb{S}_{u}(t)\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)\quad\textit{(Fubini's theorem)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{Q}_{u}(x^{\prime},t)\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\mathbb{P}(x^{\prime})\mathbb{S}_{u}(t)\frac{\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x^{\prime})}{\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{V_{N}^{\mathcal{X}}}(x^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[\widehat{u}(x^{\prime})\geq\widehat{u}(x)]}\frac{\mathbb{Q}_{o}(x^{\prime},t)}{2\,\mathbb{S}_{o}(t|x^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}(x^{\prime},t)

C.1.
Distributions

Given a dataset (i.e. a sample) of size
N
,
sorted by decreasing predicted uplift

of the uplift curve
V

V(r)
only depends on the first
rN
individuals. More precisely, the specific ordering of those
rN

V(r)
value.
V(r)
is completely defined by the distribution of the
rN

\{CO,ST,LC,SD\}
, defined in Section
5.2
) with the 2 treatment/control groups (
\{0,1\}
).

For a given

i

•

\beta_{c}(r)

\{CO,ST,LC,SD\}
.

•

C
and
r

Based on definitions of the 4 categories, we can express

\displaystyle p_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=P(Y=1|T=1,r)=\beta_{CO}(r)+\beta_{ST}(r)

\displaystyle p_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=P(Y=1|T=0,r)=\beta_{ST}(r)+\beta_{SD}(r)

C.3.
Estimators for the uplift curve

Let
\widehat{V}_{1}(r)
,
\widehat{V}_{2}(r)
and
\widehat{V}_{\nu}(r)
be point-wise estimators of the uplift curve, defined as:

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{rN}{Q_{1i}(\alpha)}

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{rN}{Q_{2i}(\alpha)}

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{\nu}(r)

\displaystyle=(1-\nu)\widehat{V}_{1}(r)+\nu\widehat{V}_{2}(r)=\sum_{i}^{rN}{\big((1-\nu)Q_{1i}(\alpha)+\nu Q_{2i}(\alpha)\big)}=\sum_{i}^{rN}{Q_{\nu i}(\alpha)}\quad\forall\nu\in[0,1]

All
Q_{1i}
and
Q_{2i}
being independent, we thus have:

\displaystyle E[\widehat{V}_{1}(r)]

\displaystyle=rNE[Q_{1}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle E[\widehat{V}_{2}(r)]

\displaystyle=rNE[Q_{2}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle E[\widehat{V}_{\nu}(r)]

\displaystyle=rN\big((1-\nu)E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]+\nu E[Q_{2}(\alpha)]\big)

\displaystyle E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=1,T=1|r)}{\alpha}-\frac{P(Y=1,T=0|r)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=1|T=1,r)P(T=1)}{\alpha}-\frac{P(Y=1|T=0,r)P(T=0)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}(r)\alpha}{\alpha}-\frac{p_{0}(r)(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=p_{1}(r)-p_{0}(r)

\displaystyle E[Q_{2}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=0,T=0|r)}{1-\alpha}-\frac{P(Y=0,T=1|r)}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=0|T=0,r)P(T=0)}{1-\alpha}-\frac{P(Y=0|T=1,r)P(T=1)}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-P(Y=1|T=0,r))P(T=0)}{1-\alpha}-\frac{(1-P(Y=1|T=1,r))P(T=1)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-p_{0}(r))(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}-\frac{(1-p_{1}(r))\alpha}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{0}(r))-(1-p_{1}(r))=p_{1}(r)-p_{0}(r)=E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle(1-\nu)E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]+\nu E[Q_{2}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle=(1-\nu)E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]+\nu E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]

\displaystyle=E[Q_{1}(\alpha)]=p_{1}(r)-p_{0}(r)

Therefore,
\forall r\in[0,1],\forall\nu\in[0,1]

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{V}_{1}(r)]=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{V}_{2}(r)]=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{V}_{\nu}(r)]=rN(p_{1}(r)-p_{0}(r))

C.4.
Preparation for the variance reduction of
\widehat{V}_{\nu}

\displaystyle E[Q_{1}(\alpha)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=1,T=1|r)}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{P(Y=1,T=0|r)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=1|T=1,r)P(T=1)}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{P(Y=1|T=0,r)P(T=0)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}(r)\alpha}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{p_{0}(r)(1-\alpha)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}(r)}{\alpha}+\frac{p_{0}(r)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle E[Q_{2}(\alpha)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=0,T=0|r)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+\frac{P(Y=0,T=1|r)}{\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(Y=0|T=0,r)P(T=0)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+\frac{P(Y=0|T=1,r)P(T=1)}{\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-P(Y=1|T=0,r))P(T=0)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+\frac{(1-P(Y=1|T=1,r))P(T=1)}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-p_{0}(r))(1-\alpha)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+\frac{(1-p_{1}(r))\alpha}{\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p_{0}(r)}{1-\alpha}+\frac{1-p_{1}(r)}{\alpha}

Thus:

\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)^{2}]=\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}

C.5.
Variance reduction of
\widehat{V}_{\nu}

All
Q_{1i}
and
Q_{2i}
being independent, we have:

Var(\widehat{V}_{\nu}(r))=rNVar(Q_{\nu}(\alpha))

So, minimizing the variance of
\widehat{V}_{\nu}
is equivalent as minimizing the variance of
Q_{\nu}(\alpha)
.

\displaystyle Var(Q_{\nu}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=Var((1-\nu)Q_{1}(\alpha)+\nu Q_{2}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=(1-\nu)^{2}Var(Q_{1}(\alpha))+\nu^{2}Q_{2}(\alpha)+2(1-\nu)\nu Cov(Q_{1}(\alpha),Q_{2}(\alpha))

Note that
Cov(Q_{1}(\alpha),Q_{2}(\alpha))=\cancel{\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)Q_{2}(\alpha)]}-\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)]\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)]
.

Differentiating
\nu\mapsto Var(Q_{\nu})
w.r.t.
\nu
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{d(Var(Q_{\nu}(\alpha)))}{d(\nu)}

\displaystyle=-2(1-\nu)Var(Q_{1}(\alpha))+2\nu Var(Q_{2}(\alpha))+2(1-2\nu)Cov(Q_{1}(\alpha),Q_{2}(\alpha))

\displaystyle=2\nu\big(Var(Q_{1}(\alpha))+Var(Q_{2}(\alpha))-2Cov(Q_{1}(\alpha),Q_{2}(\alpha))\big)

\displaystyle-2\big(Var(Q_{1}(\alpha))-2Cov(Q_{1}(\alpha),Q_{2}(\alpha))\big)

\displaystyle=2\nu\big(\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)]^{2}+\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)]^{2}+2\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)]\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)]\big)

\displaystyle-2\big(\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)^{2}]\;\cancel{-\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)]^{2}}+\cancel{\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)]\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)]}\big)

\displaystyle=2\nu\big(\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)^{2}]-\cancel{(\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)]-\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}(\alpha)])^{2}}\big)-2\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}(\alpha)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\nu}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}-2\bigg(\frac{p_{1}(r)}{\alpha}+\frac{p_{0}(r)}{1-\alpha}\bigg)

That is a linear function of
\nu
with a positive slope:
\frac{2}{\alpha(1-\alpha})>0

\forall\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Thus, solving
\frac{d(Var(Q_{\nu}))}{d(\nu)}=0
gives the
\nu
value that minimizes
Var(Q_{\nu})
:

\displaystyle\nu=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}^{2}](\alpha)}{\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}^{2}(\alpha)]+\mathbb{E}[Q_{2}^{2}](\alpha)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p_{1}(r)}{\alpha}+\frac{p_{0}(r)}{1-\alpha}\right)\alpha(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{1}(r)(1-\alpha)+p_{0}(r)\alpha

Or equivalently:

\nu+P(Y=1|r)=p_{0}(r)+p_{1}(r)

In particular, when
\alpha=0.5
, we have

\nu=\frac{p_{0}(r)+p_{1}(r)}{2}=P(Y=1|r)
