A Constructive Approach to Function Realization by Neural Stochastic Differential Equations
Tanya Veeravalli, Maxim Raginsky

Abstract
The problem of function approximation by neural dynamical systems has typically been approached in a top-down manner: Any continuous function can be approximated to an arbitrary accuracy by a sufficiently complex model with a given architecture. This can lead to high-complexity controls which are impractical in applications. In this paper, we take the opposite, constructive approach: We impose various structural restrictions on system dynamics and consequently characterize the class of functions that can be realized by such a system. The systems are implemented as a cascade interconnection of a neural stochastic differential equation (Neural SDE), a deterministic dynamical system, and a readout map. Both probabilistic and geometric (Lie-theoretic) methods are used to characterize the classes of functions realized by such systems.



I
Introduction

There is an extensive literature on function approximation by neural nets. For instance, a classical result due to Leshno et al.
[
1
]
states the following: Let
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function which is not a polynomial. Then for any continuous
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in K}\Bigg|f(x)-\sum^{N}_{i=1}c_{i}\sigma(a_{i}^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x+b_{i})\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon

(1)

(in fact, the universal approximation property holds only if
\sigma
is not a polynomial). The approximating function in (
1
) is an instance of a neural net with one hidden layer,
N
hidden units (or neurons), and activation function
\sigma
. There are also universal approximation results for multilayer neural nets
[
2
]
, as well as depth separation results
[
3
]
which show (constructively) that there exist functions that admit efficient representation by multilayer neural nets, yet require exponentially more hidden units for representation by “shallower” nets.

Recently, motivated in large part by the widespread use of deep learning, there has been a lot of interest in continuous-time abstractions of neural nets
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, aptly termed

. These are modeled by dynamical systems of the form

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=f(z(t),w(t)),\qquad z(0)=\alpha(x)

(2a)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\beta(z(1))

(2b)

where the
q

by a read-in map

p

is produced from the state

at time

by a read-out map
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, and

is a family of vector fields on

. For example, we could have something like

\displaystyle f(x,w)=C\boldsymbol{\sigma}(Ax+b),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\big(\sigma(z_{1}),\dots,\sigma(z_{k})\big)^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}},\,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}.

The problem of universal approximation is then to establish the conditions under which any continuous function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{q}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
could be approximated, to any given accuracy

This problem has been addressed in a number of recent works
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. While the methods and techniques differ, the overall philosophy of the results is

—under suitable structural assumptions on

any

desired accuracy by choosing a suitable control

. The resulting controls, however, tend to exhibit fairly high complexity unlikely to be tolerated in practical applications. For example, they may be piecewise constant, but the number of pieces will grow like
(1/\varepsilon)^{d}
, where

depends polynomially on

[
8
,
9
,
10
]
; other constructions make use of high-gain, high-frequency controls
[
11
]
, with similar complexity issues.

I-A

A constructive approach

In this paper, we approach the problem of function realization by neural dynamical systems from a
constructive
,
bottom-up
perspective. That is, instead of fixing
a priori
the class of functions to be approximated, we impose various structural restrictions on the system dynamics and then characterize the class of functions that can be realized by the system. To fix ideas, let us consider a cascade interconnection
[
12
]
of two dynamical systems of the following form:

\displaystyle\dot{w}(t)

\displaystyle=g(w(t),\theta)

(4a)

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)

\displaystyle=f(z(t),w(t)),

(4b)

where
g(\cdot,\theta)
is a family of vector fields on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
parametrized by
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. This system differs from (
2
) in one key respect: The trajectory
w(t)
is not prescribed externally, but is instead generated internally according to (
4a
). Given the read-in map
\alpha
and the read-out map
\beta
, as in (
2
), we can realize different functions of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
by tuning the parameter
\theta
[and, possibly, the initial condition
w(0)
].

Neural ODE models of this type have been considered in the literature. For example, Choromanski et al.
[
13
]
analyzed the case when
w
takes values in the orthogonal group
O(n)
, the vector fields
g(\cdot,\theta)
are of the form

\displaystyle g(w,\theta)=w\tilde{g}(w,\theta)

for some finitely parametrized map
\tilde{g}(\cdot,\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}\to{\rm Skew}(n)
, where
{\rm Skew}(n)
is the set of all
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices with real entries, and

\displaystyle f(z,w)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}(wz),

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the coordinatewise application of some scalar nonlinearity
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, as in (
3
). While Choromanski et al. did not explicitly characterize the class of functions that can be realized by this model, they showed that they enjoy certain stability properties that are advantageous in applications.

We will use cascade models like (
4
) as a starting point, but will replace the deterministic dynamics (
4a
) with a stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by a multidimensional Brownian motion. The rationale for using such
neural SDEs

[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
is twofold: First, by using Brownian motion processes as
generalized inputs

[
18
]
, we will be able to generate all the internal complexity we need while only varying the global parameters of the SDE. Second, it provides us with a sampling-based mechanism for constructing finite approximations
[
19
]
. Indeed, if
F(x;\omega)
is a random function of
x
, then the expected value
f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathbf{E}}_{\omega}[F(x;\omega)]
can be closely approximated by finite sums of the form

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}F(x;\omega_{i}),

where
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N}
are independent copies of
\omega
, with the
L^{2}
approximation error
{\mathbf{E}}[(f(x)-\hat{f}_{N}(x))^{2}]
scaling as
O(1/N)
under suitable assumptions on the second moment of
F(x;\omega)
. (While finite approximation is not the focus of this paper, our earlier work
[
20
]
contains a discussion of sampling in the context of neural SDEs.)

I-B

Previous results

In an earlier work
[
20
]
, we have obtained some constructive realizability results for a certain class of neural SDEs. Briefly, we have considered
n
-dimensional Itô SDEs

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}

\displaystyle=a(X_{t};\theta)\dif t+b(X_{t};\theta)\dif V_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x

(5)

with finitely parametrized drift and diffusion coefficients satisfying the uniform ellipticity condition—the eigenvalues of the matrix
b(x;\theta)b(x;\theta)^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}
are uniformly bounded away from
0
. Let
v
be a fixed (nonrandom) vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We say that, for each choice of
\theta
, the above system
realizes
the function
f(x;\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathbf{E}}[Y]={\mathbf{E}}[v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}X_{1}]
of the initial condition
x
. We have shown that
f(x;\theta)
can be approximated by

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)\approx c_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}z\cdot\exp\big(-c_{2}I(x,z;\theta)\big)\dif z,

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are some constants and where
I(x,z;\theta)
is the minimum of the control energy
\frac{1}{2}\int^{1}_{0}|v(t)|^{2}\dif t
among all sufficiently regular (e.g.,
L^{2}
) controls
v\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that transfer the state of the deterministic control-affine system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=a(x(t);\theta)+b(x(t);\theta)v(t)

(6)

from
x(0)=x
to
x(1)=z
. Thus, the problem of characterizing the class of functions realized in this way reduces to analyzing deterministic minimum-energy control problems, and some upper and lower bounds on
I(x,z;\theta)
are given in
[
20
]
. However, the assumption of uniform ellipticity is rather restrictive and, in particular, rules out the interesting cases when the diffusion process in (
5
) is degenerate, yet the deterministic system (
6
) is nevertheless completely controllable
[
21
,
22
]
.

II
The basic model and its properties

We start by presenting a relatively simple model which will allow for a clean analysis. It has the following form:

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}

\displaystyle=a(W_{t};\theta)\dif t+b(W_{t};\theta)\circ\dif V_{t}

(7a)

\displaystyle\dif Z_{t}

\displaystyle=h(W_{t},t)Z_{t}\dif t

(7b)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\sigma(W_{1}^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x)

(7c)

Here, (
7a
) is a Stratonovich SDE for an
n
-dimensional diffusion process
(W_{t})
with a deterministic initial condition
W_{0}=w_{0}
. It is driven by an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion
(V_{t})
, and its drift and diffusion coefficients are parametrized by a
k
-dimensional vector of parameters
\theta
. We assume throughout that the drift and the diffusion coefficients are sufficiently well-behaved (e.g., Lipschitz) to guarantee existence and uniqueness of strong solutions of the SDE. The process
(Z_{t})
is scalar, initialized at
Z_{0}=1
, and
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a continuous function. The real-valued output
Y
is obtained by multiplying
Z_{1}
by
\sigma(W_{1}^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x)
.

Remark 1
.

By adding an equation
\dif X_{t}=0
initialized at
X_{0}=x
, we can view the above system as a stochastic variant of (
4
) with internal state
(W_{t},Z_{t},X_{t})
, read-in map

\displaystyle\alpha(x)=(w_{0},1,x),

and read-out map

\displaystyle\beta(w,z,x)=z\sigma(w^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x).

The output
Y
of (
7
) is a
random
function of
x
. We take its expected value as the function realized by this system:

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathbf{E}}[Z_{1}\sigma(W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{1}x)],

(8)

where in the left-hand side we have explicitly indicated the dependence of this function on the parameters
\theta
of the SDE (
7a
). The class of functions realized in this way can be characterized as follows:

Theorem 1
.

The function (
8
) realized by the system (
7
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)={\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\exp\Bigg(\int^{1}_{0}h(W_{t},t)\dif t\Bigg)\sigma(W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{1}x)\Bigg].

(9)

Moreover, let
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}
be the second-order linear differential operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\theta}\varphi(w)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tilde{a}(w;\theta)^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}\nabla\varphi(w)+\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\{b(w;\theta)b(w;\theta)^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}\nabla^{2}\varphi(w)\}

where

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(w;\theta)=a(w;\theta)+\frac{1}{2}\sum^{m}_{i=1}\frac{\partial b_{i}}{\partial w}(w;\theta)b_{i}(w;\theta)

is the Itô-corrected drift, and where
b_{i}(w;\theta)
is the
i
th column of
b(w;\theta)
. Consider the PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}(w,t;x,\theta)=\mathcal{L}^{\theta}u(w,t;x,\theta)+h(w,t)u(w,t;x,\theta)

(10)

with the initial condition
u(w,0;x,\theta)=\sigma(w^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x)
. If the function
w\mapsto\sigma(w^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x)
is in the domain of
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}
, then

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)=u(w_{0},1;x,\theta).

Proof.

The equation (
7b
) for
Z_{t}
can be solved for each trajectory
(W_{t})
:

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\exp\Bigg(\int^{t}_{0}h(W_{s},s)\dif s\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg(\int^{t}_{0}h(W_{s},s)\dif s\Bigg).

Substituting this into the expression for
Y
and taking expectations, we obtain (
9
).

The operator
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}
is the infinitesimal generator of the diffusion process
(W_{t})
governed by the SDE (
7a
). Since
w\mapsto\sigma(w^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}x)
is in the domain of
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}
, the function

\displaystyle u(w,t;x,\theta)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\exp\Bigg(\int^{t}_{0}h(W_{s},s)\dif s\Bigg)\sigma(W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{t}x)\Bigg|W_{0}=w\Bigg]

is a solution of (

by the (converse of) the Feynman–Kac theorem
[
23
, Thm. 21.1]
.
∎

A more elaborate set-up, in the spirit of
[
13
]
, is as follows:

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}

\displaystyle=a(W_{t};\theta)\dif t+b(W_{t};\theta)\circ\dif V_{t}

(11a)

\displaystyle\dif Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}(W_{t}Z_{t})\dif t

(11b)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}Z_{1}.

(11c)

The processes
(W_{t})
and
(Z_{t})
in Eqs. (
11a
) and (

) are now evolving in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, respectively, and

in (

) is a fixed vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. With the initial condition

fixed, we introduce the read-in map
\alpha(x)=(w_{0},x)
and the read-out map
\beta(w,z)=v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}z
. The stochastic dynamics in (
11a
) generates the matrices of weights
W_{t}
, which are used to control the dynamics of internal activations

in (
11b
). The cascade form of the system allows us to first generate the random trajectory

and then, conditionally on that, solve the ODE (

. In contrast to (

(Z_{t})
for a given realization

involves solving a time-inhomogeneous ODE

\displaystyle\frac{\dif Z_{t}}{\dif t}=g_{W_{t}}(Z_{t}),\,Z_{0}=x

(12)

where we have defined

\displaystyle g_{W_{t}}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}(W_{t}z).

(13)

We can compactly express the function realized by (
11
) using the chronological exponential notation

]
as

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)={\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}\overrightarrow{\exp}\left(\int^{1}_{0}g_{W_{t}}\dif t\right)x\Bigg],

which is just shorthand for the dependence of the solution of (
12
) on the initial condition
x
and on the trajectory
(W_{t})
, but any further analysis would involve asymptotic expansions in terms of iterated integrals and Lie derivatives.

II-A

The role of Lie theory

The above construction is quite general as it does not specify the form of the drift and the diffusion coefficients
a(w;\theta)
and
b(w;\theta)
in (
7a
) and in (
11a
).
A priori
we would expect that, in order to make the model (
7
) sufficiently expressive (i.e., to realize a sufficiently rich class of functions), we would need
a
and
b
to depend nonlinearly on both
w
and
\theta
. This intuition, however, is misleading since we can let the functions
h
and
\sigma
do all the “nonlinear work” when the Lie algebra generated by the vector fields
a(\cdot;\theta),b_{1}(\cdot;\theta),\dots,b_{m}(\cdot;\theta)
is finite-dimensional.

Thus, let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra of vector fields on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
be a basis of
\mathcal{A}
,
d=\dim\mathcal{A}
. For
\theta=(\theta_{ij})_{i=0,\dots,m,j=1,\dots d}
, take

\displaystyle a(w;\theta)=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\theta_{0j}g_{j}(w)

and

\displaystyle b_{i}(w;\theta)=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\theta_{ij}g_{j}(w),\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is finite-dimensional, it is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
{\rm gl}(\ell,{\mathbb{R}})
(the Lie algebra of
\ell\times\ell
real matrices with the commutator bracket
[A,B]=AB-BA
) for some finite
\ell
by Ado’s theorem
[
25
]
. Thus, without loss of generality (and by changing
n
if necessary) we may take
g_{i}(w)=G_{i}w
for some
G_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
, so (
7a
) will take the form

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}=A(\theta)W_{t}\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{i}(\theta)W_{t}\circ\dif V^{i}_{t},

(14)

where
V^{1}_{t},\dots,V^{m}_{t}
are
m
independent scalar Brownian motion processes, and where

\displaystyle A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\theta_{0j}G_{j},

\displaystyle B_{i}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\theta_{ij}G_{j},\quad i=1,\dots,m.

As an illustration of the expressive capabilities of this type of linear parametrization, we can consider Brockett’s construction of a diffusion process on the sphere
[
22
]
: Let
A,B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
be
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices and consider the
n
-dimensional Stratonovich SDE

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}=AW_{t}\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{i}W_{t}\circ\dif V^{i}_{t}.

(15)

or the equivalent Itô SDE

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}=\Bigg(A+\frac{1}{2}\sum^{m}_{i=1}B^{2}_{i}\Bigg)W_{t}\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{i}W_{t}\dif V^{i}_{t}.

Applying Itô’s rule gives

\displaystyle\dif\,(W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{t}W_{t})

\displaystyle=W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{t}\Bigg(A+A^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}+\sum^{m}_{i=1}B^{2}_{i}\Bigg)W_{t}\dif t

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum^{m}_{i=1}W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{t}(B_{i}+B^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{i})W_{t}\dif V^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum^{m}_{i=1}W^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{t}B^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}_{i}B_{i}W_{t}\dif t

\displaystyle=0

so the Euclidean norm
|W_{t}|
stays constant for all
t
. Thus, if we choose
w_{0}\in S^{n-1}
, then the random trajectory
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
will be confined to
S^{n-1}
. The algebra
{\rm Skew}(n)
of
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices has dimension
d=n(n-1)/2
, so we can express (
15
) in the form (
14
) with a vector
\theta
of
k=(m+1)n(n-1)/2
parameters.

Similar considerations apply to the matrix-valued case. For example, if
A,B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
are
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrices and the matrix Stratonovich SDE

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}=AW_{t}\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{i}W_{t}\circ\dif V^{i}_{t},

(16)

is initialized with
W_{0}=w_{0}\in O(n)
, then all
W_{t}
will take values in
O(n)
as well
[
22
]
.

With regards to the role of
\sigma
, let us consider the family
\mathcal{F}
of the vector fields
g_{W}(z)
defined in (
13
). With typical choices of the nonlinearity
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
(e.g., the hyperbolic tangent
\sigma(r)=\tanh r
), the Lie algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}
will be infinite-dimensional. To see this, suppose that
\sigma
is
C^{\infty}
. Let
g,g^{\prime}
denote the vector fields
g_{W},g_{W^{\prime}}
for two matrices
W,W^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then, using the formula

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\boldsymbol{\sigma}(Wz)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(1)}(Wz)W

for the Jacobian of
g_{W}
, where

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(1)}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\rm diag}\big(\sigma^{\prime}(z_{1}),\dots,\sigma^{\prime}(z_{n})\big),

a straightforward but tedious computation of the iterated Lie brackets

\displaystyle{\rm ad}^{k+1}_{g}g^{\prime}={\rm ad}_{g}({\rm ad}^{k}_{g}g^{\prime}),\quad k=0,1,\dots

where
{\rm ad}^{0}_{g}g^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g^{\prime}
and
{\rm ad}_{g}g^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[g,g^{\prime}],
yields vector fields whose coordinates involve derivatives of
\sigma
of arbitrary orders. As an example, consider the case
\sigma(r)=\tanh r
. Since the derivative of
\sigma
satisfies the relation
\sigma^{\prime}(r)=1-\sigma^{2}(r)
, the
k
th iterated Lie bracket
{\rm ad}^{k}_{g}g^{\prime}
will involve polynomials of degree
k+1
in the entries of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(Wz)
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(W^{\prime}z)
. Hence, using the linear independence of the monomials
1,r,r^{2},\dots
, it is easy to see that the Lie algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}
will be infinite-dimensional. Moreover, the Lie algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}
may be infinite-dimensional even if
\sigma
is a polynomial. Consider, for example,
\sigma(r)=1+r^{3}
; then even for
n=1
the Lie algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}
will contain polynomial vector fields of arbitrary degree (and thus any continuous function can be approximated arbitrarily well on any given compact set by some element of this Lie algebra
[
11
]
). This is in sharp contrast with the result of Leshno et al.
[
1
]
on the necessity of nonpolynomial continuous activation functions for universal approximation of continuous functions by neural nets with one hidden layer.

II-B

Elaborations and extensions

The basic model in (
7
) can be extended in various ways. For instance, we can consider matrix-valued processes
W_{t}
and replace the output equation (
7c
) with

\displaystyle Y=Z_{1}v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}(W_{1}x),

where
W_{t}
takes values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
and
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
is a fixed vector. Another possibility is to let
W_{t}=(U_{t},\tilde{W}_{t})
take values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
and let

\displaystyle Y=Z_{1}U_{1}^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\tilde{W}_{1}x).

If
h\equiv 0
in (
7b
), then the class of functions realized in this way is the closed convex hull of neural nets with one hidden layer and with arbitrarily many hidden units
[
26
]
. To see this, let
(U^{\nu},\tilde{W}^{\nu})
,
\nu=1,\dots,N
, be independent copies of
(U_{1},\tilde{W}_{1})
. (In other words, we just run
N
independent copies of the stochastic dynamics (
7a
) in parallel.) Then, assuming
U_{t}
evolves on the unit sphere

Finally, some comments on the generality of the considered models are in order. Both (
7
) and (
11
) have a cascade structure, where the stochastic dynamics of the weights
W_{t}
is autonomous (for each choice of the parameters
\theta

[

to the controlled dynamics of the internal activations
Z_{t}
. Moreover, the parameters

explicitly enter only the dynamics of

. However, we can consider other models of neural SDEs discussed in the literature, for example a stochastically excited continuous-time recurrent neural net

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}

\displaystyle=\big(-X_{t}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}(X_{t})\big)\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{i}X_{t}\circ\dif V^{i}_{t}

parametrized by

B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
. In models of this type, there is no explicit separation of the internal state into weights and activations. However, we can use the results of Freedman and Willems
[
28
]
and Krener and Lobry
[
29
]
to show that such neural SDEs can be
simulated
, in a certain sense, by systems of the cascade type.

To that end, consider the
n
-dimensional stochastic system

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}

\displaystyle=f(X_{t})\dif t+g(X_{t};\theta)\circ\dif V_{t}

(18a)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}X_{t}

(18b)

with initial condition
X_{0}=x
, where the columns of the
n\times m
matrix
g(x;\theta)
are smooth vector fields on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with a smooth dependence on a
k

\theta

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the Lie algebra
\mathcal{A}
generated by
g_{1}(\cdot;\theta),\dots,g_{m}(\cdot;\theta)
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, has finite dimension
d
. Then there exist smooth vector fields
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, smooth functions
\beta_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq d
,
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and an almost surely positive stopping time
\tau
, such that the cascade system

\displaystyle\dif W_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\Bigg(\sum^{d}_{j=1}\beta_{ij}(\theta)b_{j}(W_{t})\Bigg)\circ\dif V^{i}_{t}

(19a)

\displaystyle\dif Z_{t}

\displaystyle=h(Z_{t},W_{t})\dif t

(19b)

with
W_{0}=0
,
Z_{0}=x
simulates (
18
) up to time
\tau
, i.e.,

\displaystyle Y_{t}=v^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}\varphi(W_{t},Z_{t}),\qquad 0\leq t<\tau.

Remark 2
.

The above theorem shows that we can simulate a system like (
18
), at least locally, by a cascade system like (
19
) by means of a smooth reparametrization
\theta\mapsto(\beta_{ij}(\theta))
and a smooth “decoding map”
\varphi(w,z)=x
that converts the internal weights
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and internal activations
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
into the overall state
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Observe that the transformed parameters
\beta_{ij}(\theta)
enter into (
19a
) linearly and that the vector fields
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
and the maps
h
and
\varphi
do not have any dependence
\theta
. Moreover, since we have not imposed any restrictions on the drift vector field
f
(apart from the regularity conditions needed to ensure existence and uniqueness of Stratonovich solutions), the Lie algebra generated by the vector fields
h(\cdot,w)
may be infinite-dimensional.

Proof.

We closely follow the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
29
]
(the special case of Abelian
\mathcal{A}
was considered in an earlier paper by Freedman and Willems
[
28
]
). Let
\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{d}
be a basis of
\mathcal{A}
, so that

\displaystyle g_{i}(x;\theta)=\sum^{d}_{j=1}\beta_{ij}(\theta)\tilde{g}_{j}(x),\quad i=1,\dots,m

(20)

for some smooth maps
\beta_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Let

\displaystyle\varphi(w,z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{w_{1}\tilde{g}_{1}}\circ e^{w_{2}\tilde{g}_{2}}\circ\dots\circ e^{w_{d}\tilde{g}_{d}}z,

where
e^{t\tilde{g}_{i}}
denotes the flow map of
\tilde{g}_{i}
, i.e.,
e^{t\tilde{g}_{i}}z
is the solution, at time
t
, of the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{z}(t)=\tilde{g}_{i}(z(t)),\qquad z(0)=z.

We have
\varphi(0,x)=x
. Using the methods of
[
29
]
, we can show that there exist smooth vector fields
b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
(0,x)
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w}(w,z)b_{i}(w)=\tilde{g}_{i}(\varphi(w,z)),\qquad i=1,\dots,m

for all
(w,z)\in\mathcal{U}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w}(w,z)\bigg(\sum^{d}_{j=1}\beta_{ij}(\theta)b_{i}(w)\bigg)=g_{i}(\varphi(w,z);\theta)

for all
1\leq i\leq d
and all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Now,
\varphi(w,z)
is equal to the solution, at
t=d
, of the time-inhomogeneous ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(t)=v(\xi(t),t),\qquad z(0)=z

with

\displaystyle v(z,t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=w_{d-i+1}\tilde{g}_{d-i+1}(z),\qquad i-1\leq t<i

and the Jacobian
\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z}(w,z)
is equal to the solution, at

\displaystyle\dot{\Lambda}(t)=\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}(\xi(t),t)\Lambda(t),\qquad\Lambda(0)=I_{n}.

Hence, it is invertible, so we take

\displaystyle h(z,w)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigg(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z}(w,z)\bigg)^{-1}f(\varphi(w,z))

for all
(w,z)\in\mathcal{U}
. Let
(W_{t},Z_{t})
be the solution of (
19

(W_{0},Z_{0})=(0,x)
, and let
\tau
be the first exit time from
\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\{t>0\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(W_{t},Z_{t})\not\in\mathcal{U}\}.

Then, since Stratonovich differentials obey the rules of ordinary calculus, for
0\leq t<\tau
we have

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}

\displaystyle=\dif\varphi(W_{t},Z_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w}(W_{t},Z_{t})\circ\dif W_{t}+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z}(W_{t},Z_{t})\circ\dif Z_{t}

\displaystyle=f(\varphi(W_{t},Z_{t}))\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}g_{i}(\varphi(W_{t},Z_{t});\theta)\circ\dif V^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=f(X_{t})\dif t+\sum^{m}_{i=1}g_{i}(X_{t};\theta)\circ\dif V^{i}_{t}

and
