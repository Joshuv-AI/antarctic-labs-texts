Anomaly Detection by Context Contrasting
Alain Ryser, Thomas M. Sutter, Alexander Marx, Julia E. Vogt

Abstract
Anomaly Detection focuses on identifying samples that deviate from the norm. When working with high-dimensional data such as images, a crucial requirement for detecting anomalous patterns is learning lower-dimensional representations that capture normal concepts seen during training. Recent advances in self-supervised learning have shown great promise in this regard. However, many of the most successful self-supervised anomaly detection methods assume prior knowledge about the structure of anomalies and leverage synthetic anomalies during training. Yet, in many real-world applications, we do not know what to expect from unseen data, and we can solely leverage knowledge about normal data. In this work, we propose Con 2 \text{Con}_{2} , which addresses this problem by setting normal training data into distinct contexts while preserving its normal properties, letting us observe the data from different perspectives. Unseen normal data consequently adheres to learned context representations while anomalies fail to do so, letting us detect them without any knowledge about anomalies during training. Our experiments demonstrate that our approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on various benchmarks while exhibiting superior performance in a more realistic healthcare setting, where knowledge about potential anomalies is often scarce.



1
Introduction

In anomaly detection (AD), our goal is to detect samples that deviate from the normal structure encountered during training
(
Ruff et al. 2021
)
.
Reliably detecting anomalies is essential in many safety-critical domains such as healthcare
(
Schlegl et al. 2017
;
Ryser et al. 2022
)
, finance
(
Golmohammadi and Zaiane 2015
)
, industrial fault detection
(
Atha and Jahanshahi 2018
;
Zhao et al. 2019
)
, or cyber-security
(
Xin et al. 2018
)
.
In healthcare, a common real-world example of AD is the standard screening process where doctors examine all patients for any anomalies. These datasets predominantly comprise samples from the healthy population since most individuals screened do not exhibit any diseases. The challenge lies in the specific types of anomalies that may arise during the screening, which are unknown beforehand.
Traditionally, algorithms such as isolation forest
(
Liu et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2012
)
, local outlier factor
(
Breunig et al. 2000
)
, one-class support vector machine (SVM)
(
Schölkopf et al. 2001
)
, or support vector data description (SVDD)
(
Tax and Duin 2004
)
have shown great promise toward general AD.
However, such methods are only applicable to lower-dimensional, informative data, as the curse of dimensionality makes it difficult to directly apply many of these methods to higher-dimensional datasets
(
Zimek et al. 2012
)
, requiring new solutions to the problem.

Learning appropriate representations of high-dimensional normal data, such as images, through deep representation learning has recently become a popular way of approaching this problem.
One of the earliest works on deep anomaly detection was deep SVDD
(
Ruff et al. 2018
)
, which introduced a model performing hypersphere compression by mapping normal data close to the center of a hypersphere to learn a compact representation of normality.
However, this approach is prone to so-called hypersphere collapse, where all representations collapse into a single point.
Much effort has been put into circumventing this problem, e.g., by introducing various kinds of regularization techniques
(
Oza and Patel 2018
;
Sabokrou et al. 2020
;
Chong et al. 2020
)
.
While regularization can help map samples to a concentrated space without collapse, the learned representations are often not very informative.
In recent years, various works have demonstrated that learning representations in a contrastive manner leads to more informative representations of normal data, improving AD over purely hypersphere compression-based methods
(
Sehwag et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
.
Some approaches have further demonstrated that incorporating synthetic anomalies into contrastive methods can encourage anomalous representations to be different from normal ones
(
Tack et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
However, representations learned by these methods tend to be less concentrated compared to hypersphere compression.
Additionally, simulating anomalies may not be feasible in real-world settings, which can make it difficult to apply these methods in practice.

In this work, we present

Our approach leverages the fact that there are certain transformations, so-called context augmentations, that set the whole dataset into a different context while preserving the normality of samples relative to each other.
This observation allows us to introduce a new contrastive loss function that lets us learn
context-specific

By further encouraging clusters to be
content-aligned
, we ensure a highly informative structure within each cluster by preserving the relative normality of samples independent of the context.
At test time,
\text{Con}_{2}
allows us to detect anomalies by measuring how well new samples align with the learned context clusters after transforming them into different contexts.
In summary, our contributions include

•

The introduction of context augmentations, which leverage knowledge about invariances in normal data, letting us examine samples from different perspectives.

•

A new contrastive loss function,
\text{Con}_{2}
, that applies context augmentations to learn highly informative, tightly clustered representations of normal data.

•

Experimental evaluation of our new method, achieving state-of-the-art performance on various benchmarks and superior performance on a realistic experiment in the healthcare domain, demonstrating the applicability and versatility of our method in various settings.

In the following, we will first provide some background and related work.
We then proceed to introduce our method and demonstrate its capability in various anomaly detection settings.

2
Background

In this section, we first provide the required background about the anomaly detection setting and the terminology for contrastive learning before discussing the related work.

Anomaly Detection

In the anomaly detection setting, we are given an unlabeled dataset
\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\}=X\subset\mathcal{X}
, while assuming that most samples are normal, i.e., the dataset is practically free of outliers
(
Ruff et al. 2021
)
.
The goal is to learn a model from the given dataset that discriminates between normal and anomalous data at test time.
Throughout this work, we assume the challenging case where our dataset is completely free of anomalies.
Hence, we aim to discriminate between the normal class and a completely unobserved set of anomalies at test time.
This setting is sometimes called one-class classification or novelty detection.
To achieve this goal, one straightforward approach is to approximate the distribution
p_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x})
directly using generative models
(
An and Cho 2015
;
Schlegl et al. 2019
)
.
Because we assume normal data to lie in high-density regions of
p_{\mathcal{X}}
, we can discriminate between normal and anomalous samples by applying a threshold function
p_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x})\leq\tau
, where
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
is an often task-specific threshold
(
Bishop 1994
)
.
As density-based approaches are often difficult to apply to high-dimensional data directly
(
Nalisnick et al. 2018
)
, we follow a slightly different line of work.
In this paper, we focus on learning a function
g_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
that provides us with representations that capture the normal attributes of samples in the dataset
(
Sehwag et al. 2021
;
Tack et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
, by mapping normal samples close to each other in representation space.
On the other hand, anomalies that lack the learned normal structure should be mapped to a different part of the representation space.
Given
g_{\theta}(\bm{x})
, a popular approach to detect anomalies is by defining a scoring function
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(
Breunig et al. 2000
;
Schölkopf et al. 2001
;
Tax and Duin 2004
;
Liu et al. 2008
)
.
The score function maps a representation onto a metric that estimates the anomalousness of a sample.
To identify anomalies at test time, we can use
\mathcal{S}
similarly to the density
p_{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e., we consider a new sample
\bm{x}
to be normal if
\mathcal{S}(g_{\theta}(\bm{x}))\leq\tau
, whereas
\mathcal{S}(g_{\theta}(\bm{x}))>\tau
means
\bm{x}
is an anomaly.

Contrastive Learning

Recently, contrastive learning has emerged as a popular approach for representation learning
(
Oord et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
.
By design, contrastive learning has the capability to learn representations that are agnostic to certain invariances
(
von Kügelgen et al. 2021
;
Daunhawer et al. 2023
)
, which makes contrastive learning a particularly interesting choice to learn informative representations of normal samples
(
Tack et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
, as it allows us to incorporate prior knowledge about our data into the representing learning process in the form of data augmentations.
More specifically, invariances are learned by forming positive and negative pairs over the training dataset by applying data augmentations that should retain the relevant content of a sample.
The goal is to learn an encoding function
g_{\theta}(\bm{x})
, where representations of positive pairs of samples are close and negative pairs are far from each other.
For a given pair of samples
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in X
, we can define the instance discrimination loss as
(
Sohn 2016
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Oord et al. 2019
)

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime},X)=-\log\frac{\exp{(\text{sim}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})/\tau)}}{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}\in X:\;\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}\neq\bm{x}\end{subarray}}\exp{(\text{sim}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})/\tau)}}\;.

Here, the function
\text{sim}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
corresponds to a function that measures the similarity between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
.
For the rest of our work, we assume
\text{sim}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
to be the cosine similarity between the two input vectors, as this is one of the most popular choices in the contrastive learning literature.
One of the most prominent contrastive methods is SimCLR
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, which creates positive pairs through sample augmentations.
There exists a supervised extension called SupCon
(
Khosla et al. 2021
)
, which incorporates class labels into the SimCLR loss.
For a given set of augmentations
T
, a dataset
X=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and an augmented dataset
\tilde{X}
where
|\tilde{X}|=2N
and
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i},y_{i})
,
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i+1},y_{i})\in\tilde{X}
denote two transformations of the same sample using random augmentations from
T
, SimCLR and SupCon introduce the following loss functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SimCLR}}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\ell(f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i}),f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i+1}),f_{\Theta}(\tilde{X}))+\ell(f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i+1}),f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i}),f_{\Theta}(\tilde{X}))\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}(\tilde{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in\tilde{X}}\frac{1}{N(y_{i})-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j},y_{j})\in\tilde{X}:\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\neq\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\land y_{i}=y_{j}\end{subarray}}\ell(f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}),f_{\Theta}(\tilde{X}))\;.

Here,
\Theta=\{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\}
and
N(y)=|\{(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\mid(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in\tilde{X}\land y_{i}=y\}|
denotes the number of samples in
\tilde{X}
with label
y
.
We further denote
f_{\Theta}(\tilde{X})=\{f_{\Theta}(\tilde{\bm{x}})\mid(\tilde{\bm{x}},y)\in\tilde{X}\}
and
f_{\Theta}(\bm{x})=h_{\theta^{\prime}}(g_{\theta}(\bm{x}))
, where
\bm{z}=g_{\theta}(\bm{x})
is a feature extractor and
h_{\theta^{\prime}}(\bm{z})
is a projection head that is typically only used during training
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
.

Related Work

Recently, learning useful normal representations of high-dimensional data to perform anomaly detection has become a popular line of research.
Prior work has tackled the problem from various angles, for instance, using the hypersphere compression approach mentioned earlier
(
Ruff et al. 2018
)
.
Another popular approach is to define pretext tasks such as learning reconstruction models
(
Chen et al. 2017
;
Zong et al. 2018
;
You et al. 2019
)
or predicting data transformations
(
Golan and El-Yaniv 2018
;
Hendrycks et al. 2019
;
Bergman and Hoshen 2019
)
.
While these approaches had some success in the past, the learned representations are not informative enough and outperformed by simple contrastive methods
(
Sun et al. 2022
;
Sehwag et al. 2021
)
.
Another line of work focused on estimating the training density with the help of generative models, detecting anomalies as samples from low probability regions
(
An and Cho 2015
;
Schlegl et al. 2019
;
Nachman and Shih 2020
;
Mirzaei et al. 2022
)
.
However, these methods tend to generalize better to unseen distributions than to the observed training distribution
(
Nalisnick et al. 2018
)
.
In addition to the traditional setting, where we assume training data without any labels, some works have slightly weakened this restriction and assumed access to a limited number of labeled samples.
This setting is called anomaly detection with Outlier Exposure (OE)
(
Hendrycks et al. 2018
)
, and it has been shown that even just a few labeled samples can greatly boost performance over an unlabeled dataset
(
Ruff et al. 2019
;
Qiu et al. 2022
;
Liznerski et al. 2022
)
.
Using large, pretrained models as feature extractors is a special case of OE, as additional data is not explicitly accessible.
Some approaches have been introduced that use representations from pretrained models directly in zero-shot fashion
(
Bergman et al. 2020
;
Liznerski et al. 2022
)
, while others demonstrate the benefit of fine-tuning
(
Cohen and Avidan 2022
;
Reiss and Hoshen 2023
)
.
OE has been very successful in the past, often outperforming traditional AD settings across many benchmarks, though at the cost of either requiring labeled samples or vast amounts of data for pretraining, which are both often not available or hard to obtain in more specialized domains.
Another setting that has recently gained popularity is out-of-distribution (OOD) detection.
In OOD detection, we have additional information about our dataset in the form of labels.
Anomaly detection is thus a special case of OOD detection with only a single label.
While the problem is similar, most approaches that tackle OOD detection make specific use of a classifier trained on the dataset labels
(
Hendrycks and Gimpel 2017
;
Lee et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
, which AD.

In comparison, our method operates in the traditional anomaly detection setting and can be applied to datasets without any knowledge about anomalies.
Further, while we do assume access to a dataset containing only normal samples, our method does not rely on any additional labels, as they are potentially difficult and expensive to obtain, particularly in more specialized settings.

3
Methods

In this section, we first provide the intuition behind our approach and introduce the notion of context augmentation.
Subsequently, we propose context contrasting, which allows us to learn aligned, context-clustered representations of normal data.
Finally, we present how to leverage these representations to detect anomalies at test time.

3.1
Context Augmentation

Prior work on contrastive anomaly detection either applied anomaly scores directly on top of representations learned through standard contrastive methods
(
Sehwag et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
or by simulating anomalies by applying certain image transformations, such as rotations or image permutations, to normal data to create semantically meaningful, anomaly-like data
(
Tack et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
These transformed samples are usually incorporated into contrastive approaches as negative samples that should be mapped far from observed normal samples in the representation space.
There are some heuristics for finding suitable transformations that work well on natural images, but designing appropriate augmentations remains challenging in practice, and we may need to rely on prior knowledge about anomalies to design them.
In contrast, our method does not require explicit knowledge about anomalies but solely relies on the properties of normal data.
More specifically, we leverage the observation that certain augmentations can transform normality into another context, creating a new anomaly detection task that relies on the same normal attributes as the original dataset but is observed from a different perspective.
This observation allows us to learn different context-specific representations that align in content, letting us detect anomalies as samples that do not conform to the learned structure.
Here, let
X
be our dataset containing normal samples, let
t_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
be a data augmentation, and let
X^{\prime}=\{t_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x})\mid\bm{x}\in X\}
be the dataset where samples are transformed with
t_{\mathcal{C}}
.
We then call
t_{\mathcal{C}}
a
context augmentation
if it fulfills the following properties:

Distinctiveness
There are no two samples
\bm{x}\in X
,
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
such that
\bm{x}\approx\bm{x}^{\prime}
, i.e., there is a clear distinction between the original and the context augmented distribution after applying
t_{\mathcal{C}}
.
For instance, if our normal class consists of airplanes, horizontal flipping would violate distinctiveness, as planes can naturally appear to fly to the left or the right.
Conversely, vertical flipping fulfills distinctiveness because we will most probably not encounter a plane flying upside down in a dataset containing normal samples.

Alignment
Let
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in X
, and let
\text{d}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
denote an appropriate similarity measure for samples in the input space.
Then, we require that
\text{d}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\approx\text{d}(t_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}),t_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}))
, i.e., originally similar normal samples should stay just as similar in the new context, meaning that the original and the context-augmented normal distributions should align.
For instance, in the previous airplane example, a transformation that masks out a part of a sample would violate alignment, as transforming two images of different planes in the sky could remove the areas containing the actual planes.
In this case, we would be left with the blue sky remaining, making the samples practically identical despite originally displaying different airplanes.
On the other hand, an augmentation such as color inversion keeps the original structure of a sample while changing only the style of the image, avoiding such a scenario.

Some examples of context augmentation that fulfill distinctiveness and alignment for natural images and x-ray datasets would be vertical flipping (Flip), color inversion (Invert), or histogram equalization (Equalize).
We present some examples of these augmentations in
Figure

2
.

3.2
Context Contrasting

In the following, we formalize the intuition outlined above and demonstrate how to use context augmentations to learn informative normal representations.
In particular, we leverage context augmentations to learn dense, context-specific representation clusters using our new
context contrasting
loss while keeping the representations across clusters aligned by applying
content alignment
.

First, we want to learn a concentrated space where representations are tightly packed close to each other, which has been shown to be crucial to performing effective AD
(
Ruff et al. 2021
)
.
By leveraging the distinctiveness property of context augmentations, we can learn tightly concentrated, context-specific representation clusters while avoiding the hypersphere collapse problem mentioned in
Section

1
.
To achieve this, we introduce a novel contrastive loss inspired by supervised contrastive learning, which encourages representations of samples to be clustered according to their context.
More concretely, assume we are given a set of normal samples
X_{\text{train}}
, a context augmentation
t_{\mathcal{C}}
, a set of content-preserving augmentations
\mathcal{T}
like in
Chen et al. 2020
, and let

X_{\mathcal{C}}=\{(\bm{x},0)\mid\bm{x}\in X_{\text{train}}\}\cup\{(t_{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{x}),1)\mid\bm{x}\in X_{\text{train}}\}

denote our context-augmented dataset, where we label each sample with its context.
Similar to SupCon
(
Khosla et al. 2021
)
and SimCLR
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, we then apply augmentations from
\mathcal{T}
to our dataset to create
\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}}
, where
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i},y_{i})
,
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i+1},y_{i})\in\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}}
denote two transformations of the same context-augmented sample using random augmentations from
\mathcal{T}
.
More specifically, for
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
,
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i}=t(\bm{x}^{\mathcal{C}}_{i})
and
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2i+1}=t^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{\mathcal{C}}_{i})
, where
\bm{x}^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\in X_{\mathcal{C}}
.
To learn context-specific representation clusters, we can then define our
context contrasting
loss as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Context}}(\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}}}\frac{1}{2N-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j},y_{j})\in\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}}\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\neq\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\land y_{i}=y_{j}\end{subarray}}\ell(f_{\Phi}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),f_{\Phi}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j})),

where, similar to
Chen et al. 2020
,
f_{\Phi}(\bm{x})=h_{\phi}(g_{\theta}(\bm{x}))
projects representations
\bm{z}=g_{\theta}(\bm{x})
with a projection head
h_{\phi}(\bm{z})
that gets discarded after training.
Intuitively, context contrasting encourages representations of the same context to be clustered together while pushing other context clusters away.
While this lets us learn concentrated context clusters, the representations within each cluster potentially learn different attributes of the normal training data.

We thus need an additional tool to align the representations across contexts to ensure context representations capture the same normal attributes.
Ideally, we want representations to have the same relative positions to each other in either of the context clusters, which would require our model to learn context-invariant properties of normality.
We propose to align clusters in a contrastive fashion through instance discrimination across context clusters.
More specifically, let
\Lambda(i)=\{2i,2i+1,4i,4i+1\}
, i.e.,
\Lambda(i)
corresponds to all indices of samples in
\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}}
which are augmentations of the original sample
\bm{x}_{i}\in X
.
We then define the
content alignment
loss as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Content}}(\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i\in\Lambda(k)}\sum_{j\in\Lambda(k)\setminus i}\ell(f_{\Psi}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),f_{\Psi}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j})),

where
f_{\Psi}(\bm{x})=h_{\psi}(g_{\theta}(\bm{x}))
, and
h_{\psi}
denotes another projection head that is different from
h_{\phi}
.
Content alignment ensures that all representations of the same normal sample can be matched across different contexts, encouraging alignment of the representations within each context cluster.

Finally, we combine context contrasting and content alignment to our loss function
\text{Con}_{2}
, which enables us to learn
context-specific, content-aligned
representations of normality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Con}_{2}}(\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{Context}}(\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}})+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{Content}}(\tilde{X}_{\mathcal{C}})

To account for the different scaling of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Context}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Content}}
, we need to introduce a weighting factor
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
We discuss our specific choice for
\alpha
in
Section

4.1
.

3.3
Anomaly Score

To detect anomalies with the representations learned through
\text{Con}_{2}
, we need to define a score function
\mathcal{S}(\bm{x})
that specifies the anomalousness of a given test sample
\bm{x}
.
In this work, we propose two simple ways of defining an anomaly score.
Our first approach follows a simple non-parametric nearest neighbor approach using the cosine similarity on the representation space similar to
(
Bergman et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
.
Specifically, we define the cosine distance between the training set
X_{\text{train}}
and a given test sample
\bm{x}
with transformation
t
as

s_{\text{NND}}(\bm{x};t)=-\max\limits_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in X_{\text{train}}}\frac{\langle g_{\theta}(t(\bm{x})),g_{\theta}(t(\bm{x}^{\prime}))\rangle}{\|g_{\theta}(t(\bm{x}))\|\|g_{\theta}(t(\bm{x}^{\prime}))\|}

While this approach works very well in practice, to efficiently compute
s_{\text{NND}}(\bm{x},t)
, we need to store the representations of all samples in
X_{\text{train}}
, which can be costly.
To address this problem, we propose another score function that is based on the likelihood of a multivariate normal distribution, which is more compute and memory-efficient and performs only slightly worse then
s_{\text{NND}}
in our experiments.
More specifically, we leverage the fact that we learn context clusters and compute the probability density function of a multivariate normal distribution based on the empirical mean
\overline{\mu}(g_{\theta}(t(X)))
and covariance
\overline{\Sigma}(g_{\theta}(t(X)))
given a transformation
t
, where
g_{\theta}(t(X))=\{g_{\theta}(t(\bm{x}))\mid\bm{x}\in X\}
.
We thus define

s_{\text{LH}}(\bm{x};t)=\mathcal{N}(g_{\theta}(t(\bm{x}))\mid\overline{\mu}(g_{\theta}(t(X))),\overline{\Sigma}(g_{\theta}(t(X)))).

Similar to previous works
(
Tack et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
, we further leverage test-time augmentations to increase the anomaly detection performance compared to using a single sample.
More formally, let
A
be a hyperparameter and let
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{2A}\}
be a set of
2A
test time augmentations.
We then define our final anomaly score functions
\mathcal{S}_{D}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{D}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{i=1}^{A}s_{D}(\bm{x};t_{i})+\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{i=A}^{2A}s_{D}(\bm{x};t_{i}\circ t_{\mathcal{C}})))\;\text{, where }D\in\{\text{NND},\text{LH}\}.

In the following, we first outline our experimental setting and briefly describe the baselines used in our experiments.
We then evaluate
\text{Con}_{2}
on various popular natural image benchmark datasets, demonstrating the state-of-the-art performance of our method.
Finally, we perform an experiment in a more realistic medical setting, where
\text{Con}_{2}
achieves superior performance over methods that leverage prior knowledge about anomalies, which is difficult to obtain in such a setting.

4.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate our method in the so-called one-class classification setting
(
Ruff et al. 2021
)
.
There, we typically use a classification dataset such as CIFAR10
(
Krizhevsky and Hinton 2009
)
or ImageNet30
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
;
Hendrycks et al. 2019
)
and train only on samples of a single class.
At test time, the goal is to detect whether a new sample stems from the normal class seen during training or whether it belongs to one of the remaining classes that have not been encountered.
Typically, there is a high-class imbalance between normal and anomalous samples in the one-class classification setting.
Further, setting an appropriate threshold for the anomaly score is often task-dependent.
Therefore, a popular approach to evaluating the performance of anomaly detection methods is to use the area under the receiver operator characteristic curve (AUROC)
(
Ruff et al. 2021
)
.
This metric is threshold agnostic and robust to class imbalance.
We compare our work to a number of contrastive anomaly detection baselines, such as SSD
(
Sehwag et al. 2021
)
, CSI
(
Tack et al. 2020
)
, and UniCon-HA
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
We further compare against a baseline that learns SimCLR embeddings and detects samples in nearest neighbor fashion similar to KNN+
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
, which was originally developed for out-of-distribution detection.
To ensure comparability, we run all experiments with the same ResNet18 architecture
(
He et al. 2016
)
.
Similar to our method, all baselines make use of test-time augmentations.
By default, both CSI and UniCon-HA use
40
test time augmentations, which we adopt for all baselines.
In our experiments, we set the augmentation class
\mathcal{T}
to the set of augmentations introduced by
Chen et al. 2020
.
For the context augmentation, we experiment with vertical flips (Flip), inverting the pixels of an image (Invert), i.e.,
t_{\text{Invert}}(\bm{x}_{ij})=1-\bm{x}_{ij}
, and histogram equalization (Equalize), see
Figure

2
for an illustration.
We choose hyperparameters for
\text{Con}_{2}
based on their performance on the CIFAR10 dataset and keep them constant across all experiments.
Further, we linearly anneal the hyperparameter
\alpha
in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Con}_{2}}
from
0
to
1
over the course of training to encourage the model to first learn the context-specific cluster structure while gradually aligning representations over the course of training.
We optimize our loss using the AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2018
)
, keeping its hyperparameters to the default settings of pytorch
(
Ansel et al. 2024
)
(
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
, weight decay
\lambda=0.01
), and using a learning rate of
10^{-3}
with a cosine annealing
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2022
)
schedule.
We run all experiments for
2048
epochs.
For all our experiments, we report mean and standard deviation over three seeds per class of the dataset.
Note that the average results of a dataset are thus aggregated over different one-class classification settings, one per class of the dataset.

Broader Impact & Limitations

While anomaly detection methods can bring many societal benefits, e.g., by supporting doctors in standard screening settings or detecting adverse samples in safety-critical systems, one should be very careful when designing datasets that should be considered normal.
Biases or the underrepresentation of certain groups could potentially lead to discrimination in the form of false alarms.

Our work mainly focuses on image-based anomaly detection and we do not include any experiments involving different modalities such as time-series or even multimodal data.
Nonetheless, our definition of
\text{Con}_{2}
is rather general, and it would be interesting to investigate whether the longitudinal structure of time-series or the different modalities in multimodal data could directly serve as suitable context augmentations without the need for hand-crafted transformations.
Further, we empirically demonstrate how
\text{Con}_{2}
leads to highly informative representations of normality.
However, we do not provide any theoretical guarantees for our embeddings, and it would be interesting to see how our approach compares to other representation learning methods outside anomaly detection.
Finally, extending our method to the outlier exposure or the out-of-distribution detection setting could be another interesting direction for future work.

Conclusion

In this work, we focused on anomaly detection from image data.
We identified that although methods based on self-supervised representation learning show promising results in this area, their reliance on prior knowledge of the structure of anomalies is a limitation. As such knowledge might not be available in real-world settings, we proposed
\text{Con}_{2}
instead.
Our
\text{Con}_{2}
approach lets us learn representations of normal data by leveraging context augmentations.
These transformations set the normal space into a new context, allowing us to observe normal data from different perspectives and thus learn context-specific representation clusters aligned according to the properties of the normal samples in the dataset.
We finally demonstrated how our new representation learning method allows us to detect anomalies by introducing two anomaly scores that measure sample anomalousness by how much a representation deviates from the learned context cluster.
In our experiments, we demonstrate the versatility and robustness of our method to detect anomalies in various one-class classification tasks on natural images and a real-world medical dataset.
In conclusion,
\text{Con}_{2}
is a reliable approach to learning highly informative representations of normality across various settings without making any assumption about anomalies, which is especially useful in safety-critical domains such as healthcare.
