Abstract
In this work, we focus on the study of stochastic zeroth-order (ZO) optimization which does not require first-order gradient information and uses only function evaluations. The problem of ZO optimization has emerged in many recent machine learning applications, where the gradient of the objective function is either unavailable or difficult to compute. In such cases, we can approximate the full gradients or stochastic gradients through function value based gradient estimates. Here, we propose a novel hybrid gradient estimator (HGE), which takes advantage of the query-efficiency of random gradient estimates as well as the variance-reduction of coordinate-wise gradient estimates. We show that with a graceful design in coordinate importance sampling, the proposed HGE-based ZO optimization method is efficient both in terms of iteration complexity as well as function query cost. We provide a thorough theoretical analysis of the convergence of our proposed method for non-convex, convex, and strongly-convex optimization. We show that the convergence rate that we derive generalizes the results for some prominent existing methods in the nonconvex case, and matches the optimal result in the convex case. We also corroborate the theory with a real-world black-box attack generation application to demonstrate the empirical advantage of our method over state-of-the-art ZO optimization approaches.



I
Introduction

Derivative-free optimization (DFO) methods
[
1
,
2
]
have become increasingly popular in recent years, owing to the advent of several machine learning applications where the analytical expressions of the objective functions are either expensive or infeasible to obtain. Some examples of such applications are black-box adversarial example generation in deep neural networks (DNNs), reinforcement learning, and control and management of time-varying networks with limited computational resources
[
3
]
.

Zeroth-order (ZO) methods form a special class of DFO methods which can be seen as gradient-less versions of first-order (gradient-based) optimization methods.
ZO optimization involves approximating the full/stochastic gradient of the function using only the function values, and using this gradient estimator in the first-order (FO) optimization framework.
Advantages of ZO-methods, over conventional DFO methods like direct-search based methods
[
4
]
, and trust-region methods
[
5
]
, are the ease of implementation and the convergence properties of these methods, owing to their theoretical
closeness
to FO methods.
ZO methods in the literature often have convergence rates comparable to FO methods, with an additional small-degree polynomial in the problem dimension
d

[
6
,
7
]
.

However, ZO algorithms often suffer from the high variance of gradient estimates.
The existing estimators involve choosing between saving on the function query cost
[
8
]
, and achieving higher accuracy of the gradient estimates
[
9
]
.
In this work, we propose a novel gradient
21
estimator which traverses the entire spectrum between these two extremes.
Essentially, the proposed algorithm, based on our novel estimator, improves the variance of the gradient estimator, while also saving on the function query budget.

The general ZO approach involves computing a gradient estimate
\hat{\nabla}f(\mathbf{x})
of function
f
at the point
\mathbf{x}
, and then plugging this estimate into a FO method. One way to estimate the gradient is by querying the function at a single randomly chosen point in the vicinity of
\mathbf{x}

[
10
]
.
More effectively, multi-point (e.g., two-point) approaches are used
[
11
,
7

, leading to better variance and improved complexity results.

The initial work on multi-point estimators was largely limited to convex problems.
For smooth, deterministic problems,
[
7
]
proposed the ZO gradient descent (ZO-GD) algorithm and proved
O(d/T)

A ZO-mirror descent algorithm
[

The authors also proved the result to be

[

In the nonconvex domain, the first stochastic algorithm, ZO-SGD

O(\sqrt{d}/\sqrt{T})

The same rate was achieved by

In
[
9

Following the recent progress in variance reduction methods for first-order optimization: SAGA

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we begin by presenting the formulation of the black-box optimization problems of our interest. We then review two commonly-used ZO gradient estimators, random gradient estimator (RGE) and coordinate-wise gradient estimator (CGE).
We shall define the notation wherever we introduce a new mathematical entity. We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for a tabular summary of all the notations used.

We consider the following black-box stochastic optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\Xi}F(\mathbf{x};\xi),

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}
denotes the
d
-dimensional optimization variable,

\xi\in\Xi
denotes a stochastic variable with distribution
\Xi
(e.g., distribution of training samples),
and

is a smooth, possibly nonconvex loss function.
By black-box, we mean that the objective function in (
1

To enable theoretical analysis, we impose two commonly-used assumptions on problem (
1
).

Assumption 1
.

Gradient Lipschitz continuity:

The loss function

in (
1
) has Lipschitz continuous gradient with parameter
L

f(\mathbf{y})\leq f(\mathbf{x})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}_{2}
, for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
, where
\nabla f
denotes the gradient of
f
.

Assumption 2
.

Bounded variance of stochastic gradients:

Suppose
(\mathbf{x})_{i}
denotes the
i
th coordinate of a vector
\mathbf{x}
, and
\zeta
is a given constant, then
\mathbb{E}[(\nabla F(\mathbf{x};\xi)-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}))_{i}^{2}]\leq\zeta^{2},\forall\ i
.

Assumption
1
is fairly standard in the theoretical analysis of nonconvex optimization
[
8
]
.
Assumption
2
enables a finer control on the variance of CGE
[
9
,
31
]
. It also implies that
\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x};\xi)-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\triangleq d\zeta^{2}
.

II-
0a
RGE.

Considering a set of
random
directional vectors
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{r}}}
, RGE of the individual loss function
F(\mathbf{x};\xi)
is given by the average of the finite difference approximations of the directional derivatives of
F(\mathbf{x};\xi)
along these random directions
[
13
,
32
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{RGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\xi)=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\frac{d\left[F(\mathbf{x}+\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{u}_{i};\xi)-F(\mathbf{x};\xi)\right]}{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}\mathbf{u}_{i},

(2)

where
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}>0
is a perturbation radius (also called smoothing parameter), and each
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is drawn from the uniform distribution on a unit sphere
U_{0}
centered at
\mathbf{0}

[
13
]
.
We define the smooth approximation of a function
g(\mathbf{x})
with smoothing parameter
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}
as
g_{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}[g(\mathbf{x}+\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{u})]
.
The rationale behind RGE (
2
) is that
\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{RGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\xi)
is an
unbiased
estimate of
\nabla F_{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}(\mathbf{x};\xi)
, leading to

\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}}[\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{RGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\xi)]=\nabla f_{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}(\mathbf{x})
. Note that

\nabla f_{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}(\mathbf{x})
itself is a biased approximation of the true gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
, with the bias controlled by
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}
.

II-
0b
CGE.

Different from RGE, CGE is constructed by finite difference approximations of directional derivatives along the canonical basis vectors
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
i
-th component of CGE is defined as

[
33
]

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{i}(\mathbf{x};\xi)=\frac{\left[F(\mathbf{x}+\mu_{\mathrm{c},i}\mathbf{e}_{i};\xi)-F(\mathbf{x}-\mu_{\mathrm{c},i}\mathbf{e}_{i};\xi)\right]}{2\mu_{\mathrm{c},i}}\mathbf{e}_{i},

(3)

where
\mu_{\mathrm{c},i}>0
denotes the coordinate-wise smoothing parameter.

\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{i}(\mathbf{x};\xi)
is the
full
CGE.
If a random subset of the coordinates
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[d]
is used (here
[d]
denotes the set
\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
), rather than the full coordinate set
[d]
, we get the stochastic coordinate-wise gradient estimator
[
9
,
34
]

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x};\xi)=\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}I(i\in\mathcal{I})\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{i}(\mathbf{x};\xi),

(4)

where the cardinality of
\mathcal{I}
is denoted by
|\mathcal{I}|=n_{\mathrm{c}}
,
and
I(i\in\mathcal{I})
is the indicator function which takes the value
1
if
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
0
otherwise.
Note that
[
9
]
sampled the elements of
\mathcal{I}

uniformly
, i.e.,
\mathrm{Pr}(i\in\mathcal{I})=n_{\mathrm{c}}/d
, for all
i\in[d]
.
Hence, the multiplicative factor
d/n_{\mathrm{c}}
in (
4
) ensures unbiasedness
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}}[\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x};\xi)]=\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\xi)
.
In this work, we generalize (
4
) to the case where the coordinates are instead, sampled
non-uniformly
.

Using the gradient estimate
\hat{\nabla}F(\mathbf{x};\xi)
based on either RGE or CGE, we can further define the approximation of the stochastic gradient of the objective in (
1
), over a set of stochastic samples
\{\xi\in\mathcal{B}\}
. This leads to

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}F(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}}\hat{\nabla}F(\mathbf{x};\xi).

(5)

We also remark that
compared to RGE, full CGE takes
O(d/n_{\mathrm{r}})
times more function queries if
d>n_{\mathrm{r}}
.
However, it has
O(d/n_{\mathrm{r}})
times smaller gradient estimation error
[
32
]
.
Inspired by this observation, we ask:

Can a well-designed convex combination of RGE and CGE improve the gradient estimation accuracy over RGE as well as improve the query efficiency over CGE?

III
Hybrid Gradient Estimator

Spurred by the capabilities and limitations of RGE and CGE, in what follows we propose a new hybrid gradient estimator (HGE) and its variance-controlled version using a coordinate importance sampling method.

III-
0a
Proposed HGE.

In order to achieve the desired tradeoff between estimation accuracy and query efficiency, we combine RGE with CGE to obtain HGE

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{HGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{r}},\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{c}},\mathcal{I})=\alpha\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{RGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{r}})+(1-\alpha)\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{c}},\mathbf{p}),

(6)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
is the combination coefficient,
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{r}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{c}}
are mini-batches of stochastic samples as introduced in (
5
),
\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{RGE}}F(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{r}})
is given by (
2
) and (
5
),
and
\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{c}},\mathbf{p})
denotes the stochastic CGE (
4

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x};\xi,\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{I(i\in\mathcal{I})}{p_{i}}\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{i}(\mathbf{x};\xi).

(7)

Different from (
4
), the coordinates of
\mathcal{I}
in (
7

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}F_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{c}},\mathbf{p})]=\hat{\nabla}_{\mathrm{CGE}}f(\mathbf{x})
.
In (
6
),

\alpha
and
\mathbf{p}
are design parameters.
Their optimal values help us improve the variance of HGE relative to RGE, and the function query complexity relative to CGE.
We next discuss how to optimize these parameters.

III-
0b
Design of coordinate selection probabilities

.

Recall that if no prior knowledge on gradient estimation is available, then a simple choice of

is that of equal probability across all coordinates

9
]

However in HGE, a RGE

Thus, we ask if

We next show that this question can be addressed by formulating the coordinate selection problem as the problem of gradient sparsification

Given

i

Defining

The variance can be bounded using

To determine

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{g_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}\ \quad\text{subject to}\quad\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\leq n_{\mathrm{c}},0<p_{i}\leq 1,\forall i.

\frac{|g_{i}|(n_{\mathrm{c}}-k)}{\sum_{j=k+1}^{d}|g_{j}|},&\text{ if }i\notin S_{k}.\end{cases}

∎

Technical challenges of ZO-HGD.

ZO-HGD is a non-trivial extension of both ZO-SGD and ZO-SCD because of the following differences.
First, the

[

Second, as discussed in Section

This makes choosing

Next, we elaborate on the convergence of ZO-HGD.

We begin by formally presenting the problem of generating black-box adversarial examples.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel hybrid gradient estimator (HGE) for black-box stochastic optimization, combining the advantages of the query-efficient random gradient estimator, and the more accurate coordinate-wise gradient estimator.
Using importance sampling based coordinate selection, we further improved the variance of HGE.
Building on top of this estimator, we proposed a ZO-hybrid gradient descent (ZO-HGD) algorithm, which generalized ZO-SGD and ZO-SCD.
We conducted theoretical analysis of the method for smooth functions, which are non-convex, convex and strongly convex, and rigorously demonstrated the efficiency in terms of both iteration complexity and function query cost.
The theoretical findings have been corroborated by a real-world application to generate adversarial examples from a black-box deep neural network.
The generalization of this work to nonsmooth problems is one of the future directions we plan to pursue.

\displaystyle\qquad+\eta_{t}^{2}L(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{p_{t,i}}\left[\frac{3}{|\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{c}}|}\left(\zeta^{2}+\frac{L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{c},i}^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{c},i}^{2}}{2}+2\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)_{i}^{2}\right].

(S29)

Note that
\sigma^{2}=d\zeta^{2}
. Using
p_{t,i}\geq c_{t}
for all
i\in[d]
, and
|\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{r}}|\geq 1,|\mathcal{B}_{t}^{\mathrm{c}}|\geq 1,n_{\mathrm{r}}\geq 1
, (
S29
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\eta_{t}\left\{\frac{3}{4}-6\eta_{t}L\alpha_{t}^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)-2L\frac{\eta_{t}(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}}{c_{t}}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}+\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\frac{L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle+\eta_{t}(1-\alpha_{t})L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+4\eta_{t}^{2}L\alpha_{t}^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)\left[\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}L^{2}+\sigma^{2}\right]+4L\eta_{t}^{2}(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{p_{t,i}}\left[\zeta^{2}+L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\right].

(S30)

Henceforth, we assume constant step-sizes
\eta_{t}=\eta
for all
t
, and constant combination coefficients
\alpha_{t}=\alpha
, for all
t
. We denote
P_{t}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{p_{t,i}}
.
Rearranging the terms in (
S30
), summing over
t=0
to
T-1
, and dividing by
\eta T
we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{3}{4}-\eta L6\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)-2L\frac{\eta(1-\alpha)^{2}}{c_{t}}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{x}_{T})}{\eta T}+L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha\left(\frac{L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}}{4}-L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\right)+4\eta L\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)\left[\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}L^{2}+\sigma^{2}\right]+4L\eta(1-\alpha)^{2}\left[\sigma^{2}+L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}d\right]\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}P_{t}.

(S31)

To ease the notation, we define the following constants.

\displaystyle A=4\sigma^{2}+4L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}d,\quad C=4\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)\left[\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}L^{2}+\sigma^{2}\right],\quad(\Delta f)=f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f^{*},\quad\bar{P}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}P_{t}.

Further, we select the smoothing parameters
\mu_{\mathrm{c}},\mu_{\mathrm{r}}
such that
\frac{L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}}{4}=L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
. This simplifies (
S31
) to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{3}{4}-\eta L4\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)-2L\frac{\eta(1-\alpha)^{2}}{c_{t}}\right\}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{(\Delta f)}{\eta T}+L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\eta LC+L\eta(1-\alpha)^{2}A\bar{P}_{T}.

(S32)

We choose
\eta
such that
\frac{3}{4}-\eta L6\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)-2L\frac{\eta(1-\alpha)^{2}}{c_{t}}\geq\frac{1}{2},\forall\ t
. This leads to

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{8L}\left[3\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{c_{t}}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{24L}\min\left\{3c_{t},\frac{n_{\mathrm{r}}}{d+n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right\},\forall\ t,

(S33)

where (
S33
) follows since
\left[3\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{c_{t}}\right]^{-1}
must attain its minimum value over
[0,1]
at one of the end points.
Optimizing for
\eta
in (
S32
) while satisfying (
S33
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta f)L}{T}\left(\alpha^{2}C+(1-\alpha)^{2}A\bar{P}_{T}\right)}+L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}.

(S34)

Note that in (
S34
),
L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
needs to be small to ensure convergence, and the smoothness parameter
\mu_{\mathrm{c}}
can be designed for that purpose.
However,
\sigma^{2}
being the variance, is not in our control. We choose the smoothing parameters
\mu_{\mathrm{c}},\mu_{\mathrm{r}}
to be small enough such that
\sigma^{2}\geq L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}d=L^{2}d^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}/4
.
Consequently,
A\leq 8\sigma^{2}
,
C\leq 8\sigma^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)
. Substituting in (
S34
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta f)L}{T}8\sigma^{2}\left(\alpha^{2}\left(1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)+(1-\alpha)^{2}\bar{P}_{T}\right)}+L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}.

(S35)

Then, the optimal combination coefficient
\alpha^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}}{\bar{P}_{T}}\right]^{-1}.

(S36)

Substituting (
S36
) in (
S35
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta f)L}{T}8\sigma^{2}\left(\dfrac{1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}}{1+\frac{1+\frac{d}{n_{\mathrm{r}}}}{\bar{P}_{T}}}\right)}+L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}.

(S37)

Since
\sigma^{2}\geq L^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}d^{2}
, we choose
\mu_{\mathrm{c}}
such that

\displaystyle L^{2}d\mu_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{(1+d/n_{\mathrm{r}})}{T}\dfrac{1}{1+\frac{(1+d/n_{\mathrm{r}})}{\bar{P}_{T}}}}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mu_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{(1+d/n_{\mathrm{r}})}{d^{2}T}\right)^{1/4}\dfrac{1}{\left(1+\frac{(1+d/n_{\mathrm{r}})}{\bar{P}_{T}}\right)^{1/4}}\right).

Substituting
\mu_{\mathrm{c}},\mu_{\mathrm{r}}=\frac{2\mu_{\mathrm{c}}}{\sqrt{d}}
in (
S37
) gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{T})\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{(1+d/n_{\mathrm{r}})}{T}\dfrac{1}{1+\frac{(1+d/n_{\mathrm{r}})}{\bar{P}_{T}}}}\right),

(S38)

where the first inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

For strongly convex functions, the error can be expressed either in terms of the
regret

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\mathbb{E}f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f^{*}\right)
(as in the convex case), or in terms of distance of the iterate from the optima
\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|^{2}\leq f(\mathbf{x})-f^{*},\qquad\forall\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{dom}f.

We begin with the following general result.

Lemma D.4
.

Suppose
f
satisfies Assumption
1
,
5
. Then, the smooth approximation
f_{\mu}
of the function
f
, defined as
f_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}[f(\mathbf{x}+\mu\mathbf{u})]
is also
\bar{\sigma}
-strongly convex.

Proof.

We use the following property of strongly convex functions.

\displaystyle f(\alpha\mathbf{x}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{y})\leq\alpha f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\alpha)f(\mathbf{y})-\frac{\bar{\sigma}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|^{2},\qquad\alpha\in[0,1].

Now,

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(\alpha\mathbf{x}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}\left[f\left(\alpha\mathbf{x}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{y}+\mu\mathbf{u}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}\left[f\left(\alpha(\mathbf{x}+\mu\mathbf{u})+(1-\alpha)(\mathbf{y}+\mu\mathbf{u})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}\left[\alpha f(\mathbf{x}+\mu\mathbf{u})+(1-\alpha)f(\mathbf{y}+\mu\mathbf{u})-\frac{\bar{\sigma}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}+\mu\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{y}-\mu\mathbf{u}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}\left[f(\mathbf{x}+\mu\mathbf{u})\right]+(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{0}}\left[f(\mathbf{y}+\mu\mathbf{u})\right]-\frac{\bar{\sigma}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha f_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})+(1-\alpha)f_{\mu}(\mathbf{y})-\frac{\bar{\sigma}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|^{2}

∎
