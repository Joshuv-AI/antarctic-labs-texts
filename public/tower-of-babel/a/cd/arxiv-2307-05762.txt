Approximating the Value of Energy-Parity Objectives in Simple Stochastic Games
Mohan Dantam, Richard Mayr

Abstract
We consider simple stochastic games 𝒢 {\mathcal{G}} with energy-parity objectives, a combination of quantitative rewards with a qualitative parity condition. The Maximizer tries to avoid running out of energy while simultaneously satisfying a parity condition. We present an algorithm to approximate the value of a given configuration in 2- 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤 \mathsf{NEXPTIME} . Moreover, ε \varepsilon -optimal strategies for either player require at most 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 ​ - ​ 𝖤𝖷𝖯 ​ ( | 𝒢 | ) ⋅ log ⁡ ( 1 ε ) ) {\mathcal{O}}\left(2\textrm{-}\mathsf{EXP}\left(\lvert{\mathcal{G}}\rvert\right)\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right) memory modes.

Introduction

Background.

Simple stochastic games
(SSGs)
are 2-player turn-based perfect information stochastic games played on finite graphs.
They are also called
competitive Markov decision processes

[
20
]
,
or
2\frac{1}{2}
-player games

[
13
,
12
]
.
Introduced by Shapley
[
36
]
in 1953, they
have since played a central role in the solution of many problems, e.g.,
synthesis of reactive systems

[
35
,
34
]

and formal specification and verification

[
17
,
18
,
1
]
.
Every state either belongs to one of the players (Maximizer or Minimizer)
or is a random state. In each round of the game the player who owns the current
state gets to choose the successor state along the game graph.
For random states the successor is chosen according to a predefined distribution.
Given a start state and strategies of Maximizer and Minimizer, this yields a
distribution over induced infinite plays.
We consider objectives
\mathtt{O}
that are measurable subsets of the set of possible plays, and the players
try to maximize (resp. minimize) the probability of
\mathtt{O}
.

Many different objectives for SSGs have been studied in the literature.
Here we focus on parity, mean-payoff and energy objectives.
We assign numeric rewards to transitions and priorities
(aka colors), encoded by bounded non-negative numbers, to states.
A play satisfies the (min-even)
parity objective
iff
the minimal priority that appears infinitely often in a play is even.
It subsumes all
\omega
-regular objectives, and in particular safety,
liveness, fairness, etc.
On finite SSGs, the parity objective can be seen as a special case of
the
mean-payoff objective
which requires the limit average reward per
transition along a play to be positive (or non-negative).
Mean-payoff objectives in SSGs
go back to a 1957 paper by Gillette
[
21
]
and have
been widely studied, due to their relevance for efficient control.
The
energy objective

[
6
]
requires that the
accumulated reward at any time in a play stays above some finite threshold.
The intuition is that a controlled system has some finite initial energy level
that must never become depleted.
Since the accumulated reward is not bounded a-priori, this essentially turns a finite-state game into an infinite-state one.

Energy-parity.

We consider SSGs with
energy-parity
objectives,
where plays need to satisfy both an energy and a parity objective.
The parity objective specifies functional correctness, while the energy
condition can encode efficiency or risk considerations, e.g., the system
should not run out of energy since manually recharging would be costly or risky.

Previous work.

Much work on combined objectives for stochastic systems is
restricted to Markov decision processes (MDPs)

[
8
,
9
,
4
,
28
]
.

For (stochastic) games, the computational complexity of single
objectives is often in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
, e.g., for parity or mean-payoff
objectives
[
25
]
.
Multi-objective games can be harder, e.g.,
satisfying
two different
parity objectives
leads to
\mathsf{coNP}
completeness
[
11
]
.

Stochastic mean-payoff parity games can be solved in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}

[
10
]
. However, this does not imply a solution for
stochastic energy-parity games, since, unlike in the non-stochastic
case
[
7
]
, there is no known reduction from energy-parity to mean-payoff parity
in stochastic games.
The reduction in
[
7
]
relies on the fact that
Maximizer has a winning
finite-memory
strategy for energy-parity, which does
not generally hold for stochastic games, or even MDPs
[
28
]
.
For the same reason, the direct reduction from stochastic energy-parity to ordinary
energy games proposed in
[
8
,
9
]
does not work
for general energy-parity but only for energy-Büchi; cf.
[
28
]
.

Non-stochastic energy-parity games can be solved in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}

(and even in pseudo-quasi-polynomial time
[
16
]
)
and Maximizer strategies require only finite (but exponential) memory
[
7
]
.

Stochastic energy-parity games have been studied in
[
29
]
,
where it was shown that the almost-sure problem is decidable and in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
.
That is, given an initial configuration (control-state plus current energy level),
does Maximizer have a strategy to ensure
that energy-parity is satisfied with probability
1
against any Minimizer
strategy?
Unlike in many single-objective games, such an almost-surely winning Maximizer strategy
(if it exists) requires infinite memory in general.
This holds even in MDPs and for energy-coBüchi objectives
[
28
]
.

However,
[
29
]
did not address quantitative questions about energy-parity
objectives, such as computing/approximating the value of a given
configuration, or the decidability of exact questions like
“Is the value of this configuration
\geq k
?” for some constant
k
(e.g.,
k=1/2
).

The decidability of the latter type of exact question about the energy-parity value is open,
but there are strong indications that it is very hard.
In fact, even simpler subproblems are already at least as hard as the

positivity problem for linear recurrence sequences
,
which in turn is at least as hard as the
Skolem problem

[
19
]
. (The decidability of these problems has been open for
decades; see
[
30
]
for an overview.)
Given an SSG with an energy-parity objective, suppose we remove the parity
condition (assume it is always true) and also suppose that Maximizer is passive
(does not get to make any decisions). Then we obtain an MDP where the only
active player (the Minimizer in the SSG) has a
termination objective
,
i.e., to reach a configuration where the energy level is
\leq 0
.
Exact questions about the value of the termination objective in MDPs are
already at least as hard as the positivity problem

[
31
, Section 5.2.3]
(see also

[
32
,
33
]
).
Thus exact questions about the energy-parity value in SSGs are also
at least as hard as the positivity problem.

Our contributions.

Since exact questions about the energy-parity value in SSGs are
positivity-hard,
we consider the problem of computing approximations of the value.
We present an algorithm that, given an SSG
{\mathcal{G}}
and error
\varepsilon
,
computes
\varepsilon
-close approximations
of the energy-parity value of any given configuration in 2-
\mathsf{NEXPTIME}
.
Moreover, we show that
\varepsilon
-optimal Maximizer (resp. Minimizer)
strategies can be chosen as deterministic and using only finite memory
with
{\mathcal{O}}\left(2\textrm{-}\mathsf{EXP}\left(\lvert{\mathcal{G}}\rvert\right)\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)

memory modes.
One can understand the idea as a constructive upper bound on the accuracy
with which the players need to remember the current energy level in
the game.
(This is in contrast to the result in
[
28
]
that
almost-surely winning Maximizer strategies require infinite
memory in general.)
Once the upper bound on Maximizer’s memory for
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies
is established, one might attempt a reduction from energy-parity to
mean-payoff parity, along similar lines as for non-stochastic games in

[
7
]
. However, instead we use a more direct reduction
from energy-parity to parity in a derived SSG for our approximation algorithm.

Preliminaries

A
probability distribution
over a countable set
S
is a function

f:S\to[0,1]
with
\sum_{s\in S}f(s)=1
.

{\sf supp}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}

\{{s}|\;{f(s)>0}\}
denotes the support of

f
and
\mathcal{D}(S)
is the set of all probability distributions over
S
.
Given an alphabet
\Sigma
,
let
\Sigma^{\omega}
and
\Sigma^{*}
(
\Sigma^{+}
) denote the set of infinite
and finite (non-empty) sequences over
\Sigma
, respectively.
Elements of
\Sigma^{\omega}
or
\Sigma^{*}
are called words.

Games, MDPs and Markov chains.

A
Simple Stochastic Game
(SSG) is a finite-state 2-player turn-based
perfect-information stochastic game
{\mathcal{G}}=\left(S,(S_{\Box},S_{\Diamond},S_{\ocircle}),{E},P\right)

where the finite set of states
S
is partitioned into the states
S_{\Box}
of
the player
\Box
(
Maximizer
),
states
S_{\Diamond}
of player
\Diamond
(
Minimizer
),
and chance vertices (aka random states)
S_{\ocircle}
.
Let
{E}\subseteq S\times S
be the transition relation.
We write
s{\longrightarrow}s^{\prime}
if
\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)\in{E}
and
assume that

\mathsf{Succ}({s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\{s^{\prime}\mid s{E}{}s^{\prime}\}\neq\emptyset

for every state
s
.
The
probability function

P

assigns each random state
s\in S_{\ocircle}
a distribution over
its successor states, i.e.,
P(s)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{Succ}({s}))
.
For ease of presentation, we extend the domain of
P
to

S^{*}S_{\ocircle}
by
P(\rho s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}P(s)

for all
\rho s\in S^{+}S_{\ocircle}
.
An
MDP
is a game where one of the two players does not control any
states. An MDP is
maximizing
(resp.
minimizing
)
iff
S_{\Diamond}=\emptyset
(resp.
S_{\Box}=\emptyset
).
A
Markov chain
is a game with only random states,
i.e.,
S_{\Box}=S_{\Diamond}=\emptyset
.

Strategies.

A
play
is an infinite sequence
s_{0}s_{1}\ldots\in S^{\omega}

such that
s_{i}{\longrightarrow}s_{i+1}
for all
i\geq 0
.
A
path
is a finite prefix of a play.
Let
{\it Plays}\left({\mathcal{G}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\left\{\rho=\left(q_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\,|q_{i}{\longrightarrow}q_{i+1}\right\}

denote the set of all possible plays.
A strategy of the player
\Box
(
\Diamond
) is a function

\sigma:S^{*}S_{\Box}\to\mathcal{D}(S)

(
\pi:S^{*}S_{\Diamond}\to\mathcal{D}(S)
)
that assigns to every path

ws\in S^{*}S_{\Box}
(
\in S^{*}S_{\Diamond}
)
a probability distribution over the successors of
s
.
If these distributions are always Dirac then the strategy is called

deterministic
(aka pure), otherwise it is called
randomized

(aka mixed).
The set of all strategies of player
\Box
and
\Diamond
in

{\mathcal{G}}
is denoted by
\Sigma_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}
and
\Pi_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}
,
respectively.
A play/path
s_{0}s_{1}\ldots
is compatible with a pair of strategies

(\sigma,\pi)
if
s_{i+1}\in{\sf supp}(\sigma(s_{0}\ldots s_{i}))

whenever
s_{i}\in S_{\Box}
and

s_{i+1}\in{\sf supp}(\pi(s_{0}\ldots s_{i}))
whenever
s_{i}\in S_{\Diamond}
.

Finite-memory deterministic (FD) strategies are a subclass of
strategies described by deterministic transducers

{\sf T}=\left({\sf M},{\sf m}_{0},{\sf upd},{\sf nxt}\right)
where
{\sf M}
is a finite set
of memory modes with initial mode
{\sf m}_{0}
,

{\sf upd}:{\sf M}\times{E}\mapsto{\sf M}

updates the memory mode upon observing a transition and

{\sf nxt}:{\sf M}\times S_{\odot}\mapsto S

chooses the successor state based on the current memory mode and state.
FD strategies without memory (
|{\sf M}|=1
) are
called memoryless deterministic (MD).
For deterministic strategies, there is no difference between public memory
(observable by the other player) and private memory.

Measure.
A game
{\mathcal{G}}
with initial state
s_{0}
and strategies

(\sigma,\pi)
yields a probability space

(s_{0}S^{\omega},{\mathcal{F}}_{s_{0}},\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s_{0}})

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{s_{0}}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the cylinder sets

s_{0}s_{1}\ldots s_{n}S^{\omega}
for
n\geq 0
.
The probability measure
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s_{0}}
is
first defined on the cylinder sets.
For
\rho=s_{0}\ldots s_{n}
, let

\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s_{0}}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}0
if

\rho
is not compatible with
\sigma,\pi
and
otherwise

\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s_{0}}(\rho S^{\omega})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\tau(s_{0}\ldots s_{i})(s_{i+1})
where
\tau

is
\sigma
or
\pi
or
P
depending on
whether
s_{i}\in S_{\Box}
or
S_{\Diamond}
or
S_{\ocircle}
, respectively.
By Carathéodory’s extension
theorem
[
2
]
, this defines a unique probability
measure on the
\sigma
-algebra.

Objectives and Payoff functions.

General objectives are defined by real-valued measurable functions.
However, we only consider indicator functions of measurable sets.
Hence our objectives can be described by measurable subsets
\mathtt{O}\subseteq S^{\omega}
of plays.
The payoff, under strategies
(\sigma,\pi)
,
is the probability that plays belong to
\mathtt{O}
.

We use the syntax and semantics of the LTL operators
[
14
]

{\sf F}
(eventually)
,
{\sf G}
(always) and
{\sf X}
(next)
to specify some conditions on plays.

Reachability & Safety.

A reachability objective is defined by a set of target states

T\subseteq S
. A play
\rho=s_{0}s_{1}\ldots

belongs to
{\sf F}\,T
iff
\exists i\in{\rm Nature}\,s_{i}\in T
.
Similarly,
\rho
belongs to
{\sf F}^{\leq n}T

(resp.
{\sf F}^{\geq n}T
) iff

\exists i\leq n
(resp.
i\geq n
) such that
s_{i}\in T
.
Dually, the safety objective

{\sf G}\,T
consists of all plays
which never leave
T
. We have
{\sf G}\,T=\neg{\sf F}\neg T
.

Parity.
A parity objective is defined via bounded function

{\mathit{C}ol}:S\to{\rm Nature}
that assigns non-negative priorities
(aka colors) to states. Given an infinite play

\rho=s_{0}s_{1}\ldots
, let
\text{Inf}(\rho)

denote the set of numbers that occur infinitely often in the sequence

{\mathit{C}ol}(s_{0}){\mathit{C}ol}(s_{1})\ldots
.
A play
\rho
satisfies
even parity

w.r.t.
{\mathit{C}ol}
iff the minimum of
\text{Inf}(\rho)
is even.
Otherwise,
\rho
satisfies
odd parity
.
The objective even parity is denoted by
\mathtt{EPAR}({\mathit{C}ol})

and odd parity is denoted by
\mathtt{OPAR}({\mathit{C}ol})
.
Most of the time, we implicitly assume that the coloring function is known
and just write
\mathtt{EPAR}
and
\mathtt{OPAR}
.
Observe that, given any coloring
{\mathit{C}ol}
, we have

\overline{\mathtt{EPAR}}=\mathtt{OPAR}
and

\mathtt{OPAR}({\mathit{C}ol})=\mathtt{EPAR}({\mathit{C}ol}+1)

where
{\mathit{C}ol}+1
is the function which adds
1
to the color of every
state. This justifies to consider only one of the even/odd parity objectives,
but, for the sake of clarity, we distinguish these objectives wherever necessary.

Energy/Reward/Counter based objectives.

Let
r:E\to\{-R,\dots,0,\dots,R\}
be a bounded function that assigns weights
to transitions. Depending on context, the sum of these weights in a path
can be viewed as energy, cost/reward or a counter.
If
s{\longrightarrow}s^{\prime}
and
r((s,s^{\prime}))=c
, we write
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{{\longrightarrow}}}s^{\prime}
. Let
\rho=s_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{0}}}{{{\longrightarrow}}}s_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{1}}}{{{\longrightarrow}}}\ldots
be a play.
We say that
\rho
satisfies

1.

the
k
-
energy
objective
\mathsf{EN}(k)
iff
\left(k+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\right)>0
for all
n\geq 0
.

2.

the
l
-storage condition
if
l+\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}c_{i}\geq 0

holds for every infix
s_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{m}}}{{{\longrightarrow}}}s_{m+1}\ldots s_{n}
of the play.
Let
\mathsf{ST}(k,l)
denote the set of plays that satisfy both the
k
-energy
and the
l
-storage condition. Let
\mathsf{ST}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{l}\mathsf{ST}(k,l)
. Clearly,
\mathsf{ST}(k)\subseteq\mathsf{EN}(k)
.

3.

k
-
Termination

\Term(k)
iff there exists
n\geq 0
such that
\left(k+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\right)\leq 0
.

4.

Limit
objective
\mathtt{LimInf}(\rhd z)
iff

\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\right)\rhd\;z

for
\rhd\in\{<,\leq,=,\geq,>\}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty,-\infty\}
and similarly for
\mathtt{LimSup}(\rhd z)
.

5.

Mean payoff

\mathtt{MP}(\rhd\,c)
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}

iff
\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\right)\rhd\;c
.

Observe that the objectives
k
-energy and
k
-termination are mutually
exclusive and cover all of the plays.
A different way to consider these objectives is to encode the energy level
(the sum of the transition weights so far) into the state space and
then consider the obtained infinite-state game with safety/reachability objective, respectively.

An objective
\mathtt{O}
is called
shift-invariant

iff for all finite paths
\rho
and plays
\rho^{\prime}\in S^{\omega}
,
we have
\rho\rho^{\prime}\in\mathtt{O}\iff\rho^{\prime}\in\mathtt{O}
.
Parity and mean payoff objectives are shift-invariant, but energy and
termination objectives are not.
Objective
\mathtt{O}
is called
submixing
iff for all sequences of finite
non-empty words
u_{0}
,
v_{0}
,
u_{1}
,
v_{1}\ldots
we have

u_{0}v_{0}u_{1}v_{1}\ldots\in\mathtt{O}\implies\left((u_{0}u_{1}\ldots\in\mathtt{O})\vee(v_{0}v_{1}\ldots\in\mathtt{O})\right)
.

Determinacy.

Given an objective
\mathtt{O}
and a game
{\mathcal{G}}
, state
s
has value (w.r.t to
\mathtt{O}
) iff

\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}}\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s}(\mathtt{O})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}}\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s}(\mathtt{O}).

If
s
has value then
{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\mathtt{O}}\left(s\right)}
denotes the value of

s
defined by the above equality. A game with an objective is called

weakly determined
if every state has value.
Stochastic games with Borel objectives are weakly
determined
[
26
,
27
]
.
Our objectives above are Borel, hence any boolean combination of them is also
weakly determined.
For
\varepsilon>0
and state
s
, a strategy

1.

\sigma\in\Sigma_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}
is
\varepsilon
-optimal (maximizing) iff
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s}(\mathtt{O})\geq{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\mathtt{O}}\left(s\right)}-\varepsilon
for all
\pi\in\Pi_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}
.

2.

\pi\in\Pi_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}
is
\varepsilon
-optimal (minimizing) iff
\mathcal{P}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\sigma,\pi,s}(\mathtt{O})\leq{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\mathtt{O}}\left(s\right)}+\varepsilon
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{{{\mathcal{G}}}}
.

A
0
-optimal strategy is called
optimal
.
An MD strategy is called
uniformly

\varepsilon
-optimal (resp. uniformly optimal)
if it is so from every start state.
An optimal strategy for player
\Box
from state
s

is
almost surely
winning if
{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\mathtt{O}}\left(s\right)}=1
.
By
\mathsf{AS}\left(\mathtt{O}\right)
we denote the set of states that have an almost surely winning
strategy for objective
\mathtt{O}
. For ease of presentation, we drop subscripts and superscripts wherever possible if they are clear from the context.

Energy-parity.

We are concerned with approximating the value for the combined energy-parity

-optimal strategies.

The Main Result

The following theorem states our main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=\left(S,(S_{\Box},S_{\Diamond},S_{\ocircle}),{E},P\right)
be an SSG with transition rewards in unary
assigned by function
r
and colors assigned to states by function
{\mathit{C}ol}
.
For every state
s\in S
, initial energy level
i\geq 0
and error margin

\varepsilon>0
, one can compute

1.

a rational number
v^{\prime}
such that

0\leq v^{\prime}-{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\mathtt{EN}(i)\,\cap\mathtt{EPAR}}\left(s\right)}\leq\varepsilon
in
2-
\mathsf{NEXPTIME}
.

1
1

1

We write “computing a number
v^{\prime}
in 2-
\mathsf{NEXPTIME}
” as a shorthand for the
property that questions like
v^{\prime}\leq c
for constants
c

are decidable in 2-
\mathsf{NEXPTIME}
.

2.

\varepsilon
-optimal FD strategies
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
for Maximizer
and Minimizer, resp., in 2-
\mathsf{NEXPTIME}
.
These strategies use

{\mathcal{O}}\left(2\textrm{-}\mathsf{EXP}\left(\lvert{\mathcal{G}}\rvert\right)\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)

memory modes.

For rewards in binary, the bounds above increase by one exponential.

We outline the main steps of the proof; details in the following sections.
We begin with the observation that
\mathtt{EN}(i)\subseteq\mathtt{EN}(j)
for

i\leq j
, and thus for all states
s
we have

\displaystyle{\mathtt{Lval}^{{\mathcal{G}}}\left(s\right)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\mathtt{EN}(n)\,\cap\,\mathtt{EPAR}}\left(s\right)}.

(1)

We will see that

Intuitively, for high energy levels, the precise energy level does not matter

The main steps of the approximation algorithm are as follows.

1.

Compute FD strategies

that are optimal maximizing for the objective
\Gain

in
{\mathcal{G}}
.

\Gain

Compute the value

for every

s\in S
.
See

and

3.

Consider the finite-state parity game

-approximate the value

.
If

\varepsilon
-approximate

by

.

Moreover, we obtain
\varepsilon
-optimal FD strategies

for Maximizer (resp.

for Minimizer) for

in
{\mathcal{G}}
.
Let

(resp.

) be optimal MD strategies
for Maximizer (resp. Minimizer) for the objective

in

(
k
plus the sum of the rewards so far)

<N

, play like
\hat{\pi}
at state

in
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}

We start with the case of maximizing MDPs.

Lemma 3
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}

1.

{\mathtt{Lval}^{{\mathcal{M}}}\left(s\right)}={\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\Gain}\left(s\right)}
for all states
s\in S
.

2.

Optimal strategies for
\Gain
in
{\mathcal{M}}

For any state
s\in S

\mathcal{O}(|{\mathcal{M}}|^{8})
deterministic
polynomial time if rewards are in unary, and in
\mathsf{NP}
and
\mathsf{coNP}
if
rewards are in binary.

Proof.

item

1
holds by
[
29
, Lemma 30]
.

Towards

2
, we follow the proof of
[

.
Since

[

]
.
By
[

, Theorem 18]
and
item

, an optimal strategy

By
[

, Theorem 8]

in the

and

(resp.

Lemma 4
.

For every SSG
{\mathcal{G}}
, objective

and

(resp.

Theorem 5
.

Consider an SSG
{\mathcal{G}}=\left(S,(S_{\Box},S_{\Diamond},S_{\ocircle}),{E},P\right)
with the
\Gain
objective.

1.

Optimal Minimizer strategies exist and can be chosen uniform MD.

2.

{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\Gain}\left(s\right)}

questions about it, i.e.,

for
constants

, are decidable in

.

3.

Optimal Maximizer strategies exist and can be chosen FD,

\mathcal{O}(\exp(|{\mathcal{G}}|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}))
memory modes.
Moreover, exponential memory is also necessary.

Proof.

Towards
item

1
, observe that since both the objectives

and

\mathtt{OPAR}
are shift-invariant and submixing, so is their union, i.e.,

\overline{\Gain}
is shift-invariant and submixing.
Hence, by
[

, Theorem 1.1]
,
an optimal MD strategy
\pi^{*}_{s}
for Minimizer
exists from any state
s\in S
.
Since
S
is finite and
\Gain
is shift-invariant,
we can also obtain a uniformly optimal MD strategy

\pi^{*}
, i.e.,
\pi^{*}
is optimal from every state.

Towards
item

2
,
consider the maximizing MDP
{\mathcal{G}}_{\pi^{*}}
obtained from
{\mathcal{G}}
by
fixing
\pi^{*}
(cf.
definition

).
Since
\pi^{*}
is MD, the states of

are the

{\mathcal{G}}
.
Since
\pi^{*}
is optimal for Minimizer from every state
s
,
we obtain that
{\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\Gain}\left(s\right)}={\mathtt{val}^{{\mathcal{G}}_{\pi^{*}}}_{\Gain}\left(s\right)}
for every state

by

.
By

3
, the latter is rational and can be computed in polynomial time for

.

7
Conclusion & Extensions

We gave a procedure to compute
\varepsilon
-approximations of the value of combined
energy-parity objectives in SSGs.
The decidability of questions about the exact values is open, but the problem
is at least as hard as the positivity problem for linear recurrence sequences

[
31
, Section 5.2.3]
.
Unlike almost surely winning Maximizer strategies which
require infinite memory in general
[
28
,
29
]
,

\varepsilon
-optimal strategies for either player require only finite memory
with at most doubly exponentially many memory modes.

An interesting topic for further study is whether these results can be extended
to other combined objectives where the parity part is replaced by something
else, i.e., energy-X for some objective X
(e.g., some other color-based condition like Rabin/Streett, or a quantitative
objective about multi-dimensional transition rewards).
While our proofs are not completely
specific to parity, they do use many strong properties that parity satisfies.

•

Shift-invariance of
\mathtt{EPAR}
is used in several places,
e.g. in
Lemma

6
(and thus its consequences) and
for the correctness of the constructions in
Section

6
.

•

We use the fact that
\mathtt{EPAR}
goes well together with

\mathtt{LimInf}(>-\infty)
,
i.e., the objective
\Gain=\mathtt{LimInf}(>-\infty)\,\cap\,\mathtt{EPAR}
allows
optimal FD strategies for Maximizer in MDPs; cf.
Lemma

3
.

•

The submixing property of
\mathtt{OPAR}=\overline{\mathtt{EPAR}}
is used in
theorem

5

to lift
Lemma

3
from MDPs to SSGs.

N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\max\left(h,\lceil\log_{c}\left(\varepsilon\cdot\left(1-c\right)\right)\rceil\right).

We observe that

\log_{c}\left(\varepsilon\cdot\left(1-c\right)\right)=-\ln\left(\varepsilon\cdot\left(1-c\right)\right)\cdot(-\ln(c)^{-1})
.

However,
-\ln(c)=-\ln(1-(1-c))\geq(1-c)
.
Thus
\log_{c}\left(\varepsilon\cdot\left(1-c\right)\right)\leq\ln\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{1}{1-c}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{1-c}
.
For rewards in unary, by
lemma

7
, we have

1/(1-c)\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(\exp(\lvert{\mathcal{M}}\rvert^{\mathcal{O}(1)})\right)
and

h
is only
{\mathcal{O}}(\exp\left(\lvert{\mathcal{M}}\rvert^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\right))
.
Thus
N\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(\exp(\lvert{\mathcal{M}}\rvert^{\mathcal{O}(1)})\cdot\log\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right)
.

Now consider the case where rewards are given in binary.
Following the proof of
[
3
, Lemma 3.9]
,
the bounds are derived from the size of solutions of the constructed linear
program. While the MDPs in
[
3
]
only consider unary rewards
from
\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
,
one can extend it to the case where the rewards come from the set

\left\{-R,\dots,0,\dots,R\right\}
in a natural way.
This affects the complexity of the above computed constants and thereby size of
N
.
More precisely, the proof of
lemma

7

can be split into three steps.
Firstly, given an MDP
{\mathcal{M}}
construct a new “rising”
MDP
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
. Then from this derived
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
,
construct a linear program.
From the solutions of constructed LP,
compute the required
c
and
h
.
We evaluate the effect of having non-unary rewards in each of these steps.

When rewards are given in unary, the resulting
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
has overall size

\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\rvert\leq 10\lvert{\mathcal{M}}\rvert^{4}
. More exactly,
\lvert S^{\prime}\rvert\leq 10*\lvert S\rvert^{3}*\left(\lvert S\rvert+\lvert{E}\rvert\right)
and similarly for

\lvert{E}^{\prime}\rvert
. When the rewards are given in binary, the construction results in an additional
R^{2}
factor. So the resulting
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
is pseudo-polynomially big when compared to
{\mathcal{M}}
in our case.

The constructed LP (cf.
[
3
, Fig.1]
) has
S^{\prime}+2
variables (
z_{s}
for each state,
x
for the mean payoff and
\xi
for converting the constraint
x>0
to
x\geq\xi
). Moreover all variables can be assumed non-negative. The number of constraints is bounded by
{E}^{\prime}+1
. Furthermore all the constants appearing in the constraints are either constants in the original MDP
{\mathcal{M}}
or 1 or 0.

Finally, from an optimal solution of the LP
\left(z_{s},x,\xi\right)
one can
compute
\exp\left(\frac{-x^{2}}{2\cdot(z_{\max}+x+R)^{2}}\right)
and to get
c
, then
take a rational over-approximation and also take
h
as
\lceil z_{\max}\rceil
where
z_{\max}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\max_{s\in S^{\prime}}z_{s}-\min_{s\in S^{\prime}}z_{s}
. The only difference compared to the unary rewards case here is that the one step change of the submartingale is bounded by
z_{\max}+x+R
instead of
z_{\max}+x+1
.

From the complexity point of view, both the construction of the LP and
the computation from its optimal solutions aren’t affected by
changes in the rewards, i.e., the
previous bounds for
c
,
h
and
N
in terms of
\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\rvert
still hold.
In particular,
c\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(\exp\left(1/2^{\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\rvert^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}}\right)\right)
,
h\in{\mathcal{O}}(\exp\left(\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\rvert^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\right))
and thus

N\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(\exp\left(\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\rvert^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}\right)\cdot\log\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right)
by

[
3
, Claim 6]
.

While previously,
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
is only polynomially larger
than
{\mathcal{M}}
, introducing binary rewards blows up the
construction (cf.
[
3
, Appendix A.2]
).
As a result we have that

\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}\rvert\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(2^{\lvert{\mathcal{M}}\rvert^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}}\right)
.
Therefore
N
can be doubly exponential in the size of the original MDP

{\mathcal{M}}
,
i.e.,

N\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(\exp(\exp(\lvert{\mathcal{M}}\rvert^{\mathcal{O}(1)}))\cdot\log\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right)
.
∎
