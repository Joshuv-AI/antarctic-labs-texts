Minimal quantum heat manager boosted by bath spectral filtering Preprint: APS/123-QED
M. Tahir Naseem, Avijit Misra, Özgür E. Müstecaplıoğlu, Gershon Kurizki

Abstract
We reveal the potentially important role of a general mechanism in quantum heat management schemes, namely, spectral filtering of the coupling between the heat baths in the setup and the quantum system that controls the heat flow. Such filtering is enabled by interfaces between the system and the baths by means of harmonic-oscillator modes whose resonant frequencies and coupling strengths are used as control parameters of the system-bath coupling spectra. We show that this uniquely quantum-electrodynamic mechanism, here dubbed bath spectral filtering, boosts the performance of a minimal quantum heat manager comprised of two interacting qubits or an analogous optomechanical system, allowing this device to attain either perfect heat diode action or strongly enhanced heat transistor action.

VI
Conclusions

The analysis presented here has underscored the ability of bath spectral filtering (BSF) to serve as a resource for boosting the performance of a multifunctional heat manager, particularly as heat diode (HD) or heat transistor (HT), when this manager is based on a quantum heat-control system with the minimal number of degrees of freedom.
The key to optimize HD performance, i.e. achieve perfect heat-flow rectification, is asymmetry in the coupling of the control system to left-and right-hand baths. Such asymmetric coupling between left and right hand subsystems can be incurred via the coupling of two subsystems whose minimal models are anisotropic two-qubit coupling or optomechanical system (OMS) coupling. However, restrictions on the coupling strength imply that adequate asymmetry is not always attainable, particularly for identical qubits or resonant OMS.

Remarkably, BSF that yield
strongly skewed-Lorentzian bath lineshapes
has been shown here to enable perfect rectification of an HD regardless of such restrictions. Equally remarkable is the finding that skewed-Lorentzian BSF applied to the collector and emitter baths can boost HT amplification in the optimal regime where the base heat current is very small, so that the collector and emitter heat currents are nearly equal in magnitude and flow in opposite directions. For suitable system parameters, the proposed setup in Fig.
1
can also be employed as a thermal switch
Karimi et al. 2017
or a heat valve
Ronzani et al. 2018
, and these functionalities may also be strongly boosted by skewed-Lorentzian BSF. Essentially, the beneficial role of such BSF in quantum heat management is akin to that previously predicted for qubit-based minimal heat engines and refrigerators
G-Klimovsky et al. 2015
;
Gelbwaser-Klimovsky and Kurizki 2014
;
Gelbwaser-Klimovsky and Kurizki 2015
;
Gelbwaser-Klimovsky et al. 2013
;
Ghosh et al. 2018
;
Tahir et al. 2020
that must be coupled to spectrally-separated hot and cold baths in order to attain high efficiency or power.

The guidelines for engineering the skewed-Lorentzian BSF in Eq. (11) to ensure HD and HT performance boost (as in Eq. (14)) are essentially as follows: Introduce a cut-off or at least sharp drop-off on the required spectral wing of the Lorentzian by appropriately selecting the filter-mode frequency
\tilde{\omega}_{\alpha}

;
Shahmoon et al. 2014
;
Kurizki 1990
;
Lambropoulos et al. 2000
;
Shahmoon et al. 2016
;
Liu and Houck 2017
.

The predicted BSF boost of quantum heat management may open a new avenue in our ability to pursue advantageous thermodynamic functionalities based on quantum systems, by exploiting hitherto untapped resources. As opposed to prevailing resources employed in quantum HT and HD schemes that have classical analogs, the BSF is a uniquely quantum mechanical resource: it stems from the renormalization of the interaction between the system and the bath and is manifest in their dissipative rate of energy exchange as well as in the bath-induced dispersive (Lamb ) shift
Kofman et al. 1994
;
Lambropoulos et al. 2000
(see App.

Appendix B
BSF for OMS

Generically, an optomechanical-like interaction between two subsystems labeled with A and B is of the form

\displaystyle H=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b+ga^{\dagger}a(b+b^{\dagger}),

(33)

where
a,b
and
a^{\dagger},b^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation ladder operators of excitations in corresponding subsystems. The excitation frequencies are denoted by
\omega_{a},\omega_{b}
and
g
stands for the coupling coefficient.
Subsystem
A
consists of either spins or bosons , while subsystem
B
is assumed to be always bosonic. Both subsystems are attached to their respective thermal reservoirs, which are modeled as ensembles of harmonic oscillators described by

\displaystyle H_{a-\text{bath}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\eta_{a}(q)(a^{\dagger}a_{q}+aa_{q}^{\dagger}),

(34)

\displaystyle H_{b-\text{bath}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\eta_{b}(q)(b^{\dagger}b_{q}+bb_{q}^{\dagger}),

(35)

where the rotating wave approximation is employed and the system- bath coupling strengths
\eta_{a},\eta_{b}
are assumed to be weak relative to
\omega_{a},\omega_{b}
.
After a canonical transformation (similar to the Frölich polaron or the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation) with the unitary
U=\exp{(S)}
where

\displaystyle S=\frac{g}{\omega_{b}}a^{\dagger}a(b^{\dagger}-b)

(36)

the system Hamiltonian changes to
H^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}HU
and reads

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\chi(a^{\dagger}a)^{2}+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b

(37)

and the system-bath interactions become

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{a-\text{bath}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\eta_{a}(q)(a^{\dagger}\text{e}^{\bar{g}(b^{\dagger}-b)}a_{q}+h.c.),

(38)

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{b-\text{bath}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\eta_{b}(q)(b^{\dagger}b_{q}+bb_{q}^{\dagger})

(39)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\bar{g}\eta_{b}(q)a^{\dagger}a(b_{q}+b_{q}^{\dagger}),

where
\bar{g}=g/\omega_{b}
is introduced for brevity.
For simplicity, we here assume
\omega_{b}\gg g
so that we can ignore sideband contributions in the system-bath interactions. In addition,
we will neglect the Kerr-type squeezing term in the
transformed system Hamiltonian. The spectral response function of the bosonic bath coupled to a bosonic system, when the bath is spectrally structured, e.g. has band gaps, can be calculated analogously to that of
a two-level atom in such a structured bath,e.g. in
a photonic band gap material, where a skewed-Lorentzian response function has been found
Shahmoon et al. 2014
.
For a harmonic oscillator coupled to a bosonic bath,such
studies are conspicuously absent,with one notable exception
Cresser and Dalton 1980
.
We here sketch a simplified derivation
of the spectral response in the case of a fully bosonic system-bath interaction
using the resolvent operator method.
The resolvent operator, or Green function, method has been developed for sequential decay
Mower 1966
;
Mower 1968
;
Goldhaber and Watson 1967
, and applied to quantum optical systems
noa 1965
;
Agarwal 1974
;
Dixit and Lambropoulos 1980
. It has been subsequently generalized to structured environments subsequently
Kofman et al. 1994
. Let us first write the Hamiltonian of our isolated, reduced problem as

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+V,

(40)

where

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\omega(n+1/2)|n\rangle\langle n|+\sum_{q}\omega_{q}(b_{q}^{\dagger}b_{q}),

(41)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{nq}\eta_{nq}(|n+1\rangle\langle n|b_{q}+|n\rangle\langle n+1|b_{q}^{\dagger}).

(42)

We look for the broadening and shift of each
energy level of the quantum oscillator system. For that
we assume the system is prepared in the initial state

\displaystyle|I\rangle=|n\rangle|0_{q}\rangle=|n,0_{q}\rangle,

(43)

denoting the vacuum state of the bath modes as
|0_{q}\rangle
. The interaction
V
can couple the initial state to the state

\displaystyle|B_{q}\rangle=|n-1\rangle|1_{q}\rangle=|n-1,1_{q}\rangle.

(44)

The eigenvalues of the states
|I\rangle
and
|B_{q}\rangle
are given by

\displaystyle\omega_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(n+1/2),

(45)

\displaystyle\omega_{B_{q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(n-1/2)+\omega_{q}.

(46)

The equation for the resolvent operator
R(z)
is given
by
Lambropoulos et al. 2000

\displaystyle(z-H_{0})R(z)=I+VR(z),

(47)

where
I
is the unit operator.
Taking the matrix element of Eq. (
47
)

\langle 0_{q},n|R(z)|n,0_{q}\rangle=R_{n;n}
we find

\displaystyle[z-\omega(n+1/2)]R_{n;n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{q}\eta_{n-1,q}R_{n-1,1_{q};n}

This suggests that we need an equation for the matrix element
R_{n-1,1_{q};n}
, too. Similarly then we find

\displaystyle[z-\omega(n-1/2)-\omega_{q}]R_{n-1,1_{q};n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{n-1,q}R_{n;n}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\eta_{n-2,q}R_{n-1,1_{q};n-2,2_{q}}.

The last term can be used for improving the perturbative
expansion by systematically iterating the recursive relation. Here we will be content with the lowest order
expression and drop the second order terms in interaction strength. Substituting
R_{n-1,1_{q};n}
from Eq. (
B
) into Eq. (
47
)
we get

\displaystyle R_{n;n}=\frac{1}{z-\omega(n+1/2)-W_{n}(z)}

(50)

where the so-called shift-width function is
identified to be

\displaystyle W_{n}(z)=\sum_{q}\frac{\eta_{n-1,q}^{2}}{z-\omega(n-1/2)-\omega_{q}}.

(51)

At

,
the shape is Lorentzian

\displaystyle W_{n}^{(0)}=\sum_{q}\frac{\eta_{n-1,q}^{2}}{\omega-\omega_{q}+i\epsilon}.

(52)

This expression has a similar form to that obtained for a two-level atom embedded in a bosonic bath

in the complex-z place by
contour integration with the residue theorem (principal value integration) over a contour just above
the real axis (

n
-th level,
\Delta_{n}(\omega)

n
-th level width
