Resolution for Constrained Pseudo-Propositional Logic
Ahmad-Saher Azizi-Sultan

Abstract
. This work, shows how propositional resolution can be generalized to obtain a resolution proof system for constrained pseudo-propositional logic (CPPL), which is an extension resulted from inserting the natural numbers with few constraints symbols into the alphabet of propositional logic and adjusting the underling language accordingly. Unlike the construction of CNF formulas which are restricted to a finite set of clauses, the extended CPPL does not require the corresponding set to be finite. Although this restriction is made dispensable, this work presents a constructive proof showing that the generalized resolution for CPPL is sound and complete. As a marginal result, this implies that propositional resolution is also sound and complete for formulas with even infinite set of clauses.

2.
Background

Consider the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{P}=\{p,p_{1},p_{2},\dots\}
be a finite or countably infinite set of propositional variables.

2.1.
PPL

The
alphabet

\mathcal{A}
underling the language of PPL is the union
\mathbb{N}\cup\mathcal{P}\cup\left\{\neg,+,\left(,\right)\right\}
,
where the symbols
\neg
,
+
, (, and ) resemble the negation, addition, opening and closing punctuation symbols, respectively.

The language of PPL is the set of
formulas

\mathcal{F}
which is defined to be the smallest set satisfying the two rules:

(r1)
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{F}
.

(r2) If
n\in\mathbb{N}
and the strings
\alpha
and
\beta
are formulas, then the strings
n(\alpha)
,
\neg\alpha
, and
(\alpha+\beta)
are also formulas.

For the sake of simplicity and smoothing readability, the following additional definitions and notations are adopted:

\bullet

Every formula which is a propositional variable is called
prime formula
.

\bullet

As in writing arithmetical terms, parenthesis are omitted whenever it is possible.

\bullet

A prime formula or its negation is called a
literal
and denoted by
\lambda
.

\bullet

A formula of the form
n(\lambda)
, or simply
n\lambda
, is called
pseudo-literal
.

\bullet

A formula which is a pseudo-literal or an addition of two or more pseudo-literals is said to be in
normal form
.

\bullet

A
subformula
of a formula
\alpha
is a substring occurring in
\alpha
that is itself a formula.

\bullet

Formulas will be denoted by
\alpha,\beta,\dots
, and sets of formulas by
F
and
F^{\prime}
, where these symbols may also be indexed.

Having defined formulas, it is time now to look at their meanings and semantics.
A proposition can only have one of the
truth values
, true or false.
In PPL, these values are represented by
(1,0)
for true and
(0,1)
for false.
Each of which can serve as a meaning for a prime formula.
Furthermore, assigning a meaning to some formula
F\in\mathcal{F}
, is done by the aid of the following three functions:

•

Scalar multiplication:
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where
n(z,z^{\prime})=(nz,nz^{\prime})
.

•

Negation
\neg^{*}:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where
\neg^{*}(z,z^{\prime})=(z^{\prime},z)
.

•

Addition
1
1

1

This addition is easily distinguished from the addition of formulas.

+:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, where
+((n,m),(k,l))=(n+k,m+l)
.

Yet, an
interpretation

I
is a subset of
\mathcal{P}
represented by the mapping
I:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which is defined recursively as follows:

1)

Recursion base.
If
\alpha
is a prime formula then
\alpha=p
for some
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
The recursion base for this case reads

I(\alpha)=I(p)=\begin{cases}\,(1,0)&\mbox{ if }p\in I,\\
\,(0,1)&\mbox{ if }p\notin I.\\
\end{cases}.

2)

Recursion steps.

I(\alpha)=\begin{cases}n\,(I(\beta))&\text{ if $\alpha$ is of the form }n\,\beta,\mbox{ where }n\in\mathbb{N},\\
\neg^{*}(I(\beta))&\mbox{ if $\alpha$ is of the form }\neg\beta,\\
I(\beta_{1})+I(\beta_{2})&\mbox{ if $\alpha$ is of the form }\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}.\\
\end{cases}

If two formulas have the same meaning they are called equivalent.
More precisely, it is said that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
equivalent
, in symbols
\alpha\equiv\beta
, iff for every interpretation
I
we have
I(\alpha)=I(\beta)
.

2.2.
CPPL

Given the set of constraint types
\mathcal{C}=\{>,\geq,=,<,\leq\}
, CPPL utilizes these constraint types additionally to the PPL formulas to build up its language.
The
alphabet

\mathcal{A}_{c}
underlying the language of CPPL is formed by the unions

\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{C}\cup\left\{\sim,\left(,\;\right)\right\}\cup\mathbb{N}
,
where
\sim
, (, and ) resemble the negation and opening and closing punctuation symbols, respectively.

The set of
sentences
in CPPL, denoted
\mathcal{S}
, is the smallest set satisfying the two rules:

(R1) Every triple
(\varphi,\bowtie,n)\in\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{N}
forms a sentence, denoted
\varphi^{(\bowtie,n)}
.

(R2) If the string
\Phi
is a sentence, then the string
\sim(\Phi)
is also a sentence.

Sentences are denoted by the Greek letters A, B,
\Gamma
, …, and sets of sentences are indicated by
S
and
S^{\prime}
, where these symbols may also be indexed.
Moreover, parenthesis are omitted whenever it is possible. Hence
\sim(\Phi)
is simply written as
\sim\Phi
.

It is said that an interpretation
I
is a
model
for a sentence
\Phi=\varphi^{(\bowtie,n)}
, in symbols
I\models\Phi
or
I\models\varphi^{(\bowtie,n)}
, iff
I(\varphi)=(m,l)
with
m\bowtie n
.
It is also said
I
is a model for
S
or
I\models S
, iff
I\models\Phi
for every sentence
\Phi\in S
.

A direct consequence of this definition is the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\varphi^{(\bowtie,\,n)},\psi^{(\bowtie,\,m)}\in\mathcal{S}
.
If
I
is an interpretation such that
I\models\varphi^{(\bowtie,\,n)}
and
I\models\psi^{(\bowtie,\,m)}
, then
I\models(\varphi+\psi)^{(\bowtie,\,n+m)}
.

To this end, satisfiability and semantic equivalence in CPPL can be considered analogously to propositional logic.

•

It is said that
\Phi
(resp.
S
) is
satisfiable
iff there exists an interpretation
I
such that
I\models\Phi
(resp.
I\models

S
).

•

It is said that
\Phi
(resp.
S
) is
valid
or a

I
we find that
I\models\Phi
(resp.
I\models S
).

•

It is said that
\Phi
(resp.
S
) is
unsatisfiable
iff for every interpretation
I
we have
I\not\models\Phi
(resp.
I\not\models

S
). If
\Phi
is unsatisfiable it will be abbreviated by
\bot
.

•

It is said that
S^{\prime}
is a

of
S
, denoted
S

\models

S^{\prime}
, if
I\models

S

\Rightarrow I\models

S^{\prime}
for all interpretations
I
.

•

It is said that
S
and
S^{\prime}
are

S\equiv S^{\prime}
, if
S

\models

S^{\prime}
and
S^{\prime}

\models

S

Conventionally,
S\models\Phi
(resp.
\Phi\models

S
) means
S

\models\{\Phi\}
(resp.
\{\Phi\}\models S
).
More generally,
S\models\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{k}
(resp.
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{k}\models

) is written instead of
S

\models\{\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{k}\}
(resp. {
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{k}\}\models S
), and more briefly

is written instead of

.
Analogous notations are used regarding semantic equivalence.

According to these conventions, defining negation in CPPL becomes straightforward.
Let
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathcal{S}
. If for any interpretation
I
we have
I\models\Phi\iff I\not\models\Psi
and
I\not\models\Phi\iff I\models\Psi
, then it is said that
\Psi
is the

of
\Phi
and abbreviated by
\sim\Phi=\Psi
.
Thus,
I\models\Phi\iff I\not\models\sim\Phi\iff I\models\sim\sim\Phi
. That is
\Phi\equiv\sim\sim\Phi
.

Under the presented terminology, the following theorems can be proven (see
[
3
]
).

Theorem 2.2

(Resolution theorem)
.

If
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
and
n>m^{\prime}\geq m
, then the following consequence holds:

(\varphi+m^{\prime}p+m(\neg p))^{(\bowtie,\,n)}\models(\varphi+(m^{\prime}-m)p)^{(\bowtie,\,n-m)}.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
\Phi=\varphi^{(\geq,\,n)}
is a sentence and

is the coefficient sum of
\varphi
, then the negation of
\Phi
can be represented by the sentence
(\neg\varphi)^{(\geq,\,N-n+1)}
. That is
\sim\Phi=(\neg\varphi)^{(\geq,\,N-n+1)}
.

Finally, to conclude this section, one more definition is needed.
It is said that the sentence
\varphi^{(\bowtie,\,n)}
is in
standard form
or simply

, if the formula
\varphi
is in its normal form and the symbol “
\bowtie
” corresponds to the constraint type “
\geq
”.

S
is in standard form if every sentence
\Phi\in S
is in its standard form.

Every sentence in CPPL can be transformed equivalently into a standard sentence
[
3
]
.
Thus any
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}
, can be represented as follows:

\Phi=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{i=k}n_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)^{(\geq,\,n)},

The ultimate goal of CPPL, as in every logic, is to have a system of formal proof which consists of inference rules for deducing the logical consequence of sentences from that of another.
Restricting inference rules to standard sentences results in giving fewer cases to be handled, and hence simplifies the proof system and enhances its implementational efficiency.
Thus, the indicated restriction will be utilized throughout the remaining part of this paper.

2.3.
Formal proofs

A
proof system
consists of a set of inference rules that allow for deducing sentences from another set of sentences.
If
S^{\prime}
can be deduced from
S
by one or several applications of the inference rules, then it is said that
S^{\prime}
is

from
S
and denoted by
S\vdash S^{\prime}
.

Each inference rule consists of certain premises that lead to a certain conclusion and written as
\frac{Premises}{Conclusion}
.
Thus the inference rule
\frac{S\subseteq S^{\prime}}{S^{\prime}\vdash S}
, called the initial rule, means that
S
is derivable from
S^{\prime}
whenever
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
.

Given a proof system, such as the one listed in Table

If there is a formal proof that ends up with the statement
S\vdash S^{\prime}
, it is said that
S^{\prime}
is
derivable
from
S
.
Additionally, if whenever

.

Although this proof system enables us to prove assertions by applying blindly a set of rules, it is intended for human use.

The rest of this paper is devoted to present a proof system with minimal number of rules called CPPL resolution, analogously to propositional resolution.
It takes the thinking process out of formal proofs allowing for computerized deductions.

Our goal now is to prove that the CPPL resolution is sound and complete.
Let us start with the soundness part, which is rather straightforward.

Theorem 3.1

(Soundness)
.

If
S\vdash^{r}S^{\prime}
, then
S\models S^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assuming
S\vdash^{r}S^{\prime}
means that there is a formal proof of several lines, each contains a statement of the form
X\vdash^{r}Y
justified by one of the two rules of Table

S\vdash^{r}S^{\prime}
.
Thus proving this theorem is achieved by showing that for each line of the formal proof, if
X\vdash^{r}Y
, then
X\models Y
.
But this follows from verifying the two inference rules in Table

Rule I concludes with
{S^{\prime}\vdash^{r}S}
under the premise
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
.
Thus we need to show that if
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
, then
{S^{\prime}\models S}
.
This verification is rather easy.

{I\models S^{\prime}}
means
{I\models\Phi^{\prime}}
for all
\Phi^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
.
Since
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
, it follows that
{I\models\Phi}
for all
\Phi\in S
.
Thus
S^{\prime}\models S
.

To verify Rule II we need to show that the consequence

S\models(\varphi_{1}+\beta)^{(\geq,n_{1})},\,(\varphi_{2}+\neg\beta)^{(\geq,n_{2})}
implies

S\models(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})^{(\geq,n_{1}+n_{2}-|\beta|)}

Considering the standard form, we can assume that
\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{i=k}m_{i}\lambda_{i}
. Accordingly, this means that
\neg\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{i=k}m_{i}\neg\lambda_{i}

Now, let
I\models S
. From the assumption this means that

I\models(\varphi_{1}+\beta)^{(\geq,n_{1})}

I\models(\varphi_{2}+\neg\beta)^{(\geq,n_{2})}

Taking into account Proposition

That is

For any

Conclusion

In this work, the well-known proof system of propositional resolution has been utilized for CPPL.
The resultant CPPL resolution can be viewed as a generalization of propositional resolution.
This is justified by the fact that every CNF formula, which is a finite set of clauses, can be represented equivalently by a set of sentences in CPPL
[
2
]
.
Additionally, CPPL does not require the corresponding set of sentences to be finite.
Although such a restriction is made dispensable, this work has provided us with a constructive proof showing that the CPPL resolution is sound and complete. One can conclude, as a marginal consequence of this result, that propositional resolution is maintained to be sound and complete even for formulas with infinite set of clauses.
