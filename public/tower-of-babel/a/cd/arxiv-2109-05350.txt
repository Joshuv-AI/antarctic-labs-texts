Intrinsic holonomy and curved cosets of Cartan geometries
Jacob W. Erickson

Abstract
. We provide an intrinsic notion of curved cosets for arbitrary Cartan geometries, simplifying the existing construction of curved orbits for a given holonomy reduction. To do this, we define an intrinsic holonomy group, which is shown to coincide precisely with the standard definition of the holonomy group for Cartan geometries in terms of an associated principal connection. These curved cosets retain many characteristics of their homogeneous counterparts, and they behave well under the action of automorphisms. We conclude the paper by using the machinery developed to generalize the de Rham decomposition theorem for Riemannian manifolds and give a potentially useful characterization of inessential automorphism groups for parabolic geometries.



1.
Introduction

In motivating the study of Cartan geometries, we are often told that Cartan geometries resemble Lie groups in a particular way, allowing us to extend the spirit of Klein’s Erlangen program to geometric structures like Riemannian manifolds that are not necessarily homogeneous. From this, an astonishingly extensive portion of the vast menagerie of geometric structures studied in differential geometry can be brought under a single unified perspective.

The recent renaissance in the study of parabolic geometries over the past few decades bears testament to the enormous benefit of this frame of mind. For example, using this resemblance of Cartan geometries to Lie groups, sequences of natural differential operators analogous to the (dual) Bernstein-Gelfand-Gelfand resolutions in representation theory were constructed in
[
11
]
(later simplified by
[
7
]
) for parabolic geometries of arbitrary type.

And yet, in the long shadow cast by these towering achievements of differential geometry by way of representation theory, several fundamental questions still seem astoundingly unexplored. For example, if a Cartan geometry is like a Lie group, then what do its subgroups look like? Would the notion of subgroup even be useful for Cartan geometries, let alone make sense? If so, then how much like actual subgroups can we expect them to behave?

As we will see shortly, the answers to these questions come, at least in part, from holonomy reductions. These were explored for Cartan geometries in
[
8
]
, but while the constructions in that paper were fairly straightforward to implement in most applications, the approach was extrinsic, relying on an extension of the Cartan connection to a principal connection on a larger bundle, and the role of the analogy between Cartan geometries and Lie groups was, in our opinion, somewhat obscured by this. Thus, the first part of this paper will be devoted to providing an intrinsic reframing of the machinery of
[
8
]
. To do this, we will give an intrinsic definition of holonomy, then use this intrinsic definition to specify “subgroups” and “cosets” of the Cartan geometry.

Excitingly, our notion of intrinsic holonomy allows us to make the analogy between Cartan geometries and Lie groups quite explicit, and to highlight this, we will attempt to fully commit to the pretense of doing “Lie theory” on Cartan geometries. Nearly all results in the main body of the paper are directly motivated by analogous results for Lie groups, and in some cases, proofs will deliberately mirror well-known proofs from elementary Lie theory. We have found that this “pretend Lie theory” perspective is quite appealing to those not already familiar with Cartan geometries, and hope that presenting our ideas in this somewhat avant-garde way both clarifies their role in the subject and encourages others to explore this area of geometry.

We have organized the paper as follows. After giving some preliminary definitions in Section
2
, we start in Section
3
by giving a more natural definition of holonomy, and then show that this intrinsic holonomy is actually equivalent to the extrinsic holonomy used in
[
8
]
. In Section
4
, we introduce the notion of curved cosets and demonstrate some ways in which they behave like genuine cosets of Lie groups. Section
5
deals with tractor bundles and tractor connections, and while none of the results in that section are particularly new, we think that the perspective used there is aesthetically pleasant and connects well with the machinery developed in Section
4
. The way that automorphisms of Cartan geometries interact with holonomy and curved cosets is explored in Section
6
. Finally, to exhibit possible applications beyond those described in
[
8
]
, we end the paper in Section
7
with a generalization of the de Rham decomposition theorem for Riemannian manifolds and a new situationally useful approach to essential automorphisms of parabolic geometries.

Preliminaries

We begin by recalling some basic definitions from the theory of Cartan geometries that will be used throughout the paper. The definitions of tractor connections, automorphisms, extension functors, and completeness, while standard aspects of the subject, have been deferred to later sections for clarity of exposition. Each Cartan geometry is modeled on a homogeneous geometric structure in the sense of Klein.

.

A

If

\operatorname{L}_{g^{-1}*}X_{g}\in\mathfrak{g}

with a principal
H
-bundle over a smooth manifold

with a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued
1

that satisfies a few similar properties.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose

is a smooth manifold and
(G,H)
is a model. Then, a
Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over

({G},\omega)

•

•

•

For every

is given by
\exp(t\omega^{-1}(Y)){g}={g}\exp(tY)
.

This description of geometric structure encompasses many of the various flavors of modern differential geometry, putting them all under a common framework. As a motivating example, an
m

(M,\mathrm{g})
determines a unique (torsion-free) Cartan geometry of type
(\Iso(m),\Orth(m))

Given a model
(G,H)
, a helpful example of a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
to keep in mind is the corresponding
Klein geometry

(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})
of type
(G,H)
over
G/H
, which gives the model as a Cartan geometry. When a Cartan geometry is not equivalent to the Klein geometry, its curvature indicates how they differ locally.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
({G},\omega)
be a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
. The
curvature

\Omega
of
({G},\omega)
is given by
\Omega=\mathrm{d}\omega+\frac{1}{2}[\omega,\omega]
. In particular, for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}
, the curvature applied to
\omega^{-1}(X)\wedge\omega^{-1}(Y)
is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(\omega^{-1}(X)\wedge\omega^{-1}(Y))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\omega(\omega^{-1}(X)\wedge\omega^{-1}(Y))+[X,Y]

\displaystyle=\omega^{-1}(X)\omega(\omega^{-1}(Y))-\omega^{-1}(Y)\omega(\omega^{-1}(X))

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-\omega([\omega^{-1}(X),\omega^{-1}(Y)])+[X,Y]

\displaystyle=[X,Y]-\omega([\omega^{-1}(X),\omega^{-1}(Y)]).

We will frequently use the notation
\Omega^{\omega}(X\wedge Y):=\Omega(\omega^{-1}(X)\wedge\omega^{-1}(Y))
. When
\Omega
vanishes identically, we say the Cartan geometry is

When the curvature vanishes everywhere, the Cartan geometry is locally equivalent to the model geometry. In other words, a flat Cartan geometry is equivalent to a locally homogeneous geometric structure along the lines of those used in Thurston’s geometrization conjecture.

One particularly important tool for Cartan geometries is the idea of development, which allows us to compare paths within a geometry with corresponding paths within the model.

Definition 2.4
.

Suppose
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
is a path in a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
. Then, the
development
of
\gamma
on
G
is the unique path
\gamma_{{}_{G}}:[0,1]\to G
starting at the identity in
G
such that
\gamma^{*}\omega=\gamma_{{}_{G}}^{*}\omega_{{}_{G}}
.

Intuitively,
\gamma

{G}
in terms of our Cartan connection, and
\gamma_{{}_{G}}

\gamma_{{}_{G}}

\operatorname{L}_{\gamma(0)^{-1}}\circ\,\gamma
.

We now turn to the machinery described in
[
8
]
. Given a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
over
M
, we can construct a principal
G
-bundle
\widehat{{G}}={G}\times_{H}G
and a principal connection
\widehat{\omega}
on
\widehat{{G}}
that is characterized by
j^{*}\widehat{\omega}=\omega
, where
j:{G}\to\widehat{{G}},\penalty\ {g}\mapsto({g},e)
. This allows us to define an extrinsic notion of holonomy.

Definition 2.5
.

The
extrinsic holonomy group
of
({G},\omega)
at
\widehat{u}\in\widehat{{G}}
is the holonomy group
\Hol_{\widehat{u}}(\widehat{{G}},\widehat{\omega})
of the principal connection
\widehat{\omega}
at
\widehat{u}
.

In
[
5
]
and
[
16
]
, a more natural definition of holonomy was given, where the holonomy group was defined to be the subgroup of
G
whose elements were developments along loops within the Cartan geometry. While this idea is on the right track, it has several issues that make it less viable than the extrinsic holonomy given above. For example, this alternative definition does not capture the orientation-reversing part of the holonomy of the locally Euclidean Klein bottle, since the corresponding loops on the Klein bottle do not lift to loops in the orthonormal frame bundle. In the next section, we will give a corrected version of this idea.

With holonomy in hand, we can define the notion of holonomy reduction used in
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.6
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over
M
and
X
is a homogeneous space for
G
. Then, a
holonomy reduction of
G
-type
X
for
({G},\omega)
is defined to be a section
\sigma:M\to\widehat{{G}}\times_{G}X
that is parallel with respect to the connection induced by
\widehat{\omega}
.

This is called a holonomy reduction because, for
X=G/J
, it is equivalent to a reduction of structure group on
\widehat{{G}}
from
G
to
J
such that
\widehat{\omega}
pulls back to a principal
J
-connection under the corresponding inclusion, and the smallest choice of

is the holonomy group. In other words, it is equivalent to a holonomy reduction of the extended principal bundle with the extended principal connection.

Of course, a section
\sigma
of
\widehat{{G}}\times_{G}X
is equivalent to a
G
-equivariant map
\sigma:\widehat{{G}}\to X
, and both are equivalent to an
H
-equivariant map
\sigma:{G}\to X
; we will follow the practice of not distinguishing between these maps.

We can now define curved orbits as collections of points whose fibers in
{G}
have the same image under a holonomy reduction
\sigma
.

Definition 2.7
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over
M
together with a holonomy reduction
\sigma:{G}\to X
. The
H
-type
of a point
p\in M
is the image
\sigma(q_{{}_{H}}^{-1}(p))\subseteq X
of the fiber of
q_{{}_{H}}:{G}\to M
over
p
, and the collection of all
p\in M
with the same
H
-type is called a
curved orbit
.

From this definition, it is not immediately apparent whether a curved orbit is anything more than a subset of
M
. Nevertheless, it was proved in
[
8
]
that each curved orbit is an initial submanifold of
M
, and that each of them carries a canonical geometric structure. Later, we will give an alternative perspective that makes this inherent structure obvious.

3.
Intrinsic holonomy

We begin with a corrected definition of holonomy.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over
M
. The
(intrinsic) holonomy
with respect to
{g}\in{G}
of a loop
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=q_{{}_{H}}({g})
is the element
\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}}(1)h_{\widehat{\gamma}}\in G
, where
\widehat{\gamma}
is a lift of
\gamma
to
{G}
starting at
\widehat{\gamma}(0)={g}
and
h_{\widehat{\gamma}}\in H
is such that
\widehat{\gamma}(1)h_{\widehat{\gamma}}={g}=\widehat{\gamma}(0)
. The
(intrinsic) holonomy group

\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
at
{g}
is the subgroup of
G
consisting of all such holonomies. In other words,

\Hol_{g}({G},\omega):=\left\{\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h\in G:\begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}\\
\text{ such that }\gamma(0)={g}=\gamma(1)h\end{matrix}\right\}.

Note that the holonomy of a loop
\gamma
in the base manifold only depends on that loop and the chosen basepoint
{g}\in{G}
, since if
\widehat{\gamma}
is our chosen lift starting at
{g}
, then every other lift starting at
{g}
will be of the form
\operatorname{R}_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma})
for some path
\beta
in
H
starting at the identity, and
\operatorname{R}_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma})_{{}_{G}}=\operatorname{R}_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}})
. Furthermore, this definition remains natural while still fixing the issues in the definitions given by
[
5
]
and
[
16
]
. Moreover, it is actually equivalent to the extrinsic holonomy.

Proposition 3.2
.

For every Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
,

\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)=\Hol_{({g},e)}(\widehat{{G}},\widehat{\omega}).

Proof.

The horizontal curves of
(\widehat{{G}},\widehat{\omega})
are precisely those curves that take the form
t\mapsto(\gamma(t),(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(t))^{-1}g^{\prime})
. Thus, if
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
is a lift of a loop in
M
and
h\in H
is such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)h
, then the horizontal path
t\mapsto(\gamma(t),(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(t))^{-1})
starts at
(\gamma(0),e)
and ends at

(\gamma(1),(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1))^{-1})=(\gamma(1)h,h^{-1}(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1))^{-1})=(\gamma(0),(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h)^{-1}).

Since
\Hol_{({g},e)}(\widehat{{G}},\widehat{\omega})
is a subgroup,
(\Hol_{({g},e)}(\widehat{{G}},\widehat{\omega}))^{-1}=\Hol_{({g},e)}(\widehat{{G}},\widehat{\omega})
, so the extrinsic holonomy group is equal to the intrinsic holonomy group.
∎

In a similar way, we can also simplify what we mean by a holonomy reduction.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
M
is connected. Then, a choice of basepoint
{e}\in{G}
together with a closed subgroup
J\leq G
containing the holonomy group
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
uniquely determines a holonomy reduction
\sigma:{G}\to G/J
. Conversely, a holonomy reduction
\sigma:{G}\to G/J
determines a set of possible basepoints in
{G}
.

Proof.

Parallel sections of
\widehat{{G}}\times_{G}(G/J)
are precisely those which are constant on the horizontal curves. In other words, all holonomy reductions are of the form

\sigma:(\gamma(1)h,g^{\prime})\mapsto(g^{\prime})^{-1}(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h)^{-1}\sigma(\gamma(0),e).

Thus, if we pick a point
{e}\in{G}
and a closed subgroup
J\geq\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
, and we set
\sigma({e})=eJ
, then
\sigma
is simply the map
\sigma:\gamma(1)h\mapsto(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h)^{-1}J
, where
\gamma(0)={e}
. Note that this is a well-defined map, since every element of
{G}
can be written as
\gamma(1)h
for a path
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
starting at
{e}
, and if
\gamma_{1}(1)h_{1}=\gamma_{2}(1)h_{2}
, then
\gamma_{1{}_{G}}(1)h_{1}(\gamma_{2{}_{G}}(1)h_{2})^{-1}\in\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)\leq J
, so

\sigma(\gamma_{1}(1)h_{1})=(\gamma_{1{}_{G}}(1)h_{1})^{-1}J=(\gamma_{2{}_{G}}(1)h_{2})^{-1}J=\sigma(\gamma_{2}(1)h_{2}).

Conversely, if
\sigma:{G}\to G/J
is a holonomy reduction, then
\sigma^{-1}(eJ)
gives the set of all basepoints which characterize
\sigma
in the way described above.
∎

Note that, if
\sigma^{-1}(eJ)
is empty, then we can use a

\sigma^{-1}(g^{-1}J)
is not empty; the corresponding closed subgroup will change from

It is well-known in the principal bundle context that a choice of closed subgroup

{e}
is the “identity element” of
({G},\omega)
, then this allows us to specify a “subgroup”
\sigma^{-1}(eJ)
of
({G},\omega)
, corresponding to

. In the next section, we will see that the “subgroup”
\sigma^{-1}(eJ)
is an example of what we call a
curved coset
.

4.
Curved cosets

In this section, we will define curved cosets, describe some of their properties, and show how they are related to the curved orbits of
[
8
]
. To start, however, we need a way to compare elements of a Cartan geometry with elements in its corresponding model.

Definition 4.1
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
and
{e},{g}\in{G}
. We say that
g\in G
is a
development of
{g}
from
{e}
whenever there exists a path
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
and
h\in H
such that
\gamma(0)={e}
,
\gamma(1)h={g}
, and
\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h=g
. When our choice of
{e}
is understood, we will just say that
g
is a development of
{g}
.

Unlike developments of paths, developments of points of
{G}
are not necessarily unique. Indeed, suppose
\alpha:[0,1]\to{G}
is a lift of a loop in
M
, so that
\alpha(0)={e}
and
\alpha(1)={e}k
for some
k\in H
. Then, if
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
is a path with
\gamma(0)={e}
and
\gamma(1)h={g}
for some
h\in H
, then concatenating
\alpha
with the right-translate
\operatorname{R}_{k}(\gamma)
gives a path from
{e}
to
{g}h^{-1}k
, so both
\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h
and
\alpha_{{}_{G}}(1)(k^{-1}\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)k)(h^{-1}k)^{-1}=(\alpha_{{}_{G}}(1)k^{-1})(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h)
are developments of
{g}
from
{e}
. More succinctly, if
g
is a development of
{g}
from
{e}
, then so is every element of
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)g
.

Thus, in the heuristic analogy between
({G},\omega)
and
G
, if we think of
{e}
as the “identity element”, then elements of
{G}
correspond to elements of
G
, the elements of
G
to which they correspond are given by their developments from
{e}
, and the holonomy group
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
tells us exactly if and how this correspondence fails to assign a unique group element.

In the Klein geometry
(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})
of type
(G,H)
, where the holonomy group is always trivial, the unique development of
g_{1}\in G
from
g_{0}\in G
is
g_{0}^{-1}g_{1}
, so the (left) coset
g_{0}J
is precisely the set of all
g_{1}
with development from
g_{0}
contained in
J
. Analogously, we define
curved (left) cosets
of a Cartan geometry to be sets of points with developments contained in a given subgroup of
G
.

Definition 4.2
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
. Let
{g}\in{G}
and let
J\leq G
be a subgroup containing
\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
. Then, the
curved (left) coset

{g}\bullet J
through
{g}
is given by the set of all points in
{G}
with development from
{g}
contained in
J
. In other words,

{g}\bullet J:=\left\{\gamma(1)h:\penalty\ \begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}\text{ such}\\
\text{that }\gamma(0)={g}\text{ and }\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h\in J\end{matrix}\right\}.

While the above definition works without the assumption that
J
contains
\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
,
\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
is the smallest sensible candidate for a choice of
J
, since by the considerations above,
{g}\bullet\{e\}={g}\bullet\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
. Therefore, unless we indicate otherwise, we will assume that curved (left) cosets
{g}\bullet J
are
well-defined
, meaning that
\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)\leq J
.

For a given
{g}\in{G}
,
{g}\bullet J
being well-defined does not necessarily imply that
{g^{\prime}}\bullet J
is well-defined for
{g}^{\prime}\not\in{g}\bullet J
. For
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
a path starting at
{g}
such that
\gamma(1)h^{\prime}={g}^{\prime}
, the curved coset
{g}^{\prime}\bullet J
is well-defined if and only if
J
contains
\Hol_{{g}^{\prime}}({G},\omega)=(\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h^{\prime})^{-1}\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h^{\prime}
. In other words,
{g}^{\prime}\bullet J
is well-defined for all
{g}^{\prime}\in{G}
if and only if
\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
is contained in every subgroup that is conjugate to
J
by an element of the form
\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h^{\prime}
. Because such conditions on the holonomy can be quite restrictive, we will also introduce a weaker notion of curved coset.

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
and
{g}\in{G}
. Let
J\leq G
be a subgroup such that every path component of
J
intersects
H
. Then, the
geometric leaf

{g}\circ J
through
{g}
is given by

{g}\circ J:=\left\{\gamma(1)h:\penalty\ \begin{matrix}h\in H\cap J\text{ and }\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}\text{ such}\\
\text{that }\gamma(0)={g}\text{ and }\gamma_{{}_{G}}([0,1])\subseteq J\end{matrix}\right\}.

Similar to the above, we will say that
{g}\circ J
is
well-defined
if and only if every path component of
J
intersects
H
and
\Omega^{\omega}_{{g}^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{j}\wedge\mathfrak{j})\subseteq\mathfrak{j}
for all
{g^{\prime}}\in{g}\circ J
, where
\Omega^{\omega}
is the curvature of
({G},\omega)
. Again, we could do without this assumption, but there is no real benefit to doing so: for
J^{\prime}
the subgroup of
J
generated by
J\cap H
and the identity path component
J^{\circ}
,
{g}\circ J={g}\circ J^{\prime}
, and since
\Omega^{\omega}(X\wedge Y)=[X,Y]-\omega([\omega^{-1}(X),\omega^{-1}(Y)])
, the condition
\Omega^{\omega}_{{g}^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{j}\wedge\mathfrak{j})\subseteq\mathfrak{j}
for all
{g}^{\prime}\in{g}\circ J
just says that
{g}\circ J
is a union of integral manifolds for the distribution
\omega^{-1}(\mathfrak{j})
. Thus, unless we indicate otherwise, we will assume that our geometric leaves are well-defined whenever we refer to them.

As an example, for every

,
{g}\circ K={g}K=\{{g}h:h\in K\}
is always well-defined. Moreover, if we are working over the Klein geometry
(G,\omega_{{}_{G}})
, then the geometric leaf

will be the coset

, where

is the subgroup generated by the identity path component

and
J\cap H
, so since we assume

is well-defined,

and
g\circ J=gJ=\{gj:j\in J\}
.

From their definitions, it is clear that

and
{g}\circ J
are initial submanifolds of
{G}
when they are well-defined and

is a closed subgroup of
G
, and they inherit the structure of a Cartan geometry of type
(J,H\cap J)
over the initial submanifolds

and
q_{{}_{H\cap J}}({g}\circ J)
of
{G}/(H\cap J)
, respectively, by pulling back by the inclusion map. Moreover, if
{g}h\in{g}\bullet J
for some

, then by definition developments of
{g}h
from

are contained in

, so

. Thus, we can naturally identify
q_{{}_{H\cap J}}({g}\bullet J)
with
q_{{}_{H}}({g}\bullet J)
.

We can actually say a bit more about the topology of

.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
. Let
{g}\in{G}
and let

be a closed subgroup containing
\Hol_{g}({G},\omega)
. Then,
{g}\bullet J
is an embedded closed submanifold of
{G}
.

Proof.

We will show that
{g}\bullet J
is closed because

is closed, and then we will show that, just as for Lie groups,
{g}\bullet J
being closed implies that
{g}\bullet J
is an embedded submanifold.

For a subset
U\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, we will use the notation
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}
to denote the subset
\{\exp(\omega^{-1}(X)){g}^{\prime}:X\in U\}\subseteq{G}
. Note that, for
U
open and sufficiently small,
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}
is open. Suppose that, for every open

containing
0
,
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}\cap{g}\bullet J\neq\emptyset
. Let
g^{\prime}\in G
be a development of
{g}^{\prime}
from
{g}
. Then, elements of
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}
have developments from
{g}
in

, so since
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}\cap{g}\bullet J\neq\emptyset
,

for every open

containing
0

is closed, this implies

, hence
{g}^{\prime}\in{g}\bullet J
. Thus,
{g}\bullet J
is closed.

The rest of the proof is essentially the same as the standard proof that closed subgroups are embedded submanifolds. Suppose, by way of contradiction, that for some
{g}^{\prime}\in{g}\bullet J
,
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}\cap{g}\bullet J\not\subseteq\exp(\omega^{-1}(U\cap\mathfrak{j})){g}^{\prime}
for all open neighborhoods

of
0
in
\mathfrak{g}

of neighborhoods of

in
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\cap_{i=0}^{\infty}U_{i}=\{0\}
. Then, for each

, we get an
{a}_{i}\in(\exp(\omega^{-1}(U_{i})){g}^{\prime}\cap{g}\bullet J)\setminus\exp(\omega^{-1}(U_{i}\cap\mathfrak{j})){g}^{\prime}
and, decomposing

(as a vector space) as

and

such that

,

, we have that
\exp(\omega^{-1}(v_{i})){g}^{\prime}=\exp(\omega^{-1}(Z_{i}))^{-1}{a}_{i}\in{g}\bullet J
.

Give

\|\cdot\|
. Since

is nonzero for each

, which subconverges to some

in

is compact. Since

\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\|v_{i}\|}\right\rfloor v_{i}=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\|v_{i}\|}\right\rfloor\|v_{i}\|\right)\left(\frac{1}{\|v_{i}\|}v_{i}\right)\rightarrow tv,

where

\exp(\lfloor\frac{t}{\|v_{i}\|}\rfloor\omega^{-1}(v_{i})){g}^{\prime}

\exp\left(\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\|v_{i}\|}\right\rfloor\omega^{-1}(v_{i})\right){g}^{\prime}=\exp(\omega^{-1}(v_{i}))^{\lfloor\frac{t}{\|v_{i}\|}\rfloor}{g}^{\prime}\in{g}\bullet J.

But

is closed, so
\exp(t\omega^{-1}(v)){g}^{\prime}\in{g}\bullet J

\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g}^{\prime}\cap{g}\bullet J\subseteq\exp(\omega^{-1}(U\cap\mathfrak{j})){g}^{\prime}
for some open neighborhood

∎

If we fix a point

that we think of as the “identity element”, then both

can reasonably be thought of as “subgroups” of

when they are well-defined. Moreover, once we have chosen an “identity element” for

, we can assign developments from that point, and using this, we can define

by analogy to

.

Definition 4.5
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
. Fix a point

, and let

be a subgroup containing

Here, again, we might as well always require that

Note that, in order to define these curved right cosets, we must choose a basepoint

\displaystyle J\bullet{g}

\displaystyle={g}\bullet(g^{-1}Jg)

\displaystyle=\left\{\beta(1)h:\penalty\ \begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\beta:[0,1]\to{G}\text{ such that}\\

\displaystyle=\left\{\gamma(1)h:\penalty\ \begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}\text{ such that}\\

Moreover,

Interestingly, curved cosets are so well-behaved that we can even consider the corresponding quotient manifolds.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
. Fix
{e}\in{G}
and a closed subgroup

containing
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
. Consider the map
\pi_{{}_{J}}:{G}\to J\backslash{G},{g}\mapsto J\bullet{g}
, where

admits a (necessarily unique) smooth manifold structure such that
\pi_{{}_{J}}
is a submersion.

Proof.

We will use the notation
J\bullet N=\pi_{{}_{J}}^{-1}(\pi_{{}_{J}}(N))=\cup_{{g}\in N}J\bullet{g}
for subsets

. Given an open neighborhood

of

in

that is small enough that

\exp(\omega^{-1}(u)){g}

, consider

is a development of

and

is a development of
{g}^{\prime}
. For sufficiently small

around

in

such that
\exp(\omega^{-1}(U^{\prime})){g}^{\prime}

has the quotient topology,
\pi_{{}_{J}}(\exp(\omega^{-1}(U)){g})

. Thus,

is an open map.

Suppose

is a development of

from

and

is a development of

from
{e}
. If

of

The rest of this proof is essentially the same as the standard proof for the homogeneous space

. Pick a complementary subspace

to

in

and

∎

In particular, if

5.
Relation to tractor connections

When we want to study Lie groups, it is often useful to look at their representations. Naturally, then, when we want to study Cartan geometries, it might be useful to have a corresponding notion of representation. To that end, suppose we are given a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
over a manifold
M
and a finite-dimensional representation
\rho:G\to\GLin(V)
.

We would like to find a “representation” for
({G},\omega)
corresponding to
\rho
. If we fix an “identity element”
{e}\in{G}
and
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)\leq\ker(\rho)
, then we have a convenient answer: define
\rho({g}):=\rho(g)
for each
{g}\in{G}
, where
g\in G
is some development of
{g}
from
{e}
. Unfortunately, this is generally not a useful assumption to make for
({G},\omega)
; if
G
is a connected centerless simple Lie group, for example, then the only normal subgroups of
G
are
\{e\}
and
G
itself, so if
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
is nontrivial, then this will only get us the trivial representation. Thus, while this is a good answer when it works, we would like something more general that does not have such stringent requirements on the holonomy.

Of course, the restriction
\rho|_{H}
of
\rho
to
H
is still a representation. Thus, identifying
H
with the “subgroup”
{e}\circ H={e}H
, we can try to form the “(co)induced representation” of
\rho|_{H}
to
({G},\omega)
, given by the space
\Gamma({G}\times_{H}V)
of sections of the associated bundle
{G}\times_{H}V
over
M
, which we identify with the space of
H
-equivariant functions from
{G}
to
V
. In the model case,
G
acts on
\Gamma(G\times_{H}V)
by
g\cdot\sigma:=\sigma\circ\operatorname{L}_{g^{-1}}
, and an isomorphic copy of the representation
\rho
is contained in
\Gamma(G\times_{H}V)
, given by the subspace of sections of the form
\widetilde{v}:G\to V,g\mapsto\rho(g)^{-1}v
with
v\in V
. This gives an identification of
G\times_{H}V
with the trivial bundle
G/H\times V
, where the copy of
\rho
in
\Gamma(G\times_{H}V)
is precisely the subspace that is identified with the constant sections of
G/H\times V
.

In the general case, we lack a meaningful notion of action of
({G},\omega)
on
\Gamma({G}\times_{H}V)
, and we do not necessarily have an isomorphism between
{G}\times_{H}V
and
M\times V
. However, under the identification of
G/H\times V
and
G\times_{H}V
in the model case, the exterior derivative for
G/H\times V
determines a corresponding differential operator
\nabla^{\rho}:\Gamma(G\times_{H}V)\to\Gamma(T^{\vee}(G/H)\otimes(G\times_{H}V))
given by, for
X\in T(G/H)
and
\sigma\in\Gamma(G\times_{H}V)
,

(\nabla^{\rho}\sigma)(X)=\nabla^{\rho}_{X}\sigma=\widehat{X}(\sigma)+\rho_{*}(\omega_{{}_{G}}(\widehat{X}))\sigma,

where
\widehat{X}\in TG
is an arbitrary lift of
X
to
TG
and
\rho_{*}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
is the Lie algebra homomorphism induced by
\rho
, and the kernel of this differential operator is the copy of
\rho
. We can define a corresponding differential operator
\nabla^{\rho}:\Gamma({G}\times_{H}V)\to\Gamma(T^{\vee}M\otimes({G}\times_{H}V))
given by, for
X\in TM
and
\sigma\in\Gamma({G}\times_{H}V)
,

(\nabla^{\rho}\sigma)(X)=\nabla^{\rho}_{X}\sigma:=\widehat{X}(\sigma)+\rho_{*}(\omega(\widehat{X}))\sigma,

where
\widehat{X}\in T{G}
is an arbitrary lift of
X
to
T{G}
and
\rho_{*}
is, again, the Lie algebra homomorphism induced by
\rho
. This operator
\nabla^{\rho}
is better known as the
tractor connection
for
\rho
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\rho:G\to\GLin(V)
be a representation of
G
. Given a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,H)
over
M
, the
tractor bundle
corresponding to
\rho
is defined to be the associated bundle
{G}\times_{H}V
. Identifying sections
\sigma
of
{G}\times_{H}V
with
H
-equivariant functions
\sigma:{G}\to V
, the
tractor connection

\nabla^{\rho}
is defined by

\nabla^{\rho}_{X}\sigma:=\widehat{X}(\sigma)+\rho_{*}(\omega(\widehat{X}))\sigma,

where
X\in TM
and
\widehat{X}
is some lift of
X
to
T{G}
.

Not surprisingly, tractor connections are valuable tools for working with Cartan geometries, in much the same way that representation theory is useful for studying Lie groups. As an example, for (torsion-free) Cartan geometries of type
(\Iso(m),\Orth(m))
, which correspond to Riemannian manifolds, the tractor bundle corresponding to the standard representation of
\Iso(m)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}\rtimes\Orth(m)
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is just the tangent bundle of the base manifold, and the tractor connection is the Levi-Civita connection.

For parabolic geometries, the topic of tractor connections is covered extensively in
[
9
]
, and their holonomy (defined in the usual way for vector bundle connections) has been thoroughly studied in several cases (see
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[
4
]
). Indeed, when thinking of the holonomy group of a Cartan geometry in the extrinsic sense, as the holonomy group of a principal connection on an extended principal bundle, most of the results of this section are almost certainly considered well-known in some form or another.

We can think of either the tractor connection
\nabla^{\rho}
itself or its space of parallel sections as the analogue of
\rho
for
({G},\omega)
. Indeed, we can explicitly describe these parallel sections in a way precisely analogous to the copy of
\rho
in
\Gamma(G\times_{H}V)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over
M
and
\nabla^{\rho}
is the tractor connection on
{G}\times_{H}V
associated to a representation
\rho:G\to\GLin(V)
. Fix a point
({e},v)\in{G}\times_{H}V
and a path
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma(0)=q_{{}_{H}}({e})
. For
\widehat{\gamma}:[0,1]\to{G}
a lift of
\gamma
to
{G}
with
\widehat{\gamma}(0)={e}
, the parallel transport of
({e},v)
along
\gamma
with respect to
\nabla^{\rho}
is given by
\|_{\gamma}({e},v):t\mapsto(\widehat{\gamma}(t),\rho(\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}}(t))^{-1}v)
.

Proof.

Using the usual abuses of notation for computing parallel transport,

\displaystyle\nabla^{\rho}_{\dot{\gamma}}\|_{\gamma}({e},v)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\rho(\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}})^{-1}v)+\rho_{*}(\omega(\dot{\widehat{\gamma}}))(\rho(\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}})^{-1}v)

\displaystyle=-\rho_{*}(\omega(\dot{\widehat{\gamma}}))(\rho(\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}})^{-1}v)+\rho_{*}(\omega(\dot{\widehat{\gamma}}))(\rho(\widehat{\gamma}_{{}_{G}})^{-1}v)=0.\quad\mbox{\qed}

Corollary 5.3
.

A section
\sigma\in\Gamma({G}\times_{H}V)
is parallel with respect to
\nabla^{\rho}
if and only if the corresponding
H
-equivariant function
\sigma:{G}\to V
is of the form
\sigma:{g}\mapsto\rho({g})^{-1}\sigma({e})
, where
\rho({g})
is defined to be
\rho(g)
for some development
g\in G
of
{g}
from
{e}
.

Using Lemma
5.2
, we also get an explicit description of the holonomy of a tractor connection in terms of the holonomy of the associated Cartan geometry.

Corollary 5.4
.

The isomorphism between the fiber of
{G}\times_{H}V
over
q_{{}_{H}}({e})
and
V
given by identifying
({e}h,\rho(h)^{-1}v)=({e},v)
with
v
induces an isomorphism

\Hol_{q_{{}_{H}}({e})}({G}\times_{H}V,\nabla^{\rho})\simeq\rho(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)).

It was observed in
[
8
]
that parallel sections of tractor bundles determine holonomy reductions. In our current formulation, this just amounts to the fact that, for
\widetilde{v}:{g}\mapsto\rho({g})^{-1}v
to be well-defined, we only need
\widetilde{v}_{g}=\rho(g)^{-1}v=\rho(jg)^{-1}v=\rho(g)^{-1}\rho(j)^{-1}v
for every
j\in\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
and
g
a development of
{g}
from
{e}
, so
\widetilde{v}
is well-defined if and only if
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
is contained in the stabilizer
\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(v)
. In particular, if
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)\leq\ker(\rho)
, then we can recover the full copy of
V
from the beginning of this section.

6.
Interactions with automorphisms

More than being just a principal
H
-bundle on which a Cartan connection of type
(G,H)
is defined, in the model case the Lie group
G
is also the group of symmetries for the geometric structure, acting on itself and the underlying homogeneous space by left-translation. For general Cartan geometries, geometric automorphisms act as analogues of these left-translations. In this section, we will briefly recall some elementary properties of automorphisms of Cartan geometries and describe how they interact with curved cosets and holonomy reductions.

Definition 6.1
.

Given two Cartan geometries
({G},\omega)
and
({G}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
of type
(G,H)
, an
H
-equivariant map
\varphi:{G}\to{G}^{\prime}
is a
geometric map
if and only if
\varphi^{*}\omega^{\prime}=\omega
. A
geometric isomorphism
is a bijective geometric map, and a
(geometric) automorphism
is a geometric isomorphism from a Cartan geometry to itself.

We will denote by
\Aut({G},\omega)
the group of automorphisms of
({G},\omega)
. Similar to how
a(gb)=(ag)b
for
a,b,g\in G
, for
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
,
h\in H
,
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
{g}\in{G}
we get
\varphi({g}h)=\varphi({g})h
by
H
-equivariance and
\varphi(\exp(t\omega^{-1}(X)){g})=\exp(t\omega^{-1}(X))\varphi({g})
because
\varphi^{*}\omega=\omega
. For a Cartan geometry over a connected base manifold, if we fix a point
{e}\in{G}
, then every element of
{G}
is of the form
(\exp(\omega^{-1}(X_{k}))\cdots\exp(\omega^{-1}(X_{1})){e})h
for some
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathfrak{g}
and
h\in H
, so automorphisms
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
are uniquely determined by the image
\varphi({e})
of
{e}
. In particular,
\Aut({G},\omega)
acts freely on
{G}
. We can further use this analogue of associativity to see that the orbits of
\Aut({G},\omega)
are embedded closed submanifolds, so
\Aut({G},\omega)
inherits a natural Lie group structure from its orbits on
{G}
.

For
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
and
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
a path in
{G}
,
(\varphi\circ\gamma)^{*}\omega=\gamma^{*}\varphi^{*}\omega=\gamma^{*}\omega
, so
\gamma_{{}_{G}}=(\varphi\circ\gamma)_{{}_{G}}
. Therefore, if
{e},{g}\in{G}
,
g\in G
is a development of
{g}
from
{e}
, and
a\in G
is a development of
\varphi({e})
from
{e}
, then
ag
is a development of
\varphi({g})
from
{e}
. Heuristically, this means that automorphisms look like particular left-translations to the developments.

Using the holonomy group, we can even place fairly strong restrictions on what left-translations an automorphism can look like in this sense.

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
and
{e}\in{G}
. Let
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
. Then,
\varphi({e})\in{e}\bullet\mathrm{N}_{G}(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega))
, where
\mathrm{N}_{G}(J)
denotes the normalizer of a subgroup
J\leq G
. In other words, if
\varphi({e})
develops to
a\in G
from
{e}
, then
a\in\mathrm{N}_{G}(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega))
.

Proof.

If
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
, then

\displaystyle\Hol_{\varphi({e})}({G},\omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{\alpha_{{}_{G}}(1)h\in G:\begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\alpha:[0,1]\to{G}\\
\text{ such that }\alpha(0)=\varphi({e})=\alpha(1)h\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\varphi(\beta))_{{}_{G}}(1)h\in G:\begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\beta:[0,1]\to{G}\\
\text{ such that }\beta(0)={e}=\beta(1)h\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\Hol_{e}({G},\omega).

Thus, if
\varphi({e})
develops to
a
from
{e}
, then

\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)=\Hol_{\varphi({e})}({G},\omega)=a^{-1}\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)a.\penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{\qed}

In particular, if a Cartan geometry has a large automorphism group, then the normalizer of the holonomy group must also be large. Further, if a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
is homogeneous, meaning that the automorphism group acts transitively on the base manifold, then the normalizer of the holonomy group will have an open orbit in
G/H
.

As we might expect, our curved cosets behave well under automorphisms: for
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
,
{e}\in{G}
, and
J\leq G
a subgroup containing
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
,

\displaystyle\varphi({e}\bullet J)

\displaystyle=\left\{\varphi(\gamma(1)h):\penalty\ \begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}\text{ such}\\
\text{that }\gamma(0)={e}\text{ and }\gamma_{{}_{G}}(1)h\in J\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\beta(1)h:\penalty\ \begin{matrix}h\in H\text{ and }\beta:[0,1]\to{G}\text{ such}\\
\text{that }\beta(0)=\varphi({e})\text{ and }\beta_{{}_{G}}(1)h\in J\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle=\varphi({e})\bullet J,

in the same way that
a(eJ)=aJ
. However, analogous to how
a(Jg)
is not necessarily equal to
J(ag)
unless
a\in\mathrm{N}_{G}(J)
,
\varphi(J\bullet{g})
is not necessarily equal to
J\bullet\varphi({g})
unless an analogous condition is met.

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
,
{e}\in{G}
, and
J\leq G
is a subgroup containing
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
. For
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
, if
\varphi({e})\in{e}\bullet\mathrm{N}_{G}(J)
, then
\varphi(J\bullet{g})=J\bullet\varphi({g})
for all
{g}\in{G}
.

Proof.

Suppose
a
is a development of
\varphi({e})
from
{e}
and
g
is a development of
{g}
from
{e}
. Then,
\varphi({g})
will have development
ag
, so

J\bullet\varphi({g})=\varphi({g})\bullet((ag)^{-1}J(ag))=\varphi({g})\bullet(g^{-1}(a^{-1}Ja)g)=\varphi((a^{-1}Ja)\bullet{g}).

Thus, if
a^{-1}Ja=J
, then
J\bullet\varphi({g})=\varphi(J\bullet{g})
. Note that
a^{-1}Ja
necessarily contains
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
by Proposition
6.2
, so
a^{-1}Ja\bullet{g}
is well-defined.
∎

By Proposition
6.2
, the above theorem implies that

\varphi(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)\bullet{g})=\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)\bullet\varphi({g}).

Since
a^{-1}Ja
and
J
are not disjoint, it is possible for
a^{-1}Ja\bullet{g}=J\bullet{g}
, and hence
\varphi(J\bullet{g})=J\bullet\varphi({g})
, without
\varphi({e})\in{e}\bullet\mathrm{N}_{G}(J)
. For example, this occurs for the Cartan geometry of type
(\mathbb{R}^{2}\rtimes\Orth(2),\Orth(2))
inherited by an open unit disk around the origin in the Euclidean plane: for
J
the subgroup generated by a translation that moves the origin outside of the unit disk,
\varphi
a rotation around the origin by an angle that is not an integer multiple of
\pi
, and
{e}
corresponding to the image of the identity element of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\rtimes\Orth(2)
under the restriction to the disk, we get that
\varphi({e})={e}A
, where
A
is the element of
\Orth(2)
corresponding to the chosen rotation around the origin, but
A
does not normalize
J
. However, we still get
\varphi(J\bullet{e})=J\bullet\varphi({e})=\{\varphi({e})\}
, essentially because the geometry does not “see” the translations in
J
from
{e}
.

In contrast, if
aJg\neq Jg
, then
aJg
and
Jg
are disjoint, so the corresponding condition for curved orbits is necessary and sufficient.

Theorem 6.4
.

Suppose
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
,
{e}\in{G}
, and
J\leq G
is a subgroup containing
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
. For
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega)
and
{g}\in{G}
,
\varphi(J\bullet{g})=J\bullet{g}
if and only if
\varphi({e})\in{e}\bullet J
.

Proof.

As we saw in the proof of Theorem
6.3
, with
a\in G
a development of
\varphi({e})
and
g\in G
a development of
{g}
,
\varphi(J\bullet{g})=aJa^{-1}\bullet\varphi({g})
, so if
a\in J
, then
\varphi(J\bullet{g})=J\bullet{g}
. Conversely, if
\varphi(J\bullet{g})=J\bullet{g}
, then every element
{g}^{\prime}\in\varphi(J\bullet{g})=J\bullet{g}
has developments of the form
ajg
and
j^{\prime}g
for some
j,j^{\prime}\in J
, so
ajg(j^{\prime}g)^{-1}=aj(j^{\prime})^{-1}\in\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
, hence
a\in J
.
∎

One situation where this idea applies particularly well is when the base manifold itself is a single curved orbit of
J
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Suppose
q_{{}_{H}}(J\bullet{e})=q_{{}_{H}}({G})
. Then, for
{g}\in{G}
with development
g\in G
, the
H
-equivariant extensions of the automorphisms of the induced Cartan geometry of type
(g^{-1}Jg,g^{-1}Jg\cap H)
on
J\bullet{g}
are automorphisms of
({G},\omega)
, and an automorphism
\varphi
of
({G},\omega)
restricts to an automorphism of
J\bullet{g}
if and only if
\varphi({e})\in{e}\bullet J
.

As we will see below, Corollary
6.5
could be remarkably useful, in some circumstances, for studying essential automorphisms of parabolic geometries. However, it is already quite useful on its own. It tells us, for example, that if
Q\subseteq\mathrm{Fr}(M)
is a first-order
H
-structure, where
\mathrm{Fr}(M)
is the frame bundle of
M
, and
\nabla
is an affine connection compatible with
Q
, then an automorphism
\varphi:M\to M
of the affine manifold
(M,\nabla)
restricts to an automorphism of the first-order
H
-structure if and only if, for
some
frame
u\in Q
, the derivative map
\varphi_{*q_{{}_{H}}(u)}
satisfies
\varphi_{*q_{{}_{H}}(u)}\circ u\in Q
. In other words,
\varphi
preserves
all
of
Q
if and only if it preserves a
single
frame of
Q
.

We can also consider
infinitesimal automorphisms
of
({G},\omega)
, which are vector fields
\xi
on
{G}
whose flows are automorphisms of
({G},\omega)
. Equivalently,
\xi
is an infinitesimal automorphism if and only if it is a complete vector field such that
[\xi,\omega^{-1}(X)]=0
for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\operatorname{R}_{h*}\xi=\xi
for all
h\in H
. We will denote the Lie algebra of infinitesimal automorphisms of
({G},\omega)
under the negative of the Lie bracket by
\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega)
, since this is precisely the Lie algebra of
\Aut({G},\omega)
.

Consider the subalgebra
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega))\leq\mathfrak{g}
of all
\mathrm{Ad}_{\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)}
-invariant elements of
\mathfrak{g}
. For
Z\in\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega))
, we can consider the vector field
\widetilde{Z}:{g}\mapsto\omega_{g}^{-1}(\mathrm{Ad}_{{g}^{-1}}Z)
, where
\mathrm{Ad}_{{g}^{-1}}
is, again, shorthand for
\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}
for some development
g
of
{g}
. By the considerations of the previous section, these will naturally correspond to parallel sections of the adjoint tractor bundle
{G}\times_{H}\mathfrak{g}
with respect to the tractor connection
\nabla^{\mathrm{Ad}}
. Because developments of
{g}
are all of the form
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)g
whenever
g
is a development of
{g}
,
\widetilde{Z}
is well-defined, since if
jg
were another development of
{g}
, then
\mathrm{Ad}_{(jg)^{-1}}Z=\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}(\mathrm{Ad}_{j^{-1}}Z)=\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}Z
. On the model geometry
(G,H)
, these are precisely the right-invariant vector fields, so we will call the vector fields
\widetilde{Z}

right-invariant vector fields
on
{G}
.

Since they coincide in the model geometry, we would like infinitesimal automorphisms and right-invariant vector fields to be the same thing. We will soon see that they often are not, but as we should expect, the curvature and holonomy will tell us exactly how they can differ and constrain how badly they can fail to coincide.

Given a vector field
\eta
on
{G}
and
X\in\mathfrak{g}
,

\displaystyle\Omega(\eta\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\omega(\eta\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))+[\omega(\eta),X]

\displaystyle=\eta\omega(\omega^{-1}(X))-\omega^{-1}(X)\omega(\eta)-\omega([\eta,\omega^{-1}(X)])+[\omega(\eta),X]

\displaystyle=-\omega^{-1}(X)\omega(\eta)-\omega([\eta,\omega^{-1}(X)])-[X,\omega(\eta)],

so

\omega^{-1}(X)\omega(\eta)=-\left(\mathrm{ad}_{X}\omega(\eta)+\Omega(\eta\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))+\omega([\eta,\omega^{-1}(X)])\right).

In particular, for
\xi\in\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega)
,
[\xi,\omega^{-1}(X)]=0
, so

\omega^{-1}(X)\omega(\xi)=-(\mathrm{ad}_{X}\omega(\xi)+\Omega(\xi\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))).

Thus, for
\gamma:[0,1]\to{G}
a path with
\gamma(0)={e}
,
\omega(\xi_{\gamma(t)})=\mathrm{Ad}_{\gamma_{{}_{G}}(t)^{-1}}\omega(\xi_{e})
for all
t\in[0,1]
if and only if
\Omega(\xi_{\gamma(t)}\wedge\dot{\gamma}(t))=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
, so
\xi
is a right-invariant vector field if and only if
\Omega(\xi\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))=0
for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Similarly, for
Z\in\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\Hol_{e}({G},\omega))
, we know that
\omega^{-1}(X)\omega(\widetilde{Z})=-\mathrm{ad}_{X}\omega(\widetilde{Z})
, so
\Omega(\widetilde{Z}\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))+\omega([\widetilde{Z},\omega^{-1}(X)])=0
, hence
[\widetilde{Z},\omega^{-1}(X)]=0
if and only if
\Omega(\widetilde{Z}\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))=0
. Because
\operatorname{R}_{h*}\widetilde{Z}=\widetilde{Z}
for all
h\in H
, this means that
\widetilde{Z}
is an infinitesimal automorphism if and only if it is a complete vector field and
\Omega(\widetilde{Z}\wedge\omega^{-1}(X))=0
for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Thinking of right-invariant vector fields as parallel sections of the adjoint tractor connection, this equivalence of infinitesimal automorphisms and right-invariant vector fields modulo curvature is essentially a consequence of Lemma 1.5.12 in
[
9
]
.

We can also constrain how far
\omega(\xi_{g})
can be from
\mathrm{Ad}_{{g}^{-1}}\omega(\xi_{e})
in terms of the holonomy.

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose
\xi\in\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega)
. Let
\mathfrak{hol}_{e}({G},\omega)
denote the Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
whose one-parameter subgroups are contained in
\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
. Then, if
g
is a development of
{g}
from
{e}
, then

\omega(\xi_{g})\in\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\left(\omega(\xi_{e})+\mathfrak{hol}_{e}({G},\omega)\right).

Proof.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to{G}
be the curve
t\mapsto\exp(t\xi){g}
. We want to show that
\dot{\gamma}_{{}_{G}}(0)=\omega(\dot{\gamma}(0))\in\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}(\omega(\xi_{e})+\mathfrak{hol}_{e}({G},\omega))
. Because
\xi
is an infinitesimal automorphism, we know that
g\gamma_{{}_{G}}(t)\in\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)\exp(t\omega(\xi_{e}))g
, so we get a curve
\gamma_{\Hol}:t\mapsto g\gamma_{{}_{G}}(t)g^{-1}\exp(t\omega(\xi_{e}))^{-1}\in\Hol_{e}({G},\omega)
such that
\gamma_{\Hol}(0)=e
and
\gamma_{{}_{G}}(t)=g^{-1}(\gamma_{\Hol}(t)\exp(t\omega(\xi_{e})))g
, hence

\dot{\gamma}_{{}_{G}}(0)=\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}(\dot{\gamma}_{\Hol}(0)+\omega(\xi_{e}))\in\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\left(\mathfrak{hol}_{e}({G},\omega)+\omega(\xi_{e})\right).\quad\mbox{\qed}

7.
Applications

7.1.
Product decompositions

We can use the ideas above to generalize the de Rham decomposition theorem for Riemannian manifolds. To get there, we will need a couple of definitions.

Definition 7.1
.

Suppose
(G,H)
and
(Q,K)
are model geometries. Let
i:H\to K
be a homomorphism and
\lambda:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{q}
be a linear map such that
\lambda\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{h}=\mathrm{Ad}_{i(h)}\circ\lambda
for all
h\in H
,
\lambda|_{\mathfrak{h}}=i_{*}
, and
\bar{\lambda}:\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{q}/\mathfrak{k}
is a linear isomorphism. Then, the
extension functor
corresponding to
(i,\lambda)
takes Cartan geometries of type
(G,H)
to Cartan geometries of type
(Q,K)
by

({G},\omega)\longmapsto({G}\times_{i}K,\omega_{\lambda}),

where
\omega_{\lambda}
is uniquely characterized by
j^{*}\omega_{\lambda}=\lambda(\omega)
, with
j:{g}\mapsto({g},e)
.

Using inclusion maps for both
i
and
\lambda
gives a way to reverse a holonomy reduction on a single curved orbit. That is, if
({G},\omega)
is a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
, and we make a holonomy reduction with basepoint
{e}\in{G}
and subgroup
J
such that
q_{{}_{H}}(J\bullet{e})=q_{{}_{H}}({G})
, then
J\bullet{e}
has the structure of a Cartan geometry of type
(J,H\cap J)
, and the extension functor using inclusion maps recovers
({G},\omega)
from
J\bullet{e}
.

We can, then, define product geometries as extensions of Cartesian products of two given geometries.

Definition 7.2
.

Suppose
(A,P)
,
(B,K)
, and
(G,H)
are model geometries such that
i:A\times B\hookrightarrow G
determines an extension functor from
(A\times B,P\times K)
to
(G,H)
. Let
({A},\alpha)
be a Cartan geometry of type
(A,P)
and
({B},\beta)
be a Cartan geometry of type
(B,K)
. Then, a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
is the
product of type
(G,H)
of
({A},\alpha)
and
({B},\beta)
if and only if it is geometrically isomorphic to the extension of
({A}\times{B},\alpha+\beta)
to type
(G,H)
.

Equivalently, we could say that
({G},\omega)
is the product of type
(G,H)
of
({A},\alpha)
and
({B},\beta)
if and only if, for some
{e}\in{G}
,
{e}\bullet AB
is well-defined,
q_{{}_{H}}({e}\bullet AB)=q_{{}_{H}}({G})
, and the induced geometric structure on
{e}\bullet AB
is geometrically isomorphic to
({A}\times{B},\alpha+\beta)
.

For example, the models
(\Iso(m),\Orth(m))
and
(\Iso(n),\Orth(n))
for Riemannian geometries in dimension
m
and
n
, respectively, fit together, so that the inclusion map
\Iso(m)\times\Iso(n)\hookrightarrow\Iso(m+n)
gives an extension functor from
(\Iso(m)\times\Iso(n),\Orth(m)\times\Orth(n))
to
(\Iso(m+n),\Orth(m+n))
. Thus, given two Riemannian geometries, we can take their product and extend to a new Riemannian geometry, which is just the standard Riemannian product.

By vague analogy to how a connected Lie group
G
with closed subgroups
A
and
B
such that
\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic to the product
A\times B
if and only if
A\cap B=[A,B]=\{e\}
, the following theorem gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a Cartan geometry to be a product.

Theorem 7.3
.

Suppose
(A,P)
,
(B,K)
, and
(G,H)
are model geometries such that
A\times B\hookrightarrow G
determines an extension functor from
(A\times B,P\times K)
to
(G,H)
. Let
({G},\omega)
be a Cartan geometry of type
(G,H)
over a connected smooth manifold
M
, with quotient map
q_{{}_{H}}:{G}\to M
, and fix
{e}\in{G}
such that
{e}\circ A
,
{e}\circ B
, and
{e}\circ AB
are well-defined and
q_{{}_{H}}({e}\circ AB)=M
. Then,
({G},\omega)
is the product of type
(G,H)
of
{e}\circ A
and
{e}\circ B
if and only if the following hold:

•

{e}\circ A\cap{e}\circ B=\{{e}\}
;

•

\exp(\omega^{-1}(X))\exp(\omega^{-1}(Y)){g}=\exp(\omega^{-1}(Y))\exp(\omega^{-1}(X)){g}
for every
X\in\mathfrak{a}
,
Y\in\mathfrak{b}
, and
{g}\in{e}\circ AB
such that either side is well-defined;

•

if
{g}\in{e}\circ AB
and
{g}h\in{e}\circ AB
for some
h\in H
, then
h\in PK
.

Proof.

The forward implication is clear, so we just need to prove that the conditions are sufficient to give the product structure. To do this, we will define a geometric map that is clearly an isomorphism if well-defined, then prove that it is well-defined. For the purpose of clean presentation, we will use the notation
\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))
to denote a composition
\exp(\omega^{-1}(X_{k}))\cdots\exp(\omega^{-1}(X_{1}))
throughout this proof.

Suppose the conditions given above are satisfied. Since
q_{{}_{H}}({e}\circ AB)=M
, every
{g}\in{G}
is of the form
{g}^{\prime}h
for some
{g}^{\prime}\in{e}\circ AB
and
h\in H
, and by the condition on the flows, every
{g}^{\prime}\in{e}\circ AB
is of the form
(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})pk
, where
p\in P
,
k\in K
, and each
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}
and
Y_{i}\in\mathfrak{b}
. Therefore, every
{g}\in{G}
is of the form
(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})h
for some
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}
,
Y_{i}\in\mathfrak{b}
, and
h\in H
, so we can consider a map
\varphi:{G}\to({e}\circ A\times{e}\circ B)\times_{PK}H
given by

(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})h\mapsto(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X})){e},\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e},h).

Let us suppose that
\varphi
is well-defined. Then, it is
H
-equivariant, and since the Cartan connections on the geometric leaves are given by taking the Cartan connection on
{G}
and pulling back by inclusion, it follows that
\varphi
is geometric. Moreover, since
({a},{b},h)=({a}^{\prime},{b}^{\prime},h^{\prime})
implies that there are
p\in P
and
k\in K
such that
pkh=h^{\prime}
,
{a}p={a}^{\prime}
, and
{b}k={b}^{\prime}
,
\varphi
is injective, hence bijective. Thus, if we prove
\varphi
is well-defined, then it will give us the desired geometric isomorphism.

Suppose

(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})h=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e})h^{\prime}.

Then,

\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e}=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e})h^{\prime}h^{-1},

so
h^{\prime}h^{-1}\in PK
. Writing
h^{\prime}h^{-1}=pk
for
p\in P
and
k\in K
, we get that

\displaystyle(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})k^{-1}

\displaystyle=\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})k^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e})p

\displaystyle=\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}}))(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e})p,

so

\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}}))^{-1}\left((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})k^{-1}\right)=\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))^{-1}\left((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e})p\right).

Thus, since

\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}}))^{-1}\left((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})k^{-1}\right)\in{e}\circ B

and

\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))^{-1}\left((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e})p\right)\in{e}\circ A,

both are equal to
{e}
because we are assuming
{e}\circ A\cap{e}\circ B=\{{e}\}
. It follows that
\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X})){e}=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e})p
and
\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e}=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e})k
, hence

\displaystyle\varphi\left((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e})h\right)

\displaystyle=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X})){e},\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y})){e},h)

\displaystyle=((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e})p,(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e})k,h)

\displaystyle=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e},\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e},pkh)

\displaystyle=(\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}})){e},\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e},h^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi\left((\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{X^{\prime}}))\exp(\omega^{-1}(\overline{Y^{\prime}})){e})h^{\prime}\right)\!.\penalty\ \mbox{\qed}

Of the above three conditions, the one that can be conceivably strenuous to verify is that the flows commute whenever either composition makes sense. As such, additional constraints on a geometry that can simplify this condition can be helpful in practice. Toward this end, note that there are two parts to verifying that the flow condition holds: that
\exp(\omega^{-1}(X))\exp(\omega^{-1}(Y))
and
\exp(\omega^{-1}(Y))\exp(\omega^{-1}(X))
are equal on
{e}\circ AB
wherever both are defined and that if either of them is well-defined at some point of
{e}\circ AB
, then so is the other.

The first part is fairly straightforward to get around: given two vector fields
V
and
W
,
\exp(sV)\exp(tW)=\exp(tW)\exp(sV)
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
such that both sides are well-defined if and only if
[V,W]=0

Unlike in the usual de Rham decomposition, we cannot always get a product decomposition of the geometry from a product decomposition of a subgroup containing the holonomy group when the geometry is complete and the base manifold is simply connected. \sigma_{0}:M\to{G}/\ker(\lambda)P_{+}\cong{G}/P_{+}\times_{\lambda}\mathbb{R}_{+}
that induces the same principal
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-connection as the one induced by
\sigma^{*}\omega_{0}
. Given a loop
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
, let
\widehat{\gamma}
be a lift of
\sigma_{0}(\gamma)
to
{G}/P_{+}
. Then,
\sigma(\widehat{\gamma})
is a lift of
\gamma
to
{G}
, and
\sigma(\gamma(1))\in\sigma(\gamma(0))\ker(\lambda)
, so since
\sigma(\widehat{\gamma})^{*}\omega^{\prime}=\widehat{\gamma}^{*}\sigma^{*}\omega^{\prime}=\widehat{\gamma}^{*}\upsilon
, and
\widehat{\gamma}^{*}\upsilon
takes values in
\mathfrak{g}_{-}+\ker(\lambda_{*})
by construction, the holonomy based at
{e}=\sigma(\widehat{\gamma}(0))
of the loop
\gamma
is contained in
G_{-}\ker(\lambda)
. Similarly, if
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
is a path between two points of
M
with
\gamma(0)=q_{{}_{P}}({e})
, then we can lift it to a path
\sigma(\widehat{\gamma})
with
\sigma(\widehat{\gamma}(0))={e}
and
\sigma(\widehat{\gamma})^{*}\omega^{\prime}
taking values in
\mathfrak{g}_{-}+\ker(\lambda_{*})
, so
q_{{}_{P}}({e}\bullet G_{-}\ker(\lambda))=M
.
∎

Before defining inessential and essential automorphism groups in this setting, we need to remark upon the first of two complications: while automorphisms of a parabolic geometry necessarily induce automorphisms of the underlying infinitesimal flag structure, automorphisms of the infinitesimal flag structure do not necessarily lift to automorphisms of an arbitrary overlying parabolic geometry. Thus, the automorphisms of two parabolic geometries might be completely different even if they induce the same infinitesimal flag structure. Thankfully, if we put certain mild constraints on the curvature, namely regularity and normality, then modulo some usually negligible Lie algebra cohomological restrictions on the model geometry (see Theorem 3.1.14 in
[
9
]
), this is not an issue: automorphisms of a regular infinitesimal flag structure lift to automorphisms of the (unique) overlying normal regular parabolic geometry. Thus, in a loose sense, normality is a “maximal symmetry” condition on regular parabolic geometries overlying some infinitesimal flag structure.

Definition 7.11
.

Suppose
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is a regular normal parabolic geometry of type
(G,P)
and
\lambda:G_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a homomorphism with
\lambda_{*}^{\kgf}
a scaling element. A subgroup
K\leq\Aut({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is said to be
\lambda
-inessential
if and only if the action restricts to an action by automorphisms on some subordinate
\lambda
-exact geometry for the infinitesimal flag structure induced by
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
. Otherwise, we say that
K
is
\lambda
-essential
. We say that
K
is
essential
if and only if it is
\lambda
-essential for each
\lambda:G_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\lambda_{*}^{\kgf}
a scaling element.

An automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is called
\lambda
-essential or
\lambda
-inessential according to whether the subgroup generated by
\varphi
is
\lambda
-essential or
\lambda
-inessential.

Similarly, subalgebras of
\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
are called
\lambda
-essential or
\lambda
-inessential according to whether the subgroups generated by them are
\lambda
-essential or
\lambda
-inessential, and an element of
\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is said to be
\lambda
-essential or
\lambda
-inessential according to whether the subalgebra generated by it is
\lambda
-essential or
\lambda
-inessential.

In light of Lemma
7.10
, we see that these definitions of essential and inessential are equivalent to the ones in
[
1
]
, modulo the extended choice of scaling elements from
[
10
]
.

Unfortunately, there is a second complication that prevents us from directly applying Propositions
7.7
and
7.8
: just like with automorphism groups, the holonomy groups of different parabolic geometries inducing the same infinitesimal flag structure are not required to be the same, and as far as the author is currently aware, there is no known method of using the holonomy groups of regular normal parabolic geometries to determine the holonomy groups of other parabolic geometries inducing the same infinitesimal flag structure. Thus, unlike in the case of automorphism groups, we cannot just use the holonomy group of the overlying regular normal parabolic geometry. Moreover, even if we get a holonomy reduction to
G_{-}\ker(\lambda)
at
{e}
,
q_{{}_{P}}(G_{-}\ker(\lambda)\bullet{e})
is not necessarily the whole base manifold. For most cases, this makes our definitions significantly less graceful to use than the ones from
[
1
]
.

In lieu of elegance, a clumsy global analogue of Proposition
7.8
for infinitesimal flag structures would be along the lines of the following.

Proposition 7.12
.

Suppose
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is a regular normal parabolic geometry of type
(G,P)
over
M
,
\lambda:G_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a homomorphism with
\lambda_{*}^{\kgf}
a scaling element, and
\xi\in\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
such that
q_{{}_{P}*}(\xi)
vanishes at
x\in M
. Then,
\xi
is
\lambda
-inessential if and only if there exists an
{e}\in q_{{}_{P}}^{-1}(x)
such that
\omega^{\text{norm}}_{e}(\xi)\in\ker(\lambda_{*})
and a
({G},\omega)
inducing the same infinitesimal flag structure as
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
on which there is a subordinate
\lambda
-exact geometry based at
{e}
and such that
\xi
is also an infinitesimal automorphism of
({G},\omega)
.

The only aspect of Proposition
7.12
that does not follow immediately from Proposition
7.8
and Definition
7.11
is that we can use
\omega^{\text{norm}}(\xi)
instead of
\omega(\xi)
, but this just follows because they differ by a horizontal 1-form.

While Proposition
7.12
does, more or less immediately, recover Theorem 1.2 from
[
1
]
, and it does tell us that
\xi
is
\lambda
-essential if
\omega_{e}^{\text{norm}}(\xi)\not\in\ker(\lambda_{*})
, the considerations above severely limit its ability to obtain more global results about
\lambda
-inessential infinitesimal automorphisms. However, if we are in a situation where we already know a subordinate
\lambda
-exact geometry, then Proposition
7.12
is much more useful. For example, when constructing a parabolic geometry, we often
start
with a particular Cartan geometry
(Q,\upsilon)
of type
(G_{-}\ker(\lambda),\ker(\lambda))
and get a Cartan geometry
({G},\omega)
of type
(G,P)
by using the extension functor induced by the inclusion maps. We then get a normal Cartan geometry
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
by normalizing. In this case, Proposition
7.12
tells us that
\xi\in\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega)\leq\mathfrak{aut}({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is
\lambda
-inessential if there is some
{e}\in Q\subset{G}
such that
\omega_{e}^{\text{norm}}(\xi)\in\ker(\lambda_{*})
.

Similarly, we can give a clumsy version of Proposition
7.7
for infinitesimal flag structures.

Proposition 7.13
.

Suppose
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
is a regular normal parabolic geometry of type
(G,P)
over
M
,
\lambda:G_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a homomorphism with
\lambda_{*}^{\kgf}
a scaling element, and
K\leq\Aut({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
. Then,
K
is
\lambda
-inessential if and only if there exists a
({G},\omega)
inducing the same infinitesimal flag structure as
({G},\omega^{\text{norm}})
such that
K\leq\Aut({G},\omega)
and on which there is a subordinate
\lambda
-exact geometry based at
{e}\in{G}
such that
K{e}\subseteq G_{-}\ker(\lambda)\bullet{e}
.

Again, while this can be cumbersome, if we have foreknowledge of which parabolic geometries underlying a regular infinitesimal flag structure support a subordinate
\lambda
-exact geometry, then finding whether a subgroup is
\lambda
-essential becomes significantly less complicated.
