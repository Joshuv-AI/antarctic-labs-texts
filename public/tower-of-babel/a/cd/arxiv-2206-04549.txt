Abstract
. A celebrated theorem of Spencer states that for every set system S 1 , … , S m ⊆ [ n ] S_{1},\dots,S_{m}\subseteq[n] , there is a coloring of the ground set with { ± 1 } \{\pm 1\} with discrepancy O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ ( m / n + 2 ) ) O(\sqrt{n\log(m/n+2)}) . We provide an algorithm to find such a coloring in near input-sparsity time O ~ ​ ( n + ∑ i = 1 m | S i | ) \widetilde{O}(n+\sum_{i=1}^{m}|S_{i}|) . A key ingredient in our work, which may be of independent interest, is a novel width reduction technique for solving linear programs, not of covering/packing type, in near input-sparsity time using the multiplicative weights update method.

In fact, all known algorithms for these problems (which produce a coloring with discrepancy at most a universal constant factor more than the best-known existential bounds) employ linear algebraic primitives such as solving a system of linear equations, which suffer from the matrix multiplication bottleneck. We note here that there are recent online algorithms for discrepancy minimization

which do not use any such operations and run in input-sparsity time. However, these algorithms are only guaranteed to produce a coloring matching Banaszczyk’s bound up to a polylogarithmic (as opposed to constant) factor. Moreover, in the setting of Spencer’s theorem, Banaszczyk’s bound itself only corresponds to a discrepancy of
O(\sqrt{n\log{n}})
, which is already attained with high probability by a uniformly random coloring.

In this work, we initiate the study of optimal discrepancy minimization algorithms running in near input-sparsity time, by providing such an algorithm for Spencer’s theorem.

A pleasant feature of our algorithm (see

Moreover, during the course of our algorithm, we develop a novel method for solving a certain class of linear-programs (to polylogarithmic relative accuracy) in near-input sparsity time, which may be of independent interest as a rare instance of “width reduction” for linear-programs not of covering/packing type.

1.1.
Proof outline

We use the notation in
Equation

1.1
. For simplicity, we consider the case
m=n
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|=\Theta(n^{2})
which already contains most of the ideas; later, we will sketch the modifications needed for the general case. For
C>0
, we let

\Gamma_{C}:=\left\{x\in[-1,1]^{n}:\max_{i\in[m]}\left|\sum_{j\in S_{i}}x_{j}\right|\leq C\sqrt{n}\right\}.

By a standard reduction (see
Theorem

2.2
), it suffices to devise an algorithm which runs in time
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
and finds
x\in\Gamma_{C}
such that
x
has at least
n/C
coordinates which are
\pm 1
.

Our starting point for doing this is the aforementioned theorem of Eldan–Singh
[
14
]
which shows that there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that with high probability over the choice of a random Gaussian vector
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{\otimes n}
,

\displaystyle x^{*}:=\arg\max_{x\in\Gamma_{C}}\langle g,x\rangle

has
n/C
coordinates which are
\pm 1
. In particular, we can a find a point with the properties we want by solving the above linear program. In fact, a slight modification of the argument in
[
21
, Section 3]
shows that it suffices to solve the linear program to within relative accuracy
1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
and then randomly round the approximate maximizer. However, solving the linear program to such small relative error forces us to use high-precision methods for solving linear programs, such as cutting-plane or interior-point methods, for which the matrix-multiplication bottleneck seems rather hard to circumvent. Instead, we rely on first-order optimization methods.

Stability of the linear program
:

In order to use first-order methods in time
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
, we need to show that solving the linear program to within
1/\operatorname{poly}(\log{n})
relative accuracy (and then randomly rounding the approximate solution) suffices. More concretely, we show (
Proposition

2.3
) that all points achieving at least a
(1-c_{1}/\log{n})
-factor of the optimum have
\Omega(n)
coordinates with absolute value at least
1-c_{2}/\log{n}
; these coordinates can then be randomly rounded to have absolute value
1
, while only incurring additional discrepancy
O(\sqrt{n})
. Our proof of this shares some similarities to
[
21
,
14
]
, but ultimately relies on a different phenomenon: we use a supersaturation version of Spencer’s theorem due to Spencer
[
22
]
as well as Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions to show (
Lemma

3.4
) that the expected value of the linear program is
(\sqrt{2/\pi}-\delta_{C})n
, where
\delta_{C}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, which further enables us to show that the “derivative” of the map
C\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\max_{x\in\Gamma_{C}}\langle g,x\rangle]
is sufficiently small, in a suitably averaged sense, for sufficiently large
C
(
Corollary

3.5
). This gives us the desired conclusion since, for sufficiently large
C
, if there were a point within at least a
(1-c_{1}/\log{n})
-factor of
\mathbb{E}[\max_{x\in\Gamma_{C}}\langle g,x\rangle]
with only sublinear coordinates with absolute value at least
1-c_{2}/\log{n}
, then it turns out we could find a point
x^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{C(1+c_{2}/\log{n})}
with
\langle g,x^{\prime}\rangle-\mathbb{E}[\max_{x\in\Gamma_{C(1+c_{2}/\log{n})}}\langle g,x\rangle]=\widetilde{\Omega}(n)
. However, by Gaussian concentration, this can only happen with probability at most
\exp(-\widetilde{\Omega}(n))
in the choice of
g
.

The Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU) framework:

Our task is now reduced to solving the linear program, which we rewrite in a more convenient form as a feasibility/search problem: for given

x

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in[-1,1]^{n}

\displaystyle-\langle g/\sqrt{n},x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\operatorname{OPT}_{C}

(1.2)

\displaystyle Ax

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{n}\cdot\mathbbm{1},

where
\operatorname{OPT}_{C}:=\max_{x\in\Gamma_{C}}\langle g,x\rangle/\sqrt{n}

4

We do not know
\operatorname{OPT}_{C}

a priori

and

In fact, by the stability of the linear program, it suffices to find

which satisfies the remaining inequalities up to an additive term of order

on the right hand side. By the usual multiplicative weights update method for solving linear programs (see, e.g.,

(1.3)

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}\langle g/\sqrt{n},x^{\prime}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\rho_{i}\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle\leq-\rho_{0}\operatorname{OPT}_{C}+(1-\rho_{0})C\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{n}/\log{n},

where
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{2n}

(corresponding to the “weights of the experts” in the MWU framework). Note that such an

using a greedy coordinate-by-coordinate strategy.
Therefore, if
\widetilde{O}(1)
iterations of MWU were sufficient, then we would be done

The number of iterations required by MWU to give a solution with the required accuracy is

\widetilde{O}(w^{2}/n)
, where
w
is the “width” of the procedure measuring the maximum violation of any constraint by the intermediate solutions

. In our case, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle w=O(\sqrt{n})+\max_{x^{\prime}}\langle g/\sqrt{n},x^{\prime}\rangle+\max_{x^{\prime}}\max_{i\in[2n]}|\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle|=O(\sqrt{n})+\max_{x^{\prime}}\max_{i\in[2n]}|\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle|,

where
x^{\prime}
ranges over the solutions to the single linear inequality output by different iterations of MWU and where we have used that
\max_{x^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{n}}|\langle g/\sqrt{n},x^{\prime}\rangle|=O(\sqrt{n})
as long as
\|g\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{n})
, which holds except with exponentially small probability over the choice of
g
. Therefore, if it were the case that

\max_{x^{\prime}}\max_{i\in[2n]}|\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle|=\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n}),

then
\widetilde{O}(w^{2}/n)=\widetilde{O}(1)
, as required. Unfortunately, with the greedy coordinate-by-coordinate strategy for solving the single linear inequality on the cube, the width could potentially be of order
n
, so that
\widetilde{O}(w^{2}/n)=\widetilde{O}(n)
and we get a total running time of
\widetilde{O}(n^{3})
, as for previously known algorithms.

Width reduction
:

To overcome this obstacle, we develop a novel “width reduction” technique (see the proof that
Proposition

4.2
implies
Proposition

4.1
), which guarantees that the width is
\widetilde{O}(1)
while blowing up the cost of each iteration by a factor of at most
\widetilde{O}(1)
. In the optimization literature, width reduction techniques have been famously used to solve non-negative linear programs in near input-sparsity time (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Table 1]
). However, our linear program is not of this form and our technique is completely different. In each iteration, instead of considering the simple linear inequality mentioned earlier, consider the following convex program: find
x^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{n}
minimizing

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}\langle g/\sqrt{n},x^{\prime}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\rho_{i}\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}\|Ax^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

possibly up to an additive error of
\sqrt{n}/(2\log n)
. This program has a few crucial properties:

•

By the feasibility of
Section

1.1
, it follows that the optimum value is at most the right hand side of
Equation

1.3
. Let
x^{\prime}
denote the (approximate) solution returned by the convex program. If
\|Ax^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
, then the point
x^{\prime}
is a solution to
Equation

1.3
with width
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
, which suffices for our purpose.

•

By testing at
x^{\prime}=0
, we see that the optimum value is always non-positive. In particular, any minimizer
x^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{n}
satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\rho_{i}\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)}\|Ax^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\|g\|_{2}.

Therefore, assuming
\|g\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{n})
, if the minimizer
x^{\prime}
satisfies
\|Ax^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\gg\sqrt{n}\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
then it must be that

\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\rho_{i}\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle\ll-\sqrt{n}\operatorname{poly}(\log n),

in which case
x^{\prime\prime}=\widetilde{O}(x^{\prime}/\|Ax^{\prime}\|_{\infty})
is readily seen to satisfy
Equation

1.3
with width
\widetilde{O}(1)
.

•

The program can be written as a linear min-max program (or
\ell_{\infty}
–
\ell_{1}
matrix game):

\min_{x^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{n}}\max_{y\in\Delta_{2n+1}}y_{0}\rho_{0}\left(\langle g/\sqrt{n},x^{\prime}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\rho_{i}\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}y_{i}\langle A_{i},x^{\prime}\rangle/\operatorname{poly}(\log n),

where
\Delta_{2n+1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
denotes the unit simplex consisting of probability distributions on
[2n+1]
, which one can try to solve using a saddle-point mirror descent scheme. At this point, it is natural to think that the correct geometry on
[-1,1]^{n}
is given by the
\infty
-norm, so that one runs into the usual problem of the lack of an
\widetilde{\Omega}(1)
-strongly convex mirror map on
[-1,1]^{n}
. However, we can take advantage of the fact that
\|A_{i}\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{n})
in Spencer’s problem (and that projecting onto
[-1,1]^{n}
with respect to the Euclidean norm is easy) by viewing
[-1,1]^{n}
as a subset of the
\ell_{2}
ball of radius
\sqrt{n}
and using the sublinear primal-dual framework of Clarkson, Hazan, and Woodruff
[
12
]
to show that the linear min-max program can indeed be solved to desired accuracy in time
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
(
Proposition

5.1
).

Running in near input-sparsity time
:

So far, we have assumed that
m=n
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|=\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
. The same discussion extends to handle the case of general
m\leq n^{2}

5
5

5

If
m\geq n^{2}
, then a uniformly random coloring succeeds with high probability.
under the additional assumption that the set system is “dense” i.e.
\sum_{i=1}^{m}|S_{i}|=\widetilde{O}(mn)
. To remove the density assumption, we require a few additional ingredients.

•

First, we isolate variables which appear in at most
n/\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
sets and color them using the input-sparsity time online algorithm for Banaszcyzk’s theorem due to Alweiss, Liu, and Sawhney
[
4
]
(
Appendix

A
). As mentioned earlier, this algorithm loses a polylogarithmic factor in the discrepancy guarantee, compared to Banaszczyk’s theorem. However, since we are coloring only those variables which are in at most
n/\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
sets, such a loss is acceptable.

•

At this point, each of the
n_{1}
remaining variables appears in at least
n/\operatorname{poly}(\log n)
sets and at most
m
sets. Once again, we may assume that
m\leq n_{1}^{2}
, else a uniformly random coloring suffices. Let the total number of non-zero entries in this restricted incidence matrix be
N
, so that
n_{1}n/\operatorname{poly}(\log n)\leq N\leq n_{1}m
. We isolate the at most
n_{1}/\log{n}
variables which are present in at least
(\log n)\cdot N/n_{1}
sets and color them uniformly at random. Note that a uniformly random coloring loses a factor of
\sqrt{\log{n_{1}}}
compared to Spencer’s bound, but this again acceptable, since we are only coloring at most
n_{1}/\log{n}
variables.

•

Finally, all the remaining variables have the property of being present in at most
k=(\log n)\cdot N/n_{1}
sets each and a careful implementation of
[
12
]
using a suitable (but fairly simple) data structure shows that the linear min-max program, restricted to such variables, can be solved in time
\widetilde{O}(kn_{1}+n_{1}^{2})=\widetilde{O}(N)=\widetilde{O}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|S_{i}|)
, as desired.

A_{2}
with columns indexed by
\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{Heavy}}
and
[n]\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{Heavy}}
respectively.

Note that
A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{Heavy}}}
, and by Markov’s inequality,
|\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{Heavy}}|\leq n/\log n
. Therefore, by Bernstein’s inequality and a union bound, a uniformly random

satisfies
\lVert A_{1}v_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\sqrt{n}
with probability at least
9/10
, for a sufficiently large absolute constant
C>0
.

Thus it suffices to find a suitable coloring for
A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times([n]\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{Heavy}})}
. By
Propositions

2.3
and
2.4
, in time
\widetilde{O}(n\cdot((\log n)\operatorname{nnz}(A)/n)+n^{2})=\widetilde{O}(\operatorname{nnz}(A)+n^{2})
and with probability at least
3/4
, we can find
v_{2}^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{[n]\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{Heavy}}}
such that

•

\|A_{2}v_{2}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 2C_{\ref{prop:gauss-program}}\sqrt{n\log(2m/n+2)}
, and

•

T:=\{j:|(v_{2}^{\prime})_{j}|\geq 1-2/\log{n}\}
satisfies
|T|\geq\eta_{\ref{prop:gauss-program}}n/2.

Let
v_{2}
be the random vector obtained from
v_{2}^{\prime}
by randomly rounding each coordinate in

independently to
\{\pm 1\}
in a manner such that
\mathbb{E}[(v_{2})_{j}]=(v_{2}^{\prime})_{j}
for all
j\in[T]
. Then, by Bernstein’s inequality and the union bound, it follows that with high probability,

•

\|A_{2}v_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq 4C_{\ref{prop:gauss-program}}\sqrt{n\log(2m/n+2)}
, and

•

satisfies

so that

can be obtained in time

, and with probability at least

, satisfies the conclusion of

2.2
.
∎

3.
Stability of the linear program

The goal of this section is to prove
Proposition

2.3
, which states that the linear program of Eldan and Singh
[
14
]
is logarithmically stable, in the sense that all points with objective value within a
(1-c_{1}/\log{n})
-factor of the optimum have linearly many coordinates with absolute value at least
1-c_{2}/\log{n}
.

As in the statement of the lemma, fix a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
\lVert A\rVert_{1\to\infty}\leq 1
, and for
C>0
, define

\Gamma_{A,C}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lVert Ax\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\sqrt{n\log(m/n+2)}\wedge\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\}.

Furthermore, define

(3.1)

\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C):=\mathbbm{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})}\left[\max_{y\in\Gamma_{A,C}}\langle g,y\rangle\right]/\sqrt{n}.

Our proof of
Proposition

2.3
requires a few ingredients. First, we record a trivial upper bound on
\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
\lVert A\rVert_{1\to\infty}\leq 1
. For any
C>0
,

\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C)\leq\sqrt{2n/\pi}.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma_{A,C}\subseteq[-1,1]^{n}
, we have

\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C)\leq\mathbbm{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})}\left[\max_{\lVert y\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\langle g,y\rangle\right]/\sqrt{n}=\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[|g|]=\sqrt{2n/\pi}.\qed

Next, we record a consequence of Spencer’s proof of Spencer’s Theorem
[
22
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
22
, Theorem 12]
)
.

Fix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
\lVert A\rVert_{1\to\infty}\leq 1
and let
C>0
. There exists a constant
\delta_{C}>0
(depending only on
C
and independent of
A
) such that

|\Gamma_{A,C}\cap\{\pm 1\}^{n}|\geq(2-\delta_{C})^{n},

where
\delta_{C}\to 0
as
C\to\infty
.

Remark
.

In
[
22
, Theorem 12]
, the corresponding result is only stated for
m=n
. A trivial modification of the proof however immediately gives the desired result.

We will also use concentration of Lipschitz functions with respect to the Gaussian measure.

Lemma 3.3

(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean norm. Then

\mathbb{P}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{\otimes n}}[|f(g)-\mathbb{E}[f(g)]|\geq t]\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/(2L^{2})).

The previous two lemmas allow us to show that for sufficiently large
C
, the trivial upper bound in
Lemma

3.1
is close to sharp.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
\lVert A\rVert_{1\to\infty}\leq 1
and let
C>0
. There exists a constant
\delta^{\prime}_{C}>0
(depending only on
C
and independent of
A
) such that

\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C)\geq\sqrt{2n/\pi}-\delta^{\prime}_{C}\sqrt{n},

where
\delta^{\prime}_{C}\to 0
as
C\to\infty
.

Proof.

For
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
, note that
\operatorname{sgn}(g)\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is distributed uniformly on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Furthermore if
\operatorname{sgn}(g)\in\Gamma_{A,C}
, then

\max_{y\in\Gamma_{A,C}}\langle g,y\rangle\geq\langle g,\operatorname{sgn}(g)\rangle=\lVert g\rVert_{1}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\max_{y\in\Gamma_{A,C}}\langle g,y\rangle-\|g\|_{1}\geq 0]\geq\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{sgn}(g)\in\Gamma_{A,C}]\geq\exp(-2\delta_{C}n),

where
\delta_{C}
is as in
Lemma

3.2
.

Hence, since
g\mapsto\max_{y\in\Gamma_{A,C}}\langle g,y\rangle/\sqrt{n}-\lVert g\rVert_{1}/\sqrt{n}
is
2
-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean norm, it follows from
Lemma

3.3
that we must have

\mathbb{E}[\max_{y\in\Gamma_{A,C}}\langle g,y\rangle/\sqrt{n}-\lVert g\rVert_{1}/\sqrt{n}]\geq-8(\delta_{C})^{1/2}\sqrt{n}

for sufficiently large
n
. The desired result now follows by the linearity of expectation.
∎

As a corollary, we deduce the following stability result for
\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C)
with respect to
C
.

Corollary 3.5
.

There exists an absolute constant

and a non-increasing function

Remark
.

It is an interesting problem in convex geometry to determine whether the Lipschitz constant of
C\mapsto\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(\exp(C))
is sufficiently small for all
C
sufficiently large; our proof of
Corollary

3.5
shows that this is true in an appropriate averaged sense.

Proof.

For
0\leq i\leq\lfloor 1/(9\varepsilon)\rfloor
, consider

. Note that for

, we have that
\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C^{\prime}+\varepsilon)-\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(C^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(x_{i+1})-\operatorname{OPT}_{A}(x_{i})
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(C+\lfloor 1/(9\varepsilon)\rfloor\cdot(9\varepsilon))-\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor 1/(9\varepsilon)\rfloor-1}\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(x_{i+1})-\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\delta\cdot\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\cdot\#\{i:\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(x_{i+1})-\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(x_{i})\geq\delta\cdot\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\}.

By
Lemma

, the left hand side is at most

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{C^{\prime}\sim\operatorname{Unif}[C,2C]}

\displaystyle\geq(8\varepsilon)\cdot(\lfloor 1/(9\varepsilon)\rfloor-\#\{i:\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(x_{i+1})-\operatorname{Opt}_{A}(x_{i})\geq\delta\cdot\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\})

provided
\varepsilon
is small and
C_{\ref{lem:submultiplicative}}(\delta)
is chosen so that
\delta_{C_{\ref{lem:submultiplicative}}}^{\prime}
is sufficiently small compared to

.
∎

4.
Reduction to a Minimax Problem

The remainder of this paper is devoted to establishing
Proposition

2.4
. Since we only need to solve the linear program in
Proposition

2.4
to
(1-1/\operatorname{poly}(\log n))
-relative error, it is natural to consider a first-order method for solving linear programs, such as using the multiplicative weights update (MWU) method (see
[
5
]
for an excellent introduction). Unfortunately, one immediately runs into the issue that the so-called width of the linear program can potentially by
\Theta(n)
, so that the MWU-based solver takes time
\widetilde{O}(n^{3})
. To overcome this obstacle, we introduce a novel method of ‘width reduction’, which takes advantage of the structure of our linear program.

In the next section, we will show how to implement the following key subroutine which, combined with the standard MWU approach, proves
Proposition

2.4
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
C\leq\sqrt{\log{n}}/3
and
n\geq 100
, there exists a randomized algorithm
\operatorname{Regularized-Solve}
for which the following holds. On input:

•

a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\leq n^{2}
,
\lVert A\rVert_{1\to\infty}\leq 1
, and
\max_{i\in[n]}|\operatorname{supp}(Ae_{i})|\leq k
,

•

\rho_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
,
\rho_{+},\rho_{-}\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})^{m}
with
\rho_{0}+\lVert\rho_{+}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\rho_{-}\rVert_{1}=1
,

•

{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lVert{v}\rVert_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{n}
,

•

\Lambda\in[0,2\sqrt{n}]
,

\operatorname{Regularized-Solve}(A,\rho_{0},\rho_{+},\rho_{-},{v},\Lambda)
runs in time
\widetilde{O}(kn+n^{2})
, and with probability at least
1-1/\sqrt{n}
, outputs either:

•

a certificate that the program

\operatorname{sup}_{x\in\Gamma_{A,C}}\langle x,v\rangle\geq\Lambda\sqrt{n}

is not feasible, where

\Gamma_{A,C}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\wedge\lVert Ax\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\sqrt{n\log(m/n+2)}\},\quad\text{or}

•

a point
x\in[-1,1]^{n}
satisfying
\lVert Ax\rVert_{\infty}\leq 10\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{4}
and

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}{v}^{T}x/\sqrt{n}

\displaystyle+\rho_{+}^{T}Ax-\rho_{-}^{T}Ax

\displaystyle\leq-\rho_{0}\Lambda+(C\sqrt{n\log(m/n+2)})(\lVert\rho_{+}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\rho_{-}\rVert_{1})+\sqrt{n}/(\log n)^{3}

Remark
.

Note that it is easy to compute
\arg\min_{x\in[-1,1]^{n}}-\rho_{0}v^{T}x+\rho_{+}^{T}Ax-\rho_{-}^{T}Ax
, which coincides with
-\operatorname{sgn}(-\rho_{0}v^{T}/\sqrt{n}+\rho_{+}^{T}A-\rho_{-}^{T}A)
, in time

. The content of this proposition is that we can efficiently find an approximate optimizer

with

, which is best possible up to logarithmic factors.

Proof of
Proposition

2.4
given

4.1
.

We use the notation in
[

\displaystyle\exists?x\in[-1,1]^{n}:\quad\frac{v^{T}x}{\sqrt{n}}\geq\Lambda;\quad Ax\geq-C_{m,n}\mathbbm{1};\quad-Ax\geq-C_{m,n}\mathbbm{1},

6
6

6

Remark

(Numerical Precision Issues)
.

The above analysis, as written, assumes exact arithmetic; there are two points which are numerically sensitive which can be handled using standard techniques. The first is dividing by
x_{t}
in Lines 9 and 11. By
[
12
, Lemma C.2]
, it suffices to truncate entries smaller than
\operatorname{poly}(n^{-1},\varepsilon)
.

The second point is keeping track of the vector
w_{t}
, and the induced probability distribution, in a numerically stable manner. This is discussed
[
11
, Section G.1]
; for our simplified data structure, however, a substantially simpler solution suffices, which we now sketch.

First, note that the maximum and minimum value of any
w_{t}(i)
over the course of the algorithm is bounded between
4^{-T}
and
4^{T}
. We will maintain the logarithm of each
w_{t}(i)
using
L=C(\log n+\log(1/\varepsilon))
bits, for a sufficiently large constant
C
. In particular, the version of
w_{t}(i)
we work with is within a factor of
(1\pm\varepsilon^{C}n^{-C})^{\widetilde{O}((k+n)\varepsilon^{-2}+m)}
of the true
w_{t}(i)
, which is negligible for
C
sufficiently large.

For maintaining the logarithm of the weights up to this precision in Line 13, note that, when ‘walking up’ the binary tree, it suffices to set the logarithm of the value of a parent node to be equal to the logarithm of the value of the heavier child node, in the case when the logarithms of the values of the children differ by more than
2L
. Otherwise, denoting the value of the lighter child by
z
and the heavier child by
y
, the logarithm of the value at the parent node is
\log(y+z)=\log(y)+\log(1+z/y)
, which can be computed in
O(\operatorname{poly}(\log n,\log(1/\varepsilon)))
-time to
L
digits of precision, since
2^{-2L}\leq|z/y|\leq 1
.
Finally, when ‘walking down’ the binary tree in Line 7, if the logarithms of the values of the children of a node differ by more than
L
, then it suffices to simply descend to the heavier child. Otherwise, the bias of the coin to flip is
y/(y+z)=1/(1+z/y)
, which can be computed to the desired accuracy in time
O(\operatorname{poly}(\log n,\log(1/\varepsilon)))
, noting again that
2^{-L}\leq|z/y|\leq 1
.
