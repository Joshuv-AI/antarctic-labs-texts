Second Order Elliptic Equations and Hodge-Dirac Operators
Erik Duse

Abstract
In this paper we show how a second order scalar uniformly elliptic equation on divergence form with measurable coefficients and Dirichlet boundary conditions can be transformed into a first order elliptic system with half-Dirichlet boundary condition. This first order system involves Hodge-Dirac operators and can be seen as a natural generalization of the Beltrami equation in the plane and we develop a theory for this equation, extending results from the plane to higher dimension. The reduction to a first order system applies both to linear as well as quasilinear second order equations and we believe this to be of independent interest. Using the first order system, we give a new representation formula of the solution of the Dirichlet problem both on simply and finitely connected domains. This representation formula involves only singular integral operators of convolution type and Neumann series there of, for which classical Calderón-Zygmund theory is applicable. Moreover, no use is made of any fundamental solution or Green’s function beside fundamental solutions of constant coefficient operators. Remarkably, this representation formula applies also for solutions of the fully non-linear first order system. We hope that the representation formula could be used for numerically solving the equations. Using these tools we give a new short proof of Meyers’ higher integrability theorem. Furthermore, we show that the solutions of the first order system are Hölder continuous with the same Hölder coefficient as the solutions of the second order equations. Finally, factorization identities and representation formulas for the higher dimensional Beurling-Ahlfors operator are proven using Clifford algebras, and certain integral estimates for the Cauchy transform is extended to higher dimensions.



1
Introduction

1.1
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to show that contrary to common belief, there exists a natural extension of complex analytic and quasiconformal methods to dimensions greater than 2 in the study linear elliptic partial differential equations. Using these methods we will derive global representation formulas for solutions of the Dirichlet problem for scalar linear uniformly elliptic partial differential equations with measurable coefficients on arbitrary simply connected Lipschitz domains. In particular, this will prove existence and uniqueness for weak solutions and also derive local interior estimates of higher integrability, Hölder continuity and differentiability almost everywhere.

Now what do we mean by complex analytic and quasiconformal methods? To set the stage we are interested in the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{div}A(x)\nabla u(x)=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x)=\phi(x),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega
is a simply connected Lipschitz domain,
A(x)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
and
\phi(x)\in W^{1/2,2}(\partial\Omega)
. We say that

is a weak solution of (
1.1
) if for all

we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle A(x)\nabla u(x),\nabla\varphi(x)\rangle dx=0.

(1.3)

In the plane we can introduce a and
A

\displaystyle B(z)=A(z)\nabla u(z)

1&0\end{bmatrix}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

If we set

\displaystyle A^{\star}(z)=\star^{t}A^{-1}(z)\star.

Using

\displaystyle=E(z)+B(z)=(I+A(z))E(z),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}(z)

\displaystyle=E(z)-B(z)=(I-A(z))E(z).

This gives the algebraic equation

A first key step towards a generalization of equation (

[

If a symmetric endomorphism field

\displaystyle K^{-1}|v|^{2}\leq\langle A(x)v,v\rangle\leq K|v|^{2}\,\,\,\text{ for a.e. $x\in\Omega$}

\displaystyle|v|^{2}+|A(x)v|^{2}\leq(K+K^{-1})\langle A(x)v,v\rangle\,\,\,\text{ for a.e. $x\in\Omega$}.

Letting

give rise to the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}(x)=(I-A(x))(I+A(x))^{-1}\mathcal{F}^{+}(x).

The novelty of the present paper is that using Hodge decompositions one can impose a gauge condition so that the there exists a multivector field

with the property that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x),\,\,\,\,\mathcal{F}^{+}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x),

where
(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-},\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+})
is a pair of Hodge-Dirac operators, one formally self-adjoint and the other formally skew-adjoint, taking the role of the Wirtinger derivatives
(\partial_{\overline{z}},\overline{\partial_{z}})
in the plane. More precisely, the Hodge-Dirac operators are related to the exterior derivative on multivectors (i.e. differential forms) through

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\pm}=d\pm\delta,

where
\delta=-d^{\ast}
is the interior derivative. The benefit of introducing the potential field
F
is that this allows one to use powerful integral operator techniques form Clifford and harmonic analysis to study the original Dirichlet problem. As a by product, we will see that this also allows one to generalize much of the quasiconformal methods in the plane used to to study second order elliptic equations to higher dimensions.

It is interesting to note that Clifford analysis techniques have also been used successfully in
[
61
,
62
]
to prove global well-posedness of the water wave problem in 3 dimensions.

correspond to the orthogonal decomposition of
\Lambda V
into the eigenspaces corresponding to the eigenvalues
+1
and
-1
under the main involution.

Definition 3.1

(Hodge Duality)
.

Let
V
be an
n
-dimensional Euclidean vector space and let
e_{V}
denote the volume form of
V
, which in an ON-basis
\{e_{j}\}_{j}
of

\displaystyle e_{V}=e_{1}\wedge...\wedge e_{n}.

Then, for any

we define the
left and right Hodge star map
by

\displaystyle\star w

\displaystyle:=e_{V}\,\,\llcorner\,\,w=e_{V}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\overline{w},

\displaystyle w\star

\displaystyle:=w\,\,\lrcorner\,\,e_{V}=\overline{w}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}e_{V}.

The Hodge star maps have the useful properties that
\star(w\star)=(\star w)\star=w
,
\star\star w=\widehat{w}=w\star\star
and
(w_{1}\wedge(\star w_{2}))\star=\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle
for any
w,w_{1},w_{2}\in\Lambda V
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
v\in V
and
w\in\Lambda V
, then
|v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w|=|w\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}v|=|v||w|
.

Proof.

\displaystyle|v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w|^{2}=\langle v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w,v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w\rangle=\langle w,v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w\rangle=\langle w,|v|^{2}w\rangle=|v|^{2}|w|^{2}.

∎

Note that in general it is
not
true that for any multivectors
w_{1},w_{2}
we have
|w_{1}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w_{2}|\leq|w_{1}||w_{2}|
. What is true is that we always have
|w_{1}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w_{2}|\leq 2^{n}|w_{1}||w_{2}|
where
n
is the dimension of
V
.

We now assume that the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{+}
on
V
is euclidean. We associate the anti-euclidean inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{-}=-\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{+}
on
V
. We denote the two Clifford algebras with respect to the euclidean and anti-euclidean inner product by
\Delta V^{+}
and
\Delta V^{-}
and denote the associated Clifford products by
\triangle
and
\triangledown
respectively and call them positive and negative Clifford multiplication. Note that for any vectors
v_{1},...,v_{k},u_{1},...,u_{k}\in V
we

\displaystyle\langle v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{k},u_{1}\rangle_{-}

\displaystyle=\det[\langle v_{j},u_{k}\rangle_{-}]_{j,k}=\det[-\langle v_{j},u_{k}\rangle_{+}]_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\det[\langle\widehat{v_{j}},u_{k}\rangle_{+}]_{j,k}=\langle\widehat{v_{1}\wedge...\wedge v_{k}},u_{1}\rangle_{+}.

Therefore, by multilinearity we have for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Lambda V

\displaystyle\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{-}=\langle\widehat{w_{1}},w_{2}\rangle_{+}=\langle w_{1},\widehat{w_{2}}\rangle_{+}.

We now prove the following useful anti-commutation relations for positive and negative Clifford multiplication with respect to vectors.

Proposition 3.1
.

For all
u,v\in V
and
w\in\Lambda V
we have

\displaystyle u\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}(v\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}w)=-v\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}(u\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w).

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w=\widehat{w}\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}v.

(3.4)

Proof.

We first prove (
3.4
). We use the Riesz identities for positive and negative Clifford multiplication

\displaystyle v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w

\displaystyle=v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w+v\wedge w,\quad v\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}w=-v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w+v\wedge w

\displaystyle w\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}v

\displaystyle=w\,\,\llcorner\,\,v+w\wedge v,\quad w\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}v=-w\,\,\llcorner\,\,v+w\wedge v.

By Proposition 2.6.3 in
[
51
]
,

\displaystyle v\wedge w=\widehat{w}\wedge v,\quad v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w=-\widehat{w}\,\,\llcorner\,\,v

Thus,

\displaystyle v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w

\displaystyle=v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w+v\wedge w=-\widehat{w}\,\,\llcorner\,\,v+\widehat{w}\wedge v=\widehat{w}\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}v.

We now prove (
3.3
). We use the anti-commutation relations
from

\displaystyle u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(v\wedge w)+v\wedge(u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)=\langle u,v\rangle w

from Theorem 2.8.1 in
[
51
]
and expand using the Riesz identities. This gives using Proposition 2.6.3 in
[
51
]
,

\displaystyle u\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}(v\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}w)

\displaystyle=-u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)+u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(v\wedge w)-u\wedge(v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)+u\wedge(v\wedge w)

\displaystyle=-(v\wedge u)\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w-v\wedge(u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)+\langle u,v\rangle w+v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(u\wedge w)-\langle u,v\rangle w-v\wedge(u\wedge w)

\displaystyle=(u\wedge v)\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w-v\wedge(u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)+v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(u\wedge w)-v\wedge(u\wedge w)

\displaystyle=v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)-v\wedge(u\,\,\lrcorner\,\,w)+v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(u\wedge w)-v\wedge(u\wedge w)

\displaystyle=v\,\,\lrcorner\,\,(u\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w)-v\wedge(u\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w)

\displaystyle=-v\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}(u\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w).

∎

\displaystyle\nu(x)\wedge f(x)=\nu(x)\wedge f_{T}(x),\,\,\,\nu(x)\,\,\lrcorner\,\,f(x)=\nu(x)\,\,\lrcorner\,\,f_{N}(x).

We define

\displaystyle L^{2}_{T}(\partial\Omega,\Lambda):=L^{2}(\partial\Omega,\sigma,\Lambda T\partial\Omega),\,\,L^{2}_{N}(\partial\Omega,\Lambda):=\nu(x)\wedge L^{2}(\partial\Omega,\sigma,\Lambda T\partial\Omega).

\nu(x)\wedge f(x)=0

6.1

We now consider the Dirichlet problem (

. Consider weak solution

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x)
with inverse
(\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x))^{-1}=\widehat{\mathcal{M}^{-1}}(x)
.
Moreover, by Proposition
A.1
in the Appendix, if
\|\mathcal{M}\|<1
then
\|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}\|\leq\|\mathcal{M}\|<1
.

In order not to overburden the notation, we will keep writing
\mathcal{M}(x)
instead of
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x)
. With this in mind the Beltrami equation
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)
is well defined. Splitting
F=\langle F(x)\rangle_{0}+\langle F(x)\rangle_{2}=u(x)+v(x)
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)=du(x)+\delta v(x)+dv(x),\,\,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=du(x)-\delta v(x)+dv(x).

Since
\mathcal{M}
is grade preserving by construction, the Beltrami equation
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)
reduces to the system

\displaystyle du(x)-\delta v(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(x)(du(x)+\delta v(x)),

(6.11)

\displaystyle dv(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(x)dv(x).

(6.12)

Assuming that
M<1
so that the Dirac-Beltrami operator is elliptic, this implies

\displaystyle|dv(x)|\leq M|dv(x)|<|dv(x)|

which implies that
dv(x)=0
. Hence
\mathcal{D}^{\pm}F(x)
are vector fields
v
is a closed bivector field. Solving for
\delta v
in equation (
6.11
) gives

\displaystyle\delta v(x)=(I+\mathcal{M}(x))^{-1}\circ(I-\mathcal{M}(x))(du(x))=A(x)du(x).

Since
\delta^{2}v(x)=0
, it follows that
u
solves the scalar second order (elliptic) equation

\displaystyle\delta A(x)du(x)=0.

Thus, the scalar part of
F
with
F
as above solves a linear
A
-harmonic equation. On the other hand we may from (
6.11
) solve for
du(x)
instead giving

\displaystyle du(x)

\displaystyle=(I-\mathcal{M}(x))^{-1}\circ(I+\mathcal{M}(x))(\delta v(x))

\displaystyle=A(x)^{-1}\delta v(x).

Since
d^{2}u(x)=0
in the sense of distributions it follows that
v
solves the equation

\displaystyle dA(x)^{-1}\delta v(x)=0.

(6.13)

We call (
6.13
) the
A-harmonic conjugate equation
of
\text{div}A(x)\nabla u(x)=0
.

To conclude, we have proven the following theorem:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a simply connected Lipschitz domain. Let
F\in W^{1,2}_{d,\delta}(\Omega,\Lambda^{(0,2)})
be the unique solution to the half-Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}(x)=\phi(x),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

where
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x)
is the exterior extension of a some
\mathcal{M}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
satisfying
\|\|\mathcal{M}(x)\|\|_{L^{\infty}}=m<1
.
Then
u=\langle F\rangle_{0}\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
is the unique weak solution of the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{div}\,A(x)\nabla u(x)=0&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x)=\phi(x),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.,

where
A(x)=(I-\mathcal{M}(x))\circ(I+\mathcal{M}(x))^{-1}
.

Remark 6.1
.

In the special case when
\mathcal{M}(x)=\mu(x)I_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
, for some scalar function
\mu
, we may well take the extension
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\mu(x)I_{\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
instead of
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x)
.

Finally, we recall Theorem 1 in
[
29
]
. Define the scalar function

\displaystyle\lambda(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|}{|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|}&\text{if $\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)\neq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)=0$}\end{array}\right.,

and the multivector field (being either a unit vector or 1)

\displaystyle a(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)-\lambda(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)}{|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)-\lambda(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|}&\text{if $\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)-\lambda(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)\neq 0$}\\
1&\text{if $\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)-\lambda(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)=0$}\end{array}\right..

Then as
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)
and
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)
are vectors and by Theorem 1 in
[
29
]
and Lemma
3.2
this can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=-\lambda(x)a(x)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}a(x)^{-1}.

In particular, in view of Theorem 1 in
[
29
]
we may always assume that
\mathcal{M}(x)
is a conformal linear map. Moreover, if we define the conformal map
\mathcal{C}(x)\in\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
through

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x)v=-\lambda(x)a(x)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}v\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}a(x)^{-1}

for all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}(x)\|=\lambda(x)=\frac{|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|}{|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|}\leq\frac{\|\mathcal{M}(x)\||\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|}{|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|}=\|\mathcal{M}(x)\|.

and we see that the ellipticity bounds are preserved.

6.4
Comparison to the two dimensional case

In dimension two we recall the algebraic isomorphism
\Delta^{ev}{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\cong\Lambda^{0}{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\oplus\Lambda^{2}{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\cong{\mathbb{C}}
of the even subalgebra of the euclidean Clifford algebra
\Delta{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with the complex numbers. In particular we can identify the imaginary unit
i
with the euclidean volume form
e_{12}
. Moreover, the gauge condition
dv(x)=0
for the
A
-harmonic conjugate of
u
is
automatically
satisfied and therefore vacuous in dimension two, contrary to higher dimensions. Moreover, the Hodge star map can be expressed using Clifford algebra by

\displaystyle\star w=e_{12}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w

for any multivector
w\in\Lambda V
. Hence, using
v=\star(v\star)
and setting
w=v\star
, we have

\displaystyle F=u+v=u+\star(v\star)=u+e_{12}w\cong u+iw,

where
w
is a scalar valued field. Hence, the usual stream function
v
in complex notation can be identified with
v\star
in the framework of this paper.

6.5
Multiply connected domains and inhomogeneous equations

Consider the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u(z)=0&z\in\Omega\\
u(z)=\phi(z)&z\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

on a bounded smooth domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
in the plane. If
\Omega
is simply connected it is well-known that
u
is the real of a holomorphic function
f
, i.e.,
f=u+iv
, where
v
is the harmonic conjugate of
v
. In this case the Dirichlet problem for Laplace equation becomes equivalent to the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\partial}f(z)=0&z\in\Omega\\
\mathfrak{R}e[f(z)]=\phi(z)&z\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

for holomorphic functions. If on the other hand
\Omega
is not simply connected there need not exist a global harmonic conjugate
v
of
u
, the two boundary value problems are not equivalent. If one would like to avoid the use of multivalued holomorphic functions in multiply connected domains there exist a nice substitute called the logarithmic conjugation theorem.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a finitely connected domain with
K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{n}
denoting the bounded components of the complement of
\Omega
. For each
j
, let
a_{j}
be in
K_{j}
. If
u
is a real valued harmonic function on
\Omega
, then there exists a holomorphic function
f
on
\Omega
and real numbers
c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n}
such that

\displaystyle u(z)=\mathfrak{R}e[f(z)]+c_{1}\log|z-a_{1}|+c_{2}\log|z-a_{2}|+...+c_{n}\log|z-a_{n}|

for every
z\in\Omega
.

For a proof see
[
6
]
. Using this theorem we see that the Dirichlet problem for Laplace’s equation in a multiply connected domain is equivalent to boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\partial}f(z)=0&z\in\Omega\\
\mathfrak{R}e[f(z)]=\phi(z)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\log|z-a_{j}|,&z\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Here the constants
c_{j}
are determined by the period conditions. The usefulness of Theorem
6.3
is that it reduces the boundary value problem for Laplace equation on a multiply connected domain to a boundary value problem for the Cauchy-Riemann operator. Unfortunately, the proof of Theorem
6.3
does not generalize to higher dimension. Instead we will proceed by a different route, again using Hodge theory.

Thus let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a finitely connected Lipschitz domain and we consider the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) on
\Omega
. Then
\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)
is finite dimensional. Moreover we have the unique orthogonal splitting

\displaystyle A\nabla u=B=B_{T}^{\perp}+\omega_{T},

where
B_{T}^{\perp}\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
and
\omega_{T}\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)
. We can now apply Theorem
6.1
, to get a bivector field
v
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dv(x)=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\delta v(x)=B_{T}^{\perp}(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
v_{T}(x)=0&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

This
v
is unique up to bivectors in
\mathcal{H}_{T}^{2}(\Omega)
. We define

\displaystyle F(x)=u(x)+v(x).

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)

\displaystyle=du(x)+\delta v(x),\quad\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=du(x)-\delta v(x).

On the other hand we consider the div-curl pair
(B,E)=(A(x)\nabla u(x),\nabla u(x))
. We have

\displaystyle E(x)\pm B(x)=\nabla u(x)\pm A(x)\nabla u(x)=du(x)\pm\delta v(x)\pm\omega_{T}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\pm}F(x)\pm\omega_{T}(x).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{+}F(x)+\omega_{T}(x)=(I+A(x))E(x),\quad\mathcal{D}^{-}F(x)-\omega_{T}(x)=(I-A(x))E(x).

This finally gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{-}F(x)-\omega_{T}(x)

\displaystyle=(I-A(x))\circ(I+A(x))^{-1}(\mathcal{D}^{+}F(x)+\omega_{T}(x))

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{-}F(x)-M(x)\mathcal{D}^{+}F(x)

\displaystyle=(I+M(x))\omega_{T}(x).

This leads to the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathcal{D}^{-}F(x)-M(x)\mathcal{D}^{+}F(x)=(I+M(x))\omega_{T}(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}=\phi(x),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Now
\omega_{T}
can be determined form the orthogonality criterion, namely that
\delta v\perp\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)
. Since
\delta v=\delta F
this gives us the finite linear equation system

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\delta F(x),\kappa(x)\rangle dx=0,\,\,\forall\,\kappa\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)

which uniquely determines
\omega_{T}
. Solutions of (
6.5
) are unique up to elements in
\mathcal{H}_{T}^{2}(\Omega)
. By imposing the orthogonality condition that
F\perp\mathcal{H}_{T}^{2}(\Omega)
gives us a unique solution. To conclude we have proven

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a bounded finitely connected domain. To every solution of (
1.1
) on
\Omega
there exists a unique solution
F\in W^{1,2}_{d,\delta}(\Omega,\Lambda^{(0,2)})
of the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)+(I+\mathcal{M}(x))\omega_{T}&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}=\phi&x\in\partial\Omega\\
F\perp\mathcal{H}_{T}^{2}(\Omega)\\
\omega_{T}\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)\\
\int_{\Omega}\langle\delta F(x),\kappa(x)\rangle dx=0,\,\,\forall\,\kappa\in\mathcal{H}_{T}^{1}(\Omega)\\
\end{array}\right..

We now consider the inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{div}A(x)\nabla u(x)=\text{div}\,G(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x)=\phi(x),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

on a simply connected Lipschitz domain
\Omega
and vector field
G\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

We now use Theorem
6.1
to define the bivector field
v
as the unique solution of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dv(x)=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\delta v(x)=\mathcal{A}(x)\nabla u(x)-G(x)&,x\in\Omega\\
v_{T}(x)=0,&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

We now define

\displaystyle F(x)=u(x)+v(x)

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)

\displaystyle=du(x)+\delta v(x)=du(x)+\mathcal{A}(x)du(x)-G(x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)

\displaystyle=du(x)-\delta v(x)=du(x)-\mathcal{A}(x)du(x)+G(x).

Hence,

\displaystyle du(x)=(I+\mathcal{A}(x))(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)+G(x))

which gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)

\displaystyle=(I-\mathcal{A}(x))du(x)+G(x)=(I-\mathcal{A}(x))(I-\mathcal{A}(x))(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)+G(x))+G(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)+(I+\mathcal{M}(x))G(x)

In addition
F_{T}=\phi
. Thus we have proven the following theorem.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a bounded simply connected Lipschitz domain. To each solution of (
6.5
) there exists a unique solution

F\in W^{1,2}_{d,\delta}(\Omega,\Lambda^{(0,2)})
of the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)+(I+\mathcal{M}(x))G(x)&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}(x)=\phi,&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

for every
G\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

6.6
Nonlinear uniformly elliptic equations

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a mapping that satisfies the conditions:

(i)

\mathcal{A}:x\mapsto\mathcal{A}(x,\xi)
is measurable for every
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{A}:\xi\mapsto\mathcal{A}(x,\xi)
is continuous for almost every
x\in\Omega
.

(iii)

\mathcal{A}(x,0)=0
for all
x\in\Omega
.

(iv)

\mathcal{A}
satisfies the uniform ellipticity bound

\displaystyle|\xi-\zeta|^{2}+|\mathcal{A}(x,\xi)-\mathcal{A}(x,\zeta)|^{2}\leq K\langle\mathcal{A}(x,\xi)-\mathcal{A}(x,\zeta),\xi-\zeta\rangle\text{ for a.e. $x\in\Omega$ and all $\xi,\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$}

for some
K\geq 2
.

We want to solve Dirichlet problem for the nonlinear uniformly elliptic equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{div}\,\mathcal{A}(x,\nabla u(x))=0&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x)=\phi(x)&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

with structure field
\mathcal{A}
as above for
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
\phi\in W^{1/2,2}(\partial\Omega)
.

Definition 6.2
.

Define the Cayley transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x,\xi):=(I-\mathcal{A}(x,\xi))\circ(I+\mathcal{A}(x,\xi))^{-1}

for each fixed
x
.

By Lemma
A.5

\mathcal{M}(x,\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is well defined for a.e.
x
. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}
is measurable in
x
and by Lemma
A.6

\mathcal{M}
is
\sqrt{\frac{K-2}{K+2}}
-Lipschitz continuous in
\xi
for a.e.
x
.
We can now argue precisely as in the linear case. If
\Omega
is simply connected we define the bivector field
v
to be a solution of

\displaystyle dv(x)=0,\,\,\,\delta v(x)=\mathcal{A}(x,\nabla u(x)),\,\,\,v_{T}=0,

and if
\Omega
is multiply connected we do suitable modification as in subsection
6.5
. Then an identical argument proves that the multivector field
F=u+v
solves the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x))&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}(x)=\phi(x)&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

For the converse direction we note that
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F:\Omega\to\Lambda^{1}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\oplus\Lambda^{3}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Thus
\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x))
is not a priori well-defined. We therefore extend
\mathcal{M}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to a mapping
\overline{\mathcal{M}}:\Omega\times\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by defining

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{M}}(x,w)=\mathcal{M}(x,\langle w\rangle_{1}).

It the follows that the extended map is still
\sqrt{\frac{K-2}{K+2}}
-Lipschitz. Indeed,

\displaystyle|\overline{\mathcal{M}}(x,w_{1})-\overline{\mathcal{M}}(x,w_{2})|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{M}(x,\langle w_{1}\rangle_{1})-\mathcal{M}(x,\langle w_{2}\rangle_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{K-2}{K+2}}|\langle w_{1}\rangle_{1}-\langle w_{2}\rangle_{1}|=\sqrt{\frac{K-2}{K+2}}|\langle w_{1}-w_{2}\rangle_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{K-2}{K+2}}|w_{1}-w_{2}|.

By abuse of notation we will write
\mathcal{M}
instead of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
. An identical argument as in section
6.3
, shows
u=\frac{1}{2}(F+\overline{F})
solves (
6.6
). We conclude this section by mentioning that by rewriting the second order equation (
6.6
) as a nonlinear Beltrami equation as in (
6.6
) allowed the authors of
[
1
]
to prove an improved Hölder regularity for solutions of (
6.6
).

6.7
Ellipticity of the Dirac–Beltrami equation and Null-Lagrangian

We now consider the ellipticity of Dirac–Beltrami operator
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}-\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}
. We will consider the more general case when
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}
acts on general multivector fields
F:\Omega\to\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
rather than just special case when
F:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}\oplus\Lambda^{2}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
considered in the previous section. Following
[
8
]
, the symbol
\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}(x,\xi):=\sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}}(x,\xi)
acts on multivectors
w\in\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
according to

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}(x,\xi)w=\xi\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}w-\mathcal{M}(x)\xi\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w,

where
x,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and where
\mathcal{M}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{L}(\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
such that
\|\|\mathcal{M}\|\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<1
. Assume that for some
\xi\neq 0
there exists an
w\neq 0
such that
\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}(x,\xi)w=0
. Then

\displaystyle\xi\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}w=\mathcal{M}(x)\xi\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}w.

Hence,

\displaystyle|\xi\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}w|=|\xi||w|\leq\|\mathcal{M}(x)\|\xi||w|\Longrightarrow\|\mathcal{M}(x)\|\geq 1.

Thus,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}
is elliptic on a domain
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
whenever
\sup_{x\in U}\|\mathcal{M}(x)\|<1
. By Lemma
A.2
in the appendix, this is equivalent to
A(x)
being strictly positive, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle A(x)\xi,\xi\rangle>0

for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and all
x\in U
.

The Beltrami operator
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}
can be naturally defined on
F\in\text{dom}_{max}(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+})\cap\text{dom}_{max}(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-})
, where
\text{dom}_{max}
is the maximal domain of
an unbounded operator, (e.g.
[
8
]
). In particular,

\displaystyle W_{d,\delta}^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\text{dom}_{max}(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+})\cap\text{dom}_{max}(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-})

\displaystyle=\{F\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}):F,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\}

\displaystyle=\{F\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}):F,dF,\delta F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\}.

Without any assumptions on traces of
F
, one has for Lipschitz domains in general the strict inclusion
W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)\subset W_{d,\delta}^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
. This should be contrasted with the two dimensional case. Indeed, by the identity

\displaystyle\|Df(z)\|=|f_{\bar{z}}(z)|+|f_{z}(z)|

for a mapping
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}
it follows that

\displaystyle W^{1,2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}})=\{f\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}}):f,f_{\bar{z}},f_{z}\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}})\}.

On the other hand we have for any
F\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
with
\gamma_{T}F=0
and
\Omega
a bounded
C^{2}
-domain then by Gaffney’s inequality with traces (see Corollary 2.1.6 in
[
54
]
) there exists a constant
C>0
depending only
\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\|F\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)}^{2}\leq C\int_{\Omega}(|dF(x)|^{2}+|\delta F(x)|^{2}+|F(x)|^{2})dx

Thus, if
F\in W_{d,\delta}^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
and
\gamma_{T}F\in W^{1/2,2}(\partial\Omega,\Lambda)
, we can write
F=F^{T}_{0}+\Phi
, with
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
and
F^{T}_{0}\in W_{d,\delta}^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
with
\gamma_{T}F^{T}_{0}=0
. Hence, by the Gaffney inequality

\displaystyle W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)=\{F\in W_{d,\delta}^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda):\gamma_{T}F\in W^{1/2,2}(\partial\Omega,\Lambda)\}.

If however
\Omega
is only a Lipschitz domain we have in general only inclusion, see example 10.3.2 in
[
51
]
.

We recall that the ellipticity condition (
1.8
) by Lemma
A.4
implies the distortion inequality

\displaystyle|E(x)|^{2}+|B(x)|^{2}\leq\mathcal{K}\langle E(x),B(x)\rangle.

for the div-curl couple
(B,E)=(\delta F,dF)
and some
\mathcal{K}\geq 2
.
In
[
29
]
,
\langle dF(x),\delta F(x)\rangle
is called the Jacobian of the div-curl pair
(B,E)
and denoted by
J(x,\mathcal{F})
. However, since
\mathscr{E}_{A}[u](x)=\langle dF(x),\delta F(x)\rangle
and to avoid confusion, we will instead refer to the
null-Lagrangian
, e.g.,
[
7
,
28
]
, as the the energy density of the field
F
, and write
\mathscr{E}(x,F)
. Here we recall that
\langle dF(x),\delta F(x)\rangle
is a null-Lagrangian as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle dF(x),\delta F(x)\rangle dx=0

for all
F\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Using that
2(|dF(x)|^{2}+|\delta F(x)|^{2})=|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|^{2}+|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|^{2}

and
4\langle dF(x),\delta F(x)\rangle=|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|^{2}-|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|^{2}
, the distortion inequality (
6.35
) can equivalently be expressed as

\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|^{2}+|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{K}(|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|^{2}-|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|^{2}).

(6.35)

We note that in dimension 2, using that
\|DF(x)\|^{2}=|\partial F(x)|^{2}+|\overline{\partial}F(x)|^{2}
and
\det(DF(x))=J(x,F)=|\partial F(x)|^{2}-|\overline{\partial}F(x)|^{2}
the inequality (
6.35
) can be written as

\displaystyle\|DF(x)\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{K}J(F,x),

which is the classical differential inequality characterizing
\mathcal{K}
-quasiregular mappings in the plane.
Finally we note that in the case when
\mathcal{M}(x)=\mathcal{M}
is constant in the domain
\Omega
, then for any smooth
U\Subset\Omega
it follows form (
4.3
) that the following version of Stokes’ theorem holds.

\displaystyle\int_{U}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}F(x)dx=\int_{\partial U}\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}(\nu(y))F(y)d\sigma(y)

(6.36)

for any
F\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
.

When considering the general Dirac-Beltrami operator one should also distinguish the special case when the linear operator
\mathcal{M}(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is grade preserving for almost all
x\in\Omega
, i.e., when

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x)(\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\subset\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

for all

and almost all

. Set
\mathcal{M}_{k}(x)=\mathcal{M}_{k}(x)|_{\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
and let

for all

so that

. Then for every

(where
F_{j}
=0 if
j<0
or

)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}^{\pm}F\rangle_{k}=dF_{k-1}+\delta F_{k+1},

which gives the coupled system of equations

\displaystyle dF_{k-1}-\delta F_{k+1}=\mathcal{M}_{k}(dF_{k-1}+\delta F_{k+1})

for
k=0,1,2,...,n
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\delta F_{k}

\displaystyle=(I+\mathcal{M}_{k+1})^{-1}(I-\mathcal{M}_{k+1})dF_{k-2}

\displaystyle dF_{k}

\displaystyle=(I-\mathcal{M}_{k+1})^{-1}(I+\mathcal{M}_{k+1})\delta F_{k+2}\

Defining

\displaystyle d\mathcal{A}_{k-1}(x)^{-1}\delta F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=0

(6.38)

which form an

for each

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle d\delta F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=0

or each

.

6.8
Hodge Duality

Assume that we have a self-dual Beltrami operator
\mathcal{M}(x)
, i.e., that
\mathcal{M}^{c}(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)
. Then by definition any solution

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)

is also a solution of

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}^{c}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\star\mathcal{M}(x)(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)\star)\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)\star=\mathcal{M}(x)(\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)\star)

Thus, by Lemma
4.1
, any self-dual equation also solves

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}(\widehat{F(x)}\star)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}(\widehat{F(x)}\star).

Note that if
F:\Omega\to\Lambda^{ev}V
, then
\widehat{F(x)}=F(x)
and so the self-dual equations become

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}(F(x)\star)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}(F(x)\star).

In dimension two the Hodge star map coincides with the standard complex structure
J
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. In this case, the self-duality of
\mathcal{M}(x)
is equivalent to the condition that

\displaystyle J^{-1}\mathcal{M}(x)J=\mathcal{M}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{M}(x)J-J\mathcal{M}(x)=0\text{ for a.e. $x\in\Omega$},

i.e.,
\mathcal{M}(x)
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear. In this case, Beltrami equation reduces to the
anti
-
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear Beltrami equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\overline{z}}f(z)=\mu(z)\overline{\partial f(z)}

In subsection
6.3
we saw that any solution of
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)
with
F\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\Lambda^{(0,2)})
also solved an equation of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)=\mathcal{C}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)

where
\mathcal{C}(x)
is a conformal map for a.e.
x
. In view of the discussion in this subsection it follows that
F
also solves its dual equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}(F(x)\star)=\mathcal{C}(x)\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}(F(x)\star).

7
The Cauchy Transform on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

7.1
Dirac Operators and the Cauchy Transforms

Definition 7.1

(Cauchy transform)
.

The
Cauchy transforms

\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
are acting
C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda)
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{y-x}{|y-x|^{n}}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\Phi(y)dy,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{-}\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{n}}\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}\Phi(y)dy,

(7.2)

for
\Phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda)
.

We also recall the Riesz potentials

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{\Phi(y)}{|y-x|^{n-\alpha}}dy.

where
0<\alpha<n
. We then clearly have the bound

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(x)|\leq|\mathcal{I}_{1}|\Phi|(x)|

for all
\Phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

The Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality for a Riesz potentials
[
55
, p. 119]
shows that for
1<p<n
and
p^{\ast}=\frac{np}{n-p}<+\infty
there exists a constant
A_{p}
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}_{1}f(x)\|_{p^{\ast}}\leq A_{p}\|f\|_{p},

for every
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. This immediately implies the following theorem:

Theorem 7.1
.

For all
1<p<n
such that
p^{\ast}=\frac{np}{n-p}<+\infty
, the Cauchy transforms
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
extends to continuous maps
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to L^{p^{\star}}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. In particular there exists an absolute constant
A_{p}=A(p,n)
such that for all
f\in L^{p^{\star}}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}^{\pm}F\|_{p^{\star}}\leq A_{p}\|F\|_{p}.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let
G\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda)
. Then the weak derivatives satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}\mathcal{C}^{+}G(x)=G(x)\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}\mathcal{C}^{-}G(x)=G(x).

Proof.

First assume that
F\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then by the Cauchy-Pompieu formula (
4.4
) that the solution to
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)=G(x)
is given by
F(x)=\mathcal{C}^{+}G(x)
. Since
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
, the result follows by a standard approximation argument.
∎

7.2
Hölder estimates for the Cauchy Transforms

Lemma 7.1
.

If
n=2k
then

\displaystyle\frac{\xi-x}{|\xi-x|^{n}}-\frac{\xi-y}{|\xi-y|^{n}}=\frac{(\xi-x)\big[(\xi-y)^{n-1}-(\xi-x)^{n-1}\big](\xi-y)}{|\xi-x|^{n}|y-\xi|^{n}}

If
n=2k+1
then

\displaystyle\frac{\xi-x}{|\xi-x|^{n}}-\frac{\xi-y}{|\xi-y|^{n}}=\frac{(\xi-x)\big[|\xi-y|(\xi-y)^{n-2}-|\xi-x|(\xi-x)^{n-2}\big](\xi-y)}{|\xi-x|^{n}|y-\xi|^{n}}.

Proof.

If
n=2k
, then
|\xi-x|^{n}=(|\xi-x|^{2})^{k}=(\xi-x)^{2k}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle(\xi-x)|\xi-y|^{n}-|\xi-x|^{n}(\xi-y)=(\xi-x)(\xi-y)^{2k-1}(\xi-y)+(\xi-x)(\xi-x)^{2k-1}(\xi-y)

\displaystyle=(\xi-x)\big[(\xi-y)^{2k-1}-(\xi-x)^{2k-1}\big](\xi-y).

If
n=2k+1
, then
|\xi-x|^{n}=|\xi-x|(|\xi-x|^{2})^{k}=|\xi-x|(\xi-x)^{2k}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle(\xi-x)|\xi-y|^{n}-|\xi-x|^{n}(\xi-y)=(\xi-x)|\xi-y|(y-\xi)^{2k-1}(\xi-y)-(\xi-x)|\xi-x|(\xi-x)^{2k-1}(\xi-y)

\displaystyle=(\xi-x)\big[|\xi-y|(\xi-y)^{2k-1}-|\xi-x|(\xi-x)^{2k-1}\big](\xi-y).

∎

Lemma 7.2
.

If
n=2k
,
n>2
, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\xi-x}{|\xi-x|^{n}}-\frac{\xi-y}{|\xi-y|^{n}}\bigg|\leq c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{|x-y|^{j}|\xi-x|^{n-1-j}}{|\xi-x|^{n-1}|y-\xi|^{n-1}}

If
n=2k+1
,
n>2
, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\xi-x}{|\xi-x|^{n}}-\frac{\xi-y}{|\xi-y|^{n}}\bigg|\leq c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\bigg(\delta_{|\xi-x|>|\xi-y|}\frac{|x-y|^{j}|\xi-x|^{n-1-j}}{|\xi-x|^{n-1}|y-\xi|^{n-1}}+\delta_{|\xi-x|<|\xi-y|}\frac{|x-y|^{j}|\xi-y|^{n-1-j}}{|\xi-x|^{n-1}|y-\xi|^{n-1}}\bigg).

Proof.

First assume that
n=2k
. Set
\eta=\xi-x
. Then

\displaystyle(\xi-y)^{n-1}-(\xi-x)^{n-1}=(\eta+x-y)^{n-1}+\eta^{n-1}=\eta^{n-1}+R-\eta^{n-1},

where
R
is sum of products, each containing at least one factor
(x-y)
. Moreover, we have the estimate

\displaystyle|R|\leq c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|\eta|^{n-1-j}=c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|\xi-x|^{n-1-j}

for some constant
c_{n}
depending on
n
. Thus,

\displaystyle\big|(\xi-x)\big[(\xi-y)^{n-1}-(\xi-x)^{n-1}\big](\xi-y)\big|\leq c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|\xi-x||x-y|^{j}|\xi-x|^{n-1-j}|\xi-y|.

Now assume that
n=2k+1
. If
|\xi-y|>|\xi-x|
set
\eta=\xi-x
, and we get

\displaystyle|\xi-y|^{n-1}-|\xi-x|^{n-1}=|\eta+x-y|^{n-1}-|\eta|^{n-1}\leq\big(|\eta|+|x-y|\big)^{n-1}-|\eta|^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|\eta|^{n-1-j}=c_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|\xi-x|^{n-1-j}.

If
|\xi-y|<|\xi-x|
set
\zeta=\xi-y
, and we get

\displaystyle|\xi-y|^{n-1}-|\xi-x|^{n-1}=|\zeta|^{n-1}-|\zeta+y-x|^{n-1}\leq\big(|\zeta|+|x-y|\big)^{n-1}-|\zeta|^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|\zeta|^{n-1-j}=c^{\prime}_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|\xi-y|^{n-1-j}.

∎

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
n>2
and assume that
p>n
. If
\Phi\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
then
\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi
is Hölder continuous with Hölder constant
\displaystyle\alpha=1-\frac{n}{p}
.

Remark 7.1
.

This is an extension of Theorem 4.3.13 in
[
2
]
for the Cauchy transform in the plane to higher dimension. The only subtlety in proof compared to the plane is the more complicated estimate in Lemma
7.2
.

Proof.

We may assume that
|x-y|<1
. By Lemma
7.2
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(x)-\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(y)|=\bigg|\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\bigg(\frac{\xi-x}{|\xi-x|^{n}}-\frac{\xi-y}{|\xi-y|^{n}}\bigg)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\Phi(\xi)d\xi\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\bigg(\frac{|x-y|^{j}|\xi-x|^{n-1-j}}{|\xi-x|^{n-1}|y-\xi|^{n-1}}+\frac{|x-y|^{j}|\xi-y|^{n-1-j}}{|\xi-x|^{n-1}|y-\xi|^{n-1}}\bigg)|\Phi(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\xi-x|^{j}|y-\xi|^{n-1}}+\frac{1}{|\xi-x|^{n-1}|y-\xi|^{j}}\bigg)|\Phi(\xi)|d\xi.

We now apply Hölder’s inequality with Hölder conjugate pair chosen so that
(n-1)q<n
. Then we get

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(x)-\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\|\Phi\|_{L^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}\bigg\{\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{|\xi-x|^{qj}|\xi-y|^{q(n-1)}}d\xi\bigg)^{1/q}+\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{|\xi-x|^{q(n-1)}|y-\xi|^{qj}}d\xi\bigg)^{1/q}\bigg\}.

Since

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{|\xi-x|^{qj}|\xi-y|^{q(n-1)}}d\xi=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{|\eta|^{qj}|\eta+x-y|^{q(n-1)}}d\eta

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|x-y|^{n}}{|x-y|^{qj+q(n-1)}|\zeta|^{qj}|\zeta+\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}|^{q(n-1)}}d\zeta

\displaystyle=|x-y|^{n-qj-q(n-1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{|\zeta|^{qj}|\zeta+\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}|^{q(n-1)}}d\zeta

and similarly for the other integral, and since

\displaystyle I:=\sup_{x\neq y}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{|\zeta|^{qj}|\zeta+\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}|^{q(n-1)}}d\zeta<+\infty

we find that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(x)-\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2Ic_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\|\Phi\|_{L^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x-y|^{j}|x-y|^{(n-qj-q(n-1))/q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2nIc_{n}}{\omega_{n}}\|\Phi\|_{L^{p}}|x-y|^{1-n/p}

∎

Lemma 7.4

Proposition 7.2

For any ball

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\psi(x,y)|dx\leq\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{|y-x|^{n-1}}dx+\int_{B_{1}(0)}|y|dx\leq c_{1},

for some constant
c_{1}
independent of
y
. When
|y|>1
we get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\psi(x,y)|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{|y-x|^{n-1}}dx+\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{|y|^{n}}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|y|^{n-1}}\bigg(\int_{B_{1}(0)n}\frac{1}{|1-y^{-1}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}x|^{n-1}}dx+\int_{B_{1}(0)}dx\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}}{|y|^{n-1}}

for some constant
c_{2}
independent of
y
. Let
g(y)=\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\psi(x,y)|dx
. This gives

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{n/(n-1)}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{(n-1)/n}=\int_{B_{1}(0)}c_{1}^{n/(n-1)}dy+c_{2}^{n/(n-1)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{|y|^{n}}dy<+\infty

By Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}(0)|}\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\mathcal{C}^{+}\phi(x)-(\mathcal{C}^{+}\phi)_{B_{1}(0)}|dx\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}|B_{1}(0)|}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g(y)|\phi(y)|dy

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\|\phi\|_{L^{n}}

for some constant

, and the proof is complete.
∎

8
The Beurling-Ahlfors and Riesz Transforms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

8.1
The Beurling-Ahlfors Transform on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

In the ground breaking papers
[
22
]
and
[
25
]
the authors introduced a higher dimensional generalisation of the classical Beurling-Ahlfors transform to study quasiconformal
4
-manifolds and removable singularities of quasiregular maps in even dimensions respectively. Furthermore this operator was also used to give a local formulae for characteristic classes on even dimensional quasiconformal manifolds, see
[
18
]
. This higher dimensional operator, denoted by
\mathcal{S}
is intimately connected with Hodge-Morrey decompositions of differential forms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Indeed, let
\omega\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a differential
k
-form. Then the Hodge-Morrey decomposition of
\omega
is given by

\displaystyle\omega=d\alpha+\delta\beta,

with
\alpha\in\text{ker}(\delta)\cap W^{1,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k-1}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\beta\in\text{ker}(d)\cap W^{1,p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k+1}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and where
\delta=d^{\ast}
. The Beurling-Ahlfors transform on
\mathcal{S}:L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is defined by the rule

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\omega)=d\alpha-\delta\beta,

or alternatively
\mathcal{S}=(d\delta-\delta d)\circ\Delta^{-1}
, where
\Delta
is the Laplacian.

In
[
25
]
Theorem 8.1 it is shown that the Beurling-Ahlfors operator has the following properties:

(i)

\mathcal{S}(d\alpha)=d\alpha
for all
\alpha
with
d\alpha\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}(\delta\alpha)=-\delta\alpha
for all
\alpha
with
\delta\alpha\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

(iii)

\mathcal{S}
is self-adjoint and
\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{S}=\text{Id}
and
\mathcal{S}^{-1}=\mathcal{S}
.

(iv)

\mathcal{S}
anti-commutes with the Hodge star, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\star=-\star\mathcal{S}.

(v)

\mathcal{S}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is an isometry and
\mathcal{S}
is bounded on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

For applications it is of great interest to know the
L^{p}
-norm of
\mathcal{S}
. Indeed, in the paper
[
25
]
Theorem 8.4 the authors prove the following bound

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq C(n)\max\bigg\{\frac{1}{p-1},p-1\bigg\},\,\,1<p<+\infty,

(8.1)

where
n
is the dimension and

is a polynomial in
n
not depending on
p
. In fact the authors conjecture that

for all

. This conjecture remains open so far and has been the focus of great attention over the years. It is even open in dimension

. The bound

has subsequently been improved over the years by several authors, e.g.,
[
12
]
,
[
49
]
,
[
50
]
using in particular Bellman function techniques and Burkholder’s sharp inequality for martingale transforms on
L^{p}
. The best bound so far is proven in
[
37
]
and given by

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq(1+n/2)(p-1),

(8.2)

where
p^{\star}:=\max\{p/(p-1),p\}
. In fact when
\mathcal{S}
is restricted to the spaces
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then in
[
37
]
it is shown that the

-norms are have the estimates

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{k}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\bigg(\frac{2k(n-k)}{n}+1\bigg)(p^{\star}-1)\leq(2k+1)(p^{\star}-1).

(8.3)

8.2
Factorisation formula

Let

denote the Schwartz space of differential forms on

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}):=\{\omega\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}):\sup_{x}|x^{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}\omega(x)\ |\text{ for all muti-indices $\alpha$ and $\beta$}\}.

One easily checks that

for

The Fourier transform

of a multicovector field

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\omega)(\xi)=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi})^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\omega(x)e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}dx.

In Theorem 8.2 in
[

it is shown that

is an isometry on

for every

. Let

be the orthogonal transformation

\displaystyle M(\xi)=I-2|\xi|^{-1}\xi\otimes\xi,

being the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to
\xi
. Let
\widehat{M}_{\xi}:\mathcal{L}(\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to\mathcal{L}(\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
denote its exterior extension (in the notation in
[
25
]

\widehat{M}(\xi)=M_{\#}(\xi)
). In Theorem 8.10 in
[
25
]
is shown that the Fourier multiplier of

is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}\omega)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\widehat{M}(\xi)\mathcal{F}(\omega)(\xi).

Here
\widehat{M}(\xi)
is identified with its complexification acting on
\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
so that for any

The Fourier multiplier of the Beurling transform can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}\omega)(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\xi}{|\xi|}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\widehat{\mathcal{F}(\omega)(\xi)}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\frac{i\xi}{|\xi|}.

(8.4)

Note that here

denotes the involution on

(

We will give to different proofs.

Proof 1.

Since

Proof 2.

We use the fact that

8.3
Connection to the Cauchy Transforms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We now come to the important connection between the Cauchy transforms and the Beurling–Ahlfors transform on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Theorem 8.1
.

For any
\Phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda)
, the weak derivatives satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi=\mathcal{S}\Phi,\,\,\,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}\mathcal{C}^{-}\Phi=\mathcal{S}\Phi.

(8.6)

Proof.

By definition of the Beurling–Ahlfors transform, if
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)=\Phi(x)
, then
\mathcal{S}\Phi(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)
. By Theorem
7.1
,

, and so
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}\mathcal{C}^{+}\Phi(x)
. This proves the first identity. The second follow similarly.

9.3
Dirichlet Problem for Laplace Equation

In the case of the Laplace equation with Dirichlet boundary condition, we have
A(x)\equiv I
, and so
\mathcal{M}(x)=\mathscr{C}(I)\equiv 0
. Thus, Theorem
1.1
shows that Laplace equation reduces to the homogeneous Hodge-Dirac equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=0&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}(x)=\phi(x)&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

which has the solution

\displaystyle F(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}\nu(y)\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}(\phi(y)+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-}(\phi)(y))d\sigma(y).

Using that
u(x)=\frac{1}{2}(F(x)+\overline{F(x)})
, we get

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\bigg(\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{n}}\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}\nu(y)\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}(\phi(y)+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-}(\phi)(y))+\frac{y-x}{|y-x|^{n}}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\nu(y)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}((\phi(y)+\overline{\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-}(\phi)(y)})\bigg)d\sigma(y).

If we define

\displaystyle f^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{y-x}{|y-x|^{n}}\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\nu(y)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}((\phi(y)+\overline{\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-}(\phi)(y)})d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle f^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{x-y}{|y-x|^{n}}\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}\nu(y)\text{\scriptsize{$\triangledown$}}((\phi(y)+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-}(\phi)(y))d\sigma(y),

we see that
u=f^{+}+f^{-}
is the sum of two fields such that
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}f^{+}=0
and
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}f^{-}=0
. This is analogous to the two dimensional case, where every harmonic function on a simply connected domain can be written (uniquely up to a constant) as a sum of a holomorphic and anti-homorphic function.

\Phi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with respect to the domain
\Omega
is defined to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{N}^{+}(\Phi)(x):=F(x).

It follows immediately from the definitions that we have the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{T}(\Phi)(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}\mathcal{C}_{T}^{-}(\Phi)(x).

Proposition 10.3
.

The tangential Cauchy transform has the integral representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{T}^{+}(\Phi)(x)=\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(x)-\int_{\partial\Omega}E(y-x)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\nu(y)(\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(y)+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{+}\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(y))d\sigma(y).

(10.18)

Proof.

Set
G=F-\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(x)
. Then
G
solves the homogeneous Hodge-Dirac equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}G(x)=0&x\in\Omega,\\
G_{T}(x)=-\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(x)&x\in\partial\Omega,\\
\gamma_{N}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)\perp\mathcal{H}_{T}(\Omega),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

By the definition of the tangential to normal Hilbert transform
G
is given by

\displaystyle G(x)=-\int_{\partial\Omega}E(y-x)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\nu(y)(\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(y)+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{+}\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(y))d\sigma(y)

and hence

is given by

\displaystyle F(x)=\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(x)-\int_{\partial\Omega}E(y-x)\text{\tiny{$\triangle$}}\nu(y)(\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(y)+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{+}\gamma_{T}\mathcal{C}^{+}(\chi_{\Omega}\Phi)(y))d\sigma(y).

∎

10.5

Combining the reduction steps in Subsections

-

is a simply connected Lipschitz domain. We have

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=G+H=\mathcal{C}_{T}^{+}\circ\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}\circ\mathcal{E}^{-}\circ(I+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-})\phi+\mathcal{E}^{-}\circ(I+\mathcal{H}_{TN}^{-})\phi

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{C}_{T}^{+}\circ\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}H)(x)+H(x).

Moreover, since (

) shows that (

To conclude we have proven that unique solution

is given by

\displaystyle F(x)=(\mathcal{C}_{T}^{+}\circ\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}H)(x)+H(x)

where we recall that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(f)(x)

\displaystyle=(I-\mathcal{S}_{T}\circ\mathcal{M})^{-1}\circ\mathcal{S}_{T}\mathcal{M}(f)(x)

and

through

Remark 10.1
.

One should note that the only operator in the representation formula (

is the operator

. All the other operators are either independent of both the domain

10.6
Representation formula for solutions in the non-linear uniformly elliptic case

In the nonlinear case we again prove solvability and representation formulas in a more general setting for fully non-linear Dirac–Beltrami equations which takes values in the the full De Rham complex and need not be derived from an associated second order uniformly quasilinear elliptic equation.

Theorem 10.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a bounded simply connected Lipschitz domain and assume that
\mathcal{M}:\Omega\times\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
satisfy the following.

(i)

\mathcal{M}(x,0)=0
for all
x\in\Omega

(ii)

x\mapsto\mathcal{M}(x,w)
is measurable in
x
for all
w\in\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

(iii)

\mathcal{M}(x,\cdot)
is continuous
k
-Lipschitz continuous for some
0\leq k<1
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.

Then the boundary value problem for the fully non-linear Dirac–Beltrami equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)=\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x))&x\in\Omega,\\
F_{T}(x)=\phi(x)&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

with
\phi\in W^{1/2,2}(\partial\Omega,\Lambda)
is uniquely solvable in
W^{1,2}_{d,\delta}(\Omega,\Lambda)
up to addition of constants. Moreover the solution is given by the representation formula

\displaystyle F=H+\mathcal{C}_{T}^{+}(I-\mathcal{S}_{T}\mathcal{M})^{-1}(-\mathcal{S})_{T}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H,

(10.28)

where
H
is a solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}H(x)=0&x\in\Omega,\\
H_{T}(x)=\phi(x)&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

Proof.

We take a slightly different route in the nonlinear case. Let
H\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
be a solution of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}H(x)=0&x\in\Omega,\\
H_{T}(x)=\phi(x)&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

Define
G=F-H
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}G(x)=\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)-\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H(x)=\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x))-\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H(x)=\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}G(x))-\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H(x).

Consequently
G
solves the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}G(x)=\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}G(x))-\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H(x)&x\in\Omega,\\
G_{T}(x)=0&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

if and only if
F
solves (
10.2
). Moreover, we note that both the left and right hand side lie in
L^{2}
. We may therefore apply the tangential Beurling–Ahlfors transform
\mathcal{S}_{T}
to both sides and get

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}G(x)=\mathcal{S}_{T}\mathcal{M}(x,\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}G(x))-\mathcal{S}_{T}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H(x)&x\in\Omega,\\
G_{T}(x)=0&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

Consider the operator
T:L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle T(\Phi)(x):=\mathcal{S}_{T}\mathcal{M}(x,\Phi)(x)

fo
\Phi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
. By assumption on
\mathcal{M}
and the fact that
\mathcal{S}_{T}
is an isometry on
L^{2}
we get for all
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)

\displaystyle\|T(\Phi_{1})-T(\Phi_{2})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{S}_{T}(\mathcal{M}(x,\Phi_{1}(x))-\mathcal{M}(x,\Phi_{2}(x))|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{M}(x,\Phi_{1}(x))-\mathcal{M}(x,\Phi_{2}(x))|^{2}dx\leq k^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\Phi_{1}(x)-\Phi_{2}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=k^{2}\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Thus
T
is a
k
-contraction on
L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
. It then follows from Banach’s fixed point theorem that the operator
I-T:L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\Lambda)
is a homeomorphism (
[
23
, Thm. 2.1 p. 11]
). Thus
(I-T)^{-1}
exists and is continuous. Moreover, by induction

\displaystyle\|T^{n}(\Phi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq k^{n}\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{n}\Phi=0
for every
\Phi\in L^{2}
. Thus
0
is the unique fixed point of
T
. This implies that the equation

\displaystyle(I-T)\Phi=\Psi

for
\Phi
has the unique solution given by the limit of iterations given by the method of successive approximations generalizing the Neumann series in the linear case. More precisely if we let
\Phi_{0}=0
, we set

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle=T(\Phi_{0})+\Psi=\Psi,

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle=T(\Phi_{1})+\Psi=T(\Psi)+\Psi

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}

\displaystyle=T(\Phi_{n-1})+\Psi=T(T(\Phi_{n-2})+\Psi)+\Psi=...,

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{n}=\Phi.

Thus, we see that
G
solves

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}G(x)=(I-\mathcal{S}_{T}\mathcal{M})^{-1}(-\mathcal{S}_{T})\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}H(x)&x\in\Omega,\\
G_{T}(x)=0&x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right..

From here on we can argue as in the linear case.
∎

11.1

Definition 11.1

(Critical exponents)
.

Let
p_{M}
be defined so that

\displaystyle M\|\mathcal{S}\|_{L^{p_{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda^{(1,3)}){\mathbb{R}}^{n}}=1

(11.1)

We call

the critical exponent with respect to

If Iwaniec’s conjecture is true, i.e. that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\Lambda{\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\max\{(p-1)^{-1},p-1\},

the the critical exponent is equal to

\displaystyle p_{M}=1+\frac{1}{M}.

We now recall the
L^{p}
-Gaffney inequality.

Theorem 11.1

(Proposition 4.3 in
[
52
]
)
.

Let

. Then for all

there exists a constant

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\nabla\otimes F(x)|^{p}dx\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(|dF(x)|^{p}+|\delta F(x)|^{p})dx

(11.2)

Equivalently, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(n,p)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\nabla\otimes F(x)|^{p}dx\leq C^{\prime}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F(x)|^{p}+|\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F(x)|^{p})dx

(11.3)

We note that since

Lemma 11.1
.

Let
F\in W^{1,2}_{d,\delta}(\Omega,\Lambda^{(0,2)}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})

\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F

11.2
Hölder Regularity

The proof that weak solutions of

\displaystyle\text{div}\,A(x)\nabla u(x)=0

(11.12)

are Hölder continuous goes back to the classical work of E. De Giorgi in
[
19
]
using Caccioppoli inequalities and J. Nash in
[
47
]
using parabolic equations. Later the same result was also proved using Harnack inequalities by J. Moser in
[
45
]
. Here of course as usual this result holds true under the uniform ellipticity assumption

\displaystyle\lambda|v|^{2}\leq\langle A(x)v,v\rangle\leq\Lambda|v|^{2}

for all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
x\in\Omega
for some domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
when
A
is symmetric, though the symmetry of
A
is not needed.

We will now first show that the Hölder continuity of
u
implies Hölder continuity of the associated field
F
solving the Dirac–Beltrami equation.

For the readers convenience we recall the definition of the Campanato spaces
\mathcal{L}^{p,\lambda}(\Omega,\Lambda)
.

Definition 11.2

(Camapanto spaces)
.

Set
\Omega_{\rho}(x_{0})=\Omega\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{p,\lambda}(\Omega,\Lambda):=\bigg\{F\in L^{p}(\Omega,\Lambda):\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{0}\in\Omega\\
\rho>0\end{subarray}}\rho^{-\lambda}\int_{\Omega_{\rho}(x_{0})}|F(x)-(F)_{x_{0},\rho}|^{p}dx<+\infty\bigg\},

where

\displaystyle(F)_{x_{0},\rho}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{x_{0}}(\rho)|}\int_{\Omega_{x_{0}}(\rho)}F(x)dx.

The Campanato space
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda,p}(\Omega,\Lambda)
is equipped with the semi-norm

\displaystyle[F]_{p,\lambda}^{p}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{0}\in\Omega\\
\rho>0\end{subarray}}\rho^{-\lambda}\int_{\Omega_{\rho}(x_{0})}|F(x)-(F)_{x_{0},\rho}|^{p}dx

and the norm

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p,\lambda}(\Omega,\Lambda)}:=[F]_{p,\lambda}+\|F\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\Lambda)}.

We also recall Campanato’s theorem

Theorem 11.3

(Campanato’s theorem)
.

For
n<\lambda\leq n+p
and
\alpha=(\lambda-n)/p
we have
\mathcal{L}^{p,\Lambda}(\Omega,\Lambda)\cong C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega,\Lambda)
and the Hölder semi-norm

\displaystyle[F]_{C^{0,\alpha}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Omega\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|F(x)-F(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}

is equivalent to the Campanato semi-norm
[F]_{p,\lambda}
.

For a proof for scalar functions see Theorem 5.5 in
[
31
]
. The proof for vector valued functions is the same.

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
F\in W^{1,2}_{d,\delta}(\Omega,\Lambda)
be a solution of
\mathcal{D}^{\tiny-}F=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{D}^{\tiny+}F
. Then for every
B_{r}(x_{0})\Subset\Omega
there exists a constant
C=C(n,\lambda,\Lambda)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|F(x)-(F)_{x_{0},r}|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{B_{2r}(x_{0})}|u(x)-(u)_{x_{0},2r}|^{2}dx.

In particular, for any
U\Subset\Omega
we have
[F]_{2,\lambda}\leq C[u]_{2,\lambda}
on

A.1
Cayley Transform

Definition A.1
.

Let
V
be a real finite dimensional vector space and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(V)
be such that
-1\notin\sigma(T)
. The
Cayley transform
of
T
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(T):=(I-T)\circ(I+T)^{-1}

Recall that an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(V)
is called positive if for all
x\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\langle Tx,x\rangle>0.

Lemma A.1
.

Assume that
-1\notin\sigma(T)
. Set
y=(I+T)(x)
. Then we have the following identities.

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(T)(y)

\displaystyle=(I-T)(x)

(A.1)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(y+\mathscr{C}(T)(y))

(A.2)

\displaystyle Tx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(y-\mathscr{C}(T)(y))

(A.3)

Lemma A.2
.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(V)
is positive if and only if
\|\mathscr{C}(T)\|<1
. Furthermore, in this case
\mathscr{C}
is an involution.

Proof.

If
T
is positive then
-1\notin\sigma(T)
and so
\mathscr{C}(T)
is well-defined. Set
M=\mathscr{C}(T)
. For any
x\in V
different from
0
we have

\displaystyle|x|^{2}-|Mx|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle x,x\rangle-\langle x,M^{\ast}Mx\rangle=\langle x,(I-M^{\ast}M)x\rangle

\displaystyle=\{\langle x,M^{\ast}x\rangle-\langle x,Mx\rangle=0\}=\langle x,(I-M)^{\ast}(I+M)x\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(I-M)x,(I+M)x\rangle=\{\text{ identity \eqref{eq:IdCayley1}}\}=\langle T(I+M)x,(I+M)x\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Ty,y\rangle,

with
y=(I+M)x
. Hence
\|M\|<1
and so
-1\notin\sigma(M)
. Conversely, if
\|M\|<1
, then

\displaystyle 0<(1-\|M\|)^{2}|x|^{2}=|x|^{2}-|Mx|^{2}=\langle Ty,y\rangle.

Hence
T
is positive and
-1\notin\sigma(T)
. Moreover, by definition

\displaystyle M(I+T)=I-T

which implies that
T=(I-M)(I+M)^{-1}=\mathscr{C}(M)
. Thus,
\mathscr{C}
is an involution.
∎

We note that if
T=\frac{1}{2}(T+T^{\ast})+\frac{1}{2}(T-T^{\ast})
, i.e.,
T
is decomposed into its symmetric and anti-symmetric part, then for all
x\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\langle Tx,x\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle(T+T^{\ast})x,x\rangle,

since

\displaystyle\langle(T-T^{\ast})x,x\rangle=\langle Tx,x\rangle-\langle T^{\ast}x,x\rangle=\langle Tx,x\rangle-\langle x,Tx\rangle=0.

Example A.1
.

Assume that
A=\sigma I
is an isotropic matrix with
\sigma\neq-1
. Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(A)=\frac{1-\sigma}{1+\sigma}I.

Example A.2
.

Assume that
A
is a self-adjoint matrix with eignvalues
0<\lambda_{1}<..<\lambda_{n}
and that
\lambda_{1}\leq 1\leq\lambda_{n}
. By the spectral theorem
A=R^{\ast}DR
where
R
is a rotation and
D
is a diagonal matrix in the eigenvalues of
A
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(A)

\displaystyle=(I-A)(I+A)^{-1}=(I-R^{\ast}DR)(I+R^{\ast}DR)^{-1}=R^{\ast}(I-D)R(R^{\ast}(I+D)R))^{-1}

\displaystyle=R^{\ast}(I-D)RR^{\ast}(I+D)^{-1}R=R^{\ast}(I-D)(I+D)R

\displaystyle=R^{\ast}\mathscr{C}(D)R.

Hence

\displaystyle\det(\mathscr{C}(A))=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{C}(A)\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathscr{C}(D)\|=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\bigg|\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}\bigg|=\max\bigg\{\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{1}},\frac{\lambda_{n}-1}{\lambda_{n}+1}\bigg\}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{C}(A)\|\leq\max\{\lambda_{1}^{-1},\lambda_{n}\}\leq\lambda_{1}^{-1}+\lambda_{n},

and in the special case when
\lambda_{1}^{-1}=\lambda_{n}
, then

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{C}(A)\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathscr{C}(D)\|=\max\bigg\{\frac{\lambda_{1}^{-1}-1}{1+\lambda_{1}^{-1}},\frac{\lambda_{n}-1}{\lambda_{n}+1}\bigg\}=\frac{\lambda_{n}-1}{\lambda_{n}+1}.

The example above can be generalized to normal matrices to yield the following lemma:

Lemma A.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{L}(V)
be a normal matrix such that
-1\neq\sigma(A)
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{C}(A)\|\leq\max\bigg\{\frac{1-\sigma_{1}}{1+\sigma_{1}},\frac{\sigma_{n}-1}{\sigma_{n}+1}\bigg\},

(A.4)

where
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{n}
are the smallest and largest singular value of
A
respectively.

Proof.

We use the fact that normal matrices are diagonizable by unitary matrices and that we have the relation
\sigma_{j}=|\lambda_{j}|
between the singular values and eigenvalues of
A
. Furthermore, for a normal matrix
A
we have
\|A\|=\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}.
The statement then follows from an identical argument as in the example above.
∎

Proposition A.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(V)
and assume that
\|T\|<1
. Then
\|\Lambda T\|<1.

Proof.

By polar factorization
T=R\circ S=S\circ R
. Hence
\|T\|=\|S\|
. Let
\sigma_{max}
be the largest singular value of
T
, i.e. the largest eigenvalue of
S
. Then
\|T\|=\sigma_{max}
.
Since
\Lambda T=\Lambda(S\circ R)=\Lambda S\circ\Lambda R
,
\|T\|=\|\Lambda S\|
.
By the spectral theorem
S
has an ON-eigenbasis
\{\xi_{j}\}_{j}
which induces and ON-eigenmultivector basis for
\Lambda S
. Let
\sigma_{1}\leq...\leq\sigma_{n}
be the eignvalues for

ordered according to size. Since the largest eigenvalue for

and

.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

be self-adjoint and positive. Then
\Lambda T
is self-adjoint and positive.

The self-adjointness of

. By linearity it is enough to prove that

∎

Proposition A.3
.

Let

and assume that
T
is invertible, normal (i.e.,

) and that

. Then

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(T^{-1})=-\mathscr{C}(T).

First observe that if

and

, then

\displaystyle\frac{1-x^{-1}}{1+x^{-1}}=-\frac{1-x}{1+x}.

∎

Lemma A.4

) holds. We have the identity

\displaystyle I-A=\mathscr{C}(A)\circ(I+A).

Appendix B
Iteration of Sobolev Conjugate Exponent

Define the function

\displaystyle f_{n}(x):=\frac{nx}{n-x}

for
x\in[0,n)
. Set
f_{n}^{k}(x):=\underbrace{f_{n}\circ f_{n}\circ...\circ f_{n}(x)}_{k\text{ times}}
. Proof by induction shows that

\displaystyle f_{n}^{k}(x):=\frac{n^{k}x}{n^{k}-kn^{k-1}x}=\frac{nx}{n-kx}.

This gives

\displaystyle f_{n}^{k}(2)=\frac{2n}{n-2k}.

as long as
f_{n}^{k}(2)\leq n
. The iteration stops when
f_{n}^{k}(2)\geq n
.
If
n=2m
and
k=m-1
gives

\displaystyle f_{2m}^{m-1}(2)=\frac{4m}{2m-2(m-1)}=2m=n.

So in even dimension the iteration stops at
k=m-1
giving
f_{n}^{k}(2)=n
. In odd dimension
n=2m+1
we get with
k=m

\displaystyle f_{2m+1}^{m}(2)=\frac{2(2m+1)}{2m+1-2m)}=2(2m+1)=2n.
