Estimation of binary time-frequency masks from ambient noise Thanks: J. L. R. and M. S. gratefully acknowledge support from the Austrian Science Fund (FWF): 10.55776/Y1199.
José Luis Romero, Michael Speckbacher

Abstract
. We investigate the retrieval of a binary time-frequency mask from a few observations of filtered white ambient noise. Confirming household wisdom in acoustic modeling, we show that this is possible by inspecting the average spectrogram of ambient noise. Specifically, we show that the lower quantile of the average of 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( | Ω | / ε ) ) \mathcal{O}(\log(\left|\Omega\right|/\varepsilon)) masked spectrograms is enough to identify a rather general mask Ω \Omega with confidence at least ε \varepsilon , up to shape details concentrated near the boundary of Ω \Omega . As an application, the expected measure of the estimation error is dominated by the perimeter of the time-frequency mask. The estimator requires no knowledge of the noise variance, and only a very qualitative profile of the filtering window, but no exact knowledge of it.

1.
Introduction

Time-frequency masking is the process of altering a signal
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
by weighting its time-frequency profile with a
mask
.
A standard model for such operation is based on the
short-time Fourier transform
of
f
. First a profile of
f
is built out of its time-frequency correlations with an auxiliary smooth and fast-decaying
window function

g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle V_{g}f(x,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t)\overline{g(t-x)}e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Second, a mask
m:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is applied to the time-frequency profile of
f
by solving the optimization problem

(1.2)

\displaystyle H_{m}f=\mathrm{argmin}_{\tilde{f}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big|m(x,\xi)\cdot V_{g}f(x,\xi)-V_{g}\tilde{f}(x,\xi)\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

whose solution is, more explicitly,

(1.3)

\displaystyle H_{m}f(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(x,\xi)V_{g}f(x,\xi){g(t-x)}e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot t}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

provided that
g
is normalized by

\|g\|_{2}=1
— see Section
4.2
. While the filter (
1.3
) depends on the auxiliary window function
g
, different choices of smooth and fast-decaying
g
yield qualitatively similar results.

The subclass of
binary masks
,
m(x,\xi)\in\{0,1\}
, plays a distinguished role in acoustic modeling — see, e.g.,
[
37
,
38
]
. In this case
m
is the indicator function of a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and (
1.3
) models a
hard filter
that rejects certain time-frequency components, for example, following a perceptual criterion. The
direct estimation problem
consists in producing a binary mask optimally tailored to a certain task such as the separation of speech from noise in a particular auditory setup. In contrast, the
inverse estimation problem
consists in learning the mask of a filter from (known or unknown) test inputs, for example, so as to retrieve the current calibration of a device that simulates or enhances human hearing
[
24
]
.

While the problem of retrieving a mask
m
from observations of filtered signals
H_{m}f
is challenging — as it is one example of operator identification, see
[
26
,
28
,
33
,
32
,
34
,
21
]
and the references therein — learning a binary mask is expected to be substantially easier. In fact, it is household wisdom of acoustic modeling that a binary mask is revealed by simply inspecting the filtered output of
ambient noise
. The purpose of this article is to provide a formal analysis of this fact in the idealized setting of
white
ambient noise — that is, noise whose stochastics are independent at disjoint spatial locations. Crucially,
we do not assume the variance of the noise to be known
, and, similarly, we do not assume exact knowledge of the window function
g
.

2.
Results

2.1.
Mask estimation with unknown noise level

Setup

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be a Schwartz function normalized by
\left\|g\right\|_{2}=1
, let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be a compact domain, and denote
H_{\Omega}
the filter (
1.3
) with binary mask

\displaystyle m(z)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }z\in\Omega\\
0&\mbox{if }z\notin\Omega\end{cases}.

Ambient noise model

We consider zero-mean Gaussian
complex white noise

\mathcal{N}
with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
, that is, a generalized complex-valued Gaussian stochastic process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{\mathcal{N}(z)\cdot\overline{\mathcal{N}(w)}\big\}=\sigma^{2}\cdot\delta(z-w),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{\mathcal{N}(z)\cdot\mathcal{N}(w)\big\}=0.

Measure-theoretic aspects are presented in more detail in Section
3.4
. The key assumption is
whiteness
: If
E_{1},E_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are disjoint Borel sets, then
\mathcal{N}_{|E_{1}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{|E_{2}}
are statistically independent. Equivalently, whiteness means that the noise has equal intensity at different frequencies, and thus a constant power spectral density. White noise is a mathematical idealization of empirically observable ambient noise, which has flat power spectrum over a certain range of relevant frequencies, determined by the propagation medium or generating mechanism.

The assumption that
\mathcal{N}
is complex-valued is made for mathematical convenience, as it is compatible with the use of complex exponentials in the Fourier transform. (Indeed, the modulation operation

\mathcal{N}\to e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot\ }\mathcal{N}
,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, preserves the stochastics of
\mathcal{N}
). The minor modifications necessary to treat real-valued white noise are sketched in Section
2.2
.

Data

Suppose that we observe
H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N}_{1}),\ldots,H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N}_{K})
, where
\mathcal{N}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{K}
are
K
independent realizations of complex white noise with
unknown variance

\sigma^{2}>0
.
Even though a typical realization of white noise
\mathcal{N}
is not a function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but merely a tempered distribution, the fact that
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
allows us to interpret
H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N})
distributionally — see Section
3.4
for details.

As a modeling assumption the use of (pure) white noise as input means that the effective bandwidth of the ambient noise is larger than the frequency diameter of the filtering mask
\Omega
, as the filter (
1.3
) is largely insensitive to input frequencies beyond the frequency support of
\Omega
. In other words, effective noise bandwidth limits beyond the frequency support of the mask do not need to be modeled, as they are automatically enforced by the filter (
1.3
) — see Section
2.3
. In practical terms, the input model thus requires that the ambient noise be perceived as white within a certain auditory setup, modeled by the filter (
1.3
).

Goal

We wish to estimate
\Omega
based on the available data.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to rigorously study the retrieval of
binary
time-frequency masks. The focus on such masks is motivated by certain applications, notably acoustics
[
37
,
38
]
, and leads to important differences with respect to the identification problem for general operators. For example, a standard assumption in the theory of
operator sampling

[
26
,
28
,
33
,
34
]
is that the unknown operator has
bandlimited Kohn-Nirenberg symbol

[
15
,
32
,
21
]
, which is not satisfied by the filters studied in this article.

Estimation limits

To understand what kind of performance guarantees can be expected, let us look more closely into the masking procedure (
1.2
). The time-frequency filter with binary mask
m=\chi_{\Omega}
finds the short-time Fourier transform
V_{g}H_{\Omega}f
that best matches the function
\chi_{\Omega}V_{g}f
in the quadratic mean sense. On the one hand, due to the nature of the windowing procedure defining the short-time Fourier transform, the function
V_{g}H_{\Omega}f
is smooth, independently of the regularity of the input signal
f

[
20
, Chapter 3]
(which in our case is white noise). On the other hand,
\chi_{\Omega}V_{g}f
has jump discontinuities along
\partial\Omega
. Hence, the operation
f\mapsto H_{\Omega}f
has a smoothing effect along the time-frequency components located near the boundary of
\Omega
(see Figure
1
) and thus suppresses the fine details of the boundary of
\Omega
.

(left) and
|V_{\varphi}H_{\Omega}f|
(right), with
\varphi(t)=2^{1/4}e^{-\pi t^{2}}
,
f
a linear combination of time-frequency shifts of
\varphi
, and
\Omega
a disk (top) compared to a slightly
perturbed disk (bottom).

Proposed estimator

We first fix a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which we call
reconstruction window
, normalized by
\lVert\varphi\rVert_{2}=1
. A typical choice is a Gaussian
\varphi(t)=2^{d/4}e^{-\pi t^{2}}
. We then let

(2.1)

\displaystyle\rho(z):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big|V_{\varphi}\big(H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}\big)(z)\big|^{2},\qquad z=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

be the
average observed spectrogram
. As an estimate of
\Omega
, we propose the following lower quantile of
\rho
:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}:=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\rho(z)\geq\frac{1}{4}\max_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\Big\}.

Assumptions

We assume that
\Omega
is reasonably large in the sense that its measure dominates its perimeter:

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\Omega|\geq\max\Big\{2,8\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{g}g(z)|^{2}|z|\,\mathrm{d}z\cdot|\partial\Omega|\Big\}.

Here,
\left|\partial\Omega\right|
denotes the
perimeter
of
\Omega
, that is, the
(2d-1)
-dimensional measure of its boundary (see Section
3.2
for a detailed discussion). In particular, as
\Omega
is compact, (
2.3
) requires the perimeter
|\partial\Omega|
to be finite.

To motivate the largeness condition (
2.3
), recall the above discussion of estimation limits. Our assumption means that, in the proposed recovery problem, the measure of the estimation objective
\Omega
is large with respect to the expected source of error
\partial\Omega
.

In the literature, it is most common to analyze time-frequency filters (
1.3
) by considering binary masks produced by dilation of a certain domain,
\Omega=r\cdot\Omega_{0}
, and their large-scale asymptotics,
r\to\infty

[
35
]
. In this regime, the largeness condition (
2.3
) holds for all sufficiently large
r
, as long as
\Omega_{0}
is compact and has finite perimeter,
because
|\Omega|=r^{2d}|\Omega_{0}|
, while
|\partial\Omega|=r^{2d-1}|\partial\Omega_{0}|
. Our analysis is finer and less asymptotic, as it concerns a given domain and a concrete largeness condition, that is satisfied at a concrete scale, determined by the relation between the measure and perimeter of the masking domain
\Omega
and the time-frequency concentration of the model window
g
.

Performance guarantees

The
estimation error
is the symmetric difference set

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Omega\triangle\widehat{\Omega}=\big(\Omega\setminus\widehat{\Omega}\big)\cup\big(\widehat{\Omega}\setminus\Omega\big).

Our main result shows that with overwhelming probability the estimation error is concentrated around the
boundary
of
\Omega
; see Figure
2
for an illustration. More precisely,
denoting the
r
-neighborhood of a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
by

\displaystyle E^{r}:=\big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:d(z,E)<r\big\},

the main result reads as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(Recovery up to boundary details)
.

Let
g,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\left\|g\right\|_{2}=\left\|\varphi\right\|_{2}=1
be model and reconstruction windows.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be compact and satisfying the largeness condition (
2.3
). Let
K\geq 4
, assume that
K
independent realizations of
H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N})
are observed, and consider the estimator

\widehat{\Omega}
defined in (
2.2
).

Then there exist positive constants
c,C,r>0
, that depend on
g
and
\varphi
but not on
\Omega
, such that with probability at least
1-c\cdot|\Omega^{r}|\cdot e^{-CK}
, the estimation error satisfies

(2.5)

\Omega\triangle\widehat{\Omega}\subseteq\big[\partial\Omega\big]^{r}.

Let us remark on some aspects of Theorem
2.1
:

\bullet
The estimation method
does not require knowledge of the noise level

\sigma>0
. Similarly, the performance guarantees are independent of
\sigma
.

\bullet
As discussed above, recovery up to a neighborhood of the boundary of
\Omega
is intuitively the best possible result, as the filter
H_{\Omega}
tends to suppress boundary details. Theorem
2.1
shows that given a confidence level
\varepsilon>0
this is achieved with only
\mathcal{O}\big(\log(\left|\Omega^{r}\right|/\varepsilon)\big)
measurements of ambient noise.

\bullet
While the estimation method does not require precise knowledge of the model window
g
,
its time-frequency concentration does impact the performance guarantees through the largeness condition (
2.3
).
Similarly, the correlation between the model and reconstruction windows affects the overall estimation performance. In fact, as we show in Remark
5.11
, the dependence of the constants
c,C,r
on
g
and
\varphi
can be made explicit, and the main contribution is from the following mutual correlation measure:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}g(z)|^{2}|z|\,\mathrm{d}z.

Thus, in practice, the choice of the reconstruction window
\varphi
amounts to a rough guess of the time-frequency profile of the model window
g
, with
\varphi=g
being the ideal choice. Without knowledge of
g
,
a natural choice for the reconstruction window is the standard Gaussian
\varphi(t)=2^{1/4}e^{-\pi t^{2}}
, or its time-frequency shifted version
\varphi(t)=2^{1/4}e^{-\pi(t-x)^{2}}e^{-2\pi i\xi t}
if an educated guess for the time-frequency center
(x,\xi)
of
g
is available. See Figure
2
for an illustration with
\varphi=g
or
\varphi\neq g
.

As an application of Theorem
2.1
we see that the measure of the estimation error is dominated by the perimeter of the masking domain
\left|\partial\Omega\right|
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, with the stated probability, the estimation error satisfies

\big|{\Omega\triangle\widehat{\Omega}}\big|\leq C_{1}\cdot|\partial\Omega|,

where the constant
C_{1}
depends on
\varphi
and
g
but not on
\Omega
.

Finally, we also derive a bound for the expected reconstruction error.

Theorem 2.3
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2},r>0
that depend on
g
and
\varphi
but not on
\Omega
such that
if

(2.6)

\displaystyle K\geq C_{1}\log\big(\left|\Omega^{r}\right|\big),

then

\mathbb{E}\left\{\big|{\Omega\triangle\widehat{\Omega}}\big|\right\}\leq C_{2}\cdot|\partial\Omega|.

The constant
r
in Theorem
2.3
plays a moderate role for regular domains
\Omega
. For example, in the asymptotic dilation regime, where
\Omega
is obtained by increasing dilation of a given smooth domain,
|\Omega^{r}|
can often be replaced by
|\Omega|
.

Our proofs below provide more precise versions of the main results, with additional qualitative information on the various constants. In particular, Theorem
5.12
below is a more detailed version of Theorem
2.3
, while Remark
5.13
provides additional information on the constants
C_{1},C_{2},r
.

2.2.
Real-valued noise

We now briefly discuss how to adapt the main result to
real
white noise. Consider the estimation problem in Section
2.1
, but this time under the assumption that the ambient noise is real-valued and white. Specifically, suppose that we observe
H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N}_{1}),\ldots,H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N}_{K})
, where

\mathcal{N}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{K}
are independent copies of real-valued white noise on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
.

Setting
K^{\prime}:=\lfloor K/2\rfloor
, the new stochastic processes

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{k}:=\mathcal{N}_{k}+i\cdot\mathcal{N}_{k+K^{\prime}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K^{\prime},

are independent copies of complex white noise with variance
2\sigma^{2}
. As the averaged spectrogram (
2.1
) associated with the observations
H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{1}),\ldots,H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{K^{\prime}})
can be computed from the available data,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\rho(z):=\frac{1}{K^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{K^{\prime}}\big|V_{\varphi}\big(H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{k}\big)(z)\big|^{2}=\frac{1}{K^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{K^{\prime}}\big|V_{\varphi}\big(H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}\big)(z)+i\cdot V_{\varphi}\big(H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k+K^{\prime}}\big)(z)\big|^{2},

and
K^{\prime}\leq K\leq 3K^{\prime}
as soon as
K\geq 4
, the performance guarantees from Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.3
still hold, with possibly larger constants.

Finally, note that if
\mathcal{N}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{K}

are independent copies of complex-valued white noise, then so are

\mathcal{N^{\prime}}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{K^{\prime}}
as given by (
2.7
). Hence, the
complexified estimator
(
2.8
) satisfies the performance guarantees of Theorem
2.1
regardless of whether the ambient noise is real or complex-valued.

2.3.
Related literature and technical overview

The spectrogram of a function is the squared absolute value of its short-time Fourier transform (
1.1
) and provides a useful proxy for the elusive notion of a
time-varying spectrum

[
8
]
. As with other quadratic time-frequency representations in the so-called
Cohen class
,
its use represents a compromise between many desirable but only approximately achievable properties
[
10
]
. A specific merit of the spectrogram is being non-negative and efficiently computable, which makes it a popular tool in signal processing
[
31
]
. On the other hand, the choice of the analyzing window
g
introduces a trade-off between spatial and temporal resolution. In the acoustic filter model (
1.3
),
g
is a
partially unknown degree of freedom
. An important consequence of our work is that the estimation of a binary mask succeeds based only on a rough educated guess for
g
(namely, the reconstruction window
\varphi
).

The estimator that we propose and analyze is a level set of the average observed spectrogram (
2.1
) taken with an
observation dependent threshold
(lower quantile). Level sets of spectrograms are standard tools in signal processing. For example, a common method to extract a signal embedded into noise consists in computing a spectrogram level set with a carefully chosen threshold related to the expected signal to noise ratio. See
[
19
]
for performance guarantees for signal estimation under white noise contamination based on spectrogram level sets with Gaussian windows, and
[
4
]
for a systematic study of analytic time-frequency representations of white noise.

At the technical level, we make the central assumption that the ambient noise is
Gaussian
, which translates into suitable concentration properties for various statistical quantities. Indeed, we make essential use of concentration of measure for quadratic forms
[
3
,
36
]
. In order to apply these in an analog setting, we consider adequate low-rank approximations of the filter (
1.3
).

It is a general principle that the low-rank core of a matrix can be revealed by sketching it with independent random vectors. One usually takes a number of random input vectors slightly exceeding the matrix numerical rank and then computes a reduced singular value decomposition
[
23
]
. Remarkably, as we show in Theorem
2.1
, a time-frequency filter with a
binary
mask can be learned from far fewer noise measurements, and without costly linear algebra computations. (Indeed, the numerical rank of
H_{\Omega}
is
\approx\left|\Omega\right|

[
27
, Chapter 1]
while the number of required measurements is
\approx\log(\left|\Omega\right|)
.) The proof of Theorem
2.1
exploits a priori knowledge of the
asymptotic profile of the spectrum and eigenvectors
of the filter
H_{\Omega}
, which reduces the size of the learning task. At the technical level, we build and significantly elaborate on the spectral analysis of
[
1
,
2
,
5
]
.

Finally, we point out some limitations and possible extensions of our work. First, while noise can be multifaceted
[
9
]
, ours is white and Gaussian. Second, we work with signals directly in the continuous domain, while in practice measurements are finite, and effective duration and bandwidth need to be taken into account
[
27
,
31
]
. These are modeling simplifications and we expect that our work can be suitably generalized. For example, our methods can be suitably extended to ambient noise with finite bandwidth
W
, modeled as
\mathcal{N}_{W}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(1_{[-W/2,W/2]^{d}}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N})
, where
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform and
\mathcal{N}
is white noise, provided that the frequency mask
\Omega
satisfies
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times 1_{[-W/2,W/2]^{d}}
— as, in this case,
H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}
is approximately equal to
H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{W}
. In this respect, we mention the different but related problem of
designing
suitably smooth and fast decaying input signals for operator identification
[
29
]
.

establishes the notation and preliminary results. Basic background on time-frequency analysis, noise and spectral theory is provided in Section

. The main results are then proved in Section

.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Notation

We denote balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
B_{R}(z)
and define the distance between a point
z
and a set
\Omega
by
\text{dist}(z,\Omega)=\inf\{|z-w|:\ w\in\Omega\}
. Moreover, we write
A\lesssim B
if there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that
A\leq CB
. We use
\|\cdot\|_{s}
and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
for the spectral and Frobenius norms of a matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
respectively. The convolution of two functions
f,g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
f\ast g(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(w)g(z-w)\,\mathrm{d}w
.

3.2.
Sets with finite perimeter

For a compact set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
we let
\left|\partial\Omega\right|
denote the
(2d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the topological boundary of
\Omega
. Whenever this measure is finite, the set
\Omega
has
finite perimeter
in the sense that its characteristic function
\chi_{\Omega}
has bounded variation. Moreover,
in this case,
\mathrm{Var}(\chi_{\Omega})\leq\left|\partial\Omega\right|
, as
\mathrm{Var}(\chi_{\Omega})
is the
(2d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the measure-theoretic boundary of
\Omega
, which is a subset of the topological boundary of
\Omega
. In our main results, the assumption that
\Omega
is compact, together with (
2.3
),
implies that
\left|\partial\Omega\right|<\infty
. See
[
16
, Chapter 5]
for comprehensive background on sets of finite perimeter.

We will use the following estimate on regularization with an integrable convolution kernel
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\big\|\chi_{\Omega}\ast\psi-\big(\textstyle{\int}\psi\big)\chi_{\Omega}\big\|_{1}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\psi(z)||z|\,\mathrm{d}z\cdot|\partial\Omega|,

for a proof, see, e.g.,
[
1
, Lemma 3.2]
.

3.3.
Complex Gaussian vectors

A complex Gaussian vector is a random vector
X
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
(\text{Re}(X),\text{Im}(X))
is normally distributed, has zero mean, and vanishing pseudo-covariance
\mathbb{E}\big\{XX^{t}\big\}=0
. The stochastics of such
X
are thus encoded in its covariance matrix

\displaystyle\Sigma=\mathbb{E}\big\{XX^{*}\big\},

and we write
X\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\Sigma)
.

3.4.
Complex white noise

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the spaces of real and complex valued Schwartz functions and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
their respective duals (the spaces of tempered distributions).
By the Bochner-Minlos theorem, there exists a unique probability measure on
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}
, the family of Borel subsets of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that

\int_{S_{\mathbb{R}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}e^{i\langle f,\varphi\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{R}}(\varphi)=e^{-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\|f\|^{2}},

for all
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
With the identification
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})+i\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\simeq\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we introduce the product measure
\mu_{\mathbb{C}}=\mu_{\mathbb{R}}\times\mu_{\mathbb{R}}
, defined on
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the product
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The measure space
\big(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{C}},\mu_{\mathbb{C}}\big)
is called
complex white noise
. Intuitively, we think of this space as the law of a random distribution
\mathcal{N}
.
If
f,g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{N}
is an instance of complex white noise, then
\langle f,\mathcal{N}\rangle\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\sigma^{2}\|f\|^{2}\big)

and

\mathbb{E}\left\{\langle f,\mathcal{N}\rangle\overline{\langle g,\mathcal{N}\rangle}\right\}=\sigma^{2}\langle f,g\rangle.

For more on complex white noise, see for example
[
25
, Section 6]
.

3.5.
Concentration of measure for quadratic forms

We will need the following complex version of of the Hanson-Wright inequality
[
3
,
36
]
, which we prove for completeness.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be an
n
-dimensional complex Gaussian random variable with
X\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\Sigma)
and
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
Hermitian. Then for every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(|\langle AX,X\rangle-\mathbb{E}\{\langle AX,X\rangle\}|\geq t\big)\leq 2\ \emph{exp}\left(-C_{\mathrm{hw}}\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\|\Sigma\|^{2}_{s}\|A\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\Sigma\|_{s}\|A\|_{s}}\right)\right)

for some universal constant
C_{\mathrm{hw}}>0
.

Proof.

By absorbing the matrix
\Sigma
into
A
we can assume without loss of generality that
\Sigma
is the identity matrix.
Define
Y:=(\text{Re}(X),\text{Im}(X))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as well as

\mathbb{A}:=\begin{pmatrix}\text{Re}(A)&-\text{Im}(A)\\
\text{Im}(A)&\text{Re}(A)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}.

As
A
is Hermitian, one has that
\mathbb{A}
is symmetric and
\langle AX,X\rangle=\langle\mathbb{A}Y,Y\rangle
which implies that
\|A\|_{s}=\|\mathbb{A}\|_{s}
. On the other hand, it is clear from its definition that
\|\mathbb{A}\|_{F}^{2}=2\|A\|_{F}^{2}
. Moreover, if
X\sim C\mathcal{N}(0,I)
then
Y\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,\tfrac{1}{2}I\big)
. The result then follows after an application of
[
3
, Theorem 2.5]
.
∎

4.
Time-frequency filters and the range of the short-time Fourier transform

4.1.
Basic properties of the STFT

Let
z=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
time-frequency shifts
of a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are

\pi(z)f(t)=e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot t}f(t-x),\qquad z=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) of a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to a window
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by (
1.1
) and satisfies the following
orthogonality relation

(4.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V_{g_{1}}f_{1}(z)\overline{V_{g_{2}}f_{2}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle\langle g_{2},g_{1}\rangle,\quad f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The range of the STFT,
V_{g}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, is a reproducing kernel subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
with
reproducing kernel

K_{g}(z,w)=\|g\|^{-2}_{2}\langle\pi(w)g,\pi(z)g\rangle
.
Concretely, this means that
V_{g}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is closed in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
and the following reproducing formula holds:

(4.2)

V_{g}f(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V_{g}f(w)K_{g}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w,\qquad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

As soon as
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the definition of the short-time Fourier transform (
1.1
) extends to
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the usual way and yields a continuous function
V_{g}f:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{C}
; see
[
20
, Chapter 11]
, or
[
6
]
.

4.2.
Spectral properties of time-frequency filters

Let
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\left\|g\right\|_{2}=1
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be compact. The
time-frequency localization operator

H_{\Omega}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by the formula:

(4.3)

\displaystyle H_{\Omega}f=\int_{\Omega}V_{g}f(z)\pi(z)g\,\mathrm{d}z,

where
the integral is to be interpreted in the weak sense

\displaystyle\langle H_{\Omega}f,h\rangle=\int_{\Omega}V_{g}f(z)\langle\pi(z)g,h\rangle\,\mathrm{d}z,\qquad h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Originally introduced in
[
13
]
,
H_{\Omega}
is a basic tool in signal processing that models a non-stationary filter. As an operator,
H_{\Omega}
is compact and self-adjoint. In terms of the isometry
V_{g}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, the time-frequency localization operator can be factored as
H_{\Omega}f=V_{g}^{*}(\chi_{\Omega}\cdot V_{g}f)
, which shows that
V_{g}H_{\Omega}f
is the orthogonal projection of
\chi_{\Omega}V_{g}f
onto
V_{g}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. (In fact, the same observation applies to any mask
m
in lieu of
\chi_{\Omega}
and shows the equivalence of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).)

Let
1\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq....>0
be its non-zero eigenvalues counted with multiplicities and
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the corresponding orthonormal set of eigenfunctions. This set may be incomplete if
H_{\Omega}
has a non-trivial null-space — it could also be finite if
H_{\Omega}
has finite rank, but we still write
n\in\mathbb{N}
with a slight abuse of notation. In any case,
H_{\Omega}
has infinite rank as soon as
\Omega
has non-empty interior
[
14
, Lemma 5.8]
. The set of eigenfunctions are not only orthogonal on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but they also satisfy the following
double orthogonality
property:

(4.4)

\int_{\Omega}V_{g}f_{n}(z)\overline{V_{g}f_{k}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z=\lambda_{n}\delta_{n,k}.

It is easy to see that
H_{\Omega}
is trace-class and

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}=\left|\Omega\right|.

The investigation of the fine asymptotics of the spectrum of
H_{\Omega}
is an active field of research.
Roughly stated,
H_{\Omega}
has
\approx\left|\Omega\right|
eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
that are close to 1, while
\lambda_{n}
decays fast as soon as
n
is moderately larger than
\left|\Omega\right|

[
13
]
. We will only need the following simple estimate, whose proof follows from a minor variation of
[
30
, Lemma 4.3]
and is omitted.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
\left\|g\right\|_{2}=1
, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be a compact set satisfying (
2.3
).
Then
\lambda_{n}\geq\frac{3}{4}
for every
1\leq n\leq|\Omega|/2
.

The definition of
H_{\Omega}
readily extends to distributional arguments: if
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
V_{g}f
is a continuous function, and (
4.3
) is well-defined.
It follows from basic pseudo-differential theory that

(4.6)

\displaystyle H_{\Omega}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and

(4.7)

\displaystyle\langle H_{\Omega}f,h\rangle=\langle f,H_{\Omega}h\rangle,\qquad f,h\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(Indeed, the
Weyl symbol
of
H_{\Omega}
is the Schwartz function
\chi_{\Omega}*W_{g}
, where
W_{g}
is the Wigner distribution of
f
— see
[
18
, Chapter 2 and Theorem 2.21]
.)
In particular,
f_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for all
n\geq 1
; see
[
5
,
11
,
12
]
for more refined regularity properties.

4.3.
Filtering white noise

Almost every realization of white noise
\mathcal{N}
defines a tempered distribution, and, by (
4.6
),
H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}
is a well-defined random Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
— see
[
4
, Section 3]
,
[
22
, Section 6.1]
and

[
19
, Section 3]
for more on the random function
V_{g}\mathcal{N}
.

We now note that the eigenexpansion of
H_{\Omega}
can be applied to white noise.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\left\|g\right\|_{2}=1
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
compact, and
\mathcal{N}
complex white noise with standard deviation
\sigma
. Then there exists a sequence
\alpha_{n}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
of independent complex normal variables such that, almost surely,

(4.8)

\displaystyle H_{\Omega}(\mathcal{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{n}f_{n},

with (almost sure) absolute convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.

Proof.

Let

\alpha_{n}:=\langle\mathcal{N},f_{n}\rangle
. The orthogonality of
\{f_{n}:n\geq 1\}
implies the independence of
\{\alpha_{n}:n\geq 1\}
.
By (
4.5
),
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\mathbb{E}\{|\alpha_{n}|\}<\infty
. Hence, almost surely,
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}|\alpha_{n}|<\infty
. Consider one such realization. Then
\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{n}f_{n}
is absolutely convergent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In addition, for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\Big\langle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{n}f_{n},f_{k}\Big\rangle=\lambda_{k}\alpha_{k},

by absolute convergence, while

\displaystyle\langle H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N},f_{k}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{N},H_{\Omega}f_{k}\rangle=\lambda_{k}\alpha_{k}.

On the other hand,
\langle\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{n}f_{n},h\rangle=0
for
h\in{\sf Ker}\,(H_{\Omega})=\text{span}\{f_{n}:n\geq 1\}^{\perp}
, while (
4.7
) gives

\displaystyle\langle H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N},h\rangle=\langle\mathcal{N},H_{\Omega}h\rangle=0.

This proves (
4.8
).
∎

5.
Proof of the main results

Throughout this section we let
g,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be normalized by
\left\|g\right\|_{2}=\left\|\varphi\right\|_{2}=1
. We also let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be compact with
\left|\partial{\Omega}\right|<\infty
and satisfying (
2.3
). The corresponding time-frequency localization operator is diagonalized as in Section
4.2
; we adopt the corresponding notation. We also let

\mathcal{N}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{K}
be
K
independent realizations of complex white noise with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
.

5.1.
Proof strategy

Let us outline the overall strategy to prove the main results (Theorems
2.1
–
2.3
).

• We initially assume that
\sigma=1
and study the level sets

(5.1)

\displaystyle S_{\delta}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\ \rho(z)\geq\delta\}

associated with a
deterministic threshold

\delta\in(0,1)
. The core of the proof consists in constructing an exceptional set
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\delta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
that satisfies the following with high probability:

(i)

\chi_{\Omega}(z)=\chi_{S_{\delta}}(z)
, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\backslash\Gamma
, (Theorems
5.7
and
5.9
);

(ii)

\Gamma\subset[\partial\Omega]^{r}
, for an adequate constant
r>0
independent of
\Omega
, (Propositions
5.3
and
5.8
).

Part (i) is in turn proved in two steps. We first estimate the failure probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\not=\chi_{S_{\delta}}(z))
related to each individual
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\backslash\Gamma
(Lemma
5.1
and Theorem
5.2
). Second we exploit the moduli of continuity of the short-time Fourier transform to strengthen the previously obtained estimates and derive uniform conclusions. At the technical level, this is achieved by studying an adequate local maximum operator applied to the reproducing kernel of the range of the short-time Fourier transform (Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
), which allows us to implement a net argument. See also the discussion in Section
5.3
.

• To connect the previous analysis to the quantile set (
2.2
), we show that
\|\rho\|_{\infty}\asymp 1
with high probability (Proposition
5.10
, Sections
5.4
–
5.6
).

• Finally, since the definition of
\widehat{\Omega}
is homogeneous w.r.t.
\sigma
, all results extend to general noise levels.

To formulate our arguments, we introduce the auxiliary function
\vartheta:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
:

(5.2)

\qquad\vartheta(z):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

As
\{V_{g}f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal sequence in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
V_{\varphi}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
,

(5.3)

\vartheta(z)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\leq K_{\varphi}(z,z)=1,

while (
4.5
) shows that
\vartheta
is not identically zero.

5.2.
Pointwise error estimate

We start by investigating the stochastic concentration of the functions
\rho
and
\vartheta
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\sigma=1
. There exists a universal constant
C_{1}\in(0,1]
such that for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(|\rho(z)-\vartheta(z)|\geq t\big)

\displaystyle\leq 3\ \emph{exp}\left(-C_{1}K\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\vartheta(z)^{2}},\frac{t}{\vartheta(z)}\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
and define the complex Gaussian random vector

X_{n}:=\alpha_{\text{mod}(n-1,L)+1}^{\lceil n/L\rceil},
for
n\in\{1,...,LK\}
. Clearly,
X\sim\mathcal{CN}\big(0,I_{KL}\big)
. Let us also define the matrix-valued function
M:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
,

{M}(z)_{\ell,n}:=\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)},

and the block-diagonal Hermitian matrix
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{KL\times KL}
,

\mathcal{M}(z):=\frac{1}{K}\begin{pmatrix}{M}(z)&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&{M}(z)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{KL\times KL}.

Let
P_{L}
denote the projection onto the space spanned by the first
L
eigenfunctions of
H_{\Omega}
.
It can then be verified that

\displaystyle\rho_{L}(z):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|V_{\varphi}(P_{L}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k})(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\alpha_{\ell}^{k}\overline{\alpha_{n}^{k}}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{M}(z)X,X\rangle.

In order to apply Theorem
3.1
, we need to calculate the expectation of
\langle\mathcal{M}(z)X,X\rangle
as well as the Frobenius and spectral norm of
\mathcal{M}(z)
.

The independence of the random variables
\alpha_{n}^{k}
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\rho_{L}(z)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|V_{\varphi}(P_{L}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k})(z)|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}\mathbb{E}\left\{\alpha_{\ell}^{k}\overline{\alpha_{n}^{k}}\right\}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{L}|\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}=:\vartheta_{L}(z).

The Frobenius norm of
\mathcal{M}(z)
is

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}(z)\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\|{M}(z)\|^{2}_{F}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}|\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z){V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{L}|\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{K}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{K}\vartheta(z)^{2}.

The spectral norm of
\mathcal{M}(z)
is

\|\mathcal{M}(z)\|_{s}=\frac{1}{K}\|{M}(z)\|_{s}.

Thus, by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\|{M}(z)\|_{s}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|X\|_{2}=1}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{L}\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(M(z))_{n,\ell}X_{\ell}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{n=1}^{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}|\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\vartheta_{L}(z)\leq\vartheta(z).

Therefore, by Theorem
3.1
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\left|\rho_{L}(z)-\vartheta_{L}(z)\right|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\ \text{exp}\left(-C_{hw}K\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\vartheta(z)^{2}},\frac{t}{\vartheta(z)}\right)\right).

Let us use the following abbreviations

\displaystyle R_{1}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=L+1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{\ell}^{k}\overline{\alpha_{n}^{k}}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)},

\displaystyle R_{2}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{\ell}^{k}\overline{\alpha_{n}^{k}}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)},

\displaystyle R_{3}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=L+1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}\alpha_{\ell}^{k}\overline{\alpha_{n}^{k}}V_{\varphi}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)}.

We now carry out certain estimates for
L\to\infty
, under the assumption that
\lambda_{n}\not=0
for all
n\geq 1
. We remark that the conclusions will also hold trivially if
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is finitely supported, as then
R_{1}=R_{2}=R_{3}=0
for
L
large enough.
We first estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho_{L}(z)+R_{1}(z)+R_{2}(z)+R_{3}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho_{L}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|+|R_{1}(z)|+|R_{2}(z)|+|R_{3}(z)|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho_{L}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\geq\frac{t}{4}\right)+\sum_{s=1}^{3}\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{s}(z)|\geq\frac{t}{4}\right),

where we used that at least one of the four summands needs to be larger than one fourth of
t/4
and subsequently the union bound.

Let us treat the summands separately. First, we choose
L
large enough such that
\vartheta_{L}
is a good approximation of
\vartheta
. In particular, we assume
\left|\vartheta_{L}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\leq t/8
. We note here that
L
may be chosen independently of
z
since
|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|\leq 1
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Then, using (
5.5
)
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho_{L}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\geq\frac{t}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho_{L}(z)-\vartheta_{L}(z)\right|\geq\frac{t}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\text{exp}\left(-\frac{C_{hw}}{64}K\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\vartheta(z)^{2}},\frac{t}{\vartheta(z)}\right)\right).

Let us now estimate the term
R_{1}
. Then
R_{2}
and
R_{3}
can be treated with the same arguments. First note that, by (
4.5
),

\displaystyle|R_{1}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\lambda_{\ell}|\alpha_{n}^{k}\alpha_{\ell}^{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}|\alpha_{n}^{k}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}|\alpha_{\ell}^{k}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\left(|\Omega|\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}|\alpha_{\ell}^{k}|^{2}.

This estimate implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{1}(z)|\geq\frac{t}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}|\alpha_{\ell}^{k}|^{2}\geq\frac{Kt}{4}\left(|\Omega|\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}|\alpha_{\ell}^{k}|^{2}\geq\frac{t}{4}\left(|\Omega|\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=K\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}|\alpha_{\ell}^{1}|^{2}\geq\frac{t}{4}\left(|\Omega|\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

The probability on the right hand side goes to zero as
L\rightarrow\infty
(note that the left hand side of the tail bound is independent of
L
). In particular, we may choose
L
large enough such that

\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{1}(z)|\geq\frac{t}{4}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}\cdot\text{exp}\left(-\frac{C_{hw}}{64}K\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\vartheta(z)^{2}},\frac{t}{\vartheta(z)}\right)\right).

Repeating the argument for
R_{2}
and
R_{3}
and choosing
C_{1}=\min(C_{hw}/64,1)
concludes the proof.

\Box

We now turn our attention to the level set (
5.1
).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\delta\in(0,\frac{2}{3})
,
\sigma=1
, and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
be such that
|\vartheta(z)-\chi_{\Omega}(z)|\leq\frac{\delta}{4}
. There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

(5.6)

\mathbb{P}\big(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\delta}}(z)\big)\leq 3\cdot e^{-CK}.

If in addition
z\in\Omega^{c}
, then

(5.7)

\mathbb{P}\big(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\delta}}(z)\big)\leq 3\cdot\emph{exp}\left(-C\frac{K\delta}{\vartheta(z)}\right).

Proof.

Let us first reinspect the event
A(z):=\{\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\delta}}(z)\}
. If
z\notin\Omega
, then the event
A(z)
is equivalently described by
\delta\leq\rho(z)
, and, in this case,

\delta\leq{\rho(z)}=\left|{\rho(z)}-\chi_{\Omega}(z)\right|\leq\left|{\rho(z)}-\vartheta(z)\right|+\left|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|.

Hence, by the assumption
\left|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\leq\delta/4
it follows that

A(z)\subset\left\{\left|{\rho(z)}-\vartheta(z)\right|\geq\frac{\delta}{4}\right\},

and consequently

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\delta}}(z)\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{\rho(z)}-\vartheta(z)\right|\geq\frac{\delta}{4}\right).

Therefore, (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) follow from Lemma
5.1
and the observation
\delta/\vartheta(z)\geq 4
.

If on the other hand
z\in\Omega
, then
A(z)
is characterized by
\rho(z)<\delta
and we have

1-\delta<1-{\rho(z)}=\left|{\rho(z)}-\chi_{\Omega}(z)\right|\leq\left|\rho(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|+\left|\vartheta(z)-\chi_{\Omega}(z)\right|.

Again, by the assumption
\left|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)\right|\leq\delta/4
, and
\delta<\frac{2}{3}
we obtain

A(z)\subset\left\{\left|{\rho(z)}-\vartheta(z)\right|>1-\frac{5\delta}{4}\right\}\subset\left\{\left|{\rho(z)}-\vartheta(z)\right|>\frac{1}{6}\right\},

and may then proceed as before, this time noting that

\vartheta(z)\leq 1
.

\Box

In Theorem
5.2
we required that
|\vartheta(z)-\chi_{\Omega}(z)|\leq\delta/4
. Let us now bound the measure of the set where this is not satisfied.

Proposition 5.3
.

Set

(5.8)

M({g,\varphi}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}g(z)|^{2}|z|\,\mathrm{d}z.

Then

(i)

\|\chi_{\Omega}-\vartheta\|_{1}\lesssim M({g,\varphi})\cdot|\partial\Omega|
,

(ii)

\left|\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\ |\vartheta(z)-\chi_{\Omega}(z)|\geq\delta\right\}\right|\lesssim M({g,\varphi})\cdot\frac{|\partial\Omega|}{\delta},

(iii)

\|\vartheta\|_{1}\leq|\Omega|,

(iv)

|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)|\lesssim M({g,\varphi})\cdot\emph{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)^{-1}.

Proof.

On the one hand, by the eigenexpansion
H_{\Omega}=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\langle\cdot,f_{n}\rangle f_{n}
,

\displaystyle\langle H_{\Omega}\pi(z)\varphi,\pi(z)\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}|\langle\pi(z)\varphi,f_{n}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}.

On the other hand, applying (
4.3
) directly gives

\displaystyle\langle H_{\Omega}\pi(z)\varphi,\pi(z)\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\chi_{\Omega}(w)\left|\langle\pi(z)\varphi,\pi(w)g\rangle\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\chi_{\Omega}(w)|V_{\varphi}g(z-w)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w=\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z),

which shows

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}=\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

We hence estimate

\displaystyle|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z)\right|+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{2})|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}.

If
z\in\Omega
, then

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{2})|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(1-\lambda_{n})|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\leq\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z),

where we used that
\sum_{n}|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\leq K_{\varphi}(z,z)=1
.
If on the other hand
z\in\Omega^{c}
, then

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n}^{2})|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}|V_{\varphi}f_{n}(z)|^{2}=\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z)-\chi_{\Omega}(z),

and therefore

(5.9)

|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)|\leq 2\left|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z)\right|.

Since
\|V_{\varphi}g\|_{2}=1
, by (
3.1
),

\|\chi_{\Omega}-\vartheta\|_{1}\leq 2|\partial\Omega|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}g(z)|^{2}|z|\,\mathrm{d}z,

that is,
(i)
holds. Property
(ii)
then follows by Chebyshev’s inequality. The third property follows directly
from (
4.5
):
\left\|\vartheta\right\|_{1}=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}\leq\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}=|\Omega|
.

Finally, to prove
(iv)
we define
R=\text{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
. Then
\chi_{\Omega}(z)=\chi_{\Omega}(w)
for every
|z-w|<R
which implies using (
5.9
)

\displaystyle\big|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\big|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\chi_{\Omega}\ast|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\big|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\chi_{\Omega}\ast\left(|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}\chi_{B_{R}(0)}\right)(z)\big|+2\big|\chi_{\Omega}\ast\left(|V_{\varphi}g|^{2}(1-\chi_{B_{R}(0)})\right)(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\chi_{\Omega}(z)\left(1-\int_{|z-w|<R}|V_{\varphi}g(z-w)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w\right)+2\int_{|w|\geq R}|V_{\varphi}g(w)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w.

Hence,

|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\vartheta(z)|\leq 4\int_{|w|\geq R}|V_{\varphi}g(w)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{4}{R}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}g(z)|^{2}|z|\,\mathrm{d}z.

\Box

Remark 5.4
.

Since
\vartheta\leq 1
, by Proposition

(iv)
we see that the estimate (
5.7
) improves as the distance between
z
and
\Omega
increases.

5.3.
Estimates of symmetric difference with high probability

Our subsequent estimates depend on the local supremum functional

\mathcal{K}(z,w):=\sup_{y\in B_{1}(z)}|K_{\varphi}(y,w)|,

which is a standard tool in the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, and their associated Banach spaces — see, e.g.,
[
17
]
. Since
K_{\varphi}
is the integral kernel of a projection operator, it enjoys a self-averaging property: pointwise values of
K_{\varphi}
can be obtained as averages thereof. The self-averaging property then allows one to improve estimates that hold on average, and deliver uniform conclusions. In our case, we will use this insight to turn pointwise probability estimates into locally uniform ones.

As a first step, we bound the probability that
\|(\rho-\vartheta)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},\cdot)\|_{1}
be large. We will afterwards relate this tail bound to the probabilities of the supremum (resp. infimum) of
\rho
being large (resp. small) on adequate domains — see Lemma
5.6
and Theorem
5.9
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
\sigma=1
, and
\gamma\in[\gamma_{0},1],\ \gamma_{0}>0
. Then there exist constants
c,C>0
only depending on
\varphi
and
\gamma_{0}
such that

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\gamma\right)\leq c\cdot e^{-CK}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

Proof.

Let us define

(5.11)

C_{\varphi}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathcal{K}(0,w)\,\mathrm{d}w<\infty.

Let
p\geq 1
. By Chebyshev’s and Hölder’s inequalities,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\gamma\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\leq\left(\gamma\right)^{-{p}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w){\,\mathrm{d}w}\right)^{{p}}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\leq\left(\gamma\right)^{-{p}}C_{\varphi}^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbb{E}\left\{|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|^{{p}}\right\}\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\leq\left(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{\gamma}\right)^{{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{{p}}t^{{p}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\geq t\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\ \mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w,

where we used that
C_{\varphi}\geq\int|K_{\varphi}(0,w)|\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\int|V_{\varphi}\varphi(w)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}w=1
.
Let us further estimate the integral with respect to
t
. We invoke Lemma
5.1

and use that the respective constant is
C_{1}\leq 1
to estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{{p}}t^{{p}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\geq t\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188pt\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}{{p}}t^{{p}-1}\text{exp}\left(-C_{1}K\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\vartheta(w)^{2}},\frac{t}{\vartheta(w)}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188pt=\int_{0}^{\vartheta(w)}{{p}}t^{{p}-1}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{C_{1}Kt^{2}}{\vartheta(w)^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\vartheta(w)}^{\infty}pt^{{p}-1}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{C_{1}Kt}{\vartheta(w)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188pt=\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{\vartheta(w)^{2}}{C_{1}K}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\int_{0}^{C_{1}K}t^{\frac{p-2}{2}}e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t+p\left(\frac{\vartheta(w)}{C_{1}K}\right)^{{p}}\int_{C_{1}K}^{\infty}t^{p-1}e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip-71.13188pt\leq\vartheta(w){C_{1}}^{-p}\Big[\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}+1\right)K^{-\frac{p}{2}}+{\Gamma(p+1)}K^{-p}\Big].

Hence we have thus far shown that for every
1\leq p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\gamma\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\lesssim\left(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{C_{1}\gamma}\right)^{{p}}\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}+1\right)}{K^{\frac{p}{2}}}+\frac{\Gamma(p+1)}{K^{p}}\right]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

Let us write for short
s=C_{\varphi}/C_{1}\gamma>1.
If
K/s^{2}\geq 2
, then we choose
p=2\lfloor K/2s^{2}\rfloor\geq 1
and

\displaystyle s^{p}\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}+1\right)}{K^{\frac{p}{2}}}+\frac{\Gamma(p+1)}{K^{p}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s^{2}}{K}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor}\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor!+\left(\frac{s}{K}\right)^{2\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor}\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor\right)!

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\hskip-1.0pt\right\rfloor}\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor^{-\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor}\hskip-1.0pt\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor!+\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor\right)^{-2\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\hskip-1.0pt\right\rfloor}\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor\right)!

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor},

where the final inequality follows from

\left({2n}\right)^{-2n}(2n)!=\left({2n}\right)^{-n}n!\cdot\left({2n}\right)^{-n}(n+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot 2n\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{K}{2s^{2}}\right\rfloor}\lesssim e^{-c(C_{1}\gamma/2C_{\varphi})^{2}{K}}\leq e^{-c(C_{1}\gamma_{0}/2C_{\varphi})^{2}{K}}

for a universal constant
c>0
,
we conclude that (
5.10
) holds.

Finally, if
K/s^{2}<2
, then
\gamma>\gamma_{0}
implies that
K<2C_{\varphi}^{2}/\gamma_{0}^{2}C_{1}^{2}
. Hence, in that case, setting
p=1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\gamma\right)\lesssim\frac{C_{\varphi}}{C_{1}\gamma_{0}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w,

and (
5.10
) also holds, possibly after taking a different constants
c,C
.
\Box

As an application, we obtain the following.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\sigma=1
,
\delta\in[\delta_{0},1],\ \delta_{0}>0
, and
z_{0}\in\mathcal{A}(\delta)
, where

(5.12)

\mathcal{A}(\delta):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w){\,\mathrm{d}w}\leq\frac{\delta}{2\|V_{\varphi}\varphi\|_{1}}\right\}.

There exist constants
c,C>0
only depending on
\varphi
and
\delta_{0}
such that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in B_{1}(z_{0})}\rho(z)\geq\delta\right)\leq c\cdot e^{-CK}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(z)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},z){\,\mathrm{d}z}.

Proof.

From the reproducing formula (
4.2
), almost surely

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{1}(z_{0})}\rho(z)

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V_{\varphi}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(w)K_{\varphi}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{\varphi}\varphi\|_{1}\sup_{z\in B_{1}(z_{0})}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(w)|^{2}|K_{\varphi}(z,w)|\,\mathrm{d}w

(5.14)

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{\varphi}\varphi\|_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}\rho(z)\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\frac{\delta}{\|V_{\varphi}\varphi\|_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\hskip-2.84544pt(\rho(z)-\vartheta(z))\mathcal{K}(z_{0},z)\,\mathrm{d}z\geq\left(\frac{\delta}{\|V_{\varphi}\varphi\|_{1}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(z)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(z)-\vartheta(z)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},z){\,\mathrm{d}z}\geq\frac{\delta}{2\|V_{\varphi}\varphi\|_{1}}\right).

Now the estimate follows from Lemma
5.5
.
\Box

Since we assume that
g,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
m_{g,\varphi}>0
such that

(5.15)

|\langle\varphi,\pi(z)g\rangle|\leq m_{g,\varphi}\big(1+|z|\big)^{-2(d+1)}.

If
\varphi=g
, we write
m_{\varphi}
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\sigma=1
,
\delta\in[\delta_{0},1],\ \delta_{0}>0,
and
\mathcal{A}(\delta)
as in Lemma
5.6
.
Then there exist constants
c,C>0
depending only on
\varphi
and
\delta_{0}
such that the probability of an estimation error occurring in
\Omega^{c}\cap\mathcal{A}(\delta)
satisfies

(5.16)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in\Omega^{c}\cap\mathcal{A}(\delta)}\rho(z)\geq\delta\right)\leq c\cdot|\Omega|\cdot e^{-CK}.

Proof.

By the Besicovitch covering theorem
[
7
]
, there exist points
\{z_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathcal{A}(\delta)
such that

\mathcal{A}(\delta)\subset\bigcup_{k}B_{1}(z_{k})
, and

(5.17)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\#\big\{k:\ z\in B_{1}(z_{k})\big\}\leq b_{d}<\infty,

where
b_{d}
is a universal constant depending only on the dimension
d
.

Next, we show that
\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\sum_{k}\mathcal{K}(z_{k},w)
is bounded by a constant only depending on
\varphi
. To see this, we first note that if
z\in B_{1}(z_{k})
and
|z_{k}-w|\geq n-1
, then
|z-w|\geq n-2
, and,

\bigcup_{n-1\leq|z_{k}-w|<n}B_{1}(z_{k})\subset B_{n+{1}}(w)\backslash B_{n-{2}}(w).

Hence, by (
5.17
), there are at most

b_{d}\big(|B_{n+1}(0)|-|B_{n-{2}}(0)|\big)/B_{1}(0)
such points
z_{k}
.
Consequently, (
5.15
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathcal{K}(z_{k},w)

\displaystyle\leq m_{\varphi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{{n-1\leq|z_{k}-w|<n}}\sup_{z\in B_{1}(z_{k})}(1+|z-w|)^{-2(d+1)}

\displaystyle\lesssim m_{\varphi}b_{d}\left(1+\sum_{n\geq 2}(1+(n-2))^{-2(d+1)}\big({|B_{n+{1}}(0)|-|B_{n-{2}}(0)|}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim m_{\varphi}b_{d}\left(1+\sum_{n\geq 2}n^{-2(d+1)}n^{2d}\right)\lesssim m_{\varphi}<\infty.

Taking the union bound, and applying Lemma
5.6
then shows

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{A}(\delta)\cap\Omega^{c}}\rho(z)\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in B_{1}(z_{k})}\rho(z)\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\cdot e^{-CK}\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(z)\mathcal{K}(z_{k},z){\,\mathrm{d}z}

\displaystyle\leq c\cdot e^{-CK}\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\sum_{k}\mathcal{K}(z_{k},w)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(z){\,\mathrm{d}z}

\displaystyle\lesssim m_{\varphi}c\cdot e^{-CK}|\Omega|,

where we used
\|\vartheta\|_{1}\leq|\Omega|
(see Proposition
5.3

(iii)
).

\Box

Next we estimate the size of the region
\Omega^{c}\backslash\mathcal{A}(\delta)
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\delta>0
. Then there exists a constant
c>0
only depending on
\varphi
such that

(5.18)

\left|\Omega^{c}\backslash\mathcal{A}(\delta)\right|\leq c\cdot M({g,\varphi})\cdot\frac{|\partial\Omega|}{\delta},

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w\leq c\cdot M({g,\varphi})\cdot\emph{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)^{-1},\qquad z\in\Omega^{c}.

Proof.

Observe that

\mathcal{K}(z,w)\leq m_{\varphi}\sup_{y\in B_{1}(z)}(1+|y-w|)^{-2(d+1)}\lesssim m_{\varphi}(1+|z-w|)^{-2(d+1)}.

Then we may estimate the following integral

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{c}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\int_{\Omega}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\Omega^{c}}\int_{\Omega^{c}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\lesssim m_{\varphi}\cdot|\partial\Omega|+m_{\varphi}\cdot M({g,\varphi})\cdot|\partial\Omega|,

where we applied (
3.1
) with
\psi(z)=(1+|z|)^{-2(d+1)}
and Proposition
5.3

(i)
to derive the estimates on the first and second integral respectively. Then (
5.18
) follows from Chebyshev’s inequality.

To show the second property, we set
R=\text{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
and assume that
z\in\Omega^{c}
. By Proposition
5.3

(iv)
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega+B_{R/2}(0)}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w+\int_{(\Omega+B_{R/2}(0))^{c}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\leq m_{\varphi}\int_{\Omega+B_{R/2}(0)}(1+|z-w|)^{-2(d+1)}\,\mathrm{d}w+M({g,\varphi})R^{-1}\|\mathcal{K}(z,\cdot)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq m_{\varphi}\int_{B_{R/2}(0)^{c}}(1+|w|)^{-2(d+1)}\,\mathrm{d}w+M({g,\varphi})R^{-1}\|\mathcal{K}(0,\cdot)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(m_{\varphi}+C_{\varphi}M({g,\varphi})\big)R^{-1}.

\Box

We will now consider estimates for the uniform error probability in
\Omega
. We first bound the probability that an estimation error occurs in a set of the form
\Omega\cap\{|\vartheta-1|\leq\delta\}
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\sigma=1
and
\delta\in(0,1/2]
. There exist constants
c,C>0
only depending on
\varphi
such that

(5.19)

\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{z\in\Omega\hskip 1.42271pt\cap\hskip 1.42271pt\{|\vartheta-1|\leq\delta/3\}}\rho(z)<\delta\right)\leq c\cdot|\Omega^{1}|\cdot e^{-C{K}}.

Proof.

First, we observe that

(5.20)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \inf_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}\rho(z)&\geq\rho(z_{0})-\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}|\rho(z_{0})-\rho(z)|\\
&\geq\rho(z_{0})-\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left||V_{\varphi}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(z_{0})|^{2}-|V_{\varphi}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(z)|^{2}\right|.\end{aligned}

The reproducing formula (
4.2
) and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then show that for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
0<R\leq 1
, and
z\in B_{R}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\Big||V_{\varphi}f(z)|^{2}-

\displaystyle|V_{\varphi}f(z_{0})|^{2}\Big|=\big(|V_{\varphi}f(z)|-|V_{\varphi}f(z_{0})|\big)\big(|V_{\varphi}f(z)|+|V_{\varphi}f(z_{0})|\big)

\displaystyle=\big||V_{\varphi}\pi(-z_{0})f(z-z_{0})|-|V_{\varphi}\pi(-z_{0})f(0)|\big|\big(|V_{\varphi}f(z)|+|V_{\varphi}f(z_{0})|\big)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}\pi(-z_{0})f(w)\langle\pi(z-z_{0})\varphi-\varphi,\pi(w)\varphi\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}f(w)|\big(|{K}_{\varphi}(z,w)|+|{K}_{\varphi}(z_{0},w)|\big)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}\pi(-z_{0})f(w)\langle\pi(z-z_{0})\varphi-\varphi,\pi(w)\varphi\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}w\cdot 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}f(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\varphi}^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}\pi(-z_{0})f(w)|^{2}|\langle\pi(z\hskip-1.0pt-\hskip-1.0ptz_{0})\varphi-\varphi,\pi(w)\varphi\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\langle\pi(z-z_{0})\varphi-\varphi,\pi(w)\varphi\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}f(w)|^{2}\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{3/2}C_{\varphi}^{1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}f(w)|^{2}\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\langle\pi(z-z_{0})\varphi-\varphi,\pi(w)\varphi\rangle|\,\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq T_{\varphi}(R)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}f(w)|^{2}\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w,

where

because
\varphi
is a Schwartz function. Therefore,

(5.21)

\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}|\rho(z_{0})-\rho(z)|\leq T_{\varphi}(R)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

Let now
z_{0}\in\big\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\ |\vartheta(z)-\chi_{\Omega}(z)|\leq\delta/3\big\}\cap\Omega
. Then, combining the assumption
\delta\in(0,1/2]
with (
5.20
) and (
5.21
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}\rho(z)<\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\rho(z_{0})-\vartheta(z_{0})<\delta-\vartheta(z_{0})+T_{\varphi}(R)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\rho(z_{0})-\vartheta(z_{0})<-\frac{1}{3}+T_{\varphi}(R)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\right).

In the following, we make use of

. Define the events

and

Then, we may estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{z_{0}}\cap B_{z_{0}})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\rho(z_{0})-\vartheta(z_{0})<-\frac{1}{6}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\rho(z_{0})-\vartheta(z_{0})|>\frac{1}{6}\right)\lesssim e^{-C{K}},

where we used Lemma

and
\vartheta(z_{0})\leq 1
.

Second, choose

\frac{1}{6T_{\varphi}(R_{\varphi})}\geq 2C_{\varphi},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{z_{0}}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\rho(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\frac{1}{6T_{\varphi}(R_{\varphi})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\geq\frac{1}{6T_{\varphi}(R_{\varphi})}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\hskip-3.41418pt\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w)\,\mathrm{d}w\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w){\,\mathrm{d}w}\geq\frac{1}{6T_{\varphi}(R_{\varphi})}-C_{\varphi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w){\,\mathrm{d}w}\geq C_{\varphi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|\rho(w)-\vartheta(w)|\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w){\,\mathrm{d}w}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\cdot e^{-CK}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\vartheta(w)\mathcal{K}(z_{0},w){\,\mathrm{d}w}\lesssim m_{\varphi}\cdot c\cdot e^{-C{K}},

where we used Lemma
5.5
.

Let us consider a covering of

,

points

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{z\in B_{R_{\varphi}}(z_{k})}\rho(z)<\delta\right)

as

.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\sigma=1
, and assume (

For the event

, we argue as in the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}\rho(z)

\displaystyle\leq\rho(z_{0})+\sup_{z\in B_{R}(z_{0})}|\rho(z_{0})-\rho(z)|

guarantees the existence of a constant

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\rho\|_{\infty}>2)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in\Omega^{c}\cap\mathcal{A}(1)}\rho(z)>1\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in(\Omega^{c}\backslash\mathcal{A}(1))\cup\Omega}\rho(z)>2\right)

by Theorem

. For the second term, we cover

with balls of radius

) and apply a union bound argument as in the proof of Theorem

4.2
), the averaged spectrogram formed with respect to the model window

is dominated by
\rho
(which involves the reconstruction window
\varphi
)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|V_{g}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(z)|^{2}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V_{\varphi}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(w)\langle\pi(w)\varphi,\pi(z)g\rangle\,\mathrm{d}w\right|^{2}

Next, we
use the double orthogonality property (
4.4
) of the eigenfunctions

to derive

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|V_{g}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{\ell}^{k}\overline{\alpha_{n}^{k}}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{n}\int_{\Omega}V_{g}f_{\ell}(z)\overline{V_{g}f_{n}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z

4.1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\rho\|_{\infty}\leq\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|V_{g}H_{\Omega}\mathcal{N}_{k}(z)|^{2}\leq\|V_{\varphi}g\|_{1}^{2}\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\Gamma\left(K\lfloor|\Omega|/2\rfloor\right)}^{-1}\gamma\left(K\lfloor|\Omega|/2\rfloor,10\|V_{\varphi}g\|_{1}^{2}K\lfloor|\Omega|/2\rfloor\delta\right),

,

),

\gamma\left(a,ax\right)\leq(2x)^{a/2}\Gamma\left(a\right),\quad x\in[0,1/2],\ a\geq 2,

This implies that

Theorem 5.12

Consequently, choosing

Proof.

Let us assume that
\sigma=1
. The general case then follows again by homogeneity.
We define the family of events

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{|\Omega\Delta\widehat{\Omega}|\big\}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\big\|\chi_{\Omega}-\chi_{S_{\|\rho\|_{\infty}/4}}\big\|_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\big|\chi_{\Omega}(z)-\chi_{S_{\|\rho\|_{\infty}/4}}(z)\big|\right\}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbb{P}\left(\big\{\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\|\rho\|_{\infty}/4}}(z)\big\}\cap E_{n}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z.

For

(5.29)

\mathbb{P}\left(\big\{\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\|\rho\|_{\infty}/4}}(z)\big\}\cap E_{n}\right)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{P}\left(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{b_{n}/4}}(z)\right),&\text{if }z\in\Omega\\
\mathbb{P}\left(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{a_{n}/4}}(z)\right),&\text{if }z\in\Omega^{c}\end{array}\right..

Using Propositions
5.3
and
5.10

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbb{P}\big(\big\{

\displaystyle\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{\|\rho\|_{\infty}/4}}(z)\big\}\cap E_{n}\big)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega\cup\{|\vartheta-\chi_{\Omega}|>a_{n}^{2}\}}\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\Omega^{c}\cap\{|\vartheta-\chi_{\Omega}|\leq a_{n}^{2}\}}\mathbb{P}\big(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{a_{n}/4}}(z)\big)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\lesssim(n-1)^{-K/2}\left(|\Omega|+n^{2}\|V_{\varphi}g\|_{1}^{4}M({g,\varphi})|\partial\Omega|\right)+\int_{\Omega^{c}\cap\{\vartheta\leq a_{n}^{2}\}}\mathbb{P}\big(\chi_{\Omega}(z)\neq\chi_{S_{a_{n}/4}}(z)\big)\,\mathrm{d}z

If

, then
\text{exp}\left({-C{K}/{\delta}}\right)\geq\text{exp}\left({-C{K}/{\sqrt{\vartheta(z)}}}\right)\geq\text{exp}\left({-C{K\delta}/{\vartheta(z)}}\right)
.

5.2

To estimate
\big\|\sqrt{\vartheta}\big\|_{1}

,

, and
