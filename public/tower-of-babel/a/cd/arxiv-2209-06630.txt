The maximal regularity property of abstract integro-differential equations
Sebastian Król

Abstract
. We provide a convenient framework for the study of the well-posedness of a variety of abstract (integro)differential equations in general Banach function spaces. It allows us to extend and complement the known theory on the maximal regularity of such equations. More precisely, by methods of harmonic analysis, we identify large classes of Banach spaces which are invariant with respect to distributional Fourier multipliers. Such classes include general vector-valued Banach function spaces Φ \Phi and/or the scales of Besov and Triebel-Lizorkin spaces defined by Φ \Phi . We apply this result to the study of the well-posedness and maximal regularity property of abstract second-order integro-differential equation, which models various types of elliptic and parabolic problems arising in different areas of applied mathematics.

1.
Introduction with preliminary considerations

The paper continues the well-known research program on the study of the well-posedness and maximal regularity of pseudodifferential equations in a variety of function spaces via techniques of vector-valued Fourier multiplier theory.
In a quite general framework, this program was already formulated by Amann in
[
1
, Chapter 3]
and was labelled as ’
pairs of maximal regularity
’. In one of the pioneering papers in this area,
[
2
]
, Amann showed how the boundedness of translation-invariant operators with operator-valued symbols guarantees the well-posedness of various elliptic and parabolic (integro)differential equations in a scale of vector-valued Besov spaces. His paper constitutes a conceptual background and toolbox for further extensions in the literature.
Here, we just mention the breakthrough result due to Weis
[
44
]
on the characterization of the
L^{p}
-maximal regularity of the first-order Cauchy problems and a few others, seminal papers
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
27
]
(see also the references therein).
In this paper, we provide a convenient framework for such studies. In particular, it allows us to extend and unify several results from the literature.

To describe our main results and explain difficulties arising during their studies, for the transparency, we consider only a special form of a more general abstract second-order integrodifferential equation (
20
) discussed in Section
5
; see Theorem
5.4
with Corollary
5.5
for the corresponding results.
Moreover, to keep this presentation compact, we closely follow the notation and terminology from Amann’s paper
[
2
]
, which we only roughly recall below, and refer the reader to
[
2
]
for more details in the case. It allows us to keep the novelty of our results more transparent.

Consider the following abstract evolution problem on the line:

(1)

\partial u+Au=f\quad\textrm{ in }{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X).

Here
X
is a Banach space and by
\partial
we denote a distributional derivative, that is,
\partial u(\phi):=-u(\partial\phi)
for every
X
-valued distribution
u\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(X):={\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X)
and every test function
\phi\in{\mathcal{D}}:={\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})

stands for a closed, linear operator on

with domain

equipped with the graph norm and denoted by

in this section.

Under suitable assumptions on the operator

one can show that for every tempered distribution
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X):={\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R};X)
the problem (
1
) has a unique distributional solution
u_{f}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y)
. For instance, suppose that
i\mathbb{R}\subset\rho(A)
, where
\rho(A)
stands for the resolvent set of

and that the function
a(t):=(it+A)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)
,

, has a polynomial growth at infinity.
Then, one can easily argue that the
solution operator

U
for (

) is given by

(2)

U:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)\ni f\mapsto{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\Theta(a,{\mathcal{F}}f)\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X).

Here,
\Theta
denotes a hypocontinuous, bilinear map from

into

(see the kernel theorem, e.g. in
[

stands for the space of slowly increasing, smooth

\mathbb{R}
.

One of the main questions of the distributional theory of partial differential equations, in the context of (
1
), is to specify further assumptions on
A
and

such that (
1
) is satisfied in a more classical, strong sense.
That is, naturally restricting
f
to the class of regular distributions, i.e.
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{r}(X):=L^{1}_{loc}(X)\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
, we ask when
Uf
is in the Sobolev class
W^{1,1}_{loc}(X)
, which means that
Uf
,
\partial Uf\in L_{loc}^{1}(X)
. Then, in particular, the classical derivative
(Uf)^{\prime}
of
Uf
exists a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
,
\partial Uf=(Uf)^{\prime}
,
Uf(t)\in Y
for a.e.

, and (
1
) is satisfied pointwise a.e on
\mathbb{R}
.
In other words, we would like to identify a subspace of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{r}(X)
such that the solution operator
U
maps it into
L^{1}_{loc}(Y)
or equivalently
the operator
AU
maps it into
L^{1}_{loc}(X)
.

The other related question, which is of main importance in the study of nonlinear problems associated with

, concerns the property of
maximal regularity with respect to a given Banach space

\mathbb{E}(X):=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{R};X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)

That is, under suitable assumptions on
A
and

, we would like to identify a class of Banach spaces
\mathbb{E}(X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
such that for all

each summand on the left side of (
1

\partial Uf
and
AUf
, belongs to
\mathbb{E}(X)
.
Since

, it is equivalent to say that the corresponding distributional solution

of (

) is in
\mathbb{E}(Y)
(or, equivalently,
\partial Uf\in\mathbb{E}(X)
). Therefore, this question can be rephrased as the question about the identification of those Banach spaces
\mathbb{E}(X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
, which are
invariant
with respect to
AU
.
Moreover, by the simple algebraic structure of (
1
), if (in addition)
\mathbb{E}(X)\subset L^{1}_{loc}(X)
, then we get that
Uf
is a strong solution of (
1

for every

.

The above questions constitute a natural research program, which can be easily reformulated for a variety of different types of (non)autonomous integrodifferential equations. In this paper, following
[
3
,
4

, we address such questions to an abstract integrodifferential equation (see (

Our main result, Theorem

We refer to Section
2
for the definition of spaces involved in the above formulation. In the context of our first question, we should also point out a consequence of Theorem
3.1
(i), which says that

\bigcup\Phi(X)=\bigcup_{w\in A_{2}}L^{2}(\mathbb{R};w\mathrm{d}t;X).

The sum on the left side is taken over all Banach function spaces
\Phi
such that the Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator
M
is bounded on
\Phi
and its dual
\Phi^{\prime}
, and
A_{2}
stands for the Muckenhoupt class of

-weights on
\mathbb{R}
.

Note that the formulation of the program of ’pairs of maximal regularity’ from
[
1
, Chapter III]
slightly differs from the one expressed by these two above questions.
Here, we do not a priori restrict ourselves to the class of spaces such that
\mathbb{E}(Y)\subset W^{1,1}_{loc}(X)
(cf.
[
1
, Chapter III.1.5, p.94]
).
For the problem (
1
), as was already pointed out above, if
\mathbb{E}(X)
is invariant with respect to
AU
and
\mathbb{E}(X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{r}(X)
, then a simply algebraic structure of (
1
) yields immediately that
U(\mathbb{E}(X))\subset W^{1,1}_{loc}(X)
. But for equations with a more general structure than that one of (
1
), the existence of distributional/strong solutions and
maximal regularity property
usually can be obtained under different conditions.

In any case, the basic idea comes from the study of (
1
). It relies on the study invariant subspaces with respect to operators corresponding to the solution operator for a given problem via their boundedness properties, i.e., norm estimates they satisfy.
More precisely, in the context of (
1
), the operator
U
can be expressed as a convolution operator with a distributional kernel
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
, where
\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a,\phi\right\rangle:=\left\langle a,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\phi\right\rangle
for all
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
That is,

Uf=\left({\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a\right)\ast f,

which holds for
f\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(X)\cup S(X)
(see
[
2
, Section 3]
).
Next, the methods of the singular integral operator theory allow showing the existence of an operator
T
in
{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{E}(X),\mathbb{E}(Y))
, which agrees with the operator
U
on
S(X)
(or
L^{1}_{c}(X)
).

For a Banach space
\mathbb{E}(X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
, which contains
S(X)
as a dense subset, it immediately gives that
\mathbb{E}(X)
is invariant with respect to
AU
.
However, in the case when
{\mathcal{S}}(X)
is not dense in
\mathbb{E}(X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{r}(X)
, e.g., when
\mathbb{E}(X)
is a Lorentz space
L^{p,\infty}(X)
or a Besov type space
B^{s,q}_{L^{p,\infty}}(X)
corresponding to it (see Section
2
), it is not clear how to show directly that
Tf=Uf
for every
f\in\mathbb{E}(X)
(cf.
[
23
, Problems 3.2 and 3.3]
, which arise in the study of the boundedness of Fourier multipliers on the classical Besov spaces
B^{s,q}_{\infty}(X)
, i.e., defined on the basis of
\Phi=L^{\infty}
).

Such consistency of the solution operators with their bounded
extensions
resulting from the convolution representations is one of the main problems we address in the context of general Banach spaces considered in this paper; see Proposition
4.2
for the corresponding result. To solve it, we provide a suitable adaptation of the Rubio de Francia iteration algorithm, see Theorem
3.1
, which allows for relaxing some difficulties with density arguments that arise in such and related considerations in the literature; see, e.g. Remarks
3.5
(b) and
3.2
(b).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the classes of Banach spaces with respect to which the questions on well-posedness and maximal regularity are examined. Their basic properties, which are crucial for the proofs of main results, are stated in Lemmas
3.6
and
5.3
.
Section 3 contains the main extrapolation results, see Theorem
3.8
and its specification to a class of Fourier multipliers, Proposition

.
These results are applied in Section

to derive the main results on the well-posedness and maximal regularity property of abstract integrodifferential equations (
20
); see Theorem

and Corollary
5.5
. We conclude with some comments on particular cases of (

Function spaces

For

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}

\displaystyle\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}_{w}(X),L^{p}_{w}(Y))}\|{\mathcal{R}}\|_{\Phi}\|f\|_{\Phi(X)}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}\|h\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}_{w}(X),L^{p}_{w}(Y))}\|{\mathcal{R}}|f|_{X}\|_{\Phi}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}_{w}(X),L^{p}_{w}(Y))}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{R}}(|f|_{X}){\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{R}}(|f|_{X}){\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}_{w}(X),L^{p}_{w}(Y))}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f|_{X}^{p}{\mathcal{R}}(|f|_{X})^{1-p}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{R}}(|f|_{X}){\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}f|_{Y}^{p}{\mathcal{R}}(|f|_{X})^{1-p}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{R}}(|f|_{X}){\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}f|_{Y}{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}h\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}f|_{Y}|h|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Our assumption on the dependence of the norms of
T_{j}
and (
6
) yield

\mu:=\sup_{j\in J}\sup_{f\in\Phi(X),h\in\Phi^{\prime}}\|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}_{w}(X),L^{p}_{w}(Y))}<\infty.

Consequently, for every
h\in\Phi^{\prime}
and
f\in\Phi(X)
we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}f|_{Y}|h|\mathrm{d}t\leq\mu\|{\mathcal{R}}\|_{\Phi}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}\|f\|_{\Phi(X)}\|h\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}.

Therefore, since
\Phi
has Fatou’s property, by the Lorentz-Luxemburg theorem
|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}f|_{Y}\in\Phi^{\prime\prime}=\Phi
and
\|{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi(X),\Phi(Y))}\leq\mu\|{\mathcal{R}}\|_{\Phi}\|{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}
. This completes the proof.

∎

(b) Note that the point

shows that if an operator

for every

The following result shows that Theorem

extends
[

, Theorem 4.10]
.
We start with some preliminaries.
A special class of Banach function spaces is the class of
symmetric spaces
(or
rearrangement invariant Banach function spaces
); see
[
10
]
.
For a locally integrable weight
w
on
\mathbb{R}
, we define
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{E}_{w}}:L^{0}_{+}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
by
\|f\|_{\mathbb{E}_{w}}:=\|f^{*}_{w}\|_{\mathbb{E}}
, where
f^{*}_{w}
denotes the decreasing rearrangement of
f
with respect to
w\mathrm{d}t
. By
[
10
, Theorem 4.9, p. 61]
, the space

\mathbb{E}_{w}:=\{f\in L^{0}:\|f\|_{\mathbb{E}_{w}}<\infty\}

with
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{E}_{w}}
is a symmetric space over
(\mathbb{R},w\mathrm{d}t)
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}_{w}
is a Banach function space over
(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}t)
in the sense of Section
2
.

For the convenience, we define the Boyd indices
p_{\mathbb{E}}
and
q_{\mathbb{E}}
of a symmetric space
\mathbb{E}
following
[
33
]
(in
[
10
]
the Boyd indices are defined as the reciprocals with respect to
[
33
]
).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{E}
be a symmetric space over
(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}t)
with nontrivial Boyd indices
p_{\mathbb{E}},q_{\mathbb{E}}\in(1,\infty)
.
Then, for every Muckenhoupt weight
w\in A_{p_{\mathbb{E}}}
, the Hardy-Littlewood operator
M
is bounded on
\mathbb{E}_{w}
and its dual
(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}
with respect to
(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}t)
.

Proof.

By
[
18
, Lemma 4.12]
,
M
is bounded on
\Phi=\mathbb{E}_{w}
. By the inverse Hölder inequality, there exist
p
and
q
with
1<p<p_{\mathbb{E}}\leq q_{\mathbb{E}}<q<\infty
such that
w\in A_{p}\subset A_{q}
. Therefore, by Muckenhoupt’s theorem,
M
is also bounded on
L^{p}_{w}
and
L^{q}_{w}
.
One can easily check that the operator
S(f):=M(fw)/w

(f\in L^{1}_{loc})
is bounded on

L^{p^{\prime}}_{w}
and
L^{q^{\prime}}_{w}
. Hence, it is of joint weak type
(q^{\prime},q^{\prime};p^{\prime},p^{\prime})
with respect to
(\mathbb{R},w\mathrm{d}t)
; see
[
12
, Theorem 4.11, p. 223]
. Consequently,
since
p_{\mathbb{E}^{\prime}}=q^{\prime}_{\mathbb{E}}
and
q_{\mathbb{E}^{\prime}}=p^{\prime}_{\mathbb{E}}
, we have that
1<q^{\prime}<p_{\mathbb{E}^{\prime}}\leq q_{\mathbb{E}^{\prime}}<p^{\prime}<\infty
. Next, by Boyd’s theorem
[
10
, Theorem 5.16, p.153]
, we get that
S
is bounded on
(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})_{w}
. Moreover, since
(\mathbb{R},w\mathrm{d}t)
is a resonant space (see
[
10
, Theorem 1.6, p.51]
, we have
(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})_{w}={(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}}^{w}
with equal norms, where
{(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}}^{w}
denotes the dual space of
\mathbb{E}_{w}
with respect to
(\mathbb{R},w\mathrm{d}t)
. Indeed, the inclusion
(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})_{w}\subset{(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}}^{w}
follows from
[
10
, Proposition 4.2, p. 59]
and the reverse one from the Luxemburg representation theorem
[
10
, Theorem 4.10, p. 62]
.

Finally, note that for every
f\in(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\|Mf\|_{(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\|(Mf)w^{-1}\|_{{(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}}^{w}}=\|S(fw^{-1})\|_{(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})_{w}}

\displaystyle\leq\|S\|\|fw^{-1}\|_{{(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})_{w}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|S\|\|f\|_{{(\mathbb{E}_{w})^{\prime}}},

where
\|S\|
denotes the norm of
S
on
(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})_{w}
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
The singular integral operators

To proceed, we specify a class of operators for which Theorem
3.1
applies. In particular, it allows us to derive the basic properties of generalized Besov and Triebel-Lizorkin spaces introduced in Section
2
, which are involved in the proofs of the subsequent results.

We say that a bounded linear operator
T
in
{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}(X),L^{p}(Y))
for some
p\in(1,\infty)
is a
singular integral operator
if there exists a function
k\in L^{1}_{loc}(\dot{\mathbb{R}};{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
such that for all
f\in L^{\infty}_{c}(X)
and all
t\notin\operatorname{supp}f

Tf(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k(s)f(t-s)\mathrm{d}s.

Here,
L^{\infty}_{c}(X)
stands for the space of all
X
-valued, essentially bounded, measurable functions with compact support in
\mathbb{R}
.
If, additionally,
k\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\dot{\mathbb{R}};{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
and

(7)

[k]_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}:=\max_{l=0,1}\sup_{t\neq 0}\|t^{l+1}k^{(l)}(t)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)}<\infty,

then we say that
T
is a Calderón-Zygmund operator.
The boundedness of Calderón-Zygmund operators on different types of vector-valued Banach function spaces attracted attention in the literature. Here, we mention two pioneering papers
[
9
]
, and
[
40
]
, from which some ideas we reproduce below.

For instance, by direct analysis of the proof of
[
40
, Theorem 1.6, Part I]
each Calderón-Zygmund operator satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
(ii) (it is also true for a larger class of singular integral operators; see
[
15
, Theorem 7]
). The following result makes this statement precise.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces.
Let
\{T_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be a family of Calderón-Zygmund operators with kernels
k_{j}
,
j\in J
, such that

(8)

\sup_{j\in J}\|T_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}(X),L^{p}(Y))}<\infty\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sup_{j\in J}[k_{j}]_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}<\infty.

Then,
\{T_{j}\}_{j\in J}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
(ii)
, that is,
for every
q\in(1,\infty)
and
for every
{\mathcal{W}}\subset A_{q}
with
\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}[w]_{A_{q}}<\infty

\sup_{j\in J,w\in{\mathcal{W}}}\|T_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{q}_{w}(X),L^{q}_{w}(Y)}<\infty.

Consequently, the conclusion of Theorem
3.1
(ii)
holds for
\{T_{j}\}_{j\in J}
.

In particular, if
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a resolution of the identity on
\mathbb{R}
and
\Phi
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
3.1
, then the operators
\psi_{j}(D)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, restrict to uniformly bounded operators in
{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi(X))
.

Proof.

By direct analysis of the constants involved in the main ingredients of the proof of
[
40
, Theorem 1.6, Part I]
, the norms
\|T\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{q}_{w}(X),L^{q}_{w}(Y))}
of each Calderón-Zygmund operator
T
, depend on
q\in(1,\infty)
, the constant from the
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
-condition of its kernel, and the norm of
T
as an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}(X),L^{p}(Y))
, and finally they are bounded from above if
w
varies in a subset of
A_{q}
on which
[w]_{A_{q}}
are uniformly bounded. Cf. also
[
25
]
, or
[
32
]
, for the precise dependence of
L^{q}_{w}
-norms of Calderón-Zygmund operators on the
A_{q}
-constants
[w]_{A_{q}}
of Muckenhoupt’s weights
w\in A_{q}
.

For the last statement, since
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\psi_{j}(t)=2^{-j}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\psi(2^{-j}t)-2^{-j+1}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\psi(2^{-j+1}t)

(t\in\mathbb{R},j\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
, it is straightforward to see that
\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}[{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\psi_{j}]_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}<\infty
and
\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\|{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\psi_{j}\|_{L^{1}}<\infty
.
Therefore, by Young’s inequality, we get (
8
) for
\psi_{j}(D)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Alternatively and more directly, this statement can be also derived by the argument presented in the proof of Proposition
4.2
(i) below.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

(a)
In the scalar case, i.e.
X=Y=\mathbb{C}
, the boundedness of Calderón-Zygmund operators on Banach function spaces
\Phi
such that
M
is bounded on
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
was already stated in
[
41
, Proposition 6]
. The proof given in
[
41
]
is based on a formal application of Coifman’s inequality. Theorem
3.1
allows us to provide an alternative, direct proof of this fact.

(b)
The approach via Strömberg’s sharp maximal operator.

A conceptually different approach to the study of the boundedness of Calderón-Zygmund operators was presented by Jawerth and Torchinsky
[
26
]
. It relies on the local sharp maximal function operator (
\alpha\in(0,1))
:

M^{\sharp}_{\alpha}f(t):=\sup_{Q\ni t}\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\inf\bigl\{\beta\geq 0:|\{s\in Q:|f(s)-z|>\beta\}|<\alpha|Q|\bigr\}

for every
f\in L^{1}_{loc}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

It is shown in
[
26
, Theorem 4.6]
(in the scalar case, but the vector one follows easily) that, in particular, if
T
is a Calderón-Zygmund operator, then there exist constants
\alpha
and
\mu
such that

(9)

M^{\sharp}_{\alpha}(|Tf|_{Y})\leq\mu M(|f|_{X})

for all
appropriate

f\in L^{1}_{loc}(X)
. Here, the word
’appropriate’
plays a crucial role to apply this inequality for a further study of the boundedness of
T
on some function spaces. By an analysis of the proof, we see that a priori one can consider the set of all
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(X)
such that
Tf\in L^{0}(X)
with
(|Tf|_{Y})^{*}(+\infty)=0
and for every interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,
Tf^{I}\in L^{1}(I)
with
f^{I}:=f\chi_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 2I}
, and

(Tf^{I})(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 2I}k(t-s)f(s)\mathrm{d}s\,\,\textrm{ for a.e. }t\in I.

Here, for a function
g\in L^{0}
,
g^{*}(+\infty)=0
if and only if
|\{t\in\mathbb{R}:|g(t)|>\alpha\}|<\infty
for all
\alpha>0
.
Denote the above set by
D_{T}
and set
D_{T,\Phi}:=D_{T}\cap\Phi(X)
for a Banach function space
\Phi\subset L^{1}_{loc}
. Of course we have that
L^{1}_{c}(X)\subset D(T)
.

Moreover, recall Lerner’s characterization of boundedness of
M
on a
\Phi^{\prime}
. Namely, in
[
31
, Corollary 4.2 and Lemma 3.2]
Lerner proved that if the maximal operator
M
is bounded on a Banach function space
\Phi
, then
M
is bounded on
\Phi^{\prime}
if and only if
there exists a constant
\mu
such that for every
f\in L^{0}
with
f^{*}(+\infty)=0

\|Mf\|_{\Phi}\leq\mu\|M^{\sharp}f\|_{\Phi}.

Note that by the Jawerth-Torchinsky pointwise estimate (
9
) and Lerner’s characterisation, we get for any Banach function space
\Phi
such that
M
is bounded on
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
that for every
f\in D_{T,\Phi}

(10)

\|Tf\|_{\Phi(Y)}\leq\|M(|Tf|_{Y})\|_{\Phi}\lesssim\|M^{\sharp}_{\alpha}(|Tf|_{Y})\|_{\Phi}\lesssim\|M(|f|_{X})\|_{\Phi}\lesssim\|f\|_{\Phi(X)}.

But it is not clear how to show directly (without the use of the Rubio de Francia algorithm) that
D_{T,\Phi}
is dense in
\Phi
.

Again, Theorem
3.1
(i) solves this density issue (and, in particular, relaxes a density assumption in
[
41
, Proposition 9]
).

In fact, let
f\in\Phi(X)
and set
f_{N}=f\chi_{[-N,N]}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Theorem
3.1
,
f\in L^{2}_{w}(X)
and
T
in
{\mathcal{L}}(L^{2}_{w}(X),L^{2}_{w}(Y))
for some weight
w\in A_{2}
. Then, since
f_{N}\rightarrow f
in
L^{2}_{w}(X)
, we have for some sequence
(N_{l})_{l}\subset\mathbb{N}
that
Tf_{N_{l}}(t)\rightarrow Tf(t)
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
f_{N_{l}}\in\Phi\cap L^{1}_{c}(X)
, so by (
10
),

\|Tf_{N_{l}}\|_{\Phi(Y)}\lesssim\|f_{N_{l}}\|_{\Phi(X)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\Phi(X)}.

Therefore, the Fatou property of
\Phi
implies that
Tf\in\Phi(Y)
and
\|Tf\|_{\Phi(Y)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\Phi(X)}
.

3.3.
The basic properties of generalised Besov and Triebel-Lizorkin spaces

We start with an auxiliary lemma on dense subsets of
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
and
F^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and let
\Phi
be a Banach function space over
(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}t)
such that the Hardy-Littlewood operator
M
is bounded on
\Phi
and its dual
\Phi^{\prime}
.

Then, for every

\mathbb{E}\in\left\{B^{s,q}_{\Phi},\,F^{s,r}_{\Phi}:s\in\mathbb{R},q\in[1,\infty],r\in(1,\infty)\right\}

(11)

{\mathcal{S}}(X)\subset\mathbb{E}(X)\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X),

that is,
\mathbb{E}(X)
embeds continuously into
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
. In particular,
\mathbb{E}(X)
is a Banach space.

Furthermore, the set
\Phi(X)\cap\mathbb{E}(X)
is dense in
\mathbb{E}(X)
for each
\mathbb{E}
with
q<\infty
, and in the other cases when
\mathbb{E}=B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}

(s\in\mathbb{R})
,
\Phi(X)\cap B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X)
is
\sigma(B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X),B^{-s,1}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(X^{*}))
-dense in
B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X)
. More precisely, for every
f\in\mathbb{E}(X)
we have that

\sum_{0\leq j\leq N}\psi_{j}(D)f=\psi(2^{-N}D)f\rightarrow f\quad\textrm{ as }N\rightarrow\infty

in
\mathbb{E}(X)
for each
\mathbb{E}
with
q<\infty
, and for
\mathbb{E}=B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, this convergence holds in
\sigma(B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X),B^{-s,1}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(X^{*}))
-topology.

Note that the following duality pairing for
f\in B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X)
and
g\in B^{-s,1}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(X^{*})

(s\in\mathbb{R})

(12)

\left\langle g,f\right\rangle:=\sum_{j,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\psi_{l}(D)g(t),\psi_{j}(D)f(t)\right\rangle_{X^{*},X}\mathrm{d}t,

yields a natural embedding of
B^{-s,1}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(X^{*})
into
(B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X))^{*}
.

As usual, the
\sigma(B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X),B^{-s,1}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(X^{*}))
-topology is the weakest topology on
B^{s,\infty}_{\Phi}(X)
for which every
g\in B^{-s,1}_{\Phi^{\prime}}(X^{*})
becomes continuous; see also Remark
3.7
below.

Proof.

Let
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a resolution of the identity on
\mathbb{R}
. By Corollary
3.4
,
\psi_{j}(D)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, restrict to uniformly bounded operators in
{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi(X))
and in
{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi^{\prime})
.
Set
\chi_{j}:=\psi_{j-1}+\psi_{j}+\psi_{j+1}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
\psi_{-1}\equiv 0
.
Since for every measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
with
|A|>0
there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that
M\chi_{A}\geq\frac{\delta}{\max(1,t)}

(t\in\mathbb{R})
, by the ideal property of
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
and our assumption on the boundedness of
M
we get that
{\mathcal{S}}\subset\Phi\cap\Phi^{\prime}
.

The case
\mathbb{E}=B^{s,q}_{\Phi}
.
Fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\in[1,\infty)
.
Let
(f_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
with
\|f_{l}\|_{B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)}\rightarrow 0
as
l\rightarrow\infty
. Since
\{\psi_{j}(D)\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a resolution of the identity on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
(see (
3
)) and
\Phi(X)\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{r}(X)
, by Hölder’s inequality, for every
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}
we get

\displaystyle|f_{l}(\phi)|_{X}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\psi_{j}(D)f_{l}(\phi)\right|_{X}=\left|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\psi_{j}(D)f_{l}[{\mathcal{F}}(\chi_{j}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\phi)]\right|_{X}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{j}(D)f_{l}(t)(\chi_{j}(D)\breve{\phi})(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\psi_{j}(D)f_{l}(t)\right|_{X}|(\chi_{j}(D)\breve{\phi})(t)|\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left\|2^{js}\psi_{j}(D)f_{l}\right\|_{\Phi(X)}\|2^{-js}\chi_{j}(D)\breve{\phi}\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left\|2^{jsq}\psi_{j}(D)f_{l}\right\|^{q}_{\Phi(X)}\right)^{1/q}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\|2^{-js}\chi_{j}(D)\breve{\phi}\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}^{q^{\prime}}\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{l}\|_{B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\|2^{-js}\chi_{j}(D)\check{\phi}\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}^{q^{\prime}}\right)^{1/q^{\prime}}

Here, we set
\breve{\phi}
for
\phi(-\cdot)
.
Note that in the case when
s>0
the proof is complete. For
s\leq 0
, let
\alpha>|s|+1
and
\rho(t):=(1+t^{2})^{-\alpha}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
It is easy to check that
2^{(|s|+1)j}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\rho\chi_{j}),{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},
uniformly satisfy the
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
-condition (see (
7
)) and their
L^{1}
-norms are uniformly bounded too. By Proposition
3.4
,
(2^{(|s|+1)j}\chi_{j}\rho)(D)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, restrict to uniformly bounded operators in
{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi^{\prime})
.

Therefore, since
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\rho^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\check{\phi})\in{\mathcal{S}}\subset\Phi^{\prime}
, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we get that

\displaystyle\|2^{j|s|}\chi_{j}(D)\phi\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-j}\|(2^{(|s|+1)j}\rho\chi_{j})(D)[{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\rho^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\check{\phi})]\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-j}\|(2^{(|s|+1)j}\rho\chi_{j})(D)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi^{\prime})}\|[{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\rho^{-1}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\check{\phi})]\|_{\Phi^{\prime}}.

It completes the proof in this case.

4.
Multipliers and the regularity of their kernels

In this section, we specify Theorem
3.8
for a class of Fourier multipliers in which we are typically interested in the following sections.

4.1.
The Fourier multipliers

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces.
For
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
we set

{D_{a}}:=\{f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X):{\mathcal{F}}f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{r}(X)\textrm{ and }a(\cdot){\mathcal{F}}f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y)\}

to denote the
initial
domain of a Fourier multiplier
a(D)
associated with
a
which is defined by

a(D)f:={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(a(\cdot){\mathcal{F}}f)\quad(f\in D_{a}).

If
{\mathcal{G}}(X)
is a subspace of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
(equipped with the relative topology of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
) and

has a unique extension to an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{G}}(X),{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y))
, then we denote such extension again by
a(D)
. Since
X\otimes{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{\mathcal{D}}(\dot{\mathbb{R}})
is a dense subset of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
, if
X\otimes{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathcal{G}}(X)
, then such extension of
a(D)
is unique.

In particular, as was already mentioned in Section 1, if
a\in{\mathcal{O}}_{M}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
,
then
a(D)\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X),{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y))
and

(15)

a(D)f={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(\Theta(a,{\mathcal{F}}f))\qquad f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X),

where
\Theta
denotes a hypocontinuous, bilinear map from
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))\times{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
into
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y)
(see
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
).
The multipliers symbols, which are involved in the study of maximal regularity property of evolution equations usually are of
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
-class; see, e.g. Proposition
6.1
. For instance, it is the case of the problem (
1
) discussed in Section
1
, where the
solution
operator
U
is given by a multiplier
a(D)
with
a=(i\cdot+A)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{M}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
and
Y:=D_{A}
.

Further, by the convolution theorem (see e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 3.6]
), for all

a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
and
f\in{\mathcal{S}}(X)
,

(16)

a(D)f={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a\ast f.

Here,
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(Y,X))
, that is,
\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a,\phi\right\rangle:=\left\langle a,{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\phi\right\rangle
,
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}
. For more regular symbols
a
such convolution representation of the corresponding multiplier
a(D)
allows us to use the basic techniques of harmonic analysis to study of boundedness properties/invariant subspaces of
a(D)
.
However, in general, such representation makes sense
only for
f
in a proper subset of a domain of
a(D)
. For instance, even in the case

a
in the
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
-class, in general, what one can say is that (

) holds for

, where
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(X)
denotes the space of distributions with compact support.

Depending on support conditions or integrability conditions of
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a
this expression can be extended for a larger class of functions; see e.g.
[
2
, Remark 3.2 and Theorem 3.5]
.
It leads to a question on the consistency of
a(D)
with its bounded extensions resulting from such convolution representation.
We address this question for a particular class of symbols below.

4.2.
The integral representation of
a(D)
.

For symbols
a
, which arise in the study of abstract evolution equations, usually
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a
is a tempered distribution which is a regular one only on
\dot{\mathbb{R}}
.
That is, there exists a function

such that for every
\phi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\dot{\mathbb{R}};\mathbb{C})

\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}m,\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle k,\phi\right\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k(t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t.

Let

Cf(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k(r)f(t-r)\mathrm{d}r

for every
f\in L^{1}_{c}(X)
and a.e.
t\notin\operatorname{supp}f
.
Here,
L^{1}_{c}(X)
stands for the space of all
X
-valued, measurable, Bochner integrable functions with compact support in
\mathbb{R}
.
By Young’s inequality, this integral is absolutely convergent for a.e.
t\notin\operatorname{supp}f
.

Note that, even in the case

, without any further information on

we only get that

a(D)f(t)=Cf(t)

for

and a.e.
t\notin\operatorname{supp}f
(a priori we know that

is only continuous on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
; cf. also
[
23
, Example 2.11]
). If we know that, e.g.

restricts to an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}(X),L^{p}(Y))
for some
p\in(1,\infty)
, then of course such representation holds for
f\in L^{1}_{c}(X)
.

In the case when, e.g.

is not dense in a Banach space
\mathbb{E}(X)
under consideration, if there exists an operator

in
{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{E}(X),\mathbb{E}(Y))
such that

for every

and a.e.

, we do not have any

argument to show that

is consistent with

on
\mathbb{E}(X)

is a restriction of
a(D)
to
\mathbb{E}(X)
. The Rubio de Francia iteration algorithm, Theorem

, makes it all rigorous.

The following result is a reformulation of
[

, Proposition 4.4.2, p.245]
.
It shows how Mihlin type estimates of a symbol

are reflected by its distributional kernel
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
. Recall that
\dot{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

and

then
{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}a=k
in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
for a function

is independent of

The proof of this lemma follows the idea of the proof of its scalar counterpart given in Stein
[

and is omitted here.

In the sequel, we say that

-condition and write

Furthermore, let

and

Consequently, by (

The boundedness properties of such operators are the subject of the rest of this section.

4.3.

Let

5.
Maximal regularity of integro-differential equations

In this section, we illustrate our preceding general results by an abstract integro-differential equation with a general convolution term; see (
20
) below.
We discuss the particular forms of this equation in the following section.

We start with a preliminary observation. Let
A
,
B
and
P
denote densely defined, closed, linear operators on a Banach space
X
.
Set
D_{A}
,
D_{B}
and
D_{P}
for the domains of these operators equipped with the corresponding graph norms. Since
A\in{\mathcal{L}}(D_{A},X)
, the
evaluation
of
A
on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{A})
gives an operator
{\mathcal{A}}
in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{A}),{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X))
, i.e.,
({\mathcal{A}}u)(\phi):=A(u(\phi))
for every
u\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{A})
and
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}
. The symbols
{\mathcal{B}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}
have the analogous meaning below. Note that the distributional derivative
\partial
commutes with such extensions, i.e.
\partial{\mathcal{A}}u={\mathcal{A}}\partial u
for all
u\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{A})
, and similarly for
{\mathcal{B}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Moreover, let
c\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(Z,X))
, where
Z\subset X
denotes a Banach space.
Consider the following problem

(20)

\partial{\mathcal{P}}\partial u+{\mathcal{B}}\partial u+{\mathcal{A}}u+c\ast u=f\quad\quad\textrm{ in }{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X).

For a given
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
, by a
distributional solution
of (
20
) we mean a distribution
u\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{A})
such that
\partial u\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{B})\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(D_{P})
and the convolution of
c
with
u
can be (at least formally) well-defined in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
.
Below we assume that
c
is the inverse Fourier transform of a given function
\hat{c}
in
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(Z,X))
, i.e.
c={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\hat{c}
, and define
c\ast u
by
\hat{c}(D)u
.

Moreover, we restrict our study of (
20
) to the case when
D_{A}\hookrightarrow D_{B},D_{P}
and
Z=D_{A}
. The more general case of the main results of this section, when
Z\neq D_{A}
, can be proved easily following the presented proofs. Let
Y:=D_{A}
. Since
A,B,P\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y,X)
, we get that
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{P}}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y),{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X))
. Since
\partial{\mathcal{P}}\partial u=\partial(\partial{\mathcal{P}}u)
and
\partial{\mathcal{B}}u={\mathcal{B}}\partial u
for all
u\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(Y)
, the solvability of (
20
) in the sense of distributional solutions is equivalent to that of

(21)

\partial(\partial{\mathcal{P}}u)+\partial{\mathcal{B}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u+c\ast u=f\quad\quad\textrm{ in }{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X).

Of course, it is not the case for the study of the well-posedness of (
20
) and (
21
) in the sense of strong solutions. It is emphasized in the proof of Theorem
5.4
; see also Lemma
5.2
below. We call a function
u
the
strong solution
of (
20
) if

u\in L^{1}_{loc}(D_{A}),\quad c\ast u\in L^{1}_{loc}(X),\quad u\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(D_{B}\cap D_{P}),\quad Pu^{\prime}\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(X)

and

(22)

(Pu^{\prime})^{\prime}(t)+Bu^{\prime}(t)+Au(t)+(c\ast u)(t)=f(t)\quad\quad\textrm{ a.e. }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Equivalently,
u
is a strong solution if
u\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(X)
and each summand on the left side of (
20

Proposition 5.1
.

Let

and

be as above.
Assume that for every

the operator

is invertible. Set

Suppose that

and

for

.

Then, for every
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\hat{c}\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\gamma}({\mathcal{L}}(Y,X))
(respectively,
\hat{c}\in{\mathfrak{M}}_{\gamma}({\mathcal{L}}(Y,X))

and

)(respectively,

and
a_{0},a_{1}\in{\mathfrak{M}}_{\gamma}({\mathcal{L}}(X))

In addition, if the ranges of the functions

and

satisfies the

-condition, then

and
a_{0}
,

Proof.

Note that
b\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\gamma}(\dot{\mathbb{R}};{\mathcal{L}}(Y,X))
and, since the inversion map is analytic,
a\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\gamma}(\dot{\mathbb{R}};{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
. Moreover,

has a continuous extension at

if
\hat{c}
does.
Before we proceed by induction, we need to consider the cases when
\gamma=1,2,3
separately.

In the case when

, since

, by Leibniz’ rule, for every

we get

-a^{\prime}(t)=a(t)b^{\prime}(t)a(t)\qquad(\textrm{in }{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)),

where

By our assumptions we get
b^{\prime}a,(\cdot)b^{\prime}a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
, which shows that
a\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
.

Similarly, when
\gamma=2
, we get

(26)

-a^{\prime\prime}(t)=a^{\prime}(t)b^{\prime}(t)a(t)+a(t)b^{\prime\prime}(t)a(t)+a(t)b^{\prime}(t)a^{\prime}(t),

where

b^{\prime\prime}(t)=-2P+\hat{c}^{\prime\prime}(t).

Therefore, our assumptions yield directly that
b^{\prime\prime}a,(\cdot)^{2}b^{\prime\prime}a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
and, by the step for
\gamma=1
,
b^{\prime}a^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
. Consequently,

a\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
.

For
\gamma=3
, note that

(27)

\displaystyle-a^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle a^{\prime\prime}(t)b^{\prime}(t)a(t)+a^{\prime}(t)b^{\prime\prime}(t)a(t)+a^{\prime}(t)b^{\prime}(t)a^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle+a^{\prime}(t)b^{\prime\prime}(t)a(t)+a(t)b^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)a(t)+a(t)b^{\prime\prime}(t)a^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle+a^{\prime}(t)b^{\prime}(t)a^{\prime}(t)+a(t)b^{\prime\prime}(t)a(t)+a(t)b^{\prime}(t)a^{\prime\prime}(t),

where

b^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)=\hat{c}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t).

Therefore, the steps for
\gamma=1
and
\gamma=2
show that
a\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{3}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
.

Now we can proceed by induction for
\gamma>3
. Fix
3\leq\alpha\leq\gamma
. Then

a^{(\alpha)}=\sum_{0\leq k,l,m\leq\alpha,\,l\neq 0\atop k+l+m=\alpha}a^{(k)}b^{(l)}a^{(m)}\qquad(\textrm{on }\dot{\mathbb{R}}).

By the induction step, for every
k=0,...,\alpha-1
, the function
(\cdot)^{k}a^{(k)}
is in
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
. Moreover, in the case when
k\neq 0
, since
l+m\leq\alpha-1
, by the induction, we have that the functions
(\cdot)^{l+m}b^{(l)}a^{(m)}\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
.
Therefore, consider the case of
k=0
, that is,
l+m=\alpha
. If
l=\alpha
, then

b^{(\alpha)}=\hat{c}^{(\alpha)}
, thus
(\cdot)^{\alpha}b^{(\alpha)}a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
.
In the case

, i.e.,

, we have

b^{(l)}a^{(m)}=\sum_{0\leq m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\leq m,\,m_{2}\neq 0\atop m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=m}b^{(l)}a^{(m_{1})}b^{(m_{2})}a^{(m_{3})}\qquad(\textrm{on }\dot{\mathbb{R}}).

Since
l+m_{1}\leq l+m-m_{2}\leq\alpha-1
and
m_{2}+m_{3}\leq m\leq\alpha-1
, by the induction step, we get
(\cdot)^{l+m}b^{(l)}a^{(m)}\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
. It shows that
a\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
.
Applying the formulas obtained for the function
a
, one can easily get desired statement for
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
. We omit details.

The proof in the case of the
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\gamma}
-condition follows the one given above.

For the last statement, recall that directly from the definition of
R
-boundedness, for any Banach spaces
X,Y,Z
, if
\tau,\sigma\subset{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)
and
\rho\subset{\mathcal{L}}(Y,Z)
are
R
-bounded, then the families

and

are
R
-bounded (see e.g.
[
30

). Therefore,
the proof of the last statement mimics exactly the argument given already for the usual, norm boundedness.

The following lemmas allow to derive the strong solutions of the problem (

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces such that
Y\hookrightarrow X
. Let
C
be a closed, linear operator on
X
such that
Y\hookrightarrow D_{C}
.

Let
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(Y)
be such that
\partial u
and
\partial Cu
are regular
X
-valued distribution, i.e.
\partial u,\partial Cu\in L^{1}_{loc}(X)
.

Then, for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}
the strong derivative
u^{\prime}(t)
of
u
at
t
exists in
X
,
u^{\prime}(t)\in D_{C}
and
Cu^{\prime}(t)=(\partial Cu)(t)
.

The proof of this lemma follows easily from the closedness of
C
, which yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}^{X}(\partial Cu)(t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=(\partial Cu)(\phi)=({\mathcal{C}}\partial u)(\phi)=-C\left(\int^{Y}_{\mathbb{R}}u(t)\phi^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)=

\displaystyle=C\left(\int^{X}_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}(t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)\quad\textrm{for all }\phi\in{\mathcal{S}},

and Lebesgue’s differential theorem. Here, we use the symbol
\int^{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
to indicate in which space the integration over
\mathbb{R}
is considered. Note that
\int^{X}_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}(t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t\in Y
for all
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}
.

The following result is a counterpart of the characterisation of the classical Besov spaces (
X=\mathbb{C}
,
\Phi=L^{p}
) in terms of distributional derivatives; see
[
43
, Theorem 2.3.8]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
q\in[1,\infty]
and let
\Phi
denote a Banach function space such that the Hardy-Littlewood operator
M
is bounded on
\Phi
and its dual
\Phi^{\prime}
.
Then, for every distribution
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
,
f
belongs to
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
if and only if
f
and
\partial f
belong to
B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
. Moreover, the function

(28)

B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)\ni f\mapsto\left\|f\right\|_{B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)}+\left\|\partial^{\alpha}f\right\|_{B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)}

is an equivalent norm on
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.

The proof of Lemma
5.3
reproduces the ideas of the proof of the classical case when
X=\mathbb{C}
and
\Phi=L^{p}
from
[
43
]
. Since the proof, even in the classical case, is somewhat complex and based on several ingredients in which formulation the characteristics
p
and
q
are involved, we sketch the line of this extension and underline the main supplementary observation should be made.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

First, we show that the lifting operator
{\mathcal{J}}
given by

{\mathcal{J}}f={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(1+(\cdot)^{2})^{1/2}{\mathcal{F}}f\quad(f\in{\mathcal{S}}(X))

maps
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
isomorphically onto
B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.

Let
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in N_{0}}
be the resolution of the identity on
\mathbb{R}
as in Section
2
. Set

\phi_{j}(t):=2^{-j}(1+t^{2})^{1/2}\psi_{j}(t)\quad(j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}).

In order to show that
{\mathcal{J}}f\in B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
for every
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
it is sufficient to show that there exists a constant
\mu
such that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\left\|2^{(s-1)j}\psi_{j}(D){\mathcal{J}}f\right\|_{\Phi}(X)\leq\mu\left\|2^{sj}\psi_{j}(D)f\right\|_{\Phi(X)}.

For note that for every
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle|2^{(s-1)j}\psi_{j}(D){\mathcal{J}}f(t)|_{X}

\displaystyle=|2^{js}\phi_{j}(D)f(t)|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{js}\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\phi_{j}(D)f(t-r)|_{X}}{1+|2^{j}r|}=:2^{sj}\phi^{\ast}_{j}f(t).

We show that there exists a constant
\mu
such that for every
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
and every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have that

(29)

\|\phi^{\ast}_{j}f\|_{\Phi}\leq\mu\|\phi_{j}(D)f\|_{\Phi(X)}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\|\phi_{j}(D)f\|_{\Phi(X)}\leq\mu\|\psi_{j}(D)f\|_{\Phi(X)}.

The second inequality above follows from Proposition
4.2
since

\phi_{j}(D)f={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}2^{-j}(1+(\cdot)^{2})^{1/2}\chi_{j}{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\psi_{j}{\mathcal{F}}f

and, as it is readily seen, the functions
\rho_{j}:\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto 2^{-j}(1+t^{2})^{1/2}\chi_{j}(t)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, satisfy the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{3}
-condition uniformly in
j
. Therefore,

\rho_{j}(D)
,
j\in N_{0}
, restrict to uniformly bounded operators in
{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X))
.

To get the first inequality in (
29
), by the same argument as in the proof of
[
43
, Theorem 1.3.1]
, we obtain the existence of a constant
\mu
such that for every
g\in{\mathcal{S}}(X)
and
j\in N_{0}

\phi^{\ast}_{j}g(t)\leq\mu M\left(|\phi_{j}(D)g|_{X}\right)(t).

Now by an approximation argument similar to that given in the proof of
[
43
, Theorem 1.4.1]
one can show that for every Muckenhoupt weight
w\in A_{2}
for every
f\in L^{2}_{w}(X)
we have that

\|\phi^{\ast}_{j}f\|_{L^{2}_{w}}\leq\mu\|M\|_{L^{2}_{w}}\|\phi_{j}(D)f\|_{L^{2}_{w}(X)}.

Since
\|M\|_{L^{2}_{w}}\lesssim[w]^{2}_{A_{2}}
(see
[
11
, Theorem 2.5]
), by an argument similar to that from the proof of Lemma
3.6
(the case
\mathbb{E}=F^{s,q}_{\Phi}
), we get the first inequality in (
29
).
Therefore,
{\mathcal{J}}
maps
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
into
B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.
Note that
{\mathcal{J}}
is an isomorphism onto
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)
, and by the same argument to that above one can show that
{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}f={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}(1+(\cdot)^{2})^{-1/2}{\mathcal{F}}f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
for each
f\in B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
. Therefore, the lifting property of
{\mathcal{J}}
is proved.

Now fix
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
. We show that
f,\partial f\in B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.
Obviously,
f\in B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
. For
\partial f
, note first that the function
\rho(t):=t(1+t^{2})^{-1/2}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfies the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{3}
-condition and is of
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
-class. Thus, by Proposition
4.2
,
\rho(D)
restricts to an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X))
, and

(30)

\|\partial f\|_{B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)}=\|\rho(D){\mathcal{J}}f\|_{B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)}\leq\|\rho(D){\mathcal{J}}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X))}\|f\|_{{B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)}}

Finally, let
\eta(t):=((1+t^{2})^{1/2}-1)t^{-1}
. It is readily seen that
\eta
satisfies the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{3}
-condition. Therefore,
\eta(D)
restricts to an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X))
and for every
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X)

{\mathcal{J}}f=f+\eta(D)\partial f.

Since
{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}
maps isomorphically
{B}^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
onto
{B}^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
, if
f\in B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
with
\partial f\in B^{s-1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
, then
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
. It completes the proof.

One can easily check that our additional assumption that the function
(\cdot)a(\cdot)
is in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
yields
u\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(X)
. It completes the proof of
(i)
.

(ii)
Since the inversion map is analytic,
a\in{\mathcal{C}}^{3}(\dot{\mathbb{R}};{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
, where

for

.
Note that for every

satisfies (

). Therefore, the operator

has an extension to an operator
T
in
{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}(X),L^{p}(Y))
, which assigns to each

the corresponding solution

of (
20
). A standard argument shows that
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
. Similarly, we get that the functions
a_{0}
and

given by (

) belong to

. Consequently, by Proposition

,

\mathfrak{M}_{3}
-condition provided that
\hat{c}
does.
Now, Proposition
4.2
(ii) shows that the multipliers
a(D)
,
a_{0}(D)
,
a_{1}(D)
,
\hat{c}(D)
have extensions to operators
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E}}:={\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},a}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{E}(X),\mathbb{E}(Y))
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},a_{0}},{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},a_{1}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{E}(X))
, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},\hat{c}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{E}(Y),\mathbb{E}(X))

{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},a_{0}}={\mathcal{B}}\partial{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E}},\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},a_{1}}=\partial{\mathcal{P}}\partial{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E}},\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},\hat{c}a}={\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},\hat{c}}{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E}}.

By similar arguments to those presented in the proof of the part
(i)

\Phi
-maximal regularity. If
\mathbb{E}\neq\Phi

with desired properties, i.e.
\partial{\mathcal{P}}\partial u,\,{\mathcal{B}}\partial u,\,{\mathcal{A}}u,\,c\ast u\in\mathbb{E}(X)
.

For the second statement, if
(\cdot)a(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
, then by Propositions

and

, one can argue that

{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E},(\cdot)a(\cdot)}f=\partial{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E}}f\in\mathbb{E}(X)
for every

.
The closedness of

and

{\mathcal{B}}\partial{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathbb{E}},\,{\mathcal{P}}\partial{\mathcal{T}}_{E}\in\mathbb{E}(X).

If, additionally,

is a strong solution of (

∎

6.
Particular forms of the equation (
20
)

We do not attempt to give a systematic survey on particular cases of (
20
) and address only some issues directly related to the assumptions of Theorem
5.4
. As in the previous section, we assume that
A
,
B
, and
P
are closed linear operators on a Banach space
X
such that
D_{A}\hookrightarrow D_{B},D_{P}
.

6.1.
The evolutionary differential equations

Consider the equation (
20
) with
c=0
, i.e.

(31)

\partial{\mathcal{P}}\partial u+{\mathcal{B}}\partial u+{\mathcal{A}}u=f\quad\textrm{ in }{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(X).

Such abstract evolution equation reduces to further ones, which have been intensively studied in the literature. We mention here a few of them.

When
P=0
and
B=I
, (
31
) takes the form of the problem (
1
) already considered in Section 1. Its variant on the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
, i.e. the following first-order Cauchy problem

(32)

u^{\prime}+Au=f\textrm{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\quad\textrm{ with }u(0)=0

is a model one for the study of the
L^{p}
-maximal regularity of (
31
).
We refer the reader to
[
30
]
or
[
19
]
for the background on the
L^{p}
-maximal regularity the abstract Cauchy problem (
32
).
In this context, we point out that the kernels of Fourier multipliers involved in the study of maximal regularity have supports in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which allows considering a larger class of Banach function spaces
\Phi
over
(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}t)
, and consequently proving the existence of strong solutions of (
32
) for a larger class of functions
f
; see
[
15
, Section 5]
and the references therein.

The problem (
1
) on the line was considered by Mielke
[
36
]
, who characterized its
L^{p}
-maximal regularity in the class of closed, linear operators
A
on Hilbert spaces
X
.
His result was later extended for
X
with
U\!M\!D
property by Arendt and Dueli
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
. It provides a direct counterpart of Weis’ characterisation of the
L^{p}
-maximal regularity for the Cauchy problem (
32
); see
[
44
]
.
More recently, a similar strategy was adapted, e.g. in
[
4
]
(cf. also
[
37
]
), to give a counterpart of Weis’ result for (
20
) with a special form of
c
. Note that the main point is to give a variant of Mielke’s argument from
[
36
, Lemma 2.3]
for (
20
) (cf. also a different argument given by Dore
[
20
]
for (
32
)). More precisely, one need to argue that if (
20
) has
L^{p}
-maximal regularity then there exists
\alpha>0
such that the problem

(33)

(Pv^{\prime})^{\prime}+Bv^{\prime}+Av=g+\alpha Pv-\alpha\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)Bv-e^{-\alpha|\cdot|}c\ast(e^{\alpha|\cdot|}v)

is well-posed in
L^{p}(X)
, i.e. for all
g\in L^{p}(X)
there exists a strong solution
v
in
L^{p}(Y)
. Note that the well-posedness of (
34
) in
L^{p}(X)
is equivalent to that of (
20
) in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R},e^{-\alpha|\cdot|}\mathrm{d}t;X)
, which further yields to the invertibility of the operator
b(t)
in (
23
) for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
{\mathcal{T}}
denotes the solution operator for (
20
), which exists by the assumed
L^{p}
-maximal regularity of (
20
), then (
33
) yields

(34)

v-{\mathcal{T}}\left(\alpha[P-\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)B]v+e^{-\alpha|\cdot|}c\ast(e^{\alpha|\cdot|}v)-c\ast v\right)={\mathcal{T}}g.

Therefore, to show that that the linear operator on the left side of (
34
) is invertible on
L^{p}(X)
for some
\alpha>0
, and make all considerations rigorous, one need to make some further restriction on the convolutor
c
(cf.
[
4
]
and
[
37
]
). Below we chose the simplest case, that is, when
c=0
. Then,
\|\alpha{\mathcal{T}}(P-\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)B)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(L^{p}(X))}<1
for all
\alpha
small enough and
v=(I-\alpha{\mathcal{T}}(P-\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)B))^{-1}{\mathcal{T}}g
, i.e. (
33
) is well-posed in
L^{p}(X)
.

The following result shows that the assumption made in Theorem
5.4
(ii) on the invertibility of
b(s)
with
c=0
is a consequence of the
L^{p}
-maximal regularity of (
31
).

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A,B
and
P
be closed, linear operator on a Banach space
X
with
U\!M\!D
property such that
D_{A}\hookrightarrow D_{B},D_{P}
.

If (
31
) has
L^{p}
-maximal regularity, then
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
the operator

b(t):=-t^{2}P+itB+A\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y,X)

is invertible.

Furthermore, the functions
a,a_{0},a_{1}
(given by (
24
)) are bounded, belong to the
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
-class, and satisfy the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\gamma}
-condition for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the conclusion of Theorem
5.4
(ii) holds for (

The first statement is a special case of
[
4
, Proposition 3.11]
.
The second one follows from standard argument and Proposition
5.1
.

6.2.
The special form of the convolution term

For an potential application of Theorem
5.4
, it is natural to specify the form of the convolution term in (
20
) as follows:

(35)

c=\partial c_{0}+c_{1}\quad\textrm{ with }c_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(Y_{j},X))

and further one can assume that

c_{i}=\delta\otimes C_{0,i}+c_{1,i}\textrm{ with }C_{i,0}\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y_{i},X)\textrm{ and }c_{i,1}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathcal{L}}(Y_{i},X)).

Then,

c\ast u=C_{0,0}\partial u+c_{0,1}\ast u+C_{1,0}u+c_{1,1}\ast u.

When we further take
C_{0,0}:=\alpha_{0}I
,
C_{1,0}:=\beta_{0}C
for
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
,
C\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y_{1},X)
, and

and
c_{1,1}:=\beta_{1}C

\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}\in S^{\prime}
, then the convolution term

corresponds to the one from the heat-conduction problem with memory
[

.
Such form of the convolutor

was considered in the context of (

) in
[

]
. In this

\hat{\alpha}_{1}
,
\hat{\beta}_{1}
and the operator

, which imply that

satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
5.4
; cf.
[
4
]
and references therein.

6.3.
The abstract convolution equation

Finally, we comment on (
20
) with
A=B=P=0
, that is, we deal only with the convolution equation:

(36)

c\ast u=f\quad\textrm{ in }S^{\prime}(X).

Assume that
c
is of the form (
35
),
Y_{0}:=X
,
Y_{1}:=Y\hookrightarrow X
, and
\hat{c}_{i}:={\mathcal{F}}c_{i}\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathcal{L}}(Y_{i},X))

(i=0,1)
. Then,

\hat{c}:={\mathcal{F}}c=i(\cdot)\hat{c}_{0}+\hat{c}_{1}\in L^{1}_{loc}({\mathcal{L}}(Y,X))

We specify the assumptions on
c_{i}
to get the solvability of (

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose that
\hat{c}_{i}\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}({\mathcal{L}}(Y_{i},X))
and for every
s\in\dot{\mathbb{R}}

\hat{c}(t)=it\hat{c}_{0}(t)+\hat{c}_{1}(t)\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y,X)

is invertible. Set
a(t):=\hat{c}(t)^{-1}
and
d(t):=ta(t)\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
,
t\in\dot{\mathbb{R}}
.

If
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
and
d\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
, then

a\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}(({\mathcal{L}}(X,Y))
and
d\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}({\mathcal{L}}(X))
.

Moreover, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
q\in[1,\infty]
, and every Banach function space
\Phi
such that the Hardy-Littlewood operator
M
is bounded on
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
,
the operator
a(D)
extends to an isomorphism from
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
onto
B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(Y)\cap B^{s+1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
. In particular, for every
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
, the problem (
36
) has a unique solution
u\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(Y)\cap B^{s+1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.

Proof.

The proof reproduces the arguments already presented in the proofs of Proposition
5.1
and Theorem
5.4
. We present only its line and leave the details for the reader.

First, note that in the context of Proposition
5.1
, here
b=\hat{c}=i(\cdot)c_{0}+\hat{c}_{1}
and, by Leibniz’ rule, for every
\alpha=1,2,3
we get

b^{(\alpha)}(t)=it\hat{c}_{0}^{(\alpha)}(t)+\alpha ic_{0}^{(\alpha-1)}(t)+\hat{c}_{1}^{(\alpha)}(t)\quad(t\in\dot{\mathbb{R}}).

Therefore, combining (
25
), (
26
), and (
27
) with our assumptions on
a
,
c
, and
d
we easily get that
a
and
d
satisfy the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}
-condition.

Now, following the arguments presented in the proof of Theorem
5.4
(i) we get that the multipliers
a(D)_{|}
and
d(D)_{|}
extend to operators
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}\in{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X),B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(Y))
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}=\partial{\mathcal{T}}_{a}\in{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X))
, respectively. Similarly, the multipliers
\hat{c}_{i}(D)_{|}
extend to operators
{\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{i}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(Y_{i}),B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X))

(i=0,1)
.

Moreover, by a similar density argument as the one applied in the proof of Theorem
5.4
, we have that for every
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
, the distribution
u:={\mathcal{T}}_{a}f
satisfies (
36
), where
c\ast u
is defined as

{\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{0}}\partial u+{\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{1}}u
.
To show that
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
is an isomorphism in
{\mathcal{L}}(B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X),B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(Y)\cap B^{s+1,q}_{\Phi}(X))
, first note that by Lemma
5.3
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}f\in B^{s+1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
for every
f\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
, and that
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
is onto, since each
u\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(Y)\cap B^{s+1,q}_{\Phi}(X)
is a solution of (
36
) for
f:={\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{0}}\partial u+{\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{1}}u\in B^{s,q}_{\Phi}(X)
.
As we already noted the operator
{\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{0}}\partial+{\mathcal{T}}_{\hat{c}_{1}}
is the left inverse to
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
. It completes the proof.

∎
