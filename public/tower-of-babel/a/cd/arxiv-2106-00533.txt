Efficient verification and fidelity estimation of discrete bipartite squeezed states
Russell P Rundle

Abstract
To gain an advantage, quantum technologies utilize phenomena particular to quantum mechanics. Two such phenomena are squeezing and entanglement. Having generated states that exhibit these features, verification of their generation with local measurements can be a difficult process. Here we consider the states that are generated using the two-qudit single-axis squeezing Hamiltonian, that not only produces entangled two-qudit squeezed states but also results in various forms of interesting entangled states. We show how one can use local measurements to both efficiently verify and directly estimate the fidelity of these generated states.



I
Introduction

Utilizing quantum mechanical effects can lead to advantages in quantum technologies over their classical counterparts.
It is therefore important to devise methods to effectively and efficiently verify quantum processes and states.
One could choose to reconstruct the quantum state by means of quantum state tomography
Mauro D’Ariano et al. 2003
;
James et al. 2001
;
D'Ariano et al. 2002
;
Gross et al. 2010
.
However, as the size of the quantum system in question increases, it becomes increasingly more difficult and time-consuming to fully reconstruct a quantum state.
It then becomes more important to find a more computationally-efficient method to verify that a desired state has been created; and to verify that a given computation or procedure was performed correctly.

There have been many alternative approaches to verify the generation of a quantum state
Kliesch and Roth 2021
;
Gheorghiu et al. 2019
;
Eisert et al. 2020
.
These include considering and measuring certain properties and attributes via an appropriate metric; this has been done for properties such as entanglement
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
, entropy, purity, coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014
;
Streltsov et al. 2015
, among others, see Ref.
Montanaro and de Wolf 2016
for a survey of quantum property testing.
Alternatively methods to more directly certify the creation of a state, such as estimating the fidelity
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
and quantum state verification
Pallister et al. 2018
;
Hayashi and Morimae 2015
;
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Wang and Hayashi 2019
;
Yu et al. 2019
, have been devised recently.

In quantum state verification the goal is to devise a strategy that is made up of a set of measurements, where each measurement accepts a given target state with certainty and rejects any other state with some probability
Pallister et al. 2018
.
The strategy is then a convex sum of these measurements, in a way that optimizes the rejection of a state that isn’t the target state.
Measurements are then taken in line with this strategy a given number of times.
If any of these measurements fail, we can say with certainty that the generated state is not the target state.

This method of verification assumes that the target state is pure, however in experimental situations sufficient purity often proves difficult to achieve.
Resulting in a non-zero chance that the generated state is not the target state.
It may then be more useful to instead accept that the generated state is not the target state, and calculate how close you are to generating the state, with respect to some distance measure in state space.
This is where protocols such as direct fidelity estimation are useful
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
.
By taking a sequence of measurements, one can calculate the fidelity by using the measured results with the theoretical results of the target state.

Here, we extend previous results for quantum state verification and fidelity estimation to bipartite-qudit systems.
As the dimension of qudits increase, we then need
d-1
degrees of freedom to uniquely define a bipartite qudit state, up to local rotations; we therefore consider how states that are generated by a two-qudit squeezing Hamiltonian can be verified.
This will require just a time degree of freedom and allows us to consider particular states that may be of interest.

We will first consider an extension to the quantum state verification protocol found in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
, where we will discuss the previous results for two-qubit systems and then extend this to larger discrete systems.
This will be followed by how one can extend the direct fidelity estimation method from Ref.
Flammia and Liu 2011
to bipartite qudit systems.

II
The Model: Squeezing and entanglement

Squeezed states of light are generated by squeezing a coherent state of light along a given quadrature.
Such states have been demonstrated to give an advantage in various applications
Breitenbach et al. 1997
, such as in continuous-variable quantum computing and quantum information processing
Braunstein and van
Loock 2005
;
Furusawa et al. 1998
;
Menicucci et al. 2008
.
They have also been useful in quantum metrology
Polzik et al. 1992
;
Lawrie et al. 2019
, where squeezed states of light are used in the detection of gravitational waves
Chua et al. 2014
;
Aasi et al.
;
Abadie et al.
.
Alternatively, given two modes of light, one can squeeze the state along quadratures shared by the two modes, this creates an entangled state across these two modes.
When considering optical systems restricted to Gaussian states, one way to generate entanglement between two modes is to apply this squeezing operation across the two modes
Braunstein and van
Loock 2005
.

Conversely, there has been considerable attention into the generation of squeezed states in finite Hilbert spaces.
Where spin squeezed states have proven to be a valuable resource in the generation of atomic clocks
Leroux et al. 2010
;
Louchet-Chauvet et al. 2010
.
One method to produce such states is by applying the single-axis twisting Hamiltonian
Agarwal et al. 1997
.
Further application of this Hamiltonian then produces atomic Schrödinger cat states – the superposition of many spin coherent states
Agarwal et al. 1997
.
By considering a single large spin as the symmetric subspace of many qubits, the single-twisting Hamiltonian has also been a reliable method to generate GHZ states of up to 20 qubits
Song et al. 2019
.

Analogously to a two-mode squeezed state, one can adapt the single-axis twisting Hamiltonian to generate a two-qudit squeezing operation.
Given two spin-
j
qudits, each with dimension
d=2j+1
, two-qudit squeezed states can be generated by applying the Hamiltonian

H_{d}=\mu\hat{J}^{z}_{d}\otimes\hat{J}^{z}_{d},

(1)

where
\hat{J}^{z}_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional spin operator that rotates a
d
-dimensional state around the
z
-axis.

This Hamiltonian can then be applied to an initial state
\left|\Psi(0)\right\rangle_{d}
, where

\left|\Psi(0)\right\rangle_{d}=\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{d-1}\left[\binom{d-1}{k}\binom{d-1}{k^{\prime}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|k\right\rangle\left|k^{\prime}\right\rangle.

(2)

This state is the eigenstate of the
\hat{J}^{x}_{d}\otimes\hat{J}^{x}_{d}
operator with eigenvalue
(d-1)^{2}/4
, generating a spin-coherent state that is aligned along the equator of the Bloch sphere
Arecchi et al. 1972
;
Perelomov 1986
.
For two qubits, this state is simply
(\left|0\right\rangle+\left|1\right\rangle)\otimes(\left|0\right\rangle+\left|1\right\rangle)/2
.

Applying
\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\tau H_{d}\right)
to Eq. (
2
), where
\tau=\mu t
is dimensionless time, we yield the time-dependent state

\displaystyle\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\tau\hat{J}^{z}_{d}\otimes\hat{J}^{z}_{d}}\left|\Psi(0)\right\rangle_{d}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{d-1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\tau(j-k)(j-k^{\prime})}}{2^{d-1}}\left[\binom{d-1}{k}\binom{d-1}{k^{\prime}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|k\right\rangle\left|k^{\prime}\right\rangle.

Figure 1:

The negativity of bipartite qudit states through the two-qudit squeezing evolution.
The blue curve (bottom) is the negativity of two qubits; the red (middle) line is the negativity of two qutrits; the following green curve is for two 5-level qudits.
The top to curves show the values for two 11-level and 21-level qubits in grey and black respectively.
This quantifies the entanglement between the two qudits at different times in the evolution.
At each of the troughs in the negativity particularly interesting entangled states are generated, and will be considered separately in Section
III.1
.

The evolution of this initial state under this Hamiltonian results in the state twisting around the
z
axis, causing the initial coherent state to squeeze, creating a discrete analog of two-mode squeezing as the two states entangle
Chan and Law 2006
.
This has also been considered as a method to entangle two Bose Einstein condensates
Byrnes et al. 2012
;
Byrnes 2013
;
Meng et al. 2021
, to treat Bose Einstein condensates as qubits in quantum computations.
A method to verify the creation of such states can therefore be used to verify the generation of entangled Bose Einstein condensates.

Given we are only considering pure states during the evolution, one can use multiple metrics to consider the entanglement, such as the purity
Chan and Law 2006
or von Neumann entropy.
Here we consider the negativity, where

\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\frac{||\rho^{\Gamma_{A}}||_{1}-1}{2},

(4)

\rho^{\Gamma_{A}}
is the partial transpose of the density matrix
\rho
with respect to qubit
A
.

||X||_{1}=\Tr\left[\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}\right]
is the trace norm.

From Fig.
1
, it can be seen that as the squeezing occurs, the negativity between the two systems increases.
Unlike the infinite-dimensional case, different forms of entangled states arise throughout the evolution.
These can be identified by the dips in the negativity.
A curious effect is the fractal-like behaviour throughout the evolution
Byrnes 2013
.

At
\tau=\pi
, the negativity is always equal to 1, regardless of the dimension.
In all cases, this state is of the same form; that is, locally equivalent to
(\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, and for two qubits is a maximally entangled state.
Alternatively, we can think of it as a bipartite-qubit, two-component atomic Schrödinger cat state.
Such a state will be labelled
\left|(2,2)\right\rangle
where the first 2 represents the dimension of each qudit and the second 2 represents the number of components.

As the dimension increases, the form of the state is the same, albeit with a larger overall Hilbert space, where we will label a general state in the Schmidt decomposition at
\tau=\pi
as

\left|(d,2)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|00\right\rangle_{d}+\left|11\right\rangle_{d}),

(5)

where
d
is the dimension of each qudit.

When the dimension increases, other forms of entanglement start to appear.
These can be seen in the local minima in the negativity for higher-dimensional systems.
The states at these points are bipartite-qudit,
\kappa
-component atomic Schrödinger cat states.
In the Schmidt decomposition, these states can be written as

\left|(d,\kappa)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa-1}\left|kk\right\rangle_{d}.

(6)

Note that when
\kappa=d
these state are maximally entangled states.
Alternatively, when
\kappa<d
the state is maximally in a subspace of the full Hilbert space, however the full state itself is not maximally entangled.

Note that only the two-qubit state creates a maximally entangled state.
Interestingly, when the dimension is large enough, the state
\left|(d,\kappa)\right\rangle
appears at
\tau=2\pi/\kappa
, and certain multiples thereafter – unless coinciding with a lower value of
\kappa
.
For example a
\kappa=4
state appears at
\tau=\pi/2
and
3\pi/2
, but not at
\tau=\pi
where a
\kappa=2
state appears.

All the entangled states that appear at these dips in negativity are special cases of Eq. (
3
) that have a more simple procedure for optimal verification; these cases will be considered separately in Section
III.1
.
Following this, we will consider the methods that we can verify any general state of the form Eq. (
3
).

III
Existing Verification Methods

There are cases where verification methods have already been given.
In Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
, a method to optimally verify any two-qubit state was presented, where the authors also presented a method to verify stabilizer states.
This was followed results in Ref.
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
where any multi-qubit maximally entangled state can be generated.
We also note that adaptive protocols have been presented both for the two-qubit case and for two-qudits
Li et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2019
, where such protocols require communication between measurements.
Here we are interested in generalizing a non-adaptive protocol to any two-qudit system.
The results of this will be given in Section
V
.
First, we will discuss the two-qubit case from Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
, along with the maximally entangled states considered in Ref.
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
and a discussion around certain types of special cases.

III.1
Special cases

Verification of bipartite states with local measurements can be complicated procedure, where there has been much work on providing methods to do verify arbitrary quantum states
Pallister et al. 2018
;
Hayashi and Morimae 2015
;
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Wang and Hayashi 2019
;
Yu et al. 2019
.
There are however certain states that require a much simpler procedure than the general case, it is then worth considering these cases first.

An important class of these are maximally entangled states

\left|(d,d)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left|kk\right\rangle_{d}.

(7)

It was shown in Ref.
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
such states for any dimension can be verified.
Where for prime-dimensional systems, the strategy can be simply calculated with the Weyl algebra, otherwise a strategy can be generated through a 2-design structure
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a
.

For two qubits, the verification of the maximally entangled state
\left|\psi\right\rangle=(\left|00\right\rangle_{2}+\left|11\right\rangle_{2})/\sqrt{2}
can be performed by the strategy

\Omega_{\text{Bell}}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\Pi_{2}^{1}+\Pi_{2}^{2}+\Pi_{2}^{3}\right),

(8)

made up of the projectors
\Pi_{2}^{i}
, such that each

\Pi_{2}^{i}=\sum_{k\in\{-1,1\}}\left|{\sigma_{i},k}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\sigma_{i},k}\right|_{2}\otimes{\left|{\overline{\sigma_{i},k}}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\overline{\sigma_{i},k}}\right|}_{2}

(9)

where the subscript refers to
d=2
.
The states in Eq. (
9
) are the eigenstates of the Pauli operators
\sigma_{i}\left|\sigma_{i},k\right\rangle_{2}=k\left|\sigma_{i},k\right\rangle_{2}
and
\left|\overline{\sigma_{i},k}\right\rangle_{2}
is the complex conjugate of the vector.
Note that every two-qubit maximally entangled state is simply a local rotation of this state.

When considering the evolution of Eq. (
1
), at
t=\pi
a state of this form is produced regardless the dimension of the qudits, producing a state of the form
\left|(d,2)\right\rangle
in Eq. (
5
).
When
d>2
,
\left|(d,2)\right\rangle
is not maximally entangled.
This can be seen in comparison to the the two-qutrit maximally entangled state calculated from Eq. (
7
).
Further we can see this by the value of the negativity of this state in Fig.
1
.
These are still interesting entangled states, it is therefore worthwhile discussing how such states can be verified.

To verify
\left|(d,2)\right\rangle
, consider the state
(\left|00\right\rangle_{d}+\left|11\right\rangle_{d})/\sqrt{2}
, that is equivalent to the target state up to local rotations.
This state is then an eigenstate of the first three generators of the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
algebra.
The matrix representation of these three can be given by

\Lambda_{d}^{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{i}&\vline&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\vline&0_{d-2}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
i=1,2,3
.
These are
d\times d
matrices with with the Pauli operators in the top-left.

Similarly to Eq. (
9
), we can define the projectors

\Pi_{d}^{i}=\sum_{k\in\{-1,1\}}\left|{\Lambda_{d}^{i},k}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\Lambda_{d}^{i},k}\right|\otimes{\left|{\overline{\Lambda_{d}^{i},k}}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\overline{\Lambda_{d}^{i},k}}\right|}

(11)

where
\Lambda_{d}^{i}\left|\Lambda_{d}^{i},k\right\rangle=k\left|\Lambda_{d}^{i},k\right\rangle
and
\left|\overline{\Lambda_{d}^{i},k}\right\rangle
is the complex conjugate of the vector.
The strategy for
\left|(d,2)\right\rangle
can then be built by

\Omega_{\text{Bell}}=\Pi_{d}^{1}+\Pi_{d}^{2}+\Pi_{d}^{3},

(12)

This can be generalized to measure any entangled state of the form in Eq. (
6
).
Where just a
\kappa
-dimensional subspace of
\left|(d,\kappa)\right\rangle
needs to be measured.

Note in the two-qudit squeezing evolution, that as the dimension of the qudits increases, more variants of these states emerge.
Each of the troughs in the negativity in Fig.
1
is one of these entangled states.
We can treat these states as subspaces of the full two-qudit space, much as we did in Eq. (
12
).

We now have a method to efficiently verify each of the local minima in the negativity.
However this misses out an important part of the evolution, the spin-squeezed states that are produced at the beginning.

III.2
Two-qubit verification

The optimal method to verify any two-qubit state was given in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
; we will now present the main results from Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
in order to consider a method to scale the generated strategy up to larger system sizes.
Note that other strategies have been considered that are adaptive
Li et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2019
, here our goal is to generalize the non-adaptive strategy in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
to larger qudits to create a protocol that is efficient and easily scalable.

We start with a general two-qubit state as our target state.
The general target state given in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
is the same as the Schmidt decomposition of two-qubit solution of Eq. (
3
)

\left|\psi(\tau)\right\rangle=\cos\tau\left|00\right\rangle+\sin\tau\left|11\right\rangle,

(13)

where the only difference with the target state in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
is that the
\sin
and
\cos
terms are swapped.

In Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
, the authors started with a general strategy for this state that is made up of the convex sum

\Omega=c_{1}\Omega_{1}+c_{2}\Omega_{2}+c_{3}\Omega_{3}+c_{4}\Omega_{4},

(14)

where
\Omega_{i}
is some rank-
i
strategy.
At
\tau=0
and
\tau=2\pi
all that is needed is a rank-1 strategy for optimal verification.
Since such states are themselves separable, the strategy will simply be
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\psi}\right|
.
As was shown in Section
III.1
, there is also a special case when
\tau=\pi
, where we only need the rank-2 strategy given in Eq. (
8
)

It was shown in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
, that for any other state, we need the addition of a rank-3 strategy as well as the rank-2 operator

P_{\!Z\!Z_{2}}^{+}=\left|{00}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{00}\right|_{2}+\left|{11}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{11}\right|_{2}.

(15)

The required rank-3 strategy is of the form

\Omega_{3}=\mathds{1}-\sum_{j}\eta_{j}\left|{\phi_{j}}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\phi_{j}}\right|

(16)

for some normalization
\eta_{j}
, where

\left\langle{\phi_{j}}|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle=0

(17)

for every
j
and each
\left|\phi_{j}\right\rangle
is separable.
This results in the overall strategy

\Omega=\alpha P_{\!Z\!Z_{2}}^{+}+(1-\alpha)\Omega_{3}.

(18)

Now all that is needed is the explicit form of Eq. (
16
) and the value of
\alpha
.
In Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
, the authors proved that the optimal form of Eq. (
16
) is given when
\eta_{j}=1/3
for
j=1,2,3
, and

\begin{split}\left|\phi_{j}\right\rangle=&\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\cot\tau}}\left|0\right\rangle+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\mathrm{i}\pi j}{3}}}{\sqrt{1+\tan\tau}}\left|1\right\rangle\right)\\
&\otimes\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\cot\tau}}\left|0\right\rangle+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\mathrm{i}\pi(3-2j)}{3}}}{\sqrt{1+\tan\tau}}\left|1\right\rangle\right)\end{split}

(19)

note that the coefficients are swapped here in comparison to Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
.
Also note that only three values of
j
are needed in the sum, this is equivalent to integrating over all valid values of the phases on the states.

The phase difference on the first and second qubit is fixed, this can be seen by considering the requirement in Eq. (
17
).
This requirement means that any state
\left|\phi_{j}\right\rangle
needs to have a value
\nu\sin\tau
as a coefficient of
\left|00\right\rangle
and
-\nu\cos\tau
as a coefficient of
\left|11\right\rangle
, for some normalization
\nu
.
This results in
\left\langle{\phi_{j}}|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle=\nu\sin\tau\cos\tau-\nu\cos\tau\sin\tau=0
.

One way to arrive at the states in Eq. (
19
) is then to start with a state

\begin{split}\left|\phi_{j}\right\rangle=&\nu\left(\sqrt{\sin\tau}\left|0\right\rangle+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\sqrt{\cos\tau}\left|1\right\rangle\right)\\
&\otimes\left(\sqrt{\sin\tau}\left|0\right\rangle+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\pi-\varphi)}\sqrt{\cos\tau}\left|1\right\rangle\right).\end{split}

(20)

Normalizing results in
\nu=1/\sqrt{1+2\sin\tau\cos\tau}
.
This gives the correct values in Eq. (
19
).
The next step is to integrate over the values of
\varphi
, it was shown in Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
that taking the sum over the three phases in Eq. (
19
) yields equivalent results to integrating over all phases.

Now that both parts of the strategy are generated, it is necessary to find the optimal value of
\alpha
.
This means optimizing between the
P_{\!Z\!Z_{2}}^{+}
and the
\Omega_{3}
components of the strategy.
Note that
P_{\!Z\!Z_{2}}^{+}
accepts both the target state and the orthogonal state
\left|\psi^{\perp}(\tau)\right\rangle=\sin\tau\left|00\right\rangle+\cos\tau\left|11\right\rangle
with certainty while also always rejecting
\left|01\right\rangle
and
\left|10\right\rangle
.

\Omega_{3}
, on the other hand, accepts the target state with certainty and rejects
\left|\psi^{\perp}(\tau)\right\rangle
with some given probability;
\Omega_{3}
also accepts
\left|01\right\rangle
and
\left|10\right\rangle
with some probability.

Optimizing for
\alpha
is then a balance between these two scenarios, where we want to decrease the probability of accepting
\left|\psi^{\perp}(\tau)\right\rangle
as much as possible while not increasing the probability of accepting
\left|01\right\rangle
and
\left|10\right\rangle
too much.
This results in solving
\left\langle\psi^{\perp}(\tau)\right|\Omega\left|\psi^{\perp}(\tau)\right\rangle=\left\langle 01\right|\Omega\left|01\right\rangle=\left\langle 10\right|\Omega\left|10\right\rangle
, resulting in a value of
\alpha=(2-\sin 2\tau)/(4+\sin 2\tau)
.
For more detail into the proof of this optimization, see the supplementary material of Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
.

III.3
Two-qubit fidelity estimation

In experimental settings it is known that the state created isn’t exactly the target state.
It can then be much more desirable to estimate the fidelity of the created state with respect to the target state.

This can be done in various ways, one of which is an extension of the two-qubit verification strategy, where it was shown in Ref.
Zhu and Hayashi 2019b
that the infidelity is bounded by the second highest and the lowest eigenvalue of the strategy.
These inequalities are then saturated when the strategy is of a homogenous form – when these eigenvalues are equal.

Here we will instead focus on the direct fidelity estimation protocol laid out in Refs.
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
.
In Ref.
Flammia and Liu 2011
a protocol to estimate the fidelity of multi-qubit states and processes was given by considering the characteristic function of the state.
Similar results were shown in Ref.
da Silva et al. 2011
where they also extended their results to continuous-variable systems.
In Ref.
da Silva et al. 2011
, the authors considered the use of the Wigner function for a continuous-variable system, which is the Fourier transform of the characteristic function.
We note that any informationally complete probability distribution function can be used to perform the procedure for any system, the procedure just may need some adjustment
Kliesch and Roth 2021
;
Rundle and Everitt
.

For estimating the fidelity of a general two-qubit state, we begin with introducing the two-qubit characteristic function, where

\chi_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\rho\;\sigma_{k}\otimes\sigma_{k^{\prime}}\right].

(21)

Note that the characteristic function is normalized so that
\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\chi(k,k^{\prime})^{2}=1
.
For our two-qubit state in Eq. (
13
) the characteristic function is only non-zero in six elements

\displaystyle\chi(0,0)=\chi(3,3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\chi(1,1)=-\chi(2,2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\tau

(22)

\displaystyle\chi(0,4)=\chi(4,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cos 2\tau.

This means we only need to take measurements in six bases where, from this, one can then calculate the fidelity

F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\chi_{\rho_{1}}(k,k^{\prime})\,\chi_{\rho_{2}}(k,k^{\prime}),

(23)

where
\chi_{\rho_{1}}(k,k^{\prime})
is the characteristic function for the target state in Eq. (
22
) and
\chi_{\rho_{2}}(k,k^{\prime})
is the measured characteristic function.

Following the protocol in Refs.
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
, we choose

\ell=\lceil 1/\epsilon^{2}\delta\rceil

(24)

values of
(k,k^{\prime})
, where
\epsilon
is the adaptive error and
\delta
is the failure probability
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
;
Kliesch and Roth 2021
.
Each sample will be chosen with the probability
|\chi(k_{i},k^{\prime}_{i})|^{2}
.
On average, the measurements
\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{1}
and
\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}
will be selected
25\%
of the
\ell
times each, and together take up half the basis choices for measurement.
The other half come from the other four measurement bases; where on average
\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}
will be selected
\ell\sin^{2}(2\tau)/4
times each; and
\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{z}
and
\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathds{1}
will be chosen
\ell\cos^{2}(2\tau)/4
times each.

Every time one of these measurement bases are chosen, we then need to measure each choice
\sigma_{k}\otimes\sigma_{k^{\prime}}
a total of
m_{i}
times, where

m_{i}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\ell\epsilon^{2}\chi(k_{i},k^{\prime}_{i})^{2}}\log(2/\delta)\right\rceil.

IV
Two-qutrit states

Now that we’ve discussed the existing methods for quantum state verification and direct fidelity estimation, we will consider how these can be extended for larger qudits.
We start with systems of two qutrits here before going on to how these protocols can be generalized for larger bipartite systems in Section
V
.
Note that in the case of quantum state verification, we’re more interested in generating a strategy that is both efficient and simply scalable, rather than the optimal strategy.

IV.1
Efficient quantum-state verification

As the dimension of each qudit increases, so does the number of possible substrategies.
Here we will show how an efficient strategy can be generated by just considering two substrategies.
Note also that as the dimension of the qudits increases, the possible target states in the Schmidt decomposition has two degrees of freedom to choose from.
We will therefore decrease this to one degree of freedom and consider the squeezing evolution for two qutrits; doing so allows us to consider and provide results for a subset of interesting and useful states that can be generated in systems of two qutrits.

Considering two qutrits, the time-dependent state from Eq. (

In order to provide a strategy to verify a general two-qutrit state in this evolution, we need to consider the structure of a generalized strategy.
A strategy for two qutrits can be generated as the convex sum

Now, to simplify the strategy and to create analogy to the two-qubit case, we will consider rank-3 and rank-7 substrategies.

V
Two-qudit quantum state verificaiton

Given the previous results for two-qubit systems and the extension to two-qutrit systems, we now further generalize these results to two qudits of arbitrary dimension.
We will focus on the construction of an efficient procedure to verify a general two-qudit state, where the generalization of the fidelity estimation protocol is given in App.
A
.

To generalize the construction of this strategy to a bipartite system of any dimension, we first need to define the substrategies.
Like in Eq. (
29
), the strategy is built from two parts.
The first part is the generalization of the
P_{\!Z\!Z_{d}}^{+}
operator, this is simply

P_{\!Z\!Z_{d}}^{+}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left|{dd}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{dd}\right|.

(51)

The second part is to generalize the rank-7 part of the strategy in Eq. (
29
).
In general we will require a rank-
r
strategy, where
r=(d^{2}-d+1)
.
This rank-
r
strategy needs to itself be constructed from separable states that are orthogonal to the target state.
We then in effect build a rank-
d-1
strategy from the projectors of these states, that we then subtract from the identity operator, generating a rank-
r
strategy.

In Eq. (
35
) and Eq. (
36
) we gave two general states that are orthogonal to the target state.
We then went onto making separable versions of these states.
Specifically, this involved creating a separable state,
\left|\phi_{i}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\right\rangle
, where

\langle kk\left|\phi_{i}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\right\rangle=\frac{\langle kk\left|\psi^{\perp}_{i}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{\left\langle{\phi_{i}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})}|{\phi_{i}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})}\right\rangle}},

(52)

i. e.
the
\left|kk\right\rangle
elements of the separable states are proportional to
\langle kk\left|\psi^{\perp}_{i}\right\rangle
.

To generalize these states, we first need a reliable method to create an orthonormal basis for a
d
-dimensional system.
One way is to adapt the coherent-state approach for states with an
\Su\!\left(d\right)
symmetry
Nemoto 2000
;
Tilma and Nemoto 2012
.
The procedure to do this is given in App.
C
.

The goal is to use the Euler-angle construction to compose a coherent state whose elements are the Schmidt coefficients of the target state.
That is, given the Schmidt decomposition of a general two-qutrit target state

\left|\psi\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}\left|kk\right\rangle,

(53)

we wish to create a coherent state

\left|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}\left|k\right\rangle,

(54)

finding the solution for the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
degrees of freedom.
In the spin-coherent state construction, this is defined as the Euler operator applied to the highest-weighted state,
\left|d-1\right\rangle
.

\epsilon=0.99

.

for each case, that shows the balance between the

We can then generate an orthonormal basis by applying the Euler operator to the other computational basis states,
\left|k\right\rangle
where
0\leq k<d-1
.
By reversing the step from Eq. (
54
) to Eq. (
53
) we can convert these single-qudit orthogonal states into two-qudit states in the Schmidt decomposition, which are the generalization of
\left|\psi^{\perp}_{1}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi^{\perp}_{2}\right\rangle
from Eq. (
35
) and Eq. (
36
).
This along with the states
\left|k_{1}k_{2}\right\rangle
, where
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
, builds a basis for a two-qudit strategy.

From the first
d-1
orthogonal states
\left|\psi^{\perp}_{i}\right\rangle
, and by following the procedure in App.
C
, we can then get a general set of separable states
\left|\phi_{i}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\right\rangle
for
i=1...d-1
, that are orthogonal to the target state.

The steps are now similar to the two-qutrit case, where we now generate

\Omega_{r}=\frac{1}{3^{d-1}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\left(\mathds{1}_{d}-\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}\left|{\phi_{n}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{\phi_{n}^{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\varphi})}\right|\right).

(55)

The sum over
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is an average over the phases, where we restrict this to each
\varphi_{i}=\{0,2\pi/3,4\pi/3\}
.
This results in
3^{d-1}
different phases.

We now need to build the full strategy

\Omega=\alpha P_{\!Z\!Z_{d}}^{+}+(1-\alpha)\Omega_{r},

(56)

where we want to optimize over
\alpha
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, where
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{\theta_{d-1},...,\theta_{d(d-1)/2}\}
.
Although a more efficient strategy can be found by optimizing over
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, it is a far more simple construction to just set
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=0
.
Given this compromise in optimality, we have calculated the optimal values for
\alpha
in these cases numerically.

we can then construct an efficient strategy.
From this strategy, we just need to find the second largest eigenvalue,

VI
Conclusions

Here we have provided procedures to generate efficient strategies to verify – and to directly estimate the fidelity of – two-qudit states, both with local measurements.
Although not optimal, this quantum-state verification procedure is simply extendable to a bipartite system of any size.

The strategies provided can be used to verify any two-qudit state, where a two-qudit target state in the Schmidt decomposition has
d-1
degrees of freedom.
We gave examples of how these strategies perform in the two-qudit squeezing evolution.
The states produced in this evolution are of particular interest as both two-qudit squeezed states and various entangled states are generated.
States that are of particular interest in a quantum technology setting.

We also note that the techniques used here can be used to verify a single-axis twisting Hamiltonian on a single qubit, by utilizing the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism and verifying the two-qutrit Choi matrix
Liu et al. 2020
.
Both the single-qudit and two-qudit squeezing evolutions have themselves seen significant interest, whether in creating large entangled state
Song et al. 2019
or when entangling Bose Einstein condensates
Byrnes et al. 2012
;
Byrnes 2013
;
Meng et al. 2021
.
It is therefore conceivable that we could use the strategies provided in this work to experimentally verify the creation of these bipartite squeezed and entangled Bose Einstein condensates.

We have focussed here on providing a simple procedure to generate a strategy for two qudit states, but this may have sacrificed optimality.
Future work is needed to optimize these strategies, which will require optimizing over the degrees of freedom; whether by averaging over many values or by finding an optimal value.
Further optimization may also come in the form of introducing more and different types of components to the strategy.

We also note that an adaptation of the strategy could be useful when applied to a bipartite infinite-dimensional system, if we choose to truncate the Hilbert space to some degree.
We could then apply this method to verify the creation of two-mode squeezed states.
However, this would depend on the possibility of measuring the necessary observables in such systems.

Appendix A
Fidelity estimation

Here we will discuss how the direct fidelity estimation protocols in Refs.
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
can be extended to higher-dimensional discrete systems.
A similar result has been presented in terms of a frame theoretic construction
Kliesch
.
By following these results and using an appropriate choice of operators for a characteristic function, this is a fairly simple extension.
Further, the fidelity estimation of a bipartite states is particularly straightforward when considering the Schmidt decomposition of the state.

We begin by introducing the bipartite characteristic function for two qubits

\chi_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\rho\;\sigma_{k}\otimes\sigma_{k^{\prime}}\right].

(57)

To generalize this to two-qudit states, we first need to generalize the characteristic function to higher-dimensional states.
To build a discrete characteristic function we need a set of
d^{2}
operators, we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{d}(k)
, that fully describe a single-qudit state.
Such a choice is not unique, as there are many generalizations to the Pauli operators to higher-dimensional systems.

We show two of the most common approaches, the Weyl algebra and the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
algebra (or the generalized Gell Mann matrices).
The construction of which can be found in App.
B
.

We note here that it is important that we normalize the characteristic function such that

\sum_{k}|\chi_{\rho}(k)|^{2}=1,

(58)

which is different from more standard constructions of a characteristic function, found in texts such as Refs.
Gross 2006
;
Vourdas 1997
;
Rundle et al. 2019
;
Rundle and Everitt
.

Given a suitable choice of representation to build a characteristic function, Eq. (
57
) can be generalized to

\chi_{\rho}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k})}\Tr\left[\rho\;\mathcal{D}_{d}(\mathbf{k})\right],

(59)

where
\mathbf{k}=(k,k^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{D}_{d}(\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{D}_{d}(k)\otimes\mathcal{D}_{d}(k^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{N}(k)\mathcal{N}(k^{\prime})
is the normalization to ensure Eq. (
58
) is satisfied.
This normalization can be simply calculated by requiring
\Tr\left[\mathcal{D}_{d}(k)\mathcal{D}_{d}(k)^{\dagger}\right]/\mathcal{N}_{d}(k)^{2}=1
.
In practice this results in
\mathcal{N}(k)=\sqrt{d}
in the Weyl algebra case, and
\mathcal{N}(k)=\sqrt{\Tr\left[\mathcal{D}_{d}(k)^{2}\right]}
in the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
case.
Note that we also require that

\Tr\left[\mathcal{D}_{d}(k_{1})\mathcal{D}_{d}(k_{2})^{\dagger}\right]=0
when
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
.

From this, one can then calculate the fidelity

F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\chi_{\rho_{1}}(\mathbf{k})\,\overline{\chi}_{\rho_{2}}(\mathbf{k})

(60)

where we note that the characteristic function can be complex-valued, which is generally the case when using the Weyl algebra.
Now that the characteristic function has been defined, we will now follow the protocol laid out in Ref.
Flammia and Liu 2011
for direct fidelity estimation.

Begin by choosing
\ell=\lceil 1/\epsilon^{2}\delta\rceil
values of
\mathbf{k}
, where
\epsilon
is the adaptive error and
\delta
is the failure probability.
Each sample
\mathbf{k}_{i}
will be chosen with the probability
|\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})|^{2}
, for
\mathbf{k}_{1},...,\mathbf{k}_{\ell}
.
We then need to measure
\mathcal{D}_{d}(\mathbf{k}_{i})
a total of
m_{i}
times, where

m_{i}=\left\lceil\frac{2}{\ell\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})^{2}\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})^{2}}\right\rceil\log(2/\delta).

(61)

Note that different approaches need to be taken here, depending on your choice of operators.

For each
i
, we then define the random variable

X(\mathbf{k}_{i})=\frac{\chi_{\rho_{2}}(\mathbf{k}_{i})}{\chi_{\rho_{1}}(\mathbf{k}_{i})}=\frac{\Tr\left[\rho_{2}\,\mathcal{D}_{d}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\right]}{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\chi_{\rho_{1}}(\mathbf{k}_{i})}

(62)

such that
E[X(\mathbf{k}_{i})]=\chi_{\rho_{1}}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\overline{\chi}_{\rho_{2}}(\mathbf{k}_{i})
.
We can then calculate the estimator for
X(\mathbf{k}_{i})
by

\tilde{X}(\mathbf{k}_{i})=\frac{1}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}M_{ij}.

(63)

Repeating this process, we can then calculate the estimator for the fidelity

\tilde{Y}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{X}(\mathbf{k}_{i}).

(64)

This yields an estimator to the fidelity within the range
[\tilde{Y}-2\epsilon,\tilde{Y}+2\epsilon]
with probability greater than
1-2\delta
.
More details on how to construct these for specific choice can be found in App.
B
.

Because the measurement process involves choosing
\mathbf{k}
with probability
P(\mathbf{k})=\chi_{\rho_{1}}(\mathbf{k})^{2}
, any points where
\chi_{\rho_{2}}(\mathbf{k})=0
requires no measurement to be taken.
Given the structure of the Schmidt decomposition, this results in a big reduction in the number of measurement bases.
If all the measurements are needed, we would need the full
d^{4}
described by the representation, however this is reduced to at most
d^{3}
needing to be taken if considering the Weyl operators, and
2d^{2}-d
if considering the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(2\right)
algebra.

Appendix B
Bases to measure the characteristic function

The generalization of the characteristic function in Section
A
to higher-dimensional systems can take multiple forms.
Here we consider two approaches to construct the characteristic function with discrete degrees of freedom.
These will be the Weyl algebra, also known as the clock and shift matrices.
And the generators for
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
, also known as the generalized Gell-Mann matrices.

Note that one could also describe the characteristic function of a finite-dimensional system with continuous degrees of freedom, see for example Refs.
Rundle et al. 2019
;
Rundle and Everitt
; one could then use a procedure similar to the continuous-variable calculation of the fidelity in Ref.
da Silva et al. 2011
to perform direct fidelity estimation.
Such an approach may be beneficial for particularly large Hilbert spaces, but is beyond the scope of this paper.

B.1
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
algebra

We will now focus on the construction of the characteristic function through the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
generalized Gell-Mann algebra.
The diagonal generators are constructed

\lambda_{n^{2}-1}^{d}=\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n(n-1)}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|{i}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{i}\right|\right)-\sqrt{\tfrac{2(n-1)}{n}}\left|{n}\right\rangle\!\left\langle{n}\right|,

(65)

for
n\geq 2
, and
\lambda_{0}^{d}=\mathds{1}
.

The off-diagonal generators are simply the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(2\right)
generators, spanning the full
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
space.
We can separate the off-diagonal operators into two types, the generalization of
\hat{\sigma}^{x}
and
\hat{\sigma}^{y}
, where

\begin{split}\mathsf{X}^{d}_{i,j}&=\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle j-1\right|+\left|j-1\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|\\
\mathsf{Y}^{d}_{i,j}&=\mathrm{i}\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle j-1\right|-\mathrm{i}\left|j-1\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|\end{split}

(66)

where
1\leq i\leq d-1
,
1\leq j\leq i
.
Formally these operators are then ordered with respect to an
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(2\right)
subspace of
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
, see Refs.
Tilma and Nemoto 2012
;
Rundle et al. 2019
for more detail.
However the ordering is not that important as long as it is kept track of.
We will then assume some order and refer to the algebra by the matrices
\lambda^{d}_{k}
.

The characteristic function is then

\chi_{\rho}(k)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(k)}\Tr\left[\rho\,\lambda^{d}_{k}\right],

(67)

where to satisfy Eq. (
58
) the normalization is

\mathcal{N}(k)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},&\text{for }k=0,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},&\text{for }1\leq k\leq d^{2}-1.\end{cases}

(68)

The characteristic function is informationally complete, and the full state can be reconstructed by

\rho=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(k)}\chi_{\rho}(k)\lambda^{d}_{k},

(69)

where
\mathcal{N}(k)
is the normalization required by the operators used to define the Weyl function.

The fidelity of some state,
\sigma
, with respect to a target state,
\rho
, can then be calculated through the characteristic functions, where

\Tr\left[\rho\sigma\right]=\sum_{k}\chi_{\rho}(k)\chi_{\sigma}(k).

(70)

From this we can then follow the procedure in Section
A
.
It is now worth following the proof of this procedure as outlined in Ref.
Flammia and Liu 2011
and point how and why this still holds for the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
algebra.
We want to prove that the fidelity is estimated with in the range
[\tilde{Y}-2\epsilon,\tilde{Y}+2\epsilon]
with probability greater than
1-2\delta
.
Equivalently we can express this as

\mathrm{Pr}\left[|\tilde{Y}-F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})|\leq 2\epsilon\right]\geq 1-2\delta.

(71)

We begin by introducing
Y=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i}X(\mathbf{k}_{i})
, where
Y
is an unbiased estimator of
F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
.
Therefore by Chebyshev’s inequality we can say

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[|Y-F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})|\geq\epsilon\right]&\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}\ell}\\
&\leq\delta\end{split}

(72)

from the definition of
\ell
in Section
A
.

Now that we have a relation for
Y
and the fidelity, we want to consider
\tilde{Y}
.
So far the procedure has followed previous work in Refs.
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
Kliesch and Roth 2021
, this is where a little more care is needed for the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
algebra.
Substituting Eq. (
63
) into Eq. (
64
)
we yield

\tilde{Y}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\frac{1}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}M_{ij},

(73)

where
E[\tilde{Y}]=Y
.

For the multi-qubit case, we then move onto Hoeffding’s inequality, where
M_{ij}\in\{-1,1\}
.
This results in the bounds

b_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}

(74)

and more importantly to the denominator in Hoeffding’s inequality

|b|=b_{+}-b_{-}=\frac{2}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}.

(75)

Applying Hoeffding’s inequality

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[|\tilde{Y}-Y|\geq\epsilon\right]&\leq 2\exp\left[\frac{-\epsilon^{2}\ell^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{2}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}}\right]\\
&\leq\delta\end{split}

(76)

given the definition for
m_{i}
.
By applying union bound this results in the desired

\mathrm{Pr}\left[|\tilde{Y}-F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})|\leq 2\epsilon\right]\geq 1-2\delta.

(77)

Returning to Eq. (
73
), we note that when using the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
algebra the results of
M_{ij}
change.
Any single measurement of a state with any of the off-diagonal operator
\lambda^{d}_{k}
will still yield the result
M\in\{-1,1\}
.
This leaves the inequality in Eq. (
76
) unchanged.
A measurement with any of the diagonal operators will on the other hand yield

M_{ij}\in\left\{-\sqrt{\tfrac{2(n-1)}{n}},\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n(n-1)}}\right\}.

(78)

And so for these measurements,
\tilde{Y}
is bounded by this new interval, dependent on the measurement choice.

Considering bipartite systems, we can choose two values,
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, where we say
n_{1}
the choice of a diagonal operator for the first qudit and
n_{2}
is the choice for the second qudit.
The measurement outcomes can now be anywhere between

\left\{-\sqrt{\tfrac{2(n_{1}-1)}{n_{1}}}\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}},\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)}}\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}}\right\},

(79)

resulting in the difference

2\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}}{(n_{1}-1)n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}}\leq 2,

(80)

since
0<1/(n_{2}(n_{2}-1))<1/\sqrt{2}
, and
\sqrt{2n_{1}}/(n_{1}-1)\leq 2
.

The bound to put into Hoeffding’s inequality are now

\begin{split}|b_{n_{1}n_{2}}|&=\frac{2}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}}{(n_{1}-1)n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}}\\
&\leq\frac{2}{m_{i}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})\,\chi(\mathbf{k}_{i})}\end{split}

(81)

so

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pr}\left[|\tilde{Y}-Y|\geq\epsilon\right]&\leq 2\exp\left[\frac{-\epsilon^{2}\ell^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}|b_{n_{1}n_{2}}|}\right]\\
&\leq 2\exp\left[\frac{-\epsilon^{2}\ell^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}|b|}\right]\\
&\leq\delta.\end{split}

(82)

Therefore, the results from Section
A
hold for this choice of basis.

B.2
Weyl algebra

The Weyl algebra can be constructed from the generators
Z_{d}
and
X_{d}
, defined

\begin{split}Z_{d}\left|k\right\rangle_{d}&=\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\pi/d}\left|k\right\rangle_{d},\\
X_{d}\left|k\right\rangle_{d}&=\left|k\oplus 1\right\rangle_{d},\end{split}

(83)

where the
\oplus
is addition modulo
k
.
The particulars of the construction of the characteristic function now depend on the dimension of the system in question, and there are multiple rules one can follow.
Much work has focussed on a Galois field theory construction that valid only for
d
being prime, see for example Ref.
Vourdas 1997
.
The prime construction has an advantage in that a set of
d+1
mutually orthogonal bases (MUBs) exist for such dimensions.

Here we will focus on the construction from Gross in Ref.
Gross 2006
, here we are concerned with
d
being odd.
Note that this is informationally complete for any dimension as long as we only consider the characteristic function.
Ref.
Gross 2006
focussed on generating a discrete Wigner function for prime-dimensional systems through a discrete Fourier transform from the characteristic function; although this only defines a prime-dimensional Wigner function, the characteristic function can define a system of any dimension.

Given the generators in Eq. (
83
), the Weyl algebra can by fully described by the Weyl operator

\mathcal{D}_{d}(p,q)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\pi pq/d)Z^{p}X^{q}.

(84)

The characteristic function for one qudit can then be generated by taking

\chi_{\rho}(p,q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\Tr\left[\rho\;\mathcal{D}_{d}(p,q)^{\dagger}\right].

(85)

This is an informationally complete function, where the state can be recovered

\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{q,p}\chi_{\rho}(p,q)\mathcal{D}_{d}(p,q).

(86)

The fidelity can then be calculated

F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\Tr\left[\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\right]=\sum\chi_{\rho_{1}}\overline{\chi}_{\rho_{2}}

(87)

Note that in this case that the operators are now non-Hermitian and the characteristic function may have complex values.
Of course, since the
\mathcal{D}_{d}(p,q)
operators are not observables, in practice the actual measurement of the characteristic function will be constructed from eigenstates of the given operator.
This is the advantage of choosing
p
-prime for this method, as we are guaranteed to have a set of
d+1
MUBs
Wootters and Fields 1989
.

Note that we also need to consider complex numbers.
Because of this, Eq. (
63
) may need extra care, which gives using the
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
a preferential advantage.
However, The advantage of using the Weyl algebra is that
\mathcal{N}(k_{i})=\sqrt{d}
for all
i
, resulting in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i})=d
in Eq. (
63
).

Appendix C
Generalized Euler Angles and the construction of the two-qutrit strategy

We begin by defining the generalized
\Su\!\left(d\right)
Euler angles, that can be created from a subset of the generators of
\mathfrak{su}\!\left(d\right)
, that can be found in App.
B.1
.
The definition of the Euler angles presented here will differ from more standard approaches, where we will concentrate only on the generalization of a
\hat{\sigma}^{y}
rotations.
Where we define

\mathsf{Y}^{d}_{k,1}=\mathrm{i}\left|k\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|-\mathrm{i}\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|

(88)

Note that we only require this subset of the operators for the states we want to generate.

The rotation operator is then defined

\mathcal{R}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{q=d}^{2}\prod_{p=2}^{q}\exp((-1)^{1-\delta_{q,d-1}}\mathrm{i}\mathsf{Y}^{d}_{p,1}\theta_{p-1+j(q)}),

(89)

where
j(q)=0
when
q=d
and
j(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d-q}(d-i)
otherwise.
Applying this to the highest-weighted state then yields the coherent state

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\left|d-1\right\rangle,

(90)

where

\langle k\left|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rangle_{d}=\begin{cases}\sin\theta_{d-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\cos\theta_{i},&\text{if k = 0},\\
\cos(\theta_{d-1})&\text{if $k=d-1$},\\
\sin\theta_{d-1}\sin\theta_{k}\prod_{i=k}^{d-1}\cos\theta_{i},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(91)

Note that only the first
d-1
degrees of freedom have any effect on the highest-weighted state.
This is ideal as this is the number of degrees of freedom needed to fully define the elements of the target state in the Schmidt decomposition.
The other degrees of freedom will, however, have an effect on the orthogonal states, this allows us to optimize over these extra degrees of freedom to find a more efficient strategy.

The elements of the coherent state then coincide with the elements of the target state in the Schmidt decomposition,
\left|\psi\right\rangle
, when we choose

\theta_{k}=\begin{cases}\arctan\left(\frac{\langle 2\left|\psi\right\rangle}{\langle 1\left|\psi\right\rangle}\right)&\text{if $k=1$},\\
\arccos(\langle d-1\left|\psi\right\rangle)&\text{if $k=d-1$},\\
\arcsin\left(\frac{\langle k\left|\psi\right\rangle}{N(k,d)}\right)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(92)

where

N(k,d)=\prod_{i=k+1}^{d-1}\sin(\theta_{i}).

(93)

We can now redefine the target state in terms of this coherent state structure, where

\left|\psi\right\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\left\langle{k}|{\boldsymbol{{\theta}}}\right\rangle\left|kk\right\rangle.

(94)
