Asymptotic expansion of the invariant measure for Markov-modulated ODEs at high frequency
Pierre Monmarché, Édouard Strickler

Abstract
We consider time-inhomogeneous ODEs whose parameters are governed by an underlying ergodic Markov process. When this underlying process is accelerated by a factor ε − 1 \varepsilon^{-1} , an averaging phenomenon occurs and the solution of the ODE converges to a deterministic ODE as ε \varepsilon vanishes. We are interested in cases where this averaged flow is globally attracted to a point. In that case, the equilibrium distribution of the solution of the ODE converges to a Dirac mass at this point. We prove an asymptotic expansion in terms of ε \varepsilon for this convergence, with a somewhat explicit formula for the first order term. The results are applied in three contexts: linear Markov-modulated ODEs, randomized splitting schemes, and Lotka-Volterra models in random environment. In particular, as a corollary, we prove the existence of two matrices whose convex combinations are all stable but such that, for a suitable jump rate, the top Lyapunov exponent of a Markov-modulated linear ODE switching between these two matrices is positive.

High-frequency expansion

The process

Proposition 1
.

There exist two families of operators

•

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\vvvert P_{t}^{(k)}f\right\vvvert_{j}\leq C\left\vvvert f\right\vvvert_{j+2k}\,.

(6)

•

For all
k,j\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist

and all

,

\left\vvvert S_{t}^{(k)}f\right\vvvert_{j}\leq Ce^{-\gamma t}\left\vvvert f\right\vvvert_{j+2k}\,.

(7)

•

For all

,

,

, there exists

such that for all

•

The first operators are given by

(12)

In dimension 1, it is possible to get an alternative expression for the function

The goal is now to let

Assumption 3

1.

The Markov process

2.

For all

A(s)

3.

The averaged matrix
\bar{A}
is irreducible in the sense that for all

\bar{A}_{i_{k-1},i_{k}}>0

The fact that the matrices are cooperative implies that

\theta(t)=z(t)/\rho(t)\in\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d},x_{1}+\dots+x_{d}=1\}

\rho^{\prime}(t)=(\mathbbm{1}\cdot A\left(\sigma(t/\varepsilon)\right)\theta(t))\rho(t)\,,\qquad\theta^{\prime}(t)=F_{\sigma(t/\varepsilon)}(\theta(t))

It is proven in

]
that, under Assumption

admits a unique invariant measure

on

and that for all initial conditions

, almost surely,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\rho(t)}{t}=\Lambda_{\varepsilon},

3.2
Richardson extrapolation for randomized splitting schemes

In
[
1
]
, in order to approximate the solution of an ODE of the form

\dot{y}(t)=\bar{F}(y(t))\,,

(26)

the vector field
\bar{F}
is splited as
\bar{F}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}F_{k}(x)
, where each flow
\dot{x}=F_{k}(x)
can be solved exactly. More precisely, the two main examples of interest in
[
1
]
(to which we refer for details and motivations) are the so-called Lorenz-96 model and a finite-dimensional Galerkin projection of the vorticity formulation of 2D Navier-Stokes. The trajectory
y
is then approximated by a discrete time scheme of the form

x^{\varepsilon}_{n+1}=\Phi^{1}_{\tau_{1}/\varepsilon}\circ\dots\circ\Phi^{N}_{\tau_{N}/\varepsilon}(x^{\varepsilon}_{n})

where
\Phi^{i}_{t}
is the flow associated to
F_{i}
at time
t
and
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{N}
are independent random variables distributed according to the standard exponential law. More precisely, for a fixed
t>0
,
y(t)
is approximated by
x_{n}^{\varepsilon}
with
n\varepsilon=t
, for a small
\varepsilon
. Notice that this does not enter directly our framework. However, consider the Markov-modulated ODE

\dot{x}_{\varepsilon}(t)=F_{\sigma(Nt/\varepsilon)}(x_{\varepsilon}(t))\,,

where
\sigma
is a cyclic chain on
\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
with rate
1
(i.e. it is a standard Poisson Process modulo
N
). We can take the times
\tau_{i}
to define the jump times of
\sigma
, and thus
x_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is exactly
x_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon S_{n}/N)
where
S_{n}
is the
(nN)^{th}
jump time of
\sigma
. In particular, if
n\varepsilon=t
, assuming that the vector fields
F_{k}
are bounded,

|x_{\varepsilon}(t)-x_{n}^{\varepsilon}|=|x_{\varepsilon}(t)-x_{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon S_{n}/N\right)|\leqslant\max_{k\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}\|F_{k}\|_{\infty}|\varepsilon S_{n}/N-t|\,,

and

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\varepsilon S_{n}}{N}-t\right|^{2}=\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{N}=\frac{t\varepsilon}{N}\,.

In other words,
(x_{\varepsilon}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
(which can be sampled exactly if
(x_{n}^{\varepsilon})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can) can be seen as an alternative variation of the scheme of
[
1
]
.

The convergence of
x_{n}^{\varepsilon}
to
y(t)
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
for a fixed
t=n\varepsilon
, or more precisely of the corresponding Markov transition operators, is stated in
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
, which is thus similar to our Proposition
1
at order
n=0
. Notice that, a priori, the ODE (
26
) is in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
is proven by restricting the study on the orbit of the process, which is assumed to be bounded (this is Assumption 1 of
[
1
]
), so that our results apply in this context.

The higher order expansion in Proposition
1
enables the use of a Richardson extrapolation to get better convergence rates: for instance we obtain, for a fixed
t>0
and
f\in\mathcal{A}
,

\left|f\left(\varphi_{t}(x)\right)-2\mathbb{E}\left(f(x_{\varepsilon/2}(t))\right)+\mathbb{E}\left(f(x_{\varepsilon}(t))\right)\right|\leqslant C\|f\|_{3}\varepsilon^{2}\,,

for some constant
C>0
.

3.3
Species invasion rate in a two species Lotka-Volterra model in random environment

We consider a model of two species in interaction, in a random environment, described by the following system of Lotka - Volterra equations:

\begin{cases}\frac{dX(t)}{dt}=X(t)\left(a_{10}^{\sigma(t)}-a_{11}^{\sigma(t)}X(t)+a_{12}^{\sigma(t)}Y(t)\right)\\
\frac{dY(t)}{dt}=Y(t)\left(a_{20}^{\sigma(t)}+a_{21}^{\sigma(t)}X(t)-a_{22}^{\sigma(t)}Y(t)\right)\end{cases}

(27)

We assume that
a^{s}_{11},a^{s}_{22}>0
(intraspecific competition), the other parameters can be positive or negative. The coefficients
a_{10}^{s}
and
a_{20}^{s}
represents the growth rates of species 1 and 2 respectively, while the coefficients
a_{12}^{s}
and
a_{21}^{s}
models the interaction between the two species. If for example
a_{10}^{s},a_{21}^{s}>0
while
a_{20}^{s},a_{12}^{s}<0
, we get a prey - predator system (species 1 being the prey, and species 2 the predator). Assumption
1
is also enforced in this whole section.

In the absence of species 2 and after accelerating the dynamics of
\sigma
by a factor
1/\varepsilon
, species 1 evolves according to a logistic equation in random environment:

\frac{dX(t)}{dt}=X(t)\left(a_{10}^{\sigma(t/\varepsilon)}-a_{11}^{\sigma(t/\varepsilon)}X(t)\right)\,.

(28)

We prove in the Annex the following result:

Lemma 1
.

Assume that
p_{0}:=\inf_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{10}^{s}}{a_{11}^{s}}>0
,
p_{1}:=\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{10}^{s}}{a_{11}^{s}}<+\infty
and
\inf_{s\in\mathcal{S}}a_{s}^{11}>0

(X(t),\sigma(t/\varepsilon))_{t\geqslant 0}

given by (
28

and admits a unique stationary distribution
\mu_{\varepsilon}
on
E
. Moreover,
(X(t),\sigma(t/\varepsilon))
converges in law to
\mu_{\varepsilon}
when
t
goes to infinity.

The stationary distribution
\mu_{\varepsilon}
represents the population of species 1 at equilibrium in the absence of species 2. The invasion growth rate of species 2 when species 1 is at equilibrium is then given by

\Lambda_{y}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{S}}(a_{20}^{s}+a_{21}^{s}x)\mu_{\varepsilon}(dx,ds)\,.

\frac{1}{t}\ln(Y(t))\simeq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\left[a^{\sigma(u/\varepsilon)}_{20}+a^{\sigma(u/\varepsilon)}_{21}X(u)\right]du,

28

[
4

that, indeed, the sign of
\Lambda_{y}(\varepsilon)
gives information on the local behaviour of (
27

\{y=0\}
. Denoting by

\pi

\displaystyle=(\partial_{t}-L_{\varepsilon})\left(P_{t}^{\varepsilon}f-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{k}P_{t}^{(k)}f+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{k}S_{\frac{t}{\varepsilon}}^{(k)}f\right)

The bounds obtained in the previous step yield

for some

. Interpreting

(50)

for all

for some

and some

. Indeed, then, considering a multi-index

\partial_{t}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}R_{n,t}^{\varepsilon}f=L_{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}R_{n,t}^{\varepsilon}f+\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\eta_{t}+\sum_{|\alpha^{\prime}|\leq j}B_{\alpha^{\prime}}\partial_{x}^{\alpha^{\prime}}R_{n,t}^{\varepsilon}f,

where, for all multi-index

(52)

acts coefficient-wise on

for some

depending on

based on Equation (

E_{r}

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\vvvert G_{t}\right\vvvert_{\infty}\leq C\varepsilon^{n}\left\vvvert f\right\vvvert_{j+1+2n}

for some

. Since this holds for all

, this concludes the proof by induction of (
50

R_{n,t}^{\varepsilon}f=R_{n+1,t}^{\varepsilon}f+\varepsilon^{n+1}P_{t}^{(n+1)}f+\varepsilon^{n+1}S_{\frac{t}{\varepsilon}}^{(n+1)}f,

we get that, for all

4.2
Feynman Kac formula

Lemma 2
.

Let
L
be a Markov generator on a set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. For
n\geqslant 1
,
consider
f_{t}(z)=(f_{1,t}(z),\dots,f_{n,t}(z))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
z\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,
t\geqslant 0
solution of

\partial_{t}f_{t}(z)=Lf_{t}(z)+A_{t}(z)f_{t}(z)+c_{t}(z),\quad z\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\ t\geqslant 0\,,

(53)

where
(t,z)\mapsto A
is a bounded
n\times n
matrix field,
(t,z)\mapsto c
is a bounded vector field and
Lf_{t}
is to be understood component-wise. Then, for all
t\geqslant 0
and
z\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,

f_{t}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left(E_{t}f_{0}(Z_{t})\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(E_{r}c_{t-r}(Z_{r})\right)\text{d}r\,,

where
(Z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a Markov process associated to
L
with
Z_{0}=z
and
(E_{r})_{r\in[0,t]}
is the solution of the
n\times n
matrix-valued random ODE
\partial_{r}E_{r}=E_{r}A_{t-r}(Z_{r})
with
E_{0}=I_{n}
, provided this is well-defined almost surely.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
,
z\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Then
(Z_{r},E_{r})_{r\geqslant 0}
given in the statement of the lemma is a time-inhomogeneous Markov process with generator

\mathcal{L}_{r}g(z,e)=eA_{t-r}(z)\cdot\nabla_{e}g(z,e)+Lg(z,e),

and thus, considering the vector-valued function
h(r,z,e)=ef_{t-r}(z)
, we end up with

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}\left(E_{r}f_{t-r}(Z_{r})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}\left(h(r,Z_{r},E_{r})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\partial_{r}+\mathcal{L}_{r}\right)h(r,Z_{r},E_{r})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(E_{s}\left(-\partial_{r}+A_{t-r}+L\right)f_{t-r}(Z_{r})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left(E_{r}c_{t-r}(Z_{r})\right).

Using that
E_{0}=I
, conclusion follows from

f_{t}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left(E_{0}f_{t}(Z_{0})\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(E_{t}f_{0}(Z_{t})\right)-\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}\left(E_{r}f_{t-r}(Z_{r})\right)\text{d}r\,.

∎
