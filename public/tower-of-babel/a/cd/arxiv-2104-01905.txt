Abstract
Closed form expressions to calculate the exponential of a general multivector (MV) in Clifford geometric algebras (GAs) 𝐶𝑙 p , q \mathit{Cl}_{p,q} are presented for n = p + q = 3 n=p+q=3 . The obtained exponential formulas were applied to find exact GA trigonometric and hyperbolic functions of MV argument. We have verified that the presented exact formulas are in accord with series expansion of MV hyperbolic and trigonometric functions. The exponentials may be applied ro solve GA differential equations, in signal and image processing, automatic control and robotics.



Keywords
: Clifford (geometric) algebra, exponentials of
Clifford numbers,

computer-aided theory.

1
Introduction

In Clifford geometric algebra (GA), the exponential functions with
the exponent represented by a simple blade are well-known and used
widely. In case of complex algebra (the complex number algebra is
isomorphic to
\mathit{Cl}_{0,1}
GA) the exponential can be expanded into
a trigonometric function sum by de Moivre’s theorem. In 2D vector
space, including Hamilton quaternions, the exponential is similar
to de Moivre’s formula multiplied by exponential of the scalar
part
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
In 3D vector spaces only special cases are known. Particularly,
when the square of the blade is equal to
\pm 1
, the exponential
can be expanded in de Moivre-type sum of trigonometric or
hyperbolic functions, respectively. However, general expansion in
a symbolic form in case of 3D algebras
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
,
\mathit{Cl}_{1,2}
,

\mathit{Cl}_{2,1}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{0,3}
, when the exponent is a general
multivector (MV), is more difficult. The paper
[
3
]

considers general properties of functions of MV variable for
Clifford algebras
n=p+q\leq 3
, including the exponential
function, for this purpose using the unique properties of a
pseudoscalar
I
in
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{1,2}
algebras.
Namely, the pseudoscalar in these algebras commutes with all
MV elements and
I^{2}=-1
. This allows to introduce more general
functions, in particular, the polar decomposition of all
multivectors. A different approach to resolve the problem is to
factor, if possible, the exponential into product of simpler
exponentials, for example, in the polar
form
[
5
,
6
]
. General bivector exponentials
in
\mathit{Cl}_{4,1}
algebra were analyzed in
[
7
]
. In
coordinate form, the difficulty is connected with the appearance
of both trigonometric and hyperbolic functions simultaneously in
the expansion of exponentials as well as the mixing of scalar
coefficients from different grades.

In this paper a different approach which presents the exponential
in coordinates and which is more akin to construction of de
Moivre formula was applied. Namely, to solve the problem the GA
exponential function is expanded into sum of basis elements
(grades) using for this purpose the computer algebra
(
Mathematica
package). Although in this way obtained
final formulas are rather cumbersome, however, their analysis
allows to identify the obstacles in constructing the GA
coordinate-free formulas. In the paper presented formulas
can be also applied to general purpose programming languages such
as Fortran, C
++
or Python.

In Sec.
2
the notation is introduced. The final
exponential formulas in the coordinates are presented in
Secs.
3
-
5
in a form of theorems. The
particular cases that follow from general exponential formulas are
given in Sec.
6
. Relations of GA
exponential to GA trigonometric and hyperbolic functions is
presented in Sec.
7
. Possible application of the
exponential function in solving spinorial Pauli-Schrödinger
equation are given in Sec.
8
. In
Sec.
9
we discuss further development of the
problem. In the Appendix we compare finite GA series of
trigonometric functions with the exact formulas that follow from
exponential.

Notation

In the inverse degree lexicographic ordering
used in this paper, the general MV in GA space is
expanded in the orthonormal basis

\{1,\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3},\mathbf{e}_{12},\mathbf{e}_{13},\mathbf{e}_{23},\mathbf{e}_{123}\equiv I\}
,
where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
are basis vectors,
\mathbf{e}_{ij}
are the bivectors and

I
is the pseudoscalar.
*
*

*

An increasing order
of digits in basis elements is used, i.e., we write
\mathbf{e}_{13}

instead of
\mathbf{e}_{31}=-\mathbf{e}_{13}
. This convention is reflected in
opposite signs of some terms in formulas.
The number of
subscripts indicates the grade. The scalar is a grade-0 element,
the vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}
are the grade-1 elements, etc. In the
orthonormalized basis the geometric products of basis vectors
satisfy the anticommutation relation,

\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}=\pm 2\delta_{ij}.

(2.1)

For
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{0,3}
algebras the squares of basis vectors,
correspondingly, are
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}=+1
and
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}=-1
, where

i=1,2,3
. For mixed signature algebras such as
\mathit{Cl}_{2,1}
and

\mathit{Cl}_{1,2}
we have
\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}=\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}=1
,
\mathbf{e}_{3}^{2}=-1
and

\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}=1
,
\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{e}_{3}^{2}=-1
, respectively. The general MV of
real Clifford algebras
\mathit{Cl}_{p,q}
for
n=p+q=3
can be expressed
as

\begin{split}\mathsf{A}=&\,a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+a_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+a_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}+a_{12}\mathbf{e}_{12}+a_{23}\mathbf{e}_{23}+a_{13}\mathbf{e}_{13}+a_{123}I\\
\equiv&\,a_{0}+\mathbf{a}+\mathcal{A}+a_{123}I,\end{split}

(2.2)

where
a_{i}
,
a_{ij}
and
a_{123}
are the real
coefficients, and
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+a_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+a_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}
and

\mathcal{A}=a_{12}\mathbf{e}_{12}+a_{23}\mathbf{e}_{23}+a_{13}\mathbf{e}_{13}
is, respectively, the
vector and bivector.
I
is the pseudoscalar,
I=\mathbf{e}_{123}
.
Similarly, the exponential
\mathsf{B}
will be denoted as

\begin{split}\mathsf{B}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{A}}=&\,b_{0}+b_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+b_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+ba_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}+b_{12}\mathbf{e}_{12}+b_{23}\mathbf{e}_{23}+ba_{13}\mathbf{e}_{13}+a_{123}I\\
\equiv&\,b_{0}+\mathbf{b}+\mathcal{B}+b_{123}I.\end{split}

(2.3)

We start from the
\mathit{Cl}_{0,3}
geometric algebra (GA) where the
expanded exponential in the coordinate form has the simplest MV
coefficients.

Proof
.

The simplest way to prove the above formula

\exp(\mathsf{A})
is to check explicitly its defining property:

\left.\frac{\partial\exp(\mathsf{A}t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=1}=\mathsf{A}\exp(\mathsf{A})=\exp(\mathsf{A})\mathsf{A},

(3.3)

where
\mathsf{A}

is assumed to be independent of
t
. Since we have a single MV
that always commutes with itself the multiplications from left and
right by
\mathsf{A}
coincide. After differentiation with
respect to scalar parameter
t
and then setting
t=1
we find that in
this way obtained result indeed is
\mathsf{A}\exp(\mathsf{A})
. To be sure we
also checked the Eq. (
3.3
) by series expansions
of
\exp(\mathsf{A}t)
up to order 6 with symbolic coefficients and up
to order 20 with random integers using for this purpose the
Mathematica
package
[
8
]
.

7
Relations of the exponential to GA trigonometric and hyperbolic functions

The geometric product is non-commutative. However, any two GA
functions of the same argument, for example
f(\mathsf{A})
and

g(\mathsf{A})
, that can be expanded in the Taylor series, commute:

f(\mathsf{A})g(\mathsf{A})=g(\mathsf{A})f(\mathsf{A})
. Indeed, for any chosen finite
series expansion we have a product of two polynomials of a single
variable
\mathsf{A}
. Since the MV always commutes with itself, it
follows, that a well behaved functions of the same MV argument
commute too.

As known, the elementary trigonometric and hyperbolic functions
in GA are defined by exactly the same series expansions as their
commutative counterparts

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. GA hyperbolic
functions can be defined for an arbitrary MV similarly as
ordinary functions, however, the GA trigonometric functions, in
general, only exist for real GAs that are characterized by a
commutative pseudoscalar and property

I^{2}=-1

[
3
]
, i.e., only for Clifford algebras

\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{1,2}
. In order to define them for the algebras

\mathit{Cl}_{0,3}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{2,1}
we have to introduce imaginary unit, i.e.
in these algebras trigonometric functions exist only when they are
complexified.

As known, scalar trigonometric and hyperbolic functions are linked
up through the imaginary unit
\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}
, for example

\cos(\mathrm{i}x)=\cosh(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For MV functions
similar relations also exist if apart from
\mathrm{i}
the pseudoscalar

I
is included:

\cosh(I\mathsf{A})=\cos(\mathrm{i}I\mathsf{A}),\quad\sinh(I\mathsf{A})=-\mathrm{i}\sin(\mathrm{i}I\mathsf{A}).

(7.1)

Also, trigonometric and hyperbolic functions of MV
\mathsf{A}
can be
expressed through the exponentials, if one remembers that
I^{2}=-1

for
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{1,2}
, and
I^{2}=+1
for
\mathit{Cl}_{0,3}
and

\mathit{Cl}_{2,1}
,

\begin{split}&\sin\mathsf{A}=\begin{cases}&\frac{I}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-I\mathsf{A}}-\mathrm{e}^{I\mathsf{A}})\quad\text{\ for}\quad\mathit{Cl}_{3,0},\mathit{Cl}_{1,2},\\
&\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}I\mathsf{A}}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}I\mathsf{A}})\quad\text{for}\quad\mathit{Cl}_{0,3},\mathit{Cl}_{2,1},\\
\end{cases}\\
&\cos\mathsf{A}=\begin{cases}&\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-I\mathsf{A}}+\mathrm{e}^{I\mathsf{A}})\quad\text{\ for}\quad\mathit{Cl}_{3,0},\mathit{Cl}_{1,2},\\
&\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}I\mathsf{A}}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}I\mathsf{A}})\quad\text{for}\quad\mathit{Cl}_{0,3},\mathit{Cl}_{2,1},\\
\end{cases}\\
\end{split}

(7.2)

where
I\mathsf{A}
is the dual to multivector
\mathsf{A}
. As suggested at
the beginning of this section the hyperbolic GA functions do not
require imaginary unit, thus we have

\sinh\mathsf{A}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{A}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathsf{A}}),\quad\cosh\mathsf{A}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{A}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathsf{A}}).

(7.3)

From the above formulas follows various relations between GA
trigonometric and hyperbolic functions that are analogues of the
well-known scalar relations, for example, a few of them are
given below:

\begin{split}&\cos^{2}\mathsf{A}+\sin^{2}\mathsf{A}=1,\quad\cosh^{2}\mathsf{A}-\sinh^{2}\mathsf{A}=1,\\
&\sin(2\mathsf{A})=2\sin\mathsf{A}\cos\mathsf{A}=2\cos\mathsf{A}\sin\mathsf{A},\\
&\cos(2\mathsf{A})=\cos^{2}\mathsf{A}-\sin^{2}\mathsf{A}.\end{split}

(7.4)

Also, it should be noted that GA sine and cosine functions as well
as hyperbolic GA sine and cosine functions commute:

\sin\mathsf{A}\cos\mathsf{A}=\cos\mathsf{A}\sin\mathsf{A}
and

\sinh\mathsf{A}\cosh\mathsf{A}=\cosh\mathsf{A}\sinh\mathsf{A}
.

Apart from relations between the exact hyperbolic sine-cosine
functions and the exponential given in Eq. (
7.3
) we
can write an exact formula for hyperbolic tangent as well (see
the beginning in this Section),

\tanh\mathsf{A}=\sinh\mathsf{A}\cosh^{-1}\mathsf{A}=\cosh^{-1}\mathsf{A}\sinh\mathsf{A}\,,

(7.5)

and likewise for
\coth\mathsf{A}
functions. After substitution of
exponential formulas (
7.3
) into the right hand side
of (
7.5
) we obtain general
\tanh\mathsf{A}
. However, as a
first step in deriving exact formula for
\tanh\mathsf{A}
at first
one must compute the exact inverse of hyperbolic cosine. How to
compute the inverse MV in case of general Clifford algebras is
described in
[
11
,
12
,
15
]
. For
this purpose the adjoint and determinant of MV may be needed,

\mathsf{A}^{-1}=\frac{{\mathop{\textrm{Adj}}}(\mathsf{A})}{\Det(\mathsf{A})},\qquad{\mathop{\textrm{Adj}}}(\mathsf{A})\,\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}\,{\mathop{\textrm{Adj}}}(\mathsf{A})=\Det(\mathsf{A}).

(7.6)

Here Det is the determinant of MV, which in 3D can be computed
with the help of involutions
[
9
,
11
]

\Det(\mathsf{A})=\mathsf{A}\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}\wideparen{\mathsf{A}}\wideparen{\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}},

(7.7)

where
\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}
denotes reverse MV, and

\wideparen{\mathsf{A}}
is grade inverse of MV
\mathsf{A}
. Although
the computation of inverse of general 3D MV is straightforward,
the resulting symbolic expression is too large to be presented
here. However, in the Appendix we shall make use of numerical
calculations by
Mathematica
for this purpose.

8.1
Time-dependent GA equation with a simple Hamiltonian

The spinor evolution under the action of magnetic field is
considered. The field (vector) is assumed to consist of two parts,
constant parallel to
\mathbf{e}_{3}
and rotating in
\mathbf{e}_{12}
plane with
angular frequency
\omega
,

\mathbf{B}(t)=B_{0}\mathbf{e}_{3}+B_{1}\big(\mathbf{e}_{1}\cos(\omega t)+\sigma\mathbf{e}_{2}\sin(\omega t)\big)

(8.1)

The sign number
\sigma
determines the rotation sense. When

\sigma=-1
the field of amplitude
B_{1}
is rotating clockwise
and when
\sigma=1
anticlockwise.

The time-dependent Pauli-Schrödinger equation in the presence
of homogeneous
\mathbf{B}(t)
field for a spinor
\psi
, which is the
MV of
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
algebra, is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma I\mathbf{B}(t)\psi,

(8.2)

where
\gamma
is the gyromagnetic ratio. This GA equation can be
solved by rotating frame method (in physics it is called the
rotating wave approximation) if the following rotor

S=\exp(-\sigma\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega t/2)
is applied to
Eq. (
8.2
). Multiplying from left by reverse of
S
and
then differentiating with respect to time, we find

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}(\widetilde{S}\psi)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}\psi+\widetilde{S}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}&=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\sigma\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega\widetilde{S}\psi+\widetilde{S}\gamma I\mathbf{B}(t)\psi\big)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\sigma\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega+\gamma I\widetilde{S}\mathbf{B}(t)S\big)(\widetilde{S}\psi).\end{split}

(8.3)

When
\sigma=\pm 1
, the product

\widetilde{S}\mathbf{B}(t)S=B_{0}\mathbf{e}_{3}+B_{1}\cos(\sigma\omega t)(\mathbf{e}_{1}\cos\omega t+\sigma\mathbf{e}_{2}\sin\omega t)+B_{1}\sin(\sigma\omega t)(\mathbf{e}_{2}\cos\omega t+\sigma\mathbf{e}_{1}\sin\omega t)
reduces to
time-independent field
\widetilde{S}\mathbf{B}(t)S=B_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+B_{0}\mathbf{e}_{3}
.
Therefore the GA differential equation becomes

\frac{\mathrm{d}(\widetilde{S}\psi)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega+\mathbf{e}_{23}\omega_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega_{0}\big)(\widetilde{S}\psi),

(8.4)

where
\omega_{0}=\gamma B_{0}
,
\omega_{1}=\gamma B_{1}
. Since
Eq. (
8.4
) has a constant MV coefficient its solution is
the exponential function,

(\widetilde{S}\psi)=\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega+\mathbf{e}_{23}\omega_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega_{0})\big)(\widetilde{S}\psi)_{0}.

(8.5)

At
t=0
the initial MV is
(\widetilde{S}\psi)_{0}=\psi(0)
.
Multiplying from left by
S
and expanding the second exponential
according to Subsec.
6.1
, finally, we have

\psi=\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma\mathbf{e}_{12}\omega t/2}\Big(\cos\frac{\alpha t}{2}+\alpha^{-1}(\mathbf{e}_{23}\omega_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{12}\big(\omega_{0}+\sigma\omega)\big)\sin\frac{\alpha t}{2}\Big)\psi(0),

(8.6)

where

\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}\big((\sigma\omega+\omega_{0})^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
.

Equation (
8.6
) describes the evolution of the total spinor
which is a mixture of up and down spinor states

\psi=\psi_{\uparrow}+\psi_{\downarrow}
and normalized,

\psi\widetilde{\psi}=1
. In GA the up and down spinor eigenstates
are, respectively, described by basis scalar
1
and basis
bivector
\mathbf{e}_{13}

[
16
]
. We shall assume that the spinor
initially is in the up eigenstate,
\psi(0)=\psi_{\uparrow}=1
.
Then, the evolution of the state
\psi_{\downarrow}
is given by
projecting
\psi
onto the down eigenstate

\mathbf{e}_{13}

[
16
]
. The result is

\psi_{\downarrow}=-\langle\mathbf{e}_{13}\psi\rangle+\langle\mathbf{e}_{13}\psi\mathbf{e}_{12}\rangle\mathbf{e}_{12}=\alpha^{-1}\Big(\sin\frac{\alpha t}{2}\big(\mathbf{e}_{12}\cos\frac{\alpha t}{2}-\sin\frac{\sigma\alpha t}{2}\big)\Big)

(8.7)

The probability to detect the down spin at the moment
t
then is

P_{\downarrow}(t)=\psi_{\downarrow}\widetilde{\psi}_{\downarrow}=\left(\frac{\omega_{1}\sin\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}t\sqrt{(\sigma\omega+\omega_{0})^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}\Big)}{\sqrt{(\sigma\omega+\omega_{0})^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}}\right)^{2}.

(8.8)

At resonance, when
\sigma\omega+\omega_{0}=0
(for clockwise
rotation
-\omega+\omega_{0}=0
, and for anticlockwise rotation

\omega-\omega_{0}=0
) the probability oscillates,

P_{\downarrow}(t)=\sin^{2}(\omega_{1}t/2)=\sin^{2}(\gamma B_{1}t/2)
,
with the frequency that depends on exciting field amplitude
B_{1}
.
In quantum mechanics such rotating field induced oscillations
between up and down states are called Rabi oscillations. If
magnetic field
\omega_{0}=\gamma B_{0}
changes very slowly
(adiabatically) in the interval
T>>2\pi/\omega
, then in the
vicinity of resonance the probability peaks related with Rabi
oscillations will appear, Fig.
1
. The moment of
the appearance depends on the rotation sense via sign
number
\sigma
. The observed asymmetry between (a) and (b) panels
in Fig.
1
is the manifestation of selection
rules for quantum transition under action by rotating magnetic
field.
