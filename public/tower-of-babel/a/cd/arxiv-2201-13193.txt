Mathematical analysis of a thermodynamically consistent reduced model for iron corrosion
Clément Cancès, Claire Chainais-Hillairet, Benoît Merlet, Federica Raimondi, Juliette Venel

Abstract
. We are interested in a reduced model for corrosion of iron, in which ferric cations and electrons evolve in a fixed oxide layer subject to a self-consistent electrostatic potential. Reactions at the boundaries are modeled thanks to Butler-Volmer formulas, whereas the boundary conditions on the electrostatic potential model capacitors located at the interfaces between the materials. Our model takes inspiration in existing papers, to which we bring slight modifications in order to make it consistent with thermodynamics and its second principle. Building on a free energy estimate, we establish the global in time existence of a solution to the problem without any restriction on the physical parameters, in opposition to previous works. The proof further relies on uniform estimates on the chemical potentials that are obtained thanks to Moser iterations. Numerical illustrations are finally provided to highlight the similarities and the differences between our new model and the one previously studied in the literature.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
General framework of the study

At the request of the French nuclear waste management agency ANDRA, investigations are carried out in order to evaluate the long-term safety of the geological repository of radioactive waste. The context is the following: the waste is confined in a glass matrix, placed into steel canisters and then stored in a claystone layer at a depth of several hundred of meters. The long-term safety assessment of the geological repository has to take into account the degradation of the carbon steel
used for the waste overpacks and the cell disposal liners, which are in contact with the claystone formation. The study of the corrosion processes that arise at the surface of the steel canisters and of the cell disposal liners takes part in the modelling and simulation of the repository. This has motivated the introduction of the Diffusion Poisson Coupled Model (DPCM) by Bataillon
et al.
in
[
2
]
.

The DPCM describes the oxidation of
a metal covered by an oxide layer (magnetite) in contact with the claystone. It consists in a system of drift-diffusion equations on the density of charge carriers (electrons,
F\!e^{3+}
cations and oxygen vacancies) coupled with a Poisson equation on the electric potential. Boundary conditions of Robin-Fourier type are prescribed by the electrochemical reactions and the potential drops at the interfaces with the claystone and with the metal.
They induce additional couplings in the system and involve numerous physical parameters. The system also includes equations governing the motion of the boundaries.

Up to now, no existence result has been established for the general DPCM
[
2
]
. However, a few mathematical results on some simplified versions have been obtained. The well-posedness of a two-species (electrons and ferric cations) model set on a fixed domain has been established in
[
11
]
for the stationary case and in
[
12
]
for the evolutive case. Some numerical methods for the simulation of DPCM have been introduced in
[
3
]
and implemented in the code CALIPSO. The numerical experiments proposed in
[
2
,
3
]
have highlighted the long-time behaviour of the model: after a transient period, the size of the oxide layer stays constant, while both interfaces move at the same velocity and the densities of charge carriers as the electrical potential have stationary profiles. This corresponds to a traveling wave solution to DPCM. The existence of a traveling wave solution has been proved first on a simplified version of DPCM in
[
10
]
. Recently in
[
4
]
, the existence is also established for the original DPCM thanks to a computer-assisted proof.

The description of the transport of charge carriers in the oxide layer proposed by the DPCM is similar to the transport of charge carriers in a semiconductor device as proposed by van Roosbroeck
[
29
]
. The differences come first from some reaction terms due to recombination-generation in the continuity equations and mainly from the boundary conditions, which are of Dirichlet-Neumann type in the semiconductor setting. The mathematical analysis of the drift-diffusion-Poisson system of equations for semiconductor devices is the subject of a series of seminal papers by Gajewski and Gröger
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. The strategy of proof proposed by Gajewski and Gröger relies strongly on the underlying variational structure of the model, in agreement with thermodynamics. One keypoint is a convex functional which can be interpreted as a free energy from the viewpoint of thermodynamics . This strategy has been further used in many papers dealing for instance with electro-reaction-diffusion processes
[
16
,
20
,
21
]
, spin-polarized drift-diffusion models
[
17
]
, electronic models for solar cells
[
18
,
19
]
,…

In order to obtain some mathematical results for the DPCM with a similar strategy, it is crucial to understand the impact of the moving boundary equations and of the boundary conditions on the structure of the system. Actually, the derivation of the DPCM proposed in
[
2
]
does not rely on energetic considerations. As a consequence, its thermodynamic stability is unclear and neither a satisfactory well-posedness result nor the assessment of the long-time behavior of the system have been established so far.
This paper was thought as a first step towards the derivation and the analysis of a thermodynamically consistent DPCM model. Here, we restrict our attention to a simplified setting with only two species (electrons and iron cations), the goal being to get consistent couplings
between the boundary conditions and the bulk equations. Since oxygen vacancies are not considered in our simplified setting, the boundaries are fixed throughout this paper. The derivation of a thermodynamically consistent counterpart to the full DPCM model will be the purpose of a future work.

Standard arguments show that if

is a pair of smooth functions, then
(u,v)
solves (

)–(

expresses both the relations

for

as well as the boundary value problem (

), (

) relating the electrostatic potential

to the charge density. On the other hand,

is a weak formulation of the advection diffusion equations (

), (

), (

). Moreover, if

solves (

), testing

against the functions of the form

for

, we obtain

\partial_{t}\left(u_{0}-\sum_{i=1,2}z_{i}u_{i}\right)=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,1)).

Using the initial condition, we get that any solution
(u,v)
to (

) satisfies

u_{0}=\sum_{i=1,2}z_{i}u_{i}+\rho_{\text{\rm hl}}.

The aim of this paper is to prove an existence result for problem (

3.
Free energy and a priori estimates

Before going into the details of the proof of Theorem
2.2
, we give in this section an explicit expression for the total free energy, assuming that a solution to problem (

P

) exists. Then we show that our model is thermodynamically consistent in the sense that the free energy of a solution decreases over time. Furthermore, this leads to some
a priori
estimates for the solution which turn to be crucial in the proof.
To this end we follow some ideas from
[
16
]
. Define

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(v)=\int_{0}^{v}e_{i}(y)dy\quad\textrm{ for }v\in\mathbb{R},\;i=1,2,

and

\psi_{i}(u)=\int_{e_{i}(0)}^{u}e^{-1}_{i}(z)dz\quad\textrm{for $u\in[0,\overline{u}_{1}]$ if $i=1$ and $u\geq 0$ if $i=2$.}

For the specific choice of nonlinearities of (H
2
), this provides

\begin{cases}\varphi_{1}(v)=\log(1+e^{v})-\log 2,\\
\psi_{1}(u)=u\log u+(1-u)\log(1-u)+\log 2,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{cases}\varphi_{2}(v)=e^{v}-1,\\
\psi_{2}(u)=u\log u-u+1.\end{cases}

The non-negative convex functions
\psi_{i}
satisfy
\psi_{1}(1/2)=\psi_{2}(1)=0
and are extended by
+\infty
outside of their domain of definition.

We define the Landau free energy of
v\in V
by

\Phi(v)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}v_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1,2}{\overline{u}}_{i}\varphi_{i}(v_{i})\right)dx+\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left(\dfrac{\beta^{\Gamma}}{2}v_{0}^{2}-f^{\Gamma}v_{0}\right)(\Gamma).

Its conjugate, the Helmholtz free energy, is defined for
u\in V^{*}
by

\Psi(u)=\sup_{v\in V}\left\{\left<u,v\right>-\Phi(v)\right\},

where
\left<\,,\,\right>
denotes the standard scalar product in
L^{2}(0,1)
.

Note that if
\varphi_{i}(v_{i})
,
i=1,2
, are not in
L^{1}
, the values
\Phi(v)
and
\Psi(u)
are interpreted as
+\infty
. Moreover,
\Phi
and
\Psi
turn out to be strictly convex functionals and
\Phi(0)=0
, hence for every
v\in V
the subdifferential of
\Phi
contains at most one element. More precisely one checks that
\partial\Phi=\{Ev\}
.
Then standard computations show that

(22)

\Psi(u)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}|\partial_{x}v^{*}_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1,2}{\overline{u}}_{i}\psi_{i}\left(\dfrac{u_{i}}{{\overline{u}}_{i}}\right)\right)dx+\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[\dfrac{\beta^{\Gamma}}{2}|v^{*}_{0}|^{2}\right](\Gamma),

with
v_{0}^{*}
solving the Poisson equation with Robin-Fourier boundary conditions

\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}v^{*}_{0}\partial_{x}\tilde{v}\,dx+\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[\left({\beta^{\Gamma}}v^{*}_{0}-f^{\Gamma}\right)\tilde{v}\right](\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}u_{0}\tilde{v}\,dx,\quad\forall\tilde{v}\in V.

The Helmholtz free energy of an isolated system is expected to be a Lyapunov functional. Here, we have fluxes across the interfaces
\Gamma\in\{0,1\}
which may contribute positively to the variations of
\Psi(u)
. In order to get an isolated system (and hence a Lyapunov functional), then one introduces some very elementary model for the charge carriers leaving the oxide. More precisely, we assign the energy
\xi_{1}^{0}
(resp.
\xi_{1}^{1}
) to each unit of cations entering the solution (resp. the metal) from the oxide. Similarly the energy of one unit of electrons leaving the oxide is set to
\xi_{2}^{\Gamma}
,
\Gamma\in\{0,1\}
. Therefore the free energy associated to elements leaving the oxide layer to the solution and the metal are respectively defined by

\Psi^{\Gamma}(t)=\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[(J_{i}\cdot\nu^{\Gamma})\xi_{i}^{\Gamma}\right](\Gamma)d\tau,\qquad\Gamma\in\{0,1\}.

Finally,
the total free energy
\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}
is given by

(23)

\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(t)=\Psi(u(t))+\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\Psi^{\Gamma}(t).

We prove in the following proposition the decay of the total free energy
\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}
over time. This estimate, which encodes the second principle of thermodynamics, is the key
a priori
estimate on which our analysis builds. Here and in what follows, the space
H^{1}(0,1)
is equipped with the norm

(24)

{\|w\|}_{H^{1}(0,1)}=\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w|^{2}dx+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}|w(\Gamma)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Proposition
3.1
.

Assume that
(H
1
)–(H
5
)
hold and let
(u,v)
be a solution of problem (

P

). For
0\leq s\leq t
,

\displaystyle\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(t)-\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(s)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{i}(v_{i})|\partial_{x}\xi_{i}|^{2}dxd\tau

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{s}^{t}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(v_{i})g_{i}^{\Gamma}(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\right](\Gamma)d\tau.

In particular, there holds

(25)

\Psi^{\text{\rm tot}}(t)\leq\Psi^{\text{\rm tot}}(s)\leq\Psi(0)<\infty.

As a consequence, there exists
c_{T}>0
depending on the data of the continuous problem and on
T
such that

(26)

\|v_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(0,1))}+\sum_{i=1,2}\left\|\psi_{i}\left(\dfrac{u_{i}}{\overline{u}_{i}}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(0,1))\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\leq c_{T}.

In particular this implies that
u_{i}/\overline{u}_{i}
belongs to the domain of
\psi_{i}
for almost every
(x,t)
, i.e.
0\leq u_{1}\leq\overline{u}_{1}
and
u_{2}\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
(u,v)
be a solution to problem (

P

). Then for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

u(t)=Ev(t)\in\partial\Phi(v(t)),

which is equivalent to

v(t)\in\partial\Psi(u(t))

since
\Phi
and
\Psi
are the Legendre transform of each other. Thus, for
0\leq s\leq t
,

\Psi(u(t))-\Psi(u(s))=\int_{s}^{t}\left<\dot{u}(\tau),v(\tau)\right>d\tau=-\int_{s}^{t}\left<A(v(\tau),v(\tau)),v(\tau)\right>d\tau

and consequently due to the definition (
23
) of
\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}

(27)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(t)-\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(s)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{i}(v_{i})|\partial_{x}\xi_{i}|^{2}dxd\tau

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{s}^{t}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(v_{i})g_{i}^{\Gamma}(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\right](\Gamma)d\tau\leq 0,

thanks to (
7
). Also note that
\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(t)
is finite for every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
when
(u,v)
is a solution of (

P

) since
\Psi^{\textrm{tot}}(0)=\Psi(u^{\textrm{in}})
is finite thanks to Assumption (H
5
), then we obtain the validity of (
25
).
Then one readily checks (calculations are detailed in the time discrete case in Section

, see (
47
)) that
the Helmholtz free energy corresponding to the oxide layer only remains bounded, but not uniformly w.r.t. time,
i.e.

\Psi(u(t))\leq c_{T}
for
t\in[0,T]
.
This implies (
26
) in view of (
22
).
∎

4.
Existence result for a regularized problem (

P

)

In order to obtain the existence result for problem (

P

) stated in Theorem
2.2
, we first introduce a regularized problem. It is denoted by (

P
M

) and it is obtained from (

) by cutting off all the nonlinearities applied to the chemical potentials

at a certain level

P
M

P

In the next section, for such a solution, several a priori estimates will be proved independently on the level

M

sufficiently large.

This technique was originally introduced in a series of seminal papers by Gajewski and Gröger
[
13

14

15

Let
M>0
be a fixed parameter chosen large enough to ensure

(28)

M\geq\max_{i=1,2}\|{v}_{i}^{in}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}.

We introduce the usual truncation function at level
M
given by

T_{M}(z)=\max(-M,\min(M,z))=\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\begin{cases}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ z&\text{if }|z|\leq M,\\
\penalty\ \pm M&\text{if }\pm z>M.\end{cases}

We also define by

and

(29)

\left<A_{M}(w,v),\tilde{v}\right>=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1,2}\sigma_{i}(T_{M}w_{i})\partial_{x}\xi_{i}\partial_{x}\tilde{\xi}_{i}\,dx+\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}w_{i})g_{i}^{\Gamma,\mu}(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\tilde{\xi}_{i}\right](\Gamma),

where
\xi_{i}=z_{i}v_{0}+v_{i}
and

,

.
For technical reasons that will appear later on in the proof, we also have to modify the nonlinear Robin boundary conditions (

and

(yet another parameter to be tuned later on),

denotes the following approximation of

where the functions

are the ones introduced in (H

on the interval

and

Then our regularized problem writes

\displaystyle u\in H^{1}_{\textrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+};V^{*}),\quad v\in L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+};V),\\[8.53581pt]

The next result provides the existence of (at least) one solution, still denoted by

,
to problem (

Step 1.

Let us fix
T\in[0,+\infty)
a finite but arbitrary time horizon, and set

. For

,
we define the time step

and the time intervals

.

Given a Banach space

, we denote by

the space of functions

that are constant on each of the intervals

,
1\leq j\leq n
. If

for

by

. We introduce two mappings
\Delta_{n}
and

from

into itself defined by:

(\Delta_{n}u)^{j}=\dfrac{1}{k_{n}}(u^{j}-u^{j-1}),\qquad(\tau_{n}u)^{j}=u^{j-1},\qquad 1\leq j\leq n,

where
u^{0}={u^{\textrm{in}}}
is the initial datum. We consider the discrete version of problem (

P
M

) corresponding to the time step

given by

(
P_{M,n}
)

\Delta_{n}u_{n}+A_{M}(\tau_{n}v_{n},v_{n})=0,\qquad u_{n}=E_{M}v_{n},\quad v_{n}\in B_{n}(I;V).

It can be written as

(34)

\dfrac{1}{k_{n}}(u_{n}^{j}-u_{n}^{j-1})+A_{M}(v_{n}^{j-1},v^{j}_{n})=0,\quad u^{j}_{n}=E_{M}v^{j}_{n},\quad 1\leq j\leq n,\quad u_{n}^{0}=u^{\text{\rm in}}.

Thanks to arguments similar to those of Remark
2.1

The following lemma is about the well-posedness of problem

.

Lemma
4.2
.

Under assumptions
(H
1
)–(H
5
)
, for any

, for every

, there exists a unique solution
(u_{n},v_{n})
to the problem

Proof.

We use some known results on monotone operators
[
5
, Corollaire 17]
(see also
[
23
,
6
]
). Let us fix

and define the operator

by

If such operator

is strongly monotone,

if there exists

such that

\left<F(v)-F(w),v-w\right>\geq\alpha\|v-w\|^{2},\qquad\forall\,v,w\in V,

then all the equations (

for given

(and

). This gives the unique solvability of our problem

.

Let us check the strong monotonicity of

. Let

, we compute

\displaystyle\left<F(v)-\right.

\displaystyle F(w),\left.v-w\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{k_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\lambda^{2}|\partial_{x}(v_{0}-w_{0})|^{2}+\sum_{i=1,2}{\overline{u}}_{i}(v_{i}-w_{i})[e_{i}(T_{M}v_{i})-e_{i}(T_{M}w_{i})]\bigg)\,dx

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[\beta^{\Gamma}(v_{0}-w_{0})^{2}\right](\Gamma)+\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1,2}\sigma_{i}(T_{M}y_{i})|\partial_{x}(z_{i}(v_{0}-w_{0})+(v_{i}-w_{i}))|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\Big\{r_{i}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}y_{i})(z_{i}(v_{0}-w_{0})+(v_{i}-w_{i}))

\displaystyle\phantom{a}\hskip 128.0374pt\times\left[g_{i}^{\Gamma,\mu}((z_{i}v_{0}+v_{i})-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})-g_{i}^{\Gamma,\mu}((z_{i}w_{0}+w_{i})-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\right]\Big\}(\Gamma).

\displaystyle\left<F(v)-F(w),v-w\right>\geq\;

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{k_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda^{2}|\partial_{x}(v_{0}-w_{0})|^{2}dx+\dfrac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[\beta^{\Gamma}(v_{0}-w_{0})^{2}\right](\Gamma)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{i}(T_{M}y_{i})|\partial_{x}(z_{i}(v_{0}-w_{0})+(v_{i}-w_{i}))|^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}y_{i})|z_{i}(v_{0}-w_{0})+(v_{i}-w_{i})|^{2}\right](\Gamma).

Therefore, we obtain

Testing (

), and since

Moreover,
\Phi_{M}\leq\Phi
hence
\Psi_{M}\geq\Psi
, and
\Psi_{M}(u^{\text{in}})=\Psi(u^{\text{in}})

\Psi(u_{n}^{J})+\sum_{j=1}^{J}k_{n}\left<A_{M}(v_{n}^{j-1},v_{n}^{j}),v_{n}^{j}\right>\leq\Psi(u^{\text{in}}),\qquad J\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Due to the boundary conditions,

might be negative. To circumvent this difficulty and as in Section

, we introduce the total free energy taking into account the energy of the charge carriers that left the domain over time. For

, define

\mathcal{J}^{j}_{n}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}k_{n}\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v_{n,i}^{\ell-1})g_{i}^{\Gamma,\mu}(\xi_{n,i}^{\ell}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\xi_{i}^{\Gamma}\right](\Gamma),\qquad 1\leq j\leq n,

then one checks, using the definition of

, (

) and the definition of

that

\displaystyle\Psi_{M}^{\text{\rm tot},j}-\Psi_{M}^{\text{\rm tot},j-1}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{M}(u_{n}^{j})-\Psi_{M}(u_{n}^{j-1})+k_{n}\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v_{n,i}^{j-1})g_{i}^{\Gamma,\mu}(\xi_{n,i}^{j}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\xi_{i}^{\Gamma}\right](\Gamma)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:est.PMn.05}}}{{\leq}}-k_{n}\left<A_{M}(v_{n}^{j-1},v_{n}^{j}),v_{n}^{j}\right>

\displaystyle\qquad+k_{n}\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{i}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v_{n,i}^{j-1})g_{i}^{\Gamma,\mu}(\xi_{n,i}^{j}-\xi_{i}^{\Gamma})\xi_{i}^{\Gamma}\right](\Gamma)

\displaystyle\;\Psi(u^{\text{in}}),

\displaystyle\;\Psi(u^{\text{in}}),

. As a consequence, by choosing the cutoff level sufficiently large, this will allow to prove that
(u,v)
is a solution to problem (

P

) too.

The proof is based on the Moser-Alikakos iteration technique, cf.
[
25
,
1
]
, which consists in establishing successively
L^{p}
-norms, for increasing values of
p
, of some appropriate functions of the chemical potentials.

We will first show an upper bound for
v_{2}
, then both lower bounds for
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, and finally an upper bound for
v_{1}
in three separated theorems. The starting point of each bootstrapping procedure will be provided by estimating an appropriate
L^{2}
-bound to initialize the method.

In each part of the section, we use the identity

(73)

\sigma_{i}(T_{M}v_{i})\partial_{x}v_{i}\partial_{x}u_{i}=d_{i}(\partial_{x}u_{i})^{2}

which holds almost everywhere on
(0,1)\times I
and for
i=1,2
. Indeed, on the one hand, from the chain rule and the identitities
u_{i}=\bar{u}_{i}e_{i}(T_{M}v_{i})
and
\sigma_{i}(z)=d_{i}\bar{u}_{i}e_{i}^{\prime}(z)
we have

(74)

\sigma_{i}(T_{M}v_{i})\partial_{x}[T_{M}v_{i}]=d_{i}\partial_{x}u_{i}.

On the other hand, since
\partial_{x}u_{i}=\partial_{x}[T_{M}v_{i}]=0
almost everywhere on
\{|v_{i}|\geq M\}
and
\partial_{x}[T_{M}v_{i}]=\partial_{x}v_{i}
almost everywhere else, there holds

(75)

\partial_{x}[T_{M}v_{i}]\partial_{x}u_{i}=\partial_{x}v_{i}\partial_{x}u_{i}.

Multiplying (
74
) by
\partial_{x}u_{i}
and using (
75
) yields (
73
).

In the sequel,
c
will denote different positive constants independent of
M
.

5.1.
Upper bound for
v_{2}

In order to get an upper bound for the chemical potential
v_{2}
, we first derive an upper bound for the density
u_{2}
in the following theorem.

Theorem
5.1
.

Under assumptions
(H
1
)–(H
5
)
, let
(u,v)
a solution to problem (

P
M

). There exists a constant
c>0
independent on
M
such that

\|u_{2}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq c\quad\forall t\in I.

Proof.

Let
p\geq 2
and
w=(u_{2}-k)_{+}
, where
k>0
will be fixed later. We use
(0,0,pw^{p-1})
as a test function in (

P
M

). We have
\dot{u}_{2}\in L^{2}(I,[H^{1}(0,1)]^{*})
and
w\in L^{2}(I,H^{1}(0,1))\cap L^{\infty}
, so, using a smoothing argument and classical properties of Sobolev spaces, there holds

\left<\dot{u}_{2};pw^{p-1}\right>_{(H^{1})^{*},H^{1}}=\left<\dot{w};pw^{p-1}\right>_{(H^{1})^{*},H^{1}}=\dfrac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx\right]\qquad\text{in }L^{1}(I).

Therefore
\left<\dot{u}_{2};pw^{p-1}\right>+\left<A_{M}(v,v);pw^{p-1}\right>=0
reads,

(76)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx=-Q_{1}-Q_{2}-Q_{3},

with

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})\partial_{x}v_{2}\partial_{x}(pw^{p-1})\,dx,\qquad\quad Q_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})z_{2}\partial_{x}v_{0}\partial_{x}(pw^{p-1})\,dx,

\displaystyle Q_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{2}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v_{2})g_{2}^{\Gamma,\mu}(\xi_{2}-\xi_{2}^{\Gamma})pw^{p-1}\right](\Gamma).

In order to estimate the boundary term
Q_{3}
, we note that
\xi_{2}-\xi_{2}^{\Gamma}=v_{2}(\Gamma)-[\xi_{2}^{\Gamma}-z_{2}v_{0}(\Gamma)]
and we define successively

v_{2}^{*,\Gamma}=\xi_{2}^{\Gamma}-z_{2}v_{0}(\Gamma),\qquad u_{2}^{*,\Gamma}={\overline{u}}_{2}e_{2}(T_{M}v_{2}^{*,\Gamma})\quad\text{ and }\quad w^{*,\Gamma}=(u^{*,\Gamma}_{2}-k)_{+}.

We can then write

\displaystyle Q_{3}

\displaystyle=p\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{2}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v_{2})g_{2}^{\Gamma,\mu}(v_{2}-v_{2}^{*,\Gamma})\left(w^{p-1}-(w^{*,\Gamma})^{p-1}\right)\right](\Gamma)

\displaystyle\qquad+p\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r_{2}^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v_{2})g_{2}^{\Gamma,\mu}(v_{2}-v_{2}^{*,\Gamma})(w^{*,\Gamma})^{p-1}\right](\Gamma)=:Q_{31}+Q_{32}.

Due to the monotonicity of the involved functions, it results
Q_{31}\geq 0
. Let us notice that, due to (
32
),
v_{2}^{*,\Gamma}
is bounded independently of
M
. Therefore, it is possible to choose
k
(independent of
M
) such that
w^{*,\Gamma}=0
for
\Gamma\in\{0,1\}
and we get
Q_{32}=0

Hence,

(77)

-Q_{3}\leq 0.

For
Q_{1}
, we use (
73
) and the fact that
\partial_{x}w\partial_{x}u_{2}=(\partial_{x}w)^{2}
to get

(78)

Q_{1}=p(p-1)d_{2}\int_{0}^{1}(w^{(p-2)/2}\partial_{x}w)^{2}\,dx=\dfrac{4d_{2}(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx.

We treat the term
Q_{2}
as follows.
Differentiating
w^{p-1}
and using (
74
), we get

Q_{2}=p(p-1)z_{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})\partial_{x}w\partial_{x}v_{0}w^{p-2}\,dx.

From
u_{2}=\bar{u}_{2}e_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})
and the definitions
e_{2}(z)=e^{z}
and
\sigma_{2}(z)=d_{2}\bar{u}_{2}e^{z}
,

\sigma_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})=d_{2}\bar{u}_{2}\exp(\ln(u_{2}/\bar{u}_{2}))=d_{2}u_{2},

so that we get

Q_{2}=d_{2}p(p-1)z_{2}\int_{0}^{1}u_{2}w^{p-2}\partial_{x}v_{0}\partial_{x}w\,dx.

In any case we have
0\leq u_{2}\leq k+w
, so we have
|Q_{2}|\leq Q_{21}+Q_{22}
where

\displaystyle Q_{21}

\displaystyle=d_{2}p(p-1)|z_{2}|\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-1}|\partial_{x}w|\,dx

\displaystyle Q_{22}

\displaystyle=kd_{2}p(p-1)|z_{2}|\,\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w|\,dx.

Taking into account that
\partial_{x}v_{0}
is bounded uniformy w.r.t.
M
(see Lemma
4.5
), that
p-1=(p/2-1)+p/2
and that
pw^{p/2-1}\partial_{x}w=2\partial_{x}w^{p/2}
, and applying the Young inequality, we get

\displaystyle Q_{21}

\displaystyle\leq d_{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+d_{2}(p-1)^{2}|z_{2}|^{2}\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx

(79)

\displaystyle\leq d_{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+cd_{2}\,p^{2}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx.

Arguing similarly for the term
Q_{22}
and using additionally the Young inequality:

ab\leq\dfrac{p-2}{p}a^{\frac{p}{p-2}}+\dfrac{2}{p}b^{\frac{p}{2}}\quad\text{ for }a,b\geq 0,\,p>2,

we have

(80)

Q_{22}\leq d_{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+k^{2}d_{2}(p-1)^{2}|z_{2}|^{2}\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-2}\,dx\\
\leq d_{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+d_{2}c\,p^{2}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx+d_{2}c\,p.

By collecting (
76
)–(
80
), we obtain

(81)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx+d_{2}\dfrac{2p-4}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx\leq d_{2}c_{\ref{cte:3}}p^{2}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx+d_{2}cp,\quad\forall p>2.

where
c_{3}
and the generic constant
c
depends neither on
M
nor on
p
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
c_{\ref{cte:3}}\geq 1/16
.
Now we combine the following Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality:

\|\chi\|^{3}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq c\|\chi\|^{2}_{L^{1}(0,1)}\left(\|\chi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|\partial_{x}\chi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\right)^{1/2},\qquad\forall\chi\in H^{1}(0,1),

together with the Young inequality to get that, for
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
,

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\chi^{2}\,dx\leq(1-\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{1}\chi^{2}\,dx\leq\dfrac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\chi|\,dx\right)^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}\chi|^{2}\,dx.

We apply it with
\chi=w^{p/2}
,
\varepsilon=(p-2)/(c_{\ref{cte:3}}p^{3})\in(0,1/2)
since
p\geq 4
and
c_{\ref{cte:3}}\geq 1/16
. Then, by the choice of
\varepsilon
, from (
81
) we deduce that (still for
p\geq 4
)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx\leq\dfrac{c\,p^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}w^{p/2}\,dx\right)^{2}+c_{2}p\leq c\,p^{3}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}w^{p/2}\,dx\right)^{2}+1\right].

We integrate the last inequality with respect to the time variable and choose
k
such that
w(0)=0
(
i.e.

k\geq\sup u_{2}^{in}
) getting

\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}(t)\,dx\leq c\,p^{3}\left[\sup_{s\in I}\left(\int_{0}^{1}w^{p/2}(s)\,dx\right)^{2}+1\right]\quad\forall t\in I,

that is,

(82)

\|w(t)\|^{p}_{L^{p}(0,1)}\leq c\,p^{3}\left[\sup_{s\in I}\|w(s)\|^{p}_{L^{p/2}(0,1)}+1\right]\quad\forall t\in I\text{ and }\forall p>2.

Taking inspiration in
[
18
]
, we set for
m\in\mathbb{N}
:

b_{m}=\max\left(1,\sup_{s\in I}\|w(s)\|^{2^{m}}_{L^{2^{m}}(0,1)}\right).

Choosing
p=2^{m}
in (
82
), we obtain

b_{m}\leq c\,2^{3m}b^{2}_{m-1}

and by induction, we get

b_{m}\leq c^{(2^{m-1}-1)}\,(2^{3})^{(2^{m}-m-1)}\,b_{1}^{2^{m-1}}\quad\forall m\geq 1.

Taking the power
1/2^{m}
leads to the estimate:

\|w(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2^{m}}(0,1)}\leq c^{1-1/2^{m-1}}(2^{3})^{2-(m+1)/2^{m-1}}\max\left(1,\sup_{s\in I}\|w(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}\right)\quad\forall t\in I.

Sending
m
to
+\infty
we find

(83)

\|w(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq c\left[\sup_{s\in I}\|w(s)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+1\right]\quad\forall t\in I.

The last step consists in obtaining a
L^{2}
-estimate of the norm of
w(s)
. To this aim, we can consider inequality (
81
) in the limit
p\searrow 2
:

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}\,dx\leq c\left(1+\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}\,dx\right).

We apply the Gronwall’s lemma to have

\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}(s)\,dx\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}(0)\,dx+1\right)e^{cs}\leq c,

due to the bounds on the initial data stated in (H
5
), independently of
M
.
Eventually, choosing

k\geq\max\left\{\|u_{2}^{\text{\rm in}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)},|u_{2}^{*,0}|,|u_{2}^{*,1}|\right\},

there exists a positive constant
c
which
does not depend on
M
such that
0\leq u_{2}\leq c
over
I
.
∎

5.2
.

In particular, since
u_{2}=\bar{u}_{2}e_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})=\bar{u}_{2}\exp(T_{M}v_{2})

implies that

.

5.2.
Lower bounds for

With the following theorem we derive some lower bounds for the chemical potentials

and

. For this, we write the chemical potentials and the mobilities appearing in the problem as functions of the corresponding carrier densities. More precisely, the chemical potentials

Theorem
5.3
.

Under assumptions
(H
1
)–(H
5
)
, let
(u,v)
a solution to problem (

P
M

). There exists a constant
c>0
independent on
M
such that

v_{i}\geq-c\quad\text{almost everywhere in }I\times(0,1)\text{ for }i=1,2.

Proof.

Let
p\geq 2
and
w_{i}=(-T_{M}v_{i}-k)_{+}
for
i=1,2
, where
k>0
will be fixed later. Observe that
\nabla w_{i}=-\nabla[T_{M}v_{i}]
almost everywhere on
\{w_{i}>0\}
and
\nabla w_{i}=0
almost everywhere in the rest of the domain.

We do both calculations simultaneously and to lighten the exposition we make the following abuse of notation: we drop all subscripts
i
except when a precise notation is required (we write
w
for
w_{i}
,
u
for
u_{i}
,
\sigma(T_{M}v)
for
\sigma_{i}(T_{M}v_{i})
,
\xi
for
\xi_{i}
,
\xi^{\Gamma}
for
\xi_{i}^{\Gamma}
,
etc
).

We have using the chain rule and
u=\bar{u}e_{i}(T_{M}v)
,

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx=p\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-1}\partial_{t}w\ \,dx=-p\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-1}\partial_{t}[T_{M}v]\ \,dx=-p\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{w^{p-1}}{\bar{u}e_{i}^{\prime}(T_{M}v))}\partial_{t}u.

Since by definition,
\sigma_{i}(y)=d_{i}\bar{u}_{i}e_{i}^{\prime}(z)
, this reads

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx=-pd\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{w^{p-1}}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}\partial_{t}u\,dx

Applying
\dot{u}+A_{M}(v,v)=0
with the test function

\left(0,-\dfrac{pd_{1}w_{1}^{p-1}}{\sigma_{1}(T_{M}v_{1})},0\right)\quad\text{if }i=1,\qquad\left(0,0,-\dfrac{pd_{2}w_{2}^{p-1}}{\sigma_{2}(T_{M}v_{2})}\right)\quad\text{if }i=2,

we have

(84)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx=R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3},

where,

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=pd\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(T_{M}v)\partial_{x}v\partial_{x}\left(\dfrac{w^{p-1}}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}\right)\,dx,\qquad\quad R_{2}=pdz\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(T_{M}v)\partial_{x}v_{0}\partial_{x}\left(\dfrac{w^{p-1}}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}\right)\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle=pd\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[r^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v)g^{\Gamma,\mu}(\xi-\xi^{\Gamma})\dfrac{w^{p-1}}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}\right](\Gamma).

As in the previous proof, we estimate the boundary term
R_{3}
using the fact that
\xi-\xi^{\Gamma}=v(\Gamma)-[\xi^{\Gamma}-zv_{0}(\Gamma)]
. By defining
v^{*,\Gamma}=\xi^{\Gamma}-zv_{0}(\Gamma)
and
w^{*,\Gamma}=(-v^{*,\Gamma}-k)_{+}
, we write

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle=pd\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[\dfrac{r^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v)}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}g^{\Gamma,\mu}(v-v^{*,\Gamma})\left(w^{p-1}-(w^{*,\Gamma})^{p-1}\right)\right](\Gamma)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+pd\sum_{\Gamma\in\{0,1\}}\left[\dfrac{r^{\Gamma}(T_{M}v)}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}g^{\Gamma,\mu}(v-v^{*})(w^{*,\Gamma})^{p-1}\right](\Gamma)

\displaystyle=:R_{31}+R_{32}.

Since
r^{\Gamma}
and
\sigma
are positive and
g^{\Gamma,\mu}
is increasing, we have
R_{31}\leq 0
. By choosing
k
large enough so that
w^{*,\Gamma}=0
for
\Gamma\in\{0,1\}
, we get
R_{32}=0
. Whence it results

(85)

R_{3}\leq 0.

To treat
R_{1}
, we write

(86)

\partial_{x}\left(\dfrac{pw^{p-1}}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}\right)=\dfrac{p(p-1)}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}w^{p-2}\partial_{x}w+\dfrac{p\sigma^{\prime}(T_{M}v)}{[\sigma(T_{M}v)]^{2}}w^{p-1}\partial_{x}w.

The term
R_{1}
then splits as follows:

R_{1}=-dp(p-1)\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w|^{2}\,dx-dp\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\sigma^{\prime}(T_{M}v)}{\sigma(T_{M}v)}w^{p-1}|\partial_{x}w|^{2}\,dx=:R_{11}+R_{12}.

We easily see that

R_{11}=\dfrac{-4d(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx.

Next, for
i=2
,
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}/\sigma_{2}\equiv 1
so that
R_{12}\leq 0
in this case. For
i=1
,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(y)/\sigma_{1}(y)=-\tanh(y/2)
so
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(T_{M}v_{1})/\sigma_{1}(T_{M}v_{1})\geq 0
on
\{w_{1}>0\}=\{T_{M}v_{1}<-k\}
and
R_{12}\leq 0
also in this case. We conclude that

(87)

R_{1}\leq\dfrac{-4d(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx.

Using again (
86
) and
|\sigma^{\prime}/\sigma|\leq 1
, we have for the remaining term
|R_{2}|\leq R_{21}+R_{22}
with

\displaystyle R_{21}

\displaystyle=dp^{2}(p-1)|z|\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-2}|\partial_{x}w|\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{22}

\displaystyle=dp^{2}|z|\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-1}|\partial_{x}w|\,dx.

Writing

R_{21}=2dp(p-1)|z|\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p/2-1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|\,dx.

and using the Cauchy–Schwarz and Young inequalities, we get

\displaystyle|R_{21}|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{d(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+d(p-1)p^{3}|z|^{2}\|\partial_{x}v_{0}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p-2}\,dx

(88)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{d(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+d\,c\,p^{4}\left(1+\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx\right).

Similarly,

(89)

|R_{22}|\leq\dfrac{d(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx+d\,c\,p^{4}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx.

By collecting (
85
), (
87
), (
88
) and (
89
), we obtain

(90)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx+\dfrac{2d(p-1)}{p}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}w^{p/2}|^{2}\,dx\leq d\,cp^{4}\left(1+\int_{0}^{1}w^{p}\,dx\right),\quad\forall p\geq 2.

As in the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we deduce from this estimate that

\|w(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq c\left[\sup_{s\in I}\|w(s)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+1\right]\quad\forall t\in I,

for some constant
c>0
independent of
M
. To initialize the process, we consider (
90
) with
p=2
and apply Gronwall’s lemma to deduce the bound

\sup\{\|w(s)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}:s\in I\}\leq c,

for some constant
c
depending on the
T
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}

-norm of

(bounded thanks to (H

As a conclusion, choosing

k\geq\max\{\|v_{1}^{in}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)},\|v_{2}^{in}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)},|v_{1}^{*,0}|,|v_{1}^{*,1}|,|v_{2}^{*,0}|,|v_{2}^{*,1}|\},

there holds
v_{i}(t)\geq-c
almost everywhere in

and for

with a constant
c>0
independent of

.
∎

6.
Conclusion

6.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

In this paper, we have introduced a new corrosion model inspired from the DPCM introduced in
[
2
]
. The changes we introduced are motivated by the expected compatibility of the model with thermodynamics. Indeed, they permit to establish the decay of a free energy with a control of the dissipation of energy as stated in Section
3
.

The main result of the paper is the existence of a weak solution to this new corrosion model, stated in Theorem
2.2
. In Section
4
, we have first established the existence of a solution to a regularized problem
(P_{M})
. Then lower and upper bounds for the chemical potentials
(v_{i})_{i=1,2}
have been proved in Section
5
, so that we are now able to conclude the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Indeed, to find a solution to problem (

P

) it suffices to show the existence of a solution on any finite time interval of the form
I=[0,T]
,
T>0
. Let us fix such
T>0
and let
M>0
. Proposition
4.1
ensures the existence of a solution
(u,v)
to the regularized problem (

P
M

) on
I

6.2.
Towards a comparison between the DPCM and the new model

Some numerical methods have been introduced for the simulation of the DPCM in
[
3
]
and implemented in the code CALIPSO. For the simplified two-species DPCM on a fixed domain, the numerical scheme is described and analyzed in
[
9
]
. It is based on a Scharfetter-Gummel approximation of the linear drift-diffusion fluxes.
The main change we made when designing the new model (we will call in the sequel vDPCM) relies on the new definition of the fluxes of cations which are now nonlinear with respect to the densities of cations. For the approximation of this new flux, we consider an extension of the SQRA (SQuare-Root Approximation) introduced in
[
22
]
. The scheme will be introduced and analyzed in further work.

In order to compare the two models experimentally, we consider the test case proposed in
[
4
, Appendix A]
, restricted to 2-species. We keep the same values for all the parameters, even if the changes we made should induce new values of parameters for the vDPCM. The
pH
(needed for the computation of some parameters) is set equal to 8.5.
