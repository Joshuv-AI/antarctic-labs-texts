Introduction

Even though Ramsey theory has attracted much attention from its inception almost a century ago, many questions remain elusive. This paper is primarily concerned with lower bounds for (multicolour, hypergraph) Ramsey numbers and the methods that yield them.

2.1
Preliminary results and definitions

Before proceeding to the proof of the theorem, we need several definitions and results.

Splitting indices.

For every natural number
n
, let
d(n)\subset\mathbf{N}
be the unique finite set of integers such that
n=\sum_{i\in d(n)}2^{i}
. In other words,
d(n)
is the set of non-zero indices in the binary representation of
n
. Given a finite set
S
of natural numbers,
|S|\geq 2
, its
first splitting index
is

s(S)=\max\{i\in\mathbf{N}\colon\exists x,y\in S\colon i\in d(x)\setminus d(y)\}.

That is,
s(S)
is the index of the most significant bit where two elements in
S
differ in their binary representation.

The first splitting index partitions
S
into two disjoint, non-empty subsets
S_{0}=\{x\in S\colon s(S)\notin d(x)\}
and
S_{1}=\{x\in S\colon s(S)\in d(x)\}
with
\max S_{0}<\min S_{1}
. This partition is unique and exists as soon as
|S|\geq 2
; we use the notation
(S_{0}\mathbin{\mid}S_{1})
to denote the set
S
where the partition
S_{0}\dot{\cup}S_{1}
is the partition obtained at the first splitting index.

Observation 2.1
.

Let
S=(S_{0}\mathbin{\mid}S_{1})
be a set of at least two natural numbers. If
\emptyset\neq L\subset S_{0}
and
\emptyset\neq R\subset S_{1}
then
s(L\cup R)=s(S)
and
L\cup R=(L\mathbin{\mid}R)
.

Caterpillars.

We define a family
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{N})
, whose elements we call
caterpillars
(due to their resemblance with the caterpillar trees of graph theory), as follows: The empty set and all singletons are caterpillars. A finite set of 2 or more natural integers, with partition
(S_{0}\mathbin{\mid}S_{1})
, is a caterpillar if and only if at least one of
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
is a singleton and the other is a caterpillar.

For example, the reader may check that
\{0,1,2,4\}=(((\{0\}\mathbin{\mid}\{1\})\mathbin{\mid}\{2\})\mathbin{\mid}\{4\})\in\mathcal{C}
and
\{0,1,2,3\}=(\{0,1\}\mathbin{\mid}\{2,3\})\notin\mathcal{C}
, by considering binary representations of the integers. One can depict the sequence of splits introducing the partitions using a full binary tree (see Figure
1
). Caterpillars are those subsets whose binary tree possesses a dominating root-to-leaf path, that is, the vertex set of the tree can be partitioned into a path and a collection of vertices where each vertex has a neighbour on the path.

4
Subset-colouring of hypergraphs

Recall that, given a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
and
r\in\mathbf{N}
, the integer
k=k(H;r)
is the least number of colours for which there is a colouring
[V]^{r}\to\llbracket k\rrbracket
without any monochromatic hyperedge
e\in E
of size
\geq r+1
, and
k(n,r)=\max\{k(H;r)\colon\chi(H)=n\}
. As noted by Sarkaria
[
17
]
, the problem of determining
k(n,r)
is closely related to the
“classical”
Ramsey problem discussed in previous sections. Indeed, for
n\in\mathbf{N}
, consider the complete
(r+1)
-uniform hypergraph on
rn
vertices
K_{rn}^{(r+1)}
. Clearly,
\chi(K_{rn}^{(r+1)})=n
, and we have

Observation 4.1
.

For any
n,r\in\mathbf{N}
,

k(K_{rn}^{(r+1)};r)=\min\{k\colon r_{k}(r+1;r)>rn\}.

(19)

Proof.

By definition,
k(K_{rn}^{(r+1)};r)
is the least
k
for which there is a colouring
[\llbracket rn\rrbracket]^{r}\to\llbracket k\rrbracket
without any monochromatic
(r+1)
-subset, i.e. such that
rn\nrightarrow(r+1)_{k}^{r}
, or equivalently such that
rn<r_{k}(r+1;r)
.
∎

Our main result in this section is that the values
k(n,r)
and
k(K_{rn}^{(r+1)};r)
are very close to each other:

Theorem 4.2
.

For any positive integers
n
and
r
,

k(K_{rn}^{(r+1)};r)\leq k(n,r)\leq k(K_{rn}^{(r+1)};r)+5.

(20)

Theorem
4.2
will follow from Theorem
4.3
below. From the above Observation and Theorem we conclude that the problem of finding
k(n,r)
is essentially equivalent to the problem of finding the Ramsey number
r_{k^{\prime}}(r+1;r)
for an appropriate value of
k^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
r,k\geq 2
and let
n
be such that
n\nrightarrow(r+1)_{k}^{r}
. If the hypergraph
(V,E)
admits a vertex-colouring
V\to\llbracket n\rrbracket
under which no hyperedge of size
\geq r+1
is monochromatic, then there is an
r
-subset colouring
[V]^{r}\to\llbracket k+f(r)\rrbracket
such that no hyperedge of size
\geq r+1
is monochromatic. Here

f(r)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }r=2,\\
3&\text{if }r=3,\\
4&\text{if }r\geq 4\text{ and }r+1\text{ is prime,}\\
5&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 4.4
.

In Theorem
4.3
we require only hyperedges of size at least
(r+1)
not to be monochromatic. A stronger but more natural condition is for
(V,E)
to have

at most
n
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Let
k=k(K_{rn}^{(r+1)};r)
, so that

and, by monotony,

. Theorem
4.3
then states that any hypergraph with vertex chromatic number

has an
r
-subset colouring with

∎

Remark 4.5
.

The two-sided relation with Ramsey numbers in Theorem

holds only for

. For a specific hypergraph

may be much smaller than

-uniform hypergraph on

. We have

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Let
C_{1}:V\to\llbracket n\rrbracket
be the given proper colouring of
V
.
Let

r
-subset colouring without monochromatic set in

In order to describe the colouring, we need some more definitions.
An
(

n\geq r\geq 2
, we have

\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{Log}^{(r-1)}(rn)}{\operatorname{Log}^{(r)}(rn)}+o(1)\leq k(n,r)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\operatorname{Log}^{(r-1)}(rn)+\frac{5r}{2}+O(1).

(21)

Proof.

Combining Observation
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
, we have

\min\{k\colon r_{k}(r+1;r)>rn\}\leq k(n,r)\leq\min\{k\colon r_{k}(r+1;r)>rn\}+5.

For
rn
large enough, let
k_{0}=\lceil\frac{2}{\alpha}(\log^{(r-1)}(rn)-\beta)+\frac{5r}{2}\rceil\geq\lceil 5r/2\rceil
. By Corollary
1.3
, we have

r_{k_{0}}(r+1;r)>\tower_{r}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot\left(k_{0}-\frac{5r}{2}\right)+\beta\right)\geq rn,

thus
k(n,r)\leq k_{0}+5
.

Similarly, also for large
rn
, let
k_{1}=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{\log^{(r-1)}(rn)}{\log^{(r)}(rn)}\rfloor
. By Theorem
3.2
, we have
r_{k_{1}}(r+1;r)<\tower_{r}(3k_{1}\log k_{1})\leq rn
. By the monotony of
r_{k}(r+1;r)
in
k
(Observation
3.1
), we conclude
k(n,r)>k_{1}
.
∎

Appendix A
Infinitary Ramsey combinatorics

In this section we sketch the modifications needed to extend our stepping-up lemma to infinite cardinals.

We work in Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with choice (ZFC), and we use von Neumann ordinals (so for integers we have
n=\llbracket n\rrbracket
, more generally any ordinal
\alpha=\{\beta\colon\beta<\alpha\}
, and the cardinal of any set
S
is
|S|=\min\{\alpha\text{ ordinal}\colon\alpha\simeq S\}
). As usual we also let
\omega=|\mathbf{N}|=\mathbf{N}
. We write
{+}
and
{\mathbin{\dot{+}}}
for cardinal and ordinal addition respectively.

Let
r\geq 2
be an integer, let
\tau>0
be an ordinal,
\kappa>0
and
\lambda_{\xi}\geq r+1
(for every
\xi<\tau
) be cardinals. Extend the relation
\nrightarrow
previously defined for integers by calling
X\subset\kappa
is monochromatic in colour
\xi<\tau
under
f\colon[\kappa]^{r}\to\tau
if
f\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[X]^{r}\subset\{\xi\}
and writing

\kappa\nrightarrow(\lambda_{\xi})_{\xi<\tau}^{r}

if for some
f\colon[\kappa]^{r}\to\tau
there does not exist
\xi<\tau
and a subset
X\subset\kappa
monochromatic in
\xi
such that
|X|=\lambda_{\xi}
.

Assuming the above relation holds and
r\geq 3
,
[
9
, chap. 24]
prove

2^{\kappa}\nrightarrow((\lambda_{\xi}+1)_{\xi<\tau},(r+2)_{2^{r}+2^{r-1}-4})^{r+1},

which is shorthand for
2^{\kappa}\nrightarrow(\lambda^{\prime}_{\xi}+1)^{r+1}_{\xi<\tau\mathbin{\dot{+}}(2^{r}+2^{r-1}-4)}
where
\lambda^{\prime}_{\xi}
is
\lambda_{\xi}
for
\xi<\tau
and otherwise
r+1
. We can strengthen this result somewhat:

Theorem A.1
.

Let
r\geq 3
be an integer, let
\kappa>0
be a cardinal,
\tau>0
an ordinal and
\lambda_{\xi}\geq r+1
(
\xi<\tau
) cardinals.

\displaystyle\text{If }\kappa\nrightarrow(\lambda_{\xi})_{\xi<\tau}^{r}\text{ then }2^{\kappa}\nrightarrow((\lambda_{\xi}+1)_{\xi<\tau},\underbrace{r+2,\dots,r+2}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta(r)\text{ terms,}\\
\text{each $r+2$}\end{subarray}})^{r+1},

with the integer
\eta(r)\leq 3
defined above in (
1
).

To the best of our knowledge, this improves upon known results in two cases: when
\kappa
and all
\lambda_{\xi}
are finite (corresponding to our previous finite results), and when
r=3
,
\kappa
is infinite,
\lambda_{0}
is infinite and singular and all other
\lambda_{\xi}
are finite. In all other cases, the book by
[
9
]
contains stronger statements.

Proof sketch.

The hypothesis means that there exists
f_{r}\colon[\kappa]^{r}\to\tau
for which none of the colour classes has a monochromatic set of cardinality
\lambda_{\xi}
. Due to the bijection
2^{\kappa}\simeq|\mathcal{P}(\kappa)|
it suffices to exhibit
f_{r+1}\colon[\mathcal{P}(\kappa)]^{r+1}\to\tau\mathbin{\dot{+}}\eta(r)
for which none of the first
\tau
colour classes has a monochromatic set of cardinality
\lambda_{\xi}+1
, and none of the subsequent ones has a monochromatic set of cardinality
r+2
.

To do so define the first discrepancy between two sets of ordinals
x
and
y
with
x\neq y
as

\displaystyle s(x,y)=\min((x\cup y)\setminus(x\cap y)),

which is the well-defined minimum of a non-empty set of ordinals. From there if
\sigma\subset\mathcal{P}(\kappa)
is a set of subsets of
\kappa
and
|\sigma|\geq 2
we define its first splitting index as
s(\sigma)=\min(s\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[\sigma]^{2})
, a well-defined ordinal in
\kappa
.

This gives
essentially
the same partition as before with
\sigma=(\sigma_{0}\mathbin{\mid}\sigma_{1})
where
\sigma_{0}=\{S\in\sigma\colon s(\sigma)\notin S\}
and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma\setminus\sigma_{0}
. It admittedly differs from the previous finitary construction in a minor way, as we now compare
least
significant bits, but when restricted to finite sets it is functionally equivalent. This small modification is necessary; using maxima in the definitions above would make these quantities ill-defined.

A caterpillar is a set of any size without a four-subset of the form
(L\mathbin{\mid}R)
with
|L|=|R|=2
. If
\sigma=(\sigma_{0}\mathbin{\mid}\sigma_{1})
is a caterpillar then at least one of
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{1}
is a singleton.

For any caterpillar
T\subset\mathcal{P}(\kappa)
let
\delta(T)=\{s(x,y)\colon\{x,y\}\in[T]^{2}\}=s\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[T]^{2}
.

We (still) have
[\delta(T)]^{r}=\delta\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[T]^{r+1}
, extending Observation
2.4
. This infinite case is not harder to prove since

[\delta(T)]^{r}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset T\\
|Y|<\omega\end{subarray}}[\delta(Y)]^{r}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset T\\
|Y|<\omega\end{subarray}}\delta\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[Y]^{r+1}=\delta\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}\Big(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset T\\
|Y|<\omega\end{subarray}}{[Y]^{r+1}}\Big)=\delta\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[T]^{r+1}.

Similarly, the equality
|T|=|\delta(T)|+1
still holds for every caterpillar
T
by transfinite induction.

We re-use the same colouring scheme. Let
S\in[\mathcal{P}(\kappa)]^{r+1}
. If
S\in\mathcal{C}
then
\delta(S)\in[\kappa]^{r}
, and we let
f_{r+1}(S)=f_{r}(\delta(S))<\tau
. If
S\notin\mathcal{C}
, then
S
, being finite, has a type, so we simply colour it as before.

As before, let us check the validity of this colouring only for odd
r
.

Let
X\subset\mathcal{P}(\kappa)
be monochromatic in colour
\xi
under
f_{r+1}
. We will show that
|X|\leq\lambda_{\xi}
(if
\xi<\tau
) or
|X|\leq r+1
(for
\xi\in\{\tau,\tau+1\}
). As
r+1\leq\lambda_{\xi}
we may already assume
|X|>r+1
.

First consider the case when
X\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
[X]^{r+1}\subset\mathcal{C}
, and thus
\{\xi\}=f_{r+1}\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[X]^{r+1}=f_{r}\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}(\delta\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[X]^{r+1})<\tau
, so we have to show
|X|\leq\lambda_{\xi}
.

The set
\delta(X)\subset\kappa
is monochromatic in colour
\xi
under
f_{r}
, as

f_{r}\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[\delta(X)]^{r}=f_{r}\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}(\delta\mathbin{\texttt{\textquotedbl}}[X]^{r+1})=\{\xi\}.

This fact implies that
|X|=|\delta(X)|+1<\lambda_{\xi}+1
.

The other case to consider is when
X\notin\mathcal{C}
. We show that then
|X|\leq r+1
regardless of
i
. (Recall that
r+1\leq s_{i}
always.) Suppose to the contrary that
|X|\geq r+2
, and we can assume
|X|=r+2
. Now everything is finite, and works as before.
∎
