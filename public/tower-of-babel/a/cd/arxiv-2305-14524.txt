Some criteria of rational-infinite divisibility for probability laws
A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We study the class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} of distribution functions F F that have the property of rational-infinite divisibility: there exist some infinitely divisible distribution functions F 1 F_{1} and F 2 F_{2} such that F 1 = F ∗ F 2 F_{1}=F*F_{2} . The class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} is a wide natural extension of the fundamental class of infinitely divisible distribution functions. We are interested in general conditions to belong to the class 𝑸 \boldsymbol{Q} in terms of characteristic functions. We obtain criteria that seem to be convenient for the application for some cases, and we illustrate it by several examples in the paper.

1
Introduction

Let
F
be a distribution function on the real line
\mathbb{R}
with the characteristic function

\displaystyle f(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that
F
(and the corresponding law) is called
infinitely divisible
if for every positive integer
n
there exists a distribution function
F_{1/n}
such that
F=(F_{1/n})^{*n}
, where “
*
” denotes a convolution operation, i.e.
F
is
n
-fold convolution power of
F_{1/n}
. It is well known that
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if the characteristic function
f
is represented by
the Lévy–Khinchine formula
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

with some
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and with a bounded non-decreasing
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is assumed to be right-continuous at every point of the real line with the condition
G(-\infty)=0
. We use
x\mapsto\sin(x)
as the “centering function” in the integral in (
1
) following to Zolotarev
[
28
]
and
[
29
]
, but there are also some other variants (see
[
25
]
, p. 38). It is well known that
the spectral pair

(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
. The Lévy–Khinchine formula is very important for probability theory and for it’s applications in related fields (see
[
2
]
and
[
24
]
).

There is a natural extension for the class of infinitely divisible distribution functions. We call a distribution function
F
(and the corresponding law)
rational-infinitely divisible
if there exist some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
F_{1}=F*F_{2}
. This equality may be written in terms of characteristic functions:
f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
denote characteristic functions of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. It is not difficult to show in this case that
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f
admits representation (
1
) with
\gamma=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
and
G=G_{1}-G_{2}
, where
(\gamma_{1},G_{1})
and
(\gamma_{2},G_{2})
denote the spectral pairs of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. Note that the function
G
is of bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}
(it is non-monototic in general), right-continuous at every point and
G(-\infty)=0
. The class of all functions satisfying these properties will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{V}
. The pair
(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
and by
F
as for infinitely divisible distribution functions (see
[
15
]
p. 31-32, or
[
10
]
p. 80). Now suppose that
f
is represented by formula (
1
) with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
. Following Lindner and Sato
[
18
]
, the corresponding distribution function
F
is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
. Due to the Hahn–Jordan decomposition for
G
, it is not difficult to show that
F
is rational-infinitely divisible. The first detailed analysis of the class of such distribution functions on
\mathbb{R}
was performed in
[
17
]
. There are interesting applications in theory of stochastic processes (see
[
18
]
and
[
22
]
), number theory (see
[
21
]
), physics (see
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
), and insurance mathematics (see
[
27
]
).

The class of all rational-infinitely divisible distribution functions we will denote by
\boldsymbol{Q}
. By definition, it is clear that this class contains the class
\boldsymbol{I}
of all infinitely divisible distribution functions and, moreover, it is essentially wider than the latter. So
\boldsymbol{Q}
includes all discrete distribution functions
F
, whose characteristic functions
f
are separeted from zero, i.e.
|f(t)|\geqslant\mu
for some constant
\mu>0
and for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
(see
[
1
]
and
[
12
]
). The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is also contains mixtures of discrete and absolutely continuous distribution functions, where the discrete part is non-zero and its characteristic function is separeted from zero (see
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
). Note that a rational-infinitely divisible distribution functions may have a bounded set of growth points (support of distribution) that is impossible for non-degenerate infinitely divisible distribution functions. There are also some interesting particular sufficient conditions to belong to
\boldsymbol{Q}
(see
[
17
]
). However, we think that the problem about general criteria of rational-infinite divisibility for probability laws is not completely solved. Indeed, for instance, there are no criteria that can be conveniently applied to absolutely continuous laws. In particular, we are not aware any criterion (excluding definitions) that would take a characteristic function of a Gaussian law or a stable law (or another important law) as input and confirm on the output that it is corresponded to (rational-)infinitely divisible distribution function. In this work, we propose some results for this problem. So the obtained criteria yield general methods to determine whether a given characteristic function corresponds to some rational-infinitely or purely infinitely divisible law. These criteria seem to be rather convenient and easy in applications for some cases, and we illustrate it in this paper by special examples, where we test the criteria with characteristic functions of well known absolutely continuous laws. Of course, we don’t asert that the proposed criteria are the best ways to solve the problem. Also it should be noted that we tried to formulate the criteria with priority on generality and hence, dealing with particular examples, their own additional analysis is usually needed. Some techniques for this are demonstrated in the mentioned examples.

Note that the proposal of using of the term “rational-infinitely divisible laws” for the described distributions is an attempt to reflect the key decomposition property (that
F_{1}=F*F_{2}
with some
F_{1},F_{2}\in\boldsymbol{I}
) in the class name similarly to that it was done for the important classes of stable, self-decomposable or infinitely divisible laws. We think that a classification of probability laws based on the existence and various forms of the Levy–Khinchine representation may be not convenient in the perspective. So, in the paper
[
13
]
, it was showed that an
arbitrary
characteristic function
f
admits a special Lévy–Khinchine type representation on segments, where
f(t)\neq 0
. Also there exists probability laws whose characteristic function admit representation (
1
) with spectral function
G
that has an
infinite
total variation on
\mathbb{R}
. It is rather interesting direction, but we will not discuss these questions here.

The statements of the results are given in Section 2, their proofs are provided in Section 4, the examples of application are written in Section 3. Throughout the paper, we use the following notation. We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the sets of positive integers and let
\mathbb{N}_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For every

its total variation on
\mathbb{R}
will be denoted by
\|G\|
and the total variation on
(-\infty,x]
— by
|G|(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. So we have
|G(x)|\leqslant|G|(x)\leqslant\|G\|
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
|G|(+\infty)=\|G\|
. Let
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
be the floor function, i.e. for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\lfloor x\rfloor=k
with integer
k
such that
k\leqslant x<k+1
. Below
\mathds{1}_{a}
with fixed

denotes the following function:
\mathds{1}_{a}(x)=1
for
x\geqslant a
and
\mathds{1}_{a}(x)=0
for
x<a
. We always set for the sums
\sum_{j=n}^{m}a_{j}=0
if
n>m
.

2
General results

We now only assume that
f
is a complex-valued continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
f(0)=1
and
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We define
the distinguished logarithm

\mathrm{Ln}f
, i.e.
\mathrm{Ln}f(t):=\ln|f(t)|+i\mathrm{Arg}f(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where the argument
\mathrm{Arg}f
is uniquely defined on
\mathbb{R}
by continuity with the condition
\mathrm{Arg}f(0)=0
. We next introduce the following function

\displaystyle\psi(t,h):=\mathrm{Ln}f(t)-\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{Ln}f(t-h)+\mathrm{Ln}f(t+h)\bigr),\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

Observe that

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}_{h}\mathrm{Ln}f(t):=-2\psi(t,h)=\mathrm{Ln}f(t-h)+\mathrm{Ln}f(t+h)-2\mathrm{Ln}f(t),\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

is a finite difference of the second order for
\mathrm{Ln}f
. It should be noted that the function
\psi
is often rather simpler and easier handling than
\mathrm{Ln}f
. It is also used for establishing some properties of
f
(see
[
12
]
p. 3,
[
14
]
p. 119, and
[
23
]
p. 34). The following theorem shows that
\psi
contains in fact full information about
f
.

Theorem 1

Suppose that

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)=I(t,h)+\delta(t,h)\quad\text{for any}\quad t\geqslant 0,\,\,h>0,

(4)

where the function
I
admits the following representation

\displaystyle I(t,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\quad\text{for any}\quad t\geqslant 0,\,\,h>0,

(5)

with some
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
, and the function
\delta
satisfies the following condition:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|\delta(kh_{l},h_{l})|\to 0,\quad l\to\infty,\quad\text{for any}\quad t>0,

(6)

where
(h_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
is a fixed sequence of positive reals such that
h_{l}\to 0
,
l\to\infty
, and
n_{t,l}:=\lfloor t/h_{l}\rfloor
,
t>0
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
f
admits representation (
1
) with the function
G
and
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)
. If it is additionally known that
f
is the characteristic function of some distribution function
F
, then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Here and below we use the following convention

\displaystyle\Bigl[\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\Bigr]\biggl|_{x=0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\Bigl[\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\Bigr]=\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}.

(7)

Corollary 1

Suppose that for any
t\geqslant 0
and
h>0
the formula

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)

(8)

holds with some
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
. Then (
6
) obviously holds
(
with
\delta(kh,h):=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
h>0
)
, and
f
admits representation (
1
) with the function
G
and
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)
. If it is additionally known that
f
is the characteristic function of some distribution function
F
, then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.

We note that the representation by formulas (
4
) or (
8
) is not only sufficient for formula (
1
), but it is also necessary.

Remark 1

Suppose that
f
admits representation (
1
) with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
G\in\boldsymbol{V}

(
for instance,
f
is the characteristic function of some distribution function
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}

)
. Then

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\quad\text{for all}\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

Indeed, substituting (
1
) into (
2
), for any
t,h\in\mathbb{R}
we get

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\Bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)

\displaystyle{}-i\,\tfrac{t-h+t+h}{2}\,\gamma-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(e^{itx}\,\tfrac{e^{-ihx}+e^{ihx}}{2}-i\,\tfrac{t-h+t+h}{2}\,\sin(x)\Bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x).

Sometimes it is more convenient to work with the following functions

\displaystyle\varphi_{\pm}(t,h):=e^{\pm 2\psi(t,h)}-1=\biggl(\dfrac{f(t)^{2}}{f(t-h)f(t+h)}\biggr)^{\pm 1}-1,\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

Using these functions, one can formulate the sufficient condition for representation (
1
).

Theorem 2

Suppose that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\pm}(t,h)=\pm 2I(t,h)+\rho(t,h)\quad\text{for any}\quad t\geqslant 0,\,\,h>0,

(11)

where the function
I
admits representation (
5
) with some
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
, the function
\rho
satisfies the following condition:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|\rho(kh_{l},h_{l})|\to 0,\quad l\to\infty,\quad\text{for any}\quad t>0.

(12)

Also suppose that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)\bigl|\varphi_{\pm}(kh_{l},h_{l})\bigr|^{2}\to 0,\quad l\to\infty,\quad\text{for any}\quad t>0,

(13)

where
(h_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
is a fixed sequence of positive reals such that
h_{l}\to 0
,
l\to\infty
, and
n_{t,l}:=\lfloor t/h_{l}\rfloor
,
t>0
. Then
f
admits representation (
1
) with the function
G
and
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)
. If it is additionally known that
f
is the characteristic function of some distribution function
F
, then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Let us return to the function (
2
). We saw that the admission of representation (
9
) is a criterion for rational-infinite divisibility of distribution functions. Let us write it in the following form:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta^{2}_{h}\mathrm{Ln}f(t)}{h^{2}}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\cdot\dfrac{\sin^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{hx}{2}\bigr)}{\bigl(\tfrac{hx}{2}\bigr)^{2}}\cdot(1+x^{2})\,dG(x).

If
h\to 0
then the left-hand side tends to
(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)
, when the latter exists, and the fraction formally dissapears from the integrand in the right-hand side. This idea leads us to the following result.

Theorem 3

a)
Suppose that
f
admits representation (
1
) with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
G\in\boldsymbol{V}

(
for instance,
f
is the characteristic function of some distribution function
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}

)
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+x^{2})\,d|G|(x)<\infty.

(14)

Then for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
there exists
(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)
that admits the following representation

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}(1+x^{2})\,dG(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(15)

b)
Suppose that the formula (
15
) holds with some function
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
satisfying (
14
). Then
f
admits representation (
1
) with the function
G
and
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)
. If it is additionally known that
f
is the characteristic function of some distribution function
F
, then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
.

Theorems
1
–
3
can be applied as methods of determining that a given function
f
is the characteristic for some infinitely divisible law. The following assertion, which we formulate as theorem, is a simple corollary from the previous theorems and the mentioned facts about infinitely divisible laws (see Introduction).

Theorem 4

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
1
, or Corollary
1
, or Theorem
2
, or Theorem
3

b)
hold with some non-decreasing function
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
. Then
f
is a characteristic function of some infinitely divisible distribution function
F
, i.e.
F\in\boldsymbol{I}
.

3
Examples of application

In this section, we illustrate application of Theorem
1
–
4
for characteristic functions of known infinitely divisible probability laws. Note that, of course, we will not use this knowledge in analysis to obtain the conclusions by the criteria. We always start the analysis with a given function
f
without any additional information. We deal with known distributions in order to show clearly the correctness of the results of application.

Example 1.
Here we show that the general results from the previous section can be formally applied to the characteristic function of the Gaussian law:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
(we include the degenerate case
\sigma^{2}=0
).

Since
f(t)\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f(0)=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}f(t)=it\gamma-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

The easiest way is to use Theorem
3
. So
(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\sigma^{2}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. It is easily seen that
(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}
admits representation (
15
) if we set
G(x):=\mathds{1}_{\sigma^{2}}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
(in particular,
G(x)\equiv 0
for the case
\sigma^{2}=0
). The function
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
satisfies (
14
) and it is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)
. By Theorem
3
and Theorem
4
, we conclude that
f
is the characteristic function of an infinitely divisible distribution function
F
with the spectral pair
(\gamma,G)
.

Also it is not difficult to apply another criterion. Let us consider the function
\psi
:

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}f(t)-\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{Ln}f(t-h)+\mathrm{Ln}f(t+h)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle it\alpha-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\biggl(i(t-h)\alpha-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}(t-h)^{2}}{2}+i(t+h)\alpha-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}(t+h)^{2}}{2}\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle it\alpha-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(2it\alpha-\sigma^{2}t^{2}-\sigma^{2}h^{2}\bigr)=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}h^{2}}{2},\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R}.

On account of the convention in (
7
), we can write

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}h^{2}}{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x),\quad t,h\in\mathbb{R},

with
G(x)=\mathds{1}_{\sigma^{2}}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. According to Theorem
1
with Corollary
1
and Theorem
4
, we come to the same conclusion.
\Box

Example 2.
Let us consider the function

\displaystyle f(t)=\dfrac{1}{\cosh(t)},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We first show that
f
is the characteristic function of absolutely continuous probability law. Since
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we may define the function

\displaystyle p(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}f(t)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{e^{-itx}}{\cosh(t)}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\cos(tx)}{\cosh(t)}\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

The last equality is valid, because
\cosh(\cdot)
is an even function. Next, from
[
11
]
, formula
3.981
3., we know that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\cos(as)}{\cosh(\beta s)}\,ds=\dfrac{\pi}{2\beta\cosh\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi a}{2\beta}\Bigr)}\quad\text{for any}\quad a\in\mathbb{R},\,\,\beta>0.

Therefore

\displaystyle p(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\cosh\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi x}{2}\Bigr)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

It is clear that
p(x)\geqslant 0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then we may write

\displaystyle f(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}p(x)\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

and, in particular,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\,dx=f(0)=\dfrac{1}{\cosh(0)}=1.

Thus
p
is the density of absolutely continuous distribution with function
F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}p(u)\,du
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f
is its characteristic function.

We now show by Theorem
3
and Theorem
4
that
F
is infinitely divisible. We write for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}f(t)=-\ln\cosh(t),\quad(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime}(t)=-\tanh(t),\quad(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\cosh^{2}(t)}.

Thus
(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)=-f(t)^{2}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The function
t\mapsto f(t)^{2}
is characteristic for the distribution with the density

\displaystyle p^{*2}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x-y)p(y)\,dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}p^{*2}(x)\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}(1+x^{2})\,dG(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where we set

\displaystyle G(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{p^{*2}(u)\,du}{1+u^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Due to the summability and the non-negativity of the integrand function here,
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
and it is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
. The condition (
14
) is satisfied. Thus, by Theorems
3
and
4
,
F
is infinitely divisible distribution function with the spectral components
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)=0
and
G
.

Actually, it has been already known that
f
corresponds to an infinitely divisible distribution. In
[
20
]
(see p. 88), this fact was proved by more difficult way, namely, using the Weierstrass factorization theorem and the continuity theorem (for special compositions of the Laplace distributions).
\Box

Example 3.
Here we deal with the characteristic function of the general Cauchy distribution with asymmetry (general continuous stable law with
\alpha=1
):

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma-\lambda|t|-i\dfrac{2}{\pi}\,\lambda\beta\,t\ln|t|\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(16)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda>0
, and
\beta\in[-1,1]
.

So
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
f(0)=1
, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}f(t)=it\gamma-\lambda|t|-i\dfrac{2}{\pi}\,\lambda\beta\,t\ln|t|,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us write the function
\psi
:

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}f(t)-\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{Ln}f(t-h)+\mathrm{Ln}f(t+h)\bigr)

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle it\gamma-\lambda|t|-i\dfrac{2}{\pi}\,\lambda\beta\,t\ln|t|

\displaystyle{}-\dfrac{1}{2}\biggl(i(t-h)\gamma-\lambda|t-h|-i\dfrac{2}{\pi}\,\lambda\beta\,(t-h)\ln|t-h|

\displaystyle{}\,\,\quad+i(t+h)\gamma-\lambda|t+h|-i\dfrac{2}{\pi}\,\lambda\beta\,(t+h)\ln|t+h|\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\Delta_{h}(t)+i\,\dfrac{\lambda\beta}{\pi}\,A_{h}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\,\,h\in\mathbb{R},

where for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
we denote

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl(|t-h|+|t+h|-2|t|\bigr),

\displaystyle A_{h}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(t-h)\ln|t-h|+(t+h)\ln|t+h|-2t\ln|t|.

Let us fix any
h>0
. We will show applying Fourier transforms that the functions
\Delta_{h}
and
A_{h}
admit suitable integral representations.

We first consider the triangular function
\Delta_{h}
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}(t)=\begin{cases}t+h,&t\in(-h,0],\\
h-t,&t\in(0,h),\\
0,&|t|\geqslant h.\end{cases}

We introduce

\displaystyle D_{h}(x):=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}\Delta_{h}(t)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{h}\cos(tx)(h-t)\,dt=\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{\pi x^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(18)

Here the last expression was directly calculated by the integration by parts. Since the functions
\Delta_{h}
and
D_{h}
are from
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}D_{h}(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{\pi x^{2}}\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(19)

We now turn to the function
A_{h}
. Observe that it is an odd function, and we consider it only for
t\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle A_{h}(t)=(t-h)\ln|t-h|+(t+h)\ln(t+h)-2t\ln t,

where we set
y\ln y:=0
for
y=0
. The function
A_{h}
goes to zero as
t\to\infty
. Indeed,

\displaystyle A_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\bigl(\ln(t-h)+\ln(t+h)-2\ln t\bigr)+h\bigl(\ln(t+h)-\ln(t-h)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\ln\bigl(1-(h/t)^{2}\bigr)+h\bigl(\ln\bigl(1+h/t\bigr)-\ln\bigl(1-h/t\bigr)\bigr),

where, on account of the asymptotics
\ln(1+y)\sim y
as
y\to 0
,

\displaystyle\ln\bigl(1-(h/t)^{2}\bigr)\sim-(h/t)^{2},\qquad\ln\bigl(1+h/t\bigr)-\ln\bigl(1-h/t\bigr)\sim 2h/t,\quad t\to\infty.

Thus
A_{h}(t)\sim h^{2}/t\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Next, observe that
A_{h}
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
and, moreover, it has the derivatives at every
t>0
and
t\neq h
:

\displaystyle A_{h}^{\prime}(t)=\ln|t-h|+\ln(t+h)-2\ln t,\quad A_{h}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\dfrac{1}{t-h}+\dfrac{1}{t+h}-\dfrac{2}{t}.

(20)

The function
A_{h}
is decreasing on
(h,+\infty)
, because
A_{h}^{\prime}(t)=\ln\bigl(1-(h/t)^{2}\bigr)<0
,
t>h
.

On account of above-mentioned properties of
A_{h}
, we apply the Pringsheim theorem about Fourier transforms for monotonic functions (see
[
9
]
p. 592, and
[
26
]
p. 17) and we represent

\displaystyle A_{h}(t)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\dfrac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{R}\sin(tx)\biggl(\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{0}^{r}\sin(sx)A_{h}(s)\,ds\biggr)\,dx,\quad t\geqslant 0.

(21)

Note that the formula is valid for
t=0
, because
A_{h}(0)=0
(the Pringsheim theorem covers only the case
t>0
). Let us calculate the inner integral for
x>0

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{0}^{r}\sin(sx)A_{h}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\sin(sx)A_{h}(s)\,ds+\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\sin(sx)A_{h}(s)\,ds\Biggr].

We integrate by parts:

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[-\dfrac{\cos((h-\varepsilon)x)}{x}\,A_{h}(h-\varepsilon)+\dfrac{1}{x}\,A_{h}(0)+\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\dfrac{\cos(sx)}{x}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad\,\,\,-\dfrac{\cos(rx)}{x}\,A_{h}(r)+\dfrac{\cos((h+\varepsilon)x)}{x}\,A_{h}(h+\varepsilon)+\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\dfrac{\cos(sx)}{x}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(s)\,ds\Biggr].

Since the function
s\mapsto\tfrac{\cos(sx)}{x}\,A_{h}(s)
is continuous,
A_{h}(0)=0
, and
A_{h}(r)\to 0
as
r\to\infty
, we get

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\dfrac{\cos(sx)}{x}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(s)\,ds+\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\dfrac{\cos(sx)}{x}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(s)\,ds\Biggr].

We again integrate by parts:

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[\dfrac{\sin((h-\varepsilon)x)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(h-\varepsilon)-\lim_{s\to 0+}\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(s)\biggl)-\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad\,\,\,+\dfrac{\sin(rx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(r)-\dfrac{\sin((h+\varepsilon)x)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(h+\varepsilon)-\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds\Biggr].

Here we have (see (
20
))

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0+}\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(s)\biggl)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0+}\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,\bigl(\ln(h-s)+\ln(s+h)-2\ln s\bigr)\biggl)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(rx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(r)\biggr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(rx)}{x^{2}}\,\ln\bigl(1-(h/r)^{2}\bigr)\biggr)=-\lim_{r\to\infty}\biggl(\dfrac{\sin(rx)h^{2}}{x^{2}r^{2}}\biggr)=0.

We next observe that

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(h\pm\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln|h\pm\varepsilon-h|+\ln(h\pm\varepsilon+h)-2\ln(h\pm\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\varepsilon+\ln(2h\pm\varepsilon)-2\ln(h\pm\varepsilon)=\ln\varepsilon+\ln(2/h)+O(\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon\to 0+.

and
\sin((h\pm\varepsilon)x)=\sin(hx)+O(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0+
. Hence

\displaystyle\sin((h\pm\varepsilon)x)A^{\prime}(h\pm\varepsilon)=\ln\varepsilon\cdot\sin(hx)+\ln(2/h)\cdot\sin(hx)+O(\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon\to 0+.

Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\biggl[\dfrac{\sin((h-\varepsilon)x)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(h-\varepsilon)-\dfrac{\sin((h+\varepsilon)x)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime}(h+\varepsilon)\biggr]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}O(\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon)=0.

Using (
20
), we next write :

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[-\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds-\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{x^{2}}\,A_{h}^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds\Biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\sin(sx)\biggl(\dfrac{1}{s-h}+\dfrac{1}{s+h}-\dfrac{2}{s}\biggr)\,ds

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,+\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\sin(sx)\biggl(\dfrac{1}{s-h}+\dfrac{1}{s+h}-\dfrac{2}{s}\biggr)\,ds\Biggr].

We write

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{0}^{r}\dfrac{\sin(sx)}{s}\,\,ds-\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\Biggl[\int_{0}^{h-\varepsilon}\dfrac{s\sin(sx)}{s^{2}-h^{2}}\,ds+\int_{h+\varepsilon}^{r}\dfrac{s\sin(sx)}{s^{2}-h^{2}}\,ds\Biggr],

where, since
x>0
, the first limit equals
\pi/2
(see
[
11
]

3.721
1.) and the iterated limit equals
(\pi/2)\cdot\cos(hx)
(see
[
8
]
p. 684, and
[
11
]

3.723
10.). Thus we obtain

\displaystyle J_{h}(x)=\pi\,\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}},\quad x>0.

Let us return to formula (
21
) and write:

\displaystyle A_{h}(t)=\lim_{R\to\infty}2\int_{0}^{R}\sin(tx)\,\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,dx,\quad t\geqslant 0.

Since
x\mapsto\tfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}
is an even function from
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we may rewrite this formula as follows:

\displaystyle A_{h}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sin(tx)\,\mathrm{sgn}(x)\,\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,dx=\dfrac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,\mathrm{sgn}(x)\,\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,dx,\quad t\geqslant 0.

(22)

We now combine representations (
19
) and (
22
) in formula (
17
):

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\Delta_{h}(t)+i\,\dfrac{\lambda\beta}{\pi}\,A_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{\pi x^{2}}\,dx+i\,\dfrac{\lambda\beta}{\pi}\cdot\dfrac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,\mathrm{sgn}(x)\,\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1+\beta\,\mathrm{sgn}(x)\bigr)\,\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,dx,\quad t\geqslant 0.

Thus we may write

\displaystyle\psi(t,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\bigl(1-\cos(hx)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x),\quad t\geqslant 0,\,\,h>0,

with

\displaystyle G(x):=\dfrac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\dfrac{1+\beta\,\mathrm{sgn}(u)}{1+u^{2}}\,\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

In the latter integral, the integrand function is non-negative (recall that
\beta\in[-1,1]
) and it belongs to
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. So
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
and it is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
. According to Theorem
1
with Corollary
1
and Theorem
4
, the function
f
defined by formula (

) is the characteristic for infinitely divisible law with the spectral components
\gamma
(it equals
\mathrm{Arg}f(1)
) and
G
.

Example 4.
Here we illustrate application of Theorem
2
with approximation. We consider the characteristic function of the Linnik distribution with
\alpha=1
(see
[
19
]
):

\displaystyle f(t)=\dfrac{1}{1+|t|},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It can be easily checked by Pólya’s condition that
f
is indeed a characteristic function of absolutely continuous probability law. The density function, say
p
, admits the following representation (see
[
20
]
p. 83):

\displaystyle p(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\cos(tx)f(t)\,dt=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\dfrac{\cos(tx)}{1+t}\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

We fix
h>0
and consider the function
\varphi_{+}
(see formula (
10
)):

\displaystyle\varphi_{+}(t,h)=\dfrac{f(t)^{2}}{f(t-h)f(t+h)}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(1+|t-h|)(1+|t+h|)}{(1+|t|)^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{|t-h|+|t+h|-2|t|+|t-h|\cdot|t+h|-|t|^{2}}{(1+|t|)^{2}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that
\varphi_{+}(t,h)=\tfrac{2h-2t+h^{2}-2t^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}}
for any
t\in[0,h)
, and
\varphi_{+}(t,h)=-\tfrac{h^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}}
for any
t\geqslant h
. So we rewrite
\varphi_{+}
in the following form

\displaystyle\varphi_{+}(t,h)=2\hat{\varphi}_{+}(t,h)-h^{2}f(t)^{2},\quad t\geqslant 0,

(23)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{+}(t,h)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{h-t+h^{2}-t^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}},&t<h,\\
0,&t\geqslant h.\end{cases}

According to Theorem
2
, we have to propose a good approximation of the function
\varphi_{+}(\,\cdot\,,h)
by some function
I(\,\cdot\,,h)
that admits the suitable integral representation (
5
). So we approximate
\hat{\varphi}_{+}(\,\cdot\,,h)
by the function
\Delta_{h}
that has representation of such type according to formula (
19
), and we introduce the corresponding approximation error
\rho_{1}(t,h):=\hat{\varphi}_{+}(t,h)-\Delta_{h}(t)
,
t\geqslant 0
. Next, recall that
t\mapsto f(t)^{2}
is the characteristic function for
p^{*2}
. So the component
h^{2}f(t)^{2}
in (
23
), of course, admits the integral representation

\displaystyle h^{2}f(t)^{2}=h^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}p^{*2}(x)\,dx=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}\,p^{*2}(x)\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

but it isn’t of the form (
5
). Therefore we have to replace
\tfrac{h^{2}}{2}
with
\tfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}
in the integral, i.e. we approximate
h^{2}f(t)^{2}
by

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,\tfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,p^{*2}(x)\,dx=2\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}D_{h}(x)\,p^{*2}(x)\,dx,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
D_{h}
is defined by formula (
18
). Observe that
\Delta_{h}
,
f^{2}
and
\Delta_{h}*f^{2}
are from
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and we may write

\displaystyle D_{h}(x)\,p^{*2}(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}\Delta_{h}(t)\,dt\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}f(t)^{2}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}(\Delta_{h}*f^{2})(t)\,dt,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Here the function
D_{h}\cdot p^{*2}
is from
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
too and, inverting the Fourier transform, we get

\displaystyle 2\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}D_{h}(x)\,p^{*2}(x)\,dx=(\Delta_{h}*f^{2})(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus we may write the approximation error in the form:
\rho_{2}(t,h):=h^{2}f(t)^{2}-(\Delta_{h}*f^{2})(t)
,
t\geqslant 0
.

We now represent
\varphi_{+}
as in (
11
), i.e.

\displaystyle\varphi_{+}(t,h)=2\hat{\varphi}_{+}(t,h)-h^{2}f(t)^{2}=2I(t,h)+\rho(t,h),\quad t\geqslant 0,

where
I(t,h):=\Delta_{h}(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\Delta_{h}*f^{2})(t)
, and

\displaystyle\rho(t,h):=2\hat{\varphi}_{+}(t,h)-2\Delta_{h}(t)-h^{2}f(t)^{2}+(\Delta_{h}*f^{2})(t)=2\rho_{1}(t,h)-\rho_{2}(t,h).

Let us consider the function
I
using the definition of
D_{h}
:

\displaystyle I(t,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}D_{h}(x)\,dx-\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}D_{h}(x)\,p^{*2}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}D_{h}(x)(1-\pi p^{*2}(x))\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\dfrac{1-\cos(hx)}{x^{2}}\,\biggl(\dfrac{1}{\pi}-p^{*2}(x)\biggr)\,dx,\quad t\geqslant 0.

Thus we come to the needed representation for any
t\geqslant 0
and
h>0
:

\displaystyle I(t,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}(1-\cos(hx))\,\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,\,dG(x),

with

\displaystyle G(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{\pi}-p^{*2}(u)\biggr)\,\dfrac{\,du}{(1+u^{2})},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where the integrand function belongs to
L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and it is always non-negative. The latter fact is true, because
p^{*2}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}f(t)^{2}\,dt
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and, consequently,

\displaystyle p^{*2}(x)\leqslant\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)^{2}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{\,dt}{(1+|t|)^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\,dt}{(1+t)^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{\pi},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus
G\in\boldsymbol{V}
and it is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
.

We will show that the function
\rho
satisfies condition (
12
) of Theorem
2
. We fix any sequence
(h_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
as in the statement of Theorem
2
and we first observe that

\displaystyle|\rho(kh_{l},h_{l})|=|2\rho_{1}(kh_{l},h_{l})-\rho_{2}(kh_{l},h_{l})|\leqslant 2|\rho_{1}(kh_{l},h_{l})|+|\rho_{2}(kh_{l},h_{l})|,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Here and below
l\in\mathbb{N}
is choosen arbitrarily. So
\rho_{1}(kh_{l},h_{l})=\hat{\varphi}_{+}(kh_{l},h_{l})-\Delta_{h_{l}}(kh_{l})
equals
(h_{l}^{2}+h_{l})-h_{l}=h^{2}_{l}
for
k=0
and it equals
0
else. For the function
\rho_{2}
we preliminarily observe that for any
t\geqslant 0
and
h>0

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(t,h)=\int_{-h}^{h}\Delta_{h}(s)\,ds\cdot f(t)^{2}-\int_{-h}^{h}f^{2}(t-s)\Delta_{h}(s)\,ds=\int_{-h}^{h}\bigl(f^{2}(t)-f^{2}(t-s)\bigr)\Delta_{h}(s)\,ds.

Here

\displaystyle f^{2}(t)-f^{2}(t-s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{(1+|t|)^{2}}-\dfrac{1}{(1+|t-s|)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(1+|t-s|)^{2}-(1+|t|)^{2}}{(1+|t|)^{2}(1+|t-s|)^{2}}=\dfrac{(|t-s|-|t|)(2+|t-s|+|t|)}{(1+|t|)^{2}(1+|t-s|)^{2}},

and we have the estimate

\displaystyle|f^{2}(t)-f^{2}(t-s)|\leqslant\dfrac{|s|\cdot 2(1+|t|+|t-s|)}{(1+|t|)^{2}(1+|t-s|)^{2}}\leqslant\dfrac{2|s|}{(1+|t|)(1+|t-s|)}\leqslant 2|s|.

Then for any
t\geqslant 0
and
h>0
we get

\displaystyle|\rho_{2}(t,h)|\leqslant\int_{-h}^{h}|f^{2}(t)-f^{2}(t-s)|\Delta_{h}(s)\,ds\leqslant\int_{-h}^{h}2|s|\Delta_{h}(s)\,ds\leqslant 2h\int_{-h}^{h}\Delta_{h}(s)\,ds=2h^{3}.

In particular,
|\rho_{2}(kh_{l},h_{l})|\leqslant 2h_{l}^{3}
. We now turn to checking (
12
) for any
t>0
using the remarks about
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|\rho(kh_{l},h_{l})|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|\rho_{1}(kh_{l},h_{l})|+\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|\rho_{2}(kh_{l},h_{l})|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2n_{t,l}h_{l}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|2h_{l}^{3}\leqslant 2n_{t,l}h_{l}^{2}+2n_{t,l}^{2}h_{l}^{3}.

Since
n_{t,l}=\lfloor t/h_{l}\rfloor\leqslant t/h_{l}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)|\rho(kh_{l},h_{l})|\leqslant 2th_{l}+2t^{2}h_{l},

where the sum in the right-hand side tends to zero as
l\to\infty
due to
h_{l}\to 0
by the assumption. Thus (
12
) holds.

We still have to check condition (
13
) for the function
\varphi_{+}
. According to the comment before formula (
23
), we have
\varphi_{+}(0,h_{l})=2h_{l}+h_{l}^{2}
, and
\varphi_{+}(kh_{l},h_{l})=-h_{l}^{2}/(1+kh_{l})^{2}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.
|\varphi_{+}(kh_{l},h_{l})|\leqslant h_{l}^{2}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for any
t>0
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)\bigl|\varphi_{+}(kh_{l},h_{l})\bigr|^{2}\leqslant n_{t,l}\bigl(2h_{l}+h_{l}^{2}\bigr)^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)h_{l}^{4}\leqslant n_{t,l}h_{l}^{2}(2+h_{l})^{2}+n_{t,l}^{2}h_{l}^{4}.

Using inequality
n_{t,l}\leqslant t/h_{l}
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n_{t,l}-1}\bigl(n_{t,l}-k\bigr)\bigl|\varphi_{+}(kh_{l},h_{l})\bigr|^{2}\leqslant th_{l}(2+h_{l})^{2}+t^{2}h_{l}^{2},

where the right-hand side obviously tends to zero as
l\to\infty
due to
h_{l}\to 0
. This implies (
13
) for
\varphi_{+}
.

Thus we have checked all conditions of Theorem
2
, and we conclude that the distribution function of the Linnik law with characteristic function

is rational-infinitely divisible, i.e.
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Moreover, since
G
is non-decreasing (as we have already showed above),
F
is purely infinitely divisible, i.e.
F\in\boldsymbol{I}
, with the spectral pair
\gamma=\mathrm{Arg}f(1)=0
and
G
.

,

, with

) is a known fact. It was showed in the papers

]

]
, where the authors used other non-trivial approaches.

Proof of Theorem

\displaystyle\dfrac{e^{i(n+1)hx}-1-(n+1)(e^{ihx}-1)}{(e^{ihx}-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\bigl(e^{i(n+1)hx}-1-(n+1)(e^{ihx}-1)\bigr)e^{-ihx}}{2(1-\cos(hx))}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{e^{inhx}-e^{-ihx}-(n+1)(1-e^{-ihx})}{2(1-\cos(hx))}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{e^{inhx}-1-n(1-e^{-ihx})}{2(1-\cos(hx))}.

\displaystyle|\delta(t,h)|\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|\mathrm{Ln}\bigl(1+\varphi_{\pm}(t,h)\bigr)-\varphi_{\pm}(t,h)\bigr|+\tfrac{1}{2}|\rho(t,h)|,\quad t\geqslant 0,\,\,h>0.

The first modulus less than

, when

Let us choose the sequence

a) Under the assumptions of the theorem, we first show that for any

there exists
(\mathrm{Ln}f)^{\prime}(t)
. Let us fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the relation

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{Ln}f(t+h)-\mathrm{Ln}f(t)}{h}=i\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x),

(32)

where
0<|h|<1
. We will propose majorant for the integrated function. Let
r
be a positive real number. For any

satisfying

we have the estimate

\displaystyle\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr|\leqslant\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}\biggr|+|i\sin(x)|=\biggl|\dfrac{e^{ihx}-1}{h}\biggr|+|\sin(x)|.

In the last sum, each term is bounded by
|x|
. So we have

\displaystyle\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr|\leqslant 2|x|,\quad|x|>r.

For the case

we will use the representation

\displaystyle\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)=\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}-ihx}{h}-i(\sin(x)-x).

So we have the inequality

\displaystyle\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr|\leqslant\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}-ihx}{h}\biggl|+|\sin(x)-x|.

For the last term we have

\displaystyle|\sin(x)-x|\leqslant\dfrac{|x|^{3}}{3!}\leqslant\dfrac{rx^{2}}{3!},\quad|x|\leqslant r.

Next, we represent

\displaystyle\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}-ihx}{h}=\dfrac{ix}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}(e^{iux}-1)\,du=\dfrac{ix}{h}\int_{0}^{h}(e^{i(v+t)x}-1)\,dv.

Since
\bigl|e^{i(v+t)x}-1\bigr|\leqslant\bigl|(v+t)x\bigr|\leqslant\bigl(|v|+|t|\bigr)|x|
, we get

\displaystyle\Biggl|\int_{0}^{h}(e^{i(v+t)x}-1)\,dv\Biggr|\leqslant\Biggl|\int_{0}^{h}\bigl|e^{i(v+t)x}-1\bigr|\,dv\Biggr|\leqslant\Biggl|\int_{0}^{h}\bigl(|v|+|t|\bigr)|x|\,dv\Biggr|=|x|\int_{0}^{|h|}\bigl(v+|t|\bigr)\,dv.

Therefore

\displaystyle\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}-ihx}{h}\biggr|=\Biggl|\dfrac{x}{h}\int_{0}^{h}\bigl(e^{i(v+t)x}-1\bigr)\,du\Biggr|\leqslant\dfrac{x^{2}}{|h|}\int_{0}^{|h|}\bigl(v+|t|\bigr)\,dv\leqslant\bigl(|h|+|t|\bigr)x^{2}.

Since

\displaystyle\biggl|\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr|\leqslant C_{t,r}x^{2},\quad|x|\leqslant r,

with

.

The obtained upper estimates are integrable with
\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}
by
|G|(\cdot)
over the coresponding sets :

\displaystyle\int_{|x|>r}2|x|\,\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,d|G|(x)=2\int_{|x|>r}\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{|x|}\,d|G|(x)\leqslant\dfrac{2}{r}\int_{|x|>r}(1+x^{2})\,d|G|(x)<\infty,

and

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\leqslant r}C_{t,r}x^{2}\,\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,d|G|(x)=C_{t,r}\int_{|x|\leqslant r}(1+x^{2})\,d|G|(x)<\infty.

Thus the integrand in (
32
) has the integrable majorant.

Next, we observe that the integrand in (

) converges for any

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}=\bigl(ixe^{itx}-i\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}.

(33)

Defining the right-hand side by continuity at

\displaystyle e^{itx}=1+itx+O(x^{2}),\quad\sin(x)=x-O(x^{3}),\quad x\to 0,

\displaystyle\bigl(ixe^{itx}-i\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}=\bigl((ix)^{2}t+O(x^{3})\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\to-t,\quad x\to 0.

By the following well-known convention

\displaystyle\Bigl(\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\Bigr)\biggl|_{x=0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\Bigl(\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\Bigr)=-\dfrac{t^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\biggl(\dfrac{e^{i(t+h)x}-e^{itx}}{h}-i\sin(x)\biggr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}=\lim_{h\to 0}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{h}\biggl(-\dfrac{(t+h)^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}\biggr)\biggr\}=\lim_{h\to 0}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{h}\biggl(-th-\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}\biggr)\biggr\}=-t.

Thus (
