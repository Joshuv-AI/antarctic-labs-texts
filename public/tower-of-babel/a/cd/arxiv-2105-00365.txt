On α \alpha -points of q q -analogs of the Fano plane
Michael Kiermaier Universität Bayreuth

Abstract
Arguably, the most important open problem in the theory of q q -analogs of designs is the question for the existence of a q q -analog D D of the Fano plane. It is undecided for every single prime power value q ≥ 2 q\geq 2 . A point P P is called an α \alpha -point of D D if the derived design of D D in P P is a geometric spread. In 1996, Simon Thomas has shown that there must always exist at least one non- α \alpha -point. For the binary case q = 2 q=2 , Olof Heden and Papa Sissokho have improved this result in 2016 by showing that the non- α \alpha -points must form a blocking set with respect to the hyperplanes. In this article, we show that a hyperplane consisting only of α \alpha -points implies the existence of a partiton of the symplectic generalized quadrangle W ⁡ ( q ) W(q) into spreads. As a consequence, the statement of Heden and Sissokho is generalized to all primes q q and all even values of q q .

2

2

In established symbols like

There are good reasons to consider the subset lattice as a subspace lattice over the unary

The Gaussian binomial coefficient

For

\displaystyle S|_{U}

\displaystyle=\{B\in S\mid U\leq B\}\text{,}

\displaystyle S|^{W}

\displaystyle=\{B\in S\mid U\leq B\leq W\}\text{.}

For a point

The subspace lattice

Definition 2.1

The earliest reference for subspace designs is

The first nontrivial subspace designs with

[

]

An introduction to the theory of subspace designs can be found at

Subspace designs are interlinked to the theory of network coding in various ways.
To this effect we mention the recently found

(v,k,1)_{q}

Classical combinatorial designs can be seen as the limit case

Indeed, quite a few statements about combinatorial designs have a generalization to subspace designs, such that the case

[

One example of such a statement is the following

If

(v,k,\lambda)_{q}

In particular, the number of blocks in

\#D=\lambda_{0}=\lambda\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{v}{t}_{q}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{t}_{q}}.

-
(v,k,\lambda)_{q}

In this case, the parameter set

-
(v,k,\lambda)_{q}

2.3
Spreads

A

(v,k,1)_{q}

k
-subspaces.
These objects are better known under the name

A

A set
\mathcal{S}
of
k
-subspaces is called a

(k-1)
-spread
if each point is covered by at most one element of
\mathcal{S}
.
The points not covered by any element are called

.
A recent survey on partial spreads is found in
[
17
]
.

The parameters
1
-
(v,k,1)_{q}
are admissible if and only

k
.
In this case, spreads do always exist
[
24
, §VI]
.
An example can be constructed via field reduction:
We consider
V
as a vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{k}}
and set
\mathcal{S}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{V}{1}_{q^{k}}
.
Switching back to vector spaces over

is a
(k-1)
-spread of

A

-spread

is called

-spread of

In other words,
\mathcal{S}
is geometric if every

-subspace of
V
contains either

,

blocks of
\mathcal{S}
.
It is not hard to see that the Desarguesian spread is geometric.
In fact, it follows from
[
2
, Theorem 2]
that a
(k-1)
-spread is geometric if and only if it is isomorphic to a Desarguesian spreads.

The derived designs of a
q
-analog of the Fano plane

are line spreads in
\PG(5,2)
.
These spreads have been classified in
[
21
]
into
131044

The most symmetric one among these spreads is the Desarguesian spread.
Following the notation of
[

, a point

is called an
\alpha
-point of the

-analog of the Fano plane

2.4
Generalized quadrangles

Definition 2.2
.

A
generalized quadrangle
is an incidence structure
Q=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},I)
with a non-empty set of
points

\mathcal{P}
, a non-empty set of
lines

\mathcal{L}
, and an incidence relation
I\subseteq\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{L}
such hat

1.

Two distinct points are incident with at most a line.

2.

Two distinct lines are incident with at most one point.

3.

For each non-incident point-line-pair
(P,L)
there is a unique incident point-line-pair
(P^{\prime},L^{\prime})
with
P\mathrel{I}L^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime}\mathrel{I}L
.

Generalized quadrangles have been introduced in the more general setting of generalized polygons in
[
29
]
, as a tool in the theory of finite groups.

A generalized quadrangle
Q=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},I)
is called
degenerate
if there is a point
P
such that each point of
Q
is incident with a line through
P
.
If each line of
Q
is incident with
t+1
points, and each point is incident with
s+1
lines, we say that
Q
is of
order

(s,t)
.
The
dual

Q^{\perp}
arises from
Q
by interchanging the role of the points and the lines.
It is again a generalized quadrangle.
Clearly,
(Q^{\perp})^{\perp}=Q
, and
Q
is of order
(s,t)
if and only if
Q^{\perp}
is of order
(t,s)
.

Furthermore,
Q
is said to be
projective
if it is embeddable in some Desarguesian projective geometry.
This means that there is a Desarguesian projective geometry with point set
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, line set
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, and point-line incidence relation
I^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L^{\prime}}
and for all
(P,L)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{L}
we have
P\mathrel{I}L
if and only if
P^{\prime}\mathrel{I^{\prime}}L^{\prime}
.
The non-degenerate finite projective generalized quadrangles have been classified in
[
8
, Th. 1]
, see also
[
23
, 4.4.8.]
.
These are exactly the so-called
classical generalized quadrangles
which are associated to a quadratic form or a symplectic or Hermitean polarity on the ambient geometry, see
[
23
, 3.1.1.]
.

In this article, two of these classical generalized quadrangles will appear.

1.

The symplectic generalized quadrangle
W(q)

[
23
, 3.1.1 (iii)]
, consisting of the set of points of
\PG(3,q)
together with the totally isotropic lines with respect to a symplectic polarity.
Taking the geometry as
\PG(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{4})
, the symplectic polarity can be represented by the alternating bilinear form
\beta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}+x_{3}y_{4}-x_{4}y_{3}
.
The configuration of the lines
\mathcal{L}
in

is also known as a (general) linear complex of lines, see

Under the Klein correspondence,

2.

The second one is the parabolic quadric

, whose points
\mathcal{P}
are the zeroes of a parabolic quadratic form in

, and whose lines are all the lines contained in

.
Taking the geometry as

As in projective geometries, a set
