A Model Problem for First Order Mean Field Games with Discrete Initial Data Thanks: This research was supported in part by National Science Foundation thought NSF Grant DMS-2045027.
P. Jameson Graber, Brady Zimmerman

Abstract
. In this article, we study a simplified version of a density-dependent first-order mean field game, in which the players face a penalization equal to the population density at their final position. We consider the problem of finding an equilibrium when the initial distribution is a discrete measure. We show that the problem becomes finite-dimensional: the final piecewise smooth density is completely determined by the weights and positions of the initial measure. We establish existence and uniqueness of a solution using classical fixed point theorems. Finally, we show that Newton’s method provides an effective way to compute the solution. Our numerical simulations provide an illustration of how density penalization in a mean field game tends to the smoothen the initial distribution.

1.
Introduction

Mean field games were introduced by Lasry and Lions
[
LL06a
,
LL06b
,
LL07
]
and Caines, Huang, and Malhamé
[
HMC06
]
in order to model the interactions between large numbers of agents playing a differential game; see for instance
[
BFY13
,
CD17
,
G+14
,
ACD+20
]
for an overview of the field.
In this article, we are interested in first-order, or deterministic, mean field games.
Typically, these are modeled using a first-order coupled system of partial differential equations, as follows:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}u+H(x,\nabla_{x}u)=F(x,m),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},t\in(0,T),\\
\partial_{t}m-\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathinner{\left(D_{p}H(x,\nabla_{x}u)m\right)}=0,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},t\in(0,T),\\
m(x,0)=m_{0}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\\
u(x,T)=G\mathinner{\left(x,m(x,T)\right)},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.\end{cases}

Here
H
is the Hamiltonian for an optimal control problem, which a representative agent solves for a given population density
m(x,t)
.
The functions
F
and
G
model the cost this player must pay as a function of the density
m
.
In Nash equilibrium, the density will evolve according to the continuity equation driven by the optimal feedback vector field
-D_{p}H(x,\nabla_{x}u)
.

There are now many results on the existence and uniqueness of suitably defined
weak
solutions to System (
1.1
)
[
Car15
,
Gra14
,
CG15
,
CGPT15
]
as well as regularity of solutions
[
PS17
,
GM18
,
Mun22
]
.
As far as we know, all of these results apply only when the initial distribution
m_{0}
is a density, i.e. it has no singular part with respect to Lebesgue measure.
It remains an open question how to analyze weak solutions in the case where
m_{0}
is replaced with a more general measure, which could have singular parts.

In this article, we contribute to the theory of mean field games by analyzing an example in which the initial measure is entirely discrete, i.e. it is a weighted sum of Dirac measures.
We simplify the problem by choosing
F=0
,
H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}
, and
G(x,m(x,T))=m(x,T)
.
We will also set the space dimension
d=1
.
In this case the problem can be viewed as a static, “one-shot” game in which a player starting at a point
x
only has to choose a point
y
to minimize the cost

\frac{\mathinner{\left(x-y\right)}^{2}}{2T}+m(y,T).

There is nothing particularly important about the value of
T
, so we will set
T=1/2
, and we will denote by
f(y)
the final density
m(y,T)
.
Hence the cost to a player starting at point
x
and choosing to move to point
y
is given by

(1.2)

J(x,y,f)=(x-y)^{2}+f(y),

where
f
can in principle be any probability density function, i.e. a non-negative measurable function such that
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\dif x=1
.
To avoid dealing with too many measure-theoretic issues, we will impose the restriction that
f
must be continuous.

The initial distribution will be given by
\displaystyle{m=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}}
with
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=1,a_{j}\geq 0
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
distinct points in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Thus
m
is an empirical measure in which
a_{j}
is the proportion of players initially located at each
x_{j}
.
Without loss of generality, we will assume
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}
, i.e. we put the points in the initial measure in order from left to right.

In this context, a
Nash equilibrium
is a measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
such that:

(1)

\pi(A\times\mathbb{R})=m(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}(A),\text{where }\delta_{x_{j}}(A)=

\begin{cases}1\text{ if }x_{j}\notin A\\
0\text{ if }x_{j}\in A\end{cases}
,
i.e. first marginal of
\pi
is
m
,

(2)

\pi{(\mathbb{R}\times B)}=\int_{B}f(x)\dif{x}
for a continuous density
f,
i.e. the second marginal of
\pi
is the density
f
, and

(3)

for
\pi
- a.e.
(x,y)
,
y\in\argmin{J(x,\cdot,f)},
i.e.
\pi
couples points
x
and
y
such that
y
is an optimal move starting from
x
.

In other words, given the cost function
J
, a density function
f
is chosen to possibly be an equilibrium measure. With the knowledge of this function
f
, the players choose the optimal strategy for themselves, resulting in some final distribution. If this final distribution matches
f
, then
f
is in fact an equilibrium. The idea of a “Self-Fulfilling Prophecy” can be helpful to understand this:
f
is “prophesied” to be an equilibrium measure, and, if it turns out to result in an equilibrium, then
f
in a sense “fulfilled its prophecy.” The below diagram demonstrates this phenomenon.

Figure 1.
Mean Field Games Diagram

In this article, our purpose is two-fold:

(1)

prove there exists a unique Nash equilibrium, and

(2)

compute solutions using a numerical method.

As far as we know, this constitutes the first result on existence and uniqueness of solutions to first-order mean field game with singular measures.
Our approach relies on the fact that we take an entirely discrete measure, which allows us to rewrite the problem in an equivalent finite-dimensional formulation.
Intuitively, all we need to compute is how each Dirac mass
a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
in the initial distribution will “fan out” into a density of total mass
a_{j}
centered around point
x_{j}
.
As explained below, the precise shape of this density is known through a priori considerations.
This approach makes the problem amenable to classical methods.
We believe it also provides geometric insight for what is going on more generally in density-penalized mean field games of first order.

Remark 1.1
.

The main results of this article were first announced in the second author’s undergraduate thesis
[
Zim24
]
.

2.
Reformulation of the equilibrium problem

Let
\pi
be an equilibrium, and let
f
be the corresponding final density.

Definition 2.1
.

The support of
\pi
, supp
\pi
, is the set of all
(x,y)
such that, for all
\epsilon>0
,
\pi(B_{\epsilon}(x,y))>0,
where
B_{\epsilon}(x,y)=\mathinner{\left\{(z,w)|\sqrt{(x-z)^{2}+(y-w)^{2}}<\epsilon\right\}}
, or, equivalently,
\pi(U)>0
for every open set containing
(x,y)
.

The support of
f
,
\operatorname{supp}f
, is the closure of the set of all
y
such that
f(y)>0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
(x,y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
, then
x=x_{j}
for some
j
and
y\in\operatorname{supp}f
.
Conversely, if
y\in\operatorname{supp}f
, then there exists
x_{j}
such that
(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
.

Proof.

If
(x,y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
, then for all
\epsilon>0
we have
m(B_{\epsilon}(x))=\pi(B_{\epsilon}(x)\times{\mathbb{R}})>0
and
\int_{B_{\epsilon}(y)}f(z)\dif z=\pi({\mathbb{R}}\times B_{\epsilon}(y))>0
.
It follows that
x=x_{j}
for some
j
, and
y\in\operatorname{supp}f
since for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
z\in B_{\epsilon}(y)
such that
f(z)>0
.

Conversely, suppose
y\in\operatorname{supp}f
.
For every
\epsilon>0
,
\pi({\mathbb{R}}\times B_{\epsilon}(y))=\int_{B_{\epsilon}(y)}f>0
.
Since
\pi({\mathbb{R}}\times B_{\epsilon}(y))=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi(\{x_{j}\}\times B_{\epsilon}(y))
, for each
\epsilon
there is a
j
such that
\pi(\{x_{j}\}\times B_{\epsilon}(y))>0
.
We can then find a sequence
\epsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and a fixed
j
such that
\pi(\{x_{j}\}\times B_{\epsilon_{k}}(y))>0
for all
k
, which implies
(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
.
∎

Now if
(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
, the definition of equilibrium implies
y\in E_{j}
where

E_{j}=\argmin\mathinner{\left\{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\displaystyle{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}+f(y)\right\}},

i.e.
E_{j}
is the set of minimizers
y
for the cost
J(x_{j},y,f)
.
By Lemma
2.2
,
\operatorname{supp}f\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}
; the intuitive meaning is that every player must move into one of the sets
E_{j}
.
On the other hand, for
y\in E_{j}
we have
f(y)=C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}
, where

C_{j}=\min\mathinner{\left\{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\displaystyle{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}+f(y)\right\}}.

Thus,
f
is completely determined by
C_{j}
and
E_{j}
, and these in turn are coupled by the definition of
E_{j}
.
So how do we determine
C_{j}
and
E_{j}
?
A first clue is the following proposition, whose proof is elementary:

Proposition 2.3
.

E_{j}=\mathinner{\left\{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}\geq\mathinner{\left(C_{k}-{(x_{k}-y)^{2}}\right)}_{+}\ \forall k\right\}}
, where
x_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{x,0\}
.

Proof.

If
y\in E_{j}
, then
{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}+f(y)=C_{j}
, and at the same time
{(x_{k}-y)^{2}}+f(y)\geq C_{k}
for all
k
by definition of
C_{k}
.
Recalling that
f\geq 0
, we see that
C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}=f(y)\geq\mathinner{\left(C_{k}-{(x_{k}-y)^{2}}\right)}_{+}
for all
k
.

Conversely, suppose
y\notin E_{j}
.
If
y\in E_{k}
for some other
k
, then
C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}<f(y)=C_{k}-{(x_{k}-y)^{2}}
.
If
y\notin\bigcup_{1}^{n}E_{k}
, then
f(y)=0
, hence
C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}<0
.
It follows that
C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}<\mathinner{\left({C_{k}}-(x_{k}-y)^{2}\right)}_{+}
for at least one
k
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
f_{j}(y)=C_{j}-{(x_{j}-y)^{2}}
.
Then
f(y)=\max\mathinner{\left\{0,f_{1}(y),\ldots,f_{n}(y)\right\}}
and
E_{j}=\mathinner{\left\{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f(y)=f_{j}(y)\right\}}
.

Intuitively, all the players initially concentrated at
x_{j}
should spread out according to the density function
f_{j}
over the set
E_{j}
.
We now make this intuition rigorous.

Proposition 2.5
.

E_{j}=\mathinner{\left\{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi\right\}}
, and
\int_{E_{j}}f=\int_{E_{j}}f_{j}=a_{j}
.

Proof.

If
(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
, then by definition of equilibrium, it follows that
y\in E_{j}
.
Conversely, suppose
y\in E_{j}
.
As explained below in Lemma
3.1
,
E_{j}
is an interval, and if
y
is in the interior of that interval, then
y\notin\cup_{k\neq j}E_{k}
.
By Corollary
2.4
and Lemma
2.2
we deduce that
(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
.
If
y
is on the boundary of
E_{j}
, there is a sequence
y_{k}\to y
with
y_{k}
in the interior of
E_{j}
, so
(x_{j},y_{k})\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
and thus
(x_{j},y)\in\operatorname{supp}\pi
.

To prove the remaining statements, start with the following inequality:

(2.1)

a_{j}=m(\{x_{j}\})=\pi\mathinner{\left(\{x_{j}\}\times{\mathbb{R}}\right)}=\pi\mathinner{\left(\{x_{j}\}\times E_{j}\right)}\leq\pi\mathinner{\left({\mathbb{R}}\times E_{j}\right)}=\int_{E_{j}}f.

Summing over
j
, we get

(2.2)

1=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{E_{j}}f=\int f=1.

It follows that
\int_{E_{j}}f=a_{j}
, as desired.
∎

With these results, we can now reformulate the problem using only the parameters
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
.

Definition 2.6
.

For a given vector
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

•

let
f_{j}^{(C)}(x)=C_{j}-(x-x_{j})^{2}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
;

•

let
f^{(C)}(x)=\max\{f_{1}^{(C)}(x),\ldots,f_{n}^{(C)}(x),0\}
;

•

let
E_{j}^{(C)}=\mathinner{\left\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f_{j}^{(C)}(x)=f^{(C)}(x)\right\}}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
;

•

let
F_{j}(C)=\int_{E_{j}^{(C)}}f^{(C)}(x)\dif{x}
when
E_{j}^{(C)}
is nonempty, and let
F_{j}(C)=0
if
E_{j}^{(C)}
is empty, for
j=1,\ldots,n
; and

•

let
F(C)=\mathinner{\left(F_{1}(C),\ldots,F_{n}(C)\right)}
.

We remark that, in what follows,
f_{j}^{(C)}
,
f^{(C)}
, and
E_{j}^{(C)}
will often be written more cleanly as
f_{j}
,
f
, and
E_{j}
, when it is clear from context that
C
is fixed.

To better understand these definitions, consider the following image:

Figure 2.
Visualization of the density
f(x)

The graph of
f(x)
is the solid-color part (i.e. the maximum).
Each individual curve
f_{j}(x)
can be thought of the density after the population sitting at point
x_{j}
“fans out” and forms a “bubble” surrounding that point.

With these definitions, we now state the problem we wish to solve as follows:

Problem 2.7
.

Given a vector
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, whose components are the weights in the discrete measure
m=\sum_{j=1}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
, we want to find a vector
C
such that
F(C)=a
.

By the arguments we have just given, if
f(x)
is an equilibrium density, then
f(x)=f^{(C)}(x)
where
C
is the solution to Problem
2.7
.
Conversely, suppose
C
is the solution to Problem
2.7
and let
f(x)=f^{(C)}(x)
.
To see that
f(x)
is an equilibrium density, first define a function
T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that sends every point in the interior of
E_{j}=E_{j}^{(C)}
to the point
x_{j}
.
Notice that
m
is now the
push-forward
of the density through
T
.
(We say a measure
\nu
is the push-forward of a measure
\mu
through a function
T
if
\nu(B)=T\sharp\mu(B)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mu\mathinner{\left(T^{-1}(B)\right)}
; in this case
\nu=m
and
\dif\mu=f(x)\dif x
.)
Set
\pi
to be the push-forward of the density
f
by the function
y\mapsto(T(y),y)
.
It follows that
\pi
is an equilibrium.

We now state our main result:

Theorem 2.8

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

For each
a
such that
a_{j}>0
for
j=1,\dots,n
, there exists a unique
C
such that
F(C)=a
.

Theorem
2.8
immediately implies the existence and uniqueness of a Nash equilibrium for any game where the initial distribution is discrete.
We will prove Theorem
2.8
in two sections: Section
4
shows existence, and Section
5
proves uniqueness.
These proofs rely on our analysis of
F(C)
in Section
3
, in which we establish certain formulas for
F_{j}(C)
and its derivatives, prove continuity and coercivity, and the convexity of the effective domain of
F
(namely, the set of all
C
for which
F_{j}(C)>0
for all
j
).

3.
Structure of
F(C)

The proof of Theorem
2.8
is based on various properties of
F(C)
, as given in Definition
2.6
.
Whenever
C
is fixed, we will usually suppress the argument
C
and simply write
f_{j}(x)
,
f(x)
, and
E_{j}
instead of
f_{j}^{(C)}(x)
,
f^{(C)}(x)
, and
E_{j}^{(C)}
.
In Section
3.1
, we study the set
E_{j}
, namely the set over which
f(x)=f_{j}(x)
.
We also study the effective domain of
F(C)
, over which we look for solutions to Problem
2.7
, and we prove it is convex.
In Section
3.2
we seek more or less explicit formulas for
F_{j}(C)
and its derivatives.
Section
3.3
shows that
F
is a
coercive
function on certain domains, while Section
3.4
shows that it is continuous everywhere.
In reading these technical results, it will be useful to recall that we have ordered the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
from left to right, i.e.
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}
without loss of generality.

3.1.
Properties of
E_{j}

We begin with the following claim:

Lemma 3.1
.

E_{j}
is convex and compact, hence either empty, a singleton, or a closed, bounded interval.

Proof.

First note that
f_{j}
is concave and continuous.
Then observe that for every
i
,
f_{j}-f_{i}
is affine, hence also concave and continuous.
Indeed,

(3.1)

f_{i}(x)-f_{j}(x)=C_{i}-C_{j}+2(x_{i}-x_{j})\mathinner{\left(x-\frac{x_{i}+x_{j}}{2}\right)}.

We can write
E_{j}
is the intersection of super-level sets

(3.2)

E_{j}=\cap_{i}\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f_{j}(x)-f_{i}(x)\geq 0\}\bigcap\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f_{j}(x)\geq 0\},

which is therefore convex and closed by the first three observations.
Moreover, it is compact because
\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f_{j}(x)\geq 0\}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(x-x_{j})^{2}\leq C_{j}\}
is compact.
The proof is complete.
∎

In light of this, we introduce the following notation:

Definition 3.2
.

In the case that
E_{j}
is and interval, we define
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
as the left and right end points of
E_{j}
, i.e.
[\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}]=E_{j}
.
We denote the length of
E_{j}
by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}=\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}
.

Before proceeding, we observe that Definition
2.6
leaves open the possibility that
F_{j}(C)=0
.
Since we are trying to solve
F(C)=a
where all the components of
a
are positive, the true domain of interest is given as follows:

Definition 3.3
.

\mathbb{G}=\mathinner{\left\{C_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}F(C_{j})>0\text{ for }j=1,\dots,n\right\}}
.

Corollary 3.4
.

If
C\in\mathbb{G}
, then
E_{j}
is an interval for all
j=1,\dots,n.

Proof.

First, by definition of
\mathbb{G}
, if
C\in\mathbb{G}
, then
F_{j}(C)>0
for all
j=1,\dots,n
. Further, by Definition
2.6
, it is clear that, in order for
F_{j}(C)
to be greater than
0
, then
E_{j}
cannot be empty or a singleton. Thus, by
3.1
, if
F(C)\in\mathbb{G}
, then
E_{j}
must be an interval for all
j=1,\dots,n
.
∎

The most crucial points of interest will be the intersections between parabolas, namely where
f_{i}(x)=f_{j}(x)
, or
C_{i}-(x-x_{i})^{2}=C_{j}-(x-x_{j})^{2}
.
We define the unique solution by

(3.3)

\gamma_{ij}=\displaystyle\frac{C_{i}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{i})}+\frac{x_{j}+x_{i}}{2},i\neq j.

By Equation (
3.1
),
f_{i}-f_{j}
is increasing when
i>j
and decreasing when
i<j
.
It follows that if
x>\gamma_{ij}
for some
i>j
or if
x<\gamma_{ij}
for some
i<j
, then
f_{i}(x)>f_{j}(x)
and thus
x\notin E_{j}
.
We deduce that
\beta_{j}\leq\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}
and
\alpha_{j}\geq\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}
.
Conversely, if
\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}\leq x\leq\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}
, then we deduce
f_{j}(x)\geq f_{i}(x)
for all
i
.
It follows that
E_{j}
is the intersection of the interval
\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}\leq x\leq\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}
with the set where
f_{j}(x)\geq 0
, i.e. the interval
x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\leq x\leq x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}
.
We are especially interested in the case when
E_{j}
is not empty.

The content of the following lemma is essentially to say that when
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
then Figure
2
is exactly the right visualization of the function
f^{(C)}(x)
, in the sense that the points of intersections between parabolas can be ordered from left to right.
In particular, in order to locate
E^{(C)}_{j}
, it is sufficient to identify where
f_{j}^{(C)}(x)
and its nearest neighbors
f_{j+1}^{(C)}(x)
and
f_{j-1}^{(C)}(x)
intersect.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
C\in\mathbb{G}
, we have
\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}=\gamma_{j(j+1)}
and
\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}=\gamma_{(j-1)j}
.
Thus
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
are continuous functions of
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
given by
\alpha_{j}=\max\{\gamma_{(j-1)j},x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\}
and
\beta_{j}=\min\{\gamma_{j(j+1)},x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}\}
, which results in the following formulas:

(3.4)

\alpha_{j}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{C_{j-1}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}+\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2}\text{ if }f_{j}(\alpha_{j})>0,\\
x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}},\text{ if }f_{j}(\alpha_{j})=0,\end{cases}\beta_{j}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{C_{j}-C_{j+1}}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{x_{j+1}+x_{j}}{2},\text{ if }f_{j}(\beta_{j})>0,\\
x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}},\text{ if }f_{j}(\beta_{j})=0.\end{cases}

Moreover, they satisfy the ordering property
\beta_{j-1}\leq\alpha_{j}<\beta_{j}
for all
j
(where
\beta_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-\infty
for convenience), and
\beta_{j-1}=\alpha_{j}
if and only if
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})\geq 0
.

Proof.

We claim that
\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}=\gamma_{j(j+1)}
.
Suppose to the contrary that there exists some
i>j+1
such that
\gamma_{ij}<\gamma_{j(j+1)}
.
We will now show that
E_{j+1}
is empty.
Indeed, if
x<\gamma_{j(j+1)}
, then we have
f_{j}(x)>f_{j+1}(x)
, so
x\notin E_{j+1}
.
On the other hand, since
\gamma_{j(j+1)}>\gamma_{ij}
, it follows that
f_{i}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})>f_{j}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})=f_{j+1}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})
.
Then since
f_{i}-f_{j+1}
is increasing, we see that
f_{i}(x)>f_{j+1}(x)
for all
x\geq\gamma_{j(j+1)}
, so
E_{j+1}
is empty.
This contradicts the assumption that
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
, so the claim follows.
The mirror image of this argument shows that
\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}=\gamma_{(j-1)j}
.

We have already established that
E_{j}
is the intersection of the interval
\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}\leq x\leq\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}
with the set where
f_{j}(x)\geq 0
, i.e. the interval
x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\leq x\leq x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}
.
From here it is straightforward to deduce the formulas in Equation (
3.4
).
The ordering property
\beta_{j-1}\leq\alpha_{j}
follows from
\beta_{j-1}\leq\gamma_{(j-1)j}\leq\alpha_{j}
, and
\alpha_{j}<\beta_{j}
because
E_{j}
is not empty.
If
\beta_{j-1}=\alpha_{j}
, then they both must equal
\gamma_{(j-1)j}
, which implies that
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})\geq 0
.
Conversely, if
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})=f_{j-1}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})\geq 0
, then we deduce that
x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\leq\gamma_{(j-1)j}\leq x_{j-1}+\sqrt{C_{j-1}}
, and this implies
\beta_{j-1}=\alpha_{j}=\gamma_{(j-1)j}
.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

If
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
, then the length of
E_{j}(C)
is given by

(3.5)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}=k_{j}(C)=\min\{\gamma_{j,j+1}(C),x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}\}-\max\{\gamma_{j-1,j}(C),x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\}.

In fact,
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
if and only if
k_{j}(C)>0
for all
j
.
In particular,
{\mathbb{G}}
is a convex set.

Proof.

If
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
, use Lemma
3.5
to see that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}=\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}=k_{j}(C)>0
.
If
C\notin{\mathbb{G}}
, then at least one
E_{j}
must be a singleton or empty, i.e.
f_{j}(x)
is never the maximum in
f(x)=\max\{f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x),0\}
.
We deduce that one of the following inequalities must be true:

(1)

\gamma_{j(j+1)}<\gamma_{(j-1)j}

(2)

\gamma_{(j-1)j}>x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}

(3)

\gamma_{j(j+1)}<x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}

hence
k_{j}(C)\leq 0
.

Since
k_{j}(C)
is a concave function and
{\mathbb{G}}=\{C\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}k_{j}(C)>0\ \forall j\}
, it follows that
{\mathbb{G}}
is convex.
∎

We make one more simple observation about
{\mathbb{G}}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

\mathbb{G}
is non-empty.

Proof.

To show
\mathbb{G}
is non-empty, by
3.3
, we need a
C
such that
F_{j}(C)>0
for all
j=1,\dots,n
. So, let
\epsilon>0
. Choose
\epsilon
small enough so that, if
C_{j}=\epsilon
for all
j=1,\dots,n
, then
f_{j}(\alpha_{j})=0=f_{j}(\beta_{j})
for all
j=1,\dots,n
, i.e. no
f_{j}
intersects any
f_{i},i\neq j
above the
x
-axis. Then
E_{j}=[x_{j}-\sqrt{\epsilon},x_{j}+\sqrt{\epsilon}]
for all
j=1,\dots,n
. Thus, we have

(3.6)

F_{j}(C)=\frac{4}{3}\epsilon^{3/2},

which is greater than
0
. Thus,
\mathbb{G}
is non-empty.
∎

Thus, the set
\mathbb{G}
is both non-empty and convex, which will be useful in the proof of uniqueness of equilibria.

3.2.
Computing
F_{j}(C)
and its Derivatives

In this section, we want to calculate
F_{j}(C)
and all of its derivatives for
C\in\mathbb{G}
. So, we have the following:

Proposition 3.8
.

F_{j}(C)=C_{j}(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j})-\frac{1}{3}(\beta_{j}-x_{j})^{3}+\frac{1}{3}(\alpha_{j}-x_{j})^{3}
for every
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
.

Proof.

We can compute this explicitly:

(3.7)

F_{j}(C)=\int_{\alpha_{j}}^{\beta_{j}}C_{j}-(x-x_{j})^{2}\dif x=C_{j}(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j})-\frac{1}{3}(\beta_{j}-x_{j})^{3}+\frac{1}{3}(\alpha_{j}-x_{j})^{3}.

In particular, taking into account the different possibilities for
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
(see 3.2):

\begin{split}&F_{j}(C)=\int_{\alpha_{j}}^{\beta_{j}}C_{j}-(x-x_{j})^{2}\dif x=C_{j}(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j})-\frac{1}{3}(\beta_{j}-x_{j})^{3}+\frac{1}{3}(\alpha_{j}-x_{j})^{3}\\
&\text{If }f_{j}(\alpha_{j}),f_{j}(\beta_{j})>0,\\
&F_{j}(C)=C_{j}\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j}-C_{j+1}}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{x_{j+1}+x_{j}}{2}\right)}-\frac{1}{3}\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j}-C_{j+1}}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{x_{j+1}+x_{j}}{2}-x_{j}\right)}^{3}\\
&-C_{j}\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j-1}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}+\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2}\right)}+\frac{1}{3}\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j-1}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}+\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2}-x_{j}\right)}^{3},\\
&\text{or if }f_{j}(\alpha_{j}),f_{j}(\beta_{j})=0,\\
&F_{j}(C)=\mathinner{\left(C_{j}(x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}})-\frac{1}{3}(x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}-x_{j})^{3}\right)}-\mathinner{\left(C_{j}(x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}})-\frac{1}{3}(x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}-x_{j})^{3}\right)}=\frac{4}{3}C_{j}^{3/2}.\end{split}

The other two cases, when
f_{j}(\alpha_{j})>0,f_{j}(\beta_{j})=0
and when
f_{j}(\alpha_{j})=0,f_{j}(\beta_{j})>0
, are straightforward variations of these two cases.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

These calculations show that, in particular,
0\leq F_{j}(C)\leq\frac{4}{3}C_{j}^{3/2}
for any
C
.
This will be useful later.

Proposition 3.10
.

\displaystyle\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}({C})=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}+\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}
for every
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
.

Proof.

With
F(C)
now calculated, we can easily calculate
\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}
, taking into account the different possibilities for
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
as above:

(3.8)

\begin{split}&\text{If }f_{j}(\alpha_{j}),f_{j}(\beta_{j})>0,\\
\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}=&\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j}-C_{j+1}}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{x_{{j+1}}+x_{j}}{2}\right)}+\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j}}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\right)}-{\frac{1}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}}\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j}-C_{j+1}}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{x_{{j+1}}-x_{j}}{2}\right)}^{2}\\
-&\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j-1}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}+\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2}\right)}+\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{i}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}\right)}-{\frac{1}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}}\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j-1}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}-\frac{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}{2}\right)}^{2}\\
=&\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}+\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})},\\
&\text{or if }f_{j}(\alpha_{j}),f_{j}(\beta_{j})=0,\\
\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}=&\ 2C_{j}.\end{split}

Again, the other two cases are straightforward adaptations. In any case, we have

(3.9)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}+\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}+\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})},

which is a continuous function of
C
.
Notice that this holds for every case; for example, if
f_{j}(\alpha_{j}),f_{j}(\beta_{j})=0
, then those components of the partial derivative would be 0, leaving
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}
, which is in fact
2C_{j}
in that case.
In particular, the partial derivative is seen to be continuous everywhere, since as we approach the boundary cases where
f_{j}(\alpha_{j})
or
f_{j}(\beta_{j})
goes to zero, the formula still holds in the limit.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

For every
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
,

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j-1}}}}=\displaystyle\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}
,
\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j+1}}}}=\displaystyle\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}
, and
\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{i}}}}=0
if
i\notin\{j-1,j,j+1\}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\beta_{j-1}<\alpha_{j}
.
From Lemma
3.5
, we see that
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})<0
and thus
E_{j}
does not depend on
C_{j-1}
for small perturbations of
C
.
Similarly, if
\beta_{j}<\alpha_{j+1}
, then
E_{j}
does not depend on
C_{j+1}
for small perturbations of
C
.
More generally, we deduce that
E_{j}

never
depends on
C_{i}
for
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
if
i\notin\{j-1,j,j+1\}
.

So we only consider the case when
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j}),f_{j}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})\geq 0
.
In this case Lemma
3.5
implies that
\alpha_{j}=\gamma_{(j-1)j}
and
\beta_{j}=\gamma_{j(j+1)}
.
The case
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})=f_{j}(\alpha_{j})>0
is stable under small perturbations of
C
, and we differentiate the formula from Proposition
3.8
to get

(3.10)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j-1}}}}=-\frac{1}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j}-C_{j-1}}{2(x_{j-1}-x_{j})}+\frac{x_{j-1}-x_{j}}{2}\right)}^{2}-C_{j}\right)}=\frac{f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}=\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}.

As
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})\to 0
, this formula converges to 0, which agrees with the case
f_{j}(\gamma_{(j-1)j})<0
.
Similarly, the case
f_{j}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})=f_{j}(\beta_{j})>0
is stable under small perturbations of
C
, and we differentiate the formula from Proposition
3.8
to get

(3.11)

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j+1}}}}={\frac{1}{2(x_{j}-x_{j+1})}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\left(\frac{C_{j+1}-C_{j}}{2(x_{j}-x_{j+1})}-\frac{x_{j}-x_{j+1}}{2}\right)}^{2}-C_{j}\right)}}=\frac{f_{j}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}=\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}.

As
f_{j}(\gamma_{j(j+1)}\to 0
, this formula converges to 0, which agrees with the case
f_{j}(\gamma_{j(j+1)})<0
.
This establishes the existence and continuity of the partial derivatives.
∎

We notice the following, deduced from the formulas in Lemma
3.5
and the result of Theorem
3.11
:

Remark 3.12
.

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{i}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}=\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{i}}}}

for every
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
and for every
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

This implies that
F
is in fact a gradient, i.e. there exists a smooth function
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\mathbb{G}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
F(C)=\nabla\Phi(C)
for all
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
.
This is a remarkable coincidence, as it is already well-known that the mean field game (
1.1
) is a
potential game
, meaning that there exists a potential whose critical points are Nash equilibria.
However, it must be emphasized that usually this potential is defined on the space of (paths defined on) probability measures, and its derivative is supposed to be the cost paid to individual players.
See e.g.
[
BCS17
,
CH17
,
Gra24
]
,
[
LL07
, Section 2.6]
,
[
CD17
]
and references therein.
Here the context is quite different, as we have significantly transformed the problem, so the “potential” in this case does not have precisely the same meaning as in these references.

3.3.
Coercivity

We say that the vector field
F(C)
is coercive provided that
\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}\to\infty}\frac{F(C)\cdot C}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}}=\infty
, where
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\left(C_{1}^{2}+\cdots+C_{n}^{2}\right)}^{1/2}
is the norm of the vector
C
.
This is the usual condition for a vector field to be surjective, as in the Minty-Browder Theorem
[
Bro67
]
.
There is no chance for
F(C)
to be coercive on all of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, since
F_{j}(C)=0
whenever
C_{j}\leq 0
.
However, this will not matter, is we are always aiming at restricting to vectors
C
with non-negative components.
Instead, we will use the following substitute for coercivity to prove existence of solutions.

Lemma 3.13
.

There exist constants
\delta\in(0,1)
and
M>0
such that if
C
is any vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
C_{j}=\max\{C_{1},...,C_{n}\}\geq M
, then
F_{j}(C)\geq\delta C_{j}
.

Proof.

Choose a
C_{j}
such that
C_{j}=\max\{C_{1},...,C_{n}\}\geq M
. We now seek to estimate
F_{j}({C})
. Recall that, from the discussion before Lemma
3.5
,
E_{j}
is the intersection of the interval

\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}\leq x\leq\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}
with the interval
x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\leq x\leq x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}
.
By the maximality of
C_{j}
,

\begin{split}\max\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i<j\}&\leq\max\mathinner{\left\{\frac{x_{j}+x_{i}}{2},i<j\right\}}=\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2},\\
\min\{\gamma_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i>j\}&\leq\min\mathinner{\left\{\frac{x_{j}+x_{i}}{2},i>j\right\}}=\frac{x_{j}+x_{j+1}}{2}.\end{split}

We can assume that

M\geq\max\mathinner{\left\{\mathinner{\left(\frac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{2}\right)}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i=1,\ldots,n-1\right\}}.

Then it follows that

x_{j}-\sqrt{C_{j}}\leq\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2},\quad x_{j}+\sqrt{C_{j}}\geq\frac{x_{j}+x_{j+1}}{2}.

We can now conclude that
E_{j}=[\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}]
with

\alpha_{j}\leq\frac{x_{j-1}+x_{j}}{2},\quad\beta_{j}\geq\frac{x_{j}+x_{j+1}}{2}.

This gives a lower bound on how close
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
can get to
x_{j}
.
Thus,

\begin{split}F_{j}({C})\geq\int_{\frac{x_{j-1}+x_{j}}{2}}^{\frac{x_{j}+x_{j+1}}{2}}C_{j}-(x-x_{j})^{2}\dif x=C_{j}\frac{x_{j+1}-x_{j-1}}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\left(\frac{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}{2}\right)}^{3}-\mathinner{\left(\frac{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}{2}\right)}^{3}\right)}.\end{split}

Define

\bar{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\min_{j}\mathinner{\left\{x_{j+1}-x_{j-1}\right\}}}{2},\quad\bar{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{3}\max_{j}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\left(\frac{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}{2}\right)}^{3}-\mathinner{\left(\frac{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}{2}\right)}^{3}\right)}_{+},

where we recall
x_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{x,0\}
.
Then

F_{j}(C)\geq\bar{\delta}C_{j}-\bar{M}.

Pick any
0<\delta<\min\{\bar{\delta},1\}
and assume
M\geq(\bar{\delta}-\delta)^{-1}\bar{M}
.
If
C_{j}\geq M
, we get
F_{j}(C)\geq\delta C_{j}
, as desired.
∎

We remark that Lemma
3.13
can be used to prove that
F
is coercive on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, which is defined to be the set of all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
such that
x_{j}\geq 0
for every
j
.
The reason is that if
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
C_{j}=\max\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\}
, then

F(C)\cdot C\geq F_{j}(C)C_{j}.

For the remaining details, let us first define the norm

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\max\mathinner{\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x_{1}\right\rvert},\ldots,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x_{n}\right\rvert}\right\}}

and note that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}_{\infty}=\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
Recall that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\leq\sqrt{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

Let
M,\delta
be as in Lemma
3.13
.
If
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}\geq M\sqrt{n}
, then
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{\infty}\geq M
and so

F(C)\cdot C\geq F_{j}(C)C_{j}\geq\delta C_{j}^{2}=\delta\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{\infty}^{2}\geq\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{n}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}^{2}.

Thus
F
is coercive on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
However, we also note that only Lemma
3.13
, and not coercivity per se, will be used directly to prove the existence of solutions.

3.4.
Continuity

With coercivity proved, we move to prove that
F(C)
is continuous. Before doing so, we need to explore what happens in
E_{j}=\varnothing
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathinner{\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{I}
be the set of all
C
such that
E_{j}(C)=\varnothing
for all
j\in I
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{I}
is open, i.e. for every
C\in\mathcal{C}_{I}
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that, if
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\tilde{C}-C\right\rVert}<\epsilon
, then
\tilde{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{I}
.

Proof.

Let
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
.
Define
\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)=\max\{f_{j}^{(C)}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}j\in I\}
.
Then
f^{(C)}(x)>\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)
for all
x
, because all of the sets
E_{j}(C)
,
j\in I
, are empty.
Since all of the
f_{j}^{(C)}(x)
are parabolas that go to
-\infty
as
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\to\infty
, there exists
M>0
such that if
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}>M
, then
f^{(C)}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)\geq 1
.
Now
f^{(C)}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)
is a continuous and positive function, hence it has a minimum
\delta>0
on the compact set
[-M,M]
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume
\delta\leq 1
; in particular, we have
f^{(C)}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)\geq\delta
for every
x
.
Now assume
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{C}_{j}-C_{j}\right\rvert}<\delta/2
for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.
Then
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f_{j}^{(\tilde{C})}(x)-f_{j}^{(C)}(x)\right\rvert}<\delta/2
for every
j
and every
x
.
It follows that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert f^{(\tilde{C})}(x)-f^{(C)}(x)\right\rvert}<\delta/2
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\tilde{f}^{(\tilde{C})}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)\right\rvert}<\delta/2
, so

(3.12)

\begin{split}f^{(\tilde{C})}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(\tilde{C})}(x)&=f^{(\tilde{C})}(x)-f^{(C)}(x)+f^{(C)}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)+\tilde{f}^{(C)}(x)-\tilde{f}^{(\tilde{C})}(x)\\
&>-\delta/2+\delta-\delta/2=0.\end{split}

It follows that all the sets
E_{j}(\tilde{C})
,
j\in I
, are empty, and so
\tilde{C}
must also be in
{\mathcal{C}}_{I}
. Thus,
\mathcal{C}_{I}
is open.
∎

With this in mind, we can now prove continuity:

Theorem 3.15
.

F(C)
is continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove that each component function
F_{j}(C)
is continuous.
First, let us point out that we can remove certain components from consideration if the corresponding sets
E_{j}(C)
are empty. Let
I
be the set of all indices
j
such that
E_{j}(C)=\emptyset
, so that
C\in\mathcal{C}_{I}
, the set defined in Lemma
3.14
. Take a sequence of vectors
C^{(k)}\rightarrow C
. Then, for
k
sufficiently large,
C^{(k)}\in\mathcal{C}_{I}
also. So
F_{j}(C^{(k)})=F_{j}(C)=0
for every
j\in I
. In light of this, we can remove the indices in
I
and relabel the sequence
\mathinner{\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}}\setminus I
, calling it
\mathinner{\left\{1,\dots,m\right\}}
. Then
f(x)
will still be the
\max\{f_{1}(x),f_{2}(x),\dots,f_{m}(x),0\}
, as those indices which may have been removed will not affect the maximum. Thus, we can assume (without loss of generality) for the remainder of this proof that
E_{j}(C)\neq\varnothing
for every
j
, and thus we will be able to appeal to previously derived explicit formulas for the component functions
F_{j}(C)
.

Let
j
be fixed but arbitrary.
Since
E_{j}(C)
is not empty, by Lemma
3.1
it is either an interval or a single point.
Suppose that
E_{j}(C)
is an interval.
Choose a sequence of vectors
C^{(k)}\rightarrow C
. By Proposition
3.8
,
F_{j}(C)
is simply a polynomial in terms of
C
, and thus
F_{j}(C^{(k)})\rightarrow F_{j}(C)
.

Lastly, suppose
E_{j}(C)
is a single point. Then, by the contra-positive of Corollary
3.4
,
F_{j}(C)=0
. Again, we choose a sequence of vectors
C^{(k)}\rightarrow C
. So, we need to show
F_{j}(C^{(k)})\rightarrow 0
.
If
F_{j}(C^{(k)})>0
then
E_{j}(C^{(k)})
is an interval whose length is given by
k_{j}(C^{(k)})
defined in Corollary
3.6
.
Further, since
k_{j}(C)
is a continuous function of
C
and
E_{j}(C)
is a single point, then
k_{j}(C^{(k)})\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, whenever
E_{j}(C^{(k)})
is an interval, its length shrinks to zero as
k\rightarrow\infty
, and since in any other case we have
F_{j}(C^{(k)})=0
, we deduce that
F_{j}(C^{(k)})\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
.
∎

We have now sufficiently explored various properties of
f(x)
and
F(C)
, so we may now move onto our first goal:

4.
Proving Existence of a Solution

In this section, we seek to show that a solution
C
to the equation
F(C)=a
exists for all
a
with positive components. Before we begin the proof, we state Brouwer’s Fixed Point Theorem:

Lemma 4.1
.

Every continuous function from a nonempty convex compact subset
K
of a Euclidean space to
K
itself has a fixed point.

See e.g.
[
Flo03
]
. We now begin the main result:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
with
a_{j}>0
for each
j
.
Then there exists a solution
C
to the equation
F(C)=a
.

Proof.

Let
a
be given. Define
L\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
as
L_{i}(C)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathinner{\left(C_{i}+a_{i}-F_{i}(C)\right)}_{+}
(recall
x_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{x,0\}
).
We will use the norm

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert C_{1}\right\rvert}+\cdots+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert C_{n}\right\rvert}=C_{1}+\cdots+C_{n}.

It is useful to recall that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{\infty}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}\leq n\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{\infty}.

By Lemma
3.13
, there exist constants
\delta\in(0,1)
and
M>0
such that if
C_{j}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{\infty}\geq M
, then
F_{j}(C)\geq\delta C_{j}
.
In this case,

L_{j}(C)\leq\mathinner{\left((1-\delta)C_{j}+a_{j}\right)}_{+}=(1-\delta)C_{j}+a_{j}.

In general,

L_{i}(C)\leq C_{i}+a_{i}.

Summing over all
i
, we deduce that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert L(C)\right\rVert}_{1}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\right\rVert}_{1}-\delta C_{j}.

Recall that
C_{j}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{\infty}\geq n^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}
.
Let
r>\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\right\rVert}_{1}n/\delta
.
Then we have

(4.1)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert L(C)\right\rVert}_{1}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\right\rVert}_{1}-\delta n^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}<\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}\quad\text{if}~\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}\geq r.

We now restrict to a compact, convex set.
Let
M_{r}
be the set of all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}_{1}\leq r
. Then
M_{r}
is closed and bounded, so
M_{r}
is compact, and it is clearly nonempty. Lastly,
M_{r}
is convex, as it is the intersection of two convex sets, namely,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and the closed unit ball for the 1-norm. Thus, by Lemma
4.1
, any continuous function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}M_{r}\rightarrow M_{r}
has a fixed point.

Define a function

(4.2)

P(D)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle D,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\right\rVert}_{1}\leq r\\
\frac{rD}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\right\rVert}_{1}},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert D\right\rVert}_{1}>r.\end{cases}

Note that
P
maps
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
into
M_{r}
.
Finally, let
Q(C)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=P(L(C))
for
C\in M_{r}
. Then
Q
is continuous, as it is the composition of continuous functions, and it maps
M_{r}
to itself. Thus, by Lemma
4.1
,
Q
has a fixed point
C\in M_{r}
. If
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}=r
, then

(4.3)

r=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q(C)\right\rVert}_{1}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert P(L(C))\right\rVert}_{1}.

By construction of
P
, this implies

(4.4)

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert L(C)\right\rVert}_{1}\geq r.

But this contradicts (
4.1
).
It follows that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}<r
. Thus,

(4.5)

r>\mathinner{\!\left\lVert C\right\rVert}_{1}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Q(C)\right\rVert}_{1}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert P(L(C))\right\rVert}_{1}.

Hence,
P(L(C))=L(C)
, so finally we have

(4.6)

C=Q(C)=P(L(C))=L(C).

This implies that for each
i
,

C_{i}=\mathinner{\left(C_{i}+a_{i}-F_{i}(C)\right)}_{+}.

If
C_{i}>0
, this implies

C_{i}=C_{i}+a_{i}-F_{i}(C)\Rightarrow F_{i}(C)=a_{i}.

If
C_{i}=0
, then
F_{i}(C)=0
and we get

0=\mathinner{\left(a_{i}\right)}_{+}=a_{i}

which contradicts the assumption
a_{i}>0
.
Hence
C_{i}>0
for all
i
and
F(C)=a
, as desired.
∎

5.
Proving Uniqueness of a Solution

By Theorem
4.2
, we know that for any vector
a
with positive entries there exists a solution
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
to
F(C)=a
. In this section, we prove that the solution is unique. To do so, we show that
F(C)
is strictly monotone in a classical sense.
It is interesting to note that monotonicity is often used to prove uniqueness in mean field games, the most commonly used notion being the “Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition” (cf.
[
LL07
]
).
(For a discussion on alternative monotonicity conditions, see
[
GM23
]
.)
Here, the coupling is indeed precisely through the density of players, and therefore the Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition holds.
However, it is interesting to note that the monotonicity of
F(C)
, a function of the parameters
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
, is not a direct consequence the Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition in any obvious way.

Lemma 5.1
.

On
\mathbb{G}
,
F(C)
is strictly monotone, i.e. for any
C,\tilde{C}\in\mathbb{G},(F(C)-F(\tilde{C})(C-\tilde{C})>0
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathbb{G}
is convex by Corollary
3.6
.
To show F is strictly monotone, then, it suffices to show that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{i}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}\cdot(v_{i}v_{j})>0\ \forall\ {C},{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ with }v\neq 0.

Assume for the sake of notation that
v_{0}=0=v_{n+1}
. By Lemma 2.11,

S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{i}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}v_{i}v_{j}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j}}}}(v_{j})^{2}+\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j-1}}}}(v_{{j-1}}v_{j})+\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}F_{j}(C)}{\partial{C_{j+1}}}}(v_{j}v_{{j+1}})\right)}.

Using the derivative formulas from (
3.10
),

S=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathinner{\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}+\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}+\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\right)}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}\right)}^{2}\\
-\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}\right)}\mathinner{\left(v_{j-1}v_{j}\right)}-\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\right)}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}v_{j+1}\right)}.

Since
f_{1}(\alpha_{1})=0=f_{n}(\beta_{n})
, we can rewrite this as

S=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}}{v_{j}}^{2}+\sum_{j=2}^{n}\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}\right)}^{2}-\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\alpha_{j})}{2(x_{j}-x_{j-1})}\right)}\mathinner{\left(v_{j-1}v_{j}\right)}\right)}\\
+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}\right)}^{2}-\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{{2(x_{(j+1)}-x_{j})}}\right)}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}v_{j+1}\right)}\right)}.

Since
f_{j+1}(\alpha_{j+1})
is either
f_{j}(\beta_{j})
or
0
, we can combine to get

\begin{split}S=\displaystyle&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}}{v_{j}}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\mathinner{\left(v_{j+1}\right)}^{2}-\frac{2f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}v_{j+1}\right)}+\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\mathinner{\left(v_{j}\right)}^{2}\\
=\displaystyle&\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}}{v_{j}}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathinner{\left(\frac{f_{j}(\beta_{j})}{2(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}\right)}\mathinner{\left(v_{j+1}-v_{j}\right)}^{2}>0.\end{split}

Thus,
F(C)
is strictly monotone on
\mathbb{G}
.
∎

We now have everything we need to prove uniqueness.

Theorem 5.2
.

The solution to the equation
F(C)=a
is unique.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{G}
with
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
are both solutions to
F(C)=a
. Then
F(C_{1})=F(C_{2})
, so
(F(C_{1})-F(C_{2})(C_{1}-C_{2})=0
. But this contradicts
5.1
. Thus, the solution to
F(C)
is unique.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

As a result of Lemma
5.1
, the potential
\Phi
defined in Remark
3.12
is strictly convex on
{\mathbb{G}}
.
The unique solution of
F(C)=a
can be characterized as the unique minimizer of
\Phi(C)-C\cdot a
over
C\in{\mathbb{G}}
.

We have now shown that a unique solution to
F(C)=a
exists and is unique. We now move on to numerical approximations.

6.
Numerical Simulations

We used Newton’s Method to find approximate solutions to
F(C)=a
.
By algebraically solving
\frac{4}{3}C_{j}^{3/2}=a_{j}
, we obtain an initial guess
C=C^{(0)}
such that
F_{j}(C)\leq a_{j}
for each
j
(see Remark
3.9
).
Then we define an approximating sequence
C^{(n)}
by

(6.1)

C^{(n+1)}=C^{(n)}-DF\mathinner{\left(C^{(n)}\right)}^{-1}\mathinner{\left(F\mathinner{\left(C^{(n)}\right)}-a\right)},

where
DF(C)
is the Jacobian matrix computed in Section
3.2
.
The residual
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F\mathinner{\left(C^{(n)}\right)}-a\right\rVert}
can be computed easily; in practice it becomes vanishingly small after only a few iterations.
Below we report on three simulations.
In each case, the initial distribution
m=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
is illustrated a set of
n
bars located at position
x_{j}
with height
a_{j}
.
Then the final density
f(x)
is then graphed over these bars.
The function
f(x)
is piecewise quadratic with the
j
th parabola centered at
x_{j}
and representing the spreading of the population that starts at
x_{j}
; the area under this parabola above the
x
-axis equals
a_{j}
.
This accords with our geometric intuition about the game: players spread out as much as possible so as to avoid areas of high population density, and the shape of the final density matches the quadratic cost
(x-y)^{2}
of traveling from
x
to
y
.

Figure 3.
Example 1:
x=(1,2.25,3,3.75),a=(0.25,0.25,0.25,0.25)

In the first simulation (Figure
3
), we took an initial population uniformly distributed over four points:
m=\frac{1}{4}\mathinner{\left(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2.25}+\delta_{3}+\delta_{3.75}\right)}
.
In this example, the leftmost quarter of the population is far enough away from rest that it can spread out without colliding with the others, whereas the other three are close enough to each other that players have less room to move, resulting in a narrower probability density.

Figure 4.
Example 2:
x=(1,2,\dots,10),a=random

Figure 5.
Example 3:
x=random
,
a=random

In the second simulation (Figure
4
), we let
x_{j}-x_{j-1}
be constant and let
a
be random. (Note that we did not normalize the components of
a
to sum to 1, but this does not change the shape of the solution.) Notice how in this sample the probability density has a very interesting contour. For example, the “bubble” (i.e. piece of parabola) corresponding to
x=9
barely appears because it is crowded by neighboring “bubbles.” This demonstrates how the different weights locally change the shape of the density.

In the last simulation (Figure
5
), the vectors
x
and
a
are random (and
a
is normalized to be a probability vector).
Although the initial measure is highly irregular, the final density will still be smooth, though it appears less so as the number of random points are chosen.
Indeed, as the proof of uniqueness in Section
5
suggests, the problem becomes more and more ill-conditioned as the points
x_{j}
get closer together, since the lengths of the intervals
E_{j}
necessarily get smaller.

As a final remark, we recall that
F=\nabla\Phi
for some potential
\Phi
, and that solving
F(C)=a
is equivalent to minimizing
\Phi(C)-C\cdot a
(see Remarks
3.12
and
5.3
).
Since
\Phi
is strictly convex, one might hope that Newton’s method converges globally.
However, it is not true in general that strict convexity implies global convergence for Newton’s method, as explained in this Stack Exchange discussion
[
new
]
; see also
[
BV04
]
.
The proof of Lemma
5.1
suggests that the lower bound on the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\Phi(C)
goes to zero as any of the lengths
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert E_{j}\right\rVert}
vanishes.
One can infer that the problem is better conditioned if the points
x_{j}
are sufficiently spread out and/or the weights
a_{j}
are such that the equilibrium density has a uniform lower bound on the lengths of the intervals
E_{j}
.
We see this in numerical experiments: if the distance between points
x_{j}
becomes too small, the algorithm appears not to converge.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper we have studied a mean field game with final cost equal to the population density of players.
We considered the case where the initial measure is discrete, which seems to be new in the literature.
We showed that the problem reduces to a finite dimensional problem, which can prove is well-posed by classical methods.
Our numerical simulations illustrate the “smoothing effect” that mean field games with density penalization are expected to have.
For future research, it would be interesting to see if this approach could be extended to more general mean field games with density-dependent costs and discrete initial measures.
