The inviscid limit of viscous Burgers at nondegenerate shock formation
Sanchit Chaturvedi, Cole Graham

Abstract
. We study the vanishing viscosity limit of the one-dimensional Burgers equation near nondegenerate shock formation. We develop a matched asymptotic expansion that describes small-viscosity solutions to arbitrary order up to the moment the first shock forms. The inner part of this expansion has a novel structure based on a fractional spacetime Taylor series for the inviscid solution. We obtain sharp vanishing viscosity rates in a variety of norms, including L ∞ L^{\infty} . Comparable prior results break down in the vicinity of shock formation. We partially fill this gap.



1.
Introduction

We study the one-dimensional viscous Burgers equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u^{\nu}=\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\nu}-u^{\nu}\partial_{x}u^{\nu},\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

indexed by the viscosity
\nu>0
.
We are interested in the vanishing viscosity limit
\nu\to 0^{+}
.
Formally setting
\nu=0
, we obtain

(1.2)

\partial_{t}u^{0}=-u^{0}\partial_{x}u^{0}.

This is the inviscid Burgers equation, the prototypical hyperbolic conservation law.
Inviscid Burgers famously develops shocks—if we start (
1.2
) from generic smooth initial data
\mathring{u}
, then the solution will form a shock in finite time.
In contrast, for fixed
\nu>0
, the dissipation in the viscous model is strong enough to prevent shocks: solutions of (
1.1
) remain smooth for all time.
There is thus a marked qualitative difference between the viscous and inviscid equations.
In this article, we focus on the moment when this difference first manifests.
We study the vanishing viscosity limit
u^{\nu}\to u^{0}
up to the time
u^{0}
first forms a shock.
We develop an asymptotic expansion for
u^{\nu}
as
\nu\to 0^{+}
and thereby prove sharp vanishing viscosity rates in a variety of norms.

1.1.
Background

The rich nature of the vanishing viscosity limit has long attracted a great deal of attention.
Here, we merely graze its expansive history.

Despite its name, the Burgers equation (
1.1
) was introduced by Bateman
[
5
]
, who used its traveling wave solutions to illustrate the subtlety of the small-viscosity regime in fluids.
In a similar spirit, Burgers considered (
1.1
) as a toy model for turbulence and discussed the propagation of shocks in the
\nu\to 0
limit.

Hopf provided the first thorough mathematical treatment of the Burgers equation.
In
[
27
]
, he used the eponymous Cole–Hopf transformation (discovered independently by Cole
[
18
]
) to convert (
1.1
) to the linear heat equation.
Taking
\nu\to 0
in this transformation, Hopf derived a variational formula for a particular weak solution of the inviscid problem (
1.2
).

In general, weak solutions of (
1.2
) are not unique.
However, a certain “entropy condition” inspired by the second law of thermodynamics selects a unique weak solution, known as the entropy solution.
Remarkably, this entropy solution coincides with the inviscid limit of (
1.1
) studied by Hopf.
The vanishing viscosity limit is thus deeply intertwined with the well-posedness of the
inviscid
equation (
1.2
).

The Cole–Hopf transformation is powerful, but it has a major limitation: it relies on the precise algebraic structure of the Burgers equation.
In particular, it has no analogue for general viscous scalar conservation laws, which have the form

(1.3)

\partial_{t}w^{\nu}=\nu\partial_{x}^{2}w^{\nu}-\partial_{x}(f\circ w^{\nu})

for typically convex
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Nonetheless, the Burgers example suggests that
w^{\nu}
converges to the unique entropy solution of the corresponding inviscid law as
\nu\to 0.

This general phenomenon was confirmed by Oleĭnik
[
41
]
, who used a finite difference scheme to overcome the lack of the Cole–Hopf transformation.

The above results on the inviscid limit are purely qualitative.
Kružkov
[
32
]
established the first
quantitative
rate of convergence:
\|w^{\nu}-w^{0}\|_{L_{x}^{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\nu}
.
This rate holds in a wide class of advection–diffusion equations.
However, when
f
is strictly convex, the nonlinear advection in (
1.3
)
accelerates
the convergence.
Goodman and Xin
[
24
]
showed that the
L^{1}
error in the viscous approximation of piecewise-smooth solutions is
\mathcal{O}_{\varepsilon}(\nu^{1-\varepsilon})
.
Tang and Teng
[
47
]
subsequently refined this estimate to
\mathcal{O}(\nu\log\nu^{-1})
.
Using the Cole–Hopf transformation, Wang
[
48
]
has shown that this improved rate is sharp for viscous Burgers (
1.1
).
In fact, if the initial data is smooth,
\|u^{\nu}-u^{0}\|_{L_{x}^{1}}\asymp\nu\log\nu^{-1}
precisely when
u^{0}
develops a new shock.
(See Section
2.1
for our definition of relations such as
\mathcal{O},

\lesssim
, and
\asymp
.)
This sharp case partially motivates our detailed study of the vanishing viscosity limit around shock formation.

As these results indicate, the bulk of the vanishing viscosity literature for conservation laws focuses on
L^{1}
error.
However, both
u^{\nu}
and
u^{0}
are typically much more regular (e.g., of bounded variation).
We thus wish to quantify
{u^{\nu}-u^{0}}
in stronger norms that reflect this regularity.
Goodman and Xin pioneered such estimates—they described
u^{\nu}
with great precision near isolated shocks in systems of hyperbolic conservation laws
[
24
]
.
Their methods and results are the primary motivation for our work.
In
[
24
]
, Goodman and Xin developed a complete matched asymptotic expansion for the viscous solution
u^{\nu}
when
u^{0}
has a weak well-developed Lax shock (i.e., a small, nonzero jump discontinuity satisfying certain spectral conditions).
In a tour de force, Yu extended this work to include nonlinear wave phenomena arising from discontinuous initial data
[
53
]
.

Suppose
u^{0}
solves (
1.2
) with smooth initial data.
Typically,
u^{0}
will develop a shock at a finite time
t_{*}>0
.
Fix a small time-step
\tau>0
.
Before time
t_{*}-\tau
, the inviscid solution
u^{0}
is uniformly smooth.
Thus in the pre-shock period
[0,t_{*}-\tau]
, the viscous approximation (
1.1
) can be sharply treated by standard perturbation theory.
In particular, it is straightforward to check that
\|u^{\nu}-u^{0}\|\lesssim_{\tau}\nu
in a variety of norms.
However, such estimates deteriorate as
\tau\to 0
.

On the other hand,
[
24
]
and
[
53
]
describe the viscous approximation
u^{\nu}
in great detail at times after
t_{*}+\tau
, provided the shock remains isolated and weak.
Again, however, this description degenerates as
\tau\to 0
.
Therefore, neither approach treats the vanishing viscosity limit throughout the crucial period
[t_{*}-\tau,t_{*}+\tau]
of shock formation.
In this article, we fill the first half of this gap: we characterize
u^{\nu}
for
\nu\ll 1
on the entire period
[0,t_{*}]
.
We develop an asymptotic description of
u^{\nu}
that remains valid up to the first moment of shock formation, and thus prove sharp rates of convergence for
u^{\nu}-u^{0}
in, for example,
L^{\infty}
.
Moreover, we make no use of the Cole–Hopf transformation, so our approach extends to scalar conservation laws (
1.3
) with strictly convex flux.
We leave the fascinating second stage of shock formation
[t_{*},t_{*}+\tau]
to future work.

1.2.
Setup

We now discuss the structure of
u^{0}
and
u^{\nu}
in greater detail.
Suppose
u^{0}
solves (
1.2
) with initial condition
u^{0}(0,\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}
.
We can treat the inviscid equation (
1.2
) with the method of characteristics so long as
u^{0}
remains smooth.
Along characteristics, the slope
\partial_{x}u^{0}
solves the Riccati equation
\dot{y}=-y^{2}
.
Thus negative slopes successively steepen until they reach
-\infty
in finite time.
Suppose
{\min\partial_{x}\mathring{u}<0}
.
Then
u^{0}
will first develop a shock on the characteristic corresponding to the steepest point of
\mathring{u}
, namely
\mathring{x}\coloneqq\argmin\partial_{x}\mathring{u}.

Because
\partial_{x}u^{0}
is minimized along this critical characteristic,
\partial_{x}^{2}u^{0}
always vanishes there.
Hence the local behavior of the first shock is determined by the higher-order structure of
\mathring{u}
at
\mathring{x}
.
In this article, we assume that the first shock is
nondegenerate
, meaning
\partial_{x}^{3}\mathring{u}(\mathring{x})>0
.
Precisely, we make the following assumptions on our initial data
\mathring{u}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

(H1)

\mathring{u}
is smooth;

(H2)

There exist
\mathring{c}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathring{L}>0
such that
\mathring{u}\equiv\mathring{c}
on
[-\mathring{L},\mathring{L}]^{c}
;

(H3)

\min\partial_{x}\mathring{u}<0
and
\mathring{x}=\argmin\partial_{x}\mathring{u}
is unique;

(H4)

\partial_{x}^{3}\mathring{u}(\mathring{x})>0
.

Define

t_{0}\coloneqq\left(\min\partial_{x}\mathring{u}\right)^{-1}<0.

We shift time to begin at
t_{0}<0
.
For the remainder of the paper, let
u^{0}
denote the unique entropy solution to the inviscid equation (
1.2
) with

u^{0}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}.

The method of characteristics shows that
u^{0}
is smooth until time
t_{*}=0
, when it develops an infinite slope at position

x_{*}\coloneqq\mathring{x}-\mathring{u}(\mathring{x})t_{0}.

After a Galilean transformation of spacetime, we are free to assume that

(5)

\displaystyle\mathring{x}=\mathring{u}(\mathring{x})=0,

so that in addition
x_{*}=0.

Thus, we begin with a smooth initial profile
\mathring{u}
at the negative time
t_{0}
, and the inviscid solution
u^{0}
develops its first shock at the origin in spacetime.
The hypotheses
(H1)
–
(H4)
ensure that this first shock is nondegenerate and isolated.
In particular,
u^{0}(0,\,\cdot\,)
is smooth away from
x=0
.
We depict this nondegenerate shock formation in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Nondegenerate inviscid shock formation near the origin.

At times
t<0
shortly before shock formation, the solution
u^{0}(t,\,\cdot\,)
(in blue) is smooth.
At the moment
t=0
of shock formation, the solution (in purple) is continuous but not smooth—it has a spatial cusp of regularity
\mathcal{C}^{1/3}
at
x=0
.
After shock formation, the entropy solution (in red) has developed a jump discontinuity—the shock.

We view
u^{0}
as the endpoint of a family
(u^{\nu})_{\nu\geq 0}
parameterized by the viscosity.
For simplicity, we use the same initial data for the entire family:

(1.4)

u^{\nu}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}\quad\textrm{for all }\nu>0.

This is not essential—our methods can treat more general initial data that varies smoothly in
\nu
.
However, we do not pursue this direction here.

Our principal contribution is a matched asymptotic expansion linking a traditional “outer expansion” with an “inner expansion” of a novel form that captures the viscous dynamics of (
1.1
) near the origin in spacetime, where
u^{0}
develops its first shock.
The outer expansion treats the viscous term in (
1.1
) as a small perturbation and expands in powers of
\nu
:

(1.5)

u^{\nu}\approx u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}+\nu u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}+\nu^{2}u_{2}^{\mathrm{out}}+\ldots\quad\text{as }\nu\to 0.

The leading term
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
solves the inviscid problem (
1.2
), so in fact
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=u^{0}
.
The corrections
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
for
k\geq 1
solve linear transport equations forced by
\partial_{x}^{2}u_{k-1}^{\mathrm{out}}
.

This expansion should be valid wherever the viscous term
\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\nu}
is much smaller than the advection
u^{\nu}\partial_{x}u^{\nu}
.
When
\nu\ll 1
, this relation only fails near the origin, where
u^{\nu}
develops large derivatives and viscous effects dominate.
Using the method of characteristics, we show that
u^{0}
resembles a certain inverse cubic function near the origin.
For instance, at
t=0
we have a cubic cusp:

(1.6)

u^{0}(0,x)\sim-Cx^{1/3}\quad\text{as }x\to 0.

Using this inverse cubic as an ansatz for
u^{\nu}
, we can formally check that viscous effects matter when
\left|t\right|\lesssim\nu^{1/2}
and
\left|x\right|\lesssim\nu^{3/4}
.
In Section
2
, we define a “distance”
\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\asymp\left|t\right|^{1/2}+\left|x\right|^{1/3}
that quantifies the proximity of
(t,x)
to the origin
(0,0)
.
With this notation, the viscosity matters where
\mathfrak{d}\lesssim\nu^{1/4}
.
We thus expect the outer expansion (
1.5
) to hold on the complement
\{\mathfrak{d}\gg\nu^{1/4}\}
.

A different approach becomes necessary when
\mathfrak{d}\lesssim\nu^{1/4}
.
There, (
1.6
) suggests that
u^{0}\lesssim\nu^{1/4}
.
This motivates the following “inner coordinates:”

(1.7)

T\coloneqq\nu^{-1/2}t,\quad X\coloneqq\nu^{-3/4}x,\quad U\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}u.

We wish to understand the structure of the blown-up solution
U^{\nu}(T,X)
as
\nu\to 0
.

Recall that the outer expansion is valid where
\mathfrak{d}\gg\nu^{1/4}
, which corresponds to large
T
or
X
.
Thus
U^{\nu}
should match the outer expansion at infinity.
Moreover, because the outer expansion holds at the “macroscopic” scale
\mathfrak{d}\gtrsim 1
, we are free to restrict our analysis of
U^{\nu}
to
\{\mathfrak{d}\ll 1\}
.
Thus it suffices to match
U^{\nu}
with the outer expansion on the intermediate zone where
(T,X)\to\infty
but
(t,x)\to 0
.
That is, we relate the far field of
U^{\nu}
to the behavior of the outer expansion near the origin.

In a traditional matched expansion, this correspondence is expressed through Taylor series for the outer expansion
[
22
]
.
However, our outer terms are not analytic at the origin: they exhibit fractional behavior like (
1.6
).
To capture this structure, we define a notion of functional homogeneity that is adapted to the scaling in (
1.7
); see Definition
2.2
below.
We then show that each outer term
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
admits an expansion in homogeneous functions near the origin.
For example,

(1.8)

u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=u^{0}\sim u_{0,0}+u_{0,1}+u_{0,2}+\ldots\quad\text{as }(t,x)\to(0,0)

for certain homogeneous functions
u_{0,\ell}(t,x)
satisfying
|u_{0,\ell}|\leq C_{\ell}\mathfrak{d}^{\ell+1}
.
In particular,
u_{0,0}
is the inverse cubic alluded to above.

Remark 1.1
.

Facets of the homogeneous expansion (
1.8
) have appeared previously in the literature.
The leading order
u_{0,0}
is well known; see, for instance,
[
19
, Proposition 9]
.
The time-zero slices of the first three terms of (
1.8
) were recently constructed in
[
8
, Lemma 4.11]
.

Recall that
U^{\nu}
should match
u^{0}\sim u_{0,0}+u_{0,1}+\ldots
where
(T,X)\to\infty
and
(t,x)\to 0
.
To use this correspondence, we express (
1.8
) in the inner coordinates.
Our notion of homogeneity is compatible with the inner scaling, and we can compute
u_{0,\ell}(t,x)\leftrightarrow\nu^{\ell/4}u_{0,\ell}(T,X)
under (
1.7
).
Thus the far field of
U^{\nu}
admits an asymptotic expansion in powers of
\nu^{1/4}
.
This motivates the following inner expansion:

(1.9)

U^{\nu}=U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}+\nu^{1/4}U_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}+\nu^{1/2}U_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}+\ldots\quad\text{as }\nu\to 0.

We construct functions
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}(T,X)
independent of
\nu
such that (
1.9
) holds locally uniformly in
(T,X)
.
In fact, (
1.9
) holds on the entire “microscopic” regime
\{\mathfrak{d}\ll 1\}
.
We depict the domains of the inner and outer expansions in Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
Expansion domains.

We deploy the outer expansion on the darker blue region
\{\mathfrak{d}\gtrsim 1\}
and the inner expansion on the green region
\{\mathfrak{d}\lesssim\nu^{1/4}\}.

Both are valid on the intermediate turquoise region
\{\nu^{1/4}\ll\mathfrak{d}\ll 1\}
, where we match the two expansions.
These regions are deliberately imprecise.
For a concrete choice of inner, outer, and matching regions, see Figure
4
in Section
2
.

The leading term
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
in (
1.9
) is an ancient solution of viscous Burgers with unit viscosity.
The higher-order terms solve linear advection–diffusion equations.
We emphasize that these are PDEs, not ODEs.
In contrast to many matched asymptotic expansions, including that of Goodman and Xin
[
24
]
, time retains an essential role in our inner expansion.

Remark 1.2
.

The leading term
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
has a certain universal character.
It is independent of
\mathring{u}
up to a one-parameter scaling action.
Moreover, we expect to find
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
at leading inner order in any strictly convex scalar conservation law (
1.3
).
We discuss this universality further in Appendix
A
.

Using the matched expansions (
1.5
) and (
1.9
), we construct approximate solutions of (
1.1
) that incorporate both expansions in their respective domains of validity.
If we include sufficiently many terms from each expansion, standard energy estimates imply that the approximate solution is arbitrarily close to the true solution
u^{\nu}
.

1.3.
Results

We now state an informal version of our main result.
For a precise form, see Theorem
5.1
below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u^{\nu}
solve (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) with data
\mathring{u}
satisfying
(H1)
–
(H4)
.
Given
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},
there exists an approximate solution
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
incorporating
K+1
terms from each expansion and
C(K,\mathring{u})>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
,

\big\|u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C(K,\mathring{u})\nu^{(K+2)/5}.

Moreover, as
\nu\to 0
,

(1.10)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},0)}\big\|u^{\nu}(t,\,\cdot\,)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1/2}}\to\infty\quad\text{while}\quad\sup_{t\in[t_{0},0)}\big\|u^{\nu}(t,\,\cdot\,)-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}(t,\,\cdot\,)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1/2}}\to 0.

Remark 1.3
.

The bounds (
1.10
) imply that the remainder
u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
is quantitatively more regular than
u^{\nu}
itself.
Thus the approximate solution captures the most irregular part of
u^{\nu}
.
In fact, the difference
u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
should become progressively smoother as
K
increases.
We expect it to remain uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{C}_{x}^{(K+2)/3}
for all
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
A full proof requires estimates of higher derivatives.
To simplify this article, we confine our efforts to
H^{1}
; we therefore only estimate Hölder norms up to
\mathcal{C}^{1/2}
, as in (
1.10
).
However, our methods readily extend to higher orders of regularity.

Remark 1.4
.

We anticipate that a detailed analysis based solely on the Cole–Hopf transformation would yield similar results.
However, unlike Cole–Hopf, the approach developed here naturally extends to other strictly convex scalar conservation laws.

Remark 1.5
.

For convenience, we treat smooth initial data, which allows us to state Theorem
1.1
to arbitrary order.
Naturally, the conclusions of the theorem remain true to some bounded order provided
\mathring{u}
has sufficiently many derivatives.
For instance, Theorem
1.1
holds for all
K\leq\overline{K}
if we replace
(H1)
by
\mathring{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{L}(\mathbb{R})
for
L\geq 3\overline{K}+30
.
We do not attempt to optimize the dependence of
L
on
\overline{K}
.

Our inner and outer expansions are relatively explicit, so we can use Theorem
1.1
to resolve concrete questions regarding the vanishing viscosity limit
u^{\nu}\to u^{0}
on
[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}
.
For instance, we obtain the sharp rate of convergence in
L^{\infty}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
u^{\nu}
solve (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) with data
\mathring{u}
satisfying
(H1)
–
(H4)
.
Then there exists a constant
C(\mathring{u})\geq 1
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
,

(1.11)

C(\mathring{u})^{-1}\nu^{1/4}\leq\big\|u^{\nu}-u^{0}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C(\mathring{u})\nu^{1/4}.

Remark 1.6
.

This estimate is saturated near the origin, where
\mathfrak{d}\lesssim\nu^{1/4}
.
The
\nu^{1/4}
-scale in (
1.11
) reflects the scale of
u
in (
1.7
).

1.4.
Related works

Our investigation of shock formation in weakly viscous Burgers joins an exceedingly broad literature on vanishing viscosity and shock formation in conservation laws.
We describe several related efforts, though we do not attempt to be exhaustive.
For an overview of the theory of hyperbolic conservation laws and shocks, we direct the reader to Dafermos
[
20
]
and Liu
[
35
]
as well as the references therein.

Vanishing viscosity in 1D

As the pioneering work of Hopf
[
27
]
and Oleĭnik
[
41
]
indicates, vanishing viscosity has long been intertwined with the well-posedness theory of conservation laws.
The behavior of the vanishing viscosity limit is best understood in one spatial dimension.
Oleĭnik considered strictly convex scalar conservation laws, and showed that the vanishing viscosity limit coincides with the global entropy solution of the inviscid problem.
We note that other regularizations of non-convex laws can yield non-entropy weak solutions in the “inviscid” limit
[
28
,
6
]
.

Well-posedness for
systems
of hyperbolic conservation laws is much more subtle.
Thanks to the influential work of Glimm
[
23
]
, such systems are known to admit global weak solutions given data that are small in BV.
The relationship between these solutions and vanishing viscosity limits remained open for decades.
This question was finally resolved in the breakthrough paper
[
7
]
of Bianchini and Bressan, who showed that viscous solutions converge uniquely to Glimm solutions as the viscosity tends to zero.
Their approach requires positive viscosity in each component of the system.
However, many equations of interest like compressible Navier–Stokes involve viscosity in only one component.
The nature of vanishing viscosity limits of Navier–Stokes from general small-BV data remains open.
For recent progress in this direction, see, e.g.,
[
29
]
.

As noted earlier, the vanishing viscosity limit is typically expressed as convergence in
L^{1}
.
Stronger pointwise results are known in a handful of cases.
We have already highlighted the asymptotic expansions of Goodman and Xin
[
24
]
and Yu
[
53
]
for hyperbolic conservation laws with weak isolated Lax shocks.
In
[
43
]
, Rousset proved the same result under the weaker (sharp) assumption that the viscous shock in the inner expansion is linearly stable.

Vanishing viscosity in higher dimensions

Kružkov studied scalar conservation laws in multiple spatial dimensions in
[
31
]
.
Using an
L^{1}
modulus of continuity, he showed that viscous solutions converge in
L^{1}
to inviscid solutions as
\nu\to 0
.
Much less is known about
systems
in multiple dimensions.
To our knowledge, no general inviscid weak theory is known to be compatible with vanishing viscosity limits.
In fact, it is known that the compressible Euler equations are ill-posed in BV
[
42
]
.
Nonetheless, as in one dimension, stronger local results are available for certain shock solutions.
Majda has proven local existence for shocks satisfying certain stability conditions; this seminal work is compiled in
[
38
]
.
In
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
, Guès, Métivier, Williams, and Zumbrun show that such solutions coincide with the vanishing viscosity limit.
In doing so, they prove a full asymptotic expansion à la Goodman and Xin.

Multidimensional shock formation

Shock formation in multiple spatial dimensions has become a particularly rich subject.
Early works on singularity formation include an indirect proof
[
44
]
and a symmetry-reduced construction of non-degenerate shock formation in the compressible Euler equations
[
4
]
.
In a breakthrough, Alinhac established non-degenerate shock formation in a class of quasilinear wave equations including irrotational isentropic Euler
[
2
,
3
]
.

Christodoulou revisited this problem in
[
14
]
.
By combining powerful geometric ideas with robust energy estimates, he established shock formation from small data for irrotational isentropic relativistic Euler.
For a similar treatment of nonrelativistic Euler, see
[
17
]
.
Christodoulou’s methods apply to a class of shock singularities that is more general than the non-degenerate forms treated by Alinhac.
Moreover, on a large open subset of initial data, his approach yields a complete description of the maximal smooth development of the solution in spacetime.
Control of this maximal development enabled Christodoulou to study a closely related system after shock formation
[
15
]
.
Recently, Luk and Speck have deployed these geometric methods to prove the stability of planar shocks in the presence of vorticity and entropy up to the first time of singularity
[
36
,
37
]
.
For related results including multi-speed problems, see
[
39
,
46
,
45
]
.

In a parallel program, Buckmaster, Shkoller, and Vicol have examined the local structure of non-degenerate shock formation from well-prepared initial data
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
.
The authors use modulation theory in self-similar variables to study the first point of shock formation in great detail.
They show that the shock profile resembles a self-similar solution of the Burgers equation.
This solution, denoted
u_{0,0}
in (
1.8
), features prominently in our own work.
Like the present article, these works treat non-degenerate modes of shock formation.
In the opposite direction, Buckmaster and Iyer have constructed degenerate shock formation in 2D polytropic Euler
[
9
]
.

Post-shock evolution

In one dimension, small-BV weak solutions are global in time
[
23
]
, so one can study both shock formation and subsequent shock interactions.
In contrast, multidimensional compressible Euler is ill-posed in BV spaces
[
42
]
.
Nonetheless, in the physically relevant two and three dimensions, one might hope that solutions can be weakly extended somewhat past their first singularities.
This is known in symmetry-reduced settings: weak solutions have been constructed after shock formation in compressible Euler under spherical
[
16
,
52
]
and azimuthal
[
8
]
symmetry.
In the absence of symmetry, Christodoulou considered a restricted problem wherein the Euler system is forced to remain isentropic and irrotational
[
15
]
.
In full generality, this “shock development problem” for compressible fluids remains open.

Singularity formation in related models

Inviscid Burgers develops shocks in finite time, but they can be prevented through sufficiently strong dissipation or dispersion.
Examples include viscous Burgers and the KdV equation.
In this work, we study the emergence of a singularity as the dissipative coefficient
\nu
tends to
0
.
In a different direction, one can intrinsically weaken the dissipative or dispersive operator.

The fractional Burgers and KdV equations are essential examples; they feature fractional dissipation and dispersion, respectively
[
49
]
.
Several independent groups
[
30
,
21
,
1
]
concurrently established gradient blow-up in fractal Burgers with weak dissipation (and well-posedness under stronger dissipation).
Nonetheless, the precise nature of the singularity remained unclear, and less was known for fractional KdV.
Recently, a flurry of activity rooted in modulation theory has resolved these questions.
Detailed singular descriptions are now known for fractal Burgers
[
13
]
, Burgers–Hilbert (a weak case of fractional KdV)
[
50
,
51
]
, and a broader class of dissipative and dispersive models
[
40
]
.

In a somewhat different direction, Collot, Ghoul, and Masmoudi have constructed non-degenerate gradient blow-up in a two-dimensional Burgers equation with solely transverse viscosity
[
19
]
.
Like us,
[
19
]
makes use of the self-similar Burgers solution
u_{0,0}
.

1.5.
Future directions

Next, we discuss several problems that may be susceptible to methods developed in this article.

Shock development

As noted earlier, previous results do not provide a uniform pointwise description of
u^{\nu}
throughout the time interval
[t_{*}-\tau,t_{*}+\tau]
, where
t_{*}
denotes the time of shock formation and
\tau
is a small positive constant.
(In this article, we have shifted time so that
t_{*}=0
.)
Our main result addresses the first half of this period:
[t_{*}-\tau,t_{*}]
.
However, the structure of
u^{\nu}
in the second half
[t_{*},t_{*}+\tau]
remains open.
In this period, the inviscid solution
u^{0}
develops a shock that grows from zero strength.
Presently, it is not known precisely how
u^{\nu}
approximates this emergent shock.
It seems likely that a tripartite expansion is required in this setting, corresponding to a region far from the shock, a region close to a
well-developed
shock, and a region close to shock formation.
Thus, a fusion of our approach and that of Goodman and Xin
[
24
]
seems appropriate.
We leave this intricate case to future study.

Systems in 1D

Hyperbolic systems of conservation laws are notoriously more challenging than their scalar counterparts.
In particular, they resist analysis by the comparison principle, which we use extensively to control the inner expansion.
We believe the machinery developed here may aid the study of shock formation in systems, but new ideas seem to be called for.

Higher-dimensional systems

Taking a broader view, one would like to understand the interaction between viscosity and shock formation in compressible fluids in higher dimensions.
The convergence of compressible Navier–Stokes to Euler is an important test bed.
Once a suitable shock is well-developed, the vanishing viscosity limit is known in detail thanks to
[
25
]
.
However, the behavior around shock formation remains open.

Christodoulou has shown that solutions to an Euler-like PDE can be weakly extended somewhat past the first singularity
[
15
]
.
It is desirable on physical grounds to do the same for Euler and to identify the inviscid limit of Navier–Stokes.
As a first step, one could perturb solutions that form planar shocks as in
[
36
,
37
]
and incorporate small viscosity.
One could likewise study the setting of Buckmaster, Shkoller, and Vicol
[
10
,
12
,
11
]
in the presence of small viscosity.
Their shock formation resembles Burgers, so the approach developed here may serve as a stepping stone in this direction.

Proof overview

2.1.
Notation

Throughout, constants are implicitly allowed to depend on the initial data, often through
\mathring{u}^{\prime}(0)
and
\mathring{u}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)
.
We say that functions
f
and
g
satisfy
f\lesssim g
if
\left|f\right|\leq C\left|g\right|
for some constant
C>0
that may depend on
\mathring{u}
but not on the arguments of
f
and
g
.
We also use the notation
f=\mathcal{O}(g)
for
f\lesssim g
.
We write
f\asymp g
if
f\lesssim g
and
g\lesssim f
.
If the constant
C
in these relations depends on another parameter
\gamma
, we write
f\lesssim_{\gamma}g
or
f=\mathcal{O}_{\gamma}(g)
.
Finally, we write
f=\mathchoice{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}{\scalebox{.6}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(g)
or
f\ll g
in the limit
a\to b
if
f(a)/g(a)\to 0
as
a\to b
.

2.2.
Inviscid structure

To leading order, we know that
u^{\nu}
converges to
u^{0}
.
Moreover, the viscous perturbation should be well behaved wherever
u^{0}
is smooth.
Recall that we have shifted time to begin at
t_{0}<0
, so that
u^{0}
first forms a shock at the origin in spacetime.
Thus the real challenge arises near the origin, where
u^{0}
develops an infinite slope.
As a first step, we analyze the behavior of
u^{0}
near the origin.

Because our shock formation is nondegenerate,
u^{0}(t,x)
is continuous and decreasing in
x
near
x=0
for each
t\in[t_{0},0]
.
Given
\varepsilon>0
and
t\in[t_{0},0]
, let
\mathcal{A}_{t}(\varepsilon)
denote the connected component containing
x=0
of the open preimage
u^{0}(t,\,\cdot\,)^{-1}\big((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\big)
.
Define

\varepsilon_{0}\coloneqq\sup\left\{\varepsilon>0\mid-\partial_{x}u^{0}(0,x)\geq 1\,\text{ for all }\,x\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\varepsilon)\right\}.

Note that
\varepsilon_{0}>0
because
-\partial_{x}u^{0}(0,0)=+\infty
.
Then let

(2.1)

\mathcal{A}_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}_{t}(\varepsilon_{0}),\quad\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\big\{(t,x)\in[t_{0},0]\times\mathbb{R}\mid x\in\mathcal{A}_{t}\big\},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{B}\coloneqq(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}).

For each
t\in[t_{0},0]
, the function
u^{0}(t,\,\cdot\,)\colon\mathcal{A}_{t}\to\mathcal{B}
is a continuous bijection.
Let
\omega(t,\,\cdot\,)\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}_{t}
denote its inverse.
If we apply
\partial_{t}
to the identity
u^{0}(t,\omega(t,y))=y
, the chain rule and (
1.2
) yield

(2.2)

\partial_{t}\omega(t,y)=y\quad\text{in }(t_{0},0)\times\mathcal{B}.

This so-called
hodograph transformation
is closely related to the method of characteristics.
Integrating (
2.2
) from
t=t_{0}
, we obtain

(2.3)

\omega(t,y)=\mathring{\omega}(y)+(t-t_{0})y,

where
\mathring{\omega}
denotes the inverse of
\mathring{u}\colon\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}\to\mathcal{B}
.

The identity (
2.3
) allows us to control
u^{0}
and its derivatives near
x=0
.
Indeed,
(H1)
–
5
and the chain rule imply that

\mathring{\omega}(0)=0,\quad\mathring{\omega}^{\prime}(0)=t_{0}<0,\quad\mathring{\omega}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0,\quad\mathring{\omega}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=t_{0}^{4}\mathring{u}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)>0.

Thus by Taylor’s theorem,

(2.4)

\mathring{\omega}(y)=t_{0}y-\beta_{3}y^{3}+\mathcal{O}(y^{4})\quad\text{for }\beta_{3}\coloneqq\frac{1}{6}t_{0}^{4}\partial_{x}^{3}\mathring{u}(0)>0.

Then (
2.3
) yields

(2.5)

\omega(t,y)=ty-\beta_{3}y^{3}+\mathcal{O}(y^{4}).

The first two terms dominate the behavior of
u^{0}
where it is small.
We let
\mathfrak{u}
denote the unique real solution of

(2.6)

t\mathfrak{u}-\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{3}=x.

Then (
2.5
) implies that
u^{0}\sim\mathfrak{u}
as
x\to 0
.

2.3.
Outer expansion

We can now discuss the outer expansion for
u^{\nu}
.
Away from the origin,
\partial_{x}^{2}u^{0}
is bounded and the viscous term in (
1.1
) is a small perturbation.
Applying standard perturbation theory, we propose an expansion for
u^{\nu}
in powers of
\nu
:

(2.7)

u^{\nu}\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\nu^{k}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}.

Recall that
u^{\nu}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}
for all
\nu>0
.
If (
2.7
) holds at
t=t_{0}
, we must have
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}
and
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},0)=0
for all
k\geq 1
.

Moreover, we can find evolution equations for
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
by formally substituting (
2.7
) in (
1.1
) and collecting terms of like order.
At leading (unit) order,
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
solves inviscid Burgers.
Since
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\mathring{u}
, the uniqueness of the entropy solution implies that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=u^{0}
.
For
k\geq 1
, we find

(2.8)

\partial_{t}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=-\partial_{x}\left(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)-\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}u_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}\partial_{x}u_{k-k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{k-1}^{\mathrm{out}},\quad u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0.

This is a linear passive-scalar equation with advection
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
and forcing involving the viscous term
\partial_{x}^{2}u_{k-1}^{\mathrm{out}}
and nonlinear cross-terms
u_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}\partial_{x}u_{k-k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}
from earlier in the expansion.

These passive-scalar equations can be integrated quasi-explicitly along the characteristics of
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
We can thus describe
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
with great precision if we understand the inviscid solution
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
in sufficient detail.

The outer expansion (
2.7
) should only be valid where the viscous term in (
1.1
) is much smaller than the other terms, i.e., where

(2.9)

|\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\nu}|\ll\left|u^{\nu}\partial_{x}u^{\nu}\right|.

This condition fails near the origin in spacetime.
To quantify its failure, we use
\mathfrak{u}
as a proxy for
u^{\nu}
.
Define

\mathfrak{m}(t,x)\coloneqq\big[\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}(t,x)^{2}\big]^{-1}.

Then the chain rule yields

(2.10)

\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}=-\mathfrak{m}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{u}=-6\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}\mathfrak{m}^{3}.

Plugging
\mathfrak{u}
into (
2.9
), we see that the outer expansion should be valid wherever
|\nu\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{u}|\ll\left|\mathfrak{u}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\right|
.
Using (
2.10
), this is equivalent to

(2.11)

\nu\mathfrak{m}^{2}\ll 1.

For later convenience, we define

\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}(t,x)^{-1/2}=\big[\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}(t,x)^{2}\big]^{1/2}.

We think of
\mathfrak{d}
as a “distance to the origin” that is naturally adapted to our problem.
We note, however, that it is not a metric in the technical sense.
Rewriting (
2.11
), we see that the outer expansion (
2.7
) should be valid where

(2.12)

\mathfrak{d}\gg\nu^{1/4}.

In Lemma
2.1
below, we show that

\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\asymp\max\left\{\left|t\right|^{1/2},\,\left|x\right|^{1/3}\right\}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}.

Thus (
2.12
) is equivalent to the disjunction

(2.13)

\left|t\right|\gg\nu^{1/2}\quad\text{or}\quad\left|x\right|\gg\nu^{3/4}.

For future convenience, we collect these definitions in one place:

Definition 2.1
.

The profile
\mathfrak{u}
is the unique real solution of
t\mathfrak{u}-\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{3}=x.

Its slope
\mathfrak{m}
is
-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}=\big(\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)^{-1}
and the distance
\mathfrak{d}
is
\mathfrak{m}^{-1/2}=\big(\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

These functions are ubiquitous in the remainder of the paper.
In the following lemma, we collect some of their properties.

Lemma 2.1
.

On
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have

(2.14)

\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\asymp\big(\left|x\right|\left|t\right|^{-1}\big)\vee\left|x\right|^{1/3},\quad\mathfrak{m}\asymp\big(\left|t\right|+\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)^{-1},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathfrak{d}\asymp\big(\left|x\right|\vee\left|t\right|^{3/2}\big)^{1/3}.

Moreover, for all
t<0
and
x\neq 0
,
\mathfrak{m}\leq\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\left|x\right|^{-1}
.

Proof.

Because
t<0
, we have

(2.15)

\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\big(\left|t\right|+\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)=\left|x\right|.

Suppose
\left|t\right|\geq\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}
.
Then (
2.15
) yields

(2.16)

\frac{1}{2}\left|x\right|\left|t\right|^{-1}\leq\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\leq\left|x\right|\left|t\right|^{-1}.

On the other hand, if
\left|t\right|\leq\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}
, then (
2.15
) implies that

(2.17)

(2\beta_{3})^{-1/3}\left|x\right|^{1/3}\leq\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\leq\beta_{3}^{-1/3}\left|x\right|^{1/3}.

Together, (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) imply the first part of (
2.14
).

Next,
\mathfrak{m}\asymp\big(\left|t\right|+\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)^{-1}
is immediate from Definition
2.1
.
It follows that

\mathfrak{d}=\mathfrak{m}^{-1/2}\asymp\big(\left|t\right|+\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

Hence if
\left|t\right|\geq\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}
, we obtain
\mathfrak{d}\asymp\left|t\right|^{1/2}
.
On the other hand, if
\left|t\right|\leq\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}
, (
2.17
) yields
\mathfrak{d}\asymp\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\asymp\left|x\right|^{1/3}
.
This completes the proof of (
2.14
).

Finally, the definition of
\mathfrak{m}
and (
2.15
) imply that

\mathfrak{m}=\frac{1}{\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}}\leq\frac{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\big(\left|t\right|+\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)}=\frac{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}{\left|x\right|}.\qed

2.4.
Inner expansion

When
\mathfrak{d}\lesssim\nu^{1/4}
, we must reckon with the viscosity in (
1.1
).
To focus on this region, we perform a blow-up about the origin by introducing the “inner coordinates” (
1.7
), which we recall here:

(2.18)

T\coloneqq\nu^{-1/2}t,\quad X\coloneqq\nu^{-3/4}x,\quad\textrm{and}\quad U\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}u.

These scaling exponents are motivated by (
2.13
).
The blown-up solution

U^{\nu}(T,X)\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}u^{\nu}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X)

satisfies viscous Burgers with
unit
viscosity in the blown-up domain
(\nu^{-1/2}t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}
:

(2.19)

\partial_{T}U^{\nu}=-U^{\nu}\partial_{X}U^{\nu}+\partial_{X}^{2}U^{\nu}.

We observe that this domain approaches the past half-plane
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
as
\nu\to 0
.
We postulate that
(U^{\nu})_{\nu>0}
converges locally uniformly in the
(T,X)
coordinates to a special solution
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
of (
2.19
) defined on
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Now consider the regime
\nu^{1/4}\ll\mathfrak{d}\ll 1
.
In this region,
(t,x)\to(0,0)
while
(T,X)\to\infty
as
\nu\to 0
.
Because
\mathfrak{d}\gg\nu^{1/4}
, the outer expansion is valid, and
u^{\nu}\sim u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=u^{0}
.
On the other hand,
(t,x)\to 0
implies that
u^{0}\sim\mathfrak{u}
.
Thus
u^{\nu}\sim\mathfrak{u}
in this regime.
We expect
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
to reflect this behavior.
If

\mathfrak{U}(T,X)\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}\mathfrak{u}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X)=\mathfrak{u}(T,X)

denotes the blown-up inverse cubic, we should have
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}\sim\mathfrak{U}
at infinity.
That is, matching with the outer expansion imposes a far-field condition on
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
.

In Section
4
, we construct an ancient solution
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
of (
2.19
) in
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
that resembles
\mathfrak{U}
at infinity.
See Proposition
4.2
for details.
This solution admits an explicit integral representation via the Cole–Hopf transformation.
We discuss its structure further in Appendix
A
.

The special solution
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
only involves the
leading
order
\mathfrak{u}
of
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
near the origin.
The full inner expansion must include higher-order information.
To express these higher orders, we introduce a notion of functional homogeneity that respects the natural scaling of the problem.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
r\in\mathbb{R}
, a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
r
-homogeneous
if

f\big(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda^{3}x\big)=\lambda^{r}f(t,x)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

We note that this definition strays from standard terminology, as we scale spacetime by
\big(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda^{3}x\big)
rather than
(\lambda t,\lambda x)
.

Example 2.1
.

Using Definition
2.1
, we can check that
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{d}
are
1
-homogeneous while
\mathfrak{m}
is
-2
-homogeneous.

We show that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
admits a homogeneous expansion about the origin:

(2.20)

u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}u_{0,\ell}(t,x)\quad\text{as }(t,x)\to(0,0)

for certain
(\ell+1)
-homogeneous function
u_{0,\ell}
.
In particular,
u_{0,0}=\mathfrak{u}
.
For a precise statement, see Lemma
3.4
.
Due to their increasing degrees of homogeneity, the terms in (
2.20
) vanish to successively higher order at the origin.
Quantitatively,

(2.21)

\left|u_{0,\ell}\right|\lesssim_{\ell}\mathfrak{d}^{\ell+1},

recalling that the distance
\mathfrak{d}
from Definition
2.1
vanishes precisely at
(t,x)=(0,0)
.

The change-of-variables
(t,x)=(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X)
in (
2.18
) exhibits the scaling in Definition
2.2
with
\lambda=\nu^{1/4}
.
By
(\ell+1)
-homogeneity,
u_{0,\ell}(t,x)=\nu^{(\ell+1)/4}u_{0,\ell}(T,X)
.
Accounting for the scaling
U=\nu^{-1/4}u
in (
2.18
), we see that
u_{0,\ell}(t,x)
corresponds to a term of order
\nu^{\ell/4}
in the inner coordinates.

Recall that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
prescribes the far field of
U^{\nu}
in the inner coordinates.
We have just argued that this field admits an expansion in powers of
\nu^{1/4}
corresponding to (
2.20
).
Extending this structure to the entire inner region
\{\mathfrak{d}\ll 1\}
, we are led to the inner expansion:

(2.22)

U^{\nu}\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\nu^{\ell/4}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}.

Plugging (
2.22
) into (
2.19
) and collecting like terms, we obtain the evolution equations

(2.23)

\partial_{T}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}=\ell}\partial_{X}(U_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}U_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}})+\partial_{X}^{2}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}.

We now return to the original coordinates; define

u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}(t,x)\coloneqq\nu^{(\ell+1)/4}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}\big(\nu^{-1/2}t,\nu^{-3/4}x\big).

Then we find an expansion

(2.24)

u^{\nu}\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}

that is valid when
\mathfrak{d}\ll 1
.
Matching between
u^{0}
and (
2.24
) imposes the condition
u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}\sim u_{0,\ell}
where
\nu^{1/4}\ll\mathfrak{d}\ll 1
.
By (
2.21
),
u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
is roughly of size
\mathfrak{d}^{\ell+1}
.
Therefore the series (
2.24
) represents a true hierarchy of scales when
\mathfrak{d}\ll 1
.

We have now described two expansions for
u^{\nu}
: an outer expansion (
2.7
) on
{\{\mathfrak{d}\gg\nu^{1/4}\}}
and an inner expansion (
2.24
) on
\{\mathfrak{d}\ll 1\}
.
If both are valid, the two expansions must be “compatible” in some sense on the overlap region
{\{\nu^{1/4}\ll\mathfrak{d}\ll 1\}}
.
To express this compatibility, we extend the homogeneous expansion (
2.20
) to all
k\geq 0
:

(2.25)

u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}u_{k,\ell}\quad\text{when }\mathfrak{d}\ll 1

for certain
(-4k+\ell+1)
-homogeneous functions
u_{k,\ell}
.
We then construct our inner expansion so that

(2.26)

u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}\sim\sum_{k\geq 0}\nu^{k}u_{k,\ell}\quad\text{when }\mathfrak{d}\gg\nu^{1/4}.

For precise statements, see Propositions
3.9
and
4.2
below.

We can visualize our two expansions via the grid of terms
(\nu^{k}u_{k,\ell})_{k,\ell\geq 0}
in Figure

2.5.
An approximate solution

Next, we combine our two compatible expansions to construct approximate solutions of (
1.1
).
Given
\alpha\in(0,1)
, define the inner region

I\coloneqq\big\{(t,x)\in[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}\mid\mathfrak{d}(t,x)<\nu^{\alpha}\big\},

the outer region

O\coloneqq\big\{(t,x)\in[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}\mid\mathfrak{d}(t,x)>2\nu^{\alpha}\big\},

and the matching zone

M\coloneqq\big\{(t,x)\in[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}\mid\nu^{\alpha}\leq\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\leq 2\nu^{\alpha}\big\}.

These regions are chosen so that the effect of viscosity on
u^{\nu}
is negligible in
O
but relevant in
I
.
As we shall see in Section
5
, the optimal choice for our scheme is
\alpha=1/5
.
We depict these regions in Figure
4
.

These regions are precisely delineated versions of those in Figure
2
.

Now let
\vartheta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
satisfy
0\leq\vartheta\leq 1
,
\vartheta|_{[0,1]}\equiv 1
, and
\vartheta|_{[2,\infty)}\equiv 0
.
We define the smooth cutoff

(2.27)

\theta(t,x)\coloneqq\vartheta\big(\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\nu^{-\alpha}\big),

which interpolates between
1
on
I
and
0
on
O
.

Fix
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and define the partial sums

u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{K}\nu^{k}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{K}u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}.

We define an approximate solution
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
of (
1.1
) by

u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\coloneqq\theta u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\theta)u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}.

The difference
v\coloneqq u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
satisfies a PDE of the form

\partial_{t}v=-\partial_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}v)-v\partial_{x}v+\nu\partial_{x}^{2}v+E,\quad v(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0,

for some forcing
E
satisfying
\left\|E\right\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}H_{x}^{1}}\lesssim\nu^{(2K-3)/10}
.
Exploiting the small but positive diffusion, we close an estimate on
v
in
L_{t}^{\infty}H_{x}^{1}
and conclude that

\left\|v\right\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}H_{x}^{1}}\lesssim\nu^{(K-6)/5}.

Choosing
K
large, we see that
v
is small in
L_{t}^{\infty}H_{x}^{1}
, and hence in
L_{t,x}^{\infty}
.
Thus
u^{\nu}\approx u^{\mathrm{app}}
, as desired.
This analysis can be carried out in arbitrarily high Sobolev norms, so we obtain a complete description of
u^{\nu}
to all orders.

3.
The outer expansion

In this section, we study the terms in (
2.7
).
We begin with the observation that
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
is trivial outside a compact set.
Recall
\mathring{c}
from
(H2)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists
L>0
such that on
[t_{0},0)\times[-L,L]
,
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\mathring{c}
and
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=0
for all
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

By
(H2)
,
\mathring{u}(x)=\mathring{c}
for all
\left|x\right|\geq\mathring{L}
.
Define the region

\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\big\{(t,x)\in[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}\mid|x-\mathring{c}(t-t_{0})|\geq\mathring{L}\big\}.

Using the method of characteristics, we see that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\mathring{c}
on
\mathcal{D}
.

We claim that
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=0
on
\mathcal{D}
for all
k\geq 1
.
We show this by induction.
Fix
k\geq 1
and suppose we have shown the claim for all
1\leq k^{\prime}<k
.
Note that this assumption is vacuous in the base case
k=1
.
Using (
2.8
), we see that

\partial_{t}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=-\partial_{x}\big(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}.

Now
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
starts from
0
and
\mathcal{D}
is a union of characteristics of
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
Hence
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=0
in
\mathcal{D}
, as desired.
The claim follows from induction.
To obtain the lemma, we take
L\coloneqq|\mathring{c}t_{0}|+\mathring{L}
.
∎

3.1.
Homogeneous algebra

In the remainder of the section, we focus on the behavior of the outer expansion (
2.7
) near
x=0
.
We use the inverse cubic
\mathfrak{u}
and its slope
\mathfrak{m}
from Definition
2.1
as building blocks.
It is therefore essential that we understand the derivatives of these functions in some detail.

Recall that the functions
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{m}
are homogeneous of degrees
1
and
-2
, respectively.
In light of the scaling in Definition
2.2
, the derivatives
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{x}
reduce homogeneity by
2
and
3,
respectively.
In particular, we can use Definition
2.1
and the chain rule to compute

(3.1)

\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}=-\mathfrak{m},\quad\partial_{t}\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{u}\mathfrak{m},\quad\partial_{x}\mathfrak{m}=6\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}\mathfrak{m}^{3},\quad\partial_{t}\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}^{2}(1-6\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}\mathfrak{m}).

It follows that the space of polynomials in
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{m}
is closed under differentiation.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra of
0
-homogeneous real polynomials in
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{m}
.
By
0
-homogeneity,
\mathcal{P}
is generated as an algebra by
\mathfrak{u}^{2}\mathfrak{m}
.
That is,
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{R}\big[\mathfrak{u}^{2}\mathfrak{m}\big]
.
Given
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},
we then define

\mathcal{P}(a,b)=\mathfrak{u}^{a}\mathfrak{m}^{b}\mathcal{P},

which is a real vector space of
(a-2b)
-homogeneous functions.
Using (
3.1
), we see that

(3.2)

\partial_{x}\colon\mathcal{P}(a,b)\to\mathcal{P}(a-1,b+1)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{t}\colon\mathcal{P}(a,b)\to\mathcal{P}(a,b+1)

when
a\geq 1
and
b\geq 0
.

We would like to extend this pattern to
a\leq 0
, but something new happens:
\partial_{x}\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{P}(1,3)
.
Thus
\partial_{x}\colon\mathcal{P}(0,1)\to\mathcal{P}(1,3)
.
In light of (
3.2
), we therefore
define

\mathcal{P}(-1,2)\coloneqq\mathcal{P}(1,3)
.
More generally, we extend our notation to negative indices as follows:

\mathcal{P}(-a,b)\coloneqq\mathcal{P}(-a+2\left\lceil a/2\right\rceil,b+\left\lceil a/2\right\rceil)\quad\textrm{for }a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Thus we have formally replaced
\mathfrak{u}^{-a}
by
\mathfrak{u}^{-a}(\mathfrak{u}^{2}\mathfrak{m})^{\left\lceil a/2\right\rceil}
so that the result is still a polynomial in
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{m}
.
With this convention, (
3.2
) holds for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

We now introduce some bounds adapted to this algebra.
We first note that homogeneous functions are determined by their values on the quarter-sphere

{Q}\coloneqq\big\{\left|t\right|^{2}+\left|x\right|^{2}=1,\,t<0,\,x\geq 0\big\}.

Because

0<\inf_{{Q}}\mathfrak{d}<\sup_{{Q}}\mathfrak{d}<\infty

and
\mathfrak{d}
is
1
-homogeneous, we have

(3.3)

\left|f\right|\leq C(f)\mathfrak{d}^{r}\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}

for every
r
-homogeneous function
f
that is bounded on
{Q}
.
In particular, (
3.3
) holds if
f
is continuous in
(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
.
For this reason, we frequently express estimates in terms of powers of
\mathfrak{d}
.

Next, we introduce an
(a-2b)
-homogeneous function
\mathfrak{e}(a,b)
to control polynomials in
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{m}
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

(3.4)

\mathfrak{e}(a,b)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|^{a}\mathfrak{m}^{b}&\text{if }a\geq 0,\\
\mathfrak{m}^{b+\left|a\right|/2}&\text{if }a<0.\end{cases}

If
p\in\mathcal{P}(a,b)
, then

(3.5)

\left|p\right|\leq C(p)\mathfrak{e}(a,b)\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}

for some
C(p)>0
.
The functions
\mathfrak{e}(a,b)
are refined versions of
\mathfrak{d}^{a-2b}
that track the order of vanishing at
x=0
when
t<0
.
As it happens, this vanishing is not relevant when
a<0
, so then we simply let
\mathfrak{e}(a,b)=\mathfrak{d}^{a-2b}
.

3.2.
The inviscid solution

We can now justify the expansion (
2.20
) for
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
near the origin.
In fact, we will verify the expansion wherever
\left|x\right|\ll 1
.
We recall the identity (
2.3
) for the local spatial inverse
\omega
of
u^{0}=u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
Because the inverse
\mathring{\omega}
of
\mathring{u}
is smooth on the domain
\mathcal{B}
defined in (
2.1
), it admits a Taylor expansion.
Refining (
2.4
), we have

\mathring{\omega}(y)=t_{0}y-\beta_{3}y^{3}+\sum_{m=4}^{M}\beta_{m}y^{m}+\mathcal{O}_{M}\big(y^{M+1}\big)\quad\text{as }y\to 0

for each
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 4}
and some coefficients
\beta_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using (
2.3
), we find

(3.6)

\omega(t,y)=ty-\beta_{3}y^{3}+\sum_{m=4}^{M}\beta_{m}y^{m}+\mathcal{O}_{M}\big(y^{M+1}\big)\quad\text{as }y\to 0

for each
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 4}
.
We also permit
M=3
in (
3.6
) under the convention that the empty sum is zero.

As noted earlier, this expansion suggests that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim\mathfrak{u}
where both are small.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\mathfrak{u}+\mathcal{O}\big(\left|x\right|^{1/3}|\mathfrak{u}|\big)
on
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
.

Remark 3.1
.

The rectangle
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
is simply a convenient bounded domain about
\{x=0\}
.

Proof.

Recall (
2.1
) and
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=u^{0}
.
By construction,
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
and
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,\,\cdot\,)
has inverse
\omega(t,\,\cdot\,)
.
We initially focus on
(t,x)\in\mathcal{A}
.

For
y\in\mathcal{B}
, (
3.6
) implies that we can bound
\omega(t,y)
from above and below by multiples of
ty-\beta_{3}y^{3}
.
It follows that

(3.7)

u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\asymp\mathfrak{u}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}.

Now,
\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|
is increasing in
t
, so

(3.8)

|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)|\lesssim\left|\mathfrak{u}(t,x)\right|\leq\left|\mathfrak{u}(0,x)\right|\asymp\left|x\right|^{1/3}.

Because
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
and
\mathfrak{u}
are spatial bijections near
x=0
, there exists a unique shift
h(t,x)
such that

(3.9)

\mathfrak{u}(t,x+h(t,x))=u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)

for sufficiently small
x
.
That is, for
\left|x\right|\leq c_{0}
for some
c_{0}>0
.
We will control this “distortion”
h
.
In the following calculations, we assume
(t,x)\in\mathcal{A}\cap\{\left|x\right|\leq c_{0}\}
unless stated otherwise.

By (
3.6
),

(3.10)

\left|\omega(t,y)-(ty-\beta_{3}y^{3})\right|\lesssim\left|y\right|^{4}\quad\textrm{for all }y\in\mathcal{B}.

Since
x=\omega(t,u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x))
, (
3.8
) and (
3.10
) yield

(3.11)

\left|x-\big[tu_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)-\beta_{3}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)^{3}\big]\right|\lesssim u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)^{4}\lesssim\left|x\right|^{4/3}.

Now Definition
2.1
, (
3.9
), and (
3.11
) imply

(3.12)

\left|h\right|=\left|x-(x+h)\right|=\left|x-\big[t\mathfrak{u}(t,x+h)-\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}(t,x+h)^{3}\big]\right|\lesssim\left|x\right|^{4/3}.

By reducing
c_{0}
, we can assume that
\left|h\right|\leq\left|x\right|/2
when
\left|x\right|\leq c_{0}.

Then
\left|z\right|\asymp\left|x\right|
for all
z
between
x
and
x+h
.
By Lemma
2.1
,

\left|\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}(t,z)\right|=\mathfrak{m}(t,z)\leq\frac{\left|\mathfrak{u}(t,z)\right|}{\left|z\right|}\lesssim\frac{\mathfrak{u}(t,x)}{\left|x\right|}

for such
z
.
Hence by the mean value theorem and (
3.12
),

\left|\mathfrak{u}(t,x+h)-\mathfrak{u}(t,x)\right|\lesssim\frac{\left|\mathfrak{u}(t,x)\right|}{\left|x\right|}\left|h\right|\lesssim\left|x\right|^{1/3}\left|\mathfrak{u}(t,x)\right|.

Now (
3.9
) yields

(3.13)

\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\mathfrak{u}\right|\lesssim\left|x\right|^{1/3}|\mathfrak{u}|\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}\cap\{\left|x\right|\leq c_{0}\}.

Finally,
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\mathfrak{u}
is bounded on the rectangle
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
.
Since
\left|x\right|^{1/3}\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\gtrsim 1
when
\left|x\right|\geq c_{0},
(
3.13
) holds on the full domain
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
.
∎

This lemma quickly yields stronger results.

Corollary 3.3
.

We have
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\mathfrak{u}+\beta_{4}\mathfrak{u}^{4}\mathfrak{m}+\mathcal{O}\big(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|^{5}\mathfrak{m}\big)
on
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
.

Proof.

Let
v\coloneqq u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\mathfrak{u}
.
Plugging
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
into (
3.6
) and using (
3.7
), we find

x-\big[tu_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\beta_{3}(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{3}\big]\lesssim\mathfrak{u}^{4}.

Replacing
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
by
\mathfrak{u}+v
and using Definition
2.1
, this implies that

(3.14)

\left[\left|t\right|+\beta_{3}\big(3\mathfrak{u}^{2}+3\mathfrak{u}v+v^{2}\big)\right]v\lesssim\mathfrak{u}^{4}.

By Lemma
3.2
, we can assume that
\left|v\right|\leq\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|/2
provided
\left|x\right|\leq c_{0}
for some
c_{0}>0
.
Hence

\mathfrak{u}^{2}\leq 3\mathfrak{u}^{2}+3\mathfrak{u}v+v^{2}\leq 5\mathfrak{u}^{2}.

Then Lemma
2.1
yields

\left|t\right|+\beta_{3}\big(3\mathfrak{u}^{2}+3\mathfrak{u}v+v^{2}\big)\asymp\mathfrak{m}^{-1}.

Rearranging (
3.14
), we obtain

(3.15)

v\lesssim\mathfrak{u}^{4}\mathfrak{m}\lesssim\mathfrak{u}^{2}.

Keeping one more term in (
3.6
) and following the same reasoning, we find

\left[\left|t\right|+\beta_{3}(3\mathfrak{u}^{2}+3\mathfrak{u}v+v^{3})\right]v=\beta_{4}(\mathfrak{u}+v)^{4}+\mathcal{O}\big(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|^{5}\big).

Finally, (
3.15
) allows us to simplify:

\mathfrak{m}^{-1}v=\big(\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}\big)v=\beta_{4}\mathfrak{u}^{4}+\mathcal{O}\big(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|^{5}\big).

The corollary follows for
\left|x\right|\leq c_{0}
.
Again, because
\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|\gtrsim 1
when
\left|x\right|\geq c_{0}
, the bound extends to the full rectangle
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
.
∎

We can iterate this argument to show that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
admits an expansion about
x=0
of the form

u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}u_{0,\ell}

for certain
(\ell+1)
-homogeneous functions
u_{0,\ell}
that are polynomials in
\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{m}
.
For instance,
u_{0,0}=\mathfrak{u}
and
u_{0,1}=\beta_{4}\mathfrak{u}^{4}\mathfrak{m}
.
Using identities like

\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x)=\partial_{y}\omega(t,u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x))^{-1},

we can in turn derive analogous expansions for the derivatives of
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
.

We denote the partial homogeneous sum by

u_{0,[\ell]}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}.

Then variations on the proof of Corollary
3.3
yield:

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
\ell\geq 1
, we have
u_{0,\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+3,1)
.
Moreover, for each
i,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}\big(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{0,[\ell]}\big)\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+4,i+1)\quad\text{in }[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1].

In particular, we obtain direct control on
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
:

Corollary 3.5
.

For each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have
|\partial_{x}^{i}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}|\lesssim_{i}\mathfrak{e}(-i+1,i)
in
[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

We use Lemma
3.4
with
\ell=0
, noting that
u_{0,[0]}=u_{0,0}=\mathfrak{u}
:

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|\lesssim_{i}\left|\partial_{x}^{i}\mathfrak{u}\right|+\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+4,i+1)\quad\text{in }[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1].

By (
3.2
),
\partial_{x}^{i}\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{P}(-i+1,i)
.
Thus by (
3.5
),
|\partial_{x}^{i}\mathfrak{u}|\lesssim_{i}\mathfrak{e}(-i+1,i)
.
Also,

\mathfrak{e}(-i+4,i+1)\lesssim\mathfrak{e}(-i+1,i)

in
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
, so

(3.16)

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|\lesssim_{i}\mathfrak{e}(-i+1,i)\quad\text{in }[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1].

By Lemma
3.1
,
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
is constant outside a compact set.
Hence when
i\geq 1
,
\partial_{x}^{i}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
is compactly supported and (
3.16
) holds in the entire space
[t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}
.
When
i=0
,
\mathfrak{e}(1,0)=\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|
grows at infinity, so again (
3.16
) holds in the entire space.
∎

We occasionally require a more precise bound on
\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
.

Corollary 3.6
.

For all
t\in[t_{0},0)
,

(3.17)

\left\|\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}(t,\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=\frac{1}{\left|t\right|}.

Moreover,

(3.18)

\left\|\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}=\frac{1}{\left|t\right|}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left|t\right|^{-1/2}\right).

Proof.

The first bound (
3.17
) follows from

\left|\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}(t,x)\right|=\mathfrak{m}(t,x)=\big[\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}(t,x)\big]^{-1}

and
\mathfrak{u}(t,0)=0
.
Applying Lemma
3.4
with
i=1
and
\ell=0
, we find

(3.19)

\left|\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\right|\lesssim\mathfrak{e}(3,2)\lesssim\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\lesssim\left|t\right|^{-1/2}\quad\text{in }[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1].

Since
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
is smooth away from the origin, this bound actually holds on the whole space.
Then (
3.18
) follows from (
3.17
).
∎

3.3.
First viscous corrector

Next, we consider the leading viscous corrector
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
, which solves

(3.20)

\partial_{t}u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}=-\partial_{x}\big(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}},\quad u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0.

We claim that
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
also admits a homogeneous expansion

(3.21)

u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}u_{1,\ell}

about
x=0
with
(\ell-3)
-homogeneous terms
u_{1,\ell}
.
To explain this degree of homogeneity, we note that
u_{1,\ell}
roughly corresponds to
\int\!\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\ell}\,\mathrm{d}t
.
Each spatial derivative reduces homogeneity by
3
, while the time integral increases it by
2
.
Since
u_{0,\ell}
is
(\ell+1)
-homogeneous, it is natural to expect
u_{1,\ell}
to be homogeneous of degree
(\ell+1)-3-3+2=\ell-3
.

The justification of (
3.21
) is somewhat more involved, as we do not have an implicit equation for
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
Instead, we must proceed from the PDE (
3.20
).
If we formally plug (
3.21
) into (
3.20
) and collect terms of like homogeneity, we arrive at a putative PDE for
u_{1,\ell}
:

(3.22)

\partial_{t}u_{1,\ell}=-\partial_{x}(\mathfrak{u}u_{1,\ell})-\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\partial_{x}(u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}\,u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}})+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\ell}.

This equation has the form

(3.23)

\partial_{t}u_{1,\ell}=-\partial_{x}(\mathfrak{u}u_{1,\ell})+F_{\ell}

for an
(\ell-5)
-homogeneous forcing
F_{\ell}
determined by the expansion for
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
and by earlier terms
\{u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}\}_{\ell^{\prime}<\ell}
.
We will prove the following by induction:

Lemma 3.7
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},
we have
F_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,3)
.

Before proving the lemma, we discuss our approach to (
3.23
).
The key is a change of coordinates from
(t,x)
to
(t,\mathfrak{u})
.
We refer to the latter as “dynamic coordinates.”
We denote their partial derivatives by
\overline{\partial}_{t}
and
\overline{\partial}_{\mathfrak{u}}
.
Because
\mathfrak{u}
solves (
1.2
), the chain rule yields

\partial_{t}=(\partial_{t}t)\overline{\partial}_{t}+(\partial_{t}\mathfrak{u})\overline{\partial}_{\mathfrak{u}}=\overline{\partial}_{t}-(\mathfrak{u}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u})\overline{\partial}_{\mathfrak{u}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{x}=(\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u})\overline{\partial}_{\mathfrak{u}}.

Rearranging, we see that

(3.24)

\overline{\partial}_{t}=\partial_{t}+\mathfrak{u}\partial_{x}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\overline{\partial}_{\mathfrak{u}}=-\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\partial_{x}.

Thus (
3.23
) becomes

(3.25)

\overline{\partial}_{t}u_{1,\ell}=\mathfrak{m}u_{1,\ell}+F_{\ell}.

Recall that
\mathfrak{m}^{-1}=-t+3\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}^{2}
.
It follows that
\overline{\partial}_{t}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1})=-1
.
Dividing (
3.25
) by
\mathfrak{m}
and rearranging, we find

\overline{\partial}_{t}(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}u_{1,\ell})=\mathfrak{m}^{-1}F_{\ell}.

We integrate this in
t
, holding
\mathfrak{u}
constant.
By Lemma
3.7
,
\mathfrak{m}^{-1}F_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,2)
.
Crucially,
\mathfrak{m}^{2}\sim t^{-2}
is integrable as
t\to-\infty
.
We can compute

\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathfrak{m}(s,\mathfrak{u})^{a}\;\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{a-1}\mathfrak{m}(t,\mathfrak{u})^{a-1}.

In light of Lemma
3.7
, we obtain a special solution of (
3.23
):

(3.26)

u_{1,\ell}^{F}\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathfrak{m}(s,\mathfrak{u})^{-1}F_{\ell}(s,\mathfrak{u})\;\mathrm{d}s\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,2).

We emphasize that the three powers of
\mathfrak{m}
in
F_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,3)
are essential.
The above argument breaks down if a term in
F_{\ell}
only includes two powers of
\mathfrak{m}
, due to a logarithmic divergence in the integral.
For this reason, we keep careful track of powers of
\mathfrak{m}
throughout the section.

We have made progress, but we have so far ignored the initial data
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0
.
We wish to construct a solution of (
3.23
) that also reflects this condition.
Any two solutions of (
3.23
) differ by a solution
v
of the
unforced
equation

(3.27)

\partial_{t}v=-\partial_{x}(\mathfrak{u}v).

In the dynamic coordinates
(t,\mathfrak{u})
, this becomes

\overline{\partial}_{t}v=\mathfrak{m}v.

Arguing as above, we find the general solution:
v=f(\mathfrak{u})\mathfrak{m}
for
f
smooth.
The
(\ell-3)
-homogeneous solutions are all multiples of

v_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathfrak{u}^{\ell-1}\mathfrak{m}.

Thus all homogeneous solutions of (
3.23
) have the form

(3.28)

u_{1,\ell}=u_{1,\ell}^{F}+C_{\ell}v_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell-1,1).

We can now prove Lemma
3.7
.

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

We proceed by induction.
In the base case
\ell=0
, we have

F_{0}=\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{u}=-6\beta_{3}\mathfrak{u}\mathfrak{m}^{3}\in\mathcal{P}(1,3)

as claimed.
Next, fix
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
F_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell^{\prime}+1,3)
for all
\ell^{\prime}<\ell
.
Recall that

(3.29)

F_{\ell}=-\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\partial_{x}(u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}})+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\ell}.

We must therefore control the structure of
u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}
and
u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}}
.

Since
\ell^{\prime}\geq 1
, Lemma
3.4
yields
u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell^{\prime}+3,1)
.
Next, we construct
u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}}
using the method described above, so

u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{m}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathfrak{m}^{-1}F_{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\;\mathrm{d}s+Cv_{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}

for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using the inductive hypothesis that
F_{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell-\ell^{\prime}+1,3)
, our calculations above show that
u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell-\ell^{\prime}-1,1)
, as in (
3.28
).
Combining these observations about
u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}
and
u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}}
, (
3.2
) implies that
\partial_{x}(u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}u_{1,\ell-\ell^{\prime}})\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,3)
.
Similarly, (
3.2
) yields
\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,3).

By (
3.29
), we have
F_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell+1,3)
, as desired.
The lemma now follows from induction.
∎

To uniquely determine
u_{1,\ell}
, we must choose the free parameter
C_{\ell}
in (
3.28
).
When
\ell=0
, the unforced solution
v_{\ell}=\mathfrak{u}^{-1}\mathfrak{m}
is not smooth on
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
; it is singular at
x=0
.
Our initial data is smooth, so the expansion (
3.21
) should not contain such a term.
Thus smoothness demands the choice
C_{0}=0
, and we let
u_{1,0}=u_{1,0}^{F}
.
This is the
unique
smooth homogeneous solution of

\partial_{t}u_{1,0}=-\partial_{x}(\mathfrak{u}u_{1,0})+F_{0}=-\partial_{x}(\mathfrak{u}u_{1,0})+\partial_{x}^{2}\mathfrak{u}.

In contrast,
v_{\ell}
is smooth when
\ell\geq 1
.
Thus (
3.22
) admits a
1
-parameter family of smooth homogeneous solutions.
To select one, we try to match the initial data for
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
near
x=0
as closely as possible.
Since
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0
, we want the formal homogeneous sum (
3.21
) to vanish to all orders in
x
at
(t_{0},0)
.
We choose
C_{\ell}
in (
3.28
) to arrange this.

At fixed
t<0
, we can explicitly compute

(3.30)

\mathfrak{u}(t,x)\sim xt^{-1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad m\to\left|t\right|^{-1}\quad\text{as }x\to 0.

So

(3.31)

v_{\ell}(t_{0},x)=\mathfrak{u}(t_{0},x)^{\ell-1}\mathfrak{m}(t_{0},x)\sim(-1)^{\ell-1}\left|t_{0}\right|^{-\ell}x^{\ell-1}\quad\text{as }x\to 0.

We wish to approximate
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
by
\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq 0}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}
when
\left|x\right|\ll 1
, so we choose the coefficient
C_{\ell}
of
v_{\ell}
to cancel the
x^{\ell-1}
term in the Taylor series of
\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq 0}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)
around
x=0
.
Define the operator

T_{\ell}[f]\coloneqq\frac{1}{(\ell-1)!}\partial_{x}^{\ell-1}f(t_{0},0).

Then
T_{\ell}
extracts the coefficient of
x^{\ell-1}
in the Taylor series of
f
at time
t_{0}
.
By (
3.26
) and (
3.31
),
u_{1,\ell}^{F}
is divisible by
\mathfrak{u}^{\ell+1}
and
\mathfrak{u}^{\ell+1}\asymp_{t}x^{\ell+1}
near
x=0
.
It follows that
T_{\ell}[u_{1,\ell}^{F}]=0
.
On the other hand, (
3.31
) implies that
T_{\ell}[v_{\ell}]=(-1)^{\ell-1}\left|t_{0}\right|^{\ell}
.

Applying
T_{\ell}
to the full homogeneous expansion, we find

T_{\ell}\left[\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq 0}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}\right]=C_{\ell}(-1)^{\ell-1}\left|t_{0}\right|^{\ell}+T_{\ell}\left[\sum_{0\leq\ell^{\prime}<\ell}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}\right].

We inductively choose

C_{\ell}=(-1)^{\ell}\left|t_{0}\right|^{-\ell}T_{\ell}\left[\sum_{0\leq\ell^{\prime}<\ell}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}\right]

so that

T_{\ell}\left[\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq 0}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}\right]=0.

Then we have fixed
C_{\ell}
, and thus
u_{1,\ell}
, for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Given
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, define the partial sum

u_{1,[\ell]}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}.

Our construction ensures that

(3.32)

\left|u_{1,[\ell]}(t_{0},x)\right|\lesssim_{\ell}\left|x\right|^{\ell}.

We will show that
u_{1,[\ell]}
approximates
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
near
x=0
:

Lemma 3.8
.

For each
i,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
u_{1,\ell}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell-1,1)
and

(3.33)

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}(u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{1,[\ell]})\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i,i+1)\quad\text{in }[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1].

Proof.

Fix
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
The difference
w\coloneqq u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{1,[\ell]}
solves

(3.34)

\partial_{t}w=-\partial_{x}\left[u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\left(w+u_{1,[\ell]}\right)\right]+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\partial_{t}u_{1,[\ell]}.

Using (
3.22
), we write

\partial_{t}u_{1,[\ell]}=-\sum_{0\leq\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}\leq\ell}\partial_{x}(u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}u_{1,\ell^{\prime\prime}})+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0,[\ell]}.

Rearranging (
3.34
), we obtain

(3.35)

\partial_{t}w=-\partial_{x}(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}w)+G_{\ell}

for

G_{\ell}\coloneqq-\partial_{x}\left[(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{0,[\ell]})u_{1,[\ell]}\right]-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}\leq\ell\\
\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}>\ell\end{subarray}}\partial_{x}(u_{0,\ell^{\prime}}u_{1,\ell^{\prime\prime}})+\partial_{x}^{2}(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{0,[\ell]}).

Recall from (
3.28
) that
u_{1,\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}(\ell^{\prime}-1,1)
.
Using (
3.2
), (
3.5
), and Lemma
3.4
, a calculation shows that

(3.36)

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}G_{\ell}\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+2,i+3)\quad\textrm{for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

We now prove (
3.33
) by induction.
Fix
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and suppose we have shown (
3.33
) for all
i^{\prime}<i
, noting that this assumption is vacuous in the base case
i=0
.
Differentiating (
3.35
), the derivative
z\coloneqq\partial_{x}^{i}w
satisfies

(3.37)

\partial_{t}z=-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\partial_{x}z-(i+1)(\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})z+G_{i,\ell}

for

G_{i,\ell}\coloneqq-\sum_{i^{\prime}=2}^{i+1}\binom{i+1}{i^{\prime}}\partial_{x}^{i^{\prime}}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\partial_{x}^{i+1-i^{\prime}}w+\partial_{x}^{i}G_{\ell}.

Note that
i+1-i^{\prime}<i
in the sum, so
G_{i,\ell}
only involves lower derivatives of
w
.
Using (
3.33
) for
i+1-i^{\prime}
(the inductive hypothesis) as well as Corollary
3.5
and (
3.36
), we see that

(3.38)

\left|G_{i,\ell}\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+2,i+3).

To integrate the advection equation (
3.37
), we introduce dynamic coordinates
(t,u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})
.
These are valid on
\mathcal{A}
defined in (
2.1
).
Let
\tilde{\partial}_{t}
denote partial differentiation in
t
with
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
held constant.
Noting that
\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}<0
on
\mathcal{A}
, (
3.37
) becomes

(3.39)

\tilde{\partial}_{t}z=(i+1)\left|\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|z+G_{i,\ell}.

Before proceeding, we relate
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
to
\mathfrak{u}
.
By Lemma
3.4
,

\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\mathfrak{u}\right|\lesssim\mathfrak{u}^{4}\mathfrak{m}\lesssim\mathfrak{u}^{2}.

Moreover, because
\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}<0
on
\mathcal{A}
,
\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}<0
, and
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\mathfrak{u}=0
at
x=0
, we have

\operatorname{sgn}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}=\operatorname{sgn}\mathfrak{u}=\operatorname{sgn}x\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}.

It follows that

(3.40)

u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\asymp\mathfrak{u}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}.

We show a similar relationship for the spatial derivative.
Let
{m_{0}\coloneqq-\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}>0}
on
\mathcal{A}
.
Recall from Section
2.2
that
\omega(t,u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t,x))=x
.
Differentiating, we find
m_{0}\partial_{y}\omega=1.

Now, the Taylor expansion (
2.5
) for
\omega
is valid in
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, so we can differentiate it to obtain

m_{0}(t,u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{-1}=\left|t\right|+3\beta_{3}(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{2}+\mathcal{O}\big(|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}|^{3}\big).

When
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
is small, Lemma
2.1
and (
3.40
) yield

(3.41)

m_{0}(t,u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\asymp\left[\left|t\right|+(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{2}\right]^{-1}\asymp\left[\left|t\right|+\mathfrak{u}^{2}\right]^{-1}\asymp\mathfrak{m}.

On the other hand, wherever
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\gtrsim 1
on
\mathcal{A}
, both
m_{0}
and
\mathfrak{m}
are bounded away from
0
and
\infty
, so again
m_{0}\asymp\mathfrak{m}
.
We therefore have

(3.42)

u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\asymp\mathfrak{u}\quad\textrm{and}\quad m_{0}\asymp\mathfrak{m}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}.

We now tackle (
3.39
).
Because
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
satisfies inviscid Burgers, we can compute
\tilde{\partial}_{t}m_{0}=m_{0}^{2}.

Hence

\tilde{\partial}_{t}m_{0}^{-(i+1)}=-(i+1)m_{0}^{-i}.

We can thus use
m_{0}^{-(i+1)}
as an integrating factor in (
3.39
).
We obtain a special solution:

(3.43)

z^{F}\coloneqq m_{0}^{i+1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}m_{0}^{-(i+1)}G_{i,\ell}\;\mathrm{d}s.

We emphasize that
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
is held constant in this integral.
Recalling (
3.4
), we write

\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+2,i+3)=\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|^{\alpha}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta}

for integers
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta\geq i+3
depending on
i
and
\ell
.
Then (
3.38
), (
3.41
), and (
3.42
) yield

\big|m_{0}^{-(i+1)}G_{i,\ell}\big|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|^{\alpha}\left[\left|t\right|+(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{2}\right]^{-(\beta-i-1)}.

We integrate the right side in
t
while holding
u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
fixed.
Because
\beta-i-1\geq 2
, we do not pick up a logarithm in
t
; (
3.43
) implies:

\left|z^{F}\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|^{\alpha}m_{0}^{i+1}\left[\left|t\right|+(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{2}\right]^{-(\beta-i-2)}.

Using (
3.4
), (
3.41
) and (
3.42
) again, this becomes

(3.44)

\left|z^{F}\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|^{\alpha}m_{0}^{\beta-1}\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|^{\alpha}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta-1}=\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+2,i+2).

Next, we incorporate the initial data

z(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\partial_{x}^{i}w(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=-\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,).

Here we have used
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0
.
Recall the inverse
\mathring{\omega}
of
\mathring{u}
and define

(3.45)

f(y)\coloneqq-\left|\partial_{y}\mathring{\omega}(y)\right|^{i+1}\left[z^{F}\big(t_{0},\mathring{\omega}(y)\big)+\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}\big(t_{0},\mathring{\omega}(y)\big)\right]\quad\textrm{for }y\in\mathcal{B}.

By the chain rule,
\big[(\partial_{y}\mathring{\omega})\circ\mathring{u}\big]^{-1}=\partial_{x}\mathring{u}=m_{0}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)
.
Hence we can substitute

y=\mathring{u}(x)=u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},x)

in (
3.45
) to obtain

f\big(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},x)\big)\left|m_{0}(t_{0},x)\right|^{i+1}=-z^{F}(t_{0},x)-\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}(t_{0},x)\quad\textrm{for all }x\in\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}.

We then define

(3.46)

v\coloneqq f(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})m_{0}^{i+1},

which solves the unforced equation

(3.47)

\tilde{\partial}_{t}v=(i+1)m_{0}v,\quad v(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=-z^{F}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)-\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,).

Now, (
3.30
) and (
3.44
) imply that

\left|z^{F}(t_{0},x)\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+2,i+2)(t_{0},x)\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|x\right|^{(\ell-i+2)\vee 0}.

Also, (
3.2
) and (
3.32
) yield

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}(t_{0},x)\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|x\right|^{(\ell-i)\vee 0}\quad\textrm{for }x\in\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}.

Therefore,

(3.48)

\left|z^{F}+\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|x\right|^{(\ell-i)\vee 0}

when
t=t_{0}
.
Now,
\partial_{y}\mathring{\omega}\asymp 1
on
\mathcal{B}
.
Thus (
3.45
) and (
3.48
) yield

\left|f(y)\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\left|y\right|^{(\ell-i)\vee 0}.

Hence (
3.42
) and (
3.46
) imply that

(3.49)

\left|v\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}\big((\ell-i)\vee 0,i+1\big).

Recall that
z^{F}
solves (
3.39
).
In light of (
3.47
), we see that both
z
and
z^{F}+v
solve (
3.39
) with initial data
-\partial_{x}^{i}u_{1,[\ell]}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)
.
By uniqueness, we have
z=z^{F}+v
.
Finally, (
3.44
), (
3.49
), and
\mathfrak{u}^{2}\mathfrak{m}\lesssim 1
yield

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}w\right|=\left|z\right|\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i+2,i+2)+\mathfrak{e}\big((\ell-i)\vee 0,i+1\big)\lesssim_{i,\ell}\mathfrak{e}(\ell-i,i+1)\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}.

Because
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
and
u_{1,[\ell]}
are smooth away from the origin in spacetime, this bound also holds in
[t_{0},0)\times[-1,1]
.
This proves (
3.33
) for
i
.
The lemma follows from induction.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

There is another possible approach to Lemma
3.8
due to the following curious observation:
-\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\big(\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)^{-2}
solves the PDE in (
3.20
).
It does not satisfy the initial condition, but this can be fixed by adding an appropriate solution
f(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
of the unforced equation
\partial_{t}v=-\partial_{x}(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}v)
.
We proceed much as in (
3.45
).
Recalling the inverse
\mathring{\omega}
of
\mathring{u}
, let

f\coloneqq\frac{(\partial_{x}^{2}\mathring{u})\circ\mathring{\omega}}{(\partial_{x}\mathring{u})^{3}\circ\mathring{\omega}}.

Then

u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}=-\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}}{(\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})^{2}}+f(u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}

satisfies (
3.20
).
Using Lemma
3.4
, we can construct the homogeneous expansion for
u_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
and prove Lemma
3.8
by elementary series expansion.
We believe that this exact formula is an artifact of the Cole–Hopf transformation.
More general scalar conservation laws do not seem to admit such identities, so we adopt a more robust strategy above.

3.4.
Higher viscous correctors

To close, we consider the higher-order correctors
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
for
k\geq 2
.
Each satisfies (
2.8
), which we write as

\partial_{t}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}=k}\partial_{x}(u_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}u_{k^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}})+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{k-1}^{\mathrm{out}}.

We expect each
u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
to admit a homogeneous expansion of the form

u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}u_{k,\ell}

about
x=0
.
After formal substitution, we can check that the terms in this series should satisfy

(3.50)

\partial_{t}u_{k,\ell}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}=k\\
\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}=\ell\end{subarray}}\partial_{x}(u_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}u_{k^{\prime\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}})+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{k-1,\ell}.

Structurally, this is very similar to the equation for
u_{1,\ell}
.
Define

Remark 3.3
.

We work with smooth data for convenience, but Proposition

is true, up to a point, under bounded regularity.
Precisely, one can check that the proposition holds for the values

when
\mathring{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{i+2k+\ell+4}
.
Thus, the construction of the outer expansion demands ever greater regularity as

and
\ell
increase.
The dependence on
\ell
is clear, since
\ell
corresponds to the Taylor series of
\mathring{u}
.
The regularity also depends on

because

is forced by
\partial_{x}^{2}u_{k-1}^{\mathrm{out}}

We thus need two additional derivatives as

increments.
This is not surprising: we are attempting to approximate a second-order equation by first-order equations, so we lose derivatives.

Remark 3.4
.

For simplicity, we have assumed that
u^{\nu}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}
is independent of

.
However, we could naturally allow

.
Then

would have nontrivial initial data corresponding to the

term in

about

.

Indeed, we can adapt the construction of

to form

.
Let

denote the particular solution of (

Recalling (

This selection principle for

.

and

.

4.
The inner expansion

We now turn to the inner expansion.
Recall the inner variables

T\coloneqq\nu^{-1/2}t\quad\textrm{and}\quad X\coloneqq\nu^{-3/4}x

defined in (
2.18
).
This is simply a
\lambda
-rescaling
(T,X)\mapsto(\lambda^{2}T,\lambda^{3}X)=(t,x)
with
\lambda=\nu^{1/4}
.
Thus if
f
is
\ell
-homogeneous, we have

f(t,x)=\nu^{\ell/4}f(T,X).

In particular, we make use of the following rescaled homogeneous functions:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{U}(T,X)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}\mathfrak{u}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(T,X)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nu^{1/2}\mathfrak{m}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(T,X)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}\mathfrak{d}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X).

These functions are simply
\mathfrak{u}
,
\mathfrak{m}
, and
\mathfrak{d}
evaluated at
(T,X)
rather than
(t,x)
.
We employ distinct notation to indicate that these functions are implicitly evaluated in the inner coordinates.

As noted in Section
2
, the blow-up
U^{\nu}(T,X)\coloneqq\nu^{-1/4}u^{\nu}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X)
solves viscous Burgers (
2.19
) with
unit
viscosity in the blown-up domain
(\nu^{-1/2}t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R}
.
Recalling (
2.22
), we expect
U^{\nu}
to admit an expansion of the form

U^{\nu}\sim\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\nu^{\ell/4}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}\quad\text{ when }\nu\ll 1\text{ and }\mathfrak{D}\ll\nu^{-1/4}.

Each term
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
is defined on
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Plugging this sum into (
2.19
), we formally obtain the evolution equations (
2.23
) for
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
.
For convenience, we restate them here:

(4.3)

\partial_{T}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}=\ell}\partial_{X}(U_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}U_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}})+\partial_{X}^{2}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}.

Naturally, the leading term satisfies viscous Burgers:

(4.4)

\partial_{T}U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}=-U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}\partial_{X}U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}+\partial_{X}^{2}U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}.

When
\ell\geq 1,
(
4.3
) is linear and can be written as a passive scalar equation with diffusion:

\partial_{T}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}=-\partial_{X}(U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}})-\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\ell-1}U_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}\partial_{X}U_{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}+\partial_{X}^{2}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}.

We emphasize that the sum only involves
U_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}
for
0<\ell^{\prime}<\ell
, so it is simply a forcing term.

Each
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
is an eternal solution of its respective equation, so it does not have “initial data” per se.
Rather, we choose the far-field behavior of
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
to match the outer expansion (
2.7
).
We do so through the homogeneous components
u_{k,\ell}
in (
2.25
).
Recall that
u_{k,\ell}
is
(\ell-4k+1)
-homogeneous.
Thus in the inner coordinates, the term
\nu^{k}u_{k,\ell}
in the outer expansion becomes

\nu^{k}u_{k,\ell}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X)=\nu^{k+(\ell-4k+1)/4}u_{k,\ell}(T,X)=\nu^{(\ell+1)/4}u_{k,\ell}(T,X).

On the other hand,

u^{\nu}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X)=\nu^{1/4}U^{\nu}(T,X)\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\nu^{(\ell+1)/4}U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}(T,X).

Hence
u_{k,\ell}
should contribute to
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
.
To keep the inner notation separate, we define

U_{k,\ell}(T,X)\coloneqq\nu^{k-(\ell+1)/4}u_{k,\ell}(\nu^{1/2}T,\nu^{3/4}X).

Then
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
should somehow incorporate the homogeneous components
\{U_{k,\ell}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
For fixed
k\geq 0
,
U_{k+1,\ell}
is
(\ell-4k-3)
-homogeneous, so it decays faster than
U_{k,\ell}
at infinity.
We therefore conjecture that
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
admits an asymptotic expansion about infinity of the form

(4.5)

U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}U_{k,\ell}\quad\text{as }\mathfrak{D}\to\infty\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}.

We introduce the corresponding partial sum

U_{[k],\ell}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}U_{k^{\prime},\ell}.

Using (
3.50
), we can check that
U_{[k],\ell}
satisfies

(4.6)

\partial_{T}U_{[k],\ell}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}\leq k\\
\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}=\ell\end{subarray}}\partial_{X}(U_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}U_{k^{\prime\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}})+\partial_{X}^{2}U_{[k-1],\ell}

under the convention that
U_{[-1],\ell}=0
when
k=0
.

To prove the expansion (
4.5
), we argue that
U_{[k],\ell}
is a good approximate solution of (
4.3
) at infinity.
More concretely, we study the evolution equation for the difference
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}-U_{[k],\ell}
.
We construct super- and subsolutions of this equation that are appropriately small, and conclude that there exists a solution
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
of (
4.3
) close to
U_{[k],\ell}
at infinity.

Throughout, we find it technically convenient to construct one pair
\pm W_{\text{early}}
of super- and subsolutions on the deep past
\{T<T_{\text{early}}\}
, and another pair
\pm W_{\text{late}}
on
\{T_{\text{early}}\leq T<0\}
, for some
T_{\text{early}}\ll-1
.
The early pair
\pm W_{\text{early}}
yields the solution
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
through diagonalization.
However, our construction of
W_{\text{early}}
involves a Riccati equation that blows up at finite negative time.
Before this blow-up, we hand our estimates off to
\pm W_{\text{late}}
, which ensure that
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
behaves as expected on the final bounded time interval.

The evolution equations for the differences
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}-U_{[k],\ell}
have a common form.
We can therefore construct our super- and subsolutions with one lemma.
In the following, let
\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq(-1,0)\times(-1,1)\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
denote a rectangle of spacetime near the origin and let
\partial^{*}\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq[-1,0)\times\{\pm 1\}
denote the portion of the boundary that parabolically influences the complement
\mathcal{Q}^{c}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R})\setminus\mathcal{Q}
.

Let

For each

Remark 4.1
.

The leading term

plays a particularly important role.
We discuss its structure in more detail in Appendix

.

Remark 4.2
.

According to Proposition
4.2
, once (

) holds for some

, it holds for
every

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
That is, if

agrees with the homogeneous expansion (

) to a certain finite order, it automatically agrees to every order.

\left\lceil\ell/4\right\rceil

Remark
3.4
explains this rigidity.
There, we observed that

, and hence
U_{k,\ell}
, is independent of the initial data when

.

universal
.
Suppose (
4.11
) holds for some

.
The homogeneous components of (

) have the form

It follows that the homogeneous expansion (

.

Reasoning in the opposite direction, we see that the uniqueness condition in Proposition

is sharp.
If

, there exists

such that
u_{k,\ell}
is
not
universal.
Different choices of the initial data

can lead to different homogeneous components

.
Thus there exist homogeneous expansions (
2.26
) that agree up to order

but differ thereafter.
Each expansion corresponds to a distinct solution

of (
4.3
), so the estimate (
4.11
) with

does
not
ensure the uniqueness of

.

and
O
, respectively.
In the matching zone
M
, we interpolate between these truncations.
For simplicity, we use the same number of terms from the inner and outer expansions.
Given
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we construct
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
from the partial sums
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}
defined in (
4.51
) and (
4.52
).

Let
\vartheta\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty))
be a bump function such that
\vartheta|_{[0,1]}\equiv 1
,
\vartheta|_{[2,\infty)}\equiv 0
, and
0\leq\vartheta\leq 1
.
We define the cutoff

\theta(t,x)\coloneqq\vartheta\left(\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\nu^{-\alpha}\right)

so that
\theta|_{I}\equiv 1
and
\theta|_{O}\equiv 0
.
We then define the approximate solution

(5.1)

u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\coloneqq\theta u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\theta)u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}.

Note that

u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=\mathring{u}=u^{\nu}(t_{0},\,\cdot\,).

That is, the approximate solution agrees with the true solution at the initial time
t_{0}
.
However, the functions diverge when
t>t_{0}
because
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
is not an exact solution of (
1.1
).
The “error”

(5.2)

E\coloneqq-\partial_{t}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}+\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}

captures this inexactness.
The approximate solution
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
is governed by two parameters:
\alpha
and
K
.
We choose these to make
E
appropriately small.

On
I
and
O
, the error
E
is due to our truncation of the inner and outer expansions.
The error can thus be made arbitrarily small by taking
K
large.
On
M
, there is an additional source of error—the cutoff interpolating
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
To minimize this interpolation error, we want
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}
to be as similar as possible in
M
.
By Proposition
4.5
,

\big|u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{K+2}+\nu^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-4K-3}.

The right side is minimized when the two terms are of the same order, i.e., when
\mathfrak{d}\asymp\nu^{1/5}
.
We therefore choose
\alpha=1/5
in our definition of
I,O
, and
M
, so that the interpolation error is minimal in the matching zone.
We emphasize that this exponent originates in our choice to use equally many terms from the inner and outer expansions.
If we used unbalanced truncations, a different exponent would be optimal.

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u^{\nu}
solve (
1.1
) and (
1.4
).
Fix
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and take
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
as in (
5.1
).
Then there exists a constant
C(K,\mathring{u})>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
,

(5.3)

\big\|u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R})}\leq C(K,\mathring{u})\nu^{(K+2)/5}.

Moreover, for all
K\geq 0
,

(5.4)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},0)}\big\|u^{\nu}(t,\,\cdot\,)-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}(t,\,\cdot\,)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\to 0\quad\textrm{as }\;\nu\to 0.

We can thus approximate
u^{\nu}
to arbitrary precision in
\nu
by including sufficiently many terms from the inner and outer expansions.
Furthermore, the difference
u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
is uniformly
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder.

Remark 5.1
.

As noted in Remark
1.3
, we expect
u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
to remain uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{C}_{x}^{(K+2)/3}
for all
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
This is the (quantitative) regularity of the leading terms
u_{0,K+1}
and
\nu^{K+1}u_{K+1,0}
in the difference
u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}
on
M
.

Theorem
5.1
implies the informal Theorem
1.1
stated in the introduction.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Only the first part of (
1.10
) remains to be shown.
Due to the inverse cubic term
u_{0,0}=\mathfrak{u}
on
O
, the approximate solution
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}
is only uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{C}^{1/3}
as
\nu\to 0
.
That is, if
\beta>1/3
,

(5.5)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},0)}\big\|u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}(t,\,\cdot\,)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})}\to\infty\quad\textrm{as }\;\nu\to 0.

Moreover, the higher-order terms in
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
are all smoother—it is straightforward to check that Corollaries
3.10
and
4.4
as well as Lemma
5.3
below imply that

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},0)}\big\|\big(u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}\big)(t,\,\cdot\,)\big\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2/3}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim 1.

Hence (
5.5
) holds with
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
in place of
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}
.
Thus the first part of (
1.10
) follows from the second.
∎

We can also prove Corollary
1.2
.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

By Theorem
5.1
and the triangle inequality,

(5.6)

\big\|u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\nu^{2/5}.

Recall the inviscid solution
u^{0}=u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
We claim that

(5.7)

\big\|u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{0},0)\times\mathbb{R})}\asymp\nu^{1/4}.

Indeed, by (
5.1
),

u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}-u^{0}=(u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\theta.

Applying Proposition
4.5
,

\left|(u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\theta\right|\lesssim\left(\mathfrak{d}^{2}+\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-3}\right)\theta

when
\mathfrak{d}\geq\nu^{1/4}
.
Because
\theta
is supported on
I\cup M
, the second term dominates, and

(5.8)

\left|(u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\theta\right|\lesssim\nu^{1/4}\quad\text{when }\mathfrak{d}\geq\nu^{1/4}.

When
\mathfrak{d}\leq\nu^{1/4}
, Proposition
3.9
implies that

(5.9)

\left|(u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\theta\right|=\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|=\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-\mathfrak{u}\right|+\mathcal{O}\big(\nu^{1/2}\big).

In the inner coordinates,
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
\mathfrak{U}
are independent of
\nu
and distinct—indeed, the former is smooth on
(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}
and the latter is not.
It follows that
\left|U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-\mathfrak{U}\right|\asymp 1
on
\{\mathfrak{D}\leq 1\}
.
When we return to the original coordinates, this difference is scaled by
\nu^{1/4}.

Thus (
5.9
) yields

\left|(u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}})\theta\right|=\left|u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-\mathfrak{u}\right|+\mathcal{O}\big(\nu^{1/2}\big)\asymp\nu^{1/4}\quad\text{when }\mathfrak{d}\leq\nu^{1/4}.

Together with (
5.8
), this implies (
5.7
).
Now (
1.11
) follows from (
5.6
) and (
5.7
).
∎

Remark 5.2
.

By (
5.6
),
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[0]}
is a better approximation of
u^{\nu}
than
u^{0}
alone.

5.1.
Energy estimates

We rely on an
L^{2}
approach to prove Theorem
5.1
.
We begin with the energy estimates used in the proof.
For concision, we often abbreviate
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
by
u^{\mathrm{app}}
when the particular index is insignificant.
We study the evolution equation for the difference
v\coloneqq u^{\nu}-u^{\mathrm{app}}
:

(5.10)

\partial_{t}v=-\partial_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)-v\partial_{x}v+\nu\partial_{x}^{2}v+E,\quad v(t_{0},\,\cdot\,)=0,

where
E
is defined in (
5.2
).
Before analyzing the forcing
E
, we prove energy estimates for arbitrary
E
.
In the following, we use the shorthand

\left\|f\right\|_{V_{x}}\!(t)\coloneqq\left\|f(t,\,\cdot\,)\right\|_{V}

when
V
is a norm on functions on
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
v
satisfy (
5.10
).
Then for all
\eta\in[0,1)
and
\mathfrak{t}\in[t_{0},0]
,

(5.11)

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})\leq\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|^{\eta}E^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\exp\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\big(\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+|t|^{-\eta}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

and

(5.12)

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|^{\eta}\big[(\partial_{x}E)^{2}+(\partial^{2}_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})^{2}v^{2}\big]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\big(3\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+\nu^{-1}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}^{2}+2|t|^{-\eta}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Proof.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1]
and
\mathfrak{t}\in[t_{0},0]
.
We first prove (
5.11
).
Multiplying (
5.10
) by
v
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}(v^{2})-\nu v\partial_{x}^{2}v=-v\partial_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)-v^{2}\partial_{x}{v}+Ev=-v\partial_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)-\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}(v^{3})+Ev.

We integrate over
[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}
and integrate by parts in space:

(5.13)

\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})+\nu\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}v)^{2}\leq\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}+Ev\right).

We use Young’s identity to write
2Ev\leq\left|t\right|^{\eta}E^{2}+\left|t\right|^{-\eta}v^{2}
.
Dropping the viscous term, (
5.13
) yields

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})\leq\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|^{\eta}E^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left(\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}+\left|t\right|^{-\eta}\right)\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}}^{2}.

Grönwall’s inequality implies (
5.11
).

We now turn to (
5.12
).
Differentiating (
5.10
), we find

\partial_{t}\partial_{x}v-\nu\partial_{x}^{3}v=-\partial_{x}^{2}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)-\partial_{x}(v\partial_{x}v)+\partial_{x}E.

We multiply by
\partial_{x}v
, integrate over
[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}
, and integrate by parts in space:

(5.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}(\mathfrak{t})+\nu\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}^{2}v)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\left[-\partial_{x}^{2}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)-\partial_{x}(v\partial_{x}v)+\partial_{x}E\right]\partial_{x}v.

Expanding
\partial^{2}_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}v)\partial^{2}_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{app}}\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}v)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+2\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})(\partial_{x}v)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial^{2}_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})v\partial_{x}v\;\mathrm{d}x.

Integrating the first term on the right by parts, this yields

(5.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}v)\partial^{2}_{x}(u^{\mathrm{app}}v)\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{3}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})(\partial_{x}v)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial^{2}_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})v\partial_{x}v\;\mathrm{d}x.

Similarly, we have

(5.16)

-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}v)\partial_{x}(v\partial_{x}v)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(\partial_{x}v)\partial^{2}_{x}v\;\mathrm{d}x.

Combining (
5.15
) and (
5.16
), (
5.14
) yields

(5.17)

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\!(\mathfrak{t})+2\nu\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}^{2}v)^{2}\leq\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\big[2|v(\partial_{x}v)\partial^{2}_{x}v|+2|\partial_{x}v||\partial_{x}E|\big]

\displaystyle+\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\big[3|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}|(\partial_{x}v)^{2}+2|(\partial^{2}_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})v\partial_{x}v|\big].

Now, Young’s inequality implies

\displaystyle 2|(\partial^{2}_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}})v\partial_{x}v|

\displaystyle\leq\left|t\right|^{-\eta}(\partial_{x}v)^{2}+\left|t\right|^{\eta}(\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}})^{2}v^{2},

\displaystyle 2|v(\partial_{x}v)\partial^{2}_{x}v|

\displaystyle\leq\nu(\partial_{x}^{2}v)^{2}+\nu^{-1}(\partial_{x}v)^{2}v^{2},

\displaystyle 2\left|\partial_{x}v\partial_{x}E\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|t\right|^{\eta}(\partial_{x}E)^{2}+\left|t\right|^{-\eta}(\partial_{x}v)^{2}.

Using these bounds in (
5.17
) and rearranging, we find

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\!(\mathfrak{t})\leq

\displaystyle\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|^{\eta}\left[(\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}})^{2}v^{2}+(\partial_{x}E)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\int_{[t_{0},\mathfrak{t}]\times\mathbb{R}}\left[3\left|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right|+\nu^{-1}v^{2}+2\left|t\right|^{-\eta}\right](\partial_{x}v)^{2}.

Now (
5.12
) follows from Grönwall.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

We use the viscosity to absorb an adverse term in the proof of (
5.12
).
This is a convenience, rather than a necessity.
Indeed, we could take more derivatives (two suffice) and close in
L^{2}
, which would give pointwise control on the offending factor (namely,
\partial_{x}^{2}v
).
Thus, we do not need the “good” viscous term this section.
We have already captured viscous effects in our construction of the inner and outer expansions; we do not need to use the viscosity further.

5.2.
Forcing estimates

In light of Lemma
5.2
, we need to control
E
in
H_{x}^{1}
.
The error has a different character in the three zones
O,I,
and
M
.
In
O
and
I
,
E
is the error from truncating the inner and outer expansions, respectively.
Using (
2.8
), (
4.3
), and (
4.50
), we can compute

(5.18)

\displaystyle E^{\text{out}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\partial_{t}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}+\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\leq K\\
k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}>K\end{subarray}}\nu^{k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}}\partial_{x}\left(u_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}u_{k^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)+\nu^{K+1}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{K}^{\mathrm{out}}.

and

(5.19)

E^{\text{in}}\coloneqq-\partial_{t}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}+\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}\leq K\\
\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}>K\end{subarray}}\partial_{x}\left(u_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}u_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}\right).

Let
E^{\text{match}}
denote the error in the matching zone
M
, so that:

E(t,x)=\begin{cases}E^{\text{out}}&\text{in }O\\
E^{\text{match}}&\text{in }M\\
E^{\text{in}}&\text{in }I.\end{cases}

The matching error is somewhat complicated due to the cutoff.
Define the mismatch

h\coloneqq u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}.

Then we can write

E^{\text{match}}=E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3},

for

(5.20)

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\theta E^{\text{in}}+(1-\theta)E^{\text{out}}

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(-\partial_{t}\theta+\nu\partial_{x}^{2}\theta-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\partial_{x}\theta-\theta h\partial_{x}\theta\big)h,

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big(2\nu\partial_{x}\theta+\theta(1-\theta)h\big)\partial_{x}h.

To bound
E
, we must first control the derivatives of
\theta
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Define
\theta
by (
2.27
).
Then for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

(5.21)

|\partial_{t}^{i}\partial_{x}^{j}\theta|\lesssim_{i,j}\nu^{-(2i+3j)/5}.

Proof.

Since
\theta
is locally constant outside
M
, we may assume that
\nu^{1/5}\leq\mathfrak{d}\leq 2\nu^{1/5}
.
Using (
2.27
) and the
1
-homogeneity of
\mathfrak{d}
, we write

\theta(t,x)=\vartheta\big(\mathfrak{d}(\nu^{-2/5}t,\nu^{-3/5}x)\big).

Therefore

(5.22)

(\partial_{t}^{i}\partial_{x}^{j}\theta)(t,x)=\nu^{-(2i+3j)/5}\big[\partial_{t}^{i}\partial_{x}^{j}(\vartheta\circ\mathfrak{d})\big]|_{(\nu^{-2/5}t,\nu^{-3/5}x)}.

Since
\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\asymp\nu^{1/5}
,
1
-homogeneity yields
\mathfrak{d}(\nu^{-2/5}t,\nu^{-3/5}x)=\nu^{-1/5}\mathfrak{d}(t,x)\asymp 1
.
The derivatives of
\vartheta\circ\mathfrak{d}
are bounded when
\mathfrak{d}\asymp 1
, so (
5.21
) follows from (
5.22
).
∎

We can now control
E
and
\partial_{x}E
pointwise.

Proposition 5.4
.

For all
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\left|E\right|\lesssim_{K}\begin{cases}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}&\text{in }O\cup M,\\
\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{K}&\text{in }I\cup M\end{cases}

and

\left|\partial_{x}E\right|\lesssim_{K}\begin{cases}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-4}&\text{in }O\cup M,\\
\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{K-3}&\text{in }I\cup M.\end{cases}

Proof.

We first consider
E^{\text{out}}
given by (
5.18
).
By Corollary
3.10
,

\left|\partial_{x}(u_{k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}}u_{k^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{out}})\right|\lesssim_{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}}\mathfrak{d}^{-4(k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime})-1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left|\partial_{x}^{2}u_{K}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-4K-5}.

By (
5.18
),

\left|E^{\text{out}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\leq K\\
k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}>K\end{subarray}}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}+\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}.

On
O\cup M
,
\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\leq\nu^{1/5}\ll 1
, so the dominant terms in the sum are those with
k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime}=K+1
.
Hence

\left|E^{\text{out}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\quad\text{in }O\cup M.

After a nearly identical argument for the derivative, we have the pair of estimates

(5.23)

\left|E^{\text{out}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left|\partial_{x}E^{\text{out}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\quad\text{in }O\cup M.

Next, consider
E^{\text{in}}
given by (
5.19
).
By Corollary
4.4
, we have

\left|\partial_{x}(u_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}u_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}})\right|\lesssim_{\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}}\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}-1}.

By (
5.19
),

\left|E^{\text{in}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell^{\prime},\ell^{\prime\prime}\leq K\\
\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}>K\end{subarray}}\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}-1}.

On
I\cup M
,
\mathfrak{d}\leq 2\nu^{1/5}\ll 1
, so the dominant terms in the sum are those with
\ell^{\prime}+\ell^{\prime\prime}=K+1
.
Hence

\left|E^{\text{in}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{K}\quad\text{in }I\cup M.

Again, an analogous argument for the derivative yields

(5.24)

\left|E^{\text{in}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{K}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left|\partial_{x}E^{\text{in}}\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{K-3}\quad\text{in }I\cup M.

Finally, we consider
E=E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}
given by (
5.20
) on
M
.
We observe that the upper bounds in (
5.23
) and (
5.24
) agree up to a constant in
M
, where
\mathfrak{d}\asymp\nu^{1/5}
.
Thus Lemma
5.3
, (
5.23
), and (
5.24
) imply that

(5.25)

\left|E_{1}\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{K/5}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left|\partial_{x}E_{1}\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(K-3)/5}\quad\text{in }M.

To control
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
in (
5.20
), we use Proposition
4.5
to bound the mismatch
h=u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}
:

(5.26)

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}h\right|\lesssim_{i,K}\nu^{(-3i+K+2)/5}\quad\text{in }M.

Also, Corollary
3.10
implies that

(5.27)

\left|\partial_{x}^{i}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\right|\lesssim_{i,K}\nu^{(-3i+1)/5}\quad\text{in }M.

Combining Lemma
5.3
, (
5.26
), and (
5.27
), we can check that

\left|E_{2}\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{K/5},\quad\left|\partial_{x}E_{2}\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(K-3)/5},\quad\left|E_{3}\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(K+1)/5},\quad\left|\partial_{x}E_{3}\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(K-2)/5}

in
M
.
Incorporating (
5.25
), we see that
\left|E\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{K/5}
and
\left|\partial_{x}E\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(K-3)/5}
in
M
.
The proposition follows.
∎

These pointwise bounds imply
L^{2}
estimates.

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and
t\in[t_{0},0)
,

\left\|E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+7)/2}

and

\left\|\partial_{x}E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+13)/2}.

Proof.

First suppose
t\leq-\nu^{2/5}
.
Then the time-slice
\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}
lies in
O\cup M
, so by Proposition
5.4
we have

(5.28)

\left|E\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left|\partial_{x}E\right|\lesssim_{K}\big(\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\big)^{K+1}\mathfrak{d}^{-4}\quad\text{on }\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}.

Given
p>3
, Lemma
2.1
yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{d}(t,x)^{-p}\;\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int_{0}^{\left|t\right|^{3/2}}\left|t\right|^{-p/2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\left|t\right|^{3/2}}^{\infty}\left|x\right|^{-p/3}\;\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{p}\left|t\right|^{-(p-3)/2}.

With
p=8K+10
or
8K+16
, (
5.28
) yields

\left\|E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\left|t\right|^{-(8K+7)/2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left\|\partial_{x}E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\left|t\right|^{-(8K+13)/2},

as desired.

Now suppose
t\in(-\nu^{2/5},0)
.
Define
x_{M}(t)>0
by the condition

\mathfrak{d}\big(t,x_{M}(t)\big)=2\nu^{1/5}.

Then
(t,x)\in O
if
\left|x\right|>x_{M}(t)
and
(t,x)\in I\cup M
otherwise.
Using
\left|t\right|<\nu^{2/5}
and Lemma
2.1
, we can check that

(5.29)

x_{M}(t)\asymp\nu^{3/5}.

We write

\left\|E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)=\int_{\{\left|x\right|>x_{M}\}}\left|E\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\{\left|x\right|\leq x_{M}\}}\left|E\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\eqqcolon\mathcal{I}_{1}+\mathcal{I}_{2}.

The first integral
\mathcal{I}_{1}
only involves points in
O
, so (
5.28
) holds there as well.
Thus Lemma
2.1
and (
5.29
) imply

\mathcal{I}_{1}\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\int_{x_{M}}^{\infty}\mathfrak{d}^{-(8K+10)}\;\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\int_{x_{M}}^{\infty}\left|x\right|^{-(8K+10)/3}\;\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(2K+3)/5}.

On the other hand, Proposition
5.4
yields

\mathcal{I}_{2}\lesssim\int_{0}^{x_{M}}(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4})^{2K}\;\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{K}\nu^{K/2}x_{M}+\int_{0}^{x_{M}}\left|x\right|^{2K/3}\;\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(2K+3)/5}.

Collecting these integral bounds, we find

\left\|E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(2K+3)/5}

when
\left|t\right|<\nu^{2/5}
, as desired.
The argument for
\partial_{x}E
is similar, so we omit the details.
We only note one wrinkle: the inner integral for
\partial_{x}E
involves a term of the form

\int_{0}^{x_{M}}\left|x\right|^{2(K-3)/3}\;\mathrm{d}x.

We have assumed that
K\geq 2
so that the integral is finite.
When
K=0
or
1
, different exponents arise in the lemma.
∎

5.3.
Derivative bounds

To deploy the energy estimates in Lemma
5.2
, we must also control derivatives of the approximate solution.

Proposition 5.6
.

For all
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
t\in[t_{0},0)
,

(5.30)

\big\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}(t)=\begin{cases}\left|t\right|^{-1}+\mathcal{O}_{K}\left(\left|t\right|^{-1/2}+\nu\left|t\right|^{-3}\right)&\text{if }\left|t\right|\geq\nu^{1/2},\\
\mathcal{O}_{K}(\nu^{-1/2})&\text{if }\left|t\right|<\nu^{1/2}\end{cases}

and

(5.31)

\big\|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}(t)\lesssim_{K}\left(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\right)^{-5/2}.

Proof.

First, we claim that

(5.32)

\big|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}+\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-6}\quad\text{if }\mathfrak{d}\geq\nu^{1/4}.

By (
3.19
), we have

(5.33)

\big|\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\big|\lesssim\mathfrak{d}^{-1}.

Also, Corollary
3.10
implies that

\big|\nu^{k}\partial_{x}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\big|\lesssim_{k}\nu^{k}\mathfrak{d}^{-4k-2}=(\nu^{k}\mathfrak{d}^{-4k-1})\mathfrak{d}^{-1}.

When
\mathfrak{d}\gtrsim\nu^{1/5}
and
k\geq 1
, we have
\nu^{k}\mathfrak{d}^{-4k-1}\lesssim 1
.
Hence

(5.34)

\big|\nu^{k}\partial_{x}u_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}\big|\lesssim_{k}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\quad\text{on }O\cup M.

Combining this with (
5.33
), we find

\big|\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\quad\text{on }O\cup M.

In particular, this confirms (
5.32
) on
O
.

Next, we apply (
4.55
) and find

\left|\partial_{x}u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\right|\lesssim\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-6}\quad\text{if }\mathfrak{d}\geq\nu^{1/4}.

Also, Corollary
4.4
yields
\left|\partial_{x}u_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}\right|\lesssim_{\ell}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}
when
\mathfrak{d}\lesssim 1
and
\ell\geq 1
.
Combining these observations, we have

(5.35)

\big|\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}+\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-6}\quad\text{if }\nu^{1/4}\leq\mathfrak{d}\leq 1.

This confirms (
5.32
) on
I
.
Finally, on
M
we have

(5.36)

\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}=\theta\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\theta)\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}+(\partial_{x}\theta)\big(u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\big).

By (
5.34
) and (
5.35
),

(5.37)

\big|\theta\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\theta)u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}+\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-6}.

Moreover, Proposition
4.5
and Lemma
5.3
imply that

(5.38)

\left|(\partial_{x}\theta)\big(u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}-u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)\right|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(K-1)/5}\leq\nu^{-1/5}\lesssim\mathfrak{d}^{-1}.

Combining (
5.37
) and (
5.38
), (
5.36
) yields

\big|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-1}+\nu\mathfrak{d}^{-6}.

We have thus verified (
5.32
).
Taking a supremum in
x
and using (
3.17
) and
{\mathfrak{d}^{-1}\leq\left|t\right|^{-1/2}}
we obtain

\big\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}(t)=\left|t\right|^{-1}+\mathcal{O}_{K}\left(\left|t\right|^{-1/2}+\nu\left|t\right|^{-3}\right)\quad\textrm{for }\left|t\right|\geq\nu^{1/2}.

Now if
\mathfrak{d}\leq\nu^{1/4}
,
u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}=u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}
and Corollary
4.4
implies that

(5.39)

\big|\partial_{x}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}\big|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{-1/2}.

Also,
\left|\partial_{x}\mathfrak{u}\right|\lesssim\mathfrak{d}^{-2}\leq\nu^{-1/2}
if
\mathfrak{d}\geq\nu^{1/4}
.
Combining this observation with (
5.32
) and (
5.39
), we find

\big\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}(t)\lesssim_{K}\nu^{-1/2}\quad\textrm{for }\left|t\right|\leq\nu^{1/2}.

We have thus proven (
5.30
).

We now turn to
\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}
.
Corollary
3.10
yields

(5.40)

\big|\partial_{x}^{2}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{out}}\big|\lesssim_{K}\mathfrak{d}^{-5}\quad\text{on }O\cup M,

while Corollary
4.4
implies

(5.41)

\big|\partial_{x}^{2}u_{[K]}^{\mathrm{in}}\big|\lesssim_{K}\big(\mathfrak{d}\vee\nu^{1/4}\big)^{-5}\quad\text{on }I\cup M.

Finally, Proposition
4.5
and Lemma
5.3
imply that

(5.42)

\big|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}_{[K]}\big|\lesssim_{K}\nu^{-1}\quad\text{on }M.

Combining (
5.40
)–(
5.42
) and taking the supremum in
x,
we obtain (
5.31
).
∎

5.4.
Bootstrapping

We now employ a bootstrap argument to show that the solution
v
of (
5.10
) remains suitably small.
Ultimately, we wish to control
\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}
.
To do so, we will interpolate between (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) in Lemma
5.2
.
However, the exponential in (
5.12
) depends on
\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}
itself.
We therefore bootstrap on
\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}
.
Fix
{\varepsilon\in(0,1/100)}
.
We will work to prove:

(B)

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\!(t)\leq\nu^{-\varepsilon+K+1}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-2K-3/4}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-2}.

More plainly:

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\!(t)\leq\begin{cases}\nu^{-\varepsilon+K+1}\left|t\right|^{-2K-11/4}&\quad\text{if }t\in[t_{0},-\nu^{2/5}),\\
\nu^{-\varepsilon+(2K+7)/10}\left|t\right|^{-2}&\quad\text{if }t\in(-\nu^{2/5},-\nu^{1/2}],\\
\nu^{-\varepsilon+(2K-3)/10}&\quad\text{if }t\in(-\nu^{1/2},0).\end{cases}

The following proposition is our essential tool; it states that the bootstrap hypothesis is
self-improving
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Fix
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 3}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
There exists
\bar{\nu}(K,\varepsilon)\in(0,1/100)
such that the following holds for all
\nu\in(0,\bar{\nu}]
.
Suppose there exists
t_{\mathrm{B}}\in(t_{0},0)
such that the solution
v
of (
5.10
) satisfies (
B
) on
[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}}]
.
Then for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}})
,

(5.43)

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\!(t)\leq\nu^{-\varepsilon/2+K+1}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/4}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-2}.

That is, we can shave
\nu^{-\varepsilon/2}
off the bootstrap assumption (
B
).

To prove Proposition
5.7
, we first control
v
in
L_{x}^{2}
and
H_{x}^{1}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Fix
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
.
There exists
\nu_{0}(K,\varepsilon_{0})>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0}]
and
t\in[t_{0},0)
,

(5.44)

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(t)\leq\nu^{-\varepsilon_{0}/2+2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\left(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\right)^{-1}.

Proof.

Fix a small exponent
\delta\in(0,1)
to be determined later.
Applying Lemma
5.2
with
\eta=1-\delta
, we find

(5.45)

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})\lesssim_{\delta}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left|t\right|^{1-\delta}\left\|E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\right)\exp\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\right)

for all
\mathfrak{t}\in(t_{0},0)
.
If we integrate Lemma
5.5
in time, we obtain

(5.46)

\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left|t\right|^{1-\delta}\left\|E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2-\delta}.

Similarly, Proposition
5.6
yields

(5.47)

\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\log\left(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\right)^{-1}+\mathcal{O}_{K,\delta}\left(1\right).

Combining (
5.45
)–(
5.47
), we find

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})\leq C_{0}(K,\delta)\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2-\delta}\left(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\right)^{-1}

for some
C_{0}(K,\delta)>0
.
Now choose
\delta=5\varepsilon_{0}/8
.
Then

\left\|v\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x}}\!(\mathfrak{t})\leq\nu^{\varepsilon_{0}/4}C_{0}(K,\delta)\nu^{-\varepsilon_{0}/2+2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\left(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\right)^{-1}.

If we define
\nu_{0}(K,\varepsilon_{0})\coloneqq C_{0}(K,\delta)^{-4/\varepsilon_{0}}
and take
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0}]
, then
\nu^{\varepsilon_{0}/4}C_{0}(K,\delta)\leq 1
and (
5.44
) follows.
∎

To control
\partial_{x}v
in the same manner, we need
a priori
control on
\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}
.
This is the role of the bootstrap hypothesis (
B
).

Lemma 5.9
.

Fix
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 3}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/100)
.
There exists
\nu_{1}(K,\varepsilon)\in(0,1]
such that the following holds for all
\nu\in(0,\nu_{1}]
.
Let
t_{\mathrm{B}}
be as in Proposition
5.7
.
Then for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}})
,

(5.48)

\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\!(t)\leq\nu^{-\varepsilon/2+2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-7}.

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1/200)
to be determined later.
By Lemma
5.2
,

(5.49)

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(\mathfrak{t})\lesssim_{\delta}

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left|t\right|^{1-\delta}\left[\left\|\partial_{x}E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left[3\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}+\nu^{-1}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t\right)

for all
\mathfrak{t}\in(t_{0},0)
.
We control the four terms separately.
Integrating Lemma
5.5
in time, we find

(5.50)

\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left|t\right|^{1-\delta}\left\|\partial_{x}E\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\lesssim_{K}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+9)/2-\delta}.

Next, we take
\varepsilon_{0}=2\delta
in Lemma
5.8
to control
\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}
.
In combination with Proposition
5.6
, we obtain

\left\|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\!(t)\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\lesssim_{K,\delta}\nu^{-\delta}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-6}.

Therefore

(5.51)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left|t\right|^{1-\delta}

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\!(t)\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim_{K,\delta}\nu^{-\delta}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-4-\delta}.

We observe that the right side of (
5.51
) is larger than that of (
5.50
).
It thus appears that
(\partial_{x}E)^{2}
is negligible compared to
(\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\mathrm{app}})^{2}v^{2}
in (
5.12
) and (
5.49
).

Next, we control the terms in the exponential in (
5.49
).
Using Proposition
5.6
, we can check that

(5.52)

\exp\left(3\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left\|\partial_{x}u^{\mathrm{app}}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\right)\lesssim_{K}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-3}.

Finally, the bootstrap hypothesis (
B
) yields

\nu^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\leq\nu^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{\mathrm{B}}}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\lesssim_{K}\nu^{(4K-11)/10-2\varepsilon}

when
\mathfrak{t}\in[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}})
.
Because
K\geq 3
and
\varepsilon<1/100
, we have
(4K-11)/10-2\varepsilon>0
.
Since
\nu\leq 1
,

(5.53)

\exp\left(\nu^{-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{\mathfrak{t}}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}^{2}\!(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\right)\lesssim_{K}1.

Using (
5.50
)–(
5.53
) in (
5.49
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(\mathfrak{t})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{K,\delta}\nu^{-\delta}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-7-\delta}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{K,\delta}\nu^{-3\delta/2}\nu^{2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-7}.

Now let
\delta\coloneqq\varepsilon/6
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{1}(K,\varepsilon)>0
such that

(5.54)

\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\!(\mathfrak{t})\leq\nu^{\varepsilon/4}C_{1}(K,\varepsilon)\nu^{-\varepsilon/2+2(K+1)}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/2}\big(\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-7}.

We therefore need
\nu\leq C_{1}(K,\varepsilon)^{-4/\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, because we applied Lemma
5.8
with
\varepsilon_{0}=2\delta
, we need
\nu\leq\nu_{0}(K,\varepsilon/3)
.
Therefore let

\nu_{1}(K,\varepsilon)\coloneqq\min\left\{C_{1}(K,\varepsilon)^{-4/\varepsilon},\,\nu_{0}(K,\varepsilon/3),\,1\right\}.

Then for all
\nu\in(0,\nu_{1}]
and
\mathfrak{t}\in(t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}})
, (
5.54
) implies (
5.48
).
∎

Now, the self-improvement of the bootstrap follows from interpolation.

Proof of Proposition
5.7
.

Fix
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 3}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/100)
.
Assume there exists
{t_{\mathrm{B}}\in(t_{0},0)}
such that the solution
v
of (
5.10
) satisfies (
B
) on
[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}})
.

By Morrey’s inequality,
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
for all
s>1/2
.
Thus if
s\in(1/2,1)
, we have

(5.55)

\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim_{s}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{1-s}\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{s}+\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}.

We take
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon
in Lemmas
5.8
and
5.9
:

(5.56)

\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{1-s}\!(t)\left\|\partial_{x}v\right\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{s}\!(t)\lesssim_{K,\varepsilon}\nu^{-\varepsilon/4+K+1}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/4}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-1/2-3s}

for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{B}})
.
Take
s=1/2+\varepsilon/12
.
Then we have

(5.57)

\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-1/2-3s}\leq\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-2-\varepsilon/4}\leq\nu^{-\varepsilon/8}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-2}.

Combining (
5.55
)–(
5.57
), we find

(5.58)

\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\!(t)\leq\nu^{\varepsilon/8}C(K,\varepsilon)\nu^{-\varepsilon/2+K+1}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{2/5}\big)^{-(8K+3)/4}\big(\left|t\right|\vee\nu^{1/2}\big)^{-2}

for some
C(K,\varepsilon)>0
.
Define

\bar{\nu}(K,\varepsilon)=\min\left\{C(K,\varepsilon)^{-8/\varepsilon},\,\nu_{0}(K,\varepsilon),\,\nu_{1}(K,\varepsilon)\right\}.

Then if
\nu\in(0,\bar{\nu}]
and

, (

) implies (
5.43
).
∎

We are nearly in a position to prove our main theorem.
The last ingredient is time continuity in
L^{\infty}
:

Lemma 5.10
.

The norm
\left\|v\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}
is continuous in time.

This follows from standard parabolic theory; we omit the proof.

We can now verify the bootstrap assumption (
B
) and prove Theorem
5.1
.

.

Fix

.
We wish to control

.
However, the estimate (

We therefore work with a more accurate approximation

for some

As we shall see,

Fix

Appendix A
The leading inner term

In this appendix, we examine the structure of the leading part of the inner expansion, namely
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
.
We show that
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
admits an explicit representation via the Cole–Hopf transformation.
Moreover, the behavior of
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
as
T\to\infty
hints at a viscous shock emerging from the inner expansion when
t>0
.
Thus, these calculations shed light on the structure of
u^{\nu}

after
the shock forms.

Recall the inner coordinates
(T,X)
defined in (
2.18
).
In the proof of Proposition
4.2
, we construct
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
as the limit of solutions
U^{(n)}
to the problem

(A.1)

\partial_{T}U^{(n)}=\partial_{X}^{2}U^{(n)}-U^{(n)}\partial_{X}U^{(n)},\quad U^{(n)}(-n,\,\cdot\,)=\mathfrak{U}(-n,\,\cdot\,).

We recall that
\mathfrak{U}
denotes the rescaled cubic
\mathfrak{u}
in the inner coordinates; see (
4.1
).
For convenience, we state this convergence as a lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

As
n\to\infty
,
U^{(n)}\to U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
uniformly in
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

In the proof of Proposition
4.2
, we establish locally uniform convergence along a subsequence.
However, Proposition
4.2
also states that
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
is unique, so in fact the entire sequence converges.
Moreover, the sub- and supersolutions used in the proof of Proposition
4.2
imply that

\big|U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}-U^{(n)}\big|\lesssim\mathfrak{D}^{-3},

where
\mathfrak{D}
is the rescaled distance defined by (
4.2
).
Because
\mathfrak{D}^{-3}\to 0
at infinity in
\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}
, we can upgrade locally uniform convergence to uniform.
∎

We observe that
U^{(n)}
satisfies viscous Burgers (
A.1
) with unit viscosity.
We can explicitly solve this equation via the Cole–Hopf transformation.
Thus far, we have avoided Cole–Hopf so that our methods generalize to other scalar conservation laws (
1.3
).
However, there is no harm in deploying it here, because the leading part of the inner expansion is canonically viscous Burgers.
That is, every strictly convex conservation law becomes viscous Burgers to leading order in the inner expansion.

To see this, let
(w^{\nu})_{\nu\geq 0}
solve the viscous scalar conservation law (
1.3
) with strictly convex flux
f
.
We first make a Galilean transformation to fix the inviscid shock formation at the origin with
w^{0}(0,0)=0
.
Thanks to this transformation, we can assume that
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
.
The inner expansion reflects the local structure of (
1.3
) near the origin, where
\left|w^{\nu}\right|\ll 1
.
Thus (
1.3
) becomes

\partial_{t}w^{\nu}\approx\nu\partial_{x}^{2}w^{\nu}-f^{\prime\prime}(0)w^{\nu}\partial_{x}w^{\nu}

near the origin.
Since
f^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
, this is a reparameterization of viscous Burgers.
We note, however, that higher derivatives of
f
lead to new terms in the PDE for
U_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
for
\ell\geq 1
.

Using Cole–Hopf, we derive an explicit integral representation of
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
.

Proposition A.2
.

For all
(T,X)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(A.2)

U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}(T,X)=-\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}X\zeta+\frac{1}{4}T\zeta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\zeta^{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}X\zeta+\frac{1}{4}T\zeta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\zeta^{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta}\,.

Here,
\beta_{3}>0
is the constant in the cubic equation (
2.6
) for
\mathfrak{u}
.

Proof.

We write
\mathfrak{U}_{n}(X)\coloneqq\mathfrak{U}(-n,X)
and define its renormalized integral

(A.3)

\mathfrak{H}_{n}(X)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{X}\mathfrak{U}_{n}(Z)\;\mathrm{d}Z+\log n.

Let
H^{(n)}
solve the following viscous Hamilton–Jacobi equation on
(-n,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
:

(A.4)

\partial_{T}H^{(n)}=\partial_{X}^{2}H^{(n)}-\frac{1}{2}\big(\partial_{X}H^{(n)}\big)^{2},\quad H^{(n)}(-n,\,\cdot\,)=\mathfrak{H}_{n}.

Then we can differentiate (
A.4
) to check that
\partial_{X}H^{(n)}=U^{(n)}
.
Note that the renormalization constant
\log n
in (
A.3
) disappears under differentiation.
This is a form of gauge invariance that we use to ensure that
H^{(n)}
converges as
n\to\infty
.

We now deploy the Cole–Hopf transform.
Define

\phi^{(n)}(T,X)\coloneqq\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}H^{(n)}(T,X)\right].

Then
\phi^{(n)}
solves the linear heat equation on
(-n,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
:

\partial_{T}\phi^{(n)}=\partial_{X}^{2}\phi^{(n)},\quad\phi^{(n)}(-n,\,\cdot\,)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathfrak{H}_{n}/2}.

We can thus express
\phi^{(n)}
via the heat kernel:

\phi^{(n)}(T,X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left[-\frac{(X-Z)^{2}}{4(T+n)}-\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{H}_{n}(Z)\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}Z}{\sqrt{4\pi(T+n)}}.

Fix
(T,X)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and define

\tau\coloneqq\frac{T}{n},\quad\eta\coloneqq\frac{X}{n},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\zeta\coloneqq\frac{Z}{n},

so that
\tau,\eta=\mathcal{O}_{T,X}\big(n^{-1}\big)
.
The scaling properties of the inverse cubic (
2.6
) imply that

\mathfrak{H}_{n}(Z)=n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)+\log n,

so we have

(A.5)

\phi^{(n)}(T,X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left[-n\frac{(\eta-\zeta)^{2}}{4(1+\tau)}-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{\sqrt{4\pi(1+\tau)}}.

Here, we have used the renormalization to produce an additional factor of
n^{-1/2}
.

We now expand
\mathfrak{H}_{1}
around
0
by doing the same for its derivative
\mathfrak{U}_{1}
.
Applying the chain rule and Taylor’s theorem to (
2.6
), we find

\mathfrak{U}_{1}(X)=-X+\beta_{3}X^{3}+\mathcal{O}\big(\left|X\right|^{5}\big).

Integrating, this becomes

(A.6)

\mathfrak{H}_{1}(X)=-\frac{1}{2}X^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{4}X^{4}+\mathcal{O}\big(X^{6}\big).

In particular, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(A.7)

\mathfrak{H}_{1}(X)\geq-\frac{1}{2}X^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}X^{4}\quad\text{on }[-\delta,\delta].

Moreover, (
2.6
) implies that
\left|\mathfrak{U}_{1}(X)\right|<\left|X\right|
when
X\neq 0
.
Since
\left|\mathfrak{U}_{1}(X)\right|=\mathchoice{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}{\scalebox{.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}{\scalebox{.6}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(\left|X\right|)
as
\left|X\right|\to\infty
, we have

\sup_{[-\delta/2,\,\delta/2]^{c}}\frac{\left|\mathfrak{U}_{1}(X)\right|}{\left|X\right|}<1.

Integrating from
0
, there exists
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that

(A.8)

\left|\mathfrak{H}_{1}(X)\right|\leq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}X^{2}\quad\text{on }[-\delta,\delta]^{c}.

For convenience, let

(A.9)

\mathcal{E}(\zeta;n,T,X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi(1+\tau)}}\exp\left[-n\frac{(\eta-\zeta)^{2}}{4(1+\tau)}-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)\right]

denote the integrand of (
A.5
), recalling that
(\tau,\eta)=(T/n,X/n)
.
Also, define

I_{1}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}(\zeta;n,T,X)\textup{{1}}_{\{\left|\zeta\right|\leq\delta n^{1/2}\}}\;\mathrm{d}\zeta\quad\textrm{and}\quad I_{2}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}(\zeta;n,T,X)\textup{{1}}_{\{\left|\zeta\right|>\delta n^{1/2}\}}\;\mathrm{d}\zeta,

so that (
A.5
) becomes
\phi^{(n)}=I_{1}+I_{2}
.

We first consider the main term
I_{1}
.
Recall that
\eta
and
\tau
are small, so the first term in the exponential in (
A.9
) becomes

(A.10)

-n\frac{(\eta-\zeta)^{2}}{4(1+\tau)}=-\frac{n}{4}\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{2}X\zeta+\frac{1}{4}T\zeta^{2}+\mathcal{O}_{T,X}\big(n^{-1}(\zeta^{2}+1)\big).

By (
A.7
),

\frac{1}{2}n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)\geq-\frac{n}{4}\zeta^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{16}\zeta^{4}

when
\left|\zeta\right|\leq\delta n^{1/2}
.
Therefore

-n\frac{(\eta-\zeta)^{2}}{4(1+\tau)}-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)\leq C-\frac{\beta_{3}}{32}\zeta^{4}

for some constant
C(T,X)>0
independent of
\zeta
and
n
that we allow to change from line to line.
That is,

(A.11)

\mathcal{E}(\zeta;n,T,X)\textup{{1}}_{\{\left|\zeta\right|\leq\delta n^{1/2}\}}\lesssim_{T,X}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{3}\zeta^{4}/32}.

We use this as a majorant for dominated convergence.
We can write (
A.6
) as

\frac{1}{2}n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)=-\frac{n}{4}\zeta^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\zeta^{4}+\mathcal{O}_{\zeta}\big(n^{-1}\big).

By (
A.10
), the leading term of the Gaussian kernel cancels that of the initial data and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(\zeta;n,T,X)=(4\pi)^{-1/2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}X\zeta+\frac{1}{4}T\zeta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\zeta^{4}\right).

In light of (
A.11
), dominated convergence implies that

(A.12)

\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}X\zeta+\frac{1}{4}T\zeta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\zeta^{4}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{\sqrt{4\pi}}.

Next, we control the remainder
I_{2}
.
By (
A.8
),

-\frac{1}{2}n^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{1}\big(n^{-1/2}\zeta\big)\leq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{4}n\zeta^{2}

when
\left|\zeta\right|>\delta n^{1/2}
.
On the other hand, (
A.10
) implies

n\frac{(\eta-\zeta)^{2}}{4(1+\tau)}\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon/2}{4}n\zeta^{2}-Cn^{-1}

once
n\geq C
for
C(T,X)
sufficiently large.
Therefore

\mathcal{E}(\zeta;n,T,X)\lesssim_{T,X}\exp\left[-\frac{\varepsilon}{8}n\zeta^{2}\right]

when
\left|\zeta\right|>\delta n^{1/2}
and
n\geq C
.
It follows that

(A.13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{2}=0.

Combining (
A.12
) and (
A.13
), we find

(A.14)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi^{(n)}(T,X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}X\zeta+\frac{1}{4}T\zeta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\zeta^{4}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\eqqcolon\phi(T,X)

pointwise in
(T,X)
.
It is straightforward to check that this convergence is locally uniform in
(T,X)
.
By parabolic regularity, we also have
\partial_{X}\phi^{(n)}\to\partial_{X}\phi
locally uniformly as
n\to\infty
.
Inverting Cole–Hopf, Lemma
A.1
yields

U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}U^{(n)}=-2\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\partial_{X}\phi^{(n)}}{\phi^{(n)}}=-\frac{2\partial_{X}\phi}{\phi}.

This is (
A.2
).
∎

We now briefly consider times after the shock forms.
Given
t>0
, let
s(t)
denote the position of the shock so long as it remains isolated.
Once the shock has positive strength, Goodman and Xin
[
24
]
show that the viscous effects are confined to the narrow strip
\left|x-s(t)\right|\lesssim\nu
.
There are thus three relevant coordinate systems in the problem: the original system
(u,t,x)
, the inner variables
(U,T,X)
, and the “shock variables”
(u,t,\xi)
for
\xi\coloneqq(x-s)/\nu
.
We include the solution values
u
and
U
as “coordinates” to track their relative scaling.
These coordinates are linked through the following relations:

(A.15)

(U,T,X)=\big(\nu^{-1/4}u,\nu^{-1/2}t,\nu^{-3/4}x\big)\quad\textrm{and}\quad(u,t,\xi)=\big(u,t,\nu^{-1}(x-s)\big).

Here, we consider the manner in which our inner expansion about
(t,x)=(0,0)
gives way to the shock expansion about
x=s(t)
as
t
increases.
Using the Hopf–Lax formula
[
27
,
33
]
, one can verify that
\left|s(t)\right|\lesssim t^{2}
and
\left|\dot{s}(t)\right|\lesssim t
.
When
0<t\ll 1
, this is sufficiently small that we can safely approximate
s(t)
by
0
.
If we let

h(t)\coloneqq\frac{u^{0}(t,s(t)-)-u^{0}(t,s(t)+)}{2}

denote (half) the shock strength, we can check that

(A.16)

h(t)\sim\sqrt{\frac{t}{\beta_{3}}}\quad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}.

Indeed, to leading order,
u^{0}
should still resemble a solution of the cubic equation
tu^{0}-\beta_{3}(u^{0})^{3}=x
.
The height (
A.16
) is the positive root at
x=0
.
Thus when
t\ll 1
,
u^{0}
has a weak, nearly stationary shock.

Near the shock, the viscous solution
u^{\nu}
should resemble a “viscous shock profile”
\varphi(t,\xi)
solving

\partial_{\xi}^{2}\varphi-\varphi\partial_{\xi}\varphi=0,\quad\varphi(t,-\infty)=u^{0}(t,s(t)-),\kern 5.0pt\varphi(t,+\infty)=u^{0}(t,s(t)+).

At fixed
t
, all such profiles are reparameterizations of

(A.17)

\sigma(\xi)\coloneqq-\tanh\frac{\xi}{2}.

The stationary shock of strength
h
is
h\sigma(h\xi)
.
By (
A.16
), we therefore expect

u^{\nu}(t,\nu\xi)\sim\varphi(t,\xi)\sim\sqrt{\frac{t}{\beta_{3}}}\sigma\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{\beta_{3}}}\xi\right)

when
\nu\ll 1
,
t\ll 1
, and
\left|\xi\right|\lesssim 1
.
On the other hand, if our inner expansion remains valid at small positive times, we should have

u^{\nu}(t,x)\sim u_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}(t,x)=\nu^{1/4}U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}\left(\nu^{-1/2}t,\nu^{-3/4}x\right)

for
\nu\ll 1
,
t\ll 1
,
\left|x\right|\ll 1
.
If these asymptotics for
u^{\nu}
are simultaneously valid, there must be a relationship between the inner solution
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}
and the viscous shock profile
\sigma
.
Changing to inner coordinates via (
A.15
), we expect the following:

Proposition A.3
.

For all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}\big(T,T^{-1/2}\xi\big)=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\beta_{3}}}\sigma\left(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\beta_{3}}}\right)\left[1+\mathcal{O}_{\xi}\big(T^{-2}\big)\right]\quad\text{as }T\to\infty.

Proof.

Fix
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and consider
T>0
.
By Proposition
A.2
,
U_{0}^{\mathrm{in}}=-\frac{2\partial_{X}\phi}{\phi}
with
\phi
given in (
A.14
).
Using (
A.14
), we can write

\phi\big(T,T^{-1/2}\xi\big)=\sqrt{\frac{T}{4\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\xi\theta/2}\exp\left[T^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\theta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\theta^{4}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}\theta

for
\theta\coloneqq T^{-1/2}\zeta
.
We view this as the Laplace transform of an unnormalized measure that is growing ever more concentrated around the maxima of

\Lambda(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\theta^{2}-\frac{\beta_{3}}{8}\theta^{4}.

The function
\Lambda
is simultaneously maximized at
\theta=\pm\theta_{*}
for
\theta_{*}\coloneqq\beta_{3}^{-1/2}
.
Moreover,

(A.18)

\Lambda(\theta_{*})=\frac{1}{8\beta_{3}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{*})=-1.

Using Laplace’s method, we obtain

\phi\big(T,T^{-1/2}\xi\big)=\sqrt{\frac{T}{4\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{T^{2}\left|\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{*})\right|}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\xi\theta_{*}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\xi\theta_{*}/2}\right)\exp\left[T^{2}\Lambda(\theta_{*})\right]\left[1+\mathcal{O}_{\xi}\big(T^{-2}\big)\right]

as
T\to\infty
.
Simplifying, (
A.18
) yields

(A.19)
