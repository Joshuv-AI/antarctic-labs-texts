Limitations of Stochastic Selection with Pairwise Independent Priors
Shaddin Dughmi, Yusuf Hakan Kalayci, Neel Patel

Abstract
Motivated by the growing interest in correlation-robust stochastic optimization, we investigate stochastic selection problems beyond independence. Specifically, we consider the instructive case of pairwise-independent priors and matroid constraints. We obtain essentially-optimal bounds for contention resolution and prophet inequalities. The impetus for our work comes from the recent work of Caragiannis et al. 2022 , who derived a constant-approximation for the single-choice prophet inequality with pairwise-independent priors. For general matroids, our results are tight and largely negative. For both contention resolution and prophet inequalities, our impossibility results hold for the full linear matroid over a finite field. We explicitly construct pairwise-independent distributions which rule out an ω ⁡ ( 1 Rank ) \omega\left(\frac{1}{\textbf{Rank}}\right) -balanced offline CRS and an ω ⁡ ( 1 log ⁡ Rank ) \omega\left(\frac{1}{\log\textbf{Rank}}\right) -competitive prophet inequality against the (usual) oblivious adversary. For both results, we employ a generic approach for constructing pairwise-independent random vectors — one which unifies and generalizes existing pairwise-independence constructions from the literature on universal hash functions and pseudorandomness. Specifically, our approach is based on our observation that random linear maps turn linear independence into stochastic independence. We then examine the class of matroids which satisfy the so-called partition property — these include most common matroids encountered in optimization. We obtain positive results for both online contention resolution and prophet inequalities with pairwise-independent priors on such matroids, approximately matching the corresponding guarantees for fully independent priors. These algorithmic results hold against the almighty adversary for both problems.

This finding encourages further exploration of stochastic selection problems under the same pairwise independent assumption. Later, we examine the class of matroids that satisfy the partition property — these include the most common matroids encountered in optimization. Informally, this property holds if a matroid can be approximated by a (random) partition matroid. We note the concurrent independent work of

Finally, we mention that our results deepen the existing schism between matroids that admit the partition property and those that do not, and in doing so might shed light on the matroid secretary conjecture. Much of the interest in the partition property is due to the fact — pointed out in a survey by

Second, by constructing provably “hard instances” for selection problems that are easy in the presence of the partition property, our techniques might shed light on the analogous question for the matroid secretary problem. In fact, showing that our construction for prophet inequalities remains hard in the random order model would disprove the matroid secretary conjecture. On the flip side, providing an algorithm for our construction in the random order model appears highly nontrivial, and therefore might stimulate the development of algorithmic techniques pertinent to the conjecture.

2.3
Contention Resolution

Contention Resolution
is the algorithmic task of converting a random set which is feasible “on average” to on to one which is always feasible. Contention resolution in the offline setting was originally formalized by
Chekuri et al. 2011b
for application to approximation algorithms. It has since been generalized, studied, and applied in various online models (see
[
30
,
2
,
42
]
).
An algorithm for contention resolution is referred to as a
Contention Resolution Scheme (CRS)
.

Fix a downward-closed set-system
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
over a ground set
E
of elements, as well as a
convex relaxation

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})\subseteq[0,1]^{E}
of the indicator vectors of sets in
\mathcal{I}
. Let
\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
be a vector of marginal probabilities, and let
A\subseteq E
be a set of
active
elements drawn from a known distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta(2^{E})
satisfying
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{A\sim\mathcal{D}}[e\in A]=\mu(e)
for all
e\in E
. Given these inputs, the goal of a CRS is to
select
(a.k.a.
accept
) a feasible subset of the active elements — i.e., a set
I\in\mathcal{I}
such that
I\subseteq A
.

A CRS is judged by its balance ratio: we say that a contention resolution scheme is
c
-balanced if for all
e\in E
,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in I\mid e\in A]\geq c
. Many natural classes of combinatorial constraints, including matroids, matchings, and knapsacks, admit
\Omega(1)
-balanced contention resolution schemes when the events
\{e\in A\}_{e\in E}
are jointly independent, and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
is the usual relaxation of the problem.

In the
offline
model of contention resolution, all inputs — in particular the set
A
of active elements — are given upfront. This is the most permissive model considered for contention resolution, and serves as the setting of our impossibility results. Our algorithmic results hold for the more restrictive
online
setting where elements are presented to the algorithm, which we refer to as an
online CRS (OCRS)
, in some order determined by an adversary. When
e\in E
arrives online, it is then revealed whether
e
is active (i.e., whether
e\in A
), at which point the algorithm must irrevocably decide whether to select
e
subject to feasibility. Several adversary models have been considered for online contention resolution, and our algorithmic results hold even for the most restrictive of those: the
almighty adversary
who determines the order of elements with full knowledge of all inputs including the realization of
A
, as well as the realization of any internal randomness used by the algorithm.

In this work, we focus on contention resolution for matroids when
\{e\in A\}_{e\in E}
are only pairwise independent, and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
is simply the matroid polytope. In particular,
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is a matroid and
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta_{\text{pw}}(2^{E})(\mathbf{x})
for some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
. When a CRS is
c
-balanced for all such
\mathcal{D}
, we say it is a

2.4
Prophet Inequalities

Fix a downwards-closed set system
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
. In a
prophet inequality problem
, there are
weights
(or
values
)
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
on the elements that are drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and elements arrive online in some order determined by an adversary.
We take the perspective of a
gambler
who a-priori knows
\mathcal{M}
and the distribution
\mathcal{D}
of weights, but not the realized weights
\mathbf{w}
. When an element
e
arrives online, the gambler learns
\mathbf{w}(e)
and must irrevocably decide whether or not to accept
e
, subject to accepting a feasible set of of elements
S\in\mathcal{I}
. The gambler seeks to maximize their utility
\mathbf{w}(S)=\sum_{e\in S}\mathbf{w}(e)
. The goal is to compete — in expectation — with an omniscient
prophet
who obtains the maximum possible utility
\mathop{max}\{\mathbf{w}(T):T\in\mathcal{I}\}
. When the gambler’s expected utility is an
\alpha
fraction of the prophet’s expected utility, we say that we have an
\alpha
-competitive prophet inequality for
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{D}
.

Our negative results in this paper hold even against the weakest of adversaries considered in the literature on prophet inequality problems: the
oblivious adversary
who determines the order of elements in advance as a function of only
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{D}
; the gambler, therefore, knows the (arbitrary) order at the outset. In contrast, our positive results hold even for the strongest adversary considered in the literature: the
almighty adversary
who determines the order of elements with full knowledge of all inputs, including the realized weights
\mathbf{w}
as well as any internal randomness of the gambler’s algorithm. The gambler therefore only learns the order as elements arrive online.

In this paper, we focus on prophet inequality problems where
\mathcal{M}
is a matroid and
\mathcal{D}
is a pairwise independent distribution over weight vectors , i.e.
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta_{\text{pw}}\left(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|E|}\right)
.

(Lemma 1 from
[

]

where
\mathcal{E}\vee\mathcal{F}

3
Overview of Technical Results

In this section, we present an overview of our techniques and results. First we outline our construction of pairwise stochastically independent vector families. We then show how to employ this construction to prove our main impossibility results for pairwise-independent offline matroid contention resolution, and pairwise-independent matroid prophet inequalities against the oblivious adversary. We show that our impossibility results are tight by providing algorithms with matching bounds for both problems, even against the almighty adversary. Finally, we examine matroids satisfying the partition property, which includes most common matroids encountered in combinatorial optimization. We provide constant factor algorithms for pairwise-independent contention resolution and prophet inequalities on such matroids, even against the almighty adversary.

3.1
A Recipe for Pairwise-Independent Vector Families

As our main technical tool, we present a simple and flexible recipe for constructing pairwise independent families of vectors. We instantiate this recipe in different ways for matroid prophet inequalities and contention resolution schemes. Since our construction permits encoding of a rich variety of higher order relationships between the vectors, while maintaining lower-order independence, we hope it might be of independent interest.

There are two versions of our recipe. The first version produces an
ordered
family (i.e., a tuple) of pairwise independent vectors, and the second turns that into an
unordered
family (i.e., a set) of
labeled
vectors wherein membership is pairwise independent. We require the second, unordered, version for our impossibility results. The ordered construction is the most natural, and easily generalizes to
k
-wise independence for arbitrary
k
— we present the more general construction in this paper. The unordered construction is built on its ordered counterpart, and is tailored for pairwise independence (i.e.,
k=2
).

Ordered Families

We begin with our construction of an
ordered

k
-wise independent vectors over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where

is the input matrix we get to “design” for encoding a “hard instance” of the problem at hand, while respecting
k

The key observation here, which we prove in Section

Both of our impossibility results require constructing a set of non-zero vectors

Contention Resolution on Matroids

3.3
Prophet Inequalities on Matroids

Through a somewhat more involved application of our recipe, we rule out a competitive ratio better than
O(\frac{1}{\log d})
for prophet inequalities on binary matroids of rank
d

The Impossibility Result

We begin with the following randomized construction. For arbitrarily large

and some
\kappa=\Omega(\log d)
, we define a (random) nested sequence

of subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}

(i)

has dimension
\frac{d}{2^{\ell-1}}

(ii)

is a linearly independent subset of
V_{\ell}
with size

equal to a constant fraction of its dimension.
6
6

6

In fact, we can guarantee half the dimension, i.e.

.

(iii)

Let
\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq\kappa
. Conditioned on

and

is in

with probability

(iv)

The sets

We show the existence of such random

6

be the standard basis vectors, then for each

be a random half of

be the span of

. We cannot help but speculate whether our construction, which goes to some pains in order to achieve all four properties simultaneously, can be simplified or elegantly reduced to known linear-algebraic facts.

To motivate this construction, consider the following “hard” instance of the prophet inequality problem on the matroid
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
where elements of each

are assigned weight

, and remaining elements are assigned weight

, followed by the zero-weight elements at the end. Forgive for a moment that the weights are not pairwise independent, and that guaranteeing this order is beyond the powers of the oblivious adversary — these are issues we will address later. The maximum weight independent set, which can be constructed by running the greedy algorithm on the non-zero weight elements in decreasing order of weights
S_{\kappa},\dots,S_{1}
, selects a constant fraction of each
S_{\ell}
– this follows easily from the matroid exchange axiom and Property
6
. Therefore, the prophet’s reward is
\Omega(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa}w_{\ell}\cdot n_{\ell})=\Omega(d\log d)

of each

before learning anything about flats

for

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell^{\prime}}|T_{\ell}|\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}}]\leq\frac{d}{2^{\ell^{\prime}-1}}

and multiplying both sides by

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell^{\prime}}w_{\ell}|T_{\ell}|]\leq 2d

It remains to address two issues with this prophet inequality instance: pairwise independence, and the limited powers of the oblivious adversary. For pairwise independence, we use our recipe described in Section
3.1
. We let
\Sigma_{\ell}
be the binary

But what of the oblivious adversary’s power to set the arrival order? Fortunately, now that the weight of

The Algorithm

We show that our bound of
O(1/\log\textbf{Rank})
is essentially tight for general matroids. Specifically, we obtain an
\Omega(1/\log\textbf{Rank})
matroid prophet inequality for pairwise independent distributions, even against the almighty adversary. We necessarily exploit pairwise independence, as no nontrivial guarantee is possible for general correlated distributions even for the rank one matroid.

Our algorithm is based on simple bucketing. Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a matroid with pairwise-independent random weights
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathbb{E}}
, and let
\OPT=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{w}(\mathcal{M})]
denote the expected reward of the prophet. Weights smaller than
\frac{OPT}{2\textbf{Rank}}
contribute at most half the prophet’s reward, so can be discarded. Partition the weights between
\frac{OPT}{2\textbf{Rank}}
and
3\OPT
into
O(\log\textbf{Rank})
“regular” buckets delimited by the integer powers of
2
. Weights larger than
3OPT
are assigned their own “special” bucket. The algorithm simply chooses the bucket with the largest contribution to the prophet’s reward, and greedily selects as many elements as possible from that bucket subject to feasibility.

Clearly, the greedy algorithm applied to any of the regular buckets recovers at least half of that bucket’s contribution to the prophet’s expected reward. The special bucket
B_{\infty}
requires a more careful analysis which exploits pairwise independence of the events
e\in B_{\infty}
. By Markov’s inequality,
B_{\infty}
is non-empty with probability at most
1/3
. A Lemma from
[
19
]
for pairwise-independent events implies that
\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in B_{\infty}]\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[B_{\infty}\neq\emptyset]}{1-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[B_{\infty}\neq\emptyset]}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Invoking pairwise independence again, together with the union bound, an element
e
in
B_{\infty}
is the only element in that bucket with probability at least
1/2
, even after conditioning on its weight
w_{e}
. It follows that the greedy algorithm applied to
B_{\infty}
recovers half of its contribution to the prophet’s reward.

Putting it all together, since our algorithm greedily selects from the bucket contributing most to the prophet’s reward, and there are
O(\log\textbf{Rank})
buckets, we obtain an
\Omega(1/\log\textbf{Rank})
prophet inequality for pairwise-independent distributions on matroids. Since we assumed nothing about the order in which elements are greedily selected, this holds even against the almighty adversary.

3.4
Exploiting the Partition Property

In contrast to our negative results for general matroids, we show that not all is lost for most common matroids in the optimization literature. Such matroids often satisfy the
\alpha
-partition property
for a constant
\alpha
. Informally, this means that the matroid
\mathcal{M}
can be approximated by a randomly-chosen simple partition matroid
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
on the same elements, in the sense that the weighted rank of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
approximates that of
\mathcal{M}
from below up to a factor of
\alpha
for every vector of element weights.

For prophet inequalities, we apply the pairwise-independent single-choice prophet inequality of
[
19
]
to each part of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
separately. This gives a
\frac{1}{3}
prophet inequality for
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, and therefore a
\frac{\alpha}{3}
prophet inequality for
\mathcal{M}
, for pairwise independent distributions. Since the guarantees of
[
19
]
hold even against the almighty adversary, so do ours.

For contention resolution, the situation is slightly more involved. First, we show the existence of a balanced offline CRS for pairwise-independent distributions by utilizing Theorem
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
, then we show how to exploit a duality argument as well as the aforementioned prophet inequality to convert it to an online CRS against the almighty adversary.

Let
A
be a set of elements wherein membership is pairwise-independent with marginals
\mu

\mathcal{M}
. To show the existence of an offline balanced CRS, it suffices by Theorem

to show, for every set of elements

is a constant fraction of its expected size
\mu(F)
. We begin by analyzing the set
\widetilde{A}
where membership is jointly independent with the same marginals
\mu
, and later relate
A
and
\widetilde{A}
through Lemma
2.2
. It follows from
[
23
]
that
\widetilde{A}
admits a
(1-1/e)
-balanced CRS with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, and therefore
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{\mathcal{M}}(\widetilde{A}\cap F)]\geq(1-1/e)\cdot\mu(F)
for every set of elements
F
. Invoking the partition property, we get
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\widetilde{A}\cap F)]\geq\alpha\cdot(1-1/e)\cdot\mu(F)
. We then observe that the rank function of the partition matroid
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
decomposes additively across its parts

\textbf{Rank}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\widetilde{A}\cap F)
precisely when at least one of the elements in

is in
\widetilde{A}
. Lemma
2.2
implies that the probability of a disjunction of pairwise-independent events approximates, up to a factor of
1.299
, the same quantity in the jointly independent case. In particular,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(A\cap F)]\geq\frac{1}{1.299}\cdot\alpha\cdot(1-1/e)\cdot\mu(F)

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is smaller than that of
\mathcal{M}

to conclude the existence of an
\frac{1-1/e}{1.299}\cdot\alpha

with respect to
\mathcal{M}
.

For good measure, we also show how to turn our offline CRS into an online one, even against the almighty adversary, at a cost of an additional
O(\alpha)
factor in the balance ratio. We use a duality-based construction essentially identical to that in
[
25

. At a high level, the problem of maximizing the weighted rank of active elements functions as a dual to contention resolution. An online
\beta

\beta
of the best possible offline. Our prophet inequality is such an approximation with
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{3}

\frac{1-1/e}{3.897}\cdot\alpha^{2}
against the almighty adversary.

We note that our offline CRS for matroids satisfying the partition property, together with the impossibility results for linear and binary matroids in Section

form an ordered family of
k
-wise stochastically independent vectors., i.e., for each
S\subseteq[n]
of size at most
k
the vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in S}
are mutually (stochastically) independent.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
be a matrix with
k
-wise linearly independent columns
\sigma_{1},\dots\sigma_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
,
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times m}
be a uniformly random matrix with entries
r_{i,j}\sim\operatorname{Unif}\left(0,1,\dots,q-1\right)
, and
X=R\Sigma
with column vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{n}
. For any subset
S\subseteq[n]
of size at most
k
, the vectors
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in S}
are mutually stochastically independent. Moreover, for any
i\in[n]
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}]=\frac{1}{q^{d}}
.

Proof.

First we show that each column
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
of
X
is uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. For arbitrary
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, we can express,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[R\mathbf{\sigma}_{i}=\mathbf{v}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{\ell=1}^{d}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}(\ell)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}(\ell)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{disjoint set of independent RVs}).

For any fixed
\ell\in[d]
, the set of possible solutions
(r_{\ell,1},\dots,r_{\ell,m})
to the equation
\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}_{i}(\ell)
, considering
\mathbf{\sigma}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}
as fixed, forms an affine subspace of rank
m-1
which has size of
q^{m-1}
. As each
r_{i,j}
is a uniformly random element from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the probability,

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}]=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}(\ell)\right]=\left(\frac{q^{m-1}}{q^{m}}\right)^{d}=\frac{1}{q^{d}}.

(1)

Next, we prove
k
-wise stochastic independence of the columns of
X
. Let
S\subseteq[n]
be such that
|S|\leq k
. By assumption, the corresponding columns
\left\{\sigma_{i}\right\}_{i\in S}
of
\Sigma
are linearly independent. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\left\{R\mathbf{\sigma}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\bigwedge_{\ell=1}^{d}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}_{i}(\ell)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{\ell=1}^{d}\bigwedge_{i\in S}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}_{i}(\ell)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}_{i}(\ell)\right\}\right].

\displaystyle(\text{disjoint set of independent RVs})

Consider the following system of equations with variables
(r_{\ell,1},\dots r_{\ell,m})
for a fixed
\ell\in[d]
:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}_{i}(\ell)\qquad\forall i\in S.

(2)

Since
\{\sigma_{i}\mid i\in S\}
are linearly independent, the set of solutions
(r_{\ell,1},\dots r_{\ell,m})
to the system of equations (
2
) forms an affine subspace of dimension
m-|S|
containing
q^{m-|S|}
many vectors. As each
r_{i,j}
is a uniformly random element from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the probability,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{\ell,j}\cdot\sigma_{i}(j)=\mathbf{v}_{i}(\ell)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{q^{m-|S|}}{q^{m}}\right)^{d}

\displaystyle=q^{-d\cdot|S|}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in S}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}].

Above, the second equality follows from the fact that
r_{i,j}
is a uniformly random element from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and the last equality follows from Equation (
1
). This proves that the random vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in S}
are mutually independent, as needed.
∎

Next, we present a well-known fact about random matrices defined over finite fields. For the self containment of the paper, we provide its simple proof in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times m}
be a uniformly random matrix with entries
r_{i,j}\sim\operatorname{Unif}\left(0,1,\dots,q-1\right)
. Then, for any
m<d
, we have
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\textbf{Rank}(R)=m]\geq 1-\frac{1}{q^{d-m}}.
In addition, for any
\Sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
with distinct columns, the columns of
X=R\Sigma
are distinct with probability
\geq 1-\frac{1}{q^{d-m}}
.

The last lemma essentially states that when
d>>m
, the random matrix
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times m}
becomes full rank. Therefore, the
random linear map
under
R
becomes a random linear “embedding” (or an injective mapping) with high probability. In such a scenario, when a matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
consists of distinct columns, the product
X=R\Sigma
will have distinct columns with high probability.

4.2
Unordered Pairwise Independent Vector Families

In the context of stochastic selection, we typically focus on probability distributions over sets of elements. This requires us to construct an (unordered) set of vectors
A
such that for any two distinct vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
, the events
\left\{\mathbf{u}\in A\right\}
and
\left\{\mathbf{v}\in A\right\}
are independent.

In the previous section, we introduced a technique for producing a sequence
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
of vectors that exhibit pairwise (indeed
k
-wise) stochastic independence.
To turn this into a set of vectors
A
, it is tempting to define
A=\left\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}
. However, some thought reveals that membership in this set
A
can exhibit some slight negative correlation. Even in the (high probability) event that the vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
are distinct, for distinct
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
we have
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{u}\in A]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{v}\in A]=\frac{n}{q^{d}}
, and yet
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{u}\in A\land\mathbf{v}\in A]=\frac{n(n-1)}{q^{2d}}<\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{u}\in A]\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{v}\in A]
. It might be tempting to “correct” for this negative correlation in
A
by slightly mixing with a different set
A^{\prime}
featuring positive correlation that is calibrated to “cancel out” the negative correlation in
A
. However, this is further complicated by the (low probability) event where the random linear map used in our construction is singular, and the vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
are therefore not necessarily distinct. This makes it challenging to quantify the exact “amount” of correlation in
A
in general, and therefore challenging to argue for the existence of a suitable
A^{\prime}
. To circumvent these difficulties, we take a somewhat different approach which creates
n
labeled duplicates for each vector. We describe this approach next.

Recall the construction of ordered families from the previous section: The matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
is comprised of pairwise linearly independent column vectors
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
, the matrix
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times m}
has its entries
r_{i,j}
drawn i.i.d. uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and the matrix
X=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]=R\cdot\Sigma
is the image of
\Sigma
under the linear transformation defined by matrix
R
. We create
n
labeled copies
\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{n}}
of each vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, and include
\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in A
whenever the
i
-th column of
X
equals
\mathbf{v}
, i.e.
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}
. This allows the inclusion of each vector
\mathbf{v}
in the set
A
multiple times, each occurrence distinguished by a unique
label
from a set of labels
L
.
A
is therefore a subset of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
. Without loss of generality, one can think of
L=[n]
.

Procedure 1.
Random Set Generation from Random Matrix (MatrixToSet)

Input:

X\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times n}
with pairwise independent columns
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{n}
and an ordered label set
L=\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}\}
,

Output:

A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
.

Dist - I :

\mathcal{D}_{1}

(1)

Construct an unordered set
A:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\ell_{i}}:i\in[n]\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
.

Dist II :
\mathcal{D}_{2}

(1)

Start with
A=\emptyset
. Then, for each label
\ell_{i}\in[n]
, independently with probability
\frac{1}{q^{d}}
, include all elements
\{\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}:\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\}
in
A
.

Sample

A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
w.p
1-\frac{1}{q^{d}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
w.p.
\frac{1}{q^{d}}
.

Let us proceed to understand correlation structure within this set
A
. First, we observe that for distinct indices
i,j\in[n]
and any two (possibly equal) vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, the events
\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in A\right\}
and
\left\{\mathbf{u}^{\ell_{j}}\in A\right\}
are independent as events
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{v}\right\}
and
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{u}\right\}
are assured to be independent by Lemma
4.1
. Nonetheless, this method introduces correlation between the events
\{\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in A\}
and
\{\mathbf{u}^{\ell_{i}}\in A\}
since
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{u}^{\ell_{i}}\in A\wedge\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in A]=0
for any two distinct vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and index
i\in[n]
(or label
\ell_{i}\in L
).
To overcome this, we mix
A
with another distribution that positively correlates the inclusion of labeled vectors with the same label without introducing dependencies across different labels, nor changing the marginals. We describe our procedure of converting ordered families of vectors to unordered families of vectors in Procedure
4.2
.

In the subsequent lemma, we demonstrate that the unordered family of vectors sampled according to Procedure
4.2
is pairwise independent provided the ordered family of vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
are pairwise independent and uniformly sampled from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. To maintain a smooth and uninterrupted discussion, we have postponed the detailed technical proof to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times n}
be a random matrix with pairwise independent column vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{n}
for some
n<q^{d}
where each vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is distributed uniformly in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. Then a random set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
generated by Procedure
4.2
given the inputs of
X
and
L=\left\{\ell_{1},\dots\ell_{n}\right\}
satisfies:

(1)

For any
\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in A]=\frac{1}{q^{d}}
.

(2)

For any two distinct
\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}},\mathbf{u}^{\ell_{j}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times L
, the events
\{\mathbf{v}^{\ell_{i}}\in A\}
and
\{\mathbf{u}^{\ell_{j}}\in A\}
are independent.

5
Pairwise-independent Contention Resolution on Matroids

In this section, we utilize the tool devised in the previous section to show the limits of pairwise-independent contention resolution schemes for matroids. In particular, we show that the class of linear matroids does not admit an
\omega(1/\textbf{Rank})
-balanced pairwise independent offline CRS. We complement this with a pairwise independent online CRS against the almighty adversary whose balance ratio matches our impossibility result for offline schemes up to a constant factor.

5.1
Limits of Pairwise Independent Contention Resolution

We consider the matroid
\mathcal{M}^{\times d}=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d],\mathcal{I}^{\times d})
which consists of
d
labeled copies of each element in the full linear matroid
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
.
The following theorem states the limits of pairwise independent offline contention resolution for this matroid.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
d>2
and any prime
q
, the matroid
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
does not admit a
\frac{c+1}{d}
-balanced pairwise independent offline CRS where
c
is any positive integer satisfying
q^{c-1}\geq d
.
In particular,

1.

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
does not admit a
\frac{3}{d}
-balanced pairwise independent offline CRS for
q\geq d
.

2.

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}\times[d]
does not admit a
\frac{3+\log_{2}d}{d}
-balanced pairwise independent offline CRS.

Our construction of a hard offline contention resolution instance utilizes the tool devised in Section
4
to sample pairwise independent unordered family of vectors. This unordered family will serve as the set of active elements for contention resolution. We first form a “fat” matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{c\times d}
(
c<<d
) with pairwise linearly independent columns. The existence of such a matrix can be assured by choosing
q
and
c
so that
d\leq q^{c-1}
as we show in Claim
5.2
.
Then, linear transformation of
\Sigma
via a uniformly random matrix
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times c}
yields a matrix
X=R\Sigma
whose columns
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{d}
are pairwise stochastically independent due to Lemma
4.1
.
Finally, we use Procedure
4.2
to turn the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{d})
into the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
of active elements. Lemma
4.3
ensures that for any two distinct elements
\mathbf{v}^{i},\mathbf{u}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
, the events
\{\mathbf{v}^{i}\in A\}
and
\{\mathbf{u}^{j}\in A\}
are pairwise independent. We summarize this construction in Procedure
5.1
.

Procedure 2.
Pairwise Independent Set of Active Elements in
\mathcal{M}^{\times d}=(\mathbb{F}^{d}_{q}\times[d],\mathcal{I}^{\times d})

Input:
Positive integers
d,c
and a prime
q
satisfying
q^{c-1}\geq d
.

Output:

A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
.

(1)

Let
\Sigma^{\text{CRS}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{c\times d}
be an arbitrary matrix with pairwise linearly independent column vectors.

(2)

Let
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times c}
be a matrix where each entry
r_{ij}
is uniformly and independently sampled from
\left\{0,\dots q-1\right\}
.

(3)

Set
A\leftarrow\operatorname{MatrixToSet}(R\cdot\Sigma^{\text{CRS}},[d])
.

The following claim together with Lemma
4.3
implies that the output of Procedure
5.1
follows a pairwise independent distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
.

Claim 5.2
.

Given that
d\leq q^{c-1}
, there exists a matrix
\Sigma^{\text{CRS}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{c\times d}
which consists of
d
pairwise linearly independent columns.

Proof.

Notice that there are
q^{c}-1
non-zero vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{c}
, and linear dependence partitions them into equivalence classes of size
q-1
. Therefore, by selecting one vector from each equivalence class one can generate
\frac{q^{c}-1}{q-1}>q^{c-1}
pairwise linearly independent columns. When
d\leq q^{c-1}
, the matrix
\Sigma^{\text{CRS}}
always exists.
∎

Before we show that
A
, the output of Procedure
5.1
, does not admit a
\frac{c+1}{d}
-balanced contention resolution scheme, we first verify that the marginals of
A
reside within the matroid polytope
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}^{\times d})
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]}
be the marginal probability vector of
A
where
\mu(\mathbf{v}^{i})=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{v}^{i}\in A]
when
A
is sampled according to Procedure
5.1
. Then,

\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}^{\times d})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
we know that
\mu(\mathbf{v}^{i})=\frac{1}{q^{d}}
for each
\mathbf{v}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. Thus, for any subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
, observe that

\mu(S):=\sum_{\mathbf{v}^{i}\in S}\mu(\mathbf{v}^{i})=|S|\cdot\frac{1}{q^{d}}\leq\frac{d\cdot q^{\textbf{Rank}(S)}}{q^{d}}=\frac{d\cdot q^{\textbf{Rank}(S)}}{\textbf{Rank}(S)\cdot q^{d}}\cdot\textbf{Rank}(S)\leq\textbf{Rank}(S).

Above, the first inequality holds because
|S|\leq d\cdot q^{\textbf{Rank}(S)}
as we have
d
copies of each
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
. The last inequality follow from the fact that
xq^{-x}
is a decreasing function of
x
and
\textbf{Rank}(S)\leq d
, hence,
\frac{d\cdot q^{\textbf{Rank}(S)}}{\textbf{Rank}\cdot q^{d}}\leq 1
. Thus,
\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}^{\times d})
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
q
be any prime,
c
and
d
be two integers satisfying
q^{c-1}\geq d
, and
A
be the random set sampled according to Procedure
5.1
.
By Lemma
4.3
and Claim
5.2
we know that
A
follows a pairwise independent distribution and Lemma
5.3
demonstrates that the set
A
has marginals inside the matroid polytope
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}^{\times d})
.

The final step of Procedure
5.1
invokes Procedure
4.2
. Recall that Procedure
4.2
mixes distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}(A)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}(A)\mid A\sim\mathcal{D}_{1}]\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[A\sim\mathcal{D}_{1}]+\textbf{Rank}(\mathcal{M}^{\times d})\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[A\sim\mathcal{D}_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}(A)\mid A\sim\mathcal{D}_{1}]+\frac{d}{q^{d}}

\displaystyle(\textbf{Rank}(\mathcal{M}^{\times d})=d)

\displaystyle\leq c+1

\displaystyle(q\geq 2).

Moreover, due to Lemma

we know that
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{v}^{i}\in A]=\frac{1}{q^{d}}
for any
\mathbf{v}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]
. Thus,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[|A|]=\sum_{\mathbf{v}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathbf{v}^{i}\in A]=\frac{1}{q^{d}}\cdot\left|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times[d]\right|=d.

Combining these two facts we obtain:

\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}(A)]}{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[|A|]}\leq\frac{c+1}{d}.

Finally, the characterization of distributions permitting contention resolution from Theorem
2.1
concludes the proof.

∎

5.2
Optimal Pairwise Independent OCRS

Consider a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
and a randomly chosen set of active elements
A\subseteq 2^{E}
, which is sampled from a pairwise independent distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta_{\text{pw}}(2^{E})(\mathbf{\mu})
with marginals
\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
. In online contention resolution, the elements
e\in E
arrive in some order chosen by an adversary, at which point the CRS algorithm learns whether
e
is in
A
and must irrevocably decide whether or not to select
e
for inclusion in its solution. The algorithm is required to adhere to the feasibility constraints of the matroid during these inclusions.
We assume that the arrival order is chosen by the almighty adversary who is aware of the algorithm and all random outcomes, including those internal to the algorithm.

In the previous section we showed that for a class of linear matroids no offline CRS can attain a balance ratio greater than
\frac{3}{\text{rank}(\mathcal{M})}
for pairwise independent distributions. The following theorem shows the existence a
\frac{4}{\text{rank}(\mathcal{M})}
-balanced online CRS against the almighty adversary for all matroids.

Theorem 5.4
.

For any matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
, an arbitrary vector
\mathbf{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{I}}
and distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta_{\text{pw}}(2^{E})(\mathbf{\mu})
, there exists
\frac{1}{4\textbf{Rank}(\mathcal{M})}
-balanced pairwise independent online contention resolution scheme against the almighty adversary.

Proof.

We use the simple greedy algorithm that selects each active element with a probability of
\frac{1}{2\cdot\text{rank}(\mathcal{M})}
unless it violates feasibility. This algorithm internally flips an independent random coin for each element
e\in E
, where each coin lands on “heads” with probability
\frac{1}{2\cdot\text{rank}(\mathcal{M})}
. We denote the joint occurrence of an element being active and its corresponding coin flip resulting in heads by
\mathcal{E}_{e}
, and the complement of this event is denoted by
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{e}}
.

We note two crucial observations: First, events
\left\{\mathcal{E}_{e}\right\}_{e\in E}
are pairwise independent since events
\left\{e\in A\right\}_{e\in E}
are pairwise independent. Second, given that the marginal probability vector
\mathbf{\mu}(e):=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in A]
is within the matroid polytope
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, the total sum of probabilities
\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in A]
is at most
\text{rank}(\mathcal{M})
. Consequently, the sum
\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{e}]
is at most
\frac{1}{2}
. We now bound the balance ratio of the greedy algorithm as follows.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e{\mbox{ is selected}}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{e}\wedge\bigwedge_{f\in E\setminus\left\{e\right\}}{\overline{\mathcal{E}_{f}}}\right]

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}_{e}\text{ solely occurs})

\displaystyle={\left(1-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{f\in E\setminus\left\{e\right\}}\mathcal{E}_{f}\middle|\mathcal{E}_{e}\right]\right)}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{e}]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\sum_{f\in E\setminus\left\{e\right\}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{f}\mid\mathcal{E}_{e}]\right)\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{e}]

(Union bound)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\sum_{f\in E\setminus\left\{e\right\}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{f}]\right)\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{e}]

\displaystyle(\text{Pairwise Independence})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\textbf{Rank}}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in A]

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{f\in E}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{f}]\leq\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\textbf{Rank}}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in A].

6
Pairwise-independent Prophet Inequalities on Matroids

In this section, we explore a specific instance of the pairwise independent prophet inequality problem which demonstrates the impossibility of achieving any
\omega\left(\frac{1}{\log\textbf{Rank}}\right)
-competitive algorithm against the oblivious adversary. Our focus is on a complete binary matroid of rank
2d
, comprising
n
labeled copies of each element, represented as
\mathcal{M}^{\times n}=(E^{\times n},\mathcal{I}^{\times n})
. Here,
d
is an integer power of
2
,
n=\Theta(d)
, and
E^{\times n}
is defined as
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times[n]
. The precise value of
n
will be determined in subsequent discussions. The hard instance is structured around two main elements: (i) a pairwise independent probability distribution for the weights assigned to
E^{\times n}
and (ii) a fixed order
\lambda
of elements
E^{\times n}
. This setup will ensure that any algorithm adhering to
\lambda
for processing elements inevitably fails to select an independent set that offers substantial reward, particularly due to the lack of prior knowledge regarding the weight assignments.

6.1
Construction of Weight Distribution and Arrival Order

We start by outlining the process for constructing a pairwise independent weight distribution for the elements in the matroid
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times[n]
, where
n=\Theta(d)
.
Reflecting on the core principles summarized in Section
3.3
, our approach for determining weight distribution involves generating
\kappa=\Theta(\log d)
distinct
levels
.
The elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times[n]
will be partitioned into these levels based on their respective labels. For elements assigned to the
\ell
-th level, we will assign weights, either being
2^{\ell}
or zero, in accordance with a carefully formulated distribution.

For each level
\ell\in[\kappa]
, we fix the size of labels
L_{\ell}
of level
\ell
as
d/2^{\ell}
. Subsequently, we define
n
as the cumulative sum of the sizes of these label sets, expressed as
n:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa}|L_{\ell}|
. In particular, we define
label set
L_{\ell}
as
\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{d}{2^{j}}+1,\dots,\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{d}{2^{j}}\right\}
. Note that the collection
\left\{L_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in[\kappa]}
effectively forms a partition of the set
[n]
.
Consequently, the set of vectors
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times L_{\ell}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times[n]
is categorized as belonging to the level
\ell
. Having established this framework, we now proceed to outline the pairwise independent weight distribution along with a deterministic worst-case arrival order of elements which renders this distribution challenging for a gambler.

First, we describe the
weight assignment procedure for elements in the matroid, with an approach akin to the active element distribution for the pairwise independent CRS problem, described in Procedure
5.1
.
More formally, for each level
\ell\in[\kappa]
, we begin by constructing a random matrix
\Sigma_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d\times|L_{\ell}|}
whose columns are pairwise linearly independent with probability one. Subsequent to this, we apply a linear transformation with a matrix
R
whose entries are sampled independently from
r_{(i,j)}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(1/2)
. This step produces a new random matrix
X_{\ell}:=R\Sigma_{\ell}
for each level
\ell\in[\kappa]
.

Next,
X_{\ell}
is converted into a subset
A_{\ell}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times L_{\ell}
by independently applying Procedure
4.2
across all levels. Each vector
\mathbf{v}^{i}
in
A_{\ell}
is assigned a weight of
2^{\ell}
. Notice that this process is well defined as
\left\{L_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in[\kappa]}
forms a partition of
[n]
. As we will show in Lemma
6.1
, weights sampled according to this process are pairwise independent, provided the matrix
\Sigma:=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}\ \Sigma_{2}\ \dots\Sigma_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}
comprises pairwise linearly independent columns.

Regarding the deterministic order in the prophet inequality problem, we define an arbitrary fixed sequence
\lambda_{\ell}
for the elements within each level. The worst-case order
\lambda
for the matroid
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}\times[n]
is then composed by concatenating these individual level sequences in ascending order of levels. This is expressed as
\lambda:=\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{\kappa}
. Crucially, this sequence is structured without foreknowledge of the weight realizations, ensuring that any algorithm addressing the prophet inequality will confront elements with nonzero weights in ascending order of their assigned weights.

6.1.1
Properties of
\Sigma
matrices

We encode the challenging instance of the prophet inequality problem via a careful selection of the matrices
\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{\kappa}
, each adhering to certain essential properties.
At high level, we designate a random subspace for each level
\ell
, derived from the span of a randomly chosen subset of principal basis vectors
B_{\ell}
. The matrices
\Sigma_{\ell}
are then selected in a way that their columns are linearly independent subset within
\spn(B_{\ell})
. For convenience, we think of
\Sigma_{\ell}
interchangeably as a matrix as well as the set of it’s columns, and write
e\in\Sigma_{\ell}
for
e\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
if
e
is a column of
\Sigma_{\ell}
. The following four properties define critical features of
\left\{\Sigma_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in[\kappa]}
and
\left\{B_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in[\kappa]}
which ensure that our resulting
\mathbf{w}
and
\lambda
constitute a hard instance for the pairwise-independent prophet inequality problem as explained in Section
3.3
.

(i)

B_{\kappa}\subseteq\dots\subseteq B_{2}\subseteq B_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
is a nested system with size
|B_{\ell}|=\frac{d}{2^{\ell-1}}
.

(ii)

\Sigma_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d\times d/2^{\ell}}
is a full column rank matrix with columns from
\spn(B_{\ell})
.

(iii)

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[e\in\spn(B_{\ell^{\prime}})\mid\{e\in\Sigma_{\ell}\},\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{\ell},B_{1},\ldots,B_{\ell}]=\frac{1}{2^{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}}
for all
e\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
and
\ell^{\prime}\geq\ell
.

(iv)

\Sigma:=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}\ \Sigma_{2}\ \dots\Sigma_{\kappa}\end{bmatrix}
consists of distinct columns with probability
1
.

Let us revisit the roles of these properties, briefly touched upon in Section
3.3
. We first set aside for a moment the requirement for the distribution to be pairwise independent. Consider a “hard” instance of a prophet inequality problem on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
where each column vector in some
\Sigma_{\ell}
is assigned a weight of
2^{\ell}
and the rest of the vectors are assigned weight zero. Assume that the vectors with non-zero weight appear in ascending order of weight, starting with vectors in
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\dots,\Sigma_{\kappa}
, followed by vectors with weight zero.

A greedy offline algorithm, aiming to select the maximum weight independent set, would process the non-zero-weight vectors in reverse order from
\Sigma_{\kappa}
to
\Sigma_{1}
. By utilizing the matroid exchange principle and Property
(ii)
, the algorithm can secure a constant fraction of each set
\Sigma_{\ell}
. This strategy enables the prophet to achieve a total reward of
\Omega(d\cdot\log d)
.

Conversely, a gambler must select a subset
T_{\ell}
from the columns of
\Sigma_{\ell}
without prior knowledge of the sets
\Sigma_{\ell^{\prime}}
or their corresponding weights, for all
\ell^{\prime}>\ell
. Roughly speaking, based on Properties
(i)
and
(iii)
,
we can potentially show that if
|T_{\ell}|=c\cdot|\Sigma_{\ell}|
for some constant
c
then
T_{\ell}
ends up spanning
c
-fraction of vectors from all the sets
\Sigma_{\ell^{\prime}}
for all
\ell^{\prime}>\ell
. Hence, the gambler can only select
c
fraction of vectors from
\frac{1}{c}
many levels. This leads an
O(d)
upper bound on the total reward of the gambler and rules out
\omega(1/\log d)
-competitive algorithm for the gambler.

Returning to the aspect of pairwise independence, Property
(iv)
facilitates the conversion of this “hard” instance into one where weights are pairwise independent, while maintaining the problem’s inherent difficulty. This step utilizes the tools developed in Section
4
.

6.2
Upper Bounding the Approximation Ratio

In this section, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 6.2
.

For any integers
\kappa\geq 4
and
d=2^{2\cdot\kappa}
, let
n=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa}\frac{d}{2^{\ell}}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\times n}=(E^{\times n},\mathcal{I}^{n})
where
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
is the full binary matroid with rank
2d
, i.e.,
E=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}
. Then, there is no
\omega(1/\log d)
-competitive pairwise independent prophet inequality algorithm for matroid
\mathcal{M}^{\times n}
.

In order to prove the above theorem, we show that when the weights
\mathbf{w}
and the order
\lambda
are determined according to Procedure
6.1.2
, the prophet is able to secure a reward of
\Omega(d\cdot\log d)
, where as any gambler is limited to obtaining a reward at most
O(d)
. To prove this claim, we first identify a high probability event which makes the problem challenging for the gambler. Let
\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}
be the event when the following two events happen together:

(1)

The random matrix
R
sampled in Step
(2)
of Procedure
6.1.2
has full column rank,

(2)

The set
A_{\ell}
constructed in the Step
(6)
of Procedure
6.1.2
, is sampled according to
\mathcal{D}_{1}
in Procedure
4.2
for all
\ell\in[\kappa]
simultaneously.

Notice that the two described events are mutually independent. The first event occurs with a probability of
1-\frac{1}{2^{d}}
, as
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2d\times d}
. The second event occurs with a probability of
\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\right)^{\kappa}\geq 1-\frac{\kappa}{2^{2d}}
. Together, these events imply that
\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}
occurs with a probability of at least
1-\frac{\kappa+1}{2^{d}}
.

Furthermore, when the latter event happens only labeled copies of columns in
X_{\ell}
will have a non-zero weight for each
\ell\in[\kappa]
. Additionally, if the first event occurs, at most one labeled copy of each vector will have a non-zero weight. Conceptually, when
\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}
occurs, one can imagine that
\mathbf{w}
assigns non-zero weights to columns of
X_{\ell}
and so vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}
, rather than their labeled copies, since there is only one labeled copy of each item with a non-zero weight.

Next, we demonstrate two lemmas that lower-bound the prophet’s reward and upper-bound the any gambler’s utility respectively, under the condition that
\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}
happens.

Lemma 6.3

(Lower-bound Prophet’s Reward)
.

Given
\kappa\geq 4,d=2^{2\cdot\kappa},n=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa}\frac{d}{2^{\ell}}
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}
, let
\mathbf{w}
be the random weight assignment over elements of
\mathcal{M}^{\times n}
determined according to Procedure
6.1.2
. Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\text{reward of prophet}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}]\geq\frac{\kappa\cdot d}{10}.

Lemma 6.4

(Upper-bound Gambler’s Reward)
.

Given
\kappa\geq 4,d=2^{2\cdot\kappa},n=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa}\frac{d}{2^{\ell}}
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2d}
, let
\mathbf{w}
be the random weight assignment over elements of
\mathcal{M}^{\times n}
and
\lambda
be the order of these elements determined according to Procedure
6.1.2
. Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\text{reward of gambler}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}]\leq 2d.

Finally, we utilize these lemmas to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Due to Lemma
6.3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\text{reward of prophet}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\text{reward of prophet}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}]\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{E}_{\text{hard}}]

(3)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\kappa\cdot d}{4}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\kappa+1}{2^{d}}\right)\geq\frac{\kappa\cdot d}{5}

\displaystyle(\kappa\geq 4)

(4)

and due to Lemma

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\text{reward of gambler}]

6.2.1
Proof of Lemma
6.3
: Lower-bound for Prophet’s Reward

To compute a lower-bound for prophet’s reward, or equivalently
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{M}^{\times n})]

S
and show that its expected weight is at least

Then, for each level

be a maximal subset of

\displaystyle|\widetilde{A}_{2i-1}|+|\widetilde{A}_{2i}|

\displaystyle\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{M}^{\times n})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{w}(S)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa}\mathbf{w}(\widetilde{A}_{\ell})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lceil{\kappa/2}\right\rceil}(|\widetilde{A}_{2i-1}|+|\widetilde{A}_{2i}|)\cdot 2^{2i-1}

6.4
Optimal Pairwise Independent Matroid Prophet Inequality

In this section, we present an optimal (up to a constant factor) algorithm for the pairwise independent matroid prophet inequality problem against the almighty adversary. It is important to note that although our algorithm is designed to perform against the strongest adversary, the almighty adversary; our impossibility result holds against the weakest adversary, namely the oblivious adversary.

The following theorem is the main result of the section.

Theorem 6.9
.

There exists an
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\log\textbf{Rank}}\right)
-competitive algorithm for the pairwise independent prophet inequality problem against the almighty adversary for any given matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
. Here,
Rank
is a shorthand notation for
\textbf{Rank}(\mathcal{M})
.

The algorithm defines the strategy for the gambler by dividing elements into
weight buckets
. Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
be a matroid and
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{D}
be a pairwise independent weight distribution. We use
OPT
to denote the expected value of
\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{M})
under
\mathcal{D}
. To proceed, we set
k
as the ceiling of
\log(8\textbf{Rank})
and introduce
k+2

B_{0},B_{1},\dots B_{k}
, along with
B_{\infty}
. The buckets are defined as follows:

\displaystyle B_{0}=\left[0,\frac{\textbf{OPT}}{2\textbf{Rank}}\right),B_{1}=\left[\frac{\textbf{OPT}}{2\textbf{Rank}},\frac{2\textbf{OPT}}{2\textbf{Rank}}\right),\dots,B_{k}=\left[\cdot\frac{2^{k-1}\textbf{OPT}}{2\textbf{Rank}},\frac{2^{k}\textbf{OPT}}{2\textbf{Rank}}\right),B_{\infty}=\left[\frac{2^{k}\textbf{OPT}}{2\textbf{Rank}},\infty\right).

Given any draw of the weights
w\sim\mathcal{D}
, we partition the elements into random sets
E_{0},E_{1},\dots E_{k}
and
E_{\infty}
based on their realization. We define
E_{i}=\{e\in E:\mathbf{w}(e)\in B_{i}\}
for any
i\in\{0,\dots k,\infty\}
, which represents the elements whose weight lies in bucket

. It is important to note that the sets
E_{i}
for

are random.

We define the expected optimal reward from bucket

for any

as

\textbf{OPT}(B_{i})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathop{max}_{S\subseteq E_{i},S\in\mathcal{I}}\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}(S)\right].

This represents the maximum expected weighted rank of elements belonging to bucket
B_{i}
. We further define

B^{*}=\mathop{max}_{i\in\{1,\dots,k,\infty\}}\textbf{OPT}(B_{i}).

Note that we ignore bucket
B_{0}
because every element

has weight
\mathbf{w}(e)\leq\frac{\textbf{OPT}}{2\cdot\textbf{Rank}}
, and therefore
\textbf{OPT}(B_{0})\leq\frac{\textbf{OPT}}{2}
. Next, we aim to upper-bound the expected reward of the prophet by considering the total expected optimal rewards from each bucket. By invoking the fact that
\textbf{OPT}(B_{0})\leq\frac{\textbf{OPT}}{2}
, we obtain:

\textbf{OPT}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\textbf{OPT}(B_{i})+\textbf{OPT}(B_{\infty})\leq 2\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\textbf{OPT}(B_{i})+\textbf{OPT}(B_{\infty})\right).

(5)

v\in B_{\infty}
,

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|E_{\infty}|>1\mid\mathbf{w}(e)=v]\leq\sum_{f\in E\setminus\left\{e\right\}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[f\in E_{\infty}\mid\mathbf{w}(e)=v]=\sum_{f\in E\setminus\left\{e\right\}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[f\in E_{\infty}]\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Above, the first inequality is due to union bound. The equality follows from pairwise independence of the weights. This imply that for any value
v\in B_{\infty}
, we have
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[E_{\infty}=\left\{e\right\}\mid\mathbf{w}(e)=v]\geq\frac{2}{3}
. Finally, we compute the expected weight of
S
as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{w}(S)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{w}(e)\cdot\mathbbm{1}[e\in S]]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{w}(e)\cdot\mathbbm{1}[|E_{\infty}|=1]\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\mathbf{w}(e)\in B_{\infty}]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|E_{\infty}|=1\mid\mathbf{w}(e)\wedge\mathbf{w}(e)\in B_{\infty}]\cdot\mathbf{w}(e)\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\mathbf{w}(e)\in B_{\infty}]]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{e\in E}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{2}{3}\cdot\mathbf{w}(e)\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\mathbf{w}(e)\in B_{\infty}]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{w}(B_{\infty})]\geq\frac{2}{3}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}(B_{\infty})]\qed

We now complete the proof of Theorem
6.9
.

Proof.

Let
B^{*}:=\mathop{argmax}_{B_{i}\in\left\{B_{1},\dots B_{k},B_{\infty}\right\}}\textbf{OPT}(B_{i})
as defined in Algorithm
1
. We observe that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{w}(E^{*})]=\mathop{max}_{i\in\left\{1,\dots k,\infty\right\}}\textbf{OPT}(B_{i})\geq\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot\sum_{i\in\left\{1,\dots,k,\infty\right\}}\textbf{OPT}(B_{i})\geq\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\textbf{OPT}.

By Lemma
6.10
and Lemma
6.11
, we know that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[w(S)]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\textbf{Rank}_{w}(E^{*})]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\textbf{OPT}(B^{*})
. Thus,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[w(S)]\geq\frac{1}{4(k+1)}\cdot\textbf{OPT}
completes the proof as
k=\Theta(\log\textbf{Rank})
. Since our algorithm is deterministic and the probabilistic guarantees are valid regardless of the arrival order, the approximation guarantee holds even against the almighty adversary.
∎

(

Given a rank one matroid over elements

.

For matroids satisfying the

Proof.

\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{w}(S)\mid\mathcal{M}^{\prime}]

Now, we turn our attention to proving the existence of
O(\alpha)
-balanced offline and
O(\alpha^{2})
-balanced online pairwise independent CRS for matroids that satisfy
\alpha

.

For matroids satisfying the
\alpha
-partition property for some
\alpha\in(0,1]

\frac{1}{1.299}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right)\cdot\alpha

Consider a matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})

7.3
Structural Implications

We now shift our focus to showing that full linear matroids over a finite field do not admit a partition property with strong approximation guarantees. We prove this by combining the offline CRS of Theorem
7.4
with the impossibility result for contention resolution presented in Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 7.7
.

The full linear matroid
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
of rank
d
, with
q\geq d
, does not satisfy an
\alpha
-partition property with
\alpha=\omega\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)
. Moreover, the full binary matroid
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
of rank
d
does not satisfy an
\alpha
-partition property with
\alpha=\omega\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)
.

Proof.

First, we briefly argue that duplicating elements of a matroid preserves the partition property. Suppose that
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{I})
satisfies the
\alpha
-partition property, as witnessed by a random matroid
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
as in Definition
7.1
. For a positive integer
m
, let
\mathcal{M}\times m=(E^{\times m},\mathcal{I}^{\times m})
be the matroid which includes
m
parallel duplicates
e^{1},\ldots,e^{m}
of each element
e
of
\mathcal{M}
, as described in Section
2.2
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\times m
be defined similarly. For a weight vector
\mathbf{w}
indexed by the elements
E^{\times m}
of
\mathcal{M}^{\times m}
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be such that
\widetilde{w}(e)=\mathop{max}_{i=1}^{m}w(e^{i})
. Similarly, for
S\subseteq E^{\times m}
let
\widetilde{S}=\left\{e\in E:e^{i}\in S\mbox{ for some }i\right\}
. It is clear that
\textbf{Rank}_{w}^{\mathcal{M}\times m}(S)=\textbf{Rank}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}^{\mathcal{M}}(\widetilde{S})
, and similarly
\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\times m}(S)=\textbf{Rank}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\widetilde{S})
. The following calculation for arbitrary
S\subseteq E^{\times m}
shows that
\mathcal{M}\times m
satisfies the
\alpha
-partition property, as witnessed by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\times m
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\left[\textbf{Rank}_{w}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\times m}(S)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\left[\textbf{Rank}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\widetilde{S})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\cdot\textbf{Rank}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}^{\mathcal{M}}(\widetilde{S})

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot\textbf{Rank}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathcal{M}\times m}(S)

Given that the partition property is invariant to duplicating elements, combining Theorem
5.1
and
7.4
now yields the corollary.
∎

8
Open Questions

Our results indicate that pairwise independence lends insufficient structure for constant approximations to contention resolution and prophet inequalities on matroids. More generally, it is natural to investigate the same questions for
k
-wise independence, and to quantify the optimal ratios as a function of
k
. Our impossibility results rely on the recipe presented in Section
4
, which easily generalizes to
k
-wise independence only for ordered vector families. Generalizing our unordered construction to arbitrary
k
promises to extend our results to
k
-wise independence. We conjecture an optimal bound of
O(k/\text{rank})
for contention resolution, and refrain from such conjecture for prophet inequalities.

Due to the equivalence between the matroid secretary problem and the matroid prophet secretary problem from
[
26
]
, extending our impossibility result for prophet inequalities to the random order model would disprove the matroid secretary conjecture. In fact, constructions which are
k
-wise independent might be particularly promising, since they preclude any “learning” from samples of size
k
. On the flip side, designing secretary algorithms for variants of our construction could stimulate the development of pertinent algorithmic techniques.

Above the second equality follows from the definition of the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎
