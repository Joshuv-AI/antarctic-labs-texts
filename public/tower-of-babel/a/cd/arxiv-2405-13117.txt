Memory Burden Effect in Black Holes and Solitons: Implications for PBH
Gia Dvali, Juan Sebastián Valbuena-Bermúdez​ ID, Michael Zantedeschi​ ID

Abstract
The essence of the memory burden effect is that a load of information carried by a system stabilizes it. This universal effect is especially prominent in systems with a high capacity of information storage, such as black holes and other objects with maximal microstate degeneracy, the entities universally referred to as saturons . The phenomenon has several implications. The memory burden effect suppresses a further decay of a black hole, the latest, after it has emitted about half of its initial mass. As a consequence, the light primordial black holes (PBHs), that previously were assumed to be fully evaporated, are expected to be present as viable dark matter candidates. In the present paper, we deepen the understanding of the memory burden effect. We first identify various memory burden regimes in generic Hamiltonian systems and then establish a precise correspondence in solitons and in black holes. We make transparent, at a microscopic level, the fundamental differences between the stabilization by a quantum memory burden versus the stabilization by a long-range classical hair due to a spin or an electric charge. We identify certain new features of potential observational interest, such as the model-independent spread of the stabilized masses of initially degenerate PBHs.

Introduction

The phenomenon of memory burden, originally described in
[
1

, is summarized in the following statement:

Information loaded in a system resists its decay.

Naturally, the effect is especially sound in systems with enhanced information storage capacity. This capacity can be quantified by the number and degeneracy of microstates that the system possesses for the given values of macroscopic parameters, such as, e.g., the radius
\displaystyle R
and the total energy.

Black holes are the prominent representatives of this category. This is obvious from Bekenstein-Hawking entropy
[
2

S_{\rm BH}\,=\,\pi R^{2}M_{\rm P}^{2}\,,

(1)

where
\displaystyle M_{\rm P}
is the Planck mass. Correspondingly, it was suggested in
[
1

3

that the phenomenon of memory burden must be applicable to black holes.

This effect explains, in terms of an explicit microscopic mechanism, why, at the early stages of Hawking’s decay, the information stored in a black hole cannot be released together with radiation.
This matches the semi-classical expectation.

However, an important new feature emerges. The internally maintained information back-reacts and creates resistance against the decay of a black hole.
This is the effect of the memory burden phenomenon in a black hole.

Furthermore, by performing a detailed analysis of the prototype systems, it was concluded in
[
3
]
that not only do black holes undergo the memory burden effect, but they likely are stabilized by it.
That is, in the process of a black hole decay, the memory burden grows, and after a certain characteristic time,
\displaystyle t_{\rm M}
, reaches a critical value.

\displaystyle t_{\rm M}
is bounded from above by the age of a black hole that lost about half of its initial mass.

At this point, the black hole has evolved into a “remnant” that cannot continue an ordinary quantum decay. A remarkable feature is that the remnant is macroscopic, with a mass comparable to the initial black hole.
The fate of this object cannot be determined by applying a standard semi-classical analysis that would be valid for a young black hole.

As pointed out in
[
3
]
, at the current level of understanding, it is not excluded that some new collective (classical) instability sets in, leading to a disintegration of the memory-burdened remnant.

Putting this possibility aside, after a black hole enters the memory burden phase, its evaporation must slow down dramatically. From the analyticity considerations, it was suggested in

\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}

II
Essence of Memory Burden

The memory burden effect was introduced in
[
1
]
, where it was proposed that the loaded information pattern tends to stabilize the system carrying this information.
A more detailed analysis of prototype systems was performed in two follow-up papers,
[
22
]
and
[
3
]
, with the purpose of applying the memory burden effect to cosmology and black holes, respectively.
The last work concluded that the slowdown of the black hole decay due to the effect is imminent, the latest, by its half-decay.

In this chapter, following
[
1
,
22
,
3
]
, we shall explain the essence of the memory burden phenomenon and give some helpful classification of its regimes.
In order to achieve this, we first go through the main universal characteristics of the systems with the
enhanced capacity of information storage
.
This concept refers to the energetic efficiency of quantum information storage and was introduced in
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
at the level of basic Hamiltonians. The reader can find the summary of the setup in
[
33
,
34
,
24
]
.

The main characteristics of effective Hamiltonians describing systems of enhanced information capacity can be determined by categorizing quantum degrees of freedom according to the tasks they perform.
The degrees of freedom shall be introduced as quantum oscillators in number representations.
These shall later be identified with different modes of the quantum fields.

II.1
Memory modes

The first category of the degrees of freedom is the
memory modes
. We shall introduce them as quantum fields denoted by
\displaystyle\theta^{j}
where the index
\displaystyle j=1,2,....,M
labels their “flavor”.
The total number of species is
\displaystyle M
.
The corresponding creation-annihilation operators
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger},\,\hat{a}_{j}
can satisfy either fermionic or bosonic oscillator algebras.
For definiteness, we shall focus on the bosonic one:

[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}]\,=\,\delta_{ij}\,,~~\,[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}_{j}]=0\,.

(5)

The role of the memory modes is to store information. The information is stored in the patterns of their occupation numbers. These patterns are represented via ket-vectors in the Fock space,

|p\rangle=|n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{M}\rangle\,,

(6)

where numbers
\displaystyle n_{j}\equiv\langle p|\hat{n}_{j}|p\rangle
represents the eigenvalues of the corresponding number operators,
\displaystyle\hat{n}_{j}\equiv\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}
.

The Hilbert sub-space formed by the vectors
\displaystyle|p\rangle
shall be referred to as the
memory space
. The dimensionality of it is defined by the range of the occupation numbers of the memory modes
\displaystyle n_{j}
.
These can be subject to constraints.

An example of a simple constraint is to limit each occupation number to two possible values,
\displaystyle n_{j}=0,1
.
In this case, the free part of the memory system is described by the Hamiltonian of
\displaystyle M
independent qubits,

\hat{H}_{\rm free}\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{M}\,m_{j}\,\hat{n}_{j}

(7)

where
\displaystyle m_{j}
are the energy gaps of the memory modes.
The corresponding dimensionality of the memory space is
\displaystyle n_{\rm st}\,=\,2^{M}
.
In general, the dimensionality of memory space grows exponentially with the number of memory species
\displaystyle M
. Thus, the existence of a large number of memory species is an essential condition for the efficiency of information storage.
However, it is not sufficient.

Even with large
\displaystyle M
, the Hamiltonian (
7
) does not necessarily represent a system of enhanced information capacity.
This is because the energy span of the memory space can be very large if the gaps
\displaystyle m_{j}
are high.

Therefore, the second important characteristic is the energy cost of an information pattern,

E_{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}m_{j}\,n_{j}\,,

(8)

as well as the gaps between different memory patterns,

\displaystyle|p\rangle
and
\displaystyle|p^{\prime}\rangle
,

E_{p}-E_{p^{\prime}}\,.

(9)

In short, the efficiency of the information storage by a system is determined by the density of states: the number
\displaystyle n_{\rm st}
, of states
\displaystyle|p\rangle
, that can fit within a physically meaningful smallest energy gap
\displaystyle\Delta E
.
Usually, this is set by a typical uncertainty in the system’s energy, such as the level-width.
Naturally, many flavors
\displaystyle M
and smaller gaps
\displaystyle m_{j}
achieve more efficiency.

The resulting information storage capacity is quantified by the microstate entropy of the system defined as,

S\equiv\ln(n_{\rm st})\,.

(10)

In quantum field theory (QFT), the limit to memory capacity is set by saturons
[
4
]
: the objects with microstate entropy that saturates the upper bounds (
3
) and (
4
) imposed by the validity of a given QFT description.

II.2
Master modes: assisted gaplessness

The second category of the degrees of freedom are so-called
master modes
.
We shall denote them by
\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}
, with creation-annihilation operators,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{\phi_{\alpha}},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi_{\alpha}}
,
where
\displaystyle\alpha=1,2,...,
is their flavor index.

The role of the master modes is to
assist
the memory modes in becoming gapless.
As opposed to the memory modes, which must come in a large number of flavors
\displaystyle M
, the number of master mode species can be much less.

To illustrate the mechanism of the assisted gaplessness, and the resulting effect of memory burden, a single flavor of the master mode is sufficient. The corresponding number operator shall be denoted by
\displaystyle\hat{n}_{\phi}\equiv\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi}\hat{a}_{\phi}
.

With the above conventions, the effect of assisted gaplessness
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
can be illustrated by using the following simple prototype Hamiltonian
[
1
,
22
,
3
,
24
]
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{H}\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\hat{H}_{\rm ms}\,+\,\hat{H}_{\rm mem}\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm with}:~\hat{H}_{\rm ms}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle m_{\phi}\hat{n}_{\phi}\,,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm mem}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\hat{n}_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q}\sum_{j}m_{j}\hat{n}_{j}\,,

where, for physical clarity, we have split the Hamiltonian into the master
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm ms}
and memory
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm mem}
parts, respectively.

The parameters
\displaystyle m_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle m_{j}
represent the
intrinsic
energy (or mass) gaps of the master and memory modes, i.e., the gaps around the Fock vacuum state
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=n_{j}=0
.
The number
\displaystyle N_{\phi}\gg 1
is a large number that sets the coupling between master and memory modes as
\displaystyle 1/N_{\phi}
.
Notice that in QFT systems, for a given
\displaystyle N_{\phi}
,
\displaystyle M
is bounded as,

M\leqslant N_{\phi}\,.

(12)

This bound can be understood as a manifestation of a general bound

({\rm coupling})\times({\rm number~of~species})\leqslant 1\,,

(13)

violation of which invalidates the QFT description
[
4
]
. Thus, within the validity of QFT, the maximal number of memory patterns is achieved for
\displaystyle M\simeq N_{\phi}
.

The parameter
\displaystyle q
, which we take as some positive even number, requires clarification.
This quantity parameterizes the functional dependence
of the effective energy gaps
of the memory modes on
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
.
Of course, in general, this function can be more complicated.
However, near the point of the assisted gaplessness, it is well-described by a monomial with a power
\displaystyle q
.
In this form, the assisted gaplessness takes place for the critical occupation number of the master mode,

n_{\phi}=N_{\phi}\,.

(14)

Indeed, if we keep the master mode in the Fock vacuum state,
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=0
, and form a memory pattern via the excitations of the memory modes,
\displaystyle|p\rangle=|n_{1},...,n_{M}\rangle
, the cost of energy is

E_{p}=\langle p|\hat{H}_{\rm mem}|p\rangle\,=\,\sum_{j}m_{j}n_{j}\,.

(15)

This can be extremely high if
\displaystyle m_{j}
-s are large.

However, the system possesses
another state (
14
), with the exact same memory pattern as
\displaystyle|p\rangle
,
but with a critically excited memory mode.
We shall denote it by
\displaystyle|\tilde{p}\rangle
, where tilde indicates
the difference in
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
.

On this state, the contribution to the energy
from the memory mode-dependent
part of the Hamiltonian is zero:

E_{\rm mem}\,=\,\langle\tilde{p}|\hat{H}_{\rm mem}|\tilde{p}\rangle=0\,.

(16)

This is because the effective gaps for the
memory modes are no longer given by
\displaystyle m_{j}
, but rather by
the quantities,

\omega_{j}\,\equiv\,\left(1-\frac{n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q}m_{j}\,,

(17)

which vanish on a state with
\displaystyle n_{\phi}\,=\,N_{\phi}
.

Thus, for the information pattern
it is energetically favorable that the master mode,
instead of being in the vacuum
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=0
,
is in the critical state
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=N_{\phi}
.
This is the essence of the mechanism of the
assisted gaplessness

[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
(the term was coined in
[
33
]
).

However, the price to pay is the energy of the master mode:

E_{\rm ms}\,=\,\langle\tilde{p}|\hat{H}_{\rm ms}|\tilde{p}\rangle=m_{\phi}N_{\phi}\,.

(18)

Due to this, for a given information pattern
\displaystyle p
,
the energetically optimal state is determined by the balance
between the two entries
\displaystyle E_{\rm mem}
and
\displaystyle E_{\rm ms}
.
This fixes
the occupation number of the master mode,

\displaystyle n_{\phi}
, to a certain optimal value.
The energy difference
between this optimal state
and master mode’s Fock vacuum (
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=0
), both evaluated for the same memory pattern
\displaystyle p
,
determines the energy efficiency of the information storage.

It is useful to quantify this efficiency by defining
the memory-efficiency coefficient as the ratio of the actual cost of an information pattern

\displaystyle E_{\rm mem}
to its cost in the master mode Fock vacuum:

\epsilon\,\equiv\,\frac{E_{\rm mem}}{E_{p}}\,.

(19)

Moving away from the above optimal state, requires climbing an energetic barrier. This creates a resistance against
abandoning the state of the enhanced memory capacity.
This is the essence of the memory burden effect
[
1
,
3
]
.

II.3.1
Generalities

In order to understand the energetic balance leading to the
memory burden effect, let us minimize the Hamiltonian (
37
)
with respect to
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
in a state with a memory
pattern
\displaystyle|p\rangle=|n_{1},...,n_{M}\rangle
.
The intrinsic (vacuum) energy cost of the pattern,

\displaystyle E_{p}
, is given by
(
15
).

Notice that for minimization we can use Bogoliubov approximation in which we treat the operator
\displaystyle\hat{n}_{\phi}
as a

\displaystyle c
-number
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
.
This is justified, since the occupation number around
the states of interest is macroscopic and
the
\displaystyle c
-number approximation of the operator works
up to corrections of
\displaystyle 1/n_{\phi}

(for more detailed discussion of
\displaystyle c
-number method
see
[
33
]
).

We can distinguish the two regimes depending on whether

\displaystyle E_{p}
is above or below the following critical value,

E_{*}\,\equiv\,\frac{1}{q}m_{\phi}N_{\phi}\,.

(20)

For

E_{p}\,\geqslant\,E_{*}\,,

(21)

the minimum of the energy of the system is achieved
for

n_{\phi}\,=\,N_{\phi}\left(1\,-\,\left(\frac{E_{*}}{E_{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}\right)\,,

(22)

and it is equal to,

E\,=\,m_{\phi}N_{\phi}\left(1\,-\,\frac{q-1}{q}\left(\frac{E_{*}}{E_{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}\right)\,.

(23)

Taking into account (
20
) and
(
21
), we can easily see that this energy is less than the
would-be vacuum energy cost of the pattern
\displaystyle E_{p}
.
That is, in this case, it is energetically favourable
to stabilize the system in the state with non-zero
occupation number of the master mode
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
.

On the other hand, for

E_{p}\leqslant E_{*}\,,

(24)

the minimum energy state is,

n_{\phi}=0\,,

(25)

with the energy,

E\,=\,E_{p}\,.

(26)

Of course, at the critical value
\displaystyle E=E_{*}
, the two regimes
give the same minimal energy

E\,=\,E_{p}\,=\frac{1}{q}m_{\phi}N_{\phi}\,.

(27)

That is, if the vacuum energy cost of the pattern is below
the critical value (
20
), it is not worth energetically
to keep the system in
\displaystyle n_{\phi}>0
state.
Otherwise, the system is stabilized in a state
with
\displaystyle n_{\phi}>0
by the memory pattern. This is the key ingredient of the memory burden effect.

Notice, the Hamiltonian (
11
) conserves the number of the master mode. Correspondingly, the states with arbitrary

\displaystyle n_{\phi}
represent its eigenstates and do not evolve in time.
Therefore, in order to observe the stabilizing effect of the memory burden dynamically, we must add interactions
that do not conserve
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
and allow for
quantum transitions of
\displaystyle\phi
-quanta into some
“external” degrees of freedom
\displaystyle\hat{b}^{\dagger},\hat{b}
,
with energy gaps
\displaystyle m_{b}
.
This can be achieved by the inclusion of the interaction terms
of the form,

(\hat{b}^{\dagger})^{\beta}(\hat{a}_{\phi})^{\alpha}\,+\,{\rm h.c.}\,,

(28)

where
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta
are integers.

The dynamical memory burden effect is clearly
illustrated already in the simplest case
\displaystyle\alpha=\beta=1
,
with
\displaystyle m_{\phi}=m_{b}
, which can be solved analytically
[
1
]
. The Hamiltonian (
11
) is supplemented by

\hat{H}_{\rm int}\,=\,\frac{\tilde{m}}{\sqrt{N_{\phi}}}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\phi}\,+\,\frac{\tilde{m}^{*}}{\sqrt{N_{\phi}}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi}\hat{b}\,\,+\,m_{\phi}\hat{n}_{b}\,,

(29)

where
\displaystyle\hat{n}_{b}\equiv\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}

is the
\displaystyle b
-mode number operator and

\displaystyle\tilde{m}
is a complex parameter of the dimensionality of
energy. Notice that
\displaystyle 1/\sqrt{N_{\phi}}
in the mixing terms
makes the parametrization consistent with the normalization of the coupling of the master mode in (
11
).

The above system can be solved in two ways.
We can first find the minimum of energy for
a fixed total occupation number

n_{\phi}\,+\,n_{b}\,=\,N_{\phi}\,,

(30)

using the Bogoliubov approximation for

\displaystyle a_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle b
modes. Given the constraint
(
30
), without any loss of generality,
we can parameterize these modes replacing the operators
by the
\displaystyle c
-number functions,

\hat{a}_{\phi}\,=\,\cos(\theta)\,e^{i\alpha_{\phi}}\sqrt{N_{\phi}}\,,~\hat{b}\,=\,\sin(\theta)\,e^{i\alpha_{b}}\sqrt{N_{\phi}}\,,

(31)

where
\displaystyle\alpha_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{b}
are phases and

\displaystyle\theta
is an angle that parameterizes the distribution of the occupation number among
\displaystyle a_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle b
-modes.
Notice that the phases
\displaystyle\alpha_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{b}
drop out of the minimization procedure, since they
align with the phase of
\displaystyle\tilde{m}
and
give the negative over-all sign of the mixing term.

The total effective Hamiltonian becomes a function
of a single variable
\displaystyle\theta
,

\hat{H}\,=\,\sin(\theta)^{2q}\,E_{p}\,-\,|\tilde{m}|\,\sin(2\theta)\,.

(32)

It is clear that the larger is
\displaystyle E_{p}
, the closer is the minimum
of the energy to
\displaystyle\theta=0
, which implies

\displaystyle n_{\phi}=N_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle n_{b}=0
.

For
\displaystyle qE_{p}/|\tilde{m}|\gg 1
, the system gets stabilized
in the state
of enhanced memory capacity with the depleted number
of master modes,
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}\,=\,N_{\phi}\,-\,n_{\phi}
, given by,

\frac{\Delta n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\,=\,\sin^{2}{\theta}\simeq\left(\frac{|\tilde{m}|}{qE_{p}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2q-1}}\,.

(33)

Correspondingly, this number gets transferred to the
\displaystyle b
-mode.

On the other hand, for
\displaystyle E_{p}\,=\,0
, the minimum
is achieved for

\displaystyle\theta=\pi/4
implying that the average occupation numbers
satisfy,

n_{\phi}\,=\,n_{b}\,.

(34)

These results are fully confirmed by the explicit quantum evolution of the system.

Indeed, following
[
1
,
3
]
,
let us time-evolve the system from the initial
state
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=N_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle n_{b}=0
.

For
\displaystyle E_{p}=0
, the occupation numbers evolve in time as,

\frac{n_{\phi}(t)}{N_{\phi}}\,=\,\cos^{2}\left(\frac{|\tilde{m}|}{\sqrt{N_{\phi}}}t\right)\,,~~\,\frac{n_{b}(t)}{N_{\phi}}\,=\,\sin^{2}\left(\frac{|\tilde{m}|}{\sqrt{N_{\phi}}}t\right)\,,

(35)

which, for the averaged values, reproduce (
34
).

For
\displaystyle qE_{p}/|\tilde{m}|\gg 1
, the story is very
different. The oscillation amplitude is now suppressed,
so that the average value of
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}
is
given exactly by (
33
).
For a detailed numerical analysis, see
[
3
]
.

Of course, the above example of the system’s decay represents an oversimplified
toy model, with the main purpose of illustrating the system with
a swift memory burden effect. The time evolution must be taken with a
grain of salt, especially in the later oscillatory period.

For QFT systems, such as a black hole or a soliton, submerged in infinite space, the master mode can decay into a continuum of the external
\displaystyle b
-modes. These correspond to different momentum (or spin) eigenstates of outgoing radiation. In such cases, the time-evolution of the system
is dissipative rather than oscillatory. Regardless,
as long as the system maintains the information pattern with

\displaystyle E_{p}\gg m_{\phi}
, it is subjected to memory burden, latest for

\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}/N_{\phi}\sim 1
. That is,
the memory burden phase starts latest by the time the system gets rid of an order-one fraction
of its initial energy.

The way of avoiding the memory burden would be
for the system
to get rid of the information pattern very fast
[
1
]
. However,
in systems of enhanced information capacity,
due to the assisted gaplessness, this is an extremely suppressed process
[
3
]
.
For a detailed numerical analysis
of complex prototype systems, demonstrating this outcome,
the reader is referred to the above paper.

We now move to discussions of different parameter regimes and distinguish the two extreme realizations of the memory burden effect.

II.3.2
Type-
\displaystyle I
regime

The first regime, which we call Type-
\displaystyle I
, takes place when the intrinsic frequencies of the master and memory modes are of the same order.
We shall take them to be given by an universal mass gap
\displaystyle m
:

m=m_{\phi}=m_{j}\,.

(36)

The Hamiltonian (
11
) becomes,

\hat{H}\,=\,m\,\hat{n}_{\phi}\,+\,m\,\left(1\,-\,\frac{\hat{n}_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\hat{n}_{j}\,.

(37)

Notice that it is invariant under an arbitrary

\displaystyle U(M)
transformation of the memory
modes:
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}\rightarrow U_{jk}\hat{a}_{k}
.

Let us evaluate the Hamiltonian on a memory pattern,
with total occupation number of all species given by,

\sum_{j=1}^{M}n_{j}\,=\,N_{\rm G}\,.

(38)

Due to

\displaystyle U(M)
symmetry this number can be arbitrarily
redistributed among
\displaystyle M
memory modes, which creates
the following number of degenerate
microstates
[
4
]
,

n_{\rm states}\simeq\left(1+\frac{N_{\rm G}}{M}\right)^{M}\left(1+\frac{M}{N_{\rm G}}\right)^{N_{\rm G}}\,.

(39)

The above expression represents a binomial coefficient
\displaystyle\binom{N_{\rm G}}{M}
evaluated using Stirling approximation for large
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
and

\displaystyle M
.

This degeneracy can be understood in terms of the Goldstone
phenomenon, since the occupation number of modes

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
breaks the
\displaystyle U(M)
symmetry spontaneously down
to
\displaystyle U(M-1)
.

The memory pattern back reacts on the master mode.
In order to understand this back reaction,
following
[
32
]
,
we minimize the Hamiltonian (
37
)
with respect to
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
. This gives,

\left(1\,-\,\frac{n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q-1}=\frac{N_{\phi}}{qN_{\rm G}}\,.

(40)

We see that for
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\geqslant N_{\phi}
,

\displaystyle n_{\phi}\simeq N_{\phi}
.
Plugging (
40
) in the generic
expression (
17
) for the effective gaps of the memory modes, we get,

\omega\,=\,m\left(\frac{N_{\phi}}{qN_{\rm G}}\right)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\,.

(41)

In particular, for
\displaystyle q\gg 1
,
we obtain,

\frac{\omega}{m}\,\simeq\,\frac{N_{\phi}}{qN_{\rm G}}\,.

(42)

Notice also that the contribution to the energy
from the master and memory modes

E_{\rm ms}\,=\,m\,N_{\phi}\,,~~E_{\rm mem}\,=\,\omega\,N_{\rm G}\,,

(43)

relate as

E_{\rm mem}\,\simeq\,\frac{1}{q}E_{\rm ms}\,.

(44)

From (
43
) and (
42
),
it is clear that
in type-
\displaystyle I
, the memory-efficiency coefficient is:

\epsilon_{I}\,\simeq\,\frac{\omega}{m}\simeq\,\frac{N_{\phi}}{qN_{\rm G}}\,.

(45)

In realistic systems, due to interactions with external
degrees of freedom, the occupation number
\displaystyle n_{\phi}

can change in time. In the previous section
we reproduced an example of
[
32
]

in which the transition takes place into a single
\displaystyle b
-mode. In this case, the behaviour
is oscillatory. However, when the number of external decay channels is large,
the inverse transitions practically never happen and

\displaystyle n_{\phi}
decreases irreversibly.

For example, this is what happens
in black holes
[
3
]
, where the master mode
gets depleted into outgoing Hawking particles
[
14
]
.
In such cases, the system dynamically
evolves until it gets stabilized (or semi-stabilized)
by the memory burden effect.

In other words, the information drives the system
towards the state in which the energy cost of the information pattern
is optimal.
Correspondingly, the system resists against the attempts of
driving it away from such a state.
We shall later discuss this dynamics in solitons and in black holes and
compare them.

II.3.3
Type-
\displaystyle II
regime

We shall now consider the regime
in which the intrinsic energy gaps of the memory modes
are much higher than that of the master mode:

m_{j}\gg m_{\phi}\,.

(46)

We shall refer to it as type-
\displaystyle II
.
In this case the minimization of the Hamiltonian
with respect to
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
gives,

\left(1\,-\,\frac{n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q-1}=m_{\phi}\frac{N_{\phi}}{qE_{p}}\,.

(47)

Taking into account
(
13
) and (
46
), we notice that
for a memory pattern with
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\gtrsim M
,
the r.h.s., of the above equation is much less than one.
Thus, similarly to type-
\displaystyle I
case, we have

\displaystyle n_{\phi}\simeq N_{\phi}
.
Correspondingly, the energy cost of the master mode is

E_{\rm ms}\,\simeq\,m_{\phi}N_{\phi}\,.

(48)

At the same time, the actual energy cost of the memory pattern is,

E_{\rm mem}=\left(m_{\phi}\frac{N_{\phi}}{qE_{p}}\right)^{\frac{q}{q-1}}E_{p}\simeq\frac{1}{q}m_{\phi}N_{\phi}\,.

(49)

Thus, we arrive to the same
relation (
44
) as in type-
\displaystyle I
case.

However, the efficiency coefficient
\displaystyle\epsilon
is now
extra-suppressed. For example, taking all

\displaystyle m_{j}
to be set by a single scale,
\displaystyle m_{j}=m\gg m_{\phi}
,
we get

\epsilon_{II}\,\equiv\,\frac{N_{\phi}}{qN_{\rm G}}\frac{m_{\phi}}{m}\,.

(50)

This is suppressed with respect to type-
\displaystyle I
case
(
45
) by an additional factor
\displaystyle m_{\phi}/m
.

The basic lesson is the following:

Increasing the vacuum
gaps of the memory modes with respect to the master mode,
the system becomes more and more efficient
in information cost. Correspondingly, the memory burden effect
is stronger.

III
Memory Burden in Solitonic Saturon

We shall now study the memory burden
effect in solitonic saturons.
Following
[
4
]
, we first introduce
an example of a saturon in form of a vacuum bubble.
Next, we discuss their stabilization via memory burden
effect closely following
[
10
]
.

The bubble is a solution
in a renormalizable
\displaystyle SU(N)
-invariant theory
of a scalar field
\displaystyle\Phi
in the adjoint representation
with the following Lagrangian density,

\mathcal{L}[\Phi]=\frac{1}{2}{\rm{Tr}}\,(\partial_{\mu}\Phi)(\partial^{\mu}\Phi)-V(\Phi)\,,

(51)

where the potential is chosen as,

V(\Phi)\,=\,\frac{\alpha}{2}{\rm{Tr}}\left(f\Phi-\Phi^{2}+\frac{I}{N}{\rm{Tr}}\,\Phi^{2}\right)^{2}\,.

(52)

Here
\displaystyle\alpha
is a coupling constant and
\displaystyle f
is a scale.

\displaystyle I
is an unit matrix in
\displaystyle SU(N)
-space.

The validity of QFT description imposes the following bound
on the parameters of the model,

\alpha N\lesssim 1\,.

(53)

Basically, the quantity
\displaystyle\alpha N
, which is analogous to ’t Hooft coupling, must not exceed the critical value.
In the opposite case (
\displaystyle\alpha N\gg 1
),
\displaystyle\Phi
no longer represents a good QFT degree of freedom. This is
unambiguously signalled by the breakdown of the loop-expansion, as well as, by the saturation
of unitarity in multi-particle scattering amplitudes
[
4
]
.
Notice that after we map the above theory on a generic Hamiltonian of enhanced memory capacity (
11
), it will become clear that the bound (
53
)
represents a particular case of
the bound (
13
).

The system (
51
) has multiple degenerate vacua satisfying the condition

f\,\Phi_{a}^{b}-\left(\Phi^{2}\right)^{b}_{a}+\frac{\delta^{b}_{a}}{N}\,{\rm{Tr}}\,\Phi^{2}\,=\,0\,.

(54)

We shall focus on a pair of neighbouring vacua.

In the first one, the vacuum expectation value (VEV) is

\displaystyle\Phi=0
and the global
\displaystyle SU(N)
-symmetry is unbroken.
This vacuum exhibits a mass gap

m=\sqrt{\alpha}f\,,

(55)

which sets the minimal energy cost for
all particle excitations.

In the second vacuum of our interest, the VEV is,

\Phi_{0}\,=\,f\frac{1}{\sqrt{N(N-1)}}{\rm{diag}}\left(N-1,-1,...,-1\right)\,,

(56)

and
\displaystyle SU(N)
-symmetry is spontaneously broken
down to
\displaystyle SU(N-1)\times U(1)
.
Due to spontaneous breaking of symmetry, in this vacuum we have
the set of massless Goldstone bosons

\theta^{j}(x)\,,\quad j=1,2,\dotsc,M\,,

(57)

corresponding to broken generators
\displaystyle T^{j}
.
Their total number is

M\,=\,2(N-1)\,.

(58)

These Goldstones form a
fundamental representation of the
\displaystyle SU(N-1)
group of complex dimensionality
\displaystyle N-1
with non-zero charge under
\displaystyle U(1)
.

The effective Lagrangian of the Goldstone bosons has the following form,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}\,=\,f^{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)\left(\partial^{\mu}{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)\,,

(59)

with

{\mathcal{U}}(x)\equiv f^{-1}U(x)^{\dagger}\Phi_{0}U(x)\,,

(60)

where
\displaystyle U(x)
is the space-time dependent

\displaystyle SU(N)
-transformation matrix,

\quad U(x)=\exp\left[-i\theta^{j}(x)T^{j}\right]\,.

(61)

Up to second order in
\displaystyle\theta^{j}(x)
-s
and leading order in
\displaystyle 1/N
, the Goldstone
Lagrangian can be written as,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}\simeq\frac{1}{4}f^{2}\sum_{j}\left(\partial_{\mu}\theta^{j}\right)\left(\partial^{\mu}\theta^{j}\right)\,.

(62)

III.1
Vacuum Bubbles

Due to the degeneracy of vacua, there also exist the domain wall configurations separating
them,

\Phi(x)=\phi(x)\frac{1}{\sqrt{N(N-1)}}{\rm{diag}}\left(N-1,-1,...,-1\right)\,,

(63)

where the function

\displaystyle\phi(x)
interpolates between
\displaystyle 0
and
\displaystyle f
.
We shall be interested in a spherically symmetric
bubble (closed wall) of
\displaystyle\phi=f
vacuum
embedded in
\displaystyle\phi=0
one.

Let us first consider the following configuration,

\Phi=\frac{\phi(r,t)}{f}\Phi_{0}\,.

(64)

The field
\displaystyle\phi(r,t)
satisfies the following
equation of motion,

\partial_{t}^{2}\phi-\partial_{r}^{2}\phi-\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}\phi+\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}\,=\,0\,,

(65)

where,

V(\phi)\,=\,\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\phi^{2}\left(\phi-f\right)^{2}\,.

(66)

The boundary conditions are such
that for
\displaystyle t=0
,
\displaystyle\phi(r=\infty)=0
and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi|_{t=0}=\partial_{r}\phi|_{r=0}=0
.

If the initial radius of the bubble satisfies

\displaystyle R\gg m^{-1}
, this configuration
can be approximated by

\phi(r)=\dfrac{f}{2}\left[1+\tanh\left(\dfrac{m(R-r)}{2}\right)\right]\,.

(67)

The bubble wall has a thickness
\displaystyle\delta_{w}\simeq m^{-1}

and a tension,
\displaystyle\sigma\simeq m^{3}/(6\alpha)
.
Order of magnitude wise, this remains true also
for thick-walls bubbles for which
\displaystyle R\sim\delta_{w}
.

In the interior of the bubble, the
\displaystyle SU(N)
symmetry is broken spontaneously down to
\displaystyle SU(N-1)\times U(1)
.
Due to this, there are

\displaystyle M=2(N-1)
gapless
Goldstone species localized within the bubble.
These species do not exist outside of the bubble, since

\displaystyle SU(N)
-symmetry is restored there.

As a consequence, the bubble has a large
microstate degeneracy.
This degeneracy follows from spontaneously broken

\displaystyle SU(N)
-symmetry, since a bubble obtained from
(
63
) by an arbitrary
\displaystyle SU(N)
-transformation

\displaystyle U
,

\Phi(x)\rightarrow U^{\dagger}\,\Phi(x)\,U,

(68)

represents a classical solution of the same energy.

In quantum theory, the number of degenerate microstates is not
infinite, since only the orthogonal states must be counted.
These states are obtained by redistribution of the constituent
quanta of the bubble among different
\displaystyle SU(N)
-flavors
which (in large-
\displaystyle N
) amounts to the following
number of degenerate microstates
[
4
]
,

n_{\rm states}\simeq\left(1+\frac{s(R)}{2N}\right)^{2N}\left(1+\frac{2N}{s(R)}\right)^{s(R)},

(69)

where the quantity
\displaystyle s(R)=4\pi(R\,m)^{3}/\alpha

is the time-averaged space integral of
\displaystyle\phi^{2}(r)
.
This quantity effectively measures the mean occupation number of constituent quanta of the bubble soliton
viewed as a coherent state
[
35
]
.

Taking the maximal value of
\displaystyle N
given by
(
53
), for thick-wall bubbles
\displaystyle R\sim 1/m
,
we get the following expression
for entropy,

S\sim N\sim\frac{1}{\alpha}\,.

(70)

It is clear that this expression represents the area
(
\displaystyle\sim 1/m^{2}
) in units of the Goldstone scale
\displaystyle f
.
Thus, for thick wall bubbles the entropy saturates the area-law
bound (
4
).

For
\displaystyle R\gg 1/m
, the entropy is,

S\,\sim\,N\ln(mR)\,.

(71)

Due to the above degeneracy, the bubble represents a system of enhanced capacity of the memory
storage. The memory modes are Goldstone modes

\displaystyle\theta^{j}
. The master mode is the radial mode,
\displaystyle\phi(x)
.

Setting other components to zero,
the effective Lagrangian of Goldstone modes
and of the radial mode
\displaystyle\phi
is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)\left(\partial^{\mu}{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)-V(\phi)\,.

(72)

The Goldstone modes are well-defined exceptionally
for
\displaystyle\phi\neq 0
.

III.2
Stabilization by memory burden

We can now observe the stabilization by the memory burden effect. In this, we shall closely follow
[
10
]
, where this effect was studied.
This effect requires that a non-zero information pattern
is stored in non-zero frequency excitations of Goldstone modes. That is, some of the
Goldstone modes
(i.e., the memory modes) of non-zero frequencies are occupied to numbers
\displaystyle n_{a}
.

On such a state,
\displaystyle|p\rangle\equiv|n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{M}\rangle
, the measure of the memory burden effect is the expectation value,

\langle p|\left(\partial_{\mu}{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)\left(\partial^{\mu}{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)|p\rangle\,.

(73)

In order to understand the effect, let us first consider
a classical configuration (
60
) with,

U=e^{it\omega_{j}T^{j}}\,.

(74)

It is easy to see that this gives the equation,

\partial_{t}^{2}\phi\,-\,\partial_{r}^{2}\phi-\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}\phi-\omega^{2}\phi+\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}\,=\,0\,,

(75)

with

\omega^{2}\,\equiv\,\sum_{j=1}^{M}\omega_{j}^{2}\,.

(76)

Different patterns
\displaystyle(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},...)
can be obtained from one another by
\displaystyle SU(N-1)
transformation, which leaves the
\displaystyle\Phi_{0}
invariant.

The above equation always has a stationary bubble solution with time-independent
\displaystyle\phi(r)
. The asymptotic values are

\displaystyle\phi(\infty)=0
and
\displaystyle\phi(0)\neq 0
.
Since various patterns
\displaystyle(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},...,\omega_{M})
are related by a symmetry transformation, it is sufficient to
discuss the case
\displaystyle\omega^{j}=\delta^{j1}\omega

and later generalize to different patterns.

In the thin-wall limit,
\displaystyle R\gg 1/m
, the profile of the bubble is given by (
67
), where
the radius
\displaystyle R
can be determined by extremizing the energy of
the bubble as function of
\displaystyle R
,

E=\dfrac{2\pi}{3\alpha}m^{3}R^{2}(1-\dot{R}^{2})^{-1/2}+\dfrac{2\pi}{3\alpha}m^{2}\omega^{2}R^{3}\,,

(77)

subject to the condition that the quantity

Q=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}f^{2}\omega R^{3}=\dfrac{2\pi}{3\alpha}m^{2}\omega R^{3}\,,

(78)

represents a conserved charge. This determines the
radius of the stationary bubble as,

R_{0}=\dfrac{2}{3}\frac{m}{\omega^{2}}\,.

(79)

Correspondingly, the energy of the bubble is,

E_{0}=\dfrac{\omega}{\alpha}\,\frac{m^{5}}{\omega^{5}}\,\left(\frac{40\pi}{81}\right)\,.

(80)

As already discussed in
[
10
]
, the stationary bubble solution
can be mapped on a
\displaystyle U(1)
non-topological soliton
or a
\displaystyle Q
-ball
[
36
,
37
,
38
]

formed by a complex scalar with modulus
\displaystyle\phi(r)

and the
\displaystyle U(1)
-charge
\displaystyle Q
given by (
78
) (for some implications of
\displaystyle Q
-balls, see, e.g.,
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
).
However, despite the similarity of classical solutions,
such a soliton will not exhibit a significant microstate degeneracy
and will not represent a system of enhanced capacity of information storage. Correspondingly, the charge
\displaystyle Q
cannot be
associated with any information pattern.
For a significant microstate degeneracy, having
\displaystyle SU(N)
-symmetry is crucial. Only in such a case the total charge

\displaystyle Q
can be distributed among an exponentially large number
of memory patterns.

The stationary bubbles carrying arbitrary patterns are obtained
by
\displaystyle SU(N-1)
transformations and have identical macroscopic
features such as the energy and the radius. However, they have different characteristics
with respect to an asymptotic probe with fixed
\displaystyle SU(N-1)
quantum numbers relative
to which the rotation is performed.

In other words, the information is stored in
\displaystyle SU(N-1)
-rotations of a pattern
relative to a fixed observer. Obviously, all rotated bubbles have identical energies
but they are distinguished by the observer who can perform a scattering experiment
using a fixed probe.

The stationary bubble represents a bubble stabilized by
the memory burden effect. In terms
of occupation numbers of Goldstones,
\displaystyle n_{j}
,
the quantities
\displaystyle\omega_{j}
read,

\omega_{j}^{2}\,=\,\omega\,n_{j}\,\frac{3}{2\pi f^{2}R^{3}_{0}}\,.

(81)

From the quantum perspective, it is illuminating to express the energy of the bubble in terms
of the occupation numbers of the radial (master) mode,

\displaystyle N_{\phi}
, and of the Goldstone
modes,

N_{\rm G}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{M}n_{j}\,.

(82)

Splitting the energy of the stationary bubble (
80
) into two contributions, we have:

E_{0}\,=\,E_{\text{mem}}\,+\,E_{\text{ms}}\,,

(83)

where

E_{\text{mem}}=\omega N_{\rm G}\,,~~\text{with},\,~~N_{\rm G}\equiv\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\dfrac{m^{5}}{\omega^{5}}\left(\dfrac{16\pi}{81}\right)\,,

(84)

is the energy of memory (Goldstone) modes, whereas

E_{\text{ms}}=mN_{\phi}\,,~~\text{with},\,~~N_{\phi}\equiv\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\,\dfrac{m^{4}}{\omega^{4}}\,\left(\dfrac{8\pi}{27}\right)\,,

(85)

is the energy of the master (radial) mode
\displaystyle\phi(r)
.
At the stationary point we have

E_{\text{mem}}=\dfrac{2}{3}E_{\text{ms}}\,.

(86)

Notice that this reproduces the generic equation
(
44
) with
\displaystyle q=3/2
.

From (
78
) and (
84
) it is easy to notice that we
have,

Q=N_{\rm G}\,.

(87)

That is, the classical charge in quantum theory counts the
total occupation number of Goldstone modes.
Various memory patterns are obtained by distribution of

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
among
\displaystyle M=2(N-1)
flavors of Goldstones.

Next, notice that the memory pattern with the same

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
in the

\displaystyle SU(N)
-invariant vacuum
would cost

E_{p}=mN_{\rm G}\,,

(88)

since in this vacuum the minimal energy gap of any elementary
excitation with
\displaystyle SU(N)
quantum number is
\displaystyle m
.

Taking this into account, we can evaluate the
memory-efficiency coefficient (
19
), which gives,

\epsilon\,=\,\frac{\omega}{m}\simeq\,\frac{2N_{\phi}}{3N_{\rm G}}\,.

(89)

In the last equality we have expressed
\displaystyle\omega/m

through occupation numbers via (
84
) and
(
85
).
This reproduces the equation (
45
)
with
\displaystyle q=3/2
. We thus see that the vacuum bubble
realizes the type-
\displaystyle I
memory burden effect,
with
\displaystyle q=3/2
.

The type-
\displaystyle I
nature of the memory burden
is not surprising, since
in the
\displaystyle SU(N)
invariant vacuum, the energy gaps
of all excitations are equal to
\displaystyle m
.

The stationary bubble represents a bubble stabilized by the
memory burden. Notice that for such a bubble
we have,

Q=N_{\rm G}=\dfrac{\pi}{\alpha}(mR_{0})^{5/2}\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^{3/2}\,.

(90)

A bubble with an insufficient memory burden will not be stationary. In particular, such is a bubble
with the radius larger than the critical value
\displaystyle R\gg R_{0}
.
Obviously, for such a bubble,

N_{\rm G}\ll\dfrac{\pi}{\alpha}(mR_{0})^{5/2}\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^{3/2}\,,

(91)

and

E_{\text{mem}}\ll\dfrac{2}{3}E_{\text{ms}}\,.

(92)

The memory burden is insufficient for stabilizing such a bubble.
Instead, as shown in
[
10
]
,
the bubble starts to collapse and oscillate emitting
energy. This decreases the occupation number of the master
mode. This process shall continue until the
balance is restored to (
86
). At this point the
bubble gets stabilized by the memory burden effect.

In particular, the initial bubbles of equal energies but
different values of
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
will evolve into the stabilized bubbles of different energies as we shall discuss next.

III.3
The energy splitting by memory burden

We wish to discuss the following important effect
which is characteristic of the phenomenon of memory burden stabilization.
Namely, the initially-degenerate energy states with different
memory patterns
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\neq N_{\rm G}^{\prime}
, upon stabilization by the memory burden, split in energies.

Let us start with two initial bubble states with
different values of the Goldstone occupation numbers,

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
and
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}^{\prime}
respectively.
We assume that both numbers are subcritical
(
91
). Thus, initially the memory burdens are dynamically insignificant. According to (
93
), the contribution to the energy from the memory modes is,

E_{\text{mem}}=\omega N_{\rm G}\,\sim N_{\rm G}\sqrt{\frac{m}{R}}\,.

(93)

Notice that order-of-magnitude wise this expression holds
also for (
91
), since
\displaystyle\omega\sim\sqrt{\frac{m}{R}}
.

Correspondingly, the energy splitting between the two bubble states with fixed difference,
\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm G}\equiv N_{\rm G}-N_{\rm G}^{\prime}
,

\Delta E\,\sim\,\Delta N_{\rm G}\,\sqrt{\frac{m}{R}}\,,

(94)

vanishes for
\displaystyle R\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus, for sufficiently large and equal radii, the two bubble states are nearly degenerate.

Notice that the states with different
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
are fully legitimate microstates of the same macrostate.
Semi-classically, the detection of the
Goldstone charge is not possible. In quantum theory
any attempted measurement is suppressed by powers of

\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm G}/N_{\phi}
, which vanishes in the semi-classical limit.
Thus, the microstates with different
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
are equally indistinguishable semi-classically as the microstates
with equal
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
, but relatively rotated in
\displaystyle SU(N)
-space.

Now, since the occupation numbers of Goldstones,

\displaystyle N_{\rm G},N_{\rm G}^{\prime}
, are subcritical
(
91
), the bubbles will evolve in time by oscillating and emitting energy until they get stabilized by the memory burden effects.
Since during this process the radii diminish and correspondingly the Goldstone frequencies (
\displaystyle\omega
) grow,
so does the energy splitting between the two bubble states.

At the stabilization point, the bubble energies can be highly split
if the relative difference of Goldstone numbers is large.
For example, for
\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm G}/N_{\rm G}\sim 1
, the energy splitting between the two bubble “remnants” is of order their masses. Of course, the energy of a remnant plus radiation is the same in both cases.

To summarise: For a soliton that has not yet entered
the memory burden stabilization phase (i.e., an under-burdened soliton with (
91
)), we can distinguish the two types of degenerate microstates: 1) the microstates that have the same memory burden

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
; and 2) the microstates with different values of
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
.

The first category belongs to the same orbit in

\displaystyle SU(N)
-space and, therefore, remains degenerate throughout the
bubble evolution. However, the energies of the second category
undergo the splittings according to (
94
).

The above situation is generic for the memory burden
effect. In particular, as we shall discuss, the same phenomenon takes place
in black holes. There too, after entering the memory burden
domination phase, the masses of initially-degenerate black
holes with different information patterns can become vastly different - see Fig.
1
for a schematic visual.
We shall also demonstrate this effect numerically in Sec.
VI
.

Figure 1:
Schematic view of the splitting of energies of
the microstates of either a soliton or a black hole, time-evolved from initially (nearly) degenerate microstates with different memory patterns. The solid lines represent the energies of the remnant states, whereas the black dashed line represents the total energy of the system
(the remnant mass plus the energy of emitted radiation). Notice that each solid line is not a single state but accounts for all the states that remain degenerate due to identical memory burdens.
The degeneracy can be exact due to symmetry. For example,
such are the vacuum bubble microstates related by
\displaystyle SU(N)
-symmetry.
The line can also
exhibit a finer-structure, in case the symmetry is only approximate.

Finally, we wish to stress that while the memory burden stabilizes
the systems, the converse is not excluded:
the stable systems with very little information
capacity can happily exist.
An example of such a system would be the
\displaystyle Q
-ball
with the
\displaystyle U(1)
-charge. Although the
\displaystyle U(1)
-charge carries very little information, it nevertheless can stabilize the system dynamically.

The important thing about the systems with high information content (such as saturons) is that they have no choice: the memory burden effect stabilizes them necessarily.

IV
Memory burden in black holes

Already in the original papers on memory burden
[
1
,
22
,
3
]
it has been argued
that black holes must be subjected to this phenomenon.
Furthermore, the analysis of
[
3
]
revealed
that the stabilizing nature of the effect, with high likelihood, leads to the prolongation of black hole’s life-time.
Here, we shall further scrutinize this question in light of
our analysis.

Let us first structure the arguments that justify
the presence of the memory burden effect in black holes.
This conclusion can be reached by three independent
lines of evidence.

The first line developed in
[
1
,
3
]
is based on the universality of the phenomenon.
The review of the effect given in Chapter
II
, makes it evident
that it is impossible to construct a hermitian Hamiltonian
with efficient information storage that avoids
the memory burden phenomenon.
The fact that the black holes
are efficient (in fact, the most efficient) storing devices, indicates that the effect must be present there.

Further justification is provided by the analysis
of
[
10
]
and by its continuation in the present work. This analysis shows the presence
of the memory burden effect in solitonic saturons.
These objects share with black holes all the known key features such as information horizon, area-law entropy,
thermal decay and Page-like time of information retrieval.
This gives a strong indication that memory burden
must belong to this list.

Finally, the third line of reasoning is based on
hints from a microscopic theory, a so-called
black hole’s quantum
\displaystyle N
-portrait
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. According to this theory, a black hole represents a saturated coherent state (or a condensate) of gravitons, at criticality.
The term
criticality
implies that the mean occupation number is inverse of
the gravitational coupling. In the present language, these coherent gravitons play the role of a master mode.
This theory offers a microscopic explanation of black hole properties within the domain of calculability.

In particular, the entropy of a black hole is explained
by the emergence of
\displaystyle M
“flavors” of the gapless modes in the above critical state of gravitons.
These can be described as Bogoliubov/Goldstone modes of the critical graviton condensate
[
15
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
.

In the present language,
they represent the memory modes.
In our reasoning of establishing the nature of
the memory burden in black holes, we shall borrow only
very general features of
\displaystyle N
-portrait, as this was done
in
[
1
,
3
]
.
We shall stay strictly within the calculability domain of
the QFT framework.

Namely, we shall rely on the following rather conservative (and natural) starting points:

1)
Both memory and master modes of a black hole are
describable as modes of the graviton field. Although additional field species can certainly exist, e.g.,
in form of string excitations, they do not change the essence of the effect (although, can lead to
certain modifications
[
24
]
). We come to the role
of the extra species later, in Subsec.
IV.3
.

2)
The memory modes can be classified according to
the symmetries that are left unbroken by the black hole metric. This is analogous to how in our bubble example
the Goldstone memory modes have been characterized by
their quantum numbers under the
\displaystyle SU(N-1)\times U(1)

unbroken symmetry.

Equipped with the above guidelines, we shall discuss the black hole memory burden effect. Let us first identify the master and memory modes.

The identification of the master mode is easy, since
at the initial times, this mode has macroscopic
occupation number and is therefore well-described classically.
Moreover, since the Bogoliubov approximation works for such modes, as a control parameter we can use the
suitable characteristics of the classical metric. Basically, the master modes
are Fourier harmonics of the classical metric.
For a black hole of radius
\displaystyle R
, the dominant
contribution comes from the modes of energy gap,

m_{\phi}\sim 1/R\,.

(95)

Of course, the black hole metric viewed as the coherent state of gravitons
is a distribution peaked around the above value
[
14
]
. For our purposes, it suffices to consider a single mode rather than a sharply peaked distribution.

Let us now identify the memory modes. As already said,
we shall only use very general points outlined above.
We know that the memory modes must come from
graviton modes and can be classified according to symmetries of the classical metric. Moreover, the number of their flavors,

\displaystyle M
, must be

M\sim S_{\rm BH}\,,

(96)

where
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
is the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy which
for a black hole of mass
\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}
and radius

\displaystyle R
is
[
2
]
,

S_{\rm BH}=\pi(RM_{\rm P})^{2}=4\pi\frac{M_{\rm BH}^{2}}{M_{\rm P}^{2}}\,.

(97)

There exist unique candidates fulfilling the above requirements
that can emerge as the assisted gapless modes
[
31
,
1
]
.
These are the modes of the graviton corresponding to various spherical harmonics,
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
.
Only the harmonics with momenta up to cutoff of the theory must be included, since only such modes are the legitimate
(weakly-coupled) QFT degrees of freedom.
In Einstein gravity, with no additional light species besides graviton, the cutoff is
\displaystyle M_{\rm P}
. We thus need to include
all possible spherical harmonics of graviton up to the Planck
mass.

For a black hole of radius

\displaystyle R
, this counting gives
the multiplicity of memory flavors that scales as the area of a black hole in
units of
\displaystyle M_{\rm P}
,

M\sim(RM_{\rm P})^{2}\,,

(98)

and therefore precisely
matches the entropy demand (
97
)
[
31
,
1
]
.

As an additional supporting evidence, the above fully matches the counting of gapless modes from the black hole symmetries derived in
[
50
,
49
]
.

Of course, the majority of modes comes from the highest spherical harmonics.
We thus identify the gapless memory modes of a black hole as
the modes of angular momenta
\displaystyle\sim M_{\rm P}R
.
Their counterparts in the asymptotic vacuum are the
same angular harmonics
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
of a free graviton.
However, these carry the energy gaps
\displaystyle m_{j}\sim M_{\rm P}
.

Notice that the above explains why a black hole cannot emit information efficiently
[
1
]
:
in order to “escape” from a black hole via a quantum process,
a memory mode has to climb an extremely high
energetic barrier.

The existence of modes with the same
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
but with largely split energy gaps inside and outside of a black hole is not surprising. The black hole breaks the Poincare symmetry at the scale
\displaystyle M_{\rm P}

[
4
]
. Due to this, the memory modes of a black hole,
despite having the high orbital momenta, are gapless, whereas the asymptotic modes with similar momenta
are gapped by
\displaystyle\sim M_{\rm P}
.

The above knowledge suffices for adapting the
generic Hamiltonian (
11
) to a black hole situation.
The effective Hamiltonian of black hole memory and master modes is described by (
11
) with the
understanding that index
\displaystyle j
labels spherical harmonics
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
.
Correspondingly, the intrinsic gaps of master and memory modes are:

m_{\phi}\sim\frac{1}{R}\,~{\rm and}~\,m_{j}\sim M_{\rm P}\,,

(99)

respectively.
The coupling of the master mode to memory modes is set by the standard gravitational coupling
\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm gr}(R)
evaluated at scale
\displaystyle R
,

\frac{1}{N_{\phi}}\sim\alpha_{\rm gr}(R)\sim\frac{1}{(RM_{\rm P})^{2}}\,.

(100)

As pointed out in
[
14
]
, this coupling is equal to the inverse of the
Bekenstein-Hawking entropy
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
. This is not an accident. As explained in
[
4
]
,
this equality puts
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
in accordance with the generic bound on entropy (
3
).

Now, in this description, the initial state of a classical black hole corresponds to the critical state

\displaystyle n_{\phi}=N_{\phi}
in which the
memory modes are essentially gapless,
\displaystyle\omega_{j}\,=\,0
.

As already discussed in
[
1
]
, this
explains why at the initial stages of evaporation, the black hole information cannot come out. Indeed, due to the conservation of the angular momentum, translating the information stored in the memory modes into the asymptotic quanta, would require radiating the quanta of energies
\displaystyle\sim M_{\rm P}
, which is not possible.
In addition, a pair-wise annihilation of memory modes
of frequencies
\displaystyle\omega
into the soft (low angular momentum) external quanta is suppressed as

\displaystyle\sim\omega_{j}^{5}/M_{\rm P}^{4}
and is negligible (see below).
Thus, neither the emission of the memory modes nor their
conversion into the external quanta is an option.

At the same time, black hole can emit the master mode via quantum scattering, reproducing the ordinary Hawking radiation
[
51
]
. Basically, the process is a conversion of a master mode into asymptotic quanta of similar frequencies.
This process is unsuppressed, since the occupation number of the master mode is equal to its inverse coupling.
As a result, the suppression by powers of coupling is
compensated by the occupation number,
and the rate of emission
is
[
14
]
,

\Gamma_{\rm ms}\sim\frac{1}{R}\frac{n_{\phi}^{2}}{N_{\phi}^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{R}\,,

(101)

which reproduces the Hawking rate.
This simple quantum explanation of the Hawking effect is one of the successes of
\displaystyle N
-portrait
[
14
]
. However, due to generic nature of the effect, it holds
for arbitrary saturons
[
9
,
10
]
.

Since we start in the critical state
\displaystyle n_{\phi}\,=\,N_{\phi}
, at the initial stages
the memory burden effect is weak.
At this stage, the equation (
101
) is a good approximation. It tells us that, on average,

\displaystyle n_{\phi}
decreases by
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}\sim 1

over time
\displaystyle\Delta t\sim R
.

As it is obvious from (
11
), in general, decreasing the occupation number of the master mode by
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}
, affects its gap by,

\Delta m_{\phi}\sim\frac{q}{N_{\phi}}\left(\frac{\Delta n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q-1}E_{p}\,.

(102)

Taking into account (
99
), (
100
) and
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\sim M\sim S_{\rm BH}
, this is of order,

\Delta m_{\phi}\sim q\left(\frac{\Delta n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q-1}M_{\rm P}\,.

(103)

The memory burden effect becomes dominant
by the time when
\displaystyle\Delta m_{\phi}\sim 1/R
, which gives,

\frac{\Delta n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}=(qM_{\rm P}R)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\,.

(104)

The same expression is obtained from (
47
) after
taking into account (
99
).

The effective gaps of the memory modes also grow
with
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}
as

\omega_{j}\,=\,\left(\frac{\Delta n_{\phi}}{N_{\phi}}\right)^{q}M_{\rm P}\,,

(105)

and by the time of validity of (
104
), they
become,

\omega_{j}\,=\,\frac{1}{qR}\frac{1}{(qM_{\rm P}R)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}}\,.

(106)

In the above expressions, the quantities

\displaystyle R
and
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
must be understood as the parameters of the initial black hole.

We must remember that we track the evolution of an
initial state with a given memory pattern.
The microstate entropy
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
accounts for the degeneracy
of various patterns. However, as the evolution goes on and memory modes gain energy gaps, the initially degenerate memory patterns get split in energy. Of course, the total energies
of the systems, i.e., black hole plus radiation, obtained
by evolving different initial patterns,
are degenerate.
However, the fractions of energy that remain stored in the black hole relative to radiated portion are different for
states evolved from different initial patterns
(see Fig.
1
).

The equations (
104
) and
(
106
) determine the onset of the memory burden
using as a clock the change in occupation number
of the master mode
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}
. Since before the
onset of the memory burden effect, this number changes by usual
Hawking emission, essentially it measures the time in units
of
\displaystyle R
:
\displaystyle t=\Delta n_{\phi}R
.

It also measures the onset of memory burden
in terms of the emitted
fraction of the initial mass of a black hole as

\displaystyle\Delta M_{\rm BH}/M_{\rm BH}=\Delta n_{\phi}/N_{\phi}
.

Equations (
104
) and
(
106
) depend on an unknown parameter
\displaystyle q
. However, independently of the precise value of this parameter, the tendency is very clear: the domination of the memory burden takes place latest by the time the
black hole radiates away about half of its mass.
That is, the expected upper bound on memory burden time of a black hole is

t_{\rm M}\,\sim\,S_{\rm BH}R\,.

(107)

This is the case for relatively large
\displaystyle q
.
For smaller values of
\displaystyle q
, the memory burden can dominate
sooner. For example, for
\displaystyle q=2
, this takes place
for
\displaystyle\Delta n_{\phi}\sim\sqrt{S_{\rm BH}}
, i.e.,
after the time
\displaystyle t_{\rm M}\sim\sqrt{S_{\rm BH}}R
.

Since the intrinsic gaps of the memory and master
modes are very different -
\displaystyle m_{\phi}/m_{j}\sim 1/(M_{\rm P}R)
- the memory burden effect in black holes is type-
\displaystyle II
.

The coefficient of memory efficiency for a black hole can be estimated following the reasoning of
[
1
]
.
In a black hole of mass
\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}
,
the energy difference between the most distant information patterns is
\displaystyle\sim 1/R
.
If we would store the same pattern by a non-gravitational
device of size
\displaystyle R
on top of a flat space vacuum, the energy cost would be of order

\displaystyle E_{p}\sim M_{\rm p}S_{\rm BH}
.
The memory-efficiency coefficient at the beginning
of evaporation, when black hole is still classical, is therefore

\epsilon_{\rm initial}\sim\frac{1}{(M_{\rm P}R)S_{\rm BH}}\sim\frac{1}{S_{\rm BH}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,.

(108)

By the onset of the memory burden effect this becomes,

\epsilon_{\rm MB}\sim\frac{1}{RM_{\rm P}}\sim\frac{1}{S_{\rm BH}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,.

(109)

In the next section we discuss the mass-splitting
among the stabilized black hole states.

IV.1
Spread of black hole masses

Since the mass of a stabilized black hole is a function of the
memory burden
\displaystyle E_{p}
, the prediction is that initially-degenerate black holes over time become spread in masses, as
schematically given in Fig.
1
.
This is similar to the evolution of equal mass vacuum
bubbles with different values of
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
.

In order to share with the reader a general sense of scaling,
let us analyse an oversimplified toy model in
which the memory modes of a black hole are treated as
independent qubits with the Hamiltonian (
11
) with
equal intrinsic gaps
\displaystyle m_{j}=M_{\rm P}
.
In this case,
\displaystyle E_{p}=M_{\rm P}N_{\rm G}
. Correspondingly, the memory burden effect can be
measured by the total occupation number of the memory modes
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
.
For
\displaystyle M
qubits, this number is bounded by

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\leqslant M
and the total number of patterns
is given by
\displaystyle n_{\rm st}=2^{M}
. This gives the entropy

\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}\sim M
(more precisely,
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}=M\ln 2
).

For estimating the spread in this toy model
we can use the equation (
103
) and take into account that
the memory burden effect becomes significant for
\displaystyle\Delta m_{\phi}\sim 1/R
. We also take into account that for a black hole

\displaystyle m_{\phi}\sim 1/R,~m_{j}\sim M_{\rm P}\sim M
and
\displaystyle N_{\phi}\sim M\sim S_{\rm BH}
.
However, we keep
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
as a free parameter.

Correspondingly, for a black hole of the initial mass

\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}
and the memory burden
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
,
the relative change in mass by the time of stabilization
is,

\frac{\Delta M_{\rm BH}}{M_{\rm BH}}\,\sim\,\left(\frac{S_{\rm BH}}{q(M_{\rm P}R)N_{\rm G}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}\,\sim\,\left(\frac{\sqrt{S_{\rm BH}}}{qN_{\rm G}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}\,.

(110)

One must remember that the above expression is valid
as long as

N_{\rm G}\geqslant\frac{1}{q}\sqrt{S_{\rm BH}}\,.

(111)

This inequality represents a manifestation of the general formula
(
21
) for a black hole. In the opposite case the stabilization is not efficient.
Thus, for
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
at its lower bound (
111
), the
stabilization takes place at
\displaystyle\Delta M_{\rm BH}/M_{\rm BH}\sim 1
.
On the other hand, for
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\sim S_{\rm BH}
, we
recover (
104
).

The equation (
110
) tells us that the spread in masses of stabilized remnants is determined by the statistical distribution of
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
among the initial black holes.
For simplicity of estimate, let us assume that, at the time of formation,
the occupation number of the black hole master mode is critical,
\displaystyle n_{\phi}=N_{\phi}
. That is, the memory patterns are strictly degenerate. In practice, this is expected to be
an extremely good approximation, since the initial black hole
is well-described classically up to corrections

\displaystyle\sim 1/N_{\phi}
.

In such a case, we can assume that the probability distribution
of patterns is flat, with no energy bias. Correspondingly, the probability of a pattern with given
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
is,

{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{\rm G}}\,=\,2^{-M}\frac{M!}{(M-N_{\rm G})!N_{\rm G}!}\,,

(112)

which is maximal for
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}=M/2
with

{\mathcal{P}}_{M/2}\,\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\,,

(113)

and the width of
\displaystyle\sqrt{M}/2
.
On the other hand, for
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\ll M
, the probability is exponentially suppressed as,

{\mathcal{P}}_{N_{\rm G}\ll M}\,\sim 2^{-M}\left(\frac{M{\rm e}}{N_{\rm G}}\right)^{N_{\rm G}}\,,

(114)

where we dropped polynomial factors for simplicity.
Taking into account that the black hole entropy
is
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}\sim M
, the above implies that
the probability of forming a black hole
with memory burden
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\ll S_{\rm BH}
is exponentially
small. Thus, it is expected that the
most probable black holes are the ones with

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\sim S_{\rm BH}
.

To summarize, as it is clear from the equation
(
110
), the change of the black hole mass
prior to its stabilization is set by
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
as,

\Delta M_{\rm BH}\,\propto\,N_{\rm G}^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}\,,

(115)

where the coefficient of proportionality,

\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}\,\left(\sqrt{S_{\rm BH}}/q\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}
,
is fully determined by the initial mass of a black hole.
At the same time, the memory burden
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
is determined by the mass only statistically, via (
112
).

This fact has potentially important observational
implications since it predicts a statistical spread
of
\displaystyle\Delta M_{\rm BH}
in addition to
the initial mass distribution determined
by particularities of a cosmological scenario (see Sec.
VII
).

IV.2
Fate of a black hole burdened by memory

What happens after a black hole enters the memory burden phase
requires a more detailed understanding of the picture. The two possible outcomes were discussed in
[
3
]
.

The first option is that a new classical (collective)
instability sets in and the (former) black hole evolves
through it. From our current understanding, it cannot be excluded
that due to this instability the remnant
can disintegrate via some non-linear process.

The second (more conservative) option assumes no immediate classical instability.
In such a case, the black hole continues to decay via a quantum
process. However, due to the memory burden, the process is extremely slow. In
[
3
]
, the remaining life-time of a black hole was given as,

\tau\sim R\,S_{\rm BH}^{1+k}\,,

(116)

where
\displaystyle k>0
is an integer. This form follows from the fact
that the prolonged life-time is an analytic function of
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
.
The analyticity in
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
is enforced by the requirement that the
decay rate must be analytic in occupation numbers as well as in gravitational couplings, all of which are set by
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}
. The case
\displaystyle k=0
(zero memory burden)
would correspond to a standard extrapolation of
Hawking’s decay rate.

The value
\displaystyle k>0
, can be understood
from the following argument.
In order to continue its decay, the black hole must get rid
of the memory burden. That is, the excited memory modes
must get de-excited. This can only be done by the
scattering processes that involve at least a pair of
the memory modes. The memory modes must annihilate into the
modes of lower angular momenta in order to match their energies.
That is, each mode
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
must find a partner

\displaystyle Y_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
with very close values of
\displaystyle l,m
.
Such pairs are extremely rare and their
annihilation rate is

\Gamma\sim\frac{\omega_{j}^{5}}{M_{\rm P}^{4}}\sim\frac{1}{R^{5}M_{\rm P}^{4}}\,.

(117)

In terms of the initial entropy, the life-time translates
to

\tau\gtrsim R\,S_{\rm BH}^{2}\,.

(118)

This reinforces equation (
2
),
giving
\displaystyle k=1
as the most conservative estimate.

IV.3
The Effect of Species

It is known that black holes provide a
link between the number of QFT species
\displaystyle N_{\rm sp}

and the upper bound on the scale of strong quantum gravity
[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
]
,

M_{\rm sp}\,=\,\frac{M_{\rm P}}{\sqrt{N_{\rm sp}}}\,.

(119)

The expression is fully non-perturbative and cannot be
removed by any re-summation of perturbative series.
The physical meaning of the scale
\displaystyle M_{\rm sp}
, called “species scale”, is that a black hole of size
\displaystyle<\,1/M_{\rm sp}
, cannot be treated semi-classically. This can be seen from a number of arguments.

For example, if the standard
semi-classical evaporation rate would apply to such a black hole, one would conclude
that the black hole would live shorter than its radius, which is absurd.

Similarly, the violation of the bound (
119
)
is excluded by quantum information arguments
[
55
]
:
a black hole of size
\displaystyle<1/M_{\rm sp}
, would have the
microstate degeneracy, and correspondingly the information storage capacity, exceeding the one of
Bekenstein-Hawking entropy (
97
).
In other words, the “species entropy” would exceed
the entropy of Bekenstein-Hawking
[
4
]
.

In general, the increased number of species
shortens the standard stage of black hole evaporation
by a factor
\displaystyle 1/N_{\rm sp}
. This has implication
for the memory burden, since the time
for reaching this phase is correspondingly shortened
[
24
]
:

t_{\rm M}\,\sim\,S_{\rm BH}R\frac{1}{N_{\rm sp}}\,.

(120)

The effect of the species beyond this point is less certain, since due to quantum hair, no universality in decay rates of different
species is guaranteed. In other words, the black hole can develop
a “species hair”
[
54
]
.
Therefore, the further decay of the remnant
can be biased towards some of the species.

As discussed in
[
18
]
,
already the formula (
120
)
can have important implications for PBH dark matter, as
it can shift the masses of memory burdened
black holes towards higher values. Furthermore,
assuming that decay of the remnant remains
approximately democratic in species, the
life-time in memory burden phase will subsequently
be shortened by the quantity
\displaystyle\sim 1/N_{\rm sp}
.

V
Quantum memory burden
versus classical extremality

It is well known that black holes, as well as solitons,
can be stabilized against the quantum decay by
classical
charges, either Noether or topological.
The term “classical” implies that the charge
is classically-detectable. In other words, the object must carry
a classical “hair” with respect to this charge.

In case of a black hole, in the light of
“no-hair” theorems
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
, such are the charges associated
with massless gauge fields that
can be measured via Gaussian fluxes at infinity
2
2

2

The seeming exceptions, such as a classical skyrmion hair
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
,
have been shown
[
64
,
65
]
to
be equivalent to Aharonov-Bohm type hair
under a discrete gauge symmetry
[
66
,
67
]
.
.
Usually, the amount of charge
capable of stabilization is comparable to the mass of a
black hole (or a soliton).

We now wish to confront a mechanism of stabilization of an object by
a classical hair with the one of a
quantum memory burden.
For achieving this, we first need to understand the stabilization of a black hole (or a soliton) by a classical charge in a fully quantum language of a microscopic theory.

The first steps in this direction
were undertaken in
[
14
]
.
This microscopic description was further generalized
to topological solitons in
[
35
]
.

In black hole’s quantum
\displaystyle N
-portrait
[
14
]
, a purely gravitational black hole represents a critical coherent state or a condensate of master mode gravitons.
The typical energy gaps of these modes are
\displaystyle m_{\phi}\sim 1/R
and
their mean-occupation number,

\displaystyle n_{\phi}
, is critical,
\displaystyle n_{\phi}\sim S_{\rm BH}
.

Correspondingly, as in any other system with enhanced information capacity, prior to stabilization, the master modes are the main contributors into the mass of an initially-classical black hole,

M_{\rm BH}\sim n_{\phi}\,m\sim S_{\rm BH}\frac{1}{R}\,.

(121)

In this quantum picture, the existence
of a classical charge of a black hole means that,
on top of the master mode, some other mode carrying this particular charge becomes macroscopically occupied.
In other words, a second master mode appears.

For example, the electrically
charged Reissner-Nordström black hole contains a
macroscopic occupation number of photons.
These photons compose a coherent state
describing the classical electric field of the black hole.
That is, when a black hole carries a classical charge,
the “status” of a master mode is shared between the
master graviton and some other mode.

Now, as argued in
[
14
]
,
the occupation number of the master graviton sets the upper bound on other occupation numbers.
That is, for a black hole the occupation numbers of master modes satisfy the bound,

n_{\rm any~mode}\leqslant n_{\phi}\,.

(122)

In this way, the theory
[
14
]
provides a quantum
explanation of why a black hole charge can never exceed its mass.
Notice that this is true for an arbitrary saturon. In particular, (
122
) is satisfied by saturated vacuum bubbles
discussed in section
III
.

According to
[
14
]
, a black hole reaches
extremality when the occupation number of a certain mode, other than the master graviton, saturates the above
bound. In this description, the stability of an extremal
Reissner-Nordström black hole
can be viewed as the saturation of the bound (
122
)
by the photon constituents of a black hole.

It is intuitively clear
[
14
]
as well as evident from computations
[
68
]
that in this case the black hole
evaporation must stop. The reason is that for maintaining the
saturation of the bound (
122
), a further
lowering of the occupation number of the
graviton master mode,
\displaystyle n_{\phi}
, must be accompanied by
the depletion of the electric field. This is not possible
without emission of the charged quanta. However, the fact that
the black hole of size
\displaystyle R
has an unscreened electric
charge to start with, implies that in the theory the mass
of the lightest charged particle,
\displaystyle m_{\rm e}
,
satisfies:
\displaystyle m_{\rm e}\gg 1/R
. In the opposite case, the
charge would be screened by the Schwinger effect.

Correspondingly, the quantum emission of electric charge
is exponentially suppressed, since such a process
requires a rescattering of many soft constituent quanta (of energies
\displaystyle\sim 1/R
) into a quantum of energy
\displaystyle m_{\rm e}\gg 1/R
.
The transition rate of such processes is bounded from above by
\displaystyle e^{-m_{\rm e}R}
, as indicated by a general argument given in
[
4
]
. This is confirmed by
explicit computations of corresponding multiparticle
processes
[
68
]
.
The suppression of the transitions many
\displaystyle\rightarrow
few can
also be extracted from explicit computations of graviton scattering processes
[
69
,
70
]
. The analogous suppression
is exhibited by scalar theories
[
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
,
77
,
78
]
.

Thus, the characteristics of black hole stabilization by a classical charge is the existence of a long-range classical hair with respect to that charge. At the same time, the quantum information pattern carried by the black hole
plays no role.
For example, the extremal black holes of the same electric charge and mass can carry information patterns of very different content.

In contrast, when a black hole is stabilized by a
quantum memory burden, no classical charge associated
with a long-range gauge field is required.
Of course, a macroscopic “hair” emerges in form of
a memory burden parameter,
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
.
This parameter is in principle measurable by a scattering experiment, but there is no inconsistency with the no-hair
properties: since the memory-burdened black hole is a quantum object, it has no reason to respect classical no-hair theorems.
Therefore, unlike classical extremal case,
there is no specific gauge charge or a long-range Gaussian flux associated with the quantity
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
.

The difference between the two
mechanisms is also clear from the fact
that the decay of a memory-burdened black hole
is not suppressed exponentially but only slows down as
a power-law (
2
).

Interestingly, the solitons stabilized by the memory burden
unify both features.
The main reason is the existence of two types of microstates
discussed above.

The first category of microstates have equal
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
but are distinguished by the
relative
\displaystyle SU(N)
-transformations. These remain exactly degenerate even after the memory burden sets in.
The microstates belonging
to the second category are distinguished by values of
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
.
The energies of such microstates get split after stabilization.

Both sets of microstates have counterparts in a
black hole.
As discussed, the microstates of a black hole
correspond to degeneracy of patterns obtained by
occupation numbers of different memory modes
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
.
Among these states, there exist both categories:
with equal and distinct memory burdens.
As time elapses, the level splitting develops
according to the memory burdens they carry, as given in
Fig.
1
.

Another example of a classical “charge” that can stabilize a black hole against quantum decay is the angular momentum.
The similar case in solitons reveals a certain interesting
peculiarity.

The stationary
\displaystyle SU(N)
-bubble with non-zero spin is obtained via giving the winding number
\displaystyle n
to the Goldstone mode
[
11
]
. Basically, the matrix
\displaystyle U
in (
74
) is replaced by

U=e^{i\left(t\omega+n\varphi/\sqrt{2}\right)\frac{\omega_{j}}{\omega}T^{j}}\,,

(123)

where
\displaystyle\varphi
is the polar angle and the factor
\displaystyle\sqrt{2}
is added to ensure the phase continuity.

The winding of the Goldstone phase produces the vorticity and, simultaneously, the angular momentum,

J=\int{\rm d}^{3}x\,T_{0\varphi}=n\,N_{G}=n\,Q\,,

(124)

where
\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}
is the energy momentum tensor.

The fact that the winding number

\displaystyle n
induces the spin given by (
124
)
has been known for
\displaystyle Q
-balls with
\displaystyle U(1)
-charge
[
45
,
46
,
79
]
.
The remarkable thing about the saturated
\displaystyle SU(N)
vacuum bubbles is
that the equation (
124
), describing the relation between the
maximal spin and the entropy, is strikingly similar to
the one satisfied by a black hole
[
11
]
,

J_{\rm max}=S\,.

(125)

A saturon vacuum bubble of
\displaystyle SU(N)
, which has entropy

S=N_{\rm G}\,,

(126)

reproduces the relation (
125
) due to the limited vorticity,
\displaystyle n\sim 1
, that it can sustain. Attaining a higher spin would require a larger vorticity,
\displaystyle n\gg 1
, which would increase the mass and the size of the bubble, thereby making it undersaturated.

Note that the relation (
125
) is shared by other saturons. For example, it is automatic
in saturated baryons in QCD with large number of colors and flavors
[
5
]
.

The same relation is shown
[
80
]
to be shared by a saturated version of a spinning cosmic string loop ( a so-called vorton
[
81
]
).

To summarize, the saturon bubbles of maximal spin
exhibit the exact same relation between spin and entropy
as the extremal spinning black holes. At the same time,
in extremal saturon bubbles this relation reveals an explicit
microscopic meaning in terms of vorticity.

Notice also that vorticity gives a topological meaning to the
quantum stability of the extremal bubble
[
11
]
.
Indeed, the decay of a bubble via the particle emission
is blocked because of conservation of the topological winding number. The decay is only possible if the entire vortex is ejected via a quantum tunnelling which is an exponentially suppressed process.

In this sense, the extremal spin represents another form
of a “classical” memory burden.
As in the case of the burden induced by a
pure
\displaystyle SU(N)
-charge, the burden induced by the
spin is due to a critical occupation number
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}

of a Goldstone/memory mode. For a saturon bubble,

\displaystyle\omega\sim m
, this memory mode becomes an additional master mode.
The difference from non-spinning case is that
the macroscopically-occupied memory mode has a non-zero angular momentum.

This analogy is fully extended to black holes if we
conjecture that a spinning black hole corresponds to the
one in which a mode of a particular angular harmonic
\displaystyle Y_{lm}
is macroscopically occupied. In this light, it is interesting to discuss
a conjecture of
[
11
]
stating that the extremal spin in a black hole is accompanied by vorticity,
similarly to the case of a spinning saturon bubble.
Again, as in the case of a bubble, the vorticity gives
a topological explanation to the quantum stability
of the extremal black hole.
In the case of the spin, similarly to
other classical charges, the extremal black hole does carry a classical hair in form of the angular momentum.

To summarize, there exist fundamental differences
between the black holes stabilized via
classical charges and the ones stabilized by a quantum memory burden effect.
One obvious difference is that the classical
extremality is necessarily accompanied by a long-range classical gauge hair.

This is not the case
for a black hole stabilized by the memory burden effect.
Instead, for a memory-burdened black hole, the hair is short-range and carries no Gaussian flux at infinity.

More importantly, a memory-burdened black hole carries a significant quantum hair. The significance is measured by the fact that the energy of the quantum information pattern is
comparable to the mass of the remnant.

Such a remnant is neither a black hole nor classical,
and, therefore, is not constrained by classical no-hair theorems.
Although macroscopic, it nevertheless is a quantum entity.
This “macro-quantumness” is a feature of
black holes
[
16
]
as well as of
other saturons
[
4
]
.

The above important difference between the quantum memory-burdened
and (quasi) extremal black holes must be taken into account when applying such mechanisms of stabilization to light PBH dark matter. In particular, if the naive semi-classical time-scale required for approaching extremality is longer than the memory burden
time
\displaystyle t_{\rm M}
, the latter effect becomes the dominant one and
must be taken into account.

In addition to differences at a fundamental level, this will have observational consequences.
For example, the memory-burdened PBH dark matter
[
3
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
will be subjected to the mass-spread
discussed in the present paper. This spread is absent
for PBHs stabilized by extremality within the validity of
semi-classical regimes, such as discussed in
[
82
]
(and references therein).

The fundamental difference between the prolongations
of a black hole life-time due to a classical hair versus the quantum memory burden effect, extends beyond the ordinary black holes. The example is provided by a black hole smaller than the compactification radius in the framework of large extra dimensions
[
83
]
.

Such a black hole is a solution of Einstein’s equations
in high-dimensional gravity and its radius and temperature are defined by the fundamental Planck mass,
\displaystyle M_{\rm F}
, which is
suppressed relative to the four-dimensional one by the volume
of extra space
[
83
]
:

M_{\rm F}\,=\,\frac{M_{\rm P}}{\sqrt{V_{\rm extra}}}\,.

(127)

Correspondingly, the high-dimensional black holes are colder relative
to would-be four-dimensional black holes of the same mass.
It was proposed in
[
84
,
85
]
that such black holes can serve as dark matter.

Let us confront the Kaluza-Klein (KK) hair of
young
high-dimensional black holes
with the memory burden effect. Of course, the question arises
solely from the point of view of a four-dimensional observer, since
from high-dimensional perspective these are usual classical black holes.

From four-dimensional perspective, the decay of high-dimensional black holes can be understood in terms of species.
First, as discussed in
[
52
]
, the equation (
127
) can be viewed as a particular case of (
119
), with the role of particle species played by KK gravitons and the role of the
four-dimensional species scale assumed by the fundamental Planck mass
\displaystyle M_{\rm sp}=M_{\rm F}
.

When evaluating the evaporation rate, we must take into account that the black hole is smaller than the compactification radius, correspondingly, it develops the

species hair

[
54
]
.
That is, the black hole sources a tower of massive KK gravitons to which it also evaporates.
The evaporation into many KK species is equivalent to the emission of a single high-dimensional graviton
[
56
]
.

Therefore, the evaporation of a young black hole is well-described semi-classically through a high-dimensional Hawking
process. From the point of view of a four-dimensional
observer it is an object that caries a classical species hair
[
54
]
under KK gravitons. Such a classical hair is fundamentally different from the quantum memory burden that sets in at a later stage.

This is already evident from the fact
that quantum memory burden will be experienced
by a high dimensional black hole after it gets sufficiently
“old”. Extrapolating our results, we can say that
this will happen the latest by half-decay.
Therefore, for a high-dimensional PBH, the quantum memory burden
effect must be taken into account in the same way as for
ordinary Einsteinian black holes.

VI
Numerical Analysis: stabilization of solitons

We summarize here our numerical findings. We focus on the dynamics of underburdened solitons, whose evolution, as discussed in Sec.
V
, offers analogy with evaporating black holes that are stabilised by their quantum memory.

The idea is to start with a vacuum bubble,
with the value of the charge
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
given by (
92
).
As discussed in Subsec.
III.2
,
this amount of charge is insufficient for an immediate stabilization.
In particular, for such a bubble the energy of the memory pattern
is negligible as compared to the energy of the master mode
(
92
), which constitutes the main source of the energy.

Such a bubble oscillates, emitting energy and correspondingly
shrinking in size. We map this stage of evolution on the
one of a black hole before entering the memory burden phase.

The decay process of the bubble continues up until the Goldstone charge fulfils the condition (
90
). Simultaneously, the
energy of the memory pattern catches up with the energy of the master
mode (
86
).
Correspondingly, the bubble
is stabilized by the burden of memory. At this point, the bubble becomes a stationary
\displaystyle Q
-ball.

Let us denote the reference stationary
\displaystyle Q
-ball charge by
\displaystyle Q_{\rm s}=N_{\rm G}
and its frequency by
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm s}
. We then vary the initial charge by changing the frequency of constituents, while keeping the profile fixed, to the following values

•

\displaystyle\omega=3\omega_{\rm s}/4
,
\displaystyle Q=3Q_{\rm s}/4

•

\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{\rm s}/2
,
\displaystyle Q=Q_{\rm s}/2

•

\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{\rm s}/4
,
\displaystyle Q=Q_{\rm s}/4

•

\displaystyle\omega=0
,
\displaystyle Q=0
.

resulting in underburdened configurations of different
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}
(therefore, not related by a
\displaystyle SU(N)
transformation) in the regime (
92
) and (
91
).

Our numerical analysis supports the analytical discussion of the previous Sections. Namely, the bubbles of different initial charges - but of similar energies - while they have analogous dynamics at initial times, are stabilised towards
\displaystyle Q
-ball configurations of different masses.

The statement holds true for all regimes analysed in our study - regardless of other details such as the winding number, the inclusion of new interactions and the thickness of the bubble wall. This shows that the mechanism of stabilization by memory is a general consequence of the high-memory storage capacity of the object and is only secondarily affected by other features, such as, for example, saturation or vorticity. Consequently, this further supports the prediction of Subsec.
III.3
that an analogous mass splitting is expected in the asymptotic distribution of black holes of equal initial masses but different memory burdens.

\displaystyle SU(4)
symmetric case. Unless otherwise stated, we fix
\displaystyle\alpha=m=1
in units of
\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\omega=0.3\,m

\displaystyle n=0

Thick Wall regime

So far, our numerical analysis focused on bubbles in the thin-wall regime.
Although such bubbles are undersaturated in terms of the bound
(
4

capacity of information storage.
This is fully sufficient for understanding the key features of the memory burden effect and mapping it on black holes. In fact, the
thin-wall bubbles are very well suited for
capturing the type-
\displaystyle II

effect.

On the other hand, in the thick-wall regime the bubble/
\displaystyle Q

saturon

of the area-law scaling of entropy (
4

Therefore, we shall also study this case.

As we will see, at a qualitative level, statements analogous to the ones of the previous Subsection hold also in this regime, providing further evidence for the generality of the stabilization by the burden of memory.
However, there are some quantitative differences, since the memory burden effect in thick-wall bubbles is of type-
\displaystyle I

The two panels in the third column of Fig.

One notable feature in the thick wall regime is that the constituents within the bubble have larger frequency. Therefore, it is
easier to excite modes above the asymptotic mass gap, as compared to thin-wall case. This, in turn, leads to a progressively larger relative emission of charged particles as the initial charge of the configuration decreases. This is due to the fact that the system is in the type-

Remarkably, charge emission seems alleviated in presence of
\displaystyle\chi

A further comment related to the behaviour of the system at small

Instead, only a small fraction of the initial energy is emitted; the reason being that the “oscillon” configuration is not sufficiently excited to produce asymptotic quanta. This basically results in stationary configurations. Equally stated, the oscillation frequency of the
oscillon is smaller than the asymptotic mass gap.

VII
Implications and Outlook

The goal of the present paper was to further investigate the physical nature and the extent of universality of the memory burden phenomenon
[
1
,
3
]
.
After introducing the essence and generic features of the phenomenon, we have studied its manifestations in solitons building up on the previous work
[
10
]
.

We have established a close correspondence with the expected features of the memory burden phenomenon in black holes. In this analysis, we took a double approach.
On one hand, following
[
1
,
3
]
,
we have modelled the memory burden
effect in black holes relying exclusively on their well-established features. These features unambiguously
place black holes in the category of objects of enhanced capacity
of information storage susceptible to the memory burden effect.

On the other hand, we used a microscopic theory of black hole’s quantum
\displaystyle N
-portrait
[
14
,
15
]
as the reference point for the consistency checks of our results.

The emerging picture speaks in favor of close similarities between black holes and other objects of enhanced capacity of information storage, such as the solitonic saturons
[
5
,
6
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

We have confronted the stabilization via memory burden with the stabilization by means of the classical hair and outlined the fundamental differences between the two mechanisms.

We have predicted that the memory burden effect
induces the mass splitting between initially-degenerate
black holes according to differences in their information patterns.

We have performed numerical analysis for various regimes of the solitonic memory burden, with or without the topological winding numbers. The results of these numerical simulations
fully confirm our analytic conclusions.

Let us now briefly go over some implications of the memory burden effect and the future prospects.

New dark matter window for PBH

As put forward in
[
3
]
, one immediate implication for black hole stabilization by the memory burden effect is the opening of a new window for PBH dark matter in the range of masses below
\displaystyle\sim 10^{14}
g.
The idea of PBH goes back to
[

[

However, in the standard treatment, in
which one extrapolates the Hawking
regime till the very end of black hole existence,
the PBH of masses below
\displaystyle\sim 10^{14}

The understanding that black holes undergo the memory burden effect

standard view. PBHs in the

\displaystyle 10^{14}

\displaystyle\sim 10^{8}
g)

[

Furthermore, the possibility of dark matter in

\displaystyle\sim 10^{4}\rm g
(corresponding to most conservative
value
\displaystyle k=1
in (

[
17

A further analysis of various constraints on light (
\displaystyle\lesssim 10^{4}\rm g

[
18

19

.
In particular, it was shown that PBHs that enter the memory burden phase prior to BBN epoch, are unconstrained from BBN and CMB. For the most conservative estimate of
the post memory-burden lifetime, corresponding to
\displaystyle k=1
in (
2

these are PBHs with masses below
\displaystyle 10^{9}
g. Such PBHs can easily compose the entirety of dark matter.
Further implications of the memory burden effect for PBHs

[

The analysis of the present paper predicts a spread in PBH dark matter masses, regardless of their production mechanism.
This is due to the mass-splitting of the remnants evolving from initially-degenerate black hole states, as shown in equation (

described by Fig.

Let us consider a black hole, formed at time
\displaystyle t_{\rm f}

\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}(t_{\rm f})

\displaystyle k>0

However, the mass of PBH at stabilization is determined by the

To the leading order, the burden is controlled by the occupation number of the memory modes,
\displaystyle N_{\rm G}

As we have discussed, statistically, the most probable value of this number is of the order of the PBH initial entropy
\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}

The probability of forming a black hole

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\ll S_{\rm BH}

\displaystyle N_{\rm G}\sim S_{\rm BH}

This translates as the corresponding spread in PBH masses via
(

In particular, PBHs of masses
\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}\lesssim 10^{14}{\rm g}

Implications for Inflationary Cosmology

As proposed in
[
22
,
24
]
,
the memory burden effect can be applicable to
cosmological space-times such as de Sitter or inflation.
This is due to the fact that, just like a black hole, a de Sitter Hubble patch of
radius
\displaystyle R
represents
a system of enhanced information capacity. The evidence for this is provided by Gibbons-Hawking entropy of de Sitter space
\displaystyle S_{\rm GH}

[
23
]
, which is very similar to the
Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of a black hole (
97
).

In fact, treated as a coherent state of gravitons constructed
on top of the Minkowski vacuum, the quantum portrait of
de Sitter
[
16
,
25
,
26
,
101
]
shares some key features
with a similar portrait of a black hole
[
14
]
.
For example, the Gibbons-Hawking radiation is a result of
the quantum depletion of the graviton coherent state.

It was suggested in
[
16
,
25
,
26
]

that the
back-reaction from this radiation necessarily leads
to a gradual loss of coherence and, subsequently, to a
complete breakdown of the classical description.
This happens after a so-called
quantum break-time
,
\displaystyle t_{\rm Q}
.

The concept of quantum break-time was originally introduced in
[
27
]
in the study of quantum evolution
in a prototype many-body model invoked in
[
15
]

as a toy analog of a black hole
\displaystyle N
-portrait
[
14
]
.
It was shown
[
27
]
that in the regime in which the system possesses a
Lyapunov exponent,
\displaystyle\lambda
, the quantum break-time can be logarithmically
short
\displaystyle t_{\rm Q}\sim\lambda^{-1}\ln(N)
, where

\displaystyle N
is the occupation number of constituents (in the present
language, a “master mode”).
The same system, in the classically-stable regime exhibits
a power-law break-time,
\displaystyle t_{\rm Q}\propto\sqrt{N}

[
48
]
.

The concept of quantum break-time was applied to de Sitter in
[
16
,
25
,
26
]
and
it was argued that for a cosmological constant source the
quantum break-time is bounded from above by
\displaystyle t_{\rm Q}\sim S_{\rm GH}R
. However, for a generic source, a shorter time-scale,

\displaystyle t_{\rm Q}\sim R\ln(S_{\rm GH})
, emerges.

In recent years, other aspects of the phenomenon of quantum breaking have been studied extensively in various setups
[
102
,
103
,
26
,

,

Now, based on the universality of the memory burden effect
[
1
]
, it was argued in
[
22
]

that the phenomenon must be operative in de Sitter and
must contribute into quantum breaking.

The idea of
[
22
]
is that de Sitter must posses memory modes
which are responsible for initially-degenerate microstates
accounted by the Gibbons-Hawking entropy.
It was concluded
that due to the depletion of the graviton coherent state via Gibbons-Hawking radiation
[
16
,
25
,
26
]
, the memory burden effect must set in,
latest, by the time
\displaystyle t_{\rm M}\sim S_{\rm GH}R

[
16
,
25
,
26
]
.
It is also very similar to the upper bound (
107
) on the memory burden time of a black hole.
I general, the memory burden effect is one of the main engines
of quantum breaking of de Sitter
[
22
,
24

The observational signatures of the Hubble memory burden effect still remain to be understood. In inflationary context, it is expected to create departures from the standard semi-classical spectrum of density perturbations, with the amplitude
increasing with the duration of inflation
[
22
,
24
]

In this way, the memory burden effect provides a quantum clock that records the entire duration of the inflationary phase.

Although the analysis of the present paper was not directly
intended at de Sitter, it nevertheless supports the
generic expected features previously proposed in
[
22
,
24
]
.
This motivates future studies of applications of the memory burden effect to various cosmological backgrounds.
