Learning about a changing state
Benjamin Davies

Abstract
A long-lived Bayesian agent observes costly signals of a time-varying state. He chooses the signals’ precisions sequentially, balancing their costs and marginal informativeness. I compare the optimal myopic and forward-looking precisions when the state follows a Brownian motion. I also compare the myopic precisions induced by other Gaussian processes. JEL classification : C61, D83 Keywords : information acquisition, Brownian motion, Gaussian processes



1
Introduction

This paper presents a tractible model of dynamic information acquisition.
I consider a long-lived Bayesian agent who buys independent signals of an unknown state.
In contrast to other models that assume the state is fixed, I allow it to vary over time.
This aligns my model with many real-world settings:

•

News consumption
:
How much attention should we pay to current events, given our knowledge of past events and of news cycle lengths?

•

Opinion polling
:
How many people should polling organizations survey, given their previous surveys and the persistence in people’s opinions?

•

Drug development
:
How much should laboratories invest in testing new vaccines, given their tests of previous vaccines and their beliefs about how viruses mutate over time?

•

Machine learning
:
How does “concept drift,” wherein the distribution of a target parameter changes over time, affect how much training data is needed and how often it is updated?

•

Intergenerational advice
:
How much can we learn from others’ experiences (e.g., from parents and mentors), given the difference between their context and ours?

Section
2
describes my model and discusses its assumptions.
The state follows the sample path of a Brownian motion with known drift and scale, but unknown initial value and increments.
The agent learns about the state by observing signals of its value at countably many times.
He uses these signals to form posterior beliefs and take actions with quadratic state-dependent costs.
The agent chooses the actions and signal precisions that minimize the sum of his expected action and information costs.
He makes these choices sequentially and myopically, based on the full history of previous signals but without regard for his future payoffs.

Section
3
analyzes the agent’s optimal myopic precisions.
Proposition
1
states that his optimal learning strategy has two stages: he waits until information is more beneficial than costly, then buys information at every opportunity to maintain his target posterior variance.

Sections
4
and
5
extend my model in different ways.
Section
4
augments the agent’s objective to include the present value of his future payoffs.
This preserves his optimal two-stage learning strategy, but changes the time he waits and precisions he chooses.
Intuitively, the more the agent cares about his future payoffs, the more information he buys because it allows him to buy less in the future while maintaining his target posterior variance.

Section
5
considers other state-generating processes.
It explains how to compute the optimal myopic precisions when the state follows
any
Gaussian process.
It then discusses two such processes: the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and a linear process.
The former induces similar learning dynamics to the Brownian motion: the agent may never buy information, but if he starts then he never stops.
In contrast, if the state is linear in time, and the time between signals is constant, then the agent always starts buying information eventually.
But he may also stop buying it eventually as he learns the state’s initial value and growth rate.

6
discusses related literature.
Section
7
concludes.
Appendix
A
contains proofs of my mathematical claims.

2
Model

A Bayesian agent receives signals
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots
of a time-varying state
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
.
He uses these signals to form posterior beliefs and take actions with state-dependent costs.
The agent chooses the signal precisions and actions sequentially to minimize their expected costs.

This section describes the agent’s learning and choice environment, and discusses my modeling assumptions.

2.1
The agent’s environment

Evolution of
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}

The state follows a Brownian motion with drift
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, scale
\sigma>0
, and initial value
\theta_{0}\equiv\theta(0)
.
It solves the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}\theta(t)=\mu\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W(t),

(1)

where
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is the realization a Wiener process.
This process has initial value
W(0)=0
, and iid normally distributed increments
\mathrm{d}W(t)\equiv\mathrm{d}W(t+\mathrm{d}t)-W(t)
with mean zero and variance
\mathrm{d}t
:

\mathrm{d}W(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{d}t).

The agent knows
\mu
and
\sigma
, but does not know
\theta_{0}
or
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
.
His prior specifies
\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
independently of
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Thus

\theta(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu t,\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t)

and

\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\min\{t,t^{\prime}\}

(2)

for all times
t\geq 0
and
t^{\prime}\geq 0
.

Learning about
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}

Each signal

s_{n}=\theta(t_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}

estimates the state’s value
\theta(t_{n})
at a time
t_{n}\geq 0
that is strictly increasing in
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
.
The error

\varepsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{1}{p_{n}}}}\right)

in this signal has mean zero, precision
p_{n}\equiv 1/\Var(\varepsilon_{n})
, and is independent of
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
and the errors in other signals.
The agent uses the set
\mathcal{I}_{n}\equiv\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of signals received by time
t_{n}
to form posterior beliefs about the realized path
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
via Bayes’ rule.

Choices and payoffs

At each time
t_{n}
, the agent chooses the precision
p_{n}\geq 0
and action
a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
that minimize

\E[(a_{n}-\theta(t_{n}))^{2}\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}]+cp_{n},

where
c>0
is the marginal cost of information.

1

The cost function

maps to a setting where the agent can buy any non-negative real number of iid signals at a constant per-unit price.
For example, if each signal has precision
1/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\geq 0
and price
\kappa>0
, then
m\geq 0

and cost
\kappa m=cp

.

2

Letting
c
vary with

does not change my analysis meaningfully.

His optimal action
a_{n}^{*}=\E[\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}]
equals the posterior mean of
\theta(t_{n})
given
\mathcal{I}_{n}
,
so his optimal precision
p_{n}^{*}
minimizes
3
3

3

Alternatively, the agent could minimize
\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})
subject to a budget constraint
cp_{n}\leq B
for some
B>0

(
Immorlica et al., 2021
, as in, e.g.,)
.
But then the agent would exhaust his budget, and his optimal precision would depend on the cost of new information but not the value of such information.
I highlight this tension between cost and value by including

in the agent’s objective.

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(p_{n})

\displaystyle\equiv\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E[\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}]-\theta(t_{n})}}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}}}\right]+cp_{n}

\displaystyle=\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})+cp_{n}.

Increasing
p_{n}
makes the signal
s_{n}
of
\theta(t_{n})
more expensive but more informative.
4
4

4

Thus
c
indexes the cost of information relative to the value of knowing
\theta(t_{n})
precisely.

The value of this information (i.e., the variance reduction it delivers) depends on previous signals’ precisions and on state’s variation over time.
This variation makes signals gradually lose explanatory power, making
\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})
more sensitive to recent signals’ precisions.

2.2
Discussion of modeling assumptions

Gaussian states and signals

I assume states
\{\theta(t_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and signals
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are jointly normally distributed with known means and variances.
Many related studies also make this assumption.
It allows me to derive closed-form expressions for the agent’s posterior beliefs.
It also makes posterior variances independent of signals’ realizations.
This means the agent knows the
ex post
value of the information he will receive before he receives it.
5
5

5

This would not be true if, e.g., states and signals were binary.
Then the states’ posterior variances would depend on signals’ realizations.

This knowledge is especially important when he internalizes how his choice of
p_{n}
affects his choices
p_{n+1},p_{n+2},\ldots
(see Section
4
).

Assuming
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion allows me to derive closed-form expressions for the optimal precisions
p_{n}^{*}
.
It captures how the state changes over time in response to a continuous stream of unpredictable “shocks” (e.g., news events or virus mutations).
Section
5
discusses the optimal precisions induced by other state-generating processes.

Quadratic payoffs

I assume actions
a_{n}
have quadratic costs
(a_{n}-\theta(t_{n}))^{2}
.
This makes optimal actions
a_{n}^{*}=\E[\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}]
have expected costs
\E[(a_{n}^{*}-\theta(t_{n}))^{2}\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}]=\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})
equal to posterior variances.
Many related studies also make this assumption.
It makes my model tractible and captures the intuition that information is more valuable when it yields more precise beliefs.

Frankel and Kamenica, 2019
formalize this intuition and extend it to other payoff structures.

Linear costs

I assume signals’ costs are linear in their precisions.

Pomatto et al., 2023
show that linear cost functions uniquely satisfy some attractive properties.
Such functions appear in many sequential sampling models
(
Morris and Strack, 2019
;
Wald, 1945
;
Wald, 1947
, e.g.,)
and their continuous-time analogues
(
Fudenberg et al., 2018
;
Liang et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
.
6
6

6

Non-linear cost functions also complicate my analysis.
For example, if a signal with precision
p\geq 0
has quadratic cost
cp+dp^{2}
with
c>0
and
d>0
, then the first-order condition for an optimal precision
p_{n}^{*}
is

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{R_{n}}+p_{n}^{*}}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{c+2dp_{n}^{*}}

with
R_{n}
defined as in Lemma
1
.
This condition is a cubic in
p_{n}^{*}
, with an
ex ante
unknown number of positive, real roots.
Moreover, these roots generically cannot be expressed as simple functions of the other parameters.

Countable signals

I assume the agent receives signals
s_{n}
at countably many times
t_{n}
.
This allows me to leverage results about finite collections of Gaussian variables (e.g., Lemmas
A.1
and
A.2
).
However, my analysis only requires the time steps
\Delta t_{n}\equiv t_{n}-t_{n-1}
to be strictly positive.
They could be arbitrarily small, allowing my model to approximate a setting where the agent receives information continuously.
If the
\Delta t_{n}
are constant in
n
, then the sequence
\{\theta(t_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a discrete-time random walk with Gaussian shocks (as studied, e.g., by
Dasaratha et al., 2023
and
Immorlica et al., 2021
).

Myopic preferences

I assume the agent is myopic in that his optimal precision
p_{n}^{*}
depends on the previous precisions
p_{1}^{*},p_{2}^{*},\ldots,p_{n-1}^{*}
but not the future precisions
p_{n+1}^{*},p_{n+2}^{*},\ldots
.
This makes my model tractible, and captures a setting where the agent
(i) does not know the times at which he will receive information in the future,
(ii) has cognitive limitations that prevent him from “planning ahead,” or
(iii) does not care about his future payoffs.
Section
4
compares the optimal precisions when the agent is myopic to those when he minimizes the present value of his current and future payoffs.
The myopic precisions are smaller because they ignore the future benefit of being more informed.
However, the agent’s optimal learning strategy does not differ qualitatively between the myopic and forward-looking cases.

3
Optimal myopic precisions

At each time
t_{n}
, the agent chooses the precision
p_{n}^{*}
that solves

\min_{p_{n}}\psi_{n}(p_{n})\ \text{subject to}\ p_{n}\geq 0

(3)

given his previous choices
p_{1}^{*},p_{2}^{*},\ldots,p_{n-1}^{*}
.
Lemma
1
characterizes
p_{n}^{*}
and the resulting posterior variance
\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let

R_{n}\equiv\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\{s_{n}\})

denote the variance in
\theta(t_{n})
that is not explained by the signals in
\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\{s_{n}\}
.
Then the unique solution

p_{n}^{*}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{R_{n}},0}}\right\}

(4)

to (
3
) yields posterior variance

\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})=\min\{R_{n},\sqrt{c}\}.

The “residual variance”
R_{n}
measures the agent’s knowledge of
\theta(t_{n})
before observing
s_{n}
.
It is smaller when the agent has more prior information.
Indeed, his posterior precision

\frac{1}{\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})}=\frac{1}{R_{n}}+p_{n}^{*}

equals the sum of his prior (to receiving
s_{n}
) and signal precisions.
If
R_{n}
is smaller than
\sqrt{c}
, then the agent does not buy more information about
\theta(t_{n})
because its cost outweighs its benefit.
Then

\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})\big\rvert_{p_{n}^{*}=0}=R_{n}

because the signal
s_{n}
is uninformative.
If
R_{n}
is larger than
\sqrt{c}
, then the agent buys more information and

\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})\big\rvert_{p_{n}^{*}>0}=\sqrt{c}.

Increasing
R_{n}
or decreasing
c
makes new information more net valuable, increasing the optimal precision
p_{n}^{*}
and decreasing the resulting posterior variance
\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})
.

Lemma
1
does not rely on
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
following a Brownian motion.
7
7

7

Neither does Lemma
1
rely on
\mathcal{I}_{n}
equaling
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
It holds whenever
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is a subset of
\{s_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
containing
s_{n}
.

It holds whenever the states
\theta(t_{1}),\theta(t_{2}),\ldots,\theta(t_{n})
and signals
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}
are jointly normally distributed.
This property obtains when
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Gaussian process.
The Brownian motion is one example of such a process.
I discuss other examples in Section
5
.

The Brownian motion is also a Markov process: its future values are conditionally independent of its past values given its current value.
Lemma
2
uses this Markov property to characterize the residual variance
R_{n}
in
\theta(t_{n})
given the signals in
\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\{s_{n}\}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion with scale
\sigma>0
and initial value
\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
.
Then

R_{n}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{1}&\text{if}\ n=1\\
\Var(\theta(t_{n-1})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n-1})+\sigma^{2}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})&\text{if}\ n>1.\end{cases}

(5)

The residual variance
R_{1}
equals the prior variance
\Var(\theta(t_{1}))=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{1}
because
\mathcal{I}_{1}\setminus\{s_{1}\}
is empty.
For each
n>1
, equation (
5
) decomposes the residual variance
R_{n}
in
\theta(t_{n})
into the sum of two terms:
the posterior variance
\Var(\theta(t_{n-1})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n-1})
in
\theta(t_{n-1})
given signals
s_{1}
through
s_{n-1}
, and
the variance in the integral

\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\mathrm{d}\theta(t)=\mu(t_{n}-t_{n-1})+\sigma\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\mathrm{d}W(t)

of the random increments between times
t_{n-1}
and
t_{n}
.
Intuitively, the residual variance
R_{n}
measures how accurately the agent can forecast
\theta(t_{n})
based on his knowledge of
\theta(t_{n-1})
.
His forecast is more accurate when
\Var(\theta(t_{n-1})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n-1})
and
\sigma^{2}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})
are smaller, since then his knowledge of the state is more precise and “up-to-date.”

Combining Lemmas
1
and
2
yields a closed-form expression for the optimal precision
p_{n}^{*}
:

Proposition 1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion with scale
\sigma>0
and initial value
\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
.
Define

\overline{t}\equiv\frac{\sqrt{c}-\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

If
p_{i}=p_{i}^{*}
for each
i<n
, then

p_{n}^{*}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ t_{n}\leq\overline{t}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{1}}&\text{if}\ n=1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}<t_{1}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{n}}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}<t_{n}\leq\overline{t}+(t_{n}-t_{n-1})\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}+\sigma^{2}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}+(t_{n}-t_{n-1})<t_{n}\end{cases}

(6)

and

\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})=\min\{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{n},\sqrt{c}\}.

The agent’s optimal learning strategy is as follows:
He does not buy any information until
t_{n}
exceeds
\overline{t}
.
Then he buys informative signals to maintain his target posterior variance
\sqrt{c}
.
8
8

8

If
\sigma=0
then the agent buys an informative signal at the first time
t_{n}\geq\overline{t}
, but every signal thenafter is uninformative because he can maintain his target posterior variance without buying any new information.

These signals’ precisions optimally balance the cost of information with the benefit of explaining some of the variance in the state that accumulates between times
t_{n-1}
and
t_{n}
.
This benefit is large enough to ensure
p_{n}^{*}>0
for all times
t_{n}>\overline{t}
.
Thus, the agent may wait arbitrarily long to start buying information, but once he starts he never stops.

If the timesteps
t_{n}-t_{n-1}=\Delta t>0
are constant in
n
, then the optimal “steady-state” precision

\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}+\sigma^{2}\Delta t}

and payoff

\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(p_{n}^{*})=2\sqrt{c}-\frac{c}{\sqrt{c}+\sigma^{2}\Delta t}

are both increasing in
\sigma^{2}\Delta t
.
Intuitively, if the state accumulates more variance between consecutive times, then the agent has to buy more information to obtain his target posterior variance
\sqrt{c}
.
This leaves him worse off.

Optimal forward-looking precisions

This section considers the case when the agent cares about his future payoffs.
At each time
t_{n}

p_{n},p_{n+1},\ldots\geq 0

\Psi_{n}((p_{i})_{i\geq n})\equiv\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\delta^{t_{i}-t_{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Var(\theta(t_{i})\mid\mathcal{I}_{i})+cp_{i}}}\right)

\delta\in(0,1)

9

9

For simplicity, I assume

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

Then (

.

10

10

This is because

\displaystyle R_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\Var(\theta(t_{i})\mid\mathcal{I}_{i})+\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{R_{i}}+p_{i}}}\right)^{-1}+\sigma^{2}

for each

So the dynamics of

via
R_{n}
.

,
11
11

11

Letting
t_{n+1}-t_{n}=\Delta t\not=1

This allows me to solve the agent’s problem

\min_{(p_{i})_{i\geq n}}\Psi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((p_{i})_{i\geq n}}}\right)\ \text{subject to}\ p_{i}\geq 0\ \text{for each}\ i\geq n

by modeling it as a Markov decision process

Proposition

characterizes the solution to (

It implies a similar optimal learning strategy to when the agent is myopic: he waits until information is more beneficial than costly, then buys more information at every opportunity.
But the time he waits to start buying and the amount he buys once he starts depend on his discount factor
\delta
.
Intuitively, the more the agent cares about his future payoffs, the more he is willing to pay for information because the more he benefits from being prior informed in the future.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion with scale
\sigma>0
and initial value
\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
.
Let
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+1
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
V>0

\frac{1}{c}=\frac{1}{V^{2}}-\frac{\delta}{(V+\sigma^{2})^{2}},

(9)

and define
\overline{t}\equiv(V-\sigma_{0}^{2})/\sigma^{2}
.
Then the sequence
(p_{n}^{\dagger})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

p_{n}^{\dagger}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ t_{n}\leq\overline{t}\\
\frac{1}{V}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{1}}&\text{if}\ n=1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}<t_{1}\\
\frac{1}{V}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{n}}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}<t_{n}\leq\overline{t}+1\\
\frac{1}{V}-\frac{1}{V+\sigma^{2}}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}+1<t_{n}\end{cases}

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
solves (

Moreover, if
p_{i}=p_{i}^{\dagger}
for each

\Var(\theta(t_{n})\mid\mathcal{I}_{n})=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t_{n},V}}\right\}.

The “steady-state” posterior variance

converges to
\sqrt{c}
from below as
\delta\to 0
.
In this limit, the forward-looking precision
p_{n}^{\dagger}
equals the myopic precision
p_{n}^{*}
.
But if
\delta>0

, and so

with equality if and only if

.
Indeed, differentiating (

0=-2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{V^{3}}-\frac{\delta}{(V+\sigma^{2})^{3}}}}\right)\frac{\partial V}{\partial\delta}-\frac{1}{(V+\sigma^{2})^{2}},

\partial V/\partial\delta<0

Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{n}^{\dagger}}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial\delta}\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ t_{n}\leq\overline{t}\\
\frac{1}{V^{2}}&\text{if}\ n=1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}<t_{1}\\
\frac{1}{V^{2}}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}<t_{n}\leq\overline{t}+1\\
\frac{1}{V^{2}}-\frac{1}{(V+\sigma^{2})^{2}}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \overline{t}+1<t_{n}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\geq 0

with equality if and only if
t_{n}\leq\overline{t}
.
So increasing
\delta
lowers the steady-state posterior variance
V
, making the agent better off, but raises the steady-state optimal precision
p_{n}^{\dagger}
, making him worse off.
The former effect dominates: differentiating the steady-state payoff

\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(p_{n}^{\dagger})=V+c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{V}-\frac{1}{V+\sigma^{2}}}}\right)

with respect to
\delta
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(p_{n}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{V^{2}}-\frac{1}{(V+\sigma^{2})^{2}}}}\right)}}\right]\frac{\partial V}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle\overset{\star}{=}\frac{c(1-\delta)}{(V+\sigma^{2})^{2}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\delta}

\displaystyle<0,

where
\star
holds by (
9
).
Thus, in the long run, the agent is better off at each time
t_{n}
when he cares more about his future selves.
This is because the cost of buying more information is smaller than the benefit of having more information bought by his
past
selves.
Thus, internalizing the future value of information makes the agent better off because he can “share the load” of staying up-to-date with his past selves.

5
Other state-generating processes

This section extends my analysis from Brownian motions to other Gaussian processes.
Such processes can be uniquely defined by

(i)

a function mapping times
t\geq 0
to prior means
\E[\theta(t)]
, and

(ii)

a function mapping pairs of times
t\geq 0
and
t^{\prime}\geq 0
to covariances
\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))
.

The Brownian motion described in Section
2
is the Gaussian process characterized by
\E[\theta(t)]=\mu t
and
\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\min\{t,t^{\prime}\}
.
Different covariance functions lead to different residual variances
R_{n}
and, by Lemma
1
, different optimal myopic precisions
p_{n}^{*}
.
This is because
R_{n}
depends on how the signals in
\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\{s_{n}\}
covary with each other and with the state’s value
\theta(t_{n})
.
Lemma
3
describes this dependence.
It defines
R_{n}
as a function of the state’s autocovariances and signals’ precisions when
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows an arbitrary Gaussian process.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Gaussian process.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the covariance matrix with
{ij}^{\text{th}}
entry

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G_{n}}}\right]_{ij}=\Cov(\theta(t_{i}),\theta(t_{j})),

and let
D_{n}\in(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\})^{n\times n}
be the diagonal matrix with
{ii}^{\text{th}}
entry

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[D_{n}}}\right]_{ii}=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if}\ i<n\ \text{and}\ p_{i}=0\\
1/p_{i}&\text{if}\ i<n\ \text{and}\ p_{i}>0\\
0&\text{if}\ i=n.\end{cases}

Then
R_{1}=\Var(\theta(t_{1}))
.
Moreover, if
n>1
and
(G_{n}+D_{n})
is invertible, then

\frac{1}{R_{n}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G_{n}+D_{n}}}\right)^{-1}}}\right]_{nn}.

Combining Lemmas
1
and
3
allows me to compute the optimal myopic precisions
p_{n}^{*}
recursively when
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows
any
Gaussian process.
The remainder of this section discusses two such processes.

5.1
The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Suppose
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process: it solves the SDE

\mathrm{d}\theta(t)=-\alpha\theta(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W(t),

where the parameter
\alpha>0
controls how quickly
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
reverts to its mean, and where
\sigma>0
and
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
are defined as in Section
2
.
Choosing
\alpha=\sigma^{2}/2\sigma_{0}^{2}
yields
\E[\theta(t)]=0
and

\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))=\sigma_{0}^{2}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert}}\right)

(10)

for all
t\geq 0
and
t^{\prime}\geq 0

(see
Karatzas and Shreve, 1988
, p. 358)
.
12
12

12

More generally, we have

\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))=\sigma_{0}^{2}\exp(-\alpha(t+t^{\prime}))+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exp(-\alpha\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert)-\exp(-\alpha(t+t^{\prime}))}}\right)

for all
\alpha>0
.
Then the prior variance

\Var(\theta(t))=\sigma_{0}^{2}\exp(-2\alpha t)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\exp(-2\alpha t)}}\right)

of
\theta(t)
is a weighted average of
\Var(\theta_{0})=\sigma_{0}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}/2\alpha
, and asymptotes to the latter as
t\to\infty
.
Letting
\alpha\to 0
yields the covariance function for the Brownian motion described in Section
2

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\alpha\sigma_{0}^{2}e^{-\alpha(t+t^{\prime})}+\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\alpha\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert}-e^{-\alpha(t+t^{\prime})}}}\right)}{2\alpha}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\sigma_{0}^{2}e^{-\alpha(t+t^{\prime})}-2\alpha(t+t^{\prime})\sigma_{0}^{2}e^{-\alpha(t+t^{\prime})}-\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert e^{-\alpha\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert}-(t+t^{\prime})e^{-\alpha(t+t^{\prime})}}}\right)}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((t+t^{\prime})-\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert}}\right)}{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}\min\{t,t^{\prime}\},

where the second equality holds by L’Hopital’s rule and the last inequality holds because
t+t^{\prime}-\lvert t-t^{\prime}\rvert=2\min\{t,t^{\prime}\}
.

This choice yields a stationary process with prior variances
\Var(\theta(t))=\sigma_{0}^{2}
that do not vary with
t
.
In contrast, if
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion then it is not stationary because then the prior variances
\Var(\theta(t))=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}t
grow with
t
.

Proposition
3
characterizes the optimal myopic precisions
p_{n}^{*}
when
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows an OU process.
These precisions are strictly positive if and only if the prior variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
exceeds the target posterior variance
\sqrt{c}
.
The agent either
(i) never buys information or
(ii) buys information at every opportunity.
He never waits to buy information because the prior variance never changes.
This contrasts with when
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion and the agent waits until information is more beneficial than costly to start buying it.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows an OU process with scale
\sigma>0
, initial value
\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
, and mean-reversion parameter
\alpha=\sigma^{2}/2\sigma_{0}
.
Define

\rho_{n}\equiv\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})}}\right)

(11)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
.
Then

p_{n}^{*}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \sqrt{c}\geq\sigma_{0}^{2}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}&\text{if}\ n=1\ \text{and}\ \sqrt{c}<\sigma_{0}^{2}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{\rho_{n}^{2}\sqrt{c}+(1-\rho_{n}^{2})\sigma_{0}^{2}}&\text{if}\ n>1\ \text{and}\ \sqrt{c}<\sigma_{0}^{2}\end{cases}

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The parameter (
11
) equals the Pearson correlation of
\theta(t_{n})
and
\theta(t_{n-1})
.
The square of this correlation measures how much of the variance in
\theta(t_{n})
is explained by the variance in
\theta(t_{n-1})
.
It is larger when
\sigma
and
(t_{n}-t_{n-1})
are smaller, and when
\sigma_{0}
is larger.
Increasing
\rho_{n}^{2}
makes the agent more able to forecast
\theta(t_{n})
using his estimate of
\theta(t_{n-1})
, leading him to demand less information and lowering his optimal precision
p_{n}^{*}
.

5.2
A linear process

If
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Brownian motion or an OU process with
\sigma_{0}^{2}>\sqrt{c}
, then the optimal myopic precision
p_{n}^{*}
remains bounded away from zero even as the number
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathcal{I}_{n}\setminus\{s_{n}\}}}\right\rvert=n-1
of previous signals grows without bound.
This is because the agent remains willing to buy information about shocks to the state between consecutive time steps.
However, if
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is linear in
t
, then
p_{n}^{*}
can become arbitrarily small as
n
becomes arbitrarily large.

To see why, suppose there is a linear relationship

\theta(t)=\theta_{0}+\beta t

between
\theta(t)
and
t\geq 0
, where
\theta_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
is an unknown intercept and
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is an unknown slope.
Then the state
\{\theta(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a Gaussian process with prior mean
\E[\theta(t)]=\mu t
and covariance

\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}tt^{\prime}

(12)

for all
t\geq 0
and
t^{\prime}\geq 0
.
13
13

13

This specification of
\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))
assumes
\theta_{0}
and
\beta
are independent.
I make this assumption for simplicity.

This process differs from the Brownian motion only in the specification of
\Cov(\theta(t),\theta(t^{\prime}))
, where the product of
t
and
t^{\prime}
replaces their minimum.
Yet this replacement leads to substantively different learning patterns.

6
Related literature

This paper fits into the literature on dynamic information acquisition.
Many papers in that literature
(
Bolton and Harris, 1999
;
Che and Mierendorff, 2019
;
Fudenberg et al., 2018
;
Hébert and Woodford, 2023
;
Liang et al., 2018
;
Liang et al., 2022
;
Moscarini and Smith, 2001
, e.g.,)
study the optimal strategy for learning about a fixed state before taking an action at a (possibly endogenous) time.
In contrast, this paper studies the optimal strategy for learning about a changing state before taking actions at many times.

Immorlica et al., 2021
also study such a strategy in a framework similar to mine.
But they focus on allocating a “precision budget” across possible action times, whereas I focus on the tension between the cost and value of information at every action time.

This focus is common in papers on rational inattention.
The closest such paper is by
Steiner et al., 2017
.
They consider an agent who observes arbitrary signals of a time-varying state (and its history) before taking actions at discrete times.

Steiner et al., 2017
assume the cost of each signal is proportional to the expected entropy reduction it delivers.
This cost function is prior-dependent, which complicates
Steiner et al., 2017
’s dynamic analysis because each signal changes future beliefs and, thus, future costs.
In contrast, I assume the cost of each signal is proportional to its precision.
This cost function is prior-independent, allowing me to solve the agent’s dynamic problem analytically (see Section
4
).
I also assume actions have quadratic state-dependent payoffs, whereas
Steiner et al., 2017
assume actions have arbitrary payoffs.
This makes
Steiner et al., 2017
’s analysis more general but less penetrable than mine.

My “Gaussian-quadratic” setup is decidedly tractible, which explains its use in many related papers.
Three such papers are by
Colombo et al., 2014
,
Hellwig and Veldkamp, 2009
, and
Myatt and Wallace, 2012
.
They study agents’ choices of how much to learn about a payoff-relevant state and the externalities these choices impose on other agents.
Likewise, the agent in my model makes choices that impose externalities on his future selves.
However, I allow the payoff-relevant state to vary across these selves.
Indeed, my model could also be used to study a setting where different agents want to learn different states.
I assume these states covary in a single dimension—time—but one could extend my analysis to states that covary in many dimensions.

This many-agent interpretation of my analysis connects it to the literature on social learning.
The closest paper in that literature is by
Dasaratha et al., 2023
.
They consider a sequence of agents who observe private signals and others’ actions before estimating a time-varying state.
They assume the state follows a discrete-time process, that signals are costless, and that agents have finite memories.
In contrast, I assume the state follows a continuous-time process, that signals are costly, and that the agent has an infinite memory.

7
Conclusion

This paper studies optimal learning about a time-varying state.
I assume the state follows a Gaussian process, and that a long-lived Bayesian agent receives Gaussian signals and takes actions with quadratic state-dependent costs.
These assumptions make my model tractible and yield simple expressions for the optimal signal precisions.
I discuss how these precisions depend on whether the agent is myopic or forward-looking, and on process driving the state’s evolution.

Natural extensions of my analysis are to non-Gaussian processes, multi-dimensional processes, and non-quadratic payoffs.
One could also consider an agent with mis-specified priors or limited memory.
I defer these extensions to future research.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

As stated in the text, the present value (
7
) depends on
n
only via the residual variance
R_{n}
(see Footnote
10

Let
\Psi(R_{n})
denote this present value when the agent chooses the precisions
p_{n},p_{n+1},\ldots
that solve (
8

Then

\displaystyle\Psi(R_{n})

\displaystyle=\min_{p_{n}\geq 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{R_{n}}+p_{n}}}\right)^{-1}+cp_{n}+\delta\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{R_{n}}+p_{n}}}\right)^{-1}+\sigma^{2}}}\right)}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{R_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial V_{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{V_{n}+c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{V_{n}}-\frac{1}{R_{n}}}}\right)+\delta\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{n}+\sigma^{2}}}\right)}}\right\}

.

\displaystyle=p_{n}^{\dagger}

induces posterior variance
