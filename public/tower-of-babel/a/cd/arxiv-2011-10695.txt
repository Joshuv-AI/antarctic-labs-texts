Sparse sketches with small inversion bias
Michał Dereziński, Zhenyu Liao, Edgar Dobriban, Michael W. Mahoney

Abstract
For a tall n × d n\times d matrix 𝐀 \mathbf{A} and a random m × n m\times n sketching matrix 𝐒 \mathbf{S} , the sketched estimate of the inverse covariance matrix ( 𝐀 ⊤ ​ 𝐀 ) − 1 (\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1} is typically biased: 𝔼 ⁡ [ ( 𝐀 ~ ⊤ ​ 𝐀 ~ ) − 1 ] ≠ ( 𝐀 ⊤ ​ 𝐀 ) − 1 \mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}]\neq(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1} , where 𝐀 ~ = 𝐒𝐀 \tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A} . This phenomenon, which we call inversion bias , arises, e.g., in statistics and distributed optimization, when averaging multiple independently constructed estimates of quantities that depend on the inverse covariance matrix. We develop a framework for analyzing inversion bias, based on our proposed concept of an ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -unbiased estimator for random matrices. We show that when the sketching matrix 𝐒 \mathbf{S} is dense and has i.i.d. sub-gaussian entries, then after simple rescaling, the estimator ( m m − d ​ 𝐀 ~ ⊤ ​ 𝐀 ~ ) − 1 (\frac{m}{m-d}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1} is ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -unbiased for ( 𝐀 ⊤ ​ 𝐀 ) − 1 (\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1} with a sketch of size m = O ⁡ ( d + d / ϵ ) m=O(d+\sqrt{d}/\epsilon) . In particular, this implies that for m = O ⁡ ( d ) m=O(d) , the inversion bias of this estimator is O ⁡ ( 1 / d ) O(1/\sqrt{d}) , which is much smaller than the Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \Theta(1) approximation error obtained as a consequence of the subspace embedding guarantee for sub-gaussian sketches. We then propose a new sketching technique, called LEverage Score Sparsified (LESS) embeddings, which uses ideas from both data-oblivious sparse embeddings as well as data-aware leverage-based row sampling methods, to get ϵ \epsilon inversion bias for sketch size m = O ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ d + d / ϵ ) m=O(d\log d+\sqrt{d}/\epsilon) in time O ⁡ ( nnz ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) ​ log ⁡ n + m ​ d 2 ) O({\mathrm{nnz}}(\mathbf{A})\log n+md^{2}) , where nnz {\mathrm{nnz}} is the number of non-zeros. 1
Introduction

Sketching has been widely used in the design of scalable algorithms,
perhaps most prominently in Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra
(RandNLA) due to applications in machine learning and data analysis.
In this approach, one randomly samples or computes a
random projection of the data matrix to construct a smaller matrix,
the sketch. One then uses the sketch as a surrogate to approximate
quantities of interest. The analysis of these methods typically
proceeds via a Johnson-Lindenstrauss-type argument to establish that
the geometry of the matrix is not perturbed too much under the sketching
operation. These methods have yielded state-of-the-art in
worst-case analysis, high-quality numerical implementations, and
numerous applications in machine learning
[
Mah11
,
HMT11
,
Woo14
,
DM16
,
DM18
]
.

In many cases, either to preserve the structure of the data or for algorithmic
reasons, one is interested in sparse sketches, i.e., random
transformations that are represented by matrices with most entries
exactly equal to zero. One class of sparse sketches includes row sampling
techniques, such as leverage score
sampling
[
DMM08
,
DMIMW12
,
MMY15
]
, which are
typically
data-aware
, in the sense that the sampling distribution depends on
the given data matrix. Another important class of
methods uses
data-oblivious
sparse embeddings, such as
the CountSketch
[
CCFC02
,
CW17
,
MM13
,
NN13
]
, to construct
sketches in time depending on the number of non-zeros (nnz) in the
input.

In all these cases, one can show that the sketch will be an
approximation of the solution with high probability. However,
comparatively little is known about the average performance of these
sketches.
In particular, there may be a systematic bias away from the solution,
which is problematic in many situations in statistics, machine
learning, and data analysis. Perhaps the most ubiquitous example of
this phenomenon is the systematic bias caused by matrix inversion, a
key component of algorithms in the aforementioned domains.
In this paper, we introduce the fundamental notion of inversion bias, which
provides a finer control over the sketched estimates involving
matrix inversion. We show that one can conveniently make the inversion bias
small with dense Gaussian and sub-gaussian sketches.
We also show that some sparse sketches do not have this desired property.
Then, we provide a non-trivial new construction and algorithm,
using ideas from both data-oblivious projections and data-aware
sampling, to get small inversion bias even for very sparse sketches.

1.1
Overview

Consider an
n\times d
data matrix
\mathbf{A}
of rank
d
, where
n\geq d
.
In many applications, we wish to approximate quantities of
the form
F((\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1})
, where
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
is the

d\times d
inverse data covariance and
F(\cdot)
is a linear
functional. Our goal is to provide a finer control over the effect of
matrix inversion on the quality of such estimates.
Here are some of the motivating examples:

•

The vector
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{b}
is
the solution of ordinary least squares (OLS) when
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{y}
for a
vector
\mathbf{y}
, arguably the most widely used multivariate statistical method

[
And03
,
Rao73
,
HTF09
]
,
and it is also crucial for the Newton’s method in numerical optimization

[
BV04
,
NW06
]
. In particular, accurate
approximations of this vector lead directly to improved convergence guarantees for
many optimization algorithms
[
PW16
,
WRXM18
]
.

•

The scalar
\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{x}
for a vector
\mathbf{x}
, has
numerous use-cases: When
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{a}_{i}
is one of the rows of
\mathbf{A}
, then it
represents the statistical leverage scores

[
DMM06
]
; If
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
is a standard basis vector,
then this is the squared length of the confidence interval for the

i
-th coefficient in OLS

[
And03
,
HTF09
]
.

•

The scalar
\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
for a matrix
\mathbf{C}
, is used to
quantify uncertainty in statistical results, e.g., via the mean
squared error (MSE) of estimating the regression coefficients in OLS

[
And03
,
HTF09
]
, and to
formulate widely used criteria from experimental design, e.g.,
A-designs and V-designs
[
Puk06
,
CR00
]
.

More generally,
our work is also motivated by the important problem of inverse
covariance estimation in statistics, machine learning, finance,
signal processing, and related areas

[
Dem72
,
MB06
,
FHT08
,
LF09
,
CLL11
,
LW12
,
MH15
]
. In this area, we wish to estimate statistically the
inverse covariance matrix of a population, or some of its
functionals, based on a
finite number of samples.
Furthermore, inverting covariance matrices
occurs in Bayesian statistics

[
Har69
,
GCS+13
]
, Gaussian processes

[
Ras03
]
, as well as time series analysis and control,
e.g., via the Kalman filter

[
WB95
,
BD09
]
.

When
n
and
d
are large, and particularly
when
n\gg d
, then the costs of storing the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and of
computing
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
are prohibitively large.
Matrix sketching has proven successful at drastically reducing these
costs by approximating the inverse covariance with a sketched estimate

(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}
based on a smaller matrix

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
, where
\mathbf{S}
is a random
m\times n
matrix and
m\ll n

[
Mah11
,
HMT11
,
Woo14
,
DM16
,
DM18
]
. As a concrete algorithmic motivation for our work, consider the following popular strategy for
boosting the quality of such estimates:
Construct multiple copies in parallel, based on independent sketches,
and then average the estimates. This strategy is especially useful in distributed architectures,
where storage and computing resources are
spread out across many
machines, and has commonly appeared in the literature
[
KBRR16
,
KBY+16
,
WRXM18
,
DM19
]
. While promising in practice, this
averaging technique is fundamentally limited by the
inversion bias
: even
though the sketched covariance estimate is unbiased,

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}]=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
, its inverse in general is not unbiased, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}]\neq(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
.
When the sketch size
m
is not much larger than the dimension
d
, the
size of this bias can be very significant, even as large as the
approximation error, in which case averaging becomes ineffective. Motivated by this, we ask:

When is the inversion bias small, relative to the
approximation error?

In this paper, we develop a framework for analyzing the inversion bias
of sketching, via the notion of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-unbiased
estimator (Definition
3
), and
we show how it can be used to provide improved
approximation guarantees for averaging. Through this framework, we
provide several contributions towards addressing the above question.

Sub-gaussian sketches have small inversion bias.

Arguably the most classical family of sketches consists of dense random
matrices
\mathbf{S}
with i.i.d. sub-gaussian entries. These sketches offer strong
relative error approximation guarantees via the so-called
subspace embedding

property, at the expense of high computational cost
of the matrix product
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
. We show that, upon a simple
correction, sub-gaussian sketches
are nearly-unbiased, i.e., their inversion bias is much smaller than the approximation error, which
means that averaging can be used to significantly improve the
approximation quality.
In particular, we show that, after a simple scalar
rescaling, the inverse covariance
estimator of the form
(\frac{m}{m-d}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}

achieves
\epsilon
inversion bias relative to
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}

with a sketch of size only
m=O(d+\sqrt{d}/\epsilon)

(Proposition
4
).
In contrast, to ensure that

(\frac{m}{m-d}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}
is an

{\eta}
relative error approximation of
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
via
the subspace embedding property, we need a sub-gaussian sketch of
size
m=\Theta(d/{\eta}^{2})
, which is comparatively larger if we let
\eta=\Theta(\epsilon)
. This implies that
an aggregate estimator obtained via averaging can with high probability produce
a relative error approximation that is by a factor of
O(1/\sqrt{m})

better than the approximation error offered by any one of the
estimators being averaged.

LEverage Score Sparsified (LESS) embeddings.

We show that existing algorithmically efficient sketching techniques
may not provide guarantees for the inversion bias that match those satisfied
by dense sub-gaussian sketches (see Theorem
10
for a lower bound
on leverage score sampling, and a discussion of other methods in
Appendix
C.3
). To address this, we propose a
new family of sketching methods, called LEverage
Score Sparsified (LESS) embeddings, which combines a data-oblivious sparsification
strategy reminiscent of the CountSketch with the data-aware
approach of approximate leverage score sampling. LESS embeddings
have time complexity
O({\mathrm{nnz}}(\mathbf{A})\log n+md^{2})
and achieve

\epsilon
inversion bias with the sketch of size
m=O(d\log d+\sqrt{d}/\epsilon)
, nearly matching our guarantee for sub-gaussian sketches
(Theorem
8
). Thus, our new algorithm provides a promising way to address
the fundamental problem of inversion bias, and it may have many other
applications in the future. Finally, our analysis reveals two structural
conditions for small inversion bias (Theorem
11
), one
of which (Condition
2
, called the Restricted Bai-Silverstein condition) leads
to a generalization of a classical inequality used in random matrix
theory, and should be of independent interest.

Dense Gaussian and sub-gaussian sketches have small inversion bias

Consider first the classical Gaussian sketch, i.e., where the entries of
\mathbf{S}
are
i.i.d. standard normal scaled by
1/\sqrt{m}
. In this special case, the
sketched covariance matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is
a Wishart-distributed random matrix, and we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}\big]=\tfrac{m}{m-d-1}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\quad\text{for}\quad m\geq d+2.

(1)

In other words, even though the sketched inverse covariance is not an
unbiased estimate, the bias can be corrected by simply scaling
the matrix, after which averaging can be used effectively without
encountering any inversion bias.

The key property which enables exact bias-correction for the Gaussian
sketch is
orthogonal invariance
. This property
requires that for any orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{O}
, the distributions of
the random matrices
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{O}
are identical. An example beyond
Gaussians are Haar sketches, which are uniform over all partial
orthogonal matrices. If a sketch
\mathbf{S}
is orthogonally invariant and

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is invertible with probability one, then we
can show that the inversion bias can be corrected exactly, in that,
(
1
) holds with some constant factor
c
(replacing
the factor
\frac{m}{m-d-1}
) that depends on the distribution of the sketch (see
Proposition
38
in Appendix
G
).

Exact bias-correction, achieved by the Gaussian
sketch and other orthogonally invariant sketches, is no longer
possible for general sub-gaussian sketches. Here, we consider
sketching matrices with i.i.d. entries that (after scaling by
\sqrt{m}
) have
O(1)
sub-gaussian
Orlicz norm. Consider for example the so-called Rademacher sketch,
with
\mathbf{S}
consisting of scaled i.i.d. random sign entries (which is useful for reducing the
cost of randomness relative to the Gaussian sketch). In this case, an
exact bias-correction analogous to (
1
) is
clearly infeasible for any
d>1
, simply because, with some positive
(but exponentially small) probability, the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

will be non-invertible, making the expectation undefined.
Yet, any task where we observe at most polynomially many independent
estimates (such as averaging)
should not be affected by such low-probability events,
so we need a notion of near-unbiasedness that is robust to
this. To that end, we first recall a standard definition of a relative
error approximation for a positive semi-definite matrix.

Definition 1

(Relative error approximation)
.

A positive semi-definite (p.s.d.) matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
(or a non-negative scalar) is an

{\eta}
-approximation of
\mathbf{C}
, denoted as

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\approx_{\eta}\mathbf{C}
, if

\mathbf{C}/(1+{\eta})\preceq\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\preceq(1+{\eta})\cdot\mathbf{C}.

If
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
is random and the above holds with
probability
1-\delta
, then we call it an

({\eta},\delta)
-approximation.

Remark 2

(Subspace embedding)
.

If
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
where

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is a sketch of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, then
the condition

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\approx_{\eta}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
is called the
subspace embedding property with error
\eta
.

For instance, any sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
with i.i.d.
O(1)
sub-gaussian random entries, of size

m=O((d+\ln(1/\delta))/{\eta}^{2})
, where
{\eta}\in(0,1)
, ensures
that
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
with probability
1-\delta
satisfies the subspace embedding property with
error
{\eta}
. In other words,
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is an

({\eta},\delta)
-approximation of
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
(This is known to
be tight; see, e.g.,
[
NN14
]
). As a
consequence, the same guarantee applies to the inverse

(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}
, relative to
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
.
The
\delta
failure probability makes this definition robust to the rare
events where
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is not invertible. It is natural
to desire a similar robustness in the definition of
near-unbiasedness. We achieve this as follows.

Definition 3

(
(\epsilon,\delta)
-unbiased estimator)
.

A random p.s.d. matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
is an

(\epsilon,\delta)
-unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{C}
if there is an event

\mathcal{E}
that holds with probability
1-\delta
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathcal{E}\big]\approx_{\epsilon}\mathbf{C},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\preceq O(1)\cdot\mathbf{C}\quad\text{when
conditioned on $\mathcal{E}$.}

Note that this definition only becomes meaningful if we use it
with an
\epsilon
that is much smaller than the approximation error
{\eta}
in
Definition
1
(for instance, we will often have

{\eta}=\Omega(1)
and
\epsilon\ll 1
).
Further, note the following two important aspects of
Definition
3
. First, instead of a simple
expectation, we condition on some high probability
event
\mathcal{E}
, which, similarly as in Definition
1
,
allows robustness against such corner cases as when
the sketch
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is not invertible. Second,
conditioned on the event
\mathcal{E}
, in addition to an

\epsilon
-approximation holding in expectation, we require a weaker upper
bound to hold almost surely, in terms of the target matrix
\mathbf{C}

scaled by some constant factor.
This condition is important to
guard against certain corner cases where the probability mass is
extremely skewed. For instance, suppose that
\tilde{C}
is a scalar random variable which is uniform over
[0,1]
and has an
additional probability mass of
10^{-10}
at the value

10^{100}
. Here, averaging will not prove effective at converging to
the true expectation of
\tilde{C}
, but we could still use the notion of

(\epsilon,\delta)
-unbiasedness to show that the average of
an appropriately chosen number (much smaller than
10^{10}
) of i.i.d. copies will
converge very close to
0.5
, by choosing an event
\mathcal{E}
that avoids
the
10^{100}
(see Appendix
E
).

We are now ready to state our main result for sub-gaussian sketches
(this is in fact a corollary of our more general result,
Theorem
11
, discussed in Section
4
), which
asserts that after proper rescaling, not
only the Gaussian sketch, but in fact all sub-gaussian sketches
(including the Rademacher sketch) enjoy small inversion bias.

Proposition 4

(Near-unbiasedness of sub-gaussian sketches)
.

Let

be an

(\epsilon,\delta)

Observe that the scaling

Corollary 5

Let

3
Main results: Less inversion bias with LESS embeddings

To address the high computational cost of sub-gaussian sketches, while
preserving their good near-unbiasedness properties,
we propose a new family of sketches, which we call LEverage Score Sparsified (LESS)
embeddings. A LESS embedding is defined simply as a sparsified
sub-gaussian sketch, where the sparsification is designed so as to ensure
small inversion bias for a particular matrix
\mathbf{A}
. Our
approach combines ideas from approximate leverage
score sampling (which is data-aware) with ideas from
sparse embedding matrices (which are normally data-oblivious).
Importantly, neither strategy by itself is sufficient to ensure
small inversion bias (see our lower bound in Theorem
10
and
discussion in Section
4
). Each row of a LESS embedding is
sparsified independently using a sparsification pattern defined as
follows. Recall that for a tall full rank matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we use
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
to denote the
i
th row of

\mathbf{A}
, and the
i
th leverage score of
\mathbf{A}
is defined as

l_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}
.

Definition 6

(LESS: LEverage Score Sparsified embedding)
.

Fix a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
of rank
d
with leverage scores
l_{1},...,l_{n}
, and let

s_{1},...,s_{d}
be sampled i.i.d. from a
probability distribution
(p_{1},...,p_{n})

p_{i}\approx_{O(1)}l_{i}/d
for all

. Then, the
random vector
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\big(\sqrt{\!\frac{b_{1}}{dp_{1}}},...,\sqrt{\!\frac{b_{n}}{dp_{n}}}\big)
, where

b_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}1_{[s_{t}=i]}
, is called a
leverage score sparsifier
for
\mathbf{A}
.

Sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
is a
LESS embedding
of size
m

for a matrix
\mathbf{A}
, if it consists of
m
i.i.d. row vectors distributed as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, where
\circ
denotes an entry-wise

\mathbf{x}
is a random vector with i.i.d. mean zero, unit
variance,
O(1)

4
Our techniques: Structural conditions for near-unbiasedness

In order for our analysis of inversion bias to apply to a wide range
of sketching techniques, we give two key structural
conditions for a random sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
that are sufficient to achieve provably
small inversion bias. The first is the subspace
embedding property discussed in Remark
2
,
which we now use as one of the key conditions needed in our analysis.

Condition 1

(Subspace embedding)
.

The (sketching) matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

satisfies the subspace embedding condition with
\eta\geq 0

for a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, if

\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\approx_{\eta}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
.

The second structural condition for small inversion bias is a property
of each individual row of
\mathbf{S}
. We use an
n
-dimensional random
row vector
\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
to denote the marginal distribution of a row
of
\mathbf{S}
(after scaling by
\sqrt{m}
).
This condition represents a key novelty in our analysis.

Condition 2

(Restricted Bai-Silverstein)
.

The random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies the Restricted Bai-Silverstein
condition with
\alpha>0
for a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, if

\mathrm{Var}\!\big[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\big]\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})

for all p.s.d. matrices
\mathbf{B}
such that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{P}
, where
\mathbf{P}
is the
projection onto the column span of
\mathbf{A}
.

Based on these two structural conditions, we show the following
result, which we use to prove both Proposition
4
and
Theorem
8
. In this result, we will refer to an
m\times n
sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}_{m}
, indexed by the number of rows
m
.

Theorem 11

(Structural conditions for near-unbiasedness)
.

Fix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with rank
d
and let
\mathbf{S}_{m}

consist of
m\geq 8d
i.i.d. rows distributed as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, where
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]=\mathbf{I}_{n}
.
Suppose that
\mathbf{S}_{m/3}
satisfies
Condition
1
(subspace embedding) for
\eta=1/2
, with probability

1-\delta/3
, where
\delta\leq 1/m^{3}
.
Suppose also that

\mathbf{x}
satisfies Condition
2
(Restricted Bai-Silverstein) with
some
\alpha\geq 1
.
Then
(\frac{m}{m-d}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
is an

(\epsilon,\delta)
-unbiased estimator of
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
for

\epsilon=O(\alpha\sqrt{d}/m)
.

The proof of Theorem
11
adapts and extends techniques
for analyzing the limiting Stieltjes transform for high-dimensional
random matrices in the so-called Marchenko-Pastur regime (also called the proportional or mean-field limit).
This
regime arises if we let
n
,
m
and
d
all go
to infinity and let the ratio
m/d
converge to a fixed constant
larger than unity. Crucially, our analysis is non-asymptotic, and it is
not restricted to the constant aspect ratio between the sketch size
and the dimension. Further, while classical random matrix theory
analysis considers matrix resolvents, which take the form

(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}\mathbf{A}+z\mathbf{I})^{-1}
for
z,\gamma\neq 0
, and are
well-defined with full probability, we consider the case of
z=0
where
the matrix in question may be undefined with positive probability.
We address this by defining a high probability event which ensures that the sketch
(\frac{m}{m-d}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
is well-defined and bounded, while
preserving enough of the independence structure in the conditional
distribution for the expectation analysis to go through. Specifically,
we split the sketch into three parts, and we condition on the event that each part
satisfies the subspace embedding property. This way, for any pair of
rows, there is a part of the sketch that ensures invertibility while
being independent from the two rows, which is important for the analysis.

Subspace embedding condition.

Our first structural condition for small inversion bias
(Condition
1
)
is a variant of the subspace embedding property, which is standard in the sketching
literature.
In particular, this condition immediately implies that
(\frac{m}{m-d}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{m}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
is with probability
1-\delta

O(1)

To provide some intuition behind this result, consider the variance

\mathbf{S}

By setting
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, where all leverage scores are 1 and the
restriction on
\mathbf{B}
is vacuous, we not only recover the statement of Lemma

13
, but also our new analysis uses the
Perron-Frobenius theorem to obtain a tight constant factor in the bound
(see Appendix
C
). However, when
\mathbf{A}
is a tall
matrix, then the fourth-moment assumption becomes potentially
much more broadly applicable (for example, when the leverage scores are uniform, we only need

\mathbb{E}x_{i}^{4}\leq C\cdot n/d
). In particular, consider an
i.i.d. sub-gaussian random vector
\mathbf{x}
sparsified as follows:

\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, where we let
\xi_{i}=b_{i}/\sqrt{l_{i}}
and

b_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(l_{i})
. Then, the entries satisfy the
assumptions of Theorem
14
, with
expected number of non-zeros equal to
d
. Note that this is different
than the data-oblivious sparsification discussed above, since the
entries of the vector corresponding to large leverage scores are less
likely to be zeroed-out than others. This form of sparsification
is nearly equivalent to the one we use for our LESS embeddings (see Definition

6
; our
analysis can be applied to either variant), except that it leads to a
non-deterministic level of sparsification. In
Appendix
C.2
we prove the Restricted Bai-Silverstein
condition with
\alpha=O(1)
for a leverage score sparsified vector
constructed as in Definition
6
, which has
non-independent entries.

6
Conclusions

We analyzed the phenomenon of inversion bias in sketching-based
estimation tasks involving the inverse covariance matrix. Inversion
bias is a significant bottleneck in methods that use parallel and
distributed averaging. We showed that certain classical sketching
methods (such as sub-gaussian sketches) have small inversion bias,
while many algorithmically efficient sketches (such as leverage score
sampling) may not provide such a guarantee. Finally, we developed a
new efficient sketching method, called LEverage Score Sparsified
(LESS) embeddings, which has small inversion bias and
its computational cost is nearly-linear in the input size.

Estimation of the inverse covariance matrix and its various linear
functionals is motivated by a rich body of literature in statistics,
data science, numerical optimization, machine learning, signal
processing, etc., which we summarized in detail in
Section
1.1
. Here, we additionally remark that
the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximation guarantee we provide for the averaged estimates of
the inverse covariance (see Corollaries
5
and
9
)
immediately implies corresponding approximation guarantees for linear
functionals of the inverse covariance in numerous tasks. In a
distributed environment, one can use this to build a system for
querying such functionals, by aggregating coarse estimates computed locally from

q
sketches to produce an improved global estimate with
minimal communication cost. We
illustrate this here for a family of linear functionals of the form

\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
, parameterized
by any p.s.d. matrix
\mathbf{C}
, as motivated by applications in statistical
inference (see Section
1.1
). The claim follows from
Corollary
9
by letting
\mathbf{Q}_{i}=(\frac{m}{m-d}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
.

Corollary 15

(Querying linear functionals)
.

For any matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, we can use LESS
embeddings of size
m=O(d\log(d/\epsilon\delta)+\sqrt{d}/\epsilon)
to
construct
\mathbf{Q}_{1},...,\mathbf{Q}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
in parallel
time
O({\mathrm{nnz}}(\mathbf{A})\log n+md^{2})
, where
q=O(m\log^{2}(d/\delta))
, so that with probability
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\text{For all p.s.d. matrices $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$,}\qquad\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{i}\,\approx_{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}.

In the context of distributed optimization, our results can be directly applied to
show improved convergence guarantees, for instance, in the case of the
Distributed Iterative Hessian Sketch algorithm

[
PW16
,
DBPM20
]
and Distributed
Newton Sketch method

[
WRXM18
,
DM19
]
. Here, the quantity
of interest is of the form
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{b}
for some vector
\mathbf{b}

(where
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
corresponds to the Hessian and
\mathbf{b}
corresponds to
the gradient). For those methods, an
\epsilon
-approximation guarantee
for the average of the sketched inverse covariance matrices, as in
Corollaries
5
and
9
, directly implies
that the iterates
\mathbf{x}_{t}
produced by the algorithms achieve a
convergence rate of the form
\Delta_{t}\leq O(\epsilon^{t})\cdot\Delta_{0}
, where
\Delta_{t}

represents distance from the optimum in the
t
-th iteration. We
illustrate this by applying Corollary
9

to the existing analysis of Distributed Newton Sketch, as
outlined in Section 4 of
[
DBPM20
]
, obtaining an
improved linear-quadratic convergence rate for distributed empirical risk
minimization.

Corollary 16

(Distributed Newton Sketch)
.

Consider a twice differentiable convex function of the form
f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\phi_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\phi_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
is the

i
th row of an
n\times d
data matrix
\Phi
. Given
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we
can use LESS embeddings to construct
q
independent randomized estimates

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),...,\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{q}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{t})
in
parallel time
O({\mathrm{nnz}}(\Phi)\log n+md^{2})
, where

m=O(d\log(d/\epsilon\delta)+\sqrt{d}/\epsilon)
and
q=O(m\log^{2}(d/\delta))
, so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{t})^{-1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\quad\text{with probability $1-\delta$ satisfies:}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\max\big\{\epsilon\cdot\sqrt{\kappa}\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|,\ \tfrac{2L}{\lambda_{\min}}\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}\big\}\quad\text{for}\quad\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}),

where
\kappa
,
L
,
\lambda_{\min}
are the condition number, Lipschitz constant and smallest eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})
.

This result provides an improvement over the recently proposed DPP-based sketching methods of

[
DBPM20
]
, which suffer no inversion bias but are
more expensive, as well as over other fast sketching methods like row
sampling
[
WRXM18
]
, which, as shown
in this work, may indeed suffer from large inversion bias.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

Notations.

In the remainder of the article, we follow the
convention of denoting scalars by lowercase, vectors by lowercase
boldface, and matrices by uppercase boldface letters. The norm
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm for vectors and the spectral or
operator norm for matrices, and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm
for matrices. For vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|v_{i}|
denote the
\ell_{1}
norm and
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i}|v_{i}|
denote the

\ell_{\infty}
norm of
\mathbf{v}
. We use
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
to denote the
maximum eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
. We say
\mathbf{A}\preceq\mathbf{B}

if and only if
\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}
is positive semi-definite. We use
\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}
to denote the entry-wise Hadamard product of matrices or
vectors. For random vectors or matrices, we say
\mathbf{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{B}
if
\mathbf{A}

follows the same distribution as
\mathbf{B}
. For positive
semi-definite (p.s.d.) matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, or non-negative scalars

a
and
b
, we use
\mathbf{A}\approx_{\eta}\mathbf{B}
and
a\approx_{\eta}b
to
denote the relative error approximation (Definition

1
). The big-O notation is used to absorb constant
factors in upper bounds, where the constant only depends on other big-O
constants appearing in a given statement (thus, all constants
can be made absolute).

An important linear algebraic result that will be used in proving the
restricted Bai-Silverstein inequality
(Theorem
14
) is the following
Perron-Frobenius theorem on non-negative matrices. While the most well known version of the Perron-Frobenius theorem concerns matrices with strictly positive entries, there is also a version for matrices with only non-negative entries.

Lemma 17

(Perron-Frobenius theorem,
[
Mey00
, claims 8.3.1 and 8.3.2]
)
.

For a non-negative symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
[\mathbf{A}]_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
is non-negative, i.e.,
r=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})\geq 0
. Moreover, there is a corresponding eigenvector
\mathbf{z}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=r\mathbf{z}
with non-negative entries
\mathbf{z}_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
.

Our analysis of the inversion bias (proof of Theorem

11
) crucially relies on a standard
rank-one update formula for the matrix inverse, which is given below.

Lemma 18

(Sherman-Morrison formula)
.

For an invertible matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
is invertible if and only if
1+\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\neq 0
. If this holds, then

(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}.

In particular, it follows that:

(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{-1}\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}{1+\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}.

Our proofs rely on different types of concentration and
anti-concentration inequalities, from scalars to quadratic forms of the
type
\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}
, and eventually to matrix concentration
bounds. These technical lemmas are collected in this section and will
be repeatedly used in the proofs of our main results.

A.1
Scalar concentration and anti-concentration inequalities

The Burkholder inequality
[
Bur73
]
provides
moment bounds on the sum of a martingale difference sequence. It is
used to show Lemma
25
as part of the proof of
Theorem
11
.

Lemma 19

(Burkholder inequality,
[
Bur73
]
)
.

For
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
a real martingale difference sequence with
respect to the increasing
\sigma
field
\mathcal{F}_{j}
, we have, for

L>1
, there exists
C_{L}>0

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}\Big|^{L}\bigg]\leq C_{L}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|x_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{L/2}\bigg].

The Paley-Zygmund inequality is used to establish an
anti-concentration inequality for the Binomial distribution
(Lemma

deriving a lower bound for the inversion bias of leverage score

F

Lemma 20

(Paley-Zygmund inequality,
[
PZ32

)

For any non-negative variable

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big(Z\geq\theta\,\mathbb{E}[Z]\big)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]}.

A.3
Matrix concentration inequalities

When random matrices are considered, different variants of Matrix Chernoff/Bernstein inequalities will be needed to handle the case where the random matrix under study is known to have (almost surely) bounded operator norm, or only to admit a subexponential decay for its higher order moments.

Lemma 22

(Matrix Bernstein: Bounded Case,
[
Tro12
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{X}_{i}

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}]=\mathbf{0},\quad\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\leq R\quad\text{almost surely.}

\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}^{2}]\|

we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big)\geq t\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Rt/3}\right).

Lemma 23

(Matrix Bernstein: Subexponential Case,
[
Tro12
, Theorem 6.2]
)
.

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{X}_{i}
of
d\times d

independent and symmetric random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}]=\mathbf{0},\quad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}^{p}]\preceq\frac{p!}{2}\cdot R^{p-2}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\quad\textmd{for}\quad p=2,3,...

Then, defining the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\|
, for any

we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big)\geq t\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Rt}\right).

Lemma 24

(Matrix Chernoff,
[
Tro12
, Theorem 1.1 and Remark 5.3]
)
.

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{X}_{i}
of
d\times d

independent positive semi-definite random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\big]=\mathbf{I}
and
\|\mathbf{X}_{i}\|\leq R
. Then, for any
t\geq\mathrm{e}
,
we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{\Big\|\sum_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big\|\geq t\Big\}\leq d\cdot\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}}{t}\Big)^{t/R}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{j}:\ \frac{1}{t}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Big(\sum_{i=t(j-1)+1}^{tj}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Big)\mathbf{A}\succeq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A},\quad j=1,2,3,\qquad\mathcal{E}=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{3}\mathcal{E}_{j}.

(2)

For each

\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|

x\geq 2d
we have

\mathrm{Pr}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\geq x\mid\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\leq C\alpha d/x^{2}
.
Combining this with the assumption that
\delta_{3}\leq\delta\leq 1/m^{3}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\big[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{E}}\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Pr}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{E}}\geq x\mid\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\,dx

\displaystyle\leq 2m^{2}\delta_{3}+\int_{2m^{2}}^{\infty}\mathrm{Pr}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\geq x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{m}+C\alpha d\int_{2m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,dx\leq\frac{2}{m}+C\frac{\alpha d}{m^{2}},

which implies that
\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=O(1/m+\alpha d/m^{2})=O(\alpha\sqrt{d}/m)
.
We now move on to bounding the second term in (
3
).
In the following, we will use the observation that for a p.s.d. random
matrix
\mathbf{C}
(or non-negative random variable) in the probability space of
\mathbf{S}_{m}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{C}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\prod_{j=1}^{3}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{j}})\cdot\mathbf{C}]}{\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{E})}\preceq\frac{1}{1-\delta}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{C}]\preceq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{C}].

(4)

Using the above, and the fact that event
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is independent of

\mathbf{x}_{i}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}-\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle\preceq 2\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}-\mathbf{Q}]=\frac{2\gamma}{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\big[\gamma_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\big]\preceq\frac{2\gamma}{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}].

We now bound the second term in (
3
) by using the
fact that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
implies

\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\preceq 6\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|=\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}-\mathbf{Q}]\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\leq\frac{2\gamma}{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\big[\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\big]\leq\frac{2}{m}\cdot 36.

We next bound the last term in (
3
), applying
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality twice:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,~\|\mathbf{u}\|=1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\Big[\left|\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma_{i}}-1\right|\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma_{i}}-1)^{2}\big]}\cdot\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,~\|\mathbf{u}\|=1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u})^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\gamma_{i}-\gamma)^{2}\big]}}_{T_{1}}\cdot\underbrace{\sup_{\|\mathbf{u}\|=1}\sqrt[4]{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{4}\big]}}_{T_{2}}\cdot\underbrace{\sup_{\|\mathbf{u}\|=1}\sqrt[4]{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{4}\big]}}_{T_{3}}.

To bound

, we rely on Restricted Bai-Silverstein with

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}

\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B})=(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u})^{2}=\|\mathbf{u}\|^{4}=1
.
Recall
that event
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is independent of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, so we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{4}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\big[(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{4}\big]

\displaystyle=2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{i})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{i}]+2\big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{i}]\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})+2\big(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B})\big)^{2}=2(\alpha+1),

obtaining that
T_{3}=O(\sqrt[4]{\alpha+1})
. We can similarly bound
T_{2}
by letting

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
. Note
that, conditioned on
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, we again have

\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})=\big(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}\mathbf{u}\big)^{2}\leq 6^{4},

so analogously as above we conclude
that
T_{2}=O(\sqrt[4]{\alpha+1})
.

It thus remains to bound the term
T_{1}
. First, note that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[(\gamma-\gamma_{i})^{2}]\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[(\gamma-\gamma_{i})^{2}]=2\,(\gamma-\bar{\gamma})^{2}+2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[(\gamma_{i}-\bar{\gamma})^{2}],

(5)

where
\bar{\gamma}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\gamma_{i}]=1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H})
.
To bound the second term in (
5
), we write:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[(\gamma_{i}-\bar{\gamma})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{m^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\Big[\big(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}_{-i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}])\mathbf{H}\big)^{2}\Big]+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{m^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\Big[\big(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H})-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big)^{2}\Big].

The latter term can be bounded by again using Condition
2
,
with
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, obtaining:

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{2}}{m^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\Big[\big(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H})-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\big[\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}((\gamma\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H})^{2})\big]\leq 36\cdot\frac{\alpha d}{m^{2}}.

The former term can be bounded using the Burkholder inequality for
martingale difference sequences. We state this bound as a lemma, proven
separately in Appendix
B.2
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\cdot]
be the conditional variance
with respect to event
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{2}
, see (
2
),
with
\mathbf{x}_{i}
independent of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. Then, there is an
absolute constant
C>0
such that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\!\big[\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{H})\big]\leq C\cdot d.

Using Lemma
25
, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[(\gamma_{i}-\bar{\gamma})^{2}]\leq C^{\prime}\cdot\alpha d/m^{2}
for some
absolute constant
C^{\prime}
. It remains to bound the term:

\displaystyle|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}|=\bigg|\frac{m}{m-d}-\Big(1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H})\Big)\bigg|=\frac{|d-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H})|}{m-d}.

Observe that we have:

\displaystyle\big|d-\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\mathrm{tr}((\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}])\mathbf{H})+\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{Q}]\mathbf{H})\,\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\mathrm{tr}((\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}})[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H})+\mathrm{tr}(-\mathbf{Z}_{1})+\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Z}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}_{1}+\mathbf{Z}_{2})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\mathrm{tr}((\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}})[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H})\big|+|\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Z}_{0})|+|\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Z}_{2})|.

The first two terms can be bounded similarly as we did

\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|
, obtaining that

|\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Z}_{0})|=O(\alpha d/m)
, and also:

\displaystyle\big|\mathrm{tr}((\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}})[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]\mathbf{H})\big|=\frac{\delta_{3}}{1-\delta_{3}}\big|\mathrm{tr}((\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mid\neg\mathcal{E}_{3}])\mathbf{H})\big|=O(d\delta_{3})=O(d/m^{3}).

For the last term, we have:

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Z}_{2})|

\displaystyle=\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[(\tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma_{i}}-1)\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\tfrac{\gamma-\bar{\gamma}}{\gamma_{i}}\,\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big]\Big|+\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\tfrac{\bar{\gamma}-\gamma_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\,\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}|\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}]+(m-d)\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[|\gamma_{i}-\bar{\gamma}|\big]

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}|\cdot\frac{6}{1-\delta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}]+(m-d)\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(\gamma_{i}-\bar{\gamma})^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}|\cdot\frac{6}{1-\delta}\,d+\sqrt{C^{\prime}\alpha d}.

The bound for the second term
\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\tfrac{\bar{\gamma}-\gamma_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big]\Big|

comes from the definition of
\gamma_{i}=1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, because
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big/\gamma_{i}\leq m/\gamma=m-d
.

Thus, putting this together we conclude that:

\displaystyle|\gamma-\bar{\gamma}|\cdot\big(1-\tfrac{6d}{(m-d)(1-\delta)}\big)\leq O(\alpha d/m^{2})+O(\sqrt{\alpha d}/m)=O(\sqrt{\alpha d}/m),

which for
m\geq 8d
and
\delta\leq 1/m^{3}
implies that
(\gamma-\bar{\gamma})^{2}=O(\alpha d/m^{2})
so we get
T_{1}=O(\sqrt{\alpha d}/m)
. Finally, we
obtain the bound
\|\mathbf{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mathbf{H}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\leq T_{1}\cdot T_{2}\cdot T_{3}=O(\alpha\sqrt{d}/m)
, which concludes the proof.

Lemma 26

(
[

]
)
.

For

a real martingale difference sequence with

\sigma

\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}

. We now provide a bound

-conditional

Appendix C
Restricted Bai-Silverstein inequality

In this section, we prove Theorem

14
. Specifically, we study
Condition
2
(Restricted Bai-Silverstein), the second structural condition
for small inversion bias in Theorem

11
, which describes the deviation of a quadratic form

\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}
from its mean, for a random vector
\mathbf{x}
. We start by
showing Theorem
14
, a generalized
version of the lemma of Bai and Silverstein (Lemma

13
), which applies when
\mathbf{x}

has independent entries. Then, in Appendix
C.2

we show a similar result for a
leverage score sparsified vector, constructed as in

C.1
Proof of Theorem
14

Since the assumptions on
\mathbf{x}
only depend on the leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
, and the conclusion is about the variance of a quadratic form, which only depends on the first four moments of the entries of
\mathbf{x}
, we can assume without loss of generality that the distribution of
\mathbf{x}
only depends on the leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
. We will prove the claim for such random vectors
\mathbf{x}
.

We start by proving the following result:

Proposition 27
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a fixed
n\times d
matrix with
n\geq d
, and
\mathbf{x}
be a random vector with independent entries with mean
zero and unit variance, whose distribution only depends on the

\mathbf{A}
. Then, Condition
2
(Restricted

\mathbf{A}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left((\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U})\right)\leq\alpha-2,

where
\mathbf{U}
is the
n\times d
matrix of left singular vectors of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{D}
is the
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{D}=\diag(d_{k})
, with
d_{k}=\mathbb{E}x_{k}^{4}-3
.

Proof.

The Restricted Bai-Silverstein condition is equivalent to having, for all matrices
\mathbf{B}
of the form
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, where
\mathbf{U}
is the matrix of left singular vectors of
\mathbf{A}
,

\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}]\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2}).

Let
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}
. Then this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z}]\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{M}^{2}).

(6)

First we claim that it is enough to consider diagonal matrices
\mathbf{M}
.
Suppose that we have a condition
C(\diag(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}),\alpha)
that guarantees that (
6
) holds for diagonal matrices
\mathbf{M}_{d}
, and that depends only on the leverage scores and
\alpha
.
Now, consider a general matrix
\mathbf{M}
, and suppose it has the eigendecomposition
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{O}\mathbf{M}_{d}\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
for a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{M}_{d}
. We can write the equivalences

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z}]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{M}^{2})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{O}\mathbf{M}_{d}\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{z}]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}([\mathbf{O}\mathbf{M}_{d}\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]^{2})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{z}_{d}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}_{d}\mathbf{z}_{d}]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{M}_{d}^{2})

where
\mathbf{z}_{d}=\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}
. Now we apply the condition
C(\diag(\mathbf{U}_{o}\mathbf{U}_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}),\alpha)
to
\mathbf{U}_{o}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
and the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{M}_{d}
. This condition is applicable, because
\mathbf{M}_{o}
is a diagonal matrix, and guarantees (
6
) for
\mathbf{M}_{d}
. However, we also have that the row norms of
\mathbf{U}_{o}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
are the same as the row norms of
\mathbf{U}
, because
\mathbf{O}
simply acts by an orthogonal rotation of the rows. So
\diag(\mathbf{U}_{o}\mathbf{U}_{o}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})=\diag(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})
. Thus, since the distributions of the sketches we consider only depend on the leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
, which are the diagonals of the matrix
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, the condition
C(\diag(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}),\alpha)
guarantees that (
6
) holds for the original matrix
\mathbf{M}
. This shows that it is enough to establish the condition for diagonal matrices
\mathbf{M}
.

Hence we can rotate
\mathbf{U}
by the eigenvectors
\mathbf{O}
of
\mathbf{M}
into
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
, and thus assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{M}
is diagonal,
\mathbf{M}=\diag({\mathbf{g}})
, where
{\mathbf{g}}
is a vector. Then, the condition simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}[\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}g_{i}]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{g}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Gamma}{\mathbf{g}}\leq\alpha\cdot\|{\mathbf{g}}\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{\Gamma}
is the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{z}\circ\mathbf{z}
. Here the symbol
\circ
means entrywise product.
This condition has to be true for any vector
{\mathbf{g}}
. Thus, this condition says exactly that the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{\Gamma}
is at most
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Gamma})\leq\alpha.

Also we assume that
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
, hence for any symmetric matrix
\mathbf{F}
(see e.g.,
[
BS10
,
CD11
]
and
[
MM19
, Lemma B.6.]
),

\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}]=\sum_{k}d_{k}F_{kk}^{2}+2\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{2})

(7)

where
d_{k}=\mathbb{E}x_{k}^{4}-3
. Therefore, applying this for
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{U}\diag({\mathbf{g}})\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, and matching terms, one has
\mathbf{\Gamma}=(\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U}+2\mathbf{I}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{D}=\diag(d_{k})
and with
d_{k}=\mathbb{E}x_{k}^{4}-3
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We now continue with the proof of the main claim
(Theorem
14
). Based on the above
results, as
long as the random vector
\mathbf{x}
has independent entries of
zero mean and unit variance, proving Condition
2
boils down
to the control of the fourth moment of the
distribution.

Let
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{U}\circ\mathbf{U}
, and let
\mathbf{r}_{i}
denote its rows. Note that
\mathbf{r}_{i}
have non-negative entries. Let
\mathbf{L}=\diag(1/\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|^{2})=\diag(1/\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1})=\diag(1/l_{i})
be the matrix of inverse leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
, which are also the inverse
\ell_{1}
norms of the rows
\mathbf{r}_{i}
of
\mathbf{R}
. We can simply discard the zero rows to ensure that this is well defined and
\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}>0
for all indices.

Then if we can bound
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}\right)\leq\kappa
, it follows that
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{R}\right)\leq C\kappa\leq\alpha-2
, which is our desired condition as long as
\alpha
is sufficiently large. We will show this bound with
\kappa=1
.

Note that
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}
is a symmetric matrix and has non-negative entries, because
the rows of
\mathbf{R}
,
\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\circ\mathbf{u}_{i}
are the entry-wise squares of certain vectors, and the entries of
\mathbf{L}
are all positive.
Moreover, it is readily verified that the all ones vector
\mathbf{1}_{d}
(which clearly has non-negative entries), is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
with unit eigenvalue,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{1}_{d}=\mathbf{1}_{d}.

In other words,
\mathbf{Q}
is a symmetric doubly stochastic matrix.
In more detail, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{1}_{d}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{1}_{d}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{r}_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{d}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{1}_{d}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}}.

Now, clearly, since
\mathbf{r}_{i}
have non-negative entries, we have
\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{1}_{d}=\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}
. Therefore, we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{1}_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{1}_{d}.

In the last equality, we have used that, since the columns of
\mathbf{U}
are orthogonal vectors, we
have that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{ij}=1
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
.

Hence, the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
is at least
1
. By the Perron-Frobenius theorem for non-negative matrices, it follows that the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
is paired with an eigenvector
\mathbf{v}
of non-negative entries, see e.g.,
[
Mey00
, claims 8.3.1 and 8.3.2]
.
We can write, for any such vector
\mathbf{v}\geq 0
, that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{r}_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}}\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{r}_{i}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}}.

Now, clearly
\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{v}/\|\mathbf{r}_{i}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}
. Since
each entry of each
\mathbf{r}_{i}
is non-negative, we have that
0\leq(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v})_{j}\leq(\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{ij})\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}
.
As mentioned, we also have that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{ij}=1
. Hence,

\displaystyle 0\leq(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v})_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty},\,\,j=1,\ldots,n.

Suppose
\mathbf{v}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
with eigenvalue
\lambda\geq 0
, i.e.,
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
. Based on the above inequality, we find
\|\lambda\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}
, hence
\lambda\leq 1
.
This shows that the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Q}
is at most unity.
Thus, by the above reasoning
\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{R}\right)\leq C
, and thus Condition
2
holds as long as
C+2\leq\alpha
. This finishes the proof.

C.2
Restricted Bai-Silverstein for LESS embeddings

In this section we show that a sub-gaussian random vector sparsified
using our leverage score sparsifier (LESS) satisfies Condition

2
(Restricted Bai-Silverstein) with
\alpha=O(1)
. We use
this fact in Appendix
D
to prove Theorem
8
.

Lemma 28

(Restricted Bai-Silverstein for LESS)
.

Fix a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with rank
d
and let
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
be a
leverage score sparsifier for
\mathbf{A}
. For any p.s.d. matrix
\mathbf{B}

restricted to the span of
\mathbf{A}
and any

\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=(x_{1},...,x_{n})
having independent entries with mean zero,
unit variance and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{4}]=O(1)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\big[(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\big]\leq O(1)\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2}).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1/2}
be the orthonormal basis matrix for the
span of
\mathbf{A}
, and let
\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}=\diag({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathbf{U}
. Note that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
for

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}
. It follows
that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\big[(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}]=\mathrm{Var}\big[\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}]\big],

where
\mathrm{Var}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
denotes the conditional variance with respect to

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Recall that
\xi_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{b_{i}}{dp_{i}}}
, where

b_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}1_{[s_{t}=i]}
, with
s_{t}
sampled i.i.d. from
(p_{1},...,p_{n})
and

p_{i}\approx_{O(1)}\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|^{2}/d
(here,
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
denotes the
i
th
row of
\mathbf{U}
). Thus,

\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{dp_{s_{t}}}
and it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\big[\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}}{dp_{s_{t}}}\bigg]=d\,\mathrm{Var}\bigg[\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}}{dp_{s_{1}}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq d\,\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})}{d^{2}p_{s_{1}}^{2}}\bigg]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\|\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}\|^{2}}{dp_{s_{1}}}\,\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}^{2}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}}{p_{s_{1}}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\,\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}^{2}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}}{p_{s_{1}}}\bigg]=O(1)\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{C}^{2}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})=O(1)\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2}).

The Bai-Silverstein inequality (Lemma
13
) implies that

\mathrm{Var}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}]\leq O(1)\cdot\mathrm{tr}\big((\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{2}\big)
, so we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}]\big]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathrm{tr}\big((\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{2}\big)\Big]=O(1)\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathrm{tr}\bigg(\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{dp_{s_{t}}}\Big)^{2}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\sum_{t=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})}{d^{2}p_{s_{t}}^{2}}\bigg]+O(1)\sum_{t\neq r}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{t}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}_{s_{r}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{r}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})}{dp_{s_{t}}\cdot dp_{s_{r}}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})+O(1)\,\mathrm{tr}\Big(\mathbf{C}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{p_{s_{1}}}\Big]\mathbf{C}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{2}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{p_{s_{2}}}\Big]\Big)\leq O(1)\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2}).

Thus, we obtain the desired bound:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\big[(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\big]\leq O(1)\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2}),

which completes the proof.
∎

C.3
Lower bounds for other sketching methods

In this section, we show lower bounds for Condition
2
(Restricted Bai-Silverstein) in
the context of existing fast sketching techniques. To do that, we first discuss the basic requirement of the
framework defined by Theorem
11
, namely that the
sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
must have i.i.d. rows.

Fast sketches with i.i.d. rows.

In our discussion, we will focus on three types of sketches:
approximate leverage score sampling
[
DMM06
]
,
Subsampled Randomized Hadamard
Transform
[
AC09
]
, and sparse embedding matrices
(extensions of the CountSketch
[
CW17
]
, see
[
NN13
,
Coh16
]
),
all of which can be implemented in time nearly linear in the input size.
The i.i.d. row assumption can be easily satisfied
by any row sampling sketch, including approximate leverage score
sampling. The SRHT technically does not satisfy this
assumption, however if we treat the Randomized Hadamard Transform as a
preprocessing step (given that it does not distort the covariance matrix),
then the subsampling part can be analyzed analogously as leverage score
sampling. In the case of sparse embedding matrices, the most commonly
studied variant has a fixed number of non-zeros per column of
\mathbf{S}
and
so it does not have independent rows, however, it is known that a variant
with independently sparsified entries (which fits into the setup of
Theorem
11
) achieves nearly matching approximation
guarantees
[
Coh16
]
.

Leverage score sampling.
Let
\mathbf{S}
be a row sampling sketch of
size
m
, i.e., each row is distributed independently as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, where

\mathbf{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{s}}}\mathbf{e}_{s}
and
s
is an index drawn from
distribution
(p_{1},...,p_{n})
. Given a
matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
of rank
d
, we call this an approximate
leverage score sampling sketch if
p_{i}\approx_{O(1)}l_{i}/d
for all
i
, where

l_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}
is the
i
th leverage score
of
\mathbf{A}
. We will present two lower bound constructions.

1.
Approximate sampling and arbitrary
\mathbf{A}
.
Now, suppose that
n
is even and consider the following
specific example:

\displaystyle p_{j}=\begin{cases}l_{j}/2d,&\text{for }j\leq n/2,\\[2.84526pt]
3l_{j}/2d,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Further, consider the matrix
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{P}
,
which is the projection onto the column-span of
\mathbf{A}
, and therefore
satisfies the restriction requirement in the Restricted
Bai-Silverstein condition. Then, since

\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{P}^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{P})=d
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbf{e}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{e}_{s}/p_{s}-d\big)^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(l_{s}/p_{s}-d\big)^{2}\Big]\geq(d/3)^{2}

2.
Exact sampling and a specific
\mathbf{A}
.
Suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}
, each
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is a standard
basis vector scaled by
\sqrt{d/n}
and we are sampling index

s
according to exact leverage scores, i.e., uniformly at random. Then, letting
\mathbf{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{s}}}\mathbf{e}_{s}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B})\big)^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(d\cdot\frac{\mathbf{a}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{a}_{s}}{\mathbf{a}_{s}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{s}}-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{C})\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=d^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(C_{jj}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}C_{ii}\Big)^{2}=d^{2}\cdot\Omega(1),\quad\text{if}\quad C_{ii}=\begin{cases}1/2,&\text{for even $i$,}\\
3/2,&\text{for odd $i$.}\end{cases}

In both constructions, we have
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})=\Theta(d)
, so this implies
that for leverage score sampling, Condition
2
can
only be shown with factor
\alpha=\Omega(d)
, as opposed to
O(1)
for
sub-gaussian sketches and LESS embeddings.

Data-oblivious sparse embeddings.
Let
\mathbf{S}
be a sketch of
size
m
, where each row is distributed independently as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
and
\mathbf{x}=(\sqrt{\frac{m}{s}}b_{1}r_{1},...,\sqrt{\frac{m}{s}}b_{n}r_{n})
, with
b_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\frac{s}{m})
and
r_{i}

distributed as a uniformly random sign. While this is not the most
commonly studied variant of a sparse embedding, it is known to
satisfy the subspace embedding property for sketch size
m=O(d\log d)
with sparsity level
s=O(\log d)

[
Coh16
]
, which
matches the state-of-the-art for sparse embeddings. Other sparse
embeddings have non-i.i.d. row distributions

[
CW17
,
NN13
,
MM13
]
, and so they do not fit into the
framework laid out by Theorem
11
. The key difference
between the sparsification of
\mathbf{x}
relative to our LESS embeddings
is that it is data-oblivious. We can exploit that in our lower bound
example by choosing an extremely skewed leverage score distribution
of matrix
\mathbf{A}
. In particular, suppose that
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}
and
moreover,
\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
for
i=1,...,k
(where
1\leq k\leq d
) and for
all
i>k
, the first
k
coordinates of
a_{i}
are zero. This
construction ensures that the first
k
leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
are
equal
1
. Once again setting
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Var}\Big[\frac{m}{s}b_{i}r_{i}\Big]=k\cdot\frac{m}{s}\Big(1-\frac{s}{m}\Big).

If we let
k=\Omega(d)
, then we get
\mathrm{Var}[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}]\geq\Omega(m/s)\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})
. Thus, unless we zero-out merely a constant
fraction of entries of
\mathbf{S}
, the sketching matrix will not satisfy
Condition
2
with a constant factor

\alpha=O(1)
. We conjecture that this example can be extended to show
a general lower bound on the inversion bias, as we did for approximate
leverage score sampling.

Appendix D
Subspace embedding guarantee for LESS embeddings

In this section, we prove Lemma
12
and Theorem
8
.
In particular, we prove that LESS embeddings achieve the subspace
embedding property for a sketch of size
O(d\log d)
(Lemma
12
), thus
establishing Condition
1
. Then,
at the end of the section we briefly discuss how to combine Lemmas

12
and
28
,
using the structural conditions via Theorem
11
, to obtain Theorem
8
.

D.1
Proof of Lemma
12

First, note that instead of directly showing the subspace embedding of

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
for the span of
\mathbf{A}
, it suffices to show the guarantee when
replacing
\mathbf{A}
with its orthonormal basis matrix

\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1/2}
, since

\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Then, a standard technique, e.g., as used for leverage score sampling
sketches, relies on the following decomposition of

\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}
as an average of independent rank-one p.s.d. random matrices:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U},

where
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
represents the
i
th row of
\mathbf{S}
.
For standard leverage score sampling sketches it suffices to use
the matrix Chernoff bound
[
Tro12
, Theorem 1.1]
, which
uses an almost sure bound on each rank-one matrix to ensure
concentration around the mean,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}]=\mathbf{I}
. However, in the case of
a leverage score sparsified
embedding an almost sure bound is not sufficient. Instead, we show
that the rank-one matrices
\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}
exhibit
sub-exponential tails on all of their moments, as required by the
following variant of the matrix Bernstein bound.

Lemma 29

(
[
Tro12
, Theorem 6.2]
)
.

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{X}_{i}
of
d\times d

independent and symmetric random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}]=\mathbf{0},\quad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}^{p}]\preceq\frac{p!}{2}\cdot R^{p-2}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\quad\textmd{for}\quad p=2,3,...

Then, defining the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\|
, for any
t>0
we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big)\geq t\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Rt}\right).

We apply the above result for

\mathbf{X}_{i}=\pm(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}-\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{I})
, where

\mathbf{s}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is a
leverage score sparsified sub-gaussian random vector. We next
establish the subexponential moment bound needed for the matrix
Bernstein bound.

Lemma 30
.

Fix a matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that
\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}
. Suppose that

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is a leverage score sparsifier for
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{x}
has
i.i.d.
O(1)
-sub-gaussian entries with mean zero and unit variance.
Then, there is

such that for all

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{I}\Big)^{p}\Big]\Big\|\leq\frac{p!}{2}\cdot(Cd)^{p-1}.

Now, the matrix Bernstein bound (Lemma

and
\sigma^{2}=R=\frac{Cd}{m}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{\big\|\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{I}\big\|\geq\eta\Big\}\leq 2d\cdot\exp\Big(-\frac{\eta^{2}m}{4Cd}\Big)\leq\delta\quad\text{for}\quad m\geq 4Cd\log(2d/\delta)/\eta^{2},

D.2
Proof of Lemma
30

The key part of our proof of Lemma

establishing the following concentration inequality which can be
viewed as a form of the Hanson-Wright inequality

[
RV13
]
that takes advantage of
the leverage score sparsifier
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, similarly as we did for the
Restricted Bai-Silverstein inequality (Lemma

Lemma 31
.

Fix a matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that
\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}
. Suppose that

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is a leverage score sparsifier for
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{x}
has
independent
O(1)
-sub-gaussian entries with mean zero and unit
variance. Then, there is
c=\Omega(1)
and
C=O(1)
such that for any

we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\geq t\Big\}\leq\exp\Big(-c\,\big(\sqrt{t}+t/d\big)\Big).

Proof.

We use the shorthand
\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\diag({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathbf{U}
. Similarly as for
Lemma
28
, our strategy is to
show that the sparsification
\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
preserves enough of the
structure of
\mathbf{U}
so that we can apply the classical Hanson-Wright
inequality, which is repeated below, following
[
RV13
]
,

Lemma 32

(
[
RV13
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
have independent
O(1)
-sub-gaussian entries with
mean zero and unit variance. Then, there is
c=\Omega(1)
such that
for any
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{B}
and
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{|\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B})|\geq t\Big\}\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\min\Big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\mathbf{B}\|}\Big\}\bigg).

To show that the leverage score sparsification
\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is
sufficiently accurate, we can rely on the matrix Chernoff bound,
repeated below, and the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{s_{i}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}}{dp_{s_{i}}},

where
s_{i}
are the random indices sampled from the approximate
leverage score distribution
(p_{1},...,p_{n})
(see Definition

6
). For simplicity, we only repeat the
large deviation part of the Chernoff bound, which is the one relevant to our analysis.

Lemma 33

(
[
Tro12
, Theorem 1.1 and Remark 5.3]
)
.

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{X}_{i}
of
d\times d

independent positive semi-definite random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\big]=\mathbf{I}
and
\|\mathbf{X}_{i}\|\leq R
. Then, for any
t\geq\mathrm{e}
,
we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{\Big\|\sum_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big\|\geq t\Big\}\leq d\cdot\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}}{t}\Big)^{t/R}.

We apply the matrix Chernoff to

\mathbf{X}_{i}=\frac{1}{dp_{s_{i}}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{i}}\mathbf{u}_{s_{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, noting that since

p_{i}\geq\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|^{2}/Rd
for
R=O(1)
, it follows that
\|\mathbf{X}_{i}\|\leq R
. Moreover,

\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{X}_{i}]=\mathbf{I}
, so for
t\geq O(1)\cdot d
we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big\{\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|\geq\sqrt{t}\big\}\leq d\exp\big(-\sqrt{t}\ln(\sqrt{t}/\mathrm{e})/R\big)\leq\exp(-c\sqrt{t}),

for some
c=\Omega(1)
. Also, note that

\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|\leq\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\leq Rd
almost surely, which
implies that event
\mathcal{E}:\big[\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|\leq\min\{\sqrt{t},Rd\}\big]
holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-c(\sqrt{t}+t/d))
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{E}
, it holds that

\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\leq\min\{\sqrt{t},Rd\}\cdot Rd
, so applying

for fixed
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big\{\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\geq Rd+t\ \mid\ {\boldsymbol{\xi}},\mathcal{E}\big\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\min\Big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{\|\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|}\Big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\min\Big\{\frac{t^{2}}{\min\{\sqrt{t},Rd\}\cdot{Rd}},\frac{t}{\min\{\sqrt{t},Rd\}}\Big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\big(-c(\sqrt{t}+t/Rd)\big).

Appropriately rescaling
t
, we obtain the claim.
∎

We are now ready to present the proof of Lemma

, obtaining subexponential moment bounds
for the random matrix
\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})(\mathbf{x}\circ{\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}
, thus
completing the proof of the subspace embedding guarantee for leverage
score sparsified sketches.

Proof of Lemma
30
.

Throughout the proof, we will use the shorthand

\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}=\diag({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathbf{U}
. It is easy to show by induction over
p

that:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\big(\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}-\mathbf{I}\big)^{p}}_{\mathbf{Z}^{p}}=\big(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}-1\big)^{p-1}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\underbrace{\big(\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}-\mathbf{I}\big)^{p-1}}_{\mathbf{Z}^{p-1}}.

Thus, it follows that for any
p=2,3,...
(both even and odd) we have
the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}^{p}]\big\|\leq\Big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\underbrace{|\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}-1|^{p-1}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{\mathbf{T}_{p}}\big]\Big\|+\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}^{p-1}]\big\|.

To bound the quadratic form
\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}
in the
first term, we can use
Lemma
31
. In particular, the
lemma implies that the event
\mathcal{E}:[\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\leq Cpd]
fails with probability at most
\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{pd}}

for a sufficiently large
C=O(1)
, so we have:

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{T}_{p}]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{T}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}}]\big\|+\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{T}_{p}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{E}}]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq(pd)^{p-1}\big\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}]\big\|+\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{E}})^{p}\big]

\displaystyle=(Cpd)^{p-1}+\int_{0}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathrm{Pr}\big\{\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathbf{U}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\neg\mathcal{E}}>t\big\}dt

\displaystyle\leq(Cpd)^{p-1}+p(Cpd)^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{pd}}+\int_{Cpd}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-c(\sqrt{t}+t/d)}dt.

Note that
(O(1)\,p)^{p+O(1)}d^{p-1}\leq p^{p}(O(1)\,d)^{p-1}\leq(p!/2)(O(1)\,d)^{p-1}
, and also
\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{pd}}\leq O(1/d)
, so the first two terms can be easily
bounded as desired. To bound the last term, we use the following
integral formula:

\displaystyle\int t^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t^{\theta}}dt=-\frac{\Gamma(p/\theta,\alpha t^{\theta})}{\theta\alpha^{p/\theta}}+\mathrm{const},

which follows from the definition of the upper incomplete Gamma
function
\Gamma
. Note that for
p=2,3,...
this function also satisfies:

\displaystyle\Gamma(p,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(p-1)!\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\{x<p\}\qquad\text{for}\quad x\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)!\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-c\lambda}\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda\geq 2p,\ c=\Omega(1),

where the last inequality is a standard tail bound for a Poisson random variable.
With a slight abuse of notation, we let
c
denote the minimum of the
above constant
c
and the constant
c
from Lemma

31
. We apply the integral formula in two different ways, depending
on
p
. First, if
p<d
then we have:

\displaystyle\int_{Cpd}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-c(\sqrt{t}+t/d)}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Cpd}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-c\sqrt{t}}dt=2pc^{-2p}\Gamma(2p,c\sqrt{Cpd})\leq 2c^{-2p}(2p)!\mathrm{e}^{-c^{2}\sqrt{Cpd}}.

By using the fact that
\exp(-c^{2}\sqrt{Cpd})\leq\exp(-c^{2}p)=O(1/p)
,
this expression can be bounded by
(p!/2)(O(1)\,p)^{p-1}\leq(p!/2)(O(1)\,d)^{p-1}
. Next, we consider the case when
p\geq d
. We have:

\displaystyle\int_{Cpd}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-c(\sqrt{t}+t/d)}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Cpd}^{\infty}pt^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-ct/d}dt=p(d/c)^{p}\Gamma(p,cCp)\leq p!d^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-c^{2}Cp},

where the last inequality holds as long as
C\geq 2/c
. Here, we
note that
\mathrm{e}^{-c^{2}Cp}\leq O(1/d)
since
p\geq d
, thus again
obtaining a bound of the form
(p!/2)(O(1)\,d)^{p-1}
. Putting
everything together, we conclude that:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}^{p}]\|\leq\frac{p!}{2}\,(O(1)\,d)^{p-1}+\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}^{p-1}]\|.

Recursively summing up this bound concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix E
Averaging nearly-unbiased estimators

In this section, we show that averaging improves spectral
approximation for matrix estimators with small inversion bias, and as
a consequence we prove Corollaries
5
and

9
for averaging sketched inverse covariance matrix estimators based
on sub-gaussian sketches and LESS embeddings respectively.

E.1
Conditions for effective averaging of random matrices

We start with a more general result, which should be of interest
to averaging nearly-unbiased matrix estimators in settings other than
inverse covariance matrix estimation.

Lemma 34

(Conditions for effective averaging)
.

Suppose that
\delta\leq{\epsilon}\leq{\eta}\leq 1
and

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1},...,\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{q}
are i.i.d. positive semi-definite
d
-dimensional
random matrices such that:

1.

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
is an
({\epsilon},\delta/2q)
-unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{C}
;

2.

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
is an
({\eta},\delta/2q)
-approximation of
\mathbf{C}
.

Then,
\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
is an

(\epsilon^{\prime},2\delta)
-approximation of
\mathbf{C}
for

\epsilon^{\prime}={\epsilon}+{\eta}\cdot O\big(\sqrt{\frac{\ln(d/\delta)}{q}}\,\big)
.

Proof.

For this, we use a variant of the matrix Bernstein
inequality given below.

Lemma 35

(
[
Tro12
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{X}_{i}
of
d\times d

independent and symmetric random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}]=\mathbf{0},\quad\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\leq R\quad\text{almost surely.}

Then, defining the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{i}^{2}]\|
, for any
t>0
we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big)\geq t\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Rt/3}\right).

Suppose that
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
is an

({\epsilon},\delta/2q)
-unbiased estimator and an

({\eta},\delta/2q)
-spectral approximation for
\mathbf{C}
, with
\mathcal{E}_{inv}

and
\mathcal{E}_{sub}
the associated high probability events. For
concreteness, let the
O(1)
constant factor in Definition

3
be denoted as
M
. Further, let

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i},\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i},\mathcal{E}_{sub}^{i}
be the i.i.d. copies of

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
with their associated events. Finally, let
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}
be
a random matrix obtained from conditioning
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
on

\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{sub}^{i}
, and coupled with
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
so that

\mathrm{Pr}(\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}=\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i})\geq\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i}\wedge\mathcal{E}_{sub}^{i})\geq 1-\delta/q
(this coupling can be obtained by considering a
construction of
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}
via rejection sampling from

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
). We can bound the bias of
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}
(for any
i
) by observing that:

\displaystyle-\delta/q\cdot\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i},\neg\mathcal{E}_{sub}^{i}]\preceq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i}]\preceq\frac{\delta/q}{1-\delta/q}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i}].

Since we have
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i}]\approx_{{\epsilon}}\mathbf{C}
and

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{E}_{inv}^{i},\neg\mathcal{E}_{sub}^{i}]\preceq M\cdot\mathbf{C}
, it
follows that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}]
is an
\epsilon^{\prime}
-spectral approximation
of
\mathbf{C}
for
\epsilon^{\prime}={\epsilon}+\frac{2\delta}{q}(1+{\epsilon}+M)
.

We will now apply the matrix Bernstein inequality (Lemma

35
) to the sequence of matrices:

\mathbf{X}_{i}=\frac{1}{q}\Big(\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big]\Big),\quad i=1,...,q.

Note that we have
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}\approx_{{\eta}}\frac{1}{q}\mathbf{C}
, so it
follows that
\|\mathbf{X}_{i}\|\leq({\eta}+\epsilon^{\prime})/q
and

\sum_{i}\|\mathbf{X}_{i}^{2}\|\leq({\eta}+\epsilon^{\prime})^{2}/q
. Thus, we conclude
that for
t\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big\|\geq t\,({\eta}+\epsilon^{\prime})\bigg\}\leq 2d\exp\big(-t^{2}q/4\big).

Setting
t=\sqrt{4\ln(2d/\delta)/q}
(without loss of generality,
assume that
t\leq 1
), we obtain that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\Big\|\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbf{I}\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathbf{X}_{i}\Big\|+\Big\|\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}]\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbf{I}\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq t\cdot({\eta}+\epsilon^{\prime})+\epsilon^{\prime}\leq{\epsilon}+{\eta}\cdot O\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(d/\delta)}{q}}\,\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\delta M}{q}\Big).

Note that under the assumptions that
M=O(1)
and
\delta\leq{\eta}
, we can absorb the last term into the middle term. Finally,
observe that thanks to the coupling and a union bound, the above bound holds with
probability
1-2\delta
after we replace
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\prime}
with

\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
, completing the proof of Lemma
34
.
∎

Appendix F
Inversion bias lower bound for leverage score sampling

In this section, we show a lower bound on the inversion bias of

F.1
Proof of Theorem

Without loss of generality, suppose that

(otherwise the matrix
\mathbf{A}

, since the other cases follow easily. Our construction is designed

1/2

\mathbf{S}

,
i.e., its

\sqrt{\!\frac{n}{m}}\,\mathbf{e}_{s_{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}

\mathbf{A}

scaled standard basis
vectors such that pairs of consecutive rows are given by

\mathbf{a}_{2(i-1)+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{a}_{2(i-1)+2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ \mathbf{e}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
for
i\geq 2
, whereas the first two rows are
\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
and
\mathbf{a}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4}}&&&\\
\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}&&0&\\
&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&&\\
&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&0&&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
&&&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

First, note that
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}
, and all of the squared row norms are
within
[\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{n},\frac{3}{2}\frac{d}{n}]
, so uniform sampling is
indeed a
1/2
-approximate leverage score sampling scheme.
Further, for any
\gamma>0
, the matrix

\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
is diagonal, and its diagonal entries are given by:

\displaystyle\big[\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\big]_{ii}=\begin{cases}\frac{\gamma n}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{1}_{[s_{j}=1]}+\frac{3}{4}\mathbf{1}_{[s_{j}=2]}\big)=\frac{\gamma n}{m}\cdot\frac{x+b_{1}/2}{2}&\text{for }i=1,\\
\frac{\gamma n}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{[s_{j}=2(i-1)+1]}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{[s_{j}=2(i-1)+2]}\big)=\frac{\gamma n}{m}\cdot\frac{b_{i}}{2}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
b_{i}
’s are all identically (but not independently) distributed as
{\mathrm{Binomial}}(m,1/d)

and
x
is distributed, conditionally on

b_{1}
, as
{\mathrm{Binomial}}(b_{1},1/2)
.
Here
b_{i}
denote the number of times
s_{j}\in\{2i-1,2i\}
, while
x
denotes the number of times
s_{j}=2
. Due to the symmetry of the problem, conditionally on a given value of
b_{1}
(i.e., a given value of counts
s_{j}
that are equal to either unity or two), each
s_{j}\in\{1,2\}
is distributed uniformly over
\{1,2\}
, hence the value
x
of counts
s_{j}
that are equal to two is distributed as
{\mathrm{Binomial}}(b_{1},1/2)
. This leads to the claimed distributional representation.

The key idea in the construction is that
the first diagonal entry of the sketch has more variance than the
others, and thus it will also have more inversion bias. As a
result, there is no scaling
\gamma
that will simultaneously correct
the inversion bias of the first entry and of all the other entries. To
that end, we lower bound a shifted inverse moment of the Binomial
distribution in the following lemma, potentially of independent
interest, proven at the end of this section.

Lemma 36
.

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that for any positive integer
b
, if
x\sim{\mathrm{Binomial}}(b,1/2)
then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{x+b/2}\bigg]\geq\Big(1+\frac{1}{Cb}\Big)\cdot\frac{1}{b}.

Note that the expected inverse of
\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}

is undefined since the matrix may not be invertible. Thus, as in the
definition of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-unbiased estimator, we must
condition on a high probability event which ensures invertibility. We
start by considering the largest such event,
\mathcal{E}^{*}:[\forall_{i}b_{i}>0]
. Using the fact that, conditioned on
b_{1}
, the variable
x
is
independent of
\mathcal{E}^{*}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\big]_{11}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}\Big]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\gamma n}{m}\right)^{-1}\sum_{b>0}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{2}{x+b_{1}/2}\mid b_{1}=b\Big]\,\mathrm{Pr}(b_{1}=b\mid\mathcal{E}^{*})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}\left(\frac{\gamma n}{m}\right)^{-1}\sum_{b>0}\Big(1+\frac{1}{Cb}\Big)\,\frac{2}{b}\,\mathrm{Pr}(b_{1}=b\mid\mathcal{E}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b>0}\Big(1+\frac{1}{Cb}\Big)\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\big]_{22}\mid b_{2}=b\Big]\,\mathrm{Pr}(b_{2}=b\mid\mathcal{E}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\big]_{22}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2C}\frac{d}{m}\sum_{b=1}^{2m/d}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\big]_{22}\mid b_{2}=b\Big]\,\mathrm{Pr}(b_{2}=b\mid\mathcal{E}^{*})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}\Big(1+\frac{d}{4Cm}\Big)\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\big]_{22}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}\Big],

where in
(a)
we used Lemma
36
and in
(b)
we
observed that
\big[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\big]_{22}
decreases
with
b_{2}
and moreover, since
\mathbb{E}[b_{2}]=m/d\geq 1
, it is easy to verify
that the range
[1,2m/d]
contains more than half of the probability
mass of
{\mathrm{Binomial}}(m,1/d)
.

The above derivation shows that when conditioned on
\mathcal{E}^{*}
, for any scaling
\gamma>0
the
inversion bias will be at least
\Omega(d/m)
, since the estimated
matrix
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}=\mathbf{I}
has the same entries on the diagonal,
whereas the expectation of the first two diagonal entries of the estimator

(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
differs by
a factor of
1+\Omega(d/m)
. To complete the proof of Theorem

10
, it remains to show that the same is true not just for

\mathcal{E}^{*}
, but for any event
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{*}
with sufficiently high
probability. Suppose that
\mathcal{E}
is such an event, with

\delta=\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*})\leq\mathrm{Pr}(\neg\mathcal{E})\leq\frac{1}{4C\cdot 16}(\frac{d}{m})^{2}
. Then, using
\tau_{i}=\frac{m}{\gamma n}[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}]_{ii}
as a shorthand, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]-\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*},\neg\mathcal{E}]\Big)\geq\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]-8\delta,

where we used that
\delta\leq 1/2
and, conditioned on
\mathcal{E}^{*}
, we have

\tau_{1}\leq 4
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]-\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*},\neg\mathcal{E}]\Big)\leq(1+2\delta)\,\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}].

Combining the two inequalities and using that

\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]\geq d/m
and

\delta\leq\frac{1}{4C\cdot 16}(\frac{d}{m})^{2}
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}]_{11}\mid\mathcal{E}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}]_{22}\mid\mathcal{E}\big]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\tau_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}]}{\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}]}\geq\frac{(1+\frac{d}{4Cm})\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]-8\delta}{(1+2\delta)\mathbb{E}[\tau_{2}\mid\mathcal{E}^{*}]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1+\frac{d}{4Cm}-8\delta\frac{m}{d}}{1+2\delta}\geq\frac{1+\frac{d}{8Cm}}{1+\frac{d}{32Cm}}\geq 1+\frac{d}{64Cm}.

Thus, as discussed above, we conclude that for any scaling
\gamma>0

and any event
\mathcal{E}
with probability
\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\frac{1}{4C\cdot 16}(\frac{d}{m})^{2}
,
we have
\|\mathbb{E}[(\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mid\mathcal{E}]-\mathbf{I}\|=\Omega(\frac{d}{m})
, which concludes the proof.

F.2
Proof of Lemma
36

We conclude this section with a proof of the Binomial inverse moment
bound from Lemma
36
. While existing work has
focused on asymptotic expansions of inverse moments of the Binomial

[
Zni09
]
, those precise
characterizations either break down or appear to be impractical to work with
when the variable is significantly shifted, as in our case. Thus, we
use a different strategy: reducing the inverse moment bound to
showing an anti-concentration inequality for the Binomial
distribution. For this, we use the classical Paley-Zygmund inequality,
stated below.

Lemma 37
.

For any non-negative variable
\mathbf{Z}
with finite variance and

\theta\in(0,1)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big(Z\geq\theta\,\mathbb{E}[Z]\big)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]}.

Let
x\sim{\mathrm{Binomial}}(b,1/2)
for a positive integer
b
. It follows that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{x+b/2}-\frac{1}{b}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{b}\mathrm{Pr}(x=i)\Big(\frac{1}{i+b/2}-\frac{1}{b}\Big)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=0}^{b}\mathrm{Pr}(x=i)\frac{b/2-i}{b/2+i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor b/2\rfloor}\mathrm{Pr}(x=i)(b/2-i)\Big(\frac{1}{b/2+i}-\frac{1}{3b/2-i}\Big),

where the last equality is obtained by symmetrically pairing up the
terms
i
and
b-i
in the first sum. Next, observe that for
0\leq i\leq b/2-\sqrt{b}/4
, we have:

\displaystyle(b/2-i)\Big(\frac{1}{b/2+i}-\frac{1}{3b/2-i}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{b}}{4}\Big(\frac{1}{b-\sqrt{b}/4}-\frac{1}{b+\sqrt{b}/4}\Big)=\frac{\sqrt{b}}{4}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{b}/2}{b^{2}-b/16}\geq\frac{1}{8b}.

Putting this together, we conclude that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{x+b/2}\Big]\geq\Big(1+\frac{1}{8b}\mathrm{Pr}\big\{x-b/2\leq-\sqrt{b}/4\big\}\Big)\cdot\frac{1}{b}.

(8)

Thus, it suffices to show that, with constant probability,
x
is
smaller than its mean,
b/2
, by at least
\sqrt{b}/4
. This follows
from the Paley-Zygmund inequality (Lemma
37
) by
setting
\mathbf{Z}=(x-b/2)^{2}
. Using standard formulas for the second and
fourth centered moment of the Binomial distribution, we have

\mathbb{E}[Z]=b/4
and
\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]=\frac{b}{4}(1+\frac{3b-6}{4})\leq 3b^{2}/16
. Therefore,
setting
\theta=1/4
in Lemma
37
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big(x-b/2\,\leq\,-\sqrt{b}/4\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Pr}\big(|x-b/2|\,\geq\,\sqrt{b}/4\big)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Pr}\big(Z\geq\theta\,\mathbb{E}[Z]\big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\Big(1-\frac{1}{4}\Big)^{2}\,\frac{b^{2}/16}{3b^{2}/16}=\frac{3}{32}.

Combining this with (
8
), we obtain the desired claim for
C=8\cdot 32/3
.

Appendix G
Exact bias-correction for orthogonally invariant embeddings

In this section we prove that orthogonal invariance implies no
inversion bias. This claim has been mentioned in the main text, in
Section
2
. Here we give a formal statement.

Proposition 38

(Orthogonal invariance implies no inversion bias)
.

Let
\mathbf{S}
be a random and right-orthogonally invariant matrix; specifically an
m\times n
matrix (with
m\leq n
) such that for any orthogonal
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{O}
, we have
\mathbf{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{O}
. Assume that
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
exists with probability one. Then the inversion bias is exactly correctable, i.e., there exists a constant
c=c_{m,n,d}
such that
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}^{-1}=c\cdot\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
; where
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
.

Examples of orthogonal ensembles can be constructed in the following way:

1.

Let
\mathbf{S}
have i.i.d. normal entries with variance
m^{-1}
. Due to the properties of the Wishart ensemble, the constant
c_{m,n,d}
is
c_{m,n,d}=m/(m-d-1)
.

2.

Let
\mathbf{S}_{u}
be a uniformly random
m\times n
partial orthogonal matrix (with
m\leq n
) such that
\mathbf{S}_{u}\mathbf{S}_{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
. Equivalently, these are the first few rows of a Haar matrix. Then define
\mathbf{S}=\sqrt{n/m}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{u}
, scaled such that
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
. We will call this the Haar sketch.

3.

The class of orthogonally invariant matrices has several closure properties. Specifically, it is closed with respect to left-multiplication by any matrices, right-multiplication by orthogonal matrices, and with respect to vector space operations (addition and multiplication by scalars). Several examples can be obtained this way. For instance, matrices
\mathbf{S}
of the form
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Z}
, where
\mathbf{Z}
has i.i.d. normal entries with variance
m^{-1}
, and
\mathbf{M}
is an arbitrary matrix fixed or random and independent of
\mathbf{Z}
are orthogonally invariant.

Proof.

We start with a reduction to orthogonal matrices: Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
be the SVD of
\mathbf{A}
. Here recall that
\mathbf{A}
is an
n\times d
matrix, with
n\geq d
and with full column rank, and thus
\mathbf{U}
is an
n\times d
partial orthogonal matrix with
n\geq d
,
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is
d\times d
diagonal, and
\mathbf{V}
is
d\times d
orthogonal. Our goal is to show that
\mathbb{E}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}^{-1}=c\cdot\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
, or equivalently that

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{-1}=c\cdot(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{-1}.

Then, by cancelling
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and
\mathbf{V}
above (using that they are deterministic square invertible matrices), and using that
\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}
, we see that the above inequality is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U})^{-1}=c\cdot\mathbf{I}.

Thus, the problem is reduced to studying orthogonal matrices
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}
, such that
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}
.
We claim that the right-orthogonal invariance implies that
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
for any
d\times d
orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{O}
. Here is a geometric argument. We have that
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}
are the angles that the random orthogonal rows of
\mathbf{S}
form with the fixed set of basis vectors formed by the columns of
\mathbf{U}
. Also,
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
corresponds to the same quantity, but with respect to the basis formed by
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
. Since
\mathbf{S}
is right-rotationally invariant, these angles have the same distribution.

Another, more algebraic proof is as follows. Since
\mathbf{S}
is right-rotationally invariant, for any orthogonal
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{R}
, we have
\mathbf{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}
. Thus, for any fixed matrix
\mathbf{U}
, we have
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}
. Choose a rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
such that
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, while
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}^{\perp}
is arbitrary, where
\mathbf{U}^{\perp}
is an orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{U}
. Then, multiplying the above with
\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}
we have

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}.

We get that
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
.
Next,
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}
implies that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{O}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U})^{-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{O}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{O}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{O}.

Thus, since
\mathbf{J}:=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U})^{-1}
is preserved under
conjugation by any orthogonal matrix,
\mathbf{J}
must be a multiple of the
identity matrix, so
\mathbf{J}=c\mathbf{I}_{d}
, for some
c=c_{m,n,d}
. This
finishes the proof.
∎
