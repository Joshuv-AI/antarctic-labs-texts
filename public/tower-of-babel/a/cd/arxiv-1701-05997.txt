The LFED and LNED Conjectures for Laurent Polynomial Algebras Thanks: The author has been partially supported by the Simons Foundation grant 278638
Wenhua Zhao

Abstract
. Let R R be an integral domain of characteristic zero, x = ( x 1 , x 2 , … , x n ) x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}) n n commutative free variables, and 𝒜 n := R ⁡ [ x − 1 , x ] {\mathcal{A}}_{n}\!:=R[x^{-1},x] , i.e., the Laurent polynomial algebra in x x over R R . In this paper we first classify all locally finite or locally nilpotent R R -derivations and R R - ℰ {\mathcal{E}} -derivations of 𝒜 n {\mathcal{A}}_{n} , where by an R R - ℰ {\mathcal{E}} -derivation of 𝒜 n {\mathcal{A}}_{n} we mean an R R -linear map of the form Id 𝒜 n − ϕ \operatorname{Id}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}-\phi for some R R -algebra endomorphism ϕ \phi of 𝒜 n {\mathcal{A}}_{n} . In particular, we show that 𝒜 n {\mathcal{A}}_{n} has no nonzero locally nilpotent R R -derivations or R R - ℰ {\mathcal{E}} -derivations. Consequently, the LNED conjecture proposed in [ Z4 ] for 𝒜 n {\mathcal{A}}_{n} follows. We then show some cases of the LFED conjecture proposed in [ Z4 ] for 𝒜 n {\mathcal{A}}_{n} . In particular, we show that both the LFED and LNED conjectures hold for the Laurent polynomial algebras in one or two commutative free variables over a field of characteristic zero.

2.
Classification of Locally Finite or Locally Nilpotent
Derivations and
{\mathcal{E}}
-Derivations of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

Throughout this paper
R
stands for an integral domain of characteristic zero
and
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})

n
commutative free variables.
We denote by
R[x^{-1},x]
or
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
the algebra of the Laurent polynomials
in
x
over
R
. The main purpose of this section is to classify all LF
(locally finite) or LN (locally nilpotent)
R
-derivations and

R
-
{\mathcal{E}}
-derivations of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.

First, we fix the following notations that will be used in the rest of this paper. The notations introduced in the previous section will also be freely used.

Notations and Conventions:

i)

We denote by
R^{\ast}
the set of units of
R
.
For each
k\geq 1
we let
\{e_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq k\}
be the
standard basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
,
M_{k}({\mathbb{Z}})
the set of all
k\times k
matrices
with integer entries, and
{\operatorname{I}}_{k}
the
k\times k
identity matrix.

ii)

For each nonzero
f\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
we denote by
\deg f
the (generalized)
degree
of
f(x)
, and
\mbox{ord}f
the
order
of
f
, i.e., the minimum of all the degrees of the monomials in
f(x)

with nonzero coefficients. Furthermore, we denote by
\operatorname{Supp}f
the
support

of
f
, i.e., the set of all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
such that the coefficient of
x^{\alpha}
is not zero, and
\operatorname{Poly}f
the
polytope
of
f
, i.e., the convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
spanned by
\operatorname{Supp}f
.

iii)

We denote by
\partial_{i}

(1\leq i\leq n)
the
R
-derivation
\partial/\partial x_{i}
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. Then every
R
-derivation
D
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
can be written uniquely as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)
for some
a_{i}(x)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. For all
k\geq 0
, we let

{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
denote the
R
-subspace of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

of homogeneous Laurent polynomials
of the (generalized) degree
k
.

We start with a classification of all LF or LN
R
-derivations of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
D
be an
R
-derivation of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. Then

1)

D
is locally finite, if and only if

D=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}q_{i}x_{i}\partial_{i}
for some
q_{i}\in R
.

2)

D
is locally nilpotent, (if and) only if
D=0
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{A}}
has no
nonzero locally nilpotent
R
-derivations.

Proof:

1)
The
(\Leftarrow)
part is obvious. To show the
(\Rightarrow)
part
we may obviously assume
D\neq 0
and write
D
uniquely as
D=\sum_{k=r}^{s}D_{k}

with
r\leq s
and
D_{k}

(r\leq k\leq s)
an
R
-derivation of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
such that

D_{k}{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}_{i+k}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
D_{r},D_{s}\neq 0
.

We first show
r=s=0
, i.e.,
D=D_{0}
. Below we give only a proof
for
s=0
by using the degree of elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. The proof
for
r=0
is similar (by using the order of elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
,
instead).

Assume
s\neq 0
. Then for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
m\geq 1
,

D_{s}^{m}x_{i}
and
D_{s}^{m}x_{i}^{-1}
, if not zero,
are homogeneous of the (generalized) degree
ms+1
and
ms-1
,
respectively.
On the other hand, since
D
is LF,

D^{m}x_{i}
and
D^{m}x_{i}^{-1}

(1\leq i\leq n
and
m\geq 1)
lie
inside a finitely generated
R
-submodule of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.
Note that
D_{s}^{m}x_{i}
and
D_{s}^{m}x_{i}^{-1}
, if not zero,
are the leading terms of
D^{m}x_{i}
and
D^{m}x_{i}^{-1}
,
respectively. Therefore, there exists
N\geq 1
such that

D_{s}^{m}x_{i}=D_{s}^{m}x_{i}^{-1}=0
for all

1\leq i\leq n
and
m\geq N
.

Since
D_{s}x_{i}^{-1}=-x_{i}^{2}D_{s}x_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
we have that
D_{s}x_{i}=0
, if and only if
D_{s}x_{i}^{-1}=0
. Since
D_{s}\neq 0
,
there exists
1\leq i\leq n
such that
D_{s}x_{i},D_{s}x_{i}^{-1}\neq 0
.
Let
k
(resp.,
\ell
) be the least positive
integer such that
D_{s}^{k}x_{i}=0
(resp.,
D_{s}^{\ell}x_{i}^{-1}=0)
.

If
k+\ell\geq 3
, then by the Leibniz rule we have

0=D_{s}^{k+\ell-2}1=D_{s}^{k+\ell-2}(x_{i}x_{i}^{-1})=\binom{k+\ell-2}{k-1}(D_{s}^{k-1}x_{i})(D_{s}^{\ell-1}x_{i}^{-1}).

Since
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
is an integral domain of characteristic zero, we have

D_{s}^{k-1}x_{i}=0
or
D_{s}^{\ell-1}x_{i}^{-1}=0
. But this contradicts to
either the choice of
k
or that of
\ell
. Therefore

k+\ell\leq 2
, whence
k=\ell=1
, i.e.,
D_{s}x_{i}=D_{s}x_{i}^{-1}=0
.
Contradiction. Hence
s=0
, as desired.

Now we may write
D=D_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)\partial_{i}
for some homogeneous

a_{i}(x)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

(1\leq i\leq n)
of degree one.
We need to show that for all
1\leq i\leq n

we have
a_{i}(x)=q_{i}x_{i}
for some
q_{i}\in R
.
But, up to the conjugation by a permutation (on
x_{i}
’s) automorphism of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

it suffices to show only the case for
a_{1}(x)
.

Write
a_{1}(x)=b(x)\partial_{1}+q_{1}x_{1}\partial_{1}
with
q_{1}\in R
and
b(x)

homogeneous of degree one and independent on
x_{1}
.
Then for all
m\geq 1
, it is easy to see that

D^{m}x_{1}^{-1}
as a Laurent polynomial in
x_{1}

over
R[x_{i}^{-1},x_{i}\,|\,2\leq i\leq n]

has the least degree (in
x_{1}
)
term
(-1)^{m}m!b^{m}(x)x_{1}^{-m-1}
.
Since
D
is LF (again), we have
b(x)^{m}x_{1}^{-m-1}=0

for all
m\gg 0
, whence
b(x)=0
and
statement
2)
follows.

2)
Let
D
be a LN (locally nilpotent)
R
-derivation of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.
Then
D
is LF and by statement
1)
we have

D=D_{0}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}q_{i}x_{i}\partial_{i}

for some
q_{i}\in R
. If
D\neq 0
, then
q_{j}\neq 0
for some
1\leq j\leq n
,
and for all
m\geq 1
we have
D^{m}x_{j}^{-1}=(-1)^{m}q_{j}^{m}x_{j}^{-1}\neq 0
.
Contradiction.

\Box

Remark 2.2
.

By some similar arguments as that in the proof of Proposition
2.1
it is easy to see that Proposition
2.1
also holds for the Laurent polynomial algebra in noncommutative free variables over
R
.

Next, we give a classifications of all LF or LN

R
-
{\mathcal{E}}
-derivations of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.
Let
\phi\neq 0
be an
R
-algebra endomorphism
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.
Since
\phi(1)
is an idempotent of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
is an integral domain,
we have
\phi(1)=0
or
1
. Since
\phi(1)=0
implies
\phi=0
, we have
\phi(1)=1
,
i.e.,
\phi
preserves the unity
1
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. Consequently,
it also preserves the units of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.

Since all units of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
have the form
qx^{\alpha}
with
q\in R^{*}

and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
we see that
\phi(x_{i})=q_{i}x^{\alpha_{i}}
with
q_{i}\in R^{*}
and

\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Set
q\!:=(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n})

and for each
1\leq i\leq n
write
\alpha_{i}=(\alpha_{1i},\alpha_{2i},\dots,\alpha_{ni})
and form the
n\times n
matrix
A\!:=(\alpha_{ij})\in M_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
For convenience, for the case
\phi=0
we simply let
A=0
and

q_{i}=0

(1\leq i\leq n)
.
For the rest of this paper we call the matrix
A
the

exponent matrix
of the
R
-algebra endomorphism
of
\phi
.

With the remarks and notations above it is easy to see that

\phi(x^{\beta})=q^{\beta}x^{A\beta}
for all
\beta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
In particular, both
\phi
and
{\operatorname{I}}-\phi
maps each monomial to a scalar multiple of a monomial. Therefore,
{\operatorname{I}}-\phi
is LF, if and only if
\phi
is LF, if and only if for each
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}
the set

\{\phi^{m}(x^{\alpha})\,|\,m\geq 0\}
is finite.

Proposition 2.3
.

With the notations above, for all
R
-endomorphism
\phi
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

the following statements hold:

1)

{\operatorname{I}}-\phi
is locally finite if and only if there exist
0\leq k\leq n
, an invertible and finite order
B\in M_{n-k}({\mathbb{Z}})
, and an invertible

S\in M_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle A=S\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}S^{-1}.

2)

{\operatorname{I}}-\phi
is locally nilpotent (if and) only if
\phi={\operatorname{I}}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
has no nonzero locally nilpotent
R
-
{\mathcal{E}}
-derivations.

Proof:

1)
The
(\Leftarrow)
part can be checked easily.
To show the
(\Rightarrow)
part, we assume
A\neq 0
,
for the case
A=0
is trivial,
and set for all
m\geq 1

(2.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{m}\!:={\operatorname{I}}_{n\times n}+A+A^{2}+\cdots+A^{m-1}.

Then it is easy to see inductively that for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and
m\geq 1
we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi^{m}(x^{\alpha})=q^{\tilde{A}_{m}\alpha}x^{A^{m}\alpha}.

Since
{\operatorname{I}}-\phi
is LF,
\phi
is also LF.
Then for each
1\leq i\leq n
the set
\{\phi^{m}(x_{i})\,|\,m\geq 1\}

is finite, whence by Eq. (
2.3
)
there exists
1\leq k_{i}<\ell_{i}
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle A^{k_{i}}e_{i}=A^{\ell_{i}}e_{i},

where
e=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n})
is the standard basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.

Multiplying a power of
A
to Eq. (
2.4
)
for each
1\leq i\leq n
we may assume
k_{i}=k_{j}
for
all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. We denote this integer by
k
and
set
\ell=k+\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\ell_{i}-k)
. Then
it is easy to see that
A^{k}e_{i}=A^{\ell}e_{i}

for all
1\leq i\leq n
, whence

A^{k}=A^{\ell}
.

Let
H
be the subgroup of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
formed by
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

such that
A\alpha=0
. If
H=0
, then
A
is of rank
n
,
and from the fact
A^{k}=A^{\ell}
with
1\leq k<\ell
,
we see that
A^{k-\ell}={\operatorname{I}}_{n}
. Hence the statement
holds in this case. So we assume
H\neq 0
.

By a well-known fact (e.g., see
[
H
, Theorem 1.6]
) on subgroups
of free abelian groups
there exists a basis
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{n})
of

{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
such that
H
is the (free) subgroup generated by

(d_{1}\beta_{1},d_{2}\beta_{2},\dots,d_{k}\beta_{k})
for some

1\leq k\leq n
and positive integers
d_{i}

(1\leq i\leq k)
.
Since
A(d_{i}\beta_{i})=0
implies
A\beta_{i}=0
for each

1\leq i\leq k
, we hence have
d_{i}=1
for
all
1\leq i\leq k
, and also
k\leq n-1
, for
A\neq 0
.
Therefore there exist
k\times(n-k)
matrix
C
, and

B\in M_{n-k}({\mathbb{Z}})
of rank
n-k
, and an invertible
S\in M_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})

such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle A=S\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}S^{-1}.

Set

D\!:=\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}
.
Since

D^{m}=\begin{pmatrix}0&CB^{m-1}\\
0&B^{m}.\end{pmatrix}

for all
m\geq 1
, by the fact
A^{k}=A^{\ell}

proved above we have
B^{k}=B^{\ell}
,
which implies
B^{k-\ell}={\operatorname{I}}_{n-k}
. Therefore
B
is invertible and
of finite order. So we need only to show
C=0
.

Assume otherwise, i.e.,
C\neq 0
.
Denote by
\psi
the
R
-algebra automorphism of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
that maps

x^{\alpha}
to
x^{S\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Replacing
\phi
by
\psi^{-1}\phi\psi
we may assume

A=D
. Since
\phi
is LF, so is
\phi^{m}
for all
m\geq 1
.
Replacing
\phi
by
\phi^{k-\ell}
we may assume
B={\operatorname{I}}_{n-k}
, i.e.,

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&C^{\prime}\\
0&{\operatorname{I}}_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
C^{\prime}=CB^{j-i-1}\neq 0
.

Now let
y=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
,
z=(x_{k+1},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})
,
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n-k}
such that
C\beta\neq 0
. Then for all
m\geq 1
,

\phi^{m}(z^{\beta})
up to an invertible multiplicative scalar is equal to

y^{mC^{\prime}\beta}z^{\beta}
. Since
mC^{\prime}\beta

(m\geq 1)
are all distinct,

\operatorname{Im\,}\phi
contains infinitely distinct
monomials, which contradicts to the assumption
that
\phi
is LF. Therefore
C^{\prime}=0
, and hence

, as desired.

{\mathcal{A}}[z^{-1},z]
consisting of the Laurent polynomials with the constant term in
V
. Then
\widetilde{V}
is a MS of
{\mathcal{A}}[z^{-1},z]
, if (and only if)
V
is a MS of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Next, we use Theorem
3.10
to show two special cases of
the LFED conjecture
1.2
for
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.12
.

In terms of notation as in Lemma
3.8
, assume
A_{1}=\pm{\operatorname{I}}_{k}
.
Given
f\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
such that
f^{m}\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}-\phi)
for all
m\geq 1
we have

0\not\in\operatorname{Poly}_{z}f
,
where
\operatorname{Poly}_{z}\,f
is the polytope of
f(x)(=f(y,z))
as
a Laurent polynomial in
z
over
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
.
In particular,
\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}-\phi)
is a MS of
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
.

Proof:
Note that the second statement follows from the first one, by
a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
3.7
,
2)
.

To show the first statement, we assume otherwise and fix a nonzero
f(x)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

such that
0\in\operatorname{Poly}_{z}f(y,z)
but
f^{m}\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}-\phi)
for all
m\geq 1
. We drive a contradiction as follows.

First, for all
a(y)=\sum_{\alpha\in E_{1}}c_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}

the following statements can be readily verified:

i)

if
A_{1}={\operatorname{I}}_{k}
, then
a(y)\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}-\phi_{1})
, if and only if

c_{\alpha}=0
for all
\alpha\in E_{1}
such that
q^{\alpha}=1
;

ii)

if
A_{1}=-{\operatorname{I}}_{k}
, then
\phi^{2}=\phi
, and
by
[
Z4
, Proposition 5.2]
we have

\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}-\phi_{1})=\operatorname{\rm Ker\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}+\phi_{1})
. Then it is easy to see that

a(y)\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}-\phi_{1})
, if and only if
c_{-\alpha}=-q^{\alpha}c_{\alpha}

for all
\alpha\in E_{1}
.

Then by Theorem
3.10
and the properties
i)
and
ii)
above we have

iii)

if
A_{1}=\pm I
, then
for a Laurent polynomial

h(x)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
we have that
h^{m}\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}-\phi)
for all
m\geq 1
,
if and only if
q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq n)
and the coefficients of
h(x)

satisfy a certain sequence of polynomial equations
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
K
be the field of fractions of the subring of
R

generated by
q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq n)
and the coefficients of
f(x)
.
Then by Property
iii)
above and a reduction similar
as the one in Section
4
in
[
FPYZ
]
we may assume that
K

is a subfield of the algebraic closure
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Therefore, for each prime
p\geq 2
the
p
-valuation
\nu_{p}
of
{\mathbb{Q}}

can be extended to
K
, which we will still denote by
\nu_{p}
.

Applying Theorems
3.6
and
3.10
we may assume

(3.8)

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=a_{0}(y)+\sum_{0\neq\beta\in E_{2}}a_{\beta}(y)z^{\beta},

(3.9)

\displaystyle a_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in E_{1}}c_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}

such that
a_{0}(y)\neq 0
and
a_{0}(y)\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}-\phi_{1})
.

Note that by Properties
i)
and
ii)

a_{0}(y)
can not
be a constant in
K
, so we can fix a nonzero extremal
point
\lambda\in E_{1}
of
\operatorname{Poly}_{y}a_{0}(y)
.
Let
d
be an
even
positive integer such that for all
m\geq 1

(3.10)

\displaystyle q^{dm\lambda}=1,

(3.11)

\displaystyle c_{-\lambda}^{md}

\displaystyle\neq-q^{md\alpha}c_{\lambda}^{md}.

Note that such an integer
d
does exist. This is because that,
first,

q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq k)
by Lemma
3.8
,
1)

are all roots of unity and, second,
c_{\lambda}\neq 0
and, third,
by Property
ii)
above
c_{-\lambda}=-q^{\lambda}c_{\lambda}
.

Therefore, by Properties
i)

and
ii)
above we have for all
m\geq 1

(3.12)

\displaystyle a_{0}(y)^{md}

\displaystyle\not\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}-\phi_{1}).

By Dirichlet’s prime number theorem there
exist infinitely many
m\geq 1
such that
p\!:=md-1
is prime.
Furthermore, it is well-known in Algebraic Number Theory
(e.g., see
[
W
, Theorem 4.1.7]
) that for all but finitely such primes
p
,
the values of
\nu_{p}
at
q_{i}

(1\leq i\leq k)
and
the nonzero coefficients of
f(x)
are equal to
0
.
For all primes
p
with these properties we consider

(3.13)

\displaystyle f^{p}(x)\equiv a_{0}^{p}(y)+\sum_{0\neq\beta\in E_{2}}a_{\beta}^{p}(y)z^{p\beta}\mod S_{p}[x^{-1},x],

where
S_{p}
is the subring of
K
formed by the elements

u\in K
such that
\nu_{p}(u)\geq 1
.

Choosing
p
large enough we assume
p\beta+\gamma\neq 0
for all

0\neq\beta,\gamma\in\text{Poly}_{z}\,f(y,z)
.
Then by Eq. (
3.13
)
the constant term of

f^{p+1}
(viewed as a Laurent polynomial in

{\mathcal{B}}_{1}[z^{-1},z]
) is equal to
a_{0}^{p+1}
modular

S_{p}[y^{-1},y]
.

By the choice of
p
we have

p+1=md
. Since
f^{p+1}(x)=f^{md}(x)\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}-\phi)
,
by Eq. (
3.12
) and Theorem
3.10

there exists a nonzero
b_{p+1}(x)\in S_{p}[x,x^{-1}]

such that

(3.14)

\displaystyle a_{0}^{p+1}(x)+b_{p+1}(x)

\displaystyle\in\operatorname{Im\,}({\operatorname{I}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{1}}-\phi_{1}).

Since
\lambda
is an extremal point
of
\operatorname{Poly}_{y}a_{0}(y)
, we have that

(p+1)\lambda
is an extremal point of

\operatorname{Poly}_{y}a_{0}^{p+1}(y)
, and the coefficient of

x^{(p+1)\lambda}
in

is equal to
c_{\lambda}^{p+1}
.
Then by Eqs. (
3.10
), (
3.11
), (
3.14
),
and Property
i)
,
ii)
above we have
c_{\lambda}^{p+1}+u_{p+1}=0
, where

is the coefficient of
x^{(p+1)\lambda}
in

.
Hence

, which implies

\nu_{p}(c_{\lambda}^{p+1})\geq 1
.
Therefore we also have

\nu_{p}(c_{\lambda})\geq 1
. But this contradicts to the choice
of
p
with
\nu_{p}(c_{\lambda})=0
.

Next, we show the LFEN conjecture (and also the LNED conjecture)
for the Laurent polynomial algebras
over a field of characteristic zero in one or
two commutative free variables.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
K
be a field of char zero and
{\mathcal{A}}
the Laurent polynomial algebra over
K
in one or two commutative variables. Then both the LFED Conjecture
1.2
and LNED Conjecture

holds for
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof:
By Theorem
3.1
we only need to show
the
K
-
{\mathcal{E}}
-derivation case for the LFED
Conjecture

. Furthermore,
the one variable case follows from Corollary

above. So we may assume that

with

,

is the Laurent polynomial algebra over
K

Let
\phi
be a LF
K
-algebra endomorphism of
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
.
We use the same notations as before, in particular,
let

.
If the rank of

3.3
,
