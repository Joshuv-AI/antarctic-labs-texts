Lower bounds for the first eigenvalue of the p p -Laplacian on quaternionic Kähler manifolds Thanks: The research of the first author is supported by NSF of Jiangsu Province No. SBK2023020968
Kui Wang, Shaoheng Zhang

Abstract
. We study the first nonzero eigenvalues for the p p -Laplacian on quaternionic Kähler manifolds. Our first result is a lower bound for the first nonzero closed (Neumann) eigenvalue of the p p -Laplacian on compact quaternionic Kähler manifolds. Our second result is a lower bound for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the p p -Laplacian on compact quaternionic Kähler manifolds with smooth boundary. Our results generalize corresponding results for the Laplacian eigenvalues on quaternionic Kähler manifolds proved in [ 22 ] .

1.
Introduction and Main Results

Let
(M^{m},g)
be an
m
-dimensional compact manifold, and the
p
-Laplacian of the metric
g
is defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}u:=\operatorname{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)

for
p\in(1,\infty)
. The
p
-Laplace eigenvalue equation is

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}u(x)=\lambda|u(x)|^{p-2}u(x),\quad x\in M,

and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is called a closed eigenvalue when
\partial M=\emptyset
, a Dirichlet eigenvalue if
u(x)=0
on
\partial M
when
\partial M\neq\emptyset
, a Neumann eigenvalue if
\partial_{\nu}u(x)=0
on
\partial M
when
\partial M\neq\emptyset
. Here
\nu
denotes the unit outer normal to
\partial M
.

Estimates of the first nonzero eigenvalues have been extensively studied in both mathematics and physics for a long time. In the past few decades, a number of important results on lower bounds in terms of geometric data were obtained by various authors (
[
1
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
12
,
24
,
26
,
28
,
29
]
).
One of the classical results is the following optimal lower bound for the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M^{m},g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold (possibly with a smooth convex boundary) with diameter
D
and
\operatorname{Ric}\geq(m-1)\kappa
for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mu_{1}
be the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
M
(with Neumann boundary condition if
\partial M\neq\emptyset
). Then

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\geq\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,\kappa,D),

where
\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,\kappa,D)
is the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(m-1)T_{\kappa}\varphi^{\prime}=-\mu\varphi

on the interval
[-D/2,D/2]
, and
T_{\kappa}
is defined in (
1.9
).

For
\kappa>0
, Lichnerowicz’s estimate
[
23
]
asserts that if
M
is closed with
\text{Ric}\geq(m-1)\kappa>0
, then
\mu_{1}\geq m\kappa
.
Escobar
[
11
]
proved that the same lower bound as in Lichnerowicz’s result holds if
M
is compact with a convex boundary.
If
\kappa=0
, Zhong and Yang
[
32
]
proved the sharp lower bound that
\mu_{1}\geq\pi^{2}/D^{2}
, by refining the gradient estimates of Li
[
17
]
and Li and Yau
[
18
]
.
For general
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, Theorem
1.1
was proved by Kröger
[
16
]
using the gradient estimates method, and by Chen and Wang
[
8
,
9
]
using probabilistic coupling method independently.
Recently, Andrews and Clutterbuck
[
2
]
gave a simple proof via the modulus of continuity estimates for the solutions to the heat equation, and Zhang and the first author of this paper
[
31
]
gave an elliptic proof based on
[
2
]
and
[
25
]
.

On compact quaternionic Kähler manifolds, Li and the first author of this paper
[
22
]
proved the following lower bounds of the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold (possibly with a smooth strictly convex boundary) of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
and diameter D. Suppose that the scalar curvature of
M
is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mu_{1}
be the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
M
(with Neumann boundary condition if
\partial M\neq\emptyset)
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\geq\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,\kappa,D),

where
\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,\kappa,D)
is the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(4(m-1)T_{\kappa}+3T_{4\kappa})\varphi^{\prime}=-\lambda\varphi

on
[-D/2,D/2]
, where
T_{\kappa}(t)
is defined in (
1.9
).

On compact Kähler manifolds, Li and the first author of this paper
[
20
]
proved lower bounds of the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian, which was generalized by the authors
[
30
]
to the
p
-Laplacian for
1<p\leq 2
. Blacker and Seto
[
6
]
proved a lower bound for the first nontrivial eigenvalue of the
p
-Laplacian on Kähler manifolds assuming positive Ricci curvature. Recently, Rutkowski and Seto
[
27
]
established an explicit lower bound for the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian on Kähler manifolds based off the comparison results of Li and the first author of this paper
[
20
]
. It is thus a natural question to study the first nonzero eigenvalue of the
p
-Laplacian on quaternionic Kähler manifolds. Regarding this, we prove

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
and diameter
D
, whose scalar curvature is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mu_{1,p}(M)
be the first nonzero eigenvalue of the
p
-Laplacian on M (with Neumann boundary condition if
M
has a strictly convex boundary). Assume
1<p\leq 2
, then

\displaystyle\mu_{1,p}(M)\geq\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,D),

where
\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,D)
is the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

(1.2)

\displaystyle(p-1)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(4(m-1)T_{\kappa}+3T_{4\kappa})|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime}=-\mu|\varphi|^{p-2}\varphi

on interval
[-D/2,D/2]
, where
T_{\kappa}(t)
is defined in (
1.9
).

Note that if
\kappa=0
, ODE (
1.2
) can be solved by using
p
-trigonometric functions, and we get an explicit lower bound.

Corollary 1.4
.

With the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.3
, and assume further that
\kappa=0
. Then

(1.3)

\mu_{1,p}(M)\geq(p-1)(\frac{\pi_{p}}{D})^{p},

where
\displaystyle\pi_{p}=\frac{2\pi}{p\sin(\pi/p)}
.

Now let us turn to lower bounds for the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian. For
\kappa,\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, denote by
C_{\kappa,\Lambda}
the unique solution of the initial value problem

(1.4)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\kappa\phi(t)=0,\\
&\phi(0)=1,\ \phi^{\prime}(0)=-\Lambda,\end{aligned}\right.

and denote
T_{\kappa,\Lambda}
for

\kappa,\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
by

(1.5)

\displaystyle T_{\kappa,\Lambda}(t):=-\frac{C_{\kappa,\Lambda}^{\prime}(t)}{C_{\kappa,\Lambda}(t)}.

Recall the inradius of
M
is defined by

R=\displaystyle\sup_{x\in M}d(x,\partial M),

where
d(x,\partial M)
denotes the distance function to the boundary
\partial M
given by

d(x,\partial M)=\inf_{y\in\partial M}d(x,y).

In the Riemannian setting, the following result is well-known.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(M^{m},g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold with smooth boundary
\partial M\neq\emptyset
. Suppose that the Ricci curvature of
M
is bounded from below by
(m-1)\kappa
and the mean curvature of
\partial M
is bounded from below by
(m-1)\Lambda
for some
\kappa,\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\lambda_{1}
be the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
M
. Then

\lambda_{1}\geq\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,\kappa,\Lambda,R),

where
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,\kappa,\Lambda,R)
is the first eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(m-1)T_{\kappa,\Lambda}\varphi^{\prime}=-\lambda\varphi,\\
\varphi(0)=0,\ \varphi^{\prime}(R)=0.\end{array}\right.

Here
R
denotes the inradius of
M
.

Regarding Theorem
1.5
, the special case
\kappa=\Lambda=0
is due to Li and Yau
[
18
]
and the general case is obtained by Kasue
[
14
]
.

In
[
22
]
, Li and the first author of this paper obtained an analogous theorem in the quaternionic Kähler setting.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold with smooth boundary
\partial M
and of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
.
Suppose that the scalar curvature is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, and the second fundamental form on
\partial M
is bounded from below by
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\lambda_{1}
be the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
M
. Then

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\geq\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,\kappa,\Lambda,R),

where
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,\kappa,\Lambda,R)
is the first eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(4(m-1)T_{\kappa,\Lambda}+3T_{4\kappa,\Lambda})\varphi^{\prime}=-\bar{\lambda}_{1}\varphi,\\
\varphi(0)=0,\varphi^{\prime}(R)=0,\end{cases}

where
R
denotes the inradius of
M
.

Our second main result is the following
p
-Laplace analogue of the lower bounds for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue on quaternionic Kähler manifolds.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold with smooth boundary
\partial M
and of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
.
Suppose that the scalar curvature is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, and the second fundamental form on
\partial M
is bounded from below by
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\lambda_{1,p}(M)
be the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the
p
-Laplacian on
M
.
Assume
1<p<\infty
, then

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,p}(M)\geq\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,\Lambda,R),

where
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,\Lambda,R)
is the first eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

(1.8)

\displaystyle(p-1)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\big(4(m-1)T_{\kappa,\Lambda}+3T_{4\kappa,\Lambda}\big)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime}=-\lambda|\varphi|^{p-2}\varphi

on
[0,R]
with boundary conditions
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi^{\prime}(R)=0
, and
R
denotes the inradius of
M
.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
relies on a comparison theorem for the second derivatives of distance function to the boundary proved in
[
22
, Theorem 5.7]
and Barta’s inequality.

2.
Quaternionic Kähler manifolds

In this section, we recall some basic properties about quaternionic Kähler manifolds. These properties are due to Berger
[
5
]
and Ishihara
[
13
]
. We shall follow the presentation in Kong, Li and Zhou
[
15
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A quaternionic Kähler manifold
(M^{m},g)
of quaternionic dimension
m
(the real dimension is
4m
) is a Riemannian manifold with a rank three vector bundle
V\subset\text{End}(TM)
satisfying the following properties.

(1)

In any coordinate neighborhood
U
of
M
, there exists a local basis
\big\{I,J,K\big\}
of
V
such that

\displaystyle I^{2}=J^{2}=K^{2}=-1,

\displaystyle IJ=-JI=K,

\displaystyle JK=-KJ=I,

\displaystyle KI=-IK=J,

and for all
X,Y\in TM

\langle X,Y\rangle=\langle IX,IY\rangle=\langle JX,JY\rangle=\langle KX,KY\rangle.

(2)

If
\phi\in\Gamma(V)
, then
\nabla_{X}\phi\in\Gamma(V)
for all
X\in TM
.

The Riemannian curvature tensor of
(M,g)
is defined by

R(X,Y,Z,W)=\langle-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z+\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z+\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,W\rangle.

In this paper, we are mostly concerned with the curvature properties of quaternionic Kähler manifolds.
As in
[
15
]
and
[
4
]
, we define

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(M^{m},g)
be a quaternionic Kähler manifold.

(1)

The quaternionic sectional curvature of
M
is defined as

Q(X):=\frac{R(X,IX,X,IX)+R(X,JX,X,JX)+R(X,KX,X,KX)}{|X|^{2}}.

(2)

The orthogonal Ricci curvature of
M
is defined as

\operatorname{Ric}^{\perp}(X,X):=\operatorname{Ric}(X,X)-Q(X).

First of all, all quaternionic Kähler manifolds of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
are Einstein (cf.
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
), namely there exists a constant
\kappa
such that

\text{Ric}=4(m+2)\kappa.

Moreover, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(M^{m},g)
be a quaternionic Kähler manifold of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
with
\mathrm{Ric}=4(m+2)\kappa
for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

M
has constant quaternionic sectional curvature

Q(X)=12\kappa|X|^{2},

and constant orthogonal Ricci curvature

\operatorname{Ric}^{\perp}(X,X)=4(m-1)\kappa|X|^{2}.

Proof.

See Theorem 1.3 of
[
15
]
.

\square

We end this section with the following useful lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(M^{m},g)
be a quaternionic Kähler manifold of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
with
\operatorname{Ric}=4(m+2)\kappa
for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow M
be a geodesic with unit speed and
X_{I}(t),X_{J}(t),X_{K}(t)
are parallel vector fields along
\gamma
such that
X_{I}(a)=I\gamma^{\prime}(a),X_{J}(a)=J\gamma^{\prime}(a),X_{K}(a)=K\gamma^{\prime}(a)
. Then

R(\gamma^{\prime},X_{I}(t),\gamma^{\prime},X_{I}(t))+R(\gamma^{\prime},X_{J}(t),\gamma^{\prime},X_{J}(t))+R(\gamma^{\prime},X_{K}(t),\gamma^{\prime},X_{K}(t))=12\kappa

for all
t\in[a,b]
.

Proof.

See Lemma 1.5 of
[
15
]
.

\square

3.
Modulus of Continuity Estimates

In this section, we prove modulus of continuity estimates for solutions of a fully nonlinear parabolic equation (
3.1
) on a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold, which is the key step in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold (possibly with non-empty strictly convex boundary) of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
and diameter
D
, whose scalar curvature is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose that
T\in(0,\infty]
,
1<p\leq 2
and
v\in C^{2,1}(M\times[0,T))
is a solution of

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(x,t)=|\Delta_{p}v(x,t)|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\Delta_{p}v(x,t)

with Neumann boundary condition if
\partial M\neq\emptyset
.
Suppose
\varphi(s,t)\in C^{2,1}\big([0,D/2]\times[0,T)\big)
satisfies the following properties

(1)

v(y,0)-v(x,0)\leq 2\varphi(\frac{d(x,y)}{2},0),\ x,y\in M
;

(2)

0\geq\varphi_{t}\geq|\mathcal{F}\varphi|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\mathcal{F}\varphi,\ (s,t)\in[0,D/2]\times[0,T)
;

(3)

\varphi^{\prime}(s,t)>0,\ (s,t)\in[0,D/2]\times[0,T)
;

(4)

\varphi(0,t)\geq 0,\ t\in[0,T)
.

Then

(3.2)

\displaystyle v(y,t)-v(x,t)\leq 2\varphi(\frac{d(x,y)}{2},t)

for
(x,y,t)\in M\times M\times[0,T)
.
Here one-dimensional operator
\mathcal{F}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\varphi:=(p-1)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(4(m-1)T_{\kappa}+3T_{4\kappa})|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime},

and we denote
\frac{\partial^{k}\varphi}{\partial s^{k}}
by
\varphi^{(k)}
for short throughout the paper.

Proof.

The proof proceeds as in the Kähler case in
[
30
]
but slightly more involved.
Let
\varepsilon>0
, and

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t):=v(y,t)-v(x,t)-2\varphi(\frac{d(x,y)}{2},t)-\varepsilon e^{t}.

To prove (
3.2
), it suffices to show that
C_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)<0
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

We argue by contradiction and assume that there exists
(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})
such that
C_{\varepsilon}
attains its maximum zero on

M\times M\times[0,t_{0}]
at
(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})
.
Clearly
x_{0}\neq y_{0}
, and
t_{0}>0
.
If
\partial M\neq\emptyset
,
similarly as in
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
,
the strictly convexity of
\partial M
, the Neumann condition and the positivity of
\varphi^{\prime}
rule out the possibility that
x_{0}\in\partial M
and
y_{0}\in\partial M
.

Compactness of
M
implies that there exists an arc-length minimizing geodesic
\gamma_{0}
connecting
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
such that
\gamma_{0}(-s_{0})=x_{0}
and
\gamma_{0}(s_{0})=y_{0}
with
s_{0}=d(x_{0},y_{0})/2
.
We pick an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{4m}
for
T_{x_{0}}M
with

e_{1}=\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(-s_{0}),\ e_{2}=I\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(-s_{0}),\ e_{3}=J\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(-s_{0}),\ e_{4}=K\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(-s_{0}),

and parallel transport it along
\gamma_{0}
to produce an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}(s)\}_{i=1}^{4m}
for
T_{\gamma_{0}(s)}M
with
e_{1}(s)=\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(s)
for each
s\in[-s_{0},s_{0}]
.

To calculate the derivatives, we recall the first and second variation formulas of arc length.
Suppose

\gamma(r,s):[-\delta,\delta]\times[-s_{0},s_{0}]\rightarrow M
is a smooth variation of
\gamma_{0}
such that
\gamma(0,s)=\gamma_{0}(s)
, then the variation formulas give

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\big|_{r=0}L[\gamma(r,s)]=g(T,\gamma_{r})\big|_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}},

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}\big|_{r=0}L[\gamma(r,s)]=\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\big(|(\nabla_{s}\gamma_{r})^{\perp}|^{2}-R(T,\gamma_{r},T,\gamma_{r})\big)\ \mathrm{d}s+g(T,\nabla_{r}\gamma_{r})\big|_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}},

where
T
is the unit tangent vector to
\gamma_{0}
.

First derivative inequality yields

(3.5)

\displaystyle 0\leq\partial_{t}C_{\varepsilon}(x_{0},y_{0},t_{0})=v_{t}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{t}(x_{0},t_{0})-2\varphi_{t}(s_{0},t_{0})-\varepsilon e^{t_{0}},

and

\displaystyle\nabla v(x_{0},t_{0})=\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0},t_{0})e_{1}(-s_{0}),\quad\nabla v(y_{0},t_{0})=\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0},t_{0})e_{1}(s_{0}).

Then we get

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}v(y_{0},t_{0})-\Delta_{p}v(x_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p-1)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\big(v_{11}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{11}(x_{0},t_{0})\big)+|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\sum_{i=2}^{4m}\big(v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})\big).

To calculate the second derivative along
e_{1}
, we consider the variation
\gamma(r,s):=\gamma_{0}(s+r\frac{s}{s_{0}})
, then the formulas (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) give

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\big|_{r=0}L[\gamma(r)]=2,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}\big|_{r=0}L[\gamma(r)]=0.

Hence the second derivative test for this variation produces

(3.7)

\displaystyle v_{11}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{11}(x_{0},t_{0})-2\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s_{0},t_{0})\leq 0.

To calculate the second derivative along
e_{i}
(
i=2,3,4
), we consider the variation

\displaystyle\gamma(r,s):=\operatorname{exp}_{\gamma_{0}(s)}(r\eta(s)e_{i}(s)),\ i=2,3,4,

where
\eta(s)=\frac{c_{4\kappa}(s)}{c_{4\kappa}(s_{0})}
and
c_{\kappa}(s)
is defined in (
1.10
). Clearly formulas (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) imply

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\bigg|_{r=0}L[\gamma(r)]=0,

and

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}\bigg|_{r=0}L[\gamma(r)]=\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\bigg((\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\eta^{2}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\bigg)\ \mathrm{d}s.

So this variation produces

\displaystyle v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})-\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0},t_{0})\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\bigg((\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\eta^{2}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\bigg)\ \mathrm{d}s\leq 0.

Summing over
2\leq i\leq 4
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{4}\big(v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\bigg(3(\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\eta^{2}\sum_{i=2}^{4}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\bigg)\ \mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\varphi^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\eta|_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}-\varphi^{\prime}\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\eta^{2}\big(\sum_{i=2}^{4}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})-12\kappa\big)\ \mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-6T_{4\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime},

where we have used the assumption that
\varphi^{\prime}(s,t)>0
and
\sum_{i=2}^{4}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\geq 12\kappa
, in the view of Proposition
2.1
.
Therefore we have

(3.8)

\sum_{i=2}^{4}\big(v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})\big)\leq-6T_{4\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0},t_{0}).

To calculate the second derivative along
e_{i}
(
5\leq i\leq 4m
), we consider the variation

\gamma(r,s):=\operatorname{exp}_{\gamma_{0}(s)}(r\zeta(s)e_{i}(s)),

where
\zeta(s)=\frac{c_{\kappa}(s)}{c_{\kappa}(s_{0})}
.
Similarly, the second variation formula gives

v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})\leq\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0},t_{0})\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\bigg((\zeta^{\prime})^{2}-\zeta^{2}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\bigg)\ \mathrm{d}s.

Summing over
5\leq i\leq 4m
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=5}^{4m}\big(v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-8(m-1)T_{\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime}-\varphi^{\prime}\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}\zeta^{2}\big(\sum_{i=5}^{4m}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})-4(m-1)\kappa\big)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-8(m-1)T_{\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime},

where in the last inequality we have used

\sum_{i=5}^{4m}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})=\operatorname{Ric}^{\perp}(e_{1},e_{1})\geq 4(m-1)\kappa,

in the view of Proposition
2.1
.
Thus we get

(3.9)

\sum_{i=5}^{4m}\big(v_{ii}(y_{0},t_{0})-v_{ii}(x_{0},t_{0})\big)\leq-8(m-1)T_{\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0},t_{0}).

Plugging inequalities (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) into equation (
3.6
), we have

(3.10)

\Delta_{p}v(y_{0},t_{0})-\Delta_{p}v(x_{0},t_{0})\leq 2\mathcal{F}\varphi(s_{0},t_{0}).

Let
h(t):=|t|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}t
, which is odd, increasing and convex for all
t>0
. We rewrite inequality (
3.10
) as

h\big(\Delta_{p}v(y_{0},t_{0})\big)\leq h\big(\Delta_{p}v(x_{0},t_{0})+2\mathcal{F}\varphi\big).

Applying Lemma 2.1 of
[
21
]
with

t=\Delta_{p}v(x_{0},t_{0})
and

\delta=-\mathcal{F}\varphi\geq 0
, we get

h\big(\Delta_{p}v(x_{0},t_{0})+2\mathcal{F}\varphi\big)\leq h\big(\Delta_{p}v(x_{0},t_{0}))+2h(\mathcal{F}\varphi\big),

that is

(3.11)

\frac{1}{2}\Big(|\Delta_{p}v|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\Delta_{p}v\big|_{(y_{0},t_{0})}-|\Delta_{p}v|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\Delta_{p}v\big|_{(x_{0},t_{0})}\Big)\leq|\mathcal{F}\varphi|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\mathcal{F}\varphi.

Combining (
3.5
) and (
3.11
), we get

\varphi_{t}\leq|\mathcal{F}\varphi|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\mathcal{F}\varphi-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}e^{t_{0}}<|\mathcal{F}\varphi|^{-\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\mathcal{F}\varphi,

which contradicts the inequality in assumption (2). Therefore

C_{\varepsilon}(x,y,t)<0

holds true for
(x,y,t)\in M\times M\times[0,T)
. Hence we complete the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

\square

As in the Kähler case, the modulus of continuity estimates imply lower bounds for the first nonzero eigenvalue of the
p
-Laplacian on a quaternionic Kähler manifold. We restate Theorem
1.3
here.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold of quaternionic dimension
m\geq 2
and diameter
D
, whose scalar curvature is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mu_{1,p}(M)
be the first nonzero eigenvalue of the
p
-Laplacian on M (with Neumann boundary condition if
M
has a strictly convex boundary). Assume
1<p\leq 2
, then

\displaystyle\mu_{1,p}(M)\geq\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,D),

where
\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,D)
is the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

(3.12)

\displaystyle(p-1)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-(4(m-1)T_{\kappa}+3T_{4\kappa})|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime}=-\mu|\varphi|^{p-2}\varphi

on interval
[-D/2,D/2]
.

Proof.

The proof is a slight modification of the proof in the Kähler case in
[
30
]
, so we leave the details to interested readers.

\square

4.
The First Dirichlet Eigenvalue

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
The key tools are a comparison theorem for the distance function to the boundary, and Barta’s inequality.
Let
M
be a compact manifold with smooth boundary, and define the distance function to the boundary of
M
by

\displaystyle d(x,\partial M)=\inf\Big\{d(x,y):y\in\partial M\Big\},

and the inradius of
M
by
R=\mathop{\sup}\limits_{x\in M}d(x,\partial M)
.

By choosing
\alpha=(p-1)|\nabla\varphi|^{p-2}
and
\beta=|\nabla\varphi|^{p-2}
in Theorem 5.7 of
[
22
]
, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(M^{m},g,I,J,K)
be a compact quaternionic Kähler manifold with smooth boundary, and
R
be the inradius of
M
.
Suppose that the scalar curvature is bounded from below by
16m(m+2)\kappa
for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, and the second fundamental form on
\partial M
is bounded from below by
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
p\in(1,\infty)
, and assume
\varphi
is a smooth function on
[0,R]
satisfying
\varphi^{\prime}\geq 0
.
Then for any smooth function
\psi
satisfying

\displaystyle\psi(x)\leq\varphi(d(x,\partial M))\ \ \text{for}\ \ x\in M,\ \ \text{and}\ \ \psi(x_{0})=\varphi(d(x_{0},\partial M)),

it holds

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}\psi(x_{0})\leq\bar{\mathcal{F}}\varphi(d(x_{0},\partial M)).

Here one-dimensional operator
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}\varphi=(p-1)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\big(4(m-1)T_{\kappa,\Lambda}+3T_{4\kappa,\Lambda}\big)|\varphi^{\prime}|^{p-2}\varphi^{\prime}

for all
\varphi\in C^{2}([0,R])
, and
T_{\kappa,\Lambda}
is defined by (
1.5
).

Consider the following one-dimensional eigenvalue problem on
[0,R]
that

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\bar{\mathcal{F}}\varphi=-\lambda|\varphi|^{p-2}\varphi,\\
&\varphi(0)=0,\ \varphi^{\prime}(R)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

and denote by
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(m,p,\kappa,\Lambda,R)
(written as
\bar{\lambda}_{1}
for short) the first eigenvalue of problem (
4.1
). Then it is easy to see that
\bar{\lambda}_{1}
can be characterized by

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{1}=\inf\bigg\{\frac{\int_{0}^{R}|\phi^{\prime}|^{p}C_{\kappa,\Lambda}^{4m-4}C_{4\kappa,\Lambda}^{3}\ \mathrm{d}s}{\int_{0}^{R}|\phi|^{p}C_{\kappa,\Lambda}^{4m-4}C_{4\kappa,\Lambda}^{3}\ \mathrm{d}s}\ \big|\phi\in W^{1,p}\big((0,R)\big)\backslash\{0\},\ \phi(0)=0\bigg\},

where
C_{\kappa,\Lambda}
is defined by (
1.4
).

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let
\varphi
be an eigenfunction corresponding to
\bar{\lambda}_{1}
, then

\bar{\mathcal{F}}\varphi=-\bar{\lambda}_{1}|\varphi|^{p-2}\varphi

with
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi^{\prime}(R)=0
.
Similarly as in the proof of
[
20
, Lemma 4.2]
, we can choose
\varphi
such that
\varphi(s)>0
on
(0,R]
, and
\varphi^{\prime}(s)>0
on
[0,R)
. Define a trial function for
\lambda_{1,p}(M)
by

v(x):=\varphi(d(x,\partial M)),

then Lemma
4.1
gives

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}v(x)\leq\bar{\mathcal{F}}\varphi(d(x,\partial M))

away from the cut locus of
\partial M
, and thus globally in the distributional sense, see
[
19
, Lemma 5.2]
.
Recall that
\varphi
is an eigenfunction with respect to
\bar{\lambda}_{1}
, namely

(4.3)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}\varphi(d(x,\partial M))=-\bar{\lambda}_{1}|v(x)|^{p-2}v(x),

thus we conclude from (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) that

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}v\leq-\bar{\lambda}_{1}|v|^{p-2}v,\quad x\in M.

By the construction of
v(x)
, we see
v(x)>0
for
x\in M
, and
v(x)=0
for
x\in\partial M
. Thus we conclude from Barta’s inequality (cf.
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
) that

\lambda_{1,p}(M)\geq\bar{\lambda}_{1}.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
is complete.

\square

5.
Elliptic Proof of Theorem
1.2

By using modulus of continuity for solutions to the heat equation, Li and the first author of this paper
[
22
]
proved the lower bound estimates for the first nonzero eigenvalue on quaternionic Kähler manifolds (see Theorem
1.2
).
In this section, following Ni’s method (see Section 6 of
[
25
]
), we give an elliptic proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Elliptic proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
u
be the eigenfunction with respect to the first nonzero closed eigenvalue (or Neumann eigenvalue), and
\varphi
be the first Neumann eigenfunction of (
1.1
), and we consider the quotient of the oscillations of
u
and
\varphi

Q(x,y):=\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{\varphi\big(\frac{d(x,y)}{2}\big)}

on
\overline{M}\times\overline{M}\setminus\Delta
,
where
\Delta:=\{(x,x):x\in\overline{M}\}
denotes the diagonal.
The function
Q
can be extended to a set
(\overline{M}\times\overline{M}\setminus\Delta)\cup UM
. Here
UM:=\{(x,X):x\in M,|X|=1\}
. On
UM
, with the extension
Q(x,X)
defined as

Q(x,X):=\frac{2g(\nabla u(x),X)}{\varphi^{\prime}(0)}.

Now we divide the proof into two cases.

Case 1.
the maximum of
Q
, which is clearly positive and denoted by
G
, is attained at some
(x_{0},y_{0})
with
x_{0}\neq y_{0}
. The Neumann condition and strict convexity of
M
forces that both
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
must be in
M
. Indeed if
x_{0}\in\partial M
, then taking derivative along the exterior normal direction
\nu
at
x_{0}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{x_{0}}Q(x,y_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{x_{0}}\frac{u(y_{0})-u(x)}{\varphi\big(\frac{d(x,y_{0})}{2}\big)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{G\varphi^{\prime}(\frac{d(x_{0},y_{0})}{2})}{2\varphi(\frac{d(x_{0},y_{0})}{2})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\big|_{x_{0}}d(x,y_{0})

\displaystyle<0,

which is a contradiction with the maximum assumption. Here in the last inequality, we used the convexity assumption of
M
.

As in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, there exists a length-minimizing geodesic
\gamma_{0}:[-s_{0},s_{0}]\rightarrow M
such that it connects
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
with
s_{0}=d(x_{0},y_{0})/2
. We can also choose an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}(s)\}_{i=1}^{4m}
for
T_{\gamma_{0}(s)}M
with
e_{1}(s)=\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(s)
for each
s\in[-s_{0},s_{0}]
and

e_{2}(-s_{0})=I\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(-s_{0}),\quad e_{3}(-s_{0})=J\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(-s_{0}),\quad e_{4}(-s_{0})=K\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(-s_{0}).

Then first derivative gives

\nabla u(x_{0})=\frac{G}{2}\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})e_{1}(-s_{0}),\quad\nabla u(y_{0})=\frac{G}{2}\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})e_{1}(s_{0}).

For second derivatives, from

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}\bigg|_{r=0}Q(\gamma_{0}(-s_{0}-r),\gamma_{0}(s_{0}+r))\leq 0,

we have

\displaystyle 0\geq

\displaystyle\frac{u_{11}(y_{0})-u_{11}(x_{0})}{\varphi(s_{0})}-\frac{u(y_{0})-u(x_{0})}{\varphi^{2}(s_{0})}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s_{0})

\displaystyle-2\frac{u_{1}(y_{0})+u_{1}(x_{0})}{\varphi^{2}(s_{0})}\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})+2G\big(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})}{\varphi(s_{0})}\big)^{2},

thus we have

(5.1)

0\geq u_{11}(y_{0})-u_{11}(x_{0})-G\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s_{0}).

For
i=2,3,4
, we consider the variation

\gamma(r,s)=\text{exp}_{\gamma_{0}(s)}(r\eta(s)e_{i}(s)),

where
\eta(s)=\frac{c_{4\kappa}(s)}{c_{4\kappa}(s_{0})}
. This variation gives

u_{ii}(y_{0})-u_{ii}(x_{0})-\frac{G}{2}\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}(\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\eta^{2}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\ \mathrm{d}s\leq 0.

For
5\leq i\leq 4m
, we consider the variation

\gamma(r,s)=\text{exp}_{\gamma_{0}(s)}(r\zeta(s)e_{i}(s)),

where
\zeta(s)=\frac{c_{\kappa}(s)}{c_{\kappa}(s_{0})}
.
This variation produces

u_{ii}(y_{0})-u_{ii}(x_{0})-\frac{G}{2}\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}-\zeta^{2}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\ \mathrm{d}s\leq 0.

Summing over
2\leq i\leq 4
, we obtain

\sum_{i=2}^{4}\big(u_{ii}(y_{0})-u_{ii}(x_{0})\big)-\frac{G}{2}\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})\int_{-s_{0}}^{s_{0}}3(\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\eta^{2}\sum_{i=2}^{4}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\ \mathrm{d}s\leq 0.

Using the assumption
\sum_{i=2}^{4}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})\geq 12\kappa
, we have

(5.2)

\sum_{i=2}^{4}\big(u_{ii}(y_{0})-u_{ii}(x_{0})\big)+3G\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})T_{4\kappa}(s_{0})\leq 0.

Similarly, summing over
5\leq i\leq 4m
, we get

(5.3)

\sum_{i=5}^{4m}\big(u_{ii}(y_{0})-u_{ii}(x_{0})\big)+4(m-1)G\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})T_{\kappa}(s_{0})\leq 0.

Combining (
5.1
), (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we have

\Delta u(y_{0})-\Delta u(x_{0})-G\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s_{0})+3GT_{4\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})+4G(m-1)T_{\kappa}(s_{0})\varphi^{\prime}(s_{0})\leq 0,

that is

-\mu_{1,2}(M)(u(y_{0})-u(x_{0}))+G\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,2,\kappa,D)\varphi(s_{0})\leq 0,

which proves
\mu_{1,2}(M)\geq\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,2,\kappa,D)
.

Case 2.
the maximum of
Q
is attained at some
(x_{0},X_{0})\in UM
.
It is easy to see that
X_{0}=\frac{\nabla u(x_{0})}{|\nabla u(x_{0})|}
and
G=2|\nabla u(x_{0})|
. By the assumption, we know
x_{0}\in M
. In fact if
x_{0}\in\partial M
, then taking derivarive along exterior normal direction
\nu
at
x_{0}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\bigg|_{x_{0}}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2g(\nabla_{\nabla u(x)}\nabla u(x),\nu)\big|_{x_{0}}

\displaystyle=-2\operatorname{II}(\nabla u(x_{0}),\nabla u(x_{0}))<0,

contradicting with the maximum assumption, where
\operatorname{II}
is the second fundamental form of
M
at
x_{0}
.

Now pick up an orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{4m}
at
x_{0}
so that
e_{1}=X_{0}
.
We also parallel transport it to a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

Since
|\nabla u(x)|^{2}
attains its maximum at the interior point
x_{0}
, we have

\nabla|\nabla u|^{2}(x_{0})=0,

hence

u_{1k}(x_{0})=0

for any
1\leq k\leq 4m
. Moreover, the maximum principle concludes for any
2\leq k\leq 4m
,

(5.4)

0\geq u_{1}u_{kk1}+|u_{1}|^{2}R(e_{1},e_{k},e_{1},e_{k}).

Let
x(s)=\text{exp}_{x_{0}}(-se_{1}),y(s)=\text{exp}_{x_{0}}(se_{1})
and
h(s)=Q(x(s),y(s))
. Since
Q
achieves its maximum at
(x_{0},X_{0})
, we have that

h(s)\leq h(0)=G,\quad s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),

which implies that
\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}h^{\prime}(s)=0
and
\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}h^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq 0
.

Direct calculation shows that

h^{\prime}(s)=\frac{g(\nabla u(y(s)),y^{\prime}(s))-g(\nabla u(x(s)),x^{\prime}(s))}{\varphi(s)}-h(s)\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}

and

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(s)=

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{e_{1}e_{1}}^{2}u(y(s))-\nabla_{e_{1}e_{1}}^{2}u(x(s))}{\varphi(s)}

\displaystyle-2\frac{g(\nabla u(y(s)),y^{\prime}(s))-g(\nabla u(x(s)),x^{\prime}(s))}{\varphi(s)}\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}

\displaystyle-h(s)\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}+2h(s)(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{e_{1}e_{1}}^{2}u(y(s))-\nabla_{e_{1}e_{1}}^{2}u(x(s))}{\varphi(s)}-2\frac{h^{\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}\varphi^{\prime}(s)-h(s)\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}.

Observing that
\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{h^{\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}=h^{\prime\prime}(0)
,
the second equation implies that

h^{\prime\prime}(0)=2u_{111}(x_{0})-2h^{\prime\prime}(0)-G\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}.

From Equation (
1.2
), it follows

\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}=\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\big(4(m-1)T_{\kappa}(s)+3T_{4\kappa}(s)\big)\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s)}{\varphi(s)}-\bar{\mu}_{1}=4(m-1)\kappa+12\kappa-\bar{\mu}_{1}.

Then we have

(5.5)

2u_{111}(x_{0})-G(4(m-1)\kappa+12\kappa)+G\bar{\mu}_{1}\leq 0.

Combining (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), we derive

2g(\nabla u,\nabla\Delta u)+2|u_{1}|^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{4m}R(e_{1},e_{i},e_{1},e_{i})-2|u_{1}|^{2}(4(m-1)\kappa+12\kappa)+2|u_{1}|^{2}\bar{\mu}_{1}\leq 0,

which also implies
\mu_{1,2}(M)\geq\bar{\mu}_{1}(m,2,\kappa,D)
.

\square
