A diffuse-interface Landau-de Gennes model for free-boundary problems in the theory of nematic liquid crystals
Dawei Wu, Baoming Shi, Yucen Han, Pingwen Zhang, Apala Majumdar, Lei Zhang

Abstract
We introduce a diffuse-interface Landau-de Gennes free energy for nematic liquid crystals (NLC) systems, with free boundaries, in three dimensions submerged in isotropic liquid, and a phase field is introduced to model the deformable interface. The energy consists of the original Landau-de Gennes free energy, three penalty terms and a volume constraint. We prove the existence and regularity of minimizers for the diffuse-interface energy functional. We also prove a uniform maximum principle of the minimizer under appropriate assumptions, together with a uniqueness result for small domains. Then, we establish a sharp-interface limit where minimizers of the diffuse-interface energy converge to a minimizer of a sharp-interface energy using methods from Γ \Gamma -convergence. Finally, we conduct numerical experiments with the diffuse-interface model and the findings are compared with existing works.

Introduction

Liquid crystals (LC) are an intermediate state between solids and liquids, in which positional order is partially or completely lost, but molecular anisotropy is still present
[
11
]
.
The simplest LC phase is the
nematic
phase for which the constituent molecules have no positional order, but prefer to align along certain locally preferred directions known as
directors
.
Nematic liquid crystals (NLCs) are endowed with direction-dependent physical, optical and rheological properties
[
54
]
.
NLCs exhibit intriguing morphology because of their molecular anisotropy, especially when a droplet of undetermined shape interacts with a different material, about which people have conducted many experiments.
A special droplet morphology of interest is the nematic
tactoid
, i.e. spindle-shaped droplets filled with NLC whose directors align with the surface, which tends to emerge during the nematic-isotropic phase transition induced by temperature
[
33
,
46
,
58
]
or around rod-shaped bacteria as protective sheaths
[
57
]
. NLC droplets dispersed in polymer (known in the industry as polymer-dispersed liquid crystal) also show special opto-electric properties that inspire advances in the display industry
[
15
,
34
,
55
,
63
,
68
]
.
The study of free-boundary NLC has also evoked insights into deformable anisotropic materials in other disciplines of science
[
37
,
42
,
48
]
.
These examples demonstrate the vast potential of free boundary problems for NLCs in confinement.

We need two essential ingredients to construct mathematical models for NLC problems with free boundaries:
a degree of freedom to describe the free boundary and a NLC order parameter, to describe the nematic directors or the long-range orientational ordering in the NLC phase.
For modelling NLCs, there are competing molecular-based and macroscopic models e.g., the Onsager (molecular) model based on orientational distribution function, the continuum Oseen-Frank (OF) model for uniaxial NLC phases and the NLC order parameter is a unit-vector field that models the single distinguished material direction, and the Landau-de Gennes (LdG) model that describes the NLC phase in terms of the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor order parameter, with five degrees of freedom that can describe uniaxial and biaxial NLC phases (NLC phase with a primary and a secondary nematic director)
[
25
,
61
]
.
With regard to modelling free boundaries, we quote existing methods from the field of
shape and topology optimization

[
14
]
.
The popular methods include the surface mesh method
[
5
]
that discretizes the free boundary with a finite-element mesh known as the interface segregating different phases, and the diffuse-interface method
[
62
]
that employs a smooth field (space-dependent function), known as the
phase field
, to describe the phase separation and interfacial regions.
In recent decades, there have been multiple diverse approaches to modelling free boundary-value problems for NLC systems
[
2
,
13
,
19
,
21
,
24
,
32
,
33
,
38
,
39
,
43
,
65
,
67
]
.
The common practice is to design energy functionals with respect to the shape and to the NLC order parameter, and find the stable configuration with energy minimization methods.
For instance, in
[
2
]
the authors describe a deformable two-dimensional (2D) NLC confinement with a finite-element mesh, and their energy functional consists of the LdG free energy and a penalty function for Dirichlet boundary values; they numerically find that for sufficiently strong penalty factors, the optimal shape converges to a tactoid, consistent with experimental findings. The authors of
[
13
]
study the same problem with the finite element method as well, but they choose the OF free energy instead and further penalize the energy functional with the perimeter of the free boundary; they numerically find tactoids as the optimal shape when the penalty factors are large enough. In
[
67
]
, the authors use a phase field,
\phi
, to denote separation of a NLC and a Newtonian fluid with,
\phi=1
for NLC and
\phi=-1
for fluid respectively, and their energy density consists of a boundary anchoring term, a mixing energy density of Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard form
[
6
]
to penalize the sharp phase separation (
\phi\approx\pm 1
) and smoothness of
\phi
, and finally the weighted OF free energy density masked by
\frac{\phi+1}{2}
so that it is only integrated over the NLC phase; the authors numerically minimize the energy and find multiple stable states, including one radially symmetric state and one slightly elliptical bipolar state reminiscent of the tactoid. In
[
21
]
, the authors apply the OF model to study the 2D NLC droplet in the sharp-interface case, and theoretically predict the spindle-shaped tactoid. In general, these shape/topology optimization methods have been hugely successful for NLC free boundary-value problems and there is immense potential for further theoretical and numerical exploration.

We propose a diffuse-interface model for NLC free boundary-value problems in 3D, for nematic regions submerged in an isotropic liquid, using the LdG framework to describe the NLC phase and a phase field to describe the deformable shapes respectively. Our approach is mainly inspired by
[
10
,
20
,
67
]
. The model is relatively new since we may be the first people to combine LdG theory with diffuse-interface models for NLC free boundary-value problems.
We choose the LdG model because it is the most comprehensive continuum model for NLCs to date, and is yet more computationally tractable (with fewer degrees of freedom) than the molecular-level Onsager model.
The LdG
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor order parameter contains information about the macroscopic quantities of interest — the nematic director(s) and the degree of orientational ordering about the director(s).
The LdG model is a variational model with a LdG free energy, defined in terms of the LdG
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor, and the LdG energy minimizers model the physically observable or experimentally relevant NLC configurations.
The LdG theory can describe uniaxial and biaxial NLC phases, spatially varying degrees of orientational order,
defects
of different dimensionalities (point, line or surface discontinuities of the director field
[
35
,
60
]
) including non-orientable defects of fractional degrees (
+1/2
and
-1/2
defects in 2 dimensions, arising from the intrinsic head-to-tail symmetry of the NLC phase), while the OF theory is limited to uniaxial NLC phases with constant degrees of orientational ordering and cannot describe non-orientable point defects. Therefore, the LdG theory has been exceptionally successful for describing structural transitions in confined NLC systems.
The LdG model has been widely studied in the mathematical literature, and there are multiple theoretical results on the qualitative properties of LdG energy minimizers and LdG defect structures
[
8
,
29
,
41
]
. LdG solution landscapes have also been extensively studied on fixed domains in two and three-dimensions and such studies shed powerful insight into the correlations between shape, geometry, boundary effects and stable equilibria, see e.g.
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
51
,
52
,
66
]
. Therefore, we believe that the LdG model has huge potential for NLC free boundary-value problems.
We choose the diffuse-interface model to describe the deformable shape because the phase field can be defined on a fixed grid
[
53
,
62
]
while a surface mesh requires a full finite-element mesh
[
5
]
. The degrees of freedom are therefore considerably lower for the phase field, since it only involves a scalar function, but the finite-element mesh requires the spatial coordinates of all nodes and the adjacency relations between them. Moreover, the boundary of the deformable shape is identified by variations of the phase field, so topological changes can be implemented, e.g. one region splitting into two or two regions merging into one
[
9
,
37
,
67
]
. On the contrary, the surface mesh cannot describe topological changes because the node adjacency relations are fixed.

Our work is presented as follows. In Section
2
, we define a diffuse-interface energy functional
E_{\varepsilon}
composed of the classic LdG free energy in three dimensions, a mixing energy in terms of the phase field
\phi
, an anchoring energy to account for anchoring conditions on the nematic-isotropic interface and a void energy, along with a volume constraint on the phase field
\phi
. The small parameter
\varepsilon>0
models the thickness of the diffuse interface.
In Section
3
, we establish the solvability of this model, i.e. existence of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
, with classical methods of calculus of variation
[
16
]
.
In Section
4
, we discuss the problem of minimizing
E_{\varepsilon}
with a fixed phase field. We obtain uniform bounds for the energy minimizers, independent of
\varepsilon
and address technical challenges stemming from the penalty terms, and also demonstrate uniqueness of energy minimizers (with fixed
\phi
) for sufficiently small domains. The uniqueness results follow from convexity arguments in
[
36
]
. Our discoveries agree with well-known results for the classical LdG energy on a fixed region.
In Section
5
, we present our main contribution — the limit of
E_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0,
known as the
sharp-interface limit
, with
\Gamma
-convergence
[
12
]
as the primary tool.
We prove that under appropriate assumptions, the convergence of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
to the minimizer of a sharp-interface functional,
E_{0}
, defined over deformable regions, that also models the separation of nematic and isotropic phases. The technical challenges originate from the limits of the anchoring energy, and that the mixing and the anchoring energy are absorbed as a single boundary integral in the sharp-interface energy.
In Section
6
, we conduct numerical experiments on the minimization of a reduced version of
E_{\varepsilon}
on a two-dimensional domain. We adjust hyperparameters to observe structural changes in the energy minimizer as a function of the penalty parameters, and compare them with existing results on the literature, particularly shape transitions from two-dimensional NLC-filled discs to NLC tactoids as a function of the hyperparameters. The numerical work illustrates the computational efficiency of our model and leads to several generalizations. We conclude with some perspectives in Section
7
.

2
Diffuse-interface Landau-de Gennes model

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a three-dimensional (3D) region with Lipschitz boundary, where a blob of nematic liquid crystals is surrounded by an isotropic liquid. The nematic and isotropic phases are separated by the nematic-isotropic (N-I) interface.
We propose a
diffuse-interface LdG energy

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}=E^{\rm LdG}+\omega_{p}E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}+\omega_{a}E^{\rm anch}_{\varepsilon}+\omega_{v}E^{\rm void},

(1)

where
\omega_{p},\omega_{v},\omega_{a}
are positive weights of the competing energy terms, and
\varepsilon>0
is a positive parameter in the second and third terms respectively.
The physical dimensions of
\omega_{p},\omega_{v},\omega_{a}
, which will be given later, ensure that all terms on the RHS of (
1
) have the dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m}
, i.e. the dimension of energy.
The diffuse-interface LdG energy has four contributions: (a) LdG free energy of the nematic phase, (b) mixing energy or interfacial energy associated with the N-I interface, (c) the anchoring energy on the N-I interface that promotes tangential anchoring of the nematic molecules on the N-I interface and (d) void energy (penalty) of the isotropic phase.

(a) LdG energy

We work in the LdG framework and describe the nematic phase by the tensor variable
\mathbf{Q}=((Q^{ij}))_{3\times 3}
, which is a
3\times 3
symmetric traceless matrix. The eigenvectors of the LdG
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor model the nematic directors and the corresponding eigenvalues contain information about the degree of orientational ordering about the directors
[
11
]
. Nematics are broadly classified as follows: the isotropic phase is modelled by
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}
so that there is no orientational ordering;
\mathbf{Q}
is
uniaxial
if
\mathbf{Q}
has two degenerate non-zero eigenvalues and there is a single distinguished nematic director corresponding to the eigenvector with the non-degenerate eigenvalue;
\mathbf{Q}
models a
biaxial
phase if there are three distinct eigenvalues, and hence, a primary and secondary nematic director.
A uniaxial
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor can be written as

\mathbf{Q}=s\quantity(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}-\frac 13\vb I),

with
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is the nematic director or the eigenvector corresponding to the non-degenerate eigenvalue.
The term
E^{\rm LdG}
in (
1
) is the classical LdG free energy
[
11
]

E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]=\int_{\Omega}(F_{el}+F_{b})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(2)

with the one-constant elastic energy density

F_{el}=\frac{L}{2}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}|^{2},

(3)

where
L>0
is the material-dependent elastic constant,
|\gradient\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\triangleq\sum_{i,j,k}|Q^{ij}_{x_{k}}|^{2}
. The bulk energy density is given by

F_{b}(\mathbf{Q})=\frac{A}{2}\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2}-\frac{B}{3}\tr\mathbf{Q}^{3}+\frac{C}{4}\quantity(\tr\vb Q^2 )^{2},

(4)

where
A<0
is a re-scaled temperature and
B,C>0
are material-dependent constants. We work with
A<0
so that the minimizers of
F_{b}
are a continuum of uniaxial
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors
\mathbf{Q}^{*}=s_{+}\quantity(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}-\frac 13\vb{I})
, with arbitrary unit vector
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and

s_{+}=\frac{B+\sqrt{B^{2}-24AC}}{4C}.

(5)

The dimensions of
A,B,C
are
\mathrm{N\cdot m^{-2}}
, and that of
L
is
\mathrm{N}
, so that
E^{\rm LdG}
has the dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m}
.
The one-constant elastic energy density (
3
) is analytically attractive and physically relevant for a large class of liquid crystal materials, but more general forms exist
[
25
,
45
]
.

(b) Mixing energy

The term
E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}
in (
1
) is the mixing energy. It is the well-known
Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard energy functional

[
6
,
59
]
for the binary mixture of two phases, in this case the nematic and isotropic phases respectively.

E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}[\phi]=\int_{\Omega}\left[\varepsilon|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(6)

where
\phi
is a phase field.

\varepsilon
has the length dimension
\mathrm{m}
, so the term
E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}
has dimension
\mathrm{m^{2}}
. We give the weight
\omega_{p}
the dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m^{-1}}
, so that the term
\omega_{p}E^{\rm mix}
in (
1
) has the energy dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m}
.
Informally speaking, the continuous double-well function
W(\phi)=\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}
drives the separation of
\Omega
into subdomains: the nematic subdomain with
\phi\approx 1
, the isotropic subdomain with
\phi\approx 0
, while the gradient term
|\nabla\phi|^{2}
smoothens out
\phi
to form a
diffuse interface
whose thickness is represented by
\varepsilon>0
, the
capillary width
(see Figure
1
).
In the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit, the Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard energy approximates the perimeter of the interface in the sense of
\Gamma
-convergence (the sharp interface limit)
[
44
]
, so that it models the interfacial tension as is common in numerous physical models
[
31
,
40
]
.

\phi=1
\phi=0
O(\varepsilon)
\partial D

) to isotropic (

) via diffuse N-I interface with thickness

surrounding the surface

(c) Anchoring energy

The term
E^{\rm anch}
in (
1
) enforces tangential anchoring
[
11
,
60
]
on the N-I interface and is defined by

E^{\rm anch}_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]=\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon\left|\left(\mathbf{Q}(x)+\frac{s_{+}}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\nabla\phi\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(7)

with
s_{+}
defined inf (
5
).

E^{\rm anch}_{\varepsilon}
also has dimension
\mathrm{m^{2}}
, and the weight
\omega_{a}
has dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m^{-1}}
, so that the term
\omega_{a}E^{\rm anch}
appearing in (
1
) has the energy dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m}
.

E^{\rm anch}_{\varepsilon}
is minimized when
\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi=-\frac{s_{+}}{3}\nabla\phi
, i.e. when
\nabla\phi
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
with negative eigenvalue

. The vector
\nabla\phi
, approximates the normal vector to the diffuse interface and this constraint requires that the leading nematic director (eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}
with the largest positive eigenvalue) is orthogonal to
\nabla\phi
or tangent to the N-I interface.
To keep this energy bounded in the (
\varepsilon\to 0
) sharp-interface limit, the factor
\varepsilon
is needed in (

). One can verify this fact by checking from Figure

|\nabla\phi|=O(\varepsilon^{-1})
and that the size of its support is

, making the integral in (
7
) approximately
\varepsilon O(\varepsilon^{-2})\cdot O(\varepsilon)=O(1)
as required, in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit.
This weak anchoring energy has been applied elsewhere e.g.,
[
30
]
and
[

]
.

(d) Void energy

The term
E^{\rm void}
in (
1
) penalizes the isotropic phase (
\phi\approx 0
) surrounding the nematic phase and is given by

E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(1-\phi)^{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(8)

where
|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}=\sum_{i,j}|Q^{ij}|^{2}
is the Frobenius norm.

E^{\rm void}
has dimension
\mathrm{m^{3}}
, and the weight
\omega_{v}
has dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m^{-2}}
, so that the term
\omega_{v}E^{\rm void}
in (
1
) has the energy dimension
\mathrm{N\cdot m}
.
It is inspired by similar penalty terms in the studies of phase separation in fluids
[

]
.

We impose the volume constraint

\int_{\Omega}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=V_{0},

to conserve the mass of NLCs with
0<V_{0}<|\Omega|
(
|\Omega|
is the volume of
\Omega
),
and impose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

\mathbf{Q}|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathbf{0},\quad\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

to model the isotropic phase surrounding the nematic phase.

The energy (
1
) can be nondimensionalized. Let
\bar{x}=\lambda^{-1}x
, for all

is a characteristic length scale associated with

. After substituting
\bar{x}
into the integrals and dividing the entire energy by

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]

\displaystyle=\bar{E}^{\rm LdG}+\bar{\omega}_{p}\bar{\lambda}\bar{E}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}^{\rm mix}+\bar{\omega}_{a}\bar{\lambda}\bar{E}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}^{\rm anch}+\bar{\omega}_{v}\bar{\lambda}^{2}\bar{E}^{\rm void}

(9)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{(0)}}\quantity[ \frac{1}{2} |\grad\vb{Q}|^2 + \bar\lambda^2 \bar F_b(\vb Q) ]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{x}+\bar{\omega}_{p}\bar{\lambda}\int_{\Omega^{(0)}}\quantity[ \bar{\ve} |\nabla\phi|^2+ \frac{1}{\bar\ve} \phi^2(1-\phi)^2]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{x}

\displaystyle+\bar{\omega}_{a}\bar{\lambda}\int_{\Omega^{(0)}}\bar{\varepsilon}\left|\quantity(\vb{Q}+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\nabla\phi\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{x}+\bar{\omega}_{v}\bar{\lambda}^{2}\int_{\Omega^{(0)}}\frac{1}{2}(1-\phi)^{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{x}

with the re-scaled domain
\Omega^{(0)}
, re-scaled nondimensional constants

and re-scaled LdG bulk energy
\bar{F}_{b}\triangleq\frac{1}{C}F_{b}
, subject to the re-scaled volume constraint

\int_{\Omega^{(0)}}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{x}=\bar{V}_{0},

(10)

with
\bar{V}_{0}=\lambda^{-3}V_{0}
satisfying
0<\bar{V}_{0}<|\Omega^{(0)}|
.

In all subsequent discussion, we focus on the nondimensional functional
\bar{E}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}
(
9
), which is almost identical to (
1
), except for the additional

in the leading coefficients and the bars above.
For notational simplicity, we rewrite
\Omega^{(0)}
as
\Omega

\bar{F}_{b}
is kept in order to differ from the classical bulk energy

(

The four terms of (

(

\mathscr{A}=\quantity{ (\vb Q,\phi):

3
Existence of minimizers

We use the direct method in the calculus of variations
[
16
]
to prove the existence of a minimizer for the diffuse-interface LdG functional.

Lemma 1
.

The functional
E_{\varepsilon}
(
9
) is weakly lower semi-continuous (w.l.s.c.) on the Banach space
(\mathbf{Q},\phi)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, which means that if
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{Q},\phi_{k}\rightharpoonup\phi
are weakly convergent sequences in
H_{0}^{1}
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q}_{k},\phi_{k}]\geq E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q},\phi].

Proof.

As
[
16
, §8.2,Theorem 1]
,
for an integral energy functional of the form

\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla\bm{u},\bm{u},x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where
\bm{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
F:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
if
F
is uniformly bounded below and convex with respect to its first argument, then
the functional is w.l.s.c. with respect to
\bm{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
We look at the individual terms.

Examine the form (
9
) with
\bm{u}=(\mathbf{Q},\phi):\Omega\to\mathcal{S}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}
. The energy density is bounded from below because all terms are positive except for the LdG bulk energy
\bar{F}_{b}=C^{-1}F_{b}
(
4
), which satisfies

\bar{F}_{b}(\mathbf{Q})\geq m_{b}>-\infty,\quad\forall\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}

(17)

for some number
m_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
that can be explicitly computed
[
45
, eq. (41)]
.
Moreover, the terms involving the gradients, which read

\frac{1}{2}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\omega_{p}\lambda\varepsilon|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\omega_{a}\lambda\varepsilon\left|\quantity(\vb{Q}+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\nabla\phi\right|^{2},

are convex with respect to
(\gradient\mathbf{Q},\nabla\phi)
since they are positive-definite quadratic polynomials. Therefore, the energy density of
E_{\varepsilon}
(
9
) satisfies the criteria above, and is w.l.s.c. over the space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
∎

Proposition 2
.

The functional,
E_{\varepsilon}
in (
9
), has at least one global minimizer
(\mathbf{Q}^{*}_{\varepsilon},\phi^{*}_{\varepsilon})
in the admissible set
\mathscr{A}
specified in (
11
).

Proof.

The admissible set
\mathscr{A}
(
11
) is nonempty since there always exists a smooth
\phi\in H_{0}^{1}
such that
\int_{\Omega}\phi=V_{0}
.

Moreover, all the terms in
E_{\varepsilon}
are bounded from below (recall (
17
)), yielding the following coercivity condition:

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+\omega_{p}\varepsilon|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\lambda^{2}m_{b}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2}\|\gradient\mathbf{Q}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\omega_{p}\varepsilon\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lambda^{2}m_{b}|\Omega|.

The weak lower semi-continuity of
E_{\varepsilon}
over the feasible set is guaranteed by Lemma
1
since the admissible set
\mathscr{A}
is a closed subspace of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, the existence of a global minimizer follows from the direct method in the calculus of variations.
∎

Corollary 3
.

For each fixed
\phi\in H_{0}^{1}
, the w.l.s.c. and coercivity argument still applies with respect to the variable
\mathbf{Q}
. Hence, there exists a
\mathbf{Q}
-minimizer,
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})
such that

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(\phi)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathbf{Q}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})}E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q},\phi].

4
Maximum principle on a fixed domain

In this section, we prove that for a fixed
\phi
, the critical points of
E_{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\mathbf{Q}
(referred to as
\mathbf{Q}
-critical points above) are bounded in
L^{\infty}
uniformly, as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The maximum principle and uniqueness results have been studied thoroughly in the LdG framework; see
[
36
,
41
]
, but results for the diffuse-interface model (
1
) involve different techniques, especially when dealing with the anchoring penalty (
7
).

4.1
Uniform maximum principle

Before introducing the uniform maximum principle, we improve the regularity of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
with the
L^{p}
theory of second-order elliptic equations
[
22
]
. Let

W^{m,p}(\Omega)=\quantity{u\in L^p(\Omega): \|u\|_{W^{m,p}}^p = \sum_{k=0}^m \int_\Omega|\nabla^k u|^p <\infty}

be the general Sobolev spaces.

Lemma 4
.

If
\phi\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
, then a
\mathbf{Q}
-critical point,
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})
of
E_{\varepsilon}[\cdot,\phi]
(
9
), is in
W^{2,p}
for all
p<\infty
and in
C^{1,\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<1
.
Since
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
is a weak solution, continuity means that
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
is equal to a
C^{1,\alpha}
function a.e..

Proof.

By definition, the critical point
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
is a solution of the equation (
14
).
Examining the right-hand side, we find that it is a cubic polynomial of
\mathbf{Q}
(because of the bulk energy derivative), and its coefficients, excluding constants, are determined by
\phi,\nabla\phi
, which in turn are uniformly bounded by assumption.

If
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in L^{p}(\Omega),
then the RHS (right-hand side) of (
14
) is in
L^{p/3}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, by the
L^{p}
theory of elliptic equations
[
22
]
,
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{2,\frac{p}{3}}
if
p>3
.
This enables us to improve the regularity of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
through a chain of Sobolev embeddings.

In our case, we have
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in H_{0}^{1},
which implies that
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in L^{6}
from the Sobolev embedding theorem in 3D.
Using the arguments above iteratively, we obtain
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{2,p}
for all
p<\infty
, and with one extra step of embedding, this yields
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1,\alpha}
for all
\alpha<1
.
∎

Under stronger smoothness assumptions, we prove that
\mathbf{Q}
-critical points of the nondimensional energy (
9
) are
uniformly
bounded. The bound is uniform in the sense that it is independent of the capillary width
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\phi_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
be a phase field, with the following extra assumptions.

(i)

\phi_{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]
for all
x
.

(ii)

We assume that there exists a
C^{2}
vector field
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
defined on
\Omega
satisfying

\begin{cases}|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}|\leq 1,\\
\|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{2}(\Omega)}\leq M_{N}<\infty\end{cases}

for some uniform constant
M_{N}
, such that within the diffuse interface

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\overline{\{x\in\Omega:0<\phi(x)<1\}}

it holds that

\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}=|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}.

(19)

Suppose
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
is a
\mathbf{Q}
-critical point of
E_{\varepsilon}[\cdot,\phi_{\varepsilon}]
(
9
).
Then for any fixed
\lambda_{0}>\lambda
,
there exists a uniform constant
M
determined by
A,B,C,\omega_{v},\lambda_{0}

and the bound
M_{N}
, but independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}|\leq M\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}.

The bound is given in the a.e. sense.

Proof.

In the following proof, we drop the subscript
\varepsilon
for notational simplicity.

Take the dot product of
\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
with the Euler-Lagrange equation (
14
), where
\alpha
is a number yet to be determined. Recall the operator
\mathscr{P}_{0}
as defined in (
16
).

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\triangle\mathbf{Q}

(20)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\underbrace{(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\quantity[ \qty(\frac{A}{C}+\tr\vb Q^2) \vb Q - \frac{B}{C} \mathscr{P}_0(\vb Q^2)]}_{(I)}+\omega_{v}\lambda^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}\underbrace{(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\mathbf{Q}}_{(II)}

\displaystyle+\omega_{a}\lambda\varepsilon\underbrace{(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\mathscr{P}_{0}\quantity[(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)\qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I) + \qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)]}_{(III)}.

For the LHS (left-hand side) of (
20
), we compute

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\triangle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\triangle(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})-(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\triangle(\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\triangle(|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2})-|\bm{\nabla}(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})|^{2}-\alpha(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\triangle(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}).

We have used the elementary equality
\mathbf{A}:\triangle\mathbf{A}=\frac{1}{2}\triangle(|\mathbf{A}|^{2})-|\gradient\mathbf{A}|^{2}
(
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
). It is well-defined in the weak sense because
\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\in C^{0}\cap H^{1}
by the regularity estimate from Lemma
4
.

For the RHS of (
20
), we compute the three terms separately. The first term is

\displaystyle(I)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\quantity[\qty(\frac{A}{C}+\tr\vb Q^2 ) \vb Q -\frac{B}{C} \qty(\vb Q^2-\frac{\tr\vb Q^2}{3}\vb I)]

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{A}{C}+\tr\vb Q^2)(\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{Qn})-\frac{B}{C}\quantity(\tr\vb Q^3+\alpha|\vb{Qn}|^2-\frac{\alpha\tr\vb Q^2}{3}).

The second term equals

(II)=\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2}+\alpha\tr(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}\vb Q)=\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{Qn}.

We have extensively used the equality
\tr\mathbf{AB}=\tr\mathbf{BA}
.

Suppose first that
x\in\Gamma
, so by the assumption (
19
), we have
\nabla\phi(x)=|\nabla\phi(x)|\mathbf{n}(x)
. Then we write

\displaystyle(III)

\displaystyle=|\nabla\phi|^{2}(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\mathscr{P}_{0}\quantity[(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}) \qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I) + \qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n})]

\displaystyle=2|\nabla\phi|^{2}(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\mathscr{P}_{0}\quantity[(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}) \qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)]

using
\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{|\nabla\phi|^{2}}\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi
and that
\mathbf{Q}
is symmetric.
The image of
\mathscr{P}_{0}
has zero trace and hence, the product is unchanged if we add
\frac{s_{+}}{3}\mathbf{I}
to
\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
.
Let
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathbf{Q}+\frac{s_{+}}{3}\mathbf{I}
(so
\tr\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}=s_{+}
), and we get

\displaystyle(III)

\displaystyle=2|\nabla\phi|^{2}(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\mathscr{P}_{0}((\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})

\displaystyle=2|\nabla\phi|^{2}(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\quantity[(\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n})\tilde{\vb Q}-\frac{\tr((\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n})\tilde{\vb Q})}{3} \vb I]

\displaystyle=2|\nabla\phi|^{2}\quantity[ |\tilde{\vb Q}\vb n|^2 +\frac{2\alpha-s_+}{3} (\vb n\cdot\tilde{\vb Q}\vb n)].

Setting
\alpha=\frac{s_{+}}{2}
eliminates the term
\frac{2\alpha-s_{+}}{3}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{n})
, which leads us to

(III)=2\left|\quantity(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\nabla\phi\right|^{2},\quad x\in\Gamma.

If
x\notin\Gamma
, then
\phi
is a constant in a neighbourhood of
x
, so
\nabla\phi(x)\equiv 0
and the third term equals zero. Therefore, we have

(III)\geq 0.

Then, substituting the above in (
20
), and removing all positive terms on the RHS, we get the following inequality satisfied by the function
|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2}
on the entire
\Omega
. This is known as a
subsolution condition
.

-\triangle\quantity(|\vb Q+\alpha\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}|^2)+2T(\mathbf{Q})\leq 0.

(21)

The function
T(\mathbf{Q})
is defined to be

\displaystyle T(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=T^{(1)}(\mathbf{Q})+\lambda^{2}T^{(2)}(\mathbf{Q}),

(22)

\displaystyle T^{(1)}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=\alpha(\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}):\triangle(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=(\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{2}-\frac{B}{C}\tr\mathbf{Q}^{3}+\alpha(\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2})(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{Qn})+\frac{3A+B\alpha}{3C}\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2}-\frac{B}{C}\alpha|\mathbf{Qn}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha\quantity(\frac{A}{C}+\omega_v(1-\phi)^2)\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{Qn}.

Despite the complexity of its expression,
T(\mathbf{Q})
is basically a fourth order polynomial in terms of
\mathbf{Q}
, with uniformly bounded coefficients and a strictly positive leading term,
\lambda^{2}(\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{2}
.
Therefore, there exists a number
R_{0}
depending on
A,B,C,\lambda,\omega_{v}
and the
C^{2}
-norm,
M_{N}
, from assumption (ii), such that

T(\mathbf{Q})\geq 0,\quad\forall|\mathbf{Q}|\geq R_{0}.

(23)

Note that
\alpha=\frac{s_{+}}{2}
is also determined by
A,B,C
according to (
5
).

Finally, we use some standard techniques as in
[
36
, App. 2]
for an example.
Define the cut-off

\psi_{k}=\max\quantity{ |\vb Q+\alpha\vb{n}\otimes\vb{n}|^2-k, 0},

such that
\psi_{k}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
for
k>\alpha^{2}
because
\mathbf{Q}|_{\partial\Omega}\equiv 0
and that
|\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2}\leq\alpha^{2}
.
By the chain rule, we also have

\nabla\psi_{k}=\begin{cases}\nabla(|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2}),&|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2}>k,\\
0,&|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2}\leq k,\end{cases}

(24)

Multiplying both sides of (
21
) with
\psi_{k}
, integrating by parts and eliminating positive terms, we get an inequality

\int_{\Omega}\nabla(|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2})\cdot\nabla\psi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}\psi_{k}T(\mathbf{Q})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0,\quad k>\alpha^{2}.

(25)

By (
24
), the first term of the LHS of (
25
) is simply
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{k}|^{2}.

The second term of (
25
) is supported on the region where
\psi_{k}\neq 0
, i.e.
|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|^{2}>k
.
By the triangle inequality of the Frobenius norm,

|\mathbf{Q}|\geq|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|-|\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|=|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|-\alpha.

We choose
k>(R_{0}+\alpha)^{2}
to ensure
|\mathbf{Q}|>R_{0}
whenever
\psi_{k}
is nonzero. Thus, according to (
23
),
T(\mathbf{Q})
is positive on the support of
\psi_{k}
, and

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{k}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0\Rightarrow\psi_{k}\equiv 0,

i.e.,
|\mathbf{Q}+\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}|
is uniformly bounded by
k
for all
k>(R_{0}+\alpha)^{2}
. Therefore, using the triangle inequality on
\mathbf{Q}
and
\alpha\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
again, we get

\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\mathbf{Q}|\leq R_{0}+2\alpha,

(26)

which is the uniform bound we desired.

Evidently, the bound (
26
) depends on the choice of
R_{0}
, which ensures the positivity of
T(\mathbf{Q})
for all
|\mathbf{Q}|>R_{0}
. Hence, to complete the proof, we also need to study the dependence of
R_{0}
(
23
) on
\lambda
.
Closely observing the expressions of
T^{(1)}
and
T^{(2)}
(
22
) and noting that their coefficients are controlled by a uniform bound dependent on
A,B,C,\omega_{v},M_{N}
but not on
\lambda
, we get

\displaystyle T^{(1)}(\mathbf{Q})\geq-C_{1}(|\mathbf{Q}|+1),

\displaystyle T^{(2)}(\mathbf{Q})\geq\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{4}-C_{2},

with constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending on
A,B,C,\omega_{v},M_{N}
.
T^{(1)}
is affine in
\mathbf{Q}
and
T^{(2)}
is degree-4 in
\mathbf{Q}
with leading term
(\tr\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{2}
.
In order that
T(\mathbf{Q})\geq 0
, it suffices to force the lower bound to be positive, i.e.

T^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}T^{(2)}\geq\lambda^{2}\quantity(\frac 12 |\vb Q|^4 - C_2)-C_{1}(|\mathbf{Q}|+1)\geq 0.

Multiply both sides by
\lambda^{\frac{2}{3}}
, and this reduces to checking that

\frac{1}{2}|\lambda^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{Q}|^{4}-C_{1}|\lambda^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{Q}|\geq C_{1}\lambda^{\frac{2}{3}}+C_{2}\lambda^{\frac{8}{3}}.

The RHS can be bounded in terms of
C_{1},C_{2},\lambda_{0}
for all
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
.
The LHS is a degree-4 polynomial of
|\lambda^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{Q}|
as a whole. By the elementary property of polynomial functions, there exists
C_{3}>0
depending on
C_{1},C_{2},\lambda_{0}
,
such that the inequality holds whenever
|\lambda^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{Q}|>C_{3}
i.e.
|\mathbf{Q}|>C_{3}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}
.
Therefore, we take
R_{0}=C_{3}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}
in the uniform bound (
26
) and get

\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\mathbf{Q}|\leq C_{3}\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}+2\alpha\leq M\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}},\quad\forall\lambda<\lambda_{0}

with
M=C_{3}+2\alpha\lambda_{0}^{\frac{2}{3}}
dependent on
A,B,C,L,\omega_{v},\lambda_{0},M_{N}
as desired.
∎

Remark 1
.

We construct a typical example of
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
.
Take
h(x)
, the signed distance function
[
44
]
to a smooth region
D
representing the droplet, such that the level set
\{h=0\}
equals
\partial D
, that
|\nabla h|=1
a.e., and that
h(x)
is smooth in a neighbourhood of
\partial D
as long as
\partial D
is smooth
[
22
]
.
We let
\phi_{\varepsilon}
be an approximation to the
\tanh
function, also known as the standing wave
[
18
]
:

\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&h<-2\sqrt{\varepsilon},\\
\text{linear connection},&-2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq h\leq-\sqrt{\varepsilon},\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\frac{h(x)}{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon}\right),&|h|<\sqrt{\varepsilon},\\
\text{linear connection},&\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq h\leq 2\sqrt{\varepsilon},\\
1,&h>2\sqrt{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

Such a sequence is known to approximate minimizers of the Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard energy (our mixing energy).
We can take
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}=-\zeta(x)\nabla h,
where
\zeta(x)\geq 0
is a smooth cutoff function that equals 1 near
\partial D
and equals zero outside of a fixed neighbourhood of
\partial D
, within which
h
is smooth. Since the definition of
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
is not related to
\varepsilon
, its
C^{2}
norm is obviously uniform with respect to
\varepsilon
.
By definition,
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}
is supported on the region
\{|h|\leq 2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\}
, an area of size
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
near
\partial D
, so the support of
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}
is contained inside the region where
\zeta\equiv 1
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Since
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is a decreasing function of
h
,
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}
is parallel to
-\nabla h
.
Therefore, when
\varepsilon\to 0
, the desired relation
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}=\frac{|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}|}{|\nabla h|}\cdot(-\nabla h)=|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}
holds within the support of
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}
.

4.2
Uniqueness on small domains

An immediate consequence of the maximum principle is the uniqueness of the critical points when
\lambda
is sufficiently small.

Proposition 6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5
, there exists a
\lambda_{1}>0
depending on
\Omega,A,B,C,L,\omega_{v}
and the bound
M_{N}
,
such that for all
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
, the
\mathbf{Q}
-critical point of
E_{\varepsilon}[\cdot,\phi]
(
9
) is unique.

Proof.

Following the approach of Lemma 8.2 from
[
36
, App. 2]
, we only need to prove the strict convexity of the energy functional
E_{\varepsilon}
on the closed convex set

X_{\lambda}=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0}):\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\mathbf{Q}|\leq M\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}\right\},

where
M=M(A,B,C,L,\omega_{v},\lambda_{0},M_{N})
is the constant stated in Theorem
5
, with
\lambda_{0}=1
.
By definition,
X_{\lambda}
contains all critical points of the energy. We highlight the dependence on
\lambda
since the bound changes as
\lambda\to 0
.
It suffices to check that

\frac{E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{h},\phi]+E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{h},\phi]}{2}\geq E_{\varepsilon}\quantity[\vb Q,\phi]

for any
\mathbf{Q}\in X_{\lambda}
and
\mathbf{h}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})
, and that the strict inequality holds when
\mathbf{h}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
Since the void energy and the anchoring energy are positive-definite quadratic polynomials with respect to
\mathbf{Q}
, they are both convex, and one needs to check the LdG energy alone:

E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]=\int_{\Omega}\quantity[\frac 12 |\nabla\vb Q|^2 + \lambda^2\bar F_b(\vb Q)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The mixing energy is fixed by the fixed choice of
\phi
, for
\mathbf{Q}
-critical points of
E_{\varepsilon}[\cdot,\phi]
.

Then, strong convexity of
E^{\rm LdG}
is equivalent to the positivity of the second-order central difference

\Delta(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{h})=E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{h}]+E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{h}]-2E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]

for all
\mathbf{Q}\in X_{\lambda}
and
\mathbf{h}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{S}_{0})\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. From the expression of
E^{\rm LdG}
, we compute

\Delta(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{h})=\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{h}|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\int_{\Omega}\quantity[\frac{A}{C} \tr\vb h^2
-\frac{2B}{C}\tr(\vb{Qh}^2) + 2(\tr\vb{Qh})^2+\tr\vb Q^2 \tr\vb h^2+\frac 12(\tr\vb h^2)^2].

Since
|\mathbf{Q}|\leq M\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}
, we estimate a lower bound of
\Delta(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{h})
by removing all positive terms and amplifying
\mathbf{Q}
to its largest norm. We get

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{h}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\lambda^{2}\int_{\Omega}\quantity[\frac{|A|}{C} \tr\vb h^2 + \frac{2B}{C}\cdot M\lambda^{-\frac 23} \tr\vb h^2]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{h}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-M^{\prime}\lambda^{\frac{4}{3}}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{h}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

where the constant
M^{\prime}
is scaled from
M
by a constant related to
\frac{|A|}{C},\frac{B}{C}
.
We note that the factor
\lambda^{2}
absorbs the unbounded
\lambda^{-\frac{2}{3}}
(as
\lambda\to 0
) from the uniform maximum value introduced in Theorem
5
.

Poincaré’s inequality
[
22
]
implies that

\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{h}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{1}{C_{4}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{h}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

so there exists a
\lambda_{1}=\min\{\lambda_{0},(C_{4}M^{\prime})^{-\frac{3}{4}}\}
such that when
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
, the quantity
\Delta(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{h})
is always strictly positive for all
\mathbf{h}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
Therefore,
\bar{E}^{LdG}
is strictly convex on
X_{\lambda}
, and the uniqueness of
\mathbf{Q}
-critical points follows naturally.
∎

5
Sharp-interface limit

The limiting behaviour of
E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]
(
9
) in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit, is known as the
sharp-interface limit
.
A famous result of the sharp-interface limit concerns the Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard functional (as used in our mixing energy (
6
)), which states that it converges to the interface perimeter, as a
\Gamma
-limit in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit
[
44
,
56
]
.
We are interested in the sharp-interface limit because it guarantees that solving the continuous diffuse-interface problem (e.g. the Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard functional) approximates the difficult-to-compute sharp-interface problems (e.g. interface perimeter).

Our main result is:

Theorem 7
.

Let
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{*},\phi_{\varepsilon}^{*})\in\mathscr{A}
be a minimizer of
E_{\varepsilon}
(
9
) in its admissible space (
11
). We make the following assumptions as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

(i)

We assume that
\{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{*}\}
is a sequence of uniformly bounded continuous functions that
H^{1}
-weakly converge to
\mathbf{Q}_{0}\in C^{0}\cap H_{0}^{1}
.

(ii)

We assume that
\{\phi_{\varepsilon}^{*}\}
converges to the indicator function
I_{D_{0}}=\begin{cases}1,&x\in D_{0},\\
0&x\notin D_{0}\end{cases}
in
L^{1}
, where
D_{0}\subset\subset\Omega
(abbreviation for
D_{0}\subset\Omega
and
\partial D_{0}\subset\Omega
) is a smooth region such that
|D_{0}|=V_{0}
;

Then,
(\mathbf{Q}_{0},D_{0})
minimizes the energy functional

\displaystyle E_{0}[\mathbf{Q},D]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\quantity[ \frac{L}{2} |\grad\vb{Q}|^2 + \lambda^2 \bar F_b(\vb Q) ]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\omega_{v}\lambda^{2}\int_{D^{c}}\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(27)

\displaystyle+\frac{\omega_{p}\lambda}{3}\int_{\partial D}\quantity[1+\frac{\omega_a}{\omega_p}\left| \qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\bm\nu\right|^2 ]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S(x)

amongst all tensor functions
\mathbf{Q}\in C^{0}\cap H_{0}^{1}
and regions
D\subset\subset\Omega
with smooth boundary satisfying
|D|=V_{0}
, where
\bm{\nu}
represents the unit normal vector of
\partial D
.

Remark 2
.

The sharp-interface limit (
27
) also comprises the LdG free energy and penalty factors on the void, the perimeter and the weak anchoring on
\partial D
.
The first integral is identical to
E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]
, and the second is identical to
E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q},I_{D}]
(
8
) where
I_{D}
is the indicator function of
D
.
Using the elementary inequality
\frac{1+|x|}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}\leq 1+|x|,
we find that

1\leq\frac{\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(D)+\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{a}}{\omega_{p}}}\int_{\partial D}\left|\quantity(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\bm{\nu}\right|}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}\quantity[1+\frac{\omega_a}{\omega_p}\left|\qty(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\bm\nu\right|^2 ]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S(x)}\leq\sqrt{2}.

Thus, the boundary integral is the sum of the perimeter and an
L^{1}
anchoring penalty.

5.1
Proof by
\Gamma
-convergence

Theorem

\Gamma
-convergence, which is composed of an upper bound estimate and a lower bound estimate
[
12
]
.

We re-write the diffuse-interface functional (

E_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]=E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]+\omega_{v}\lambda^{2}E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q},\phi]+\omega_{p}\lambda\int_{\Omega}\quantity[\ve a_{\vb Q}(\nabla\phi) + \ve^{-1}W(\phi)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where
W(s)=s^{2}(1-s)^{2}
is the double-well function,
and the sharp-interface functional (

E_{0}[\mathbf{Q},D]=E^{\rm LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]+\omega_{v}\lambda^{2}E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q},I_{D}]+\frac{\omega_{p}\lambda}{3}\int_{\partial D}|a_{\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\nu})|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S(x).

(29)

Note that the expressions (

) and (

a_{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)=|\xi|^{2}+\frac{\omega_{a}}{\omega_{p}}\left|\quantity(\vb Q+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\xi\right|^{2},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(30)

suggesting the required connection between
E_{\varepsilon}
and
E_{0}
.

We first present a compactness result.

Proposition 8
.

Work with the same assumptions as in Theorem
7
. Then, for any sequence
\{(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\phi_{\varepsilon})\}

(a)

If

is bounded for all

\{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\}

.

(b)

If

is precompact in

, and any cluster point of
\{\phi_{\varepsilon}\}
takes binary values
0
and

.

(c)

Denote by

(31)

If

and

\varepsilon
, then
\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
and

*
precompact as Radon measures on
\Omega
.

means that there exists a subsequence

and another measure

Especially, if

is uniformly bounded for all

Then, we state the
\Gamma
-convergence of
E_{\varepsilon}
to

Proposition 9
.

Work with the same assumptions as in Theorem
7
, and the following properties hold for all continuous
\mathbf{Q}\in H_{0}^{1}
and smooth regions

(a)

) and (

), while Claims (III) and (IV) are about the final term.

\mathbf{Q}

5.2
Detailed proof

We start with compactness.

Proof of Proposition
8
.

(a) Weak precompactness of
\{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\}
. We notice that the LdG elastic energy (
3
) is a multiple of the
H^{1}
norm of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, so the uniform boundedness of
E_{\varepsilon}
implies that

\|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}}\leq M

for some uniform constant
M
. Since
H_{0}^{1}
is a Hilbert space, the bounded sequence
\{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\}
is always weak precompact
[
16
]
.

(b) Precompactness of
\{\phi_{\varepsilon}\}
.
We recall that

E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}\left[\varepsilon|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}W(\phi_{\varepsilon})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

is the well-known Van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard energy
[
6
,
59
]
.
The rest of the proof follows from a standard method (see e.g.
[
56
, Prop. 3]
).

First,
W(s)=O(s^{4})
as
s\to\infty
, so the fact that
E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded implies that
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{4}
. Using an elementary inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab
to cancel out
\varepsilon
, we get

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}^{\rm mix}[\phi_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}2\sqrt{\varepsilon|\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}W(\phi_{\varepsilon})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(38)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

where the chain rule on
\Phi
(
31
) is used.
Hence,
\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})
is uniformly bounded in
\mathrm{BV}(\Omega)
, the space of functions with bounded variation
[
17
,
23
]
. By the compactness of
\mathrm{BV}
in
L^{1}
, there exists a
L^{1}
convergent subsequence
\{\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}})\}
.
Since
\Phi
is a strictly monotonic increasing function on
\mathbb{R}
,
\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}=\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}))
converges in measure. But since
\{\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}
is also uniformly bounded in
L^{4}
, the convergence is in
L^{1}
as well.

Moreover, we notice that

\int_{\Omega}W(\phi_{\varepsilon})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\varepsilon E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon}\to 0,\ \text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

so the
L^{1}
limit
\phi
must satisfy that
W(\phi)=0,
a.e., which means it only takes binary values
0
and
1
.

(c) Weak-
*
precompactness of the vector-valued Radon measures.
By (
38
), we have known that
\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})
are uniformly bounded in
L^{1}
. We regard
\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})
as a vector-valued bounded Radon measure, i.e. a functional of continuous vector-valued functions
\bm{g}\in C_{0}(\Omega)
defined through

\left<\bm{g},\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})\right>\triangleq\int_{\Omega}\bm{g}\cdot\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

By the well-known Riesz representation theorem
[
17
, Thm. 1.38]
, Radon measures on
\Omega
constitute the dual space of
C_{0}(\Omega)
, the space of continuous functions on
\Omega
with compact support, for all bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega
.
L^{1}(\Omega)
equipped with its norm is a closed subspace of the Radon measures.
By Banach-Alaoglu theorem, bounded Radon measures are weak-
*
compact, so
\{\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})\}
is weak-
*
precompact
[
17
, Thm. 1.41]
.

Similarly, we take the anchoring density
\varepsilon|(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}+s_{+}\mathbf{I}/3)\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}
from
E_{\varepsilon}^{\rm anch}
and the double-well term
\varepsilon W(\phi_{\varepsilon})
from
E_{\varepsilon}^{\rm mix}
and perform the same operation as (
38
) to get the uniform boundedness of

M\geq\int_{\Omega}\left|\quantity(\vb Q_\ve+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

so
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}+s_{+}\mathbf{I}/3)\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})
is also uniformly bounded in
L^{1}
. By the triangle inequality,

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})=\quantity(\vb Q_\ve+\frac{s_+}{3}\vb I)\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})-\frac{s_{+}}{3}\nabla\Phi(\phi_{\varepsilon})

is uniformly bounded in
L^{1}
. We apply the same reasoning as before and complete the proof.
∎

Claims (I) and (II) can be obtained by means of basic calculus.

Proof of Claim (I).

Assume w.l.o.g. that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

for otherwise the conclusion is trivial.
Due to the weak compactness of
H_{0}^{1}
, we extract a subsequence denoted by
\{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\text{ and }\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{Q}\text{ in }H_{0}^{1}.

Note that since
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{Q}
in
L^{1}
by assumption, the weak limit of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
in
H_{0}^{1}
must also be
\mathbf{Q}
.
Hence, we have by the w.l.s.c. property
[
16
, sect. 8.2, Theorem I]
that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Since the bulk energy
\bar{F}_{b}(\mathbf{Q})
is a degree-4 polynomial
\bar{F}_{b}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}})\to\bar{F}_{b}(\mathbf{Q})
strongly by the compact Sobolev embedding
H_{0}^{1}\overset{c}{\hookrightarrow}L^{4}
in 3 dimensions
[
1
,
22
]
.
Thus,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\bar{F}_{b}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\bar{F}_{b}(\mathbf{Q})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

For the void penalty
E^{\rm void}
(
8
), we once again extract a subsequence
\{\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}\to I_{D}
(w.l.o.g. the same subsequence as before) such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}},\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}]=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\phi_{\varepsilon}]\text{ and }\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to I_{D},\text{a.e.}.

Recall the expression (
8
) of
E^{\rm void}
. It follows from Fatou’s lemma
[
47
]
that

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\phi_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(1-\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}})^{2}|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}(1-\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}})^{2}|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=E^{\rm void}[\mathbf{Q},I_{D}].

Adding the expressions above gives (
34
).
∎

Proof of Claim (II).

The same LdG energy is featured on both sides of (
35
), so we only have to check the void energy. Since
\phi_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{Q}\in C^{0}
are uniformly bounded and since
\phi_{\varepsilon}\to I_{D}
a.e., it follows from the dominated convergence theorem that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(1-\phi_{\varepsilon})^{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(1-I_{D})^{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

which yields (
35
), as required.
∎

Then, we prove Claim (III) with properties of Radon measures
[
17
]
, as well as the method of
[
44
, Prop. 1]
.

Proof of Claim (III).

We first argue that we can assume
\phi_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
[0,1]
.
Otherwise, we can define the truncated function

\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\max\{\min\{\phi(x),1\},0\}=\begin{cases}0,&\phi(x)<0,\\
\phi(x),&0\leq\phi(x)\leq 1,\\
1,&\phi(x)>1.\end{cases}

Recall that
I_{D}
takes binary values 0 and 1, so obviously
|\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(x)-I_{D}(x)|\leq|\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)-I_{D}(x)|
for all
x
. Hence,

\|\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}-\phi_{0}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|\phi_{\varepsilon}-\phi_{0}\|_{L^{1}}\to 0,

i.e. the sequence
\{\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}\}
converges in
L^{1}
as well.
In addition, it follows from the definition of the double-well function
W
and the chain rule that

W(\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})\leq W(\phi_{\varepsilon}),\ \nabla\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}=I_{\{0<\phi_{\varepsilon}<1\}}\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon},

so

\int_{\Omega}[\varepsilon^{-1}W(\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon a_{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}}(\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}[\varepsilon^{-1}W(\phi_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon a_{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}}(\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon})]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore, in order to prove Claim (III), it suffices to check that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}[\varepsilon^{-1}W(\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon a_{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}}(\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{1}{3}\int_{\partial D}|a_{\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\nu})|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S,

with
\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}\to I_{D}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
(
\varepsilon\to 0
) and the extra assumption
0\leq\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
.

Extract a subsequence
\{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon_{k}},\phi_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}\triangleq\{\mathbf{Q}_{k},\phi_{k}\}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}[\varepsilon^{-1}W(\tilde{\phi}_{k})+\varepsilon a_{\mathbf{Q}_{k}}(\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{k})]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}[\varepsilon^{-1}W(\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon a_{\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}}(\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{\varepsilon})]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

By (
28
), the LHS of the above formula equals the sum of
E^{\rm mix}_{\varepsilon_{k}}[\phi_{k}]
and
E^{\rm anch}_{\varepsilon_{k}}[\mathbf{Q}_{k},\phi_{k}]
, so these terms are bounded with respect to
\varepsilon
.
With the compactness result from Proposition
8
, we can assume further that the Radon measures
\nabla\Phi(\phi_{k})
and
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\nabla\Phi(\phi_{k})
are both weak-
*
convergent by extracting another subsequence.
Since we also assume that
\phi_{k}\to I_{D}
in
L^{1}
(so
\Phi(\phi_{k})\to\Phi(I_{D})
in
L^{1}
by the Lipschitz continuity of
\Phi
(
31
)) and that
\mathbf{Q}_{k}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{Q}
in
H_{0}^{1}
, the respective weak-
*
limits of these measures must be

\nabla\Phi(\phi_{k})\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\mathrm{D}\Phi(I_{D}),\ \mathbf{Q}_{k}\nabla\Phi(\phi_{k})\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\mathbf{Q}\mathrm{D}\Phi(I_{D}),

(39)

where
\mathrm{D}
stands for the weak derivative as a generalized function, as distinguished from
\nabla
.

Proof of (
39
).

It suffices to prove a component-wise result. That is, for all function sequences
f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
g_{n}\to g
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, satisfying further that
f_{n},f\in C^{0}\cap H_{0}^{1}
,
f_{n},f,g_{n},g
are uniformly bounded, and that
\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}
and
f_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}
both weak-
*
converge to some Radon measure on
\Omega
, it must hold that

\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{x_{i}}g,\ f_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}f\partial_{x_{i}}g.

Up to extracting a subsequence, we may assume further that
f_{n}\to f
and
g_{n}\to g
a.e..
We need to verify for each
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
that

\int_{\Omega}\psi\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\psi\mathrm{D}_{x_{i}}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\ \int_{\Omega}\psi f_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}g_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\psi f\mathrm{D}_{x_{i}}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Using the definition for weak derivatives and integration by parts, we see that the condition is equivalent to

\int_{\Omega}g_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}g\partial_{x_{i}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\ \int_{\Omega}(f_{n}g_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}\psi+\psi g_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}f_{n})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}(fg\partial_{x_{i}}\psi+\psi g\partial_{x_{i}}f)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The first convergence is obviously correct, as
\partial_{x_{i}}\psi
is smooth.
By Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, since
f_{n}g_{n}\to fg
a.e. and
\{f_{n}g_{n}\}
is uniformly bounded by assumption,

\int_{\Omega}f_{n}g_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}fg\partial_{x_{i}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\psi g_{n}\to\psi g
a.e. and
\partial_{x_{i}}f_{n}\to\partial_{x_{i}}f
weakly in
L^{2}
,

\int_{\Omega}\psi g_{n}\partial_{x_{i}}f_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\psi g\partial_{x_{i}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Hence, the second convergence is also correct.
The correctness of (
39
) is then established.
∎

Using the same elementary inequality technique as in (
38
), we compute that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\quantity[\ve a_{\vb Q_k}(\nabla\phi_k) + \ve^{-1}W(\phi_k)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

Step 3. Integration with coarea formula.

Finally, we evaluate the functional (

\phi_{\varepsilon}

We utilize the following coarea formula for a Lipschitz function

and an integrable function

[

17
,
23
]
, which translates volume integrals to surface integrals.

\int_{\Omega}f(x)|\nabla h|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\int_{\{h=r\}\cap\Omega}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S(x).

(61)

The steps are identical to those of Modica
[

.

By (

Using the differential equation (

\varepsilon a(x,\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon^{-1}(W(\phi_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)a(x,\nabla h^{a})=\varepsilon^{-1}(W(\phi_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon).

Thus, we transcribe the integral into

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}^{a}(\phi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\alpha<\phi_{\varepsilon}<\beta\}}\varepsilon^{-1}(2W(\phi_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\delta_{\varepsilon}}^{\eta_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}}{|\nabla h^{a}|}(2W(\phi_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S(x),

Substitute the definition (

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}^{a}(\phi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\delta_{\varepsilon}}^{\eta_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\frac{2W(\tilde{\chi}_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta_{\varepsilon}))+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon|\nabla h^{a}|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S(x)

Lemma 18

Under the assumptions of Lemma

7
Conclusion and discussions

In this paper, we propose a diffuse-interface LdG energy functional,
E_{\varepsilon}
(
1
) for the free boundary between a nematic droplet and an isotropic surrounding. The model incorporates a LdG
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor and a phase field
\phi
as order parameters, with an extra hyperparameter
\varepsilon
introduced to describe the width of interface.

We establish the existence (Proposition
2
) of global minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
.
For fixed
\phi
, we have also shown that the
\mathbf{Q}
-critical points satisfy the maximum principle (Theorem
5
) and uniqueness (Proposition
6
) under certain conditions.
The existence, uniqueness and regularity properties, as a whole, ensure the solvability of mathematical models centred around the diffuse-interface LdG functional,
E_{\varepsilon}
.
We also prove the sharp-interface limit of
E_{\varepsilon}
when
\varepsilon\to 0
, in the sense of
\Gamma
-convergence (Theorem

The sharp-interface limit of the diffuse-interface LdG energy is defined in terms of a nematic region

The theoretical work is complemented by numerical tests that yield physically interpretable results, which are also consistent with existing observations in other free-boundary NLC models. In particular, we observe the transition form radial to polar and tactoidal states when adjusting the scale
\lambda

There are multiple generalisations of our work.
The diffuse-interface framework is a powerful tool in describing deformable regions, and can be used to model other LC phases such as smectic phases with free boundaries

For example, there is room for improvement for our theoretical results such as the maximum principle and uniqueness, which is only proven in the fixed-

To summarise, our work is a firm stepping stone into the hugely complex and fascinating field of NLC shape morphologies with free boundaries, and will contribute to mathematical toolboxes for studying optimal shapes for prescribed conditions, and the inverse problem of finding optimal conditions for a prescribed or desired NLC morphology.
