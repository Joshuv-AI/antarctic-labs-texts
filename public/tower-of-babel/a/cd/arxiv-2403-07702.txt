Lipschitz maps with prescribed local Lipschitz constants
Aidan Backus, Ng Ze-An

Abstract
. Let Γ \Gamma be a closed subset of a complete Riemannian manifold M M of dimension ≥ 2 \geq 2 , let f : M → N f:M\to N be a Lipschitz map to a complete Riemannian manifold N N , and let ψ \psi be a continuous function which dominates the local Lipschitz constant of f f . We construct a Lipschitz map which agress with f f on Γ \Gamma and whose local Lipschitz constant is ψ \psi .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
primary: 54C20, 26A16

1.
Introduction

A basic problem in metric geometry concerns the extension of Lipschitz maps: given metric spaces
X,Y
, a closed set
\Gamma\subset X
and a Lipschitz map
f:\Gamma\to Y
, can we extend
f
to a Lipschitz map
u:X\to Y
so that “
u
does not stretch
X
more than
f
stretches
\Gamma
”?
This problem is partially resolved by the
Kirszbraun–Valentine theorem

[
LS97
,
GK17
]
, which, under a suitable hypothesis on the Alexandrov curvatures of
X,Y
, furnishes an extension
u
whose global Lipschitz constant

\mathrm{Lip}(u,X):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in X\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\dist_{Y}(u(x),u(y))}{\dist_{X}(x,y)}

is as small as possible.
For example, if
\Gamma
is a closed subset of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
f:\Gamma\to\mathbf{R}^{D}
is a Lipschitz map, then
f
admits an extension
u
to all of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that

\mathrm{Lip}(u,\mathbf{R}^{d})=\mathrm{Lip}(f,\Gamma).

We refer the reader to
[
GK17
, §3]
for an elegant proof of the Kirszbraun-Valentine theorem in the setting of hyperbolic geometry; the same argument also applies in the euclidean setting.

Because the Kirszbraun–Valentine extension
u
of
f
can be nonunique, it is natural to impose further conditions on
u
by constraining the
local Lipschitz constants

Lu(x):=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathrm{Lip}(u,B(x,r)).

Previous work has considered maps which minimize
Lu
in various senses; such maps are called
tight
or
\infty
-harmonic

[
Cra08
,
SS12
,
NS12
]
.
The
\infty
-harmonic scalar fields are exactly the viscosity solutions of the
\infty
-Laplacian

\langle\nabla^{2}u,\nabla u\otimes\nabla u\rangle=0.

The existence and uniqueness of tight maps is a fascinating and challenging open question
[
SS12
, §5]
.

In this paper, we complement the study of tight maps by exhibiting a sort of extreme nonuniqueness for the Kirszbraun–Valentine theorem.
Given any continuous function
\psi
which is greater than the local Lipschitz constant of a Lipschitz extension of
f
, we construct a Lipschitz extension of
f
with local Lipschitz constant
\psi
.
More precisely, we show:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian manifold such that
\dim M\geq 2
, and let
N
be a complete Riemannian manifold.
Let
\Gamma\subseteq M
be a closed set and
\psi:M\setminus\Gamma\to\mathbf{R}_{+}
be a continuous function.
Then for every Lipschitz map
f:M\to N
such that
Lf\leq\psi
on
M\setminus\Gamma
, there exists a Lipschitz map
u:M\to N
, homotopic to
f
relative to
\Gamma
, such that

(1.1)

\ Lu=\psi\text{ on }M\setminus\Gamma.

By taking
\Gamma=\emptyset
,
N=\mathbf{R}
, and
f=0
in Theorem
1.1
, we conclude:

Corollary 1.2
.

For every continuous function
\psi:M\to\mathbf{R}_{+}
, there exists a Lipschitz function
u:M\to\mathbf{R}
with
Lu=\psi
.

Corollary
1.2
does not characterize those functions which can appear as local Lipschitz constants.
Indeed,
u(x,y):=1_{x>0}x

has
Lu(x,y)=1_{x\geq 0}
.
If
\psi
appears as a local Lipschitz constant, then
\psi
is necessarily upper-semicontinuous and “lower-semicontinuous on average” (see Lemma
2.4
).
However, we do not know if this condition is sufficient; our method of proof does not seem to be able to show that such a function appears as a local Lipschitz constant.
We therefore pose:

Problem 1.3
.

Let
\psi:M\to\mathbf{R}_{+}
be an upper-semicontinuous function.
What conditions on
\psi
are necessary and sufficient for there to exist a Lipschitz function
u:M\to\mathbf{R}
such that
Lu=\psi
?

Let
u:M\to N
be a map between closed manifolds which minimizes its Lipschitz constant in its homotopy class.
In applications to Teichmüller theory and its generalizations by the Thurston school, the
stretch set

\{Lu=\mathrm{Lip}(u,M)\}
of the map
u
plays a crucial role
[
GK17
,
DU22
,
Thu98
]
.
Theorem
1.1
shows that any closed set which contains the so-called “maximally-stretched geodesic lamination” (see
[
GK17
, Theorem 5.1]
) can appear as a stretch set.

1.1.
Idea of the proof

It is tempting to look for solutions of the eikonal equation (
1.1
) using Perron’s method.
Of course, if
\dim N\geq 2
, then (
1.1
) is a strongly coupled system, so the usual formulation of Perron’s method
[
Ish92
]
fails.
However, this scheme would fail even when
\dim N=1
, because Perron’s method as usually formulated gives a viscosity solution, but viscosity solutions of (
1.1
) do not have to satisfy
u|_{\Gamma}=f
in the classical sense
[
CIL92
, §7.C]
.

So we need a strategy for constructing
nonviscosity
solutions of (
1.1
).
If we take
\tilde{u}:=f
, then
\tilde{u}
is a subsolution of (
1.1
) in the sense that (classically)

\begin{cases}L\tilde{u}\leq\psi&\text{ on }M\setminus\Gamma,\\
\tilde{u}=f&\text{ on }\Gamma.\end{cases}

The idea, as in Perron’s method, is to iteratively modify
\tilde{u}
in order to increase
L\tilde{u}
, without ever violating that
\tilde{u}
is a subsolution.
We have to carry out this process carefully, however, because our modifications are artificial oscillations, which could experience destructive interference in the limit.

The trick is to observe that, since
Lu(x)
(as opposed to, say, the Frobenius norm
|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(x)|_{2}
) is determined by the behavior of
u
along small line segments near
x
, and a line segment has positive codimension, we can choose line segments so that every point of
U
is suitably close to a line segment, while ensuring that all line segments do not interact with each other.
Then our modifications of
\tilde{u}
will only be along the line segments.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Metric geometry

Throughout, we fix complete Riemannian manifolds
M,N
, and assume that
\dim M\geq 2
.
We denote by
B(x,r)
the ball of radius
r
centered on
x\in M
.
If we want to consider balls in another metric space
X
, we write
B_{X}(x,r)
.
If
B:=B(x,r)
, then
\Phi(B):=B(x,r^{2})
.

If
X
is a metric space,
x\in X
, and
Y\subseteq X
is a set, then

\dist(x,Y):=\inf_{y\in Y}\dist(x,y).

If
Z\subseteq X
, then we put

\dist(Y,Z):=\inf_{y\in Y}\dist(y,Z),

which we carefully note is not the Hausdorff distance.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
W\subseteq M
be an open set and
r>0
.
An
r
-packing
of
W
is a set
\mathscr{P}
of disjoint balls
B(x,r)\Subset W
, such that if
B(x,r)\in\mathscr{P}
then the injectivity radius of
M
at
x
is at most
r
.

In particular, if
B(x,r)
is contained in an
r
-packing, then
\dist(x,\partial W)\geq r
.
An
r
-packing
\mathscr{P}
of
W
is
maximal
if there does not exist an
r
-packing
\mathscr{Q}
of
W
with
\mathscr{Q}\supset\mathscr{P}
.
Every packing is contained in a maximal packing, by Zorn’s lemma.

Let
d:=\dim M
.
The
d-1
-dimensional upper Minkowski content
of a set
X\subseteq M
is

\mathcal{M}^{d-1}(X):=\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\{x\in M:\dist(x,X)<r\})}{2r}.

In particular, if
X
is a smooth submanifold of
M
of dimension
d-1
, then
\mathcal{M}^{d-1}(M)
is the surface area of
M
.

2.2.
Maxima of upper-semicontinous functions

Let
S
be a compact Hausdorff space.
If
f:S\to\mathbf{R}
is an upper-semicontinuous function, then
f
attains its maximum somewhere on
S
.
We want to show that we can compute the maximum of
f
by testing against finite subsets of
S
.

We refer the reader to
[
Kel75
]
for a review of nets in point-set topology.
The set
\Fin(S)
of finite subsets of
S
is a directed set under
\subseteq
, so we can think of a function
\Theta:\Fin(S)\to\mathbf{R}
as a net.
By definition, the equation

L=\lim_{X\in\Fin(S)}\Theta(X)

means that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
X\in\Fin(S)
such that if
Y\in\Fin(S)
and
X\subseteq Y
, then

|\Theta(Y)-L|<\varepsilon.

From this definition it follows that for any upper-semicontinuous function
f:S\to\mathbf{R}
,

(2.1)

\max_{x\in S}f(x)=\lim_{X\in\Fin(S)}\max_{x\in X}f(x).

2.3.
Lipschitz maps

Let
X
be a metric space, and
u:X\to N
a Lipschitz map.
Then the
local Lipschitz constant
is

Lu(x):=\limsup_{r\to 0}\mathrm{Lip}(u,B_{X}(x,r)).

Since
\mathrm{Lip}(u,B_{X}(x,r))
is a nondecreasing function of
r
, this limit superior is actually a limit and an infimum, and the function
Lu
is upper-semicontinuous.
It is easily seen for fixed
x
that
u\mapsto Lu(x)
is a seminorm on the space of Lipschitz maps.
If
X
is a smooth manifold, then for almost every
x\in X
,
Lu(x)=|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(x)|_{\infty}
, where
|\cdot|_{\infty}
is the operator norm on matrices, and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\in\Hom(TX,u^{*}(TN))
is the derivative of
u
.
We refer the reader to
[
Cra08
, §4]
for a more careful discussion of the local Lipschitz constant.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K
be a convex compact subset of
M
and
u:K\to N
a Lipschitz map.
Then

(2.2)

\mathrm{Lip}(u,K)=\max_{z\in K}Lu(z).

In this lemma, we are taking
Lu
to be computed in the metric space
K
.
In other words,

Lu(z)=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathrm{Lip}(u,B_{K}(z,r))=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathrm{Lip}(u,K\cap B(z,r)).

Proof.

Clearly for any
x\in K
,
Lu(x)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(u,K)
.
Conversely, let
x,y\in K
.
Since
K
is convex, there is a minimizing geodesic
\ell\subseteq K
from
x
to
y
.
Then
\ell\subseteq K
, and by
[
Cra08
, Lemma 4.2(d)]
,

\displaystyle\dist_{N}(u(x),u(y))\leq\max_{z\in\ell}Lu(z)\dist_{M}(x,y)\leq\max_{z\in K}Lu(z)\dist_{M}(x,y).

The proof of the cited lemma goes through when
Lu(z)
is computed in the metric space
\ell
, not in the ambient metric space
M
.
Similarly, the proof assumes that
N=\mathbf{R}
, but the proof goes through generally.
∎

Let
K>0
and let
(u_{n})
be a sequence of maps on a compact metric space
X
, such that
u_{n}\to u
pointwise and
\mathrm{Lip}(u_{n},X)\leq K
.
Then it follows from the Arzela-Ascoli theorem that
u_{n}\to u
uniformly and

(2.3)

\mathrm{Lip}(u,X)\leq K.

In fact, we can do better, and show that
L
is lower-semicontinuous:

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
u_{k},u:M\to N
are maps with
u_{k}\to u
in
L^{1}
and
\mathrm{Lip}(u_{k},U)\leq C
.
Then for almost every
x\in M
,

Lu(x)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}Lu_{k}(x).

Proof.

We first use the Lebesgue differentiation theorem to write, for almost every
x\in M
,

Lu(x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}Lu(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(y)|_{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

We now use lower-semicontinuity of
|\cdot|_{\infty}
-total variation to bound

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(y)|_{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{k}(y)|_{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}Lu_{k}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

The lower-semicontinuity of
|\cdot|_{\infty}
-total variation can be proven, analogously to the scalar case
[
Giu84
, Theorem 1.9]
, using the duality between the norms
\||\cdot|_{\infty}\|_{L^{1}}
and
\||\cdot|_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}
on matrix-valued functions (where
|\cdot|_{1}
is the trace norm), and we omit the details.

Because
Lu_{k}(y)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(u_{k},U)\leq C
, we can apply dominated convergence, and then use the Lebesgue differentiation theorem again: for almost every
x\in M
,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}Lu_{k}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}\liminf_{k\to\infty}Lu_{k}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\liminf_{k\to\infty}Lu_{k}(x).\qed

Let
u
be a Lipschitz map.
Though
Lu
is not lower-semicontinuous, it satisfies the following “averaged” version of lower-semicontinuity, which was implicit in
[
SS12
, §1.4.2]
:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
u:M\to N
be a Lipschitz map, and let
\psi:M\to\mathbf{R}_{+}
a lower-semicontinuous function.
If
Lu\leq\psi
on almost all of
M
, then
Lu\leq\psi
on
M
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in M
.
Then there is an open neighborhood
V
of
x
such that:

(1)

If
y\in V
, then
\psi(y)\leq\psi(x)+\varepsilon
.

(2)

The image
u_{*}(V)
is contained in a convex subset of
N
.

Let
\Delta\subset V^{2}
be the diagonal over
V
.
Then the difference quotient

f(y,z):=\frac{\dist_{N}(u(x),u(y))}{\dist_{M}(x,y)}

satisfies

Lu(x)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(u,V)\leq\sup_{y,z\in V^{2}\setminus\Delta}f(y,z).

Since
\Delta
is a null subset of
V^{2}
and
f
is continuous on
V^{2}\setminus\Delta
, this supremum is the
L^{\infty}
norm of
f
.

Given
y,z\in V
, let
[y,z]
be a minimizing geodesic from
y
to
z
.
We estimate using the mean value and Jensen inequalities

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\iint_{V^{2}}\frac{1}{\dist_{M}(y,z)^{p}}\int_{[y,z]}Lu(w)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\right]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\iint_{V^{2}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[y,z]}Lu(w)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\right]^{1/p}.

Let
E(y,w)
be the set of points in
V
on the line
\ell
through
y,w
which are to the right of
w
, where
\ell
is oriented so that the vector
w-y
points to the right.
Then
w\in[y,z]
iff
z\in E(y,w)
, so we can rewrite the bounds of integration using Fubini’s theorem as follows:

\iint_{V^{2}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[y,z]}Lu(w)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\iint_{V^{2}}\int_{E(y,w)}Lu(w)^{p}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\dist_{M}(y,z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w.

The point is that
w
now ranges over
V
, and we know that for almost every
w\in V
,

Lu(w)\leq\psi(x)+\varepsilon.

We therefore can estimate this integral, and undo the change in the order of integration:

\displaystyle\iint_{V^{2}}\int_{E(y,w)}Lu(w)^{p}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\dist_{M}(y,z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\leq(\psi(x)+\varepsilon)^{p}\iint_{V^{2}}\int_{E(y,w)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\dist_{M}(y,z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=(\psi(x)+\varepsilon)^{p}\iint_{V^{2}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[y,z]}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=(\psi(x)+\varepsilon)^{p}\mathrm{vol}(V)^{2}.

In other words, we can estimate for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle Lu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq(\psi(x)+\varepsilon)\lim_{p\to\infty}\mathrm{vol}(V)^{2/p}=\psi(x)+\varepsilon.\qed

Finally, we observe that for functions on
\mathbf{R}
, the Lipschitz constant is preserved by taking maxima:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbf{R}
be an interval and
f,g:I\to\mathbf{R}
Lipschitz functions.
Then

\mathrm{Lip}(\max(f,g),I)\leq\max(\mathrm{Lip}(f,I),\mathrm{Lip}(g,I)).

Proof.

Let
h:=\max(f,g)
,
K:=\max(\mathrm{Lip}(f,I),\mathrm{Lip}(g,I))
, and
x,y\in I
.
We may assume that
x<y
.
We have a suitable estimate on
|h(x)-h(y)|
if both
f(x)\geq g(x)
and
f(y)\geq g(y)
, or if
g(x)\geq f(x)
and
g(y)\geq f(y)
.
So we may assume that
f(x)>g(x)
and
g(y)>f(y)
.
Then there exists
z\in(x,y)
such that
f(z)=g(z)
, and we estimate

\displaystyle|h(x)-h(y)|

\displaystyle=|f(x)-g(y)|\leq|f(x)-f(z)|+|g(z)-g(y)|

\displaystyle\leq K|x-z|+K|z-y|=K|x-y|.\qed

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

3.1.
Modifying the Lipschitz constant on a packing

We want to show that given a packing
\mathscr{P}
and a Lipschitz map
u
, we can modify
u
along a line segment close to the center of each ball in
\mathscr{P}
, while ensuring that
u
is held fixed away from the centers of the balls in
\mathscr{P}
.
Recalling our notation
\Phi(B(x,r)):=B(x,r^{2})
, our goal in this section is to prove:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
V\subseteq M
be an open set,
0<r\leq 1
, and
\psi:V\to\mathbf{R}_{+}
a continuous function.
Let
u:V\to N
satisfy
Lu\leq\psi
.
Then for every
r
-packing
\mathscr{P}
of
V
, there exists
v:V\to N
such that:

(1)

For every
x\in V
,
Lv(x)\leq\psi(x)
.

(2)

For every
x\in V\setminus\bigcup\{\Phi(B):B\in\mathscr{P}\}
,
v(x)=u(x)
.

(3)

For every
\varepsilon>0
and every
B\in\mathscr{P}
, there exists a closed line segment
\ell\Subset\Phi(B)
such that

\mathrm{Lip}(v,\ell)>\max_{x\in\ell}\psi(x)-\varepsilon.

(4)

v
is homotopic to
u
relative to
\partial V
.

We are going to work our way up to the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
First, we show how to improve
Lu
at a single point in a ball

without modifying
u
away from
B
.
We use this to show to modify
u
along a line segment inside a prescribed open set

without modifying
u
away from
W
.
By taking
W=\Phi(B)
where
B\in\mathscr{P}
, we deduce Proposition
3.1
.

On Riemannian manifolds, we need the following lemma, which is essentially obvious on euclidean space:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
S\subseteq M

, and let
u:M\to N
be a continuous map.
There exists a family of smooth maps
\chi_{t}:M\to\mathbf{R}^{D}

,

(1)

\mathrm{Lip}(\chi_{t},S)\geq t
.

(2)

\supp\chi_{t}\subseteq S
.

(3)

For each
s\geq 0
,

(3.1)

\mathrm{Lip}(\chi_{t}-\chi_{s},S)\lesssim|t-s|

where the implied constant only depends on the geometry of
N
near the image of
S
under
u
.

(4)

For each
z\in S
,
|\chi_{t}(z)|
is smaller than the injectivity radius of

u(z)
.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}
be the center of
S
.
By replacing

Furthermore, we may shrink

\varepsilon_{0}>0

For

By identifying

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that

, and let

on

Let

, and let

.

in normal coordinates centered at

In other words, we identify the tangent space

We first remark that

\psi
is continuous, and

Let

We can embed the space of maps

at

Then we can think of

We then estimate using (

) and the fact that
\mathrm{Lip}(\chi_{t}-\chi_{s},M)\lesssim t-s

(3.2)

so we conclude from (

\displaystyle\leq C.\qed

By assumption,

On the other hand,

So by the claim, there exists

Then:

(1)

For every

(2)

Since

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
W\subseteq V
be an open set and
\psi:V\to\mathbf{R}_{+}

Let

satisfy
Lu\leq\psi
.
Then there exists
v:V\to N
such that:

(1)

For every

,
Lv(x)\leq\psi(x)
.

(2)

For every

,

.

(3)

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a closed minimizing geodesic segment

such that

(4)

is homotopic to
u
relative to
\partial V
.

Choose a ball

, and apply Lemma

to the ball

), (

), and (

For each
\rho>0
, let

be the set of closed line segments in

.

), we are going to apply compactness and contradiction in the limit
\rho\to 0
.

If (

) fails, then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for every

is upper-semicontinuous, and the limit in the definition of

∎

3.2.
Iterating the packing procedure

We are going to iteratively apply Proposition

to finer and finer packings; at each dyadic scale, we get some improvement to a given Lipschitz map

.
Later we shall take a limit as the scale goes to

; it is important that we choose our packings so carefully that in the limit, our improvements do not cancel each other.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

be an open set such that

(1)

We proceed by induction. Assume that we have defined

So there is a maximal

-packing

of

.
Let

By Proposition

, we can find
u_{i}:W\to\mathbf{R}^{D}

.
We will later show that for every dyadic scale

does not intersect

y_{i}\in\ell
such that

Lu(y_{i})>\max_{z\in\ell}\psi(z)-\varepsilon\geq\psi(y_{i})-\varepsilon.

But
|y_{i}-q|<2^{-i}
, so
|y_{i}-x|<2^{-i+5}
.

We can now take
i\to\infty
and apply upper-semicontinuity of
Lu
and continuity of
\psi
to deduce that for every
x\in V\setminus Z
,

Lu(x)\geq\psi(x)-\varepsilon.

Since
Lu(x)
is upper-semicontinuous and
Z
is null, this inequality holds for every
x\in V
.
Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary,
Lu\geq\psi
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Removing the geometric assumptions

We finally remove Assumption
3.7
.
We first remove the assumption on the curvature tensor by a scaling argument, and then remove the other assumptions by taking a compact exhaustion of
M\setminus\Gamma
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Theorem
1.1
holds if we in addition assume for
V:=M\setminus\Gamma
that
V
is precompact in
M
, and
\mathcal{M}^{d-1}(\partial V)<\infty
.

Proof.

Since
V
is precompact in
M
,
\mathrm{vol}(V)<\infty
and
|\mathrm{Riem}_{M}|
is bounded on a neighborhood of
\overline{V}
.
Moreover, Theorem
1.1
is invariant under rescaling the metric on
M
, provided that we rescale
\psi
as well.
So it is no loss to assume that
|\mathrm{Riem}_{M}|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
on a neighborhood of
\overline{V}
.
Then we have met Assumption
3.7
, so the conclusion follows from Lemma
3.10
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
V:=M\setminus\Gamma
, and choose a sequence
(V_{n})
of precompact open subsets of
V
such that:

(1)

V_{n+1}\supseteq V_{n}
.

(2)

\bigcup_{n}V_{n}=V
.

(3)

V_{0}=\emptyset
.

(4)

\partial V_{n}
is smooth.

Also let
\Gamma_{n}:=\overline{V}_{n-1}\cup(M\setminus V_{n})
if
n\geq 1
.
Let
u_{0}:=f
, and suppose that we are given
n\geq 1
and maps
u_{0},\dots,u_{n-1}
such that:

(1)

For each
m\leq n-1
,
Lu_{m}=\psi
on
V_{m}
.

(2)

For each
1\leq m\leq n-1
,
u_{m}=u_{m-1}
on
\Gamma_{m}
.

Since
\partial(V_{n}\setminus\Gamma_{n})
is a compact smooth hypersurface,
\mathcal{M}^{d-1}(\partial(V_{n}\setminus\Gamma_{n}))<\infty
.
By Lemma
3.11
applied to with
M
replaced by
V_{n}
and
\Gamma
replaced by
\Gamma_{n}
, we can find a map
u_{n}
such that
u_{n}=u_{n-1}
on
\Gamma_{n}
and
Lu_{n}=\psi
on
V_{n}\setminus\overline{V}_{n-1}
.

Claim
.

For every
x\in\overline{V}_{n-1}
,
Lu_{n}(x)=\psi(x)
.

Proof of claim.

We first observe that the result holds if
x\in V_{n-1}
, since the germs of
u_{n}
and
u_{n-1}
at
x
agree in that case.
So
Lu_{n}=\psi
on
V_{n}\setminus\partial V_{n-1}
.

Let
x\in\partial V_{n-1}
.
By upper-semicontinuity of
Lu_{n}
, continuity of
\psi
, and the fact that the claim holds away from
\partial V_{n-1}
, we deduce
Lu_{n}(x)\geq\psi(x)
.
Conversely, since
\partial V_{n-1}
is a smooth hypersurface, it is null, so by Lemma
2.4
, continuity of
\psi
, and the fact that the claim holds away from
\partial V_{n-1}
,
Lu_{n}(x)\leq\psi(x)
.
∎

Therefore
u_{n}
satisfies the inductive assumption.
It is clear that for each
m
,
u_{n}|_{V_{m}}
stabilizes when
n=m
.
So
(u_{n})
converges locally uniformly to a map
u
with
Lu=Lu_{n}
on
V_{n}
.
Therefore
Lu=\psi
.
∎
