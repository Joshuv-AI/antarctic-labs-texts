Generalized Inverse Based Decoding
Ferucio Laurenţiu Ţiplea, Vlad Florin Drăgoi

Abstract
The concept of Generalized Inverse based Decoding (GID) is introduced, as an algebraic framework for the syndrome decoding problem (SDP) and low weight codeword problem (LWP). The framework has ground on two characterizations by generalized inverses (GIs), one for the null space of a matrix and the other for the solution space of a system of linear equations over a finite field. Generic GID solvers are proposed for SDP and LWP. It is shown that information set decoding (ISD) algorithms, such as Prange, Lee-Brickell, Leon, and Stern’s algorithms, are particular cases of GID solvers. All of them search GIs or elements of the null space under various specific strategies. However, as the paper shows the ISD variants do not search through the entire space, while our solvers do even when they use just one Gaussian elimination. Apart from these, our GID framework clearly shows how each ISD algorithm, except for Prange’s solution, can be used as an SDP or LWP solver. A tight reduction from our problems, viewed as optimization problems, to the MIN-SAT problem is also provided. Experimental results show a very good behavior of the GID solvers. The domain of easy weights can be reached by a very few iterations and even enlarged.

Introduction

McEliece’s cryptosystem

Rapid evolution of quantum computing
[
LGBNA2021
,
Pres2018
,
TaFu2019
]
,
as well as Shor’s famous quantum algorithm for discrete logarithm
and factorization
[
Shor1994
]
are urging public key cryptography
to move away from number theoretic based solutions. Initiated in
2015 by the NIST, the post-quantum standardization process searches
for quantum secure techniques for key exchange and digital signatures.
Round 3 candidates
[
PQC
]

for key exchange/key encapsulation mechanism are
either code-based or lattice-based solutions. Amongst them, we
discover one of the oldest public key encryption scheme, proposed
in 1978 by McEliece
[
McEl1978
]
. McEliece had the idea to generate
a linear code that admits an efficient decoding algorithm
(the private key) and to mask its structure (the masked code is
the public key). Under the assumption that the public code is
indistinguishable from a random code, breaking the scheme resumes
to solving the syndrome decoding problem (SDP) for random codes,
which is NP-complete
[
BeET1978
]
. So, until one proves that
there exist NP-complete problems that can be solved in polynomial
time by quantum computers (such a result would be major breakthrough
in complexity theory
[
BeVa1997
,
Simo1994
]
)
the McEliece scheme is considered quantum secure.

As being the foundation of the McEliece scheme, a significant
interest in finding algorithms for SDP emerged. Also, having
a good estimation of the work factor required by algorithms for
SDP is mandatory to asses the security level of the aforementioned scheme
[
EsBe2021
,
HaSe2013
,
BBCPS2019
,
Pete2010
]
.

Information set decoding

Intiated in 1962 by E. Prange
[
Pran1962
]
, Information Set Decoding
(ISD) is a well-known technique for solving several fundamental problems
in coding theory, e.g., SDP, low weight codeword
problem (LWP), and even code equivalence problem (CEP) (see
[
HuKS2021
]
).
When
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
both SDP and LWP are NP-Complete (see
[
BeET1978
]

for SDP and
[
Vard1997
]
for LWP). Up-to-date, for solving SDP and LWP,
the latest variants of ISD
[
BJMM2012
,
MaOz2015
,
BoMa2018
]
are the most
efficient techniques, in term of
time complexity. The decisional SDP takes as input the parity-check matrix

\bm{H}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-k,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
of a linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
over a finite field
with
q
elements
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, a syndrome vector
\bm{s}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n-k}
and an
integer
t
, and asks if there is a solution to the equation
\bm{H}\bm{x}=\bm{s}

satisfying
\left|\bm{x}\right|\leq t,
where “
\left|\bm{x}\right|
” denotes the Hamming weight of the
vector
\bm{x}
. In SDP, syndrome vectors are always non-zero. When
\bm{s}=\bm{0}

the problem becomes LWP. The main idea behind the original ISD technique
(Prange’s approach)
is to pick a sufficiently large set of error-free coordinates such that
the corresponding columns of
\bm{H}
form an invertible submatrix. This is equivalent
to computing two matrices
\bm{P}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
and
\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{S}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})

such that

\bm{P}\bm{H}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}&\bm{I}_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}.
This gives us

\bm{x}=\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\bm{P}\bm{s}\end{pmatrix}.
The correct information
set for SDP is found when
\left|\bm{P}\bm{s}\right|\leq t.
In other words, this procedure
will stop eventually, as there is a permutation which sends the support of
the solution outside the information set.
Relaxation of certain architectural constraints of Prange’s
algorithm or the addition of optimizations, mainly based on the birthday
paradox (also known as the meet-in-the-middle approach), has led to many
improvements

[
LeBr1988
,
Ster1988
,
Leon1988
,
Dume1989
,
Dume1991
,
CoGF1991
,
CaCh1998
,
FiSe2009
,
BeLP2011
,
MaMT2011
,
BJMM2012
,
MaOz2015
,
BoMa2018
]
.

The difficulty of solving SDP highly depends on the range of values for
the parameter
t.
In a cryptographic context, it is frequent to select hard
instances, i.e., where
t
is close to the Gilbert-Varshamov bound or
sub-linear in the code-length
[
BeET1978
,
Gilb1952
,
Vars1957
]
.
When
t
is linear in
n
, optimizations
to Prange’s algorithm have better complexity results even in the first term
in the exponent. However, when
t
is sub-linear in
n
, which is the case
of all NIST code-based submissions, the advantage of all the improvements
vanish asymptotically. To be more precise, for
t=o(n)
and
n\to\infty

the work factor of existing algorithms for SDP equals

2^{-t\log(1-k/n)(1+o(1))}

[
CaSe2016
]
.

It worths mentioning that ISD is not the only technique for solving SDP.
For example, statistical decoding has a quite different approach

[
FoKI2007
,
Jabr2001
,
Over2006
,
DeTJ2017
]
. However, it does not achieve
performance comparable to even the simplest ISD techniques, e.g.,
Prange’s algorithm.

Both SDP and LWP can be seen as particular cases of some well-known
generic problems, Coset Weight Problem (CWP) and Subspace Weight Problem
(SWP)
[
BeET1978
]
.
The difference between CWP and SDP, repectively between SWP and LWP,
resides in the input matrix
\bm{A}
which for CWP and SWP is an arbitrary matrix
from
{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}).
Notice that when
m=n-k\leq n
and
\text{\rm rank}(\bm{A})=n-k
,
CWP becomes SDP. In order to stay as general as possible, in this
paper we will present solutions for CWP/SWP and restrict to full
rank matrices when discussions move towards coding theory.

Generalized inverse of a matrix

Since one has to solve a system of linear equations to find a solution
for CWP, the idea of computing the inverse of the matrix
\bm{A}
comes
natural in mind. However, as
\bm{A}
is not square, we can not apply
this technique here.
Nevertheless, the concept of matrix inverse exists in the case of
non-square matrices.
It is known as
generalized inverse
(GI). Given a matrix

\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
, a GI for
\bm{A}
is a matrix
\bm{X}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,m}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfying
\bm{A}\bm{X}\bm{A}=\bm{A}.
Several types of
inverses are known, such as, reflexive, normalized, and
pseudo-inverse (or Moore-Penrose inverse
[
Moor1920
,
Penr1955
]
).
In particular, the Moore-Penrose inverse is a helpful tool
when minimum norm solutions are required over the field of
real or complex numbers. However, when moving to finite fields,
things change a lot, mainly due to the geometrical properties
of the scalar product. Results regarding the GI
in arbitrary and finite fields exist

[
Pear1968
,
Fult1978
,
BeGr2006
]
and are going to be used
and extended here in the context of linear codes.

There were several attempts to use GI of a
matrix in cryptography and coding theory

[
WuDa1998
,
Sun2001
,
NgDa2013
,
NgDa2017
,
Fini2005
]
.
In 1998, Wu and Dawson
[
WuDa1998
]
have proposed a public-key
cryptosystem based on GIs, but three years later
it was cryptanalyzed
[
Sun2001
]
.
Dang and Nguyen
[
NgDa2013
,
NgDa2017
]
have used
pseudo-inverses, the strongest form of a GI, to
design key exchange protocols and protocols for privacy-preserving
auditing data in cloud.

The only reference of GIs with respect to SDP is by Finiasz
[
Fini2005
]

(
\bm{H}
is the parity-check matrix,

\bm{S}
is the syndrome, and the threshold is
w
):

“For instance, when
w
is
larger than
n/2
, solving SD becomes easy, as computing a
pseudo-inverse
\bm{H}^{-1}
of
\bm{H}
and computing
\bm{H}^{-1}\cdot\bm{S}

will return a valid solution with large probability. However, for
smaller values of
w
, when a single solution exists, finding it
becomes much harder.”

The pseudo-inverse of a matrix, when it exists, is unique. Its use
in enumerating a space of possible values is doomed to failure.
But if the inverse concept is relaxed, we can broaden the search
spectrum, and things become affordable. But the question is:
how affordable? Can we list the entire possible solution space?
This is the question our paper wants to answer.

Contributions

We discuss below the contributions that our work makes.

GI based solvers for CWP and SWP.

Our first contribution is to propose an algebraic formalism based on the
GI of a matrix to address both CWP and SWP. This formalism
allows us to have a unified vision of the two problems, and it provides the
main tools for understanding all the algorithmic improvements for solving
CWP and SWP.

We begin by a careful inspection of the solutions of a linear system of
equations
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with
\bm{b}\not=\bm{0}
, and prove that all its solutions
can be obtained only by GIs. More precisely, we show that

\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}\}=\{\bm{X}\bm{b}\;|\;\bm{X}\text{ is a GI of }\bm{A}\}.

This characterization allows us to attack CWP in a very direct way:
sample GIs (by means of some strategy) until a solution with the desired
Hamming weight is reached.
For example, one could fix a transformation
(\bm{P},\bm{Q})\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}({\mathbb{F}})\times\mathrm{S}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

with
\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}&\bm{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}
for some

\bm{V}
, and then search solutions of the form
\bm{X}\bm{b}
, where

\bm{X}\in\left\{\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Z}\\
\bm{I}_{r}-\bm{V}\bm{Z}\end{pmatrix}\bm{P}\mid\bm{Z}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-m,m}({\mathbb{F}})\right\}.

This method covers the whole space of solutions of the system
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
.

For SWP, we prove first that the null space of
\bm{A}
can be
characterized by

\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{0}\}=\{(\bm{Y}-\bm{X})\bm{b}\;|\;\bm{Y}\text{ is a GI of }\bm{A}\},

where
\bm{X}
(
\bm{b}
, resp.) is an arbitrary but fixed GI of
\bm{A}
(non-zero vector, resp.).
Thus, this characterization allows us to design a generic algorithm for SWP as
the one above: fix first a GI
\bm{X}
of
\bm{A}
and a non-zero vector
\bm{b}
, and
then sample GIs
\bm{Y}
of
\bm{A}
until a solution with the desired Hamming
weight is reached. The sampling of GIs is with respect to some strategy.
For instance, if we decompose
\bm{A}
into

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}&\bm{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}
, then the null
space of
\bm{A}
is

\left\{\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Z}\\
-\bm{V}\bm{Z}\end{pmatrix}\bm{P}\bm{b}\bigm|\bm{Z}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-m,m}({\mathbb{F}})\right\}.

So, the sampling can be on arbitrary matrices
\bm{Z}
.

We will use the terminology
GI based Decoding
(GID)
to refer to any of the GI-based techniques presented above, and

GID solver
for any algorithm that falls under it.

Information set decoding versus GID.

Our GID technique works as a common denominator for many existing
information set decoding techniques, such as Prange, Lee-Brickell, Leon,
Stern, Finiasz-Sendrier (and probably all). It explains the essence of
all these methods in a very clear and unified way.
For instance, we show in the paper that Prange’s algorithm computes
particular GIs until it finds the desired solution, without
covering the entire space of solutions.
More exactly, given
\bm{H}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-k,n}({\mathbb{F}})
a parity-check matrix of
a linear code and a syndrome
\bm{s}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n-k}
, Prange’s algorithm generates
solutions to the equation
\bm{H}\bm{x}=\bm{s}
of the form
\bm{X}\bm{s}
, where

\bm{X}\in\left\{\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\bm{I}_{r}\end{pmatrix}\bm{P}\mid(\bm{P},\bm{Q})\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}({\mathbb{F}})\times\mathrm{S}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}),\ (\exists\bm{V}:\,\bm{P}\bm{H}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}&\bm{I}_{r}\end{pmatrix})\right\}.

However, as we prove in the paper,
\bm{P}
must be in
\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})

to cover the entire space of solutions. The same holds for the other
ISD techniques discussed in paper, and probably for all techniques
that share Prange’s idea.

In terms of GID, each ISD technique is just a strategy to search a
partial subspace of the space of solutions or of the null space. This
view allows us to easily convert each such ISD technique into one
working for SDP or LWP.

A tight reduction to MIN-SAT.

Both CWP and SWP can be viewed as decision problems associated to two
optimization problems, namely the
minimum coset weight problem

(MIN-CWP) and the
minimum subset weight problem
(MIN-SWP).
Our GI-based approach allows to tightly reduce these optimization
problems (MIN-CWP and MIN-SWP) to the well-known MIN-SAT problem, when

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
.
As the reduction is very tight, we expect many techniques working for MIN-SAT
to apply to the two problems.

Reaching easy weights by means of GID.

Our simulations have shown that for small length codes, GID solvers
behave very similar to ISD decoders in terms of performance.
We have also noticed through simulations that there is an interval of
Hamming weights where the GID solvers manage to efficiently find solutions
for CWP and SWP. For example
when
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
the interval is symmetric and centered in
n/2
(see
[
DAST2019
]
).
Our simulations suggest that it is rather easy in general to find solutions
within this range. However, we know that hard instances exist even for this
interval (see for example
[
Vard1997
]
).
Our simulations show that for codes of length up to
n=3000
, with just
one
(\bm{P},\bm{Q})
decomposition, we have reached solutions with Hamming weights in the
range
[r\frac{q-1}{q}-\sqrt{n},r\frac{q-1}{q}+n-r+\sqrt{n}]
in only a few
seconds on an ordinary laptop computer.

Preliminaries

We fix the basic notation on linear algebra and coding

[

,

,

,

]
).

Generic fields are denoted by
{\mathbb{F}}
. When we want to emphasize

.

stands for the
n

over

, and written in column form.
The

, where

,

.
The cardinality of

.
We shall simply denote this as

.
If

is a non-empty subset of

,

stands

to

, that is, the vector

(the cardinality of

) that is obtained

by removing all entries on positions outside

.
The operator “

The set of
m\times n
matrices with elements in
{\mathbb{F}}
is denoted

{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
. Matrices will be denoted by uppercase bold
letters, such as
\bm{A}
.
\bm{A}(i,j)
denotes the element of
\bm{A}
at
the intersection of row
i
and column
j
.

\bm{I}_{r}
stands for the identity matrix of size

, and

\bm{I}_{m,n,r}
is
\bm{I}_{r}
extended with zeroes to an

matrix, i.e.,

\bm{I}_{m,n,r}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}
.

\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})
denotes the general linear group of order
m

over
{\mathbb{F}}
(i.e., the group of all invertible matrices

\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,m}({\mathbb{F}})
). Its subgroup consisting of

.

\pi_{\bm{Q}}
stands for the permutation induced by

\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{S}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
, and

through the permutation

.

As usual,

(

,

)

.

) of

is

.
Any matrix

whose rows form a basis

.

is a generator matrix

for the dual code

.

3
The GI of a matrix over arbitrary fields

The GI of a matrix has been much studied over the
fields of real and complex numbers. Not all the results valid in this
context remain valid when moving to an arbitrary field, especially
to finite fields. Consequently, in this section, we will recall some
results that are valid for matrices over arbitrary fields, and when
needed, we will specialize them to finite fields.
We will mainly follow
[
BeGr2006
,
Pear1968
,
Fult1978
]
, but we draw
attention to the fact that some results will be presented in our own
approach, which we consider appropriate for the case of finite fields.

3.1
Definitions and existence

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be an arbitrary field and
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})

be a matrix. A GI of
\bm{A}
is any matrix

\bm{X}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,m}({\mathbb{F}})
that fulfills

\bm{A}\bm{X}\bm{A}=\bm{A}.

(1)

There are specialized cases of GI, but we do not
mention them here because they are not used in our paper.

From the definition one can easily see that
\bm{A}^{-1}
is the only
GI of
\bm{A}
when
\bm{A}
is non-singular.
That is, in such a case, the GI exists and is unique.
Before moving on to the analysis of the existence of the generalized
inverse in the general case, let us analyze in more detail its
definition and the connection with solving systems of linear
equations. Let
\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
stand for the set of GIs
of
\bm{A}
.

Theorem 1

(
[
BeGr2006
]
)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
\bm{X}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,m}({\mathbb{F}})
. Then,

\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})\ \ {\Leftrightarrow}\ \ (\forall\bm{b}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{A}))(\bm{X}\bm{b}\text{ is a solution to }\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}).

Theorem 2

(
[
BeGr2006
]
)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
\bm{b}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{A})
, and
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
.
Then,
\bm{x}
is a solution to
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
if and only if

\bm{x}=\bm{X}\bm{b}+(\bm{I}-\bm{X}\bm{A})\bm{c}
, for some
\bm{c}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
.

It is also well known that
{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{I}-\bm{X}\bm{A})={\mathcal{N}}(\bm{A})

(see, for instance,
[
BeGr2006
,
Rohd2003
]
). Therefore,
by Theorem
2
,
\bm{X}\bm{b}
is a solution to the system
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}

(when it is consistent) and any other solution can be obtained
by adding arbitrary elements from the null space of
\bm{A}
to
\bm{X}\bm{b}
.

GIs exist for all matrices over arbitrary fields

[
Pear1968
,
Fult1978
]
. A first step in showing this is based on the
following theorem.

Theorem 3

(
[
BeGr2006
]
)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
\bm{P}\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})
,
and
\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
. Then, the function

f:\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A}){\rightarrow}\mathcal{GI}(\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q})
given by
f(\bm{X})=\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{X}\bm{P}^{-1}
,
for any
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
, is a bijection.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
f
is well-defined and
one-to-one. It remains to prove that any GI
\bm{Y}

of
\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}
is of the form
\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{X}\bm{P}^{-1}
, for some

\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
.

If
\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q})
, then
\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}\bm{Y}\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}
,
which is equivalent to
\bm{A}\bm{Q}\bm{Y}\bm{P}\bm{A}=\bm{A}
, since
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}

are non-singular matrices. However, this shows that

\bm{X}=\bm{Q}\bm{Y}\bm{P}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
and
\bm{Y}=\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{X}\bm{P}^{-1}
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\bm{A},\bm{B}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
. If
\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\bm{B}
for some
matrices
\bm{P}\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})
and
\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
then:

1.

\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})=\{\bm{Q}\bm{X}\bm{P}\mid\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{B})\}
;

2.

|\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})|=|\mathcal{GI}(\bm{B})|
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
3
to
\bm{B}
and
\bm{A}=\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{B}\bm{Q}^{-1}
.
∎

3.2
Computing GIs

To facilitate expression, a pair
(\bm{P},\bm{Q})
of matrices as in
Corollary
1
will often be called a
transformation
of

\bm{A}
. The first part of this corollary shows that the set

\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
does not depend on the transformation we apply to
\bm{A}
.
As a result, it suggests the following method for computing
GIs of
\bm{A}
:

1.

Transform the matrix
\bm{A}
through elementary operator
matrices
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
into a matrix
\bm{B}=\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}
for which one
can easily compute GIs;

2.

For each GI
\bm{X}
of
\bm{B}
,
\bm{Q}\bm{X}\bm{P}
is a
GI of
\bm{A}
. Besides, all GIs of

\bm{A}
are obtained in this way.

As an example, one may use the canonical form of
\bm{A}
,

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\bm{I}_{m,n,r}

[
Gent2017
]
. Thus, computing generalized
inverses for
\bm{A}
is reduced to computing GIs for

\bm{I}_{m,n,r}
. We present below some general constructions that
also include this case.
Even if the results are trivial to prove, we prefer to present them
in the form of a proposition to highlight their usefulness further.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
\bm{X}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n,m}({\mathbb{F}})
be matrices.

1.

If
\bm{A}
and
\bm{X}
are divided into blocks of appropriate sizes,

\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}&\bm{A}_{2}\\
\bm{A}_{3}&\bm{A}_{4}\end{pmatrix}
and

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}&\bm{X}_{2}\\
\bm{X}_{3}&\bm{X}_{4}\end{pmatrix}
,
respectively, then
\bm{X}
is a GI of
\bm{A}
if and
only if the following matrix equations are fulfilled:

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3})\bm{A}_{1}+(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{4})\bm{A}_{3}=\bm{A}_{1}\\
(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3})\bm{A}_{2}+(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{4})\bm{A}_{4}=\bm{A}_{2}\\
(\bm{A}_{3}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{3})\bm{A}_{1}+(\bm{A}_{3}\bm{X}_{2}+\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{4})\bm{A}_{3}=\bm{A}_{3}\\
(\bm{A}_{3}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{3})\bm{A}_{2}+(\bm{A}_{3}\bm{X}_{2}+\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{4})\bm{A}_{4}=\bm{A}_{4}\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

2.

If
\bm{A}
and
\bm{X}
are divided into blocks of appropriate sizes,

\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}&\bm{A}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
and

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}\\
\bm{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
, respectively,
then
\bm{X}
is a GI of
\bm{A}
if and
only if the following matrix equations are fulfilled:

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{2})\bm{A}_{1}=\bm{A}_{1}\\
(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{2})\bm{A}_{2}=\bm{A}_{2}\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

3.

If
\bm{A}
and
\bm{X}
are divided into blocks of appropriate sizes,

\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}\\
\bm{A}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
and

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}&\bm{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
, respectively,
then
\bm{X}
is a GI of
\bm{A}
if and
only if the following matrix equations are fulfilled:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{A}_{1}(\bm{X}_{1}\bm{A}_{1}+\bm{X}_{2}\bm{A}_{2})=\bm{A}_{1}\\
\bm{A}_{2}(\bm{X}_{1}\bm{A}_{1}+\bm{X}_{2}\bm{A}_{2})=\bm{A}_{2}\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

Proof.

Directly from (
1
).
∎

Example 1
.

We present below some cases of application of Proposition
1
.

1.

If
\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}
,
then the GIs of
\bm{A}
have the form

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{X}_{2}\\
\bm{X}_{3}&\bm{X}_{4}\end{pmatrix}
, where

\bm{X}_{2}
,
\bm{X}_{3}
, and
\bm{X}_{4}
are arbitrary matrices (of appropriate sizes)
over
{\mathbb{F}}
.
When
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
we have
|\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})|=q^{mn-r^{2}}
(see
[
Fult1978
]
);

2.

If
\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{A}_{2}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}
,
then the GIs of
\bm{A}
have the form

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}&\bm{X}_{2}\\
\bm{X}_{3}&\bm{X}_{4}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
\bm{X}_{1}
,
\bm{X}_{2}
,
\bm{X}_{3}
, and
\bm{X}_{4}
are arbitrary matrices
(of appropriate sizes) over
{\mathbb{F}}
that satisfy
\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3}=\bm{I}_{r}
.

3.

If
\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}&\bm{I}_{r}\end{pmatrix}
,
then the GIs of
\bm{A}
have the form

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}\\
\bm{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
\bm{X}_{1}
and
\bm{X}_{2}
are arbitrary matrices
(of appropriate sizes) over
{\mathbb{F}}
that satisfy
\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{X}_{2}=\bm{I}_{r}
.

4.

If
\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}
,
then the GIs of
\bm{A}
have the form

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}\\
\bm{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
\bm{X}_{2}
is a matrix (of appropriate size) over
{\mathbb{F}}
.

5.

If
\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}\\
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}
,
then the GIs of
\bm{A}
have the form

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
, where

\bm{X}_{2}
is a matrix (of appropriate size) over
{\mathbb{F}}
.

Even if the set of GIs of a matrix
\bm{A}
is the
same regardless of the transformation applied to the matrix,
the equations that define them may be different.
For instance, a matrix of rank
r
can be transformed into the
form in Example
1
(1) and in the form in
Example
1
(2).
The equations that define the GI will be different,
even if, in the end, we get the same set of GIs.
But, as will be seen later, these equations will more or less
facilitate working with GIs (enumeration, processing,
convergence to solution).
Due to this, given a transformation

(\bm{P},\bm{Q})
for
\bm{A}
, we will denote by
\mathcal{GI}_{\bm{P},\bm{Q}}(\bm{A})

the set of GIs of
\bm{A}
obtained from the
GIs of
\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}
. It is clear that

\mathcal{GI}_{\bm{P},\bm{Q}}(\bm{A})=\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
.

4
The coset and subspace weight problems and the GI

This section will discuss possible applications of the generalized
inverse in solving two closely related hard problems in coding theory.
The first of them is the
coset weight
problem, and the
second is the
subspace weight
problem, a subproblem of the
first one.

4.1
Generic GID solver for the coset weight problem

The coset weight problem is as follows.

Coset Weight Problem (CWP)

Instance:

\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m}
,
and positive integer
t
, where
{\mathbb{F}}
is a finite field;

Question:

Is there any solution
\bm{x}_{0}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
to
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
such
that
\left|\bm{x}_{0}\right|\leq t
?

In coding theory, CWP occurs in the context of

syndrome decoding
, where
\bm{A}
is a full rank matrix of size

m\times n
with
m<n
and
\bm{b}
is a syndrome.
For this reason, it is also called the

syndrome decoding
problem (more details about it are provided
in Section
).

CWP is NP-complete when
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}

[
BeET1978
]
.
However, both highlighting easy instances and constructing probabilistic
polynomial-time algorithms to solve this problem can be of major
importance when the problem is used to design secure cryptographic
primitives.

In this section, we will analyze CWP through the generalized
inverses of the matrix
\bm{A}
. We will present the results, as much as
possible, for the case of a general finite field
{\mathbb{F}}
. But, where
necessary, we will restrict the analysis to
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

The main strategy is the following. Given a CWP instance
(\bm{A},\bm{b},t)
,
we will compute GIs
\bm{X}
of the matrix
\bm{A}
and
check the solution’s weight. In fact, once a GI
\bm{X}

is computed, we have two approaches we can follow:

1.

Consider only solutions
\bm{x}=\bm{X}\bm{b}
;

2.

Consider solutions
\bm{x}=\bm{X}\bm{b}+(\bm{I}-\bm{X}\bm{A})\bm{c}
, where

\bm{c}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
.

However, we will show that any solution to the system

\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
can be expressed in the form
\bm{X}\bm{b}
, where

\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with full row rank and
\bm{b}\in{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{A})

with
\bm{b}\not=0
.
Then,

\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}\}=\{\bm{X}\bm{b}\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})\}.

Proof.

The inclusion “
\supseteq
” follows simply from the fact that

\bm{X}\bm{b}
is a solution to
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
, for any
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
.
Showing that the two sets have the same number of elements will
end the proof.

It is straightforward to verify that

\left|\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}\}\right|=q^{n-m}
,
since
\bm{A}
has full rank.

Let

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}&\bm{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}
.
be a transformation of
\bm{A}
.
By Corollary
1
, the sets
\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
and
\mathcal{GI}(\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q})

are isomorphic and hence we can restrict to evaluate

\left|\{\bm{X}\bm{b}\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{GI}(\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q})\}\right|
.
Any GI of
\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}
has the form

\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}\\
\bm{I}_{m}-\bm{V}\bm{X}_{1}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
\bm{X}_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-m,m}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
(Example
1
(3)).
As
\bm{b}\not=\bm{0}
,
\bm{X}_{1}\bm{b}
can take any value in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n-m}
.
So, the number of solution

\bm{X}\bm{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}\bm{b}\\
(\bm{I}_{m}-\bm{V}\bm{X}_{1})\bm{b}\end{pmatrix}

is exactly
q^{n-m}
. This shows that the two sets have the same number
of elements.
∎

Hence, a generic GID solver for CWP samples

\bm{X}\leftarrow\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A})
until
\left|\bm{X}\bm{b}\right|\leq t
.
The main problem we get is how to do the sampling.
Our approach is to apply transformations to
\bm{A}
to easily calculate
GIs of the transformed matrix and to transfer the
result to
\bm{A}
.

Theorem
3
tells us that a single transformation of

\bm{A}
suffices to generate all its GIs.
Our simulations and previous results on existing ISD algorithms show
that it is more efficient to use different transformations for

\bm{A}
and run through several GIs for each transformation.
According to this, we present below a generic GID solver for CWP.

Algorithm 1

GID solver for CWP

1:

function

CWGI_solve
(
\bm{A},\bm{b},t
)

2:

repeat

3:

Choose a transformation
(\bm{P},\bm{Q})
of
\bm{A}
;

4:

\bm{X}\leftarrow\mathcal{GI}_{\bm{P},\bm{Q}}(\bm{A})
until
\left|\bm{X}\bm{b}\right|\leq t

or no more sampling is allowed;

5:

until
a solution
\bm{X}\bm{b}
is found or no more transformation is
allowed;

6:

return
solution
\bm{X}\bm{b}
or “fail”.

It should be understood that steps 3 and 4 are performed under
various strategies, each leading to a variant of this generic algorithm.
This will be clear in Section
when we discuss Prange,
Lee-Brickell, Leon, Stern, and Finiasz-Sendrier’s algorithms.

In the following, we will analyze some transformations that can
be applied to the matrix
\bm{A}
, focusing on the following three
aspects:

1.

The general form of a GI
\bm{X}
of
\bm{A}
;

2.

Particularities of the solution
\bm{X}\bm{b}
;

3.

Algorithmic issues.

4.1.1
A general rank case

Our first result here, regarding the form of the GI,
follows directly from Corollary
1
and Proposition
1
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
\bm{P}\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})
,
\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
and
r>0
be an integer such that
r\leq\text{\rm rank}(\bm{A})\leq min\{m,n\}
and

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{A}_{2}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{A}_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Then, any GI
\bm{X}
of
\bm{A}
is of the form

\bm{X}=\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}&\bm{X}_{2}\\
\bm{X}_{3}&\bm{X}_{4}\end{pmatrix}\bm{P},

(6)

where
\bm{X}_{1}
,
\bm{X}_{2}
,
\bm{X}_{3}
, and
\bm{X}_{4}
are matrices of appropriate
sizes that verify the matrix equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3}&=\bm{I}_{r}\\
\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{3}&=\bm{0}\\
(\bm{X}_{2}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{4})\bm{A}_{4}&=\bm{0}\\
\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{4}\bm{A}_{4}&=\bm{A}_{4}\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

Remark 1
.

The transformation (
5
) can always be obtained by
Gaussian elimination. If no particular constraints are imposed on
the matrices
\bm{A}_{2}
and
\bm{A}_{4}
,
\bm{Q}
can be obtained as a
permutation matrix.
It is also interesting to remark that
\bm{X}_{4}
in (
6
)
must be a GI of
\bm{A}_{4}
(see (
7
)).

Lemma 1
.

With the above notation, if the system
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
is consistent, then

\bm{X}\bm{b}=\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime}+\bm{X}_{2}\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime\prime}\\
\bm{X}_{3}\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime}+\bm{X}_{4}\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},

(8)

where
\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime}=(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[1,r]}
and
\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime\prime}=(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[r+1,m]}
.

Proof.

Directly from (
6
).
∎

Equation (
8
) gives us some flexibility in choosing the
matrix
\bm{X}
’s blocks to minimize the solution
\bm{X}\bm{b}
’s weight.
The following procedure could be used:

1.

Sample
\bm{X}_{4}
from
\mathcal{GI}(\bm{A}_{4})
;

2.

Compute
\bm{X}_{2}
by
\bm{X}_{2}=-\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{4}
;

3.

Generate a matrix
\bm{X}_{3}
such that
\bm{A}_{4}\bm{X}_{3}=\bm{0}
;

4.

Compute
\bm{X}_{1}
by
\bm{X}_{1}=\bm{I}_{r}-\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3}
.

The procedure is repeated as long as
\left|\bm{X}\bm{b}\right|
is greater than
some given threshold
t
.
The weight of
\bm{X}\bm{b}
depends on
\bm{P}
,
\bm{Q}
, and the
matrix
\bm{X}
’s blocks. If
\bm{Q}
is a permutation, then it can be
neglected in choosing the matrix
\bm{X}
’s blocks because it does
not change the weight.

Remark 2
.

The above analysis is kept, with minor modifications, also for the
case where
\bm{I}_{r}
occupies another position in (
5
).
For example, if

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}&\bm{I}_{r}\\
\bm{A}_{3}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}

(9)

then the blocks of the GI
\bm{X}
must verify the
matrix equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{X}_{3}&=\bm{I}_{r}\\
\bm{A}_{3}\bm{X}_{1}&=\bm{0}\\
(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}+\bm{X}_{4})\bm{A}_{3}&=\bm{0}\\
\bm{A}_{3}\bm{X}_{2}\bm{A}_{3}&=\bm{A}_{3}\end{aligned}\right.

(10)

In this case,
\bm{X}_{2}
is a GI of
\bm{A}_{3}
, and
Lemma
1
holds true.

4.1.2
Rank deficient matrices

This is a sub-case of the previous case, which deals with rank
deficient matrices. Our first result follows directly from
Corollary
1
and Proposition
1
.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
,
\bm{P}\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})
, and
\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})

such that
rank(\bm{A})=r<min\{m,n\}
and

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{A}_{2}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Then, any GI
\bm{X}
of
\bm{A}
is of the form

\bm{X}=\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}&\bm{X}_{2}\\
\bm{X}_{3}&\bm{X}_{4}\end{pmatrix}\bm{P},

(12)

where
\bm{X}_{1}
,
\bm{X}_{2}
,
\bm{X}_{3}
, and
\bm{X}_{4}
are matrices of appropriate
sizes that verify the matrix equation

\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3}=\bm{I}_{r}.

(13)

Remark 3
.

The transformation (
11
) can always be obtained by
Gaussian elimination. If no requirement is imposed on the matrix

\bm{A}_{2}
,
\bm{Q}
can be obtained as a permutation matrix.

Lemma 2
.

With the above notation, if the system
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
is consistent, then

\bm{X}\bm{b}=\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime}\\
\bm{X}_{3}\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

(14)

where
\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime}=(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[1,r]}
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
1
, we only have to prove that

\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime\prime}=\bm{0}
, where
\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime\prime}=(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[r+1,m]}
.

Let
\bm{x}_{0}
be an arbitrary but fixed solution to
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
. That is,

\bm{A}\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{b}
. Then,

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\bm{P}\bm{b}&=&\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{x}_{0}\\[2.15277pt]
&=&\bm{P}\bm{P}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{A}_{2}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{x}_{0}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\prime}+\bm{A}_{2}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}\\
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix},\end{array}

where
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\prime}=(\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{x}_{0})_{[1,r]}
and

\tilde{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}=(\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{x}_{0})_{[r+1,n]}
. Therefore,
\bar{\bm{b}}^{\prime\prime}=\bm{0}
.
∎

When
\bm{Q}
is a permutation, Lemma
2
allows us to obtain
solutions to
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with any desired weight distribution
on
\pi_{\bm{Q}}([r+1,n])
, as the next theorem shows.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\bm{A}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m,n}({\mathbb{F}})
with
\text{\rm rank}(\bm{A})=r<\min\{m,n\}
,
\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m}
,

\bm{P}\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\mathbb{F}})
, and
\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{S}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
such that

(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[1,r]}\not=\bm{0}
and

\bm{P}\bm{A}\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{A}_{2}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}

(15)

Then, for any set
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\pi_{\bm{Q}}([r+1,n])
, a solution
\bm{x}
to

\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with the property

\mathrm{Supp}(\bm{x})|_{\pi_{\bm{Q}}([r+1,n])}={\mathcal{I}}
, can efficiently be computed.

Proof.

Let
\bm{A}
,
\bm{b}
,
\bm{P}
, and
\bm{Q}
as in the theorem’s hypothesis.
Let
\bm{X}
be a GI of
\bm{A}
.
Then, Lemma
2
leads to

\bm{X}\bm{b}=\bm{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}_{1}(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[1,r]}\\
\bm{X}_{3}(\bm{P}\bm{b})_{[1,r]}\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{X}_{1}
and
\bm{X}_{3}
verify the matrix equation

\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3}=\bm{I}_{r}
.

Now, let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a subset of
\pi_{\bm{Q}}([r+1,n])
,
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
, and

1\leq j\leq r
be such that
(\bm{P}\bm{b})(j)\not=0
.
Then, there exists
r+1\leq k\leq n
such that
\pi_{\bm{Q}}(k)=i
.
Moreover,

(\bm{X}\bm{b})(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}{\bm{X}_{3}}(k-r,j)(\bm{P}\bm{b})(j).

We choose now the block
\bm{X}_{3}
by

{\bm{X}_{3}}(k-r,\ell)=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if $\pi_{\bm{Q}}(k)=i\in{\mathcal{I}}$ and $\ell=j$}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
r+1\leq k\leq n
and
1\leq\ell\leq r
.
Then,
\bm{X}_{1}=\bm{I}_{r}-\bm{A}_{2}\bm{X}_{3}
.

It is straightforward to check that
\bm{x}=\bm{X}\bm{b}
verifies

\mathrm{Supp}(\bm{x})|_{\pi_{\bm{Q}}([r+1,n])}={\mathcal{I}}
.
Moreover,
\bm{x}
can be computed in polynomial time in the size of
\bm{A}
.
∎
