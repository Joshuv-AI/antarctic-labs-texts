The Precession Caused by Gravitational Waves
Ali Seraj, Blagoje Oblak

Abstract
We show that gravitational waves cause freely falling gyroscopes to precess relative to fixed distant stars, extending the stationary Lense-Thirring effect. The precession rate decays as the square of the inverse distance to the source, and is proportional to a suitable Noether current for dual asymptotic symmetries at null infinity. Integrating the rate over time yields a net rotation—a ‘gyroscopic memory’—whose angle reproduces the known spin memory effect but also contains an extra contribution due to the generator of gravitational electric-magnetic duality. The angle’s order of magnitude for the first LIGO signal is estimated to be Φ ∼ 10 − 35 \Phi\sim 10^{-35} arcseconds near Earth, but the effect may be substantially larger for supermassive black hole mergers.



I
Introduction

Consider an observer floating freely in outer space, carrying a spinning gyroscope. The observer looks at fixed distant stars in order to measure the gyroscope’s orientation. When a localized source emits a burst of gravitational waves that crosses the observer’s path, the gyroscope precesses and eventually settles in a new orientation (Fig.
1
); the corresponding rotation angle carries a ‘memory’ of the wave profile. The goal of this letter is to describe this precession and the ensuing memory effect.

II.1
Bondi coordinates and metric

Choose an origin in space and label the points of the four-dimensional space-time manifold by
retarded Bondi coordinates
: an areal distance
r
, a retarded time
u
, and angular coordinates
\theta^{a}
(
a=1,2
) on a unit celestial sphere with metric
q_{ab}(\theta)\,\text{d}\theta^{a}\text{d}\theta^{b}
(see Fig.
1

future null infinity

i.e.
the region
r\to\infty
with finite

r

1/r

Flanagan:2015pxa

\begin{split}\text{d}s^{2}\sim&-\big(1-\tfrac{2m}{r}-\tfrac{2F}{r^{2}}\big)\text{d}u^{2}-2\big(1-\tfrac{C^{2}}{16r^{2}}\big)\text{d}u\,\text{d}r\\
&+\big(r^{2}q_{ab}+rC_{ab}+\tfrac{1}{4}q_{ab}C^{2}\big)\text{d}\theta^{a}\,\text{d}\theta^{b}\\
&+2\big(\tfrac{1}{2}D^{b}C_{ab}+\tfrac{1}{r}\left[\tfrac{2}{3}L_{a}-\tfrac{1}{16}\partial_{a}(C^{2})\right]\big)\text{d}u\,\text{d}\theta^{a},\end{split}

(1)

where the functions
m
,
F
,
C_{ab}
and
L_{a}
only depend on
(u,\theta)

C^{2}\equiv C_{ab}C^{ab}
to reduce clutter, with indices raised and lowered thanks to the metric
q_{ab}
;
D_{a}
is the covariant derivative on
S^{2}

\text{d}s^{2}=-\text{d}u^{2}-2\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}r+r^{2}q_{ab}\,\text{d}\theta^{a}\,\text{d}\theta^{b}
in the limit
r\to\infty

The most important quantity in (
1

asymptotic shear
tensor
C_{ab}(u,\theta)
, which contains all the information about radiation. Its time-dependence is unconstrained and determines the
news tensor

N_{ab}\equiv\partial_{u}C_{ab}
that will play a key role below. The function
m(u,\theta)
is the
Bondi mass aspect
and
L_{a}(u,\theta)
is the
angular momentum aspect
, respectively measuring ‘densities’ of energy and angular momentum at null infinity. Their time-dependence is fixed by the shear and news tensors through so-called
balance equations

Flanagan:2015pxa

\displaystyle\dot{m}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}D_{a}D_{b}N^{ab}-\tfrac{1}{8}N_{ab}N^{ab},

(2a)

\displaystyle\dot{L}_{a}

\displaystyle=D_{a}m+\tfrac{1}{2}D^{b}D_{[a}D^{c}C_{b]c}-\mathcal{J}_{a},

(2b)

where
\dot{X}\equiv\partial_{u}X
while
\mathcal{J}
is a local quadratic flux

\mathcal{J}_{a}\equiv{-}\tfrac{1}{4}D_{b}(N^{bc}C_{ac}){-}\tfrac{1}{2}D_{b}N^{bc}C_{ac}

(3)

that will turn out to affect orientation memory in section
III.2
. The remaining function
F
in (
1

F=-\tfrac{1}{32}C^{2}-\tfrac{1}{6}\left(D_{a}L^{a}\right)-\tfrac{1}{8}(D\cdot C)^{2}

The full metric (
1

Grant:2021hga

2

II.2
Tetrad carried by a freely falling observer

Consider a freely falling observer at large
r
in an asymptotically flat metric (
1
). We wish to build an orthonormal tetrad
\{{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\mu}}}|\mu=0,1,2,3\}
such that
{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{0}}}={\boldsymbol{u}}
is the observer’s proper velocity while
{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{i}}}
(
i=1,2,3
) are space-like vectors pointing towards fixed ‘distant stars’ at infinity. (Hatted indices label tetrad elements, and they are raised/lowered using the inertial Minkowski metric.) In practice, Bondi coordinates heavily rely on a choice of origin—the location of the source of radiation. Accordingly, we first build a ‘source-oriented’ tetrad
\{{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{\mu}}}\}
, then perform angle-dependent rotations so as to produce the desired frame
\{{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\mu}}}\}
, which we call ‘star-oriented’.

Our starting point is the observer’s proper velocity

{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{0}}}={\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{0}}}={\boldsymbol{u}}=\gamma(\partial_{u}+v^{r}\partial_{r}+v^{a}\partial_{a}),

(4)

i.e.
the zeroth element of both tetrads. In these terms, solving the geodesic equation for the metric (
1
) with the condition
{\boldsymbol{u}}\sim\partial_{u}
at large
r
(the observer is asymptotically at rest) yields

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=1+\tfrac{m_{0}}{r}+\tfrac{\gamma_{2}}{r^{2}},\quad\gamma_{2}\equiv\tfrac{1}{16}C^{2}+\smallint\!m

(5a)

\displaystyle v^{r}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{m-m_{0}}{r}+\tfrac{1}{r^{2}}\big(-\gamma_{2}-\tfrac{1}{6}D_{a}L^{a}-\tfrac{1}{8}(D\cdot C)^{2}\big),

(5b)

\displaystyle v^{a}

\displaystyle={-}\tfrac{1}{2r^{2}}D_{b}C^{ab}{+}\tfrac{1}{r^{3}}\big(D^{a}\gamma_{2}{-}\tfrac{2}{3}L^{a}{+}\tfrac{1}{2}C^{ab}D^{c}C_{bc}\big),

(5c)

with
m_{0}\equiv m(u_{0},\theta)
the initial Bondi mass aspect and
\int m\equiv\int_{u_{0}}^{u}du^{\prime}\,m
. Only spatial tetrad elements thus remain to be found. In the source-oriented case, one first obtains the radial vector
{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r}}}
by following an outgoing null radial geodesic, projecting out the
{\boldsymbol{u}}
component of its velocity and finally expanding in
1/r
, which yields
{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r}}}\sim\tfrac{1}{\gamma}(1+\tfrac{1}{r^{2}}C^{2}/16)\partial_{r}-{\boldsymbol{u}}
. The tetrad is then completed by picking an orthonormal dyad
{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;\!}}
on the unit sphere and expanding angular tetrad elements as

{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{a}}}\sim\tfrac{1}{r}{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;b}}(\delta^{a}_{b}-\tfrac{1}{2r}C^{a}_{b}+\tfrac{1}{16r^{2}}C^{2}\delta^{a}_{b})(\partial_{a}+\tfrac{1}{r}D_{a}\smallint\!m\,\partial_{r}).

(6)

This furnishes an explicit source-oriented Lorentz frame
\{{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{0}}},{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r}}},{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{a}}}\}
, written here perturbatively in
1/r
.

Obtaining a tetrad whose spatial vectors point towards fixed distant stars requires an extra step (see Fig.
3
), as the spatial vectors
\{{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r}}},{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{a}}}\}
need to be rotated in an angle-dependent manner. This is intuitively obvious: even in pure Minkowski space, the source-oriented tetrad of a freely falling observer must rotate continuously so that its vector
{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r}}}
points towards the origin. Any gyroscope with non-zero angular velocity trivially precesses relative to this tetrad, even without radiation. One may therefore compensate this effect by defining transformed tetrad vectors

{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{i}}}=R_{\hat{i}}{}^{\hat{j}}(\theta)\,{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{j}}},

(7)

where the local rotation matrix is a path-ordered exponential
R(\theta)=P\exp\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}\overline{\omega}
of the spin connection
\overline{\omega}
of the flat space triad
\{\partial_{r}-\partial_{u},\tfrac{1}{r}{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;\!}}\}
, with
{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;\!}}
the spherical dyad introduced above (
6
). Explicitly, this background spin connection has components
\overline{\omega}_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{r}}}=\zeta_{{\hat{a}}a}\text{d}\theta^{a}
and
\overline{\omega}_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}={\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;a}}D_{b}\zeta_{{\hat{b}}a}\text{d}\theta^{b}
. The observer thus uses flatness at infinity to adjust their frame by a rotation that is purely determined by fixed asymptotic structures
(q_{ab},{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;b}})
, regardless of the presence of bulk radiation.

The choice of path defining
R(\theta)
generally affects its value, but it is ultimately irrelevant: for our purposes, it suffices that
R(\theta_{0})=\mathbb{I}
be the identity at the observer’s initial angular position
\theta_{0}
,
i.e.
that the source-oriented and star-oriented tetrads initially coincide. As a result, the only relevant contribution of
R(\theta)
to the transformation law of the spin connection
\omega\to R\omega R^{-1}+R\text{d}R^{-1}
stems from the inhomogeneous term, which cancels as desired the uninteresting rotation due to the observer’s motion on a celestial sphere. A star-oriented tetrad has thus been defined, and one may finally use it to measure the precession of gyroscopes.

Precession and memory

This section presents our results: a formula for the gyroscopic precession rate in terms of shear and news tensors, and the resulting expression for net orientation memory (see eqs. (
11

15

III.1
Precession as dual mass

Any small freely falling gyroscope has a spin vector
\boldsymbol{S}
that is parallel-transported along its world line:
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\boldsymbol{S}=0
in terms of proper velocity
{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Now let
\{{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\mu}}}\}
be a tetrad at the gyroscope’s location such that
{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{0}}}={\boldsymbol{u}}
. Then the spin vector may be written as
\boldsymbol{S}=S^{{\hat{i}}}{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{i}}}
and parallel transport becomes equivalent to a precession equation

\frac{\text{d}S^{\hat{i}}(\tau)}{\text{d}\tau}=\Omega^{\hat{i}}_{\;\hat{j}}(\tau)\,S^{\hat{j}}(\tau),

(8)

where
\tau
is the observer’s proper time and the angular velocity (precession rate) matrix
\Omega^{\hat{i}}_{\;\hat{j}}(\tau)
is the projection along
{\boldsymbol{u}}
of the spin connection
\omega
of the tetrad
\{{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\mu}}}\}
:

\Omega^{\hat{i}\hat{j}}=-u^{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}{}^{\hat{i}\hat{j}},\quad\omega_{\mu}{}^{\hat{\mu}\hat{\nu}}\equiv f^{\hat{\mu}}{}_{\alpha}\nabla_{\mu}f^{\hat{\nu}\alpha}.

(9)

The gyroscope’s precession rate is thus wholly determined by the spin connection evaluated along the observer’s trajectory. In practice, it is simpler to compute the spin connection of the source-oriented tetrad
\{{\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{\mu}}}\}
defined in eqs. (
4
)–(
6
), then use the rotation (
7
) to obtain the spin connection of
{\boldsymbol{f}_{\hat{\mu}}}
. At leading order in
1/r
along the world line of the observer, this yields

\displaystyle\omega_{{\hat{r}}{\hat{a}}}

\displaystyle\sim{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;a}}\big(\tfrac{1}{4r^{2}}N_{ab}D_{c}C^{bc}\text{d}u{+}\tfrac{D^{b}C_{ab}}{2r^{2}}\text{d}r{+}\tfrac{N_{ab}}{2}\text{d}\theta^{b}\big),

(10a)

\displaystyle\omega_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}

\displaystyle\sim-\tfrac{1}{2r^{2}}{\zeta_{\hat{a}}^{\;\;a}}{\zeta_{\hat{b}}^{\;\;b}}\big(D_{[a}D^{c}C_{b]c}-\tfrac{1}{2}N_{c[a}C^{c}{}_{b]}\big)\text{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3})\text{d}r+\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})\text{d}\theta^{a}

(10b)

where
A_{[a}B_{b]}\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}(A_{a}B_{b}-A_{b}B_{a})
. Note that this only depends on the shear
C_{ab}
and the news
N_{ab}
, without any influence of mass or angular momentum aspects: the latter only appear in subleading terms that are neglected here. (This notably includes Lense-Thirring precession
(
Misner:1973prb

It is straightforward to obtain the angular velocity matrix (
9

4

\Omega_{{\hat{a}}\hat{r}}=\mathcal{O}(r^{-3})

\Omega_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}\sim\frac{\epsilon_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}}{r^{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\equiv\tfrac{1}{4}D_{a}D_{b}\widetilde{C}^{ab}-\tfrac{1}{8}N_{ab}\widetilde{C}^{ab}

(11)

where the

\widetilde{C}_{ab}\equiv\epsilon_{ca}C_{b}{}^{c}
in terms of the Levi-Civita tensor density on the unit

{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}
orthogonal to incoming radiation. Furthermore, the precession rate is proportional to a celestially local current

dual covariant mass aspect

;
Freidel:2021qpz
closely related to the symmetry of asymptotically flat gravity under so-called dual supertranslations

;
Godazgar:2018dvh
;
Kol:2019nkc
;
Godazgar:2019dkh
;
Godazgar:2020gqd
;
Godazgar:2020kqd
;
Oliveri:2020xls
;
Kol:2020ucd
. As announced in section

For future reference, it is useful to write tensors on celestial spheres in terms of (pseudo-)scalar functions with definite parity. Let therefore angular momentum and shear be written as

\displaystyle L_{a}

\displaystyle\equiv D_{a}L^{+}+\epsilon_{ab}D^{b}L^{-},

(12a)

\displaystyle C_{ab}

\displaystyle\equiv D_{(a}D_{b)}C^{+}-\tfrac{1}{2}q_{ab}D^{2}C^{+}+\epsilon_{c(a}D_{b)}D^{c}C^{-},

where

III.2
Orientation memory

One can readily write the solution of the precession equation (
8
) as a time-ordered exponential of the matrix
\Omega
acting on some initial spin
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{init}}
. In practice, only the first non-trivial term of this expansion is reliable, since the angular velocity (
11
) is of order
\mathcal{O}(1/r^{2})
anyway and higher-order terms of the exponential series are affected by subleading
1/r
corrections that have been neglected here. Accordingly, the leading-order change of orientation of the gyroscope’s axis is given by
\Delta S^{{\hat{r}}}=\mathcal{O}(r^{-3})
and
\Delta S^{{\hat{a}}}=\Phi\epsilon^{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}S^{{\hat{b}}}_{\text{init}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3})
, where the rotation angle in the
{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}
plane is obtained by integrating the covariant dual mass aspect (
13
) over time:

\Phi=\int\!\text{d}u\,\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}{r^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{r^{2}}\Big[\Delta L^{-}+\int\!\text{d}u\big(\mathcal{J}^{-}-\tfrac{1}{8}N_{ab}\widetilde{C}^{ab}\big)\Big].

(14)

The fact that
\Phi\neq 0
is the aforementioned memory effect: the passage of waves entails a permanent change of orientation, sensitive to a specific combination
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
of metric components. The latter can be rewritten more suggestively by ‘inverting’ the parity decomposition (
12
) under the assumption (without loss of generality) that
L^{\pm}
have vanishing average on
S^{2}
while
C^{\pm}
both have vanishing harmonics of order
\ell=0,1
. This rephrases the memory effect (
14
) as

\Phi=\frac{8\pi}{r^{2}}\Big(\Delta Q_{Y}+\mathcal{F}_{Y}-\tfrac{1}{64\pi}\int\text{d}u\,N_{ab}\widetilde{C}^{ab}\Big),

(15)

where all terms on the right-hand side are evaluated at
\theta
and we have introduced a divergence-free vector field
Y^{a}(\theta^{\prime})\equiv\epsilon^{ab}D_{b}G(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, while
Q_{Y}\equiv\tfrac{1}{8\pi}\oint\sqrt{q}\text{d}^{2}\theta^{\prime}\,Y^{a}L_{a}
is the associated super-angular momentum charge
Compere:2019gft
and
\mathcal{F}_{Y}\equiv\tfrac{1}{8\pi}\oint\sqrt{q}\text{d}^{2}\theta^{\prime}\,Y^{a}\mathcal{J}_{a}
is its flux. This makes it manifest that the first two terms of gyroscopic memory (namely
\Delta Q+\mathcal{F}
) reproduce the spin memory effect
Pasterski:2015tva
. Crucially, however, Eq. (
15
) involves an additional nonlinear term
\propto\int N_{ab}\widetilde{C}^{ab}
; the latter is nothing but the Hamiltonian generator of local electric-magnetic duality transformations on radiative phase space endowed with its standard symplectic form
Ashtekar:1981bq
. This exhibits once more the deep relation between gyroscopic memory and crucial gravitational symmetries.

The non-vanishing value of (
15
) also illustrates the general memory mechanism suggested in Fig.
2
. In the context of displacement memory
Strominger:2014pwa
, the ‘metric perturbation’ of Fig.
2
is the shear
C_{ab}
and the net change
\Delta C_{ab}
measures the angular deviation of nearby geodesics. Gyroscopic memory is more subtle in that respect, as the perturbation should now be seen as a time integral of dual shear through the dual covariant mass aspect of Eq. (
11
). The presence of such time-integrated metric perturbations is typical of subleading memory effects
Nichols:2017rqr
;
Nichols:2018qac
;
Seraj:2021qja
.

To conclude, let us estimate the magnitude of the memory effect (
15
): it falls of as
1/r^{2}
and is in this sense dominant with respect to Lense-Thirring precession, which falls off as
1/r^{3}
. Could it then be possible to observe the precession described here? The answer is unclear at the moment, as realistic values of (
15
) are tiny. Indeed, elementary dimensional analysis suggests that the order of magnitude of (
15
) for a bound binary system with mass scale
M
is

\Phi\sim\frac{G^{2}}{c^{4}}\frac{M^{2}}{r^{2}}\simeq 2\times 10^{-39}\Big(\frac{M/M_{\odot}}{r/1\,\text{Mpc}}\Big)^{2}

(16)

where
G
is Newton’s constant,
c
is the speed of light in vacuum and
M_{\odot}
is the solar mass. This is manifestly exceedingly weak for the common values of mass and distance (
M\simeq 30M_{\odot}
and
r\simeq 400\,\text{Mpc}
for the seminal LIGO observation
LIGOScientific:2016aoc
). It is nevertheless conceivable that the effect will some day be observable in extreme events such as supermassive black hole mergers, for which values of the order of
\Phi\simeq 10^{-26}\,\text{rad}
are not far-fetched. Also note that the effect is independent of the gyroscope’s spin and inertia, so one may even resort to distant pulsars (whose high mass and low volume reduce non-gravitational environmental effects) as radiation probes. Small rotations of a pulsar’s axis could then conceivably be measured: for instance, a
\Phi\sim 10^{-26}\,\text{rad}
change in the angle of an idealized narrow beam emitted
10^{3}
light-years away from the Solar system modifies the position of the resulting light spot on Earth by about
10^{-7}m
