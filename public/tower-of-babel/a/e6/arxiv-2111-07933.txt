Quantum algorithms for approximate function loading
Gabriel Marin-Sanchez, Javier Gonzalez-Conde, Mikel Sanz

Abstract
Loading classical data into quantum computers represents an essential stage in many relevant quantum algorithms, especially in the field of quantum machine learning. Therefore, the inefficiency of this loading process means a major bottleneck for the application of these algorithms. Here, we introduce two approximate quantum-state preparation methods for the NISQ era inspired by the Grover-Rudolph algorithm, which partially solve the problem of loading real functions. Indeed, by allowing for an infidelity ϵ \epsilon and under certain smoothness conditions, we prove that the complexity of the implementation of the Grover-Rudolph algorithm without ancillary qubits, first introduced by Möttönen et al , results into 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 k 0 ​ ( ϵ ) ) \mathcal{O}(2^{k_{0}(\epsilon)}) , with n n the number of qubits and k 0 ​ ( ϵ ) k_{0}(\epsilon) asymptotically independent of n n . This leads to a dramatic reduction in the number of required two-qubit gates. Aroused by this result, we also propose a variational algorithm capable of loading functions beyond the aforementioned smoothness conditions. Our variational Ansatz is explicitly tailored to the landscape of the function, leading to a quasi-optimized number of hyperparameters. This allows us to achieve high fidelity in the loaded state with high speed convergence for the studied examples.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{k^{*}}2^{k-1}+2\sum_{k=k^{*}+1}^{n}(80k-398)

\displaystyle=2^{k^{*}}-1+2(n-k^{*})(20k^{*}+20n-179),

(16)

see the Appendix A for further details in gates decomposition. Notice that this result is only valid for
k^{*}>6
, but since we are interested in the asymptotical behaviour of the protocol, it is not an issue. All in all, we obtain that the complexity of this process is exponentially dependent on
k^{*}
with an extra polynomial term.

On the other hand, if a singularity is found in
(0,1)
, we end up with multiple clusters of angles in each block. The reason behind this is that the clusters are formed with contiguous angles. Therefore, the reduction of gates is not significant, and the protocol can not be performed efficiently (polynomial). However, if the representatives of the disjointed clusters are equal, then we can create a single cluster, so the reduction is doable. In this sense, we have numerically observed that far from the singularities, all angles converge to a value of
\pi/2
, but we have not found an analytical proof yet.

Let us see an example of a function that meets the previous description and analyze the outcome of the protocol. Consider the function

f(x)=\frac{1}{N}e^{x^{3/2}}

(17)

in
[0,1]
, where
N
is the normalization factor, and a system of
n=10
qubits. The second derivative of its logarithm is

\displaystyle\log f(x)=x^{\frac{3}{2}}-\log N

\displaystyle\implies\partial_{x}\log f(x)=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}

(18)

\displaystyle\implies\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)=\frac{3}{4\sqrt{x}}.

(19)

Hence,
\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)
has a singularity at
x=0
.
First of all, since
\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)
is a monotonic decreasing function, its maximum in the interval
[2^{-k+1},1]
is found in
x=2^{-k+1}
, for any
k=~1,\dots,n
. Then, we can set
k_{\text{max}}=1
. Next, we need to compute the limit of
\left|\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)|_{x=2^{-k+1}}\right|2^{-2k+2}
:

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)|_{x=2^{-k+1}}\right|2^{-2k+2}=\frac{3}{4\sqrt{x}}|_{x=2^{-k+1}}2^{-2k+2}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{3}{4}2^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)}2^{-2k+2}=\frac{3}{4}2^{-\frac{3}{2}k+\frac{3}{2}}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0.

(20)

Therefore, the difference in the angles is bounded and we can select a
k^{*}\geq 1
for which the conditions of Th.
4
are satisfied in
[2^{-k^{*}+1},1]

\frac{\delta_{k}}{8}\eta\leq\pi,

(21)

with
\eta=\left|\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)|_{x=2^{-k+1}}\right|=\frac{3}{4}2^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{3}{32}2^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)}2^{-k+1}=\frac{3}{32}2^{\frac{1}{2}(1-k)}.

(22)

Since this term is decreasing, its maximum is found when
k=1
, with a value of
\frac{3}{32}<\pi
. Thus, this condition is met for any
k=1,\dots,n
, so we can select
k^{*}=1

with
\epsilon=0.01
and
\eta=\left|\partial^{2}_{x}\log f(x)|_{x=2^{-k^{*}+1}}\right|=0.75
using Eq. (

k_{0}=2
. Then, the last block to remain unclustered must be the maximum between
k_{0}
and
k^{*}
, which in this case is 2.

III.3
Analysis of resilience to noise in
NISQ
era

In this subsection, we present a theoretical and numerical analysis of how experimental errors affect different clustering levels in our first protocol and their impact on the final fidelity. The crucial point here is that when digital accuracy increases, the number of gates requested grows exponentially. As the introduction of these gates implies a growing experimental error in
NISQ
quantum processors, we observe a trade-off, see Fig.
6
, between the clusterization error and the experimental error, quite similar to the trade-off observed in digital quantum simulations between the number of Trotter steps and the experimental error in Refs.
Urtzi
;
Urtzi2
. This balance is crucial for algorithms in noisy quantum processors. Reproducing a similar reasoning as the one in the aforementioned reference and references thereof, we first propose an approximated model of how the experimental error combined with the clustering error of our algorithm scales as a function of the number of non-parallel two-qubit gates. Then, we perform some numerical simulations introducing multiple realistic noises in our algorithm to support our theoretical predictions.

IV
Variational Quantum Circuit

IV.1
Ansatz

Based on the previous protocol, we propose a variational Ansatz for loading functions beyond the conditions required by Th. 1. We consider
(z+s+1)p(k)
hyperparameters for each
k
-qubit block satisfying
k>k_{0}
, where
z
and
s
are respectively the number of zeros and singular points in the function,
p(k)
is polynomial number in
k
denoting the number of hyperparameters allowed per singular point/zero for the
k
-th block, and
k_{0}=\text{max}\{\ k\ |\ z+s+1\geq 2^{k}\}
. Assuming
p(k)\geq 1
, the minimum number of hyperparameters needed to capture the singular behavior of the function is
z+s+1
. Therefore, for each
k
-qubit block
F_{k}^{(k-1)}(\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\theta})
comprising more than
z+s+1
parameters, we cluster the angles which do not correspond to the position of zeros or singularities.
In this form, this proposal establishes an intuitive correlation between the number of hyperparameters
p(k)
and the expressability of the circuit to capture the details of the target functions in the most relevant points. By using the decomposition of the multi-controlled rotations
MIKKO1GATES
;
MIKKO3GATES
, the number of two-qubit gates which must be included in the variational circuit sums up to

\#\text{TQGs}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}2^{k-1}+(s+z)\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{n}p(k)(80k-398)

=2^{k_{0}}-1+r_{k_{0}+1}(n,s+z),

(28)

with
m
the number of zeros/singularities and
r_{k_{0}+1}(n,s+z)
growing polynomially with the system size
n
. A remarkable advantage of this Ansatz is the training procedure, since Grover-Rudolph algorithm provides a suitable set of initial training angles which considerably enhances the convergence of the protocol with respect to a random initialization. This fact, together with a scaling in the number of hyperparameters
p(k)
substantially slower than the system size, allow us to avoid the training procedure to get stuck into both local minima and barren plateaus. In Fig.
8
, we illustrate how to select the hyperparameters of the variational circuit for a general function containing multiple zeros and singular points.

IV.2
Training Process

We proceed to illustrate the training method for the variational circuit proposed in this article. This process consists of iterative steps in which a loss function that measures how far the outcome is from the desired state is recursively minimized to obtain the optimal parameters.

Consider a function
f
and
n
qubits. The desired quantum state is

\ket{\Psi(f)}_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}f_{l}\ket{l},

(29)

where the
f_{l}
terms are discrete approximations to the objective function, as presented in Def.
1
.
Now, given a set of angles
\bm{\theta}
, our Ansatz
U(\bm{\theta})
returns the state

\ket{\Psi\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}_{n}=U(\bm{\theta})\ket{0},

(30)

where
\ket{0}\equiv\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
, and the components of the obtained state are products of sines and cosines.

Let us now introduce the mean squared error loss function, defined as

\displaystyle L(n,f,\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(f_{l}-\Psi_{l}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(f_{l}^{2}+\Psi_{l}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{2}-2f_{l}\Psi_{l}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right),

(31)

where
\Psi_{l}\left(\bm{\theta}\right):=\innerproduct{l}{\Psi({\bm{\theta}})}_{n}
.

This process aims to find the optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}
for which
\ket{\Psi\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}_{n}
approximates
\ket{\Psi(f)}_{n}
, which is equivalent to minimizing the loss function. Here, we use the gradient descent method
gradient
to do so.

Then, given any angle
\theta_{l}^{(k-1)}
, with
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
l\in~\{0,2^{k-1}-1\}
, its value gets updated after each training step in the following way:

\theta_{l}^{(k-1)}=\theta_{l}^{(k-1)}-\gamma\frac{\partial L(n,f,\bm{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{l}^{(k-1)}},

(32)

where
\gamma
is the learning rate. Let us now compute the expression of the derivative:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(n,f,\bm{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{j}^{(k-1)}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{l=0}^{2^{n}-1}2\left(\Psi_{l}({\bm{\theta}})-f_{l}\right)\frac{\partial\Psi_{l}(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{j}^{(k-1)}}.

(33)

We can observe how this initialization reduces drastically the number of steps, as well as enables us to achieve the largest fidelities.

Appendix B: Theorem Proofs

B.1 Relation Between the Difference in the Angles and the Target Function

Theorem 2
.

Let
f
be a continuous function such that
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and consider a block comprising a uniformly controlled rotation of
k
qubits. Then, the difference between two contiguous angles is bounded by the second derivative of the logarithm of
f
in the following way:

\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{l+1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}\right|\leq\frac{\delta_{k}^{2}}{4}\max_{y^{\prime}\in[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\left|\left.\left(\partial^{2}_{y}\log f(y)\right)\right\rvert_{y=y^{\prime}}\right|,

(56)

where
\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}
,
l\in\{1,\dots,2^{k-1}-1\}
.

Proof.

The discrete expression of the angles for a block comprised of uniformly controlled rotation with
k
qubits, first introduced in
GROVER
, is given by

\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}=2\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{\int_{l\delta_{k}}^{(l+1/2)\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}{\int_{l\delta_{k}}^{(l+1)\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}\right),

(57)

with
\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}
and
l\in\{1,\dots,2^{k-1}-1\}
.
We can define the continuous extension of the previous function as

\theta^{(k-1)}(y)\vcentcolon=2\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}\right),

(58)

where the original expression can be recovered replacing
y=l\delta_{k}
.

Now, given a function
g
and a displacement
\mu
, we define the numerical derivative of
g
with respect to
\mu
as

\partial_{y}^{(\mu)}g(y)\vcentcolon=\frac{g(y+\mu)-g(y)}{\mu}.

(59)

Notice that for
\mu\rightarrow 0
,
\partial_{y}^{(\mu)}g(y)\rightarrow\partial_{y}g(y)
, the usual derivative. Then, for two consecutive angles, we have

\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{l+1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}\right|=\left|\left.\delta_{k}\left(\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k})}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)\right)\right\rvert_{y=l\delta_{k}}\right|.

(60)

Now, we require a connection between the difference in the angles and the partial derivative of Eq.(
58
). The Mean Value Theorem guarantees that the numerical derivative, which corresponds to the slope of the straight line connecting
\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}
and
\theta^{(k-1)}_{l+1}
, is constrained by the maximum absolute value of the exact derivative in that interval. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{l+1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}\right|\leq\delta_{k}\max_{y^{\prime}\in[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\left|\left.\left(\partial_{y}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)\right)\right\rvert_{y=y^{\prime}}\right|.

(61)

Let us now develop the term of the exact derivative of the function
\theta^{(k-1)}(y)
:

\partial_{y}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)

(62)

=\frac{\left(f(y+\delta_{k})-f(y)\right)\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx-\left(f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)\right)\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}.

By introducing
\left(f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y+\delta_{k}/2)\right)
in the first term and reorganizing the expression, we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt=\frac{\left(f(y+\delta_{k})-f(y+\delta_{k}/2)\right)\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\frac{\left(f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)\right)\left(\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx-\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\right)}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt=\frac{\left(f(y+\delta_{k})-f(y+\delta_{k}/2)\right)\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\frac{\left(f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)\right)\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt=\frac{\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\cdot\left(\frac{f(y+\delta_{k})-f(y+\delta_{k}/2)}{\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}-\frac{f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}\right),

(63)

where the first term is positive. Now, since
\left(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}\right)^{2}=a+b-2\sqrt{ab}\geq 0
, we have the well-known inequality for the geometric and arithmetic means
\sqrt{ab}\leq\frac{a+b}{2}
. Then, if we select
a=\frac{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}
and
b=\frac{\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}
, it leads to

\frac{\sqrt{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}

\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx+\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}=\frac{1}{2}.

(64)

With this result, we get the following inequality for the absolute value of the derivative of
\theta^{(k-1)}(y)
:

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{y}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{f(y+\delta_{k})-f(y+\delta_{k}/2)}{\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}-\frac{f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}\right|.

(65)

By using again the numerical derivative defined in Eq. (
59
), we can simplify the previous expression in the following way:

\displaystyle\frac{f(y+\delta_{k})-f(y+\delta_{k}/2)}{\int_{y+\delta_{k}/2}^{y+\delta_{k}}f(x)dx}-\underbrace{\frac{f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y))}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}}_{\vcentcolon=h(y)}

(66)

\displaystyle=h(y+\delta_{k}/2)-h(y)=\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}h(y).

(67)

Also, since
f
must be integrable, its primitive
F
exists. Then,

\displaystyle h(y)=\frac{f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)}{\int_{y}^{y+\delta_{k}/2}f(x)dx}=\frac{f(y+\delta_{k}/2)-f(y)}{F(y+\delta_{k}/2)-F(y)}=\frac{\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}f(y)}{\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}F(y)}.

(68)

Let us now plug it into the inequality

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{y}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\cdot\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}\left(\frac{\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}f(y)}{\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}F(y)}\right)\right|

=\frac{\delta_{k}}{4}\left|\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}\left(\frac{\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}f(y)}{\partial_{y}^{(\delta_{k}/2)}F(y)}\right)\right|.

(69)

If we follow the same argument as in Eq. (
61
), we have that the term of the numerical derivative is upper bounded by the exact derivative, in the absolute value, which corresponds to the second derivative of the function’s logarithm. Hence, the following inequality holds

\left|\partial_{y}\theta^{(k-1)}(y)\right|_{[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{4}\max_{y^{\prime}\in[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\left|\left.\left(\partial^{2}_{y}\log f(y)\right)\right\rvert_{y=y^{\prime}}\right|.

(70)

Finally, by plugging this result in Eq. (
61
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{l+1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}\right|\leq\max_{y^{\prime}\in[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\left|\frac{\delta_{k}^{2}}{4}\max_{y^{\prime}\in[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\left|\left.\left(\partial^{2}_{y}\log f(y)\right)\right\rvert_{y=y^{\prime}}\right|\right|

=\frac{\delta_{k}^{2}}{4}\max_{y^{\prime}\in[l\delta_{k},(l+1)\delta_{k}]}\left|\left.\left(\partial^{2}_{y}\log f(y)\right)\right\rvert_{y=y^{\prime}}\right|,

(71)

as we wanted to proof.

\blacksquare

, the result in Corollary
2
holds with the modification in the bound

\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{l+1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}\right|\leq\frac{\delta_{k}^{2}\cdot\eta}{4L^{2}},

(73)

where
L=x_{\text{max}}-x_{\text{min}}
and
\delta_{k}=\frac{L}{2^{k-1}}
.

Proof.

The change of variables that map the
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
with
x\in[x_{\text{max}}-x_{\text{min}}]
is given by

x=x_{\text{min}}+x^{\prime}L.

(74)

Now, by using the chain rule, we obtain:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial y^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y^{\prime}}{\partial y^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}L\implies\left|\partial^{2}_{y}\log f(y)\right|\leq\frac{\tilde{\eta}}{L^{2}}.

(75)

\blacksquare

Corollary 4
.

Let
\eta
be such that
\left|\partial^{2}_{y}\log f(y)\right|\leq\eta~\forall y\in[0,1]
. Then, the difference between any two angles is

\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l^{\prime}}\right|\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{4}\eta,

(76)

\forall l,l^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,2^{k-1}\}
.

Proof.

The worst scenario is when
l=1
and
l^{\prime}=2^{k-1}
. In this case, by using the triangular inequality, we have

\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{2^{k-1}}\right|\leq\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{2}\right|+\cdots+\left|\theta^{(k-1)}_{2^{k-1}-1}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{2^{k-1}}\right|\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{4}\eta,

(77)

since there are less than
\delta_{k}^{-1}
elements in the sum.

\blacksquare

In this situation, we can define the representative angle
\tilde{\theta}^{(k-1)}
for the clustering process as the one corresponding to the middle part of the interval, satisfying

\left|\tilde{\theta}^{(k-1)}-\theta^{(k-1)}_{l}\right|\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{8}\eta\quad\forall l\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{k-1}-1\}.

(78)

B.2 Error Bound of the Algorithm

V.0.1
Relation Between the Difference in the Angles and the Fidelity

Let us consider a system with
n
qubits, thus the unitary gate to prepare the quantum state representing the target density function can be written in terms of the blocks as

\mathfrak{U}_{n}=\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(n-1)}\right)\cdots\mathcal{U}_{0}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(0)}\right),

(79)

where we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k-1}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(k-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq F_{k}^{k-1}(\bm{y},\bm{\theta}^{(k-1)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-k)}.

(80)

Let
\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n}
denote the operation
\mathfrak{U}_{n}
given a representative with an error
\eta_{k}
between the angles for each block, i.e.
|\theta_{l}^{(k-1)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(k-1)}|\leq\eta_{k}
for
l=0,\dots,2^{k-1}-1
and
k=1,\dots,n
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider a system of
n
qubits and an error
\eta_{k}
between any angle of the
k
-th block and its representative such that
\eta_{k}\leq\pi
, with
k=1,\dots,n
. Then, the fidelity between the final states with and without clustering,
F=|\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\mathfrak{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}|^{2}
, satisfies

F\geq\prod_{k=1}^{n}\cos^{2}\left(\eta_{k}/2\right).

(81)

Proof.

We use induction to prove the inequality. Hence, we start with the elemental case of
n=1
and later proceed assuming it is satisfied for
n-1
and check if it holds for
n
:

•

n=1
:

\displaystyle\bra{0}\mathfrak{U}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{1}\ket{0}=

\displaystyle\bra{0}R_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta^{(0)})R_{y}(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)})\ket{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{0}R_{y}^{\dagger}\left(\theta^{(0)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(0)}\right)\ket{0}=\cos\left(\frac{\theta^{(0)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(0)}}{2}\right)

(82)

Notice that the angles, given by

\displaystyle\theta^{(n-1)}_{l}=2\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{\int_{x_{\text{min}}+l\delta_{n}}^{x_{\text{min}}+(l+1/2)\delta_{n}}f(x)dx}{\int_{x_{\text{min}}+l\delta_{n}}^{x_{\text{min}}+(l+1)\delta_{n}}f(x)dx}}\right),

(83)

take values between
2\arccos(1)=0
and
2\arccos(0)=\pi
. Then, since the cosine is a decreasing function in the interval
[0,\pi/2]
and
\eta\leq\pi
, we have that

\displaystyle|\theta^{(0)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(0)}|\leq\eta_{1}

\displaystyle\implies\cos\left(\frac{\theta^{(0)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(0)}}{2}\right)\geq\cos\left(\eta_{1}/2\right)

\displaystyle\implies F\geq\cos^{2}\left(\eta_{1}/2\right).

(84)

•

n>1
: Assume the condition holds for
n-1
. We aim to recover the expression of
\bra{0}^{\otimes n-1}\mathfrak{U}_{n-1}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n-1}\ket{0}^{\otimes n-1}
so we can use the induction hypothesis. Therefore, as we did for
n=1
, we sandwich the operator

\left(F_{n}^{n-1}(\bm{y},\bm{\theta}^{(n-1)})\right)^{\dagger}F_{n}^{n-1}(\bm{y},\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-1)})

(85)

with the state
\ket{0}
at each side. From now on during this proof, to simplify the notation, we denote the gate
F_{k}^{k-1}(\bm{y},\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(k-1)})
as
U_{k-1}(\bm{\theta}^{(k-1)})
. Then, for
n
:

\displaystyle\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\mathfrak{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{0}\left(\theta^{(0)}\right)\cdots\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{n-1}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(n-1)}\right)\mathcal{U}_{n-1}\left(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-1)}\right)\cdots\mathcal{U}_{0}\left(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=\bra{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\bra{0}\left(U_{0}^{\dagger}(\theta^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-1)}\right)\cdots\left(U_{n-2}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}^{(n-2)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\cdot\left(U_{n-1}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}^{(n-1)})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(U_{n-1}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-1)})\right)\left(U_{n-2}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-2)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\cdots\left(U_{0}(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-1)}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\ket{0}

\displaystyle=\bra{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\left(U_{0}^{\dagger}(\theta^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-2)}\right)\cdots\left(U_{n-2}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}^{(n-2)})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1}=0}^{1}\ket{i_1\ldots i_{n-1}}\bra{i_1\ldots i_{n-1}}\cdot\bra{0}R_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(n-1)})\ket{0}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(U_{n-2}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-2)})\right)\cdots\left(U_{0}(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-2)}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}

(86)

Notice that we now we can substitute the rotation terms in the following way:

\displaystyle\bra{0}R_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(n-1)})\ket{0}

=\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(n-1)}}{2}\right)\geq\cos\left({\eta_{n}/2}\right),

(87)

since
\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}^{(n-1)},\tilde{\theta}^{(n-1)},\eta_{n}\in[0,\pi]~\forall i_{1},\dots i_{n-1}\in\{0,1\}
. However, before introducing the inequality, we need to check that the rest of the terms have the same sign. To do so, we first sandwich the terms corresponding to the block
n-2
with the state
\ket{0}
as well:

\displaystyle\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\mathfrak{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle=\bra{0}^{\otimes(n-2)}\left(U_{0}^{\dagger}(\theta^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-3)}\right)\cdots\left(U_{n-3}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}^{(n-3)})\right)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1}=0}^{1}\ket{i_1\ldots i_{n-2}}\bra{i_1\ldots i_{n-2}}\cdot\bra{0}R_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)})\ket{i_{n-1}}\bra{i_{n-1}}R_{y}(\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)})\ket{0}\right.

\displaystyle\cdot\left.\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}-\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}}{2}\right)\right)\cdot\left(U_{n-3}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-3)})\right)\cdots\left(U_{0}(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-3)}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-2)}.

(88)

Here, since all the angles are between 0 and
\pi
, we have that

\displaystyle\bra{0}R^{\dagger}_{y}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)})\ket{0}\bra{0}R_{y}(\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)})\ket{0}=\cos\frac{\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)}}{2}\cos\frac{\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)}}{2}\geq 0,

(89)

\displaystyle\bra{0}R^{\dagger}_{y}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)})\ket{1}\bra{1}R_{y}(\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)})\ket{0}=\sin\frac{\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)}}{2}\sin\frac{\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)}}{2}~\geq 0,

(90)

\forall i_{1},\dots i_{n-2}\in\{0,1\}
. Then, by proceeding analogously with the rest of the blocks, we obtain that all terms are positive. Hence, we can apply the inequality:

\displaystyle\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\mathfrak{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\geq\bra{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\left(U_{0}^{\dagger}(\theta^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-2)}\right)\cdots\left(U_{n-3}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}^{(n-3)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-2}=0}^{1}\ket{i_1\ldots i_{n-2}}\bra{i_1\ldots i_{n-2}}\cdot\otimes\sum_{i_{n-1}=0}^{1}R_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)})\ket{i_{n-1}}\bra{i_{n-1}}R_{y}(\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(U_{n-3}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-3)}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\right)\cdots\left(U_{0}(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-2)}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\cdot\cos\left({\eta_{n}/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\bra{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\left(U_{0}^{\dagger}(\theta^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-2)}\right)\cdots\left(U_{n-3}^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta}^{(n-3)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-2}=0}^{1}\ket{i_1\ldots i_{n-2}}\bra{i_1\ldots i_{n-2}}\cdot\otimes R_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-2}}^{(n-2)})R_{y}(\tilde{\theta}^{(n-2)})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(U_{n-3}(\bm{\tilde{\theta}}^{(n-3)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}\right)\cdots\left(U_{0}(\tilde{\theta}^{(0)})\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(n-2)}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\cdot\cos\left({\eta_{n}/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\bra{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\mathfrak{U}_{n-1}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n-1}\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-1)}\cdot\cos\left({\eta_{n}/2}\right).

(91)

Finally, using the induction hypothesis, we conclude that

\displaystyle\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\mathfrak{U}_{n}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\cos\left({\eta_{k}/2}\right)\cdot\cos\left({\eta_{n}/2}\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\cos\left({\eta_{k}/2}\right)

\displaystyle\implies F\geq\prod_{k=1}^{n}\cos^{2}\left(\eta_{k}/2\right).

(92)

\blacksquare

Notice that one of the conditions of the previous Theorem is that
\eta_{k}\leq\pi

\forall k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Recall that, as seen in Eq. (
78
),
\eta_{k}\equiv\frac{\delta_{k}}{8}\eta
. Therefore, we obtain the following condition for the value of
\eta
for Th.
3
to be satisfied:

\frac{\delta_{k}}{8}\eta\leq\pi\implies\eta\leq 2^{k-1}\cdot 8\pi.

(93)

In particular, if this holds for the smallest
k
, with value
1
, it will be satisfied for the rest of the blocks. Then, we can write the previous condition simply as
\eta\leq 8\pi
.

V.0.2
Expression of
k_{0}

Assume only clusterization for
k>k_{0}
:

\displaystyle F\geq\prod_{k=k_{0}+1}^{n}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\eta}{4\cdot 2^{k}}\right)\geq\prod_{k=k_{0}+1}^{n}e^{-2\left(\frac{\eta}{4\cdot 2^{k}}\right)^{2}}=e^{-2\frac{\eta^{2}}{16}\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{n}4^{-k}},

(94)

since
\cos(x)\geq e^{-x^{2}}
for
x\lessapprox\pi/2
. Also, the sum is a geometric series with value

\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{n}4^{-k}=\frac{1}{3}\left(4^{-k_{0}}-4^{-n}\right).

(95)

Then,

\displaystyle F\geq e^{-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta^{2}}{16}\left(4^{-k_{0}}-4^{-n}\right)}\coloneqq F_{k_{0}}.

(96)

Let us finally find an expression for
k_{0}
given minimum fidelity
F_{k_{0}}=1-\epsilon
:

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta^{2}}{16}\left(4^{-k_{0}}-4^{-n}\right)=\log{F_{k_0}}

(97)

\displaystyle\left(4^{-k_{0}}-4^{-n}\right)=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{16}{\eta^{2}}\log{F_{k_0}}

(98)

\displaystyle 4^{-k_{0}}=4^{-n}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{16}{\eta^{2}}\log{F_{k_0}}

(99)

\displaystyle-k_{0}\log{4}=\log{\left(4^{-n}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{16}{\eta^2}\log{F_{k_0}}\right)}

(100)

\displaystyle k_{0}=-\frac{\log{\left(4^{-n}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{16}{\eta^2}\log{1-\epsilon}\right)}}{\log{4}}.

(101)

Note that in the limit for both
F_{0}\to 1
and
\eta\to\infty
, we have that

k_{0}\to-\log(4^{-n})/\log(4)=n,

(102)

as expected.

Also, since
k_{0}
must be an integer, we take the ceiling of its value. In addition, we consider the minimum of this parameter to be 2, so the condition of
\eta\leq 8\pi
is satisfied. Hence, the final expression for
k_{0}
is

k_{0}=\max\left\{\lceil-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(4^{-n}-\frac{96}{\eta^{2}}\log(1-\epsilon))\rceil,2\right\},

(103)

where we have defined
\epsilon\equiv 1-F_{0}
.

Once we have a fixed number of uniformly controlled rotation blocks,
k_{0}
, for which there is no clustering, we count the number of necessary gates. The gate
F_{k}^{k-1}(\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\theta})
can be implemented with
2^{k-1}
CNOTs and
2^{k-1}
single-qubit gates
MIKKO3GATES
. Thus, the total number of gates is:

\displaystyle\#\text{CNOTs}=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}2^{k-1}=2^{k_{0}}-1,

(104)

\displaystyle\#\text{SQGs}\;\;\;=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}2^{k-1}+\sum_{k=k_{0}+1}^{n}1=2^{k_{0}}-1+n-k_{0}.

(105)

B.3 Final Protocol

Definition 1
.

Let
f\colon[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}]\to
I​R
+
be a positive function in
L^{2}([x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}])
. We define the
n
-qubit normalized representative state of
f(x)
as the
n
-qubit state
|f(x)\rangle_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}f(x_{\text{min}}+j\delta_{n})|j\rangle
, with
\delta_{n}=\frac{x_{\text{max}}-x_{\text{min}}}{2^{n}-1}
and
\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}f^{2}(x_{\text{min}}+j\delta_{n})=1
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f:[0,1]\rightarrow
I​R
+
be a positive integrable function in
L^{2}([0,1])
and
0\leq\eta\leq 8\pi
a constant such that
\eta=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\left|\partial^{2}_{x}\log f^{2}(x)\right|
. Then, it is posible to approximate the
n
-qubit representative state of
f(x)
,
|f(x)\rangle_{n}
, by a quantum state
|\Psi(f)\rangle_{n}
such that the fidelity
|\langle\Psi(f)|f(x)\rangle_{n}|^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon
with at most
2^{k_{0}(\epsilon)}-1
two-qubit gates, with

k_{0}(\epsilon)=\max\left\{\lceil-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(4^{-n}-\frac{96}{\eta^{2}}\log(1-\epsilon))\rceil,2\right\},

(106)

and the circuit to perform it is given in Fig.
14
.

Proof.

Using Th.
2
and Cor.
4
, we can define the representative angle for each block as in Eq. (
78
). Then, by applying Th.
3
with the development between Eqs. (
94
) and (
101
), it is clear that the fidelity will be larger than or equal to
1-\epsilon
. Finally, Eq. (
104
) states that the number of two-qubit gates necessary to realize this protocol is
2^{k_{0}}-1
, as we wanted to prove.

\blacksquare
