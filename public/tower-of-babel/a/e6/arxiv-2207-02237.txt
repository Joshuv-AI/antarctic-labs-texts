Geometric structure of thermal cones
A. de Oliveira Junior, Jakub Czartowski, Karol Życzkowski, Kamil Korzekwa

Abstract
The second law of thermodynamics imposes a fundamental asymmetry in the flow of events. The so-called thermodynamic arrow of time introduces an ordering that divides the system’s state space into past, future and incomparable regions. In this work, we analyse the structure of the resulting thermal cones, i.e., sets of states that a given state can thermodynamically evolve to (the future thermal cone) or evolve from (the past thermal cone). Specifically, for a d d -dimensional classical state of a system interacting with a heat bath, we find explicit construction of the past thermal cone and the incomparable region. Moreover, we provide a detailed analysis of their behaviour based on thermodynamic monotones given by the volumes of thermal cones. Results obtained apply also to other majorisation-based resource theories (such as that of entanglement and coherence), since the partial ordering describing allowed state transformations is then the opposite of the thermodynamic order in the infinite temperature limit. Finally, we also generalise the construction of thermal cones to account for probabilistic transformations.



I
Introduction

Thermodynamic evolution of physical systems obeys a fundamental asymmetry imposed by nature. Known as the thermodynamic arrow of time
Eddington 1928
, it is a direct manifestation of the second law of thermodynamics, which states that the entropy of an isolated system cannot decrease
Fermi 1956
;
Callen 1985
. In other words, the thermodynamic evolution inherently distinguishes the past from the future: systems spontaneously evolve to future equilibrium states, but do not spontaneously evolve away from them. Even though recognition of the thermodynamic arrow of time is an old discussion
Boltzmann 1895
;
Zermelo 1896
, it still raises deep questions relevant both to philosophy and the foundations of physics
Prigogine 2000
;
Price 2004
. Despite many attempts, the full understanding of the time asymmetry in thermodynamics seems to be still beyond our reach. In recent years, we have seen renewed interest in exploring the thermodynamics of a few quanta, which motivated the development of a powerful theoretical toolkit
Goold et al. 2016
;
Deffner and Campbell 2019
;
Landi and Paternostro 2021
;
vom Ende and Dirr 2022
that allows one to revisit old questions.

The toolkit in question, providing a robust approach to study thermodynamics of small systems, is given by the resource-theoretic framework
Janzing et al. 2000
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
;
Brandão et al. 2015
;
Lostaglio 2019
. It not only recovers the macroscopic results, but also presents a suitable platform to address relevant problems within the field of
thermodynamics, including a rigorous derivation of the third law
Wilming and Gallego 2017
and study of cooling mechanisms
Lostaglio et al. 2018
;
Lostaglio 2019
. Furthermore, it provides a perfect setup for modelling thermalisation in many-body physics
Sparaciari et al. 2021
. Regarding the thermodynamic arrow of time, a resource-theoretic analysis was performed in Ref.
Korzekwa 2017
, where the author investigated this problem from the point of view of order theory. However, Ref.
Korzekwa 2017
focused on structural differences between classical and quantum theories in contrast to the geometric aspects of thermal cones that we investigate here.

In this work we aim at characterising the thermodynamic arrow of time by investigating allowed transformations between energy-incoherent states that arise from the most general energy-conserving interaction between the system and a thermal bath. These transformations encode the structure of the thermodynamic arrow of time by telling us which states can be reached from a given state, here called the
present state
, in accordance with the laws of thermodynamics. Under these constraints, the state space can then be naturally decomposed into three parts: the set of states to which the present state can evolve is called the
future thermal cone
; the set of states that can evolve to the present state is called the
past thermal cone
; while states that are neither in the past nor the future thermal cone form the
incomparable region
.

Although substantial insights have been obtained by studying the future thermal cone
Lostaglio et al. 2018
;
Mazurek and Horodecki 2018
;
Mazurek 2019
, explicit characterisation of the incomparable region and the past thermal cone has not been performed. Our main results consist of two theorems addressing this gap. The first one yields a geometric characterisation of the past majorisation cone (i.e., the set of probability distributions majorising a given distribution) by means of explicit construction, which, together with the incomparable region and future thermal cone, fully specifies the time-like ordering in the probability simplex in the limit of infinite temperature. The second result, derived using a novel tool of an embedding lattice, generalises the first one to the case of finite temperatures.
Similar results are also obtained for probabilistic scenarios
Vidal 1999
.
The approach presented here allows us to conduct a rigorous study of the causal structure of the thermal cones.
It is known from earlier works that the future (thermal) cone is convex
Bhatia 1996
;
Lostaglio et al. 2018
; our results extend this knowledge by showing rigorously that the past (thermal) cone can always be decomposed into
d!
convex parts and, in the zero-temperature limit, only one of them retains a non-zero volume, rendering the entire past thermal cone convex. We also introduce new thermodynamic monotones given by the volumes of the past and future thermal cones.

Our results can also be seen as an extension of the famous Hardy-Littlewood-Pólya theorem
Hardy et al. 1952
, as they specify the past cone and the incomparable region in addition to the previously studied future cone. Therefore, they can also be employed to study other majorisation-based resource theories, such as the theory of entanglement
Nielsen 1999
or coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014
;
Du et al. 2015
. Concerning local operations, an analogy between special relativity and the set of pure states of bi-partite systems was previously made in Ref.
Życzkowski and Bengtsson 2002
, where the authors correspondingly divided the state space into three parts. Here, we consider a more general partial-order structure, the thermomajorisation order, which generalises the previous and recovers it in the limit of infinite temperature.

The paper is organised as follows. We set the scene in Sec.
II
by recalling the resource-theoretic approach to thermodynamics and introducing the necessary concepts. We also collect there known results concerning the conditions for state transformation under majorisation and thermomajorisation. In Sec.
III
, we state the first of our main results concerning the construction of majorisation cones and discuss its interpretation within the thermodynamic setting and in other majorisation-based theories.
This construction is also generalised for probabilistic transformations
. Sec.
IV
is devoted to the analogous study of the thermal cones, generated by thermomajorisation relation, where we also introduce the tool of embedding lattice, instrumental for the proof of the second main result. Sec.
V
, we introduce thermodynamic monotones given by the volumes of the past and future thermal cone, discuss their intuitive operational interpretation and describe their properties.
We also comment on the different natures of future and past cones for entanglement transformations.
Finally, we conclude with an outlook in Sec.
VI
. The technical derivation of the main results can be found in Appendix
A
.
In Appendix
B
, we derive the analogue of the majorisation cones for probabilistic transformations. Next, Appendix
C
presents methods used to obtain insights into the volumes of majorisation cones in the context of quantum entanglement. Appendix
D
shows how to obtain the coherent equivalents of the future and past cones together with the incomparable regions for coherent states under thermal operations
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
and Gibbs-preserving operations
Faist et al. 2015
.

II
Setting the scene

II.1
Thermodynamic evolution of energy-incoherent states

In what follows, we study the thermodynamics of finite-dimensional quantum systems in the presence of a heat bath at temperature
T
. The system under investigation is described by a Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}E_{i}\left|E_{i}\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle E_{i}\right|
, and it is prepared in a state
\rho
; while the heat bath, with a Hamiltonian
H_{E}
, is in a thermal equilibrium state,

\gamma_{E}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{E}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H_{E}}\right)},

(1)

where
\beta=1/k_{B}T
is the inverse temperature with
k_{B}
denoting the Boltzmann constant. The evolution of the joint system is assumed to be closed, and therefore described by a unitary operator
U
, which is additionally enforced to conserve the total energy,

[U,H\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes H_{E}]=0.

(2)

More formally, the set of allowed thermodynamic transformations is modelled by
thermal operations

Janzing et al. 2000
;
Brandão et al. 2015
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
, which consists of completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps that act on a system
\rho
with Hamiltonian
H
as

{\cal E}(\rho)=\textrm{Tr}_{E}\left(U\left(\rho\otimes\gamma_{E}\right)U^{\dagger}\right),

(3)

with
U
satisfying Eq. (
2
) and the state
\gamma_{E}
given by Eq. (
1
) with an arbitrary Hamiltonian
H_{E}
. Note that the energy conservation condition, Eq. (
2
), can be interpreted as encoding the first law of thermodynamics. Moreover, the fact that the heat bath is in thermal equilibrium implies that every thermal operation
{\cal E}
is a map which preserves the Gibbs state,

{\cal E}(\gamma)=\gamma,

(4)

with
\gamma
being the thermal Gibbs state of the system given by Eq. (
1
) with
H_{E}
replaced by
H
. Thus, Eq. (
4
) combined with a contractive distance measure
\delta
resulting in
\delta(\rho,\gamma)\geq\delta({\cal E}(\rho),{\cal E}(\gamma))=\delta({\cal E}(\rho),\gamma)
, incorporates the core physical principle of the second law of thermodynamics as it captures the idea of evolution towards thermal equilibrium.

In this work we focus on
energy-incoherent states
, i.e., quantum states
\rho
that commute with the Hamiltonian
H
.
This restriction can be justified in the quasi-classical regime, in which systems are quantised and possess only a small number of energy levels, but the decoherence is so strong that the interference effects between the different energy levels become negligible. Energy-incoherent
states can be equivalently represented by a
d
-dimensional probability vectors
\boldsymbol{p}
of their eigenvalues, which coincide with populations in the energy eigenbasis. Note that
\gamma
commutes with
H
and thus can be represented by a probability vector

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\frac{1}{Z}\left(e^{-\beta E_{1}},...,e^{-\beta E_{d}}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad Z=\sum_{i}e^{-\beta E_{i}}.

(5)

Here, one should note the difference between the state
\gamma
and the probability vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
The most general evolution between two probability vectors,
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, is described by a stochastic matrix
\Lambda
, where
\Lambda
satisfies
\Lambda_{ij}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}\Lambda_{ij}=1
. In the thermodynamic context, however, we may restrict the analysis to stochastic matrices preserving the Gibbs state,
i.e., to
\Lambda
such that
\Lambda\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, in short called Gibbs-preserving (GP) matrices in the literature
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
;
Faist et al. 2015
.
This is because of the following theorem linking the existence of a thermal operation between incoherent states to the existence of a Gibbs-preserving stochastic matrix between probability distributions representing these states
Janzing et al. 2000
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
;
Korzekwa 2016
.

Theorem 1

(Theorem 5 of Ref.
Janzing et al. 2000
)
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be quantum states commuting with the system Hamiltonian
H
, and
\gamma
its thermal Gibbs state with respect to the inverse temperature
\beta
. Denote their eigenvalues by
\boldsymbol{p}
,
\boldsymbol{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, respectively. Then, there exists a thermal operation
{\cal E}
, such that
{\cal E}(\rho)=\sigma
, if and only if there exists a stochastic map
\Lambda
such that

\Lambda\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}~~\mathrm{and}~~\Lambda\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{q}.

(6)

As a result of the above theorem, while studying thermodynamic transformations between energy-incoherent states, we can replace CPTP maps and density matrices with stochastic matrices and probability vectors, respectively. Then, the rules describing what state transformations are allowed under thermal operations, can be expressed by a partial-order relation between probability vectors corresponding to initial and final states. In the infinite-temperature limit, these rules are encoded by the majorisation relation
Marshall et al. 1979
, and in the finite-temperature case by thermomajorisation
Ruch et al. 1980
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
.

II.2
Infinite temperature and the majorisation order

and

, respectively. While

(since
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)

f_{\boldsymbol{q}}(x)
), both states are incomparable with
\boldsymbol{c}
, as their majorisation curves cross with
f_{\boldsymbol{c}}(x)
.

In the infinite temperature case,
\beta=0
, the thermal Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is described by a uniform distribution

\boldsymbol{\eta}=\frac{1}{d}(1,...,1).

(7)

Theorem
1
implies that a state
\boldsymbol{p}
can be mapped to
\boldsymbol{q}
if and only if there exists a bistochastic matrix (
\Lambda
, such that
\Lambda\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}
), which transforms
\boldsymbol{p}
into
\boldsymbol{q}
. To formulate the solution we need to recall the concept of majorisation
Marshall et al. 1979
:

Definition 1

(Majorisation)
.

Given two
d
-dimensional probability distributions
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, we say that
\boldsymbol{p}

majorises

\boldsymbol{q}
, and denote it by
\boldsymbol{p}\succ\boldsymbol{q}
, if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\downarrow}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}^{\downarrow}\quad\text{for all}\quad k\in\{1\dots d\},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{p}^{\downarrow}
denotes the vector
\boldsymbol{p}
rearranged in a non-increasing order.

Equivalently, the majorisation relation can be expressed in a more geometric way by defining a
majorisation curve
, i.e., a piecewise linear curve
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
obtained by joining the origin
(0,0)
and the points
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{\downarrow}_{i}\right)
, for
k\in\{1,...,d\}
. Then, a distribution
\boldsymbol{p}
majorises
\boldsymbol{q}
if and only if the majorisation curve
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)
of
\boldsymbol{p}
is always above that of
\boldsymbol{q}
,

\boldsymbol{p}\succ\boldsymbol{q}\iff\forall x\in\left[0,1\right]:~f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)\geq f_{\boldsymbol{q}}(x)\,.

(9)

Since majorisation does not introduce a total order, a given pair of states
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
may be incomparable with each other: neither
\boldsymbol{p}
majorises
\boldsymbol{q}
, nor
\boldsymbol{q}
majorises
\boldsymbol{p}
. In terms of majorisation curves, this implies that both curves intersect each other (see Fig.
1
).

We are now ready to state the connection between majorisation relation and bistochastic state transformations captured by the celebrated Hardy-Littlewood-Pólya theorem
Hardy et al. 1952
.

Theorem 2

(Theorem II.1.10 of Ref.
Bhatia 1996
)
.

There exists a bistochastic matrix
\Lambda
,
\Lambda\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}
, mapping
\boldsymbol{p}
to
\boldsymbol{q}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{p}\succ\boldsymbol{q}
.

Furthermore, it is important to mention that the majorisation order forms a lattice structure
Cicalese and Vaccaro 2002
, within which there exists a natural time-like hierarchy of elements.

Definition 2

(Lattice)
.

A partially ordered set
(L,\leq)
forms a lattice if for every pair of elements
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in L
, there exists a least upper bound, called
join
and denoted by
\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}
, such that
\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}\geq\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}\geq\boldsymbol{q}
; and a greatest lower bound, called
meet
and denoted by
\boldsymbol{p}\wedge\boldsymbol{q}
, such that
\boldsymbol{p}\wedge\boldsymbol{q}\leq\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{p}\wedge\boldsymbol{q}\leq\boldsymbol{q}
.

Viewing the lattice structure of majorisation order from the perspective of the laws governing state transformation under bistochastic matrices, it becomes clear that a join can be understood as the last point of the common “past” for a given two elements of the lattice, and similarly, a meet can be seen as the first point of the common future
Korzekwa 2017
. The existence of both join and meet within the majorisation order lattice has been proven in Ref.
Cicalese and Vaccaro 2002
and will be instrumental in constructing the past and incomparable regions.

II.3
Finite temperature and thermomajorisation order

Theorem
2
can be generalised from bistochastic matrices to Gibbs-preserving matrices, and the aim of this section is to present such a result without focusing on its derivation (for details see Ref.
Korzekwa 2016
). To achieve this, we first need to introduce the thermodynamic equivalent of the majorisation partial order, which can be achieved by first presenting the concept of
\beta
-ordering, and then introducing the notion of thermomajorisation curves
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
. For a given initial state
\boldsymbol{p}
and a thermal Gibbs distribution
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
with inverse temperature
\beta
, the
\beta
-ordering of
\boldsymbol{p}
is defined by a permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
that sorts
p_{i}/\gamma_{i}
in a non-increasing order,

\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(i)}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(i)}}\geq\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(i+1)}}{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(i+1)}}\>\>,\>\>\text{for}\>\>i\in\{1,...,d-1\}.

(10)

Thus, the
\beta
-ordered version of
\boldsymbol{p}
is given by

\boldsymbol{p}^{\,\beta}=\left(p_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(1)},\dots,p_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(d)}\right).

(11)

Note that each permutation belonging to the symmetric group,
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
, defines a different
\beta
-ordering on the energy levels of the Hamiltonian
H
.

A thermomajorisation curve
f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\left[0,1\right]
is defined as a piecewise linear curve composed of linear segments connecting the point
(0,0)
and the points defined by consecutive subsums of the
\beta
-ordered form of the probability
\boldsymbol{p}^{\beta}
and the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\beta}
,

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma^{\,\beta}_{i},~\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{\,\beta}_{i}\right):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(i)},~\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(i)}\right),

(12)

for
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

Finally, given two
d
-dimensional probability distributions
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, and a fixed inverse temperature
\beta
, we say that
\boldsymbol{p}

thermomajorises

\boldsymbol{q}
and denote it as
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}
, if the thermomajorisation curve
f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
is above
f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
everywhere, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}\iff\forall x\in[0,1]:~f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)\geq f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(x)\,.

(13)

As before, it may happen that given two vectors,
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, are incomparable, meaning that
f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
and
f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
cross at a some point (see Fig.
2
). Furthermore, the thermomajorisation order cannot be seen as a lattice in the sense that for
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, which do not share the same
\beta
-order, there is no unique join or meet
Korzekwa 2017
.

We can now state the generalisation of Theorem
2
from bistochastic matrices to Gibbs-preserving matrices which, via Theorem
1
, yields the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a thermal operation between two energy-incoherent states
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
;
Ruch et al. 1978
.

Theorem 3

(Theorem 1.3 of Ref.
Janzing et al. 2000
)
.

There exists a Gibbs-preserving matrix
\Lambda
,
\Lambda\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, mapping
\boldsymbol{p}
to
\boldsymbol{q}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}
.

\boldsymbol{c}

f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(x)
.

III
Majorisation cones

The reachability of states under bistochastic matrices can be studied by introducing the notion of
majorisation cones
, defined as follows:

Definition 3

(Majorisation cones)
.

The set of states that a probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
can be mapped to by bistochastic matrices is called the
future cone

{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
. The set of states that can be mapped to
\boldsymbol{p}
by bistochastic matrices is called the
past cone

{\cal T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})
. The set of states that are neither in the past nor in the future cone of
\boldsymbol{p}
is called the
incomparable region

{\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

Definition
3
provides us with a stage for studying thermodynamics of energy-incoherent states in the infinite-temperature limit. What is more, it also allows us to explore a more general class of state transformations whose rules are based on a majorisation relation, e.g., the resource theories of entanglement
Nielsen 1999
or coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014
;
Du et al. 2015
;
Streltsov et al. 2016
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
. These more general settings are discussed in more detail in Sec.
III.2
, whereas now we focus on thermodynamic transformations.

III.1
Geometry of majorisation cones

The future cone can be easily characterised by employing the Birkhoff’s theorem
Bhatia 1996
stating that every bistochastic matrix can be written as a convex combination of permutation matrices. Thus, the set of
d\times d
bistochastic matrices is a convex polytope with
d!
vertices, one for each permutation in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Combining this observation with Theorem
2
, we obtain the future cone of
\boldsymbol{p}
:

Corollary 4

(Future cone)
.

For a
d
-dimensional probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
, its future cone is given by

{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})=\operatorname{conv}\left[\left\{\Pi\boldsymbol{p}\,,\mathcal{S}_{d}\ni\boldsymbol{\pi}\mapsto\Pi\right\}\right],

(14)

where
\Pi
denotes a permutation matrix corresponding to the permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}
with
d
elements, and
\operatorname{conv[S]}
the convex hull of the set
S
.

The above corollary implies that the future cone of
\boldsymbol{p}
is a convex set with all distributions lying in
{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
being majorised by
\boldsymbol{p}
. Since the
d
-dimensional sharp distribution,
(0,...,1,..,0)
, majorises all probability distributions, its future cone is the entire probability simplex, which we will denote by
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{d}
. For later convenience, we will introduce the concept of
d!
Weyl chambers, each composed of probability vectors which can be ordered non-decreasingly by a common permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. In particular, we will refer to the chamber corresponding to the identity permutation as the
canonical Weyl chamber
.

If there is no transformation mapping
\boldsymbol{p}
into
\boldsymbol{q}
nor
\boldsymbol{q}
into
\boldsymbol{p}
, we say that these two states are incomparable. The incomparable region can be characterised by incorporating into the analysis the concept of quasi-probability distributions, which are defined by relaxing the non-negativity condition on the entries of a normalised probability distribution. The following result, the proof of which is employing the lattice structure of majorisation order and can be found in Appendix
A
, specifies the incomparable region of
\boldsymbol{p}
.

Lemma 5

(Incomparable region)
.

For a
d
-dimensional probability distribution
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},...,p_{d})
, consider the quasi-probability distributions
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
constructed for each
n\in\{1,...,d\}
,

\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}=\left(t^{(n)}_{1},p_{n}^{\downarrow},...,p_{n}^{\downarrow},t^{(n)}_{d}\right),

(15)

with

\displaystyle t_{1}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}p_{i}^{\downarrow}-(n-2)p_{n}^{\downarrow},

\displaystyle t_{d}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=1-t^{(n)}_{1}-(d-2)p_{n}^{\downarrow},

(16)

and define the following set

\mathbb{T}:=\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{d-1}\operatorname{conv}\left[{\cal T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{t}^{(j)}\right)\cup{\cal T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{t}^{(j+1)}\right)\right].

(17)

Then, the incomparable region of
\boldsymbol{p}
is given by

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})=\left[\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{T})\backslash{\cal T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\right]\cap\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{d},

(18)

where the backslash
\backslash
denotes the set difference and
\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{T})
represents the interior of
\mathbb{T}
.

We will refer to the quasi-probability distributions
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
as
tangent vectors
. The intuition behind this name and the importance of
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
can be explained by noticing that any convex function
g(x)
lies fully under its tangent at any point
y
, denoted as
t_{y}(x)\geq g(x)
, with equality guaranteed only for
t_{y}(y)=g(y)
. It follows from the definition of
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
that its majorisation curve
f_{\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}}(x)
is parallel to the
n
-th linear piece of
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)
for
x\in\left[(n-1)/d,\,n/d\right]
, and the first and last elements of
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
guarantee tangency and normalisation. Finally, since the adjacent linear fragments of
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)
share the elbows of the function, the consequent tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)},\,\boldsymbol{t}^{(n+1)}
are both tangent at a selected elbow,
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(n/d)=f_{\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}}(n/d)=f_{\boldsymbol{t}^{(n+1)}}(n/d)
. Therefore, any convex combination of the form
a\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}+(1-a)\boldsymbol{t}^{(n+1)}
will be ”tangent” at the
n
-th elbow of the
\boldsymbol{p}
majorisation curve. The fact that
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
may be a quasi-probability distribution does not pose a problem, since this vector can be projected back onto the probability simplex. The projected vector
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}_{\text{proj}}
, and can be obtained by successively applying the map

\left\{t^{(n)}_{m-1},\,t^{(n)}_{m}\right\}\longmapsto\left\{\min\left(t^{(n)}_{m-1}+t^{(n)}_{m},\,t^{(n)}_{m-1}\right),\,\max\left(t^{(n)}_{m},\,0\right)\right\}

(19)

to pairs of entries of
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
going from
m=d
to
m=2
. In each step, the map either zeros the second component by shifting its value to the first one or, if the second component is non-negative, it leaves them both unperturbed. Geometrically, the state is shifted along the edges of the future cone of
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
and every time it hits a plane defining one of the faces of the probability simplex
\Delta_{d}
, a new direction is selected, until the state is composed exclusively of non-negative entries.

Using Lemma
5
, we can now prove the following theorem that specifies the past cone.

Theorem 6

(Past cone)
.

The past cone of
\boldsymbol{p}
is given by

{\cal T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{d}\backslash\operatorname{int}(\mathbb{T})\,.

(20)

Proof.

One only needs to use the fact that

{\cal T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{d}\texttt{\textbackslash}\left({\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})\cup{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})\right)\,,

(21)

and employ Lemma
5
to replace
{\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})
in the above with Eq. (
18
).
∎

Let us make a few comments on the above results. First, note that the incomparable region arises only for

Third, although the past cone is not convex as a whole, it is convex when restricted to any single Weyl chamber.
Therefore, we may note that the tangent vectors

III.2
Links to other resource theories

The two well-known examples of majorisation-based resource theories, where our results are also applicable, include the resource theories of entanglement and coherence. These are defined via the appropriate sets of free operations and free states: local operations and classical communication (LOCC) and separable states in entanglement theory
Horodecki et al. 2009
, and incoherent operations (IO) and incoherent states in coherence theory
Baumgratz et al. 2014
. Within each of these theories, there exists a representation of quantum states via probability distributions that is relevant for formulating state interconversion conditions under free operations. In entanglement theory, a pure bipartite state
\rho=\left|\Psi\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\Psi\right|
can be written in terms of the Schmidt decomposition given by
|{\Psi}\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}|{\psi_{i},\psi^{\prime}_{i}}\rangle
, and represented by a probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
with
p_{i}=|a_{i}|^{2}
. Then, Nielsen’s theorem
Nielsen 1999
states that an initial state
\boldsymbol{p}
can be transformed under LOCC into a target state
\boldsymbol{q}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{p}\prec\boldsymbol{q}
. Similarly, in the resource theory of coherence with respect to a fixed basis
\{|{i}\rangle\}
, one can represent a pure state
\rho=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\psi\right|
by a probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
with
p_{i}=|\langle i|\psi\rangle|^{2}
. Then, a given initial state
\boldsymbol{p}
can be transformed into
\boldsymbol{q}
via incoherent operations if and only if
\boldsymbol{p}\prec\boldsymbol{q}

Du et al. 2015
.

Therefore, we observe that the partial order emerging in the two cases is precisely the opposite to the thermodynamic order in the infinite temperature limit (For more details see Ref.
Kollas 2016
). Consequently, the thermodynamic past and future become the future and past for entanglement and coherence, while the incomparable region remains unchanged (see Fig.
5
).
Note that, for entanglement and coherence, sharp states
\boldsymbol{s}
are in the future cone of any given state, while for thermodynamics (at
\beta=0
), they are in the past. The flat distribution
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is in the past of any state in entanglement and coherence theories, whereas in thermodynamics it is in the future.

One can make a general remark concerning resource monotones, applying to the entanglement, coherence and thermodynamic scenarios alike. Consider an entangled state
|{\psi}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{N}
with the associated Schmidt coefficients
\boldsymbol{p}
and concurrence
\mathcal{C}(|{\psi}\rangle)
as an example of a resource monotone
Hill and Wootters 1997
. If another state
|{\phi}\rangle
with Schmidt coefficients
\boldsymbol{q}
is in the future cone of
|{\psi}\rangle
,
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
, then
\mathcal{C}(|{\phi}\rangle)\leq\mathcal{C}(|{\psi}\rangle)
. Otherwise, if it lies in its past cone,
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})
we know that
\mathcal{C}(|{\phi}\rangle)\geq\mathcal{C}(|{\psi}\rangle)
. However, if the two states are incomparable,
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})
, nothing can be said about the relation between both concurrences.

III.3
Probabilistic majorisation cones

Finally, it should be observed that the notion of majorisation cones, as presented until now, deals with deterministic transformations. However, this approach can be extended to probabilistic transformations using Vidal’s criterion for entanglement
Vidal 1999
;
Vidal et al. 2000
and coherence transformations
Zhu et al. 2017
under LOCC and IO, respectively. In the case of probabilistic transformations of bipartite entangled states under LOCC, this is captured by the following theorem.

Theorem 7

(Theorem 1 of Ref.
Vidal 1999
)
.

Consider two bipartite pure states
|{\psi}\rangle
and
|{\phi}\rangle
, whose Schmidt decompositions are described by probability vectors
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, respectively. The maximal transformation probability from
|{\psi}\rangle
to
|{\phi}\rangle
under LOCC is given by

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\min_{1\leq k\leq d}\frac{\sum_{j=k}^{d}p^{\downarrow}_{j}}{\sum_{j=k}^{d}q^{\downarrow}_{j}}.

(22)

In Appendix
B
we discuss the extension of majorisation cones to probabilistic ones, denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p};\mathcal{P})
, with
i\in\{-,\emptyset,+\}
and
\mathcal{P}
being the minimal probability of transformation. Here we will limit ourselves to a brief qualitative discussion
about the behaviour of the probabilistic majorisation cones as the transformation probability changes from
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,\cdot)=1
to
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,\cdot)<1
(see Fig.
6
). Note that the only common points of the future and past for
\mathcal{P}=1
are the current state of the system
\boldsymbol{p}
and its permutations. Conversely, for
\mathcal{P}<1
this is not the only case; consequently, we may define the
interconvertible region
of
\boldsymbol{p}
at the probability level
\mathcal{P}
as the intersection between the probabilistic past and probabilistic future,
\mathcal{T}_{\leftrightarrow}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})\equiv\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})\cap\mathcal{T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})
. This region is non-empty for every
\mathcal{P}<1
. It is easily shown that the future and the past cones grow as the probability of transformation decreases,
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})
and
\mathcal{T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{T}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})
for
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}>\mathcal{P}
. Therefore, the only region that decreases together with
\mathcal{P}
is the incomparable region,
\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\supset\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})
. Interestingly, for every state
\boldsymbol{p}
, we observe that there is a critical value
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, at which no two states are incomparable, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})=\emptyset
[see Figs.
6
a, e
]. Analogous results hold in the context of coherence, as Theorem
7
has its counterpart when considering pure state transformations under IO operations
Zhu et al. 2017
;
Cunden et al. 2021
.

Finally, it is worth mentioning, that a criterion similar to the Vidal’s criterion was established for probabilistic transformation in the context of thermal operations
Alhambra et al. 2016
. In this case, the construction of the probabilistic cones for majorisation generalises directly to the thermomajorisation by using the construction of thermomajorisation cones which we will introduce in the next section.

IV
Thermal cones

Let us turn our attention to a more general scenario, assuming that the temperature is finite,
\beta>0
. In this case, the reachability of energy-incoherent states under Gibbs-preserving matrices can be studied by introducing the notion of
thermal cones
, defined as follows:

Definition 4

(Thermal cones)
.

The set of states that an energy-incoherent state
\boldsymbol{p}
can be mapped into by Gibbs-preserving matrices is called the
future thermal cone

{\cal T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
. Similarly, the set of states that can be mapped to
\boldsymbol{p}
by Gibbs-preserving matrices is called the
past thermal cone

{\cal T}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})
. Finally, the set of states that are neither in the past nor in the future of
\boldsymbol{p}
is called the
incomparable thermal region

{\cal T}^{\,\beta}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

Despite apparent similarities, the case of
\beta>0
turns out to be significantly harder than
\beta=0
. Difficulties stem mostly from a simple fact demonstrated in Ref.
Korzekwa 2017
– even though thermomajorisation forms a lattice in each
\beta
-order, it does not provide a common lattice for the entire probability simplex. Thus, before extending Lemma
5
and Theorem
6
to the thermal setting (proofs of which rely heavily on the existence of a join), we will introduce an
embedding lattice
– a structure which encompasses thermomajorisation order as its subset – and we will demonstrate operations shifting to and from the newly introduced picture.

IV.1
Embedding lattice

To define a
\beta
-dependent embedding
\mathfrak{M}
of the simplex
\Delta_{d}
into a subspace
\Delta^{\mathfrak{M}}_{d}\subset\Delta_{2^{d}-1}
, illustrated by a graphical example in Fig.
7
, we first introduce the vector
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\mathfrak{M}}
with entries given by all possible partial sums of the Gibbs distribution,

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\mathfrak{M}}=\left\{\sum_{i\in I}\gamma_{i}:\,I\in 2^{\{1,\ldots,d\}}\right\},

(23)

with
2^{\{1,\ldots,d\}}
denoting the power set of
d
indices. Moreover, we enforce that it is ordered non-decreasingly, i.e., for
i>j
we have
\Gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}\geq\Gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{j}
. Then, the embedded probability vector,
\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathfrak{M}}:=\mathfrak{M}(\boldsymbol{p})
, is defined by

\displaystyle p^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}

\displaystyle=f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\Gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}\right)-f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\Gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i-1}\right),

(24)

where
f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
is the thermomajorisation curve of
\boldsymbol{p}
.

Within this embedding, the thermomajorisation indeed proves to be almost-standard majorisation relation between the embedded distributions,

\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}~\Longleftrightarrow~\forall j:~\sum_{i=1}^{j}p^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{j}q^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}~\overset{\text{def}}{\Longleftrightarrow}~\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathfrak{M}}\succ_{\mathfrak{M}}\boldsymbol{q}^{\mathfrak{M}},

(25)

where the last symbol
\succ_{\mathfrak{M}}
denotes the majorisation variant related to the embedding lattice. Finally, we note that the only deviation from the standard majorisation lies in the convexity condition in the embedding space, which is imposed not on the probabilities
p^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}
themselves, but on their rescaled versions,

i\leq j\Rightarrow\frac{p^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}}{\gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{i}}\geq\frac{p^{\mathfrak{M}}_{j}}{\gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{j}},

(26)

with scaling factors directly related to the embedded majorisation curve of the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathfrak{M}}
. This ordering should be compared (but not confused) with the
\beta
-ordering introduced in Eq. (
10
), pointing to a relation with thermomajorisation which we will use to show the lattice structure of the introduced space.

The projection
\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
of an arbitrary probability vector
\boldsymbol{q}\in\Delta_{2^{d}-1}
satisfying Eq. (
26
) onto a selected
\beta
-order
\boldsymbol{\pi}
in the original space can be defined descriptively as taking only those elbows of the embedded majorisation curve that match the values of cumulative Gibbs distribution for the selected permutation. Formally, the projected vector,
\boldsymbol{q}^{\mathfrak{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\left(\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right)^{\,\beta}
, is entry-wise defined by

\left(q^{\mathfrak{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)_{i}=\sum^{k(i)}_{j=k(i-1)}q_{j},

(27)

with the indices
k(i)
defined by the requirement that
\Gamma^{\mathfrak{M}}_{k(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}(j)}
.

In particular, it is worth noting two properties of the embedding
\mathfrak{M}
and projections
\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. First, given a vector
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{d}
with a
\beta
-order
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
, we find that by construction
\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(\mathfrak{M}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\right)=\boldsymbol{p}
, which follows directly from Eq. (
27
). On the other hand, for
\boldsymbol{\pi}\neq\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
we find that
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left(\mathfrak{M}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\right)
. The statement is easily shown by observing that the Lorenz curve of
\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\left(\mathfrak{M}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)\right)
connects by line segments
d+1
points of the Lorenz curve corresponding
f^{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\sum_{i}\left(\mathfrak{M}(p)^{\mathfrak{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)_{i}\right)
and therefore majorisation is resolved by linear approximation of a convex function,
f[(1-t)x+ty]\geq(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)
for any
x,y
. The second property is concerned with
\boldsymbol{q}\in\Delta_{2^{d}-1}
satisfying Eq. (
26
) and can be summarised as the fact that projecting and re-embedding the vector will always give the object majorised by the original vector:
\boldsymbol{q}\succ_{\mathfrak{M}}\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{q}))
. It follows similarly to the prior majorisation by the argument of linear approximation of a convex function.

It is necessary to stress that the introduced embedding structure is distinct from the one used in the usual method of reducing thermomajorisation to majorisation
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
. Most importantly, the standard approach requires going to the limit of infinite embedding dimension in order to recover thermomajorisation for arbitrary
\beta
as a special case of standard majorisation. In our proposition, a thermomajorisation curve in dimension
d
is embedded within a
2^{d}-1
-dimensional space. The main difference lies in the non-constant widths of the segments of the Lorenz curve and the fact that once the embedded vector
\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathfrak{M}}
is constructed, its entries should not be subject to reordering. Finally, we present the argument proving that the embedding together with embedded majorisation indeed provide a lattice structure.

Embedded majorisation curve

\mathfrak{M}:\Delta_{d}\rightarrow\Delta_{2^{d}-1}
of
d

\boldsymbol{p}

2^{d}-1

\mathfrak{P}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}}

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
. In the

\gamma_{ij}=\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{j}
.

Corollary 8
.

The subset of the probability simplex
\Delta_{2^{d}-1}
satisfying Eq. (
26
) and subject to the embedded majorisation
\succ_{\mathfrak{M}}
defined in Eq. (
25
) forms a lattice.

Proof.

The embedded majorisation
\succ_{\mathfrak{M}}
may be reinterpretted as thermomajorisation defined for a specific Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathfrak{M}}
and restricted to a particular Weyl chamber of the probability space
\Delta_{2^{d}-1}
by comparing Eq. (
26
) with an analogous sorting rule from Eq. (
10
). This direct isomorphism between thermomajorisation order
\succ_{\beta}
restricted to a single Weyl chamber, known to provide a lattice structure, and the embedded majorisation
\succ_{\mathfrak{M}}
proves the statement.
∎

IV.2
Geometry of thermal cones

As already mentioned, for finite temperatures the rules underlying state transformations are no longer captured by a majorisation relation, but rather by its thermodynamic equivalent known as thermomajorisation
Ruch et al. 1980
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
. As a result, Birkhoff’s theorem cannot be employed anymore, and the characterisation of the future thermal cone is no longer given by Theorem
4
. However, the set of Gibbs-preserving matrices still forms a convex set
Mazurek and Horodecki 2018
;
Mazurek 2019
, and the extreme points of the future thermal cone can be constructed by employing the following lemma:

Lemma 9

(Lemma 12 of Ref.
Lostaglio et al. 2018
)
.

Given
\boldsymbol{p}
, consider the following distributions
\boldsymbol{p}^{\,\boldsymbol{\pi}}\in{\cal T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
constructed for each permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in{\cal S}_{d}
. For
i\in\left\{1,\dots,d\right\}
:

1.

Let
x_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}e^{-\beta E_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-1}\left(j\right)}}
and
y_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=f^{\,\beta}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(x_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)
.

2.

Define
p^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{i}:=y^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}(i)}-y^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}(i)-1}
, with
y_{0}:=0
.

Then, all extreme points of
{\cal T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
have the form
\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
for some
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
. In particular, this implies that
{\cal T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
has at most
d!
extremal points.

The above lemma allows one to characterise the future thermal cone of
\boldsymbol{p}
by constructing states
\boldsymbol{p}^{\,\boldsymbol{\pi}}
for each
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
, and taking their convex hull. It is worth mentioning that

To wrap up the considerations of the geometry of thermal cones, let us go back, once again, to the analogy between the thermal cones and special relativity. Consider that given a specific division of space-time into future, past and space-like regions, one is able to recover the specific event generating it, which we may refer to as Present. Concisely – there is a one-to-one relation between events and the divisions of space-time they generate. The situation is exactly reflected for thermal cones with

V
Volume of thermal cones

In the previous sections, we characterised and discussed the behaviour of thermal cones by introducing explicit constructions of the past, the future and the incomparable region. It is then natural to ask what is the role played by their volumes in quantifying the resourcefulness of different states
Cunden et al. 2020
;
Cunden et al. 2021
. Thus, for a
d
-dimensional probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}

we define the relative volumes of its thermal cones as

\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p}):=\frac{V\left[\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})\right]}{V(\Delta_{d})}\quad\text{with}\quad i\in\{\emptyset,-,+\},

(35)

where
V
denotes the volume measured using the Euclidean metric.

We start our analysis of volumes of thermal cones by presenting an operational interpretation in terms of guessing probabilities for the future and past of a given state subject to a thermal evolution; such interpretation provides a solid basis for presenting the aforementioned volumes as resource-theoretic monotones. Subsequently, we proceed to an in-depth analysis of the volumes with a particular focus on their behaviour as a function of the inverse temperature
\beta
.
Finally, we explain how to modify the analysis to obtain meaningful volumes of entanglement cones.

V.1
Interpretation

Consider a task of predicting the future, which roughly translates to guessing a state
\boldsymbol{q}
by having knowledge that it has originated from a given prior state
\boldsymbol{p}
. In this case, the probability of correctly guessing a state that is
\epsilon
-distant from
{\boldsymbol{q}}
is given by

\text{Pr}\left({\boldsymbol{q}},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})\right)=\frac{V(\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{q}))}{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})},

(36)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{q})
is an
\epsilon
-ball centred at
\boldsymbol{q}
. We may get rid of the dependence on
\epsilon
and
\boldsymbol{q}
by taking the ratio for two different states
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{2}
,

\frac{\text{Pr}\left[\boldsymbol{q},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})\right]}{\text{Pr}\left[\boldsymbol{q},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})\right]}=\frac{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})}{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})}.

(37)

Thus, the ratio of volumes yields a relative probability of guessing the future of two different states. In particular, if
\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})
, then also
\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})\subset\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})
and in consequence

\frac{\text{Pr}\left[\boldsymbol{q},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})\right]}{\text{Pr}\left[\boldsymbol{q},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})\right]}>1.

(38)

This can be understood as follows – as the evolution of a system progresses, the future becomes easier to guess or, in other words, more predictable.

In complete analogy, we may define a game in which, instead of guessing – or predicting – the future of a state
\boldsymbol{p}
, one has to guess its past. For such a game, it is easy to show that

\frac{\text{Pr}\left[\boldsymbol{q},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})\right]}{\text{Pr}\left[\boldsymbol{q},\epsilon\,|\,\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})\right]}=\frac{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})}{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})},

(39)

so that the ratio of volumes yields the relative probability of guessing the past. Given
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})
we have
{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{1})}/{\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2})}<1
, which simply means that as the evolution towards equilibrium progresses, one finds the past of a given state harder and harder to guess correctly.

V.2
Properties

We now show that the volumes of the thermal cones are thermodynamic monotones, i.e., functions of a state that decrease under thermal operations.

Theorem 15
.

The relative volumes
\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}
and
1-\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}=\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}+\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{\emptyset}
are thermodynamic monotones. Moreover, both monotones are faithful, taking the value 0 only when applied to the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

Proof.

One can straightforwardly show that both quantities decrease monotonically under thermodynamic operations. This is a simple consequence of the fact that for
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
connected via a thermal operation, we have
\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})\subset\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
and
\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})\subset\mathcal{T}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})
which automatically implies
\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})\leq\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
and
\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})\geq\mathcal{V}^{\,\beta}_{-}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

In order to demonstrate its faithfulness, first note that every state that is not thermal can be mapped to a thermal state, thus showing that
1-\mathcal{V}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=0
. Similarly, the Gibbs state cannot be mapped via thermal operations to anything else than itself, thus
\mathcal{V}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=0
. Now, in order to show that both monotones are non-zero for any state different from the Gibbs state, it suffices to demonstrate that
\mathcal{V}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})>0
for any
\boldsymbol{p}\neq\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. It is enough to consider, without loss of generality, a state
\boldsymbol{p}
thermalised within a
(d-1)
-dimensional subspace, i.e.,
{p_{i}}/{p_{j}}={\gamma_{i}}/{\gamma_{j}}
for all
i\neq j\neq 1
. For
k\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
, using
\beta
-swaps between levels
1
and
k
defined as
Lostaglio et al. 2018

\left\{p_{1},\,p_{k}\right\}\longmapsto\left\{\frac{\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{1}}p_{1}+p_{k},\,\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{1}}p_{1}\right\},

we generate
d-1
new points shifted from the original state by displacements
(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k})_{i}=\delta_{k}\epsilon_{ik}
defined by the Levi-Civita symbol
\epsilon_{ik}
and
\delta_{k}\neq 0
. The entire set
\{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}\}_{k=2}^{d}
of displacements is linearly independent; therefore, they define a
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex of non-zero volume. Conversely, if we assume that
\delta_{k}=0
for any
k
, we are led to a conclusion that the system is thermalised between levels
1
and
k
and, by transitivity, it must be equal to the Gibbs state
\gamma
, which leads to a contradiction with the initial assumption. Finally, we restore the full generality by noticing that any state
\boldsymbol{q}
contains in its future cone states thermalised in any of the
(d-1)
-dimensional subspaces and, consequently, their entire future cone. Therefore, the non-zero volume of the latter implies the non-zero volume of the former.
∎

The non-full rank state with the largest thermal incomparable region is given by

\boldsymbol{g}=\frac{1}{Z}\left(e^{-\beta E_{1}},...,e^{-\beta E_{d-1}},0\right)\quad\text{where}\quad Z=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}e^{-\beta E_{i}}.

(40)

Consider an arbitrary non-full rank state
\boldsymbol{p}

V.3
The volumes of entanglement cones

Finally, we will briefly discuss the general qualitative aspects of the volumes of entanglement cones based on the numerical considerations. Detailed formal methods used in order to obtain them are discussed in Appendix

Naturally, depending on the context, the probability distribution

The distribution of Schmidt coefficients induced by the uniform Haar measure in the space of pure bipartite states is significantly different from the flat distribution in the probability simplex
\Delta_{d}

VI
Outlook

In this paper, we have investigated the structure of the thermodynamic arrow of time by dividing the space of energy-incoherent states into the past, the future, and the incomparable region, in analogy with the future, past, and spacelike regions of Minkowski spacetime, respectively. These regions of the probability simplex, called thermal cones, encode in a natural way the achievability of state transformations under thermal operations. In particular, we considered the energy-incoherent states in the presence of a thermal bath at a finite temperature and in the limit of temperature going to infinity. The incomparable and past thermal cones were fully characterised and carefully analysed in both regimes. Furthermore, we identified the volumes of the thermal cones as thermodynamic monotones and performed detailed analysis of their behaviour under different conditions. Our results can also be applied directly to the study of entanglement, as the order defined on the set of bipartite pure entangled states by local operations and classical communication is the opposite of the thermodynamic order in the limit of infinite temperature. In this context, the future thermal cone becomes the past for entanglement, and the past becomes the future. Moreover, a similar extension to coherence resource theory can also be drawn.

There are quite a few research directions that one may want to take to generalise and extend the results presented in this paper.
First, one may generalise our analysis beyond the energy-incoherent states to the full space of quantum states, where the available tools are comparatively scarce
Korzekwa 2017
;
Lostaglio et al. 2015
;
Gour et al. 2018
.

As explained in Appendix
D
, for qubit systems one can employ different known techniques to construct coherent thermal cones under both Gibbs-preserving and thermal operations, as shown in Fig.

.

Beyond the qubit case, the problem is still open.

Note that this problem was formally solved in Ref.

Second, a newly introduced notion of continuous thermomajorisation was presented in Ref.
Lostaglio and Korzekwa 2022
;
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
, where the authors obtained the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a Markovian thermal process underlining a given state transformation. A natural task then is to provide an equivalent construction of the past and incomparable regions for that memoryless thermodynamic ordering. Finally, we point out a possible technical extension of our results. Our investigation was performed in the spirit of single subsystems, but a possible extension comprising many non-interacting subsystems (possibly independent and identically distributed) could be done by defining a proper function to analyse the behaviour of the thermal cones.

A.1
Infinite temperature

A.1.1
Tangent vector

The first notion we will need in the proof is that of a
tangent probability vector
, called the tangent vector for short, which will prove to be an essential ingredient.

Let
\Delta_{d}
be the set of probability vectors of dimension
d
with real entries,
\Delta_{d}=\left\{(p_{1},...,p_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\right\}
, and let us restrict ourselves to vectors ordered in a non-increasing order, i.e.,
p_{i}\geq p_{i+1}
. In order to avoid complication we assume, without loss generality, that all
p_{i}\neq p_{j}
. In what follows, we will denote probability vectors by bold lowercase letters
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{d}
and their corresponding cumulative counterparts by bold uppercase vectors
\boldsymbol{P}:P_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{i}
with
i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
. For any vector
\boldsymbol{p}
we introduce a tangent vector
\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{p})\equiv\boldsymbol{t}\in\Delta_{d}
by imposing that all its components except the first and the last are equal,
t_{i}=t_{j}
for all
1<i<j<d
; furthermore, we require that the cumulative vector
\boldsymbol{T}
agrees with the vector
\boldsymbol{P}
in at most two consecutive points, i.e.,
T_{i}=P_{i}
and
T_{j}>P_{j}
for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,d-1\right\}\backslash\{i\}
or
T_{j}=P_{j}
for
j\in\left\{i,i+1\right\}
and
T_{j}>P_{j}
elsewhere. The two imposed conditions follow the intuition of tangency and, by construction, satisfy the majorisation relation
\boldsymbol{t}\prec\boldsymbol{p}
. Furthermore, note that
T_{0}=P_{0}=0
and
T_{d}=P_{d}=1
and thus are naturally excluded from the considerations.

Indeed, assuming equality between
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{P}
at exactly two consecutive points restricts the tangent vectors to a set of
d
unique probability vectors
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
defined as

\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{p})\equiv\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}=\left(t^{(n)}_{1},p_{n},...,p_{n},t^{(n)}_{d}\right),

(44)

for
1\leq n\leq d
with the first and last components given by

t_{1}^{(n)}=\sum_{i}^{n-1}p_{i}-(n-2)p_{n}\quad,\quad t_{d}^{(n)}=1-t^{(n)}_{1}-(d-2)p_{n}.

(45)

Observe that the tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
that agree with the Lorenz curve of
\boldsymbol{p}
at two successive points can be used to construct all possible tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{t}
that satisfy the condition of agreement at at least a single point,
T_{i}=P_{i}
. Indeed, consider a vector
\boldsymbol{t}(\lambda,i)\equiv\boldsymbol{t}=(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{t}^{(i)}+\lambda\boldsymbol{t}^{(i+1)}
. Direct calculation shows that it is tangent at just one point,
T_{i}=\lambda T_{i}^{(i)}+(1-\lambda)T_{i}^{(i+1)}=P_{i}
. Similarly we arrive at
T_{j}>P_{j}
for
j\neq i
and
0<\lambda<1
.

Thus, starting with a discrete set of
d
tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
we recover the entire continuous family of tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{t}(\lambda,i)
. Indeed, this argument can be further formalised by considering the Lorenz curves
f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)
and
f_{\boldsymbol{t}}(x)
. Considering the left and right derivatives of the former,
\lim_{x\rightarrow\frac{i}{d}_{-}}f^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)=d\cdot p_{i}
and
\lim_{x\rightarrow\frac{i}{d}_{+}}f^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)=d\cdot p_{i+1}
, we obtain the extremal slope values for the tangent lines at the
i
-th elbow. Now, considering the second Lorenz curve by construction we have that
f_{\boldsymbol{t}}(i/d)=f_{\boldsymbol{p}}(i/d)
and its derivative at this point,
f^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{t}}(i/d)=d\left[(1-\lambda)p_{i}+\lambda p_{i+1}\right]
, span all values between the extremal slope values
d\cdot p_{i}
and
d\cdot p_{i+1}
, therefore exhausting the family of possible tangent lines at the
i
-th elbow.

A.1.2
Lattice

Lattices provide a setting within which it is natural to represent the precedence or succession of elements within a given set. In particular, they can be used to equip a given set with a time-like structure, as provided by the definition of a lattice (see Definition
2
).

It is well known that the partially ordered set
(\Delta_{d},\succ)
of
d
-dimensional probability vectors with real entries in non-increasing order under majorisation forms a lattice
Cicalese and Vaccaro 2002
;
Korzekwa 2017
. In particular, within this setting, the join
\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}
can be interpreted as the last common past point of
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
. Similarly, the meet
\boldsymbol{p}\wedge\boldsymbol{q}
can be seen as the first common future point of the pair
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}
. The procedure to obtain the join and meet has been illustrated in Ref.
Korzekwa 2017
, and since part of our proof relies on the existence of the join, we will now review the algorithm used to construct it.

To construct the join of
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, we start with a probability vector
\boldsymbol{r}^{(0)}
with elements defined by

r_{i}^{(0)}=\max\left\{\boldsymbol{P}_{i},\boldsymbol{Q}_{i}\right\}-\max\left\{\boldsymbol{P}_{i-1},\boldsymbol{Q}_{i-1}\right\}.

(46)

At this stage, it may occur that the entries of
\boldsymbol{r}^{(0)}
are not ordered non-increasingly. However, it is possible to arrive at a properly ordered probability vector
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}
defining the actual join in no more than
d-1
steps. In each step
k\geq 0
we define
N\geq 2
as the smallest point of increase between two consecutive components of the probability vector
r^{(k)}
, that is,
r^{(k)}_{N}>r^{(k)}_{N-1}
. Next,
M\leq N-1
is defined in such a way that by introducing constant probabilities for the entries with
i\in\{M,\ldots,N\}
the growth is eliminated. It is done by requiring that

r^{(k)}_{M}\geq\frac{\sum_{i=M}^{N}r_{i}^{(k)}}{N-M+1}=:a_{k}.

(47)

Thus, the next iterative step
\boldsymbol{r}^{(k+1)}
is defined by setting its components as

r_{i}^{(k+1)}=\begin{cases}a_{k}&\text{for }i\in\{M,\ldots,N\}\\
r_{i}^{(k)}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(48)

This construction is repeated until for some
k^{\prime}
the probability vector
\boldsymbol{r}^{(k^{\prime})}\equiv\boldsymbol{r}
is ordered non-increasingly; in this way we get the proper join.

A.1.3
Incomparable region and the boundaries

As a final piece of information needed to understand the proofs, we introduce the definition of the boundary of the past cone.

Definition 5

(Boundary of the past thermal cone)
.

Consider a
d
-dimensional energy incoherent state
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
with
d\geq 3
. We define the boundary of the past thermal cone as the set of probability vectors
\boldsymbol{q}\succ\boldsymbol{p}
for which the cumulative vector
\boldsymbol{Q}
is equal to the cumulative vector
\boldsymbol{P}
at least one point. In other words,
\boldsymbol{P}_{j}<\boldsymbol{Q}_{j}
for some proper subset of the indices
j
and
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{i}
for all other indices.

One may define the boundary of the future cone in a similar way by changing the direction of the inequalities. By considering the common part of the boundary between future and past, one comes to a simple observation:

Observation 19

(Common point of future and past cones)
.

A point that lies simultaneously at the common part of the boundary between the future and the past must fulfil
\forall_{i}P_{i}=Q_{i}
. Therefore, for
\beta=0
we have the equality of
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
up to a permutation, giving a total of
d!
common points between the future and the past of any vector
\boldsymbol{p}
.

Equipped with the notion of tangent vectors
\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}
, the join
\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}
and the boundary of the past cone, we are now prepared to tackle the Lemma
5
concerning the incomparable region, and this is done by proving the following result:

Lemma 20
.

Consider
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\Delta_{d}
and assume that
\boldsymbol{p}\nsucc\boldsymbol{q}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{q}
belongs to the incomparable region of
\boldsymbol{p}
,
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})

\boldsymbol{t}
tangent to
\boldsymbol{p}
,
\boldsymbol{q}\in{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{t})
, with

\boldsymbol{t}\equiv\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{p};\lambda,n)=\lambda\boldsymbol{t}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{p})+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{t}^{(n+1)}(\boldsymbol{p}),

for some
n\in\{1,d-1\}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]

Proof.

To prove the “if” direction, we take a probability vector lying in the interior of the future of the tangent vector,
\boldsymbol{q}\in\operatorname{int}[{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{t})]
. Then, to prove that
\boldsymbol{q}\in{\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})

\boldsymbol{q}\nsucc\boldsymbol{p}
. By construction, we have
T_{k}\geq P_{k}
, for every
k\neq n
, with equality when
k=n
, so
P_{n}=T_{n}>Q_{n}
with the equality excluded by putting
\boldsymbol{q}
in the interior of the future. Consequently,
P_{n}>Q_{n}
, and thus
\boldsymbol{q}\nsucc\boldsymbol{p}
and by the initial assumption,
\boldsymbol{p}\nsucc\boldsymbol{q}
. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{q}\in{\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

In order to demonstrate the “only if” direction, let us take an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{q}\in{\cal T}_{\emptyset}(\boldsymbol{p})
and recall that there always exists the last common past point for
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
called join,
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{p}\vee\boldsymbol{q}
. From the construction of the join
\boldsymbol{r}
, it is found that the entries of the cumulative distribution
\boldsymbol{R}
will be divided into three subsets, namely points common with
\boldsymbol{P}

\boldsymbol{Q}
and the ones lying above either, that is
I_{p}:=\left\{i:0<i<d,\,R_{i}=P_{i}\right\}
,
I_{q}:=\left\{i:0<i<d,\,R_{i}=Q_{i}\right\}
and
J:=\left\{0<j<d,\,:R_{j}>\max(P_{j},Q_{j})\right\}
, respectively. In particular, looking at equation (
48

M-1\in I_{p}
and
N+1\in I_{q}
, or
M-1\in I_{p}
and
N+1\in I_{q}
or by invoking geometric intuition, endpoints of the flat fragments of
\boldsymbol{R}
will join
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
. Thus, at each step, the sets
I_{p}
and
I_{q}
will be non-empty.
By this argument, we may choose any index
i\in I_{p}
and construct a tangent vector
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
for the join
\boldsymbol{r}
at the
i
-th elbow.

\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\equiv\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{r};\mu,i)=\mu\boldsymbol{t}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{r})+(1-\mu)\boldsymbol{t}^{(i+1)}(\boldsymbol{r})

(49)

for any
\mu\in[0,1]

\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\succ\boldsymbol{r}\succ\boldsymbol{q}

\boldsymbol{p}

(50)

∎

The last step necessary in order to demonstrate the Lemma

\boldsymbol{t}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{p})
and
\boldsymbol{t}^{(i+1)}(\boldsymbol{p})

\bigcup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{p};\lambda,i))=\text{conv}\left[\mathcal{T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{t}^{(i)}\right)\cup\mathcal{T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{t}^{(i+1)}\right)\right]

which is always a proper probability distribution ordered non-increasingly,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\downarrow}
, therefore providing a proper Lorenz curve.

Following a similar procedure for the future cone
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{P})
leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\leq\frac{1-P_{k}}{1-Q_{k}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{-1}-\mathcal{P}^{-1}P_{k}\geq 1-Q_{k}

(59)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Q_{k}\geq\mathcal{P}^{-1}P_{k}+(1-\mathcal{P}^{-1})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{q}\succ\hat{\boldsymbol{p}},

with the second auxiliary distribution

\hat{p}_{i}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}^{-1}p^{\downarrow}_{1}+(1-\mathcal{P}^{-1})&\text{for }i=1,\\
\mathcal{P}^{-1}p^{\downarrow}_{i}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(60)

In contrast to the case of the past cone, the distribution
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}
in this formulation is not ordered non-increasingly beyond a certain value of
\mathcal{P}
. At first glance, one might think that reordering should solve the problem; however, it would be equivalent to a decrease in the probabilistic future with decreasing
\mathcal{P}
, which creates a contradiction. The solution is provided by noting that Vidal’s criterion deals with rescaled entries of the Lorenz curve rather than the probabilities
per se
. Therefore, the Lorenz curve for
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}
should remain convex for all values of
\mathcal{P}
without the need for reordering.

Consider the following critical values of
\mathcal{P}
, namely,

\mathcal{P}_{n}=(n-1)p^{\downarrow}_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}p^{\downarrow}_{i}+1,

(61)

for which the first
n
entries of the distribution
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}
will not be ordered non-increasingly, resulting in an improper Lorenz curve. The resulting non-convexity is controlled by replacing

\left\{\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{n}\right\}\rightarrow\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{p}_{i}\left\{1,\ldots 1\right\},

(62)

which ensures that
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\downarrow}
.

This way, the auxiliary ordered distributions
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}
, together with the construction for the deterministic majorisation cones presented in Appendix
A
, provide the full construction of the probabilistic cones, with the additional cautionary note that the role of future and past majorisation cones is reversed when we consider entanglement and coherence theories.

Appendix C
Volumes of entanglement majorisation cones

Consider a uniform Haar distribution of pure states in a composed space
|{\psi}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{N}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{M}
with
N\leq M
. The partial tracing induces a measure in the space of reduced states,
\rho=\operatorname{Tr}_{2}|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|
, characterised by the distribution of eigenvalues
\Lambda=\left\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\right\}
of the reduced state
Życzkowski and Sommers 2001
:

P_{N,M}(\Lambda)=C_{N,M}\delta\left(1-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}^{M-N}\theta(\lambda_{i})\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2},

(63)

where
\delta
and
\theta
are the Dirac delta and Heavyside step functions, respectively. The normalisation constant is given by

C_{N,M}=\frac{\Gamma(NM)}{\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\Gamma(M-j)\Gamma(N-j+1)},

(64)

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. Before we continue with our discussion, let us first understand the role played by all factors in Eq. (
63
). As previously mentioned,
C_{N,M}
is the normalisation. The delta function ensures that the spectrum sums to one (is normalised), whereas the step function will guarantee that it is positive. Now, notice that the first product (capital pi notation) essentially does not influence the behaviour of the distribution for
N=M
and introduces the repelling of the faces of the probability simplex otherwise, as it goes to zero whenever
\lambda_{i}=0
for any
i
. The second product is responsible for the repelling from distributions with any two entries equal, since it goes to zero whenever
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
for any
i\neq j
.

Figure 16:

Isovolumetric sets for entanglement
3\times M
bipartite systems
. The density
P_{3,M}(\Lambda)
of Schmidt coefficients of pure states for qutrit-quMit systems depends heavily on the dimension
M
of the second system. Panels (a-d) and (e-h) present the isovolumetric lines for past, incomparable and future regions and the density of the states for

the density
P_{3,M}
is more and more concentrated around the regions close to the centre [compare (d and (h)]. This affects the subset of states with large future volume, making it smaller [(c) and (g)] as well as the set of states with large past volume, enlarging it [(a) with (e)].

Sampling from the
P_{N,M}
distribution, ordinarily done by generating state vectors
|{\psi}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{NM}
which would be computationally prohibitive for large
M
, can be achieved using only
O(N)
random numbers for any dimension of the secondary system. It has been demonstrated in Ref.
Cunden et al. 2020
that the distribution
P_{N,M}
is precisely the Laguerre unitary ensemble generated by Wishart matrices of size
N
and parameter
M

Giacomo Livan 2018
and, in turn, generated using a tridiagonal method containing only
O(N)
random real numbers
Dumitriu and Edelman 2002
, which indeed allows one to study the
P_{N,M}
distributions for arbitrary high-dimensional ancillary systems.

The procedure for generating the isovolumetric lines for the majorisation cones with a given distribution,
P_{N,M}
, proceeds as follows:

1.

Generate a sample of
n
sets of eigenvalues
\{\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}\}
taken from the distribution
P_{N,M}
using the tridiagonal method.

2.

Consider regularly spaced grid of points
S
in a single chamber of the full probability simplex
\Delta_{N}
(e.g.,
p_{1}\geq p_{2},...,p_{d})
in order to avoid repeated counting (achieving
N!
decrease in operations)

3.

For each
\boldsymbol{p}\in S
consider its majorisation cones
\mathcal{T}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})
and divide
S
into
S_{i}\equiv\left\{\Lambda_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})\right\}
.

4.

This way we arrive at the approximations of the volumes of the three regions,

\mathcal{V}_{i}\approx\frac{|S_{i}|}{n},

where
|X|
denotes the number of elements in a set
X
.

We applied this method for
N=3
with
M=3
, displayed in the main text of Fig.
13
, and additionally with
M=6
and
30
, as shown in Fig.
16
. These two cases show the significant dependence of the isovolumetric lines on the size of the environment.

Appendix D
Coherent thermal cones for a two-level system

This appendix explains how to use the results of Refs.
Lostaglio et al. 2015
and
Korzekwa 2017
to construct the future and past thermal cones for a two-level system under thermal and Gibbs-preserving operations.

D.0.1
Coherent thermal cones for thermal operations

Consider initial and target states of a two level system,
\rho
and
\sigma
, with both written in the energy eigenbasis as

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}p&c\\
c&1-p\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}q&d\\
d&1-q\end{pmatrix},

(65)

where
c
and
d
are assumed to be real without loss of generality, which amounts to considering a cross-section of the Bloch ball in the
XZ
plane. Moreover, the thermal ground state occupation of the considered two-level system will be denoted by
\gamma
. It has been shown that for thermal operations, the coherences of the initial and target states have to satisfy the following inequality
Lostaglio et al. 2015
,

d\leq c\frac{\sqrt{[q(1-\gamma)-\gamma(1-p)][p(1-\gamma)-\gamma(1-q)]}}{|p-\gamma|}.

(66)

Thus, we find the boundary of the future thermal cone by saturating Eq. (
66
), and solving it for
q
we obtain the achievable ground state occupation as a function of target coherence
d
,

\displaystyle q_{1}(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\gamma-p)\sqrt{c^{2}(1-2\gamma)^{2}+4\gamma d^{2}(1-\gamma)}}{2\gamma c(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(p-\gamma)-2\gamma p(1-\gamma)}{2\gamma c(\gamma-1)}.

(67)

Therefore, the coherent future thermal cone is given by the region delimited by Eq. (
D.0.1
) from one side and a line segment connecting
(-c,p)
and
(c,p)
. To characterise the coherent past thermal cone, it will be convenient to introduce a number
d_{\text{cross}}\geq 0
defined by the relation
d_{\text{cross}}^{2}+q(d_{\text{cross}})^{2}=1
.

The coherent past thermal cone is generically composed of two disjoint regions. The first region is contained between a line segment connecting the points
(c,p)
and
(d_{\text{cross}},p)
, the curve
q_{1}(d)
for
d\in[c,d_{\text{cross}}]
and the boundary of the Bloch ball, together with its reflection with respect to the
Z
-axis.
The second one is obtained in a similar manner by focussing on the past state rather than the target, and thus by solving Eq. (
66
) with interchanges
p\leftrightarrow q
and
c\leftrightarrow d
. This results in

\displaystyle q_{2}(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma c^{2}+\sqrt{c^{2}(p-\gamma)^{2}\left[(1-2\gamma)^{2}d^{2}-4c^{2}(\gamma-1)\gamma\right]}}{2\left[d^{2}+(\gamma-1)\gamma c^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\>\>\>\>+\frac{d^{2}[p-\gamma-2\gamma p(1-\gamma)]}{2\left[d^{2}-(1-\gamma)\gamma c^{2}\right]},

(68)

with
d\in[d_{\textrm{min}},d_{\textrm{max}}]
, where
d_{\textrm{min}}
and
d_{\textrm{max}}
are real positive solutions of equation
q(d)^{2}+d^{2}=1
, such that
d_{\text{cross}}\leq d_{\text{min}}\leq d_{\text{max}}
. However, for large values of coherence
c
, we note that this second region may not appear at all. Finally, the incomparable region
\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\rho)
is obtained by subtracting the past and future cones from the entire Bloch ball.

D.0.2
Coherent thermal cones for Gibbs-preserving operations

Consider a parametrisation of qubit states
\rho
in the Bloch sphere representation,

\rho=\frac{\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\rho}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2},

(69)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
denotes the vector of Pauli matrices. The Bloch vectors of the starting state
\rho
, target state
\rho^{\prime}
and the Gibbs state
\gamma
are given by:

\boldsymbol{r}_{\rho}=(x,y,z),\quad\boldsymbol{r}_{\rho^{\prime}}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}),\quad\boldsymbol{r}_{\gamma}=(0,0,\zeta),

(70)

where the
z
coordinate of the Gibbs state can be related to the partition function
Z
by
\zeta=2Z^{-1}-1\geq 0
.

According to Ref.
Korzekwa 2017
, there exists a GP quantum channel
{\cal E}
such that
{\cal E}(\rho)=\rho^{\prime}
if and only if
R_{\pm}(\rho)\geq R_{\pm}(\rho^{\prime})
for both signs, where
R_{\pm}(\rho)=\delta(\rho)\pm\zeta z
and

\delta(\rho):=\sqrt{(z-\zeta)^{2}+(x^{2}+y^{2})(1-\zeta^{2})}.

(71)

Consequently, the future thermal cone
{\cal T}_{+}(\rho)
of any qubit state
\rho
under GP operations can be directly constructed from the above result. For a generic qubit state
\rho
, we first orient the Bloch sphere so that its
XZ
plane coincides with the plane containing
\rho
and a thermal state
\gamma
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{r}_{\rho}=(x,0,z)
. Then, define two disks,
D_{1}(\rho)
and
D_{2}(\rho)
with corresponding circles
C_{1}(\rho)
and
C_{2}(\rho)
, of radii

R_{1}(\rho)=\frac{R_{-}(\rho)+\zeta^{2}}{1-\zeta^{2}},\quad R_{2}(\rho)=\frac{R_{+}(\rho)-\zeta^{2}}{1-\zeta^{2}},

(72)

centred at

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}(\rho)&=&[0,0,\zeta(1+R_{1}(\rho))],\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{2}(\rho)&=&[0,0,\zeta(1-R_{2}(\rho))].\end{array}

(73)

Therefore, the future thermal cone under GP quantum channels is given by the intersection of two disks of radii
R_{1}(\rho)
and
R_{2}(\rho)
centred at
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}(\rho)
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{2}(\rho)
,
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\rho)=D_{1}(\rho)\cap D_{2}(\rho)
.

The incomparable region is given by mixed conditions, i.e.,
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\rho)
if and only if
R_{\pm}(\rho)\geq R_{\pm}(\rho^{\prime})
and
R_{\mp}(\rho)<R_{\mp}(\rho^{\prime})
, or in terms of the disks given beforehand,
\mathcal{T}_{\emptyset}(\rho)=D_{1}(\rho)\cap D_{2}(\rho)\backslash\mathcal{T}_{+}(\rho)
. Finally, the past cone
\mathcal{T}_{-}(\rho)
can be easily given by subtracting the future cone and the incomparable region from the entire Bloch ball.
