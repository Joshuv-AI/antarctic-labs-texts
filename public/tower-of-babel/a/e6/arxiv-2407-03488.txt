Failures of Compositionality: A Short Note on Cohomology, Sheafification and Lavish Presheaves
Benjamin Merlin Bumpus, Matteo Capucci, James Fairbanks, Daniel Rosiak

Abstract
In many sciences one often builds large systems out of smaller constituent parts. Mathematically, to study these systems, one can attach data to the component pieces via a functor F F . This is of great practical use if F F admits a compositional structure which is compatible with that of the system under study (i.e. if the local data defined on the pieces can be combined into global data). However, sometimes this does not occur. Thus one can ask: (1) Does F F fail to be compositional? (2) If so, can this failure be quantified? and (3) Are there general tools to fix failures of compositionality? The kind of compositionality we study in this paper is one in which one never fails to combine local data into global data. This is formalized via the understudied notion of what we call a lavish presheaf: one that satisfies the existence requirement of the sheaf condition, but not uniqueness. Adapting Čech cohomology to presheaves , we show that a presheaf has trivial zeroth presheaf -Čech cohomology if and only if it is lavish . In this light, cohomology is a measure of the failure of compositionality. The key contribution of this paper is to show that, in some instances, cohomology – a canonical measure of non-compositionality – can itself display compositional structure. Formally, we show that, given any Abelian presheaf F : 𝖢 ​ 𝗈𝗉 → 𝖠 F\colon\mathsf{C}\op\to\mathsf{A} and any Grothendieck pretopology J J , if F F is flasque and separated, then the zeroth cohomology functor H 0 ​ ( − , F ) : 𝖢 ​ 𝗈𝗉 → 𝖠 H^{0}(-,F)\colon\mathsf{C}\op\to\mathsf{A} is lavish . This result follows from the observation that, for separated presheaves, H 0 ​ ( − , F ) H^{0}(-,F) can be written as a cokernel of the unit of the adjunction given by sheafification. This last fact is of further independent interest since it shows that cohomology is a measure of “distance” between separated presheaves and their closest sheaves (their sheafifications). On the other hand, the fact that H 0 ​ ( − , F ) H^{0}(-,F) is a lavish presheaf has unexpected algorithmic consequences.

Discussion

3.1
.

As applied category theorists we are often interested in systems (broadly construed) which display some sort of compositional structure. We then study their various aspects via functors which collect relevant information about the system at hand. To be of practical use, one defines such functors
F
in the hope that they admit a compositional structure which is compatible with that of the system under study. Thus for any such assignment
F
there are three obvious questions that occupy a central role in applied category theory:

1.

Does
F
fail to be compositional?

2.

If so, can this failure be quantified? and

3.

Are there general tools to fix failures of compositionality?

3.2
.

For many in applied category theory, failures of compositionality are witnessed by
laxity
. This statement, which is by now almost folklore, has been recently studied in depth by Puca, Hadzihasanovic, Genovese and Coecke
[
8
]
. They faithfully reduce the question of measuring the failures of compositionality to the more conceptually elegant question of measuring how far a given morphism is from being an isomorphism. One of their contributions is to pursue an invariant-based approach to the question of measuring laxity: they introduce certain posets – the zeroth and first homotopy posets – which collect information on laxity (or lack thereof).

3.3
.

In contrast, in this paper we study a lack of compositionality whose flavor is absolutely standard in other areas of mathematics such as (applied) algebraic topology, algebraic geometry and differential geometry
[
6
]
. Our emphasis is born out recent developments in sheaf theoretic tools for the study of
algorithmic
compositionality
[
1
]
. Indeed, as was recently observed
[
1
]
, many computational problems can be cast as presheaves which take inputs objects to sets of solutions (solution spaces). Examples include
\Hcoloring
,
\vc
,
\clique
,
\planardeletion
,
\chordaldeletion
and many others
[
2
]
. As it turns out
[
1
]
, if one is working in a sufficiently well-behaved category (i.e. an adhesive category) and one is equipped with a subcanonical topology, then the property of being a sheaf is sufficient to guarantee fast
7
7

7

Here we are working within the realm of multivariate or parameterized complexity theory whereby, given some secondary measurement
k
on the input, one says that an algorithm runs in
\fpt
time parameterized by
k
if the algorithm scales linearly in the total input size, but only boundedly in the parameter. For example, if we are given an
n
-vertex graph obtained as colimit of a diagram
d
whose constituent pieces are all graphs on at most
k
vertices, then an algorithm running in time
f(k)\cdot n^{\Oa(1)}
(where
f
is any computable function not depending on either
n
or
k
) is considered efficient within this paradigm. For more on this topic we refer the reader to standard textbooks in parameterized complexity
[
4
,
5
,
3
]
.
dynamic programming algorithms for the associated decision problem
8
8

8

Given a functor taking inputs to solution spaces, the associated decision problem asks: “is the solution space non-empty?”
.

3.4
.

In this algorithmically-minded context, one might be forgiven for thinking that detecting and quantifying a failure of compositionality is intellectually satisfying, but of little practical use, if it is not accompanied with general methods for fixing such failures. By this we mean that, given a presheaf
F\colon\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{FinSet}
on a site
(\mathsf{C},K)
, we desire to construct a new presheaf
\tilde{F}\colon\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{FinSet}
such that the following three conditions are satisfied:

(C1)

\tilde{F}
is a
sheaf
on
(\mathsf{C},K)
and

(C2)

for all
x\in\mathsf{C}
,
\tilde{F}(x)=\emptyset
if and only if
F(x)=\emptyset
(i.e. the associated decision problems agree) and

(C3)

there is a computable function
g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
|\tilde{F}x|\leq g(|Fx|)
for all
x\in\mathsf{C}
(i.e. solution spaces grow boundedly).

This is nothing other than a computational reduction (a parameterized one, for that matter, where
g
is ensuring the boundedness of the relevant parameters), but cast in the language of (pre-)sheaves.

3.5
.

Sheafification, the canonical method for associating a sheaf to every presheaf
[
10
]
, can be seen to
not satisfy

9
9

9

Indeed one should not expect it to: since there are problems that are known to be intractable parameterized by tree-width, unless the
W
-hierarchy collapses, no general method can exist to turn
any
presheaf into a sheaf satisfying the above requirements.
the conditions
(C1)
,
(C2)
and
(C3)
and thus we are left in need for radically new ideas. Furthermore, in writing the present note, we observed that the first of the three conditions mentioned in Paragraph
3.4
can be weakened: instead of requiring
\tilde{F}
to be a sheaf, one can simply require it to be a
lavish
presheaf. This follows from the observation that the algorithm for deciding sheaves presented in
[
1
]
yields the correct answer not only on sheaves, but actually on any lavish presheaf.
10
10

10

As already observed by Spivak (personal communication), lavish presheaves are somehow more natural in algorithmic contexts where uniqueness of a solution is often of secondary importance to its existence.

3.6
.

All of the aforementioned observations allow us to conclude that if
F\colon\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Ab}
is any flasque and separated presheaf on a sufficiently nice site
(\mathsf{C},K)
(i.e. one where
\mathsf{C}
is adhesive and
K
is subcanonical), then, since
H^{0}(-,F)
is lavish (Theorem
2.12
), there is a fast (
\fpt
-time) algorithm to determine
H^{0}(x,F)
on any given object
x
and cover
U
. This is a very surprising result since it amounts to an efficient algorithm for computing the obstructions to compositionality.

3.7
.

This result does not directly address the aforementioned desiderata of Paragraph
3.4
, and moreover, although flasqueness turns up often in fields such as algebraic geometry
[
11
]
, it remains to be studied which natural algorithmic problems can be encoded as
flasque
presheaves. Despite this fact, this note makes it clear that:

•

cohomology can be adapted to measure the failure of lavishness;

•

presheaf
cohomology and flasque presheaves are severely understudied and appear to be a rich area for further results; and

•

investigating the cohomology of computational problems is fruitful and completely understudied.
