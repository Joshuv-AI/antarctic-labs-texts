The role of antisymmetric functions in nonlocal equations
Serena Dipierro, Giorgio Poggesi, Jack Thompson, Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
. We use a Hopf-type lemma for antisymmetric super-solutions to the Dirichlet problem for the fractional Laplacian with zero-th order terms, in combination with the method of moving planes, to prove symmetry for the semilinear fractional parallel surface problem . That is, we prove that non-negative solutions to semilinear Dirichlet problems for the fractional Laplacian in a bounded open set Ω ⊂ ℝ n \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} must be radially symmetric if one of their level surfaces is parallel to the boundary of Ω \Omega ; in turn, Ω \Omega must be a ball. Furthermore, we discuss maximum principles and the Harnack inequality for antisymmetric functions in the fractional setting and provide counter-examples to these theorems when only ‘local’ assumptions are imposed on the solutions. The construction of these counter-examples relies on an approximation result that states that ‘all antisymmetric functions are locally antisymmetric and s s -harmonic up to a small error’.

35B50, 35N25, 35B06

1.
Introduction and results

In this paper, we are concerned with maximum principles for antisymmetric solutions to linear, nonlocal partial differential equations (
PDE
) where the nonlocal term is given by the fractional Laplacian and with the application of such maximum principles to the method of moving planes.

Since its introduction by Alexandrov
[
Ale62
,
Ale62a
]
and the
subsequent refinement by Serrin
[
Ser71
]
, the method of moving planes has become a standard technique for the analysis of overdetermined
PDE
, and for proving symmetry and monotonicity results for solutions, see
[
GNN79
,
GNN81
,
DS07
,
FK08
,
BD13
,
FW14
,
SS15
,
FW16
]
. In recent years, nonlocal
PDE
, particularly the ones involving the fractional Laplacian, have received a lot of attention, due to their consolidated ability to model complex physical phenomena, as well as their rich analytic theory. The method of moving planes has been also adapted in several works so that it may be applied to nonlocal problems, see

[
FJ15
,
BMS18
,
CFSW18
,
CFMN18
,
BVDV21
,
CDPPV21
]
.

The method of moving planes typically leads to
considering the difference between a given solution and its reflection, and such a difference is an antisymmetric function. However, in the local case, ‘standard’ maximum principles
can be directly applied to the moving plane method,
without specifically relying on the notion of
antisymmetric functions, since the ingredients required
are all of ‘local’ nature and can disregard the ‘global’ geometric
properties of the objects considered. Instead,
in the nonlocal world, new maximum principles need to be developed which take into account the extra symmetry, to
compensate for global control of the function under consideration,
for example see
[
FJ15
]
.

We will now state our main results.
Let
s\in(0,1)
and
n\geqslant 1
be an integer. The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
is defined by

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=c_{n,s}\operatorname{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy

where

(1.1)

\displaystyle c_{n,s}=\frac{s4^{s}\Gamma\big(\frac{n+2s}{2}\big)}{\Gamma(1-s)}

is a positive normalisation constant and
\operatorname{P.V.}
refers to the Cauchy principle value. Moreover, let
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ s.t. }x_{1}>0\}
and
B_{1}^{+}=B_{1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

Our first result proves that ‘all antisymmetric functions are locally antisymmetric and
s
-harmonic up to a small error’. This is the analogue of
[
DSV17
, Theorem 1]
for antisymmetric functions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
k\in\mathbb{N}
and that
f\in C^{k}(\overline{B_{1}})
is
x_{1}
-antisymmetric. For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exist a smooth
x_{1}
-antisymmetric function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and a real number
R>1
such that
u
is
s
-harmonic in
B_{1}
,
u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}
, and

\displaystyle\|u-f\|_{C^{k}(B_{1})}<\varepsilon.

When we say that
u
is
x_{1}
-antisymmetric we mean that
u(-x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=-u(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In §
2.1
we will give a more general definition of antisymmetry for an arbitrary plane. As an application of Theorem
1.1
, we can construct some counter-examples to the local Harnack inequality and strong maximum principle for antisymmetric
s
-harmonic functions, as follows:

Corollary 1.2
.

There exist
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
such that, for all
C>0
, there exists an
x_{1}
-antisymmetric function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is non-negative and
s
-harmonic in
\Omega
, and

\displaystyle\sup_{\Omega^{\prime}}u>C\inf_{\Omega^{\prime}}u.

Corollary 1.3
.

There exist
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and an
x_{1}
-antisymmetric function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is non-negative and
s
-harmonic in
\Omega
, is equal to zero at a point in
\Omega
, but is not identically zero in
\Omega
.

For our second result, we use
a Hopf-type lemma (see the forthcoming Proposition
1.5
) in combination with the method of moving planes to establish symmetry for the
semilinear fractional parallel surface problem
, which is described in what follows.
Suppose that
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and bounded, and let
B_{R}
denote the ball of radius
R>0
centered at 0. Let
\Omega=G+B_{R}
, where, given sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A+B
denotes the ‘Minkowski sum’ of
A
and
B
, defined as

\displaystyle A+B=\{a+b\text{ s.t. }a\in A,b\in B\}.

Consider the semilinear fractional equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=f(u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\end{array}\right.

with the overdetermined condition

(1.5)

\displaystyle u=c_{0}\qquad\text{on }\partial G,

for some (given)
c_{0}\geqslant 0
. The
semilinear fractional parallel surface problem
asks the following question:
for which
G
does the problem (
1
)-(
1.5
) admit a non-trivial solution? The answer is that
G
must be a ball. More specifically, we have the following
result:

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
G
is a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{1}
boundary. Let
\Omega=G+B_{R}
,
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz, and
c_{0}\geqslant 0
. Furthermore, assume that there exists a non-negative function
u\in C^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is not identically zero and satisfies, in the pointwise sense,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=f(u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\\
u&=c_{0}\hfil\quad\>&\text{on }\partial G.\end{array}\right.

Then
u
is radially symmetric,
u>0
in
\Omega
, and
\Omega
(and hence
G
) is a ball.

In the local setting (i.e.,
s=1
), symmetry and
stability results for (
1.4
) were obtained in
[
CMS15
]
and
[
CMS16
]
.
Such analysis was motivated by the study of invariant isothermic surfaces of a nonlinear nondegenerate fast diffusion equation (see
[
MS10
]
).

More recently in
[
CDPPV21
]
, the nonlocal case
s\in(0,1)
was addressed
and the analogue to Theorem
1.4
, as well as its stability generalization, were proved in the particular case
f\equiv 1
; those results were obtained by making use of the method of moving planes as well as a (quantitative) Hopf-type lemma (see
[
CDPPV21
, formula (3.3)]
), which could be obtained as an application of the boundary Harnack inequality for antisymmetric
s
-harmonic functions proved in
[
CDPPV21
, Lemma 2.1]
. The arguments used in
[
CDPPV21
]
to establish such a boundary Harnack inequality rely on the explicit knowledge of the fractional Poisson kernel for the ball. However, due to the general nonlinear term
f
in (
1.4
), here the method of moving planes leads to considering a linear equation involving the fractional Laplacian but with zero-th order terms for which no Poisson formula is available. To overcome this conceptual difficulty, we provide the following Hopf-type lemma which allows zero-th order terms.

Proposition 1.5
.

Suppose that
c\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{+})
,
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(B_{1})
is
x_{1}
-antisymmetric, and satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }B_{1}^{+},\\
u&\geqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
u&>0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }B_{1}^{+}.\end{array}\right.

Then

(1.12)

\displaystyle\liminf_{h\to 0}\frac{u(he_{1})}{h}>0.

This result has previously been obtained in
[
SV19
, Proposition 2.2]
though the proof uses a different barrier.

Proposition
1.5
establishes that antisymmetric solutions to (
1.5
) must be bounded from below by a positive constant multiple of
x_{1}
close to the origin. At a first glance this is surprising as solutions to (
1.5
) are in general only
s
-Hölder continuous up to the boundary, as proven in
[
RS14
]
. Hence, it would appear more natural to
consider
\liminf_{h\to 0}\frac{u(he_{1})}{h^{s}}
instead of (
1.12
), see for example
[
FJ15
, Proposition 3.3]
. However, the (anti)symmetry of
u
means that Proposition
1.5
is better understood as an interior estimate rather than a boundary estimate.
Indeed, we stress that, differently from the classical Hopf lemma,
Proposition
1.5
is not concerned with the growth of a solution from a boundary point, but mostly with the growth from a reflection point in the antisymmetric setting (notice specifically that (
1.12
) provides a linear growth from the origin, which is
an interior point). In this sense, the combination of the antisymmetric geometry
of the solution and the fractional nature of the equation
leads to the two structural differences between Proposition
1.5
and several other Hopf-type lemmata available in the literature for the nonlocal
case, namely the linear (instead of
s
-Hölder) growth
and the interior (instead of boundary) type of statement.

A similar result to Proposition
1.5
was obtained in
[
LC19
, Theorem 1]
for the entire half-space instead of
B_{1}^{+}
. This entails that
[
LC19
, Theorem 1]
was only applicable (via the method of moving planes) to symmetry problems posed in all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whereas our result can be applied to problems in bounded domains. Moreover,
[
LC19
, Theorem 1]
is proven by contradiction while the proof of Proposition
1.5
, in a similar vein to the original Hopf lemma, relies on the construction of an appropriate barrier from below, see Lemma
3.1
. Hence, using a barrier approach also allows us to obtain a quantitative version of Proposition
1.5
leading to quantitative estimates for the stability of Theorem
1.4
, which we plan to address in an upcoming paper.

It is also natural ask whether the converse to Theorem
1.4
holds. We recall that if
\Omega
is known to be a ball, i.e.,
\Omega:=B_{\rho}
, then the radial symmetry of a bounded positive solution
u
to

(1.13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}-\Delta u&=f(u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }B_{\rho}\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{on }\partial B_{\rho}\end{array}\right.

is guaranteed for a large class of nonlinearities
f
. For instance, this is the case for
f
locally Lipschitz, by the classical result obtained via the method of moving planes by Gidas-Ni-Nirenberg
[
GNN81
,
GNN79
]
. Many extensions of Gidas-Ni-Nirenberg result can be found in the literature, including generalizations to the fractional setting (see, e.g.,
[
BLW05
]
).
We also recall that an alternative method pioneered by P.-L. Lions in
[
Lio81
]
provides symmetry of nonnegative bounded solutions to (
1.13
) for (possibly discontinuous) nonnegative nonlinearities; we refer to
[
Ser13
,
KP94
,
BFK03
,
DPV22
]
for several generalizations.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we recall some standard notation, as well as provide some alternate proofs for the weak and strong maximum principle for antisymmetric functions. Moreover, we will prove Theorem
1.1
and subsequently prove Corollary
1.2
and Corollary
1.3
. In Section
3
, we will prove Proposition
1.5
and in Section
4
we will prove Theorem
1.4
. In Appendix
A
we give some technical lemmas needed in the paper.

2.
Maximum principles and counter-examples

2.1.
Definitions and notation

Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer,
s\in(0,1)
, and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set. The fractional Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as

\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\bigg\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ s.t. }[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty\bigg\}

where
[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is the Gagliardo semi-norm

\displaystyle[u]_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

As usual we identify functions that are equal except on a set of measure zero. It will also be convenient to introduce the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}_{0}(\Omega)=\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ s.t. }u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\}.

Suppose that
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function and that
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. A function
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
weak
or
generalised
solution of
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu=0
(resp.
\geqslant 0,\leqslant 0
) in
\Omega
if

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(u,v):=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy+\int_{\Omega}c(x)u(x)v(x)\mathop{}\!dx=0\,(\geqslant 0,\leqslant 0)

for all
v\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
,
v\geqslant 0
. Also, it will be convenient to use the notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy

for each
u,v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

A function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
antisymmetric
with respect to a plane
T
if

\displaystyle u(Q_{T}(x))=-u(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
Q_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the reflection of
x
across
T
. A function
u
is
x_{1}
-antisymmetric
if it is antisymmetric with respect to the plane
T=\{x_{1}=0\}
. In the case
T=\{x_{1}=0\}
,
Q_{T}
is given explicitly by
Q_{T}(x)=x-2x_{1}e_{1}
. When it is clear from context what
T
is, we will also write
Q(x)
or
x_{\ast}
to mean
Q_{T}(x)
.

We will also make use of standard notation: the upper and lower half-planes are given by
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ s.t }x_{1}>0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ s.t }x_{1}<0\}
respectively, and, for all
r>0
, the half-ball of radius
r
is given by
B_{r}^{+}=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\cap B_{r}
. We also denote the positive and negative part of a function
u
by
u^{+}(x)=\max\{u(x),0\}

and
u^{-}(x)=\max\{-u(x),0\}
. Furthermore, the characteristic function of a set
A
is given by

\displaystyle\chi_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x\in A,\\
0,&\text{if }x\not\in A.\end{cases}

2.2.
Maximum principles

We now present some results of maximum principle type for antisymmetric functions.

Proposition 2.1

(A weak maximum principle)
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a strict open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, and
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
ia a measurable and non-negative function. Let
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be
x_{1}
-antisymmetric. If
u
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu\leqslant 0
in
\Omega
then

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}u\leqslant\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\Omega}u^{+}.

Likewise, if
u
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
then

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}u\geqslant-\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\Omega}u^{-}.

In our proof, we use that
c\geq 0
. We refer, e.g., to
[
FJ15
, Proposition 3.1]
for a weak maximum principle where the non-negativity of
c
is not required.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

It is enough to prove the case
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu\leqslant 0
in
\Omega
. Suppose that
\ell:=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\Omega}u^{+}<+\infty
, otherwise we are done.

For each
v\in H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
,
v\geqslant 0
, rearranging
\mathfrak{L}(u,v)\leqslant 0
gives

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)\leqslant-\int_{\Omega}c(x)u(x)v(x)\mathop{}\!dx\leqslant 0

provided that
uv\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
.

Let
w(x):=u(x)-\ell
and
v(x):=(u(x)-\ell)^{+}\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}(x)=w^{+}(x)\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}(x)
. Note that for all
x\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle u(x)v(x)

\displaystyle=((u(x)-\ell)^{+})^{2}+\ell(u(x)-\ell)^{+}\geqslant 0.

Here we used that
(u-\ell)^{+}(u-\ell)^{-}=0
. To prove the proposition, it is enough to show that
v=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As
u(x)-u(y)=w(x)-w(y)
, expanding then using that
w^{+}(x)w^{-}(x)=0
gives

\displaystyle(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))

\displaystyle=((v(x)-v(y))^{2}+v(x)(v(y)-w(y))+v(y)(v(x)-w(x))

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(v,v)+c_{n,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{v(x)(v(y)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy.

Observe that

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{v(x)(v(y)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\frac{w^{+}(x)w^{-}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\frac{w^{+}(x)w(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy.

Making the change of variables
y\to y_{\ast}
, where
y_{\ast}
denotes the reflection of
y
across the plane
\{x_{1}=0\}
, then writing
w=w^{+}-w^{-}
gives

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{v(x)(v(y)-w(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\frac{w^{+}(x)w^{-}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\frac{w^{+}(x)(w(y)+2\ell)}{|x-y_{\ast}|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}w^{+}(x)w^{-}(y)\bigg(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\frac{1}{|x-y_{\ast}|^{n+2s}}\bigg)\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\frac{w^{+}(x)(w^{+}(y)+2\ell)}{|x-y_{\ast}|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dx\mathop{}\!dy

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.

Thus,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)\geqslant\mathcal{E}(v,v)\geqslant 0.

Combining (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) gives that
\mathcal{E}(v,v)=0
which implies that
v
is a constant in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
v=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}
, we must have that
v=0
as required.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

It follows directly from Proposition
2.1
that if
u
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu=0
in
\Omega
then

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}|u|\leqslant\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\setminus\Omega}|u|.

Next, we prove the strong maximum principle for antisymmetric functions. This result is not new in the literature, see
[
FJ15
, Corollary 3.4]
; however, Proposition
2.3
provides an alternate elementary proof.

Proposition 2.3

(A strong maximum principle)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
x_{1}
-antisymmetric and
(-\Delta)^{s}u
is defined pointwise in
\Omega
. If
u
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u+cu\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
, and
u\geqslant 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
then either
u>0
in
\Omega
or
u
is zero almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that in Proposition
2.3
, as in
[
FJ15
, Corollary 3.4]
, no sign assumption is required on
c
. The reason Proposition
2.3
holds without this assumption is due to the sign assumption on
u
which is not usually present in the statement of the strong maximum principle.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Due to the antisymmetry of
u
, by a change of variables we may write

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (-\Delta)^{s}u(x)&=c_{n,s}\operatorname{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\bigg(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\frac{1}{|x_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s}}\bigg)(u(x)-u(y))\mathop{}\!dy\\
&\qquad\qquad+2c_{n,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\frac{u(x)}{|x_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy\end{aligned}

where
x_{\ast}=x-2x_{1}e_{1}
is the reflection of
x
across
\{x_{1}=0\}
. Suppose that there exists
x_{\star}\in\Omega
such that
u(x_{\star})=0
. On one hand, (
2.3
) implies that

(2.4)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{\star})

\displaystyle=-c_{n,s}\operatorname{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\bigg(\frac{1}{|x_{\star}-y|^{n+2s}}-\frac{1}{|(x_{\star})_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s}}\bigg)u(y)\mathop{}\!dy

where we used that
u
is antisymmetric. Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|x_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s}}<\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}},\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\,x\neq y,

equation (
2.4
) implies that
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{\star})\leqslant 0
with equality if and only if
u
is identically zero almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. On the other hand,
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{\star})=(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{\star})+c(x_{\star})u(x_{\star})\geqslant 0
. Hence,
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{\star})=0
so
u
is identically zero a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.
∎

2.3.
Counter-examples

The purpose of this subsection is to provide counter-examples to the classical Harnack inequality and the strong maximum principle for antisymmetric
s
-harmonic functions. A useful tool to construct such functions is the following antisymmetric analogue of Theorem 1 in
[
DSV17
]
. This proves that ‘all antisymmetric functions are locally antisymmetric and
s
-harmonic up to a small error’.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Due to
[
DSV17
, Theorem 1.1]
there exist
R>1
and
v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

in

,

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}
, and
\|v-f\|_{C^{k}(B_{1})}<\varepsilon
. In fact, by a mollification argument, we may take

to be smooth, see Remark

here below.

Let

denote the reflection of

across
\{x_{1}=0\}
, and

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\big(v(x)-v(x_{\ast})\big).

It is easy to verify that

(and of course

\displaystyle u(x)-f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(v(x)-f(x)\big)+\frac{1}{2}\big(f(x_{\ast})-v(x_{\ast})\big)\qquad\text{for all }x\in B_{1}

we also obtain

Remark 2.5
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. If
f\in C^{k}(\overline{B_{1}})
there exists
\mu>0
such that
f\in C^{k}(B_{1+2\mu})
. Rescaling then applying
[
DSV17
, Theorem 1.1]
, there exist
\tilde{R}>1+\mu
and
\tilde{v}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{v}=0
in
B_{1+\mu}
,
\tilde{v}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\tilde{R}}
, and
\|\tilde{v}-f\|_{C^{k}(B_{1+\mu})}<\varepsilon/2
.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the standard mollifier

\displaystyle\eta(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}Ce^{-\frac{1}{|x|^{2}-1}},&\text{if }x\in B_{1},\\
0,&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1},\end{cases}

with
C>0
chosen so that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(x)\mathop{}\!dx=1
. For each
\delta>0
, let
\eta_{\delta}(x)=\delta^{-n}\eta(x/\delta))
and

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}(x):=(\tilde{v}\ast\eta_{\delta})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\delta}(x-y)\tilde{v}(y)\mathop{}\!dy.

By the properties of mollifiers,

\displaystyle\|\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}-f\|_{C^{k}(B_{1})}\leqslant\|\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}-\tilde{v}\|_{C^{k}(B_{1})}+\|\tilde{v}-f\|_{C^{k}(B_{1})}<\varepsilon

provided
\delta
is sufficiently small. Since
\tilde{v}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows from
[

, Proposition 4.18]
that
\operatorname{supp}\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}(x)\subset\overline{B_{\tilde{R}+\delta}}
. Moreover, via the Fourier transform,
(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}=\big((-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{v}\big)\ast\eta_{\delta}
, so
[
Bre11
, Proposition 4.18]
again implies that
\operatorname{supp}\big((-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}\big)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1+\mu-\delta}
.

Hence, setting
R=\tilde{R}+\delta
,
v=\tilde{v}^{(\delta)}
, we have constructed a function

such that
v
is
s
-harmonic in
B_{1}
,
v=0
outside
B_{R}
and
\|v-f\|_{C^{k}(B_{1})}<\varepsilon
. Moreover,

v(x_{\ast})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\delta}(y)\tilde{v}(x_{\ast}-y)\mathop{}\!dy=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\delta}(y)\tilde{v}(x-y_{\ast})\mathop{}\!dy=-v(x)

via the change of variables

and using that

.

In this section, we prove Proposition

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that

. Then there exists an
x_{1}
-antisymmetric function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi+c\varphi&\leqslant 0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }B_{2}^{+}\setminus B_{1/2}(e_{1}),\\

Let
\zeta
be a smooth

-antisymmetric cut-off function such that the support of
\zeta
is contained in

where

is the standard mollifier defined in Remark

is smooth with compact support, we have that

Moreover,

-antisymmetric since

is

-antisymmetric, so it follows that there exists

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}\zeta(x)+c\zeta(x)\leqslant C(1+\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2}^{+})})x_{1}\qquad\text{in }B_{2}^{+}

using that

.

Next, let
\tilde{\zeta}
be a smooth
x_{1}
-antisymmetric function such that

in

,

in

in

. Recall that, given an

-antisymmetric function

Hence, for each

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{\zeta}(x)

\displaystyle=-c_{n,s}\int_{B_{3/8}(e_{1})}\bigg(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\frac{1}{|x_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s}}\bigg)\tilde{\zeta}(y)\mathop{}\!dy.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}-\frac{1}{|x_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+2s}{2}\int_{|x-y|^{2}}^{|x_{\ast}-y|^{2}}\frac{\mathop{}\!d\tau}{\tau^{\frac{n+2s+2}{2}}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{Cx_{1}y_{1}}{|x_{\ast}-y|^{n+2s+2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant Cx_{1}

with

and

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi+c\varphi\leqslant C(1-\alpha+\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2}^{+})})x_{1}\leqslant 0\qquad\text{in }B_{2}^{+}\setminus B_{1/2}(e_{1})

is large enough.

\displaystyle m=\inf\{\mu<M\text{ s.t. }\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}\subset\Omega\text{ for all }\lambda\in(\mu,M)\}.

As is standard in the method of moving planes, we will consider the function

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=u(x)-u(Q_{\lambda}(x)),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

Here, as usual,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime})\geqslant C|\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}|^{-\frac{2s}{n}}\to\infty\quad\text{ as }\,\lambda\to M^{-},

, the set
\{v_{\tilde{m}-\varepsilon}\leqslant 0\}\cap\Omega_{\tilde{m}-\varepsilon}^{\prime}
is non-empty. Let
\Pi_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega_{\tilde{m}-\varepsilon}^{\prime}
be an open set such that
\{v_{\tilde{m}-\varepsilon}\leqslant 0\}\cap\Omega_{\tilde{m}-\varepsilon}^{\prime}\subset\Pi_{\varepsilon}
. By making
\varepsilon
smaller we may choose
\Pi_{\varepsilon}
such that
|\Pi_{\varepsilon}|
is arbitrarily close to zero. Hence, applying Proposition
4.1
then Proposition
2.3
gives that
v_{\tilde{m}-\varepsilon}>0
in
\Pi_{\varepsilon}
which is a contradiction. This proves (
4.2
).

Since
v_{m}\geqslant 0
in
H_{m}^{\prime}
, Proposition
2.3
implies that either (
4.1
) holds or
v_{m}>0
in
\Omega_{m}^{\prime}
. For the sake of contradiction, let us suppose that

(4.3)

\displaystyle v_{m}>0\qquad\text{in }\Omega_{m}^{\prime}.

By definition,
m
is the minimum value of
\lambda
for which
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}\subset\Omega
. There are only two possible cases that can occur at this point.

Case 1: There exists
p\in(\Omega_{m}^{\prime}\cap\partial\Omega)\setminus T_{m}
.

Case 2: There exists
p\in T_{m}\cap\partial\Omega
such that
e_{1}
is tangent to
\partial\Omega
at
p
.

If we are in Case 1 then there is a corresponding point
q\in(G_{m}^{\prime}\cap\partial G)\setminus T_{m}\subset\Omega_{m}^{\prime}
since
\partial G
is parallel to
\partial\Omega
. But
u
is constant on
\partial G
, so we have

\displaystyle v_{m}(q)=u(q)-u(Q_{m}(q))=0

which contradicts (
4.3
).

If we are in Case 2 then there exists
q\in T_{m}\cap\partial G
such that
e_{1}
is tangent to
\partial G
at
q
. Since
u
is a constant on
\partial G
, the gradient of
u
is perpendicular to
\partial G
and so
\partial_{1}u(q)=0.
Moreover, by the chain rule,

\displaystyle\partial_{1}(u\circ Q_{m})(q)=-\partial_{1}u(q)=0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\partial_{1}v_{m}(q)=0.

However, this contradicts Proposition
1.5
. Thus, in both Case 1 and Case 2 we have shown that the assumption (
4.3
) leads to a contradiction, so we conclude that (
4.1
) is true.

This concludes the main part of the proof. The final two steps are to show that
u
is radially symmetric and that
\operatorname{supp}u=\overline{\Omega}
. Due to the previous arguments we know that for all
e\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\lambda\in[m(e),M(e))
,

\displaystyle u(x)-u(Q_{\lambda,e}(x))\geqslant 0\qquad\text{in }H_{\lambda,e}^{\prime}

where
Q_{\lambda,e}=Q_{\lambda}
,
H^{\prime}_{\lambda,e}=H_{\lambda}^{\prime}
as before but we’ve included the subscript
e
to emphasise that the result is true for each
e
. It follows that for every halfspace we either have
u(x)-u(Q_{\partial H}(x))\geqslant 0
for all
x\in H
or
u(x)-u(Q_{\partial H}(x))\leqslant 0
for all
x\in H
. By
[
Wet10
, Proposition 2.3]
, we can conclude that there exists some
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
x\mapsto u(x-z)
is radially symmetric. Moreover, since
u\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
and not identically zero,
[
Wet10
, Proposition 2.3]
also implies that
x\mapsto u(x-z)
is non-increasing in the radial direction.

It follows that
\operatorname{supp}u
is a closed ball. (Note that we are using the convention

\operatorname{supp}u=\overline{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ s.t. }u(x)\neq 0\}}
). We claim that
\operatorname{supp}u=\overline{\Omega}
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that this is not the case. Then there exists a direction
e\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\lambda\in(m(e),M(e))
such that
\Omega^{\prime}_{\lambda}\cap\operatorname{supp}u=\varnothing
. It follows that
v_{\lambda}\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. This is a contradiction since we previously showed that
v_{\lambda}>0
in
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
for all
\lambda\in(m(e),M(e))
.
∎

n=1
, Theorem
1.4
reads as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that
G
is a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega=G+B_{R}
,
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz, and
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, assume that there exists a non-negative function
u\in C^{s}(\mathbb{R})
that is not identically zero and satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=f(u)\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega,\\
u&=c_{0}\hfil\quad\>&\text{on }\partial G.\end{array}\right.

Then, up to a translation,
u
is even,
u>0
in
\Omega
, and
G=(a,b)
for some
a<b
.

Proof.

In one dimension the moving plane
T_{\lambda}
is just a point
\lambda
and there are only two directions
T_{\lambda}
can move–from right to left or from left to right. We will begin by considering the case
T_{\lambda}
is moving from right to left. Let
(a-R,b+R)
,
a<b
, be the connected component of
\Omega
such that
\sup\Omega=b+R
. Using the same notation as in the proof of Theorem
1.4
, it is clear that
M=b+R
and
m=\frac{a+b}{2}
. For all
\lambda\in(\frac{a+b}{2},b+R)
, let

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=u(x)-u(-x+2\lambda),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we obtain

(4.7)

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}

\displaystyle>0\qquad\text{in }(-\infty,\lambda)

for all
\lambda\in(\frac{a+b}{2},b+R)
and

\displaystyle v_{\frac{a+b}{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0\qquad\text{in }(-\infty,(a+b)/2).

Then the overdetermined condition
u(a)=u(b)=c_{0}
implies that

\displaystyle v_{\frac{a+b}{2}}(b)

\displaystyle=u(b)-u(a)=0.

Hence, Proposition
2.3
gives that

(4.8)

\displaystyle v_{\frac{a+b}{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R},

that is,
u
is an even function about the point
\frac{a+b}{2}
. Moreover, (
4.8
) implies that
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus(a-R,b+R)
.

Now suppose that we move
T_{\lambda}
from left to right. If
\Omega
is made up of at least two connected components then repeating the argument above we must also have that
u\equiv 0
in
(a-R,b+R)
. However,
u
is not identically zero, so
\Omega
can only have one connected component. Finally, (
4.7
) implies that
u
is strictly monotone in
(\frac{a+b}{2},b+R)
which further implies the positivity of
u
.
∎

Appendix A
Technical lemmas

In this appendix, we list several lemmas that are used throughout the paper.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded sets in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
respectively. Suppose that
\phi_{t}(x):\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{I}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous in
\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{I}
and define

\displaystyle m(t)=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\phi_{t}(x).

Then
m\in C(\overline{I})
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{I}
is compact,
\phi
is uniformly continuous. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
(\bar{x},\bar{t})\in\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{I}
be arbitrary. There exists some
\delta>0
(indepedent of
\bar{t}
and
\bar{x}
) such that if
(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{I}
and
\lvert(x,t)-(\bar{x},\bar{t})\rvert<\delta
then

\displaystyle\lvert\phi_{t}(x)-\phi_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})\rvert<\varepsilon.

In particular, we may take
x=\bar{x}
to conclude that if
t\in\overline{I}
and
\lvert t-\bar{t}\rvert<\delta
then

\displaystyle\lvert\phi_{t}(\bar{x})-\phi_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})\rvert<\varepsilon

for any
\bar{x}\in\overline{\Omega}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(\bar{x})>\phi_{\bar{t}}(\bar{x})-\varepsilon\geqslant m(\bar{t})-\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }\bar{x}\in\overline{\Omega}

Minimising over
\bar{x}
we obtain

\displaystyle m(t)\geqslant m(\bar{t})-\varepsilon.

Similarly, we also have that

\displaystyle m(\bar{t})\geqslant m(t)-\varepsilon.

Thus, we have shown that if
t\in\overline{I}
and
\lvert t-\bar{t}\rvert<\delta
then

\lvert m(t)-m(\bar{t})\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon.\qed

Lemma A.2
.

Let
R>0
and
\zeta_{R}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}\zeta_{R}&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }(-R,R),\\
\zeta_{R}&=g_{R}\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus(-R,R),\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle g_{R}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}R,&\text{if }x>R,\\
-R,&\text{if }x<-R.\end{cases}

Then

(A.3)

\displaystyle x\mapsto\frac{\zeta_{R}(x)}{x}\qquad\text{is defined at }x=0

and there exists a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(s)>0
such that, as
R\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta_{R}(x)}{x}\to c_{0}\qquad\text{in }C_{\textrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

By the scale-invariance property of the fractional Laplacian, we may write

(A.4)

\displaystyle\zeta_{R}(x)

\displaystyle=R\zeta_{1}(x/R)

where
\zeta_{1}
is the solution to (
A.2
) with
R=1
.
The function
\zeta_{1}
is given explicitly via the Poisson kernel, (for more details see
[
Gar19
, Section 15]
)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{s}\big(1-x^{2}\big)^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)}\frac{g_{1}(t)}{(t^{2}-1)^{s}|x-t|}\mathop{}\!dt,\qquad x\in(-1,1).

where
a_{s}
is a positive normalisation constant. Using the definition of
g_{1}
and a change of variables, we obtain

(A.5)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(x)=2a_{s}x\big(1-x^{2}\big)^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{(t^{2}-x^{2})(t^{2}-1)^{s}}.

Provided
|x|<1/2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(t^{2}-x^{2})(t^{2}-1)^{s}}\leqslant\frac{1}{(t^{2}-(1/2)^{2})(t^{2}-1)^{s}}\in L^{1}((1,\infty))

so by (
A.5
) and the dominated convergence theorem

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\zeta_{R}(x)}{x}

\displaystyle=R\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\zeta_{1}(x/R)}{x}=2a_{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{t^{2}(t^{2}-1)^{s}}=:c_{0}.

This proves (
A.3
).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\zeta_{R}(x)}{x}-c_{0}\bigg|

\displaystyle=2a_{s}\bigg|\int_{1}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{\big(1-(x/R)^{2}\big)^{s}}{t^{2}-(x/R)^{2}}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}\bigg)\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{(t^{2}-1)^{s}}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2a_{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\bigg|\frac{t^{2}\big((1-(x/R)^{2})^{s}-1\big)+(x/R)^{2}\big)}{t^{2}\big(t^{2}-(x/R)^{2}\big)}\bigg|\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{(t^{2}-1)^{s}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2a_{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\bigg|\frac{\big(1-(x/R)^{2}\big)^{s}-1}{t^{2}-(x/R)^{2}}\bigg|\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{(t^{2}-1)^{s}}

(A.6)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2a_{s}|x|^{2}}{R^{2}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{t^{2}|t^{2}-(x/R)^{2}|(t^{2}-1)^{s}}.

Suppose that
x\in\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\mathbb{R}
. Then for
R>0
sufficiently large,

(A.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|t^{2}-(x/R)^{2}|}\leqslant C\qquad\text{for all }t>1

and, by Bernoulli’s inequality,

(A.8)

\displaystyle|\big(1-(x/R)^{2}\big)^{s}-1|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{R^{2}}.

Combining (
A.6
), (
A.7
), and (
A.8
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\zeta_{R}(x)}{x}-c_{0}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{R^{2}}\bigg(\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{(t^{2}-1)^{s}}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{}\!dt}{t^{2}(t^{2}-1)^{s}}\bigg)\to 0

as
R\to\infty
.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and that
\lambda(\Omega)
is a Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
in
\Omega
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{n}{2s}|B_{1}|^{1+2s/n}c_{n,s}|\Omega|^{-\frac{2s}{n}}.

where
c_{n,s}
is the defined in (
1.1
).

The result in Proposition
A.3
is not new (see
[
Fra18
,
YY13
]
); however, to the authors’ knowledge, the proof is original and simple.

Proof of Proposition
A.3
.

Let
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{ \ }l @{\quad\: \;} l}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=\lambda(\Omega)u\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0\hfil\quad\>&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\\
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=1.\hfil\quad\>\end{array}\right.

The existence of such a function can be proved via semi-group theory, see for example
[
BBKRSV09
, Chapter 4]
.

Using the integration by parts formula for the fractional Laplacian, see
[
DRV17
, Lemma 3.3]
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda(\Omega)=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(\Omega^{c})^{2}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy\mathop{}\!dx\geqslant c_{n,s}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\mathop{}\!dy\mathop{}\!dx.

For
x\in\Omega
, if
B_{r}(x)
is the ball such that
|B_{r}(x)|=|\Omega|
then by
[
DPV12
, Lemma 6.1]
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}\frac{\mathop{}\!dy}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(x)}\frac{\mathop{}\!dy}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}=\frac{n}{2s}|B_{1}|^{1+2s/n}|\Omega|^{-\frac{2s}{n}}.

Hence,

\lambda(\Omega)\geqslant\frac{n}{2s}|B_{1}|^{1+2s/n}c_{n,s}|\Omega|^{-\frac{2s}{n}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\frac{n}{2s}|B_{1}|^{1+2s/n}c_{n,s}|\Omega|^{-\frac{2s}{n}}.\qed
