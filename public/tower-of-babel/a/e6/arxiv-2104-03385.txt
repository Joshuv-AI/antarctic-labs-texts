Primary decomposition of modules: a computational differential approach
Justin Chen, Yairon Cid-Ruiz

Abstract
. We study primary submodules and primary decompositions from a differential and computational point of view. Our main theoretical contribution is a general structure theory and a representation theorem for primary submodules of an arbitrary finitely generated module over a polynomial ring. We characterize primary submodules in terms of differential operators and punctual Quot schemes. Moreover, we introduce and implement an algorithm that computes a minimal differential primary decomposition for a module.

13N10, 13N99, 13E05, 14C05.

1.
Introduction

The existence of primary decompositions has long been known, since the classical works of Lasker
[
25
]
and Noether
[
28
]
: over a Noetherian commutative ring, every proper submodule of a finitely generated module can be expressed as a finite intersection of primary submodules.
Accordingly, one can view primary submodules as the basic building blocks for arbitrary modules.
In this paper, we study the central notions of primary submodules and primary decompositions from a differential and computational point of view.

Let
\mathbb{k}
be a field of characteristic zero and
R
a polynomial ring
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
The main objective of this paper is to characterize primary
R
-submodules with the use of
differential operators
and
punctual Quot schemes
.
We achieve this goal in
Theorem 3.2
(see also
Section 3
), which can be seen as an extension from ideals to
R
-modules of the representation theorem given in
[
8
]
.
Consequently, we introduce an algorithm that computes a minimal
differential primary decomposition
for arbitrary finitely generated modules (see
§4.2
for the precise definitions).
This algorithm, along with others (see
Section 4
), have been implemented in the computer algebra system
Macaulay2

[
16
]
.

The program of characterizing ideal membership in a polynomial ring with differential conditions was initiated by Gröbner
[
17
]
in the 1930s, and he successfully employed Macaulay’s theory of inverse systems to characterize membership in an ideal primary to a rational maximal ideal.
Nevertheless, a complete differential characterization of primary submodules over a polynomial ring was obtained in 1970 by analysts, in the form of the

of Ehrenpreis
[
12
]
and Palamodov
[
30
]

Subsequent algebraic approaches were given in
[
4
]
and
[
29
]
.
More recently, the study of primary ideals and primary submodules via differential operators has continued in e.g.
[
11
]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

,

Let

Let

Following Palamodov’s terminology, we say that

In a similar fashion to

The

Recently, the notion of a

This notion is a natural generalization of Noetherian operators for (not necessarily primary) modules.
Let

We now wish to describe

(1)

U=\big\{w\in R^{r}\mid\delta(w)\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}\text{ for all }\delta\in\mathfrak{A}_{i}\text{ and }1\leq i\leq k\big\},

where each

In

We note that the existence of differential primary decompositions can always be achieved from the existence of Noetherian operators for a primary submodule.
Indeed, if

is an

-submodule with primary decomposition

for

Therefore, the challenge is to compute a

Typically a minimal differential primary decomposition does not directly give an ordinary primary decomposition (see

Building on

This algorithm is an extension of

The

2.
A general primary decomposition algorithm

We begin with a general algorithm for primary decomposition of modules, inspired by the work of Eisenbud-Huneke-Vasconcelos
[
13
]
for ideals, with a particular focus on computational aspects.
Although primary decomposition for ideals has been well-studied in the literature, the case of modules is considerably less prominent (which we hope to remedy with this exposition!); cf.
[
31
]
as well as
[
24
,
23
,
22
]
for some treatments.

We start with some reductions to the main case of interest.
First, we reduce to the case of polynomial rings: let
\mathbb{k}
be an arbitrary field, and
T
a finitely generated
\mathbb{k}
-algebra.
If
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring with a surjection
\pi:R\twoheadrightarrow T
,
then for any
T
-module
N
, one has
\text{Ass}_{R}(\hbox{}_{R}N)=\{\pi^{-1}(P)\mid P\in\text{Ass}_{T}(N)\}
, where
\hbox{}_{R}N
denotes
N
viewed as an
R
-module.
In this way we may compute associated primes and primary components of
N
over
T
, by first computing that of
\hbox{}_{R}N
over
R
, and then applying
\pi
.

Next, to simplify the notation on modules, note that if
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
is a submodule, then a primary decomposition of
M^{\prime}
in
M
can be obtained by lifting a primary decomposition of
0
in
M/M^{\prime}
.
Thus we may always take
M^{\prime}=0
(a benefit afforded by working with general modules), and state the problem as follows: for a finitely generated module
M\neq 0
over a polynomial ring
R
, find submodules
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{s}\subseteq M
such that
\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}Q_{i}=0
and
|\text{Ass}_{R}(M/Q_{i})|=1
.
The decomposition should moreover be
minimal
, in the sense that
\bigcap_{j\neq i}Q_{j}\neq 0
for all
i
, and also
\text{Ass}_{R}(M/Q_{i})=\text{Ass}_{R}(M/Q_{j})\iff i=j
.

The primary decomposition algorithm described here proceeds in 2 steps: first, find all associated primes of
M
, and second, determine valid
P_{i}
-primary components
Q_{i}
for each associated prime
P_{i}
(note that by uniqueness of associated primes from a primary decomposition, such a decomposition will automatically be minimal).
For the first step, following
[
13
]
, we first reduce the problem of computing all associated primes of a module, to computing minimal primes of ideals:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

For any
i\geq 0
, the associated primes of
M
of codimension
i
are precisely the minimal primes of
\ann\text{Ext}_{R}^{i}(M,R)
of codimension
i
.

In view of this, we may compute
\text{Ass}_{R}(M)
via oracles to compute (1) a free resolution of a module
M
(and thus any Ext modules
\text{Ext}_{R}^{\bullet}(M,\cdot)
), and (2) minimal primes of any ideal
I\subseteq R
, which we henceforth assume are given (in practice, both are well-optimized in
Macaulay2
).
Note that following the above procedure iteratively will naturally produce a list of associated primes which are weakly ordered by codimension (e.g. all codimension 1 primes appear before any codimension 2 primes, etc.).

For the second step, namely producing valid primary components, we proceed inductively.
Order the associated primes
P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}
of
M
by a linear extension of the partial order by inclusion, i.e.
P_{i}\subseteq P_{j}\implies i\leq j
(note that this is automatic if the associated primes are weakly ordered by codimension, as in the previous paragraph).
In particular, as a base case
P_{1}
is a minimal prime of
M
(i.e. a minimal prime of
\ann M
).
Primary components to minimal primes are uniquely determined, and can be obtained as follows:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
P\in{\text{Spec}}(R)
, and let
M\to M_{P}
be the localization map. Then:

(1)

[
14
, Theorem 3.10(d)]

\ker(M\to M_{P})
equals the intersection of all
P_{i}
-primary components of
0
in
M
for
P_{i}\in\text{Ass}_{R}(M)
,
P_{i}\subseteq P
(in particular, this intersection is uniquely determined by
M
and
P
).

(2)

[
14
, Proposition 3.13]
Suppose
f\in R
is such that for all
P_{i}\in\text{Ass}_{R}(M)
, one has
f\in P_{i}\iff P_{i}\not\subseteq P
.
Then
\ker(M\to M_{P})=\ker(M\to M_{f})=0:_{M}f^{\infty}
.

An element
f
as in
Section 2
(2) can be obtained as follows: for each associated prime
P_{i}
not contained in
P
, choose a generator
g_{i}
of
P_{i}
not contained in
P
; then take
f:=\prod_{P_{i}\not\subseteq P}g_{i}
.
Taking
P=P_{i}
for some minimal prime
P_{i}
of
M
in
Section 2
shows that given an oracle to compute saturations, we may obtain (the unique) primary components corresponding to minimal primes of
M
.

It then remains to compute a valid
P
-primary component
Q
, for an embedded prime
P
.
In this case such a
Q
is not unique; indeed there are always infinitely many valid choices for
Q
.
The next proposition gives one class of such choices:

Proposition 2.3

(
[
13
]
, p. 27-28)
.

For
P\in\text{Ass}_{R}(M)
and
j\geq 0
, fix generators
P=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
for
P
, and set
P^{[j]}:=(f_{1}^{j},\ldots,f_{m}^{j})
.
Then for
j\gg 0
, the submodule
Q_{[j]}:=\hull(P^{[j]}M,M)
is a valid
P
-primary component of
0
in
M
.

Here
\hull(N,M)
is the equidimensional hull of
N
in
M
, i.e. the intersection of all primary components of
N
of maximal dimension.
There are a number of ways to compute
Q_{[j]}
: the first is via
Section 2
, viz.
Q_{[j]}=\ker(M\to(M/P^{[j]}M)_{P})
.
Another method is given in
[
13
, Theorem 1.1(2)]
, which is a general way to compute hulls via iterated Ext modules, and yet another method is given in
[
13
, Algorithm 1.2]
.

To find a stopping criterion for the exponent
j
in
Section 2
, note that inductively, we may assume that
P_{i}
-primary components for any
P_{i}\subsetneq P
have already been computed, and so their intersection
\mathcal{V}:=\bigcap_{P_{i}\subsetneq P}Q_{i}
is also known.
By
Section 2
, we also know
\mathcal{U}:=\bigcap_{P_{i}\subseteq P}Q_{i}
.
Then for any
j\geq 0
, the submodule
Q_{[j]}
in
Section 2
is a valid
P
-primary component if and only if
Q_{[j]}\cap\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}
.
We may thus find a valid
P
-primary component as follows: initialize
j
at some starting value, and compute
Q_{[j]}
.
If
Q_{[j]}\cap\mathcal{V}\neq\mathcal{U}
, then increment
j
, and repeat until a valid candidate
Q_{[j]}
is found.

Remark 2.4
.

A few remarks are in order concerning efficiency of the algorithm described above:

(1)

Both choices of starting value of
j
, and the function used to increment
j
, are relevant considerations for efficiency of the algorithm.
If the starting value of
j
is too small, or the increment function grows too slowly, then invalid candidates may be computed many times.
On the other hand, the computation time for
Q_{[j]}
tends to increase as
j
increases, so it is desirable not to take
j
unnecessarily large.
The current implementation in
Macaulay2
uses the increment
j\to\lceil\frac{3j}{2}\rceil
(the starting value is more complicated, depending on the degrees of generators of
P
and of
\ann M
).

(2)

The use of bracket powers
P^{[j]}
rather than ordinary powers
P^{j}
in
Section 2
is also for efficiency: if either
j
or the number of generators of
P
is large, then
P^{j}
may take much longer to compute than
P^{[j]}
.

(3)

Of the three methods given above for computing embedded components
Q_{[j]}
, usually the first method (namely as a kernel of a localization map) is the most efficient, although this is not always the case (in some examples, the second method can be drastically faster).
Note that in the first method, it is necessary to compute
\text{Ass}_{R}(M/P^{[j]}M)
, which is in general strictly bigger than
\{P\}
.

(4)

The necessity of realizing
\ker(M\to M_{P})
as a saturation in
Section 2
(2) stems from the fact that
M_{P}
typically does not have a finite presentation as an
R
-module.
Although one could also express
\ker(M\to M_{P})
as a saturation
0:_{M}(P^{\prime})^{\infty}
, where
P^{\prime}
equals the intersection of all associated primes of
M
not contained in
P
, it is almost always much more efficient to compute saturations by a single element, than by general ideals.

(5)

As a general rule, computation of associated primes is the most time-consuming step in this procedure.
Once the associated primes are known, the minimal primary components tend to be computed very quickly, and the time for computing the embedded components can vary based on the method chosen (cf. point (3) above).
In particular, this algorithm tends to perform well for modules whose free resolutions can be cheaply computed (e.g. when the number of variables is small).

The main differences between the algorithm presented here and the one given in
[
13
, Section 4]
are the stopping criterion for the exponent
j
in
Section 2
, the use of bracket powers as opposed to ordinary powers, and the methods used to compute hulls.
In summary, the algorithm described here reduces general primary decomposition for modules to the following tasks (some of which can be seen as special cases of primary decomposition):

(1)

computation of Ext modules (in fact,
\text{Ext}_{R}^{\bullet}(\cdot,R)
suffices),

(2)

computation of minimal primes of an ideal
I\subseteq R
,

(3)

computation of colon modules (of which saturations and annihilators are special cases),

(4)

computation of intersections of submodules.

3.
A representation theorem for primary submodules

We now leave the classical picture, and adopt a differential point of view.
Our main theorem in this section parametrizes primary submodules of a free module of finite rank in terms of
punctual Quot schemes
,
vector spaces closed under differentiation
and
subbimodules of the Weyl-Noether module
.
This extends the main result of
[
8
]
to the case of modules.
As a simple corollary, we also obtain a representation theorem for primary submodules of an arbitrary module.

Setup 3.1
.

For the rest of this section we fix the following notation:

–

Let
\mathbb{k}
be a field of characteristic zero, and
R:=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
a polynomial ring over
\mathbb{k}
.

–

For an integer
r\geq 0
, let
R^{r}
be a free
R
-module of rank
r
.

–

Let
{\mathfrak{p}}\in{\text{Spec}}(R)
be a prime ideal with codimension
c:=\text{ht}({\mathfrak{p}})
.

–

The residue field of
{\mathfrak{p}}
is denoted
\mathbb{F}:=k({\mathfrak{p}})=\text{Quot}(R/{\mathfrak{p}})=R_{\mathfrak{p}}/{\mathfrak{p}}R_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

–

A subset of variables
\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}\}\subseteq\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
is
independent modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}
if their images in
R/{\mathfrak{p}}
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{k}
, or equivalently
\mathbb{k}[x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}]\cap{\mathfrak{p}}=\{0\}
.
After possibly permuting the variables, we may assume that
\{x_{c+1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
is a
basis modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}
, i.e. a maximal set of independent variables modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}
(see
[
27
, Example 13.2]
).

–

Let
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{k}(x_{c+1},\ldots,x_{n})
denote the field of rational functions in the basis variables (which is a purely transcendental extension of
\mathbb{k}
), and
S
be the polynomial ring

S:=\mathbb{k}(x_{c+1},\ldots,x_{n})[x_{1},\ldots,x_{c}]
(which is a localization of
R
, as
S\cong\mathbb{L}\otimes_{\mathbb{k}[x_{c+1},\ldots,x_{n}]}R
).

–

The
Weyl algebra
and the
relative Weyl algebra
are denoted by
D_{n}:=R\big<\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\big>
and
D_{n,c}:=R\big<\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{c}}\big>\subseteq D_{n}
, respectively.

–

The
multiplicity
of a
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary submodule
U\subseteq R^{r}
is defined as
{\text{length}}_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}\left(R_{\mathfrak{p}}^{r}/U_{\mathfrak{p}}\right)
.

–

For an integer
m\geq 0
, the
punctual Quot scheme
is a parameter space
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]^{r}\right)
whose
\mathbb{F}
-points parametrize all
\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]
-submodules
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]^{r}
of colength
m
, i.e. which satisfy

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]^{r}/\mathbb{V}\right)=m
.

–

We say that
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m}\in(D_{n})^{r}\cong{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}(R^{r},R)
is a set of
Noetherian operators
for a
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary submodule

3.1.
A basic recap on differential operators

In this subsection, we recall basic properties of differential operators to be used in the proof of the main theorem (for further details, the reader is referred to
[
18
, §16]
).
For
R
-modules
M
and
N
, we regard
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
as an
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R)
-module, by setting

\left((s\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}t)\delta\right)(w)=s\delta(tw)\quad\text{ for all }\delta\in\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N),\;w\in M,\;s,t\in R.

We use the bracket notation
[\delta,s](w):=\delta(sw)-s\delta(w)
for
\delta\in\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
,
s\in R
and
w\in M
.

Unless otherwise specified, whenever we consider an
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R)
-module as an
R
-module, we do so by letting
R
act via the left factor of
R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
M
and
N
be
R
-modules.
The
m
-th order
\mathbb{k}
-linear differential operators
, denoted

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N)\subseteq\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
,
form an
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R)
-module that is defined inductively by

(i)

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{0}(M,N):=\text{Hom}_{R}(M,N)
.

(ii)

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N):=\big\{\delta\in\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)\,\mid\,[\delta,s]\in{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m-1}(M,N)\text{ for all }s\in R\big\}
.

The set of all
\mathbb{k}
-linear differential operators from
M
to
N

is the
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R)
-module

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}(M,N):=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty}{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N).

Following the notation used in
[
10
,
8
,
9
]
, subsets
\mathcal{E}\subseteq{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
are viewed as systems of differential equations, and their solution spaces over
\mathbb{k}
are defined as

{\rm Sol}(\mathcal{E}):=\big\{w\in M\,\mid\,\delta(w)=0\text{ for all }\delta\in\mathcal{E}\big\}=\bigcap_{\delta\in\mathcal{E}}{\rm Ker}(\delta).

Example 3.5
.

As
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{k}
of characteristic zero,
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}(R,R)
is the Weyl algebra
D_{n}=R\langle\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\rangle=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}R\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}
.

We next describe differential operators via the module of principal parts.
Consider the multiplication map
\mu:R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R\to R
,
s\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}t\mapsto st
, and define
\Delta_{R/\mathbb{k}}:=\text{Ker}(\mu)
, which is an ideal in
R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R
.
One can alternatively define differential operators as follows:

Proposition 3.6

(
[
20
, Proposition 2.2.3]
)
.

Let
M,N
be
R
-modules.
Then
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N)
is the
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R)
-submodule of
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
annihilated by
\Delta_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m+1}
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
M
be an
R
-module.
The
module of
m
-th principal parts
of
M
is defined as

P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M):=\frac{R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}M}{\Delta_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m+1}\left(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}M\right)}.

This is a module over
R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R
and thus also over
R
.
For simplicity, set
P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}:=P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R)
.

For any
R
-module
M
, consider the universal map

d^{m}:M\to P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M)
,
w\mapsto\overline{1\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}w}
.
The next result is a fundamental characterization of differential operators.

Proposition 3.8

(
[
18
, Proposition 16.8.4]
,
[
20
, Theorem 2.2.6]
)
.

Let
M
and
N
be
R
-modules and let
m\geq 0
.
Then the following map is an isomorphism of
R
-modules:

\displaystyle{\left(d^{m}\right)}^{*}:\text{Hom}_{R}\left(P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M),N\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\cong}{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N),

\displaystyle\varphi\quad

\displaystyle\mapsto\quad\varphi\circ d^{m}.

We recall an explicit description of differential operators on free modules.
Let
J\subseteq R
be an ideal and consider the canonical map
\pi:R\twoheadrightarrow R/J
.
We wish to describe
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(F,R/J)
for a free
R
-module
F=R^{r}
.
Since
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(F,R/J)\cong{{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R,R/J)}^{r}
(see e.g.
[
10
, Lemma 2.7]
), it is enough to describe
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R,R/J)
.
We have an induced map:

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{K}}^{m}(\pi):{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{K}}^{m}(R,R)\to{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{K}}^{m}(R,R/J),\quad\delta\mapsto\overline{\delta}=\pi\circ\delta.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
8
, Lemma 2]
)
.

With the above notation, the following statements hold:

(i)

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R,R/J)=\bigoplus_{\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq m}(R/J)\overline{\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}}

where

\overline{\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}}=\pi\circ\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}.

(ii)

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(\pi)
is surjective:
a differential operator
\epsilon=\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq m}\overline{s_{\alpha}}\overline{\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}}\in{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{K}}^{m}(R,R/J)
with
s_{\alpha}\in R

can be lifted to

\delta=\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq m}s_{\alpha}\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}\in{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R,R)
.

The notation below will be useful for describing the
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R)
-action:

Notation 3.10
.

Let
T:=R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
be a polynomial ring in
2n
variables, where
x_{i}
represents
x_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}1
and
y_{i}
represents
1\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}x_{i}-x_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}1
.
The action of

on
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
is defined as follows: for all
\delta\in\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
and

,

(x_{i}\cdot\delta)(w)\,:=\,x_{i}\delta(w)\quad\text{ and }\quad(y_{i}\cdot\delta)(w)\,:=\,\delta(x_{i}w)-x_{i}\delta(w)=\left[\delta,x_{i}\right](w)

for all

.

Remark 3.11
.

Viewing

and
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R,R/J)
as

-modules yields the following useful descriptions:

(i)

P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}\,=\,\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n},\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq m}Ry_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots y_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.

(ii)

Under the isomorphism
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(R,R/J)\cong\text{Hom}_{R}(P_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m},R/J)
(cf.
§3.1
), the dual basis element
\left(y_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots y_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\right)^{*}
corresponds to the differential operator
\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!}\overline{\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}}
.

Proof.

For explicit computations, see
[
9
, §5]
.
∎

The next proposition describes the differential operators of an arbitrary finitely generated module.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
0\to K\to F\to M\to 0
be a short exact sequence of
R
-modules where

is
R
-free of finite rank.
Let

be an
R
-module.
Then, for all

, we have

{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N)\,=\,\big\{\delta\in{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(F,N)\mid\delta(K)=0\big\}.

Proof.

By left exactness of
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(\bullet,N)
, one has
{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(M,N)\hookrightarrow{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(F,N)
(see e.g.
[
10
, Lemma 2.7]
).
The inclusion “

” is clear.
Conversely, if
\delta\in{\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{k}}^{m}(F,N)
and

, then there is a unique map
\overline{\delta}\in\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{k}}(M,N)
induced by

, and by

,

Then

∎

3.2.
Punctual Quot schemes and Macaulay inverse systems

We are now ready to begin describing the correspondences in our main result.
The purpose of this subsection is to parametrize
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary
R
-submodules of a free module
R^{r}
via punctual Quot schemes and Macaulay inverse systems.

Let
\mathbb{B}
be the power series ring
\mathbb{B}:=\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]
over the residue field
\mathbb{F}=R_{\mathfrak{p}}/{\mathfrak{p}}R_{\mathfrak{p}}
and denote its maximal ideal by
{\mathfrak{n}}:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{c})\subseteq\mathbb{B}
.
The punctual Quot scheme
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)
parametrizes all quotients

\mathbb{B}^{r}\,=\,\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]^{r}\,\twoheadrightarrow\,T

such that
T
has finite length
m
, and consequently,
T
is only supported at the maximal ideal
{\mathfrak{n}}
.
More precisely, the
\mathbb{F}
-rational points of
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)
correspond to
\mathbb{B}
-submodules
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{r}
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V})=m

(in this paper,
a point in
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)
will always mean
an
\mathbb{F}
-rational point in
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)
).
This parameter space has been considered in several papers, e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
19
]
.
When
r=1
,
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)
coincides with the
punctual Hilbert scheme

{\rm Hilb}^{m}\left(\mathbb{F}[[y_{1},\ldots,y_{c}]]\right)
studied by Briançon
[
2
]
and Iarrobino
[
21
]
.

The following basic fact shows that any
point in
{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)
can be identified with a
(\mathbb{B}/{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B})
-submodule of
\mathbb{B}^{r}/{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B}^{r}
.

Proposition 3.13
.

For any
\mathbb{B}
-submodule
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{r}
with colength
m=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V}\right)
, one has
\mathbb{V}\supseteq{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B}^{r}
.

Proof.

Consider the associated graded module

\text{gr}(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V})\;:=\;\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathfrak{n}}^{k}\mathbb{B}^{r}/\left({\mathfrak{n}}^{k+1}\mathbb{B}^{r}+\left({\mathfrak{n}}^{k}\mathbb{B}^{r}\cap\mathbb{V}\right)\right)

which satisfies
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\text{gr}(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V})\right)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V}\right)
.
If
\mathbb{V}\not\supseteq{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B}^{r}
, then
\left[\text{gr}(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V})\right]_{k}\neq 0
for all
0\leq k\leq m
, so
m=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V}\right)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\text{gr}(\mathbb{B}^{r}/\mathbb{V})\right)\geq m+1
, a contradiction.
∎

Following the approach of
[
8
]
, we have an injection

\eta:R\hookrightarrow\mathbb{B}\,,\qquad\begin{matrix}x_{i}&\mapsto&y_{i}+\overline{x_{i}},&\!\!\!\!\!\mbox{ for }1\leq i\leq c,\\
x_{j}&\mapsto&\overline{x_{j}},&\quad\,\mbox{ for }c+1\leq j\leq n,\end{matrix}

where
\overline{x_{i}}
denotes the class of
x_{i}
in
\mathbb{F}
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
We now define the induced injection

\gamma:R^{r}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{r},\quad(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})\in R^{r}\mapsto(\eta(f_{1}),\ldots,\eta(f_{r}))\in\mathbb{B}^{r}.

The map
\gamma
provides the first correspondence
(a)

\leftrightarrow

(b)
in our main theorem.

Theorem 3.14
.

With the above notation, there is a bijection

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{${\mathfrak{p}}$-primary $R$-submodules of $R^{r}$}\\
\mbox{of multiplicity $m$ over ${\mathfrak{p}}$}\end{array}\right\}\par&\longleftrightarrow&\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{points in }{\rm Quot}^{m}(\mathbb{B}^{r})\\
\end{array}\right\}\\
U&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{B}\cdot\gamma(U)+{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B}^{r}\\
U=\gamma^{-1}(\mathbb{V})&\longleftarrow&\mathbb{V}.\end{array}

Proof.

The canonical map

,

gives a bijection between
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary
R
-submodules and
{\mathfrak{p}}S
-primary

-submodules (see e.g.
[

, Theorem 4.1]
). By
[

induces an isomorphism of local rings
S/{\mathfrak{p}}^{m}S\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{B}/{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B}
.
Accordingly, we obtain a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{B}^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{r}/{\mathfrak{p}}^{m}S^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{B}^{r}/{\mathfrak{n}}^{m}\mathbb{B}^{r}.}
\cong
\gamma

By

, any
\mathbb{B}
-submodule
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{r}
with

Similarly, any

Therefore, the result follows because under the above identifications the constancy of multiplicity equal to

∎

We next recall the well-known Macaulay inverse systems for modules.

Since

(for more details see e.g.
[

Let
\mathbb{E}
be the polynomial ring
\mathbb{E}:=\mathbb{F}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{c}]

-module by letting

act as

, i.e.

for any

Since

We describe Macaulay inverse systems via Matlis duality.

denote the Matlis dual functor (see e.g.

For any

The above discussion then recovers the following well-known result.

Theorem 3.15

(Macaulay inverse systems)
.

With the above notation, there is a bijection

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{points in }{\rm Quot}^{m}\left(\mathbb{B}^{r}\right)\\
\end{array}\right\}\par&\longleftrightarrow&\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{$m$-dimensional $\mathbb{F}$-subspaces of}\\
\mbox{$\mathbb{E}^{r}$ closed under differentiation}\end{array}\right\}\\
\mathbb{V}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\\
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{W}^{\perp}&\longleftarrow&\mathbb{W}.\\
\end{array}

, Proposition 3]
, it follows that

\{w\in R^{r}\mid\delta(w)\in{\mathfrak{p}}\text{ for all }\delta\in\mathcal{G}\}=\text{Sol}(\mathcal{E})=\Gamma^{-1}(\mathbb{V})=U
, which is
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary by
§4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

When we consider whole sets of variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
, we can describe primary submodules in quite different ways.
This obstacle tells us that without restricting the variables, the best extension of
Theorem 3.2
that we can obtain is that: we can always recover a primary submodule from any of its possibly many ways to encode it with Noetherian operators.
In
Theorem 3.2
we obtained a
bijection
between four different sets of objects, but if we allow whole sets of variables a bijection is not attainable.
For instance, see
[
8
, Example 10]
, where a primary ideal of codimension two in
\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}]
is represented with two quite different sets of Noetherian operators.
One of them is given by sixteen differential operators with constant coefficients in
D_{4}
, and the other is given by eleven differential operators (some of them with polynomial coefficients) in
D_{4,2}\subset D_{4}
.

4.2.
Differential primary decomposition for modules

The main goal of this subsection is to provide an algorithm that computes a
minimal differential primary decomposition
for general modules.
The concept of a
differential primary decomposition
was introduced in
[
9
]
, and an algorithm for the case of ideals was given in
[
9
, Algorithm 5.4]
.

First, we extend the notation in
Section 3
, for multiple primes
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
.
For a set of variables
\mathcal{S}=\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\ell}}\}\subseteq\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, consider the corresponding
relative Weyl algebra
,
which consists of
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{S}]
-linear differential operators on
R
:

D_{n}(\mathcal{S}):={\text{Diff}}_{R/\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{S}]}(R,R)=R\big<\partial_{x_{i}}\mid x_{i}\not\in\mathcal{S}\big>\subseteq R\langle\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\rangle=D_{n}.

We now recall the definition of the main object of interest in this section.

Definition 4.6

(cf.
[
9
, Definition 5.2, Definition 4.1]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq R^{r}
be an
R
-module,
with associated primes
\text{Ass}(R^{r}/U)=:\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}\}
.
A
differential primary decomposition
of
U
is a list of triples

({\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathfrak{A}_{1}),\;({\mathfrak{p}}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathfrak{A}_{2}),\;\ldots,\;({\mathfrak{p}}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathfrak{A}_{k})

where
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is a basis modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
and

\mathfrak{A}_{i}\subseteq D_{n}(\mathcal{S}_{i})^{r}
is a finite set of differential operators such that

\qquad U_{\mathfrak{p}}\cap R^{r}\,\,=\;\bigcap_{i:{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathfrak{p}}}\!\!\big\{w\in R^{r}\mid\delta(w)\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}\text{ for all }\delta\in\mathfrak{A}_{i}\big\}\quad\text{for each ${\mathfrak{p}}\in\text{Ass}(R^{r}/U)$.}

These conditions imply that

U\;=\;\big\{w\in R^{r}\mid\delta(w)\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}\text{ for all }\delta\in\mathfrak{A}_{i}\text{ and }1\leq i\leq k\big\}
.
The
size
of the differential primary decomposition is defined to be
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\mathfrak{A}_{i}|
.

Definition 4.7

(
[
32
]
,
[
9
, Definition 4.3]
)
.

For an
R
-submodule
U\subseteq R^{r}
, its
arithmetic multiplicity

is the non-negative integer

{\text{amult}}(U)\,\,:=\sum_{{\mathfrak{p}}\in\text{Ass}(R^{r}/U)}\!\!\!\text{length}_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}\left(\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{0}\Big(R^{r}_{\mathfrak{p}}/U_{\mathfrak{p}}\Big)\right)\quad=\sum_{{\mathfrak{p}}\in\text{Ass}(R^{r}/U)}\!\!\!\text{length}_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}\left(\frac{\left(U_{\mathfrak{p}}:_{R^{r}_{\mathfrak{p}}}{({\mathfrak{p}}R_{\mathfrak{p}})}^{\infty}\right)}{U_{\mathfrak{p}}}\right).

In
[
9
]
, it was shown that the arithmetic multiplicity is the minimal size for a differential primary decomposition of a module (see
[
9
, Theorems 3.6, 4.6, 5.3]
).
We point out that a differential primary decomposition
should not
be thought of as a notion directly related to an ordinary primary decomposition.
In
§4.2
, a differential primary decomposition is carefully designed to recover the localizations of a module along its associated primes; recall that the embedded components of a primary decomposition are not an invariant of a module, but the localizations at the associated primes are.
Given an
R
-module
U\subseteq R^{r}
and a differential primary decomposition
({\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathfrak{A}_{1}),\ldots,({\mathfrak{p}}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathfrak{A}_{k})
, the
\mathbb{k}
-vector space
\{w\in R^{r}\mid\delta(w)\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}\text{ for all }\delta\in\mathfrak{A}_{i}\}
may not be an
R
-module for some
1\leq i\leq k
.
Below we have an example signaling this important phenomenon.

Example 4.8
.

Let
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},x_{2}]
and
I=(x_{1}^{2},x_{1}x_{2})\subset R
be an ideal.
The associated primes of
I
are
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}=(x_{1})
and
{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}=(x_{1},x_{2})
.
A minimal differential primary decomposition of
I
is given by the two triples
({\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\{x_{1}\},\{1\})
and
({\mathfrak{p}}_{2},\{\},\{\partial_{x_{1}}\})
, and the arithmetic multiplicity of
I
is
{\text{amult}}(I)=2
.
We have the following equality

I=\{f\in R\mid f\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\}\;\cap\;\{f\in R\mid\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\}.

Notice that
V_{2}=\{f\in R\mid\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\}
is the
\mathbb{k}
-vector space of polynomials
f\in R
such that
x_{1}
does not appear in the expansion of
f
.
Therefore, since
1\in V_{2}
and yet
x_{1}\not\in V_{2}
, it follows that
V_{2}
is not an
R
-module.

Finally, an ordinary primary decomposition of
I
is given by
I={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\cap(x_{1}^{2},x_{2})
, where the primary components are described as
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}=\{f\in R\mid f\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}\}
and
(x_{1}^{2},x_{2})=\{f\in R\mid f\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\text{ and }\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}\in{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}\}
in terms of Noetherian operators – notice that this gives the
non-minimal
differential primary decomposition
({\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\{x_{1}\},\{1\})
and
({\mathfrak{p}}_{2},\{\},\{1,\partial_{x_{1}}\})
.

By using our representation theorem (
Theorem 3.2
), we can extend
[
9
, Algorithm 5.4]
from ideals to modules.
The following algorithm computes a minimal differential primary decomposition for a module.
We note that this algorithm does not depend on computing a primary decomposition.

Algorithm 4.9

(Differential primary decomposition for modules)
.

Input:
An
R
-submodule
U\subseteq R^{r}
.

Output:
A differential primary decomposition for
U
of minimal size
{\rm amult}(U)
.

(1)

Compute the set of associated primes
\text{Ass}(R^{r}/U)=:\{{\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}\}
, e.g. via
Theorem 2.1
.

(2)

For
i
from
1
to
k
do:

(2.1)

Compute a basis
\mathcal{S}_{i}
modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
, and let
\mathbb{F}_{i}:=k({\mathfrak{p}}_{i})
be the residue field of
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
.

(2.2)

Compute
\mathcal{U}:=U_{{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}}\cap R^{r}
– this is the intersection of
all primary components of
U
whose associated prime is contained in
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
, e.g. via
Section 2
.

(2.3)

Compute
\mathcal{V}:=\mathcal{U}:_{R^{r}}{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}^{\infty}
– this is the intersection of
all primary components of
U
whose associated prime is strictly contained in
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
, e.g. via
Section 2
.

(2.4)

Find
m>0
giving the isomorphism in
[
9
, Proposition 4.5]
(i):

\mathcal{V}/\mathcal{U}\xrightarrow{\cong}(\mathcal{V}+{{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}}^{m}R^{r})/(\mathcal{U}+{{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}}^{m}R^{r}).

(2.5)

By using
Theorem 3.2
and
§4.1
, compute the
\mathbb{F}_{i}
-vector subspaces
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{H}
of the Weyl-Noether module

\mathbb{F}_{i}\otimes_{R}D_{n}(\mathcal{S}_{i})^{r}
.
These are
(R\otimes_{\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{S}_{i}]}R)
-modules that correspond to the
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
-primary submodules
\mathcal{U}+{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}^{m}R^{r}
and
\mathcal{V}+{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}^{m}R^{r}
, respectively.

(2.6)

Compute an
\mathbb{F}_{i}
-basis
\overline{\mathfrak{A}_{i}}
of an
\mathbb{F}_{i}
-vector space complement
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{H}
in
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{G}
.

(2.7)

Lift the basis
\overline{\mathfrak{A}_{i}}
to a subset
\mathfrak{A}_{i}\subseteq D_{n}(\mathcal{S}_{i})^{r}
.

(3)

Return the triples
({\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathfrak{A}_{1})
,
\ldots
,
({\mathfrak{p}}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathfrak{A}_{k})
.

Proof of correctness of
§4.2
.

This algorithm is correct because it realizes the steps in the proof of
[
9
, Theorem 5.3 (i)]
, as generalized by
Theorem 3.2
and
§4.1
.
(For the case of ideals, see
[
9
, Algorithm 5.4]
.)
∎

Remark 4.10
.

The reverse process of
§4.2
can also be achieved.
If we are given a differential primary decomposition
({\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathfrak{A}_{1})
,
\ldots
,
({\mathfrak{p}}_{k},\mathcal{S}_{k},\mathfrak{A}_{k})
as the output of
§4.2
, then via
§4.1
we can compute the
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
-primary submodule
U_{i}\subseteq R^{r}
that corresponds to
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
, so that
U=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}
.
In this way a differential primary decomposition gives a primary decomposition as in
Section 2
.
In general though, this “dual” algorithm for primary decomposition will be slower than the algorithm given in
Section 2
, as the bulk of the computation in
§4.2
is subsumed in step (2.5) which entails heavy computations over the residue field
\mathbb{F}_{i}
.

5.
A differential description of joins

In this short section, we provide an intrinsic differential description of the ideal join of an ideal and a primary ideal with respect to the maximal irrelevant ideal. This result was implicitly obtained in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 7.1]
, but the statement was not explicitly given as the objective was to get an extension of a result of Sullivant
[
33
, Proposition 2.8]
.

Let
\mathbb{k}
be a field of characteristic zero,
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring and
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\subseteq R
be the maximal irrelevant ideal.
If
J
and
K
are ideals in
R
, then their
join
is given by the new ideal

J\star K\,\,\,:=\,\,\,\Big(J(\mathbf{v})\,+\,K(\mathbf{w})\,+\,\left(x_{i}-v_{i}-w_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\right)\Big)\,\,\cap\,\,R,

where
J(\mathbf{v})\subseteq\mathbb{k}[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]
is the ideal
J
with new variables
v_{i}
substituted for
x_{i}
and
K(\mathbf{w})\subseteq\mathbb{k}[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}]
is the ideal
K
with
w_{i}
substituted for
x_{i}
.

The next definitions are obtained by mimicking
[
8
, Definition 4]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
M\subseteq R
be an
\mathfrak{m}
-primary ideal.
We compute a finite dimensional
\mathbb{k}
-subspace
\mathfrak{A}(M)\subseteq\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
of differential operators with constant coefficients by performing the following steps:

(i)

Interpret the variable
x_{i}
as
\partial_{z_{i}}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(ii)

Compute
M^{\perp}=\left\{F\in\mathbb{k}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]\mid f\cdot F=0\mbox{ for all }f\in M\right\}
.

(iii)

Let
\mathfrak{A}(M)\subseteq\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
be the image of
M^{\perp}
under the map
\mathbf{z}^{\alpha}\mapsto\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}
.

A
\mathbb{k}
-subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
is said to be
closed under the bracket operation
if
[\delta,x_{i}]\in V
for all
\delta\in V
and
1\leq i\leq n
.
Recall that
\left[\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{i}}^{\alpha_{i}}\cdots\partial_{x_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}},\,x_{i}\right]=\alpha_{i}\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{i}}^{\alpha_{i}-1}\cdots\partial_{x_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
1\leq i\leq n
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
be a finite dimensional
\mathbb{k}
-subspace closed under the bracket operation.
We compute an
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary ideal
\mathfrak{B}(V)\subseteq R
by performing the following steps:

(i)

Interpret the variable
x_{i}
as
\partial_{z_{i}}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(ii)

Let
W\subseteq\mathbb{k}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
be the image of
V
under the map
\partial_{\mathbf{x}}^{\alpha}\mapsto\mathbf{z}^{\alpha}
.

(iii)

Let
\mathfrak{B}(V)=W^{\perp}=\left\{f\in R\mid f\cdot F=0\mbox{ for all }F\in W\right\}
.

The following result can be easily deduced from
[
8
, Theorem 7.1]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
J\subseteq R
be an ideal.
Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

If
M\subseteq R
is an
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary ideal, then

J\star M\;=\;\big\{f\in R\mid\delta\cdot f\in J\;\text{ for all }\;\delta\in\mathfrak{A}(M)\big\}.

(ii)

If
V\subseteq\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
is a finite dimensional
\mathbb{k}
-subspace closed under the bracket operation, then

\big\{f\in R\mid\delta\cdot f\in J\;\text{ for all }\;\delta\in V\big\}\;=\;J\star\mathfrak{B}(V).

Proof.

(i) This is the statement of
[
8
, Theorem 7.1 (i)]
.

(ii) Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
be a finite dimensional
\mathbb{k}
-subspace closed under the bracket operation, and set
M
to be the corresponding
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary ideal
M=\mathfrak{B}(V)
.
Notice that
V=\mathfrak{A}(M)
and
M=\mathfrak{B}(V)
, as both
\mathfrak{A}(M)
and
\mathfrak{B}(V)
are computed via Macaulay inverse systems.
By
[
8
, Theorem 7.1 (i)]
, we obtain

\big\{f\in R\mid\delta\cdot f\in J\;\text{ for all }\;\delta\in V\big\}\;=\;J\star M,

and so the result follows.
∎

Of particular interest is the case when
J
is a prime ideal.
Let
{\mathfrak{p}}\in{\text{Spec}}(R)
be a prime ideal.
A
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary ideal
Q\subseteq R
is said to be
representable by differential operators with constant coefficients
if there exist
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m}\in\mathbb{k}[\partial_{x_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}]
such that
Q=\{f\in R\mid\delta_{i}\cdot f\in{\mathfrak{p}}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq m\}
.
A
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary
Q\subseteq R
is said to be
representable by the join construction
if there exists an
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary ideal
M\subseteq R
such that
Q={\mathfrak{p}}\star M
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
{\mathfrak{p}}\in{\text{Spec}}(R)
be a prime ideal and
Q\subseteq R
be a
{\mathfrak{p}}
-primary ideal.
Then,
Q
is representable by differential operators with constant coefficients if and only if
Q
is representable by the join construction.

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem 5.3
.
∎
