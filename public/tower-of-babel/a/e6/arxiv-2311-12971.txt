An improved method to measure C 12 / 13 C \rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C and N 14 / 15 N \rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N abundance ratios: revisiting CN isotopologues in the Galactic outer disk 2023 An improved method to measure C 12 / 13 C \rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C and N 14 / 15 N \rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N abundance ratios: revisiting CN isotopologues in the Galactic outer disk – 29
Yichen Sun, Zhi-Yu Zhang, Junzhi Wang, Lingrui Lin, Padelis P. Papadopoulos, Donatella Romano, Siyi Feng, Yan Sun, Bo Zhang, Francesca Matteucci

Abstract
The variations of elemental abundance and their ratios along the Galactocentric radius result from the chemical evolution of the Milky Way disks. The C 12 / 13 C \rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C ratio in particular is often used as a proxy to determine other isotopic ratios, such as O 16 / 18 O \rm{}^{16}O/^{18}O and N 14 / 15 N \rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N . Measurements of CN 12 \rm{}^{12}CN and CN 13 \rm{}^{13}CN (or C 15 ​ N \rm C^{15}N ) – with their optical depths corrected via their hyper-fine structure lines – have traditionally been exploited to constrain the Galactocentric gradients of the CNO isotopic ratios. Such methods typically make several simplifying assumptions (e.g. a filling factor of unity, the Rayleigh–Jeans approximation, and the neglect of the cosmic microwave background) while adopting a single average gas phase. However, these simplifications introduce significant biases to the measured C 12 / 13 C \rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C and N 14 / 15 N \rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N . We demonstrate that exploiting the optically thin satellite lines of 12 CN constitutes a more reliable new method to derive C 12 / 13 C \rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C and N 14 / 15 N \rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N from CN isotopologues. We apply this satellite-line method to new IRAM 30-m observations of 12 CN, 13 CN, and C 15 N N = 1 → 0 N=1\to 0 towards 15 metal-poor molecular clouds in the Galactic outer disk ( R gc > R_{\rm gc}> 12 kpc), supplemented by data from the literature. After updating their Galactocentric distances, we find that C 12 / 13 C \rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C and N 14 / 15 N \rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N gradients are in good agreement with those derived using independent optically thin molecular tracers, even in regions with the lowest metallicities. We therefore recommend using optically thin tracers for Galactic and extragalactic CNO isotopic measurements, which avoids the biases associated with the traditional method.

Introduction

The isotopic abundance ratios of carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen, namely
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
(
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
),
\rm{}^{16}O/^{18}O
(
R_{\rm{}^{16}O/^{18}O}
), and
\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N
(
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
), are crucial for constraining the evolutionary
chemical history of the Milky Way
(
Wilson &
Rood 1994
;
Romano 2022
)
. Systematic
variations in these ratios are caused by the different time scales according to
which different isotopes are ejected in the interstellar medium by stars of
different initial masses

(
Nomoto
et al. 2013
;
Karakas &
Lattanzio 2014
;
Cristallo et al. 2015
;
Limongi &
Chieffi 2018
)
. Furthermore, the
growth history of the Galactic thin disk has a significant impact on these
ratios, resulting in strong evolutionary features

(
Matteucci &
D’Antona 1991
;
Henkel &
Mauersberger 1993
;
Romano &
Matteucci 2003
;
Romano et al. 2017
;
Romano et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. In
particular,
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
,
R_{\rm{}^{16}O/^{18}O}
, and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
generally increase with
Galactocentric distances (
R_{\rm gc}
)

(
Wilson &
Rood 1994
;
Wouterloot et al. 2008
;
Jacob et al. 2020
;
Zhang
et al. 2020
;
Chen
et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, though

R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
shows a decreasing trend in the Galactic outer disk
(
Colzi et al. 2022
)
.

The production of metal elements and their isotopes depends on nucleosynthesis
and stellar evolutionary processes
(
Burbidge et al. 1957
;
Meyer 1994
)
. For
example, the
\rm{}^{12}C
production is dominated by triple-
\alpha
reactions

(
Timmes
et al. 1995
;
Woosley &
Weaver 1995
)
, while
\rm{}^{13}C
can be produced by the CNO-I
cycle,
\rm{}^{12}C
-burning, or proton-capture nucleosynthesis

(
Meynet &
Maeder 2002b
;
Botelho et al. 2020
)
. The
\rm{}^{14}N
can be synthesized through
the cold CNO cycle in the H-burning zone of stars

(
Karakas &
Lattanzio 2014
;
Romano 2022
)
,
\rm{}^{12}C
-burning in the low-metallicity
fast massive rotators, and proton-capture reactions in the hot convective
envelope of intermediate-mass stars

(
Marigo 2001
;
Pettini et al. 2002
;
Meynet &
Maeder 2002a
;
Limongi &
Chieffi 2018
;
Botelho et al. 2020
)
. The
production of
\rm{}^{15}N
and
\rm{}^{13}C
can happen in hot-CNO cycles in
H-rich material accreting on white dwarfs

(
Audouze
et al. 1973
;
Wiescher et al. 2010
;
Romano 2022
)
. The Galactic
\rm{}^{15}N
is
contributed by novae
(
Romano et al. 2017
)
and proton ingestion in the He shell of
massive stars
(
Pignatari
et al. 2015
)
.

Despite extensive study, the isotopic ratio gradients in the low-metallicity
outer disk of the Milky Way remain poorly constrained. To date, measurements of
only one target, WB89-391, at a Galactocentric distance
R_{\rm gc}

\gtrsim
12 kpc,
have been made for the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
ratio using CN isotopologues
(
Milam et al. 2005
)
.
This single measurement (
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}

\sim
134) critically constrains the
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
and
R_{\rm{}^{16}O/^{18}O}
gradients derived from C-bearing isotopologues. To fully
constrain all CNO isotopic ratios in the outer Galactic disk, more data and
more precise measurements of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
are needed.

The
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
ratios in the ISM are determined using pairs of molecular
isotopologues. Measurements of
\rm{}^{12}C^{18}O/^{13}C^{18}O

(
Langer &
Penzias 1990
;
Wouterloot &
Brand 1996
;
Giannetti
et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
or
\rm{}^{12}C^{34}S/^{13}C^{34}S

(
Yan et al. 2023
)
may be limited to
nearby strong targets because of the weak emission of
\rm{}^{13}C^{18}O
or
\rm{}^{13}C^{34}S

expected in the metal-poor outer disk clouds. Molecules such as
\rm H_{2}CO/H_{2}^{13}CO

(
Henkel
et al. 1982
;
Henkel
et al. 1985
;
Yan et al. 2019
)
,
\rm CH^{+}/^{13}CH^{+}

(
Ritchey
et al. 2011
)
, and
\rm CH/^{13}CH

(
Jacob et al. 2020
)

can be good tracers to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
through their absorption lines.

\rm H_{2}CO
isotopologues can be absorbed by the CMB but their absorption lines are still weak because of low abundance.
The CH and
\rm CH^{+}
absorption lines are in rare cases of strong background continuum and/or high column density.
The optically-thick line pairs, such as
13
CN and
12
CN
N=1\to 0
, which require
corrections to their optical depths that are mostly determined by fitting
hyper-fine-structure (HfS) lines
(
Savage et al. 2002
;
Milam et al. 2005
)
, could be adopted
to measure
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
in the outer disk regions. This method can also
derive
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
without any assumptions of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}

(
Adande &
Ziurys 2012
)
.

The current commonly used method for deriving the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
from CN isotopologues, with the HfS
fitting, makes several
assumptions and approximations. In order to derive the column density
(
Mangum &
Shirley 2015
, i.e.,)
, it
assumes identical excitation temperature of
12
CN and
13
CN. However differential radiative trapping among spectral lines,
due to different optical depths (if these are significant), can yield different excitation temperatures between the spectral lines.
Also, a single gas phase is typically used (necessitated by the small number of transitions per species typically available,
and lack of standard molecular cloud models), while a range of excitation conditions is present in molecular clouds impacting the
volume-average excitation temperatures of the CN isotopologues. In addition, most studies
(
Savage et al. 2002
;
Milam et al. 2005
, e.g.,)
adopt the
Rayleigh-Jeans (R-J) approximation, which becomes increasingly poor when
h\nu_{\rm ik}/k_{\rm B}T_{\rm ex}\lesssim 1
starts approaching unity (and is of course inappropriate when
h\nu_{\rm ik}/k_{\rm B}T_{\rm ex}\gtrsim 1
). In the cold (
T_{\rm kin}\sim 15-20
K), often sub-thermal line excitation conditions (
T_{\rm ex}
<
T_{\rm kin}
) prevailing in the bulk of molecular clouds in the quiescent ISM of the Galaxy, the R-J approximation
is simply not good enough for abundance ratio studies conducted using the CN
N=1\to 0
and
N=2\to 1
at 112 GHz and 224 GHz.
In addition, the cosmic microwave background (CMB) must be taken into account given that for the low-temperature
molecular clouds where such isotopologue studies are conducted (and with the possible sub-thermal excitation of the lines utilized)
, the line Planck temperature
J(\nu_{\rm ik},T_{\rm ex})=h\nu_{\rm ik}/k_{\rm B}\,\left(e^{h\nu_{\rm ik}/k_{B}T_{\rm ex}}-1\right)^{-1}

can be so low that the
J(\nu_{\rm ik},T_{\rm CMB})
is no longer negligible for the frequencies used, even for the low
T_{\rm CMB,0}=2.72
K)
in the local Universe
(see
Zhang et al. 2016
, for even more serious effects in the high-z Universe where
T_{\rm CMB}(z)=(1+z)T_{\rm CMB,0}
)
.
All these assumptions/approximations have been used for such studies conducted in widely different ISM environments,
ranging from the very cold and quiescent ISM in the Galactic outer disk
(
Milam et al. 2005
)
, where they become questionable, up to the warm, dense ISM in starburst galaxies
(
Henkel et al. 1993
;
Henkel
et al. 2014
;
Tang
et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
where the aforementioned assumptions/approximations should be adequate.

In this work, we introduce an improved method to derive
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
from CN isotopologues and compare it with current methods. We list the basic assumptions of the current methods of using CN isotopologues
to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
. We also present new
12
CN,
13
CN and C
15
N
N=1\to 0
observations in a small sample of molecular clouds with
R_{\rm gc}

>
12 kpc, which allows new constraints on the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
outer gradients. Section
2
presents the observations and the data reduction.
In Section
3
, we list the current methods of deriving
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
with CN isotopologues and show their underlying assumptions and drawbacks. In Section
4
, we introduce a new method to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
. In Section
5
, we present newly measured isotopic ratios and data derived from the improved traditional methods and the new method. In Section
6
, we compare the Galactic
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
obtained by the different methods. We also compare CN isotopologues and the optically thin tracers
\rm{}^{12}C^{18}O/^{13}C^{18}O
, and we discuss physical and chemical processes that may bias the abundances. We present the main conclusion in Section
7
.

3.1
Assumptions in the ‘traditional’ models

First, we list the common assumptions in deriving
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
and
\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N
from
emission lines of CN isotopologues. Here we take a simple example to obtain
abundance ratios of
\rm{{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
\rm{{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
from
12
CN,
13
CN, and
\rm C^{15}N
with their
N=1\to 0

transition lines. For higher
N
levels, the method is essentially the same. To
perform such derivations, several basic assumptions are needed for all models
(details are shown in Appendix
B
) :

1.

Column density ratios of isotopologues represent abundance ratios of isotopes, meaning that astrochemical effects are neglected.

2.

In all regions, the populations at the energy levels that give rise to the HfS lines are assumed to have identical
T_{\rm ex}
among them.

3.

Differences in the dipole moment matrix, the rotational partition function, the upper energy level, and the degeneracy of the upper energy between
12
CN,
13
CN, and C
15
N are ignored.

4.

The
N=1\to 0
transitions of
12
CN,
13
CN, and C
15
N have identical rotational temperatures.

With these assumptions, ratios between column densities of
12
CN,
13
CN, and C
15
N (hereafter, we only consider the
12
CN and
13
CN pair, which is identical to the
12
CN and C
15
N pair. ) equal their respective optical depth ratios, for
N=1\to 0
:

\frac{N_{\rm{}^{12}CN}}{N_{\rm{}^{13}CN}}=\frac{\tau_{\rm{}^{12}CN}}{\tau_{\rm{}^{13}CN}},

(4)

where
N_{\rm{}^{12}CN}
and
N_{\rm{}^{13}CN}
are column densities of
12
CN and
13
CN, respectively.
\tau_{\rm{}^{12}CN}
and
\tau_{\rm{}^{13}CN}
are the total optical depths of
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
, respectively.

Ratios between the main beam temperatures of isotopologue lines would satisfy:

\frac{T_{\rm b,^{12}CN}}{T_{\rm b,^{13}CN}}=\frac{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm m,^{12}CN}}}{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm m,^{13}CN}}}=\frac{T_{\rm mb,^{12}CN}}{T_{\rm mb,^{13}CN}}

(5)

where
T_{\rm b,^{12}CN}
and
T_{\rm b,^{13}CN}
are the peak brightness
temperatures of the main components of
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
,
respectively. The main beam temperature of
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
main
component is
T_{\rm mb,^{12}CN}
and
T_{\rm mb,^{13}CN}
, respectively.
The right-hand side of Equation
5
is satisfied if we assume the beam filling
factors
f
of
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
to be the same. Should the sources
have been resolved by the 30-m beam, the same assumption would then have to be
made for the corresponding irreducible (source-structure)-beam
coupling factors
\rm\eta_{\rm c}

(
Kutner &
Ulich 1981
, e.g.)
. As
\tau_{\rm m,^{12}CN}
and

we indicate the optical depths of the main HfS

at

3.2
Formula to derive
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
and
\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N
in the literature

The traditional equation to derive
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
is presented in
Savage et al. 2002
:

\frac{\rm{{}^{12}C}}{\rm{{}^{13}C}}=\frac{(3/5)\tau_{\rm m}T_{\rm ex,^{12}CN}}{T^{\star}_{\rm R,^{13}CN}/\eta_{\rm c,^{13}CN}},

(6)

where
T^{\star}_{\rm R,^{13}CN}
is the line temperature measured from the observed spectra,

\eta_{\rm c,^{13}CN}
is the beam efficiency,
T_{\rm ex,^{12}CN}
is the excitation
temperature of
12
CN
N=1\to 0
main component; and the factor 5/3 is a conversion factor
from the column density ratio of the main components between
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
to
all components of
12
CN and
13
CN in this transition.

However, this Equation
6
and the corresponding Equations 2 and 3
in
Savage et al. 2002
, where
T^{\rm\star}_{\rm R}/\eta_{\rm c}
appears, contain two issues:
First,
\eta_{\rm c}
is set as the antenna beam efficiency. This is incorrect since at the
NRAO 12-m telescope the
T^{\rm\star}_{\rm R}
scale is already corrected for both atmosphere and
all telescope efficiency factors
7
7

7

User’s Manual For The NRA0 12M Millimeter-Wave Telescope, J. Mangum, 01/18/00

, while
\eta_{\rm c}
stands for an
irreducible
(source-structure)-beam coupling factor, instead of a beam efficiency
(see
Kutner &
Ulich 1981
, for details)
.
Second, the background correction is only considered in the denominator.

T^{\rm\star}_{\rm R}/\eta_{\rm c}=T_{\rm R}-T_{\rm bg}
, where
T_{\rm R}
and
T_{\rm bg}
are the source radiation temperature and background emission (the CMB), respectively.
The excitation temperature,
T_{\rm ex}
in the nominator, on the other hand, does not subtract
T_{\rm bg}
.
Unfortunately, the same problems exist also in
Milam et al. 2005
.

If both
T^{\rm\star}_{\rm R}
and
\eta_{\rm c}
were set as their original definitions, i.e.,

T^{\star}_{\rm R}
is the observed source antenna temperature corrected for atmospheric attenuation, radiative loss, and rearward and forward scattering and spillover, and
\eta_{\rm c}
as the efficiency at which the source couples to the telescope beam, then Equation
6
still stands, as long as the background temperature is negligible, i.e. the target is warm enough compared to the CMB.
However, the coupling factor,
\eta_{\rm c}
, is unknown because the source size and geometry are unclear, unless we assume that the sources are big enough to cover the whole forward beam.

Besides the aforementioned assumptions and problems the traditional method
contains also the following unstated assumptions:

•

A beam filling factor of
\sim
1 for both
13
CN and
12
CN
N=1\to 0
lines.
Equations 2 and 3 in
Savage et al. 2002
can only be understood if the source geometric
beam filling factor
f_{\rm s}
is set to
\sim
1 (i.e. extended targets fully resolved
in both
12
CN and
13
CN), and the intrinsic (source structure)-beam coupling factor
\eta_{\rm c}

is also
\sim
1.

•

The Rayleigh-Jeans approximation is adopted for expressing line radiation temperature,

•

Negligible contribution from the CMB emission,

•

The line optical depth is uniformly distributed within the beam size – a flat spatial distribution.

Most of these dense gas clumps are spatially compact within
<
1 pc scales
(
Wu
et al. 2010
;
Tafalla et al. 2002
, e.g.)
,
especially for those targets from the outer Galactic disk. Most main-beam of single-dish telescopes could cover the emitting regions of
12
CN and
13
CN lines.
Therefore, we update Equation
6
as follows,
to accommodate the temperature definition by the IRAM 30-m telescope (with identical
assumptions listed above):

\frac{\rm{{}^{12}C}}{\rm{{}^{13}C}}=\frac{(3/5)\tau_{\rm m}T_{\rm ex,^{12}CN}}{T^{\star}_{\rm A,^{13}CN}/\eta_{\rm mb,^{13}CN}},

(7)

where
T^{\rm\star}_{\rm A}
is the corrected antenna temperature, or, the forward beam brightness
temperature
(
Wilson et al. 2013
)
,
\eta_{\rm mb,^{13}CN}(=\frac{B_{\rm eff}}{F_{\rm eff}})
is
the main beam efficiency of
13
CN
N=1\to 0
, with
B_{\rm eff}
and
F_{\rm eff}
being the telescope beam efficiency
and forward efficiency, respectively;

T_{\rm ex,^{12}CN}
is the excitation temperature of
12
CN
N=1\to 0
main
component; The factor 5/3 is a conversion factor from the column density ratio
of the main components between
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
to all components of

12
CN and
13
CN in this transition.

We re-organized this “traditional” method and present the detailed derivation
in Appendix
B
. Note that a similar formula has also been adopted
to derive
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}

(
Adande &
Ziurys 2012
, e.g.,)

3.2.1
The hyper-fine structure of CN: fitting the optical depth

For
12
C
14
N and
\rm{}^{12}C^{15}N
, the nuclear spin of
\rm{}^{14}N

and
\rm{}^{15}N
couples in the total angular momentum, which generates
hyper-fine structures
(
Skatrud et al. 1983
;
Saleck
et al. 1994
, here labeled by

\boldsymbol{F}
;)
. For more complex

13
C
14
N, the angular momentum
\boldsymbol{J}
first couples with the
nuclear spin of
13
C atom to form an angular momentum

\boldsymbol{F_{13}}
, which further couples with the nitrogen nuclear spin to
form the total angular momentum
\boldsymbol{F}

(
Bogey
et al. 1984
)
.

For a
T_{\rm ex}
common among the various HfS CN satellite lines (which could
be different from e.g., the rotational excitation temperature
T_{\rm rot}
,
and
T_{\rm kin}
), their corresponding optical depth ratios are fixed by the
ratios of the corresponding
S
factors (line strengths) in the matrix element
\mu_{\rm ul}

(see Equations 62, 75 in
Mangum &
Shirley 2015
, but also
Skatrud et al. 1983
) that
enters the expression of the Einstein coefficients
A_{\rm ul}
of the hyperfine
lines. Should these lines be optically thin, the line optical depth ratios are
also the ratios of line strengths (assuming a common
T_{\rm ex}
among the
satellite lines involved).

One can derive the optical depths of
\rm{}^{12}CN

N=1\to 0
lines from main beam
temperature ratios between the nine components of the hyperfine structure lines

(
Savage et al. 2002
;
Milam et al. 2005
, or, a subset of them, e.g.  five components in )
, using:

\frac{T_{\rm mb,m,^{12}CN}}{T_{\rm mb,sat,^{12}CN}}=\frac{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm m,^{12}CN}}}{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm sat,^{12}CN}}}=\frac{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm m,^{12}CN}}}{1-e^{-R_{\rm h}\cdot\tau_{\rm m,^{12}CN}}},

(8)

where
T_{\rm mb,m,^{12}CN}
and
T_{\rm mb,sat,^{12}CN}
are the peak
main beam temperature of the main component and the satellite component of

12
CN
N=1\to 0
, respectively. We label the optical depths of the main
component and the satellite component as
\tau_{\rm m,^{12}CN}
and
\tau_{\rm sat,^{12}CN}
, and
R_{\rm h}
is the intrinsic intensity ratio between the satellite
line and the main component. This of course assumes that all these lines share the same
T_{\rm ex}
. This does not necessarily mean full local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE). Only common
excitation among HfS lines would work out, as
T_{\rm ex}
could be different from

T_{\rm rot}
or
T_{\rm kin}
).

We performed HfS fitting with the package developed by
Estalella 2017
,
which shows better robustness and stability than the HfS fitting method
provided in CLASS/Gildas (For details, see
Appendix
C
). The fitted optical depths of the main
line of

The R-J approximation gives deviation in the methods in

is smaller, in a non-linear fashion. At the

\leq 5

\sim 0.5

T_{\rm ex}\sim 4.3

Considering the CMB temperature

(

Appendix
B

T_{\rm ex,^{12}CN}-T_{\rm CMB}

negligible). However, for
T_{\rm ex}\sim
5.4 K, and
\sim
3.0 K,

\sim
50% and
\sim

Savage et al. 2002

\sim
(3–5) K. Therefore the CMB

detections, we
estimate the upper limits of the total velocity-integrated intensity
I
of
the two strongest lines.

3.5
Radiative transfer for multiple
T_{\rm ex}
layers

In real molecular clouds, the excitation temperature often shows an inhomogeneous distribution
inside molecular clouds, as multiple
T_{\rm ex}
layers
(
Zhou et al. 1993
;
Myers et al. 1996
, e.g., )
. To test for the possible effects of such excitation temperature differentials
on the derivation of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
, we set up a simple
toy model with two different
T_{\rm ex}
layers (Fig.
2
). In this model,
the background layer has a high excitation temperature
T_{\rm ex,H}
(H for hot) while
the foreground layer has a low excitation temperature
T_{\rm ex,C}
(C for cold).
Detailed description and derivation are shown in Appendix
B.4.1
.

Figure 2:
The toy model of molecular clouds with two
T_{\rm ex}
layers. .

Such a model indicates that the column density ratio and the brightness temperature ratio estimated from optical depth would systematically deviate from the intrinsic ratios with a simplified one-layer assumption. This is because the measured excitation temperature of
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
will change with the optical depth with multiple
T_{\rm ex}
layers. In our toy model, for
T_{\rm ex,H}=30
K,
T_{\rm ex,C}=10
K,
\tau_{\rm H}=\tau_{\rm C}=\tau
and
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
(intrinsic)=60, the intrinsic column density ratio and the intrinsic brightness temperature ratio will be
\sim
10% and
\sim
15% lower than the estimated ratios, respectively, when the optical depth of
12
CN main component
\tau^{\rm main}_{\rm eff}\sim 1
(i.e.
\sim
10% and
\sim
15% deviations from Eq.
4
and Eq.
5
, respectively). When
\tau^{\rm main}_{\rm eff}\sim 1
, which is close to our measured
\tau
of
12
CN main components in our targets, such deviations will cause a
\sim
17% decrease of the derived
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
compared to the intrinsic ratio (red dashed line in Fig.
3
and details in Appendix
B.4.1
).

Multiple
T_{\rm ex}
layers are significant when velocity spread is limited within linewidths of micro-turbulence and thermal motion, or when more than one fore/background cloud has identical
V_{\rm LSR}
. Observational evidence of self-absorption in
\rm{}^{12}CO

(
Enokiya
et al. 2021
)
,
\rm HCO^{+}

(
Richardson et al. 1986
)
, and even
\rm{}^{13}CO

(
Sandell &
Wright 2010
)
, which indicates multiple
T_{\rm ex}
layers, are found in multiple sources of the CN sample
(
Wouterloot &
Brand 1989
;
Savage et al. 2002
)
.

Other issues can also influence the derivation of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
. For example, the excitation temperature of
12
CN may be higher than that of
13
CN because of large optical depths and radiative trapping thermalizing the lines of the most abundant isotopologue at densities
\rm n=n_{crit,thin}(1-e^{-\tau})/\tau<n_{crit,thin}
. Non-LTE will also affect the derivation by changing the relative intensities between
12
CN line components. These are beyond the scope of this work and wait for future investigation.

3.6
The method to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
in extragalactic targets

Figure 3:
The theoretical values of derived
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
compared with the intrinsic values of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
for the traditional HfS method and the satellite line method, varying with the optical depth of
12
CN main component. We assume that
T_{\rm ex,H}=30
K,
T_{\rm ex,C}=10
K,
\tau_{\rm H}=\tau_{\rm C}=\tau
and
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
(intrinsic) = 60.

The observations of CN isotopologues in the extragalactic star-burst galaxies provide
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
derived from the following equation
(
Henkel
et al. 2014
;
Tang
et al. 2019
)
:

\frac{\rm{}^{12}C}{\rm{}^{13}C}=\frac{I_{\rm I,^{12}CN}\cdot\frac{\tau_{\rm I}}{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm I}}}+I_{\rm II,^{12}CN}\cdot\frac{\tau_{\rm II}}{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm II}}}}{1.082I_{\rm{}^{13}CN}}

(10)

Here,
I_{\rm I,^{12}CN}
and
I_{\rm II,^{12}CN}
are the total integrated intensities of
12
CN
N=1\to 0

J=3/2\to 1/2
and
12
CN
N=1\to 0

J=1/2\to 1/2
, respectively.
\tau_{\rm I}
and
\tau_{\rm II}
are the total optical depth of the blended line components in
N=1\to 0

J=3/2\to 1/2
and
J=1/2\to 1/2
, respectively.

This method will have the same deviation to
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
in Section
3.5
when the targets have complex excitation layers. Because of the large optical depth of
12
CN and the small optical depth of
13
CN, the effective
T_{\rm ex}
of
12
CN transitions will be smaller than that of
13
CN in our toy model, which causes the underestimation of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
. In addition, the optical depth is derived from an equation similar to Eq.
5

(
Tang
et al. 2019
, e.g., Eq. (1) in)
. In our toy model, the
\tau_{\rm I}
and
\tau_{\rm II}
derived in this way may be overestimated. The
T_{\rm ex}
layers will be more complex in reality than in our toy model, while differential excitation of
the lines due to radiative trapping will only add to such complexity. Thus the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
ratio estimated in this way still contains highly unclear uncertainties.

4
New method: deriving
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
and
\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N
with CN satellite lines and isotopologues

The satellite transitions of
12
CN
N=1\to 0
, including
J=1/2\to 1/2,F=1/2\to 1/2
(113.123 GHz) and
J=3/2\to 1/2,F=1/2\to 3/2
(113.520 GHz) are expected to be optically-thin because the intrinsic relative line strengths of
12
CN
N=1\to 0
at these transitions are
\sim 3.6\%
of that of the main component, which means an optical depth
\tau_{\rm s,^{12}CN}\sim 3.6\%
of
\tau_{\rm main,^{12}CN}
.

Therefore, the effect of different
T_{\rm ex}
layers on the derivation to
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
should be small based on our analysis. If the optical depth of the
12
CN
N=1\to 0,J=3/2\to 1/2,F=1/2\to 3/2
is less than 1, the theoretical deviation of the derived
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
will be
\lesssim
0.5%. This deviation is much smaller than the deviation in the traditional HfS method and can be ignored (see Fig
3
).

The
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
can be derived from the following equation:

\frac{\rm{}^{12}C}{\rm{}^{13}C}=\frac{R^{\rm I/sum}_{13}}{R^{\rm I/sum}_{12}}\frac{I_{1}+I_{2}}{I_{\rm{}^{13}CN}}

(11)

Here,
R^{\rm I/sum}_{12}
is the integrated intensity ratio between the sum of the two satellite lines and the sum of all the nine lines of
12
CN
N=1\to 0
.
R^{\rm I/sum}_{13}
is the ratio between the integrated intensities of the two strongest line components and that of all the components of
13
CN
N=1\to 0
.
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are the integrated intensities of
12
CN
N=1\to 0

J=1/2\to 1/2,F=1/2\to 1/2
and
12
CN
N=1\to 0

J=3/2\to 1/2,F=1/2\to 3/2
, respectively.
I_{\rm{}^{13}CN}
is the integrated intensity of the two strongest components of
13
CN
N=1\to 0
. With the criteria in Section
2.3.2
, if
I_{1}+I_{2}
is larger than the 3-
\sigma
value of the corresponding integrated intensity, we treat it as a detection.

This method still assumes a common
T_{\rm ex}
among the energy levels involved in the lines used
(i.e., the CTEX assumption,
Mangum &
Shirley 2015
, their Section 12)
. However, we sum up the integrated intensities of the two satellite lines to
deduce the effect from hyperfine anomalies of
12
CN, which has been observed in several studies
(
Bachiller
et al. 1997
;
Hily-Blant et al. 2010
, e.g.,)
.

Similarly, we can derive
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
with the same method by replacing the relative intensity ratio and the integrated line intensity of
13
CN
N=1\to 0
with those of C
15
N
N=1\to 0
.

6.4
Astrochemical effects

Although the satellite method can reduce the deviation of abundance ratio measurements (Fig.
7
(b)), astrochemical effects may still bias the
measured molecular abundances. This can introduce additional discrepancies between
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
of the same target derived from different tracers, and that between different targets (thus in different chemical environments) but derived from the same tracer.

\bullet
UV self-shielding:
When exposed to UV radiation, more abundant
\rm{}^{12}C
-bearing molecules can self-shield themselves in the inner clouds, making them less vulnerable to destruction by UV photons. On the other hand, the less abundant
\rm{}^{13}C
-bearing isotopologues would be more easily destroyed even in the dense interior of clouds. This self-shielding effect can lead to an overestimation of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
in emission line pairs such as
\rm C^{18}O
and
\rm{}^{13}C^{18}O
, or
\rm CN
and
\rm{}^{13}CN

(
van Dishoeck &
Black 1988
;
Röllig &
Ossenkopf 2013
)
. Given the high localization of
strong Photon-Dominated Regions (PDRs) around O stars in molecular clouds (
\rm L\leq
0.1 pc), such FUV-induced fractionization effects cannot affect
globally-averaged isotopologue ratios over galaxy-sized molecular gas reservoirs, but they can affect local ISM regions like those observed in our study.

\bullet
Depletion:
In dense and well-shielded regions, the temperature of dust can drop below the CO condensation temperature, typically around 17 K
(
Nakagawa 1980
, e.g.,)
. As a result, CO isotopologues freeze out onto the surface of dust grains
(
Willacy
et al. 1998
;
Savva et al. 2003
;
Giannetti
et al. 2014
;
Feng
et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. Observations suggest that the depletion factors of
\rm{}^{12}C^{18}O
and
\rm{}^{13}C^{18}O
vary with density or temperature, but no systematic differences have been found
(
Savva et al. 2003
;
Giannetti
et al. 2014
)
.

\bullet
Chemical fractionation:
In addition, the difference between the zero-point energy of
\rm{}^{12}CO
and
\rm{}^{13}CO

causes the carbon isotopic fractionation reactions
(
Watson
et al. 1976
;
Langer et al. 1984
, e.g.  )
:

{}^{13}{\rm C}^{+}+\hskip 2.84544pt^{12}{\rm CO}\leftrightarrow\hskip 2.84544pt^{13}{\rm CO}+\hskip 2.84544pt^{12}{\rm C}^{+}+35\hskip 2.84544pt{\rm K}

(12)

The CN isotopologues have a similar effect
(
Kaiser
et al. 1991
;
Roueff
et al. 2015
)
:

{}^{13}{\rm C}^{+}+\hskip 2.84544pt^{12}{\rm CN}\leftrightarrow\hskip 2.84544pt^{13}{\rm CN}+\hskip 2.84544pt^{12}{\rm C}^{+}+31.2\hskip 2.84544pt{\rm K}

(13)

Both gas-phase and grain-phase chemical networks have been adopted to model such fractionation effects on CO and CN molecules
(
Smith &
Adams 1980
;
Roueff
et al. 2015
;
Colzi et al. 2020
;
Loison et al. 2020
, e.g. )
. The experiments in
Smith &
Adams 1980
show
\rm{}^{13}C
and
\rm{}^{18}O
fractionation with a temperature
\lesssim
80 K.
Ritchey
et al. 2011
suggest that
\rm{}^{12}CO/^{13}CO
and
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
evolution have inverse trends in the fractionation model in diffuse gas. Gas-phase models in
Roueff
et al. 2015
show that the diversion of
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
from original
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C
is not significant with a temperature
\sim 10
K. These models show stable
\rm C^{14}N/C^{15}N
with an evolution time larger than
10^{6}
years.
This is consistent with the fractionation work containing gas and dust
(
Colzi et al. 2018
)
, where the
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
becomes stable after an evolution time
\sim 10^{7}
years. Recent results in
Colzi et al. 2022
further find that nitrogen fractionation partly contributes to the scatter in the
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
measurements, while higher angular resolution observations are needed to disentangle local effects from nucleosynthesis contribution.

A detailed astrochemical study of the molecules used in such Galactic
isotope ratio surveys, subjected to the varying far-UV radiation, cosmic ray, and pressure environments found in the sources used for such surveys
will be particularly valuable. Should any
R_{\rm gc}
-systematic astrochemistry effects be found,
such a study could yield valuable corrections to the (isotopologue)
\rightarrow
(isotope) abundance ratio conversion
and reduce the scatter in Figure
7
.

6.5
Comparing with abundance ratios from stars

The
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
ratio can also be measured from stars.
Botelho et al. 2020
found local
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
values of
\sim 70-100
in 63 Solar twins, higher than those derived from
the interstellar medium (
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
\sim 40-60
with
R_{\rm gc}

\sim
8 kpc). Recently,
Zhang
et al. 2021
found an
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
ratio of
97^{+25}_{-18}
in an isolated brown dwarf with an age of
\sim 125
Myr. This value is similar to the ratio found in the Sun
(
Ayres et al. 2013
)
and higher than the previously measured
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
ratio in the Solar neighborhood
(
Milam et al. 2005
)
.

This is reasonable since abundance ratios measured from stars reflect the abundance of their parental ISM typically several billion years ago, which may be different from those in the local ISM because of stellar moving and the time evolution of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
. Recent studies based on Gaia and LAMOST data reveal that young (
<
100 Myr) stellar clusters in the Solar neighbourhood exhibit a variety of metallicity in their member stars, which indicates strong inhomogeneous mixing or fast star formation
(
Fu et al. 2022
)
. These young clusters span a wide range of age and non-circular orbits, indicating multiple spatial origins
(
Fu et al. 2022
)
.

6.6
Homogeneous mixing in the Solar neighbourhood

Optical spectroscopic observations of N and O towards Galactic H
ii
regions indicate well-mixed gas in the Galactic plane
(
Esteban
et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, at least in the second and the third quadrants
(
Arellano-Córdova
et al. 2020
)
. Fig.
29
in Appendix

shows the location of those H
ii
regions and molecular clouds with
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
in this work. Most targets are located in the same quadrants as those H

regions, indicating similar abundance ratios at the same Galactic radii.

However, these studies only consider the major isotopes of carbon and nitrogen, and the behavior of the rare isotopes
\rm{}^{13}C
and
\rm{}^{15}N
may not be the same as that of the major isotopes. In fact, the production and enrichment of these rare isotopes often involve different mechanisms from those of the major isotopes, such as novae
(
Romano &
Matteucci 2003
;
Cristallo
et al. 2009
;
Romano 2022
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, molecular tracers can be affected by additional chemical biases that may increase the scatter in measured abundances compared to those obtained from stellar tracers.

6.7
Future prospects

Our analysis of the different methods used to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
indicates that the radiative transfer conditions may play an important role in affecting the basic assumptions of those methods. In particular, deriving isotopic ratios from lines of two molecular transitions with significantly different optical depth values (e.g.,
12
CN and
13
CN
N=1\to 0
main components, with
\tau
\sim
1 and
\tau
\sim
0.01, respectively) after performing optical depth corrections is highly non-trivial because of the expected non-uniform excitation conditions within the sources.

For the Galactic targets, parts of the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
have been derived from
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN/C^{15}N

(
Savage et al. 2002
;
Milam et al. 2005
;
Adande &
Ziurys 2012
)
or
\rm{}^{14}NH_{3}/^{15}NH_{3}

(
Chen
et al. 2021
)
, with optical depth correction. After correcting the results using the full Planck function approximation and including the
T_{\rm CMB}
, the results are still subjected to the uncertainties of the underlying line excitation structure of the sources.
It is always preferable to use the optically-thin line components, such as
\rm C^{18}O/^{13}C^{18}O
or the optical-thin satellite lines of
12
CN and
\rm{}^{14}NH_{3}
with their isotopologues to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
in Galactic targets.

For extragalactic sources the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
can be derived from the
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
with optical depth correction
(
Henkel &
Mauersberger 1993
;
Henkel
et al. 2014
;
Tang
et al. 2019
)
but this method has the same issues as those used in the Milky Way, and it is hard to separate the optically-thin satellite line from the blended
12
CN line components. It is better to use isotopologues with optically thin transitions (e.g.,
\rm C^{18}O/^{13}C^{18}O
) for deriving isotopic ratios, but this requires longer integration time with high sensitivity instruments. According to our 2/15 detection rate of
\rm{}^{13}CN
with an integration time
\sim 0.5-10
hours of our targets (see in Table
1
,
4
and
5
), we suggest that an observing time of more than six hours may be required to detect
\rm{}^{13}C^{18}O
in the targets of the outer disk with a sensitivity similar to that of IRAM 30-m.

On the other hand, all the current methods are based on LTE assumptions. Non-LTE will not only cause different excitation between isotopologues but also change the relative intensities of hyperfine structures
(
Henkel et al. 1998
, e.g., )
. These issues will be examined by the non-LTE models in the future.

7
Conclusions

We examine the assumptions and shortcomings of three different methods used to derive
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
from
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
namely: (a) the one using HfS fitting to do optical-depth correction of
12
CN, adopted in the literature, (b) the improved
\tau
-correction method incorporating the Planck radiation temperature and the CMB contribution, (c) the method deriving
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
from the ratio of optically-thin
12
CN satellite lines and
13
CN lines. We also point out the similar issues of the methods of deriving
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
from
12
CN and C
15
N. We observed
\rm{}^{12}CN
,
\rm{}^{13}CN
, and
\rm C^{15}N

N=1\to 0
towards 15 molecular clouds on the outer Galactic disk (
R_{\rm gc}\sim\rm 12\ kpc-24\ kpc
). The Galactic
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
gradients obtained from different methods are shown by combining
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
derived from our new observations and those in the literature revised by our improved methods. Our results are as follows:

(i). The current method for deriving
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
in the literature adopts the Rayleigh-Jeans approximation and neglects the CMB, which then highly overestimates
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
when the excitation temperature of
12
CN lines is
<
10 K. The improved
\tau
-correction method using the Planck equation and considering the CMB avoids these systematic overestimates, however, it still adopts the assumption of uniform excitation conditions. We show the latter still yields the under-estimate of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
using a simple 2-phase excitation model. Scaling the intensity ratio of optically thin
12
CN satellite lines and
13
CN to the column density ratio of
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
avoids the shortcomings of the other two methods and is a better method for deriving reliable
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
from CN isotopologues.

(ii). Our method requires long integration time. For most targets, we can only set lower limits, but for the objects with the longer integration time (G211.59 and WB89 380), we measure
\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C\sim
60. WB89 391, the target located at the outermost Galactic radius in the literature, shows no detection of
\rm{}^{13}CN
from our IRAM 30-m data, which is inconsistent with the previous result reported in
Milam et al. 2005
. With
\sim
8
\times
better sensitivity, our new data sets a lower limit of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
\gtrsim 36
in WB89 391 in the optically thin condition. We also give
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}

\sim
220 from
\rm{}^{12}CN/C^{15}N
for one target and
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}

\sim
300 from
\rm H^{13}CN/HC^{15}N
with
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
for two targets, at
R_{\rm gc}\sim 12
kpc. These ratios are much lower than
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
derived from
\rm H^{13}CN/HC^{15}N
or
\rm HN^{13}C/H^{15}NC
multiplying
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
gradient in
Milam et al. 2005
, in better agreement with predictions of chemical evolution models.

(iii). The updated Galactic gradient of
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
(derived from
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
with
\tau
-correction) yields systematically lower values than previous results. The updated
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
gradient from CN optically-thin satellite lines is systematically steeper than the one from optical-depth correction. Such changes will regulate the Galactic
R_{\rm{}^{14}N/^{15}N}
deriving from other ratios (e.g.,
\rm H^{13}CN/HC^{15}N
) multiplying
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
. In addition, the
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
obtained from
\rm{}^{12}CN/^{13}CN
in the optically thin condition is more consistent with
R_{\rm{}^{12}C/^{13}C}
from
\rm{}^{12}C^{18}O/^{13}C^{18}O
than the one derived from the
\tau
-corrected method.

B.1

In this section, we illustrate our derivation of

equals the column density ratio between
\rm{}^{12}CN
and
\rm{}^{13}CN
(i.e. ignore the astrochemistry effects). The column density can be described as
(
Mangum &
Shirley 2015
)

N_{\rm tot}=\frac{3h}{8\pi^{3}|\mu_{\rm lu}|^{2}}\frac{Q_{\rm tot}}{g_{\rm u}}\frac{{\rm exp}(\frac{E_{\rm u}}{kT_{\rm ex}})}{{\rm exp}(\frac{h\nu}{kT_{\rm ex}})-1}\int\tau_{\nu}dv

(14)

Here,
N_{\rm tot}
means the total molecular column density of all energy levels.
h
is the Planck constant and
\mu_{\rm lu}
represents the dipole moment matrix element.
Q_{\rm tot}
represents the rotational partition function.
E_{\rm u}
and
g_{\rm u}
represent the energy level and the degeneracy of the upper-level u, respectively.
In this work, we assume the molecular structures of
\rm{}^{12}CN
and
\rm{}^{13}CN
are similar so the difference between
\mu_{\rm lu}
,
Q_{\rm tot}
,
E_{\rm u}
and
g_{\rm u}
can be ignored. We also assume a common excitation temperature
T_{\rm ex}
10
10

10

While strictly speaking
LTE is when
T_{\rm ex}
=
T_{\rm kin}
, it is unlikely that all the rotational energy levels would have a common
excitation achieved without the aid of frequent collisions.
.
Then the column density ratio of
12
CN and
13

can be described as:

\frac{N_{\rm tot,^{12}CN}}{N_{\rm tot,^{13}CN}}=\frac{\int\tau_{\nu,\rm^{12}CN}dv}{\int\tau_{\nu,\rm^{13}CN}dv}=\frac{\tau_{12}}{\tau_{13}}

(15)

Here
\tau_{12}
and
\tau_{13}

\rm{}^{12}CN

\rm{}^{13}CN

\rm{}^{12}CN
and
\rm{}^{13}CN

N=1\to 0

R_{12}
and
R_{13}
as conversion factors from the main component to all states of
\rm{}^{12}CN
and
\rm{}^{13}CN

\frac{N_{\rm main,^{12}CN}}{N_{\rm tot,^{12}CN}}=R_{12},\quad\frac{N_{\rm main,^{13}CN}}{N_{\rm tot,^{13}CN}}=R_{13}

We will also have:

\frac{N_{\rm main,^{12}CN}}{N_{\rm main,^{13}CN}}=\frac{R_{12}\tau_{12}}{R_{13}\tau_{13}}=\frac{\tau_{\rm m,12}}{\tau_{\rm m,13}}

\rm{}^{12}CN

\rm{}^{13}CN

T_{\rm R}=f[J_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex})-J_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})][1-e^{-\tau_{\nu}}]

\Psi_{\rm s}

source brightness distribution function

Ulich 1981

\omega_{\rm s}
the solid angle range subtended by the

\Omega_{\rm mb}
=
\int_{\omega_{\rm d}}P_{\rm n}d\omega
, with
\omega_{\rm d}
the beam forward-pattern (typically Gaussian

The

Planck function for the black body radiation:

T_{\rm b}=\frac{c^{2}}{2k_{\rm B}\nu^{2}}B_{\rm\nu}(T)=J_{\rm\nu}(T)\equiv\frac{\frac{h\nu}{k}}{e^{\frac{h\nu}{kT}}-1}

(19)

If the line is optically thin, Equation
18
becomes

T_{\rm R}=f[J_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex})-J_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})]\tau_{\nu}

(20)

Insert Equation
20

14

N_{\rm tot}={\rm A}({\rm mol},T_{\rm ex})\int\tau_{\nu}dv

Here the

N_{\rm tot}={\rm A}({\rm mol},T_{\rm ex})\int\frac{T_{\rm R}}{f[J_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex})-J_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})]}dv

as the source-averaged column density, with

N_{\rm tot,V}=\frac{\tau}{1-e^{-\tau}}\frac{{\rm A}({\rm mol},T_{\rm ex})}{f[J_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex})-J_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})]}\left[\frac{1}{\Delta V}\int T_{\rm R}dv\right]

For the

\rm{}^{12}CN

\frac{N_{\rm main,^{12}CN}}{N_{\rm main,^{13}CN}}=\frac{\tau}{1-e^{-\tau}}\frac{\int T_{\rm R,^{12}CN}dv}{\int T_{\rm R,^{13}CN}dv}

B.4.1
A two
T_{\rm ex}
layer toy model

In Fig.
2
we show a toy model cloud with two layers having different
T_{\rm ex}
for the same molecule/transition (e.g.,
12
CN). From the right to the left, there is the background, a layer with a higher
T_{\rm ex}
, a layer with a lower
T_{\rm ex}
, and the observer. Indicatively, we set the high excitation temperature
T_{\rm ex,H}
=30 K and the lower excitation temperature
T_{\rm ex,C}
=10 K. The optical depth of the high
T_{\rm ex}
layer and the low
T_{\rm ex}
layer is
\tau_{\rm H}
and
\tau_{\rm C}
, respectively. The intensity at a certain frequency on the background, on the interface of the two layers, and the intensity observed by us are labeled as
I_{\rm\nu,0}
,
I_{\rm\nu,1}
and
I_{\rm\nu,2}
, respectively.

We use this toy model in order to explore, in a simple manner, the possible effects of underlying excitation variations
(i.e. a non-uniform
T_{\rm ex}
) on the extracted abundance ratios Assuming uniform
T_{\rm ex}
conditions within
each layer the emergent line brightnesses will be
(
Mangum &
Shirley 2015
, e.g.,)
:

I_{\rm\nu,1}=I_{\rm\nu,0}e^{-\tau_{\rm H}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm H}})

(42)

I_{\rm\nu,2}=I_{\nu,1}e^{-\tau_{\rm C}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm C}})

(43)

where
B_{\nu}
is the Planck function. Here, the spectral line optical depths
are strong functions of frequency:
\tau_{\rm H,C}
=
\tau_{0}\,\phi(\nu-\nu_{0})
,
with
\phi(\nu-\nu_{0})
a normalized emission line profile, strongly peaked
at some central frequency, and a width determined by the gas motions within each layer.
The assumption of a common line profile function

\phi(\nu-\nu_{\circ})=\phi_{\rm C}(\nu-\nu_{0})=\phi_{\rm H}(\nu-\nu_{0})
for both gas layers
is equivalent to the assumption of micro-turbulent and/or thermal gas velocity fields dominating
both gas layers. Indeed only under this assumption, the radiative transfer formalism
that follows is applicable
13
13

13

For the macroturbulent/supersonic gas velocity fields of
molecular clouds, the velocity gradients are so steep that
\phi_{\rm C}(\nu-\nu_{0})\neq\phi_{\rm H}(\nu-\nu_{0})
even for
neighbouring gas cells. This is why line absorption occurs only locally within such cells,
and line optical depths are not added across a line of sight (the LVG approximation used
to solve radiative transfer in molecular clouds is based
on this, with
\tau=f(\vec{V})
being a very strong function of the velocity field.)

(with front layer gas able to absorb line radiation emanating from
the back layer). Thus we can combine the two previous equations to find:

I_{\rm\nu,2}=[I_{\rm\nu,0}e^{-\tau_{\rm H}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm H}})]e^{-\tau_{\rm C}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm C}})

(44)

In spectroscopic observations, we often measure the line intensity against a non-zero continuum radiation field. This
may emanate from the source itself (e.g., dust continuum, synchrotron) and/or from a background source (e.g., the CMB).
This (line)-(continuum) differential measurement is obtained spectrally by subtracting the continuum
as it is measured at neighbouring frequencies that are not line dominated. If the source itself does
not emit any continuum, the subtraction of the OFF-source measurement (as done in single-dish observations)
also yields such a (line)-(continuum) differential (with the OFF-line part of such spectrum
\sim
0).
The intensity measured then is:

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\nu}

\displaystyle=I_{\rm\nu,2}-I_{\rm\nu,0}

(45)

\displaystyle=[I_{\rm\nu,0}e^{-\tau_{\rm H}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm H}})]e^{-\tau_{\rm C}}

\displaystyle+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm C}})-I_{\rm\nu,0}

\displaystyle=B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm H}})e^{-\tau_{\rm C}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm C}})

\displaystyle-I_{\rm\nu,0}[1-e^{-(\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C})}]

Setting the background emission to be a blackbody means
I_{\rm\nu,0}=B_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})
, and we will have:

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\nu}

\displaystyle=B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm H}})e^{-\tau_{\rm C}}+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm C}})

(46)

\displaystyle-B_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})[1-e^{-(\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C})}]

where
T_{\rm bg}
is the background source temperature which here we set to be the CMB temperature (
T_{\rm bg}=T_{\rm CMB}=2.73
K). Eq.
46
is the same as Eq. 2 in
Myers et al. 1996
, showing that the two-layer
T_{\rm ex}
model benefits understanding the line profiles of a collapsing cloud
(
Zhou et al. 1993
;
Myers et al. 1996
, e.g., )
.

An observer that assumes a uniform
T_{\rm ex}
single-layer source to analyze its line emission from the two layers, he/she will then assume an effective optical depth and an effective excitation temperature as

\tau_{\rm eff}
and
T_{\rm ex,eff}
, defined from:

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\nu}

\displaystyle=[B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,eff})-B_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})](1-e^{-\tau_{\rm eff}})

(47)

\displaystyle=B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,eff})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm eff}})-B_{\nu}(T_{\rm bg})(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm eff}})

From Eq.
46
and Eq.
47
, we then have:

\tau_{\rm eff}=\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C}

(48)

and

\displaystyle B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,eff})\cdot[1-e^{-(\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C})}]

\displaystyle=B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})\cdot[e^{-\tau_{\rm C}}-e^{-(\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C})}]

(49)

\displaystyle+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})\cdot[e^{-(\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C})}-e^{-\tau_{\rm H}}]

reordering the Equation above, we obtain:

B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,eff})=B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})\cdot a+B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})\cdot b

(50)

a=\frac{e^{\tau_{\rm H}}-1}{e^{\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C}}-1},\quad b=\frac{e^{\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C}}-e^{\tau_{\rm H}}}{e^{\tau_{\rm H}+\tau_{\rm C}}-1}

(51)

We can find that
a+b\equiv 1
, it means that the effective
B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,eff})
is actually a weighted-averaged value of
B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,H})
and
B_{\nu}(T_{\rm ex,C})
, with two weighting factors
a
and
b
. The weighting factors
a
and
b
are both functions of
\tau_{\rm H}
and
\tau_{\rm C}
, which means the effective
T_{\rm ex}
of
12
CN and
13
CN emission lines should be different because of their different optical depth.

In Fig.
13
(a), we show the change of
a
and
b
with optical depth
\tau
, assuming
\tau_{\rm H}=\tau_{\rm C}=\tau
. The
a
and
b
have monotonous and opposite trends to change with
\tau
. The effective temperature of
12
CN main component will be much lower than the effective temperature of the optically thin
13
CN main component.

If the linewidth is dominated by macro-turbulence or bulk motion, instead of thermal/micro-turbulence, the two-layer
T_{\rm ex}
model may not play a dominant role. This might
also affect the case in this work, in which the observed linewidths are
\sim 1.5-4\ {\rm km\cdot s^{-1}}
. The radiation between gas layers may not be well-coupled.
However, such radiative coupling effects would still bias the isotopic ratios for all methods that need optical-depth corrections.

Appendix C
Comparison of two HfS fitting procedures

In this section, we test the performances of two procedures that can be used for the HfS fitting. We generate
\rm{}^{12}CN

N=1\to 0
spectra artificially. Then we use the HfS procedure in CLASS and the procedure developed by
Estalella 2017
to fit these spectra and test the accuracy and scatter of the results.

We assume the line widths of
12
CN
N=1\to 0
components are the same. We also assume that both the intrinsic line and the optical depth
\tau
have Gaussian profiles. If
\tau=0
, the profiles of the line components can be described as:

T_{\rm A}^{\star}(v)=Ae^{-\frac{(v-v_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

(57)

Here
\sigma={\rm FWHM}/2\sqrt{\rm 2ln2}
and we assume that
\sigma=2\ {\rm km~s^{-1}}
.
A
refers to the intrinsic intensity of a line component, so the total profile can be described as:

T_{\rm A}^{\star}(v)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}e^{-\frac{(v_{\rm k}-v_{0,k})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

(58)

Here
A_{k}
indicates the intensities of different line components. We assume
A=5
K for the main component.

For the line components with the optical depth, we have the following equation:

\frac{T^{\star}_{\rm A,hf}}{T^{\star}_{\rm A,m}}=\frac{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm hf}}}{1-e^{-\tau_{\rm m}}}

(59)

Here
\tau_{\rm hf}
and
T^{\star}_{\rm A,hf}
are the central optical depth and the peak antenna temperature of a certain HfS line component, respectively, while
\tau_{\rm m}
and
T^{\star}_{\rm A,m}
refer to the same properties of the main component. Because the difference between the main beam efficiency at the frequency of the main component and the satellite lines is very small (< 0.1% for IRAM 30-m), we replace the
T_{\rm mb}
in Eq.
8
with the antenna temperature
T^{\star}_{\rm A}
.

We have
\tau_{\rm hf}=R_{\rm hf}*\tau_{\rm m}
. Here,
R_{\rm hf}
is the column density ratio between an HfS line component and the main component. So line profiles can be described as follows:

\displaystyle T_{\rm A}^{\star}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A(1-e^{-\tau_{\rm hf}\frac{(v_{\rm k}-v_{0,k})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}})

(60)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}A(1-e^{-R_{\rm hf,k}\tau_{\rm m}\frac{(v_{\rm k}-v_{0,k})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}})

The basic equation HfS fitting procedures used is
59
. With the known
R_{\rm hf}
and the observed antenna temperature
T^{\star}_{\rm A,hf}
of multiple HfS line components, the optical depth of the main component (
\tau_{\rm m}
) and
A
can be derived by the fitting of line profiles. Then we can derive the excitation temperature
T_{\rm ex}
with Equation
31
:

T_{\rm ex}=T_{\rm A}(1-e^{-\tau})/(\eta_{\rm mb}f)+T_{\rm bg}

(61)

Here we consider
T_{\rm ex}
in the Rayleigh-Jeans approximation because the procedure in CLASS output
T_{\rm ex}
under R-J approximation. We use this equation to derive
T_{\rm ex}
from the procedure of
Estalella 2017
in our test.

We generated a list of values ranging from 0 to 5 to be the list of
\tau_{\rm m}
, with an interval at 0.1, to generate ideal
\rm{}^{12}CN
spectra. There are 500 spectra, which is enough for the test. Figure
15
shows the fitting results of the two procedures and the comparison with ideal values. The procedure from
Estalella 2017
has a slightly larger RMS compared with the CLASS procedure, and the accuracy of the
T_{\rm ex}
is not as good as in CLASS. However, the stability of the procedure from
Estalella 2017
is much better than the procedure in CLASS while the results from CLASS include some points that differ a lot from the ideal values. To avoid the risk that the fitting to the real spectra is too bad, we choose the procedure from
Estalella 2017
to fit our observed
\rm{}^{12}CN

N=1\to 0
spectra.

The linear fitting functions of Galactic

and

gradients

We use the Markov Chain Monte Carlo procedures
14
14

14

Use the Python procedure
emcee

(
Goodman &
Weare 2010
;
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
.
to fit linear functions for the measurements shown in Fig.

(b) and Fig.

The linear fitting function of the Galactic

or

(

;

. For these reasons, we highly recommend using the direct measurements of

and

of individual targets, instead of values fitted from curves.
