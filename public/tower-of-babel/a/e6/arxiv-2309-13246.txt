Can I Trust the Explanations? Investigating Explainable Machine Learning Methods for Monotonic Models
Dangxing Chen

Abstract
. In recent years, explainable machine learning methods have been very successful. Despite their success, most explainable machine learning methods are applied to black-box models without any domain knowledge. By incorporating domain knowledge, science-informed machine learning models have demonstrated better generalization and interpretation. But do we obtain consistent scientific explanations if we apply explainable machine learning methods to science-informed machine learning models? This question is addressed in the context of monotonic models that exhibit three different types of monotonicity. To demonstrate monotonicity, we propose three axioms. Accordingly, this study shows that when only individual monotonicity is involved, the baseline Shapley value provides good explanations; however, when strong pairwise monotonicity is involved, the Integrated gradients method provides reasonable explanations on average.



1.
Introduction

In recent decades, machine learning (ML) models have achieved many successes. In comparison with traditional methods, machine learning models are often capable of increasing accuracy at the expense of black-box functionality. The importance of model explanation is particularly important for highly regulated industries such as the financial sector
(
OCC 2021
)
. As an example, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) confirmed that anti-discrimination law requires companies to provide detailed explanations when denying an application for credit when using machine learning methods
1
1

1

https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-acts-to-protect-the-public-from-black-box-credit-models-using-complex-algorithms/
. In response to the growing regulatory requirements, researchers are investigating explainable machine learning methods.

Explainable machine learning (XML) methods have been successfully used in the past to achieve great success in machine learning. Popular used methods include SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
, Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME)
(
Ribeiro et al. 2016
)
, Integrated Gradients (IG)
(
Sundararajan et al. 2017
)
, Anchors
(
Ribeiro et al. 2018
)
, and Sensitivity-based methods
(
Horel and Giesecke 2020
)
. As a result of these methods, we have gained a better understanding of how ML models function
(
Boardman et al. 2022
;
Bussmann et al. 2021
;
Horel et al. 2018
)
.

In this paper, we address the attribution problem, which involves allocating the prediction score of a model for a given input to its base features. The attribution to a base feature can be understood as the importance of the feature in the prediction. Credit scoring, for instance, can utilize attribution to understand how each feature contributes to the credit score. The SHAP and IG have been successfully applied to the attribution problem. Further, their use has been demonstrated to comply with a number of theoretical properties, as discussed in
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
;
Sundararajan et al. 2017
;
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
;
Lundstrom et al. 2022
)
. While extensive analyses have been conducted, the majority of results have been based on black-box machine learning models without domain knowledge.

Modeling and ensuring conceptual soundness require domain knowledge: the true model should be consistent with the underlying theories. A number of studies have demonstrated that physics-informed machine learning (PIML)
(
Karniadakis et al. 2021
;
Greydanus et al. 2019
)
improved black-box machine learning models in terms of interpretation and accuracy by enforcing conservation laws, for example. Finance and other applications often require monotonicity. A person’s credit score should be decreased when there is one more past due balance on the account, for example. It is possible to achieve better generalization and interpretation when monotonicity is successfully enforced
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Milani Fard et al. 2016
;
You et al. 2017
;
Repetto 2022
)
. Such models can be categorized as finance-informed machine learning models (FIML) or more generally science-informed machine learning models (SIML). In addition, monotonicity is often associated with fairness. As an example, with other factors being equal, a person with more past dues should have a lower credit score. The violation of monotonicity may result in unfair consequences that could cause damage to our society.

In this paper, we ask the following question.
Can attribution methods deliver consistent scientific explanations if SIML models contain certain scientific knowledge? If so, to what extent?
Specifically, do attribution methods preserve monotonicity for monotonic ML models?

In the past, monotonicity has been considered for XML methods. In
(
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
)
, it is shown that among different SHAP methods, the baseline Shapley value (BShap) method, which is a generalization of Shapley-Shubik
(
Friedman and Moulin 1999
)
, preserves demand individual monotonicity. However, as recently highlighted in
(
Chen and Ye 2022
;
Chen and Ye 2023
;
Gupta et al. 2020
;
Cotter et al. 2019
)
, individual monotonicity is not the only problem to be addressed; pairwise monotonicity is just as important. The concept of pairwise monotonicity refers to the comparison of features within a given pair. For instance, a past due of more than 60 days should be considered more serious than one of less than 60 days. Pairwise monotonicity is a requirement of fairness that is informed by domain knowledge. It is unfortunate that pairwise monotonicity has been neglected in the existing literature.

This paper extends monotonicity results to a broader range of cases. As a summary, we have made the following contributions:

(1)

We propose three new axioms concerning the average preservation of three types of monotonicity. Accordingly, a good attribution method should satisfy these axioms if the underlying model is monotonic.

(2)

As we show, IG preserves all average monotonicity axioms, but failing to preserve demand individual monotonicity.

(3)

While BShap preserves individual monotonicity and weak pairwise monotonicity, it fails to preserve average strong pairwise monotonicity.

A number of examples are used to illustrate our results. BShap is appropriate when only individual monotonicity is considered and is better suited if demand individual monotonicity is required. On the other hand, when strong pairwise monotonicity is involved, IG provides reasonable explanations on average.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces attributions, Integrated gradients, (baseline) Shapley value, axioms, and types of monotonicity. In Section 3, we propose three monotonic-related axioms and analyze IG and BShap for these axioms. In Section 4, we present an empirical example. Section 5 concludes.

2.
Preliminaries

For problem setup, assume we have
\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is the dataset with
n
samples and
m
features and
\mathcal{Y}
is the corresponding numerical values in regression and labels in classification. For
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define
[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
to be the hyperrectangle. We denote a class of functions
f:[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
, or simply
\mathcal{F}
. For simplicity, we assume
\mathbf{x}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]^{m}
and
f
is differentiable almost everywhere.

2.1.
Attribution

Following
(
Lundstrom et al. 2022
)
, we call the point of interest
\mathbf{x}
to explain as an explicand and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
a baseline. The Baseline Attribution Method that interprets features’ importance is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Baseline Attribution Method (BAM))
.

Given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
,
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
, a baseline attribution method is any function of the form
\mathbf{A}:[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\times[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\times\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\mathbf{0}
. We may also write
\mathbf{A}
and denote
A_{i}
as the
i
th attribution of
\mathbf{A}
for simplicity.

2.1.1.
Integrated Gradients

Integrated Gradients
(
Sundararajan et al. 2017
)
is a simple yet useful tool to measure features’ importance.

Definition 2.2

(Integrated Gradients (IG))
.

Given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in[a,b]
and
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
, the integrated gradients attribution of the
i
-th component of
\mathbf{x}
is defined as

(1)

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt.

In practice, this is usually calculated through numerical approximation

(2)

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\frac{k}{n}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))}{\partial x_{i}},

with 20 - 300 calls of gradient.
For simplicity, we often use
\text{IG}_{i}
for
\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
.

2.1.2.
(Baseline) Shapley Value (BShap)

The Shapley value
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017
)
takes as input a set function
v:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The Shapley value produces attributions
s_{i}
for each player
i\in M
that add up to
v(M)
. The Shapley value of a player
i
is given by:

(3)

\displaystyle s_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq M\backslash i}\frac{|S|!(|M|-|S|-1)!}{M!}(v(S\cup i)-v(S)).

Here, we focus on the Baseline Shapley (Shap), which calculates

(4)

\displaystyle v(S)=f(\mathbf{x}_{S};\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{M\backslash S}).

That is, baseline values replace the feature’s absence. We denote Bshap attribution by
\text{BS}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
and
\text{BS}_{i}
sometimes.

2.2.
Axioms

Many desirable characteristics of an attribution technique have been identified in the literature, such as sensitivity, implementation variance, and completeness. Interested readers are referred to
(
Lundstrom et al. 2022
;
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
;
Sundararajan et al. 2017
)
for detailed discussion. This paper focuses on the axiom of monotonicity.
(
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
)
introduced demand individual monotonicity as one of the desired axioms. The attributions for a feature increase with increasing feature values if the model is monotonic with respect to that feature, all else being fixed, including the baseline value. The paper
(
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
)
showed that BShap is a powerful tool that preserves the monotonicity of demand individual demands and other desirable characteristics.

2.3.
Individual and pairwise monotonicity

Three types of monotonicity are discussed here. Without loss of generality, we assume that all monotonic features are monotonically increasing throughout the paper. Suppose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is the set of all individual monotonic features and
\neg\boldsymbol{\alpha}
its complement, then the input
\mathbf{x}
can be partitioned into
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
. Individual monotonicity is defined as follows.

Definition 2.3

(Individual Monotonicity)
.

We say
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
if

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\leq f(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),

(5)

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{*}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\text{ s.t. }\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq\mathbf{x}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},

where
\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}
means
x_{\alpha_{i}}\leq x_{\alpha_{i}}^{\prime},\forall{i}
.

Example 2.4
.

In the case of credit scoring, given that
f
represents the probability of default and
x_{\alpha}
represents the number of past dues, then
f
should increase monotonically with respect to
x_{\alpha}
.

In practice, certain features are intrinsically more important than others. Analog to (
2.3
), we partition
\mathbf{x}=(x_{\beta},x_{\gamma},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
. Without sacrificing generality, we assume that
x_{\beta}
has greater significance than
x_{\gamma}
. Lastly, we require that all features exhibiting pairwise monotonicity also exhibit individual monotonicity. Pairwise monotonicity can be categorized into two types: strong and weak. As a more general definition, weak pairwise monotonicity is presented below.

Definition 2.5

(Weak Pairwise Monotonicity)
.

We say
f
is weakly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
if

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=f(x_{\beta},x_{\gamma}+c,\mathbf{x}_{\neg})\leq f(x_{\beta}+c,x_{\gamma},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})=f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),

(6)

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{*}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\text{ s.t. }x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma},c>0.

Weak pairwise monotonicity compares the significance of
x_{\beta}
and
x_{\gamma}
at the same magnitude. The following is an example.

Example 2.6
.

An applicant who intends to study STEM in graduate school is required to take the GRE general (170 in math and 170 in verbal) as one of the factors for admission. Assume
f
is the admitted probability,
x_{\beta}
is the student’s math score, and
x_{\gamma}
is the student’s verbal score. Due to the importance of math in STEM,
f
should be weakly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
. In this case, it is necessary to fulfill the condition
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
. If the student has the same level of math and verbal skills, it is more desirable to see improvement in math. It is a different story when a student has a strong math score but a weak verbal score. There is often a requirement for a minimum verbal score in schools in order to ensure effective communication. A student who has a strong math score but a very weak verbal score may be able to improve his/her chances of admission more significantly if he/she improves his/her verbal score.

In addition, there is a stronger condition of pairwise monotonicity, known as strong pairwise monotonicity, which is independent of the condition that
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
. Here is the definition.

Definition 2.7

(Strong Pairwise Monotonicity)
.

We say
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
if

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=f(x_{\beta},x_{\gamma}+c,\mathbf{x}_{\neg})\leq f(x_{\beta}+c,x_{\gamma},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})=f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),

(7)

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{*}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\text{ s.t. }c>0.

Accordingly,
x_{\beta}
is always more important than
x_{\gamma}
regardless of its value. Below is an example.

Example 2.8
.

In credit scoring, suppose
f
calculates the probability of default,
x_{\beta}
counts the number of past dues that have been outstanding for more than two months, and
x_{\gamma}
counts the number of past dues that are outstanding between one and two months. As
x_{\beta}
is always more important than
x_{\gamma}
,
f
should be strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
. The pairwise relationship is strong here since whenever there is an additional past due, a longer past due is always more serious.

3.
Monotonic Preserving

In related applications, monotonicity is an important component in domain knowledge. This section discusses axioms derived from different types of monotonicity and analyzes IG and BShap behaviors.

3.1.
Demand Individual Monotonicity

Consider two explicands
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}+c,\dots,x_{m})
, where
c>0
. We restate the axiom of demand individual monotonicity proposed in
(
Friedman and Moulin 1999
)
.

Definition 3.1

(
Demand Individual Monotonicity (DIM)
)
.

Suppose
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{\alpha}
. We say a BAM preserves demand individual monotonicity if

(8)

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq A_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f),\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{*}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}].

Example 3.2
.

Consider Example
2.4
, whenever there is an additional past due, the attribution of
x_{\alpha}
should be increased since
x_{\alpha}
caused the change.

As discussed in
(
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
)
, BShap preserves DIM. It is presented here without the contents of the cost-sharing problem.

Theorem 3.3
.

BShap preserves DIM.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{1}
. Suppose
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(x_{1}+c,\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
for
c>0
. Then

\displaystyle\text{BS}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)-\text{BS}_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq M\backslash 1}\frac{|S|!(|M|-|S|-1)!}{M!}\Delta f_{S},

where

\displaystyle\Delta f_{S}=f(x_{1}+c;\mathbf{x}_{S},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup 1)}^{\prime})-f(x_{1};\mathbf{x}_{S},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup 1)}^{\prime})\geq 0

because of individual monotonicity. Thus, we conclude.
∎

IG has unfortunately produced a negative result.

Remark 3.4
.

IG doesn’t preserve DIM.

The following example in
(
Friedman and Moulin 1999
)
provides a counterexample with a comparison between IG and BShap.

Example 3.5
.

Consider the function
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}
with baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0)
. For IG, it can be shown that
\text{IG}_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\frac{x_{1}x_{2}^{2}}{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}}
. Note
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\text{IG}_{1}=\frac{x_{2}^{2}(x_{2}-x_{1})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{3}}
, therefore DIM is not preserved. It is important to note that the path for the new explicand has been changed. As a result, the new path cannot guarantee greater attributions for the explicand.

For BShap, we have
\text{BS}_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{2(x_{1}+x_{2})}
, which preserves DIM. The main difference is that in the BShap calculation, the new path covers the old path.

3.2.
Average Individual Monotonicity

DIM concerns individual monotonicity for each change. The average result is considered here as a weaker case. The following axiom is motivated by this fact.

Definition 3.6

(
Average Individual Monotonicity (AIM)
)
.

Suppose
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{\alpha}
, then we say a BAM preserves average individual monotonicity if

(9)

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq 0,\forall\mathbf{x}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]\text{ s.t. }\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}.

Example 3.7
.

Consider Example
2.4
,
x_{\alpha}
should always provide positive contributions.

Clearly, DIM is a stronger condition, as stated in the following.

Theorem 3.8
.

If a BAM preserves DIM, then it preserves AIM.

Proof.

Suppose we want to explain
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, let
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in Definition
3.1
, then it follows from the definition.
∎

As a result, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.9.

BShap preserves AIM.

In spite of the fact that the IG fails to preserve DIM, it preserves AIM.

Theorem 3.10
.

IG preserves AIM.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{\alpha}
, then

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(x_{\alpha}-x_{\alpha}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt\geq 0,

since
x_{\alpha}\geq x_{\alpha}^{\prime}
and
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\geq 0
.
∎

As shown in Example
3.5
, if we consider a new path, IG will still contribute positively to the feature, but the contribution of the features may decrease. To this end, we ask: does the failure of DIM pose a significant concern? DIM is derived from a somewhat implicit baseline of
\mathbf{x}
. It is important to recall that the increase is relative to
\mathbf{x}
. However, the attribution we seek to calculate is essentially an average calculation. Consider the following example.

Example 3.11
.

Consider Example
2.8
with a concrete example, where
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\min(0.2x_{1}+0.1x_{2},0.3)
and
x_{1}
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{2}
. At
\mathbf{x}=(1,1)
with baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0)
, the corresponding attribution should be
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=(0.2,0.1)
, due to linearity. Now we consider
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(3,1)
,
f(\mathbf{x}^{*})=0.3
and DIM requires that
A_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq 0.2
. In this case, we wish to satisfy this requirement. However, if we consider
\mathbf{x}^{!}=(0,4)
with
f(x^{!})=0.3
. From the perspective of
x^{!}
,
x_{1}
does not incur an additional cost. Accordingly, a unit of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
should be attributed equally. This results in attributions at
(2,2)
of
(0.15,0.15)
, which violates the principle of DIM.

Remark 3.12
.

In light of Example
3.11
, we argue that DIM may not be necessary in some cases. Although DIM makes sense for some features, it can be achieved through the use of a historical baseline, as in
(
Alam et al. 2022
)
. Taking into consideration the time at which there is an increase in monotonic features of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and we have
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
, then AIM provides positive attributions for
A(\mathbf{x}_{t+1},\mathbf{x}_{t},f)
, which demand individual monotonicity.

3.3.
Average Weak Pairwise Monotonicity

Weak pairwise monotonicity compares the importance of features of the same magnitude. Therefore, if two features have the same magnitude, we would expect the more important feature to have more attributions. In light of this, we propose the following axiom.

Definition 3.13

(
Average Weak Pairwise Monotonicity (AWPM)
)
.

Suppose
f
is weakly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
,
x_{\beta}>x_{\beta}^{\prime}
and
x_{\gamma}>x_{\gamma}^{\prime}
. Suppose for an explicand
\mathbf{x}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
, we have
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
. Then we say a BAM preserves average weak pairwise monotonicity if

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{\beta}-x_{\beta}^{\prime}}A_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq\frac{1}{x_{\gamma}-x_{\gamma}^{\prime}}A_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f).

Example 3.14
.

Consider Example
2.6
, math scores should contribute more attributions when a student has the same verbal and math scores.

IG and BShap both preserve AWPM with appropriate baselines. For simplicity, we only show results for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
here.

Theorem 3.15
.

For
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
, IG preserves AWPM.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is weakly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
. For
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
, we have
\frac{1}{x_{\beta}-x_{\beta}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{x_{\gamma}-x_{\gamma}^{\prime}}
. Since
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
in
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
,
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
for
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
,
\forall t\in[0,1]
.
f
is weakly monotonic respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
, therefore
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\beta}}(\mathbf{x})\geq\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\gamma}}(\mathbf{x})
if
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
in
\mathbf{x}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=(x_{\beta}-x_{\beta}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\beta}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt

\displaystyle\geq(x_{\beta}-x_{\beta}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\gamma}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt

\displaystyle=(x_{\gamma}-x_{\gamma}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\gamma}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt

\displaystyle=\text{IG}_{\gamma}.

∎

Theorem 3.16
.

For
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
, BShap preserves AWPM.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose
f
is weakly monotonic with respect to
x_{1}
over
x_{2}
. For
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
, we have
\frac{1}{x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{x_{2}-x_{2}^{\prime}}
. Recall that

\displaystyle\text{BS}_{1}=\sum_{S\subseteq M\backslash 1}\frac{|S|!(|M|-|S|-1)!}{M!}(v(S\cup 1)-v(S)).

For
\text{BS}_{2}
, we use the symmetry, for each
S
here, we consider
S^{\prime}
such that
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are swapped within
S
, and everything else is left unchanged. That is, if
2\notin S
,
S^{\prime}=S
; if
2\in S
, then
S^{\prime}=(S\backslash 2)\cup 1
. For this arrangement, we only need to show that
v(S\cup 1)-v(S)\geq v(S^{\prime}\cup 2)-v(S^{\prime})
in the summation. If
2\notin S
, we have

\displaystyle v(S\cup 1)-v(S)

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},0;\mathbf{x}_{S},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup\{1,2\})}\textquoteright)

\displaystyle-f(0,0;\mathbf{x}_{S},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup\{1,2\}}\textquoteright),

\displaystyle v(S^{\prime}\cup 2)-v(S^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f(0,x_{2};\mathbf{x}_{S^{\prime}},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S^{\prime}\cup\{1,2\})}\textquoteright)

\displaystyle-f(0,0;\mathbf{x}_{S^{\prime}},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S^{\prime}\cup\{1,2\})}\textquoteright).

Since
S=S^{\prime}
and
x_{1}=x_{2}
, by the definition of weak pairwise monotonicity, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{1},0;\mathbf{x}_{S},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup\{1,2\})}\textquoteright)\geq f(0,x_{2};\mathbf{x}_{S^{\prime}},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S^{\prime}\cup\{1,2\})}\textquoteright),

and therefore
v(S\cup 1)-v(S)\geq v(S^{\prime}\cup 2)-v(S^{\prime})
.

If
2\in S
, and we have

\displaystyle v(S\cup 1)-v(S)

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},x_{2};\mathbf{x}_{S\backslash 2},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup 1)}\textquoteright)

\displaystyle-f(0,x_{2};\mathbf{x}_{S\backslash 2},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup 1)}\textquoteright),

\displaystyle v(S^{\prime}\cup 2)-v(S^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},x_{2};\mathbf{x}_{S^{\prime}\backslash 1},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S^{\prime}\cup 2)}\textquoteright)

\displaystyle-f(x_{1},0;\mathbf{x}_{S^{\prime}\backslash 1},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S^{\prime}\cup 2)}\textquoteright).

Since
S\backslash 2=S^{\prime}\backslash 1
,
S\cup 1=S^{\prime}\cup 2
, and
x_{1}=x_{2}
, by the definition of weak pairwise monotonicity, we have

\displaystyle f(0,x_{2};\mathbf{x}_{S\backslash 2},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S\cup 1)}\textquoteright)\leq f(x_{1},0;\mathbf{x}_{S^{\prime}\backslash 1},\mathbf{x}_{M\backslash(S^{\prime}\cup 2)}\textquoteright),

and therefore
v(S\cup 1)-v(S)\geq v(S^{\prime}\cup 2)-v(S^{\prime})
.

Since
v(S\cup 1)-v(S)\geq v(S^{\prime}\cup 2)-v(S^{\prime})
for all
S
with corresponding
S^{\prime}
, we conclude that
\text{BS}_{1}\geq\text{BS}_{2}
.

∎

This result also suggests baseline points. We need
x_{\beta}^{\prime}=x_{\gamma}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in order to preserve AWPM.

3.4.
Average Strong Pairwise Monotonicity

When there is strong pairwise monotonicity, one feature is always more important than another. Consequently, when considering attributions, the average attribution of the more important ones should always be greater. In light of this, we should consider the following axiom.

Definition 3.18

(
Average Strong Pairwise Monotonicity (ASPM)
)
.

Suppose
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
,
x_{\beta}>x_{\beta}^{\prime}
,
x_{\gamma}>x_{\gamma}^{\prime}
, and
\mathbf{x}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
. Then we say a BAM preserves average strong pairwise monotonicity if

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{\beta}-x_{\beta}^{\prime}}A_{\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq\frac{1}{x_{\gamma}-x_{\gamma}^{\prime}}A_{\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f).

Example 3.19
.

Consider Example
2.8
. As a general rule, we should always expect
x_{\beta}
to contribute more than
x_{\gamma}
on average, since
x_{\beta}
is of greater importance. As an alternative, the interpretation may mislead people to believe that payments between one and two months past due can be more serious.

Clearly, AWPM preserving is a special case of ASPM for
x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}
. Thus, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.20
.

If a BAM preserves ASPM, it also preserves AWPM for the same features.

As a result of IG, ASPM is preserved and we provide the results.

Theorem 3.21
.

IG preserves ASPM.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{\beta}
over
x_{\gamma}
, then
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\gamma}}(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\beta}}(\mathbf{x})
,
\forall\mathbf{x}\in[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{\beta}-x_{\beta}^{\prime}}\text{IG}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\beta}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\gamma}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x_{\gamma}-x_{\gamma}^{\prime}}\text{IG}_{\gamma}.

∎

BShap doesn’t preserve ASPM, as we documented here.

Remark 3.22
.

BShap doesn’t preserve ASPM.

A counterexample is provided here, as well as comparisons with the IG.

Example 3.23
.

Consider the function with two features
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\log(1+10x_{1}+9x_{2})
, where
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{1}
over
x_{2}
. This example mimics the diminishing marginal effect, that is, the impact of
f
decreases as
x_{1}+x_{2}
increases. Consider the explicand
\mathbf{x}=(4,1)
and baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0)
.

By calculations, we have
\textbf{BS}\approx\left[\begin{matrix}2.7\\
1.3\end{matrix}\right]
. It is evident that ASPM is not preserved. BShap requires a stronger condition to preserve the ASPM, for example, if the averaged change of
x_{\beta}
always has a greater impact on
f
. Such a condition, however, is not realistic in applications such as credit scoring. In addition, we apply the IG, and we obtain
\textbf{IG}\approx\left[\begin{matrix}3.2\\
0.7\end{matrix}\right]
. ASPM is successfully maintained.

Consider Example
2.8

Providing the customer with this interpretation would cause the customer to incorrectly believe that longer past dues have less impact, leading to a catastrophic outcome.

5.
Conclusion

We investigate attribution methods for monotonic models in this paper. New axioms are proposed which must be satisfied by attribution methods. We analyze IG and BShap methods. By analyzing and illustrating, we demonstrate that BShap provides satisfactory explanations when only individual monotonicity is involved, but IG provides reasonable explanations when strong pairwise monotonicity is involved.

In the future, we would like to pursue several directions. (1) We have focused on monotonicity in this paper, but it is necessary to investigate more domain knowledge in order to gain a better understanding of the behavior of ML models. (2) According to our analysis, neither IG nor BShap satisfies all monotonic axioms. It is necessary to modify existing methods or propose new methods in order to meet monotonicity requirements. (3) IG satisfies all monotonicity axioms on average, however, it is necessary that the underlying models are differentiable. Additionally, features such as the number of defaults are naturally discrete. Although IG can still be applied in this case using neural networks, it uses function values at points that are not useful in practice. For this reason, it is desirable to have a modified Shapley value method that is able to preserve strong pairwise monotonicity.

In general, we believe that domain knowledge, such as monotonicity, plays an essential role in the interpretation of models. In sectors with high stakes, such as the finance sector, we must take these factors into account when we explain the models.
