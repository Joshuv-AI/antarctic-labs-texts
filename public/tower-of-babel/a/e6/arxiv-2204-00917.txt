1
Overview

Information Geometry (IG) is one of the modern outcomes of various lines of research. The first non-parametric version of IG dates back to the work of J. W. Gibbs. In his 1902 monograph titled
Elementary Principles in Statistical Mechanics
, Gibbs presented an innovative approach using the “theory of error” to describe uncertainty in mechanical systems. His program deploys new concepts intended to describe the time evolution of probability distributions. We review these concepts below in contemporary mathematical language.

Strictly positive probability densities describe uncertainty in some measurable space. Because of the strict positivity, any such density
q
is of exponential form

q=\mathrm{e}^{-v}\quad\text{where}\quad v=\log\frac{1}{q}\ .

The logarithmic expression is related to the Boltzmann entropy, while the exponential expression refers to the Maxwell distribution of densities. Gibbs assumes
v
to be bounded below and rewrites as

q=\mathrm{e}^{c-w}\quad\text{where $c$ is a constant and $\inf w=0$}\ .

The next step is the mechanical interpretation of
w
, the computation of its mean value
{\Expectation}_{q}\left[w\right]
, and the variance expression as
w-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[w\right]
. In conclusion, Gibbs obtains the key equation

q=\mathrm{e}^{u-H(q)},\text{where ${\Expectation}_{q}\left[u\right]=0$, hence $H(q)=-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\log q\right]$.}

The expression above has many consequences. First, the ”typical value” of
u
is 0. Hence, the typical value of
-\log q
is
H(q)
. This observation is the starting point of the applications of this formalism to Information Theory made by C. E. Shannon in his
A Mathematical Theory of Communication
(1948). Second, the random variable
u
is uniquely defined and, in turn, uniquely defines the probability density:

u=\log q-\int q\log q\quad\text{and}\quad q=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{u}}{\int\mathrm{e}^{u}}=\mathrm{e}^{u-\log\int\mathrm{e}^{u}}\ .

Third, an interesting time evolution
t\mapsto q(t)
is given by a linear model in
u
as

t\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{tu-\log\int\mathrm{e}^{tu}}\ .

The last equation is a case of what was subsequently called an exponential family. See the monograph
brown:86
. An exponential family is a family of probability densities of the form

q=\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}u_{j}-\psi(\theta)\right)\cdot p\ .

If the random variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}
are affinely independent, then the map
q\mapsto\theta
is well defined and provides an example of a chart in the geometrical sense of the term. This opens the way to the idea of considering statistical models as manifolds.

R. A. Fisher has provided a further ingredient in his “Mathematical Foundations of Theoretical Statistics” (1922). He considers a statistical model
\theta\mapsto q_{\theta}
versus a reference probability density
p
and studies the likelihood
q_{\theta}/p
in the logarithmic scale
\theta\mapsto\log q_{\theta}-\log p
. In such a scale, the velocity of variation is
\frac{d}{d\theta}\log q_{\theta}=\dot{q}_{\theta}/q_{\theta}
and is named Fisher’s score, while its squared intensity is the variance

{\Expectation}_{q_{\theta}}\left[\left(\frac{\dot{q}_{\theta}}{q_{\theta}}\right)^{2}\right]=\int\frac{\dot{q}_{\theta}^{2}}{q_{\theta}}\ ,

which is named Fisher’s Information. It is relevant to note that
{\Expectation}_{q_{\theta}}\left[\frac{d}{d\theta}\log q_{\theta}\right]=0
.

The use of differential geometry in the study of statistical models was first devised by C.R. Rao
rao:45
who recognized that the Information matrix, that is, the variance matrix of
\nabla\log q_{\theta}
defines a Riemannian manifold on the model parameters. It was later recognized by B. Efron
efron:1975
;
efron:1978
that the affine geometry of the exponential families provides a more interesting geometric setup that connects with such fundamental topics as entropy, information, and Gibbs distribution. See also
dawid:75
;
dawid:1977as
. It was the work of Čenkov
cenkov:1982
and Amari
amari:85
to connect the various branches in the topic we call Information Geometry.

The modern form of this line of research is due to A. Amari and I. Nagaoka; see the monograph
amari|nagaoka:2000
. Their presentation of the affine geometry of statistical models depends on the standard formalism of differential manifolds of finite dimension. We present here a version that holds in a non-parametric case, as it was the original approach by Gibbs.

2.1

Normed vector spaces and Banach spaces are instances of topological vector spaces, but there are other instances of interest. In our applications, the topological vector spaces of interest will be Banach spaces of random variables. We are not going to use any especially sophisticated notion from Functional Analysis. Remark 1

Many models in IG involve the use of a distance or divergence. Riemannian manifolds have a distance induced by the metric and a notion of geodesic, that is, a curve between two points of minimal length. However, affine manifolds do not have a distance, and the notion of an auto-parallel curve replaces the notion of geodesic. Geodesics or auto-parallel curves provide a notion of a “straight line”, hence a geometry. Moreover, we shall see that the computation of the velocity of the lifted curve
t\mapsto(x(t),\overset{\star}{x}(t))
defines a notion of acceleration so that auto-parallel curves have zero acceleration.

In sec.
2.2
below, we introduce the first example of differential geometric construction in statistics. The original form of the argument is the classical Hellinger’s divergence between probability densities,

\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{q}-\sqrt{p}\right)^{2}=1-\int\sqrt{pq}\ .

In Hellinger divergence, each density maps into its square root, a random variable belonging to the unit sphere of the
L^{2}
space, and half the squared
L^{2}
-norm provides a measure of the divergence.

Orthogonal projection

We construct a frame bundle according to def.

2.3
Square root embedding

Unit sphere geometry has been one of the first attempts to give a geometrical structure to the set of probability densities via the square root embedding
p\mapsto\sqrt{p}
. The argument applies to probability measures

kakutani:1948
. See the monograph
ay|jost|le|schwachhofer:2017
for a presentation of IG along these lines. The
L^{p}
case is discussed in
gibilisco:2020

The sphere
M
of
L^{2}(\mu)
maps onto the set of probability densities by the square function,
\Sq\colon M\ni\rho\mapsto\rho^{2}\in L^{1}(\mu)
. In fact,
p=\rho^{2}
is non-negative and has
\int p\,d\mu=\int\rho^{2}\,d\mu=1
. Conversely, given any probability density
p\in P(\mu)
, it holds
\sqrt{p}\in M
. Unfortunately, this transformation is not smooth unless the probability space has a finite number of atoms. In the infinite case, the set
P(\mu)\subset L^{1}(\mu)
and the set of non-negative points on the sphere have an empty interior, so it is impossible to restrict the transformation to a homeomorphism. In other words, the square mapping is smooth but not locally invertible. This obstruction leaves two options: using the full sphere manifold or restricting the manifold with a model vector space with a stronger topology.

We can rephrase the argument above in the language of manifolds. The expression of
\Sq
in the orthogonal projection atlas is

\Sq_{\alpha}=\Sq\circ s_{\alpha}^{-1}\colon u\mapsto\left(u+\sqrt{1-\left\|u\right\|^{2}}\alpha\right)^{2}\ ,

(21)

(22)

If

, and the conditions for the inverse theorem do not hold but in the finite case. The presentation of probability densities in eq. (

2.4
Affine
space

We turn now to our main subject,
affine manifolds
. We begin with a general definition and shall turn to the specific case of statistical manifolds in sec.
3.1
.

The word ”affine” above refers to the geometrical construction of vectors associated with displacements according to classical H. Weyl’s axioms, see
(
weyl:1952
, sec. I.2)
. The L. Schwartz’ textbook
(
schwartz:1981
, sec. III.1)
has the same presentation in a slightly different language.

Let
M
be a set and
V
a real finite-dimensional vector space. A
displacement
mapping is a mapping

M\times M\ni(P,Q)\mapsto\overrightarrow{PQ}\in V\ ,

(36)

such that

1.

for each fixed
P
the partial mapping
s_{P}\colon Q\mapsto\overrightarrow{PQ}
is
1-to-1 and onto
, and

2.

the
parallelogram law
or
Chasles rule
,
\overrightarrow{PQ}+\overrightarrow{QR}=\overrightarrow{PR}
, holds true.

The notation
Q=P+\overrightarrow{PQ}
shows the action of the vector space
V
on the set
M
. From the parallelogram law, it holds
\overrightarrow{PP}=0
and
\overrightarrow{PQ}+\overrightarrow{QP}=0
. The structure
(M,V,\overrightarrow{\phantom{pq}})
is an
affine space
. The corresponding affine manifold is derived from the atlas of charts
s_{P}\colon M\to V
,
P\in M
. Notice that the change of chart is the choice of a new origin. Such a structure supports a full geometrical development. See, for example, the monograph
nomizu|sasaki:94
.

Because of our non-parametric perspective, we re-define the object to fit our def.
1
of a manifold. We consider a generalization of Weyl’s axioms that allows for a family of (possibly infinite-dimensional) topological vector spaces instead of a single finite-dimensional vector space. Cf.
(
lang:1995
, p. 42)
.

Definition 6
(Affine space)

Let
M
be a set and let
B_{\mu}
,
\mu\in M
, be a family of toplinear spaces. Let
({\mathbb{U}}_{\nu}^{\mu})
,
\nu,\mu\in M
be a family of toplinear isomorphisms
{\mathbb{U}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\colon B_{\nu}\to B_{\mu}
satisfying the
cocycle conditions
AF0 below.

(AF0)

{\mathbb{U}}_{\mu}^{\mu}=I
and
{\mathbb{U}}_{\nu}^{\rho}{\mathbb{U}}_{\mu}^{\nu}={\mathbb{U}}_{\mu}^{\rho}
.

We call
{\mathbb{U}}_{\nu}^{\mu}
the
parallel transport
from
B_{\nu}
onto
B_{\mu}
.
Consider a
displacement
mapping

\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}\colon(\nu,\mu)\mapsto s_{\nu}(\mu)\in B_{\nu}

(37)

defined on a subset of the product space
\Dom\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}\right)\subset M\times M
. We assume

(AF1)

For each fixed
\nu
the mapping
M_{\nu}\ni\mu\mapsto s_{\nu}(\mu)=\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}(\nu,\mu)
is 1-to-1.

(AF2)

\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+{\mathbb{U}}_{\mu_{2}}^{\mu_{1}}\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}(\mu_{2},\mu_{3})=\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{3})
.

We will say that the structure
(M,(B_{\mu})_{\mu\in M},({\mathbb{U}}_{\nu}^{\mu})_{\mu,\nu\in M},\operatorname{\mathbb{S}})

When the vector space does not depend on the point
\mu
,
B_{\mu}=B
, and the parallel transport maps are the identity, we recover Weyl’s definition. Note that in our definition, the domain of
s_{\mu}
map could be smaller than

, and the image could be smaller than the vector space.

Def.
6
(AF2) with
\mu_{1}=\mu_{3}=\nu
and
\mu_{2}=\mu
becomes

\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}(\nu,\mu)+{\mathbb{U}}_{\mu}^{\nu}\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}(\mu,\nu)=0\ .

(38)

Let us compute, where defined, the
change-of-origin
map
s_{\mu}\circ s_{\nu}^{-1}
in an affine space. At
\rho=s_{\nu}^{-1}(w)
,

, it holds

(39)

Notice that the change-of-origin map extends to an affine map, a top linear isomorphism.

An affine space provides a family of candidates to charts

Definition 7
(Affine manifold)

Let

(AF3)

Many exciting ways exist to give the set

Flat:

As

Mixture:

If

, then we can define

for all

\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)+\frac{q}{p}\left(\frac{r}{q}-1\right)=\frac{r}{p}-1\ .

(47)

The inverse mapping is

and it is defined on the open set

. The expression of the displacement is

D_{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{S}}_{p}(u,v)[k]=(1+u)^{-1}k
.

Exponential:

With the same

\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}u=u-\int u\ q\cdot dm

\left(\log\frac{q}{p}-\int\log\frac{q}{p}\ p\cdot dm\right)+\\
\left(\log\frac{r}{q}-\int\log\frac{r}{q}\ p\cdot dm-\int\left(\log\frac{r}{q}-\int\log\frac{r}{q}\ p\cdot dm\right)\ dm\right)=\\
\left(\log\frac{r}{p}-\int\log\frac{r}{p}\ p\cdot dm\right)

(49)

The expression of the displacement is

Definition 14
(Gradient)

3
Non-parametric
statistical
affine manifolds

Let us focus now on affine manifolds whose base set is the vector space of signed measures
\mathbb{M}
on a given measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. We refer to
ay|jost|le|schwachhofer:2017
for a full treatment of Information Geometry in
\mathcal{M}
. We refer to
rudin:1987-3rd
for basic Functional Analysis and Measure theory. More advanced textbooks are
aliprantis|border:2006
and
brezis:2011fasspde
. We recall that the set of finite measures on a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{X})
is a lattice and a convex pointed cone; see
(
aliprantis|border:2006
, Ch. 10-11)
.

A
signed measure
is the numerical difference of two finite measures,
\mu=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}
. There exists a unique minimal decomposition
\mu=\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}
, the
Jordan decomposition
, where the positive part
\mu_{+}
and the negative part
\mu_{-}
have disjoint supports, that is,
\mu_{-}\wedge\mu_{+}=0
. The measure
\left|\mu\right|=\mu_{+}+\mu_{-}
is the absolute value of
\mu
and
\mu\mapsto\left\|\mu\right\|_{\operatorname{TM}}=\int d\left|\mu\right|
is the
total variation norm
. The affine subspace of signed measures with total mass
\tau
is
\mathbb{M}_{\tau}
. In particular, we are interested in the affine subspace
\mathbb{M}_{1}=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{M}\,\middle|\,\mu(X)=1\right\}
and in the vector subspace
\mathbb{M}_{0}=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{M}\,\middle|\,\mu(X)=0\right\}
. The affine subspace
\mathbb{M}_{1}
contains the closed convex set of probability measures
\mathbb{P}
, while
\mathbb{M}_{0}
is its tangent subspace.

The integral induces a natural pairing on
\mathbb{M}\times\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
, that is,
(\mu,f)\mapsto\int f\ d\mu
. The space
\mathbb{M}_{0}
is closed in the weak topology. We have
\left\langle\mu,f\right\rangle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\left\|f\right\|_{\operatorname{TM}}
, and the weak convergence induced on
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
implies the point-wise convergence.

For each curve
t\mapsto\mu(t)
in
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and for any topology on
\mathbb{M}
compatible with the operations of vector space such that
\mathbb{M}_{0}
is closed, it holds
\frac{d}{dt}\mu(t)=\dot{\mu}(t)\in\mathbb{M}_{0}
provided the curve is differentiable at
t
, that is

\lim_{h\to 0}\left(h^{-1}(\mu(t+h)-\mu(t))-\dot{\mu}(t)\right)=0

(85)

.

The following special case is of high interest. Assume that the curve is smooth and stays in
\mathbb{P}
. In such a case,
\mu_{s}(A)=0
implies that
s
is a minimum point of
t\mapsto\mu_{t}(A)
, hence
\dot{\mu}_{s}(A)=0
so that the absolute continuity
\dot{\mu}_{t}<<\mu_{t}
holds. The
Fisher’s score

\overset{\star}{\mu}_{t}=\frac{d\dot{\mu}_{t}}{d\mu_{t}}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t})
is defined for smooth statistical models. Notice that
\int\overset{\star}{\mu}_{t}\ d\mu_{t}=\dot{\mu}_{t}(X)=0
.

For any topological vector space on
\mathbb{M}
such the the mapping
\mu\mapsto\int f\ d\mu=\left\langle f,\mu\right\rangle
is continuous, the
Fisher-Rao equation
holds,

\frac{d}{dt}\int f\ d\mu_{t}=\int f\ d\dot{\mu}_{t}=\int\left(f-\int f\ d\mu_{t}\right)\ d\dot{\mu}_{t}=\\
\int\left(f-\int f\ d\mu_{t}\right)\ \frac{d\dot{\mu}_{t}}{d\mu_{t}}\ d\mu_{t}=\int\left(f-\int f\ d\mu_{t}\right)\ \overset{\star}{\mu}_{t}\ d\mu_{t}=\\
\left\langle f-\int f\ d\mu_{t},\overset{\star}{\mu}_{t}\right\rangle_{\mu_{t}}\ .

(86)

Notice that
\overset{\star}{\mu}_{t}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t})
, possibly a different space for each
t
.

The existence of a common dominating measure is an option to be considered, that is,
\mu(t)=p_{t}\cdot\mu
. Assume moreover
p(t)>0

\mu
-almost surely. In such a case,

\overset{\star}{\mu}(t)=\frac{\dot{p}_{t}\cdot\mu}{p_{t}\cdot\mu}=\frac{\dot{p}_{t}}{p_{t}}=\frac{d}{dt}\log p_{t}\quad\text{$\mu$-a.s.}

(87)

The basic scheme above has various qualifications in statistical application. A few examples, not further developed in this chapter, are listed below. The cases of interest are in the following sections.

3.1

This section sets the exponential affine geometry already described in Examples
1

Let the base set
M
be the set of all probability measures equivalent to a reference
\sigma
-finite measure
m
. That is,
\mu=p\cdot m
and
p>0

m

As all the measures

Let us assume that

M

B

B\hookrightarrow\cap_{p\in M}L^{1}(p)\hookrightarrow L^{0}(m)\ .

(92)

4
Banach spaces of random variables as coordinate spaces

In our definition of affine statistical manifold, we consider a set of probability measures
M

, where

is a topological vector space, a

and the family of parallel transport

1.

is an toplinear isomorphism of

, and

2.

The set image of

, the set of all coordinates, is open in

Definition 15

Given a Young function

Proposition 4

L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)

\left\{f\in L^{0}(\mu)\,\middle|\,\int\Phi(\left|f\right|)\ d\mu\leq 1\right\}

\Phi

\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}\leq\rho\quad\text{if, and only if,}\quad\int\Phi(\rho^{-1}\left|f\right|)\ d\mu\leq 1\ ,

(111)

that is,

\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}=\inf\left\{\rho\,\middle|\,\int\Phi(\rho^{-1}\left|f\right|)\ d\mu\leq 1\right\}\ .

(112)

1.

If

\left\|c\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}=c
if, and only if
\Phi(1)=1
, which is the case for power functions, but is not the case for the other examples in the table.

2.

In the case
\Phi(x)=\left|x\right|^{\alpha}

\Phi(x)=\alpha^{-1}\left|x\right|^{\alpha}

\alpha^{-1/\alpha}

\times
the Lebesgue norm.

3.

We have assumed the reference measure
\mu
to be a probability measure. Reference to a probability measure is not part of the general theory of Orlicz spaces, it is a specific feature of the application we seek.

4.

The convergence of a sequence
(f_{n})
to zero in

, that is
\lim_{n\to\infty 0}\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}=0

\left\|\epsilon^{-1}f_{n}\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}\leq 1
definitively. The condition of norm convergence in terms of integrals is

\int\Phi(\epsilon^{-1}f_{n})\ d\mu\leq 1\quad\text{definitively for all $\epsilon>0$.}

(113)

Now, for all
0<\lambda<1
it holds
\Phi(\lambda x)\leq\lambda\Phi(x)
, so that

\int\Phi(\epsilon^{-1}f_{n})\ d\mu\leq\lambda\int\Phi((\lambda\epsilon)^{-1}f_{n})\ d\mu\leq\lambda\quad\text{definitively for all $\epsilon>0$.}

(114)

In conclusion,

\lim_{n\to\infty 0}\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\Phi(\epsilon^{-1}f_{n})\ d\mu=0\ ,\quad\epsilon>0\ .

(115)

If a growth condition of the form

For each couple of conjugate Young function

(116)

The duality pairing is

L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)\times L_{\Psi}\left(\mu\right)\ni(u,v)\mapsto\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{\mu}=\int uv\ d\mu\ .

(117)

If the norms of

\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{\mu}\leq 2\left\|u\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{\Phi_{*}}\left(\mu\right)}\ .

(118)

Each element of an Orlicz space is associated via the duality pairing (
117

Orlicz norm

\left\|f\right\|_{{L_{\Psi}\left(\mu\right)}^{*}}=\sup\left\{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}\,\middle|\,\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\Psi}\left(\mu\right)}\leq 1\right\}\ .

(119)

\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\Phi}\left(\mu\right)^{*}}\leq 1

\int\Phi(f)\ d\mu=\sup\left\{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}-\int\Psi(g)\ d\mu\,\middle|\,g\in L_{\Psi}\left(\mu\right)\right\}\ .

(120)

but we can compute the

\sup\left\{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}-\int\Psi(g)\ d\mu\,\middle|\,\int\Psi(g)d\mu>1\right\}\leq\\
\sup\left\{\left\|g\right\|_{L_{\Psi}\left(\mu\right)}-\int\Psi(g)\ d\mu\,\middle|\,\int\Psi(g)d\mu>1\right\}\leq 0\ .

(121)

With that, (

\int\Phi(f)\ d\mu=\sup\left\{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}-\int\Psi(g)d\mu\,\middle|\,\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\Psi}\left(\mu\right)}\leq 1\right\}\leq 1

\left(L_{\exp_{2}^{*}}\left(m\right)\right)^{*}\hookrightarrow(L^{2}(m))^{*}=L^{2}(m)
so that each element of the dual is a random variable. That is, a linear functional on
L_{\exp_{2}^{*}}\left(m\right)
of norm
k
is of the form
f\mapsto\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{m}
with
\left|\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{m}\right|\leq k\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\exp_{2}^{*}}\left(m\right)}
. The dual is
L_{\exp_{2}}\left(m\right)
with the Orlicz norm. In conclusion

L_{\exp_{2}}\left(\mu\right)={L_{\exp_{2}^{*}}\left(\mu\right)}^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\cosh_{2}^{*}}\left(\mu\right)={L_{\cosh_{2}^{*}}\left(\mu\right)}^{*}\ .

(143)

The reverse duality, reflexivity, does not hold unless the sample space is finite. The dual of
L_{\exp_{2}}\left(\mu\right)
contains functionals that are not representable as functions. This is similar to the well known case
(L^{1}(m))^{*}=L^{\infty}(m)
and
(L^{\infty}(m))^{*}\subsetneq L^{1}(m)
. See the general references already cited above.

Remark 6
(Analytic bi-lateral Laplace transform)

The random variable
u
belongs to
L_{\exp_{2}}\left(m\right)
if, and only if, the Laplace transform of the image probability measure
u_{*}(m)
is finite on an open interval containing 0. In such a case, the Laplace transform itself is analytic at 0. See, for example,
malliavin:1995

Remark 7
(Densities with finite entropy)

Let us discuss the relation between the following properties: 1) the probability density
p
belongs to the conjugate space
L_{\exp_{2}^{*}}\left(m\right)
; 2) the density
p
has integrable logarithm
-\infty<\int\log p\ dm\leq 0
; 3) the density
p
has finite entropy
0\leq\int p\log p\ dm<+\infty
. A classical reference is
cover|thomas:2006
. The function
y\mapsto y\log y
,
y>0
, is convex. Hence, the increment is bounded by the derivatives at the extreme points,
\log y+1\leq(1+y)\log(1+y)-y\log y\leq\log(1+y)+1\leq y+1
. From the upper bound, integration gives
\int\exp_{2}^{*}(p)\ dm\leq\int p\log p\ dm+1
, that is, 3) implies 1). From the lower bound, if 2) holds, then
\int p\log p\ dm\leq\int\exp_{2}^{*}(p)\ dm-\int\log p\ dm
, that is, 1) and 2) imply 3).

We shall first define the moment functional and the cumulant functional. These are non-parametric versions of the moment-generating and cumulant-generating functions, respectively. Given a probability measure
\mu
on the measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})
, we define
B_{\mu}=\left\{u\in L_{\cosh_{2}}\left(\mu\right)\,\middle|\,\int u\ d\mu=0\right\}
, where the Orlicz space
L_{\cosh_{2}}\left(\mu\right)
is defined in sec.
4.1
.
B_{\mu}
is a Banach space when the Luxemburg norm of
L_{\cosh_{2}}\left(\mu\right)
is restricted to the sub-space. The
moment functional
is the convex mapping

M_{\mu}\colon B_{\mu}\ni u\mapsto\int\mathrm{e}^{u}\ d\mu\in]0,\infty]\ .

(144)

The
proper domain
of
M_{\mu}
,
\left\{u\in B_{\mu}\,\middle|\,M_{\mu}(u)<\infty\right\}
, is a convex subset of
B_{\mu}
that contains the unit ball of
L_{\cosh_{2}}\left(\mu\right)
. In fact,
\int\cosh_{2}u\ d\mu\leq 1
implies
\int\mathrm{e}^{u}\ d\mu=M_{\mu}(u)\leq 4
. It follows that the interior of the proper domain of
M_{\mu}
is an open convex set,
\mathcal{S}(\mu)=\left\{u\in B_{\mu}\,\middle|\,k_{\mu}(u)<\infty\right\}^{\circ}
.

The
cumulant functional
is defined by for
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mu)
by
K_{\mu}(u)=\log M_{\mu}(u)
. For all
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mu)
and
h\in B_{\mu}
, the mapping
t\mapsto M_{\mu}(h+th)=\int\mathrm{e}^{th}\ \mathrm{e}^{u}\ d\mu
is the Laplace transform of the random variable
h
for the finite measure
\mathrm{e}^{u}\cdot d\mu
and it is defined on a neighborhood of 0. It follows from standard results that the mapping is infinitely differentiable at
t=0
with
k
’th derivative. See, for example,
brown:86
.

\left.\frac{\partial M_{\mu}(u+\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}h_{j})}{\partial t_{1}\cdots\partial t_{k}}\right|_{t_{,}\dots,t_{k}=0}=\int h_{1}\cdots h_{k}\ \mathrm{e}^{u}\ d\mu\ .

(145)

The multi-linear mapping
B_{\mu}\times\cdots\times B_{\mu}\ni(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})\to h_{1}\cdots h_{k}\mathrm{e}^{u}
is bounded into
L^{1}(\mu)
. In fact,

\int\left|h_{1}\cdots h_{k}\right|\mathrm{e}^{u}\ d\mu\leq\left(\int\left|h_{1}\cdots h_{k}\right|^{n}\ d\mu\right)^{1/n}\left(\int\mathrm{e}^{nu/(n-1)}\ d\mu\right)^{(n-1)/n}

(146)

and we can chose
n
such that
nu/(n-1)\in s_{\mu}
. For such an
n
, the first factor is bounded by

\int\left|h_{1}\cdots h_{k}\right|^{n}\ d\mu\leq n!^{k}\int\mathrm{e}^{h_{1}+\cdots h_{k}}\ d\mu\ ,

(147)

where the RHS integral is bounded if
h_{1}+\cdot+h_{k}\in\mathcal{S}(\mu)
. This proves the boundedness. In other words, the mapping
\mathcal{S}(\mu)\ni u\mapsto M_{\mu}(u)
is infinitely Gateaux-differentiable, and the derivatives are continuous linear operators. In compact notation, the derivative is

d^{k}M_{\mu}(u)[h_{1}\cdots h_{k}]=\int h^{k}\mathrm{e}^{u}\ d\mu\ .

(148)

We shall not discuss stronger differentiability conditions. See, for example, in
cena:2002
the proof that the moment functional is Frechét-differentiable on
\mathcal{S}(\mu)
and analytic on the open unit ball. The moment functional and the cumulant functional are the normalizing constants for probability densities of exponential form. The support
M
of the affine manifold of interests here is the
maximal exponential model

\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
consisting of all probability densities of the form

q=M_{\mu}(u)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{u}=\exp\left(u-K_{\mu}(u)\right)\ ,\quad u\in\mathcal{S}(\mu)\ .

(149)

The Kullback-Leibler divergence is
\operatorname{D}\left(p\middle|q\right)=\int\log\frac{p}{q}\ p\ dm
. Consider positive densities
q=\mathrm{e}^{u-K_{p}(u)}\cdot p
,
q\in\Maxexp\left(p\right)
, and
r=(1+v)\cdot p
,
v\in\prescript{*}{}{B}_{p}
. A simple computation shows that

\operatorname{D}\left(r\middle|q\right)+\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{p}=\operatorname{D}\left(r\middle|p\right)+\operatorname{D}\left(p\middle|q\right)\ .

The case where
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{p}=0
is sometimes called Pythagorean
Theorem for divergences. The result implies a conjugation statement. In fact,
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{p}\leq\operatorname{D}\left(r\middle|p\right)+\operatorname{D}\left(p\middle|q\right)
and
r=q
gives
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{p}=\operatorname{D}\left(q\middle|p\right)+\operatorname{D}\left(p\middle|q\right)
.

5.2
Covariant derivatives, tensor bundle, acceleration

In discussing higher-order geometry, one needs to define bundles whose fibers are the product of multiple copies of the mixture and exponential fibers.

As a first example, the
full bundle
is

\prescript{1}{}{S}^{1}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)=\left\{(q,\eta,w)\,\middle|\,q\in\Maxexp\left(\mu\right),\eta\in{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right),w\in S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\right\}\ .

(169)

There is a duality pairing
{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\times S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\ni(\eta,w)\mapsto\left\langle\eta,w\right\rangle_{q}
and the dual of
{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
is
S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
. The full bundle is our setup to discuss second-order geometry, such as covariant derivatives.

More generally,
{}^{h}\!S^{k}
will denote the case with
h
mixture factors and
k
exponential factors. We use both notations for expected values in the following sections,
\int F\ pd\mu={\Expectation}_{p}\left[F\right]
.

Let us compute the expression of the velocity at time
t
of a smooth
curve in the exponential bundle:

t\mapsto\gamma(t)=(q(t),w(t))\in S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)=\prescript{0}{}{S}^{1}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\ ,

(170)

where
q(t)\in\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
and
w(t)\in S_{q(t)}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
is a
q(t)
-centered random variable in the Orlicz space
L_{\cosh_{2}}\left(\mu\right)
.

In the chart centered at
p

\gamma_{p}(t)=s_{p}(\gamma(t))=\left(s_{p}(q(t)),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)\right)\ ,

(171)

and, consequently, the time derivative has two components,

\frac{d}{dt}s_{p}(q(t))=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\log\frac{q(t)}{p}-{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\log\frac{q(t)}{p}\right]\right)=\frac{\dot{q}(t)}{q(t)}-{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\frac{\dot{q}(t)}{q(t)}\right]=\\
\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}\frac{\dot{q}(t)}{q(t)}=\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}\frac{d}{dt}\log q(t)\ ,

(172)

and

\frac{d}{dt}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\left(w(t)-{\Expectation}_{p}\left[w(t)\right]\right)=\dot{w}(t)-{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\dot{w}(t)\right]\ .

(173)

By expressing the tangent at each time
t
in the chart centred at the current position
q(t)
, from the first component, we obtain the
velocity
,

\overset{\star}{q}(t)=\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\frac{d}{dt}s_{p}(q(t))=\dot{u}(t)-{\Expectation}_{q(t)}\left[\dot{u}(t)\right]=\frac{d}{dt}\log q(t)=\frac{\dot{q}(t)}{q(t)}\ .

(174)

Notice that
t\mapsto(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t))
is a curve in the exponential statistical bundle whose expression in the chart centered at
p
is
t\mapsto(u(t),\dot{u}(t))
. The mapping
q\mapsto(q,\overset{\star}{q})
is the
lift
of the curve to the statistical bundle.

Let us turn to the interpretation of the second component in
(
173

), we define an exponential

derivative by setting

\frac{\Deriv}{dt}w(t)=\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\frac{d}{dt}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)=\\
\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\left(\dot{w}(t)-{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\dot{w}(t)\right]\right)=\dot{w}(t)-{\Expectation}_{q(t)}\left[\dot{w}(t)\right]\ .

(175)

The notation

will generically denote the covariant time derivative in a given transport or connection, whose choice will depend on the context. When necessary, we use

\mDeriv

Let us now do the computation in the

. The curve is

(176)

Proposition 7
(Duality of the covariant derivatives)

For each smooth curve in the full statistical bundle,

t\mapsto(q(t),\eta(t),w(t))\in\prescript{1}{}{S}^{1}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\ ,

it holds

(180)

Proof

In an arbitrary reference density
p
,

\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\eta(t),w(t)\right\rangle_{q(t)}=\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}\eta(t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)\right\rangle_{p}=\\
\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}\eta(t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)\right\rangle_{p}+\left\langle\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}\eta(t),\frac{d}{dt}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)\right\rangle_{p}=\\
\left\langle\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\frac{d}{dt}\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}\eta(t),w(t)\right\rangle_{q(t)}+\left\langle\eta(t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\frac{d}{dt}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{q(t)}^{p}w(t)\right\rangle_{p}\ ,

Remark 11

Let us briefly justify our unusual presentation of a classical topic such as covariant derivatives. As the manifold structure we discuss is an affine space with global charts, the geometry of the tangent bundle of the manifold follows in terms of explicitly defined parallel transports on its expression as a statistical bundle. Example 18
(Gradient of the entropy)

The computation of the natural gradient does not require, in some cases, the computation in charts. For example, if conditions for existence and smoothness are satisfied, the derivative of the entropy function

\frac{d}{dt}\Entropy\left(q(t)\right)=-\frac{d}{dt}{\Expectation}_{1}\left[q(t)\log q(t)\right]=-{\Expectation}_{1}\left[\dot{q}(t)(\log q(t)+1)\right]=\\

\Entropy\left(\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))\right)=-\int(u(t)-K_{p}(u(t))\ \mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))\ d\mu=\\
-dK_{p}(u(t))[u(t)]+K_{p}(u(t)]\ .

\nabla F_{p}
of

p

The last term of the total derivative does not require any comment, and we can write it as the ordinary
Euclidean gradient:

D_{4}F_{p}\big(u(t),\zeta(t),v(t),c(t)\big)\big[\dot{c}(t)\big]=\nabla F_{p}\big(u(t),\zeta(t),v(t),c(t)\big)\cdot\dot{c}(t)\ .

Let us consider the second and third terms together. It is a
computation of the fiber derivative and does not involve the
representation in the chart. Given

\alpha\in{}^{*}S_{p}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
and
\beta\in S_{p}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, that
is,
(\alpha,\beta)\in\prescript{1}{}{S}^{1}_{p}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, we have

D_{2}F_{p}(u,\zeta,v,c)[\alpha]+D_{3}F_{p}(u,\zeta,v,c)[\beta]=\left.\frac{d}{dt}F_{p}(u,\zeta+t\alpha,w+t\beta,c)\right|_{t=0}=\\
\left.\frac{d}{dt}F(q,\eta+t{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\alpha,v+t{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\beta,c)\right|_{t=0}=\mathbb{F}F(q,\eta,w,c)[({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\alpha,{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\beta)]=\\
\left\langle{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\alpha,\Grad_{\text{m}}F(q,\eta,w,c)\right\rangle_{q}+\left\langle\Grad_{\text{e}}F(q,\eta,w,c),{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\beta\right\rangle_{q}\ ,

where
\mathbb{F}
denotes the fiber derivative in
\prescript{1}{}{S}^{1}_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, which is expressed, in turn, with the relevant gradients. Consider that the inner
product always has
{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
first, followed by
S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
and that the subscript to the
\Grad
symbol displays which
component of the full bundle is considered.

We have that

\frac{\Deriv}{dt}w(t)={\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\dot{v}(t)\ ,\quad\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\eta(t)={\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}\dot{\zeta}(t)\ .

Putting together all the contributions, we have proved that

\frac{d}{dt}F\big(q(t),\eta(t),w(t),c(t)\big)=D_{1}F_{p}\big(u(t),\zeta(t),v(t),c(t)\big)\big[{\mathbb{U}}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))}^{p}\overset{\star}{q}(t)\big]+\\
\left\langle\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\eta(t),\Grad_{\text{m}}F\big(q(t),\eta(t),w(t),c(t)\big)\right\rangle_{q(t)}+\\
\left\langle\Grad_{\text{e}}F\big(q(t),\eta(t),w(t),c(t)\big),\frac{\Deriv}{dt}w(t)\right\rangle_{q(t)}+\\
\nabla F\big(q(t),\eta(t),w(t),c(t)\big)\cdot\dot{c}(t)\ ,

To identify the first term in the total derivative above, consider the geodesic” case,

q(t)=\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t)),\quad\eta(t)={\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))}\zeta,\quad w(t)={\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))}v,\quad c(t)=c\ ,

so that the first term reduces to
D_{1}F_{p}(u(t),\zeta,v,c)[{\mathbb{U}}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))}^{p}\overset{\star}{q}(t)]
. It
follows that the proper way to compute the first gradient is to
consider the function on
\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
defined by

q\mapsto F_{\zeta,v,c}(q)=F(q,{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}\zeta,{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}v,c)

which has a natural gradient whose chart representation is precisely
that first term. ∎

We have concluded the computation of the total derivative of a parametric function on the full bundle. Notice that the computation of the natural gradient for
\Grad_{\text{m}}F\big(q,\eta,w,c\big)
and
\Grad_{\text{e}}F\big(q,\eta,w,c\big)
is done by fixing the variables in the fibers to be translations of fixed ones.

We are going to discuss the following examples of gradient flow on the full statistical bundle: the scalar function
L(q,w)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}
; the
cumulant
function
L(q,w)=K_{q}(w)
; the
conjugate cumulant
function
H(q,\eta)={\Expectation}_{q}\left[(1+\eta)\log(1+\eta)\right]
.

Example 20
(Scalar function
\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}
)

On the statistical bundle, consider the scalar function given by the pairing

L(q,w)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}\ .

In chart, we have
L_{p}(u,v)=L\left(\mathrm{e}_{p}(u),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}v\right)=d^{2}K_{p}(u)[v,v]
from Eq. (
161
), where
K_{p}(u)
is the expression in the chart at
p
of Kullback–Leibler divergence of
q\mapsto\operatorname{D}\left(p\middle|q\right)
.

From Eq. (
162
), we write the derivative with respect to
u
in the direction
h
as

\frac{1}{2}d^{3}K_{p}(u)[v,v,h]=\frac{1}{2}{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\left(v-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[v\right]\right)^{2}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right]=\\
\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[w\right]^{2},\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q}h\right\rangle_{q}

which, in turn, identifies the gradient as
\Grad\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}=\frac{1}{2}(w^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[w^{2}\right])\in{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
. The exponential gradient is
\Grad_{\text{e}}\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}=w
.

Example 21
(Cumulant functional)

If
L(q,w)=K_{q}(w)
, then

L_{p}(u,v)=K_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left(\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}v\right)=\log{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\mathrm{e}^{v-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[v\right]}\right]x=\\
\log{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\mathrm{e}^{u-K_{p}(u)+v-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[v\right]}\right]=\log{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\mathrm{e}^{u+v-K_{p}(u)-dK_{p}(u)[v]}\right]=\\
K_{p}(u+v)-K_{p}(u)-dK_{p}(u)[v]\ .

The derivative with respect to
u
in the direction
h
is

dK_{p}(u+v)[h]-dK_{p}(u)[h]-d^{2}K_{p}(u)[v,h]=\\
{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u+v)}\left[h\right]-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[h\right]-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\left({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}v\right)\left({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u+v)}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right]-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[h\right]-{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[w\left({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u+v)}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]-\left\langle w,{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right\rangle_{q}\ .

The expected value of the factor
\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u+v)}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}
in the first term of the RHS equals

{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\mathrm{e}^{v-(K_{p}(u+v)-K_{p}(u))}\left(\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\mathrm{e}^{v-(K_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}v)+dK_{p}(u)[v])}\left({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}v-K_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}v)}\left(\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\mathrm{e}^{w-K_{q}(w)}\left({\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\right]=\left\langle\frac{e_{q}(w)}{q}-1,\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right\rangle_{q}\ .

In conclusion,
\Grad K_{q}(w)=\left(\frac{e_{q}(w)}{q}-1\right)-w
. The expectation gradient is

\Grad_{\text{e}}K_{q}(w)=\frac{\mathrm{e}_{q}(w)}{q}-1\ .

Example 22
(Conjugate cumulant functional)

The conjugate cumulant functional

{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\colon(q,\eta)\mapsto H(q,\eta)={\Expectation}_{q}\left[(1+\eta)\log(1+\eta)\right]\ ,\quad\eta>-1\ ,

is the Legendre transform of the cumulant function
K_{q}
,

H(q,\eta)=\left\langle\eta,(\Grad K_{q})^{-1}(\eta)\right\rangle_{q}-K_{q}\left((\Grad K_{q})^{-1}(\eta)\right)\ .

In particular, the fiber gradient of
H_{q}
is
\Grad_{\text{m}}H(q,\eta)=\log(1+\eta)-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\log(1+\eta)\right]
which is the inverse of the fiber gradient of
K_{q}
. Notice that
r=(1+\eta)q
is a density, and
\operatorname{D}\left(r\middle|q\right)=H(q,\eta)
.

Let us compute the gradient. The expression of the conjugate cumulant functional in the chart at
p
is

H_{p}(u,\zeta)={\Expectation}_{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\left[\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\right)\log\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\right)\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}{p}+\zeta\right)\log\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\right)\right]\ .

As, for
h\in S_{p}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
,

\displaystyle d\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}{p}+\zeta\right)[h]=\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}{p}\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\ ,

\displaystyle d\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\right)[h]=-\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\ ,

the derivative of
H_{p}
with respect to
u
in the direction
h
is given by

DH_{p}(u,\zeta)[h]={\Expectation}_{p}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}{p}\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right)\log\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\right)\right]-\\
{\Expectation}_{p}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}{p}+\zeta\right)\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\right)^{-1}\frac{p}{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}\zeta\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right]=\\
{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\log(1+\eta)\prescript{\text{m}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right]-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\zeta{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u)}h\right]\ ,

hence
\Grad H(q,\eta)=\log(1+\eta)-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\log(1+\eta)\right]-\eta
.

5.4
Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms on the full Statistical Bundle

The dually affine geometry of the statistical bundle is naturally well suited for describing the dynamics of probability densities in a Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalism; see
pistone:2018lagrange
;
chirco|malago|pistone:2020
. This is apparent from the previous examples.

The Lagrangian formulation of mechanics derives the fundamental laws of force balance from variational principles. In our context, the exponential model
\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
corresponds to the configuration space, while the statistical bundle is associated with the velocity phase space.
For a given smooth curve
q\colon[0,1]\ni t\mapsto q(t)
in
\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
and its lift
t\mapsto(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t))\in S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, we introduce a generic Lagrangian function

L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t))\colon S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}

(199)

and define an action as the integral of the Lagrangian along the curve over the fixed time interval
[0,1]
,

q\mapsto\mathcal{A}(q)=\int_{0}^{1}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t)\ dt\ .

(200)

Hamilton’s principle states that this function has a critical point at a solution within the space of curves on
\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
. We have

Proposition 10
(Euler-Lagrange equation)

If
q
is an extremal of the action integral, then

\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t)=\Grad L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t)\ .

(201)

Proof

Let us express the action integral in the exponential chart
s_{p}
centered at
p
. If
q(t)=\mathrm{e}^{u(t)-K_{p}(u(t))}\cdot p
, with
t\mapsto u(t)\in S_{p}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, we have

L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t)=L\left(\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t)),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{\mathrm{e}_{p}(u(t))}\dot{u}(t),t\right)=L_{p}(u(t),\dot{u}(t),t)\ ,

so that the expression of the Euler-Lagrange equation in a chart is given by

D_{1}L_{p}(u(t),\dot{u}(t),t)[h]=\frac{d}{dt}D_{2}L_{p}(u(t),\dot{u}(t),t)[h]\ .\quad t\in[0,1]\ ,h\in S_{p}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\ .

(202)

Consider first the RHS of eq. (
202
). From Proposition (
9
) we have

D_{1}L_{p}(u(t),\dot{u}(t),t)[h]=\left\langle\Grad L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h\right\rangle_{q(t)}\ .

On the left-hand side, we have

D_{2}L_{p}(u(t),\dot{u}(t))[h]=\left\langle\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h\right\rangle_{q(t)}\ .

The derivation formula of (
180
) gives

\frac{d}{dt}D_{2}L_{p}(u(t),\dot{u}(t),t)[h]=\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h\right\rangle_{q(t)}=\\
\left\langle\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h\right\rangle_{q(t)}+\left\langle\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t),\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h\right\rangle_{q(t)}=\\
\left\langle\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t),\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h\right\rangle_{q(t)}\ ,

because
\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\prescript{\text{e}}{}{\mathbb{U}}_{p}^{q(t)}h=0
. As
h
is arbitrary, the conclusion follows.

Hamiltonian mechanics

At each fixed density
q\in\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, and each time
t
, the
partial mapping

S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\ni w\mapsto L_{q,t}(w)=L(q,w,t)
is defined on
the vector space
S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, and its gradient mapping in the
duality of
{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\times S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
is

w\mapsto\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q,w,t)
. The standard Legendre transform
the argument provides the intrinsic form of the Hamilton equations under
the following assumption.

Assumption 5.1

We restrict our attention to Lagrangians such that the
fiber gradient mapping at
q
,

w\mapsto\eta=\Grad_{\text{e}}L_{q}(w)
is a 1-to-1 mapping from

S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
to
{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
. In particular, this true
when the partial mappings
w\mapsto L_{q}(w)
are strictly convex for
each
q
.

In the finite-dimensional context, this assumption is
equivalent to the assumption that the fiber gradient is a
diffeomorphism of the statistical bundles

\Grad_{\text{2}}L\colon S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\to{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
. This is related to the properties of
regularity
and
hyper-regularity, cf.
(
abraham|marsden:1978
, sec. 3.6)
. The bilinear form

{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\times S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)\ni(\eta,w)\mapsto\left\langle\eta,w\right\rangle_{q}={\Expectation}_{q}\left[\eta w\right]
will always be written in
this order. The Legendre transform of
L_{q,t}
is defined
for each
\eta\in{}^{*}S_{q}\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
of the image of
\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q,\cdot,t)
, so that the Hamiltonian is

H(q,\eta,t)=\left\langle\eta,(\Grad_{\text{e}}L_{q,t})^{-1}(\eta)\right\rangle_{q}-L(q,(\Grad_{\text{e}}L_{q,t})^{-1}(\eta))\ .

(203)

If
t\mapsto q(t)
a solution of Euler-Lagrange
(
201
), the curve

t\mapsto\zeta(t)=(q(t),\eta(t))
in
{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, where

\eta(t)=\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t),t)
is the

momentum
. The mixture bundle
{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
then plays the role of the cotangent bundle in mechanics.

Proposition 11
(Hamilton equations)

When (
5.1
) holds, the momentum curve satisfies the
Hamilton equations
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\Deriv}{dt}\eta(t)&=-\Grad H(q(t),\eta(t),t)\\
\overset{\star}{q}(t)&=\Grad_{\text{m}}H(q(t),\eta(t),t).\end{aligned}\right.

(204)

Moreover,

\frac{d}{dt}H(q(t),\eta(t),t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H(q(t),\eta(t),t)\ .

(205)

The special intrinsic form of the Hamilton equations follows from the covariant derivatives and the gradients of the statistical bundles.

Example 23
(Mechanics of
\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}
)

The scalar function
\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}
of Example
20
corresponds to the kinetic energy Lagrangian in mechanics. In this case, as first shown in
pistone:2018lagrange
, the Euler–Lagrange equations are equivalent to the equations of geodesic motion, whose solution coincides with the one-dimensional exponential families.

Now, if
L(q,w)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,w\right\rangle_{q}
is our Lagrangian, then via Legendre transform, we obtain the Hamiltonian
H(q,\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\eta,\eta\right\rangle_{q}
. The gradients are

\displaystyle\Grad H(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\eta^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\Grad_{\text{m}}H(q,\eta)

\displaystyle=\eta

\displaystyle\Grad L(q,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(w^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[w^{2}\right])

\displaystyle\Grad_{\text{e}}L(q,w)

\displaystyle=w

For
\overset{\star}{q}=w\in{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, the Euler-Lagrange equation is

\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\overset{\star}{q}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overset{\star}{q}(t)^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q(t)}\left[\overset{\star}{q}(t)^{2}\right]\right)\ ,

where the covariant derivative is computed in
{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
, that
is,
\frac{\Deriv}{dt}\overset{\star}{q}(t)=\ddot{q}(t)/q(t)
. In terms of the
exponential acceleration
\overset{\star\star}{q}(t)=\ddot{q}(t)/q(t)-\left(\overset{\star}{q}(t)^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q(t)}\left[\overset{\star}{q}(t)^{2}\right]\right)
, the Euler-Lagrange
equation reads

\overset{\star\star}{q}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\left((\overset{\star}{q}(t))^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q(t)}\left[(\overset{\star}{q}(t))^{2}\right]\right)\ ,

consistently with the result in Example 22.

The Hamilton equations are

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\Deriv}{dt}\eta(t)&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta^{2}-{\Expectation}_{q}\left[\eta^{2}\right]\right)\\
\overset{\star}{q}(t)&=\eta(t)\end{aligned}\right.\ ,

with the covariant derivative again computed in
{}^{*}\!S\!\Maxexp\left(\mu\right)
.

The conserved energy is

H(q(t),\eta(t))=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\overset{\star}{q}(t),\overset{\star}{q}(t)\right\rangle_{q(t)}=\frac{1}{2}{\Expectation}_{1}\left[\frac{{\dot{q}(t)}^{2}}{q(t)}\right]\ .

which reflects in the conservation of the
Fisher information
.
