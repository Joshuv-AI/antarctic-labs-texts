Exact SDP Formulation for Discrete-Time Covariance Steering with Wasserstein Terminal Cost
Isin M. Balci, Efstathios Bakolas

Abstract
In this paper, we present new results on the covariance steering problem with Wasserstein distance terminal cost. We show that the state history feedback control policy parametrization, which has been used before to solve this class of problems, requires an unnecessarily large number of variables and can be replaced by a randomized state feedback policy which leads to more tractable problem formulations without any performance loss. In particular, we show that under the latter policy, the problem can be equivalently formulated as a semi-definite program (SDP) which is in sharp contrast with our previous results that could only guarantee that the stochastic optimal control problem can be reduced to a difference of convex functions program. Then, we show that the optimal policy that is found by solving the associated SDP corresponds to a deterministic state feedback policy. Finally, we present non-trivial numerical simulations which show the benefits of our proposed randomized state feedback policy derived from the SDP formulation of the problem over existing approaches in the field in terms of computational efficacy and controller performance.



I
Introduction

In this paper, we consider Covariance Steering (CS) problems for discrete-time linear stochastic systems.
In this particular class of stochastic optimal control problems, one tries to find a control policy that will steer the probability distribution of the state, or more precisely the first two moments of the latter distribution, to a goal distribution in finite time or infinite time.
Specifically, we address a soft constrained version of the CS problem in which the objective is defined as finding a control policy that will minimize the sum of the expected value of a running cost (e.g., control effort) and a terminal cost which corresponds to a distance metric in the space of probability distributions.
To this end, we choose the (squared) Wasserstein distance
[
1
]
.

Main Contributions:
As we have already mentioned, finite horizon discrete-time CS problems are typically treated as optimization problems in the relevant literature. The latter optimization problems are either convexified by semi-definite relaxations which prohibit finding the exact solutions to the CS problem or they are cast as generic nonlinear programs, for which there are no guarantees of convergence or global optimality in general.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper that formulates the CS problem with a Wasserstein distance terminal cost as a convex semi-definite program specifically without relying on any convex relaxations of non-convex constraints.

We first show that the optimal value of the CS problem with state history feedback control policies is lower bounded by the optimal value of the CS problem with the randomized state feedback policy.
Then, we formulate an SDP for solving the CS problem with a Wasserstein distance terminal cost and show that this SDP formulation is exact.
Then, we express the Wasserstein distance in a convenient form that allows us to express the objective function of the SDP as a linear function of the decision variables.
Next, we show that the optimal policy, whose parameters are obtained by solving the associated SDP, corresponds to a deterministic feedback policy.
Finally, we show the efficacy of our formulation in terms of controller’s performance and computation time via numerical simulations.

II-B

Wasserstein Distance

The Wassertein distance defines a distance metric in the space of probability distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In particular, for two random variables over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with probability density functions
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, their squared Wasserstein distance is denoted as
W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{1},\rho_{1})
and defined as:

\displaystyle W^{2}_{2}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\mathbb{E}_{y}[\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert^{2}_{2}]

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
denotes the space of all probability distributions of the random variable
y=[x_{1}^{\mathrm{T}},x_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
over
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with finite second moments and marginals
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
on
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, respectively.

Typically, the Wasserstein distance for two arbitrary probability density functions does not admit an analytic expression and its computation can be a hard task. However, if both
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
correspond to the densities of two Gaussian distributions with mean vectors
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covariance matrices
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{+}_{n}
, then the Wasserstein distance admits the following closed form expression
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=

\displaystyle\lVert\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}\right)

\displaystyle-2\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\sqrt{\Sigma_{2}}\Sigma_{1}\sqrt{\Sigma_{2}}\right)^{1/2}\right).

(2)

II-C

Problem Formulation

Let us consider the following discrete-time stochastic linear system:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=A_{k}x_{k}+B_{k}u_{k}+w_{k},

(3)

where
\{x_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
is the state process over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
is the control process over
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\{w_{k}\}_{0}^{N-1}
is the disturbance process over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In addition,
A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are known matrices for all
k
.
We consider the case where
\{w_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
is a Gaussian white noise process i.e.
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}]=\bm{0}
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
and
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}w_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}]=\bm{0}
for all
k\neq\ell\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
and
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}w_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}]=W_{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}
.
We also assume that the initial state
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\Sigma_{0})
and

\mathbb{E}[x_{0}w_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}]=\bm{0}
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
.

The main objective in this paper is to find a control policy such that the distribution of the terminal state
x_{N}
under the policy is close to a desired terminal Gaussian distribution in terms of the Wasserstein metric
while minimizing the expected value of the quadratic control cost.
Next, we state the precise problem formulation.

Problem 1
.

Let
\mu_{0},\mu_{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{d}\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{++}
,
\{R_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
,
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
\lambda>0
and
\{A_{k},B_{k},W_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
be given where
W_{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}
and
R_{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{m}^{++}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Furthermore, let
\Pi_{0}
denote the space of admissible causal control policies
\{m_{0}(X^{0}),m_{1}(X^{1}),\dots,m_{N-1}(X^{N-1})\}
for system (
3
) with
u_{k}=m_{k}(X^{k})
where
X=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{k}\}
is the state sequence up to time step
k
. Then, find a policy
\pi\in\Pi_{0}
that solves the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{0}}

\displaystyle~~J(\pi)

(4a)

s.t.

\displaystyle~~\eqref{eq:system-dynamics}\quad\forall k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}

\displaystyle~~u_{k}=m_{k}(X^{k})

(4b)

\displaystyle~~x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\Sigma_{0})

(4c)

where
J(\pi):=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}u_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}u_{k}]+\lambda W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{N},\rho_{\mathrm{d}})
. In addition,
\rho_{N}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{d}}
denote the probability density functions of the terminal state distribution and the desired distribution, respectively.

Remark
.

Note that Problem
1
is an infinite-dimensional problem because the optimization is over
\Pi_{0}
which is the set of all causal policies.
Thus, the variables of Problem
1
are the control laws
m_{k}(X^{k})
which are functions of state sequences.

III
Solution via State History Feedback Policy

Since Problem
1
is an infinite-dimensional optimization problem, it is generally computationally intractable.
To improve computational tractability,
we restrict the set of admissible policies to the one that are affine functions of states visited up to time step
k
as follows:

\displaystyle m^{(1)}_{k}(X^{k})=v^{(1)}_{k}+\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}K^{(1)}_{k,\tau}(x_{\tau}-\bar{x}_{\tau}),

(5)

where
\bar{x}_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}[x_{\tau}]
.
We denote the space of policies that are defined as in (
5
) as
\Pi_{1}
.
We observe that all policies
\pi\in\Pi_{1}
can be parametrized by
v^{(1)}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
K^{(1)}_{k,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
for all
k,j\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
with
k\geq j
.

III-A

Difference of Convex Functions Program Formulation

Next, we will show that Problem
1
can be recast as a difference of convex functions program (DCP).
To this aim, we will have to introduce the following variables which will facilitate the subsequent discussion and analysis.
Let us consider the concatenated vectors
\bm{x}:=\operatorname{vertcat}(x_{0},\dots,x_{N})
,
\bm{u}:=\operatorname{vertcat}(u_{0},\dots,u_{N-1})
,
\bm{w}:=\operatorname{vertcat}(w_{0},\dots,w_{N-1})
and
\bm{v}:=\operatorname{vertcat}(v_{0}^{(1)},\dots,v_{N-1}^{(1)})
.
Then, the relationship between the concatenated vectors are given as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Gamma}x_{0}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{u}+\mathbf{G_{w}}\bm{w},

(6a)

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{v}+\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{x}-\bm{\bar{x}}),

(6b)

where the matrices
\bm{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(N+1)\times n}
,
\mathbf{G_{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(N+1)\times mN}
,

are derived from (
3
) and

is derived from (
5
).
Interested readers can refer to
[
9
]
for detailed derivations.

Under the state history feedback policy (
5
), the mean of the concatenated state vector

is an affine function of

as

\bm{\bar{x}}:=\bm{\Gamma}\mu_{0}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{v}.

To be able to express the covariance of

as a convex quadratic function of the decision variables, we utilize the following bijective variable transformation:

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}-\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\mathcal{K}})^{-1},

(7a)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Theta}(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}-\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})^{-1}.

(7b)

Using (
6
)-(
7
), and the matrix inversion lemma
[
25
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})\mathbf{\bar{S}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})^{\mathrm{T}},

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{\bar{S}}\bm{\Theta}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(8b)

By utilizing (
8
), one can express the objective function
J(\pi)

of Problem
1
with admissible policy space

in terms of

as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}(\bm{v},\bm{\Theta}):=J_{1}(\bm{v})+J_{2}(\bm{\Theta})+J_{3}(\bm{\Theta})-J_{4}(\bm{\Theta})

(9)

where
J_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,4\}
, are defined as
[
22
]
:

\displaystyle J_{1}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle:=\bm{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}\bm{v}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{\Gamma}\mu_{0}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{v})-\mu_{\mathrm{d}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

(10a)

\displaystyle J_{2}(\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\mathcal{R}}\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{\bar{S}}\bm{\Theta}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)

(10b)

\displaystyle J_{3}(\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle:=\lambda\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})~\mathbf{\bar{S}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}\big)

(10c)

\displaystyle J_{4}(\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle:=2\lambda\operatorname{tr}\bigg(\big(\sqrt{\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})~\mathbf{\bar{S}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad(\mathbf{I}_{n(N+1)}+\mathbf{G_{u}}\bm{\Theta})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\sqrt{\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}}\big)^{1/2}\bigg)

(10d)

where
\bm{\mathcal{R}}:=\operatorname{bdiag}\left(R_{0},\dots,R_{N-1}\right)\in\mathbb{S}_{mN}^{++}
and
\mathbf{F}:=[\bm{0},\dots,\bm{0},\mathbf{I}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n(N+1)}
.

Remark
.

Note that in
[
22
]
, we showed that the objective function of Problem
1
when the admissible policy space is
\Pi_{1}
can be written as a difference of two convex functions, which does not necessarily mean that it is non-convex.
However, in our more recent work
[

, we showed that the objective function

is in general non-convex and admits more than one local minima.
We also provided a sufficient condition under which the objective function becomes convex, but it is not possible to check whether this condition holds without solving Problem

IV
Randomized State Feedback Policy

In this section, we define a new set of policies which are comprised of randomized affine state feedback policies, denoted as
\Pi_{2}
.
Each policy
\pi\in\Pi_{2}
is a sequence of feedback laws
\{m^{(2)}_{0}(x_{0}),m^{(2)}_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,m^{(2)}_{N-1}(x_{N-1})\}
where each
m^{(2)}_{k}
is a function from

to the set of

The functions

\displaystyle m^{(2)}_{k}(x_{k})=v^{(2)}_{k}+K^{(2)}_{k}(x_{k}-\bar{x}_{k})+n_{k}

(11)

Since every policy

is parametrized by the terms

1

Given the discrete-time stochastic linear system (
3

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{k}\mu_{k}+B_{k}v_{k}^{(2)},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

where
\mu_{k}
and

denote the mean vector and the covariance matrix of

for all

\displaystyle=A_{k}\mu_{k}+B_{k}v_{k}^{(1)}

for all

\{K_{k}^{(2)},Q_{k}^{(2)}\}
is given by

\displaystyle U_{k}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=U^{(1)}_{k}-L_{k}(\Sigma^{(1)}_{k})^{-1}L_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}+L_{k}(\Sigma^{(1)}_{k})^{-1}L_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=U_{k}^{(1)}

(19)

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

Note that Lemma
1
does not only apply to state history feedback policies (
5
).
For any linear or nonlinear control policy
\pi\in\Pi_{0}
which induces a state process with non-singular covariance matrix for all time steps, there exists a policy
\tilde{\pi}\in\Pi_{2}
which induces state and control processes which have the same first and second moments as the induced state and control processes under policy
\pi\in\Pi_{0}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
J^{\star}_{1}=J(\pi_{1}^{\star})
, where
\pi_{1}^{\star}\in\Pi_{1}
is the minimizer of Problem
1
over
\Pi_{1}
, and
J_{2}^{\star}=J(\pi_{2}^{\star})
, where
\pi_{2}^{\star}
is the minimizer of Problem
1
over
\Pi_{2}
.
Then,
J^{\star}_{2}\leq J_{1}^{\star}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{1}^{\star}\in\Pi_{1}
be the optimal policy that solves Problem 1 over
\Pi_{1}
and let
\{v^{\star}_{k},K^{\star}_{k,\ell}\}_{k=0,\ell=0}^{N-1,k}
be the corresponding set of parameters.
Now, let
\{v^{\prime}_{k},K^{\prime}_{k},Q^{\prime}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
be the policy parameters of
\pi_{2}^{\prime}\in\Pi_{2}
which are defined as in (
17
).
Then, from Lemma
1
, we can show that the first two moments of the control and state sequences under policy
\pi_{1}^{\star}
are equal to the ones under policy
\pi_{2}^{\prime}
.
Since both policies are affine functions of the state
x_{k}
, then the distribution of
x_{k}
will be Gaussian at each time step
k
, and the distribution of the terminal state
x_{N}
under both policies will also be Gaussian with the same mean and covariance. Thus, the Wasserstein distance terms in the objective function of Problem
1
are equal for both policies.
Furthermore, the running cost term at time step
k
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}u_{k}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}\mathbb{E}[u_{k}u_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}]\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\bar{u}_{k}+\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}U_{k}\right).

(20)

Thus, the running cost is a function of the first two moments of
u_{k}
.
Since the values of
\bar{u}_{k},U_{k}
are equal under both policies
\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi^{\star}
, the running cost terms in (
20
) are equal at all time steps for both policies.
Thus, we conclude that
J(\pi_{2}^{\star})\leq J(\pi_{2}^{\prime})=J(\pi_{1}^{\star})
.
∎

V
SDP formulation for Randomized State Feedback Policy

In Section
IV
, we showed that the optimal value of Problem
1
over randomized state feedback policies defined in (
11
) is upper bounded by the optimal value of the same problem over state history feedback policies defined in (
5
).
Furthermore, the number of parameters that is required to parametrize the control policies in
\Pi_{2}
is less than
\Pi_{1}
.
However, Problem
1
can be recast as a difference of convex functions program using the policy class
\Pi_{1}
as shown in Section
III
.

In this section, we will formulate Problem
1
with admissible policy space
\Pi_{2}\subset\Pi
as a convex semi-definite program using a suitable variable transformation.
In particular, the finite-dimensional problem can be formulated as follows from (
12
), (
13
):

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{k},\mu_{k},\\
K_{k},Q_{k},\Sigma_{k}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{NLP}}(\{v_{k},K_{k},Q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1})

(21)

s.t.

\displaystyle~~\eqref{eq:state-mean-random-policy},\eqref{eq:state-cov-under-random-policy}\quad\forall k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}

where

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{NLP}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big(v_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}v_{k}+\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}(K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}+Q_{k})\right)\Big)

\displaystyle~~~~+\lambda\Big(\lVert\mu_{N}-\mu_{\mathrm{d}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{N}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~\qquad-2\mathrm{tr}\big((\sqrt{\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}}\Sigma_{N}\sqrt{\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}})^{1/2}\big)\Big)

(22)

since
\mathbb{E}[u_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}u_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[u_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\mathbb{E}[u_{k}]+\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}U_{k}\right)
.
The sum of the first two terms of the objective function in (
V
) corresponds to the running cost whereas the third term corresponds to the terminal cost which is taken to be the squared Wasserstein distance between the terminal state density and the desired density. The constraints (
12a
) and (
12b
) impose the mean and the covariance dynamics given in respective equations.
The optimization problem given in (
21
) is a nonlinear program (NLP) which is generally non-convex due to the presence of the terms
\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)
in the running cost and the nonlinear equality constraints (
12b
).
In order to convexify the latter problem, we first introduce the following variable transformation which is often used in convex optimization based approaches for optimal control and robust control problems for discrete-time linear systems
[
27
,
6
,
28
]
:

\displaystyle P_{k}=K_{k}\Sigma_{k}.

(23)

By introducing the new variable
P_{k}
in (
23
), we can eliminate the bilinear terms in (
12b
).
Now, observe that the term
K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}
that appears in (
V
) and (
12b
) is equal to
P_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}P_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}
under
(
23
)
.
With this observation, we define a new decision variable
M_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle M_{k}=P_{k}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}P_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}+Q_{k}.

(24)

By using
M_{k}
and
P_{k}
, the nonlinear equality constraint (
12b
) can be written equivalently as follows:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{k}\Sigma_{k}A_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}+A_{k}P_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}B_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}+B_{k}P_{k}A_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\qquad+B_{k}M_{k}B_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}+W_{k}.

(25)

The following lemmas will be used to formulate Problem (
21
) as a standard SDP.

Lemma 2
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}
,
Y\in\mathbb{S}_{m}^{++}
and
N\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then, there exists a matrix
Q\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}
such that

\displaystyle X=NY^{-1}N^{\mathrm{T}}+Q

(26)

if and only if

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X&N\\
N^{\mathrm{T}}&Y\end{array}\right]\succeq\bm{0}.

The proof of Lemma
2
is straight-forward and therefore is omitted.
The equivalence of (
26
) and (
2
) can be seen by applying Schur’s complement formula to (
2
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}\times\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined as:

\displaystyle\varphi(M,N):=-\operatorname{tr}\left((\sqrt{M}N\sqrt{M})^{1/2}\right).

(29)

Then,
\varphi(M,N)
can be equivalently written as:

\displaystyle\varphi(M,N)

\displaystyle=\min_{L\in\mathcal{M}(N,M)}~~-\operatorname{tr}\left(L\right),

(30)

where the set
\mathcal{M}(N,M)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\bigg\{L\in\mathbb{S}^{+}_{n}~|~\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}N&M^{-1/2}L\\
LM^{-1/2}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{array}\right]\succeq\bm{0}\bigg\}.

Proof.

Let us consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{L\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}}

\displaystyle-\operatorname{tr}\left(L\right)

(33a)

s.t.

\displaystyle(\sqrt{M}N\sqrt{M})^{1/2}\succeq L

(33b)

It follows readily that the minimizer
L^{\star}
of the latter problem is equal to
(\sqrt{M}N\sqrt{M})^{1/2}
. Thus, the optimal value of the objective function of the optimization problem in (
33
) is equal to the value of
\varphi(M,N)
which is defined in (
29
). Therefore,

(
33b
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sqrt{M}N\sqrt{M}\succeq L^{2}

(34a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow N\succeq M^{-1/2}L\mathbf{I}_{n}LM^{-1/2}

(34b)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}N&M^{-1/2}L\\
LM^{-1/2}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{array}\right]\succeq\bm{0}

The relation in (
34a
) holds since the function
f(X)=X^{2}
is monotonically non-decreasing for all
X\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}
in the Löwner partial order sense.
The relation in (
34b
) holds due to the congruence transform with
M^{-1/2}
.
The final step of the proof in (
34
) follows from direct application of Schur’s complement formula to (
34b
).
∎

Using Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, we can write the problem in (
21
) as a standard SDP as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{k},\mu_{k},L\\
P_{k},\Sigma_{k},M_{k}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{SDP}}(\{v_{k},P_{k},M_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1},L)

(35a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:state-mean-random-policy},\eqref{eq:nonlinear-equality-partA}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{k}&P_{k}\\
P_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}&\Sigma_{k}\end{array}\right]\succeq\bm{0}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{N}&\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}^{-1/2}L\\
L\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}^{-1/2}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{array}\right]\succeq\bm{0}

\displaystyle~L,\Sigma_{k},M_{k}\succeq\bm{0}

(35f)

where the constraints (
12a
), (
25
), (
35
) and (
35f
) are imposed for all
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
.
In addition, the objective function
J_{\mathrm{SDP}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{SDP}}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}v_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}v_{k}+\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}M_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt+\lambda\big(\lVert\mu_{N}-\mu_{\mathrm{d}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{N}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}-2L\right)\big)

(36)

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{\mu^{\prime}_{k},v^{\prime}_{k},\Sigma^{\prime}_{k},K^{\prime}_{k},Q^{\prime}_{k}\}
be the minimizer of the NLP in (
21
), and let
\{\mu^{\prime\prime}_{k},v^{\prime\prime}_{k},\Sigma^{\prime\prime}_{k},P^{\prime\prime}_{k},M^{\prime\prime}_{k},L\}
be the minimizer of the SDP in (
35
). Then,

v^{\prime}_{k}=v^{\prime\prime}_{k}
,
\mu^{\prime}_{k}=\mu^{\prime\prime}_{k}
,
\Sigma^{\prime}_{k}=\Sigma^{\prime\prime}_{k},K^{\prime}_{k}=P^{\prime\prime}_{k}(\Sigma^{\prime\prime}_{k})^{-1},Q^{\prime}_{k}=M^{\prime\prime}_{k}-P^{\prime\prime}_{k}(\Sigma^{\prime\prime}_{k})^{-1}P^{\prime\prime\mathrm{T}}_{k}
, for all
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
.

Proof.

The equalities
\mu^{\prime}_{k}=\mu^{\prime\prime}_{k}
and
v^{\prime}_{k}=v^{\prime\prime}_{k}
follow immediately given that the problem in (
35
) and the problem in (
21
) share the constraint in (
12a
) that enforces the state mean dynamics and in addition, the terms related to the mean dynamics in the objective functions
J_{\mathrm{NLP}}
and
J_{\mathrm{SDP}}
are equal. By the definition of
M_{k}
in (
24
), it follows that
\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}M_{k}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}(K_{k}\Sigma_{k}K_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}+Q_{k})\right)
. By utilizing the definition of
M_{k}
, the state covariance dynamics in (
12b
) can be equivalently expressed as in (
24
) and (
25
).
Furthermore, we observe that (
35
) is equivalent to (
24
) from Lemma
2
.
Finally, the last term of the objective function of the NLP in (
V
) can be expressed as the optimal value of a relevant minimization problem in view of (
3
).
Hence, the SDP problem in (
35
) is equivalent to the NLP problem in (
21
).
∎

Remark
.

The problem given in (
35
) can be decomposed into two optimization problems.
One for steering the mean and one for steering the covariance.
This is because the objective function
J_{\mathrm{SDP}}
is equal to the sum of
J_{\mathrm{SDP},\mathrm{mean}}
and
J_{\mathrm{SDP},\mathrm{cov}}
, where
J_{\mathrm{SDP},\mathrm{mean}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}v_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}v_{k}+\lambda\lVert\mu_{N}-\mu_{\mathrm{d}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
and
J_{\mathrm{SDP},\mathrm{cov}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{k}M_{k}\right)+\lambda\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{N}+\Sigma_{d}-2L\right)
.
The mean steering problem can be formulated as an equality constrained convex quadratic program as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{v_{k},\mu_{k}}~

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}v_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}v_{k}+\lambda\lVert\mu_{N}-\mu_{\mathrm{d}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

s.t.

(
12a
)

Although the proposed control policy (
11
) for the solution to the CS problem corresponds to a randomized state feedback policy, it turns out that the optimal policy for the Problem in (
35
) corresponds to a deterministic policy, i.e.,
Q_{k}=\bm{0}
for all
k
.
This observation is formally stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let us assume that
A_{k}
is non-singular for all
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
and let
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi_{2}
be the optimal policy that solves the optimization problem in (
21
) and let

\{v_{k},K_{k},Q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
be its corresponding parameters. Then,
Q_{k}=\bm{0}
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
.

Proof.

Let us assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
Q_{\ell}\neq\bm{0}
for some
\ell\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
.
Let
\{\Sigma_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
be the induced state covariance sequence under the optimal policy
\pi^{\prime}
, and
M_{\ell},P_{\ell}
be defined as in (
23
), (
24
), respectively.
Let us now consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{M_{\ell},P_{\ell}}

\displaystyle~\operatorname{tr}\left(R_{\ell}M_{\ell}\right)

(37a)

s.t.

\displaystyle~\Sigma_{\ell+1}=A_{\ell}\Sigma_{\ell}A_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}+A_{\ell}P_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}B_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\qquad~~~~+B_{\ell}P_{\ell}A_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}+B_{\ell}M_{\ell}B_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}+W_{\ell}

(37b)

\displaystyle~\begin{bmatrix}M_{\ell}&P_{\ell}\\
P_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}&\Sigma_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\succeq\bm{0}

(37c)

which can be written in a more compact way after dropping the subscripts as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{M,P}

\displaystyle~\operatorname{tr}\left(RM\right)

(38a)

s.t.

\displaystyle~Z=\Sigma+P^{\mathrm{T}}Y^{\mathrm{T}}+YP+YMY^{\mathrm{T}}

(38b)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}M&P\\
P^{\mathrm{T}}&\Sigma\end{bmatrix}\succeq\bm{0}

(38c)

where
Z=A_{\ell}^{-1}(\Sigma_{\ell+1}-W_{\ell})A_{\ell}^{-\mathrm{T}}
and
Y=A_{\ell}^{-1}B_{\ell}
.
Furthermore, the dual problem associated with the problem in (
38
) is given by

\displaystyle\max_{H\in\mathbb{S}_{n},Q\in\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}}

\displaystyle~\operatorname{tr}\left(H(Z-\Sigma)-Q_{22}\Sigma\right)

(39a)

s.t.

\displaystyle~Q_{11}-(R-YHY^{\mathrm{T}})=\bm{0}

(39b)

\displaystyle~Q_{12}^{\mathrm{T}}=-HY

(39c)

where
Q=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}Q_{11}&Q_{12}\\
Q_{12}^{\mathrm{T}}&Q_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
.
From complementary slackness, we conclude that the optimal values of the primal and dual variables satisfy the following equality:

\displaystyle Q\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&P\Sigma^{-1}\\
\bm{0}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}M-P\Sigma^{-1}P^{\mathrm{T}}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\Sigma\end{bmatrix}=\bm{0}

(40)

since
\Sigma
is non-singular in view of Assumption
1
.
From (
40
), we obtain:

\displaystyle Q_{11}(M-P\Sigma^{-1}P^{\mathrm{T}})=\bm{0}

(41a)

\displaystyle Q_{12}^{\mathrm{T}}(M-P\Sigma^{-1}P^{\mathrm{T}})=\bm{0}

(41b)

Now, let
\mathcal{M}=M-P\Sigma^{-1}P^{\mathrm{T}}
. By multiplying both sides of dual feasibility constraint (
39b
) by
\mathcal{M}
on the left, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(Q_{11}-(R-YHY^{\mathrm{T}}))

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

(42a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}Q_{11}+\mathcal{M}YHY^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}R,

(42b)

where in our derivations we have used that
\mathcal{M}Q_{11}=\bm{0}
from (
41a
) and
\mathcal{M}YHY^{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{0}
from (
39c
) and (
41b
). We obtain
\mathcal{M}R=\bm{0}
. Since
R\succ\bm{0}
, we have
\mathcal{M}=\bm{0}
.
Thus, we have
M_{\ell}-P_{\ell}\Sigma_{\ell}P_{\ell}^{\mathrm{T}}=Q_{\ell}=\bm{0}
.
This contradicts our initial assumption that there exists
\ell\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
such that
Q_{\ell}\neq\bm{0}
and thus, we conclude that
Q_{k}=\bm{0}
for all
k
.
This completes the proof.
∎

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed a class of covariance steering problems for discrete-time stochastic linear systems with Wasserstein terminal cost.
We showed that the proposed state feedback policies have significant advantages over previous policy parametrizations and formulated an SDP to find the optimal policy parameters by using suitable variable transformations.
Then, we showed that the optimal policy is deterministic for the problems that we addressed.
Finally, we demonstrate the efficacy of our approach by extensive numerical simulations.

In our future work, we plan to extend our approach to robust and nonlinear covariance control problems as well as dynamic games with covariance assignment constraints.
