Compound Poisson process approximation under β \beta -mixing and stabilization
Nicolas Chenavier, Moritz Otto

Abstract
. We establish Poisson and compound Poisson approximations for stabilizing statistics of β \beta -mixing point processes and give explicit rates of convergence. Our findings are based on a general estimate of the total variation distance of a stationary β \beta -mixing process and its Palm version. As main contributions, this article (i) extends recent results on Poisson process approximation to non-Poisson/binomial input, (ii) gives concrete bounds for compound Poisson process approximation in a Wasserstein distance and (iii) illustrates the applicability of the general result in an example on minimal angles in the stationary Poisson-Delaunay tessellation. The latter is among the first (nontrivial) situations in Stochastic Geometry, where compound Poisson approximation can be established with explicit extremal index and cluster size distribution.



Keywords:
compound Poisson point process; Wasserstein distance; Palm distribution; coupling; beta-mixing; Delaunay tessellation.

60D05 . 60G55 . 60G70 . 60K35

1.
Introduction

One of the most active fields in Stochastic Geometry concerns extremes of geometric features that are based on point processes. To modelize this, let
z:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a center function and let
g:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbf{N}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
,
k\geq 1
, be measurable and translation-invariant, where
\mathbf{N}
denotes the set of locally finite couting measures. Given a point process
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a natural quantity is:

\Xi=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\xi_{\neq}^{k}}g(\mathbf{x},\xi)\delta_{z(\mathbf{x})}.

The point process
\Xi
often arises to deal with extremes in Stochastic Geometry. For example, if
k=1
,
z(x)=x
,
g(x,\xi)={\mathds{1}}\{d(x,\xi\setminus\{x\})>v_{n}\}{\mathds{1}}\{x\in W_{n}\}
, where
d(x,\xi\setminus\{x\})
denotes the distance between
x
and
\xi\setminus\{x\}
and where
W_{n}=[-n^{1/d}/2,n^{1/d}/2]^{d}
is an arbitrary large window, then
\Xi
counts the points in
W_{n}
for which the distance to the nearest neighbor is larger than some (suitable) threshold
v_{n}
. To describe the point process
\Xi
, many Poisson approximation results have been recently established. For instance, in
[
33
,
34
]
, Poisson approximations for dependent thinnings of point processes that have a density with respect to a Poisson process are derived. In
[
36
,
37
]
, the Malliavin calculus on the Poisson space is used to determine scaling limits for Poisson
U
-statistics. In
[
11
,
12
,
14
,
30
]
, the Chen-Stein method is applied to deal with the order statistics of various quantities for random tessellations. In
[
13
]
, Poisson approximation for the volume of
k
-th nearest neighbor balls is discussed in the Euclidean space and in the hyperbolic space. Rates of convergence for the Poisson approximations are generally provided, in terms of total variation distance and Kantorovich-Rubinstein (KR) distance. To the best of our knowledge, thanks to a coupling approach, the most precise results for statistics of Poisson point processes can be found in
[
6
,
29
]
. These results are formulated in the strong KR distance and have been generalized to Poisson hyperplanes in
[
28
]
and to Gibbs processes in
[
22
]
. However, Poisson process approximation in the KR distance requires a Palm coupling which is in general hard to establish. Therefore, in this paper we focus on (compound) Poisson approximation for the large class of
\beta
-mixing processes in a weaker Wasserstein distance.

Poisson approximation in general requires two assumptions: a mixing condition for the point process
\xi
and anti-clustering condition arising in
g(\mathbf{x},\xi)
. When the latter is not satisfied, the exceedances (namely the set of points
z(\mathbf{x})
such that
g(\mathbf{x},\xi)\neq 0
) can be realized into clusters. In this case, under suitable assumptions, the point process
\Xi
can be approximated by a compound Poisson point process (see
[
5
,
15
]
for examples concerning random tessellations and the
k
-th nearest neighbor graph). However, to the best of our knowledge, no rate of convergence is established in the context of Stochastic Geometry.

In this paper, we make explicit the rate of convergence for compound Poisson approximation. As first results, we establish general upper bounds in terms of total variation distance and Wasserstein distance (Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
). Then we apply the latter to have precise estimates for stabilizing statistics, first for deriving Poisson approximation (Theorem
3.1
) then for deriving compound Poisson approximation (Theorem
3.2
). Our results are based on Palm couplings. We illustrate our main theorems through two examples. The first one concerns the largest distances to the nearest neighbor when the underlying point process is the so-called Gauss-Poisson process and extends a result of
[
11
]
. The second one is unprecedented and concerns the smallest angles in a Poisson-Delaunay tessellation. In particular, we make explicit the so-called extremal index and the cluster size distribution. Except a recent work due to Basrak
et al.

[
5
]
it is the first time it is done in the context of Stochastic Geometry.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some notions on point processes and give two technical results. In Section
3
, we apply the latter to obtain Poisson and compound Poisson approximations with explicit rates of convergence when the function
g
is exponentially stabilizing. Our theorems are illustrated through the two examples discussed above in Section
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some notions on point processes and establish two technical results which will be used to derive our main theorems.

2.1.
Basic notions on point processes

2.1.1.
Point processes

First, we give some notation. In what follows, we denote by
||\cdot||
the Euclidean norm of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, and by
\mathcal{B}^{d}
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. For any
B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
, we denote by
|B|
the volume of
B
. We write
\mathbf{N}
for the space of all
\sigma
-finite counting measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}
for the space of all finite counting measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We equip
\mathbf{N}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}
with the corresponding
\sigma
-algebras that are induced by the mappings
\omega\mapsto\omega(B)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
. The latter are denoted
\mathcal{N}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
and are the Borel
\sigma
-algebras with respect to the Fell topologies on
\mathbf{N}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{N}}
, respectively (see e.g.
[
35
]
, p. 563).

A
point process
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a random variable
\xi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbf{N}
defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. The point process is said
simple
if
\xi(\{x\})\in\{0,1\}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case, with a slight abuse of notation, we identify the point process
\xi
to its support, which is a random closed subset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Now, let
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots
be finite measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
compound Poisson point process
with parameters
\pi_{1},\,\pi_{2},\dots
is a point process
\psi
of the form

\displaystyle\psi(B)=\sum_{i\geq 1}i\zeta(B\times\{i\}),\quad B\in\mathcal{B}^{d},

where
\zeta
is a Poisson point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
with intensity measure
\mathbb{E}[\zeta]
given by
\mathbb{E}[\zeta(B\times\{i\})]=\pi_{i}(B)
for
B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
. In what follows, we let
\pi:=\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}+\dots
and write
\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots)
for the distribution of a compound Poisson process with parameters
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots
and
\mathsf{Po}(\pi)
for the distribution of a Poisson process with intensity measure
\pi
. Intuitively,
\pi_{i}
is the intensity measure for clumps of size
i
. If
\psi
is stationary, then there exists
\gamma>0
(referred to as the
intensity
of
\psi
) and a distribution
\mathbb{Q}
(referred to as the
cluster size distribution
) on
\mathbb{N}^{*}:=\{1,2,\dots\}
such that
\pi_{i}(B)=\gamma|B|\mathbb{Q}(i)
for any
i\geq 1
. In this case,
\psi
can be identified to its support, which is a random set of points of the form
\{(x_{k},m_{k}):k\geq 1\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
, where
\{x_{k}:k\geq 1\}
is a stationary Poisson point process of intensity
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and where the
m_{k}
’s are i.i.d., with distribution
\mathbb{Q}
, and independent of the
x_{k}
’s. We have the
relation

\zeta(B\times\{i\})=\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathds{1}}\{x_{k}\in B\}{\mathds{1}}\{m_{k}=i\}.

2.1.2.
Beta-mixing and exponential decay of dependence

Let
\xi
be a point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To quantify the spatial decay of dependence, we introduce the notion of
\beta
-mixing coefficient
. The latter is defined as follows (see
[
16
]
): for any
A,B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
,

\beta_{A,B}^{\xi}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mu}_{1}\in\mathcal{N}_{A}}\int_{{\mu}_{2}\in\mathcal{N}_{B}}\left|\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\xi_{A}\in\mathrm{d}\mu_{1},\xi_{B}\in\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\,\right)-\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\xi_{A}\in\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}\,\right)\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\xi_{B}\in\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\,\right)\right|,

where
\xi_{C}:=\xi\cap C
denotes the restriction of
\xi
to
C
and
\mathcal{N}_{C}
is the induced
\sigma
-field of
\mathcal{N}
on
C
, for any
C\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
. In what follows, we let
\mathsf{diam}(A)=\sup\{||x-y||,x,y\in A\}
and
d(A,B)=\inf\{||x-y||,x\in A,y\in B\}
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
.

In the sense of
[
16
]
, we say that the point process
\xi
has the
exponential decay of dependence
(
\mathsf{EDD}
) property if there are constants
\theta_{0}\in[0,+\infty)
and
\theta_{i}\in(0,+\infty),\,1\leq i\leq 4,
such that for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
with
d(A,B)\geq\theta_{3}\log(\mathsf{diam}(A)\vee\mathsf{diam}(B)\vee\theta_{4})
,

\displaystyle\beta^{\xi}_{A,B}\leq\theta_{1}(\mathrm{diam}(A)^{\theta_{0}}\vee 1)(\mathrm{diam}(B)^{\theta_{0}}\vee 1)e^{-\theta_{2}d(A,B)}.

Roughly speaking, the
\mathsf{EDD}
property says that the total variation distance between the law of
(\xi_{A},\xi_{B})
and the law of the independent union of
\xi_{A}
and
\xi_{B}
decays exponentially fast as the
distance between
A
and
B
becomes large.

There is a wide class of point processes that satisfy the
\mathsf{EDD}
property, including Gibbs point processes with nearly finite range potentials, determinantal point processes with fast decay kernels (in the sense of
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
),
r
-dependent point processes and Boolean models (see Section 3 in
[
16
]
).

2.1.3.
Palm processes

We briefly recapture some basic facts from Palm theory that we need to formulate and prove our results. Let
\xi
be a point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We call
\xi^{x}
a
Palm version
of
\xi
at the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if for any measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
,

(2.1)

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\xi)\xi(\mathrm{d}x)\,\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\xi^{x})\mathbb{E}[\xi](\mathrm{d}x)\,\right],

where
\mathbb{E}[\xi]
denotes the
intensity measure
defined by
\mathbb{E}[\xi](A)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\xi(A)\,\right]
for any Borel subset
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

More generally, we will need to consider the Palm version of
\xi
with respect to a subprocess. To do it, let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
h:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbf{N}\to\{0,1\}
,
z:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two measurable functions such that
h
is translation-invariant and
z
is translation-covariant, which means that
z(x_{1}+y,\dots,x_{k}+y)=z(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})+y
for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact subset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define a subprocess
\zeta
of
\xi
by

(2.2)

\zeta:=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\xi_{\neq}^{k}}h\left(\mathbf{x},\xi_{D_{z(\mathbf{x})}}\right)\delta_{z(\mathbf{x})},

where
D_{z}:=D+z
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We call
\xi^{z,\zeta}
a Palm version of
\xi
with respect to
\zeta
if, for any measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\xi)\zeta(\mathrm{d}x)\,\right]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,\xi^{x,\zeta})\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}x)\,\right].

Intuitively,
\xi^{x,\zeta}
describes the process
\xi
given that
x
is an atom of the subprocess
\zeta
.

2.1.4.
Distances of point processes

We introduce below some distances following the same notation as
[
4
]
. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
d_{0}(x,y):=||y-x||\wedge 1
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the set of all functions
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\sup_{x\neq y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|k(x)-k(y)|/d_{0}(x,y)\leq 1,

and define a distance
d_{1}
between finite measures
\mu
and
\chi
by

\displaystyle d_{1}(\mu,\chi):

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\neq\chi(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
0,\quad&\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\chi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=0,\\
m^{-1}\sup_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\int k\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int k\,\mathrm{d}\chi\right|,\quad&\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\chi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=m>0.\end{cases}

If
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\chi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{y_{i}}
are finite counting measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d_{1}
is alternatively given by

d_{1}(\mu,\chi)=\min\limits_{\pi\in S_{m}}\left\{m^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}d_{0}\left(x_{i},y_{\pi(i)}\right)\right\}.

The metric
d_{1}
is sometimes called the
Kantorovich
1,1
- or
Wasserstein-metric induced by
d_{0}
in the literature, see e.g.
[
6
]
.

We define the distance
\mathbf{d_{2}}
between two finite point processes
\xi
and
\nu
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{d_{2}}(\xi,\nu):=\sup\limits_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,f(\xi)\,\right]-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,f(\nu)\,\right]\right|,

where
\mathcal{F}
is the class of all 1-Lipschitz functions with respect to
d_{1}
. By the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality (see
[
38
, Theorem 5.10]
), we have

(2.3)

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\xi,\nu)=\inf\limits_{C\in\Sigma(\xi,\nu)}\int_{{\mathbf{N}}\times{\mathbf{N}}}d_{1}\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\right)\,C(\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})),

where
\Sigma(\xi,\nu)
is the set of all probability measures on
{\mathbf{N}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
with marginal distributions
\mathbb{P}(\xi\in\cdot)
and
\mathbb{P}(\nu\in\cdot)
.

For finite measures
\mu,\chi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we also write

\displaystyle\widehat{d}_{1}(\mu,\chi):=\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{\mu^{\prime}\leq\mu:\,\mu^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\chi(\mathbb{R}^{d})}d_{1}(\mu^{\prime},\chi),\quad&\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})\geq\chi(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\widehat{d}_{1}(\chi,\mu),\quad&\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\chi(\mathbb{R}^{d}).\end{cases}

2.2.
Technical results

2.2.1.
A bound for the total variation distance

Let
A
be a subset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
A^{c}
is compact and contains the origin. In what follows, we write
A_{z}=A+z
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume that the mapping

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}:(z,\omega)\mapsto(z,\omega_{A_{z}})

is product measurable. In our applications, the set
A
will be the complement of a ball centered at the origin. If so,
[
2
, page 15]
shows that the mapping in (
2.4
) is product measurable.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\xi
be a simple point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact sets and let
F:\mathbf{N}\to[0,+\infty)
be a measurable function. For some compact
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a measurable function
h:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbf{N}\to\{0,1\}
, let
\zeta
be the process given at (
2.2
). Then we have for any
p>0,\,q>0
with
1/p+1/q\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B}\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{z}}^{z,\zeta})-F(\xi_{A_{z}})]\,\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}z)\right|\leq 4\left(\mathbb{E}[\zeta(B)^{p}]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\ \left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\sup_{z\in B}F(\xi_{A_{z}})^{q}\,\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

Given two point processes
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
, denote the total variation distance between
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
by

d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\sup_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{N}}|\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\xi_{1}\in\mathbf{A}\,\right)-\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\xi_{2}\in\mathbf{A}\,\right)|.

As a direct consequence of the above lemma, we derive an upper bound for the total variation distance between
\xi
and its Palm process.

Proposition 2.2
.

With the same notation as in Lemma
2.1
, if
\xi
and
\zeta
are stationary, then for any
p>0,\,q>0
with
1/p+1/q\leq 1
,

d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\xi_{A},(\xi^{o,\zeta})_{A})\leq 4\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\zeta(B)^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}}{\mathbb{E}[\zeta](B)}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

The above inequality generalizes
[
19
, Theorem 1]
in the sense that the Palm version of
\xi
is with respect to
\zeta
and not with respect to
\xi
. In particular, if
\xi
is stationary and
D=\{o\}
, we have

(2.5)

d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\xi_{A},(\xi^{o})_{A})\leq 4\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\xi(B)^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}}{\mathbb{E}[\xi](B)}(\beta^{\xi}_{B,B+A})^{1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

By definition of
\xi^{z,\zeta}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B}\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{z}}^{z,\zeta})]\,\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}z)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{z\in\zeta}{\mathds{1}}\{z\in B\}F(\xi_{A_{z}})\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\xi_{\neq}^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in B\}h(\mathbf{x},\xi_{D_{z(\mathbf{x})}})F(\xi_{A_{z(\mathbf{x})}})\Big].

In the same way, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B}\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{z}})]\,\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}z)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\xi_{\neq}^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in B\}h(\mathbf{x},\xi_{D_{z(\mathbf{x})}})\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{z(\mathbf{x})}})]\Big].

Now, let
\tilde{\xi}_{B+D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\xi_{B+D}
and
\tilde{\xi}_{A+B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\xi_{A+B}
be independent point processes. We couple
(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})
and
(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})
in such a way that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})\neq(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A}))=d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A}),(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A}))=:2\beta_{B+D,B+A}^{\xi}.

This is possible due to the coupling lemma
[
3
, p. 254]
. Because
A_{z(\mathbf{x})}=(A+B)\cap A_{z(\mathbf{x})}
and
D_{z(\mathbf{x})}=(B+D)\cap D_{z(\mathbf{x})}
provided that
z(\mathbf{x})\in B
, we get

\int_{B}\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{x}}^{x,\zeta})-F(\xi_{A_{x}})]\,\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}x)=\mathbb{E}[H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{A+B})]-\mathbb{E}[H(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{A+B})],

where
H(\mu,\nu):=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mu_{\neq}^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in B\}h\left(\mu_{D_{z(\mathbf{x})}}\right)F\left(\nu_{A_{z(\mathbf{x})}}\right)
. The following argument is adopted from
[
40
, Lemma 1]
to our setting. Let
\delta>0
,
\gamma:=\frac{1}{1+\delta}
and define

\displaystyle M:=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\mathbb{E}[H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})^{1+\delta}],\mathbb{E}[H(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})^{1+\delta}]\right\},

\displaystyle E:=

\displaystyle\left\{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbf{N}^{2}:\,H(\mu,\nu)\leq M^{\gamma}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{-\gamma}\right\}.

Then we have by the definition of
E
and (
2.6
),

(2.7)

\Big|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A}){\mathds{1}}\{(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})\in E\}-H(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A}){\mathds{1}}\{(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})\in E\}\,\right]\Big|\\
\begin{split}&\leq M^{\gamma}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{-\gamma}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})\neq(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})\,\right)\\
&=2M^{\gamma}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{\gamma\delta}.\end{split}

Moreover, because
1<M^{-\gamma\delta}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{\gamma\delta}H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})^{\delta}
on the event
E
, we have

(2.8)

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A}){\mathds{1}}\{(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})\notin E\}\,\right]\\
\leq M^{-\gamma\delta}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{\gamma\delta}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})^{1+\delta}{\mathds{1}}\{(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})\notin E\}\,\right]\leq M^{1-\gamma\delta}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{\gamma\delta},

and analogously,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,H(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A}){\mathds{1}}\{(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})\notin E\}\,\right]\leq M^{1-\gamma\delta}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{\gamma\delta},

Combining (
2.7
), (
2.8
), (
2.9
) and using the fact that
1-\gamma\delta=\gamma=\frac{1}{1+\delta}
, we have

(2.10)

\Big|\int_{B}\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{x}}^{x,\zeta})-F(\xi_{A_{x}})]\,\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}x)\Big|\\
\leq 4\max\{\mathbb{E}[H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})^{1+\delta}],\mathbb{E}[H(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})^{1+\delta}]\}^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{1-\frac{1}{1+\delta}}.

From Hölder’s inequality we find for any
p,q\in[1,+\infty]
with
1/p+1/q=1/(1+\delta)
,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[H(\xi_{B+D},\xi_{B+A})^{1+\delta}])^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[(\zeta(B))^{1+\delta}\big(\sup_{x\in B}F(\xi_{A_{x}})\big)^{1+\delta}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}[\zeta(B)^{p}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\ \Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in B}F(\xi_{A_{x}})^{q}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Since an analogous bound holds for
(\mathbb{E}|H(\tilde{\xi}_{B+D},\tilde{\xi}_{B+A})|^{1+\delta})^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}
as well, we deduce from (
2.10
) that

\Big|\int_{B}\mathbb{E}[F(\xi_{A_{x}}^{x,\zeta})-F(\xi_{A_{x}})]\,\mathbb{E}[\zeta](\mathrm{d}x)\Big|\leq 4\Big(\mathbb{E}[\zeta(B)^{p}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\ \Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in B}F(\xi_{A_{x}})^{q}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}(\beta^{\xi}_{B+D,B+A})^{1-\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

∎

2.2.2.
A general upper bound for compound Poisson process approximation

Now, let
\Xi
be a simple point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
C
be a compact subset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider below the subprocess

\displaystyle\Xi^{(i)}:=\sum_{z\in\Xi}{\mathds{1}}\{\Xi(C_{z})=i\}\delta_{z}.

The following proposition provides an upper bound between
\Xi
and a compound Poisson point process.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\Xi
be a simple finite point process such that
\mathbb{E}[\Xi(\mathbb{R}^{d})]<\infty
. Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
A^{c}
is compact, contains the origin and satisfies the measurability condition (
2.4
). Let
\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots)
be the distribution of a compound Poisson process with parameters
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
, …Then

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi,\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots))\leq e^{\mathbb{E}[\Xi(\mathbb{R}^{d})]}\Big(\sum_{i\geq 1}\Big\{|i\pi_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d})-\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]|+\min\{\pi_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}]/i\}\hat{d}_{1}(\pi_{i},\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}]/i)\Big\}\\
+\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big({\sup_{y\in A^{c}}(d_{0}(y,z))}+2\mathbb{E}[\Xi(A_{z}^{c})]\big)\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi](\mathrm{d}z)+2\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi^{zi})_{A_{z}},\Xi_{A_{z}})\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}](\mathrm{d}z)\Big\}\Big).

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Let

\displaystyle i\pi^{\prime}_{i}(\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}](\mathrm{d}z).

and
\pi^{\prime}:=\sum_{i\geq 1}\pi_{i}^{\prime}
. We first bound
\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi,\mathsf{CP}(\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2},\dots))
. To do it, let us consider a coupling
(\tilde{\Xi}^{zi},\tilde{\Xi})
of
(\Xi^{zi},\Xi)
such that
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Xi}^{zi}\neq\tilde{\Xi})=d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\Xi^{zi},\Xi)
, where
\Xi^{zi}
is the Palm version of
\Xi^{(i)}
. According to
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
, we have

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi,\mathsf{CP}(\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2},\dots))\leq e^{\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[d_{1}((\Xi^{z})_{A_{z}^{c}},\Xi^{z}(A_{z}^{c})\delta_{z})]\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi](\mathrm{d}z)\\
+\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Xi}^{zi}(A_{z})\neq\tilde{\Xi}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))+\mathbb{E}[\hat{d}_{1}((\tilde{\Xi}^{zi})_{A_{z}},\tilde{\Xi})]\right)\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}](\mathrm{d}z)\Big\}.

Here, we use the bound
d_{1}((\Xi^{z})_{A_{z}^{c}},\Xi^{z}(A_{z}^{c})\delta_{z})\leq{\sup_{y\in A_{z}^{c}}d_{0}(z,y)}
a.s. Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Xi}^{zi}(A_{z})\neq\tilde{\Xi}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\leq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Xi}(A_{z}^{c})>0)+\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Xi}^{zi}(A_{z})\neq\tilde{\Xi}(A_{z}))\leq\mathbb{E}[\Xi(A_{z}^{c})]+d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi^{zi})_{A_{z}},\Xi_{A_{z}}).

The definition of
\hat{d}_{1}
gives that

\hat{d}_{1}((\tilde{\Xi}^{zi})_{A_{z}},\tilde{\Xi})\leq{\mathds{1}}\{\tilde{\Xi}_{A_{z}^{c}}\neq\emptyset\}+{\mathds{1}}\{(\tilde{\Xi}^{zi})_{A_{z}}\neq\tilde{\Xi}_{A_{z}}\},

from which we get that

\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{d}_{1}((\tilde{\Xi}^{zi})_{A_{z}},\tilde{\Xi})\big]\leq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Xi}_{A_{z}^{c}}\neq\emptyset)+\mathbb{P}((\tilde{\Xi}^{zi})_{A_{z}}\neq\tilde{\Xi}_{A_{z}})\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\Xi(A_{z}^{c})\,\right]+d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi^{zi})_{A_{z}},\Xi_{A_{z}}).

Therefore,

\mathrm{d}_{2}(\mathcal{L}\Xi,\mathsf{CP}(\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2},\dots))\leq e^{\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\sup_{y\in A_{z}^{c}}d_{0}(z,y)+2\mathbb{E}[\Xi(A_{z}^{c})]\big)\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi](\mathrm{d}z)\\
+2\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi^{zi})_{A_{z}},\Xi_{A_{z}})\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi^{(i)}](\mathrm{d}z)\Big\}.

To complete the argument, we use that by
[
4
, Theorem 4.6]
,

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots),\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\pi_{2}^{\prime},\dots))\leq e^{\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sum_{i\geq 1}\Big\{i|\pi_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d})-\pi^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|+\min\{\pi_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\pi^{\prime}_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}\hat{d}_{1}(\pi_{i},\pi^{\prime}_{i})\Big\}.

The assertion now follows if we plug in the concrete forms of the
\pi_{i}^{\prime}s
, use the fact that
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\Xi(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\right]
and apply the triangle inequality

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi,\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\pi_{2}^{\prime},\dots))\leq\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi,\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots))+\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots),\mathsf{CP}(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\pi_{2}^{\prime},\dots)).

∎

3.
Process approximation for stabilizing statistics

In this section, we derive Poisson and compound Poisson approximations for stabilizing statistics with explicit rates of convergence. Let
\xi
be a stationary point process with intensity
\gamma>0
and
q
th correlation function
\rho_{q}
for
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that the latter is given by
the identity:

\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{i\leq q}\xi(A_{i})\Big]=\int_{A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{q}}\rho_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{q}

for any pairwise disjoint Borel sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{q}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
g_{n}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbf{N}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
z:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable functions such that
g_{n}
is translation-invariant and
z
is translation-covariant. For a given compact set
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
W_{n}:=n^{1/d}W
and define

(3.1)

\Xi_{n}[\omega]=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\omega_{\neq}^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\delta_{n^{-1/d}z(\mathbf{x})},\quad\omega\in\mathbf{N},

When
\omega=\xi
, we simply write
\Xi_{n}:=\Xi_{n}[\xi]
and refer to the latter as the
point process of exceedances
in
W
. Due to the stationarity of
\xi
and translation-invariance of
g_{n}
, we find that the intensity measure
\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}]
of
\Xi_{n}
is given by

\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}](\mathrm{d}z)=n\Big(\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W\}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi^{\mathbf{x}})]\rho_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\Big){\mathds{1}}\{z\in W\}\mathrm{d}z=:n\gamma_{n}{\mathds{1}}\{z\in W\}\mathrm{d}z.

We assume that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}>0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Second, we require that
g_{n}
is
exponentially stabilizing
in the spirit of
[
6
]
. To make this precise, we assume that there is a
stabilization radius

R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\omega)\in[r(\mathbf{x}),+\infty]
, where
r(\mathbf{x})=\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}||x_{i}-z(\mathbf{x})||
; that is

g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega\cap B_{R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\omega)}(z(\mathbf{x})))

holds for any
\omega\in\mathbf{N}
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\omega_{\neq}^{k}
, where
B_{r}(z)
denotes the (closed) ball centered at
z
with radius
r
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
. The event
\{R_{n}(o,\omega)\leq r\}
is supposed to be measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{N}_{B_{r}(o)}
. We also assume for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\cdot):\mathbf{N}\to\mathcal{F},\,\omega\mapsto B_{R_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)}(z(\mathbf{x}))
is a stopping set, i.e. for any compact sets
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\{\omega\in\mathbf{N}:\,\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\subseteq S\}=\{\omega\in\mathbf{N}:\,\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega\cap S)\subseteq S\},

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the set of closed subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3.1.
Poisson approximation

In this section, we impose that the stabilization radius satisfies the following property: for any sequence
(r_{n})
such that
r_{n}\geq(\log n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
and for
n
large enough,

-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,r_{n}^{-1}\log\Big(\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in[0,1]^{d}\}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\xi^{\mathbf{x}})>r_{n})\rho_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\Big)=:c_{\mathsf{es}}>0.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
W
be a compact subset in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
g_{n}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times\mathbf{N}\to\{0,1\}
be measurable and translation-invariant and let
\xi
be a stationary point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies the
\mathsf{EDD}
property, has intensity
\gamma>0
and
q
th correlation function
\rho_{q}
for
1\leq q\leq 2k
. We assume that (
3
) and (
3.1
) hold, define
\Xi_{n}:=\Xi_{n}[\xi]
by
(
3.1
)
and let
\pi
be a finite measure on
W
. Then

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n},\mathsf{Po}(\pi))\leq\hat{d}_{1}(n\gamma_{n}\mathsf{Leb}_{W},\pi)+e^{n\gamma_{n}|W|}\Big(c_{1}\gamma_{n}(\log n)^{d}+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\\
+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\gamma^{k+\ell}\int{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n},\,z(\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell},\mathbf{y})\in B_{b_{n}}(z(\mathbf{x}))\}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})g_{n}((\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell},\mathbf{y}),\xi^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})]\,\rho_{k+\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Big),

where the integral is over
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ell}
,
\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-\ell})
,
\mathsf{Leb}_{W}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
W
,
b_{n}:=[\theta_{2}^{-1}(4+2\theta_{0}/d)+4c_{\mathsf{es}}^{-1}]\log n
and
c_{1},c_{2}
are positive constants that do not depend on
\xi
and
g_{n}
.

Proof.

For
s_{n}:=2c_{\mathsf{es}}^{-1}\log n
, we define
\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega):=g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega){\mathds{1}}\{R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\omega)\leq s_{n}\}
as well as the truncated process

\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}[\omega]:=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\omega_{\neq}^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\delta_{n^{-1/d}z(\mathbf{x})}

and set
\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}:=\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}[\xi]
. By the triangle inequality for the
\mathbf{d_{2}}
distance, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n},\mathsf{Po}(\pi))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n},\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n,\sf{tr}})+\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n,\mathsf{t}r},\mathsf{Po}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}]))+\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathsf{Po}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}]),\mathsf{Po}(\pi)).

Since
\Xi_{n,\sf{tr}}(A)\leq\Xi_{n}(A)
for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we obtain from (
2.3
) and assumption (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n},\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n,\sf{tr}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\hat{d}_{1}(\Xi_{n},\Xi_{n,\sf{tr}})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]-\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\sf{tr}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi^{\mathbf{x}}){\mathds{1}}\{R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\xi^{\mathbf{x}})>s_{n}\}]\rho_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq|W_{n}|\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in[0,1]^{d}\}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\xi^{\mathbf{x}})>s_{n})\rho_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{n},

for some
\alpha_{1}>0
, where we have used the concrete form of
s_{n}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathsf{Po}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}]),\mathsf{Po}(\pi))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathsf{Po}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}]),\mathsf{Po}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}]))+\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathsf{Po}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}]),\mathsf{Po}(\pi))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]-\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\sf{tr}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]+\hat{d}_{1}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}],\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{n}+\hat{d}_{1}(n\gamma_{n}\mathsf{Leb}_{W},\pi).

In what follows, we provide an upper bound for
\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}},\mathsf{Po}(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}\,\right]))
. To do it, we apply Proposition
2.3
with
A_{z}:=\{z\}^{c}
. This gives

(3.2)

\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}},\mathsf{Po}(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}\,\right]))\\
\begin{split}&=\mathbf{d_{2}}(\mathcal{L}\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}},\mathsf{CP}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}],0,0,\dots))\\
&\leq 2e^{\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})]}\Big(\int_{W}\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(\{z\})]\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}](\mathrm{d}z)+\int_{W}d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{\{z\}^{c}},(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{\{z\}^{c}})\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}](\mathrm{d}z)\Big).\end{split}

Due to the stationarity of
\xi
and translation-invariance of
g_{n}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}](\{z\})=0
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, implying that the first integral on the right-hand side vanishes. We let
a_{n}:=n^{-1/d}b_{n}
and bound the total variation distance in the second integral by

d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{\{z\}^{c}},(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{\{z\}^{c}})\\
\begin{split}&\leq\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B_{a_{n}}(z)\setminus\{z\})]+\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z}(B_{a_{n}}(z)\setminus\{z\})]+d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}},(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}})\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B_{a_{n}}(z))]+\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z!}(B_{a_{n}}(z))]+d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}},(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}}),\end{split}

where
\Xi^{z!}:=\Xi^{z}\setminus\{z\}
. Here, we have for some constant
\alpha_{2}>0
,

\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B_{a_{n}}(z))]\leq n\gamma_{n}|B_{a_{n}}\cap W|\leq\alpha_{2}\,\gamma_{n}(\log n)^{d}.

Moreover,

\int_{W}\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z!}(B_{a_{n}}(z))]\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}](\mathrm{d}z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ell}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell},\mathbf{y})\in B_{n^{1/d}a_{n}}(z(\mathbf{x}))\}\\
\times\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi)\tilde{g}_{n}((\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell},\mathbf{y}),\xi)\Big],

which gives

\int_{W}\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z!}(B_{a_{n}}(z))]\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}](\mathrm{d}z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\gamma^{k+\ell}\int\limits_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ell}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell},\mathbf{y})\in B_{n^{1/d}a_{n}}(z(\mathbf{x}))\}\\
\times\mathbb{E}[\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})\tilde{g}_{n}((\mathbf{x}_{k-\ell},\mathbf{y}),\xi^{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})]\,\rho_{k+\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

For
z\in W
let
\xi^{z,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}}
be a Palm version of
\xi
at
z
with respect to
\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}
. Then we have
\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}[\xi^{z,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}}]
. Since
R_{n}
is a stabilization radius for
\tilde{g}_{n}
and since
\tilde{g}_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\omega)=0
when
R_{n}(z(\mathbf{x}),\omega)>s_{n}
, we know from the stopping set property of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, applied to
S=B_{s_{n}}(W_{n}\setminus B_{n^{1/d}a_{n}}(z))
, that
(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}}
is measurable with respect to
(\xi^{z,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}})_{B_{s_{n}}(W_{n}\setminus B_{n^{1/d}a_{n}}(z))}
(and analogously for
(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}}
), where
B_{s}(A):=A+B_{s}
for some Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s>0
, with
B_{s}:=B_{s}(o)
. This allows us to bound the total variation distance in (
3.2
) with
B_{n}^{-}:=B_{n^{1/d}w}\setminus B_{b_{n}}
, where
w:=\mathsf{diam}(W)
, by

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}},(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\xi^{z,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}})_{B_{s_{n}}(W_{n}\setminus B_{n^{1/d}a_{n}}(z))},\xi_{B_{s_{n}}(W_{n}\setminus B_{n^{1/d}a_{n}}(z))})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\xi^{z,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}})_{B_{s_{n}}(z+B_{n}^{-})},\xi_{B_{s_{n}}(z+B_{n}^{-})})

\displaystyle=d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\xi^{o,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}})_{B_{s_{n}}(B_{n}^{-})},\xi_{B_{s_{n}}(B_{n}^{-})}),

where the last equality follows from the stationarity of
\xi
. Let
D_{\mathbf{x}}=B_{s_{n}}(z(\mathbf{x}))
and
h_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)={\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}\tilde{g}_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
. Since
h_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi)
only depends on
\xi_{D_{\mathbf{x}}}
, we have

\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\xi_{\neq}^{k}}h_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi_{D_{\mathbf{x}}})\delta_{n^{-1/d}z(\mathbf{x})}.

Then we obtain from Proposition
2.2
with
p=2
,
q=\infty
,
B=B_{1}
,
A:=B_{s_{n}}(B_{n}^{-})
and from the
\mathsf{EDD}
property of
\xi
that

(3.3)

d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\xi^{o,\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}})_{B_{s_{n}}(B_{n}^{-})},\xi_{B_{s_{n}}(B_{n}^{-})})\\
\begin{split}&\leq\frac{4(\mathbb{E}[{\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}}(B)^{2}])^{1/2}}{\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B)]}\beta(\xi_{B_{s_{n}+1}},\xi_{B_{s_{n}+1}(B_{n}^{-})})^{1/2}\\
&\leq\frac{4(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B)^{2}])^{1/2}}{\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B)]}\left(\theta_{1}[(wn^{1/d}+s_{n}+1)(s_{n}+1)]^{\theta_{0}}e^{-\theta_{2}(b_{n}-2s_{n}-2)}\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

This gives, for some positive constant
\alpha_{3}
and for
n
large enough,

\int_{W}d_{\mathsf{TV}}((\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}^{z})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}},(\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}})_{B_{a_{n}}(z)^{c}})\,\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}](\mathrm{d}z)\\
\begin{split}&\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(W)]}{\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B)]}\frac{4e^{\theta_{2}}\left(\theta_{1}[(wn^{1/d}+s_{n}+1)(s_{n}+1)]^{\theta_{0}}\right)^{1/2}}{n^{2+\theta_{0}/d}}(\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n,\mathsf{tr}}(B)^{2}])^{1/2}\\
&\leq\frac{\alpha_{3}}{n},\end{split}

which concludes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

3.2.
Compound Poisson approximation

Next we consider compound Poisson process approximation. Given a sequence of Borel sets
C_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
o\in C_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the processes

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Xi_{n}^{(i)}:=\sum_{z\in\Xi_{n}}{\mathds{1}}\{\Xi_{n}((C_{n})_{z})=i\}\delta_{z}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\xi_{\neq}^{k}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}{\mathds{1}}\{\Xi_{n}((C_{n})_{z(\mathbf{x})})=i\}g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\xi)\delta_{n^{-1/d}z(\mathbf{x})},\quad i\geq 1,

where
(C_{n})_{x}:=x+C_{n}
. In particular, we have
\Xi_{n}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\Xi_{n}^{(i)}
.

We need a slightly stronger stabilization condition than for Poisson approximation and impose that the stabilization radius satisfies the following property: for any
\ell=0,\dots,k
and for any sequence

4.1.
Maximum nearest-neighbor distances in the Gauss-Poisson process

In this section, we give an application of Theorem
3.1
in a situation where the input process
\xi
is not Poisson. The latter is the Gauss-Poisson process and is defined as follows (see e.g.
[
8
]
, Example 5.6). Let
\Phi
be a point process defined in the following way:
\Phi
has an isotropic distribution and is composed of zero, one or two points with probability
p_{0}\neq 0
,
p_{1}\neq 0
and
p_{2}=1-(p_{0}+p_{1})
. If
\Phi
contains only one point then that point is the origin 0. If
\Phi
is composed of two points then these are separated by a unit distance and have midpoint
o
. Given a stationary Poisson point process
\eta
of intensity 1 in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\Phi(x)
,
x\in\eta
, be a family of i.i.d. point processes with the same distribution as
\Phi
. The
Gauss-Poisson
process is defined as

\xi=\bigcup_{x\in\eta}(x+\Phi(x)).

Notice that when
p_{2}=0
, a Gauss-Poisson process is a stationary Poisson point process with intensity
p_{1}
. The intensity of
\xi
is
\gamma=p_{1}+2p_{2}
. The Gauss-Poisson process was introduced by Newman
[
25
]
and investigated by Milne and Westcott
[
24
]
. It has a potential application in statistical mechanics (see
[
25
]
, p. 350) and could be used as a model for molecular motion (see
[
24
]
p. 169). It is also a Boolean model which satisfies the
\mathsf{EDD}
property (see
[
16
, Lemma 3.4]
). The fact that the Palm distribution of a Gauss-Poisson process is explicitly available makes it very tractable for the application of Theorem
3.1
.

In all this section, we let
W_{n}:=[-n^{1/2}/2,n^{1/2}/2]^{2}
and

(4.1)

\Xi_{n}:=\Xi_{n}[\xi]=\sum_{x\in\xi}{\mathds{1}}\{x\in W_{n}\}{\mathds{1}}\{\xi(B_{v_{n}}(x)\setminus\{x\})=0\}\ \delta_{n^{-1/2}x}.

Here, the threshold
v_{n}
is chosen such that
v_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and

(4.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}(W_{n})]=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbb{P}(\xi^{o!}(B_{v_{n}})=0)=\tau

for some fixed constant
\tau>0
.

4.1.1.
The threshold
v_{n}

We provide below an explicit value of
v_{n}
. According to (5.53) on p. 175 in
[
8
]
, we know that

\mathbb{P}(\xi^{o!}(B_{v})=0)=\frac{1}{p_{1}+2p_{2}}e^{-(p_{1}\pi v^{2}+p_{2}(2\pi v^{2}-a(v)))}\times\left\{\begin{split}&p_{1}+2p_{2},\quad&0\leq v<1\\
&p_{1},&v\geq 1;\end{split}\right.

and

a(v)=2v^{2}\arccos\frac{1}{2v}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4v^{2}-1}\text{ for }v\geq 1

and equals zero otherwise. Following the same computations as in
[
11
]
, p. 2949, it can be proved that the threshold

(4.3)

v_{n}=\frac{4p_{2}+\left(8p_{2}^{2}+8\pi(p_{1}+p_{2})\left(\log\left(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{1}+2p_{2}}\right)+\log n-\log\tau\right)\right)^{1/2}}{4\pi(p_{1}+p_{2})}

satisfies the property

\mathbb{E}[\Xi_{n}(W_{n})]=\tau+O((\log n)^{-1/2}).

4.1.2.

The following theorem claims that the point process

is asymptotically Poisson as

goes to infinity.

Theorem 4.1

Let
\tau>0
and

As an application of the above theorem, we deal with the largest distances to the nearest neighbor. To do it, for any

, let us denote by

the nearest neighbor of

With the same notation as in Theorem

A similar result, only stated for the maximum, has been established in
[
11

and are extensively used in many areas such as surface reconstruction, mesh generation, molecular modeling, and medical image segmentation, see e.g.
[

7

and
[
27

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})_{\neq}^{3}
in general position, we denote by
\Delta(\mathbf{x})
the triangle spanned by
\mathbf{x}
and by
z(\mathbf{x})
its circumcenter. We also let
\alpha_{\min}(\Delta(\mathbf{x}))
the minimum of angles of
\Delta(\mathbf{x})
.

In all this section, the Delaunay tessellation is based on a stationary Poisson point process
\eta
(or on a Palm version of
\eta
) of intensity
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular,
\eta
satisfies the
\mathsf{EDD}
property. Now, let
\tau>0
be fixed and
W_{n}=[-n^{1/d}/2,n^{1/d}/2]
. We consider the point process

(4.6)

\Xi_{n}:=\Xi_{n}[\eta]=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\eta_{\neq}^{3}}{\mathds{1}}\{z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}\}g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\eta)\delta_{n^{-1/2}z(\mathbf{x})},

where, for any
\omega\in\mathbf{N}
in general position and for any

, we write

g_{n}(\mathbf{x},\omega)={\mathds{1}}\{\alpha_{\min}(\Delta(\mathbf{x}))<v_{n}\}{\mathds{1}}\{\Delta(\mathbf{x})\in\textbf{Del}(\omega)\}.

The threshold

appearing in the above expression will be chosen in such a way that

.

4.2.1.
The threshold

We give below an explicit value of

. To do it, we first express

. Recall that

[

According to

In particular, with this choice, we have

4.2.2.
Compound Poisson approximation

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\eta

\mathbb{R}^{2}

\Xi_{n}

The above theorem can be interpreted as follows. When the size of the window

converges to a compound Poisson point process

of intensity

and where the

\mathbb{Q}(2)=\tfrac{1}{3}

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\eta_{\neq}^{3}:z(\mathbf{x})\in W_{n}}\alpha_{\min}(\Delta(\mathbf{x}))\leq v_{n}\,\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\,\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})=0\,\right)=e^{-\frac{3}{4}\tau}.

In the sense of
[

\theta=\tfrac{3}{4}
. Intuitively, it means that the mean size of a typical cluster of exceedances is
\theta^{-1}=\tfrac{4}{3}=\mathbb{Q}(1)+2\mathbb{Q}(2)

et al.

[
5
]
on the

[
15

, a potential applicability of the main results of that paper to Poisson-Delaunay tessellations is discussed. However, our method is more general, since it establishes compound Poisson approximation in the

distance with an explicit rate of convergence.

Proof of (3)
. We start with this assertion since it is the simplest case. Roughly, the latter can be explained by the following heuristic argument. If a Delaunay triangle

). To prove (3), we first notice that

In the above equation, the event

has disappeared because, on the complement of this event, it is unlikely that

. Now, to deal with the right-hand side, we apply the transformation defined by the differentiable mapping

(z,r_{0},u)\longmapsto(z+r_{0}u,z-r_{0}u)=:(x_{1},x_{2}).

The function

4r_{0}
. Writing
a(z\pm r_{0}u,x_{3},x_{4})

\displaystyle p_{n}(2)=\frac{8\pi n}{\tau}\int_{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})\times(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-a(\pm r_{0}e_{1},x_{3},x_{4})}{\mathds{1}}\{x_{3},x_{4}\notin B(\pm r_{0}e_{1},x_{3})\cup B(\pm r_{0}e_{1},x_{4})\}

Here we apply twice the Blaschke-Petkantschin formula from Theorem 5 in

\displaystyle\frac{32\pi n}{\tau}\int_{\frac{2r_{0}}{\sqrt{3}}}^{\infty}\int_{\frac{2r_{0}}{\sqrt{3}}}^{\infty}

It is an elementary calculation to derive that
