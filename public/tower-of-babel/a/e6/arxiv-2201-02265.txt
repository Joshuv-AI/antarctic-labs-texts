Learning to be adversarially robust and differentially private
Jamie Hayes, Borja Balle, M. Pawan Kumar

Abstract
. We study the difficulties in learning that arise from robust and differentially private optimization. We first study convergence of gradient descent based adversarial training with differential privacy, taking a simple binary classification task on linearly separable data as an illustrative example. We compare the gap between adversarial and nominal risk in both private and non-private settings, showing that the data dimensionality dependent term introduced by private optimization compounds the difficulties of learning a robust model. After this, we discuss what parts of adversarial training and differential privacy hurt optimization, identifying that the size of adversarial perturbation and clipping norm in differential privacy both increase the curvature of the loss landscape, implying poorer generalization performance.

In the previous section we have seen examples of the increased risk brought about by robust and private optimization, and that theory and practice are in alignment.
We now take a deeper look at why private and robust optimization is difficult.
In particular, we concretize the connection between the smoothness of the loss landscape, adversarial training and the magnitude of learned parameters. Once again, we use the example of binary logistic regression on separable data.

Adversarial training

We again consider binary logistic regression with loss
\ell_{a}(\theta)=\max_{\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert\leq c}\log(1+e^{-y(x+\delta)^{T}\theta})=\log(1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{*}})
.
We show in
appendix

B
that:

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{a}(\theta^{*})=\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}\bigg(I-\frac{\theta^{*}\theta^{*^{T}}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}}\bigg),

where
\theta^{*}
is the optimal solution, perfectly separating data from the two classes.
Clearly
\nabla^{2}\ell_{a}(\theta^{*})
is a positive semi-definite matrix with eigenvalues at
0
and
\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}
– we can take the eigenvector
v
to be
\theta^{*}
and then
(I-\frac{\theta^{*}\theta^{*^{T}}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}})\theta^{*}=0
, and we can also take the eigenvector
v
to be orthogonal to
\theta^{*}
resulting in:

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}\bigg(I-\frac{\theta^{*}\theta^{*^{T}}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}}\bigg)v=\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}v

Thus, the curvature of our loss landscape is completely determined by the ratio of adversarial budget to the magnitude of the optimal parameters, and as
c
decreases we achieve smoother solutions.

Role of clipping

We define the clipping operation on a function,
f
, as
clip(f,k)=\min(1,\frac{k}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert})f
, for some
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Xie et al. 2018
showed that that gradient clipping implies bounded weight parameters.
We can assume the optimal parameter takes the form
\theta^{*}_{clip}:=h(k)\frac{\theta}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}
, where
h(k)
is a monotonically increasing function of the gradient clipping parameter,
k
.
Note, we also assume we can find the optimum under the clipping norm
k>0
, and so we can repeat the above analysis.
This leaves the smoothness of the loss landscape determined by
\frac{c}{2h(k)}
.
Similar to the purely adversarial setting, this has the intuitive property that decreasing the clipping norm decreases the smoothness of the loss landscape.

Role of noise

The landscape sharpness is governed by the ratio of adversarial noise to weight norm.
If we perform noisy gradient descent:
\theta^{t+1}=\theta^{t}-\eta(\nabla+b)
, where
b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, then
\theta^{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta^{t}-\eta\nabla,\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.
We can use Jensen’s inequality to upper bound the expected norm of
\theta^{t+1}
:

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}\right\rVert]\leq\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}\right\rVert^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\eta\nabla\right\rVert^{2}+d\sigma^{2}}

This implies the expected norm is larger with an additional Gaussian noise term added to gradients.

3.
Discussion

Recent work by
Song et al. 2019
has empirically shown a tension between privacy and robust learning. In particular, it is shown that six state-of-the-art defense methods designed to reduce the success of adversarial examples
increase
the risk of membership inference attacks due to overfitting on the training set
(
Shokri et al. 2017
)
.
The phenomenon of overfitting in adversarially robust optimization has also been observed by
Rice et al. 2020
.
If
Song et al. 2019
showed that robust models are less private,
Tursynbek et al. 2020
recently identified the contrapositive relation, empirically showing that differentially private models come at the expense of robustness.
Meanwhile,
Ghazi et al. 2021
shows that the sample complexity of learning both robust and private halfspaces is worse than learning with either property by itself.

. The logistic loss has the following first and second derivatives:

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\ell(\theta)=\frac{-ye^{-yx^{T}\theta}x}{1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta}}

(5)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta)=\frac{e^{-yx^{T}\theta}xx^{T}}{(1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta})^{2}}

\nabla L(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{-y_{i}e^{-y_{i}x_{i}^{T}\theta}x_{i}}{1+e^{-y_{i}x_{i}^{T}\theta}}
.
By Taylor expansion we have:

(7)

\displaystyle L(\theta^{t+1})=L(\theta^{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla L(\theta^{t}))\leq L(\theta^{t})-\eta_{t}\left\lVert\nabla L(\theta^{t})\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{8}\left\lVert\nabla L(\theta^{t})\right\rVert^{2}

where
\eta_{t}
is the learning rate at step

.
For simplicity, we assume gradient descent is performed with a constant learning rate
\eta_{t}:=\eta
for every step

.
Our following analysis will depend on a sufficiently small learning rate, which we specify at the relevant places.
Because
L
is smooth (bounded Hessian), we can apply standard gradient descent convergence analysis. For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

(8)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-2\eta\langle\nabla L(\theta^{t}),\theta^{t}-\theta\rangle+\eta^{2}\left\lVert\nabla L(\theta^{t})\right\rVert^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-2\eta(L(\theta^{t})-L(\theta))+\eta^{2}\left\lVert\nabla L(\theta^{t})\right\rVert^{2}\qquad\text{(by convexity of $\ell$)}

(10)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-2\eta(L(\theta^{t})-L(\theta))+\frac{\eta}{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}(L(\theta^{t})-L(\theta^{t+1}))\qquad\text{(by Taylor expansion)}

(11)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}+(\frac{\eta}{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}-2\eta)L(\theta^{t})+2\eta L(\theta)-\frac{\eta}{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}L(\theta^{t+1})

If
\eta<4
, the contribution of the

term is negative and so:

(12)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}+2\eta L(\theta)-\frac{\eta}{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}L(\theta^{t+1})

If we set
\theta^{0}=0
, then
\theta^{1}=-\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{-y_{i}x_{i}}{2}
and
\left\lVert\theta^{1}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\eta}{2}\leq 1
as long as
\eta<2
.
Under the assumption that
L(\theta^{t+1})\leq L(\theta^{t})
, summing the above between
s=1,...,t

(13)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert\theta^{1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-t\bigg(\frac{\eta}{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}L(\theta^{t+1})-2\eta L(\theta)\bigg)

(14)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle L(\theta^{t+1})\leq\frac{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}{t\eta}(\left\lVert\theta^{1}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{2})+2(1-\frac{\eta}{8})L(\theta)

Since we are free to choose
\theta
, we set it to
\theta:=\frac{\log t}{\gamma}u
, then:

(15)

\displaystyle L(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+e^{\frac{-y\log t}{\gamma}x^{T}u})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+e^{-\log t})=\log(\frac{t+1}{t})

Together with the fact that
\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert=\frac{\log t}{\gamma}
, we get the final upper bound:

(16)

\displaystyle L(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\frac{\eta}{8}}{t\eta}\bigg(1+(\frac{\log t}{\gamma})^{2}\bigg)+2(1-\frac{\eta}{8})\log(\frac{t+1}{t})

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{8-\eta}{8t\eta}\bigg(1+(\frac{\log t}{\gamma})^{2}\bigg)+(\frac{8-\eta}{4})\log(\frac{t+1}{t})

We next find convergence rates under gradient-based adversarial training.
Let
\ell_{a}(\theta)=\max_{\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert\leq c}\log(1+e^{-y(x+\delta)^{T}\theta})=\log(1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert})
. We first find the first and second derivative of this loss.

If we let
h(\theta):=e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}
, then
\ell_{a}(\theta)=\log(1+h(\theta))
and
\nabla\ell_{a}(\theta)=\frac{h^{\prime}(\theta)}{1+h(\theta)}
, where
h^{\prime}(\theta)=h(\theta)r(\theta)
if we take
r(\theta):=-yx+c\frac{\theta}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}
.
Similarly:

(18)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{a}(\theta)=\frac{h(\theta)r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}+h(\theta)^{2}r^{\prime}(\theta)+h(\theta)r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+h(\theta))^{2}}

To bound the above we must bound
r^{\prime}(\theta)=c(\frac{I}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}-\frac{\theta\theta^{T}}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{3}})
.
We first note that the problem can be reduced to finding a lower bound to
\left\lVert\theta^{t}\right\rVert
for any
t>0
because
\left\lVert r^{\prime}(\theta)\right\rVert\leq\frac{2c}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}
.
If we let
\theta^{0}=0
, then
\ell_{a}
is no longer differentiable at zero, so we take the sub-gradient to be:

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}(-yx+cr)}{1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}}\in\partial\ell_{a}(\theta^{1})

where
r
is a vector with
\left\lVert r\right\rVert\leq 1
.
For example, we can take
r=0
, and then the sub-derivative becomes
-\frac{yx}{2}\in\partial\ell_{a}(\theta^{1})
, and is bounded above like
\theta^{1}=\frac{\eta_{0}}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}x_{i}
, and
\langle\theta^{1},u\rangle\geq\frac{\eta_{0}\gamma}{2}
.
We then take
\eta_{t}:=\eta
for
t>1
such that
\frac{\eta_{0}}{2}>\eta
.
We then note the inner product between
-\nabla\ell_{a}(\theta)
and
u
is strictly positive:

(20)

\displaystyle\langle-\nabla\ell_{a}(\theta),u\rangle\geq\frac{e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}}{1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}}(\gamma-c)>0

and so

(21)

\displaystyle\langle\theta^{2},u\rangle=\langle\theta^{1}-\eta\nabla L_{a}(\theta^{1}),u\rangle=\langle\theta^{1},u\rangle+\eta\langle-\nabla L_{a}(\theta^{1}),u\rangle\geq\langle\theta^{1},u\rangle\geq\eta\gamma

By a similar argument for any
t>1
,
\langle\theta^{t},u\rangle\geq\eta\gamma
.
Because
u
is the global minimizer it follows that
\left\lVert\theta^{t}\right\rVert\geq\eta\gamma
.
We have a lower bound on
\theta^{t}
for any
t
and so
\left\lVert r^{\prime}(\theta)\right\rVert\leq\frac{2c}{\eta\gamma}
.

The other term we upper bound is
r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}=xx^{T}-2cyx^{T}\frac{\theta}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}+c^{2}\frac{\theta\theta^{T}}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{2}}
, and since we assume bounded data we can bound this like
\left\lVert r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}\right\rVert\leq(1+c)^{2}
.
Piecing this altogether gives:

(22)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}L_{a}(\theta)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{h(\theta)r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}}{(1+h(\theta))^{2}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\frac{h(\theta)(1+h(\theta))r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+h(\theta))^{2}}\right\rVert

(23)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{h(\theta)r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}}{(1+h(\theta))^{2}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\frac{h(\theta)r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+h(\theta))}\right\rVert

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{h(\theta)}{(1+h(\theta))^{2}}\right\rVert\left\lVert r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\frac{h(\theta)}{(1+h(\theta))}\right\rVert\left\lVert r^{\prime}(\theta)\right\rVert

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+c)^{2}}{4}+\frac{2c}{\eta\gamma}

Letting
s:=\frac{(1+c)^{2}}{4}+\frac{2c}{\eta\gamma}
,
now that we have a bound on the second derivatives we can again use the Taylor expansion of gradient descent:

(26)

\displaystyle L_{a}(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle=L_{a}(\theta^{t}-\eta\nabla L_{a}(\theta^{t}))

(27)

\displaystyle\leq L_{a}(\theta^{t})-\eta\left\lVert\nabla L_{a}(\theta^{t})\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{s\eta^{2}}{2}\left\lVert\nabla L_{a}(\theta^{t})\right\rVert^{2}

Then for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(28)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-2\eta(L_{a}(\theta^{t})-L_{a}(\theta))+\frac{\eta}{1-\frac{s\eta}{2}}(L_{a}(\theta^{t})-L_{a}(\theta^{t+1}))

As long as
\eta
satisfies
s\eta<1
, the
L_{a}(\theta^{t})
contribution is negative and we get:

(30)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}+2\eta L_{a}(\theta)-\frac{2\eta}{2-s\eta}L_{a}(\theta^{t+1})

Isolating
\eta
in the condition
s\eta<1
gives
\eta<\frac{4(\gamma-2c)}{\gamma(1+c)^{2}}
which also implies that we require
c<\frac{\gamma}{2}
for our results to hold.
Summing over the iterations as before gives:

(32)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert\theta^{1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-t(\frac{2\eta}{2-s\eta}L_{a}(\theta^{t+1})-2\eta L_{a}(\theta))

(33)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{2t\eta}{2-s\eta}L_{a}(\theta^{t+1})\leq\left\lVert\theta^{1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}+2t\eta L_{a}(\theta)

By choosing
\theta=\frac{\log t}{\gamma-c}u
we again get
\ell_{a}(\theta)=\log(1+\frac{1}{t})
and
\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert=\frac{\log t}{\gamma-c}
, giving the final upper bound:

(34)

\displaystyle L_{a}(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2-s\eta}{2t\eta}(\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\theta^{1}\right\rVert^{2}+2t\eta L_{a}(\theta))

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2-s\eta}{2t\eta}\bigg((\frac{\log t}{\gamma-c})^{2}+(1+c)^{2}\bigg)+(2-s\eta)\log(1+\frac{1}{t})

Privacy / No robustness.
We next find convergence rates under gradient descent with differential privacy.
At each step we add noise; we do not clip gradients as they are already
1
-Lipschitz and we assume here the clipping norm is larger than one.
Let
\overline{\nabla L(\theta)}=\nabla L(\theta)+b
, where
b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.
Throughout the following analysis we find convergence results under expectations taken over the noise added for differential privacy.

Our analysis is almost identical to the non-private case, and we sometimes write
\nabla
or
\overline{\nabla}
where the context is clear.
Firstly, the Taylor expansion follows:

(36)

\displaystyle L(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle=L(\theta^{t}-\eta\overline{\nabla})

(37)

\displaystyle\leq L(\theta^{t})-\eta\nabla^{T}\overline{\nabla}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\overline{\nabla}^{T}\nabla^{2}\overline{\nabla}

Note that
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{4}
and
\overline{\nabla}=\nabla+b
. Although we take expectations over the random noise in following, we omit the notation
\mathbb{E}
for conciseness:

(38)

\displaystyle L(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq L(\theta^{t})-\eta\left\lVert\nabla\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{8}(\left\lVert\nabla\right\rVert^{2}+d\sigma^{2})

Then for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and again taking expectations over the noise,

(39)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\theta^{t+1}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}-2\eta\langle\nabla+b,\theta^{t}-\theta\rangle+\eta^{2}\left\lVert\nabla+b\right\rVert^{2}

(40)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\theta^{t}-\theta\right\rVert^{2}+2\eta L(\theta)-\frac{8\eta}{8-\eta}L(\theta^{t+1})+\frac{8d\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{8-\eta}

Repeating the same process as in the non-private case we get:

(41)

\displaystyle L(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8-\eta}{8t\eta}\bigg(1+d\sigma^{2}+(\frac{\log t}{\gamma})^{2}\bigg)+(\frac{8-\eta}{4})\log(\frac{t+1}{t})+\eta d\sigma^{2}

Privacy / robustness.
We can repeat the analysis as in non-private and robust case, while taking expectations over the random noise to give the upper bound:

(42)

\displaystyle L_{a}(\theta^{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2-s\eta}{2t\eta}\bigg((1+c)^{2}+d\sigma^{2}+(\frac{\log t}{\gamma-c})^{2}\bigg)+(2-s\eta)\log(1+\frac{1}{t})+\eta d\sigma^{2}

Importantly, if we compare the non-private case to the private case for either the robust or non-robust setting, we take an equivalent hit in convergence that depends on the variance of noise and dimensionality of data.

Appendix B
Binary logistic regression with clipping and adversarial training.

The following analysis only considers a single input, but can extended to a batch of inputs through simple averaging.
We again consider binary logistic regression with loss
\ell_{a}(\theta)=\max_{\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert\leq c}\log(1+e^{-y(x+\delta)^{T}\theta})=\log(1+e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert})
.
As stated previously the first and second derivatives are given by:

(48)

\displaystyle\nabla\ell_{a}(\theta)=\frac{h(\theta)r(\theta)}{1+h(\theta)}

(49)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{a}(\theta)=\frac{h(\theta)r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}+h(\theta)^{2}r^{\prime}(\theta)+h(\theta)r^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+h(\theta))^{2}}

where
h(\theta):=e^{-yx^{T}\theta+c\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}
,
r(\theta):=-yx+c\frac{\theta}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}
, and
r^{\prime}(\theta)=c(\frac{I}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert}-\frac{\theta\theta^{T}}{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{3}})
.
At the optimal
\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta}\ell_{a}(\theta)
, we have
-yx+c\frac{\theta^{*}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}=0\implies\theta^{*}=\frac{yx\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}{c}
. The
h(\theta)r(\theta)r(\theta)^{T}
term in the second derivative vanishes at the optimum and
h(\theta^{*})=1
and we are left with:

(50)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{a}(\theta^{*})=\frac{r^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2}=\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}\bigg(I-\frac{\theta^{*}\theta^{*^{T}}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}}\bigg)

Clearly
\nabla^{2}\ell_{a}(\theta^{*})
is a positive semi-definite matrix with eigenvalues at
0
and
\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}
– we can take the eigenvector
v
to be
\theta^{*}
and then
(I-\frac{\theta^{*}\theta^{*^{T}}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}})\theta^{*}=0
, and we can also take the eigenvector
v
to be orthogonal to
\theta^{*}
resulting in:

(51)

\displaystyle\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}\bigg(I-\frac{\theta^{*}\theta^{*^{T}}}{\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert^{2}}\bigg)v=\frac{c}{2\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert}v

Thus, the curvature of our loss landscape is completely determined by the ratio of adversarial noise to the magnitude of the optimal parameters, and as
c
decreases we achieve smoother solutions.

Appendix C
Excess risk analysis for robust & private learning

So far we have concentrated our analysis on logistic regression.
We now give a simple risk analysis for general convex losses for robust learning with differential privacy.

Let
\ell(\cdot,(\cdot,\cdot)):\mathcal{W}\times(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a loss function with the following properties:
L
-Lipschitz,
G
-smooth, convex and linear in its parameters,
\ell(w,(x,y))=\ell(yw^{T}x)
,
\forall w\in\mathcal{W}
,
\left\lVert w\right\rVert\leq\beta
,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\forall(x,y)\in(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
,
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq 1
and
y\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Consider two batch updates that differ in their final element:

(52)

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}\nabla\ell(y_{i}w_{t}^{T}x_{i})x_{i}+y_{n}\nabla\ell(y_{n}w_{t}^{T}x_{n})x_{n}+z

(53)

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}\nabla\ell(y_{i}w_{t}^{T}x_{i})x_{i}+y_{n}^{\prime}\nabla\ell(y_{n}^{\prime}w_{t}^{T}x_{n}^{\prime})x_{n}^{\prime}+z

Setting
A=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}\nabla\ell(y_{i}w_{t}^{T}x_{i})x_{i}
,
B=y_{n}\nabla\ell(y_{n}w_{t}^{T}x_{n})x_{n}
,
B^{\prime}=y_{n}^{\prime}\nabla\ell(y_{n}^{\prime}w_{t}^{T}x_{n}^{\prime})x_{n}^{\prime}
, we have
P=\mathcal{N}(A+B,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
and
Q=\mathcal{N}(A+B^{\prime},\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. To achieve
(\epsilon,\delta)-DP
, we must bound
\alpha_{M}(\lambda)=\max_{X,X^{\prime}}\alpha_{M}(\lambda;X,X^{\prime})
as defined in
(
Abadi et al. 2016
)
, where
\alpha_{M}(\lambda)=\lambda D_{\lambda+1}(P||Q)
is as defined in
(
Bun & Steinke 2016
)
. This results in the following:

(54)

\displaystyle\alpha_{M_{k}}(\lambda)=\lambda D_{\lambda+1}(P\rVert Q)=\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)\left\lVert B-B^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}

Under the assumption that
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq 1
,
y\in\{\pm 1\}
, and
\ell
is L-Lipschitz, we have the following upper bound:

(55)

\displaystyle\left\lVert B-B^{\prime}\right\rVert\leq 2L\implies\alpha_{M_{k}}(\lambda)\leq\frac{2\lambda(\lambda+1)L^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}

Summing over all iterations gives:

(56)

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}(\lambda)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{T}\alpha_{M_{k}}(\lambda)\leq\frac{2\lambda(\lambda+1)L^{2}T}{\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{c\lambda^{2}L^{2}T}{\sigma^{2}}

for some constant
c
. Then for some
c^{\prime}
, taking
\sigma^{2}=\frac{c^{\prime}L^{2}T\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\epsilon^{2}}
will give
(\epsilon,\delta)-DP
according to Theorem 2.2 in
(
Abadi et al. 2016
)
.

Turning to the robust setting, we wish to solve
\min_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\max_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{p}\leq r}\ell\big(yw^{T}(x+v)\big)
, and in the convex setting the inner-maximization can be given in closed-form as:

(57)

\displaystyle\max_{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{p}\leq r}\ell\big(yw^{T}(x+v)\big)=\ell(yw^{T}x-r\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{q})

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. If we set
p=\infty
, then squared Euclidean distance between
B
and
B^{\prime}
becomes

(58)

\displaystyle\left\lVert B-B^{\prime}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert y_{n}\nabla\ell_{n}x_{n}-y_{n}^{\prime}\nabla\ell_{n}^{\prime}x_{n}^{\prime}-r\sign(w)\nabla\ell_{n}+r\sign(w)\nabla\ell_{n}^{\prime}\right\rVert

(59)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d}(1+r)L

This similarly holds for
p=2
by noting that that the derivative of
\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}
is of unit length and replacing
\sign{(w)}
with this unit length value. Then
(\epsilon,\delta)-DP
holds by taking
\sigma^{2}=\frac{c^{\prime}(1+r)^{2}L^{2}T\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\epsilon^{2}}
.

First note that if
\ell
is
L
-Lipschitz, then
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\hat{g}\right\rVert^{2}]=\left\lVert g\right\rVert^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert^{2}]\leq L^{2}+d\sigma^{2}
.
Assuming
\ell
is
\lambda
-strongly convex, then by Theorem 1 of
(
Shamir & Zhang 2013
)
the bound on excess risk is given by the following:

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(w_{T})-\ell(w^{*})]\leq\frac{17(L^{2}+d\sigma^{2})(1+\log(T))}{\lambda T}

where for
t>0
the per step learning rate satisfies
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{\lambda t}
.

For private learning we can plug in
\sigma^{2}=\frac{c^{\prime}L^{2}T\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\epsilon^{2}}
and for robust and private learning we can plug in
\sigma^{2}=\frac{c^{\prime}(1+r)^{2}L^{2}T\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\epsilon^{2}}
.
