Approaching the Soundness Barrier: A Near Optimal Analysis of the Cube versus Cube Test
Dor Minzer, Kai Zheng

Abstract
The Cube versus Cube test is a variant of the well-known Plane versus Plane test of Raz and Safra [ 10 ] , in which to each 3 3 -dimensional affine subspace C C of 𝔽 q n \mathbb{F}_{q}^{n} , a polynomial of degree at most d d , T ⁡ ( C ) T(C) , is assigned in a somewhat locally consistent manner: taking two cubes C 1 , C 2 C_{1},C_{2} that intersect in a plane uniformly at random, the probability that T ⁡ ( C 1 ) T(C_{1}) and T ⁡ ( C 2 ) T(C_{2}) agree on C 1 ∩ C 2 C_{1}\cap C_{2} is at least some ε \varepsilon . An element of interest is the soundness threshold of this test, i.e. the smallest value of ε \varepsilon , such that this amount of local consistency implies a global structure; namely, that there is a global degree d d function g g such that g | C ≡ T ⁡ ( C ) g|_{C}\equiv T(C) for at least Ω ⁡ ( ε ) \Omega(\varepsilon) fraction of the cubes. We show that the cube versus cube low degree test has soundness poly ⁡ ( d ) / q \poly(d)/q . This result achieves the optimal dependence on q q for soundness in low degree testing and improves upon previous soundness results of poly ⁡ ( d ) / q 1 / 2 \poly(d)/q^{1/2} due to Bhangale, Dinur and Navon [ 4 ] .

Low degree testing

\mathbb{F}_{q}
where

is
thought of as large, a degree parameter

much smaller than

polynomial

The most basic example of these encoding schemes and tests is given by the line versus line test. To define this, let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of all lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

to

1.

Choose a point

2.

Choose two lines
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
uniformly and independently conditioned on
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\ni x

Read

It is clear that if

Early works

To strengthen the PCP theorem however (and more precisely, to improve on the soundness guarantee), it became clear that one has to be able to analyze these tests for as small of
a soundness parameter

[
3
]
and other variants of the line versus line test were considered.
Most relevant to us are the higher dimensional analogs of the tests, which are the plane versus plane test
[
10
]
and the cube versus cube test
[
4
]
. In these tests, instead
of encoding a polynomial

using its table of restrictions to lines, one encodes

using its table of restrictions to planes (in the case of the
plane versus plane test) and to
3

In the cube versus cube test, a polynomial
f\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
is encoded using its cubes table
T
, which assigns to each affine cube
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
a degree at most
d

which is equal to the restriction

1.

Choose an affine plane
P\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
uniformly at random.

2.

Choose two affine cubes
C_{1},C_{2}
uniformly at random such that
C_{1},C_{2}\supseteq P
.

3.

Read
T(C_{1})
and
T(C_{2})
from the cubes table and accept if and only if the restrictions to
P

T(C_{1})|_{P}=T(C_{2})|_{P}

It is clear that a valid table of restrictions
T
passes the test with probability
1

T

T

d

More generally, one can consider tests where affine cubes and planes are replaced with affine subspaces of dimensions

contains supposed restrictions of

to each dimension

Choose an affine dimension

Choose two affine dimension

Read

Indeed the cube versus cube test is the

10
]
. As discussed earlier,
the primary reason these tests were considered is that they admit a very low soundness error. The soundness error of a test is defined to be the smallest

such that if the test passes with probability at least
s

d
function

for an
\Omega(s)
-fraction of the dimension
k
affine subspaces

For the plane vs plane test, Raz and Safra
[
10

[
5

). An improved analysis of this test was given by Moshkovitz and Raz
[
9

, who showed that
s\geqslant\poly(d)/q^{1/8}
. We note
that the natural lower bound on

is
\Theta(1/q)
, since there are tables of assignments
T
on which the test passes with probability
\Theta(1/q)
yet all degree
d
functions
agree with at most
o(1/q)
of the entries of

. Indeed, take
h=cq^{3}
for some small absolute constant

, and pick affine subspaces
W_{1},\ldots,W_{h}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

of dimension
n-1
uniformly at random; for each

, also pick a degree
d
polynomial
f_{i}\colon W_{i}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
. To define the table
T
, for each plane
P
pick
the first
i
such that
P\subseteq W_{i}
if such
i
exists and define
T(P)=f_{i}|_{P}
; otherwise, pick
T(P)
randomly. It is not hard to see that if we pick planes,
P_{1},P_{2}
, randomly that intersect in a line, and
P_{1}\subseteq W_{i}
, then
P_{2}\subseteq W_{i}
with probability
\Omega(1/q)
. In this case, one can show that with constant probability
both
P_{1},P_{2}
are assigned by

, and hence in expectation, the test passes with probability
\Omega(1/q)
. It can be shown though, that as each
f_{i}
is chosen randomly
and each

contains
O(1/q^{3})
of the planes, that no degree
d
polynomial agrees with
T
on more than

of the planes.

In light of this, an intriguing open question is what the soundness threshold for the plane versus plane and other low degree tests is.
For the Raz-Safra plane versus plane test, the best known soundness analysis is still due to Moshkovitz and Raz in
[
9
]
. Motivated by this question and more recently,
Bhangale, Dinur, and Livni Navon
[
4
]
suggested to study the cube versus cube test, and managed to show that its soundness threshold is higher than that
known in the Raz-Safra test. Specifically, they showed that the soundness threshold of the cube versus cube test is
s\geqslant\poly(d)/q^{1/2}
, and
ask whether it is the case that the true soundness threshold of the cube versus cube test is linear in
1/q
. Our main result confirms that this is indeed the case.

More precisely, the cube versus cube test studied in
[
4
]
is slightly different than the one presented above. The test they consider is the generalized test above
with
k=3
and
\ell=0
, meaning the tester picks a point
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and then independently two cubes
C_{1},C_{2}
that contain

and

assign to

[

though, the acceptance probability of the various cube versus cube tests are all virtually the same for any cubes

Theorem 1.1
.

For a table

k\leqslant\frac{n}{2}

(1-o(1))\alpha_{krk}(T)\leqslant\alpha_{kr^{\prime}k}(T)\leqslant\alpha_{krk}(T)+(1+o(1))q^{-(k-2r^{\prime}+r+1)}.

In this paper, we improve the analysis in

4
]
and show that the cube versus cube test has soundness
\frac{10^{7}d^{6}}{q}

as large, and

Suppose

is a cubes table such that for each affine cube

is a degree

\Omega(\varepsilon)

We note that while Theorem

implies our soundness bound also holds for the the cube versus cube test with point intersection considered in

1.2
Proof Overview

Our proof strategy relies heavily on ideas from
[
4
]
, but to get the optimal soundness threshold our argument uses more refined expansion
as well as sampling arguments.

High level description.

Suppose that the cube versus cube test passes with probability
\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{10^{7}d^{6}}{q}
. For a point
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and a value

\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, define

\mathcal{C}_{x}=\{C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}~|~C\text{ is a $3$-dimensional cube, $x\in C$}\},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}=\{C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}~|~C\in\mathcal{C}_{x}\text{ and $T(C)_{|x}=\sigma$}\}.

In words,
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
is the set of cubes containing
x
on which
T
gives the point
x
the value
\sigma
. We may view
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
as a partition
of
\mathcal{C}_{x}
according to the value given to
x
. In
[
4
]
, the authors show that this partition cannot consist of only parts that are small, otherwise
the contribution to the acceptance probability of the test of these cube would be small. Our argument observes further properties of these partitions. At a high
level, we show that if we take
x
and
y
randomly and consider the partition
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}
of the cubes containing
both
x
and
y
, then this partition cannot consist only of small parts. This suggests that the partitions
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
and
\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}
are correlated.
In fact, in a sense we are able to show that (in the part that contributes to the acceptance probability of the test), they are in
1
to
1
correspondence
in the sense that for each
\sigma
we can identify a single
\tau
so that
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y}
and
\mathcal{C}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}
are roughly the
same set. We then show that for a sufficiently large fraction of
x
’s and
\sigma
, there is a global function agreeing with
T
on almost all of
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
. The above correlation between partitions then allows us to argue that the global functions of
x
and
y
agree provided the parts
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
and

\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}
are matching according to the above described
1
-to-
1
correspondence. Using this, we are able to show that for some
x
, the global
function on
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
agrees with sufficiently many global functions on
\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}
for other
y
’s, and enough so that this already gives agreement

\Omega(\varepsilon)
with the table
T
. A more detailed overview follows.

A more detailed description.

Consider the following way of generating a pair of cubes
intersecting in a plane: sample
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, sample a plane
P
containing both
x
and
y
, and sample
C_{1},C_{2}
cubes containing
P
. We note that the points
x
and

y
partition the set of cubes
C
containing them into
q^{2}
sets:

\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}:=\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau},\qquad\text{for all }\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.

We further note that for the test on
C_{1},C_{2}
to pass, they both must belong to the same part in the partition, i.e. they must both be in
C_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}

for some
\sigma,\tau
. Thus, fixing
x
and
y
, this shows that the probability of the test passing (conditioned on that) is related to the expansion properties of
the partition
\left(\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}
in the affine Grassmann graph. We formally define this graph in the
next section, but informally in our context this graph contains all of the cubes
C
containing both
x
and
y
, and two cubes are adjacent if they intersect in a plane.
Thus, we get that the probability that the test passes is at most

\sum\limits_{\sigma,\tau}\Pr_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}\supseteq P\ni x,y}[C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}]=\sum\limits_{\sigma,\tau}\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\left(1-\Phi\left(\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\right).

Here,
\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)
is the relative measure of
\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
among all cubes containing
x,y

and
\Phi_{xy}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)
is the edge expansion of
\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
in the above defined Grassmann graph.
Using the spectral properties of the Grassmann graph, we show that this sum is at most
O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)+\sum\limits_{\sigma,\tau}\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}
, and thereby conclude (using the fact that the probability the cube versus cube test passes with probability
at least
\varepsilon
) that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\right]\geqslant\varepsilon.

(1)

Intuitively, this says that the partitions given by
(C_{x,\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}
and
(C_{y,\tau})_{\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}
are somewhat correlated
with each other. To make use of this correlation, we must pass first to subsets of
C_{x,\sigma}
on which we already know that we have some global structure.

For that, we use ideas from
[
4
]
. Specifically, we identify and consider the pairs
(x,\sigma)
that contribute almost all of the acceptance probability of the test, and show that for
each such
(x,\sigma)
one may find a global degree
d
function
g_{x,\sigma}
that agrees with
T
on almost all of
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
. Thus, letting
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
be the set
of cubes containing
x
on which
T
and
g_{x,\sigma}
agree, we get that
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
are very close to each other. This motivates us to
define
\mathcal{F}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}=\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
and
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
are very close to each other, one expects (
1
) to imply something
similar about the partial partition
\mathcal{F}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
, and we show that this in indeed the case (though not quite as obviously as one may initially expect). Namely, we
show that (
1
) implies that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\right]\geqslant\Omega(\varepsilon).

(2)

We note now that while the sum is over
q^{2}
terms, for each
\sigma
there exists at most a single
\tau
for which the corresponding summand is non-zero. Indeed,
fixing
\sigma
means that we look at
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
on which we have some global degree
d
function
g_{x,\sigma}
, and hence the only viable option for
\tau

is
\tau=g_{x,\sigma}(y)
. Moreover, by averaging considerations it turns out that we can have at most
O(1/\varepsilon)
many
\sigma
’s for which the pair
(x,\sigma)

contributes to the above sum. Hence we may find
x
and
\sigma
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,g_{x,\sigma}(y)\end{subarray}\right\}}\right)^{2}\right]\geqslant\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}).

Let
\eta=\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})
. Sampling
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
uniformly, it is clear that the expected value of

\mu_{x,y}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y})
is
\eta
, and in fact one can show that a relatively strong concentration
holds. This concentration is strong enough to show that the fraction of
y
’s for which
\mu_{x,y}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y})\geqslant 2\eta

is very small – small enough so that discarding them from the above inequality only incurs a small loss. Furthermore, we can also neglect
y
’s such that

\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,g_{x,\sigma}(y)\end{subarray}\right\}}\right)\leqslant c\varepsilon
for some sufficiently small absolute constant
c
, so altogether we get that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,g_{x,\sigma}(y)\end{subarray}\right\}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{c\varepsilon\leqslant\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,g_{x,\sigma}(y)\end{subarray}\right\}}\right)\leqslant 2\eta\right\}}\right]\geqslant\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}).

Doing a dyadic partitioning, we get that there is a
t\geqslant c\varepsilon
such that

t^{2}p_{t}\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right),\qquad\text{ where }~~p_{t}=\Pr_{y}\left[\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,g_{x,\sigma}(y)\end{subarray}\right\}}\right)\in[t,2t)\right].

Taking
Y
to be the set of
y
’s such that
\mu_{x,y}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,g_{x,\sigma}(y)\end{subarray}\right\}}\right)\in[t,2t)
,
we show
g_{x,\sigma}\equiv g_{y,\tau}
for all
y\in Y
. Hence,
g_{x,\sigma}
agrees with the table
T
on all of the cubes
in
\mathcal{F}=\cup_{y\in Y}\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}
, and to complete the proof it suffices to show that
\mu(\mathcal{F})\geqslant\Omega(\varepsilon)
. This is done by
applying a standard spectral argument and using the fact that
\mu(Y)=p_{t}\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{t^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

Throughout the paper, we let
q
denote the field size,
n
denote the dimension, and let

\mathcal{C}
be the set of all
affine
cubes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. An affine cube is a linear
3
-dimensional subspace with all points translated by some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. We henceforth refer to affine cubes as simply cubes.

For points
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we denote

\mathcal{C}_{x}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}\;|\;x\in C\},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{C}_{x,y}=\mathcal{C}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y}.

We also let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of all lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and likewise let
\mathcal{L}_{x}
denote the set of all lines containing
x
. We let
\mu
be the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{C}
,
\mu_{x}
be the uniform measure over
\mathcal{C}_{x}
and
\mu_{x,y}
be the uniform measure over

\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
. Abusing notation, we will sometimes use
\mu
to also denote the uniform measure over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and it will be clear from context if we are referring
to the uniform measure over
\mathcal{C}
or over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

For two functions
f
and
g
, we say that
f
is
\delta
-close to
g
if they differ on at most a
\delta
fraction of their inputs. That is,
\Pr_{x}[f(x)\neq g(x)]\leqslant\delta
, where the space of inputs
x
is the domain of
f
and
g
. We will also write
f\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\delta}}}{{\approx}}}\,g
to denote that
f
is
\delta
-close to
g
.

Finally, we will have a cubes table
T
which assigns to each cube
C\in\mathcal{C}
a degree
d
polynomial
T(C)\colon C\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Given such table,
we define

\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x}\;|\;T(C)(x)=\sigma\},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}=\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau},

and we denote for convenience
\varepsilon(T)=\alpha_{323}(T)
, i.e. the probability that the cube versus cube test on
T
passes.

Henceforth, we suppose that
T
is a cubes table that passes the cube versus cube test with probability
\varepsilon(T)=\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{10^{7}d^{6}}{q}
.

2.2
Grassman and Affine Grassman Graphs

In this section we introduce the Grassman and Affine Grassman graphs. At times it will be helpful to think about the cube versus cube test in terms of these graphs and use known results about expansion in these graphs.

Definition 1
.

The
Grassman graph

\G(k,\ell)
is the graph with vertex set consisting of all
k
-dimensional linear subspaces and edges between all pairs of subspaces
(U_{1},U_{2})
satisfying
\dim(U_{1}\cap U_{2})=\ell
.

Definition 2
.

The
Affine Grassman graph

\AG(k,\ell)
, is the graph with vertex set consisting of all
k
-dimensional affine subspaces and edges between all pairs of subspaces
(V_{1},V_{2})
satisfying
\dim(V_{1}\cap V_{2})=\ell
.

If we let
G=\AG(3,2)
we can think of the cube versus cube test as choosing a random edge
(C_{1},C_{2})\in E
in the graph and checking if
T(C_{1})
and
T(C_{2})
agree on their intersection. Thus we may let
S
be the set of edges
(C_{1},C_{2})\in E
where
T(C_{1})|_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}}=T(C_{2})|_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}}
. Since
\varepsilon
is the exact pass probability of the test, we have

\Pr\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\right]=\varepsilon.

If we fix a point
x
and condition on
C_{1},C_{2},
and
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
containing
x
, then notice that with the remaining degrees of freedom, choosing
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
corresponds to choosing two linear cubes that intersect in a plane. Equivalently, this is choosing a random edge from the induced subgraph of
G
on
\mathcal{C}_{x}
, which we denote by
G_{x}
. Notice that
G_{x}
is isomorphic to
\G(3,2)
. If we further condition on
C_{1},C_{2},
and
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
containing two points
x
and
y
, then the random choice of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
is equivalent to choosing two linear planes that intersect in a line, or a random edge in
G_{xy}
which is isomorphic to
\G(2,1)
.

If we fix a point
x
and condition on
C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
, then notice that the test can only pass if
C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
as well. This is where expansion in the Grassman graphs plays a role. Conditioned on
C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
, and
C_{2}
intersecting
C_{1}
in a plane containing
x
, the probability that

C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
is precisely
1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})
, where
\Phi_{x}
denotes expansion in
G_{x}
. Likewise, if we fix two points
x,y
, and condition on
C_{1}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
, then we can lower bound the pass probability by
1-\Phi_{xy}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)
, which is the probability that
C_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
. The following fact about expansion in
\G(k,k-1)
allows us to freely convert these quantities to measures.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
be a set of
k
-dimensional subspaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and let
\Phi(A)
denote the expansion of
A
in
\G(k,k-1)
. Then

\mu(A)-\frac{1}{q^{n-k+1}-2}\leqslant 1-\Phi(A)\leqslant\mu(A)+\frac{1}{q}.

Proof.

Let
F
denote the indicator function for
A
. Thus
F
is a function that takes
k
-dimensional subspaces as input and is
1
on those in
A
and
0
otherwise. Let
T
denote the adjacency operator of
\G(k,k-1)
. By
[
7
, Theorem 9.4.1]
, the eigenvalues of
T
are

\lambda_{r}=\frac{-(q^{k}-1)+q^{r}(q^{k-r}-1)(q^{n-k-r+1}-1)}{(q^{k}-1)(q^{n-k+1}-2)},

for
r=0,1\ldots,k
. It is easily seen that for
r=0
we get an eigenvalue of
1
, and for any other
r
the corresponding eigenvalue is at most
q^{-r}
.
Thus, we may write
F=F_{0}+F_{1}+\ldots+F_{k}
where
F_{r}
is an eigenvector of
T
of eigenvalue
\lambda_{r}
, where
F_{0}\equiv\mu(S)
, and so

1-\Phi(A)=\frac{\langle F,TF\rangle}{\mu(S)}=\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\left(\mu(A)^{2}+\sum_{r\geqslant 1}\lambda_{r}\|F_{r}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leqslant\mu(A)+\frac{1}{q}\frac{\sum_{r\geqslant 1}\|F_{r}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mu(A)}\leqslant\mu(A)+\frac{1}{q},

where we use the fact that
\sum_{r\geqslant 1}\|F_{r}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{r\geqslant 0}\|F_{r}\|_{2}^{2}=\|F\|_{2}^{2}=\mu(A)
.
For the lower bound, we note that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{r}
are nonnegative for
r<k
, while
\lambda_{k}=\frac{-1}{q^{n-k+1}-2}
.
∎

2.3
Bipartite Inclusion Graphs

Next, we review some results regarding sampling edges in bipartite inclusion graphs that we will use later. These results were used in the context of direct product testing in
[
8
]
and to analyze the cube versus cube test in
[
4
]
.

Let
G(A,B)
denote the bipartite inclusion graph with vertices
A\cup B
and edges
E
, where
A
consists of subspaces of some dimension
i
(possibly
i=0
if
A
is a set of points) in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
,
B
consists of subspaces of some dimension
j>i
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, and
(a,b)\in E
if
a\subseteq b
. Also let
\lambda(G)
denote the second largest singular value of a graph
G
and for any vertex
v
in the graph, let
N(v)
denote its neighborhood. It is known that such graphs satisfy the following sampling property:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G=G(A,B)
be a bi-regular bipartite graph. For every subset
B^{\prime}\subset B
of measure
\mu>0
and every
E^{\prime}\subset E

\left|\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B^{\prime}\\
a\in N(b)\end{subarray}}[(a,b)\in E^{\prime}]-\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in N(a)\cap B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}[(a,b)\in E^{\prime}]\right|\leqslant\frac{\lambda(G)}{\sqrt{\mu}}.

For completeness, we list the singular values of the bipartite inclusion graphs that we will use.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
m\geqslant 6
, the bipartite inclusion graphs for subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
have the following second singular values:

1.

For
G_{1}=G(\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{C}_{x})
,
\lambda(G_{1})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
.

2.

For
G_{2}=G(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{C}_{x})
,
\lambda(G_{2})\approx\frac{1}{q}
.

3.

For
G_{3}=G(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}\setminus\{x\},\mathcal{C}_{x})
,
\lambda(G_{3})\approx\frac{1}{q}
.

4.

For
G_{4}=G(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}\setminus\ell,\mathcal{C}_{\ell})
,
\lambda(G_{4})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
.

5.

For
G_{5}=G(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m},\mathcal{C})
,
\lambda(G_{5})\approx\frac{1}{q^{3/2}}
.

6.

For
G_{6}=G(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3},\mathcal{L})
,
\lambda(G_{6})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
.

We use
\approx
to denote equality up to a multiplicative factor of
1+o(1)
, where
o(1)
is a function of
q
that approaches
0
as
q\xrightarrow{}\infty
.

We refer the reader to the appendix of
[
4
]
for the proofs of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
.

3
The Main Argument

3.1
Local Agreement with Degree d polynomials

In this section we show how to construct local degree
d
polynomials
g_{x}
, that agree with an
\Omega(\varepsilon)
fraction of the cubes in
C_{x}
. The strategy is similar to that of
[
4
]
. The following notion of excellent
(x,\sigma)
pairs will be relevant to our analysis. This notion was introduced in an analysis of direct product tests
[
8
]
and also used in
[
4
]
.

Recall that
\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{10^{7}d^{6}}{q}
is the exact pass probability of the test. Let
\gamma
be a small constant factor times
d^{-3}
, say
\gamma=\frac{1}{1000d^{3}}
.

Definition 3
.

Call a pair
(x,\sigma)
excellent if the following are true:

•

\mu_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\geqslant\varepsilon/5

•

For
C_{1}\in C_{x,\sigma}
chosen uniformly at random, a random line
\ell\subset C_{1}
containing
x
, and
C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
chosen uniformly conditioned on containing
\ell
, we have that

\Pr_{C_{1},\ell,C_{2}}[T(C_{1})|_{\ell}\neq T(C_{2})|_{\ell}]\leqslant\gamma.

Henceforth we let
X
denote the set of all excellent pairs
(x,\sigma)
. Notice that if we let

p_{x,\sigma}=\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma},\\
C_{2}\cap C_{1}=\ell\ni x\end{subarray}}[T(C_{1})(x)=T(C_{2})(x),\;T(C_{1})|_{\ell}\neq T(C_{1})|_{\ell}],

then the second condition holds if
p_{x,\sigma}\leqslant\gamma\left(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\right)
, since

\Pr_{C_{1},\ell,C_{2}}[T(C_{1})|_{\ell}\neq T(C_{2})|_{\ell}]=\frac{p_{x,\sigma}}{1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})}

where the probability is with respect to the distribution in the second point of Definition
3
, and the inequality is due to Lemma
2.1
.

This leads to the following observation:

Lemma 3.1
.

If
(x,\sigma)\notin X
, then either
\mu_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})<\varepsilon/5
or
p_{x,\sigma}>\gamma\left(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\right)
.

The main result of this section shows that for an excellent
(x,\sigma)
, there exists a degree
d
polynomial
g
that agrees with almost all of the cubes in
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
. The same result has been proved in
[
4
]
under a stronger assumption on the largeness of
\varepsilon
.
We begin by defining the function
f_{x,\sigma}
by plurality over all cubes in
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
.

Definition 4
.

For a pair
(x,\sigma)
, define
f_{x,\sigma}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{q}
so that
f_{x,\sigma}(y)
is the most common value of
T(C)(y)
over cubes
C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
containing
y
. If there are no such cubes, assign the value arbitrarily.

Since
(x,\sigma)
is excellent, it follows immediately that
f_{x,\sigma}
usually agrees with
T
on points in cubes
C\in C_{x,\sigma}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
f_{x,\sigma}
as defined above,

\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma},y\in C}[f_{x,\sigma}(y)=T(C)(y)]\geqslant 1-\gamma.

Proof.

Let

\gamma_{y}=\Pr_{C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{y}\cap\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}}[T(C_{1})(y)\neq T(C_{2})(y)].

Since
f_{x,\sigma}(y)
is the most common value of
T(C)(y)
over cubes
C\in\mathcal{C}_{y}\cap\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
. We can write,

1-\gamma_{y}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{y}\cap\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}}[T(C)(y)=\alpha]^{2}\leqslant\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{y}\cap\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}}[T(C)(y)=f_{x,\sigma}(y)].

Since this inequality holds for any
y
, it also holds under expectation over any distribution of
y
.
Take the distribution as: choose
C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
uniformly, and then
y\in C
uniformly different from
x

(or equivalently, a uniformly random line
\ell\subseteq C
containing

x
and then
y\in\ell
uniformly different from
x
). Then, denoting by
\ell_{x,y}
the line that
passes through
x
and
y
, we get that

\displaystyle\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma},y\in C}[T(C)(y)=f_{x,\sigma}(y)]\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{C,y}[1-\gamma_{y}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma},y\in C_{1}\\
C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y}\end{subarray}}[T(C_{1})(y)=T(C_{2})(y)]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma},y\in C_{1}\\
C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y}\end{subarray}}[T(C_{1})|_{\ell_{x,y}}=T(C_{2})|_{\ell_{x,y}}]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\gamma,

where the last inequality is due to the assumption that
(x,\sigma)
is excellent.
∎

Finally, we show that
f_{x,\sigma}
is close to a degree
d
polynomial by using the following robust characterization of low degree polynomials due to Rubinfeld and Sudan.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
11
]

Let
f:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
N_{y,h}=\{y+i(h-y)\;|\;i\in[d+1]\}
. If,

\Pr_{y,h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}[\;\exists p\text{ such that }p|_{N_{y,h}}=f|_{N_{y,h}}]\geqslant 1-\delta,

for
\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2(d+2)^{2}}
, then
f
is
2\delta
-close to some degree
d
polynomial
g
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x}}[T(C)\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{2\gamma}}}{{\approx}}}\,f|_{C}]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10}
, then
f
is
4d\gamma
close to some degree
d
polynomial
g
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x}\;|\;T(C)\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{2\gamma}}}{{\approx}}}\,f|_{C}\}
. Fix
C\in\mathcal{F}
. We first show that for almost all lines in
C
,
f
agrees with the degree
d
function
T(C)
on almost all points on the line. We do this using Lemma
2.2
and the spectral properties of

G_{C}=G(A\cup B,E)
– the bipartite inclusion graph where
A
is the set of all points in
C
,
B
is the set of all affine lines in
C
, and
E
is the set of all point-line pairs
(x,\ell)
such that
x\in\ell
. This graph is the same as
G_{5}
in Lemma
2.3
, with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\cong\mathcal{C}
, so
\lambda(G_{C})\leqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{q}}
.

Let
A^{\prime}=\{y\in A\;|\;T(C)(y)\neq f(y)\}
, and let
B^{\prime}=\{\ell\in B\;|\;|N(\ell)\cap A^{\prime}|\geqslant 3\gamma|N(\ell)|\}
, where
N(\ell)
consists of all of the points contained in
\ell
. In other words,
A
is the set of points in
C
where
f
disagrees with
T(C)
, and
B^{\prime}
is the set of lines where
f
and
T(C)
disagree on at least
3\gamma
of the points. Then, by Lemma
2.2

\left|\Pr_{\ell\in B^{\prime},y\in N(\ell)}[y\in A^{\prime}]-\Pr_{y\in A,y\in C\cap B^{\prime}}[y\in A^{\prime}]\right|\leqslant\frac{\lambda(G_{C})}{\sqrt{\frac{|B^{\prime}|}{|B|}}}.

By construction,
\Pr_{\ell\in B^{\prime},y\in N(\ell)}[y\in A^{\prime}]\geqslant 3\gamma
and by assumption

\Pr_{y\in A,C\in N(y)\cap B^{\prime}}[y\in A^{\prime}]\leqslant\Pr_{y\in A}[y\in A^{\prime}]\leqslant 2\gamma,

so
|B^{\prime}|\leqslant\left(\frac{\lambda(G_{C})}{\gamma}\right)^{2}|B|\leqslant\gamma|B|
. Thus, for any cube
C\in\mathcal{F}
,
f
is
3\gamma
-close to a degree
d
polynomial on at least a
1-\gamma
fraction of the lines in
C
. We next show that
\mathcal{F}
contains enough cubes to cover nearly all lines.

Consider the bipartite inclusion graphs
G_{1}=G(\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{C}_{x})
and
G_{2}=G(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{C}_{x})
, which have second singular values at most
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
and
\frac{1}{q}
respectively by Lemma
2.3
. Let
E^{\prime}
denote the set of line-cube pairs
(\ell,C)
such that
\ell\subseteq C
and
T(C)|_{\ell}\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{3\gamma}}}{{\approx}}}\,f|_{\ell}
. Recall that
|\mathcal{F}|/|\mathcal{C}_{x}|\geqslant\varepsilon/10
, so by Lemma
2.2
on
G_{1}
,

\left|\Pr_{\ell,C\in N(\ell)\cap F}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\notin\mathcal{L}_{x}]-\Pr_{C\in F,\ell\in C}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\notin\mathcal{L}_{x}]\right|\leqslant\frac{1/\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon/10}}=\sqrt{\frac{10}{q\varepsilon}}\leqslant\gamma.

By Lemma
2.2
on
G_{2}
,

\left|\Pr_{\ell,C\in N(\ell)\cap F}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{x}]-\Pr_{C\in F,\ell\in C}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{x}]\right|\leqslant\frac{1/q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon/10}}=\sqrt{\frac{10}{q^{2}\varepsilon}}\leqslant\gamma.

We showed previously that for every
C\in\mathcal{F}
,
\Pr_{\ell\in C}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}]\geqslant 1-\gamma
. Let
p=|\mathcal{L}_{x}|/|\mathcal{L}|
be the probability that a randomly chosen line contains
x
. Then,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\ell}[\exists C\text{ s.t. }(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr_{\ell,C\in N(\ell)\cap F}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=(1-p)\Pr_{\ell,C\in N(\ell)\cap F}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\notin\mathcal{L}_{x}]+p\Pr_{\ell,C\in N(\ell)\cap F}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{x}]

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-p)\Pr_{C\in F,\ell\in C}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\notin\mathcal{L}_{x}]+p\Pr_{C\in F,\ell\in C}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}\;|\;\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{x}]-\gamma

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\ell\in C}[(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}]-\gamma

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-2\gamma.

Therefore,
f
is
3\gamma
-close to a degree
d
polynomial on at least
1-2\gamma
of the lines in
\mathcal{L}
. This allows us to bound the probability that, for a randomly chosen neighborhood,
f
is equal to a low degree polynomial on that neighborhood. To do this, we view picking a random neighborhood as randomly picking a line
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, and then
y,h\in\ell
uniformly at random.

\displaystyle\Pr_{y,h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}[\exists C\text{ s.t. }f|_{N_{y,h}}=T(C)|_{N_{y,h}}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr_{\ell}[\exists C\text{ s.t. }(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\cdot\Pr_{\ell,y,h\in\ell}[f|_{N_{y,h}}=T(C)|_{N_{y,h}}\;|\;\exists C\text{ s.t. }(\ell,C)\in E^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-2\gamma)(1-(d+2)3\gamma)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-5d\gamma.

Since
\gamma=\frac{1}{1000d^{3}}\leqslant\frac{1}{10(d+2)^{3}}
. Theorem
3.1
now implies that there is a degree
d
polynomial
g
such that
f
is
4d\gamma
close to
g
.
∎

Combining Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
we get that for each excellent
(x,\sigma)
, the plurality vote function
f_{x,\sigma}
is
4d\gamma
-close to a degree
d
polynomial.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each excellent
(x,\sigma)
, the function
f_{x,\sigma}
is
4d\gamma
-close to a degree
d
polynomial.

Proof.

Using Markov’s inequality and Lemma
3.2
,
\Pr_{C\in C_{x}}[T(C)\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{2\gamma}}}{{\approx}}}\,f_{x,\sigma}|_{C}]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mu_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\geqslant\varepsilon/10
. Thus,
f_{x,\sigma}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.3
, and as a result, it is
4d\gamma
-close to some degree
d
polynomial.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

If
(x,\sigma)
is excellent, then there exists a degree
d
polynomial
g_{x,\sigma}
such that
g_{x,\sigma}
agrees with
T(C)
for at least
1-2\sqrt{\gamma}
of the cubes
C\in C_{x,\sigma}
.

Proof.

Let
g_{x,\sigma}
be the degree
d
polynomial guaranteed by Lemma
3.4
and define,

\mathcal{F}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x}\;|\;f_{x,\sigma}|_{C}\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\sqrt{\gamma}}}}{{\approx}}}\,T(C)\},\qquad A^{\prime}=\{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\;|\;f_{x,\sigma}(z)\neq g_{x,\sigma}(z)\}.

Then
\mu(A^{\prime})\leqslant 4d\gamma
and and by Markov’s inequality and Lemma
3.2
,
\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F})\geqslant(1-\sqrt{\gamma})\mu_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10}
.

The bipartite inclusion graph
G_{3}=G(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{x\},\mathcal{C}_{x})
has second singular value at most
1/q
by Lemma
2.3
. By Lemma
2.2
we have,

\left|\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{F},y\in N(C)}[y\in A^{\prime}]-\Pr_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus x,C\in N(y)\cap\mathcal{F}}[y\in A^{\prime}]\right|\leqslant\frac{1/q}{\sqrt{\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F})}}\leqslant\frac{1/q}{\sqrt{\varepsilon/10}}\leqslant 4\gamma.

Since
\Pr_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{x\},C\in N(y)\cap\mathcal{F}}[y\in A^{\prime}]\leqslant 2\mu(A^{\prime})
, where the factor of
2
accounts for the fact that
y\neq x
, it follows that
\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{F},y\in N(C)}[y\in A^{\prime}]\leqslant 8d\gamma+4\gamma\leqslant 9d\gamma
. By applying Markov’s inequality again, for at least
1-\sqrt{\gamma}
of the cubes
C\in\mathcal{F}
,
g_{x,\sigma}
and
f_{x,\sigma}
agree on at least
1-9d\sqrt{\gamma}
of the points in
C
. Finally, since these cubes are in
\mathcal{F}
,
g_{x,\sigma}
and
T(C)
must agree on at least
1-\sqrt{\gamma}-9d\sqrt{\gamma}>\frac{d}{q}
of the points in
C
. By the Schwartz-Zippel lemma,
g_{x,\sigma}|_{C}=T(C)
for such
C
, and these cubes make up at least
(1-\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}>1-2\sqrt{\gamma}
of the cubes in
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
.
∎

Concluding this section.

For each
(x,\sigma)\in X
and degree
d
polynomial
g_{x,\sigma}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
be the set of all cubes
C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
satisfying
T(C)=g_{x,\sigma|C}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}=\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
. By Lemma
3.5
,
\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})\geqslant(1-2\sqrt{\gamma})\mu_{x}(C_{x,\sigma})
and
\mu_{x}(D_{x,\sigma})\leqslant\frac{2\sqrt{\gamma}}{1-2\sqrt{\gamma}}\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})\leqslant\sqrt{5\gamma}\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
.

3.2
Pruning the Pass Probability

Throughout this section, we consider the distribution
\mathcal{D}
over tuples
(x,\sigma,C_{1},y,\tau,C_{2})
defined by the following process:

1.

Choose
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
uniformly at random.

2.

Choose
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with weight proportional to
\mu_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})
.

3.

Choose
C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
uniformly at random.

4.

Choose
y\in C_{1}
uniformly at random and different from
x

5.

Choose
\tau\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with weight proportional to the fraction of cubes
C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
with
\dim(C\cap C_{1})=2
satisfying
T(C)(y)=\tau
.

6.

Choose
C_{2}
uniformly at random over
\{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\;|\;\dim(C\cap C_{1})=2\}
.

Notice that the marginal distribution over
x,y
is uniform, and conditioned on
x,y

the marginal distribution over
(C_{1},C_{2})
is uniform among all pairs of cubes intersecting in a plane
P
that contains both
x
and
y
.
In particular, the marginal of
(C_{1},C_{2})
is uniform among cubes that intersect in a plane, and so letting

S=\{(C_{1},C_{2})\;|\;\dim(C_{1}\cap C_{2})=2,\;T(C_{1})|_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}}=T(C_{2})|_{C_{1}\cap C_{2}}\}
we have

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\right]\geqslant\varepsilon.

(3)

The distribution
\mathcal{D}
can also be generated by either first choosing
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and proceeding symmetrically, or by first choosing
(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and then choosing
(C_{1},C_{2})
as a random edge in the induced subgraph of
\AG(3,2)
on
C_{xy}
and assigning
(\sigma,\tau)
accordingly.

The main goal of this section is to prove the following lemma, asserting that we may focus on the cases in which
C_{1},C_{2}
lie in

\mathcal{F}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}=\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}
. Namely,
we show that a constant fraction of the probability on the left hand side of (
3
) comes from the event that
C_{1},C_{2}
are both in
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\text{ and }C_{1},C_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Lemma
3.6
. Towards this end, recall from Section
3.1
that for each
(x,\sigma)\in X
we write
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}=\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
. Therefore, we can deduce Lemma
3.6
by showing that the probability of passing the test,
i.e.
(C_{1},C_{2})\in S
in conjunction of either one of the events: (a)
(x,\sigma)\notin X
and (b)
(x,\sigma)\in X
but
C_{1}\in D_{x,\sigma}
, is small. We begin
by bounding the first event.

Lemma 3.7
.

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,\;(x,\sigma)\notin X\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Proof.

Consider the following alternative way of sampling according to
\mathcal{D}
. First, choose a point
x
uniformly at random, then a random
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with weight proportional to
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
, a uniformly at random
C_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
, and finally a uniformly at random
C_{2}
that intersects
C_{1}
in a plane
P\ni x
. The point
y
can then be chosen at random from
P
and
\tau=T(C_{2})(y)
. Viewing
\mathcal{D}
this way we see that

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,\;(x,\sigma)\notin X\right]\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{(x,\sigma)\notin X}(1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma}))\right],

since after
x
and
\sigma
are fixed,
(C_{1},C_{2})\in S
only if
T(C_{1})
and
T(C_{2})
agree on
x
and
C_{2}\in C_{x,\sigma}
. This probability, with
C_{x,\sigma}
fixed, is precisely
1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma})
.

If
(x,\sigma)\notin X
, then either
\mu(C_{x,\sigma})<\varepsilon/5
, or
p_{x,\sigma}+\frac{\gamma}{q^{n-2}-2}\geqslant\gamma\mu(C_{x,\sigma})
by Lemma
3.1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{(x,\sigma)\notin X}(1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma}))\right]\leqslant

\displaystyle\;\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x}(C_{x,\sigma})<\varepsilon/5}(1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma}))\right]

\displaystyle+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{p_{x,\sigma}>\gamma\left(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\right)}(1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma}))\right].

We bound each term on the right hand side separately. For the first term, applying Lemma
2.1
yields

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x}(C_{x,\sigma})<\varepsilon/5}(1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma}))\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{5}+\frac{1}{q}.

For the second term, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{p_{x,\sigma}>\gamma\left(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma})\right)}(1-\Phi_{x}(C_{x,\sigma}))\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[\frac{p_{x,\sigma}}{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1000d^{4}}{q}.

The last inequality is due to the fact that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\sigma}\left[p_{x,\sigma}\right]
can be bounded by the probability that two degree at most
d
entries
T(C_{1})
and
T(C_{2})
disagree on a line
\ell\subseteq C_{1}\cap C_{2}
, but agree on a point
x\in\ell
. By the Schwartz-Zippel lemma, this probability is at most
d/q
.

Putting everything together gives:

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,\;(x,\sigma)\notin X\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{5}+\frac{1000d^{4}}{q}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

∎

As
(x,\sigma)
and
(y,\tau)
have the same marginal distribution, we get

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,(y,\tau)\notin X\right]\leqslant\varepsilon/4
, and it follows by the union bound that

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land((x,\sigma)\notin X\lor(y,\tau)\notin X)\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(4)

Next, we bound the contribution from
C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,\;(x,\sigma)\in X,\;C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}\right]\leqslant 7\gamma\varepsilon

Proof.

First we rewrite the probability as,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,\;(x,\sigma)\in X,\;x\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma)\in X}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma})\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\;|\;C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}\right]\right].

We fix
(x,\sigma)\in X
and upper bound
\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\;|\;C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}\right]
.

Conditioned on
C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
, the distribution
\mathcal{D}
chooses
C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
uniformly at random, a plane
P\subseteq C_{1}

containing
x
, and
C_{2}
containing
P
.
For any
C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
, we have by definition that
T(C_{1})\neq g_{x,\sigma}|_{C_{1}}
, so by the Schwartz-Zippel Lemma we have

T(C_{1})|_{P}=g_{x,\sigma}|_{P}
with probability at most
d/q
. In the case that
T(C_{1})|_{P}\neq g_{x,\sigma}|_{P}
, we immediately get that

T(C_{1})|_{P}\neq T(C_{2})|_{P}
for any
C_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
that contains
P
(as
T(C_{2})=g_{x,\sigma}|_{C_{2}}
).
Furthermore,
T(C_{1})|_{P}\neq T(C_{2})|_{P}
for all
C_{2}\notin\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
as
p\ni x
.
Thus, if the plane
P
is chosen so that
T(C_{1})|_{P}\neq g_{x,\sigma}|_{P}
, then the test can pass only if
C_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
.
Averaged over all
C_{1}\in D_{x,\sigma}
the probability of picking such a plane
p\subseteq C_{1}
and then
C_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
is at most
1-\Phi_{x}(D_{x,\sigma}
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\;|\;C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}\right]\leqslant\frac{d}{q}+1\cdot(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}))\leqslant\frac{d+1}{q}+\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}),

where we use Lemma
2.1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma)\in X}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma})\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\;|\;C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma)\in X}\mu(\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma})^{2}\right]+\frac{d+1}{q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma)\in X}5\gamma\mu(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})^{2}\right]+\frac{d+1}{q}.

Finally notice that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[5\gamma\mu(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})^{2}\right]\leqslant 5\gamma\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\mu(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})\right]+\frac{1}{q^{n-2}-2}\right)\leqslant 5\gamma\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{q^{n-2}-2}\right)\leqslant 6\gamma\varepsilon,

where we use the lower bound of Lemma
2.1
with
k=3
for the first inequality. The second inequality holds since
\mu(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})(1-\Phi_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}))
is exactly the probability that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are both in
\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
,
in which case the test passes. Hence, when this quantity is summed over all
\sigma
such that
(x,\sigma)\in X
and averaged over all
x
,
the value obtained can be at most the overall pass probability of the test.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(x,\sigma,C_{1},y,\tau,C_{2})
be a tuple chosen from
\mathcal{D}
with
(C_{1},C_{2})\in S
. Then,

\mathcal{D}(x,\sigma,C_{1},y,\tau,C_{2})=\mathcal{D}(y,\tau,C_{2},x,\sigma,C_{1})=\mathcal{D}(x,\sigma,C_{2},y,\tau,C_{1})=\mathcal{D}(y,\tau,C_{1},x,\sigma,C_{2}).

Proof.

Since
(C_{1},C_{2})\in S
, the table entries
T(C_{1})
and
T(C_{2})
must agree on
x
and
y
. Thus,
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}
and
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}
. Now suppose
\mathcal{D}
is generated by first choosing cubes
C_{1},C_{2}
uniformly at random conditioned on
\dim(C_{1}\cap C_{2})=2
. Clearly the probability of choosing
(C_{1},C_{2})
or
(C_{2},C_{1})
is equal. Once the plane
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
is fixed, the pair
(x,y)
is chosen uniformly at random over distinct ordered pairs of points in
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
, while
\sigma
and
\tau
are set to
T(C_{1})(x)
and
T(C_{2})(y)
respectively. Again, it is clear that the pairs
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
have the same probability of being chosen. The result of the lemma then follows.
∎

By Lemma
3.9
, the bound in Lemma
3.8
still holds when either (or both) of
(x,\sigma)
or
C_{1}
are replaced by
(y,\tau)
or
C_{2}
respectively. Thus, letting
E
be the event

E=(C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma})\lor(C_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma})\lor(C_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{y,\tau})\lor(C_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{y,\tau}),

it follows that

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land(x,\sigma)\in X\land(y,\tau)\in X\land E\right]\leqslant 28\gamma\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(5)

We are now ready to prove Lemma
3.6
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Let
H
denote the event that both
(x,\sigma)
and
(y,\tau)
are in
X
. By (
4
) and (
3
) we get that

\varepsilon\leqslant\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\right]=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\right]+\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land\bar{H}\right]\leqslant\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\right]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

so
\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
If
H
holds, then we can write
\mathcal{C}_{x,\sigma}=\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{x,\sigma}
and
\mathcal{C}_{y,\tau}=\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}\sqcup\mathcal{D}_{y,\tau}
. Let
H^{\prime}
be the event that
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}
, then

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leqslant\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\right]=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\land H^{\prime}\right]+\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\land E\right],

and by (
5
) we have
\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\land E\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
and so

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S\land H\land H^{\prime}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
.
∎

3.3
Bumps in measure are negligible

Throughout this section, let
\mathcal{F}_{x}
be an arbitrary set of cubes containing the point
x
and suppose
\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})=\eta
(in our setting,
\mathcal{F}_{x}
will be

\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}
for some
(x,\sigma)\in X
). For
c>1
, let
Y_{c}=\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\;|\;\mu_{x,y}(\mathcal{F}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y})\geqslant c\eta\}
,
i.e. the set of points
y
such that
\mathcal{F}_{x}
is significantly denser in
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
. The goal of this section is to show that the mass of
Y_{c}
is small, namely:

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
Y_{c}=\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus{x}\;|\;\mu_{x,y}(\mathcal{F}_{x}\cap\mathcal{C}_{y})>c\eta\}
, for
c>1
. Then,
\mu(Y_{c})\leqslant\frac{4}{(c-1)^{2}\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})q^{2}}
.

Towards the proof of Lemma
3.10
, consider the distribution
\nu_{x}
over
\mathcal{C}_{x}
generated by choosing a random
y\in Y_{c}
and then a random cube
C

containing
x
and
y
. Note that
\nu_{x}(C)=\frac{z(C)}{|Y_{c}||\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|}
, where
z(C)=|C\cap Y_{c}|
.

Lemma 3.11
.

We have:

1.

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[z(C)]=\mu(Y_{c})(q^{3}-1)
.

2.

\var[z(C)]\leqslant 2\mu(Y_{c})q^{4}
.

Proof.

The first item is clear using linearity of expectation, as the probability that any fixed point
y\neq x
from
Y_{c}
is contained in a random cube containing
x
is
\frac{q^{3}-1}{|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}|}
.

For the second item, we write

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{C}[z(C)^{2}]=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in Y_{c}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{C}[\mathbbm{1}_{y_{1}\in C}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{2}\in C}].

If
x,y_{1},y_{2}
are contained in a line, then the expectation in the sum is
|Y_{c}|\frac{q^{3}-1}{q^{n}}
. This can happen for at most
|Y_{c}|q
pairs
y_{1},y_{2}
. Otherwise the expectation in the sum is
|Y_{c}|^{2}\frac{(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)}{q^{n}(q^{n}-q)}
. Overall this yields

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{C}[z(C)^{2}]\leqslant|Y_{c}|q\frac{q^{3}-1}{q^{n}}+|Y_{c}|^{2}\frac{(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)}{q^{n}(q^{n}-q)}\leqslant\mu(Y_{c})q^{4}+\mu(Y_{c})^{2}(q^{3}-1)^{2}\frac{q^{n}}{q^{n}-q}.

Since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[z(C)]=\mu(Y_{c})(q^{3}-1)
, the second term on the right hand side essentially cancels out and we can crudely bound the variance by

\var[z(C)]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[z(C)^{2}]-(\mu(Y_{c})(q^{3}-1))^{2}\leqslant\mu(Y_{c})q^{4}+\mu(Y_{c})^{2}(q^{3}-1)^{2}\frac{q}{q^{n}-q}\leqslant 2\mu(Y_{c})q^{4}.\qed

Proof of Lemma
3.10
.

We have

|\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})-\nu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})|^{2}\leqslant\left(\sum_{C\in\mathcal{F}_{x}}|\mu_{x}(C)-\nu_{x}(C)|\right)^{2}\leqslant|\mathcal{F}_{x}|\sum_{C\in\mathcal{F}_{x}}(\mu_{x}(C)-\nu_{x}(C))^{2},

where the first inequality is due to the triangle inequality, and the second inequality is Cauchy-Schwartz. Fix some
y\neq x
, and recall that
\nu_{x}(C)=\frac{z(C)}{|Y_{c}||\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|}

and
\mu_{x}(C)=\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{C}[z(C)]}{|Y_{c}||\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|}=\frac{1}{|C_{x}|}
, so

(\mu_{x}(C)-\nu_{x}(C))^{2}=\frac{(z(C)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[z(C)])^{2}}{|Y_{c}|^{2}|\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|^{2}}.

Therefore we may bound
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{F}_{x}}(\mu_{x}(C)-\nu_{x}(C))^{2}\leqslant\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x}}(\mu_{x}(C)-\nu_{x}(C))^{2}\leqslant\frac{\var[z(C)]|\mathcal{C}_{x}|}{|Y_{c}|^{2}|\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|^{2}}
, which with the previous inequality and Lemma
3.11
implies that

|\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})-\nu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})|^{2}\leqslant 2\frac{|Y_{c}||\mathcal{F}_{x}||\mathcal{C}_{x}|q^{4}}{|Y_{c}|^{2}|\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|^{2}q^{n}}\leqslant\frac{4\eta q^{n}}{|Y_{c}|q^{2}}=\frac{4\eta}{\mu(Y_{c})q^{2}}.

where we use the facts that
|\mathcal{C}_{x}|/|\mathcal{C}_{x,y}|\leqslant\frac{q^{3n}q^{3}}{q^{2n}q^{3}(q^{3}-1)}\leqslant\frac{q^{n}}{q^{3}-1}
and
|\mathcal{F}_{x}|=\eta|\mathcal{C}_{x}|
. By definition,
\nu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x})\geqslant c\eta
, so
the left hand side is least
(c-1)^{2}\eta^{2}
, and combining this with the above inequality yields
\mu(Y_{c})\leqslant\frac{4}{(c-1)^{2}\eta q^{2}}
.
∎

3.4
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we use the results of Sections
3.2
and
3.3
to prove Theorem
1.2
.
Namely, we find a degree
d
polynomial that agrees with an
\Omega(\varepsilon)
-fraction of the entries in
T
.

We start by rewriting the probability in Lemma
3.6
as an expectation over random points
x
and
y
. This can be seen as generating
\mathcal{D}
by first choosing
x
and
y
, and then choosing
(C_{1},C_{2})
as two random cubes in
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
that intersect in a plane, and setting
\sigma=T(C_{1})(x)

and
\tau=T(C_{2})(y)
. Lemma
3.6
then states

\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(C_{1},C_{2})\in S,\;C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\left(1-\Phi_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\right)\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Applying Lemma
2.1
we get

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{q}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(6)

At this point, we would like to restrict to
{\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
such that
\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)
is not significantly larger than
\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})
and
\mu_{y}(\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau})
. Let
H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)
denote the indicator for the event that
\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)>2\mu_{x}(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})
.

Lemma 3.12
.

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}(H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)+H_{y,\tau}(x,\sigma))\right]\leqslant\frac{400\log(5/\varepsilon)}{q^{2}}.

Proof.

We bound

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma)\in X}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\sum_{(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)\right]\right].

Fix an
(x,\sigma)\in X
and denote
\eta=\mu_{x}(F_{x,\sigma})
. Let
A_{y}(j)
denote the indicator of the event that
2^{j}\eta\leqslant\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{x,\sigma,y}\right)<2^{j+1}\eta
. Since all cubes in
F_{x,\sigma}
agree with a function
g_{x,\sigma}
, for each
y
there can only be one
\tau
, namely
\tau=g_{x,\sigma}(y)
, such that
\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)>0
. Thus, denoting
{\mathcal{F}}_{x,\sigma,y}={\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}
for this
\tau
, for all
(x,\sigma)\in X
we have:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\sum_{(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)\right]\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\mu_{x,y}({\mathcal{F}}_{x,\sigma,y})^{2}H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)\right]

We start by performing dyadic partitioning of the inner expectation with
(x,\sigma)
fixed.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\mu_{x,y}({\mathcal{F}}_{x,\sigma,y})^{2}H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)\right]\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\log(1/\eta)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}[A_{y}(j)\mu_{x,y}({\mathcal{F}}_{x,\sigma,y})^{2}]\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\log(1/\eta)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}[A_{y}(j)(2^{j+1}\eta)^{2}].

Using Lemma
3.10
gives

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}[A_{y}(j)(2^{j+1}\eta)^{2}]\leqslant(2^{j+1}\eta)^{2}\mu(Y_{2^{j}})\leqslant\frac{100\eta}{q^{2}}.

Combining everything, we get that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)\right]\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma)\in X}\log(1/\eta)\frac{100\mu(\mathcal{F}_{x,\sigma})}{q^{2}}\right]\leqslant\frac{200\log(5/\varepsilon)}{q^{2}}.

Similarly,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}H_{y,\tau}(x,\sigma)\right]\leqslant\frac{200\log(5/\varepsilon)}{q^{2}}
,
and the proof is concluded.
∎

Let
X^{\prime}(x,y)=\{(\sigma,\tau)\;|\;(x,\sigma),(y,\tau)\in X,\;H_{x,\sigma}(y,\tau)+H_{y,\tau}(x,\sigma)=0\}
.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.12
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{400\log(5/\varepsilon)}{q^{2}}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{5}.

Also,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10}}\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10},

and so

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,y}\left[\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)>\frac{\varepsilon}{10}}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10}.

Therefore, there is some
x
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)>\frac{\varepsilon}{10}}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{10},

and we fix such
x
henceforth.
For this point
x
, there are at most
5/\varepsilon
values
\sigma
such that
(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)
for some pair
(y,\tau)
. Consequently for this
x
, there is a value
\sigma
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\sum_{\tau:(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)>\frac{\varepsilon}{10}}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{50}.

Let
\eta=\mu_{x}(F_{x,\sigma})
. For each
y
, note that only
\tau=g_{x,\sigma}(y)
such that
(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)
can contribute to the above sum, and the corresponding summand

\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)
is between
\varepsilon/10
and
2\eta
, and so there is some
t\in[\varepsilon/10,2\eta)
such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y}\left[\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\in[t,2t]}\mathbbm{1}_{(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)}\right]\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{50\log(10/\varepsilon)}.

Let
Y
be the set consisting of
x
and all
y
’s such that
\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\in[t,2t]
and
(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)
for
\tau=g_{x,\sigma}(y)
;
writing
p_{t}=\Pr_{y}\left[y\in Y\right]
, we get that

t^{2}p_{t}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{200\log(10/\varepsilon)}
.

For
y\in Y
, let
\tau=g_{x,\sigma}(y)
and denote
\mathcal{F}_{y}=\mathcal{F}_{y,\tau}
and
\mathcal{F}=\cup_{y\in Y}\mathcal{F}_{y}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

For all
y\in Y
we have that
g_{x,\sigma}\equiv g_{y,\tau}
.

Proof.

Assume towards contradiction that
g_{x,\sigma}\not\equiv g_{y,\tau}
.
We choose a cube in
C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
uniformly at random and bound the probability that
g_{x,\sigma}|_{C}=g_{y,\tau}|_{C}
. Note that any cube in
C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
is of the form
x+\spa(y-x,z_{1},z_{2})
, so we may choose a cube in
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}
uniformly at random by choosing
z_{1},z_{2}
, such that
z_{1}\notin x+\spa(y-x)
,
z_{2}\notin x+\spa(y-x,z_{1})
. By the Schwartz-Zippel Lemma, the probability that
g_{x,\sigma}
and
g_{y,\tau}
are equal on both
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
is at most
(\frac{2d}{q})^{2}=\frac{4d^{2}}{q^{2}}
. The factors of
2
account for the fact that
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are not uniform over all possible points. Thus,

\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\leqslant\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)\leqslant\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{x,y}}\left[g_{x,\sigma}|_{C}=g_{y,\tau}|_{C}\right]\leqslant\frac{4d^{2}}{q^{2}},

and contradiction.
∎

Thus, denoting
g=g_{x,\sigma}
we get that
g|_{C}=T(C)
for all
C\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. We finish the proof by
showing that
\mu(\mathcal{F})\geqslant\Omega(\varepsilon)
. Towards this end, consider the bipartite inclusion graph
G_{5}=G(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m},\mathcal{C})
, which has second singular value at most
q^{-3/2}
by Lemma
2.3
. By Lemma
2.2
,

\left|\Pr_{y\in Y,C\ni y}\left[C\in\mathcal{F}\right]-\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C},y\in C}\left[C\in\mathcal{F}\right]\right|\leqslant\frac{q^{-3/2}}{\sqrt{\mu(Y)}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{3}p_{t}}}\leqslant t\frac{\sqrt{200\log(10/\varepsilon)}}{\sqrt{q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}}},

where we used
p_{t}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{200t^{2}\log(10/\varepsilon)}
. Since for each
y\in Y
we have
(\sigma,\tau)\in X^{\prime}(x,y)
for
\tau=g_{x,\sigma}(y)
, we have

\mu_{y}(\mathcal{F}_{y})\geqslant\frac{\mu_{x,y}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\sigma\\
y,\tau\end{subarray}}\right)}{2}\geqslant\frac{t}{2}.

Therefore,
\Pr_{y\in Y,C\ni y}\left[C\in\mathcal{F}\right]\geqslant\frac{t}{2}
, so we get

\mu(\mathcal{F})=\Pr_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\left[C\in\mathcal{F}\right]\geqslant\frac{t}{2}-t\frac{\sqrt{200\log(10/\varepsilon)}}{\sqrt{q^{3}\varepsilon^{2}}}\geqslant\frac{t}{3},

where we used the fact that
\varepsilon\geqslant\frac{10^{7}d^{6}}{q}
. Since
t\geqslant\varepsilon/10
, the proof is concluded.
