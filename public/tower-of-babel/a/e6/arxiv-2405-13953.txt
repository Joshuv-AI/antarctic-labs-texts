1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background on the Allen–Cahn and abelian Higgs models

Area of geometric shapes is one of the oldest geometric functional considered in mathematics.
Given an ambient Riemannian manifold

(M^{n},g)
(possibly the flat Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and given an integer
1\leq k\leq n-1
,
one looks for
k
-dimensional objects, such as
k
-dimensional submanifolds or singular versions of them,
which are
critical points
for the
k
-area
\mathcal{H}^{k}
.
These are called
minimal submanifolds
(provided they are regular enough, depending on the context).

Besides its intrinsic interest, the study of minimal submanifolds
in a given ambient often reveals global topological structure, especially when coupled with curvature information.

These applications motivate a systematic existence and regularity theory of such critical points.
In spite of its apparent simplicity, it is notoriously difficult to use the area functional directly in the context of the
calculus of variations
, especially when
k\geq 2
.
Leaving out a number of very important ways to deal with this problem,
such as the approach via parametrizations when
k=2
, see, e.g.,
[
23
,
45
,
47
,
46
]
among others,
area-minimizing
currents
and
sets of finite perimeter
in the context of minimization,
[
17
,
25
]
and the monographs
[
26
,
50
]
, and the Almgren–Pitts theory involving
varifolds
,
[
2
,
44
]
. In this paper we focus on the approximation of minimal surfaces as limit of
diffuse
physical energies.

Starting from the pioneering ideas of De Giorgi, Modica
[
39
]
, Ilmanen
[
34
]
, and
Hutchinson–Tonegawa
[
33
]
,
it was understood that smooth critical points
u:M\to\mathbb{R}
for
the
Allen–Cahn energy

E_{\varepsilon}(u):=\int_{M}\left[\varepsilon|du|^{2}+\frac{(1-u^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\right]

are effective diffuse approximations of minimal hypersurfaces.
The Allen–Cahn functional is a well studied model for phase transitions; a typical critical point
u
takes values in
[-1,1]
, with
u\approx\pm 1
(the pure phases) except in a transition region of thickness
\approx\varepsilon
, where most of the energy concentrates. Roughly speaking, this region is an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of a minimal hypersurface, which acts as an interface between the two phases, and the energy density decays exponentially fast away from this interface.

This understanding brought a novel, PDE-based
way to attack variational problems for the co-dimension-one area
[
29
]
,
which often allows to obtain more refined results compared to other methods
[
14
]
.

In co-dimension two, similar attempts have been made by looking at the same energy for maps
u:M\to\mathbb{C}
, replacing
u
with
|u|
in the second term. This corresponds to a simplified version of the Ginzburg–Landau model of superconductivity,
popularized by Bethuel–Brezis–Hélein
[
9
]
, where one neglects the magnetic field. The asymptotic analysis of this energy is substantially more involved, due to the lack of the aforementioned exponential decay, and brought mixed results:
see, for instance,
[
38
,
8
]
in the positive direction
and
[
43
]
in the negative one.

On the other hand, including the magnetic field and looking at the so-called
self-dual regime
(also called
critical coupling
),
we can consider the alternative energy

E_{\varepsilon}(u,\alpha):=\int_{M}\left[|du-i\alpha u|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha|^{2}\right].

Apart from the different normalization, it differs from the previous energies by an additional variable, the one-form
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(M;\mathbb{R})
, which twists the Dirichlet term and appears in the Yang–Mills term
|d\alpha|^{2}
(indeed,
the latter equals
|F_{\nabla}|^{2}
, where
F_{\nabla}
is the curvature of the connection
\nabla:=d-i\alpha
on the trivial complex line bundle
\mathbb{C}\times M
).

This energy, in this specific self-dual regime (i.e., the choice of constants in front of each term), is well known in gauge theory, where it is often called
U(1)
-Yang–Mills–Higgs
,
or simply
abelian Higgs model
.
It received a thorough treatment in dimension
2
, with a complete classification of critical planar pairs
(u,\nabla)
of finite energy by Taubes
[
51
,
52
]
. See also
[
32
]
for the case of Riemann surface and
[
12
]
for Kähler manifolds.
Recently, in
[
42
]
, Stern and the third-named author developed the asymptotic analysis in arbitrary Riemannian manifolds, obtaining the precise co-dimension-two analogue of the result by Hutchinson–Tonegawa: see
Theorem

4.1
below. Related facts, including
\Gamma
-convergence and the gradient flow convergence to mean curvature flow, have also been verified, by Parise, Stern, and the third-named author
[
41
,
40
]
.

Based on some new functional inequalities
[
30
]
, the second-named author recently obtained
a quantitative refinement of the work of Taubes, who showed (among other facts)
that critical pairs on the plane minimize the energy among pairs with the same degree at infinity:
namely, in
[
31
]
a quantitative
stability
is proved; the precise statement is recalled in

Theorem

4.7
. Together with the main result from
[
42
]
, this result will be instrumental for the analysis in the present paper.

1.2.
Savin’s theorem

Since the work of De Giorgi
[
18
]

and Allard
[
2
]
, it is known that almost-flat
minimal submanifolds enjoy an
improvement of flatness
,
i.e., they become even closer to a plane at smaller scales, in a quantitative way. Iteration of this improvement of flatness is the key mechanism in proving (quantitative) regularity of minimal surfaces. The key analytical fact behind this decay property is the observation that the linearization of the minimal graph equation is the Laplace equation, whose solutions enjoy similar decay properties.

A related question, in the spirit of the classical Liouville theorem, is whether globally defined objects should be planar. The famous
Bernstein’s conjecture
predicts that this is always true for minimal graphs
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, which are automatically (locally) area-minimizing hypersurfaces.
In view of the improvement of flatness, this question quickly reduces to understanding whether any blow-down is necessarily a hyperplane. Bernstein’s question was answered affirmatively
by the works of Fleming, De Giorgi, Almgren, and Simons for
n\leq 8
, while Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti produced a counterexample for
n=9
, whose blow-down corresponds to the Simons cone, in
[
11
]
.

By analogy, De Giorgi conjectured that
critical points
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of the Allen–Cahn energy
with
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}>0
(so that level sets are graphs)
are just rotations of a one-dimensional solution
u=u(x_{n})
, at least when
n\leq 8
.
The question has been solved by Ghoussoub–Gui for
n=2
, in
[
28
]
, by Ambrosio–Cabré for
n=3
, in
[
4
]
, and by Barlow–Bass–Gui under additional regularity for the level sets, in
[
6
]
. Finally, in
[
49
]
Savin settled the conjecture for all
n\leq 8
under the assumption that
u(x^{\prime},x_{n})\to\pm 1
as
x_{n}\to\pm\infty
, for any fixed
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. In fact, his main contribution could be phrased as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Savin’s theorem)
.

A local minimizer
u
for Allen–Cahn enjoys improvement of flatness. In particular, if any blow-down is a hyperplane,
then the blow-down is unique.

Here the blow-downs can be understood in terms of energy concentration, or by looking at the blow-downs of the zero set
\{u=0\}
with respect to the (local) Hausdorff convergence of sets.

The previous statement implies the resolution of De Giorgi’s conjecture for
n\leq 8
, with the extra assumption mentioned above.
Indeed, it is known that this condition, together with
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}>0
, implies that
u
is a local minimizer; moreover, any blow-down gives a vertical area-minimizing cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, hence an area-minimizing cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, which is known to be necessarily a hyperplane for
n\leq 8
.

In other words, uniqueness of the blow-down relies on two ingredients: improvement of flatness and a classification of blow-downs. While the second one can be directly exported from the setting of minimal hypersurfaces, the first one needs to be proved
before
passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, and this is the difficult part settled by Savin.

Finally, using the maximum principle (see, e.g.,
[
24
,
7
]
), one can deduce the following.

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the previous assumptions,
u
is one-dimensional.

As for minimal graphs, De Giorgi’s conjecture (even with the extra assumption used by Savin) is false for
n\geq 9
:
a counterexample has been constructed by Del Pino–Kowalczyk–Wei, in
[
22
]
.

Savin’s approach uses viscosity techniques, resembling the Krylov–Safanov theory in spirit. In particular,
while his groundbreaking methods have a wide range of applicability, even beyond variational equations, it is not always clear how one can extend these techniques to the vectorial setting, where the maximum principle does not apply; see however
[
48
,
21
]
.

Recently, Wang
[
54
]
obtained a variational proof of Savin’s theorem, following the strategy of Allard’s proof of excess decay for stationary varifolds. Wang’s paper has been the starting point for our investigation of the regularity properties of the zero set of solutions of the Yang–Mills–Higgs equations.

1.3.
Main results

We consider the energy

E_{\varepsilon}:=\int e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),\quad e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla):=|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}|F_{\nabla}|^{2}.

Note that
E_{\varepsilon}
is just a rescaling of
E_{1}
, for
\varepsilon>0
.
The main result of the paper could be summarized as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Savin’s result, as stated in
Theorem

1.1
,
holds for critical pairs
(u,\nabla)
for
E_{1}
, in any dimension
n\geq 2
.

The following is the precise statement of the excess decay for critical points.

Theorem 1.4

(Tilt-excess decay)
.

For any
n\geq 3
and small enough
0<\rho\leq\rho_{0}(n)
, there exist constants
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\rho),\tau_{0}(n,\rho)
such that the following holds. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point for
E_{\varepsilon}
on the unit ball
B_{1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
u(0)=0
, and the energy bound

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{1}|}\int_{B^{n}_{1}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}.

Then at least one of the following statements is true:
either

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho}^{n},\overline{S})\leq C\rho^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S),

for some
(n-2)
-plane
\overline{S}
with
\|P_{\overline{S}}-P_{S}\|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)}
, where
P_{S}
is the orthogonal projection onto
S
, the plane minimizing
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho}^{n},\cdot)
, or

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)\leq\max\{C\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}},e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}\},

where
\bm{\mathrm{E}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)
and
C=C(n)
,
K=K(n)
are independent of
\rho
.

Remark 1.5
.

To be precise, we assume also the pointwise bounds
Eq.

4.1
–
Eq.

4.2
, which are automatically true if
(u,\nabla)
is a critical pair on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with energy growth
O(R^{n-2})
on
B_{R}^{n}
.

Here
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is the
excess
, defined in
Eq.

5.1
below, which naturally splits into two parts,
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
, measuring how far a solution is from being two-dimensional and from solving the first order
vortex equations
, respectively. We also note that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
parallels the notion of excess in the theory of varifolds and does not depend on the orientation, while
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
sees the orientation and should be thought of as the stronger notion of excess in the setting of currents. While in principle the previous result establishes a quantitative decay only for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, it is enough to obtain the following.

Corollary 1.6
.

If
(u,\nabla)
is an entire critical point on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with

0<\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{R}|}\int_{B^{n}_{R}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}(n),

then this limit is
2\pi
and the blow-down is a unique plane.

The previous limit always exists by the monotonicity formula for
E_{\varepsilon}
, see
[
42
]
. By a simple compactness argument and Allard’s theorem, it is easy to see that the assumption guarantees that any blow-down is an
(n-2)
-dimensional plane. The key assertion is that, in view of improvement of flatness, the blow-down is
unique
.

Another simple consequence of the techniques is the following fact, a diffuse version of the
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of minimal graphs.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point for
E_{\varepsilon}
as above.
Given
\alpha\in[0,1)
and
\gamma>0
, if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}(n,\alpha,\gamma)
and
\tau_{0}\leq\tau_{0}(n,\alpha,\gamma)
then the vorticity set
\{|u|\leq\frac{3}{4}\}\cap B_{1/2}^{n}
is contained in a
C(n,\alpha,\gamma)\varepsilon^{1/(1+\alpha)}
-neighborhood of the graph of a function

f:B_{1}^{n-2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

with
\|f\|_{C^{1,\alpha}}\leq\gamma
.

Differently from the co-dimension one setting,
where uniqueness of the blow-down (with multiplicity one)
implies via the maximum principle that
u
is one-dimensional, at the present time we are not able to conclude that, in the setting of
Corollary

1.6
,
the solution
u
is two-dimensional. Here we formulate the following variant of the
Gibbons conjecture
.

Conjecture 1.8
.

An entire critical point
(u,\nabla)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{R}|}\int_{B^{n}_{R}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)=2\pi

and, writing any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
x=(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, also

\lim_{|y|\to\infty}|u(y,z)|=1,\quad\text{uniformly in $z$},

is necessarily two-dimensional, i.e., it is the pullback through the projection
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the standard solution in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with degree
\pm 1
, up to translation and change of gauge.

It is interesting to note that, if we allow for multiplicity higher than one in the blow-down,
this conjecture (with the appropriate energy assumption) does
not
hold for the non-magnetic Ginzburg–Landau energy mentioned before, see
[
16
]
. It is not clear if such rigidity with higher multiplicity should be expected for the energy considered in the present work.
On the other hand, our excess decay is strong enough to give an affirmative answer up to dimension
4
. With a more involved argument, we are able to settle it also for local minimizers in all dimensions
n\geq 2
, thus obtaining a full analogue of Savin’s theorem.

Theorem 1.9
.

The previous conjecture holds for critical points in dimension
2\leq n\leq 4
, as well as for local minimizers in all dimensions
n\geq 2
,
even without the second assumption that
\lim_{|y|\to\infty}|u(y,z)|=1
uniformly in
z
: the pair
(u,\nabla)
is two-dimensional, up to rotation and change of gauge.

The techniques used in this paper resemble those of
[
54
]
at several places.
However, there are several key differences which require substantially new ideas. For instance, in order to construct the Lipschitz approximation, Wang uses a generic level set of
u
.
The fact that typical level sets share effectively properties of minimal hypersurfaces is often used in
[
54
]
, as well as in
[
34
,
53
,
14
]
and many other works in the Allen–Cahn setting.
For the abelian Higgs model, level sets of
u
can be arbitrarily irregular, due to gauge invariance; while we can always pass to a local Coulomb gauge, we do not expect such effective properties of typical preimages of
u
.

Rather, in the present setting, we rely on the results from
[
31
]
in order to control in a fine way the behavior of
u
on many (but not all) two-dimensional slices perpendicular to the reference plane. For instance, we are able to bound the distance of the actual zero set from a certain function giving the “center of mass” of each slice, which is used as a Lipschitz approximation and allows to derive a Caccioppoli-type inequality.

In the case of minimizers, this refined control also allows us to deform a nearly flat minimizing pair
(u,\nabla)
in the interior to gain a
stronger
decay of the excess. This deformation process also requires a very involved gauge fixing argument, since generically
(u,\nabla)
could be very irregular in an arbitrary gauge. The following theorem is the precise statement of the improved tilt-excess decay that we obtain for minimizers. Note that in the statement below,
\beta
can indeed be any power. This is fundamental for proving
Theorem

1.9
for minimizers, where we need to take
\beta=n-2
.

Theorem 1.10
.

For any
\beta>0
and small enough
0<\rho\leq\rho_{0}(n,\beta)
there exist
\tau_{0}(n,\beta,\rho)>0
,
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\beta,\rho)>0
with the following property. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a local minimizer of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
B^{n}_{1}
with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
u(0)=0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{1}|}\int_{B^{n}_{1}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0},

and let
S
minimize
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)
.
Then, after a suitable rotation, at least one of the following statements is true: either

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho}^{n},\overline{S})\leq C\rho^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S),

for some new oriented
(n-2)
-plane
\overline{S}
with
\|P_{\overline{S}}-P_{S}\|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}
, or

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B^{n}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\varepsilon^{\beta},

where
C=C(n,\beta)
is independent of
\rho
.

Then, by taking
\beta\geq n-2
, we obtain a direct proof of
Theorem

1.9
in the case of minimizers.

2.
Basic definitions

While we work on the trivial Hermitian line bundle over the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is worth to recall the definition of Hermitian line bundle over a general manifold.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Hermitian line bundle
over a smooth manifold
M
is a complex line bundle
L\rightarrow M
(i.e., a complex vector bundle with typical fiber
\mathbb{C}
) equipped with a
Hermitian metric
, whose real part will be denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
;
thus, for any two smooth sections
s,t\in\Gamma(L)
,
the function
p\mapsto\langle s(p),t(p)\rangle
is smooth and real-valued, and satisfies
\langle is(p),it(p)\rangle=\langle s(p),t(p)\rangle=\langle t(p),s(p)\rangle
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
metric connection
is a map
\nabla
which assigns to each vector field
\xi\in\Gamma(TM)
an endomorphism
\nabla_{\xi}:\Gamma(L)\rightarrow\Gamma(L)
with the following properties:

(i)

\nabla_{\xi+\eta}s=\nabla_{\xi}s+\nabla_{\eta}s
;

(ii)

\nabla_{\phi\xi}s=\phi\nabla_{\xi}s
;

(iii)

\nabla_{\xi}(\phi s)=(\xi\phi)s+\phi\nabla_{\xi}s
;

(iv)

\xi(\langle s,t\rangle)=\langle\nabla_{\xi}s,t\rangle+\langle s,\nabla_{\xi}t\rangle
,

for any sections
s,t\in\Gamma(L)
, vector fields
\xi,\eta\in\Gamma(TM)
, and function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

On the trivial bundle
L=\mathbb{C}\times M
,
we can always write a metric connection
\nabla
as

\nabla=d-i\alpha,

for a real-valued one-form,
meaning that
\nabla_{\xi}s=ds(\xi)-i\alpha(\xi)s
.

In general, for two vector fields
\xi
and
\eta
, typically
\nabla_{\xi}
and
\nabla_{\eta}
do not commute, meaning that the connection has nontrivial
curvature
. Formally, the curvature
F_{\nabla}
is given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle F_{\nabla}(\xi,\eta)(s)=[\nabla_{\xi},\nabla_{\eta}]s-\nabla_{[\xi,\eta]}s.

A simple computation shows that
F_{\nabla}
is a two-form with values in the Lie algebra of
U(1)
, i.e., in imaginary numbers; we will sometimes use the real-valued two-form
\omega
given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle F_{\nabla}(\xi,\eta)(s)=:-i\omega(\xi,\eta)s.

On the trivial bundle, if
\nabla=d-i\alpha
then we simply have

\omega=d\alpha.

We will use the inner product on two-forms induced by the following quadratic form:

\displaystyle|\omega|^{2}=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}|\omega(e_{j},e_{k})|^{2},

where
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is a local orthonormal frame for
TM
.

3.
The
U(1)
-Yang–Mills–Higgs equations

For a section
u\in\Gamma(L)
and a (metric) connection
\nabla
on a Hermitian line bundle
L\rightarrow M
over a smooth Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, given a parameter
\varepsilon>0
, we define the
U(1)
-Yang–Mills–Higgs energy as

(3.1)

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla):=\int_{M}\left[|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|F_{\nabla}|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\right],

where
F_{\nabla}
is the curvature of
\nabla
and
|F_{\nabla}|
is defined to be
|\omega|
(with
\omega
as in
Eq.

2.2
). Equivalently, on the trivial bundle, for any section
u
(viewed as a function
M\to\mathbb{C}
) and connection
\nabla=d-i\alpha
we have

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla=d-i\alpha)=\int_{M}\left[|du-iu\alpha|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\right].

A smooth pair
(u,\nabla)
gives a critical point for the Yang–Mills–Higgs energy if and only if it satisfies the system of partial differential equations:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\nabla^{*}\nabla u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}(1-|u|^{2})u,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}d^{*}\omega

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla u,iu\rangle,

where
\nabla^{*}
is the adjoint of
\nabla
, while
d^{*}
is the adjoint of
d:\Omega^{1}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{2}(M)
, given by

\displaystyle(d^{*}\omega)(e_{k})=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\nabla_{e_{j}}\omega)(e_{j},e_{k})

for some (and hence any) orthonormal frame
\{e_{j}\}
.

We now recall some Bochner-type identities from
[
42
, Sections 2–3]
.
Since
\omega
is a closed two-form, after taking the exterior derivative in
Eq.

3.3
we get

(3.4)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\Delta_{H}\omega+|u|^{2}\omega=\psi(u),

where
\Delta_{H}=dd^{*}+d^{*}d
is the Hodge Laplacian and

(3.5)

\displaystyle\psi(u)(e_{j},e_{k}):=2\langle i\nabla_{e_{j}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle.

One easily sees that the modulus
|u|^{2}
satisfies the equation

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Delta\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}=|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}(1-|u|^{2}).

We also recall the following Bochner identity for
|\nabla u|^{2}
:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Delta\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}(3|u|^{2}-1)|\nabla u|^{2}-2\langle\omega,\psi(u)\rangle+\mathcal{R}_{1}(\nabla u,\nabla u),

where
\mathcal{R}_{1}=\operatorname{Ric}(e_{j},e_{k})\langle\nabla_{e_{j}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle
and
\nabla^{2}_{e_{j},e_{k}}u=\nabla_{e_{j}}(\nabla_{e_{k}}u)
.

Next, we define the gauge-invariant Jacobian, which plays an important role
in the
\Gamma
-convergence theory
[
41
]
, similar to the classical Jacobian in the
\Gamma
-convergence for the Ginzburg–Landau energy with no magnetic field, see
[
1
,
8
,
37
]
. It is the two-form given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle J(u,\nabla):=\psi(u)+(1-|u|^{2})\omega.

We have the trivial pointwise bound

(3.9)

\displaystyle|J(u,\nabla)|\leq e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),

where
e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)
is the integrand in
Eq.

3.1
.

We define
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
to be the dual current to the Jacobian, formally identified by the duality formula

(3.10)

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\xi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{M}J(u,\nabla)\wedge\xi,

for any
(n-2)
-form
\xi\in\Omega^{n-2}(M)
. Note that by
Eq.

3.4
the Jacobian can be written as

\displaystyle J(u,\nabla)=\omega+\varepsilon^{2}\Delta_{H}\omega.

In particular, this shows that the gauge-invariant Jacobian is a closed two-form. This, in duality, implies that
\partial\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=0
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is an
(n-2)
-dimensional cycle.

4.
Preliminary estimates

4.1.
The energy concentration set

It was proved by the third-named author and Stern in
[
42
]
that, for a sequence
(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})
with
\varepsilon\to 0
, one can extract a subsequence such that the energy density converges to (the weight of) a stationary integer-rectifiable
(n-2)
-varifold. We restate the main result of
[
42
]
in the following theorem, see
[
42
, eq. (6.35)]
for the conclusion on the Jacobian.

Theorem 4.1

(The varifold limit)
.

Let
L\rightarrow M
be a Hermitian line bundle over a closed, oriented Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
of dimension
n\geq 2
and let
(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})
be a family of critical points of
E_{\varepsilon}
, satisfying the uniform energy bound

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\leq\Lambda<\infty.

Then, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the energy measures

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2\pi}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{vol}_{g},

converge subsequentially, in duality with
C^{0}(M)
, to a measure
\mu
which is the weight of a stationary integral
(n-2)
-varifold
V
. Also, for all
0\leq\delta<1
,

\displaystyle\spt(V)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\{|u_{\varepsilon}|\leq\delta\},

in the Hausdorff topology. The
(n-2)
-currents dual to the curvature forms
\frac{1}{2\pi}\omega_{\varepsilon}
and Jacobians
\frac{1}{2\pi}J(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})
converge subsequentially to the same limit, an integral cycle
\Gamma
with
|\Gamma|\leq\mu
.

Remark 4.2
.

The previous result admits a local version, proved in the same way (assuming the bounds
Eq.

4.1
and
Eq.

4.2
below, which in the closed case follow from the maximum principle): assume that we have an increasing sequence of open sets
U_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a sequence of smooth pairs
(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})
, each defined on the trivial bundle
\mathbb{C}\times U_{\varepsilon}
and critical for
E_{\varepsilon}
; if we have

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{K}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})<\infty

for any compact subset
K\subset U:=\bigcup_{\varepsilon}U_{\varepsilon}
, as well as
Eq.

4.1
–
Eq.

4.2
,
then there exist a limiting varifold
V
and a limiting cycle
\Gamma
satisfying the same conclusions as above (up to a subsequence).

We will use the above theorem (in its local version) in several soft arguments by compactness and contradiction; in particular, we will use it to obtain information for any blow-down limit of an entire solution.

4.2.
Modica-type bounds and exponential decay

Actually,
[
42
]
contains some additional information which will be used frequently in the paper, including a Modica-type bound which was first proved in dimension two in
[
36
, Theorem III.8.1]
. We record the following propositions in the non-compact case of
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with the trivial bundle
L=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

A critical point
(u,\nabla)
for
E_{\varepsilon}
,
on the trivial bundle on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfies

(4.1)

|u|\leq 1

everywhere.

Proof.

The bound
|u|\leq 1+C(n)\varepsilon^{2}
on the unit ball
B_{1}(0)

can be shown as in
[
40
, Proposition A.2]
,
and the claim follows by scaling.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Modica-type bounds)
.

Assuming also that the energy on a ball
B_{R}
is
O(R^{n-2})
for
R
large enough, we have the pointwise bounds

(4.2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon|F_{\nabla}|\leq\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon},\quad|\nabla u|\leq\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as in
[
42
]
; however, in the Euclidean space, the Modica-type bound has no error terms. First, define
\xi_{\varepsilon}
to be the
discrepancy
:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\xi:=\varepsilon|F_{\nabla}|-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}.

Arguing as in
[
42
, Section 3]
, we see that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Delta\xi\geq\frac{|u|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\xi.

For the positive part
\xi^{+}
, this immediately implies that

\Delta\xi^{+}\geq 0

in the distributional sense, i.e.,
\xi
is subharmonic.
Under the energy growth assumption, we have

\int_{B_{R}(0)}|\xi|=O(R^{n-1}),

which gives
\xi^{+}\equiv 0
, as claimed.

For the second bound,
proceeding as in
[
42
, eqs. (5.5)–(5.6)]
,
we check that

\displaystyle w:=|\nabla u|-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{\varepsilon}

satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta w\geq\frac{|u|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}w+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(w+\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\left(2w+\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right).

Again, this implies that
w^{+}
is subharmonic,
and hence
w^{+}\equiv 0
.
∎

We also record the following exponential decay
of energy, which plays a key role in the paper.

Proposition 4.5

(Exponential decay away from the vorticity set)
.

There exist constants
K(n)>0
and
C(n)>0
such that, defining
Z:=\{|u|\leq\frac{3}{4}\}
and
r(p):=\operatorname{dist}(p,Z)
, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq C\frac{e^{-Kr(p)/\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Proof.

As in
[
42
, Corollary 5.2]
,
we compute that on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Z
we have

\Delta\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}.

Exponential decay now follows as in
[
42
, Proposition 5.3]
, using also the previous Modica-type bounds.
∎

4.3.
Inner variations and monotonicity

In this section we recall the inner variation formulas for critical points. With respect to any orthonormal basis
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
for
TM
, we define the
(0,2)
-tensors
\nabla u^{*}\nabla u
and
\omega^{*}\omega
by

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\nabla u^{*}\nabla u)(e_{j},e_{k})

\displaystyle:=\langle\nabla_{e_{j}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\omega^{*}\omega(e_{i},e_{j})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega(e_{i},e_{k})\omega(e_{j},e_{k}).

We define the
stress-energy tensor
to be

(4.8)

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla):=e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)-2\nabla u^{*}\nabla u-2\varepsilon^{2}\omega^{*}\omega.

Then, for any pair
(u,\nabla)
satisfying
Eq.

3.2
–
Eq.

3.3
, the inner variation formula then reads

(4.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla))=0,

meaning that, for any compactly supported vector field
X
,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),DX\rangle=0.

A core tool in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
is the
monotonicity formula
from
[
42
, Theorem 4.3]
, which is cleaner in the case of the trivial line bundle
L=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
over the flat Euclidean space
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We state this version of the theorem for convenience and give a short proof.

Proposition 4.6

(Monotonicity formula)
.

Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point for
E_{\varepsilon}
on the trivial line bundle
L=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the normalized energy

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(p,r):=r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(p)}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

satisfies

(4.11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(p,r)=2r^{1-n}\int_{B_{r}(p)}\left(\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}-\varepsilon^{2}|\omega|^{2}\right)+2r^{2-n}\int_{\partial B_{r}(p)}(|\nabla_{\nu}u|^{2}+|\iota_{\nu}\omega|^{2}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
p=0
. By approximation we can take
X(x)=\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{B_{r}(0)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}e_{k}
in
Eq.

4.10
, obtaining

\displaystyle r\int_{\partial B_{r}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}(n-2)e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)+2\int_{B_{r}}\left(\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}-\varepsilon^{2}|\omega|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2r\int_{\partial B_{r}}\left(|\nabla_{\nu}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|\iota_{\nu}\omega|^{2}\right).

Since

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\tilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(x,r)

\displaystyle=(2-n)r^{1-n}\int_{B_{r}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)+r^{2-n}\int_{\partial B_{r}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

\displaystyle=2r^{1-n}\int_{B_{r}}\left(\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}-\varepsilon^{2}|\omega|^{2}\right)+2r^{2-n}\int_{\partial B_{r}}(|\nabla_{\nu}u|^{2}+|\iota_{\nu}\omega|^{2}),

we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

4.4.
Quantitative stability in two dimensions and the vortex equations

In this section we record some results regarding the existence, uniqueness, and quantitative stability of critical points for
Eq.

3.1
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. First of all note that for
\varepsilon=1
the energy
E_{1}
of
any
pair
(u,\nabla)
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle E(u,\nabla)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left[|\nabla u|^{2}+|F_{\nabla}|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4}\right]

(4.12)

\displaystyle=2\pi|N|+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla_{1}u\pm i\nabla_{2}u|^{2}+\left|\star\omega\mp\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2}\right|^{2},

where
N
is the vortex number of
(u,\nabla)
, given by

\displaystyle N:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\star\omega.

Thus
(u,\nabla)
is a minimizer of the total energy among pairs with the same vortex number if and only if it satisfies the first-order system of
vortex equations
:

(4.13)

\displaystyle\nabla_{1}u\pm i\nabla_{2}u=0\text{ and }\star\omega=\pm\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}.

These are also called
Bogomol’nyi equations
(after
[
10
]
) or
self-dual equations
, and arise in many self-dual gauge theories. Taubes, in
[
51
]
, proved that we can prescribe the zero set
u^{-1}(0)=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
: given any finite collection of
k\geq 0
points, counted with multiplicity, there exists a solution
(u,\nabla)
to the vortex equations (with either choice of signs, corresponding to vortex number
N=k
and
N=-k
, respectively) with this prescribed zero set;
moreover, the solution is unique up to change of gauge.

In
[
31
]
the second-named author improved the previous results by proving a (sharp) quantitative stability for critical points of
E_{1}
. We record these results in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.7

(Uniqueness and stability in two dimensions)
.

On the trivial line bundle over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, any critical point
(u,\nabla)
of finite energy for
E_{1}
is actually a minimizer with
E_{1}(u,\nabla)=2\pi|N|
. Moreover, up to change of gauge, any minimizer is uniquely characterized by its zero set
u^{-1}(0)=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
(counted with multiplicity, according to the local degree of
u
around any zero) and orientation. Letting
\mathcal{F}
be the moduli space of all minimizers, the following quantitative stability estimates hold:

(4.14)

\displaystyle\inf_{(u_{0},\nabla_{0})\in\mathcal{F}}(\|u-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+\|F_{\nabla}-F_{\nabla_{0}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})})\leq C_{|N|}\left(E_{1}(u,\nabla)-2\pi|N|\right),

for some constant
C_{|N|}>0
and all pairs such that the discrepancy
E_{1}(u,\nabla)-2\pi|N|\leq\delta_{|N|}
is small enough.

Proof.

Existence and uniqueness were proved in
[
51
,
52
]
,
while quantitative stability was obtained in
[
31
]
.
∎

The proof of the above theorem uses weighted estimates developed in
[
30
]
. Vaguely,
Theorem

4.7
tells us that in the vanishing
\varepsilon
limit, two-dimensional slices perpendicular to the energy concentration set resemble minimizing vortex solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the special case of pairs
(u,\nabla)
with vortex number
\pm 1
, we also have the stability of the Jacobian and the energy density, given by the following proposition.

Proposition 4.8
.

Given a pair
(u,\nabla=d-i\alpha)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with vortex number
\pm 1
, obeying the bound

\|d|u|\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|u\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\Lambda,

if the discrepancy
E_{1}(u,\nabla)-2\pi
is small enough then

(4.15)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\inf_{(u_{0},\nabla_{0})\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\|J(u,\nabla)-J(u_{0},\nabla_{0})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|e_{1}(u,\nabla)-e_{1}(u_{0},\nabla_{0})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\right)\\
&\leq C(\Lambda)\sqrt{E_{1}(u,\nabla)-2\pi}.\end{aligned}

Proof.

See
[
31
, Appendix C]
.
∎

5.
Quantifying flatness and the excess

We assume that
n\geq 3
throughout the rest of the paper,
unless otherwise stated.

5.1.
Excess definitions

In this section we introduce a way to measure
flatness
of a pair
(u,\nabla)
. Inspired by the definition of tilt-excess by De Giorgi
[
18
]
, we define the
Yang–Mills–Higgs excess
as

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),S)&:=\frac{r^{2-n}}{2\pi}\int_{B_{r}(x)}[e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)-J(u,\nabla)\wedge e_{S}^{*}]\\
&\phantom{:}=\mu_{\varepsilon}(B_{r}(x))-\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{B_{r}(x)}e_{S}^{*}\rangle\end{aligned}

for any
oriented

(n-2)
-plane
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the associated
(n-2)
-vector
e_{S}
and
(n-2)
-covector
e_{S}^{*}
. Take an oriented orthonormal basis of
S=\operatorname{span}\{e_{3},\dots,e_{n}\}
and extend it to an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then by a completion of squares we see that the excess splits into two terms:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2},

(5.2)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),S)

\displaystyle:=\frac{r^{2-n}}{2\pi}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\left[\sum_{k=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{k}}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{(j,k)\neq(1,2)}\omega(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}\right],

(5.3)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),S)

\displaystyle:=\frac{r^{2-n}}{2\pi}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\left[|\nabla_{e_{1}}u+i\nabla_{e_{2}}u|^{2}+\left|\varepsilon\omega(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right].

Note that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
quantifies how flat the solution is in the directions tangent to
S
, while
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
quantifies the error in the vortex equations on perpendicular slices.
Moreover,
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
does
not
depend on the orientation of
S
(while
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
do).

The Yang–Mills–Higgs excess is a key tool in our analysis.
For
S:=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, with a slight abuse of notation, we define

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}[e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)-J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})]

for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, and similarly

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}}\left[\sum_{k=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{k}}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{(j,k)\neq(1,2)}\omega(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}\right],

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}}\left[|\nabla_{e_{1}}u+i\nabla_{e_{2}}u|^{2}+\left|\varepsilon\omega(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right].

5.2.
The tilt-excess decay statement

Parallel to De Giorgi’s
[
18
]
and Allard’s
[
2
]
regularity theorems, we aim to prove a
decay of the excess
up to scale
\varepsilon
, compare with
[
54
, Theorem 3.3]
. More precisely, our goal is to show
Theorem

1.4
,
which is one of the main results of the present work. For convenience, we recall its statement here.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
n\geq 3
and small enough
0<\rho\leq\rho_{0}(n)
there exist constants
C(n)>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\rho),\tau_{0}(n,\rho)
such that the following holds.
Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point for the energy
E_{\varepsilon}
, given by
Eq.

3.1
, with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Assume that
u
satisfies the bounds
Eq.

4.1
and
Eq.

4.2
, that
u(0)=0
, and the energy bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{1}|}\int_{B^{n}_{1}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}.

Then at least one of the following statements is true:
either

(5.4)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho}^{n},\overline{S})\leq C(n)\rho^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S),

for some
(n-2)
-plane
\overline{S}
with
\|P_{\overline{S}}-P_{S}\|\leq C(n)\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)}
, where
P_{S}
is the orthogonal projection onto
S
, the plane minimizing
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho}^{n},\cdot)
, and
\|\cdot\|
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, or

(5.5)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)\leq\max\{C(n)\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}},e^{-{K(n)}/{\varepsilon}}\},

where
\bm{\mathrm{E}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},S)
.

Note that thanks to
Proposition

4.3
and
Proposition

4.4
, if
u
is an entire solution such that
\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)=O(R^{n-2})
, then
Eq.

4.1
and
Eq.

4.2
are statisfied. In particular by scaling we deduce the following.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any small enough
0<\rho\leq\rho_{0}(n)
, there exist constants
C(n),R_{0}(n,\rho)>0
and
\tau_{0}(n,\rho)
with the following property. Let
(u,\nabla)
be an entire critical point for
E_{1}
, with the energy bound

\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{1}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}.

Then for all
R\geq R_{0}
at least one of the following statements is true:
either

(5.6)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho R}^{n},\overline{S})\leq C(n)\rho^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S),

for some
(n-2)
-plane
\overline{S}
with
\|P_{\overline{S}}-P_{S}\|\leq C(n)\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S)}
and
S
minimizing
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},\cdot)
, or

(5.7)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S)\leq\max\{C(n)R^{-2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}},e^{-{K(n)R}}\},

where
\bm{\mathrm{E}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S)
.

5.3.
Blow-up at multiplicity one points

Allard’s regularity theorem
[
2
]
asserts that the energy concentration set in
Theorem

4.1
is locally a
C^{1,\alpha}
submanifold around points of multiplicity one. We use this to show that, for any blow-down, the energy concentration set is a flat
(n-2)
-plane.

Proposition 5.3

(Multiplicity one and vanishing of excess)
.

For any
\delta>0
there exist
\tau_{0}(n,\delta)>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\delta)>0
small enough with the following property. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point for
E_{\varepsilon}
on the unit ball
B_{1}^{n}
, with
u(0)=0
and
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, as well as the energy bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{1}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}

and
Eq.

4.1
–
Eq.

4.2
.
Then, after a suitable rotation and, possibly, a conjugation of
(u,\nabla)
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{1/2}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\delta,

where we write
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
to mean
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
.
As a consequence, given an entire critical point
(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})
for
E_{1}
, with
u(0)=0
and the energy bound

\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|B_{R}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{1}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}(n),

then the previous limit is
2\pi
and
we can find oriented
(n-2)
-planes
S(R)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla},B_{R}^{n},S(R))=0.

Proof.

The proof is a standard argument by compactness and contradiction.

Local case.
Assume that there are sequences
(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})
and
\tau_{\varepsilon}\to 0
(as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
) such that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\leq(2\pi+\tau_{\varepsilon})|B_{1}^{n-2}|

and, on the other hand,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon},B_{1/2}^{n},S(\varepsilon))>0,

for any choice of oriented
(n-2)
-planes
S(\varepsilon)

(where, with abuse of notation, we write
\varepsilon
to mean a sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
). We apply
Theorem

4.1
: up to extracting a subsequence, we have

e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\,dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}2\pi\,d\mu_{V}

in duality with
C^{0}_{c}
, where
V
is a stationary integral
(n-2)
-varifold whose weight
\mu_{V}
obeys the bound

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mu_{V}(B_{1}^{n})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\leq|B_{1}^{n-2}|.

Moreover there exists an integral
(n-2)
-cycle
\Gamma
such that
J(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\rightharpoonup 2\pi\Gamma
as currents and
|\Gamma|\leq\mu_{V}
.

Since
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
, by the clearing-out lemma
[
42
, Corollary 4.4]
we get that
0\in\spt(\mu_{V})
, so that
\Theta^{n-2}(\mu_{V},0)\geq 1

since
V
is an integral stationary varifold. Because of
Eq.

5.9
, the monotonicity formula for stationary varifolds is saturated, showing that
V
must be a cone with respect to the origin; we extend it to a stationary cone
\tilde{V}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since
\Theta^{n-2}(\mu_{\tilde{V}},x)\geq 1=\Theta^{n-2}(\mu_{\tilde{V}},0)

for all
x\in\spt(\mu_{\tilde{V}})
,
we see that
\tilde{V}
is a cone with respect to any
x\in\spt(\mu_{\tilde{V}})
, and hence a plane (since the tangent plane exists for a.e. point
x
).
Thus, up to a rotation,
V
is the multiplicity-one varifold associated to
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
.

Moreover, the argument used in
[
42
, Section 6.2]
to show integrality of
V

actually reveals that the limiting density is the sum of the absolute values of the degrees of

\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{|u_{\varepsilon}|}\Big|_{\partial D_{i}\times\{z\}}

along typical slices
B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}
with
|z|<\frac{1}{2}
,
where
D_{1},\dots,D_{N}\subset B_{1/2}^{2}

are suitable disjoint disks (depending on
z
)
such that
u_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,z)\neq 0
on
B_{1/2}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i}D_{i}
(see in particular the proof of
[
42
, Proposition 6.6]
and the conclusion of
[
42
, Proposition 6.7]
).
Since the limiting density is
1
and, eventually,
u_{\varepsilon}(y,z)\neq 0

for
y\in\partial B_{1/2}^{2}
and
z\in B_{1/2}^{n-2}
,
we see that

\operatorname{deg}\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{|u_{\varepsilon}|}(\cdot,z)=1\quad\text{from $\partial B_{1/2}^{2}$ to $S^{1}$}

eventually. As in
[
42
, Lemma 6.11]
,
we deduce that
\Gamma=\pm\llbracket\{0\}\times B^{n-2}_{1}\rrbracket
.
We conclude that, after possibly replacing
(u,\nabla)

with the conjugate pair, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon},B_{1/2}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{n}}[e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})-J(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\wedge e_{3}^{*}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{n}^{*}]

\displaystyle=\mu_{V}(B_{1/2}^{n})-\langle\Gamma,\bm{1}_{B_{1/2}^{n}}e_{3}^{*}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{n}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

which is the desired contradiction.

Entire case.
For the case of an entire solution
(u,\nabla)
, we perform a rescaling: writing
\nabla=d-i\tilde{\alpha}
, let

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x):=u(\varepsilon^{-1}x),\quad\nabla_{\varepsilon}:=d-i\alpha_{\varepsilon}\text{ with }\alpha_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha(\varepsilon^{-1}x).

Again, by applying
Theorem

4.1
, up to extracting a subsequence we have
e_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\,dx\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}d\mu_{V}
, for a stationary integral
(n-2)
-varifold
V
, and the Jacobians
J(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{\varepsilon})\rightharpoonup\Gamma
for an integral
(n-2)
-cycle
\Gamma
with the pointwise bound
|\Gamma|\leq\mu_{V}
. Using the monotonicity formula for
E_{\varepsilon}
, we see that

\mu_{V}(B_{R}^{n})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon^{n-2}}{2\pi}\int_{B_{R/\varepsilon}^{n}}e_{1}(u,\nabla)

is a constant multiple of
R^{n-2}
, and hence

V
is a cone around the origin with
\mu_{V}(B^{n}_{1})\leq 1+\frac{\tau_{0}}{2\pi}
. Then, by Allard’s regularity theorem
[
2
]
, we see that for
\tau_{0}(n)
small enough, after a suitable rotation,
V
is the varifold associated to
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
.
In particular, this shows the conclusion on the energy limit.

As before, we also have
\Gamma=\pm\llbracket\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\rrbracket
, concluding that for
R:=\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}
(where
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
is our subsequence)
the statement holds for the plane
S(R):=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, either for
(u,\nabla)
or the conjugate pair
(\overline{u},\overline{\nabla}=d+i\alpha)
(depending on
R
). Since the initial sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
was arbitrary, we deduce that

(5.10)

\min_{S\in\Gr(n,n-2)}\min\{\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S),\bm{\mathrm{E}}(\overline{u},\overline{\nabla},B_{R}^{n},S)\}\to 0

as
R\to\infty
. Finally, letting
S(R)
realize the minimum over
S\in\Gr(n,n-2)
, since
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}
does not distinguish between
(u,\nabla)
and
(\overline{u},\overline{\nabla})
we have

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S(R))\to 0.

As a consequence, we must have

(5.11)

\sup_{R^{\prime}\in[R,2R]}\|P_{S(R^{\prime})}-P_{S(R)}\|\to 0,

since otherwise we would find sequences
S(R_{k})\to\hat{S}
and
S(R_{k}^{\prime})\to\hat{S}^{\prime}\neq\hat{S}
(with
R_{k}\leq R_{k}^{\prime}\leq 2R_{k}
) for which

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},\hat{S})+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},\hat{S}^{\prime})\to 0\quad\text{as $R=R_{k}\to\infty$},

thanks to the assumption
\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{1}(u,\nabla)=O(R^{n-2})
. If
\hat{S}\cup\hat{S}^{\prime}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, this immediately gives
\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{1}(u,\nabla)=o(R^{n-2})
, contradicting the assumption
u(0)=0
and the clearing-out lemma. Otherwise,
their span is
(n-1)
-dimensional; letting
e_{1}
be a unit vector orthogonal to it and completing to an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
such that
e_{2}\perp\hat{S}
, we deduce that

\int_{B_{R}^{n}}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{j}}u|^{2}+|\omega|^{2}\right]=o(R^{n-2}).

Because of
Eq.

5.10
, we also have

\min\{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},\hat{S}),\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(\overline{u},\overline{\nabla},B_{R}^{n},\hat{S})\}\to 0,

giving

\int_{B_{R}^{n}}\left[||\nabla_{e_{1}}u|-|\nabla_{e_{2}}u||^{2}+\left||\omega(e_{1},e_{2})|-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2}\right|^{2}\right]\to 0,

giving again the contradiction
\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{1}(u,\nabla)=o(R^{n-2})
.

Having established
Eq.

5.11
, the claim follows by a straightforward continuity argument: for
R
large enough we cannot have that

\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S(R)),\quad\bm{\mathrm{E}}(\overline{u},\overline{\nabla},B_{R^{\prime}}^{n},S(R^{\prime}))

are both small, for some
R^{\prime}\in[R,2R]
, since this would imply that

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S(R))+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(\overline{u},\overline{\nabla},B_{R}^{n},S(R))

is also small, which would give again small normalized energy on
B_{R}^{n}
; the same holds interchanging the roles of
R
and
R^{\prime}
, completing the proof.
∎

Z=\{|u|\leq\frac{3}{4}\}
.

Lemma 5.4

(Soft height bound)
.

For any

there exist

and
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\sigma)>0
with the following property. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point for

on

, with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and

, as well as the energy bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{1}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}

and

4.1
–
Eq.

.
Then, after a suitable rotation, the zero set is contained in a small neighborhood of

Following the same strategy as in the proof of

Remark 5.5
.

We will often use the following observation:
if the excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
is suitably small on
B_{1}^{n}
, then the same conclusion holds without any rotation.
The same holds under other assumptions forcing the vorticity set to concentrate on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, such as energy close to
|B_{1}^{n-2}|\cdot 2\pi
on the cylinder
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}
, for a critical pair defined there (with
u(0)=0
).

In the following lemma we essentially show that if
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
is small in a ball of radius larger than
\varepsilon
, then
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is small as well.

Lemma 5.6

(
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
vanishing implies
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
vanishing)
.

For any
\delta,\Lambda>0
there exist
\tau_{0}(n,\delta,\Lambda)>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\delta,\Lambda)>0
small enough with the following property. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical pair for
E_{\varepsilon}
on the unit ball
B_{1}^{n}
,
with
u(0)=0
,

E_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0},

and
Eq.

4.1
–
Eq.

4.2
, as well as
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
.
Let
x\in B_{1-\delta}^{n}
be a point such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\leq s\leq 1-|x|}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{s}^{n}(x),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\tau_{0}.

Then, up to conjugating the pair,

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\leq s\leq 1-|x|}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{s/2}^{n}(x),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\delta.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma is basically the equivalence of the (second-order) Euler–Lagrange equations and the (first-order) vortex equations in two dimensions.

By contradiction, assume we have a sequence
(u_{k},\nabla_{k})
of critical points for
E_{\varepsilon}
, with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
,
and a sequence of points
x_{k}\in B_{1-\delta}^{n}
and radii
s_{k}\in[\varepsilon_{k},1-|x_{k}|]
such that

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u_{k},\nabla_{k},B^{n}_{s_{k}}(x_{k}),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\rightarrow 0,\quad\liminf_{k\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u_{k},\nabla_{k},B^{n}_{s_{k}/2}(x_{k}),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\geq\delta.

We now distinguish a few cases depending on the behavior of the limit
\varepsilon_{k}/s_{k}
, which we can assume to exists and to belong to
[0,1]
, and on the distance of
x_{k}
from the vorticity set
Z_{k}=\{x\in B_{s_{k}}^{n}(x_{k}):|u_{k}|\leq 3/4\}
.

Case 1:
\varepsilon_{k}/s_{k}\to 0
and
\dist(x_{k},Z_{k})/s_{k}\to 0
.
Since the energy concentration varifold is a plane with multiplicity 1 (as in the previous proof), recalling that
1-|x_{k}|\geq\delta
and
x_{k}
has vanishing distance from the vorticity set, we immediately see that

\frac{1}{|B_{1-|x_{k}|}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{1-|x_{k}|}(x_{k})}e_{\varepsilon_{k}}(u_{k},\nabla_{k})\to 2\pi.

Defining the map
\phi_{k}(x):=x_{k}+s_{k}x
, we consider the pullback pair

(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{\nabla}_{k}):=\phi_{k}^{*}(u_{k},\nabla_{k}),

which is critical for
E_{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}}
,
where
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}:=\varepsilon_{k}/s_{k}
. Moreover,
we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{|B_{1}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{1}^{n}}e_{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}}(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{\nabla}_{k})\leq 2\pi

by monotonicity of the energy.

Since
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}\to 0
and
0
has vanishing distance from
\{|\tilde{u}_{k}|\leq\frac{3}{4}\}\cap B_{1}^{n}
, as in the previous proof,
the energy concentration varifold
V
is a plane
S
passing through the origin, with multiplicity 1, while
the limiting cycle
\Gamma=\pm\llbracket S\rrbracket
. By possibly replacing
(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})
with their conjugate, we can assume that
\Gamma=\llbracket S\rrbracket
.
Also, the stress-energy tensors

T_{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}}(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{\nabla}_{k}),

viewed as matrix-valued measures,
converge (up to subsequences) to a limit
T
such that
dT(x)=P_{T_{x}V}\,d\mu_{V}(x)
, where
P_{T_{x}V}
is the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space
T_{x}V

(cf.
[
42
, Section 6.1]
).
Hence, the fact that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{\nabla}_{k},B_{1}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\to 0

implies
T_{x}V=\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
a.e., giving
S=\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
. Since

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{B_{1/2}^{n}}[e_{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}}(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{\nabla}_{k})-J(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{\nabla}_{k})\wedge e_{S}^{*}]=2\pi[\mu_{V}(B_{1/2}^{n})-\langle\Gamma,\bm{1}_{B_{1/2}^{n}}e_{S}^{*}\rangle]=0

, we get the desired contradiction in this case.

Case 2:
\varepsilon_{k}/s_{k}\to 0
and
\dist(x_{k},Z_{k})/s_{k}\to 2d>0
.
By performing the same scaling as in the previous step we get that
|\tilde{u}_{k}|
converges uniformly to
1
in
B_{d}^{n}(0)
, which immediately implies that both excesses converges to
0
in
B_{ds_{k}}^{n}(x_{k})
and thus the statement of the theorem with
s/2
replaced by
ds
. A covering argument then allows to pass to
s/2
.

Case 3:
\varepsilon_{k}/s_{k}\to\overline{\varepsilon}>0
.
Note that this implies that
s_{k}\to 0
.

After passing to a local Coulomb gauge, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we get local uniform
C^{\ell}
bounds on
B_{R_{k}}^{n}
,
with
R_{k}:=\delta/s_{k}
, since by monotonicity we have local uniform bounds on the energy here, see
[
42
, Appendix]
. By Arzelà–Ascoli we obtain a subsequential limit
(\tilde{u}_{\infty},\tilde{\nabla}_{\infty})
in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
By the definition of
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
(cf.
Eq.

5.2
), we see that
(\tilde{\nabla}_{\infty})_{\partial_{k}}\tilde{u}_{\infty}=0
for all
3\leq k\leq n
and
\tilde{\omega}_{\infty}(e_{j},e_{k})=0
for all
(j,k)\neq(1,2)
.
As in
[
42
, Proposition 6.7]
(after
[
42
, eq. (6.30)]
), up to a further change of gauge,
the limiting pair depends only on the first two coordinates.
By the equivalence of first-order and second-order vortex equations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
52
]
(cf. also the end of the proof of
[
42
, Proposition 6.7]
), we see that
(\tilde{u}_{\infty},\tilde{\nabla}_{\infty})
solves the first-order vortex equations up to conjugation;
this yields a contradiction for
k
large enough.
∎

.

For every
\sigma>0
there exist constants
\eta(n,\sigma),C(n,\eta)>0
such that if

and

satisfying

at

Moreover, given

there are

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{C\varepsilon}^{n}(p),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\eta

In the next lemma we show that
the energy on each slice is approximately the excess on the slice plus the degree of
u
on the boundary.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
(u,\nabla)
be an arbitrary smooth pair defined on
\overline{B}^{2}_{1}\times\overline{B}^{n-2}_{1}
(not necessarily a critical point) with

\displaystyle e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)(x)\leq e^{-K/\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all }x\in\partial B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}

and
|u(x)|\geq\frac{1}{2}
for all
x
in the same set.
Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{deg}(u/|u|,\partial B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\})+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\right|\leq 4\varepsilon e^{-K/\varepsilon},

for all
z\in B_{1}^{n-2}
, up to conjugating the pair.

Proof.

First of all, by a completion of squares, since

J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})=2\langle i\nabla_{1}u,\nabla_{2}u\rangle+(1-|u|^{2})\omega(e_{1},e_{2}),

we see that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\\
&=(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}\left[|\nabla_{1}u|^{2}+|\nabla_{2}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\omega(e_{1},e_{2})^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\right]\\
&=(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times x}\left[|i\nabla_{1}u-\nabla_{2}u|^{2}+\left|\varepsilon\omega(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})\right]\\
&=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2}).\end{aligned}

We then define the modulus
r:B_{1}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and the phase
\theta:B^{2}_{1}\setminus\{r=0\}\rightarrow S^{1}
by

\displaystyle r(y):=|u(y,z)|,\quad\theta(y):=\frac{u}{|u|}(y,z).

Writing
\nabla=d-i\alpha
, we also have

r^{2}(d\theta-\alpha)(y)=\langle\nabla u,iu\rangle(y,z)

(note that
\theta
and
\alpha
are not gauge-invariant). Recalling that
J(u,\nabla)=d\alpha+d\langle\nabla u,iu\rangle
, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}}J(u,\nabla)=\int_{\partial B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}}[(1-r^{2})\alpha(\tau)+r^{2}\partial_{\tau}\theta],

where
\tau
is the tangent vector to
\partial B^{2}_{1}
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}}J(u,\nabla)=2\pi\operatorname{deg}(u/|u|,\partial B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\})+\int_{\partial B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}}(1-r^{2})[\alpha(\tau)-\partial_{\tau}\theta],

and the last integrand is bounded by

(|u|^{-2}-1)|\langle\nabla u,iu\rangle|\leq 4(1-|u|^{2})|\nabla u|\leq 4\varepsilon e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

in absolute value. Combining these bounds, the claim follows.
∎

6.
Slicing the current and Lipschitz approximation

In this section, inspired by
[
5
,
37
,
19
]
, we slice the currents
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
dual to the Jacobians

6.1.
Slicing identities and BV estimates

We start by defining vertical slices.

Definition 6.1
.

We define the
vertical slices
of the current
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
,
(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{z}=\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},P,z\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{1}(0)}\langle(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{z},\psi\rangle\phi(z)\,dz=\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\psi(y)\phi(z)dz\rangle,

for any two functions
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B^{2}_{1})
and
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B^{n-2}_{1})
, where
P:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
is the projection on the last

coordinates.

In the next lemma we derive BV estimates for the slices, given a smooth pair
(u,\nabla)
defined on
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}
.

Lemma 6.2

(BV-type estimate)
.

Define the function
\Phi_{\psi}:B^{n-2}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\psi}(z):=\langle(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{z},\psi\rangle.

Then, assuming
\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi\Lambda
, the total variation of
\Phi_{\psi}(x)
is bounded by
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|D\Phi_{\psi}|(B^{n-2}_{1})^{2}\leq\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\Lambda\min\{C(n)\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{E}}\},

where
|D\Phi_{\psi}|
denotes the total variation measure, and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
are measured on
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}

(without normalization).

Proof.

The notation and line of argument is inspired from
[
19
, Lemma A.1]
. For any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B^{n-2}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})
we define the
(n-3)
-form
\alpha
by

\displaystyle\alpha:=\sum_{k=3}^{n}(-1)^{k-1}\phi_{k}(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})dx_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{k-1}\wedge dx_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n},

so that

\displaystyle d\alpha=(\operatorname{div}\phi)(z)dz.

Now, writing
x=(y,z)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{1}}\Phi_{\psi}(z)\operatorname{div}\phi(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{n-2}_{1}}\langle(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{z},\psi\rangle(\operatorname{div}\phi)(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\psi(y)(\operatorname{div}\phi)(z)dz\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},d(\psi\alpha)\rangle-\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},d\psi\wedge\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},d\psi\wedge\alpha\rangle,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\partial\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=0
. Now notice that
d\psi\wedge\alpha
is a linear combination of
(n-2)
-covectors of the form

\displaystyle dx_{j}\wedge dx_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{k-1}\wedge dx_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}\quad\text{with }j=1,2,\ k=3,\dots,n.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle|\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},d\psi\wedge\alpha\rangle|\leq\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,2\\
k=3,\dots,n\end{subarray}}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}}[2|\langle i\nabla_{e_{j}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle|+(1-|u|^{2})|\omega(e_{j},e_{k})|],

which, by Cauchy–Schwarz, is bounded by

\displaystyle\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot C(n)\sqrt{\Lambda}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}.

Taking the supremum over the functions
\phi
with
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
, we get the BV bound

\displaystyle|D\Phi_{\psi}|(B^{n-2}_{1})\leq C(n)\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{\Lambda}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}.

We can also estimate in the following way. Set
B:=B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}
and

\vec{e}_{n-2}=e_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{n},\quad e^{*}_{n-2}:=dx_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n},

and let us write
d\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\overrightarrow{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|
(viewing
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
as a measure with values in
\Lambda_{n-2}\mathbb{R}^{n}
).
Since
d\psi\wedge\alpha
does not have any
e^{*}_{n-2}
-component, if we write
\vec{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}=(\vec{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\vec{e}_{n-2})\vec{e}_{n-2}+\vec{R}

(where the dot denotes the scalar product in
\Lambda^{n-2}\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we get

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},d\psi\wedge\alpha\rangle=\int_{B}\vec{R}\cdot(d\psi\wedge\alpha)\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|,

and moreover

\displaystyle\int_{B}|\vec{R}|^{2}\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle=\int_{B}(1-(\vec{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\vec{e}_{n-2})^{2})\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B}(1-\vec{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\vec{e}_{n-2})\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle=2e(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},B,\vec{e}_{n-2}),

where
e(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},B,\vec{e}_{n-2})
is the
current excess
defined by

\displaystyle e(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},B,\vec{e}_{n-2}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B}|\overrightarrow{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}-\vec{e}_{n-2}|^{2}\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|.

Hence,

\displaystyle|\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},d\psi\wedge\alpha\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{B}\overrightarrow{R}\cdot(d\psi\wedge\alpha)\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|\right|

\displaystyle\leq|d\psi\wedge\alpha|\int_{B}|\vec{R}|\,d|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle\leq\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{2e(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},B,\vec{e}_{n-2})}\sqrt{|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|(B)}\,.

Again, taking the supremum over the functions
\phi
with
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
, we get

\displaystyle|D\Phi_{\psi}|(B^{n-2}_{1})\leq\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{2e(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},B,\overrightarrow{e}_{n-2})}\sqrt{|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|(B)}.

From the pointwise bound of the Jacobian
|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)
we see that

\displaystyle e(\Gamma_{\varepsilon},B,\overrightarrow{e}_{n-2})=|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|(B)-\langle\Gamma_{\varepsilon},\bm{1}_{B}\overrightarrow{e}_{n-2}\rangle\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

The previous bounds, together with
|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|(B)\leq\Lambda
, give the conclusion.
∎

6.2.
Lipschitz approximation of the barycenter

Parallel to the regularity theory of minimal currents, we define a Lipschitz approximation of the barycenter of the slices of
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
(see for instance
[
19
, Lemma A.2]

•

we use
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
as shorthand for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})
,
and similarly for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
;

•

as already mentioned, for any

we denote the excess on the slice

by
(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}

and similarly for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}

•

we write
M_{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}(z)
to denote the maximal function of
(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}

•

we fix a cut-off function

and

on

Proposition 6.4

(Lipschitz approximation)
.

Given
0<\eta\leq\eta_{0}(n)
small enough, there exist

\tau_{0}(n,\eta)>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\eta)>0

such that the following holds.
Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical pair for
E_{\varepsilon}
, defined on
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}
,
satisfying
u(0)=0
,
Eq.

4.1
–
Eq.

4.2
, and the energy bound

\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}.

Then, up to conjugating
(u,\nabla)
, for
0<\eta\leq\eta_{0}(n)
small enough there exists a Lipschitz approximation
h:B^{n-2}_{3/4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the following properties:

(i)

\operatorname{Lip}(h)\leq C\eta
and
\int_{B^{n-2}_{3/4}}|dh|^{2}\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
;

(ii)

h|_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}=\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}
for a set
\mathcal{G}^{\eta}\subseteq B_{3/4}^{n-2}
such that
|B^{n-2}_{3/4}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}|\leq C\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}
;

(iii)

\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}\times(B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta})}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq C\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}+e^{-{K}/\varepsilon}
;

(iv)

\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}\frac{|dh|^{2}}{2}\leq(1+\delta)\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\,dz+e^{-K/\varepsilon}
with
\delta(n,\eta)>0
such that
\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\delta(n,\eta)=0
.

Here
C=C(n)>0
and
K=K(n)>0
, provided that
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
.

Proof.

We define the
good set
to be

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\eta}:=\{z\in B^{n-2}_{3/4}\,:\,M_{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}(z)\leq\eta^{2}\}.

By the weak
L^{1}
bound and Vitali’s covering lemma, we can bound the measure of the complement of the good set, namely the
bad set
, by

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(B^{n-2}_{3/4}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta})\leq C(n)\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}.

Bounding energy on the bad set.
To check that the third conclusion holds, we introduce another
bad set

\mathcal{B}^{\eta}
, defined on the
n
-dimensional space:
it is the set of points
x=(y,z)\in B_{3/4}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}
such that, for some radius
r\in(0,\frac{1}{50})
, we have
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r}^{n}(x),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})>\eta^{2}
.

By Vitali’s covering lemma, we can cover
\mathcal{B}^{\eta}
with balls
B_{5r_{i}}(x_{i})

such that the balls
B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})
are disjoint and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})>\eta^{2}
.
By monotonicity of the energy, the energy on each dilated ball
B_{5r_{i}}(x_{i})
is at most
C(n)r_{i}^{n-2}
, giving

\int_{\mathcal{B^{\eta}}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq\sum_{i}C(n)r_{i}^{n-2}\leq\sum_{i}\frac{C(n)}{\eta^{2}}r_{i}^{n-2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\frac{C(n)}{\eta^{2}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}

(recall that the excess on a ball
B_{r}(x)
is normalized by a factor
r^{2-n}
).
Since the measure of
B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}
obeys the same bound,
it is enough to show that

\int_{S}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq C(n)

for
\eta
small enough, where
S:=(B_{3/4}^{2}\times\{z\})\setminus\mathcal{B}^{\eta}
.

We denote by
d_{Z}
the distance from the vorticity set
Z=\{|u|\leq\frac{3}{4}\}
.
As we can see from the proof of
Lemma

5.4
, its conclusion holds without any rotation in the present situation (as necessarily energy concentrates along
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
as
\tau_{0},\varepsilon_{0}\to 0
). Hence, we can assume that,
for any
(y,z)\in S
on this slice, we have
d_{Z}(y,z)\geq\frac{1}{200}
unless
|y|<\frac{1}{100}
.

Given
s\geq\varepsilon
, by
Lemma

5.7
we know that if

d_{Z}(y,z),d_{Z}(y^{\prime},z)<s,

for two points
(y,z),(y^{\prime},z)\in S
with
|y|,|y^{\prime}|<\frac{1}{100}
,
then

|y-y^{\prime}|\leq Cs,

provided that
\eta
,
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon/s
are small enough.
With this observation in hand, we can apply
Proposition

4.5
(giving
e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)(y,z)\leq Ce^{-K\min\{d_{Z}(y,z),1/10\}/\varepsilon}
on
S

\int_{S}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{3}}\int_{0}^{2}e^{-Kt/\varepsilon}|\{y\in B_{1/100}^{2}\,:\,d_{Z}(y,z)<t\}|\,dt+\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{3}}e^{-K/(200\varepsilon)}.

The previous observation says that
\{y\in B_{1/100}^{2}\,:\,d_{Z}(y,z)<t\}

.
We deduce that the last integral is bounded by

\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{3}}\int_{0}^{2}e^{-Kt/\varepsilon}C\max\{t^{2},\varepsilon^{2}\}\,dt\leq C,

giving the desired bound

\int_{S}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq C(n).

Bounds in terms of
(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}
.
Now we establish Dirichlet energy bounds for
\Phi_{\psi}
on the good set, for
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2})

, we can use

6.3
to bound

\displaystyle|d\Phi_{\psi}|^{2}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=1,2}\sum_{k=3}^{n}\left[\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}(-1)^{j}\left(\langle 2i\nabla_{e_{j}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle+(1-|u|^{2})\omega(e_{j},e_{k})\right)\partial_{e_{3-j}}\psi\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left[\int_{B^{2}_{3/4}\times\{z\}}|\nabla_{e_{1}}u|^{2}+|\nabla_{e_{2}}u|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right](\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}.

Since

S=B_{3/4}^{2}\times\{z\}

.
As a consequence,

\displaystyle|d\Phi_{\psi}|^{2}(z)\leq C(n)\|d\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathcal{G}^{\eta}.

Bounds in terms of
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}
.
Also, we can use
Lemma

6.2
(cf.
[
19

) to conclude that

|d\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}|^{2}(z)\leq 2\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|(B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z))}{|B_{r}^{n-2}|}+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}.

(indeed, this bound follows by applying
Lemma

6.2
and its proof
with

To conclude, we have

|\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|(B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z))\leq|B_{r}^{n-2}|+\int_{B_{r}^{n-2}(z)}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+|B_{r}^{n-2}|e^{-K/\varepsilon}

by

where we can actually replace

with the excess on the smaller disk

denoted by
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}(B_{1/2}^{2})

Now, fixing

5.6

Remark 6.7
.

We remark that the function

7.
Harmonic approximation and a Caccioppoli-type estimate

7.1.
Harmonic approximation

In this section we show that the Lipschitz approximation of
Proposition

6.4
nearly satisfies the Laplace equation. We achieve this by relating the stress-energy tensor to the slices of
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
using the self-dual discrepancy excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
. Then we use this with uniform
W^{1,2}
bounds to show that the Lipschitz approximation is well approximated in
L^{2}
by a harmonic function. To begin with, we state a very well-known lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

For any
\nu>0
small there exists
\tau(n,\nu)>0
with the following property. Let
f
be a function in
W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{n})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{n}}|\nabla f|^{2}\leq 1,\quad\left|\int_{B_{1}^{n}}\langle df,d\phi\rangle\right|\leq\tau\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}},

for any
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1}^{n})
. Then there exists a harmonic function
w:B_{1}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{n}}|dw|^{2}\leq 1,\quad\int_{B_{1}^{n}}|w-f|^{2}\leq\nu.

Moreover, if
f
has zero average, we can choose
w
so that
w(0)=0
.

Proof.

The claim follows easily from Rellich’s compact embedding theorem: see for instance
[
20
, Lemma 6.1]
.
For the second part, by the mean value property of harmonic functions and
\int f=0
one gets that

|B_{1}^{n}|\lvert w(0)\rvert=\left\lvert\int_{B_{1}}w-\int_{B_{1}^{n}}f\right\rvert\leq C(n)\|w-f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\nu}.

The function
w-w(0)
satisfies the conclusion of the lemma.
∎

Proposition 7.2

(Harmonic approximation)
.

Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point of
E_{\varepsilon}
as in the previous section and let
h:B_{3/4}^{n-2}
be the Lipschitz approximation built in
Proposition

6.4
for
\eta
. Then there exist constants
C(n),K(n)>0
such that, for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B^{n-2}_{3/4},\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\langle dh,d\phi\rangle\right|\leq C(\eta^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}+e^{-K/\varepsilon})\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Moreover, given any
\nu>0
, if
e^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is small enough (depending on
n,\eta,\nu
),
there exists a harmonic function
w:B^{n-2}_{3/4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
w(0)=0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}}|dw|^{2}\leq C,\quad\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}}|(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})^{-1/2}(h-c)-w|^{2}\leq\nu,

where
c
is the average of
h
.

Proof.

First, we define the vector field
X:=\phi(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})e_{1}
for any compactly supported test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{n-2})
, and we test
Eq.

4.9
with
\psi(x_{1},x_{2})X
, where
\psi
is a smooth cut-off function such that
\psi=1
on
B^{2}_{1/2}
and
\psi=0
outside of
B_{3/4}^{2}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\langle T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),DX\rangle\right|\leq Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}},

thanks to the fact that
d\psi
is supported in the annulus
B^{2}_{3/4}\setminus B^{2}_{1/2}
and the exponential decay away from the vorticity set
Z
, which intersects
B_{3/4}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}

only inside
B_{1/4}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}
. Then, since
DX
is traceless, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\langle T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),DX\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}\left[\langle\nabla_{e_{1}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega(e_{1},e_{j})\omega(e_{k},e_{j})\right]\partial_{e_{k}}\phi.

Except for
j=2
, the integral of the terms involving the curvature
\omega
is bounded by
C(n)\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, giving

(7.1)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}[\langle\nabla_{e_{1}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle+\varepsilon^{2}\omega(e_{1},e_{2})\omega(e_{k},e_{2})]\partial_{e_{k}}\phi\right|\leq C(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+e^{-K/\varepsilon})\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}.

We now want to relate the expression in the left-hand side with the identity
for
d\Phi_{\chi x_{1}}
obtained in
Remark

6.3
, which in particular gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\langle d\Phi_{\chi x_{1}},d\phi\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}[\langle 2i\nabla_{e_{2}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle+(1-|u|^{2})\omega(e_{2},e_{k})]\partial_{e_{k}}\phi\right|

\displaystyle\quad+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}},

or equivalently

(7.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left|\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\langle d\Phi_{\chi x_{1}},d\phi\rangle\right|&\leq C\left|\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}\left[-\langle i\nabla_{e_{2}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle+\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2}\omega(e_{k},e_{2})\right]\partial_{e_{k}}\phi\right|\\
&\quad+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}.\end{aligned}

We observe that the two integrals in
Eq.

7.1
and
Eq.

7.2

differ by the integral of

\sum_{k=3}^{n}\left[\langle\nabla_{e_{1}}u+i\nabla_{e_{2}}u,\nabla_{e_{k}}u\rangle+\left(\omega(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2}\right)\omega(e_{k},e_{2})\right]\partial_{e_{k}}\phi.

Hence, recalling the definition of
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
and using Cauchy–Schwarz, we conclude that

\left|\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}}\langle d\Phi_{\chi x_{1}},d\phi\rangle\right|\leq C(n)(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{E}}}+e^{-K/\varepsilon})\|d\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Repeating the same for
\Phi_{\chi x_{2}}
, we arrive at the same conclusion for
\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}
,
integrated against any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{n-2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
To conclude we note that thanks to items (i) and (ii) of
Proposition

6.4
,

\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}|dh|\leq C\eta|B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}|\leq C\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta}

and, in view of
Remark

6.3
, Cauchy–Schwarz, item (iii) of
Proposition

6.4
and the assumption
e^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
,

\int_{B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}|d\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}\left(\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\right)^{1/2}\leq C\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta}.

The second part follows from
Lemma

7.1
,
noting that the normalized function
\tilde{h}:=(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})^{-1/2}h

has Dirichlet energy bounded by
C(n)
by item (i) of
Proposition

6.4
.
∎

7.2.
Caccioppoli-type estimates

The starting point in the regularity theory of elliptic partial differential equations is the Caccioppoli–Leray bound, obtained by testing the equation with
\phi^{2}u
,
where
\phi
is a cut-off function and
u
is the solution. We aim to do something similar in spirit. Here the
function
that we deal with is the barycenter of the energy measure at any slice. This suggests that testing the stress-energy tensor with
\phi^{2}x_{1}e_{1}
and
\phi^{2}x_{2}e_{2}
is an appropriate choice.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical point of
E_{\varepsilon}
as in the previous section.
For any
\sigma>0
there exist
\varepsilon_{0}(n,\sigma)
and
\tau_{0}(n,\sigma)
small enough such that the following Caccioppoli-type estimate holds:

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\phi^{2}(z)(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}\,dz\leq C\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\Delta\phi^{2}+C(\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}})\|D^{2}\phi\|_{\infty},

for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B^{n-2}_{3/4})
, where
C=C(n)
and
K=K(n,\sigma)
.

Proof.

First, we define the vector fields

\displaystyle X:=\sum_{k=3}^{n}\partial_{k}\phi^{2}(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{2}e_{k},\quad Y:=\phi^{2}(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})(x_{1}e_{1}+x_{2}e_{2})

and we calculate their derivatives:

\displaystyle DX

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{3\leq j,k\leq n}\partial_{e_{j},e_{k}}^{2}\phi^{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})e_{j}\otimes e_{k}^{*}+\sum_{k=3}^{n}\partial_{e_{k}}\phi^{2}(x_{1}e_{k}\otimes e_{1}^{*}+x_{2}e_{k}\otimes e_{2}^{*})

\displaystyle DY

\displaystyle=\phi^{2}(e_{1}\otimes e_{1}^{*}+e_{2}\otimes e_{2}^{*})+\sum_{k=3}^{n}\partial_{e_{k}}\phi^{2}(x_{1}e_{1}\otimes e_{k}^{*}+x_{2}e_{2}\otimes e_{k}^{*}).

Then we test
Eq.

4.9
with
\chi X
and
\chi Y
, where
\chi=\chi(x_{1},x_{2})
is a smooth cut-off function such that
\chi=1
on
B_{1/2}^{2}
and
\chi=0
on
B^{2}_{1}\setminus B_{3/4}^{2}
. We note that the terms containing
d\chi
are supported in
(B^{2}_{3/4}\setminus B^{2}_{1/2})\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}
, where
|T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)|\leq C(n)e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)
is very small by the exponential decay. Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\langle T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),DY\rangle-\langle T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla),DX\rangle\right|\leq C\|D^{2}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}e^{-K/\varepsilon}.

Using the previous expansion of
DX
and
DY
, together with the symmetry of
T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)
, we see that the above integrand equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{3\leq j,k\leq n}T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)(e_{j},e_{k})\partial_{e_{j},e_{k}}^{2}\phi^{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})-\sum_{j=1,2}T_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)(e_{j},e_{j})\phi^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{2}\left[e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\Delta\phi^{2}-2\sum_{3\leq j,k\leq n}(\nabla u^{*}\nabla u+\varepsilon^{2}\omega^{*}\omega)(e_{j},e_{k})\partial_{e_{j},e_{k}}^{2}\phi^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-2\left[e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)-|\nabla_{e_{1}}u|^{2}-|\nabla_{e_{2}}u|^{2}-\sum_{j=1,2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{2}\omega(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}\right]\phi^{2}.

By the Modica-type inequality
Eq.

4.2
, the last expression multiplying
-2\phi^{2}
is bounded below by

\sum_{k=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{k}}u|^{2}+\frac{(1-|u|^{2})^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}-\varepsilon^{2}\omega(e_{1},e_{2})^{2}\geq\sum_{k=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{k}}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{(j,k)\neq(1,2)}\omega(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}

(where the last sum is over all pairs
(j,k)\neq(1,2)
with
j<k
), which is the integrand in the definition of
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
.
Hence, combining the previous bounds, we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\phi^{2}(z)(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{2}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\Delta\phi^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}{2}\sum_{3\leq j,k\leq n}(\nabla u^{*}\nabla u+\varepsilon^{2}\omega^{*}\omega)(e_{j},e_{k})\partial_{e_{j},e_{k}}^{2}\phi^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C\|D^{2}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}e^{-K/\varepsilon}.

Now, by the soft height bound, we can assume that the vorticity set
Z

intersects
B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}
in a small cylinder

B_{\sigma}^{2}\times B_{3/4}^{n-2}
; the conclusion follows by exponential decay, up to replacing
K
with another constant
K(n,\sigma)
.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

In the statement of
Proposition

7.3
we can replace the first term of the right-hand side as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\phi^{2}(z)(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}\,dz

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}[(x_{1}-c_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-c_{2})^{2}]e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\Delta\phi^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C(\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}})\|D^{2}\phi\|_{\infty},

provided that
|c|\leq C\sigma
for
C=C(n)
. The proof is essentially the same.

8.
Proof of decay of the tilt-excess

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.4
:
roughly speaking, we prove that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, the first part of the excess, decays up to scales where it becomes comparable with
\varepsilon^{2}
. We will deduce this from the excess decay property of harmonic functions, stated in the next elementary lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

Given a harmonic function
w:B^{n}_{1}(0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have the decay estimate

(8.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{\rho}^{n}(0)}|w(x)-w(0)-dw(0)[x]|\leq C(n)\rho^{2}\|dw\|_{L^{2}},

for
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.

Proof.

By a Taylor expansion, the left-hand side is bounded by
\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}\|D^{2}w\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2}^{n})}
,
which is bounded by
C(n)\rho^{2}\|dw\|_{L^{2}}
by the mean-value property of harmonic functions.
∎

8.1.
Proof of the excess decay in the case of small
|dw(0)|

First, we prove
Theorem

1.4
when the harmonic approximation has
|dw(0)|\leq\delta
, for a small
\delta>0
to be chosen later.
We dilate the ball
B_{1}^{n}
to
B_{\sqrt{2}}^{n}
(and replace
\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}
),
in such a way that it includes
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}
; we also assume that
S=\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
in the statement.

Let
c
be the average of
h
on the ball
B_{3/4}^{n-2}
. The construction of
h
shows that

|c|\leq C\sigma+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq C\sigma

for
\varepsilon
small enough (depending on
n,\sigma
).

We apply the Caccioppoli-type estimates in
Proposition

7.3
, with
x_{1}-c_{1}
and
x_{2}-c_{2}
in place of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, see
Remark

7.4
. Taking
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{2\rho}^{n-2})
to be a cut-off function with
\phi=1
on
B_{\rho}^{n-2}
and
|D^{2}\phi|\leq C(n)\rho^{-2}
we get

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\phi^{2}(z)(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}\,dz

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times B^{n-2}_{3/4}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)[(x_{1}-c_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-c_{2})^{2}]\Delta\phi^{2}+C\rho^{-2}(\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}).

The contribution of the bad set
B_{3/4}^{n-2}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta}
can be bounded using the soft height bound of
Lemma

5.4
and energy estimate on the bad set (item (iii) in
Proposition

6.4
), obtaining

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times(B^{n-2}_{3/4}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\eta})}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)[(x_{1}-c_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-c_{2})^{2}]\Delta\phi^{2}\leq C\rho^{-2}(\sigma^{2}+e^{-K/\varepsilon})\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}.

On the good set
\mathcal{G}^{\eta}
,
we apply
Lemma

A.2
to estimate the
second moment
of good slices as follows:

(8.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)[(x_{1}-c_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-c_{2})^{2}]\Delta\phi^{2}-\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}\varepsilon^{2}v_{0}\,\Delta\phi^{2}\right|\\
&\leq C\rho^{-2}(\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}^{1/2}+\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}|h-c|^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}^{1/2}+e^{-K\sigma/\varepsilon})\end{aligned}

(see also
Remark

A.3
),
where
h
is the Lipschitz approximation obtained in
Proposition

6.4
.
Note that the term containing
v_{0}
disappears once integrated on
B_{2\rho}^{n-2}
, as
v_{0}
is a constant and
\Delta\phi^{2}
has zero integral.

Combining the previous bounds, we arrive at

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B^{n-2}_{3/4}}\phi^{2}(z)(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}\,dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\rho^{-2}\int_{B_{2\rho}^{n-2}}|h-c|^{2}+C\rho^{-2}\left[(\sigma^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}+\left(1+\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\right)e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}^{1/2}\right].

Assuming
e^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
,
we now apply
Proposition

7.2
and
Lemma

8.1
.
Since
\|(h-c)-\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, we have

\int_{B_{2\rho}^{n-2}}|h-c|^{2}\leq 2\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+2\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}\int_{B_{2\rho}^{n-2}}|w|^{2}\leq 2\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(\rho^{4+(n-2)}+\delta^{2}\rho^{2+(n-2)}).

Thus, for some
C=C(n)
and
K=K(n,\sigma)
, we get

\displaystyle\rho^{2-n}\int_{B_{\rho}^{n-2}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(\rho^{-n}\nu+\rho^{2}+\delta^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+C\rho^{-n}\left[(\sigma^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}+\left(1+\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}\right)e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}^{1/2}\right].

We now choose
\eta,\rho
and,
subsequently
,
\delta,\sigma,\nu
to be small enough.
The claim follows (with the same plane
\overline{S}=\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
) once we assume that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
is small enough.

8.2.
Tilting the picture

In the general case, before applying the Caccioppoli-type estimate, we need to tilt the picture slightly to ensure that
|dw|(0)
is small enough.
We assume that
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
minimizes
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},\cdot)
.

Consider a rotation
R\in SO(n)
bringing
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
to the graph of the linear map
\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)
.
Since
w
is harmonic with the bound
\|dw\|_{L^{2}}\leq C
, we have
|\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}
. Hence, we can find
R
such that

(8.3)

\displaystyle\|R-I\|\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2},\quad\|(P_{\mathbb{R}^{n-2}}\circ R-I)\circ P_{\mathbb{R}^{n-2}}\|\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1},

for a dimensional constant
C=C(n)
:
indeed, calling
S
the graph of
\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)
, using the spectral theorem we can find an orthonormal basis
\{v_{3},\dots,v_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}

such that the vectors
P_{S}(v_{i})
form an orthogonal basis of
S
, so that
\langle P_{S}(v_{i}),v_{j}\rangle=\langle P_{S}(v_{i}),P_{S}(v_{j})\rangle=0

for
i\neq j
. Thus,
P_{\mathbb{R}^{n-2}}\circ P_{S}(v_{i})
is parallel to
v_{i}
and

\frac{P_{S}(v_{i})}{|P_{S}(v_{i})|}=\frac{(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)[v_{i}],v_{i})}{\sqrt{1+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}|dw(0)[v_{i}]|^{2}}}

(under the identification
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
).

We extend
\{v_{3},\dots,v_{n}\}
to an orthonormal basis
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The desired rotation is
obtained by sending
v_{i}
to
\frac{P_{S}(v_{i})}{|P_{S}(v_{i})|}
for
i\geq 3
and
v_{1},v_{2}
to suitable unit vectors
v_{1}+O(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}})
and
v_{2}+O(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}})
, obtained for instance via the Gram–Schmidt algorithm on the collection
\{\frac{P_{S}(v_{3})}{|P_{S}(v_{3})|},\dots,\frac{P_{S}(v_{n})}{|P_{S}(v_{n})|},v_{1},v_{2}\}
.
For
i\geq 3
, since
|P_{S^{\perp}}v_{i}|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}
we have
|P_{S}v_{i}|\geq 1-C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, and hence the previous formula gives

(8.4)

\displaystyle R(v_{i})=R(0,v_{i})=(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)[v_{i}],v_{i})+O(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})\quad\text{for }i\geq 3.

Then we define the rotated pair
(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})
as follows:

(8.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}:=R^{*}u,\quad\tilde{\nabla}:=R^{*}\nabla.

First we prove that the excess changes proportionally after this rotation.

Lemma 8.2

(Tilted excess estimate)
.

There exists a dimensional constant
C(n)
such that, for a pair
(u,\nabla)
as in
Theorem

1.4
with small enough
\tau_{0},\varepsilon_{0}>0
and a rotation
R
as above, the tilted excess is bounded by the initial excess; more precisely,

(8.6)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla},B_{1}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1},\quad\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla},B_{1}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

Proof.

Take an orthonormal basis
{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}}
for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\{e_{3},\dots,e_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
. Then, recalling the definition of the excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla},B_{1}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}^{n}}\left[\sum_{k=3}^{n}|\nabla_{Re_{k}}u|^{2}+\sum_{(j,k)\neq(1,2)}\varepsilon^{2}\omega(Re_{j},Re_{k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+C\|R-I\|^{2}E_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

\displaystyle\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}.

The second line above follows from the elementary bounds

|\nabla_{Re_{k}}-\nabla_{e_{k}}u|\leq\|R-I\||\nabla u|

and

|\omega(Re_{j},Re_{k})-\omega(e_{j},e_{k})|\leq 2\|R-I\||\omega|.

We estimate
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
in a similar way:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla},B_{1}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{n}_{1}}\left[|\nabla_{Re_{1}}u+i\nabla_{Re_{2}}u|^{2}+\left|\varepsilon\omega(Re_{1},Re_{2})-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}+C\|R-I\|^{2}E_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

\displaystyle\leq C(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}).

This is indeed the desired conclusion.
∎

Then we claim that the Lipschitz approximations
h
and
\tilde{h}
are approximately a rotation of one another. To do this, we first notice that the Lipschitz approximation
h_{0}
of the zero set in
Proposition

6.6
(applied with
\delta=\sigma
) behaves well under rotations: take
\tilde{h}_{0}
to be the function whose graph is obtained by rotating of the graph of
h_{0}
by
R^{-1}
(cf.
[
54
, Section 8.2]
).
For
z
in the domain of
\tilde{h}_{0}
, there exists
z^{\prime}\in B_{3/4}^{n-2}
such that

(\tilde{h}_{0}(z),z)=R^{-1}({h}_{0}(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime}).

Since
\|(P_{\mathbb{R}^{n-2}}\circ R-I)\circ P_{\mathbb{R}^{n-2}}\|\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
and
|h_{0}|\leq\sigma
, we have
|z^{\prime}-z|\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}\sigma
.
Moreover, we have
\operatorname{Lip}(h_{0})\leq\sigma
, giving
|h_{0}(z^{\prime})-h_{0}(z)|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}\sigma
.
Thus, assuming
\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}\leq\sigma
,

(\tilde{h}_{0}(z),z)=R^{-1}({h}_{0}(z),z)+O(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}\sigma);

recalling
Eq.

8.4
, we see that
R(0,z)=(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)[z],z)+O(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}|z|)
, so that

(8.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{0}(z)=h_{0}(z)-\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)[z]+O(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}\sigma),

with an implicit constant
C(n)
. Note that
\tilde{h}_{0}
can be taken as a Lipschitz approximation of the zero set of the tilted pair: in order to have the conclusion of
Proposition

6.6
, the only property that we care about is that its graph covers the zeros of
\tilde{u}
, except some exceptional ones projecting on a set of measure at most
C(n)\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}{\eta^{2}}
, and this holds for the rotated graph.

8.3.
Proof of the excess decay in the general case

Now we can use
Eq.

8.7
and the
L^{2}
bound from
Proposition

6.6
to conclude the proof of the tilt-excess decay theorem in the general case.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Recall that
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
minimizes
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{n},\cdot)
.
Let
\tilde{h}
be the Lipschitz approximation of the barycenter (built in
Proposition

6.4
) for the tilted pair
(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})
. We have

\displaystyle|\tilde{h}(z)-(h(z)-\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)[z])|\leq|\tilde{h}-\tilde{h}_{0}|+|h-h_{0}|+|\tilde{h}_{0}-(h_{0}-\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}}dw(0)[z])|.

We combine the main estimate of
Proposition

6.6
and
Eq.

8.6
–
Eq.

8.7
to see that

(8.8)

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{1/2}}|\tilde{h}(z)-(h(z)-\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2}dw(0)[z])|^{2}

(8.9)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\eta^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\right)\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+C\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}.

We assume in the sequel that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}},e^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1},

so that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{1/2}}|\tilde{h}(z)-(h(z)-\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2}dw(0)[z])|^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\eta^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\right)\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}.

Now, taking the harmonic approximation for the tilted pair to be
\tilde{w}
, we can see that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{1/2}}|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}^{1/2}\tilde{w}(z)-\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2}(w(z)-dw(0)[z])|^{2}\,dz

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B^{n-2}_{1/2}}[|h-\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2}w|^{2}+|\tilde{h}-\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}^{1/2}\tilde{w}|^{2}+|\tilde{h}(z)-(h(z)-\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2}dw(0)[z])|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\nu+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\eta^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\right)\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}

(the last line follows from
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, as we saw in
Eq.

8.6
).
Since

\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}^{1/2}\tilde{w}(z)-\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}^{1/2}(w(z)-dw(0)[z])

is harmonic,
its differential at the origin

\displaystyle|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}^{1/2}d\tilde{w}(0)|^{2}\leq C\left(\nu+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\eta^{2}}+\sigma^{2}\right)\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}.

Since
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}\geq\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
,
this tells us that
|d\tilde{w}(0)|
can be made arbitrarily small, reducing to the previous situation.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

In all the results obtained so far we were assuming that the center of the ball (or cylinder) belongs to the zero set, but actually they also hold if it belongs to the vorticity set
Z=\{|u|\leq\frac{3}{4}\}
, since this is enough to guarantee that it belongs to the support of the energy concentration measure in compactness arguments.

9.
Iteration arguments and Morrey-type bounds

9.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.9
: the case of critical pairs for
2\leq n\leq 4

We prove the following theorem, which is the first part of
Theorem

1.9
.

Theorem 9.1
.

For
2\leq n\leq 4
, there exists
\tau_{0}(n)>0
such that the following holds. If
(u,\nabla)
is an entire critical point for the energy
E_{1}
, given by
Eq.

3.1
for
\varepsilon=1
, with
u(0)=0
and the energy bound

(9.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}^{n}}\left[|\nabla u|^{2}+|F_{\nabla}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-|u|^{2})^{2}\right]\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0},

then
(u,\nabla)
is two-dimensional. More precisely,
we have
(u,\nabla)=P^{*}(u_{0},\nabla_{0})
up to a change of gauge, where
P
is the orthogonal projection onto a two-dimensional subspace and
(u_{0},\nabla_{0})
is the standard degree-one solution of Taubes
[
51
]
(or its conjugate), centered at the origin.

Proof.

We can assume
n\in\{3,4\}
.
First, we claim that it is enough to show that

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}R^{2}\min_{S}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S)=0.

Indeed, once this is done, we have

R^{4-n}\int_{B_{R}^{n}}\left[\sum_{a=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{a}^{R}}u|^{2}+\sum_{(a,b)\neq(1,2)}\omega(e_{a}^{R},e_{b}^{R})^{2}\right]\to 0

as
R\to\infty
, for a suitable choice of planes
S(R)
, where
\{e_{1}^{R},\dots,e_{n}^{R}\}

is an orthonormal basis such that
S(R)
is spanned by
\{e_{3}^{R},\dots,e_{n}^{R}\}
. Extracting a limit
S(R)\to S
along a subsequence and assuming without loss of generality that
S=\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, the fact that
n\leq 4
and Fatou’s lemma give

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\sum_{a=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{a}}u|^{2}+\sum_{(a,b)\neq(1,2)}\omega(e_{a},e_{b})^{2}\right]=0.

As in the proof of
Lemma

5.6
,
this implies that
(u,\nabla)
depends only on the first two coordinates up to a change of gauge,
and the conclusion follows from the classification of planar solutions by Taubes
[
51
]
.

We now turn to the previous claim.
By
Proposition

5.3
, we have
\frac{1}{|B_{R}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{R}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\to 2\pi

as
R\to\infty
, as well as

\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S(R))\to 0

for suitable oriented planes
S(R)
, up to conjugating the pair. Arguing as in the proof of
Proposition

5.3
,
we see that
S(R)
, viewed as an unoriented plane, has vanishing distance from any unoriented plane
S
minimizing
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S)
; hence, we can assume that
S(R)
minimizes
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
on
B_{R}^{n}
.

The proof now becomes an elementary iteration argument. In
Theorem

1.4
we first fix
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
C\rho^{2}\leq\rho
and then
\tau
and
\varepsilon_{0}
accordingly.
Let
C^{\prime}>\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}
.
Without loss of generality we can also assume that

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S(R))>0,\quad\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S(R))\in(0,1)

are small enough to allow applying
Theorem

1.4
on
B_{R}^{n}

(by rescaling our pair),
for all
R\geq C^{\prime}
. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
let us define
the minimum excess on each ball
B_{C^{\prime}\rho^{-k}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k):=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{C^{\prime}\rho^{-k}},S(C^{\prime}\rho^{-k})).

Then
Theorem

1.4
gives

(9.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{either }\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)&\leq{\rho}\overline{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{1}(k+1)\\
\text{or }\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)&\leq\max\{\rho^{2k}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k+1)|^{2}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k+1)},e^{-K\rho^{-2k}}\},\end{aligned}

where
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k):=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{C^{\prime}\rho^{-k}},S(C^{\prime}\rho^{-k}))
. By
Proposition

5.3
, we have

(9.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)=0,\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k)=0.

In order to iterate
Eq.

9.2
, we define

\displaystyle f(k):=\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)+2k\log\rho^{-1}

and

\displaystyle\quad g(k):=\max\left\{2\log|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k+1)|+\frac{1}{2}\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k+1),-K\rho^{-2k}+2k\log\rho^{-1}\right\}.

Then
Eq.

9.2
can be rewritten in terms of the functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

(9.4)

\displaystyle f(k)\leq f(k+1)-\lambda\quad\text{or}\quad f(k)\leq g(k),

where
\lambda:=3\log\rho^{-1}
. Condition
Eq.

9.3
also means that

(9.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f(k)-2k\log\rho^{-1}=-\infty,\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}g(k)=-\infty.

We claim that if
f,g
satisfy
Eqs.

9.4
and
9.5
then

\displaystyle f(k)\leq\sup_{m\geq k}[g(m)-\lambda(m-k)].

We prove this by contradiction: assume that there is some index
k_{0}
such that

(9.6)

\displaystyle f(k_{0})+\lambda(m-k_{0})>g(m)\quad\text{for all }m\geq k_{0}.

In particular we have
f(k_{0})>g(k_{0})
, so that
Eq.

9.4
and
Eq.

9.6
give

\displaystyle f(k_{0}+1)\geq f(k_{0})+\lambda>g(k_{0}+1).

By induction, we see that for all
m\geq k_{0}

\displaystyle f(m)\geq f(k_{0})+\lambda(m-k_{0}).

Taking the limit
m\to\infty
and noting that
\lambda>2\log\rho^{-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(k_{0})\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}[f(m)-\lambda(m-k_{0})]\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}[f(m)-2m\log\rho^{-1}]+2k_{0}\log\rho^{-1}=-\infty,

where we used
Eq.

9.5
in the last equality.
This is a contradiction, proving our claim.

As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle f(k)\leq\sup_{m\geq k}[g(m)-\lambda(m-k)]\leq\sup_{m\geq k}g(m).

Since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}g(k)=-\infty
by
Eq.

9.5
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f(k)=-\infty.

In other words, we have
\rho^{-2k}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)\to 0
, as desired.
∎

9.2.
Proof of
Corollary

1.6
and
Theorem

1.7

Given any
n\geq 3
and
(u,\nabla)
as in
Theorem

1.7
, for any
\tau_{0}^{\prime}>0

a standard compactness argument shows that

\frac{1}{|B_{r}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}^{\prime}

for all
x\in Z\cap B_{3/4}^{n}
and
r=\frac{1}{8}
, provided that
\tau_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
are taken small enough,
and hence also for
r\leq\frac{1}{7}
by energy monotonicity.

This, together with
Proposition

5.3
, implies that, for some oriented planes
S(x,r)
,

\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),S(x,r))\leq\delta

for some
\delta>0
to be chosen momentarily and
C(n,\delta)\varepsilon\leq r\leq\frac{1}{8}
.
As in the previous proof, we can assume that
S(x,r)
minimizes
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
on the ball
B_{r}(x)
.

Given
\alpha\in[0,1)
, we first fix
\rho
such that
C\rho^{2}\leq\rho^{2\alpha}
where
C
is the constant appearing in
Theorem

1.4
. We now choose
\delta,\tau_{0}
small such that
Theorem

1.4
applies on each ball
B_{r}(x)
with
x\in Z\cap B_{3/4}^{n}
, compare with
Remark

8.3
. We then consider

(9.7)

\max\{M\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{1/(1+\alpha)}\}\leq r\leq\frac{1}{8}

where
M
chosen large enough to ensure that

e^{-Kr/\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{r^{2}}

if
\varepsilon/r\leq 1/M
. Applying the scaled version of
Theorem

1.4
(with
\varepsilon
replaced by
\varepsilon/r
), and noticing that
\sup_{0<s\leq\delta}|\log\delta|^{2}\delta^{1/2}\leq 1
, we finally obtain that either

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{\rho r}(x),S(x,\rho r))\leq\rho^{2\alpha}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),S(x,r))

or

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r):=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),S(x,r))\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{r^{2}}\leq r^{2\alpha}.

This immediately implies

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r)\leq C(n,\alpha)r^{2\alpha}\quad\text{for all $x\in Z\cap B_{3/4}^{n}$ and any radii satisfying \lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{e:radii}.}

Moreover, if
S(x,r)
is different from
S(x,r^{\prime})
, for some
r^{\prime}\in[r,2r]
,
then we can find an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}

such that
\{e_{3},\dots,e_{n}\}
spans
S(x,r)
and
e_{2}
belongs to the span of the two planes,
with

e_{2}=v+w,\quad v\in S(x,r)\text{ and }w\in S(x,r^{\prime}),\quad|v|+|w|\leq C(n)\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{S(x,r^{\prime})}\|^{-1},

as the next simple lemma shows.

Lemma 9.2
.

Given two different planes
S,S^{\prime}\in\Gr(n,k)
, there exists a unit vector
e\in(S+S^{\prime})\cap S^{\perp}

such that
e=v+w
, with
v\in S
,
w\in S^{\prime}
, and
|v|,|w|\leq C(n)\|P_{S}-P_{S^{\prime}}\|^{-1}
.

Proof.

We can assume that
S+S^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S\cap S^{\prime}=\{0\}
(otherwise we work on
(S\cap S^{\prime})^{\perp}
), so that
n=2k
.
We can also assume without loss of generality that
\|P_{S}-P_{S^{\prime}}\|_{op}<c(n)
for a constant
c(n)>0
to be determined momentarily, since otherwise the
statement follows from an immediate compactness argument (using the fact that, if
S_{j}\to S_{\infty}
and
S_{j}^{\prime}\to S_{\infty}^{\prime}
, then each unit vector in
S_{\infty}+S_{\infty}^{\prime}
has vanishing distance from
S_{j}+S_{j}^{\prime}
, even when the former sum has smaller dimension).

It is elementary to check that the statement holds when
k=1
:
in this case, calling
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
the angle between the lines
S
and
S^{\prime}
,
we have
\|P_{S}-P_{S^{\prime}}\|=\sqrt{2}\sin\theta
, and we can find vectors as in the statement with
|v|,|w|\leq\frac{1}{\sin\theta}
.

Let
\tilde{e}
be an eigenvector of
P_{S}-P_{S^{\prime}}
, corresponding to an eigenvalue
\lambda
with
0<|\lambda|=\|P_{S}-P_{S^{\prime}}\|_{op}<c(n)
.
Then

P_{S}\tilde{e}-P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}=\lambda\tilde{e},

so that in particular
P_{S}\tilde{e},P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\neq 0
and

P_{S}P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}=(1-\lambda)P_{S}\tilde{e}.

Similarly we have

P_{S^{\prime}}P_{S}\tilde{e}=(1+\lambda)P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}.

From the equation

\langle P_{S^{\prime}}P_{S}\tilde{e},P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\rangle=\langle P_{S}\tilde{e},P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\rangle=\langle P_{S}\tilde{e},P_{S}P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\rangle

we deduce that

|P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}|^{2}=\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}|P_{S}\tilde{e}|^{2}.

In particular, calling
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
the angle between the vectors
P_{S}\tilde{e}
and
P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}
, these identities easily give

\sin^{2}\theta=1-\frac{\langle P_{S}\tilde{e},P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\rangle^{2}}{|P_{S}\tilde{e}|^{2}|P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}|^{2}}=\lambda^{2}.

We now take

Z:=\operatorname{span}\{P_{S}\tilde{e},P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\},

which is a two-dimensional plane. By the case
k=1
, we can find

e\in Z,\quad v\in\operatorname{span}\{P_{S}\tilde{e}\},\quad w\in\operatorname{span}\{P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}\}

such that
e\perp P_{S}\tilde{e}
is a unit vector and
|v|,|w|\leq\lambda^{-1}
.
In order to conclude, it suffices to check that
e\perp S
. Writing

e=\alpha P_{S}\tilde{e}+\beta P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e},

we have

P_{S}e=\alpha P_{S}\tilde{e}+\beta P_{S}P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e}=[\alpha+\beta(1-\lambda)]P_{S}\tilde{e}.

Since
e\perp P_{S}\tilde{e}
, we have

0=\langle\alpha P_{S}\tilde{e}+\beta P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e},P_{S}\tilde{e}\rangle=\alpha|P_{S}\tilde{e}|^{2}+\beta\langle P_{S}P_{S^{\prime}}\tilde{e},P_{S}\tilde{e}\rangle=[\alpha+\beta(1-\lambda)]|P_{S}\tilde{e}|^{2}.

Since
P_{S}\tilde{e}\neq 0
, we have
\alpha+\beta(1-\lambda)=0
, proving the claim.
∎

Since
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(x,r)\leq\delta
, we have

r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x)}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq C(n)\delta+C(n)r^{2-n}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{k}}u|^{2}+\sum_{(j,k)}\varepsilon^{2}\omega(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}\right].

Since the left-hand side is close to
2\pi
, and in particular larger than
\pi

(for
r\geq C\varepsilon
), using the previous fact from linear algebra for the term
\nabla_{e_{2}}u
we obtain

1\leq C(n)[\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r)+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r^{\prime})](\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{S(x,r^{\prime})}\|^{-2}+1),

and thus, since
\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{S(x,r^{\prime})}\|\leq C(n)
,

(9.8)

\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{S(x,r^{\prime})}\|\leq C(n)\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r)+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r^{\prime})}\leq C(n,\alpha)r^{\alpha}.

As a consequence, summing over dyadic scales, we have

\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{S(x,s)}\|\leq C(n,\alpha)\max\{r,s\}^{\alpha}

for
\max\{C(n,\alpha)\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{1/(1+\alpha)}\}\leq r,s\leq\frac{1}{8}
.

A similar argument works varying the center: for two different points
x,x^{\prime}\in Z\cap B_{3/4}^{n}
, looking at the balls
B_{r}(x)\subset B_{2r}(x^{\prime})
with
r:=|x-x^{\prime}|
, we also have

(9.9)

\displaystyle\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{S(x^{\prime},r)}\|\leq C(n,\alpha)r^{\alpha},

provided that
r=|x-x^{\prime}|\in[\max\{C(n,\alpha)\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{1/(1+\alpha)}\},\frac{1}{16}]

Actually, the previous proof gives some extra information, which will be crucial in the sequel.
We record it in the next proposition.

Proposition 9.3
.

Up to a rotation, we have
\|P_{S(x,r)}-P_{\mathbb{R}^{n-2}}\|\leq\gamma

\gamma>0

x\in Z\cap B_{3/4}^{n}

Let
C

C\rho^{2}\leq\rho

and

accordingly. Letting

9.8

\|P_{k+1}-P_{k}\|\leq C\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(x,r_{k})}.

∎

Proposition 9.4
.

For any

.

Proof of
Corollary

.

We have already seen in

We can then apply
Proposition

9.3
: for any
\gamma>0

\|P_{S(0,R)}-P_{S(0,R^{\prime})}\|\leq\gamma

for
R<R^{\prime}
large enough
(we use

We deduce that the limit

\lim_{R\to\infty}S(0,R)

exists.
∎

Proposition 9.5
.

Up to a rotation,
the vorticity set
\tilde{Z}:=Z\cap[B_{1/2}^{2}\times B_{1/2}^{n-2}]
is included in a
C(n,\gamma)\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the graph of a
C^{1}
map

f:B_{1/2}^{n-2}\to B_{\gamma}^{2}

with
\operatorname{Lip}(f)\leq\gamma
, if we assume that
\tau_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
are small enough (depending on

).

Proof.

Indeed, as seen in the proof of
Proposition

5.3
, for
\varepsilon
small enough
we have
u(\cdot,z)\neq 0
on
\partial B_{1/2}^{2}
, for all
z\in B_{1/2}^{n-2}
,
and the degree of

is
\pm 1
on this circle. Hence, each slice

intersects the zero set.

Moreover, using
Lemma

5.4
on

, we see that

\tilde{Z}\subseteq B_{\gamma}^{2}\times B_{1/2}^{n-2}
. Also,

5.4

implies that for all

and

we have

(9.10)

\displaystyle Z\cap B_{r}(x)\subseteq B_{\gamma r}(x+S(x,r)),

where

is the
\gamma r
-neighborhood of the affine plane

.
We now take a collection of points
\{z_{k}\}\subset B_{1/2}^{n-2}
with pairwise distance at least

and
B_{1/2}^{n-2}\subseteq\bigcup_{k}B_{5C(n,\gamma)\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{k})
. For each

\gamma

Hence, the assignment

-Lipschitz function

∎

We are now in position to prove

.

Proof of

1.7
.

Let
\eta>0
small such that

applies.
We first remark that the previous points

can be taken such that

and

,
we have

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{r}(x),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq c(n)\eta^{2}

and radii

We can apply this with

;

9.5
and exponential decay of energy away from

once we take

large enough, for any

Once we take

As a consequence of

, we deduce that

|h(z_{k})-y_{k}|=|h(z_{k})-h_{0}(z_{k})|\leq\varepsilon.

We immediately deduce that

\tilde{Z}
is included in a
C(n)\varepsilon
-neighborhood of the graph of

, which is the only consequence of the claim which we will use in the sequel.

Now let
\overline{\rho}:=\max\{M\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{1/(1+\alpha)}\}

(with

as in

9.7
) and
consider
another
finite collection of points
\{z_{k}\}\subset B_{1/2}^{n-2}
such that the balls
B_{\overline{\rho}}^{n-2}(z_{k})
are disjoint and the dilated balls
B_{4{\overline{\rho}}}^{n-2}(z_{k})
cover
B_{1/2}^{n-2}
.
Let
x_{k}=(y_{k},z_{k})
be a point in
\tilde{Z}
for each

.

On
B_{10\overline{\rho}}^{n}(x_{k})
we consider the Lipschitz approximation
h_{k}
built with respect to the rotated picture, obtained as a graph over
S_{k}:=x_{k}+S(x_{k},10{\overline{\rho}})
. When viewed as a graph over
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, it becomes a function
\tilde{h}_{k}
defined on the slightly smaller ball
B_{5{\overline{\rho}}}^{n-2}(z_{k})
.

By a scaled version of
Proposition

6.4
, we have

\int_{B_{30{\overline{\rho}}/4}^{n}(x_{k})\cap S_{k}}|dh_{k}|^{2}\leq C{\overline{\rho}}^{n-2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{10{\overline{\rho}}}^{n}(x_{k}),S(x_{k},10{\overline{\rho}}))\leq C{\overline{\rho}}^{n-2+2\alpha}.

In particular, by Poincaré,

\int_{B_{30{\overline{\rho}}/4}^{n}(x_{k})\cap S_{k}}|h_{k}-(h_{k})|^{2}\leq C{\overline{\rho}}^{n+2\alpha}.

Now, as in

8.7
, we observe that

|\tilde{h}_{k}(z)-h_{k}(z)-A_{k}(z)|\leq C{\overline{\rho}}\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{10{\overline{\rho}}}^{n}(x_{k}),S(x_{k},10{\overline{\rho}}))}\leq C{\overline{\rho}}^{1+\alpha}

for a suitable affine function
A_{k}

(where, with abuse of notation,
h_{k}(z)
means

composed with the isometry
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\to S_{k}
). Combining these two bounds, we get

\int_{B_{5{\overline{\rho}}}^{n-2}(z_{k})}|\tilde{h}_{k}-A_{k}^{\prime}|^{2}\leq C{\overline{\rho}}^{n+2\alpha}

.

\varphi_{k}
subordinated to the cover
\{B_{4\overline{\rho}}^{n-2}(z_{k})\}

and we let

Since the zero set is within a

-neighborhood of the graph of
\tilde{h}_{k}

(on the set

,

\int_{B_{2{\overline{\rho}}}^{n-2}(z)}|f-A_{z}|^{2}\leq C{\overline{\rho}}^{n+2\alpha}

for any

, for a suitable affine function
A_{z}
depending on

.

Thus, taking a standard mollifier

,

and using the previous bound, we deduce that

|dg-dA_{z}|\leq C\overline{\rho}^{\alpha}\quad\text{on }B_{\overline{\rho}}^{n-2}(z),

and in fact

[dg]_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{\overline{\rho}}^{n-2}(z))}\leq C.

Finally, recalling that

completing the proof of the

. Since

-neighborhood of the graph of

It is clear from the proof that we can actually make

up to decreasing

and
\varepsilon_{0}

∎

10.
Constructing competitors for local minimizers: a good gauge

In this section we prepare the ground to construct competitors for minimizers and to show that the full excess decays as long as it is above
\varepsilon^{\beta}
, for any
\beta>0
, giving a proof of
Theorem

1.10
. To investigate minimizers, we construct competitors with the same boundary conditions and compare the energies to show that the excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is effectively approximated by the Dirichlet energy of the harmonic approximation.

To do this, we need to construct competitors modeled on graphs in the interior and then glue them to the boundary condition, while controlling the error terms. We pullback the
\varepsilon
-rescaled degree-one solution along the graph of the Lipschitz approximation, as obtained in
Proposition

6.4
. Then we gauge fix in balls of size
\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|
and, using the estimates at that scale, we define a global gauge by a partition of unity. In this gauge we can interpolate between the intial pair and the new one with good estimates.

10.1.
The pullback pair

Here we introduce the pullback pair
(u_{f},\nabla_{f})
whose zero set is prescribed to be the graph of a Lipschitz function
f:B^{n-2}\rightarrow B^{2}_{1}
. We prove that the excess of these pairs are well approximated by the Dirichlet energy of
f
, as in the following proposition.

Proposition 10.1

(Constructing the pullback pair)
.

There exist
\eta_{0}(n),\varepsilon_{0}(n)>0
small enough with the following property. Given any
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and a Lipschitz function
f:B^{n-2}_{1}\rightarrow B^{2}_{1/2}
with
Lip(f)=\eta\leq\eta_{0}
, there exists a pair
(u_{f},\nabla_{f})
obeying the following estimate:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B_{1}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon}(u_{f},\nabla_{f})=|B^{n-2}_{1}|+(1+O(\eta^{2}))\int_{B^{n-2}_{1}}\frac{|df|^{2}}{2}+O(e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}),

with implicit constants
C(n)
.
Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle u_{f}^{-1}(0)=\operatorname{graph}(f).

Proof.

To construct
(u_{f},\nabla_{f})
we pull back the planar degree-one solution of Taubes
[
51
]
, via the map
Q_{\varepsilon}:B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

\displaystyle Q_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{(x_{1},x_{2})-f(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})}{\varepsilon}.

Then we define
(u_{f},\nabla_{f})
by

(10.1)

\displaystyle(u_{f},\nabla_{f}):=Q_{\varepsilon}^{*}(u_{0},\nabla_{0}),

where
(u_{0},\nabla_{0})
is the degree-one solution in
[
51
]
with
u_{0}(0)=0
(unique up to change of gauge). First, we note
that, since
dQ_{\varepsilon}(x)[e_{k}]=-\partial_{e_{k}}f_{1}(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})e_{1}-\partial_{e_{k}}f_{2}(x_{3},\dots,x_{n})e_{2}
, we have
the following identities for
k=3,\dots,n
:

(10.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |(\nabla_{f})_{k}u_{f}|^{2}(x)&=\varepsilon^{-2}|\partial_{e_{k}}f_{1}(\nabla_{0})_{e_{1}}u_{0}+\partial_{e_{k}}f_{2}(\nabla_{0})_{e_{2}}u_{0}|^{2}\left(Q_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\\
&=\varepsilon^{-2}\frac{|\partial_{k}f|^{2}}{2}|\nabla_{0}u_{0}|^{2}\left(Q_{\varepsilon}(x)\right),\end{aligned}

where we omitted the argument of
f
and we used the fact that
(\nabla_{0})_{e_{2}}u_{0}=i(\nabla_{0})_{e_{1}}u_{0}
for solutions of the vortex equations
Eq.

4.13
. We also have

(10.3)

\displaystyle|(\nabla_{f})_{e_{1}}u_{f}|^{2}(x)+|(\nabla_{f})_{e_{2}}u_{f}|^{2}(x)=\varepsilon^{-2}|\nabla_{0}u_{0}|^{2}\left(Q_{\varepsilon}(x)\right).

We also compute for the curvature term
-i\omega_{f}:=F_{\nabla_{f}}=F_{Q_{\varepsilon}^{*}(\nabla_{0})}=Q_{\varepsilon}^{*}(F_{\nabla_{0}})
that

(10.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1,2}\varepsilon^{2}\omega_{f}(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}(x)=\varepsilon^{-2}\omega_{0}(e_{1},e_{2})^{2}\left(Q_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)|\partial_{e_{j}}f|^{2}\quad\text{for $j=1,2$ and $k=3,\dots,n$},

and moreover

(10.5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\omega_{f}(e_{1},e_{2})^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}\omega_{0}^{2}(e_{1},e_{2})\left(Q_{\varepsilon}(x)\right),

as well as

(10.6)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\omega_{f}(e_{k},e_{\ell})^{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}|df|^{4}\omega_{0}(e_{1},e_{2})^{2}\left(Q_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\quad\text{for $3\leq k<\ell\leq n$}.

To compute
E_{\varepsilon}(u_{f},\nabla_{f})
, we use
Eq.

10.2
–
Eq.

10.6
to see that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{n-2}_{1}}\left[\int_{B^{2}_{1}}\varepsilon^{-2}e_{1}(u_{0},\nabla_{0})(Q_{\varepsilon}(x))\left(1+\frac{|df|^{2}}{2}+O(|df|^{4})\right)+O(e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}})\right]

\displaystyle=2\pi\left[|B^{n-2}_{1}|+\int_{B_{1}^{n-2}}\frac{|df|^{2}}{2}+O(|df|^{4})\right]+O(e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}).

In the above display we used the exponential decay from
[
36
, Chapter III, Theorem 8.1]
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{1/(2\varepsilon)}^{2}}e_{1}(u_{0},\nabla_{0})=O(e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}).

Recalling that
|df|\leq\eta
we get the desired estimate.
∎

10.2.
Constructing the interpolation gauge

In this section we find a gauge transformation
(u,\nabla)\mapsto(e^{i\xi}u,\nabla-id\xi)
for which the new pair is
L^{2}
-close to the pullback pair
(u_{h},\nabla_{h})
constructed in
Proposition

10.1
, where
h:B^{n-2}_{1}\rightarrow B^{2}_{1/2}
is the Lipschitz approximation built in
Proposition

6.4
. Since this is the most technical part of the paper, we provide an overview of the arguments.

Step 1.
We cover the vortex set with cylinders
\{B_{5C|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}^{2}(y_{k})\times B^{n-2}_{5C\varepsilon}(x_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}
with
x_{k}=(y_{k},z_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
such that
B^{n-2}_{5C\varepsilon}(z_{k})
is a Vitali cover of
B^{n-2}_{1}
. Then we name a cylinder
good
if the excess on it is small, and
bad
otherwise. We also define a partition of unity, subordinate to this cover, with derivatives at most
C\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Step 2.
In each cylinder we pass to the Coulomb gauge, via a function
\xi_{k}
with mean equal to the mean of
\theta_{h}-\theta
on an appropriate annulus away from the vortex set (note that
\theta-\theta_{h}
is well-defined far from the vortex set of both
u
and
u_{h}
) Then we use Gaffney- and Poincaré-type inequalities from
Appendix

B
to derive estimates for
e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}
and
(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}
, where we write
\nabla=d-i\alpha
. Far from the vorticity set we modify the gauge so that
e^{i\xi_{k}}u
and
u_{h}
have the same phase. Then we use the exponential decay away from the vortex set, which is where the error
\varepsilon^{\beta}
comes from; however, this will be enough to show the classification result in all dimensions, since we are free to take
\beta
arbitrarily large.

Step 3.
We use the estimates on
(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}
(and the mean condition) and Poincaré–Gaffney-type inequalities from
Appendix

B
to bound
\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}
on overlapping cylinders.

Step 4.
We patch together the
\xi_{k}
’s with the partition of unity defined in the first step to obtain the function
\xi
. Then we use the bounds on
\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}
to derive estimates on
(\alpha+d\xi)-\alpha_{h}
and
e^{i\xi}u-u_{h}
.

Proposition 10.2

(The interpolation gauge)
.

For any
\beta>0
there exist
\tau_{0}(n,\beta),\varepsilon_{0}(n,\beta)>0
and
C_{0}(n,\beta)>0
with the following property. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a critical pair for
E_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
u(0)=0
,
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, and the energy bound

\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{2}|}\int_{B_{2}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}.

Moreover, let
h:B_{1}^{n-2}\rightarrow B^{2}_{1/2}
be the Lipschitz approximation defined in
Proposition

6.4
(for a suitable
\eta
chosen later on) and let
(u_{h},\nabla_{h})
be the pullback pair constructed in
Proposition

10.1
. Then, on a given annulus

\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}:=B^{2}_{1}\times(B^{n-2}_{s+\delta}\setminus\overline{B}^{n-2}_{s})

with
\delta\in[C_{0}\varepsilon,\frac{1}{16}]
and
s\leq\frac{3}{4}
, we can find a gauge transformation

\displaystyle(u,\nabla)\mapsto(e^{i\xi}u,\nabla-id\xi),

via a smooth function
\xi:\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that:

(i)

letting
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
be the projection onto the last
n-2
coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi}u-u_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi)-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C(n,\beta)|\log\varepsilon|^{8}\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}[\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+\bm{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}]+\varepsilon^{\beta};

(ii)

letting

\displaystyle Z:=\{|u|\leq 3/4\},\quad Z_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}:=\{x=(y,z)\,:\,\operatorname{dist}(x,Z\cap(B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}))\leq C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|\},

the function
e^{i\xi}u\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
has the same phase as
u_{h}
far from the vortex set, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i\xi}u}{u_{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{5C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}.

Proof.

First we choose
\varepsilon_{0},\tau_{0}
small enough so that
Theorem

1.7
applies (for
\alpha=0
). We divide the proof into several steps.

Covering arguments and a partition of unity.
To begin with,
by
Theorem

1.7
, we can find a collection of points

\{x_{k}=(y_{k},z_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset Z\cap\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}

satisfying the following.

(i)

The projected collection
\{z_{k}=P(x_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset B^{n-2}_{s+\delta}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s}
gives a Vitali covering of the projected annulus
P(\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta})
:

(10.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &P(\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta})=B^{n-2}_{s+\delta}\setminus\overline{B}^{n-2}_{s}\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}),\\
&B_{C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{j})\cap B_{C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{k})=\emptyset\quad\text{for all }j\neq k.\end{aligned}

This last line shows in particular that
N\leq C(n)\varepsilon^{2-n}
, where by now
C_{0}
depends only on
n
.

(ii)

As a direct consequence of
Theorem

1.7
, we can guarantee that

(10.8)

\displaystyle Z_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}\cap\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}^{2}(y_{k})\times B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}).

(iii)

We say that a point
x_{k}=(y_{k},z_{k})
is a
good
point if

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\leq r\leq 10C_{0}\varepsilon}r^{2-n}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B^{2}_{1}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z_{k}),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\eta_{0}^{2},

where with a certain abuse of notation we have set

(10.9)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B^{2}_{1}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})=\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z)}\left[\sum_{k=3}^{n}|\nabla_{e_{k}}u|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{(j,k)\neq(1,2)}\omega(e_{j},e_{k})^{2}\right]

(note the absence of normalization)
Let the set of
good indices
be
G
. We also denote the set of bad ones by
B:=\{1,\dots,N\}\setminus G
.

(iv)

Again as a direct consequence of
Theorem

1.7
we get that

(10.10)

\displaystyle|u_{h}|,|u|>\frac{3}{4}\quad\text{on }\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}(B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\setminus B^{2}_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k}))\times B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}).

Since both
u
and
u_{h}
have degree
1
on each of the previous domains (up to conjugating
(u,\nabla)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
),
the
difference of phases

\theta-\theta_{h}
is well-defined on these domains.

(v)

We also define a partition of unity
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, subordinate to the cylinders:

(10.11)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}\in C^{1}_{c}(B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k})),\quad 0\leq\phi_{k}\leq 1,

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}=1\quad\text{on }Z_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}\cap\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}.

We also require that
|d\phi_{k}|\leq\tilde{C}\varepsilon^{-1}
for all
k=1,\dots,N
.

(vi)

Up to modifying the
\phi_{k}
’s, we can define
\phi_{0}\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|})
with
0\leq\phi_{0}\leq 1
and

\displaystyle\phi_{0}=1\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{5C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|},

\displaystyle\phi_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}=1\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}.

Lastly,
|d\phi_{0}|\leq\tilde{C}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|^{-1}
.

Note that
C_{0}>0
is some large enough constant that we are still free to choose later on (
\tilde{C}
above depends on
C_{0}
). In the sequel we also use the following notation for the excess on each ball:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k):=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{10C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}),\mathbb{R}^{n-2}).

We define
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k)=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}(k)
similarly. Since the balls
B^{n-2}_{C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k})
are disjoint,
the dilated balls have bounded overlap (i.e., at most
C(n)
of them intersect), giving

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k)\leq C\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z},

and the same holds for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
. With abuse of notation, we also write

(10.12)

|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k):=\begin{cases}\int_{B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k})}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}&\text{if }k\in G,\\
\int_{B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k})}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}&\text{if }k\in B.\end{cases}

Remark 10.3
.

We will often use the following observation implicitly.
For all
z\in B_{1}^{n-2}
, the results in the previous section show that

r^{2-n}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{2}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\tilde{\eta}_{0}^{2}

for an (arbitrarily) small
\tilde{\eta}_{0}
and all radii
\Lambda\varepsilon\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}
, for some
\Lambda

depending on
n,\tilde{\eta}_{0}
.
If
k\in G
then, for all
z\in B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{k})
,
we have

(C_{0}\varepsilon)^{{2-n}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{2}\times B_{C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq 10^{n-2}\eta_{0}^{2}.

As a consequence of
Lemma

5.7
, if we take
C_{0}\geq C(n)
large and
\eta_{0}^{2}
very small,
we have

r^{2-n}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{1}^{2}\times B_{r}^{n-2}(z),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq\tilde{\eta}_{0}^{2}

also for
r\in(0,\Lambda\varepsilon)
, since we have
C^{1}
control on the pair at this scale.
We now fix
\tilde{\eta}_{0}
such that we actually apply
Proposition

6.4
for
\eta:=\tilde{\eta}_{0}
,
so that
every
z\in B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{k})
gives a good slice
.

Gauge fixing on each small cylinder with bounds.
Fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and consider the unique solution
\xi_{k}
to the following Neumann boundary value problem:

(10.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta\xi_{k}=d^{*}(\alpha-\alpha_{h})&\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{k}:=B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}^{2}(y_{k})\times B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}),\\
\partial_{\nu}\xi_{k}=-(\alpha-\alpha_{h})(\nu)&\text{at }\partial\mathcal{C}_{k},\\
\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}[(\theta+\xi_{k})-\theta_{h}]=0&\text{where }\mathcal{A}_{k}:=(B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\setminus B^{2}_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k}))\times B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}).\end{cases}

Recall that
\theta-\theta_{h}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{A}_{k}
. Then we perform the following gauge transformation in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
:
writing
\nabla=d-i\alpha
, we transform

\displaystyle(u,\alpha)\mapsto(e^{i\xi_{k}}u,\alpha+d\xi_{k}).

Since
d^{*}[(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}]=0
and
[(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}](\nu)=0
, we can use the Gaffney–Poincaré-type inequality in
Lemma

B.1
:

(10.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}\leq C(n,C_{0})|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}|d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}|^{2}.

We bound separately the contributions of the good set and the bad set, using
Lemma

10.4
and
Lemma

10.5
below,
obtaining

(10.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{4}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)}+C\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}

for some
C=C(n,C_{0},\beta)=C(n,\beta)
.

Gauge fixing far from the vortex set with bounds.
Far from the vortex set, in the set
\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}
, we gauge fix via a function
\xi_{0}
such that
e^{i\xi_{0}}u/u_{h}
is real-valued. Hence, we define

(10.16)

\displaystyle\xi_{0}:=\theta_{h}-\theta\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|};

note that a priori
\xi_{0}
is well-defined only in the quotient
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, but this is enough to have a well-defined gauge transformation (in fact, since the vorticity set is included in a
C\varepsilon
-neighborhood of a graph, we can check that
\theta_{h}-\theta
is a well-defined real number).

We can estimate
e^{i\xi_{0}}u-u_{h}
and
(\alpha+d\xi_{0})-\alpha_{h}
in this domain using the exponential decay (
Proposition

4.5
), as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi_{0}}u-u_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi_{0})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}}[\varepsilon^{-2}||u|-|u_{h}||^{2}+2|\alpha-d\theta|^{2}+2|\alpha_{h}-d\theta_{h}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\varepsilon^{-2}e^{-K(n)C_{0}|\log\varepsilon|}.

In the last inequality, we used the following observation: since each slice intersects the zero set and
|\log\varepsilon|\geq C(n)
,
using
Lemma

5.7
we see that on
B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}
the distance from
\{|u|\leq 3/4\}
is comparable
with the distance from
(B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\})\cap\{|u|\leq 3/4\}
.

Taking
C_{0}
large enough, we see that

(10.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi_{0}}u-u_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi_{0})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\leq\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}.

Difference of local gauges in the overlap regions.
Fix
1\leq j<k\leq N
such that

\displaystyle\Omega_{j,k}:=\mathcal{C}_{j}\cap\mathcal{C}_{k}\neq\emptyset.

Notice that we can bound the
L^{2}
norm of the difference
d\xi_{k}-d\xi_{j}
as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j,k}}|d\xi_{j}-d\xi_{k}|^{2}\leq 2\int_{\mathcal{C}_{j}}|(\alpha+d\xi_{j})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}+2\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}.

By
Eq.

10.15
we then have

(10.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j,k}}|d(\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}(|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(j)}+|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)})+C\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}.

Our goal is to apply a Poincaré-type inequality on
\Omega_{j,k}
to estimate
\xi_{k}-\xi_{j}
. To this aim, we first look at
\xi_{j},\xi_{k}
on an appropriate annulus. By the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{j},\mathcal{C}_{k}
and the structure of the vortex set in
Theorem

1.7
we can see that
|x_{j}-x_{k}|\leq 20C_{0}\varepsilon
. We name the midpoint
x_{j,k}=(y_{j,k},z_{j,k}):=\frac{x_{j}+x_{k}}{2}
and we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{j,k}:=[B^{2}_{3C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{j,k})\setminus B^{2}_{2C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{j,k})]\times[B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{j})\cap B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k})]\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}_{k}\subseteq\Omega_{j,k}.

So we can compute that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{j,k}}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2}\leq 2\int_{\mathcal{A}_{j}}|(\theta+\xi_{j})-\theta_{h}|^{2}+2\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}|(\theta+\xi_{k})-\theta_{h}|^{2}.

We know that
(\theta+\xi_{j})-\theta_{h}
and
(\theta+\xi_{k})-\theta_{h}
have zero mean on
\mathcal{A}_{j}
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, respectively. Hence, we can apply
Lemma

B.3
on each annulus to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{j,k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathcal{A}_{j}}|d(\theta+\xi_{j})-d\theta_{h}|^{2}+C\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}|d(\theta+\xi_{k})-d\theta_{h}|^{2},

and we can bound

|d(\theta+\xi_{j})-d\theta_{h}|\leq|\alpha-d\theta|+|\alpha_{h}-d\theta_{h}|+|\alpha+d\xi_{j}-\alpha_{h}|.

As before, on
\mathcal{A}_{j}
we have

|\alpha-d\theta|^{2}+|\alpha_{h}-d\theta_{h}|^{2}\leq|u|^{-2}|\nabla u|^{2}+|u_{h}|^{-2}|\nabla_{h}u_{h}|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}

by exponential decay, and the same holds for
k
. Together with
Eq.

10.15
we thus estimate

(10.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{j,k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}(|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(j)}+|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)})+C\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}.

By
Eq.

10.18
–
Eq.

10.19
, using
Lemma

B.3
and
Remark

B.4
, we arrive at

(10.20)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j,k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}(|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(j)}+|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)})+\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}.

We also need to estimate the difference of
\xi_{k}-\xi_{0}
for all
1\leq k\leq N
. Defining

\displaystyle\Omega_{0,k}:=\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap[\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}\setminus Z_{C_{0}|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|}],

we see that

\displaystyle\Omega_{0,k}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}:=[B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\setminus B^{2}_{(C_{0}/2)\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})]\times B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k}).

Note that by
Eq.

10.16
we have
\xi_{0}=\theta_{h}-\theta
in
\Omega_{0,k}
. Hence, we can apply
Lemma

B.3
and compute that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\Omega_{0,k}}|\xi_{k}-\xi_{0}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}}|(\theta+\xi_{k})-\theta_{h}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}}|d(\theta+\xi_{k})-d\theta_{h}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}}[|\alpha-d\theta|^{2}+|\alpha_{h}-d\theta_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}],

where again we used the fact that
(\theta+\xi_{k})-\theta_{h}
has zero mean on
\mathcal{A}_{k}\subset\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}
.
Summing the previous bounds and noting that
there are at most
(C\varepsilon^{2-n})^{2}
pairs
(j,k)
, while
any point belongs to at most
C=C(n,C_{0})
domains
\Omega_{j,k}
, we arrive at

(10.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq N}\int_{\Omega_{j,k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C\bm{\mathrm{E}}+C\varepsilon^{\beta+1},

for some
C=C(n,\beta)
(recall
Eq.

10.12
).

Constructing the global gauge via the partition of unity
.
Recall the definition of the partition of unity in
Eq.

10.11
. We define the global gauge transformation function as follows:

(10.22)

\displaystyle\xi:=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{k}\xi_{k}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}.

Then we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}\varepsilon^{-2}\left|e^{i\xi}u-\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{k}e^{i\xi_{k}}u\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{k}|\xi-\xi_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j<k}\int_{\Omega_{j,k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C\bm{\mathrm{E}}+C\varepsilon^{\beta+1}.

In particular,

(10.23)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi}u-u_{h}|^{2}&\leq 2\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}\left[\varepsilon^{-2}\left|e^{i\xi}u-\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{k}e^{i\xi_{k}}u\right|^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{k}|e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{8}\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C\bm{\mathrm{E}}+C\varepsilon^{\beta+1},\end{aligned}

where we used
Lemma

10.4
and
Lemma

10.5
to estimate the term involving
e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}
.

Moreover, for the connection part, we can bound

(10.24)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}|(\alpha+d\xi)-\alpha_{h}|^{2}\\
&\leq 2\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N}\phi_{k}|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}+\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}d\phi_{k}\xi_{k}\right|^{2}\right].\end{aligned}

The first term is bounded by
Eq.

10.15
and
Eq.

10.17
.
We are left to bound the last term. Since the functions
\phi_{k}
form a partition of unity, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}d\phi_{k}(z)=0.

We can then write

\sum_{k=0}^{N}d\phi_{k}\xi_{k}=\sum_{j,k=0}^{N}\phi_{j}d\phi_{k}(\xi_{k}-\xi_{j}).

Since
|d\phi_{k}|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}
, the last term above is bounded by

C\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{j<k}\int_{\Omega_{j,k}}|\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}|^{2},

which is a quantity that we already estimated.
Combining these bounds, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi}u-u_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi)-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{8}\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}[\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+\bm{1}_{\mathcal{G}^{\eta}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}]+\varepsilon^{\beta}.

This is indeed the desired conclusion.
∎

We now turn to the bounds which were postponed in the previous proof.

Lemma 10.4
.

Assume that
k\in G
. Then

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{8}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k)+C\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}

and

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\eta^{-2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(k)+C\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}.

Proof.

We bound each part separately.

Estimating
d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}
.
Recalling the definition of
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
in
Eq.

10.9
and the construction of
\alpha_{h}
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha(e_{1},e_{2})-d\alpha_{h}(e_{1},e_{2})|^{2}+C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(k)+C\int_{B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{k})}|dh|^{2}.

We are going to use some estimates from
[
31
]
which are slightly more refined than

Eq.

4.14
. Compared to the main result of
[
31
]
, these hold under some additional assumptions, which are however satisfied on good slices:
in particular, for any
z\in B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}^{n-2}(z_{k})
, the function
u
vanishes linearly at a unique point along the slice
B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}
. We will often compare
u
with the function

u_{h_{0}}
, where
h_{0}
is the function built in
Proposition

6.6
, whose graph approximates the zero set; along the good slice, this function vanishes at the same point as
u
,
and is just a translation of the standard degree-one planar solution.

Specifically, using an
\varepsilon
-rescaling of
[
31
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
31
, Corollary 3.3]

(applied with
N=1
; see also the part below
[
31
, Corollary 3.3]
for the choice of functions to which this result is applied),
we have the following estimate:

(10.25)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}[\varepsilon^{-2}||u|-|u_{h_{0}}||^{2}+|u_{h_{0}}|^{2+1/2}|(\alpha-d\theta)_{(1,2)}-(\alpha_{h_{0}}-d\theta_{h_{0}})_{(1,2)}|^{2}\\
&\phantom{\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}[}+\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha(e_{1},e_{2})-d\alpha_{h_{0}}(e_{1},e_{2})|^{2}]\\
&\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z},\end{aligned}

for an absolute constant
C
, where the subscript
(1,2)
means that we restrict the one-form along the slice.

Now by the construction in
Proposition

10.1
we can see that
(u_{h},\alpha_{h})
, along the slice
B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}
, is equal to
(u_{h_{0}},\alpha_{h_{0}})
translated to vanish at the barycenter
\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}(z)
. As shown in
Lemma

A.1
,
the translation is by a vector
v
with
|v|\leq C\varepsilon|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}}+e^{-K/\varepsilon}
. By the mean value theorem, we then have

(10.26)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}^{2}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}[\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha_{h}(e_{1},e_{2})-d\alpha_{h_{0}}(e_{1},e_{2})|^{2}+|(\alpha_{h}-\alpha_{h_{0}})_{(1,2)}|^{2}\\
&\phantom{\int_{B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}^{2}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}[}+\varepsilon^{-2}|u_{h}-u_{h_{0}}|^{2}]\\
&\leq C\varepsilon^{2}|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\cdot|v|^{2}\cdot C\varepsilon^{-4}\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+e^{-K/\varepsilon},\end{aligned}

since
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}
is bounded on good slices and the differential of each quantity
(such as
\varepsilon d\alpha_{h}(e_{1},e_{2})
and so on) is bounded by
C\varepsilon^{-2}
.
The claimed estimate follows by combining the previous bounds (together with item (iv) from
Proposition

6.4
, which gives
|dh|^{2}(z)\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+e^{-K/\varepsilon}
).

Estimating
e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}
.
Writing formally
u=|u|e^{i\theta}
and using a similar notation for
u_{h}
and
u_{h_{0}}
, recall that on the annulus

\mathcal{A}_{k,z}:=[B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\setminus B^{2}_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})]\times\{z\}

the differences
\theta-\theta_{h}
,
\theta-\theta_{h_{0}}
and
\theta_{h}-\theta_{h_{0}}
are well-defined.
We record the following estimate:

(10.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k,z}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\theta_{h}-\theta_{h_{z}}|^{2}\leq C|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}.

This holds again by the mean value theorem, since
|(d\theta_{h})_{(1,2)}|(y)\leq C|y-y_{k}|^{-1}
.
We are going to use the Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg-type inequality from
Lemma

B.2
, which implies that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}^{2}(y_{k})}|y-h_{0}(z)|^{2}|f(y)|^{2}\leq CR^{3/2}\int_{B_{R}^{2}(y_{k})}|y-h_{0}(z)|^{2+1/2}|df(y)|^{2},\quad\text{for $f\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{R}^{2}(y_{k}))$},

with
R:=5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|
(since there exists a biLipschitz transformation sending
B_{R}^{2}(y_{k})
to itself and mapping the origin to
h_{0}(z)
).
Recalling that the standard degree-one solution vanishes linearly at the origin, by the construction of
u_{h_{0}}
in
Proposition

10.1
we have

\displaystyle C^{-1}\leq\frac{|u_{h_{0}}|(y,z)}{\min\{\varepsilon^{-1}|y-h_{0}(z)|,1\}}\leq C

on the good slice,
for some universal constant
C
. Moreover,

\displaystyle 1\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{-1}|y-h_{0}(z)|}{\min\{\varepsilon^{-1}|y-h_{0}(z)|,1\}}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|\quad\text{for all $y\in B_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}^{2}(y_{k})$}.

Hence, given a
C^{1}
function
f
on
B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}

vanishing near the boundary, we can write

(10.28)

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}|u_{h_{0}}|^{2}|f|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}|u_{h_{0}}|^{2+1/2}|df|^{2}.

To estimate
ue^{i\xi_{k}}-u_{h_{0}}
, we first notice that
u
and
u_{h_{0}}
have the same unique zero point (with the same degree around it), and hence the difference
\theta-\theta_{h_{0}}
gives a well-defined smooth function on the full slice.

We define a cut-off
\chi:B^{2}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\chi=1
on
B^{2}_{C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})
and
\chi=0
outside of
B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})
, with
|d\chi|\leq C|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|^{-1}
. Then we use the first term of
Eq.

10.25
to bound
|u|-|u_{h_{z}}|
and
Eq.

10.28
to see that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}\chi^{2}|e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h_{0}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}\chi^{2}\left[\frac{\left||u|-|u_{h_{0}}|\right|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{|u_{h_{0}}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}|(\theta+\xi_{k})-\theta_{h_{0}}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C(\bm{\mathrm{I}}+\bm{\mathrm{II}}),

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle:=|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}|u_{h_{0}}|^{2+1/2}\chi^{2}|d(\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h_{0}})_{(1,2)}|^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle:=|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}|u_{h_{0}}|^{2+1/2}|d\chi|^{2}|\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h_{0}}|^{2}.

First we estimate
\bm{\mathrm{I}}
using the second term in
Eq.

10.25
and
Eq.

10.26
(to replace
\alpha_{h_{0}}
with
\alpha_{h}
):

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{6}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{B^{2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|}(y_{k})\times\{z\}}\chi^{2}|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}.

Then we estimate
\bm{\mathrm{II}}
: we note that
|d\chi|
is supported in
\mathcal{A}_{k,z}
and
|d\chi|\leq C|\varepsilon\log\varepsilon|^{-1}
, and hence we can use
Eq.

10.27
to estimate

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k,z}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h}|^{2}.

Putting
\bm{\mathrm{I}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{II}}
together and integrating over
B^{n-2}_{5C_{0}\varepsilon}(z_{k})
, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h_{0}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{6}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)}+C|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}|\alpha+d\xi_{k}-\alpha_{h}|^{2}+C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{8}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)}+C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{\beta+3n},

where we used
Eq.

10.15
(which uses only the previous bound on
d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}
).
Recalling that we imposed

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}(\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h})=0,

we can apply
Lemma

B.3
(suitably rescaled) and
Eq.

10.15
another time to see that

(10.29)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\varepsilon^{-2}|\theta+\xi_{k}-\theta_{h}|^{2}\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}|d(\theta+\xi_{k})-d\theta_{h}|^{2}\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}[|\alpha-d\theta|^{2}+|\alpha_{h}-d\theta_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi_{k})-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{6}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}{(k)}+\varepsilon^{\beta+3n}\end{aligned}

up to taking
C_{0}
large enough (the last inequality follows from the exponential decay of energy);
combining these bounds with
Eq.

10.26
,
we get the desired bound for
e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}
.
∎

Lemma 10.5
.

For
k\in B
we have

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}{(k)}.

Proof.

On the bad set we simply use
L^{\infty}
bounds: we have

(10.30)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi_{k}}u-u_{h}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|d\alpha-d\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\varepsilon^{n-2}\\
&\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}{(k)},\end{aligned}

where we used the definition of bad index in the last inequality.
∎

Corollary 10.6
.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}_{s,\delta}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|e^{i\xi}u-u_{h}|^{2}+|(\alpha+d\xi)-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\leq C(n,\beta)|\log\varepsilon|^{10}\int_{P(\mathcal{A}_{s-\delta,3\delta})}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+\varepsilon^{\beta}.

Proof.

Recalling that for a good
z
the sliced excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}
is small,
it suffices to split the integral of
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}
on the two sets
\{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\leq\varepsilon^{\beta+1}\}

and
\{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}>\varepsilon^{\beta+1}\}
. On the second one, we bound
|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{2}
,
while on the first one we have
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{\beta+1}|\log\varepsilon|^{2}
.
∎

11.
Proof of a stronger decay of tilt-excess for local minimizers

11.1.
Strong approximation of the excess for minimizers

In this section we use variational arguments and the estimates from
Proposition

10.2
to construct competitors. As a consequence, we prove that the full excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is well approximated by the Dirichlet energy of a harmonic approximation
w
built as in
Proposition

7.2
.

Proposition 11.1

(Strong harmonic approximation of minimizers)
.

For any
\nu,\beta>0
and any radius
0<s<1
there exist small constants
\varepsilon_{0}(n,s,\nu,\beta),\tau_{0}(n,s,\nu,\beta),\eta_{0}(n,\nu,\beta)>0
with the following properties. Let
(u,\nabla)
be a minimizer of
E_{\varepsilon}
defined on
B^{n}_{2}(0)
, with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}

and the energy bound

\frac{1}{|B_{2}^{n-2}|}\int_{B_{2}^{n}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0}.

After a suitable rotation, let
h:B^{n-2}_{1}\to B^{2}_{1}
be the Lipschitz approximation defined in
Proposition

6.4
with
\eta:=\eta_{0}
. Then the following holds,
assuming

Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq C\varepsilon^{\beta}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}:=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B^{n}_{2},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})

for some
C=C(n,\nu,\beta)
and
K=K(n)
:
there exists a harmonic function
w:B_{1}^{n-2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(i)

\int_{B_{1}^{n-2}}|dw|^{2}\leq C(n)
;

(ii)

we have

\int_{B_{1}^{n-2}}\left|\frac{h-(h)_{B_{1}^{n-2}}}{\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}}-w\right|^{2}\leq\nu,

where
(h)_{B_{1}^{n-2}}
is the average;

(iii)

most importantly, we have

\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

Proof.

We prove the statement by compactness and contradiction. Fix
\nu,\beta,s
and assume that there exist sequences
\varepsilon_{k},\tau_{k}\to 0
and a sequence of minimizers
(u_{k},\nabla_{k})
for
E_{\varepsilon_{k}}
with the previous energy bound for
\tau_{0}=\tau_{k}
, violating the conclusion. Moreover, let
h_{k}:B^{n-2}_{1}\to B^{2}_{1}
be the Lipschitz approximation for the threshold
\eta_{0}
, to be chosen below.

Lower bound on the energy of the given pair.
By item (i) in
Proposition

6.4
we have

\displaystyle\int_{B^{n-2}_{1}}|dh_{k}|^{2}\leq C(n)\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)},\quad\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}:=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u_{k},\nabla_{k},B^{n}_{2},\mathbb{R}^{n-2}),

for
k
large enough.
Hence, up to a subsequence, we can extract a weak limit

\frac{h_{k}-(h_{k})_{B_{1}^{n-2}}}{\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}}}\rightharpoonup w

in
W^{1,2}
, so that

\int_{B_{1}^{n-2}}|dw|^{2}\leq C(n).

By
Lemma

7.1
and
Proposition

7.2
,
w
is harmonic with
w(0)=0
.
This shows that the first two conclusions hold, so we must have

(11.1)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}^{(k)}>\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}.

By
Lemma

5.8
and the bound
Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\nu}{5}\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}
, this gives

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{s}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon_{k}}(u_{k},\nabla_{k})>|B_{s}^{n-2}|+\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\frac{4\nu}{5}\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}.

Let
a,b\in(s,1)
with
a<b
, which we write as
b=a+4\delta
.
Calling
\mathcal{G}^{k}
the good set for
(u_{k},\nabla_{k})
,
since the indicator function
\bm{1}_{(B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s})\cap\mathcal{G}^{k}}\to 1

strongly
L^{2}(B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s})
, we have

\bm{1}_{(B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s})\cap\mathcal{G}^{k}}\frac{dh_{k}}{\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}}}\rightharpoonup dw

weakly in this space, and hence

\int_{B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{(B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s})\cap\mathcal{G}^{k}}\frac{|dh_{k}|^{2}}{2\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}}.

Using item (iv) in
Proposition

6.4
and the assumption
\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}\geq C\varepsilon_{k}^{\beta}\geq Ce^{-K/\varepsilon_{k}}
, we deduce

\int_{B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{(B^{n-2}_{a}\setminus B^{n-2}_{s})\cap\mathcal{G}^{k}}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}^{(k)}}{\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}}.

Combined with
Eq.

11.1
, this gives

\int_{B^{n-2}_{a}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}^{(k)}>\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}\int_{B_{a}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\frac{3\nu}{4}\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}.

Using again
Lemma

5.8
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}\geq C\varepsilon_{k}^{\beta}\geq Ce^{-K/\varepsilon_{k}}
, we obtain

(11.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{a}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon_{k}}(u_{k},\nabla_{k})>|B_{a}^{n-2}|+\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}\int_{B_{a}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\frac{3\nu}{5}\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}.

Note that, for a fixed small
\delta>0
to be specified later, we can find
a
and
b=a+4\delta
in
(s,1)
such that

(11.3)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a}^{n-2}}[|dh_{k}|^{2}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}|dw|^{2}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}^{(k)}]\leq C(n,s)\delta\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)},

along a subsequence, by the classical pigeonhole argument.

Now we take a cut-off function
\chi
such that
\chi=1
on
B_{a}^{n-2}
and
\chi=0

outside of
B_{a+\delta}^{n-2}
, and we let

f_{k}:=(1-\chi)h_{k}+\chi(\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}}w+(h_{k})_{B_{1}^{n-2}}).

Since
\|h_{k}-(h_{k})_{B_{1}^{n-2}}-\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}}w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=o(\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)})
,
the Dirichlet energy of
f_{k}
on
B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a}^{n-2}
is

\int_{B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a}^{n-2}}\left[(1-\chi)^{2}\frac{|dh_{k}|^{2}}{2}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}(2\chi-\chi^{2})\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}\right]+o(\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}).

In particular, by
Eq.

11.3
we have

(11.4)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a}^{n-2}}|df_{k}|^{2}\leq C\delta\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}.

We apply
Proposition

10.1
to obtain a new pair
(u_{f_{k}},\nabla_{f_{k}})
.

Construction of the competitor.
We want to glue the latter to
(u_{k},\nabla_{k})
in a suitable annular region
and obtain a new pair whose energy in
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{b}^{n-2}
is strictly lower than
(u_{k},\nabla_{k})
, obtaining a contradiction to minimality.
From now on, we restrict attention to the region
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{b}^{n-2}
.
We will also drop the subscript
k
in the sequel. Note that
f=h
on
B_{a+4\delta}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a+\delta}^{n-2}
.

For technical reasons, it will be convenient to glue on an annulus of width
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
We first select
t\in[a+2\delta,a+3\delta]
such that

(11.5)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{t+2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2}\setminus B_{t-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2}}[\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+|dh|^{2}]\leq C(n,s,\delta)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

We first apply
Proposition

10.2
and
Corollary

10.6
to replace
(u,\nabla)

with a gauge-equivalent pair, still denoted
(u,\nabla)
,
such that
\frac{u}{|u|}=\frac{u_{f}}{|u_{f}|}
on
(B_{1}^{2}\setminus B_{1/2}^{2})\times B_{b}^{n-2}
, with

\int_{\mathcal{A}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|u-u_{h}|^{2}+|\alpha-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\leq C|\log\varepsilon|^{10}\int_{P(\hat{\mathcal{A}})}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+\varepsilon^{\beta},

where
\mathcal{A}:=B_{1}^{2}\times(B_{t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2}\setminus B_{t}^{n-2})

and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}:=B_{1}^{2}\times(B_{t+2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2}\setminus B_{t-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2})
.
In particular, by
Eq.

11.5
we have

(11.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}[\varepsilon^{-2}|u-u_{h}|^{2}+|\alpha-\alpha_{h}|^{2}]\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\log\varepsilon|^{10}\int_{P(\hat{\mathcal{A}})}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+\varepsilon^{\beta}=o(\bm{\mathrm{E}})+\varepsilon^{\beta},

where the notation
o(\bm{\mathrm{E}})=o(\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)})
indicates a quantity infinitesimal with respect to
\bm{\mathrm{E}}^{(k)}
, as
k\to\infty
.

We take another cut-off function
\varphi
with
\varphi=1
on
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{t}^{n-2}

and
\varphi=0
outside of
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2}
.
On
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{b}^{n-2}
, we define

\tilde{u}:=(1-\varphi)u+\varphi u_{h},\quad\tilde{\alpha}:=(1-\varphi)\alpha+\varphi\alpha_{h}.

We claim that

(11.7)

\displaystyle\int_{P(\mathcal{A})}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{z}\leq o(\bm{\mathrm{E}})+C\varepsilon^{\beta}.

Once this is done, using
Lemma

5.8
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{P(\mathcal{A})}e_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})\leq|P(\mathcal{A})|+o(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z})+C\varepsilon^{\beta},

and hence by
Proposition

10.1
, together with
Eq.

11.3
and
Eq.

11.4
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{b}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})

\displaystyle\leq|B_{b}^{n-2}|+(1+O(\eta_{0}^{2}))\int_{B_{b}^{n-2}}\frac{|df|^{2}}{2}+\int_{B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a}^{n-2}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+o(\bm{\mathrm{E}})+C\varepsilon^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq|B_{b}^{n-2}|+\bm{\mathrm{E}}\int_{B_{a}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\int_{B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{a}^{n-2}}[|df|^{2}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}]+C\eta_{0}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{E}}+o(\bm{\mathrm{E}})+C\varepsilon^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq|B_{b}^{n-2}|+\bm{\mathrm{E}}\int_{B_{a}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\nu}{5}\bm{\mathrm{E}},

once we take
\eta_{0}
and
\delta
small enough.
In the same way,
Eq.

11.2
gives

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}^{2}\times B_{b}^{n-2}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)>|B_{b}^{n-2}|+\bm{\mathrm{E}}\int_{B_{a}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\frac{2\nu}{5}\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

This gives a contradiction: near
\partial B_{1}^{2}\times B_{t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2}
,
using the fact that
\tilde{u}
and
u_{f}
have the same phase,
it is easy to modify
(\tilde{u},\tilde{\nabla})
in order to make it agree with

(u,\nabla)
(while this already holds on
\partial B_{1}^{2}\times(B_{b}^{n-2}\setminus B_{t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{n-2})
),
in a way which changes the energy by
O(e^{-K/\varepsilon})\leq\frac{\nu}{5}\bm{\mathrm{E}}
:
it is enough to interpolate between the two pairs on the set

(B_{1}^{2}\setminus B_{1/2}^{2})\times B_{b}^{n-2},

using the fact that here the energy density is exponentially small,
and hence we can write
|\tilde{u}-u_{f}|=|(1-|\tilde{u}|)-(1-|u_{f}|)|\leq e^{-K/\varepsilon}
and
|\tilde{\alpha}-\alpha_{f}|\leq e^{-K/\varepsilon}
(since, writing
\tilde{u}=e^{i\tilde{\theta}}
, we have

|d\tilde{\theta}-\tilde{\alpha}|\leq|\tilde{u}|^{-1}|\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{u}|
and similarly
|d\tilde{\theta}-\alpha_{f}|=|d\theta_{f}-\alpha_{f}|\leq|u_{f}|^{-1}|\nabla_{f}u_{f}|
).

Bounding the energy on the interpolation annulus.
It remains to check the previous claim.
We first write

\displaystyle 2\pi\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}_{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}\sum_{j\geq 3}|\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{j}}\tilde{u}|^{2}+\sum_{(j,j^{\prime})\neq(1,2)}\varepsilon^{2}|d\tilde{\alpha}(e_{j},e_{j^{\prime}})|^{2}+|\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\tilde{u}+i\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\tilde{u}|^{2}+\left|\varepsilon d\tilde{\alpha}(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|\tilde{u}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=:\bm{\mathrm{I}}+\bm{\mathrm{II}}+\bm{\mathrm{III}}+\bm{\mathrm{IV}}.

We start by bounding
\bm{\mathrm{II}}
:
we have

d\tilde{\alpha}=(1-\varphi)d\alpha+\varphi d\alpha_{h}+d\varphi\wedge(\alpha_{h}-\alpha).

Hence, using the fact that
|d\varphi|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1/2}
, we have

\bm{\mathrm{II}}\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C|dh|^{2}(z)+C\varepsilon^{2}\cdot C\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}|\alpha_{h}-\alpha|^{2}.

The last term is bounded by the left-hand side of
Eq.

11.6
; together with
Eq.

11.5
, this gives the desired bound.

As for
\bm{\mathrm{IV}}
, we note that

\frac{1-|\tilde{u}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}=(1-\varphi)\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+\varphi\frac{1-|u_{h}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon^{-1}|u-u_{h}|),

and hence
\bm{\mathrm{IV}}
is the squared norm of

(1-\varphi)\left[\varepsilon d\alpha(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right]+\varphi\left[\varepsilon d\alpha_{h}(e_{1},e_{2})-\frac{1-|u_{h}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right]+O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\alpha-\alpha_{h}|)+O(\varepsilon^{-1}|u-u_{h}|).

Thus, we have

\bm{\mathrm{IV}}\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C\varepsilon\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}|\alpha-\alpha_{h}|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}|u-u_{h}|^{2},

again bounded by
Eq.

11.5
and
Eq.

11.6
.

We finally turn to
\bm{\mathrm{I}}
; the bound for
\bm{\mathrm{III}}

is obtained in the same way and hence will be skipped.
We note that

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=d[(1-\varphi)u+\varphi u_{h}]-i[(1-\varphi)u+\varphi u_{h}][(1-\varphi)\alpha+\varphi\alpha_{h}]

\displaystyle=(1-\varphi)\nabla u+\varphi\nabla_{h}u_{h}+(u_{h}-u)d\varphi+O(|u-u_{h}||\alpha-\alpha_{h}|).

Hence,

\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}|\tilde{\nabla}_{e_{j}}\tilde{u}|^{2}\leq C\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}+C|dh|^{2}(z)+C\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}|u-u_{h}|^{2}+C\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times\{z\}}|\alpha-\alpha_{h}|^{2}.

Again, the last two terms are bounded by the left-hand side of
Eq.

11.6
.
This completes the proof of
Eq.

11.7
, and hence the proof of the proposition.
∎

11.2.
Proof of
Theorem

1.10

In this section we finish the proof of the stronger decay of excess for minimizers. We rescale
B_{1}^{n}(0)
to
B_{2}^{n}(0)
and
apply
Proposition

11.1
, with some
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\nu>0
to be chosen later
and with
\beta+1
in place of
\beta
.
We obtain that either
\bm{\mathrm{E}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{2}^{n}(0),\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq C\varepsilon^{\beta+1}
or
the conclusions of
Proposition

11.1
hold true
(provided that the picture is rotated in such a way that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is small enough).

In the first situation, we clearly have

\min_{S}\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{2}^{n},S)\leq\varepsilon^{\beta}
for
\varepsilon
small enough and we are done.
Hence, in the sequel, we can assume that we are in the second situation.

We will assume for simplicity that
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
minimizes
\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{2}^{n},\cdot)
and that

(11.8)

|dw(0)|\leq\delta

with
\delta>0
to be chosen momentarily.

Since
|dw(z)-dw(0)|\leq s\sup_{B_{s}^{n-2}}|D^{2}w|
on
B_{s}^{n-2}
, we have

\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}|dw|^{2}\leq C(n)s^{n-2}\delta^{2}+C(n)s^{n}\sup_{B_{s}^{n-2}}|D^{2}w|^{2}\leq C(n)s^{n-2}(\delta^{2}+s^{2}),

where the last inequality comes from the bound
\|dw\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(n)
and standard elliptic estimates.

By item (iii) from
Proposition

11.1
we then have

\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\leq\bm{\mathrm{E}}\int_{B_{s}^{n-2}}\frac{|dw|^{2}}{2}+\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}\leq C(n)s^{n-2}(\delta^{2}+s^{2}+s^{2-n}\nu)\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

This immediately gives

\bm{\mathrm{E}}(u,\nabla,B_{s}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n-2})\leq C(n)(\delta^{2}+s^{2}+s^{2-n}\nu)\bm{\mathrm{E}}.

The theorem follows under the assumption (
11.8
) by taking
\delta,s
and
subsequently

\nu
small enough.
The general case can be reduced to this one by the very same argument of Section
8.2
;
the only differences here are that we use item (ii) from
Proposition

11.1
in order to bound

\|h-(h)_{B_{1}^{n-2}}-\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}}w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\nu\bm{\mathrm{E}}

and that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
is replaced by
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
throughout that argument.

11.3.
Proof of
Theorem

1.9
: the case of minimizers

We prove the following theorem, which contains the second part of
Theorem

1.9
.

Theorem 11.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, there exists
\tau_{0}(n)>0
such that the following holds. If
(u,\nabla)
is an entire, local minimizer for the energy
E_{1}
, with
u(0)=0
and the energy bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B^{n-2}_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}^{n}}\left[|\nabla u|^{2}+|F_{\nabla}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-|u|^{2})^{2}\right]\leq 2\pi+\tau_{0},

then
(u,\nabla)
is two-dimensional. More precisely,
we have
(u,\nabla)=P^{*}(u_{0},\nabla_{0})
up to a change of gauge, where
P
is the orthogonal projection onto a two-dimensional subspace and
(u_{0},\nabla_{0})
is the standard degree-one solution of Taubes
[
51
]
(or its conjugate), centered at the origin.

Proof.

We can assume
n\geq 3.

We proceed exactly as in the proof of
Theorem

1.9
: letting
\beta:=n-2>0
,
it is enough to prove that

\lim_{R\to\infty}R^{\beta}\min_{S}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}(u,\nabla,B_{R}^{n},S)=0.

This follows from the stronger excess decay statement for minimizers, using
the same iteration argument employed in the proof of
Theorem

1.9
.
∎

Appendix A
Barycenter and variance of good slices

We show two lemmas which give a more refined control of a critical pair on a
good slice

B_{1}^{2}\times\{z\}
, with
z\in\mathcal{G}^{\eta}
, the good set defined in
Eq.

6.2
.
We assume that
(u,\nabla)
is a critical pair for
E_{\varepsilon}
, defined on
B_{1}^{2}\times B_{1}^{n-2}
,
with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and

E_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq|B_{1}^{n-2}|(2\pi+\tau_{0})

(as well as
Eq.

4.1
–
Eq.

4.2
).
Under this assumption, we have

\int_{(B_{3/4}^{2}\setminus B_{1/2}^{2})\times\{z\}}e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)\leq e^{-K(n)/\varepsilon}

for
z\in B_{3/4}^{n-2}
, since this part of the slice is far from the vorticity set. Recall that the barycenter

h(z)=\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}(z)

was defined using a cut-off function
\chi
supported in
B_{3/4}^{2}
, with
\chi=1
on
B_{1/2}^{2}

(the notation in the subscript means
\chi\cdot(x_{1},x_{2})
).

Lemma A.1

(Barycenter of a good slice)
.

For
\varepsilon_{0},\tau_{0},\eta_{0}>0
small enough, if
\eta\leq\eta_{0}
and
z\in\mathcal{G}^{\eta}
, then we have the following estimate (for a possibly different
K=K(n)
):

\displaystyle|h(z)-h_{0}(z)|\leq C(n)\varepsilon|\log(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}|(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}^{1/2}+e^{-K/\varepsilon},

where
h_{0}
is the map from
Proposition

6.6
giving the zero set on good slices.

In other words, the barycenter of the good slice is close to the actual zero of
u
here
(unique in
B_{1/2}^{2}\times\{z\}
).

Proof.

Recall that, by definition, we have

\displaystyle h(z)=\Phi_{\chi(x_{1},x_{2})}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\{z\}}(x_{1},x_{2})J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}.

Since the integral of the Jacobian on
B_{1/2}^{2}\times\{z\}

is
2\pi+O(e^{-K/\varepsilon})
(see, e.g., the proof of
[
42
, Lemma 6.11]
),
we get

h(z)-h_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\{z\}}[(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z)]J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})+Ce^{-K/\varepsilon}.

On the other hand, using the notation from
Proposition

10.1
,
we have

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times\{z\}}[(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z)]J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})(e_{1},e_{2})\right|\leq Ce^{-K/\varepsilon},

by symmetry of the standard planar solution.

Moreover,
u(\cdot,z)
vanishes linearly at
h_{0}(z)
, as observed in
Lemma

5.7
.
We can then apply a rescaling of
[
31
, Theorem C.1]
, which gives

(A.1)

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\{z\}}|J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})-J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})(e_{1},e_{2})|\leq C\sqrt{(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}}+Ce^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}.

Selecting a radius
C(n)\varepsilon\leq r\leq\frac{1}{4}
, we have

e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)(y,z)\leq C(n)\varepsilon^{-2}e^{-K|y-h_{0}(z)|/\varepsilon}\quad\text{on }[B_{1/2}^{2}\setminus B_{r}^{2}(h_{0}(z))]\times\{z\}

for a possibly different
K
,
since as observed in
Lemma

5.7
the distance from the vorticity set
Z
is comparable to the distance from

Z\cap(B_{3/4}^{2}\times\{z\})\subseteq B_{C(n)\varepsilon}^{2}(h_{0}(z))\times\{z\},

on good slices.
Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\{z\}}|(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z)||J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})-J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})(e_{1},e_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq r\int_{B_{r}^{2}(h_{0}(z))\times\{z\}}|J(u,\nabla)(e_{1},e_{2})-J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})(e_{1},e_{2})|

\displaystyle\quad+C\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\setminus B_{r}^{2}(h_{0}(z))}|y-h_{0}(z)|e^{-K|y-h_{0}(z)|/\varepsilon}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq Cr\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}}+C\varepsilon e^{-{Kr}/{\varepsilon}}

for a possibly different
K
.
Taking
r:=M\varepsilon|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|
for big enough
M
, we get

r\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}}+\varepsilon e^{-{Kr}/{\varepsilon}}\leq M\varepsilon\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|+\varepsilon\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}}\leq C(n)\varepsilon\sqrt{\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|

(recall that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, by definition of good set), unless
r<C(n)\varepsilon
or
r>\frac{1}{4}
.
The situation
r<C(n)\varepsilon
cannot happen, once
M
is taken large enough, while in the last case we obtain

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}\leq e^{-K^{\prime}/\varepsilon}
and thus we are done again, by taking
r:=\frac{1}{4}
above.
∎

Next we show that on a good slice the variance is close to
\varepsilon^{2}v_{0}
, where
v_{0}
is the variance of the standard degree-one planar solution.

Lemma A.2

(Variance of a good slice)
.

For any
\sigma\in(\varepsilon,1)
such that
|h_{0}(z)|\leq\sigma
, we have the following estimate on good slices, for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|c|\leq\sigma
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times\{z\}}[(x_{1}-c_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-c_{2})^{2}]e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)-\varepsilon^{2}v_{0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\varepsilon^{2}|\log(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}|^{2}\sqrt{(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}}+C(n)\sigma^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}+C(n)|h(z)-c|^{2}\sqrt{(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}}+C(n)e^{-{K\sigma}/{\varepsilon}},

for a possibly different
K=K(n)
.

Proof.

First of all, since the integrand in the definition of excess
\bm{\mathrm{E}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
upper bounds
e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)-J(u,\nabla)
, we can replace
e_{\varepsilon}(u,\nabla)
with
J(u,\nabla)
, up
to an error bounded as follows: for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
, we bound separately the contribution of
B_{2\sigma}^{2}
and the complement (where we use exponential decay) obtaining the error

9\sigma^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{1})_{z}+C(n)e^{-K\sigma/\varepsilon};

as for
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2}
, we argue as in the previous proof, obtaining the error

C(n)|h_{0}(z)-c|^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}+C(n)\varepsilon^{2}|\log(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}|^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}+e^{-K/\varepsilon},

where the first term comes from replacing
c
with the actual location
h_{0}(z)
of the zero.
Moreover, by definition of
v_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\{z\}}|(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z)|^{2}J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})(e_{1},e_{2})=\varepsilon^{2}v_{0}+O(e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}});

since
|(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z)|^{2}-|(x_{1},x_{2})-c|^{2}=2\langle(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z),c-h_{0}(z)\rangle-|c-h_{0}(z)|^{2}

and

\int_{B_{1/2}^{2}\times\{z\}}[(x_{1},x_{2})-h_{0}(z)]J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})=O(e^{-K/\varepsilon}),

we obtain

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B^{2}_{1/2}\times\{z\}}|(x_{1},x_{2})-c|^{2}J(u_{h_{0}},\nabla_{h_{0}})(e_{1},e_{2})=|h_{0}(z)-c|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}v_{0}+O(e^{-{K}/{\varepsilon}}).

As in the previous proof, we can replace
J(u_{0},\nabla_{0})
with
J(u,\nabla)
here, up to an error of the form
C(n)(|h_{0}(z)-c|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|\log\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{z}|^{2})\sqrt{(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}}
(using
Eq.

4.15
from
Proposition

4.8
).
Finally, we can bound

|h_{0}(z)-c|^{2}\leq 2|h_{0}(z)-h(z)|^{2}+2|h(z)-c|^{2}\leq 2|h(z)-c|^{2}+C(n)\varepsilon^{2}|\log(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}|^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{2})_{z}

using the previous proposition, and the claim follows.
∎

Appendix B
Poincaré–Gaffney-type inequalities

In the construction of the interpolation gauge in
Proposition

10.2
we make frequent use of Poincaré-type inequalities for functions and differential forms. These inequalities are well known; we present the special cases used in this paper for the convenience of the reader.

The following lemma is a consequence of results first appeared in the original paper of Gaffney
[
27
]
(see also
[
35
]
for a systematic treatment on manifolds with boundary), but for our application we need it to hold uniformly for cylinders of the form
B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}
of arbitrarily small width
r>0
.

Lemma B.1

(Poincaré–Gaffney-type inequality for thin cylinders)
.

Given a
1
-form
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{B}^{2}_{1}\times\overline{B}_{r}^{n-2})
with
r\leq 1
and the Neumann boundary condition
\alpha(\nu)=0
on
\partial(B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r})
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B_{r}^{n-2}}|\alpha|^{2}\leq C(n)\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}}[|d\alpha|^{2}+|d^{*}\alpha|^{2}].

Proof.

Since
B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}
is a convex domain and
\iota_{\nu}\alpha=0
at its boundary, we can apply
[
15
, Remark 9]
to see that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}}|\nabla\alpha|^{2}\leq\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}}[|d\alpha|^{2}+|d^{*}\alpha|^{2}].

Now we rescale the domain with the map
\phi:B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}\rightarrow B^{2}_{1}\times B_{r}^{n-2}
given by

\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=(x_{1},x_{2},rx_{3},\dots,rx_{n}),

and define
\tilde{\alpha}(x):=\alpha(\phi(x))
(notice that this is different from the pullback
\phi^{*}(\alpha)
). Then we claim that there exists a constant
C(n)>0
such that

(B.1)

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\tilde{\alpha}|^{2}\leq C(n)\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\nabla\tilde{\alpha}|^{2}\,.

We prove this by compactness and contradiction. By homogeneity, suppose there exists a sequence
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}
with
\iota_{\nu}\tilde{\alpha}_{k}=0
on
\partial(B_{1}^{2}\times B^{n-2}_{1})
and

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\tilde{\alpha}_{k}|^{2}=1,\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\nabla\tilde{\alpha}_{k}|^{2}=0.

Note that by the display above we have the bound
\|\tilde{\alpha}_{k}\|_{W^{1,2}(B^{2}_{1}\times B_{1}^{n-2})}\leq 2
for all large
k\geq 0
.
Up to extracting a subsequence, we can assume that
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}
converges weakly
to
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}
in
W^{1,2}
. By Rellich–Kondrachov, the convergence is strong in
L^{2}
. Thus,

\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}|^{2}=1,\quad\nabla\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}=0.

Hence,
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}=v
is a constant covector. The boundary condition passes to the limit, giving that
v(\nu)=0
on
\partial(B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1})
; since the normal vectors to the boundary of this domain span all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we get that
v=0
, a contradiction establishing
Eq.

B.1
. Then we compute that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}}|\alpha|^{2}

\displaystyle=r^{n-2}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\tilde{\alpha}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(n)r^{n-2}\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{1}}|\nabla\tilde{\alpha}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}}|\nabla\alpha|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\int_{B^{2}_{1}\times B^{n-2}_{r}}[|d\alpha|^{2}+|d^{*}\alpha|^{2}],

as desired.
∎

The next lemma is a weighted Poincaré estimate for functions in two dimensions.

Lemma B.2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any compactly supported function
f\in C^{1}_{c}(B^{2}_{R})
the following weighted Poincare type estimate holds:

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{R}}|x|^{2}|f|^{2}(x)\leq CR^{3/2}\int_{B^{2}_{R}}|x|^{5/2}|df|^{2}(x).

Proof.

By scaling the domain, we can assume that
R=1
. Then by
[
13
, eq. (1.4)]
(for the choice of constants
\alpha:=5/4
,
a:=1
,
p=q=r:=2
,
\gamma,\sigma:=1/4
) we can see that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{2}_{1}}|x|^{2}|f|^{2}(x)\leq\int_{B^{2}_{1}}|x|^{1/2}|f|^{2}(x)\leq C\int_{B^{2}_{1}}|x|^{5/2}|df|^{2}(x).

This is indeed the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma B.3

(Poincaré inequality on a thin annulus)
.

Given
a>b\geq c\geq 0
, there exists a constant
C(n,a,b,c)>0
with the following property. Let
f
be a function in
W^{1,2}((B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{c})\times\Omega)
, where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n-2}

is a convex bounded domain, such that

\displaystyle\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{b})\times\Omega}f=0.

Then the following Poincaré inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{c})\times\Omega}|f|^{2}\leq C(n,a,b,c)\operatorname{diam}(\Omega)^{2}\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{c})\times\Omega}|df|^{2}.

Proof.

First we apply the standard Poincaré inequality on each two dimensional slice
(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{c})\times\{z\}
for any
z\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{c})\times\{z\}}|f|^{2}\right]\,dz

\displaystyle\leq C(a,b,c)\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{c})\times\Omega}|df|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\left|\int_{(B^{2}_{c}\setminus B^{2}_{b})\times\{z\}}f\right|^{2}\,dz.

Notice that the function
g(z):=\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{b})\times\{z\}}f
has zero average on
\Omega
. Hence we can apply the Poincaré inequality on
\Omega
to see that

\int_{\Omega}|g|^{2}\leq C(n)\operatorname{diam}(\Omega)^{2}\int_{\Omega}|dg|^{2}.

Indeed, it is well-known that the Poincaré inequality on a convex domain holds with a constant depending only on its diameter and
n
.
This yields the desired conclusion.
∎

Remark B.4
.

The same conclusion holds if we assume that

\int_{(B^{2}_{a}\setminus B^{2}_{b})\times\Omega^{\prime}}f=0

for some
\Omega^{\prime}
with
|\Omega^{\prime}|\geq\alpha|\Omega|
(the constant depending also on
\alpha
).
