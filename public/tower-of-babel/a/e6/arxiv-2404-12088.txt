1.
Introduction

We deal with the initial value problem for the fractional Hardy-Hénon equation with fractional Brownian noise

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u(t)+(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}u(t)=|x|^{-\gamma}|u(t)|^{p-1}u(t)+\mu\,\partial_{t}B^{H}(t),\\
u(0)=u_{0},\end{array}\right.

is the linear heat semi-group.

The development of stochastic calculus for fractional Brownian motion (
fBm

In recent years, the investigation of SPDEs driven by fractional noise has garnered significant attention within the mathematical community. This growing interest stems from both theoretical considerations and the broad range of applications in fields such as physics, biology, hydrology, and other scientific disciplines. Of particular significance is the study of the well-posedness of semilinear stochastic parabolic equations driven by infinite-dimensional fractional noise, which has been a subject of special interest. Preliminaries

2.1.
Fractional Brownian motion

In the subsequent discussion,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
denotes a complete probability space. Fractional Brownian motion (
fBm
) was initially introduced and investigated by Kolmogorov
[
25
]
within the framework of Hilbert spaces. Let
[0,T]
represent a time interval with an arbitrary fixed
T>0
. A
fBm
with a Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
is a centered Gaussian process denoted as
B^{H}
, possessing the following covariance structure:

(2.5)

R(s,t):=E(B^{H}(t)B^{H}(s))=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right),

where
s,t\in[0,T].
It is worth emphasizing that when the Hurst parameter takes the value of
H=1/2
, the process
B^{1/2}(t)
corresponds to the standard Brownian motion.

The
fBm
can be alternatively defined as the unique self-similar Gaussian process exhibiting stationary increments

E[(B^{H}(t)-B^{H}(s))^{2}]=|t-s|^{2H},

and
H
-self similar

\left(\frac{1}{c^{H}}B^{H}(ct),\,t\geq 0\right)\overset{d}{=}\left(B^{H}(t),\,t\geq 0\right),

for all
c>0.
Here, the symbol
\overset{d}{=}
denotes equality in the distributional sense. Furthermore, the process
B^{H}
can be expressed using the following Wiener integral representation:

(2.6)

B^{H}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K^{H}(t,s)\,dW(s).

In the above representation,
W={W(t);,t\in[0,T]}
denotes a Wiener process, and
K^{H}(t,s)
represents the kernel defined as follows:

(2.7)

K^{H}(t,s)=c_{H}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}+c_{H}(\frac{1}{2}-H)\int_{s}^{t}(r-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}(1-(1+(\frac{s}{r}))^{\frac{1}{2}-H})\,dr,

where
c_{H}
is given by

c_{H}=\left(\frac{2H\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-H)}{\Gamma(H+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(2-2H)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

From (
2.7
) we obtain

\frac{\partial K^{H}}{\partial t}(t,s)=c_{H}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-H}(t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}.

It is noteworthy that when
H>1/2
, the kernel
K^{H}(t,s)
exhibits regularity and can be expressed in a simpler form as follows:

K^{H}(t,s)=c_{H}s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s}^{t}(r-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}r^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\,dr.

Let
\varepsilon_{H}
denote the linear space of step functions on
[0,T]
with the following form:

(2.8)

\varphi(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}1_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

where
t_{1},...,t_{n}\in[0,T],

n\in\mathbb{N},

a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and by
\mathcal{H}
the closure of
\varepsilon_{H}
with respect to the scalar product

\langle 1_{[0,t]},\,1_{[0,s]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=R(t,s).

For
\varphi\in\varepsilon_{H}
, characterized by the form (
2.8
), we define its Wiener integral with respect to the
fBm
as follows:

\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{s}\,dB^{H}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(B^{H}(t_{i+1})-B^{H}(t_{i})).

Evidently, the mapping

\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}1_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\longrightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{s}\,dB^{H}(s),

serves as an isometry between
\varepsilon_{H}
and the linear space
\text{span}\{B^{H}(t),\;t\geq 0\}
when regarded as a subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The image of an element
\Phi\in\mathcal{H}
under this isometry is referred to as the Wiener integral of
\Phi
with respect to
B^{H}
.

For any
s<T
, let us consider the operator
L^{\ast}_{H}:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}([0,T])
given by

(L^{\ast}_{H}\varphi)(s)=K^{H}(T,s)\varphi(s)+\int_{s}^{T}(\varphi(r)-\varphi(s))\frac{\partial K^{H}}{\partial r}(r,s)\,dr.

When
H>1/2
, the operator
L^{\ast}_{H}
has the simpler expression

(L^{\ast}_{H}\varphi)(s)=\int_{s}^{T}\varphi(r)\frac{\partial K^{H}}{\partial r}(r,s)\,dr.

We refer to
[
1
]
for the proof that
L^{\ast}_{H}
constitutes an isometry between
\mathcal{H}
and
L^{2}([0,T])
. Consequently, we can establish the following relationship between the Wiener process
W
and the fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
:

\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s)\,dB^{H}(s)=\int_{0}^{t}(L^{\ast}_{H}\varphi)(s)\,dW(s),

for every
t\in[0,T]
and
\varphi 1_{[0,t]}\in\mathcal{H}
if and only if
L^{\ast}_{H}\varphi\in L^{2}([0,T]).
Furthermore, it is worth recalling that for
\phi,\chi\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\phi(s)||\chi(t)||t-s|^{2H-2}\,dsdt<\infty
, their scalar product in
\mathcal{H}
can be expressed as:

(2.9)

\langle\phi,\chi\rangle_{H}=H(2H-1)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(s)\chi(t)|t-s|^{2H-2}\,dsdt.

This formula establishes the inner product relationship between
\phi
and
\chi
in the space
\mathcal{H}
.

It is important to note that in general, the existence of the right-hand side of equation (
2.9
) requires careful justification (see
[
37
]
). However, since we will be exclusively working with Wiener integrals over Hilbert spaces, it is worth highlighting that if
X
is a Hilbert space and
u\in L^{2}([0,T];X)
is a deterministic function, the relation (
2.9
) holds. Moreover, the right-hand side of (
2.9
) is well-defined in
L^{2}(\Omega,X)
if
L^{\ast}_{H}u
belongs to
L^{2}([0,T]\times X)
.

2.2.
Cylindrical fractional Brownian motion

Following the approach in
[
17
]
, we define the standard cylindrical fractional Brownian motion in
X
as the formal series:

(2.10)

B^{H}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e_{n}b_{n}^{H}(t),

where
\{e_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
represents a complete orthonormal basis in
X
, and
b_{n}^{H}
denotes a one-dimensional
fBm
.

It is widely recognized that the infinite series (
2.10
) does not converge in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, which implies that
B^{H}(t)
is not a well-defined
X
-valued random variable. However, for any Hilbert space
{\mathcal{N}}
such that
X\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}
, where the embedding of
X
into
{\mathcal{N}}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator, the series (
2.10
) defines a
{\mathcal{N}}
-valued random variable. Consequently,
\{B^{H}(t),\,t\geq 0\}
can be regarded as a
{\mathcal{N}}
-valued identity fractional Brownian motion (Id-
fBm
).

Following the approach introduced for cylindrical Brownian motion in
[
14
]
, it is possible to define a stochastic integral of the form:

(2.11)

\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,{d}B^{H}(t),

where
f:[0,T]\mapsto\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
and
Y
represents another real and separable Hilbert space. The expression (
2.11
) corresponds to a random variable valued with
Y
that remains independent of the choice of
{\mathcal{N}}
.

Consider a deterministic function
f
with values in
\mathcal{L}_{2}(X,Y)
, which represents the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators from
X
to
Y
. We now introduce the following assumptions on
f
:

i)

For each
x\in X
,
f(.)x\in L^{p}([0,T];Y),
for
qH>1.

ii)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\|f(s)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(X,Y)}\,\|f(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(X,Y)}\,|s-t|^{2H-2}\,dsdt<\infty.

From
[
17
, (2.15), p. 230]
, it is established that the stochastic integral (
2.11
) can be properly defined as:

(2.12)

\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,{d}B^{H}(t):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{{T}}f(t)e_{n}\,db_{n}^{H}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{{T}}(L_{H}^{\ast}fe_{n})\,db_{n}^{{H}}(t),

where
b_{n}^{H}
represents the standard Brownian motion associated with the fractional Brownian motion
b_{n}^{H}
through the representation formula (
2.6
). It is important to note that since
fe_{n}\in L^{2}([0,T];Y)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the variables
{\int_{0}^{T}fe_{n}\,db_{n}^{H}}
are mutually independent (see
[
17
]
).
The series (
2.12
) is finite and can be expressed as:

(2.13)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|L^{\ast}_{H}(fe_{n})\|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\parallel\parallel fe_{n}\parallel_{\mathcal{H}}\parallel_{Y}^{2}<\infty.

In the case where
X=Y=\mathcal{H}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}f(s)e_{n}db^{H}_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e_{m}\int_{0}^{t}\langle f(s)e_{n},e_{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,db_{n}^{H}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e_{m}\int_{0}^{t}\langle K_{H}^{*}(f(s)e_{n}),e_{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,db_{n}^{H}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}^{*}(f(s)e_{n})\,db_{n}^{H}(s).

2.3.
Smoothing effect

Consider the linear homogeneous equation associated to (
1.1
)

(2.14)

\partial_{t}u+(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}u=0,\quad u(0)=u_{0}.

It is well known that the operator
(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}
, for
\theta>0
, generates a semi-group
e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}
whose kernel
E_{\theta}
is smooth, radial, and satisfies the scaling property.

(2.15)

E_{\theta}(x,t)=t^{-\frac{N}{\theta}}E_{\theta}(t^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}x,1):=t^{-\frac{N}{\theta}}K_{\theta}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}x\right),

where

(2.16)

K_{\theta}(x)=(2\pi)^{-N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,e^{-|\xi|^{\theta}}\,d\xi.

Hence, the solution of (
2.14
) may be formally realized via convolution by

(2.17)

u(t,x)=\left(e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}\right)u_{0}(x)=\bigg(t^{-\frac{N}{\theta}}K_{\theta}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}\cdot\right)\ast u_{0}\bigg)(x),

whenever this representation makes sense.
We recall the following point-wise estimate for the kernel
K_{\theta}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
35
, Lemma 2.1, p. 463]

Let
\theta>0
and
N\geq 1
. Then, we have

(2.18)

\left|K_{\theta}(x)\right|{\leq C}\left(1+|x|\right)^{-N-\theta},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
C=C(\theta,N)
is a positive constant. As a result, we get
K_{\theta}\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for any
1\leq r\leq\infty
.

Remark 2.1
.

(i)

The inequality (
2.18
) can be derived from
[
4
, Theorem 2.1, p. 263]
in the case where
\theta<2
.

(ii)

For
\theta=2m
with

being an integer, a more refined estimate than (

) is provided in
[

. More precisely, the following estimate holds:

(2.19)

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,e^{-|\xi|^{2m}}\,d\xi\right|\leq C\exp\left(-\kappa_{m}|x|^{\frac{2m}{2m-1}}\right),

where

(2.20)

\kappa_{m}=(2m-1)(2m)^{-\frac{2m}{2m-1}}\;\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4m-2}\right).

See also
[

,

.

(iii)

The fact that

belongs to the space

implies that the solution to equation (

), given by

From (

Remark 2.2

(i)

(iii)

The semigroup

\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}\|_{L^{q}-L^{q}}\leq 1.

For

{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}(t)=e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}|\cdot|^{-\gamma}.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

,

such that

(2.24)

\|{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}(t)\varphi\|_{L^{q}}\leq Ct^{-\frac{N}{\theta}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q})-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{p}},\quad t>0.

Remark 2.3
.

(i)

The proof of (
2.24
) for
\theta=2
was given in
[
3
, Proposition 2.1, p. 121]
.

(ii)

The estimate (
2.24
) for
\theta\in 2\mathbb{N}
was derived in
[
43
, Proposition 3.8]
.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

To enhance reader convenience, we provide a comprehensive proof for any
\theta>0
. As one will notice, our proof incorporates arguments borrowed from
[
3
,
43
]
. By employing a scaling argument, we can reduce the task of proving (
2.24
) to the case of
t=1
. In other words, it is sufficient to establish the inequality

(2.25)

\|{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}(1)\varphi\|_{L^{q}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{p}}.

To achieve this, we employ the following decomposition of the singular weight
|x|^{-\gamma}
:

(2.26)

|x|^{-\gamma}=|x|^{-\gamma}\chi_{\{|x|<1\}}+|x|^{-\gamma}\chi_{\{|x|\geq 1\}}:=f(x)+g(x).

It is evident that
f\in L^{a}
holds for all
a<\frac{N}{\gamma}
and
g\in L^{b}
holds for all
b>\frac{N}{\gamma}
. Using (
2.23
), it becomes apparent that
\frac{N}{\gamma}-\frac{p}{p-1}>0
and
\frac{\gamma}{N}>\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}
. Consequently, one can select a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
such that:

(2.27)

\frac{\gamma}{N+\gamma\varepsilon}\geq\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\gamma}{N-\gamma\varepsilon}\leq 1-\frac{1}{p}.

Let us consider
r
and
s
with
r,s\geq 1
satisfying the condition:

(2.28)

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{\gamma}{N-\gamma\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{p},\quad\frac{1}{s}=\frac{\gamma}{N+\gamma\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{p}.

By employing the smoothing effect (
2.21
) and applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

(2.29)

\begin{split}\|{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}(1)\varphi\|_{L^{q}}&\leq\|{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,0}(1)(f\varphi)\|_{L^{q}}+\|{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,0}(1)(g\varphi)\|_{L^{q}}\\
&{\leq C}\|f\varphi\|_{L^{r}}+\|g\varphi\|_{L^{s}}\\
&{\leq C}\left(\|f\|_{L^{\frac{N}{\gamma}-\varepsilon}}+\|g\|_{L^{\frac{N}{\gamma}+\varepsilon}}\right)\,\|\varphi\|_{L^{p}}.\end{split}

This finishes the proof of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

3.
Proof of the main result

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
N,\theta,\gamma,p,q
be as defined in (
1.4
). Then, there exists
1<r<\infty
such that:

(3.30)

\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{\gamma}{N}\right)<\frac{1}{r}<\frac{1}{q}.

Furthermore, the following properties hold

\displaystyle 1-p\sigma>0,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{N}{\theta}\left(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}-p\sigma>0,

\displaystyle 1+(1-p)\sigma-\frac{N(p-1)}{r\theta}-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}>0,

where

(3.31)

\sigma=\frac{N}{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{r}\right).

The proof of Lemma
3.1
is straightforward. It is worth emphasizing that one can choose

r=\frac{2pq}{p+1}.

In addition, with any
r
satisfying (
3.30
), we have

(3.32)

\frac{1}{p}\max\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{\gamma}{N},\;\;\frac{p}{q}-\frac{\theta}{N}\right)<\frac{1}{r}<\frac{1}{p}\min\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{N},\;\;\frac{1}{q}-\frac{\gamma}{N}+\frac{\theta}{N},\;\;\frac{p}{q}\right).

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

First, we consider the linear Cauchy problem

(3.33)

\left\{\begin{matrix}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{Z}}(t)+(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}{\mathbf{Z}}(t)=\mu\,\partial_{t}B^{H}(t),\quad t>0,\\
{\mathbf{Z}}(0)=0.\\
\end{matrix}\right.

The mild solution of (
3.33
) is given by

{\mathbf{Z}}(t)=\mu\int_{0}^{t}\,{\rm e}^{-(t-s)(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}\,dB^{H}(s).

Since
qH>1,

H>\frac{N}{2\theta}
and
\frac{1}{2}<H<1
, we know from
[
12
]
that the mild solution
{\mathbf{Z}}
belongs to
C([0,T];L^{q})
for any
T>0
. For
\sigma>0
and
1<r<\infty
, as specified in Lemma
3.1
, we define

(3.34)

{\mathbf{K}}(T)=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\|_{q}+\sup_{0{<}t\leq T}\,\left(t^{\sigma}\,\|{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\|_{r}\right).

Now we are ready to give the detailed proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will apply the Banach fixed-point theorem to the integral equation (
1.2
) with
\mu\neq 0
. Let us fix
M>2\mathcal{K}\|u_{0}\|_{q}
with
\mathcal{K}
being given in (
2.21
). For
T>0
, let
\mathbf{X}_{T}
be defined as

(3.35)

\mathbf{X}_{T}=\Bigg\{u\in C_{T}(L^{q})\cap C({(0,T]},L^{r});\quad\max\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|{u(t)}\|_{q},\sup_{0{<}t\leq T}\,\left(t^{\sigma}\,\|{u}(t)\|_{r}\right)\right)\leq M\Bigg\}.

We endow
\mathbf{X}_{T}
with the metric

d(u,v)=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{q}+\sup_{0{<}t\leq T}\left(t^{\sigma}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{r}\right),

where
r,{q}
and
\sigma
are as in Lemma
3.1
.
We also define the operator
\Phi:\mathbf{X}_{T}\to{C_{T}(L^{q})\cap C({(0,T]},L^{r})}
by

(3.36)

\Phi(u)(t)=e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}(t-s)(|u(s)|^{p-1}u(s))\,ds+{\mathbf{Z}}(t),

where
{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}
is given by (
2.22
). We aim to demonstrate that
\Phi(\mathbf{X}_{T})\subset\mathbf{X}_{T}
and that
\Phi
acts as a contraction under appropriate choice of
T>0
.
First, let us show that
\Phi
maps
\mathbf{X}_{T}
into itself for suitable choice of
T
. For
u\in\mathbf{X}_{T}
, we have by using (
2.21
), (
2.24
) and Lemma
3.1

(3.37)

\begin{split}\|\Phi(u)(t)\|_{q}&\leq\|e^{-t(-\Delta)^{\theta/2}}u_{0}\|_{q}+\|{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\|_{q}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\mathbf{S}}_{\theta,\gamma}(t-s)(|u(s)|^{p-1}u(s))\right\|_{q}\,ds\\
&\leq\mathcal{K}\|u_{0}\|_{q}+\mathbf{K}(T)+C\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{N}{\theta}(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{q})-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}}\,\left\||u(s)|^{p}\right\|_{\frac{r}{p}}\,ds\\
&\leq\mathcal{K}\|u_{0}\|_{q}+\mathbf{K}(T)+CM^{p}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{N}{\theta}(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{q})-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}}\,s^{-p\sigma}\,ds\\
&\leq\mathcal{K}\|u_{0}\|_{q}+\mathbf{K}(T)+CM^{p}\,T^{a+b-1}\,\mathcal{B}(a,b),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{B}
is the standard Beta function and

(3.38)

a=1-\frac{N}{\theta}\left(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-\frac{\gamma}{\theta},\quad b=1-p\sigma.

Arguing similarly as above, we obtain

(3.39)

t^{\sigma}\|\Phi(u)(t)\|_{r}\leq\mathcal{K}\|u_{0}\|_{q}+\mathbf{K}(T)+CM^{p}\,T^{a+b-1}\,\mathcal{B}(a-\sigma,b),

with
a,b
defined as in (
3.38
). From Lemma
3.1
, we have
a>0,b>0
,
a+b-1>0
, and
a-\sigma>0
. Therefore, for
T>0
small enough, we obtain
\Phi(\mathbf{X}_{T})\subset\mathbf{X}_{T}
due to the choice of
M>2\mathcal{K}\|u_{0}\|_{q}
.
Next, we aim to show that
\Phi
acts as a contraction for
T>0
small enough. Let
u,v\in\mathbf{X}_{T}
. Observing that

\left||u|^{p-1}u-|v|^{p-1}v\right|{\leq p}\,|u-v|\left(|u|^{p-1}+|v|^{p-1}\right),

and using Proposition
2.2
together with Hölder’s inequality, we infer

(3.40)

\begin{split}\|\Phi(u)(t)-\Phi(v)(t)\|_{q}&\leq\,{C(H)}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{a-1-p\sigma}\big\||u(s)|^{p-1}u(s)-|v(s)|^{p-1}v(s)\big\|_{\frac{q}{p}}\,ds\\
&\leq\,{C(H)}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{a-1-p\sigma}\,\|u(s)-v(s)\|_{q}\big(\|u(s)\|_{q}^{p-1}+\|v(s)\|_{q}^{p-1}\big)\,ds\\
&\leq{C(H)}\ M^{p-1}\,T^{a-p\sigma}\,d(u,v),\end{split}

where
\sigma
and
a
are given by (
3.31
) and (
3.38
) respectively. Likewise as above, we also obtain

(3.41)

t^{\sigma}\|\Phi(u)(t)-\Phi(v)(t)\|_{r}\leq\,{C(H)}\ M^{p-1}\,T^{a+\sigma}\,d(u,v).

Plugging estimates (
3.40
) and (
3.41
) together, we get

(3.42)

d(\Phi(u),\Phi(v))\leq\,{C(H)}\ M^{p-1}\left(T^{a-p\sigma}+T^{a+\sigma}\right)\,d(u,v).

In the estimates (
3.40
), (
3.41
), and (
3.42
), we use the notation
C(H)
to emphasize the implicit dependence of the constant on the Hurst parameter
H
associated with the noise
B^{H}
.

Given that
a>0
,
\sigma>0
, and
a-p\sigma>0
, we can deduce from (
3.42
) that
\Phi
exhibits contraction properties for sufficiently small
T>0
. By invoking the Banach fixed point theorem, we successfully conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

4.
Conclusion and open problems

In this paper, we have investigated the local well-posedness of the fractional Hardy-Hénon equation driven by fractional Brownian motion (
fBm
). By carefully selecting the parameters of the equation and employing the properties of the fractional heat kernel, we established the existence and uniqueness of mild solutions in a suitable Lebesgue spaces. Our results extend previous work on deterministic and stochastic versions of the Hardy-Hénon equation by incorporating the effects of fractional noise, which introduces additional challenges due to the irregularity of the
fBm
.

The key contributions of this work include the introduction of a mild solution framework for the fractional stochastic Hardy-Hénon equation, which allows us to handle the singular drift term and the fractional noise simultaneously. We applied the Banach fixed-point theorem to prove local existence and uniqueness under suitable conditions on the parameters of the equation. Additionally, we conducted a careful analysis of the stochastic convolution term, which arises from the
fBm
and plays a crucial role in the well-posedness of the problem.

Despite these advancements, several open problems remain. First, while we have established local well-posedness, the question of global existence and uniqueness of solutions remains open. Extending the local solution to a global one would require additional a priori estimates and a deeper understanding of the long-time behavior of the solution, particularly in the presence of the singular drift term and fractional noise.

Second, the regularity of the solutions, both in space and time, is an important open problem. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate whether the solutions belong to higher-order Sobolev or Besov spaces and how the regularity depends on the Hurst parameter
H
of the
fBm
.

Third, for deterministic versions of the Hardy-Hénon equation, blow-up phenomena have been extensively studied
[
26
,
27
,
38
]
. It would be valuable to explore whether similar blow-up results hold in the stochastic setting and how the presence of fractional noise influences the blow-up behavior.

Finally, while we have focused on
fBm
, it would be interesting to consider other types of noise, such as Lévy noise or multiplicative noise. Extending the results to these cases would broaden the applicability of the theory and provide a more comprehensive understanding of stochastic partial differential equations with singular drift terms.
