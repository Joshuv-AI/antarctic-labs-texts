Poissonian resetting of subdiffusion in a linear potential
A. A. Stanislavsky

Abstract
Resetting a stochastic process is an important problem describing the evolution of physical, biological and other systems which are continually returned to their certain fixed point. We consider the motion of a subdiffusive particle with a constant drift under Poissonian resetting. In this model the stochastic process is Brownian motion subordinated by an inverse infinitely divisible process (subordinator). Although this approach includes a wide class of subdiffusive system with Poissonian resetting by using different subordinators, each of such systems has a stationary state with the asymmetric Laplace distribution in which the scale and asymmetric parameters depend on the Laplace exponent of the subordinators used. Moreover, the mean time for the particle to reach a target is finite and has a minimum, optimal with respect to the resetting rate. Features of Lévy motion under this resetting and the effect of a linear potential are discussed. Key words: stochastic resetting, stochastic processes, anomalous diffusion, Lévy flights, nonequilibrium statistical mechanics

The derivation of quantitative analytical results in the theory of stochastic resetting may be based on different approaches. The simplest of them uses Poissonian resetting. In this case, the dynamics includes a stochastic mixture of resetting to the initial position with the rate
r
(long-range movements) and ordinary diffusion (local steps) with diffusion constant
D

All of them give the same results. In the study we use the renewal equation as a simpler method, although the master equation is also used. The paper is organized as follows. Starting with Brownian motion under Poissonian resetting in a linear potential, we emphasize that the stationary distribution takes the asymmetric Laplace form. Moreover, it persists if Brownian motion is subordinated by an inverse infinitely divisible random process. Next, we show that in this case there is also an optimum choice of the resetting rate. Finally, the substitution of Brownian motion for Lévy flights in a linear potential and under Poissonian resetting gives a stationary state with the asymmetric Linnik distribution.

2
Brownian motion in potential

Brownian motion under resetting has been already considered in potentials and without them
[
10
,
27
]
. It is clear that the former is reduced to the latter, if the potential tends to zero. The interesting result follows from Brownian motion with a constant drift
\mu
in the positive
x
direction under Poissonian resetting with the rate
r
. This corresponds to an unbounded linear potential
[
1
]
, and the master equation approach to this problem was considered in
[
26
]
. The equation reads

\dfrac{\partial p_{1}(x,t)}{\partial t}=-\mu\dfrac{\partial p_{1}(x,t)}{\partial x}+D\dfrac{\partial^{2}p_{1}(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}-rp_{1}(x,t)+r\delta(x-X_{r}),

(2.1)

with initial condition
p_{1}(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
as the Dirac delta function, and
X_{r}
denotes the position to which the particle returns after resetting. Instead of the master equations we use a simpler way based on renewal equations giving the same solution. For this case, the Green function in the absence of resetting (in other words, the propagator) has the following form

G_{1}(x,t|x_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\piup Dt}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_{0}-\mu t)^{2}}{4Dt}\right],

(2.2)

where
x_{0}
is the initial position in which
G_{1}(x,0|x_{0})=\delta(x-x_{0})
. In the presence of resetting, the probability density function (PDF)
p_{1}(x,t|x_{0})
is a sum of two terms and is written as

p_{1}(x,t|x_{0})=\mathrm{e}^{-rt}G_{1}(x,t|x_{0})+r\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}\,G_{1}(x,\tau|X_{r})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2.3)

Immediately note that the renewal equation (
2.3
) holds for more general stochastic processes with their propagators, which can be different from this case having
G_{1}(x,t|x_{0})
as shown in
[
28
]
. The evolution of equation (
2.3
) in time is shown in figure
2
. Recall the derivation of equation (
2.3
) in appendix
A
, following from
[
1
]
. The stationary state (
t\to\infty
) of equation (
2.3
) is determined only by the second term which can be found exactly
[
1
]
. It acquires the analytical form

p_{1}(x,\infty|X_{r})=\frac{r}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr}}\exp\left[{\frac{\mu(x-X_{r})}{2D}-\frac{|x-X_{r}|\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr}}{2D}}\right].

(2.4)

Obviously, the stationary distribution is asymmetric with respect to the resetting position
X_{r}
by virtue of different exponential decays in the downstream (
x>X_{r}
) and upstream (
x<X_{r}
) directions. Moreover, this is nothing else but the asymmetric Laplace distribution
[
29
,
30
]
. In common notation, the latter reads

f(x;m,\lambda,\kappa)=\frac{\lambda}{\kappa+1/\kappa}\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{(\lambda/\kappa)(x-m)}&\text{for $x<m$},\\
\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\kappa(x-m)}&\text{for $x\geqslant m$}.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Here,
m
is the location parameter,
\lambda>0
is the scale parameter, and
\kappa>0
is the asymmetry parameter. As applied to equation (
2.4
), they depend on the values
X_{r}
,
r
,
D
and
\mu
, namely

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{r},\qquad\lambda=\sqrt{r/D},

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr}-\mu\right)/\sqrt{4Dr}.

(2.6)

The first two parameters are typical of the ordinary Laplace distribution, whereas the asymmetric parameter
\kappa
leads to the latter distribution, if only
\kappa=1
, i. e.,
\mu=0
. The result is based on the consideration of Brownian motion as a stochastic process under resetting. Below we show that the asymmetric Laplace distribution is a stationary state for Poissonian resetting of other stochastic processes.

Note that equation (
2.2
) is a normal variance-mean mixture with respect to both the shift parameter and the scale parameter, but since these parameters are strictly dependent, i. e., the position (mean) parameter of the mixed normal law is proportional to its variance, then the mixture (
2.2
) may be characterized by one parameter. That is why the mixture (
2.2
) is called variance-mean
[
31
,
32
]
. In fact, the function
r\mathrm{e}^{-rt}
in the integrand of
p_{1}(x,\infty|X_{r})
is the mixing probability density.

3
Ordinary subdiffusion in potential

Consider the subdiffusion instead of Brownian motion as a stochastic process under Poissonian resetting with the constant rate
r
. Moreover, the subdiffusion includes an unbounded linear potential. In this case, the Green function (also known as the propagator for the subdiffusion equation) is expressed in terms of the subordination integral

G_{\alpha}(x,t|x_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{1}(x,\xi|x_{0})\,g_{\alpha}(\xi,t)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(3.1)

for which the PDF
G_{1}(x,\xi|x_{0})
describes probabilistic properties of the parent process, whereas the PDF
g_{\alpha}(\xi,t)
is related to the directing process which is inverse
\alpha
-stable. The latter has a simple Laplace transform
[
34
,
35
]

\bar{g}_{\alpha}(\xi,u)=u^{\alpha-1}\exp\left({-\xi u^{\alpha}}\right),

(3.2)

which we use for our analysis. Let us also remind that the
\alpha
-stable Lévy process is often considered as a continuous limit of a sequence of nonnegative, independent, identically distributed random variables (obeying an
\alpha
-stable PDF with
0<\alpha<1

[
33
]
) representing waiting-time intervals between subsequent jumps of a walker. If such a process is denoted by
U_{\alpha}(\tau)
, its inverse
\alpha
-stable process is defined as
S_{\alpha}(t)=\inf\{\tau>0:U_{\alpha}(\tau)>t\}
.

Then, the time-dependent renewal equation, accounting for Poissonian resetting, yields

p_{\alpha}(x,t|x_{0})=\mathrm{e}^{-rt}G_{\alpha}(x,t|x_{0})+r\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}\,G_{\alpha}(x,\tau|X_{r})\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(3.3)

similar to equation (
2.3
), but with another propagator corresponding to the given case. This equation has a stationary state. An example of evolving to such a state in time is represented in figure
3
. For
\alpha=1
, the PDF
g_{\alpha}(\xi,t)
becomes the Dirac delta-function (no subordination), and equation (
3.3
) is transformed into equation (
2.3
) for Brownian motion. Therefore, the index 1 is used in equation (
2.3
). As usual, the stationary state is determined by the second term of equation (
3.3
). Therefore, we consider only this and take:

p_{\alpha}(x,\infty|x_{0})=r\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}\,G_{\alpha}(x,\tau|X_{r})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(3.4)

From equations (
3.1
), (
3.2
) and integrating over
\xi
, we find the subduffusive Green function under the potential as the inverse Laplace transform

G_{\alpha}(x,t|X_{r})=\frac{1}{2\piup\mathrm{i}}\int_{\rm Br}\exp\left[{ut+\frac{\mu(x-X_{r})}{2D}-\frac{|x-X_{r}|\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Du^{\alpha}}}{2D}}\right]\,\frac{u^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Du^{\alpha}}},

(3.5)

where
{\rm Br}
is the Bromwich contour, i. e., the straight line from
u=\sigma-\mathrm{i}\infty
to
u=\sigma+\mathrm{i}\infty
, where
\sigma
is chosen so that all the singularities of the integrand are located to the left from the line. In fact, equation (
3.4
) is the Laplace transform of the function
G_{\alpha}(x,t|X_{r})
, whereas equation (
3.5
) presents the inverse Laplace transform giving the same function. Thus, the stationary solution of equation (
3.3
) can be found from the integrand of equation (
3.5
), calculating the integral

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}(x,\infty|x_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(s-r)\,\exp\left[{\frac{\mu(x-X_{r})}{2D}-\frac{|x-X_{r}|\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Ds^{\alpha}}}{2D}}\right]\,\frac{s^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}s}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Ds^{\alpha}}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr^{\alpha}}}\,\exp\left[{\frac{\mu(x-X_{r})}{2D}-\frac{|x-X_{r}|\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr^{\alpha}}}{2D}}\right],

having many similarities with equation (
2.4
). In this case, the PDF is also asymmetric about the resetting position
X_{r}
because of different exponential decays in the downstream (
x>X_{r}
) and upstream (
x<X_{r}
) directions. We again obtain the asymmetric Laplace distribution as a stationary state mentioned above. However, its scale and asymmetry parameters are modified, namely

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{r},\qquad\lambda=\sqrt{r^{\alpha}/D},

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr^{\alpha}}-\mu\right)/\sqrt{4Dr^{\alpha}}.

(3.7)

The difference between equation (
2.6
) and equation (
3.7
) is only in the substitution
r\to r^{\alpha}
, but the PDF form is saved.

4
Subordination by infinitely divisible processes

The next one in line for our consideration is a more general case, when the subordinator is described by an inverse infinitely divisible distribution. Such a distribution has the following Laplace transform
[
36
]

\bar{g}_{\Psi}(\xi,u)=\frac{\bar{\Psi}(u)}{u}\mathrm{e}^{-\xi\bar{\Psi}(u)},

(4.1)

where
\bar{\Psi}(u)
is the Laplace exponent expressed in terms of Bernstein functions
[
37
]
. In this instance the propagator is

G_{\Psi}(x,t|x_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{1}(x,\xi|x_{0})\,g_{\Psi}(\xi,t)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(4.2)

Using equation (
4.1
), it is not difficult to find the function
G_{\Psi}(x,t|x_{0})
as the inverse Laplace transform, namely

G_{\Psi}(x,t|x_{0})=\frac{1}{2\piup\mathrm{i}}\int_{\rm Br}\exp\left[{ut+\frac{\mu(x-X_{r})}{2D}-\frac{|x-X_{r}|\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(u)}}{2D}}\right]\frac{\bar{\Psi}(u)}{u\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(u)}}\mathrm{d}u.

(4.3)

The corresponding Green function
p_{\Psi}(x,\infty|x_{0})
takes the form of equation (
2.3
) expressed in terms of the propagator
G_{\Psi}(x,t|x_{0})
. Therefore, we omit it. Obviously, then the second term determines a stationary state of this equation, and this term is nothing else but the Laplace transform for
t\to\infty
. Consequently, the PDF of the stationary state satisfies

p_{\Psi}(x,\infty|x_{0})=\frac{\bar{\Psi}(r)}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(r)}}\exp\left[{\frac{\mu(x-X_{r})}{2D}-\frac{|x-X_{r}|\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(r)}}{2D}}\right].

(4.4)

Consequently, the brief analysis of the anomalous diffusion with a linear potential leads to the distribution, similar to equations (
2.4
) and (
3.6
), if
t\to\infty
, in such systems under Poissonian resetting. Then, the parameters, characterizing the asymmetric Laplace distribution, are convenient to represent as

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{r},\quad\lambda=\sqrt{\bar{\Psi}(r)/D},

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(r)}-\mu\right)/\sqrt{4D\bar{\Psi}(r)}.

(4.5)

This result shows that the development affects both scale and asymmetry parameters of the distribution, but its asymmetric Laplace form and peak position do not change.

5
Optimum resetting

According to
[
1
]
, the renewed equation for survival probabilities is written as

Q_{r}(x_{0},t)=\mathrm{e}^{-rt}Q_{0}(x_{0},t)+r\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}\,Q_{0}(X_{r},\tau)\,Q_{r}(x_{0},t-\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(5.1)

where
Q_{r}(x_{0},t)
denotes
Q_{r}(x_{0},t|X_{r})
shorthandedly, as well as similarly for
Q_{0}
, to lighten the notations. In equation (
5.1
) the first term is related to trajectories without resetting, whereas the second term represents trajectories under resetting. After the Laplace transform and when the initial position and resetting position coincide, we have

\bar{Q}_{r}(X_{r},s)=\frac{\bar{Q}_{0}(X_{r},r+s)}{1-r\bar{Q}_{0}(X_{r},r+s)}.

(5.2)

This is a very general result for Poissonian resetting, also applied to non-Gaussian stochastic processes. Using the Laplace transform of the subdiffusive propagator
G_{\Psi}(x,t|x_{0})
in the general form (
4.3
), we find the clear expression for
\bar{Q}_{0}(X_{r},r+s)
, using

\bar{Q}_{0}(x_{0},s)=\frac{1-\exp\Big\{-\frac{x_{0}}{2D}\left[\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(s)}-\mu\right]\Big\}}{s}.

(5.3)

Then, the mean time for the particle to reach a target is
\langle T(X_{r})\rangle=\bar{Q}_{r}(X_{r},0)
expressed in terms of the survival probability
Q_{0}(X_{r},t)
in the absence of resetting. Thus, the mean time of one-dimensional subordinated diffusion with Poissonian resetting yields

\langle T(X_{r})\rangle=\frac{1}{r}\left(\mathrm{e}^{z_{\Psi}}-1\right),

(5.4)

where
z_{\Psi}
has the following form

z_{\Psi}=\frac{X_{r}}{2D}\left[\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4D\bar{\Psi}(r)}-\mu\right].

(5.5)

When
\mu\geqslant 0
, the value
z_{\Psi}
varies from zero to infinity. However, if
\mu<0
, then
z_{\Psi}\geqslant{2|\mu|X_{r}}/{2D}
. This imposes certain conditions on finding the optimum resetting rate. For
r\to 0
and
r\to\infty
, the mean time
\langle T(X_{r})\rangle
tends to infinity, whereas between them the optimum resetting rate is located.

As an illustrative example, we consider the ordinary subdiffusion having
\bar{\Psi}(s)=s^{\alpha}
with
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
. It is convenient to define a reduced variable

z=\frac{X_{r}}{2D}\left[\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4Dr^{\alpha}}-\mu\right],

(5.6)

and rewriting the resetting rate
r
in terms of
z
, we have

r=\Biggl[\left(\frac{\mu}{X_{r}}\right)z+\left(\frac{D}{X_{r}^{2}}\right)z^{2}\Biggr]^{1/\alpha}.

(5.7)

Substituting equation (
5.7
) into equation (
5.4
), the mean time in terms of
z
is written as

\langle T_{r}\rangle=\frac{X_{r}^{2/\alpha}z^{1-1/\alpha}}{(X_{r}\mu+Dz)^{1/\alpha}}\left[\frac{\exp(z)-1}{z}\right].

(5.8)

To find the optimum resetting rate, we look for a solution to the equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle T_{r}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}r}=\frac{\alpha X_{r}^{2/\alpha}z^{1-1/\alpha}}{(X_{r}\mu+2Dz)(X_{r}\mu+Dz)^{1/\alpha-1}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle T_{r}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}z}\right]=0.

(5.9)

Substituting equation (
5.8
) into equation (
5.9
), we get the following transcendental equation

\left[\left(z-\frac{2}{\alpha}+1\right)\exp(z)-1+\frac{2}{\alpha}\right]\mathrm{Pe}=\left[\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{z}{2}\right)\exp(z)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right]z\,,

(5.10)

where
\mathrm{Pe}={\mu X_{r}}/{2D}
denotes the Péclet number which is the ratio between the rates of advective and diffusive transport
[
38
]
. For obvious reasons, the value
z=0
will not be taken into account as a root of equation (
5.10
). The parameter
\alpha
characterizes the contribution of random traps in which the particle is frozen in motion for a random time interval from the
\alpha
-stable distribution (figure
1
). If
\alpha=1
, no traps exist which is typical of an ordinary diffusion. Another limit case, having
\alpha=0
, manifests the particle confinement forever. In the pure-subdiffusion limit,
\mathrm{Pe}=0
, equation (
5.10
) is reduced to a simple form

1-\exp(-z)=\frac{z\alpha}{2},

(5.11)

which has a single root for each value
\alpha

[
9
,
39
]
. For example, the pure-diffusion case gives
z\simeq 1.5936
mentioned in
[
1
,
26
]
. The tendency of the parameter
\alpha
from one to zero leads to a monotonous increase in the value of the root to infinity. It should be noted that for
\alpha=1
, the transcendental equation (
5.10
) is simplified to

\left[\left(z-1\right)\exp(z)+1\right]\mathrm{Pe}=\left[\left(1-\frac{z}{2}\right)\exp(z)-1\right]z,

(5.12)

obtained in another way for pure diffusion in
[
26
]
. The left-hand side of equation (
5.10
) can be equal to zero not only because of
\mathrm{Pe}=0
. The point is that the equation

F_{1}(z,\alpha)=\left[\left(z-\frac{2}{\alpha}+1\right)\exp(z)-1+\frac{2}{\alpha}\right]=0

(5.13)

also has a positive root depending on the parameter
\alpha
. Note that the roots (not equal to zero) of equations (
5.11
), (
5.13
) are different for the same value
\alpha
. The graphic solution of equation (
5.10
) with the optimum resetting rate is represented in figure
4
, plotting the left-hand side,
F_{1}(z,\alpha)\,\mathrm{Pe}
(colored lines), and the right-hand side,
F_{2}(z,\alpha):=\left\{\left[({1}/{\alpha})-({z}/{2})\right]\exp(z)-({1}/{\alpha})\right\}z
(black line), of this equation. The solutions, denoted as
z^{\star}>0
, are the
z
values for which
F_{1}(z,\alpha)\,\mathrm{Pe}
and
F_{2}(z,\alpha)
intersect. Consequently, for
\mathrm{Pe}>0
, equation (
5.10
) has a single positive solution
z^{\star}
. Only if
\alpha=1
, for
\mathrm{Pe}<1
, equation (
5.10
) has one non-trivial positive solution, whereas for
\mathrm{Pe}\geqslant 1
the optimum restart rates are always zero. This effect was called a restart transition
[
26
]
. It means that the restart speeds up the first-passage process for
\mathrm{Pe}<1
, but not for
\mathrm{Pe}\geqslant 1
. The feature is due to the fact that the equation
F_{1}(z,1)=0
has only a trivial solution at
z=0
and
\lim_{z\to 0}F_{2}(z,1)=0
, but
F_{2}(0,1)/F_{1}(0,1)=1
. Although equation (
5.10
) has two positive roots for
\mathrm{Pe}<0
, the lesser of them does not satisfy the condition
z\geqslant 2|\mathrm{Pe}|
, following from equation (
5.6
). Therefore, it is just neglected.

6
Asymmetric non-Laplace stationary state

The Laplace form of stationary states in equations (
2.4
), (
3.6
) and (
4.4
) is conditioned by a direct or indirect (through subordination) connection with Brownian motion. If it is another stochastic process, for example, Lévy motion, keeping the Poissonian resetting, then the stationary state undergoes changes. Let us consider this case below. Lévy motion consists of space jumps belonging to the domain of attraction of
\beta
-stable distribution with
0<\beta<2

[
33
]
, and this stochastic process is a a continuous limit of the sum of such jumps. The PDF of Lévy motion does not have such a clear analytical form as Brownian motion. Therefore, we consider the characteristic function as the Fourier transformation of the Green function, namely

\hat{p}(k,t|x_{0})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x,t|x_{0})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(6.1)

For the
\beta
-stable Lévy motion, the characteristic exponent is equal to
|k|^{\beta}
. Applying the resetting equation (
2.3
) with the corresponding propagator
G(x,t|x_{0})
, it is easy to study this relation in the Fourier space, passing from
x
to
k
. As a result, the stationary state of the characteristic function is described by the Laplace transform integral

\hat{p}(k,\infty|X_{r})=r\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}\,\hat{G}(k,\tau|X_{r})\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(6.2)

If
\mu=0
, it is not difficult to get

\hat{p}(k,\infty|X_{r})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kX_{r}}}{1+{\mathcal{D}}|k|^{\beta}/r},

(6.3)

where
{\mathcal{D}}
is a generalized diffusive constant. This PDF satisfies
p(x,0)=\delta(x)
or
\hat{p}(k,0)=1
. The term
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kX_{r}}
defines the PDF maximum located at
X_{r}
, and the PDF expression itself is nothing else, but the Linnik distribution
[
40
]
. Linnik distributions are infinitely divisible, and they have an infinite density peak at zero for
0<\beta\leqslant 1
and a finite peak at zero for
1<\beta\leqslant 2

[
41
]
. The Lévy motion in a constant force field has the same Lévy distribution as calculated for the free Lévy motion, but in the Galilei transformed system
x\to x-\mu t

[
42
]
. Therefore, the general expression
\hat{p}(k,\infty|X_{r})
for any
\mu
takes the following form

\hat{p}(k,\infty|X_{r})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kX_{r}}}{1+{\mathcal{D}}|k|^{\beta}/r+\mathrm{i}\mu k/r},

(6.4)

describing the asymmetric Linnik distribution
[
29
,
32
]
as a stationary state. The evolution of
p(x,t|x_{0})
to this state is illustrated in figure
5
. Here, the value
\mu
acts as a skew parameter. The asymmetric distribution is obtained so that each branch of it (left-hand and right-hand) turns out to be a copy of the corresponding branches of different symmetric Linnik distributions.

7
Conclusions

Eventually, we have shown that the linear potential, acting on the subordinated Brownian motion (subdiffusion), leads to an asymmetric form of the Laplace distribution as a stationary state typical of a wide class of subdiffusion under Poissonian resetting. If the potential tends to zero, the stationary PDF takes the ordinary Laplace form
[
39
]
. Each asymmetric Laplace PDF is characterized by three parameters:
X_{r}
is the location parameter,
\lambda
is the scale parameter, and
\kappa
is the asymmetric parameter. The first of them is the same for any stochastic process under resetting, whereas the second one depends on
r
and
D
. The contribution of
r
is determined by the Laplace exponent of the stochastic process subjected to resetting, whereas
D
is not. By changing the rate
r
(reset protocol) at a constant value
D
and finding the scaling parameter of the Laplace distribution, one can restore the Laplace exponent of its directing random process. As the stochastic process under resetting is a subordinated Brownian motion, it is not difficult to establish the latter exactly. If the parameter
\kappa\neq 1
, then the subordinated Brownian motion is in a linear potential. Knowing
\kappa
, we can find the value
\mu
related to the potential. The parameter
\kappa
is dependent on
r
,
D
and
\mu
. The Lévy processes with resetting in potential manifest also a stationary state, but its PDF is a generalization of the asymmetric Laplace distribution in the sense of asymmetric geometrically infinitely divisible PDFs. This analysis also brings us to another interesting feature induced by resetting: the mean first passage time is minimized for an optimum choice of the resetting rate.

Appendix A
Derivation of an integral relation

Let a particle diffuse under Poissonian resetting. Then, the first renewal equation is written down as

p_{1}(x,t|x_{0})=\mathrm{e}^{-rt}G_{1}(x,t|x_{0})+r\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{f}\,\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau_{f}}\,p_{1}(x,t-\tau_{f}|X_{r}),

(A.1)

where the first resetting at time
\tau_{f}
has started from
t=0
. Taking the Laplace transform yields

\bar{p}_{1}(x,s|x_{0})=\bar{G}_{1}(x,r+s|x_{0})+r\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{-st}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}p_{1}(x,t-\tau|X_{r})\,.

(A.2)

The second term becomes

r\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-st^{\prime}-(r+s)\tau}\,p_{1}(x,t^{\prime}|X_{r})=\frac{r}{r+s}\,\bar{p}_{1}(x,s|X_{r}),

(A.3)

so that the Laplace transform of the first renewal equation obeys

\bar{p}_{1}(x,s|x_{0})=\bar{G}_{1}(x,r+s|x_{0})+\frac{r}{r+s}\,\bar{p}_{1}(x,s|X_{r})\,.

(A.4)

Taking
x_{0}=X_{r}
, the last expression is transformed into the following form

\bar{G}_{1}(x,r+s|X_{r})=\frac{s}{r+s}\,\bar{p}_{1}(x,s|X_{r})\,.

(A.5)

Substituting it in (
A.4
), one obtains

\bar{p}_{1}(x,s|x_{0})=\bar{G}_{1}(x,r+s|x_{0})+\frac{r}{s}\,\bar{G}_{1}(x,r+s|X_{r})\,.

(A.6)

The inverse Laplace transform gives

p_{1}(x,t|x_{0})=\mathrm{e}^{-rt}G_{1}(x,t|x_{0})+r\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}\,G_{1}(x,\tau|X_{r})\,,

(A.7)

which is just the last renewal equation equal to equation (
2.3
). Using this approach, the equivalence of (
A.1
) and (
A.7
) was proved in
[
1
]
.
