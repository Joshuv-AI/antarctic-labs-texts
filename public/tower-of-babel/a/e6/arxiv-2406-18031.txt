Real-time Structure Flow
Juan David Adarve, Robert Mahony

Abstract
This article introduces the structure flow field; a flow field that can provide high-speed robo-centric motion information for motion control of highly dynamic robotic devices and autonomous vehicles. Structure flow is the angular 3D velocity of the scene at a given pixel. We show that structure flow posses an elegant evolution model in the form of a Partial Differential Equation (PDE) that enables us to create dense flow predictions forward in time. We exploit this structure to design a predictor-update algorithm to compute structure flow in real time using image and depth measurements. The prediction stage takes the previous estimate of the structure flow and propagates it forward in time using a numerical implementation of the structure flow PDE. The predicted flow is then updated using new image and depth data. The algorithm runs up to 600 Hz on a Desktop GPU machine for 512x512 images with flow values up to 8 pixels. We provide ground truth validation on high-speed synthetic image sequences as well as results on real-life video on driving scenarios.

Introduction

Reliable and robust motion control is a central component of all autonomous robotic systems.
As robotic vehicles become more mobile and begin to operate in highly unstructured and dynamic environments, such as urban streetscapes, the requirements on the perception subsystem of the robot are correspondingly more demanding.
Classically, most autonomous robot use a combination of Simultaneous Localisation And Mapping (SLAM)
[
CCC+16
]
along with dedicated sensor-control feedback for motion control.
More specifically, SLAM algorithms are used to provide large scale (sparse) environment representations appropriate for path planning and decision processes, while real-time motion control loops are closed using high-bandwidth direct sensor feedback.
Sensor systems capable of providing a dense, high-bandwidth, 3D spatiotemporal (structure and motion) representation of an outdoor environment, such as LIDAR, are expensive, and often bulky and heavy.
In contrast, high frame-rate vision systems offer a rich, dense, and dynamic sensor modality for low-weight and low-price.
Vision systems, however, require sophisticated post processing to extract spatiotemporal information.
Modern dense visual SLAM implementations
[
MCR15
,
WSMG+16
]
demonstrate the potential of vision systems to provide situational awareness to autonomous robotic vehicles, although these algorithms still suffer from limitations such as; (relatively) low update rates, static environment assumptions and limited map size.

We propose the following aspirational goal requirements for a modern vision based spatiotemporal perception system capable of providing real-time situational awareness for a robot moving in a highly unstructured and dynamic environment such as a streetscape:

•

Egocentric (robo-centric) map representation.

•

Dense range estimation to the environment.
Benchmark: angular resolution 0.1 degrees.

•

Dense 3D-velocity (structure flow) estimation.
Benchmark: resolution 0.05rad.s
-1
, (for example, 0.05m.s
-1
error in 1m.s
-1
velocity at 1m distance).

•

Real-time (high-bandwdith) updates.
Benchmark: updates at 300 Hz.

Such a system would provide sufficient information for robust local path planning and obstacle avoidance.
High level path planning and decision processes could be undertaken separately based on more traditional large scale SLAM algorithms.
The present work also continues development (see also
[
AM16a
]
) of a new paradigm of vision processing algorithms based on real-time filtering concepts, that we believe will provide an important tool in the future development of autonomous robotic systems.

The technical contributions of this article are twofold.
First, we introduce a new robo-centric spatiotemporal representation of motion, the
structure flow
.
The structure flow is a 3-vector assigned to each ‘pixel’ in the image comprised of the three-dimensional Euclidean velocity between the robot and the environment (the scene flow
[
VBR+99
]
) scaled by the inverse range of the scene.
Analogously to optic flow, structure flow encodes information about the visually perceived motion and ego-motion of the scene.
However, structure flow generalises optic flow by including a component in the normal direction of the image that is closely linked to visual divergence.
Structure flow will have a similar utility as optic flow for control of robotic vehicles
[
HM02
,
RF05
,
HRHM08
,
Sri11
]
with the added advantage that the normal component of structure flow directly yields the divergent flow properties used in many of the obstacle avoidance algorithms
[
MB12
]
.
A key advantage of structure flow is that, for reasonable assumptions on the environment and robot motion, it is possible to derive a dynamic model of the field evolution in the form of a Partial Differential Equation (PDE).
Moreover, the structure flow PDE follows the same pattern as the partial differential equation modeling the evolution of the inverse depth field
[
BR09
,
ZAR12
]
.
We exploit this to forward propagate estimates of the structure flow and the inverse depth in image coordinates to make predictions of future motion and structure of the environment.

The second technical contribution of the paper lies in proposing an iterative algorithm to compute structure flow in real-time from stereo image data.
The approach taken is a highly distributed predictor-update algorithm that we implement on a GPU.
The prediction step is a numerical implementation of a PDE integration
scheme that propagates the current estimate of the structure flow forward in time.
Error in the structure flow estimate can then be estimated across a sequence of temporal frames, and we use a simple least-squares regularised update to iteratively correct errors in the structure flow estimate.
The resulting algorithm runs on a Nvidia GTX-780 GPU, processing
512\times 512
at approximately 600 Hz for flow vectors up to 8 pixels in magnitude.
The relative performance of the algorithm far outperforms (10-20 times faster) all scene flow algorithms in the literature in terms of processing speed.
Although it is less accurate than some classical algorithms, its advantages in speed, dense estimation, and robustness make it highly suitable for real-world mobile robotic applications.

Related Work

The estimation of motion from image sequences is a heavily studied problem in computer vision and robotics
[
Bar94
,
BSL+11
]
.
The importance of these motion fields in the control of mobile robotic vehicles is equally important, and has been studied for more than thirty years in applications such as visual control of aerial vehicles
[
RF05
,
Sri11
,
HHMR12
]
and visual odometry
[
SF11
]
.

The most common motion field considered is the
optical flow
field, defined as the displacement of points in the image plane
[
Bar94
]
.
Algorithms for the computation of flow can be classified as local or global based methods, from which the seminal works of Lucas and Kanade (local)
[
LK81
]
and Horn and Schunck
[
HS81
]
(global) marked a distinction between local and global methods. Recent algorithms can be found in benchmark datasets such as Middlebury
[
BSL+11
]
and Kitti
[
GLSU13
]
.
In robotics, an underlying requirement for flow algorithms is their capability to run at real-time frequencies.
Real time methods
[
PLBC14
,
AM16a
]
are usually local-based methods, which can be efficiently implemented using GPU or FPGA hardware.
The vast majority of optical flow algorithms developed in by the computer-vision community in the last ten years are focused on accuracy and not on computational efficiency
[
BSL+11
,
GLSU13
]
.

A more recent visual flow is
scene flow
, defined as the three-dimensional velocity field of points moving in the world
[
VBR+99
]
expressed in the ‘image’ frame of the robot.
Stereoscopic algorithms use image sequences captured using a stereo camera to estimate the 3D scene flow.
Early work by Patras
et al.

[
PAT96
]
formulates the joint estimation of disparity and motion (scene flow) from stereo sequences.
Huguet and Devernay
[
HD07
]
proposed a variational approach for the joint computation of disparity and optical flow. This formulation of optical flow plus temporal disparity change is known as
disparity flow

[
GY06
]
.
Wedel
et al.

[
WBV+11
]
decouple the variational formulation of Huguet
et. al.
to compute flow and disparity in two separate stages while preserving stereo constraints.
Vogel
et. al.

[
VSR15
]
compute scene flow by means of a piece-wise planar segmentation of stereo images for which a 3D rotation and translation (scene flow) is computed.
The accuracy of recent two-pair stereo based algorithms is compiled on the Kitti scene flow dataset by Menze and Geiger
[
MG15
]
.
Algorithms using RGB-D sensors to compute scene flow combine monocular images and depth measurements to estimate the 3D velocity field.
Letouzey
et. al.

[
LPB11
]
combine sparse features points and dense smoothness constraints to estimate dense scene flow from RGB-D data. Hadfield and Bowden
[
HB11
]
use a particle filter formulation to track points in the scene.
Quiroga
et. al.

[
QDC12
]
use brightness and depth data in a Lukas-Kanade type tracking framework to compute scene flow parametrized as a rotation plus translation transform.

If only depth/range measurements are available it is still possible to compute
range flow
.
Yamamoto
et. al.
derived the range flow constraint
[
YBBR93
]
, a partial differential equation to model the change of depth.
Differential methods to compute range flow suffer from a 3D version of the aperture problem similar to that found in optical flow methods
[
SJB02
]
.
Herbst
et. al.

[
HRF13
]
use this differential constraint on depth and combined with color image to derive a variational method to compute scene flow following a formulation similar to Brox
et. al.

[
BBPW04
]
for computing optical flow.

In terms of real-time capable dense 3D motion estimation algorithms, most methods use Graphics Processing Units (GPU) for implementing per-pixel level operations in parallel.
Gong
[
Gon09
]
reports 12 Hz optical and disparity flow estimation on QVGA image resolution (
320\times 240
).
Rabe
et. al.

[
RMWF10
]
reported 25 Hz frequency on VGA (
640\times 480
) image sequences using GPU.
The algorithm of Wedel
et. al.

[
WBV+11
]
reports frame rates of 20 Hz also at QVGA resolution.
RGB-D flow by Herbst
et. al.

[
HRF13
]
runs between 8-30 Hz at QVGA depending on the amount of smoothing required.

is the orthogonal projection onto

\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}=(\mathbf{I}_{3}-\boldsymbol{\eta}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}).

(2)

Optical, scene and structure flow

Let

For the present derivation we will assume that

With this assumption, the second order kinematics of the camera are

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}_{c}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\Omega_{\times}\mathbf{v}_{c}+\boldsymbol{a}_{c}

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=0

(4)

Both

Let

Let

With these assumptions, the first and second order kinematics of a point

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\Omega_{\times}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{v}_{c},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

The approach taken in this paper is to parameterize the environment surrounding the robot by direction

The range or depth map around the robot is a single valued depth field

Although range

(8)

where

We will choose
0pt_{\text{ref}}=1

notation in the remainder of the paper.
However, one should still think of

as a dimensionless ratio of ranges related to the perceived visual angle of an object at range

as observed on the sphere of radius

is intrinsically a visual measure or angle, whereas
0pt(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
is extrinsically related to the physical scene and has units of metres.

The scene flow is defined as the 3D velocity of the environment relative to the camera

]

to evolve such that

The scene flow is the vector field

In particular,

A more common notation in the literature used for the total derivative is

We have chosen to use a more explicit notation in this paper to provide more clarity in the derivations.

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{s}(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{0pt(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)}(\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{v}_{c}).

3.2
Evolution equations

This section derives the fundamental partial differential equations that model the temporal change of image brightness, structure flow and inverse depth on the spherical camera.

3.2.1
Image brightness

Let
Y(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
denote the image brightness in the direction
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in S^{2}
at time
t
associated to point
\mathbf{x}(t)
in the scene.
That is
Y:S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is modeled as a scalar field on the sphere.
A common assumption on differential methods for computing either optical or scene flow is that image brightness is constant and the surface is Lambertian.
That is, the brightness value corresponding to point
\mathbf{x}(t)
as seen by the camera does not change with the evolution of
\mathbf{x}(t)
in time.
This assumption corresponds to the well known constant brightness condition on the
total derivative
of the image brightness field,

\frac{\mathrm{d}Y}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}Y(\boldsymbol{\eta}(\tau))}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right|_{\tau=t}=0.

(18)

Expressing the total derivative of
Y(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
in terms of its partial derivatives one obtains

\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial Y}{\partial t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}Y}{\mathrm{d}t}

(19)

Here
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in T_{\eta}S^{2}
denotes the image gradient and it is an element of the tangent space of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.
Moreover,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right|_{\tau=t}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
is the optical flow vector at
\boldsymbol{\eta}
defined in Equation (
16
).
Expressing PDE (
19
) in terms of structure flow, one has

\frac{\partial Y}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}

(20)

Equation (
20
) is the classical brightness conservation equation
[
Bar94
]
expressed in spherical camera coordinates and with respect to structure flow.

3.2.2
Structure flow

Analogous to the brightness conservation, we consider
\mathbf{w}(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
as a continuous vector field on the sphere. The relative change with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
t
is

\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}

(21)

where
\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{w}
at
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}(\boldsymbol{\eta}(\tau),\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right|_{\tau=t}
is the optical flow as derived in (
16
).
In this case, the total flow is not conserved, that is,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\neq 0
, and one must substitute (
17
) to derive the associated PDE

\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}\rangle-\Omega_{\times}\mathbf{w}+\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathbf{w}}

(22)

Equation (
22
) describes a non-linear transport process where the structure flow is propagated on the sphere at a velocity equal to the induced optical flow
\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}
.
Additionally, the source terms inject or remove energy from the structure flow field according to the kinematics of the robot and the scene relative to it.

3.2.3
Depth and inverse depth

Similar partial differential equations can be derived for the conservation of the depth and inverse depth fields,
0pt(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
and
\rho(\boldsymbol{\eta},t)
, respectively. Considering the total derivatives of depth and inverse depth developed in Equations (
13
) and (
14
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial 0pt}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial 0pt}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}+0pt\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}\rangle

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}-\rho\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}\rangle

(24)

The conservation of inverse depth has previously been reported in the literature
[
BR09
,
ZAR12
]
.

In practice, Equation (
24
) is preferred since
\rho
is a dimensionless ratio (
8
) that has an intrinsic interpretation as visual measure.
Note also that (
24
) has the same structure as the first two terms of (
22
) which leads to desirable computational properties of the combined system of equations considered in Section
4
.

4.1
Spherical image data structure

Numerical implementation of the different stages of the algorithm are performed directly on the Spherepix data structure
[
AM16b
]
, a data structure based on spherical image geometry.
This allows us to preserve the original geometry used to derive the conservation equations in Section
3.2
, ensures robustness and consistency of the algorithm over wide field of view, and provides data in an ideal format for incorporation into robotic control algorithms.

Figure 4:
Spherepix grid. Spherical coordinate
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}
and its associated tangent plane
T_{\eta_{ij}}S^{2}
. Vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in T_{\eta_{ij}}S^{2}
can be represented as a 2-vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in the (row, column) direction.

Figure
4
illustrates the relevant properties of a Spherepix image.
At a given pixel
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}\in S^{2}
, neighboring points
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are projected onto tangent space coordinates
\boldsymbol{\mu}
using the orthographic projection

\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}\boldsymbol{\eta}.

(27)

As points
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in T_{\eta_{ij}}S^{2}
only have two degrees of freedom, it is convenient to express them as 2D plane coordinates. Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the 2D coordinate representation of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. One has

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}

(28)

where
\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}
is an orthonormal basis matrix at
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}
.
The row entries of
\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}
correspond to the normalized projections of pixels
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i,j+1}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i+1,j}
on the tangent space of
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}
.
The transformation from 2D to 3D tangent space coordinates is

\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}\boldsymbol{\beta}

(29)

since
\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}
is orthonormal and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
lies in the tangent space.
Finally, a scalar
\Delta\mu_{ij}=\left\|\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i,j+1}\right\|
measures the pixel separation between two neighbors in tangent space.

4.2
Brightness parameters extraction

Raw image measurements
Y
from the camera are used to fit a linear brightness model for each pixel
p=(i,j)
.
The model parameters correspond to the image gradient
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}_{p}\in T_{\eta_{p}}S^{2}
and the constant term
\hat{Y}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
at
p
.

Computation of these parameters is performed in two stages.
First, the cost function (
30
) is minimized for parameters
\{\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{Y}_{p},\hat{Y}_{p}\}
expressed in local 2D plane coordinates.

\varepsilon_{p}=\sum_{q\in\Lambda_{p}}G(p,q)\|Y_{q}-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{Y}_{p}(p-q)-\hat{Y}_{p}\|^{2},

(30)

where
\Lambda_{p}
is a
5\times 5
support window of pixels centered on
p
, and
G(p,q)
is a Gaussian weight mask
G=g^{\top}g
with
g=[1,4,6,4,1]/16
.
Thanks to the spatial symmetry of the support window and weight function, it is possible to efficiently solve for
\{\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{Y}_{p},\hat{Y}_{p}\}
as a series of 1D convolutions in the row and column axis.
The two-dimensional image gradient
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{Y}_{p}
is then expressed as a 3D tangent vector
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}_{p}
following Equation (
29
)

\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}_{p}=\Delta\mu_{p}\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$p$}}^{\top}\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{Y}_{p}.

(31)

4.3
Inverse depth parameters extraction

In this paper we assume that depth measurements are available and are inverted for the data fitting process.
We fit a linear model comprised of parameters
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{\rho}_{p}\in T_{\eta_{p}}S^{2}
for the inverse depth gradient vector and
\hat{\rho}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
for the constant term.
In particular for the computation of
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{\rho}_{p}
, one must properly handle object occlusions to remove undesired large gradient vectors.
At each pixel location, we compute the gradient vector on both sides of a discontinuity and select the appropriate one.
That is, the two-dimensional gradient vector
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\rho}_{p}=(\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}}\hat{\rho}_{p},\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}}\hat{\rho}_{p})^{\top}
is computed as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}}\hat{\rho}_{p}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{+}$}}\rho_{p}&\text{if}~~|\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{+}$}}\rho_{p}|\leq|\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{-}$}}\rho_{p}|\\
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{-}$}}\rho_{p}&\text{otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

(32)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}}\hat{\rho}_{p}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{+}$}}\rho_{p}&\text{if}~~|\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{+}$}}\rho_{p}|\leq|\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{-}$}}\rho_{p}|\\
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{-}$}}\rho_{p}&\text{otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

(33)

where
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{${}^{+}$}}
,
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{${}^{-}$}}
denote forward and backward difference operators in the
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
(column) and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
(row) axis. Table
1
defines these as well as the central difference operator.

\delta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}}u_{ij}

\delta_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}}u_{ij}

backward

\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{-}$}}=u_{j}-u_{j-1}

\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{-}$}}=u_{i}-u_{i-1}

central

\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{0}$}}=u_{j+1}-u_{j-1}

\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{0}$}}=u_{i+1}-u_{i-1}

forward

\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{+}$}}=u_{j+1}-u_{j}

\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{+}$}}=u_{i+1}-u_{i}

Difference operators in beta coordinates.

The 3D vector representation
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{\rho}_{p}\in T_{\eta_{p}}S^{2}
is

\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{\rho}_{p}=\Delta\mu_{p}\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$p$}}^{\top}\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{\rho}_{p}

(34)

The constant parameter term is given by the raw inverse depth data; that is,
\hat{\rho}_{p}=\rho_{p}
.

4.4
State prediction (
k\rightarrow k+
)

The prediction stage of the filter uses the current state estimate to create predictions of the structure flow forward in time.
In the present paper, we choose to ignore the source terms derived from inertial measurements in the PDE (
36
).
That is, we assume

\Omega_{\times}\mathbf{w}-\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathbf{w}}\approx 0.

(35)

In practice, for high frame rates and the scenarios that we consider, these terms have sub-pixel magnitudes and are at least an order of magnitude less significant than the other terms in the propagation algorithm.
In particular, the contribution of this term is proportional to the magnitude of
\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{c}
and inversely proportional to frame rate.
Thus, for high frame rates and moderate motion these terms become insignificant.
Figure
5
plots the

.
Cost function (
42
) uses low resolution image and inverse depth measurements, as well as the predicted flow from the propagation stage to create a new structure flow estimate
\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-16.56667pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 6.51944pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+1}}}}_{{\kern-12.89166pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 6.51944pt{}}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-16.56667pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 6.51944pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+1}}}}_{{\kern-12.89166pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 6.51944pt{}}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-11.27802pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 4.06969pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+1}}}}_{{\kern-8.65302pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 4.06969pt{}}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-10.11525pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 2.90692pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+1}}}}_{{\kern-7.49025pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 2.90692pt{}}}}
. The cost function

^{H}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}=\gamma_{1}\|^{H}E_{Y}\|^{2}+\gamma_{2}\|^{H}E_{\rho}\|^{2}+\gamma_{3}\|^{H}E_{t}\|^{2}

(42)

consists of three data terms:
{}^{H}E_{Y}
(image brightness conservation),
{}^{H}E_{\rho}
(depth measurements) and
{}^{H}E_{t}
(regularisation based on previous estimate).
Scalar gains
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\gamma_{3}
control the relative weight of each term.

The image data term
E_{Y}
is based on the image conservation PDE (
20
). It is defined as

^{H}E_{Y}=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}^{k+1}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}^{k+1}+\Delta\mu^{2}(\hat{Y}^{k+1}-\hat{Y}^{k})

(43)

where
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}^{k+1}
,
\hat{Y}^{k+1}
and
\hat{Y}^{k}
are the image gradient and constant terms computed at the image model block. The temporal difference term is multiplied by
\Delta\mu^{2}
to compensate for the transformation of gradient vector
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}^{k+1}
to tangent space coordinates.
This cost term only constrains one degree of freedom of
\mathbf{w}
, corresponding to the tangential component of structure flow in the direction of the image gradient vector
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{Y}
.
This is the well known aperture problem in optical flow estimation.

The inverse depth data term
E_{\rho}
uses the inverse depth conservation PDE (
24
) to recover the structure flow from state
\rho^{k}
and measurements and
\hat{\rho}^{k+1}
. The data term is

{}^{H}E_{\rho}=~

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{\rho}^{k+1}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}^{k+1}

\displaystyle+\Delta\mu^{2}\left((\hat{\rho}^{k+1}-\rho^{k})+\hat{\rho}^{k+1}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{k+1}\rangle\right)

(44)

Analogously to the image cost term, the scalar component of Equation (
44
) is multiplied by
\Delta\mu^{2}
to compensate for the transformation of vector quantities to 3D tangent space coordinates. As PDE (
24
) includes the effect of
\mathbf{w}
along normal direction
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, it is possible to recover both tangent and normal components of the flow field.
For the tangent component, it still suffers from a form of aperture problem where only flow in the direction of the inverse depth gradient can be recovered
[
SJB02
]
.

Finally, the temporal smoothing or regularisation term,
E_{t}
, includes the predicted flow
\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-16.56667pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 6.51944pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+}}}}_{{\kern-12.89166pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 6.51944pt{}}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-16.56667pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 6.51944pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+}}}}_{{\kern-12.89166pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 6.51944pt{}}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-11.27802pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 4.06969pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+}}}}_{{\kern-8.65302pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 4.06969pt{}}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}}}_{{{\scalebox{0.6}{}}}}}\mathbf{w}^{{\kern-10.11525pt{\scalebox{0.6}{H}}\kern 2.90692pt{\scalebox{0.7}{k+}}}}_{{\kern-7.49025pt{\scalebox{0.6}{}}\kern 2.90692pt{}}}}
as prior solution to the cost function

{}^{H}E_{t}=\mathbf{w}^{k+1}+\mathbf{w}^{k+}

4.6
Numerical prediction

Considering the real-time nature of the structure flow filtering algorithm, we desire a numerical scheme that is fast and robust to noisy input for solving the propagation equations in Section
4.4
.
We propose a numerical scheme based on upwind finite differences
[
Tho95
]
following a similar approach to our previous work on optical flow algorithms
[
AM16a
]
.

Consider Equation (
53
) as a discrete version of prediction Equations (
36
) and (
38
).

\frac{\mathbf{w}^{n+1}-\mathbf{w}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}^{n}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}}\mathbf{w}^{n}-\mathbf{w}^{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{n}\rangle

(53)

where
\mathbf{w}^{n+1}
is the unknown flow at time index
n+1
. For convenience in the notation, we omit pyramid level index
h
and pixel coordinate
(i,j)
, as the scheme runs identically at every level and for each pixel. Index
n
refers to an internal time index for the numerical scheme running for
n=0,\dots,N-1
with
\Delta t=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
and
N
being a parameter describing the number of iterations. Initial conditions of the problem are set to
\mathbf{w}^{0}:=\mathbf{w}^{k}
and the output of the scheme is
\mathbf{w}^{N-1}=:\mathbf{w}^{k+}
.

Equation (
53
) is a non-linear PDE on
\mathbf{w}
for which direct application of finite difference methods are not reliable
[
Tho99
, p. 140]
.
To break this non-linearity we use the idea of
dominant optical flow
.
Optical flow in 2D tangent plane coordinates and pixel units is computed as

\Phi_{ij}:=\begin{pmatrix}u_{ij}\\
v_{ij}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\Delta\mu}\mathbf{B}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}\mathbf{P}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ij}$}}\mathbf{w}_{ij}

(54)

The dominant optical flow
\hat{\Phi}_{ij}=(\hat{u}^{n}_{ij},\hat{v}^{n}_{ij})
is calculated as the largest flow component in the row and column axis

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}u_{i,j-1}&\text{if}~~\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{0}$}}|u_{ij}|>0\\
u_{i,j+1}&\text{otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

(55)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}v_{i-1,j}&\text{if}~~\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{0}$}}|v_{ij}|>0\\
v_{i+1,j}&\text{otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

(56)

with
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{0}$}}
and
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{0}$}}
the central difference operators defined in Table
1
.

Functions
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(\cdot;\hat{u})
and
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}$}}(\cdot;\hat{v})
define the upwind finite difference operators in column and row axis. They are computed as

\displaystyle\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(f;\hat{u})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{-}$}}f&\text{if}~~\hat{u}>0\\
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{+}$}}f&\text{otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

(57)

\displaystyle\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}$}}(f;\hat{v})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{-}$}}f&\text{if}~~\hat{v}>0\\
\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{+}$}}f&\text{otherwise}\end{matrix}\right.

(58)

These operators evaluate the direction of the dominant flow in the column and row axis and select the adequate upwind difference operator.
By using the dominant optic flow the remaining PDE evolution is linear and has a stable solution.

The iterations of the numerical scheme are as follows.
First, the dominant optical flow
\hat{u}^{n}
is calculated from
\mathbf{w}^{n}
. Then, the propagation takes place in the
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
(column) axis for all points in the spherical grid as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}^{n}-\Delta t\left[\hat{u}^{n}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(\mathbf{w}^{n};\hat{u}^{n})+\mathbf{w}^{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{n}\rangle\right]

(59)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{*}

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{n}-\Delta t\left[\hat{u}^{n}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{n};\hat{u}^{n})+\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{n}\rangle\right]

(60)

\displaystyle\rho^{*}

\displaystyle=\rho^{n}-\Delta t\left[\hat{u}^{n}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(\rho^{n};\hat{u}^{n})+\rho^{n}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{n}\rangle\right]

(61)

\displaystyle Y^{*}

\displaystyle=Y^{n}-\Delta t\hat{u}^{n}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(Y^{n};\hat{u}^{n})

(62)

producing the propagated fields
\mathbf{w}^{*}
,
\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{*}
,
\rho^{*}
and
Y^{*}
.
Next, the dominant flow
\hat{v}^{*}
is calculated from
\mathbf{w}^{*}
and the propagation takes place in the
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
(row) axis

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}^{*}-\Delta t\left[\hat{v}^{*}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}$}}(\mathbf{w}^{*};\hat{v}^{*})+\mathbf{w}^{*}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{*}\rangle\right]

(63)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{*}-\Delta t\left[\hat{v}^{*}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}$}}(\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{*};\hat{v}^{*})+\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta$}\mathbf{w}^{*}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{*}\rangle\right]

(64)

\displaystyle\rho^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\rho^{*}-\Delta t\left[\hat{v}^{*}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}$}}(\rho^{*};\hat{u}^{*})+\rho^{*}\langle\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{w}^{*}\rangle\right]

(65)

\displaystyle Y^{n+1}

\displaystyle=Y^{*}-\Delta t\hat{v}^{*}\delta_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}$}}(Y^{*};\hat{v}^{*})

(66)

The numerical scheme is stable if inequality in Equation (
67
) is satisfied for all points in the grid for all
n
.

\Delta t\max_{n,i,j}\left\{|\hat{u}^{n}_{ij}|,|\hat{v}^{n}_{ij}|\right\}\leq 1

(67)

Given this stability condition where
\Delta t=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
, one needs to choose an suitable number of iterations
N
of the numerical scheme depending on the maximum flow expected in the application.
In particular, as the camera frame rate increases, the magnitude of the estimated flow decreases and hence, less numerical iterations are required, leading to excellent numerical properties for real-time high-speed vision applications.

5
Experimental Validation

5.1
GPU implementation

We developed a GPU implementation of the filtering algorithm for its deployment in real-time applications.
Computations within each stage of the algorithm are pixel independent and can easily be parallelized on GPU hardware.
The different stages of the algorithm are programmed and connected using a pipeline pattern.
We run the implementation on a Desktop computer with an Intel i7-4790K CPU and a Nvidia GTX-780 graphics card with 2304 CUDA cores and 3GB of memory.

5.2
Ground truth evaluation

To validate the accuracy of the proposed algorithm, we require video sequences recorded at high frame rates that sample the dynamics of the environment smoothly.
We used the Blender source files of the Urban Canyon Dataset from Zhang
et. al.

[
ZRFS16
]
to render a photo-realistic city environment in which a 300Hz camera is attached to the front part of a moving vehicle. For the evaluation, we consider a subset of the trajectory (Figure

) composed of 10000 frames.
Images are rendered using a 360 degrees panoramic camera and then mapped to a front facing spherepix grid of
512\times 512
pixels.

The structure flow ground truth

6
Conclusions and Future Work

This article introduced the structure flow field as the three-dimensional vector field encoding the velocity of the surrounding environment relative to the camera body fixed frame and scaled by the inverse depth.
We presented partial differential equations expressed in spherical geometry modeling the temporal evolution of the structure flow field, image brightness, depth and inverse depth fields.
These PDEs can be used both to derive predictors to propagate these quantities forward in time, or as innovation terms to estimate the structure flow from measured data.

A real-time algorithm for computing structure flow is proposed.
The algorithm is based on a filtering architecture that incrementally computes the flow from temporal sequences of brightness and depth data.
The algorithm achieves frame rates of the order of 600 Hz at
512\times 512
pixel resolution and 172 Hz at
1024\times 1024
for flow vectors up to 8 pixels.
Experimental validation of the algorithm using simulated high-speed video with ground truth is provided, as well as results on real-life video sequences using a stereo camera.

Assumption (
35
) is a practical assumption that allows implementation of the algorithm in the absence of a calibrated and time-synchronised inertial measurement unit.
Such a system was unavailable for the field tests that we undertook and would have involved considerable complexity in systems integration.
It is clear that inclusion of the exogenous acceleration terms would improve results, however, we do not believe that the improvement will be particularly significant due to the relatively small value of these terms (Figure
5
).
More significantly, the current formulation of the filter algorithm relies on the availability of depth measurements.
A natural extension is to express the cost functions relying on depth measurements in terms of image disparity for a coupled stereo pair.
Such approach will allow us to formulate the state update equations purely on image data and is the topic of ongoing investigation.
