Riemannian optimization on the symplectic Stiefel manifold using second–order information Thanks: Submitted to the editors 04/12 – 2024.
Rasmus Jensen, Ralf Zimmermann

Abstract
Riemannian optimization is concerned with problems, where the independent variable lies on a smooth manifold. There is a number of problems from numerical linear algebra that fall into this category, where the manifold is usually specified by special matrix structures, such as orthogonality or definiteness. Following this line of research, we investigate tools for Riemannian optimization on the symplectic Stiefel manifold. We complement the existing set of numerical optimization algorithms with a Riemannian trust region method tailored to the symplectic Stiefel manifold. To this end, we derive a matrix formula for the Riemannian Hessian under a right-invariant metric. Moreover, we propose a novel retraction for approximating the Riemannian geodesics. Finally, we conduct a comparative study in which we juxtapose the performance of the Riemannian variants of the steepest descend-, conjugate gradients- and trust region methods on selected matrix optimization problems that feature symplectic constraints.



keywords

symplectic Stiefel manifold, Riemannian optimization, retraction, trust region method, steepest descend method, nonlinear conjugate gradient method

Introduction

Riemannian optimization considers problems, where the feasible set
\M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a Riemannian manifold. Specifically, constrained problems of the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),\ \textnormal{such that}\ x\in\M,

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function,
formally become unconstrained problems

\min_{x\in\M}f(x)

(2)

with smooth
f:\M\to\mathbb{R}
, when viewed from the intrinsic perspective of the manifold.
Manifolds have been used in numerical problems for several decades, see
[
21
]
.
In numerical linear algebra applications, maps
f
from subsets of the matrix space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{nm}
to
\mathbb{R}
are of special interest.
In the seminal paper
[
14
]
, the Stiefel and Grassmann manifolds are considered for their use in numerical linear algebra applications. In particular, the authors provide essential geometric tools for implementing Riemannian generalizations of the classical (Euclidean) nonlinear conjugate gradient– and Newton methods. Since then, a substantial amount of literature has been produced, as well as textbooks; see for example
[
2
,
8
,
30
]
.

Riemannian optimization techniques can be applied to problems such as the orthogonal Procrustes problem and the symmetric eigenvalue problem
[
14
,
1
]
, the nonlinear eigenvalue problem
[
38
]
, low–rank matrix completion
[
9
]
, computation of the singular value decomposition
[
32
]
, multivariate statistics
[
25
]
and to obtain low–rank solutions of Lyapunov equations
[
34
]
.

Optimization methods can be differentiated according to their order. First-order methods only use gradient information, while second–order methods also leverage the Hessian or suitable approximations of it.
In practice, this is a trade-off: for first-order methods, the individual iterations are usually inexpensive, but the number of iterations until numerical convergence is high. For second–order methods, the computational effort per iteration is comparatively high, but the total iteration count is low, see e. g.
[
26
]
.

Methods in Riemannian optimization which exploit second–order information include the Newton method and trust-region method
[
1
,
14
]
. Approximate second–order information is exploited in the nonlinear conjugate gradients method and quasi–Newton methods
[
14
,
23
,
31
]
.

1.1
Original contributions

This paper focuses on Riemannian optimization techniques on the symplectic Stiefel manifold when equipped with a right–invariant Riemannian metric as in
[
5
]
.
We complement the existing set of first–order and second–order optimization approaches by transferring the Riemannian trust region method to the manifold under consideration.

To this end, we derive a matrix formula for the Riemannian Hessian for smooth functions on the symplectic Stiefel manifold under the right-invariant metric.
Moreover, we provide a new retraction based on the associated geodesics.

By means of numerical experiments, we juxtapose the Riemannian steepest descend, the Riemannian nonlinear conjugate gradients and the Riemannian trust region.
We consider the latter method using the exact metric Hessian operator and using the projected Euclidean Hessian operator.

We apply the various optimization methods to selected problems in numerical linear algebra.
More precisely, we consider the problems of computing the nearest symplectic matrix to a given matrix
[
5
]
, computing the first
k
symplectic eigenvalues of a symmetric positive definite matrix
[
33
]
and of computing the proper symplectic decomposition
[
5
,
6
]
.

So far, mainly first–order gradient descend approaches have been utilized

2
The symplectic Stiefel manifold

In this section we collect the necessary background theory for the symplectic Stiefel manifold. We complement the existing theory by deriving a representation for its normal space when viewed as a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}
, and we obtain a matrix formula for the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space. We introduce a new retraction, which is observed to numerically approximate the true geodesics better than the existing ones.
We discuss a low–rank formula for this retraction and discuss the computation of its directional derivative, which will eventually serve as a vector transport. Lastly, we provide a matrix formula for the Riemannian Hessian.

The symplectic Stiefel manifold is the set

\spst(2n,2k)=\quantity{U\in\R^{2n\times 2k}: U^TJ_{2n}U=J_{2k} },\ \ J_{2m}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{m}\\
-I_{m}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

It is a closed embedded smooth manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}
of dimension
(4n-2k+1)k
, as shown for example in
[
16
]
. Briefly; consider the smooth map
h:\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}\to\textnormal{skew}(2k)
,
U\mapsto h(U)=U^{T}J_{2n}U-J_{2k}
, where
\textnormal{skew}(2k)
denotes the set of all skew symmetric matrices. The set
h^{-1}(0)
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}
and the rank of the differential
\D h(U):\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}\to\textnormal{skew}(2k)
is
k(2k-1)
, since it is surjective onto
\textnormal{skew}(2k)
. This implies that
h
is a submersion, which verifies the claim
[
16
]
.

It is not a compact manifold though, as it is unbounded; the diagonal matrix
D_{n}=\textnormal{diag}\quantity{n,n^{-1}},n\in\mathbb{N}
, is in
\spst(2,2)
, but
[D_{n}]_{11}
can grow arbitrarily large with respect to any metric.

The symplectic Stiefel manifold is conveniently expressed as the set

\spst(2n,2k)=\quantity{U\in\R^{2n\times 2k}:U^+U=I_{2k}},

where
U^{+}=J_{2k}^{T}U^{T}J_{2n}
is called the
symplectic inverse
. In the special case
n=k
, we recover the symplectic group
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)=\quantity{M\in\R^{2n\times 2n}:M^+M=I_{2n}}
. In
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
, the symplectic inverse
M^{+}
is contained in
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
since
(M^{+})^{+}M^{+}=MM^{+}
and as
M^{+}M=I_{2n}\Leftrightarrow MM^{+}M=M
implies that
MM^{+}=I_{2n}
. Since it holds for
M,N\in\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
that
(MN)^{+}MN=I_{2n}
, we have that indeed
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
is a group. All elements of the symplectic group are invertible, and thus the column vectors of
M\in\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
span
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Likewise, elements in the symplectic Stiefel manifold feature full column rank, which follows from the fact that
U^{T}J_{2n}U=J_{2k}
.

In
[
5
]
, the authors consider the geometry of the symplectic Stiefel manifold under the quotient space representation
\spst(2n,2k)=\textnormal{Sp}(2n)/\textnormal{Sp}(2(n-k))
. We only provide a short review, and refer to
[
5
]
for the details. The reader will find that many results are analogous to the corresponding results on the classical Stiefel manifold, see e.g.
[
14
]
.

For the symplectic group, the tangent space at
M\in\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
is

\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)=\quantity{\Delta\in\R^{2n\times 2n}: \Delta^+M+M^+\Delta=0},\\
\dim(\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n))=(2n+1)n.

(4)

Of course, the latter matches the dimension the symplectic group,

\dim(\textnormal{Sp}(2n))=(2n+1)n
.
We denote the set of Hamiltonian matrices by

\hamil(2n)=\quantity{\Omega\in\R^{2n\times 2n}:\Omega^+=-\Omega},

and note that again
\dim(\hamil(2n))=(2n+1)n
.
Taking
\Delta=M\Omega
, with
\Omega\in\hamil(2n)
, we have that
\Omega^{+}+\Omega=0
holds, showing that
\Delta=M\Omega
for any
\Omega\in\hamil(2n)
is a tangent vector. Taking instead
\Delta=\Omega M
we have that
M^{+}\Omega^{+}M+M^{+}\Omega M=0\Leftrightarrow\lx@nobreakspace\Omega^{+}+\Omega=0
, and thus
\Delta=\Omega M
too is a tangent vector. Therefore,

\displaystyle\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)

\displaystyle=M\Omega:\Omega\in\quantity{M\Omega:\Omega\in\hamil(2n)}(2n)=\Omega M:\Omega\in\quantity{\Omega M:\Omega\in\hamil(2n)}(2n).

Consider the bilinear form
g_{M}:\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)\times\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)\to\mathbb{R},M\in\textnormal{Sp}(2n)

g_{M}(X_{1},X_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\tr((X_1M^+)^TX_2M^+).

(5)

This form is positive definite, thus a metric, since for
X=\Omega M\in\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
,

g_{M}(X,X)=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Omega^T\Omega)>0\quad\forall X\neq 0.

The metric
g_{M}
varies smoothly with
M
, and it is therefore a Riemannian metric on
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
. It is right–invariant in the sense that
g_{MN}(X_{1}N,X_{2}N)=g_{M}(X_{1},X_{2})
for
M,N\in\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
. The Riemannian metric induces a norm on the tangent space of
M
,
\|X\|_{M}=\sqrt{(g_{M}(X,X))}
. We will sometimes write
g
instead of
g_{M}
when the context is clear.

2.1
The symplectic Stiefel manifold as a quotient space

In
[
5
]
, the authors show that the quotient space
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)/\textnormal{Sp}(2(n-k))
coincides with the symplectic Stiefel manifold
\spst(2n,2k)
. The quotient map
\pi:\textnormal{Sp}(2n)\to\spst(2n,2k)
is given by

\pi(M)=ME=U,\ \ E=\begin{bmatrix}[c|c]I_{k}&0_{k}\\
\hline\cr 0_{n-k\times k}&0_{n-k\times k}\\
\hline\cr 0_{k}&I_{k}\\
\hline\cr 0_{n-k\times k}&0_{n-k\times k}.\end{bmatrix},

in the sense that

ME=\big[M^{(1:k)}\mid M^{(k+1:n-1)}\mid M^{(n:n+k)}\mid M^{(n+k+1:2n)}\big]E=\big[M^{(1:k)}\mid M^{(n:n+k)}\big]=U.

The tangent space of a quotient manifold
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}/\mathcal{Q}
can be identified with a certain subspace of the tangent space of the total manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
The construction is as follows.
Let
x\in\mathcal{N}
and let
p\in\mathcal{M}
be such that
\pi(p)=x
.
Decompose the total tangent space as

\T_{p}\M=\V_{p}^{\pi}\M\oplus\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\pi,g}\M,

(6)

where
\V_{p}^{\pi}\M=\ker\quantity(\D\pi(p))\pi(p)
is the so–called vertical space, and
\D\pi(p)
is the differential of the quotient map. The orthogonal complement
\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\pi,g}\M=\ker\quantity(\ker\qty(\D\pi(p)))^{\perp}
is called the horizontal space.
After fixing a specific
p\in\mathcal{M}
with
\pi(p)=x
, for any tangent vector
w\in\T_{x}\mathcal{N}

there is one and only one horizontal tangent vector
v\in\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\pi,g}\M
such that
\D\pi(p)[v]=w
, see
[
24
]
. Notice that the vertical space is independent of the metric, but the horizontal space is not, since the notion of orthogonality depends on the Riemannian metric
g
.

It is shown in
[
5
, p. 11]
that the horizontal space under the Riemannian metric in (
5
) is

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\pi,g}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)&=\bar{\Omega}M:\bar{\Omega}=\Omega P+P\Omega-P\Omega P\in\quantity{\bar\Omega M:\bar\Omega=\Omega P+P\Omega-P\Omega P\in\hamil(2n)}(2n),\\
\ P&=J_{2n}^{T}UU^{+}J_{2n},\quad U=\pi(M).\end{array}

(7)

The set of Hamiltonian matrices
\bar{\Omega}
satisfying the condition in (
7
) is denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{U}\hamil(2n)

and consequently

\T_{U}\spst(2n,2k)=\bar{\Omega}U:\bar{\Omega}\in\mathcal{H}_{U}\quantity{\bar\Omega U:\bar\Omega\in\H_U\hamil(2n)}(2n).

For each tangent

and a specific representative

of

, there exists a unique horizontal lift
\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\pi,g}}=\bar{\Omega}(\eta)M
, where

\bar{\Omega}(\eta)=\eta(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}+J_{2n}U(U^{T}U)^{-1}\eta^{T}(I_{2n}-J_{2n}^{T}U(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}J_{2n})J_{2n}.

(8)

Consequently, the Riemannian metric on
\T_{U}\spst(2n)
is computed as

g_{U}(X_{1},X_{2})=\tr(X_1^T(I_{2n}-\frac{1}{2}J_{2n}^TU(U^TU)^{-1}U^TJ_{2n})X_2(U^TU)^{-1}).

(9)

The formula is complicated by the symplectic structure, but is in close analogy to the corresponding formula for the canonical metric on the classical Stiefel manifold.
We refer the reader to
[
5
, p. 11–12]
for the details.

2.4
Geodesics and retractions

In
[
5
]
, the authors obtain the geodesics on
\spst(2n,2k)
under the quotient space construction as the images of the horizontal geodesics on the symplectic group
\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
: Given
M\in\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
and
\eta\in\T_{M}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
the associated geodesic in the total space is

\gamma(t)=\exp(t(\eta M^+-(\eta M^+)^T))\exp(t(\eta M^+)^T)M.

(16)

If
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{M}^{\pi,g}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
, then
\gamma^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{\gamma(t)}^{\pi,g}\textnormal{Sp}(2n)
for all
t
, meaning that we have
horizontal geodesics
.
By a standard result on quotient spaces
[
27
, Corollary 7.46]
, horizontal geodesics are mapped to geodesics in the quotient space under the quotient map
\pi
. Consequently, the geodesics on
\spst(2n,2k)
are
\pi(\gamma(t))
. Given
U=\pi(M)
and
\Delta\in\T_{U}\spst(2n,2k)
, one obtains

\varphi(t)=\exp(t(\bar\Omega(\Delta)-\bar\Omega(\Delta)^T))\exp(t\bar\Omega(\Delta)^T)U.

(17)

This follows from computing the horizontal lift of
\Delta
,
\Delta_{\mathcal{H}_{M}}^{\pi,g}=\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)M
, inserting in (
16
) and composing with
\pi
.

The formulas (
16
) and (
17
) are not well suited for practical applications, because the matrix exponential of a Hamiltonian matrix is unstable. In Riemannian optimization, it is standard practice to employ retractions rather than using the geodesics. A retraction is a map

R:\M\times\T\M\to\M,(x,v)\mapsto R_{x}(v),

(18)

so that (1)
R_{x}(0)=x
and (2)
\D R_{x}(0)[\xi]=\xi
,
\forall\xi\in\T_{x}\M

[
8
, Definition 3.47]
. We can view retractions as first–order approximations of the Riemannian exponential map. By employing the following approximation

\exp(2X)\approx(I+X)(I-X)^{-1},

(19)

we recover the so-called
Cayley transformation

[
20
, pp. 128]

\cay(X)=(I+X)(I-X)^{-1},

(20)

which has the property that
\cay:\hamil(2n)\to\textnormal{Sp}(2n)

[
5
]
. Replacing the matrix exponentials in (
17
) with the Cayley transformation yields the following
Cayley retraction

R_{U}(\Delta)=\cay\quantity(\frac{1}{2}\qty(\bar\Omega(\Delta)-\bar\Omega(\Delta)^T))\cay\quantity(\frac{1}{2}\bar\Omega(\Delta)^T)U.

(21)

To verify that this indeed is a retraction we note that
\bar{\Omega}(0)=0
and thus
\cay(0)=I
, so indeed
R_{U}(0)=U
. Computing
\D R_{x}(0)[\xi]
yields

\displaystyle\D R_{U}(0)[\xi]

\displaystyle=\evaluated{\dv{x}R_U(0+x\xi)}_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\D\cay(0)\quantity[\frac{1}{2}(\bar\Omega(\xi)-\bar\Omega(\xi)^T)]U+\D\cay(0)\quantity[\frac{1}{2}\bar\Omega(\xi)^T]U.

From the following calclation, using the fact that
\D(I-X)[Y]=-Y
and because
(I+X)
commutes with
(I-X)^{-1}

\displaystyle\D((I-X)^{-1}(I+X))[Y]

\displaystyle=-(I-X)^{-1}(-Y)(I-X)^{-1}(I+X)+(I-X)^{-1}Y

\displaystyle=(I-X)^{-1}\quantity(Y(I+X)+Y(I-X))(I-X)^{-1}

\displaystyle=2(I-X)^{-1}Y(I-X)^{-1}.

(22)

we readily obtain
\D R_{U}(0)[\xi]=\xi
.

The computation of

Without further comments we use the result appearing in

that every tangent vector

\bar{\Delta}=UA+H

Having the two components defined above, we can calculate, for
\Delta=\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)U
, the matrix
\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)

\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)=\begin{bmatrix}J_{2n}^{T}UJ_{2k}&(\bar{\Delta}^{+}\quantity(I_{2n}-\frac{1}{2}UU^+))^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\Delta}^{T}\quantity(I_{2n}-\frac{1}{2}UU^+)^{T}\\
-U^{T}\end{bmatrix}=:YX^{T},

From the construction above, we can mimic the ideas presented in
[

leading to low–rank formulas for computing the retraction (

\displaystyle R_{U}(t\Delta)=

\displaystyle\quantity(I_{2n}+t\hat X\qty(I_{8k}-\frac{t}{2}\hat Y^T\hat X)^{-1}\hat Y^T)

(23)

\displaystyle\cdot\quantity(-U+(tH+2U)\qty(I_{2k}-\frac{t}{2}A+\frac{t^2}{4}H^+H)^{-1}).

Notice that the formula (

One can also consider the (simpler) retraction

2.5
Vector transport on
\spst(2n,2k)

Moving tangent vectors between tangent spaces of a Riemannian manifold
\M
requires a so–called vector transport
[
30
]

\tran:\T\M\times\T\M\to\T\M.

Vector transport can be considered as a first–order approximation to the Riemannian parallel transport
[
13
, Chapter 2.3]
analogous to retractions being first–order approximations of the Riemannian exponential map.
Vector transport is only preferred over Riemann parallel transport in applications if the associated computations are either cheaper or more stable.

Given a retraction
R
, a point
x\in\M
and three tangent vectors
\eta,\xi,\phi\in\T_{x}\M
, a vector transport must satisfy
[
8
, Definition 10.68]

1.

\tran_{\eta}(\xi)\in\T_{R_{x}(\eta)}\M
(The vector
\xi
is transported to a vector determined by the direction
\eta
and the retraction
R_{x}
),

2.

\tran_{0_{x}}(\xi)=\xi
(The vector
\xi
is left unchanged whenever
R_{x}(0_{x})=x
) and

3.

\tran_{\eta}(a\xi+b\phi)=a\tran_{\eta}(\xi)+b\tran_{\eta}(\phi)
(linearity)

A standard way to construct a vector transport is to use the
differential of a retraction

[
8
]

\D R_{x}(\eta):\T_{x}\M\to\T_{R_{x}(\eta)},\ \xi\to\D R_{x}(\eta)[\xi].

Alternatively, the so–called
inverse retraction

[
39
]
can be utilized, if available.
We can also employ the orthogonal projections (
11
) and (
13
); given a point
x\in\spst(2n,2k)
,
P:\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}\to\T_{x}\spst(2n,2k)
, which yields condition 1 in the definition of a vector transport,
P(V)=V
if
V\in\T_{x}\spst(2n,2k)
, which is condition 2.
Finally, since projections are linear, condition 3 is also satisfied. The Riemannian parallel transport preserves the norm of the vector that is transported. Any vector transport can be enhanced to feature this property by a suitable scaling.

2.6
The Riemannian Hessian of a scalar function on
\spst(2n,2k)

Computing the Riemannian Hessian of a function
f:\M\to\mathbb{R}
can be done by computing the covariant derivative of the Riemannian gradient
\textnormal{grad}f
, along a geodesic
\varphi(t)
. Alternatively, one can efficiently read off the so–called Christoffel form (a bilinear 2–1 tensor) from the geodesic differential equation, and use this in turn to compute the Riemannian Hessian. We will follow that path, and refer the reader to Appendix
A
for a brief summary of the theory. We derive the Riemannian Hessian for a scalar function on the symplectic Stiefel manifold under the Riemannian metric
g
in (
5
) (and, as an aside, for the symplectic group as well).

From the geodesic formula in (
17
) we have, by setting
\Omega:=\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle(\Omega-\Omega^{T})\exp(t(\Omega-\Omega^T))\exp(t\Omega^T)U

\displaystyle+\exp(t(\Omega-\Omega^T))\Omega^{T}\exp(t\Omega^T)U,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0)=

\displaystyle(\Omega-\Omega^{T})(\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\Omega^{T}U)+(\Omega^{T})^{2}U.

By (
39
) in the appendix, this yields the Christoffel symbols, and consequently the Riemannian Hessian.

Lemma 5
.

Let
U\in\spst(2n,2k)
and let
\Delta\in T_{U}\spst(2n,2k)
be a tangent vector and let
\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)
be as in (
8
).
The Christoffel symbols
\Gamma_{g}
with respect to the right-invariant metric
g
of (
5
) on
\spst(2n,2k)
are given by

\Gamma_{g}(\Delta,\Delta)=-(\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)-\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)^{T})(\Delta+\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)^{T}U)-(\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)^{T})^{2}U.

(26)

The Hessian at
U
of a smooth scalar function
f:\spst(2n,2k)\to\mathbb{R}
under the metric (
5
) is the endomorphism
\hess f(U):\T_{U}\spst(2n,2k)\to\T_{U}\spst(2n,2k)
,

\hess f(U)[\Delta]=\evaluated{\dv{t}}_{t=0}\textnormal{grad}f(c(t))+\Gamma_{g}(\textnormal{grad}f(U),\Delta),

where
c:t\mapsto c(t)
is an arbitrary curve on
\spst(2n,2k)
such that
c(0)=U,c^{\prime}(0)=\Delta
and

\evaluated{\dv{t}}_{t=0}\textnormal{grad}f(c(t))
is the (standard) derivative of the Riemannian gradient of
f
.

Considering the general formula for the Riemannian gradient in (
15
) one can write down an expression for
\evaluated{\dv{t}}_{t=0}\textnormal{grad}f(c(t))
, which also features in our implementation.

A symbolic formula for the Christoffel symbols for general inputs
\Gamma_{g}(\Delta,\tilde{\Delta})
can be obtained by applying the polarization identity (
40
), but this does not lead to a computational reduction.

Practical tests revealed that it is numerically beneficial to perform a certain normalization procedure. By definition, and also readily seen in eq. (
38
), the Christoffel form is bilinear, and thus we can write

\Gamma_{g}(\Delta,\Delta)=\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}\Gamma_{g}\quantity(\frac{\Delta}{\|\Delta\|_F}\frac{\Delta}{\|\Delta\|_F}).

and use this when we compute
\Gamma_{g}(\textnormal{grad}f(U),\Delta)
via the polarization identity. It is our experience that this stabilizes the computation significantly, and ensures that
\hess f(U)[\Delta]\in\T_{U}\spst(2n,2k)
, i.e., the the output of the Hessian stays on the tangent space numerically.

The Riemannian Hessian is computationally costly to evaluate, relative to the Riemannian gradient and other quantities, used in practical algorithms. This is is seen by noting that
\bar{\Omega}(\Delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
, and as the formulas do not reduce due to nonlinearity, there seems to be little room for improvement, unless one works with numerical approximations.

2.7
Approximation of the Riemannian Hessian

The optimization literature knows a large variety of methods for incorporating approximate second-order information without calculating the full Hesse operator. As just one example, we remind the reader of the various forms (Polak-Ribiére, Fletcher-Reeves, etc.) for computing the
\beta
-coefficients in nonlinear CG.
In the special case of a Riemannian submanifold endowed with the Eucludean metric, the Riemannian Hesse 1–1 form of a smooth map
f:\M\to\mathbb{R}
is obtained from a projection,
[
8
, Corrolary 5.16]

\hess f(x)[\Delta]=P_{x}\quantity(\D\overline\grad f (x)[\Delta])\overline{\textnormal{grad}}f(x)[\Delta],\ \ \Delta\in\T_{x}\M,

(27)

where
\overline{\textnormal{grad}}f
is a smooth extension of
\textnormal{grad}f
.
Obviously, (
27
) is cheaper to obtain than the Hessian operator associated with the right-invariant metric, while it still can be expected to encode valuable second-order information.
In the experiments we will consider it as an approximation of the true Riemannian Hessian.

3
Optimzation algorithms on Riemannian manifolds

In this section, we consider problems from numerical linear algebra which all can be approached via Riemannian optimization methods on
\spst(2n,2k)
.
Our goal is to conduct a performance study for the Riemannian variants of the steepest descend method
(R–SD)
, the nonlinear conjugate gradients method
(R–CG)
and the trust region method
(R–TR)
. We have chosen to leave out Newton’s method, as it would require to solve large equation systems with the Hessian as the operator. We also tested the Riemannian limited–memory BFGS method
[
22
]
, which is implemented in the Manopt package for Matlab
[
10
]
, which is a general purpose toolbox for Riemannian optimization. This method avoids to compute the true Hessian, but rather it relies on a special approximation. In our initial tests, we did not see the BFGS method to be competitive when compared with the other three methods under consideration. We believe that much customization would be required. Thus we chose to not include it in our experiments.

3.2

\hess f(x_{k})[\xi]

[
8
, Chapter 6]
,
[
1
]

, and
\hat{H}_{k}(\xi)=P_{x_{k}}(\D\overline{\textnormal{grad}}f(x_{k})[\xi])
.

3.3
Implementation

The R–SD and R–CG method utilize the (BB method) in Algorithm
2
for line searching. For R–TR we use the implementation provided in the Manopt package.

We have implemented the symplectic Stiefel manifold in the Manopt framework in order to use the built–in features of the package for optimization. The symplectic Stiefel manifold is not available in the Manopt package for Matlab, but it is available in a package for Julia, Manifolds.jl
[
4
]
.
The retraction, vector transport, metric, norm etc. have all been implemented according to the theory outlined in Section
2
. We use this structure to perform numerical experiments, since we use the Manopt implementation of R–TR. We also leverage this structure when testing our manual implementation of R–SD and R–CG.

The algorithms terminate when
\|\textnormal{grad}f(x_{k})\|_{x_{k}}<10^{-6}
, or when the step size
\tau_{k}
in R–SD or R–CG becomes smaller than
10^{-11}
.

We have made minor changes to the script
trustregions.m
from Manopt package to make our manifold implementation compatible with the Manopt framework. All files are available at

4.2
The symplectic eigenvalue problem

This example follows
[
16
]
. The goal is to solve

\min_{X\in\spst(2n,2p)}f(X)=\min_{X\in\spst(2n,2p)}\tr(X^TAX).

(33)

This objective function is a special case of the Brockett cost function
[
11
]

b(X)=\tr(X^TAXN-2BX^T),\quad\text{with }A\text{ sym. pos. def.},B=0,N=I\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Minimizing (
33
) is of interest when solving the symplectic eigenvalue problem
[
33
, Theorem 4.1]
.
The minimizer
X^{*}\in\spst(2n,2p)
spans a symplectic subspace, and can be used to compute the trailing symplectic eigenvalues and associated symplectic eigenvectors of
A
. It holds that the smallest
p
symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1},\dots,d_{p}
are related to the true minimizer
X_{\textnormal{opt}}
via

2\sum_{i=1}^{p}d_{i}=f(X_{\textnormal{opt}}).

For more details, including algorithms, see
[
33
]
.

The Euclidean gradient and Hessian of (
33
) are

\nabla f(X)=2AX,\ \ \nabla^{2}f(X)[\Delta]=2A\Delta.

(34)

From Williamson’s theorem
[
35
]
, we have that any symmetric positive definite matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
admits the decomposition

S^{T}AS=\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix},

where
D=\textnormal{diag}\quantity{\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n}
are the symplectic eigenvalues. These coincide with the positive imaginary parts of the eigenvalues of
JA
.

We perform the following numerical experiment inspired by
[
17
,
33
]
.

\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
–matrix

L(l,c,d)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{l-2}&&&&&&&&\\
&c&&&&&d&\\

The proper symplectic decomposition

Computing the proper symplectic decomposition (PSD) of a matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2m}
is a central task in symplectic model order reduction of Hamiltonian systems
[
3
,
6
,
12
]
. The problem can be seen as the symplectic analog to the proper orthogonal decomposition from classical snapshot–based model order reduction.
The latter is obtained from a (truncated) singular value decomposition of the data matrix
[
29
]
. The PSD problem is

\min_{U\in\spst(2n,2k)}f(U)=\min_{U\in\spst(2n,2k)}\|S-UU^{+}S\|^{2}_{F}.

(35)

In practical applications, usually
k\ll n
. The gradient and Hessian are

\displaystyle\nabla f(U)=

\displaystyle-2\quantity(\qty(I_{2n}-UU^+)SS^TJ_{2n}^T U J_{2k}-J_{2n}SS^T(I_{2n} - UU^+)^TUJ_{2k} ),

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(U)[\Delta]=

\displaystyle-2\quantity(A(U,\Delta)SS^TJ_{2n}UJ_{2k}+(I_{2n}-UU^+)SS^TJ_{2n}^T\Delta J_{2k})

(36)

\displaystyle+2\quantity(J_{2n}SS^TA(U,\Delta)^TUJ_{2k}+J_{2n}SS^T(I_{2n}-UU^+)\Delta J_{2k}),

where
A(U,\Delta)=\D(I-UU^{+})[\Delta]=-\Delta U^{+}-(\Delta U^{+})^{+}
. In the implementation, we have ensured to store and recycle computations of the numerous matrix–matrix products appearing in the expressions for the gradient and the Hessian. We generate a matrix of rank
2r,r=40
,
T\in\spst(2n,2r)
, generate another random matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{2r\times 2m}
, normalize it
C=\frac{C}{\|C\|_{F}}
, and set
S=TC
. We consider unknowns
U\in\spst(2n,2k)
for
k=10,20,40
. When
k=r=40
we expect the minimizer
x^{*}
to fulfill
f(x^{*})\approx 0
, where clearly
x^{*}=T
is a solution.

5
Discussion and conclusion

In the numerical experiments conducted in this study, R–TR2 outperformed R–SD, R–CG and R–TR1 with respect to runtime in the nearest symplectic matrix problem and the symplectic eigenvalue problem. With respect to stability and numerical accuracy, R–TR1 and R–TR2 produced the best results. On the one hand, this is as expected, because R–TR makes use of second–order or approximate second–order information. On the other hand, one cannot conclude that R–CG and R–SD suffer more from numerical errors
per iteration. The number of iterations for these methods is much larger than that for R–TR, so that numerical errors accumulate over a longer iteration history. Of course, R–TR could be further improved, if one succeeds in making a single iterations in R–TR computationally more efficient.
In our experiments, R–TR always converged properly, whereas for R–SD and R–CG, there was a case, where the step size became so small
that the algorithm stopped without reaching a point with near-zero gradient.

Even though it approximates second–order information, R–CG does not converge faster than R–SD in our experiments, and the iteration count is large. We believe that this indicates that the line search is not fulfilling the requirements leading to favourable convergence patterns for R–CG. This is in contrast to what the authors of
[
36
]
observed. We emphasize that we have taken care to ensure that the numerical competition between the methods considered in this study is fair. The comparison with results from other publications should be made with caution due to the different user parameters that occur in optimization algorithms, not least among them the numerical stopping criterion and the target accuracy.

The retraction used in the numerical experiments approximates the geodesics on the manifold to comparably high accuracy. Yet, to compute this retraction requires more effort than for the simple retraction (
24
), and the runtime of R–SD observed in this work is generally much slower than the runtime that is reported for R–SD in the works
[
5
,
16
,
17
]
for similar experiments. These works, exclusively or partly, make use of the simple retraction (
24
). This supports the usual observation that the closeness of the retraction to the Riemannian exponential plays a subordinate role in iterative optimization schemes and that numerical efficiency should prioritized.

The discordance between the the experiments conducted in this work and those appearing in the works
[
16
,
17
,
36
]
can also be due to the different choice of Riemannian metrics, as different metrics gives rise to different formulas for the Riemannian gradient.

Appendix A
Differentiation of vector fields on Riemannian manifolds

In this appendix we will recall the background theory for the results presented in Section
2.6
. These results can be found in any standard textbook on Riemannian geometry, for example
[
13
]
. The interested reader may also consult
[
8
]
.

Let
\M
be a Riemannian manifold with metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x}
for
x\in\M
and let
\nabla
denote the Riemannian connection and let
\mathfrak{X}(\M)
denote the space of vector fields on
\M
. Below we summarize the properties of the Levi–Civita connection which allows to differentiate a vector field along directions indicated by another vector field.

Theorem 6
(Levi–Civita connection).

The Levi–Civita connection is the unique
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear smooth map on
(\M,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x})

\nabla:\mathfrak{X}(\M)\times\mathfrak{X}(\M)\to\mathfrak{X}(\M),(X,Y)\mapsto\nabla_{X}Y

such that

1.

\nabla_{hX}Y=h\nabla_{X}Y

2.

\nabla_{X}(hY)=X(h)+h\nabla_{X}Y
(Leibnitz–rule)

3.

\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X=[X,Y]
(torsion free),

4.

Z\langle X,Y\rangle=\langle\nabla_{Z}X,Y\rangle+\langle X,\nabla_{Z}Y\rangle
(metric)

for
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(\M)
and
h\in C^{\infty}(\M,\mathbb{R})
.

We will make use of the Christoffel formalism indicated in
[
14
]
. Briefly; a smooth vector field on
\M
,
x\mapsto V(x)\in\T_{x}\M
can be written in the local coordinates as

V(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(x)\partial_{i}|_{x},

where
\partial_{1}|_{x},\dots,\partial_{n}|_{x}
are the canonical basis of the tangent space
\T_{x}\M
in the geometric sense. The
Christoffel symbols
are scalar coefficient functions
\Gamma_{i,j}^{k}:\M\to\mathbb{R},x\to\Gamma_{i,j}^{k}(x)
so that in local coordinates, the covariant derivative of
X(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(x)\partial_{i}|_{x}\in\mathfrak{X}(\M)
along
Y(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}(x)\partial_{i}|_{x}\in\mathfrak{X}(\M)
, which is (suppressing the variable
x
)

\nabla_{X}Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\partial_{i}(\beta_{j})\partial_{j}+\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\nabla_{\partial_{i}}\partial_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\partial_{i}(\beta_{j})\partial_{j}+\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i,j}^{k}\partial_{k}.

In other words, the Christoffel symbols are in some sense ‘corrector terms’, ensuring that
\nabla_{X}Y\in\T_{x}\M
, whenever
X(x)\in\T_{x}\M
. We will later be interested in the special case when
X=c^{\prime}(t)
, for a smooth curve
c:\mathbb{R}\supset I\to\M,t\mapsto(\gamma_{1}(t),\dots,\gamma_{n}(t))
with the associated directional derivative along the coordinate lines,
c^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(t)\partial_{i}|_{c(t)}
. This is
the covariant derivative along a curve
, and is written as
\icov X(t)=\nabla_{c^{\prime}(t)}X(t)
. Using the same representation for
X(t)
as earlier,
\icov X(t)
is represented in the basis
\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}
as

\icov X(t)=\alpha^{\prime}(t)+\Gamma(\alpha(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t)),

(37)

where

\Gamma(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}u^{T}\Gamma^{1}v\\
\vdots\\
u^{T}\Gamma^{m}v\end{pmatrix},

(38)

is the so-called
Christoffel function
and thus
\alpha_{k}^{\prime}(t)+\Gamma^{k}(\alpha(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t))
are the coefficient for the basis element
\partial_{k}
at
c(t)
.

If
\gamma
is a geodesic, then
\icov\gamma^{\prime}(t)\equiv 0
and thus (
37
) in this case reduces to

0=\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)+\Gamma(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t)),

(39)

which is also stated in
[
14
]
. What is important is that the Christoffel symbols depend solely on the local coordinates and the Riemannian metric, and thus when then Christoffel function have been identified, we can use it when the curve of interest is not necessarily a geodesic. By applying the polarization identity

\Gamma(\Delta,\xi)=\frac{1}{4}\quantity(\Gamma(\Delta+\xi,\Delta+\xi)-\Gamma(\Delta-\xi,\Delta-\xi)),

(40)

we can recover the Christoffel function for two different inputs, as required in the genral formula (
37
).

Finally, for
X(t)=\textnormal{grad}f(c(t))
,
c(0)=x,c^{\prime}(0)=\Delta
for a smooth map
f:\M\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that the Riemannian Hessian is computed as

\icov\textnormal{grad}f(c(t))t=0=\hess f(x)[\Delta].

(41)

Appendix B
Derivation of the low–rank formula for the retraction (
21
)

In this appendix we will derive a low–rank formula for the Cayley retreaction presented in this paper.

We employ the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula

(A+UCV)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}U(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U)^{-1}VA^{-1}

and consider the retraction as a curve
t\mapsto R_{U}(t\Delta)
,

R_{U}(t\Delta)=\cay\quantity(\frac{t}{2}\qty(\bar\Omega(\Delta)-\bar\Omega(\Delta)^T))\cay\quantity(\frac{t}{2}\bar\Omega(\Delta)^T)U.

(42)

First we tackle the rightmost part of
R_{U}(t\Delta)
,

\displaystyle\cay\quantity(\frac{t}{2}XY^T)U

\displaystyle=\quantity(I_{2n}+\frac{t}{2}XY^T)\quantity(I_{2n}-\frac{t}{2}XY^T)^{-1}U

\displaystyle=\quantity(I_{2n}+\frac{t}{2}XY^T)\quantity(I_{2n}+\frac{t}{2}X\qty(I_{4k}-\frac{t}{2}Y^TX)^{-1}Y^T)U

\displaystyle=U+\quantity(\frac{t}{2}X\qty(I_{4k}-\frac{t}{2}Y^TX)+\frac{t^2}{4}XY^TX+\frac{t}{2}X)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\quantity(I_{4k}-\frac{t}{2}Y^TX)^{-1}Y^{T}U

\displaystyle=U+tX\quantity(I_{4k}-\frac{t}{2}Y^TX)^{-1}Y^{T}U.

(43)

Because
\bar{\Delta}=UA+H
(this can be seen similarly to how the authors do in
[
5
, pp. 13]
), we have
A=U^{+}\bar{\Delta},H=\bar{\Delta}-UA
and thus one obtains

\displaystyle Y^{T}X=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}A&-I_{2k}\\
H^{+}H-\frac{1}{4}A^{2}&\frac{1}{2}A\end{bmatrix},

and thus

I_{4k}-\frac{t}{2}Y^{T}X=\begin{bmatrix}I_{2k}-\frac{t}{4}A&\frac{t}{2}I_{2k}\\
-\frac{t}{2}(H^{+}H-\frac{1}{4}A^{2})&I_{2k}-\frac{t}{4}A\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

To invert the matrix in (
44
), we use the method of block–inversion by Schur complements
[
37
]
,

M=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{A}&\hat{B}\\

Appendix C
Basic calculation rules

Here we mention three useful calculation rules for matrices on
\spst(2n,2k)
, which all follow directly from the definition of the manifold.

Lemma 7
.

Let
U\in\spst(2n,2k)
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2k}
. The following properties hold

•

((U^{T}U)^{-1})^{+}=((U^{T}U)^{+})^{-1}

•

(v^{+}U+U^{+}v)^{+}=v^{+}U+U^{+}v

•

U^{+}(U^{T})^{+}=(U^{T}U)^{+}
