Which Schubert Varieties are Hessenberg Varieties?
Laura Escobar, Martha Precup, John Shareshian

Abstract
. After proving that every Schubert variety in the full flag variety of a complex reductive group G G is a general Hessenberg variety, we show that not all such Schubert varieties are adjoint Hessenberg varieties. In fact, in types A and C, we provide pattern avoidance criteria implying that the proportion of Schubert varieties that are adjoint Hessenberg varieties approaches zero as the rank of G G increases. We show also that in type A, some Schubert varieties are not isomorphic to any adjoint Hessenberg variety.



1.
Introduction

Hessenberg varieties have been studied by applied mathematicians, combinatorialists, geometers, representation theorists, and topologists. See
[
AH19
]
for a survey of some recent developments. Our goal is to understand better the structure of these varieties, and in particular what restrictions on such structure exist. To this end, we address herein a question raised by Tymoczko:

Is every Schubert variety in a full flag variety a Hessenberg variety?

As we will discuss below, conditions known to be satisfied by the most closely studied Hessenberg varieties are also satisfied by Schubert varieties. So, Tymoczko’s question is pertinent.
There are several possible interpretations of the question. Moreover, there are several ways to define a Hessenberg variety, giving rise to progressively more general classes of varieties. The answer to Tymoczko’s question depends on the chosen definition, as we shall see.

Let
G
be a connected, reductive complex algebraic group. Fix a Borel subgroup
B\leq G
and a maximal torus
T\leq B
. Let
\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{b},
and
\mathfrak{h}
be, respectively, the Lie algebras of
G,B,
and
T
. We write
Ad
for the adjoint representation of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let
N=N_{G}(T)
be the normalizer of
T
in
G
and let
W=N/T
be the associated Weyl group. The flag variety
\mathcal{B}:=G/B
is the union of Schubert cells
C_{w}
, over all
w\in W
. For each
w
, the Schubert variety
X_{w}
is the closure of
C_{w}
in
\mathcal{B}
.

Our first definition of a Hessenberg variety is as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Given
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and a subspace
H
of
\mathfrak{g}
such that
[\mathfrak{b},H]\subseteq H
, the
adjoint Hessenberg variety

\mathcal{B}(x,H)
consists of those
gB\in\mathcal{B}
such that
Ad(g^{-1})(x)\in H
.

Adjoint Hessenberg varieties were defined and studied by De Mari, Procesi and Shayman in
[
DMPS92
]
, after being defined and studied for
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
only by De Mari and Shayman in
[
DMS88
]
. In both
[
DMS88
]
and
[
DMPS92
]
, it is assumed that
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq H
and that
x
is a generic (regular semisimple) element of
\mathfrak{g}
. We make neither assumption here, as otherwise the topology of
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is restricted considerably. Indeed, it follows from the results in
[
DMS88
,
DMPS92
]
that if
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq H
and
x
is regular semisimple, then the Euler characteristic
\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H))
is equal to
|W|
. In particular, the only Schubert variety in
\mathcal{B}
that is a Hessenberg variety under these assumptions is
\mathcal{B}
itself.

A larger class of varieties than that given in Definition
1.1
is defined in
[
GKM06
]
by Goresky, Kottwitz and MacPherson, who allow an arbitrary representation of
G
, rather than restricting to the adjoint representation.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\psi:G\rightarrow GL(V)
be a (finite-dimensional, rational) representation. Given
x\in V
and a
B
-invariant subspace
H
of
V
, the
Hessenberg variety

\mathcal{B}(x,H)
consists of those
gB\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\psi(g^{-1})x\in H
.

We will always use the modifier “adjoint” when referring to the Hessenberg varieties described in Definition
1.1
and sometimes use the modifier “general” when discussing the Hessenberg varieties described in Definition
1.2
. We will use repeatedly, and without reference, the fact that if
H
is a subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
, then
[\mathfrak{b},H]\subseteq H
if and only if
H
is
Ad(B)
-invariant (see for example
[
Kum02
, Definition 8.1.22]
). So, every adjoint Hessenberg variety is a general Hessenberg variety. The subspace
H
appearing in either definition is called a
Hessenberg space
.

With definitions in hand, we turn to possible interpretations of Tymoczko’s question, and list three precise questions.

Question 1.3

(
the equality problem).

Is it true that for every
G,B,
and Schubert variety
X_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{B}=G/B
, there is a Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\subseteq\mathcal{B}
such that
X_{w}=\mathcal{B}(x,H)
?

Question 1.4

(
the isomorphism problem).

Is it true that for every
G,B
and Schubert variety
X_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{B}=G/B
, there is a Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\subseteq\mathcal{B}
such that
X_{w}
is isomorphic with
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
?

Question 1.5

(
the general isomorphism problem).

Is it true that for every
G,B
and Schubert variety
X_{w}\subseteq G/B
, there exist a complex reductive group
G^{\ast}
with Borel subgroup
B^{\ast}
and a Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}(x^{\ast},H^{\ast})\subseteq G^{\ast}/B^{\ast}
such that
X_{w}
is isomorphic with
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}(x^{\ast},H^{\ast})
?

The answer to Question
1.3
(and therefore to Questions
1.4
and
1.5
) is “Yes” for general Hessenberg varieties, as our first main result shows. (Relevant terminology will be discussed in Sections
2
and
3
.)

Theorem 1.6

(See Theorem
3.6
below)
.

Let
\lambda
be a strictly dominant weight for
G
, with associated highest weight representation
\psi:G\rightarrow GL(V(\lambda))
and highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
. Given
w\in W
, let
\dot{w}
be a representative of
w
in
N
, and let
H_{w^{-1}(\lambda)}
be the Demazure module generated by
\dot{w}^{-1}(v_{\lambda})
. Then
X_{w}=\mathcal{B}(v_{\lambda},H_{w^{-1}(\lambda)})
.

Theorem
1.6
follows directly from a result of Bernstein, Gelfand and Gelfand in
[
BGG73
]
.

The situation is more interesting when we consider adjoint Hessenberg varieties. We obtain negative results of various types. Before describing these, we discuss why the consideration of Schubert varieties is appropriate in the study of adjoint Hessenberg varieties.

We know of no restrictions on the structure of general Hessenberg varieties, and it is reasonable to wonder what such restrictions might exist. In addition to Theorem
1.6
, one can point to Section 9 of
[
OY16
]
, in which various interesting curves and surfaces are shown to be general Hessenberg varieties, as evidence that such restrictions are not so easy to come by.
A certain class of general Hessenberg varieties is examined by Chen-Vilonen-Xue
[
CVX20
]
in their study of Springer Theory for symmetric spaces.

Adjoint Hessenberg varieties are a different matter.
Given
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, we consider the usual Jordan decomposition
x=x_{s}+x_{n}
with
x_{s}
semisimple and
x_{n}
nilpotent. If
x_{n}
is regular in a Levi subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, then
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
admits an affine paving for every Hessenberg space
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
. This is proved under the assumption
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq H
by Tymoczko in type A (see
[
Tym06b
]
) and by the second author for arbitrary
G
(see
[
Pre13
]
), although the assumption is not necessary for the relevant arguments in either paper. When
H
is a nilpotent subspace, Fresse proved
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is paved by affines for all
x
when
G
is a classical group in
[
Fre16
]
and Xue has extended these results to groups of type
G_{2}
,
F_{4}
, and
E_{6}
in
[
Xue20
]
. So, in some sense,
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is paved by affines for “most”
x,H
, and indeed for all
x,H
in type A.
This restricts considerably the structure of adjoint Hessenberg varieties.
One can ask if there are any obvious additional restrictions. As Schubert varieties admit affine pavings, Tymoczko’s question is a good starting point in the search for such constraints.

We turn now to our results. First, if the root system
\Phi
for
G
has an irreducible component not of type
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
, then there are some
y\in W
such that the Schubert variety
X_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
is not equal to any adjoint Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 1.7

(See Theorem
3.7
below)
.

If some nonabelian simple ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic with neither
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
nor
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
, then there is some
w\in W
such that no adjoint Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
is equal to
X_{w}
. In particular, assume that the root system
\Phi
for
G
is irreducible and not of type
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
. Let
\theta
be the highest root in
\Phi
and let
W_{\theta}
be the stabilizer of
\theta
in
W
. Let
w_{0}
be the longest element of
W
. If
y
is a nonidentity element of
W_{\theta}
, then no adjoint Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
is equal to
X_{yw_{0}}
.

We observe in the case where
\Phi
is irreducible, there is some function
f
such that
[W:W_{\theta}]\geq f(\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathfrak{h})
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)=\infty
. So, Theorem
1.7
applies to an eventually negligible portion of the Schubert varieties in
\mathcal{B}
. When
\Phi
is of type A or type C, we can do much better.

Theorem 1.8

(See Theorem
4.4
below)
.

Assume
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
or
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and let
B
be the Borel subgroup of
G
consisting of upper triangular matrices. Fix
w
in the Weyl group
W=S_{n}
of
G
. If there exist
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and a Hessenberg space
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=X_{w}
, then
w
avoids the pattern
[4231]
.

It follows from Theorem
1.8
and the Marcus–Tardos Theorem (see
[
MT04
]
) that the number of Schubert varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
that are (equal to) adjoint Hessenberg varieties grows at most exponentially in
n
. So, in type A, the portion of Schubert varieties that are equal to adjoint Hessenberg varieties is eventually negligible. A more precise enumerative result appears in Section
4
, along with a proof of the theorem. Given Theorem
1.8
, one might hope that the set of Type A Schubert varieties that are (equal to) adjoint Hessenberg varieties is characterized by pattern avoidance. This is not the case. We show in Section
4
that
X_{[14235]}
is an adjoint Hessenberg variety in
SL_{5}({\mathbb{C}})/B_{5}({\mathbb{C}})
, but
X_{[1423]}
is not an adjoint Hessenberg variety in
SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
.

We also obtain a negative answer to the isomorphism question for adjoint Hessenberg varieties in type A, although our result applies to far fewer Schubert varieties than Theorem
1.8
.

Theorem 1.9

(See Theorem
5.1
below)
.

Suppose
n\geq 6
.
Assume that
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
or
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
B
is the subgroup of
G
consisting of upper triangular matrices. Let
w_{0}
be the longest element of the Weyl group
W=S_{n}
, and for
i\in[n-1]
, let
s_{i}\in W
be the transposition
(i,i+1)
. If
3\leq i\leq n-3
, then there do not exist
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and Hessenberg space
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is isomorphic with
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
.

We prove in Section
5
that under the conditions given in Theorem
1.9
, there is no irreducible adjoint Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
with the same Betti numbers as
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
. In fact, we show that unless
(n,i)\in\{(8,3),(8,5)\}
, no such
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
has the same Euler characteristic as
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
.
We use the following result, reminiscent of the restriction
\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H))=|W|
obtained by De Mari, Procesi and Shayman in
[
DMPS92
]
under the assumption that
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq H
and
x
is regular semisimple.

Proposition 1.10

(See Proposition
5.3
below)
.

Let
G
and
B
be as in Theorem
1.9
. If the adjoint Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is irreducible and of codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
, then
\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H))
is divisible by
(n-2)!
.

We do not know the answer to the general isomorphism problem for adjoint Hessenberg varieties, and pose the following question. Observe that
[653421]\in S_{6}
is the “first” Schubert variety to which Theorem
1.9
applies.

Question 1.11
.

Is there an adjoint Hessenberg variety (in a flag variety for an arbitrary reductive group) that is isomorphic to the Schubert variety
X_{[653421]}
in the flag variety of type
A_{5}
?

One might also consider Question
1.11
for the Schubert variety
X_{[4231]}
in the flag variety of type
A_{3}
. It follows from either Theorem
1.7
or Theorem
1.8
that
X_{[4231]}
is not equal to any adjoint Hessenberg variety, and
X_{[4231]}
is the “first” type A Schubert variety to which these theorems apply. However, there is an adjoint Hessenberg variety in
SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
with the same Betti numbers as
X_{[4231]}
(see Example
5.2
below).

We have a type C version of Theorem
1.8
. Consider the embedding
\phi:Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
whose image stabilizes the alternating form
\langle.,.\rangle
given by

\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&i<j=2n+1-i,\\
-1&i>j=2n+1-i\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Here
e_{1},\ldots,e_{2n}
denotes the standard basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
. One obtains from this the embedding
\phi^{\ast}
of the type C Weyl group into
S_{2n}
whose image consists of those permutations
w
satisfying
w_{2n+1-i}=2n+1-w_{i}
for all
i
.

Theorem 1.12

(See Theorem
6.8
below)
.

Let
G=Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
and let
B\leq G
be the Borel subgroup whose image under
\phi
consists of upper triangular matrices. Fix
w\in W
. If there exist
x\in\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
and a subspace
H
of
\mathfrak{g}
such that
[\mathfrak{b},H]\subseteq H
and
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=X_{w}
, then
\phi^{\ast}(w)
avoids the pattern
[4231]
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.12
utilizes an interesting fact relating adjoint Hessenberg varieties in types A and C. Let
E
be the linear transformation on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
such that
\langle v,w\rangle=v^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}}Ew
for all
v,w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
, and define the automorphism
\sigma
of
G_{A}:=SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
by

\sigma(A)=E(A^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}})^{-1}E^{-1}.

Then
G_{C}:=\phi(Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}}))
is the group of
\sigma
-fixed points in
G_{A}
. Moreover, if
B_{A}
is the group of upper triangular matrices in
G_{A}
, then
\sigma
fixes
B_{A}
setwise, and
B_{C}:=(B_{A})^{\sigma}
is the image under
\phi
of the Borel subgroup of
Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
described in Theorem
1.12
. We observe that the action of
\sigma
on
G_{A}
induces an automorphism of the variety
\mathcal{B}_{A}:=G_{A}/B_{A}
. The map
\phi^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{B}_{C}:=G_{C}/B_{C}
to
\mathcal{B}_{A}:=G_{A}/B_{A}
sending
gB_{C}
to
\phi(g)B_{A}
is a well-defined embedding, and
(\mathcal{B}_{A})^{\sigma}=\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{C})
(see for example
[
LR08
, Proposition 6.1.1.1]
).

Theorem 1.13

(See Theorem
6.1
below)
.

If
{\mathcal{V}}_{C}
is an adjoint Hessenberg variety in the type C flag variety
\mathcal{B}_{C}
, then there is some adjoint Hessenberg variety
{\mathcal{V}}_{A}
in the type A flag variety
\mathcal{B}_{A}
such that
\phi^{\prime}({\mathcal{V}}_{C})=({\mathcal{V}}_{A})^{\sigma}
.

and

2.
Definitions, Notation, and Preliminary Results

We review here various known results about algebraic groups, Weyl groups, flag varieties, and Hessenberg varieties. A reader familiar with basic facts about these objects can skip this section and refer back when necessary. Facts stated without reference or argument can be found in at least one of
[
BB05
,
Bor91
,
FH91
,
Hum78
]
.

As in the introduction,
G
is a reductive algebraic group with Borel subgroup
B
and maximal torus
T\leq B
. The Lie algebras of
G,B,T
are denoted, respectively, by
\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{h}
. The Weyl group of
G
is
W:=N_{G}(T)/T
. We fix a representative
\dot{w}\in N_{G}(T)
for each Weyl group element
w\in W
.

2.1.
The root system and the Bruhat order

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
admits a Cartan decomposition,

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}.

Here
\Phi\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is the root system for
\mathfrak{g}
, and each root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
is a
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying

[h,x]=\gamma(h)x

whenever
h\in\mathfrak{h}
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
. We can choose a set of simple roots
\Delta\subseteq\Phi
such that each
\gamma\in\Phi
is either a non-negative linear combination of elements of
\Delta
or a non-positive such combination. This gives a decomposition
\Phi=\Phi^{+}\sqcup\Phi^{-}
. We may (and do) choose
\Delta
so that

\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Phi^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}.

There is a partial order on
\Phi
given by

(2.1)

\beta\preceq\gamma\textup{ whenever }\gamma-\beta\textup{ is a nonnegative linear combination of positive roots.}

Each finite, irreducible root system
\Phi
contains a unique maximal element with respect to this order called the
highest root
of
\Phi
and denoted herein by
\theta\in\Phi^{+}
.

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathfrak{h}
the Cartan subalgebra of diagonal matrices, we write
\Phi=\{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}:1\leq i,j\leq n\}
with positive roots
\Phi^{+}=\{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi:i<j\}
. Here
\epsilon_{i}:\mathfrak{h}\to{\mathbb{C}}
denotes projection to the
i
-th diagonal entry. We assume furthermore that
\Delta=\{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i+1}:1\leq i\leq n-1\}
. The highest root of
\Phi
in this case is
\theta=\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{n}
.

The restriction of the adjoint action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}
to
N_{G}(T)
preserves the Cartan decomposition and factors through
T
. Thus we get an action of
W
on
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
that restricts to an action on
\Phi
. For each
\alpha\in\Delta
, there is the simple reflection
s_{\alpha}\in W
, which acts on
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
as the reflection through the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha
. In the type A case, if
\alpha=\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i+1}
we write
s_{\alpha}=s_{i}
for the corresponding simple transposition exchanging
i
and
i+1
in
W=S_{n}
. The set
S
of simple reflections generates
W
, and the length
\ell(w)
of
w\in W
is the shortest length of a list of elements of
S
(called a reduced word) whose product is
w
.

The
Bruhat order

\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}
is the partial order on
W
defined by
v\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}w
if some reduced word for
v
is a subword of some reduced word for
w
. We write
w_{0}
for the unique maximal element of
W
in the Bruhat order.

2.2.
The flag variety and Schubert varieties

The group
G
acts on the flag variety
\mathcal{B}:=G/B
by translation. Each
B
-orbit in this action contains exactly one coset
\dot{w}B
(
w\in W
), and so

\mathcal{B}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}B\dot{w}B/B.

We write
C_{w}
for
B\dot{w}B/B
, called a
Schubert cell
. Each Schubert cell is isomorphic to the affine space
{\mathbb{C}}^{\ell(w)}
. The closure of
C_{w}
in
G/B
is the
Schubert variety

X_{w}:=\overline{C_{w}}
. We have that

X_{w}=\bigsqcup_{v\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}w}C_{v}.

2.3.
Adjoint Hessenberg varieties

Let
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a Hessenberg space, that is, a subspace such that
[\mathfrak{b},H]\subseteq H
, and fix
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. Equivalently,
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is
B
-invariant with respect to the Adjoint action (see for example
[
Kum02
, Definition 8.1.22]
). Given a root
\gamma\in\Phi
, let
\pi_{\gamma}
denote the projection of
\mathfrak{g}
to the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
. As
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}=1
and the
T
-module
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\beta\in\Phi\setminus\{\gamma\}}\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
has no quotient isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}
, it follows from basic facts about direct sums that, for any Hessenberg space
H
in
\mathfrak{g}
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\textup{ if }\ \pi_{\gamma}(H)\neq 0\ \textup{ then }\ \mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\subseteq H.

We will use (
2.2
) and its consequences repeatedly below, frequently without reference.

As in the introduction, we define the adjoint Hessenberg variety corresponding to
x
and Hessenberg space
H
by

\mathcal{B}(x,H):=\{gB\in\mathcal{B}:g^{-1}\cdot x\in H\}.

Here
g\cdot x:=Ad(g)(x)
.

2.4.
Type A: The tableau criterion, pattern avoidance, and flags in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

Now we consider the case
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
or
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. We record here various results that will be used below.
In this case,
W
is isomorphic to the symmetric group
S_{n}
. We write elements of
S_{n}
in one-line notation,

w=[w_{1}w_{1}\cdots w_{n}]

where
w_{i}=w(i)
in the natural action of
w
on
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

Given
w\in S_{n}
and
1\leq j\leq k\leq n
, we write
I_{j,k}(w)
for the
j^{th}
smallest element of
\{w_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}
. So, for example, if
w=[52341]\in S_{5}
, then
I_{2,4}(w)=3
, as
3
is the second smallest element of
\{5,2,3,4\}
. The following characterization of the Bruhat order can be found in
[
BB05
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Tableau Criterion)
.

Let
v,w\in S_{n}
. Then
v\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}w
if and only if
I_{j,k}(v)\leq I_{j,k}(w)
for all
1\leq j\leq k\leq n
.

Given
v\in S_{m}
and
w\in S_{n}
with

, we say that

contains the pattern

if there exist

such that, for all

,
w_{i_{j}}<w_{i_{k}}
if and only if
v_{j}<v_{k}
. So, for example,
[631524]\in S_{6}
contains the pattern
[4231]\in S_{4}
, realized by the subsequence
6352
. We say
w

avoids

if
w
does not contain the pattern
v
. For example,
[631524]
avoids
[4321]
. We write
S_{n}(v)
for the set of all

avoiding
v
. Marcus and Tardos proved in
[

]
that (as conjectured independently by Stanley and Wilf) for every fixed

|S_{n}(v)|
grows exponentially with

. It follows immediately that

(2.3)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|S_{n}(v)|}{|S_{n}|}=0.

In this type A setting, we take
B
to be the Borel subgroup consisting of upper triangular matrices in
G
. Write
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
for the standard basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. We set
F_{k}:={\mathbb{C}}\{e_{j}:j\in[k]\}
, and observe that

is the stabilizer in
G
of the flag

{\mathcal{F}}_{\bullet}:=(0=F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset\ldots\subset F_{n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}).

As

acts transitively on the set

\mathsf{Flag}(n):=\{\mathcal{V}_{\bullet}=(0=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\ldots\subset V_{n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}):\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{i}=i\}

of all full flags in

, we obtain a bijection
\mathcal{B}=G/B\to\mathsf{Flag}(n)
defined by

(2.4)

\displaystyle gB\mapsto g{\mathcal{F}}_{\bullet}:=(0=gF_{0}\subset gF_{1}\subset\ldots\subset gF_{n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}).

Let us assume (temporarily) that a Hessenberg space
H
contains the Borel algebra
\mathfrak{b}
of upper triangular matrices in
\mathfrak{g}
. In this context, we define the
Hessenberg vector

h=h(H):=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
by setting

to be the largest integer

such that the span
{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{ij}\}
of the elementary matrix
E_{ij}
is contained in
H
, if such

exists, and
h_{j}=j
otherwise. We observe that the sequence

is weakly increasing (as

is

-invariant) and satisfies

for all

(as

). Moreover, the Hessenberg space

is determined by, and uniquely determines, the Hessenberg vector

. A direct computation shows that under the restriction of the bijection from (

) to

we have

(2.5)

\mathcal{B}(x,H)\simeq\{{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in\mathsf{Flag}(n):xV_{i}\subseteq V_{h_{i}}\mbox{ for all }i\in[n]\mbox{ where }h=h(H)\}.

We will make use this identification below whenever it is convenient to do so.

We will also consider the image of a Schubert variety
X_{w}=\overline{B\dot{w}B/B}
under this identification. To this end,
for
w\in S_{n}
and

, we set

Then the correspondence between cosets and flags maps
X_{w}
to the set of all
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
satisfying

for all

, see
[

, §10.5]
.

2.5.
Representations of reductive groups

Returning to the setting of an arbitrary reductive algebraic group
G
, let
\psi:G\rightarrow GL(V)
be a (rational, finite-dimensional) representation. Then the differential
d\psi:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{gl}(V)
is a Lie algebra homomorphism. Both
\psi(T)
and
d\psi(\mathfrak{h})
are diagonalizable and thus there exist (finitely many)
weights

\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
such that

•

V=\bigoplus_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}
, where

•

d\psi(h)(v)=\lambda(h)v
for all
v\in V_{\lambda}
and
h\in\mathfrak{h}
, and

•

\psi(t)
acts as a scalar transformation on
V_{\lambda}
for all
t\in T
and all
\lambda
.

The subspaces
V_{\lambda}
are called
weight spaces
of
V
. We write
\Lambda
for the set of all weights of all representations of
G
, which forms a lattice in
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
. The set of weights of the adjoint representation
Ad:G\rightarrow GL(\mathfrak{g})
is
\Phi\sqcup\{0\}
, hence
\Phi\subseteq\Lambda
.

If
\psi
is irreducible, then there is a unique
1
-dimensional
\psi(B)
-invariant subspace of
V
, which is a weight space. The associated weight
\lambda
is called the
highest weight
of
\psi
, and we write
V(\lambda)
for
V
. Any nonzero
v_{\lambda}\in V(\lambda)_{\lambda}
is a
highest weight vector
in
V(\lambda)
.

A weight
\lambda\in\Lambda
is
dominant
if
\lambda
is the highest weight for some irreducible representation. The action of
W
on
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
induces an action of
W
on
\Lambda
. We observe that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{\lambda}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{w(\lambda)}
for every weight
\lambda
for
V
and every
w\in W
. A dominant weight
\lambda
is
strictly dominant
if the stabilizer of
\lambda
in
W
is trivial. The partial order
\preceq
on
\Phi
defined above in (
2.1
) extends to a partial order on
\Lambda
. We write
\rho\preceq\lambda
if
\lambda-\rho
is a nonnegative linear combination of positive roots.

We remark that every representation
\psi^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is of the form
\psi^{\prime}=d\psi
for some representation
\psi
of
G
, and that such a
\psi^{\prime}
is irreducible if and only if
\psi
is irreducible. Thus we may also use the terminology defined above when referring to representations of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Finally, a representation
\psi^{\prime}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{gl}(V)
makes
V
a module for the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{g})
, and we make no distinction between such representations and modules.
Given dominant
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we refer to
V(\lambda)
as a
highest weight module
for
G
, or equivalently
U(\mathfrak{g})
.

3.
Highest weight Hessenberg varieties

In this section we study Questions
1.3
,
1.4
, and
1.5
for
general
Hessenberg varieties of Definition

(see Theorem

Then we show that the answer to the equality problem, Question

Let
\psi:G\to GL(V(\lambda))
be the irreducible representation of

with highest weight

and therefore a union of Schubert varieties. We call the Hessenberg variety

Highest weight Hessenberg varieties defined using the adjoint representation have been studied by Tymoczko and by Abe–Crooks in
[

,

]
. In the adjoint case, the highest weight is the highest root

.
We fix nonzero

, so

is a highest weight vector for the adjoint representation of

Abe and Crooks give an explicit description of of the highest weight Hessenberg variety

as a union of Schubert varieties in the type A case whenever

. The following result, due to Tymoczko (see
[

, Prop. 4.5]
), describes a collection of Schubert varieties equal to highest weight adjoint Hessenberg varieties.

Proposition 3.1

(Tymoczko)

Suppose
\gamma\in\Phi
is a root in the same
W
-orbit as
\theta
and let

be the maximal length element of

. Let

be the
B
-submodule of
\mathfrak{g}
generated by

. Then

.

The

defined in Proposition

Example 3.2

.

3.3.
Schubert varieties that are not adjoint Hessenberg varieties

We prove herein that for “most” reductive

above.

Theorem 3.7
.

If some nonabelian simple ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
is isomorphic with neither
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
nor
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
, then there is some
w\in W
such that no adjoint Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
is equal to
X_{w}
. In particular, assume that the root system
\Phi
for
G
is irreducible and not of type
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
. Let
\theta
be the highest root in
\Phi
and let
W_{\theta}
be the stabilizer of
\theta
in
W
. Let
w_{0}
be the longest element of
W

\mathcal{B}
is equal to

We begin by recalling some well-known facts about the structure of reductive groups. Given reductive
G
, let
Z^{0}
be the connected component of the identity in the center

. Then
G=Z^{0}G^{\prime}
, with the commutator subgroup
G^{\prime}\leq G
being semsimple. Moreover,
Z^{0}\cap G^{\prime}
is finite. (See for example
[
Spr09

is semisimple, there exist nonabelian simple algebraic groups

such that
G^{\prime}
is the central product
L_{1}\circ\ldots\circ L_{k}
(see for example
[
Spr09
, Theorem 8.1.5]
). Set
K:=Z^{0}\cap G^{\prime}
and
\overline{G}:=G/K\cong G^{\prime}/K\times Z^{0}/K
. The projection of
G
onto
\overline{G}
induces a surjection of Lie algebras, which has trivial kernel as the finite group
K
has trivial Lie algebra. So, if
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{z}
are the respective Lie algebras of
G
,
G^{\prime}
and

, then
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{z}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
. Each

has finite center
Z(L_{i})\leq Z(G^{\prime})
, and
Z(G^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z(L_{i})
. For

, let
\mathfrak{l}_{i}
be the Lie algebra of
L_{i}
. Considering the projection of
G^{\prime}
onto

. The upshot of all this is that

•

;

•

, then

acts trivially on both
\mathfrak{z}
and
\mathfrak{l}_{j}

•

if, for each

, then

For each

x_{0}\in\mathfrak{z}
and
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{l}_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
. As
B=eB\in X_{yw_{0,j}}
, we see that
x\in H
. If
x_{j}=0
then
\dot{w}\cdot x=x
for all
w\in W_{j}
. In particular,
\dot{w}_{0,j}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, which is impossible since
w_{0,j}\not\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}yw_{0,j}
. So, we assume now that
x_{j}\neq 0
.

By Lemma
3.9
, there is some
b\in B_{j}
such that
0\neq b^{-1}\cdot x
projects nontrivially onto
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta_{j}}
. As
X_{yw_{0,j}}
is
B
-invariant and contains
\dot{y}\dot{w}_{0,j}B
, we see that
(b\dot{y}\dot{w}_{0,j})^{-1}\cdot x\in H
. Now

(b\dot{y}\dot{w}_{0,j})^{-1}\cdot x=\dot{w}_{0,j}^{-1}\cdot(\dot{y}^{-1}\cdot(b^{-1}\cdot x)).

As
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta_{j}}
is
B_{j}
-invariant, we see that
b^{-1}\cdot x
projects nontrivially onto
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta_{j}}
. As
y\in(W_{j})_{\theta_{j}}
, also
\dot{y}^{-1}\cdot(b^{-1}\cdot x)
projects nontrivially onto
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta_{j}}
. Finally,
w_{0,j}^{-1}=w_{0,j}
maps
\theta_{j}
to
-\theta_{j}
and it follows that
(b\dot{y}\dot{w}_{0,j})^{-1}\cdot x
projects nontrivially onto
\mathfrak{g}_{-\theta_{j}}
.

As
H
projects nontrivially onto the
1
-dimensional root space
\mathfrak{g}_{-\theta_{j}}
, we see that
\mathfrak{g}_{-\theta_{j}}\subseteq H
. Since
\mathfrak{g}_{-\theta_{j}}
generates the
B_{j}
-module
\mathfrak{l}_{j}
we must have
\mathfrak{l}_{j}\subseteq H
. In particular,
x_{j}\in H
, hence
x-x_{j}\in H
. As

Ad(L_{j})
fixes
x-x_{j}
, it follows that for all
w\in W_{j}
,

\dot{w}^{-1}\cdot x=x-x_{j}+\dot{w}^{-1}\cdot x_{j}\in H.

In particular,
\dot{w}_{0,j}^{-1}\cdot x\in H
, leading again to the impossibility
\dot{w}_{0,j}B\in X_{yw_{0,j}}
.
∎

Example 3.10
.

Consider the Adjoint representation of

4.
The equality question for type A adjoint Hessenberg varieties and pattern avoidance

Assume that
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
throughout.
In this section we prove Theorem
1.8
, which says that if
w\in S_{n}
contains the pattern
[4231]
, then there is no adjoint Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
equal to
X_{w}
.
We will see in Example
4.7
that there is no pattern avoidance criterion characterizing the set of type A Schubert varieties that are adjoint Hessenberg varieties.
Theorem
1.8
remains true if we take
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, as can be shown with minor modifications, omitted herein, to our proofs.

As mentioned earlier, Theorem
1.8
is much more powerful than Theorem
1.7
when only type A is considered. Indeed, Theorem
1.7
implies that the number of
w\in S_{n}
such that
X_{w}
is not equal to an adjoint Hessenberg variety grows at least quadratically with
n
, while Theorem 1.8 says that the number of such
w
is at least
n!-c^{n}
for some constant
c
.

We begin by recording two basic facts that we will use repeatedly. Each matrix
x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
can be written as a linear combination of elementary matrices
\{E_{ij}:(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]\}
. We define
{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}:\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
to be the coordinate function returning the coefficient of
E_{ij}
in such a combination.
Throughout this section, we write
g\cdot x
for
Ad(g)(x)
whenever
g\in SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
with
{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}(x)\neq 0
for some
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
such that
i\neq j
. If
(k,\ell)\in[n]\times[n]
with
k\leq i
and
\ell\geq j
then there exists
b\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{k,\ell}(b\cdot x)\neq 0
.

Proof.

First suppose that
(k,\ell)=(j,i)
. This implies
j<i
and so
b_{\alpha}:=I+\alpha E_{ji}\in B
for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
A direct computation shows that
{\mathbf{c}}_{ji}(b_{\alpha}\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{ji}(x)+\alpha({\mathbf{c}}_{ii}(x)-{\mathbf{c}}_{jj}(x))-\alpha^{2}{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}(x)
. Thus, there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{ji}(b_{\alpha}\cdot x)\neq 0
.
If
i\neq\ell
the lemma will follow from the existence of
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{i\ell}(b_{1}\cdot x)\neq 0
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{k\ell}(b_{2}\cdot(b_{1}\cdot x))\neq 0
.
Symmetrically, if
j\neq k
the lemma will follow from the existence of
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{kj}(b_{1}\cdot x)\neq 0
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{k\ell}(b_{2}\cdot(b_{1}\cdot x))\neq 0
.
Therefore, to settle the case
(k,\ell)\neq(j,i)
it suffices to consider the cases
i=k
and
j=\ell
.

When
k=i
and
\ell=j
the statement of the Lemma is immediate so let us assume that this is not the case.
If
k=i
then for each
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
b_{\alpha}:=I+\alpha E_{j\ell}\in B
and a direct computation shows that
{\mathbf{c}}_{i\ell}(b_{\alpha}\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{i\ell}(x)-\alpha{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}(x)
. Thus, there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{i\ell}(b_{\alpha}\cdot x)\neq 0
.
Similarly, If
\ell=j
then
b_{\alpha}:=I+\alpha E_{ki}\in B
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{kj}(b_{\alpha}\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{kj}(x)+\alpha{\mathbf{c}}_{ij}(x)
, which implies that there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{kj}(b_{\alpha}\cdot x)\neq 0
.
∎

Note that Lemma
4.1
gives us an explicit description of the partial order
\leq
on
\Phi
defined in Section
3.1
. Indeed, we have
\mathfrak{g}_{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}}={\mathbb{C}}\{E_{ij}\}
and the lemma tells us that there exists
b\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{k\ell}(b\cdot E_{ij})\neq 0
so
\mathfrak{g}_{\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}}
is contained in the Demazure module
H_{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}}
. In summary,

\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{k}\leq\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow k\leq i\ \textup{ and }\ \ell\geq j.

Note that this description of
\leq
can also be obtained from Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Say
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is a Hessenberg space and
E_{ij}\in H
with
i\neq j
. If
k,\ell\in[n]
with
k\neq\ell
and both
k\leq i
and
\ell\geq j
hold, then
E_{k\ell}\in H
. In other words, if
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\subseteq H
then
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\subseteq H
for all
\gamma\in\Phi
such that
\beta\leq\gamma
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
there exists
b\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{k,\ell}(b\cdot E_{ij})\neq 0
.
Since
H
is
B
-invariant, we have
b\cdot E_{ij}\in H
implying

\mathfrak{g}_{\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}}\subseteq H
and

E_{k\ell}\in H
.
∎

Theorem
1.8
will follow directly from the next result.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume
w\in S_{n}
and
1\leq i<j<k<\ell\leq n
with
w_{\ell}<w_{j}<w_{k}<w_{i}
. Define
\tau\in S_{n}
by

\tau_{m}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{m}&m\not\in\{j,k\},\\
w_{k}&m=j,\\
w_{j}&m=k.\end{array}\right.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and let
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
be a Hessenberg space. If the Schubert cell
C_{w^{-1}}
is contained in the adjoint Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, then
\dot{\tau}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
.

Proof.

Given
w
,
x
, and
H
as in the statement, we assume
C_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, and write
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}
for
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(x)
whenever
p,q\in[n]
with
p\neq q
. Set

x^{\prime}:=\sum_{p\neq q}{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}E_{pq},\qquad\text{and}\qquad s:=x-x^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}.

Then
x=x^{\prime}+s
. We will show that both
\dot{\tau}\cdot x^{\prime}
and
\dot{\tau}\cdot s
lie in
H
, thus proving the lemma.

We know that
\dot{w}\cdot x\in H
, since
\dot{w}^{-1}B\in C_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
. In particular,
E_{pq}\in H
whenever
p\neq q
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(\dot{w}\cdot x)\neq 0
. As
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(\dot{w}\cdot x^{\prime})={\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(\dot{w}\cdot x)
whenever
p\neq q
, it follows that
\dot{w}\cdot x^{\prime}\in H
. Therefore,
\dot{w}\cdot s=\dot{w}\cdot x-\dot{w}\cdot x^{\prime}\in H
.

We see now that to show
\dot{\tau}\cdot s\in H
, it suffices to show

(4.1)

\dot{w}\cdot s-\dot{\tau}\cdot s\in H.

For
p\in[n-1]
, we write
h_{p}
for
E_{pp}-E_{p+1,p+1}\in\mathfrak{h}
. There exist
c_{p}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

s=\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}c_{p}h_{p}.

We observe that
\dot{w}\cdot E_{mm}=E_{w_{m},w_{m}}
for all
m\in[n]
, and similar for
\dot{\tau}
. A direct computation shows that, whether or not
k=j+1
, we obtain

\dot{w}\cdot s-\dot{\tau}\cdot s=(c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k})(E_{w_{k},w_{k}}-E_{w_{j},w_{j}}).

If
(c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k})=0
, then (
4.1
) holds. So, we assume from now on that

(4.2)

c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k}\neq 0.

In order to show that (
4.1
) holds, we must show that

(4.3)

E_{w_{k},w_{k}}-E_{w_{j},w_{j}}\in H.

We claim that there is some
b\in B
with
{\mathbf{c}}_{jk}(b\cdot x)\neq 0
. Given this claim, as
i<j
and
\ell>k
, Lemma
4.1
implies that there exists
b^{\prime}\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{i\ell}(b^{\prime}b\cdot x)\neq 0
. It follows that

(4.4)

{\mathbf{c}}_{w_{i}w_{\ell}}(\dot{w}b^{\prime}b\cdot x)\neq 0.

Since
(\dot{w}b^{\prime}b)^{-1}B\in C_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, we see that
\dot{w}b^{\prime}b\cdot x\in H
. It follows from (
4.4
) that
E_{w_{i}w_{\ell}}\in H
. Using Lemma
4.2
, we see that
E_{w_{k}w_{j}}\in H
. Finally, as
E_{w_{j}w_{k}}\in\mathfrak{b}
and
H
is a Hessenberg space, we get

E_{w_{k}w_{k}}-E_{w_{j}w_{j}}=-[E_{w_{j}w_{k}},E_{w_{k}w_{j}}]\in H.

Thus (
4.3
) and (
4.1
) hold.

We aim now to prove the claim that there exists
b\in B
with
{\mathbf{c}}_{jk}(b\cdot x)\neq 0
. By Lemma
4.1
, such
b
exists if there are
p,q
such that all of
p\neq q
,
j\leq p
,
q\leq k
, and
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}\neq 0
hold. We may therefore assume no such
p,q
exist. In that case (see for example
[
Car72
, p.61]
)
{\mathbf{c}}_{jk}(b\cdot x^{\prime})=0
for all
b\in B
. Now we choose

b:=I+E_{jk}\in B.

From direct calculation (or by
[
Car72
, p.61]
), we see that

b\cdot s=s-(c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k})E_{jk}.

It follows now from (
4.2
) that

{\mathbf{c}}_{jk}(b\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{jk}(b\cdot x^{\prime})+{\mathbf{c}}_{jk}(b\cdot s)\neq 0

as desired. This completes our proof that
\dot{\tau}\cdot s\in H
.

It remains to show that
\dot{\tau}\cdot x^{\prime}\in H
. Equivalently, we must show that, for all
p,q\in[n]
with
p\neq q
,

(4.5)

\mbox{if }{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}\neq 0\mbox{ then }E_{\tau_{p}\tau_{q}}\in H.

Since
\dot{w}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, we know that
E_{w_{p},w_{q}}\in H
whenever
p\neq q
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}\neq 0
. Therefore, (
4.5
) holds whenever
\{p,q\}\cap\{j,k\}=\varnothing
.
For all remaining pairs
(p,q)
, we will choose appropriate pairs
(d,e)
and
(s,t)
of indices such that

(w_{s},w_{t})=(\tau_{p},\tau_{q}),\ \ d\leq p,\ \ e\geq q,\ \ w_{s}\leq w_{d},\ \text{and}\ w_{t}\geq w_{e}.

Assertion (
4.5
) follows in each case from an argument of the following form:

“Since
{\mathbf{c}}_{p,q}\neq 0
, it follows from Lemma
4.1
that there is some
b\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{d,e}(b\cdot x)\neq 0
. Therefore,
{\mathbf{c}}_{w_{d}w_{e}}(\dot{w}b\cdot x)\neq 0
. As
(\dot{w}b)^{-1}B\in C_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, we see that
E_{w_{d}w_{e}}\in H
. By Lemma
4.2
,
E_{w_{s}w_{t}}\in H
.”

An exhaustive list of pairs
(p,q)
and corresponding pairs
(d,e)
and
(s,t)
is given in the table below.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr(p,q)&(d,e)&(s,t)\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr(j,k)&(i,\ell)&(k,j)\\
\hline\cr(j,i)&(i,j)&(k,i)\\
\hline\cr(j,q),q\not\in\{i,k\}&(i,q)&(k,q)\\
\hline\cr(\ell,j)&(k,\ell)&(\ell,k)\\
\hline\cr(p,j),p\not\in\{k,\ell\}&(p,k)&(p,k)\\
\hline\cr(k,j)&(j,k)&(j,k)\\
\hline\cr(k,q),q\neq j&(j,q)&(j,q)\\
\hline\cr(\ell,k)&(i,\ell)&(\ell,j)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

We conclude that (
4.5
) holds and our proof is complete.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.8
, restated here for the reader’s convenience.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
w\in S_{n}
. If some adjoint Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\subseteq SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
is equal to the Schubert variety
X_{w}
, then
w
avoids the pattern
[4231]
.

Proof.

It is well-known and straightforward to show that, given permutations
\sigma
and
w
,
w
contains the pattern
\sigma
if and only if
w^{-1}
contains
\sigma^{-1}
. Since
[4231]
is its own inverse, the theorem will follow once we show that if
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=X_{w^{-1}}
, then
w
avoids the pattern
[4231]
.

If
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=X_{w^{-1}}
then
C_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
. If
w
contains the pattern
[4231]
then
\dot{\tau}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, with
\tau
as in Lemma
4.3
. We observe that
\ell(\tau)>\ell(w)
, since
\tau
is obtained from
w
by exchanging the positions of two letters that appear in ascending order in
w
. It follows that
\tau\not\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}w
and
\tau^{-1}\not\leq_{\mathsf{Br}}w^{-1}
. This forces
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\neq X_{w^{-1}}
.
∎

As mentioned in the introduction, the Marcus–Tardos Theorem guarantees that the number
\left|S_{n}(4231)\right|
of permutations in
S_{n}
avoiding
[4231]
is bounded above by some exponential function of
n
. In particular,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left|S_{n}(4231)\right|}{\left|S_{n}\right|}=0.

So, for large
n
, Schubert varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
that are also Hessenberg varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
are extremely rare. To our knowledge, the best exponential bounds for
\left|S_{n}(4231)\right|
known currently are (for all large enough
n
)

10.271^{n}\leq\left|S_{n}(4231)\right|\leq 13.5^{n},

due to Bevan, Brignall, Elvey Price and Pantone in
[
BBEPP20
]
. It is worth remarking that we do not know if there is any constant
c>1
such that the number of Schubert varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
that are (equal to) Hessenberg varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
is at least
c^{n}
.

We can use similar reasoning as that used in the proof of in Lemma
4.3
to argue that certain Schubert varieties in
SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
are not equal to any Hessenberg variety in
SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
. These arguments do not extend as easily to pattern avoidance results, but we include one example here for the sake of clarity. Example
4.6
below completely characterizes which Schubert varieties are equal to Hessenberg varieties in
SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
w\in\{[4123],[2341]\}
then no adjoint Hessenberg variety is equal to
X_{w^{-1}}
.

Proof.

First we consider
w=[4123]
. Note that permutations
\sigma=[2314]
and
\tau=[1342]
are the Bruhat-minimal elements of
S_{4}
such that
\sigma\nleq w
and
\tau\nleq w
. We argue that given
x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
and Hessenberg space
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
such that
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\subseteq SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
is a
B
-invariant Hessenberg variety with
X_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, then either
\dot{\sigma}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
or
\dot{\tau}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
. This proves
X_{w^{-1}}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
and thus
X_{w^{-1}}
is not equal to a Hessenberg variety.

To begin, write

x=\sum_{(p,q)\in[4]\times[4]}{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}E_{pq}

where
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}={\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(x)
for all
p,q
.

If
\{q\in[4]:{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}\neq 0\,\textup{ for some }\,p\neq q\}=\varnothing
(i.e. if
x
is a diagonal matrix) then we set
q_{0}=5
. Otherwise, set
q_{0}:=\min\{q\in[4]:{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}\neq 0\,\textup{ for some }\,p\neq q\}
.

Suppose first that
q_{0}=1
or
q_{0}=2
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists
b\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{12}(b\cdot x)\neq 0
and thus
{\mathbf{c}}_{41}(\dot{w}\cdot b\cdot x)\neq 0
. Since
X_{w^{-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
we know
b^{-1}\dot{w}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
and therefore
E_{41}\in H
. It follows that
H=\mathfrak{sl}_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
. So,
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
contains both
\dot{\sigma}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
and
\dot{\tau}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
in this case.

We assume now that
q_{0}\geq 3
. This implies, in particular, that

{\mathbf{c}}_{21}={\mathbf{c}}_{31}={\mathbf{c}}_{41}={\mathbf{c}}_{12}={\mathbf{c}}_{32}={\mathbf{c}}_{42}=0,

hence

(4.6)

\quad\ x={\mathbf{c}}_{11}E_{11}+{\mathbf{c}}_{22}E_{22}+\sum_{(p,q)\in[4]\times\{3,4\}}{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}E_{pq}.

If
{\mathbf{c}}_{11}\neq{\mathbf{c}}_{22}
then there exists
b\in B
such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{12}(b\cdot x)\neq 0
. (This is easily verified by direct calculation.) Since
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is
B
-invariant we see that
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\mathcal{B}(b\cdot x,H)
. Thus, if
{\mathbf{c}}_{11}\neq{\mathbf{c}}_{22}
we can argue
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=SL_{4}({\mathbb{C}})/B
as above. So, we assume
{\mathbf{c}}_{11}={\mathbf{c}}_{22}
for the remainder of the proof.

If
q_{0}=3
, then
{\mathbf{c}}_{p3}\neq 0
for some
p\in\{1,2,4\}
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists
b\in B
such that

5.
The isomorphism question for type A adjoint Hessenberg varieties

Our goal is to prove the following result, which is a restatement of Theorem
1.9
above.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
n\geq 6
.
Assume that
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
or
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
B
is the Borel subgroup of
G
consisting of upper triangular matrices. Let
w_{0}
be the longest element of the Weyl group
W=S_{n}
, and for
i\in[n-1]
, let
s_{i}\in W
be the transposition
(i,i+1)
. If
3\leq i\leq n-3
, then there do not exist
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and subspace
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
such that
[\mathfrak{b},H]\subseteq H
and
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is isomorphic with
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
.

Assume that we have fixed
n\geq 4
. As each Schubert variety
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
is irreducible of codimension one in the flag variety
\mathcal{B}
, our plan is to study closely the structure of adjoint Hessenberg varieties of codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
. In particular, we study the Euler characteristic of irreducible adjoint Hessenberg varieties of codimension one. We will see that in all but two cases, if
3\leq i\leq n-3
, no such variety has the same Euler characteristic as
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
. The remaining two cases will be handled by examining Betti numbers.

A few remarks are in order.
First, Proposition
3.1
tells us that for all
n\leq 3
, every Schubert variety is equal to an adjoint Hessenberg variety and that for all
n
,
X_{s_{1}w_{0}}
and
X_{s_{n-1}w_{0}}
are adjoint Hessenberg varieties.
Second, Theorem
5.1
does not resolve the isomorphism question for the Schubert varieties
X_{s_{2}w_{0}}
and
X_{s_{n-2}w_{0}}
whenever
n\geq 4
.
In fact, the existence Hessenberg varieties in
\mathcal{B}
with the same Betti numbers as
X_{s_{2}w_{0}}
and
X_{s_{n-2}w_{0}}
renders the methods used to prove Theorem
5.1
useless. Recall that
\theta=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{n}
is the highest root in
\Phi
. We define the Hessenberg space

H(\overline{-\theta}):=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi\setminus\{-\theta\}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

As is standard, we define the Poincaré polynomial of a space
X
as

\textup{Poin}(X,q):=\sum_{i\geq 0}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{i}(X;{\mathbb{C}})q^{i}.

Example 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 4
and suppose
v=s_{2}w_{0}
or
v=s_{n-2}w_{0}
. We then have

(5.1)

\textup{Poin}(X_{v},\sqrt{q})=[n-2]_{q}!\left([n]_{q}[n-1]_{q}-q^{2n-3}-q^{2n-4}\right).

(
Here
[n]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}
and
[n]_{q}!=\prod_{j=1}^{n}[j]_{q}
.
)
Using the Betti number formulas from either
[
Tym06b
]
or
[
Pre13
]
along with (
5.1
), one can check that if
x=E_{1,n-1}+E_{2,n}
or
x=E_{1,2}+E_{2,n}
then
\textup{Poin}(X_{v},q)=\textup{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta})),q)
. One can also confirm this equality for
n\leq 9
using the tables of Poincaré polynomials found on Tymoczko’s website
[
Tym
]
.

We do not know if either of
X_{s_{2}w_{0}}
and
X_{n-2w_{0}}
is isomorphic to either of the Hessenberg varieties appearing in Example
5.2
.

We now commence our study of codimension one adjoint Hessenberg varieties. Our main goal is to prove Proposition
1.10
, restated below for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is a Hessenberg space such that
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is irreducible and has codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
, then the Euler characteristic
\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H))
is divisible by
(n-2)!
.

Proposition
5.3
will be combined with Lemma
5.4
below to reduce the proof of Theorem
5.1
to the examination of two special cases.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}
be the set of simple reflections in
S_{n}
. For
i\in[n-1]
, let
W(i)
be the subgroup of
S_{n}
generated by
S\setminus\{s_{i}\}
. Then

\chi(X_{s_{i}w_{0}})=n!-\left|W(i)\right|=n!-i!(n-i)!.

Proof.

It follows directly from the definitions that
W(i)\cong S_{i}\times S_{n-i}
so
|W(i)|=i!(n-i)!
. The bijection on
S_{n}
sending
w
to
ww_{0}
is an anti-automorphism of the Bruhat order (see for example
[
BB05
, Proposition 2.3.4]
). It follows that the number of Bruhat cells
C_{u}
not contained in
X_{s_{i}w}
is equal to the number of
u\in S_{n}
such that
s_{i}\not\leq u
, which is the number of elements of
W(i)
.
∎

The remainder of this section consists of the proof of Proposition
5.3
followed by the proof of Theorem
5.1
. The key step is the next result, which we will prove after stating a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume that
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and that
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is a Hessenberg space. If
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
has dimension
{{n}\choose{2}}-1
, then at least one of

•

H=H(\overline{-\theta})
, or

•

x
has an eigenspace of dimension
n-1

must hold.

In order to prove Lemma
5.5
, we define two more “large” Hessenberg spaces. Set
\beta_{1}:=\varepsilon_{n-1}-\varepsilon_{1}
and
\beta_{2}:=\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{2}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
we define the Hessenberg space

H(\overline{\beta}_{i}):=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi\setminus\{-\theta,\beta_{i}\}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

Assume temporarily that
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
,
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is a Hessenberg space. The map
\iota:SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
sending
g(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))
to
gB
is an isomorphism. Note that
\iota
factors through the identification of each of
G/B
and
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))
with the variety of full flags in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Thus, if
x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
then
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\iota(\mathcal{B}(x,H\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})))
, while if
x\not\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
then
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\varnothing
. Therefore, the next assumption is harmless.

Assumption 5.6
.

If
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is a Hessenberg space, then
H\not\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

We continue now under our original assumption that
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
or
G=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Given Assumption
5.6
, the next lemma holds for Hessenberg spaces in either
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
or
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a Hessenberg space satisfying Assumption
5.6
.

(1)

If
\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathfrak{g}/H=1
then
H=H(\overline{-\theta})
.

(2)

If
\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathfrak{g}/H=2
then
H=H(\overline{\beta_{i}})
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
.

(3)

If
\dim_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathfrak{g}/H>2
then
H\subset H(\overline{\beta_{i}})
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

If
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\subset H
, then
H
contains the Demazure module in
\mathfrak{g}
generated by the root vector
E_{\gamma}
so
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\subseteq H
for all
\beta\geq\gamma
by (
3.1
). Now if
\pi_{-\theta}(H)\neq 0
then
\mathfrak{g}_{-\theta}\subset H
and, as
E_{n1}\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\theta}
generates the Demazure module
\mathfrak{g}\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, we have
H=\mathfrak{g}
. Therefore every proper Hessenberg subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
is contained in
H(\overline{-\theta})
and (1) follows. Moreover, if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathfrak{g}/H=2
then
H\subset H(\overline{-\theta})
. For all
\gamma\in\Phi\setminus\{-\theta\}
we have
\gamma\geq\beta_{1}
or
\gamma\geq\beta_{2}
. Thus
E_{n-1,1}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{1}}
and
E_{n2}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta_{2}}
together generate the
B
-module
H(\overline{-\theta})\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, and assertion (2) follows. The poset of Hessenberg spaces in
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying Assumption
5.6
, ordered by inclusion, is graded by dimension. Now (3) follows from (2).
∎

For
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
, set

\mathsf{mult}_{c}(x):=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(x-cI).

Lemma
5.5
follows directly from Lemma
5.7
and the next result.
Indeed, if
H\neq H(\overline{-\theta})
then by Lemma
5.7
we have
H\subseteq H(\overline{\beta_{i}})
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Since
\dim\mathcal{B}(x,H)\leq\dim\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta_{i}}))
, Proposition
5.8
below implies that
\max_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}(\mathsf{mult}_{c}(x))\geq n-1
whenever
\dim\mathcal{B}(x,H)={{n}\choose{2}}-1
.
As
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\varnothing
or
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\mathcal{B}
whenever
x
is scalar, Lemma
5.5
follows.

Proposition 5.8
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta}_{i}))
has dimension at most

\max\left({{n}\choose{2}}-2,{{n-1}\choose{2}}-1+\max_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}(\mathsf{mult}_{c}(x))\right).

Proof.

We claim first that it suffices to consider the case
i=1
. Let
\sigma_{0}\in GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
be the involution mapping
e_{i}
to
e_{n+1-i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Define an automorphism
r:G\rightarrow G
by

r(g)=\sigma_{0}(g^{-1})^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}}\sigma_{0}.

We observe that if
b\in B
then
(b^{-1})^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}}
is lower triangular, and thus
r(b)\in B
. Therefore the map
\overline{r}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
sending
gB
to
r(g)B
is a well defined automorphism of
\mathcal{B}
. A direct calculation shows that

\overline{r}(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta_{2}}))=\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{0}x^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}}\sigma_{0},H(\overline{\beta_{1}})).

Since
x
and
\sigma_{0}x^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}}\sigma_{0}
are conjugate, we see that the dimensions of
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta_{1}}))
and
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta_{2}}))
are equal. Our claim follows.

Noting that
h(H(\overline{\beta}_{1}))=(n-2,n,\ldots,n)
, we consider the map
\pi:\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta}_{1}))\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
given by

\pi({\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet})=V_{1}.

As
\pi
is the restriction to
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta}_{1}))
of the projection of the product of Grassmannians
\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{G}(k,n)
onto its first factor,
\pi
is a regular map. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an irreducible component of
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{\beta}_{1}))
and let
Y\in\pi({\mathcal{C}})
. According to
[
Har92
, Corollary 11.13]
,

(5.2)

\dim{\mathcal{C}}\leq\dim\pi({\mathcal{C}})+\dim\pi^{-1}(Y).

The fiber
\pi^{-1}(Y)
consists of all flags
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
such that
V_{1}=Y
and
Y+xY\leq V_{n-2}
.
If
Y
is not
x
-invariant, then
Y+xY
is two-dimensional.
In this case, we define the (surjective) map
\zeta
from
\pi^{-1}(Y)
to the Grassmannian of
(n-4)
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}/(Y+xY)
(a projective variety) sending
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
to
V_{n-2}/(Y+xY)
.
For every such subspace
Z^{\prime}=Z/(Y+xY)
,
\zeta^{-1}(Z^{\prime})
is isomorphic to the product
\mathsf{Flag}_{n-3}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Indeed,
\zeta^{-1}(Z^{\prime})
consists of all
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
such that
V_{1}=Y
and
V_{n-2}=Z
. Choosing
V_{2},\ldots,V_{n-3}
is equivalent to choosing a flag in
V_{n-2}/V_{1}
, and choosing
V_{n-1}
is equivalent to choosing a
1
-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}/V_{n-2}
. It follows from
[
Har92
, Theorem 11.14]
that
\pi^{-1}(Y)
is irreducible. We may apply
[
Har92
, Theorem 11.12]
and well-known facts about Grassmannians and flag varieties to get

(5.3)

\dim\pi^{-1}(Y)=\dim\mathbb{G}(n-4,n-2)+\dim(\mathsf{Flag}_{n-3}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1})=2(n-4)+{{n-3}\choose{2}}+1.

Combining (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we get

\dim{\mathcal{C}}\leq n-1+2(n-4)+{{n-3}\choose{2}}+1={{n}\choose{2}}-2,

and the claim of the Proposition follows in this case.

We are left with the case where every
Y\in\pi({\mathcal{C}})
is
x
-invariant. We consider the map from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
sending
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
to the eigenvalue of
x
on
V_{1}
. As
{\mathcal{C}}
is irreducible and therefore connected, and the set of eigenvalues of
x
is discrete, there exists some eigenvalue
c
of
x
such that every
Y\in\pi({\mathcal{C}})
is spanned by an element of
\ker(x-cI)
.
It follows that

(5.4)

\dim\pi({\mathcal{C}})\leq\mathsf{mult}_{c}(x)-1.

For any
Y\in\pi({\mathcal{C}})
,
\pi^{-1}(Y)
consists of all flags
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
such that
V_{1}=Y
. It follows that
\pi^{-1}(Y)
is isomorphic with
\mathsf{Flag}_{n-1}
. Combining (
5.2
) and (
5.4
), we get

\dim{\mathcal{C}}\leq{{n-1}\choose{2}}+\mathsf{mult}_{c}(x)-1.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Assume that

and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is a Hessenberg space. If
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is irreducible of codimension one in

, then
\mathcal{B}(x,H)

(

)
one of

.

Proof.

We observe that if
x
has an eigenspace of dimension

, then

is either semisimple or conjugate to

for some scalar

. We consider first the case where

such that
xe_{i}=ce_{i}
for

, and
xe_{n}=de_{n}
. If

, then
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\mathcal{B}
. So, we assume that
H\neq\mathfrak{g}
. By Lemma
5.7
(1),
H\subseteq H(\overline{-\theta})
and so
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
. We will show that
B(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
has two irreducible components, one of which is
X_{s_{n-1}w_{0}}
with the other isomorphic to
X_{s_{1}w_{0}}
. The claim of the lemma in this case then follows immediately.

Since
h(H(\overline{-\theta}))=(n-1,n,\ldots,n)
, we see that
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
consists of all flags
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}
such that
xV_{1}<V_{n-1}
. In particular, if
V_{1}
is
x
-invariant, then
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
. Define

{\mathcal{C}}_{1}:=\{{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}:V_{1}\leq{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{i}:i\in[n-1]\}\}.

As
{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{i}:i\in[n-1]\}=\ker(x-cI)
, we see that
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
. By the flag description of Schubert varieties and the fact that
s_{n-1}w_{0}=[n-1\,n\,n-2\cdots 2\,1]
, it follows that

{\mathcal{C}}_{1}=X_{s_{n-1}w_{0}}.

Now set

{\mathcal{C}}_{2}:=\{{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}:e_{n}\in V_{n-1}\}.

We will show that
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))={\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, and that, with
\sigma_{0}
the involution mapping
e_{i}
to
e_{n+1-i}
as above,
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}=\sigma_{0}(X_{s_{1}w_{0}})
, thereby completing our examination of the case where
x
is semisimple. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. If
V_{1}
is
x
-invariant, then
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
. If
V_{1}
is not
x
-invariant, then
V_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\{e_{n}+y\}
for some nonzero
y\in{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{i}:i\in[n-1]\}
. Now
xV_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\{de_{n}+cy\}
. If
c=0
then
d\neq 0
and
xV_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\{e_{n}\}\leq V_{n-1}
. If
c\neq 0
then

V_{1}+xV_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\left\{e_{n}+y,e_{n}+y-\frac{1}{c}\left(de_{n}+cy\right)\right\}={\mathbb{C}}\{e_{n}+y,e_{n}\},

the second equality holding since
d\neq c
. Now
xV_{1}<V_{1}+xV_{1}<V_{n-1}
. In either case,
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
so
\mathcal{C}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
.

Conversely, say
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))\setminus{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
. If
V_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\{e_{n}\}
, then
e_{n}\in V_{n-1}
. Otherwise,
V_{1}={\mathbb{C}}\{e_{n}+y\}
for some nonzero
y\in{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{i}:i\in[n-1]\}
. Arguing as in the paragraph just above, we see that
e_{n}\in V_{1}+xV_{1}
and therefore
e_{n}\in V_{n-1}
. We conclude that
{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
and
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}
as claimed.

Now
s_{1}w_{0}=[n\,n-1\cdots 3\,1\,2]
. By the flag description of Schubert varieties,
X_{s_{1}w_{0}}=\{{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}:e_{1}\in V_{n-1}\}
. Now

\sigma_{0}(X_{s_{1}w_{0}})=\sigma_{0}(\{{\mathcal{V}}_{\bullet}:e_{1}\in V_{n-1}\})=\{{\mathcal{F}}_{\bullet}:e_{n}\in F_{n-1}\}={\mathcal{C}}_{2},

the second equality following from
\sigma_{0}e_{1}=e_{n}
.

It remains to examine the case where
x
is conjugate to
cI+E_{1n}
for some
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
(and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
is an arbitrary Hessenberg space). We claim that in this case, either
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=\varnothing
or
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=B(E_{1n},H)
. If
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\neq\varnothing
then there is some
g\in G
such that
g^{-1}\cdot x\in H
. Write
g=b_{1}\dot{w}b_{2}
with
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
and
w\in S_{n}
. We see that
\dot{w}^{-1}b_{1}^{-1}\cdot x\in b_{2}\cdot H=H
. Now

\dot{w}^{-1}b_{1}^{-1}\cdot x=cI+\dot{w}^{-1}b_{1}^{-1}\cdot E_{1n}=cI+dE_{w_{1},w_{n}}

for some
d\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
. In particular, we see that
\pi_{\varepsilon_{w_{1}}-\varepsilon_{w_{n}}}(H)\neq 0
, hence
E_{w_{1},w_{n}}\in H
. It follows that
cI\in H
. Now, for arbitrary
g\in G
,
g^{-1}\cdot x=cI+g^{-1}\cdot E_{1n}
lies in
H
if and only if
g^{-1}\cdot E_{1n}\in H
. The claim follows.

Now
\mathcal{B}(E_{1n},H)
is a highest weight Hessenberg variety for the adjoint representation and is therefore a union of Schubert cells. In particular, if
B(x,H)
is irreducible and of codimension one, then
\mathcal{B}(x,H)=X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
for some
i\in[n-1]
. We observe that if
1<i<n-1
then
s_{i}w_{0}
maps
1
to
n
and
n
to
1
and so lies in the same coset of the stabilizer of
\theta=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{n}
as
w_{0}
. On the other hand, neither
s_{1}w_{0}
nor
s_{n-1}w_{0}
lies in the coset and so both are longest representatives of the coset containing them. The lemma follows from Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
3.7
(and Lemma
5.4
).
∎

We record the novel geometric results from the proof of Lemma
5.9
below.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
x
be a semisimple matrix such that there exists distinct constants
c,d
with
xe_{i}=ce_{i}
for
i\in[n-1]
and
xe_{n}=de_{n}
. The adjoint Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
is a union of two irreducible components, one equal to the Schubert variety
X_{s_{n-1}w_{0}}
and the other isomorphic to the Schubert variety
X_{s_{1}w_{0}}
.

The following lemma, when combined with Lemmas
5.5
and
5.9
, will complete the proof of Proposition
5.3
.

Lemma 5.11
.

For every
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, the Euler characteristic
\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta})))
is divisible by
(n-2)!
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
x=x_{s}+x_{n}
with
x_{s}
diagonal and
x_{n}
an upper triangular, nilpotent matrix satisfying the assumptions of
[
Pre13
, Corollary 4.9]
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
be the set of all
w\in W
such that the Schubert cell
C_{w}
has nonempty intersection with
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
. By
[
Pre13
, Theorem 5.4]
and its proof, the affine spaces
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))\cap C_{w}
with
w\in{\mathcal{C}}(x,H)
determine an affine paving of
\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
. So,

\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta})))=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{\ast}(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta})))=|{\mathcal{C}}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))|.

Now by
[
Pre13
, Proposition 3.7]
,
w\in{\mathcal{C}}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
if and only if
Ad(\dot{w}^{-1})(x_{n})\in H(\overline{-\theta})
. We now write

x_{n}=\sum_{j<k}c_{jk}E_{j,k}

for some
c_{jk}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
It follows that
{\mathcal{C}}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
fails to contain exactly those
w\in W
such that
w(-\theta)=\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
for pairs
j<k
satisfying
c_{jk}\neq 0
. In particular,
W\setminus{\mathcal{C}}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))
is a union of cosets of the stabilizer of
-\theta
in
W
. As this stabilizer is isomorphic with
S_{n-2}
, the lemma follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Observe that
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
is irreducible. So, if there is some adjoint Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x,H)\subseteq\mathcal{B}
isomorphic with
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
, then
i!(n-i)!
is divisible by
(n-2)!
by Proposition
5.3
and Lemma
5.4
. However, if
n\geq 9
and
3\leq i\leq n-3
, then
0<i!(n-i)!<(n-2)!
. Moreover, inspection shows that if
6\leq n\leq 8
and
3\leq i\leq n-3
, then
(n-2)!
does not divide
i!(n-i)!
unless
(n,i)\in\{(8,3),(8,5)\}
.

We assume now that
n=8
and
i\in\{3,5\}
. In this case, we have
\chi(X_{s_{i}w_{0}})=8!-3!5!
. If
\mathcal{B}(x,H)
is irreducible of codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
then we have by Lemmas
5.5
and
5.9
that
H=H(\overline{-\theta})
. Let
x=x_{s}+x_{n}
be the decomposition of

as in the proof Lemma
5.9
. Arguing as in that proof, we get that

\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta})))=|\mathcal{C}(x,H(\overline{-\theta}))|=8!-6!\,|\{(j<k):c_{jk}\neq 0\}|.

It follows immediately that if
\chi(\mathcal{B}(x,H(\overline{-\theta})))=8!-3!5!
, then
x_{n}=E_{jk}
for some

. Finally, one can use the formulas given in
[

, Corollary 5.5]
to check that there is no Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}(x_{s}+E_{jk},H(\overline{-\theta}))
in the flag variety
\mathcal{B}=SL_{8}({\mathbb{C}})/B
with Betti numbers equal to those of the Schubert varieties
X_{s_{3}w_{0}}
and

. For the sake of brevity, we omit these computations.
∎

6.
Type C adjoint Hessenberg varieties

Recall from the introduction that the type C flag variety can be identified as the fixed-point set of a type A flag variety under a certain automorphism
\sigma
. Similarly, the Type A and Type C Schubert varieties are closely connected as each type C Schubert variety is the variety of
\sigma
-fixed points of a type A Schubert variety (see
[
LR08
, Chapter 6]
). The first main result of this section is that the same is true of type C Hessenberg varieties. Namely Theorem
6.1
below says that every type C adjoint Hessenberg variety is the variety of
\sigma
-fixed points of a type A Hessenberg variety and implies Theorem
1.13
. With this groundwork in place, we establish the type C pattern avoidance result stated in Theorem
1.12
(see Theorem
6.8
below). We remark that many of our proofs in this section would be considerably easier and shorter were we to assume that every Hessenberg space
H
contains the Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
.

We begin by fixing the notation needed to define the automorphism
\sigma
.
Let
E
be the
2n\times 2n
block matrix

E=\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
-J&0\end{bmatrix}

where
J
is the
n\times n
matrix with
1
’s on the anti-diagonal, and
0
’s elsewhere.
We follow
[
LR08
, Chapter 6]
and identify
Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
with the fixed point set of the involution
\sigma:SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\to SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
defined by
\sigma(A)=E(A^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}})^{-1}E^{-1}
.
Explicitly, consider the embedding
\phi:Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\hookrightarrow SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
whose image stabilizes the alternating form
\langle-,-\rangle
defined by

\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&j=2n+1-i,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(Here
e_{1},\ldots,e_{2n}
is the standard basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
.)
Then
G_{C}:=\phi(Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}}))
is the group of
\sigma
-fixed points in
SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Throughout this section we identify
Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
with
G_{C}
.

The maximal torus
T
in
SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
consisting of diagonal matrices and fixed Borel subgroup
B
in
SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
consisting of upper triangular matrices are stable under
\sigma
, and
T^{\sigma}
(respectively,
B_{C}:=B^{\sigma}
) is a maximal torus (respectively, Borel subgroup) in
G_{C}
.
For
i\in[2n]
, set

i^{\prime}=2n+1-i.

Let
W
be the Weyl group of
Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
. The embedding
\phi
induces an embedding
\phi^{*}:W\to S_{2n}
with image
W_{C}:=\phi^{*}(W)
consisting of those
w\in S_{2n}
satisfying
w(i)^{\prime}=w(i^{\prime})
for all
i\in[2n]
. We call such
w

signed permutations
.

We will write
\sigma
for the differential
d\sigma
which is the involution of the Lie algebra given by

\sigma:\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\to\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}}),\;\sigma(x)=Ex^{\mathsf{\mathsf{tr}}}E.

We identify
\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
with
\mathfrak{g}_{C}:=\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})^{\sigma}
. We observe that
\mathfrak{h}^{\sigma}
is the Lie algebra of
T^{\sigma}
, and

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\sigma}=\{\textup{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{2n})\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}}):d_{i}=-d_{i^{\prime}}\}.

The involution
\sigma
also induces an involution of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
defined by

{\sigma}:\mathfrak{h}^{*}\to\mathfrak{h}^{*},\;\;\sigma(\epsilon_{i})=-\epsilon_{i^{\prime}}.

We describe now a surjective map from the type A root system
\Phi_{A}
to the type C root system
\Phi_{C}
known as the
folding map
.
Set
\bar{\epsilon}_{i}:=\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i^{\prime}}
and note that, by definition,
\bar{\epsilon}_{i}=-\bar{\epsilon}_{i^{\prime}}
. The folding map is now defined to be

(6.2)

\displaystyle\varphi:\Phi_{A}\to\Phi_{C},\;\;\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\epsilon_{i}}-\bar{\epsilon_{j}}).

Let
H_{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{C}
be a type C Hessenberg space.
Our first goal is to construct a type A Hessenberg space
H
whose
\sigma
-fixed points are the elements of
H_{C}
.
Define
\Phi_{H_{C}}:=\{\gamma\in\Phi_{C}:\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\subseteq H_{C}\}
and set

(6.3)

\displaystyle\Phi_{H}:=\{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi_{A}:\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})\in\Phi_{H_{C}}\}\subseteq\Phi_{A}.

With
H\cap\mathfrak{h}
to be described below, we define
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
to be the subspace such that

(6.4)

\displaystyle H=(H\cap\mathfrak{h})\oplus\bigoplus_{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi_{H}}{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{ij}\}.

Given
i,j\in[2n]
, we set

h_{ij}:=[E_{ij},E_{ji}]=E_{ii}-E_{jj},

and define

H\cap\mathfrak{h}:={\mathbb{C}}\{h,h_{ij}:h\in H_{C}\cap\mathfrak{h}^{\sigma}\,\textup{ and }\,i,j\in[2n]\,\textup{ such that }\,\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j},\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{i}\in\Phi_{H}\}.

We observe that the action of
\sigma
on
SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
induces an automorphism of the type A flag variety
\mathcal{B}_{A}:=SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
, which will also be denoted by
\sigma
. The map
\phi^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{B}_{C}:=G_{C}/B_{C}
to
\mathcal{B}_{A}
sending
gB_{C}
to
gB
is a well-defined embedding, and
(\mathcal{B}_{A})^{\sigma}=\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{C})
(see, for example,
[
LR08
, Proposition 6.1.1.1]
).

We can now state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 6.1
.

Given a type
C
Hessenberg space
H_{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{C}
, let
H
be the subspace of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
defined as in (
6.4
) above.

(1)

The subspace
H
is a type A Hessenberg space such that
H^{\sigma}=H_{C}
.

(2)

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{C}
. The image under
\phi^{\prime}
of the type C Hessenberg variety
\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
is
\mathcal{B}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}
.

We prove first that in Theorem
6.1
, (1) implies (2).
The proof of Theorem
6.1
(1) is delayed until after Lemma
6.5
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
(
2
)
.

We wish to show that
\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C}))=\mathcal{B}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}
as varieties.
The set
\mathcal{G}_{C}(x,H_{C}):=\{g\in G_{C}:g^{-1}\cdot x\in H_{C}\}
is a
B_{C}
-invariant subvariety of
G_{C}
whose image under the morphism
\mu:G_{C}\to\mathcal{B}_{C}
is
\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H):=\{g\in SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}}):g^{-1}\cdot x\in H\}
.
Since
G_{C}=SL_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})^{\sigma}
, we have that
\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}=\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H)\cap G_{C}
is a subvariety of
G_{C}
.

Let us show that
\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}=\mathcal{G}_{C}(x,H_{C})
, as subvarieties of
G_{C}
.
By (1) of Theorem
6.1
we have

\mathcal{G}_{C}(x,H_{C})=\{g\in G_{C}:g^{-1}\cdot x\in H,\ g^{-1}\cdot x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})^{\sigma}\}.

Given
g\in G_{C}
, since
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{C}
, we have
g^{-1}\cdot x\in\mathfrak{g}_{C}
and thus
g^{-1}\cdot x
is
\sigma
-stable.
It follows that the constraints imposed by
g^{-1}\cdot x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})^{\sigma}
are redundant, so indeed
\mathcal{G}_{C}(x,H_{C})=\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}
.
In particular,

\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C}))=\phi^{\prime}(\mu(\mathcal{G}_{C}(x,H_{C})))=\phi^{\prime}(\mu(\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}))

as subvarieties of
\mathcal{B}
.
Finally, note that by definition of the maps,

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(\mu(\mathcal{G}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}))

\displaystyle=\phi^{\prime}(\{gB_{C}\in\mathcal{B}_{C}:\sigma(g)=g,\ g^{-1}\cdot x\in H\})

\displaystyle=\{gB\in\mathcal{B}:\sigma(g)=g,\ g^{-1}\cdot x\in H\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{A}(x,H)^{\sigma}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Now we develop the tools needed to prove Theorem
6.1
(1). Recall the partial order
\leq
on the root system
\Phi
defined in Section
3.1
above, and the concrete description of that order given in Lemma
3.3
.
Our next result tells us that the folding map interacts nicely with
\sigma
, is compatible with
\leq
, and is well-behaved with respect to the
W_{C}
-action. A proof can be found in
[
LR08
, Chapter 6.1]
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\varphi:\Phi_{A}\to\Phi_{C}
be the folding map defined as in (
6.2
). This map satisfies each of the following conditions.

(1)

\varphi(\Phi_{A}^{+})=\Phi_{C}^{+}
.

(2)

Given
\gamma\in\Phi_{C}
,
\varphi^{-1}(\gamma)
is precisely the
\sigma
-orbit of any
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Phi_{A}
such that
\varphi(\gamma^{\prime})=\gamma
.

(3)

The map
\varphi
is compatible with the partial ordering
\leq
on
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{C}
, that is, given
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Phi_{A}
we have
\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}
implies
\varphi(\gamma_{1})\leq\varphi(\gamma_{2})
.

(4)

\varphi
is equivariant with respect to the canonical action of
W_{C}
on
\Phi_{A}
and
\Phi_{C}
.

For use below, we recall that there is a simple description of the partial order
\leq
on the root system
\Phi_{A}
, given by

(6.5)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\leq\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow k\leq i\textup{ and }\ell\geq j.

Consider the surjective linear map

\bar{\sigma}:\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})\to\mathfrak{g}_{C},\;\;\bar{\sigma}(x)=x+\sigma(x).

Note that
\bar{\sigma}
is not a Lie algebra homomorphism.
However, the next lemma tells us that
\bar{\sigma}
maps the root spaces of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
onto those of
\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
h\in\mathfrak{h}^{\sigma}
. For all
1\leq k,\ell\leq 2n
with
k\neq\ell
, we have
\bar{\sigma}(E_{k\ell})\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})}
and
\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)=(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)
.

Proof.

Since
E_{k\ell}
is a root vector corresponding to the root
\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\in\Phi_{A}
we have
[h,E_{k\ell}]=(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)E_{k\ell}
. As
\sigma
is an involution of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
\sigma(h)=h
we get

\sigma([h,\sigma(E_{k\ell})])=[h,E_{k\ell}]=(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)E_{k\ell}\Rightarrow[h,\sigma(E_{k\ell})]=(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)\sigma(E_{k\ell}).

Thus,

\displaystyle[h,\bar{\sigma}(E_{k\ell})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[h,E_{k\ell}]+[h,\sigma(E_{k\ell})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)E_{k\ell}+(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)\sigma(E_{k\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)\bar{\sigma}(E_{k\ell}).

To conclude the argument, we have only to show that
\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)=(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)
. Since
h\in\mathfrak{h}^{\sigma}
, we have
\epsilon_{k}(h)=-\epsilon_{k^{\prime}}(h)
for all
k\in[2n]
(see (
6.1
)). Thus

\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\epsilon}_{k}-\bar{\epsilon}_{\ell})(h)=\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{k^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\ell}+\epsilon_{\ell^{\prime}})(h)=(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})(h)

as desired.
∎

Before arguing that
H
is a type A Hessenberg space, we prove it is
\sigma
-invariant.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
H_{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
be a type C Hessenberg space and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
be the subspace defined as in (
6.4
) above. Then
\sigma(H)=H
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\sigma(H)\subseteq H
. To do this, we check that

(1)

\sigma(E_{ij})\in H
for all
i,j
such that
\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi_{H}
, i.e., such that
\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})\in\Phi_{H_{C}}
,

(2)

\sigma(h_{ij})\in H

\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j},\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{i}\in\Phi_{H}
, and

(3)

\sigma(h)\in H

Condition (3) follows immediately from the facts that

. Note that
\sigma(E_{ij})\in{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\}

\displaystyle\varphi(\epsilon_{j^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{i^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\epsilon}_{j^{\prime}}-\bar{\epsilon}_{i^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\epsilon}_{i}-\bar{\epsilon}_{j})=\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})

we do indeed get

Finally, we prove (2). Suppose

such that
\pm(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})\in\Phi_{H}
, and consider

\sigma(h_{ij})=\sigma(E_{ii}-E_{jj})=-E_{i^{\prime}i^{\prime}}+E_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=h_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}.

By (

) we have
\varphi(\epsilon_{j^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{i^{\prime}})=\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})\in\Phi_{H_{C}}

\epsilon_{j^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{i^{\prime}}\in\Phi_{H}
and similarly
\epsilon_{i^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{j^{\prime}}\in\Phi_{H}

By the definition of
H
, this implies

, as desired.
∎

Next, we argue that
\Phi_{H}
is an upper order ideal with respect to the partial order
\leq
on
\Phi_{A}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
H_{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{C}
be a type C Hessenberg space. The corresponding subset
\Phi_{H}\subset\Phi_{A}
defined as in (

) is an upper order ideal with respect to the partial order
\leq
on
\Phi_{A}
.

Proof.

Suppose

\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi_{H}
and let
\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\in\Phi_{A}

\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\geq\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}
. By Lemma
6.2
,
\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})\geq\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})

Since

is an upper order ideal with respect to the partial order

∎

Proof of Theorem

and
\ell\geq i

and

•

•

and

If

Case 3:
Suppose

and thus

\displaystyle[\bar{\sigma}(E_{ij}),\bar{\sigma}(E_{ji})]

We may write each matrix in

We begin with the following statement, which is a Type C analogue of Lemma

Lemma 6.6

Let

Proposition 6.7
.

Assume that

and that
\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})\subseteq G_{C}/B_{C}
is a type C Hessenberg variety. Let

as in (

is contained in
\mathcal{B}(x,H)

Proof.

We write

x=h+\sum_{\gamma\in\Phi_{C}}d_{\gamma}E_{\gamma}

where

,

is a nonzero root vector in
\mathfrak{g}_{\gamma}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{C}
, and

{\mathbf{c}}_{\gamma}(x)=d_{\gamma}

To prove

\displaystyle(u\dot{w})^{-1}\cdot x=\dot{w}^{-1}u^{-1}\cdot h+\dot{w}^{-1}u^{-1}\cdot x_{1}\in H.

Now we compute

\displaystyle u^{-1}\cdot x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Ad(\exp(-y))(x_{1})=\exp(\ad_{-y})(x_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\ad_{-y}^{m}(x_{1})=x_{1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\gamma\in\Phi_{C}}\frac{1}{m!}d_{\gamma}\ad_{-y}^{m}(E_{\gamma}).

\gamma\in\Phi_{C}

\varphi^{-1}(\gamma)=\{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j},\epsilon_{j^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{i^{\prime}}\}

\ad_{-y}^{m}(E_{\gamma})\in{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{k\ell}:k\leq i,\ell\geq j\,\textup{ or }\,k\leq j^{\prime},\ell\geq i^{\prime}\}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\{h_{k\ell}:j\leq k<\ell\leq i\,\textup{ or }\,i^{\prime}\leq k<\ell\leq j^{\prime}\}.

(6.10)

\displaystyle u^{-1}\cdot x_{1}\in\bigoplus_{d_{\gamma}\neq 0}\,\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi_{A}\\
\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})=\gamma\end{subarray}}\left({\mathbb{C}}\{E_{k\ell}:k\leq i,\ell\geq j\}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\{h_{k\ell}:j\leq k<\ell\leq i\}\right).

Next, we have that

\displaystyle u^{-1}\cdot h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Ad(\exp(-y))(h)=\exp(\ad_{-y})(h)=h+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\ad_{-y}^{m}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\,\sum_{p<q}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(y)(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})(h)\ad_{-y}^{m-1}(E_{pq}).

Applying similar reasoning as above and using the fact that

(6.11)

\displaystyle u^{-1}\cdot h-h\in\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q}\in\Phi_{A}^{+}\\

Note that
\dot{w}^{-1}\cdot h\in H_{C}\subseteq H
by assumption. Thus equations (

(6.12)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\bigoplus_{d_{\gamma}\neq 0}\,\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}\in\Phi_{A}\\
\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})=\gamma\end{subarray}}\left({\mathbb{C}}\left\{E_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}:k\leq i,\ell\geq j\right\}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\left\{h_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}:j\leq k<\ell\leq i\right\}\right)\subseteq H

and

(6.13)

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q}\in\Phi_{A}\\
(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})(h)\neq 0\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{C}}\left\{E_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}:k\leq p,\ell\geq q\right\}\subseteq H.

First we establish (
6.12
). If
d_{\gamma}\neq 0
, Lemma
6.6
implies that for each
\beta\in\Phi_{C}
with
\beta\geq\gamma
there exists
b\in B_{C}
such that,

0\neq{\mathbf{c}}_{\beta}(b\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{w^{-1}(\beta)}(\dot{w}^{-1}b\cdot x).

Our assumption that
B_{C}\dot{w}B_{C}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
now implies
E_{w^{-1}(\beta)}\in H_{C}
and thus
w^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi_{H_{C}}
for all
\beta\geq\gamma
. Note that by Lemma
6.2
, for all
\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\in\Phi_{A}
with
\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}\geq\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}
we have
\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell})\geq\gamma
.
This implies
w^{-1}(\varphi(\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{\ell}))\in\Phi_{H_{C}}
and, since
\varphi
is
W_{C}
-equivariant, we have
\varphi(\epsilon_{w^{-1}_{k}}-\epsilon_{w^{-1}_{\ell}})\in\Phi_{H_{C}}
. Using the description of
\leq
for
\Phi_{A}
from (
6.5
) we have now proved:

\displaystyle d_{\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})}\neq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\epsilon_{w^{-1}(k)}-\epsilon_{w^{-1}(\ell)}\in\Phi_{H}\;\textup{ for all }\;k\leq i\;\textup{ and }\;\ell\geq j

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle E_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}\in H\;\textup{ for all }\;k\leq i\;\textup{ and }\;\ell\geq j.

It follows that

\displaystyle d_{\varphi(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j})}\neq 0\textup{ and }i>j

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle E_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}},E_{w^{-1}_{\ell},w^{-1}_{k}}\in H\;\textup{ for all $k,\ell$ such that }\;j\leq k<\ell\leq i

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle h_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}\in H\;\textup{ for all $k,\ell$ such that }\;j\leq k<\ell\leq i,

where the last implication follows from the fact that
[\mathfrak{b},H]\subseteq H
. This concludes the proof of (
6.12
).

Next we prove (
6.13
). Fix
\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q}\in\Phi_{A}
such that
(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})(h)\neq 0
. First, we note that if
\varphi(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})\geq\gamma
for some
\gamma\in\Phi_{C}
such that
d_{\gamma}\neq 0
, then

{\mathbb{C}}\left\{E_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}:k\leq p,\ell\geq q\right\}\subseteq H

by (
6.12
). Thus it suffices to consider the case in which
\varphi(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})\not\geq\gamma
for any
\gamma\in\Phi_{C}
such that
d_{\gamma}\neq 0
.
This last assumption implies
E_{pq}
is not a summand of
u^{-1}\cdot x_{1}
for any
u\in U
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(u^{-1}\cdot x_{1})=0
for all
u\in U
.
Consider
u_{pq}:=I_{n}+\bar{\sigma}(E_{pq})\in B_{C}
. Applying Lemma
6.3
and using properties of the adjoint action we have

\displaystyle u_{pq}^{-1}\cdot h=h-[\bar{\sigma}(E_{pq}),h]+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i!}\ad_{-\bar{\sigma}(E_{pq})}^{i}(h)=h+(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})(h)\bar{\sigma}(E_{pq}).

This implies

\displaystyle u_{pq}^{-1}\cdot x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{pq}^{-1}\cdot h+u_{pq}^{-1}\cdot x_{1}=h+(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})(h)\bar{\sigma}(E_{pq})+u_{k\ell}^{-1}\cdot x_{1}.

Since
\bar{\sigma}(E_{pq})\in\mathfrak{g}_{\varphi(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})}
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(u_{pq}^{-1}\cdot x_{1})=0
, it follows that
{\mathbf{c}}_{\varphi(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})}(u_{pq}^{-1}\cdot x)=(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})(h)\neq 0
.
Since
u_{pq}\in B_{C}
, the assumption
B_{C}\dot{w}B_{C}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
implies
\dot{w}^{-1}bu_{k\ell}^{-1}\cdot x\in H_{C}
for all
b\in B_{C}
.
Furthermore, Lemma
6.6
implies that for each
\beta\geq\varphi(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})
there exists
b\in B_{C}
such that

0\neq{\mathbf{c}}_{\beta}(bu_{pq}^{-1}\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{w^{-1}(\beta)}(\dot{w}^{-1}bu_{pq}^{-1}\cdot x)

and thus we have
w^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi_{H_{C}}
for all
\beta\geq\varphi(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{q})
. In particular, arguing as in the proof of (
6.12
) we have

E_{w^{-1}_{k}w^{-1}_{\ell}}\in H\,\textup{ for all }\,k\leq p\textup{ and }\ell\geq q.

This establishes (
6.13
) and completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Theorem
1.12
.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
G=Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
and let
B\leq G
be the Borel subgroup whose image under
\phi
consists of upper triangular matrices. Fix
w\in W
. If there exist
x\in\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
and a Hessenberg space
H_{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})=X_{w^{-1}}^{C}
, then
\phi^{\ast}(w)
avoids the pattern
[4231]
.

We remark that since
[4231]
is its own inverse, this theorem implies that if
\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})=X_{w}^{C}
, then
\phi^{\ast}(w)
avoids the pattern
[4231]
.
So Theorem
6.8
implies Theorem
1.12
.

Proof.

Suppose
w\in W_{C}
contains the pattern
[4231]
.
Seeking a contradiction, suppose there exists
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{C}
and a type
C
Hessenberg space
H_{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{C}
such that
X_{w^{-1}}^{C}=\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
.
By Proposition
6.7
,
B\dot{w}^{-1}B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,H)
where
H\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
is the type A Hessenberg space defined using
H_{C}
as in (
6.4
) above.
To obtain a contradiction, we show that there exists
v\in W_{C}
such that
v\nleq_{\mathsf{Br}}w
and
\dot{v}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}_{A}(x,H)
.
Given this statement, we would then have by Theorem
6.1
that
\dot{v}^{-1}B\in(\mathcal{B}_{A}(x,H))^{\sigma}=\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
contradicting our assumption that
X_{w^{-1}}^{C}=\mathcal{B}_{C}(x,H_{C})
.

Since
w
contains the pattern
[4231]
there exist
i,j,k,\ell
such that
1\leq i<j<k<\ell\leq 2n
and
w_{\ell}<w_{j}<w_{k}<w_{i}
. Consider
\tau=(w_{j},w_{k})w
where
(w_{j},w_{k})
is the transposition exchanging
w_{j}
and
w_{k}
, so
\tau\in S_{2n}
is as defined in the statement of Lemma
4.3
.
By Lemma
4.3
,
\dot{\tau}^{-1}B\in\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, that is,
\dot{\tau}^{-1}\cdot x\in H
.
If
j^{\prime}=k
then
\tau\in W_{C}
and taking
v=\tau
accomplishes the desired goal.
We may therefore assume
j^{\prime}\neq k
for the remainder of the proof.

We have that
1\leq\ell^{\prime}<k^{\prime}<j^{\prime}<i^{\prime}\leq 2n
and
w_{i^{\prime}}<w_{k^{\prime}}<w_{j^{\prime}}<w_{\ell^{\prime}}
.
Let
v=(w_{j^{\prime}},w_{k^{\prime}})\tau=(w_{j^{\prime}},w_{k^{\prime}})(w_{j},w_{k})w
, where
(w_{j^{\prime}},w_{k^{\prime}})
is the transposition exchanging
w_{j^{\prime}}
and
w_{k^{\prime}}
. Note that
v\nleq_{\mathsf{Br}}w
since
\ell(v)>\ell(w)
and
v\in W_{C}
.
In order to argue that
\dot{v}\cdot x\in H
, we write
x=s+x^{\prime}
where

s=\sum_{p\in[2n-1]}c_{p}(E_{pp}-E_{p+1,p+1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^{\prime}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(p,q)\in[2n]\times[2n]\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(x)E_{pq}

and show
\dot{v}\cdot s,\dot{v}\cdot x^{\prime}\in H
. Note that
\dot{w}\cdot s,\dot{w}\cdot x\in H
by assumption.
From the proof of Lemma
4.3
we have that
\dot{\tau}\cdot s\in H
and

\dot{w}\cdot s-\dot{\tau}\cdot s=(c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k})(E_{w_{k}w_{k}}-E_{w_{j}w_{j}})\in H.

Since
\sigma(H)\subset H
, we see that

\displaystyle\sigma(\dot{w}\cdot s-\dot{\tau}\cdot s)

\displaystyle=(c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k})(-E_{w_{k^{\prime}}w_{k^{\prime}}}+E_{w_{j^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle=(c_{k^{\prime}-1}-c_{k^{\prime}}-c_{j^{\prime}-1}+c_{j^{\prime}})(E_{w_{j^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}}}-E_{w_{k^{\prime}}w_{k^{\prime}}})\in H,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
s\in\mathfrak{h}_{C}
(see (
6.1
)).
A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\dot{w}\cdot s-\dot{v}\cdot s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{j-1}-c_{j}-c_{k-1}+c_{k})(E_{w_{k}w_{k}}-E_{w_{j}w_{j}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+(c_{k^{\prime}-1}-c_{k^{\prime}}-c_{j^{\prime}-1}+c_{j^{\prime}})(E_{w_{j^{\prime}}w_{j^{\prime}}}-E_{w_{k^{\prime}}w_{k^{\prime}}}),

which lies in
H
, and therefore
\dot{w}\cdot s\in H
implies
\dot{v}\cdot s\in H
.

Next we show that
\dot{v}\cdot x^{\prime}\in H
by proving that
E_{v_{p}v_{q}}\in H
whenever
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(x)\neq 0
. Let
p\neq q
be such that
{\mathbf{c}}_{pq}(x)\neq 0
. Let
\tau^{\prime}=(w_{j^{\prime}},w_{k^{\prime}})w
, and note that we have
\dot{\tau}^{\prime}\cdot x\in H
by Lemma
4.3
. In fact, by the proof of Lemma
4.3
applied to both
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
we have

\{p,q\}\cap\{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}=\varnothing\Rightarrow E_{v_{p}v_{q}}=E_{\tau_{p}\tau_{q}}\in H

and

\{p,q\}\cap\{j,k\}=\varnothing\Rightarrow E_{v_{p}v_{q}}=E_{\tau^{\prime}_{p}\tau^{\prime}_{q}}\in H.

To complete the proof, suppose that
\{p,q\}\cap\{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}\neq\varnothing
and
\{p,q\}\cap\{j,k\}\neq\varnothing
.
If
p\in\{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}
and
q\in\{j,k\}
then
\ell^{\prime}<p
and
q<\ell
.
By Lemma
4.1
there exists
b\in B
such that
0\neq{\mathbf{c}}_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}(b\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{w_{\ell^{\prime}}w_{\ell}}(\dot{w}b\cdot x)
.
Since
b^{-1}\dot{w}^{-1}B\in C_{w^{-1}}\subset\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, we must have
E_{w_{\ell^{\prime}}w_{\ell}}\in H
.
Now by Lemma
4.2
, since
v_{p}\leq w_{\ell^{\prime}}
and
v_{q}\geq w_{\ell}
, we have
E_{v_{p}v_{q}}\in H
, as desired.
Next, we consider the case in which
p\in\{j,k\}
and
q\in\{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}
.
Since
i<p
and
q<i^{\prime}
, by Lemma
4.1
there exists
b\in B
such that
0\neq{\mathbf{c}}_{ii^{\prime}}(b\cdot x)={\mathbf{c}}_{w_{i}w_{i^{\prime}}}(\dot{w}b\cdot x)
.
Since
b^{-1}\dot{w}^{-1}B\in C_{w^{-1}}\subset\mathcal{B}(x,H)
, then
E_{w_{i}w_{i^{\prime}}}\in H
.
Now by Lemma
4.2
, by
v_{p}\leq w_{i}
and
v_{q}\geq w_{i^{\prime}}
we have
E_{v_{p}v_{q}}\in H
, as desired. This concludes the proof.
∎
