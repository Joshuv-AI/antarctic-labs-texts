On the intersecting family process
Patrick Bennett, Alan Frieze, Andrew Newman, Wesley Pegden

Abstract

Introduction

We study the following process introduced by Bohman, Cooper, Frieze, Martin and Ruszinkó
[
6
]
: consider the random sequence
{\cal I}_{k}=(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{m})
where
E_{i}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
and (i)
E_{1}
is uniformly random and (ii)
E_{i+1}
is randomly chosen from the
k
-subsets of
[n]
that are not already chosen and that intersect each of
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{i}
. The process continues until no further sets can be added i.e. until
\{E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{m}\}
is a maximal intersecting family. We will abuse terminology and sometimes consider
{\cal I}_{k}
to be a set of edges (i.e. a
k
-uniform hypergraph) instead of a sequence of edges.

We denote the hypergraph comprising the first
r
accepted edges by
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
. For a set
S\subseteq[n]
, we let
e_{r}(S)
denote the number of edges
E_{i},i\leq r
such that
E_{i}\cap S\neq\emptyset
.
For our purposes, an intersecting family is
trivial
if it is of the form
{\mathcal{A}}_{x}=\left\{E\in\binom{[n]}{k}:x\in E\right\}
where
x
is some fixed element of
[n]
. Now
|{\mathcal{A}}_{x}|=\binom{n-1}{k-1}
and the famous result of Erdős, Ko and Rado
[
9
]
is that if
k\leq n/2
then any intersecting family of
k
-sets is bounded in size by
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
and that the maximum is achieved only by trivial families. The aim of
[
6
]
was to see when the process
{\cal I}_{k}
produces a trivial family w.h.p. Their main result is the following:

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
be the event that
{\cal I}_{k}={\mathcal{A}}_{x}
for some
x\in[n]
. If
k=c_{n}n^{1/3}<n/2
then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}({\mathcal{E}}_{0})=\begin{cases}1&c_{n}\to 0\\
\frac{1}{1+c^{3}}&c_{n}\to c\\
0&c_{n}\to\infty\end{cases}.

So if
c_{n}\to c
in this theorem, then in the limit, there is a positive probability of
\frac{c^{3}}{1+c^{3}}
that
{\cal I}_{k}
is not trivial. What can be said about this case?

Patkós
[
13
]
considered this question and showed that in the random intersecting process we study here that for
k=c_{n}n^{1/3}
with
c_{n}\rightarrow c
with probability

\left(\frac{c^{3}}{1+c^{3}}\right)\left(\frac{3}{3+c^{3}}\right)

{\cal I}_{k}
is a
Hilton–Milner
-type hypergraph. A Hilton–Milner-type hypergraph was first described in
[
10
]
and is a
k
-uniform hypergraph obtained by specifying a single vertex
v
and a single edge
F
that does not contain
v
and the edges are
F\cup\{E\in\binom{[n]}{k}\mid v\in E,F\cap E\neq\emptyset\}
. Our goal here is to extend beyond the two possibilities considered so far, the trivial system and the Hilton–Milner system, to further understand the full distribution of the asymptotic behavior in the critical regime.

Our first result (Theorem
2
below) applies to the case where
k=c_{n}n^{1/3}
where
c_{n}\rightarrow c
. We describe two randomly generated families
{\cal I}^{*},{\cal I}^{**}
, which are approximately the same size, such that w.h.p.
{\cal I}^{*}\subseteq{\cal I}_{k}\subseteq{\cal I}^{**}
. Furthermore
{\cal I}^{*},{\cal I}^{**}
can be determined from the very early evolution of the process.

We define two hitting times which will help determine
{\cal I}^{*},{\cal I}^{**}
. We let
r_{0}
be the first step
r
such that
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
has a vertex of degree three. We let
J
be the set of vertices of degree two in
{\mathcal{H}}_{r_{0}-1}
. A set
S\subseteq J
is said to be
independent
if no edge of
{\mathcal{H}}_{r_{0}-1}
contains more than one member of
S
. For
r\geq r_{0}
we let
{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
be the family of all
S\subseteq J
that are independent and which meet every edge
E_{r_{0}},\ldots,E_{r}
. Let
r_{1}
be the first step
r
when
{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
is an intersecting family, and set
{\mathcal{S}}:={\mathcal{S}}_{r_{1}}
to be that intersecting family. (We show below that w.h.p.
{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
becomes an intertsecting family before the process terminates.) To summarise:

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=\text{the first step $r$ such that ${\mathcal{H}}_{r}$ has a vertex of degree three.}

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=\text{the first step $r$ when ${\mathcal{S}}_{r}$ is an intersecting family.}

In the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
{\mathcal{S}}
has a natural interpretation as an intersecting family of matchings of a complete graph. Both the size of this complete graph and the actual intersecting family are random, and in general
{\mathcal{S}}
will not be a uniform hypergraph. In the appendix we explain this random process to generate
{\mathcal{S}}
outside the broader context of its role in Theorem
2
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
k={\left\lfloor cn^{1/3}\right\rfloor}
for some positive constant
c
. Then we have the following.

(a)

Let
b=b(n)\rightarrow\infty
. Then w.h.p.
r_{0}\leq r_{1}\leq b
.

(b)

W.h.p.
{\cal I}^{*}\subseteq{\cal I}_{k}\subseteq{\cal I}^{**}
where

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{*}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{H}}_{r_{0}-1}\cup\{E:E\mbox{ contains some $S\in{\mathcal{S}}$ and intersects every edge of ${\mathcal{H}}_{r_{0}-1}$}\},

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{**}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{H}}_{r_{0}-1}\cup\{E:E\mbox{ intersects each $S\in{\mathcal{S}}$ and intersects every edge of ${\mathcal{H}}_{r_{0}-1}$}\}.

(c)

W.h.p.
|{\cal I}^{**}\setminus{\cal I}^{*}|=o(|{\cal I}^{*}|)
and

\frac{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}{\binom{n}{k}}=(1+o(1))\frac{\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}c^{3(r_{0}-1-|S|)}}{k^{r_{0}-1}}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(d)

The sequence

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\{\frac{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(r_{0}=i+1)}{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(r_{0}>i+1)}\right\}_{i\geq 0}}

has exponential generating function

e^{x}(e^{x^{2}/(2c^{3})}-1).

Note that the distribution of
r_{0}
can be recovered from part (
d
). Regarding part (
b
), we note that the containment
{\cal I}^{*}\subseteq{\cal I}_{k}\subseteq{\cal I}^{**}
is not in general strict at either end. For example
{\cal I}^{*}
may not be maximally intersecting, and
{\cal I}^{**}
may not be intersecting at all. We suspect that it may be possible to give a better estimate for where the final
{\cal I}_{k}
falls in between
{\cal I}^{*}
and
{\cal I}^{**}
, but that this information cannot be determined from the first few edges.

We say that a family
{\cal J}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
is a
j
-junta
if there is some
J\subseteq[n]
with
|J|=j>0
and a family
{\cal J}^{*}
of subsets of
J
such that

{\cal J}=\{T\in\binom{[n]}{k}:T\cap J\in{\cal J}^{*}\}.

We say that
{\cal J}
is
generated by
(J,{\cal J}^{*})
. Juntas are relevant here because, roughly speaking, they can provide a simple “certificate” that a family is intersecting. Specifically if
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq{\cal J}
for some junta
{\cal J}
generated by
(J,{\cal J}^{*})
with
{\cal J}^{*}
intersecting, then
{\mathcal{H}}
must be intersecting. For example a trivial intersecting family is a 1-junta, sometimes called a
dictatorship
. For a second example a Hilton–Milner-type hypergraph is almost (with the exception of one edge) contained in a dictatorship. Using this terminology, Theorem
2
has the following corollary.

Corollary 3
.

Let
k={\left\lfloor cn^{1/3}\right\rfloor}
where
c>0
, and
b=b(n)\rightarrow\infty
. Then w.h.p. there is some
b
-junta
{\cal J}
such that
|{\cal I}_{k}\setminus{\cal J}|=o(|{\cal I}_{k}|)
. Furthermore
{\cal J}
is determined by the hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}_{b}
.

Bohman, Frieze, Martin, Ruszinkó and Smyth
[
7
]
considered the case where
n^{1/3}\ll k\ll n^{5/12}
. They prove that w.h.p. the structure of
{\cal I}_{k}
satisfies the following: there exists a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
with
\tau
edges and a vertex
v
such that the following holds: (i)
n\tau^{3}/6k^{3}
converges to the exponential distribution with mean 1 and (ii)
{\cal I}_{k}
consists of all
E\in\binom{[n]}{k}
that (a) contain
v
and (b) meet every edge of
{\mathcal{H}}
that does not contain
v
.
So in this regime we also have that w.h.p.
{\cal I}_{k}
is almost (with the exception of the
poly(n)
edges of
{\mathcal{H}}
) contained in a dictatorship. It is interesting to note that, combining the results of
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and Theorem
2
, for the whole regime
k\ll n^{5/12}
we have that w.h.p.
{\cal I}_{k}
is almost contained in a relatively small junta of order
k^{2}/n^{1/3}
, and furthermore this small junta is a dictatorship unless
k=\Theta(n^{1/3})
.
This is not a shortcoming in our proof: indeed, for
k=\Theta(n^{1/3})
and sufficently small fixed
\varepsilon>0
, Theorem
2
implies that there is a probability bounded away from zero that no dictatorship contains more than
1-\varepsilon
proportion of
{\cal I}_{k}
.

We also make a little progress on the case where
k\gg n^{1/2}
. In particular we give non-trivial upper and lower bounds on
|{\cal I}_{k}|
. Let
N=\binom{n}{k}
and
d=\binom{n-k}{k}
. There is a trivial lower bound of
N/(d+1)
on the minimum size of a maximal intersecting family. We prove that w.h.p.
|{\cal I}_{k}|
is significantly larger.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
k\gg n^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n
. Then w.h.p.
\Omega\left(\frac{N\log N}{d}\right)\leq|{\cal I}_{k}|\leq O(N^{1-\eta})
for some constant
\eta>0
.

Our proof of Theorem
4
will resemble the analysis of some similar processes that have been studied. Suppose we are given a graph
G
. In the
random greedy independent set process
, or just
independent process
, we choose a random sequence
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
of vertices of
G
where
v_{i+1}
is randomly chosen from all vertices not already chosen and not adjacent to any chosen vertex. Then the process we are studying is equivalent to the independent process on the Kneser graph
K(n,k)
which has vertex set
\left\{v_{S}:S\in\binom{[n]}{k}\right\}
, and where
v_{S}
is adjacent to
v_{S^{\prime}}
whenever
S\cap S^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Wormald
[
14
]
was the first to study the independent process, which he analyzed on random regular graphs. Lauer and Wormald
[
12
]
extended the analysis from random regular graphs to all regular graphs of sufficiently high girth. Bennett and Bohman
[
4
]
analyzed a generalization of this process to
d
-regular hypergraphs with
d\rightarrow\infty
sufficiently fast assuming a relatively mild upper bound on codegrees. So, one could hope that we could just use some existing analysis of the independent process and apply it to the Kneser graph. Unfortunately, all the results for general deterministic graphs (or at least the ones we could find) assume a similar upper bound on vertex codegrees which the Kneser graph does not satisfy. Indeed, for say
k=cn
for constant
c
, the graph
K(n,k)
has pairs of vertices whose codegree is the same order of magnitude as their degrees. Even worse, every vertex in the whole graph has high codegree with a few other vertices. Thus, our main contribution in this regime is to carry out an analysis of the process that resembles previous work but allows for a few pairs of vertices with high codegree. The analysis uses the so-called differential equation method (see
[
5
]
for a gentle introduction).

To put the above results in some context we point out another related but distinct random model of intersecting families. Improving a result of Balogh, Das, Delcourt, Liu and Sharifzadeh
[
1
]
, Balogh, Das, Liu, Sharifzadeh and Tran
[
2
]
and Frankl and Kupavskii
[
11
]
showed that if
n\geq 2k+\Omega((k\log k)^{1/2})
then almost all intersecting families are trivial. Balogh, Garcia, Li and Wagner
[
3
]
reduced the lower bound on
n
to
2k+100\log k
. Dinur and Friedgut
[
8
]
proved that when
k=pn
for constant
0<p<1/2
, every intersecting family is (up to a small number of members) contained in an
O(1)
-junta. In
[
8
]
they also proved that when
k=o(n)
every intersecting family is almost contained in a trivial family (a dictatorship).

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we prove Theorem
2
. In Sections
3
and
4
we prove Theorem
4
, where in each of the two sections we consider a certain range of
k
. The proofs for each regime are almost identical, but the error bounds are somewhat different. In Section
5
we give some concluding remarks.

Proof of Theorem
2

2.1
The first
o(\sqrt{\log n})
steps: generating
\mathcal{S}

We examine the first
r
steps in the process for
r=o(\sqrt{\log n})
. The most important part of the random intersecting process in these first few steps is the generation of a (not necessarily uniform) intersecting hypergraph
\mathcal{S}
that will be the
\mathcal{S}
in the definition of
{\cal I}^{*}
and
{\cal I}^{**}
.
The random intersecting hypergraph
\mathcal{S}
in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
is described on its own in the appendix.
To describe
\mathcal{S}
here we introduce some notation.

Denote by
V(\mathcal{H}_{r})
the set of all vertices of degree at least one in
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
, and by
U(\mathcal{H}_{r})
the set of vertices of degree exactly one. For any set
S
of vertices of degree at least two in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
, let
E_{r}(S)
denote the set of edges of
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
that contain a vertex in
S
and let
e_{r}(S)=|E_{r}(S)|
. Let
\chi_{r}(S)=e_{r}(S)-2|S|
and let
\chi^{*}_{r}=\max\{\chi_{r}(S)\mid S\subseteq V(\mathcal{H}_{r})\setminus U(\mathcal{H}_{r})\}
. At each step
r
, we let

\mathcal{S}_{r}=\{S\subseteq V(\mathcal{H}_{r})\setminus U(\mathcal{H}_{r})\mid\chi_{r}(S)=\chi^{*}_{r}\}

Here we will describe three regimes for
\mathcal{S}_{r}
: a growing regime, a diminishing regime, and a stable regime. As the name suggests,
\mathcal{S}
will denote the unchanging
\mathcal{S}_{r}
within the stable regime.

The growing regime coincides with all steps before vertices of degree three show up. Letting
r_{0}
denote the number of edges when the first vertex of degree three appears we have by the following lemma that w.h.p. for
r<r_{0}
,
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is just the collection of all independent sets of vertices of degree two in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
and
\chi^{*}_{r}=0
.
A hypergraph
H
is
simple
if
E_{1},E_{2}\in E(H)
implies that
|E_{1}\cap E_{2}|\leq 1
.

Lemma 5
.

If
r=o(\sqrt{\log n})
and
\mathcal{H}_{r-1}
is simple and has no vertices of degree at least 3 then the probability that
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is not simple is
O(1/k^{1-o(1)})
.

This lemma will follow as a corollary to another lemma we prove shortly, so we save the proof for later. By part (
d
) of Theorem
2
,
r_{0}
will take
some value bounded by any slowly growing function of
n
, and we use without further remark that with high probability
r_{0}
is smaller than any function that tends to infinity.

By simplicity, before vertices of degree three appear the size of
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is given by the following lemma: a hypergraph will be called intersecting if its edge set defines an intersecting family.

Lemma 6
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a
k
-uniform simple intersecting hypergraph on
r
edges with maximum degree two then for each
1\leq m\leq r/2
the number of independent sets of vertices of degree two of size
m
is

\frac{r!}{(r-2m)!m!2^{m}}.

Proof.

Let
I_{r,m}
denote the number of independent sets of vertices of degree two of size
m
when
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the assumptions. Then,
I_{r,m}
satisfies the recurrence,

I_{r,m}=\frac{\binom{r}{2}I_{r-2,m-1}}{m}.

Indeed we can pick any of the
\binom{r}{2}
vertices of degree two to start building an independent set. We then delete the selected vertex and the two edges that contain it and then take an independent set of vertices of degree two of size
m-1
from the remaining hypergraph. However, this overcounts by a factor of
m
because of the choice of the first vertex.

Now clearly
I_{r,1}=\binom{r}{2}=\frac{r!}{(r-2)!1!2^{1}}
. And by induction using the recurrence above we have

I_{r,m}=\frac{r(r-1)(r-2)!}{2m(r-2-2m+2)!(m-1)!2^{m-1}}=\frac{r!}{(r-2m)!m!2^{m}}=\binom{r}{2m}\binom{2m}{m}2^{-m}.

∎

We next show that once vertices of degree three appear
\chi^{*}_{r}
starts to increase by one at each step, and that
\mathcal{S}_{r}
decays, until it reaches an intersecting family at time
r_{1}
with
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}_{r_{1}}
.

Before we prove how the random intersecting process stabilizes to
\mathcal{S}
, we introduce some definitions:

Definition 1
.

An edge
E
that extends the intersecting family
\mathcal{H}_{r}
to an intersecting family
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
is said to be an
almost simple extension
if for every pair of distinct
x,y\in E
that are already contained in a common edge in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
, both
x
and
y
have degree at least two in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Definition 2
.

Given a
k
-uniform intersecting family
\mathcal{H}_{r}
on
r
edges, an edge
E
that extends the intersecting family is
good
if it is an almost simple extension and the set of vertices
S
of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
of degree at least two that belong to
E
satisfies
\chi_{r}(S)=\chi_{r}^{*}
. Otherwise we say the extension is
bad
.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose that
r=o(\sqrt{\log n})
and that
\mathcal{H}_{r}
has been generated by the random intersecting process via good extensions with
\mathcal{S}_{r}
as defined above, then the probability that
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
is generated by selecting a bad extension of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is at most
1/k^{1-o(1)}
.

Proof.

We first show that the number of extensions that are not almost simple is at most
k^{-(r+1-\chi_{r}^{*}+o(1))}\binom{n}{k}
.

We have two cases to consider, either
E
contains two vertices of degree one in the same edge of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
or else it contains one vertex of degree one and one vertex of degree two from an edge of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
. To build such an extension in the former case we first choose an edge (
r
choices) and then two vertices of degree one from it (at most
k^{2}
choices). Next we choose a set
S
of vertices of degree at least two to belong to the new edge. Since
\mathcal{H}_{r}
has been built out of good extensions, the number of vertices of degree at least two is at most
\binom{r}{2}
. So we have at most
2^{r^{2}}
choices for
S
. Once the two vertices of degree one in a common edge and
S
have been selected for
E
we have covered
1+e_{r}(S)
edges, so we have
r-e_{r}(S)-1
edges still to cover. We cover these remaining edges with vertices of degree one since
S
already accounts for the vertices of degree larger than 1 that we will use. So we have to pick at least one vertex from the remaining
r-e_{r}(S)-1
edges. Lastly we choose another
k-(r-e_{r}(S)-1)-|S|-2
vertices from the ground set. So, using the fact that
k\sim cn^{1/3}
, we see that the number of extensions that are not almost simple is at most

rk^{2}2^{r^{2}}k^{r-e_{r}(S)-1}\binom{n}{k-(r-e_{r}(S)+|S|+1)}\leq k^{r-e_{r}(S)+1+o(1)}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{r-e_{r}(S)+|S|+1}\binom{n}{k}\\
=\frac{1}{k^{r+1-(e_{r}(S)-2|S|)+o(1)}}\binom{n}{k}

This is therefore at most what we claimed since
e_{r}(S)-2|S|\leq\chi^{*}_{r}
.

For the other type of not almost simple extensions we have the upper bound of

rkr^{2}2^{r^{2}}k^{r-e_{r}(S)}\binom{n}{k-(r-e_{r}(S)+|S|+1)}\leq\frac{1}{k^{r+1-(e_{r}(S)-2|S|)+o(1)}}\binom{n}{k}

So again using
e_{r}(S)-2|S|\leq\chi^{*}_{r}
we arrive at the same conclusion as in the first case.

Next we turn our attention to almost simple extensions for which the chosen set
S
has
\chi_{r}(S)\leq\chi^{*}_{r}-1
. Let
\nu(S)
for
S
a collection of vertices of degree at least two in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
be the number of almost simple extensions which contain
S
and otherwise meet each edge of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
at a vertex of degree one.
To build such an extension we choose a vertex of degree one from the
r-e_{r}(S)
edges not covered by
S
. For each such edge
E
there are between
k-r^{2}
and
k
choices. This leaves
k-(r-e_{r}(S))-|S|
vertices of
E
to be chosen from
[n]\setminus V({\mathcal{H}}_{r})
.
Thus,

\left(k-r^{2}\right)^{r-e_{r}(S)}\binom{n-kr}{k-(r-e_{r}(S))-|S|}\leq\nu(S)\leq k^{r-e_{r}(S)}\binom{n}{k-(r-e_{r}(S))-|S|}.

(1)

Thus, since
r=o(k^{1/2})
,

\nu(S)=e^{O(r^{2}/k)}\frac{k^{2r-2e_{r}(S)+|S|}}{n^{r-e_{r}(S)+|S|}}\binom{n}{k}=e^{O(r^{2}/k)}\frac{k^{2r-2e_{r}(S)+|S|}}{(c^{-1}k)^{3(r-e_{r}(S)+|S|)}}\binom{n}{k}\\
=e^{O(r^{2}/k)}\frac{c^{3(r-e_{r}(S)+|S|)}}{k^{r-e_{r}(S)+2|S|}}\binom{n}{k}\sim\frac{c^{3(r-e_{r}(S)+|S|)}}{k^{r-e_{r}(S)+2|S|}}\binom{n}{k}.

(2)

Thus the number of almost simple extensions with
\chi_{r}(S)<\chi^{*}_{r}-1
is at most

k^{-(r-e_{r}(S)+2|S|+o(1))}\binom{n}{k}\leq k^{-(r+1+\chi_{r}^{*}+o(1))}\binom{n}{k}

The total number of extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is at least the number of good extensions and by our estimate on
\nu(S)
when
\chi_{r}(S)=\chi_{r}^{*}
we have the number of good extensions is at least
k^{-(r+\chi_{r}^{*}+o(1)))}\binom{n}{k}
and the claim follows.
∎

We see that Lemma
5
follows from Lemma
7
.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

If
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is simple with no vertices of degree at least 3 then
\mathcal{H}_{r}
has been generated by good extensions with
\mathcal{S}_{r}
as the collection of independent sets of vertices of degree two. If
\mathcal{H}_{r}
has no vertices of degree at least 3 and is simple then the only good extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
are simple extensions, thus the probability of a non-simple extension is at most
1/k^{1-o(1)}
.
∎

Lemma
7
implies that w.h.p., step
r_{0}
coincides exactly with the first time we take
S\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
with
S\neq\emptyset
and take a good extension with
S
as the set of vertices in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
of degree at least two. It follows from (

) that

is selected at each step for the next good extension with probability proportional
(c^{-3})^{|S|}
. So, if

has maximum degree two and

\mathbb{P}(r_{0}=r+1\mid r_{0}>r)=\frac{e^{O(r^{2}/k)}\sum_{s\geq 1}\binom{\sigma}{s}c^{-3s}}{\sum_{s\geq 0}\binom{\sigma}{s}c^{-3s}}=e^{O(r^{2}/k)}\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+c^{-3})^{\sigma}}\right).

(3)

and so for

\mathbb{P}(r_{0}>r)\leq\prod_{\rho=1}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{(1+c^{-3})^{\binom{\rho}{2}}}+O\left(\frac{r^{2}}{k}\right)\right)<\frac{1+O(r^{3}/k)}{(1+c^{-3})^{r(r+1)/2}}.

Clearly a maximal intesecting family has size at least
k
. Thus w.h.p.
r_{0}\leq\omega=\omega(n)
for any function
\omega\to\infty,\omega=o(k)
.

Note that in general
\chi_{r+1}(T)\leq\chi_{r}(T)+1
for
T\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
. Now for any
T\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
so that
T\cap S=\emptyset
, with
S
selected for the good extension to
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
we observe that
\chi_{r+1}(T)=\chi_{r}(T)
while for any
T\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
such that
T\cap S\neq\emptyset
,
\chi_{r+1}(T)=\chi_{r}(T)+1
. Thus if
T\cap S=\emptyset
then
T\notin\mathcal{S}_{r+1}
while if
T\cap S\neq\emptyset
then
T
remains in
\mathcal{S}_{r+1}
.

Suppose now that
{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
is not intersecting and that
T\in{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
. Then if

then
T\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{r+1}
. So,

\mathbb{P}(T\in{\mathcal{S}}_{r+1})\leq 1-\frac{e^{O(r^{2}/k)}c^{-3|T|}}{\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}_{r}}c^{-3|S|}}

and so, assuming

,

\mathbb{P}(T\in{\mathcal{S}}_{r_{0}+\rho})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\rho}\left(1-\frac{e^{O((r_{0}+i)^{2}/k)}c^{-3|T|}}{\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}_{r_{0}}}c^{-3|S|}}\right)^{i}\leq\exp\left\{-\rho\frac{e^{O((r_{0}+\rho)^{2}/k)}c^{-3\binom{r_{0}}{2}}}{\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}_{r_{0}}}c^{-3|S|}}\right\}

This implies that if

then

w.h.p. So, without further remark we have that with high probability

is smaller than any function of
n
tending to infinity. This verifies part (a) of Theorem
2
.

Step

to step

We’ve shown that with high probability in the first

. We now want to show that conditioned on knowing the final, stable family
\mathcal{S}
, and the two hitting times

and

and

, the probability that we add

We first lower bound

. Let

2|S|
of the edges

(4)

Of course some (at most
r

are only open

Now we upper bound the number of open edges

at some step

2.3
From step

onward

In this section we handle the remainder of the process. We will show that for

)

, which together with results in the previous subsection implies that

, and that in turn implies that
{\cal I}^{*}\subseteq{\cal I}_{k}
. Let

. For
E\in\binom{[n]}{k}
and knowing
r_{0}
,
r_{1}
, and
{\mathcal{S}}
, let

{\mathcal{D}}(E)=\{F\in{\cal I}^{*}\mid F\cap E=\emptyset\}

Suppose

, so that

is disjoint with some set

. We lower bound
|{\mathcal{D}}(E^{\prime})|
. Quite crudely, there is at least one choice for some set of vertices

that is disjoint with

and such that

, and from here we can pick from each edge

not intersecting

and

. So we count edges

to be at least

\binom{n-k-2r_{0}}{k-2r_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{k^{4r_{0}+o(1)}}\binom{n}{k}

If we pick a set from

for the total number of extensions.
Thus the probability that

is at most

(1-k^{-(3r_{0}+1+o(1))})^{n^{L}}\leq\exp\left\{-k^{3L-3r_{0}+1+o(1)}\right\}.

Thus, the expected number of open edges

at step

is at most

\binom{n}{k}\exp\left\{-k^{3L-3r_{0}+1+o(1)}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{3k\log k-k^{3L-(3r_{0}+1+o(1))}\right\}=o(1),

.

2.4
The size of
\mathcal{I}^{*}
and
\mathcal{I}^{**}

Theorem
2
tells us that our final intersecting family
\mathcal{I}_{k}
satisfies

\mathcal{I}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{**}.

While in general we do not have a complete description of
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, we do have enough to conclude that following

Lemma 8
.

Conditional on the process having reached step
r_{1}
with stable family
{\mathcal{S}}_{r_{1}}={\mathcal{S}}
, we have w.h.p.

\frac{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}{\binom{n}{k}}=(1+o(1))\frac{\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}c^{3(r_{0}-1-|S|)}}{k^{r_{0}-1}}\qquad\mbox{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

Proof.

It suffices to find a lower bound on
\mathcal{I}^{*}
and an upper bound on
\mathcal{I}^{**}
. Both approximations are based largely on the fact that in the growing regime every
S\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
,
r\leq r_{0}-1
has
\chi_{r}(S)=0
and in the diminishing regime and stable regime every
S\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
,
r_{0}\leq r
has
\chi_{r}(S)=r-r_{0}+1
. Thus
\chi_{r_{1}}(S)=r_{1}-r_{0}+1
. We now bound
|\mathcal{I}^{**}|
. For any set
T
of vertices of degree at least two in
\mathcal{H}_{r_{1}}
we have
\chi_{r_{1}}(T)\leq r_{1}-r_{0}+1
with
\chi_{r_{1}}(T)=r_{1}-r_{0}+1
if and only if
T\in\mathcal{S}
. For each choice of
T
the number of hyperedges of
\mathcal{I}^{**}
so that
T
is the set of vertices of degree at least two in
\mathcal{H}_{r_{1}}
belonging to the hyperedge is at most

k^{r_{1}-e(T)}\binom{n}{k-(|T|+r_{1}-e_{r_{1}}(T))}.

We sum this over the at most
2^{r^{2}}
choices for
T
, but the largest order terms come from
T\in\mathcal{S}
, so we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}^{**}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{c^{3(r_{1}-(e_{r_{1}}(S)-|S|))}}{k^{r_{1}-(e_{r_{1}}(S)-2|S|))}}\binom{n}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{c^{3(r_{0}-1-|S|))}}{k^{r_{0}-1}}\binom{n}{k},

after using
\chi_{r_{1}}(S)=r_{1}-r_{0}+1
.

On the other hand we have that for any fixed
S\in\mathcal{S}
the number of sets that contain

and meet all other sets in
\mathcal{H}_{r_{1}}
in a vertex of degree one is at least

(k-(r_{1})^{2})^{r_{1}-e(S)}\binom{n-kr_{1}}{k-(|S|+r_{1}-e_{r_{1}}(S))}=(1-o(1))\frac{c^{r_{0}-1-|S|}}{k^{r_{0}-1}}\binom{n}{k}.

We can sum this over all choices of

to arrive at the conclusion.
∎

This completes the proof of part (c) of Theorem
2
.

We finally consider part (d). The distribution of
r_{0}
can be recovered from the following generating function.

Theorem 9
.

Consider the random intersecting process with
k={\left\lfloor cn^{1/3}\right\rfloor}
where
c>0
is a constant
and let
X
be the random variable counting the number of steps until a vertex of degree three appears, then for
n\rightarrow\infty
the following sequence

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\{\frac{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X=r+1\mid X>r)}{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X>r+1\mid X>r)}\right\}_{r\geq 0}}

has exponential generating function

e^{x}(e^{x^{2}/(2c^{3})}-1).

Proof.

By Lemma
5
we have that if
r=o(\sqrt{\log n})

then w.h.p. either
\mathcal{H}_{r}
has no vertices of degree at least three or it is a simple hypergraph
. We first verify

Lemma 10
.

\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1\mid X>r)}{\mathbb{P}(X>r+1\mid X>r)}

(6)

is asymptotically equal to

\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1\mid X>r,SIMPLE(r))}{\mathbb{P}(X>r+1\mid X>r,SIMPLE(r))}.

(7)

Proof.

The ratio of the expressions in (
6
), (
7
) is

\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r,SIMPLE(r))}{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r)}\times\frac{\mathbb{P}(X>r+1)}{\mathbb{P}(X>r+1,SIMPLE(r))}.

(8)

Now we bound the probability
p_{r}
that
X>r
holds but that
SIMPLE(r)
fails to hold. Clearly

p_{1}=\mathbb{P}(X>1,\neg SIMPLE(1))\leq\mathbb{P}(\neg SIMPLE(1))=0.

For
1<r=o(\sqrt{\log n})
we have

\displaystyle p_{r}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>r,\neg SIMPLE(r))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>r,\neg SIMPLE(r),SIMPLE(r-1))+\mathbb{P}(X>r,\neg SIMPLE(r-1))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\neg SIMPLE(r)\mid X>r-1,SIMPLE(r-1))+\mathbb{P}(X>r-1,\neg SIMPLE(r-1))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k^{1-o(1)}}+p_{r-1},

after using Lemma
5
to bound the first summand. Thus
p_{r}=O(\sqrt{\log n}/k^{1-o(1)})=O(1/k^{1-o(1)})
.

Going back to (
8
),

1-\frac{\mathbb{P}(X>r,\neg SIMPLE(r))}{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r)}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r,SIMPLE(r))}{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r)}\leq 1

It follows then that

\mathbb{P}(X=r+1)\geq\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,SIMPLE(r)).

We show later in the proof of Theorem
9
that

\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,SIMPLE(r))=\zeta_{r}

for some
\zeta_{r}>0
. So with our estimate on
p_{r}
we have that

\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r,SIMPLE(r))}{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1,X>r)}=1-O(k^{-(1-o(1))}).

A similar argument deals with the second product in (
8
).
∎

If
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is a simple
k
-uniform intersecting hypergraph with maximum degree two then
each pair of edges of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
intersect in a unique vertex of degree two
. In this case
{\mathcal{H}}_{r+1}
will have vertices of degree three if and only if we select a nonempty set
S
of the vertices of degree two of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
to belong to the new edge. The event that

is not an independent set of vertices of degree two is negligible as is the event that the extension of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
to
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
is not almost simple by Lemma

. Thus the number of almost simple extensions that do not add a vertex of degree three is

(1+o(1))k^{r}\binom{n}{k-r}\sim k^{r}\left(\frac{c^{3}}{k^{2}}\right)^{r}\binom{n}{k}.

(9)

On the other hand to count the number of almost simple extensions that do add a vertex of degree three we have a choice of how many vertices of degree three we add. To add
m
vertices of degree three we must (with high probability) select an independent set of
m
vertices of degree two in
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Applying Lemma
6
we see that the number of almost simple extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{r}
that create vertices of degree three is

(1+o(1))\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\frac{r!}{(r-2m)!m!2^{m}}k^{r-2m}\binom{n}{k-(r-2m)-m}\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\frac{r!}{c^{3m}(r-2m)!m!2^{m}}k^{r}\left(\frac{c^{3}}{k^{2}}\right)^{r}\binom{n}{k}.

(10)

Thus from (

) and (

), we have for each

,

\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=r+1\mid X>r)}{\mathbb{P}(X>r+1\mid X>r)}\sim\sum_{m=1}^{{\left\lfloor r/2\right\rfloor}}\frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-2m+1)}{m!(2c^{3})^{m}}\sim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-2m+1)}{m!(2c^{3})^{m}}

as

.

Now we just verify the exponential generating function is what we claimed:

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-2m+1)}{r!m!(2c^{3})^{m}}x^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r=2m}^{\infty}\frac{x^{r}}{(r-2m)!m!(2c^{3})^{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x^{2}/(2c^{3}))^{m}}{m!}\sum_{r=2m}^{\infty}\frac{x^{r-2m}}{(r-2m)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x^{2}/(2c^{3}))^{m}}{m!}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s}}{s!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\exp\left\{x^{2}/(2c^{3})\right\}-1\right)e^{x}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4
when

In this section we assume
k=cn
where
c=c(n)\geq\zeta
for some constant
\zeta>0
.
Let
G=K(n,k)
be the Kneser graph. Recall that
G
has vertex set
\left(v_{S}:S\in\binom{[n]}{k}\right)
, and
v_{S}
is adjacent to
v_{S}^{\prime}
when
S\cap S^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

Let
g(x):=x\log(x)
for
x>0
and let
g(0)=0
. Stirling’s formula gives us that for any constants
a\geq b\geq 0
we have

\binom{an}{bn}=\exp\{[g(a)-g(b)-g(a-b)]n+O(\log n)\}.

(11)

Thus
G
has
N
vertices and is
d
-regular for

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{cn}=e^{p_{N}(c)n+O(\log n)}\qquad\text{ where }p_{N}(c)=-g(c)-g(1-c),

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\binom{n-cn}{cn}=e^{p_{d}(c)n+O(\log n)}\qquad\text{ where }p_{d}(c)=g(1-c)-g(c)-g(1-2c).

We claim that
p_{d}(c)<p_{N}(c)
for all
0<c<1/2
. Indeed,
p_{d}(0)=p_{N}(0)=0
. Now since
g^{\prime}(x)=1+\log x
we have

\frac{d}{dc}(p_{N}(c)-p_{d}(c))=\frac{d}{dc}(-2g(1-c)+g(1-2c))=2\log(1-c)-2\log(1-2c)>0.

Thus,

N^{\varepsilon_{1}}<d<N^{1-\varepsilon_{1}}

(12)

for some
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}(c)>0
.

Now we claim that

each vertex has codegree at most
dN^{-\varepsilon_{2}}
with all but at most
dN^{-\varepsilon_{2}}
vertices, for some
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{2}(c)
.

(13)

Indeed, suppose
|S\cap S^{\prime}|\leq(c-\delta)n
for some
\delta>0
. Then
|S\cup S^{\prime}|\geq(c+\delta)n
, and so the number of sets
S^{\prime\prime}
that are disjoint with both
S
and
S^{\prime}
(i.e. the codegree of
v_{S},v_{S^{\prime}}
) is at most

\binom{n-(c+\delta)n}{cn}=e^{p_{1}(c,\delta)n+O(\log n)}\qquad\text{ where }p_{1}(c,\delta)=g(1-c-\delta)-g(c)-g(1-2c-\delta)]n.

(14)

We claim that
p_{1}(c,\delta)<p_{d}(c)
for some
\delta=\delta(c)>0
. Indeed, we have
p_{1}(c,0)=p_{d}(c)
and

\frac{\partial p_{1}}{\partial\delta}(c,0)=-\log(1-c)+\log(1-2c)<0.

Thus the codegree bound on line (
14
) is at most
dN^{-\varepsilon_{2}}
for some
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{2}(c)
.

Now for a fixed set
S
, the number of
S^{\prime}
such that
|S\cap S^{\prime}|>(c-\delta)n
is at most

\binom{cn}{(c-\delta)n}\binom{n}{\delta n}=e^{p_{2}(c,\delta)n+O(\log n)}\qquad\text{ where }p_{2}(c,\delta)=g(c)-g(c-\delta)-2g(\delta)-g(1-\delta).

We claim that for sufficiently small
\delta
we have
p_{2}(c,\delta)<p_{d}(c)
. This follows from the fact that
p_{d}(c)>0
,
p_{2}(c,0)=0
and
p_{2}
is continuous in
\delta
. This verifies (
13
).

We let
\varepsilon=\min\left\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\right\}
for the remainder of Section
3
. Both of (
12
) and (
13
) remain true with
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
replaced by
\varepsilon
respectively.

3.1
The good event

Let
G
be any
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices where
N^{\varepsilon}<d<N^{1-\varepsilon}
. Assume that for each vertex
v
, there are at most
dN^{-\varepsilon}
vertices
u
whose codegree with
v
is at least
dN^{-\varepsilon}
. We run the random greedy independent set process on
G
: initially
I(0)=\emptyset
. After
r
steps, let
I(r)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
be the set of vertices in the independent set constructed so far. Let
V(r)
be the set of available vertices (i.e. the vertices not adjacent to any vertex in
I(r)
). We choose a random vertex
v\in V(r)
and put
v_{r+1}=v
.

Let
D_{v}(r)
be the number of available neighbors of
v
, for
v\in V(r)
.

Let
B(r)
be the set of vertices
w\in V(r)
such that some vertex
u\in I(r)
has codegree with
w
at least
dN^{-\varepsilon}
.

Let
C_{v}(r):=D_{v}(r)\cap B(r)
.

Let

t=t(r)=\frac{dr}{N}.

Define the error function

f(t):=N^{-\varepsilon/20}e^{10t}.

(15)

Let the
good event

{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
be the event that the following inequalities (
16
), (
17
) and (
18
) all hold for all
r^{\prime}\leq r
and where
t^{\prime}=t(r^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle\left||V(r^{\prime})|-Ne^{-t^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq Nf(t^{\prime})

(16)

\displaystyle\left||D_{v}(r^{\prime})|-de^{-t^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq df(t^{\prime})\mbox{ for all $v\in V(r^{\prime})\setminus B(r^{\prime})$}

(17)

\displaystyle|C_{v}(r^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq dN^{-\varepsilon/10}\mbox{ for all $v\in V(r^{\prime})$}.

(18)

We will show that w.h.p. the good event
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
holds where

r_{end}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{1000}\frac{N\log N}{d}.

In particular this will imply that w.h.p. the process lasts to at least step
r_{end}
, proving the lower bound on
|{\cal I}_{k}|
in Theorem
4
for the case of
k=cn
. Indeed,

e^{10t}\leq N^{\varepsilon/1000}\text{ implying that }f(t)\leq N^{-\varepsilon/25}=o(1)

for all
r\leq r_{end}
, inequalities (
16
) and (
17
) imply that for all
r\leq r_{end}
we have

|V(r)|=(1+o(1))Ne^{-t},\qquad|D_{v}(r)|=(1+o(1))de^{-t}\mbox{ for all $v\in V(r)\setminus B(r).$}

In particular
|V(r_{end})|
is positive and so the process lasts to step
r_{end}
. The upper bound in Theorem
4
comes from the upper bound on
|V(r_{end})|
implied by (
16
).

We will sometimes use the bounds (which hold for all
r\leq r_{end}
in the good event
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
)

|V(r)|\geq{N^{1-\varepsilon/10}},\qquad|B(r)|\leq N^{1-\varepsilon/2}.

(19)

The first bound follows from our estimate of
V(r_{end})
. The second bound follows since
|B(r_{end})|\leq r_{end}dN^{-\varepsilon}
.

3.2
Dynamic concentration of
V(r)

Here we bound the probability that
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
fails due to condition (
16
). We define variables
V^{+}
and
V^{-}
as follows.

V^{\pm}=V^{\pm}(r):=\begin{cases}|V(r)|-N(e^{-t}\pm f(t))&\text{if ${\mathcal{E}}_{i-1}$ holds},\\
V^{\pm}(r-1)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(20)

Note that if
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
fails due to condition (
16
) then we either have that
V^{+}(r_{end})>0
or
V^{-}(r_{end})<0
. To show that those events are unlikely we will establish
V^{+}
is a supermartingale, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V^{+}(r)|\;|I(r)]\leq 0
. Similarly we will show that
V^{-}
is a submartingale.

First we show
\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V^{+}(r)|\;|I(r)]\leq 0
. If
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
fails then
\Delta V^{+}(r)=0
by definition, so we assume
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
holds. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|V(r)|}\sum_{v\in V(r)}(1+D_{v}(r))

(21)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{|V(r)|}\left(|V(r)|-|B(r)|\right)\left(de^{-t}-df(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\left(1-\frac{N^{1-\varepsilon/2}}{N^{1-\varepsilon/10}}\right)\left(de^{-t}-df(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-d\left(e^{-t}-f(t)\right)+O\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/3}\right).

We turn to bounding the one-step change in
N(e^{-t}+f(t))
(the deterministic part of
V^{+}
). We use Taylor’s thorem:

Theorem 12
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function twice differentiable on the closed interval
[a,b]
. Then, there exists a number
\tau
between
a
and
b
such that

g(b)-g(a)=g^{\prime}(a)(b-a)+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{2}(b-a)^{2}.

(22)

Since
\Delta t(r)=\frac{d}{N}
, we have for some
\tau\in(t(r),t(r+1))
that

\displaystyle\Delta N(e^{-t}\pm f(t))

\displaystyle=N(-e^{-t}\pm f^{\prime}(t))\cdot\frac{d}{N}+\frac{1}{2}N(e^{-\tau}\pm f^{\prime\prime}(\tau))\cdot\frac{d^{2}}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle=d(-e^{-t}\pm f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(\frac{d^{2}}{N}\right).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta V^{+}(r)\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle\leq-d\left(e^{-t}-f(t)\right)-d(-e^{-t}+f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/3}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=d(f(t)-f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/3}\right)

\displaystyle=-\Omega\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/20}\right).

Thus
V^{+}
is a supermartingale. Now we will show that
V^{-}
is a submartingale. From (
21
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|V(r)|}\sum_{v\in V(r)}(1+D_{v}(r))

(24)

\displaystyle\geq-1-\frac{1}{|V(r)|}\Bigg[\left(|V(r)|-|B(r)|\right)\left(de^{-t}+df(t)\right)+|B(r)|d\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq-\left(de^{-t}+df(t)\right)-\frac{N^{1-\varepsilon/2}}{N^{1-\varepsilon/10}}d-1

\displaystyle\geq-\left(de^{-t}+df(t)\right)+O\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/3}\right).

Thus, similar to (
23
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta V^{-}(r)\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle\geq-d\left(e^{-t}+f(t)\right)-d(-e^{-t}-f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/3}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(dN^{-\varepsilon/20}\right).

We use the following concentration inequality.

Theorem 13

(Freedman)
.

Suppose
Y_{0},Y_{1},\dots
is a supermartingale such that
\Delta Y_{j}\leq C
for all
j
, and let
W_{m}=\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq m}{\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta Y_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]
. Then, for all positive reals
\lambda
,

\mathbb{P}(\exists m:W_{m}\leq b\text{ and }Y_{m}-Y_{0}\geq\lambda)\leq\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(b+C\lambda)}\right).

(26)

We apply Freedman’s theorem to the supermartingale
V^{+}
. In particular we want to bound the probability that it is positive at step
r_{end}
. We have at step
0
that
V^{+}(0)=-Nf(0)=-N^{1-\varepsilon/20}
. If
V^{+}(r_{end})
is positive then
V^{+}(r_{end})-V^{+}(0)>N^{1-\varepsilon/20}
. Thus we will use
\lambda=N^{1-\varepsilon/20}
. We bound the one-step change to determine
C
. We have
|\Delta|V(r)||\leq d
and
|\Delta N(e^{-t}+f(t))|\leq 2d
so we can use
C=3d
. Note that we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta V^{\pm}(r)|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta|V(r)|\;|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V(r)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]\leq d^{2}.

Thus we have
W_{r_{end}}\leq r_{end}\cdot d^{2}=O(Nd\log N)
and we can take
b=O(Nd\log N)
. Freedman’s theorem gives us
that the probability that
V^{+}(r_{end})
is positive is at most

\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(b+C\lambda)}\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{N^{2-\varepsilon/10}}{Nd\log N+dN^{1-\varepsilon/20}}\right)\right)=o(1).

Similarly one can apply Freedman’s theorem to the submartingale
-V^{-}
using the same values of
\lambda,C,b
to show that w.h.p.
-V^{-}(r_{end})
is not positive. Thus we have w.h.p. that
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
does not fail due to condition (
16
).

3.3
Dynamic concentration of
D_{v}(r)

Here we bound the probability that
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
fails due to condition (
17
). We define variables
D_{v}^{+}
and
D_{v}^{-}
as follows.

D_{v}^{\pm}=D_{v}^{\pm}(r):=\begin{cases}|D_{v}(r)|-d(e^{-t}\pm f(t))&\text{if ${\mathcal{E}}_{i-1}$ holds and $v\notin B(r)$},\\
D_{v}^{\pm}(r-1)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(27)

If
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
fails due to condition (
17
) then we either have that
D_{v}^{+}(r_{end})>0
or
D_{v}^{-}(r_{end})<0
for some
v
. Similarly to the last subsection, we will show these events are unlikely using Freedman’s theorem. First we verify that
D_{v}^{+}
is a supermartingale. As before we can assume
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
holds. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta|D_{v}(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{u\in D_{v}(r)}\mathbb{P}(\mbox{$u\notin V(r+1)$ and $v\notin B(r+1)$})

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{u\in D_{v}(r)}\frac{D_{u}(r)-dN^{-\varepsilon}}{|V(r)|}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{(de^{-t}-df(t)-dN^{-\varepsilon/10})^{2}}{Ne^{-t}+Nf(t)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d^{2}}{N}e^{-t}\cdot\frac{(1-e^{t}f(t)-e^{t}N^{-\varepsilon/10})^{2}}{1+e^{t}f(t)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d^{2}}{N}e^{-t}\cdot\left(1-3e^{t}f(t)\right)+O\left(d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/15}\right)

Meanwhile the one-step change in
d(e^{-t}+f(t))
is

\frac{d^{2}}{N}(-e^{-t}+f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(d^{3}N^{-2}\right)

by Taylor’s theorem. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta D_{v}^{+}(r)\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{d^{2}}{N}e^{-t}\cdot\left(1-3e^{t}f(t)\right)-\frac{d^{2}}{N}(-e^{-t}+f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/15}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{N}(3f(t)-f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/15}\right)

\displaystyle=-\Omega\left(d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/20}\right)

Thus
D_{v}^{+}
is a supermartingale, and similarly
D_{v}^{-}
is a submartingale. Now we apply Freedman’s theorem.
As in the last section, bounding the probability that
-D_{v}^{-}(r_{end})>0
is entirely similar to bounding the probability that
-D_{v}^{+}(r_{end})>0
, so from here we will only show the work for the latter.
We have

\displaystyle|\Delta D_{v}^{+}(r)|

\displaystyle\leq|\Delta D_{v}(r)|+|\Delta de^{-t}|+|\Delta df(t)|

\displaystyle\leq dN^{-\varepsilon}+O(d^{2}/N)

\displaystyle\leq 2dN^{-\varepsilon}.

Thus we can use
C=2dN^{-\varepsilon}
.
If
D_{v}^{+}(r_{end})>0
then
D_{v}^{+}(r_{end})-D_{v}^{+}(0)>df(0)=dN^{-\varepsilon/20}

and so we use
\lambda=dN^{-\varepsilon/20}
. Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta D_{v}^{+}(r)|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]={\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta|D_{v}(r)|\;|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[(\Delta|D_{v}(r)|)^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\;|\Delta D_{v}^{+}(r)|\;|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\;|\Delta|D_{v}(r)|\;|\;|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]+C|\Delta de^{-t}|+C|\Delta df(t)|

\displaystyle=O(d^{3}N^{-1-\varepsilon}).

Thus we can take
b=O(r_{end}\cdot d^{3}N^{-1-\varepsilon})=O(d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon/2})
. Freedman’s theorem gives us a failure probability of at most

\exp\left\{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(b+C\lambda)}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\frac{d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon/10}}{d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon/2}+dN^{-\varepsilon}\cdot dN^{-\varepsilon/20}}\right)\right\}=o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

This probability is small enough to beat a union bound over
N
choices of
v
. Thus we have w.h.p. that
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
does not fail due to condition (
17
).

3.4
The upper bound on
C_{v}

We now bound the probability that
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
fails due to condition (
18
). We define variables
C_{v}^{+}
as follows.

C_{v}^{+}=C_{v}^{+}(r):=\begin{cases}|C_{v}(r)|-d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/2}i&\text{if ${\mathcal{E}}_{i-1}$ holds},\\
C_{v}^{+}(r-1)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(28)

We show that
C_{v}^{+}
is a supermartingale. We have

\mathbb{E}[\Delta|C_{v}(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]\leq\frac{d\cdot dN^{-\varepsilon}}{|V(r)|}\leq\frac{d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon}}{N^{1-\varepsilon/10}}\leq d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/2}

and so
\mathbb{E}[\Delta|C_{v}^{+}(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]\leq d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/2}-d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/2}=0
. We apply Freedman’s theorem. We have
\Delta C_{v}^{+}(r)\leq\Delta C_{v}(r)\leq dN^{-\varepsilon}
and so we use
C=dN^{-\varepsilon}
. Note that we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta C_{v}^{+}(r)|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]={\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta C_{v}(r)|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\Delta C_{v}(r)^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq dN^{-\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[|\Delta C_{v}(r)||\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leq dN^{-\varepsilon}\cdot d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/2}

\displaystyle\leq d^{3}N^{-1-3\varepsilon/2}

Thus we have
W_{r_{end}}\leq r_{end}\cdot d^{3}N^{-1-3\varepsilon/2}=O(d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon})
and we can take
b=O(d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon})
. Using
\lambda=dN^{-\varepsilon/4}
, Freedman’s theorem gives us that the probability we ever have
C_{v}^{+}(r)>\lambda
is at most

\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(b+C\lambda)}\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon/2}}{d^{2}N^{-\varepsilon}+dN^{-\varepsilon}\cdot dN^{-\varepsilon/4}}\right)\right)=o(1/N),

which is small enough to beat the union bound over the
N
choices for
v
. Thus w.h.p. we have for each
v
that
C_{v}^{+}(r)\leq\lambda
and so

C_{v}(r)\leq d^{2}N^{-1-\varepsilon/2}r_{end}+\lambda<dN^{-\varepsilon/10}.

Thus w.h.p.
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
does not fail due to condition (
18
). This completes our proof of Theorem
4
for the case when
k=\Theta(n)
.

4
Proof of Theorem
4
when
k=o(n)

In this section we assume
\zeta^{-1}n^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n\leq k\leq\zeta n
for a small constant
\zeta>0
. We use the same notation as in Section
3
, so
k=cn
.

For say
|x|\leq 1/2
, we have
g(1-x)=-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}+O(x^{3})
and so we can write

\displaystyle p_{N}(c)

\displaystyle=-g(c)+c-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}+O(c^{3}),

\displaystyle p_{d}(c)

\displaystyle=-g(c)+c-\frac{3}{2}c^{2}+O(c^{3}).

\displaystyle p_{N}(c)-p_{d}(c)

\displaystyle=c^{2}+O(c^{3}).

Thus, we have that

d=Ne^{-(c^{2}+O(c^{3}))n}.

With
p_{1},p_{2}
as in Section
3
, we now have

\displaystyle p_{1}(c,\delta)-p_{d}(c)

\displaystyle=-c\delta+O(c^{3}).

\displaystyle p_{2}(c,\delta)

\displaystyle=\delta\log\left(\frac{c}{\delta^{2}}\right)+2\delta-\frac{\delta^{2}}{c}+O(\delta^{2}).

Let
\delta=0.9c
, and

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle:=\frac{d}{N}=\exp\{-c^{2}n+O(c^{3}n)\}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle:=d\exp\left\{-c\delta n+O(c^{3}n)\right\}=d\exp\{-0.9c^{2}n+O(c^{3}n)\}

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle:=\exp\left\{\left(\delta\log\left(\frac{c}{\delta^{2}}\right)+2\delta-\frac{\delta^{2}}{c}\right)n+O(\delta^{2}n)\right\}=\exp\left\{-0.9(c\log c)n+O(cn)\right\}

Arguing as in Section
3
, we see that for each vertex
v
, there are at most
\alpha
vertices
u
whose codegree with
v
is at least
\beta
.

We assume that

\gamma\leq\log^{-100}N

which holds when
c
is at least some large constant times
n^{-1/2}\log^{1/2}n
(i.e. for
\zeta
small enough).

4.1
The good event

Let
t=t(r)=\gamma r.
Define the error function

f(t):=\gamma^{0.1}e^{10t}.

(29)

Note that this is different from our error function
f(t)
from Section
3.1
. Let the
good event

{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
be the event that the following inequalities (
30
), (
31
) and (
32
) all hold for all
r^{\prime}\leq r
:

\displaystyle\left||V(r^{\prime})|-Ne^{-t^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq Nf(t^{\prime})

(30)

\displaystyle\left||D_{v}(r^{\prime})|-de^{-t^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq df(t^{\prime})\mbox{ for all $v\in V(r^{\prime})\setminus B(r^{\prime})$}

(31)

\displaystyle|C_{v}(r^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{-0.1}\alpha\mbox{ for all $v\in V(r^{\prime})$}.

(32)

We will show that w.h.p. the good event
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}

In particular this will imply that w.h.p. the process lasts to at least step

, proving the lower bound in Theorem

for the case

. Indeed, we have
e^{10t}\leq\gamma^{-0.01}
and so

and

for all

. Now lines (

) and (

) imply that for all

|V(r)|=(1+o(1))Ne^{-t},\qquad|D_{v}(r)|=(1+o(1))de^{-t}\mbox{ for all $v\in V(r)\setminus B(r).$}

In particular
|V(r_{end})|
is positive and so the process lasts to step
r_{end}
. The upper bound in Theorem
4
comes from the bound on
|V(r_{end})|
implied by (

).

We will sometimes use the bounds (which hold for all

in the good event
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
)

|V(r)|\geq\frac{1}{2}Ne^{-\gamma r_{end}}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{0.001}N,\qquad|B(r)|\leq\alpha r_{end}=0.001\alpha\gamma^{-1}\log\gamma^{-1}.

(33)

4.2
Dynamic concentration of
V(r)

Here we bound the probability that
{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}
fails due to condition (
30
). We define variables
V^{+}
and
V^{-}
exactly as in (
20
) (but now
f(t)
is as in (
29
)). We now
establish that
V^{+}
is a supermartingale.

First we show
\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V^{+}(r)|\;|I(r)]\leq 0
. If
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
fails then
\Delta V^{+}(r)=0
by definition, so we assume
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
holds. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|V(r)|}\sum_{v\in V(r)}(1+D_{v}(r))

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{|V(r)|}\left(|V(r)|-|B(r)|\right)\left(de^{-t}-df(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\left(1-\frac{0.001\alpha\gamma^{-1}\log\gamma^{-1}}{\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{0.001}N}\right)\left(de^{-t}-df(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-d\left(e^{-t}-f(t)\right)+O\left(\alpha\gamma^{-0.001}\log\gamma^{-1}\right).

We turn to bounding the one-step change in
N(e^{-t}+f(t))
. Since
\Delta t(r)=\frac{d}{N}
, we have for some
\tau\in(t(r),t(r+1))
that

\displaystyle\Delta N(e^{-t}+f(t))

\displaystyle=N(-e^{-t}+f^{\prime}(t))\cdot\frac{d}{N}+\frac{1}{2}N(e^{-\tau}+f^{\prime\prime}(\tau))\cdot\frac{d^{2}}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle=d(-e^{-t}+f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(d^{2}N^{-1}\right).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta V^{+}(r)\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle\leq-d\left(e^{-t}-f(t)\right)-d(-e^{-t}+f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(\alpha\gamma^{-0.001}\log\gamma^{-1}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=d(f(t)-f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(\alpha\gamma^{-0.001}\log\gamma^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\Omega\left(d\gamma^{0.1}\right),\qquad\text{ since $\alpha/d\leq\gamma^{0.8}$}.

Thus
V^{+}
is a supermartingale. To see that
V^{-}
is a submartingale, we replace (

\mathbb{E}[\Delta V^{-}(r)\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]\geq-d\left(e^{-t}-f(t)\right)-d(-e^{-t}-f^{\prime}(t))+O\left(\alpha\gamma^{-0.001}\log\gamma^{-1}\right)=\Omega\left(d\gamma^{0.1}\right).

We apply Freedman’s theorem to the supermartingales
V^{+}
and
-V^{-}
to show w.h.p. neither of them becomes positive. The calculations for
-V^{-}
is entirely similar to
V^{+}
so we only show the latter. If
V^{+}(r_{end})>0
then
V^{+}(r_{end})-V^{+}(0)>Nf(0)=N\gamma^{0.1}
. Thus we use
\lambda=N\gamma^{0.1}
.
We bound the one-step change to determine

. We have
|\Delta|V(r)||\leq d
and
|\Delta N(e^{-t}+f(t))|\leq 2d
so we can use

. Note that we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta V^{+}(r)|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{V}}ar[\Delta|V(r)|\;|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\Delta|V(r)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k},{\mathcal{E}}_{k}]\leq d^{2}.

Thus we have

and we can take

. Freedman’s theorem gives us a failure probability of at most

\exp\left\{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(b+C\lambda)}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\Omega\left\{\frac{N^{2}\gamma^{0.2}}{d^{2}\gamma^{-1}\log\gamma^{-1}+Nd\gamma^{0.1}}\right\}\right\}=o(1).

{\mathcal{E}}_{r_{end}}

30

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta|D_{v}(r)|\;|I(r),{\mathcal{E}}_{r}]

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{u\in D_{v}(r)}\mathbb{P}(\mbox{$u\notin V(r+1)$ and $v\notin B(r+1)$})

\displaystyle|\Delta D_{v}^{\pm}(r)|

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\beta+O(d\gamma)

(36)

(37)

where on the second line we used that

Appendix: Describing

In the proof of Theorem

{\cal I}_{k}
when

. Recall that at the beginning of the random intersecting process, before vertices of degree three appear,

is a simple hypergraph in which every pair of edges intersects in a unique vertex. If we represent the hyperedges as vertices then an independent set of vertices of degree two in

corresponds to a matching in the complete graph

is determined

of matchings on

. For

, and

we can describe how
{\mathcal{S}}
is generated by Procedure
1
below.
For a collection of finite set
\mathcal{N}
,
\text{rand}_{c}(\mathcal{N})
refers to sampling a set
S
from
\mathcal{N}
with probability proportional to

.

Input :

Output :
A random number

\mathcal{M}
of pairwise intersecting matchings of the complete graph
K_{n}

n\leftarrow 1
;

G\leftarrow K_{n}
;

\mathcal{N}\leftarrow\text{ All matchings on $G$}
;

\mathcal{M}\leftarrow\emptyset
;

while

\mathcal{M}=\emptyset

if

M=\emptyset

;

M
to
\mathcal{M}
;

\mathcal{N}\leftarrow\text{ All matchings on $G$ that intersect $M$}

while

M

\mathcal{M}
;

\mathcal{N}\leftarrow\text{ All matchings on $G$ that intersect every matching in $\mathcal{M}$}

Random matching procedure
