Equidistribution and freeness on Grassmannians
Tim Browning, Tal Horesh, Florian Wilsch

Abstract
. We associate a certain tensor product lattice to any primitive integer lattice and ask about its typical shape. These lattices are related to the tangent bundle of Grassmannians and their study is motivated by Peyre’s programme on “freeness” for rational points of bounded height on Fano varieties.

14G05 (11N45, 11P21,
14M15, 20G20)

1.
Introduction

Understanding the density of rational points on smooth Fano varieties lies at the confluence of algebraic geometry, harmonic analysis and analytic number theory. The guiding conjecture is due to Manin
[
12
]
, and its refinement by Peyre
[
15
]
.
Given a smooth Fano variety
V
defined over a number field
k
such that

V(k)
is Zariski dense in
V
, and given an anticanonical height function

H\colon V(k)\to\mathbb{R}
,
it is conjectured that there exists a thin set of rational points
Z\subset V(k)

and an explicit
constant
c_{V,H}>0

such that

(1.1)

\#\{x\in V(k)\setminus Z:H(x)\leqslant B\}\sim c_{V,H}B(\log B)^{\varrho(V)-1},\quad\text{as }B\to\infty,

where
\varrho(V)=\rank\Pic(V)
.
(Here, as proposed by Serre
[
22
, § 3.1]
, a subset
Z\subset V(k)
is said to be
thin
if it is
a finite union of subsets which are either contained in a proper closed subvariety of
V
, or contained
in some
\pi(Y(k))
where
\pi\colon Y\to V
is a generically finite dominant morphism, with
\deg(\pi)>1
and
Y
irreducible.)

Despite major progress by
Lehmann, Sengupta and Tanimoto
[
14
]
on identifying problematic thin sets for Fano varieties, they can be hard to work with in general. An alternative path based on a notion of “freeness” has surfaced in recent work of
Peyre
[
16
]
. It posits the idea that the distribution of “sufficiently free” rational points of bounded height on smooth Fano varieties should conform to the
asymptotic behaviour in (
1.1
), without the need to first identify appropriate thin sets
of rational points. This conjecture has been confirmed for smooth hypersurfaces over
\mathbb{Q}
of low degree by Browning and Sawin
[
6
]
.
However, at the same time it has also been shown by Sawin
[
18
]
that
the proposal fails for the Hilbert scheme
\Hilb^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
, since the thin subset of rational points consisting of points that lift to a certain double cover contains many points with relatively large freeness.
(In fact, as discussed in
[
17
, § 4]
, Peyre has supplemented his freeness proposal with an “all the heights” variant, which explains this example.)

The primary aim of this paper is to study the notion of freeness in the classical setting of
Grassmannian varieties over
\mathbb{Q}
. Not only shall we be able to show that Peyre’s freeness conjecture holds true for Grassmannians, but we shall even be able
to prove a natural equidistribution statement for certain tangent lattices that emerge in the definition of freeness.

For any integers
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
,
there is a natural
\mathbb{Q}
-scheme
\Gr(m,n)
whose
\mathbb{Q}
-rational points
\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
coincide with
m
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
The Grassmannian is a smooth
\mathbb{Q}
-variety of dimension
m(n-m)
, admitting a
Plücker embedding

\iota\colon\Gr(m,n)\to\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\binom{m}{n}-1}.

The Picard group
\Pic(\Gr(m,n))
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
and it is generated by the divisor

\mathscr{L}=\iota^{*}\mathscr{O}(1)
. The anticanonical bundle is
\omega^{-1}=\mathscr{L}^{\otimes n}
. This confirms that
\Gr(m,n)
is Fano and allows one to define an
anticanonical height function
H=H_{\omega^{-1}}
on
\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
.
On setting
\Lambda=V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for a linear subspace
V
,
a rational point
x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
is the same thing as a primitive lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of rank
m
. Then
H(x)=\covol(\Lambda)^{n}
, where

\covol(\Lambda)
is the covolume of the lattice.
Schmidt
[
19
]
has shown that

(1.2)

\#\{x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(x)\leqslant B\}=c_{m,n}B+O\left(B^{1-\max\left\{\frac{1}{mn},\frac{1}{(n-m)n}\right\}}\right),

for any
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
,
where

(1.3)

c_{m,n}=\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{m}\frac{V(n)V(n-1)\dots V(n-m+1)}{V(1)V(2)\dots V(m)}\frac{\zeta(2)\dots\zeta(m)}{\zeta(n)\dots\zeta(n-m+1)}

and

V(N)=\vol\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant 1\}

for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
This agrees with the conjectured asymptotic formula (
1.1
), with
Z=\emptyset
.
Moreover, Peyre
[
15
, § 6]
has shown
that the leading constant
c_{m,n}
agrees with his prediction.
In fact, Franke, Manin and Tschinkel
[
12
]
have a far-reaching generalisation of Schmidt’s result to arbitrary Flag varieties over arbitrary number fields and Peyre has confirmed his conjectured constant for this more general class.

In
[
16
, Déf. 4.11]
,
Peyre defines a freeness function
\ell(x)
on points
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})
,
for any smooth Fano
\mathbb{Q}
-variety.
This function takes values in
[0,1]
and its precise definition will be recalled in (
2.7
).
When
V=\Gr(m,n)
is the Grassmannian it admits a particularly succinct description.
Associated to
x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
is the tensor product lattice

T_{\Lambda}=\Lambda^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi},

where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is the primitive rank
m
lattice associated to
x
,
with
\Lambda^{*}
being the
dual lattice
and

\Lambda^{\pi}=(\Lambda^{\perp})^{*}
the
factor lattice
. (The precise definitions of these will be recalled in Section
2
.) The lattice
T_{\Lambda}

has rank
m(n-m)
and will henceforth be referred to as the
tangent lattice
.
The freeness function
\ell(x)
measures the extent to which
T_{\Lambda}
is lopsided, with a small value of
\ell(x)
corresponding to the existence of an unusually large largest successive minimum.

Suppose for the moment that
m=1
, so that
\Gr(1,n)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n-1}
.
In this case it follows from Peyre
[
16
, Prop. 7.1]
that

(1.4)

\ell(x)\geqslant\frac{n-1}{n}

for rational points
x\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
.
The same bound applies to the case
m=n-1
, by duality.
For the remaining values of
m
, it turns out that the freeness function can become arbitrarily small as one goes over rational points on the Grassmannian
\Gr(m,n)
. In fact we have the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m,n
be integers such that

1<m<n-1
. Then there exist infinitely many
x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
such that
\ell(x)=0
.

Let
1\leqslant k<m
.
Suppose we fix a
k
-dimensional linear subspace
L
of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
Then among the linear subspaces parameterised by
\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
is the set
V_{L}
of those that contain
L
.
Any point in
V_{L}
whose height is large compared to that of
L
will have low freeness. The example constructed to prove Theorem
1.1
is of exactly this sort.
Although all points of low freeness have this kind of special structure, they do not form a thin subset.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m,n
be integers such that
1<m<n-1
.
Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
, the set

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):\ell(x)<\varepsilon\}

of non-
\varepsilon
-free points is not thin.

For a suitable range of
\varepsilon>0
, Peyre suggests focusing on the restricted counting function which only counts

\varepsilon
-free points
, meaning those points
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})
for which
\ell(x)\geqslant\varepsilon
.
For suitable Fano varieties it is expected that such a restriction captures the behaviour articulated in the Manin–Peyre prediction. The following result confirms this for Grassmannians.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any integers

1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
and any
0\leqslant\varepsilon<1
, we have

\#\{x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(x)\leqslant B,~\ell(x)\geqslant\varepsilon\}=c_{m,n}B+O\left(B^{1-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}}(\log B)^{2m-2}\right),

as
B\to\infty
,
where
c_{m,n}
is given by (
1.3
).

We note that Theorem
1.3
is a direct consequence of (
1.2
) when

m=1
or
m=n-1
, at least if
\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{n}{n-1}
. We shall give two distinct proofs of the
asymptotic formula

\#\{x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(x)\leqslant B,~\ell(x)\geqslant\varepsilon\}\sim c_{m,n}B
. The first is based on adapting the geometry of numbers arguments developed by Schmidt
[
19
]
and leads to the power saving error term present in Theorem
1.3
.
This is the object of
Section
4
.
The second proof occurs in Section
6
, but it doesn’t give a power saving.
We have decided to give the second proof since it is based on a general equidistribution statement of independent interest,
which is discussed in
Section
5
and which gives access to further results.
Indeed, using this equidistribution statement, we can also address a question raised by Peyre
[
16
, § 9]
concerning an alternative counting function, in which one orders rational points by the
maximal slope

\mu_{\max}(T_{\Lambda})
of their tangent lattice, as defined
in Section
2
, instead of by height. In the same spirit as counting sufficiently free points, this approach excludes most points whose tangent lattice is very lopsided, since such points
lead to tangent lattices whose maximal slope is large compared to the height. We shall prove the following result in Section
6
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any integers

1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
, we have

\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):\mu_{\max}(T_{\Lambda})\leqslant\log B\}=c_{m,n}^{\prime}B^{m(n-m)}+o\left(B^{m(n-m)}\right),

where
0<c_{m,n}^{\prime}<c_{m,n}
is given in (
6.2
).

In particular, this confirms the expectation expressed in
[
16
, Rem. 7.8]
for projective space.
This alternative counting function behaves quite differently from the standard counting function with respect to an anticanonical height function. In particular, it is compatible with products by
[
16
, Rem. 7.21(b)]
. Thus it follows from
Theorem
1.4
that

\#\{x\in X(\mathbb{Q}):\mu_{\max}(T_{x})\leqslant\log B\}=c_{X}B^{\dim X}+o\left(B^{\dim X}\right),

for a suitable constant
c_{X}>0
, when
X
is a product of Grassmannians. A striking feature of this example is that the rank of
\Pic(X)
, which appears in (
1.1
), can become arbitrarily large.

Returning to his asymptotic formula (
1.2
), Schmidt
[
20
,
21
]
also proved various counting statements about lattices drawn from the set

(1.5)

\mathscr{L}_{m,n}=\{\text{$\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a lattice of rank $m$}\}.

This is a homogeneous space for the group
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})

and carries an
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant measure that is unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar and induced by a Riemannian metric.
The space
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
has infinite volume with respect to this measure, but rescaling induces a natural projection
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}
to the subspace

\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}=\{\Lambda\in\mathscr{L}_{m,n}:\covol(\Lambda)=1\}

of
unimodular lattices
.
Restricting the Riemannian metric on
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
to
\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}
endows the latter with a finite measure, which we normalise to a probability measure.
Schmidt focuses on proving
equidistribution statements about the projections of the lattices in
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
to the space of
similarity classes of rank
m
lattices. However, as
worked out by
Horesh and
Karasik
[
13
, Thm. 3.2 (3)]
,
it is also possible to show that primitive lattices in

\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
equidistribute in

\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}
,
as
\covol(\Lambda)\to\infty
.
Theorem
1.3
tells us that among the rational points
x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
,
it is rare to find points whose associated tangent lattice
T_{\Lambda}=\Lambda^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}
is very lopsided.
Our final result is an equidistribution statement about the set of tangent lattices

\{T_{\Lambda}:\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)\}
,
as
H(\Lambda)\to\infty
.
Such a result fits into the landscape of recent work by Aka, Einsiedler and Shapira
[
1
,
2
]
, which is concerned with the equidistribution of the lattices
\mathbf{v}^{\perp}
, as one runs over primitive integer solutions
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\text{prim}}^{n}
to the equation
v_{1}^{2}+\cdots+v_{n}^{2}=N
, as
N
runs to infinity over suitable sets of positive integers. Indeed, although we shall not do so here, the lattices

\mathbf{v}^{\perp}
can be interpreted as tangent lattices associated to points on the affine quadric.

For
\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)
, the tangent lattices
T_{\Lambda}
are
lattices of rank
m(n-m)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
. They
belong to the
submanifold

\mathscr{G}_{m,n}=\{\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\perp}:\Lambda\in\mathscr{L}_{m,n}\}\subset\mathscr{L}_{m(n-m),n^{2}},

a precise description of which is given in Section
5
.
We shall construct a natural
probability measure on

\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}=\mathscr{G}_{m,n}\cap\mathscr{U\!L}_{m(n-m),n^{2}}
,
which we denote by
\nu
, paving the way to a proof of the following equidistribution result.

Theorem 1.5
.

With respect to
\nu
,
the projections of the set

\{T_{\Lambda}:\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})\}

to
\mathscr{U\!L}_{m(n-m),n^{2}}

equidistribute in the manifold
\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
, as
H(\Lambda)\to\infty
, with rate of convergence

O(H(\Lambda)^{-\frac{1}{16n^{4}}})
.

The explicit equidistribution statement is given below in
Theorem
5.7
, with a variant phrased in terms of probability measures in Theorem
5.8
. The proof takes place
in Section
5
and
involves a reduction to an equidistribution statement about
pairs of lattices
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\pi})
,
for primitive
\Lambda\in\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
,
that appears in recent work of
Horesh and Karasik
[
13
, Thm. 3.2 (4)]
.

2.
Basic facts about lattices

Recall that a lattice
\Lambda
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, or more generally, of an
N
-dimensional real vector space equipped with an inner product.
There exists an integer
r\leqslant N
and linearly independent vectors

\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\Lambda=\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}\{\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots\mathbf{b}_{r}\}
. We then say that
the
rank
of
\Lambda
is
\rank(\Lambda)=r
and call
\Lambda
a
full
lattice if
r=N
.
The
covolume
is
\covol(\Lambda)=\sqrt{\det(B^{\mathrm{t}}B)}=\sqrt{\det(\langle\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}\rangle)_{i,j}}
,
where the
basis matrix

B
is the
N\times r
matrix formed from the column vectors
\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r}
.
This definition is independent of the choice of basis.
We say that a lattice
\Lambda
is
unimodular
if
\covol(\Lambda)=1
. It will be convenient to denote
by
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}

the subspace
\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda)

that a lattice
\Lambda
generates.

A sublattice
\Lambda
of a lattice
\Gamma
is said to be
primitive in
\Gamma

if there is no other sublattice
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
of the same rank which
properly contains
\Lambda
.
A lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is said to be
integral
if it is contained
in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.
We say that an integral lattice is primitive if it is primitive in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.
Note that an integral lattice

\Lambda
is primitive if and only if

\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\Lambda
.

In this section we collect together some facts about lattices,
most of which are taken from the book by Cassels
[
8
]
.
A special role in our work will be played by the dual lattice and the factor
lattice, and so we begin by defining these.

Dual, orthogonal, and factor lattice

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a lattice of rank
r
with basis matrix
B
.
The
dual lattice
is defined to be

\Lambda^{*}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}:\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all $\mathbf{y}\in\Lambda$}\}.

This lattice has basis matrix
B(B^{\mathrm{t}}B)^{-1}
.
It immediately follows that
\rank(\Lambda^{*})=r
and

\covol(\Lambda^{*})=\covol(\Lambda)^{-1}
.
Moreover, we have
(\Lambda^{*})^{*}=\Lambda
.

Now suppose that
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N}
is a primitive lattice.
The
orthogonal lattice

\Lambda^{\perp}
is the primitive lattice

\Lambda^{\perp}=\left\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}:\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z}\rangle=0\text{ for all $\mathbf{z}\in\Lambda$}\right\}.

We have
(\Lambda^{\perp})^{\perp}=\Lambda
and

\covol(\Lambda)=\covol(\Lambda^{\perp})
.

If
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal
projection, then we define the
factor lattice

\Lambda^{\pi}
to be the projection
\pi(\mathbb{Z}^{N})
.
We have
\Lambda^{\pi}=(\Lambda^{\perp})^{*}
and so

\covol(\Lambda^{\pi})=\frac{1}{\covol(\Lambda)}.

Successive minima and slopes

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a lattice of rank
r
.
For each
1\leqslant k\leqslant r
, let
s_{k}(\Lambda)
be the least
\sigma>0
such that

\Lambda
contains at least
k
linearly independent vectors of Euclidean length
bounded by
\sigma
.
The
s_{k}(\Lambda)
are the
successive minima
of
\Lambda
, and they
satisfy

0<s_{1}(\Lambda)\leqslant s_{2}(\Lambda)\leqslant\cdots\leqslant s_{r}(\Lambda).

(Note that when we speak of successive minima, we shall always mean with respect to the Euclidean norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
.)
It follows from Minkowski’s second convex body theorem
[
8
, § VIII.1]

that

(2.1)

\covol(\Lambda)\leqslant\prod_{i=1}^{r}s_{i}(\Lambda)\ll_{N}\covol(\Lambda),

where the implied constant depends only on the dimension
N
of the ambient vector space.
Appealing to work of
Banaszczyk
[
3
, Thm. 2.1]
, we have

(2.2)

1\leqslant s_{k}(\Lambda)s_{r-k+1}(\Lambda^{*})\leqslant r,

for
1\leqslant k\leqslant r
.

The
slope
of a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of rank
r
is defined to be

\mu(\Lambda)=-\frac{1}{r}\log\covol(\Lambda).

The

maximal slope

\mu_{\text{max}}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is the
maximum of the slopes of all non-zero sublattices of
\Lambda
. On the other hand,
the
minimal slope

\mu_{\text{min}}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is the
minimum of the slopes of all quotients
\Lambda/\mathrm{M}
, as
\mathrm{M}\subset\Lambda
runs over proper and primitive sublattices.
According to
Bost and Chen
[
5
, Eq. (2)]
, the maximal and minimal slopes are related via the formula

(2.3)

\mu_{\text{max}}(\Lambda^{*})=-\mu_{\text{min}}(\Lambda),

where
\Lambda^{*}
is the dual of
\Lambda
.
Appealing to Theorems 1 and 3 of Borek
[
4
]
, we can also deduce that

(2.4)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\log s_{r}(\Lambda)+\mu_{\text{min}}(\Lambda)\leqslant c_{r}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\log s_{1}(\Lambda)+\mu_{\text{max}}(\Lambda)\leqslant c_{r},

for a certain explicit constant
c_{r}>0
depending only on the rank of
\Lambda
.

Tensor products

Suppose we are given two lattices
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
\rank(\Lambda_{i})=r_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
We may form the tensor product lattice

\Lambda_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2}
, which has rank
r_{1}r_{2}
.
Recall that
\Lambda_{1}=(\Lambda_{1}^{*})^{*}
.
Then we see that

\Lambda_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2}=(\Lambda_{1}^{*})^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2}
, which is isomorphic to the space

\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Lambda_{1}^{*},\Lambda_{2})
via the map which takes an elementary tensor

\varphi\otimes v_{2}\in(\Lambda_{1}^{*})^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2}

to the linear map
\Lambda_{1}^{*}\to\Lambda_{2}
defined by

(\varphi\otimes v_{2})(v_{1})=\varphi(v_{1})v_{2}
,
for
v_{1}\in\Lambda_{1}^{*}
and
v_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}
.
The covolume of the tensor product lattice is

(2.5)

\covol(\Lambda_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2})=\covol(\Lambda_{1})^{r_{2}}\covol(\Lambda_{2})^{r_{1}}.

Several authors have investigated the relationship between the maximal slopes of tensor product lattices and the maximal slopes of the factors.
Appealing to Chen
[
9
, Thm. 1.1]
, for example, one finds that

\mu_{\max}(\Lambda_{1})+\mu_{\max}(\Lambda_{2})\leqslant\mu_{\max}(\Lambda_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2})\leqslant\mu_{\max}(\Lambda_{1})+\mu_{\max}(\Lambda_{2})+r_{1}+r_{2}.

Applying this to
(\Lambda_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2})^{*}=\Lambda_{1}^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2}^{*}
, we deduce that

\mu_{\min}(\Lambda_{1})+\mu_{\min}(\Lambda_{2})-r_{1}-r_{2}\leqslant\mu_{\min}(\Lambda_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2})\leqslant\mu_{\min}(\Lambda_{1})+\mu_{\min}(\Lambda_{2}).

Once taken in conjunction with (
2.4
), this implies that

(2.6)

s_{r_{1}}(\Lambda_{1})s_{r_{2}}(\Lambda_{2})\ll s_{r_{1}r_{2}}(\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda_{2})\ll s_{r_{1}}(\Lambda_{1})s_{r_{2}}(\Lambda_{2}),

where the implied constants depend only on
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
.

Freeness

Suppose for the moment that
V
is a smooth Fano
\mathbb{Q}
-variety
of dimension
r
, extended to a smooth
projective
scheme
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
over
\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
.
Any rational point
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})
extends to a unique integral point

x\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z})
of this scheme.
The pullback
(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}}})_{x}
of its tangent bundle
\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}}}

along
{x}
is a
free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
r
inside

(\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x}\otimes\mathbb{R}=(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}}})_{x}\otimes\mathbb{R}
.
Fixing a Riemannian metric on
V(\mathbb{R})
induces an inner product on

(\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x}\otimes\mathbb{R}
and makes
(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}}})_{x}
a lattice.
The model
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
induces norms on
(\mathscr{T}_{V})\otimes K_{v}
at all finite places

K_{v}
, and we get an
adelic metric
on

\mathscr{T}_{V}
, as in
[
16
, Ex. 3.4]
.
The adelic metric on the tangent bundle induces an adelic norm on the
anticanonical bundle
\omega_{V}^{\vee}=\bigwedge^{r}\mathscr{T}_{V}
,
hence an anticanonical height.
The logarithmic anticanonical height is defined
in
[
16
, Déf. 3.11]
, and it satisfies

h(x)=-\log\covol((\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x})=r\mu((\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x}),

by
[
16
, Déf. 4.1, Rem. 4.2]
.
In
[
16
, Déf. 4.11]
,
Peyre defines the
freeness
of
x
to be

(2.7)

\ell(x)=\frac{\max\left\{\mu_{\min}((\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x}),0\right\}}{\mu((\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x})}=\frac{\max\left\{r\mu_{\min}((\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x}),0\right\}}{h(x)}.

Since the minimal slope is bounded by the slope, we have

0\leqslant\ell(x)\leqslant 1.

3.
Tangent lattices for Grassmannians

We now interpret the above in the case
V=\Gr(m,n)
of Grassmannians.
A rational point
x\in V(\mathbb{Q})
is the same thing as a primitive
lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
rank
m
.

Following
[
10
, § 3.2.3–3.2.4]
, for example, we may now indicate the construction of
the tangent bundle of
\Gr(m,n)
.
Consider the
trivial bundle
\mathscr{O}_{V}^{\oplus n}
, carrying the standard inner product.
It admits the subbundle
\mathscr{S}\subset\mathscr{O}_{V}^{\oplus n}
whose fibre at a point

\Lambda
is
\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the quotient bundle

\mathscr{Q}=\mathscr{O}_{V}^{\oplus n}/\mathscr{S}
.
Then

\mathscr{T}_{V}\cong\mathscr{H}\!om(\mathscr{S},\mathscr{Q})\cong\mathscr{S}^{\vee}\otimes\mathscr{Q}.

The inner product on
\mathscr{O}_{V}^{\oplus n}
induces inner products on these other
bundles (using the canonical isomorphism

\Lambda^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}
induced by the scalar product).
Thus it also induces a Riemannian metric.
All of these constructions work over
\Spec\mathbb{Z}
, giving rise to a smooth
projective
integral model
\Gr_{\mathbb{Z}}(m,n)
together with bundles

\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(with
(\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{Z}})_{\Lambda}=\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
),

\mathscr{Q}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(with
(\mathscr{Q}_{\mathbb{Z}})_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\Lambda)
,
and
\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}}}=\mathscr{H}\!om(\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathscr{Q}_{\mathbb{Z}})
. Note that

\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\Lambda
is isometric to
\Lambda^{\pi}
via the orthogonal projection.
For a point
x=\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
, we are interested in the

tangent lattice

T_{\Lambda}=(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}}})_{x}=\mathscr{H}\!om(\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathscr{Q}_{\mathbb{Z}})_{x}=\Hom(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\Lambda)\cong\Lambda^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}

inside

\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}\otimes\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}^{\perp}\cong\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}\cong(\mathscr{T}_{V})_{x}\otimes\mathbb{R},

where all isomorphisms are isometries.
Note that the dual of the tangent lattice is
T_{\Lambda}^{*}\cong\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\perp}
.
In view of (
2.5
),
this lattice has covolume

(3.1)

\begin{split}\covol(T_{\Lambda})&=\covol(\Lambda^{*})^{n-m}\covol(\Lambda^{\pi})^{m}\\
&=\covol(\Lambda)^{m-n}\cdot\frac{1}{\covol(\Lambda)^{m}}\\
&=\covol(\Lambda)^{-n}.\end{split}

The logarithmic anticanonical height of
x=\Lambda
is thus

h(x)=-\log\covol(\Lambda^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{\pi})=n\log\covol(\Lambda).

The corresponding exponential height is

H(x)=\covol(\Lambda)^{n}.

It follows from (
2.4
) that there is an explicit constant
c_{n}>0

such that

0\leqslant\log s_{m(n-m)}(T_{\Lambda})+\mu_{\min}(T_{\Lambda})\leqslant c_{n},

where
s_{m(n-m)}(T_{\Lambda})
is the largest successive minimum of the
tangent lattice.
Hence, the definition (
2.7
) yields

(3.2)

\begin{split}\ell(x)&=\frac{\max\left\{(\frac{m(n-m)}{n})\mu_{\min}(T_{\Lambda}),0\right\}}{\log\covol(\Lambda)}\\
&=\frac{\max\left\{-(\frac{m(n-m)}{n})\log s_{m(n-m)}(T_{\Lambda}),0\right\}}{\log\covol(\Lambda)}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log\covol(\Lambda)}\right),\end{split}

for any primitive rank
m
lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
representing a point

x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
.

One notes that

s_{m(n-m)}(T_{\Lambda})\geqslant\covol(T_{\Lambda})^{\frac{1}{m(n-m)}}=\covol(\Lambda)^{-\frac{n}{m(n-m)}},

by (
2.1
) and (
3.1
).
This argument suggests that if
x
is “typical”, in the sense that
the successive minima of
T_{\Lambda}
all have equal order of magnitude, then

\ell(x)=1+o(1)
, as
H(x)\to\infty
.

Projective space

We proceed by discussing the quantities we’ve introduced in the most familiar case

m=1
,
corresponding to
projective space
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n-1}
.
The lattice
T_{\Lambda}
then admits a particularly concrete description, as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x\in\Gr(1,n)(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
be identified with
the lattice
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{x}
, for
a primitive vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Then

T_{\Lambda}
is isometric to
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathbf{x}^{\perp})^{*}
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that
\covol(\Lambda)=\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}
. Let
us put
\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap{\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}
, which we note is
a primitive lattice of rank
n-1
. We begin by showing that

(3.3)

\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}=\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-2}{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}}.

Clearly,
{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}}=\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{x}
, which is a
primitive lattice of rank one contained in the space
\mathbb{R}\mathbf{x}
. We
compute
\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}
using the fact that

\Lambda^{\pi}={\left({\Lambda^{\perp}}\right)^{*}}

when
\Lambda
is primitive. Recalling the definition of the dual lattice,
we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}

\displaystyle={\left({\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}}\right)^{*}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\left({\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}}\right)_{\mathbb{R}}:\left\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all $\mathbf{y}\in{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}}$}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{t\mathbf{x}:t\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }\left\langle t\mathbf{x},m\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}$}\right\}.

But

\left\langle t\mathbf{x},m\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=tm\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=tm\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
Moreover,
tm\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
if and only if

t\in\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-2}\mathbb{Z}
.
We conclude that

\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}=\left\{t\mathbf{x}:t\in\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-2}\mathbb{Z}\right\}=\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-2}\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{x}=\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-2}{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}},

as required for (
3.3
).

Next we observe that

T_{\Lambda}=(\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{x})^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{x})^{\pi}=\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}.

We wish to prove that this is isometric to

\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}.

We know that

\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}\subset\left({\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\perp}}\right)_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\mathbf{x}

and
{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}\subset\Gamma_{\mathbf{x},\mathbb{R}}={\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}
,
because the dual lattice always lives in the same space as the original lattice,
and the factor lattice always lives in the orthogonal space.
Thus
T_{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbf{x}\otimes{\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}
.
We claim that the map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\mathbf{x}\otimes{\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\mapsto\alpha\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\mathbf{w}

is an isometry, where the inner product on the tensor product is the
product of
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
in each of the
components.
In the light of (
3.3
),
this will suffice to complete the proof of the lemma, since then

\varphi
clearly maps the lattice

\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{\pi}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}

to
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}{\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}
.
To check the claim, it suffices to find an orthornormal basis of

\mathbb{R}\mathbf{x}\otimes{\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}
that is taken to an
orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{x}^{\perp}
. Clearly, the orthonormal basis

\left\{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{w}_{n-1}\right\}

suffices,
where
\left\{\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{n-1}\right\}
is an orthonormal basis
for
{\mathbf{x}^{\perp}}
.
∎

We next consider what the definition (
3.2
) has to say when

m=1
. Applying Lemma
3.1
, we obtain
h(x)=n\log\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}
and

\ell(x)=\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\max\left\{1-\frac{\log s_{n-1}((\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathbf{x}^{\perp})^{*})}{\log\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}},0\right\}+O\left(\frac{1}{h(x)}\right),

if
x\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
is represented by the primitive vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
But

\log s_{n-1}((\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathbf{x}^{\perp})^{*})=-\log s_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathbf{x}^{\perp})+O(1),

by (
2.2
). Since
s_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathbf{x}^{\perp})\geqslant 1
, this shows that

\ell(x)\geqslant\frac{n-1}{n}+o(1),

as
h(x)\to\infty
,
which essentially recovers (
1.4
).

The general case

Let
m\geqslant 1
and let
\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
.
The largest successive minimum of the tangent lattice
T_{\Lambda}=\Lambda^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}
is
closely related to those of
\Lambda^{*}
and
\Lambda^{\pi}
. Indeed, it follows from (
2.6
) that

s_{m}(\Lambda^{*})s_{n-m}(\Lambda^{\pi})\ll s_{m(n-m)}(T_{\Lambda})\ll s_{m}(\Lambda^{*})s_{n-m}(\Lambda^{\pi}),

where the implied constants depend only on
n
.

Returning to (
3.2
),
there exists a constant
C>0
, which depends only on
n
, such that
any given
x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})
has
\ell(x)<\varepsilon+o(1)
if and only if

s_{m(n-m)}(T_{\Lambda})\geqslant C\left(\covol(\Lambda)\right)^{-\frac{\varepsilon n}{m(n-m)}}.

On redefining
C
, this is equivalent to

s_{m}(\Lambda^{*})s_{n-m}(\Lambda^{\pi})\geqslant C\left(\covol(\Lambda)\right)^{-\frac{\varepsilon n}{m(n-m)}}.

It now follows from (
2.2
) that

\ell(x)<\varepsilon+o(1)
if and only if

(3.4)

s_{1}(\Lambda^{\perp})s_{1}(\Lambda)\leqslant C\left(\covol(\Lambda)\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{m(n-m)}},

after a further modification to
C
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
1<m<n-1
. In particular, it follows
that
n\geqslant 4
.
Our task is to show that there are infinitely many
x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})

such that
\ell(x)=0
.
For this we work directly with the definition (
3.2
) of
\ell(x)

in terms of slopes.
Consider the two vectors
\mathbf{u}=(q,1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and

\mathbf{v}=(1,-q,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
for an arbitrary positive integer
q
.
Let

\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n}
be the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We take

\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{e}_{3}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{e}_{m+1}.

This is a primitive lattice of rank
m
, with

\covol(\Lambda)=q
and
s_{1}(\Lambda)=1
.
The orthogonal complement is

\Lambda^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{v}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{e}_{m+2}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{e}_{n}.

This is a primitive lattice of rank
n-m
, with

\covol(\Lambda^{\perp})=q
and
s_{1}(\Lambda^{\perp})=1
.
On appealing to (
2.3
), it now follows that

\mu_{\min}(T_{\Lambda})=-\mu_{\max}(\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\perp})\leqslant\log\covol(\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{e}_{m+1}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{n}))=0,

whence
\ell(x)=0
.
∎

Let
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):\ell(x)\leqslant\varepsilon\}
be the set of
non-
\varepsilon
-free points on
\Gr(m,n)
. The remaining task for this section is to prove that this set is not thin,
as claimed in Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\varepsilon>0
and
1<m<n-1
, then the image of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is dense in

\prod_{v}\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}_{v})
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a finite set of places, and let

U_{v}\subset\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}_{v})
be open subsets for
v\in S
.
We want to find a
\Lambda\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
whose image lies in

\prod_{v\in S}U_{v}
.
By weak approximation, there exists
\Lambda_{0}\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q})

whose image lies in

\prod_{v\in S}U_{v}
.
Pick any rational line
l\subset\Lambda_{0,\mathbb{R}}=\Lambda_{0}\otimes\mathbb{R}

and any rational hyperplane

H\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\Lambda_{0}\subset H
. We have
l=\langle\mathbf{u}\rangle
and
H=\langle\mathbf{v}\rangle^{\perp}

for primitive
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Consider the subvariety

X=\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n):l\subset\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}\subset H\}\subset\Gr(m,n),

which is isomorphic to
\Gr(m-1,n-2)
. Our
assumption
on
m
ensures that
\Gr(m-1,n-2)
is smooth and of positive dimension. Thus the non-empty
open subsets
U_{v}\cap X(\mathbb{Q}_{v})
contain infinitely many

\mathbb{Q}_{v}
-points for all
v\in S
.
An application of weak approximation on
\Gr(m-1,n-2)
therefore shows
that there are infinitely many

\Lambda^{\prime}\in X(\mathbb{Q})
whose images lie in
U_{v}\cap X(\mathbb{Q}_{v})
for all

v\in S
.

Let
C>0
be sufficiently small that (
3.4
) implies that the
corresponding point is not
\varepsilon
-free.
Since there are only finitely many points in
\Gr(m-1,n-2)(\mathbb{Q})
of bounded
height, we can find
\Lambda^{\prime}\in X(\mathbb{Q})
whose images are in
U_{v}

for all

v\in S
, with

H(\Lambda^{\prime})>\left(C^{-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{2}\right)^{\frac{m(n-m)}{\varepsilon}}.

Since the corresponding lattice
\Lambda
satisfies

s_{1}(\Lambda)\leqslant\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{2}
and
s_{1}(\Lambda^{\perp})\leqslant\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{2}
,
we have
P^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Appealing to work of Serre
[
22
, Corollary 3.5.4]
, the image of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
would be
nowhere dense if it where thin.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

We note that
\Omega_{0}
is always a thin set, since it fails to be Zariski dense. Indeed, when

1<m<n-1
, we find that
\Omega_{0}

is the union of the proper subvarieties
\{P\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):\mathbf{u}\in P\}
,
where
\mathbf{u}
runs over the finitely many
vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of norm
O(1)
.
If, on the other hand,
m
is
1
or
n-1
, then
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\emptyset

for
\varepsilon<\frac{n-1}{n}
by (
1.4
).

4.
Free rational points dominate

In this section we give our first proof of Theorem
1.3
.
We must provide an upper bound for the quantity

E_{\varepsilon}(B)=\#\{x\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(x)\leqslant B,~\ell(x)<\varepsilon\},

with the aim being to show that
E_{\varepsilon}(B)=o(B)

for any
0\leqslant\varepsilon<1
.
Switching to the language of primitive lattices,
we write
P(m,n)
for the set of primitive lattices
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which have rank
m
.
It then follows from (
3.4
) that
E_{\varepsilon}(B)
is at most

\displaystyle\#\left\{\Lambda\in P(m,n):\covol(\Lambda)\leqslant B^{\frac{1}{n}},~s_{1}(\Lambda^{\perp})s_{1}(\Lambda)\leqslant C\left(\covol(\Lambda)\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{m(n-m)}}\right\},

for a suitable constant
C>0
depending only on
n
.
It is convenient to
break the range for the covolume and the sizes of
s_{1}(\Lambda)
and
s_{1}(\Lambda^{\perp})

into dyadic intervals. Thus we put

(4.1)

E_{\varepsilon}(B)\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R=2^{j}\\
1\leqslant R\leqslant 2B^{1/n}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1}=2^{j_{1}},~S_{2}=2^{j_{2}}\\
1\leqslant S_{1}S_{2}\leqslant CR^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{m(n-m)}}\end{subarray}}\widetilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(R,S_{1},S_{2}),

where

\widetilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(R,S_{1},S_{2})=\#\left\{\Lambda\in P(m,n):\begin{array}[]{l}R/2<\covol(\Lambda)\leqslant R,\\
S_{1}/2<s_{1}(\Lambda)\leqslant S_{1},\\
S_{2}/2<s_{1}(\Lambda^{\perp})\leqslant S_{2}\end{array}\right\}.

Let

P(m,n;R,S)=\left\{\Lambda\in P(m,n):\covol(\Lambda)\leqslant R,~s_{1}(\Lambda)\leqslant S\right\}.

Then, since
the
covolume is preserved under taking the orthogonal complement, we see that

(4.2)

\widetilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(R,S_{1},S_{2})\leqslant\min\left\{\#P(m,n;R,S_{1}),~\#P(n-m,n;R,S_{2})\right\}.

Our attention now shifts to estimating

\#P(m,n;R,S)

for given
1\leqslant m<n
and
R,S\geqslant 1
.

Let
r\geqslant 1
be an integer and let

s_{1},\dots,s_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
with

1\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant s_{r}.

We let
P_{r,n}(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
be the set of primitive lattices
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}

of rank
r

whose
i
th successive minimum lies in the interval
[s_{i},2s_{i})
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
. We shall need the following result.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

\#P_{r,n}(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\ll s_{1}^{n+r-1}s_{2}^{n+r-3}\cdots s_{r}^{n+1-r},

where the implied constant only depends on
n
.

Proof.

This is extracted from Lemma 6 of
Schmidt
[
19
]

in work of Browning, Le Boudec and Sawin
[
7
, Lemma 3.18]
.
∎

The following general inequality will facilitate our application of
Lemma
4.1
.
Let
r\geqslant 1
and let

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{r},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\geqslant 0
.
Then

(4.3)

\xi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\xi_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}\leqslant\left(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{r}\right)^{\frac{\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}}{r}},

provided that
1\leqslant\xi_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\xi_{r}
and
\alpha_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\alpha_{r}
.
This is easily proved by induction on
r
. Indeed, on putting
\beta=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r-1}
, the induction hypothesis yields

\displaystyle\xi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\xi_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{r-1}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{r-1}}\cdot\xi_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{r}\right)^{\frac{\beta+\alpha_{r}}{r}}\cdot\left(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{r-1}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{r(r-1)}-\frac{\alpha_{r}}{r}}\cdot\xi_{r}^{\alpha_{r}-\frac{\beta+\alpha_{r}}{r}}.

Noting that
\beta/(r-1)\geqslant\alpha_{r}
, we may take
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{r-1}\leqslant\xi_{r}
in the second factor and then immediately
arrive at the right hand side of (
4.3
).

We are interested in the case
r=m
. Breaking into dyadic intervals, we see that

\#P(m,n;R,S)\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant s_{m}\ll R\\
s_{1}\dots s_{m}\ll R\\
s_{1}\leqslant S\end{subarray}}\#P_{m,n}(s_{1},\dots,s_{m}),

where
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}
run over powers of
2
, subject to the stated inequalities.
But Lemma
4.1
yields

\displaystyle\#P_{m,n}(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})

\displaystyle\ll s_{1}^{n+m-1}s_{2}^{n+m-3}\cdots s_{m}^{n+1-m}.

Hence it follows from (
4.3
) that

s_{2}^{n+m-3}s_{3}^{n+m-5}\cdots s_{m}^{n+1-m}\ll\left(s_{2}\cdots s_{m}\right)^{\frac{\Delta}{m-1}},

where

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m-2}(n+1-m+2k)=(m-1)(n-1).

Thus

\displaystyle\#P(m,n;R,S)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant s_{m}\ll R\\
s_{1}\cdots s_{m}\ll R\\
s_{1}\leqslant S\end{subarray}}s_{1}^{n+m-1}\left(s_{2}\cdots s_{m}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\ll R^{n-1}(\log R)^{m-2}\sum_{1\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant S}\frac{s_{1}^{n+m-1}}{s_{1}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\ll S_{1}^{m}R^{n-1}(\log R)^{m-2}.

Returning to (
4.2
), we
apply the inequality
\min\{\alpha,\beta\}\leqslant\alpha^{\frac{n-m}{n}}\beta^{\frac{m}{n}}
for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, in order to deduce that

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}_{\varepsilon}(R,S_{1},S_{2})

\displaystyle\ll\left(S_{1}^{m}R^{n-1}(\log R)^{m-2}\right)^{\frac{n-m}{n}}\cdot\left(S_{2}^{n-m}R^{n-1}(\log R)^{n-m-2}\right)^{\frac{m}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant(S_{1}S_{2})^{\frac{m(n-m)}{n}}R^{n-1}(\log R)^{2m-2}.

But then,
on returning to (
4.1
) and summing over dyadic intervals for
R,S_{1},S_{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(B)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R=2^{j}\\
1\leqslant R\leqslant 2B^{1/n}\end{subarray}}R^{n-1+\varepsilon}(\log R)^{2m-2}

\displaystyle\ll B^{1-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}}(\log B)^{2m-2}.

This gives an explicit power saving error term if and only if
\varepsilon<1
.
This therefore completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

5.
Tangent lattices equidistribute

The goal of this
section is to prove Theorem
1.5
, namely to establish equidistribution of the projections of the
tangent lattices
{\Lambda^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}
in
\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}

with respect to
a certain probability measure
\nu
which will be defined below.

Spaces of lattices

We begin by describing the spaces
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
and
\mathscr{G}_{m,n}
of lattices as quotients.
Recalling the space
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
defined in (
1.5
), we note that it is isomorphic to

\mathscr{L}_{m,n}\cong\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})&\Mat_{m,n-m}(\mathbb{R})\\
0_{n-m,m}&\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{R})\end{smallmatrix}\right]\cap\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\right).

For our needs, however, it will be useful to view it as the non-homogeneous
quotient
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}\cong\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})/\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
,
where
\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})
is the open subset of
\Mat_{n\times m}(\mathbb{R})

consisting of matrices of full rank.

With the latter point of view, the subspace
\mathscr{G}_{m,n}
can be described as a quotient

Y/\GL_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{Z})\subset\operatorname{Mat}_{n^{2}\times m(n-m)}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})/\GL_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{Z})
, where

Y=\left\{A\otimes B:\begin{array}[]{l}A\in\Mat_{n\times m}(\mathbb{R}),~B\in\Mat_{n\times(n-m)}(\mathbb{R})\\
\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(A)=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(B)^{\perp}\end{array}\right\},

and where we interpret
A\otimes B
as
(a_{i,j}B)_{i,j}
for
A=(a_{i,j})\in\Mat_{n\times m}(\mathbb{R})
.
(Note that the conditions in
Y
imply that
A
and
B
must be of full rank.)

Next, we extend the notion of a factor lattice to lattices that are not necessarily integral: Recall that a rank
m
lattice
\Lambda
is

primitive inside a full unimodular lattice

\Gamma
if there is no rank
m
sublattice of
\Gamma
that properly contains
\Lambda
.
(Note that when
m<n
, every lattice is primitive with respect to infinitely many full unimodular lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.)
For each full unimodular lattice
\Gamma
in which
\Lambda
is primitive, we define the
factor lattice of
\Lambda
with respect to
\Gamma
as
{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}=\pi(\Gamma)
, where
\pi
is
the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
{(\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda))^{\perp}}
. We have

\covol({\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}})=\frac{1}{\covol(\Lambda)}.

In particular,
{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}
is unimodular if and only if
\Lambda
is.

Consider the space of pairs

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}=\left\{(\Lambda,{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}):\text{$\Lambda$ primitive of rank $m$ in
a full unimodular lattice $\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ }\right\}.

We observe that

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}\cong\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})&\Mat_{m,n-m}(\mathbb{R})\\
0_{n-m,m}&\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})\end{smallmatrix}\right]\cap\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

To see this, note that the first
m
columns of any matrix in a given coset span the same lattice
\Lambda
, and the projections of the
last
n-m
columns of such a matrix span

{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}
, where
\Gamma
is the full lattice spanned by the
columns of the matrix. The space
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
is also a
\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
-homogeneous
space since

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}\cong\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})&\Mat_{m,n-m}(\mathbb{R})\\
0_{n-m,m}&\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right\}.

Subspaces of unimodular lattices

For the sake of defining the measure
\nu
appearing in Theorem
1.5

and of proving Proposition
5.1
below, we briefly discuss the
subsets of unimodular elements inside
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
,
\mathscr{G}_{m,n}
,
and
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
. Let
\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}
denote the subset of unimodular lattices in

\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
, and observe that
\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}\cong\mathscr{L}_{m,n}/D
, where

D=\{\operatorname{diag}(\alpha^{-\frac{1}{m}}\operatorname{I}_{m},\alpha^{\frac{1}{n-m}}\operatorname{I}_{n-m}):\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\}\cong\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

This is a one-parameter subgroup of diagonal matrices in
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover,
\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}\cong\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times,1}(\mathbb{R})/\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
,
where
\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times,1}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of matrices
M

of full rank that satisfy
\det(M^{\mathrm{t}}M)=1
.

Recall that
\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}=\mathscr{G}_{m,n}\cap\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}
.
Defining

Y^{1}=\{A\otimes B\in Y:\det(A)^{n-m}\det(B)^{m}=1\},

we get

\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}\cong Y^{1}/\GL_{m(n-m)}(\mathbb{Z}).

Let
\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}
be the subset of pairs in
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}

for which
\Lambda
(and therefore also
{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}
)
is unimodular.

The space
\mathscr{L}_{m,n}
decomposes as

\mathscr{L}_{m,n}\cong\mathscr{U\!L}_{m,n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}

via
\Lambda\mapsto(\covol(\Lambda)^{-1/m}\Lambda,\covol(\Lambda))
,
with inverse map
\alpha^{1/m}L\mapsfrom(L,\alpha)
.

For
\mathscr{G}_{m,n}
, we use the decomposition

\mathscr{G}_{m,n}\cong\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0},

given by

\Lambda\mapsto\left(\covol(\Lambda)^{-\frac{1}{m(n-m)}}\Lambda,\covol(\Lambda)^{-1}\right),

with the inverse map being given by

\alpha^{-1/(m(n-m))}L\mapsfrom(L,\alpha)
.

Lastly,
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
decomposes as

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}\cong\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}

via

(\Lambda,{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}})\mapsto(\covol(\Lambda)^{-\frac{1}{m}}\Lambda,\covol(\Lambda)^{\frac{1}{n-m}}{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}},\covol(\Lambda)),

the inverse map being given by

(\alpha^{\frac{1}{m}}L,\alpha^{-\frac{1}{n-m}}{L^{\pi,\Gamma^{\prime}}})\mapsfrom(L,{L^{\pi,\Gamma^{\prime}}},\alpha).

In all three cases, we denote by
u
the projection to the unimodular component. In particular,
\mathscr{G}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
maps
T_{\Lambda}\mapsto(u(T_{\Lambda}),H(\Lambda))
, for
\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)
.

The decomposition of
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
extends to a decomposition of
homogeneous measure spaces as follows.
Let
\vol_{\mathscr{P}_{m,n}}
be an
\SL_{n}
-invariant measure on

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
(as described, for example, in
[
11
, Thm. 2.51]
).
Then the Iwasawa decomposition of the Haar measure on
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
yields

\displaystyle(\mathscr{P}_{m,n},\vol_{\mathscr{P}_{m,n}})\cong(\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n},\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}})\times(\mathbb{R}_{>0},x^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x),

for a finite measure
\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}}
on
\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}
, and we normalise all measures so that this measure is a probability measure.

From pairs
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\pi})
to tensors
{\Lambda^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}

We now summarise our strategy for proving Theorem
1.5
.
We construct a diffeomorphism
\varphi^{1}\colon\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
that will enable us to apply an equidistribution
statement for the primitive lattices in
\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}
with
respect to the measure
\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}}
.
The measure in our equidistribution theorem will be the pushforward
\nu=\varphi^{1}_{*}\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}}
of
\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The map

\varphi\colon\mathscr{P}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{G}_{m,n}

sending
(\Lambda,{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}})\mapsto{\Lambda^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}

is a diffeomorphism, as is its restriction

\varphi^{1}\colon\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}

to the unimodular elements. Moreover, we have
\varphi=\varphi^{1}\times(\,\cdot\,)^{n}
.

Let

U=\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{I}_{m}&\Mat_{m,n-m}(\mathbb{R})\\
0&\operatorname{I}_{n-m}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right\},

so that

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}=\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\left(U\rtimes\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})&0\\
0&\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right).

Clearly,
{\Lambda^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}
is a lattice of rank

\rank({\Lambda^{*}})\cdot\rank(\Lambda^{\pi})=m(n-m)
inside the space

\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
.
The diffeomorphism will
be constructed in three steps:

(i)

define a differentiable map
\tilde{\varphi}\colon\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\to Y
;

(ii)

obtain a diffeomorphism
\hat{\varphi}\colon\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/U\to Y
; and

(iii)

reduce
\hat{\varphi}
modulo the integral subgroups to
\varphi\colon\mathscr{P}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{G}_{m,n}
.

Step
(i)

Let
\tilde{\varphi}\colon\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\to Y
be the map which is defined as
follows. For
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, let
A_{g}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})

be the matrix consisting of the first
m
columns of
g
.
Let
\tilde{A}_{g}=A_{g}(A_{g}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{g})^{-1}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})
,
and let
\tilde{B}_{g}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times(n-m)}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})
be the matrix obtained from
projecting the columns of
g
to the orthogonal subspace of
\tilde{A}_{g}

(or
A_{g}
, as they span the same space). Then we define

\tilde{\varphi}(g)=\tilde{A}_{g}\otimes\tilde{B}_{g},

which clearly lies in
Y
.
We note that
\tilde{\varphi}
is differentiable since it is a composition of differentiable maps.
Moreover, if
g=(A|B)
generates a lattice
\Gamma
and
A
generates a lattice
\Lambda
,
then
\tilde{\varphi}(g)
generates
\Lambda^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}}
.

Step
(ii)

By the definition of
\tilde{B}_{g}
, the map
\tilde{\varphi}

is invariant under the unipotent subgroup
U
. On the other hand,
multiplying
g
by a constant
\lambda
changes
\tilde{A}_{g}
by
\lambda^{-1}

and
\tilde{B}_{g}
by
\lambda
, so
\tilde{\varphi}
is also invariant under
the center of
\GL_{n}
. Hence
\tilde{\varphi}
descends to the quotient

X=\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/U
, and we denote this map by
\hat{\varphi}\colon X\to Y
.

Lemma 5.2
.

\hat{\varphi}\colon X\to Y
is a diffeomorphism.

Proof.

For
C\in Y
, write
C=A\otimes B
with
A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})
and
B\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times(n-m)}^{\times}(\mathbb{R})
. For
\tilde{A}=A(A^{\mathrm{t}}A)^{-1}
, let

\psi\colon Y\to\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the map
C\mapsto(\tilde{A}|B)
.
Indeed,
(\tilde{A}|B)
has full rank by the assumptions on
A
and
B
,
and it is independent of the choice of
A
and
B
as any other representation

C=\lambda^{-1}A\otimes\lambda B
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, leads to

(\widetilde{\lambda^{-1}A}|\lambda B)=\lambda(\tilde{A}|B),

which has the same class as
(\tilde{A}|B)
in
\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

We claim that the map
\hat{\psi}\colon Y\to X
obtained by reducing
\psi

mod
U
is the inverse of
\hat{\varphi}
.
Note that the matrix
\psi\circ\hat{\varphi}(A|B)

differs from
(A|B)
by adding multiples of the first columns to
the last columns, i.e., by an element of
U
, so

\hat{\psi}\circ\hat{\varphi}=\id_{X}
.
Conversely, the tensor
\hat{\varphi}\circ\hat{\psi}(A\otimes B)
is again

A\otimes B
, since
\tilde{\tilde{A}}=A
, and
A
,
B
are already
orthogonal, so the orthogonalisation step in
\hat{\varphi}
changes
nothing. Thus
\hat{\varphi}\circ\hat{\psi}=\id_{Y}
. Finally,
\hat{\varphi}

is a differentiable since
\tilde{\varphi}
is, and
\hat{\psi}
is
differentiable because
\psi
is.
∎

Step
(iii)

We first note that
\hat{\varphi}
descends to the quotient. Indeed, for a matrix
(A|B)\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})

and
(\gamma_{m},\gamma_{n-m})\in\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})\times\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have

\hat{\varphi}\colon\left(A|B\right)(\gamma_{m},\gamma_{n-m})\mapsto(A\gamma_{m}\otimes B\gamma_{n-m})=(A\otimes B)(\gamma_{m}\otimes\gamma_{n-m}),

so
(A|B)(\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})\times\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z}))
maps into
(A\otimes B)\GL_{m(n-m)}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Also note that
\varphi
is surjective since
\hat{\varphi}
is. The crucial
part is then to show injectivity, which we do using two technical lemmas.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
A\otimes B\in Y
. If
g\in\GL_{m(n-m)}(\mathbb{R})
is such that
(A\otimes B)g\in Y
,
then
g
is an elementary tensor.

Proof.

Write
(A\otimes B)g=A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime}\in Y
.
Since
g\in\GL_{m(n-m)}(\mathbb{R})
, it follows that

\Span(A\otimes B)=\Span(A^{\prime}\otimes B^{\prime})
,
which means that

\Span(A)\otimes\Span(B)=\Span(A^{\prime})\otimes\Span(B^{\prime})
.
As a result,
\Span(A)=\Span(A^{\prime})
and
\Span(B)=\Span(B^{\prime})
,
and therefore there exist
g_{m}\in\GL_{m}(\mathbb{R})
and
g_{n-m}\in\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{R})

such that
A^{\prime}=Ag_{m}
and
B^{\prime}=Bg_{n-m}
.
Thus
(A\otimes B)(g-g_{m}\otimes g_{n-m})=0
,
which implies that
g=g_{m}\otimes g_{n-m}
,
since
A\otimes B
has full rank.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
A\otimes B\in Y
, and let
\gamma\in\GL_{m(n-m)}(\mathbb{Z})

such that
(A\otimes B)\gamma\in Y
. Then
\gamma=\gamma_{m}\otimes\gamma_{n-m}

for
\gamma_{m}\in\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\gamma_{n-m}\in\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Let
A\otimes B
and
\gamma
be as in the statement.
Appealing to Lemma
5.3

and using the fact that
\Mat_{m(n-m)\times m(n-m)}(\mathbb{Z})=\Mat_{m\times m}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes\Mat_{(n-m)\times(n-m)}(\mathbb{Z})
,
we have
\gamma=\gamma_{m}\otimes\gamma_{n-m}
, with
\gamma_{i}\in\GL_{i}(\mathbb{R})\cap\Mat_{i\times i}(\mathbb{Z})

for
i\in\{m,n-m\}
. Since

\det(\gamma_{m})^{n-m}\det(\gamma_{n-m})^{m}=\det(\gamma_{m}\otimes\gamma_{n-m})=\det(\gamma)\in\{\pm 1\},

it follows that
\det(\gamma_{m}),\det(\gamma_{n-m})\in\{\pm 1\}
.
We conclude that
\gamma_{m}\in\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\gamma_{n-m}\in\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

As already noted,
\varphi
is well-defined and surjective. It is also
injective by Lemma
5.4
, since if two elements in

Y
are equivalent modulo
\GL_{m(n-m)}(\mathbb{Z})
, then their pre-images
are equivalent modulo
\GL_{m}(\mathbb{Z})\times\GL_{n-m}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus
\varphi

is also bijective, and therefore the inverse map of
\hat{\varphi}
descends
to an inverse map of
\varphi
. Since
\hat{\varphi}
and its inverse are
differentiable, so are
\varphi
and its inverse.

Composing
\varphi
with the decompositions of
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
and
\mathscr{G}_{m,n}

results in

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{P}_{m,n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{G}_{m,n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(L,{L^{\pi,\Gamma}},\alpha)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\alpha^{\frac{1}{m}}L,\alpha^{-\frac{1}{n-m}}{L^{\pi,\Gamma}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha^{-\frac{n}{m(n-m)}}{L^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{L^{\pi,\Gamma}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces({L^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{L^{\pi,\Gamma}},\alpha^{n}),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

hence
\varphi
restricts to a diffeomorphism

\varphi^{1}\colon\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}\to\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
that satisfies

\varphi=\varphi^{1}\times(\cdot)^{n}
, as required.
∎

The main equidistribution theorem

The equidistribution statement in Theorem
1.5
employs
counting in subsets of the spaces
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
and
\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}

satisfying the following boundary condition.

Definition 5.5
.

The (topological) boundary of a set inside a manifold
\mathscr{M}

is said to be
controlled
if it consists of finitely many subsets of
embedded
C^{1}
submanifolds whose dimension is strictly smaller
than
\dim\mathscr{M}
.

Having proved that the space
\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
is diffeomorphic to

\mathscr{G}_{m,n}
via
\varphi
, we may apply the following counting result for pairs in

\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
, in order to deduce our main equidistribution result about tangent lattices.

Theorem 5.6

(Horesh–Karasik
[
13
]
)
.

Let
m,n
be integers such that
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
. Let
E\subseteq\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}
be a subset
whose boundary is controlled.
For any
B\geqslant 1
, let
E_{B}=E\times(0,B]\subset\mathscr{P}_{m,n}
denote the
subset of pairs
(\Lambda,{\Lambda^{\pi,\Gamma}})
that project to
E
and for
which
\covol(\Lambda)\leqslant B
.
Then

\#\left\{(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\pi})\in E_{B}:\text{$\Lambda$ primitive}\right\}=c_{m,n}\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}}(E)B^{n}+O_{\varepsilon,E}(B^{n(1-\delta_{E})+\varepsilon}),

for any
\varepsilon>0
, where
c_{m,n}
was defined in (
1.3
)
and

\delta_{E}=\begin{cases}\frac{\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rceil}{4n^{2}}&\text{ if $E$ is bounded,}\\
\frac{\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rceil}{4n^{2}\cdot[2(\max(m,n-m)-1)(n^{2}-1)+n^{2}]}&\text{ if $E$ is not bounded.}\end{cases}

To be precise, this result follows on combining
[
13
, Thm. 3.2(4)]

with the discussion in
[
13
, Remark 3.3]
around
normalising the invariant measures to get probability measures, thus obtaining the leading constant.
We are now ready to prove the following result, which is a more precise version of
Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
m,n
be integers such that
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
. Let
E\subseteq\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
be a subset
whose boundary is controlled.
Define

E_{B}=E\times(0,B]\subset\mathscr{G}_{m,n},

for
B\geqslant 1
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):T_{\Lambda}\in E_{B}\}}{\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(\Lambda)\leqslant B\}}

\displaystyle=\nu(E)+O_{E}(B^{-\frac{1}{16n^{4}}}).

Proof.

We claim that

\delta_{E}>\frac{1}{16n^{4}}

in the statement of Theorem
5.6
. This is obvious when

E
is bounded. Alternatively, when

E
is not bounded, we note that

2(\max(m,n-m)-1)(n^{2}-1)+n^{2}\leqslant 2(n-2)(n^{2}-1)+n^{2}<2(n-1)n^{2},

for any
1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
and
n\geqslant 2
. But then it follows that

\delta_{E}>\frac{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)}{4n^{2}\cdot 2(n-1)n^{2}}=\frac{1}{16n^{4}},

as claimed.

The set
E_{B}
consists of all the lattices
L\in\mathscr{G}
whose
normalisation lies in
E
and for which

\covol(L)^{-1}\leqslant B
.
It contains precisely those
T_{\Lambda}
for primitive

\Lambda
that project to
E
and have
H(\Lambda)\leqslant B
.
Since
\varphi
defines a bijection between the pairs
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\pi})
,
where
\Lambda
is primitive, and the elements

T_{\Lambda}={\Lambda^{*}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda^{\pi}
,
where
\Lambda
is primitive, we deduce that the number of tangent lattices in

E_{B}
is in fact equal to the number of
“primitive” pairs in
\varphi^{-1}(E_{B})
.
The latter can be estimated by Theorem
5.6

since Proposition
5.1
implies that

\varphi^{-1}(E_{B})=(\varphi^{1})^{-1}(E)\times(0,B^{1/n}]
,
where
\varphi^{1}
is a diffeomorphism, so that in particular

(\varphi^{1})^{-1}(E)
has controlled boundary.
On taking a sufficiently small choice of
\varepsilon
in
Theorem
5.6
, an application of this result
now yields

\displaystyle\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):T_{\Lambda}\in E_{B}\}

\displaystyle=c_{m,n}\vol_{\mathscr{U\!P}_{m,n}}((\varphi^{1})^{-1}(E))B+O_{E}\left(B^{1-\frac{1}{16n^{4}}}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{m,n}\nu(E)B+O_{E}\left(B^{1-\frac{1}{16n^{4}}}\right).

The statement of the theorem now follows with the asymptotic expression (
1.2
)
for the denominator.
∎

The following reformulation of Theorem
5.7
will be very useful in applications. Call a Borel set

E\subset\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
a
continuity set
if
\nu(\partial E)=0
, and denote by

C^{b}(\mathscr{UG}_{m,n})
the space of bounded, continuous functions
\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Let

N_{m,n}(B)=\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(\Lambda)\leqslant B\}

be the number of rational points on
\Gr(m,n)
of height at most
B
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let

\nu_{B}=\frac{1}{N_{m,n}(B)}\sum_{H(\Lambda)\leqslant B}\delta_{u(T_{\Lambda})}

be the sequence of probability measures on

\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
counting tangent lattices of points of bounded height.
Then
\nu_{B}
converges weakly to
\nu
as
B\to\infty
, in the sense that

(i)

\lim_{B\to\infty}\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{B}=\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\quad

for all
f\in C^{b}(\mathscr{UG}_{m,n})
,

(ii)

\lim_{B\to\infty}\nu_{B}(E)=\nu(E)\quad

for all continuity sets
E\subset\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
, and

(iii)

\limsup_{B\to\infty}\nu_{B}(E)\leqslant\nu(E)\quad

for all closed
E\subset\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
.

Moreover,

(iv)

let
f\colon\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a bounded, continuous function.
Let

(5.1)

N_{m,n}(f;B)=\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(\Lambda)\leqslant f(u(T_{\Lambda}))B\}.

Then

\frac{N_{m,n}(f;B)}{N_{m,n}(B)}\to\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu,\qquad B\to\infty.

Proof.

The statements
(i)
,
(ii)
,
and
(iii)
are equivalent. It suffices to
check
(ii)
on a basis of the topology closed under
finite intersections. Since the intersection of two boundary-controlled open
sets is boundary-controlled and open, and since all open balls along local
charts are boundary controlled, the assertion follows.

The last statement can be proved analogously to
[
15
, Prop. 3.3 (b)]
for continuous functions with compact support.
It follows for arbitrary bounded, continuous functions
f
using Prokhorov’s theorem, cutting off
f
outside progressively large compact subsets of
\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
.
∎

6.
Consequences of equidistribution

Equidistribution and freeness

As a first application of the equidistribution theorem, we reprove Theorem
1.3
.

Alternative proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Recall that
E_{\varepsilon}(B)=\#\Omega_{\varepsilon}(B)
, where

\Omega_{\varepsilon}(B)=\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):\ell(\Lambda)\leqslant\varepsilon,H(\Lambda)\leqslant B\}.

Let

\kappa=\limsup_{B\to\infty}\frac{E_{\varepsilon}(B)}{N_{m,n}(B)}.

We want to prove that
\kappa=0
.
Note that
\mu(\alpha L)=\mu(L)-\log\alpha
for all lattices
L
.
Applying this to quotient lattices yields
\mu_{\min}(u(L))=\mu_{\min}(L)-\mu(L)
.
Moreover, recall that the logarithmic anticanonical height verifies
h(\Lambda)=\log H(\Lambda)=-\log\covol(T_{\Lambda})
, whence

(6.1)

\mu(T_{\Lambda})=\frac{h(\Lambda)}{m(n-m)}.

Thus
\ell(\Lambda)\leqslant\varepsilon
is equivalent to

\mu_{\min}(u(T_{\Lambda}))\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon-1}{m(n-m)}h(\Lambda).

For
R>0
, set

\displaystyle\Omega^{(0)}(R)

\displaystyle=\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):H(\Lambda)\leqslant R\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\Omega^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}(R,B)

\displaystyle=\left\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):\mu_{\min}(u(T_{\Lambda}))\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon-1}{m(n-m)}\log R,\ H(\Lambda)\leqslant B\right\}.

We note that

\Omega_{\varepsilon}(B)\subset\Omega^{(0)}(R)\cup\Omega^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}(R,B),

for all
R>0
.
Clearly,

\limsup_{B\to\infty}\frac{\#\Omega^{(0)}(R)}{N_{m,n}(B)}=0.

To bound the number of elements of the second set, let

Z_{\varepsilon}(R)=\left\{L\in\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}:\mu_{\min}(L)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon-1}{m(n-m)}\log R\right\},

and note that

\Omega^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}(R,B)=\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n)(\mathbb{Q}):u(T_{\Lambda})\in Z_{\varepsilon}(R),\ H(\Lambda)\leqslant B\}.

Since
\mu_{\min}
is clearly continuous,

Z_{\varepsilon}(R)
is closed. It follows that

\kappa\leqslant\limsup_{B\to\infty}\frac{\#\Omega^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}(R,B)}{N_{m,n}(B)}\leqslant\nu(Z_{\varepsilon}(R)),

by Theorem
5.8

(iii)
.
The sets
Z_{\varepsilon}(R)
form a decreasing sequence whose intersection is empty, hence

\nu(Z_{\varepsilon}(R))\to 0
as
R\to\infty
, completing the proof.
∎

Counting by maximal slope

In
[
16
, § 9]
, Peyre puts forward an alternative way of involving
the well-shapedness of tangent lattices in point counting. He suggests ordering rational points
x

on a Fano variety
V
with a smooth integral model
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
by the maximal slope of
their tangent lattice, instead of by height, so as to count the quantity

N_{V}^{\mu_{\max}}(B)=\#\{x\in V(\mathbb{Q}):\mu_{\max}(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}},x})\leqslant\log B\}.

Since the logarithmic anticanonical height
h(x)=\log H(x)
verifies

h(x)=\dim(V)\mu(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}},x})\leqslant\dim(V)\mu_{\max}(\mathscr{T}_{V_{\mathbb{Z}},x}),

bounding the maximal slope automatically also bounds the anticanonical height,
yielding a trivial upper bound

N_{V}^{\mu_{\max}}(B)\leqslant N_{V}(B^{\dim V}),

where
N_{V}(B)=\{x\in V(\mathbb{Q}):H(x)\leqslant B\}
. For
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}=\Gr(1,n)
, this results in the upper bound

N_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}^{\mu_{\max}}(B)\leqslant c_{1,n}B^{n-1}(1+o(1)),

by (
1.2
). In
[
16
, Rem. 7.8]
, Peyre provides the lower bound

N_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}^{\mu_{\max}}(B)\gg_{\eta}B^{n-1-\eta}

for any
\eta>0
, and expects that
N_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}^{\mu_{\max}}(B)\sim c_{1,n}^{\prime}B^{n-1}

for a suitable constant
c_{1,n}^{\prime}>0
.
Theorem
1.4

confirms this expectation for projective space and provides an
analogous asymptotic formula for all other Grassmannians.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let

N^{\mu_{\max}}_{m,n}(B)=\#\{\Lambda\in\Gr(m,n):\mu_{\max}(T_{\Lambda})\leqslant\log B\}.

Recall that
\mu(\alpha L)=\mu(L)-\log\alpha
. Applying this
to all sublattices of
T_{\Lambda}
, we see that

\mu_{\max}(T_{\Lambda})=\mu_{\max}(u(T_{\Lambda}))+\mu(T_{\Lambda})
.
Using this and (
6.1
), the condition

e^{\mu_{\max}(T_{\Lambda})}\leqslant B
can be seen to be equivalent to

H(\Lambda)^{1/(m(n-m))}\leqslant e^{-\mu_{\max}(u(T_{\Lambda}))}B.

Hence

N^{\mu_{\max}}_{m,n}\left(B^{\frac{1}{m(n-m)}}\right)=N_{m,n}(f;B),

for
f=e^{-m(n-m)\mu_{\max}}
, in the notation of (
5.1
).
Since
\mu_{\max}
is obviously continuous, so is
f
, and since

\mu_{\max}(L)\geqslant 0
for unimodular
L
, the function
0\leqslant f\leqslant 1
is bounded.
An application of Theorem
5.8

(iv)
now yields

N^{\mu_{\max}}_{m,n}(B)\sim c_{m,n}^{\prime}B^{m(n-m)},

where

(6.2)

c_{m,n}^{\prime}=c_{m,n}\int_{L\in\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}}e^{-m(n-m)\mu_{\max}(L)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu.

It remains to prove that
0<c_{m,n}^{\prime}<c_{m,n}
.
Note that the non-empty open subsets
U_{1}=\{\mu_{\max}(L)<1\}
and

U_{2}=\{\mu_{\max}(L)>1\}
of
\mathscr{UG}_{m,n}
have positive measure.
Now
f|_{U_{1}}>e^{-m(n-m)}
, hence
\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu>0
. This implies that
c_{m,n}^{\prime}>0
.
From
f\leqslant 1
, we can deduce the inequality
\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\leqslant 1
,
and since
f|_{U_{2}}\leqslant e^{-m(n-m)}<1
, the inequality is strict, whence

c_{m,n}^{\prime}<c_{m,n}
.
∎
