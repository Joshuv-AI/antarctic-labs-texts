Convergence of Multiscale Reinforcement Q-Learning Algorithms for Mean Field Game and Control Problems
Andrea Angiuli, Jean-Pierre Fouque, Mathieu Laurière, Mengrui Zhang

Abstract




Abstract

We establish the convergence of the unified two-timescale Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithm presented in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
. This algorithm provides solutions to Mean Field Game (MFG) or Mean Field Control (MFC) problems depending on the ratio of two learning rates, one for the value function and the other for the mean field term.
Our proof of convergence highlights the fact that in the case of MFC several mean field distributions need to be updated and for this reason we present two separate algorithms, one for MFG and one for MFC.
We focus on a setting with finite state and action spaces, discrete time and infinite horizon. The proofs of convergence rely on
a generalization of the two-timescale approach of
[
Borkar, 1997
]
.
The accuracy of approximation to the true solutions depends on the smoothing of the policies. We provide a numerical example illustrating the convergence.

1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a type of machine learning technique that enables an agent to learn in an interactive environment by trial and error using feedback from its own actions and experiences, which is formalized through the concept of Markov Decision Process (MDP). For a general introduction, we refer to
[
Sutton and Barto, 2018
]
. In the past decade, reinforcement learning has attracted a growing interest and led to several breakthroughs in various fields from classical games, such as Atari
[
Mnih et al., 2015
]
or Go
[
Silver et al., 2016
]
, to robotics (see e.g.
[
Gu et al., 2017
,
Vecerik et al., 2017
]
), and more recently to applications in training models to follow human feedback (see e.g.
[
Ouyang et al., 2022
]
). While classical RL focuses on a single agent, multi-agent RL (MARL) aims at extending the paradigm to situations in which multiple agents learn while interacting. We refer to e.g.
[
Busoniu et al., 2008
,
Zhang et al., 2021
]
for more background, and to
[
Lanctot et al., 2017
,
Yang and Wang, 2020
]
for game-theoretic perspectives. Although great successes have been achieved, in particular in classical games, they remain mostly limited to situations with a small number of players. The scalability of model-free MARL methods in terms of the number of agents is a key challenge.

Concurrently, the past decade has witnessed the development of the theory of mean field games (MFGs), introduced by Lasry and Lions
[
Lasry and Lions, 2007
]
, and Caines, Huang and Malhame
[
Huang et al., 2006
]
. MFGs provide a framework to study games with a very large number of anonymous players interacting in a symmetric manner. The theory has been extensively investigated.
While the classical concept of MFG focuses on the notion of Nash equilibrium, it can also be relevant to consider the notions of social optimum, which gives rise to so-called mean field control (MFC) problems. We refer to
[
Bensoussan et al., 2013
,
Carmona and Delarue, 2018
]
for more background on this topic.

In the past few years, the question of learning solutions to MFGs and MFC problems using model-free RL methods has gained momentum. Various methods in different settings have been proposed in the literature; see
[
Laurière et al., 2022
]
for a survey. With the exception of
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
, which is the basis of the present paper, these methods focus on solving one of the two types of problems, MFG or MFC.
On the one hand, to learn MFGs solutions, two classical families of methods are those relying on strict contraction and fixed point iterations (e.g.,
[
Guo et al., 2019
,
Cui and Koeppl, 2021
,
Anahtarci et al., 2023
]
with tabular Q-learning or deep RL), and those relying on monotonicity and the structure of the game (e.g.,
[
Elie et al., 2020
,
Perrin et al., 2020
,
Laurière et al., 2022
]
using fictitious play and tabular or deep RL). Two-timescale analysis to learn MFG solutions has been used in
[
Mguni et al., 2018
,
Subramanian and Mahajan, 2019
]
. On the other hand, learning solutions to MFC amounts to solving an MDP in which the state is the mean field. This type of problems has been called mean field Markov decision processes and has been studied e.g. in
[
Carmona et al., 2023
,
Motte and Pham, 2022
,
Gu et al., 2021
,
Motte and Pham, 2023
]
. This leads to value-based RL methods such as Q-learning
[
Carmona et al., 2023
,
Gu et al., 2021
]
. Other methods include policy gradient
[
Carmona et al., 2019
]
, actor-critic methods
[
Frikha et al., 2023
]
or model-based RL methods
[
Pasztor et al., 2021
]
.

Recently,
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
proposed a common RL algorithm to solve MFGs and MFCs using a single algorithm by adjusting the learning rates. The algorithm iteratively updates an estimation of the population distribution and an estimation of the representative player’s value function. Depending on the rates at which these updates are done, the same algorithm can converge to the MFG solution or the MFC solution. At a high level, the method combines the classical Q-learning updates
[
Watkins, 1989
]
with updates of the mean field using a two-timescale scheme.

In this paper, we analyze multi-timescales algorithms that have been introduced and studied numerically in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
for mean field games and mean field control problems.
As in these references, the problems are studied in the context of finite state and action spaces, and in infinite time horizon. The key technical ingredient is the theory of stochastic approximation, see
[
Borkar, 1997
]
, and in particular results pertaining to multiple timescales. Algorithms used in model-free RL are typically asynchronous and based on samples. To simplify the presentation, we start by analyzing the convergence of an idealized algorithm which uses synchronous and expectation-based updates.
We then consider a version where the expected-based updates are replaced by sample-based updates.
Last, we study a realistic algorithm, with asynchronous, sample-based updates.
Intuitively, each of these two steps corresponds to incorporating one approximation: when the number of samples is large enough, sample-based updates mimic expectation-based updates, and when every state-action pair is visited frequently enough in an asynchronous method, the algorithm’s behavior is close to its synchronous counterparts. Our proof of convergence reveals that in the case of MFC, one needs to update multiple population distributions, one for each (state, action) point. For clarity, we write two separate algorithms, one for MFG and one for MFC, and we provide a proof of convergence for each.

Related works.

As mentioned above, this paper proves the convergence of the algorithms proposed in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
. The algorithms have been extended to the finite time horizon setting in

[
Angiuli et al., 2023b
]
and to deep actor-critic methods in
[
Angiuli et al., 2023a
]
.
Closely related to this family of methods, learning MFG solutions using RL and multiple timescales has been studied in

[
Mguni et al., 2018
,
Subramanian and Mahajan, 2019
]
. These methods focus purely on he MFG setting, while our method presents a unified algorithm for both MFG and MFC.
Besides the multi-timescale aspect, another feature of our work is the fact that the algorithms use the trajectory of a single representative player to learn the asymptotic mean field distribution.
Along a similar direction,
[
Zaman et al., 2023
]
proposed and analyzed an RL algorithm for MFGs in a setting where the representative agent does not have access to an oracle that can provide the mean field information.
Last, the question of approximating the solution to mean field dynamics using the trajectory of a single particle has been studied in
[
Du et al., 2023b
,
Du et al., 2023a
,
Du et al., 2023c
]
, in the continuous space setting.

(1)

Let

Classical Q-learning.

Classical RL aims at solving a Markov Decision Process (MDP) using the following setting. At each discrete time
n
, the agent observes her state
X_{n}
and chooses an action
A_{n}
based on it. Then the environment evolves and provides the agent with a new state
X_{n+1}
and reports a reward
r_{n+1}
. The goal of the agent is to find an optimal policy
\pi
that assigns to each state an optimal probability of actions in order to maximize the expected cumulative rewards. The problem can be recast as the problem of learning the optimal state-action value function, also called Q-function:
Q^{\pi}(x,a)
represents the expected cumulative discounted rewards when starting at state
x
, using an action
a
, and then following policy
\pi
. Mathematically,

Q^{\pi}(x,a)=\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{n}r_{n+1}|X_{0}=x,A_{0}=a\right],

where
r_{n+1}=r(X_{n},A_{n})
is the instantaneous reward,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is a discounting factor,
X_{n+1}
is distributed according to a transition probability which depends on
X_{n}
and the action drawn from
\pi(X_{n})
. The goal is to compute the optimal Q-function defined as:

Q^{*}(x,a)=\max_{\pi}Q^{\pi}(x,a).

To this end, the Q-learning method was introduced by
[
Watkins, 1989
]
. The basic idea is to iteratively sample an action
A_{n}\sim\pi(X_{n})
according to a behavior policy
\pi
, observe the induced state
X_{n+1}
and reward
r_{n+1}
, and then update the Q-table according to the formula:

Q(X_{n},A_{n})\leftarrow Q(X_{n},A_{n})+\rho\left[r_{n+1}+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(X_{n+1},a^{\prime})-Q(X_{n},A_{n})\right]

where
\rho\in(0,1]
is a learning rate.

Note that in our case, to be consistent with most of the MFG and MFC literature,
we will minimize costs instead of maximizing rewards
.

The notions of mean field game equilibrium and mean field control optimum will be presented in the following sections. For now, let us present the RL algorithm introduced in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
.

3
Mean Field Game

3.1
Mean Field Game Formulation

In the context of Asymptotic MFG introduced in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
, Section 2.2]
, we can view the problem faced by an infinitesimal agent among the crowd as an MDP parameterized by the population distribution. Hence, given a population distribution
\mu
, the Q-function for a single agent can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}_{\mu}(x,a):=f(x,a,\mu)+\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma^{n}f(X_{n}^{\pi,\mu},A_{n},\mu)\right],

(2)

where the expectation is over the randomness of
X^{\pi,\mu}
and
A
such that,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{0}^{\pi,\mu}=x,A_{0}=a,\\
X_{n+1}^{\pi,\mu}\sim p(\cdot|X_{n}^{\pi,\mu},A_{n},\mu),\qquad n\geq 0\\
A_{n}\sim\pi(\cdot|X_{n}^{\pi,\mu}),\qquad n\geq 0\end{cases}

where
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta^{|\mathcal{A}|}
is a policy taking the state
x
as input and outputting a probability distribution over the action set.
We denote by
\Pi
the set of all such policies.

The optimal Q-function is defined as:

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{\mu}(x,a):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}Q^{\pi}_{\mu}(x,a).

(3)

Since
\mu
is fixed, Equation (3.20) in
[
Sutton and Barto, 2018
]
can be applied, so that it satisfies the Bellman equation:

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{\mu}(x,a)=f(x,a,\mu)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*}_{\mu}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

(4)

In short, we denote this Bellman equation (
4
) by
Q^{*}_{\mu}=\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q^{*}_{\mu}
, where for every
\mu
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mu}
is defined as: for every
Q:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q:=f(x,a,\mu)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

To be more precise,
Q^{*}_{\mu}
is the fixed point of the Bellman operator
\mathcal{B}_{\mu}
. The Bellman equation (
4
) implies that there is at least one optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, i.e., such that:

Q^{\pi^{*}}_{\mu}(x,a)=Q^{*}_{\mu}(x,a),\qquad\hbox{ for all $(x,a)$.}

Indeed, we can take for
\pi^{*}
any policy such that the support of
\pi^{*}(\cdot|x)
is included in the set of actions that minimize
Q^{*}_{\mu}(x,\cdot)
.
However, there could be multiple optimal policies.

Now we introduce the notion of (mean field) Nash equilibrium.

Definition 3.1
.

(\hat{\mu},\hat{\pi})
forms a Nash equilibrium if the following two statements hold:

1.

(best response)
Q^{\hat{\pi}}_{\hat{\mu}}=Q^{*}_{\hat{\mu}}

2.

(consistency)
\hat{\mu}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(X_{n}^{\hat{\pi},\hat{\mu}})
.

To be more precise, the first statement means that
\hat{\pi}
is optimal for a representative infinitesimal player when the population distribution is
\hat{\mu}
, and the second statement means that
\hat{\mu}
is a fixed point of the function
\hat{\mu}\mapsto\mathrm{P}^{\hat{\pi},\hat{\mu}}\hat{\mu}
where
\mathrm{P}^{\hat{\pi},\hat{\mu}}
is the transition kernel according to the
\hat{\mu}
and policy
{\hat{\pi}}
, see (
1
).

3.2
Idealized Two-timescale Iteration Approach

In order to solve the problem we defined above (Definition
3.1
), let us first introduce an idealized deterministic two-timescale approach. We consider the following iterative procedure, where both variables are updated at each iteration but with different rates, denoted by

(5)

where

to be Lipschitz continuous. In order to obtain this property, we use
\softmin_{\phi}
instead of
\argmin
under function
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and we require Lipschitz continuity of
f
. By
[
Borkar, 1997
]
, we will obtain that, if
\rho^{\mu}_{n}/\rho^{Q}_{n}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
, the solution
(\mu_{n},Q_{n})_{n}
to the above iterations (
5
) has a behavior that is described (in a sense to be made precise later) by the following system of ODEs, in the regime where
\epsilon
is small and
t
goes to infinity:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q_{t},\mu_{t}),\\
\dot{Q}_{t}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q_{t},\mu_{t}),\end{cases}

(6)

where
\rho^{\mu}_{n}/\rho^{Q}_{n}
is thought of being of order
\epsilon\ll 1
.

3.3
Convergence of the Idealized Two-timescale Iteration Scheme

In this section, we will establish the convergence of the idealized two-timescale approach defined in (
5
).

We introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 3.2
.

The cost function
f
is bounded and is Lipschitz with respect to
\mu
, with Lipschitz constant denoted by
L_{f}
when using the
L^{1}
norm.
The transition kernel
p(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot,\mu)
is also Lipschitz with respect to
\mu
, with Lipschitz constant denoted by
L_{p}
when using the
L^{1}
norm. In other words, for every
x,a,\mu,\mu^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle|f(x,a,\mu)-f(x,a,\mu^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}=L_{f}\sum_{x}|\mu(x)-\mu^{\prime}(x)|,

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}}|p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)-p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{\prime})|=\|p(\cdot|x,a,\mu)-p(\cdot|x,a,\mu^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq L_{p}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}=L_{p}\sum_{x}|\mu(x)-\mu^{\prime}(x)|.

Under Assumption
3.2
, we now show that the functions
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
are Lipschitz continuous.

Proposition 3.3
.

If Assumption
3.2
holds, then
the function
(Q,\mu)\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu)
is Lipschitz with respect to both
Q
and
\mu
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{\prime},\mu)\|_{\infty}\leq\phi|\mathcal{A}|\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

(7)

and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq(L_{p}+2-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}^{\phi})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq(L_{p}+2-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},

(8)

where

\displaystyle c_{min}^{\phi}:=

\displaystyle\min_{x,x^{\prime},Q,\mu}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}(x^{\prime},x)=\min_{x,x^{\prime},Q,\mu}\sum_{a}{\softmin}_{\phi}Q(x^{\prime},a)p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x,x^{\prime},a,\mu}p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu)\sum_{a}{\softmin}_{\phi}Q(x^{\prime},a)=\min_{x,x^{\prime},a,\mu}p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu)=:c_{min}

(9)

Proof.

First, we note that for two policies
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{P}^{\pi,\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\pi^{\prime},\mu}\mu\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}|\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mu(x^{\prime})\sum_{a}\pi(a|x^{\prime})p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu)-\pi^{\prime}(a|x^{\prime})p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu))|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mu(x^{\prime})|\sum_{a}\sum_{x}(\pi(a|x^{\prime})p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu)-\pi^{\prime}(a|x^{\prime})p(x|x^{\prime},a,\mu))|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mu(x^{\prime})\sqrt{\sum_{a}1^{2}}\|\pi(x^{\prime})-\pi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}\sum_{x^{\prime}}\mu(x^{\prime})\|\pi(x^{\prime})-\pi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}\max_{x^{\prime}}\|\pi(x^{\prime})-\pi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}.

(10)

Applying this with
\pi(x)={\softmin}_{\phi}Q(x)
and
\pi^{\prime}(x)={\softmin}_{\phi}Q^{\prime}(x)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{\prime},\mu)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{\prime},\mu}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{1/2}\max_{x^{\prime}}\|{\softmin}_{\phi}Q(x^{\prime})-{\softmin}_{\phi}Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi\max_{x^{\prime}}\|Q(x^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}|\phi\max_{x^{\prime}}\|Q(x^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\phi|\mathcal{A}|\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

For
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, we first make the following remark: we can find
p(i,j)>\epsilon
, so that
1-N\epsilon>0
, and then define
q(i,j)=(p(i,j)-\epsilon)/(1-N\epsilon)
. Thus,
P=(1-N\epsilon)Q+\epsilon J
, where
J
is the
N\times N
matrix with all entries 1. We use this fact to calculate the total variation. Hence we have:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu})\|_{1}+\|\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\|_{1}+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}^{\phi})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}+L_{p}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(L_{p}+2-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}^{\phi})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(L_{p}+2-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}.

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

If Assumption
3.2
holds,
then the function
(Q,\mu)\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q,\mu)
is Lipschitz with respect to both
Q
and
\mu
.

Proof.

We note that:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q^{\prime},\mu)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q-\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|\cdot,\cdot,\mu)(\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}))\|_{\infty}+\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(\gamma+1)\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where we used the fact that

\|\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|\cdot,\cdot,\mu)(\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}))\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{x,a}\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)\|(\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}))\|_{\infty},

with
p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)\leq 1
and
\|(\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\prime}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}))\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. Hence:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q,\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|f(\cdot,\cdot,\mu)-f(\cdot,\cdot,\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}+\|\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}|p(x^{\prime}|\cdot,\cdot,\mu)-p(x^{\prime}|\cdot,\cdot,\mu^{\prime})||\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})|\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(L_{f}+\gamma L_{p}\|Q\|_{\infty})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{X}|(L_{f}+\gamma L_{p}\|Q\|_{\infty})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

∎

Now that we have these Lipschitz properties, we show that the second ODE for
Q_{t}
in the system (
6
) has a unique global asymptotically stable equilibrium (GASE).

Proposition 3.5
.

If Assumption
3.2
holds, then for any given
\mu
, the ODE
\dot{Q}_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q_{t},\mu)
has a unique GASE, that we will denote by
Q^{*}_{\mu}
. Moreover,
Q^{*}_{\mu}:\Delta^{|\mathcal{X}|}\to\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
is Lipchitz.

Proof.

We will use in the proof the fact that, by definition of the
Q
table,
\|Q^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\|f\|_{\infty}
.
By the procedure we used to prove Proposition
3.4
, we have
\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q-\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
, where
\gamma<1
implies that
Q\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q
is a strict contraction. As a result, by the contraction mapping theorem
[
Sell, 1973
]
, a unique GASE exists. Furthermore by
[
Borkar and Soumyanatha, 1997
, Theorem 3.1]
,
Q_{t}
will converge to it. We denote this unique GASE by
Q^{*}_{\mu}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|Q^{*}_{\mu}-Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q^{*}_{\mu}-\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{\prime}}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q^{*}_{\mu}-\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{\prime}}Q^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{\prime}}Q^{*}_{\mu}-\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{\prime}}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu)-\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}+\gamma\|Q^{*}_{\mu}-Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{X}|(L_{f}+\gamma L_{p}\|Q^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\gamma\|Q^{*}_{\mu}-Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{X}|(L_{f}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}L_{p}\|f\|_{\infty})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\gamma\|Q^{*}_{\mu}-Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}.

As a result, we have
\|Q^{*}_{\mu}-Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{L_{f}|\mathcal{X}|+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}L_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{X}|}{1-\gamma}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. So
Q^{*}_{\mu}
is uniformly Lipschitz with respect to
\mu
.
∎

Remark 3.11

In the literature, the question of non-uniqueness of a best response to a given mean field (and in particular, to the equilibrium mean field) is known to be challenging. A usual approach is to regularized the problem by introducing a penalty in a way that helps to ensure uniqueness of the optimal policy, see e.g.
[
Wang et al., 2020

in continuous time RL. We refer for instance to
[

Anahtarci et al., 2023

in the literature on learning mean field games. For instance, one can incorporate an entropy penalization in the reward, which encourages the policy to be distributed over all the actions, while keeping larger probabilities on the optimal actions. This is related to optimizing over soft-min policies, as we do in this paper. We leave the detailed analysis of the connection between the two approaches for future work.

We still use the notations introduced in Section

4.1
Mean Field Control Formulation

In the context of Asymptotic MFC introduced in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
, Section 2.2]
, we can consider modified Q-functions and population distributions. For an admissible policy
\pi
, we define the McKean–Vlasov-dynamics, MKV-dynamics for short, by
p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{\pi})
so that
\mu^{\pi}
is the limiting distribution of the associated process
(X_{n}^{\pi})
. We define the policy
\tilde{\pi}^{(x,a)}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{(x,a)}(x^{\prime}):=\begin{cases}\delta_{a}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{if}\hskip 14.22636ptx^{\prime}=x,\\
\pi(x)\hskip 5.69054pt\text{for}\hskip 8.53581ptx^{\prime}\neq x.\end{cases}

(13)

Note that the policy
\tilde{\pi}^{(x,a)}
depends on
(x,a)
although most of the time we will omit this explicit dependence in the notation.
Then, the modified Q-function is given by

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(x,a):=f(x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\pi}})+\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{n\geq 1}\gamma^{n}f(X_{n}^{\pi},A_{n},\mu^{\pi})\right],

(14)

where, for a given
(x,a)
,
\mu^{\tilde{\pi}}
is the limiting distribution of
X^{\tilde{\pi}}_{n}
, and where
the expectation is along the Markovian dynamics

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{0}^{\pi}=x,A_{0}=a,\\
X_{n+1}^{\pi}\sim p(\cdot|X_{n}^{\pi},A_{n},\mu^{\pi}),\\
A_{n}\sim{\pi}(X_{n}^{\pi}).\end{cases}

(15)

The optimal Q-function is defined as:

\displaystyle Q^{*}(x,a):=\inf_{\pi}Q^{\pi}(x,a).

(16)

Assumption 4.1
.

We assume that there exists a unique policy
\pi^{*}
such that:
Q^{*}(x,a):=Q^{\pi^{*}}(x,a)
for every
(x,a)
.
We also assume that for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\{a\in\mathcal{A}:Q^{*}(x,a)=\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*}(x,a^{\prime})\}
is a singleton, whose element is a pure control denoted by
\alpha^{*}(x)=\argmin_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*}(x,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}
. In other words, the policy
\pi^{*}
is a pure control
\alpha^{*}
.

Under Assumption
4.1
, by Theorem 2 in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
,
Q^{*}
satisfies the McKean–Vlasov Bellman equation:

\displaystyle Q^{*}(x,a)=f(x,a,\tilde{\mu}^{*})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\tilde{\mu}^{*})\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),

(17)

where

\tilde{\mu}^{*}:=\mu^{\tilde{\pi}^{*}}
is the invariant distribution associated to the policy
\tilde{\pi}^{*}
which implicitly depends on
(x,a)
and is defined by (
13
).

In short, we denote the Bellman equation (
17
) by
Q^{*}=\mathcal{B}Q^{*}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is defined for every
Q^{\pi}
and the limiting distribution
\mu^{\tilde{\pi}}
induced by the policy
\tilde{\pi}
by:

\mathcal{B}Q^{\pi}(x,a):=f(x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\pi}})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\pi}})\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\pi}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

(18)

As in the case of MFGs, we introduce the operator
\mathcal{B}_{\mu}
defined for every matrix
Q
and every probability distribution
\mu
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mu}Q(x,a):=f(x,a,\mu)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

(19)

So
\mathcal{B}
given by (
18
), and
\mathcal{B}_{\mu}
are related by:

\mathcal{B}Q^{\pi}=\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{\tilde{\pi}}}Q^{\pi}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
V^{\pi}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbbm{R}
be the value function for the policy
\pi
defined as:

V^{\pi}(x)=\mathbbm{E}\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}\gamma^{n}f(X_{n}^{\pi},A_{n},\mu^{\pi})|X_{0}^{\pi}=x\right]

following the dynamics (
15
).

If we consider a policy
\pi
which corresponds to a pure control
\alpha
, we have:

Lemma 4.3
.

[
Angiuli et al., 2022
, Lemma 3]
Letting
\pi(x)=\delta_{\alpha(x)}
, we have:

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(x)

\displaystyle=Q^{\pi}(x,\alpha(x)).

In turn, we define the optimal value function and we recall:

Theorem 4.4
.

[
Angiuli et al., 2022
, Theorem 5]

\displaystyle V^{*}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\pi}V^{\pi}(x)=\min_{a}Q^{*}(x,a).

Note that by taking
\delta_{\alpha^{*}(x)}=\pi^{*}(x)
in (
17
), one obtains the Bellman equation for
V^{*}=V^{\pi^{*}}
:

\displaystyle V^{*}(x)=f(x,\pi^{*}(x),\mu^{*})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}p(x^{\prime}|x,\pi^{*}(x),\mu^{*})V^{*}(x^{\prime}),

(20)

noting that
\pi^{*}
and
\mu^{*}
depend on
V^{*}
.

Definition 4.5
.

A pure control policy
\alpha^{*}
is a solution to the MFC problem if

V^{*}(x):=V^{\alpha^{*}}(x)=\inf_{\pi}V^{\pi}(x).

Lemma 4.6
.

The solution
\alpha^{*}
of the MFC problem can be characterized as follows:

1.

Define
Q^{*}
by

Q^{*}(x,a)=Q^{\alpha^{*}}(x,a)=f(x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}})V^{*}(x^{\prime}).

2.

Check that
\alpha^{*}(x)=\argmin_{a}Q^{*}(x,a)
, so that
Q^{*}
satisfies the Bellman equation (
17
).

4.2
Idealized Two-timescale Iteration Approach for MFC

We will solve the problem defined above using again a two-timescale approach as for MFG in Section
3.2
, but this time with learning rates in the regime
\rho^{Q}_{n}/\rho^{\mu}_{n}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. In the MFC case the idealized algorithm should keep track of a vector of probability distributions indexed by
(x,a)
, i.e., there is one distribution
\mu^{(x,a)}
for each
(x,a)
.

The finite difference equation system for the MFC problem becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu_{n+1}^{(x,a)}=\mu_{n}^{(x,a)}+\rho_{n}^{\mu}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{(x,a)}(Q_{n},\mu_{n}^{(x,a)}),\\
Q_{n+1}(x,a)=Q_{n}(x,a)+\rho^{Q}_{n}\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q_{n},\mu_{n}^{(x,a)})(x,a),\end{cases}

(21)

where the functions
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\to\Delta^{|\mathcal{X}|}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
are defined by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)(y)=\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq x}\mu(x^{\prime})p(y|x^{\prime},{\softmin}_{\phi}Q(x^{\prime}),\mu)+\mu(x)p(y|x,a,\mu)-\mu(y),\\
\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q,\mu)(x,a)=f(x,a,\mu)+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)\min_{a^{\prime}}Q(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q(x,a).\end{cases}

and where the first function can also be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)=\mu\mathrm{\tilde{P}}_{(x,a)}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu,

which defines
\mathrm{\tilde{P}}_{(x,a)}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}
.

Roughly speaking, by
[
Borkar, 1997
]
, in this regime, the solution
(\mu_{n},Q_{n})_{n}
to the
above iterations (
21
) has a behavior that is described (in a sense to be made precise later) by the following system of ODEs, in the regime where
\epsilon
is small and
t
goes to infinity:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\dot{\mu}^{(x,a)}_{t}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q_{t},\mu^{(x,a)}_{t}),\\
\dot{Q}_{t}(x,a)=\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q_{t},{\mu}^{(x,a)}_{t})(x,a),\end{cases}

(22)

where
\rho^{Q}_{n}/\rho^{\mu}_{n}
is thought of being of order
\epsilon\ll 1
.

4.3
Convergence of the Idealized Two-timescale Iteration for MFC

In this section, we establish the convergence of the idealized two-timescale approach defined in (
21
). In order to do it, let us introduce our additional assumptions:

Assumption 4.7
.

The learning rate
\rho^{Q}_{n}
and
\rho^{\mu}_{n}
are sequences of positive real numbers satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\rho^{Q}_{n}=\sum_{n}\rho^{\mu}_{n}=\infty,\qquad\sum_{n}|\rho^{Q}_{n}|^{2}+|\rho^{\mu}_{n}|^{2}<\infty,\qquad\rho^{Q}_{n}/\rho^{\mu}_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}0.

With Assumption
3.2
, we preserve Proposition
3.3
and Proposition
3.4
which become:

Proposition 4.8
.

If Assumption
3.2
holds, then
the function
(Q,\mu)\mapsto\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)
is Lipschitz with respect to both
Q
and
\mu
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q^{\prime},\mu)\|_{\infty}\leq\phi|\mathcal{A}|\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

(23)

and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq(L_{p}+2-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}.

(24)

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q^{\prime},\mu)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\mu\mathrm{\tilde{P}}_{(x,a)}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu\mathrm{\tilde{P}}_{(x,a)}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{\prime},\mu}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{\prime},\mu}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\phi|\mathcal{A}|\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

and,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu)-\mathcal{\tilde{P}}^{(x,a)}_{2}(Q,\mu^{\prime})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu\mathrm{\tilde{P}}_{(x,a)}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{\tilde{P}}_{(x,a)}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\|_{1}+\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(L_{p}+2-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}.

∎

Now that we have this Lipschitz property, we show that the ODEs for
\mu^{(x,a)}_{t}
in the system (
22
) have unique global asymptotically stable equilibria (GASE).

Proposition 4.9
.

Suppose Assumption
3.6
hold. For any given
Q
,
{\dot{\mu}_{t}}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(Q,\mu_{t})
has a unique GASE, that will denote by
{\mu}^{*\phi}_{Q}
(where we omit the dependency on
(x,a)
). Moreover,
{\mu}_{Q}^{*\phi}:\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\to\Delta^{|\mathcal{X}|},Q\mapsto\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.8
, we have
\|\mu\mathrm{\tilde{P}}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}-\mu^{\prime}\mathrm{\tilde{P}}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\|_{1}\leq(L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}
, where
L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}<1
implies that
\mu\mapsto\mathrm{\tilde{P}}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}\mu
is a strict contraction. As a result, by contraction mapping theorem
[
Sell, 1973
]
, a unique GASE exists and furthermore, by
[
Borkar and Soumyanatha, 1997
, Theorem 3.1]
, it is the limit of
\mu_{t}
denoted by
{\mu}^{*\phi}_{Q}
(at each
(x,a)
). Then,

\displaystyle\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{\prime},\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}}\|_{1}+\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{\prime},\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}})-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{\prime},\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}})\|_{1}+(L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\phi|\mathcal{A}|\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{1}+(L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{1}.

As a result, we have
\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}-\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\phi|\mathcal{A}|}{|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}-L_{p}}\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{1}
, which is the uniform Lipschitz property of
\mu^{*\phi}_{Q}
with respect to
Q
for each
(x,a)
.
∎

Assumption 4.10
.

The second ODE in the system (
22
) along the GASE of the first ODEs, that is

\dot{Q}_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(Q_{t},{\mu^{*\phi}_{Q_{t}}})
, has a unique GASE that we will denote by
Q^{*\phi}
.

In Appendix
C
, we show how to derive the existence and uniqueness of such a GASE using a contraction argument. However, as in the MFG case, this argument requires an upper bound on the choice of the parameter
\phi
which limits its use in our context of deriving the convergence of our algorithm to a solution of the MFC problem. Therefore, in what follows we work under Assumption
4.10
which could alternatively be verified by means of Lyapunov functions for instance.

Let
V^{*\phi}(x)=\min_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*\phi}(x,a^{\prime})
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. From (
21
), we define
V_{n}(x)=\min_{a^{\prime}}Q_{n}(x,a^{\prime})
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then we have the following convergence result.

Theorem 4.11
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
,
3.6
,
4.7
and
4.10
hold. Then,
(\mu_{n},V_{n})
defined above converges to
(\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}},V^{*\phi})
as
n\to\infty
, where we recall that
\mu_{n}
and
\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}
depend on
(x,a)
.

Proof.

With Assumption
3.2
,
3.6
,
4.7
,
4.10
, and the results of Propositions
3.3
,
3.4
, and
4.9
, the assumptions of
[
Borkar, 1997
, Theorem 1.1]
are satisfied, and therefore guarantees the convergence in the statement.
∎

Next, we show that the limit point given by the algorithm, that is
(\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}},V^{*\phi})
, provides an approximation of an MFC solution as follows.

Definition 4.12
.

Construction of an MFC solution:

1.

Set
\alpha^{*}(x)=\argmin_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*\phi}(x,a^{\prime})

2.

For each
(x,a)
, define
\tilde{\alpha}^{*}
by (
13
) and compute
\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}}
solution to

\mu(y)=\sum_{x^{\prime}\neq x}\mu(x^{\prime})p(y|x^{\prime},\alpha^{*}(x^{\prime}),\mu)+\mu(x)p(y|x,a,\mu),\quad\forall y.

3.

Compute
V^{\alpha^{*}}
by using Definition
4.2
.

The following result shows that
(\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}},V^{*\phi})
are close to
(\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}},V^{\alpha^{*}})
.

Theorem 4.13
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
,
3.6
,
4.7
and
4.10
hold.
Let
(\mu^{*},V^{\alpha^{*}})
given by Definition
4.12
.
Let
\delta(\phi)
be the action gap defined as
\delta(\phi)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}(\min_{a\notin\argmin_{a}Q^{*\phi}}Q^{*\phi}(x,a)-\min_{a}Q^{*\phi}(x,a))>0
, and
\delta(\phi)=\infty
if
Q^{*\phi}(x)
is constant with respect to
a
for each
x
.
Then,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}-\mu^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{2|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp(-\phi\delta(\phi))}{|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}-L_{p}},\\
\|V^{*\phi}-V^{\alpha^{*}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{(L_{f}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}L_{p}\|f\|_{\infty})2|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp(-\phi\delta(\phi))}{(1-\gamma)(|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}-L_{p})}.\end{cases}

(25)

Proof.

We follow the same lines as in the proof of Theorem
3.14
. We have:

\displaystyle\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}-\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|{\softmin}_{\phi}Q^{*\phi}(x)-\argmin Q^{*\phi}(x)\|_{2}+(L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}-\mu^{*}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2|\mathcal{A}|^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp(-\phi\delta(\phi))+(L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu^{*\phi}-\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}}\|_{1},

which gives the first inequality in (
25
).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\|V^{*\phi}-V^{\alpha^{*}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}}V^{*\phi}-\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{*}}V^{\alpha^{*}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}}V^{*\phi}-\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}}V^{\alpha^{*}}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}}V^{\alpha^{*}}-\mathcal{B}_{\mu^{*}}V^{\alpha^{*}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|V^{*\phi}-V^{\alpha^{*}}\|_{\infty}+(L_{f}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}L_{p}\|f\|_{\infty})\|\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}-\mu^{*}\|_{1},

which gives the second inequality in (
25
).
∎

We are now ready for the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.14
.

Under the assumptions in Theorem
4.13
, and additionally
\delta=\liminf_{\phi\to\infty}\delta(\phi)>0
,
the pure policy
\alpha^{*}
defined by item 1 in Definition
4.12
is a solution to the MFC problem characterized in Lemma
4.6
.

Proof.

We use the characterization of a solution given by Lemma
4.6
. Define
Q^{*}
by

Q^{*}(x,a)=Q^{\alpha^{*}}(x,a)=f(x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}})V^{\alpha^{*}}(x^{\prime}).

Then, we need to check that
\alpha^{*}(x)=\argmin_{a}Q^{*}(x,a)
.

From our algorithm (
21
) we have

f(x,a,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}})V^{*\phi}(x^{\prime})>V^{*\phi}(x)\quad\text{for}\quad a\neq\alpha^{*}(x).

The difference between the left-hand side and the right-hand side above is larger than the gap
\delta(\phi)
. Under the condition that the errors in
(
25
) are small enough (by choosing
\phi
large enough), we can replace
V^{*\phi}
by
V^{\alpha^{*}}
and
\mu^{*\phi}_{Q^{*\phi}}
by
\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}}
and obtain:

f(x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}})+\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}})V^{\alpha^{*}}(x^{\prime})>V^{\alpha^{*}}(x)\quad\text{for}\quad a\neq\alpha^{*}(x),

i.e.
Q^{*}(x,a)>V^{*}(x)
for
a\neq\alpha^{*}(x)
. Recalling Lemma
4.6
,
\alpha^{*}
is indeed an optimal policy.
∎

5
Example

We present a very simple example to illustrate the performance of our algorithm with comparison with the explicit solutions for the MFG and MFC problems.

5.1
The Model

Let us consider the state space
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{0}=0,x_{1}=1\}
and the action space
\mathcal{A}=\{stay=0,move=1\}
. The markovian dynamics is given by

p(x^{\prime}|x,a)=\begin{cases}1-p,&\hbox{ if }x^{\prime}=a(x)\\
p,&\hbox{ if }x^{\prime}\neq a(x),\end{cases}

where
0<p<1

represents a small noise parameter, and where as such we impose
0<p<0.5
. Note that the dynamics does not depend on the population distribution
\mu
and therefore, in the notation of the previous sections,
L_{p}=0
.

Let us consider the cost function:

f(x,a,\mu)=x+c\mu_{0},

where
c>0
and
\mu_{0}
is the probability to be at
x_{0}
under the distribution
\mu
(one may think of
\mu_{0}
as
1-\bar{\mu}
). Note that

|f(x,a,\mu)-f(x,a,\mu^{\prime})|=c|\mu_{0}-\mu^{\prime}_{0}|\leq L_{f}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},

with
L_{f}=c/2
.

Recall that
c_{min}
is defined in Proposition
3.3
. In this example we have:

c_{min}=\min_{x,x^{\prime},a,\mu}p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu)=p>0.

Therefore, in this example Assumptions
3.2
and
3.6
are satisfied.

In sections
5.2.2
and
5.2.4
we will show that the other assumptions are satisfied.
Thus, in this example, depending on the learning rates satisfying either Assumption
3.8
for MFG or Assumption
4.7
for MFC, we can apply
either Theorem
3.15
or Theorem
4.14
.

5.2
Theoretical Solutions

There are 4 pure policies
\alpha
given by:

\{(s,s),(s,m),(m,s),(m,m)\},

associated to the corresponding limiting distributions
\mu^{\alpha}
:

\{(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}),(1-p,p),(p,1-p),(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\}.

5.2.1
MFG

For a given distribution
\mu
, the Q-Bellman equation reads:

Q^{*}_{\mu}(x,a)=f(x,a,\mu)+\gamma\left[(1-p)Q^{*}_{\mu}(a(x),s)\wedge Q^{*}_{\mu}(a(x),m)+pQ^{*}_{\mu}(1-a(x),s)\wedge Q^{*}_{\mu}(1-a(x),m)\right].

One can check that the optimal strategy is
\alpha^{*}=(s,m)
which corresponds to the case

Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,s)<Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,m),\quad Q^{*}_{\mu}(1,m)<Q^{*}_{\mu}(1,s).

This is independent of the distribution
\mu
which affects
Q
only as an additive shift, so that the fixed point in the MFG problem is achieved by taking
\mu^{*}=\mu^{\alpha^{*}}=\mu^{(s,m)}=(1-p,p)
. The optimal Q-values are given by

Q^{*}(0,s)=\frac{c(1-p)+\gamma p}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,m)=Q^{*}(0,s)+1,

Q^{*}(0,m)=\frac{c(1-p)+\gamma^{2}(2p-1)+\gamma(1-p)}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,s)=Q^{*}(0,m)+1,

so that
Q^{*}(0,m)-Q^{*}(0,s)=Q^{*}(1,s)-Q^{*}(1,m)=\gamma(1-2p)>0
as
0<p<0.5
.

The computation above shows that there is unique solution to the MFG, so that Assumption
3.10
is satisfied.

5.2.2
Verification of Assumption
3.7
and lower bound for the gap in Theorem
3.15

From above, for any given
\mu
, we have the following statement

Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,s)=\frac{c(1-\mu_{1})+\gamma p}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}_{\mu}(1,m)=Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,s)+1,

Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,m)=\frac{c(1-\mu_{1})+\gamma^{2}(2p-1)+\gamma(1-p)}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}_{\mu}(1,s)=Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,m)+1,

so that
Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,m)-Q^{*}_{\mu}(0,s)=Q^{*}_{\mu}(1,s)-Q^{*}_{\mu}(1,m)=\gamma(1-2p)>0
as
0<p\ll 1
.

Then we can compute the soft-min policy
\pi
defined by
\pi(a|x)={\softmin}_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu}(x)(a)
.

It is independent of
\mu
, so that:

\pi(s|0)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))},\quad\pi(m|0)=\frac{\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))},

\pi(s|1)=\frac{\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))},\quad\pi(m|1)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}.

Then, we compute the GASE
\mu^{*\phi}
in Assumption
3.7
by solving
\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu^{*\phi}},\mu^{*\phi})=0
. Since we have two states, it is enough to write this equation at one point, say
x=1
.

\displaystyle\mu^{*\phi}_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-\mu^{*\phi}_{1})\left[\pi(s|0)p(1|0,s)+\pi(m|0)p(1|0,m)\right]+\mu^{*\phi}_{1}\left[\pi(s|1)p(1|1,s)+\pi(m|1)p(1|1,m)\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\mu^{*\phi}_{1})\left[p\pi(s|0)+(1-p)\pi(m|0)\right]+\mu^{*\phi}_{1}\left[(1-p)\pi(s|1)+p\pi(m|1)\right]

\displaystyle=(p\pi(s|0)+(1-p)\pi(m|0))-\mu^{*\phi}_{1}(p\pi(s|0)+(1-p)\pi(m|0))+\mu^{*\phi}_{1}((1-p)\pi(s|1)+p\pi(m|1))

\displaystyle=p\pi(s|0)+(1-p)\pi(m|0)

\displaystyle=p\pi(s|0)+(1-p)(1-\pi(s|0))

\displaystyle=(1-p)-(1-2p)\pi(s|0)

\displaystyle=(1-p)\left[\frac{\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}\right]+p\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}\right]\in(0,0.5)\quad\mbox{for}\quad p<0.5.

It remains to prove that the unique
\mu^{*\phi}
defined above is a GASE for
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
Let us define the function

g(x)=(1-p)\left[\frac{\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}\right]+p\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\phi\gamma(1-2p))}\right]-x,

so that
g(\mu^{*\phi}_{1})=0
for
\mu^{*\phi}_{1}\in(0,0.5)
. We have
g(x)>0
for
0<x<\mu^{*\phi}_{1}
and
g(x)<0
for
\mu^{*\phi}_{1}<x<0.5
. Therefore,
L(x)=(x-\mu^{*\phi}_{1})^{2}
is
a Lyapunov function for the ODE
\dot{y}=g(y)
since
L^{\prime}(y)g(y)<0
and
L^{\prime}(\mu^{*\phi}_{1})g(\mu^{*\phi}_{1})=0
. Repeating the argument with
\mu^{*\phi}_{0}=1-\mu^{*\phi}_{1}
, one deduces that
\mu^{*\phi}
is the unique GASE in Assumption
3.7
.

Regarding the gap in Theorem
3.15
, we have:

\delta(\phi)=\min\left(Q^{*}_{\mu^{*\phi}}(0,m)-Q^{*}_{\mu^{*\phi}}(0,s),Q^{*}_{\mu^{*\phi}}(1,s)-Q^{*}_{\mu^{*\phi}}(1,m)\right)=\gamma(1-2p)=\delta>0,

for
p<0.5.

5.2.3
MFC

As in Section
4.1
, for a strategy
\alpha
, we define the strategy
\tilde{\alpha}^{(x,a)}
by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}^{(x,a)}(x^{\prime}):=\begin{cases}a\hskip 8.53581pt\text{if}\hskip 14.22636ptx^{\prime}=x,\\
\alpha(x^{\prime})\hskip 5.69054pt\text{for}\hskip 8.53581ptx^{\prime}\neq x.\end{cases}

Then, the MKV-Q-Bellman equation reads:

Q^{*}(x,a)=f(x,a,\mu^{\tilde{\alpha^{*}}(x,a)})+\gamma\left[(1-p)Q^{*}(a(x),s)\wedge Q^{*}(a(x),m)+pQ^{*}(1-a(x),s)\wedge Q^{*}(1-a(x),m)\right].

One can check that the optimal strategy is
\alpha^{*}=(m,s)
which corresponds to the case

Q^{*}(0,m)<Q^{*}(0,s),\quad Q^{*}(1,s)<Q^{*}(1,m),

and to the distribution
\mu^{*}=(p,1-p)
.
The optimal Q-values are given by

Q^{*}(0,m)=\frac{cp-\gamma p+\gamma}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,s)=Q^{*}(0,m)+1,

Q^{*}(0,s)=\frac{c}{2}+\gamma\frac{cp-2\gamma p+\gamma+p}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,m)=Q^{*}(0,s)+1,

so that

In the previous section, we introduced an idealized deterministic algorithm relying on the operators
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, which involve expectations that we assumed we could compute perfectly. However, in practical situations, those expectations are unknown and instead we use samples. Then we can use the classical theory of stochastic approximation to obtain convergence of algorithms with sample-based updates. In this section, we assume that the updates are still done in a synchronous way. The case of asynchronous updates is discussed in the next section.

In this section, we consider the following setting. Let us assume that for any
(\mu,x,a)

\displaystyle X^{{}^{\prime}}_{x,a,\mu}\sim p(\cdot|x,a,\mu),

and we define the system as follows for MFG,

\displaystyle\mu_{n+1}=\mu_{n}+\rho_{n,X_{n},A_{n}}^{\mu}(\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q_{n},\mu_{n})+\mathbf{P}_{n})

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(X_{n},A_{n})=Q_{n}(X_{n},A_{n})+\rho^{Q}_{n,X_{n},A_{n}}({\mathcal{T}_{2}}(Q_{n},{\mu}_{n})(X_{n},A_{n})+\mathbf{T}_{n}(X_{n},A_{n}))

(x,a)

In turns, for MFC, we define the following system,

\displaystyle\mu^{(x,a)}_{n+1}=\mu^{(x,a)}_{n}+\rho_{n,X^{(x,a)}_{n},A^{(x,a)}_{n}}^{\mu}(\mathcal{\tilde{P}}_{2}(Q_{n},\mu^{(x,a)}_{n})+\mathbf{P}^{(x,a)}_{n})

(x,a)

(x,a)

•

\sum_{k}\rho^{Q}_{k,x,a}=\infty,

\sum_{k}|\rho^{Q}_{k,x,a}|^{2}<\infty.

(ii)
\rho^{Q}_{k+1,x,a}\leq\rho^{Q}_{k,x,a}

•

\sum_{k}(\rho^{Q}_{k,x,a})^{(1+q)}<\infty,\hskip 20.0pt\forall q\geq r.

(iv) for

\sup_{k}\frac{\rho^{Q}_{[\xi k],x,a}}{\rho^{Q}_{k,x,a}}<\infty,

•

(v) for

D(k):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\rho^{Q}_{\ell,x,a}

\frac{D([yk])}{D(k)}\to 1,

uniformly in

.

In the asynchronous setting, we update step-by-step the Q table along the trajectory generated randomly. In order to ensure convergence, every state-action pair needs to be updated repeatedly. We introduce
\nu(x,a,n)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{(X_{m},A_{m})=(x,a)\}}

(x,a)

Assumption 7.3

\Delta>0

(x,a)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\nu(x,a,n)}{n}\geq\Delta\>\>\>\>\>a.s.

Furthermore, letting

N(n,\xi)=\min\left\{m>n:\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}\rho^{Q}_{k,x,a}>\xi\right\}

for

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided precise assumptions ensuring the convergence of the two-timescale RL algorithm proposed in
[
Angiuli et al., 2022
]
and we clarified that in the MFC case, one population distribution for each state/action point needs to be updated. The proof relies on the theory of stochastic approximation. In order to use a two-timescale argument as in
[
Borkar, 1997
]
, we smooth the strategies using a soft-min function to regularize the dependency on the population distributions. One of the main properties of the algorithms is that they are characterized by the ratio of two learning rates leading to the solution of a Mean Field Game problem or of a Mean Field Control problem. To illustrate this key fact, we give an example satisfying the sets of assumptions for the two problems.

Appendix A
Lipschitz property of the 2 scale operators

We define the
\softmin_{\phi}
function in the following way:

\displaystyle{\softmin}_{\phi}(z)=\left(\frac{e^{-\phi z_{i}}}{\sum_{j}e^{-\phi z_{j}}}\right)_{i=1,2,...,|\mathcal{A}|}

(35)

where
z\in\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
. By
[
Gao and Pavel, 2018
]
,
\softmin_{\phi}
is Lipschitz continuous for the
L^{2}
-norm and it is
\phi
-Lipschitz,

\displaystyle\|{\softmin}_{\phi}(z)-{\softmin}_{\phi}(z^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\phi\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{2},\hskip 20.0ptz,z^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}

(36)

Moreover, since
|\mathcal{A}|
is finite, all the norms on
\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
are equivalent, so that

\displaystyle\|{\softmin}_{\phi}(z)-{\softmin}_{\phi}(z^{\prime})\|_{1}\leq\phi\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|}\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{1}

(37)

We also define an
\argmine
function in the following way:

\displaystyle\argmine(z)_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{k}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{if }z_{i}=\min z\\
0\hskip 28.45274pt\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
i=1,2,...,|\mathcal{A}|
. To be more clear,
\argmine
will assign equal to probability to the minimal value in
z
, and
0
probability to others. Then we know that as
\phi\to 0
, the
\softmin_{\phi}
function is smoother and smoother and the corresponding Lipschitz coefficient
L_{s}(\phi)\to 0
. On the opposite, the
\softmin_{\phi}
will converge to
\argmine
as
\phi\to\infty
.

To alleviate the notation, we will write
Q(x):=(Q(x,a))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
for any
Q\in\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
, which implies
Q(x),\softmin_{\phi}Q(x),\argmine Q(x)\in\mathbbm{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
. We also introduce a more general transition kernel, which can take an input of a probability vector over actions instead of a single action: for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\pi\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{A}|},\mu\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{X}|}
,

\displaystyle p(x^{\prime}|x,\pi,\mu)=\sum_{a}\pi(x)(a)p(x^{\prime}|x,a,\mu).

(38)

We also define:

\displaystyle f(x,\pi,\mu)=\sum_{a}\pi(x)(a)f(x,a,\mu).

(39)

Appendix B
Proof of existence and uniqueness of a GASE to the first O.D.E. for
\mu_{t}
in (
6
)

To prove that the GASE exists for the first O.D.E. in (
6
), we will use a strict contraction property, which requires to control
\phi
properly. We introduce the additional assumption:

Assumption B.1
.

0<\phi<\frac{|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}-L_{p}}{|\mathcal{A}|}\frac{1-\gamma}{L_{f}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}L_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}}=:\phi_{max}
.

With this assumption, we have
(\phi|\mathcal{A}|\frac{L_{f}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}L_{p}\|f\|_{\infty}}{1-\gamma}+L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})<1
. We also have
(\phi|\mathcal{A}|\frac{L_{f}+\gamma L_{p}\|Q^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}}{1-\gamma}+L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})<1
, which will give the strict contraction property we need in the next proposition.

Proposition B.2
.

Under Assumptions
3.2
,
3.6
and
B.1
,
\dot{\mu}_{t}=\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu_{t}},\mu_{t})
has a unique GASE, that we will denote by
\mu^{*\phi}
.

Proof.

We show that
\mu\mapsto\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu}\mu
is a strict contraction. First, recall that, from Proposition
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}\mu^{\prime})\|_{1}+\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu}\mu^{\prime}-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q,\mu^{\prime}}\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min}^{\phi})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}+L_{p}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+L_{p}-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}.

We have:

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu^{\prime}}\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu}\mu\|_{1}+\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu^{\prime}}\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu)\|_{1}+\|\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu}\mu-\mathrm{P}^{\softmin_{\phi}Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu^{\prime}}\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu},\mu)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}},\mu)\|_{1}+(1+L_{p}-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\phi|\mathcal{A}|\|Q^{*}_{\mu}-Q^{*}_{\mu^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+(1+L_{p}-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(\phi|\mathcal{A}|\frac{L_{f}+\gamma L_{p}\|Q^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}}{1-\gamma}+L_{p}+1-|\mathcal{X}|c_{min})\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},

which shows the strict contraction property under Assumption
B.1
. As a result, by contraction mapping theorem
[
Sell, 1973
]
, a unique GASE exists and furthermore by
[
Borkar and Soumyanatha, 1997
, Theorem 3.1]
,
\mu_{t}
will converge to it.
∎

Appendix D
MFC Optimal Policy

Recall that we assume
c>2\gamma
and
p<0.5
. Let us consider a general policy
\pi
characterized by
p_{0s}
and
p_{1s}
in
[0,1]
:

\pi(s|0)=p_{0s},\quad\pi(s|1)=p_{1s}.

Then, we can compute the limiting/invariant distributions under the policies
\tilde{\pi}^{(x,a)}
characterized by the probabilities to be at state
1
:

\mu_{1}^{(0,s)}=\frac{p}{1-(1-2p)\pi(s|1)},\quad\mu_{1}^{(0,m)}=\frac{1-p}{2-2p-(1-2p)\pi(s|1)},

\mu_{1}^{(1,s)}=\frac{1-p-(1-2p)\pi(s|0)}{1-(1-2p)\pi(s|0)},\quad\mu_{1}^{(1,m)}=\frac{1-p-(1-2p)\pi(s|0)}{2-2p-(1-2p)\pi(s|0)}.

1. First case: if
Q^{\pi}(0,s)-Q^{\pi}(0,m)>0
and
Q^{\pi}(1,m)-Q^{\pi}(1,s)>0
, by the Bellman equation
Q^{\pi}=\mathcal{B}Q^{\pi}
as defined in (
18
),
we can compute
Q^{\pi}
:

Q^{\pi}(0,m)=\frac{c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+\gamma(1-p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)})}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{\pi}(1,s)=Q^{\pi}(0,m)+1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)},

Q^{\pi}(0,s)=c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+\gamma\frac{c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+\gamma(1-p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)})}{1-\gamma}+\gamma p(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}),

Q^{\pi}(1,m)=Q^{\pi}(0,s)+1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)}.

Then by
Q^{*}(x,a):=\inf_{\pi}Q^{\pi}(x,a)
, we deduce that
p_{1s}=1
and
p_{0s}=0
(corresponding to the pure policy
(m,s)
), so that:

Q^{*}(0,m)=\frac{cp-\gamma p+\gamma}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,s)=Q^{*}(0,m)+1,

Q^{*}(0,s)=\frac{c}{2}+\gamma\frac{cp-2\gamma p+\gamma+p}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,m)=Q^{*}(0,s)+1,

implying that our starting conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Q^{*}(0,s)-Q^{*}(0,m)=c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}-\gamma(1-2p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)})=\frac{1}{2}(1-2p)(c-2\gamma)>0,\\
Q^{*}(1,m)-Q^{*}(1,s)=c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)}-c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}-\gamma(1-2p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)})=\frac{1}{2}(1-2p)(c-2\gamma)>0.\end{cases}

2. Second case: if
Q^{\pi}(0,s)-Q^{\pi}(0,m)<0
and
Q^{\pi}(1,m)-Q^{\pi}(1,s)<0
,
we can compute
Q^{\pi}
,

Q^{\pi}(0,s)=\frac{c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+\gamma p(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)})}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{\pi}(1,m)=Q^{\pi}(0,s)+1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)},

Q^{\pi}(0,m)=c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+\gamma\frac{c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+\gamma p(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)})}{1-\gamma}+\gamma(1-p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)}),

Q^{\pi}(1,s)=Q^{\pi}(0,m)+1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}.

Then by
Q^{*}(x,a):=\inf_{\pi}Q^{\pi}(x,a)
, we get that
p_{1s}=1
and
p_{0s}=0
, so that

Q^{*}(0,s)=\frac{c/2+\gamma p}{1-\gamma},\quad Q^{*}(1,m)=Q^{*}(0,s)+1,

Q^{*}(0,m)=cp+\gamma\frac{cp+\gamma p}{1-\gamma}+\gamma(1-p),\quad Q^{*}(1,s)=Q^{*}(0,m)+1.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+\gamma(1-2p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)})=\frac{1}{2}(1-2p)(2\gamma-c)<0\\
c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}-c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)}+\gamma(1-2p)(1-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)})=\frac{1}{2}(1-2p)(2\gamma-c)<0\end{cases}

as
2\gamma<c
which breaks our assumption. As a result, under this case, we do not have a better policy than
(m,s)
obtained in the first case..

3. Third Case: if
Q^{\pi}(0,s)-Q^{\pi}(0,m)<0
and
Q^{\pi}(1,s)-Q^{\pi}(1,m)<0
,
we can compute
Q^{\pi}
as,

Q^{\pi}(0,s)=\frac{(1-\gamma+\gamma p)c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+\gamma p(c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}+1)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma(1-2p))},\quad Q^{\pi}(1,m)=Q^{\pi}(0,s)-c\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(1,m)}+1,

Q^{\pi}(1,s)=\frac{(1-\gamma+\gamma p)(c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}+1)+\gamma pc\mu_{0}^{(0,s)}}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma(1-2p))},\quad Q^{\pi}(0,m)=Q^{\pi}(1,s)-1-c\mu_{0}^{(1,s)}+c\mu_{0}^{(0,m)}.

Then by
Q^{*}(x,a):=\inf_{\pi}Q^{\pi}(x,a)
, we get that
p_{1s}=1
and
p_{0s}=0
, so that

Q^{*}(0,s)=\frac{(1-\gamma+\gamma p)\frac{c}{2}+\gamma p(cp+1)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma(1-2p))},\quad Q^{*}(1,m)=\frac{(1-\gamma+\gamma p)\frac{c}{2}+\gamma p(cp+1)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma(1-2p))}+1,

and

Q^{*}(0,m)-Q^{*}(0,s)=(1-2p)(\gamma-\frac{c}{2})-1<0

which is a contradiction. As a result, under this case, we do not have a better policy than
(m,s)
.

Similarly we do not have a better policy than
(m,s)
for case
Q^{\pi}(0,s)-Q^{\pi}(0,m)>0
and
Q^{\pi}(1,m)-Q^{\pi}(1,s)>0
.

In conclusion, we have shown that the unique solution to the MFC problem is the pure policy
(m,s)
,
so that Assumption
4.1
