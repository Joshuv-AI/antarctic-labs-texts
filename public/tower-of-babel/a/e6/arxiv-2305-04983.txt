Low-Degree Testing Over Grids Thanks: A conference version of this paper has appeared in RANDOM 2023.
Prashanth Amireddy, Srikanth Srinivasan, Madhu Sudan

Abstract
We study the question of local testability of low (constant) degree functions from a product domain 𝒮 1 × ⋯ × 𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n} to a field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} , where 𝒮 i ⊆ 𝔽 \mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F} can be arbitrary constant sized sets. We show that this family is locally testable when the grid is “symmetric”. That is, if 𝒮 i = 𝒮 \mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{S} for all i i , there is a probabilistic algorithm using constantly many queries that distinguishes whether f f has a polynomial representation of degree at most d d or is Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) -far from having this property. In contrast, we show that there exist asymmetric grids with | 𝒮 1 | = ⋯ = | 𝒮 n | = 3 |\mathcal{S}_{1}|=\cdots=|\mathcal{S}_{n}|=3 for which testing requires ω n ​ ( 1 ) \omega_{n}(1) queries, thereby establishing that even in the context of polynomials, local testing depends on the structure of the domain and not just the distance of the underlying code. The low-degree testing problem has been studied extensively over the years and a wide variety of tools have been applied to propose and analyze tests. Our work introduces yet another new connection in this rich field, by building low-degree tests out of tests for “junta-degrees”. A function f : 𝒮 1 × ⋯ × 𝒮 n → 𝒢 f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{G} , for an abelian group 𝒢 \mathcal{G} is said to be a junta-degree- d d function if it is a sum of d d -juntas. We derive our low-degree test by giving a new local test for junta-degree- d d functions. For the analysis of our tests, we deduce a small-set expansion theorem for spherical noise over large grids, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

The main problem considered in this paper is “low-degree testing over grids”. Specifically given a degree parameter
d\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geqslant 0}
and proximity parameter
\delta>0
we would like to design a tester (a randomized oracle algorithm) that is given oracle access to a function
f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{F}
where

\mathbb{F}
is a field and
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
are arbitrary finite sets, and accepts if
f
is a polynomial of degree at most
d
while rejecting with constant probability (say
1/2
) if
f
is
\delta
-far (in relative Hamming distance) from every degree
d
polynomial. The main goal here is to identify settings where the test makes
O(1)
queries when
d,1/\delta
and
\max_{i\in[n]}\{|\mathcal{S}_{i}|\}
are all considered constants. (In particular the goal is to get a query complexity independent of
n
.)

Low-degree testing:

The low-degree testing problem over grids is a generalization of the classical low-degree testing problem which corresponds to the special case where
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field and
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\cdots=\mathcal{S}_{n}=\mathbb{F}
. Versions of the classical problem were studied in the early 90s
[
BLR93
,
BFL91
,
BFLS91
]
in the context of program checking and (multi-prover) interactive proofs. The problem was formally defined and systematically studied by Rubinfeld and Sudan
[
RS96
]
and played a central role in the PCP theorem
[
AS98
,
ALM+98
]
and subsequent improvements. While the initial exploration of low-degree testing focussed on the case where
d\ll|\mathbb{F}|
(and tried to get bounds that depended polynomially, or even linearly, on
d
), a later series of works starting with that of Alon, Kaufman, Krivelevich, Litsyn and Ron
[
AKK+05
]
initiated the study of low degree testing in the setting where
d>|\mathbb{F}|
.
[
AKK+05
]
studied the setting of
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
and this was extended to the setting of other constant sized fields in
[
JPRZ09
,
KR06
]
. An even more recent sequence of works
[
BKS+10
,
HSS13
,
HRS15
,
KM22
]
explores so-called “optimal tests” for this setting and these results have led to new applications to the study of the Gowers uniformity norm, proofs of XOR lemmas for polynomials
[
BKS+10
]
, and novel constructions of small set expanders
[
BGH+15
]
.

Part of the reason for the wide applicability of low-degree testing is the fact that evaluations of polynomials form error-correcting codes, a fact that dates back at least to the work of Ore
[
Ore22
]
. Ore’s theorem (a.k.a. the Schwartz-Zippel lemma) however applies widely to the evaluations of polynomial on entire “grids”, i.e., sets of the form
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
and bounds the distance between low-degree functions in terms of the degree
d
and minimum set size
\min_{i}\{|\mathcal{S}_{i}|\}
. This motivated Bafna, Srinivasan and Sudan
[
BSS20
]
to introduce the low-degree testing problem over grids. They proposed and analyzed a low-degree test for the special case of the Boolean grid, i.e., where
|\mathcal{S}_{1}|=\cdots=|\mathcal{S}_{n}|=2
. This setting already captures the setting considered in
[
AKK+05
]
while also including some novel settings such as testing the Fourier degree of Boolean functions (here the domain is
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
while the range is
\mathbb{R}
). The main theorem in
[
BSS20
]
shows that there is a tester with constant query complexity, thus qualitatively reproducing the theorem of
[
AKK+05
]
(though with a worse query complexity than
[
AKK+05
]
which was itself worse than the optimal result in
[
BKS+10
]
), while extending the result to many new settings.

In this work we attempt to go beyond the restriction of a Boolean grid. We discuss our results in more detail shortly, but the main outcome of our exploration is that the problem takes on very different flavors depending on whether the grid is symmetric (
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\cdots=\mathcal{S}_{n}
) or not. In the former case, we get constant complexity testers for constant
|\mathcal{S}_{i}|
whereas in the latter setting we show that even when
|\mathcal{S}_{i}|=3
low-degree (even
d=1
) testing requires superconstant query complexity. (See
Theorem

1.3
for details.)
In contrast to previous testers, our tester goes via “junta-degree-tests”, a concept that has been explored in the literature but not as extensively as low-degree tests, and not been connected to low-degree tests in the past. We describe this problem and our results for this problem next.

Junta-degree testing:

A function
f:\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{G}

is said to be a

d

\mathcal{G}
is an abelian group, a function

).

While in principle the problem could also be considered over non-abelian groups, in such a case it not clear if there is a fixed bound on the number of juntas that need to be summed to get to a function of bounded junta-degree.

In the special case where
|\mathcal{S}_{i}|=2

Junta-degree testing considers the task of testing if a given function has junta-degree at most
d

In our work we give testers for this problem for general constant

is bounded. We then use this tester to design our low-degree test over symmetric grids. We turn to our results below. Even though our primary motivation in studying low-

|\Omega|=2
. The problem of

A recent work of Arora, Bhattacharyya, Fleming, Kelman and Yoshida

1.2
Technical contributions

All low-degree tests roughly follow the following pattern: Given a function
f
on
n
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
they select some
k=O_{d}(1)
new variables
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
and substitute
x_{i}=\sigma_{i}(y)
, where
\sigma_{i}
’s are simple random functions, to get an
O(1)
-variate function
g(y)=f(\sigma(y))
; and then verify
g
is a low-degree polynomial in
k
variables by brute force. When the domain is
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
for some field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
\sigma_{i}
’s can be chosen to be an affine form in
y
— this preserves the domain and ensures degree of
g
is at most the degree of
f
, thus at least ensuring completeness. While soundness of the test was complex to analyze, a key ingredient in the analysis is that for any pair of points
a\neq b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
,
\sigma(a)
and
\sigma(b)
are uniform independent elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(over the randomness of
\sigma
). At least in the case where
f
is roughly
1/q^{k}
distance from the degree
d
family, this ensures that with constant probability
g
will differ from a degree
d
polynomial in exactly one point making the test reject. Dealing with cases where
f
is much further away is the more complex part that we won’t get into here.

When the domain is not
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
affine substitutions no longer preserve the domain and so we can’t use them in our tests. In the cases of the domain being
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
,
[
BSS20
]
used much simpler affine substitutions of the form
x_{i}=c_{i}y_{j(i)}
where
c_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
uniformly and independently over
i
and
j(i)\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
uniformly though not independently over
i
. Then
[
BSS20
]
iteratively reduce the number of variables as follows: When only
r

x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
remain, they pick two uniformly random indices
i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}
and identify
x_{j}
with
x_{i}
, and then rename the
r-1
remaining variables as
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r-1}
. At the end when
r=k
, they pick a random bijection between
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
. This iterative identification eventually maps every variable
x_{i}
to some variable
y_{j(i)}
. The nice feature of this identification scheme is it leads to a sequence of functions
f_{n},f_{n-1},...,f_{k}
with
f_{r}
being a function of
r
variables on the same domain, and of degree at most
d
if
f=f_{n}
has degree at most
d
. If however we start with
f_{n}
being
very

d

f_{r}
is very far from high-degree functions while
f_{r-1}
is only

. This step is the key to this argument and depends on the fact that
f_{r-1}
involves very small changes to
f_{r}
. Summing over
r
then gives the constant probability that the final function
f_{k}
(or equivalently

) is far from degree
d
polynomials. This still leaves
[
BSS20
]
with the problem of dealing with functions
f
that are close to codewords: Here they use the fact that this substitution ensures that
\sigma(a)
is distributed uniformly in
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
for every
a\in\{-1,+1\}^{k}
. It is however no longer true that
\sigma(b)
is uniform conditioned on
\sigma(a)
for
b\neq a
, but it is still the case that if
b
is moderately far in Hamming distance from

then
\sigma(b)
has sufficient entropy conditioned on
\sigma(a)
. (Specifically
\sigma(b)
is distributed uniformly on a sphere of distance

from

), ensures that if
f
is somewhat close to a low-degree polynomial then
g
is far from every degree
d
polynomial on an appropriately chosen subset of
\{-1,+1\}^{k}
and so the test rejects.

To extend this algorithm and analysis to the setting on non-Boolean domains we are faced with two challenges: (1) We cannot afford to negate variables (using the random variables
c(i)
above) when the domain is not
\{-1,+1\}
– we can only work with identification of variables (or something similar). (2) The increase in the domain size forces us to seek a general spherical hypercontractivity result on non-Boolean alphabets and this is not readily available. Overcoming either one of the restrictions on its own seems plausible, but doing it together (while also ensuring that the sequence of restrictions/identifications do not make the distance to the family being tested to abruptly drop in distance as we go from
f_{n},f_{n-1},\dots
to

As a first step in our proof we extend the approach of
[
BSS20
]
to junta-degree testing over the domain

for arbitrary finite

. (It is relatively simple to extend this further to the case of
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
— we don’t discuss that here.) This is achieved by using substitutions of the form

is a random bijection. While this might increase the degree of the function, this preserves the junta-degree (or reduces it) and makes it suitable for analysis of the junta-degree test, which we now describe: Following the template of a low-degree test stated at the beginning of this subsection, the junta-tester would simply check whether

j(.)

of variables. The permutations

We note that the hypercontracitivity setting we are considering and analyzing in this part is not sufficient to get a direct analysis of low-degree testing. Such an analysis would require hypercontractivity for more delicate noise models than the simpler “
q

The other differences of our junta-degree test analysis compared to that of
[
BSS20
]
are mainly to account for the fact that we are aiming for junta-degree testing over any (abelian) group whereas the low-degree testing ideas of
[
BSS20
]
and other prior work utilize the properties of polynomials over fields. We also give a cleaner proof as compared to
[
BSS20
]
for the fact that the sequence of functions of fewer and fewer variables obtained by the random identifications (along with permutations) does not abruptly decrease in distance to the junta-degree-

).

We then return to the task of low-degree testing: For this we design a new test: We first test the given oracle for junta-degree
d
, then if it passes, we pick a fresh random identification scheme setting
x_{i}=y_{j(i)}
for uniform independent
j(i)\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and verify (by brute-force) that the resulting
k
variate function has degree at most
d
. The advantage with this two stage tester is that in the second stage the given function is already known to be close to a polynmial of degree at most
sd
where
s=\max_{i}\{|\mathcal{S}_{i}|\}
. This makes the testing problem closer to a polynomial identity testing problem, though the problem takes some care to define, and many careful details to be worked out in the analysis.
A particular challenge arises from the fact that the first phase only proves that our function is only
close
to a low-degree polynomial and may not be low-degree exactly – so in the second stage we have to be careful to sample the function on essentially uniform inputs. This prevents us from using all of
\mathcal{S}^{k}
when looking at the restricted function
g(y)
, but only allows us to use balanced inputs in
\mathcal{S}^{k}
(where a balanced input has an equal number of coordinates with each value
v\in\mathcal{S}
). In turn understanding what the lowest degree of function can be given its values on a balanced set leads to new algebraic questions.

Section

4
gives a full proof of the low-degree test and analysis spelling out the many technical questions and our solutions to those.

The final testing-related result we prove is an impossibility result, showing that while low-degree functions are locally testable over
\mathcal{S}^{n}
, this cannot be extended to general grids
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
. From a coding theory perspective, this reveals that local testability of even polynomial evaluations codes requires more structure than simply having a large distance.

\displaystyle P^{(h)}|_{h^{\prime}}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{r}\\
\#a\leqslant d\\
a_{h}=0\\
a_{h^{\prime}}=0\end{subarray}}g_{a}^{(h)}\cdot\prod_{i\in[L]:a_{i}\neq 0}(\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})-\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{L+i}))\prod_{i\in[L+1..r]:a_{i}\neq 0}\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})

(26)

and

\displaystyle P^{(h^{\prime})}|_{h}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{r}\\
\#a\leqslant d\\
a_{h}=0\\
a_{h^{\prime}}=0\end{subarray}}g_{a}^{(h^{\prime})}\cdot\prod_{i\in[L]:a_{i}\neq 0}(\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})-\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{L+i}))\prod_{i\in[L+1..r]:a_{i}\neq 0}\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})

(27)

Again, since (

) says that

as functions, the representations on the RHS of (

) and (
27
) must be identical; i.e., for all

such that
\#a\leqslant d
and
a_{h}=a_{h^{\prime}}=0
, we have

\displaystyle g_{a}^{(h)}=g_{a}^{(h^{\prime})}.

(28)

Now we will construct

by setting
g_{a}=g_{a}^{(h)}
if
a_{h}=0
and
0
otherwise in (
24
). The choice of
h
here does not matter because of (
28
). For this

, we can see that

\displaystyle P|_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{r}\\
\#a\leqslant d\\
a_{h}=0\end{subarray}}g_{a}\cdot\prod_{i\in[L]:a_{i}\neq 0}(\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})-\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{L+i}))\prod_{i\in[L+1..r]:a_{i}\neq 0}\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{r}\\
\#a\leqslant d\\
a_{h}=0\end{subarray}}g^{(h)}_{a}\cdot\prod_{i\in[L]:a_{i}\neq 0}(\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{i})-\delta_{a_{i}}(x_{L+i}))\prod_{i\in[L+1..r]:a_{i}\neq 0}\delta_{a_{i}}(a_{i})=P^{(h)}(x).

This finishes the proof of
3.8
and the large-distance lemma.
∎

4
Low-degree testing

Moving on to the second half of this paper, we will describe our low-degree test now.

The degree test (
Deg
):

Given query access to

, the following test (called

) works to test whether
f
is degree-
d
. We may assume that
s=\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert\geqslant 2
as
f
is a constant function otherwise.

Test
\text{\sc Deg}(f)
: gets query access to
f:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}

•

Run
\text{\sc Junta-deg}(f)
to check if
f
is junta-degree-
d
.

•

Run
\text{\sc Weak-deg}(f)
.

•

Accept iff both the above tests accept.

In the above description, the sub-routine
Weak-deg
corresponds to the following test.

Test
\text{\sc Weak-deg}(f)
: gets query access to
f:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}

•

Choose a map
\mu:[n]\to[K]
u.a.r. where
K=t(d+1)
and
t=s^{3}
.

•

For
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{K})\in\mathcal{S}^{K}
, define
x_{\mu}(y)=(y_{\mu(1)},\dots,y_{\mu(n)})
.

•

Define the function
f^{\prime}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)^{K/t}\to\mathbb{F}
as
f^{\prime}(y)=f(x_{\mu}(y))
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)
, defined in
Section

, is the “balanced” subset of
\mathcal{S}^{t}
.

•

Accept iff

is degree-
d
.

We now move on to the analysis of this test, proving
Theorem

1.3
. We prove a slightly different version below which would imply
Theorem

1.3
by simply repeating the
Deg
test
O(1/\varepsilon)
times.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
of size
s
if a function
f:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}
is degree-
d
, then
Deg
always accepts. Otherwise,

\Pr[\text{\sc Deg}\text{~rejects~}f]\geqslant\varepsilon\delta

for some
\varepsilon=(sd)^{-O(s^{3}d)}
, where
\delta=\delta^{\prime}_{d}(f)
is the distance to the degree-
d
family.

Proof.

Let
g:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}
be a closest junta-degree-
d
function to
f
i.e.,
\delta_{d}(f)=\delta(f,g)
.
There are four cases, three of which are trivial. Here
\varepsilon_{2}={K}^{-O(K)}
is a small enough threshold to be decided later.

•

Case 1:
\delta^{\prime}_{d}(f)=0
i.e.,
f
is degree-
d
.

If
f
has a degree-
d
polynomial, then
Junta-deg
always succeeds. As identifying variables does not decrease the degree,
Weak-deg
also succeeds. Hence, the
Deg
test always accepts.

•

Case 2:
\delta_{d}(f)>\varepsilon_{2}
.

In this case, the
Junta-deg
part of
Deg
rejects
f
with probability at least
s^{-O(s^{2}d)}\delta_{d}(f)\geqslant K^{-O(K)}
. Hence,

\Pr[\text{\sc Deg}\text{~rejects~}f]\geqslant(sd)^{-O(s^{3}d)}\geqslant(sd)^{-O(s^{3}d)}\delta^{\prime}_{d}(f).

•

Case 3:
\delta_{d}(f)\leqslant\varepsilon_{2}
and
g
is degree-
d
.
Since any degree-
d
function is also junta-degree-
d
and
g
is the closest junta-degree-
d
function to
f
, we have
\delta(f,g)=\delta_{d}(f)=\delta^{\prime}_{d}(f)
. Therefore,

\Pr[\text{\sc Deg}\text{~rejects~}f]\geqslant\Pr[\text{\sc Junta-deg}\text{~rejects~}f]\geqslant\varepsilon\delta_{d}(f)=\varepsilon\delta^{\prime}_{d}(f).

•

Case 4:
\delta_{d}(f)\leqslant\varepsilon_{2}
but
g
is not degree-
d
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}
be the points where
f
and
g
differ; thus
\left\lvert E\right\rvert/s^{n}=\delta_{d}(f)\leqslant\varepsilon_{2}
. Let

V_{\mu}=\left\{x_{\mu}(y):y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)^{K/t}\right\}.

Suppose
\mu:[n]\to[K]
is such that
V_{\mu}\cap E=\emptyset
and
\text{\sc Weak-deg}~\text{~rejects~}g
. Then
Weak-deg
does not distinguish between
f
and
g
and hence rejects
f
as well. We will show that both these events occur with good probability. For the first probability, we will upper bound

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mu}\left[V_{\mu}\cap E\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\mu}\left[\exists y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)^{K/t}:x_{\mu}(y)\in E\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lvert\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)^{K/t}\right\rvert\cdot\Pr_{\mu}\left[\text{For fixed arbitrary $y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)^{K/t}$},x_{\mu}(y)\in E\right].

Note that since all points in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)^{K/t}
contain an equal number of occurrences of all the elements of
\mathcal{S}
,
x_{\mu}(y)
is uniformly distributed in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
for a uniformly random
\mu
. Hence, the above probability is

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mu}\left[V_{\mu}\cap E\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert^{K}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert E\right\rvert}{s^{n}}\leqslant s^{K}\varepsilon_{2}<\frac{1}{2K^{d}}.

(by setting
\varepsilon_{2}:=1/4s^{K}K^{d}\geqslant K^{-O(K)}
)

The remainder of this section is dedicated to proving the following claim.

Claim 4.2
.

\Pr_{\mu}[\text{\sc Weak-deg}~\text{rejects}~g]\geqslant 1/K^{d}.

Assuming this claim,

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{\sc Deg}~\text{rejects}~f]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr[\text{\sc Weak-deg}~\text{rejects}~f]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr[\text{\sc Weak-deg}~\text{rejects}~g]-\Pr[V_{\mu}\cap E\neq\emptyset]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{K^{d}}-\frac{1}{2K^{d}}=\frac{1}{2K^{d}}\geqslant\frac{\delta^{\prime}_{d}(f)}{2K^{d}}.

This finishes the analysis of the low-degree test assuming

∎

4.1
Soundness of
Weak-deg

Proof of

.

We will need the following lemma about the vector space formed by functions over

Lemma 4.3

For
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{K}

\displaystyle f\text{~is degree-}d\iff\forall i,~c_{i}=0\text{~or~}m_{i}\text{~is degree-}d.

(

•

–

Let

Then we can write each

We argue that each

Now assume that there exists a

4.2
Existence of a good
\mu

We will show for any function

that is not degree-

\mu:[n]\to[K]
such that the function

is also not degree-

were to be

, but is particularly tricky in our setting.

Let
D=d+1
. We will give a map
\mu:[n]\to[t]\times[D]\equiv[tD]=[K]
instead. Let
P
be the polynomial with individual degree at most

representing

is
d^{\prime}>d
and let
m(x)=c\cdot x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\ell}}^{a_{\ell}}
be a monomial of

of degree

for some non-zero

, where

for all

and
i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}
are some distinct elements of

and

as

is junta-degree-

. Then, we define

as follows for

\displaystyle\mu(i)=\begin{cases}(1,j),\text{~if}~i=i_{j}\text{ for some }j\in[\ell]\\

It is easy to inspect that

(call it

) is a polynomial in variables

, and is of degree

– this is because the monomial

, upon this substitution turns to

which cannot be cancelled by

for any other monomial
m^{\prime}(x)
of

, as if

contains the variable

and on the other hand if

, then the individual degree of

in the two substitutions differs for some

is

. As we can express the function

for

as a polynomial in

of individual degree at most

, we can further transform

so that it has individual degree at most
s-1
, while maintaining the properties that it still only contains the variables

(i.e., the first “row”) and has degree

and computes the function
g^{\prime}(y)
. The following claim then completes the proof of the existence of a good

by setting

and

.

Claim 4.4
.

For formal variables
y\equiv(y_{1},\dots,y_{w})\equiv(y_{(i,j)})_{(i,j)\in[t]\times[w]}
, let

be a polynomial of degree

containing only the variables from the first row. Then the degree of

as a function over

is exactly

.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
w
. The base case
w=1
is crucial and it is equivalent to the following claim:

Claim 4.5
.

For
0\leqslant d^{\prime}\leqslant s-1
, the function
f_{d^{\prime}}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)\to\mathbb{F}
defined as
f_{d^{\prime}}(z)=z_{1}^{d^{\prime}}
for

has degree exactly
d^{\prime}
.

Assuming the above claim, let
w>1
be arbitrary. As
d^{\prime}=0
is trivial to handle, we will assume that
d^{\prime}\geqslant 1
. Hence,
Q
contains at least one monomial
m
of degree
d^{\prime}
and containing some variable
y_{(1,j)}
with individual degree
a\in[s-1]
. Without loss of generality, suppose
j=w
. Since
4.5
states that the function
y_{(1,w)}^{a}
is linearly independent of degree-
(a-1)
functions over
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)
, there exists a function
C:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)\to\mathbb{F}
such that for any

\displaystyle\left\langle C,f\right\rangle=\begin{cases}1\text{~if~}f=y_{(1,w)}^{a}\text{~i.e.,~}a\text{-th power of the last coordinate}\\
0\text{~if~}f\text{~is degree-}(a-1).\end{cases}

(30)

Now we decompose
Q
as a polynomial over variables in the first

columns and coefficients being the monomials over variables in the last column: that is

\displaystyle Q(y)=\sum_{\alpha\in[0..a]}Q^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot y_{(1,w)}^{\alpha},

(31)

where

represents the variables in the

. Here, we are using the fact that

Towards a contradiction, suppose there is some degree-

such that

for all

. We may decompose

as follows:

\displaystyle R(y)=\sum_{\alpha\in[0..s-1]^{t}}R^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot y_{w}^{\alpha}

and for all

Fixing

\displaystyle\left\langle C,Q(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1},y_{w})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle C,\sum_{\alpha\in[0..a]}Q^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot y^{\alpha}_{(1,w)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in[0..a]}Q^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot\left\langle C,y^{\alpha}_{(1,w)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=Q^{\prime}_{a}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1}).

(using (
30
))

Similarly,

\displaystyle\left\langle C,R(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1},y_{w})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle C,\sum_{\alpha\in[0..s-1]^{t}}R^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot y^{\alpha}_{w}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in[0..s-1]^{t}}R^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot\left\langle C,y_{w}^{\alpha}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in[0..s-1]^{t}:~\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert_{1}\geqslant a}R^{\prime}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{w-1})\cdot\left\langle C,y_{w}^{\alpha}\right\rangle

(using (

))

As a polynomial in the variables of

.

Proof of
4.5
.

The upper bound of
d^{\prime}
on the degree is trivial. For the lower bound, it suffices to prove the claim for
d^{\prime}=s-1
as we can obtain a polynomial computing
z_{1}^{s-1}
by multiplying
z_{1}^{s-1-d^{\prime}}
to any polynomial computing
z_{1}^{d^{\prime}}
.

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose
z_{1}^{s-1}
is degree-
(s-2)
as a function. By symmetry, then
z_{i}^{s-1}
is also degree-
(s-2)
for all
i\in[t]
. Recall that any function
f:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)\to\mathbb{F}
can be expressed as a (not necessarily unique) polynomial
P
of individual-degree at most
s-1
. Replacing the factor

in each monomial of

with corresponding degree-
(d-2)
functions, we observe that

can be expressed as a polynomial of individual-degree at most
s-2
. The monomials of individual-degree at most
s-2
form a spanning set for the functions computed by such polynomials. Its dimension is

. On the other hand, the vector space of all functions

is of dimension
\left\lvert\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S},t)\right\rvert=\frac{t!}{(\left(t/s\right)!)^{s}}
, which can be shown to be strictly larger than

by using

and Stirling’s estimates. Thus, there exists a function

which cannot be represented as a polynomial of individual-degree at most

, a contradiction.
∎

5
More general domains

In the previous sections, we gave a junta-degree-

5.1
Junta-degree testing

We will now prove

Proof of

Let

i
. This is stated in more detail below:

Test
\text{\sc Junta-deg}(f)
: gets query access to
f:[s_{1}]\times\dots[s_{n}]\to\mathcal{G}

•

Define
f_{\lambda}:\mathcal{T}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{T}_{n}\to\mathcal{G}
as

f_{\lambda}((x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n}))=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

•

Accept
f
iff
f_{\lambda}
is junta-degree-
d
(check this using
Junta-deg
from
Section

3
; note
\left\lvert\mathcal{T}_{i}\right\rvert
is identical for all
i\in[n]
).

If is clear that
f
is junta-degree-
d
iff
f_{\lambda}
is junta-degree-
d
. We need to argue that the distance is preserved as well, i.e.,
\delta_{d}(f)=\delta_{d}(f_{\lambda})
. Then, the probability that the above test rejects
f
is equal to the probability of
\text{\sc Junta-deg}(f_{\lambda})
rejecting which is proportional to
\delta_{d}(f_{\lambda})=\delta_{d}(f)
.

Let
g
be a closest junta-degree-
d
function to
f
. Then
\delta_{d}(f_{\lambda})\leqslant\delta_{d}(f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda})=\delta_{d}(f,g)=\delta_{d}(f)
, where we used
\delta_{d}(f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda})=\delta_{d}(f,g)
since
f_{\lambda}
and
g_{\lambda}
can be treated as functions over
[s_{1}]\times[L_{1}]\times\dots\times[s_{n}]\times[L_{n}]
but the output never depends on the values of the even coordinates. For the other direction, let
h:\mathcal{T}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{T}_{n}\to\mathcal{G}
be a closest junta-degree-
d
function to
f_{\lambda}
. Let
h^{b}:[s_{1}]\times\dots\times[s_{n}]\to\mathcal{G}
be defined as

h^{b}(x)=h((x_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},b_{n}))
. We claim that there exists some
b
such that
\delta(f_{\lambda},h^{b}_{\lambda})\leqslant\delta(f_{\lambda},h)
. This is because the expectation of
\delta(f_{\lambda},h^{b}_{\lambda})
is
\delta(f_{\lambda},h)
:

\displaystyle\E_{b\in[L_{1}]\times\dots\times[L_{n}]}\left[\delta(f_{\lambda},h^{b}_{\lambda})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{b\in[L_{1}]\times\dots\times[L_{n}]}\left[\delta(f,h^{b})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{b\in[L_{1}]\times\dots\times[L_{n}]}\left[\Pr_{x\in[s_{1}]\times\dots\times[s_{n}]}\left[f(x)\neq h^{b}(x)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{(x,b)}\left[f(x)\neq h(x,b)\right]=\delta(f_{\lambda},h).

Hence for such a
b
,

\displaystyle\delta_{d}(f)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta(f,h^{b})

(as
h^{b}
is junta-degree-
d
)

\displaystyle=\delta(f_{\lambda},h^{b}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta(f_{\lambda},h)=\delta_{d}(f_{\lambda}).

∎

5.2
Degree testing impossibility

In the previous section, we have shown that low-degree functions over
\mathcal{S}^{n}
are locally testable. It is natural to ask whether this can be extended to general product domains of the form
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
(for potentially different sets
\mathcal{S}_{i}
). The previous subsection addresses this question for junta-degree. Unlike junta-degree testing, we will prove that this is not possible in general for degree testing. In fact, the lower bound (on the number of queries) we prove is in a scenario where
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}_{i}\right\rvert=3
and degree
d=1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be any field of size at least
n+2
with distinct elements
\{0,1,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
. For
i\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{0,1,a_{i}\}
. We will refer to
0,1
as Boolean elements and the remaining as non-Boolean ones. Let
\zeta(b)=b
if
b
is Boolean and
\star
otherwise.

As degree-
d
functions over
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
form a linear subspace over
\mathbb{F}
, by
[
BHR05
]
any test can be converted to a one-sided, non-adaptive one without changing the number of queries or the error by more than a factor of
2
. Thus, without loss of generality let
Test
be a one-sided, non-adaptive test for the degree-
1
family of the following form, where
\mathcal{D}
is some distribution over matrices
\mathbb{F}^{\ell\times n}
with the row vectors from
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
P:\mathbb{F}^{\ell}\to\{\text{true},\text{false}\}
is some predicate.

•

Sample
M\sim\mathcal{D}
and let the rows of
M
be
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

•

Query
f
to construct the vector
f_{M}=(f(x^{(1)}),\dots,f(x^{(\ell)}))\in\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
.

•

Accept
f
iff
P(f_{M})
is true.

We will show that if
Test
accepts degree-
1
functions with probability 1 and rejects
\Omega(1)
-far functions with probability
\Omega(1)
, then
\ell=\Omega(\log n)
.

As
Test
is one-sided, for all
M\in\textnormal{Supp}(\mathcal{D})
,

\displaystyle f_{M}\in\textnormal{colspace}(M)\implies P(f_{M})\text{~is true}.

(32)

Here
Supp
(
\cdot
) refers to the support and
colspace
(
\cdot
) is the column space. Fix an arbitrary
M\in\textnormal{Supp}(\mathcal{D})
. For an
i\in[n]
, suppose that there exists a
j\neq i\in[n]
such that

\displaystyle\forall k\in[\ell],~\zeta(x^{(k)}_{i})=\zeta(x^{(k)}_{j}).

(33)

That is, the
i
-th and
j
-th columns of
M
are “identical”, upon the identification of
a_{i}
and
a_{j}
(with the symbol
\star
).
Let
g(x)=x_{i}(x_{i}-1)
be a function with domain
\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\dots\mathcal{S}_{n}
and co-domain
\mathbb{F}
; note that
g
is 0 iff the
i
-th coordinate is Boolean. We note that
g
is not degree-
1
, indeed it is
1/3
-far from being degree-
1
(by an application of
2.7
). Therefore, the
k
-th coordinate of
g_{M}
is
0
if
x^{(k)}_{i}
is Boolean and
a_{i}(a_{i}-1)\neq 0
otherwise. Consider the column vector
M_{i}-M_{j}
, where
M_{i}
denotes the
i
-th column of
M
. By (
33
), its
k
-th coordinate is

\displaystyle(M_{i}-M_{j})_{k}=\begin{cases}0,\text{~if~}\zeta(x^{(k)}_{i})\neq\star,\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\neq 0,\text{~otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore,
g_{M}=\frac{a_{i}(a_{i}-1)}{a_{i}-a_{j}}(M_{i}-M_{j})
is in the column space of
M
, so
Test
accepts
g
because of (
32
). As
\zeta(M_{i}):=(\zeta(x^{(k)}_{i}))_{k\in[\ell]}
can take at most
3^{\ell}
distinct values, for any
M\in\textnormal{Supp}(\mathcal{D})
there are at most
3^{\ell}
many
i\in[n]
such that there does not exist a
j\neq i
such that (
33
) holds. Call such
i\in[n]
,
bad for
M
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr_{i\in[n]}\left[\text{\sc Test}\text{~rejects~}x_{i}(x_{i}-1)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\sim\mathcal{D}\\
i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\left[\text{\sc Test}\text{~rejects~}x_{i}(x_{i}-1)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\sim\mathcal{D}\\
i\in[n]\end{subarray}}\left[i\text{~bad for~}M\right]\leqslant 3^{\ell}/n.

But the LHS is
\Omega(1)
as for any
i\in[n]
,
x_{i}(x_{i}-1)
is
\Omega(1)
-far from degree-
1
. Hence, the number of queries
Test
makes is
\ell\geqslant\Omega(\log n)=\omega_{n}(1)
.
∎

6
Small-set expansion for spherical noise

In this section, we will prove a small-set expansion theorem for spherical noise, which we have used for (
10
) in the proof of the small-distance lemma of junta-degree testing.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be an arbitrary function.

Definition 6.1

(
Bernoulli noise operator).

For
\nu\in[0,1]
,

N_{\nu}f(x)=\E_{y\sim N_{\nu}(x)}\left[f(y)\right]=\E_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\otimes n}}[f(x+y)].

Definition 6.2

(
Spherical noise operator).

For
\nu\in[0,1]
such that
\nu n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

S_{\nu}f(x)=\E_{y\sim S_{\nu}(x)}[f(y)]=\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}[f(x+y)].

For the rest of this section, let
\rho\in[0,1]
and
\nu=(1-1/s)(1-\rho)\in[0,1]
: it is easy to check that if each coordinate of
x
is retained with probability
\rho
and randomized (uniformly over
\mathbb{Z}_{s}
) with probability
1-\rho
, the resulting string is distributed according to
N_{\nu}(x)
.

We will use the following lemma:

Lemma 6.3

(
Small-set expansion for bernoulli noise, e.g.
[
O’D14
]
).

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}
be such that
\Pr_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left[x\in A\right]=\delta
. Then, for
q\geqslant 2
and
0\leqslant\rho\leqslant\frac{1}{q-1}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)^{1-2/q}
we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim N_{\nu}(x)\end{subarray}}\left[x\in A\text{~and~}y\in A\right]\leqslant\delta^{2-2/q},

(34)

where
\nu=(1-1/s)(1-\rho)
.

If
s\geqslant 3
, we reduce the case of spherical noise to bernoulli noise and then use the above lemma.

Theorem 6.4

(
Small-set expansion for spherical noise).

Let
s\geqslant 3
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}
be such that
\Pr_{x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left[x\in A\right]=\delta
. Then, for any
\nu\in[1/32,1]

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim S_{\nu}(x)\end{subarray}}\left[x\in A\text{~and~}y\in A\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\delta^{1+\lambda},

(35)

where
\lambda=\frac{1}{2^{14}\log s}
.

Remark 6.5
.

When the size of the domain
s
is equal to
2
and
\nu\in[1/32,31/32]
, the above statement still holds (with the factor
2
replaced with some other constant factor
C
) as proved by
[
Pol19
]
(or Corollary 2.8 in
[
BSS20
]
).

Proof.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be the indicator function of
A
and consider its Fourier representation as in
Definition

2.9
:

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)\chi_{\alpha}(x).

Then the probability in (
34
) is equal to

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim N_{\nu}(x)\end{subarray}}\left[x\in A\text{~and~}y\in A\right]=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\otimes n}\end{subarray}}\left[f(x)f(x+y)\right]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\alpha)\right\rvert^{2}\E_{y\sim\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\otimes n}}\left[\chi_{\alpha}(y)\right]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\alpha)\right\rvert^{2}\rho^{\#\alpha},

(36)

(using
Lemma

2.12
)

and similarly the probability in (
35
) is equal to

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim S_{\nu}(x)\end{subarray}}\left[x\in A\text{~and~}y\in A\right]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\alpha)\right\rvert^{2}\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}\left[\chi_{\alpha}(y)\right].

(37)

We will show that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}
, the quantity
\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}\left[\chi_{\alpha}(y)\right]
above is upper bounded by
2\cdot\widetilde{\rho}^{\#\alpha}
for some constant
\widetilde{\rho}
.

\displaystyle\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}\left[\chi_{\alpha}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}\left[\chi_{\alpha_{1}}(y_{1})\cdots\chi_{\alpha_{n}}(y_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}\\
\mu\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}\setminus\{0\}\\
y\sim 0^{\overline{I}}\circ\mu^{I}\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{i\in I}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(y_{i})\prod_{i\notin I}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(y_{i})\right]

(where
I
and
\mu
are independent)

\displaystyle=\E_{I,\mu,y}\left[\prod_{i\in I}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\mu_{i})\right]

(where
I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n},\mu\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}\setminus\{0\},
and
y\sim 0^{\overline{I}}\circ\mu^{I}
)

\displaystyle=\E_{I}\E_{\mu\sim(\mathbb{Z}_{s}\setminus\{0\})^{I}}\left[\prod_{i\in I}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\mu_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{I}\left[\prod_{i\in I}\E_{\mu_{i}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}\setminus\{0\}}[\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\mu_{i})]\right].

(38)

Now we note that the inner term

\displaystyle\E_{\mu_{i}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}\setminus\{0\}}\left[\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\mu_{i})\right]=\begin{cases}1,\text{~if~}\alpha_{i}=0,\text{~and}\\
\frac{1}{s-1}\left(\sum_{\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}\setminus\{0\}}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\mu_{i})\right)=\frac{1}{{s-1}}\left(s\E_{\mu_{i}\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}}[\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\mu_{i})]-1\right)=\frac{-1}{s-1}\text{~otherwise.}\end{cases}

(39)

Therefore, denoting the coordinates of
\alpha
with non-zero entries by
J\subseteq[n]
, plugging (
39
) into (
38
) gives

\displaystyle\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}[\chi_{\alpha}(y)]

\displaystyle=\E_{I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}}\left[\left(\frac{-1}{s-1}\right)^{\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\E_{I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert}\right]

(as
s\geqslant 3
)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pr_{I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}}\left[\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert<\nu k/2\right]\cdot 1+\E_{I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert}\middle|\ \left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert\geqslant\nu k/2\right].

Denoting
\left\lvert J\right\rvert=\#\alpha
by
k
, we observe that
\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert
is distributed according to the hypergeometric distribution of
k
draws (without replacement) from a population of size
n
and
\nu n
many success states. Hence, by a tail bound
[
Hoe94
]

\Pr[\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert<\nu k/2]\leqslant e^{-\nu^{2}k/2}.

Using
\nu\geqslant 1/32
,

\displaystyle\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}[\chi_{\alpha}(y)]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Pr_{I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}}\left[\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert<\nu k/2\right]\cdot 1+\E_{I\sim\binom{[n]}{\nu n}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert}\middle|\ \left\lvert J\cap I\right\rvert\geqslant\nu k/2\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{-k/2^{11}}+2^{-k/64}\leqslant 2\cdot 2^{-k/2^{11}}=2\cdot\widetilde{\rho}^{k}

where
\widetilde{\rho}:=2^{-2^{-11}}
.

Plugging the above bound in (
37
), we get

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim S_{\nu}(x)\end{subarray}}\left[x\in A\text{~and~}y\in A\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\alpha)\right\rvert^{2}\E_{y\sim\mathcal{E}_{\nu}}\left[\chi_{\alpha}(y)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\alpha)\right\rvert^{2}\widetilde{\rho}^{\#\alpha}

\displaystyle=2\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathbb{Z}_{s}^{n}\\
y\sim N_{\widetilde{\nu}}(x)\end{subarray}}{\left[{x\in A\text{~and~}y\in A}\right]}

(using, where
\widetilde{\nu}=(1-1/s)(1-\widetilde{\rho})
)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\delta^{2-2/q}.

For the last step above, we are using (
36
) and
Lemma

6.3
with
q:=2+\frac{1}{2^{12}\log s}=:2+\varepsilon
; here we can verify that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q-1}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)^{1-2/q}\geqslant\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)^{1-2/q}\geqslant\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)^{\varepsilon/3}\geqslant s^{-2\varepsilon}=\widetilde{\rho}

as needed to invoke the bernoulli small-set expansion lemma.
Hence, the above probability is at most
2\delta^{1+\lambda}
for
\lambda=\varepsilon/4
.
∎
