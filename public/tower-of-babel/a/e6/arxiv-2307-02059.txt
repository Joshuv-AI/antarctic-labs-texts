Noise Decoupling for State Transfer in Continuous Variable Systems
Fattah Sakuldee, Behnam Tonekaboni

Abstract
We consider a toy model of noise channels, given by a random mixture of unitary operations, for state transfer problems with continuous variables. Assuming that the path between the transmitter node and the receiver node can be intervened, we propose a noise decoupling protocol to manipulate the noise channels generated by linear and quadratic polynomials of creation and annihilation operators, to achieve an identity channel, hence the term noise decoupling. For random constant noise, the target state can be recovered while for the general noise profile, the decoupling can be done when the interventions are fast compared to the noise. We show that the state at the transmitter can be written as a convolution of the target state and a filter function characterizing the noise and the manipulation scheme. We also briefly discuss that a similar analysis can be extended to the case of higher-order polynomial generators. Finally, we demonstrate the protocols by numerical calculations.

II
Framework

In this section, we will introduce the setup of our systems and the noise models. We begin with an overview of the noise model and then discuss the mathematical framework and formulation for the noise in our model. We will also recall the Wigner representation of states and the phase space transformations of the elementary noise operations.

II.1
Noisy Transfer Channels

The basic state transfer (or communication) scheme in quantum information is a protocol to send a quantum state
\rho
on a given Hilbert space
\mathcal{H},
from one node (transmitter) to another (receiver). The channel through which the transfer has been done is practically modeled as a completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) transformation
\mathcal{C}
on a set of bounded operators
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then the received state is formally written as
\mathcal{C}[\rho].
In the ideal scenario, such a channel is known and the information inside
\rho
can be extracted from the received state by standard state tomography
(
14
)
, or parameters characterization when only partial quantities are relevant.

However, when the communication involves disturbance from the environment or the imperfection of the medium encompassing the channel, the channel becomes partially unknown, resulting in a noisy channel. The simplest model for these channels is
{\mathcal{C}}_{\vec{\lambda}}=\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\lambda}}\circ\mathcal{C},
where
\mathcal{E}_{\vec{\lambda}}
is a noise operation modeled as a stochastic CPTP map on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),
characterized by a parameters vector
\vec{\lambda}.

We model the noise operation as a random unitary operation equipped with a probability space
(\Omega,\mu)
in which the map is represented by

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}=\int_{\Omega}d\mu(\omega_{\lambda})\mathcal{U}_{\omega_{\lambda}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{U}_{\omega_{\lambda}}[\rho]=\hat{U}_{\omega_{\lambda}}\rho\hat{U}_{\omega_{\lambda}}^{\dagger}
on a state
\rho,
and
\hat{U}_{\omega_{\lambda}}
is a unitary element for the map
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
(more on that in the next section). The parameter
\lambda,
which is one dimensional, denotes a
path
from the transmitter to the receiver and
\omega_{\lambda}
is a particular noise configuration for a given path
\lambda.

The main task of noise suppression is to modify the noisy channel
{\mathcal{C}}_{\lambda}
in order to achieve the noiseless channel
\mathcal{C}.

For simplicity, let the channel
\mathcal{C}
be absorbed in the state
\rho,
the problem then becomes equivalent to manipulating the noise operation
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
to approach an identity channel
\mathcal{I}.
In this picture, one simplest example is a communication wire where the parameter
\lambda
associates the distance from the transmitter node towards the receiver node, and the
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
is noise at the given position on the transfer path from the origin.
In the next section, we will consider the structure of this operator in detail.

II.2
Continuous Variables Systems and Noise Operations

In this section, we will recall the framework on which we are working in this article. Particularly, we first introduce the continuous variable systems that we are interested in, namely optical systems, and then introduce the noise model.

II.2.1
Harmonic oscillator as continues variable system

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space of square-integrable complex-valued functions on a real line, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\{\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}:\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(x)|^{2}dx<\infty\}.

(2)

Following conventions in quantum mechanics of continuous variables, we define position operator
\hat{x}
and momentum operator
\hat{p}
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
as multiplicative operator
\hat{x}\psi(x){=}x\psi(x)
and derivative operator
\hat{p}\psi(x){=}-i\partial_{x}\psi(x)
respectively. The position and momentum operators follow canonical commutation relation
[\hat{x},\hat{p}]:=\hat{x}\hat{p}-\hat{p}\hat{x}=i,
where
i
is the unit of imaginary numbers. In terms of position and momentum operators, one can define an annihilation operator
\hat{a}:=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{x}+i\hat{p})
and a creation operator
\hat{a}^{\dagger}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{x}-i\hat{p}),
and a number basis
\{\phi_{n}:n=0,1,\ldots\}
where
\hat{a}\phi_{n}=\sqrt{n}\phi_{n-1}
for
n>0
and
\hat{a}\phi_{0}=0
for a vacuum state
\phi_{0}.
Following the canonical commutation relation of position and momentum operators, one has
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1.
We also use a Dirac notation for the states and the dual states, i.e.
\ket{\psi}
represents the function
\psi
and
\ket{n}
represents
\phi_{n}
for
n=0,1,\ldots.

Furthermore, we define a displacement operator
\hat{D}(\alpha)
and a squeeze operator
\hat{S}(z)
as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\exp(\alpha\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{a}),

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{S}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp[(z^{*}\hat{a}^{2}-z(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2})/2].

(4)

Note that the displacement parameter
\alpha,
is a complex number where the real part is to
displace
in position space and the imaginary part is to
displace
in momentum space. Similarly, the squeezing parameter
z,
is a complex number. Here, for the sake of simplicity and without loss of generality, we will mainly discuss real positive squeezing parameters. On the operation level we also write
\mathcal{D}(\alpha)[\rho]=\hat{D}(\alpha)\rho\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{S}(z)[\rho]=\hat{S}(z)\rho\hat{S}^{\dagger}(z),
for a state
\rho.
Remark here that other displacement and squeeze operators are unitary operators, and when we assign the arguments to be random, these two exemplify the random unitary noise.

II.2.2
Noise model

We consider noise generated by creation and annihilation operators of degree
2.
This is a direct generalization of displacement noise and squeezing noise. We model this type of noise by setting
\omega_{\lambda}
in Eq. (
1
) to be a map
\omega_{\lambda}:\ell\mapsto(\alpha_{\ell},z_{\ell})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
for
\ell
lying on the path
\lambda.
We then consider the unitary elements for the map
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
of the form

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\omega_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}\exp\Big[\int_{0}^{|\lambda|}\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell))d\ell\Big],

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}\exp\Big[\int_{0}^{|\lambda|}\hat{G}(\alpha_{\ell},z_{\ell})d\ell\Big],

(5)

where
|\lambda|
denotes a path length,

\displaystyle\hat{G}

\displaystyle(\alpha_{\ell},z_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\Big[\dfrac{1}{2}(z^{*}_{\ell}\hat{a}^{2}-z_{\ell}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2})+(\alpha_{\ell}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}_{\ell}\hat{a})\Big],

(6)

for a position
\ell
on the path
\lambda,
and
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
is an ordering operator with respect to the path
\lambda
defined by

\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}\Big[\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell))\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell^{\prime}))\Big]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell))\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell^{\prime})),&\ell\leq\ell^{\prime}\\
\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell^{\prime}))\hat{G}(\omega_{\lambda}(\ell)),&\ell^{\prime}<\ell.\end{array}\right.

For a simple communication wire, one can consider variables
(x,p)
for the transverse position of a photon carrying a message, and the parameter
\ell
indicates the position along the propagating (longitudinal) direction. The action generated by
\hat{G}(\alpha_{\ell},z_{\ell})
is a contribution of the noise at the longitudinal position
\ell
from the propagating origin. Hence
\mathcal{E}_{\omega_{\lambda}}
represents the accumulation of noise during the propagation up to the longitudinal position
|\lambda|
on the wire.

Despite its simplicity, the above model of the noise has been employed to emulate several types of realistic environmental disturbance in optical systems. For instance, the model of noise with random displacement operators has been used to display the detector noise with imperfect efficiency
(
20
;
24
;
31
)
; Gaussian thermal loss
(
40
)
; or an error in Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill coding
(
41
;
42
;
43
)
. Other phenomena that can be associated with squeezing noise include a gate noise
(
25
;
26
)
; channel noise induced crosstalk
(
27
)
; atmospheric noise in free space communication
(
28
)
; or the photon loss during a propagation
(
30
)
. Note that many situations mentioned here may incorporate both displacement and squeezing types.

II.3
Wigner Representation and Gaussian Manipulation Framework

As we explained above, our formulation considers only Gaussian operations, i.e., operations whose generators are at most in the second order of the creation and annihilation operators. We further assume that the state
\rho
is also given by a mixture of coherent states. It then becomes useful to consider here Wigner functions
(
44
;
1
)
, where both coherent states (and their mixtures) and Gaussian operations (and their averages) can be illustrated as simple functions and their transformations on phase space.
In particular, we express Wigner function of a density matrix
\rho
as

W_{\rho}(x,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dye^{-ipy}\bra{x+\tfrac{y}{2}}\rho\ket{x-\tfrac{y}{2}}.

(7)

We also write the Wigner function with a single complex argument as
W_{\rho}(\alpha)=W_{\rho}(x,p)
where
\alpha=(x+ip)/\sqrt{2}.
Remark that the Wigner function is normalized, i.e.,
\displaystyle\int dx\int dp\penalty\ \!W_{\rho}(x,p)=1,
but may be non-positive.

In parallel, we employ a Wigner transformation for the operator (a dual Wigner function), namely

W^{G}(x,p)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dye^{ipy}\bra{x-\tfrac{y}{2}}\hat{G}\ket{x+\tfrac{y}{2}}=W^{G}(\alpha),

(8)

for the operator
\hat{G}.
Here the expectation value of the operator
\hat{G}
reads

\displaystyle\big\langle\hat{G}\big\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\hat{G}\rho)=\int dx\int dp\penalty\ \!W^{G}(x,p)W_{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\int d\alpha^{*}\penalty\ \!W^{G}(\alpha)W_{\rho}(\alpha).

(9)

For illustration, let us consider a mixture of coherent states
\rho

\rho=\sum_{r}p_{r}\rho_{r},

(10)

where
\sum_{r}p_{r}=1,

p_{r}>0,
and
\rho_{r}=\ket{\beta_{r}\vphantom{\beta_{r}}}\!\bra{\beta_{r}\vphantom{\beta_{r}}},

\displaystyle\ket{\beta_{r}}

\displaystyle=\hat{D}\left(\beta_{r}\right)\ket{\psi_{\sigma_{r}}},

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\sigma_{r}}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\psi_{\sigma_{r}}(x)\ket{x},

\displaystyle\psi_{\sigma_{r}}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{e^{-x^{2}/\sigma_{r}^{2}}}{2\pi\sigma_{r}^{2}}\bigg)^{1/4},

(11)

with a complex number
\beta_{r}
indicating the center of the Gaussian state
\rho_{r}
on the phase space, equipped with a spreading parameter
\sigma_{r}^{2},
i.e., a displaced vacuum state of Harmonic oscillator with modified variance. Even though the elements
\rho_{r}
are Gaussian, the state Eq. (
10
) itself is not necessarily Gaussian, and its Wigner function reads

\displaystyle W_{\theta}(x,p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}p_{r}W_{r}(x,p),

(12)

\displaystyle W_{r}(x,p)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\pi}e^{-\frac{(x-\theta_{r})^{2}}{2\sigma_{r}^{2}}}e^{-2\sigma_{r}^{2}p^{2}}.

(13)

The mixture of Gaussian state Eq. (
10
) is a well-known example of a resourceful class of states called non-Gaussian states
(
45
;
46
)
. One notorious example is a Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill state or a uniform mixture of infinitely many displaced vacuum states with the same inter-distance, which is a promising candidate for fault-tolerant encoding
(
47
)
. Remark that our noise operation Eq. (
1
) with the elements Eq. (
5
) can disturb this type of states by deforming the mixture coefficients, resulting in the deterioration of the useful information.

In this representation, both displacement and squeeze operators can be associated with transformations of Wigner functions on phase space. For displacement operator
\hat{D}(\alpha),
one can define an associate transformation on phase space
T_{D}(\alpha)
as

T_{D}(\alpha^{\prime})[W_{\rho}](\alpha):=W_{\mathcal{D}(\alpha^{\prime})[\rho]}(\alpha)=W_{\rho}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}),

(14)

and similarly the squeeze operator
\hat{S}(z)
can be associated with
T_{S}(z)
via

T_{S}(z^{\prime})[W_{\rho}](\alpha):=W_{\mathcal{S}(z^{\prime})[\rho]}(\alpha).

(15)

The exact form of the latter operation is complicated in general, but one of the comprehensive cases is the case when the squeezing parameter
z
is positive real, i.e.,
z=|z|,
in which the transformation corresponds to scaling of the phase space axes. In particular

T_{S}(\gamma)[W_{\rho}](x,p)=W_{\mathcal{S}(\gamma)[\rho]}(x,p)=W_{\rho}(e^{-\gamma}x,e^{\gamma}p),

(16)

where
\gamma=|z|.

The squeeze operators and their transformations are additive in the squeezing parameters, i.e.,
T_{S}(\gamma_{1})T_{S}(\gamma_{2})=T_{S}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})
for
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
real positive. For complex squeezing parameters, one can re-parameterize the squeeze operator into

\hat{S}(z)=\hat{S}_{\hat{c}(z)}(\gamma)=\exp\bigg[\dfrac{\gamma}{2}(\hat{c}(z)^{2}-{\hat{c}^{\dagger}(z)}^{2})\bigg],

(17)

where we assume a Bogoliubov-like transformation
\hat{c}(z)=(\sqrt{t}\hat{a}+\sqrt{r}\hat{a}^{\dagger})e^{i\theta},

t,r>0
and
t+r=1,
and we write
z=\gamma(t-r)e^{i\theta}
with
\gamma>0.
The corresponding transformation on the phase space similar to Eq. (
16
) can also be achieved for real coordinates associated with the new operators
\hat{c}(z)
and
\hat{c}^{\dagger}(z).
However, the additivity property does not hold for the complex parameter. This can be easily seen from the dependency of the parameters in the definition of
\hat{c}(z)
and
\hat{c}^{\dagger}(z),
which can be different for different sections of the path.

Until now one can observe that in some expressions the representation in real coordinate
(x,p)
is more transparent than the complex coordinate
\alpha
and vice versa. Hence, for convenience, we will use real coordinate
(x,p)
and complex coordinate
\alpha
interchangeably throughout this paper.

III
Noise Decoupling Protocols for Displacement Noise

Here we recall the general ideas of dynamical decoupling (DD) protocol and briefly discuss the connection to our set-up. We then elaborate on the mechanism for inserting the control operations in our toy model. We discuss the formalism for the displacement noise as manipulation on phase space and then we conclude by revisiting the DD descriptions for that case.

III.1
Dynamical Decoupling Scheme

Dynamical decoupling protocols
(
35
;
36
;
37
)
is a well-known control tool for manipulation of signal generated or passed through a system interacting with an environment. The principle is to interlace the dynamical evolution by additional local cyclic unitary operators, leading to an effective Hamiltonian where the system is decoupled from the environment.

In particular, consider a system
\mathcal{S}
governed by a coupled evolution to an environment
\mathcal{B},

\hat{U}_{0}(t)=e^{-it\hat{H}_{0}},
where
\hat{H}_{0}=\hat{H}_{S}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{SB}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{S}_{\alpha}\otimes\hat{B}_{\alpha}
and a separable initial state
\rho(0)=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{B}(0).

Note that
\hat{S}_{\alpha}
and
\hat{B}_{\alpha}
are operators acting locally on the systems and the environment respectively. The dynamical decoupling is a technique of perturbatively inserting a set of cyclic control operators
\hat{U}_{1}(t),
equipped with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{1}(t),
to modify the dynamics in such a way that the effects of the noise are suppressed in the asymptotic limit
(
37
)
. To do so, we define a control set
\mathcal{C}_{S}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
— a set of bounded operators on the system contains
\hat{H}_{1}(t)
such that
\hat{U}_{1}(t)=\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\exp[-i\int_{0}^{t}du\hat{H}_{1}(u)]=\hat{U}_{1}(t+T_{C})
of a period
T_{C},
where
\mathcal{T}
is a time order operation.
It is proven that, for arbitrary interaction
\hat{H}_{SB},
by putting an arbitrary fast control into the reduced evolution, the average of an observable
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{C}_{S}
can be given by
(
36
)

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{tr}[\hat{A}\rho_{S}(NT_{C})]=\operatorname{tr}[\hat{A}\rho_{S}(0)],

(18)

where
\rho_{S}(t)=\operatorname{tr}_{B}[\hat{U}_{tot}(t)\rho(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{tot}(t)],
and the controlled coupled evolution reads
\hat{U}_{tot}(t)=\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\exp[-i\int_{0}^{t}du{H}(u)]
with
{H}(t)=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{1}(t)\hat{S}_{\alpha}\hat{U}_{1}(t)\otimes\hat{B}_{\alpha}.
If the control set can be arbitrary, i.e., it covers the whole Hilbert space
\mathcal{C}_{S}=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S}),
the expression Eq. (
18
) can be restated as
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{S}(NT_{C})=\rho_{S}(0).
Let
\mathcal{I}_{S}
denote an interaction space or a space of all effective local operators on the system
S
generated by all
\hat{S}_{\alpha}
except
\hat{H}_{S}.
For a special case, the time integral of the control operators
\hat{U}_{1}
reads

\int_{0}^{T_{C}}du\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{1}(u)\mathcal{I}_{S}\hat{U}_{1}(u)=0,

(19)

which is a shorthand notation for
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{C}}du\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{1}(u)\hat{A}\hat{U}_{1}(u)=0
for all
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{I}_{S}.

In this case, if the interaction space
\mathcal{I}_{S}
is known, one can have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{S}(NT_{C})=e^{-iNT_{C}\overline{H}_{S}}\rho_{S}(0)e^{iNT_{C}\overline{H}_{S}},

(20)

where
\overline{H}_{S}
is a perturbed local Hamiltonian, i.e., a time average of the local Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S},

\overline{H}_{S}=\dfrac{1}{T_{C}}\int_{0}^{T_{C}}du\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{1}(u)\mathcal{H}_{S}\hat{U}_{1}(u).

In other words, the influence of the environment on the system is removed asymptotically.

The procedure above is enabled by two mechanisms. First, the lowest-order Magnus approximation

\hat{U}_{tot}(NT_{C})\approx\exp(-iNT_{C}\overline{H}_{0})=\displaystyle\exp[-iN\int_{0}^{T_{C}}du{H}(u)]

which can be achieved within the given limit. Second, is the symmetry of the average induced by the control set
\mathcal{C}_{S}.
This can be seen from writing
\hat{U}_{1}(t)=\hat{g}_{j}
for
t\in[j\Delta t,(j+1)\Delta t)
and
\Delta t=T_{C}/|\mathcal{G}|,
where
\mathcal{G}=\{\hat{g}_{j}\}
denotes a finite group of unitary operators generating
\mathcal{C}_{S}.
The lowest order Hamiltonian can then be written as

\overline{H}_{0}=\sum_{\alpha}\overline{S}_{\alpha}\otimes\hat{B}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha}\Bigg(\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{j}\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{S}_{\alpha}\hat{g}_{j}\Bigg)\otimes\hat{B}_{\alpha}.

(21)

The group average of an individual term
\overline{S}_{\alpha}
commutes with
\mathcal{G}
and hence
\mathcal{C}_{S}.
The dynamical decoupling argument Eq. (
18
) then follows automatically.
Similarly, in the special case, Eq. (
19
) can be re-expressed in term of group average as

\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{j}\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{j}\mathcal{I}_{S}\hat{g}_{j}=0,

(22)

being a condition for decoupling in Eq. (
20
).

The easiest example of this procedure is the Hahn spin echo experiment where one can decouple the influence of the external field from a spin at the time
\tau
by inversion at
\tau/2

(
48
)
. For instance, for spin dynamics generated by a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\omega}=B_{\omega}\hat{\sigma}_{z}
where
B_{\omega}
is a static random magnetic field, the dynamical map reads
\hat{U}_{\omega}(t)=e^{-itB_{\omega}\hat{\sigma}_{z}}
and we observe that the inversion at
\tau/2
of the evolution
\hat{U}_{\omega}(\tau)
leads to

\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{U}_{\omega}\!\!\left(\tfrac{\tau}{2}\right)\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{U}_{\omega}\!\!\left(\tfrac{\tau}{2}\right)=e^{-i(\tfrac{\tau}{2})B_{\omega}\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\hat{\sigma}_{x}}e^{-i(\tfrac{\tau}{2})B_{\omega}\hat{\sigma}_{z}}=\mathbbm{1},

(23)

since
\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\hat{\sigma}_{x}=-\hat{\sigma}_{z}.
The second inversion operator
\hat{\sigma}_{x}
is used for simplicity. In this example the interaction space
\mathcal{I}_{S}
is generated by the operator
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
while the control space
\mathcal{C}_{S}
is generated by
\hat{\sigma}_{x}.
In other words one can consider
\{\mathbbm{1},\hat{\sigma}_{x}\}
a control group (given that the identity operator is a trivial element), and hence the condition
\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\hat{\sigma}_{x}=-\hat{\sigma}_{z}
is simply the group average

\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\mathbbm{1}+\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\hat{\sigma}_{x}\right)=0,

i.e., the group average of the interaction space
\mathcal{I}_{S}
vanishes. For a nonstatic magnetic field, one needs to incorporate the perturbation into the formulation as in the standard setup above, and the decoupling can be achieved in the asymptotic limit. Remark here that this also exemplifies the case with classical noise as the environmental degree of freedom is represented as a function, e.g. the magnetic field.

For CV systems, there are only a few studies on the application of dynamical decoupling thereon. One of them was done by Vitali and Tombesi in Ref.
(
32
)
where they considered a unitary evolution of two oscillators. It is observed that such an evolution can be reversed by a perturbation of a local parity operator on phase space; in this sense, the perturbed Hamiltonian is the original Hamiltonian with a minus sign. Another recent work by Arenz et al.
(

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\overline{G}_{\omega_{k}}=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}d\tilde{\mu}\hat{G}_{\omega}
, and by the vanishing of average noise operator Eq. (
52
), one could expect that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Bigg(\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{k}\circ\mathcal{U}_{\omega_{k}}\Bigg)\circ\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{I}.

(63)

The next section will discuss this scenario, as well as its related spectroscopy filters, for the squeezing noise and the combined noise introduced in Sec.
II.2
.

IV
Noise Decoupling Protocols for Squeezing Noise and Combine Noise

We have demonstrated the implementation of the DD protocol for the case of displacement noise. We will show that a similar picture can be obtained for the squeezing noise with real parameters. We further illustrate that, under the condition that the control operations can be inserted arbitrarily fast and the noise is stationary, one can also obtain a complete decoupling in the asymptotic limit for the generic squeezing noise and the noise generated by any quadratic polynomial in creation and annihilation operators. Lastly, we give an overview of a possible extension of the protocol to the case for the noise operators generated by a polynomial in creation and annihilation operators of a degree higher than two.

IV.1
Manipulation on Squeezing Noise with Real Positive Squeezing Parameters

Now let us consider the cases when there is only squeezing noise, where we propose a control set inspired by the noise decoupling perspective and then discuss the manipulation in Wigner representation including filtering mechanism and noise spectroscopy.
We begin with the average of noise space
\mathcal{I}_{S},
which is, in this case, generated by
\hat{a}^{2}
and
(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}.
Now we consider

\mathcal{G}_{S}=\{\mathbbm{1},\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}=e^{-i\pi\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}/2}\}

(64)

as a control set
2
2

2

In principle, to make a clear comparison to the noise decoupling scheme, one should consider a cyclic group
\{\mathbbm{1},\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},\hat{R}_{\frac{3\pi}{2}},\hat{R}_{\pi}\}
. Although the set
\mathcal{G}_{S}
is not a cyclic group, it suffices to consider this set for the control of squeeze operators since the squeeze operators have two-fold symmetry, making the action of
\hat{R}^{2}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}
similar to that of
\mathbbm{1},
and
\hat{R}^{3}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}
to
\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},
respectively.
. The average of the noise space for the set
\mathcal{G}_{S}
is zero, i.e.

\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{S}|}\sum_{\hat{g}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{j}\mathcal{I}_{S}\hat{g}_{j}=0,

(65)

since for a real number
\omega,

e^{i\omega\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\hat{a}e^{-i\omega\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}}=e^{i\omega}\hat{a};

or

\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\hat{a}^{2}\mathbbm{1}+\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\hat{a}^{2}\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Big)=\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbbm{1}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\mathbbm{1}+\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Big)=0.

(66)

In other words, the control set
\mathcal{G}_{S}
can be used to modify squeezing noise, and a similar decoupling scheme within the same spirit can be formulated as in the displacement noise.

From the operation picture, similar to the action of
\hat{\Pi},
which reverses displacement operators, the rotation
\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}
does reverse squeeze operators, i.e.,

\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\hat{S}(z)\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}=\hat{S}(-z)=\hat{S}^{\dagger}(z),

(67)

regardless of the parameter
z.
We will use this identity to construct a filter similar to the case of displacement noise. To do so, for the squeezing noise with manipulation

{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\circ\mathcal{S}(z_{n})\circ{\mathcal{A}}_{n-1}\circ\mathcal{S}(z_{n-1})\circ\cdots\circ{\mathcal{A}}_{{1}}\circ\mathcal{S}(z_{1})\circ{\mathcal{A}}_{{0}},

(68)

one can set

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cdot\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},&\text{\penalty\ even\penalty\ }n,\\
\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cdot\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},&\text{\penalty\ odd\penalty\ }n,\end{array}\right.,

(69)

for
k=1,\ldots,n,
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cdot\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}.
Specifically, in parallel with the displacement noise, we define
\mathcal{M}_{k}
and
\mathcal{N}_{k}
via
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{k}\circ\mathcal{N}_{k}
for
2\leq k\leq n-1,
and choose

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\mathbbm{1}\cdot\mathbbm{1},&\text{\penalty\ even\penalty\ }n,\\
\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cdot\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},&\text{\penalty\ odd\penalty\ }n,\end{array}\right.,\mathcal{M}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cdot\hat{R}^{\dagger}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},&\text{\penalty\ even\penalty\ }n,\\
\mathbbm{1}\cdot\mathbbm{1},&\text{\penalty\ odd\penalty\ }n,\end{array}\right.

for
k=1,\ldots,n-1,
and,
\mathcal{A}_{0}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}
are defined as above.
Finally, we arrived at

\mathcal{S}(s_{n}z_{n})\circ\mathcal{S}(s_{n-1}z_{n-1})\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{S}(s_{1}z_{1}),

(74)

where
s_{k}=(-1)^{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,n.

However, unlike the displacement noise, without further assumption, the Eq. (
74
) cannot be combined trivially into a squeeze operation with a parameter comprising the parts. Hence from now on, we focus on real positive parameters
z_{k}=\gamma_{k}>0,
and later will consider an approximation for general squeezing parameters within an appropriate limit. We write

\mathcal{S}(s_{n}\gamma_{n})\circ\mathcal{S}(s_{n-1}\gamma_{n-1})\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{S}(s_{1}\gamma_{1})=\mathcal{S}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\gamma_{k}\bigg).

(75)

Clearly, for the path-independent noise, only one intervention or
n=2
suffices to decouple the noise from the state at the receiver. For the path-dependent noise, without limiting procedure, it leads to a convolution between the target state and a filter function constituted by noise operations and our modification operations.

Recall that

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{S}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k})}[W_{\rho}](\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle=W_{\rho}\left(e^{-\Gamma_{n}}x_{0},e^{\Gamma_{n}}p_{0}\right),

(76)

where
\Gamma_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\gamma_{k}.
For the filter picture in this case one may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\penalty\ \!W_{\rho}\left(e^{-\Gamma_{n}}x_{0},e^{\Gamma_{n}}p_{0}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\Big[f_{n}\ast W_{\rho}\Big](x_{0},p_{0}),

(77)

where for
x\neq 0
and
p\neq 0,

f_{n}(x,p)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i(r\ln|x|+t\ln|p|)}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\Gamma_{n}(r-t)}\big]drdt,

(78)

otherwise, we write

f_{n}(x,p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-it\ln|p|}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\Gamma_{n}r}\big]dt,&x=0\text{\penalty\ and\penalty\ }p\neq 0,\\
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ir\ln|x|}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-i\Gamma_{n}t}\big]dr,&x\neq 0\text{\penalty\ and\penalty\ }p=0,\\
1,&x=p=0.\end{array}\right.

(79)

The form of the filter function here is obtained simply by changing variables from
(x,p)
to
(u,v)=(\ln|x|,\ln|p|)
for
x\neq 0
and
p\neq 0,
following by performing Fourier transform concerning new variables and using the same analysis as in the case of displacement noise. We skip the detailed analysis of the filter functions and revisit the cases of non-additive noise.

IV.2
Manipulation on Squeezing Noise with Complex Squeezing Parameters

For squeezing noise with complex parameters, as previously discussed, one can no longer employ the additivity of the actions on phase space in the case of positive real parameters. To overcome this issue one needs to consider large
n
limit, or equivalently when the quantity
\delta\ell=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}(\ell_{k}-\ell_{k-1})
approaches zero. With this one can sloppily suppose a Trotter-like limit for Eq. (
74
) as

\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{S}(s_{k}z_{k})\approx\mathcal{S}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}z_{k}\bigg),

(80)

for large
n.

Following this one can derive a filter for the noise similar to the case of positive squeezing parameters.

In general, the relation Eq. (
80
) may not hold since the operators involved in the actions are unbounded. However, the range of the parameters is controlled by the distribution
\tilde{\mu},
which is usually supposed to concentrate in some relevant region, and provides a reasonable approximation for the relation Eq. (
80
).
Roughly speaking, by setting
R=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|z_{k}|,
the difference between the left and right hand sides in Eq. (
80
) is controlled by
\mathcal{O}\bigg\{\exp\big[R^{2}(\delta\ell)^{2}\big]\bigg\}.
The contribution from large
R^{2}
is suppressed by the small mass
\tilde{\mu}
around its region while the the contribution
(\delta\ell)^{2}
vanishes within the limit.
In other words, for squeezing noise with complex parameters, it is possible to choose a set of parameters to achieve the filter picture for the noise decoupling scheme via Eq. (
80
), at least in the sense of approximation.

IV.3
Displacement and Squeezing Combine Noise and Observations on Non-Gaussian Noise

Now let us briefly discuss the condition for the generic noise in our model Eq. (
5
). First, recall the form of generator Eq. (
6
)

\displaystyle\hat{G}

\displaystyle(\alpha_{\ell},z_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\Big[\dfrac{1}{2}(z^{*}_{\ell}\hat{a}^{2}-z_{\ell}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2})+(\alpha_{\ell}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}_{\ell}\hat{a})\Big].

(81)

It can be seen that the noise space
\mathcal{I}_{S}
is generated by
\{\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger},(\hat{a})^{2},(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}\}.

We define

\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbbm{1},\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{2}},\hat{R}_{\pi},\hat{R}_{\frac{3\pi}{2}}\},

(82)

leading to

\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\hat{g}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}}\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{j}\mathcal{I}_{S}\hat{g}_{j}=0,

(83)

and hence
\mathcal{G}
can be termed as a control group for the generic noise in our model. Note that control operators in the cases of displacement noise and squeezing noise are also generated by
\mathcal{G}.
By employing the control operators from the group
\mathcal{G}
and using the limiting procedure similar to Eq. (
80
), one will be able to formulate a similar analysis as in the displacement noise, e.g. noise decoupling picture Eq. (
63
) or the filter for noise spectroscopy Eq. (
38
).

Lastly, let us consider a possible extension of the noise beyond the second order in creation and annihilation operators—non-Gaussian noise.
For Gaussian noise (our model), one can see that the control space is simply generated by a cyclic group of rotation with fundamental angle
\dfrac{\pi}{2}.
Now we show that one can construct a control group for non-Gaussian noise also from this principle. In particular, let us consider a noise operator Eq. (
5
) with the replacement of the elementary generators by

\hat{G}(b_{m},\ldots,b_{1})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\big[b_{k}(\ell){\hat{a}}^{k}+{b}^{*}_{k}(\ell)(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{k}\big],

(84)

a degree
m
polynomial in the creation and annihilation operators with (random) complex coefficients
b_{k}(\ell)
and
{b}^{*}_{k}(\ell)
for
k=1,\ldots,m.
The noise space in this case is generated by
\{{\hat{a}}^{k},(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}.
Now we set
\hat{g}_{1}=\hat{R}_{\frac{\pi}{m}}=e^{-i\pi\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}/m}
and the group

\mathcal{G}=\{\hat{g}_{1}^{j}=\hat{R}_{\frac{j\pi}{m}}\}_{j=0}^{2m-1},

(85)

generated by
g_{1}.
Since
\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{a}\hat{g}_{1}=e^{i\pi/m}\hat{a},
it follows that

\sum_{j=0}^{2m-1}(\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{1})^{j}(\hat{a})^{p}\hat{g}_{1}^{j}=\Bigg(\dfrac{1-e^{2ip\pi}}{1-e^{ip\pi/m}}\Bigg)\hat{a}^{p}=0

for
p\leq m.
Hence, we will have

\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\hat{g}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}}\hat{g}^{\dagger}_{j}\mathcal{I}_{S}\hat{g}_{j}=0,

(86)

or the group
\mathcal{G}
here can be considered as a control group for the noise with the generators Eq. (
84
) as claimed.

VI
Conclusions

We considered state transfer problems with continuous variables where the channel by which the state passes is noisy. This problem is inspired by quantum communication by using continuous degrees of freedom in real physical settings, e.g. a photon carrying information via optical fiber or free space communication between satellite and ground stations, where in both cases one can expect a distortion of the signals by the noise from the medium. We modeled such noise as a random mixture of unitary operations. The noisy propagators of the state can be interpreted similarly as random unitary channels, where in this case we employ the path length as a dynamical parameter. We introduced a noise decoupling protocol based on this analogy, where we suppose that the path between the transmitter node and the receiver node can be intervened, allowing us to insert control operations to modify the effective generator of the propagators in such a way that the effect of the noise vanishes, i.e. to achieve an identity channel.

We provided detailed analyses for the noise channels generated by linear and quadratic polynomials of creation and annihilation operators. We observed that the target state can be recovered for the general noise profile when the random variables do not depend on the path length, while for the case with path-dependent but ergodic and stationary noise, such a situation can be achieved for fast interventions. Furthermore, in principle, a similar analysis can be extended to the case of higher-order polynomial generators. We demonstrated our results numerically, where one can see the increase in fidelity improved by the proposed interventions. This suggests a promising technique of noise decoupling protocols to improve the efficiency of communication tasks in real physical settings, e.g. fiber and free space communications, as previously mentioned.

Finally, we remark here that, in our noise model and the similar model literature, the noise comprises two components: the noise generators and the noise profile. The dynamical decoupling technique only deals with the noise generators and analytically avoids the errors caused by the noise characteristics by using the limit procedure and the consideration in an asymptotic regime. In practice, this type of error is important and has been studied in the field of quantum control for several decades in the name of dynamical pulse optimization. A similar limitation of the technique remains in the CV case; we can find a control group for i.e. non-Gaussian noise generators, however, to enable the complete decoupling in the generic scenario, one needs to employ the limit procedure, e.g. the Trotter product-like approximation and has to accept the practical error given by the approximation. In this work, we have shown that the basic principle of dynamical decoupling can be applied and one can find control sets for higher-order noise generators. It is then interesting to consider transferring techniques such as control optimization from dynamical decoupling to noise decoupling in CV systems and develop a practical noise decoupling protocol in CV communication systems. We left these open questions for further studies.
