On fine fluctuations of the complexity of the QuickSelect algorithm
Jasper Ischebeck, Ralph Neininger

Abstract
. The Quickselect algorithm (also called FIND) is a fundamental algorithm for selecting ranks or quantiles within a set of data. Grübel and Rösler showed that the number of key comparisons required by Quickselect considered as a process of the quantiles α ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in[0,1] converges within a natural probabilistic model after normalization in distribution within the càdlàg space D ⁡ [ 0 , 1 ] D[0,1] endowed with the Skorokhod metric. We show that the residual process in the latter convergence after normalization converges in distribution towards a mixture of Gaussian processes in D ⁡ [ 0 , 1 ] D[0,1] . A similar result holds for the related algorithm QuickVal. Our method is applicable to other cost measures such as the number of swaps (key exchanges) required by Quickselect, or cost measures being based on key comparisons taking additionally into account that the cost of a comparison between two keys may depend on their values, an example being the number of bit comparisons needed to compare keys given by their bit expansions. For all the arising mixtures of Gaussian limit processes, we also discuss the Hölder continuity of their paths.



1.
Introduction and results

In 1961, Hoare
[
13
]
introduced the Quickselect algorithm, which he called FIND, to select a key (an element) of a given rank from a linearly ordered finite set of data. We assume that the data are distinct real numbers. To be definite, a simple version of the Quickselect algorithm is given as follows:
\FIND(S,k)
has as input a set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
of distinct real numbers of size
n
and an integer
1\leq k\leq n
. The algorithm
\FIND
operates recursively as follows:
If
n=1
we have
k=1
and
\FIND
returns the single element of
S
. If
n\geq 2
and
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
the algorithm first chooses an element from
S
, say
s_{j}
, called pivot, and generates the sets

\displaystyle S_{<}:=\{s_{i}\,|\,s_{i}<s_{j},i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{j\}\},\quad S_{\geq}:=\{s_{i}\,|\,s_{i}\geq s_{j},i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{j\}\}.

If
k=|S_{<}|+1
, the algorithm returns
s_{j}
. If
k\leq|S_{<}|
, recursively
\FIND(S_{<},k)
is applied. If
k\geq|S_{<}|+2
, recursively
\FIND(S_{\geq},k-|S_{<}|-1)
is applied. Note that
\FIND(S,k)
returns the element of rank
k
from
S
. There are various variants of the algorithm, in particular regarding how the pivot element is chosen and how
S
is partitioned into the subsets
S_{<}
and
S_{\geq}
.

In a standard probabilistic model one assumes that the data are ordered, i.e. given as a vector
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, and are randomly permuted, all permutations being equally likely. This can be achieved assuming that the data are given as
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
where
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\mathrm{unif}[0,1]
, the uniform distribution over the unit interval
[0,1]
. This is the probabilistic model considered below. Note that the randomness is within the data, while the algorithm is deterministic.

Various cost measures have been considered for Quickselect, mainly the number of key comparisons required, which we also analyse. Furthermore, we analyse the number of swaps (key exchanges) required and cost measures which are based on key comparisons, where the cost of a comparison may depend on the values of the keys
s_{i}
,
s_{j}
, and the number of bit comparisons required to decide whether
s_{i}<s_{j}
is a prominent example.

For analysis purposes a related process, called
\mathrm{QuickVal}
, has been considered, see
[
24

10

\mathrm{QuickVal}
for an

. To be definite,

\displaystyle S_{<}:=(U_{j_{1}},\ldots,U_{j_{m-1}}),\quad S_{\geq}:=(U_{j_{m+1}},\ldots,U_{j_{n}}),

The number of key comparisons required by
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}),\alpha)
is then given by the (finite) sum

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{\alpha,k,n}.

Fill and Nakama
[
10

showed (considering more general complexity measures) that for each
\alpha\in[0,1]
almost surely

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}S_{\alpha,n}\to S_{\alpha}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}I_{\alpha,k},\quad(n\to\infty).

The latter convergence also holds in
L_{p}
, see Fill and Matterer
[
8
, Proposition 6.1]
.

We take the point of view that such an almost sure asymptotic result may be considered a strong law of large numbers (SLLN). The subject of the present paper is to study the fluctuations in such SLLN, sometimes called a central limit analogue. We study these fluctuations as processes in the metric space
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
of càdlàg functions endowed with the Skorokhod metric; see
Section

2.1
for the definitions and Billingsley
[
2
]
for background on weak convergence of probability measures on metric spaces in general and on
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
in particular. Note that, by definition,
(S_{\alpha,n})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}
and
(S_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}
have càdlàg paths almost surely.

The analysis of
\mathrm{QuickVal}
is usually considered an intermediate step to analyze the original
\FIND
algorithm. Grübel and Rösler
[
11
]
already pointed out that a version of
\FIND
such as stated above with
C^{\ast}_{n}(k)
denoting the number of key comparisons for finding rank
k
within
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
does not lead to convergence within
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
after the normalization
\alpha\mapsto\frac{1}{n}C^{\ast}_{n}(\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor+1)
, where here and below the convention
C^{\ast}_{n}(n+1):=C^{\ast}_{n}(n)
is used. To overcome this problem they propose a version that does not stop in case a pivot turns out to be the rank to be selected by including the pivot in the list
S_{<}
and proceeding until a list of size
1
is generated. Moreover, their pivots are chosen uniformly at random. The number of key comparisons
C^{\prime}_{n}(k)
for Grübel and Rösler’s
\FIND
-version has the property that

(5)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n}C^{\prime}_{n}\big(\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor+1\big)\right)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}(S_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[0,1]},\quad\mbox{ in }(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}}),

see
[
11
, Theorem 4]
. We may also obtain right-continuous limits with our deterministic choice of pivots by just recursively calling
\FIND(S_{\geq},0)
in case the pivot turns out to be the rank sought. We denote the number of key comparisons for this version by
C_{n}(k)
, which is close to Grübel and Rösler’s
\FIND
-version and also satisfies (
5
).

The convergence in (
5
) could only be stated weakly (not almost surely) since Grübel und Rösler’s
\FIND
-version due to randomization within the algorithm does not have a natural embedding on a probability space. Note that the formulation of the
\mathrm{QuickVal}
complexity does have such an embedding which, e.g., makes the almost sure convergence in (
4
) possible. Also, it is easy to see that we have the distributional equality

(6)

\big(C_{n}\big(\lvert\{U_{i}≤\alpha:1≤i≤n\}\rvert\big)\big)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\overset{d}{=}(S_{\alpha,n})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}.

This allows to naturally couple the complexities on one probability space, which we call its
natural coupling
. See
[
8
, page 807]
for a related discussion of natural couplings.

1.1.
Results

In this section, we collect our results for three different complexity measures, the number of key comparisons, the number of key exchanges (also called swaps), and measures being based on key comparisons taking additionally into account that the cost of a comparison between two keys may depend on their values, an example being the number of bit comparisons needed to compare keys given by their bit expansions.

Part of our results has been announced in the extended abstract
[
14
]
.

1.1.1.
Number of key comparisons

As the normalized process of fluctuations we define

(7)

\displaystyle G_{n}:=(G_{\alpha,n})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}:=\left(\frac{S_{\alpha,n}-nS_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{\mathrlap{\alpha\in[0,1]}}.

Then we have the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S_{\alpha,n}
be the number of key comparisons required by
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}),\alpha)
and
(S_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}
as in (
4
). Then for the fluctuation process
G_{n}
defined in (
7
) we have

\displaystyle G_{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}G_{\infty}\;\mbox{ in }(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})\qquad(n\to\infty),

where
G_{\infty}
is a mixture of centred Gaussian processes with random
covariance function given by

(8)

\Sigma_{\infty,\alpha,\beta}:=\sum_{k=0}^{J}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}I_{\alpha,j\vee k}+\mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha\neq\beta\}}(J+1)\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}(I_{\beta,j})-S_{\alpha}S_{\beta},\quad\alpha,\beta\in[0,1],

where
J=J(\alpha,\beta):=\max\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\,|\,\tau_{\alpha,k}=\tau_{\beta,k}\}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
. We have the representation

(9)

G_{\infty}\overset{d}{=}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}}Z_{\phi}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})},

with
Z_{\phi}
defined in Section
2.4
.

Remark 1.2
.

An alternative representation of the random covariance function in (
8
) is as follows: With a random variable
V
uniformly distributed over
[0,1]
and independent of the
(U_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we have

(10)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\infty,\alpha,\beta}=\Cov\big(J(V,\alpha),J(V,\beta)\>\big|\>\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\big),

with the
\sigma

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\sigma\big\{I_{\phi}\mid\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}\big\}.

Remark 1.3
.

A related functional limit law for the complexity of Radix Selection, an algorithm to select ranks based on the bit expansions of the data, with a limiting Gaussian process with a covariance function related to (
10
) can be found in
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
. See
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
for another related functional limit law.

Remark 1.4
.

In the recent preprint Fill and Matterer
[
9
, Theorems 5.1 and 6.1.]
convergence of the one-dimensional marginals for the functional limit law in Theorem
1.1
is shown, in distribution and with all moments. See also the PhD thesis, Matterer
[
20
, Theorem 6.4]

To transfer Theorem
1.1
to
\FIND

C_{n}(0):=C_{n}(1)
and
C_{n}(n+1):=C_{n}(n)
are used.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
C_{n}(k)
be the number of key comparisons required to select rank

within a set of
n

\FIND
with the natural coupling (
6

Let

\displaystyle\left(\frac{C_{n}(\lfloor t(n+1)\rfloor)-nS_{\Lambda_{n}(t)}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}G_{\infty}\;\mbox{ in }(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}}),

where

is the process defined in Theorem

.

1.1.2.
Number of swaps

Usually, QuickSelect is implemented in-place, which means that it only requires the memory for the list
S
of values and a bounded amount of additional memory. This is achieved by generating the sets
S_{<}
and
S_{\geq}
by swapping values within the ordered list that contains the elements of
S
so that the elements of
S_{<}
and
S_{\geq}
are moved to contiguous parts of the original list. Such a procedure is called
partition
. There are various
partition
procedures, we are discussing two of them.

The original
partition
procedure by Hoare
[
12
]
searches the list
S
from both ends at once: It repeatedly finds the index
i=\min\{2≤i≤n\mid U_{i}>U_{1}\}
of the leftmost element bigger than the pivot and the index
j:=\max\{2≤j≤n\mid U_{j}<U_{1}\}
of the rightmost element smaller than the pivot. If
i<j
, it swaps
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
. Else the algorithm terminates.

A simpler, but less efficient implementation is the so-called Lomuto partition scheme
[
1
,
3
,
19
]
that only searches from one end of
S
. It keeps track of the amount
i
of elements at the start of the list it has already swapped. In every step, it finds the index
j:=\max\{2≤j≤n\mid U_{j}<U_{1}\}
of the rightmost element smaller than the pivot. If
i+1<j
, it swaps
U_{i+1}
and
U_{j}
and increases
i
by one. Otherwise, the algorithm terminates.

Both partition schemes only compare elements to the pivot, so the model of randomness is preserved within the sublists
S_{<}
and
S_{\geq}
. However, their original order is not preserved, so QuickSelect run on
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
will usually not select the same pivots as QuickSelect on
U_{1},\dots,U_{n+1}
. For convenience, we assume that the pivot to split a sublist
S^{\prime}
of
S
is the element of
S^{\prime}
that came first in the original list
S
. We call this choice of the pivots a
suitable embedding
.

(i) Hoare’s partition.

For Hoare’s partition procedure, via a hypergeometric distribution, for details see Section
2.5
, the expected number of swaps in step
k
given
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
is approximately
nI_{\alpha,k+1}(I_{\alpha}-I_{\alpha,k})/I_{\alpha,k}
, which leads to the limit process
L=(L_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}
given by

(12)

L_{\alpha}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{I_{\alpha,k+1}(I_{\alpha}-I_{\alpha,k})}{I_{\alpha,k}},\quad\alpha\in[0,1].

With the
Z_{\phi}
appearing in Theorem
1.1
and
\{Y_{\phi}\,|\,\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}\}
a set of i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables being independent of
\{Z_{\phi}\,|\,\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}\}
and of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
the limiting process
G^{\mathrm{swap}}=(G^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}
is given by

(13)

G^{\mathrm{swap}}_{\alpha}:=\;\sum_{\mathclap{\phi\in\{\phi(k,\alpha)\,|\,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}}}\quad Y_{\phi}\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}^{3/2}}+Z_{\phi 0}\frac{I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}+Z_{\phi 1}\frac{I_{\phi 0}}{I_{\phi}}-Z_{\phi}\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}^{2}},\quad\alpha\in[0,1].

The
Y_{\phi}
represent fluctuations caused by the hypergeometric distribution related to the present partition procedure, while the terms containing
Z_{\phi}
represent fluctuations around the limit (
12
).
Then we have the following result for key exchanges corresponding to Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
K_{\alpha,n}
be the number of key exchanges required by
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\dots,U_{n}),\allowbreak\alpha)
with Hoare’s partition algorithm in a suitable embedding. Then, as

, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{K_{\alpha,n}-nL_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}G^{\mathrm{swap}}\quad\mbox{in }(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}}).

Furthermore, the latter convergence also holds jointly with the convergence for the comparisons,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{S_{\alpha,n}-nS_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}G_{\infty}\quad\mbox{in }(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}}),

from
Theorem

1.1
, when
G^{\mathrm{swap}}
and
G_{\infty}
are constructed as in (
13
) and (
50
) respectively using the same

.

(ii) Lomuto’s partition.

The Lomuto partition procedure is simpler to implement and much easier to analyze. It swaps the pivot and every element smaller than the pivot, so the amount of swaps at some path
\phi\in\{0,1\}^{*}
is given by
S_{\phi 0}+1
. Thus, the limiting process
G^{\mathrm{Lo}}=(G^{\mathrm{Lo}}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in[0,1]}
is

G^{\mathrm{Lo}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Z_{\phi(\alpha,k)0},\quad\alpha\in[0,1].

Our proof for the number of key comparisons in Theorem
1.1
can be straightforwardly transferred to yield:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
K_{\alpha,n}^{\mathrm{Lo}}
be the number of key exchanges required by
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\dots,U_{n}),\alpha)
with Lomuto’s partition procedure in a suitable embedding. Then, as
n\to\infty
, we have

(14)

\left(\frac{K_{\alpha,n}^{\mathrm{Lo}}-n\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}I_{\phi(\alpha,k)0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}G^{\mathrm{Lo}}\quad\mbox{in }(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}}).

1.1.3.
\varepsilon
-tame cost functions

We now consider the model where the cost to compare two keys depends on their values. These costs are described by a measurable
cost function

\beta:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,\infty)
, and we require that they have a polynomial tail, that is: There are constants
c
,
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
u\in[0,1],x\in\mathbb{N}
and for
V
being
\unif[0,1]
distributed

\mathbb{P}\left(\beta(u,V)≥x\right)\leq cx^{-1/\varepsilon}.

This condition is called
(c,\varepsilon)
-tameness
, see
[
10
,
20
,
9
]
, and
\beta
is called
\varepsilon
-tame
if it is
(c,\varepsilon)
-tame for some
c>0
. Note that, e.g.,
\beta
counting the number of bit comparisons is
\varepsilon
-tame for all
\varepsilon>0
.
The costs of
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}),\alpha)
in this model are given by

S^{\beta}_{\alpha,n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\tau_{\alpha,k}<i≤n}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}(U_{i})\beta(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k}},U_{i})

and the limit is, with
V
being
\unif[0,1]
distributed and independent of the
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
, given as

S^{\beta}_{\alpha,\infty}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}(V)\beta(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k}},V)\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\right]

[
9
]
show for
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
that for fixed
\alpha\in[0,1]
the resulting residual

G^{\beta}_{\alpha,n}:=\frac{S^{\beta}_{\alpha,n}-nS^{\beta}_{\alpha,\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}

converges to a mixed centred Gaussian random variable
G^{\beta}_{\alpha,\infty}
in distribution and with all moments. It is possible to combine them to a mixture of centred Gaussian processes

(15)

\displaystyle G^{\beta}_{\infty}=(G^{\beta}_{\alpha,\infty})_{\alpha\in[0,1]},

defined by the conditional covariance functions given, with

(16)

X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}:=\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}(V)\cdot\beta(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k,n}},V),

by

(17)

\Cov\big(G^{\beta}_{\alpha,\infty},G^{\beta}_{\gamma,\infty}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\big)=\Cov\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k},\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}X^{\beta}_{\gamma,k}\>\big|\>\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\right),\quad\alpha,\gamma\in[0,1].

We show later, in
Lemma

2.6
, that the latter covariance is well-defined for
s<\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Note that now the total cost for all key comparisons required by
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}),\alpha)
or
\FIND((U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}),k)
is no longer determined by the fact that the ranks of
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
form an uniformly random permutation. Here, the distribution of the
U_{i}
matters. We only consider the uniform distribution as for the other cost measures in the present paper and leave other distributions for future work. We have the following result corresponding to Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.8

(
\varepsilon
-tame cost function)

It is obvious from the covariance function that the limit process

The first metric we consider is

is coarser than the Euclidean topology on
[0,1]
.

The second metric we consider is the canonical metric of the (mixed) Gaussian process

given by

(19)

We show in

that the metrics

and

are a.s. equivalent.

Theorem 1.9
.

For the limit processes

2.1.
On Weak Convergence in
D[0,1]

Within the space
D[0,1]
of all càdlàg functions, closeness of functions
f,g\in D[0,1]
is measured in the Skorokhod metric

(20)

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{SK}}(f,g)=\inf_{\lambda}\max\big\{\lVert f\circ\lambda-g\rVert_{\infty},\,\lVert\lambda-\id\rVert_{\infty}\big\},

where the infimum is taken over all increasing bijections
\lambda:\>[0,1]\to[0,1]
and
\id
denotes identity.

To obtain weak convergence on
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
we first show tightness so that our sequences of processes in Theorems
1.1
,
1.6
and
1.8
have weakly convergent subsequences respectively, and then show uniqueness of the limit. The tightness on
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
can be shown using an equivalent of the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, whereas the uniqueness follows from the fdd convergence.

For some
x\in D[0,1]
and
\delta>0
, we define a version of a modulus of continuity by

(21)

w^{\prime}_{x}(\delta)=\inf_{\{t_{i}\}}\max_{0<i≤r}\sup_{t_{i-1}≤s,u<t_{i}}\lvert x(s)-x(u)\rvert.

The infimum is taken over finite sets
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
with

\begin{cases}0=t_{0}<\dots<t_{r}=1\text{ and}\\
t_{i}-t_{i-1}>\delta\quad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,r.\end{cases}

We start recalling a well-known tightness criterion:

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 13.2 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\{P_{n}\}_{{n\in\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of probability measures on
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
. The family
\{P_{n}\}
is tight if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

For each
\eta>0
, there exists an
a>0
such that

(22)

\sup_{n\geq 1}P_{n}(\{x:\>\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}>a\})≤\eta,

(2)

For all
\varepsilon,\eta>0
, there exist
0<\delta<1
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(23)

\sup_{n\geq n_{0}}P_{n}(\{x:\>w^{\prime}_{x}(\delta)≥\varepsilon\})≤\eta.

We derive from the latter theorem our own criteria for convergence:

Proposition 2.2

(Convergence Criterion)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be a sequence of random variables in
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
. Suppose that for every
K\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist random càdlàg step functions
X^{K}_{1},X^{K}_{2},\dots
such that their jumps are all contained in the set
\{U_{\phi}\mid\lvert\phi\rvert<K\}
. If

(1)

For all
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in[0,1]
, the marginals
\mathcal{L}(X_{n}(\alpha_{1}),\dots,X_{n}(\alpha_{n}))
converge weakly to some distribution
\mu_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}}
, and

(2)

For all
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\lVert X_{n}-X^{K}_{n}\big\rVert_{\infty}>\varepsilon\Big)=0,

then
X_{n}
converges weakly to some random process
X
on
(D[0,1],d_{\mathrm{SK}})
as
n\to\infty
, and for
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in[0,1]
, we have

(24)

(X(\alpha_{1}),\dots,X(\alpha_{r}))\sim\mu_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}}.

Proof.

We first show that the set of distributions
\{\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}},\dots\}
is tight using
Theorem

2.1
.
Observe that since
I_{\phi}>0
a.s. for all
\phi\in\{0,1\}^{*}
, there exists for every
K
a random variable
\delta_{K}
such that all points in
\{U_{\phi}\mid\lvert\phi\rvert<K\}
are at least
\delta_{K}
away from 0, 1 and each other.
Hence, for every
\delta≤\delta_{K}
, the points
\{0,1\}\cup\{U_{\phi}\mid\lvert\phi\rvert<K\}
are a valid choice for the grid
\{t_{0}<\dots<t_{r}\}
in (
21
). But since the processes
X^{K}_{n}
are constant between these points,

w^{\prime}_{X_{n}}(\delta)≤\max_{0<i≤r}\sup_{t_{i-1}≤s,u<t_{i}}\lvert X_{n}(s)-X_{n}(u)\rvert≤2\big\lVert X_{n}-X^{K}_{n}\big\rVert_{\infty}.

To show (
23
), let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, for every
n,K\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(w^{\prime}_{X_{n}}(\delta)>\varepsilon)≤\mathbb{P}(\delta_{K}<\delta)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\lVert X_{n}-X_{n}^{K}\rVert_{\infty}>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big).

For
n
and
K
sufficiently large, the last term is arbitrarily small; by then choosing
\delta
small, the first term is also small, showing (
23
). We now can use (
23
) to show (
22
): Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Every subinterval with length at least
2^{-r}
contains a dyadic number
j2^{-r}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,

\lVert X_{n}\rVert_{\infty}≤w^{\prime}_{X_{n}}(2^{-r})+\max_{j=0,\ldots,2^{r}}\>\big\lvert X_{n}(j2^{-r})\big\rvert.

By (
23
), the first term fulfills

(25)

\mathbb{P}\big(w^{\prime}_{X_{n}}(2^{-r})>1\big)<\eta/2

for sufficiently large
r
and
n
.
By our first condition,
(X_{n}(j2^{-r})\mid 0≤j≤2^{r})
converges weakly for
n\to\infty
, so it is a tight family on
\mathbb{R}^{2^{r}}
. Therefore there exists a
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(26)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j=0,\ldots,2^{r}}\>\big\lvert X_{n}(j2^{-r})\big\rvert>\rho\right)<\eta/2.

Combining (
25
) and (
26
), we get (
22
) for all
n
bigger than some
n_{0}
, by possibly further enlarging
\rho
, this also holds for
n<n_{0}
.

By
Theorem

2.1

\{\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}},\dots\}
is tight, so
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
has a subsequence converging in distribution to some random variable
X
. By Theorem 13.1 in
[
2
]
and the discussion preceding it, condition (1) implies that
X
is the unique limit and that

X(\alpha_{1}),\dots,X(\alpha_{r})\sim\mu_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}}

for
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in[0,1]
such that
X
is a.s. continuous in
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
. For (
24
), it remains to show that for all
a\in[0,1]
the function
X
is a.s. continuous in
a
.

On
D[0,1]
, the function
x\mapsto x(a)-x(a-)
has discontinuities, but we may approximate it with

J_{\delta,a}(x):=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}x(a+s)\mathrm{d}s-\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{-\delta}^{0}x(a+s)\mathrm{d}s\longrightarrow x(a)-x(a-)\quad(\delta\downarrow 0).

Since Skorokhod convergence implies pointwise convergence almost everywhere and convergence of the maximum, we obtain that
J_{\delta,a}
is a continuous function.

We show that
J_{\delta,a}(X)\to 0
for
\delta\to 0
a.s. Let
\varepsilon>0
, then by weak convergence

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left(J_{\delta,a}(X)>\varepsilon\right)≤\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(J_{\delta,a}(X_{n})>\varepsilon\right).

For any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
J_{\delta,a}≤\lVert X_{n}-X^{K}_{n}\rVert_{\infty}
if there is no
U_{\phi}
,
\lvert\phi\rvert<K
closer than
\delta
to
a
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(J_{\delta,a}(X_{n})>\varepsilon\right)≤\inf_{K\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert X_{n}-X^{K}_{n}\rVert_{\infty}>\varepsilon\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\phi:\>\lvert\phi\rvert<K,\,\lvert U_{\phi}-a\rvert<\delta\right).

The first term is small for
n
,
K
sufficiently large, the second term is small for
\delta
sufficiently small. With this,
X
is a.s. continuous in
a
.

∎

2.2.
Perturbation of the data

\mathrm{QuickVal}
splits an interval
[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})
by the first value falling into
[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})
denoted by
U_{\tau_{\phi}}
. Obviously, this implies dependencies between the data
U_{i}
and the lengths
I_{\phi}
of the intervals
[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})
. In the present section we construct a perturbed sequence
(\widetilde{U}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
to the data
(U_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that we gain independence of
(\widetilde{U}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
from the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
of the interval lengths defined in (
11
). In particular, we aim that conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
the number of data
(\widetilde{U}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{U}_{n})
falling into an interval
[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})
is binomial
B(n,I_{\phi})
distributed, see Lemma
2.4
below.

Every value
U_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, falls successively into subintervals generated by
\mathrm{QuickVal}
until becoming a pivot element. These subintervals correspond to the path between the root of the corresponding binary search tree and the node where
U_{i}
is inserted. Let
\phi_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
denote the node where
U_{i}
is inserted. Hence, we have
\tau_{\phi_{i}}=i
and
U_{i}=L_{\phi_{i}}+I_{\phi_{i}0}
.

Let
(V_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d.
\mathrm{unif}[0,1]
random variables being independent of
(U_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. We define

(27)

\widetilde{U}_{i}:=L_{\phi_{i}}+I_{\phi_{i}}V_{i}.

Lemma 2.3
.

The sequence
(\widetilde{U}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (
27
) consists of i.i.d.
\mathrm{unif}[0,1]
distributed random variables and is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\widetilde{U}_{i}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
and
\widetilde{U}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{U}_{i-1}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We use infinitesimal notation to denote this claim by

\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{U}_{i}\in\mathrm{d}u\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\infty},\widetilde{U}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{U}_{i-1}\right)=\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(u)\mathrm{d}u,\quad i\in\mathbb{N}.

For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
the random variables
\widetilde{U}_{i}
and
U_{i}
fall into the same interval
[L_{\phi_{i}},R_{\phi_{i}})
, hence
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{i-1}
are determined by
\widetilde{U}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{U}_{i-1}
. Let us additionally condition on
\phi_{i}
, then, by definition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{U}_{i}\in\mathrm{d}u\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\infty},\widetilde{U}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{U}_{i-1},\phi_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{I_{\phi_{i}}}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\phi_{i}},R_{\phi_{i}})}(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Note that
\phi_{i}
denotes one of the
i
external nodes of the binary search tree with internal nodes denoted by
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{i-1}
. We denote by
\mathrm{Ext}_{i-1}
the set of the labels of these external nodes. Hence, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{\infty},\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{i-1}
the label
\phi_{i}
is chosen from
\mathrm{Ext}_{i-1}
with probability given by the length of the corresponding interval, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\phi_{i}=\phi\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\infty},\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{i-1})=I_{\phi}
for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Ext}_{i-1}
. Thus, by the law of total probability we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{U}_{i}\in\mathrm{d}u\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\infty},\widetilde{U}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{U}_{i-1}\right)=\sum_{\phi\in\mathrm{Ext}_{i-1}}I_{\phi}\frac{1}{I_{\phi}}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(u)\mathrm{d}u.

This implies the assertion.
∎

The
\widetilde{U}_{i}
are now coupled with the
U_{i}
but independent of the
I_{\phi}
. To compare with the number of key comparisons required by
\mathrm{QuickVal}((U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}),\alpha)
we define

\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,k,n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}(\widetilde{U}_{i}).

Lemma 2.4
.

Conditional on
I_{\alpha,k}
we have that
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,k,n}
has the binomial B
(n,I_{\alpha,k})
distribution. Moreover, for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

we have

(28)

S_{\alpha,k,n}\leq(\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,k,n}-1)^{+}≤S_{\alpha,k,n}+k-1.

Proof.

The conditional distribution of
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,k,n}
follows from Lemma
2.3
.
Recall that
S_{\alpha,k,n}
is defined as
\sum_{i=\tau_{\alpha,k}}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{L_{\alpha,k-1}\leq U_{i}<R_{\alpha,k-1}\}
. By definition,
U_{i}
and
\widetilde{U}_{i}
are in the interval

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. If

appears as a pivot after the

k

itself does not contribute to

For the right inequality, assume for some

2.4.
Explicit construction of the limit process

Note that on a step-wise level, for finitely many
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
of paths,

G_{\phi_{1},n},\dots,G_{\phi_{m},n}
converge, conditional on interval sizes, jointly to normally distributed random variables
Z_{\phi_{1}},\allowbreak\dots,Z_{\phi_{m}}
, with covariances given by

(47)

\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\phi}Z_{\psi}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\right]=\big\lvert[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})\cap[R_{\psi},L_{\psi})\big\rvert=\big((R_{\phi}\wedge R_{\psi})-(L_{\phi}\vee L_{\psi})\big)^{+}

for
\phi,\psi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
, where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denotes the length of an interval.
Using the Kolmogorov extension theorem
[
15
, Theorem 6.17]
, one can construct the entire family

Lemma 2.11
.

Almost surely, for all but finitely many
\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
we have

(48)

\lvert Z_{\phi}\rvert<2\sqrt{\lvert\phi\rvert I_{\phi}}.

Proof.

Note that
Z_{\phi}
has the normal distribution with variance
I_{\phi}-I_{\phi}^{2}>I_{\phi}
. Let
Z
be
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
-distributed. Then

(49)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert Z_{\phi}\rvert>2\sqrt{\lvert\phi\rvert I_{\phi}}\right)≤\mathbb{P}\left(Z>2\sqrt{\lvert\phi\rvert}\right)≤\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{2\sqrt{\lvert\phi\rvert}}^{\infty}ye^{-y^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}y=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-2\lvert\phi\rvert}.

Since
e^{2}>2
, the sum of these probabilities over all
\phi
remains finite and hence the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies the assertion.
∎

With
Lemma

2.11
and
Lemma

2.6
,
\sum_{\phi}Z_{\phi}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})}
converges uniformly a.s. and

(50)

G_{\infty}\overset{d}{=}\sum_{\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}}Z_{\phi}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\phi},R_{\phi})},

since left- and right-hand sides of the latter display have the same covariance function and thus the same finite dimensional distributions (cf.
[
2
, Theorem 13.1]
). We can thus use the left- and right-hand sides of (

) as an explicit construction of

and henceforth assume pointwise equality in (

).

2.5.
Number of swaps

In this section, we present a proof for Theorem
1.6
.

In Hoare’s partition algorithm applied to a uniform permutation, the number of swaps is well known to be, conditional on the rank of the pivot element, hypergeometrically distributed, see, e.g.,
[
18
,
19
,
4
]
. The parameters of this hypergeometric distribution, in our notation, are
n-1
(population size),
S_{0}+1
(number of trials) and
n-S_{0}
(number of successes).
Let
K_{\phi,n}
denote the number of swaps at the path
\phi\in\{0,1\}^{*}
when searching within
n
elements. On the
\lvert\phi\rvert
-th level, the swaps at previous levels may have changed the order of the elements. Let
I_{1},\dots,I_{S_{\phi,n}}
denote the indices of elements that are compared to the pivot
U_{\phi}
in the order in which the procedure
partition
sees them. In this notation, the number of swaps at level
k
is given by

(51)

K_{\phi,n}=\lvert\{U_{I_{i}}\mid 1≤i≤S_{\phi 0,n},U_{I_{i}}>U_{\phi}\}\rvert.

Now we use a variation of the perturbation argument from Section
2.2
as follows. We extend
I
by the indices
I_{S_{\phi,n}+1},\dots,I_{\widetilde{S}_{\phi,n}}
of the elements that only contribute to
\widetilde{S}_{\phi_{n}}
in arbitrary order and let

(52)

\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}:=\left\lvert\{\widetilde{U}_{I_{i}}\mid 1≤i≤\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n},\widetilde{U}_{I_{i}}>U_{\phi}\}\right\rvert.

By
Lemma

2.4
,
S_{\phi 0,n}≤\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n}≤S_{\phi 0,n}+\lvert\phi\rvert+1
and we therefore sum over at most
\lvert\phi\rvert+1
more elements compared to
K_{\phi,n}
. Thus,
K_{\phi,n}\leq\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}≤K_{\phi,n}+\lvert\phi\rvert+1
. With (
52
) also the limit process can be interpreted: We have that
\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n}
is close to
nI_{\phi 0}
and the probability of a value to be bigger than the pivot is
I_{\phi 1}/I_{\phi}
, so we expect that
\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}
is close to
n\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}
. The fact that we draw without replacement does asymptotically not matter as
n\to\infty
. Hence, we define the limit process as

(53)

\displaystyle K_{\phi,\infty}

\displaystyle:=\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}},

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,\infty}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}K_{\phi(\alpha,k),\infty}.

As for comparisons in (
31
), we define a process
\overline{W}_{n}
based on the perturbed
\widetilde{K}_{\alpha,n}
:

(54)

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{\phi,n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}-nK_{\phi,\infty}}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle\overline{W}^{\leq K}_{\alpha,n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\overline{W}_{\alpha,k,n},

\displaystyle\overline{W}^{\leq K}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\big(\overline{W}^{\leq K}_{\alpha,n}\big)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}.

and the contributions per step for

as

(55)

\displaystyle\overline{G}_{\phi,n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{K_{\phi,n}-nK_{\phi,\infty}}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle\overline{G}^{\leq K}_{\alpha,n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\overline{G}_{\phi(\alpha,k),n},

\displaystyle\overline{G}^{\leq K}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\big(\overline{G}^{\leq K}_{\alpha,n}\big)_{\alpha\in[0,1]}.

Our result for swaps corresponding to
Lemma

2.5
is as follows:

Lemma 2.12
.

For the number of swaps in Hoare’s partition procedure, above some level
K\in\mathbb{N}
we have, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, weak convergence in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
of

for comparisons in

Proof.

Note that because
\lvert K_{\phi,n}-\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}\rvert≤\lvert\phi\rvert+1

\overline{W}^{\leq K}_{\alpha,n}

. Then, the variables
\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}
for

are hypergeometrically distributed and independent from each other. By
[
16
]
we have conditional convergence

(57)

\left(\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}-\frac{\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n}\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,n}}{\widetilde{S}_{\phi,n}}\right)\left(\frac{{\widetilde{S}_{\phi,n}}^{1.5}}{\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n}\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,n}}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}Y_{\phi}

with

and

hold and if

.
These conditions holds almost surely, so (

By the strong law of large numbers and Slutzky’s lemma, we obtain

(\widetilde{S}_{\phi})_{\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}}
. The next component is the influence of variations in
\widetilde{S}_{\phi,n}
. For this, define the centred sums
\widehat{S}_{\phi,n}:=\widetilde{S}_{\phi,n}-nI_{\phi,n}

\widehat{S}_{\phi,n}=O\left(\sqrt{n\log\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n}\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,n}}{\widetilde{S}_{\phi,n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(nI_{\phi 0}+\widehat{S}_{\phi 0,n})(nI_{\phi 1}+\widehat{S}_{\phi 1,n})}{(nI_{\phi}+\widehat{S}_{\phi,n})}

\displaystyle=n\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}+\widehat{S}_{\phi 0,n}\frac{I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}+\widehat{S}_{\phi 1,n}\frac{I_{\phi 0}}{I_{\phi}}-\widehat{S}_{\phi,n}\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}^{2}}+o(\sqrt{n})

almost surely using the Taylor expansion
\frac{1}{a+x}=\frac{1}{a}-\frac{x}{a\texttwosuperior}+\mathrm{O}(x^{2})

for all
\lvert\phi\rvert≤k
jointly. Since

, it thus converges towards a mixed centred Gaussian process formed by the right-hand side of (

Z_{\phi}
.
∎

(61)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\lvert\phi\rvert=k:\>\lvert\overline{W}_{\phi,n}\rvert>a^{-k}+n^{-0.5},\,K_{1}≤k\right)\leq C_{b}\exp\left(-b^{k}\right)+c(k,n)

where the double sequence
c(k,n)
satisfies

(62)

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor 4.5\log n\rfloor}c(k,n)\to 0.

Proof.

We fix some path
\lvert\phi\rvert=k
and condition on the interval sizes
I_{\phi 0}
and
I_{\phi 1}
. Note that the distribution of
\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}
is invariant under permutations of the elements, so we can, without loss of generality, assume that the partition algorithm processes the elements in their original order.

For any
1\leq t\leq n
, consider how many elements the indices
i
and
j
from Hoare’s partition algorithm have encountered before reaching the element
U_{t}
.
The number
\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,t}
of elements larger than
U_{\phi}
left of
t
is binomially
B\big(t,I_{\phi 1}\big)
-distributed and independent of the number
\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n-t}
of elements smaller than
U_{\phi}
right of
t
, which is

B\big(\widetilde{S}_{\phi}-t,I_{\phi 0}\big)
-distributed. Now if both quantities are smaller than some
x\in\mathbb{N}
, then both indices reach
U_{t}
before swapping
x
elements. When on the other hand both
\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,t}
and
\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n-t}
are at least
x
, then
i
and
j
will swap at least
x
times before reaching
U_{t}
. Hence, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
we can bound, where the symbol
\lessgtr
can be replaced by either
<
(less than) at all three occurrences or by
>
(larger than),

(63)

\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}\lessgtr x\right)≤\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,t}\lessgtr x\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n-t}\lessgtr x\right).

Choosing
t=\lfloor n\tfrac{I_{\phi 0}}{I_{\phi}}\rfloor=n-\lceil n\tfrac{I_{\phi 0}}{I_{\phi}}\rceil
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert\overline{W}_{\phi,n}\rvert\geq a^{-k}+n^{-0.5}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\widetilde{K}_{\phi,n}-n\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}\right\rvert≥\sqrt{n}a^{-k}+1\right)

\displaystyle≤\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\widetilde{S}_{\phi 1,t}-n\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}\right\rvert≥\sqrt{n}a^{-k}+1\right)

(64)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\widetilde{S}_{\phi 0,n-t}-n\frac{I_{\phi 0}I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}\right\rvert≥\sqrt{n}a^{-k}+1\right).

The latter difference is not centred because of rounding, but at most 1 of from centring. This offsets with the additional
n^{-0.5}
, and by Chernoff bounds we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert\overline{W}_{\phi,n}\rvert≥a^{-k}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle≤\exp\left(-\frac{\lfloor n\frac{I_{\phi 0}}{I_{\phi}}\rfloor a^{-2k}}{n(2I_{\phi 1}+a^{-k}n^{-0.5})}-\frac{\lceil n\frac{I_{\phi 1}}{I_{\phi}}\rceil a^{-2k}}{n(2I_{\phi 0}+a^{-k}n^{-0.5})}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle≤\exp\left(-\frac{a^{-2k}}{2I_{\phi}+a^{-k}n^{-0.5}}\right).

This is the same bound as in (
38
) in the proof of
Lemma

2.9
and the remaining of the present proof can then be done analogously.
∎

Proof of

.

By the same proof as for

2.7
, we conclude that

converges to 0 in probability. This guarantees tightness, and with the fdd convergence in

∎

2.6.
\varepsilon
-tame cost functions

In the present section we present a proof for Theorem

First note that the expression in (

) is well-defined in view of the following lemma using
\varepsilon
-tameness, cf.
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
:

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
[L,R)\subseteq[0,1]
be an interval of length
I=R-L>0
and
u\in[L,R)
.
For
V
being
\unif[0,1]
distributed set
X:=\mathbf{1}_{[L,R)}(V)\beta(u,V)
. Then, for every
s\in(0,\varepsilon^{-1})
, uniformly in
L,R
, we have

\mathbb{E}[X^{s}]=I\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[X^{s}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}V\in[L,R)\right]=\mathrm{O}\Big(I^{1-\varepsilon s}\Big).

Proof.

The tail distribution
F(x)=\mathbb{P}(X≥x)
for
x>0
is bounded by both
\mathbb{P}(V\in[L,R))=I
and
\mathbb{P}(\beta(u,V)>x)≤C_{\beta}x^{-\varepsilon^{-1}}
for some constant
C
. These two bounds are equal when
x=(I/C_{\beta})^{-\varepsilon}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{s}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}sx^{s-1}F(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle≤I\int_{0}^{(I/C_{\beta})^{-\varepsilon}}sx^{s-1}\mathrm{d}x+C_{\beta}\int_{(I/C_{\beta})^{-\varepsilon}}^{\infty}sx^{s-1-\varepsilon^{-1}}\mathrm{d}x

(66)

\displaystyle=O\left(I^{1-s\varepsilon}\right)+O\left(I^{-s\varepsilon+1}\right),

where
s\varepsilon<1
is required for the second integral to exist.
∎

We use the same approach as for the other cost measures: First, we show that the costs at some level are close to a sum of independent random variables to which we apply the central limit theorem. Then we show that the residual below some level is converging to
0
.
Since our cost now depends on the actual values
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
instead of just whether the
U_{i}
are contained in the respective interval or not, we cannot define
\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,k,n}
as before. Instead, we only replace
U_{i}
with
\widetilde{U}_{i}
if it is below some level
K\in\mathbb{N}
, defining for
i\in\mathbb{N}

(67)

\widetilde{U}^{(K)}_{i}:=\begin{cases}U_{i}&\lvert\phi_{i}\rvert>K,\\
\widetilde{U}_{i}&\lvert\phi_{i}\rvert≤K\end{cases}

and then for
k≤K

(68)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=\tau_{\alpha,k}+1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}\left(U_{i}\right)\beta\left(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k}},U_{i}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}^{\beta,K}_{\alpha,k,n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}\left(\widetilde{U}^{(K)}_{i}\right)\beta\left(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k}},\widetilde{U}^{(K)}_{i}\right)

(70)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha,k}^{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}\left(v\right)\beta\left(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k}},v\right)\mathrm{d}v.

Note that, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{K}
, the values

\widetilde{U}^{(K)}_{1},\widetilde{U}^{(K)}_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. unif[0,1] and hence

\widetilde{S}^{(K)}_{\alpha,k,n}
is a sum of i.i.d. random variables distributed as
X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}
, with expectation
I_{\alpha,k}^{\beta}
. With these random variables, we define similar to before

(71)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\frac{S_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta}-nI_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta}}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle W_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta,K}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\widetilde{S}_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta,K}-nI_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta}}{\sqrt{n}}.

The
G_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta}
are the single steps within the residual process, and
W_{\alpha,k,n}^{\beta,K}
are the normalized sum of i.i.d. random variables used to approximate them.

Lemma 2.15
.

The residuals
\sum_{k=0}^{K}G_{\alpha,k,n}
above some level
K\in\mathbb{N}
converge conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{K}
to a mixture of centred Gaussian processes with random covariance function given by

(72)

(\alpha,\gamma)\mapsto\Cov\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k},\sum_{k=0}^{K}X^{\beta}_{\gamma,k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{K}\right),

with
X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}
as in (
16
).

Proof.

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{K}
,
\sum_{k=0}^{K}W^{\beta,K}_{\alpha,k,n}
is a step function with
2^{K}
different values. These values are each sums of
n
independent,
\sum_{k=0}^{K}X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}
-distributed variables. By the multivariate central limit theorem
\sum_{k=0}^{K}W^{\beta,K}_{\alpha,k,n}
thus converges to a mixed Gaussian process with covariances (
72
).
The difference between
\sum_{k=0}^{K}W^{\beta,K}_{\alpha,k,n}
and
\sum_{k=0}^{K}G^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}
is at most the different costs of
U_{\phi}
and
\widetilde{U}_{\phi}
at paths
\phi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
,
\lvert\phi\rvert≤k
. Those are finitely many and are then divided by
\sqrt{n}
. By Slutzky’s lemma,
\sum_{k=0}^{K}G^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}
hence also converges to the mixed Gaussian process.
∎

With the same technique as before (see
Lemma

2.8
and the proof of
Theorem

1.1
), we can prove
Theorem

1.8
with the following proposition:

Proposition 2.16
.

For all

the residuals below level

,

to

:

(73)

\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\Big\lvert\sum_{k>K}G_{\alpha,k,n}\Big\rvert\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0

This is a consequence of the following lemma:

Lemma 2.17
.

For
s≥2
, we have uniformly in
\alpha,k,n,I_{\alpha,k}

(74)

\mathbb{E}\left[\big\lvert G^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}\big\rvert^{s}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k}\right]=O\left(I_{\alpha,k}^{2-s\varepsilon}n^{2-\frac{s}{2}}+k^{\frac{s}{2}}I_{\alpha,k}^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)s}\right).

First, let

be even. Then decompose

into

\displaystyle\big(G^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}\big)^{s}

\displaystyle≤n^{-\frac{s}{2}}\left(S^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}-(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}-(\tau_{\alpha,k}\wedge n)I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\right)^{s}

(75)

\displaystyle≤2^{s}n^{-\frac{s}{2}}\left(\left(S^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}-(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\right)^{s}-(\tau_{\alpha,k}\wedge n)^{s}(I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k})^{s}\right).

The first part is a centred sum of
(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}
independent,
X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}
-distributed random variables; the second part arises from
n-(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}=n\wedge\tau_{\alpha,k}
. If we condition on
\tau_{k}
, expand the sum in

and group the resulting terms by the number of occurrences of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}-(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\right)^{s}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k},\tau_{\alpha,k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{i=\tau_{\alpha,k}+1}^{n}\big(\mathbf{1}_{[L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k})}\left(U_{i}\right)\beta\left(U_{\tau_{\alpha,k}},U_{i}\right)-I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\big)\Big)^{s}\nonscript\>\bigg|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k},\tau_{\alpha,k}\bigg]

(76)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{s/2}\binom{(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}}{l}\sum_{i_{1}+\dots+i_{l}=s\atop i_{1},\dots,i_{l}≥2}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\mathbb{E}\left[(X^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}-I_{\alpha,k}^{\beta})^{i_{j}}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k}\right].

By

, and the sum in (

This holds uniformly over all
\tau_{\alpha,k}
. When
\tau_{\alpha,k}≥n
, the latter sum vanishes, so by multiplying with
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\alpha,k}≤n\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}I_{\alpha,k})≤1\wedge I_{\alpha,k}n

(78)

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{s}{2}}\mathbb{E}[S^{\beta}_{\alpha,k,n}-(n-\tau_{\alpha,k})^{+}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k}]=O\Big(I^{2-\varepsilon s}_{\alpha,k}n^{2-\frac{s}{2}}+I^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)s}_{\alpha,k}\Big),

To bound the second summand appearing in (

implies

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{s}{2}}(\tau_{\alpha,k}\wedge n)^{s}\left(I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\alpha,k}^{\frac{s}{2}}\left(I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\right)^{s}.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
,

is the sum of
k
independent geometrically distributed variables with success probabilities

and thus smaller in probability than a sum of

independent Geom(

)-distributed random variables

. Thus

(80)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{\alpha,k}^{\frac{s}{2}}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k}\right]≤\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{j}\Big)^{\frac{s}{2}}\nonscript\>\bigg|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}I_{\alpha,k}\bigg]≤k^{\frac{s}{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{1}^{\frac{s}{2}}\nonscript\>\big|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}I_{\alpha,k}\big]=O(k^{s/2}I_{\alpha,k}^{-s/2}).

Together with

, this implies

(81)

\mathbb{E}\left[n^{-\frac{s}{2}}(\tau_{\alpha,k}\wedge n)^{s}\left(I^{\beta}_{\alpha,k}\right)^{s}\nonscript\>\middle|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}L_{\alpha,k},R_{\alpha,k}\right]=O\left(k^{s/2}I_{\alpha,k}^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)s}\right).

This completes the proof of the present lemma for

∎

\displaystyle≤\big(1+\mathrm{o}(1)\big)\log_{2}\left(d_{2}(\alpha,\beta)\right)\left(\frac{1}{4}\log_{2}\frac{3}{2}\right)(1-2\varepsilon).

This implies
d_{2}

\frac{1}{4}\log_{2}\frac{3}{2}(1-\varepsilon)
.
∎

Lemma 2.20
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
s>\frac{1}{\delta}
we have almost surely for all but finitely many
\phi,\psi\in\{0,1\}^{\ast}
that

(93)

\frac{I_{\phi\psi}}{I_{\phi}^{1-\delta}}≤\lvert\psi\rvert{\left(\frac{2}{1+s}\right)}^{{\lvert\psi\rvert}/s}.

In particular, for
\gamma>0
, the following holds uniformly in
\alpha
:

(94)

\sum_{k>J}\sqrt{k}I_{\alpha,k}^{\gamma}=\sum_{k>J}I_{\alpha,k}^{\gamma(1-\delta)}k^{1.5}{\left(\frac{2}{1+s}\right)}^{\gamma k/s}=\mathrm{O}\left(J^{1.5}I_{\alpha,J}^{\gamma(1-\delta)}\right).

Proof.

Note that
I_{\phi\psi}/I_{\phi}
is the product of
\lvert\psi\rvert
i.i.d. unif[0,1] random variables and independent of
I_{\phi}
, which is also a product of
\lvert\phi\rvert
i.i.d. unif[0,1] random variables. By Markov bounding,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\left(\frac{I_{\phi\psi}}{I_{\phi}^{1-\delta}}\right)}^{s}≤\lvert\psi\rvert^{s}{\left(\frac{2}{1+s}\right)}^{\lvert\psi\rvert}\right)

\displaystyle≤\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\frac{I_{\phi\psi}}{I_{\phi}}\right)}^{s}I_{\phi}^{s\delta}\right]\lvert\psi\rvert^{-s}{\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)}^{\lvert\psi\rvert}

\displaystyle={\left(\frac{1}{1+s}\right)}^{\lvert\psi\rvert}{\left(\frac{1}{1+s\delta}\right)}^{\lvert\phi\rvert}\lvert\psi\rvert^{-s}{\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)}^{\lvert\psi\rvert}

(95)

\displaystyle=2^{-\lvert\psi\rvert-\lvert\phi\rvert}{\left(\frac{2}{1+s\delta}\right)}^{\lvert\phi\rvert}\lvert\psi\rvert^{-s}.

Since
s\delta>1
and
s>1
, the sum of these probabilities over all
\phi
and
\psi
converges. Hence, as in previous arguments, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies the assertion.
∎
