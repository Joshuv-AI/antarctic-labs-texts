On a quadratic Poisson algebra and integrable Lotka-Volterra systems with solutions in terms of Lambert’s W function
Peter H. van der Kamp, D.I. McLaren, G.R.W. Quispel

Abstract
We study a class of integrable inhomogeneous Lotka-Volterra systems whose quadratic terms are defined by an antisymmetric matrix and whose linear terms consist of three blocks. We provide the Poisson algebra of their Darboux polynomials, and prove a contraction theorem. We then use these results to classify the systems according to the number of functionally independent (and, for some, commuting) integrals. We also establish separability/solvability by quadratures, given the solutions to the 2- and 3-dimensional systems, which we provide in terms of the Lambert W function.

The quest for integrating ordinary differential systems with quadratic terms has a long history. In 1879, P. Hoyer studied the system

\dot{x}_{3}&=c_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+c_{2}x_{3}x_{1}+c_{3}x_{1}x_{2},\end{split}

In this paper we study inhomogeneous LV systems of the form

\dot{x}_{i}=x_{i}\,\big(r_{i}+\sum_{j>i}x_{j}-\sum_{j<i}x_{j}\big),\qquad r_{i}=\begin{cases}b,&i=1,\ldots,k,\\

), or two blocks, if

. We will adopt the following definitions. An

•

Liouville integrable:

•

Nonholonomically integrable:

•

Liouville superintegrable:
the system is Liouville integrable, and admits more functionally independent integrals than required.

•

Liouville maximally superintegrable:
the system is Liouville integrable, and admits

•

Integrable-or-not:
the system is not a priori Liouville integrable, but admits more than

Each notion of integrability implies, in principle, that the system can be integrated by quadratures. For systems which are integrable-or-not, this is not necessarily the case.

We have identified nine integrable families of the form (
8
). In even dimensions, we define
the families E1-E5:

\displaystyle l=0,k\equiv m\equiv 1

(E1)

\displaystyle l=0,k\equiv m\equiv 0

(E2)

\displaystyle k\equiv 0,l\equiv m\equiv 1

(E3)

\displaystyle k\equiv m\equiv 1,l\equiv 0

(E4)

\displaystyle k\equiv l\equiv m\equiv 0,

(E5)

in odd dimensions we define O1-O3:

\displaystyle k\equiv l\equiv m\equiv 1

(O1)

\displaystyle k\equiv 1,l\equiv m\equiv 0,b=0

(O2)

\displaystyle k\equiv m\equiv 0,l\equiv 1,c=0,

(O3)

and N1 is given by

\displaystyle l=0,k\equiv 1,m\equiv 0,

(N1)

where equivalence is taken modulo 2. We remark that the case

is equivalent to

, the case
k\equiv l\equiv 0,m\equiv 1,d=0
is equivalent to the case

, and the case

is equivalent to the case

, by a linear transformation.

We show that the E (even) and O (odd) cases are Liouville integrable, and that the N case is Nonholonomically integrable. We further classify the O and E systems with respect to their number of integrals; some are Liouville maximally superintegrable, some are nonholonomically integrable and others only have
n-3
integrals. We also define the following cases:

\displaystyle k\equiv 1,l\equiv m\equiv 0,b\neq 0

(M1)

\displaystyle k\equiv m\equiv 0,l\equiv 1,c\neq 0,

(M2)

and note that the case
k\equiv l\equiv 0,m\equiv 1,d\neq 0
is equivalent to

8
). We also show that the entire family is separable and solvable by quadratures, given the solution to the special cases
k=l+1=m=1
and
k=l=m=1
. This is somewhat surprising as it seems that the M systems are neither Liouville integrable, nor nonholonomically integrable.

The paper is organised as follows.
In section
2
, we define two sets of Darboux functions, sums of consecutive variables and products of consecutive variables with alternating exponents, and provide the quadratic Poisson algebra they satisfy (with respect to the bracket given in
[
22
, Equation (3.6)]
. We identify functions (on even-dimensional spaces) that act as scalings.

In section
3
, we provide the Hamiltonian theory of the general class of systems of inhomogeneous LV systems, and show that for systems whose quadratic part is the same as (
8
) the Hamiltonian (if it exists) can be written in terms of the scaling functions.

In section
4
, we provide an alternative proof for the Liouville maximally superintegrability of equation (
5
) and of a similar but odd-dimensional system. We distinguish between integrals that depend on all the variables (which could be called global), and integrals that depend on a subset of the variables within a block, i.e. variables
x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j}
, with
r_{i}=r_{i+1}=\cdots=r_{j}
. These will be called local integrals. Using local integrals the proofs of involutivity and independence simplify quite drastically.
In section
5
we explain how for given values of
k,l
and
m
, one can find out how many independent integrals exist, that are products of powers of the Darboux functions and an exponential factor. In higher dimensional systems, we find that the bulk of the independent integrals can be given in terms of local integrals. However, in all cases one needs a few global ones (at least one, at most three). The origin of these global integrals can be understood by what we have dubbed the contraction theorem. By a linear transformation, we can reduce the system to a system where one or more blocks are smaller in size. Integrals from the contracted system can be lifted to integrals for the original system. And, conveniently, lifted integrals commute with local integrals.

In section
6
, we provide sets of sufficiently many integrals for the various notions of integrability. These sets are inferred from integrals we have calculated in low dimensions. In section
7
, we prove that the sets of integrals postulated in section
6
, are functionally independent in any dimension, and, in the Liouville integrable cases, sufficiently many of them Poisson commute. In section
8
, we provide the general solution for the two and three dimensional systems of the form (
8
) with
(k,l,m)=(1,0,1)
and
(k,l,m)=(1,1,1)
, and we provide a separation of variables for the general system in any dimension. The final section comprises a summary and outlook.

2
A quadratic Poisson algebra

All known integrals of the systems (
3
-
5
) can be expressed in terms of

P_{i,j}=x_{i}+x_{i+1}+\cdots+x_{j},\qquad Q_{i,j}=x_{i}^{-1}x_{i+1}x_{i+2}^{-1}\cdots x_{j}^{(-1)^{j-i+1}},

(9)

where it is understood that
P_{i,j}=0
and
Q_{i,j}=1
when
i>j
.
The Poisson brackets of these functions, with respect to

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=x_{i}\,x_{j},\qquad i<j,

(10)

are provided in the following lemma, which is proved in Appendix A.

Lemma 1
.

Let
[m,p]=[i,j]\cap[k,l]
, and let, taking
k\leq l
,

c^{k,l}_{a}=\begin{cases}0&a<k\\
1&k\leq a\leq l\\
0&a>l,\end{cases}\text{ when }l\not\equiv k,\text{ and }c^{k,l}_{a}=\begin{cases}-1&a<k\\
0&k\leq a\leq l\\
1&a>l\end{cases}\text{ when }l\equiv k,

(11)

where, here and in the sequel, equivalence is taken modulo 2.
The Poisson algebra of the functions (
9
) is a quadratic Poisson algebra:

\displaystyle\{P_{i,j},P_{k,l}\}=

\displaystyle P_{i,j}\,P_{k,l}-P_{m,p}^{2}-2\,P_{m,p}\,P_{l+1,j}\qquad(k\geq i)

(12)

\displaystyle\{P_{i,j},Q_{k,l}\}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a=i}^{j}c^{k,l}_{a}\,x_{a}\right)Q_{k,l}

(13)

\displaystyle\{Q_{i,j},Q_{k,l}\}=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a=i}^{j}(-1)^{a+i+1}\,c^{k,l}_{a}\right)Q_{i,j}\,Q_{k,l}.

(14)

The sum in (
13
) can be expressed as

\sum_{a=i}^{j}c^{k,l}_{a}\,x_{a}=\begin{cases}P_{m,p}&k\not\equiv l,\\
-P_{i,\min(j,k-1)}+P_{\max(l+1,i),j}&k\equiv l.\end{cases}

The sum in (
14
) has values
0
or
\pm 1
. We have

\sum_{a=i}^{j}(-1)^{a+i+1}c^{k,l}_{a}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{m+i+1}\,\#_{m,p}&k\not\equiv l,\\
\#_{i,\min(j,k-1)}-(-1)^{\max(i,l+1)+i}\#_{\max(i,l+1),j}&k\equiv l,\end{cases}

where
\#_{m,p}\in\{0,1\}
tells whether the interval
[m,p]
contains an even or an odd number of integers. Taking
i=j
,
k=1
, and
l=n
odd, we find
\{x_{i},Q_{1,n}\}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
which shows that
Q_{1,n}
is a Casimir function.

A
scaling
, with respect to variable
x_{j}
, on a Poisson manifold is a function
\sigma_{j}
such that when
P
is a homogeneous polynomial in
x_{j}
, of weight (or degree)
d
, the Poisson bracket with
\sigma_{j}
is
\{P,\sigma_{j}\}=dP
.

Lemma 2
.

On the even
n
-dimensional Poisson manifold with bracket (
10
), the functions

\sigma_{j}=\ln\left(Q_{1,j-1}^{(-1)^{j}}\,Q_{j+1,n}^{-1}\right)

(15)

are scalings. The total scaling is

\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}=\ln\left(Q_{1,n}\right).

(16)

Proof.

We have
\{x_{k},\sigma_{j}\}=x_{k}\delta_{k,j}
, which follows from Lemma
1
. The equality (
16
) is a special case of

\sum_{i=k}^{l}\sigma_{i}=\begin{cases}\ln\left(Q_{k,l}\right)&k\not\equiv l\\
\ln\left(Q_{1,k-1}^{(-1)^{k}}\,Q_{l+1,n}^{-1}\right)&k\equiv l,\\
\end{cases}

(17)

which is proved by induction.
∎

The expression (
17
) will be referred to as a partial scaling, and denoted by
\sigma_{k}^{l}=\sum_{i=k}^{l}\sigma_{i}
.

Hamiltonians for LV-systems

The family of Lotka-Volterra equations

\dot{x}_{i}=x_{i}(r_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_{j})

(18)

being the elements of a matrix

{\bf r}\in{\rm Im}({\bf A})

\dot{{\bf x}}=\{{\bf x},H\}

For equations of the form

\dot{x}_{i}=x_{i}\,\big(r_{i}+\sum_{j>i}x_{j}-\sum_{j<i}x_{j}\big),

-1&0&1&\cdots&1\\
-1&-1&0&\cdots&1\\

•

The Hamiltonian case. When

so that, for

(22)

•

The Poisson case. When

is

, and with

{\rm Ker}({\bf A})={\rm Sp}({\bf u})

r_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}r_{i},

in which case we obtain, for

When

H=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{j}+r_{j}\sigma_{j}\right),

Theorem 6

(Contraction theorem)
.

Consider an inhomogeneous Lotka-Volterra system of the form (
20
) with
r_{i}=r
, for
i=l,l+1,\dots,m
. The new variables

y_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&i<l,\\
P_{l,m}&i=l,\\
x_{i-l+m}&i>l.\end{cases}

satisfy an inhomogeneous LV system of the form (
20
) in dimension
n+l-m
with

\tilde{r}_{i}=\begin{cases}r_{i}&i<l,\\
r&i=l,\\
r_{i-l+m}&i>l.\end{cases}

If
F(y)
is an integral of the
y
-system, then
F(y(x))
is an integral of the
x
-system. If
F(y)
and
G(y)
are commuting integrals of the
y
-system, the integrals also commute as functions of
x
. Moreover, for any function
J(y)
, if
K(x_{l},x_{l+1},\ldots,x_{m})
is a local homogeneous integral of weight 0 of the
x
-system, then
\{J(y(x)),K(x)\}=0
.

Proof.

To establish that the
y
-system is of the form (
20
), the main thing we need to prove is
\dot{y}_{l}=y_{l}(b-P_{1,l}+P_{m+1,n})
. This goes as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=l}^{m}\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=l}^{m}x_{i}(b-P_{1,i-1}+P_{i+1,n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=l}^{m}x_{i}(b-P_{1,l-1}-P_{l,i-1}+P_{i+1,m}+P_{m+1,n})

\displaystyle=y_{l}(b-P_{1,l-1}+P_{m+1,n})+Z,

where

Z=\sum_{i=l}^{m}x_{i}(-P_{l,i-1}+P_{i+1,m})=\sum_{i,j=l}^{m}x_{i}A_{i,j}x_{j}=0

as
A
is antisymmetric. If
F(y)
is an integral of the
y
-system, then

\dot{x}\cdot\nabla F(y(x))=\sum_{i<l,i>m}\dot{x}_{i}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}+\sum_{l\leq i\leq m}\dot{x}_{i}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{i<l,i>l}\dot{y}_{i}\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{i}}+\dot{y}_{l}\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{l}}=\dot{y}\cdot\nabla F(y)=0,

as by the chain rule we have, for
l\leq i\leq m
,

\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{l}}\frac{\partial y_{l}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{l}}.

For commuting integrals
F(y)
and
G(y)
we have

\displaystyle\{F(y(x)),G(y(x))\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j<l,i,j>m}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial G(y(x))}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}+\sum_{i<l,i>m,l\leq j\leq m}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial G(y(x))}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\sum_{l\leq i\leq m,j<l,j>m,}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial G(y(x))}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}+\sum_{l\leq i,j\leq m}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial G(y(x))}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j<l,i,j>l}\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial G(y)}{\partial y_{j}}\{y_{i},y_{j}\}+\sum_{i<l,i>l}\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial G(y)}{\partial y_{l}}\{y_{i},y_{l}\}+\sum_{j<l,j>l,}\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{l}}\frac{\partial G(y)}{\partial y_{j}}\{y_{l},y_{j}\}

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{l}}\frac{\partial G(y)}{\partial y_{l}}\{y_{l},y_{l}\}=\{F(y),G(y)\}=0.

Finally, if
K(x_{l},x_{l+1},\ldots,x_{m})
is a homogeneous local integral of weight 0, then it is also an integral of the homogeneous (and Hamiltonian) LV-system
\dot{x}_{i}=\{x_{i},y_{l}\}
,
i=l,\ldots,m
. Hence

\displaystyle\{F(y(x)),K(x)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<l,i>m,l\leq j\leq m}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial K(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}+\sum_{l\leq i,j\leq m}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial K(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<l,i>m}(-1)^{[i>m]}x_{i}\frac{\partial F(y(x))}{\partial x_{i}}\sum_{l\leq j\leq m}\frac{\partial K(x)}{\partial x_{j}}x_{j}+\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y_{l}}\sum_{l\leq j\leq m}\frac{\partial K(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\{y_{l},x_{j}\}

\displaystyle=(\sum_{i<l,i>m}0)+0.

\dot{D}&=D(d-B-C),\end{split}

(34)

where
B=P_{1,k}
,
C=P_{k+1,k+l}
,
D=P_{k+l+1,n}
. We express the solutions for
B(t),C(t),D(t)
in terms of the Lambert W function (
W
) in the following propositions, whose proofs are given in
Appendix C. We note that the use of the Lambert W function to solve Lotka-Volterra and other biological models is not new, cf.
[
7
,
14
,
21
]
and references therein.

Proposition 9
.

Let

F(p)=\int^{p}\frac{1}{bq\left(W(R(q))+1\right)}dq,\quad\text{with }R(q)=\frac{q^{d/b}}{b{\rm e}^{(q+b+c_{1})/b}}.

The solution to Lotka-Volterra system (
33
) is, in terms of the inverse function
F^{-1}
, given by
B(t)=F^{-1}(t+c_{2})
and
D(t)=W(R(B(t)))
.

Proposition 10
.

Let

G(p)=\int^{p}\frac{1}{bq\left(W(T(q))+1\right)}dq,\quad\text{with }T(q)=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{q+b}{b}}\left(q^{\frac{d}{b}}c_{1}-q^{\frac{b+d}{b}}c_{2}\right)}{b^{2}}.

The solution to Lotka-Volterra system (
34
) is, in terms of the inverse function
G^{-1}
, given by

B(t)=G^{-1}(t+c_{3}),\quad C=\frac{c_{2}\left(bB-B^{\prime}\right)}{\left(c_{1}-c_{2}B\right)},\quad D=-\frac{c_{1}\left(bB-B^{\prime}\right)}{B\left(c_{1}-c_{2}B\right)}.

We now introduce a linear change of variables which provides a separation of variables for equation (
8
), i.e. in the transformed system each equation will depend on only one variable (as well as on the solution of (
33
) or (
34
)). Define

y_{i}=\begin{cases}P_{1,i},&i=1,\ldots,k,\\
P_{k+1,i},&i=k+1,\ldots,k+l,\\
P_{k+l+1,i},&i=l+1,\ldots,k+l+m=n.\end{cases}

Each of these variables is a Darboux polynomial (and hence the transformed system is of Lotka-Volterra form). Their cofactors are obtained from (
26
) and
[
23
, Thm 3.1]
, cf. the footnote on page 6. We have
\dot{y}_{i}=y_{i}C_{i}
, with

C_{i}=\begin{cases}b+P_{i+1,n},&i=1,\ldots,k,\\
c-P_{1,k}+P_{i+1,n},&i=k+1,\ldots,k+l,\\
d-P_{1,k+l}+P_{i+1,n},&i=l+1,\ldots,k+l+m=n,\end{cases}

which can be rewritten as

C_{i}=\begin{cases}b+B+C+D-y_{i},&i=1,\ldots,k,\\
c-B+C+D-y_{i},&i=k+1,\ldots,k+l,\\
d-B-C+D-y_{i},&i=l+1,\ldots,k+l+m=n,\end{cases}

where
B,C,D
are known functions of
t
, given in Proposition
9
or Proposition
10
. Thus, each equation has the form
\dot{y}=y(f(t)-y)
,
which can be solved explicitly, for any function
f
, by using an integrating factor. With
\dot{F}(t)=f(t)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{y}}{y^{2}}-\frac{f(t)}{y}=-1

\displaystyle\implies e^{F(t)}\frac{\dot{y}}{y^{2}}-e^{F(t)}\frac{f(t)}{y}=-e^{F(t)}

\displaystyle\implies\frac{d}{dt}\left(-\frac{e^{F(t)}}{y}\right)=-e^{F(t)}

\displaystyle\implies-\frac{e^{F(t)}}{y(t)}=-\frac{e^{F(0)}}{y(0)}-\int e^{F(t)}dt,

from which
y(t)
can be found.

9
Summary and concluding remarks

We have introduced Darboux functions
P_{i,j}
and
Q_{i,j}
and provided, in Lemma
1
, the quadratic Poisson algebra they satisfy, with respect to the bracket

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=a_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}
,
where

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&i<j\\
0&i=j\\
-1&i>j\end{cases}

(35)

which is the interaction matrix of Lotka-Volterra system (
8
) under study. The Poisson algebra was subsequently used to prove the involutivity of local integrals
I_{i,j}=P_{i,j}Q_{i,j}
and
I_{k,l}
, when
i\equiv j\equiv k\equiv l\mod 2
and
[k,l]\subset[i,j]
. The Poisson algebra may become interesting in its own right. One could study the relations needed for the algebra to satisfy the Jacobi identity.

Our Lotka-Volterra system (
8
) naturally splits up into 11 families, depending on whether the parameters
k,l,m
are even or odd, and whether
b,c=b+d,d
vanish or not. In section
3
, for the cases E1-E5 and O1-O3, we provided their Hamiltonian functions, in terms of the scalings functions we introduced, cf. Lemma
2
. The cases N1, M1 and M2 do not seem to be Hamiltonian.

We have provided, for each class of systems E1-E5, O1-O3 and N1, sets of sufficiently many integrals for the various notions of integrability. All Hamiltonian systems (the E and O cases) are Liouville integrable. Furthermore, the systems E1 with
n=2
, O1 with
n=3
and O2 with
k=1
are maximally superintegrable (
n-1
functionally independent integrals). The other E and O systems have either
n-2
or
n-3
functionally independent integrals. The non-Hamiltonian N1 systems admit
n-2
integrals, and, as they are also measure preserving, cf. Proposition
8
, this implies they are nonholonomically integrable. The main tool which we developed and employed to establish the existence of a sufficient number of (commuting) integrals in each case, is the contraction theorem, Theorem
6
. It states that (commuting) integrals of a system with smaller blocks can be lifted to (commuting) integrals, which then also commute with local integrals present.

The classes for which we were not able to establish integrability (in the sense of sufficiently many integrals) are M1 and M2. For these systems we (only) found
n-3
functionally independent integrals. As an example, the 5-component system M1 with
(k,l,m)=(1,2,2)
reads

\begin{split}\dot{x}_{1}&=x_{1}\left(b+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}\right),\\
\dot{x}_{2}&=x_{2}\left(b+d-x_{1}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}\right),\\
\dot{x}_{3}&=x_{3}\left(b+d-x_{1}-x_{2}+x_{4}+x_{5}\right),\\
\dot{x}_{4}&=x_{4}\left(d-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}+x_{5}\right),\\
\dot{x}_{5}&=x_{5}\left(d-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}-x_{4}\right).\end{split}

(36)

The system (
36
) admits two functionally independent integrals

\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}}\left(x_{2}+x_{3}\right)^{b}}{x_{1}^{b+d}},\quad\frac{x_{1}\left(x_{4}+x_{5}\right)}{x_{2}+x_{3}}.

We cannot exclude the possibility that
a third integral for (
36
) might perhaps be constructed by a method different than the one considered in the present paper.

In Propositions
9
,
10
we have provided explicit solutions, in terms of the Lambert W function, for the 2- and 3-component Lotka-Volterra systems (
33
), (
34
). We subsequently showed how to integrate each
n
-component Lotka-Volterra equation in the class considered, by separation of variables and using an integrating factor.

We believe, but have not proven, that systems of the form (
8
), but with different
r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(e.g. containing more than
3
blocks) are not integrable.

We have studied Lotka-Volterra systems with a very specific interaction matrix, given by (
35
). We have formulated the contraction theorem in this setting. However, as shown in Appendix B, contraction can be applied in more general LV-systems. The full scope of contraction remains to be explored.
