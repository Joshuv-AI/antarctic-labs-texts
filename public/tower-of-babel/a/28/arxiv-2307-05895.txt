1.
Introduction

Let
F
be a real quadratic number field with the ring of integers
{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
.
For a ring
R
, let
K_{2}R
be the Milnor group of
R
. The kernel of the surjective homomorphism

K_{2}F\to\bigoplus_{\mathfrak{p}}({\mathcal{O}}_{F}/\mathfrak{p})^{\times},

given by the tame symbols at all finite primes
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
, is called the tame kernel of
F
and is known to be isomorphic to
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
.
A theorem of Garland
[
4
]
shows that
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
is a finite abelian group. For any prime
\ell
, we write
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(\ell)
the
\ell
-primary subgroup of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
. In this article, we shall determine an explicit structure for
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)
for several families of real quadratic number fields
F
where the discriminant of
F
can have arbitrarily finitely many prime factors.

For any odd prime
\ell
, we denote by
\left(\frac{\cdot}{\ell}\right)
the Legendre symbol.
The main result in this article is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
be a real quadratic number field with discriminant
D=4\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
where
\ell_{i}
are distinct primes such that
\ell_{1}\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
and
\ell_{i}\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
for
2\leq i\leq n
. Assume that
\left(\frac{\ell_{i}}{\ell_{j}}\right)=-1
for
2\leq i<j\leq n
,
\left(\frac{\ell_{1}}{\ell_{i}}\right)=1
for
i\geq 3
,
\left(\frac{\ell_{1}}{\ell_{2}}\right)=-1
and
n
is even. Then

K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n-1}\times({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{\delta}{\mathbb{Z}})

where
\delta
is an integer at least
3
.

We remark that for
n=2
, the above theorem is due to Qin
[
10
]
(see also
[
2
]
for a lower bound). So the theorem can be viewed as a generalization of Qin’s result to discriminant of
F
with arbitrarily finitely many prime factors. Some numerical data (included in section §
4
) suggest that
\delta
is always
3
. However, we currently see no way of proving it theoretically.

For a finite abelian group
\mathfrak{A}
and any integer
n\geq 0
, we write
r_{2^{n}}(\mathfrak{A})
for the number of cyclic components of
\mathfrak{A}
of order divisible by
2^{n}
. The number
r_{2^{n}}(\mathfrak{A})
is called the
2^{n}
-rank of
\mathfrak{A}
. Therefore Theorem
1.1

shows that
r_{8}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})
is always one. As far as we know, it is difficult to determine the
8
-rank for
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
. In fact, our proof for Theorem
1.1
can not recover the result of Qin
[
10
]
for
n=2
, in fact, the proof does depend on the result of Browkin and Schinzel
[
2
]
showing that
r_{8}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})\geq 1
when
n=2
. In addition to Theorem
1.1
for
r_{8}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=1
,
we also get the following theorem for
r_{8}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=0
, in which we determine the
4
-rank for
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
with discriminant
D>0
.

(1)

Assume that
D=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
where
\ell_{i}
are distinct primes such that
\ell_{i}\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
and
\left(\frac{\ell_{i}}{\ell_{j}}\right)=-1
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Assume further that
n
is odd. Then

K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}.

(2)

Assume that
D=4\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
satisfying the prime divisors
\ell_{1}\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
and
\ell_{i}\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8\,(2\leq i\leq n)
. Assume that
n
is odd and
\left(\frac{\ell_{i}}{\ell_{j}}\right)=-1
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Then

K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)
is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n-1}\times({\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}})
(resp.
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
) if
n\geq 3
(resp.
n=1
).

For
n=1
, the above theorem is due to Browkin and Schinzel
[
2
, Corollary 1]
(see also
[
10
]
). Fortunately our proof for Theorem
1.2
recovers the case of Browkin and Schinzel. We also remark that the Theorem
1.2
is essentially used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Now, we say something about the main ideas in the proof for Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Previously, the
2^{n}
-rank
r_{2^{n}}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})
was determined in an arithmetic way, i.e., using Hilbert symbols or solving certain Diophantine Equations in integers (see
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
18
]
). It may be possible that the method in
[
10
]
,
[
9
]
may show Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. However, we stress that our method showing Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

is analytic. The main tool is the Birch-Tate formula (see Theorem
2.1
), which relates the order of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
to
L(\chi_{F},-1)
(see Corollary
2.3
). Here,
\chi_{F}
is the Dirichlet character associated to
F/{\mathbb{Q}}
and
L(\chi_{F},s)
is the Dirichlet L-functions associated to
\chi_{F}
. The Birch-Tate formula also shows that
L(\chi_{F},-1)
is a non-zero rational number. Thus if one can get a good estimate on the
2
-adic valuation for
L(\chi_{F},-1)
, then one sees a rough picture of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)
. Although there are well-known special value formulas for
L(\chi_{F},-1)
, it is very difficult to get enough information on the
2
-adic valuation for
L(\chi_{F},-1)
when the number of prime divisors of
D
becomes large. To get around this difficulty, we use
an induction method for imprimitive Dirichlet L-functions. This method is inspired by Zhao who proved some cases for the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for elliptic curves with complex multiplication. By using this induction argument, we can get a more precise estimate on the
2
-adic valuation for
L(\chi_{F},-1)
when
D
has an arbitrarily number of prime factors. Then by combining a well-known method for determining the
2
-rank and
4
-rank for ideal class groups and
K
groups, we prove both Theorems. In the last step, we use a relation (due to Feng and Yue
[
17
]
) between the
4
-ranks of ideal class groups and
K
groups.

In the final part of the introduction, we briefly outline the contents of the paper. In next section §
2
, we will state the Birch-Tate formula and give a precise relation between
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
and
L(\chi_{F},-1)
. The main part of the paper is §
3
, where we prove a special value formula for imprimitive Dirichlet L-functions. Then by using the induction argument, we get the required estimate on the
2
-adic valuation for the Dirichlet L-values (see Theorem
3.8
). In the final section §
4
, we summarise the method to compute the
2
(resp.
4
)-rank of both the ideal class groups and
K
groups. We then prove both theorems and give some numerical data in the last section.

2.
Birch-Tate formula

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a number field and
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{K}}
the ring of integers in
{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
\zeta_{\mathcal{K}}(s)
be the Dedekind zeta function of
{\mathcal{K}}
. For any ring
R
, we let
K_{2}R
denote the Milnor group of
R
(equivalently, the second K group of
R
). By a theorem of Garland
[
4
]
,
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{K}}
is a finite abelian group.
In this section, for a real quadratic number field
F
,
we will state the Birch-Tate formula, which gives
a precise relation between the order of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}

and
\zeta_{F}(-1)
. Therefore, we can reduce the study of the structure of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
to the zeta values.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a totally real number field. The original Birch-Tate conjecture (see
[
13
]
for history and
[
7
]
for an update) predicts the following equality

\zeta_{\mathcal{F}}(-1)=\frac{\#(K_{2}({\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{F}}))}{\#(W_{2}(\mathcal{F}))},

where
W_{2}(\mathcal{F})=H^{0}(\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{Q}}/{\mathbb{Z}}(2))
. By Wiles’s theorem on Iwasawa main conjecture (
[
16
]
, see also
[
5
]
for abelian number fields) and a remark made by Kolster (see
[
14
]
), the above equality holds up to a power of
2
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{F}
is an abelian extension over
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
the equality holds. Therefore, for a real quadratic number field
F
, we have the following Birch-Tate formula.

Theorem 2.1
.

Keep the above notations, then we have

(2.1)

\zeta_{F}(-1)=\frac{\#(K_{2}({\mathcal{O}}_{F}))}{\#(W_{2}(F))}.

The term
w_{2}(F)=\#(W_{2}(F))
is easy to compute, we list the result in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Write
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
with
D
the discriminant of
F
. Then

(1)

If
D=5
,
w_{2}(F)=120
.

(2)

If
D=8
,
w_{2}(F)=48
.

(3)

If
D>8
,
w_{2}(F)=24
.

Proof.

This is a well-known results. We include a proof for readers’ convenience. From
[
6
]
, we have the formula

w_{2}(F)=4\cdot\prod_{[\mathfrak{L}:F]=2}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\#(\mu(\mathfrak{L}))

where
\mathfrak{L}/F
runs over all quadratic extensions of
F
, and
\mu(\mathfrak{L})
is the group of roots of unity in
\mathfrak{L}
. By noting that a field extension over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with degree
4
can only contain
4,5,6,8
-th roots of unity, the assertion in the lemma follows.
∎

When
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{2})
or
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{5})
, one can easily determine the structure of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
(at least for the
2
-primary part). From now on, we assume that
D>8
.

Corollary 2.3
.

For
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
satisfying
D>8
, we have

\#(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=24\cdot\zeta_{F}(-1).

In particular, if we denote by
\chi_{F}
the Dirichlet character associated to
F/{\mathbb{Q}}
, then

\#(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=-2\cdot L(\chi_{F},-1).

Here,
L(\chi_{F},s)
is the Dirichlet L-series associated to
\chi_{F}
.

Proof.

Denote by
\zeta(s)
the Riemann’s zeta function.
The corollary follows by the well-known formula

\zeta_{F}(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_{F})

and
\zeta(-1)=-\frac{1}{12}
.
∎

3.
A special value formula and induction method

We know from section §
2
that one can reduce the study of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
to
L(\chi_{F},-1)
.
It is also well-known that
L(\chi_{F},-1)
is a nonzero rational number
and there is a special value formula for
L(\chi_{F},-1)
in terms of the generalized Bernoulli numbers.
However, for any prime
\ell
, to determine the precise
\ell
-adic valuation of
L(\chi_{F},-1)
is very difficult (even for
\ell=2
). To overcome this difficulty, we borrow some ideas of Zhao, who proved some cases for the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for elliptic curves with complex multiplication (see
[
19
]
). The ideas consist of two main ingredients: one is a special value formula for imprimitive L-values of Dirichlet characters at
s=-1
; the other is an induction method for these imprimitive L-functions. By using Zhao’s method, we can get a more precise estimate on the
2
-adic valuation of
L(\chi_{F},-1)
when
D
has arbitrarily finitely many prime factors. This key result will help us to determine the
2
-primary part of
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
in the next section.

Let us first recall some basic facts on Dirichlet L-functions and generalized Bernoulli numbers. Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character with conductor
f_{\chi}=f
. Let
g
be any positive integer such that
f
divides
g
. We define
L^{(g)}(\chi,s)(s\in{\mathbb{C}})
to be the analytic continuation of the following power series

\sum^{\infty}_{n=1,\,(n,g)=1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}},\qquad\textrm{where }{\mathrm{Re}}(s)>1.

This is an imprimitive Dirichlet L-series when
f\neq g
. When
f=g
,
L^{(g)}(\chi,s)
is a primitive L-series and we simply denote it by
L(\chi,s)
.

We define the generalized Bernoulli number
B_{n,\chi,g}
via the following identity

(3.1)

\sum^{g}_{z=1}\frac{\chi(z)te^{zt}}{e^{gt}-1}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}B_{n,\chi,g}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

For each integer
n\geq 0
, we recall the definition of the Bernoulli polynomial
B_{n}(X)
in the following equality

(3.2)

\frac{te^{Xt}}{e^{t}-1}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}B_{n}(X)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

We remark that the dependence of
B_{n,\chi,g}
on
g
in (
3.1
) can be ignored by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any integer
g
such that
f\mid g
, the number
B_{n,\chi,g}
is independent of
g
. We denote this number by
B_{n,\chi}
. Then

B_{n,\chi}=g^{n-1}\sum^{g}_{a=1}\chi(a)\cdot B_{n}\left(\frac{a}{g}\right).

Furthermore, if
\chi
is non-trivial and
\chi(-1)=1
, we have

(3.3)

B_{2,\chi}=\frac{1}{g}\sum^{g}_{a=1}\chi(a)a^{2}.

Proof.

Set
y=\frac{g}{f}
and make a change of variables

z=b+cf,\qquad 1\leq b\leq f,\,0\leq c\leq y-1.

Noting that
\chi
has conductor
f
, we have

(3.4)

\sum^{g}_{z=1}\chi(z)\cdot\frac{te^{zt}}{e^{gt}-1}=\sum^{f}_{b=1}\sum^{y-1}_{c=0}\chi(b)\cdot\frac{te^{t(b+cf)}}{e^{gt}-1}.

Since the following identity

\sum^{y-1}_{c=0}e^{tcf}=\frac{e^{tg}-1}{e^{tf}-1},

the right hand side of (
3.4
) turns to be

\sum^{f}_{b=1}\sum^{y-1}_{c=0}\chi(b)\cdot\frac{te^{t(b+cf)}}{e^{gt}-1}=\sum^{f}_{b=1}\chi(b)\cdot\frac{te^{bt}}{e^{ft}-1}.

Therefore, we get

\sum^{g}_{z=1}\chi(z)\cdot\frac{te^{zt}}{e^{gt}-1}=\sum^{f}_{b=1}\chi(b)\cdot\frac{te^{bt}}{e^{ft}-1}

and the first assertion follows. Next, by formula (
3.2
), we get

\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}g^{n-1}\sum^{g}_{a=1}\chi(a)B_{n}\left(\frac{a}{g}\right)\cdot\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\sum^{g}_{a=1}\chi(a)\frac{te^{at}}{e^{gt}-1}.

Comparing to (
3.1
), the first equality in the lemma follows. Finally, note that
B_{2}(X)=X^{2}-X+\frac{1}{6}
, then formula (
3.3
) follows from the first equality. Here, we use the fact that
\chi
is non-trivial and
\chi(-1)=1
. Therefore, the lemma follows.
∎

For any integer
n\geq 1
,
the following special value formula for
L(\chi,s)
at
s=1-n
is well-known

(3.5)

L(\chi,1-n)=-\frac{B_{n,\chi}}{n}.

One can find a detailed proof in
[
15
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Next, we give a special value formula for imprimitive Dirichlet L-functions. For this purpose, we introduce some notation. For a positive integer
M\equiv 0
or
1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 4
, we put
\chi_{M}=\left(\frac{M}{\cdot}\right)
, where
\left(\frac{M}{\cdot}\right)
is the Jacobian symbol. Here, we make the convention that
\left(\frac{M}{-1}\right)=1
since
M>0
(see also
[
12
]
). Let
d_{i}(1\leq i\leq n)
be discriminant of some real quadratic fields such that the greatest common divisor of
d_{i},d_{j}(i\neq j)
is
1
or
4
.
For any
d=d_{i_{1}}\cdots d_{i_{s}}
where
\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s}\}
is a non-empty subset of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
, we let
d^{\prime}=D/d
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},s)
be the imprimitive Dirichlet L-function associated to
\chi_{d}
. Then

(3.6)

L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},-1)=-\frac{1}{2D}\sum^{D}_{a=1}\chi_{Dd^{\prime}}(a)a^{2}.

Proof.

By the Euler product of
L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},s)
and that
\chi_{d}(\ell)=\left(\frac{d}{\ell}\right)
for any prime
\ell\nmid d
, we get

L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},s)=\prod_{\ell\nmid d^{\prime}}\left(1-\left(\frac{d}{\ell}\right)\ell^{-s}\right)^{-1},\qquad\textrm{where}\quad{\mathrm{Re}}(s)>1.

Since for these
\ell\nmid d^{\prime}
, we always have

\chi_{Dd^{\prime}}(\ell)=\chi_{d}(\ell)
. Therefore, the above equality implies that

L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},s)=L(\chi_{Dd^{\prime}},s).

Here, we should note that the conductor of
\chi_{Dd^{\prime}}
is
D
. Now, our formula (
3.6
) follows from the above equality, formulas (
3.5
) and (
3.3
). Therefore, the Proposition follows.
∎

The next example are the very few cases where we can get a precise
2
-adic valuation for
L(\chi_{F},-1)
only using formula (
3.6
).
We denote by
v=v_{2}
the normalized additive valuation on
{\mathbb{Q}}_{2}
such that
v(2)=1
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
p\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
be a prime and
d=4p
. For
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})
, we have

v(L(\chi_{d},-1))=1.

Our motivation for this example comes from some arithmetic results by Browkin-Schinzel in
[
2
]
.
Of course, the discriminant
D_{F}=d
of
F
is greater than
8
, thus, from Birch-Tate formula, we know that
24\zeta_{F}(-1)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. By
\zeta(-1)=\frac{1}{12}
, formula (
3.6
) and

\zeta_{F}(-1)=\zeta(-1)L(\chi_{d},-1),

we see that
\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{a=1}\chi_{d}(a)a^{2}
is an integer. By
\chi_{d}
takes zero value on even integers and recall that
d=4p
, we get

(3.7)

\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{a=1}\chi(a)a^{2}=\frac{1}{4p}\sum^{2p}_{b=1}\chi_{d}(2b-1)\cdot(2b-1)^{2}.

Since
\chi_{d}
is nontrivial and
\chi_{d}(-1)=1
, one can easily check that

\sum^{2p}_{b=1}\chi_{d}(2b-1)=\sum^{2p}_{b=1}\chi_{d}(2b-1)b=0.

Thus, from formula (
3.7
), we get

(3.8)

\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{a=1}\chi(a)a^{2}=\frac{1}{p}\sum^{2p}_{b=1}\chi_{d}(2b-1)\cdot b^{2}.

Since
\chi_{d}(2b-1)=\left(\frac{p}{2b-1}\right)
, if we break up the second sum in (
3.8
) according as
b=2c
or
b=2c-1
(
1\leq c\leq p
), by modulo
8
and noting that

(2c-1)^{2}\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8\quad\textrm{and }\quad\sum^{p}_{c=1}\left(\frac{p}{4c-3}\right)=0,

then we get

\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{a=1}\chi(a)a^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{p}\sum^{p}_{c=1}\left(\frac{p}{4c-1}\right)4c^{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8.

Now, the example will follow from the formula (
3.6
) and the following claim

\sum^{p}_{c=1}\left(\frac{p}{4c-1}\right)c^{2}\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2.

To show this claim, since the Jacobian symbol takes values in
\pm 1
, thus, by modulo
2
, we have

\sum^{p}_{c=1}\left(\frac{p}{4c-1}\right)c^{2}\equiv\sum^{p}_{c=1}c^{2}-\left(\frac{p+1}{4}\right)^{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2.

Then one can easily check the right hand side in the congruence is indeed an odd integer, and our claim follows.

Recall that
D=d_{1}\cdots d_{n}
with each
d_{i}
is a discriminant of some real quadratic fields satisfying
(d_{i},d_{j})=1
or
4
for
i\neq j
. We remark that when
n
is large, it is hardly to get a precise
2
-adic valuation of
L(\chi_{D},-1)
, so now we introduce the induction method to get around this problem. Let us define

\mathcal{G}=\{a\in{\mathbb{Z}}:1\leq a\leq D,\,\chi_{d_{i}}(a)=1\,\textrm{ for any }1\leq i\leq n\}.

The heart of the induction method is to take sum of all the imprimitive L-values
L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},-1)
, where
d=d_{i_{1}}\cdots d_{i_{s}}
such that
\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s}\}
runs over all non-empty subset of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

We have

(3.9)

\sum_{d=d_{i_{1}}\cdots d_{i_{s}}}L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},-1)=-\frac{2^{n-1}}{D}\sum_{1\leq a\leq D,\,a\in{\mathcal{G}}}a^{2}+\frac{D}{6}\varphi(D)+\frac{1}{12}\prod_{p\mid D}(1-p).

Here, the first sum is taken over all
d_{i_{1}}\cdots d_{i_{s}}
such that
\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{s}\}
runs over all non-empty subset of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
. The last product is taken over all prime divisors
p
of
D
. The function
\varphi(D)
is the Euler function.

Before proving the Proposition, we need some elementary facts on Mobius function
\mu
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume
D
as above and
D>1
, then we have

\sum_{m\mid D}\mu(m)=0,\qquad\sum_{m\mid D}\mu(m)\frac{D}{m}=\varphi(D),\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sum_{m\mid D}\mu(m)m=\prod_{p\mid D}(1-p).

Proof.

All are standard facts on Mobius functions, for a detailed proof, see
[
1
]
.
∎

Now, we come back to the proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Writing
d=d_{i_{1}}\cdots d_{i_{s}}
and adding up the formulas (
3.6
) for all such
d\neq 1
, we get

\sum_{d\neq 1}L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},-1)=-\frac{1}{2D}\sum^{D}_{a=1}\chi_{D}(a)a^{2}\sum_{d^{\prime}\neq D}\left(\frac{d^{\prime}}{a}\right).

Here, we recall that
d^{\prime}=\frac{D}{d}
and
\chi_{Dd^{\prime}}(a)=\chi_{D}(a)\left(\frac{d^{\prime}}{a}\right)
.
By the following identity

\sum_{d^{\prime}\mid D}\left(\frac{d^{\prime}}{a}\right)=\prod^{n}_{i=1}\left(1+\left(\frac{d_{i}}{a}\right)\right),

we see that

\sum_{d\neq 1}L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},-1)=-\frac{1}{2D}\sum^{D}_{a=1}\chi_{D}(a)a^{2}\left(\prod^{n}_{i=1}\left(1+\left(\frac{d_{i}}{a}\right)\right)-\left(\frac{D}{a}\right)\right).

For the term on the right hand of the above formula, by separating the bracket, we know the first term is non-zero if and only if
a\in{\mathcal{G}}
. Noting that
\chi_{D}(a)=\left(\frac{D}{a}\right)
, then we get

(3.10)

\sum_{d\neq 1}L^{(D)}(\chi_{d},-1)=-\frac{2^{n-1}}{D}\sum_{1\leq a\leq D,\,a\in{\mathcal{G}}}a^{2}+\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{1\leq a\leq D,\,(a,D)=1}a^{2}.

To handle the second sum on right hand side in (
3.10
), we invoke some relation on arithmetic function on
{\mathbb{Z}}
. For two such arithmetic functions
h_{1},h_{2}
, their Dirichlet product is given as follows

(3.11)

(h_{1}*h_{2})(n)=\sum_{m\mid n}h_{1}(m)h_{2}(n/m).

For the two arithmetic functions
\mathfrak{h}_{1}(n)=\sum_{1\leq a\leq n,\,(a,n)=1}\frac{a^{2}}{n^{2}}
and
\mathfrak{h}(n)=\sum_{1\leq a\leq n}\frac{a^{2}}{n^{2}}
, one can easily show that
\mathfrak{h}_{1}=\mu*\mathfrak{h}
(see also
[
1
, Exercise 2.14]
). Now, write the second sum in right hand side of (
3.10
) as

\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{1\leq a\leq D,\,(a,D)=1}a^{2}=\frac{D}{2}\sum_{1\leq a\leq D,\,(a,D)=1}\frac{a^{2}}{D^{2}}.

Applying formula (
3.11
) and Lemma
3.5
, one can directly check the following equality holds

\frac{D}{2}\sum_{1\leq a\leq D,\,(a,D)=1}\frac{a^{2}}{D^{2}}=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{m\mid D}\mu(D/m)\cdot(m+1)(2m+1)\cdot\frac{D}{m}.

By using Lemma
3.5
again, we see that

\frac{1}{12}\sum_{m\mid D}\mu(D/m)\cdot(m+1)(2m+1)\cdot\frac{D}{m}=\frac{D}{6}\varphi(D)+\frac{1}{12}\prod_{p\mid D}(1-p).

The Proposition now follows.
∎

i\neq j
. Set
r_{kk}=\sum_{i\neq k}r_{ik}
. We make the convention that
p_{1}=2
if
2\mid D_{K}
.
We then define the Redei matrix
R_{K}
associated to
K
to be

R_{F}=(r_{ij})
. Here, the matrix
R_{F}
is viewed as a
t\times t
-matrix over the finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
with
2
-elements. Denote by
r(R_{K})
the rank of the matrix
R_{F}
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. Recall that the narrow class group
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K}
of
K
is
the quotient of the group of fractional
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
-ideals by the subgroup of principal
ideals
(x)=x{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
with generator of positive norm
N(x)
. Here, we write
N=N_{K}
for the norm map from
K
to
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Keep the notations as above. Then

(1)

r_{2}(\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K})=t-1
.

(2)

If
K
is a real quadratic field and
r_{4}(\mathcal{C}_{K})=0
, then
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{K})=t-1
(resp.
t-2
) if
-1\in N(K^{\times})
(resp.
-1\notin N(K^{\times})
).

(3)

r_{4}(\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K})=t-1-r(R_{K})
. In particular, if the
4
-rank of
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K}
is zero, then so is the ideal class group
\mathcal{C}_{K}
.

Proof.

All the assertions are well-known. One can find a proof in
[
11
, Theorems (2.1) and (3.1)]
. The in particular part in (3) can be shown in the following way. Write
\mathfrak{P}
(resp.
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
) for the group generated by principal ideals (resp. principal ideals with positive norm generate). Then we have the following exact sequence

0\to\mathfrak{P}/\mathfrak{P^{+}}\to\mathcal{C}^{+}_{K}\to\mathcal{C}_{K}\to 0.

Since
\mathfrak{P}/\mathfrak{P^{+}}
is annihilated by
2
, we see that the in particular part in (3) follows.
∎

We now write
F
for a real quadratic number field with discriminant
D
and put
E={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-D})
. Let
S
be the set of primes in
F
lying above
2
. We denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{F,S}
the ring of
S
-integers in
F
and put
\mathcal{C}_{F,S}
the ideal class group of
{\mathcal{O}}_{F,S}
.
We omit the proof for the below Proposition and one can find a proof in
[
2
]
and
[
17
]
(see also
[
8
]
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Keep the notions as above. Denote by
s=\#(S)
, then we have

(1)

r_{2}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=1+r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{F,S})+s
.

(2)

\left|r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})-r_{4}(\mathcal{C}_{E})\right|\leq 1
.

Now for a finite abelian group
\mathfrak{A}
, we recall that
\mathfrak{A}(2)
is the
2
-primary subgroup of
\mathfrak{A}
.
The following two propositions prove the Theorem
1.2
in the introduction.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
D=p_{1}\cdots p_{n}
satisfying
p_{i}
are primes such that
p_{i}\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
and
\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{j}}\right)=-1
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. If
n
is odd, then we have

K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}.

Proof.

Since
n
is odd, so
D\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
. Then
2
is inert in
F
. Therefore,
s=1
and
\mathcal{C}_{F,S}=\mathcal{C}_{F}
. We claim that
-1\in N_{F}(F^{\times})
, in fact, by modulo every
p\mid D
and noting that
p\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 4
, there exists integers
x,y,z
not all zero satisfying

x^{2}-y^{2}D\equiv-z^{2}\ \mathrm{mod}\ p.

Note also that
x^{2}-y^{2}D=-z^{2}
has solutions in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus by Legendre’s theorem (see
[
9
, Lemma (2.1)]
),
x^{2}-y^{2}D=-z^{2}
has nontrivial solutions in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Therefore, the claim follows.
By Proposition
4.1
, we get
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{F})=n-1
. Note Proposition
4.2
, then
r_{2}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=n+1
. Now the Proposition follows from (2) in Proposition
3.6
and Corollary
2.3
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
F={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
with discriminant
D=4\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
satisfying the prime divisors
\ell_{1}\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
and
\ell_{i}\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8\,(2\leq i\leq n)
. Assume that
n
is odd and
\left(\frac{\ell_{i}}{\ell_{j}}\right)=-1
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Then
Then

K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2)
is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n-1}\times({\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}})
(resp.
({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
) if
n\geq 3
(resp.
n=1
).

Proof.

The case for
n=1
is easy, we left to the reader. We now assume that
n\geq 3
.
Since
\ell_{1}\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 4
, by the same argument as in Proposition
4.3
, we have
-1\notin N(F^{\times})
. Thus from Proposition
4.1

we see that
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{F})=n-1
. Now, writing
D=p_{1}^{*}\cdots p_{t}^{*}
and recalling the convention that
p^{*}_{1}=-4
, we know the Redei matrix
R_{F}
is given as the following block matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{2}&\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}&B_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\right),

where
A_{2}
(resp.
\beta_{1}
) is a
2\times 2
(resp.
(n-1)\times 2
) matrix with all entries equal to
1
,
B_{n-1}
is a
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix with all non-diagonal entries equal to
1
and all diagonal entries equal to
0
, and
\alpha_{1}
is a
2\times(n-1)
matrix with all elements in the first (resp. second) row equal to
0
(resp.
1
). By applying an elementary row operation to
R_{F}
, we see that
r(R_{F})=n
. Therefore, from Proposition
4.1
, we get
r_{4}(\mathcal{C}_{F})=0
. Note that
2
is ramified in
F
, so
s=1
. One can easily show that the unique prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
F
lying above
2
is not principal. So the ideal class of
\mathfrak{p}
generates a subgroup in
\mathcal{C}_{F}
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
. Therefore, we have

r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{F,S})=n-2
. From Proposition
4.2
, we get
r_{2}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=n
. To get the
4
-rank for
K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}
, we must study the
4
-rank for
E={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-D})
. By Proposition
4.1
,
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{E})=n-1
and the Redei matrix
R_{E}
is given by
B_{n}
. since
n
is odd, we see that
r(B_{n})=n-1
. Therefore,
r_{4}(\mathcal{C}_{E})=0
and from Proposition
4.2
, we only have
r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})\leq 1
. On the other hand, by (1) in Theorem
3.8
and Corollary
2.3
, we have

v(\#(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F}))=n+1.

Then by combining
r_{2}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=n
and
r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})\leq 1
, we must have
r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=1
,
r_{8}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=0
and
the proposition follows.
∎

Now, we can prove the Theorem
1.1
. It is the application of (2c) in Proposition
3.8
mentioned in section §
3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The heart part of the proof is (2c) in Theorem
3.8
, which we have already proven. So to complete the proof, we only need to
show that
r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})\leq 1
and
r_{2}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=n
. In fact, from (2c) in Theorem
3.8
and Corollary
2.3
, then the isomorphism in the Theorem follows.

Since
\ell_{1}\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
we know that
-1\notin N(F^{\times})
. Thus from Proposition
4.1
, we have
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{F})=n-1
.
The Redei matrix
R_{F}
is given as

R_{F}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C&\gamma_{1}\\
\gamma_{2}&B_{n-2}\\
\end{array}\right)\qquad C\in M_{3}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}),

where
B_{n-2}
is the same matrix given in Proposition
4.4
, and
C,\gamma_{i}(i=1,2)
can be explicitly determined. Here, we should note that the sum of all elements in the each column is zero.
We can show that
B_{n-2}
is of full rank, and the rank of
R_{F}
is
n
. Therefore, from Proposition
4.1
, we have
r_{4}(\mathcal{C}_{F})=0
. Note that the ramified prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
lying above
2
is not principal. Therefore,
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{F,S})=n-2
and
s=1
. So we have
r_{2}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})=n
by Proposition
4.2
. To show that
r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{F})\leq 1
, we consider the field
E={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-D})
, an easy calculation using Proposition
4.1
shows that
r_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{E})=n-1
,
r(R_{E})=n-1
and
r_{4}(\mathcal{C}_{E})=0
. Thus, by Proposition
4.2
we must have
r_{4}(K_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{E})\leq 1
. The theorem now follows.
∎
