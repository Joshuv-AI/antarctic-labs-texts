Abstract
We study estimation problems in safety-critical applications with streaming data. Since estimation problems can be posed as optimization problems in the probability space, we devise a stochastic projected Wasserstein gradient flow that keeps track of the belief of the estimated quantity and can consume samples from online data. We show the convergence properties of our algorithm. Our analysis combines recent advances in the Wasserstein space and its differential structure with more classical stochastic gradient descent. We apply our methodology for predictive maintenance of safety-critical processes: Our approach is shown to lead to superior performance when compared to classical least squares, enabling, among others, improved robustness for decision-making.



keywords

Wasserstein gradient flows, streaming data, predictive maintenance

1
Introduction

Providing performance guarantees for parameter estimation algorithms operating with streaming data is a key challenge when developing methods for safety-critical applications across various domains of engineering and data science. Ideally, one should be able to (i) efficiently handle streaming data in real time, without resorting to computationally expensive one-shot numerical routines, and (ii) rigorously quantify the uncertainty related to the estimated quantity. In this paper, we focus on probabilistic approaches to uncertainty quantification rather than set-based ones
(
Combettes 1993
)
.

A prominent approach for parameter estimation with streaming data is
Recursive Least Squares (RLS)
. In
RLS
, the online solution is obtained by “updating” the previous solution with the latest measurement.
RLS
avoids the need to store and invert large data matrices and provides probabilistic guarantees on its estimate when the process is linear and all distributions are Gaussian.
RLS
is a special case of Bayes filter (see e.g.,
Särkkä 2013
;
Sullivan 2015
), whose many variants (e.g., particle filters, extended Kalman filters, etc.) are the dominant approaches for inference using non-Gaussian distributions. Bayes filter is powerful but inflexible, it can be challenging to integrate side information and it can be difficult to implement due to the need to compute high-dimensional integrals.

In this work, we propose a different approach based on the theory of optimization in Wasserstein probability spaces
(
Jordan et al. 1998
;
Ambrosio et al. 2005
;
Lanzetti et al. 2022
)
. We pose the parameter estimation problem as an optimization problem in the probability space and devise a stochastic projected gradient flow to iteratively compute its optimal solution using samples obtained from streaming data. Our approach maintains and iteratively improves an estimate of the probability measure of the estimated quantities and does not require a-priori assumptions on the probability measures (e.g., Gaussianity), but rather works in the space of all probability measures with finite second moment. Our proposed framework is more flexible than the Bayes filter in the sense that its intuitive to add side information e.g., constraints on the support of the final distribution or on the variance (indeed, it can be used to recover maximum likelihood estimator for stochastic least squares problems
(
Rigollet and Weed 2018
)
).

A motivating application of interest is
Predictive Maintenance (PdM)
, where the goal is to efficiently maintain a safety-critical process (e.g., with minimal interruption) before an unsafe event occurs (see
Pech et al. 2021
for a recent survey of results).
The
PdM
problem is challenging from an online algorithmic perspective since, in practice, there is often only historical data on the nominal operation and little or no data on the unsafe operation.
Moreover, the problem calls for careful risk analysis: Too conservative decisions impact performance and efficiency, while unsafe events, if they occur, might lead to catastrophic failures.
The current state-of-the-art consists of rule-based methods and estimation strategies that rely on predetermined distribution models
(
Hu and Chen 2020
;
Kanso et al. 2022
)
.
With our work, we learn the model of the underlying process without a-priori assumptions on its probability measure to improve the overall performance by reducing conservativeness.

Our contributions are twofold. First, we propose a novel stochastic projected gradient flow for optimization in the probability space that operates on streaming data and study its convergence properties. Our analysis combines tools from optimal transport and differential calculus in the probability space with more classical projected stochastic gradient descent. We prove that similar to the Euclidean setting, our scheme yields convergence to a ball around the optimal solution.
Second, we apply our scheme to the predictive maintenance of the damping ratio of a second-order system and demonstrate improved performance relative to a classical least-squares approach.

2.0.2
Basics in Measure Theory

We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of (Borel) probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and by
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty\}
the space of probability measures with finite second moment.
We denote the Dirac measures at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\delta_{x}
, defined by
\delta_{x}(A)=1
if and only if
x\in A
.
We denote the support of a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The pushforward of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
via a (Borel) map
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
T_{\#}\mu
and defined by
(T_{\#}\mu)(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))
for all
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
Borel. For any
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
T_{\#}\mu
-integrable it holds

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\mathrm{d}(T_{\#}\mu)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(T(x))\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

A sequence of probability measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
converges narrowly to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
for all bounded continuous
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

2.0.3
Wasserstein distance

The (type 2) Wasserstein distance between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\left(\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x-y}^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is the set of transport plans, that is, of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose first marginal is
\mu
and second marginal is
\nu
; i.e.,
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}):(\proj_{1})_{\#}\gamma=\mu,(\proj_{2})_{\#}\gamma=\nu\}
where
\proj_{1}
and
\proj_{2}
are projection operators (e.g.,
\proj_{1}(x,y)=x
). We denote by
\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
the (non-empty) set of optimal couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
. It is well-known that the Wasserstein distance is a distance on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

2.0.4
Geodesic convexity

Given
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the geodesic between them by
\mu_{t}=((1-t)\proj_{1}+t\proj_{2})_{\#}\gamma
, where
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is an optimal transport plan between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. Since optimal transport plans are generally not unique, there might exist multiple geodesics between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
.
Accordingly, a functional
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is
\alpha
-geodesically convex if for all
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists a geodesic
\mu_{t}
so that
J(\mu_{t})\leq(1-t)J(\mu_{0})+tJ(\mu_{1})-\frac{\alpha}{2}t(1-t)W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{2}
. For instance,
\mu\mapsto\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[V\right]
is
\alpha
-geodesically convex if and only if
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\alpha
-convex (i.e., convex with convexity parameter
\alpha
) and
\mu\mapsto\var^{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]
is geodesically convex (with
\alpha=0
), where
\var^{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]
denotes the variance of
x_{i}
.

2.0.5
Wasserstein gradient

A function
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
is a Wasserstein gradient of a real-valued functional over the probability space
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
if it approximates
J
“linearly”; i.e., for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)

J(\nu)-J(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x)}^{\top}(y-x)\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+o(W_{2}(\mu,\nu)),

where
o(W_{2}(\mu,\nu))
denotes high-order term.
Wasserstein gradients are well-defined for many functionals of practical interest. In particular, we have
\nabla_{\mu}\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[V\right]=\nabla V
for any smooth
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with at most quadratic growth (i.e., the Wasserstein gradient of an expected value is simply the gradient of the function in the expected value) and
\nabla\var^{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]=2(x_{i}-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right])
. For the Wasserstein gradients of more functionals, we refer to
Lanzetti et al. 2022
.

Stochastic Projected Gradient Descent in Probability Spaces

We construct our estimation method by encoding our objectives in an optimization problem and adapting a gradient descent algorithm to operate using samples from the system obtained with streaming data. Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle J(\mu)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\Theta,

we seek to minimize a real-valued lower semi-continuous

J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
over the probability space subject to a support constraint. The functional

can encode standard expected values of real-valued quantities, but also other costs such as the variance, Wasserstein distance from a reference probability measure, or Kullback-Leibler divergence.
We impose the following assumption on (
1

Assumption 3.1

The set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is closed and convex.

Since we only have access to streaming data, we cannot evaluate

exactly. Thus, we solve (
1

we have access to an unbiased noisy estimate of the Wasserstein gradient of

\displaystyle\mu(k+1)

\displaystyle=\proj_{\text{supp}\subset\Theta}\left[(\Id-\tau\xi_{k})_{\#}\mu(k)\right]

(2)

\displaystyle\mu(0)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\Id

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu(k)),

\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\proj_{\text{supp}\subset\Theta}\left[\cdot\right]

\Theta

We make the following assumption on our noisy gradients:

Assumption 3.2

(Finite second moment)

The estimate of the gradient has bounded variance. In particular, there exists
\sigma>0

\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\xi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}+C(J(\mu)-J(\mu^{\ast})).

\mu

\mu

3.2

3.1
Projections in the Wasserstein Space

Our proposed algorithm includes a projection onto the set of probability measures with support in
\Theta
, denoted by
\proj_{\text{supp}\subset\Theta}\left[\cdot\right]
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\proj_{\text{supp}\subset\Theta}[\mu]=\argmin\limits_{\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\bar{\mu})\subset\Theta.

Our next result states the projection of a probability measure onto the set of probability measures with support contained in
\Theta
is (i) well-defined and (ii) results from pushforward of
\mu
via the projection operator
\proj_{\Theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\Theta
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Intuitively, we can thus compute projections by “projecting every (infinitesimal) particle of
\mu
to
\Theta

Proposition 3.1

(Projections)

Let Assumption
3.1
hold.
Then,
\proj_{\Theta}\left[\cdot\right]
is well-defined and for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\proj_{\text{supp}\subset\Theta}\left[\mu\right]=\left(\proj_{\Theta}[\cdot]\right)_{\#}\mu.

(3)

Since every point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be embedded to a probability measure
\delta_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Assumption
3.1
is necessary for the existence of a unique projection. Indeed, if it fails to hold, then the projection operator is ill-defined even on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In Proposition
3.1
, we show that it is also sufficient.

5
Predictive Maintenance of the Damping Ratio

Consider the second-order system

\ddot{z}+a\dot{z}+b(z-r+\varepsilon)=0,

(16)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
are parameters,
\varepsilon
is measurement noise with bounded variance and
r\in\mathbb{R}
is a reference signal. Our goal is to monitor the damping ratio

\zeta\coloneqq\frac{a}{2\sqrt{b}}

(17)

and ensure that it does not violate the safe lower bound
\zeta_{\min}\in\mathbb{R}
. This leads to the following safe set for
(a,b)
:

Q_{\mathrm{safe}}=\left\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\frac{a}{2\sqrt{b}}\geq\zeta_{\min}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The parameters
a
and
b
vary slowly with time according to the equation

y(t)=\begin{bmatrix}a(t)\\
b(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}-\lambda_{1}t\\
b_{0}+\lambda_{2}t\end{bmatrix},

(18)

where

We are interested in deciding when to perform maintenance on the system (

), which resets
t=0
and the parameters

and

. Performing maintenance is expensive and it is desirable to do it as infrequently as possible while still ensuring that

and

remain in

. Ideally we would always maintain the system at time

t^{\star}=\sup\{t\geq 0:y(t)\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}\}.

(19)

However, in practice
\theta
is unknown and we cannot directly measure

, hence we must infer

from noisy data, use it to estimate

, and account for the uncertainty in our estimation in our decision-making process.

5.1
Estimation

The parameters
y
cannot be directly measured but must be estimated based on trajectories of the system (
16
). For a fixed value of
t
(remember that
a(t)
and
b(t)
vary slowly relative to the dynamics of (
16
)) applying Euler discretization with a sampling period
\Delta t>0
to (
16
) yields the discrete-time system

x_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta t\\
-\Delta tb&1-\Delta ta\end{bmatrix}x_{k}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\Delta tb\end{bmatrix}(r+\varepsilon_{k}),

(20)

where the state is
x=(z,\dot{z})
, which we can rewrite as

x_{k+1}=A(y)x_{k}+B(y)(r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}).

For sufficiently small
\Delta t
and
a,b>0
, (
16
) is robustly stable about
z=r
. We then measure trajectories
\{\hat{x}_{k},r_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
of (
20
) and estimate
y(t)
using the least-squares estimator

\hat{y}(t)=\argmin_{y}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|\hat{x}_{k+1}-A(y)\hat{x}_{k}-B(y)r_{k}\|^{2}.

(21)

The noise term
\varepsilon
in (
20
) introduces noise in the estimator (
21
). Thus, in practice, we obtain noisy measurements

\hat{y}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}a(t)\\
b(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}-\lambda_{1}t\\
b_{0}+\lambda_{2}t\end{bmatrix}+w(t)

where the noise term
w(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is uncorrelated in time and is assumed to have bounded variance. In this case, the noise
w
results from the propagation of
\varepsilon
through the argmin in (
21
) and so might not be not zero-mean.
In our case study, we generate trajectories of 100s with sampling time
\Delta t=0.001
s and suppose
\varepsilon_{k}
is uniform on
[-3,+3]
.

5.2
Probabilistic Predictive Maintenance

To ensure robustness and careful decision-making, we adopt a probabilistic approach and encapsulate our belief about
\theta=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
in a probability distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
that will enable us to quantify our uncertainty about
\theta
. This opens the floor to stochastic and (distributionally) robust decision-making; e.g., with
\nu_{t}
denoting the probability distribution of
y(t)
, we can use a chance constraint

t^{\star}=\sup\left\{t\geq 0:{P}^{\nu_{t}}[Q_{\mathrm{safe}}]={P}^{x\sim\nu_{t}}[x\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}]\geq 1-\alpha\right\}

for some confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
or the mean prediction

t^{\star}=\sup\left\{t\geq 0:\mathbb{E}^{\nu_{t}}\left[x\right]=\mathbb{E}^{x\sim\nu_{t}}\left[x\right]\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}\right\}.

Each day
k
, we obtain degradation data
\{\hat{y}_{k},t_{k}\}
from the system. To put our PDM problem (
18
) in the form of (
10
) we consider the difference between two consecutive measurements, happening every
t_{k+1}-t_{k}=T>0
time units, and obtain a new measurement function

\tilde{y}=y(t+T)-y(t)=\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda_{1}T\\
\lambda_{2}T\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{w}_{1}\\
\tilde{w}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

for which we have data
\{\hat{y}_{k+1}-\hat{y}_{k}\}
. Thus we have
W=\text{diag}(-T,T)
. The noise
\tilde{w}
is zero-mean, since
\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{w}_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[w_{i}(t+T)-w_{i}(t)\right]=0
, and has variance

\var\left[\tilde{w}_{i}\right]=\var\left[w_{i}(t+T)\right]+\var\left[w_{i}(t)\right]\eqqcolon\sigma_{w}^{2},

and the parameters are known to lie in the set
\Theta=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
which defines the support or
\mu
.

To obtain a practical implementation, we implement (
14
) using particles, i.e., in the setting of probability measures that have finitely many samples of form
\mu(k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
with
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this work,
N=1000
. In this case, the update equation for probability measures (
14
) simplifies to

\mu(k+1)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\proj_{\Theta}[x_{i}-\tau\xi_{k}(x_{i},y_{k})]}.

(22)

where
\tau>0
is a step size and
\xi
is the unbiased estimator of the Wasserstein gradient from Lemma
4.1
. That is, one can simply pushforward all “particles” of
\mu(k)
and then project them individually to
\Theta
. It suffices therefore to keep track of the location
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of every particle
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. In particular, the update rule is then
x_{i}\mapsto\proj_{\Theta}[x_{i}-\tau\xi_{k}(x_{i},y_{k})]
, which can be evaluated in parallel for each particle
i
.
Accordingly, the computational complexity is the one of running parallel projected gradient descent iterations. As such, we are also subject to the usual trade-offs of gradient descent (e.g., optimality vs. number of iterations). In our case study, each gradient step takes on average 0.0025s (with standard deviation 0.0033s, Matlab implementation on a MacBook Pro with 2.3 GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i5).

6
Conclusions

In this work, we present a novel stochastic Wasserstein gradient flow method to efficiently perform estimation in probability spaces with streaming data.
Our formal results provide a convergence analysis of our online stochastic optimization method, which provides convergence to a ball around the optimal solution, similar to the standard Euclidean setting.
We illustrate the utility of the proposed method in an application of predictive maintenance to show the benefit over classical approaches such as simple least-squares with Gaussianity assumptions.
Overall, our method provides a flexible online estimation tool to estimate a rich set of processes without any assumptions on the model of the underlying distribution.
Future work will consider providing further results under relaxed settings such as non-strong convexity and applications for predictive maintenance of real-world physical examples.

A.1
Proof of Proposition
3.1

{pf}

We split the proof in three parts.

Existence:
Since the Wasserstein is lower semi-continuous (w.r.t. narrow convergence), and has compact (w.r.t. narrow convergence) level sets, it suffices to prove that the set
\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}):\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset\Theta\}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is closed (w.r.t. narrow convergence). Let
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
so that
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{n})\subset\Theta
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges narrowly to
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We seek to prove that
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset\Theta
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{m}(x)
be continuous and bounded so that (i)
f_{m}(x)=1
if
x\in\Theta
and (ii) converges pointwise to
1_{\Theta}(x)
as
m\to\infty
. As
\Theta
is closed, such
f_{m}
can always be constructed.
Since
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{n})\subset\Theta
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}(x)=\int_{\Theta}f_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}(x)=\nu_{n}(\Theta)=1.

Moreover, as
f_{m}
is continuous and bounded, for any fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
the definition of narrow convergence gives

1=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(x).

We can now deploy dominated convergence (
|f_{m}|
is uniformly dominated by an
\nu
-integrable function) to conclude

\displaystyle 1=\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lim_{m\to\infty}f_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\mathrm{\Theta}}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\nu(\Theta).

Thus,
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset\Theta
, and the set
\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset\Theta\}
is closed (w.r.t. narrow convergence), as desired.

Uniqueness:
Assume two non-equal projections
\bar{\mu}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
exist. Let
\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{1})
and
\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{2})
, and let
\mu_{1/2}
be the generalized geodesics with
\mu_{1/2}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\proj_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\proj_{3}\right)_{\#}\gamma
,
where
\gamma\in\Gamma(\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{2})\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{d}})
results from gluing

and
\gamma_{2}

Ambrosio et al. 2005

Ambrosio et al. 2005
for an introduction to generalized geodesics.
Since
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are both concentrated on
\Theta\times\Theta
,
\gamma
must also have support in
\Theta\times\Theta\times\Theta
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{1/2})

\displaystyle\subset\left(\frac{1}{2}\proj_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\proj_{3}\right)(\Theta\times\Theta\times\Theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Theta\oplus\frac{1}{2}\Theta=\Theta,

where the last equality follows from the convexity of
\Theta
and the definition of Minkovsky sum of sets. This shows that
\mu_{1/2}
is feasible.
The squared Wasserstein distance from
\mu
is known to be 2-convex along this geodesic. Thus,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu_{1/2},\bar{\mu})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}W_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu})^{2}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}W_{2}(\mu_{1},\bar{\mu})\!-\!\frac{1}{4}W_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=W_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu})^{2}-\frac{1}{4}W_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1})^{2}.

Since
\bar{\mu}_{0}\neq\bar{\mu}_{1}
,
W_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1})>0
.
However, this implies
W_{2}(\mu_{1/2},\bar{\mu})<W_{2}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu})
, which contradicts optimality of
\bar{\mu}_{0}
and
\bar{\mu}_{1}
.

Equation (
3
):
We will use Kantorovich duality. Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset\Theta
and let

be 0 in
\Omega
and
+\infty

1_{\Theta}
is zero

-a.e., and so
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\Theta}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=0

\nu\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus, Kantorovich duality (e.g., Chapter 5 in
Villani 2009
), with

\displaystyle W_{2}(\nu,\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\Theta}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{\Theta})^{c}(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

(23)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{\Theta}(x)-\norm{x-y}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf_{x\in\Theta}\norm{x-y}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Moreover,
\proj_{\Theta}[\cdot]
is trivially a (possibly suboptimal) transport map from
\mu

(\proj_{\Theta}[\cdot])_{\#}\mu
. Thus,

\displaystyle W_{2}((\proj_{\Theta}[\cdot])_{\#}\mu,\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x-\proj_{\Theta}[x]}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf_{x\in\Theta}\norm{x-y}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

where the last equality follows from the definition of projection. We can now combine (

Since
\mathrm{supp}({(\proj_{\Theta}[\cdot])_{\#}\mu})\subset\Theta
and projections are unique, we establish (
3
