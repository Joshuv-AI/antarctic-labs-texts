More on minimal renormalon subtraction
*

Abstract
The minimal renormalon subtraction (MRS) [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] technique is summarized. A new result is a study of the scale dependence of the pole-mass– MS ¯ \overline{\rm MS} -mass ratio in MRS perturbation theory. As expected, the scale dependence is much milder than in standard perturbation theory, but it is a bit larger than other truncation effects such as omitting the N 3 LO term or varying the normalization of the renormalon subtraction.



Andreas S. Kronfeld

1
Introduction

Many quantities calculated with lattice gauge theory have to be “matched” to continuum QCD.
For quantities that run, such as
\alpha_{\text{s}}
and quark masses, it is conventional to quote results in the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme, making
perturbation theory unavoidable.
The uncertainty introduced by truncating the perturbative series is notoriously difficult to estimate.

It is therefore noteworthy that the error budget for the
\overline{\rm MS}
quark masses of ref.
[
1
]
quotes a
negligible uncertainty from perturbative truncation.
Figure 4 of ref.
[
1
]
demonstrates a small shift in the final results when the order of perturbation
theory changes from NLO to NNLO and an imperceptible shift from NNLO to N
3
LO (counting the one-loop term as LO).
The total uncertainty is dominated by statistics, fitting systematics, and the external parametric error of
\alpha_{\text{s}}
.
It ranges from subpercent for bottom, charm, and strange to 1–2% for down and up.

To tame the perturbative series, which in this case is the relation between the pole mass and the
\overline{\rm MS}
mass, the key ingredient
was a rearrangement and reinterpretation of the series dubbed “minimal renormalon subtraction” (MRS)
[
2
]
,
which in turn relied on an expression for the normalization of factorial growth of the series coefficients
[
3
]
.
The MRS method has been generalized and clarified in work underway during Lattice 2023 and meanwhile
published
[
4
]
.
This report summarizes the main points and shows plots examining the scale dependence of the MRS quark mass.
At the conference, I showed plots of the static energy (the others were not yet ready).
The two sets of plots look very similar.
References
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
did not study how MRS perturbation theory depends on

s=\mu/\bar{m}
, where
\mu
is the scale in
\alpha_{\overline{\rm MS}}(\mu)
, and
\bar{m}=m_{\overline{\rm MS}}(\bar{m})
.
Showing the quark-mass plots here fills a gap while avoiding rote repetition of ref.
[
4
]
.
In particular,
fig.

3
(right) suggests that the truncation uncertainty in the quark-mass results of
ref.
[
1
]
may have been underestimated, but arguably not so much that it would alter the quadrature sum of
all uncertainties.

2
Statement of the problem

References
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
start with a relation for the mass of a heavy-light
hadron,

M=m_{\text{pole}}+\bar{\Lambda}+\frac{\mu_{\pi}^{2}}{2m_{\text{pole}}^{2}}+\cdots,

(1)

where
M
is the hadron mass,
m_{\text{pole}}
is the quark “pole” mass,
\bar{\Lambda}
is the energy of gluons and light quarks,
and the last term is the heavy quark’s kinetic energy.
A spin-dependent term of order
1/m_{\text{pole}}^{2}
can also arise; for this discussion, it can be lumped into the last term.
In perturbation theory, the pole mass is obtained from the self-energy by finding the pole of the propagator and substituting
iteratively, because the self-energy itself depends on
m_{\text{pole}}

[
5
]
.
The relation can be written

m_{\text{pole}}=\bar{m}\left(1+\sum_{l=0}r_{l}(\mu/\bar{m})\alpha_{\text{s}}^{l+1}(\mu)\right),

(2)

and the coefficients grow rapidly:
r_{l}(1)=\{0.424,1.035,3.693,17.21\}
for
l=\{0,1,2,3\}
.
(The numbers correspond to the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme with
n_{f}=3
massless quarks and no massive quark sea.)

If
m_{\text{pole}}
solves the perturbative pole condition, so does
m_{\text{pole}}-\delta m
, where
\delta m
is anything of order

\Lambda\sim\mu\mathrm{e}^{-1/2\beta_{0}\alpha_{\text{s}}(\mu)}
(the QCD scale).
In the hadron mass,
\delta m
can be absorbed into
\bar{\Lambda}
, which isn’t defined without specifying exactly what

m_{\text{pole}}
means in
eq.

1
.
This ambiguity, often called the “renormalon” ambiguity
[
6
,
7
]
, is related to the growth of the
coefficients, which is factorial.
Indeed, the MRS procedure introduces some rearrangements and eventually snips off a
\delta m
(given below,
eq.

10
),
absorbing it into
\bar{\Lambda}
to define
\bar{\Lambda}_{\text{MRS}}
.
Then
M=m_{\text{MRS}}+\bar{\Lambda}_{\text{MRS}}+\cdots
.

There are many problems of the form
eq.

1
.
Generically,

Q^{p}\mathcal{R}(Q)=Q^{p}R(Q)+C_{p}\Lambda^{p}+\cdots,\qquad R(Q)=\sum_{l=0}r_{l}(\mu/Q)\alpha_{\text{s}}^{l+1}(\mu),

(3)

where
\mathcal{R}
is some dimensionless observable with one short-distance scale
Q
.
In the following, formulas are written for arbitrary
p

[
4
]
, although we are mostly interested in
p=1
.

3
Factorial growth

It has been known for a long time (see, e.g., ref.
[
8
]
) that the coefficients in the series of

eqs.

2
and
3
grow at
large
orders as

r_{l}\sim\left(\frac{2\beta_{0}}{p}\right)^{l}\frac{\Gamma(l+1+pb)}{\Gamma(1+pb)}R_{0}\equiv R_{l}

(4a)

where
b=\beta_{1}/2\beta_{0}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n_{f}=3}}{{=}}32/81\approx 0.4
.
Komijani
[
3
]
found an expression for the
l
-independent normalization
R_{0}

R_{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\infty}}(k+1)\frac{\Gamma(1+pb)}{\Gamma(k+2+pb)}\left(\frac{p}{2\beta_{0}}\right)^{k}f_{k},

(4b)

where the coefficients
f_{k}
are straightforwardly related to the
r_{l}
(cf.
eq.

6
, below).
Komijani observed that since
\bar{\Lambda}
in
eq.

1
(more generally,
C_{p}\Lambda^{p}
in
eq.

3
) does not depend on

\bar{m}
(or
Q
in
eq.

3

[
4
]
), a derivative with respect to
\bar{m}
(or
Q
) yields a series without
the leading factorial growth; the coefficients of that series are the
f_{k}
.
In
eq.

4b
, the upper limit
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\infty}
is an asymptotic consideration.
If
L
of the
r_{l}
are known, then only
L
of the
f_{k}
are known.
Thus, any practical application must truncate the sum in
eq.

4b
to have upper limit
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}L-1}
.

An unexpected—perhaps remarkable—finding
[
4
]
is a similar set of formulas that hold at every order, not just
large orders.
My version reads

r_{l}=f_{l}+\left(\frac{2\beta_{0}}{p}\right)^{l}\frac{\Gamma(l+1+pb)}{\Gamma(1+pb)}\sum_{k=0}^{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}l-1}}(k+1)\frac{\Gamma(1+pb)}{\Gamma(k+2+pb)}\left(\frac{p}{2\beta_{0}}\right)^{k}f_{k}.

(5)

Note that the relation is
=
(equals) not
\sim
(asymptotically goes like).
Gaze at
eq.

5
and see that the growing part (in front of the sum) is exactly like
eq.

4a
and that the sum
itself is almost like the normalization,
eq.

4b
, albeit with upper limit the depending on
l
.

Equation

5
is nothing but the inverse of the formula for the
f_{k}
in a carefully chosen scheme.
In any scheme for
\alpha_{\text{s}}
,

f_{k}=r_{k}-\frac{2}{p}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(l+1)\beta_{k-1-l}r_{l}.

(6)

The algebra can be simplified by changing from the
\overline{\rm MS}
scheme, which is used in the literature to report the

r_{l}

[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, to a scheme with

\beta_{j}=\beta_{0}(\beta_{1}/\beta_{0})^{j}
.
With these
\beta_{j}
, the
\beta
-function can be summed as a geometric series, so this scheme is known as the “geometric scheme”.
Without loss, it is possible to define the coupling in this scheme,
\alpha_{\text{g}}
, such that
\Lambda_{\text{g}}=\Lambda_{\overline{\rm MS}}
.

Equation

6
implies a matrix that in the geometric scheme has a simple recursive structure.
The matrix has infinite extent but is lower triangular and can be inverted row-by-row.
The recursive nature of the matrix elements yields the
\Gamma
functions in
eq.

5
.

Has anything been gained?
After all, results are published for the
r_{l}
and the
f_{k}
are obtained (after deciding what
p
should be) from
eq.

6
.
For the first
L
terms,
eq.

5
simply returns the original information.
For
l\geq L
, however,
eq.

5
suggests an approximation as follows.
For the first
L
coefficients
r_{l}
, use the published information.
For the rest, namely
r_{l}
with
l\geq L
, truncate
eq.

5
, which amounts to dropping the first term and using

eq.

4
with upper limit on the sum in
R_{0}
at
L-1
.
The truncation is systematic, once
l
is large enough, because both
f_{l}
and any terms omitted from the sum in
eq.

5

are factorially smaller than the terms that are retained.

4
Reinterpreting the series

4.1
Approach of ref.
[
4
]

In the approximation just described, the series is approximated with

R(Q)=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}r_{l}\alpha^{l+1}+\sum_{l=L}^{\infty}R_{l}\alpha^{l+1},

(7)

using the
L
terms in the literature
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
and
approximating the rest from the obvious truncation of
eq.

5
.
If the lower limit of the second sum is lowered to
0
, then the sum can be defined via Borel summation.
To add those
L
terms in, they also have to be subtracted out, i.e.,

R(Q)=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}[r_{l}-R_{l}]\alpha^{l+1}+\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}R_{l}\alpha^{l+1}.

(8)

Then (see ref.
[
4
]
for details)

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}R_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}=R_{0}\frac{p}{2\beta_{0}}\mathcal{J}(pb,1/2\beta_{0}\alpha_{\text{g}})+\delta R,

(9)

where
\mathcal{J}
has a convergent expansion in its second argument, i.e., in
1/\alpha_{\text{g}}
.
The last term in
eq.

9
is explicitly of order
\Lambda_{\overline{\rm MS}}^{p}
, so it can be absorbed into the power term in

eq.

3
(or
\bar{\Lambda}
in
eq.

1
).
For example, for the pole mass (where
p=1
)

\delta m=\bar{m}\,\delta R=-R_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}b\pi}\frac{\Gamma(-b)}{2^{1+b}\beta_{0}}\left[\bar{m}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1/[2\beta_{0}\alpha_{\text{g}}(\bar{m})]}}{[\beta_{0}\alpha_{\text{g}}(\bar{m})]^{b}}\right],

(10)

where the sign in
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}b\pi}
exposes an ambiguity (from a choice of contour in a Borel integration), and the quantity in
brackets [ ] is immediately recognizable as
\Lambda_{\text{g}}=\Lambda_{\overline{\rm MS}}
.

In summary, the “minimal renormalon subtraction” (MRS) interpretation of a perturbative series is

R(Q)\to R_{\text{MRS}}(Q)\equiv\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}[r_{l}-R_{l}]\alpha^{l+1}+R_{0}\frac{p}{2\beta_{0}}\mathcal{J}(pb,1/2\beta_{0}\alpha_{\text{g}}).

(11)

For the pole mass,
r_{l}-R_{l}=\{-0.096,-0.004,0.128,-0.127\}
(at
\mu=\bar{m}
), much smaller than the
r_{l}
given in

section

2
.
For
l=3
, for example,
0.127
is much smaller than
17.2
.

4.2
Contrast with ref.
[
2
]

The reasoning given in ref.
[
2
]
is a bit different.
If the formulas hold only for asymptotically large orders, it is a prescription to add and subtract them and a practical concession
to truncate:

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}r_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}\to\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}[r_{l}-R_{l}]\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}+\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}R_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}\to\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}[r_{l}-R_{l}]\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}+R_{0}\frac{p}{2\beta_{0}}\mathcal{J}.

(12)

The reasoning of ref.
[
4
]
is that medium and large orders are introduced in a well-founded approximation, as
sketched in
eqs.

7
,
8
,
9
and
11
:

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}r_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}\to\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}r_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}+\sum_{l=L}^{\infty}R_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}\to\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}[r_{l}-R_{l}]\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}+R_{0}\frac{p}{2\beta_{0}}\mathcal{J}.

(13)

Standard, fixed-order perturbation theory is merely

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}r_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1}\to\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}r_{l}\alpha_{\text{g}}^{l+1},

(14)

the poor convergence of which is the
raison d’être
for more sophisticated approaches.
