C p C_{p} -Theory for Model Theorists
Clovis Hamel, Franklin D. Tall

Abstract
We present applications of C p C_{p} -theory, the branch of general topology concerned with spaces of real-valued continuous functions, to model theory, mostly in the context of continuous logics. We include C p C_{p} -theoretic results and proofs in a self-contained way for model theorists who are not familiar with the techniques of this field. We further generalize some results of Casazza and Iovino, and of the authors, involving the definability of Banach spaces including isomorphic copies of c 0 c_{0} or ℓ p \ell^{p} , after a problem posed by Odell and Gowers.

Introduction

C_{p}
-theory is a subfield of general topology. Given a topological space
X

C_{p}(X)
is the collection of continuous real-valued functions on
X

\mathbb{R}^{X}

C_{p}
-theory is concerned with the interrelations between the topology on

and the topology on
C_{p}(X)
. First identified as a distinct field by A. V. Arhangel’skiĭ, it has grown to the point of having a monograph
[
2
]
, a four-volume problem book
[
37
]

[

]

Theorem 1.1

If

is countably compact and

, then the closure of

in
C_{p}(X)
is compact if and only if

is countably compact in
C_{p}(X)
.

The usefulness of Grothendieck’s work in model theory, especially with regard to stability was recognized by J. Iovino
[
21
]
, I. Ben Yaacov
[
4
]
and A. Pillay
[
28
]
, all in the context of logics satisfying the compactness theorem.

When Iovino described his use of Grothendieck’s theorem in his solution with Casazza
[
7
]
of a problem of Gowers
[
16
]
to the second author, the latter realized it could be considerably generalized by using the methods and results of
C_{p}
-theory. This was done in the recent
[
18
]
, with the goal of introducing model theory to
C_{p}
-theorists unfamiliar with mathematical logic in general, and with model theory in particular. Here we instead target a model-theoretic audience unfamiliar with
C_{p}
-theory and not very knowledgeable of general topology. Our goal is to present enough

-theory so that model theorists can use it, as well as to again give an example of its usefulness by further generalizing the results of
[
7
]
and
[
18
]
.

It is worth mentioning that

We have omitted some

We will obtain our undefinability results using special cases which are easy to prove, but thought we should include the general cases for those interested.

The main application of the results occurs with regard to Gowers’ problem
[
16
]
which asks whether every infinite dimensional explicitly definable Banach space includes an isomorphic copy of either

or
\ell^{p}
. All the definitions are given below but a model theorist would probably recognize at once that this question intersects her field as it is mainly about definability. There are two fundamental facts that motivate Gowers’ problem:

(1)

or
\ell^{p}
.

(2)

An interesting characteristic is that the norm of Tsirelson’s space appears on both sides of the equality in its definition; this has become what analysts call an

[

devoted to the Tsirelson space.) After Tsirelson, many other pathological Banach spaces were constructed using similar techniques. Of course, the first problem to face is to formalize the question, and E. Odell says in his book
[
26
]
that this is a logician’s task. The natural logics to approach this problem are

as they provide ample room for approximation and the analyst’s

[
7
]
and
[
9
]
presented both a formulation and a solution of this problem in the context of compact continuous logics, i.e., continuous logics which satisfy the Compactness Theorem. We shall present here generalizations of some results obtained in
[

and
[

to a wide class of logics with the help of

-theory, in particular to non-compact, countable fragments of continuous

We shall allow ourselves to repeat well-known

-theoretic results and proofs from the literature for a reasonably self-contained exposition so that this work can serve as an introduction for model theorists wishing to learn

The relationship of Grothendieck’s Theorem with double limit conditions was explored by V. Pták

Definition 2.25

Let

Proposition 2.26

A space

Proof.

Suppose

\mathcal{U}
over
\kappa

The importance of compactness in Model Theory is well understood: the pioneers of the discipline built around this topological concept some of the fundamental pillars. Even though some famous open problems on compactness remain open, topologists have reached a deep understanding of the subject, and model theorists have also extensively explored the applications of compactness in first-order logic and its variations. In the last decades, the work of S. Angelic spaces
were introduced by Fremlin and appeared in Pryce

[

Connections between NIP and compact spaces are explored in
[

Definition 6.2

A topological space is a

We will show

Every

Every Lindelöf

A perfect pre-image of a

This is Theorem 3.7.25 in

Proof.

Given an arbitrary Hausdorff space

by declaring closed all subsets of

that have closed intersections with each compact subspace of

. This new space is called

and can be shown to be a

-space. Every continuous map

from

to a Hausdorff space

induces a continuous map

from

to

, by assigning to

the image

is perfect since

is. Define a function

and it follows that

is perfect. But

is one-to-one, so it’s a homeomorphism, and so

∎

(

The following result is well-known:

Proposition 6.7
.

The continuous image of a countably compact space is countably compact.

Proof.

Let

be countably compact and

a continuous function. Suppose

is an infinite subset of

, and let

be obtained by picking one point from each

. Then

is infinite and so has a limit point

. Then

is a limit point of

, since if

is an open set about

, the pre-image

is an open set about

.

Definition 6.8
.

Let
X
be a topological space.

(a)

The
density

of

is the smallest cardinality of a dense subset of
X
.

(b)

A

of

is a family

of subsets of
X
such that given any
x\in X
and open set
U
containing
x
, there is an

satisfying
x\in A\subseteq U
. The
network weight

nw(X)
of
X
is the smallest cardinality of a network of
X
.

(c)

The

of
X
is the minimal weight of all the spaces onto which
X
can be continuously mapped.

We see that if
X
is compact, then
nw(X)=w(X)
(see
[
2
]
): given any Hausdorff space
X
, we can construct a Hausdorff space

such that
w(Y)\leq nw(X)
and
Y
is a continuous one-to-one image of
X
. To see this, let
\mathcal{A}
be a network of

of size
nw(X)
. Consider all the pairs of elements
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
for which there are disjoint open subsets
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
such that
A_{1}\subseteq U_{1}
and
A_{2}\subseteq U_{2}
. It suffices to define the topological space
Y
as the one with the same underlying set as

and with the topology generated by the all the open sets of

obtained as above. Thus the identity map is as desired. That

for every compact space

follows from the preceding discussion and the well-known fact that a continuous one-to-one map between a compact space and a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism.

Some other relatively easy facts regarding these cardinals are the following:
iw(X)\leq nw(X)
,
d(X)\leq w(X)
,
nw(X)=nw(C_{p}(X)
and

for every

. See

2
]
, p. 26, for a detailed exposition. Now we proceed with two definitions that arise naturally when studying topological function spaces:

Definition 6.9
.

Let
X
be a topological space.

(a)

X
is
\kappa
-monolithic
if

whenever
\left|A\right|\leq\kappa
. We say that
X
is

if it is
\kappa
-monolithic for every
\kappa
.

(b)

X
is
\kappa
-stable
if for every continuous image
Y
of

we have:
iw(Y)\leq\kappa
if and only if
nw(Y)\leq\kappa
. We say that
X
is

if it is
\kappa
-stable for every
\kappa
.

Notice that being monolithic is inherited by all subspaces. Examples of monolithic spaces include all metric spaces and spaces with a countable network, whereas examples of stable spaces include all compact spaces and Lindelöf
\Sigma
-spaces (as we shall prove below). The following theorem shows that stability and monolithicity are intertwined notions for
X
and

.

Theorem 6.10

(Arhangel’skiĭ)
.

X
is
\kappa
-stable if and only if
C_{p}(X)
is
\kappa
-monolithic.

Proof.

Assume
C_{p}(X)
is
\kappa
-monolithic and let
f:X\to Y
be a continuous surjection. Since always
iw(Y)\leq nw(Y)
, we also assume that
iw(Y)\leq\kappa
. By Lemma
2.2
,
C_{p}(Y)
is homeomorphic to a subspace of
C_{p}(X)
and so it’s
\kappa
-monolithic. In particular, by looking at a dense subset of
C_{p}(Y)
and using the definition of
\kappa
-monolithicity,
nw(C_{p}(Y))\leq d(C_{p}(Y))
. Using that
iw(Y)=d(C_{p}(Y))
and
nw(Y)=nw(C_{p}(Y)
, we obtain
nw(Y)\leq iw(Y)
as desired. Conversely, assume that
X
is
\kappa
-stable and let
M\subseteq C_{p}(X)
be such that
\left|M\right|<\kappa
. Define
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
as the diagonal product of

, i.e.
f(x)=\langle g(x):g\in M\rangle
, and let

. By our choice,
w(Y)\leq\left|M\right|\leq\kappa
as in the proof of Theorem
2.5
.

The rest of the proof is a bit dense. Although the theorem is important, the reader is unlikely to use the
real quotient topology
, so it is not unreasonable to take the rest on faith. But we shall proceed.

Let
Y^{*}
be the topological space with the same underlying set as

but endowed with the

is the strongest completely regular topology for which

is continuous (see
[
2
]
, p. 14). Then the identity

is a continuous bijection. By the definition of the i-weight,

.

Conversely, since

defines a continuous surjection from

onto

, i.e. the real quotient map

, and

is
\kappa
-stable,

and

. Also, the space

is homeomorphic to the closed subspace

of

since

is a real quotient map (see Section 0.4 of

for a detailed discussion of real quotient maps). Now let

where

is the projection

. Also,

and thus
