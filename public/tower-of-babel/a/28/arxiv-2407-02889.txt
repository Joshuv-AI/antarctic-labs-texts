Axial Bianchi I meets drifting extragalactic sources
Thomas Schücker

Abstract




We recompute
ab initio
the drift of comoving sources in axial Bianchi I universes. Our result resolves the long-standing disagreement between the computations by Quercellini et al. from 2009 and by Marcori et al. from 2018 in favour of the latter. These computations have a
potential impact on Gaia data analysis and cosmological parameter estimation.

Key-Words: cosmological parameters

1
Introduction

As early as 1933, Georges Lemaître
[
1
]
, following a recommendation by Albert Einstein, considers (tri-axial) Bianchi I universes without using this name. They generalize flat Robertson-Walker universes by allowing three different scale factors in three orthogonal directions:

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm{d}}\tau^{2}=\hbox{\rm{d}}t^{2}-a(t)^{2}\hbox{\rm{d}}x^{2}-b(t)^{2}\hbox{\rm{d}}y^{2}-c(t)^{2}\,\hbox{\rm{d}}z^{2},\quad a(t),\,b(t),\,c(t)>0

(1)

These universes are homogeneous and anisotropic with a 3-dimensional Abelian isometry group. Its Lie algebra is the first in the list of Luigi Bianchi’s famous classification from 1898
[
2
]
of the real, 3-dimensional Lie algebras. In the same work he also

•

exhibits 3-dimensional Riemannian manifolds on which these Lie algebras act transitively as Killing vectors,

•

he exhibits 3-dimensional Riemannian manifolds on which 4-dimensional Lie algebras act in the same way and shows that they all contain the 3-dimensional Lie algebras from his list as ideals,

•

and he shows that 5-dimensional Lie algebras cannot act in this way.

For Bianchi I this manifold is simply the 3-dimensional Eulidean space and the manifolds for the other Lie algebras of type Bianchi II - IX underlie the corresponding higher
Bianchi universes, whose study began in the late sixties
[
3
]
-
[
5
]
.

The universes with four Killing vectors are special cases, which are called locally rotationally symmetric (L.R.S.) or axial.

The proper time element of the axial Bianchi I universe is obtained by choosing two equal scale factors, e.g.
b=a
. Then there is a fourth Killing vector,
x\partial_{y}-y\partial_{x}
, generating rotations around the
z
-axis.
Note that if all three scale factors are different, the Bianchi I universe has three privileged axes, the
x
-,
y
- and
z
-axis. The axial Bianchi I universe has one privileged axis.
If all three scale factors are equal, we are back at flat Robertson-Walker universes with the maximally symmetric Euclidean 3-space, three translations, three rotations
and no privileged axis.

1.2
Aim and motivation of choices

The purpose of this work is resolving the mentioned disagreement and presenting correct drift equations ready to be confronted with the new high-quality Gaia data. Our calculation is
ab initio
and
low tech
and concerns the drift of a
comoving source
in
axial
Bianchi I universes filled with comoving dust and admitting
small anisotropy
. We also include an
observer’s small peculiar velocity
.

Let us comment our choices:

ab initio:

Our only prerequisites are the geodesic equation, Einstein’s equation and the definition of cosmic time
t
.

low tech:

Our aim is that an experimental colleague can verify all calculations.

As Quercellini et al. we work in coordinates and use Cartesian coordinates
(x,y,z)
and their spherical coordinates
(r,\varphi,\theta)
.

All approximations are first order Taylor expansions in
four small
dimensionless quantities:

•

Light of atomic period
T
is emitted by an extragalactic source and observed with Doppler-shifted period
T_{D}
, spectroscopic redshift and incoming direction. We suppose that its time of flight
t_{of}
is superior to that of light from Andromeda
t_{of}>10^{14}\,
s. Admitting the detection limit of the James Webb Space Telescope,
T_{D}<10^{13}\,
s, we define the small dimensionless parameter
\epsilon_{s}\vcentcolon=T_{D}/t_{of}<10^{-27}
,
\cdot_{s}
for spectroscopic, and use it to compute the redshift by means of a first order Taylor expansion in
\epsilon_{s}
.

•

The incoming direction is recorded over a tracking time of say 10 years, which we also denote by
T_{D}
. We define
\epsilon_{d}\vcentcolon=T_{D}/t_{of}<3\cdot 10^{-6}
,
\cdot_{s}
for drift.

•

We allow the observer to have a small peculiar velocity
V
divided by the speed of light (that we set to 1).
We will restrict the fit to
V<10\,\%.

•

We allow for a small time-dependent anisotropy,
\eta(t)
with
|\eta(t)|<10\,\%
between emission and reception today,
t\in[t_{e},t_{0}]
.

These four small quantities are considered independent variables, not necessarily of the same order of magnitude.
The drift equations e.g.  (
66
) are of the form

\delta\theta\sim(a\,\eta+b\,V)\,\epsilon_{d}
.
We denote by the symbol
\sim
an equality neglecting terms in
\epsilon_{d}^{2}
,
\eta^{2}
,
V^{2}
or
\eta\,V
and higher orders, such that each neglected term is less than 1 %. We suppose that all functions encountered are analytic and acknowledge that the domains of convergence must be controlled during the fit by reducing the domain of input parameters as necessary. In the example we must control that the coefficients
a
and
b
remain of order 1.

comoving source:

Peculiar velocities of the sources are not included because they would add three more parameters for each single source. But we may hope that in a large enough sample the peculiar velocities of the sources average out and become less and less important with increasing redshift.

observer’s small peculiar velocity:

A small peculiar velocity of the observer is considered because it only adds three more parameters, which might be relevant for sources with redshifts below 0.5.

Axial

Bianchi universes have four Killing vectors
while generic Bianchi universes have only three Killing vectors. In this sense the axial Bianchi universes are closer to the ‘Robertson-Walker’ universes that obey the cosmological principle (six Killing vectors) than the generic Bianchi universes. Now the four Killing vectors of axial Bianchi I universes imply geodesics with four conserved quantities, which in turn produce equations for redshift and drift that are much simpler and involve less arbitrary parameters in their fit compared to tri-axial Bianchi I universes.

Nevertheless axial Bianchi I universes are sufficient to trace the errors in Quercellini et al.’s calculation.

small anisotropy:

As the cosmological principle with its six space-like Killing vectors is quite successful in fitting today’s data it is a wise choice to remain close to Robertson-Walker universes. For axial Bianchi I universes this means small anisotropy
\eta(t)\vcentcolon={\textstyle\frac{2}{3}}[c(t)/a(t)-1]\rightarrow 0
.
We will be more precise about this parametrization and motivate it at the beginning of section 5.
Note that there are only two other axial universes admitting this limit: axial Bianchi V and IX.
Axial Bianchi V however is incompatible with Einstein’s equation sourced by comoving dust
[
11
]
[
12
]
.

During our calculation we carefully separate the three parts:

(i) kinematics:

position of the source on the celestial sphere as a function of the observer’s cosmic time and the redshift of the source,
(ii) dynamics:
Einstein’s equation with cosmological constant and comoving dust,

(iii) approximation of small anisotropy:

|\eta(t)|\ll 1
.

On the other hand Quercellini et al. mix kinematics and dynamics in their calculation.

The path of a light ray

The axial Bianchi I metric can be written

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm{d}}\tau^{2}=\hbox{\rm{d}}t^{2}-a(t)^{2}\left[\hbox{\rm{d}}x^{2}+\hbox{\rm{d}}y^{2}\right]-c(t)^{2}\,\hbox{\rm{d}}z^{2},\quad a(t),\,c(t)>0.

(2)

\dot{}\vcentcolon=\hbox{\rm{d}}/\hbox{\rm{d}}q

\displaystyle A\vcentcolon=a(t(q))^{2}\dot{x},\ B\vcentcolon=a^{2}\dot{y},\ C\vcentcolon=c^{2}\dot{z}\quad\text{and}\quad Ay-Bx=0.

The last equation implies that if

-direction. Otherwise the geodesic remains in the plane defined by the

\,\frac{B}{a^{2}}\,\\[1.42262pt]

\displaystyle\,\frac{\tilde{C}\tilde{W}_{\tilde{0}}}{c_{\tilde{0}}}\,\sim\,\frac{CW_{\tilde{0}}}{c_{\tilde{0}}}\left[1+\,\frac{N^{2}}{A^{2}+B^{2}}\,\frac{N}{C}\left(1-\left\{\frac{CW_{0}}{c_{0}}\right\}^{2}\right)\gamma\right]\sim\,\frac{CW_{\tilde{0}}}{c_{\tilde{0}}}\left[1+\,\frac{N}{C}\,\gamma\right].

We Taylor develop the leading term

around
t_{0}
viz

w(t_{0}+T_{D})\sim w_{0}+T_{D}\,w^{\prime}_{0}
:

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{C^{2}}\,\frac{c^{2}}{a^{2}}+1\right)^{-1/2}\,,

(38)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{C^{2}}\,\frac{c^{2}}{a^{2}}\,(H-H_{c})\left(\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{C^{2}}\,\frac{c^{2}}{a^{2}}+1\right)^{-3/2},\quad H\vcentcolon=\,\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\,\quad H_{c}\vcentcolon=\,\frac{c^{\prime}}{c}\,,

(39)

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\theta,\quad\quad w_{0}^{\prime}\ =\ \sin^{2}\theta\,\cos\theta\,(H_{0}-H_{c0}).

(40)

Therefore

\displaystyle W_{\tilde{0}}\,\frac{C}{c_{\tilde{0}}}\sim\cos\theta\,[1+\sin^{2}\theta\,(H_{0}-H_{c0})\,T_{D}],

(41)

and

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{\theta}\sim\cos\theta\left[1+\sin^{2}\theta\,(H_{0}-H_{c0})\,T_{D}+\,\frac{\gamma}{\cos\theta}\right].

(42)

The apparent angular drift of the comoving source
\delta\theta\vcentcolon=\tilde{\theta}-\theta
then takes the form

\displaystyle\delta\theta\sim-\sin\theta\,\cos\theta\,(H_{0}-H_{c0})\,T_{D}-\,\frac{\gamma}{\sin\theta}\,.

(43)

We need a second angle to localize the direction of the incoming photons on the celestial sphere. Following standard conventions we choose the angle
\varphi
in the

\vec{x}^{\prime}
into this plane and the positive

and the unit vector in the positive

\displaystyle\vec{x}^{\prime}_{\perp}\vcentcolon=\,W\begin{pmatrix}\,\frac{A}{a^{2}}\,\\[1.42262pt]

0\end{pmatrix},\quad\vec{u}_{x}\vcentcolon=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{a}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(44)

Then the angle
\varphi

\displaystyle\cos\varphi=\,\frac{\vec{x}^{\prime}_{\perp 0}\cdot\vec{u}_{x0}}{\sqrt{\vec{x}^{\prime}_{\perp 0}\cdot\vec{x}^{\prime}_{\perp 0}}}\,=\,\frac{A}{\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}}\,,

(45)

and we retrieve the usual relations of spherical coordinates:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\cos\varphi\,\sin\theta,

(46)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\sin\varphi\,\sin\theta,

(47)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\quad\quad\,\cos\theta.

Note that if

Our last task is to compute the same angle

for the second photon at its arrival time

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{\varphi}=\,\frac{\tilde{\vec{x}}^{\prime}_{\perp\tilde{0}}\cdot\vec{u}_{x\tilde{0}}}{\sqrt{\tilde{\vec{x}}^{\prime}_{\perp\tilde{0}}\cdot\tilde{\vec{x}}^{\prime}_{\perp\tilde{0}}}}\,=\,\frac{\tilde{A}}{\sqrt{\tilde{A}^{2}+\tilde{B}^{2}}}\,\sim\,\frac{\,\frac{A}{N}\,+\alpha}{\sqrt{\,\frac{A^{2}+B^{2}}{N^{2}}\,-2\,\frac{C}{N}\,\gamma}}\,.

At this point we must resist a further linearization in

and

5
Linearizing the scale factors

We know that the anisotropy of the universe cannot be large and we will concentrate on axial Bianchi I universes close to an underlying maximally symmetric one with only one scale factor
a_{F}(t)
,
\cdot_{F}
for Friedman. It is convenient to parameterize the small deviation
\eta(t)
in the following way:

\displaystyle a=\vcentcolon a_{F}\,[1-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta],\quad c=\vcentcolon a_{F}\,[1+\eta].

(50)

Other linearizations are viable in Bianchi I universes
[
13
]
. This is not true in the two other axial universes that admit maximal symmetry in the limit of small
\eta\,
: Bianchi V and IX. There the above linearization is mandatory
[
12
]
. (As we are presently working on axial Bianchi IX universes and their flat limit, axial Bianchi I, I choose this parameterization here.)

Indicating the linearization in
\eta
and
V
(neglecting cross-terms
\eta V
) also by
\sim
we have,

\displaystyle\eta_{0}=0,\quad H\sim H_{F}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{\prime},\quad H_{c}\sim H_{F}+\eta^{\prime},

(51)

and

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\,\frac{a_{F}}{N}\left[1-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-3\cos^{2}\theta\right)\eta\right],

(52)

\displaystyle\underline{z}_{0}+1

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\,\frac{a_{F0}}{a_{Fe}}\left[1+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-3\cos^{2}\theta\right)\eta_{e}+\cos\varphi\,\sin\theta\,V_{x}+\cos\theta\,V_{z}\right],

(53)

\displaystyle F_{c}

\displaystyle\sim\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Na_{F0}}\left(3\,\sin^{2}\theta\,\eta_{e}+\cos\varphi\,\sin\theta\,V_{x}+\cos\theta\,V_{z}\right),

(54)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\chi_{e0}+O(\eta),\quad\quad J\ \sim\ \chi_{e0}+O(\eta),

(55)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\epsilon_{d}\,(3\,\cos\varphi\,\sin\theta\,\cos^{2}\theta\,\eta_{e}+[1-\cos^{2}\varphi\,\sin^{2}\theta]\,V_{x}

(56)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\cos\varphi\,\sin\theta\,\cos\theta\,V_{z}\,),

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt-\sin\theta\,\epsilon_{d}\left(3\,\sin\theta\,\cos\theta\,\eta_{e}+\cos\varphi\,\cos\theta\,V_{x}-\sin\theta\,V_{z}\right),

(57)

where
\chi_{e0}

is the conformal time of flight of the photon = the comoving geodesic ‘distance’ between
\vec{x}_{e}
and
\vec{x}_{0}
in the underlying Friedman universe,

\displaystyle\chi_{e0}\vcentcolon=\int_{t_{e}}^{t_{0}}\,\frac{1}{a_{F}}\,\quad\text{and}\quad t_{of}\sim a_{F0}\,\chi_{e0}.

(58)

Consider first the drift with respect to
\varphi
, equation (
49
), and linearize it in
\eta
and
V
, but not in
T_{D}/\!\!\left\{a_{F0}\chi_{e0}\right\}\sim\epsilon_{d}
:

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{\varphi}\sim\frac{\cos\varphi+[3\cos\varphi\,\cos^{2}\!\theta\,\eta_{e}+(1-\cos^{2}\!\varphi\,\sin^{2}\!\theta)\!\!\left\{V_{x}/\sin\theta\right\}-\cos\varphi\,\cos\theta\,V_{z}]\,\epsilon_{d}}{\sqrt{1+2\cos\theta[3\cos\theta\,\eta_{e}+\cos\varphi\,\cos\theta\left\{V_{x}/\sin\theta\right\}-V_{z}]\,\epsilon_{d}}}\,.

(59)

For
\sin\theta=0
, the angle
\varphi
is ill-defined and equation (
59
) is singular:

\displaystyle\lim_{\sin\theta\,\rightarrow\,0}\cos\tilde{\varphi}\ =\lim_{\sin\theta\,\rightarrow\,0}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\cos\varphi}\left\{\,\frac{V_{x}}{\sin\theta}\right\}\epsilon_{d}}\,.

(60)

If not only
V\ll 1
, but also
|\{V_{x}/\sin\theta\}|\ll 1
, we may further
[
14
]
linearize
\cos\tilde{\varphi}
:

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{\varphi}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\cos\varphi\left[1+\,\frac{\sin^{2}\varphi}{\cos\varphi}\,\frac{V_{x}}{\sin\theta}\,\epsilon_{d}\right].

(61)

In this case the drift with respect to the angle
\varphi
is,

\displaystyle\delta\cos\varphi

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{\varphi}-\cos\varphi\sim\,{\sin^{2}\varphi}\,\frac{V_{x}}{\sin\theta}\,\epsilon_{d},

(62)

\displaystyle\delta\varphi

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\sin\varphi}\,\,\delta\cos\varphi\sim-\,{\sin\varphi}\,\frac{V_{x}}{\sin\theta}\,\epsilon_{d}\,.

(63)

In particular if
V_{x}
vanishes, then
\varphi
is constant and the drift remains in the plane defined by the
z
-axis and this constant angle
\varphi
.

We encounter the well-known singularity of polar coordinates at the poles: If
\sin\theta=0
, then our source lies on the
z
-axis, there is no
\theta
-drift and the azimuthal angle
\varphi
is ill-defined. Now if
\sin\theta
is small, our perturbative parameter

becomes large and the linear approximation fails. That means that for sources close to the
z
-axis we cannot use the
\varphi
-drift to constrain the parameters to be fitted. We anticipate that this truncation will not reduce the statistical power of the fit significantly.

The definition of the angle
\theta
does not involve the projection into the

-plane and linearizing the
\theta
-drift is straight-forward:

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{\theta}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\cos\theta\left[1-\sin^{2}\theta\left(3\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{F0}\chi_{e0}\,\eta^{\prime}_{0}+\eta_{e}\right)+\cos\varphi\,\frac{V_{x}}{\sin\theta}\,-\,\frac{V_{z}}{\cos\theta}\,\right)\epsilon_{d}\right]\hskip-2.84526pt,

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt\delta\cos\theta

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\sin^{2}\theta\,\cos\theta\left(3\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{F0}\chi_{e0}\,\eta^{\prime}_{0}+\eta_{e}\right)+\cos\varphi\,\frac{V_{x}}{\sin\theta}\,-\,\frac{V_{z}}{\cos\theta}\right)\epsilon_{d},

(65)

\displaystyle\delta\theta

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\left(3\sin\theta\,\cos\theta\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{F0}\chi_{e0}\,\eta^{\prime}_{0}+\eta_{e}\right)+\cos\varphi\,\cos\theta\,V_{x}-\sin\theta\,V_{z}\right)\epsilon_{d}\,.

Note that the first term,

, in the drift (

-direction today,
H_{c0}-H_{0}\sim{\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}\eta^{\prime}_{0}>0

The second term,

-direction from emission until today,

is positive and, since we have set

is negative.

If we adopt Einstein’s equations with cosmological constant and dust, then

and

are given by,

\displaystyle a_{F}(t)=a_{F0}\left(\frac{\cosh\left(\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t\right)-1}{\cosh\left(\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t_{0}\right)-1}\right)^{1/3},\hskip 11.38109ptt_{0}={\textstyle\frac{2}{3}}\,\frac{\text{arc}\tanh\,\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda 0}}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda 0}}}\,\frac{1}{H_{F0}}\,,\hskip 11.38109pt\Omega_{\Lambda 0}\vcentcolon=\,\frac{\Lambda}{3\,H_{F0}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}_{0}\,\frac{a_{F0}^{3}}{a_{F}^{3}}\quad\text{implying}\quad\eta(t)=-\,{\textstyle\frac{2}{3}}\,\frac{\eta^{\prime}_{0}}{H_{F0}}\,\frac{\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda 0}}}{1-\Omega_{\Lambda 0}}\left[\coth\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t\right)-1/\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda 0}}\right].

Note that the scale factors today
a_{F0},\,a_{0}
and
c_{0}
are non-essential parameters and may be chosen arbitrarily. We follow the usual convention and set them equal implying
\eta(t_{0})=0
. On the other hand

is an essential parameter also called ‘Hubble stretch’.

We take

Gyr,
\Omega_{\Lambda 0}=0.7
and
H_{0}=72
km/s/Mpc implying

Gyr,

Let us compute the ratio

of the second term in the linearized drift equation (

, we find

Gyr and by numerical integration

\displaystyle\,\frac{\eta_{e}}{{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}a_{F0}\chi_{e0}\,\eta^{\prime}_{0}}\,=-\,4.38.

In contrast to Friedman universes (axial) Bianchi I universes are not isotropic. This anisotropy manifests itself via a direction-dependent Lemaître-Hubble ‘constant’ (quantified by the Hubble stretch
\eta^{\prime}_{0}

This is in contradiction

6
Conclusion

The second term in the drift equation (
66
), which is proportional to
\eta_{e}
and which we find to be dominant, depends on the redshift of the source via the inverse of the direction-dependent relation (
53
). It is therefore phenomenologically more
challenging for a fit of the Gaia catalogue of quasars,
which come with precise redshift measurements up to
\underline{z}_{0}=4.7
.
Both terms might help to reduce the Hubble tension.

We look forward to this fit.

Appendix A
Noether’s theorem for geodesics

With the four Killing vectors,
\partial_{x},\,\partial_{y},\,\partial_{z}
and
x\partial_{y}-y\partial_{x}
, Emmy Noether’s theorem yields four conserved quantities on any geodesic
x^{\mu}(q)
. We can compute them conveniently by defining a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(x,p)\vcentcolon={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}g^{\mu\nu}(x)\,p_{\mu}p_{\nu}
and by using Hamilton’s equations with
\dot{\ }\vcentcolon={\hbox{\rm{d}}}/{\hbox{\rm{d}}q}
,

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{\mu}=\ \ \frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_{\mu}}\,=\,p^{\mu}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\dot{p}_{\mu}=-\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\,.

(70)

We interpret the first Hamiton equation as definition of the 4-momentum
p_{\mu}
; then equation (
70
) is nothing but the geodesic equation. For the conserved quantity generated by a Killing vector
\xi=\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
we may take
-\xi^{\mu}p_{\mu}
.
The four Killing vectors, then yield the four conserved quantities,

\displaystyle A=-p_{x}=a^{2}\dot{x},\ B=-p_{y}=a^{2}\dot{y},\ C=-p_{z}=c^{2}\dot{z};\ Ay-Bx=-p_{x}y+p_{y}x=0,

(71)

which are the familiar 3-momentum and the
z
-component of angular momentum of the point-particle moving on the geodesic trajectory
x^{\mu}(q)
.
