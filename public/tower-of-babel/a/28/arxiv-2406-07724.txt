Nitsche stabilized Virtual element approximations for a Brinkman problem with mixed boundary conditions
David Mora, Jesus Vellojin, Nitesh Verma

Abstract
In this paper, we formulate, analyse and implement the discrete formulation of the Brinkman problem with mixed boundary conditions, including slip boundary condition, using the Nitsche’s technique for virtual element methods. The divergence conforming virtual element spaces for the velocity function and piecewise polynomials for pressure are approached for the discrete scheme. We derive a robust stability analysis of the Nitsche stabilized discrete scheme for this model problem. We establish an optimal and vigorous a priori error estimates of the discrete scheme with constants independent of the viscosity. Moreover, a set of numerical tests demonstrates the robustness with respect to the physical parameters and verifies the derived convergence results.



1
Introduction and problem statement

We are interested in the numerical approximation by the virtual element method
of the Brinkman system with mixed boundary conditions, that is, Dirichlet conditions
on one part on the boundary and slip conditions on the rest of the boundary. These boundary conditions, representing the inflow/outflow in domain, and flow through the boundary wall as well fluid slipping along the boundary wall, have been introduced in the several applications such as water treatment, reverse osmosis and so on.
The Brinkman equations can be seen as an extension of Darcy’s law to describe
the laminar flow behavior of a viscous fluid within a porous material
with possibly heterogeneous permeability, so that the flow is dominated
by Darcy regime on a part of the domain and by Stokes on the other parts of the domain.
In the last years, the numerical solution of this system has acquired great
interest due to high practical importance in different areas,
including several industrial and environmental applications, such as,
filtering porous layers, oil reservoirs, the study of foams, among others.

The virtual element method (VEM) introduced in
[
12
]
, belong to the
so-called polytopal methods for solving PDEs by using general polygonal/polyhedral meshes.
These methods have received substantial attention in the last years, for instance,
hybrid high order method
[

,

,

,

]
,

[

virtual element method

The VEM can be seen as an extension of Finite Elements
Method (FEM) to polygonal or polyhedral decompositions.
The VEM has been applied for different problems in fluid mechanics, see for instance,

The so-called Nitsche methods can be regarded
as a stabilization technique where some terms are added to the
variational formulation so that the boundary conditions can be incorporated in
a weak form. One of the main advantages of the Nitsche method is its versatility. It can be applied to a wide range of partial differential equations, including elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic equations. For instance,
finite element discretizations with Nitsche method has evolved to handle general boundary conditions
[
35
]
, including interface problems
[
33
]
, unilateral and frictional contact
[
21
,
22
]
, or membrane filtration processes
[
20
]
, among others. On each reference, we observe that a properly formulated penalty terms in the Nitsche method ensure consistency and stability of the numerical solution, even for complex problems and irregular domains. This stability property is crucial for obtaining reliable and accurate results in practical simulations. Moreover, the Nitsche method can be relatively straightforward to implement compared to other approaches for enforcing boundary conditions. Its penalty-based formulation simplifies the incorporation of boundary conditions into the variational formulation of the problem. By the nature of the method, this can be seamlessly integrated with modern numerical techniques such as adaptive mesh refinement, parallel computing, higher-order finite elements, or virtual elements method. In the latter, the literature regarding Nitsche’s method is scarce, with a recent contribution by
[
13
]
. Here, the authors study the extension to virtual elements of the Lagrange multiplier method, in its stabilized formulation as proposed by Barbosa and Hughes
[
6
]
, and the Nitsche method
[
39
]
. They proved stability and optimal error estimates, under custom conditions on the stabilization parameters. The results are extended for two and three dimensional geometries with curved domains, where the numerical experiments assess the performance of the method, suggesting a viable alternative to the corresponding scheme in the finite element method.

In the present contribution we propose a Nitsche method
for the Brinkman system with mixed boundary conditions.
We consider Dirichlet condition on a part of the boundary and
slip conditions on the rest of the boundary. This approach have been employed in several Navier-Stokes models to impose a slip boundary condition. We have for example the work from
[
42
]
, where they compare the Lagrange multiplier and Nitsche’s method for the weakly imposition of the slip boundary condition on curved domains. Recently, in
[
32
]
the authors study the weak imposition of the slip-boundary through Nitsche’s method and projected normals between the computational and the continuous domain, and also explore the well-known Babuška-Sapondzhyan Paradox. The type
of conditions behind the models presented in the aforementioned works are important in different areas for fluid flow problems.
In particular, these boundary conditions appears naturally in the analysis of numerical methods for desalinization processes, filtration, among others.
For that reason, in this work, we extend the results presented
in
[
43
]
for the new model problem. Unlike the Dirichlet boundary conditions, the slip-boundary conditions are inadequate to impose strongly for discrete solution. This drawback is due to the unavailability of the degrees of freedom for the normal component and normal derivative of the function on the boundary in the discrete space.
It is well know that different strategies can be considered
for imposing mixed conditions, including slip conditions, such as, Lagrange multiplier method.
However, we here propose a symmetric discrete variational formulation
by adding Nitsche terms in order to incorporate the boundary conditions
considered in the model problem. Moreover, we discretize
the problem, by using the virtual element method presented in
[
9
]

for the Stokes problem. We define a new Nitsche term to impose the slip boundary condition for the discrete scheme using the piecewise polynomial projection on each polygon
K
(in the mesh) on the boundary. We establish that the discrete problem is well posed
by proving a global inf-sup condition and we write stability by using
appropriate mesh-parameter dependent norms. Under rather mild assumptions on the polygonal meshes
and by using interpolation estimates, the convergence rate is proved to
be optimal in terms of the mesh size
h
. We would like to emphasis that the constants in the derivation of error estimates are independent of the physical parameters in this model problem. The trace inequality for the piecewise polynomials are utilised in the analysis in order to prove the stability.
In summary, the advantages of the proposed Nitsche VEM method
for the Brinkman problem are, on the one hand,
the possibility to use general polygonal meshes,
and on the other hand, the possibility of an easy imposition
of general boundary conditions, including non homogeneous
Dirichlet conditions, slip conditions, among others.

Remark 3.2

The imposition of Dirichlet boundary condition using the interpolant is not enough with complex domains, and the slip boundary conditions are not easy to impose in numerical experiments. Thus, we proceed here with the Nitsche’s technique for mixed boundary imposition.

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{2}:\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}(K)\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},\qquad Q_{h}^{k}:=\{q_{h}\in Q:q_{h}|_{K}\in Q_{h}^{k}(K)\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

Lemma 4.5

Proof

From
[

]

\displaystyle\sup_{(0\neq){\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}\cap\mathbf{V}}\frac{b({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathbf{V}}}\geq\beta\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}^{k}.

For

,
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle\sup_{(0\neq){\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}\cap\mathbf{V}}\frac{b_{h}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}}=\sup_{(0\neq){\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}\cap\mathbf{V}}\frac{b({\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},q_{h})}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathbf{V}}}.

independent of
h
and all the physical parameters.

Proof

from (

yield

\displaystyle m_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

Proof

Let
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}:=(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})-(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{k}=\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}\times Q_{h}^{k}
, then,
as a consequence of the global inf-sup condition in Theorem
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{((\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq)(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{k}}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}\pm(\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h}))}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{((\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq)(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{k}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}_{h}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})-\mathcal{A}_{h}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h}))}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}}+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{h}((\boldsymbol{u},p)-(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h}),(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h}))}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}}\right).

We start the error analysis with estimating the consistency error for any
(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})\in\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}\times Q_{h}^{k}

E_{c}(\boldsymbol{u},p):=\mathcal{L}_{h}((\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h}))-\mathcal{A}_{h}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},r_{h})).

Use of the definition of discrete formulation (
3.6
) leads to

\displaystyle E_{c}(\boldsymbol{u},p)

\displaystyle=F_{h}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-G(r_{h})-m_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-b_{h}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},p)+b_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},r_{h})

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\rangle-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(m_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})+a_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}b^{K}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},p)-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}\int_{e}(p\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}p(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}\int_{e}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}}}\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{n}^{t}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}}}\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}))(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s.

The boundary conditions for continuous solution
\boldsymbol{u}
(
2.3
)-(
2.4
) and the continuous formulation (
2.5
) implies

\displaystyle E_{c}(\boldsymbol{u},p)

\displaystyle=\Big(\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\rangle-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\Big)+\Big(m(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})+a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},p)-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}\int_{e}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{n}^{t}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{n})(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(m_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})+a_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}b^{K}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},p)-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}\int_{e}(p\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}p(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}\int_{e}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}}}\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{n}^{t}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}}}\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}))(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\Big(\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\rangle-(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\Big)+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(m^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-m_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(a^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-a_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}\int_{e}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}})\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{n}^{t}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}})\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}))(\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=:\sum_{j=1}^{5}T_{j}.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with
C_{\mathbb{K}}
is a constant depending on the bound
\|\mathbb{K}\|_{\infty,\Omega}
gives

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathbb{K}}\Big(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|(I-\Pi^{0,k-1}_{K})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,K}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|\mathbb{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathbb{K}}h^{k}|\boldsymbol{f}|_{k-1,\Omega}\|\mathbb{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Referring to
[
38
]
for the estimates of the term
T_{2}
as

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(m^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-m_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big((\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})_{0,K}-(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\Pi^{0,k}_{K}\boldsymbol{u},\Pi^{0,k}_{K}\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-S^{0}_{K}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u},(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}),(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{w}_{h})_{0,K}-((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u},(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}))_{0,K}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u},\mathbb{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{w}_{h})_{0,K}-S^{0}_{K}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u},(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathbb{K}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Big(\|(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u})\|_{0,K}\|(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{0,K}+\|(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,K}\|(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\|\mathbb{K}^{-1}\|_{\infty,K}\|(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,K}\|(\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{0,k}_{K})\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{0,K}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathbb{K}}h^{\min\{r,k\}}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}\|\mathbb{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Use of polynomial approximation Lemma
5.1
and polynomial consistency leads to

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big(a^{K}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})-a_{h}^{K}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi},\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\nu\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\pi})\|_{0,K}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\nu\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\|_{0,K}^{2}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\min\{r,k\}}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}\,\nu\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{w}_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}.

Combination of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
4.3
gives

\displaystyle T_{4}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{D}}h_{e}^{-1/2}\|\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}})\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}\|_{0,e}h_{e}^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{0,e}

\displaystyle\leq\nu C_{\text{tr}}\Big(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K})\boldsymbol{u})\|_{0,K}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{1/2,h,D}

\displaystyle\leq\nu C_{\text{tr}}h^{\min\{r,k\}}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{1/2,h,D}.

In similar manner asthebound for term
T_{4}
, there holds

\displaystyle T_{5}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}h_{e}^{-1/2}\|\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}})\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}\|_{0,e}h_{e}^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\|_{0,e}

\displaystyle\leq\nu C_{\text{tr}}\Big(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}((\mathbf{I}-\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}})\boldsymbol{u})\|_{0,K_{e}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{1/2,h,N}

\displaystyle\leq\nu C_{\text{tr}}h^{\min\{r,k\}}|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\|_{1/2,h,N}.

The use of the estimates of
T_{1}
–
T_{5}
and the continuity of discrete form arrive to

\displaystyle C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)-(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}+C_{\mathbb{K}}h^{\min\{k,r\}}\Big({|\boldsymbol{f}|_{\max\{r,k\}-1,\Omega}}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}.

The triangle inequality along with the previous bound for
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}
yield

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)-(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)-(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}+C\,h^{\min\{k,r\}}\Big({|\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}_{\max\{r,k\}-1,\Omega}}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Taking
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}=\boldsymbol{u}_{I}
,
q_{h}|_{K}=\Pi^{0,k-1}_{K}p
followed with the use of Lemma
5.2
and regularity estimate (
5.1
) then we have the estimates

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)-(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{h}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\min\{k,r\}}\Big({|\boldsymbol{f}|^{2}_{\max\{r,k\}-1,\Omega}}+|\boldsymbol{u}|_{1+r,\Omega}^{2}+|p|_{r,\Omega}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle{\leq Ch^{\min\{r,k\}}(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\max\{r,k\}-1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{r+\frac{1}{2},\Gamma_{D}})},

where
C
is independent of
\nu
and
h
. The proof is complete.

Remark 5.1

The global inf-sup condition for the continuous Taylor-Hood virtual element spaces cannot be followed in the same manner due to the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}
is a piecewise discontinuous polynomial space of degree
k-1
while
Q_{h}^{k}
is continuous polynomial space of degree
k-1
, and hence the error analysis for these spaces do not follow from the previous theorem and requires a different treatment though the coercivity on the kernel of
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

6
Numerical results

This section is devoted to explore different numerical experiments to verify the performance and robustness of the proposed method. The numerical approach involves the utilization of the Dune-Fem library
[
24
]
, specifically the Dune-Vem module
[
23
]
for generating the computational spaces
\mathbf{V}_{h}^{k}
and
Q_{h}^{k}
. We resort to the
DivfreeSpace
+
\mathbb{P}_{0}
on polygonal meshes to construct these spaces. Then, similar to
[
9
]
, a more accurate pressure is recovered by an element-wise post-processing procedure. For completeness, we represent the pressure surface plots using the
\mathbb{P}_{0}
discrete approximation.

In order to tackle the resulting sparse linear system
\boldsymbol{Ax}=\boldsymbol{F}
, arising from the discretization process, we employ the sparse linear solver
spsolve
from the Scipy library
[
44
]
with UMFPACK .
A critical parameter in our simulations is the Nitsche parameter, which is chosen to satisfy
\gamma_{D}=\gamma_{N}=\gamma
, with
\gamma\geq 100(k+1)^{2}
, where
k
represents the order of the numerical scheme employed (see also
[
13
]
).

We denote by
N
the number of elements in the mesh. To study the rate of convergence, we use the relations
\mathcal{O}(h)\approx\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
. By
e(\cdot)
we denote the error associated with the quantity
\cdot
in its natural norm, and will denote by
h_{i}
the mesh size corresponding to a refinement level
i
. The experimental convergence order is computed as

r(\cdot)=\frac{\log(e_{i}(\cdot))-\log(e_{i+1}(\cdot))}{\log(h_{i})-\log(h_{i+1})}\approx\frac{\log(e_{i}(\cdot))-\log(e_{i+1}(\cdot))}{-2(\log(N_{i})-\log(N_{i+1}))},

where
N_{i}
and
N_{i+1}
are two consecutive measures of the number of elements for the refinements
h_{i}
and
h_{i+1}
, respectively.

When evaluating the performance of our numerical scheme, we explore different geometric configurations, thereby assessing its robustness and versatility. Triangle and quad meshes are generated using the Dune mesher or the GMSH software
[
31
]
through the Pygmsh interface
[
40
]
. Voronoi and other polygonal meshes are constructed using Polymesher
[
41
]
and Matlab, respectively.

For the experiments we consider that the domain is partitioned considering the following types of meshes:

•

\mathcal{T}_{1,h}:=
A mesh using triangles

•

\mathcal{T}_{2,h}:=
A mesh with quadrilaterals

•

\mathcal{T}_{3,h}:=
A mesh with a Voronoi tessellation

•

\mathcal{T}_{4,h}:=
A mesh with non-convex polygons

Other mesh types were considered and the results were similar to those presented below.

6.3
Applications with several boundary conditions

This experiment aims to test the behavior of our method in applications where several geometric conditions are assumed. Let
\boldsymbol{g}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{2}
be two vector fields such that

\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\qquad(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{N}.

Then, the discrete right-hand sides forms with additional Nitsche terms are given as follow (cf. Section
3.1
).

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle:=F_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\gamma_{N}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}h_{e}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{n}^{t}(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}({\Pi^{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},k}_{K_{e}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\boldsymbol{n}))(\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}(\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{t})(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t})\,\mathrm{d}s

\widetilde{G}(q_{h}):=G(q_{h})+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{N}}\int_{e}\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot(q_{h}\boldsymbol{n})\,\mathrm{d}s.

We divide the test in three cases, that we detail below.

6.3.1
Flow past cylinder

We first focuse on the simulation of a cross-flow around a cylinder between two paralel plates. This phenomena can be characterized in a 2D model, for which we take
R=0.05
as the radius of the cylinder, and the length and height of the channel are given by
L=0.82
and
H=0.41
, respectively. The resulting domain is of the form

\Omega:=(0,L)\times(0,H)\backslash D_{R}(x_{c},y_{c}),

where
D_{R}
is the disk of radius
R
and centered at
(x_{c},y_{c})=(0.2,0.2)
. We divide the boundaries as
\partial\Omega:=\Gamma_{\text{in}}\cup\Gamma_{\text{wall}}\cup\Gamma_{\text{circ}}\cup\Gamma_{\text{out}}
, corresponding to the inlet, walls, inner circle and outlet boundary, respectively. Each of these subdomains have the following data.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{w}\cup\Gamma_{\text{circ}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\quad(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\quad\text{ with }\boldsymbol{g}_{1}(x,y)=\left(\frac{6u_{\max}y(H-y)}{H^{2}},0\right)^{\texttt{t}},\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{in}},

\displaystyle(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{out}},

with
u_{\max}=2
. The viscosity is taken to be
\nu=1
. Note that
\boldsymbol{g}_{1}
corresponds to a Poiseuille flow and the condition over
\Gamma_{\text{out}}
is a free flow boundary condition, which is beyond the scope of the proposed theory.

We have depicted the computed velocity data in Figure
6.4
. Here, we observe that the boundary conditions are applied correctly, having no-slip around the inner circle and the walls. Also, by comparing the plots of
\boldsymbol{u}_{1,h}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2,h}
in the outflow we observe that is similar to that of Stokes and Navier-stokes on a channel that ends at
\Gamma_{\text{out}}
. To observe the behavior at the inlet, we have also plotted the velocity profiles in Figure
6.5
. Here, we observe that the parabolic and zero-tangential stress profiles are properly imposed. We finish this part of the experiment showing the surface plot of the pressure drop on two different meshes in Figure
6.6
.

6.3.2
Flow into a backwards facing step

A classical benchmark test for Stokes, Navier-Stokes or Brinkman equations is the backward facing step. Let us consider
H:=1
and a channel given by

\Omega:=(0,9H)\times(0,2H)\backslash\{(0,2H)\times(0,H)\},

where we have a backward facing step in
(2H,H)
. We consider as the inlet region the left boundary, where we weakly impose

\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\quad(\nu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{t},\quad\text{with }\quad\boldsymbol{g}_{1}(x,y)=\left(u_{\max}-0.5(y-1.5H)^{2},0\right)^{\texttt{t}}.

As before, the inflow consist of a Poiseuille type flow, with
u_{\max}=0.125
. In the outlet (right boundary), we impose a zero stress boundary condition
(\nu\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and no-slip boundary conditions on the rest of
\partial\Omega
. The viscosity is taken to be
\nu=1
. The experiment was carried in two different Voronoi meshes: one with 4096 elements, and the other with 16384. The behavior for both meshes where similar, and we present the results with the coarsest mesh. The physical parameters were taken as
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{I}
and
\nu=1
. The approximated velocity field and pressure are presented in Figure
6.7
. Here, we have some well-known phenomena that include singularities at the reentrant corner and recirculation zones. The negative pressure values is valid due to the incompressible formulation of the problem, which allows to write the variable
p
in terms of the gauge pressure. To observe the weak imposition of the boundary conditions at the inlet, we depict the velocity components in Figure
6.8
, where we have a parabolic behavior of the first velocity component together with the tangential stress provided by the Poiseuille flow.

6.3.3
The lid-driven cavity flow

We end this section by performing the classical lid-driven cavity test where we model the steady flow of an immiscible fluid in a box. We consider the unit square domain
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
. The viscosity is taken to be
\nu=10^{-3}
. The test is initially done with
\mathbb{K}=\kappa\mathbb{I}
, with
\kappa=10^{8}
. We take
\Gamma_{N}=\emptyset
and
\Gamma_{D}=\Gamma_{\text{wall}}\cup\Gamma_{\text{lid}}
, where
\Gamma_{\text{wall}}
corresponds to the bottom, left and right boundarires, and
\Gamma_{\text{lid}}
is the top boundary. We set
\boldsymbol{u}|_{\Gamma_{\text{lid}}}=(1,0)^{\texttt{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{u}|_{\Gamma_{\text{wall}}}=\boldsymbol{0}
. We note that this type of boundary condition is also not covered by the theory.

The approximate velocities and pressure (displayed in Figure
6.9
) remain stable and corner singularities are clearly observed. Moreover, to assess the robustness of the method with respect to the choice of
\mathbb{K}
, we run several tests varying
\mathbb{K}
and keeping
\nu=10^{-3}
. Plots of the streamlines of each case are presented on Figure
6.10
, where we confirm the stability of the approximation for each choice of
\mathbb{K}
.
