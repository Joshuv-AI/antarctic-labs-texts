Twin Vertices in Fault-Tolerant Metric Sets and Fault-Tolerant Metric Dimension of Multistage Interconnection Networks
S. Prabhu, V. Manimozhi, M. Arulperumjothi, Sandi Klavžar

Abstract
A set of vertices S ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) S\subseteq V(G) is a resolving set of a graph G G if for each x , y ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) x,y\in V(G) there is a vertex u ∈ S u\in S such that d ⁡ ( x , u ) ≠ d ⁡ ( y , u ) d(x,u)\neq d(y,u) . A resolving set S S is a fault-tolerant resolving set if S ∖ { x } S\setminus\{x\} is a resolving set for every x ∈ S x\in S . The fault-tolerant metric dimension (FTMD) β ′ ​ ( G ) \beta^{\prime}(G) of G G is the minimum cardinality of a fault-tolerant resolving set. It is shown that each twin vertex of G G belongs to every fault-tolerant resolving set of G G . As a consequence, β ′ ​ ( G ) = n ​ ( G ) \beta^{\prime}(G)=n(G) iff each vertex of G G is a twin vertex, which corrects a wrong characterization of graphs G G with β ′ ​ ( G ) = n ​ ( G ) \beta^{\prime}(G)=n(G) from [Mathematics 7(1) (2019) 78]. This FTMD problem is reinvestigated for Butterfly networks, Benes networks, and silicate networks. This extends partial results from [IEEE Access 8 (2020) 145435–145445], and at the same time, disproves related conjectures from the same paper.

a,b\in V(G)
there is a

[

Chartrand and Zhang in

The idea arose from the assumption that a faulty sensor will not cause device failure because the remaining sensors will still be able to deal with the invasion force. It was formally introduced in
[
19
]
. A resolving set
F

is a resolving set of

d(u,x)\neq d(u,y)
and

\beta^{\prime}(G)

Twin vertices

The
open neighborhood
of a vertex
u\in V(G)
is
N(u)=\{v\in V(G):uv\in E(G)\}
, the
closed neighborhood
of
u
is
N[u]=N(u)\cup\{u\}
. Different vertices
u,v\in V(G)
are
twins
if either
N[u]=N[v]
or
N(u)=N(v)
. We further say that
u
is a
twin vertex
, if there exists
v\neq u
such that
u
and
v
are twins. Note that if
N[u]=N[v]
, then
uv\in E(G)
, and if
N(u)=N(v)
, then
uv\notin E(G)
. Twins are a very natural concept, hence no wonder that they were differently named in the literature. For instance, many authors say that
u
and
v
are
true twins
or
adjacent twins
when
N[u]=N[v]
holds, and
false twins
or
non-adjacent twins
when
N(u)=N(v)
, cf.
[
3
,
26
]
.

The role of twins for the metric dimension has been clarified in
[
18
]
. For the fault-tolerant metric dimension, we have the following observation.

Lemma 2.1

If
u
is a twin vertex of a graph
G
, and
S
is a fault-tolerant resolving set of
G
, then
u\in S
. In particular, if
S
is the set of twin vertices of
G
, then
\beta^{\prime}(G)\geq|S|
.

Proof.
Let
u
be a twin vertex of
G
and let
v\neq u
be a vertex such that
u
and
v
are twins. Let
S
is a fault-tolerant resolving set of
G
. Since
d(u,x)=d(v,x)
for every
x\neq u,v
(cf.
[
18
, Lemma 2.3]
), and since
S
is a resolving set, at least one of
u
and
v
belongs to
S
. If
u\in S
there is nothing to prove. Assume hence that
v\in S
. Since
S
is a fault-tolerant resolving set, the set
S\setminus\{v\}
is a resolving set. If
u\notin S
, then the twins
u
and
v
have the same distance to all the vertices of the resolving set
S\setminus\{v\}
, a contradiction.

\square

Lemma
2.1
will be very useful in our consideration of interconnection networks in the next section. Before that, we point to a couple of errors from
[
35
]
.

Chartrand, Eroh, Johnson, and Oellermann
[
7
]
proved that (i)
\beta(G)=n(G)-1
iff
G
is complete; and that (ii) if
n(G)\geq 4
, then
\beta(G)=n(G)-2\Leftrightarrow

G
belongs to one of the following graph classes
K_{s}+\overline{K_{t}}

(s\geq 1,t\geq 2)
,
K_{s,t}

(s,t\geq 1)
, and
K_{s}+(K_{1}\cup K_{t})

(s,t\geq 1)
. Parallel results to (i) and (ii) were erroneously claimed in
[
35
, Theorem 7]
and
[
35
, Theorem 8]
, respectively. In particular, in the first of these results the authors claim that
\beta^{\prime}(G)=n(G)\Leftrightarrow G
is complete. The correct result reads as follows.

Proposition 2.2

Let
G
be a connected graph. Then
\beta^{\prime}(G)=n(G)
if and only if each vertex of
G
is a twin vertex.

Proof.
If each vertex of
G
is a twin vertex, then
\beta^{\prime}(G)=n(G)
by Lemma
2.1
.

Conversely, suppose that
u\in V(G)
is not a twin vertex. Then we claim that
V(G)\setminus\{u\}
is a fault-tolerant resolving set. To show it, we only need to consider a pair of vertices
u
and
x
, where
x
is an arbitrary vertex from
V(G)\setminus\{u\}
. Clearly,
0=d(x,x)<d(u,x)
. In addition, since
u
and
x
are not twins, there exits a vertex
y
adjacent to exactly one of
u
and
x
. Hence
d(u,y)\neq d(x,y)
. We conclude that if
G
contains a vertex which is not a twin, then
\beta^{\prime}(G)<n(G)
.

\square

The graphs from Proposition
2.2
can be described as follows. Its vertex set can be divided into disjoint parts of the order at least two, each part containing vertices that are pairwise twins. Moreover, each of these parts induces either a full graph or a null graph. Between each pair of these parts, there are either all possible edges or none at all.

As mention in the introduction, the fault-tolerant metric dimension is just the
2
-metric dimension, and the two results of this section were actually already established in
[
11
]
. In particular, our Proposition
2.2
is
[
11
, Corollary 5]
. We have nevertheless derived these results also here from the following reasons. First, in this way we are self-sufficient. Second, the arguments are fairly simple. And finally,
[
11
, Corollary 5]
is deduced from
[
11
, Proposition 4]
, where the latter result is stated in a slightly different and more involved language.

[
24
]
. Here, we determine the fault-tolerant metric dimension for two classes of such networks—butterfly networks and Benes networks. These networks were presented by Manuel et al.
[
29
]
, and investigated from differents angles, see
[
29
]
for their metric dimension,
[
20
]
for their degree-based topological indices,
[

]
for their Wiener index,
[

]
for their Zagreb indices and polynomials, and
[
30
]
for their crossing number.

For
r\geq 3
, the
r
dimensional
butterfly network

BF(r)
is defined as follows. Its vertices are pairs
[s,j]
, where
s
runs over all
r
-bit binary strings, and
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,r\}
. The vertices
[s,j]
and
[s^{\prime},j^{\prime}]
are adjacent iff
|j-j^{\prime}|=1
, and either
s=s^{\prime}
or
s
and
s^{\prime}
differ precisely in the
j^{\rm th}
bit. Note that the order and the size of
BF(r)
are
2^{r}(r+1)
and
r2^{r+1}
, respectively. In the
normal representation
of
BF(r)
, the first coordinate of the vertex is interpreted as the row of the vertex and its second coordinate is a column called
level
of the vertex. See Fig.
1
where
BF(3)
is drawn.

Figure 1:
Normal representation of
BF(3)

In
[
17
, Corollary 5.3]
it was proved that
\beta^{\prime}(BF(r))\leq 4\cdot 2^{r}
and conjectured
[
17
, Conjecture 5.4]
that the equality holds here, that is,
\beta^{\prime}(BF(r))=4\cdot 2^{r}
. In our next result we determine
\beta^{\prime}(BF(r))
which disproves the conjecture.

Theorem 3.1

If
r\geq 3
, then
\beta^{\prime}(BF(r))=2^{r+1}
.

Proof.
From the definition of
BF(r)
we infer that the vertices
[i-1,0]
and
[i-1+2^{r-1},0]
are twins for each
i\in[2^{r-1}]
. (We use the convention
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.) Moreover, for each
i\in[2^{r-1}]
, the vertices
[2i-2,r]
and
[2i-1,r]
are also twins. That is, each vertex from the first and the last level of
BF(r)
is a twin vertex. Hence
BF(r)
contains (at least)
2^{r+1}
twin vertices, therefore
\beta^{\prime}(BF(r))\geq 2^{r+1}
by Lemma
2.1
.

To prove that
\beta^{\prime}(BF(r))\leq 2^{r+1}
, we are going to show that the set
X
consisting of the vertices from the first and the last level of
BF(r)
forms a fault-tolerant resolving set. For this sake, let
[s,i]
and
[t,j]
be arbitrary vertices of
BF(r)
. We need to show that they are distance distinguished by two vertices of
X
. If at least one of
[s,i]
and
[t,j]
lies in
X
, then the conclusion is clear, hence we may assume in the rest that
i,j\in[r-1]
. Suppose first that
i\neq j
and assume w.l.g that
i<j
. Then
d([s,i],[s,0])<d([t,j],[s,0])
and
d([t,j],[t,r])<d([s,i],[t,r])
. As
[s,0],[t,r]\in X
, we have required two vertices in this case.

It remains to consider vertices
[s,i]
and
[t,i]
, where
i\in[r-1]
and
s\neq t
. Let
s=s_{1}\ldots s_{r}
and
t=t_{1}\ldots t_{r}
. The following facts will be used in subsequent lines. If
x=x_{1}\ldots x_{r}
and
d([x,0],[s,i])=i
, then
x_{i+1}=s_{i+1},\ldots,x_{r}=s_{r}
. Similarly, if
d([x,r],[s,i])=r-i
, then
x_{1}=s_{1},\ldots,x_{i}=s_{i}
. The binary labels
s
and
t
are different, hence they differ in at least one coordinate. Assume first that there exists an index
k>i
such that
s_{k}\neq t_{k}
. Then we infer that
d([s,0],[s,i])=i<d([s,0],[t,i])
and that
d([t,0],[t,i])=i<d([t,0],[s,i])
. Hence the vertices
[s,0],[t,0]\in X
distinguish
[s,i]
and
[t,j]
. In the second case we may thus assume that
s_{i+1}=t_{i+1},\ldots,s_{r}=t_{r}
. Let
k
be an index such that
s_{k}\neq t_{k}
. By the case assumption,
k\leq i
. Consider now the vertices
[s,r]
and
[t,r]
. Suppose that
d([s,r],[s,i])=r-i=d([s,r],[t,j])
. Then, by the above fact,
t_{1}=s_{1},\ldots,t_{i}=s_{i}
. But this means that
t=s
, a contradiction. It follows that
d([s,r],[s,i])=r-i<d([s,r],[t,i])
. Similarly we obtain that
d([t,r],[t,i])=r-i<d([t,r],[s,i])
. Hence in this case the vertices
[s,r]
and
[t,r]
from
X
distinguish
[s,i]
and
[t,j]
and we are done.

\square

r\geq 3
, the
r
-dimensional
Benes network

is defined similarly as
BF(r)
. The vertices are again ordered pairs
[s,i]
, where
s
runs over all
r
-bit binary strings, but now
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2r\}
.
The edges in
B(r)
up to level
r
are just as in
BF(r)
, while later on, the edges are vertically reflected. The formal definition should be clear from Fig.
2

is shown in its normal representation.

B(3)

The order and the size of
B(r)
are
2^{r}(2r+1)
and
r2^{r+2}
, respectively. In
[
17
, Corollary 4.3]
it was proved that
\beta^{\prime}(B(r))\leq 13\cdot 2^{r-1}
and conjectured
[
17
, Conjecture 4.4]
that the equality holds here, that is,
\beta^{\prime}(B(r))=13\cdot 2^{r-1}
. In our next result we determine
\beta^{\prime}(B(r))
which disproves the conjecture.

Theorem 3.2

If
r\geq 3
, then
\beta^{\prime}(B(r))=3\cdot 2^{r}
.

Proof.
Following the arguments adopted in Theorem
3.1
, we observe first that each vertex from the first, the middle, and the last level of
B(r)
is a twin vertex. Hence
B(r)
contains (at least)
3\cdot 2^{r}
twins, therefore
\beta^{\prime}(B(r))\geq 3\cdot 2^{r}
by Lemma
2.1
.

To prove that
\beta^{\prime}(B(r))\leq 3\cdot 2^{r}
, we claim that the set
X
consisting of the vertices from the first, the middle, and the last level of
B(r)
forms a fault-tolerant resolving set. To show it we take arbitrary vertices
[s,i]
and
[t,j]
of
B(r)
. If at least one of them is in
X
, then two vertices from
X
clearly distinguish them. Hence we may assume
i,j\in[2r-1]
. If
i\neq j
, where
i<j
, then
d([s,i],[s,0])<d([t,j],[s,0])
and
d([t,j],[t,2r])<d([s,i],[t,2r])
. Hence it remains to consider vertices
[s,i]
and
[t,i]
, where
i\in[2r-1]
and
s\neq t
. If
i\in[r-1]
, then we proceed analogously as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, hence we omit the details. And if
i\in\{r+1,\ldots,2r-1\}
, then by the vertical symmetry of
B(r)
over the level
r
(with respect to the normal representation of
B(r)
) we get the required conclusion is an analogous way.

\square

4

From a synthetic perspective, the tetrahedron’s focal hub represents the silicon particle, while the corner ions speak to the oxygen atoms, while the corner vertices represent oxygen atoms. We get distinct silicate structures by arranging different ways of tetrahedra. Papers

[

]
and honeycomb
[

]
networks used in computer science networks
[

]
. This is achieved by suggesting an addressing scheme to the vertices of the silicate networks as suggested for hexagonal in
[
13
]
and honeycomb
[

]
.

The metric dimension of the silicate networks
SL(n)
was determined in 2011 as follows.

Theorem 3.3

[
32
]

If
n\geq 2
, then
\beta(SL(n))=6n
.

For the FTMD of silicate networks it was proved in
[

that

6n+1\leq\beta^{\prime}(SL(n))\leq 21n\,.

In our last result, we give an exact formula for FTMD of silicate networks.

Theorem 3.4

If
n\geq 2
, then
\beta^{\prime}(SL(n))=12n
.

Proof.
SL(n)
contains
6n
pairs of twin vertices, as shown in Fig
3
for
SL(2)
. Hence
SL(n)
contains
12n
twin vertices and thus
\beta^{\prime}(SL(n))\geq 12n
by Lemma
2.1
. To prove the reverse inequality, we claim that these
12n
vertices form a fault-tolerant resolving set. Instead of proving it directly, we recall from the proof of
[
32
, Theorem 5]
that the set of boundary vertices (the
6n
outermost vertices in the standard drawing of
SL(n)
, cf. Fig.
3
) of
SL(n)
is a (minimum) resolving set of
SL(n)
. But then it straightforwardly follows that these boundary
6n
vertices, together with their respective twins, form a (minimum) fault-tolerant resolving set.

\square
