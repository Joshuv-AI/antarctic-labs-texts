Abstract
. The finite-rate-of-innovation (FRI) framework which corresponds a signal/image to a structured low-rank matrix is emerging as an alternative to the traditional sparse regularization. This is because such an off-the-grid approach is able to alleviate the basis mismatch between the true support in the continuous domain and the discrete grid. In this paper, we propose a two-stage off-the-grid regularization model for the image restoration. Given that the discontinuities/edges of the image lie in the zero level set of a band-limited periodic function, we can derive that the Fourier samples of the gradient of the image satisfy an annihilation relation, resulting in a low-rank two-fold Hankel matrix. In addition, since the singular value decomposition of a low-rank Hankel matrix corresponds to an adaptive tight frame system which can represent the image with sparse canonical coefficients, our approach consists of the following two stages. The first stage learns the tight wavelet frame system from a given measurement, and the second stage restores the image via the analysis approach based sparse regularization. The numerical results are presented to demonstrate that the proposed approach is compared favorably against several popular discrete regularization approaches and structured low-rank matrix approaches.

1.
Introduction

Image restoration, including image denoising, deblurring, inpainting, etc., is one of the most fundamental processes in imaging sciences with a wide range of applications. Image restoration aims to obtain an image of high-quality from a given measurement which is degraded during the process of imaging, acquisition, communication, etc. Mathematically, it is in general modeled by the following linear inverse problem:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}

(1)

where
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is the degraded measurement or the observed image,
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is a certain additive noise or a measurement error, and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}
is some linear operator which takes different forms depending on the specific image restoration problems.

Since the operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}
is in general ill-conditioned or non-invertible, a proper regularization on the images to be recovered is needed to obtain a high quality recovery from the ill-posed linear inverse problem
Eq.

1
. One of the most successful example is the sparse regularization based on the following
\ell_{1}
norm minimization

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}~\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(2)

In
Eq.

2
, we design a linear transform
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
so that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}
is close to zero in the smooth region. Since the inherent thresholding to solve
Eq.

2
is connected with the nonlinear evolution PDEs
[
23
]
, we can say that
Eq.

2
aims to maintain the sharpness of image singularities while regularizing smooth regions at the same time.

Meanwhile, if we know the exact locations of image singularities as oracle, we can restore the image
\boldsymbol{u}
with sharp edges by solving the following least squares problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}~\lambda\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}\right)_{{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(3)

which can be understood as the variational formulation of

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}~~~\text{subject to}~~\big(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}\big)_{{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{c}}={\mathbf{0}}.

In
Eq.

3
,
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
is the set of indices corresponding to the image singularities;
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}
is close to zero on
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{c}
. Therefore, as long as
|{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{c}|
(the cardinality of
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{c}
) is sufficiently large,
Eq.

3
is well-posed, and the image singularities will be well kept in the restored image
[
38
]
.

Even though both
Eqs.

2
and
3
are able to restore an image with sharp edges, the major challenge lies in how to estimate
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}
as accurately as possible. In many image restoration tasks, the degraded measurements are available only, which makes it difficult to obtain the exact information on the image singularities. Most importantly, in many practical applications, the true singularities can lie in the continuous domain or at least may not be exactly aligned with the discrete grid, and the discretization will lead to the
basis mismatch

[
21
,
48
,
66
]
between the true singularities and the discrete image grid. Such a basis mismatch can distort information on the image singularities, and thus can yield the performance bottleneck
[
66
]
.

In contrast to the conventional discrete sparse regularization, the continuous domain regularization exploits the sparsity prior in continuous domain, thereby alleviating the basis mismatch
[
45
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this “off-the-grid” approach stems from the Prony’s method
[
53
]
which corresponds a superposition of a few sinusoids to a structured low-rank matrix for the Dirac stream retrieval with unknown knots. Hence, we can adopt the so-called
structured low-rank matrix (SLRM) approach

[
20
,
49
,
65
]
for the restoration of a superposition of a few sinusoids whose Fourier transform (or inverse transform) is a Dirac stream
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, together with the subspace method for the support retrieval
[
60
]
. In addition to the spectrally sparse signals, the SLRM can also be used to restore Fourier samples of a one dimensional piecewise constant signal
[
6
,
62
]
as in this case the Fourier samples of a derivative becomes the superposition of a few sinusoids. Finally, the works in
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
50
]
have successfully extended the SLRM to the two dimensional images under a mild assumption on the image singularity curves to overcome the nonisolated image singularity curves in the two dimensional space. In addition, such an assumption on the curves has also enabled the estimation of the image singularity curves via the subspace method
[
48
]
.

In this paper, we propose a two-stage continuous domain regularization approach for the piecewise constant image restoration. Our two-stage approach follows the previous works
[
12
,
48
]
which correspond the Fourier samples of gradient to the structured low-rank matrices. Here we note that the SVD of a Hankel matrix induces tight frame filter banks due to its underlying convolutional structure. In other words, if we can associate a signal/image with a low-rank Hankel matrix, its right singular vectors form tight frame filter banks under which the canonical coefficients have a group sparsity according to the index of filters
[
12
, Theorem 3.2]
. Further motivated by the annihilating subspace method in
[
48
]
and the data-driven tight frame in
[
14
]
, we first construct the tight frame filter banks from the low frequency Fourier samples of a degraded measurement. Once we have constructed the tight frame filter banks, we adopt a
sparse regularization
via the wavelet frame analysis approach
[
15
]
as an image restoration model via the continuous domain regularization. Even though the wavelet frame based analysis (e.g.
[
13
]
) approach has been widely used for the sparse approximation of a discrete image, our analysis approach comes from the relaxation of the structured low-rank matrices generated by the Fourier samples for the continuous domain regularization.

1.1.
Overviews of related works

Our two-stage approach for the piecewise constant image restoration is closely related to the recent SLRM framework for the two dimensional functions in
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
50
]
. Briefly speaking, if the singularity curves of a piecewise constant/holomorphic function, i.e. the supports of the first order (real/complex) derivatives of a target image, lie in the zero level set of a band-limited function called the
annihilating polynomial
, the Fourier transform of the derivatives can be annihilated by the convolution with the annihilating filter. This annihilation relation in turn corresponds the Fourier samples of derivatives to the structured low-rank matrices. Based on this framework, the continuous domain regularization for the piecewise constant image restoration is proposed and studied in
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
, together with a restoration guarantee
[
45
]
.

Apart from the signal/image restoration, the SLRM framework can also be applied to the source/support estimation from a few (contagious) discrete samples. In the pioneer work
[
53
]
, Prony’s method retrieves the point sources of a Dirac stream from the roots of the annihilating polynomial. Since the Prony’s method is numerically unstable in spite of the theoretical guarantee in the noiseless setting
[
32
]
, several annihilating subspace methods have been proposed as stable alternatives in the literature. Most widely used methods include MUSIC
[
60
]
, ESPIRIT
[
57
]
, and the matrix pencil
[
36
]
. In the two dimensional piecewise constant image setting, the similar method has been introduced under the assumption that the singularities lie in the zero set of an annihilating polynomial. Using the tool of algebraic geometries, the authors in
[
48
]
present the necessary and the sufficient conditions on the low frequency samples for the singularity estimation. Based on these conditions, the subspace method for the singularity estimation is further introduced in
[
48
]
, which can be viewed as a two dimensional parallel of MUSIC algorithm.

Based on the SLRM framework and the corresponding subspace method, a similar two-stage approach for the piecewise constant image restoration is already presented in
[
48
]
. More precisely, the authors in
[
48
]
introduces a Cadzow denoising (e.g.
[
9
]
) for the annihilating subspace estimation/the tight frame filter banks construction, and a least squares linear prediction (LSLP) model penalizing the
\ell_{2}
norm of the canonical coefficients corresponding to annihilating subspaces for the image restoration. While the Cadzow denoising in
[
48
]
computes the SVD of a two-fold Hankel matrix at each iteration, our tight frame filter bank construction adopts the data-driven tight frame construction in
[
14
]
which computes the SVD of a smaller matrix. In addition, compared to the LSLP model in
[
48
]
, our restoration model is less sensitive to the rank of the Hankel matrix, as we implicitly exploit the annihilating property via the sparsity promoting
\ell_{1}
norm. Finally, since we consider the sparsity in the entire transform domain rather than the group sparsity according to the index of filters, our restoration model is expected to be more robust to the noise/the image modeling.

Finally, a similar wavelet frame relaxation can also be found in the previous work
[
12
]
, where the data-driven tight frame is used for the piecewise constant image restoration via the continuous domain regularization. While the previous data-driven tight frame model in
[
12
]
is a
balanced approach

[
10
,
19
]
which assumes the distance between the canonical coefficients and the sparse coefficients, our proposed model is an
analysis approach

[
15
]
which directly penalizes the sparse canonical coefficients. Notice that it is well known that the analysis approach reflects the structure of a target image better than the synthesis approach and the balanced approach (e.g.
[
13
]
), and the similar arguments can be applied to our case as well; our proposed analysis approach is expected to reflect the SLRM structure than the previous balanced approach in
[
12
]
.

Organization and notation of paper

Section

2
. To be more specific, we begin with the brief review on the structured low-rank matrix framework for the piecewise constant images and the corresponding edge estimation from the annihilating relations. 2.
Proposed image restoration approach

2.1.
Structured low-rank matrix for piecewise constant images

In this section, we briefly review the continuous domain approach for two dimensional images. Interested readers can refer to
[
48
]
and references therein for more detailed surveys.

Throughout this paper, we consider the SLRM framework for the following piecewise constant function

\displaystyle u(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}1_{\Omega_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}),~~~~~~~\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

(6)

from its Fourier samples

\displaystyle\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\mathscr{F}}(u)({\boldsymbol{k}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}u(\boldsymbol{x})e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},~~~~~~{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{O}},

(7)

where the sample grid
{\mathbb{O}}
is defined as
Eq.

4
. In
Eq.

6
,
\alpha_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
1_{\Omega}
denotes the characteristic function on a set
\Omega
:
1_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
if
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
, and
0
otherwise. Without loss of generality, we assume that each
\Omega_{j}
lies in
[-1/2,1/2)^{2}
for simplicity. (However, it is not difficult to generalize the setting into an arbitrary rectangular region
[-L_{1}/2,L_{1}/2)\times[-L_{2}/2,L_{2}/2)
.) We further assume that
Eq.

6
is expressed with the smallest number of characteristic functions such that
\Omega_{j}
’s are pairwise disjoint. Then the discontinuities of
u
agrees with
\Gamma=\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\partial\Omega_{j}
, called the
singularity set
of
u

[
48
]
.

In general, it is difficult to directly establish the SLRM framework without any further information on
\Gamma
. Nevertheless, the authors in
[
48
]
assume that there exists a finite rectangular and symmetric index set
{\mathbb{K}}
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma\subseteq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})=0\right\}~~~~~\text{with}~~~~~\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}}{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}})e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}.

(8)

Following the previous works in
[
11
,
47
,
48
]
, any function
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})
in the form of
Eq.

8
will be called the
trigonometric polynomial
, and the zero level set
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})=0\right\}
the
trigonometric curve
. For a trigonometric polynomial
\varphi
in
Eq.

8
, the degree of
\varphi
is defined as an ordered pair of degrees in each coordinate, and is denoted by
\deg(\varphi)
. In particular, a trigonometric polynomial with the smallest degree is called the
minimal polynomial
. Under this setting, the authors in
[
47
,
48
]
derived that the Fourier transform of
\nabla u=\left(\partial_{1}u,\partial_{2}u\right)
is annihilated by the convolution with the Fourier coefficients
{\boldsymbol{a}}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}\right\}
of
\varphi
. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\ast\widehat{\varphi}\right)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}}{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}){\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=0,~~~~~~{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

(9)

where the convolution acts on
{\mathscr{F}}(\partial_{1}u)
and
{\mathscr{F}}(\partial_{2}u)
separately.

In this sense, we call the trigonometric polynomial
\varphi
in
Eq.

8
the
annihilating polynomial
, and its Fourier coefficient
{\boldsymbol{a}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}\right\}
the
annihilating filter
with support
{\mathbb{K}}
. Using the tool of algebraic geometries, the authors in
[
48
]
proved that there exists the unique minimal polynomial associated with a given trigonometric curve, and that the degree of a trigonometric polynomial bounds the number of connected components (i.e.
J
in
Eq.

6
), from which we can estimate the degrees of freedom of an image to be restored
[
48
]
.

In our setting, the Fourier transform of
u
is sampled on the grid
{\mathbb{O}}
in
Eq.

4
with
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
large enough to guarantee a high image resolution. Hence,
Eq.

9
becomes the finite system of linear equations

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}}{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)({\boldsymbol{l}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}){\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=0,~~~~~~~{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{O}}:{\mathbb{K}}.

(10)

In the matrix-vector multiplication form, we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right){\boldsymbol{a}}={\mathbf{0}}.

(11)

Hence, the two-fold Hankel matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)
has a nontrivial nullspace. Indeed, as long as the filter support
{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}
defining
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)
is sufficiently large,
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)
will have a nontrivial nullspace. To see this, let
\varphi
in
Eq.

8
be the minimal polynomial with the support
{\mathbb{K}}
, and let
{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}
be the assumed filter support strictly containing
{\mathbb{K}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)\right)\leq|{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}|-|{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}:{\mathbb{K}}|,

(12)

which shows that the two-fold Hankel matrix constructed by
{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}
is
rank deficient

[
48
]
. Roughly speaking,
Eq.

12
means that if
\varphi
in
Eq.

8
is the minimal polynomial for the image singularities
\Gamma
with Fourier coefficients
{\boldsymbol{a}}
supported on
{\mathbb{K}}
,
e^{-2\pi i\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})
is also an annihilating polynomial for
\boldsymbol{m}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}:{\mathbb{K}}
, or equivalently,
{\boldsymbol{a}}(\cdot-\boldsymbol{m})
is also an annihilating filter
[
12
]
.

Apart from the low-rank property
Eq.

12
of the two-fold Hankel matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)
, we can also derive the necessary and the sufficient conditions on the number of Fourier samples (i.e. the size of
{\mathbb{O}}
) to guarantee the unique recovery of the singularities from the annihilating relation
Eq.

10
(or
Eq.

11
)
[
48
]
. To be more precise, assume that
u(\boldsymbol{x})
be as in
Eq.

6
with the singularity set
\Gamma
satisfying
Eq.

8
with the minimal polynomial
\varphi
of the degree
(K_{1},K_{2})
. If we can restore
\Gamma
using the samples
{\mathscr{F}}(u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}
on the
N\times N
grid
{\mathbb{O}}
, then it must be

\displaystyle 2\left(N-K_{1}\right)\left(N-K_{2}\right)\geq\left(K_{1}+1\right)\left(K_{2}+1\right)-1,

(13)

which gives the necessary condition on
{\mathbb{O}}
for the estimation of
\Gamma

[
48
, Proposition 3.1]
. Conversely, let
\varphi
in
Eq.

8
be the minimal polynomial with the Fourier coefficients
{\boldsymbol{a}}
supported on
{\mathbb{K}}
, and let
{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}
be a rectangular grid strictly containing
{\mathbb{K}}
. If a filter
{\boldsymbol{b}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}\right\}
on
{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}}{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)({\boldsymbol{l}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}){\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=0,~~~~~~~{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}+{\mathbb{K}},

(14)

there exists a trigonometric polynomial
\eta
with Fourier coefficients supported on
{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}:{\mathbb{K}}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\eta\varphi\right)(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{k}})e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}.

Hence, the trigonometric polynomial corresponding to the filter
{\boldsymbol{b}}
is also an annihilating polynomial, which gives a sufficient condition on the sample grid
{\mathbb{O}}

[
48
, Theorem 3.4]
.

In general, it is challenging to identify the minimal annihilating filter from the nullspace of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)

[
48
]
. Nevertheless, it is still possible to apply the subspace algorithm such as MUSIC
[
60
]
to our setting. For the sake of clarity, let
{\boldsymbol{B}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\boldsymbol{b}}_{1}&\cdots&{\boldsymbol{b}}_{R}\end{array}\right]
be an orthonormal basis for
{\mathscr{N}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)\right)
, and let
\varphi_{m}
be the trigonometric polynomial corresponding to
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{m}
. Then it follows that
\varphi_{m}=\eta_{m}\varphi
for some trigonometric polynomial

. We define the following

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{R}\left|\varphi_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{R}\left|\eta_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(15)

Obviously, we have
\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma
. In addition, it is also proved in
[
48
]
that
\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq 0
almost everywhere in
\Gamma^{c}
, which makes it possible to estimate
\Gamma
from
{\mathscr{N}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)\right)
. Finally,
\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be expressed as the reciprocal of pseudospectra:

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{B}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{2}

(16)

where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}
with
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\prime}
. Note that
Eq.

16
can be understood as a two dimensional parallel to MUSIC. See
[
47
,
48
]
for details.

2.2.
Image restoration model

Let
{\boldsymbol{f}}
be a degraded measurement modeled as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{v}={\mathscr{F}}(u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
with
u
defined as in
Eq.

6
, and
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is some additive noise/measurement error. In what follows, with a slight abuse of notation, we assume the linear operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}
acts on the Fourier samples.

Since the two-fold Hankel matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)\big|_{{\mathbb{O}}}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2M_{1}\times M_{2}}
defined as
Section

1.2
is low-rank, we can consider

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}~\mathrm{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right)~~~~~\text{subject to}~~~~{\boldsymbol{f}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}

(18)

as a continuous domain regularization for the piecewise constant image restoration. In
Eq.

18
,
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}:{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\left(2\pi ik_{1}\boldsymbol{v}({\boldsymbol{k}}),2\pi ik_{2}\boldsymbol{v}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right),~~~~~{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},k_{2})\in{\mathbb{O}},

(19)

which reflects the samples of
{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=2\pi i{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\left(2\pi i\xi_{1}\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),2\pi i\xi_{2}\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)
.

In the literature, there are numerous tractable relaxations of
Eq.

18
, which is an NP-hard problem in general. Some of them include the convex nuclear norm relaxation (e.g.
[
16
,
29
]
), the iterative reweighted least squares (IRLS) for the Schatten
p
-norm minimization
[
31
,
35
,
44
,
49
]
, and the nonconvex alternating projections
[
17
,
18
]
etc. In this paper, considering the SVD of a low-rank Hankel matrix and inspired by the previous work in
[
11
]
for the piecewise smooth image restoration, we propose another relaxation of
Eq.

18
, by presenting another interpretation of
[
12
, Theorem 3.2]
.

To begin with, we let
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M_{2}}
be
K_{1}\times K_{2}
filters supported on
{\mathbb{K}}
, and we introduce

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}

\displaystyle=\big[{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}(-\cdot)}^{T},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}(-\cdot)}^{T},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M_{2}}(-\cdot)}^{T}\big]^{T},

(20)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\big[{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{a}}}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{a}}}_{2}},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{a}}}_{M_{2}}}\big],

(21)

where
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is a discrete convolution under the periodic boundary condition:

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)({\boldsymbol{k}})=\left({\boldsymbol{a}}\ast\boldsymbol{v}\right)({\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}}-\boldsymbol{m})\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{m}).

In other words, both
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{*}
are concatenations of discrete convolutions.

Under this setting, we assume that
\mathrm{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right)=r\ll 2M_{1}\wedge M_{2}
. Considering its full SVD
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)={\boldsymbol{X}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{*}
, we let

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}=M_{2}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(:,m)}

(22)

by reformulating each
{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{(:,m)}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M_{2}}
into a
K_{1}\times K_{2}
filter supported on
{\mathbb{K}}
. Then
[
12
, Theorem 3.2]
tells us that
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
in
Eq.

20
defined by using the filters
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M_{2}}
in
Eq.

22
satisfies

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{2}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{a}}}_{m}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right)={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v},

(23)

and for
m=r+1,\cdots,M_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)({\boldsymbol{k}})={\mathbf{0}},~~~~~{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{O}}:{\mathbb{K}},

(24)

where the discrete convolution
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
acts on each component of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}
.

In other words, if we know the SVD of a two-fold Hankel matrix
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2M_{1}\times M_{2}}
as an oracle, we can explicitly construct tight frame filter banks under which
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}
admits a sparse canonical representation, and the sparsity of canonical coefficients can be grouped according to the index of filters
[
12
]
. Here, motivated by
[
11
]
, we assume that a priori estimation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
is available with the SVD

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{*},

whose detailed method will be discussed in the following subsection. We define the tight frame transform
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
in
Eq.

20
via

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}=M_{2}^{-1/2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{(:,m)},~~~~~m=1,\cdots,M_{2}.

Since then
Eq.

24
holds approximately under this
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
, we remove the group sparsity in the canonical coefficients for the better sparse approximation instead. This leads us to solve

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}~\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right\|_{1}

(25)

where the weighted
\ell_{1}
norm is defined as

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right\|_{1}=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{2}}\gamma_{m}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right\|_{1}

to reflect the different weights related to the singular values. That is,

\displaystyle\gamma_{m}=\frac{\nu}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(m,m)}+\varepsilon}

with some
\nu>0
and a small
\varepsilon>0
to avoid the division by zero.

Note that the similar idea can be found in the previous related works. More precisely, since the filters
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{r+1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M_{2}}
form an orthogonal basis for
{\mathscr{N}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right)
, we can also consider the following
\ell_{2}
minimization model

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}~\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\sum_{m=r+1}^{M_{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(26)

which is the least squares linear prediction (LSLP) model in
[
48
]
to solve the ideal low-pass filter deconvolution. Indeed, if we know the exact right singular vectors
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)={\boldsymbol{X}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{*}
as an oracle, the above LSLR model will be more appropriate from the viewpoint of
Eqs.

23
and
24
, as our model implicitly reflects
Eq.

24
by the
\ell_{1}
norm. However, since we only have the degraded measurement in many practical cases, the challenge lies in how to find the wavelet frame transform
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
(and the rank
r
) satisfying
Eq.

24
as exactly as possible. Nevertheless, since we relax the sparsity of the tight frame coefficients over the entire range of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
, (i.e. not necessarily grouped as in
Eq.

24
), we can expect that our model
Eq.

25
is able to achieve more flexibility, leading to the performance gain in the image restoration.

We mention that
Eqs.

23
and
24
are originally to propose the following data-driven tight frame (DDTF) relaxation

\displaystyle\begin{split}&~~~\min_{\boldsymbol{v},{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}}~\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)-{\boldsymbol{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\right\|_{0}\\
&\text{subject to}~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}},\end{split}

for the compressed sensing MRI restoration via the continuous domain regularization. In

27
,
\ell_{0}
norm
\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\right\|_{0}
encodes the number of nonzero elements in
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. At this moment, we see that our model
Eq.

25
has the following advantages over the data-driven tight frame model
Eq.

27
. First of all, given that
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
is estimated appropriately, we may not require to further learn it at each iteration with additional computational costs. Second, since
Eq.

25
is convex while
Eq.

27
is nonconvex, we can easily expect better behavior and theoretical support for the numerical algorithms. Most importantly, from the viewpoint of wavelet frame based image restoration, the model
Eq.

25
is an
analysis approach

[
15
]
while the data-driven tight frame model
Eq.

27
is (a relaxation of) the
balanced approach

[
10
,
19
]
(See
Appendix

A
for the brief description on the wavelet frame based image restoration models). Note that the analysis approach reflects the structure of a target image better than other approaches under some mild assumptions on the tight frame (e.g.
[
13
]
). Hence, the same arguments can be applied to our case; even though both
Eq.

25
and
Eq.

27
are derived from
Eqs.

23
and
24
, the analysis approach
Eq.

25
reflects the SLRM framework better than the balanced approach
Eq.

27
.

.

In our proposed image restoration model

to construct appropriate tight frame filter banks. However, since what we need is that the filter banks satisfy

24
in
, a proper estimation of the null space of the two-fold Hankel matrix will be sufficient. Indeed, it is proved in

that we can estimate the null space given that a sufficient number of low frequency samples are available. More precisely, let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2M_{1}\times M_{2}}:\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}^{(:,m)}={\mathbf{0}}~~\text{for}~~m=r+1,\cdots,M_{2}\right\}.

(45)

Under this reformulation, the solutions to
Eqs.

43
and
44
have the closed form solutions; namely, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}^{(:,m)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left(\beta\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}^{(:,m)}+\beta_{2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n)}^{(:,m)}\right)/\left(\beta+\beta_{2}\right)&\text{if}~m=1,\cdots,r\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=M_{2}^{-1/2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{V}}}^{*}~~\text{where}~~\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}+\beta_{3}\beta^{-1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{U}}}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{S}}}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{V}}}^{*}~~\text{is the SVD}.

(48)

Therefore, we only compute the SVD of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}+\beta_{3}\beta^{-1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M_{2}\times M_{2}}
, leading to the computational efficiency over the SVD of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{(n+1)}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2\widetilde{M}_{1}\times M_{2}}
required to solving
Eq.

36
.

Notice that, based on the framework of
[
2
,
4
,
7
,
64
]
, we can verify that the sequence generated by
Algorithm

2
globally converges to a stationary point provided that a linear operator
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}}
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}\neq{\mathbf{0}},

(49)

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})=1
if
{\boldsymbol{k}}={\mathbf{0}}
;
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})=0
if
{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
. In words, since
\widehat{1}=\delta
,
Eq.

49
is equivalent to the assumption that every constant does not belong to the nullspace of an operator in the image domain, which is standard in the theoretical analysis of variational problems (e.g.
[
3
]
). In addition,
Eq.

49
holds in many practical cases including the image deblurring and the compressed sensing MRI, as, in particular, the compressed sensing MRI is required to sample
{\mathbf{0}}
frequency to avoid the DC-off set (e.g.
[
42
]
).

For the computational cost, we first mention that we do not explicitly formulate
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{(n+1)}\right)
at each iteration step. To simplify the discussion, we consider
K_{1}=K_{2}=K
, i.e. the square patch, without loss of generality. Then both
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}
are computed by using
4r
two dimensional convolutions/fast Hankel matrix-vector multiplications
[
41
]
directly from
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{(n+1)}
, requiring
O(r\widetilde{N}^{2}\log\widetilde{N})
operations. In addition, we can update
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}^{(:,m)}+\beta_{3}\beta^{-1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}^{(:,m)}
directly after computing
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}}_{(n+1)}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}^{(:,m)}
in a single loop. Hence, together with the SVD of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}+\beta_{3}\beta^{-1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{(n)}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K^{2}\times K^{2}}
requiring
O(K^{6})
operations, we need
O(r\widetilde{N}^{2}\log\widetilde{N}+K^{6})
operations for
Sections

2.3
and
48
, which requires less computational costs than the direct computation of SVD in
Eq.

36
.

For the memory storage, we do not need the full storage of
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2\widetilde{M}_{1}\times M_{2}}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M_{2}\times M_{2}}
either. From
Section

2.3
, both
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{H}}}_{(n+1)}^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}
eventually contain at least
M_{2}-r
zero column vectors. Hence, it suffices to store at most
r
nonzero column vectors for these two matrices. Since
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{(n+1)}
is the largest matrix to be stored,
Algorithm

2
requires
O(r\widetilde{N}^{2})
memory storages.

Finally, we mention that, we obtain low frequency samples
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
by solving
Eq.

37
and set
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
via
Eq.

33
rather than directly setting
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}}
in
Eq.

37
as an output. For the explanation of the reason, we consider the SVD

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{*}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\|}&\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\perp}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\|}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\perp}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{\|}^{*}\\
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{\perp}^{*}\end{array}\right].

In
Section

2.3
, we have
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\|}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2\widetilde{M}_{1}\times r}
,
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\perp}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2\widetilde{M}_{1}\times(M_{2}-r)}
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\|}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\perp}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(M_{2}-r)\times(M_{2}-r)}
,
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{\perp}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M_{2}\times r}
, and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{\perp}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{M_{2}\times(M_{2}-r)}
. If we set

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}=M_{2}^{-1/2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\|}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\|}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{array}\right]:=M_{2}^{-1/2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\|}&\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{\perp}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\|}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{array}\right],

Eq.

41
in
Algorithm

2
(with
\beta_{3}=0
) gives the following SVD

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)^{*}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{C}}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{\|}&\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{\perp}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{2}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\|}^{2}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{array}\right]=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{2}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\|}^{2}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{array}\right]{\boldsymbol{I}}.

This means that
Eqs.

40
and
41
is closely related to the rank
r
approximation of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)
with the right singular vectors and the singular values as outputs. However, the assumption that the underlying image is piecewise constant with edges described by a trigonometric curve is approximately true in practice
[
48
]
. Since the low-rank approximation will strongly force edges to be given as a trigonometric curve in our setting, the use of such truncated singular values will be too restrictive for the practical applications. Hence, we restore low frequency samples
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
from
Eq.

37
for the sake of flexibility.

Hence, the similar arguments are still available in our case; the continuous domain regularization is able to reduce the basis mismatch between the true edge set in the continuum and the discrete grid, leading to the improvements in both indices and visual qualities. Indeed, even though the on-the-grid sparse regularization models

and

Most importantly, the results demonstrate that among the continuous domain regularizations
Eqs.

55
,
56
,
57
and
58
, our proposed model
Eq.

55
performs best. First of all, the LSLP model
Eq.

56
shows some artifacts near the edges. Notice that the operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}
corresponding to the derivatives in the image domain amplifies the noise in the high frequencies. Since the LSLP model penalizes the canonical coefficients corresponding to the annihilating filters only, it is likely that such an amplified noise in the high frequencies may not be fully suppressed by
Eq.

56
. In contrast, since the proposed model promotes the sparsity on the entire range of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
, we can achieve a better denoising effect in spite of the amplified noise, leading to the better restoration results with less artifacts near the edges.

In addition, comparing
Eqs.

55
and
57

Eq.

55
consistently outperforms the balanced approach
Eq.

57
. The results again verify the well-known literature; the analysis approach can reflect the structure of the target signal/image better than the synthesis/balanced approach (e.g.
[
13
]
). Notice that
Eq.

24

{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}
is sparse under the right singular vectors of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. This means that, the analysis approach
Eq.

55
is able to reflect the low-rank structure of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}\right)
better than the balanced approach
Eq.

57
, as the numerical results in
Section

3.1
show that
Eq.

37
is able to obtain a reasonable approximation of right singular vectors.

Finally, the results also demonstrate that
Eq.

55
are less sensitive to the image restoration tasks compared to other continuous domain regularization approaches
Eqs.

56
,
57
and
58
. In particular, we can see that the LRHDDTF model

. In summary, our proposed analysis approach

shows the overall better restoration quality in both the indices and the image quality, in addition to the less sensitiveness to the image restoration tasks.

Conclusion and future directions

In this paper, we propose a two-stage continuous domain regularization approach for the piecewise constant image restoration. Our two-stage approach is based on the fact that the SVD of a Hankel matrix induces tight frame filter banks due to its underlying convolutional structure. More precisely, the first stage
Eq.

37
learns tight frame filter banks from low frequency samples of a degraded measurement. Using the tight frame filter banks learned from the first stage, the second stage restores a piecewise constant image via a wavelet frame based analysis approach
Eq.

25
. Since we use the data-driven tight frame as an alternative to the structured low-rank matrix, the proposed
Eq.

37
achieves a faster computation than the conventional SVD based Cadzow denoising
Eq.

36
, with a numerically equivalent filter learning result. In addition, since we adopts the analysis approach, our proposed image restoration model reflects the underlying structured low-rank matrix better than the previous balanced approach in
[
12
]
. Finally, since we consider the sparsity in the entire transform domain rather than the group sparsity according to the index of filters, our proposed model
Eq.

25
is more robust to the noise/the image modeling.

For the future work, we plan to extend our two-stage approach to the piecewise smooth image restoration. Indeed, since the wavelet frame based sparse regularization model for the piecewise smooth image restoration via the continuous domain regularization is readily available in
[
11
]
as a byproduct of the structured low-rank matrix framework for the piecewise smooth functions, it suffices to develop a method to learn tight frame filter banks from a given degraded measurement. To do this, we need to establish a sufficient condition for the number of low frequency samples to guarantee the estimation of image singularities from annihilating relations. Unfortunately, since the annihilating relations for piecewise smooth functions in
[
11
]
involves blind deconvolutions, it is not clear for us at this moment under what conditions we can restore the singularity set from uniform Fourier samples. Nevertheless, this is definitely a future direction we would like to work on, as it is more appropriate to model natural images as piecewise smooth functions (e.g.
[
16
]
).

To broaden the scope of applications, we also plan to apply the idea in this paper to other image restoration tasks. First of all, since the ideal low-pass filter deconvolution can be viewed as a Shannon’s wavelet superresolution problem (e.g.
[
43
]
), it will not be difficult to apply our approach to the generic wavelet superresolution problem. In addition, even though a careful design on the resampling strategy on the frequency domain
[
40
]
is required, the application to the sparse angle CT is another important application of this approach. Finally, motivated by the recent works on the deep learning techniques combined with the structured low-rank matrix approaches
[
37
,
39
,
51
,
52
]
, we can also consider developing a deep learning/a convolutional neural network framework based on the structured low-rank matrix frameworks. In particular, we will focus on developing a convolutional neural network framework from the cascade structure of the structured low-rank matrix framework in
[
12
]
, designed for the piecewise smooth image restoration via continuous domain regularization.

Appendix A
Preliminaries on tight wavelet frames

In this section, we provide a brief introduction on tight wavelet frames and the wavelet frame based image restoration. Interested readers may consult
[
10
,
24
,
25
,
56
,
61
]
for detailed surveys on tight wavelet frames, and
[
13
]
for detailed reviews on the wavelet frame based image restoration. For the sake of simplicity, we only discuss the real valued wavelet tight frame systems, but note that it is not difficult to extend the idea to the complex case.

Let
{\mathscr{H}}
be a Hilbert space equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. A (Bessel) sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq{\mathscr{H}}
is called a tight frame on
{\mathscr{H}}
if

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle|^{2}~~~~~\text{for all}~~~\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathscr{H}}.

(62)

Given
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq{\mathscr{H}}
, we define the analysis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}:{\mathscr{H}}\to\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathscr{H}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}=\{\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\in\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}).

The synthesis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}:\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathscr{H}}
is defined as the adjoint of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\mapsto{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\boldsymbol{c}}(n)\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\in{\mathscr{H}}.

Then
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is a tight frame on
{\mathscr{H}}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}
. It follows that, for a given tight frame
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, we have the following canonical expression:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n},

with
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}=\{\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
being called the canonical tight frame coefficients. Hence, the tight frames are extensions of orthonormal bases to the redundant systems. In fact, a tight frame is an orthonormal basis if and only if
\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\|=1
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

One of the most widely used class of tight frames is the discrete wavelet frame generated by a set of finitely supported filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M}\}
. Throughout this paper, we only discuss the two dimensional undecimated wavelet frames on
\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
for our purpose. Given
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\ell_{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
, we define a convolution operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}:\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\to\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
by

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{u})({\boldsymbol{k}})=({\boldsymbol{a}}\ast\boldsymbol{u})({\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{l}})\boldsymbol{u}({\boldsymbol{l}})~~~~~\text{for}~~~\boldsymbol{u}\in\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2}).

(63)

Given a set of finitely supported filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M}\}
, define the analysis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
and the synthesis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}
by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}

\displaystyle=\big[{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}(-\cdot)}^{T},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}(-\cdot)}^{T},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M}(-\cdot)}^{T}\big]^{T},

(64)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}

\displaystyle=\big[{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M}}\big],

(65)

respectively. Then, the direct computation can show that the rows of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
form a tight frame on
\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
(i.e.
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}
) if and only if the filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}\}
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{l}}){\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}({\boldsymbol{l}})=\boldsymbol{\delta}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{if}~{\boldsymbol{k}}={\mathbf{0}},\\
0&\text{if}~{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq{\mathbf{0}},\end{array}\right.

called the
unitary extension principle
(UEP) condition
[
34
]
. Finally, for a two dimensional discrete image on the finite grid,
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
in
Eq.

63
with a finitely supported
{\boldsymbol{a}}
denotes the discrete convolution under the periodic boundary condition throughout this paper.

For the image restoration, we assume that there exists a tight wavelet frame
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
defined as
Eq.

64
with filters
\left\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{M}\right\}
such that
\boldsymbol{u}
is sparse under
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
. This leads us to solve

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}~\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}

(68)

and restore
\boldsymbol{u}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}
. In
Eq.

68
, the first term is a data fidelity term, the third
\ell_{1}
norm with the parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left[\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{M}\right]^{T}
and
\gamma_{j}>0
promotes the sparsity of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. The second term penalizes the distance between
{\boldsymbol{c}}
and its projection onto
{\mathscr{R}}({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}})
. Since we obtain the image via
\boldsymbol{u}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}
, the second term in fact forces the sparse coefficient
{\boldsymbol{c}}
close to the canonical coefficient of the restored image. Since the magnitude/decay of the canonical coefficient reflects the regularity of the image under some mild conditions on the tight frame system
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}

[
8
]
, we can see that the second and third terms promote a balance between the sparsity of coefficient and the regularity of image.

We can further impose the flexibility on
Eq.

68
by introducing the weight on the second term:

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}~\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1},

(69)

called the balanced approach
[
10
,
19
]
. If
\mu=0
, then
Eq.

69
becomes the synthesis approach
[
22
,
27
,
30
]

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}~\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1},

(70)

as we aim to find the sparsest coefficient that synthesizes the image. On the other hand, if
\mu=\infty
, then it must be
({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{c}}={\mathbf{0}}
. Since we then have
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in{\mathscr{R}}({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}})
, it follows that
{\boldsymbol{c}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}
for some
\boldsymbol{u}
. Hence,
Eq.

68
becomes the analysis approach
[
15
]

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}~\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1}.

(71)

Since the analysis approach promotes the sparse canonical coefficient, it emphasizes the regularity of the restored image.

These three approaches are equivalent if and only if
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}
, i.e.
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
forms an orthonormal basis. However, since we consider the redundant system, each approach will lead to the different restoration results. Notice that the coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{c}}
obtained from the synthesis approach
Eq.

70
is much sparser than the canonical coefficient
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}
obtained from the analysis approach
Eq.

71
. It is empirically observed that the synthesis approach
Eq.

70
tends to yield some artifacts in the restored image. In contrast, the analysis approach
Eq.

71
usually have less artifacts as it promotes the regularity of the image along the singularities. Theoretically, it has been proved in
[
13
]
that, under a suitable choice of parameters, the analysis approach
Eq.

71
can be seen as sophisticated discretization of a variational model including the Rudin-Osher-Fatemi model
[
58
]
, which enables a geometric interpretation on
Eq.

71
.

Appendix B
Data driven tight frames

As mentioned in the previous section, wavelet frames are widely used for the sparse approximation of an image. To explore a better sparse approximation, the authors in
[
14
]
proposed a
data-driven tight frame
approach, inspired by
[
1
,
55
]
. Specifically, given a two dimensional image
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, a tight frame system
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
defined as in
Eq.

64
, which is generated by a set of finitely supported
p\times p
filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{p^{2}}\}
supported on a
p\times p
grid
{\mathbb{K}}
and satisfying
Appendix

A
, is constructed via the following minimization

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}}~\|{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{0}~~~~~\text{subject to}~~~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}},

(72)

with the
\ell_{0}
norm
\|{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{0}
encoding the number of nonzero entries in the coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{c}}
.

To solve
Eq.

72
, the authors in
[
14
]
presented that if
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p^{2}\times p^{2}}
satisfies
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}=p^{-2}{\boldsymbol{I}}
, after reformulating into
p\times p
filters supported on
{\mathbb{K}}
, its column vectors generate filters satisfying the UEP condition
Appendix

A
. Hence, under such an assumption on the filters
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{p^{2}}
, we can rewrite
Eq.

72
as

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{C}},{\boldsymbol{A}}}~\|{\boldsymbol{C}}-\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\right){\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{0}~~~~~\text{subject to}~~~~{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}=p^{-2}{\boldsymbol{I}},

(73)

to construct filter banks. In
Eq.

73
,
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(N-p+1)^{2}\times p^{2}}
is a Hankel matrix generated by the
p\times p
patches of
\boldsymbol{u}
,
{\boldsymbol{C}}
is the corresponding matrix formulation of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
, and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm of a matrix. To solve
Eq.

73
, the alternating minimization method with closed form solutions for each stage is presented in
[
14
]
. More explicitly, for
n=0
,
1
,
2
,
\cdots
, we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{C}}_{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\boldsymbol{C}}}~\lambda^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{0}+\|{\boldsymbol{C}}-\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\right){\boldsymbol{A}}_{(n)}\|_{F}^{2}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\lambda}\left(\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\right){\boldsymbol{A}}_{(n)}\right)

(74)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{A}}_{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{T}=p^{-2}{\boldsymbol{I}}}~\left\|\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\right){\boldsymbol{A}}-{\boldsymbol{C}}_{(n+1)}\right\|_{F}^{2}=p^{-1}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}

(75)

where
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\lambda}
is a hard thresholding defined as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\lambda}\left({\boldsymbol{C}}\right)^{(j,k)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{(j,k)}&~\text{if}~|{\boldsymbol{C}}^{(j,k)}|>\lambda\\
0&~\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
