Functional Expansions
Bruno Dupire, Valentin Tissot-Daguette

Abstract
Path dependence is omnipresent in many disciplines such as engineering, system theory and finance. It reflects the influence of the past on the future, often expressed through functionals. However, non-Markovian problems are often infinite-dimensional, thus challenging from a conceptual and computational perspective. In this work, we shed light on expansions of functionals. First, we treat static expansions made around paths of fixed length and propose a generalization of the Wiener series − - the intrinsic value expansion (IVE). In the dynamic case, we revisit the functional Taylor expansion (FTE). The latter connects the functional Itô calculus with the signature to quantify the effect in a functional when a “perturbation” path is concatenated with the source path. In particular, the FTE elegantly separates the functional from future trajectories. The notions of real analyticity and radius of convergence are also extended to the path space. We discuss other dynamic expansions arising from Hilbert projections and the Wiener chaos, and finally show financial applications of the FTE to the pricing and hedging of exotic contingent claims.



Keywords —

Functional Itô Calculus, Taylor Expansion, Path Signature,
Wiener Chaos, Contingent Claims

Introduction

Traditional problems in many disciplines rely on the strong hypothesis that the future solely depends on the present. This is the Markov property, which tremendously simplifies the study of causal relationships in physics, biology, or social science.
In finance,
it allows to express the price and hedge of vanilla options in terms of the spot value of the underlying.
This is often no longer the case when the payoff becomes path-dependent (also called exotic) or the stock dynamics is more complex. The lack of Markovian representation can have serious consequences from a computational perspective as the problem is usually infinite-dimensional.

In some cases, a Markovian framework can be recovered by enlarging the state process accordingly.
As an illustration, consider an at-the-money lookback option, i.e.

g(X_{T})=(\max_{0\leq u\leq T}x_{u}-x_{0})^{+}
with
underlying
X
and maturity
T
.
If
X
is Markov and
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\max_{0\leq u\leq t}x_{u})_{t\in[0,T]}
, then
(X,Y)
is also Markov under mild assumptions. In turn, the price of the lookback option at
t\in[0,T]
will be function of
(t,x_{t},y_{t})
only.
The same remedy may be applied when
the dynamics of the stock depends on past information, e.g.
path-dependent volatility
[
24
]
. Taking
the Hobson-Rogers model

[
26
]
, the underlying becomes Markov when the offset processes
-
capturing historical trends of the stock
-
are added to the state.

Nevertheless, there are situations where path dependence cannot be absorbed by finitely many features.
We characterize such frameworks as
fully non-Markovian
.
Among others,
we can mention
American options whose reward depends on a moving average of the underlying
[
6
]
.
Also, the pricing problem in rough volatility models belong to this category, even for the simplest instrument. Either way, the option price becomes a
functional
of the path so far, calling for the development of new mathematical tools.

One avenue consists of employing
series expansions
to project exotic payoffs onto a finite-dimensional subspace arising, e.g., from orthogonal polynomials
[
6
]
, the Wiener chaos
[
30
,
37
]
, or the Karhuhnen-Loève expansion
[
47
]
.
Series expansions can also be applied to approximate stock processes with fully non-Markovian dynamics; see, e.g.,
[
11
]
.
Similar in spirit, the
path signature
showed promising results in option pricing and financial modeling problems entailing path dependence
[
3
,
14
,
35
]
.
These works motivate the present paper as the use of the signature
is justified by a deep yet overlooked result: the
functional Taylor expansion
(FTE). First proposed by
Fliess 1981
,
Fliess 1983
,
Fliess 1986
, it is a generalization of the Taylor expansion where classical derivatives and monomials are respectively replaced by functional derivatives and the signature. Further historical notes and details will be given at the beginning of
Section

3
.

Outline.
We devote this study to the

(1.1)

The kernels
(\psi_{k})
consists of higher-order Fréchet derivatives of

;

[

41

7
]

As can be seen, Volterra’s approach relies on pointwise values of the added path

.
In contrast, the Wiener series and chaos expansion
[
49
,
50
]
encode a

g(X+Y)=\sum_{k<K}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\phi_{k}(t_{1},...,t_{k})dy_{t_{1}}\cdots dy_{t_{k}}+R_{K}(X,Y).

(1.2)

In the chaos expansion,
X,Y
are typical Brownian paths and as shown in

(\phi_{k})
are expectations of iterated Malliavin derivatives of

.
It turns out that a pathwise expansion can be obtained

and employing Stratonovich integrals instead of Itô ones.
This leads to our first contribution: the

, set forth in
Theorem

2.4
. To the best of our knowledge, neither the intrinsic value expansion nor a variation of it has been discussed
in the literature.

Alternatively, one can study the local behavior of a ”running” functional
-
denoted by
f-
when extending the source path. This is the core
of
Section

3
, concerned with
dynamic

expansions. Let us give some insight.
In classical calculus, say on the real line, the Taylor expansion reads,

f(x+y)=\sum_{k<K}f^{(k)}(x)\frac{y^{k}}{k!}+R_{K}(x,y),\hskip 9.24994ptx,y\in\R,\hskip 9.24994ptf\in\calC^{K}(\R).

(1.3)

The shock

is fragmented into its scaled powers

,

(x)
.
In the path space, a similar decomposition can be formulated: If

(1.4)

where

3.10

The indexes

Notations and Definitions.
We employ the framework from the functional Itô calculus
[
16
]
. Fix throughout a horizon
T>0
, which can be interpreted as the maturity of a financial derivative. Let
\Lambda_{t}=\calD([0,t],\R)
be the Skorokhod space of càdlàg paths of length
t\in[0,T]
.
Given
X_{t}\in\Lambda_{t}
,
X_{s}
denotes the whole trajectory up to time
s\leq t
, while
x_{s}=X_{t}(s)
is the value at time
s
.
Moreover, write
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Lambda_{t}
for the collection of all càdlàg paths.

Definition 1.1
.

A
functional
is a map
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda\to\R
, while a
T-
functional
refers to any map
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda_{T}\to\R
.

The distance between elements of
\Lambda
is measured according to

d_{\Lambda}(X_{t},Y_{s})=t-s+\sup_{0\leq u\leq t}|x_{u}-y_{u\wedge s}|,\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq s\leq t\leq T.

(1.5)

We say that a functional is
\Lambda-
continuous
if it is continuous with respect to
d_{\Lambda}
. The
\Lambda_{T}-
continuity of
T-
functionals is defined similarly, where we remark that
d_{\Lambda}
coincides with the uniform topology. We now collect important notions:

(i)

We recall the spatial and temporal
functional derivatives
introduced in
[
16
]
, namely

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0}\,\frac{f(X^{h}_{t})-f(X_{t})}{h},\

\displaystyle X^{h}_{t}(s)\,\,=x_{s}+h\,\mathds{1}_{\{s\,=\,t\}},\hskip 9.24994pts\leq t,

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta t\downarrow 0}\,\frac{f(X_{t,\delta t})-f(X_{t})}{\delta t},\

\displaystyle X_{t,\delta t}(s)=x_{s\wedge t},\hskip 29.02193pts\leq t+\delta t,

whenever these quantities exist.
We note that
\Delta_{x}
and
\Delta_{t}
need not commute; see
Example

5
.

(ii)

Let
\oplus\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda^{2}\to\Lambda
be the noncommutative binary operation that concatenates paths in a continuous fashion. In other words, if
X\in\Lambda_{s}
,
Z\in\Lambda_{u}
then
Y=X\oplus Z\in\Lambda_{s+u}
is given by

y_{r}=x_{r\wedge s}+z_{(r-s)^{+}}-z_{0},\hskip 9.24994ptr\in[0,s+u].

If

u>T-s
, we define instead
X\oplus Z=X\oplus Z_{T-s}\in\Lambda_{T}
.

(iii)

If
\Q
is a measure on
\Lambda
and
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda\to\R
, we write
\E^{\Q}[f(Y_{t})\ |\ X_{s}]
for the
conditioned expectation
of
f(Y_{t})
given that
Y_{t}
coincides with
X_{s}
up to
s<t
. In other words,

\E^{\Q}[f(Y_{t})\,|\,X_{s}]=\int_{\Lambda_{t-s}}f(\underbrace{X_{s}\oplus Z}_{=\ Y_{t}})\Q(dZ),\hskip 9.24994pts\leq t.

(1.6)

To avoid ambiguity, we favor
the letters
Y,\ Z
over

X
to represent random or future paths.

2
Static Expansions

Functional expansions are traditionally made for paths of fixed length. Without loss of generality, we restrict ourselves to paths
in
\Lambda_{T}
.
Contrary to
\Lambda
, the restriction
\Lambda_{T}
has the advantage of being a vector space: we can add paths, have a clear sense of directions, etc. For simplicity, we often express static expansions around the null path of length
T
.

2.1
T-
functionals and Embeddings

It proves helpful to make
the distinction between functionals defined on
\Lambda
and
T-
functionals (those restricted to
\Lambda_{T}
).
If
X\in\Lambda_{T}
is a stock price trajectory, then
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda_{T}\to\R
can be viewed as the payoff of a claim with maturity
T
. Depending on the term sheet of the claim, it is a priori unclear
how to quantify the value of the payoff having observed the price path only up to some intermediate time
t<T
only.
We here address this question by
embedding
T-
functionals into the space of ”running” functionals.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\frakF
be a set of functionals and
\frakF_{T}
is the restriction of
\frakF
to
\Lambda_{T}
.
Then an
embedding
is an operator
\iota\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frakF_{T}\hookrightarrow\frakF
such that
f=\iota g\in\frakF

satisfies
f|_{\Lambda_{T}}=g
for all
g\in\frakF_{T}
.

Notice that many embeddings may exist as the only constraint is that the embedded functional coincides with a precribed
T-
functional on
\Lambda_{T}
.
We here focus our attention on the following class of embeddings which resonates with many meanings in finance.
Let
\Q
be a probability measure on
\Lambda
and define

\iota_{\Q}g(X_{t})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}[g(Y_{T})\,|\,X_{t}],

(2.1)

with the conditioned expectation
\E^{\Q}[\ \cdot\ |\ \cdot\ ]
given in (
1.6
). In
Definition

2.1
, we may choose
\frakF_{T}
,
\frakF
to be the set of uniformly bounded
(T-)
functionals so that clearly
\iota_{\Q}g\in\frakF
and is well-defined. Clearly,
\iota_{\Q}g(X_{T})=g(X_{T})
so that
\iota_{\Q}
is indeed an embedding for every
\Q
. We stress that
X_{t}
need not be in the support of
\Q
as the latter is only introduced to extrapolate the future.
In financial terms and provided that
\Q
is a risk-neutral measure,
\iota_{\Q}g
is the
price functional
of the claim
g
under the
”model”
\Q
. Taking for instance the measure
\Q_{\sigma}
such that the scaled canonical process
Y/\sigma
is
Brownian motion under
\Q_{\sigma}
,
\sigma>0
, then

\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g
gives the Bachelier price of
g
with zero interest rates and volatility
\sigma
.
An important case arises when
\sigma\downarrow 0
, leading to the
intrinsic functional
,

\iota_{0}g(X_{t})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\iota_{\Q_{0}}g(X_{t})=g(X_{t,T-t}).

(2.2)

The intrinsic functional is central in
Section

2.4
where we introduce the
intrinsic value expansion.

2.2
Volterra Series

Vito Volterra (1860-1940) was arguably the first mathematician to introduce and study functional expansions.
His findings have had tremendous influence in nonlinear systems, where the Volterra expansion allows to express the solution functional linking the input path to the output (see, e.g.,
[
48
,
41
,
7
]
).
His idea was to approximate a Fréchet differentiable
T-
functional
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda_{T}\to\R
by a multivariate
function taking
pointwise values of the input path.

For simplicity, suppose that
x_{0}=0
and consider the regular partitions
\Pi^{N}=\{s_{n}=\frac{nT}{N},\ n=0,\ldots,N\}
,
N\geq 1
.
Then
g(X_{T})
is estimated by

g^{N}(x_{s_{1}},...,x_{s_{N}})=g(X_{T}^{N})
with the piecewise constant path

X_{T}^{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_{s_{n}}\mathds{1}_{[s_{n},s_{n+1})}+x_{T}\mathds{1}_{\{T\}}.

If
g^{N}
is real analytic and
\bm{0}=(0,...,0)\in\R^{N}
, a Taylor expansion of
h\mapsto g^{N}(h\ x_{s_{1}},...,h\ x_{s_{N}})

around
h=0
gives

\displaystyle g^{N}(x_{s_{1}},\ldots,x_{s_{N}})

\displaystyle=g^{N}(\bm{0})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\partial_{n}g^{N}(\bm{0})x_{s_{n}}+\sum_{1\leq n\leq m\leq N}\partial_{nm}g^{N}(\bm{0})\,x_{s_{n}}x_{s_{m}}+\ldots

(2.3)

Put differently,
(
2.3
)
is an expansion of
g(X_{T}^{N})
around the flat path
X_{0,T}
.
Now consider the
Fréchet derivatives of
g

in the direction of the Dirac mass
\delta(\cdot-t)
,
t\in[0,T]
,
i.e. the family

(F_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
such that

D_{Y_{T}}g(X_{T})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(X_{T}+hY_{T})-g(X_{T})}{h}=\int_{0}^{T}F_{t}g(X_{T})y_{t}dt,\hskip 9.24994ptY_{T}\in\Lambda_{T},

when the above is well-defined.
From the definition of
g^{N}
, choose
Y_{T}=\mathds{1}_{[s_{n},s_{n+1})}
to obtain

\partial_{n}g^{N}(\bm{0})=D_{\mathds{1}_{[s_{n},s_{n+1})}}g(X_{0,T})=\int_{s_{n}}^{s_{n+1}}F_{t}g(X_{T})dt.

(2.4)

Similarly, defining the iterated Fréchet derivatives
F_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}=F_{t_{1}}\cdots F_{t_{k}}
yields

\partial_{n_{1}\cdots n_{k}}g^{N}(\bm{0})=\int_{s_{n_{k}}}^{s_{n_{k}+1}}\cdots\int_{s_{n_{1}}}^{s_{{n_{1}}+1}}F_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}g(X_{0,T})dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}.

Owing to
(
2.4
)
and the fact that
X^{N}
equals
x_{s_{n}}
on
[s_{n},s_{n+1})
for all
n\leq N
,
we can rewrite
(
2.3
)
as

\displaystyle g(X^{N}_{T})

\displaystyle=g(X_{0,T})+\int_{0}^{T}F_{t}g(X_{0,T})x_{t}^{N}dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}F_{t_{1}t_{2}}g(X_{0,T})x_{t_{1}}^{N}x^{N}_{t_{2}}dt_{1}dt_{2}+\ldots

Letting
N\to\infty
gives, at least formally, the
Volterra series
,

g(X_{T})=g(X_{0,T})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\triangle_{k,T}}F_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}g(X_{0,T})\,x_{t_{1}}\cdots x_{t_{k}}\,dt^{\otimes k},

(2.5)

with the notation
dt^{\otimes k}=dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}
and the simplexes,

\triangle_{k,t}=\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in[0,t]^{k}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{k}\},\hskip 9.24994ptk\geq 1,\hskip 9.24994ptt\in[0,T].

(2.6)

If
\psi_{0}=g(X_{0,T})
and
\psi_{k}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=F_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}g(X_{0,T})
denotes the
Volterra kernels
, then
(
2.5
)
can be written more compactly as

g(X_{T})=\psi_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}V_{k}\psi_{k}(X_{T}),

with the operators

V_{k}\psi_{k}(X_{t})=\int_{\triangle_{k,t}}\psi_{k}\,x^{\otimes k}dt^{\otimes k},\hskip 9.24994ptX_{t}\in\Lambda_{t}.

Note that
V_{k}\psi_{k}
is a
homogeneous functional of degree
k
in the sense that
V_{k}\psi_{k}(\gamma X_{t})=\gamma^{k}V_{k}\psi_{k}(X_{t})
with
\gamma X_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\gamma x_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
,
\gamma\neq 0
. For instance,
V_{1}\psi_{1}
is a linear functional,
V_{2}\psi_{2}
is homogeneous quadratic, and so on.
By construction, we see that the kernels
can be retrieved from Dirac impulses of
g(X_{T})
at specific dates.
Fig.

2(a)
shows the approximation of
\psi_{1}
as in
(
2.4
)
obtained by bumping a single
value of the discretized path.

Example 1
.

Let
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda_{T}\to\R

g(X_{T})=\psi_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}V_{k}\psi_{k}(X_{T})
. Consider the embedding (see

. In other words, we

. It turns out that

2.3
Wiener Series

As seen above, the Volterra series expresses
T-
functionals
-
seen as the output of a system
-

in terms of pointwise values of the source path.
In contrast, the Wiener series and homogeneous chaos expansion
[
50
,
49
]
-
corresponding to the deterministic and stochastic case, respectively
-
encode the path with its infinitesimal increments.
We now present a seemingly unconventional derivation of the Wiener series by borrowing, deliberately, anachronistic elements from the Malliavin calculus.

Let
X\in\Lambda_{T}
be a continuous path of finite variation.
Assume again that
x_{0}=0
, the knowledge of
(x_{s_{1}},...,x_{s_{N}})
is equivalent to the knowledge of the increments
\delta x_{s_{n}}=x_{s_{n}}-x_{s_{n-1}}
,
n=1,\ldots,N
. We can therefore write
g^{N}(\delta x_{s_{1}},...,\delta x_{s_{N}})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=g(X_{T}^{N})
with
X_{T}^{N}
as in
Section

2.2
.
Similarly, write the Taylor expansion of
h\mapsto g^{N}(h\ \delta x_{s_{1}},...,h\ \delta x_{s_{N}})

at
h=0
. That is,

\displaystyle g^{N}(x_{s_{1}},\ldots,x_{s_{N}})

\displaystyle=\phi^{N}_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi^{N}_{1}(s_{n})\delta x_{s_{n}}+\sum_{1\leq n\leq m\leq N}\phi^{N}_{2}(s_{n},s_{m})\,\delta x_{s_{n}}\delta x_{s_{m}}+\ldots

with
\phi^{N}_{0}=g^{N}(\bm{0})\ (=g(X_{0,T})\ \forall N)
and
\phi^{N}_{k}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\partial_{n_{1}...n_{k}}g^{N}(\bm{0})
whenever
t_{l}\in(s_{n_{l-1}},s_{n_{l}}]
,
l=1,\ldots,k
.
Defining
\phi_{0}=g(X_{0,T})
and provided that
\phi_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\phi_{k}^{N}
exist for all
k\geq 1
, a passage to the limit yields, heuristically, the
Wiener series
,

\displaystyle g(X_{T})=\phi_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\triangle_{k,T}}\phi_{k}\,dx^{\otimes k}=\phi_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\triangle_{k,T}}\phi_{k}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\,dx_{t_{1}}\cdots dx_{t_{k}}.

(2.7)

Let us express the kernels explicitly.
First, note that
\partial_{n}g^{N}(\delta x_{s_{1}},...,\delta x_{s_{N}})=D_{s_{n}}g(X_{T}^{N})
, where
D_{\cdot}
is the
Malliavin derivative

D_{t}g(X_{T})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=D_{\mathds{1}_{[t,T]}}g(X_{T})=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{g(X_{T}+h\mathds{1}_{[t,T]})-g(X_{T})}{h}.

(2.8)

Indeed, bumping an increment of
X^{N}_{T}

induces a parallel shift in the path
following the shock; see
Fig.

2(b)
. Letting
N\to\infty
and evaluating the result at the flat path
X_{0,T}
leads to
\phi_{1}(t_{1})=D_{t_{1}}g(X_{0,T})
. Similarly, writing
D_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}=D_{t_{1}}\cdots D_{t_{k}}
gives
\partial_{n_{1}...n_{k}}g^{N}(\delta x_{s_{1}},...,\delta x_{s_{N}})=D_{s_{n_{1}}\cdots s_{n_{k}}}g(X_{T}^{N})
. In conclusion, the kernels in the Wiener series are iterated Malliavin derivatives, namely

\phi_{k}(t_{1},...,t_{k})=D_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}g(X_{0,T})
.

Remark 2.2
.

The Volterra and Wiener series
share many similarities.
For a comprehensive comparison between the Volterra and Wiener chaos expansion, see
Palm and Poggio 1977
.
In particular, the authors show how
\psi_{k},\ \phi_{k}
relate to one another; when
\phi_{k}
is smooth, then

\psi_{k}=(-1)^{k}\partial_{t_{1}...t_{k}}\phi_{k}
using integration by parts.
This can be seen from
Fig.

2(b)
in the case
k=1
.
Alternatively, observe that the Malliavin derivative is
given by a Dirac impulse of the ”white noise” process
\dot{X}=\frac{d}{dt}X
.
Consequently, the Volterra kernels of
\dot{X}
are precisely the Wiener kernels of
X
.

So far, the paths are of finite variation so that the iterated integrals in
(
2.7
)
are in the Riemann-Stieltjes sense.
Before proceeding with the stochastic case (
Section

2.5
)
where the integrals are in the Itô sense,
we propose
a generalization of the Wiener series to paths of finite quadratic variation along a given sequence of partitions.

The Clark-Ocone formula (

In contrast, the functional Itô calculus permits to rewrite this integrand

, the kernels are computed according to the corresponding natural embedding
(intrinsic and price functional, respectively).
But the main difference comes from the paths themselves: In the intrinsic value expansion, the paths can be quite arbitrary, as long as their roughness is controlled along some given sequence of partitions. In the Wiener chaos expansion, they must be ”typical” Brownian paths.

To bring the expansions closer, we compute the IVE of
X_{T}\to g(X_{T}+Y_{T})

as in
Theorem

2.4
and thereafter take expectation with respect to
Y_{T}
under
\Q
. This yields

\E^{\Q}[g(X_{T}+Y_{T})]=\E^{\Q}[g(Y_{T})]+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\triangle_{k,T}}\E^{\Q}[D_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}g(Y_{T})]\,\circ dx^{\otimes k}.

(2.32)

The right side of (
2.32
) closely resembles the Wiener chaos expansion of
g(X_{T})
(
2.31
), except that the integrals are in the sense of Stratonovich instead of Itô. If
X_{T}
is a typical Brownian path and admissible for the IVE, subtracting (
2.31
) to (
2.32
) yields the bias representation

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}[g(X_{T}+Y_{T})]-g(X_{T})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\triangle_{k,T}}\E^{\Q}[D_{t_{1}\cdots t_{k}}g(Y_{T})]\,[\circ\ dx^{\otimes k}-dx^{\otimes k}],\hskip 9.24994pt\Q-a.s.

(2.33)

We remark that the first term on the right side of (
2.33
) vanishes
when
t\mapsto\E^{\Q}[D_{t}g(Y_{T})]
(equal to
\phi_{1}
) is of finite variation. Also, the second term simply reads
\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\E^{\Q}[D_{tT}g(Y_{T})]dt
using Itô’s isometry.

3
Dynamic Expansions

3.1
Functional Taylor Expansion

The Taylor expansion
lies at the core of calculus and finds applications in countless fields. It provides an explicit
approximation of smooth functions by polynomials. A natural question is whether the Taylor expansion
can be extended to more general spaces such as
\Lambda
.
In particular, one may wonder whether there are functionals echoing
the monomials.
A natural candidate is the family of
signature functionals
. The path signature, first studied by
Chen 1957
, has gained much attention in recent years
[
3
,
4
,
14
,
35
]
, due notably
to its universal approximation property.
Indeed, density results à la Stone-Weierstrass exist
[
31
,
14
]

allowing, financially speaking, to both price and replicate exotic payoffs with signature elements; see
[
35
,
4
]
. However, the Stone-Weierstrass theorem is existential instead of constructive. As such, a procedure that explicitly generates the replication of a claim would be preferred.

In response to this appeal,
we propose a pathwise
Taylor expansion for path-dependent payoffs:
the
functional Taylor expansion
(FTE).
To the best of our knowledge, this generalization has been first proposed by
Fliess 1981
,
Fliess 1983
,
Fliess 1986
, termed the
causal Taylor expansion
, for
T-
functionals and continuously differentiable paths. Interestingly, the
causal derivative
in
[
19
]

shares similarities with the spatial derivative from the functional Itô calculus
[
16
]
. The main difference is that the discontinuous ”bump” in the latter is replaced by the vertical segment joining the terminal value of the original path and the bumped one. Moreover, the causal derivative were defined on the whole horizon, i.e. for
T-
functionals only.
More recently, using the functional derivatives in
[
16
]
,

Litterer and Oberhauser 2014
establish a functional Taylor expansion
-
called
Chen-Fliess approximation
-
where the underyling path
comes from the strong solution of a stochastic differential equation with smooth vector fields.

Another
approach is given in

Buckdahn et al. 2015
where the authors build a Taylor expansion for
stochastic processes
suitable to characterize viscosity solutions of path-dependent partial differential equations (PPDEs). In particular, a pathwise estimate of the remainder is given in
[
8
]
, although still requiring
a stochastic framework.
It is worth noting that the spatial functional derivative in
Buckdahn et al. 2015

is slightly different from the ones in
[
16
]
and the present article (in fact, weaker) and arises from the martingale part in the Doob-Meyer decomposition.
In a subsequent work,

Buckdahn et al. 2020
introduce the rough functional Taylor expansion where the path derivative is in the sense of
Gubinelli 2004
. The signature is thus given by iterated rough integrals either defined pathwise in the one-dimensional case or using higher order ”lifts” of the path otherwise; see
Lyons 1998
,
Friz and Hairer 2020
.

In this work, we adopt the pathwise framework pioneered by
Föllmer 1981

combined with tools from the functional Itô calculus
[
16
]
.
Consequently, our Taylor expansion is made ”
\omega
by
\omega
” and thus model-free.
Choosing a similar avenue as
Bally et al. 2016
where pathwise functional Itô formulae are derived,
we establish a pathwise functional Stratonovich formula (
Theorem

3.2
) for one-dimensional continuous paths of finite quadratic variation along a given sequence of partition. This provides, in passing, a way to construct Stratonovich integrals as well as the signature in a pathwise manner. We stress that our construction of the signature relies on limits of simple Riemann sums and thus differs from the more
abstract definition in rough path theory
[
34
,
21
]
given by imposed algebraic relations.
In
Section

3.1.4
, we eventually state the functional Taylor expansion
in various forms.
In particular, the flexibility of the proposed framework allows, in
Theorem

3.10
, to expand a functionals
after a path which may not be regular at all.

The FTE presents several applications in control theory, engineering and finance.
In
Section

4
, we focus on the latter and outline the main use of the FTE when pricing or hedging derivatives.
As also pointed out in
[
31
]
, the FTE is of great relevance
in
cubature methods
(see
Lyons and Victoir 2004
,
Crisan et al. 2013
and the references therein).
In short, cubature is a generalization of Gaussian quadrature to the path space;
the exact integration of polynomials
turns into a perfect fit of expectation of signature elements.
This goes, however, beyond the scope of this work.

3.1.1
Functional Stratonovich Formula

One of the main ingredients in the FTE is a variation of the Functional Itô formula
[
16
]
.
In
Theorem

3.2
, we simultaneously employ a pathwise setting similar to
Föllmer 1981
and Stratonovich integrals as in
Litterer and Oberhauser 2014
.
See also
Bally et al. 2016
and the references therein for pathwise functional Itô formulae, also along the lines of Föllmer’s approach. First, we define a suitable class of integrators.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Pi=(\Pi^{N})
be a sequence of partitions of
[0,T]
with vanishing mesh size, that is
|\Pi^{N}|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{t_{n}\in\Pi^{N}}|t_{n}-t_{n-1}|\overset{N\uparrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. For fixed
t\in[0,T]
,
we say that
X\in\Lambda_{t}
is a
\Pi
-integrator
if
X
is continuous and such that the quadratic variation function along
\Pi
, i.e.,

[0,t]\ni s\mapsto\langle X\rangle^{\Pi}_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{|\Pi_{s}^{N}|\downarrow 0}\sum_{t_{n}\in\Pi^{N}_{s}}(x_{t_{n}}-x_{t_{n-1}})^{2},\hskip 18.49988pt\Pi^{N}_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{t_{n}\wedge s\ |\ t_{n}\in\Pi^{N}\},

(3.1)

exists and is continuous.
We write
\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
for the set of
\Pi
-integrators in
\Lambda_{t}
and
\Omega^{\Pi}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
.

Notice that if
X\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
, then
X_{s}\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{s}
for all
s\leq t
using the sequence of ”stopped”
partitions
\Pi_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\Pi_{s}^{N})
defined in
(
3.1
)
. The choice of
\Pi
may considerably impact the richness of
\Omega^{\Pi}
. Good candidates are refining partitions, that is
\Pi^{1}\subseteq\Pi^{2}\subseteq\ldots
such as the dyadic partition
\Pi^{N}=\{Tn2^{-N}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ n=0,\ldots,2^{N}\}
. In effect, having a nested structure in the sequence of partitions guarantees the almost sure convergence of the quadratic variation for some
stochastic processes, e.g. Brownian motion
[
43
, Proposition 2.12.]
.

To compute the spatial derivatives of
f
at a continuous path
X\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
, we need to make sure that
f
is well-defined when adding vertical bumps to
X
. To this end, we consider the enlargement

\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}_{t}=\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}+\calJ_{t}
, where
\calJ_{t}
contains all piecewise constant paths in
\Lambda_{t}
of finite variation.
Defining as usual
\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}_{t}
gives the sequence of strict inclusions
\Omega^{\Pi}\subset\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}\subset\Lambda
.

As is customary, regularity conditions are required on the function to expand. More specifically, consider the classes of functionals defined on some subset
\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq\Lambda
,

\C^{K,L}(\Lambda^{\prime})=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda^{\prime}\to\R\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\Delta_{\alpha}f\text{ exists and is $\Lambda-$continuous }\,\forall\,\alpha\text{ s.t. }\,|\alpha|_{0}\leq K,\,|\alpha|_{1}\leq L\},\hskip 9.24994ptK,L\geq 0.

Taking
K=L=\infty
gives the class
\C^{\infty,\infty}(\Lambda^{\prime})
of
smooth functionals
in
\Lambda^{\prime}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(
Functional Stratonovich formula
)

Let
X\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
for some
t\in[0,T]
and
f\in\C^{1,2}(\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi})
.
Then,

\displaystyle f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=f(X_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\Delta_{t}f(X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\Delta_{x}f(X_{s})\circ dx_{s},

(3.2)

where the last integral implicitly depends on the sequence of partitions
\Pi
associated to
X
.

Proof.

See
Section

A.1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Although it is necessary to work with càdlàg trajectories to define the spatial derivative,
Theorem

3.2
and in turn, the functional Taylor expansion, is established for continuous paths.
Indeed, doing so prevents the presence of jump terms which would make the FTE more general but much more intricate.

Example 4
.

Let
X\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
and
f(X_{s})=h(x_{s})
for some real function
h\in\calC^{2}(\R)
. Then clearly
f\in\C^{1,2}(\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}_{t})
since
\Delta_{t}f=0
,
\Delta_{x}f=h^{\prime}
, and
\Delta_{xx}f=h^{\prime\prime}
. We can thus apply the pathwise Stratonovich formula to obtain

h(x_{t})=h(x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(x_{s})\circ dx_{s}.

We here recover the well-known fact that Stratonovich integrals yield a first order calculus.

Example 5
.

Let us verify the pathwise Stratonovich formula the time integral
f(X_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})ds
where the pair
(\varphi,X)
satisfies
\int_{0}^{t}|\varphi(X_{s})|ds<\infty
.
Since
\{s\in[0,t]\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,X_{t}(s)\neq X^{h}_{t}(s)\}=\{t\}
has Lebesgue measure zero for all
h\neq 0
, we conclude that
\Delta_{x}f\equiv 0
. For the time derivative, we obtain

\Delta_{t}f(X_{t})=\lim_{\delta t\downarrow 0}\,\frac{1}{\delta t}\int_{0}^{\delta t}\varphi(X_{t,u})du=\varphi(X_{t}).

As expected, this gives

f(X_{t})=\underbrace{f(X_{0})}_{=\ 0}+\int_{0}^{t}\underbrace{\Delta_{t}f(X_{s})}_{\varphi(X_{s})}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\underbrace{\Delta_{x}f(X_{s})}_{=\ 0}\circ\,dx_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})ds.

We also remark that
\Delta_{t}\Delta_{x}f=0
and
\Delta_{x}\Delta_{t}f=\Delta_{x}\varphi
provided that
\Delta_{x}\varphi
exists. Hence
\Delta_{t},\Delta_{x}
do not commute when
\Delta_{x}\varphi\neq 0
.

3.1.2
Spatial Anti-derivative and Pathwise Integration

In the same spirit as Föllmer’s pathwise Itô calculus

[
22
]
,
Theorem

3.2
allows to incidentally define pathwise Stratonovich integrals
\int y\circ dx
where the integrator may be of infinite variation. However, there are constraints on the integrand:
Y
must be the spatial derivative of some functional
f\in\C^{1,2}
evaluated at
X
. In other words, the admissible integrands correspond to all delta hedging strategies where
f
is interpreted as the price functional of an option.

In the path-independent case
as in
Föllmer 1981
, the integrands
are simply the continuously differentiable
functions (both in time and space) of the path, i.e.
y_{t}=h(t,x_{t}),\ h\in\calC^{1,1}(\R)
. Indeed, it suffices to apply the Stratonovich formula to
H(t,x_{t})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int^{x_{t}}_{x_{0}}h(t,y)dy
.
Back to our framework, we construct a spatial anti-derivative as follows.
Given
\varphi\in\C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi})
, write
X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X_{t}^{\varepsilon-x_{t}}

(
\Longrightarrow X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}(t)=\varepsilon
) and define

\Phi(X_{t})=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{t}}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})\ d\varepsilon.

(3.3)

Then, we easily see that

\frac{\Phi(X^{\delta x}_{t})-\Phi(X_{t})}{\delta x}=\frac{1}{\delta x}\int_{0}^{\delta x}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})\ d\varepsilon\,\overset{\delta x\to 0}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 17.07182pt}}\,\varphi(X_{t})

and
\Phi
is a spatial anti-derivative as desired.
Moreover,
\Delta_{t}\Phi(X_{t})=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{t}}\Delta_{t}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})d\varepsilon
.
Since
\Phi(X_{0})=0
, we can invoke and rearrange the functional Stratonovich formula to obtain the pathwise integral

\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})\circ dx_{s}=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{t}}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})\ d\varepsilon-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{s}}\Delta_{t}\varphi(X_{s}^{(\varepsilon)})d\varepsilon\ ds.

(3.4)

In particular, when
\Delta_{t}\varphi=0
, we simply have
\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})\circ dx_{s}=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{t}}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})\ d\varepsilon.

Thus,
the Stratonovich integral
can be made pathwise for
all
functionals in
\C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi})
. Incidentally, this allows us to properly define signature functionals in
\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}
;
see
Proposition

3.5
. It is important to point out that this simple construction works only for one-dimensional paths.

Remark 3.4
.

From the pathwise functional Itô formula
[
1
,
5
]
, note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s-})dx_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{t}}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})\ d\varepsilon-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Delta_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{xx}\right)\Phi(X_{s})\ ds.

(3.5)

The right side of (
3.5
) thus provides a pathwise definition of Itô integrals.

Example 6
.

Similar to
Example

5
, we compute the functional derivatives of the Stratonovich integral
f(X_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})\circ dx_{s}
defined on
\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}
. From the above discussion, we obtain as expected that

\Delta_{t}f(X_{t})=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\Delta_{x}f(X_{t})=\Delta_{x}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{t}}\varphi(X_{t}^{(\varepsilon)})\ d\varepsilon=\varphi(X_{t}).

3.1.3
Signature Functionals and Properties

The FTE entails a collection of objects that characterizes the underlying path in its entirety. This is given by the
signature

[
10
,
34
]
which can be seen as the infinite skeleton of a path.

Notations.
A
word
is a sequence
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{k}
of letters from the alphabet
\{0,1\}
. The number of
0
’s and
1
’s in
\alpha
is denoted by
|\alpha|_{0}
,
|\alpha|_{1}
, respectively. The
length
of
\alpha
is therefore
|\alpha|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|\alpha|_{0}+|\alpha|_{1}
.
We will often use the special words
\bm{0}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=0\ldots 0
such that
|\bm{0}_{k}|=|\bm{0}_{k}|_{0}=k
and
\mathds{1}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=1\ldots 1
such that
|\mathds{1}_{k}|=|\mathds{1}_{k}|_{1}=k
.
Also, for convenience, we write

\alpha-j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{k-j}
where
|\alpha|=k
and
0\leq j\leq k-1
. Finally,
\A
denotes the set containing all the words.

Next, we enlarge a path
X\in\Lambda
with the time itself
and henceforth set

x^{0}_{t}=t
,
x^{1}_{t}=x_{t}
. This is a common procedure used to enrich the signature, see
[
4
,
14
,
35
]
and
Remark

3.6
below.
The letters
0,1
are therefore identified with the time
t
and path
x
, respectively. The definition of the signature is a byproduct of the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Pi
be a sequence of partitions as in
Definition

3.1
. Recall also the simplexes
(\triangle_{k,t})
defined in
(
2.6
)
.
Then for all
X_{t}\in\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi}
and
\alpha\in\A
,
the iterated Stratonovich integral

\displaystyle\calS_{\alpha}(X_{t})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{\triangle_{k,t}}\circ\,dx^{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\circ\,dx^{\alpha_{1}}_{t_{1}}\cdots\circ dx^{\alpha_{k}}_{t_{k}},\hskip 18.49988pt(k=|\alpha|)

(3.6)

is well-defined pathwise.
Moreover,
\calS_{\alpha}\in\C^{\infty,\infty}(\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi})
for all
\alpha\in\A
,
and satisfies the recursion,

\Delta_{t}\calS_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\calS_{\alpha-1},&\alpha_{k}=0\\
0,&\alpha_{k}=1\\
\end{cases},\hskip 18.49988pt\Delta_{x}\calS_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\calS_{\alpha-1},&\alpha_{k}=1\\
0,&\alpha_{k}=0\\
\end{cases}.\hskip 18.49988pt(k=|\alpha|)

(3.7)

Proof.

See
Section

A.2

∎

Together with the constant functional
\calS_{\varnothing}\equiv 1
, we call the collection
\calS=\{\calS_{\alpha}\in\C^{\infty,\infty}(\bar{\Omega}^{\Pi})\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \alpha\in\A\}
the
signature
in
\Omega^{\Pi}
.
When we are given a specific path
X\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{t}
, then the
signature of
X
is understood as the image
\calS(X)=\{\calS_{\alpha}(X_{u})\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ u\in[0,t],\ \alpha\in\A\}
.
Notice that the integrals in the above definition are in the Riemann-Stieltjes (respectively Itô) sense when the integrator (respectively integrand) is of bounded variation. In such situations, the symbol
\circ
may be removed.
Assuming
x_{0}=0
and writing
\calS_{\alpha}
instead of
\calS_{\alpha}(X_{t})
for brevity, the first signature functionals are given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}&&\calS_{\varnothing}&&\\
&\calS_{0}&&\calS_{1}&\\
\calS_{00}&\calS_{01}&&\calS_{10}&\calS_{11}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}&&1&&\\
&t&&x_{t}&\\
\frac{t^{2}}{2}&\int_{0}^{t}s\,dx_{s}&&\int_{0}^{t}x_{s}ds&\frac{x_{t}^{2}}{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix}.

(3.8)

As can be seen,
each entry in the above infinite pyramid
generates two descendants by either integrating the former with respect to
t\sim 0
or
x\sim 1
. Inversely, we gather from (
3.7
) that the functional derivatives permit to retrieve the antecedent by removing the rightmost letter of a given word.

Remark 3.6
.

It is essential to enlarge one-dimensional paths with the time itself. Otherwise, the signature would only consists of the rightmost diagonal of the signature tree
(
3.8
)
, namely

\calS_{\mathds{1}_{k}}(X_{t})=\frac{\delta x_{t}^{k}}{k!}
,
k\geq 0
. Hence, the only information of the path contained in the signature would be its endpoints.
Moreover, adding the increasing path
t\mapsto t
ensures that the signature map
X\mapsto\calS(X)
is invertible; see
[
25
]
. In fact, knowing the words
\alpha^{(k)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=1\bm{0}_{k+1}\,
,
k\geq 0
, is enough to show the injectivity of the signature. Indeed, observe that

\calS_{\alpha^{(k)}}(X_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}x_{s}\frac{(t-s)^{k}}{k!}ds,

which are scaled
L^{2}([0,t])
product of
X_{t}
with the time-reversed monomials
s\mapsto(t-s)^{k}
. As the latter form a complete basis of
L^{2}([0,t])
, the path can thus be uniquely retrieved from
(\calS_{\alpha^{(k)}}(X_{t}))_{k\geq 0}
. See Section 2.3 in
[
47
]

for further details.

Remark 3.7
.

Contrary to rough path theory
[
34
,
21
]
where iterated integrals arise from an imposed algebraic property
-
namely, Chen’s identity
[
10
]
-
our framework allows to express the signature as limits of trapezoidal Riemann sums. This exhibits a clear practical advantage as the integrals are directly interpretable. Admittedly, a fair comparison is difficult as we here assume one space dimension
and different conditions on
X
(finite quadratic variation along a sequence versus
p-
variation metric in rough path theory).

We now collect several properties of signature functionals.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\calS
be the signature in

and fix two words
\alpha,\gamma
.
Then the following properties holds:

(i)

(
Higher derivatives
)
\Delta_{\gamma}\calS_{\alpha}\neq 0
if and only if

for some

(ii)

(

\forall\ \A^{\prime}\subseteq\A
,

, and coefficients
(c_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\A^{\prime}}
such that

,
then
c_{\alpha}=0

\forall\ \alpha\in\A^{\prime}
.

(iii)

(

) The signature functionals restricted to

(iv)

(

)

.
∎

. Assertion

.

3.1.4
Main Results

We first state the functional Taylor expansion (FTE) in general form (
Theorem

3.10
) and later discuss some important consequences
(
Corollaries

3.11
and
3.12
).
Multiple derivatives are denoted by
\Delta_{\alpha}=\Delta_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\Delta_{\alpha_{k}}
. For instance,

\Delta_{001}f=\Delta_{t}(\Delta_{t}(\Delta_{x}f))
, where we stress again that
0,1
is identified with
t,x
, respectively. Besides if
\alpha=\varnothing
, then
\Delta_{\alpha}
is the identity operator, i.e.
\Delta_{\varnothing}f=f
.

Theorem 3.10
.

(
Functional Taylor expansion (FTE)
)

Let
f\in\C^{K,K+1}(\Lambda)
and fix two paths
X_{s}\in\Lambda_{s}
,
Y_{u}\in\Omega^{\Pi}_{u}
with
s,u\in[0,T]
.
Then, we have,

\displaystyle f(X_{s}\oplus Y_{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|<K}\Delta_{\alpha}f(X_{s})\calS_{\alpha}(Y_{u})+R_{K}(X_{s},Y_{u}),

(3.9)

\displaystyle R_{K}(X_{s},Y_{u})

3.1.5

First, we give in

3.2.1
Hilbert Projections

Another procedure to expand functionals

An illustration is given

One may wonder which ONB yields the best approximation

in some suitable sense

3.2.2
Dynamic Wiener Chaos Expansion

We refer the reader to the beginning of

In particular, we consider a stochastic basis

It is well-known that the Wiener chaos expansion can be extended from random variables to stochastic processes

(possibly non-adapted to the Brownian filtration), then one can apply

The chaos expansion for processes is of great importance in Malliavin calculus as it allows to define, among other things, the Skorokhod integral
[

As our focus is on non-anticipative functionals, we henceforth suppose that

An application of Doob’s functional representation

shows that every right-continuous process

\forall\ t\in[0,T],

\Q-
a.s. We therefore expect that a ”dynamic” Wiener chaos expansion is available, namely a chaos expansion for functionals in

.

(

Every functional

(3.32)

Proof.

See
[
15
, Chapter 2]
.

4
Applications

4.1
Pricing of Path-dependent Claims

The functional Taylor expansion has obvious applications in the pricing of exotic options.
In what follows, fix a risk-neutral measure
\Q
and assume zero interest rate. Moreover, the paths represent the evolution of the underlying stock price.
If
g\in L^{1}(\Lambda_{T})
represents the payoff of a path-dependent claim of European type, then its value (or price) with respect to
\Q
is given by

v_{0}=\E^{\Q}[g(Y_{T})]
. If
g
can be nearly replicated by a signature payoff, i.e.
g\approx\sum_{\alpha\in\A^{\prime}}c_{\alpha}\calS_{\alpha}
where
\A^{\prime}
is a finite subset of
\A
, then

v_{0}\approx\sum_{\alpha\in\A^{\prime}}c_{\alpha}\E^{\Q}[\calS_{\alpha}(Y_{T})].

(4.1)

Thus, a pricing method can be devised provided that both the coefficients
(c_{\alpha})
and risk-neutral prices of the ”primitive” securities
(\calS_{\alpha})
are computed efficiently.

This was carried out by
Arribas et al. 2020
and
Lyons et al. 2019
, where
(c_{\alpha})
are calculated by regressing realizations of the payoff against the signature functionals.
The expected signature is either known explicitly
[
4
]
or implied by the market
[
35
]
.

If a functional Taylor (or Maclaurin) expansion was available for
g
, we would set
c_{\alpha}=\Delta_{\alpha}g(X_{0})
in view of
Eq.

4.1
. However,
g
is a priori defined only for paths of length
T
of its temporal derivative may not exist. A remedy is to consider an embedding
\iota\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frakF_{T}\hookrightarrow\frakF
(see
Definition

2.1
) with
\frakF_{T},\frakF
to be specified. In the context of pricing, we see that a natural choice is
\frakF_{K}=L^{1}(\Lambda_{T})
and
\frakF=\bar{L}^{1}(\Lambda)
, the subspace of functionals
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Lambda\to\R
such that
f(Y)
is bounded in
L^{1}(\Q)
.
Although other embeddings can be used, we here choose the family of ”Bachelier embeddings”
(\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}})_{\sigma\geq 0}
seen in
Section

2.1
, namely

\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g(X_{t})=\E^{\Q_{\sigma}}[g(Y_{T})\,|\,X_{t}],

where the scaled canonical process
Y/\sigma
is
\Q_{\sigma}-
Brownian motion. For all
\sigma\geq 0
, then
\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g\in\bar{L}^{1}(\Lambda)
from Jensen’s inequality and the tower property of expectations. Hence

\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}
embeds
L^{1}(\Lambda_{T})
into
\bar{L}^{1}(\Lambda)
.
If
\iota_{{\Q_{\sigma}}}g\in\C^{K,K+1}
for some
K\geq 1
, we can choose
\A^{\prime}=\{\alpha\in\A\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ |\alpha|\leq K\}
and apply the FTE to obtain

\displaystyle v_{0}=\E^{\Q}[g(Y_{T})]=\E^{\Q}[\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g(Y_{T})]\approx\sum_{|\alpha|\leq K}\underbrace{\Delta_{\alpha}\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g(X_{0})}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ c_{\alpha,\sigma}}\E^{\Q}[\calS_{\alpha}(Y_{T})].

(4.2)

Provided that the signature claims are priced correctly (namely the ”model”
\Q
is calibrated to the market), then (
4.2
) gives an explicit approximation for the value of
g
. We emphasize that the Bachelier model is solely used to compute the coefficients.
Note that each value of
\sigma
will generate a distinct sequence of coefficients
(c_{\alpha,\sigma})
and in turn a distinct approximation of the price. In view of
Theorem

3.9
, the coefficients will nevertheless be unique once expressed in the basis
\{\calS_{\beta 1}|_{\Omega_{T}^{\Pi}}\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ |\beta|<K\}
. Because expectations with respect to an atomless measure
tend to smooth out the integrand, we favor
\sigma>0
to compute
(c_{\alpha,\sigma})
over the degenerate case
\sigma=0
associated to the intrinsic functional.

Remark 4.1
.

If
g
is a path-independent payoff, i.e.
g(X_{T})=h(x_{T})
for some
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R\to\R
, then
\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g(X_{t})=\E^{\Q_{1}}[h(x_{t}+\sigma Y_{T-t})]
,
t<T
, is a smooth function of
x_{t}
. However, the regularity of
\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g
is no longer guaranteed for general path-dependent payoffs. As an illustration, consider a fixed strike, at-the-money lookback call option, i.e.
g(X_{T})=\max_{0\leq t\leq T}x_{t}-x_{0}
. Assuming no interest rates, a simple calculation shows that
\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g(X_{t})=u(t,x_{t},\max_{0\leq s\leq t}x_{s})
, where

\displaystyle u(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=x-x_{0}+(m-x)\left[2\Phi\left(\frac{m-x}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}\right)-1\right]+2\sigma\sqrt{T-t}\ \phi\left(\frac{m-x}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}\right),

where
\phi,\ \Phi
is the PDF and CDF of the standard normal distribution, respectively. Although
u
is clearly a smooth function in
m
,
\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g
does not even belong to
\C^{0,1}(\Lambda)
as the functional spatial derivative of
\max_{0\leq s\leq t}x_{s}
is not
\Lambda-
continuous.
One way to sort out this issue is to regularize the running maximum as in
[
16
, Example 2]
and compute thereafter the needed functional derivatives in (
4.2
).

4.2
Static Hedging

The FTE possesses also immediate applications
to static hedging.
Consider a claim
g
with embedding
f=\iota_{\Q_{\sigma}}g\in\C^{K,K+1}
,
\sigma>0
. Suppose we are given a replicating portfolio
\varphi=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq K}c_{\alpha}\calS_{\alpha}
of
g
such that
\Delta_{\alpha}f(X_{0})=c_{\alpha}
for all
|\alpha|\leq K
. We shall see that the FTE allows to quantify the hedging error
|g-\varphi|
. First, applying
Corollary

3.12
to the difference
f-\varphi
gives

f(X_{t})-\varphi(X_{t})=R_{K}(X_{t})=\sum_{|\alpha|=K}\int_{\triangle_{K,t}}\Delta_{\alpha}f(X_{t_{1}})\circ\,dx^{\alpha},

(4.3)

for any price path
X\in\Omega^{\Pi}
. If
g
belongs to the space spanned by the signature functionals up to order
K
, we would obtain a perfect hedge, i.e.
R_{K}\equiv 0
. Otherwise, the hedging error can be bounded as we now explain.
In line with financial markets, we assume that
X
is the piecewise interpolation of
tick data and is in particular Lipschitz continuous.
If also
\sup_{|\alpha|=K}|\Delta_{\alpha}f(X)|\leq C^{K}
for all
X\in\Omega^{\text{Lip}}
,
C<\infty
, then

Proposition

3.16
gives

R_{K}(X_{t})=R_{K}(X_{0},X_{t})=\calO(\frac{t^{K}}{K!})
.
Choosing
t=T
, the hedging error for the claim
g
is therefore bounded by

|g(X_{T})-\varphi(X_{T})|\leq|R_{K}(X_{T})|=\calO\left({\textstyle\frac{T^{K}}{K!}}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\ X_{T}\in\Omega^{\text{Lip}}_{T}.

We emphasize that the above upper bound holds pathwise, matching the needs of exotic option traders to be protected against any future scenario.

5
Conclusion

This paper gathers and intends to elucidate
expansions in the path space. In particular, we draw a distinction between static expansions
(Volterra, Wiener, IVE) and dynamic ones
-
in particular the functional Taylor expansion (FTE) lying at the heart of this work. We can summarize the latter as a pathwise tool combining the functional Itô calculus and the signature to unravel path dependence. We then establish parallels between the FTE and static expansions such as the intrinsic value expansion and the celebrated Wiener chaos. As seen in the applications, the FTE proves useful for the pricing and hedging of exotic claims as it effectively separates the payoff functional from the price path. For instance, this separation can be incorporated into numerical methods such as cubature schemes to guarantee pricing accuracy and speed up computation.

We deem the study of path functionals a necessity in modern quantitative finance, given the omnipresence of path dependence. As such, it seems that many works can be undertaken in this direction.
In particular, one can investigate financial applications of the Volterra and Wiener series, used extensively in nonlinear systems but barely in finance. Another avenue would be to tackle non-Markovian problems with the FTE, such as the pricing of American options involving path-dependent features in the payoff, the dynamics, or both.
