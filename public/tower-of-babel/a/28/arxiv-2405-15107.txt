Is Algorithmic Stability Testable? A Unified Framework under Computational Constraints
Yuetian Luo, Rina Foygel Barber

Abstract
Algorithmic stability is a central notion in learning theory that quantifies the sensitivity of an algorithm to small changes in the training data. If a learning algorithm satisfies certain stability properties, this leads to many important downstream implications, such as generalization, robustness, and reliable predictive inference. Verifying that stability holds for a particular algorithm is therefore an important and practical question. However, recent results establish that testing the stability of a black-box algorithm is impossible, given limited data from an unknown distribution, in settings where the data lies in an uncountably infinite space (such as real-valued data). In this work, we extend this question to examine a far broader range of settings, where the data may lie in any space—for example, categorical data. We develop a unified framework for quantifying the hardness of testing algorithmic stability, which establishes that across all settings, if the available data is limited then exhaustive search is essentially the only universally valid mechanism for certifying algorithmic stability. Since in practice, any test of stability would naturally be subject to computational constraints, exhaustive search is impossible and so this implies fundamental limits on our ability to test the stability property for a black-box algorithm.

Thus, verifying the stability of a given algorithm is an important and practical question. Some algorithms can easily be shown to be stable, such as

Consequently, this motivates the question of whether we can estimate the stability of an algorithm through empirical evaluation, rather than theoretical calculations. This idea is formalized in the work
Kim and Barber, (2023)
where the authors consider inferring the stability of an algorithm through “

” testing without peering inside the construction of the algorithm. Under the setting when
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{A}
countably infinitely many times,
Kim and Barber, (2023)
established a fundamental bound on our ability to test for algorithmic stability in the absence of any assumptions on the data distribution and the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.

However, in many common settings, such as categorical features or a binary response, we may have
finite

\mathcal{X}
and/or
\mathcal{Y}

The results of
Kim and Barber, (2023)

\mathcal{X}
and/or
\mathcal{Y}
to be infinite, cannot be applied to characterize the hardness of testing stability in these settings—and indeed, we would expect the testing problem to be fundamentally easier if there are only finitely many possible values of the data. On the other hand, in
practice, we only have a finite amount of computational resources in order to
run our test of stability. This leads to a key motivating question: is it possible to construct a black-box test
of algorithmic stability in finite data spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, and what role do computational constraints play in this question?

2
Background and problem setup

In this section, we formalize some definitions and give additional background for studying algorithmic stability via black-box tests. In (
1
), we have defined the algorithm as a deterministic map given the training data. In many settings, commonly used algorithms might also include some form of randomization—for instance, stochastic gradient
descent type methods. To accommodate this, we will expand the definition of an algorithm to take an additional argument,

\mathcal{A}:\ \cup_{n\geq 0}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\big\{\textnormal{measurable functions $\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$}\big\}.

(2)

The fitted model
{\widehat{f}}_{n}=\mathcal{A}\big(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]};\xi\big)
is now obtained by running algorithm
\mathcal{A}

on data set
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
, with randomization provided by the argument
\xi\in[0,1]
,
which acts as a random seed. Of course, the general definition (
2
)
of a randomized algorithm also accommodates the deterministic (i.e., non-random) setting and we refer any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that depends only on the data set
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
, and ignores the second argument
\xi
as deterministic algorithm. In this paper, we focus on the setting where
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric in the training data—that is, permuting the order of the
n
data points does not change the output of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}[(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n});\xi]=\mathcal{A}[(x_{\sigma(1)},y_{\sigma(1)}),\ldots,(x_{\sigma(n)},y_{\sigma(n)});\xi^{\prime}],

for some coupling of the randomization terms
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\sim\textnormal{Unif}[0,1]
.

2.1
Algorithmic stability and hypothesis testing formulation

There are many different notions of algorithmic stability in the literature (see, e.g.,
Bousquet and Elisseeff, (2002)
and Appendix A of
Shalev-Shwartz et al., (2010)
for some comparisons). In this work, we follow
Kim and Barber, (2023)
and consider testing the following notion of stability:

Definition 1

(Algorithmic stability)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a symmetric algorithm.
Let
\epsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1)
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stable with respect to training data sets of size
n
from a data distribution
P
—or, for short, the triple
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stable—if

\mathbb{P}\{|{\widehat{f}}_{n}(X_{n+1})-{\widehat{f}}_{n-1}(X_{n+1})|>\epsilon\}\leq\delta,

where

{\widehat{f}}_{n}=\mathcal{A}\left(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]};\xi\right),\quad{\widehat{f}}_{n-1}=\mathcal{A}\left(\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i\in[n-1]};\xi\right),

and where the data is distributed as
(X_{i},Y_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{iid}}}{{\sim}}P
, and the random seed
\xi\sim\textnormal{Unif}[0,1]
is drawn independently of the data.

In the setting of predictive inference for regression problems, this notion of stability has appeared in
Barber et al., (2021)
where it was shown that this condition is sufficient for predictive coverage guarantees of the jackknife and jackknife+ methods. Earlier work by
Steinberger and Leeb, (2023)
also established coverage properties for jackknife under a similar stability property.

Defining a hypothesis test.

Given a fixed
\epsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1)
, we are interested in testing

H_{0}:(\mathcal{A},P,n)\textnormal{ is not }(\epsilon,\delta)\textnormal{-stable}\quad\textnormal{v.s.}\quad H_{1}:(\mathcal{A},P,n)\textnormal{ is }(\epsilon,\delta)\textnormal{-stable}

with some desired bound
\alpha\in(0,1)
on the type-I error—that is, a bound on the probability that we falsely certify an algorithm as stable. In this work, we consider the problem of running this hypothesis test with limited
access to data from
P
—specifically, we assume that the available data consists of a labeled data set,
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[N_{\ell}]}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{iid}}}{{\sim}}P
, and an unlabeled data set,
\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{X_{N_{\ell}+1},\cdots,X_{N_{\ell}+N_{u}}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{iid}}}{{\sim}}P_{X}
, where
P_{X}
is the marginal distribution of
X
under
P
(and
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{D}_{u}
are sampled independently). With the aim of avoiding placing any assumptions on the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and on the distribution
P
, we would like to seek a test
{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})\in\{0,1\}
that satisfies the following notion of
assumption-free
validity:

\mathbb{P}_{P}\{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1\}\leq\alpha\textnormal{ for any $(\mathcal{A},P,n)$ that is not $(\epsilon,\delta)$-stable}.

(3)

Although our notation accommodate randomized algorithms, in some cases if we know we are working with deterministic algorithms, we might not need to assume the above strong notion of validity, instead we can require its deterministic conterpart:

\mathbb{P}_{P}\{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1\}\leq\alpha\textnormal{ for any $(\mathcal{A},P,n)$ that is not $(\epsilon,\delta)$-stable and }\mathcal{A}\textnormal{ is deterministic}.

(4)

2.2
Black-box tests with computational constraints

To understand the difficulty of testing algorithmic stability, we need to
formalize the limitations on what information is available to us, as the analyst, for performing the test.
For example, if we know the distribution
P
of the data, then for many algorithms, we might be able to study its stability property analytically. Clearly, this is not the setting of interest, since in practice we cannot rely on assumptions about the data distribution—but in fact, even if
P
is unknown, analyzing the stability for simple algorithms such as kNN or ridge regression is still tractable
(
Devroye and Wagner,, 1979
;
Bousquet and Elisseeff,, 2002
)
. In modern settings, however, a state-of-the-art algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is typically far more complex and may be too complicated to analyze theoretically. For this reason, we consider the black-box testing framework introduced in
Kim and Barber, (2023)
which analyzes the stability of algorithm
\mathcal{A}
via empirical evaluations, rather than theoretical calculations. The intuition is this: if
\mathcal{A}
is implemented as a function in some software package, our procedure is allowed to call the function, but is not able to examine the implementation of
\mathcal{A}
(i.e., we cannot read the code that defines the function).

A high-level illustration of a black-box test of interest is provided in Figure
1
and the formal definition is provided in Definition
2
. Compared to the definition of black-box test given in
Kim and Barber, (2023)
, here we focus on black-box tests with computational constraints. In particular, the computational budget for training is characterized by
B_{\textnormal{train}}
—the total number of data points that we are allowed to input to the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, over all calls to
\mathcal{A}
(for example, when running the test, we can choose
to train
\mathcal{A}
on a data set of size
n
, up to
B_{\textnormal{train}}/n
many times).

Input:

labeled data
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}
,

unlabeled data
\mathcal{D}_{u}
,

algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

seeds
\zeta,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots

Iterate for rounds
r=1,2,\ldots,{\widehat{r}}
, subject to

computational constraint
\sum_{r=1}^{{\widehat{r}}}|\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell}|\leq B_{\textnormal{train}}

Generate labeled data set
\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell}
and random seed
\xi^{(r)}

Call the algorithm to train a model:

{\widehat{f}}^{(r)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell};\xi^{(r)})

Output:

{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})\in\{0,1\}

Figure 1:
Black-box test with computational constraints. Here in round
r
, the generating process of the new data set
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(r)}
and random seed
\xi^{(r)}
for training can depend on the input data and on all the past information from previous rounds—e.g., we may create a new data set by resampling from past data. The input random seeds
\zeta,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{iid}}}{{\sim}}\textnormal{Unif}[0,1]
may be used to provide randomization if desired. The procedure stops at a data-dependent stopping time
{\widehat{r}}
, with the constraint that the total number of training points used in calls to
\mathcal{A}
cannot exceed the budget

Definition 2

(Black-box test under computational constraints)
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}=\cup_{m\geq 0}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{m}
denotes the space of labeled data sets of any size, and let
\mathfrak{D}_{u}=\cup_{m\geq 0}(\mathcal{X})^{m}
denotes the space of unlabeled data sets of any size.
Consider any function
{\widehat{T}}
that takes as input an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, a labeled data set
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}
, and an unlabeled data set
\mathcal{D}_{u}\in\mathfrak{D}_{u}
,
and returns a (possibly randomized) output
{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})\in\{0,1\}
.
We say that
{\widehat{T}}
is a black-box test with computational budget
B_{\textnormal{train}}>0
for model training if it can be defined as follows:
for some functions
g^{(1)},g^{(2)},\dots
and
g
, and for random seeds,
\zeta^{(1)},\zeta^{(2)},\dots,\zeta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{iid}}}{{\sim}}\textnormal{Unif}[0,1]
,

1.

For each stage
r=1,2,3,\ldots
,

(a)

If
r=1
, generate a new labeled data set and a new random seed,

(\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(1)},\xi^{(1)})=g^{(1)}(\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u},\zeta^{(1)}).

•

If some stopping criterion is reached or
|\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\ell}|>B_{\textnormal{train}}
, exit the for loop; otherwise compute the fitted model
{\widehat{f}}^{(1)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(1)};\xi^{(1)})
.

(b)

If
r\geq 2
, generate a labeled data set and a new random seed,

(\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(r)},\xi^{(r)})=g^{(r)}\big(\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u},(\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(s)})_{1\leq s<r},({\widehat{f}}^{(s)})_{1\leq s<r},(\zeta^{(s)})_{1\leq s\leq r},(\xi^{(s)})_{1\leq s<r}\big).

•

If some stopping criterion is reached or
\sum_{j=1}^{r}|\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(j)}|>B_{\textnormal{train}}
, exit the for loop; otherwise compute the fitted model
{\widehat{f}}^{(r)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(r)};\xi^{(r)})
.

2.

Let
{\widehat{r}}
denote the total number of rounds when stopping.

3.

Finally, return

{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=g\big(\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u},(\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{(r)})_{1\leq r\leq{\widehat{r}}},({\widehat{f}}^{(r)})_{1\leq r\leq{\widehat{r}}},(\zeta^{(r)})_{1\leq r\leq{\widehat{r}}},(\xi^{(r)})_{1\leq r\leq{\widehat{r}}},\zeta\big).

The random seeds
\zeta^{(1)},\zeta^{(2)},\dots
are included to allow for randomization at each stage of the test, if desired. For example,
if the data set
\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell}
is computed via subsampling from the available real data
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}
(e.g., a bootstrap sample),
then
\zeta^{(r)}
may be used to generate the random subsample. Similarly, the random seed
\zeta
is included
to allow for randomization, if desired,
in the final step of the test—i.e., computing
{\widehat{T}}\in\{0,1\}
.

We note that for the black-box test defined in Definition
2
, while it can not examine the theoretical properties of
\mathcal{A}
, it knows the analytic expression of fitted models
{\widehat{f}}^{(r)}
(See also Appendix
A
for an alternative type of black-box test). This offers significantv flexibility to the analyst—and thus, establishing the hardness of testing algorithmic stability in this setting is a strong result.

An example of a black-box test.

As an example of the type of test that would satisfy Definition
2
, we can consider a simple sample-splitting based testing procedure. Recall that
N_{\ell}
and
N_{u}
denote the number of labeled and unlabeled data points we have. Define

\kappa=\kappa(n,B_{\textnormal{train}},N_{\ell},N_{u})=\min\left\{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n},\frac{N_{\ell}}{n},\frac{N_{\ell}+N_{u}}{n}\right\}.

(5)

Then,
\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
is the largest number of copies of independent datasets—each consisting of
n
labeled training points and one unlabeled test point—that can be constructed from
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{D}_{u}
and we can run under the computational constraint.

•

For each
k=1,\ldots,\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
, train models

\begin{split}{\widehat{f}}^{(k)}_{n}&=\mathcal{A}[(X_{(k-1)n+1},Y_{(k-1)n+1}),\ldots,(X_{kn},Y_{kn});\xi^{(k)}],\\
{\widehat{f}}^{(k)}_{n-1}&=\mathcal{A}[(X_{(k-1)n+1},Y_{(k-1)n+1}),\ldots,(X_{kn-1},Y_{kn-1});\xi^{(k)}],\end{split}

with
\xi^{(k)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\textnormal{Unif}[0,1]
, and compute the difference in predictions at test point
X_{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor n+k}

\Delta^{(k)}=\Big|{\widehat{f}}^{(k)}_{n}(X_{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor n+k})-{\widehat{f}}^{(k)}_{n-1}(X_{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor n+k})\Big|.

(6)

•

Compute
B=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}{\mathds{1}}(\Delta^{(k)}>\epsilon)
, and compare this test statistic against the Binomial
(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)
distribution, returning
1
if
B
is sufficiently small and
0
otherwise.

3
Main results on black-box test for algorithmic stability with computational constraints

In this section, we present the main results on power upper bounds of testing algorithmic stability for computationally constrained black-box tests. First, given any
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
, let us define

\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{P}\{|{\widehat{f}}_{n}(X_{n+1})-{\widehat{f}}_{n-1}(X_{n+1})|>\epsilon\},

(7)

i.e.,
\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}
is the smallest possible value of
\delta^{\prime}
so that
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
is
(\epsilon,\delta^{\prime})
-stable.

Next, we state two results regarding the power of testing algorithmic stability for any algorithms or deterministic algorithms.

Theorem 1
.

Fix any
\epsilon\geq 0
,
\delta\in[0,1)
. Let
{\widehat{T}}
be any black-box test with computational budget
B_{\textnormal{train}}
defined in Definition
2
. Suppose
{\widehat{T}}
satisfies the assumption-free validity condition (
3
) at level
\alpha
. Then for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
that is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stable (i.e.,
\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}\leq\delta
), the power of
{\widehat{T}}
is bounded as

•

(computational constraint due to budget
B_{\textnormal{train}}
)

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\cdot\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}.

(8)

.

•

(statistical constraint due to limited samples in
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
)

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\cdot\min\Bigg\{\frac{\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}}}{1-\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\wedge 1},\frac{\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\widetilde{\delta}}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}+N_{u}}{n}}}{1-\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{|\mathcal{X}|}\wedge 1}\Bigg\},

(9)

where
\widetilde{\delta}=(\delta+\frac{1}{n})\wedge 1
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix any
\epsilon\geq 0
,
\delta\in[0,1)
. Let
{\widehat{T}}
be any black-box test with computational budget
B_{\textnormal{train}}
defined in Definition
2
. Suppose
{\widehat{T}}
satisfies the assumption-free validity condition (
4
) at level
\alpha
with respect to the class of deterministic algorithms. Then for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
that is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stable (i.e.,
\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}\leq\delta
) and
\mathcal{A}
is deterministic,

•

(computational constraint due to budget

) if the distribution
P
satisfies

\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}(\{x,y\})<0.2,

C>0
such that the power of
{\widehat{T}}
is bounded as

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\left(\alpha+C/n\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta-\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}.

(10)

•

(statistical constraint due to limited samples in
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
) the power of
{\widehat{T}}
is bounded by (

).

From Theorems
1
and
2
, we can see the power of any universally valid test is bounded by the minimum of three terms.
Here we take a closer look at each of the three terms to better understand how this limit on power arises. Let us take the randomized algorithm as an illustration example.

•

First term ((
8
) or (
10
)): computational constraint due to budget
B_{\textnormal{train}}
.
The first term is low—that is, not much larger than
\alpha
, meaning that the power cannot be much better than random—whenever the ratio
B_{\textnormal{train}}/n
is small.
This is intuitive: this ratio describes the number of times we are able to call
\mathcal{A}
on training samples of size
n
,
and we need this number to be large in order to distinguish between stability (i.e., large perturbations occur with small probability
\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}\leq\delta
)
versus instability (i.e., large perturbations occur with probability
>\delta
).

•

Second term (the first part of the bound in (
9
)): statistical constraint due to limited samples in
\mathcal{Y}
. The second term
shows that power is low whenever
we have
both
of the following: the ratio
N_{\ell}/n
is not too large, and the ratio
B_{\textnormal{train}}/|\mathcal{Y}|
is close to 0.
For the first quantity, a bound on
N_{\ell}/n
means that we do not have access to ample labeled data—the available data
sampled from
P
provides only

N_{\ell}/n
many independent data sets of size
n
, so it is not easy to empirically estimate how often instability may occur.
For the second ratio, if
B_{\textnormal{train}}/|\mathcal{Y}|\approx 0
then this means that we do not have sufficient computational budget
to allow an exhaustive search over
\mathcal{Y}
(i.e., we cannot afford to test the behavior of
\mathcal{A}
on all possible values of
Y
)
to compensate for the limited amount of available labeled data.

•

Third term (the second part of the bound in (
9
)): statistical constraint due to limited samples in
\mathcal{X}
. The interpretation for this term is analogous to the previous term,
with the difference that we are given
N_{\ell}+N_{u}
many draws of
X
due to the additional unlabeled data set
\mathcal{D}_{u}
.

In particular, the power can be only slightly higher than
\alpha
(i.e., only slightly better than random),
unless
all
of the three terms in the minimum are large: to have high power,
we would need
B_{\textnormal{train}}\gg n

to enable sufficiently many calls to
\mathcal{A}
,
and moreover, to search over
\mathcal{Y}
space we need either
N_{\ell}\gg n
or
|\mathcal{Y}|
to be relatively small,
and similarly to search over
\mathcal{X}
space we need either
N_{\ell}+N_{u}\gg n
or
|\mathcal{X}|
to be relatively small.
Note the second and third terms are statistical limits, due to limited data (i.e.,
N_{\ell}
and
N_{u}
), but
these statistical limits can be overcomed if
|\mathcal{X}|
and
|\mathcal{Y}|
are small enough to permit exhaustive search (under the computation constraint

). In practice, we might expect the algorithm to become more stable as

increases, and we will be interested in testing whether the algorithm is
(\epsilon_{n},\delta_{n})
-stable with
\epsilon_{n},\delta_{n}\to 0

suggests that as long as one of the three terms in (

or

, then it is even impossible to tell whether

are smaller than a constant, let alone tell they go to zero.

A nearly matching bound.

We will now show that the upper bound on the power given in Theorem

based on sample-splitting. Recall that the binomial test in Section

\begin{split}{\widehat{T}}_{\textnormal{Binom}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=\begin{cases}1,&B<k_{*},\\
1,&B=k_{*}\textnormal{ and }\zeta\leq a_{*},\\

(11)

for a random value

Then a similar result as the Theorem 1 in

Proposition 1
.

The test

and satisfies the assumption-free validity condition (

\mathbb{P}({\widehat{T}}_{\textnormal{Binom}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1)=\alpha\cdot\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\Big\lfloor\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}\wedge\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}\wedge\frac{N_{\ell}+N_{u}}{n}\Big\rfloor}.

An unlimited budget?

While our result is stated for a black-box test run under a prespecified computational constraint,
B_{\textnormal{train}}
,
it is also natural to ask what power might be obtained if we do not specify a budget. In fact, our result applies
to this setting as well. Suppose that we require our black-box test
{\widehat{T}}
to terminate at a finite time (i.e.,
{\widehat{r}}
must be finite),
but we do not specify a budget—that is,
{\widehat{r}}
can be arbitrarily large, and
\sum_{r=1}^{{\widehat{r}}}|\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell}|
can be arbitrarily large.
In this setting, we can simply apply the results of Theorem
1
while taking
B_{\textnormal{train}}\to\infty
.
In particular,

\lim_{B_{\textnormal{train}}\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\wedge 1\right)=\begin{cases}1,&|\mathcal{Y}|=\infty,\\
0,&|\mathcal{Y}|<\infty,\end{cases}

and the same is true for
|\mathcal{X}|
. Therefore, even with an unlimited budget, if we require
{\widehat{T}}
to return an answer in finite time then
Theorem

implies the following bounds on power:

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}},\textnormal{ if $|\mathcal{Y}|=\infty$; \quad}\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\widetilde{\delta}}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}+N_{u}}{n}},\textnormal{ if $|\mathcal{X}|=\infty$}.

Comparison with existing work.

Our result is a strict generalization of

Kim and Barber, (2023, Theorem 3)
, which proves that power is bounded as

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}}
whenever
|\mathcal{Y}|=\infty
.
2
2

2

More precisely, their work assumes that the cardinality of the space
\mathcal{Y}
is uncountably infinite,
but this is because they allow the test

Of course, this is a special case of our main result, obtained by taking

.
The existing result in their work does not provide a bound on power when

their result does not apply in the common binary classification setting

4
Discussion

In this paper, we provide a unified power upper bound for any universally valid black-box test for testing algorithmic stability under computational constraints. We reveal the computational budget can be the dominant constraint when the budget
B_{\textnormal{train}}
is small. At the same time, we illustrate that when the data space is finite, if the sample size is limited then certifying stability is possible only if the data spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are small enough to essentially permit an exhaustive search.

As we have mentioned in Section
2.2
for the black-box test presented in Definition
2
, while the training algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can only be studied empirically via calls to
\mathcal{A}
(that is, we cannot examine its theoretical properties, or, say, read the code that defines the implementation of
\mathcal{A}
), in contrast the fitted model
{\widehat{f}}^{(r)}
returned by
\mathcal{A}
at the
r
th round
is
transparent to us—we know its analytic expression in constructing the final test. This is a less restrictive notion of black-box testing, giving more information to the analyst. This is called a black-box test with
transparent models
by
Kim and Barber, (2023)
. An even stronger constraint is the notion of a black-box test with
black-box models
, where the fitted models cannot be studied analytically and instead can only be studied via evaluating the models on finitely many test points. In Appendix
A
, we also establish the power upper bound for black-box test with black-box models in testing algorithmic stability.

There are also a couple of interesting directions worth exploring in the future. First, as we mentioned in Section
3
that the power achieved by the simple binomial test is only tight when
B_{\textnormal{train}}
is relatively small to
|\mathcal{X}|
and
|\mathcal{Y}|
, it is interesting to see whether we could develop procedures which can close the gap between the current upper and lower bounds on power in general scenarios. Second, our hardness result on testing algorithmic stability suggests that we should look for relaxation for solving this testing problem. One possible way would be to relax the notion of stability we consider. For example, in Section 5.2 of
Kim and Barber, (2023)
, the authors discussed it might be possible to test a weaker notion of data-conditional stability. The second possible way would be to relax the test we consider. It would be interesting to consider a partially black-box setting, where some part of the algorithm’s mechanism is known and some part is unknown—for instance, this arises when we use a complex black-box algorithm for building features, but then run a simple regression to use these features for prediction. Finally, in this work, we focus on testing
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stability defined in Definition
1
; another popular stability notion in learning theory is the hypothesis stability proposed in
Bousquet and Elisseeff, (2002)
. The distinction between these two notions is that our notion of stability is defined based on the perturbation of prediction, whille hypothesis stability is defined based on the perturbation of loss. An interesting open question is whether there is a unified way to treat different notions of stability under the black-box testing framework.

5
Proof of Theorem
1

The theorem can be separated into three claims and we will prove each of these claims separately. Respectively, these three results reflect the bounds on power that are due to limited
Y
data and
X
data (statistical limits), and to a limited number of calls to
\mathcal{A}
(a computational limit). We will begin with the power upper bound due to limited
Y
data.

5.1
Statistical limit: bounding power due to limited
Y
data

The high-level idea of the proof is the following: Step 1, we show that for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, there is a event such that on that event
\{\mathcal{E}_{y}\}
, the power is lower, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}({\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{y})\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}}
; Step 2, we show if the result in Step 1 is true, then we could integrate these events under the computational constraint and the ultimate power will also be low as well.

The key lemma we need in Step 1 is the following. For any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, define
\mathcal{E}_{y}
as the event that
y\not\in\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\ell}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})}_{\ell}
, when running the test
{\widehat{T}}
. Then

Lemma 1
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
,
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{y}\}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{N_{\ell}}{n}}
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is postponed to appendix, but the proof idea of Lemma
1
is given as follows: we will construct a corrupted distribution
P^{\prime}
, and a modified algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
so that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is not stable when the input data consists of
y
. Then we could show under proper conditions on
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
,
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P^{\prime},n)
is not stable, and it is difficult to distinguish between
\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{A},P
given the event
\mathcal{E}_{y}
happens. Finally, the low power of test on
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
comes from the validity of the test when it examines the unstable tuple
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P^{\prime},n)
.

Then we can integrate the result in Lemma
1
on different events
\mathcal{E}_{y}
and get the bound. Without loss of generality, we can assume
|\mathcal{Y}|>B_{\textnormal{train}}
,
since otherwise the bound is trivial.
Fix any integer
M
with
B_{\textnormal{train}}<M\leq|\mathcal{Y}|
(note that
|\mathcal{Y}|
may be finite or infinite),
and let
y_{1},\dots,y_{M}\in\mathcal{Y}
be distinct.
We then have

\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{y_{i}}\}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\mathds{1}}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{y_{i}}\}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathds{1}}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\mathds{1}}\{\mathcal{E}_{y_{i}}\}\right]\\
\geq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathds{1}}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\cdot(M-B_{\textnormal{train}})\right]=\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\cdot(M-B_{\textnormal{train}}),

where the inequality holds since, due to the computational constraint,

y\in\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\ell}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})}_{\ell}
can hold for at most
B_{\textnormal{train}}
many values
y\in\mathcal{Y}
—and therefore,
at least
M-B_{\textnormal{train}}
many of the events
\mathcal{E}_{y_{1}},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{y_{M}}
must hold.
Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{y_{i}}\}}{M-B_{\textnormal{train}}}\leq\frac{M\cdot\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{N_{\ell}/n}}{M-B_{\textnormal{train}}},

where the last step applies Lemma
1
.
Since this holds for any
M
with
B_{\textnormal{train}}<M\leq|\mathcal{Y}|
, we have

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{N_{\ell}/n}\cdot\inf_{M\,:\,B_{\textnormal{train}}<M\leq|\mathcal{Y}|}\frac{M}{M-B_{\textnormal{train}}}.

If
|\mathcal{Y}|<\infty
, then
\inf_{M\,:\,B_{\textnormal{train}}<M\leq|\mathcal{Y}|}\frac{M}{M-B_{\textnormal{train}}}=\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{|\mathcal{Y}|-B_{\textnormal{train}}}
.
If instead
|\mathcal{Y}|=\infty
, then
\inf_{M\,:\,B_{\textnormal{train}}<M\leq|\mathcal{Y}|}\frac{M}{M-B_{\textnormal{train}}}=1
. For both cases, then, we have obtained the desired result.

In this section, we prove the power upper bound due to a limited number of calls to

Recall that when we run the test
{\widehat{T}}

are carried out with data set
\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell}
and with random seed

Fixing any subset

Define also the function

the conditional probability of an unstable prediction, as a function of the random seed

(here the probability is taken with respect
to training and test data sampled i.i.d. from

). By definition, we have
\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)]=\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}
, where the expected

. Then we can show a similar result as in Lemmas

and
2

{\widehat{T}}
given

Lemma 3
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
,
for any measurable set

, if
\textnormal{ Leb}(R)+(1-\textnormal{Leb}(R))\cdot\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)\mid\xi\not\in R]>\delta
,

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{R}\}\leq\alpha
, where
\textnormal{Leb}(R)
denote the Lebesgue measure of
R
.

The proof of Lemma
3
is provided in the appendix. With this lemma in place, we are ready to bound the power.
Fixing any integer
M\geq 1
, standard measure-theoretic arguments (e.g., Lemma 2.5 of Chapter 2 in
Bennett and Sharpley, (1988)
) allow us to construct a partition
[0,1]=S_{1}\cup\dots\cup S_{M}
,
where
S_{i}
is measurable and has Lebesgue measure
1/M

\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)\mid\xi\in S_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)]=\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}
, for each

.

Next, fix any integer
M_{0}\leq M
with
M_{0}>M\cdot\frac{\delta-\delta_{\epsilon}^{*}}{1-\delta_{\epsilon}^{*}}

I\subset[M]
with
|I|=M_{0}

,
and observe that
\textnormal{Leb}(R_{I})=M_{0}/M>\frac{\delta-\delta_{\epsilon}^{*}}{1-\delta_{\epsilon}^{*}}
by construction, and
\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)\mid\xi\not\in R_{I}]=\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}
by construction of the partition
S_{1}\cup\dots\cup S_{M}
.
Then by Lemma
3

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{R_{I}}\}\leq\alpha
.
Now we sum over all subsets
I

\sum_{I\subset[M],|I|=M_{0}}\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{R_{I}}\}=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathds{1}}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\cdot\!\!\!\!\sum_{I\subset[M],|I|=M_{0}}\!\!\!\!{\mathds{1}}\{\mathcal{E}_{R_{I}}\}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\cdot{M-\lfloor\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}\rfloor\choose M_{0}},

where the inequality holds as follows: defining
\widehat{I}\subset[M]
as the
set of all indices
i\in[M]

\{|\mathcal{D}^{(r)}_{\ell}|=n
and
\xi^{(r)}\in S_{i}\}
holds for some

we have
\sum_{I\subset[M],|I|=M_{0}}{\mathds{1}}\{\mathcal{E}_{R_{I}}\}={M-|\widehat{I}|\choose M_{0}}
. And, due to the computational constraint,
there are at most
\lfloor\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}\rfloor

and so we must have

Combining all our calculations, then,

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\frac{\sum_{I\subset[M],|I|=M_{0}}\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1;\mathcal{E}_{R_{I}}\}}{{M-\lfloor\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}\rfloor\choose M_{0}}}\leq\frac{{M\choose M_{0}}\cdot\alpha}{{M-\lfloor\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}\rfloor\choose M_{0}}}.

\mathbb{P}\{{\widehat{T}}=1\}\leq\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}

Fix any

Define a new distribution

P^{\prime}=c\cdot(P_{X}\times\delta_{y})+(1-c)\cdot P,

P_{X}

X
under

y

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D};\xi),&\textnormal{ otherwise},\end{cases}

denotes that at least one of the response values in the data points contained in
\mathcal{D}
is equal to
y
,
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathcal{D};\xi)

\mathcal{D}

n

.

Next we verify that
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P^{\prime},n)
is not stable. Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}

n

P^{\prime}

Let

P
.

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}_{P^{\prime}}\{|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)(X_{n+1})-\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathcal{D}_{n-1};\xi)(X_{n+1})|>\epsilon\}\geq\left[1-(1-c)^{n}\right]+(1-c)^{n}\delta^{*}_{\epsilon},

where the last step holds by validity of
{\widehat{T}}
(
3
), since
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},P^{\prime},n)
is not
(\epsilon,\delta)
-stable.
We have established that this is true for any

\mathbb{P}_{P}\{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1;\mathcal{E}_{y}\}\leq\inf_{c>1-\left(\frac{1-\delta}{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}\right)^{1/n}}\alpha(1-c)^{-N_{\ell}}=\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{N_{\ell}/n}.

\leq\alpha+{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}\geq 1-\delta}\right\}.

After marginalizing over
q
, then,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1,q_{c(\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\ell})}=\cdots=q_{c(\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})}_{\ell})}=0}\right\}\\
\leq\alpha+\mathbb{P}\left\{{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}\geq 1-\delta}\right\}.

Now we calculate

\mathbb{P}\left\{{q_{c(\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\ell})}=\cdots=q_{c(\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})}_{\ell})}=0}\ \middle|\ {{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u}),\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})}}\right\}\geq(1-\rho)^{{\widehat{r}}}\geq(1-\rho)^{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}},

with equality for the first step in the case that the values
c(\mathcal{D}^{(1)}),\dots,c(\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})})
are all distinct, and with the last step holding due to the computational constraint
{\widehat{r}}\leq\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1,q_{c(\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{\ell})}=\cdots=q_{c(\mathcal{D}^{({\widehat{r}})}_{\ell})}=0}\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1}\right\}\cdot(1-\rho)^{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}.

Combining all our calculations, and rearranging terms,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1}\right\}\leq(1-\rho)^{-\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}\left[\alpha+\mathbb{P}\left\{{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}\geq 1-\delta}\right\}\right].

Now for our final step, we need to bound this probability. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}}\right]=(1-\rho)\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\\
=(1-\rho)\mathbb{P}\left\{{|[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi)](X_{n+1})-[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{n-1},\xi)](X_{n+1})|\leq\epsilon}\right\}=(1-\rho)(1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}),

by definition of
p_{\mathbf{i}}
.
And, by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}\geq(1-\rho)(1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon})+t}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}p_{\mathbf{i}}^{2}}\right\}

for all
t>0
, since
\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}
is a sum of independent terms, with the term indexed
\mathbf{i}
lying in
p_{\mathbf{i}}
.
Finally,
\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}p_{\mathbf{i}}^{2}\leq\|p\|_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\leq\|p\|_{\infty}
. Combining with the calculation above, then,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbf{I}_{n,M}}(1-q_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot p_{\mathbf{i}}\geq(1-\rho)(1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon})+t}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{2t^{2}}{\|p\|_{\infty}}\right\},

and therefore, if we choose
\rho>\frac{\delta-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1}\right\}\leq(1-\rho)^{-\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}\left[\alpha+\exp\left\{-\frac{2(\rho(1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon})-(\delta-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}))^{2}}{\|p\|_{\infty}}\right\}\right].

Setting
\rho=\frac{\delta-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{n}}{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{{\widehat{T}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1}\right\}\leq\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta-\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{B_{\textnormal{train}}}{n}}\left[\alpha+\exp\left\{-\frac{2}{n^{2}\|p\|_{\infty}}\right\}\right].

Finally, since we have assumed
\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}P(\{(x,y)\})\leq\gamma<0.2
, we can take
M=6
in the following lemma so that
\|p\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2.5})
:

Lemma 4
.

Fix any
M\geq 2
. Suppose
\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}P(\{(x,y)\})\leq\gamma
for some constant
0\leq\gamma<\frac{1}{M-1}
.
Then there exists a partition

such that, for a dataset

where

is a constant depending only on
M
and
\gamma
.

This completes the proof.

Proof of Proposition
1

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem 1 in
Kim and Barber, (2023)
, we include it here for completeness.
First, we have
B\sim\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta^{*}_{\epsilon})
, where
\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}
is defined in (
7
). Now, we verify the validity of this test. Fix any
(\mathcal{A},P,n)
such that
\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}>\delta
, then

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}({\widehat{T}}_{\textnormal{Binom}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1)&=\mathbb{P}(B<k_{*})+a_{*}\mathbb{P}(B=k_{*})\\
&=(1-a_{*})\mathbb{P}(B<k_{*})+a^{*}\mathbb{P}(B\leq k_{*})\\
&\overset{(a)}{\leq}(1-a_{*})\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)<k_{*})+a_{*}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)\leq k_{*})\\
&=\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)<k_{*})+a_{*}\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)=k_{*})\\
&\overset{(b)}{=}\alpha,\end{split}

where (a) is because

\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)

k_{*}

Next, let us compute the power when

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Binomial}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor,\delta)=0)=(1-\delta)^{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}>\alpha

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}({\widehat{T}}_{\textnormal{Binom}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{D}_{\ell},\mathcal{D}_{u})=1)=a_{*}\mathbb{P}(B=0)=\frac{\alpha}{(1-\delta)^{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}}\cdot(1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon})^{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}=\alpha\left(\frac{1-\delta^{*}_{\epsilon}}{1-\delta}\right)^{\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}.\end{split}

This finishes the proof of this proposition by recalling the definition of

in (

Proof of Theorem

The first three terms in the power bound are the same as the ones appearing

5.2
, except now we define the event

In the setting of Theorem

,
for any

).

Lemma 6
.

Let
M\geq 2
and let
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{M})\in\Delta_{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M}
, where
\Delta_{M}
is the probability simplex. Let
p_{n,\mathbf{q}}
be the PMF of the Multinomial
(n,\mathbf{q})
distribution, and assume
q_{m}>0
for all
m
. Then for all
n\geq 1
,

\max_{\mathbf{i}}p_{n,\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{i})\leq\frac{C_{M}}{\sqrt{n^{M-1}\cdot\prod_{m=1}^{M}q_{m}}},

where the maximum is taken over all
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{M})
where the
i_{m}
’s are nonnegative integers with
\sum_{m}i_{m}=n
, and where the constant
C_{M}
depends only on
M
.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

We will prove the result by induction. First consider the case
M=2
—then the Multinomial
(n,\mathbf{q})
distribution is equivalent to the Binomial
(n,q_{1})
distribution. We will use a CLT argument. Specifically, by the Berry–Esseen theorem,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left\{{\textnormal{Binomial}(n,q_{1})\leq x}\right\}-\Phi\left(\frac{x-n\mu}{\sqrt{n}\sigma}\right)\right|\leq 0.56\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{n}\sigma^{3}}

where
\mu=q_{1}
,
\sigma^{2}=q_{1}(1-q_{1})
, and
\rho=q_{1}(1-q_{1})(q_{1}^{2}+(1-q_{1})^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
are calculated from the moments of the Bernoulli. Therefore,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left\{{\textnormal{Binomial}(n,q_{1})\leq x}\right\}-\Phi\left(\frac{x-nq_{1}}{\sqrt{nq_{1}(1-q_{1})}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{0.56}{\sqrt{nq_{1}(1-q_{1})}}.

Then for any
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})

p_{n,\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{i})=\mathbb{P}\left\{{\textnormal{Binomial}(n,q_{1})=i_{1}}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{{\textnormal{Binomial}(n,q_{1})\leq i_{1}}\right\}-\mathbb{P}\left\{{\textnormal{Binomial}(n,q_{1})\leq i_{1}-\epsilon}\right\}\\
\leq\left(\Phi\left(\frac{i_{1}-nq_{1}}{\sqrt{nq_{1}(1-q_{1})}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{i_{1}-\epsilon-nq_{1}}{\sqrt{nq_{1}(1-q_{1})}}\right)\right)+\frac{1.12}{\sqrt{nq_{1}(1-q_{1})}}.

Taking
\epsilon\to 0
proves that

p_{n,\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{i})\leq\frac{1.12}{\sqrt{nq_{1}(1-q_{1})}}=\frac{1.12}{\sqrt{nq_{1}q_{2}}}.

Setting
C_{2}=1.12
, this completes the proof for the case
M=2
.

Now we proceed by induction. Suppose
M\geq 3
. Let

\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{M-2},q_{M-1}+q_{M})\in\Delta_{M-1}

and

\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}=\left(\frac{q_{M-1}}{q_{M-1}+q_{M}},\frac{q_{M}}{q_{M-1}+q_{M}}\right)\in\Delta_{2}.

Then we can decompose the Multinomial
(n,\mathbf{q})
PMF as follows:

p_{n,\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{i})=p_{n,\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}((i_{1},\dots,i_{M-2},i_{M-1}+i_{M}))\cdot p_{i_{M-1}+i_{M},\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}}((i_{M-1},i_{M})).

The intuition is that first we sample over
M-1
categories (by merging bins
M-1
and
M
into a single bin), then we sample from a Binomial to separate this last merged bin into its two components.
By induction,

p_{n,\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}((i_{1},\dots,i_{M-2},i_{M-1}+i_{M}))\leq\frac{C_{M-1}}{\sqrt{n^{M-2}\cdot\prod_{m=1}^{M-2}q_{m}\cdot(q_{M-1}+q_{M})}}

and

p_{i_{M-1}+i_{M},\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}}((i_{M-1},i_{M}))\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{n\cdot\left(\frac{q_{M-1}}{q_{M-1}+q_{M}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{q_{M}}{q_{M-1}+q_{M}}\right)}}\leq\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{q_{M-1}+q_{M}}}{\sqrt{nq_{M-1}q_{M}}}.

Combining these calculations, and setting
C_{M}=C_{M-1}C_{2}
, we have proved the desired bound.
∎

is a discrete measure supported on
\mathcal{Z}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
. If
P_{1}(\mathcal{Z})\geq 1-\frac{\gamma}{2}
, then by
Dudley et al., (2011, Proposition A.1)
, we can find some
C^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
with
P_{1}(C^{\prime})=1-\frac{\gamma}{2}
.
Then defining

C=(\mathcal{Z}\backslash C^{\prime})\cup\mathcal{Z}_{0}
, we have

P_{1}(C)=P_{1}(\mathcal{Z}\backslash C^{\prime})=P_{1}(\mathcal{Z})-P_{1}(C^{\prime}),

where the first step holds since
P_{1}
is nonatomic and so
P_{1}(\mathcal{Z}_{0})=0
. And,

P_{0}(C)=P_{0}(C\cap\mathcal{Z}_{0})=P_{0}(\mathcal{Z}_{0})=P_{0}(\mathcal{Z})=1-P_{1}(\mathcal{Z}),

since
C\supseteq\mathcal{Z}_{0}
, and
P_{0}
is supported on
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
. Therefore,

P(C)=P_{0}(C)+P_{1}(C)=1-P_{1}(C^{\prime})=\frac{\gamma}{2}.

This completes the proof for this case.

If instead
P_{1}(\mathcal{Z})<1-\frac{\gamma}{2}
, then we have
P_{0}(\mathcal{Z})>\frac{\gamma}{2}
. Since
P_{0}
is a discrete distribution, we can therefore find a finite set of distinct points
z_{1},\dots,z_{K}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{0}(\{z_{k}\})>\frac{\gamma}{2}
. Without loss of generality assume that

P(\{z_{1}\})\geq\dots\geq P(\{z_{K}\}).

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{K}P(\{z_{k}\})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{0}(\{z_{k}\})>\frac{\gamma}{2}
, the integer

K_{0}=\min\left\{k_{0}:\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}}P(\{z_{k}\})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\right\}

is well-defined (i.e., the set above is nonempty).
Now define
C=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{K_{0}}\}
. By definition of
K_{0}
we have

P(C)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}P(\{z_{k}\})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}.

To complete the proof we need to show that
P(C)\leq\gamma
. If
K_{0}=1
, then

P(C)=P(\{z_{1}\})\leq\gamma,

by assumption. If instead
K_{0}\geq 2
, then

P(C)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}P(\{z_{k}\})=P(\{z_{K_{0}}\})+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}-1}P(\{z_{k}\})<\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}=\gamma,

where the last step holds since
P(\{z_{K_{0}}\})\leq P(\{z_{1}\})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}-1}P(\{z_{k}\})
, and we must have
\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}-1}P(\{z_{k}\})<\gamma/2
(since if not, this would contradict the definition of
K_{0}
). This completes the proof.
∎
