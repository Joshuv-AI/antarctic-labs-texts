Flagged Perturbations and Anchored Resolutions
Keller VandeBogert

Abstract
. In this paper, we take advantage of a reinterpretation of differential modules admitting a flag structure as a special class of perturbations of complexes. We are thus able to leverage the machinery of homological perturbation theory to prove strong statements on the homological theory of differential modules admitting additional auxiliary gradings and having infinite homological dimension. One of the main takeaways of our results is that the category of differential modules is much more similar than expected to the category of chain complexes, and from the K-theoretic perspective such objects are largely indistinguishable. This intuition is made precise through the construction of so-called anchored resolutions, which are a distinguished class of projective flag resolutions that possess remarkably well-behaved uniqueness properties in the (flag-preserving) homotopy category. We apply this theory to prove an analogue of the Total Rank Conjecture for differential modules admitting a ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} -grading in a large number of cases.

This reinterpretation of projective flags in terms of perturbations is useful since it allows us study projective flag resolutions in the context of homological perturbation theory, which seeks to transfer properties such as homotopy equivalence between perturbed objects. 1.2.
Uniqueness of Anchored Resolutions

Assume for the moment that
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbf{k})
is a local ring (or positively-graded
\mathbf{k}
-algebra). In
[
BE22
]
, Brown–Erman defined the notion of a minimal free resolution of a differential module. Their goal was to initiate the development a homological theory of differential modules that mimicked the classical theory for modules, motivated by the connection they had noticed between differential modules and the more general theory of Tate resolutions over toric varieties.

One of the first steps in this program would be to define the notion of a
minimal
free resolution of a differential module in such a way that these resolutions exist and are
unique
up to isomorphism. This turns out to be a nontrivial task: although free flag resolutions always exist, it is not always possible to choose a free flag resolution that is minimal (in the sense that the differential has entries in the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
). Brown–Erman’s solution to this problem was to instead define a minimal free resolution of a differential module
D
as a quasi-isomorphism of differential modules
F\xrightarrow{\sim}D
, where the differential of
F
is minimal and the ambient
R
-module is free, but also defined in such a way that there is a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widetilde{F}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

where
\widetilde{F}
is a free flag resolution of
D
, and the inclusion
F\hookrightarrow\widetilde{F}
is a split inclusion with contractible cokernel. This definition of a minimal free resolution satisfies both of the conditions posed above: they exist and are unique up to isomorphism. However, one potential shortcoming of this definition is that a minimal free resolution of a differential module is not necessarily a free flag anymore. This fact is rather inconvenient since direct summands of free flags can exhibit some pathological behavior that is not possible for free flags themselves (see Example
4.4
). One of our motivating goals for this paper is to instead define a notion of minimal resolution that exists, is unique, but
also
is a free flag resolution:

Definition 1.3
.

An anchored resolution
F
is
quasiminimal
if the anchor is a minimal free resolution.

Interestingly, Brown–Erman had already proved in
[
BE22
, Theorem 3.2]
that anchored resolutions exist, a fact which they called “Degeneration to the homology”. In fact, their proof of existence can alternatively be interpreted as an application of the perturbation lemma. The advantage of using the perturbation lemma to prove this result is that we can prove a functorial version of Brown–Erman’s “Degeneration to the homology”, which, combined with Theorem
1.2
yields the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume
D
is a differential module with finitely generated homology. Then
D
admits a quasiminimal anchored resolution, and any such resolution is unique up to isomorphism.

Moreover, every anchored resolution is unique up to flag-preserving homotopy and contains the quasiminimal anchored resolution as a (flag-preserving) direct summand.

Thus, Theorem
1.4
shows that quasiminimal anchored resolutions in the category of differential modules are the direct analogues of the classical notion of minimal free resolutions in the category of
R
-modules. Theorem
1.4
also removes all finiteness assumptions on the projective dimension of the homology imposed in
[
BE22
]
, and also makes no assumptions on the ambient grading nor the degree of the differential in the homogeneous case. It also shows that one does not need to leave the category of free flags in order to have a well-behaved notion of minimal free resolutions for differential modules, nor make any finiteness assumptions on the projective dimension of the homology.

1.3.
A Total Rank Conjecture for Free Flags

In the work
[
ABI07
]
, Avramov–Buchweitz–Iyengar recognized the significance of projective flags to understanding and lending new perspectives on the classical rank conjectures arising in the context of algebraic topology
[
Hal85
,
Car86
]
. One of their main results was the establishment of lower bounds on the
projective class
of a differential module
D
, which can be thought of as the analogue of projective dimension, purely in terms of the height of the annihilator
\operatorname{Ann}_{R}H(D)
. They furthermore conjectured that for any projective flag
D
, there is an inequality

\operatorname{rank}_{R}D\geqslant 2^{c},\quad\text{where}\ c:=\operatorname{codim}_{R}\operatorname{Ann}_{R}H(D).

This conjecture is now known to be false with counterexamples being provided (even in the category of chain complexes) by Iyengar–Walker
[
IW18
]
, but it still begs the question: is there an analogue of the Total Rank Conjecture in the category of differential modules?

One of the reasons this question can now be tackled is that the existence/uniqueness of anchored resolutions implies that the category of differential modules is largely indistinguishable from the category of chain complexes, at least from the K-theoretic perspective. This is crucial since the techniques of Walker
[
Wal17
,
VW23
]
used to prove the Total Rank Conjecture for resolutions of modules heavily take advantage of an understanding of certain K-theoretic operations on the full subcategory of perfect complexes with finite length homology.

Before stating the theorem, we need to define two types of objects. Firstly, a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded projective flag is simply a projective flag that also admits a splitting

F=F_{0}\oplus F_{1},

where the differential of
F
has
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-degree
1
(for the more precise definition see Definition
A.9
). The homology of any such
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded object splits into a degree
0
and
1
portion. Secondly, we will say that a differential module is
homologically finite
if
\operatorname{pd}_{R}H(D)<\infty
. Then we have the following analogue of the Total Rank Conjecture for differential modules:

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume
R
is either

(1)

A quasi-Roberts ring (such as any locally complete intersection) with no
2
-torsion, or

(2)

any ring of equicharacteristic
\neq 2
.

Let
D
be any homologically finite
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded projective flag with
H(D)=H_{0}(D)
.
3
3

3

One can instead assume
H(D)=H_{1}(D)
and the theorem still holds.
Then there is an inequality

\operatorname{rank}_{R}D\geqslant 2^{c},

where

.

Note that if one does not assume

then Theorem

]

2.
Flagged Perturbations of Complexes

In this section, we initiate our study of projective flags through the lens of homological perturbation theory. We first begin by recalling the notions of perturbation and two versions of the perturbation lemma: the first version (Lemma

) is the more well-known version that applies to deformation retracts, but the second version (Lemma

) originally due to Huebschmann–Kadeishvili

]
applies to arbitrary homotopy equivalences. We then extend a construction of Stai
[

]

-graded setting, and in turn use this to define

-graded Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions (Definition

). These Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions will be a convenient “standard resolution” that we will continually use as an intermediary object between anchored resolutions and arbitrary differential modules.

Subsection

proves the aforementioned functorial version of the “Degeneration to the homology” proved by Brown–Erman
[
BE22
]
in the
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded setting by taking advantage of the fact that Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions are obtained as flagged perturbations of particularly simple complexes. For full background on

-graded differential modules/flag structures, see the Appendix.

2.1.
The Perturbation Lemma and its Naturality

In this subsection, we recall some of the more standard facts of homological perturbation theory. For more details and many different variants of the perturbation lemma, see, for instance,
[

,

,

,

,

]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D\in\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
. A

\delta
of
D
is any morphism of the underlying
R
-module of

(d^{D}+\delta)^{2}=0.

A perturbation
\delta
is

-graded if the resulting differential

is
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded. The notation

will denote the perturbed differential module, equipped with the new differential

.

Remark 2.2
.

We do not assume as in
[

]
that the perturbation
\delta
has the same degree as the differential

of the differential module

. Since the computations involved in the proof of the perturbation lemma are completely formal in nature and only use the fact that the differential squares to

[

]
), but we will have no need for this level of generality here.

Example 2.3
.

As mentioned previously, the main point of the perturbation lemma is to be able to transfer perturbations along homotopy equivalences. The most well-known version of the perturbation lemma is actually stated for a particularly well-behaved type of homotopy equivalence, known as a (strong) deformation retract.

0&0&\nu\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}d^{F^{B(D^{\prime})}}\nu&-d^{F^{B(D^{\prime})}}s+\alpha_{D^{\prime}}\psi&d^{F^{B(D^{\prime})}}\nu-d^{F^{B(D^{\prime})}}s^{\prime}-\alpha_{D^{\prime}}h+\nu+\gamma_{D^{\prime}}\nu\\
0&d^{F^{H(D^{\prime})}}\psi&-d^{F^{H(D^{\prime})}}h+\beta_{D^{\prime}}\nu\\
0&0&d^{G^{B(D)}}\nu\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\nu d^{F^{B(D)}}&sd^{G^{B(D)}}+\nu\alpha_{D}&\nu d^{F^{B(D)}}+\nu-s\beta_{D}+\nu\gamma_{D}-s^{\prime}d^{G^{B(D)}}\\
0&\psi d^{F^{H(D)}}&-hd^{G^{B(D)}}+\psi\beta_{D}\\
0&0&\nu d^{G^{B(D)}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\nu&-s&\nu-s^{\prime}\\
0&\psi&-h\\
0&0&\nu\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d^{F^{B(D)}}&\alpha_{D}&\operatorname{id}_{F^{B(D)}}+\gamma_{D}\\
0&d^{F^{H(D)}}&\beta_{D}\\
0&0&d^{G^{B(D)}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, it remains to verify that the morphism
\Phi
is compatible with the augmentation maps
\eta:G\to D
,
\eta^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to D^{\prime}
. Recall that the maps
\nu
and
\psi
were chosen from the start to be induced by the restriction of the initial map
\varphi:D\to D^{\prime}
to the cycles and boundaries of
D
and
D^{\prime}
. However, the differentials of
G
and
G^{\prime}
are built from perturbing horseshoe resolutions of the underlying modules of
D
and
D^{\prime}
by an additional identity map (as in Construction

). Moreover, the morphism

is just a perturbation of the morphism of horseshoe resolutions:

\begin{pmatrix}\nu&-s&\nu-s^{\prime}\\

0&0&\nu\end{pmatrix},

and this morphism has been constructed precisely so that it extends the morphism of underlying

-modules

. Thus by construction the diagram of the lemma commutes, completing the proof.
∎

(Uniqueness of Cartan–Eilenberg Resolutions Up To Homotopy)

If

2.3.
Degeneration to the Homology via the Homological Perturbation Lemma

In this section we use the perturbation lemma to deduce that anchored resolutions can be obtained explicitly as deformation retracts of Cartan–Eilenberg resolutions. Combining the naturality of the perturbation lemma with Lemma

actually yields the flag-preserving analogue of Lemma

not

is only an existence statement – we still need to prove that we can obtain

anchored resolution as a deformation retract of a Cartan–Eilenberg resolution in order to deduce Theorem

. A dg-analogue of this degeneration to the homology can also be found in work of Keller
[

Theorem 2.23

(Brown–Erman Degeneration to the Homology)
.

Let

\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential module and let

Remark 2.24
.

In the case

Proof.

As in Construction

2.4.
Inheritance Properties of Flagged Perturbations and their Anchors

In this section, we explore some additional consequences of the perturbation that seem to be extremely nontrivial to try to prove directly. The following theorem can intuitively be stated as saying that projective flags inherit homotopy equivalences and deformation retracts coming from the anchor. This further supports the terminology “anchor” as we see that any kind of property (up to homotopy) of the anchor will also be inherited by the projective flag because of the perturbation lemma.

Theorem 2.30
.

Let

anchored on

is a deformation retract of
D

Proof.

Proof of (1):
This is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.11

Proof of (2):
By Remark

is of no loss of generality to assume that

is a strong deformation retract of

, whence the result follows from the standard perturbation lemma (Lemma

Remark 2.31
.

Theorem

Assume

3.
Anchored Resolutions and Flag-Preserving Morphisms

In this section, we introduce anchored resolutions and prove that these objects are indeed unique up to flag-preserving homotopy. As mentioned in the Introduction, this gives a notion of quasiminimal resolutions which are the “best of both worlds” in the sense that they always exist, are unique, and

3.14
), which in turn is the consequence of a more general fact that realizes every anchored resolution as a flag-preserving deformation retract of a Cartan–Eilenberg resolution.

Theorem 3.9
.

Every
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded anchored projective resolution is a flag-preserving deformation retract of a Cartan–Eilenberg resolution.

Proof.

Let
D
be any projective flag anchored on a resolution
F^{H(D)}
. Then we may construct a Cartan–Eilenberg resolution of
D
as in Corollary
3.8
. The proof then follows by showing that the perturbation lemma applied as in the proof of Theorem
2.23
yields
precisely
the anchored resolution

.

To see this, we use the explicit formulas outlined in Remark
2.25
. Firstly, note that in our notation the map
\gamma:F^{B(D)}\to F^{B(D)}[-1]
restricted to a component
F_{i}^{H(D)}
of
F^{B(D)}
is simply the identity map, sending
F_{i}^{H(D)}
to the same module but with the homological degree dropped by
1
. The map
\alpha:F^{H(D)}\to F^{B(D)}
is the projection
F^{H(D)}\to F_{\geqslant 1}^{H(D)}\subset F^{B(D)}[-1]
, which restricted to a component
F_{i}^{H(D)}
is again just the identity map but viewed as dropping the homological degree by
1
. Finally, the map
\beta:F^{B(D)}\to F^{H(D)}[-1]
restricted to a component
F^{H(D)}_{i}
of
F^{H(D)}_{\geqslant j}
is the map
\delta_{j-1}^{D}:F^{H(D)}_{i}\to F^{H(D)}_{i-j}
.

With these formulas now in hand, the Cartan–Eilenberg resolution of
D
admits a deformation retract onto a perturbation of
F^{H(D)}
with differential given by the formula

\delta_{0}^{D}+\beta(1+(\gamma^{\bullet})+(\gamma^{\bullet})^{2}+\cdots)\alpha.

Let
f_{i}\in D_{i}
; then
\alpha(f_{i})=f_{i}
but viewed in homological degree
i
of
G^{B(D)}
. Applying
(\gamma^{\bullet})^{\ell}
to
f_{i}
again yields the element
f_{i}
, but now viewed in homological degree
i-\ell
of
F_{\geqslant\ell}^{H(D)}
. The map
\beta
restricted to this component is precisely the map
\delta_{\ell-1}^{D}
; it thus follows that the differential of the perturbation of
F^{H(D)}
is the sum

\delta_{0}^{D}+\delta_{1}^{D}+\cdots.

Since this is precisely the differential of the original flag
D
, the result follows.

Remark 3.12
.

Notice that the flag resolution obtained in Theorem
3.11
is not necessarily an anchored resolution, and indeed, it is not always possible to construct an anchored resolution that comes equipped with a flag-preserving augmentation map. Moreover, one cannot just apply the perturbation lemma to the projective flag resolution
\widetilde{F}
naïvely to obtain a smaller projective flag resolution equipped with a flag-preserving augmentation map. This is because the component
\operatorname{id}_{F_{j}^{B_{\ell}^{r}(D)}}:G_{j}^{B_{\ell}^{r}(D)}\to F_{j}^{B_{\ell}^{r}(D)}
drops the filtration degree by
r
, and thus after applying the perturbation lemma we will obtain a differential module
\widetilde{D}
equipped with a filtration that instead satisfies

d^{\widetilde{D}}(\widetilde{D}^{i})\subset\widetilde{D}^{i+r-2},

and likewise the augmentation map
\eta:\widetilde{D}\to D
will satisfy
\eta(\widetilde{D}^{i})\subset D^{i+r-2}
. Thus
\widetilde{D}
does not necessarily have a flag structure after employing the perturbation lemma.

Remark 3.13

Notice that there is a subtle shift in logic that does not allow one to deduce Theorem

4.
Applications to K-Theory of Linear Factorizations and Rank Conjectures

In this section, we finally apply the results on anchored resolutions established throughout this paper to the analogue of the total rank conjecture for free flags. We begin by recalling the definition of a cyclic Adams operation as defined in
[
BMTW17a
,
BMTW17b
]
, as well as some other basic facts related to free flags and their homological properties.

4.1.
Cyclic Adams Operations on
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded Differential Modules

We recall the definition of cyclic Adams operations, as well as establish some more basic properties of free flags. We then introduce the operator
\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}
, which can be viewed as a sort of “derived” eigenspace operator (similar to one introduced in
[
BMTW17a
]
). We then prove that, when restricted to homologically finite differential modules, these operators see no difference between a differential module and its homology after taking Euler characteristic.

We first begin by establishing some standard notation that we will use throughout this section.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
D\in\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
be any
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential module with finite length homology. We use the notation
h_{i}(D):=\ell_{R}(H_{i}(D))
. The
Euler characteristic
is defined via

\chi(D):=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{i}h_{i}(D).

The total homology is defined via

h(D):=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}h_{i}(D).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
D,D^{\prime}\in\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R\operatorname{-proj})
be two projective flags of finite class, where
d\neq 1
. If
D
has finite length homology, then there is an inequality

h(D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime})\leqslant h(D)\cdot\operatorname{rank}_{R}(D^{\prime}).

In particular, the tensor product
D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime}
also has finite length homology.

Proof.

Let
r
denote the class of
D
and define a flag structure on
D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime}
as follows: given an integer
t\geqslant 0
, we can uniquely write
t=i+jr
for
0\leqslant i\leqslant r-1
. Then for any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
define

(D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime})_{t,\ell}:=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{d-1}D_{i,s}\otimes_{R}D^{\prime}_{j,\ell-s}.

The grading here is chosen to “prioritize” the first tensor factor
D
over
D^{\prime}
. Moreover, by the class assumption on
D
combined with the spectral sequence of Lemma
A.23
associated to this filtration, there is a spectral sequence

E^{r+1}_{i+jd,\ell}=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{d-1}H_{i,s}(D)\otimes_{R}D^{\prime}_{j,\ell-s}\implies H_{\ell}(D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime}).

Thus
H(D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime})
is a subquotient of a finite length
R
-module, and taking
R
-module lengths yields the desired inequality.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that even though the
r
th page of the above spectral sequence consists of direct sums of terms of the form
H_{i,s}(D)\otimes_{R}D^{\prime}_{j,\ell-s}
, the differentials are not literally of the form
\operatorname{id}_{H_{i,s}(D)}\otimes d^{D^{\prime}}
.

This is prominent in the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-perturbation of the Koszul complex given in Example
2.3
. In a little more detail, view
K_{\bullet}
as the exterior algebra on a free
R
-module with basis
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
, where the differential is induced by sending
e_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
. At some point in the associated spectral sequence, we must compute the homology of a complex of the form

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{k}\otimes_{R}K_{3}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{k}\otimes_{R}K_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{k}\otimes_{R}K_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{k}\otimes_{R}K_{0}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}

If the map
\sigma
were merely induced by
\mathbf{k}\otimes\delta
(where
\delta
is the original perturbation of
K_{\bullet}
) then we would find that there is an equality
h(K_{\delta}\otimes_{R}K_{\delta})=6
, which would be a contradiction to the proof of Theorem
4.15
proved later. Instead, this map is induced by the differential of
K_{\delta}\otimes_{R}K_{\delta}
applied to the element

e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3}\otimes 1-\Delta_{2,1}(e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3})+\Delta_{1,2}(e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3})-1\otimes e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3}\in(K_{\delta}\otimes_{R}K_{\delta})_{3},

where
\Delta_{i,j}:\bigwedge^{i+j}\to\bigwedge^{i}\otimes\bigwedge^{j}
is a homogeneous component of the exterior algebra comultiplication. The induced page differential would be the image of the component of the above element mapping to
K_{0}\otimes_{R}K_{0}
, which is precisely

\delta(e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3})\otimes 1-1\otimes\delta(e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3})=1\otimes 1-1\otimes 1=0.

Thus
\sigma=0
and there is actually an equality
h(K_{\delta}\otimes K_{\delta})=h(K_{\delta})\operatorname{rank}_{R}(K_{\delta})=8
.

The reason we mention this is that, classically, the equality
h(D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime})=h(D)\operatorname{rank}_{R}D^{\prime}
for
complexes
of free modules implies that the homology
H(D)
must annihilate all entries of the differentials of
D^{\prime}
. This detail is a key fact used to prove the “extended” formulation of the Total Rank Conjecture
[
VW23
, Conjecture 1.11]
, which ensures that the smallest possible resolution with finite length homology is in fact the Koszul complex on a system of parameters. In the
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded setting (with
d>1
) the most we can say is that the equality
h(D\otimes_{R}D^{\prime})=h(D)\operatorname{rank}_{R}D^{\prime}
implies that the homology
H(D)
annihilates the differentials of the
anchor
of
D^{\prime}
.

Example 4.4
.

Continuing with the perturbation of the Koszul complex as in Example
2.3
(and Remark
4.3
above), we can also see that Lemma
4.2
fails even when
D
and
D^{\prime}
are assumed to be deformation retracts of free flags. It is straightforward to verify that the perturbation
K_{\delta}
admits a deformation retract onto the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential module
D
with

D_{1}=R^{\oplus 3}\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}-x_{2}&-x_{3}&0\\
x_{1}&0&-x_{3}\\
0&x_{1}&x_{2}\end{pmatrix}}D_{0}=R^{\oplus 3}\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}x_{3}&x_{2}x_{3}&x_{3}^{2}\\
-x_{1}x_{2}&-x_{2}^{2}&-x_{2}x_{3}\\
x_{1}^{2}&x_{1}x_{2}&x_{1}x_{3}\end{pmatrix}}D_{1}

Then it is a consequence of the discussion in Remark
4.3
that
h(D\otimes_{R}D)=8
, whereas
h(D)\operatorname{rank}_{R}(D)=6
, so the inequality of Lemma

fails even for well-behaved summands of free flags (these homology computations can also be verified directly using the package
[
FPTV24
]
in Macaulay2
[
GS97
]
).

Now we recall the definition of cyclic Adams operations for
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential modules (that is, linear factorizations of
0
). For full details, see
[
BMTW17a
]
:

Definition 4.5
.

Let
p>0
be any prime. Let
P
be a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded
R
-complex, where
R
is any ring such that
p
is invertible and
R
contains a primitive
p
th root of unity, denoted
\zeta
. The symmetric group
\Sigma_{p}
acts on the
p
th tensor power
T^{p}(P):=P^{\otimes p}
by graded commutators:

(i\ i+1)\cdot(x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{i}\otimes x_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{p})=(-1)^{|x_{i}|\cdot|x_{i+1}|}x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{i+1}\otimes x_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{p}.

There is an embedding of the cyclic group
C_{p}\cong\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\subset\Sigma_{p}
sending
1\mapsto\sigma:=(1\ 2\ \dots\ p)
; this action induces a well-defined endomorphism of complexes

\sigma:T^{p}(P)\to T^{p}(P),

in which case we may consider the
\zeta^{i}
eigenspaces of
T^{p}(P)
for each
0\leqslant i\leqslant p-1
, denoted
T^{p}(P)^{(\zeta^{i})}
. The
p
th
cyclic Adams operator

\psi^{p}_{cyc}
is the map

\psi^{p}_{cyc}:K_{0}(R)\to K_{0}(R),\quad\text{defined by}

\psi^{p}_{cyc}([P]):=[T^{p}(P)^{(1)}]-[T^{p}(P)^{(\zeta)}].

To define the cyclic Adams operations
\psi^{k}_{cyc}
for composite integers
k
, one writes
k=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdot p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{e_{\ell}}
as a product of prime numbers and sets

\psi^{k}_{cyc}:=\left(\psi^{p_{1}}_{cyc}\right)^{e_{1}}\circ\left(\psi^{p_{2}}_{cyc}\right)^{e_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ\left(\psi^{p_{\ell}}_{cyc}\right)^{e_{\ell}}.

This is well-defined by
[
BMTW17b
, Section 4]
.

In the work
[
BMTW17b
]
, the authors show that the cyclic Adams operations satisfy the “Gillet–Soulé axioms” for Adams operations, even on categories of linear factorizations. Although their definition of a cyclic Adams operation requires the existence of roots of unity/invertibility of certain elements, it has the significant advantage that one can easily extend these Adams operations to act on more general objects than just chain complexes. Moreover, the requirement that roots of unity exist will be of no real significance for our purposes, since this may always be assumed up to taking some faithfully flat extension.

In the following theorem, the notation
\psi^{k}
denotes the Adams operation of Gillet–Soulé
[
GS87
]
and the notation
K_{0}(R)
denotes the Grothendieck group of chain complexes of free
R
-modules.

Theorem 4.6

(
[
BMTW17a
, Corollary 6.14]
)
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
be any integer and assume
R
is a local Noetherian ring containing all primitive
t
-th roots of unity for
t\leqslant k
, with
k!
invertible. Then there is an equality of operators on
K_{0}(R)
:

\psi^{k}_{cyc}=\psi^{k}.

The above theorem will be essential for the proofs in Subsection
4.2
. This is because, even though we know how to take cyclic Adams operations of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential modules, we then need to be able to understand how these operators interact after taking Euler characteristic/Dutta multiplicities. The cases in which these interactions are best understood are for the Gillet–Soulé version of the Adams operation, whence Theorem
4.6
will be our tool for freely interchanging between the two notions of Adams operation.

Next, we must introduce a “derived” version of the eigenspace operator
P\mapsto T^{p}(P)^{(\zeta)}
for some root of unity
\zeta
. A very similar operator was introduced in
[
BMTW17a
]
when the ambient ring was assumed to be regular — this assumption bypassed any of the subtleties related to the difference between quasi-isomorphism versus homotopy equivalence for linear factorizations over arbitrary local rings. However, now that the notion of an anchored resolution gives us a particularly well-behaved class of free flag resolutions, we can extend the definition to arbitrary rings using this machinery.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
be any integer and assume
R
is a local Noetherian ring containing a primitive
k
th root of unity, with
k
invertible. Given any (not necessarily primitive)
k
th root of unity
\zeta
, let
\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}:D_{\operatorname{DM}}^{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}(R)\to K(\operatorname{Flag}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R\operatorname{-proj}))
denote the functor defined as follows:

\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}(D):=T^{k}(P)^{(\zeta)},

where
P\xrightarrow{\sim}D
is any anchored free resolution of
D
.

Note that the uniqueness of anchored resolutions up to homotopy combined with the lifting properties of Theorem
3.2
guarantee that the above definition of
\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}
is indeed well-defined and does not depend on the quasi-isomorphism class of the underlying differential module.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
D
be any
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential module with finite length homology. Then there is an equality

\chi\left(\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}(D)\right)=\chi\left(\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}(H(D))\right).

Proof.

Let
P\xrightarrow{\sim}D
denote a free flag resolution of
P
anchored on a finite free resolution
F^{H(D)}
of
H(D)
. Then the complex
T^{k}(P)^{(\zeta)}
is anchored on the complex
T^{p}(F^{H(D)})^{(\zeta)}
, and the spectral sequence of Lemma
A.23
ensures that

\chi(T^{p}(P)^{(\zeta)})=\chi(T^{p}(F^{H(D)})^{(\zeta)}).

By definition,
\chi(T^{p}(F^{H(D)})^{(\zeta)})=\chi\left(\mathbf{t}^{p}_{\zeta}(H(D))\right)
.
∎

4.2.
Interactions with Dutta Multiplicities and Euler Characteristic

We first recall/observe some properties of extension of scalars along the Frobenius map when applied
to free flags with finite length homology. In particular, we recall the definition of “Dutta multiplicity”, a notion typically reserved for chain complexes but, as we will see, can be extended to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential modules without much difficulty.

Given a free flag of
R
-modules
P
, we write

F(P)
for the flag of
R
-modules obtained from
P
by extension of scalars along the Frobenius endomorphism
r\mapsto r^{p}
of
R
.
For an integer
e\geqslant 0
, we write
F^{e}(P)
for the
e
-th iterate of this functor.
Concretely, if bases are chosen for each component of
P
, so that the defining endomorphism
d^{P}
is represented by a matrix, then
F^{e}(P)
is isomorphic to the free flag
whose component free modules are the same as those of
P
, but with each entry of
d^{P}
replaced by its
p^{e}
power.

We first need to show that a certain class of limits exists for homologically finite free flags with finite length homology:

Proposition 4.9
.

Assume
(R,\mathfrak{m},k)
is a complete local ring of characteristic
p>0
that has a perfect residue field
\mathbf{k}=R/\mathfrak{m}
. Given a homologically finite
P\in\operatorname{Flag}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R\operatorname{-proj})
with finite length homology and an integer
i\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
, the limit

\lim_{e\to\infty}\frac{h_{i}(F^{e}P)}{p^{e\cdot\dim R}}

exists.

Proof.

Choose any bounded anchored resolution
\widetilde{P}
of
P
. The flag
P
is in fact homotopy equivalent to
\widetilde{P}
and thus it is of no loss of generality to assume that
P
is itself an anchored resolution. By Lemma
A.23
there is a spectral sequence

E^{2}_{r,s}=H_{r}(F^{e}P_{\bullet,s})\implies H_{r+s}(F^{e}P).

Since
D_{\bullet,s}
is a bounded complex of free modules with finite length homology for any
s=0,\dots,d-1
, each of the limits
h_{i}^{\infty}(D_{\bullet,j})
exists (by
[
Rob98
, Theorem 7.3.3]
). Since the homology of
F^{e}P
has a filtration whose associated graded pieces are subquotients of
F^{e}
applied to the anchor, again the result of Roberts implies that the limit
\lim_{e\to\infty}\frac{h_{i}(F^{e}P)}{p^{de}}
exists.
∎

Definition 4.10
.

With
R
as in Proposition
4.9
and any
P\in\operatorname{Flag}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R\operatorname{-proj})
with finite length homology, define

h_{i}^{\infty}(P)=\lim_{e\to\infty}\frac{h_{i}(F^{e}P)}{p^{de}}\quad\text{and}\quad h^{\infty}(P)=\sum_{i}h_{i}^{\infty}(P).

The

Dutta multiplicity
of
P
is

\chi_{\infty}(P)=\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}h^{\infty}_{i}(P)=\lim_{e\to\infty}\frac{\chi(F^{e}P)}{p^{de}}.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
R
be any complete local ring of positive characteristic with perfect residue field. Assume
k\geqslant 1
is a unit and
R
contains a primitive
k
th root of unity. Then for any homologically finite differential module
D
, there is an equality

\chi_{\infty}\left(\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}(D)\right)=\chi_{\infty}\left(\mathbf{t}^{k}_{\zeta}(H(D))\right).

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lemma
4.8
where one instead applies the
e
th iterate of Frobenius to the spectral sequence of Lemma
A.23
and takes limits to deduce the desired equality.
∎

Recall in the following corollary that a ring
R
is
quasi-Roberts
if there is an equality of K-theoretic operators
\chi\circ\psi^{k}=k^{\dim R}\chi
, where
\psi^{k}
denotes the
k
th Adams operator of Gillet–Soulé
[
GS87
]
. Any ring that is locally a complete intersection is quasi-Roberts; work of Miller–Singh
[
MS00
]
constructs an example of a non quasi-Roberts Gorenstein ring.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
R
be a local ring and
k>1
any integer so that
R
contains all
t
-th roots of unity, for
t\leqslant k
and
k!
is a unit. Assume
D
is any bounded, homologically finite free flag with finite length homology.

(1)

Assume that
R
is a quasi-Roberts ring. Then there is an equality

\chi(\psi^{k}_{cyc}(D))=k^{\dim R}\chi(D).

(2)

Assume that
R
is a complete local ring of positive characteristic with perfect residue field. Then there is an equality

\chi_{\infty}(\psi^{k}_{cyc}(D))=k^{\dim R}\chi_{\infty}(D).

Remark 4.13
.

For many K-theoretic purposes, the existence of anchored resolutions implies that the category of differential modules is largely indistinguishable from the category of complexes. A shadow of this fact was used in the work
[
BMTW17a
]
, where the authors proved the equality of Corollary
4.12
(1)
holds for
regular
local rings. The regularity assumption ensured that the boundaries of a linear factorization will also have finite projective dimension. The advantage of having Theorem
2.23
combined with the uniqueness of anchored resolutions is that one now only needs the
homology
to have finite projective dimension to prove similar results.

Remark 4.14
.

If we instead assume that
R
is a regular local ring, then it suffices to assume that
k
itself is a unit in
R
. This follows from the fact that
\psi_{cyc}^{k}([K])=k^{d}[K]
as proved in
[
BMTW17b
]
only under the assumption that
k
is a unit, where
K
denotes the K-class of the Koszul complex on a system of parameters for
R
.

Proof.

Proof of (1):
By definition of the cyclic Adams operation, there is an equality

\chi\circ\psi^{k}_{cyc}(D)=\sum_{\zeta}\zeta\cdot\chi\left(\mathbf{t}_{\zeta}^{k}(D)\right),

and by Lemma
4.8
we have
\chi\left(\mathbf{t}_{\zeta}^{k}(D)\right)=\chi\left(\mathbf{t}_{\zeta}^{k}(H(D))\right)
for any
k
th root of unity
\zeta
. Choosing a finite minimal free resolution
P
of
H(D)
, we thus find that

\chi\circ\psi^{k}_{cyc}(D)=\chi\circ\psi^{k}_{cyc}(P),

which, combined with the assumption on
R
and the fact that
\psi^{k}_{cyc}=\psi^{k}
by Theorem
4.6
, implies the equality

\chi\circ\psi^{k}_{cyc}(D)=\chi\circ\psi^{k}(P)=k^{\dim R}\chi(P).

The result thus follows after noting that
\chi(P)=\chi(D)
.

Proof of (2):
The proof in this case is almost identical, except that the Euler characteristic is replaced with the Dutta multiplicity. Recall that by definition there is an equality

\chi_{\infty}\circ\psi^{k}_{cyc}(D)=\sum_{\zeta}\zeta\cdot\chi_{\infty}\left(\mathbf{t}_{\zeta}^{k}(D)\right),

and by an identical argument to the proof of
(1)
, Corollary
4.11
yields the equality

\chi_{\infty}\circ\psi^{k}_{cyc}=\chi_{\infty}\circ\psi^{k}(P),

where
P\xrightarrow{\sim}H(D)
is any finite free resolution of
H(D)
. Thus the statement reduces to the fact that
\chi_{\infty}\circ\psi^{k}=k^{\dim R}\chi_{\infty}
on the category of perfect complexes with finite length homology, which is a well-known result of Kurano–Roberts
[
KR00
, 3.1]
.
∎

4.3.
The Avramov–Buchweitz–Iyengar Total Rank Conjecture

In this section, we combine all of the results established thus far to prove a version of the Total Rank Conjecture over the category of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded differential modules. We conclude with some further discussion and questions about methods of relaxing hypotheses, as well as a question about the validity of the “extended” total rank conjecture for differential modules (see
[
VW23
, Conjecture 1.11]
).

Theorem 4.15
.

Remark A.20

Over a regular local ring, any quasi-isomorphism between objects whose ambient

Choosing this sequence to be of length

A.3.
Twisting the Categories of Free Flags and an Equivalence with
A_{\infty}
-modules

In this subsection, we introduce a “twisted” version of the categories
\operatorname{Flag}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
and
\operatorname{PFlag}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
. As previously mentioned, the main difference in these two categories comes from a difference in sign convention for the notions of morphisms and homotopies. While this sign convention may seem inconsequential, it turns out that this change in perspective allows us to identify the category of projective flags with a category of
A_{\infty}
-modules over a particularly simple algebra (see Lemma
A.41
). We first introduce the relevant notation for sign conventions that we will use:

Definition A.26
.

Given a homogeneous element
z\in M
of a graded
R
-module, define

\overline{z}:=(-1)^{|z|+1}z,\quad\text{where}\ |z|:=\deg z.

For a nonhomogeneous element, the notation
\overline{z}
simply extends by linearity.

Every graded
R
-module comes equipped with an automorphism
\tau
which is defined by

\tau(z):=-\overline{z}.

Remark A.27
.

One thing to be careful with is that we will often work with objects that have a
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-grading, but the notation
\overline{\cdot}
is defined
only
to take account of the
\mathbb{N}
-grading.

The following proposition is a straightforward verification:

Proposition A.28
.

Let
\varphi
and
\psi
be graded endomorphisms of some
\mathbb{Z}
-graded module
M
. Define
\tau_{*}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(\tau,-)
and
\tau^{*}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(-,\tau)
. Then there are equalities

\tau^{*}(\varphi\circ\psi)=\varphi\circ\tau^{*}(\psi)=-\tau^{*}(\varphi)\circ\overline{\psi},

\tau^{*}(\varphi)\circ\tau^{*}(\psi)=-\varphi\circ\overline{\psi},\quad\text{and}

\tau_{*}\varphi=-\tau^{*}(\overline{\varphi}).

Before defining the categories
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}
and
\operatorname{PFlag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
, it will be useful to define the following variant of the Hom complex (to take into account auxiliary gradings).

Definition A.29
.

Let
C=C_{\bullet,\bullet}
denote a
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded complex, so that for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
the object

C_{\bullet,j}

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded complex. Given two
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded complexes
C
and
D
, define the
bigraded Hom complex

\operatorname{Hom}^{\bullet,\bullet}(C,D)
via

\operatorname{Hom}_{R}^{i,j}(C_{\bullet,\bullet},D_{\bullet,\bullet}):=\prod_{a\in\mathbb{Z},\ b\in\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(C_{a,b},D_{a-i,b-i+j}),

where the second indices are taken modulo
d
, and the differential is the standard differential of the Hom complex (after ignoring the auxiliary
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-grading):

d^{\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(C,D)}(\varphi):=d^{D}\circ\varphi-(-1)^{i}\varphi\circ d^{C},\quad\text{where}\ \varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}^{i,j}_{R}(C,D).

The
endomorphism complex

\operatorname{End}_{R}(C)
is simply defined via
\operatorname{End}_{R}(C):=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(C,C).

Remark A.30
.

Notice that the additional bigrading coming from the auxiliary
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-grading is not what one may initially expect it to be. We have chosen this “totalized” convention for convenience of defining objects in
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
and easily seeing the compatibility with anchors of projective flags.

We can now define the relevant categories of this section:

Definition A.31

(The Categories
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
and
\operatorname{PFlag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
)
.

An object of
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
is the data of a
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\times\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded chain complex
C
equipped with a sequence of maps
\delta_{1}^{C},\dots,\delta_{\ell}^{C},\dots
of
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-degree
-1
satisfying

\delta_{i}^{C}\in\operatorname{End}^{i+1,1}(C),\quad\text{and}\quad d^{C}\delta_{i}^{C}-\overline{\delta_{i}^{C}}d^{C}=-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\delta_{j}^{C}}\delta_{i-j}^{C}\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 1.

(A.1)

A morphism
\varphi:(C,\delta^{C})\to(D,\delta^{D})
is a sequence of elements
\varphi_{0},\dots,\varphi_{\ell},\dots
that satisfy

\varphi_{i}\in\operatorname{Hom}^{i,0}(C,D),\quad d^{D}\varphi_{i}+\overline{\varphi_{i}}d^{C}=-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\overline{\varphi_{j}}\delta_{i-j}+\delta_{j}\varphi_{i-j}\right)\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 0.

(A.2)

The composition of morphisms
\varphi:(C,\delta^{C})\to(D,\delta^{D})
,
\psi:(D,\delta^{D})\to(E,\delta^{E})
is defined via

(\psi\circ\varphi)_{i}:=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\psi_{j}\varphi_{j-i}\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 0.

(A.3)

Two morphisms
\varphi,\psi:(C,\delta^{C})\to(D,\delta^{D})
are homotopic if there is a sequence of maps
\sigma_{0},\dots,\sigma_{\ell},\dots
with

\sigma_{i}\in\operatorname{Hom}^{i-1,-1}(C,D),\quad\varphi_{i}-\psi_{i}=-\sum_{j=0}^{i}\left(\overline{\delta_{j}^{D}}\sigma_{i-j}+\overline{\sigma_{j}}\delta_{i-j}^{C}\right)\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 0,

(A.4)

where the above summation uses the convention that
\delta_{0}^{C}=d^{C}
and
\delta_{0}^{D}=d^{D}
. A nullhomotopy is any morphism homotopic to the
0
map.

Remark A.32
.

An alternative perspective on the category
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}(R)
can be given by the theory of Koszul operads
[
LV12
]
. More precisely, let
D:=R[t]/(t^{2})
where
t
has degree
2
. As described in
[
LV12
, Chapter 10.3.7]
, an object of
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}(R)
is equivalently a
D_{\infty}
-module (that is, a dg module over the cobar complex applied to the quadratic dual coalgebra).

Remark A.33
.

One can reinterpret the equalities of Definition
A.31
as follows: the equation (

) can equivalently be rewritten as

d^{\operatorname{End}(C)}(\delta_{i})=-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\delta_{j}^{C}}\delta_{i-j}^{C}\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 1.

Equation (

) can equivalently be rewritten

d^{\operatorname{Hom}(C,D)}(\varphi_{i})=-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\overline{\varphi_{j}}\delta_{i-j}+\delta_{j}\varphi_{i-j}\right)\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 0,

and equation (

) may be rewritten as

\varphi_{i}-\psi_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left(\overline{\delta_{j}^{D}}\sigma_{i-j}+\overline{\sigma_{i-j}}\delta_{j}^{C}\right)-d^{\operatorname{Hom}(C,D)}(\sigma_{i})\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 0.

These reformulations will be particularly useful, since they allow us to rephrase the existence of certain types of morphisms and homotopies as showing that certain elements are boundaries in the Hom complex.

.

Remark A.35

Unpacking the data of a

As it turns out, when restricted to the category of projective

). Note that we cannot replace

with

Consider the unfolding
\operatorname{Unfold}^{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}(D)

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau}

for all

On morphisms

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}\varphi_{j}(\tau^{*}\delta^{D}_{j-i})=-\sum_{j=0}^{i}(\tau^{*}\delta^{D^{\prime}}_{j})\overline{\varphi_{i-j}}

\displaystyle\implies

.

Notice that the functor
\Phi^{-1}
is always well-defined regardless of whether the ambient
R
-module is projective, but the functor
\Phi
need not be well-defined since the filtration induced by the flag structure is not necessarily split.

We recall some definitions replaced to
A_{\infty}
-algebras and
A_{\infty}
-modules. For in-depth details on the theory of
A_{\infty}
-algebras in general, see, for instance,
[
LH03
,
Kel01
,
Sag10
,
CSLW18
,
Let23
]
. For our purposes, we will be looking at
A_{\infty}
-modules over associative algebras, which still have a surprisingly rich structure relative to just modules.

Definition A.38

(
A_{\infty}
-Algebras and Modules)
.

A (homologically graded, strictly unital, connected)
A_{\infty}
algebra
A
is a nonnegatively-graded
R
-module

A=\bigoplus_{i\geqslant 0}A_{i}

equipped with maps
m_{i}:A^{\otimes i}\to A
of degree
i-2
satisfying the
Stasheff identities
:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s+t,\\
r,t\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r+st}m_{r+t+1}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}\otimes 1^{\otimes t})=0,\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 1.

A left
A_{\infty}
-module
M
over an
A_{\infty}
-algebra
A
is a nonnegatively-graded
R
-module

M=\bigoplus_{i\geqslant 0}M_{i}

equipped with maps
m_{i}^{M}:A^{\otimes i-1}\otimes_{R}M\to M
of degree
i-2
satisfying a similar set of Stasheff identities:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s+t,\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s,t\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r+st}m_{r+t+1}^{M}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}\otimes 1^{\otimes t})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}m_{r+1}^{M}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}^{M})=0,\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 1.

(A.1)

A
morphism of
A_{\infty}
-modules

\varphi:M\to N
is a family of maps

\varphi_{i}:A^{\otimes i-1}\otimes_{R}M\to N

of degree
i-1
such that for each
i\geqslant 1
there is an equality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s+t\\
r\geqslant 0,\ t,s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r+st}\varphi_{r+t+1}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}\otimes 1^{\otimes r})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\varphi_{r+1}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}^{M})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=u+v,\\
u\geqslant 0,\ v\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}m_{1+u}^{N}(1^{\otimes u}\otimes\varphi_{v})\quad\text{for all}\ i\geqslant 1.

(A.2)

Finally, a morphism of
A_{\infty}
-modules
\varphi:M\to N
is
nullhomotopic
if there is a sequence of maps

\sigma_{i}:A^{\otimes i-1}\otimes M\to N

of degree
i
such that for all
i\geqslant 1
there is an equality:

\varphi_{i}=\sum_{s=1}^{i}(-1)^{i-s}m_{i-s+1}^{N}(1^{\otimes i-s}\otimes\sigma_{s})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s+t\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s,t\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r+st}\sigma_{1+r+t}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}\otimes 1^{\otimes t})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s,\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\sigma_{r+1}(1^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}^{M}).

(A.3)

The category of (left)
A_{\infty}
-modules over
A
with the above definition of morphisms is denoted
\operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\infty}}(A)
. If
A
and
M
have a
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-grading, then all of the above data is assumed to have the same degree with respect to the
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-grading.

Remark A.39
.

A_{\infty}
-modules are sometimes referred to as “polydules”, which is terminology arising from the thesis of Lefevre-Hasegawa
[
LH03
]
; see also
[
JMS09
]
.

Remark A.40
.

We assume that all our
A_{\infty}
-algebras, modules, and morphisms are
unital
, implying that the following identities hold:

m_{2}^{M}(1\otimes m)=m,\quad\text{and for}\ i>2,\ m_{i}^{M}(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i-1}\otimes m)=0\quad\text{if}\ a_{j}=1\ \text{for some}\ 1\leqslant j<i.

A similar set of identities holds for morphisms.

As previously noted, there is an equivalence of categories
\operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\infty}}\left(\frac{R[t]}{(t^{2})}\right)\cong\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
. This result also helps explain the origin of the seemingly arbitrary change in the sign convention for defining
\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
.

Lemma A.41
.

View
R[t]/(t^{2})
as a
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
-graded
R
-algebra, where
t
is an indeterminate of bidegree
(2,1)
. Then there is a homotopy-preserving equivalence of categories

\operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\infty}}\left(\frac{R[t]}{(t^{2})}\right)\cong\operatorname{Flag}^{\tau}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R).

Proof.

Notice that the algebra
A:=R[t]/(t^{2})
being viewed as an
A_{\infty}
-algebra with
m_{i}=0
for
i=1
or
i>2
, and
m_{2}
is the standard multiplicative structure on this algebra.

Let
M
be a graded left
A_{\infty}
-module over
A
and define the maps

\delta_{i}^{M}:=m_{i+1}^{M}(t^{\otimes i}\otimes-):M[2i]\to M.

This map must have degree
i-1
and thus by definition induces a degree
0
map

M[2i]\to M[i-1]\implies\delta_{i}^{D}\in\operatorname{End}^{i+1}(M).

Moreover, since
m_{i}(t^{\otimes i})=0
for all
i\geqslant 1
on the algebra
R[t]/(t^{2})
, the Stasheff identities (
A.1
) imply that there is an equality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}m_{r+1}^{M}(t^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}^{M}(t^{\otimes s-1}\otimes-))=0\implies\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\delta_{r}^{M}\circ\delta_{s-1}^{M}=0.

Isolating the terms where
r=0
and
s=1
and reindexing the above sum yields the equality of Equation (
A.1
).

Let
\varphi:M\to N
be a morphism of
A_{\infty}
-modules over
A
. Define the maps

\psi_{i}:=\varphi_{i+1}(t^{\otimes i}\otimes-):M[2i]\to N.

These maps have degree
i
and thus induce degree
0
maps

M[2i]\to N[i]\implies\psi_{i}\in\operatorname{Hom}^{i}(M,N).

The identity (
A.2
) implies that there is an equality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\varphi_{r+1}(t^{\otimes r}\otimes m_{s}^{M}(t^{\otimes s-1}\otimes-))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=u+v,\\
u\geqslant 0,\ v\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}m_{1+u}^{N}(t^{\otimes u}\otimes\varphi_{v}(t^{\otimes v-1}\otimes-))

\implies\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+s\\
r\geqslant 0,\ s\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\psi_{r}\circ\delta_{s-1}^{M}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=u+v,\\
u\geqslant 0,\ v\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}\delta_{u}^{N}\circ\psi_{v-1}.

Again, isolating terms and reindexing the summations yields the equality (
A.2
).

Finally, assume that the morphism of
A_{\infty}
-modules
\varphi:M\to N
is a nullhomotopy via some sequence of degree
i
maps
\sigma_{i}:A^{\otimes i-1}\otimes M\to N
. Define

\psi_{i}:=\varphi_{i+1}(t^{\otimes i}\otimes-):M[2i]\to N,\quad h_{i}:=\sigma_{i+1}(t^{\otimes i}\otimes-):M[2i]\to N.

The map
h_{i}
induces a degree
0
map

M[2i]\to N[i+1]\implies h_{i}\in\operatorname{Hom}^{i-1}(M,N).

Again, a nearly identical argument shows that the identity (
A.3
) reduces to equation (
A.4
).
∎

A.4.
The Category of Homotopy-Commutative Diagrams

In this section, we define the category of homotopy-commutative diagrams of differential modules. Again, this category serves as a formal construction that is quite helpful for understanding the compatibility between homotopy equivalent representatives of objects under many of the standard operations on the category of differential modules, such as mapping cones. Most of the results here are straightforward computations and extensions of known facts over the category of complexes, but we provide details for sake of completeness/convenience of reference.

Definition A.42

(Homotopy-Commutative Diagrams)
.

Let
R
be any commutative ring. Define
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{Ch}(R))
to be the category with objects

\operatorname{Obj}\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)):=\{\text{morphisms of $\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}$-graded differential modules}\ \varphi:C\to D\}.

Morphisms of
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
are defined to be triples
(\psi,\nu,h):(\varphi:C\to D)\to(\varphi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to D^{\prime})
where

•

\psi:C\to C^{\prime}
and
\nu:D\to D^{\prime}
are morphisms of differential modules, and

•

h:C\to D^{\prime}[1]
is a homotopy with
\varphi^{\prime}\psi-\nu\varphi=d^{D^{\prime}}h+hd^{C}
.

In other words, morphisms are represented by diagrams that commute up to some fixed homotopy
h
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{C^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{D^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}

Given morphisms
(\psi,\nu,h):(\varphi:C\to D)\to(\varphi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to D^{\prime})
and
(\psi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},h^{\prime}):(\varphi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to D^{\prime})\to(\varphi^{\prime\prime}:C^{\prime\prime}\to D^{\prime\prime})
the composition is defined as

(\psi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},h^{\prime})\circ(\psi,\nu,h):=(\psi^{\prime}\circ\psi,\nu^{\prime}\circ\nu,\nu^{\prime}\circ h+h^{\prime}\circ\psi),

and the identity map
\operatorname{id}_{\varphi:C\to D}
is the morphism

\operatorname{id}_{\varphi:C\to D}:=(\operatorname{id}_{C},\operatorname{id}_{D},0).

The following is a straightforward and satisfying verification:

Observation A.43
.

Given any commutative ring
R
, the category
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
is well-defined.

Remark A.44
.

Notice that
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
is not equivalently the category of arrows in the homotopy category of
\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)
, since we keep track of the homotopy that makes the corresponding diagram commute. Indeed, in view of the following lemma, we know that this cannot be true since there is no functorial cone construction on the homotopy category.

An advantage of using the category
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
over the standard homotopy category
K(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
is that there is a legitimately functorial construction of the mapping cone in
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
:

Lemma A.45
.

There is a well-defined functor

\operatorname{Cone}:\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))\to\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R)

defined on objects via
\operatorname{Cone}(\varphi:C\to D)=\operatorname{Cone}(\varphi)
, the standard mapping cone in the category of differential modules. On morphisms
(\psi,\nu,h):(\varphi:C\to D)\to(\varphi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to D^{\prime})
it is defined to be the morphism of differential modules:

\operatorname{Cone}(\psi,\nu,h):=\operatorname{Cone}(\varphi)\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}\psi&-h\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}}\operatorname{Cone}(\varphi^{\prime}).

Proof.

The only nontrivial check to perform is that
\operatorname{Cone}
is compatible with the morphisms in
\operatorname{HArrow}(\operatorname{DM}_{\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}}(R))
. Let us first check that
\operatorname{Cone}(\psi,\nu,h)
is a well-defined morphism of differential modules; this comes down the the following computation:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}d^{D^{\prime}}&-\varphi^{\prime}\\
0&-d^{C^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi&-h\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}d^{D^{\prime}}\psi&-d^{D^{\prime}}h-\varphi^{\prime}\nu\\
0&-d^{C^{\prime}}\nu\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\psi d^{D}&hd^{C}-\psi\varphi\\
0&-\nu d^{C}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\psi&-h\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d^{D}&-\varphi\\
0&-d^{C}\end{pmatrix}.

Next, we show that
\operatorname{Cone}
commutes with compositions; let
(\psi,\nu,h):(\varphi:C\to D)\to(\varphi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to D^{\prime})
and
(\psi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},h^{\prime}):(\varphi^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to D^{\prime})\to(\varphi^{\prime\prime}:C^{\prime\prime}\to D^{\prime\prime})
. Then:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cone}(\psi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},h^{\prime})\circ\operatorname{Cone}(\psi,\nu,h)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{\prime}&-h^{\prime}\\
0&\nu^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi&-h\\
0&\nu\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{\prime}\psi&-\psi^{\prime}h-h^{\prime}\nu\\
0&\nu^{\prime}\nu\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cone}((\psi^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},h^{\prime})\circ(\psi,\nu,h)).

∎

The following notation will be employed ubiquitously throughout the paper:

Notation A.46

(Homotopy Equivalences)
.

We will use the notation

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{h,\quad C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{D,\quad h^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

to denote a homotopy equivalence between differential modules
C
and
D
via the homotopies
h
and
h^{\prime}
. More precisely, the maps
p:C\to D
and
i:D\to C
satisfy

\iota\circ p=d^{C}h+hd^{C},\quad p\circ\iota=d^{D}h^{\prime}+h^{\prime}d^{D}.

Lemma A.47
.

Assume that there are homotopy equivalences

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h,\quad C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D,\quad h^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s,\quad C^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{D^{\prime},\quad s^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{\prime}}

and that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{C^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{E^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

commutes up to some homotopy
\sigma
(that is,
(\varphi,\psi,\sigma):(\nu:C\to E)\to(\nu^{\prime}:C^{\prime}\to E^{\prime})
). Then the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{D^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{E^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu^{\prime}\iota^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\prime}\varphi\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

commutes up to the homotopy
(\sigma+\nu^{\prime}h\varphi)\iota
(that is, there is a well-defined morphism
(p^{\prime}\varphi\iota,\psi,(\sigma+\nu^{\prime}s^{\prime}\varphi)\iota):(\nu\iota:D\to E)\to(\nu^{\prime}\iota^{\prime}:D^{\prime}\to E^{\prime})
).

Proof.

This is a straightforward computation:

\displaystyle\nu^{\prime}\circ\underbrace{\iota^{\prime}p^{\prime}}_{=\operatorname{id}_{C^{\prime}}+d^{C^{\prime}}s^{\prime}+s^{\prime}d^{C^{\prime}}}\circ\varphi\iota-\psi\nu\iota=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\nu^{\prime}\varphi}_{=\psi\nu+d^{E^{\prime}}\sigma+\sigma d^{C}}\circ\iota+\nu^{\prime}d^{C^{\prime}}s^{\prime}\varphi\iota+\nu^{\prime}s^{\prime}d^{C^{\prime}}\varphi\iota-\psi\nu\iota

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{E^{\prime}}\sigma\iota+\sigma d^{C}\iota+d^{E^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}s^{\prime}\varphi\iota+\nu^{\prime}s^{\prime}\varphi\iota d^{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{E^{\prime}}(\sigma+\nu^{\prime}s^{\prime}\varphi)\iota+(\sigma+\nu^{\prime}s^{\prime}\varphi)\iota d^{D}.

∎
