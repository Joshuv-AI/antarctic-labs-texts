Introduction

The purpose of this note is to extend the work done in connection with complex quadrature rules for oscillatory integrals

\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)e^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega x}{\mathrm{d}}x.

Evaluation of such integrals via the standard Gaussian quadratures can become extremely expensive numerically for large values of
\omega
, motivating the development of new quadrature rules. It was shown in
[
1
]
that using the zeros of polynomials
p_{n}^{\omega}
which satisfy

\int_{-1}^{1}x^{k}p^{\omega}_{n}(x)h(x)e^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega x}{\mathrm{d}}x=0,\quad k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\},

(1)

where
h(x)=1
identically yields a quadrature rule with ‘good‘ properties that naturally reduces to the usual quadrature rule when
\omega\to 0
. For more on this and different computational methods, see the monograph by Deaño, Huybrechs, and Iserles
[
4
]
. In this note, we will be interested in the asymptotic analysis of the polynomials
p_{n}^{\omega}
arising in the slightly more general situation where

h(x)=h^{*}(x)(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta},\ \alpha,\beta>-1

(2)

and
h^{*}(z)
is holomorphic in a certain region of the plane.

Overview of the Paper

Three regimes, separated by the geometry of the zero-attracting curve associated with
p_{n}^{\omega}
(denoted
\gamma_{\lambda}
), are considered in this work. The main tool for the analysis carried out in all three regimes is the Riemann-Hilbert problem (RHP) for orthogonal polynomials and the Deift-Zhou nonlinear steepest descent method, where the initial RHP is transformed to a normalized RHP with the help of the so-called
g
-function, and a global parametrix and a set of local parametrices are constructed. The details of these constructions differ from one regime to the other, and depend on the geometry of
\gamma_{\lambda}
. With this in mind, Section
2
serves as a quick reminder of results pertaining to the zero-attracting curve associated with
p_{n}^{\omega}
for all possible values of
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
. In Section
3
asymptotic formulas for
p_{n}^{\omega}(z)
are stated for the subcritical, critical, and supercritical regimes for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\gamma_{\lambda}
. Similar formulas can be obtained for
z\in\gamma_{\lambda}
, but such calculation is omitted for brevity. Proof of the formula for the supercritical case is provided in Section
4
, and sketches of the proofs for the subcritical and critical regimes are provided in Sections
5
,
6
, respectively.

This work should be viewed as an extension of the work in
[
3
,
2
]
. Some of the main differences include analyzing polynomials
p_{n}^{\omega}
in the critical case, allowing for more general weights, including ones with an algebraic singularities at the end-points
z=1,z=-1
, and using a different construction of the global parametrix while analyzing the supercritical regime than the one studied in
[
2
]
(compare leading term in (
33
) below with the one obtained in
[
2
, Theorem 2.4]
).

Geometry

Since the weight of orthogonality is complex-valued, it is known that the zeros of
p_{n}^{\omega}
may not accumulate onto the interval
[-1,1]
. It turns out that
[-1,1]
is the zero-attracting curve in the case where the value
\omega
is fixed (see the appendix of
[
3
]
). When
\omega
is allowed to vary with
n
as
\omega=\lambda n,\ \lambda\geq 0
, the situation becomes more interesting as we enter the world of varying orthogonality. The work of Gonchar and Rakhmanov
[
5
]
suggested that one ought to consider a curve
\gamma_{\lambda}
to which
[-1,1]
is deformable and satisfies the S-property:

\dfrac{\partial\left(U^{\mu_{\lambda}}+\mathrm{Re}(V)\right)}{\partial{\bf n}^{+}}(z)=\dfrac{\partial\left(U^{\mu_{\lambda}}+\mathrm{Re}(V)\right)}{\partial{\bf n}^{-}}(z)\quad\forall z\in\gamma_{\lambda},

where
U^{\mu_{\lambda_{\lambda}}}(z):=-\int\log|z-s|{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\lambda}(s)
and
\mu_{\lambda}
is the equilibrium measure on
\gamma_{\lambda}
in the external field
\mathrm{Re}(V)
(in our setting,
V(z)=-{\mathrm{i}}\lambda z
). They further show that such curves are formed by the trajectories of a quadratic differential
-Q_{\lambda}(z)({\mathrm{d}}z)^{2}
where
Q_{\lambda}
is given by

Q_{\lambda}(z)=\left(\int\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\lambda}(s)}{s-z}+\dfrac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}\right)^{2}=\left(\int\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\lambda}(s)}{s-z}-\dfrac{\lambda{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\right)^{2}.

(3)

To obtain a formula for
Q_{\lambda}
, it is common to assume something about the support of
\mu_{\lambda}
to be proven later on. This was done by Deaño, who showed the following: define

\varphi(z):=z+w(z),\quad w(z)=(z^{2}-1)^{1/2},\ z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\gamma_{\lambda},\quad w(z)=z+\mathcal{O}(z)\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty

(4)

and let
\lambda_{cr}
be the unique solution of

2\log\left(\dfrac{2+\sqrt{\lambda_{cr}^{2}+4}}{\lambda_{cr}}\right)-\sqrt{\lambda_{cr}^{2}+4}=0\quad(\lambda_{cr}\approx 1.325...).

(5)

The following theorem appeared in
[
3
]
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
V(z)=-{\mathrm{i}}\lambda z
and
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{cr})
. Then,

1.

there exists a smooth curve
\gamma_{\lambda}
connecting
z=1
and
z=-1
that is a part of the level set

\mathrm{Re}(\phi(z))=0

where

\phi(z)=2\log\varphi(z)+{\mathrm{i}}\lambda w(z).

(6)

2.

The measure

{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\lambda}(z)=-\dfrac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\dfrac{2+{\mathrm{i}}\lambda z}{w(z)}\ {\mathrm{d}}z
is the equilibrium measure on
\gamma_{\lambda}
in the external field
\mathrm{Re}(V(z))
.

3.

\gamma_{\lambda}
has the S-property in the field
\mathrm{Re}(V(z))
.

Remark 1
.

In fact, Deaño’s proof shows that for
\lambda=\lambda_{cr}
,
\gamma_{\lambda}
is a union of two smooth curves that meet at
2{\mathrm{i}}/\lambda_{cr}
.

Remark 2
.

Observe that with this theorem in mind, one can calculate the function
Q_{\lambda}(z)
via Privalov’s lemma and (
3
) and find

Q_{\lambda}(z)=\dfrac{1}{4}\dfrac{(2+{\mathrm{i}}\lambda z)^{2}}{z^{2}-1}.

(7)

As for the supercritical case
\lambda\in(\lambda_{cr},\infty)
, Celsus and Silva showed in
[
2
]
that (most of) the zeros of
p_{n}^{\omega}
accumulate on two disconnected arcs,
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
(which depend on
\lambda
) that appear as trajectories of the quadratic differential
-Q_{\lambda}(z;x_{*})({\mathrm{d}}z)^{2}
where

Q_{\lambda}(z;x):=-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\dfrac{(z-z_{\lambda}(x))(z+\overline{z_{\lambda}(x)})}{z^{2}-1},\quad\text{and}\quad z_{\lambda}(x)=x+\dfrac{2{\mathrm{i}}}{\lambda},

(8)

and
x_{*}
is some special value. More precisely, combining their work with Gonchar and Rakhmanov’s, we deduce that the counting measure associated with
p_{n}^{\omega}
weakly converges to
\mu_{\lambda}
. The density of
\mu_{\lambda}
is given in the following theorem, due to Celsus and Silva
[
2
]
:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\lambda>\lambda_{cr}
and define
Q_{\lambda}(z):=Q_{\lambda}(z,x_{*})
, where
x_{*}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
is the unique value for which

\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{z_{\lambda}(x_{*})}^{1}Q_{\lambda}(s){\mathrm{d}}s\right)=0

and
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}x_{*}(\lambda)=1
. Then, there exist analytic arcs
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
such that
\gamma_{1}
is an arc connecting
-1
to
-\overline{z_{\lambda}(x_{*})}
that lies in the left half-plane,
\gamma_{2}
is the reflection of
\gamma_{1}
across the imaginary axis, and they satisfy

\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{-1}^{z}Q_{\lambda}^{1/2}(s){\mathrm{d}}s\right)=0\quad\forall z\in\gamma_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{z_{\lambda}(x_{*})}^{z}Q_{\lambda}^{1/2}(s){\mathrm{d}}s\right)=0\quad\forall z\in\gamma_{2}.

(9)

The equilibrium measure,
\mu_{\lambda}
in the external field
\mathrm{Re}(V)
has the following density

{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\lambda}(s)=-\dfrac{1}{\pi{\mathrm{i}}}Q_{\lambda}^{1/2}(s)\ {\mathrm{d}}s,\quad s\in\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2},

(10)

where we take the branch of
Q_{\lambda}^{1/2}
holomorphic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})
and behaves as
Q_{\lambda}^{1/2}(z)=\lambda{\mathrm{i}}/2+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-1}\right)
as
z\to\infty.

Schematic representation of critical graph of
-Q_{\lambda}(z)\ ({\mathrm{d}}z)^{2}

, with

2
]
for a proof.

Asymptotics: One-cut Case

Let
\lambda_{cr}
be as in (
5
). In the non-critical case (
\lambda<\lambda_{cr}
), the situation was described completely for
h(x)=1
identically in
[
3
]
. To extend this result to
h(x)
as in (
2
), we need the following Szegő function

S_{h}(z):=\exp\left\{\dfrac{w(z)}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\int_{\gamma_{\lambda}}\dfrac{\log[(w_{+}h)(x)]}{z-x}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}x}{w_{+}(x)}\right\},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\gamma_{\lambda},

(11)

where

is as in (
4
) and
h^{*}(z)
is holomorphic in a neighborhood containing the compact set delimited by
\gamma_{\lambda}\cup[-1,1]
. Properties of

will be discussed in Section
5
.

Theorem 3.1

(
Subcritical Case
\lambda<\lambda_{cr}
).

Let
0\leq\lambda<\lambda_{cr}
and
h(z)
be as above. Then for
n
large enough, polynomials
p_{n}^{\omega}
have degree exactly
n
and locally uniformly for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\gamma_{\lambda}

p_{n}^{\omega}(z)=\left(\dfrac{\varphi(z)}{2}\right)^{n}\exp\left(-\dfrac{{\mathrm{i}}n\lambda}{2\varphi(z)}\right)\left(\dfrac{S_{h}(\infty)}{S_{h}(z)}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)\right)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(12)

When
\lambda=\lambda_{cr}
, the geometry of
\gamma_{\lambda}
changes. More precisely,
\gamma_{\lambda}
is no longer an analytic arc, but rather a union of two analytic arcs, see
[
3
]
. However, by slightly changing the analysis, we may still write an asymptotic formula for
p_{n}^{\omega}
.

Theorem 3.2

(
Critical Case
\lambda=\lambda_{cr}
).

Let
\lambda=\lambda_{cr}
and
h(z)
be as above. Then for
n
large enough, polynomials
p_{n}^{\omega}
have degree exactly
n
and locally uniformly for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\gamma_{\lambda}

p_{n}^{\omega}(z)=\left(\dfrac{\varphi(z)}{2}\right)^{n}\exp\left(-\dfrac{{\mathrm{i}}n\lambda}{2\varphi(z)}\right)\left(\dfrac{S_{h}(\infty)}{S_{h}(z)}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\right)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(13)

We sketch a proof of Theorems
3.1
,
3.2
in Sections
5
,
6
, respectively.

3.2
Asymptotics: Two-cut Case

To present the results when
\lambda>\lambda_{cr}
, we construct the main term of the asymptotics using the approach of
[
6
]
relying on Theta functions, instead of the meromorphic differential approach taken in
[
2
]
. We introduce those here. Let
z_{*}=z_{\lambda}(x_{*})
(see Theorem
2.2
) and

\gamma(z):=\left(\frac{z+\overline{z_{*}}}{z-z_{*}}\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right)^{1/4},\quad z\in\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}),

(14)

where
\gamma(z)
is holomorphic off
\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}
and the branch is chosen so that
\gamma(\infty)=1
. Further, set

A(z)=\frac{\gamma(z)+\gamma^{-1}(z)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad B(z):=\frac{\gamma(z)-\gamma^{-1}(z)}{-2{\mathrm{i}}}.

(15)

The functions
A(z)
and
B(z)
are holomorphic in
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})
,
A(\infty)=1,\quad B(\infty)=0
, and

A_{\pm}(s)=\pm B_{\mp}(s),~~s\in(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})\setminus\{\pm 1,z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}\}.

(16)

3.2.1
Riemann Surface

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
be the Riemann surface associated with the algebraic equation
y^{2}=Q_{\lambda}(z)
, with
Q_{\lambda}
as in Theorem
2.2
. This surface is realized as two copies of
{\mathbb{C}}
cut along
\gamma_{1,2}
and glued together in such a way that the right side of
\gamma_{i}
on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(0)}
, the first sheet, is connected with the left side of the same arc on the second sheet,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(1)}
. Furthermore,
\pi:\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}
be the natural projection. We will denote points on the surface with boldface symbols
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{s}
and their projections by regular script
z,s,t
and
F^{(i)}(z)
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, stands for the pull-back under
\pi({\boldsymbol{z}})
of a function
F({\boldsymbol{z}})
from
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(i)}
into
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})
. Note that for a fixed
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})
, the set
\pi^{-1}(z)
contains exactly two elements, one on each sheet, and we denote by
z^{(k)}
the unique point satisfying
z^{(k)}\in\pi^{-1}(z)\cap\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(k)}
.

Denote by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
a cycle on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
that passes through
\pi^{-1}(-\overline{z_{*}})
and
\pi^{-1}(z_{*})
and whose natural projection is the arc
\hat{\gamma}
that smoothly meets
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
at
z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}
, belongs to the region delimited by infinite trajectories in Figure
1
, and agrees with the orthogonal trajectory of
-Q(z)({\mathrm{d}}z)^{2}
in a small neighborhood of
z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}
. We assume that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\cap(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})=\{z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}\}
and orient
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
towards
\boldsymbol{-}\overline{z_{*}}
within
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(0)}
. Similarly, we define
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to be a cycle on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
that passes through
\pi^{-1}(-1)
and
\pi^{-1}(-\overline{z_{*}})
and whose natural projection is
\gamma_{1}
. We orient
\boldsymbol{\beta}
so that
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
form the right pair at
\pi^{-1}(-\overline{z_{*}})
.

Since this is a surface of genus 1, the linear space of holomorphic differentials is of dimension 1, and is generated by (we slightly abuse the notation
w
here)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\left(\oint_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}t}{w(\boldsymbol{t})}\right)^{-1}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}z}{w(\boldsymbol{z})},

(17)

where

\displaystyle w(z^{(k)})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left[(z^{2}-1)(z-z_{*})(z+\overline{z_{*}})\right]^{1/2}(z),\ z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}),

(18)

\displaystyle w(z^{(k)})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}z^{2}+\mathcal{O}(z)\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.

(19)

\mathcal{H}
is normalized so that
\oint_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathcal{H}=1
, and under this normalization, Riemann showed that

\mathrm{Im}(\mathsf{B})>0,\quad\text{where }\quad\mathsf{B}:=\oint_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{H}.

(20)

Given this normalized differential, we can define the Abel Map
\mathcal{A}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\int_{1}^{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\mathcal{H}
where the path of integration is chosen to lie in
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\}
. This function is holomorphic on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
that satisfies

(\mathcal{A}_{+}-\mathcal{A}_{-})({\boldsymbol{z}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\beta}\setminus\pi^{-1}(-1),\\
-\mathsf{B},&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}\setminus\pi^{-1}(-1).\end{array}\right.

(21)

3.2.2
Szegő Function

Let

\tilde{S}_{h}(z^{(k)}):=\exp\left\{\dfrac{1}{4\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{\pi^{-1}(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})}\log(h)\Omega_{z^{(k)},z^{(1-k)}}\right\}\quad\text{for}\quad k=0,1,

(22)

where

is as in (

\Omega_{z^{(k)},z^{(1-k)}}
is the meromorphic differential on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
with simple pole at

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\tilde{S}_{h}
be as above and
h(z)=h^{*}(z)(1-z)^{\alpha}(1+z)^{\beta}
where
h^{*}(z)
is holomorphic, non-vanishing in a neighborhood of
\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}\cup\hat{\gamma}
and
h(z)
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of each point of
(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})\setminus\{\pm 1,z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}\}
. Furthermore, define

(23)

Then
\tilde{S}_{h}
is holomorphic and non-vanishing on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\pi^{-1}(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})\}

\tilde{S}_{h}(z^{(k)})\cdot\tilde{S}_{h}(z^{(1-k)})=1

\tilde{S}_{h}
possesses continuous traces on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cup\pi^{-1}(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})\setminus\{\pi^{-1}(\pm 1)\pi^{-1}(z_{*}),\pi^{-1}(-\overline{z_{*}})\}

\tilde{S}_{h,+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\tilde{S}_{h,-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}c_{h}},&s\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}\setminus\{z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}\},\\
1/h(s),&s\in\pi^{-1}(\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})\setminus\{\pi^{-1}(\pm 1)\}.\end{array}\right.

(24)

Furthermore, we have

\tilde{S}_{h}(z^{(0)})=|z-e|^{-\alpha_{e}/2},\quad e\in\{\pm 1,z_{*},-\overline{z_{*}}\},

where
\alpha_{e}=0
for

,

when

and
\alpha_{e}=\beta
when

For a proof of this, see
[
6

3.2.3
Theta Function

Let
\theta(z)
be the function defined by the sum

\theta(u)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\exp\left\{\pi{\mathrm{i}}\mathsf{B}k^{2}+2\pi{\mathrm{i}}uk\right\}.

For convenience, we remind the reader of its properties here. This function is holomorphic in
{\mathbb{C}}
and satisfies the quasi-periodicity relations

\theta(u+j+\mathsf{B}m)=\exp\left\{-\pi{\mathrm{i}}\mathsf{B}m^{2}-2\pi{\mathrm{i}}um\right\}\theta(u),\qquad j,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

(25)

It is also known that
\theta(u)
vanishes only at the points of the lattice
\frac{\mathsf{B}+1}{2}+m+n\mathsf{B},\ m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Furthermore, let
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
denote the continuation of
\mathcal{A}
onto
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
by
\mathcal{A}_{+}
and define
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,k}
by the equation

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,k})=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\left(p^{(k)}\right)+c_{h}+n\left(\dfrac{1}{2}+\mathsf{B}\tau\right)+j_{n,k}+m_{n,k}\mathsf{B},\ j_{n,k},m_{n,k}\in{\mathbb{Z}},

(26)

where
p={\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{Im}(z_{*})/(1-\mathrm{Re}(z_{*})))
and

\tau:=-\dfrac{1}{\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\int_{\hat{\gamma}}Q_{\lambda}^{1/2}(s){\mathrm{d}}s.

(27)

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
is of genus one,
\mathcal{A}
is bijective and equation (
26
) defines
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,k}
uniquely. In fact, by considering the branch choices in the definition of

, the following holds.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\tau
be given by (

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,k}={\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,k}(\lambda)
as in (

), and

Proof.

It follows from (

(\cdot)^{1/4}

\gamma(p)=1

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}(B/A)(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(0)},\\
-(A/B)(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}^{(1)},\end{array}\right.

(28)

is a rational function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
with two simple zeros

and
p^{(0)}
and two simple poles
\infty^{(1)}
and
p^{(1)}
(if it happens that

(29)

while the relations (

\int_{p^{(0)}}^{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,0}}\mathcal{H}=\int_{p^{(1)}}^{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,1}}\mathcal{H}\quad\text{ modulo }{\mathbb{Z}}+\mathsf{B}{\mathbb{Z}}.

Let
