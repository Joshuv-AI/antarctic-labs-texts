Frank-Wolfe Meets Metric Entropy
Suhas Vijaykumar

Abstract
. The Frank-Wolfe algorithm has seen a resurgence in popularity due to its ability to efficiently solve constrained optimization problems in machine learning and high-dimensional statistics. As such, there is much interest in establishing when the algorithm may possess a “linear” O ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) O(\log(1/\epsilon)) dimension-free iteration complexity comparable to projected gradient descent. In this paper, we provide a general technique for establishing domain specific and easy-to-estimate lower bounds for Frank-Wolfe and its variants using the metric entropy of the domain. Most notably, we show that a dimension-free linear upper bound must fail not only in the worst case, but in the average case : for a Gaussian or spherical random polytope in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with poly ⁡ ( d ) \mathrm{poly}(d) vertices, Frank-Wolfe requires up to Ω ~ ​ ( d ) \tilde{\Omega}(d) iterations to achieve a O ⁡ ( 1 / d ) O(1/d) error bound, with high probability. We also establish this phenomenon for the nuclear norm ball. The link with metric entropy also has interesting positive implications for conditional gradient algorithms in statistics, such as gradient boosting and matching pursuit. In particular, we show that it is possible to extract fast-decaying upper bounds on the excess risk directly from an analysis of the underlying optimization procedure.



1.
Introduction & Related Work

Constrained, high-dimensional convex optimization problems are ubiquitous in modern statistics and machine learning, and many promising approaches have emerged to solve them. In particular, for well-behaved objectives, the projected gradient descent algorithm of
Beck and Teboulle 2009
has been shown to need only
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations to find an
\epsilon
-aproximate solution
(
Bubeck 2015
)
. Unfortunately, it can require a costly “projection step” at each iteration, which is particularly expensive in high dimensional problems.

In contrast, conditional gradient algorithms such as Frank-Wolfe only require the solution of a linear program at each iteration, which is a major improvement when the ambient dimension is large
(
Frank and Wolfe 1956
;
Jaggi 2013
)
. However, even for well-behaved objectives, the worst case dimension-free iteration complexity scales as
\Omega(1/\epsilon)
. This has led to a search for algorithmic variants and high-level conditions that produce a “linear”
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
iteration complexity, where the polyhedral geometry of the domain has played a major role.

At the core of the paper is a basic and well-known geometric idea: if every point in the domain

2.
Preliminaries

We now introduce the central objects studied in the paper.

Definition 1
.

Given a subset
T
of a Hilbert space
H
, the
k
-convex hull
is given by

\conv_{k}(T)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}t_{i}\,\middle|\,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in T,\lambda\in\Delta(k)\right\},

where
\Delta(k)
denotes the probability simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The regular (closed) convex hull is denoted
\conv(T)
.

This motivates our definition of a
sparse programming algorithm
, namely an optimization algorithm over
\conv(T)
whose outputs take values in
\conv_{k}(T)
.

Definition 2
.

A
k
-sparse programming algorithm
is a function
A(T,f)
which, given access to a cost function
f
and a set
T
,
outputs some
z\in\conv_{k}(T)
. The set of all such functions is denoted
\mathbb{A}(k)

We do not restrict the computational complexity of the candidate function in any way, nor the manner in which it depends on
T
and
f
, as that is irrelevant for our results.
By definition, the
k^{\text{th}}
iterate of Frank-Wolfe and all of its usual variants comprises a
k
-sparse programming algorithm—including the “away steps” and “fully corrective” variants, as well as the local oracle construction of
Garber and Hazan 2016
.
Since we ignore all properties other than sparsity, the central quantity we study is the following.

Definition 3
.

For a given error tolerance
\epsilon>0
, the
sparse programming complexity

S_{F}(T,\epsilon)
of a set
T
, relative to a class
F
of cost functions
H\to\mathbb{R}
, is the smallest integer
k
such that

\inf_{A\in\mathbb{A}(k)}\sup_{f\in F\atop x\in\conv(T)}f(A(T,f))-f(x)\leq\epsilon.

We always consider the distance-squared cost functions

F(H)=\left\{f_{x}:y\mapsto\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\,\middle|\,x\in H\right\},

and suppress the subscript
F
. Note that this class of cost functions is somewhat ideal in that all its elements are
2
-smooth and
2
-strongly convex. This serves to isolate the dependence of our lower bounds on the geometry of the domain alone.

The starting point of our work is the relation between the sparse programming complexity and the classical
approximate Carathéodory
problem in convex geometry, which aims to bound the following quantity.

Definition 4
.

The
compressibility
of a set
T\subset H
is given by

(1)

c(T,\epsilon)=\inf\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\,\middle|\,\conv(T)\subset\conv_{k}(T)+\epsilon B(H)\right\},

where
B(H)
denotes the unit ball in
H
.

In other words, these numbers quantify how many points of
T
are necessary to
\epsilon
-approximate the convex hull.
Since finding
y
such that
x\in y+\epsilon B(H)
is equivalent to finding an
\epsilon^{2}
-approximate minimizer of the cost function
z\mapsto\left\lVert x-z\right\rVert^{2}
, which belongs to
F_{H}
, we deduce the following.

Lemma 1
.

For any
T\subset H
, the compressibility satisfies

(2)

c(T,\epsilon)\leq S(T,\epsilon^{2}).

This relation was noted by
Combettes and Pokutta 2021
, who showed that modern convergence guarantees for Frank-Wolfe can be used to improve estimates on compressibility. In other words, a convergence guarantee for any sparse programming algorithm provides a
deterministic proof
of low compressibility.

In this work, we pursue a similar line of reasoning using a classical idea often attributed to Maurey that relates
the compressibility to the metric entropy
(
Pisier 1980
)
.
The metric entropy may be defined as follows.

Definition 5
.

The
covering number

N(\epsilon,T,d)
of a set
T\subset H
with respect to a metric
d
is defined as the smallest cardinality of a set
U
such that

\sup_{x\in T}\inf_{y\in U}d(x,y)\leq\epsilon.

The
metric entropy
is the natural logarithm of the covering number,
\log N(\epsilon,T,d)
.

Unless otherwise indicated, we take
d
to be the norm in
H
and omit this argument. It is straightforward to show that the metric entropy of
\conv_{k}(T)
satisfies the following bound, which forms the basis for our results.

Lemma 2
.

Let
T
be a subset of the unit ball in
H
. Then

(3)

\log N(\epsilon,\conv_{k}(T))\leq k(\log N(\epsilon/2,T)+\log(6/\epsilon)).

We observe that if the compressibility satisfies
c(T,\epsilon)\leq k
, the metric entropy of
\conv(T)
cannot be much larger than that of
\conv_{k}(T)
. By (
3
), the metric entropy of
\conv_{k}(T)
is in turn only roughly
k
times the metric entropy of
T
.

Thus, a lower bound on the metric entropy of
\conv(T)
relative to that of
T
can be translated into a domain-specific lower bound on the compressibility. By (
2
), we recover a lower bound on the sparse programming complexity. This is the essence of Proposition
1
and Theorem
3
below.

Remark 1
.

Carathéodory’s theorem

(
Artstein-Avidan et al. 2015
, Theorem A.1.3)
, a classical result in convex geometry, implies that for
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

d+1\geq c(0,T)\geq c(\epsilon/2,T).

Thus, the above argument cannot give a non-trivial lower bound on the iteration complexity that is larger than the ambient dimension,
d
. It is therefore quite surprising that it recovers tight bounds on the iteration complexity of the form
\tilde{\Omega}(d\log(1/\epsilon))
.

f\lesssim g

3.
Lower Bounds

In view of the above discussion, bounds on the metric entropy can be used to place restrictions on the convergence rate of any sparse programming algorithm. This section explores various applications of this observation, both for constructing general-purpose bounds and for studying specific examples. Omitted proofs are contained in Appendix
B
. We begin with the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P
be a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
n
vertices, of unit diameter, whose
\ell^{2}(d)
covering numbers are denoted
N(P,\epsilon).

Then, for any
\epsilon\leq 1
the sparse programming complexity for quadratic objectives over
P
satisfies

S(P,\epsilon^{2}/4)\geq\frac{\log N(P,\epsilon)}{3+\log n+\log(1/\epsilon)}.

Proof.

Without any loss of generality, we may assume that
P
is a subset of the unit ball. Suppose that there exists a sparse programming algorithm which, for all
p\in P
, converges to within a tolerance of
(\epsilon/2)^{2}
for the objective
\left\lVert x-p\right\rVert^{2}
in fewer than
t
iterations.
Then, for each
p\in P
there exist
t
vertices
(x_{1}(p),\ldots,x_{t}(p))
and
\lambda\in\Delta(t)
with the property that

\left\lVert p-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{i}x_{i}(p)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon/2.

Now, let
T
be a minimal covering of
\Delta^{t}
in
\ell^{1}(d)
at resolution
\epsilon/2
, and let
[\lambda]
denote the projection of
\lambda\in\Delta^{t}
onto the nearest element of
T
(breaking ties arbitrarily). It is straightforward to verify the following facts.

Lemma 3
.

If
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert\leq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq t
, and
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
, then we have

(4)

\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{t}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda^{\prime}_{i})u_{i}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}.

Lemma 4
.

The
\ell_{1}(t)
covering numbers of the probability simplex
\Delta(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{t}
satisfy

(5)

N(\Delta(t),\epsilon,\ell^{1}(t))\leq\left(\frac{3}{\epsilon}\right)^{t}

By (
4
) and the triangle inequality, we have

\left\lVert p-\sum_{i=1}^{t}[\lambda]_{i}x_{i}(p)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon.

Moreover, the total number of possible values taken by the expression

\sum_{i=1}^{t}[\lambda]_{i}x_{i}(p)

can be upper bounded as

\#T\binom{n}{t}\leq\#T\left(\frac{en}{t}\right)^{t}\leq\left(\frac{6en}{t\epsilon}\right)^{t},

where we use the estimate of
\#T
given by (
5
).
Thus,

\displaystyle\log N(P,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq t(\log n+\log(6e)-\log t+\log(1/\epsilon))

\displaystyle\leq t(\log n+\log(6e)+\log(1/\epsilon)).

Rearranging and noting that the choice of sparse programming algorithm was arbitrary, we deduce that

\frac{\log N(P,\epsilon)}{\log(6e)+\log n+\log(1/\epsilon)}\leq S(\epsilon^{2}/4).

Noting that
\log(6e)\leq 3
, the proof is complete.
∎

As a first illustration of our result, let’s consider the unit ball in
\ell^{1}(d)
, which we’ll denote by
B_{1}(d)
. Plugging in the number of vertices and a volumetric estimate of the covering number gives the following lower bound.

Example 1

(
\ell^{1}(d)
ball)
.

S(B_{1}(d),\delta/d)\geq\frac{d\log(1/4\delta)}{3+\frac{3}{2}\log d+\log(1/2\delta)}

Thus, when the dimension
d
is comparable to the inverse error tolerance
1/\epsilon
, the number of iterations required for any sparse programming algorithm is
\tilde{\Omega}(1/\epsilon)
.

Proof.

By applying a volume argument (Lemma
9
in appendix) to the inscribed Euclidean ball, which has radius
1/\sqrt{d}
, one obtains

N(B_{1}(d),\epsilon)\geq\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d}.

Taking
\epsilon=4\delta/\sqrt{d}
and plugging into Proposition (
1
) gives us the lower bound.
∎

The above example illustrates a general approach for constructing lower bounds for arbitrary polytopes by means of the volume argument.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P
be a polytope of diameter at most
1
. Then

(6)

S(P,\epsilon^{2}/4)\geq d\left(\frac{\log(\varv(P)^{\frac{1}{d}})+\log(1/\epsilon)}{3+\log n+\log(1/\epsilon)}\right),

where
\varv(P)={\vol_{d}(P)\Gamma(1+\textstyle\frac{d}{2})}/{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}
is the ratio between
\vol_{d}(P)
and the volume of the unit Euclidean ball,
B_{2}(d)
.

Note that
P
can be isometrically transformed to lie inside the unit ball, hence
\varv
is at most
1
. By restricting to the affine hull of
P
we must have
n\geq d+1
or else the bound is vacuous. Meanwhile, for the naive implementation of Frank-Wolfe to be tractable in moderate to high dimension, we must have
n\leq\mathrm{poly}(d)
, so the first summand of the denominator would be at most
O(\log d)
.

We show subsequently that a random polytope with
\mathrm{poly}(d)
vertices has
\varv^{\frac{1}{d}}\geq\Omega(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\sqrt{d}}})
, so our lower bound for the
\ell^{1}(d)
ball applies in the average case, up to constants. Thus, it appears that the “curse of dimensionality,” namely the necessity for
\tilde{\Omega}(d)
iterations to achieve an error tolerance of order
1/d
, is a somewhat generic phenomenon.

Remark 2
.

In a hypothetical scenario where
\varv(P)^{\frac{1}{d}}=\Omega(1)
and
n\leq\mathrm{poly}(d)
, our result (
6
) would imply that
d/\log(d)
iterations are required to achieve a
constant
error tolerance
\epsilon<\varv(P)^{\frac{1}{d}}
, which would contradict the known
O(1/\epsilon)
dimension-free iteration complexity of Frank-Wolfe
(
Jaggi 2013
, Theorem 1)
. This implies that any sequence of polytopes
d\mapsto P_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
containing an
\Omega(1)
fraction of their circumscribed spheres cannot have a polynomial-in-
d
number of vertices.

Indeed, Maurey’s lemma (see
Pisier 1980
), which may be derived as a consequence of Frank-Wolfe
(
Mirrokni et al. 2017
;
Combettes and Pokutta 2021
)
, tells us that a polytope
P
of unit diameter on
n
vertices has entropy at most
C\epsilon^{-2}\log(n)
, while
\varv(P)^{\frac{1}{d}}\geq c
implies the entropy is at least
cd\log(1/\epsilon)
(by the volume argument). Choosing
\epsilon=1/e
verifies that
n\geq e^{cd/(Ce^{2})}
.

We complete our study of finite polytopes with an average-case lower bound showing that the inequality
\varv^{\frac{1}{d}}\gtrsim 1/\sqrt{d}
satisfied by the
\ell^{1}(d)
ball is generically satisfied by a polytope with polynomially many vertices.

Theorem 2
.

Let
v=(v_{1},\,v_{2},\ldots,v_{m})
be
m
points independently distributed according to the uniform measure on the sphere
S^{d-1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1\right\}
, with
d\geq 6
. Let
P=\conv(v)
denote their convex hull. Then there exists a universal
C
such that if
\alpha d^{\alpha}\geq m\geq Cd\log^{2}(d)\log(1/\eta)
for some
\alpha>0
, it holds with probability
1-\eta
that

\varv(P)\geq\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d},\quad S(P,\delta/d)\geq\frac{d\log(1/\delta)}{C(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})\log d+\log(1/\delta)}.

Moreover, for any particular choice of
m
, the two statements hold with probability at least
1-2\exp(d\log(m)-m/20)
.

Proof.

We will provide a proof for the spherical case stated above; the promised extension to Gaussian random polytopes is postponed to the Appendix
C.6
. The basis of the proof is a bound due to
Dyer et al. 1992
and adapted to our context by
Pivovarov 2007
, who also gives an analogous result for the Gaussian case. While the full proof can be found in the referenced paper, a sketch of the argument is provided in Appendix
C.4
for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 5

(
Pivovarov 2007
)
.

Let
B_{2}(d)
denote the unit ball in
\ell^{2}(d)
, and suppose that
d\geq 6
. Then

(7)

\mathbb{P}\left(rB_{2}(d)\not\subset P\right)\leq 2\exp(d\log(m)-n\mu(r)),

where
\mu(r)
is the measure of the spherical cap of height
r
, namely
\mu(r)=\mathbb{P}(\left\langle v_{1},\,u\right\rangle\geq r)
for a unit vector
u
.

In order to complete the argument, we need a good lower bound on the quantity
\mu(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\sqrt{d}}})
so that the probability bound in (
7
) converges to
0
. Fortunately, we are able to show the following.

Lemma 6
.

For all
d\geq 6
,

\mu\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)>\frac{1}{20}.

The bound follows from a more general lower bound on the size of spherical caps (Proposition
4
in the appendix), which uses a coupling of the high-dimensional spherical and Gaussian distributions. Armed with this bound, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{B_{2}(p)}{2\sqrt{d}}\not\subset P\right)\leq 2\exp(d\log(m)-m/20)\leq\eta,

as long as
m\geq Cd\log^{2}(d)\log(1/\eta)
for some large enough universal constant
C
. Finally, we note that on the complimentary event, we have

P\supset\frac{B_{2}(p)}{2\sqrt{d}}\implies\varv(P)\geq\varv\left(\frac{B_{2}(p)}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d},

which is what we aimed to show. On this event, we may lower bound the sparse programming complexity by plugging the above volume estimate into Theorem
1
with
\epsilon=\sqrt{\delta/(4d)}
. Noting that
\log(n)\leq\alpha\log(d)+\log(\alpha)
, we find that

S\left(P,\frac{\delta}{16d}\right)\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}d\log(1/\delta)}{3+\alpha\log d+\log\alpha+\log(\sqrt{4d/\delta})}.

Since we have assumed
d\geq 6\geq e
, the extra terms in the denominator may be absorbed into an appropriate universal constant factor
C
adjoining
(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})\log(d)
, yielding the result.
∎

Remark 3
.

It is evident from the proof of Lemma
5
that the result can be extended to any independent random points
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R^{d}}
each satisfying

\inf_{\left\lVert u\right\rVert=1}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle v_{i},\,u\right\rangle\geq\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geq\delta

for some
\delta>0
, i.e. the vertices cannot be too concentrated near any linear subspace.

Large or infinite vertex sets

If the domain
P
admits a more sophisticated linear programming oracle—for example, in cases where a self-concordant barrier can be efficiently computed—then it is possible to dispense with the requirement that
n=\mathrm{poly}(d)
, which improves the situation considerably.

As an example in which our lower bounds begin to fail, we consider the suitably normalized unit cube
B_{\infty}(d)/\sqrt{d}=[\nicefrac{{-1}}{{\sqrt{d}}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}}]^{d}
, and rescale the error tolerance accordingly.

Example 2

(Unit cube)
.

S(B_{\infty}(d),d\epsilon^{2}/4)\geq\frac{d(\log(1/\epsilon)-C)}{3+d\log 2+\log(1/\epsilon)}

In particular, taking
\epsilon=e^{-C}\sqrt{2\delta/(d\log d)}
, we find that
\Omega(\log(d/\delta))
iterations are required to achieve an error tolerance of
e^{-2C}\delta/\log(d)
.

Remark 4
.

Here, the number of iterations required to achieve a
constant
error tolerance
\epsilon
is
\Omega(\log d)
. This would seem to contradict an
O(1/\epsilon)
bound, but reflects the fact that the diameter of the domain—in this case
\sqrt{d}
—is hidden in the constant of the usual bound (see e.g.
Jaggi 2013
).

In this case the dimension must be exponential in the number of iterations for an
O(1/\epsilon)
upper bound to be tight. For more reasonable values of
\epsilon
and
d
, the lower bound behaves like
\log(1/\epsilon)
. We therefore cannot rule out an
O(d\log(1/\epsilon))
iteration complexity upper bound for conditional gradient on the cube, nor even
o(d\log(1/\epsilon))
for reasonable values of
d
and
\epsilon
.

This differs significantly from the best known upper bounds for conditional gradient procedures on the unit cube, which take the form
O(d^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))

(
Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi 2015
;
Garber and Hazan 2016
;
Peña and Rodríguez 2019
)
.
In fact, as we remarked in the introduction, the sparse programming complexity in dimension
d
can never be greater than
(d+1)
for any
\epsilon
, by Carathéodory’s theorem.

These considerations extend naturally to other polytopes with exponentially many vertices that arise in combinatorial optimization such as matroid polytopes, flow polytopes, the Birkhoff polytope, and the
k
-marginal polytope on an
n
-clique.

Infinite vertex sets

It is also possible to consider optimization over convex sets
S=\conv(V)
for infinite sets
V
, provided one replaces the cardinality
n=\#V
with the covering number at scale
\epsilon
. The proof follows along the same lines as Proposition
1
, and is deferred to the Appendix
C.7
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a subset of the unit Euclidean ball.
Then, for any
\epsilon\leq 1
the sparse programming complexity for quadratic objectives over
\conv(V)
satisfies

S(V,\epsilon^{2}/4)\geq\frac{\log N(\conv(V),\epsilon)}{4+\log N(V,\epsilon/2)+\log(1/\epsilon)}.

A nice application of Theorem
3
is given by the nuclear norm ball, which is another setting where Frank-Wolfe finds frequent use and where a sharp lower bound may be derived from the entropy calculation.

Example 3

(Nuclear norm ball)
.

For
1\leq m<n
, let
B_{1}(n,m)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denote the set of
m\times n
matrices
A
whose singular values
\sigma_{i}(A)
satisfy

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{S^{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\sigma_{i}(A)|\leq 1.

Then
Fazel et al. 2004
showed that

B_{1}(n,m)=\conv(V),\quad V=\left\{uv^{\top}\,\middle|\,u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}=\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}=1\right\}.

In this context, we have by Theorem
3
that, whenever
m\geq e
,

S(V,\epsilon^{2}/4)\geq\frac{mn\left(\log(1/\epsilon)-\log(m\wedge n)/2\right)}{6+m+n+2\log(1/\epsilon)}

Taking
\epsilon=\sqrt{\delta/(m\wedge n)}
, we find that
mn\log(1/\delta)/[C(m+n)]
iterations are required to achieve an error tolerance of
\delta/[4(m\wedge n)]
, giving a sharp
\Omega(1/\delta)
lower bound when
m=\Theta(n)=\Theta(1/\delta)
.

Remark 5
.

A corresponding linear rate for the nuclear norm ball cannot be derived from results known to the authors, since the set of extreme points is not discrete. Thus, sharpness of the
mn\log(1/\delta)/(m+n)
lower bound remains unresolved.

Strictly convex sets

As the most extreme example in which our lower bound fails, let
V=\partial B
denote the boundary of a closed, strictly convex set
B
. In this case, the entropy of
V
and
B
are equal up to constants and an additive factor
\log(1/\epsilon)
, and our lower bound is at most constant, for any value of
\epsilon
.

This underscores the open problem highlighted by
Garber and Hazan 2015
of determining whether dimension-independent linear rates (or linear rates of any kind) are attainable for conditional gradient algorithms over strongly convex sets.

Lemma 7
.

Let
B
be a strictly convex subset of the unit ball, namely
B=\left\{x\,\middle|\,\varphi(x)\leq 0\right\}
for some function
\varphi
which satisfies

\varphi(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)<\lambda\varphi(x)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(y)
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and let
V=\partial B=\left\{x\,\middle|\,\varphi(x)=0\right\}
denote the boundary. Then

\log N(B,2\epsilon)\leq 2(\log N(V,\epsilon/2)+\log(6/\epsilon))

Proof.

Let
u
be an arbitrary unit vector and put
\psi_{x}(\lambda)=x+\lambda u
. Then,
\left\{\lambda\,\middle|\,\psi_{x}(\lambda)\in B\right\}
is a closed and bounded interval
[l_{x},r_{x}]\subset\mathbb{R}
, and we can easily verify using strict convexity that
\psi_{x}(l_{x}),\psi_{x}(r_{x})\in\partial B
. It follows that
B\subset\conv_{2}(\partial B)
, since
x
is a convex combination of
\psi_{x}(l_{x})
and
\psi_{x}(r_{x})
. The proof is then complete by applying Lemma
2
.
∎

4.
Stochastic Sparse Programming

In this section, we’ll show that it is possible to extract statistical guarantees simply by analyzing the convergence rate of a conditional gradient algorithm. This is done by appealing to the low complexity of the set of conditional gradient iterates.

For simplicity of exposition, we’ll illustrate this section’s results using the Gaussian sequence model.
Let’s suppose we observe

y=\mu^{*}+g/\sqrt{n},

where
g
is a standard Gaussian in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is unknown. We will be interested in controlling the excess mean square error relative to some class
F
, namely

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\mu};F)=\left\lVert\mu^{*}-\hat{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\inf_{\mu\in F}\left\lVert\mu^{*}-\mu\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

We now state a bound that allows us to exploit the sparsity of Frank-Wolfe iterates as a form of regularization, although it applies to other forms of regularization as well.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\hat{\mu}
be an
\epsilon
-approximate minimizer of the empirical risk that takes values in
G\subset F
, let
\bar{\mu}\in F
be the minimizer of the true risk, and suppose that
F
is convex. Then

(8)

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\mu};F)\leq\epsilon+\sup_{\mu\in G-\bar{\mu}}\left\{\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}\left\langle g,\,\mu\right\rangle-\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}\leq\epsilon+\frac{8}{n}\left(\sup_{\mu\in\bar{\mu}-G}\left\langle\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert},\,g\right\rangle\right)^{2}.

Remark 6
.

The utility of the above bound is that it is a sharp, localized bound, for example capable of recovering the oracle rates in sparse recovery, and yet it only depends on the local complexity of the approximating set
G
and the approximation quality
\epsilon
.

Suppose we would like to approximate
\mu^{*}
using a finite, normalized dictionary,

D=\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{m}\}\subset S^{d-1}

To simplify computations, we assume
m\geq e
. Let
\epsilon(k)
denote the error obtained by running a sparse programming algorithm for
k
steps with the objective
x\mapsto\left\lVert y-x\right\rVert^{2}
(the empirical risk), and note that the output
\hat{\mu}_{k}
of the procedure belongs to the linear span of at most
k
elements of
D
. Putting
G=\cup_{S\in\binom{D}{k}}\vspan(S)
, we can compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\mu\in G}\left\langle\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert},\,g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{S\subset D\atop\#S=k}\sup_{\mu\in\vspan(S)}\left\langle\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert},\,g\right\rangle

Note that the inner supremum is over a subset of a norm ball in a hyperplane of dimension
k
, so it is
1
-subgaussian (Lemma
11
) with expectation at most
\sqrt{k}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{k}+\mathbb{E}\sup_{S\subset D\atop\#S=k}\left((1-\mathbb{E})\sup_{\mu\in\vspan(S)}\left\langle\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert},\,g\right\rangle\right)\lesssim\sqrt{k\log(m)}

by the standard sub-Gaussian maximal inequality (Lemma
12
). We obtain the following

Proposition 3
.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{k}
denote the output of running a sparse programming algorithm for
k
steps to minimize the empirical risk in some convex class
F\subset\vspan(D)
, and let
\epsilon(k)
denote the optimization error. Then with probability at least
1-\eta
,

(9)

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\mu}_{k};F)\lesssim\epsilon(k)+\frac{k\log(m)}{n}+\frac{\sqrt{k\log(m)\log(1/\eta)^{2}}}{n}.

Convex Aggregation

If we take
F=\conv(D)
, it is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\mu\in G-\bar{\mu}}\left\{\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}\left\langle g,\,\mu\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{\log(m)/n},

which matches the minimax rate in the convex aggregation problem, attained by the exact empirical risk minimizer
\hat{\mu}

(
Tsybakov 2003
)
.
Now, if the empirical risk minimization problem is “well-conditioned” for Frank-Wolfe, so that with high probability

\epsilon(k)\ll\sqrt{\log(m)/n}\ll 1/k

for some
k(n)
, then we may improve upon the minimax rate. For one concrete example, suppose
\bar{\mu}
belongs to the
n^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}
-relative interior of
\conv(D)
, for some
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
. We can then appeal to the following result adapted from
Garber and Hazan 2015
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
y
belongs to the
r
-relative interior of
\conv(D)
. Then the sub-optimality of the
k^{\text{th}}
iterate of Frank-Wolfe for the objective
x\mapsto\left\lVert y-x\right\rVert^{2}
satisfies

\epsilon(k)\leq\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{16}\right)^{k}\leq\exp\left\{\frac{-r^{2}k}{16}\right\}.

Applying the result to our context, we obtain the following rate. The crux of the proof is showing that under the same hypotheses,
y
also belongs to the relative interior with high probability.

Corollary 1
.

If
\bar{\mu}
belongs to the
cn^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2}}
relative interior of
\conv(D)
for some
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq 1
, and
\log(m)\vee\log(1/\eta)^{2}\leq C\sqrt{n}
for a sufficiently large universal constant
C
, then we may choose
k=32\alpha n^{{1-\alpha}}\log(n)/c
and deduce that with probability
1-2\eta

\mathcal{E}(\hat{\mu}_{k})\lesssim\frac{\mathrm{polylog}(m,n,1/\eta)}{n^{\alpha}}.

Remark 7
.

Note that the radius of
\conv(D)
is
\Theta(1)
, so values of
\alpha\leq 1
are reasonable. Thus, the result reflects an unusual fast rate phenomenon that responds to the centrality of
\bar{\mu}
. One explanation for this (and Theorem
4
) is that the volume of a high-dimensional polytope is generally concentrated at the boundary.

Remark 8
.

It would be very interesting to design adaptive variants of the procedure considered in this section, using e.g. the duality gap. However, that is beyond the scope of our current work.

5.
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied conditional gradient algorithms from the perspective that they output a convex (or linear) combination of at most
t
atoms, where
t
is the number of iterations. We establish that the set of outputs of any such procedure must has metric entropy bounded in terms of the number of iterations. If a convergence guarantee holds, this places restrictions on the entropy of the domain; we use this fact to derive domain-specific and sharp lower bounds in numerous settings of interest, as well as in canonical random domains with high probability. As a secondary application, we show that a dimension-free convergence rate which improves on the general
O(1/\epsilon)
bound can be used to establish fast rates in statistical estimation.

C.4.
Sketch of Lemma
5

Here we sketch a proof of the inequality (
7
), proved by
Pivovarov 2007
, that if
p=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots p_{m})
are
m
independent points distributed according to the uniform measure on the Euclidean sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
P=\conv(p)
denotes their convex hull, then

\mathbb{P}(P\not\supset rB_{2}(d))\leq 2\exp(d\log(m)-n\mu(r))

Proof.

Consider the
\binom{m}{d}
potential facets of
P
, each determined by a subset of
d
points (since no
d+1
points are coplanar, almost surely). If
P\not\supset rB_{2}(d)
, then at least one of these subsets must form a facet that intersects
rB_{2}(d)
. We may thus bound the probability that a particular subset forms a facet that intersects
rB_{2}(d)
, and then apply a union bound over all subsets.

To this end, suppose that for a given subset of indices
A\subset[m]
of size
d
, the corresponding points
p_{A}
form a facet that intersects
rB_{2}(p)
. Then both of the half-spaces determined by the affine hull of
p_{A}
have probability at most
1-\mu(r)
with respect to the uniform distribution on the sphere. Moreover, since
p_{A}
forms a facet, each of the remaining
n-d
points must independently lie in the same one of these two half-spaces. This event can therefore have probability at most
2(1-\mu(r))^{m-d}
, conditional upon any realization of
p_{A}
.
By a union bound over all possible subsets
A
, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(rB_{2}(p)\not\subset P\right)\leq 2\binom{m}{d}(1-\mu(r))^{m-d}\leq 2\exp(d\log(m)-n\mu(r)),

where the latter inequality follows from standard estimates such as Stirling’s approximation. This completes our sketch of Lemma
5
.
∎

Remark 10
.

It is quite clear that this proof can be relaxed to arbitrary independent distributions of the points

, where

is replaced by any uniform upper bound on the probability that each point lies on either side of any hyperplane that intersects

, where

E.2.
Probability Lemmas

The following concentration result for Gaussian suprema, which is a consequence of the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality, is heavily used throughout the paper.

Theorem E.1

(Borell’s Inequality,
Adler et al. 2007
)
.

Let
(g_{t})_{t\in T}
be a separable and almost surely bounded Gaussian process, and put
\sigma^{2}=\sup_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}g_{t}^{2}
. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in T}g_{t}>\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}g_{t}+u\right)\leq e^{\frac{-u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

In particular, noting that if
g
is a standard Gaussian in
\mathbb{R^{d}}
and
T
a subset of the unit ball, then
t\mapsto\left\langle g,\,t\right\rangle
is a separable and a.s. bounded Gaussian process indexed by
t
and
\mathbb{E}\left\langle g,\,t\right\rangle^{2}=\left\lVert t\right\rVert^{2}
, we have the following immediate corollary.

Lemma 11
.

Let
g
be a standard Gaussian in
\mathbb{R^{d}}
and let
T
be a subset of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in T}\left\langle g,\,t\right\rangle>\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}\left\langle g,\,t\right\rangle+u\right)\leq e^{\frac{-u^{2}}{2}}.

The final tools that we frequently use throughout the paper are the following maximal inequalities for Gaussian and sub-Gaussian random variables.

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that we are given random variables
(X_{i})_{i\in S}
, with
\#S=n
, each of which satisfies

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>u)\leq e^{\frac{-u^{2}}{2}}

for all
u>0
. Then we have

(23)

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]\leq\sqrt{2\log n}+\sqrt{\pi/2}.

In the special case where the
X_{i}
are independent Gaussian random variables, we have

(24)

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]\leq\sqrt{2\log n}

Proof.

For (
24
), we refer the reader to
Chatterjee 2014
and its accompanying proof. For (
23
) we closely mimic the proof of
Talagrand 2014
.
By a union bound and the fact that a probability can be at most
1
, for any
u>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in S}X_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}>0\}>u\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in S}X_{i}>u\right)\leq ne^{\frac{-u^{2}}{2}}\wedge 1

Then we can integrate the tail to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}>0\}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in S}X_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}>0\}>u\right)\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}(ne^{\frac{-u^{2}}{2}}\wedge 1)\,du

\displaystyle\leq A+\int_{A}^{\infty}ne^{-u^{2}/2}\,du

\displaystyle\leq A+\frac{n}{A}\int_{A}^{\infty}ue^{-u^{2}/2}\,du

Making the substitution
v=u^{2}/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle=A+\frac{n}{A}\int_{A^{2}/2}^{\infty}e^{-v}\,du

\displaystyle=A+\frac{n}{A}e^{-A^{2}/2}.

Choosing
A=\sqrt{2\log(n)}
gives

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\log n}+\sqrt{1/(2\log n)}

Since
1/(2\log n)\leq\pi/2
for
n\geq 2
, we deduce the result for all
n\geq 2
. Meanwhile, for the case
n=1
we have that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}/2}\leq\sqrt{\pi/2}

since the Gaussian density is symmetric and integrates to
1
.
∎

. Then, for any
z\in K
,

\left\lVert y-z\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert y-\hat{z}\right\rVert^{2}\geq\left\lVert\hat{z}-z\right\rVert^{2}.

Proof.

Note that by strict convexity of the squared norm,
\hat{x}
must be unique. By translation, we may assume
y=0
. There are two cases. If
0\in K
then
0=\hat{z}
and the statement is trivial. On the other hand, suppose
0\not\in K
. Let
C
be a minimal ball centered at
0
which contains
\hat{z}
. Then, the tangent plane to
C
at
\hat{z}
, which is perpendicular to
\hat{z}
must separate
0
from
K
(or else, using convexity of
K
and strict convexity of the squared norm, we can find a better point than
\hat{z}
by interpolation). It follows that
\left\langle\hat{z},\,z\right\rangle>0
. Finally, we may compute

\displaystyle\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\hat{z}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle z,\,z\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{z},\,\hat{z}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle z,\,z\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{z},\,\hat{z}\right\rangle-2\left\langle\hat{z},\,z\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert z-\hat{z}\right\rVert^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎
