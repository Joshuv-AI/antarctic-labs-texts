Fixed point groups of involutions of type O ⁡ ( q , k ) \Orth(\q,k) over a field of characteristic two
Mark Hunnell

Abstract
For O ⁡ ( q , k ) \Orth(\q,k) , the orthogonal group over a field k k of characteristic 2 with respect to a quadratic form q \q , we discuss the isomorphism classes of fixed points of involutions. When the quadratic space is either totally singular or nonsingular, a full classification of the isomorphism classes is given. We also give some implications of these results for a general quadratic space over a field of characteristic 2 2 .

Preliminaries

The following definitions can be found in
[
17
]
. We also refer to
[
13

[

Let
k
be a field of characteristic 2 and
W
a vector space defined over
k
. We call
\q:W\rightarrow k
a
quadratic form
if it satisfies
\q(aw)=a^{2}\q(w)
for all
a\in k
,
w\in W
. There exists a corresponding symmetric bilinear form
\mathrm{B}:W\times W\rightarrow k
such that
\q(w+w^{\prime})=\q(w)+\q(w^{\prime})+\mathrm{B}(w,w^{\prime})
for all
w,w^{\prime}\in W
. Over fields of characteristic
2
nonsingular symmetric bilinear forms are also symplectic.

The pair
(W,\q)
is called a
quadratic space
. The subspace of
W

\mathrm{rad}(W)=\{x\in W\ |\ \mathrm{B}(x,w)=0,\text{ for all }w\in W\}.

is called the
radical
of
W
.

Given a quadratic form, there exists a basis of
W
, consisting of
x_{i},y_{i},g_{j}
, where
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
and
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,s\}
and field elements
a_{i},b_{i},c_{j}\in k
such that

\q(w)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}(a_{i}e_{i}^{2}+e_{i}f_{i}+b_{i}f_{i}^{2})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{j}z_{j}^{2}

when
w=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e_{i}x_{i}+f_{i}y_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}z_{j}g_{j}
. We denote this quadratic form by

\q=[a_{1},b_{1}]\perp[a_{2},b_{2}]\perp\cdots\perp[a_{r},b_{r}]\perp\langle c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{s}\rangle

(1)

and so
\rad(W)=\spa\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{s}\}
. The representation of
\q
given in
1
is called the
quadratic signature
of
\q
. We say that such a quadratic form is of type
(r,s)
. A nonzero vector

is an
isotropic vector
if
\q(w)=0
,
V
is an
isotropic vector space
if it contains isotropic elements and
anisotropic
otherwise. The space
W
is called
nonsingular
if
s=0
, and
totally singular
if
r=0
. Furthermore,
W
is called
nondefective
if
s=0
or
\rad(W)
is anisotropic. A
hyperbolic plane
has a quadratic form isometric to the form
[0,0]
and will be denoted by
\mathbb{H}
. We will call
\q^{\prime}
a
subform
of
\q
if there exists a form
\mathrm{p}
such that
\q\cong\q^{\prime}\perp\mathrm{p}
. In this case,
p
is called a completion of
\q^{\prime}
with respect to
\q
.

Sometimes we will need to refer to a diagonal bilinear form

\mathrm{B}(w,w^{\prime})=a_{1}w_{1}w^{\prime}_{1}+a_{2}w_{2}w^{\prime}_{2}+\cdots+a_{l}w_{l}w^{\prime}_{l},

which we denote
\langle a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{l}\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}
following
[
17
]
. Throughout the paper we will use the calligraphy math font to denote sets of vectors in
W
such as
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{l}\}
and
\overline{\mathcal{U}}=\spa\mathcal{U}
. The next result is Proposition 2.4 from

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\q
be a quadratic form over

. Then

\q\cong m\times\mathbb{H}\perp\widetilde{\q_{r}}\perp\widetilde{\q_{s}}\perp d\times\langle 0\rangle,

with
\widetilde{q_{r}}
nonsingular,
\widetilde{\q_{s}}

\widetilde{\q_{r}}\perp\widetilde{q_{s}}
anisotropic. The form
\widetilde{\q_{r}}\perp\widetilde{\q_{s}}
is uniquely determined up to isometry. In particular

and

We call

the

and

the
defect
of
\q
. If

\q\cong m\times\mathbb{H}\perp\widetilde{\q_{r}}\perp d\times\langle 0\rangle\perp\widetilde{\q_{s}},

with respect to the basis

\{x_{1},y_{1},\ldots x_{m},y_{m},\ldots,x_{r},y_{r},g_{1},\ldots g_{d},g_{d+1},\ldots,g_{s}\},

we will call

\mathrm{def}(W)=\mathrm{span}\{g_{1},\ldots g_{d}\},

the
defect
of
W
. If
\mathcal{U}
is a basis for a subspace

of

, we will refer to the restriction of

to
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
with respect to this basis by

.

Let
G
be an algebraic group, then an automorphism
\theta:G\rightarrow G
is an

\theta^{2}=\id
,
\theta\neq\id
. The automorphism

G(k)^{\theta}=\{\gamma\in G(k)\ |\ \theta\gamma\theta^{-1}=\gamma\},

this is often denoted

\theta
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{\tau}
denote conjugation by

.

We often consider groups that leave a bilinear form or a quadratic form invariant. If
\mathrm{B}
is a bilinear form on a nonsingular vector space

we will denote the

of
(W,\q)
over a field

\Sp(\mathrm{B},k)=\{\varphi\in\GL(W)\ |\ \mathrm{B}(\varphi(w),\varphi(w^{\prime}))=\mathrm{B}(w,w^{\prime})\text{ for }w,w^{\prime}\in W\}.

The classification of involutions for

has been studied in

. For any quadratic space

\Orth(\q,k)=\{\varphi\in\GL(W)\ |\ \q(\varphi(w))=\q(w)\text{ for all }w\in W\}.

When

We will need to make use of some facts about quadratic spaces stated in the following lemmas. The first statement outlines some standard isometries for quadratic forms over a field of characteristic

\q

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\q
be a quadratic form on a vector space
W
and suppose
\alpha\in k
. Then the following are equivalent representations of
q
on
W
:

1.

[a,a^{\prime}]=[a,a+a^{\prime}+1]=[a^{\prime},a]=[\alpha^{2}a,\alpha^{-2}a^{\prime}]

2.

[a,a^{\prime}]\perp[b,b^{\prime}]=[a+b,a^{\prime}]\perp[b,a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}]=[b,b^{\prime}]\perp[a,a^{\prime}]

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in k
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Suppose
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{1},...,b_{n}\}
span the same vector space over

and
\q=[a_{1},c_{1}]\perp\ldots\perp[a_{n},c_{n}]
. Then there exist
c_{i}^{\prime}\in k
,
1\leq i\leq n
, such that
\q=[b_{1},c_{1}^{\prime}]\perp\ldots\perp[b_{n},c_{n}^{\prime}]
.

For a map
\theta\in\GL(W)
we define the
residual space
of
\theta
to be the image of
\theta+\id
and the
residue
of
\theta
to be the dimension of the residual space.

3
Summary of involution results

Here we summarize the results from
[
18
]
that we need to talk about the classification of the fixed points groups of each
\Orth(\q,k)
-conjugacy class of an involution.

If
W
is a nonsingular vector space with respect to
\q
we can define a
transvection
on
W
induced by
w\in W
and
a\in k

\tau_{w,a}(z)=z+a\mathrm{B}(w,z)w,

and an
orthogonal transvection
is of the form
\tau_{w,\frac{1}{\q(w)}}
for
w\in W
with
\q(w)\neq 0
. In this case we denote the transvection by
\tau_{w}
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is the set of mutually orthogonal vectors inducing a product of orthogonal transvections
\tau
we call
\mathcal{U}
the
inducing set
for

\tau=\tau_{\mathcal{U}}=\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{l}\}
and
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}\}
be sets of mutually orthogonal vectors in a nonsingular subspace of
W
. In what follows we will factor involutions into a product of involutions of a given type (orthogonal transvection, basic null, basic radical), such a factorization is called
reduced
if it uses the minimum number of factors. We refer to the number of factors in a reduced factorization as the
length
of an involution.

Lemma 3.1
.

Two orthogonal involutions given by reduced products of orthogonal transvections are equal,
\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}=\tau_{x_{l}}\cdots\tau_{x_{2}}\tau_{x_{1}}
, if and only if
\overline{\mathcal{U}}=\overline{\mathcal{X}}
.
and

\left\langle\q(u_{1}),\q(u_{2}),\ldots,\q(u_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}\cong\left\langle\q(x_{1}),\q(x_{2}),\ldots,\q(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}.

The classification of
\Orth(\q,k)
-conjugacy classes of involutions given by a product of transvections in a nonsingular space can then be described.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}
and
\tau_{x_{l}}\cdots\tau_{x_{2}}\tau_{x_{1}}
be orthogonal involution written as a product of transvections on
W
such that
\phi\in\Orth(\q,k)
. Then

\phi\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}\phi^{-1}=\tau_{x_{l}}\cdots\tau_{x_{2}}\tau_{x_{1}}

if and only if

\left\langle\q(u_{1}),\q(u_{2}),\ldots,\q(u_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}\cong\left\langle\q(x_{1}),\q(x_{2}),\ldots,\q(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}},

and
\dim(\overline{\mathcal{U}})=\dim(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
.

The last stipulation,
\dim(\overline{\mathcal{U}})=\dim(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
, distinguishes between elements
\tau
whose reduced length is
\mathrm{res}(\tau)+1
, called hyperbolic involutions, and those whose reduced length is
\mathrm{res}(\tau)
, called diagonal.

There is one other type of nonsingular involution called a null involution.

Definition 3.3
.

A plane
P=\spa\{x,y\}
is hyperbolic (or Artinian) if both of the following are satisfied:

1.

\q(x)=\q(y)=0

2.

B(x,y)\neq 0.

If
x,y
span a hyperbolic plane, we can rescale to assume
B(x,y)=1
. Proposition 188.2 of
[
26
]
guarantees that every nonsingular nonzero isotropic vector is contained in a hyperbolic plane.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\eta
be an involution of
\Orth(\q,k)
where
(W,\q)
is a quadratic space, and let
\mathbb{P}
be the orthogonal sum of two hyperbolic planes. Then an involution
\eta
is called a basic null involution in
\mathbb{P}
if all of the following are satisfied:

1.

\eta
leaves
\mathbb{P}
invariant

2.

\eta
fixes a 2-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{P}
where every vector has norm zero

3.

\eta|_{\mathbb{P}^{C}}=\id_{\mathbb{P}^{C}}
, where
\mathbb{P}^{C}
is the complement of
\mathbb{P}
in
W
.

A
null involution
is a product of basic null involutions. Note that if
\eta
is a null involution and
\tau
is a transvection, then
\tau\eta
is a product of transvections
[
26
]
, so we only need to consider the conjugacy of null involutions themselves.

Proposition 3.5
.

Two null involutions are
\Orth(\q,k)
-conjugate if and only if they have the same length.

Finally we consider involutions acting only on the radical of
W
.

Proposition 3.6
.

\Orth(\q_{\rad(W)},k)\cong\GL_{d}(k)\ltimes\Mat_{d,s-d}(k)
where
d
is the defect of
\rad(W)
.

Proof.

Let
d
be the defect of
W
. We can choose a basis of the form

\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{d},g_{d+1},\ldots,g_{s}\}

where
\q(g_{i})=0
for
1\leq i\leq d

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{is}}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\},

and the subspace spanned by

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{an}}=\{g_{d+1},\ldots,g_{s}\}

is anisotropic. We claim we can represent an isometry on
\mathrm{rad}(W)
by

\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}\psi&M\\
0&\id\end{bmatrix}.

We must have
\Psi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{an}}}=\id
otherwise there would exist two elements in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{an}}
with the same norm, and since the space is totally singular the sum of the two vectors would have norm zero. The restriction
\Psi|_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{is}}}=\psi\in\GL_{d}(k)
since
\psi
must be invertible but has no other contraints due to the bilinear form and the norm being identically zero for
\overline{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{is}}}
. The isometric image of an element from
\overline{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{is}}}
cannot include any components from
\overline{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{an}}}
since that would imply that
\overline{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{an}}}
was not anisotropic. Lastly the
M
block is free since we can add any isotropic vector from the the radical to any vector in
W
and leave the quadratic form (and bilinear form) invariant.

We can factor

\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}\psi&M\\
0&\id\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\psi&0\\
0&\id\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\id&M\\
0&\id\end{bmatrix}

where
\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\psi&M\\
0&\id\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\id&B\\
0&\id\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\psi&M\\
0&\id\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]^{-1}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\id&\psi B\\
0&\id\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
.
∎

A
radical involution
is an element
\rho\in\Orth(\q,k)
or order
2
defined on vectors in the radical of
W
. Notice that a nontrivial orthogonal transformation on
\rad(W)
detects a defective vector in
W
, since if
\rho(g)=g^{\prime}
then
\q(g+g^{\prime})=\q(g)+\q(g^{\prime})=0
. A
basic radical involution
is a radical involution
\rho_{i}\in\Orth(\q,k)
such that
\rho_{i}(g_{i})=g_{i}^{\prime}
, where
g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime}
are linearly independent vectors in
\rad(W)
with
\q(g_{i})=\q(g_{i}^{\prime})
, and
\rho_{i}
acts trivially on the complement of
\overline{\{g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime}\}}
. All radical involutions can be written as a finite product of basic radical involutions.

Proposition 3.7
.

Two basic radical involutions
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are
\Orth(\q,k)
-conjugate if and only if
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
act non-trivially on isometric vectors.

Corollary 3.8
.

All radical involutions of length
m
with quadratic signature isometric to

\langle\q(g_{1}),\q(g_{2}),\ldots,\q(g_{m})\rangle,

are
\Orth(\q,k)
-conjugate.

4
Totally singular spaces

We are now ready to discuss the isomorphism classes of the fixed point groups of our involutions of
\Orth(\q,k)
, which can be used to study the corresponding symmetric
k
-varieties. First, we discuss the fixed point groups of involutions acting on a totally singular vector space. This in turn leads to a discussion about fixed point groups of diagonal involutions on nonsingular subspaces.

Let us consider a vector space
W
such that
W=\rad(W)
. Recall that in such a vector space any quadratic form will be of the form

\q=\langle\q(g_{1}),\q(g_{2}),\ldots,\q(g_{s})\rangle,

and by Proposition
2.1
we have an isometry

\q\cong\langle 0,\ldots,0,\q(g_{1}),\q(g_{2}),\ldots,\q(g_{s-d})\rangle,

where
d
is the defect of
\q
.

By Proposition
3.6
we have
\Orth(\q_{\rad(W)},k)\cong\GL_{d}(k)\ltimes\Mat_{d,s-d}(k)
. For an element
\delta\in\Orth(\q,k)
to be fixed by conjugation with the involution
\rho
we need
\rho\delta\rho=\delta
.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\q
is a quadratic form on
W=\mathrm{rad}(W)
and
\rho\in\Orth(\q,k)
is an involution on
W
such that
\rho(g_{i})=g_{i}^{\prime}
and quadratic signature

\langle\q(g_{1}),\q(g_{2}),\cdots,\q(g_{n})\rangle

then
\Orth(\q,k)^{\mathcal{I}_{\rho}}\cong\Orth(\q_{\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{H}},k)\ltimes\Mat_{n,s-n}(k)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{g_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,g_{n}^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{s-2n}\}

with
\rho(h_{i})=h_{i}
and
\overline{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{H}}=W
.

Proof.

Let
\langle\q(g_{1}),\q(g_{2}),\ldots,\q(g_{n})\rangle
be the signature of a radical involution
\rho
such that
\rho(g_{i})=g_{i}^{\prime}
and
g_{i}^{\prime}\neq g_{i}
for any
i
or
i^{\prime}
. Notice that
\rho(g_{i}+g_{i}^{\prime})=g_{i}+g_{i}^{\prime}
and further that
\rho(g_{i})=g_{i}+(g_{i}+g_{i}^{\prime})
. Now we order a basis for
W
,

\mathcal{W}=\{g_{1}+g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}+g_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,g_{n}+g_{n}^{\prime},h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{s-2n},g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}\}

such that
\overline{\mathcal{W}}=W
and
\rho(h_{i})=h_{i}
. With respect to this basis we can represent
\rho
as the block matrix

\rho=\begin{bmatrix}\id&0&\id\\
0&\id&0\\
0&0&\id\end{bmatrix},

and so any
\delta\in\Orth(\q,k)^{\mathcal{I}_{\rho}}

\delta=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{11}&\delta_{12}&\delta_{13}\\
\delta_{21}&\delta_{22}&\delta_{23}\\
\delta_{31}&\delta_{32}&\delta_{33}\\
\end{bmatrix},

such that
\delta\rho=\rho\delta
is of the form

\delta=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{11}&\delta_{12}&\delta_{13}\\
0&\delta_{22}&\delta_{23}\\
0&0&\delta_{11}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{11}&0&0\\
0&\delta_{22}&\delta_{23}\\
0&0&\delta_{11}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\id&\delta_{12}^{\prime}&\delta_{13}^{\prime}\\
0&\id&0\\
0&0&\id\\
\end{bmatrix}

where
\delta_{ij}^{\prime}=\delta_{11}^{-1}\delta_{ij}
. Notice that
\delta_{12}^{\prime}
and
\delta_{13}^{\prime}
can take any matrix value, since
\overline{\{g_{1}+g_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,g_{n}+g_{n}^{\prime}\}}
is contained in the defect of
W
and the span of the remaining basis vectors would be

\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{G}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{s-2n},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\},

Now the subgroup

\mathsf{M}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{11}&0&0\\
0&\delta_{22}&\delta_{23}\\
0&0&\delta_{11}\\
\end{bmatrix}\bigg|\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\delta_{22}&\delta_{23}\\

is such that

which is the subgroup of

that leaves the subspace

invariant.
The subgroup

\mathsf{M}^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\id&\delta_{12}^{\prime}&\delta_{13}^{\prime}\\

\end{bmatrix}\bigg|\delta_{12}^{\prime}\in\Mat_{n,d^{\prime}}(k),\delta_{13}^{\prime}\in\Mat_{n,s-n-d^{\prime}}(k)\right\},

Remark 4.2

Notice that if

5
Nonsingular spaces

Now we look at fixed point groups of diagonal involutions on nonsingular spaces.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\tau=\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}
be a reduced diagonal orthogonal involution with inducing set
\mathcal{U}
, then
\varphi\in\Orth(\q,k)^{\mathcal{I}_{\tau}}
if and only if
\varphi(\overline{\mathcal{U}})=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\Orth(\q,k)^{\mathcal{I}_{\tau}}
notice that
\tau\varphi\tau^{-1}=\varphi
if and only if
\varphi\tau\varphi^{-1}=\tau
and so

\varphi\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}\varphi^{-1}=\tau_{\varphi(u_{l})}\cdots\tau_{\varphi(u_{2})}\tau_{\varphi(u_{1})}=\tau_{u_{l}}\cdots\tau_{u_{2}}\tau_{u_{1}}.

By Lemma
3.1

\varphi(\overline{\mathcal{U}})=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
.

Now assume
\varphi\in\Orth(\q,k)
and
\varphi(\overline{\mathcal{U}})=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
then

\langle\q(\varphi(u_{1})),\q(\varphi(u_{2})),\ldots,\q(\varphi(u_{l}))\rangle_{B}=\langle\q(u_{1}),\q(u_{2}),\ldots,\q(u_{l})\rangle_{B}.

So by Theorem
3.2
we have
\varphi\tau\varphi^{-1}=\tau
.
∎

We begin with the case that the length of
\tau
is maximized in the nonsingular space. We denote by
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}
the matrix representation of the resriction of
\varphi
to the subspace
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
with respect to the basis
\mathcal{U}
. Recall that

\q_{\mathcal{U}}\cong\langle\q(u_{1}),\q(u_{2}),\ldots,\q(u_{l})\rangle,

since
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
is a totally singular subspace of
W
. In this case, Proposition
2.1
yields

\q_{\mathcal{U}}\cong m^{\prime}\times\langle 0\rangle\perp\langle c_{m^{\prime}+1},\ldots,c_{l}\rangle.

This corresponds to a basis of
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\{u_{1}^{\prime},u_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,u_{l}^{\prime}\}
such that
\q(u_{i}^{\prime})=c_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq l
and
c_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq m^{\prime}
. Since the vectors in
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
are not in
\rad(W)
there exist non-singular completions within
W
that form a basis for a nonsingular subspace. A nonsingular completion of
\q
is of the form

[0,\q(v_{1})]\perp[0,\q(v_{2})]\perp\ldots\perp[0,\q(v_{m^{\prime}})]\perp[c_{m^{\prime}+1},\q(v_{m^{\prime}+1})]\perp\ldots\perp[c_{l},\q(v_{l})],

where
\mathrm{B}(u_{i}^{\prime},v_{i})=1
,
\mathrm{B}(u_{i}^{\prime},v_{j})=0
when
j\neq i
, and
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}\}
which spans a totally singular subspace. Notice that for
1\leq i\leq m^{\prime}
we can choose
v_{i}^{\prime}=v_{i}+\q(v_{i})u_{i}^{\prime}
. We will refer to the vectors of this completion as
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Now

\mathrm{B}(u_{i}^{\prime},v_{i}^{\prime})=\mathrm{B}(u_{i}^{\prime},v_{i})+\q(v_{i})\mathrm{B}(u_{i}^{\prime},u_{i}^{\prime})=\mathrm{B}(u_{i}^{\prime},v_{i})=1,

and

\q(v_{i}^{\prime})=\q(v_{i})+\q(v_{i})^{2}\q(u_{i}^{\prime})+\q(v_{i})B(v_{i},u_{i}^{\prime})=\q(v_{i})+\q(v_{i})=0.

So there is a basis
\mathcal{W}
of this nonsingular space of the form

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=\{u_{1}^{\prime},v_{1}^{\prime},u_{2}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,u_{l}^{\prime},v_{l}^{\prime}\},

with the corresponding quadratic form

\q_{\mathcal{W}}\cong m^{\prime}\times[0,0]\perp[c_{m^{\prime}+1},d_{m^{\prime}+1}]\perp\ldots\perp[c_{l},d_{l}],

where
\q(v_{i}^{\prime})=d_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq l
.

Let us consider
\varphi\in\Orth(\q,k)^{\mathcal{I}_{\tau}}
. Since
\varphi
leaves
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
invariant we have

\varphi=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}&\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\\
0&\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}\end{bmatrix},

where
\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}:\overline{\mathcal{V}}\to\overline{\mathcal{V}}
and
A:\overline{\mathcal{V}}\to\overline{\mathcal{U}}

\mathrm{B}(\varphi(u_{i}),\varphi(v_{j}))=\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(u_{i}),\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}(v_{j})+\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(Av_{j}))=\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(u_{i}),\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}(v_{j}))=\mathrm{B}(u_{i},v_{j}).

So, we have shown the following

Proposition 5.2
.

The map
\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}
is completely determined by
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}
.

We will now refer to
\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}=\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}
and using the matrix representation of
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}
we have
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}=(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-1})^{T}

\varphi=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}&\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\\

We define the additive group

\mathsf{A}(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)=\{A\in\text{Lin}(\overline{\mathcal{V}},\overline{\mathcal{U}})\ |\ \q(Av)=\mathrm{B}(v,Av)\}.

Notice that
\mathsf{A}(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)

as an additive group since

for

for

Lemma 5.3
.

If
u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}
and
v\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}
, then
\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(u),v)=\mathrm{B}(u,\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(u,\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}(\varphi(u),\varphi\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(u),(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}+\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A)\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(u),v+\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(u),v),

since
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v)\in\mathcal{U}
.
∎

In the next result we see that this gives us an isomorphism of the additive group
\mathsf{A}(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}\in\Orth(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)
and
A\in\mathsf{A}(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)
we have that

\Phi_{\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}}(A)=\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*},

is an isomorphism of
\mathsf{A}(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)
.

Proof.

The map
\Phi_{\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}}(A)
is a homomorphism with respect to addition in
\mathsf{A}(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)
and

\displaystyle\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=\q(A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=B(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v),A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v))

\displaystyle=B(v,\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{-*}(v)),

by Lemma
5.3
and the fact that
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}\in\Orth(\q_{\mathcal{U}},k)
, we have the result.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

For
\varphi=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}&\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}A\\
0&\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
we have
\varphi\in\Orth(\q,k)^{\mathcal{I}_{\tau}}
if and only if
\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}\in\Orth(\q,k)
and
\q(v+Av)=\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v))
for

a nonsingular completion of
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
in
W
.

Proof.

This can be shown with the following computation. Let us compute

\displaystyle\q(\varphi(v))

\displaystyle=\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v)+\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(Av))

\displaystyle\q(v)

\displaystyle=\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v))+\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(Av))+\mathrm{B}(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v),\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}(Av))

\displaystyle\q(v)

\displaystyle=\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v))+\q(Av)+\mathrm{B}(v,Av)

\displaystyle\q(v)+\q(Av)+\mathrm{B}(v,Av)

\displaystyle=\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v))

\displaystyle\q(v+Av)

\displaystyle=\q(\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}^{*}(v)).

\varphi_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle(\theta_{\mathcal{U}},D)(\id,C)(\theta_{\mathcal{U}},D)^{-1}

Proposition 6.1

\Orth(\q,k)

The map
