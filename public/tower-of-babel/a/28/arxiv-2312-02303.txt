A pseudo-resolvent approach to abstract differential-algebraic equations
Hannes Gernandt, Timo Reis

Abstract
We study linear abstract differential-algebraic equations (ADAEs), and we introduce an index concept which is based on polynomial growth of a pseudo-resolvent. Our approach to solvability analysis is based on degenerate semigroups. We apply our results to some examples such as distributed circuit elements, and a system obtained by heat-wave coupling.



1
Introduction

We study abstract differential-algebraic equations (ADAEs)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)=Ax(t)+f(t),\quad Ex(0)=Ex_{0},

(1)

where
x_{0}\in X
,
f:[0,t_{f}]\to Z
,
E:X\rightarrow Z
,
A:X\supset{\rm dom}\,A\rightarrow Z
are linear, and
X
,
Z
are Banach spaces. In most of our results, we will additionally assume that
E
is bounded,
A
is densely defined and closed, and

f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
. The main difficulty of the equations of the form (
1
) come from the possible non-invertibility of
E
. Systems of the form (
1
) may occur in two different settings:

a)

Finite dimensional differential-algebraic systems which are coupled with partial differential equations
. Such a coupling typically emerges from boundary action at the involved PDEs. In this case, the state space is typically a Cartesian product of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a function space, the operators
E
and
A
in (
1
) are block operator matrices. Typical examples are electrical circuits which further contain semiconductor devices or parasitic effects like crosstalk or heat propagation
[
44
]
. Another example is formed by multibody systems coupled with components from continuum mechanics
[
40
]
.

b)

Systems governed by degenerate PDEs
. These occur in spatial and time-dependent processes which have the property that there are spatial subdomains in which the dynamics is restricted or even not present. In this type
E
is usually a multiplication operator on a function space, whereas
A
is a spatial differential operator. An example is the vibrating string in which the spatially dependent string density and/or tension vanish in a part. Further,
magnetoquasistatic approximations
of the electromagnetic field can be also described by infinite dimensional systems of type (
1
), see e.g.
[
8
]
.

Our main assumption is that the ADAE (
1
) is
regular
, i.e. we have a non-empty
resolvent set

\displaystyle\rho(E,A):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \text{$\lambda E-A:{\rm dom}\,A\to Z$ is bijective}\}.

If
\lambda\in\rho(E,A)
, then the closed graph theorem
[
2
, Thm. 7.9]
implies that the operator
(\lambda E-A)^{-1}:Z\to X
exists and that it is bounded.

The degenerate Cauchy problems of the above form were studied in numerous works. We mention the two monographs by
Favini
and
Yagi

[
17
]
and
Showalter

[
13
]
. Both follow the ansatz via multi-valued linear operators. In
[
17
]
, a classical solution concept is considered, which requires additional smoothness assumptions on
f
, whereas in
[
13
]
, ADAEs with certain monotonicity assumptions on the involved operators
E
and
A
are considered.
Motivated by the approach to finite dimensional DAEs via the
tractability index

[
30
]
, ADAEs on Hilbert spaces are considered in
[
37
,
36
]
under the additional assumption that a certain projector chain is existent and stagnant. A normal form has been constructed by means of this projector chain, and this allows to derive a wide solution theory and in particular the determination of
consistent initial values
, i.e., the set of
x_{0}\in X
such that (
1
) with given
f
possesses a solution.
There are also recent works
[
46
,
47
]
, where ADAEs are studied, and a maximal space of initial values such that the solution of the degenerate Cauchy problem exists and is unique. The construction of this space of initial values is based on a previous construction from
[
10
]
for finite dimensional
X=Z
, using so-called
Wong sequences
. It is however assumed that
X=Z
are Hilbert spaces and that
E
and
A
are bounded, which particularly implies that the assumptions in
[
37
,
36
]
are fulfilled in this situation.
Furthermore, certain closedness assumptions are made on the spaces in the Wong sequences, which exclude nearly all practically relevant examples.
The approach via Wong sequences has been further used in
[
45
]

for the analysis of ADAEs with operators
E
and
A
as in our situation, however with the additional assumption that
f
vanishes.
Previously, ADAEs of the form
\frac{d}{dt}Ex(t)=Ax(t)
were also considered in
[
6
]
, where it is assumed that the domains of powers of a linear relation become stationary after a finite number of steps. In the case where
X=Z
and
E={\rm id}
, this implies that
A
is indeed bounded. Hence, this assumption excludes many practical examples arising from partial differential equations.

Besides
X,Z
being Hilbert spaces, a joint assumption
[
37
,
36
,
46
,
47
,
45
]
is that the resolvent is
polynomially bounded
on some half-plane. That is, for some
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
holds
\mathbb{C}_{\omega}^{+}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:{\rm Re}\,\lambda>\omega\}\subseteq\rho(E,A)
, and there exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
M>0
with

\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|\leq M|\lambda|^{k-1},\quad\text{for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\omega}^{+}$.}

(2)

If
E
and
A
are matrices, then the resolvent is rational, whence (
2
) is always fulfilled in the finite dimensional case as long as the resolvent
\rho(E,A)
is non-empty.
It further follows from the Weierstraß canonical form, see e.g.
[
10
]
, that the smallest
k
such that (
2
) holds for some
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, equals the so-called
(Kronecker) index of the DAE

[
30
,
29
]
. However, in contrast to the finite dimensional case, the polynomial growth rate does not coincide with the tractability index of ADAEs
[
37
]
.

In this note, we present an approach to the solution theory of ADAEs based on
pseudo-resolvents

[
23
]
, which are holomorphic operator-valued functions that satisfy a resolvent identity. Using a growth assumption similar to (
2
), we derive an operator
A_{R}
associated with the pseudo-resolvent. This operator can be used to obtain an ordinary differential equation
hidden in the ADAE (
1
). Under additional assumptions it can be shown that the operator that describes the ordinary differential equations generates a strongly continuous semigroup.

We develop a novel solution theory, which makes use of the concepts of
linear relations
(or also called
multi-valued operators
), and
degenerate semigroups
, where the latter is a generalization of the well-known concept of strongly continuous semigroup.
Based on these results, we will derive a generalization of the variations of constants formula, which gives rise to mild solutions of the ADAE (
1
). We will further present relations to weak and classical solutions of ADAEs. In doing so, we neither have to impose any monotonicity assumptions on
E
and
A
nor existence and stagnancy of certain subspace iterations or projector chains.

Our solution theory mainly uses the assumption
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
for some positive integer
k
, which is a dissipativity condition for the pseudo-resolvent restricted to a certain subspace
For finite dimensional systems,
E,A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the condition
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
is equivalent to the DAE (
1
) having nilpotency index at most
k
and in this sense the develop solution theory applicable to ADAEs of higher index. Furthermore, it is shown that under certain dissipativity and nonnegativity assumptions on the operators
E
and
A
, we can guarantee that
\mathbf{(D_{1})}
or
\mathbf{(D_{2})}
is fulfilled. This is in alignment with recent results for semi-dissipative Hamiltonian DAEs, i.e.
E
is nonegative and
A
is dissipative, where it is shown that their nilpotency index is at most two
[
32
]
. This class of DAEs is a subclass of
port-Hamiltonian
DAEs which are based on an unifying energy-based system formulation that has received a lot of attention in recent years, see
[
MehU23
]
.

Besides our approach there are many results on solutions theory for ADAEs available which will be briefly discussed in the following. In
[
42
]
the authors already use pseudo-resolvent for the solution theory, but their solution theory is based on the assumption of so called weak
(E,p)
-radiality, which is also a polynomial growth bound on the pseudo-resolvent which appears to be slightly stronger then our condition
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
. Furthermore, no conditions on the coefficients ensuring weak
(E,p)
-radiality were obtained.

In
[
46
,
47
]
the authors generalize the Wong sequence approach to solve DAEs from
[
10
]
to ADAEs with bounded coefficients in Hilbert spaces using (among other assumptions) considered a polynomial growth condition on the resolvent
s\mapsto(sE-A)^{-1}
.

In
[
26
]
the authors studied dissipative DAEs in the sense that a certain dissipativity condition holds and develop a solution theory for the homogeneous case. However their assumptions imply that our condition
\mathbf{(D_{1})}
holds. Here we give also dissipativity based conditions under which
\mathbf{(D_{2})}
holds and in that sense our results can be seen as generalization of
[
26
]
to higher index.

More recently,
[
33
]
also considered dissipative ADAEs in a slightly more general framework compared to
[
26
]
allowing for a Hamiltonian weight operator that is inspired by port-Hamiltonian systems. Compared to this, we focus here on developing a more general framework for the solution of ADAEs.

In
[
17
]
also pseudo-resolvents are considered in Banach spaces, but their assumptions to guarantee existence of classical solutions are stronger and no mild or weak solutions are considered.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we consider solution concepts for ADAEs, and we briefly present the main concepts of this article, namely linear relations, pseudo-resolvents and degenerate semigroups. The interrelation between these concepts is - in conjunction with ADAEs - be elaborated in Section
3
. A brief discussion of different index concepts for regular ADAEs (
1
) can be found in Section
6
. In Section
7
we consider a special class of ADAEs in which the operators fulfill a certain dissipativity conditions, and in Section
8
, we study solvability of ADAEs via pseudo-resolvents. Finally, in Section
9
we provide several examples where abstract differential-algebraic equations arise.

\mathbb{N}
is the set of natural numbers including zero. For Banach spaces

and
Y
,

stands for the space of bounded operators mapping from

to

, and we further abbreviate

.
The norm in a space

\|\cdot\|_{{X}}

{\rm dom}\,A

is
I_{X}

.

For an interval

, the space of

C^{0}(I;X)=C(I;X)
. Further, for

.

2
Solutions concepts for ADAEs

The following solution concepts for ADAEs of type (
1
) are considered in this article.

Definition 2.1

(Solution concepts)
.

Let
X
,
Z
be normed spaces, let
E:X\to Z
and
A:X\supset{\rm dom}\,A\to Z
be linear, let
t_{f}>0
,
f:[0,t_{f}]\to Z
and
x_{0}\in X
.

(a)

x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is called classical solution of (
1
), if
Ex(\cdot)\in C([0,t_{f}];Z)\cap C^{1}((0,t_{f});Z)
,
(Ex)(0)=Ex_{0}
,
x(t)\in{\rm dom}\,A
for all
t\in(0,t_{f})
, and it holds
\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)=Ax(t)+f(t)
for all
t\in(0,t_{f})
.

(b)

x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is called a mild solution of (
1
), if
f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
,
Ex(\cdot)\in C([0,t_{f}];Z)
,
x\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];X)
, and for almost all
t\in[0,t_{f}]
, it holds
\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau){\rm d}\tau\in{\rm dom}\,A
with

\displaystyle Ex(t)-Ex_{0}=A\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau){\rm d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau){\rm d}\tau.

(3)

(c)

x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is called weak solution of (
1
), if
A
is densely defined,
Ex(\cdot)\in C([0,t_{f}];Z)
with
(Ex)(0)=Ex_{0}
, and for all
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
, it holds that
t\mapsto\langle f(t),z^{\prime}\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}])
,
t\mapsto\langle Ex(t),z^{\prime}\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\in W^{1,1}([0,t_{f}])
with weak derivative fulfilling

\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\langle Ex(t),z^{\prime}\rangle=\langle x(t),A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\rangle+\langle f(t),z^{\prime}\rangle

(4)

for almost all
t\in[0,t_{f}]
and all
z^{\prime}\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}
.

We call
x_{0}\in X
a classical (mild, weak) consistent initial value for
\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)=Ax(t)+f(t)
, if (
1
) has a classical (mild, weak) solution.

Note that the notion of weak solutions has been used in
[
37
]
, whereas, in the articles
[
47
,
46
]
, mild solutions are studied. The definition of weak solution involves the dual of
A
, which is given by the operator
A^{\prime}:Z^{\prime}\supset{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
which is defined on the set of bounded linear functionals
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
for which the functional
{\rm dom}\,A\to\mathbb{C}:x\mapsto\langle Ax,z^{\prime}\rangle
has a bounded extension to
X
, that is,

{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}=\{z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}\,:\,\exists c>0\text{ s.t.\ }|\langle Ax,z^{\prime}\rangle|\leq c\,\|x\|\,\forall x\in X\}.

If
A
is densely defined, then
A^{\prime}
is well-defined by

\langle x,A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\rangle_{X,X^{\prime}}=\langle Ax,z\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\;\forall\,x\in{\rm dom}\,A,\,z^{\prime}\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}.

Remark 2.2
.

The definition of classical, mild and weak solutions can be easily extended to the infinite time horizon
[0,\infty)
. Namely, we say that
x:[0,\infty)\to X
is a classical (mild, weak) solution of (
1
), if, for all
t_{f}>0
, the restriction of
x
to
[0,t_{f}]
is a classical (mild, weak) solution of (
1
). For mild solutions, we have to impose that

f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}([0,\infty);X)
, whereas for weak solutions
f:[0,\infty)\to X
has to fulfill that
t\mapsto\langle f(t),z^{\prime}\rangle\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}([0,\infty))
for all
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
.

Next we show the relation between these solution concepts.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X
,
Z
be Banach spaces, let
E\in L(X,Z)
and
A:X\supset{\rm dom}\,A\to Z
be linear and closed. Further, let
t_{f}>0
,
x_{0}\in X
and
f:[0,t_{f}]\to Z
. Then the following holds:

(a)

If
f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
, and
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a classical solution of (
1
), then
x
is a mild solution of (
1
).

(b)

If
t\mapsto\langle f(t),z^{\prime}\rangle\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}])
for all
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
,
A
is densely defined, and
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a classical solution of (
1
), then
x
is a weak solution.

(c)

If
A
is densely defined, and
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a mild solution of (
1
), then
x
a weak solution of (
1
).

(d)

If,
X
and
Z
are reflexive,
A
is densely defined,
f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
and
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a weak solution of (
1
), then
x
is a mild solution of (
1
).

Proof.

(a)

As a consequence of Pettis’s measurability theorem
[
15
, Thm. II.1.2]
and the continuity of
Ex:[0,t_{f}]\to Z
, we have that
Ex
is Bochner integrable. Now an integration of (
1
) of
[0,t]
for
0\leq t\leq t_{f}
yields

Ex(t)-Ex(0)=\int_{0}^{t}Ax(\tau){\rm d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau){\rm d}\tau.

Since, by
[
5
, Prop. 1.1.7.]
, we have
\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau){\rm d}\tau\in{\rm dom}\,A
with

A\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau){\rm d}\tau=\int_{0}^{t}Ax(\tau){\rm d}\tau,

we immediately obtain that
x
is a mild solution.

(b)

Assume that
t\mapsto\langle f(t),z^{\prime}\rangle\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}])
for all
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
,
A
is densely defined, and
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a classical solution, and let
z^{\prime}\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}
. Now applying
z^{\prime}
to
\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Ex=Ax+f
and using the definition of
A^{\prime}
, we obtain (
4
). In particular,
x
is a weak solution.

(c)

Assume that
A
is densely defined,
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a mild solution of (
1
), and let
z^{\prime}\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}
. Then, by applying
z^{\prime}
to (
3
), we obtain

\langle Ex(t),z^{\prime}\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}-\langle Ex_{0},z^{\prime}\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\\
=\left\langle A\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau){\rm d}\tau,z^{\prime}\right\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}+\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau){\rm d}\tau,z^{\prime}\right\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}.

Hence, the weak form of the fundamental theorem of calculus
[
2
, Thm. E3.6]
implies that
\langle Ex(\cdot),z^{\prime}\rangle_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\in W^{1,1}_{\rm loc}([0,t_{f}])
and that (
4
) holds on
[0,t_{f}]
. That is,
x
is a weak solution.

(d)

Assume that
X
and
Z
are reflexive,
A
is densely defined,
f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
and
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a weak solution of (
1
).
By another use of the weak form of the fundamental theorem of calculus
[
2
, Thm. E3.6]
, we obtain from an integration of (
4
) on
[0,t]
for
0\leq t\leq t_{f}
that

\langle Ex(t),z^{\prime}\rangle-\langle Ex(0),z^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle x(\tau),A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\rangle{\rm d}\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\langle f(\tau),z^{\prime}\rangle{\rm d}\tau.

The property
f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
gives

\int_{0}^{t}\langle f(\tau),z^{\prime}\rangle d\tau=\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau),z^{\prime}d\tau\right\rangle.

Since
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is continuous, we can infer from Pettis’s measurability theorem
[
15
, Thm. II.1.2]
that
x(\cdot)
is Bochner integrable, and thus

\int_{0}^{t}\langle x(\tau),A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\rangle d\tau=\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau,A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\right\rangle,

which gives

\left\langle Ex(t)-Ex(0)-\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)d\tau,z^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau,A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\right\rangle\quad\forall z^{\prime}\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}.

Consequently, the functional
{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}\to\mathbb{C}
with
z^{\prime}\mapsto\langle\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau,A^{\prime}z^{\prime}\rangle
has a bounded extension to
Z^{\prime}
, whence
\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime\prime}
. Now the reflexivity of
X
leads to
\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau\in{\rm dom}\,A
with

\left\langle Ex(t)-Ex(0)-A\int_{0}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau-\int_{0}^{t}f(\tau)d\tau,z^{\prime}\right\rangle=0\quad\forall z^{\prime}\in{\rm dom}\,A^{\prime}.

(5)

Further, by using that
X
and
Z
are reflexive and
A
is densely defined, we obtain from
[
28
, Thm. III.5.29]
that
A^{\prime}
is densely defined as well. Consequently, (
5
) implies (
3
).

3
Main concepts

The first concept that will be of major importance is that of
linear relations
, which will be more intensively treated in Section
4
.

Definition 3.1

(Linear relation)
.

Let
X
be a normed space. A (closed) linear relation
L
in
X
is a (closed) subspace
L
of
X\times X
.

Linear operators can be identified as linear relations via their graphs. Hence linear relations can be interpreted as multi-valued linear operators. In the context of ADAEs (
1
), the linear relations

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{l}&=\,\left\{\ (y,z)\in Z\times Z\ \left|\ \vphantom{(y,z)\in Z\times Z}\exists\,x\in{\rm dom}\,A\;\text{s.t.}\;y=Ex\,\wedge z=Ax\ \right.\right\},\\
L_{r}&=\,\left\{\ (x,w)\in{\rm dom}\,A\times X\ \left|\ \vphantom{(x,w)\in{\rm dom}\,A\times X}Ew=Ax\ \right.\right\},\end{split}

(6)

will be used. Next, we show that classical and mild solutions can be expressed by means of the linear relations
L_{r}
and
L_{l}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
,
Z
be normed spaces, let
E:X\to Z
be linear and bounded, and let
A:X\supset{\rm dom}\,A\to Z
be linear, densely defined and closed, let
t_{f}>0
,
f:[0,t_{f}]\to Z
and
x_{0}\in X
. Further, let
L_{l}\subset Z\times Z
and be defined as in (
6
).

(a)

Let
x:[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow X
be a classical solution of the ADAE (
1
). Then

\displaystyle Ex(0)=Ex_{0}\text{ and }\left(Ex(t),\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)-f(t)\right)\in L_{l}\;\;\forall t\in[0,t_{f}].

(b)

Conversely, if
z\in C^{1}((0,t_{f});Z)\cap C([0,t_{f}];Z)
with
z(0)=Ex_{0}
fulfills

\displaystyle\left(z(t),\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}z(t)-f(t)\right)\in L_{l}\;\;\forall t\in[0,t_{f}],

(7)

then there exists some
x:[0,t_{f}]\to{\rm dom}\,A
with
z(t)=Ex(t)
for all
t\in[0,t_{f}]
. This function
x
is a classical solution of (
1
).

(c)

Assume that the ADAE (
1
) is regular, let
f\in L^{1}([0,t_{f}];Z)
and
\lambda\in\rho(E,A)
. If
x:[0,t_{f}]\to X
is a mild solution of (
1
), then

Lemma 3.3

Let

(b)

Another important concept in conjunction with regular ADAEs are
pseudo-resolvents
in the sense of Hille
[
23

, see also
[

Definition 3.4

(Pseudo-resolvent)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be open. A mapping
R:\Omega\rightarrow L(X)
is called a pseudo-resolvent, if the following resolvent identity holds

\displaystyle\frac{R(\lambda)-R(\mu)}{\mu-\lambda}=R(\lambda)R(\mu),\quad\text{for all $\lambda,\mu\in\Omega$, $\lambda\neq\mu$.}

(11)

Note that pseudo-resolvents are holomorphic with
R^{\prime}(\lambda)=-R(\lambda)^{2}
. Further, by using

R(\mu)=R(\lambda)(I-(\lambda-\mu)R(\lambda))^{-1},

we can use the Neumann series
[
2
, Sec. 5.7]
to obtain that a power series expansion of the resolvent at
\lambda_{0}\in\Omega
is given by

R(\mu)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)^{n}R(\lambda_{0})^{n+1}.

(12)

Any regular ADAEs can be associated with the
left and right resolvents

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} R_{l}:&&\rho(E,A)\to&\,L(Z),&&\text{with}\quad R_{l}(\lambda)=E(A-\lambda E)^{-1},\\
R_{r}:&&\rho(E,A)\to&\,L(X)&&\text{with}\quad R_{r}(\lambda)=(A-\lambda E)^{-1}E,\end{aligned}

(13)

and it is straightforward to verify that these mappings are pseudo-resolvents in the sense of Definition
3.4
.

By using Lemma
3.3
, we see that, for
\mu\in\rho(E,A)
, the (classical, weak, mild) solutions
x
of the ADAE (
1
) are connected to those of the
pseudo-resolvent differential equation

\displaystyle\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}R_{r}(\mu)x_{\mu}(t)=x_{\mu}(t)+g_{\mu}(t),

(14)

via

x_{\mu}(t)=e^{-\mu t}x(t)
and
g_{\mu}(t)=e^{-\mu t}(A-\mu E)^{-1}f(t)
for all
t\in I
.
Our solution theory for ADAEs presented in this article is indeed based on the solution of pseudo-resolvent differential equations (
14
) (or an analogous pseudo-resolvent differential equation for
R_{l}
).

We further use the close connection between pseudo-resolvents and
degenerate semigroups
.

Definition 3.5

(Degenerate semigroup)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space. A strongly continuous mapping
T:(0,\infty)\rightarrow L(X)
that satisfies

\displaystyle T(t+s)=T(t)T(s),\quad\text{for all $s,t>0$,}

(15)

and
\sup_{0<t\leq 1}\|T(t)\|<\infty
is called degenerate semigroup.

We will oftentimes assume that
X
is reflexive. In this case, it can be concluded from
[
4
, Cor. 2.2]
that the strong limit
T(0):=\lim_{\tau\searrow\,0}T(\tau)
exists and therefore
T(0)
is bounded. The property (
15
) implies that
T(0)
is a bounded projector onto
X_{0}:={\rm ran}\,T(0)
. The restriction
(T(t)|_{X_{0}})_{t\geq 0}
is then a strongly continuous semigroup on
X_{0}
. Further, for
X_{1}={\rm ran}\,(I-T(0))
, we have
T(t)|_{X_{1}}=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Further note that for any degenerate semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
there exist
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
M\geq 1
, such that

\|T(t)\|\leq Me^{\omega t}\quad\forall\,t\geq 0,

which we refer to as
\omega
-stability
of
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
. If
\omega<0
we say that the semigroup is
exponentially stable
. For an
\omega
-stable degenerate semigroup, the Laplace transform defines a pseudo-resolvent

\displaystyle R(\lambda)z:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}T(t)z{\rm d}t\quad\text{for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\omega}^{+}$,}

(16)

see
[
3
, Prop. 2.2]
.

4.1
Linear relations and pseudo-resolvents

We recall some basic notions for

X

[

Linear relations can be viewed as multi-valued linear operators and in this sense one can introduce the well known notions of

and

\displaystyle{\rm dom}\,L

\displaystyle:=\{x\in X\,|\,(x,y)\in L\},

\displaystyle\ker L

\displaystyle:=\{x\in X\,|\,(x,0)\in L\},

\displaystyle{\rm ran}\,L

\displaystyle:=\{y\in Y\,|\,(x,y)\in L\},

\displaystyle{\rm mul}\,L

\displaystyle:=\{y\in Y\,|\,(0,y)\in L\}.

Linear operators can be identified with linear relations via their graphs.

Moreover, one can define the
inverse
of a linear relation

L^{-1}:=\{(y,x)\in Y\times X\,|\,(x,y)\in L\}.

In the following, we assume that
L
is in
X\times X
and consider for

We say that

and
{\rm ran}\,(L-\lambda)=X
. If

is closed, then, by
[

is open.
Moreover, the closedness of

[

implies that

is the graph of a bounded linear operator, which is, for convenience, also denoted by
(L-\lambda)^{-1}\in L(X)
. It is shown e.g. in
[

that the function
\lambda\mapsto(L-\lambda)^{-1}\in L(X)
fulfills the resolvent identity, i.e.,

\displaystyle(L-\lambda)^{-1}-(L-\mu)^{-1}=(\mu-\lambda)(L-\lambda)^{-1}(L-\mu)^{-1},\quad\lambda,\mu\in\rho(L),

(17)

whence
\lambda\mapsto(L-\lambda)^{-1}\in L(X)
on

is a pseudo-resolvent.

Note that (
17

\ker(L-\lambda)^{-1}
and
{\rm ran}\,(L-\lambda)^{-1}
are independent of the particular choice of
\lambda\in\rho(L)

We have seen that a closed linear relation defines a pseudo-resolvent. In the following, we show that also the converse is true in some sense. That is, by starting with a pseudo-resolvent

5
Decoupling of spaces and linear relations

Given a regular ADAE (
1
) and hence a pseudo-resolvent

In the following, we present a space decomposition which is inspired by the ideas on Wong sequences
[
12
,
10
,
46
,
47
]
, namely, for sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle X=\overline{{\rm ran}\,R(\lambda)^{k}}\oplus\ker R(\lambda)^{k}.

(18)

If
X
is finite dimensional this decomposition follows from the famous Weierstraß canonical form
[
18
]
. The smallest
k
such that (
18
) holds, is the index of the associated DAE, see
[
12
, p. 37]
and
[
10
, Prop. 2.3]
. The subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{k}:={\rm ran}\,R(\lambda)^{k}
and
\mathcal{W}_{k}:=\ker R(\lambda)^{k}
for
k\geq 0
are independent from the choice of
\lambda
and called
Wong sequences
, see
[
10
, Lem. 2.2]
. Wong sequences were generalized in
[
46
,
47
,
45
]
to Banach and Hilbert spaces but using geometric approach from
[
10
]
, which imposes strong additional assumptions on the involved operators
E
and
A
.

To establish the space decomposition (
18
) in Banach spaces for
k=1
, a growth condition has been imposed in numerous works
[
4
,
6
,
17
,
27
,
43
]
.

\mathbf{(G_{1})}
: There exists some
M>0
and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\Omega\cap\mathbb{R}
such that

\text{$(\lambda_{n})\to\infty\,$ and }\,\|\lambda_{n}R(\lambda_{n})\|\leq M\quad\forall\,n\geq 1.

It has been pointed out by Kato in
[
27
]
that this condition implies

\displaystyle\ker R(\lambda)\cap\overline{{\rm ran}\,R(\lambda)}=\{0\}\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\Omega,

where it has been moreover shown that, under the additional assumption that
X

locally sequentially weakly
compact
, i.e. every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent
subsequence,

\displaystyle X=\overline{{\rm ran}\,R(\lambda)}\oplus\ker R(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\Omega,

(19)

Note that, by
[
2
, Thm. 8.8]
, this
property is fulfilled, if
X
is reflexive.

In the remainder, we show that a decomposition (
18
) is possible, if the growth condition
\mathbf{(G_{1})}
is replaced by some weaker assumption.
To this end,we present a result that, loosely speaking, kernels and ranges of products of pseudo-resolvents do not alter with the change of the resolvent points.

Lemma 5.1
.

[
42
, Thm. 2.1.2]

Let
X
be a Banach space and let
R:\Omega\rightarrow L(X)
be a pseudo-resolvent and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}\in\Omega
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. Then

\displaystyle\ker R(\lambda_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot R(\lambda_{\ell})=\ker R(\mu_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot R(\mu_{\ell}),

\displaystyle{\rm ran}\,R(\lambda_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot R(\lambda_{\ell})={\rm ran}\,R(\mu_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot R(\mu_{\ell}).

Next we introduce a polynomial growth condition for the resolvent, which generalizes
\mathbf{\mathbf{(G_{1})}}
. Namely, for
k\geq 1
,

\mathbf{(G_{k})}
: There exist
M>0
and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\Omega\cap\mathbb{R}
such that

\text{$(\lambda_{n})\to\infty\,$ and }\,\|\lambda_{n}^{2-k}R(\lambda_{n})\|\leq M\quad\forall\,n\geq 1.

We first show that this growth condition implies that kernels and ranges of pseudo-resolvents stabilize at the
k
th power. We will make use of the dual pseudo-resolvent
R^{\prime}:\Omega\to L(X^{\prime})
, which is given by

\displaystyle\langle R^{\prime}(\lambda)x^{\prime},x\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}:=\langle x^{\prime},R(\lambda)x\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}\quad\forall\penalty\ x\in X,\,x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime},\,\lambda\in\Omega.

(20)

A:X\supseteq{\rm dom}\,A\rightarrow Z

(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
,
k\geq 1
. Then the following holds.

(a)

If

fulfills

\mathbf{(R_{k}^{w})}
holds.

(b)

If
\mathbf{(R_{k}^{w})}
holds then

fulfills
\mathbf{(G_{k+1})}
. Further,
R_{l}
does not fulfill
\mathbf{(G_{k-1})}
.

(c)

If

is bounded and
\mathbf{(R_{k}^{w})}
holds then
R_{l}
fulfills
\mathbf{(G_{k})}
.

(d)

If
A
is bounded and
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
holds then
R_{l}
fulfill
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
.

Proof.

An essential ingredient in the proof is the resolvent identity

\displaystyle(\mu-\lambda)(A-\mu E)^{-1}E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(A-\mu E)^{-1}-(A-\lambda E)^{-1}

(39)

which holds for all
\mu,\lambda\in\rho(E,A)
.

(a)

Assume that
R_{l}
satisfies
\mathbf{(G_{k})}
for some

. Then
\mathbf{(R_{k}^{w})}
follows, since, for some
\mu\in\rho(E,A)
, (

) gives

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\geq\|\lambda_{n}^{-k+2}E(A-\lambda_{n}E)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle=\|\lambda_{n}^{-k+2}(A-\mu E)(A-\mu E)^{-1}E(A-\lambda_{n}E)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\geq\|(A-\mu E)^{-1}\|^{-1}\|\lambda_{n}^{-k+2}(\mu-\lambda_{n})^{-1}((A-\mu E)^{-1}-(A-\lambda_{n}E)^{-1})\|.

(b)

Assume that
\mathbf{(R_{k}^{w})}
holds. Then, by

\|E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|\leq\|E\|\|(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|,

we obtain that
\mathbf{(G_{k+1})}
holds. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\geq\|(A-\mu E)^{-1}\|^{-1}\|(A-\mu E)^{-1}E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle=\|(A-\mu E)^{-1}\|^{-1}\|(\mu-\lambda)^{-1}((A-\mu E)^{-1}-(A-\lambda E)^{-1})\|,

which means that
R_{l}
does not fulfill
\mathbf{(G_{k-1})}
.

(c)

Assume that
A
is bounded. Then (
39
) leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|&=\|(A-\mu E)(A-\mu E)^{-1}E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|\\
&\leq\|A-\mu E\|\|(\mu-\lambda)^{-1}((A-\mu E)^{-1}-(A-\lambda E)^{-1})\|.\end{split}

(40)

This implies that
R_{l}
fulfills
\mathbf{(G_{k})}
.

(d)

Assume that
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
holds for
k\geq 1
and that
A
is bounded.
If
k=1
then (
40
) together with
\mathbf{(R_{1})}
implies
\mathbf{(D_{1})}
. If
k\geq 2
and let
x\in{\rm ran}\,R_{l}(\mu)^{k-1}
. Then
x=E(A-\mu E)^{-1}y
for some

, and we obtain

\displaystyle\|E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}x\|

\displaystyle=\|E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}E(A-\mu E)^{-1}y\|

\displaystyle=\|E(\lambda-\mu)^{-1}((A-\mu E)^{-1}-(A-\lambda E)^{-1})y\|.

Repeating the argument for
k\geq 3
,
y=E(A-\mu E)^{-1}z
and
z\in{\rm ran}\,R_{l}(\mu)^{k-3}
, another application of (
40
) shows that
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
holds. Further,
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
implies

\|\lambda^{-k+1}(\lambda E^{\prime}-A^{\prime})^{-1}\|=\|\lambda^{-k+1}(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|\leq M,

i.e.
(E^{\prime},A^{\prime})
fulfills
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
.
As a consequence,

R_{l}^{\prime}(\lambda)=(E(A-\lambda E)^{-1})^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}-\lambda E^{\prime})^{-1}E^{\prime}=((E^{\prime})^{-1}A^{\prime}-\lambda)^{-1}

\mathbf{(D_{k})}
.

∎

Note that it is straightforward to show that an analogous statement to Proposition

.

Note that for unbounded

\|x\|_{\mu E-A}=\|(\mu E-A)x\|_{Z}
for some

. Then, for all

,

\displaystyle\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|_{\mu E-A}:=\|(\mu E-A)(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|\phantom{:}=\|I+(\mu-\lambda)E(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|.

This can be used to show equality between the index of

and the resolvent index. Namely, by

|\lambda^{-k+1}|\|I+(\mu-\lambda)E(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|=\|\lambda^{-k+1}(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|_{\mu E-A}\leq M

we are led to the existence of some

\|\lambda^{-k+2}E(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|\leq\hat{M}.

In other words,
\mathbf{(G_{k})}
holds. We briefly remark that, by Proposition

, the norm

.

6.2
Comparison with tractability index

As another index concept for infinite dimensional DAEs, we recall the
tractability

index
from
[
37
,
35
]
, see also
[
31
]
. For Hilbert spaces
X
and
Z
, assume that
E\in L(X,Z)
has closed range, and
A:{\rm dom}\,A\to Z
, such that
\rho(E,A)\neq\emptyset
. The ADAE (
1
) is said to have
tractability index

k
, if the following sequence exists

\displaystyle E_{0}:=E,

\displaystyle A_{0}:=A,

(41)

\displaystyle Q_{i}\in L(X)\cap L({\rm dom}\,A),

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{2}=Q_{i},P_{i}^{2}=P_{i},

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle ran}\,Q_{i}=N_{i}:=\ker E_{i},

\displaystyle\ker Q_{i}={\rm ran}\,P_{i}\supset\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}N_{j}

\displaystyle E_{i+1}:=E_{i}-A_{i}Q_{i},

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle dom}\,E_{i+1}={\rm dom}\,E_{i}\cap({\rm dom}\,A_{i}+\ker Q_{i})

\displaystyle A_{i+1}:=A_{i}P_{i},

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle dom}\,A_{i+1}={\rm dom}\,A_{i}+{\rm ran}\,Q_{i},

and
N_{k}=\{0\}
, and, if
k\geq 1
,
N_{k-1}\neq\{0\}
. It is shown in
[
37
, Thm. 4.1]
and that ADAEs with tractability index
k
, and the additional property that

{\rm ran}\,E+A\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}N_{i}
is closed, can be transformed into an upper triangular form

\displaystyle WET=\begin{bmatrix}N&0\\
0&I\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\in L(X_{1}\times X_{2},X_{1}\times X_{2}\times X_{3}),\quad WAT=\begin{bmatrix}I&K\\
0&\mathfrak{U}\\
0&\Gamma\end{bmatrix}

(42)

for some Hilbert spaces
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
and
W\in L(Z,X_{1}\times X_{2})
injective with dense range,
T\in L(X_{1}\times X_{2},X)
bijective, some closed and densely defined operators
\mathfrak{U}:X_{2}\supseteq{\rm dom}\,\mathfrak{U}\rightarrow X_{2}
,
K:X_{2}\supseteq{\rm dom}\,K\rightarrow X_{1}
,
\Gamma:X_{2}\supseteq{\rm dom}\,\Gamma\rightarrow X_{3}
, and some nilpotent
N\in L(X_{1})
with index
k
, i.e.
N^{k}=0
and
N^{k-1}\neq 0
. It has been shown in
[
35
, Sec. 2.2.1]
that there exist (even practically motivated) operator pencils which are not equivalent to a block diagonal pencil. In other words, it is not always possible to achieve the above form (
42
) in which
K=0
. Further note that, if additionally

\ker E_{i}\subset{\rm dom}\,A,\quad i=0,\ldots,k-1,

(43)

then
E_{k}:X\to Z
is bounded, and the construction of
W
in
[
37
]
yields that
W
is bijective, that is, also the inverse of
W
is bounded. It follows further from

[
35
, Thm. 2.8]

that

K\in L\Big({\rm dom}\,\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{smallmatrix}\right],X_{1}\Big).

(44)

The transformed pencil (
42
) corresponds to a triangular ADAE of the form

\displaystyle\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\begin{bmatrix}N&0\\
0&I\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I&K\\
0&\mathfrak{U}\\
0&\Gamma\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(t)\\
f_{2}(t)\\
f_{3}(t)\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}Nx_{1}(0)\\
x_{2}(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}Nx_{10}\\
x_{20}\end{pmatrix},

(45)

for

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}=T^{-1}x(t),\;\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(t)\\
f_{2}(t)\\
f_{3}(t)\end{pmatrix}=Wf(t),\;\begin{pmatrix}x_{10}\\
x_{20}\end{pmatrix}=T^{-1}x_{0}.

Since
N
is nilpotent with index
k
, we obtain that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle(\lambda N-I)^{-1}=-I-\ldots-(\lambda N)^{k-1}.

(46)

Hence, by further invoking (
44
), regularity of the ADAE
(
1
) implies regularity of the “lower right block” of (
45
) with, in particular,

\rho(E,A)=\rho\left(\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{bmatrix}\right)

and

(\lambda WET-WAT)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}(\lambda N-I)^{-1}\penalty\ \penalty\ &(\lambda N-I)^{-1}K\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}\\
-\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\\
0&\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}\\
-\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(47)

Note that the resolvent of

\Big(\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}I\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\Big],\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{smallmatrix}\Big]\Big)

has the form

\begin{bmatrix}sI-\mathfrak{U}\\
-\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(sI-\mathfrak{U}|_{\ker\Gamma})^{-1}\,&\,-\Gamma|_{\ker sI-\mathfrak{U}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\in L(X_{2}\times X_{3},X_{2}).

(48)

Further, by (
44
), we have

K\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}\\
-\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\in L(X_{2}\times X_{3},X_{1})\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\rho(E,A).

In the following proposition we show that - under a certain additional assumption - a finite tractability index implies that
\mathbf{(R_{k_{t}})}
and
\mathbf{(G_{k_{t}})}
hold.

Proposition 6.3
.

Consider an ADAE (
34
) with tractability index
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that the operators in (
42
) satisfy

\displaystyle\exists\penalty\ \omega>0:\quad(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho\left(\begin{bmatrix}I\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{bmatrix}\right),\text{ and }\quad\sup_{\lambda>\omega}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\right\|<\infty,\quad\sup_{\lambda>\omega}\left\|K\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\right\|<\infty.

then
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and
k
is the smallest number such that
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
holds. If, additionally, (
43
),
k
is the smallest number such that
\mathbf{(G_{k})}
holds.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(\omega,\infty)
. The bounded invertibility of
\lambda E-A
follows from (
47
).
Define the constants

M_{1}=\|W\|\,\|T\|,\quad M_{2}:=\sup_{\lambda>\omega}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\right\|,\quad M_{3}:=\sup_{\lambda>\omega}\left\|K\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}\\
\Gamma\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\right\|

Then, by using (
47
), we obtain for all
\lambda\in(\omega,\infty)
that

\displaystyle\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle=\|T(\lambda WET-WAT)^{-1}W\|

\displaystyle\leq M_{1}\|(\lambda WET-WAT)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq M_{1}(\|(\lambda N-I)^{-1}\|+M_{2}+M_{3}).

Then (
46
) immediately implies
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
.
Next, we show that for
k
is the smallest number such that
\mathbf{(R_{k})}
holds. If
k=0
, then nothing has to be shown. Hence, we assume that
k\geq 1
, and we show that
\mathbf{(R_{k-1})}
does not hold. Since the projector chain (
41
) does not stagnate until
k
, we have
\ker E_{k-1}\neq 0
. Let
z\in\ker E_{k-1}
. Then the construction of
W
in
[
37
]
yields that
z\in{\rm ran}\,W
with

W^{-1}z=\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

for some
z_{1}\in\ker N^{k-1}
. This gives, by invoking (
47
),

\displaystyle\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}z\|

\displaystyle\geq\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}\|(\lambda WET-WAT)^{-1}W^{-1}z\|

\displaystyle=\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}\|(\lambda N-I)^{-1}z_{1}\|

\displaystyle=\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda^{i}N^{i}z_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle=\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}\lambda^{k-1}\left\|\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda^{i-k+1}N^{i}z_{1}\right)+N^{k-1}z_{1}\right\|.

Since the latter factor does not converge to zero for
\lambda\to\infty
, we have that
\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}z\|
cannot be bounded on
(\omega,\infty)
by a polynomial of degree smaller than
k-1
. This means that
\mathbf{(R_{k-1})}
is not fulfilled.

Now assume that further (
43
) holds. To show
\mathbf{(G_{k})}
is true, and that
k
is the smallest number with this property, we first make use of the fact that,
by (
47
) and (
48
),

\displaystyle E(\lambda E-A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=W^{-1}(WET)(\lambda WET-WAT)^{-1}W

\displaystyle=W^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}N(\lambda N-I)^{-1}&(\lambda N-I)^{-1}K(\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}|_{\ker\gamma})^{-1}\\
0&(\lambda I-\mathfrak{U}|_{\ker\gamma})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}W.

Since, as previously stated, (
43
) implies boundedness of
W^{-1}
, and, further,
(\lambda N-I)^{-1}=-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda^{i-1}N^{i}
, an application of an argumentation completely analogous to the one in the first part of the proof yields the desired result.
∎

7
Dissipativity-based conditions

In this section, we present some sufficient conditions for a closed linear relation
L
fulfilling
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
. These conditions are based on the
\omega
-dissipativity condition

\displaystyle\forall\lambda>\omega:\quad\|y-\lambda x\|\geq(\lambda-\omega)\|x\|,\quad\forall\,(x,y)\in L.

(49)

Note that the latter implies that
\ker(L-\lambda)=\{0\}
for all
\lambda>\omega
.

First note that, by the Hahn-Banach theorem, for
x\in X
, there exists some
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
with
\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}=\left\langle x^{\prime},x\right\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}
. In other words, the
duality set

\mathcal{J}(x)=\left\{\ x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}\ \left|\ \vphantom{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}}\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}=\left\langle x^{\prime},x\right\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}\ \right.\right\}

is non-empty. In the case where
X
is a Hilbert space, we have
\mathcal{J}(x)=\{x\}
, where we use the identification
X\cong X^{\prime}
by the Riesz representation theorem.

Below, we provide a characterization of the
\omega
-dissipativity condition and a sufficient condition and for a half-axis to be in the resolvent set.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
L
be a closed linear relation in
X
.

(a)

L
fulfills the
\omega
-dissipativity condition (
49
) if, and only if,

\displaystyle\forall\,(x,y)\in L\;\;\exists\,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(x)\,:\quad{\rm Re}\,\left\langle x^{\prime},y\right\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}\leq\omega\|x\|^{2}.

(50)

(b)

Assume that
L
fulfills the
\omega
-dissipativity condition (
49
), and there exists some
\lambda_{0}>\omega
, such that
L-\lambda_{0}
is surjective. Then
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(L)
.

(c)

Assume that
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(L)
. Then
L
fulfills the
\omega
-dissipativity condition (
49
) if, and only if, its pseudo-resolvent
R:\rho(L)\to L(X)
of
L
fulfills

\displaystyle\forall\,\lambda>\omega:\quad\|R(\lambda)\|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda-\omega}.

Proof.

By considering
L-\omega
instead of
L
, it is no loss of generality to assume that
\omega=0
in the sequel.

(a)

First assume that (
50
) holds for
\omega=0
. Let
(x,y)\in L
. Since, in the case where
x=0
, (
49
) trivially holds, we can assume that
x\neq 0
. Let
\lambda>0
, and let
y^{\prime}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}(y-\lambda x)
, and set
z_{\lambda}^{\prime}=y_{\lambda}^{\prime}/\|y_{\lambda}^{\prime}\|
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda\|x\|

\displaystyle\leq\|y-\lambda x\|

\displaystyle=\left\langle y-\lambda x,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle\,\|x\|

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\,\left\langle y,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle-\lambda{\rm Re}\,\left\langle x,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\min\{\lambda\|x\|-{\rm Re}\,\left\langle y,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle,\lambda{\rm Re}\,\left\langle x,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle+\|y\|\}.

This gives

{\rm Re}\,\left\langle y,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\|x\|-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\|y\|\leq{\rm Re}\,\left\langle x,z_{\lambda}^{\prime}\right\rangle.

By Alaoglu’s theorem
[
2
, Sec. 8.7]
, there exists some
z^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
which is an accumulation point of the sequence
(z_{n}^{\prime})
. Then
\|z^{\prime}\|\leq 1
, and

{\rm Re}\,\left\langle y,z^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\|x\|\leq{\rm Re}\,\left\langle x,z^{\prime}\right\rangle.

Hence,
x^{\prime}:=\|x\|\cdot z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(x)
, and

{\rm Re}\,\left\langle y,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq 0,

which shows that (
50
) is true for
\omega=0
.

For the reverse implication, assume that (
50
) holds for
\omega=0
. Since (
50
) is trivially fulfilled for
x=0
, we can now assume that
x
is nonzero. Then

\displaystyle\|y-\lambda x\|\|x\|\geq\left\langle\lambda x-y,x^{\prime}\right\rangle=\lambda\|x\|^{2}-\left\langle y,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq\lambda\|x\|^{2}.

This shows that (
49
) holds for
\omega=0
.

(b)

Assume that
L
is
0
-dissipative
L-\lambda_{0}
is surjective for some
\lambda_{0}>0
. Since
0
-dissipativity directly implies that
\ker L-\lambda_{0}=\{0\}
, we obtain that
(L-\lambda_{0})^{-1}
can be regarded as a bounded operator
R(\lambda_{0})\in L(X)
, and (
49
) (with
\omega=0
) moreover yields that
\|R(\lambda_{0})\|\leq\tfrac{1}{\lambda}_{0}
. Hence, the power series (
12
)
converges for all
\lambda\in(0,2\lambda_{0})
. By the Neumann series
[
2
, Sec. 5.7]
, we have

R(\lambda)=R(\lambda_{0})(I-(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)R(\lambda_{0}))^{-1}.

Now let
x\in X
. Then
for
y=(I-(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)R(\lambda_{0}))^{-1}x
, we have

\displaystyle(R(\lambda)x,x)

\displaystyle=(R(\lambda_{0})(I-(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)R(\lambda_{0}))^{-1}x,x)

\displaystyle=(R(\lambda_{0})y,(I-(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)R(\lambda_{0}))y)

\displaystyle=(R(\lambda_{0})y,y-(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)R(\lambda_{0}))y)\in R(\lambda_{0})^{-1}+(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)

\displaystyle=(L-\lambda_{0})+(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)

\displaystyle=L-\lambda.

Hence,
L-\lambda
is surjective for all
\lambda\in(0,2\lambda_{0})
.
Repeating this argumentation for
3/2\lambda_{0}
, we obtain that

L-\lambda
is surjective for all
\lambda\in(0,3\lambda_{0})
. Proceeding in this way, we see that
L-\lambda
is surjective for all
\lambda>0
.

(c)

By rephrasing
x\rightsquigarrow R(\lambda)y
,
y-\lambda x\rightsquigarrow x
in (
49
), we obtain that
\omega
-dissipativity is equivalent to

\displaystyle\forall\,\lambda>\omega:\quad\|y\|\geq(\lambda-\omega)\|R(\lambda)y\|,\quad\forall\,y\in X,

and the desired result follows immediately.

∎

If the linear relation is represented by an operator pencil
\lambda E-A
via
L=L_{l}=AE^{-1}
, then (
49
) is equivalent to

\forall\lambda>\omega:\quad\|(\lambda E-A)x\|\geq(\lambda-\omega)\|Ex\|,\quad\forall\,x\in{\rm dom}\,A,

(51)

which is, by Proposition
7.1
(
a
), equivalent to

\forall\,x\in X\;\;\exists\,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(Ex)\,:\quad{\rm Re}\,\left\langle z^{\prime},(A-\omega E)x\right\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}\leq 0.

If, additionally,
X
is a Hilbert space, the latter is equivalent to

{\rm Re}\,(Ex,(A-\omega E)x)_{X}\leq 0,\quad\forall x\in{\rm dom}\,A,

where, hereby,
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
stands for the inner product in
X
. Note that ADAEs with this property have recently been studied in
[
26
]
.

As a consequence of the above findings, we can formulate a result on regularity of operator pencils belonging to a dissipative relation, and give an estimate for the left resolvent. The latter has also been obtained for the case of Hilbert spaces
[
26
, Thm. 3.6]
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
X
,
Z
be Banach spaces, let
E\in L(X,Z)
, and let
A:X\supseteq{\rm dom}\,A\rightarrow Z
be closed and densely defined, such that
L_{l}=AE^{-1}
is
\omega
-dissipative for some
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
\ker E\cap\ker A=\{0\}
, and
\lambda E-A
is surjective for some
\lambda>\omega
, then
(\omega,\infty)\subset\rho(E,A)
, and

\displaystyle\forall\lambda>\omega:\quad\|E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda-\omega}.

(52)

In particular, the left resolvent
\lambda\mapsto E(A-\lambda E)^{-1}
fulfills
\mathbf{(D_{1})}
with
M=1
.

Proof.

Proposition
7.1
(
b
) implies that
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(L)
and hence

\lambda E-A
is surjective for all
\lambda>\omega
. Furthermore, (
51
) together with
\ker E\cap\ker A=\{0\}
yields
\ker\lambda E-A=\{0\}
for all
\lambda>\omega
. Thus,
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
. Further, (
52
) follows from Proposition
7.1
(
c
).
∎

As a consequence of the above result, (
50
)
implies the existence of an
\omega
-stable degenerate semigroup, see also
[
26
]
for the case of Hilbert spaces.

Corollary 7.3
.

Under the assumptions of Corollary
7.2
and, further,
Z
is locally sequentially weakly
compact, it holds that

Z=\overline{{\rm ran}\,E(A-\mu E)^{-1}}\oplus\ker E(A-\mu E)^{-1}\quad\forall\,\mu>\omega.

(53)

Moreover, the operator
A_{R}
as in (
26
) generates an
\omega
-stable semigroup

Remark 8.4
.

(i)

In
[
37
]
inhomogeneous ADAEs in Hilbert spaces where solved based on the Laplace transform. Here it was assumed that
\mathbb{C}_{\omega}^{+}\subseteq\rho(E,A)
for some
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and that the tractability index is finite, see Section
6
. Here, instead of (
60
), the ADAE
E\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}x(t)=Ax(t)+f(t)
,
t\in[0,t_{f}]
, was considered. Hence for the existence of solutions, the stronger assumption
f\in W^{k,1}([0,t_{f}];X)
is required.

(ii)

The differentiability assumption on
f_{K}
can be relaxed if
R(\mu)^{i}f_{K}=0
for some
i<k-1
. Then it is sufficient to assume
f_{K}\in W^{i,2}([0,t_{f}],\ker R(\mu)^{k})
for the existence of a classical solution and
f_{K}\in W^{i-1,2}([0,t_{f}],\ker R(\mu)^{k})
for the existence of a mild (weak) solution.

(iii)

If the pseudo-resolvent
R
associated with an ADAE fulfills (
\mathbf{D_{1}}
), then (
67
) is trivially fulfilled. Hence Proposition
8.3
guarantees the existence of mild solution if
f_{K}\in L^{2}([0,t_{f}],\ker R(\mu))
. It follows from Proposition
7.6
that the assumption (
\mathbf{D_{1}}
) is e.g. fulfilled for standard boundary control systems that are rewritten as ADAEs. In this particular case, the conditions for the existence of a mild solution is slightly weaker then weak differentiability assumptions on the inputs which were used in
[
25
]
.

If
X
is a Hilbert space and
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
holds, then we can use the space decomposition
X=\mathcal{V}_{k}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{k}\oplus\ldots\mathcal{W}_{1}
and the staircase form (
31
) applied to the pseudo-resolvent to solve the pseudo-resolvent equation (
61
) and hence the inhomogeneous ADAE (
60
).

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space and
R:\Omega\rightarrow L(X)
be a pseudo-resolvent which fulfills
\mathbf{(D_{k})}
for some
k\geq 1
and let the associated operator
A_{R}
be the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup and
f=\hat{f}_{\mu}:[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow X
the inhomogeneity in (
61
). Assume that
f_{i}:=P_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}f\in C^{k+1-i}([0,t_{f}],X)
holds for all
i=1,\ldots,k
and
f_{k+1}:=P_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}f\in C([0,t_{f}],X)
. Then for each initial value
x_{k+1}^{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
there exists a unique classical solutions of (
61
) with
x_{k+1}(0)=x_{0}
.

Proof.

According to the space decomposition
X=\mathcal{V}_{k}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{k}\oplus\ldots\mathcal{W}_{1}
we write
x=(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{1})
, and abbreviate
R_{i+1,j}:=P_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}R(\mu)|_{\mathcal{W}_{j}}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,k-1
and
R_{k+1,j}:=P_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}R(\mu)|_{\mathcal{W}_{j}}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
. Therefore, (
61
) can be rewritten using
the staircase form (
31
)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}R(\mu)|_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}&R_{k+1,k}&R_{k+1,k-1}&\ldots&R_{k+1,1}\\
0&0&R_{k,k-1}&\ldots&R_{k,1}\\
&\ddots\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ &&\ddots&\\
&&\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \ddots&&R_{2,1}\\
&&&0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ &0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\begin{bmatrix}x_{k+1}\\
x_{k}\\
\vdots\\
x_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{k+1}\\
x_{k}\\
\vdots\\
x_{1}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}f_{k+1}\\
f_{k}\\
\vdots\\
f_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(74)

Step-by-step solution of (
74
) starting from the last row results in
x_{1}=-f_{1}
and

x_{i}=-f_{i}-\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}R_{i,j}x_{j},\quad i=2,\ldots,k,

where the derivatives exist and are continuous since
f_{i}\in C^{k+1-i}([0,t_{f}],X)
. Furthermore,
\sum_{j=1}^{k}R_{k+1,j}x_{j}
is continuously differentiable.

It remains to derive a classical solution
x_{k+1}
for the first line in (
74
). To this end we combine the fixed terms and rewrite the first line with continuous inhomogeneity
f_{k+1}+\tfrac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\sum_{j=1}^{k}R_{k+1,j}x_{j}
. Therefore we can apply Proposition
8.3
(a) which yields that for all
x_{k+1}^{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
there exists a unique classical solution
x_{k+1}
such that
x_{k+1}(0)=x_{k+1}^{0}
holds. This finishes the proof.
∎

9.1
Heat-wave coupling

The following system of PDEs from
[
7
,
39
]
describes a wave equation on the interval
(-1,0)
coupled at
\xi=0
with a heat equation on the interval
(0,1)

u_{tt}(t,\xi)=u_{\xi\xi}(t,\xi),\penalty\ \xi\in(-1,0),\quad w_{t}(t,\xi)=w_{\xi\xi}(t,\xi),\penalty\ \xi\in(0,1).

Furthermore, we impose Dirichlet boundary conditions at both endpoints, interface conditions at
\xi=0
and initial values

\displaystyle w(t,1)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle u_{\xi}(-1,t)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle u_{\xi}(t,0)

\displaystyle=w_{\xi}(t,0),

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,0)

\displaystyle=w(t,0),

\displaystyle w(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=w_{0}(\xi),

\displaystyle u_{t}(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=v_{0}(\xi),

\displaystyle u(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(\xi).

We follow
[
39
]
and write this altogether as an ADAE, using the characteristic function
\chi_{I}
on an interval
I
, in the following way on
X=Z=L^{2}([-1,1])\times L^{2}([-1,1])

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&\chi_{(-1,0)}I\end{pmatrix}}_{=:E}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t,\xi)\\
x_{2}(t,\xi)\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&\partial_{\xi}\\
\partial_{\xi}&-\chi_{(0,1)}\end{pmatrix}}_{=:A}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}(t,\xi)\\
x_{2}(t,\xi)\end{pmatrix},\quad(Ex)(0)=Ex_{0}

\displaystyle x_{1}(t,\xi)=\begin{cases}u_{t}(\xi,t),&\text{if $\xi<0$,}\\
w(\xi,t),&\text{if $\xi>0$,}\end{cases},\quad x_{2}(\xi,t)=\begin{cases}u_{\xi}(\xi,t),&\text{if $\xi<0$,}\\
w_{\xi}(\xi,t),&\text{if $\xi>0$,}\end{cases}

where
E
is clearly bounded and
A
is densely defined on
X
with domain

\displaystyle{\rm dom}\,A

\displaystyle:=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X:x_{1},x_{2}\in W^{1,2}([-1,1]),x_{2}(-1)=x_{1}(1)=0\right\},

\displaystyle x_{0}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}(\xi)\\
v_{0}(\xi)\end{pmatrix},\,\text{if $\xi<0$ and}\quad x_{0}(\xi):=\begin{pmatrix}w_{0}(\xi)\\
0\end{pmatrix},\,\text{if $\xi>0$.}

The invertibility of the off-diagonal entries
\partial_{\xi}
on their respective domains implies that
A
is also invertible and therefore
0\in\rho(E,A)\neq\emptyset
. Furthermore, we have from integration by parts that for all
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\rm dom}\,A

\displaystyle(Ax,x)_{L^{2}([-1,1])^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&\partial_{\xi}\\
\partial_{\xi}&-\chi_{(0,1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)_{L^{2}(-1,1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{\xi}x_{2}(\xi)\overline{x_{1}}(\xi)d\xi+\int_{-1}^{1}(\partial_{\xi}x_{1}(\xi)-\chi_{(0,1)}(\xi)x_{2}(\xi))\overline{x_{2}(\xi)}d\xi

\displaystyle=\underbrace{[x_{1}(\xi)x_{2}(\xi)]_{\xi=-1}^{1}}_{=0}-\int_{-1}^{1}x_{2}(\xi)\overline{\partial_{\xi}x_{1}}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +\int_{-1}^{1}(\partial_{\xi}x_{1}(\xi)-\chi_{(0,1)}(\xi)x_{2}(\xi))\overline{x_{2}(\xi)}d\xi.

Therefore, we have for all
x=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in{\rm dom}\,A
that

{\rm Re}\,(Ax,x)_{L^{2}(-1,1)^{2}}=-\int_{-1}^{1}\chi_{(0,1)}(\xi)|x_{2}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\leq 0

which implies with Proposition
7.5
that
\mathbf{(D_{2})}
holds for
R_{l}
. Furthermore, the condition (
32
) is fulfilled, which implies that
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}EA^{-1}\\
I_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right]({\rm ran}\,E)
is the graph of a maximal dissipative operator which can be used to obtain the solutions of that ADAE. Indeed, to verify (
32
), let
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\ker E
then
x_{1}=0
and
x_{2}\equiv 0
on
(-1,0)
. If
A(0,x_{2})\in{\rm ran}\,E
then looking at the second component yields
x_{2}=0
on
(0,1)
and therefore
x=0
. Note that this condition can be used to obtain the solution of the heat-wave system using the staircase form (
32
).

9.2
Distributed element circuits

An electrical circuit with distributed resistance
R
, capacitance
C
and inductivity
L
on the spatial domain
[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
can be described by the system of partial differential equations

\displaystyle L(x)\dot{I}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-R(x)I(t,x)-\frac{\partial V(t,x)}{\partial x},

\displaystyle C(x)\dot{V}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial I(t,x)}{\partial x}-G(x)V(t,x),

\displaystyle V(t,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(t),\,-I(1,t)=i_{1}(t)

where we assume that
L,C,R,G\in L^{\infty}([0,1])
are nonnegative and that
L
and
C
are bounded away from zero on their support. This can be rewritten as an ADAE with
X=L^{2}([0,1])\times L^{2}([0,1])
,
Z=L^{2}([0,1])\times L^{2}([0,1])\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

E=\begin{pmatrix}L(\cdot)&0\\
0&C(\cdot)\\
0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}-R(\cdot)&-\partial_{\xi}\\
-\partial_{\xi}&-G(\cdot)\\
\delta_{1}&0\\
0&\delta_{0}\end{pmatrix}

with
{\rm dom}\,A:=W^{1,2}([0,1])\times W^{1,2}([0,1])
and
\delta_{x}
is the point evaluation at
x\in[0,1]
. We show in the following that the resolvent
R_{l}
fulfills
\mathbf{(D_{2})}
by applying Proposition
7.5
.

First, note that
[
41
, Thm. 2.1]
implies that
E
has closed range. The dissipativity of
A
can be shown analogously as for the heat-wave coupling.

Next, we show that
0\in\rho(A)
. To prove invertibility of
A
, we consider

W^{1,2}_{x,0}([0,1]):=\{g\in W^{1,2}([0,1])\penalty\ |\penalty\ g(x)=0\},\penalty\ x=0,1,\quad\partial_{\xi,x}:=\partial_{\xi}|_{W^{1,2}_{x,0}([0,1])}.

and first show the invertibility of

\displaystyle\hat{A}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}-R(\cdot)|_{W^{1,2}_{1,0}([0,1])}&-\partial_{\xi,0}\\
-\partial_{\xi,1}&-G(\cdot)|_{W^{1,2}_{1,0}([0,1])}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle{\rm dom}\,\hat{A}

\displaystyle:=W^{1,2}_{1,0}([0,1])\times W^{1,2}_{0,0}([0,1]).

Clearly, interchanging the block columns of
\hat{A}
preserves the invertibility and hence the invertibility of
\hat{A}
would follow from the invertibility of
\partial_{\xi,0}
together with the invertibility of the Schur complement
\partial_{\xi,1}-G\partial_{\xi,0}^{-1}R
. This can easily be concluded from Fredholm theory, see
[
21
, p. 358]
.
Indeed,
\partial_{\xi,1}-G\partial_{\xi,0}^{-1}R
is a Fredholm operator with index zero, since
\partial_{\xi,1}
is invertible and

\partial_{\xi,0}^{-1}
as well as
G\partial_{\xi,0}^{-1}R
are compact. To prove the invertibility of the Schur complement it is therefore enough to prove that it is injective. Assume conversely that there is an
g\in W^{1,2}_{1,0}([0,1])
satisfying

\partial_{\xi,1}g(x)-R\partial_{\xi,0}^{-1}Gg(x)=g^{\prime}(x)-R(x)\int_{0}^{x}G(\tau)g(\tau)d\tau=0,\quad x\in[0,1].

If we assume that
g(0)>0
then
R,G\geq 0
implies that the derivative is nonnegative for all
x\in[0,1]
. Invoking
g\in W^{1,2}_{1,0}([0,1])
, leads to
0=g(1)\geq g(0)=1
, the desired contradiction. The same conclusion holds for
g(0)<0
.
Thus
g=0
, and hence
\partial_{\xi,1}-G\partial_{\xi,0}^{-1}R
is invertible, meaning that
\hat{A}
is also invertible.

This together with the surjectivity of the point evaluations
\delta_{0},\delta_{1}
, implies that
A
is surjective. Moreover,
A
is injective, since
A(g_{1},g_{2})=0
with
g_{i}\in W^{1,2}([0,1])
,
i=1,2
implies
\delta_{1}g_{1}=0
and
\delta_{0}g_{2}=0
. Hence
g=(g_{1},g_{2})\in W^{1,2}_{0,0}([0,1])\times W^{1,2}_{1,0}([0,1])
. The invertibility of
\hat{A}
implies
g=0
.
