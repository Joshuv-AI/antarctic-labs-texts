Landau and leading singularities in arbitrary space-time dimensions Preprint: MPP-2022-129
Wojciech Flieger 1, William J. Torres Bobadilla 1

Abstract
Using the decomposition of the D D -dimensional space-time into parallel and perpendicular subspaces, we study and prove a connection between Landau and leading singularities for N N -point one-loop Feynman integrals by applying the multi-dimensional theory of residues. We show that if D = N D=N and D = N + 1 D=N+1 , the leading singularity corresponds to the inverse of the square root of the leading Landau singularity of the first and second type, respectively. We make use of this outcome to systematically provide differential equations of Feynman integrals in canonical forms and the extension of the connection of these singularities at the multi-loop level by exploiting the loop-by-loop approach. Illustrative examples with the calculation of Landau and leading singularities are provided to supplement our results.

By elaborating on the decomposition of the space-time dimension into two independent subspaces (parallel and perpendicular) and an extensive use of the multivariate theory of residues given by Leray, we prove that for one-loop integrals leading singularities are expressed as the inverse of the square root of Landau singularities.

II.2
One-loop decomposition

The decomposition into parallel and perpendicular subspaces will help us to make sense of the formal
D
-dimensional integral (
1

The one-loop scalar Feynman integral in the discussed decomposition (

\displaystyle J_{N}^{\left(1\right),D}=\frac{1}{\imath\pi^{D/2}}\int\prod_{i=1}^{D_{\parallel}}d\ell_{\parallel}^{i}\int\,d^{D_{\perp}}\ell_{\perp}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}}\,,

\displaystyle J^{\left(1\right),D}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{D_{\perp}-1}}{\imath\pi^{D/2}}\int\prod_{i=1}^{D_{\parallel}}d\ell_{\parallel}^{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,d\ell_{\perp}\ell_{\perp}^{D-D_{\parallel}-1}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}}\,,

As we will be mostly concerned with the integrands, this form is enough for our analysis. In general, convergence of (

To elucidate further the convergence of integral (

Integration measure in one-loop Feynman integrals, as suggested by Ref.

\displaystyle d^{D}\ell_{1}=d^{4}\ell_{1,[4]}\,d^{-2\epsilon}\ell_{1,[-2\epsilon]}\,.

\displaystyle d^{4-2\epsilon}\ell_{1}=d\ell_{1}^{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge d\ell_{1}^{4}\wedge d\mu^{2}\,

With this decomposition in mind, we continue our analysis in parallel and perpendicular subspaces.

The dimension of the parallel subspace is associated with the number of linearly independent external momenta. Let us then assume that this subspace is spanned by independent external momenta,

\displaystyle\ell_{\parallel}^{\alpha}=\ell_{1,\left[D_{\parallel}\right]}^{\alpha}

We now introduce the following change of variables

\displaystyle\ell_{\parallel}^{1}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{D_{\parallel}}p_{j}^{1}a_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\ell_{\parallel}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{D_{\parallel}}p_{j}^{2}a_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\ell_{\parallel}^{D_{\parallel}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{D_{\parallel}}p_{j}^{D_{\parallel}}a_{j}\,.

Now, we can express the differential form

\displaystyle d\ell_{\parallel}^{1}\wedge\ldots d\ell_{\parallel}^{D_{\parallel}}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D_{\parallel}}p_{j}^{1}\,da_{j}\right)\wedge\ldots\wedge\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D_{\parallel}}p_{j}^{D_{\parallel}}\,da_{j}\right)\,,

\displaystyle d\ell_{\parallel}^{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge d\ell_{\parallel}^{D_{\parallel}}=

\displaystyle\det\left(J\right)da_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge da_{D_{\parallel}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}p_{1}^{1}&\ldots&p_{D_{\parallel}}^{1}\\

Notice that the determinant of the Jacobian matrix

To put it in a more useful form, we make use of the identity

\det(J^{T}gJ)=\det(J)^{2}\det(g)\Rightarrow\det(J)=\pm\sqrt{\det(J^{T}gJ)/\det(g)}

where

By calculating the product,

J^{T}gJ=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}p_{1}^{1}&p_{2}^{2}&\ldots&p_{1}^{D_{\parallel}}\\

p_{1}^{2}&\ldots&p_{D_{\parallel}}^{2}\\

(14)

we get,

\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

(15)

Therefore, we have,

\det(J^{T}gJ)=\det(p_{i}\cdot p_{j})\,.

(16)

Compiling all together, we have the following formula for the Jacobian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\left[D_{\parallel}\right]}=\det\left(J\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{\left(-1\right)^{D_{\parallel}-1}\det\left(p_{i}\cdot p_{j}\right)}

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\imath\right)^{D_{\parallel}}\sqrt{-\det\left(p_{i}\cdot p_{j}\right)}\,.

(17)

Feynman propagators

’s,

\displaystyle=\left(\ell_{1}+P_{i}\right)^{2}-m_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{L}\frac{d^{D}\ell_{i}}{\imath\,\pi^{D/2}}

By taking into account the parametrization of loop momenta (

\displaystyle d^{LD_{\parallel}}\ell_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\left[D_{\parallel}\right]}^{(L)}\prod_{i=1}^{L}\prod_{j=1}^{D_{\parallel}}da_{ij}\,,

\displaystyle d^{\frac{L\left(L+1\right)}{2}}\Lambda

III
Landau Singularities

We will focus on the form of the Landau equations in the decomposition of the space-time in terms of parallel and perpendicular components.

Let us first look at what form Landau equations take for our definition of
D
-dimensional integral (
6
).
Landau equations can be derived by requiring that polar sets
D_{j}
are in non-general positions, i.e. when differential
dD_{j}
are not linearly independent together with the requirement that we are confined to a singular variety of the integrand (on-shell conditions).
Thus, we have,

\displaystyle D_{i}=0\,\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,N,

(25a)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}dD_{i}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\left(\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell_{\parallel}^{1}}d\ell_{\parallel}^{1}+\ldots+\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell_{\parallel}^{D_{\parallel}}}d\ell_{\parallel}^{D_{\parallel}}+\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell_{\perp}}d\ell_{\perp}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell_{\parallel}^{1}}\right)d\ell_{\parallel}^{1}+\ldots+\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell_{\perp}}\right)d\ell_{\perp}=0\,.

(25b)

Taking into account the independence of differential
d\ell
the brackets in the last line of (
25
) give the well-known form of the Landau equations.
In principle one can study singularities of subdiagrams by imposing
\alpha_{i}=0
for some
i
, then Eq. (
25a
) in general should be stated as
D_{i}=0\text{ or }\alpha_{i}=0
. However, since we are interested in leading Landau singularities, all
\alpha
’s are non-zero.

We impose that the Landau equations has nontrivial solution for
\alpha
’s which happens when
\det(\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell^{j}})
vanishes. By multiplying by the transpose of the matrix
(\frac{\partial D_{i}}{\partial\ell^{j}})
, this system can be put into the form,

\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\left(q_{i}\cdot q_{j}\right)=0\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,N\,.

(26)

There is one such system for each independent loop in the diagram.
Together with on-shell conditions and momentum conservation,
this gives us the equation for the leading Landau variety which we also call Landau singularity. In practice solving Landau equations for complicated Feynman diagrams can be very difficult. However, for one-loop diagrams, as we have only one equation of the type (
26
) it simplifies a lot.
Thus,
here and in the following, we define, for one-loop diagrams,

\text{LanS}_{n}^{(1)}=\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})\,,\quad i,j=1,\ldots,N\,,

(27)

which should be understood that on-shell conditions and momentum conservation are taken into account. Then, the solution
\text{LanS}_{n}^{(1)}=0
gives us the Landau singularity for one-loop diagrams. In this respect, Eq. (
27
) is given by the Gram determinant of internal momenta which can vanish identically if the number of internal momenta is larger than the dimension of the space. We will study the dimensional dependence of Landau singularities for one-loop diagrams.

In the following, we will explore Landau equations in the parametrization of the loop momenta according to (
4
).
To start, we will observe in one-loop Feynman integrals that solutions to these
equations, because of the decomposition of the dimension,
are cast in
\lambda_{11}=\ell_{\perp}^{2}
once on-shell conditions are imposed.

III.1
One-loop Landau singularities

Because of the decomposition of the space-time dimension into parallel and perpendicular
subspaces that was laid out in Sec.

\text{LanS}_{n}^{(1)}=\lambda_{11}\det\left((p_{i}\cdot p_{j})_{(n-1)\times(n-1)}\right),

(28)

which holds only when on-shell conditions are imposed.
To achieve that, let us first express the Gram determinant of propagators (

in terms of parallel and perpendicular components,

Proposition III.1
.

\begin{split}&{\rm{LanS}}_{n}^{(1)}=\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})=\det((q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}+\lambda_{11})_{n\times n})\\
&=\det((q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel})_{n\times n})\\
&+\lambda_{11}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\det\left[(q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}(1-\delta_{jk}(1-\frac{1}{q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}})))_{n\times n}\right]\\
&=\lambda_{11}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\det\left[(q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}(1-\delta_{jk}(1-\frac{1}{q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}})))_{n\times n}\right]\,,\end{split}

(29)

with
q_{i,\parallel}^{\alpha}=\ell_{1,[D_{\parallel}]}^{\alpha}+P_{i}^{\alpha}
,
according to Fig.

Proof.

We work in a matrix form and write
q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}
as
q_{ij}
;
we compactly express

in a column form as follows,

|Q_{1}+\Lambda_{11},Q_{2}+\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}+\Lambda_{11}|\,,

(30)

where
Q_{j}=(q_{1j},\ldots,q_{nj})^{T}
and
\Lambda_{11}
should be understood as a column vector with all entries equal to
\lambda_{11}
.

We now use multi-linearity of the determinant, starting from the additivity,

\begin{split}&|Q_{1}+\Lambda_{11},Q_{2}+\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}+\Lambda_{11}|\\
&=|Q_{1},Q_{2}+\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}+\Lambda_{11}|\\
&+|\Lambda_{11},Q_{2}+\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}+\Lambda_{11}|\,.\end{split}

(31)

After repeating this procedure until having no more summation
within any of the determinants and using the fact that if two columns of the determinant are the same it vanishes,
we are left with,

\begin{split}&|Q_{1}+\Lambda_{11},Q_{2}+\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}+\Lambda_{11}|\\
&=|Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{n}|+|\Lambda_{11},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{n}|\\
&+|Q_{1},\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}|+\ldots+|Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{11}|\,.\end{split}

(32)

Further, by applying the homogeneity, we get,

\displaystyle|Q_{1}+\Lambda_{11},Q_{2}+\Lambda_{11},\ldots,Q_{n}+\Lambda_{11}|

\displaystyle=|Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{n}|+\lambda_{11}|I,Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{n}|

(33)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{11}|Q_{1},I,\ldots,Q_{n}|+\ldots+\lambda_{11}|Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,I|\,,

where

Moreover, as there are only

Thus, by factoring out
\lambda_{11}
in (
III.1

∎

Now we are in the position to show that the sum in Proposition
III.1
is equal to the Gram determinant of independent external momenta.

Proposition III.2
.

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\det(q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}(1-\delta_{jk}(1-\frac{1}{q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}})))
is equal to
\det\left((p_{i}\cdot p_{j})_{(n-1)\times(n-1)}\right)

Proof.

By making use of Lemma
B.1
, we can write
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\det(q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}(1-\delta_{jk}(1-\frac{1}{q_{i,\parallel}\cdot q_{j,\parallel}})))
as
|I(Q_{2}-Q_{1})(Q_{3}-Q_{1})\ldots(Q_{n}-Q_{1})|

|I^{\prime}(Q^{\prime}_{2}-Q^{\prime}_{1})(Q^{\prime}_{3}-Q^{\prime}_{1})\ldots(Q^{\prime}_{n}-Q^{\prime}_{1})|

where, in particular,

By the Laplace expansion along the first column, we get the

(n-1)\times(n-1)

(-1)^{n+1}|(Q^{\prime\prime}_{2}-Q^{\prime\prime}_{1})(Q^{\prime\prime}_{3}-Q^{\prime\prime}_{1})\ldots(Q^{\prime\prime}_{n}-Q^{\prime\prime}_{1})|

Then, by subtracting the last row, which has elements

For the one-loop scalar bubble the parametrization of the loop momentum,

\displaystyle=m_{1}^{2}-p_{1}^{2}\,a_{1}^{2}\,,

One-loop triangle integral

We continue with the explicit calculation of the Landau singularity of the one-loop triangle

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{K}}\left(p_{1}^{2},p_{2}^{2},p_{3}^{2}\right)}\bigg[2p_{2}^{2}\left(m_{1}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}+p_{1}^{2}\right)

(49a)

\displaystyle a_{2}

IV
Leading Singularities

By continuing with the same strategy in the decomposition
of space-time dimension presented in Sec.
II.1
,
we now focus on leading singularities of one-loop Feynman integrals.
By leading singularities we understand residues in Leray’s sense evaluated over all polar sets, i.e.,

LS=\int_{\delta^{m}\sigma}\phi=(2\pi i)^{m}\int_{\sigma}res^{m}[\phi]\,,

(52)

where
\phi
is a closed form with
m
polar sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
,
\sigma\in Z_{p-m}(S_{1}\cap\ldots\cap S_{m})
is a cycle and
\delta^{m}\sigma
is its composed co-boundary (see Appendix
A
). It can happen that the number of polar sets is larger than the dimension of the space, in this situation Leinartas decomposition
leinartas1978
;
Aizenberg1994
allows to write
\phi
as a sum of forms, each having at most as many polar sets as the dimension of the space.

To carry out this analysis, we present in this section illustrative computations
of selected one-loop topologies, and provide the Reader with
additional examples in Appendix
C
.

Additionally, because of the pattern displayed by explicit calculations
of leading singularities in the various examples,
we conjecture and prove the structure of these singularities in particular space-time dimensions – a connection between Landau and leading singularities.
Similar results to those presented in this section can already be found in
Abreu:2017ptx
.
However, whereas authors of the mentioned paper focused on dimensional regularisation and
limited the analysis to a
class of integrals where the integer dimension of the space-time is always even, omitting cases like the one-loop scalar triangle in the three space-time dimensions,
we study the integrals in arbitrary space-time dimensions.

In the last part of this section, we consider differential equations of one-loop Feynman integrals,
showing that from the knowledge of their leading singularities
the construction of differential equations in canonical form
is straightforwardly carried out.
We illustrate this with explicit calculations.

Connection between Landau and leading singularities

Inspired by the results obtained at one-loop with higher multiplicity

VI
Conclusions

Elaborating on the decomposition of the space-time dimension into two independent and complementary subspaces, parallel and perpendicular, in this paper, we carried out a study of the analytic properties of Feynman integrals within this framework. We observed that the various features of Feynman integrals can systematically be tracked down to an analysis at the integrand level, in which techniques and results based on integrand reduction methods were crucial for the results presented in this paper.

Since it is known that Feynman integrals display singularities depending on kinematic
invariants in given space-time dimensions, we considered Landau and leading singularities as well
as the connections between them.
For Landau singularities, because of the way how the space-time dimension was decomposed,
we provided and proved the algorithm to calculate these singularities
by focusing only on a linear system of equations.
We observed that Landau singularities can be cast in the single variable (
39
), present
in the loop-momentum parametrization, whose value is determined by imposing
on-shell conditions.

Regarding leading singularities,
we observed for one-loop scalar Feynman integrals that the number of integrations is lessened to the number of independent external momenta (
E
). This feature, obtained as a product of the general decomposition of the space-time dimension in terms of parallel and perpendicular directions,
led us to perform an integrand analysis, finding a connection between leading and Landau singularities. In detail, we noted and proved that leading singularities in
D=E
and
D=E+1
, respectively, correspond to the inverse of the square root of the leading Landau singularity of the first and second type. This connection was at first glance noted with the explicit calculation of leading and Landau singularities of up-to six-point one-loop scalar Feynman integrals. Then, to prove at all multiplicities, we use the multi-dimensional theory of residues provided by Leray.

As a by-product of the connection between Landau and leading singularities,
we observed that one-loop scalar Feynman integrals can be cast in a very simple product of
d\log
forms.
We checked this for integrals up to six points and stated the conjecture that it holds
for all one-loop scalar Feynman integrals.
Additionally, we briefly discussed the connection of our work with the method of differential equations in dimensional regularisation.
In this matter, the knowledge of leading singularities for an arbitrary one-loop graph
makes the problem of finding the canonical form for a differential equation much simpler.

On top of the aforementioned one-loop analysis,
we obtained, promising preliminary results for Feynman multi-loop integrals by applying developed methods.
We considered particular families of integrals, namely
L
-loop three- and four-point ladder diagrams.
Treating them as iterated integrals, we were able to apply Leray’s residues in the loop-by-loop approach to establish their leading singularities.

Our work which was focused mostly on the one-loop Feynman integrals just scratch the surface of possible applications of methods presented here and other ideas coming from mathematics. We were able to identify possible directions for further studies

1.

The first natural extension of this work is to investigate the application of multi-dimensional residues to general multi-loop Feynman integrals, starting from two loops, and its connection to the notion of leading singularity.
At two loops, one can already observe that the number of poles is rarely equal to the degree of the form, so even by taking the global residue, we cannot reduce this form to the zero form, which means that there is some integration which is left. Here the crucial point will be the determination of cycles of integration. In the one-loop case, this problem was already studied in
Abreu:2017ptx
in the context of the calculation of non-maximal residues.

2.

Further investigation of the connection between the differential equation method, and Landau singularities.
On the one hand, it would be interesting to see how much we can learn from the knowledge of leading singularities to find the canonical form for differential equations for Feynman integrals
as well as using the knowledge of Landau singularities in integrals that are known to be described
by elliptic curved and beyond.
On the other hand, it would be very interesting to see if one can recover the full alphabet from Landau singularities by considering all possible residues for a given diagram. In that context, it would also be interesting to look at the connection to cluster algebras.

3.

Lastly, it would be very interesting to investigate the
d\log
structure of Feynman integrals.
The multi-dimensional residues can shed new light on this issue.
It would be ideal to find a set of criteria which determine whether a given differential form has a full
d\log
structure. In connection to this, it also would be interesting to see if it is possible to determine the geometry necessary for a given Feynman diagram by considering its Landau singularities.

All the above perspectives can be explored within the approach proposed in this work.

Appendix A
Leray residues

We will give a brief introduction, without any proofs, to the multi-dimensional theory of residues due to Leray.
It is one of the possible extensions of the one-dimensional residue theory.
It is particularly useful in our application as it introduces a residue operator that acts directly on differential forms.
For a detailed presentation and proofs of this theory, we refer the Reader to the original work of Leray
Leray_1959
or
Pham2011full
;
Aizenberg1994
.

Let
X
be a complex analytic manifold and
S
a closed analytic submanifold of complex codimension 1.
If
\phi

X-S
with a pole of first order on
S
, then in a neighbourhood
U_{a}

of any point

the form
\phi
can be represented as,

\phi=\frac{ds}{s}\wedge\psi+\theta\,,

(108)

where,
s=s_{a}(z)
, is a defining function for the manifold
S
in
U_{a}
,
and
\psi
,
\theta
are forms which are regular on
U_{a}
. The restriction
\psi|_{S}
is called the residue form of the form
\phi
,
\psi|_{S}\equiv res[\phi]
.

Theorem A.1
.

For an arbitrary closed form
\phi
of degree
p
on
X-S
and a cycle

(149)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{64}\Big[-s_{12}^{2}s_{45}^{2}s_{234}^{2}-\left(s_{34}s_{61}s_{123}+\left(s_{23}s_{56}-s_{123}s_{234}\right)s_{345}\right){}^{2}

\displaystyle+2s_{12}s_{45}\big(s_{123}s_{234}\left(s_{234}s_{345}-s_{34}s_{61}\right)+

\displaystyle+s_{23}s_{56}\left(2s_{34}s_{61}-s_{234}s_{345}\right)\big)\Big]+m^{2}\det\left(p_{i}\cdot p_{j}\right)\,,

(150)

with the Gram determinant calculated in terms of the kinematic scales (

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
IV.2
in momentum representation

Proposition D.1
.

The leading singularity of an
n
-point one-loop Feynman integral in
D=n
space-time dimensions is equal to
\pm 1/\left(2^{n}\sqrt{(-1)^{D-1}\rm{LanS}}\right)

Proof.

The one-loop Feynman integrand
\omega
has the following form,

\omega=\frac{d^{n}k}{D_{1}\ldots D_{n}}=\frac{dk_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dk_{n}}{D_{1}\ldots D_{n}}\,,

(152)

where
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})
is an
n
-dimensional loop momentum vector and
D_{i}=(k+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}p_{j})^{2}-m_{i}^{2}
with
p_{j}=(p_{j1},\ldots,p_{jn})
being
n
-dimensional external momenta. From the definition, the Leading singularity is the highest co-dimensional residue of the Feynman integral. Thus, let us calculate composite Leray residue of degree
n
of the
\omega
,

res^{n}[\omega]=\frac{dk_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dk_{n}}{dD_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dD_{n}}\,.

(153)

Each
dD_{i}
is given by,

\begin{split}dD_{i}=2&(k_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}p_{j1})dk_{1}+(-2)(k_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}p_{j2})dk_{2}+\ldots+\\
&+(-2)(k_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}p_{jn})dk_{n}\,.\end{split}

(154)

Hence, we get,

\displaystyle res^{n}[\omega]=\frac{dk_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dk_{n}}{\Big(2k_{1}dk_{1}+\ldots+(-2)k_{n}dk_{n}\Big)\wedge\ldots\wedge\Big(2(k_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{j1})dk_{1}+\ldots+(-2)(k_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{jn})dk_{n}\Big)}\,.

(155)

By the Lemma
B.2
this can be written as,

res^{n}[\omega]=\frac{dk_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dk_{n}}{\det(A)dk_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge dk_{n}}=\frac{1}{\det(A)}\,,

(156)

where,

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}2k_{1}&-2k_{2}&\ldots&-2k_{n}\\
2(k_{1}+p_{11})&-2(k_{2}+p_{12})&\ldots&-2(k_{n}+p_{1n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2(k_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{j1})&-2(k_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{j2})&\ldots&-2(k_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{jn})\end{array}\right)\,.

(157)

By the linearity of determinants,
\det(A)
can be written as,

\begin{split}&\det(A)=\\
&\begin{vmatrix}2k_{1}&\ldots&-2k_{n}\\
2k_{1}&\ldots&-2k_{n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2(k_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{j1})&\ldots&-2(k_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{jn})\end{vmatrix}+\\
&\begin{vmatrix}2k_{1}&\ldots&-2k_{n}\\
2p_{11}&\ldots&-2p_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2(k_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{j1})&\ldots&-2(k_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{jn})\end{vmatrix}\,,\end{split}

(158)

where the first term is equal to zero and by repeating this procedure we end up with,

\displaystyle\det(A)=\begin{vmatrix}2k_{1}&\ldots&-2k_{n}\\
2p_{11}&\ldots&-2p_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2p_{(n-1)1}&\ldots&-2p_{(n-1)n}\end{vmatrix}=

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1}2^{n}\begin{vmatrix}k_{1}&\ldots&k_{n}\\
p_{11}&\ldots&p_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{(n-1)1}&\ldots&p_{(n-1)n}\end{vmatrix}\,.

(159)

Let us denote by
B
the following matrix,

B=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}2k_{1}&\ldots&-2k_{n}\\
2p_{11}&\ldots&-2p_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2p_{(n-1)1}&\ldots&-2p_{(n-1)n},\end{array}\right)\,,

(160)

and we have
\det(B)=\det(A)
.
On the other hand, we have,

\begin{split}&\det(BgB^{T})=\det(B)\det(g)\det(B^{T})=\\
&=\det(B)^{2}\det(g)\Rightarrow\det(B)=\pm\sqrt{\det(BgB^{T})/\det(g)}\,,\end{split}

(161)

where
g=diag(1,-1,\ldots,-1)
is a metric tensor and
\det(g)=(-1)^{n-1}
.

However,
BgB^{T}
is equal to,

BgB^{T}=2^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}k\cdot k&k\cdot p_{1}&\ldots&k\cdot p_{n-1}\\
k\cdot p_{1}&p_{1}\cdot p_{1}&\ldots&p_{1}\cdot p_{n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k\cdot p_{n-1}&p_{1}\cdot p_{n-1}&\ldots&p_{n-1}\cdot p_{n-1}\end{array}\right).

(162)

Let us express this matrix in terms of
q_{i}=k+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}p_{j}
vectors,

\begin{split}k\cdot k&=q_{1}\cdot q_{1}\,,\\
k\cdot p_{i}&=q_{1}\cdot(q_{i+1}-q_{i})=q_{1}\cdot q_{i+1}-q_{1}\cdot q_{i}\,,\\
p_{i}\cdot p_{j}&=(q_{i+1}-q_{i})\cdot(q_{j+1}-q_{j})=\\
&=q_{i+1}\cdot q_{j+1}+q_{i}\cdot q_{j}-q_{i+1}\cdot q_{j}-q_{i}\cdot q_{j+1}\,.\end{split}

(163)

This gives us

\begin{split}&BgB^{T}=\\
&2^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}q_{1}\cdot q_{1}&q_{1}\cdot q_{2}-q_{1}\cdot q_{1}&\ldots&q_{1}\cdot q_{n}-q_{1}\cdot q_{n-1}\\
q_{1}\cdot q_{2}-q_{1}\cdot q_{1}&q_{2}\cdot q_{2}+q_{1}\cdot q_{1}-2q_{1}\cdot q_{2}&\ldots&q_{2}\cdot q_{n}+q_{1}\cdot q_{n-1}-q_{2}\cdot q_{n-1}-q_{1}\cdot q_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
q_{1}\cdot q_{n}-q_{1}\cdot q_{n-1}&q_{2}\cdot q_{n}+q_{1}\cdot q_{n-1}-q_{2}\cdot q_{n-1}-q_{1}\cdot q_{n}&\ldots&q_{n}\cdot q_{n}+q_{n-1}\cdot q_{n-1}-2q_{n-1}\cdot q_{n}\end{array}\right).\end{split}

(164)

Let us now transform
\det(BgB^{T})
, firstly by performing operations on rows. Let us add the first row to the second row and then a new second row from the third row and repeat this procedure until we reach the n-th row. After this, we will have,

\begin{split}&\det(BgB^{T})=\\
&=2^{2n}\begin{vmatrix}q_{1}\cdot q_{1}&q_{1}\cdot q_{2}-q_{1}\cdot q_{1}&\ldots&q_{1}\cdot q_{n}-q_{1}\cdot q_{n-1}\\
q_{1}\cdot q_{2}&q_{2}\cdot q_{2}-q_{1}\cdot q_{2}&\ldots&q_{2}\cdot q_{n}-q_{2}\cdot q_{n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
q_{1}\cdot q_{n}&q_{2}\cdot q_{n}-q_{1}\cdot q_{n}&\ldots&q_{n}\cdot q_{n}-q_{n-1}\cdot q_{n}\end{vmatrix}\,.\end{split}

(165)

Let us now do the same procedure but with respect to columns,

\begin{split}&\det(BgB^{T})=\\
&=2^{2n}\begin{vmatrix}q_{1}\cdot q_{1}&q_{1}\cdot q_{2}&\ldots&q_{1}\cdot q_{n}\\
q_{1}\cdot q_{2}&q_{2}\cdot q_{2}&\ldots&q_{2}\cdot q_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
q_{1}\cdot q_{n}&q_{2}\cdot q_{n}&\ldots&q_{n}\cdot q_{n}\end{vmatrix}=2^{2n}\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})\,.\end{split}

(166)

This is exactly the function which together with on-shell conditions provides the Landau variety, but on-shell conditions are satisfied as we calculated the residue around polar sets
D_{1}=\ldots=D_{n}=0
. Combining together all the results we have,

\begin{split}&\det{A}=\det(B)=\pm\sqrt{\det(BgB^{T})/\det(g)}=\\
&=\pm\sqrt{2^{2n}(-1)^{n-1}\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})}=\\
&=\pm 2^{n}\sqrt{(-1)^{n-1}\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})}\,,\end{split}

(167)

and finally

res^{n}[\omega]=\frac{1}{\det(A)}=\frac{\pm 1}{2^{n}\sqrt{(-1)^{n-1}\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})}}\,,

(168)

that ends our proof.
∎
