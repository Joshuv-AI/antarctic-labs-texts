Scalable Analysis of Bipartite Experiments
Liang Shi, Edvard Bakhitov, Kenneth Hung, Brian Karrer, Charlie Walker, Monica Bhole, Okke Schrijvers

Abstract
. Bipartite Experiments are randomized experiments where the treatment is applied to a set of units (randomization units) that is different from the units of analysis, and randomization units and analysis units are connected through a bipartite graph. The scale of experimentation at large online platforms necessitates both accurate inference in the presence of a large bipartite interference graph, as well as a highly scalable implementation. In this paper, we describe new methods for inference that enable practical, scalable analysis of bipartite experiments: (1) We propose CA-ERL, a covariate-adjusted variant of the exposure-reweighted-linear (ERL) estimator ( Harshaw et al. 2021 ) , which empirically yields 60-90% variance reduction. (2) We introduce a randomization-based method for inference and prove asymptotic validity of a Wald-type confidence interval under graph sparsity assumptions. (3) We present a linear-time algorithm for randomization inference of the CA-ERL estimator, which can be easily implemented in query engines like Presto or Spark. We evaluate our methods both on a real experiment at Meta that randomized treatment on Facebook Groups and analyzed user-level metrics, as well as simulations on synthetic data. The real-world data shows that our CA-ERL estimator reduces the confidence interval (CI) width by 60-90% (compared to ERL) in a practical setting. The simulations using synthetic data show that our randomization inference procedure achieves correct coverage across instances, while the ERL estimator has incorrectly small CI widths for instances with large true effect sizes and is overly conservative when the bipartite graph is dense.

1.
Introduction

Bipartite experiments
(
Zigler and Papadogeorgou 2018
)
are randomized experiments where interventions are applied to a set of units (randomization units) that are distinct from the set of units upon which the treatment effect is measured (analysis units). The randomization units and the analysis units are connected with each other through a bipartite graph. There are many examples of bipartite experiments in real world, e.g., tech companies striving to measure treatment effects of a group-level intervention on user-level engagement, online marketplace platforms being interested in measuring treatment effects on consumer behavior from seller-side interventions. However, potential interference due to the bipartite graph dependence structure causes big challenges in the treatment effect estimation.

Several estimators have been proposed in the literature to estimate treatment effects in different settings for bipartite experiments. Under the partial interference assumption
(
Sobel 2006
;
Hudgens and Halloran 2008
)
, where the bipartite graph can be partitioned into non-overlapping clusters, treatment effect estimators for graph-cluster randomization
(
Ugander et al. 2013
;
Karrer et al. 2021
)
are directly applicable. For general interference, inverse-propensity-weighted (IPW) estimators
(
Aronow and Samii 2017
;
Doudchenko et al. 2020
)
are shown to be unbiased and have good asymptotic properties under a typical set of assumptions. However, IPW-based estimators generally suffer from high variance in practical applications. More recently, Harshaw et al.
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
proposed an alternative estimator, the Exposure-Reweighted Linear (ERL) estimator, which is unbiased under a linear exposure-response model and has good asymptotic properties under mild assumptions on the sparsity of the bipartite graph.

Given an estimator, the variance estimation under interference is also difficult due to the cross-units dependence and the fact that we only observe one potential outcome for each unit in an experiment. For bipartite experiments,
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
proposed an analytical variance estimator which is asymptotically consistent under the linear exposure-response model and a relatively sparse graph. However, this estimator is computationally costly and not easy to implement for large-scale real world applications. Alternatively, randomization-based inference has a long tradition in causal inference since Fisher’s
(
Fisher 1925
)
seminal work. It has also been recently applied to test for network interference
(
Athey et al. 2018
)
. In this paper, we explore the randomization-based approach to construct confidence intervals for the treatment effect estimator in bipartite experiments and provide conditions for its validity.

Furthermore, this paper introduces a covariate-adjusted variant of the ERL estimator for bipartite experiments. Covariate adjustment has been shown to achieve significant variance reduction for unit-
(
Lin 2013
;
Deng et al. 2013
)
and cluster-randomized experiments
(
Karrer et al. 2021
)
. We demonstrate that it can also lead to substantial variance reduction (up to 90%) in bipartite experiments. Then, we apply a randomization-based inference approach and show that the proposed method is more robust and scalable than the asymptotic inference approach introduced in
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
. Lastly, we evaluate the performance of our proposed estimation procedure with simulations and real bipartite use cases at Meta. In summary, the contributions of this paper include:

1.

Covariate-adjusted variant of the ERL estimator which leads to substantial variance reduction;

2.

Robust randomization-based inferential method for general bipartite graphs;

3.

Scalable implementation of the estimation procedure for applications at Meta’s scale.

The treatment assignment influences the potential outcomes only through a linear combination, i.e., the exposure of outcome unit

A covariate-adjusted Exposure-Reweighted Linear estimator

In this section, we introduce a covariate-adjusted variant of the original ERL estimator for the variance reduction purpose. Let
X_{i}
be the vector of observed covariates of the analysis unit
i
that are independent of the treatment assignment vector (e.g., pre-experiment outcome variable). We define the covariate-adjusted ERL estimator (CA-ERL) for GATE as follows:

Definition 0 (Covariate-Adjusted Exposure-Reweighted Linear Estimator).

(5)

\widehat{\tau}({\lambda})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}(Z)-\lambda f_{i}(X_{i}))\psi_{i}(Z),

where
f_{i}(X_{i})
is a function of the covariates, and
\lambda
is a parameter that controls for the estimator’s variance.

With
\lambda=0
, we obtain the original ERL estimator in (
2
). For exposition convenience, let
\widehat{\tau}_{f}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(X_{i})\psi_{i}(Z)
. Hence,
\widehat{\tau}(\lambda)=\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}-\lambda\widehat{\tau}_{f}
.

To understand the bias and variance of the adjusted estimator, let us consider a simple scenario:
\lambda\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Z
and
f_{i}(X_{i})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Z
, i.e., both
\lambda
and
f_{i}(X_{i})
are independent of the treatment assignment. Common choices for
f_{i}(X_{i})
in practical applications include

1

the pre-experiment outcome variable,
Y_{i}^{\text{pre}}
, or

2

machine learning models that predict the outcome without using the experiment data in the training process (e.g., using per-experiment data).

In this case, we can show that
\E[\lambda\widehat{\tau}_{f}]=\lambda\E[\widehat{\tau}_{f}]=0
, i.e., the adjusted term does not add additional bias to the ERL estimator. Hence, under the linear exposure-response model, the above adjusted estimator is also unbiased for GATE.

Lemma 2 (Unbiasedness of CA-ERL).

Suppose
\lambda\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Z
and
f_{i}(X_{i})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Z
, the CA-ERL estimator is unbiased for GATE under the linear exposure-response model.

Proof.

The result follows directly from the unbiasedness of the unadjusted ERL estimator,

\E[\widehat{\tau}(\lambda)]=\E[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}-\lambda\widehat{\tau}_{f}]=\E[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}]-\lambda\E[\widehat{\tau}_{f}]=\E[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}]=\tau.\qed

The variance of the adjusted estimator can be written as,

(6)

\V[\widehat{\tau}(\lambda)]=\V[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}]-2\lambda\cov[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}},\widehat{\tau}_{f}]+\lambda^{2}\V[\widehat{\tau}_{f}],

which is always smaller than the variance of the original ERL estimator if
\lambda<2\cov[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}},\widehat{\tau}_{f}]/\V[\widehat{\tau}_{f}]
. It can be shown that the variance-minimizing
\lambda
is given by

(7)

\lambda^{*}=\frac{\cov[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}},\widehat{\tau}_{f}]}{\V[\widehat{\tau}_{f}]},

with the minimal variance

(8)

\V[\widehat{\tau}(\lambda^{*})]=\V[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}](1-\rho^{2}),

where
\rho
is the correlation between
\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{f}
. We can see that the more correlated
\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{f}
are, the more variance reduction we can achieve.

However, we have to estimate
\lambda^{*}
since both the variance and covariance terms in (
7
) are unknown. If we use the plug-in estimators for covariance and variance to estimate
\lambda^{*}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\lambda}^{*}=\widehat{\cov}[\widehat{\tau}^{\text{ERL}},\widehat{\tau}_{f}]/\widehat{\V}[\widehat{\tau}_{f}]
,
\widehat{\lambda}^{*}
will also depend on the treatment assignment and hence can potentially introduce bias to the adjusted estimator. The bias of
\widehat{\tau}(\widehat{\lambda}^{*})
is given by

\mathrm{Bias}[\widehat{\tau}(\widehat{\lambda}^{*})]=-\E[(\widehat{\lambda}^{*}-\lambda^{*})\widehat{\tau}_{f}]=-\E[\widehat{\lambda}^{*}\widehat{\tau}_{f}].

Assuming both
\widehat{\lambda}^{*}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{f}
converge at the rate of
1/\sqrt{n^{c}}
with
c>0
, one can see that the bias term is
\mathcal{O}(1/n^{c})
, which is negligible compared to the convergence rate of the estimator
\widehat{\tau}(\lambda^{*})
.

4.1.
Sharp vs Weak Null

As we pointed out earlier, our method is designed to test the sharp or Fisher’s null hypothesis
(
Fisher 1925
)
, assuming that all units are completely unaffected by the treatment. Although potentially restrictive in some applications, the sharp null is a reasonable hypothesis in online experimentation where the effect size is small.

An alternative, more common hypothesis, is the weak or Neyman’s null hypothesis
(
Neyman 1990
)
of zero average treatment effect, in our case GATE. However, as pointed out in
(
Wu and Ding 2021
)
, using Fisher randomization test (FRT) to test the weak null is a non-trivial task as it is hard to ensure that an FRT procedure correctly controls for Type-I error under the weak null. In the subsequent sections, we show that our inference procedure produces valid confidence intervals under both the sharp and weak null hypotheses.

Intuitively, the sharp null implies the weak null. However,
Ding 2017
showed that in case of imbalanced experiments, we can end up rejecting the weak null, but not rejecting the sharp null. This paradoxical result is driven by the fact that FRT is asymptotically less powerful than Neyman’s test in many cases.

Subsequent work demonstrates that this result crucially depends on the FRT procedure
(
Wu and Ding 2021
)
. The classic FRT procedure is based on calculation of
p
-values for a given test statistic. While the choice of the test statistic does not matter for binary outcomes
(
Ding and Dasgupta 2016
)
, it is crucial for non-binary outcomes. Hence, if we use a proper test-statistic under the sharp null, we can actually correctly control for the Type-I error under the weak null, albeit conservatively
(
Wu and Ding 2021
)
.

4.2.
Validity of randomization inference

6.
Discussion and Conclusion

Building upon the original work of
Harshaw et al. 2021
, this paper presents a covariate-adjusted variant of the ERL estimator for bipartite experiments. Covariate adjustment is a common technique for analyzing regular A/B tests in order to reduce variance. Here, we show that it can be directly applied to bipartite experiments for the same purpose. Under the same linear exposure and response assumptions as in
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
, the covariate-adjusted ERL estimator is also unbiased for the global average treatment effect. We also propose a randomization-based inference method for variance estimation and study its theoretical properties. We prove that the Wald-type confidence interval based on the randomization inference is asymptotically valid under graph sparsity assumptions. In simulations, we demonstrate that the randomization-based inference method is more robust compared to alternative approaches previously proposed in literature, especially when the bipartite graph is dense. Our empirical evaluation of the covariate-adjusted estimator shows substantial precision gains against the unadjusted estimator and should always be applied for practical applications when data on covariates is available.

In addition, we present a practical and scalable algorithm for practitioners for the randomization inference procedure of the covariate-adjusted estimator. The algorithm is based on the proposed randomization inference, which allows easy Monte-Carlo estimation of the additional variance and covariance terms needed for the covariate adjustment. The algorithm has linear time complexity and is easily parallelizable allowing it to take advantage of modern computing infrastructure. The algorithm can be implemented using common query engines like Presto
(
Sun et al. 2023
)
or Spark
(
Zaharia et al. 2016
)
for large-scale real world applications.

Our randomization inference approach is generally applicable to any experiment under interference. Variance estimation for experiments in presence of interference has been challenging due to the between-units dependence structure. The randomization inference approach provides a simple and robust solution as long as the experiment design is known, i.e., we can draw samples of the treatment assignment vector from the randomization mechanism. The confidence intervals should also be asymptotically valid with relatively sparse interference structure.

One assumption throughout the paper is that the bipartite graph is independent of the treatment and is known to the researcher or analyst. In reality, the true bipartite graph is rarely or only partially known. Under these circumstances, how a misspecified or a treatment-dependent bipartite graph affects the treatment effect estimation becomes crucial for practical decision making, which is also relevant for general experimentation under interference and deserves more future research.

\displaystyle~\left|\frac{\cov[H_{i}(Z),H_{j}(Z)]}{\V[H_{i}(Z)]\V[H_{j}(Z)]}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{\left|\corr[H_{i}(Z),H_{j}(Z)]\right|}{\sqrt{\V[H_{i}(Z)]\V[H_{j}(Z)]}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\frac{\bar{d}}{p(1-p)},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma A.2 in
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
and the upper bound on the correlation coefficient.

Collecting terms gives us the following bound on the approximation error

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2k\bar{d}^{2}}{n}M^{2}\cdot\frac{\bar{d}}{p(1-p)}

(13)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\bar{d}^{3}}{n}\right).

A.2.
Validity of the confidence interval

This part of the proof closely follows
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
. Define a random variable
Z=\frac{\tau-\hat{\tau}}{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\tau}]}
. By Theorem 4.3 in
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
,
Z
converges in distribution to a standard normal,
Z\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Define
Z^{\prime}=\frac{\tau-\hat{\tau}}{\mathbb{V}_{\text{RV}}[\hat{\tau}]}
and observe that

Z^{\prime}=Z\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\tau}]}{\mathbb{V}^{RV}[\hat{\tau}]}}.

Combining the bound in (
14
) with the graph growth condition
\bar{d}^{14}=o(n)
gives us

\left|\widehat{\V}_{\text{RV}}[\hat{\tau}]-\mathbb{V}[\hat{\tau}]\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\bar{d}^{4}}{n}\right)=o(n^{-2/7})\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}).

Under
3
, we have
\mathbb{V}[\hat{\tau}]=\Omega(n^{-1})
, and hence,

\frac{\left|\widehat{\V}_{\text{RV}}[\hat{\tau}]-\mathbb{V}[\hat{\tau}]\right|}{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\tau}]}=o(n^{-2/7}),

which ensures the ratio of the randomization variance estimator and the true variance converges to
1
in probability, i.e.,
\frac{\widehat{\V}_{\text{RV}}[\hat{\tau}]}{\V[\hat{\tau}]}\overset{p}{\to}1
. Hence, by Slutsky’s theorem,
Z^{\prime}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Now, we evaluate the probability of coverage in the limit. Let
\text{CI}(\hat{\tau})=\hat{\tau}\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\sqrt{\widehat{\V}_{\text{RV}}[\hat{\tau}]}
, and
F_{n}
be the cumulative distribution function of
Z^{\prime}
. By rearranging terms, we can rewrite the coverage probability in terms of the tails of
Z^{\prime}
as follows:

\displaystyle~\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau\in\text{CI}(\hat{\tau})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\tau-\hat{\tau}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\V}_{\text{RV}}[\hat{\tau}]}}\leq\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\leq Z^{\prime}\leq\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha/2)\leq Z^{\prime}\leq\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\{F_{n}\left(\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\right)-F_{n}\left(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha/2)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\Phi\left(\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\right)-\Phi\left(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha/2)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(1-\alpha/2)-\alpha/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~1-\alpha.
