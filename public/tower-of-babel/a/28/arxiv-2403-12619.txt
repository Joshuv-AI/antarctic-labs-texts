Abstract
Non-Bayesian social learning is a framework for distributed hypothesis testing aimed at learning the true state of the environment. Traditionally, the agents are assumed to receive observations conditioned on the same true state, although it is also possible to examine the case of heterogeneous models across the graph. One important special case is when heterogeneity is caused by the presence of malicious agents whose goal is to move the agents toward a wrong hypothesis. In this work, we propose an algorithm that allows discovering the true state of every individual agent based on the sequence of their beliefs. In so doing, the methodology is also able to locate malicious behavior.

II
Social Learning Model

A set of agents
\mathcal{N}
builds confidences on each hypothesis
\theta
from a finite set
\Theta
through interactions with the environment and among the agents.
The agents communicate according to a fixed combination matrix
A\in\mathcal{[}0,1]^{\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}}
, where each nonzero element
a_{\ell,k}>0
indicates a directed edge from agent
\ell
to agent
k
and defines the level of trust that agent
k
gives to information arriving from agent
\ell
.
Each agent
k
assigns a total confidence level of
1
to its neighbors. This assumption makes the combination matrix
A
left stochastic, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}a_{\ell k}=1,\;\forall k\in\mathcal{N}

(1)

Another common assumption, ensuring global truth learning for homogeneous environments, is that
A
is strongly connected. This implies the existence of at least one self-loop with a positive weight and a path with positive weights between any two nodes
[
44
]
.
This condition allows us to apply the Perron-Frobenius theorem
[
45
, Chapter 8]
,
[
46
]
, which ensures that the power matrix
A^{s}
converges exponentially to
u\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
as
s\rightarrow\infty
. Here,
\mathds{1}
is the vector of all 1s and
u
is the Perron eigenvector of
A
associated with the eigenvalue at
1
and is normalized as follows:

\displaystyle Au=u,\qquad u_{\ell}>0,\qquad\textstyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}u_{\ell}=1.

(2)

Each agent assigns an initial
private
belief
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,0}(\theta)\in[0,1]
to each hypothesis
\theta\in\Theta
, forming a probability mass function with the total confidence summing up to
1
, i.e.,
\sum_{\theta}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,0}(\theta)=1
.

To avoid excluding any hypothesis initially, we assume
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,0}(\theta)>0
for all
\theta
.

Subsequently, agents iteratively update their belief vectors by interacting both with the environment and with their neighbors.
At each time instance
i
, agent
k
receives an observation from the environment conditioned on its true state, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\sim L_{k}(\zeta|\theta_{k}^{\star})
or
L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})
for brevity.
In this notation, the observation
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}
arises from the likelihood model
L_{k}(\zeta|\theta_{k}^{\star})
, which is parameterized by the unknown model
\theta_{k}^{\star}
.
For example, the entire network may be following the same and unique model
\theta^{\star}
, while a few malicious agents may be following some other model
\theta\neq\theta^{\star}
.
The observations
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\}
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) over time.
The local Bayesian update performed by agent
k
at time
i
takes the following form
[
7
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta)=\frac{L_{k}^{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\mid\theta)\boldsymbol{\mu}^{1-\delta}_{k,i-1}(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}L^{\delta}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\mid\theta^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mu}^{1-\delta}_{k,i-1}(\theta^{\prime})},\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{N},

(3)

where
\delta\in(0,1)
plays the role of an adaptation parameter and it controls the importance of the newly received observation relative to the information learned from past interactions.
The denominator in (
3
) serves as a normalization factor, ensuring that the resulting
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}
is a probability mass function.
We refer to
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}
as the
public
(or intermediate) belief due to the next communication step, which involves a geometric averaging computation
[
4
,
9
,
2
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta)=\frac{\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{a_{\ell k}}_{\ell,i}(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{a_{\ell k}}_{\ell,i}(\theta^{\prime})},\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{N}.

(4)

At each iteration
i
, each agent
k
estimates its true state
\theta_{k}^{\star}
based on the belief vector (either private or public) by selecting the hypothesis with the highest confidence:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k,i}\triangleq\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k,i}(\theta).

(5)

In the homogeneous environment case
[
4
,
9
,
2
,
7
]
, i.e., when
\theta_{k}^{\star}=\theta^{\star}
for each
k
, it can be proved that every agent finds the truth asymptotically with probability
1
.

The work
[
20
]
considers nonhomogeneous environments with community-structured graphs; it establishes that, as
\delta\to 0
, the entire network converges to
one
solution, while in contrast, a larger
\delta
activates the mechanism of
local
adaptivity.
While this property works well with community-structured graphs, some
sparsely
located malicious agents might be heavily influenced by their neighbors or require too large
\delta
. The method we derive estimates the true state of each agent in an inverse manner, allowing it to operate effectively with graphs of general structure and with any
\delta
.

III
Inverse Modeling

In this section, we explain how we can identify malicious agents (or the true state
\theta_{k}^{\star}
for each agent) by observing sequences of public beliefs.
Importantly, we will not assume knowledge of the combination matrix
A
.

To begin with, we introduce the following common assumption, essentially requiring the observations to share the same support region
[
8
,
47
,
19
]
.

Assumption 1

(
Bounded likelihoods).

There exists a finite constant
b>0
such that for all
k\in\mathcal{N}
:

\displaystyle\Bigg|\log\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\theta)}{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\theta^{\prime})}\Bigg|\leq b

(6)

for all
\theta,\;\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

\blacksquare

Now, consider a
sequence
of public beliefs measured closer to the steady state:

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}\}_{i\gg 1},\;k\in\mathcal{N}

(7)

When an agent cannot distinguish between
\theta^{\star}_{k}
and another
\theta
due to
L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})=L_{k}(\theta)
, we will treat this
\theta
as a valid model for the agent as well.
To accommodate this possibility, we define
\Theta_{k}^{\star}
as the optimal hypotheses subset for each individual agent, denoted by
\Theta_{k}^{\star}=\{\theta_{k}^{\star}\}\cup\{\theta\neq\theta_{k}^{\star}\mid L_{k}(\theta)=L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})\}
.
Then, we reformulate the problem by stating that our aim is to recover the optimal hypotheses subset for each agent:

\displaystyle\{\Theta_{k}^{\star}\},\;k\in\mathcal{N}.

(8)

We denote the level of informativeness of any pair of hypotheses
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
at each agent
k
by:

\displaystyle d_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}\sim L_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star})}\log\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}|\theta)}{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}|\theta^{\prime})}

(9)

It is clear that this value is equal to zero if both
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
belong to the optimal subset
\Theta_{k}^{\star}
. Additionally,
d_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta)
will be positive for any
\theta\notin\Theta^{\star}_{k}
since

\displaystyle d_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta)=D_{\textup{KL}}\left(L_{k}\left(\theta_{k}^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta\right)\right)>0

(10)

and, in turn,
d_{k}(\theta,\theta_{k}^{\star})
is always negative:

\displaystyle d_{k}(\theta,\theta_{k}^{\star})=-D_{\textup{KL}}\left(L_{k}\left(\theta_{k}^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta\right)\right)<0

(11)

Here,
D_{\textup{KL}}
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two distributions:

\displaystyle D_{\textrm{KL}}\big(L_{k}(\theta^{\star})\mid\mid L_{k}(\theta)\big)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\theta^{\star})}\log\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\theta^{\star})}{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\theta)}

(12)

Properties (
10
)–(
11
) allow us to conclude that the optimal hypotheses subset
\Theta_{k}^{\star}
consists of all
\theta
for which:

\displaystyle\Theta_{k}^{\star}=\{\theta\colon d_{k}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\geq 0,\;\ \forall\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta\}

(13)

Our aim is to develop an algorithm that learns
\Theta_{k}^{\star}
based on the available information (
7
).

In
[
47
, Appendix A]
, it is shown that the adaptive social learning iterations (
3
)–(
4
) can be expressed in the following compact linear form:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}=(1-\delta)A^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i-1}+\delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
are matrices of size
|\mathcal{N}|\times(|\Theta|-1)
, and for each
k
and
j
, their entries take the log-ratio form:

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}]_{k,j}\triangleq\log\frac{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta_{0})}{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k,i}(\theta_{j})},\;\;[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}]_{k,j}\triangleq\log\frac{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\mid\theta_{0})}{L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\mid\theta_{j})}.

(15)

for any ordering
\Theta=\{\theta_{0},\dots,\theta_{|\Theta|-1}\}
. The expectation of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
, relative to the observations
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k,i}\}_{k}
, is given by:

\displaystyle[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}\triangleq[\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}]_{k,j}=\textrm{ }

\displaystyle D_{\textup{KL}}\left(L_{k}\left(\theta_{k}^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-D_{\textup{KL}}\left(L_{k}\left(\theta_{k}^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta_{0}\right)\right),

(16)

and it allows us to rewrite (
9
) in a slightly different manner:

\displaystyle d_{k}(\theta_{j_{1}},\theta_{j_{2}})=[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j_{2}}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j_{1}}

(17)

Furthermore, it is shown in
[
19
]
that we can estimate
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
by utilizing the publicly exchanged beliefs with the following accuracy
[
19
, Theorem 2]
:

\displaystyle\;\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}_{i}-\overline{\mathcal{L}}\|_{\textrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leq\frac{1}{M}\textup{Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)+O(\mu/\delta^{2})+O\left(1/\delta^{5}M^{2}\right)

(18)

where
\mu
is a small positive learning rate for a stochastic gradient implementation,
M
is a batch size of data used to compute the estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}_{i}
, and
R_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}-\overline{\mathcal{L}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{i}-\overline{\mathcal{L}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Thus, the informativeness (
17
) can be estimated by using

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{j_{1}},\theta_{j_{2}})=[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j_{2}}-[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j_{1}}

(19)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}
is the estimate of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
from the last available iteration.
Based on (
13
), we can now identify the optimal hypotheses subset
\Theta_{k}^{\star}
defined in (
13
) as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}\triangleq\arg\max_{\theta_{j_{1}}}\sum_{\theta_{j_{2}}}\mathbb{I}\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{j_{1}},\theta_{j_{2}})>0\right\}

(20)

where
\mathbb{I}\left\{x\right\}
is an indicator function that assumes the value
1
when its argument is true and is
0
otherwise.

Algorithm 1

Inverse learning of heterogeneous states

The following result establishes the probability of error.

Theorem 1

(
Probability of error).

The probability of choosing a wrong hypothesis
\theta\notin\Theta_{k}^{\star}
for agent
k\in\mathcal{N}
is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\theta\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}\right\}\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{4}{M}\textup{Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)\sum_{\theta^{\star}\in\Theta_{k}^{\star}}D^{-1}_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{k}\left(\theta^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta\right)\big)

\displaystyle+O(\mu/\delta^{2})+O\left(1/\delta^{5}M^{2}\right)

(21)

Proof.

First, we upper bound the probability using the definition of
d(\cdot,\cdot)
and its estimate from (
9
) and (
19
), along with the properties of probability.
For any
\theta_{j}\notin\Theta_{k}^{\star}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\theta_{j}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\;\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\exists\theta_{k}^{\star}\in\Theta_{k}^{\star}\colon\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta_{j})<0\right\}

\displaystyle\;\leq\sum_{\theta_{k}^{\star}\in\Theta_{k}^{\star}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta_{j})<0\right\}

(22)

Next, we estimate the probability of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta_{j})
being negative for some fixed
\theta_{j}
and
\theta_{k}^{\star}
using (
19
), while denoting
j^{\star}_{k}
as the index of
\theta_{k}^{\star}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta_{j})<0\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j}-[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}<0\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\;\mathbb{P}\Big\{[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}-\left([\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\leq[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathbb{P}\Big\{\left|[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}\right|+\left|[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}\right|

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\leq[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}\right|\leq\left([\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}\right)/2\right\}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\times\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}\right|\leq\left([\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}\right)/2\right\}

(23)

We can transform the result (
18
) from
[
19
, Theorem 2]
into:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}\right|\leq\frac{1}{M}\textup{Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)+O(\mu/\delta^{2})+O\left(1/\delta^{5}M^{2}\right)

(24)

By Markov’s inequality
[
46
]
, for any
a>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}\right|\leq a\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{aM}\textup{Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)+O(\mu/\delta^{2})+O\left(1/\delta^{5}M^{2}\right)

(25)

Also, by the definition of KL divergence we have that:

\displaystyle[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}=

\displaystyle\;D_{\textup{KL}}\left(L_{k}\left(\theta_{k}^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta_{j}\right)\right)>0.

(26)

Thus, (
23
) can be upper bounded by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\star},\theta_{j})<0\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\bigg(1-\frac{\frac{2}{M}\textup{Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)+O(\mu/\delta^{2})+O\left(1/\delta^{5}M^{2}\right)}{[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j}-[\overline{\mathcal{L}}]_{k,j^{\star}_{k}}}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx 4M^{-1}\textup{Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}\right)D^{-1}_{\textup{KL}}\big(L_{k}\left(\theta_{k}^{\star}\right)\mid\mid L_{k}\left(\theta\right)\big)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+O(\mu/\delta^{2})+O\left(1/\delta^{5}M^{2}\right)

(27)

using the Taylor’s expansion for any small
x
, namely,
(1+x)^{2}=1+2x+O(x^{2})
.

Combining (
22
) with (
27
) we get the desired statement.
∎

The model’s performance is influenced by parameters
\delta
and
\mu
, with
\mu
being arbitrarily small. As shown in
[
20
]
, when
\delta
is close to 1, agents rely more on their own observations, making it easier to reveal their true state in social learning. This aligns with the derived result.

In this section, we consider the image dataset MIRO (Multi-view Images of Rotated Objects)
[

For each class, there are 10 objects, and each of the objects has 160 different perspectives.

A network of agents wishes to solve a binary hypotheses problem to distinguish between states

Each agent has its own convolutional neural network (CNN) classifier.
These CNNs are trained to distinguish classes

The CNN architecture consists of three convolutional layers: 6 output channels,

max pooling; 32 channels,

,

, and

For generating a combination matrix (see Fig.

Since we only have 10 objects of each class, having only a handful of objects as a test subset is not enough to provide a reliable accuracy metric.
Thus, we perform a cross-validation procedure where at first, we train the CNNs on 9 objects from each class, leaving 1 object from each class for testing purposes.
On average, the cross-validation accuracy of standalone classifiers is
0.68
.
The value is relatively low due to a small training set and limited observation available at each agent.
Given that many folds had some classifiers with an accuracy below 0.5, we decided to retain only those folds where each agent achieved at least 0.5 accuracy.
As a result, we are left with 72 folds instead of 100 with the mean accuracy of standalone classifiers equal to
0.81
.
