Abstract
. The Chow ring of ℳ g \mathcal{M}_{g} is known to be generated by tautological classes for g ≤ 9 g\leq 9 . Meanwhile, the first example of a non-tautological class on ℳ g \mathcal{M}_{g} is the fundamental class of the bielliptic locus in ℳ 12 \mathcal{M}_{12} , due to van Zelm. It remains open if the Chow rings of ℳ 10 \mathcal{M}_{10} and ℳ 11 \mathcal{M}_{11} are generated by tautological classes. In these cases, a natural first place to look is at the bielliptic locus. In genus 10 10 , it is already known that classes supported on the bielliptic locus are tautological. Here, we prove that all classes supported on the bielliptic locus are tautological in genus 11 11 . By Looijenga’s vanishing theorem, this implies that they all vanish.

Introduction

The Chow rings of the moduli spaces of smooth and stable curves, possibly with marked points, have been central objects of study in algebraic geometry since Mumford’s seminal paper
[
16
]
. A complete description of the Chow ring of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g}
of smooth curves of genus
g
has been obtained for
g\leq 9
in a series of papers over the past 40 years:

•

Genus
2
by Mumford in 1983
[
16
]
,

•

Genus
3
and
4
by Faber in 1990
[
9
,
10
]
,

•

Genus
5
by Izadi in 1995
[
13
]
,

•

Genus
6
by Penev and Vakil in 2015
[
17
]
,

•

Genus
7,8,9
by the authors in 2021
[
2
]
.

In each of these cases, the Chow ring is shown to be equal to the
tautological ring
.
If
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
denotes the universal curve, then the tautological subring
R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})\subseteq A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
is defined to be the subring generated by the classes
\kappa_{i}:=f_{*}(c_{1}(\omega_{f})^{i+1})
.

Even more recently, there has been significant progress in the study of the Chow rings of the moduli spaces
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
of stable curves of genus
g
with
n
marked points
[
5
,
6
]
. The Chow ring
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
and its tautological subring
R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
are of fundamental importance in a wide variety of fields, including enumerative geometry and physics
[
14
,
19
]
. The first step in the study of
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
is to compute
A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n})
. Therefore,
A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n})
is an important object of study both in its own right and for its applications to computing
A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})
.

The tautological ring
R^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
is highly structured. By a theorem of Looijenga,
R^{i}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
vanishes when
i>g-2
and by combining results of Faber and Looijenga,
R^{g-2}(\mathcal{M}_{g})=\mathbb{Q}
, generated by the class of the hyperelliptic locus
[
15
,
11
]
.
Looijenga’s vanishing theorem can be used to obtain interesting vanishing results for classes of codimension at least
g-1
by demonstrating that such a class is tautological.

In general, however, tautological classes do not generate the entire Chow ring. In fact, van Zelm
[
18
]
has produced an explicit algebraic cycle on
\mathcal{M}_{12}
which is
not
tautological.
A curve
C
is called
bielliptic
if it admits a degree
2
map
C\to E
for
E
an elliptic curve.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{g}\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}
denote the locus of bielliptic curves. A dimension count using the Riemann–Hurwitz formula shows that
\mathcal{B}_{g}\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}
has codimension
g-1
.
In
[
18
]
,
van Zelm proves that
[\mathcal{B}_{12}]
is
not
tautological. Furthermore, for all
g\geq 12
, van Zelm shows the closure of the bielliptic locus
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{g}\subset\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
has non-tautological fundamental class.

In light of van Zelm’s results, it remains an open problem of particular interest whether the Chow rings of
\mathcal{M}_{10}
and
\mathcal{M}_{11}
are generated by tautological classes. To approach this problem, one might try stratifying the moduli space by gonality, as we did in
[
2
]
. However, difficulties arise both with large and small gonality. In large gonality, we do not have good control over the Hurwitz space parametrizing covers of degree at least
6
, which are general in
\mathcal{M}_{g}
for
g\geq 9
. In small gonality, we do not know if certain fundamental classes in a further stratification are tautological when
g\geq 10
. See Section
2.1
for a discussion of this issue.

A natural first place to check for non-tautological classes is the bielliptic locus, following van Zelm. In
[
2
]
, we proved that
[\mathcal{B}_{10}]
is tautological. The argument there relies on a special coincidence of small numbers that holds only in genus
10
, allowing us to bypass the aforementioned issues with fundamental classes in small gonality. Here, we settle the remaining case of genus
11
, using a new technique.

Theorem 1.1
.

The fundamental class of the bielliptic locus
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]
is tautological. As a consequence, all classes supported on
\mathcal{B}_{11}\subset\mathcal{M}_{11}
are tautological.

Combining this result with Looijenga’s vanishing theorem
[
15
]
, we see that all classes supported on
\mathcal{B}_{11}\subset\mathcal{M}_{11}
are in fact zero for codimension reasons.

Theorem
1.1
provides the first positive evidence that Chow ring of
\mathcal{M}_{11}
may be generated by tautological classes.
The proof demonstrates an important concept that we hope may find other future applications: even when descriptions of a space as a degeneracy locus fail to occur in the correct codimension, excess intersection formulas may still provide enough information to deduce a desired class is tautological.

1.1.
Overview of the proof

Our proof will utilize the Hurwitz space
\mathcal{H}_{4,g}
, parametrizing degree
4
, genus
g
covers of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
up to automorphisms of the target. We write
\beta:\mathcal{H}_{4,g}\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
for the forgetful map. The basic idea is to find a tautological class on
\mathcal{H}_{4,11}
that pushes forward along
\beta
to a
non-zero
multiple of
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]
. A little care is needed to make this precise because the map
\beta
is not proper, since our degree
4
map could acquire a base point. However,
\beta
becomes proper after throwing out curves of lower gonality from the source and target.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{3}\subset\mathcal{M}_{g}
denote the locus of curves of gonality at most
3
. Then

\beta^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}_{4,g}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{g}^{3})\to\mathcal{M}_{g}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g}^{3}

is proper, and
\beta^{\prime}_{*}
sends tautological classes on
\mathcal{H}_{4,g}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{g}^{3})
to tautological classes on
\mathcal{M}_{g}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{g}^{3}
by
[
4
, Theorem 1.7]
. Here, the tautological subring of an open subset of
\mathcal{H}_{4,g}
or
\mathcal{M}_{g}
is defined to be the image of the tautological ring under restriction. Fortunately, it is already known that all classes supported on
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{3}
are tautological
[
2
, Section 1.1(1)]
. Thus, by excision, it will suffice to show that the image of
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]
in
\mathcal{M}_{11}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{11}^{3}
is tautological.

Figure 1.
Modulo classes supported on
\mathcal{M}_{11}^{3}
, we realize
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]
as a non-zero multiple of
\beta^{\prime}_{*}[T]
, which is tautological.

We shall do this by finding a tautological class
T\in A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,11}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{11}^{3}))
whose push forward along
\beta^{\prime}
is a non-zero multiple of
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]\in A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{11}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}_{11}^{3})
(see Figure
1
).
Our class
T
will be supported on
the preimage
\iota:\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11})\subset\mathcal{H}_{4,11}
, that is
T=\iota_{*}t
for some
t\in A^{*}(\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11}))
.
For
g\geq 6
, any degree
2
map from a genus
g
curve to an elliptic curve
C\to E
is unique by the Castelnuovo–Severi inequality.
Also by the Castelnuovo–Severi inequality, the preimage
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{g})
consists of degree
4
covers of the form
C\to E\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
(the unique degree
2
cover
C\to E
, followed by any degree
2
cover
E\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
). The fibers
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{g})\to\mathcal{B}_{g}
are therefore
1
-dimensional (identified with
\Pic^{2}E
). Hence, our
t
should have codimension
1
in
A^{*}(\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11}))
, and consequently
T=\iota_{*}t
has codimension
5
inside
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,11}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{11}^{3}))
.

In genus
10
, we found that
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{10})

may be interpreted as a degeneracy locus occurring in the “expected codimension.” We used this to show that the fundamental class of
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{10})
is tautological in
A^{*}(\mathcal{H}_{4,10}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{10}^{3}))
. We also proved that
A^{*}(\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{10}))
is generated by restrictions of tautological classes on
\mathcal{H}_{4,10}
. Pushing forward, it follows that all classes supported on
\mathcal{B}_{10}
are tautological.

In genus
11
, however, the analogous construction fails:
descriptions of
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11})\subset\mathcal{H}_{4,11}
as a degeneracy locus occur in the wrong codimension. However, the
expected class
of an appropriate degeneracy locus on
\mathcal{H}_{4,11}
yields a tautological
class in
A^{5}(\mathcal{H}_{4,11}\smallsetminus\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{M}_{11}^{3}))
, which — using excess intersection formulas — is the push forward of
a divisor
t
supported on
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11})
.
The key step is then to
show that
t

meets each fiber of
\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11})\to\mathcal{B}_{11}
with non-zero degree
.
Then, even though it remains unknown if
[\beta^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{11})]
is tautological on
\mathcal{H}_{4,11}
, we manage to show that a non-zero multiple of
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]
, and hence
[\mathcal{B}_{11}]
itself, is tautological in
A^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{11})
.

Outline of the paper

In Section
2
, we explain how to realize

2.3.
The excess Porteous formula

The usual Porteous formula tells us the fundamental class of the locus where a map of vector bundles drops rank when it occurs in the expected codimension. If
\sigma:A\to B
is a map of vector bundles of ranks
a
and
b
on
X
, then the locus
D_{k}(\sigma)
where
\sigma
has rank
k
or less has expected codimension
(a-k)(b-k)
. As a special case, the locus
D_{b-1}(\sigma)
, where
\sigma
fails to be surjective has expected codimension

(2.3)

a-b+1.

When
D_{k}(\sigma)
occurs in the expected codimension, its fundamental class is given by a universal formula in terms of the Chern classes of
A
and
B
(see
[
12
, Section 14.4]
):

(2.4)

[D_{k}(\sigma)]=\Delta^{a-k}_{b-k}(c(B)/c(A))\in A^{(a-k)(b-k)}(X).

Above,
c(B)/c(A)
means we formally expand the ratio of total Chern classes; then given a class
c=1+c_{1}+c_{2}+\ldots
where
c_{i}
is the codimension
i
component,

\Delta^{p}_{q}(c)
denotes the determinant of the
p\times p
matrix
(c_{q+j-i})_{1\leq i,j\leq p}
. For us, the precise formula is not so important. It is enough just to know that
\Delta^{a-k}_{b-k}(c(B)/c(A))
is some polynomial in the Chern classes of
A
and
B
.

Now we consider the case when
D=D_{k}(\sigma)
does not necessarily have the expected codimension. Assume that
D_{k-1}(\sigma)=\varnothing
.
Then, on
D_{k}(\sigma)
, there are kernel and cokernel bundles

0\rightarrow K\rightarrow A|_{D}\to B|_{D}\to C\rightarrow 0.

We have
\rank K=a-k
and
\rank C=b-k
.
Suppose further that
\iota:D\subset X
is a local complete intersection of codimension
d
with normal bundle
\mathcal{N}_{D/X}
.
Let
p=(a-k)(b-k)-d
be the difference of the expected and actual codimensions.
Then, by
[
12
, Example 14.4.7]
, we have

(2.5)

\iota_{*}\left(\left[c(K^{\vee}\otimes C)/c(\mathcal{N}_{D/X})\right]_{p}\right)=\Delta^{a-k}_{b-k}(c(B)/c(A))

where on the left, we formally expand the ratio of total Chern classes and then take the codimension

This expression is called the

.
When

is the expected codimension, the left hand side is
\iota_{*}[1]
which is just the fundamental class of
D
in

. Hence, (

) generalizes the usual Porteous formula (

).
