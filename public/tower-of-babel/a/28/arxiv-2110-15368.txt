Clustering of steady-state correlations in open systems with long-range interactions
Andrew Y. Guo, Simon Lieu, Minh C. Tran, Alexey V. Gorshkov

Abstract
Lieb-Robinson bounds are powerful analytical tools for constraining the dynamic and static properties of non-relativistic quantum systems. Recently, a complete picture for closed systems that evolve unitarily in time has been achieved. In experimental systems, however, interactions with the environment cannot generally be ignored, and the extension of Lieb-Robinson bounds to dissipative systems which evolve non-unitarily in time remains an open challenge. In this work, we prove two Lieb-Robinson bounds that constrain the dynamics of open quantum systems with long-range interactions that decay as a power-law in the distance between particles. Using a combination of these Lieb-Robinson bounds and mixing bounds which arise from “reversibility”—naturally satisfied for thermal environments—we prove the clustering of correlations in the steady states of open quantum systems with long-range interactions. Our work provides an initial step towards constraining the steady-state entanglement structure for a broad class of experimental platforms, and we highlight several open directions regarding the application of Lieb-Robinson bounds to dissipative systems.



I
Introduction

While the speed of information transfer is always bounded by the speed of light, many quantum platforms operate in a non-relativistic regime where typical velocities are far below this threshold.
Nevertheless, the Schrödinger equation admits fundamental limits to the rate at which correlations can spread throughout the system.
Such bounds are known as Lieb-Robinson bounds and are connected to a diverse array of phenomena, including the decay of correlations in the ground state
Hastings and Koma 2006
, generation of topological order
Bravyi et al. 2006
;
Bravyi et al. 2010
, efficiency of classical/quantum simulation
Osborne 2006
;
Tran et al. 2019a
, hardness of bosonic sampling tasks
Deshpande et al. 2018
, heating rates in periodically driven Floquet systems
Abanin et al. 2015
;
Tran et al. 2019b
, and signatures of quantum chaos
Lashkari et al. 2013
;
Guo et al. 2020
.

To date, most formulations of Lieb-Robinson bounds apply to closed systems that evolve via a unitary time-evolution operator.
In such systems, recent advances have proved tight information-transfer bounds for interaction ranges that span the whole spectrum from local
Chen and Lucas 2021
;
Wang and Hazzard 2020
to highly non-local regimes
Tran et al. 2019a
;
Chen and Lucas 2019
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020
;
Tran et al. 2021a
, and have been saturated via explicit state-transfer protocols
Eldredge et al. 2017
;
Tran et al. 2020
;
Tran et al. 2021b
.
While a complete picture for quantum information transfer has emerged for closed quantum systems, the analogous question for systems that evolve
non-unitarily
in time remains less well understood.
For a broad range of quantum platforms (including noisy quantum simulators), interactions with a larger environment are unavoidable and must be taken into account to accurately describe dynamics.
While progress in this direction has been made
Poulin 2010
;
Descamps 2013
;
Cubitt et al. 2015
;
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
;
Sweke et al. 2019
, the question of how the fundamental rate of information transfer differs for systems that interact with some larger environment remains unanswered.

Indeed, the notion of a Lieb-Robinson bound in an open system may seem
a priori
surprising from the point of view of quantum trajectories
Plenio and Knight 1998
. In this picture, in a time-step
dt
the system’s wavefunction either evolves via a non-unitary evolution operator, or a quantum jump discontinuously alters the state.
A specific trajectory belonging to a spatially-local Hamiltonian with local dissipation can transfer information faster than the limit set by the Hamiltonian’s Lieb-Robinson bound
Ashida and Ueda 2018
. Intuitively, this is because conditioning on measurements is an inherently nonlocal process. As an extreme example, it is possible to create a highly-entangled (GHZ) state from a product state in a time
t=\mathcal{O}\left\lparen 1\right\rparen
using only locally entangling gates and measurements, for a specific outcome of the measurements
Pham and Svore 2013
. This would violate the Lieb-Robinson bound for local systems, which gives
t=\Omega\left\lparen r\right\rparen
for distance
r

big
. After averaging over trajectories, the state of the system can be represented via a density matrix
\rho
which evolves via a master equation:
d\rho/dt=\mathcal{L}(\rho)
. Subsequently, the notion of a Lieb-Robinson bound is properly restored upon averaging over trajectories.

In this work, we make progress on the question of the fundamental rates of information propagation in open systems by proving a broad class of Lieb-Robinson bounds for systems with long-range interactions—specifically those that decay as a power-law
1/r^{\alpha}
in the distance
r
between particles, for some
\alpha>0
.
Such power-law-decaying interactions feature in experimental platforms relevant to quantum computation and simulation, such as Rydberg atoms
Saffman et al. 2010
, trapped ion crystals
Britton et al. 2012
;
Monroe et al. 2021
, polar molecules
Yan et al. 2013
, and nitrogen-vacancy color centers in diamond
Yao et al. 2012
.
In all of these platforms, interactions with a larger environment cannot be neglected, and a Markovian description of system dynamics is often justified. In such systems, improved understanding of the fundamental rates of information transfer has spurred the development of optimal protocols for quantum information processing and state transfer
Eldredge et al. 2017
;
Tran et al. 2021b
.

In addition to bounding dynamics of open long-range systems, we use these Lieb-Robinson bounds to constrain the entanglement structure of the corresponding steady states.
For closed systems, Lieb-Robinson bounds have played an important role in proving rigorous statements on the decay of correlations in gapped ground states
Hastings and Koma 2006
. This justifies the use of finite-dimensional matrix-product-state representations of the ground state in one-dimensional systems with local interactions
Hastings 2007
.
In this work, we prove the clustering of correlations in the steady states of open power-law systems, which may serve as a first step towards establishing an area-law scaling of entanglement for these systems, similar to what was done in Ref.
Gong et al. 2017
for the closed case.

This paper is organized as follows: in
Section

II
, we summarize the existing Lieb-Robinson bounds for open long-range systems and present two new bounds that are tighter for particular regimes of the power-law exponent
\alpha
.
The first yields a polynomial light cone for
\alpha>2d
, using a technique pioneered in Ref.
Tran et al. 2019a
.
The second gives a linear light cone for
\alpha>3
in 1D, using the method from Ref.
Chen and Lucas 2019
.
In
Section

III
, we also prove the clustering of correlations in the steady states of open long-range systems.
Specifically, we provide bounds on the extent of the covariance correlations and mutual information under certain assumptions on the Liouvillian mixing times.
We also prove a stability theorem for the stationary state under local Liouvillian perturbations, generalizing the results of Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
.

II
Lieb-Robinson bounds for open long-range systems

In this section, we review the results of the previous best-known Lieb-Robinson bounds for open long-range systems and state two new Lieb-Robinson bounds.

As a general set-up, we consider evolution by a long-range Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}(t)
that acts on a lattice
\Lambda
consisting of finite-level systems at each site.
We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the finite-dimensional Hilbert space representing all possible states of the system and by
\mathcal{B(H)}
the space of all operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
For an operator
O\in\mathcal{B(H)}
, we will be interested in how its expectation value changes as a function of time:
\langle O(t)\rangle=\tr[O(t)\rho]=\tr[O\rho(t)]
, where
\rho
is the initial state of the system, which evolves (in the Schrödinger picture) via
\rho(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}t}\rho
. For these purposes,
the time-evolution of
O
can be expressed as
O(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}O
, where
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is the adjoint Lindblad superoperator, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}O=+i[H,O]+\sum_{i}\left[L_{i}^{\dagger}OL_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i},O\}\right],

(1)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian and
L_{i}
are Lindblad operators (also referred to as “jump” operators)
Breuer and Petruccione 2010
. We emphasize that
O(t)
is
not
equivalent to the Heisenberg-Langevin time evolution for the operator
O
. For example, if the system has a unique steady state, all operators
O(t)
will be proportional to the identity at long times:
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}O(t)\sim\mathbb{I}
. Thus two operators that do not commute at
t=0
will start to commute at long times.

We will state the Lieb-Robinson-type bounds in this paper in terms of time-independent Liouvillians. However, we note that the proofs can be generalized with minor modifications to the case of time-dependent Liouvillians—i.e. those for which both
H
and
L_{i}
are allowed to vary in time.

To impose the long-range condition on
\mathcal{L}
, we decompose it into
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{Z\subset\Lambda}\mathcal{L}_{Z}
, where for any pair of sites
i,j
, we have the condition

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\ni i,j}\|\mathcal{L}_{Z}\|_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{O\in\mathcal{B(H)}}\frac{\|\mathcal{L}_{Z}O\|}{\|O\|}\leq\frac{1}{\text{dist}(i,j)^{\alpha}},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the standard operator norm, or
\infty
-norm, and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the superoperator, or “
\infty\rightarrow\infty
” norm (referred to as such because the second term in
Eq.

2
uses the operator
\infty
-norm in both the numerator and the denominator).
Here
\text{dist}(i,j)
is the distance between
i
and
j
, and
\alpha
is the positive real parameter that controls the long-ranged nature of the interaction.

II.1
Prior work on open-system Lieb-Robinson bounds

In Ref.
Sweke et al. 2019
, Sweke
et al.
generalized the Lieb-Robinson bound in Ref.
Hastings and Koma 2006
for
\alpha>d
to open systems.
Letting
A\in\mathcal{B}(X)
be an operator supported on
X
,
K_{Y}\in\mathbb{L}_{Y}
be a Liouvillian supported on
Y
, and
e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}
be the evolution under the adjoint Liouvillian superoperator.
The corresponding superoperator bound is:

\displaystyle\left\|K_{Y}(e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A)\right\|\leq C\|K_{Y}\|_{\infty}\left\|A\right\|\absolutevalue{X}\absolutevalue{Y}\left(\frac{e^{vt}-1}{r^{\alpha}}\right),

(3)

where
r\coloneqq d(X,Y)
, and
C
and
v
are
\mathcal{O}\left\lparen 1\right\rparen
constants.
In the closed-system picture, the conventional Lieb-Robinson-type bound can be recovered by choosing
K_{Y}
such that
K_{Y}(O_{X})=i[O_{X},O_{Y}]
and replacing
\|K_{Y}\|_{\infty}
with
2\|O_{Y}\|
.

For this conventional bound, the velocity scales as
v\propto 2^{\alpha}
, which diverges in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.
To recover the Lieb-Robinson bound for short-range interacting systems, an improved bound is required that uses a slight modification of the technique from Ref.
Sweke et al. 2019
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|K_{Y}(e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A)\right\|\leq C\|K_{Y}\|_{\infty}\left\|A\right\|\absolutevalue{X}\absolutevalue{Y}\biggl(&\frac{e^{\tilde{v}t}}{[(1-\mu)r]^{\alpha}}\\
&+e^{\tilde{v}t-\mu r}\biggr),\end{split}

(4)

where
\mu\in(0,1)
and
\tilde{v}
are constants, and
\tilde{v}
is independent of
\alpha
.
The closed-system version of this bound was first proven in Ref.
Gong et al. 2014
for two-body interactions and later generalized to
k
-body interactions in Ref.
Tran et al. 2019b
.
In Ref.
Sweke et al. 2019
, Sweke
et al.
also prove Lieb-Robinson-type bounds for
\alpha\leq d
.
For this regime of
\alpha
, one needs to restrict to a finite-sized lattice, due to the energy being (in general) non-additive for subsystems
Dauxois et al. 2002
.
Denoting the system size of the lattice by
N\coloneqq\absolutevalue{\Lambda}
,
the combined strength
J
of the terms acting on a single site scales as
J=\Theta\left\lparen N^{1-\alpha/d}\right\rparen
for
\alpha<d
and
J=\Theta\left\lparen\log{N}\right\rparen
for
\alpha=d

big
;
Guo et al. 2020
.
The bound then becomes:

\displaystyle\left\|K_{Y}(e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A)\right\|\leq C\|K_{Y}\|_{\infty}\left\|A\right\|\absolutevalue{X}\absolutevalue{Y}\left(\frac{e^{Jt}-1}{Jr^{\alpha}}\right).

(5)

The effective Lieb-Robinson velocity in this case diverges in the thermodynamic limit, but is finite for all finite
N
.

II.2
Power-law light-cone bound for
\alpha>2d

We prove a Lieb-Robinson bound for
\alpha>2d
using the truncation-of-unitaries-approach presented by Tran
et al.

Tran et al. 2019b
.
The technique takes as input the existing open-systems Lieb-Robinson bound in
Eq.

4
and bootstraps it to obtain a tighter bound:

\displaystyle\left\|K_{Y}(e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A)\right\|\leq C\|K_{Y}\|_{\infty}\left\|A\right\|\frac{t^{\alpha-d}}{r^{\alpha-2d}}.

(6)

This bound yields the power-law light-cone contour
t=r^{\frac{\alpha-2d}{\alpha-d}}
.
The proof of this bound involves approximating the time evolution of the operators by a sequence of operators that span successively larger and larger subsets of the lattice, and bounding the error of each successive approximation by the existing Lieb-Robinson bound.
We provide the full details of the derivation in
Appendix

E
.

II.3
Linear light-cone bound for
d=1
,
\alpha>3

Finally, we prove a bound with a linear light cone for open-long-range systems with
\alpha>3
in
d=1
dimensions based on the techniques developed in Ref.
Chen and Lucas 2019
.
In the process, we tighten the tail of the Lieb-Robinson bound given in that work from
1/r
to approximately
1/r^{\alpha-2}
.
The authors of Ref.
Chen and Lucas 2019
proved the following bound for the closed-system dynamics of Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{ij}H_{ij}
consisting of two-body terms:

\displaystyle\left\|[e^{iHt}Ae^{-iHt},B]\right\|\leq C\left\|A\right\|\left\|B\right\|\frac{t}{r},

(7)

where
B\in\mathcal{B}(Y)
is an operator supported on
Y
.
Likewise assuming a two-body Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{ij}\mathcal{L}_{ij}
, we obtain the following open-systems bound:

\displaystyle\left\|K_{Y}(e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A)\right\|\leq C\left\|K_{Y}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|A\right\|\frac{t}{r^{\alpha-2-o(1)}},

(8)

where the
o(1)
denotes some constant that can be made arbitrarily small.
The result yields a linear light cone
t\gtrsim r
for all
\alpha>3
.
The proof roughly proceeds by expanding out the evolution operator
e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}
into a series of products of Liouvillian terms
\mathcal{L}_{ij}
.
For each term in the series, we select out a subsequence of terms that move the operator forward (i.e. towards
Y
) and integrate out the other terms.
By only taking into account the contributions from the terms in the subsequences, we are able to obtain a tighter Lieb-Robinson bound.
We provide the mathematical details of the proof in
Appendix

F
.

III
Bounds on correlations in the steady states of open long-range systems

In this section, we prove the clustering of correlations in the steady states of open long-range systems.
We first state a lemma that describes how to use a modified version of the Lieb-Robinson bounds stated in the previous section to bound how far operators can spread under evolution by the (adjoint) Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
.
Specifically, we give a bound on the error of approximating the time-evolution of an operator
A
supported on a site
X\in\Lambda
by a truncated adjoint Liouvillian that only acts on ball of radius
r
centered on a site
X\in\Lambda
(see
Fig.

1
).

Figure 1:
The evolution of an operator
A
initially supported on
X
by an adjoint Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
can be approximated by the same operator evolved by the truncated version of the Liouvillian,
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}
, supported on a ball of radius
r
around
X
, up to an error given by
C(r,t)
.

Lemma 1

(Bounds on the error incurred by approximating of time-evolved operators by local ones)
.

Let
A
be an operator initially supported on a site
X\in\Lambda
and let
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
be the restriction of the long-range Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
to the ball of radius
r
centered on
X
.
Let
\tilde{A}(t)
be the time-evolved version of
A
under
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}
.
Then the error in the approximation of
A(t)
by
\tilde{A}(t)
is bounded by

\displaystyle\|A(t)-\tilde{A}(t)\|\leq K\|A\|\,\mathcal{C}(r,t),

(9)

where
K
is some constant, and
\mathcal{C}(r,t)
is a modified version of the standard Lieb-Robinson bound adapted to the problem of locally approximating time-evolved operators.
In the large-
r
limit, the tightest-known bounds for open systems with long-range interactions with
\alpha>d
scale asymptotically as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(r,t)\propto\begin{cases}\displaystyle e^{vt}/r^{\alpha-d},&\alpha>d,\\
\displaystyle t^{\alpha-d+1}/r^{\alpha-3d},&\alpha>3d,\\
\displaystyle t^{2}/r^{\alpha-3},&\alpha>3,d=1.\end{cases}

(10)

For
\alpha\leq d
, the bounds also depend on the system size of the lattice
N\coloneqq|\Lambda|
.
When
r\propto N^{1/d}
, the bounds scale as follows:

\mathcal{C}(N,t)\propto\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{e^{\Theta\left\lparen N^{1-\alpha/d}\right\rparen t}-1}{\Theta\left\lparen N^{1-\alpha/d}\right\rparen},&\alpha<d,\\
\displaystyle\frac{e^{\Theta\left\lparen\log(N)\right\rparen t}-1}{\Theta\left\lparen\log(N)\right\rparen},&\alpha=d.\end{cases}

(11)

This concludes the statement of
Lemma

1
.

The proof of
Lemma

1
follows straightforwardly from the open-system Lieb-Robinson bounds detailed in
Section

II
.
In particular, the three lines of
Eq.

10
follow from
Eq.

4
,
Eq.

6
, and
Eq.

8
, respectively,
while
Eq.

11
comes from
Eq.

5
.
In
Appendix

A
, we provide the details of the derivation of the bounds in
Lemma

1
.
We now proceed to derive the bounds on clustering of correlations in the steady states of gapped, reversible Liouvillians.

III.1
Bound on covariance correlations

In this first section, we show how open-system Lieb-Robinson bounds constrain the correlations in the steady state of a Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
with dissipative gap
\lambda
.

The dissipative gap
\lambda>0

is defined as the magnitude of the least-negative non-zero real part of an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
.
(Throughout this work, we shall also assume that the Liouvillian is
primitive
, i.e. it has a unique steady state such that
\mathcal{L}
has one eigenvalue of zero.)
In addition to the Lieb-Robinson bounds, we will also appeal to certain “mixing bounds” which describe how fast arbitrary initial states (or various correlation functions) converge to the steady state.

For the mixing bounds, we need to impose “reversibility” on the Liouvillian. We say that a Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
is
s
-reversible if there exists some operator
\sigma
such that
\Gamma_{s}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{s}
is satisfied; the superoperator
\Gamma_{s}
is defined via
\Gamma_{s}(f)=(\sigma^{s}f\sigma^{1-s}+\sigma^{1-s}f\sigma^{s})/2
and
s\in[0,1]
. For
s
-reversible Liouvillians, it is easy to see that
\sigma
is the steady state of
\mathcal{L}
(since
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I})=0
).
A sufficient condition for a Liouvillian to satisfy
s
-reversibility (for all
s
) is if the dissipators
L_{i}
satisfy a detailed-balance condition (and the Hamiltonian is zero,
H=0
), which is naturally obeyed for systems coupled to a thermal bath
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
. (More explicitly, the detailed-balance condition is satisfied if dissipators come in energy raising/lowering pairs with respect to some effective Hamiltonian
\bar{H}
—for example, if
[\bar{H},L_{\pm}]=\pm\omega L_{\pm}
and
|L_{-}|/|L_{+}|=\exp(2 \beta\omega)
where
\beta^{-1}
is an effective temperature.)

Returning to the topic of correlations, we let
\rho
be a quantum state defined on the lattice
\Lambda
.
We are interested in the
covariance correlation
between non-overlapping
X,Y\in\Lambda
:

\displaystyle T_{\rho}(X:Y):=\text{sup}_{\left\|f\right\|=\left\|g\right\|=1}|\text{Tr}[(f\otimes g)(\rho_{XY}-\rho_{X}\otimes\rho_{Y})]|,

(12)

where
f
and
g
are Hermitian operators with
f
supported on region
X
and
g
supported on region
Y
, and where
\rho_{X}
is the reduced density matrix constructed from
\rho
by tracing over the complement of
X
. Our goal is to bound this correlation function in terms of
\lambda
and the distance between
X
and
Y
.

We follow Theorem 9 in Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
.
Let
\sigma
be the steady state of the Liouvillian.
From the right-hand side of
Eq.

12
, we define

\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f,g)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}[(fg+gf)\sigma]-\text{Tr}[f\sigma]\text{Tr}[g\sigma],

(13)

which is equivalent to the term inside the sup (because
f
and
g
commute).
Now we use the bound (which follows directly from the triangle inequality)

|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f,g)|\leq|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f_{t},g_{t})|+|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f,g)-\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f_{t},g_{t})|.

(14)

Here
f_{t}
and
g_{t}
are the time-evolved versions of
f
and
g
under
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
.
This step allows us to relate a
static
covariance to time-dependent quantities; we will use dynamical bounds to constrain the form of the latter, then pick an optimal time which maximally bounds the static covariance.

The first term on the right is constrained by the variance bound for
s
-reversible, primitive Liouvillians (see Appendix
B
)

|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f_{t},g_{t})|\leq 4\left\|f\right\|\left\|g\right\|e^{-2\lambda t},

(15)

where
\lambda
is the dissipative gap of
\mathcal{L}
.
Intuitively, this relationship can be understood as follows: the operators
f_{t},g_{t}
both evolve (in time) toward an operator that is proportional to the identity, so the covariance between them will eventually tend to zero as a function of time. The rate at which this occurs is set by the dissipative gap of the system.

To bound the second term, we use the relation
\Tr[\sigma f_{t}]=\Tr[\sigma f]
, which holds for all observables
f
.
This gives:

\displaystyle|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f,g)-\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f_{t},g_{t})|

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(|\Tr[(fg-f_{t}g_{t})\sigma]+\Tr[(gf-g_{t}f_{t})\sigma]|)

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(|\Tr[((fg)_{t}-f_{t}g_{t})\sigma]+\Tr[((gf)_{t}-g_{t}f_{t})\sigma]|)

(18)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(\|(fg)_{t}-f_{t}g_{t}\|+\|(gf)_{t}-g_{t}f_{t}\|)

(19)

\displaystyle\leq K\left\|f\right\|\left\|g\right\|\mathcal{C}(r,t),

(20)

where
r\coloneqq d(X,Y)
. We obtain the inequality in the final line using the open-system Lieb-Robinson bounds
\mathcal{C}(r,t)
given in
Lemma

1
.
Specifically, we use the following Lemma, which is itself a restatement of Corollary 7 in Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
:

Lemma 2

(Time-evolution of spatially separated observables)
.

Take two operators
A
and
B
supported on
X,Y\in\Lambda
respectively such that
r\coloneqq d(X,Y)
, and let
A(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}A
and
B(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}B
be their time-evolution under the adjoint Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
.
We also define
(AB)(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}(AB)
.
Then the following bound holds:

\displaystyle\|(AB)(t)-A(t)B(t)\|\leq K\|A\|\|B\|\mathcal{C}(r,t),

(21)

where
\mathcal{C}(r,t)
is given by
Lemma

1
and
K
is some constant that depends on lattice parameters.

Lemma

2
bounds the difference between operators that evolve together in the Heisenberg picture as opposed to evolving separately.
Again we emphasize that
A(t)
,
B(t)
, and
(AB)(t)
are
not
equivalent to Heisenberg-Langevin evolution, a fact that is at the core of this bound.
We defer the short proof of
Lemma

2
to
Appendix

C
and move on to proving the bound on the covariance correlations.

Theorem 1

(Bounds on steady-state covariance correlations)
.

Consider Hermitian operators
f,g
which are supported on two non-overlapping subsets
X
and
Y
of the
d
-dimensional cubic lattice
\Lambda
, and let
\mathcal{L}
be an
s
-reversible Liouvillian with stationary state
\sigma
and dissipative gap
\lambda
that satisfies the conditions in
Eq.

2
.
Then there exists a constant
c>0
which only depends on
\lambda,v
such that

T_{\sigma}(X:Y)\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle c\left(r^{\alpha-d}\right)^{\frac{-2\lambda}{v+2\lambda}},&\alpha>d,\\
\displaystyle c\frac{\log(r)^{\alpha-d+1}}{r^{\alpha-3d}},&\alpha>3d,\\
\displaystyle c\frac{\log(r)^{2}}{r^{\alpha-3}},&\alpha>3,d=1.\end{cases}

(22)

Proof.

From our previous analysis [see Eqs. (
15
) and (
20
)] on the covariance correlation in
Eq.

14
, we have

III.2
Stability result and mutual information bound

In this section, we will use the aforementioned bounds to constrain steady-state properties of open systems with power-law interactions.
In addition to the newly-derived Lieb-Robinson bounds, we will appeal to a “mixing bound” which provides an upper bound to how fast an arbitrary initial state will converge to the steady state. The following mixing bound was derived in Ref.
Kastoryano and Temme 2013
, and generalizes the mixing bound of classical Markov chains to quantum semigroups:

Lemma 3
.

Consider a primitive Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
that has a full-rank steady state
\sigma
, and is
\frac{1}{2}
-reversible. Then an arbitrary initial state
\rho
will converge to
\sigma
at a rate bounded by

\lVert\rho(t)-\sigma\lVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{2\log( \lVert\sigma^{-1} \lVert)}e^{-\beta t},

(29)

where
\beta
is called the log-Sobolev constant associated with
\mathcal{L}
.

Intuition can be gained by considering an “infinite-temperature” steady state
\sigma=\mathbb{I}/d_{H}
where
d_{H}
is the dimension of the Hilbert space. The mixing bound above states that the coefficient in front of the exponential will scale as
\sqrt{\log(d_{H})}
, i.e. it will increase with the dimension of the Hilbert space. This is because the convergence toward the unique steady state from an arbitrary initial state can be slow if the dimension of the Hilbert space is large.

Theorem 2

(Effect of perturbations on reduced steady-state density matrix)
.

Let
X,Y
be two non-overlapping subsets of a
d
-dimensional cubic lattice
\Lambda
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{1}
be a primitive and
\frac{1}{2}
-reversible Liouvillian with log-Sobolev constant
\beta
, and let
\mathcal{L}_{2}
be a Liouvillian perturbation, acting trivially outside of
X
. Let
\rho
be the stationary state of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, and let
\sigma
be the stationary state of
\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Then,

\lVert\rho_{Y}-\sigma_{Y}\lVert_{1}\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{r^{\alpha-d}}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{v+2\beta}},&\alpha>d,\\
\displaystyle c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\log(r)^{\alpha-d+1}}{r^{\alpha-3d}},&\alpha>3d,\\
\displaystyle c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\log(r)^{2}}{r^{\alpha-3}},&\hskip-20.00003pt\alpha>3,d=1,\end{cases}

(30)

where
c
is a constant and
r
is the distance between
X
and
Y
.

The theorem basically says that the effects of local perturbations in the Liouvillian will not be felt significantly by the steady state of the system at sufficiently distant locations.
We prove the theorem by first introducing a time-evolved state to interpolate between the two steady states.
This allows us to use a combination of mixing-time and Lieb-Robinson bounds to restrict the terms in this bound.
Then we apply the same minimization procedure used in
Theorem

1
for the covariance-correlations bound to arrive at the stated bounds in
Eq.

30
[each of which follow directly from the three cases in
Eq.

10
].
We defer the proof of this result, which is similar to the proof of
Theorem

1
, to
Appendix

D
.

We now prove a bound on the mutual information in the steady state. The mutual information between two regions
A,B
is defined as

I_{\rho}(A:B)=S(\rho_{AB}||\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}),

(31)

where
S(\rho||\sigma)=\tr[\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)]
is the relative entropy. The following theorem holds.

Theorem 3

(Clustering of mutual information)
.

Let
A,B
be two non-overlapping subsets of a
d
-dimensional cubic lattice
\Lambda
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a primitive and
\frac{1}{2}
-reversible Liouvillian with log-Sobolev constant
\beta
. Let
\rho
be the stationary state of
\mathcal{L}
. Then the mutual information between the two regions
I_{\rho}(A:B)
is bounded by

I_{\rho}(A:B)\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{r^{\alpha-d}}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{v+2\beta}},&\alpha>d,\\
\displaystyle c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\log(r)^{\alpha-d+1}}{r^{\alpha-3d}},&\alpha>3d,\\
\displaystyle c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\log(r)^{2}}{r^{\alpha-3}},&\hskip-20.00003pt\alpha>3,d=1,\end{cases}

(32)

where
c
is a constant and
r
is the distance between
A,B
.

The significance of this result is that the mutual-information correlations in the steady state of an open long-range system decay as a power-law in the distance between regions.
This bound, which relies on the existence of the log-Sobolev constant, is tighter than the naive bound that would result from simply applying the bound on the covariance correlation in
Theorem

1
to
I_{\rho}(A:B)
.

Proof.

We define the semi-group
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
to be the terms in
\mathcal{L}
that act entirely within balls of radius
r/2
centered around
A
and
B
, and let
\sigma
be the steady state of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
. Simple manipulations imply:

\displaystyle I_{\rho}(A:B)

\displaystyle=-S(\rho_{AB})+S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})

(33)

\displaystyle\leq-S(\rho_{AB})-\tr[\rho_{A}\log\sigma_{A}]-\tr[\rho_{B}\log\sigma_{B}]

(34)

\displaystyle=S(\rho_{AB}||\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}).

(35)

where we have used
S(\rho||\sigma)\geq 0
to obtain the inequality.
The RHS further satisfies the inequality:

S(\rho_{AB}||\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\leq\log( ||\rho_{AB}^{-1} ||)||\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}||_{1},

(36)

which is a standard result (c.f. Eq. (36) in Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
).
From here, we can apply the bounds in
Theorem

2
, using
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathcal{L}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
,
Y=A\cup B
, and
X=\Lambda\setminus Y
.
∎

IV
Summary and outlook

In this work, we have proven generalized Lieb-Robinson bounds which constrain the dynamics of open, Markovian systems with power-law interactions and used them to constrain correlations in the steady state.

We comment briefly on the tightness of the bounds derived in this work. Intuitively, one might expect that the presence of dissipation should lead to tighter Lieb-Robinson bounds for open systems than for their closed counterparts, since the presence of decoherence from a bath might limit the speed of quantum information transfer.
In this work, we have generalized the proof of Lieb-Robinson bounds from closed system dynamics to Markovian evolution (
a priori
, such bounds need not exist for Markovian dynamics).
However, our bounds only depend on interaction range and the dimension of the lattice.
Any bound that only depends on these two inputs cannot be tighter than the corresponding closed-system Lieb-Robinson bound, since the latter is a special case of former.
As such, the saturating protocols for closed systems
Tran et al. 2020
;
Tran et al. 2021b
can be used to saturate open Lieb-Robinson bounds such as those uncovered in
Lemma

1
.
In the future, it would be interesting to add another degree of freedom into formulations of open Lieb-Robinson bounds: the dissipative gap. (Some progress has been made in showing that Lieb-Robinson velocities can get tighter in dissipative systems
Descamps 2013
.)
In principle, it might be possible to derive Lieb-Robinson bounds that reduce to closed-system ones when the dissipative gap is zero, and get tighter in the presence of non-zero dissipation.
Then one can develop protocols that saturate the dissipative-gap-dependent bounds.
Another question in this direction is whether the conditional evolution generated via a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian can also exhibit a dissipative-gap-dependent Lieb-Robinson bound that reduces to the conventional one in the dissipationless limit.

Setting aside the idea of a Lieb-Robinson bound that depends on the dissipative gap, there is still the question of generalizing the best-known closed-system bounds to Markovian evolution. In particular, the recent Lieb-Robinson bounds in Refs.
Kuwahara and Saito 2020
and
Tran et al. 2021a
both provide opportunities for generalization to open systems.
Such a result would likely require a modification of the interaction-picture technique first developed in
Foss-Feig et al. 2015
and used in both subsequent works to open-system dynamics.
Generalizing these bounds would directly lead to tighter bounds on operator spreading in
Lemma

1
and allow us to prove tighter bounds on correlation clustering in steady states (
Theorem

1
and
Theorem

3
)

Another way to probe the tightness of the steady-state correlation bounds derived in this work would be to improve the mixing bounds, which currently require the open system to be in thermal equilibrium.
It would be interesting to derive more general mixing bounds which also apply to systems that are out of thermal equilibrium.

One of the salient applications of Lieb-Robinson bounds is in rigorous proofs on the stability of the spectral gap in topologically ordered quantum matter. For example, Ref.
Bravyi et al. 2010
used closed-system Lieb-Robinson bounds to show that spatially local perturbations will not close energy gaps in the toric code, thus leading to phase stability against arbitrary local noise. Can we use a similar approach to show that local perturbations will not close the dissipative gap in a topologically-ordered open system? A robust qubit steady-state structure would be useful toward the quest of passive quantum error correction
Lidar et al. 1998
.

Lieb-Robinson bounds can be used to prove area-law entanglement scaling in the ground state of one-dimensional systems with local interactions
Hastings 2007
. This result helps to rigorously justify the validity of the matrix-product state ansatz for the ground state of such systems. For closed systems with power-law interactions, Lieb-Robinson bounds can be used to further extend area-law scaling to certain broad classes of systems
Gong et al. 2017
. Do the results presented in this paper have similar implications for area-law scaling of the steady state? This would have direct implications for the matrix-product operator ansatz in modeling open systems.

Finally, the Lieb-Robinson-type bounds we proved apply for the operator, or
\infty
-norm.
However, there exists a hierarchy of Lieb-Robinson-like bounds that have the potential to be tighter for certain information processing tasks such as scrambling and transferring a quantum state of a local subsystem without knowledge of the initial state of the rest of the system.
These bounds can use other norms such as the Frobenius norm defined by
\|O\|_{F}=\sqrt{\Tr{O^\dag O}}

Tran et al. 2020
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2021
;
Yin and Lucas 2020
;
Chen and Lucas 2021
or apply to free-particle systems
Guo et al. 2020
;
Tran et al. 2020
.
It would be interesting to generalize these bounds to open systems as well.

Appendix B
Variance bound for reversible Liouvillians

Here we provide a derivation of the covariance bound used in Eq. (
15
). We show that
s
-reversibility is important for this bound to hold. We define the variance of an observable
f
in the steady state
\sigma
as
\text{Var}[f]=\text{Tr}[f^{2}\sigma]-\text{Tr}[f\sigma]^{2}
, which is real and positive. We wish to find a bound for
\text{Var}[f_{t}]
for the time-evolved observable
f_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}f
.

The Liouvillian is a non-Hermitian superoperator, which means that each eigenvalue has right and left eigenoperators:

\mathcal{L}(r_{i})=\lambda_{i}r_{i},\qquad\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(l_{i})=\lambda_{i}^{*}l_{i}.

(45)

From the structure of the adjoint Liouvillian (
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
), it is clear that
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I})=0
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity operator. This implies that one of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}
is zero, and the corresponding right eigenoperator
\sigma
is called the steady state and satisfies
\mathcal{L}(\sigma)=0
and
e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\sigma)=\sigma
. The eigenoperators are “bi-orthonormal” via the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product:
\text{Tr}[l_{i}^{\dagger}r_{j}]=\text{Tr}[r_{i}^{\dagger}l_{j}]=\delta_{ij}
.

We define the superoperator
\Gamma_{s}(f)=(\sigma^{s}f\sigma^{1-s}+\sigma^{1-s}f\sigma^{s})/2
where
s\in[0,1]
and
\sigma
is a full-rank, Hermitian operator with positive eigenvalues. We say that a Liouvillian is
s
-reversible for some
s\in[0,1]
if

\Gamma_{s}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{s}
.
By acting both sides on the operator
\mathbb{I}
, we see that
\sigma
is the steady state, i.e. that
\mathcal{L}(\sigma)=0
. Imposing reversibility implies that the spectrum must be real because the Liouvillian is pseudo-Hermitian with a positive-definite metric
Mostafazadeh 2002
.

The dynamics preserves Hermiticity of a density matrix, which implies that
\mathcal{L}(f^{\dagger})=[\mathcal{L}(f)]^{\dagger}
, and the same for the adjoint:
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(f^{\dagger})=[\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(f)]^{\dagger}
, where
f
is an arbitrary operator. This implies that (right and left) eigenoperators with real eigenvalues must be Hermitian. For
s
-reversible Liouvillians, the entire spectrum is real, which implies that all eigenoperators are Hermitian.

Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
derives a bound for the time-evolved variance in a
s
-reversible system:

\text{Var}[f_{t}]\leq e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}\text{Var}[f(t=0)],

(46)

where
\{-\lambda_{i}\}
is the real, non-positive spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
, sorted from smallest to largest magnitude with
\lambda_{0}=0,\lambda_{1}>0
. (
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
, i.e. the dissipative gap.) Here we derive this bound using the properties of the eigenoperators of
\mathcal{L}
.

Consider a general Hermitian operator which we write in terms of left eigenoperators

f=\sum_{j}c_{j}l_{j}\Rightarrow f_{t}=\sum_{j}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}t}l_{j},

(47)

where
c_{j}
are real because
f
is Hermitian.
Noting that
\text{Tr}[l_{j}\sigma]=0
for
j\neq 0
, we find

\text{Var}[f_{t}]=\text{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{j\neq 0}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}t}l_{j}\right)^{2}\sigma\right]=\sum_{j\neq 0}c_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t},

(48)

where in the last equality we have used
\text{Tr}[l_{i}r_{j}]=\text{Tr}[l_{i}\Gamma_{s}(l_{j})]=\delta_{ij}
. From this, it is easy to see that
\lambda_{i>1}\geq\lambda_{1}
implies the bound Eq. (
46
). For the more general case of a complex spectrum, it is not clear how to repeat the derivation above. We therefore find that
s
-reversibility is sufficient for the bound to hold. (It is unclear whether
s
-reversibility is necessary for the bound.)

Given the bound Eq. (
46
), one can repeat the steps outlined in Eqs. [49-55] in Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
to obtain the bound used in Eq. (
15
) of the main text. For completeness, we include these steps below:

\displaystyle|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f_{t},g_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\text{Var}(f_{t})\text{Var}(g_{t})}

(49)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2t\lambda_{1}}\sqrt{\text{Var}(f)\text{Var}(g)}.

(50)

The inequality in (
49
) is due to Holder’s inequality. The variance can be bounded by

\displaystyle\sqrt{\text{Var}(f)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Tr[\sigma(f-\Tr[\sigma f])^{2}]}

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lVert(f-\Tr[\sigma f])^{2}\lVert}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f-\Tr[\sigma f]\lVert

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f\lVert+|\Tr[\sigma f]|

(54)

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert f\lVert.

(55)

Putting together (
50
) and (
55
) leads to the desired bound (where
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
, i.e. the dissipative gap):

|\text{Cov}_{\sigma}(f_{t},g_{t})|\leq 4\left\|f\right\|\left\|g\right\|e^{-2\lambda t},

(56)

which matches
Eq.

15
in the main text.

Appendix C
Bound on the difference between two operators evolving separately versus evolving together

In this section, we provide the proof of the bound in

.

Take two operators

\displaystyle\|(AB)(t)-A(t)B(t)\|\leq K^{\prime}\|A\|\|B\|\mathcal{C}(r,t),

where
\mathcal{C}(r,t)
is given by the Lieb-Robinson-type bound corresponding to the system in question (see
Lemma

1
) and
K^{\prime}
is some constant that depends on lattice parameters.

Proof.

We define the semi-group
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}
to be the terms in
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
that act entirely within balls of radius
r/2
centered around
X
and
Y
.
Then, let
\tilde{A}(t)
be the time-evolved version of
A
under
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\dagger}
and likewise for
\tilde{B}(t)
.
By definition, this implies that
\tilde{A}(t)\tilde{B}(t)=(\widetilde{AB})(t)
.
We then get

\displaystyle\|(AB)(t)-A(t)B(t)\|\leq\|(AB)(t)-(\widetilde{AB})(t)\|+\|A(t)B(t)-\tilde{A}(t)\tilde{B}(t)\|.

(58)

The first term on the RHS of

may be bounded by the Lieb-Robinson bound stated in
Lemma

1
(for an operator that is initially supported on two sites instead of one). The second term can be bounded by

\displaystyle\|A(t)B(t)-\tilde{A}(t)\tilde{B}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A(t)(B(t)-\tilde{B}(t))\|+\|(A(t)-\tilde{A}(t))\tilde{B}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A\|\|B(t)-\tilde{B}(t)\|+\|A(t)-\tilde{A}(t)\|\|B\|,

(60)

using

and the submultiplicativity of the operator norm.
Using the Lieb-Robinson bound again, we get

Appendix D
Effect of perturbations on reduced steady-state density matrix

In this section, we provide the proof of
Theorem

2
. The argument hews closely to that of Lemma 11 in Ref.
Kastoryano and Eisert 2013
, but uses the Lieb-Robinson bounds for open long-range systems given in the main text.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X,Y
be two non-overlapping subsets of a
d
-dimensional cubic lattice
\Lambda
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a primitive and
s
-reversible Liouvillian with log-Sobolev constant
\beta
, and let
\mathcal{Q}
be a local Liouvillian perturbation, acting trivially outside of
X
. Let
\rho
be the stationary state of
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\sigma
be the stationary state of
\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q}
. Then,

\lVert\rho_{Y}-\sigma_{Y}\lVert_{1}\leq\begin{cases}c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{r^{\alpha-d}}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{v+2\beta}},&\alpha>d,\\
c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\log(r)^{\alpha-d+1}}{r^{\alpha-3d}},&\alpha>3d,\\
c\log( \lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\log(r)^{2}}{r^{\alpha-3}},&\alpha>3,\end{cases}

(62)

where
c
is some constant, and
r
is the distance between
X
and
Y
.

Proof.

We use the following definition of the trace norm:

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\rho-\sigma\lVert_{1}=\max_{0\leq A\leq\mathbb{I}}\tr[A(\rho-\sigma)],

(63)

for positive semi-definite
A
. This implies

\lVert\rho_{Y}-\sigma_{Y}\lVert_{1}=2\tr[(A_{Y}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{Y^{c}})(\rho-\sigma)],

(64)

where
A_{Y}=\tr_{Y^{c}}[\text{argmax}_{0\leq A\leq\mathbb{I}}\tr[A(\rho-\sigma)]]
.
We use the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\tr[(A_{Y}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{Y^{c}})(\rho-\sigma)]

\displaystyle=\tr[(A_{Y}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{Y^{c}})\left[(e^{\mathcal{L}t}-e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q})t})(\phi)+(\sigma-e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q})t}(\phi))+(e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\phi)-\rho)\right]]

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\tr[(A_{Y}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{Y^{c}})(e^{\mathcal{L}t}-e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q})t})(\phi)]+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tr_{Y^{c}}[\sigma-e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q})t}(\phi)]\lVert_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tr_{Y^{c}}[e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\phi)-\rho]\lVert_{1},

(66)

where
\phi
is an arbitrary state. Note that we have introduced two time-evolved operators in this step.
We will now use a combination of mixing bounds and Lieb-Robinson bounds to restrict the RHS.
The last term is bounded via the log-Sobolev bound:

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tr_{Y^{c}}[e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\phi)-\rho]\lVert_{1}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(\lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\beta t}.

(67)

This is basically an upper bound on how fast an arbitrary initial state must converge towards the steady state.
The second term in Eq. (
65
) can be bounded using a combination of Lieb-Robinson bounds and the log-Sobolev bound:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\tr_{Y^{c}}[\sigma-e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q})t}(\phi)]\lVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\tr[A_{Y}e^{(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{Q})t}(\sigma-\phi)]

(68)

\displaystyle=\tr[e^{(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger})t}(A_{Y})(\sigma-\phi)]

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\tr[(e^{(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger})t}-e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t})(A_{Y})(\sigma-\phi)]+\tr[e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}(A_{Y})(\sigma-\phi)].

(70)

The last term can again be bounded via the log-Sobolev bound:

\displaystyle\tr[e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}(A_{Y})(\sigma-\phi)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\sigma-\phi)\lVert_{1}\leq\left(2\log(\lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\beta t}.

(71)

The first term can be bounded via the Lieb-Robinson bound:

\displaystyle\tr[(e^{(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger})t}-e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t})(A_{Y})(\sigma-\phi)]

\displaystyle\leq\tr[(e^{(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger})t}-e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t})(A_{Y})]\lVert\sigma-\phi\lVert_{1}

(72)

\displaystyle\leq 2\tr[(e^{(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger})t}-e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t})(A_{Y})]

(73)

\displaystyle\leq 2\tr[(e^{(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger})t}-e^{\mathcal{L}_{X^{c}}^{\dagger}t})(A_{Y})]+2\tr[(e^{\mathcal{L}_{X^{c}}^{\dagger}t}-e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t})(A_{Y})]

(74)

\displaystyle\leq K\lVert A_{Y}\lVert\,\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{C}(r,t),

(75)

where
\mathcal{L}_{X^{c}}
is the Liouvillian restricted to terms that do not intersect
X
.
K
is an arbitrary constant, and
\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{C}(r,t)
is the Lieb-Robinson bound stated in
Lemma

1
.

The first term in Eq. (
65
) can be bounded using the Lieb-Robinson approach above. Gathering all the bounds together leads to

\lVert\rho_{Y}-\sigma_{Y}\lVert_{1}\leq K_{1}\left(\log(\lVert\rho^{-1} \lVert)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\beta t}+K_{2}\,\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{C}(r,t)

(76)

for arbitrary constants
K_{1},K_{2}
. We wish to pick a time
t
that minimizes the RHS.
We now note that the RHS has the same functional form as the function that we needed to minimize for the covariance correlation bound. Repeating the minimization procedure outlined in Theorem
1
, we arrive at the stated bounds in
Eq.

30
of the main text.
∎

will remember the distance from
y
to the last length-
q
jump.
For the sake of the proof, let
c_{q}=\infty
for all other
q
.

•

For
i=2
to
m
:

–

\Gamma_{i}=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}):\textrm{dist}((x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),y)<c_{q(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}.

–

Update
c_{q}=\textrm{dist}(\lambda^{\prime}_{i},y)
, where
q
is the order of the jump
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
.

•

\Gamma_{m+1}=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\}
is the set of all possible jumps.

The point of this construction is that each sequence
\bm{\beta}
appears exactly once.
We can then rewrite

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{Y}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{1})}}\dots\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{k})}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{I}(\{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{1})},\dots,\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{k})}\})\\
\text{length}(\bm{\beta})=n\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\beta}}\left|A\right),

(109)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{Y}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{1})}}\dots\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{k})}}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}\sum_{j_{m+1}=0}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{m+\sum_{l=1}^{m}{j_{l}}}}{(m+\sum_{l=1}^{m}{j_{l}})!}\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma_{m+1}}^{j_{m+1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{m+1}}\dots\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma_{1}}^{j_{1}}\left|A\right),

(110)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\mathbb{P}_{Y}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{1})}}\dots\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{k})}}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}\int_{\Delta^{m}(t)}dt_{1}\dots dt_{m}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma_{m+1}}^{j_{m+1}}(t-t_{m})}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{m+1}}\dots\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{1}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma_{1}}^{j_{1}}t_{1}}\left|A\right),

(111)

where
\Delta^{m}(t)
is the simplex defined by
0\leq t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{m}\leq t
.
Now, we use the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{S}(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{1})}}\dots\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime(q_{k})}}\sum_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\frac{1}{q_{1}^{\alpha N_{q_{1}}}}\dots\frac{1}{q_{k}^{\alpha N_{q_{k}}}}

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\binom{r2^{q}}{N_{q_{1}}}\dots\binom{r2^{q}}{N_{q_{k}}}\binom{m}{N_{q_{1}},\dots,N_{q_{k}}}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\frac{1}{2^{\alpha q_{1}N_{q_{1}}}}\dots\frac{1}{2^{\alpha q_{k}N_{q_{k}}}}

(113)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\prod_{i=1,\dots,k}\left[\binom{r2^{q}_{i}}{N_{q_{i}}}\frac{t^{N_{q_{i}}}}{N_{q_{i}}!}\frac{1}{2^{\alpha q_{i}N_{q_{i}}}}\right],

(114)

where in the last two lines we use the fact that
m=N_{q_{1}}+\dots+N_{q_{k}}
.
Plugging this bound into
Eq.

105
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{P}_{Y}e^{\mathcal{L}t}\left|A\right)\right\|\leq-1+\prod_{q}\left[1+\frac{3}{2}\binom{r2^{q}}{N_{q}}\frac{t^{N_{q}}}{N_{q}!}\frac{1}{q^{\alpha N_{q}}}\right].

(115)

Now we use
1+x\leq e^{x}
to bound

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{P}_{Y}e^{\mathcal{L}t}\left|A\right)\right\|\leq-1+\exp\left[\frac{3}{2}\sum_{q}\binom{r2^{q}}{N_{q}}\frac{t^{N_{q}}}{N_{q}!}\frac{1}{q^{\alpha N_{q}}}\right].

(116)

Let
q_{*}
be the largest integer such that
2^{q_{*}(\gamma+1)}\leq(\mu r)^{1-\gamma}
.
Note that
\mu r/2^{q(\gamma+1)}>1
for all
q\leq q_{*}
.
We divide the sum in
Eq.

116
into two parts:

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\binom{r2^{q}}{N_{q}}\frac{t^{N_{q}}}{N_{q}!}\frac{1}{2^{\alpha qN_{q}}}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sum_{q=1}^{q_{*}-1}\binom{r2^{q}}{N_{q}}\frac{t^{N_{q}}}{N_{q}!}\frac{1}{2^{\alpha qN_{q}}}}_{=S_{1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{q=q_{*}}^{r}\frac{rt}{2^{(\alpha-1)q}}}_{=S_{2}}.

(117)

First, we estimate
S_{2}
:

\displaystyle S_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-2^{-\alpha}}\frac{rt}{2^{q_{*}(\alpha-1)}}\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{1-2^{-\alpha}}\mu^{(1-\alpha)/(\gamma+1)}}_{=c_{3}}\frac{t}{r^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\gamma+1}-1}}.

(118)

Next, we estimate
S_{1}
.
Note that
N_{q}\geq\frac{\mu r}{2^{q(\gamma+1)}}
for all
q
:

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=1}^{q_{*}-1}\left(\frac{e^{2}rt}{N^{2}_{q}2^{(\alpha-1)q}}\right)^{N_{q}}

(119)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=1}^{q_{*}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{N_{q}},

(120)

where we have used the Stirling’s approximation
x!>x^{x}e^{-x}
.
When
q\rightarrow 1
,
N_{q}\propto r
.
The corresponding term in
S_{1}
decays with
r
at least exponentially as
(t/r)^{r}
.
On the other hand, when
q\rightarrow q_{*}
,
N_{q}\rightarrow 1
and the corresponding term in
S_{1}
is instead suppressed by
2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}
for all
\alpha>3+2\gamma
.
This limit analysis suggests that we should use two different bounds on
S_{1}
for small
q
and large
q
.
For that, we define

\displaystyle q_{0}\equiv\lfloor\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\log_{2}(\mu r^{\kappa})\rfloor\leq\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\log_{2}(\mu r^{\kappa})

(121)

and divide up
S_{1}
into two sums over
q\leq q_{0}
and
q_{0}<q\leq q_{*}
:

\displaystyle S_{1}\leq\underbrace{\sum_{q=1}^{q_{0}-1}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{N_{q}}}_{=S_{1a}}+\underbrace{\sum_{q=q_{0}}^{q_{*}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{N_{q}}}_{=S_{1b}}.

(122)

First, we take the sum over
q\leq q_{0}
. We assume that
\alpha>2\gamma+3
and
t\leq\mu^{2}r/e^{2}
, so that the inner summand satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)\leq 1

(123)

for all
q\leq q_{0}
. Because
N_{q}
decreases with
q
, we upper bound

\displaystyle S_{1a}=\sum_{q=1}^{q_{0}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{N_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}\right)^{N_{q_{0}}}\sum_{q=1}^{q_{0}}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)N_{q}}

(124)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}\right)^{\frac{\mu r}{2^{q_{0}(\gamma+1)}}}

(125)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}\right)^{r^{1-\kappa}}

(126)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{r}e^{-r^{1-\kappa}},

(127)

where in the last line we further assume
t\leq\mu^{2}r/e^{2}
.
This gives the sum over
q\leq q_{0}
in the term
S_{1}
.
To bound the sum over
q_{0}<q\leq q_{*}
, we note that
N_{q-1}\geq N_{q}+1
for all
q<q_{*}
.
To prove this, suppose
N_{q-1}=N_{q}
.
That means

\displaystyle\frac{\mu r}{2^{(q-1)(\gamma+1)}}<N_{q-1}=N_{q}\leq\frac{\mu r}{2^{q(\gamma+1)}}+1

(128)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1>(2^{\gamma+1}-1)\frac{\mu r}{2^{q(\gamma+1)}}>\frac{\mu r}{2^{q(\gamma+1)}},

(129)

which contradicts with
\mu r/2^{q(\gamma+1)}>1
for all
q<q_{*}
.
Therefore,
N_{q-1}\geq N_{q}+1
for all
q<q_{*}
.
Since
N_{q_{*}}=1
, it follows that
N_{q_{*}-n}\geq n+1>n
for all
n\geq 1
.
We make the substitution
n=q_{*}-q
to obtain

\displaystyle S_{1b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=q_{0}}^{q_{*}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{q(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{N_{q}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{q_{*}-q_{0}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{(q_{*}-n)(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{n},

(130)

again assuming that
\alpha>3+2\gamma
and
e^{2}t/(\mu^{2}r)<1
.
Now, using the fact that
q_{*}-n\geq q_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle 2^{(q_{*}-n)(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\leq 2^{q_{0}(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\leq r^{\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}.

(132)

Plugging this into the sum yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{q_{*}-q_{0}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}2^{(q_{*}-n)(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{q_{*}-q_{0}}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r}r^{\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}\right)^{n}

(133)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}}\sum_{n=0}^{q_{*}-q_{0}-1}\left(\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}}\right)^{n}

(134)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}}}

(135)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{2\frac{e^{2}}{\mu^{2}}}_{=c_{2}}\frac{t}{r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}},

(136)

assuming that
\frac{e^{2}t}{\mu^{2}r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Combining everything, we have

\displaystyle S_{1}+S_{2}\leq c_{1}\left(\frac{t}{r}e^{-r^{1-\kappa}}\right)+c_{2}\frac{t}{r^{1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)}}+c_{3}\frac{t}{r^{\frac{\alpha-1}{1+\gamma}-1}}.

(137)

We make the simplification that
\kappa=1-\gamma
, so that

\displaystyle 1-\kappa(2\gamma+3-\alpha)=1-(1-\gamma)(\alpha-3-2\gamma)=\alpha-2\underbrace{-2\gamma-\gamma\alpha+3\gamma+2\gamma^{2}}_{=o(1)}.

(138)

In addition, for all
\gamma>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\gamma}
that may depend on
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle e^{-r^{\gamma}}\leq c_{\gamma}\frac{1}{r^{\alpha-3}}

(139)

for all
r>0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{t}{r}e^{-r^{\gamma}}\leq c_{\gamma}\frac{t}{r^{\alpha-2}}.

(140)

Substituting
Eqs.

140
and
138
into
Eq.

137
and letting
c=c_{1}c_{\gamma}+c_{2}+c_{3}
, we have the desired bound:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{P}_{Y}e^{\mathcal{L}t}\left|A\right)\right\|\leq c\frac{t}{r^{\alpha-2-o(1)}},

(141)

which is exactly
Eq.

8
in the main text.
